Towards Landau-Ginzburg models for cominuscule Spaces via the exceptional cominuscule family
Peter Spacek, Charles Wang

Abstract
. We present projective Landau-Ginzburg models for the exceptional cominuscule homogeneous spaces 𝕆 ​ ℙ 2 = E 6 sc / P 6 \mathbb{OP}^{2}=E_{6}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{6} and E 7 sc / P 7 E_{7}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{7} , known respectively as the Cayley plane and Freudenthal variety. These models are defined on the complement X can ∨ X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}} of an anti-canonical divisor of the “Langlands dual homogeneous spaces” 𝕏 ∨ = P ∨ \ G ∨ \mathbb{X}^{\vee}=P^{\vee}\backslash G^{\vee} in terms of generalized Plücker coordinates, analogous to the canonical models defined for Grassmannians, quadrics and Lagrangian Grassmannians in [ MR20 , PRW16 , PR13 ] . We prove that these models for the exceptional family are isomorphic to the Lie-theoretic mirror models defined in [ Rie08 ] using a restriction to an algebraic torus, also known as the Lusztig torus , as proven in [ Spa22 ] . We also give a cluster structure on ℂ ⁡ [ X ∨ ] \mathbb{C}[X^{\vee}] , prove that the Plücker coordinates form a Khovankii basis for a valuation defined using the Lusztig torus, and compute the Newton-Okounkov body associated to this valuation. Although we present our methods for the exceptional types, they generalize immediately to the members of other cominuscule families.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a projective homogeneous space
X=G/P
, for a simple and simply-connected
G
and parabolic subgroup
P
. The (small) quantum cohomology of such
X
has a long history of research, starting initially with the consideration of Grassmannians by Witten in
[
Wit95
]
—see
[
Ful04
]
for a survey. In this article, we will be considering mirror symmetry statements regarding the (small) quantum cohomology ring of
X
via a
Landau-Ginzburg model
. In this context, a Landau-Ginzburg model consists of a mirror space
X^{\vee}
and a superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{q}
which is a regular function on
X^{\vee}
such that the
Jacobi ring
of
\mathcal{W}_{q}
is isomorphic to the (small) quantum cohomology ring of
X
with localized quantum parameters, i.e.

qH^{*}(X)_{\mathrm{loc}}\cong\mathbb{C}[X^{\vee}]/\langle\partial\mathcal{W}_{q}\rangle.\vskip-2.5pt

In
[
Rie08
]
, a Lie-theoretic construction of a Landau-Ginzburg model is given and shown to be isomorphic to Peterson’s presentation of the small quantum cohomology of general projective homogeneous spaces as given in
[
Pet97
]
. The superpotential of this model is defined completely intrinsically, using natural maps on the Langlands dual Lie group
G^{\vee}
, namely the dual maps associated to a set of Chevalley generators on the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
of
G^{\vee}
.

In practice it is more convenient to have a Landau-Ginzburg model whose superpotential is expressed in terms of coordinates on the mirror space. For example, such LG-models are used in
[
PRW16
]
and
[
Cas20
]
to prove mirror conjectures for certain homogeneous spaces on the level of
D
-modules and homological algebra, respectively, and in
[
RW19
]
to construct a family of polytopes characterizing toric degenerations of Grassmannians; we will remark more on this towards the end of the section. This is especially the case for homogeneous spaces that have natural coordinates in the form of (generalized) Plücker coordinates. Hence, the results in
[
Rie08
]
were considered for a number of families of homogeneous spaces that were particularly accessible, namely Grassmannians
[
MR20
]
, quadrics
[
PRW16
]
and Lagrangian Grassmannians
[
PR13
]
. These families are all
cominuscule
homogeneous spaces, which implies that the Plücker coordinates on the Langlands dual homogeneous space
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=P^{\vee}\backslash G^{\vee}
can be defined in terms of projections on a
minuscule
representation. The structure of these representations is particularly well-understood—see e.g.
[
Gre08
]
or
[
Gec17
]
—and for the families mentioned above the representations are classical matrix representations.

In this paper, we consider the exceptional cominuscule family: the Cayley plane,
E_{6}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{6}
(often written as
\mathbb{OP}^{2}
), and the Freudenthal variety,
E_{7}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{7}
, where
E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}
denotes the simply-connected Lie group of type
E
and rank
n
. Although less studied than the classical homogeneous spaces, the geometry of the exceptional cominuscule family is still well-known—see e.g.
[
IM05
]
for an overview and calculation of the Chow ring of the Cayley plane. Moreover, the ring structure of the quantum cohomologies of the exceptional family has been described in detail in
[
CMP08
]
; there the quantum cohomologies of all cominuscule homogeneous spaces are presented in a uniform way. However, to our knowledge, the only known Landau-Ginzburg models available until now are Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic model and the first author’s Laurent polynomial expression for the model on an algebraic torus, sometimes called the
Lusztig torus
, as given in
[
Spa22
]
, neither of which give expression in terms of coordinates on the mirror space.

We present Plücker coordinate Landau-Ginzburg models
(X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}},\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}})
for the exceptional cominuscule family. The superpotentials are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}}{p_{0}}+\frac{p_{9}^{\prime}}{p_{8}}+\frac{q_{13}}{q_{12}}+\frac{q_{17}}{q_{16}}+\frac{q_{21}}{q_{20}}+\frac{q_{25}}{q_{24}}+q\frac{p_{5}^{\prime\prime}}{p_{16}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}}{p_{0}}+\frac{q_{19}}{q_{18}}+\frac{q_{28}}{q_{27}}+\frac{q_{37}}{q_{36}}+\frac{q_{37}^{\prime}}{q_{36}^{\prime}}+\frac{q_{46}}{q_{45}}+\frac{q_{55}}{q_{54}}+q\frac{p_{10}}{p_{27}}\vskip-3.00003pt

for
E_{6}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{6}
and
E_{7}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{7}
respectively, where the
p_{i}
are Plücker coordinates and the
q_{i}
are certain polynomials in the Plücker coordinates given in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
. The superpotentials are regular functions on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
outside of an anticanonical divisor
D
which defines the mirror space as
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}=\mathbb{X}^{\vee}\setminus D
; we follow the convention of
[
PRW16
]
in calling such models
canonical Landau-Ginzburg models
. Our formulas give an interesting quantum Schubert calculus interpretation of our superpotentials above (see Remark
3.3
for more details): for a given term of a superpotential, its numerator is equal to the quantum product of
\sigma_{1}
with its denominator, multiplied by the degree of its denominator as a polynomial in the Plücker coordinates. Because the sum of the degrees of the denominators is equal to the index
I
in each case—
12
for the Cayley plane and
18
for the Freudenthal variety—we see that
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}
is equal in some sense to
I
copies of the hyperplane class, and thus represents the anticanonical class, e.g. in the Jacobi ring. Similar observations were made in type
A
in
[
MR20
]
and in type
C
in
[
PR13
]
. We are working on a generalization of this observation that we believe holds for all cominuscule spaces, and this result will appear in a future article.

Our main tool for producing these formulas is the expression of generalized minors in terms of the coordinates of the Lusztig torus, which we summarize in Algorithms
3.11
and
3.12
. One of our main results can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
)
.

For cominuscule
E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n}
, the canonical Landau-Ginzburg model is isomorphic to Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic Landau-Ginzburg model.

We will introduce the Lie-theoretic Landau-Ginzburg model in Section
2.2
.

Including our results, canonical models are now available for four out of the five cominuscule families: Grassmannians, Lagrangian Grassmannians, quadrics, and the exceptional family. The remaining family, consisting of the orthogonal Grassmannians
\mathrm{OG}(n,2n)
of maximal isotropic subspaces of
\mathbb{C}^{2n}
with respect to the standard inner product, is expected to have a model that is mostly similar to that of the Lagrangian Grassmannians. All canonical models, including for the exceptional case, have superpotentials consisting of the sum of quotients of polynomials in the Plücker coordinates. For Grassmannians these polynomials were linear, whereas for quadrics, Lagrangian Grassmannians, and (conjecturally) orthogonal Grassmannians these polynomials are quadratic. In contrast, the canonical model for the Cayley plane has a term with cubic polynomials, and the canonical model for the Freudenthal variety has a term with quartic polynomials as well as two terms with cubic polynomials.

In fact, our methods apply to all cominuscule homogeneous spaces, with completely analogous proofs. The models obtained in this way will coincide with the canonical models for the Grassmannians, Lagrangian Grassmannians, and quadrics, and our methods will provide similar models for the orthogonal family. In the present form, our approach only handles one member of a family at the time; however, with general representation theoretic knowledge and the associated combinatorial tools, it should be possible to perform our methods either inductively or in generality. This is in contrast with some of the previous approaches using properties of the particular representations.

Specifically, the methods in
[
PRW16
,
PR13
,
MR20
]
use specific details about the fundamental representations of the corresponding Lie groups to express their generalized minors as literal minors of matrix representations obtained from the associated standard representations. This dependence makes generalizing these approaches impractical, especially since the representations of
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
cannot be given as wedge products of natural representations. On the other hand, our approach is similar in spirit to that of
[
PR18
]
, which compares both the Lie-theoretic and canonical LG-models with an intermediate obtained from the Lusztig torus, although the details in our situation are significantly more complicated. For example, the torus expansions of Plücker coordinates obtained in Lemma 7.3 of
[
PR18
]
contain up to two terms, while the example computed in Appendix
A
here has
45
terms. Furthermore, the numerators and denominators of some of the terms of our potentials are of degree
3
or
4
in Plücker coordinates, complicating matters further.

In
[
PRW16
]
, it is noted that the results of
[
GLS08
]
imply that the coordinate rings of
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}
and
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
carry a cluster structure. In particular, we view
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}
as an open cluster variety and
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
as a compactification of
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}
given by frozen variables, whose divisors form an anticanonical divisor. On the level of coordinate rings, this means that we obtain
\mathbb{C}[X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}]
from
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
by inverting frozen variables. In Section
4
, we give a Plücker coordinate description for the exceptional cominuscule family of the cluster structures given in
[
GLS08
]
. The cluster variables are given in Lemmas
4.3
and
4.5
, and the quivers are given in Figures
4.1
and
4.2
. In order to compute the Plücker coordinate expressions for the cluster variables, we formulated Algorithm
4.6
for expressing generalized minors in terms of Plücker coordinates. This algorithm is reminiscent of the
subduction algorithm
for
Khovanskii bases
, and we were able to use it to define a
valuation

\nu
on the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
. With this, we proved the following theorems:

Theorem 1.2

(Lemma
5.2
and Proposition
5.6
)
.

For minuscule
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=(E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n})^{\vee}
, the valuation
\nu
defined on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
above has
one-dimensional leaves
, and the associated
Newton-Okounkov body

\Delta(\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}],\nu)
is equal to the convex hull of the valuations of the Plücker coordinates on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
.

Theorem 1.3

(Proposition
5.7
)
.

For minuscule
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=(E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n})^{\vee}
, the Plücker coordinates form a Khovanskii basis for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
with respect to the valuation
\nu
defined on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
above.

Although we only gave the statements above for
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n}
, our proofs do not depend on the exceptional types in a meaningful way, and we propose in Conjecture
5.9
that the Plücker coordinates form a Khovanskii basis for a family of valuations depending on a choice of reduced expression for a particular Weyl group element. In particular, we obtain from this a family of Newton-Okounkov bodies as the convex hulls of the valuations of Plücker coordinates. While our conjecture makes sense for any homogeneous space, we are unsure what additional conditions—if any—are required to obtain Khovanskii bases and Newton-Okounkov bodies.

We expect that our main results—these Plücker coordinate canonical models, the Plücker coordinate cluster structures, and the Khovanskii bases and Newton-Okounkov bodies—can be formulated type-independently for cominuscule and possibly more general homogeneous spaces. This stems from the fact that many of the intermediate results are type-independent themselves: the Lie-theoretic model of
[
Rie08
]
holds for general homogeneous spaces; the Laurent polynomial expression for this model in
[
Spa22
]
holds for all cominuscule spaces and is currently being extended to adjoint spaces by the first author; the Lie-theoretic and canonical models are compared using results by
[
GLS11
]
which hold on the coordinate rings of general unipotent cells; and finally the cluster structures given by
[
GLS08
]
are also valid on general unipotent cells. In fact, in Proposition
3.8
we give a criterion expressing the generators of the coordinate rings given in
[
GLS11
]
in terms of Plücker coordinates that holds for general cominuscule homogeneous spaces. Thus, the only obstruction to presenting canonical models and cluster structures type-independently is expressing the generalized minors appearing in
[
GLS08
]
and
[
GLS11
]
in terms of Plücker coordinates.

In
[
RW19
]
, the authors study a combinatorial version of mirror symmetry in the form of an equality of polytopes defined using the auxiliary data of
cluster structures
. There, cluster structures are used to construct a family of Newton-Okounkov associated with
X
and a family of polytopes associated with the superpotential on
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}
. These families are indexed by
seeds
in the cluster structures, and it is shown that these two polytopes coincide for any choice of seed. While we do not pursue the story here, we provide many of the necessary ingredients, and it would be interesting to explore the combinatorics and see whether there is any relation between our Newton-Okounkov bodies and those coming from the cluster structure.

Apart from being of interest on their own, the canonical models for homogeneous spaces have appeared in a number of other contexts as well. Firstly, it was conjectured in
[
Rie08
]
that solutions to the quantum differential equations of homogeneous spaces can be obtained from oscillatory integrals associated to the Landau-Ginzburg models. In
[
LT17
]
this conjecture was proven for
minuscule
homogeneous spaces using the language of
D
-modules and the Langlands correspondence. However, as noted there, the isomorphism given in this proof is not constructed explicitly. In
[
PRW16
]
, an explicit injective morphism between the
D
-modules in question is given using the canonical mirror model for quadrics; for odd-dimensional quadrics (that are cominuscule but
not
minuscule) an implicit isomorphism was known before so that their morphism gave an isomorphism; for even-dimensional quadrics (that are both cominuscule
and
minuscule) the results of
[
LT17
]
implied that their injective morphism is in fact an isomorphism. We believe that the canonical model presented for the exceptional cominuscule family will lead to an analogous explicit morphism of
D
-modules, which will then be an isomorphism by
[
LT17
]
, but we do not verify this here.

Finally, in
[
Cas20
]
, the canonical model for Grassmannians was modified to prove
homological mirror symmetry
for Grassmannians
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
with
n
prime. This is an isomorphism of triangulated categories between the Fukaya category of a variety and the (bounded) derived category of coherent sheaves on its mirror. With canonical models available for four out of the five cominuscule families, these results form promising first steps in homological mirror symmetry for homogeneous spaces.

The outline of this article is as follows. In Section
2
, we set up our conventions and notation, and recall background on Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic Langdau-Ginzburg models, the Cayley plane, and the Freudenthal variety. In Section
3
, we define our canonical models, give their Plücker coordinate expressions, and prove the isomorphism with Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic model. In Section
4
, we review background on cluster structures and give Plücker coordinate cluster structures for the Cayley plane and Freudenthal variety, and in Section
5
, we define our Newton-Okounkov body and show that the Plücker coordinates give Khovanskii bases in both cases. In the appendices, we provide extra data and examples from our computations in Sections
3
and
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

In the first two subsections (Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
), we will consider general complete homogeneous spaces
X
for a simple and simply-connected complex algebraic group
G
of rank

. These spaces are also often called

. We will work with these spaces in their (Plücker) embeddings as the closed orbits of the highest weight vectors in the projectivizations of the corresponding irreducible representations. Afterwards, beginning from Subsection

is
cominuscule
.

2.1.
Conventions and notation

We write
\mathfrak{g}
for the Lie algebra of

and choose a set
(e_{1},f_{1},h_{1},\ldots,e_{n},f_{n},h_{n})

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{u}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{-}
with
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{-}
generated by
\{e_{i}\}
and
\{f_{i}\}
respectively, and with
\mathfrak{t}
spanned by
\{h_{i}\}
.

The Cartan decomposition of
\mathfrak{g}
gives rise to subgroups of
G

U_{+}
and
U_{-}
be the (upper and lower) unipotent subgroups of
G
with
\mathfrak{u}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{-}
as Lie algebras, and let
T
be the maximal torus of
G
with
\mathfrak{t}
as Lie algebra. We obtain a Borel subgroup
B_{+}=TU_{+}
and its opposite
B_{-}=TU_{-}
; they have
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{-}
as Lie algebras. The Borel subgroup defines aa unique parabolic subgroup

containing

\mathfrak{u}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{+}\oplus\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{u}_{-}=\mathfrak{g}

the set of indices such that

\mathfrak{p}=\langle e_{i},h_{i},f_{j}~|~i\in\{1,\ldots,n\},~j\in I_{P}\rangle.

In this case we say that

Denote by

.

As

{\ell_{0}}=\ell+{\ell_{P}}
). However, it will be more convenient to use the reversed reduced expression for
w_{0}

w_{0}=s_{r_{{\ell_{0}}}}\cdots s_{r_{\ell+1}}s_{r_{\ell}}\cdots s_{r_{1}},

(2.7)

obtained from
w_{0}=w_{0}^{-1}
.

Finally, we associate two elements in
G^{\vee}
to each
s_{i}\in W
:

\dot{s}_{i}=x^{\vee}_{i}(1)y^{\vee}_{i}(-1)x^{\vee}_{i}(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{s}_{i}=x^{\vee}_{i}(-1)y^{\vee}_{i}(1)x^{\vee}_{i}(-1)=\dot{s}_{i}^{-1}.

(2.8)

For an arbitrary
w\in W
with reduced expression
w=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{d}}
we find an associated element in
G^{\vee}
by setting
\dot{w}=\dot{s}_{i_{1}}\cdots\dot{s}_{i_{d}}
and
\bar{w}=\bar{s}_{i_{1}}\cdots\bar{s}_{i_{d}}
. Note that
\bar{w}
and
\dot{w}^{-1}
have the reverse product of simple reflections, i.e.
\dot{w}^{-1}=\bar{s}_{i_{d}}\cdots\bar{s}_{i_{1}}
. We write

T^{P}=(T^{\vee})^{W_{P}}\subset T^{\vee}

(2.9)

for the part of
T^{\vee}
that is invariant under the action
W_{P}\times T^{\vee}\to T^{\vee}
given by
(w,t)\mapsto\dot{w}t\dot{w}^{-1}
. Clearly,
T^{P}
has dimension
\#I^{P}
.

2.2.
Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic mirror model and a local Laurent polynomial expression

The mirror models we construct will be shown to be isomorphic to the Landau-Ginzburg models that have been constructed for general homogeneous spaces in
[
Rie08
]
. In this subsection we will briefly discuss the construction given in
[
Rie08
]
and the local Laurent polynomial expression for its superpotential given by the first author in
[
Spa22
]
.

2.2.1.
The Lie-theoretic mirror model

In this subsection
X=G/P
denotes a general homogeneous spaces with
P
an arbitrary parabolic subgroup. The mirror model consists of a subvariety of the open Richardson variety associated to
(w_{P},w_{0})
. This open Richardson variety is given by:

X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}=\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{w_{P},w_{0}}=\bigl(B^{\vee}_{+}w_{P}B^{\vee}_{-}\cap B^{\vee}_{-}w_{0}B^{\vee}_{-}\bigr)/B^{\vee}_{-}~\subset~G^{\vee}/B^{\vee}_{-}.

(2.10)

There exists an isomorphism

\Psi:X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}

(2.11)

to the variety

\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}=B^{\vee}_{-}\bar{w}_{0}^{-1}\cap U^{\vee}_{+}T^{P}\bar{w}_{P}U^{\vee}_{-}~\subset~G^{\vee}.

(2.12)

(See
[
Rie08
]
, Section 4.)

Remark 2.4
.

Compared to
[
Rie08
]
, we have translated the definition of
\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
by right multiplication by
\bar{w}_{0}^{-1}
. For a detailed description of
\Psi
, see Remark 3.1 of
[
Spa22
]
.

In
[
Pet97
]
, the quantum cohomology of a generalized flag variety
G/P
is presented as the coordinate ring of what is now known as the
Peterson variety
(see e.g.
[
Rie08
]
, paragraph 3.2). Inside this (non-reduced) variety there is an open stratum whose coordinate ring is isomorphic to the quantum cohomology localised at the quantum parameters (see e.g.
[
Rie08
]
, equation (3.2)). In
[
Rie08
]
, a function on
\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
is defined whose critical locus is isomorphic to this open stratum. The isomorphism
\Psi
of equation (
2.11
) then yields a subvariety of
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}
whose coordinate ring is isomorphic to the localized quantum cohomology of
X
.

These results have been reformulated in
[
MR20
]
, Theorem 6.5; see also
[
PR13
]
, Section 4. There, a Jacobi ring of
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}
with respect to a superpotential replaces the critical locus in the original formulation. That is, the localised quantum cohomology of
X=G/P
is isomorphic to the coordinate ring of
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}
modulo the ideal generated by derivatives of the superpotential:

Theorem 2.5

(
[
Rie08
]
, Theorem 4.1)
.

Let
X=G/P
be a complete homogeneous space with
G
a simple, simply-connected algebraic group over
\mathbb{C}
and with
P
a parabolic subgroup. There exists a superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}:X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}\to\mathbb{C}
(given in Definition
2.8
) such that

qH^{*}(X)_{\mathrm{loc}}\cong\mathbb{C}[X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}]/\langle\partial\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\rangle,

where
qH^{*}(X)_{\mathrm{loc}}
is the (small) quantum cohomology of
X
with all quantum parameters inverted and where
\langle\partial\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\rangle
is the ideal generated by the derivatives of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}
along
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}
.

The superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}
is presented in
[
PR13
]
as the pull-back of a superpotential defined on
\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
along the isomorphism
\Psi:X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}\overset{\sim}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
from equation (
2.11
). To state this superpotential, we introduce to the following subset of
U^{\vee}_{-}
:

U_{-}^{P}=U^{\vee}_{-}\cap B^{\vee}_{+}\bar{w}_{P}\bar{w}_{0}B^{\vee}_{+}=U^{\vee}_{-}\cap B^{\vee}_{+}(\dot{w}^{P})^{-1}B^{\vee}_{+}~\subset~U^{\vee}_{-}.

(2.13)

(Note that
w_{P}w_{0}=(w^{P})^{-1}
, so that
\bar{w}_{P}\bar{w}_{0}
and
(\dot{w}^{P})^{-1}
differ by a torus element and the two definitions of
U_{-}^{P}
coincide.) This set has the following property:

Lemma 2.6

(
[
PR13
]
, Section 4)
.

Every
z\in\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
has a
unique
decomposition
z=u_{+}t\bar{w}_{P}u_{-}
with
u_{+}\in U^{\vee}_{+}
,
t\in T^{P}
and
u_{-}\in U_{-}^{P}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The proof of this result in
[
PR13
]
can be carried over to the general case without any modification, so it will be omitted here.

The superpotential on
\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}
is now defined as follows:

Definition 2.8
.

Define the superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}}:\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}\to\mathbb{C}
as the map:

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}}:\quad z=u_{+}t\bar{w}_{P}u_{-}\longmapsto\mathcal{E}^{*}(u_{+}^{-1})+\mathcal{F}^{*}(u_{-}),

where
\mathcal{E}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(e^{\vee}_{i})^{*}
and
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f^{\vee}_{i})^{*}
, and where the decomposition of
z=u_{+}t\bar{w}_{P}u_{-}
is the unique decomposition with
u_{-}\in U_{-}^{P}
as stated in Lemma
2.6
. Moreover, the superpotential
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}:X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\times T^{P}\to\mathbb{C}
mentioned in Theorem
2.5
is given by
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}=\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}}\circ\Psi
with
\Psi
given in equation (
2.11
).

2.2.2.
A local Laurent polynomial expression for the superpotential

From this point forward, we will restrict our attention to
cominuscule
homogeneous spaces. Recall that parabolic subgroups containing a given Borel subgroup can be associated to a subsets of vertices of the Dynkin diagram, with the Borel subgroup corresponding to the full diagram and a maximal parabolic corresponding to a single vertex. We will write
P=P_{k}
if
P
is maximal parabolic and associated to the
k
th vertex. The homogeneous space
X=G/P_{k}
is called cominuscule if the fundamental coweight
{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
is
minuscule
, i.e. it satisfies

\langle\!\langle{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}},\alpha\rangle\!\rangle\in\{-1,0,+1\},\quad\forall\alpha\in\Phi,

(2.14)

where
\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
denotes the dual pairing between the character and the cocharacter lattice.

In
[
Spa22
]
a Laurent polynomial expression is given for
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}}
restricted to the following open, dense subvariety:

\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{P}=B^{\vee}_{-}\bar{w}_{0}^{-1}\cap U^{\vee}_{+}T^{P}\bar{w}_{P}U^{\circ}_{-}~\subset~\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P},

where
U^{\circ}_{-}\subset U_{-}^{P}
is the open, dense algebraic torus defined by

U^{\circ}_{-}=\{y^{\vee}_{r_{\ell}}(a_{\ell})\cdots y^{\vee}_{r_{1}}(a_{1})~|~a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\}.

(2.15)

Recall that
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{\ell})
is the sequence of indices of our fixed reduced expression for
w^{P}
(see equation
2.6
). The fact that
U^{\circ}_{-}\subset U_{-}^{P}
is open and dense is well-known, see for example Lemma 4.8(ii) of
[
PR13
]
or Lemma 5.2 of
[
Spa22
]
. The superpotential is parametrized by a certain Weyl group element: let
w^{\prime\prime}
be the minimal representative of the coset
w_{P}s_{k}W_{P}
and set
w^{\prime}=w^{P}(w^{\prime\prime})^{-1}
, writing its length as
\ell(w^{\prime})={\ell^{\prime}}
, then define
\mathcal{I}
as the set of reduced subexpressions of
w^{\prime}
in the fixed reduced expression of
w^{P}
.

The Laurent polynomial superpotential is now given as follows:

Proposition 2.9

(
[
Spa22
]
, Theorem 5.7)
.

Let
X=G/P
be a cominuscule complete homogeneous space with
G
a simply-connected, simple, complex algebraic group and
P=P_{k}
a (maximal) parabolic subgroup. The restriction
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{P}}
of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\vee}_{P}}
to
\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{P}
has the following Laurent polynomial expression:

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{P}}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}+q\frac{\sum_{(i_{j})\in\mathcal{I}}\prod_{j=1}^{{\ell^{\prime}}}a_{i_{j}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}}.

Here
z\in\mathcal{Z}^{\circ}_{P}
is uniquely decomposed as
z=u_{+}t\bar{w}_{P}u_{-}
with
u_{-}=y^{\vee}_{r_{\ell}}(a_{\ell})\cdots y^{\vee}_{r_{1}}(a_{1})\in U^{\circ}_{-}
. Also,
q\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is given by
q=\alpha^{\vee}_{k}(t)
with
t\in T^{P}
.

In Corollary 8.12 of
[
Spa22
]
, it is shown that the numerator of the quantum term can be enumerated using subsets of a quiver defined in
[
CMP08
]
and that these subsets can be obtained from a given one using two elementary moves, which can be represented graphically in the same way: by replacing a vertex in the subset with the one below (resp. above) it in the same column under the condition that all outgoing (resp. incoming) arrows at the original vertex do not point to elements in the subset. (See Lemma 8.9 and Proposition 8.11 of
[
Spa22
]
.) This quiver will appear again later, see Remark
4.4
. We would like to note that these combinatorial operations coincide with the “excited moves” defined on
d
-complete posets in
[
NO19
]
, where equivariant
K
-theory is used to derive a skew hook formula.

2.3.
Generalized Plücker coordinates and the coordinate ring of
(U_{-}^{P})^{T}

For cominuscule homogeneous spaces
X=G/P_{k}
, a consequence of the geometric Satake correspondence is that the Schubert classes that form a basis of the cohomology are in one-to-one correspondence with a set of projective coordinates called
(generalized) Plücker coordinates
on the
Langlands dual homogeneous space

\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=P^{\vee}_{k}\backslash G^{\vee}=\widetilde{P}^{\vee}_{k}\backslash\widetilde{G}^{\vee}.

(2.16)

(Both expressions give the same homogeneous space, and we mostly use the second.) See
[
MR20
]
, p. 3, for more references regarding the geometric Satake correspondence. Thus, if we reformulate the superpotential in Definition
2.8
in terms of Plücker coordinates, we obtain a description of the localized quantum cohomology in terms of Schubert classes and relations between them. Plücker coordinate expressions for Rietsch’s superpotential have been given for Grassmannians
[
MR20
]
, quadrics
[
PRW16
]
, and Lagrangian Grassmannians
[
PR13
]
. In
[
PRW16
]
, this was obtained using a description of the coordinate ring of
U_{-}^{P}
by
[
GLS11
]
.

2.3.1.
Minuscule representations and generalized Plücker coordinates.

With the assumption that
X=G/P
is cominuscule, we will be able to define the (generalized) Plücker coordinates on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}=\widetilde{P}^{\vee}\backslash\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
using a minuscule representation. To do this, we first recall a few properties of such representations. Since we consider minuscule representations of
\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
with corresponding roots
\Phi^{\vee}
, we adapt our notation to this situation.

First, we will need a general statement concerning the structure of minuscule representations and the action of Chevalley generators; this result can be found in
[
Gre08
]
or
[
Gec17
]
. Fix a non-degenerate, symmetric, bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
on the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}
(e.g. the Killing form) and let
c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=2(\mu^{\vee},\alpha^{\vee}_{i})/(\alpha^{\vee}_{i},\alpha^{\vee}_{i})
for
\mu^{\vee}
an element of the weight lattice.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
V(\lambda^{\vee})
be a minuscule representation, i.e. a highest weight representation with as highest weight
\lambda^{\vee}
satisfying (
2.14
). Denote its set of weights by
M(\lambda^{\vee})
. Then the following holds:

(i)

For all
\mu^{\vee}\in M(\lambda^{\vee})
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})\in\{-1,0,+1\}
.

(ii)

Each weight space is one-dimensional.

(iii)

Given a highest weight vector
v_{\lambda^{\vee}}^{+}
, there exists a basis of weight vectors
\{v_{\mu^{\vee}}\}
such that
v_{\lambda^{\vee}}=v_{\lambda^{\vee}}^{+}
and such that

\displaystyle e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\mu^{\vee}+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=-1$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle f^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\mu^{\vee}-\alpha^{\vee}_{i}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=+1$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle h^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})\,v_{\mu^{\vee}}.

(iv)

For any vector
v\in V(\lambda^{\vee})
and
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\displaystyle(e^{\vee}_{i})^{2}\cdot v

\displaystyle=0=(f^{\vee}_{i})^{2}\cdot v,

\displaystyle e^{\vee}_{i}e^{\vee}_{j}e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v

\displaystyle=0=f^{\vee}_{i}f^{\vee}_{j}f^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v\qquad\text{if $a_{ij}=c_{i}(\alpha^{\vee}_{j})=-1$,}

\displaystyle e^{\vee}_{i}f^{\vee}_{j}\cdot v

\displaystyle=0=f^{\vee}_{j}e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v~\qquad\text{if $a_{ij}<0$.}

Combining this result with the definition of
\dot{s}_{i}
and
\bar{s}_{i}
in (
2.8
), we find

Corollary 2.11
.

With the notation of Theorem
2.10
,
s_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=\mu^{\vee}-c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})\,\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
and

\displaystyle\dot{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\mu^{\vee}+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=-1$,}\\
v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=\hphantom{+}0$},\\
-v_{\mu^{\vee}-\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=-f^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=+1$,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\bar{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-v_{\mu^{\vee}+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=-e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=-1$,}\\
v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=\hphantom{+}0$,}\\
v_{\mu^{\vee}-\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=f^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=+1$.}\end{array}\right.

Conversely, we have

\displaystyle e^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\mu^{\vee}+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=\dot{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}=-\bar{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=-1$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle f^{\vee}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v_{\mu^{\vee}-\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=\bar{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}}=-\dot{s}_{i}\cdot v_{\mu^{\vee}},&\text{if $c_{i}(\mu^{\vee})=+1$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

With these general facts about the structure of minuscule representations, we return to defining the Plücker coordinates on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
. Recall that by assumption
{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
is minuscule; this implies that the fundamental weight
-w_{0}\cdot{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
is minuscule as well:

\langle\!\langle-w_{0}\cdot{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}},\alpha\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle-w_{0}\cdot{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}},-w_{0}\cdot\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}},\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\in\{-1,0,+1\}

(where
\alpha\in\Phi
and
\alpha^{\prime}=-w_{0}\cdot\alpha
), compare (
2.14
). Note that
-w_{0}\cdot{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
equals
{\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}}
for
\sigma:\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,n\}
the symmetry involution of the Dynkin diagram of
\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
(note that
\sigma
is trivial in the cases of type
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{2n}
and
E_{7}
). We will use the minuscule representation
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})=V(-w_{0}\cdot{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}})
to define the Plücker coordinates as follows.

By Theorem
2.10
, there is a natural weight basis for
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})
, which we denote by
\{v_{i}\}
. Writing
v_{0}
for the basis vector of
lowest
weight (i.e. of weight
-{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
), the dual representation
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})^{*}
has highest weight vector
v_{0}^{*}
(of weight
{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
), where
\{v_{i}^{*}\}
is the dual basis. Note that
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})^{*}
is a
right
-representation with action
v_{i}^{*}\cdot g=\bigl(v\mapsto v_{i}^{*}(g\cdot v)\bigr)
, and that
[v_{0}^{*}]\cdot\widetilde{P}^{\vee}=[v_{0}^{*}]\in\mathbb{P}\bigl(V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})^{*}\bigr)
. Hence, we obtain an embedding
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}\bigl(V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})^{*}\bigr)
given by
\widetilde{P}^{\vee}g\mapsto[v_{0}^{*}\cdot g]
, using the fact that
\widetilde{P}^{\vee}
and
\{y^{\vee}_{k}(a)~|~a\in\mathbb{C}\}
generate
\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
. This induces the coordinates:

Definition 2.12
.

For any weight basis vector
v_{i}
as above and
g\in\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
, define
p_{i}:\widetilde{G}^{\vee}\to\mathbb{C}
by

p_{i}(g)=(v_{0}^{*}\cdot g)(v_{i})=v_{0}^{*}(g\cdot v_{i}).

(2.17)

These maps induce projective coordinates on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
called
(generalized) Plücker coordinates
.

We will abuse notation and call the maps
p_{i}:\widetilde{G}^{\vee}\to\mathbb{C}
Plücker coordinates as well.

2.3.2.
The coordinate ring of
U_{+}^{P}=(U_{-}^{P})^{T}\subset\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
.

Recall from equation (
2.13
) that we defined
U_{-}^{P}=U^{\vee}_{-}\cap B^{\vee}_{-}(\dot{w}^{P})^{-1}B^{\vee}_{-}
. In Proposition 8.5 of
[
GLS11
]
, the coordinate rings of these
unipotent cells
have been described using a dual PBW basis compatible with a reduced expression. However, the result is formulated using cells of
U^{\vee}_{+}
instead of
U^{\vee}_{-}
: we will have to use the
transposition anti-automorphism

\cdot^{T}
to translate their results to cells of
U^{\vee}_{-}
. Recall that this anti-automorphism is given by mapping
x^{\vee}_{i}(a)
to
y^{\vee}_{i}(a)
and vice versa, while acting trivially on
\widetilde{T}^{\vee}
. Note that
\bar{s}_{i}^{T}=\dot{s}_{i}=\bar{s}_{i}^{-1}
.

We would like to emphasize that the results in
[
GLS11
]
hold for general unipotent cells, but we will only consider the case of
U_{-}^{P}
for
P=P_{k}
with
{\varpi_{k}}
cominuscule here.

Writing
U_{+}^{P}
for the transpose of
U_{-}^{P}
, we clearly have

U_{+}^{P}=(U_{-}^{P})^{T}=U^{\vee}_{+}\cap B^{\vee}_{+}\dot{w}^{P}B^{\vee}_{+}.

(2.18)

The coordinates are defined on
U_{+}^{P}
using the embedding of
U^{\vee}_{+}
in the completed universal enveloping algebra
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
of
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
. Namely, they form a subset of a basis for the
graded dual
of
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
. As the universal enveloping algebra is in particular a infinite-dimensional vector space, we need to be more careful in defining the dual. We will make use of the Chevalley generators
(e^{\vee}_{1},f^{\vee}_{1},h^{\vee}_{1},\ldots,e^{\vee}_{n},f^{\vee}_{n},h^{\vee}_{n})
.

Consider the Cartan (or root space) decomposition

\mathfrak{g}^{\vee}=\mathfrak{t}^{\vee}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha^{\vee}\in\Phi^{\vee}}\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{\alpha^{\vee}},

where for
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}
a positive root we have that
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
and
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{-\alpha^{\vee}}\subset\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{-}
are one-dimensional subspaces (see for example
[
Hum78
]
, Section 8.5). Clearly,
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}=\mathbb{C}e^{\vee}_{i}
is spanned by the corresponding Chevalley generator,
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+s_{i}(\alpha^{\vee}_{j})}=\mathbb{C}[e^{\vee}_{i},e^{\vee}_{j}]
is spanned by the commutator of the generators if
(s_{i}s_{j})^{2}\neq 1
, and so on.

Now, consider the
positive root lattice

\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}\cong\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
, i.e. all non-negative integral linear combinations of the simple roots
\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
. For
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
, denote by
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})
the subspace spanned by those pure tensors
e^{\vee}_{i_{1}}\cdots e^{\vee}_{i_{j}}
such that exactly
d_{i}
of the factors have index
i
. For example,
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee}_{i})=\mathbb{C}e^{\vee}_{i}=\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}_{i}}
for
\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\in\Delta\!^{\vee}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(2\alpha^{\vee}_{1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{2})=\mathbb{C}e^{\vee}_{1}e^{\vee}_{1}e^{\vee}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}e^{\vee}_{1}e^{\vee}_{2}e^{\vee}_{1}\oplus\mathbb{C}e^{\vee}_{2}e^{\vee}_{1}e^{\vee}_{1}
. (If
2\alpha^{\vee}_{1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{2}\in\Pi^{\vee}
, note that
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{2\alpha^{\vee}_{1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{2}}=\mathbb{C}[e^{\vee}_{1},[e^{\vee}_{1},e^{\vee}_{2}]]\subset\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(2\alpha^{\vee}_{1}+\alpha^{\vee}_{2})
.) This endows
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
with an
\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
-grading that naturally extends the root space decomposition of
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
(i.e.
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})
for
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
). The graded dual is defined as the direct sum of the duals of these finite-dimensional subspaces,

\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}\bigr)^{*}_{\mathrm{gr}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha^{\vee}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}}\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})\bigr)^{*}.

To define a basis on this dual, we start by recalling that we fixed a reduced expression for the minimal representative
w^{P}\in W^{P}
of the coset
w_{0}W_{P}
and denoted its sequence of indices by
(r_{i})_{i=1}^{\ell}
where
\ell=\ell(w^{P})
, see equation (
2.6
). As a Weyl group element,
w^{P}
maps a set
\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}=\{{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(j)~|~j=1,\ldots,\ell\}\subset\Pi^{\vee}
of positive roots to negative roots. It is well known that these are given by

{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(1)=\alpha^{\vee}_{r_{\ell}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(j)=s_{r_{\ell}}\cdots s_{r_{\ell-j+2}}(\alpha^{\vee}_{r_{\ell-j+1}})\quad\text{for $j\in\{2,\ldots,\ell\}$,}

(2.19)

(for example, this follows from
[
Hum78
]
, Section 10.2). Note that
s_{r_{\ell}}\cdots s_{r_{\ell-j+2}}
are the first
j-1
factors of the reduced expression for
(w^{P})^{-1}
obtained by reversing the simple reflections in (
2.6
). Since
w_{0}=w^{P}w_{P}
, it is easy to see that
\Pi^{\vee}=\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}\sqcup\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
, where
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
is the set of positive (co-) roots mapped to negative ones by
w_{P}
. We also remark that both
\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}
and
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
are
bracket closed
subsets: if
\alpha^{\vee},\beta^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}
and
\alpha^{\vee}+\beta^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}
then
\alpha^{\vee}+\beta^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}
, and the same holds for roots in
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
. (This follows from the linearity of action of the Weyl group elements on roots.) See also
[
GLS11
]
, Section 4.3, and the references therein.

For each of the positive roots
{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)
,
m\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, we choose a basis element
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)
for
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)}
. In particular, we take
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(1)=e^{\vee}_{k}
, as
{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(1)=\alpha^{\vee}_{k}
.

Remark 2.13
.

In
[
GLS11
]
, the basis elements for the one-dimensional subspaces
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)}
are not defined more explicitly, nor will we need an explicit choice of basis vector in the following. However, it is easy to define a set of basis elements
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)\in\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)}
for
m\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
using the following algorithm: consider the defining expression of
{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)
in (
2.19
); remove all simple reflections acting trivially; replace each remaining simple reflection
s_{i}
by the corresponding
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits(e^{\vee}_{i})
; and finally replace
\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
with the corresponding
e^{\vee}_{i}
.

Now, we complete the elements
\{e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)~|~m=1,\ldots,\ell\}
to a basis of
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
by choosing generating elements in
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}}
for
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}\setminus\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}=\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
. To simplify notation, we will denote these additional elements by
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)
for
m\in\{\ell+1,\ldots,\ell+{\ell_{P}}\}
, where
\ell+{\ell_{P}}={\ell_{0}}=\ell(w_{0})
is the number of positive roots. Here we will also assume that the Chevalley generators
e^{\vee}_{i}
are part of this basis. With a basis for
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
, we can in turn define a PBW basis for
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
, the elements of which we denote by

e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(\mathbf{m})=\bigr(e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(1)\bigr)^{m_{1}}\bigl(e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(2)\bigr)^{m_{2}}\cdots\bigl(e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}({\ell_{0}})\bigr)^{m_{\ell_{0}}},

(2.20)

for
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{{\ell_{0}}})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{{\ell_{0}}}
. As each
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)
is homogeneous with respect to the grading on
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
(this follows from Remark
2.13
), the PBW basis elements are homogeneous as well.

We can now define the dual PBW basis for
\bigl(\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}\bigr)^{*}_{\mathrm{gr}}
by taking the finite-dimensional vector space dual bases and extending the functionals trivially on the complement. We denote these dual basis elements
p^{\text{GLS}}_{\mathbf{m}}
, so that
p^{\text{GLS}}_{\mathbf{m}}\bigl(e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(\mathbf{n})\bigr)
equals
1
if
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\ell_{0}}
and
0
otherwise. Whenever
\mathbf{m}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)
with a
1
at the
m
th position, we simply write
p^{\text{GLS}}_{m}=p^{\text{GLS}}_{\mathbf{m}}
; indeed, in this case we have
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(\mathbf{m})=e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(m)
. This basis is called the
dual PBW basis compatible with the reduced expression for
w^{P}
. Using the natural embedding of
U^{\vee}_{+}
into
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
, we can consider the maps
p^{\text{GLS}}_{m}
on
U^{\vee}_{+}
as well.

From the results in
[
GLS11
]
, it follows that the coordinate ring of
U_{+}^{P}
is generated by the dual basis elements
p^{\text{GLS}}_{m}
for
m\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
where
\ell=\ell(w^{P})
. However, to fully express the coordinate ring, we also need to introduce a number of
generalized minors
in the sense of Fomin and Zelevinsky
[
FZ99
]
. Recall that the highest weight space of any highest weight representation is one-dimensional. Thus, taking a highest weight vector
v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}
, we can define the projection
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}})\to\mathbb{C}v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}
(parallel to a basis of weight vectors). We denote the coefficient
c
such that a vector
v\in V({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}})
projects to
cv_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}
by

\bigl\langle v,v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}\bigr\rangle=c.

(2.21)

Note that the highest weight representation
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}})
is implicit in the notation. Using the action of
\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
on these representations, we obtain maps
\widetilde{G}^{\vee}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
g\mapsto\langle g\cdot v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+},v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}\rangle
. For
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
, the generalized minors are given in Definition 1.4 of
[
FZ99
]
as

\Delta_{w_{1}({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}),w_{2}({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}})}(g)=\Bigl\langle(\bar{w}_{1})^{-1}g\bar{w}_{2}\cdot v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+},v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}\Bigr\rangle.

(2.22)

(
[
FZ99
]
gives a different formula, but their formula can easily be seen to agree with this expression. See for example Proposition 7.2 of
[
GLS11
]
.) In particular, we will be concerned with the following minors:

\Delta_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}},(w^{P})^{-1}({\varpi^{\vee}_{i}})}(g)=\Bigl\langle g(\dot{w}^{P})^{-1}\cdot v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+},v_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{i}}}^{+}\Bigr\rangle.

(2.23)

Note that we wrote
(\dot{w}^{P})^{-1}=\overline{(w^{P})^{-1}}
to simplify the expression; it has the reverse reduced expression compared to
w^{P}
. We can now state the result:

Proposition 2.14

(
[
GLS11
, Proposition 8.5]
)
.

The cell
U_{+}^{P}=(U_{-}^{P})^{T}\subset\widetilde{G}^{\vee}
, see equation (
2.18
), is an affine variety with coordinate ring isomorphic to

\mathbb{C}[p^{\text{GLS}}_{1},\ldots,p^{\text{GLS}}_{\ell}][\Delta_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{1}},(w^{P})^{-1}({\varpi^{\vee}_{1}})}^{-1},\ldots,\Delta_{{\varpi^{\vee}_{n}},(w^{P})^{-1}({\varpi^{\vee}_{n}})}^{-1}].

(2.24)

3.
The canonical models for cominuscule
E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n}

In this section we will define
canonical mirror models

(X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}},\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}})
for the Cayley plane and the Freudenthal variety in terms of Plücker coordinates. (The terminology “canonical” follows that of
[
PRW16
]
.) In addition to the Plücker coordinate expressions, our main results are:

Theorem 3.1
.

For cominuscule
E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n}
, the variety
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}
is isomorphic to the open Richardson variety
X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}=\mathcal{R}^{\vee}_{w_{P},w_{0}}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For cominuscule
E_{n}^{\mathrm{sc}}/P_{n}
, the pull-back of
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}
under the isomorphism of Theorem
3.1
equals
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}}
. In other words, the Landau-Ginzburg model
(X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}},\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}})
is isomorphic to Rietsch’s Lie-theoretic Landau-Ginzburg model
(X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}},\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Lie}})
.

We follow the general approach as used in
[
PR13
,
PRW16
]
. In particular, we will use a presentation of the coordinate ring of
U_{-}^{P}
derived from the results in
[
GLS11
]
(which we introduced in Section
2.3
) and express it in terms of Plücker coordinates using representation theory. However, the varieties considered in these references are homogeneous under
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}
and
\mathrm{Spin}_{2n}
, and the representation theory of these groups is well-established (although by no means trivial). For the groups of type
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
, the representations have a more complicated structure. Moreover, in contrast with
[
PR13
,
PRW16
]
, we will perform most calculations restricted to the open, dense torus
U^{\circ}_{-}
(or more precisely: its transpose) to simplify the computations. This method is in fact similar to the approach in
[
PR18
]
, where the analogous torus is considered for odd-dimensional quadrics; however, the representation-theoretic considerations there are significantly more straightforward compared to the methods that had to be developed for the exceptional cominuscule family.

We will denote the homogeneous polynomials appearing in the numerators and denominators of our superpotentials
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}
by
q_{i}
; this should not cause any confusion with quantum parameters as cominuscule homogeneous spaces always have
\mathop{\mathrm{rk}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}H_{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})=1
and thus also only a single quantum parameter, which we denote by
q
.

Remark 3.3
.

We observed that the numerators and denominators in our superpotentials are related in the quantum cohomology ring of
X
as follows. After identifying Plücker coordinates with Schubert classes via the geometric Satake correspondence, we may interpret the numerator and denominator of each term in our superpotentials as elements of the quantum cohomology ring by interpreting all products as quantum products. We observe after these identifications that for each term in our superpotentials, its numerator equals the quantum product of its denominator with the hyperplane class
\sigma_{1}
, scaled by the degree of the denominator (or numerator) as a polynomial in Plücker coordinates. For example, for our Cayley plane superpotential in equation (
3.3
), we compute that
q_{25}=3\sigma_{1}*q_{24}
where all products (including those in the Plücker coordinate expressions of
q_{24}
and
q_{25}
) are interpreted as quantum products.

In order to verify this relationship between the numerators and denominators of the superpotential, we used the description of the quantum cohomology ring for the Cayley plane presented in Proposition 3 and Theorem 31 of
[
CMP08
]
as well as the description of the quantum cohomology ring of the Freudenthal variety using Theorems 6 and 34 of
[
CMP08
]
.

Similar statements have appeared in type
A
in e.g.
[
MR20
,
Kal22
]
, and in type
B
in
[
PR18
, Section 9]
. This scaling by degree was not observed in the type-
A
Grassmannians
\mathrm{Gr}(k,n)
because there each term was a ratio of degree-
1
expressions in the Plücker coordinates, while the type
B
superpotential had one term (
W_{m-1}
in the notation of
[
PR18
]
) which contributed only
p_{1}
, despite being a ratio of degree-
2
polynomials. Furthermore, in type
C
,
[
PR13
]
observed that each term of their superpotential contributes only one hyperplane class
p_{1}
. These observations suggest the existence of a general construction of canonical Plücker coordinate superpotentials for certain homogeneous spaces, and we are investigating this topic in an upcoming article.

X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\overset{\varphi}{\longrightarrow}U_{-}^{P}\overset{\pi}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{X}^{\vee}\supset X^{\vee}_{\mathrm{can}}.

(3.7)

Recall from equation (

The map

to

and its universal cover in

The map

is an isomorphism that cannot be expressed so explicitly. It is given by mapping

(with

) to the unique

such that

. Here, the variety

) as

that each

has a unique decomposition as

with

,

and

. The map

Remark 3.4
.

This criterion holds for general cominuscule homogeneous spaces, and we will prove it in this context. Thus, until the end of this subsection,

We first need the following characterization of the set
\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}=\{{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)~|~m=1,\ldots,\ell\}
, and of its complement
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
which coincides with the positive roots mapped to negative roots by
w_{P}
. This characterization is a classical result, although not readily available in literature, so we will include its proof using fundamental results for the reader’s convenience. It can also easily be obtained from the bijection between Weyl group elements and inversion sets of roots, see e.g. Section 3.1 of
[
BCMP16
]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Every positive root
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\in\Pi^{\vee}=\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}\sqcup\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
has
d_{k}\in\{0,1\}
. Moreover,
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
if and only if
d_{k}=0
; and
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}
if and only if
d_{k}=1
.

Proof.

The fact that
{\varpi^{\vee}_{k}}
is minuscule is well-known to be equivalent to the fact that the longest root
\alpha^{\vee}_{0}\in\Phi^{\vee}
has coefficient
1
in front of
\alpha^{\vee}_{k}
in the decomposition of
\alpha^{\vee}_{0}
in terms of simple roots. (See e.g. exercise VI.24(c) of
[
Bou68
]
.) Thus, we know that
d_{k}\in\{0,1\}
.

Now, it is well known that there is a sequence of (not necessarily distinct) simple roots
(\alpha^{\vee}_{i_{j}})_{j=1}^{h}
, where
h=0pt(\alpha^{\vee})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
is the height of
\alpha^{\vee}
, such that we have
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\alpha^{\vee}_{i_{j}}
and such that for every
h^{\prime}\leq h
the partial sum
\sum_{j=1}^{h^{\prime}}\alpha^{\vee}_{i_{j}}\in\Phi^{\vee}
is a root as well. (See Corollary 10.2 of
[
Hum78
]
.)

Suppose
d_{k}=0
, then we clearly have that
i_{j}\neq k
for all
j
. Since
\{\alpha^{\vee}_{i}~|~i\neq k\}\subset\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
and
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
is bracket closed, every partial sum lies in
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
and thus
\alpha^{\vee}
as well. Conversely, since
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
can be considered as the positive roots of the root system with as Dynkin diagram the diagram of
G^{\vee}
with the
k
th vertex removed, we have that
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
implies that
d_{k}=0
.

As
\Pi^{\vee}_{w^{P}}
is the complement of
\Pi^{\vee}_{P}
in
\Pi^{\vee}
, the statement follows.
∎

Recall that we defined a
\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
-grading on the completed universal enveloping algebra
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
of
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+}
in Section
2.3
. Here,
\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}\cong\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
is the positive root lattice of all non-negative integral linear combinations of the simple roots
\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
. We defined
\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})\subset\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}
as the subspace spanned by pure tensors
e^{\vee}_{i_{1}}\cdots e^{\vee}_{i_{j}}
such that exactly
d_{i}
of the factors have index
i
, where
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\alpha^{\vee}_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
. We noted there that
\mathfrak{u}^{\vee}_{+\alpha^{\vee}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})
for
\alpha^{\vee}\in\Pi^{\vee}
, so we directly conclude that:

Corollary 3.6
.

For
j\in\{1,\ldots,{\ell_{0}}\}
,
e^{\vee}_{w^{P}}(j)\in\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}({\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(j))
. Let
d_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
{\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
. If
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, then
d_{k}=1
. Else, i.e.
j\in\{\ell+1,\ldots,{\ell_{0}}\}
, we have
d_{k}=0
.

We also need the following simple observation regarding the action of the elements of the completed universal enveloping algebra on
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})
:

Lemma 3.7
.

If
u\in\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})
for
\alpha^{\vee}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Delta\!^{\vee}
and
v\in V({\varpi^{\vee}_{\sigma(k)}})
a vector of weight
\mu
, then
u\cdot v
is either a vector of weight
\mu+\alpha^{\vee}
or the zero vector.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.10
, the statement holds for the Chevalley generators
e^{\vee}_{i}\in\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee}_{i})
. By definition
u\in\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}(\alpha^{\vee})
for
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}\alpha^{\vee}_{i}
can be decomposed into a linear combination of pure tensors in which each
e^{\vee}_{i}
appears
d_{i}
times. The statement now follows by induction.
∎

Now, recall that the Plücker coordinates

are defined using the minuscule representation

and its dual, where

. The criterion relating Plücker coordinates to the dual PBW basis defined in

is given as follows:

If

Proof.

Let

On the other hand,

to

By Lemma

\widehat{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}_{+}\bigl({\beta^{\vee}_{P}}(m)\bigr)
map

to

with

satisfying

. Corollary

implies that of the coefficients

(where

. Thus, at least one of

Thus, it remains to show that

is a non-zero multiple of

∎

(1)

In this section we first review the cluster structures presented in

for homogeneous spaces of simply-laced type. We then give Plücker coordinate descriptions for these cluster structures using an algorithm we formulated for expressing generalized minors as polynomials in the Plücker coordinates.

4.1.
Cluster algebras and varieties

A
cluster structure
on a commutative algebra
A
is a combinatorial presentation of
A
in terms of
seeds
and
mutations
. Cluster algebras come in two types,
\mathcal{A}
-cluster algebras and
\mathcal{X}
-cluster algebras, differing in the definition of their mutations. We will only consider
\mathcal{A}
-cluster algebras here. To define seeds, we need to fix the field
\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of rational functions in
n
variables.

A seed
\Sigma
of rank
l
is a pair
\Sigma=(\mathbf{x},B)
and an integer
m\geq l
, where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{m}
is a tuple of algebraically independent elements, and
B
is an
m\times l
integer matrix whose upper
l\times l
submatrix
M
is skew-symmetrizable, meaning that there is a diagonal matrix
D
with positive integer entries on the diagonal such that
DM=-(DM)^{T}
. The elements of
\mathbf{x}
are called
cluster variables
, and the first
l
are called
mutable
while the remaining
m-l
are called
frozen
. Furthermore,
B
is called an
exchange matrix
, and in the case that
D=I
, then we may think of
B
as the adjacency matrix of a directed graph
Q
known as a
quiver
. Mutation is an operation on seeds that produces new seeds. For
1\leq k\leq l
, the mutation of
\Sigma
in direction
k
is a new seed
\Sigma^{\prime}=(\mu_{k}(\mathbf{x}),\mu_{k}(B))
. We will not work with mutations here, so we omit their full definition.

Given an
initial seed

\Sigma
, let
S_{\!\mathcal{A}}
denote the set of cluster variables appearing in any seed
\Sigma^{\prime}
reachable from
\Sigma
by any (finite) sequence of
\mathcal{A}
-mutations. Then

is an
\mathcal{A}
-cluster algebra if

. Finally, an affine variety

is an
\mathcal{A}
-cluster variety if
\mathbb{C}[X]
is an
\mathcal{A}
-cluster algebra. To each frozen variable

, we associate a divisor

, and to each seed

we associate a torus
\mathbb{T}_{\!\Sigma}\hookrightarrow X
. The union of all tori

is a dense, open subset of

often called the

, and

is a
(partial) compactification
of

, where the union is over the set of frozen variables. Roughly,

can be thought of as generalizations of toric varieties where the dense torus is replaced by a union of (infinitely many)
cluster tori
identified along birational
mutation
maps, and the torus-invariant divisors are replaced by divisors corresponding to
frozen variables
.

For our purposes, we will need a concrete description of the cluster structure in terms of Plücker coordinates, and we will provide such descriptions for the cominuscule exceptional types in Section
4
. Cluster structures for homogeneous spaces
P^{-}\backslash G
were given in
[

]
for

of simply-laced Dynkin type in terms of generalized minors. Their general construction depends on a choice of Lie group

, parabolic subgroup

, and reduced expression for the longest word
w_{0}
of the Weyl group
W
of
G
, having as prefix a reduced expression for the longest word
w_{P}
of the subgroup

. We present a simplified version of their construction for our particular cases of the Cayley plane and the Freudenthal variety.

4.3.
Cluster algebras and mirror symmetry

In the version of mirror symmetry we have been working with so far, the superpotentials
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{can}}
defined on the Langlands dual spaces encode enumerative information about the original spaces via their Jacobi rings. One of the main reuslts in
[

provides a combinatorial interpretation of mirror symmetry using cluster structures on Grassmannians through two families of polytopes,

and

Instead, we used the following algorithm. In a preprocessing step, we first compute the expansions of the Plücker coordinates in torus coordinates on

Algorithm 4.6
.

(1)

For a given generalized minor
\phi(i,{\varpi^{\vee}_{r_{i}}})
, first compute its expansion in torus coordinates using Algorithm
3.11
with the direction change of Remark
4.2
.

(2)

Fix the monomial order
<
on

a_{1}>a_{2}>\dots>a_{\ell}
of the torus coordinates.

(3)

Let

(4)

(5)

Next, let

(6)

If

(7)

Else if we obtain

5.
Valuations, Khovanskii bases, and Newton-Okounkov bodies

Algorithm
4.6
is very reminiscent of the
subduction algorithm
for
Khovanskii bases
. In this section we will show that the Plücker coordinates form a Khovanskii basis for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
, and we then use this to give a
Newton-Okounkov body
for
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
. Throughout this section, we denote by
A
a domain that is also a finitely generated algebra over an algebraically closed field
k
, and by
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{Q}^{r}
a subgroup of the rational lattice with an ordering
\succ
of its elements that respects the group operation.

Definition 5.1

(
[
KM19
]
, Definition 2.1)
.

A
valuation
is a function
\nu:A\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\Gamma
such that

(1)

For all
f,g\in A\setminus\{0\}
with
f+g\neq 0
, we have
\nu(f+g)\succeq\min_{\succ}(\nu(f),\nu(g))
.

(2)

For all
f,g\in A\setminus\{0\}
, we have
\nu(fg)=\nu(f)+\nu(g)
.

(3)

For all
f\in A\setminus\{0\}
and
0\neq c\in k
, we have
\nu(cf)=\nu(f)
.

For
g\in\Gamma
, let
F_{\succeq g}
be the vector space
\{f\in A\setminus\{0\}\mid\nu(f)\succeq g\}\cup\{0\}
, and define
F_{\succ g}
analogously. We then define the
associated graded algebra

\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(A)=\bigoplus_{g\in\Gamma}F_{\succeq g}/F_{\succ g}.

If for all
g\in\Gamma
, the quotient
F_{\succeq g}/F_{\succ g}
is at most one-dimensional, then we say that
\nu
has
one-dimensional leaves
.

The following lemma gives an important family of valuations with one-dimensional leaves.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
S\subset k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a finitely generated subalgebra of a polynomial ring, and let
\nu
be the valuation sending
f\in S
to the exponent vector of its degree-lexicographically minimal term. Then
\nu
has one-dimensional leaves.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and suppose
a,b\in F_{\succeq g}\setminus F_{\succ g}
. (If
a,b\in F_{\succ g}
, then
a,b=0
in the quotient
F_{\succeq g}/F_{\succ g}
.) Then
\nu(a)=\nu(b)=g
, so the minimal terms of
a
and
b
have the same exponent vector. Thus, there is some constant
c\in k
such that the minimal terms of the expansions of
a
and
cb
are equal, so that
\nu(a-cb)\succ g
, and hence
a-cb\in F_{\succ g}
. Hence,
a=cb
in
F_{\succeq g}/F_{\succ g}
, and we conclude that
F_{\succeq g}/F_{\succ g}
has dimension at most
1
.
∎

We next define
Khovanskii bases
and the
Newton-Okounkov body
associated to a valuation.

Definition 5.3

(
[
KM19
]
, Definition 2.5)
.

A subset
B\subset A
is called a
Khovanskii basis
for
A
with respect to the valuation
\nu
if the image of
B
in
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(A)
is a set of algebra generators for
\mathrm{gr}_{\nu}(A)
. Khovanskii bases give a
subduction algorithm
for writing elements of
A
as polynomials in the elements of
B
.

Definition 5.4

(
[
KM19
]
, Definition 2.21)
.

Let
A
further be positively graded by
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. The
Newton-Okounkov body

\Delta(A,\nu)
associated to
A
and a valuation
\nu
is the closed convex set

\Delta(A,\nu)=\overline{\mathrm{conv}\!\left(\bigcup_{i>0}\tfrac{1}{i}{\nu(A_{i}\setminus\{0\})}\right)}

where
A_{i}
denotes the set of homogeneous elements of
A
of degree
i
.

Although the Newton-Okounkov body associated to a valuation
\nu
is generally very far from being a polytope, the following proposition gives an important family of examples where
\Delta(A,\nu)
is a polytope.

Proposition 5.5

(
[
KM19
]
, Corollary 2.24, Remark 2.25)
.

Let
Y
be a
d
-dimensional projective variety
Y
embedded in some
\mathbb{P}^{N}
, and let
A
denote the homogeneous coordinate ring of
Y
with respect to this embedding. Let
\nu
be a valuation on
A
with one-dimensional leaves. Then
\deg(Y)=\mathrm{Vol}(\Delta(A,\nu))
, where
\deg(Y)
is computed with respect to the projective embedding of
Y
and
\mathrm{Vol}
refers to the normalized lattice volume so that standard lattice
d
-simplex has volume
\frac{1}{d!}
. If furthermore
A
has a finite Khovanskii basis with respect to
\nu
, then
\Delta(A,\nu)
is a rational polytope and
\mathrm{Proj}(A)
has a degeneration to a toric variety whose normalization is the toric variety associated to
\Delta(A,\nu)
.

Now let
A=\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
be the coordinate ring of the Cayley plane (
n=6
) or the Freudenthal variety (
n=7
), with
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
-grading given by the total degree in Plücker coordinates. We use the degree-lexicographic term order
<
on
\mathbb{C}[a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}]
from Algorithm
4.6
to define a valuation
\nu_{n}:A\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
as follows. Given a polynomial
f
in the Plücker coordinates, compute its expansion in the coordinates of
U_{+}^{\circ}
using Algorithm
3.11
, and define
\nu_{n}(f)
to be the exponent vector of the degree-lexicographic minimal term
\mathrm{in}_{<}(f)
of
f
. It is straightforward to check that
\nu_{n}
is a valuation. Furthermore, because the image of the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}[a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}]
induced by
U_{-}^{\circ}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
is a finitely generated subalgebra, Lemma
5.2
implies that
\nu_{n}
has one-dimensional leaves. Proposition
5.5
thus shows that the normalized volume of
\Delta(A,\nu_{n})
is equal to the degree of
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
. By Definition
5.4
, the suitably scaled valuation of any homogeneous element of
A
is contained in the Newton-Okounkov body
\Delta(A,\nu_{n})
, and thus so is the convex hull of the set of suitably scaled valuations of any finite subset of homogeneous elements of
A
. In particular, if we can find
B\subset A
such that

\delta(B,\nu_{n})=\mathrm{conv}\!\left(\bigcup_{i>0}\tfrac{1}{i}{\nu_{n}(B\cap A_{i}\setminus\{0\})}\right)

has normalized volume equal to the degree of
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
, then
\delta(B,\nu_{n})=\Delta(A,\nu_{n})
.

Proposition 5.6
.

The Newton-Okounkov body
\Delta(A,\nu_{n})
is equal to the convex hull of the valuations of the Plücker coordinates on
\mathbb{X}^{\vee}
.

Proof.

The proof is by direct computation. We give the valuations of Plücker coordinates in Appendix
B
, and we used Polymake
[
GJ00
,
AGH+17
]
to verify that their convex hulls have the correct volumes.

For the Cayley plane (
n=6
), we obtain a
16
-dimensional polytope with volume
78
, which is equal to the degree of the Cayley plane. For the Freudenthal variety (
n=7
), we obtain a
27
-dimensional polytope with volume
13110
, which is equal to the degree of the Freudenthal variety. We conclude that in both cases, the respective Newton-Okounkov bodies are given by the convex hulls of the valuations of the respective Plücker coordinates.
∎

In particular,
\Delta(A,\nu_{n})
is a rational polytope (in fact a
0/1
-polytope, which follows from the fact that we used a minuscule representation) with vertices given by the valuations of Plücker coordinates. Moreover, we computed in each case that each polytope contains no lattice points other than its vertices.

At this point, we would like to conclude that the Plücker coordinates form Khovanskii bases. However, the converse direction of Proposition
5.5
requires some further assumptions, e.g. as in Proposition 17.4 of
[
RW19
]
. These are satisfied in our case because we work with the projectively normal minimal (Plücker) embeddings of homogeneous spaces
P\backslash G
into the projectivizations of the corresponding irreducible representations
V({\varpi^{\vee}_{n}})
, and because our Newton-Okounkov bodies do not contain any extra lattice points. We summarize this discussion in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.7
.

The Plücker coordinates give Khovanskii bases for the coordinate rings
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
with respect to the valuations
\nu_{n}
.

Remark 5.8
.

Even though the Plücker coordinates give a finite Khovanskii basis for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{X}^{\vee}]
with respect to the valuation
\nu_{n}
, this is not sufficient to guarantee that Algorithm
4.6
terminates for any input. If this is the case, then in the language of Definition 3.8 and Section 5 of
[
KM19
]
,
\nu_{n}
is called a
subductive valuation
and corresponds to a
prime cone
of the associated tropical variety. While it would be interesting to investigate this further, we do not pursue this here.

None of the above statements depended on the exceptional types in a crucial way. For any homogeneous space
P\backslash G
, we may choose a reduced expression for
w^{P}
which thus gives a parametrization of the corresponding
U_{+}^{\circ}
. We then define a valuation
\nu
on
A=\mathbb{C}[P\backslash G]
by expanding any element in the coordinates of
U_{+}^{\circ}
and taking its degree-lexicographic minimal term, and this valuation will have one-dimensional leaves by an analogous proof to the proof of Lemma
5.2
. We have not verified it ourselves, but we believe the following statement holds
mutatis mutandis
:

Conjecture 5.9
.

Let
X=P\backslash G
be a minuscule homogeneous space with coordinate ring
A
and valuation
\nu
associated to a choice of reduced expression for
w^{P}
as defined above. Then the Plücker coordinates of
X
form a Khovanskii basis for
A
, and the associated Newton-Okounkov body
\Delta(A,\nu)
is a rational polytope which is equal to the convex hull of the valuations of Plücker coordinates.

When
X
is not minuscule, the Plücker coordinates are more difficult to understand, the valuations of the Plücker coordinates will no longer be
0/1
-vectors, and the Newton-Okounkov body may be less well-behaved. However, it would still be interesting to investigate to what extent the above conjecture holds. Moreover if
X
is neither minuscule nor cominuscule,
w^{P}
is no longer
fully commutative
, so that different reduced expressions may not be related by swapping adjacent transpositions, and it would be interesting to investigate the relationship between the Newton-Okounkov bodies obtained for different reduced expressions, and whether there is any relation to cluster mutation.
