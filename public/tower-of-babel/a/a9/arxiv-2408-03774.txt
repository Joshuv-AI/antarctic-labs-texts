Abstract
. We provide an estimate for the number of nontrivial integer points on the Pellian surface t 2 − d ​ u 2 = 1 t^{2}-du^{2}=1 in a bounded region. 1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Pell equation

A
Pell equation
is a Diophantine equation of the form

(1.1)

t^{2}-du^{2}=1,

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is a positive integer that is not a square.
When
d
is a negative integer or a perfect square, the only possible integer solutions are the trivial ones
(t,u)=(\pm 1,0)
.
In this article, we assume that
d\geqslant 2
is not a perfect square. For given
d
, it is convenient to write a solution as
\eta_{d}=t+u\sqrt{d}
. Dirichlet’s unit theorem tells us that the set of integer solutions of (
1.1
) is isomorphic to the group of integers and there exists a unique
fundamental solution

\varepsilon_{d}=t_{1}+u_{1}\sqrt{d}
satisfying

\{\eta_{d}:\text{ solution of (\ref{Pell})}\}=\{\pm\varepsilon_{d}^{n},\ n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
t_{1},u_{1}\geqslant 1
.

Note that
t_{1}^{2}=1+du_{1}^{2}\geqslant 1+d
, so we obtain the lower bound

(1.2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d}\geqslant\sqrt{d+1}+\sqrt{d}.

The equality holds if and only if
d+1
is a perfect square.

Let
h(d)
be the class number of quadratic forms of determinant
1
1

1

We use the term
determinant

d
instead of
discriminant

\Delta
, in order to align with Hooley
[
7
]
and differ from the regular definition
\Delta(ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2})=b^{2}-4ac
. See also
[
4
, Chapter 1.3.D]
for the history and connection between these two terms.

d
. Specifically,
h(d)
is the number of properly primitive classes of indefinite forms
ax^{2}+2bxy+cy^{2}
with determinant
d=b^{2}-ac
. This unusual definition was originally due to Gauss. The advantage is that any integer can be a determinant, instead of having to meet certain criteria modulo
4
.

In order to find an upper bound of
\varepsilon_{d}
, the class number formula
[
7
, Formula (3)]
tells us that

(1.3)

\displaystyle h(d)\log\varepsilon_{d}=\sqrt{d}L_{d}(1),\text{ where }L_{d}(1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
m\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{d}{m}\Big)\frac{1}{m}.

The size of
L_{d}(1)
fluctuates within relatively narrow ranges. From
[
3
, Equation (3.11)]
, we have
L_{d}(1)\ll\log d
. Therefore, on using
h(d)\geqslant 1
, we know that

(1.4)

\log\varepsilon_{d}\ll\sqrt{d}\log d.

1.2.
Density of integer points on the Pellian surface

In this paper we shall study the following counting function of nontrivial integer solutions to the Pell equation with bounded height:

(1.5)

\displaystyle N(B)=\#\{(t,d,u)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:t^{2}-du^{2}=1,\max(|t|,|d|,|u|)\leqslant B,u\neq 0\}.

The growth rate of
N(B)
is closed related to the average size of fundamental solutions.

For
\alpha>0
and
x\geqslant 2
, Hooley
[
7
]
introduced the counting function

S(x,\alpha)=\#\{\eta_{d}:2\leqslant d\leqslant x,\varepsilon_{d}\leqslant\eta_{d}\leqslant d^{\frac{1}{2}+\alpha}\}.

For
0<\alpha\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
, he
[
7
, Theorem 1]
has proved that

(1.6)

S(x,\alpha)\sim\frac{4\alpha^{2}}{\pi^{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}x\text{, when }x\rightarrow\infty.

For
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
, Hooley has suggested a conjecture
[
7
, Conjecture 1]
for the asymptotic behavior of
S(x,\alpha)
when
x\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, his conjecture implies that

(1.7)

S(x,\alpha)\ll_{\alpha}x^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}x.

We are now ready to reveal our first result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
.

(i)

We have

B^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log B)^{2}\ll N(B)\ll_{\epsilon}B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon}.

(ii)

If Hooley’s Conjecture (
1.7
) is true for
\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
, then we also have

N(B)\ll B^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log B)^{2}.

We will combine results from Fouvry and Jouve
[
5
]
and Reuss
[
9
]
for the upper bound in Part(i). The lower bound will be deduced from (
1.6
).

1.3.
The log K3 surface and
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve solutions

A smooth cubic surface
U\subset\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
is said to be
log K3
, if there is a morphism from a smooth, projective surface
\tilde{X}
to the completion
X
of
U
in
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
, which is an isomorphism over
U
, and such that
\tilde{D}=\tilde{X}\setminus U
is a divisor with strict normal crossings whose class in
\mathrm{Pic}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\tilde{X})
is
\omega_{\tilde{X}}^{\lor}
. In particular, it follows from the adjunction formula that
U
is
\log
K3, if
X
itself is smooth over
\mathbb{Q}
.

Let
U
be a log K3 surface. We denote

\displaystyle N_{U}(B)=\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in U(\mathbb{Z}):\max(|x_{i}|)\leqslant B\}.

When
U(\mathbb{Z})
contains an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve
\phi
that is defined over
\mathbb{Z}
, the curve contributes
\asymp B^{\frac{1}{\deg(\phi)}}
points to
N_{U}(B)
. We typically expect the contribution from
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curves to dominate the counting function. Hence it is natural to study the subset
U(\mathbb{Z})^{\circ}
obtained by removing the points in
U(\mathbb{Z})
that lie on any
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. This leads us to consider the following counting function

\displaystyle N_{U}^{\circ}(B)=\#\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in U(\mathbb{Z})^{\circ}:\max(|x_{i}|)\leqslant B\}.

In a recent paper, Browning and Wilsch
[
1
, Conjecture 1.1]
have proposed the following conjecture for the specified logarithmic growth for a certain class of log K3 surfaces.

Conjecture 1.2

(Browning–Wilsch)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{A}^{3}
be a cubic surface that is smooth and log K3 over
\mathbb{Q}
and that is defined by a cubic polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. Denote by
\rho_{U}
the Picard number of
U
over
\mathbb{Q}
and by
b
the maximal number of components of
\tilde{D}(\mathbb{R})
that share a real point. Then

N_{U}^{\circ}(B)\ll_{U}(\log B)^{\rho_{U}+b},

when
B\rightarrow\infty
.

Let
U_{\mathrm{P}}=V(t^{2}-du^{2}-1)\subset\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}}^{3}
be the surface defined by the Pell equation. Surprisingly, every integer point
(t_{0},d_{0},u_{0})
on
U_{\mathrm{P}}
lies on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. This can be verified by the parametrization (see Zapponi
[
14
, Proposition 3]
)

\displaystyle t(z)=

\displaystyle\ (t_{0}+1)u_{0}^{4}z^{2}+2(t_{0}+1)u_{0}^{2}z+t_{0},

\displaystyle d(z)=

\displaystyle\ (t_{0}+1)^{2}u_{0}^{2}z^{2}+2(t_{0}+1)^{2}z+d_{0},

\displaystyle u(z)=

\displaystyle\ u_{0}^{3}z+u_{0}.

This parametrization is consistent with the lower bound for
N(B)
in Theorem
1.1
.

Instead of studying the original Pellian surface, we shall investigate the integer points on the Pellian equation

(1.8)

\displaystyle t^{2}-(z^{2}+k)u^{2}=1,

for some non-zero
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Changing a variable
y=t+uz
takes us to the equation

(1.9)

\displaystyle 2uyz=y^{2}-ku^{2}-1.

We will show in Section
3.2
that this equation defines a log K3 surface when
k=3
.

If we choose
k=1
, then the fundamental solution of (
1.8
) can be calculated as

\varepsilon_{z^{2}+1}=2z^{2}+1+\sqrt{z^{2}+1}\cdot 2z.

Therefore, the integer solution
(y,u,z)
to the equation (
1.9
) that corresponds to the fundamental solution
t_{1}+u_{1}\sqrt{z^{2}+1}
to the Pell equation (
1.8
) lies on the
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve

y(z)=4z^{2}+1,\quad u(z)=2z,

defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. According to the structure of integer solutions to the Pell equation, one may prove by induction that every integer point on the surface defined by (
1.8
) lies on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Indeed, one may check that the above phenomena happens whenever
k=\pm 1,\pm 2
, or
\pm 4
.

Therefore, we shall focus on the example

(1.10)

\displaystyle t^{2}-(z^{2}+3)u^{2}=1,

with
k=3
, as well as the corresponding log K3 surface defined by

(1.11)

\displaystyle 2uyz=y^{2}-3u^{2}-1.

We will show in Proposition
3.1
that for any integer solution
(t,u,z)
to the equation (
1.10
), if
3\nmid z
,
z^{2}+3
is square-free and
u\neq 0
, then it does not lie on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
.

In Theorem
1.1
, we used Hooley’s heuristics on the typical size of the fundamental solutions to assess the size of the counting function
N(B)
in (
1.5
). Our next result uses a reverse process to extract information about the typical size of the fundamental solutions, assuming Conjecture
1.2
.

1.4.
An almost all lower bound for fundamental solutions

Hooley
[
7
, Conjecture 2]
has proposed the conjecture that for almost all
d
, we have
\log\varepsilon_{d}\gg d^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}
. However, we are still very far away from this result. In the same paper, Hooley has firstly shown that for almost all
d
, we have
\varepsilon_{d}\gg d^{\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon}
. Reuss
[
9
, Corollary 10]
has improved Hooley’s result to
\varepsilon_{d}\gg d^{3-\epsilon}
.

Let
U
be the surface defined by the equation (
1.11
). We will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
.
Assume Conjecture
1.2
is true for the surface
U
. Let
d(z)=z^{2}+3
. Then for almost all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
3\nmid z
and square-free
d(z)
, we have

\displaystyle\log\varepsilon_{d(z)}\gg d(z)^{\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon}.

Remark 1.4
.

The classical work of Ricci
[
10
]
shows that there is a positive proportion of
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
3\nmid z
such that
d(z)=z^{2}+3
is square-free.

Golubeva
[
6
]
has shown that for almost all primes
p
and
d=5p^{2}
, we have
\log\varepsilon_{d}\gg d^{\frac{1}{4}}
. However, much less result is known when
d
is square-free.
For any positive integer
d
, there is a unique way to decompose
d=d_{1}d_{2}^{2}
, where
d_{1}
is square-free. We call
\mathrm{sf}(d):=d_{1}
the
square-free part
of
d
.
The best previous result for the lower bound of the size of fundamental solution for an infinite set of square-free
d
is that of Yamamoto
[
13
, Theorem 3.2; Example (II)]
, in which
\log\varepsilon_{d}\gg(\log d)^{3}
, where
d=\mathrm{sf}\big((3\cdot 2^{m}+3)^{2}-8\big)
. By combining Theorem
1.3
and Remark
1.4
, we obtain infinitely many square-free
d
such that
\log\varepsilon_{d}\gg d^{\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon}
, under the assumption that Conjecture
1.2
is true for the surface
U
.

We recall that the fundamental unit of a real quadratic field
K
is defined as

\varepsilon_{K}=\frac{a+b\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}}{2},

where
\Delta_{K}
is the discriminant of
K
, and
(a,b)
is the pair of smallest positive integers satisfying

a^{2}-\Delta_{K}\cdot b^{2}=\pm 4
.
We shall deduce following result.

Corollary 1.5
.

Assume Conjecture
1.2
is true for the surface
U
. Then there exists infinitely many real quadratic fields
K
, such that

(1.12)

\displaystyle\log\varepsilon_{K}\gg\Delta_{K}^{\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon},

when
\Delta_{K}\rightarrow\infty
.

1.5.
The average of class numbers

Hooley
[
7
, Conjecture 7]
has conjectured that

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leqslant Z}h(d)\sim c_{1}\,Z\cdot\log^{2}Z,

for an explicit constant
c_{1}>0
.
Unsurprisingly, we understand this conjecture as little as Hooley’s other conjectures on the average size of fundamental solutions to the Pell equation.

Sarnak
[
11
]
has considered a similar question, but in a slightly different setting. Let
\mathscr{D}=\{\Delta>0:\Delta\equiv 0,1(\mod 4),d\text{ not a square}\}
be the set of positive discriminants. Let
H(\Delta)
denote the number of inequivalent primitive binary quadratic forms
ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}
of discriminant
\Delta=b^{2}-4ac
, and let
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\Delta}
be the fundamental solution of the Pellian equation
t^{2}-\Delta u^{2}=4
. Let
\mathscr{D}_{x}=\{\Delta\in\mathscr{D}:\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\Delta}\leqslant x\}
. He has shown that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\#\mathscr{D}_{x}}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathscr{D}_{x}}H(\Delta)=\frac{16}{35}\frac{Li(x^{2})}{x}+O(x^{\frac{2}{3}+\epsilon}).

Note that this ordering is different from the usual one.

In a subsequent paper
[
12
]
, Sarnak studied the behavior of
H(d)
along a thin sequence
\Delta(z)=z^{2}-4
, where the fundamental solutions are equal to
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{\Delta(z)}=(z+\sqrt{z^{2}-4})/2
. He has shown that

\displaystyle\sum_{z\leqslant Z}H(z^{2}-4)\sim c_{2}\,Z^{2}\cdot(\log Z)^{-1},

where
c_{2}>0
is an explicit constant.

Let us return to the special values of
d(z)=z^{2}+3
. The average of the class number is dominated by the contributions when
z
is a multiple of 3, namely when the integer point
(t,z,u)
lies on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
. Using Yamamoto’s Theorem
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
, which will be introduced in Section
3
, one may deduce that

(1.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\leqslant Z,3\,\nmid\,z\\
\mu^{2}(z^{2}+3)=1\end{subarray}}h(z^{2}+3)\ll Z^{2}\cdot(\log Z)^{-2}.

Let
U
be the surface defined by the equation (
1.11
). Conditionally, we will prove the following improvement.

Theorem 1.6
.

Assume Conjecture
1.2
is true for the surface
U
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\leqslant Z,3\,\nmid\,z\\
\mu^{2}(z^{2}+3)=1\end{subarray}}h(z^{2}+3)\ll Z^{\frac{9}{5}}\cdot(\log Z)^{\frac{3}{5}}.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1.
The initial decomposition of the Pell equation

Fouvry and Jouve
[
5
]
have considered the following decomposition, which can be traced back to Legendre and Dirichlet.
The Pell equation
(\ref{Pell})
can be rearranged as

(t+1)(t-1)=du^{2}.

Note that
\gcd(t-1,t+1)|2
, so there are three possibles splittings of
d
and
u
, as follows:

•

If
t
is even then
u=u_{1}u_{2},d=d_{1}d_{2}
and
d_{1}u_{1}^{2}-d_{2}u_{2}^{2}=2
.

•

If
t
is odd and
4\nmid d
, then
u=2u_{1}u_{2},d=d_{1}d_{2}
and
d_{1}u_{1}^{2}-d_{2}u_{2}^{2}=1
.

•

If
t
is odd and
4|d
, then
u=u_{1}u_{2},d=4d_{1}d_{2}
and
d_{1}u_{1}^{2}-d_{2}u_{2}^{2}=1
.

We call the above splittings as the
initial decomposition of the Pell equation
.

For
\theta=\pm 1,\pm 2
, we consider the following function

N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})=\#\{(d_{1},d_{2},u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:d_{i}\sim D_{i},u_{i}\sim U_{i},d_{1}u_{1}^{2}-d_{2}u_{2}^{2}=\theta\},

where the notation
n\sim N
means
N<n\leqslant 2N
.
Reuss
[
9
, Theorem 5]
has applied the approximate determinant method to show that

(2.1)

\displaystyle N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll(D_{2}U_{2})^{\varepsilon}\min\big((U_{1}U_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}+U_{1}+U_{2},(D_{1}D_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}+D_{1}+D_{2}\big),

where

\log M=\frac{9}{8}\frac{\log(D_{1}D_{2})\log(U_{1}U_{2})}{\log(D_{1}U_{1}^{2})}.

We also need a result by Fouvry and Jouve
[
5
, Lemma 8]
, which is particularly useful when
D_{1}
or
D_{2}
is very small.
For
D_{1}\leqslant D_{2}
, we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll(D_{1}D_{2})^{\varepsilon}\big((D_{1}D_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}+D_{1}U_{2}\big).

By combining (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we may prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. Assume that
D_{1}\leqslant D_{2}
,
D_{1}D_{2}\leqslant B
and
D_{1}U_{1}^{2}=D_{2}U_{2}^{2}=B
, then for any
\theta=\pm 1
or
\pm 2
, we have

N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon}.

Proof.

When
D_{1}U_{2}\leqslant B^{\frac{7}{12}}
, then (
2.2
) implies

N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll B^{\epsilon}(B^{\frac{1}{2}}+B^{\frac{7}{12}})\ll B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon}.

Next we observe that if
\log(D_{1}D_{2})=k\log B
, then

(2.3)

\displaystyle m:=\frac{\log M}{\log B}=\frac{9}{16}k(2-k).

Since
0\leqslant k\leqslant 1
, the function
m(k)
is an increasing function.

When
D_{1}U_{2}>B^{\frac{7}{12}}
, then considering
D_{2}U_{2}^{2}=B
, we know

D_{1}D_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=D_{1}U_{2}B^{-\frac{1}{2}}>B^{\frac{1}{12}}.

Since
D_{1}D_{2}\leqslant B
, we obtain

D_{2}D_{1}^{-1}=(D_{1}D_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot(D_{1}D_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{-\frac{4}{3}}<B^{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{9}}=B^{\frac{2}{9}}.

On the other hand, using
D_{1}\leqslant D_{2}
, we know

D_{1}D_{2}=(D_{1}^{-1}D_{2})^{3}\cdot(D_{1}D_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{4}\geqslant(D_{1}D_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{4}>B^{\frac{1}{3}}.

In other words, we have
k>\frac{1}{3}
. Since
m(k)
is increasing when
0\leqslant k\leqslant 1
, we have
m(k)>m(\frac{1}{3})=\frac{5}{16}
. Hence we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle M>B^{\frac{5}{16}}.

Therefore, it follows that

M>B^{\frac{2}{9}}\geqslant D_{2}D_{1}^{-1},\text{ which implies }(D_{1}D_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}>D_{2}\geqslant D_{1}.

If we further assume that
k\leqslant\frac{2}{3}
, then by (
2.1
) we have

N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll B^{\epsilon}(D_{1}D_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll B^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{2}{3}+m(\frac{2}{3}))+\epsilon}=B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon},

since
m(k)+k
is also an increasing function.

The remaining case is when
D_{1}U_{2}>B^{\frac{7}{12}}
and
k>\frac{2}{3}
. Note that

U_{1}U_{2}=(BD_{1}^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot(BD_{2}^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}=B^{1-\frac{k}{2}}.

By (
2.4
), we obtain that

M>B^{\frac{5}{16}}>B^{\frac{1}{9}}\geqslant(D_{2}D_{1}^{-1})^{\frac{1}{2}}=U_{1}U_{2}^{-1},\text{ which implies }(U_{1}U_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}>U_{1}\geqslant U_{2}.

We also have

(U_{1}U_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}=B^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{k}{2}+m(k))}.

Note that the function

-\frac{k}{2}+m(k)=\frac{1}{16}k(10-9k)

is decreasing when
k>\frac{5}{9}
. Therefore, by the assumption
k>\frac{2}{3}
and (
2.1
) we have

N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\ll B^{\epsilon}(U_{1}U_{2}M)^{\frac{1}{2}}\ll B^{\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{3}+m(\frac{2}{3}))+\epsilon}=B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon}.

∎

As a remark, one may find that the extreme case appears when
k=\frac{2}{3}
and
D_{1}U_{2}\geqslant B^{\frac{7}{12}}
. This is when
D_{1}D_{2}=B^{\frac{2}{3}}
and
B^{\frac{5}{18}}\leqslant D_{1}\leqslant B^{\frac{1}{3}}\leqslant D_{2}\leqslant B^{\frac{7}{18}}
.

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ \#\{(t,d,u)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:t^{2}-du^{2}=1,d\leqslant B^{\frac{1}{2}},\eta_{d}\leqslant 4B\}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ \sum_{d\leqslant B^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left[\frac{\log 4B}{\log\varepsilon_{d}}\right]\,\ll\,\sum_{d\leqslant B^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left[\frac{\log 4B}{\log d}\right]

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\ \log B\cdot\frac{B^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\log B}\,=\,B^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This implies that
M_{2}(B)=M_{3}(B)+O(B^{\frac{1}{2}})
, where

\displaystyle M_{3}(B)=\#\{(t,d,u)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:t^{2}-du^{2}=1,B^{\frac{1}{2}}<d\leqslant B,t\sim B\}.

If we assume Hooley’s Conjecture (
1.7
) is true for
\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
, we have

\displaystyle M_{3}(B)\leqslant\#\{(t,d,u)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:t^{2}-du^{2}=1,d\leqslant B,t\leqslant 2d^{2}\}\ll B^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log B)^{2}.

By using (
2.5
), this completes the proof of part (2) of Theorem
1.1
.

Unconditionally, we may divide
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
into
\ll(\log B)^{2}
dyadic intervals. By using the initial decomposition of the Pell equation and Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle M_{2}(B)\ll\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}D_{1}D_{2}\leqslant B,D_{1}\leqslant D_{2}\\
D_{1}U_{1}^{2}=D_{2}U_{2}^{2}=B,\ \theta=\pm 2\end{subarray}}N_{\theta}(D_{1},D_{2},U_{1},U_{2})\cdot(\log B)^{2}\ll B^{\frac{7}{12}+\epsilon}.

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We recall the equation (

For all

3.1.
The
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve solutions

The main result of this section is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(t,u,z_{0})
be an integer solution of (
1.10
) such that
3\nmid z_{0}
,
d(z_{0})
square-free and

. Then it does not lie on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Yamamoto
[
13
, Theorem 3.1; Example 4 (I)]
has proved the following lower bound for the fundamental unit of a special class of real quadratic fields.

Theorem 3.2

(Yamamoto)
.

Let
K_{\alpha}=\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{\alpha^{2}\pm 4p}\big)
for a given prime
p
. Assume that
p
splits in
K_{\alpha}
. Then we have

\log\varepsilon_{K_{\alpha}}\gg(\log\Delta_{K_{\alpha}})^{2}.

Let
K
be a real quadratic field. Let
t_{1}+u_{1}\sqrt{d}
be the fundamental solution of the Pell equation for
d=c^{2}\Delta_{K},c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. We have
a^{2}-\Delta_{K}\cdot b^{2}=4
for
a=2t_{1}
and
b=2cu_{1}
. Therefore, for any
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
we have

(3.1)

\varepsilon_{K}\leqslant\varepsilon_{c^{2}\Delta_{K}}.

The following corollary gives a lower bound for the fundamental solution of (
1.10
).

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
z\geqslant 2
which is not a multiple of
3
, and let
d(z)=z^{2}+3
. We have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\log\varepsilon_{d(z)}\gg\big(\log\mathrm{sf}(d(z))\big)^{2}.

Proof.

We choose
\alpha=2z
and
p=3
in Theorem
3.2
. When
3\nmid z
, we know that
p
splits in the real quadratic field
K_{z}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{4z^{2}+12})=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d(z)})
. Therefore, by (
3.1
) and Theorem
3.2
, we get

\log\varepsilon_{d(z)}\geqslant\log\varepsilon_{K_{z}}\gg(\log\Delta_{K_{z}})^{2}\gg\big(\log\mathrm{sf}(d(z))\big)^{2}.

∎

Note that by using Corollary
3.3
and the class number formula (
1.3
), we may obtain the upper bound (
1.13
) for the average of class numbers.

Remark 3.4
.

The lower bound (
3.2
) is indeed sharp. For example, Golubeva
[
6
]
has shown that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{d(z)}\leqslant 2\,\bigg(\Big(\frac{z+\sqrt{d(z)}}{3}\Big)^{n}\Big(\frac{2+\sqrt{d(z)}}{z+1}\Big)\Big(\frac{z-1+\sqrt{d(z)}}{2}\Big)\bigg)^{2},

for
z=3^{n}+1
.

We also need the following lemma on the square-free part of polynomial values.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a separable polynomial with degree
\geqslant 2
. Then for almost all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we have

\mathrm{sf}(f(z))\geqslant z^{\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon},

for any
\epsilon>0
.

Proof.

We consider the quantity

\displaystyle Q_{f}(S,Z)=\#\{(z,r,s)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:1\leqslant z\leqslant Z,1\leqslant s\leqslant S,f(z)=sr^{2}\}.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a polynomial satisfying our assumptions. By a result of Luca–Shparlinski
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have

\displaystyle Q_{f}(S,Z)\ll_{f}Z^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}S^{\frac{3}{4}},

for any
\epsilon>0
.
We may choose
S=Z^{\frac{2}{3}-2\epsilon}
, so that

Q_{f}(S,Z)=o(Z)
. This implies that for almost all
z\leqslant Z
, we have
\mathrm{sf}(f(z))\geqslant Z^{\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon}\geqslant z^{\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon}
, for any
\epsilon>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be a non-constant polynomial, and let
g(x)=f(x)^{2}+3
. Then for almost all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and so we have

\log\mathrm{sf}(g(z))\gg\log z,

where the implied constant depends on the choice of
f
.

Proof.

We may decompose

g(x)=c\cdot h_{1}(x)\cdot h_{2}(x)^{2},

where
c\in\mathbb{Z}
,
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, and
h_{1}
separable.

We shall firstly show that
\deg(h_{1})>0
.
If not, then we may consider the formula

f(z)^{2}+3=g(z)=c\cdot h_{2}(z)^{2}

for integer variable
z
.
By comparing the leading coefficients of both sides, we know
c
is a perfect square, hence without loss of generalization, we may assume that
c=1
. However, the only integer solution to the equation
z_{1}^{2}+3=z_{2}^{2}
is that
z_{1}=\pm 1
and
z_{2}=\pm 2
, contradicting the assumption that
f
is a non-constant polynomial.

Let
g_{1}=c\cdot h_{1}(z)
. Since
2\,|\,\deg(g)
by its definition, we know that
\deg h_{1}\geqslant 2
.
By Lemma
3.5
, we know
\mathrm{sf}(g_{1}(z))\gg z^{\frac{2}{3}-\epsilon}
for almost all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Note that
\mathrm{sf}(g(z))=\mathrm{sf}(g_{1}(z))
, we have

\log\mathrm{sf}(g(z))=\log\mathrm{sf}(g_{1}(z))\gg\log z,

for almost all
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
∎

Now we are ready to give the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

If the integer solution
(t,u,z_{0})
lie on an
\mathbb{A}^{1}
-curve defined over
\mathbb{Z}
, then there exists non-constant polynomials
t,u,d,f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, such that

f(0)=z_{0},\quad d(x)=f(x)^{2}+3\quad\text{ and }\quad t(x)^{2}-d(x)u(x)^{2}=1.

Hence we have

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log t(x)}{\log d(x)}=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\deg(t)\log x}{\deg(d)\log x}=\frac{\deg(t)}{\deg(d)}<\infty.

If
u\neq 0
, then by the structure of the integer solutions to the Pell equation, we must have
2|t(z)|\geqslant 2t_{1}(z)=\varepsilon_{d(z)}+\varepsilon_{d(z)}^{-1}>\varepsilon_{d(z)}
for
z\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Hence by Corollary
3.2
, Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\rightarrow\infty\\
3\,\nmid\,f(z)\end{subarray}}\Big|\frac{\log t(z)}{\log d(z)}\Big|\gg

\displaystyle\ \limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\rightarrow\infty\\
3\,\nmid\,f(z)\end{subarray}}\frac{\log\varepsilon_{d(z)}}{\log d(z)}

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle\ \limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\rightarrow\infty\\
3\,\nmid\,f(z)\end{subarray}}\frac{\big(\log\mathrm{sf}(d(z))\big)^{2}}{\deg(d)\log z}

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle\ \limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\rightarrow\infty\\
3\,\nmid\,f(z)\end{subarray}}\frac{(\log z)^{2}}{\log z}\,\rightarrow\,\infty.

Therefore, we require that

3|f(z)\text{ for all sufficiently large }z,
in particular
3|f(3z)
for all sufficiently large

, which implies
3|f(0)
. This contradicts the assumption
3\nmid z_{0}
.
∎

Recall that the changing of variable

Lemma 3.7

We have

N_{U}^{\circ}(B)\geqslant\#\{z\leqslant B:3\nmid z,\mu^{2}(d(z))=1,\varepsilon_{d(z)}\leqslant B\}.

Proof.

Recall that all the solutions of (

When

We have

By Corollary

|u_{1}|>|z|
, for

This implies that

Therefore, if

Proof of Corollary

For the same reason as in Remark

Proof of Theorem

Let

\displaystyle\ \#\{z\leqslant Z:3\nmid z,\mu^{2}(d(z))=1,\log\varepsilon_{d(z)}\leqslant\log B\}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\leqslant Z,3\,\nmid\,z\\
