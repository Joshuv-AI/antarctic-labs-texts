Near-Optimal Algorithms for Linear Algebra in the Current Matrix Multiplication Time
Nadiia Chepurko, Kenneth L. Clarkson, Praneeth Kacham, David P. Woodruff

Abstract
In the numerical linear algebra community, it was suggested that to obtain nearly optimal bounds for various problems such as rank computation, finding a maximal linearly independent subset of columns (a basis ), regression, or low-rank approximation, a natural way would be to resolve the main open question of Nelson and Nguyen (FOCS, 2013). This question is regarding the logarithmic factors in the sketching dimension of existing oblivious subspace embeddings that achieve constant-factor approximation. We show how to bypass this question using a refined sketching technique, and obtain optimal or nearly optimal bounds for these problems. A key technique we use is an explicit mapping of Indyk based on uncertainty principles and extractors, which after first applying known oblivious subspace embeddings, allows us to quickly spread out the mass of the vector so that sampling is now effective. We thereby avoid a logarithmic factor in the sketching dimension that is standard in bounds proven using the matrix Chernoff inequality. For the fundamental problems of rank computation and finding a basis, our algorithms improve Cheung, Kwok, and Lau (JACM, 2013), and are optimal to within a constant factor and a poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ ( n ) ) \operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n)) -factor, respectively. Further, for constant-factor regression and low-rank approximation we give the first optimal algorithms, for the current matrix multiplication exponent.

4
Technical Overview

The only known oblivious subspace embedding for a
k
dimensional subspace with
o(k\log(k))
rows is a dense matrix of
O(k)
rows with independent Sub-Gaussian random variables. This embedding can be applied to a matrix
A
in time
\Omega(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\cdot k)
. All other subspace embedding constructions that are faster to apply have at least
\Omega(k\log(k))
rows. Obtaining a subspace embedding with few rows is important to speed up the further downstream tasks such as finding a maximal set of linearly independent rows of a matrix, computing approximate leverage scores, low rank approximation, etc.

We analyze the properties required of a
k
-dimensional subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=\tilde{O}(k)
, such that a sparse random sign matrix with
o(k\log(k))
rows can be a subspace embedding for
V
. The advantage of the sparsity is that the embedding can be applied to a vector quickly. Suppose every unit vector in the subspace
V
has at least a constant
c
fraction of coordinates that have a magnitude of at least
\tilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{k})
. Let
x
be an arbitrary unit vector in the subspace
V
. Now consider a random matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
where each entry is either
0
with probability
1-p
and
\pm 1
with probability
p/2
each. For
p=\Theta(1/d)
, as at least a constant
c
fraction of the coordinates of the vector
x
have a magnitude
\tilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{k})
, each row of the matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
has
\Omega(1)
probability of hitting one of the large coordinates of the vector
x
. Conditioned on a row
\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}
hitting one of the large coordinates of
x
, we have
|\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x|\geq\tilde{\Omega}(1/\sqrt{k})
with probability
\geq 1/2
by using the random signs. Thus, with at least a constant probability, for a row
\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}
,
|{\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x}|^{2}\geq\tilde{\Omega}(1/k)
. If the matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
has
\Omega(k)
rows, using the Chernoff bound, we have that with very high probability,
\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}^{2}\geq\tilde{\Omega}(1)
, which suffices to union bound over a suitable net of unit vectors in a
k
-dimensional subspace. On the other hand, showing that
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}
is small and that it does not increase the norm of any unit vector by a lot is much easier. For the probability
p
that we consider, each row and column of the matrix
\boldsymbol{G}
only has
O(1)
nonzero entries with high probability. As all the nonzero entries are at either
\pm 1
, we can bound the operator norm
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}
by
O(1)
. This implies that for any unit vector
x
,
\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq O(1)
.

The above argument shows that if a subspace has the property that every unit vector in the subspace has a
large
number of
large
coordinates, then a random sparse sign matrix is a subspace embedding with small distortion for that subspace. We call subspaces having this property
flat
. But of course, the column space of the matrix to which we want to apply the embedding may not have this property. Let
V_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the column space of the given matrix
A
. If we can find a linear map
\mathcal{F}
that maps vectors in the subspace
V_{1}
to a
flat
subspace
V_{2}
and if
\mathcal{F}
preserves the Euclidean norms of the vectors, then we have that
\|\boldsymbol{G}\mathcal{F}x\|_{2}\approx\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{2}\approx\|x\|_{2}
for all vectors
x\in V_{1}
. As we show later, by paying some cost in running time, we can assume that
n=O(k\log(k))
by first applying a series of suitable
OSNAP
embeddings. To obtain such a mapping
\mathcal{F}
, we use the
\ell_{2}\rightarrow\ell_{1}
embedding

of
[
22
]
.
We show that recursively applying the linear map

gives a linear map
\mathcal{F}:n\rightarrow n^{1+o(1)}
with the property that for all unit vectors
x
,
\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{2}\approx 1
and
\|{\mathcal{F}x}\|_{1}\geq\tilde{\Omega}(\sqrt{n})
. This property immediately shows that the vector
\mathcal{F}x
must have a large number of large coordinates and therefore that the subspace range(
\mathcal{F}
) is
flat
. We only obtain that a
1/n^{o(1)}
fraction of the coordinates are large but it is sufficient for our purposes. We also show that the sequence of
OSNAP

[

]
which we call Indyk, and the sparse random sign embeddings can be applied to a matrix

in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{2+\gamma+o(1)})
for any constant

.

\boldsymbol{g}
be a vector of i.i.d. normal random variables. Then we have the probability that
|\langle v,\boldsymbol{g}\rangle|\leq\|v\|_{2}/n^{\gamma}
is at most
1/n^{\gamma}
. If
\boldsymbol{g}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
are independent Gaussian vectors for
t=O(1/\gamma)
, then at least one of the values
|\langle v,\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\rangle|
is at least
\|v\|_{2}/n^{\gamma}
with probability
\geq 1-1/n^{2}
. If
\boldsymbol{G}
is a matrix with
\boldsymbol{g}_{j}
as its columns, we therefore have that
\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}/n^{2\gamma}
for all
i
. We also argue that
\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}^{2}=O(\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}\log(n))
for all
i\in[n]
. Now the matrix
AR\boldsymbol{G}
and the approximations
\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}^{2}
can be computed in time
O(\gamma^{-1}(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{2}))
.
Therefore we can obtain over-estimates to the leverage scores. Using over-estimates to the leverage score sampling probabilities, we first sample rows and then compute accurate leverage scores only for the rows that are sampled.
Then we employ a rejection step, in which we reject rows randomly based on the probabilities computed using accurate leverage scores, and finally we show that we obtain a sample from the leverage score sampling distribution. As we compute accurate leverage scores only for the rows that are sampled in the first stage, we do not incur the
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\log(n))
factor. We then compose our leverage score embedding with an OSNAP embedding to obtain a
1+\varepsilon
embedding with
O(k\log(k)/\varepsilon^{2})
rows, which is faster than previous constructions.

Computing Linearly Independent Rows.

We give an algorithm to compute a maximal set of linearly independent rows of a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of rank
k
in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n)))
. Using the rank-preserving sketches of
[
8
]
, we can assume without loss of generality that

for a constant
c
. The crucial idea here is that a leverage score sample of the matrix

, with high probability, must contain a set of
k
linearly independent rows. Therefore, directly applying the above leverage score sampling algorithm for constant
\varepsilon
gives, in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+n^{\gamma}k^{2+o(1)}+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n)))

\gamma

O(k\exp(\operatorname{poly}(\log\log k)))
rows of the matrix
A
that must contain a set of
k
linearly independent rows. To obtain a running time that does not depend on
\gamma
, we show that instead of running leverage score sampling on the matrix
A
, we can apply reductions as in
[
8
]
to reduce the problem to computing linearly independent rows of a sub-matrix

with
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{\prime})\leq\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n))
and with
n/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n))
rows. This reduction can be performed in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\log\log(n))
. After this reduction, we perform leverage score sampling for the matrix
A^{\prime}
as described above with constant
\varepsilon
and
\gamma=O(1/\log(n))
to obtain a matrix
\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}
that selects and scales

rows randomly according to the leverage score distribution such that for all

,
\|\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}A^{\prime}x\|_{2}=(1\pm 1/2)\|A^{\prime}x\|_{2}
. In particular, the guarantee implies that
\text{rowspace}(\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}A^{\prime})=\text{rowspace}(A^{\prime})
. Therefore there are
k
linearly independent rows among the
O(k\exp(\operatorname{poly}(\log\log k)))
rows sampled by
\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}

\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}A^{\prime}

O(k)
rows that, with high probability, contain a set of
k
linearly independent rows. These rows can now be identified in time
O(k^{\omega})
. Thus, we obtain that in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log n)+k^{2+o(1)})
, we can compute a set of
k
linearly independent rows of a rank
k
matrix
A
. As discussed above, the subspace embedding having

rows turns out to be crucial to obtain a running time that depends on

Finally, we give an algorithm to compute a

Flattening the vectors

In this section, we argue that there is a linear mapping

We show that an explicit
\ell_{2}\rightarrow\ell_{1}

]
can be used to obtain such a mapping
\mathcal{F}
. First we define
(\varepsilon,l)

Definition 5.1

(
(\varepsilon,l)

A bipartite graph

and

, with each left node having degree

\mathcal{P}
be

such that for all

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\mathcal{P}}[i]\leq 1/l
. Consider the distribution over

1.

Sample

from distribution
\mathcal{P}

2.

Sample
t\in[d]
uniformly at random and set
j=\Gamma_{G}(i)_{t}
. Here
\Gamma_{G}(i)
is the ordered set of neighbors of
i
in the graph
G
and
\Gamma_{G}(i)_{t}
is the
t
-th neighbor in the ordered set.

Let
G(\mathcal{P})
be the distribution of the element
j
sampled by the above process and let
\mathcal{I}
be the uniform distribution over the set
B
. The graph
G
is an
(\varepsilon,l)
extractor if
\sum_{j\in B}|\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{G(\mathcal{P})}[j]-1/b|\leq\varepsilon
. We stress that this property must hold for every distribution
\mathcal{P}
with
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{\mathcal{P}}[i]\leq 1/l
for all

.

See
[
22
]
and references therein for explicit constructions of extractors. Indyk uses the following extractor: Fix a
\delta=\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
and let
L=O(1/\delta^{2})
and
s=\sqrt{n}
. Let
G
be an
(\varepsilon,l)
extractor with
A=[Ln]
,
B=[b]
for
b=n^{1/2-\kappa}
,
\kappa>0
,
l=(1-\delta)^{2}s/L
, left degree

and right degree
\Delta=O(nLd/b)
.

In the following it will be helpful to have an abbreviation.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathrm{epll}(n)
denote the class of functions in
\exp(\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n)))
as integer
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Theorem 5.3

(Theorem 1.1 of
[
22
]
)
.

For any
\zeta,\kappa>0
, there is an explicit linear mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
m=O(nLd)=n\log^{O(1)}(n)/\zeta^{O(1)}
and a partitioning of the coordinate set
[m]
into sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{b}
, for
b=n^{1/2-\kappa}
, each of size at most
\Delta=n^{1/2+\kappa}\mathrm{epll}(n)/\zeta^{O(1)}
, such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|x\|_{2}=1
,

(1-O(\zeta))\sqrt{Ldb}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(Fx)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{Ldb}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that all the partitions
B_{j}
have the same size
\Delta
by appending
0
-valued coordinates and so we have
m=n\cdot\mathrm{epll}(n)/\zeta^{O(1)}
.

We now prove the following lemma which essentially shows that an application of Indyk’s embedding to a unit vector shrinks the Euclidean norm by a lot, while keeping the
\ell_{1}
norm
\Omega(1)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
n
be an arbitrary integer and
0<\zeta,\kappa<c
for a small enough constant
c
. There is an explicit linear mapping
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
m=n\cdot\mathrm{epll}(n)/\zeta^{O(1)}
and a partitioning of
[m]
into equal sized sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{b}
where

b=n^{1/2-\kappa}

and each set
B_{j}
satisfies

|B_{j}|=\Delta=n^{1/2+\kappa}\mathrm{epll}(n)/\zeta^{O(1)},

such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(1-O(\zeta))\|x\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(Fx)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}

and

\frac{1}{b}(1-O(\zeta))\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|Fx\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(Fx)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{b}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

In the proof of the above theorem, Indyk uses the
(\varepsilon,l)
construction specified above with
\delta=\zeta
and
\varepsilon=\zeta^{2}
. Indyk also defines
(Fx)_{B_{j}}:=(Dx)_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}
for
j\in[b]
, where
D
is a concatenation of certain
L
orthonormal matrices and
\Gamma_{G}(j)\subseteq A
is the set of neighbors of
j\in B
in the graph
G
. For any unit vector
x
, we have
\|Dx\|_{2}^{2}=L
and as the left degree of
G
is exactly equal to
d
, we have
\|Fx\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j}\|(Fx)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j}\|(Dx)_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}^{2}=d\|Dx\|_{2}^{2}=Ld
.

Let
y=Dx
and let
S
be the set of the
s
largest magnitude entries of
y
. Define
z=y_{[a]-S}
where
z
is obtained by zeroing out the coordinates of the set
S
.
Indyk 2007
showed that

\sum_{j=1}^{b}\left|\frac{1}{\rho^{2}d}\|z_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}^{2}-1/b\right|\leq\varepsilon

where
\rho\geq\sqrt{L}(1-\delta)
. The inequality implies that

\sum_{j}\|z_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\rho^{2}d(1-\varepsilon)\geq Ld(1-\delta)^{2}(1-\varepsilon).

As
\|y_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}\geq\|z_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}
, we get

\sum_{j}\|y_{\Gamma_{G}(j)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq Ld(1-\delta)^{2}(1-\varepsilon)

and plugging in
\delta=\zeta
and
\varepsilon=\zeta^{2}
, we obtain

Ld(1-O(\zeta))\leq\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(Fx)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq Ld.

Hence, the matrix
F/\sqrt{Ldb}
satisfies that for any vector
x
,

\frac{1}{b}(1-O(\zeta))\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(\frac{F}{\sqrt{Ldb}}x)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{b}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

From the above theorem, we already have

(1-O(\zeta))\|x\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{b}\|(\frac{F}{\sqrt{Ldb}}x)_{B_{j}}\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}.

Therefore, scaling the matrix
F
gives the proof.
∎

We apply the above lemma recursively to each of the partitions
B_{j}
for
\Theta(\log\log(n))
levels to obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5.5
.

Given any
n
, there is an explicit map
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
m=n\cdot\mathrm{epll}(n)
such that for all unit vectors
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{1}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{4}

and

\frac{1}{2}\leq\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1.

Further, given any vector
x
, the vector
\mathcal{F}x
can be computed in
n^{1+o(1)}

Proof.

Let
N=\Theta(\log\log(n))
and
\zeta=\Theta(1/\log\log(n))
. Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{b_{1}}
be the partitions of the coordinates of the range of
F
from the Lemma
5.4
. We recursively apply the lemma for each of the partitions for
N
levels to obtain
\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
m=n\cdot\mathrm{epll}(n)
. Define
n_{0}=n
and let
n_{i}
be the number of entries in each of the
i
-th level partitions. Also, let
b_{0}=1
and
b_{i}
be the number of partitions an
(i-1)
-th level partition is mapped into. From Lemma
5.4

b_{i}=n_{i-1}^{1/2-\kappa}

and

The following lemma lower bounds the number of partitions in the
N
-th level.

Lemma 5.6
.

The total number of partitions in the
N
-th level is given by
B=b_{0}\cdot b_{1}\cdots b_{N}
and

B\geq n/2.

Proof.

We have
B=b_{1}\cdots b_{N}=(n_{0}\cdots n_{N-1})^{1/2-\kappa}
. As
n_{i}\geq n^{(1/2+\kappa)^{i}}
, we have that
n_{0}\cdots n_{N-1}\geq n^{\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(1/2+\kappa)^{i}}
. Now,

\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}(1/2+\kappa)^{i}={(1-(1/2+\kappa)^{N})}/{(1/2-\kappa)}

which implies
B\geq n^{1-(1/2+\kappa)^{N}}
. For

(1/2+\kappa)^{N}\leq 1/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n))
and
B\geq n/2
.
∎

This lemma implies that the
N
-th level has the partitions
\mathcal{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{B}
of
[m]
with

and

such that for any unit vector

\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}\leq(1-O(\zeta))^{N}\|x\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{B}\|(\mathcal{F}x)_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{2}

and

\frac{1}{2B}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{(1-O(\zeta))^{N}}{B}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{B}\|(\mathcal{F}x)_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{B}\|x\|_{2}^{2}.

Finally, for a unit vector
x
,

\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{B}\|(\mathcal{F}x)_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\|_{2}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{B}\|{(\mathcal{F}x)_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}}\|_{1}=\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{1}

and

\frac{1}{2B}=\frac{1}{2B}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\mathcal{F}x\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{B}\|(\mathcal{F}x)_{\mathcal{B}_{j}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{B}\|x\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{B}.

By scaling the map
\mathcal{F}
by
\sqrt{B}
, we complete the proof.
∎

We now have the following corollary.

.

\boldsymbol{G}A

Proof.

Define the

for some values of

and

to be chosen later. The random variables
\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}
are mutually independent. Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
be the number of nonzero entries in the
i

\boldsymbol{G}
and let
\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}
be the number of nonzero entries in the
i
-th column of
\boldsymbol{G}
. By the Chernoff bound, for

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\geq(1+\delta)\cdot mp]\leq\exp(-\delta mp/4)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}\geq(1+\delta)\cdot Mp]\leq\exp(-\delta Mp/4).

Let
p
be such that
p|\text{Large}(x)|\geq 10
for all

, there is a value of
p

pm\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)
. By a union bound, we obtain that with probability
\geq 99/100
, for all

and

\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)
. Thus, with probability

\max_{i}\sum_{j}|\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}|=\max_{i}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)\,\text{and}\,\max_{j}\sum_{i}|\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}|=\max_{j}\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k).

We now have that
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{(\max_{i}\sum_{j}|\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}|)(\max_{j}\sum_{i}|\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}|)}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)
, which implies that for any vector

\|\boldsymbol{G}\cdot Ay\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)\|Ay\|_{2}.

Let the event that

\mathcal{E}
.

We now show a contraction lower bound. Let
x
be an arbitrary unit vector in the column space of the matrix

\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}
is

. We have

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\text{$\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}$ is \emph{bad}}]=(1-p)^{|\text{Large}(x)|}\leq\exp(-p|\text{Large}(x)|)\leq\exp(-10)\leq 1/100.

Thus,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\text{$\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}$ is \emph{good}}]\geq 99/100
.

We say a row
\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}
is

\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}

good

\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\textnormal{{nnz}}(\boldsymbol{G}_{i*})-j}\boldsymbol{G}_{ij^{\prime}}x_{j^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}x_{j}

\boldsymbol{G}

1/2
,
\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}x_{j}

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\textnormal{{nnz}}(\boldsymbol{G}_{i*})-j}\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}x_{j}

\geq 1/2

|\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x|\geq|x_{j}|\geq\eta

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\text{$\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}$ is \emph{large}}\,|\,\text{$\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}$ is \emph{good}}]\geq 1/2

which implies that

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[|\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x|\geq\eta]=\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\text{$\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}$ is \emph{large}}]\geq(1/2)\cdot(99/100)\geq 1/4.

Let
l
denote the number of
large

\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[l\leq(1/2)\cdot M\cdot(1/4)]\leq\exp(-M/32).

\mathcal{E}
. We have

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[l\leq M/8\,|\,\mathcal{E}]\leq\frac{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[l\leq M/8]}{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[\mathcal{E}]}\leq 2\exp(-M/32).

Therefore, conditioned on the event
\mathcal{E}

which implies that

\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}^{2}\geq\sum_{\text{\emph{large}}\,i}|\boldsymbol{G}_{i*}x|^{2}\geq l\eta^{2}\geq\frac{l}{k\,\mathrm{epll}(k)}\geq\frac{M}{8k\,\mathrm{epll}(k)}.

In what follows, we condition on the event
\mathcal{E}
. For
M=k\cdot\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(k))

x

\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(k))}{\mathrm{epll}(k)}.

By suitably scaling
\boldsymbol{G}

x

\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)\|x\|_{2}

and for any unit vector
x

\geq 1-\exp(-k\cdot\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(k)))

\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}\geq 2.

The column space of the matrix
A
has dimension at most
k

\mathcal{N}

\|\boldsymbol{G}x\|_{2}\geq 2.

Now conditioning on this event, for an arbitrary

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{G}y\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{G}(x_{y}+(y-x_{y}))\|_{2}

Theorem 6.3

(Subspace Embedding)
.

{n\times k}

n

The matrix

\boldsymbol{S}=4\cdot\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\mathcal{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}

OSNAP
for
k

\gamma=0.1

k

6.2
. We have with probability
\geq 9/10
, for any vector

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|Ax\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\|Ax\|_{2}.

Condition on the above event. From Theorem
6.1

\frac{1}{4}\|Ax\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\|\mathcal{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\|Ax\|_{2}.

By Theorem

\mathcal{F}

\|\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\mathcal{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)\|\mathcal{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot Ax\|_{2}\leq\mathrm{epll}(k)\|Ax\|_{2}

There is nothing special about

\displaystyle=16\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{S}_{2},\boldsymbol{S}_{1}}[\|\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\mathcal{F}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{2}M\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}]

Theorem 7.1

(Leverage Score Sampling)
.

Given a full column rank matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}

\ell_{i}^{2}
for

be the leverage score of the

\min(1,r\cdot(\ell_{i}^{2}/k))\geq p_{i}\geq\min(1,r\cdot\beta\cdot(\ell_{i}^{2}/k))

n\times n

\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}
be defined as follows: for each

With probability
\geq 1-\exp(-\Theta(k))

nonzero entries.

The following lemma shows that a subspace embedding

Lemma 7.2
.

If
S
is a
\beta
subspace embedding for the column space of a full rank matrix

i.e., for any vector

{\|Ax\|_{2}}\leq\|SAx\|_{2}\leq\beta\|Ax\|_{2}

and if

\ell_{i}^{2}/\beta^{2}\leq\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}\leq\ell_{i}^{2},

where

is the leverage score of the
i
-th row of
A
.

The proof of the lemma is in Appendix
A.1
. Using our fast subspace embedding with
k\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(k))
rows and
\beta=\mathrm{epll}(k)
, the above lemma shows that if we can compute the values

, then we can obtain a
1+\varepsilon
subspace embedding with
k\cdot\mathrm{epll}(k)/\varepsilon^{2}

Often, the row norms

are approximated with
\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{G}
is a Gaussian matrix with
O(\log n)
columns using the fact that for an arbitrary vector

Lemma 7.3

Let

\boldsymbol{S}
independently with probability

\min(1,s\frac{\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}}{\|AR\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}})\geq f_{i}\geq\min(1,(s/16)\frac{\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}}{\|AR\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}}).

The algorithm returns the random subset
\boldsymbol{S}
along with the probabilities

Proof.

Let

with probability
\geq 99/100

\boldsymbol{G}_{1}AR\boldsymbol{G}_{2}
can be computed in

\boldsymbol{G}_{3}
be a Gaussian matrix with
O(\log(n))
columns. With probability
\geq 99/100

\text{for all $i\in[n],$}\quad\frac{1}{2}\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}_{3}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{(Event $\mathcal{E}_{2}$)}.

We note that we

Now, let

\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}

\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}_{4}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\max_{j\in[t]}\langle A_{i*}R,\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\rangle^{2}\geq\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}/n^{\gamma}
. By Lemma 1 of
[

]

\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}_{4}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O(\log(n))\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}

\frac{\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}}{n^{\gamma}}\leq\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}_{4}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\log(n)\|A_{i*}R\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{(Event $\mathcal{E}_{3}$)}.

We compute
AR\boldsymbol{G}_{4}
and all squared row norms
\|A_{i*}R\boldsymbol{G}_{4}\|_{2}^{2}
in time

Define

We need to show that this procedure is well-defined. We have

.
∎

7.2

Let

Theorem 7.5
.

Given a full-rank matrix

for any constant
\gamma.

Proof.

We first find a
1+\varepsilon
subspace embedding
\boldsymbol{S}
for the column space of
[A,b]
. From Theorem
7.4
,
\boldsymbol{S}A
and
\boldsymbol{S}b
can be computed in at most
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-3}n^{\gamma+o(1)}k^{2+o(1)}+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log k))
time. We can also compute a preconditioner
R
using the fast subspace embedding from Theorem
6.2
such that

\kappa(AR)=\mathrm{epll}(k)

by first computing
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{fast}}A=QR^{-1}
and then inverting

to obtain

for any constant
\gamma
. We also have that

Let
x^{*}
be a solution such that

\|\boldsymbol{S}ARx^{*}-\boldsymbol{S}b\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\min_{x}\|\boldsymbol{S}ARx-\boldsymbol{S}b\|_{2}.

Then, we have

\|ARx^{*}-b\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{S}ARx^{*}-\boldsymbol{S}b\|_{2}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\|\boldsymbol{S}Ax_{\text{opt}}-\boldsymbol{S}b\|_{2}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{1-\varepsilon}\|Ax_{\text{opt}}-b\|_{2}.

We first compute an approximate solution for the regression problem as follows: let
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{fast}}
be the subspace embedding with

rows for the column space of
[A,b]
. Let

Let

be the optimal solution for the regression problem

for the regression problem

we can compute a

approximate solution for the linear regression problem
(A,b)
.
∎

\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\geq\Theta(k^{2})

\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)/5\geq\Theta(k\sqrt{\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)})
which implies that for all
P
,
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A_{Q_{P}})\leq(2/5)\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)
. This, in particular, implies that for
M=Q_{L}
, that corresponds to the set of rows contributing to a linearly independent set of rows of
(\boldsymbol{S}A)
, we have
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A_{M})\leq(2/5)\cdot\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)
if
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)\geq\Theta(k^{2})
.
∎

Recursively applying the above lemma, we obtain the following.

Corollary 7.11
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be an arbitrary matrix of rank
k
. There is a matrix
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times ck}
with either
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{\prime})\leq\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)/\log(n)
or
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{\prime})\leq\Theta(k^{2})
such that

•

\textnormal{rank}(A^{\prime})=\textnormal{rank}(A)=k
, and

•

m\leq n/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n))

•

linearly independent rows of
A^{\prime}
correspond to linearly independent rows of
A
.

The reduction
A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
can be performed in
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\log\log(n))
time.

Proof.

Let
N=\Theta(\log\log(n))
and
A^{(0)}=A
. Starting with
i=0
, we apply the above reduction
A^{(i)}\rightarrow A^{(i+1)}
to obtain a matrix with
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{(i+1)})\leq(2/5)\cdot\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{(i)})
. Then

\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{(N)})\leq\max((2/5)^{N}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A),\Theta(k^{2}))\leq\max(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)/\log(n),\Theta(k^{2})).

The time complexity is
O(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{(i)})+k^{\omega}))=O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\log\log(n))
.
∎

We have now reduced the general problem of computing
k
linearly independent rows of a rank-
k

n\times d
matrix
A
to computing
k
linearly independent rows of a rank-
k

m\times ck
matrix
A^{\prime}
with
m\leq n/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n))
and
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A^{\prime})\leq O(\max(k^{2},\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)/\log(n)))
. Using these reductions, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 7.12
.

Given an arbitrary matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of rank
k
, Algorithm
5
computes a set of
k
linearly independent rows of the matrix
A
in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n))+k^{2+o(1)})
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{ck\times d}
be a rank preserving sketch which implies
\textnormal{rank}(A\boldsymbol{S}^{\mathsf{T}})=\textnormal{rank}(A)=k
with probability
1-O(1/k)
. Condition on this event. Let
M\subseteq[n]
,
|M|=k
be such that rows of the sub-matrix
(A\boldsymbol{S}^{\mathsf{T}})_{M}=A_{M}\boldsymbol{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
are linearly independent. Then,
k\geq\textnormal{rank}(A_{M})\geq\textnormal{rank}(A_{M}\boldsymbol{S}^{\mathsf{T}})=k
which implies
\textnormal{rank}(A_{M})=k
. Thus, we only have to find
k
linearly independent rows of the
n\times ck
matrix
B=A\boldsymbol{S}^{\mathsf{T}}
. We also have
\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)=O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A))
. Using the above corollary, we can find an
m\times ck
sub-matrix
B^{\prime}
such that
\textnormal{rank}(B^{\prime})=k
,
\textnormal{{nnz}}(B^{\prime})\leq O(\max(\textnormal{{nnz}}(B)/\operatorname{poly}(\log(n)),\Theta(k^{2}))
and

.

From Theorem
7.4
, using
\gamma=1/\log(n)
, in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(B^{\prime})\log(n)+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n))+k^{2+o(1)}+m\gamma^{-1})=O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+k^{\omega}\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(n))+k^{2+o(1)})
, we can compute a row sampling matrix
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{lev}}
that samples

\|\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{lev}}B^{\prime}x\|_{2}^{2}\in(1\pm 1/10)\|B^{\prime}x\|_{2}^{2}

for all vectors

. This, implies that the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{lev}}B^{\prime}
has rank

linearly independent rows.

As

is a leverage score sampling matrix, the rows of

. Thus, a set of

.

Applying the row reduction
\operatorname{poly}(\log\log(k))

of dimension

7.4
Low-Rank Approximation

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be an arbitrary matrix. We want to compute a matrix
B
of rank at most
k
such that

\|A-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Let
\text{OPT}_{A}
denote
\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
. Our main theorem for Low-Rank Approximation (LRA) is as follows.

Theorem 7.13
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
,
k<\min(n,d)
be a rank parameter and
\varepsilon>0
be an accuracy parameter. There is an algorithm that outputs matrices
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
,
V^{\mathsf{T}}V=I_{k}
, such that with
\Omega(1)
probability,

\|A-VX\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

The algorithm runs in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+{\varepsilon^{-1}}(n+d)k^{\omega-1}+\varepsilon^{-1}k(nd^{\gamma+o(1)}+dn^{\gamma+o(1)})+\operatorname{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}k))
for any constant
\gamma>0
.

In the following sections, we will describe how to compute the left factor
V
and the right factor
X
. We are not very careful with probabilities, as we only have to condition over the success of
O(1)
events, and all these events can be chosen to have a success probability
1-c
for any absolute constant
c>0
without affecting the time complexity.

We start with a residual sampling algorithm that lets us obtain a subspace containing a
1+\varepsilon
approximation given a subspace that is only
O(1)
approximate.

7.4.1
Residual Sampling

Suppose we have a subspace
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\|A-A\mathbb{P}_{V}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq K\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

The following theorem of
[
19
]
shows that sampling
O(K\cdot k/\varepsilon)
rows of the matrix
A
with probabilities proportional to the squared distances of the rows to the subspace
V
gives a subspace that along with
V
contains a
1+\varepsilon
rank-
k
approximation to the matrix
A
.

Theorem 7.14

(Theorem 2.1 of
[
19
]
)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a subspace. Let
E=A-A\mathbb{P}_{V}
, the matrix formed by projecting each row of
A
away from the subspace
V
. Let
\boldsymbol{S}
be a random sample of
s
rows of
A
from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
such that row
i
is chosen with probability
p_{i}\geq\alpha\|E_{i*}\|_{2}^{2}/\|E\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
. Then for any non-negative integer
k
,

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\boldsymbol{S}}[\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textnormal{rank}\text{-}k\,B\\
\text{rowspan}(B)\subseteq V+\text{rowspan}(A_{\boldsymbol{S}})\end{subarray}}\|A-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}]\leq\|A-A_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\frac{k}{s\alpha}\|E\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Instead of sampling
s
rows independently from the distribution
p
, we can also sample each
i\in[n]
with probability
q_{i}:=\min(1,sp_{i})
and obtain the same result for the resulting random subset of rows. Sampling each
i\in[n]
independently with probability
q_{i}
lets us use the sampling framework from Lemma
7.3
.

Lemma 7.15

(Sampling each row independently)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
V
be a subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
E=A-A\mathbb{P}_{V}
. Sample each
i\in[n]
independently with a probability
q_{i}:=\min(1,sp_{i})
, with
p_{i}\geq\alpha\|E_{i*}\|_{2}^{2}/\|E\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
to obtain a random subset
\boldsymbol{S}\subseteq[n]
. For any nonnegative integer
k
,

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{\boldsymbol{S}}[\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textnormal{rank}\text{-}k\,B\\
\text{rowspan}(B)\subseteq V+\text{rowspan}(A_{\boldsymbol{S}})\end{subarray}}\|A-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}]\leq\|A-A_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}+\frac{k}{s\alpha}\|E\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

The proof of this lemma is in Appendix
A.2

7.4.2
Computing the left factor of an approximation

Let
\boldsymbol{T}
be a CountSketch matrix with
\Theta(k^{2})
columns. In
[
13
]
, the authors show that
\boldsymbol{T}
is a projection cost preserving sketch, i.e., with probability
9/10
, for all projection matrices
P
of rank at most
O(k)
,

\|(I-P)A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=(1\pm 1/10)\|(I-P)A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

Let
\boldsymbol{S}
be a CountSketch matrix with
\Theta(k^{4})
rows. Then, with probability
\geq 99/100
,
\boldsymbol{S}
is a subspace embedding for the matrix
A\boldsymbol{T}
and therefore for any matrix
X
,

\|\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}X-\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=(1\pm 1/10)\|A\boldsymbol{T}X-A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

We can relate
\text{OPT}_{A}
and
\text{OPT}_{\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}}
as follows:

\textnormal{OPT}_{\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}}=\|\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-[\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}X\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{11}{10}\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|A\boldsymbol{T}-A\boldsymbol{T}X\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\frac{11}{10}\textnormal{OPT}_{A\boldsymbol{T}}

where the inequality follows from the subspace embedding property of
\boldsymbol{S}
for the column space of
A\boldsymbol{T}
. Now,

\textnormal{OPT}_{A\boldsymbol{T}}=\min_{\text{{rank}-$k$ projections}\,P}\|(I-P)A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{10}{9}\min_{\text{{rank}-$k$ projections}\,P}\|(I-P)A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=\frac{10}{9}\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

Here, the inequality follows as
\boldsymbol{T}
is a projection cost preserving sketch for
k
dimensional projections. Thus,
\textnormal{OPT}_{\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}}\leq(11/9)\textnormal{OPT}_{A}
.

Boutsidis and Woodruff 2017
show that for any matrix
M
, there exists a sub-matrix
M^{\prime}
of
M
, with
O(k/\varepsilon)
columns such that there is a rank
k
matrix
B
,
\text{colspan}(B)\subseteq\text{colspan}(M^{\prime})
, and
\|M-B\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|M-[M]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
. They also give an algorithm to find such a subset of columns. As
\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}
is a
O(k^{4})\times O(k^{2})
matrix, using their algorithm, we can compute in time
\operatorname{poly}(k)
, a column selection matrix
\Omega
that selects
O(k)
columns of
\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}
such that

\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega X\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\textnormal{OPT}_{\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}}\leq 2\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

We now have

\|(\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega)(\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega)^{+}\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega X\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq 2\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

Using the property that
\boldsymbol{S}
is a subspace embedding for the column space of
A\boldsymbol{T}
, we have

\|A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega(\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T})^{+}\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{20}{11}\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

Let
U
be a matrix with orthonormal columns such that
\text{colspan}(A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega)=\text{colspan}(U)
. Therefore,

\|UU^{\mathsf{T}}A\boldsymbol{T}-A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\|(A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega)(\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega)^{+}\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}-A\boldsymbol{T}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{20}{11}\textnormal{OPT}_{A}

which finally implies, as
\boldsymbol{T}
is a projection cost preserving sketch for
O(k)
dimensional projections, that

\|UU^{\mathsf{T}}A-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq({10}/{9})({20}/{11})\textnormal{OPT}_{A}\leq 3\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

Thus,
\text{colspan}(U)
is an
O(k)
dimensional subspace with
\|(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq 3\textnormal{OPT}_{A}
. As,
\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{S}
are CountSketch matrices, the matrices
A\boldsymbol{T}
and
\boldsymbol{S}A\boldsymbol{T}
can be computed in time
\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)
. The matrix
\Omega
can be computed in time
\operatorname{poly}(k)
and the matrix
A\boldsymbol{T}\Omega
is obtained by selecting the appropriate columns of matrix
A\boldsymbol{T}
. The orthonormal matrix
U
can be computed in time
O(nk^{\omega-1})
. Using
U
, we now obtain a larger subspace of dimension
O(k/\varepsilon)
that spans a
1+\varepsilon
approximation.

Using Lemma
7.15
, we have that if
columns
of the matrix
A
are sampled independently to obtain a subset
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}\subseteq[d]
such that
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[j\in\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}]\geq\min(1,sp_{j})
for
s=O(k/\varepsilon)
,
p_{j}=\|(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})A_{*j}\|_{2}^{2}/\|(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
, then with probability
\geq 99/100
, the subspace
\text{colspan}(U)+\text{colspan}(A^{\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}})
spans columns of a
k
dimensional matrix that is a
(1+\varepsilon)
rank-
k
approximation for
A
.

Lemma
7.3
shows how to sample
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}
from such a distribution. In the notation of Lemma
7.3
, we have
T_{1}=O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+nk)
and
T_{2}=nk
. Therefore, with probability
\geq 95/100
, we can obtain a sample
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}
from a distribution over subsets of
[d]
such that independently,
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[j\in\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}]\geq\min(1,O(k/\varepsilon)p_{j})
in time
O(\gamma^{-1}(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+nk)+nk\log(d)+\varepsilon^{-1}d^{\gamma}nk\log^{2}(d))=O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-1}nkd^{\gamma+o(1)})
for any small constant
\gamma
. Let
M=[U\,A^{\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}}]
. We have with probability
\geq 9/10
, that

\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|MX-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

To obtain a good
k
-dimensional subspace within the column space of
M
, we can sketch and solve the above problem. Let
\boldsymbol{T}_{1}
be a CountSketch matrix with
O((k/\varepsilon)^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
rows. Then with probability
\geq 99/100
,
\boldsymbol{T}_{1}
is an affine embedding for
(M,A)
and therefore for any matrix
X
,
\|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}MX-\boldsymbol{T}_{1}A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\|MX-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
. Let
X_{\boldsymbol{T}_{1}}
be the optimal solution for
\min_{{\text{rank-}}k\,X}\|\boldsymbol{T}_{1}MX-\boldsymbol{T}_{1}A\|_{\mathsf{F}}
. As
X_{\boldsymbol{T}_{1}}
is optimal, the rows of the matrix
X_{\boldsymbol{T}_{1}}
must be spanned by the rows of the matrix
\boldsymbol{T}_{1}A
, which implies that

\min_{\text{rank-}k\,X}\|MX\boldsymbol{T}_{1}A-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

This problem can now be solved by sketching on the left and the right with
\boldsymbol{T}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{T}_{2}
, where
\boldsymbol{T}_{2}
is a CountSketch matrix with
\operatorname{poly}(k/\varepsilon)
rows, and then solving the sketched problem optimally. The time complexity of sketching is
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(M)+\textnormal{{nnz}}(A))=O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+nk/\varepsilon)
, and the sketched problem can be solved in time
\operatorname{poly}(k/\varepsilon)
. Thus in time
O(\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+nk/\varepsilon+\operatorname{poly}(k/\varepsilon))
, we can compute a rank
k
matrix
X
such that

\|MX\boldsymbol{T}_{1}A-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\textnormal{OPT}_{A}.

We can also compute a decomposition of
X=X_{1}\cdot X_{2}
where
X_{1}
has
k
columns in time
\operatorname{poly}(k/\varepsilon)
, which implies that the
k
dimensional column span of
MX_{1}
is a
1+O(\varepsilon)
approximate rank
k
singular subspace i.e.,
\|(MX_{1})(MX_{1})^{+}A-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\textnormal{OPT}_{A}
. The matrix
MX_{1}
can be computed in time
O(nk^{\omega-1}/\varepsilon)
and a matrix
V
which is an orthonormal basis for the column space of the
n\times k
matrix
MX_{1}
can be computed in time
O(nk^{\omega-1})
. Thus, in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-1}nkd^{\gamma+o(1)}+\varepsilon^{-1}nk^{\omega-1}+\operatorname{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}k))
, we can compute a left factor for a
1+\varepsilon
rank-
k
approximation of
A
. Thus, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 7.16
.

Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, a rank parameter
k
and accuracy parameter
\varepsilon
, we can compute a matrix
V
with
k
orthonormal columns in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-1}nkd^{\gamma+o(1)}+\varepsilon^{-(\omega-1)}nk^{\omega-1}+\operatorname{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}k))
such that

\|A-VV^{\mathsf{T}}A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

7.4.3
Computing a right factor given a left factor

Given a matrix
V
with
k
orthonormal columns such that

\min_{X}\|VX-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2},

we want to compute a rank
k
matrix
\tilde{X}
that satisfies
\|V\tilde{X}-A\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}
.

For
i\in[n]
, let
p_{i}=\|V_{*i}\|_{2}^{2}/k
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}
is a sampling matrix with
s=O(k\log(k))
rows such that each row of
\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}
is independently equal to
e_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}/\sqrt{sp_{i}}
with a probability
p_{i}
. Then we have

\text{for all vectors $x$},\|\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}Vx\|_{2}^{2}\in(1\pm 1/2)\|Vx\|_{2}^{2}.

Let
M_{2}=V^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}^{\mathsf{T}}
and let
V_{M_{2}}
be a matrix with
k
orthonormal columns such that
\text{colspan}(V_{M_{2}})=\text{rowspan}(M_{2})
. Let
S_{2}
be the BSS-Sampling matrix returned by the dual set spectral sparsification algorithm of
[
6
]
on the inputs
V_{M_{2}},\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}(I-VV^{\mathsf{T}})A\boldsymbol{T}
with a parameter
4k
, where
\boldsymbol{T}
is a CountSketch matrix with
O(k^{2})
columns. The matrix
S_{2}
selects
4k
rows of the matrix
\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}A
. Let
R_{1}=S_{2}\boldsymbol{S}_{\textnormal{lev}}A
. Lemma 6.7 of
[
6
]
shows that

\|A-AR_{1}^{+}R_{1}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq O(1)\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

As the matrix
R_{1}
has
4k
rows, an orthonormal basis
U
for the rowspace of
R_{1}
, with
4k
orthonormal columns, can be computed in time
dk^{\omega-1}
. We can then perform residual sampling of rows of
A
with respect to the subspace
U
using the Lemma
7.3
. Here
T_{1}=\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+dk
and
T_{2}=dk
. Thus, we can sample rows from a distribution defined by the probabilities
\min(1,(s/16){\|A_{i*}(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{2}^{2}}/{\|A(I-UU^{\mathsf{T}})\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}})
, for
s=O(k/\varepsilon)
in time
O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-1}dkn^{\gamma+o(1)})
. Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{\text{res}}^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
be the rows sampled. Let

. The matrix
R
has
O(k/\varepsilon)

Now, as in proof of the Theorem 5.1 of
[

]
, we have with proabability
\geq 9/10
,

\|A-VV^{\mathsf{T}}AR^{+}R\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2},

which implies

\min_{X}\|A-VXR\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\|A-[A]_{k}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.

By sketching the problem on the left and the right with CountSketch matrices

and

with

such that

Thus, we can compute two matrices

with
k

is a

approximate rank-

Frobenius norm approximation to the matrix

, in time

O(\gamma^{-1}\textnormal{{nnz}}(A)+\varepsilon^{-1}(n+d)k^{\omega-1}+\varepsilon^{-1}k(nd^{\gamma+o(1)}+dn^{\gamma+o(1)})+\operatorname{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}k)).
