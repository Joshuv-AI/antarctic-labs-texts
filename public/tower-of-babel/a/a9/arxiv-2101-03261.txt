Optimal Control and Numerical Methods for Hybrid Stochastic SIS Models Thanks: This research was supported in part by Air Force Office of Scientific Research under Grant FA9550-18-1-0268.
Ky Tran, George Yin

Abstract
This work focuses on optimal controls of a class of stochastic SIS epidemic models under regime switching. By assuming that a decision maker can either influence the infectivity period or isolate infected individuals, our aim is to minimize the expected discounted cost due to illness, medical treatment, and the adverse effect on the society. In addition, a model with the incorporation of vaccination is proposed. Numerical schemes are developed by approximating the continuous-time dynamics using Markov chain approximation methods. It is demonstrated that the approximation schemes converge to the optimal strategy as the mesh size goes to zero. Numerical examples are provided to illustrate our results.



Key words.
Controlled regime-switching diffusion; SIS epidemic model; Markov chain approximation; isolation; vaccination.

Brief Title.
Control and Numerics for
Hybrid Stochastic SIS Models

1
Introduction

This work focuses on optimal controls and numerical methods for a class of stochastic epidemic models under regime switching.
A SIS system stems from the
compartmental models
[
19
]
, which subdivides the population into susceptible (S) and infected (I) classes. The word
SIS is
an abbreviation of susceptible-infected-susceptible. That is,
a susceptible individual becomes infected, and later on becomes susceptible again; see
[
2
,
5
,
19
]
for detailed discussions, and
[
14
]
and references therein for different applications.
The models include in particular
diseases being
sexually transmitted and
diseases being transmitted by
bacteria,
in which there is no permanent immunity.
For such diseases, a promising model is the following classical deterministic SIS epidemic model

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[\mu N-\beta S(t)I(t)+\gamma I(t)-\mu S(t)\big]dt,\quad S(0)=s_{0},\\
dI(t)=\big[\beta S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu+\gamma\big)I(t)\big]dt,\quad I(0)=i_{0},\end{cases}

(1.1)

subject to
S(t)+I(t)=N
, along with the initial values
S(0)=s_{0}>0
and
I(0)=i_{0}>0
, where
S(t)
and
I(t)
are the numbers of susceptible and infected
individuals at time
t
in a population of size
N
, respectively;
\mu
and
\gamma^{-1}
are the average death rate and the average infectious period, respectively. The parameter

\beta
is the disease transmission coefficient with
\beta=\lambda/N
and
\lambda
being the disease contact rate of an infective individual. More specifically,
\lambda
is the per day average number of adequate contacts of an infective so that after an adequate contact with an infective, a susceptible individual becomes infected. There is a variety of applications of SIS models. To mention just a few, we refer to
[
13
]
for a white noise parameter perturbation of system (
1.1
), and
[
14
]
for a SIS model with Markovian switching; for SIS models with vaccination we refer to
[
16
,
23
,
35
,
34
]
. All the papers above focus on the asymptotic properties of the diseases. The reader can also find related works on long-time behaviors of various epidemic models in
[
10
,
27
,
31
]
.

Although SIS epidemic models and
various epidemic models have been well studied, the work on optimal control of stochastic epidemic models is relatively scarce and the problem is
largely open. The objective of such control problems is to identify effective strategies for minimizing the impacts of infectious diseases through a set of mixed control strategies including treatments, vaccination, isolation, and health promotion campaigns, etc.
Most of the published papers focus on deterministic epidemics and the primary method applied to solve the associated control problems is Pontryagin’s maximum principle. To mention just a few, we refer to
[
26
]
for a work in the early days of this area,
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
15
]
for a deterministic SIR model with isolation and/or vaccination,
[
8
,
29
]
for controlled epidemic network models. In
[
18
]
, the authors studied two stochastic SIS models with external parameter noise and scaled additive noise to minimize the long term average costs. In
[
20
]
, the authors investigated SIS meta-population models on networks by comparing different approaches numerically. The recent work
[
12
]
studies a SIS model under complete and incomplete observation of the state process by investigating the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and the Kolmogorov forward equation respectively.
Recently, increasing attention has been devoted to optimal control of the deterministic SIR model due to the appearance of the highly infectious diseases such as COVID-19; see
[
4
,
11
,
25
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, not much attention has been given to
stochastic hybrid SIS models
to date. The main difficulties come from the complexity of the model. Even for the stochastic SIS without regime switching, the methods and results in
[
12
]
only works under a set of strict assumptions on the model and the cost function.
In this work, we focus on a stochastic SIS model under Markovian switching.
We develop numerical approximation schemes by approximating the continuous-time dynamics by Markov chains and then showing that the approximations converge to the correct optimal strategy as the mesh size goes to zero.
Note that the addition of the Markovian switching is to account for environment changes that cannot be modeled by Brownian type of noise, but rather displaying jump behavior.
We use the Markov chain approximation methodology developed by Kushner and Dupuis
[
22
]
; see also
[
30
]
. Motivated by recent developments in modeling of epidemics, we consider two different models. The first
one is a direct extension of (
1.1
), in which the decision maker is able to control the recovery rate to some extent or isolate a part of the infected individuals. In the second
model, we incorporate the vaccination into the formulation. That is, the decision maker is able to control the recovery rate and also to take a decision on the fraction of vaccinated individuals.
Moreover, treating a general cost function, we
take into account of any cost associated with the control and either the outbreak size or the infectious burden under the assumption that there are limited control resources.

The rest of the work is organized as follows. Section
2

begins with the problem formulation.
Section
3
presents
numerical algorithms based on the Markov chain approximation method.
Section
4
focuses on a model with the incorporation of vaccination.
In Section
5
, we present several examples.
Finally, the paper is concluded with some further remarks. To facilitate the reading, all proofs are placed in an appendix at the end of the paper in order not to interrupt the flow of presentation.

2
Formulation

Inspired by the recent trend in modeling using regime-switching models, we use a continuous-time Markov chain to model environment changes that are not covered in the usual diffusion models.
Assume throughout the paper that both the Markov chain
\alpha(t)

and the scalar standard Brownian motion
w(\cdot)
are defined
on a complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}(t),\mathbb{P})
, where
\{\mathcal{F}(t)\}
is a filtration satisfying the usual condition (i.e., right continuous, increasing, and
\mathcal{F}(0)
containing all the null sets).

Started with
the classical deterministic SIS epidemic model
given in (
1.1
),
we
normalize the population size to one by replacing
S
and
I
with
S/N
and
I/N
, respectively.
We obtain

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[\mu-\lambda S(t)I(t)+\gamma I(t)-\mu S(t)\big]dt,\quad S(0)=s_{0},\\
dI(t)=\big[\lambda S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu+\gamma\big)I(t)\big]dt,\quad I(0)=i_{0},\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
S(t)
and
I(t)
are the fraction of susceptible and infected
individuals at time
t
, respectively.
Throughout the paper, we use
i
and
s
to denote the state variables of
I(t)
and
S(t)
, respectively, with
i_{0}
and
s_{0}
being the initial data.
Taking into account the environmental noise, the system parameters
\mu
,
\lambda
,
\gamma
may experience abrupt changes, which is modeled by a Markov chain
\alpha(\cdot)
as in
[
14
]

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)-\mu_{\alpha(t)}S(t)\big]dt,\\
dI(t)=\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}\big)I(t)\big]dt,\\
S(0)=s_{0},\quad I(0)=i_{0},\quad\alpha(0)=\ell_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.2)

We suppose that
\alpha(\cdot)
is a continuous time Markov chain taking values in a finite set
\mathcal{M}=\{1,2,\dots,m_{0}\}
generated by
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{\iota\ell})_{m_{0}\times m_{0}}
.
The long time behavior of (
2.2
) has been studied in
[
14
]
.
A key parameter of the system is the average number of contacts per
infected people
per day

\lambda_{\alpha(t)}
, which can be
perturbed by white noise; that is,
\lambda_{\alpha(t)}\to\lambda_{\alpha(t)}+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}\dot{w}(t)
with
w(\cdot)
being a scalar standard Brownian motion independent of
\alpha(\cdot)
. Thus,

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)-\mu_{\alpha(t)}S(t)\big]dt-\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
dI(t)=\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}\big)I(t)\big]dt+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
S(0)=s_{0},\quad I(0)=i_{0},\quad\alpha(0)=\ell_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.3)

As in
[
12
,
18
,
20
]
, we suppose that the natural development of the disease can be influenced by a decision maker. In particular, the decision maker is able to control the magnitude of the recovery rate to some extent. Such a control can be performed by increasing the treatment capacity or the efficiency of medication.
To be more specific,
\gamma_{\alpha(t)}
is the recovery rate without any action by the decision maker, while
\gamma_{\alpha(t)}+C(t)

is the recovery rate at time
t
if a control
C(t)\geq 0
is applied at
t
. As in
[
20
]
,
C(t)
can be interpreted as the product
C(t)=\widetilde{C}(t)\widetilde{\zeta}
, where
\widetilde{C}(t)
is the proportion of the infected population treated at time
t
and
\widetilde{\zeta}
is the treatment effectiveness.
We can also regard
C(t)
as per-capital rate of isolation, which
has a direct effect only on infected individual. Thus, the model under consideration can be regarded as a standard isolation model; see
[
6
,
15
]
. To indicate that we have limited resources to handle the epidemic, we suppose that
C(\cdot)
takes values in a nonempty compact set
\mathcal{U}
of
[0,\infty)
, where
0\in\mathcal{U}
. The controlled version is given by

\begin{cases}dS(t)=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)-\mu_{\alpha(t)}S(t)\big]dt-\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
dI(t)=\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}+C(t)\big)I(t)\big]dt+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
S(0)=s_{0},\quad I(0)=i_{0},\quad\alpha(0)=\ell_{0}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Before proceeding further, we state the following result regarding the existence of a unique positive solution to (
2.4
).

Theorem 2.1
.

For any given initial value
(s_{0},i_{0},\ell_{0})\in(0,1)^{2}\times{\cal{M}}
satisfying
s_{0}+i_{0}=1
and
C(t)\equiv c_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
, equation (
2.4
) has a unique global solution
(S(t),I(t),\alpha(t))\in(0,1)^{2}\times{\cal{M}}
and
S(t)+I(t)=1
for any
t\geq 0

with probability one.

Given that
I(t)+S(t)=1
,

I(t)
, the fraction of
infected individuals, obeys the stochastic Lotka-Volterra model with Markovian switching given by

dI(t)=I(t)\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}-\mu_{\alpha(t)}-\gamma_{\alpha(t)}-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}I(t)-C(t)\big]dt+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)\big[1-I(t)\big]dw(t).

(2.5)

For convenience, we define

b(i,\ell,c)=i\big(\lambda_{\ell}-\mu_{\ell}-\gamma_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}i-c\big),\quad a(i,\ell)=\sigma^{2}_{\ell}i^{2}(1-i)^{2}.

As in
[
6
,
15
]
, we choose the eradication time
\tau
as the terminal time of planning (that is, time horizon). Formally, let
\zeta
be a positive constant such that
\xi<1
, then

\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0:I(t)\leq\xi\}.

(2.6)

To make the definition in (
2.6
) meaningful, we assume that the initial number of infected units
I(0)
is strictly greater than
\xi
. Thus,
\tau
is the first time at which the state variable
I
drops to
\xi
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}
denote the collection of all admissible controls with initial value
(i,\ell)\in(0,1)\times\mathcal{M}
. A strategy
C(\cdot)
will be in
\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}
if

C(t)
is

\mathcal{F}(t)
-adapted and
C(t)\in\mathcal{U}
for any
t\geq 0
.
We suppose that
increasing the recovery rate is costly and the
treatment of the
infected individuals also create additional costs. The cost is described by the bounded cost function

F:[0,1]\times{\cal{M}}\times[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
.
For a control strategy
C(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}
, we define the cost
functional as

J(i,\ell,C(\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}_{i,\ell}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\delta t}F(I(t),\alpha(t),C(t))dt,

(2.7)

where
\delta>0
is the discounting factor and
\mathbb{E}_{i,\ell}
denotes the expectation with respect
to the probability law when the process
(I(t),\alpha(t))
starts with initial condition
(i,\ell)
. The goal is to minimize the
cost functional and find an optimal strategy
C^{*}(\cdot)
such that

J(i,\ell,C^{*}(\cdot))=V(i,\ell):=\inf\limits_{C(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}_{i,\ell}}J(i,\ell,C(\cdot)).

(2.8)

Formally, the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation of the underlying problem is given by

\min\limits_{c\in\mathcal{U}}\Big[b(i,\ell,c)\dfrac{dV(i,\ell)}{d\,i}+\dfrac{1}{2}a(i,\ell)\dfrac{d^{2}V(i,\ell)}{d\,i^{2}}+\sum\limits_{\iota=1}^{m_{0}}\Lambda_{\ell\iota}V(i,\iota)+F(i,\ell,c)-\delta V(i,\ell)\Big]=0,

(2.9)

for all
(i,\ell)\in[\xi,1]\times{\cal{M}}
, with the boundary condition
V(\xi,\ell)=0
for any
\ell\in{\cal{M}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the examples in Section
5
, we will work on a specific cost function of the form

F(i,\ell,c)=a_{0}+a_{1}i+a_{2}ic^{2},

where
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
, and
a_{2}
are the
weighting factors, representing the cost per unit time of the components
1
,
i
,
ic^{2}
, respectively.
In particular,
\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{0}dt
is the cost due to the time period needed for outbreak eradication, while
\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{1}I(t)dt
is the cost that infected individual creates for the society due to lost working hours and standard medical care, not including the treatment
C(\cdot)
.
\int_{0}^{\tau}a_{2}I(t)C^{2}(t)dt
is the cost of treating infected individuals. We assume that the cost is proportional to the number of infected individuals, and the cost per each patient depends quadratically on the treatment effort
C(t)
. As in
[
6
]
, if one is interested in minimizing the sum of the eradication time and the total epidemic size, one can use the cost function given by

F(i,\ell,c)=a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda_{\ell}(1-i)i+a_{2}ic^{2},

where
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
are the weighting factors, representing the cost per unit time of epidemic duration and the cost of a single new infection, respectively.

Our standing assumptions are
as follows.

(A)

1.

The system parameters
\mu_{\ell}
,
\gamma_{\ell}
,
\lambda_{\ell}
are all nonnegative for each
\ell\in{\cal{M}}
.

2.

The control set
\mathcal{U}
is a nonempty compact set in
[0,\infty)
with
0\in\mathcal{U}
.

3.

For any
\ell\in\mathcal{M}
, the cost function
F(\cdot,\ell,\cdot)
is bounded, continuous, and nonnegative.

Remark 2.3
.

Under assumption (A), for any initial value
(i,\ell)\in(0,1)\times{\cal{M}}
and
C(t)\equiv c\in\mathcal{U}
, equation (
2.5
) has the unique global solution
(I(\cdot),\alpha(\cdot))\in(0,1)\times{\cal{M}}
for all
t\geq 0
; see Theorem
2.1
.
It follows from the boundedness of
F(\cdot)
that
V(i,\ell)<\infty
for any
(i,\ell)\in(0,1)\times{\cal{M}}
.

Although our work is motivated by the presence of a Markovian switching random environment, our results and the numerical schemes we developed can also be used to the corresponding control problems of diffusion models without switching. In particular, one can simply take
{\cal{M}}=\{1\}
.

We are in a position to construct a numerical procedure for
solving the optimal control problem.

3
Numerical Algorithm

Following the Markov chain approximation method in
[
22
,
30
]
, we construct a controlled Markov chain
in discrete time to approximate the controlled switching diffusions.

3.1
Approximation Algorithm of the Combined Process

Let
h>0
be a discretization parameter for the continuous state variable.
Define

S_{h}:=\{i=kh:k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},k\leq 1/h\}\times{\cal{M}}.

Let
\{(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n}):n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}

be a discrete-time controlled Markov chain with state space
S_{h}

such that
the controlled Markov chain well approximates the local behavior of the controlled diffusion
\big(I(t),\alpha(t)\big)
. At any discrete-time step
n
, the magnitude of the control component
C^{h}_{n}
must be specified. The space of controls is

\mathcal{U}
.
Let
C^{h}=\{C^{h}_{n}\}
be a sequence of controls. We denote by
p^{h}((i,\ell),(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c)
the transition probability from state
(i,\ell)
to another state
(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})
under the control
c
. Denote
\mathcal{F}^{h}_{n}=\sigma\{I^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k},k\leq n\}
.

The sequence
C^{h}
is said to be admissible if it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

C^{h}
is

\sigma\{I^{h}_{0},\dots,I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{0},\dots,\alpha^{h}_{n},C^{h}_{0},\dots,C^{h}_{n-1}\}
-adapted,

(b)

For any
(i,\ell)\in S_{h}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\{(I^{h}_{n+1},\alpha^{h}_{n+1})=(i,\ell)|\mathcal{F}^{h}_{n}\}=\mathbb{P}\{(I^{h}_{n+1},\alpha^{h}_{n+1})=(i,\ell)|I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n},C^{h}_{n}\}=p^{h}((I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n}),(i,\ell)|C^{h}_{n}),

(c)

(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\in S_{h}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

The collection
of all admissible control sequences
for initial state
(i,\ell)
will be denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,\ell}
.
For each

(i,\ell,c)\in S_{h}\times\mathcal{U}
,
we define
a family of the interpolation intervals
\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)
. The values of
\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)
will be specified later. Then we define

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle t^{h}_{0}=0,\quad\Delta t^{h}_{k}=\Delta t^{h}(I^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k}),\quad t^{h}_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\Delta t^{h}_{k}.\\
\end{array}

(3.1)

For
(i,\ell)\in S_{h}
and
C^{h}\in\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,\ell}
, the
cost functional for the controlled Markov chain is defined as

J^{h}(i,\ell,C^{h})=\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{h}}e^{-\delta t_{k}^{h}}F(i^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k})\Delta t^{h}_{k},

(3.2)

with

\eta_{h}=\inf\{n\geq 0:I^{h}_{n}\leq\xi\}.

The value function of the controlled Markov chain is

V^{h}(i,\ell)=\inf\limits_{C^{h}\in\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,\ell}}J^{h}(i,\ell,C^{h}).

(3.3)

The corresponding dynamic programming equation for the discrete approximation is given by

V^{h}(i,\ell)=\min\limits_{c\in\mathcal{U}}\bigg[e^{-\delta\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)}\sum\limits_{(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in S_{h}}V^{h}(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})p^{h}\big((i,\ell),(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c\big)+F(i,\ell,c)\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)\bigg],

for any
(i,\ell)\in S_{h}.

3.2
Transition Probabilities and Local Consistency

Let
\mathbb{E}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}
,
\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}
denote the conditional expectation and covariance with given

\{I_{k}^{h},\alpha^{h}_{k},C_{k}^{h},k\leq n,I_{n}^{h}=i,\alpha^{h}_{n}=\ell,C^{h}_{n}=c\},

respectively. Define

\Delta I_{n}^{h}=I_{n+1}^{h}-I_{n}^{h}.

Our objective is to define transition probabilities
p^{h}((i,\ell),(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c)
so that the controlled Markov chain
\{(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\}
is locally consistent with
the controlled switching diffusion (
2.5
)
in the sense that the following conditions hold:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}\Delta I_{n}^{h}={a}(i,\ell,c)\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)+o(\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)),\\
&\displaystyle\mathbb{C}{\rm ov}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}\Delta I_{n}^{h}=a(i,\ell,c)\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)+o(\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)),\\
&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}(\alpha^{h}_{n+1}=\ell^{\prime})=\Lambda_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)+o(\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c))\quad\text{for}\quad\ell\neq\ell^{\prime},\\
&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{h,c}_{i,\ell,n}(\alpha^{h}_{n+1}=\ell)=1+\Lambda_{\ell\ell}\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)+o(\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)),\\
&\displaystyle\sup\limits_{n,\ \omega}|\Delta I_{n}^{h}|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad h\to 0.\end{array}

(3.4)

Using the procedure in
Inspired by

[
22
]
,
for
(i,\ell)\in S_{h}
, we define

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)=a(i,\ell,c)+h|b(i,\ell)|-h^{2}\Lambda_{\ell\ell}+h,\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,\ell),(i+h,\ell)|c\right)=\dfrac{a(i,\ell)/2+\big(b(i,\ell,c)\big)^{+}h}{Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,\ell),(i-h,\ell)|c\right)=\dfrac{a(i,\ell)/2+\left(b(i,\ell,c)\right)^{-}h}{Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,\ell),(i,\ell^{\prime})|c\right)=\dfrac{h^{2}\Lambda_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}{Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)}\text{ for }\ell\neq\ell^{\prime},\quad p^{h}\left((i,\ell),(i,\ell)|c\right)=\dfrac{h}{Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)},\\
&\displaystyle\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)=\dfrac{h^{2}}{Q_{h}(i,\ell,c)},\end{array}

(3.5)

where for a real number
r
,
r^{+}=\max\{r,0\}
,

r^{-}=-\min\{0,r\}
.
Set
p^{h}\left((i,\ell),(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c\right)=0
for all unlisted values of
(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in S_{h}
.
Assumption (A) guarantees that
the transition probabilities in (
4.7
) are well-defined. Using the above transition probabilities,
we can check that the local-consistency conditions of
\{(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\}
in (
3.4
) are satisfied.

Lemma 3.1
.

The Markov chain
\{(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\}
with transition probabilities
\{p^{h}(\cdot)\}
defined in (
4.7
) is locally consistent with (
2.5
).

3.3
Continuous-Time Interpolation and Time Rescaling

To proceed, we construct a continuous-time interpolation of the approximating chain.
For use in this construction, we define

n^{h}(t)=\max\{n:t^{h}_{n}\leq t\},t\geq 0
.
The discrete time processes associated with the controlled Markov chain
\{(I^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\}
are defined as follows. Let

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle B^{h}_{0}=M^{h}_{0}=0,\quad B^{h}_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}^{h}_{k}\Delta I_{k}^{h},\quad M^{h}_{n}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}(\Delta I_{k}^{h}-\mathbb{E}^{h}_{k}\Delta I_{k}^{h}),\quad n\geq 1.\end{array}

(3.6)

The piecewise constant interpolation processes, denoted by

I^{h}(\cdot),\alpha^{h}(\cdot),B^{h}(\cdot),M^{h}(\cdot),C^{h}(\cdot)

are naturally defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle I^{h}(t)=I^{h}_{n^{h}(t)},\quad\alpha^{h}(t)=\alpha^{h}_{n^{h}(t)},\quad C^{h}(t)=C^{h}_{n^{h}(t)},\\
&\displaystyle B^{h}(t)=B^{h}_{n^{h}(t)},\quad M^{h}(t)=M^{h}_{n^{h}(t)},\quad t\geq 0.\end{array}

(3.7)

Define
\mathcal{F}^{h}(t)=\sigma\{I^{h}(s),\alpha^{h}(s),C^{h}(s):s\leq t\}
.
We have

I^{h}(t)=i+B^{h}(t)+M^{h}(t).

(3.8)

Recall that
\Delta t^{h}_{k}=h^{2}/Q_{h}(I^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k})
. It follows that

\begin{array}[]{ll}B^{h}(t)&\!\!\!\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n^{h}(t)-1}b(I^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k})\Delta t^{h}_{k}\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}b(I^{h}(u),\alpha^{h}(u),C^{h}(u))du-\int_{t^{h}_{n^{h}(t)}}^{t}b(I^{h}(u),\alpha^{h}(u),C^{h}(u))du\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}b(I^{h}(u),\alpha^{h}(u),C^{h}(u))du+\varepsilon^{h}_{1}(t),\end{array}

(3.9)

with
\{\varepsilon_{1}^{h}(\cdot)\}

being an
\mathcal{F}^{h}(t)
-adapted process satisfying
\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T_{0}]}\mathbb{E}|\varepsilon_{1}^{h}(t)|=0
for
T_{0}\in(0,\infty).

Define

\tau_{h}=t^{h}_{\eta_{h}}.

The
cost functional from (
3.2
) can be rewritten as

J^{h}(i,\ell,C^{h})=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}e^{-\delta t}F(I^{h}(t),\alpha^{h}(t),C^{h}(t))dt.

(3.10)

3.4
Relaxed controls

For our analysis, it is more convenient to use the notion of relaxed controls.
We first briefly recall the notion of “relaxed control”, which arises naturally in the weak convergence analysis for the approximation to the optimal control problems.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
be the
\sigma
-algebra of Borel subsets of
\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty)
. An admissible relaxed control or simply
a relaxed control
m(\cdot)
is a measure on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
such
that

m(\mathcal{U}\times[0,t])=t\quad\text{for all}\quad t\geq 0.

Given a relaxed control
m(\cdot)
,
there is a probability measure
m_{t}(\cdot)
defined on the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U})
such that
m(dcdt)=m_{t}(dc)dt
.

With the given probability
space, we say that
m(\cdot)
is an admissible relaxed stochastic control
for
(w(\cdot),\alpha(\cdot))
or
(m(\cdot),w(\cdot),\alpha(\cdot))
is admissible, if (i) for each fixed
t\geq 0
,
m(t,\cdot)
is a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
, and for each fixed
\omega
,
m(\cdot,\omega)
is a deterministic relaxed control; (ii) the function defined by
m(A\times[0,t])
is
\mathcal{F}(t)
-adapted for any
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U})
. As a result,
with probability one, there is a measure
m_{t}(\cdot,\omega)

on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{U})
such that

m(dcdt)=m_{t}(dc)dt
.

Remark 3.3
.

For a sequence of controls
C^{h}=\{C^{h}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
, we define a sequence of equivalent relaxed controls
as follows.
First, we set
m_{t^{h}_{n}}^{h}(dc)=\delta_{C^{h}_{n}}(dc)
, where
\delta_{C^{h}_{n}}(\cdot)
is the probability measure concentrated at
C^{h}_{n}
. Then
m^{h}(\cdot)
is defined by
m^{h}(dcdt)=m_{t}(dc)dt
. That is,

m^{h}(B\times[0,t])=\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\int_{B}\delta_{C^{h}(u)}(dc)\Big)du,\quad B\in\mathcal{B}({\mathcal{U}})\quad\text{and}\quad t\geq 0.

Let
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
denote the space of all
relaxed controls
on
\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty)
.
Then
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))

can be metrized using the Prohorov metric in the usual way as in
[
22
, pp.
263-264]
. With the Prohorov metric,
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))

is a compact space. It follows that any sequence of relaxed controls has a convergent subsequence.
Moreover,
a sequence
\eta_{n}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
converges to
\eta\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
if and only if for all continuous functions with compact support
\Psi(\cdot)
on
\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty)
,

\int_{\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty)}\Psi(c,t)\eta_{n}(dc,dt)\to\int_{\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty)}\Psi(c,t)\eta(dc,dt).

Note that for a sequence of ordinary controls
C^{h}=\{C^{h}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
, the associated relaxed control
{m}^{h}(dcdt)
belongs to
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{U}\times[0,\infty))
.
Note also that the limits of the “relaxed
control representations” of the ordinary controls might not be ordinary controls, but
only relaxed controls.

With the notion of relaxed control given above, we can write (
3.10
) as

J^{h}(i,\ell,C^{h})=J^{h}(i,\ell,m^{h}(\cdot))=\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau_{h}}e^{-\delta t}F(I^{h}(t),\alpha^{h}(t),c)m_{t}^{h}(dc)dt.

(3.11)

Note also that the value function defined in (
2.8
) can be rewritten as

V(i,\ell)=\inf\{J(i,\ell,m(\cdot)):m(\cdot)\,\,\,\text{ is an admissible relaxed control}\},

where

J(i,\ell,m(\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}_{i,\ell}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\delta t}F\big(I(t),\alpha(t),c\big)m_{t}(dc)dt.

4
A Stochastic SIS Model with Vaccination

Building on our approach of the SIS models,
we consider a SIS epidemic model with vaccination proposed in
[
16
,
23
]
; see also
[
34
,
35
]
for closely related models. We assume that
S(t)
,
I(t)
, and
{\mathfrak{V}}(t)
are the fraction of infectious, susceptible, and vaccinated individuals at time
t
, respectively. The evolution of
(S(\cdot),I(\cdot),{\mathfrak{V}}(\cdot))
is given by

\begin{cases}{dS(t)}&=\big[\mu(1-q(t))-\lambda S(t)I(t)-(\mu+p(t))S(t)+\gamma I(t)+\varepsilon{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big]dt,\\
{dI(t)}&=\big[\lambda S(t)I(t)-(\mu+\gamma)I(t)\big]dt,\\
{d{\mathfrak{V}}(t)}&=\big[\mu q(t)+p(t)S(t)-(\mu+\varepsilon){\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big]dt,\end{cases}

(4.1)

where

q(\cdot)
is the fraction of vaccinated for newborns,

p(\cdot)
is the proportional coefficient of vaccinated for the susceptible,
\varepsilon
is the rate of losing their immunity for vaccinated individuals. The other parameters are understood as in the preceding sections; that is,
\mu
and
\gamma^{-1}
are the average death rate and the average infectious period respectively,
\lambda
is the disease contact rate of an infective individual. Note that if
{\mathfrak{V}}(0)=p(t)=q(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
, then
{\mathfrak{V}}(t)=0
for any
t\geq 0
and the system (
4.1
) reduces to (
2.1
).

Using the same methods as in the preceding sections, we obtain the following controlled SIS system under regime switching in random environment

\begin{cases}{dS(t)}&=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}(1-q(t))-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)-(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+p(t))S(t)+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)+\varepsilon_{\alpha(t)}{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big]dt\\
&\hskip 156.49014pt-\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
{dI(t)}&=\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)-\big(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}+C(t)\big)I(t)\big]dt+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}S(t)I(t)dw(t),\\
{d{\mathfrak{V}}(t)}&=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}q(t)+p(t)S(t)-(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\varepsilon_{\alpha(t)}){\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big]dt,\end{cases}

(4.2)

where
\alpha(\cdot)
is a Markov chain taking values in
\mathcal{M}=\{1,2,\dots,m_{0}\}
and
w(\cdot)
is a scalar standard Brownian motion independent of
\alpha(\cdot)
.
We suppose that
C(\cdot)
takes values in a nonempty compact set
\mathcal{U}
of
[0,\infty)
. Suppose
\mathcal{V}_{p}
and
\mathcal{V}_{q}
are nonempty compact subsets of
[0,1]
. Moreover,
p(\cdot)
and
q(\cdot)
takes the values in
\mathcal{V}_{p}
and
\mathcal{V}_{q}
, respectively.
Compared to the related formulations in
[
6
,
15
]
, we also consider the possibility that vaccinated individuals lose their immunity.
We suppose that the initial value is
(s_{0},i_{0},{\mathfrak{v}}_{0})\in(0,1)^{2}\times[0,1)
satisfying
s_{0}+i_{0}+{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}=1
. Thus, we exclude the trivial case
i_{0}=0
or unrealistic cases such as
i_{0}=1
and
s_{0}=0
. A fundamental result on
the existence of a unique global solution of (
4.2
) is given below.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any given initial value
(s_{0},i_{0},{\mathfrak{v}}_{0},\ell_{0})\in(0,1)^{2}\times[0,1)\times{\cal{M}}
satisfying
s_{0}+i_{0}+{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}=1
,
C(t)\equiv c_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
,
p(t)\equiv p_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{p}
and
q(t)\equiv q_{0}\in\mathcal{V}_{q}
,
the equation (
4.2
) has a unique global solution
(S(t),I(t),{\mathfrak{V}}(t),\alpha(t))\in(0,1)^{2}\times[0,1)\times{\cal{M}}
and
S(t)+I(t)+{\mathfrak{V}}(t)=1

for any
t\geq 0

with probability one.

Since
S(t)+I(t)+{\mathfrak{V}}(t)=1
for any
t\geq 0
, we need only
consider the last two equations; that is,

\begin{cases}{dI(t)}&=\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}\big(1-I(t)-{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big)I(t)-\big(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\gamma_{\alpha(t)}+C(t)\big)I(t)\big]dt\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}\big(1-I(t)-{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big)I(t)dw(t),\\
{d{\mathfrak{V}}(t)}&=\big[\mu_{\alpha(t)}q(t)+p(t)\big(1-I(t)-V(t)\big)-(\mu_{\alpha(t)}+\varepsilon_{\alpha(t)}){\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big]dt.\end{cases}

(4.3)

Let
\mathcal{A}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}
denote the collection of all admissible controls with initial value
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)\in(0,1)\times[0,1)\times\mathcal{M}
satisfying
0<i+{\mathfrak{v}}\leq 1
; that is,

I(0)=i,\quad{\mathfrak{V}}(0)={\mathfrak{v}}.

Then Theorem
4.1
indicates that
\mathcal{A}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}\neq\emptyset.

The control component is
(C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot))
.
A strategy
(C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot))
will be in
\mathcal{A}_{i,v,\ell}
if
\big(C(t),p(t),q(t)\big)
is
\mathcal{F}(t)
-adapted and

\big(C(t),p(t),q(t)\big)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}_{p}\times\mathcal{V}_{q}\ \hbox{ for any }\ t\geq 0.

The cost is described by the bounded cost function

F:[0,1]\times{\cal{M}}\times\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}_{p}\times\mathcal{V}_{q}\to[0,\infty)
.
For a control strategy
(C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot))\in\mathcal{A}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\alpha}
, we define the
cost functional as

J(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\delta t}F\big(I(t),\alpha(t),C(t),p(t),q(t)\big)dt,

(4.4)

where
\delta>0
is the discounting factor and
\mathbb{E}_{i,v,\ell}
denotes the expectation with respect
to the probability law when the process
(I(t),{\mathfrak{V}}(t),\alpha(t))
starts with initial condition
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
. The goal is to minimize the
cost functional and find an optimal strategy
(C^{*}(\cdot),p^{*}(\cdot),q^{*}(\cdot))
such that

J(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,C^{*}(\cdot),p^{*}(\cdot),q^{*}(\cdot))=V(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell):=\inf\limits_{(C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot))\in\mathcal{A}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\alpha}}J(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,C(\cdot),p(\cdot),q(\cdot)).

(4.5)

We use the same method as in the preceding section to construct a discrete-time controlled Markov chain
\{(I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n}):n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
with the state space

T_{h}:=\big(\{(k_{1}h,k_{2}h):k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},k_{1}+k_{2}\leq 1/h\}\cap[0,1]\big)^{2}\times{\cal{M}}.

At any discrete-time step
n
, the magnitude of the control component
(C^{h}_{n},p^{h}_{n},q^{h}_{n})
must be specified. The space of controls is

\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}_{p}\times\mathcal{V}_{q}
.
Let
(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})=\{(C^{h}_{n},p^{h}_{n},q^{h}_{n})\}
be a sequence of controls.
Denote
\mathcal{F}^{h}_{n}=\sigma\{I^{h}_{k},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k},p^{h}_{k},q^{h}_{k},k\leq n\}
.

The sequence
(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})
is said to be admissible if it satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})
is

\sigma\{I^{h}_{0},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{0},\alpha^{h}_{0}\dots,I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n},C^{h}_{0},p^{h}_{0},q^{h}_{n}\dots,C^{h}_{n-1},p^{h}_{n-1},q^{h}_{n-1}\}
-adapted,

(b)

For any
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)\in T_{h}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathbb{P}\{(I^{h}_{n+1},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n+1},\alpha^{h}_{n+1})=(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|\mathcal{F}^{h}_{n}\}\\
&\qquad=\mathbb{P}\{(I^{h}_{n+1},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n+1},\alpha^{h}_{n+1})=(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n},C^{h}_{n},p^{h}_{n},q^{h}_{n}\}\\
&\qquad=p^{h}((I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n}),(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|C^{h}_{n},p^{h}_{n},q^{h}_{n}),\end{array}

(c)

(I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n})\in T_{h}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

The class of all admissible control sequences
(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})
for initial state
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
will be denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}
.
We need to define the transition probabilities
p^{h}((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i^{\prime},{\mathfrak{v}}^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c,p,q)
so that the controlled Markov chain
\{(I^{h}_{n},{\mathfrak{V}}^{h}_{n},\alpha^{h}_{n}):n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}
is locally consistent with respect to the controlled diffusion (
4.2
). To proceed, we denote

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle b_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c)=\lambda_{\ell}(1-i-{\mathfrak{v}})i-(\mu_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell}+c)i,\quad a_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)=\sigma_{\ell}^{2}(1-i-{\mathfrak{v}})^{2}i^{2},\\
&\displaystyle b_{2}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)=\mu_{\ell}q+p(1-i-{\mathfrak{v}})-(\mu_{\ell}+\varepsilon_{\ell}){\mathfrak{v}}.\end{array}

(4.6)

In particular, for
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)\in T_{h}
and
(c,p,q)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{V}_{p}\times\mathcal{V}_{q}
, we define

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)=a_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)+h|b_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c)|+h|b_{2}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)|-h^{2}\Lambda_{\ell\ell}+h,\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i+h,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{a_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)/2+\big(b_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c)\big)^{+}h}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i-h,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{a_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)/2+\left(b_{1}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c)\right)^{-}h}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i,{\mathfrak{v}}+h,\ell)|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{\big(b_{2}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)\big)^{+}h}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i,{\mathfrak{v}}-h,\ell)|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{\left(b_{2}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)\right)^{-}h}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell^{\prime})|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{h^{2}\Lambda_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)}\text{ for }\ell\neq\ell^{\prime},\\
&\displaystyle p^{h}\left((i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell),(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)|c,p,q\right)=\dfrac{h}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)},\quad\Delta t^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)=\dfrac{h^{2}}{Q_{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,c,p,q)}.\end{array}

(4.7)

For
(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)\in T_{h}
and
(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})\in\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}
, the cost functional
for the controlled Markov chain is given by

J^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})=\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{h}}e^{-\delta t_{k}^{h}}F(i^{h}_{k},\alpha^{h}_{k},C^{h}_{k},p^{h}_{k},q^{h}_{k})\Delta t^{h}_{k}.

(4.8)

The value function is

V^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)=\inf\limits_{(C^{h},p^{h},q^{h})\in\mathcal{A}^{h}_{i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell}}J^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell,C^{h},p^{h},q^{h}).

(4.9)

The main convergence result in this case is given below.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
V(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
and
V^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
be the value functions defined in (
4.5
) and (
4.9
), respectively. Then
V^{h}(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)\to V(i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
as
h\to 0
.

Examples

Throughout this section, we suppose the discounting factor is
\delta=0.05
. Also, we work with
{\cal{M}}=\{1,2\}
and
\xi=0.02
.

Example 5.1
.

We start with the model given by (
2.5
). That is,

dI(t)=I(t)\big[\lambda_{\alpha(t)}-\mu_{\alpha(t)}-\gamma_{\alpha(t)}-\lambda_{\alpha(t)}I(t)-C(t)\big]dt+\sigma_{\alpha(t)}I(t)\big[1-I(t)\big]dw(t).

(5.1)

For
(i,\ell)\in(\xi,1]\times{\cal{M}}
, we take the initial control
C_{0}(i,\ell)\equiv\max\mathcal{U}

and set the initial value

V^{h}_{0}(i,\ell)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\delta t}F(1,\ell,C_{0})dt
.
We outline how to find the sequence
V^{h}_{n}(\cdot)
as follows.
For each
(i,\ell)\in S_{h}
and control
c\in\mathcal{U}
,
we compute

V_{n+1}^{h}(i,\ell\,|\,c)=e^{-\delta\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c)}\sum\limits_{(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})\in S_{h}}V^{h}_{n}(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})p^{h}\big((i,\ell),(i^{\prime},\ell^{\prime})|c\big)+F(i,\ell,c)\Delta t^{h}(i,\ell,c).

Note also that if
i\leq\xi
, then
V_{n}^{h}(i,\ell)=0
for any
\ell
and
n
.
We choose the control
C^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell)
and record an improved value

V^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell)
by

C^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell)={\rm arcmin}_{c\in\mathcal{U}}V_{n+1}^{h}(i,\ell\,|\,c),\quad V^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell)=V^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell\,|\,C^{h}_{n+1}(i,\ell)).

The iterations stop as soon as the
increment

V^{h}_{n}(\cdot)-V^{h}_{n+1}(\cdot)

reaches a predetermined
tolerance level.
We set the error tolerance to be
10^{-8}
.
Our specific example is motivated by
[
14
, Example 6.2.1]
. The system parameters are given by

\mu_{1}=0.45,\quad\mu_{2}=0.05,\quad\gamma_{1}=0.35,\quad\gamma_{2}=0.15,\quad\lambda_{1}=2,\quad\lambda_{2}=2.4,\quad\sigma_{1}=0,\quad\sigma_{2}=1.

Suppose that the generator
\Gamma
of the Markov chain

Further Remarks

This paper focused on numerical methods for optimal control of SIS epidemic models. We considered two models incorporating treatments, isolation, and vaccination. Using the Markov chain approximation method, we are able to treat a hybrid model affected by two types of environmental fluctuations with a general cost consideration.
The convergence of the algorithms was proved. Several numerical examples were used to demonstrate the performance of our algorithm.
Some interesting questions deserve further investigation. One can study a set of mixed controls consisting of vaccination, isolation, and social distancing, etc. One can also apply the approach in this work to investigate a variety of control problems of epidemic models, which becomes an urgent issue due to
COVID-19.
Thus, the techniques and the simulation study might be of interests
to researchers in various disciplines.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Since the coefficients of (
4.2
) are locally Lipschitz continuous,
there is a unique local solution
(I(t),{\mathfrak{V}}(t),\alpha(t))
on
t\in[0,\zeta)
, where
\zeta
is the explosion time.
Now, by (
4.2
),
S+I+{\mathfrak{V}}
satisfies

\begin{cases}&d(S+I+{\mathfrak{V}})(t)=\mu_{\alpha(t)}\big(1-S(t)-I(t)-{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\big)dt,\\
&S(0)+I(0)+{\mathfrak{V}}(0)=1,\end{cases}

which implies

S(t)+I(t)+{\mathfrak{V}}(t)=1\quad\text{for}\quad t\in[0,\zeta).

(A.1)

Let
k_{0}
be a sufficiently large positive integer
such that
s_{0},i_{0}\in\left({1}/{k_{0}},1\right)
. For each
k\geq k_{0}
, we define

\tau_{k}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:I(t)\leq\frac{1}{k}\quad\text{or}\quad S(t)\leq\frac{1}{k}\right\}.

(A.2)

Clearly the sequence
\{\tau_{k}\}

is monotonically increasing. Let
\tau_{\infty}:=\lim\limits_{l\to\infty}\tau_{k}
. Then
\tau_{\infty}\leq\zeta
. It suffices to show
that
\tau_{\infty}=\infty
with probability one. If this were false, there would
exist a
T>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
P\{\tau_{\infty}\leq T\}>\varepsilon
.
Therefore we can find some
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
such that

P\{\tau_{k}\leq T\}>\varepsilon\quad\text{for}\quad k\geq k_{1}.

(A.3)

We fix
\omega\in\Omega
. For
t\in[0,\tau_{\infty})
, by (
A.1
) and (
A.2
),
I(t)\in(0,1]
.
We have from the third equation in (
4.2
) that

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\!\!\displaystyle{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\leq{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mu_{\alpha(u)}q_{0}+p_{0}-\big[\mu_{\alpha(u)}+\varepsilon_{\alpha(u)}+p_{0}\big]{\mathfrak{V}}(u)\Big)du\leq{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}+K_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\big(1-{\mathfrak{V}}(u)\big)du,\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\geq{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\mu_{\alpha(u)}q_{0}-\big[\mu_{\alpha(u)}+\varepsilon_{\alpha(u)}+p_{0}\big]{\mathfrak{V}}(u)\Big)du\geq{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}-K_{2}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathfrak{V}}(u)du,\end{array}

(A.4)

where
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are nonnegative numbers.
Since
{\mathfrak{v}}_{0}\in[0,1)
it follows from (
A.4
) that
{\mathfrak{V}}(t)\in[0,1)
for any
t\in[0,\tau_{\infty})
. Combining this fact and (

), we have

(S(t),I(t),{\mathfrak{V}}(t))\in(0,1)^{2}\times[0,1)\quad\text{for}\quad t\in[0,\tau_{\infty})\quad\text{with probability one}.

To proceed, we
consider the function

\Phi(s,i)=\frac{1}{i}+\dfrac{1}{s},\quad(s,i)\in(0,1)^{2},

and define

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal L}\Phi(s,i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)&\!\!\!\displaystyle=\big(\mu_{\ell}(1-q_{0})-\lambda_{\ell}si-(\mu_{\ell}+p_{0})s+\gamma_{\ell}i+\varepsilon_{\ell}{\mathfrak{v}}\big)\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}(s,i)\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle\qquad+\big(\lambda_{\ell}si-(\mu_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell}+c_{0})i\big)\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial i}(s,i)+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial s^{2}}\sigma^{2}_{\ell}s^{2}i^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial i^{2}}\sigma^{2}_{\ell}s^{2}i^{2}.\end{array}

Note that for our study, it suffices to consider
\Phi
to be independent of the switching states.
We have
\Phi(s,i)>0

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\!\!\!\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial s}(s,i)=-\dfrac{1}{s^{2}},\quad\dfrac{\partial\Phi}{\partial i}(s,i)=-\dfrac{1}{i^{2}},\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial s^{2}}(s,i)=\dfrac{2}{s^{3}},\quad\dfrac{\partial^{2}\Phi}{\partial i^{2}}(s,i)=\dfrac{2}{i^{3}}\end{array}

It follows that

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal L}\Phi(s,i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)&\!\!\!\displaystyle=-\big(\mu_{\ell}(1-q_{0})-\lambda_{\ell}si-(\mu_{\ell}+p_{0})s+\gamma_{\ell}i+\varepsilon_{\ell}{\mathfrak{v}}\big)\dfrac{1}{s^{2}}\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle\qquad-\big(\lambda_{\ell}si-(\mu_{\ell}+\gamma_{\ell}+c_{0})i\big)\dfrac{1}{i^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{2}{s^{3}}(s,i)\sigma^{2}_{\ell}s^{2}i^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\dfrac{2}{i^{3}}(s,i)\sigma^{2}_{\ell}s^{2}i^{2}.\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle\leq K\Phi(s,i),\end{array}

(A.5)

for some positive constant

independent of
(s,i,{\mathfrak{v}},\ell)
.
By Dynkin’s formla,
we obtain for

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}\Phi\left(S(\tau_{k}\wedge t),I(\tau_{k}\wedge t)\right)&\!\!\!\displaystyle=\Phi\left(s_{0},i_{0}\right)+\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{\tau_{k}\wedge t}{\cal L}\Phi\left(S(u),I(u),{\mathfrak{V}}(u),\alpha(u)\right)du\\
&\!\!\!\displaystyle\leq\Phi\left(s_{0},i_{0}\right)+K\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\Phi\left(S(u\wedge\tau_{k}),I(u\wedge\tau_{k})\right)du.\end{array}

The Gronwall inequality yields that

\mathbb{E}\Phi\left(S(s\wedge\tau_{k}),I(\tau_{k}\wedge T)\right)\leq\Phi(s_{0},i_{0})e^{KT}.

(A.6)

Note that for each
