Diffusion, phase behavior and gelation in a two-dimensional layer of colloids in osmotic equilibrium with a polymer reservoir Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Sam E. Griffiths, Nick Koumakis, Aidan T. Brown, Teun Vissers, Patrick B. Warren, Wilson C. K. Poon

Abstract
The addition of enough non-adsorbing polymer to an otherwise stable colloidal suspension gives rise to a variety of phase behavior and kinetic arrest due to the depletion attraction induced between the colloids by the polymers. We report a study of these phenomena in a two-dimensional layer of colloids. The three-dimensional phenomenology of crystal-fluid coexistence is reproduced, but gelation takes a novel form, in which the strands in the gel structure are locally crystalline. We compare our findings with a previous simulation and theory, and find substantial agreement.

What we have summarised so far pertains to bulk colloid-polymer mixtures. At first sight, there is little incentive to study two-dimensional (2D) systems: presumably, any difference to bulk behavior would be merely quantitative. However, this intuition turns out to be incorrect.

An early 2D study extended the ‘primitive’ theory for bulk phase behavior

III
Theory

Previously, a mean-field van der Waals free-volume theory was used to predict the wall freezing transition in a grand canonical system.
Dinsmore et al. 1997
In our semi-grand canonical ensemble, Fig.
1
(b), the approach simplifies considerably, as there is no need to equilibrate colloids in the wall layer and the bulk. The semi-grand canonical free energy
F
with ideal depletants is

F=F_{0}-\rho_{\mathrm{d}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\langle{V_{\text{free}}}\rangle_{0},

(2)

where
F_{0}
is the free energy of a reference system of 2D-confined hard spheres, and
\langle V_{\text{free}}\rangle
is the volume available to depletants of bulk number density
\rho_{\mathrm{d}}
averaged over the positions of the colloids in the reference system. The depletants are mutually non-interacting (ideal), and exert an osmotic pressure
\rho_{\mathrm{d}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T
in the bulk, which acts as a reservoir. They cannot approach
\leq r_{\mathrm{d}}
to the surface of a colloid.

For
F_{0}
we use published free energies of the hard disc fluid and hexagonally-ordered crystal
Zeng and Oxtoby 1990
that account well for two-dimensional freezing. For the former (
cf.
Eq. (18) in Ref.
Zeng and Oxtoby 1990
):

\frac{F_{0}^{\mathrm{(fluid)}}}{N_{\mathrm{c}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}=\ln\left[\frac{4\eta_{\mathrm{c}}}{\pi}\right]-1-\ln(1-\eta_{\mathrm{c}})+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{c}}}{1-\eta_{\mathrm{c}}},

(3)

where
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}
is the area fraction of colloids. After the first two ideal terms, the next two terms give the excess free energy per particle after Rosenfeld.
Rosenfeld 1990
For the 2D crystal, we follow Hall’s procedure for 3D hard-sphere crystals
Hall 1972
and fitted

\frac{F_{0}^{\mathrm{(crystal)}}}{N_{\mathrm{c}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}=\ln\left[\frac{4\eta_{\mathrm{c}}}{\pi}\right]-1+c_{0}+c_{1}\beta_{\mathrm{c}}-2\ln\beta_{\mathrm{c}}\,

(4)

to the 2D crystal curve in Fig. 2 of Ref.
Zeng and Oxtoby 1990
Here,
\beta_{\mathrm{c}}=4(1-\eta_{\mathrm{c}}/\eta_{\mathrm{max}})
where
\eta_{\mathrm{max}}=\pi\sqrt{3}/6\approx 0.907
is hexagonal close-packing. Least-squares fitting gives
c_{0}\approx 3.08
and
c_{1}\approx 0.30

3

For the free volume we use the standard expansion,

V_{\text{free}}=\mathrm{const}-N_{\mathrm{c}}V_{1}+{\textstyle{\displaystyle\sum}_{\>i>j}}\>V_{2}(r_{ij})+\dots

(5)

is the excluded volume for an isolated colloidal particle allowing for the overlap with the depletion layer at the wall,

V.2
Gelation

Strikingly, our ramified clusters are locally crystalline, Fig.
4

Griffiths, Turci, and Royall 2017
The absence of such structures in 2D is the origin of locally-crystalline ramified clusters in our system; it is presumably also related to why crystallization is ‘easier’ in 2D.

In 3D, gelation in colloid-polymer mixtures is triggered by a coarsening spinodal gas-liquid phase separation texture that kinetically arrests. In our system, the theoretical fluid spinodal (dashed line in Fig.

VI
Summary and concluding remarks

We have studied experimentally a layer of colloids at the bottom of a glass capillary in the presence of smaller polymers. The combination of gravitational sedimentation and the depletion attraction induced between the spheres and the wall tightly confined the spheres to a 2D layer, in which we have been able to deduce the sphere-wall gap from fitting measured diffusivities to a hydrodynamic theory.
Faxén 1921
;
HB73
;
Chio and Tse 2020

The depletion attraction between the spheres induced by the polymers drives them into F-X coexistence at colloid concentrations
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}
very much lower than that needed for 2D crystallization (
\eta_{\mathrm{c}}=0.67
) in the absence of polymers. A free-volume theory
Poon and Warren 1994
adapted to our semi-grand canonical system gives a good account of the F-X coexistence boundary.

At high polymer concentration, the depletion attraction drives the formation of ramified clusters that are locally crystalline, confirming a previous simulation.
Cerdà et al. 2004
However, the cluster size distribution and cluster growth dynamics show quantitative differences with these simulations, which we speculate as due to particle adhesion and/or the screening hydrodynamic interactions by the wall. Future simulations may explore the validity of these proposals.

The 500-word 1954 letter by Asakura and Oosawa published in this journal
Cipelletti, Martens, and Ramos 2020
together with Vrij’s later detailed treatment based on the AO picture
Vrij 1976
marked the start of modern research into depletion-driven phenomena. A first theoretical account of the phase behavior in colloid-polymer mixtures integrates out the polymeric degrees of freedom and uses an AO potential for the inter-particle interaction.
Gast, Hall, and Russel 1983
Later, a ‘primitive model’ that takes explicit account of the polymers’ centre-of-mass degrees of freedom successively predicts polymer partitioning in coexisting phases.
Lekkerkerker et al. 1992
Its applicability was confirmed by bulk experiments.
Ilett et al. 1995
Interestingly, this primitive model uses a semi-grand canonical ensemble
Lekkerkerker 1990
as a calculational device.

It is gratifying that seven decades from ‘AO’ and three decades from the semi-grand canonical model, we are able to perform experiments in a well-characterized ensemble of this kind, fit our dynamical (diffusion) data by appealing to an AO form of the interaction between particles and wall, Fig.
3
, and account for the equilibrium phase behavior using a modified version of the original primitive model, Fig.
4
(a).

Appendix A
Approximate estimate of fluid binodal

In the presence of non-adsorbing polymers the cohesive free energy of the hexagonally-ordered crystal can be approximated by calculating the energy required to break the AO ‘bonds’, as in a solid-state physics problem.
Kittel 2004
By matching this to the colloid chemical potential in the fluid phase, one can estimate of the location of the fluid binodal. Let the crystal co-ordination number be
z
. Then

F^{\mathrm{(crystal)}}\approx F_{0}^{\mathrm{(crystal)}}-\frac{zN_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\times\rho_{\mathrm{p}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,V_{2}(2R)

(23)

where the second factor in the second term is the AO bond free energy, and for simplicity we use the contact value of
V_{2}(r)
. Equating the resulting colloid chemical potential to that in the two-dimensional fluid derived from Eq. (
3
) gives

\ln\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{c}}}{1-\eta_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{c}}(3-2\eta_{\mathrm{c}})}{(1-\eta_{\mathrm{c}})^{2}}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}-\frac{z}{2}\times\rho_{\mathrm{p}}V_{2}(2R)

(24)

where
\mu_{0}
is the chemical potential in the unperturbed crystal, which in the spirit of the approach we shall suppose constant.

For simplicity we neglect the excluded volume overlaps in the fluid (one can show that they are small) and tacitly omit the
V_{1}
terms which contribute only a common constant to the chemical potentials. Since at coexistence the chemical potentials of the fluid and crystal are the same,
\mu_{0}
can be obtained from the known fluid coexistence composition in the absence of added polymer,
viz.
Eq. (
24

\eta_{\mathrm{c}}\approx 0.670

\rho_{\mathrm{p}}=0

24

\eta_{\mathrm{c}}

Appendix B
Hydrodynamic interactions

We present heuristic arguments that in a wall-bound cluster containing
N
particles, the wall ‘screens’ the hydrodynamic interactions such that the cluster mobility
\sim 1/N
, at least in the scaling limit. We start with the familiar result that a point force
{\mathbf{f}}
in an unbounded fluid generates a velocity field
{\mathbf{v}}
at a distance
{\mathbf{r}}
with (Oseen tensor)
Doi86

{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{1}{8\pi\mu r}({\mathbf{I}}+\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}\,\hat{{\mathbf{r}}})\cdot{\mathbf{f}}\,.

(25)

In this
{\mathbf{I}}
is the unit tensor,
\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}={\mathbf{r}}/r
with
r=|{\mathbf{r}}|
, and
\mu
is the fluid viscosity. Similarly, Blake and Chwang showed that a point force at a height
h
above the wall generates a flow field which behaves to leading-order in far-field as
Blake74

{\mathbf{v}}=\frac{12hz}{8\pi\mu r^{3}}\>\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}\,\hat{{\mathbf{r}}}\cdot{\mathbf{f}}

(26)

Here, the no-slip boundary condition is coincident with the
z=0
plane, and
{\mathbf{r}}=(x,y)
is now the in-plane distance between the point
(x,y,z)
where the velocity is measured and the point
(0,0,h)
where the force is applied. Crucially, according to Eq. (
26
), for
z\sim h
the far-field decays as
1/r^{3}
rather than
1/r
as in Eq. (
25
) for an unbounded fluid.
not

At this point we recall that the mobility of a fractal cluster is essentially determined by the behavior of
\langle{1/r_{ij}^{\>n}}\rangle
where the average is taken over all pairs of particles in the cluster,
Wiltzius1987
and according to the above we should take
n=1
for freely-suspended clusters or
n=3
for wall-bound clusters. In terms of the pair distribution function
g(r)\sim r^{\,d_{\mathrm{f}}-d}
,

\langle{1/r_{ij}^{\>n}}\rangle\sim\frac{{\textstyle\int}\,r^{-n}\,g(r)\,r^{\,d-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}r}{{\textstyle\int}\,g(r)\,r^{\,d-1}\,{\mathrm{d}}r}\sim R^{-n}

(27)

where
R\sim N^{1/d_{\mathrm{f}}}
is the cut-off in
g(r)
. But this
only
holds when the integral in the numerator is dominated by this cut-off, which requires
d_{\mathrm{f}}>n
. Plainly this is the case for freely-suspended clusters (unless they happen to be fractal dust with
d_{\mathrm{f}}<1
), and so one expects that the cluster mobility
\sim N^{-1/d_{\mathrm{f}}}
. This scaling behavior has been widely confirmed,
VanSaarloos1987
;
Warren1994
;
Sorensen2011
and corresponds to the fact that the flow field is screened from the interior of the cluster. For wall-bound clusters though, it is
not
the case that the integral in the numerator in Eq. (
27
) is dominated by the upper limit since that would require
d_{\mathrm{f}}>3
which is impossible in
d=2
dimensions. Hence one concludes (perhaps a little tentatively!) that hydrodynamic interactions should be negligible in far-field for wall-bound clusters, or in other words the flow field, already screened by the wall, is not further significantly reduced in the interior of the cluster. It follows that the frictional drag should be extensive in the number of particles, and the cluster mobility should scale as
\sim 1/N
as claimed.

This conclusion obviously demands numerical verification, using Stokesian dynamics or similar methods.
Cichocki2000
;
Swan2007
;
Gauger2009
For now though, we close with a couple of remarks higlighting the subtleties of this hydrodynamic problem. First, it is interesting to note that the mobilities of individual particles are in a sense
decoupled
from the hydrodynamic interactions, since the former are sensitive to the gap between the particles and the wall (see section
IV.1
), which can be made arbitrarily small, whereas the latter are essentially controlled by the heights of the particle centres above the wall which are limited by the particle radii. Second, whilst the presence of the wall couples the rotational and translational modes, in a cluster the rotational modes are partly supressed if the particles are mutually hindered from all rolling in the same direction (essentially as a non-trivial consequence of the near-field hydrodynamics). So, it seems doubtful that the individual particle friction coefficients are simply additive, but this may not necessarily change the extensivity of the overall cluster drag coefficient.
