Small area estimation using multiple imputation in three-parameter logistic models
Cristian Tellez-Piñerez, Universidad de Santo Tomás, Colombia

Abstract
We propose a novel methodology relating item response theory methods with small area estimation strategies in the presence of missing data. Specifically, we propose an unbiased estimator for the average ability parameter of three-parameter logistic models. Thus, we carry out an extensive simulation study in order to compare our estimator with the well-known Horvitz-Thompson estimator. According to our experiments with synthetic data, our proposal has substantial lower standard errors than its competitor. In addition, we perform an actual application by considering the Mathematics results of the 2015 Program for International Student Assessment (PISA), and also, compare our results with previous analyses. Our findings strongly suggest that our methodology is a high competitive alternative for generating compelling official statistics.



Keywords:
Small Area Estimation, Item Response Theory, Missing data, PISA.

Introduction

Educational assessment can be understood as the process of using collected information about attitudes, beliefs, knowledge, and skills to improve learning in academic programs
(
Allen, 2004
)
.
These data are typically obtained from standardized tests applied to students for assessing planned learning goals (
Kuh et al., 2014
,
ICFES, 2015
,
OECD, 2016
,
UNESCO, 2019
).
However, standardized tests for educational assessment are currently facing a serious issue: A decreasing number of participants per application due to lack of access and financial restrictions.

A common problem is the presence of missing data in response strings.
For example, in the Colombian institute for educational assessment, previous test applications as well as findings included in technical manuals (e.g.,
ICFES, 2014
), show that students would take more than fifteen hours to go through all the questions in the entire test.
Exposing each student to a fifteen-hours test is not only a counterproductive strategy for the final scores, but also promotes cognitive conflicts from a pedagogical point of view
(
Álvarez et al., 2007
)
.
This is also the case with other tests, including Program for International Student Assessment (PISA,
OECD, 2014
), Trends in the International Study of Mathematics and Sciences (TIMMS,
Mullis et al., 2015
), and Saber 3

(

, among many others.

Estimation and analysis of standardized tests are mostly carried out employing Item Response Theory (IRT) methods (e.g.,
Lord, 1980
,
Martínez Arias, 1995
,
Muñiz, 1997
,
Hambleton and Swaminathan, 2013
).
IRT models intend to explain the relationship between latent traits, i.e., unobservable characteristics or attributes, and their manifestations, i.e., observed outcomes, responses or performance
(
Embretson and Reise, 2013
)
.
In particular, three-parameters logistic models (3PLMs, e.g.,
Paek and Cole, 2019

According to
Sulis and Porcu, 2017

To the best of our knowledge, there is a substantial lack of research about non-response methods in the context of standardized tests (e.g.,
Adams and Darwin, 1982
,
Baker and Kim, 2004
,
Sulis and Porcu, 2017
).
Therefore, our foundational aim relies on extending IRT methodologies accounting for missing data, while achieving similar quality indicators at lower costs.

In contrast with standard methods, our proposal incorporates estimates of the ability parameter using the Fay-Herriot approach
(

)
within the framework of IRT modeling where multiple imputation tasks are needed.
Such a methodology has in itself two major contributions. On the one hand, it has profound practical implications to deal properly with problematic data structures involving missing data, and on the other, it resolves challenging theoretical issues associated with the production of reliable official statistics.
We highlight that this is a complex task demanding auxiliary variables correlated with the ability of the students (such as parental socio-economic level, parental education, school infrastructure, among others, according to
Treviño et al., 2010
), as well as sophisticated statistical tools for computing the resulting estimator together with its Mean Square Error (MSE).

2
Theoretical development of the estimator

In this section, we present our approach for estimating the average in small areas using multiple imputation in three-parameter logistic models (3PLMs, e.g.,
Paek and Cole, 2019
).
Firstly, we review the estimation of plausible values. Secondly, we use small area estimation theory as well as the Fay-Herriot model in order to obtain expressions of the ability mean estimator along with its Mean Square Error (MSE).

2.1
Ability estimation using plausible values and 3PLMs

We have a finite universe
U=\bigcup_{d=1}^{D}U_{d}
of size
N=\sum_{d=1}^{D}N_{d}
distributed in
d
domains, where
U_{d}
is the population in domain
d
of size
N_{d}
.
Also, let
s=\bigcup_{d=1}^{D}s_{d}
be the sample of size
n=\sum_{d=1}^{D}n_{d}
under consideration, where
s_{d}
is the sample in domain
d
of size
n_{d}
drawn under a particular sampling design
p(\cdot)
.
It may happen that for some domain
d
, either
s_{d}
turns out to be empty or
n_{d}
is not large enough in order to enable reliable estimations.
Moreover, indicator variables
\xi_{ij}
registering whether individual
j
answers item
i
correctly,
\xi_{ij}=1
, or not,
\xi_{ij}=0
, are observed, for
j=1,\ldots,n_{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,I
.

IRT modeling assumes that individuals are endowed with an ability to answer items correctly. Let
\bm{\theta}
be an array containing the ability parameters of all the individuals under consideration.
In order to find the distribution of
\bm{\theta}
, there must be available known individual-level auxiliary information, typically stored in a vector of variables
\bm{x}_{j}^{\textsf{I}}
for each
j=1,\ldots,n_{d}
(the sub-index
I
emphasizes the notion of auxiliary information at the level of individuals or subjects).
Thus, the probability distribution of
\bm{\theta}
in the population is not only conditional on the observed indicator variables
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}
, but also on the auxiliary information
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}
, i.e.,

\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}},\,\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}\right)\propto\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\mid\bm{\theta},\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}\right)\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}\right)\,,

where
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}
is a matrix storing all the individual-level auxiliary information.
Assuming conditional independence between
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}
as in
Rubin and Schenker, 1991
, which is quite reasonable in practice, it is easy to see that

\textsf{p}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}},\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}})\propto\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\mid\bm{\theta}\right)\,\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}\right)\,.

Under this setting, the main goal relies on finding the conditional distribution of
\bm{\theta}
given
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}
and
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}
, which in turn
depends on two conditional distributions.
Firstly,
\textsf{P}(\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\mid\bm{\theta})
,
the response chain distribution of students given their ability, considered here as a standard 3PLM
(
Bock and Aitkin, 1981
)
.
Secondly,
\textsf{P}(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}})
, the ability distribution of students given the auxiliary information, regarded here as a multivariate Normal distribution with mean
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}}\bm{\Gamma}
and covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, where both
\bm{\Gamma}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
need to be estimated. On this point, we are implicitly stating that the parameter space of each component of
\bm{\theta}
is the real line, even though most of the ability mass lies between
-3
and
3
(e.g.,
de Andrade et al., 2000
).

It is straightforward to see that the conditional distribution
\textsf{p}(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}},\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}})
is completely determined by handling the unknown parameters in
\textsf{p}(\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\mid\bm{\theta})
as well as those in
\textsf{p}(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}})
.
Existing IRT methods typically deal with this setting by,
first, using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm (e.g.,
Bock and Aitkin, 1981
) to estimate the unknown parameters in
\textsf{P}(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{I}})
, and
then, using the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm (e.g.,
Fox, 2010
) to draw
L
plausible values (i.e., abilities estimates) for each individual
j
in domain
d
.
In this spirit, let
\theta_{dj\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}
be the
\ell
-th MH plausible value (hence the upper-index
pv
) of individual
j
in domain
d
, for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
, with
j=1,\ldots,J
and
d=1,\ldots,D
.
Also, let
\gamma_{d}=g(\theta_{dj\ell}^{\textsf{pv}})
be an arbitrary function of
\theta_{dj\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}
characterizing a specific feature in the
d
-th domain (e.g., the mean). Thus, an estimate of
\gamma_{d}
and its corresponding variance can be found by noticing that

\textsf{p}(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}})=\int\textsf{p}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}},\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)\,\textsf{p}\left(\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)\,\textsf{d}\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}}\,,

where
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}}
is composed of all those unobserved indicators variables not given in
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}
, and the integral is carried out over the space parameter of
\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}}
. As a consequence, for the average of the individuals’ abilities
\gamma_{d}
, taking
g(\cdot)
as the mean of the
\theta_{dj\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left[\textsf{E}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}},\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right]\simeq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\hat{\theta}_{d\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}=\hat{\gamma}_{d}

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left[\textsf{Var}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}},\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right]+\textsf{Var}\left[\textsf{E}\left(\gamma_{d}\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{nobs}},\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right)\mid\bm{\xi}_{\textsf{obs}}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\textsf{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{d\ell}^{\textsf{pv}})+\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)\frac{1}{L-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(\hat{\theta}_{d\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}-\hat{\gamma}_{d})^{2}\,,

where

\hat{\theta}_{d\ell}^{\textsf{pv}}
is the
\ell
-th plausible value in domain
d
, and

\textsf{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{d\ell}^{\textsf{pv}})
depends on the specific formulation of the sampling design and corresponds to the average of the estimated variances for each plausible value.

2.2
Proposed estimator

Here, we take a step further and go beyond the exiting IRT literature as presented above in order to carry out our main task:
Combining IRT methods with small area estimation strategies accounting for missing data.
Thus, inspired in Small Area Estimation (SAE) methods, we propose estimating
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{D})
, as a result of the model

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{Z}\bm{u}+\bm{e}\,,

(2)

where

\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}
is a fixed-effects matrix storing all the area-level auxiliary information (the sub-index
A
emphasizes the notion of auxiliary information at the level of areas or domains),

\bm{\beta}
is a vector of unknown constants,

\mathbf{Z}
is a random-effects design matrix,
and finally,
\bm{u}
and
\bm{e}
are independent random vectors such that
\bm{u}\sim\textsf{N}\left(\bm{0},\,\mathbf{V}_{u}\right)
and
\bm{e}\sim\textsf{N}\left(\bm{0},\,\mathbf{V}_{e}\right)
, with known
\mathbf{V}_{u}
and
\mathbf{V}_{e}
.
Thus, under the previous specification it follows directly that
\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right)=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{Z}^{\textsf{T}}+\mathbf{V}_{e}=\mathbf{V}
,
and also, following standard results about linear mixed-effects models (e.g.,
McCulloch and Searle, 2004
), it can be shown that the optimal unbiased linear predictor of
\bm{\tau}=\mathbf{L}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{M}\bm{u}
is
\hat{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{L}\hat{\bm{\beta}}+\mathbf{M}\hat{\bm{u}},
with

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\hat{\bm{u}}=\left(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{V}_{u}\right)^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right)\,.

Now, building on ideas given in
Harville, 1977
and
Morales and Molina, 2015
, we consider an unbiased linear predictor of the form
\hat{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{Q}_{0}+\mathbf{Q}_{1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
, where
\mathbf{Q}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
are two conformable matrices.
Since
\hat{\bm{\tau}}
is unbiased, we have that
\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}\right)=\mathbf{0}
, but

\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\bm{\tau}}\right)=\mathbf{Q}_{0}\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right)+\mathbf{Q}_{1}=\mathbf{Q}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{Q}_{1}
and
\textsf{E}\left(\bm{\tau}\right)=\mathbf{L}\bm{\beta}
. Then,

\mathbf{0}=\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}\right)=\mathbf{Q}_{0}\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right)+\mathbf{Q}_{1}-\mathbf{L}\bm{\beta}=\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}-\mathbf{L}\right)\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{Q}_{0}\,,

which requires that both
\mathbf{Q}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}=\mathbf{L}
hold for the previous chain of equalities to be satisfy.

Thus, the best predictor
\hat{\tau}
can be found by minimizing
\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}\right)
subject to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}=\mathbf{L}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\bm{\tau}}-\bm{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{L}\bm{\beta}-\mathbf{M}\bm{u}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\right)+\textsf{Var}\left(\mathbf{M}\bm{u}\right)-2\textsf{Cov}\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}},\mathbf{M}\bm{u}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}-2\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\,,

with
\mathbf{C}=\textsf{Cov}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}},\,\bm{u}\right)
.
Since
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}
does not
depend on
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
, the minimization problem given above can be restated as
minimize
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}-2\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}
subject to
\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}=\mathbf{L}
,
whose corresponding Lagrangian function is

\ell\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\,\bm{\Lambda}\right)=\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}-2\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}+2\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}-\mathbf{L}\right)\bm{\Lambda}\,.

Taking partial derivatives with respect to both
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
, we get that

\frac{\partial\ell\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\,\bm{\Lambda}\right)}{\partial\mathbf{Q}_{1}}=2\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}-2\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}+2\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\bm{\Lambda}=\mathbf{0}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\mathbf{V}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}+\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\bm{\Lambda}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\,,

and

\frac{\partial\ell\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1},\,\bm{\Lambda}\right)}{\partial\text{$\Lambda$}}=2\left(\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}-\mathbf{L}\right)=\mathbf{0}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}=\mathbf{L}^{\textsf{T}}\,,

that in turn can be rewritten in matrix form as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{V}&\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\\
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}&\bm{0}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\bm{\Lambda}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{L}^{\textsf{T}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\bm{\text{$\Lambda$}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{V}&\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\\
\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}&\bm{0}\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{L}^{\textsf{T}}\end{array}\right)\,.

In this way,
using standard results from matrix algebra and letting
\mathbf{G}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\right)^{-1}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\bm{\text{$\Lambda$}}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{V}^{-1}-\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}&\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\\
\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}&-\mathbf{G}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\text{$\mathbf{L}^{\textsf{T}}$}\end{array}\right)\,,

which means that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{L}^{\textsf{T}}+\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{C}\mathbf{M}^{\textsf{T}}\right]^{\textsf{T}}

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right)\,,

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix.

Recall from our earlier discussion that the linear predictor takes the form
\hat{\bm{\tau}}=\mathbf{Q}_{1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
because it is assumed to be unbiased from the beginning. Thus, substituting
\mathbf{Q}_{1}
by the expression found in Eq. (
7
), we get that the Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (BLUP) of
\bm{\tau}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathbf{L}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\right)\right]\hat{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}+\mathbf{M}\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\hat{\bm{\beta}}+\mathbf{M}\hat{\bm{u}}\,,

where
\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathbf{G}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}
and
\hat{\bm{u}}=\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\right)
.

Now, our goal is to see through the previous expression in order to adapt the Fay-Herriot model,

\hat{\gamma}_{d}=\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\bm{\beta}+u_{d}+e_{d}\,,\qquad d=1,\ldots,D\,,

(8)

where
\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\bm{\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}x_{\textsf{A}\,dk}\,\beta_{k}
is a linear predictor of fixed effects and
u_{d}
is a domain-specific random effect. As usual, all the random effects are assumed to be independent and identically distributed with zero mean and variance
\sigma_{u}^{2}
.
Furthermore,
e_{d}
is the sampling error associated with the sampling design
p(\cdot)
, which is also assumed to be independent of
u_{d}
, and also, such that
\textsf{E}_{p}[e_{d}\mid\hat{\gamma}_{d}]=0
and
\textsf{Var}_{p}[e_{d}\mid\hat{\gamma}_{d}]=\sigma_{d}^{2}
. As a final remark, each
\sigma_{d}^{2}
can be calculated in a straightforward fashion using Eq. (
1
).

Using the results provided in this section and rewriting model (
8
) in matrix form as in Eq. (
2
), it follows that

\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\bm{\beta}+\mathbf{Z}\bm{u}+\bm{e}

is equivalent to

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\gamma}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{\gamma}_{D}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x_{\textsf{A}\,11}&\cdots&x_{\textsf{A}\,1p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{\textsf{A}\,D1}&\cdots&x_{\textsf{A}\,Dp}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\beta_{p}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{D}\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e_{1}\\
\vdots\\
e_{d}\end{array}\right)\,,

which means that

\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{I}_{D}
and

\mathbf{V}=\textsf{diag}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{D}^{2}\right)
.
As a consequence, we have that the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) of
\bm{\beta}=\left(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p}\right)
and
\bm{u}=\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{D}\right)
are respectively

\tilde{\bm{\beta}}=\left(\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\bm{u}}=\mathbf{C}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{-1}\left(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}-\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right)\,,

(9)

with
\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\textsf{diag}\left(1/\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\right),\ldots,1/\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{D}^{2}\right)\right)
and
\hat{\bm{\gamma}}=(\hat{\gamma}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\gamma}_{D})
.

Therefore, the BLUP proposed in this article is

\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{B}}=\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\hat{\gamma}_{d}+\frac{\sigma_{d}^{2}}{\sigma_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\,.

(10)

Also, the Empirical Best Linear Unbiased Predictor (EBLUP) of the mean of the small area
\gamma_{d}
under model (
8
) can be obtained just by replacing
\sigma_{u}^{2}
by its corresponding estimate
\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}
in Eq. (
10
), i.e.,

\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\hat{\gamma}_{d}+\frac{\sigma_{d}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}=\left(1-B_{d}\right)\hat{\gamma}_{d}+B_{d}\,\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\,,

(11)

with
B_{d}=\sigma_{d}^{2}/(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2})
.

The proposed estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}
is unbiased for
\gamma_{d}
, for
d=1,\ldots,D
.
Indeed, consider the difference

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}-\gamma_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-B_{d}\right)\hat{\gamma}_{d}+B_{d}\,\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\left[B_{d}\,\gamma_{d}+\left(1-B_{d}\right)\gamma_{d}\right].

Now, by letting
\alpha_{d}=1-B_{d}
,
\gamma_{d}=\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\bm{\beta}+u_{d}
, and
\hat{\gamma}_{d}=\gamma_{d}+e_{d}
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}-\gamma_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{d}\left(\gamma_{d}+e_{d}\right)+\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\alpha_{d}\gamma_{d}-\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\gamma_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{d}e_{d}+\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\left[\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,\bm{\beta}+u_{d}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{d}e_{d}+\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\left[\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta})\right]-\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)u_{d}\,.

Then, taking expected values,

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}-\gamma_{d}\right)=\textsf{E}\left(\alpha_{d}e_{d}+\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)\left[\bm{x}_{\textsf{A}\,d}^{\textsf{T}}\,(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta})\right]-\left(1-\alpha_{d}\right)u_{d}\right)=0\,,

because
\textsf{E}\left(e_{d}\right)=\textsf{E}\left(u_{d}\right)=0
, and also,
\textsf{E}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta})=\bm{0}

since
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
is an unbiased estimator for
\bm{\beta}
.

2.3
Mean square error of the proposed estimator

Here, we follow very closely
Kackar and Harville, 1984
,
Prasad and Rao, 1990
as well as
Ghosh and Rao, 1994
, in order to obtain an expression for the Mean Square Error (MSE) of the proposed estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}
.
Specifically, we consider the variance component estimation method, which require us to find three quantities explicitly, namely,
g_{1d}(\sigma_{u}^{2})
,
g_{2d}(\sigma_{u}^{2})
, and
g_{3d}(\sigma_{u}^{2})
, for
d=1,\ldots,D
.
We refer the reader to the previous references for details about such a method. However, we outline below some fundamental details.

First, in order to calculate
g_{1d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)
, we need to tak into account that

\mathbf{V}_{u}=\sigma_{u}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{D}

and

\mathbf{V}_{e}=\sigma_{d}^{2}\mathbf{W}_{N}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{s}^{-1}=\textsf{diag}\left(\mathbf{V}_{s_{1}}^{-1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{s_{D}}^{-1}\right)
,
where
\mathbf{W}_{N}
is a
N\times N
diagonal matrix of weights induced by the sampling design
p(\cdot)
, with
N
the population size,
s=\cup_{d=1}^{D}s_{d}
is the full sample under consideration, with
s_{d}
the sample in domain
d
, and

\mathbf{V}_{s_{d}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{d}^{2}}\left(\mathbf{W}_{s_{d}}-\frac{B_{d}}{w_{d}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{d}}\bm{w}_{n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,,

where
w_{d}
is the weight of domain
d
, for
d=1,\ldots,D
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{u}\mathbf{Z}_{s}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}_{e\,s}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{s}\mathbf{V}_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{u}^{4}}{\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n_{1}}-\frac{B_{1}}{n_{1}}\bm{w}_{n_{1}}\bm{1}_{w_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{W}_{n_{D}}-\frac{B_{D}}{n_{D}}\bm{w}_{n_{D}}\bm{1}_{D}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\textsf{diag}\left(\frac{B_{1}}{w_{1}}\bm{w}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\frac{B_{D}}{w_{D}}\bm{w}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\textsf{diag}\left(B_{1},\ldots,B_{D}\right)\,,

and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{s}=\mathbf{V}_{u}(\mathbf{I}_{D}-\mathbf{Z}_{s}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}_{e\,s}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{s}\mathbf{V}_{u})=\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\textsf{diag}\left(1-B_{1},\ldots,1-B_{D}\right)\,,

with
\mathbf{Z}_{s}=\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}\right)
and

\mathbf{V}_{e\,s}=\textsf{diag}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n_{1}}-\frac{B_{1}}{n_{1}}\bm{w}_{n_{1}}\bm{1}_{w_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{W}_{n_{D}}-\frac{B_{D}}{n_{D}}\bm{w}_{n_{D}}\bm{1}_{D}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,.

The previous result is quite useful because we want to estimate the average of the plausible values for all the individuals within domain
d
, i.e.,
\eta=\bm{a}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\gamma}_{\textsf{U}}
, with

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\left(\bm{0}_{N_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}_{N_{d-1}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}_{N_{d+1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}_{N_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,,

with
\bm{\gamma}_{\textsf{U}}
is the population parameter, leading us directly to consider all those individuals included the sample as well as those that did not, i.e,
s
and
r
, respectively. As a consequence,

\displaystyle g_{1d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{r}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{r}\mathbf{T}_{s}\mathbf{Z}_{r}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{a}_{r}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{N_{d}^{2}}\,\textsf{diag}\left(1-B_{1},\ldots,1-B_{D}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{N_{d}^{2}}\left(1-B_{d}\right)\left(N_{d}-n_{d}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}^{2}\left(1-B_{d}\right)\,,

for
n_{d}<<N_{d}
.

Now, in order to compute
g_{2d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)
, we need to get

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{r}\mathbf{T}_{s}\mathbf{Z}_{s}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\textsf{diag}\left(1-B_{1},\ldots,1-B_{D}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}^{2}\,\textsf{diag}\left(\left(1-B_{1}\right)\,1_{N_{1}-n_{1}}\bm{1}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\left(1-B_{D}\right)\,\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}\bm{1}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,,

which means that,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{r}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{Z}_{r}\mathbf{T}_{s}\mathbf{Z}_{s}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}_{e\,s}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{d}}\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{\sigma_{d}^{2}}\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\left(1-B_{1}\right)\,\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}}\bm{1}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\left(1-B_{D}\right)\,\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}\bm{1}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\mathbf{W}_{s}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{d}}\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{\sigma_{d}^{2}}\left(1-B_{d}\right)\left(N_{d}-n_{d}\right)\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{w}_{n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-f_{d}\right)B_{d}\,\hat{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{d}\,,

with
f_{d}=n_{d}/N_{d}
is the sampling fraction and
\hat{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{d}=\frac{1}{\sum w_{d}}\sum_{k\in s_{d}}x_{dk}\bm{w}_{dk}
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{r}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,r}=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,r}=\left(1-f_{d}\right)\bar{\bm{x}}_{d}\,,

(14)

with
\bar{\bm{x}}_{d}=\frac{1}{N_{d}}\sum_{k\in d}x_{dk}
.
Using together Eqs. (
13
) and (
14
), along with
n_{d}<<N_{d}
,
we finally get that

\displaystyle g_{2d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bar{\bm{x}}_{d}-B_{d}\,\hat{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{d}\right)\left(\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,s}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{V}_{s}^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{\textsf{A}\,s}\right)^{-1}\left(\bar{\bm{x}}_{d}-B_{d}\,\hat{\bar{\bm{x}}}_{d}\right)^{\textsf{T}}\,.

(15)

Lastly, in order to
g_{3d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)
, we need to get

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{d}}\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{\sigma_{d}^{2}}\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}\right)\textsf{diag}\left(\bm{1}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{1}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\mathbf{W}_{n_{1}}-\frac{B_{1}}{w_{1}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{1}}\bm{w}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{W}_{n_{D}}-\frac{B_{D}}{w_{D}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{D}}\bm{w}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{d}}\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{1}_{N_{d}-n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\textsf{diag}\left(\frac{B_{1}}{w_{1}}\bm{1}_{N_{1}-n_{1}}\bm{w}_{n_{1}}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\frac{B_{D}}{w_{D}}\bm{1}_{N_{D}-n_{D}}\bm{w}_{n_{D}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\frac{B_{d}}{w_{d}}\frac{N_{d}-n_{d}}{N_{d}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\nabla\bm{b}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\left(1-f_{d}\right)\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{d}^{2}}\frac{1}{w_{d}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\\
\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\left(1-f_{d}\right)\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{u}^{2}}\frac{1}{w_{d}}\,\bm{w}_{n_{d}}^{\textsf{T}},\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}},\ldots,\bm{0}^{\textsf{T}}\end{array}\right)

and therefore,

g_{3d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)=\left(1-f_{d}\right)^{2}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{d}^{2}}{w_{d}}\right)\cdot\\
\textsf{tr}\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)^{2}&\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{u}^{2}}\\
\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{d}^{2}}\,\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{u}^{2}}&\left(\frac{\partial B_{d}}{\partial\sigma_{u}^{2}}\right)^{2}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{d}^{2}\right)&\textsf{Cov}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{d}^{2},\,\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)\\
\textsf{Cov}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{d}^{2},\,\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)&\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)\end{array}\right)\right\}

which means that,

g_{3d}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\right)=\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{d}^{2}}{w_{d}}\right)^{-3}\,\frac{1}{w_{d}^{2}}\,\textsf{Var}\left(\sigma_{u}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{d}^{2}-\sigma_{d}^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)

(17)

for
n_{d}<<N_{d}
.

The estimation of the MSE for the proposed estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}
can be now obtained taking into account our findings given in Eqs. (
12
), (
15
), and (
17
). Specifically, considering
\sigma_{u}^{2}
instead of
\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}
, it follows that

g_{1d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\sigma_{d}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}=\sigma_{d}^{2}\,\left(1-B_{d}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{2d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{d}^{2}}{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)^{2}a=B_{d}^{2}\,a\,,

where
B_{d}=\sigma_{d}^{2}/(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2})
and
a=\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}
, with
\mathbf{F}=(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2})^{-1}\,\sum_{d=1}^{D}\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}\,\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}
.
The component
g_{3d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)
depends on the estimation method of the variance components. Either way,
g_{3d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)
takes the form

g_{3d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=\frac{\sigma_{d}^{4}}{\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{3}}\,\textsf{var}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)\,,

where

\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=\frac{2}{D}\left[\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{4}+\frac{2\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sigma_{d}^{2}+\frac{1}{D}\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sigma_{d}^{4}\right]\quad\text{or}\quad\textsf{Var}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=2\left[\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right]^{-1}\,,

using the Prasad-Rao moment estimator
(
Prasad and Rao, 1990
)
, or
either the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimator or the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator, respectively. If the latter,
g_{3d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)
simplifies to

g_{3d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}}\right)\,\frac{2\,B_{d}^{2}}{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{2}}\,.

Finally, on the one hand, if either Prasad-Rao or REML are used, then the MSE estimator is given by

\widehat{\textsf{MSE}}(\,\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}\,)=g_{1\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)+g_{2\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)+2g_{3\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)\,,

but on the other, if ML is used, then

\widehat{\textsf{MSE}}(\,\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}\,)=g_{1\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)+g_{2\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)+2g_{3\,d}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}\right)-b\,\nabla g_{1}\,,

with

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left[\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right]^{-1}\cdot

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\textsf{tr}\left\{\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{-1}\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}\,\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{-2}\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}\,\bm{x}_{d\,\textsf{A}}^{\textsf{T}}\right)\right\}

and
\nabla g_{1}=\sigma_{d}^{4}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{u}^{2}+\sigma_{d}^{2}\right)^{-2}
.

3
Simulation study

In this section, we carry out a simulation study in order to assess the statistical properties of the proposed estimators. The aim of this simulation is to analyze the Relative Bias,

SB_{d}=\frac{\gamma_{d}-\hat{\gamma}_{d}}{\hat{\gamma}_{d}}
,
the Relative Standard Error
EER_{d}=\frac{RMSE(\hat{\gamma}_{d})}{\gamma_{d}}\times 100\%
,
as well as the Relative Mean Square Error
RMSE\left(\hat{\gamma}_{d}\right)=\sqrt{MSE\left(\hat{\gamma}_{d}\right)}

associated with the proposed estimator versus the Horvitz-Thompson estimator (
Narain, 1951
,
Horvitz and Thompson, 1952
), the calibration estimator, and the composite estimator.

Our simulation study follows the following steps:

1.

Simulate a population of
N=100,000
students with 150 items per student and
D=500
domains, along with two auxiliary variables associated with the ability
\theta
for each student.

2.

Set 10%, 20% and 30% of missing responses per student completely at random in the population.

3.

Define two (2) auxiliary variables at the domain level correlated with
\theta_{d}
on three levels
(high
\left(>80\%\right)
, medium
\left(60\%\,,\,80\%\right)
and low
\left(<60\%\right)
).

4.

Estimate five (5) plausible values for each student according to Equation
??
.

5.

Consider a different number of domains in the sample using different sampling fractions as
f_{d}=30\%,\,50\%,\,\text{and}\,70\%
on the total number of domains in the population.

6.

For the domains selected in step 5., select a random sample from the population using simple random sampling with sampling fraction
f_{n}=5\%,\,10\%,\text{and}\,\,20\%
, considering the Horvitz-Thompson estimate,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Dir}}
, the calibration estimate,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Cal}}
, and the composite estimator,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Comp}}
, the proposed estimator,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}
using the REML method,
for each selected domain, along with its standard deviation.

7.

Calculate the relative biases,
SB_{d},
and the relative standard errors,
EER_{d}
, by domain, for each of the estimators
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Dir}}
,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Cal}}
,
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{Comp}}
, and
\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{\textsf{P}}
, and compute:

(a)

SBP=\frac{{\sum_{d=1}^{D}SB_{d}}}{D}
(average relative bias).

(b)

EERP=\frac{{\sum_{d=1}^{D}EERP_{d}}}{D}
(average relative standard error).

8.

Repeat steps 5. to 7. for 100,000 random samples and compute:

(a)

\overline{SBR}=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{100000}SBP_{r}}}{100000}

(mean of the average relative biases).

(b)

\overline{EERP}=\frac{{\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{100000}EERP_{r}}}{100000}

(mean of the average relative standard error).

5
Discussion

This paper shows practical and methodological advantages of integrating small area estimation, item response theory, and multiple imputation.
It is possible to show from a theoretical and empirical perspective that the proposed estimator for the ability mean is unbiased.
In addition, in all of the scenarios where the proposed estimator is tested, it has a lower
average relative standard error than its competitors, considering simple random sampling.
On the one hand, by varying the sample fractions and the domain fractions, the proposed estimator has lower mean relative standard errors compared to the other estimators used in the simulation.
This behaviour is achieved under all the correlation scenarios.

Finally, when the percentage of missing data varies, the relative standard errors increases as the rate of missing data does. The proposed estimator always obtains the
lowest relative standard errors compared to the other estimators studied
in the simulation. Moreover, in the scenario with 30% of missing
data and a small sampling fraction, the relative standard
errors did not exceed 5% on average, implying that these estimates are quite low.

Appendix B
Notation

Matrices and vectors with entries consisting of subscripted variables are denoted by a boldfaced version of the letter for that variable. For example,
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
denotes an
n\times 1
column vector with entries
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. We use
\bm{0}
and
\bm{1}
to denote the column vector with all entries equal to 0 and 1, respectively, and
\mathbf{I}
to denote the identity matrix. A subindex in this context refers to the corresponding dimension; for instance,
\mathbf{I}_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix. The transpose of a vector
\bm{x}
is denoted by
\bm{x}^{\textsf{T}}
; analogously for matrices. Moreover, if
\mathbf{X}
is a square matrix, we use
\text{tr}(\mathbf{X})
to denote its trace and
\mathbf{X}^{-1}
to denote its inverse. The norm of
\bm{x}
, given by
\sqrt{\bm{x}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{x}}
, is denoted by
\|\,\bm{x}\|\,
.
