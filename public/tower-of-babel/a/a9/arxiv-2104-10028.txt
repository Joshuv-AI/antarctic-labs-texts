Abstract




Asymptotic Expansion of Laplace-Fourier-Type Integrals

Sara Konrad
∗
, Matthias Bartelmann

August 9, 2026

Abstract

We study the asymptotic behaviour of integrals of the Laplace-Fourier type

P(k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x\;,

with
k\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in
d\geq 1
dimensions, with
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and sufficiently well-behaved functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. Our main result is

P(k)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}}{|k|^{sd/2}}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)

for
|k|\to\infty
, where
A
is the Hessian matrix of the function
f
at its critical point, assumed to be at
x_{0}=0
. In one dimension, the Hessian is replaced by the second derivative,
A=f^{\prime\prime}(0)
. We also show that the integration domain
\Omega
can be extended to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
without changing the asymptotic behaviour.

1
Introduction

Integrals over rapidly oscillating integrands occur frequently in physics and are often difficult to handle numerically. Their asymptotic behaviour for large parameter values (e.g. large wave numbers) is interesting in view of estimates, studies of limiting behaviour, and testing numerical solutions. In this paper, we study the asymptotic behaviour of integrals of the form

P(k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x\;,\quad k>0\;,

(1)

under fairly general assumptions for
k\to\infty
, first in one, then in
d
dimensions. Specifically, we shall assume that the integral in (
1
) converges absolutely and that
s\geq\alpha
, with
\alpha
to be defined below. Our main results, derived in Sect.
2
for
d=1
and in Sect.
3
for
d>1
, will be:

1.

In one dimension,

P(k)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-k^{s}f(0)}}{k^{s/2}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2-s}}{2f^{\prime\prime}(0)}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad k\to\infty\;,

(2)

where
x_{0}=0
is a critical point of
f
.

2.

In
d>1
dimensions,

P(k)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}}{|k|^{sd/2}}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad k\to\infty\;,

(3)

where
A
is the Hessian matrix of
f
at its critical point, assumed to be in
x_{0}=0
.

With this result, we extend the existing literature in two ways: we derive the asymptotic behaviour of Laplace-Fourier type integrals in one dimension and extend them to an arbitrary number
d
of dimensions. In one dimension, detailed studies exist with a different focus
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, and to our knowledge, the generalization to
d
dimensions is new.

2
One-dimensional case

2.1
Erdélyi’s theorem for Laplace integrals

In one dimension, we begin with Erdélyi’s theorem
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
for Laplace integrals, which states:

Theorem 1

(Erdélyi)
.

Let
I(\lambda)
be an integral of the form

I(\lambda)=\int_{a}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x)}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\;,

(4)

where
f(x)
is a real function of the real variable
x
, while
g(x)
may be real or complex. Then, if

1.

f(x)>f(a)
for
x\in(a,b)
and

\inf_{[a+\delta,b)}f(x)-f(a)>0

(5)

for
\delta>0
;

2.

f^{\prime}(x)
and
g(x)
are continuous in a neighbourhood of
a
, except possibly at
a
;

3.

f
and
g
admit asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle\sim f(a)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(x-a)^{k+\alpha}\;,

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}(x-a)^{k+\beta-1}\;,

(6)

f
can be term-wise differentiated,

f^{\prime}(x)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(k+\alpha)(x-a)^{k+\alpha-1}

(7)

for
x\to a^{+}
, where
\alpha>0
and
\mathrm{Re}\,\beta>0
; and

4.

I(\lambda)
converges absolutely for sufficiently large
\lambda
; then

the integral
I(\lambda)
has the asymptotic expansion

I(\lambda)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(a)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)\,c_{n}}{\lambda^{\nu}}

(8)

for
\lambda\to\infty
, where
\nu:=(n+\beta)/\alpha
. The coefficients
c_{n}
can be expressed by
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
as

c_{n}=\frac{1}{\alpha a_{0}^{\nu}}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{b_{n-m}}{m!}\,d_{m,n}\quad\mbox{with}\quad d_{m,n}=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}x^{m}}\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{a_{0}}\,x^{j}\right)^{-\nu}\;.

(9)

Beginning with this result, we insert the derivatives
d_{m,n}
into the coefficients
c_{n}
from (
9
) and write the asymptotic expansion
I(\lambda)
from (
8
) for some positive integer
M
that is to be constrained later, as

\displaystyle I(\lambda)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(a)}}{\alpha}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\nu}}\,\frac{bac_{n-m}}{m!}\,d_{m,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(a)}}{\alpha}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\nu}}\,\frac{b_{n-m}}{m!}\,d_{m,n}+\phi_{M}(\lambda)\right]\;,

(10)

where we have assumed that the inner sum over
n
in the second line exists for all
0\leq m\leq M
. To abbreviate notation, we have defined

\phi_{M}(\lambda):=\sum_{n=M}^{\infty}\sum_{m=M}^{n}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\nu}}\,\frac{b_{n-m}}{m!}\,d_{m,n}\;.

(11)

and introduce the symbol

I_{m}(\lambda):=\sum_{n=m}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\nu}}\,\frac{b_{n-m}}{m!}\,d_{m,n}

(12)

for the sums over
n
remaining in (
10
). If the outer sum over
m
in (
10
) exists for
M\to\infty
, can write the asymptotic expansion
I(\lambda)
as

I(\lambda)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(a)}}{\alpha}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}I_{m}(\lambda)\;.

(13)

Note that this is indeed an asymptotic expansion, since the symbols
I_{m}(\lambda)
are an asymptotic sequence as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, denoted by
\{I_{m}\}
, because for all
m\geq 0
,

I_{m+1}(\lambda)=o\left(I_{m}(\lambda)\right)\quad\text{as}\quad\lambda\rightarrow\infty\;;

(14)

see
[
7
, p. 10, definition 2]
. The sum over
n
in the functions
I_{m}(\lambda)
defined in (
12
) can be carried out for some choices of
\alpha
and
\beta
, for which we shall give examples below.

2.2
Laplace-Fourier integrals in one dimension

We now wish to study the asymptotic behaviour of the Fourier integral

I(\lambda,k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x)}x^{\,\beta-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\,\mathrm{d}x

(15)

for
\lambda\to\infty
, with
\mathrm{Re}\,\beta>0
and the fixed parameter
k>0
. For doing so, we apply Erdélyi’s theorem, setting

g(x)=x^{\beta-1}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\;.

(16)

Moreover, without loss of generality, we shift the origin such that
a=0
and take the integral
I(\lambda,k)
over the open domains
\Omega=(0,b)
and assume that
f(x)
satisfies the conditions of Erdélyi’s theorem with
a=0
. The coefficients
b_{n}
defined in (
6
) are given by

b_{n}(k)=\frac{(\mathrm{i}k)^{n}}{n!}\;,

(17)

and the symbols
I_{m}
defined in (
12
) are given by

I_{m}(\lambda,k)=\frac{1}{m!(a_{0}\lambda)^{(m+\beta)/\alpha}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+\frac{m}{\alpha}\right)}{n!}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{1/\alpha}}\right)^{n}d_{m,n+m}\;.

(18)

We now show that, for fixed
\lambda,k
, the symbols
I_{m}
as in (
18
) converge for each finite
m
when
\alpha>1
. For
\alpha=1
, the symbols converge if
\left|\frac{k}{a_{0}\lambda}\right|<1
.

Proof.

For
m>0
, the derivatives
d_{m,n}
as defined in (
9
) can be written as

d_{m,n}=\sum_{p=1}^{m}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+p\right)}{\Gamma\left(\nu\right)}\,D_{m,p}\left(a_{0},\dotsm,a_{m-p+1}\right)\;,

(19)

where
D_{m,p}
are finite coefficients that are independent of
n
. To see this, note that any of the
m
derivatives applied in (
9
) acts on the parenthesis raised to the power of
-\nu
, or on the sum in the parenthesis. The former derivatives create the Gamma functions in front, the latter the coefficients
D_{m,p}
. As the limit
x\to 0
is taken, the expression in the parenthesis becomes unity, thus raising it to any power becomes independent of the exponent.
1
1

1

A systematic way to compute these coefficients in terms of partial ordinary Bell polynomials is presented in
[
8
]
.
Thus, for each
m
there exists a positive constant
K_{m}>0
such that the following estimate holds

\left|d_{m,n}\right|\leq K_{m}\sum_{p=1}^{m}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+p\right)}{\Gamma\left(\nu\right)}\leq m\cdot K_{m}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+m\right)}{\Gamma\left(\nu\right)}\;.

(20)

Using (
17
), (
18
) and (
20
), the symbols
I_{m}
are then bound by

\left|I_{m}(\lambda,k)\right|\leq\frac{K_{m}}{(m-1)!(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{m+\beta}{\alpha}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+m+\frac{m}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma\left(n+1\right)}\left|\frac{k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{1/\alpha}}\right|^{n}\;.

(21)

For
\alpha>1
, the sum in eq. (
21
) is absolutely convergent, which can be seen by d’Alembert’s ratio test. We consider the limit of the ratio of two consecutive terms

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1+\beta+(1+\alpha)m}{\alpha}\right)}{\Gamma(n+2)}\left|\frac{k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right|^{n+1}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\left(n+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+\beta+(1+\alpha)m}{r+1}\right)}\left|\frac{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{k}\right|^{n}\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\right|\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1+(1+\alpha)m+\beta-\alpha}{\alpha}+1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1+(1+\alpha)m+\beta-\alpha}{\alpha}+1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{n+1+(1+\alpha)m+\beta}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\lim_{n\to 0}n^{-1+\frac{1}{\alpha}}=0\;,

where we made use of Gautschi’s inequality
x^{1-s}<\frac{\Gamma(x+1)}{\Gamma(x+s)}<(x+1)^{1-s}
, for
0<s<1
and
x>0

[
9
]
. Thus, the sums (
21
) are finite for
m>0
and
\alpha>1
. Since
d_{0,n}=1
for all
n
, an analogous proof shows
|I_{0}(\lambda,k)|<\infty
.
For
\alpha=1
, the inequality (
21
) becomes

\displaystyle\left|I_{m}(\lambda,k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{K_{m}}{(m-1)!(a_{0}\lambda)^{m+\beta}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\nu+2m\right)}{n!}\left|\frac{k}{a_{0}\lambda}\right|^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{K_{m}}{(m-1)!(a_{0}\lambda)^{m+\beta}}\Gamma(2m+\beta){}_{1}F_{0}\left(2m+\beta;;\left|\frac{k}{a_{0}\lambda}\right|\right)\;,

(22)

where the last step is valid for
\left|\frac{k}{a_{0}\lambda}\right|<1
and
{}_{p}F_{q}
denotes the generalized hypergeometric function. The case
m=0
is easier because
d_{0,n}=1
for all
n
, but otherwise proceeds analogously.
∎

Now that we have shown, that (
13
) together with the symbols
I_{m}(\lambda,k)
is a valid asymptotic expansion for Laplace-Fourier integrals
I(\lambda,k)
as
\lambda\to\infty
, we compare our expansion (
13
) to the expansion (
9
) from Erdélyi’s theorem.
Since the symbols
I_{m}
originate from a resummation of Erdélyi’s series, they contain the coefficients
b_{n}
to infinite order, thus the total information about the phase function
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}
.
Considering now Laplace-Fourier integrals with related exponential factors
\lambda=k^{s}
implies
P(k)=I(k^{s},k)
. The symbols
I_{m}
are then specialized to

I_{m}(k^{s},k)=\frac{1}{m!(a_{0}k^{s})^{(m+\beta)/\alpha}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Gamma\left(\nu+\frac{m}{\alpha}\right)\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}k^{1-s/\alpha}}{a_{0}^{1/\alpha}}\right)^{n}d_{m,n+m}\;.

(23)

We find for
s\geq\alpha

I_{m}(k^{s},k)=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{sm+s\beta}{\alpha}}\right)\;,\quad\text{as }k\to\infty\;,

(24)

showing that the symbols
I_{m}(k^{s},k)
denote an asymptotic sequence as
k\rightarrow\infty
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Comparing this sequence to the terms in the expansion (
9
) from Erdélyi’s theorem,

\frac{\Gamma(\nu)c_{n}}{k^{s\nu}}=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{sn+s\beta}{\alpha}+n}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty\;,

(25)

shows that the terms in our asymptotic expansion (
13
) fall of much faster when
s>\alpha
. For
s=\alpha
, the sequence from Erdélyi’s theorem even fails to be asymptotic.

2.3
Examples

For convenience, we introduce

x_{\alpha}:=\frac{4}{a_{0}\lambda}\left(\frac{k}{4}\right)^{\alpha}\;.

(26)

Note that for
\lambda=k^{s}
and
s=\alpha
,
x_{\alpha}
is a positive constant, while for
s>\alpha
,
x_{\alpha}>0
approaches
0
in the limit
k\to\infty
.

2.3.1
One-sided domain
\Omega=(0,b)

On the one-sided domain
\Omega
, we can then directly apply Erdélyi’s theorem by inserting the
b_{n}
from (
17
) into (
12
). Since
d_{0,n}=1
for all
n
, we then have

I_{0}(\lambda,k)=\frac{1}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\beta/\alpha}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\nu)}{n!}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{1/\alpha}}\right)=\frac{1}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{\beta/\alpha}}\ {}_{1}\Psi_{0}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}(\beta/\alpha,1/\alpha)\\
-\end{smallmatrix};\mathrm{i}x_{1}\right]\;

(27)

for
m=0
, where
{}_{p}\Psi_{q}
denotes the Fox-Wright function. For this sum to converge, we must require
\alpha\geq 1
and thus
\nu\leq n+\beta
. For
\alpha=1
and arbitrary
\beta>0
, we arrive at

I_{0}(\lambda,k)=\Gamma(\beta)\left(\frac{x_{1}}{k\,(1-\mathrm{i}x_{1})}\right)^{\,\beta}\;.

(28)

2.3.2
Two-sided domain
\Omega=(-b_{1},b_{2})

On the two-sided domain
\Omega
, we assume that Erdélyi’s theorem applies to
I(\lambda,k)
separately on the intervals
(0,b_{1})
with
x\to-x
and
(0,b_{2})
. Furthermore, we restrict
\alpha
to being an even integer because otherwise
f(x)
could not have a maximum in the origin. In this case, it is advantageous to note that
b_{n}\to(-1)^{\,\beta-1+n}b_{n}
and
d_{m,n}\to(-1)^{m}d_{m,n}
for
x\to-x
.

Then, after adding the results for the intervals
(-b_{1},0)
and
(0,b_{2})
, only those summands in
I_{m}(\lambda,k)
from (
12
) remain for integer
\beta
which have even
n
if
\beta=2l-1
is odd, or which have odd
n
if
\beta=2l
is even, where
l\in\{1,2,\dots\}
. We use this to write

I_{m}(\lambda,k)=2\,\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil m/2\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2n+\beta}{\alpha}\right)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{(2n+\beta)/\alpha}}\frac{(\mathrm{i}k)^{2n-m}}{m!(2n-m)!}d_{m,2n}&\beta=2l-1\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lfloor m/2\rfloor}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{2n+1+\beta}{\alpha}\right)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{(2n+1+\beta)/\alpha}}\frac{(\mathrm{i}k)^{2n+1-m}}{m!(2n+1-m)!}d_{m,2n+1}&\beta=2l\\
\end{cases}\;,

(29)

with the factor of
2
accounting for the results on both intervals.

Some special cases for the leading term
I_{0}(\lambda)
may be worth noting:

(i)

For
\alpha=2
and integer
\beta
, the asymptotic expansion of
f(x)
begins with a term quadratic in
x
, and the envelope of the Fourier factor in (
16
) has an integer power of
x
. In this case, we have

I_{0}(\lambda,k)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 2\left(\frac{4x_{2}}{k^{2}}\right)^{l+1/2}\,\Gamma\left(l+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(l+\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2};-x_{2}\right)&\beta=2l+1\\
\displaystyle 2\mathrm{i}k\left(\frac{4x_{2}}{k^{2}}\right)^{l+1/2}\,\Gamma\left(l+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(l+\frac{1}{2};\frac{3}{2};-x_{2}\right)&\beta=2l\\
\end{cases}\;.

(30)

(ii)

For
\alpha=4
and
\beta=1
, the asymptotic expansion of
f(x)
begins with a term quartic in
x
, and the Fourier factor has a constant amplitude in
x
. Then, the result is

I_{0}(\lambda,k)=2\sqrt{2}\,\frac{x_{4}^{1/4}}{k}\left[2\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right){}_{0}F_{2}\left(;\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{4};\frac{x_{4}}{4}\right)-8x_{4}^{1/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}\right){}_{0}F_{2}\left(;\frac{5}{4},\frac{3}{2};\frac{x_{4}}{4}\right)\right]\;.

(31)

In the expressions (
30
) and (
31
),
{}_{p}F_{q}
is the generalised hypergeometic function.

In view of applications in physics, we investigate in more detail situations in which
f(x)
has an asymptotic expansion beginning with a term quadratic in
x
, and the amplitude of the Fourier factor is constant. Then,
\alpha=2
and
\beta=1
. In this case, we return directly to the definition (
12
) for the summands
I_{m}(\lambda,k)
in the asymptotic expansion (
13
) of the integral
I(\lambda,k)
. Since
\beta
is odd now, we can restricting the sum over
n
to even
n
, and since the integration domain is two-sided, we need to double the result. Then, from (
12
),

I_{0}(\lambda,k)=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{(a_{0}\lambda)^{n+1/2}}\frac{(\mathrm{i}k)^{2n}}{(2n)!}=2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a_{0}\lambda}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{4a_{0}\lambda}\right)^{n}=2\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a_{0}\lambda}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x_{2}}\;,

(32)

where we have used

\Gamma\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{(2n)!}{n!4^{n}}\sqrt{\pi}

(33)

in the second step. With

d_{1,2n}=-\frac{2n+1}{2}\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad d_{2,2n}=(2n+1)\left[\frac{2n+3}{4}\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}}-\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}}\right]\;,

(34)

we further find

\frac{I_{1}(\lambda,k)}{I_{0}(\lambda,k)}=\frac{\mathrm{i}A}{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{I_{2}(\lambda,k)}{I_{0}(\lambda,k)}=\frac{B}{k^{2}}

(35)

with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=\frac{a_{1}}{a_{0}}\,x_{2}\left(2x_{2}-3\right)\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=-\frac{a_{2}}{a_{0}}\,x_{2}\left(4x_{2}^{2}-12x_{2}+3\right)-\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{4a_{0}^{2}}\,x_{2}\left(8x_{2}^{3}-60x_{2}^{2}+90x_{2}-15\right)\;.

(36)

We finally specialise this result to integrals of the form

P(k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\,\mathrm{d}x

(37)

with
s\geq 2
and
f
satisfying the previously stated conditions. Substituting
\lambda\to k^{s}
in (

) then immediately leads to the asymptotic series

P(k)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-k^{s}f(0)}}{k^{s/2}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a_{0}}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-x_{2}}\left[1+\mathrm{i}\,\frac{A}{k}+\frac{B}{k^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-3}\right)\right]

(38)

for
k\to\infty
, with
x_{2}
specialized to
x_{2}=k^{2-s}/(4a_{0})
. Note that the terms
A
and
B
in (
34
) are both of order
\mathcal{O}(k^{s-2})
here. The result (
2
) follows from (
34
) by noting that
a_{0}=f^{\prime\prime}(0)/2
.

3
d
-dimensional case

Before we can now turn to the case of an arbitrary number
d
of dimensions, we need to prepare some concepts and notation. Here and below,
D
denotes the derivative of a function with respect to its arguments.

3.1
Preliminary remarks

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x_{0}\in\Omega
a non-degenerate critical point of a smooth function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
on
\Omega
with
f\in C^{\infty}
. Then, by Morse’s lemma, neighbourhoods
U
,
V
of the points
y=0
and
x=x_{0}
and a diffeomorphism
h:U\to V
exist such that

\left(f\circ h\right)(y)=f(x_{0})+\frac{1}{2}y^{\top}Qy\;,\quad Q=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}\right)\;,

(39)

where the Jacobian
H:=Dh
of
h
has unit determinant at
y=0
,
\det H_{0}=1
with
H_{0}:=H(0)
. Then, by the chain rule and
(Df)(x_{0})=0
,
H_{0}
diagonalises the Hessian matrix

A:=\left(D^{2}f\right)\Bigr|_{x=x_{0}}

(40)

of
f
in
x_{0}
such that

Q=H^{\top}_{0}AH_{0}\;.

(41)

With the same chart
h:U\to V
, the Jacobian
H=Dh
and a smooth function
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with
g\in C^{\infty}
, we define the function
G:U\to\mathbb{R}
by
G(y)=(g\circ h)(y)\det H(y)
. We further introduce multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d})
and agree on the notation

\displaystyle|\alpha|

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\ldots+\alpha_{d}\;,

\displaystyle\alpha!

\displaystyle:=\alpha_{1}!\alpha_{2}!\cdots\alpha_{d}!\;,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\alpha)

\displaystyle:=\Gamma(\alpha_{1})\Gamma(\alpha_{2})\cdots\Gamma(\alpha_{d})\;,

\displaystyle\mu^{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\mu_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots\mu_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\;,

(42)

where
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{d})
is a
d
dimensional real valued vector.
We finally define the symbol

\delta(\alpha)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{all $\alpha_{j}$ even}\\
0&\mbox{else}\end{cases}

(43)

and the derivative operator

D^{\alpha}G(0)=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial^{\alpha_{1}}y_{1}\ldots\partial^{\alpha_{d}}y_{d}}G(y)\Biggr|_{y=0}\;.

(44)

With these preparations, we can now continue with the following theorem for the asymptotic behaviour of a class of integrals on
\Omega

[
7
]
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
J(\lambda)
be the integral

J(\lambda)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x)}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

(45)

with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose asymptotic behaviour we wish to determine for
\lambda\to\infty
. We assume that

1.

f,g\in C^{\infty}
on
\Omega
;

2.

J(\lambda)
converges absolutely for sufficiently large
\lambda
;

3.

f
has a minimum at, and only at,
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that

\rho(\varepsilon):=\inf_{\Omega\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})}f(x)-f(x_{0})>0

(46)

for all
\varepsilon>0
, where
B_{\varepsilon}(x_{0})
is the open ball with radius
\varepsilon
around
x_{0}
; and that

4.

the Hessian matrix
A
of
f
in
x_{0}
is positive definite.

Then, the integral
J(\lambda)
has the asymptotic expansion

J(\lambda)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x_{0})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{\lambda^{d/2+n}}

(47)

for
\lambda\to\infty
, with

c_{n}=\sum_{|\alpha|=2n}\delta(\alpha)\left(\frac{2}{\mu}\right)^{(\alpha+1)/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\right)\frac{D^{\alpha}G(0)}{\alpha!}\;.

(48)

The
\mu_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, are the (real and positive) eigenvalues of
A
.

3.2
Laplace-Fourier integrals in
d
dimensions

We apply the result (
47
) with the coefficients (
48
) to Laplace-Fourier-type integrals,

J(\lambda,k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x\;,

(49)

whose asymptotic behaviour we wish to determine for
\lambda\to\infty
, where
|k|
is assumed to be a large parameter at the same time. Without loss of generality, we set
x_{0}=0
here. For integrals of this type, the function
G
defined above is

G(y)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kh(y)}\det H(y)\;.

(50)

Since only the derivatives of
G
acting on the exponential factor increase the powers of
k
, and since additionally
G(0)=1
, we can state

D^{\alpha}G(0)\sim\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[\mathrm{i}\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)_{j}\right]^{\alpha_{j}}=\mathrm{i}^{|\alpha|}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty

(51)

and move on to write

c_{n}(k)\sim\sum_{|\alpha|=2n}\delta\left(\alpha\right)\left(-1\right)^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{2}{\mu_{j}}\right)^{(\alpha_{j}+1)/2}\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha_{j}+1}{2}\right)\frac{\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}}{\alpha_{j}!}\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;.

(52)

Since the factor
\delta(\alpha)
ensures that only even
\alpha_{j}
can occur in the sum, it is convenient to define a multi-index
n
with elements
n_{j}=\alpha_{j}/2
. Inserting (
33
) into (
52
) and noting that
\prod\mu_{j}=\det A
, we arrive at

c_{n}(k)\sim\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\det A}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{|n|=n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{n_{j}!}\frac{\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)_{j}^{2j}}{\mu_{j}^{n_{j}}}\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;.

(53)

Since (
41
) implies that
H_{0}Q^{-1}H_{0}^{\top}=A^{-1}
, the remaining sum equals

\sum_{|n|=n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{n_{j}!}\frac{\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)_{j}^{2j}}{\mu_{j}^{n_{j}}}=\frac{1}{n!}\left[\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)^{\top}Q^{-1}\left(H^{\top}_{0}k\right)\right]^{n}=\frac{\left(k^{\top}A^{-1}k\right)^{n}}{n!}

(54)

allowing us to write (
47
) in the form

J(\lambda,k)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda f(0)}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{\lambda^{d}\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2\lambda}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad\lambda\to\infty\;.

(55)

For integrals of the type

P(k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x

(56)

with
s\geq 2
, we substitute again
\lambda\to|k|^{s}
in (
50
). We then obtain immediately from (
55
)

P(k)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{|k|^{sd}\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;,

(57)

which proves the result (
3
).

3.3
Extension to
\mathbb{R}^{d}

We finally wish to extend the integration domain
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We begin with the conditions imposed in Theorem
2
, but relax conditions (1) and (2) to

1^{\prime}
.

the Hessian
A=D^{2}f
exists in
x_{0}=0
and

2^{\prime}
.

f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

and add as a further condition:

3b.

The infimum

\sigma(\varepsilon):=\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(0)}|f(0)|-|f(x)|>0

(58)

for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Note that conditions (3) and (3b) together imply that
f(0)<0
. Then, the integral

P(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x

(59)

has the asymptotic expansion (
57
).

To prove this statement, we consider the integral

\displaystyle\bar{P}(k):

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}-1+|k|^{s}f(x)\right]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=P(k)-(2\pi)^{N}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(k)+|k|^{s}\tilde{f}(k)

\displaystyle=P(k)+|k|^{s}\tilde{f}(k)

(60)

for
k>0
, where
\tilde{f}
denotes the Fourier transform of
f
. We further split the integration domain as

\bar{P}(k)=\left(\int_{\Omega}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega}\right)\left[\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}-1+|k|^{s}f(x)\right]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x=:\bar{P}_{\Omega}(k)+\bar{P}_{\bar{\Omega}}(k)\;.

(61)

For the asymptotic expansion of
\bar{P}_{\Omega}(k)
, we can estimate

\bar{P}_{\Omega}(k)\leq\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\mathrm{d}x+\left[1+|k|^{s}|f(0)|\right]V_{\Omega}\;,

(62)

where
V_{\Omega}
is the volume of the domain
\Omega
. Since the second term on the right-hand side of (
62
) is exponentially suppressed compared to the asymptotic expansion (
57
) of the first term (recall that
f(0)<0
), we can conclude that

\bar{P}_{\Omega}(k)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{|k|^{sd}\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;.

(63)

For
\bar{P}_{\bar{\Omega}}(k)
, we estimate

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{\bar{\Omega}}(k)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{|k|^{ns}}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega}\left|f(x)\right|^{n}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{|k|^{ns}}{n!}\left[|f(0)|-\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]^{n-2}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}x}_{=:\,C\,<\,\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{\left[|f(0)|-\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]^{2}}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}\left[f(0)+\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]}-1+|k|^{s}\left[f(0)+\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{C}{\left[|f(0)|-\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}\left[f(0)+\sigma(\varepsilon)\right]}\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;.

(64)

Again, this result is exponentially suppressed by the factor
\exp(-|k|^{s}\sigma(\varepsilon))
compared to the asymptotic expansion (
57
). Since
\tilde{f}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we can conclude

\displaystyle P(k)

\displaystyle=\bar{P}(k)-|k|^{s}\tilde{f}(k)\sim\bar{P}(k)\sim\bar{P}_{\Omega}(k)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{|k|^{sd}\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\quad|k|\to\infty\;,

(65)

as claimed.

4
Summary

We have shown here that Laplace-Fourier-type integrals of the form

P(k)=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx}\,\mathrm{d}x

(66)

behave asymptotically like

P(k)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-|k|^{s}f(0)}\sqrt{\frac{(2\pi)^{d}}{|k|^{sd}\det A}}\exp\left(-\frac{k^{\top}A^{-1}k}{2|k|^{s}}\right)

(67)

for
|k|\to\infty
under the following conditions:

1.

The function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
has a negative minimum at
x_{0}=0
;

2.

the Hessian
A
of
f
in
x_{0}=0
exists and is positive definite;

3.

f
is square integrable on
\Omega
; and

4.

the exponent
s\geq 2
.

This result is valid for
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
. We began in Sect.
2
by applying Erdélyi’s theorem
1
to the more general class (
15
) of integrals in one dimension, discussed special cases, and restricted the general result to the case (
66
) with
s\geq 2
. We extended the discussion to
d>1
dimensions in Sect.
3
and finally showed that the integration domain can be
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Integrals of this type with rapidly oscillating integrands frequently occur in physics, most notably in statistical physics. Asymptotic expressions like (
67
) can help to understand the behaviour of such integrals in the limit of small scales or large momenta,
|k|\to\infty
, and as a test case for numerical integrations. In a forthcoming paper, we shall apply our result to the formation of small-scale cosmic structures.
