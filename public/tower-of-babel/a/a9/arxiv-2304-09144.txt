Probabilistic Laws on Infinite Groups
Gideon Amir, Guy Blachar, Maria Gerasimova, Gady Kozma

Abstract
. We study the probability that certain laws are satisfied on infinite groups, focusing on elements sampled by random walks. For several group laws, including the metabelian one, we construct examples of infinite groups for which the law holds with high probability, but the group does not satisfy the law virtually. On the other hand, we show that if an infinite group satisfies the law x 2 = 1 x^{2}=1 with positive probability, then it is virtually abelian.

1.
Introduction

The commuting probability of a finite group
G
, denoted
\mathbb{P}([x,y]=1)
, is the probability that two random elements from
G
commute with each other. The following is a well-known theorem of Gustafson
[
7
]
:

Theorem
.

If
\mathbb{P}([x,y]=1)>\frac{5}{8}
then
G
is abelian.

We refer to such a phenomenon as a gap: when considering the set of commuting probabilities over all finite groups as a subset of
[0,1]
, there is a gap close to
1
.

More generally, for a finite group
G
and a word
1\neq w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
, we define the probability that
w
is satisfied on
G
as the probability that
d
independent uniformly chosen elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
of
G
satisfy
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})=1
:

\mathbb{P}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\frac{\left|\left\{(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})\in G^{d}:w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})=1\right\}\right|}{\left|G\right|^{d}}.

A natural question is whether for a given word
w
, these probabilities also present a similar behavior.

It is also well known (and with the same proof as Gustafson’s theorem) that for a fixed
k
, the
k
-step nilpotent law
[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]=[[\dotsb[[x_{1},x_{2}],x_{3}]\dotsb],x_{k}]=1
presents a gap.

Considering power laws
x^{m}=1
, several results have been established. Laffey showed the existence of a gap for the laws
x^{2}=1
,
x^{3}=1

[
15
]
,
x^{4}=1

[
17
]
and
x^{p}=1
for prime
p
on non-
p
-groups
[
16
]
. For general exponent
m
, a result of Mann and Martinez
[
22
]
proves the existence of a gap for
x^{m}=1
for all groups with a fixed number of generators. Another result in this direction is the recent work of Delizia, Jezernik, Moravec and Nicotera
[
2
]
that the metabelian law
[[x,y],[z,w]]=1
and the 2-Engel law
[[x,y],y]=1
satisfy a gap.

Although all known cases indeed satisfy a gap for finite groups, the general case is still an open question:

Question 1.1
.

Does any law satisfy a gap for finite groups?

The commuting probability of a finite group does not only present a gap, but also reveals structural information about the group. A well-known theorem of Peter Neumman
[
24
, Theorem 1]
states:

Theorem
.

If
G
is a finite group for which
\mathbb{P}([x,y]=1)\geq\varepsilon>0
, then
G
is (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-abelian-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded), i.e.
G
contains subgroups
H^{\prime}\vartriangleleft H<G
such that
[G:H]
and
\left|H^{\prime}\right|
are bounded by a function of
\varepsilon
, and such that
H/H^{\prime}
is abelian.

We call such a behavior
positivity
and say that “the commutativity law satisfies positivity” (see this more formally in Definition
2.4
below).

An interesting phenmenon is that sometimes, if a law holds with probability at least
\varepsilon
, then
another
law holds virtually. We quote two results of this type. For the law
x^{2}=1
, a result of Mann
[
21
]
shows that if
\mathbb{P}\left(x^{2}=1\textrm{ on }G\right)\geq\varepsilon>0
, then
G
is (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-abelian-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded). The example of dihedral groups shows that it is not true that the group is (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-(satisfying
x^{2}=1
)-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded).

For the
2
-step nilpotent law
[[x_{1},x_{2}],x_{3}]=1
, a result of Eberhard and Shumyatsky
[
3
]
shows that such groups are not (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-(
2
-step-nilpotent)-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded), but are (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-(
3
-step-nilpotent)-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded).

We remark that in the nilpotent case, this phenomenon is due to our requirement that the excess parts are bounded
only
by
\varepsilon
. If one allows them to depend also on the number of generators, the picture changes completely. Indeed, Shalev
[
27
]
showed that if
G
is
d
-generated, and
\mathbb{P}\left([x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]=1\textrm{ on }G\right)\geq\varepsilon>0
, then
G
is (
k
-step-nilpotent)-by-(
(k,d,\varepsilon)
-bounded).

Again, the general case remains unknown:

Question 1.2
.

If a law
w
is satisfied on a finite group
G
with probability larger than
\varepsilon
, does the group
G
contain a large subgroup (of index bounded as a function of
\varepsilon
, and possibly also of the number of generators of
G
) that satisfies a law which depends only on
w
?

1.1.
Infinite groups

One may ask for a generalization of these concepts to infinite groups. As we can no longer sample elements according to a uniform distribution, one needs to define how to measure the probability of satisfying a law on an infinite group. When the group is locally compact, for example, a natural choice for a measure is the Haar measure.

Our main interest in this paper is the case of infinite, finitely generated discrete groups. Following the ideas of
[
1
]
, we use a sequence of probability measures
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
on
G
, and define the probability that
G
satisfies the law
w
with respect to
M
as

(1)

\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}\left(\left\{(g_{1},\dots,g_{d}):w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})=1\right\}\right).

If
G
is finitely generated, natural choices for
\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
include taking a random walk measure on
G
, or a uniform measure on a sequence of balls centered at the identity and converging to
G
. We will omit
M
if it is the random walk measure with respect to some natural generators (this is the case we are most interested in).

In previous works by Antólin-Martino-Ventura
[
1
]
and later by Tointon
[
28
]
, the commutativity probability was studied. These works prove analogous results to the finite case:

Theorem

(Tointon
[
28
, Theorem 1.9]
)
.

(1)

If
\mathbb{P}\left([x,y]=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>\frac{5}{8}
, then
G
is abelian. (In other words, the commutator word satisfies a gap also for infinite finitely generated groups.)

(2)

If
\mathbb{P}\left([x,y]=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>0
, then
G
is virtually abelian.

(The results of
[
1
]
and
[
28
]
are more general than for random walks, we quoted only the random walk results for simplicity). Similar results are proved for the nilpotent case in
[
23
]
.

In this paper we consider other examples of laws. Our goal is to answer the two questions above for various laws. Namely, do these laws satisfy a gap for infinite finitely generated groups? If the probability that a law is satisfied on a group is positive, does the group virtually satisfy the same law, or perhaps another one?
We start with sketching an example showing that very different behaviour may occur in the infinite case.

1.2.
A lamplighter example

Consider the group
G\coloneqq\mathrm{F}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
i.e. a lamplighter with the base group being
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
and the lamps being free groups with 2 generators (exact definitions will be given in Subsection
2.3
). We will use this group to show that the metabelian law
[[x,y],[z,w]]=1
has no gap for infinite groups. It is easy to see that
G
does not satisfy the metabelian law virtually (thus we get that the metabelian law satisfies neither a gap nor a positivity result with the same example).

Consider first a single commutator,
[x,y]
. The walker (i.e. the
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
projection) is at the identity. The lamps can be found on a random subset of places visited by the following process: do a random walk on
n
steps. Then do another random walk on
n
steps. Then repeat the first walker in reverse, and then the second.

The other commutator,
[z,w]
gives a second, independent, subset of a random loop. The crucial property of
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
that we exploit is that two such loops do not intersect (except at the identity) with positive probability (independent of the length of the loops). We conclude that with positive probability the lamps positions of
[x,y]
and
[z,w]
are disjoint. This, of course, means that these elements commute. This shows that the metabelian law does not satisfy positivity for infinite groups.

All this was using the natural generators of
G
. To get a no gap result we modify the generators as follows. Denote the generators of
\mathrm{F}_{2}
by
a
and
b
. Then the walker is allowed to act on the
a
lamp in its current position, but the
b
lamp is acted upon in a different position (shifted with respect to its current position by a fixed, large offset). See Figure
1
. This modification means that even when the loops performed by the walkers intersect, with high probability you will only have lamps of one type in the intersection (at each site, either both acted with an
a
or both acted with a
b
). This modification means that by changing the generators (their number remains
160
) one may make
\mathbb{P}([[x,y],[z,w]]=1\textrm{ on }G)
arbitrarily close to 1. This shows that the metabelian law does not even satisfy a gap. Full details of the proof can be found in §
6
.

Figure 1.
The solid lines are where
a
was deposited, and the dotted lines are where
b
was deposited. The proof works because the top solid loop does not intersect the bottom dotted loop, and vice versa.

It is interesting to note how residual finiteness enters into the picture. Recall that the metabelian law
does
satisfy a gap for finite groups
[
2
]
. This means (see §
3
) that it satisfies a gap for residually finite groups. Thus we get a new manifestation of the fact that a lamplighter with non-commutative lamps is not residually finite (this fact is far from new, see
[
5
]
).

By varying the base group one may get additional examples of laws with no gap. In §
11
we take the base group to be an infinite Burnside group to get that the law
[x^{k},y^{k}]
(with some restrictions on
k
) does not satisfy a gap. In §
7
we take as the base group a certain generalization of the infinite dihedral group in order to handle laws like
[x^{k},[y,z]]
(exact definition of what does “like” means will be found there).

1.3.
Power laws

We present two results about power laws. The first is about the law
x^{2}
. We have already mentioned the result of Mann that if
\mathbb{P}(x^{2}=1)\geq\varepsilon
on a finite group
G
then
G
is (
\varepsilon
-bounded)-by-abelian-by-(
\varepsilon
-bounded). This example prompted Leemann and de la Salle
[
18
]
to conjecture that if an infinite group
G
satisfies
x^{2}=1
with positive probability, it is virtually abelian. We prove this conjecture in Theorem
5.1
.

The infinite dihedral group satisfies the law
x^{2}=1
with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
but does not satisfy this law virtually. In Proposition
5.13
we show a generalization of this fact to any power. The example is the semi-direct product
\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
where the action of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
is given by powers of the companion matrix of
1+\dotsb+x^{m}
.

Moving from positivity results to gaps, we present one result on this topic. We show that the law
x^{3}=1
satisfies a gap when
\limsup
is replaced by
\liminf
in (
1
) (Theorem
5.6
). The case of powers bigger than 3 remains open.

1.4.
Additional results

For the case of
residually finite
groups, we show in §
3
that (under certain conditions), the answer to both positivity and gap questions is the same as the finite case.

In §
4
we show that for laws of the form
w^{\prime}=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}),x_{n+1}]
, a positive answer for
w
to one of these questions implies a positive answer for
w^{\prime}
to the same question, generalizing the case of the
k
-step nilpotent law.

We focused so far on probabilities with respect to random walk, but it seems quite natural to also consider probabilities taken uniformly on balls in the Cayley graph of
G
. We believe that these measures are less natural, and we demonstrate that with one result. In §
12
we show that many laws cannot satisfy a gap result with respect to uniform elements of balls. The example is simply the product with a finite group which does not satisfy the law.

1.5.
A lemma on random walks

We end this introduction mentioning one lemma for readers interested in random walks on groups. A well-known open problem in this area is whether random walk on any transient group spends on more than
r^{2}
time in a ball of radius
r
, with the best result known to us being that of Lyons, Peres, Sun and Zheng
[
20
]
. We use the techniques of
[
20
]
for the
path of a word
, namely for the elements of a group visited by
w(R^{(1)},\dotsc,R^{(d)})
, where
w
is some word in
d
letters and
R^{(1)},\dotsc,R^{(d)}
are
k
independent random walks. See the exact statement in §
10
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The probability of satisfying a law

By a
word
we mean a nontrivial element of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
denote the standard generators of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
. Any word
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
defines the
word map
on any group
G
, which we also denote
w\colon G^{d}\to G
, defined as follows: for
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
, the value of
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})
is given by substituting
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}
for
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
. We interpret any word as a law on groups by considering the equation
w(x_{1},\dots x_{d})=1
(where
1
is the identity element of
G
).

For a measure
\mu
on a group
G
, write
\mu
also for the measure
\mu\times\cdots\times\mu
induced on
G^{d}
. We use the ideas presented in
[
23
]
:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word.

(1)

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
G
. We define the
probability that
G
satisfies
w
with respect to
\mu
as

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\mu\left(\left\{(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})\in G^{d}:w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})=1\right\}\right).

(2)

Let
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
be a sequence of probability measures on
G
. We define the
probability that
G
satisfies
w
with respect to
M
as

\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right).

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. We present here several choices for sequences of probability measures on
G
, which we will consider throughout the paper.

Our main interest will be
random walk measures
. These are sequences of the form
M=\left\{\mu_{n}=\mu^{*n}\right\}
, where
\mu
is a finitely supported generating probability measure on a group
G
with
\mu(1)>0
. In other words,
\mu_{n}
is the probability measure induced by a random walk with step distribution
\mu
after
n
steps. It will occasionally be important to consider random walks also as a stochastic process (i.e. a measure on paths in the group) and then we will always use a right random walk, i.e.
R_{n+1}=R_{n}x_{n}
where the
x
are i.i.d. with law
\mu
.

Another type of sequences of interest is given in
[
28
]
as follows:

Definition 2.2
.

We say that a sequence of probability measures
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
on
G

measures index uniformly
, if
\mu_{n}(xH)\to 1/[G:H]
uniformly over all
x\in G
and all subgroups
H\leq G
. Similarly, for a sequence of random variables taking value in
G
we say that they measure index uniformly if their laws measure index uniformly.

For example, (lazy) random walk measures satisfy this property
[
28
, Theorems 1.11 and 1.12]
.

An additional way to define a sequence of measures on a group is by taking the uniform measures on balls centered at the identity. Formally, we take a finite symmetric generating set
S
of
G
which contains the identity, and let
\mu_{n}
be the uniform measure on
S^{n}
(i.e. the ball of radius
n
around the identity in the Cayley graph). While such sequences of measures usually do not measure index uniformly (for instance, see
[
28
, Remark after Theorem 1.12]
), they are of interest on their own. We discuss these measures in §
12
.

2.2.
Gaps and positivity

In this paper, we deal with two properties of laws. The first one concerns the values of the probabilities that the law is satisfied:

Definition 2.3
.

Fix a family of sequences of measures
\mathcal{M}
, and let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word.

(1)

We say that
w
satisfies a
gap
with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, if there is a function
\phi\colon\mathbb{N}\to(0,1)
such that for any
r
-generated group
G
and sequences of measures
M\in\mathcal{M}
on
G
, if
\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>1-\phi(r)
, then
G
satisfies
w
. In this case we also say that
w
satisfies a
\phi
-gap
.

(2)

If
w
satisfies a gap with respect to a constant function
\phi(r)=\varepsilon
, we say that
w
satisfies a
strong gap
(or an
\varepsilon
-gap
) with respect to
\mathcal{M}
. In this case, the probability that
w
is satisfied cannot lie in the interval
(1-\varepsilon,1)
.

We note that this definition might depend on the family of probability measures one considers. Unless explicitly mentioned, we will assume that
\mathcal{M}
is the family of random walk measures.

Summarizing the results mentioned in the introduction in this language, for finite groups it is known the the laws
[x,y]=1
,
x^{2}=1
,
x^{3}=1
,
x^{4}=1
,
[x,y,y]=1
and
[[x,y],[z,w]]=1
satisfy a strong gap, while
x^{n}=1
satisfies a weak gap. For finitely generated groups, it is known that
[x,y]=1
and
[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]=1
satisfy a strong gap.

We move to discuss the second property of laws we will study. We say that a group
virtually
satisfies a law
w
, if its has a finite index subgroup which satisfies
w
. If a group virtually satisfies a law, it has a positive probability to satisfy it. This leads to the following:

Definition 2.4
.

Fix a family of sequences of measures
\mathcal{M}
, and let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word.

(1)

We say that
w
satisfies a
positivity property
with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, if there is a function
\psi\colon(0,1)\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that the following holds: for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, any
r
-generated group
G
and any
M\in\mathcal{M}
on
G
, if
\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)\geq\varepsilon
, then
G
has a subgroup of index
\leq\psi(\varepsilon,r)
which satisfies
w
.

(2)

We say that
w
satisfies a
strong positivity property
with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, if it has a positivity property with respect to a function
\psi(\varepsilon,r)
which depends only on
\varepsilon
.

For example, Tointon
[
28
, Theorem 1.9]
showed that the commutativity law satisfies a strong positivity property.

Remark
.

If
G
virtually satisfies a law, it must completely satisfy some other law. In particular, if
G
contains a non-abelian free subgroup, it cannot virtually satisfy any law.

Indeed, let
\Lambda\leq G
be a finite index subgroup that satisfies a law
w
. There exists a normal subgroup
N\vartriangleleft G
with
N\leq\Lambda
and
[G:N]=k<\infty
. Therefore
g^{k}\in N\leq\Lambda
for all
g\in G
, so
G
satisfies the law
w(x_{1}^{k},\dots,x_{d}^{k})=1
.

Remark 2.5
.

If
G
virtually satisfies a law, then each subgroup
H
of
G
also virtually satisfies this law. Indeed, let
\Lambda
be a finite index subgroup of
G
that satisfies this law, then
\Lambda\cap H
is a finite index subgroup in
H
that also satisfies this law.

2.3.
Wreath products

A primary construction that we will use in this paper is the wreath product of two groups. We recall here the definition and a basic property regarding laws of wreath products.

Let
G,H
be two groups. The
wreath product
(or
lamplighter group
)
H\wr G
is the semidirect product
\left(\bigoplus_{g\in G}H\right)\rtimes G
, where
G
acts on
\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
by permuting the indices, i.e.

g\cdot(h_{a})_{a\in G}=(h_{g^{-1}a})_{a\in G}.

The group
G
is called the
base group
of
H\wr G
, and
H
is called the
lamp group
of
H\wr G
. Each element of
H\wr G
can be written in the form
(L,g)
, where
g\in G
is the
lamplighter position
, and
L\in\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
is the
lamp configuration
. For an element
(L,g)
we define its
support

\operatorname{supp}(L,g)=\{a\in G:L(a)\neq 1\}
.

We make the following observation:

Proposition 2.6
.

If
H
does not satisfy a law
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
, then for any infinite group
G
the lamplighter group
H\wr G
does not satisfy the law
w
virtually.

Proof.

Let us take
\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
, which we identify with the subgroup of all elements with trivial lamplighter position. Assume by contradiction that there exists a finite index subgroup
\Lambda<H\wr G
that satisfies the law
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
. Then note that
\Lambda\cap\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
is a finite index subgroup in
\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
. Let us take representatives
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
of the left cosets of
\bigoplus_{g\in G}H/(\Lambda\cap\bigoplus_{g\in G}H).
We take the set
A=\cup_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{supp}(t_{i})\subset G
and consider
g_{0}\in G\setminus A
. Let us consider
h_{1},\dots h_{d}\in H
such that
w(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})\neq 1
, and take
y_{1},\dots,y_{d}\in\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
such that
y_{i}(g_{0})=h_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,d
. Then for any
i=1,\dots,d
there exists
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
z_{i}:=t_{j}^{-1}y_{i}\in\Lambda\cap\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
, so
z_{i}(g_{0})=h_{i}
. Therefore, since
w(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})(g_{0})=w(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})
, we get that
w(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})\neq 1
. A contradiction.
∎

2.4.
Notation

A “word” is a sequence
w
of letters in some alphabet. We denote by
|w|
the number of letters in
w
. Let
G
and
H
be groups and let
g\in G
and
h\in H
. Then the notation
h\delta_{g}
refers to the element of
\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
which takes value
h
at
g
and takes the unit of
H
at any
g^{\prime}\neq g
.

We use the following standard group notation. We use
C_{G}(x)
for the centralizer of
x
in
G
, namely,
\{y\in G:[x,y]=1\}
. We use
C_{G}(H)=\bigcap_{h\in H}C_{G}(h)
. We use
Z(G)
for the center of
G
, namely,
\{g\in G:C_{G}(g)=G\}
.

We use
c
and
C
to denote constants that depend only on the law in question, and, for infinite groups, on the walk. Their value may change from line to line, and even within the same line. We use
c
for constants which are “sufficiently small” and
C
for constants which are “sufficiently large”. When we want to specify specific constants we will use
c^{\prime}
,
c^{\prime\prime}
, etc. These change value only from lemma to lemma.

3.
Residually finite groups

In this section we study the questions of gap and positivity for laws on residually finite groups. We show that, when working with sequences of probability measures that measure index uniformly, these questions are equivalent to the same questions on finite groups.

Let
G
be a group, and let
N\vartriangleleft G
be a normal subgroup. Any probability measure
\mu
on
G
induces a probability measure
\overline{\mu}
on
G/N
by taking the pushforward of
\mu
with respect to the natural projection
G\to G/N
. The following lemmas follow directly from the definition of measuring index uniformly and the pushforward measure:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
be a sequence of probability measures on
G
that measures index uniformly. Let
U_{G}
denote the uniform probability measure on
G
. Then
\mu_{n}\to U_{G}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
N\vartriangleleft G
be a normal subgroup. Let
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
be a sequence of probability measures on
G
, and let
\overline{M}=\left\{\overline{\mu_{n}}\right\}
. Let
w
be a word. Then

\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\;\mathrm{on}\;G\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\overline{M}}\left(w=1\;\mathrm{on}\;G/N\right).

Proof.

For
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\in G
, if
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})=1
in
G
, then
w(g_{1}N,\dots,g_{d}N)=1
in
G/N
, so the probability of satisfying
w
cannot decrease.
∎

We can now combine the above lemmas to study the general case of residually finite groups. Recall that
N\vartriangleleft_{f}G
if
N
is a normal subgroup of
G
of finite index.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated residually finite group, and let
M
be a sequence of probability measures on
G
that measures index uniformly. Then

\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\;\mathrm{on}\;G\right)=\inf\left\{\mathbb{P}\left(w=1\;\mathrm{on}\;G/N\right):N\vartriangleleft_{f}G\right\}.

Proof.

The left hand side is not bigger than the right hand side by Lemma
3.2
. For the opposite direction, as
G
is finitely generated and residually finite, one can find a sequence of finite indexed normal subgroups
N_{k}\vartriangleleft_{f}G
such that
N_{k+1}\subseteq N_{k}
and
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}N_{k}=1
. Therefore

\left\{w=1\textrm{ on }G\right\}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{w=1\textrm{ on }G/N_{k}\right\},

so by continuity of probability we have

\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G/N_{k}\right)\geq\inf\left\{\mathbb{P}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G/N\right):N\vartriangleleft_{f}G\right\}.

∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word, and let
\mathcal{M}
denote a family of sequences of probability measures that measure index uniformly.

(1)

If
w
satisfies a gap for all finite groups, then
w
satisfies a gap for all residually finite groups with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, with the same gap function
\varepsilon(r)
.

(2)

If
w
satisfies a positivity result for all finite groups, then
w
satisfies a positivity result for all residually finite groups with respect to
\mathcal{M}
.

It follows that if
w
satisfies a strong gap for all finite groups then it satisfies a strong gap for residually finite groups, because strong gap is simply a weak gap with a gap function
\varepsilon(r)
bounded below.

Proof.

Let
G
be an
r
-generated residually finite group, and let
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
be a sequence of probability measures on
G
that measures index uniformly.

(1)

This is a direct corollary of Proposition
3.3
.

(2)

Assume that
w
satisfies a weak positivity property for all finite groups. That is, there is a function
\psi\colon(0,1)\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and any finite
r
-generated group
\Lambda
, if
\mathbb{P}(w=1\textrm{ on }\Lambda)\geq\varepsilon
then
\Lambda
has a subgroup of index
\leq\psi(\varepsilon,r)
satisfying
w
.

If
\mathbb{P}_{M}\left(w=1\textrm{ on }G\right)\geq\varepsilon
, the same holds for
G/N
for any finite indexed normal subgroup
N\vartriangleleft G
. Therefore, for any such
N
, the group
G/N
contains a subgroup of index
\leq\psi(\varepsilon,r)
which satisfies
w
, so there is a subgroup
N\leq H\leq G
such that
[G:H]\leq\psi(\varepsilon,r)
and
w(H,\dots,H)\subseteq N
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the set of subgroups of
G
of index
\leq\psi(\varepsilon,r)
. As
G
is finitely generated, it has finitely many such subgroups and their number only depends on
r
and
\psi(\varepsilon,r)
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Corollary 1.1.2]
). Hence
K=\bigcap_{H\in\mathcal{T}}H
is a finite index subgroup of
G
, and its index is bounded by a function of
\varepsilon
and
r
. Note that
w(K,\dots,K)\subseteq\bigcap_{N\vartriangleleft_{f}G}N=\left\{e\right\}
, so
K
satisfies
w
, as required.∎

4.
Derived laws

Fix a word
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
. We define the
derived word of
w
to be
w^{\prime}=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),x_{d+1}]\in\mathrm{F}_{d+1}
. In this section we show that if
w
satisfies a gap or positivity result, then so does
w^{\prime}
. This is a result with similar flavor to results in
[
27
]
. We now state our main theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word, and consider
w^{\prime}\in\mathrm{F}_{d+1}
. Let
\mathcal{M}
be the family of sequences of probability measures that measure index uniformly. Then:

(1)

If
w
satisfies a gap with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, then
w^{\prime}
satisfies a gap with respect to
\mathcal{M}
;

(2)

If
w
satisfies a positivity result with respect to
\mathcal{M}
, then
w^{\prime}
satisfies a positivity result with respect to
\mathcal{M}
.

Our strategy is similar to the proofs of
[
14
, Proposition 2.3]
and
[
27
, Proposition 1.8]
. We first prove a version of
[
28
, Proposition 2.1]
:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
G
be a group, and let
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
be two sequences of random variables taking value in
G
. Assume that
y_{n}
measures index uniformly and that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}([x_{n},y_{n}]=1)>\varepsilon.

Then, for any
\delta>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}([G:C_{G}(x_{n})]<1/\delta)>\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{1-\delta}.

As usual,
C_{G}(x)
is the centralizer of
x
in
G
.

Proof.

By definition, there is a sequence
\left\{n_{k}\right\}
of nonnegative integers such that
\mathbb{P}([x_{n_{k}},y_{n_{k}}]=1)>\varepsilon
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}[y_{n_{k}}\in C_{G}(x_{n_{k}})]>\varepsilon
. Write

A=\left\{x\in G:[G:C_{G}(x)]<1/\delta\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle<\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\in A)+\mathbb{E}[y_{n_{k}}\in C_{G}(x_{n_{k}})\,|\,x\notin A]\cdot\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\notin A)\leq

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\in A)+\left(\frac{1}{[G:C_{G}(x)]}+o(1)\right)(1-\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\in A))\leq

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\in A)+\delta(1-\mathbb{P}(x_{n_{k}}\in A))+o(1)

where the second inequality used the fact that
y_{n}
measures index uniformly. The conclusion follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

(1)

Suppose that
w
satisfies a gap with respect to a function
\phi
. We may assume that
\phi(r)>\frac{1}{2}
for all
r
. Let
G
be an
r
-generated group, and let
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d+1}
be sequences of random variables measuring index uniformly on
G
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([w(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}),x_{d+1}]=1\textrm{ on }G\right)=\varepsilon>\frac{\phi(r)+1}{2}

(each
x_{i}
is a sequence of variables depending on
n
, but we suppress the dependence on
n
in the notation). We apply Lemma
4.2
with
x_{\textrm{{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:markov}}}}=w(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d})
,
y_{\textrm{{Lemma\penalty\ \ref{lem:markov}}}}=x_{d+1}
and
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
. Note that it is important here that in Lemma
4.2
only
y
needs to measure index uniformly, and
x
does not need to do that. We get

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([G:C_{G}(w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}))]<2\right)>\frac{\varepsilon-\frac{1}{2}}{1-\frac{1}{2}}>\phi(r).

Of course, if
[G:C_{G}(w(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d})]<2
then in fact
w(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d})\in Z(G)
, the center of
G
.

Consider the
r
-generated group
\overline{G}=G/Z(G)
. Using the induced sequences of random variables
\overline{x_{i}}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(w(\overline{x_{1}},\dotsc,\overline{x_{d}})=1\textrm{ on }\overline{G}\right)>\phi(r)
; therefore
\overline{G}
satisfies
w
. But then
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in Z(G)
for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in G
, so
G
satisfies
w^{\prime}
.

From the proof we also see that if
w
satisfies a strong gap, i.e.
\phi
is constant, then so does
w^{\prime}
.

(2)

Assume that
w
satisfies a positivity property with respect to a function
\psi\colon(0,1)\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
G
be an
r
-generated group and let
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d+1}
be random variables on
G
measuring index uniformly such that

\mathbb{P}\left(w^{\prime}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d+1})=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>\varepsilon.

Using Lemma
4.2
as in the previous part we get

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([G:C_{G}(w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}))]<\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)>\frac{\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}{1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}.

Let
N
denote the intersection of all subgroups of
G
of index at most
\frac{2}{\varepsilon}
. This is a normal subgroup of
G
, and its index is bounded as a function of
r
and
\frac{2}{\varepsilon}
. Then we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in C_{G}(N)\right)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}.

Consider the group
\overline{G}=G/C_{G}(N)
. By the above,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})C_{G}(N)=C_{G}(N)\right)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon}.

Therefore
G/C_{G}(N)
contains a subgroup
H/C_{G}(N)
of index bounded by
\psi(\frac{\varepsilon}{2-\varepsilon},r)
that satisfies
w
. We therefore conclude that
H\cap N
satisfies
w^{\prime}
, and its index is bounded as a function of
\varepsilon
and
r
.∎

5.
Power laws

5.1.
Positivity for
x^{2}=1

In this section we prove

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. If

\mathbb{P}\left(x^{2}=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>0

with respect to some random walk, then
G
is virtually abelian.

Recall that our standing assumption is that all random walks are lazy, i.e. have positive probability to stay in the same place for one turn. This fact is not crucial (the theorem holds with essentially the same proof without this assumption), but is convenient because it implies that random walk measures index uniformly.

For the rest of this subsection, we prove the above theorem. Let
G
be a finitely generated group, and let
R_{n}
be a (lazy) random walk on
G
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{2}=1)=\alpha>0.

Take a sequence
\left\{m_{k}\right\}
such that
\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k}}^{2}=1)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
for all
k
.

Lemma 5.2
.

There exists
k_{0}
such that for any
k\geq k_{0}
,

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k}+s}^{2}=1)>\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}.

Proof.

Suppose on the contrary that no such
k_{0}
exists, i.e. there are infinitely many values of
k
such that

(2)

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k}+s}^{2}=1)\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}.

Write
M=\left\lceil\frac{4}{\alpha}\right\rceil
. We construct a sequence
\left\{k^{\prime}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{M}
as follows. Let
k^{\prime}_{1}
be the first index satisfying (
2
). Let
k^{\prime}_{2}
be the first index satisfying (
2
) and such that

\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{1}}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{2}}}^{2}=1)<\frac{\alpha}{4}

(which exists because
\frac{\alpha}{4}>\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}
). Let
k^{\prime}_{3}
be the first index satisfying (
2
) and such that

\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{j}}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{3}}}^{2}=1)<\frac{\alpha}{8}\textrm{ for }j=1,2.

We continue inductively to define
\left\{k^{\prime}_{i}\right\}
for
1\leq i\leq M
, where
k^{\prime}_{i}
is chosen as the first index satisfying (
2
) such that

\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{j}}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{i}}}^{2}=1)<\frac{\alpha}{4(i-1)}\textrm{ for }j=1,\dots,i-1.

Such indices exist since for any
i\leq M
one has
\frac{\alpha}{4(i-1)}>\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}
.

As
\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{i}}}^{2}=1)\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}
for any
1\leq i\leq M
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}(R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{i}}}^{2}=1,R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{1}}}^{2}\neq 1,\dots,R_{m_{k^{\prime}_{i-1}}}^{2}\neq 1)>\frac{\alpha}{2}-(i-1)\cdot\frac{\alpha}{4(i-1)}=\frac{\alpha}{4}.

But this is a sequence of
M=\left\lceil\frac{4}{\alpha}\right\rceil
pairwise disjoint events with probability larger than
\frac{\alpha}{4}
, a contradiction.
∎

Write
\beta=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([G:C_{G}(R_{n})]\leq\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}.

As usual,
C_{G}(R_{n})
is the centralizer of
R_{n}
in
G
.

Proof.

Fix one
n
such that

(3)

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{2}=1,R_{n+s}^{2}=1)>\beta>0.

By the previous lemma, there are infinitely many such
n
. Then there is a sequence
s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots
such that

\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{2}=1,R_{n+s_{i}}^{2}=1)\geq\beta.

Write
u=R_{n}^{-1}R_{n+s_{i}}
, which has the same distribution as
R_{s_{i}}
and is independent of
R_{n}
. Note that the equations
R_{n}^{2}=R_{n+s_{i}}^{2}=1
imply that
u^{-1}=R_{n}uR_{n}^{-1}
, so

\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}=R_{n}uR_{n}^{-1})\geq\beta.

If
R^{\prime}_{n}
is an independent copy distributed like
R_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(u^{-1}=R_{n}uR_{n}^{-1}=R^{\prime}_{n}u(R^{\prime}_{n})^{-1})\geq\beta^{2},

so

\mathbb{P}([(R^{\prime}_{n})^{-1}R_{n},u]=1)\geq\beta^{2}.

As
(R^{\prime}_{n})^{-1}R_{n}
has the same distribution as
R_{2n}
, and both are independent of
u
,

\mathbb{P}([R_{2n},u]=1)\geq\beta^{2}.

Hence it follows (similarly to Lemma
4.2
),

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{P}\left([R_{2n},u]=1:R_{2n}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}.

Since
u
is distributed like a random walk of length
s_{i}
and is independent of
R_{2n}
, taking
i
to infinity gives

\mathbb{P}\left([G:C_{G}(R_{2n})]\leq\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2}.

By Lemma
5.2
there are infinitely many
n
such that (
3
) holds. This proves the lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Since
G
is finitely generated, it has finitely many subgroups of any finite index. Therefore, writing

Q=\bigcap_{H\leq G,[G:H]\leq 2/\beta^{2}}H,

we have
[G:Q]<\infty
.

Now,

\mathbb{P}(R_{2n}\in C_{G}(Q))=\mathbb{P}([R_{2n},Q]=1)\geq\frac{1}{2}\beta^{2},

so
[G:C_{G}(Q)]\leq\frac{2}{\beta^{2}}<\infty
.
Therefore
Q\cap C_{G}(Q)
is an abelian subgroup of
G
of finite index, as required.
∎

We give an immediate corollary from the above theorem.

Corollary 5.4
.

(1)

The law
x^{2}=1
satisfies a gap.

(2)

The value of
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{2}=1)
does not depend on the random walk’s step distribution
\mu
, and it is a limit rather than a
\limsup
.

Proof.

We first prove that
x^{2}=1
satisfies a gap. By Theorem
5.1
, any group which satisfies
x^{2}=1
with positive probability is virtually abelian, and thus residually finite. Therefore the gap follows from the gap in the finite case.

For the second part, if
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{2}=1)>0
for some random walk
R_{n}
on
G
, Theorem
5.1
shows that
G
is virtually abelian. In particular,
G
is virtually nilpotent, so the corollary follows from
[
23
, Corollary 1.14]
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.1
(
2
) can be generalized to show the following:

Proposition 5.5
.

If a group
G
satisfies
[x^{2},y]
probabilistically,
G
is virtually
2
-step nilpotent.

The changes required are straightforward and we omit the details.

5.2.
Strong gap for
x^{3}=1

In this subsection, our aim is to prove the following result:

Theorem 5.6
.

Consider a finitely generated group
G
with a finite symmetric generating set
S
containing the identity, and let
R_{n}
be a random walk on
G
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that if

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}\left(R_{n}^{3}=1\right)>1-\varepsilon

then
x^{3}=1
holds in
G
(and therefore
G
is finite).

Our proof also finds an explicit value of
\varepsilon
such that the above theorem holds. However, we believe that this
\varepsilon
is not optimal, and that the theorem can be proved for
\limsup
instead of
\liminf
.

We prove this result in several steps. Let
G
be a group, and let
x,y,z,w\in G
. A product of
x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1},z^{\pm 1},w^{\pm 1}
is called
simple
if any letter appears at most once (thus there are only finitely many simple products of any four elements). We say that
x,y,z,w
are
good
if any simple product of them has order
3
. For example, if
x,y,z,w
are good then
(z^{-1}xw)^{3}=1
and
(yx^{-1}w^{-1}z)^{3}=1
, but not necessarily
(xyx^{-1}z)^{3}=1
.

Note that the assumption of the theorem suggest that if
x,y,z,w
are independent random walks with respect to
S
, then
x,y,z,w
are good with high probability. Indeed, any simple product of
x,y,z,w
is also distributed like a random walk with respect to
S
(with a different number of steps), and therefore has order
3
with high probability.

Our method of proof is as follows. We prove that if
x,y,z,w
are good, then
[x,y,z,w]=1
(we originally verified this with a GAP program, but here we give a human proof of this fact). Therefore, if
\mathbb{P}\left(R_{n}^{3}=1\right)>1-\varepsilon
for small enough
\varepsilon
, the group
G
is nilpotent with positive probability. Therefore,
G
is virtually nilpotent, so it is residually finite, and as
x^{3}=1
has a gap on finite groups we are finished.

Recall the following standard notation. For any
a,b\in G
write
a^{b}=bab^{-1}
, and for any
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in G
we defined inductively
[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]=[[a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}],a_{n}]
.

Lemma 5.7
.

If
b^{3}=(ab)^{3}=(ab^{-1})^{3}=1
, then
[a^{b},a]=1
.

Proof.

This follows from the identity

[a^{b},a]=\left(\left(ab^{-1}\right)^{b}\right)^{3}\left(b^{2}a^{-1}\right)^{3}\left(\left(b^{-1}\right)^{a}\right)^{3}

which holds in any group.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

If
[a^{b},a]=1
and
a^{3}=1
then
[a,b]^{3}=1
,
[b,a,a]=1
and
[a,b]^{-1}=[a^{-1},b]
.

Proof.

Note that
[b,a]=a^{b}a^{-1}
, so

(4)

\displaystyle[a,b]^{3}

\displaystyle=\left(a^{b}a^{-1}\right)^{3}=(a^{b})^{3}a^{-3}=1

\displaystyle[b,a,a]

\displaystyle=[[b,a],a]=[a^{b}a^{-1},a]=1

(5)

\displaystyle[a,b]^{-1}

\displaystyle=bab^{-1}a^{-1}=a^{b}a^{-1}=a^{-1}a^{b}=a^{-1}bab^{-1}=[a^{-1},b].

The lemma is thus proved.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

If
[a^{b},a]=[a^{c},a]=[a^{b},a^{c}]=[b^{a},b]=[(bc)^{a},bc]=[c^{b},c]=[c^{ba},c]=1
, then
[a,b,c]^{-1}=[a,c,b]
.

Proof.

In any group

(6)

[a,bc]=[a,b][a,c]^{b}\textrm{ and }[ab,c]=[b,c]^{a}[a,c],

so

[a,bc,c]=[[a,b][a,c]^{b},c]=[[a,c]^{b},c]^{[a,b]}[a,b,c].

Note that
[a,c]^{b}=(c^{a}c^{-1})^{b}=c^{ba}(c^{-1})^{b}
, so by the assumptions
[[a,c]^{b},c]=1
, and thus

[a,bc,c]=[a,b,c].

In a similar manner,

[a,bc,b]=[[a,b][a,c]^{b},b]=[[a,c]^{b},b]^{[a,b]}[a,b,b]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}[[a,c]^{b},b]^{[a,b]}=[[a,c]^{[a,b]b},b^{[a,b]}].

As
[b^{a},b]=1
, the elements
b
and
[a,b]
commute, which gives
(*)
and also
b^{[a,b]}=b
. In addition,
[a,b]=a(a^{-1})^{b}
and
[a,c]=a(a^{-1})^{c}
, so our assumptions imply that
[a,b]
and
[a,c]

5.3.
Non-Positivity result for
x^{m}=1

We now show that the law
x^{m}=1
does not satisfy a positivity property. We use an example similar to the Frobenius group example which appeared in
[
16
]
.

Let us choose a matrix
A\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m-1}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
A^{m}=I
and
\det(A-I)\neq 0
. Then we have
{(A-I)(A^{m-1}+\cdots+A+I)=0}
and since
\det(A-I)\neq 0
we have
A^{m-1}+\cdots+A+I=0
. For example, one can take the companion matrix of
1+x+\dotsb+x^{m-1}
, namely the matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1\cdots m-1}
with

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i=j+1,\\
-1,&\text{if }j=m-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Consider the semidirect product
G=\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, namely, with the action of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
given by powers of
A
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
G=\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, and let
R_{n}
be a (lazy) random walk on
G
. Then

x^{m}=1
with probability at least
\frac{\phi(m)}{n}
with respect to the random walk
R_{n}
, where
\phi(m)
is the Euler’s totient function, but
G
does not satisfy
x^{m}=1
virtually.

Proof.

Let us take
(v,k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have

\displaystyle(v,k)^{m}

\displaystyle=((A^{k(m-1)}+\cdots+A^{2k}+A^{k}+I)v,mk)

\displaystyle=((A^{k(m-1)}+\cdots+A^{2k}+A^{k}+I)v,0).

If
k
and
m
are coprime, then the formula above can simplified and we get

(v,k)^{m}=((A^{m-1}+\cdots+A+I)v,0)=(0,0).

Let
X_{k}=\{(v,k),\text{ where }v\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}\}\subset G
. We note that
\mathbb{P}(R_{n}\in X_{k})\to\frac{1}{m}
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{m}=1)\geq\mathbb{P}(R_{n}\in\cup_{\gcd(k,m)=1}X_{k})\to\frac{\phi(m)}{m}
, where
\gcd(k,m)
is the greatest common divisor and this proves the first claim.

Now we show that
G
does not contain a torsion finite index subgroup of exponent
n
. First note that
X_{0}
is a subgroup of
G
of index
m
such that for any
x\in X_{0}\setminus{\{0\}}
we have
x^{m}\neq 1
, so
X_{0}
does not satisfy the law
x^{m}=1
virtually. Therefore, by Remark
2.5
, the group
G
does not satisfy the law virtually.
∎

Remark 5.14
.

Let us note that we can get a higher probability by considering the group
G=\mathbb{Z}^{p-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, where
p
the largest prime factor of
m
.
G
satisfies
x^{p}=1
and hence
x^{m}=1
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{p}=\frac{\varphi(p)}{p}\geq\frac{\varphi(m)}{m}
. It follows from the proof that
G
does not satisfy the law
x^{m}=1
virtually,

6.
Commutators of balanced laws

In the coming sections, we will be interested in laws of the form
[w_{1},w_{2}]
. We will usually assume that
w_{1},w_{2}
are laws on disjoint letters. We will show that in many cases, such laws do not satisfy a gap result, and also do not satisfy a positivity result.

In fact, our results are somewhat stronger. We will show that for such laws, we can find a group
\Gamma
, which contains a non-abelian free group as a subgroup, and satisfies the following: one can find generating sets
S
of
\Gamma
so that the probabilty that
\Gamma
satisfies
[w_{1},w_{2}]
with respect to an
S
-random walk can be arbitrarily close to
1
.

We make the following differentiation. We call a law
balanced
if it has trivial abelianization. We will separate our discussion of commutators of laws according to whether the inner laws are balanced or not.

Remark 6.1
.

If
G
has an element of infinite order and
w
is a non-balanced law, then
G
cannot satisfy
w
virtually. Indeed, let
H_{0}\cong\mathbb{Z}
be a subgroup generated by an element of infinite order and
\Lambda
be a finite index subgroup of
G
, then
\Lambda\cap H_{0}
is a non-trivial finite index subgroup of
H_{0}
. Let
w=w(x_{1},\dots x_{n})
be a non-balanced law and the total degree of some variable
x_{k}
is not zero. Let us fix some
a\neq 1
in
\Lambda\cap H_{0}
. For
x_{i}=1
for
i\neq k
and
x_{k}=a
we have
w(x_{1},\dots x_{n})\neq 1
, so any finite index subgroup
\Lambda
does not satisfy
w
and
G
does not satisfy
w
virtually.

In this section we discuss the case of a commutator of two balanced laws. We first present the proof for the metabelian law
[[x,y],[z,w]]=1
; we then generalize it to an arbitrary commutator law
[w_{1},w_{2}]=1
(where
w_{1},w_{2}
are balanced).

6.1.
Word paths of random walks

Definition 6.2
.

For a word
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
and random walks
R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)}
, we write
\gamma=\gamma(w;R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)})
to be the path defined by
w
when substituting the random walks
R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)}
. Namely, if
w=a_{1}^{\varepsilon_{1}}\dotsb a_{\ell}^{\varepsilon_{l}}
where
a_{j}\in\{1,\dotsc,d\}
and
\varepsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
. Then
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\dotsc,\gamma_{\ell n})
with

(12)

\gamma_{jn+k}=\begin{cases}\big(R_{n}^{(a_{1})}\big)^{\varepsilon_{1}}\dotsb\big(R_{n}^{(a_{j})}\big)^{\varepsilon_{j+}}R_{k}^{(a_{j+1})}&\varepsilon_{j+1}=1\\
\big(R_{n}^{(a_{1})}\big)^{\varepsilon_{1}}\dotsb\big(R_{n}^{(a_{j})}\big)^{\varepsilon_{j}}\big(R_{n}^{(a_{j+1})}\big)^{-1}R_{n-k}^{(a_{j+1})}&\varepsilon_{j+1}=-1.\end{cases}

for all
j\in\{0,\dotsc,\ell-1\}
and
k\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, and with
\gamma_{0}
being the unit of the group.

In words, the path is constructed by first following
(R_{n}^{(i_{1})})^{\varepsilon_{1}}
, then following the steps of
(R_{n}^{(i_{2})})^{\varepsilon_{2}}
and so on. In particular, it is indeed a path in the Cayley graph, i.e.
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i+1}
are always neighbors.

6.2.
The metabelian law

We turn to studying the law
[[x,y],[z,w]]=1
. Our aim is to show that this law does not satisfy a general gap result as well as any positivity result. We do this by studying the wreath product
H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
for a non-abelian group
H
, and proving the following theorem:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
H
be a non-abelian group generated by two elements
a,b
, and consider the wreath product
\Gamma=H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a symmetric generating set
S
of
\Gamma
of size
160
such that, if
X_{n},Y_{n},Z_{n},W_{n}
are independent simple random walks on
\Gamma
where each step is uniform on
S
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([[X_{n},Y_{n}],[Z_{n},W_{n}]]=1\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

In other words,
\Gamma
satisfies the metabelian law with probability
\geq 1-\varepsilon
with respect to the simple random walk induced by
S
.

In fact, the generating set can be taken as follows: Let
\pm e_{i}
denote the
10
standard generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
. We then take
S=\{s_{1}ms_{2}:s_{1},s_{2}\in\{(a^{\pm 1}\delta_{\underline{0}},\underline{0}),(b^{\pm 1}\delta_{ke_{1}},\underline{0})\},m\in\{(\underline{\mathrm{id}_{H}},\pm e_{i})\}\}
for a large enough
k
.

Remarks
.

(1)

The same theorem also holds for random walks when taking the following
14
generators:
\pm e_{i}
,
(a^{\pm 1}\delta_{\underline{0}},\underline{0})
and
(b^{\pm 1}\delta_{ke_{1}},\underline{0})
. However, we work with the larger generating set to simplify the proof.

(2)

A similar result also holds when
H
is generated by more than
2
elements. In such cases, one needs to choose a finite generating set
S
of
H
, and for the generating set of
H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
apply each generator in a “far away” position from the others.

(3)

One can use this result to find a sequence of probability measures
M=\left\{\mu_{n}\right\}
that measures index uniformly, such that
\mathbb{P}_{M}\left([[x,y],[z,w]]=1\textrm{ on }\Gamma\right)=1
. To do this, for each
n
one takes a generating set
S^{(n)}
of
\Gamma
such that one has
\mathbb{P}_{S^{(n)}}\left([[x,y],[z,w]]=1\textrm{ on }G\right)>1-\frac{1}{n}
. For each
n
, take
\mu_{n}
to be the distribution of a random walk with step distribution uniform on
S^{(n)}
, after many steps. By taking this number of steps to be large enough, one can make sure that the supports of
\mu_{n}
cover all of
G
,
M
measures index uniformly, and
\mathbb{P}_{M}\left([[x,y],[z,w]]=1\textrm{ on }\Gamma\right)=1
.

Before proving the theorem, let us mention its implications. Taking
H=\mathrm{F}_{2}
to be the free group on
2
generators, we get that the group
F_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
satisfies the metabelian law with probability arbitrarily close to 1.
Because of its free subgroups it does not satisfy any law, and hence does not satisfy and law virtually. Thus the metabelian law does not satisfy neither gap nor positivity.

We turn to the proof of Theorem
6.3
. We will need the following observation on commutators in wreath products:

Fact 6.4
.

Let
L_{1},L_{2}\in\bigoplus_{g\in G}H
. If for any
x\in G
we have
[L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)]=\mathrm{id}_{H}
, then in the wreath product
H\wr G
we have
[(L_{1},\mathrm{id}_{G}),(L_{2},\mathrm{id}_{G})]=\mathrm{id}_{H\wr G}
.

We will also need several estimates on intersections of random paths in
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
, given by the following two lemmas. All random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
are taken with respect to the standard generating set.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\gamma=(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=(Y_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be two independent simple random walks paths on
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
starting at
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
respectively. Then there is a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\right)<C\cdot d(x_{0},y_{0})^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Here and below the notation
\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}
for two paths stands for the set of vertices appearing in both paths.

Proof.

Let
r=d(x_{0},y_{0})
, and denote by
K=|\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}|
the number of intersections between the paths. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\right)=\mathbb{P}(K\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}[K]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=Y_{m})=\sum_{m+n\geq r}\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=Y_{m}).

Let
S_{n}
be a simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
. Then
Y_{m}-X_{n}-(y_{0}-x_{0})
has the same distribution as
S_{n+m}
. It is well known that
\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=x)\leq Cn^{-5/2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{5}
. Hence

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\right)\leq\sum_{n+m\geq r}\mathbb{P}(S_{n+m}=x_{0}-y_{0})\leq\sum_{n+m\geq r}C\frac{1}{(n+m)^{\frac{5}{2}}}\leq C^{\prime}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}

as required.
∎

We remark that the correct asymptotic behavior is
\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\varnothing)\approx d(x_{0},y_{0})^{-1}
, but we will have no use for this extra precision, so we will not prove it.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\{X_{i},Y_{i},Z_{i},W_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be four simple random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
.
Consider each of the random walks as a path in
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
, and denote by
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
the paths (of length
4n
) described by the commutators
[X_{n},Y_{n}],[Z_{n},W_{n}]
respectively. Denote by
\gamma^{\prime}+v
the translation of
\gamma^{\prime}
by the vector
v
.

Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
k_{0}
, independent of
n
, such that for
k\geq k_{0}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap(\gamma^{\prime}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing\right)<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Notice that each of the commutator paths is a loop starting at the origin that can be divided into two simple random walk paths of length
2n
each: for instance,
\gamma
can be divided into
X_{n}Y_{n}
and
\overleftarrow{Y_{n}}\overleftarrow{X_{n}}
. The arrow indicates that the path is traversed in the opposite direction, though this will not matter to us. Therefore by symmetry

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap(\gamma^{\prime}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(X_{n}Y_{n}\cap(Z_{n}W_{n}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing)+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(\overleftarrow{Y_{n}}\overleftarrow{X_{n}}\cap(Z_{n}W_{n}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing)+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(X_{n}Y_{n}\cap(\overleftarrow{W_{n}}\overleftarrow{Z_{n}}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing)+

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(\overleftarrow{Y_{n}}\overleftarrow{X_{n}}\cap(\overleftarrow{W_{n}}\overleftarrow{Z_{n}}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing).

Each of the terms can be bounded above by the intersection probability of two infinite simple random walks in
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
starting distance
k
apart. By Lemma
6.5
this is bounded by
Ck^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, so the lemma follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main theorem.

.

(2)

x\notin\gamma^{\prime}\cup(ke_{1}+\gamma^{\prime})
. In this case
L_{2}(x)=\mathrm{id}_{H}
hence
[L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)]=\mathrm{id}_{H}
.

(3)

x\in(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime})\setminus((ke_{1}+\gamma)\cup(ke_{1}+\gamma^{\prime}))
. In this case
L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)\in A
and thus
[L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)]=\mathrm{id}_{H}
.

(4)

x\in((ke_{1}+\gamma)\cap(ke_{1}+\gamma^{\prime}))\setminus(\gamma\cup\gamma^{\prime})
. In this case
L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)\in B
and thus
[L_{1}(x),L_{2}(x)]=\mathrm{id}_{H}
.

It now follows from Fact
6.4
that
[[X_{n},Y_{n}],[Z_{n},W_{n}]]
is trivial. Therefore

\mathbb{P}\left([[X_{n},Y_{n}],[Z_{n},W_{n}]]=1\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap(ke_{1}+\gamma^{\prime})=(\gamma+ke_{1})\cap\gamma^{\prime}=\varnothing\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

as required.
∎

6.3.
The general case

The ideas of the previous theorem can be generalized to give the following result:

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
w_{1}\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
and
w_{2}\in\mathrm{F}_{d^{\prime}}
be two balanced laws such that
[w_{1},w_{2}]
is not trivial. Let
H
be a non-abelian group generated by two elements
a,b
, and consider the wreath product
\Gamma=H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a symmetric generating set
S
of
\Gamma
of size
160
such that, if
R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)},S_{n}^{(1)},\dots,S_{n}^{(d^{\prime})}
are independent simple random walks on
\Gamma
where each step is uniform on
S
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([w_{1}(R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)}),w_{2}(S_{n}^{(1)},\dots,S_{n}^{(d^{\prime})})]=1\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

In fact, the generating set
S
can be taken as in Theorem
6.3
.

In particular, for any two balanced laws
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
the law
[w_{1},w_{2}]
does not satisfy a gap or a positivity result.

The proof of this theorem will be similar to the proof of Theorem
6.3
.
We consider again the group
\Gamma=H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5}
with the same generating set as above.

We first give an analog of Lemma
6.5
on the intersections of such paths. Note that these are no longer random walk paths, but can still be decomposed into segments of random walks.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)}
and
S_{n}^{(1)},\dots,S_{n}^{(d^{\prime})}
be
d+d^{\prime}
independent random walks on
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
of length
n
. Set
\gamma=\gamma(w_{1},R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)})
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma(w_{2},S_{n}^{(1)},\dots,S_{n}^{(d^{\prime})})
to be the corresponding paths.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
k_{0}
, independent of
n
, such that for
k\geq k_{0}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap(\gamma^{\prime}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing\right)<\varepsilon.

Proof.

Denote by
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma^{\prime}_{i}
the
i
’th step in
\gamma
and in
\gamma^{\prime}
. We begin as in Lemma
6.5
: Denote by
K=|\gamma\cap(\gamma^{\prime}+ke_{1})|
the number of intersections between the paths. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\gamma\cap(\gamma^{\prime}+ke_{1})\neq\varnothing\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(K\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}[K]\leq\sum_{i=0}^{|\gamma|}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{|\gamma^{\prime}|}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{i}=\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}+ke_{1})=

(13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{|\gamma|}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{|\gamma^{\prime}|}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=ke_{1}).

Examining the definition of
\gamma
(
12
) we see that
\gamma_{i}
is the sum of several copies of various
R_{n}^{(j)}
, some with positive sign and some with negative sign, as well as one copy of
R_{k}^{(j_{0})}
for some
j_{0}
(coming from the random walk path on which the
i
’th step lies). We wish to write
\gamma
as a combination of
independent
random walks, so we write
R_{n}^{(j_{0})}=R_{k}^{(j_{0})}+(R_{n}^{(j_{0})}-R_{k}^{(j_{0})})
and note that
R_{n}^{(j_{0})}-R_{k}^{(j_{0})}
is itself a random walk, and is independent of
R_{k}^{(j_{0})}
(and, of course, of the other
R_{n}^{(j)}
). We have thus arrived at a representation

\gamma_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}b_{j}X_{m_{j}}^{(j)}

where
b_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m_{j}=n
except, possibly, for two values of
j
, and
X^{(j)}
are independent random walks. Further, each value of
m_{j}
different from
n
appears for at most
2|w|
different values of
i
, and each
|b_{j}|\leq|w_{1}|
.

Repeating this calculation for
\gamma^{\prime}
we may write

\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d+d^{\prime}+2}b_{j}X_{m_{j}}^{(j)}.

Rearrange the nonzero terms
X^{(j)}
so that
m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq\dotsb
.
Let
l=\max\left\{|w_{1}|,|w_{2}|\right\}
. As
|b_{j}|\leq l
for all
j
, we get that if
m_{1}<\frac{k}{l(d+d^{\prime}+2)}=c^{\prime}k
, then
|\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}|<k
, and cannot be equal to
ke_{1}
.
Note that after rearranging, the number of
(i,i^{\prime})
for which
m_{1}=M
is at most
2Ml^{2}
, for any
M<n
. For
M=n
it will be enough to use the trivial bound that the number of couples
(i,i^{\prime})
with
m_{1}=n
is smaller than the total number of couples,
|w_{1}||w_{2}|n^{2}
.

Condition on the values of
X_{m_{2}}^{(2)},\dots,X_{m_{d+d^{\prime}+2}}^{(d+d^{\prime}+2)}
. We get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=ke_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=ke_{1}\,\bigg|\,\sum_{j=2}^{d+d^{\prime}+2}b_{j}X_{m_{j}}^{(j)}=v\bigg)

\displaystyle=\max_{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{P}\big(b_{1}X_{m_{1}}^{(1)}=ke_{1}-v\big)\leq Cm_{1}^{-5/2}.

Using the multiplicity of possible values for
m_{1}
explained above and the fact that if
m_{1}<c^{\prime}k
then this probability is zero allows to estimate the sum in (
13
),

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{|\gamma|}\sum_{i^{\prime}=0}^{|\gamma^{\prime}|}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=ke_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{M=c^{\prime}k}^{n}\sum_{(i,i^{\prime}):m_{1}=M}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{i}-\gamma^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}=ke_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{M=c^{\prime}k}^{n-1}2Ml^{2}\cdot CM^{-5/2}+l^{2}n^{2}\cdot Cn^{-5/2}\leq Ck^{-1/2}

(the last constant may depend on
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
). With (
13
) the lemma is proved.
∎

We can now prove the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
6.7
.

This is essentially the same proof as Theorem
6.3
. Since tha base group
\mathbb{Z}^{5}
is abelian and the laws
w_{1},w_{2}
are balanced, each one of them defines a loop. By Lemma
6.8
, the probability that the
w_{1}
-loop and the translation of the
w_{2}
-loop are disjoint (and vice versa) can be made arbitrarily close to
1
by choosing
k
large enough. The rest of the proof remains verbatim as the proof of the metabelian case.
∎

7.
Commutators of a power and a balanced law

Let us take a matrix
A_{0}=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
of a cyclic permutation, that is

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if}\quad j-i\equiv 1\pmod{m},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For any
d
that divides
m
, let us consider the subspace
V_{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
defined by

V_{d}=\left\{(x_{1},\cdots x_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}:\forall 1\leq i\leq\frac{m}{d}:\quad x_{i}+x_{i+\frac{m}{d}}+\cdots+x_{i+\frac{(d-1)m}{d}}=0\right\}.

We have
\dim V_{d}=m-\frac{m}{d}
, and each
V_{d}
is
A_{0}
-invariant. Let us fix a primitive
m
-th root of unity
\xi
. The vectors
v_{k}:=(1,\xi^{k},\xi^{2k},\cdots,\xi^{(m-1)k})
are eigenvectors of
A_{0}
, i.e.
A_{0}v_{k}=\xi^{k}v_{k}
. Note also that
v_{k}\in V_{d}
if and only if
d
does not divide
k
, so the number of such
v_{k}
is exactly
m-\frac{m}{d}
, and they form a basis of
V_{d}
.

Let us define the space
V=\cap_{1\neq d|m}V_{d}
. By the construction,
V
has the basis
\left\{v_{k}\right\}_{\gcd(k,m)=1}
, hence
\dim V=\varphi(m)
, where
\varphi
is the Euler’s totient function, and the eigenvalues of
A_{0}|_{V}
are exactly the primitive
m
-th roots of unity
\xi^{k}
with
\gcd(k,m)=1
. The space
V
is defined by rational equations, so
V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(m)}
. Moreover,
A_{0}\cdot V=V
and
A_{0}\cdot\mathbb{Z}^{m}=\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, hence
A_{0}\cdot(V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m})=V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}.

Fix a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
e_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\varphi(m)
of
V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}
. For convenience, we identify
V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}
with
\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(m)}
, by identifying the
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
e_{i}
with the standard generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(m)}
. Let
A
be the matrix of
A_{0}|_{V}
in the basis
\left\{e_{i}\right\}
. Observe that since
A_{0}^{m}=1
, we have
A^{m}=1
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
A
be the matrix defined above, and let
f(x)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
. Then for the matrix
M=f(A)
we have either
M=0
or
\det(M)\neq 0.

Proof.

As
A
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues
\xi^{k}
for any
\gcd(k,m)=1
, the matrix
f(A)
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues
f(\xi^{k})
for such
k
. If
f(\xi^{k})=0
for some
k
with
\gcd(k,m)=1
, then
f
is divisible by the cyclotomic polynomial
\Phi_{m}(x)
, and thus
f(\xi^{j})=0
for any
j
with
\gcd(m,j)=0
, i.e.
M=f(A)=0
. Otherwise,
M
has no zero eigenvalues, so
\det(M)\neq 0
.
∎

Let us consider the group
G_{m}=\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(m)}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, where the semidirect product is taken using the matrix
A
defined as above. Consider a simple random walk
R_{n}
on
G_{m}
corresponding to the generating set
(\pm e_{i},k),(0,k)
where
i=1,\ldots,\varphi(m)
,
k=0,\ldots,m-1\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
H
be a non-abelian group generated by two elements
a
and
b
and consider the wreath product
\Gamma=H\wr G_{m}^{5}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
and any balanced law
w=w(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
there exists a symmetric generating set
S
on
\Gamma
such that if
X_{n}
,
Y^{(1)}_{n},\dots,Y^{(d)}_{n}
are independent simple random walks of
\Gamma
where each step is uniform on
S
, then

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([(X_{n})^{m},w(Y^{(1)}_{n},\dots,Y^{(d)}_{n})]=1\right)\geq\left(\frac{\varphi(m)}{m^{d+1}}\right)^{5}-\varepsilon.

In other words,
\Gamma
satisfies the law
[x^{m},w]
with positive probability with respect to the simple random walk induced by
S
.

As before, the theorem holds one
H
is taken to be the free group on two generators, and in this case, since this group contains a free subgroup it cannot satisfy any law virtually, so we conclude that
[x^{m},w(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{d})]
does not satisfy positivity.

To prove this theorem we will use a strategy similar to the proof of Theorem
6.3
. In particular, recall the definition of the path of a word
(Definition
6.2
). Let
R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d+1)}
be independent random walks on
G_{m}^{5}
and let
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
be the paths of
\big(R_{n}^{(d+1)}\big)^{m}
and
w(R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)})
. Our goal is to prove that
\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q\neq\varnothing)
is small for an appropriate
q\in G_{m}^{5}
.

First, we want to simplify the statement. We will assume
q=(q_{0},\dotsc,q_{0})
for some
q_{0}\in G_{m}
and then

\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q\neq\varnothing)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leq mn\\
l\leq n|w|\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}((\gamma^{\prime}_{l,1})^{-1}\gamma_{k,1}=q_{0})^{5},

where
\gamma_{l}^{\prime}
is the
l
-th step of
\gamma^{\prime}
,
\gamma_{k}
is the
k
-th step of
x^{m}
and also
\gamma_{l,1}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{k,1}
are the first “coordinates” of
\gamma_{l}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{k}
respectively.
As in the proof of Lemma
6.8
, we may write

(\gamma_{l,1}^{\prime})^{-1}\gamma_{k,1}=\widehat{w}_{k,l}(R_{r}^{(1)},R_{n-r}^{(2)},R_{r^{\prime}}^{(3)},R_{n-r^{\prime}}^{(4)},R_{n}^{(5)},\ldots R_{n}^{(d+3)})

where
R_{r}^{(1)},R_{n-r}^{(2)},R_{r^{\prime}}^{(3)},R_{n-r^{\prime}}^{(4)},R_{n}^{(5)},\ldots R_{n}^{(d+3)}
are independent random walks and where
r
depends on
k
, and
r^{\prime}
on
l
. Hence

\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q\neq\varnothing)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leq nm\\
l\leq n|w|\end{subarray}}\left(\mathbb{P}(\widehat{w}_{k,l}(R_{r}^{(1)},R_{n-r}^{(2)},R_{r^{\prime}}^{(3)},R_{n-r^{\prime}}^{(4)},R_{n}^{(5)},\ldots R_{n}^{(d+3)})=q)\right)^{5}.

Therefore the proof of Theorem
7.2
boils down to getting an upper bound on the probability that a word evaluated at some random walks will hit a far-away point. This was done in §
6
for abelian groups. Here
G_{m}
is not abelian, however we can exploit its structure to get a similar result.

Lemma 7.3
.

For any fixed word
\widehat{w}=\widehat{w}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{u})
there is a finite set
B\subset\mathrm{Mat}_{\varphi(m)}(\mathbb{Z})
such that for any
(v_{i},k_{i})\in G_{m}
we have

\widehat{w}((v_{1},k_{1}),\ldots,(v_{u},k_{u}))=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{u}b_{i}\cdot v_{i},*\right)

for some
b_{i}\in B
, where
*
is some element of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Multiplication and taking the inverse in
G_{m}
is given by multiplication of the first coordinate by the powers of
A
. So we can take as
B
a set of all sums of
\pm A^{k}
of length at most
|\widehat{w}|
.
∎

Let
q=(v_{0},k_{0})\in G_{m}
, and let
R_{*}^{i}=(v_{i},k_{i})
be random walks on
G_{m}
. Write
B=B(\widehat{w})
and
\widehat{w}_{k,l}(R_{r}^{(1)},R_{n-r}^{(2)},R_{r^{\prime}}^{(3)},R_{n-r^{\prime}}^{(4)},R_{n}^{(5)},\ldots,R_{n}^{(d+3)})=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d+3}b_{i}\cdot v_{i},*\right)
by the previous lemma. The
b_{i}
’s only depend on
k_{1},\dotsc,k_{d+3}
, but actually we do not need to calculate them. Instead we can use the fact that
B
is finite together with a union bound. Thus we need to bound the probabilities
\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}b_{i}v_{i}=v_{0}\right).

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
n>0
and any
b\in B\setminus\{0\}
if
R_{n}
is random walk on
G_{m}
and if
R_{n}=(v,k)
then

\mathbb{P}(b\cdot v=v_{0})\leq Cn^{-\varphi(m)/2}e^{-c|v_{0}|^{2}/n}.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.1
for
b\in B\setminus\{0\}
we have
\det(b)\neq 0
. Now
\mathbb{P}(b\cdot v=v_{0})=\mathbb{P}(v=b^{-1}\cdot v_{0})
and
|b^{-1}\cdot v_{0}|\geq c|v_{0}|
with
c=1/\max_{b\in B\setminus\{0\}}||b||
. The lemma then follows from the usual heat kernel bound for random walks in
\mathbb{Z}^{\varphi(m)}
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

For any fixed lengths of random walks
R^{(i)}_{*}
and any
b_{1},\dots,b_{d+3}\in B
, denoting
R_{*}^{(i)}=(v_{i},k_{i})
we have:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i}b_{i}\cdot v_{i}=v_{0}\right)\leq Cs^{-\varphi(m)/2}e^{-c|v_{0}|^{2}/s}.

where
s=\sum_{i:b_{i}\neq 0}\mathrm{len}(R^{(i)})
.

Proof.

Direct computations show that convolution of two bounds from the lemma above with
n=n_{1}
and
n=n_{2}
is the function of the same type with
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
and maybe different constants.
∎

We now return to
\widehat{w}_{k,l}=\widehat{w}_{k,l}(R_{r}^{(1)},R_{n-r}^{(2)},R_{r^{\prime}}^{(3)},R_{n-r^{\prime}}^{(4)},R_{n}^{(5)}\ldots,R_{n}^{(d+3)})
. We wish to simply sum over the finite set of possibilities for the
b_{i}
, but we need to restrict the set slightly. We therefore note that
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
cannot both be zero. This is because
R_{r}^{(1)}
and
R_{n-r}^{(2)}
appear in
\widehat{w}_{k,l}
together, except the very last letter in
\widehat{w}_{k,l}
which is
R_{r}^{(1)}
. The same holds for
b_{3}
and
b_{4}
. We get that
s\geq\min\{r,n-r\}+\min\{r^{\prime},n-r^{\prime}\}
. Denote this value by
s^{\prime}
.
Since
s^{-\frac{\varphi(m)}{2}}e^{\frac{-|v_{0}|^{2}}{s}}
as a function of
s
takes a maximum value at
s=\frac{2|v_{0}|^{2}}{\varphi(m)}
, we get

s^{-\varphi(m)/2}e^{-|v_{0}|^{2}/s}\leq\max_{s\geq s^{\prime}}s^{-\varphi(m)/2}e^{-|v_{0}|^{2}/s}\leq C\max\{s^{\prime},|v_{o}|^{2}\}^{-\varphi(m)/2}.

This allows to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{w}_{k,l}=q)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\dotsc,b_{d+3}\in B\\
b_{1}\neq 0\textrm{ or }b_{2}\neq 0\\
b_{3}\neq 0\textrm{ or }b_{4}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sum_{j}b_{j}v_{j}=v_{0}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b_{1},\dotsc,b_{d+3}\in B}C\max\{s^{\prime},|v_{0}|^{2}\}^{-5\varphi(m)/2}\leq C\max\{s^{\prime},|v_{0}|^{2}\}^{-5/2}

for
q=(q_{0},\dotsc,q_{0})
and
q_{0}=(v_{0},k_{0})
. We sum these over
k
and
l
and use the fact that
s^{\prime}
(which is a function of
k
and
l
) takes any value
S
no more than
4m|w|(S+1)
times. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k\leq mn}\sum_{l\leq n|w|}\mathbb{P}(\widehat{w}_{k,l}=q)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S=1}^{\infty}\sum_{(k,l):s^{\prime}=S}C\max\{S,|v_{0}|^{2}\}^{-5/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S=1}^{\infty}4m|w|(S+1)\cdot C\max\{S,|v_{0}|^{2}\}^{-5/2}\leq\frac{C}{|v_{0}|}.

Thus we proved the the following statement.

Proposition 7.6
.

For
q=(q_{0},\ldots,q_{0})
,
q_{0}\in G_{m}
,
q_{0}=(v_{0},k_{0})
we have

\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q\neq\varnothing)<C|v_{0}|^{-1}.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
7.2

Proof.

Let us take
q=(q_{0},\dots q_{0})\in G_{m}^{5},
where
q_{0}=(v_{0},k_{0})\in G_{m}
with large enough
|v_{0}|
. We will choose the following generating set of
\Gamma=H\wr G_{m}

\{s_{1}ms_{2}:s_{1},s_{2}\in\{a^{\pm 1}\delta_{0},b^{\pm 1}\delta_{q}\},m\in\{(\pm e_{i},k)\}_{i,k}^{5}\}.

In the formula above,
a^{\pm 1}\delta_{0}
and
b^{\pm 1}\delta_{q}
are both elements of
\oplus_{G_{m}}H
(which is embedded in
\Gamma
) while
(\pm e_{i},k)
are elements of
G_{m}
, and
G_{m}^{5}
is also embedded in
\Gamma
(the parameter
i
takes values in
\{1,\dotsc,5\}
and
k
in
\{1,\dotsc,m\}
). Note that the projection of the
n
-th step of the simple random walk on
\Gamma
corresponding to this generating set is the
n
-th step of the simple random walk that corresponds to
S^{5}
of
G_{m}^{5}
.

We will use the same idea as in the previous section:
(X_{n})^{m}
and
w(Y^{(1)}_{n},\dots,Y^{(d)}_{n})
commute if the corresponding paths
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
in the base group are loops and
\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q=\varnothing
,
\gamma q\cap\gamma^{\prime}=\varnothing
. Similar to the proof of Proposition
5.13
we get that
\gamma
is a loop with probability
\left(\frac{\varphi(m)}{m}\right)^{5}
, and
\gamma^{\prime}
is a loop if all
Y^{(i)}_{n}\in H\wr\mathbb{Z}^{5\varphi(m)}<\Gamma
, which happens independently for each
i
with probability
\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)^{5}
.
And finally, by Proposition
7.6
we have

\mathbb{P}(\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}q\neq\varnothing)+\mathbb{P}(\gamma q\cap\gamma^{\prime}\neq\varnothing)\leq\varepsilon

for large enough
|q_{0}|
. Therefore

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left([(X_{n})^{m},w(Y^{(1)}_{n},\dots,Y^{(d)}_{n})]=1\right)\geq\left(\frac{\varphi(m)}{m^{d+1}}\right)^{5}-\varepsilon.

as required.
∎

Remark
.

The theorem can also be proved as a corollary of Lemma
11.2
below (at the expense of replacing the power 5 with 8), but the resulting simplification is not particularly significant.

8.
Commutators of a power and a non-balanced law

Let us consider the general Heisenberg group of dimension
2m-1

H_{2m-1}=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc|c}1&-&u^{t}&-&a\\
\hline\cr 0&1&&0&\mid\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&v\\
0&0&&1&\mid\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right):u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},a\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

For convenience we will write elements of
H_{2m-1}
as triples
(u,a,v)
, where
{u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{m-1},a\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Then multiplication is given by the formula

(u_{1},a_{1},v_{1})\cdot(u_{2},a_{2},v_{2})=(u_{1}+u_{2},a_{1}+a_{2}+u^{t}_{1}v_{2},v_{1}+v_{2}).

Let us take a matrix
A
as in §
7
. Define an action of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
on
H_{2m-1}
by

1*\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc|c}1&-&u&-&a\\
\hline\cr 0&1&&0&\mid\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&v\\
0&0&&1&\mid\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc|c}1&-&u^{t}A&-&a\\
\hline\cr 0&1&&0&\mid\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&A^{-1}v\\
0&0&&1&\mid\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\cdots&0&1\end{array}\right)

This is indeed an action of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
on
H_{2m-1}
since it preserves multiplication and
A^{m}=1
.

Let us consider the semidirect product
H_{2m-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
G=H_{2m-1}\rtimes_{A}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
and let
\mu
be any finitely supported symmetric generating measure of
G
with
\mu(1)>0
, and let
R_{n}
be a random walk with step distribution
\mu
. Then for any law
w(x,y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
with a total degree of some
y_{i}
not equal to zero we have
[x^{m},w]=1
with probability at least
\frac{\phi(m)}{m}
with respect to the random walk
R_{n}
, where
\phi(m)
is the Euler’s totient function, but G does not satisfy
[x^{m},w]=1
virtually.

Note that, unlike our other results on commutators, that all require the two words to have different letters, in this result the letter
x
might be common to both terms. The unbalanced letter, though, must be one of the
y_{i}
.

Proof.

Let us take
x=\left(\left(u,a,v\right),k\right)\in G
. We have
x^{m}=((u^{\prime},a^{\prime},v^{\prime}),km)
, where

\displaystyle(u^{\prime})^{t}

\displaystyle=u^{t}\big(I+A^{k}+\dots+A^{k(m-1)}\big),

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle=ma+u\big((m-1)I+(m-2)A^{k}+\dots+A^{(m-2)k}\big)v,

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\big(I+A^{-k}\dots+A^{-k(m-1)}\big)v.

If
k
and
m
are coprime, then
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}=0
, so
x^{m}
lies in the center of
G
and
[x^{m},w]=1
for any
y_{1},\dots y_{l}\in G
.
Let
R^{x}_{n}
,
R^{y_{1}}_{n},\dots,R^{y_{d}}_{n}
be the set of independent random walks. Let

X_{k}=\{((u,a,v),k),\text{ where }(u,a,v)\in H\}\subset G
. We note that
\mathbb{P}(R^{x}_{n}\in X_{k})\to\frac{1}{m}
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\mathbb{P}([(R_{n}^{x})^{m},w(R_{n}^{x},R_{n}^{y_{1}},\dots,R_{n}^{y_{d}})]=1)\geq\mathbb{P}(R_{n}^{x}\in\cup_{\gcd(k,m)=1}X_{k})\to\frac{\phi(m)}{m},

where
\gcd(k,m)
is the greatest common divisor and this proves the first claim.

Now let us show that
G
does not contain a finite index subgroup which satisfies the given law. Let us assume that there is a finite index subgroup
\Lambda
of
G
such that for any
x,y_{1}\dots y_{d}\in\Lambda
one has
[x^{m},w(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})]=1
. Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m-1}\}
be the standard generating set of
\mathbb{Z}^{m-1}
. Let us consider the subgroups
K_{u}<H<G
and
K_{v}<H<G
defined as follows:

K_{u}=\{(u,a,v)\mid u=\lambda e_{1},a=0,v=0,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cong\mathbb{Z},

K_{v}=\{(u,a,v)\mid u=0,a=0,v=\lambda e_{1},\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cong\mathbb{Z}.

Note that
\Lambda\cap K_{u}
and
\Lambda\cap K_{v}
are finite index subgroups of
K_{u}
and
K_{v}
respectively, and hence are nontrivial. Let us take
g\in(\Lambda\cap K_{u})\setminus\{0\}
and
h\in(\Lambda\cap K_{v})\setminus\{0\}
. Let us fix an index
i
such that
w(x,y_{1},\dots y_{d})
has a total degree of
y_{i}
not equal to zero. Then for
x=g,y_{i}=h
and
y_{j}=0
for
j\neq i
we have
x^{m}=(\lambda e_{1},0,0)
,
w=(u,a,\mu e_{1})
, where
\lambda
and
\mu
are non-zero integers. Hence,
[x^{m},w(x,y_{1},\cdots,y_{l})]=(0,\lambda\mu,0)\neq 1.
Thus we get elements
x,y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\in\Lambda
that do not satisfy
[x^{m},w]=1
. A contradiction.
∎

9.
Commutators of a law with itself

Let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word, and consider the law
[w,w]\in\mathrm{F}_{2d}
given by

[w,w](x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots,y_{d})=[w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),w(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})].

Our goal is to show that if
w
does not satisfy a gap or a positivity result, the same holds for
[w,w]
. The case where
w
is balanced is covered by §
6
, so we focus on the case where
w
is not balanced.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
R_{n}
be a random walk on
H\wr G
with a step distribution
\mu
. Let
R_{n}^{\prime}
be a random walk on
G
with a step distribution
\pi_{*}(\mu)
where
\pi
is a natural projection
\pi:H\wr G\to G
. If
G
satisfies a law
w
with probability
1-\varepsilon
with respect to
R_{n}^{\prime}
, and
H
is any abelian group, then
H\wr G
satisfies
[w,w]
with probability at least
(1-\varepsilon)^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
H
is abelian, we know that
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}H
is also abelian and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}([w(R_{n}^{x_{1}},\dots R_{n}^{x_{d}}),w(R_{n}^{y_{1}},\dots,R_{n}^{y_{d}})]=1)\geq

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(w(R_{n}^{x_{1}},\dots R_{n}^{x_{d}}),w(R_{n}^{y_{1}},\dots,R_{n}^{y_{d}})\in\ker\pi\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}H\right)=

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{P}(w(\pi(R_{n}^{x_{1}}),\dots\pi(R_{n}^{x_{d}}))=1\in G))^{2}.

Hence,
H\wr G
satisfies the law
[w,w]
with a probability at least
(1-\varepsilon)^{2}.

∎

Proposition 9.2
.

Suppose that an infinite
G
does not satisfy a law
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
virtually, and that
H
is a group that does not satisfy
w
. Then
H\wr G
does not satisfy
[w,w]
virtually.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be a finite index subgroup of
\Gamma=H\wr G
. We want to show that
\Lambda
does not satisfy the law
[w,w]
.

Let
\Gamma_{0}=\left\{(\boldsymbol{1},g):g\in G\right\}\cong G
, i.e. all elements of
\Gamma
with trivial lamps. Since
[\Gamma_{0}:\Gamma_{0}\cap\Lambda]<\infty
and
\Gamma_{0}\cong G
, it has no subgroups of finite index which satisfy
w
, so there are elements
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}\in\Lambda\cap\Gamma_{0}
such that
w(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})=(\boldsymbol{1},g_{0})\neq 1
.
Let
g_{1},g_{2},\dots\in G
such that

\left|g_{j}^{-1}g_{i}\right|\geq 2|g_{0}|

for all
i,j
. Consider the subgroup
\Gamma_{1}=\left\{(L,e):\operatorname{supp}(L)\subseteq\left\{g_{1},g_{2},\dots\right\}\right\}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}H
which does not satisfy
w
virtually. Again we have
[\Gamma_{1}:\Gamma_{1}\cap\Lambda]<\infty
; therefore there are elements
x_{1},\dots,x_{r}\in\Gamma_{1}\cap\Lambda
such that
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=(L_{0},e)\neq 1
.
Finally, we have
w(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})\cdot w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=(L_{1},g_{0})
and
w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})\cdot w(t_{1},\dots,t_{r})=(L_{0},g_{0})
where
\operatorname{supp}(L_{1})\subseteq\left\{g_{1}g_{0},g_{2}g_{0},\dots\right\}
which does not intersect
\operatorname{supp}(L_{0})
, so
L_{1}\neq L_{0}
and hence
[w(t_{1},\dots,t_{r}),w(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})]\neq 1
.
∎

Corollary 9.3
.

(1)

If
w
does not satisfy a (strong) gap, neither does
[w,w]
.

(2)

If
w
does not satisfy a (strong) positivity result, neither does
[w,w]
.

10.
Occupation measure of random walk paths in balls

The aim of this section is to provide upper bounds on the occupation measure of a path
\gamma(w;R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)})
in balls (of the Cayley graph of
G
). These results will be used in the following section, and were put in a separate section since they may be of independent interest.

We start with two lemmas that estimate conditioned random walks
for transient groups, with the eventual aim of proving Lemma
10.3
.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group with
superpolynomial growth and let
S
be some set of generators. Let

\left\{R_{t}\right\}_{t\leq n}
be a random walk on the group
G
with respect to
S
. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\big(\max_{x\in G}\mathbb{E}\big(|\{t<n:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,\big|\,R_{n}\big)\big)\leq Cr^{3}.

The constant
C
may depend on
G
and on
S
, but not on
r

and
n
.

In words, the typical number of visits to a ball of radius
r
is
not more than
Cr^{3}
, even when one conditions on
R_{n}
, the
place where the random walk “ends”, as long as
R_{n}
is typical.
Let us remark that this does not hold for every value of
R_{n}
.
For example, a random walk on the free group, conditioned on
R_{n}=1

i.e. on returning to the identity, visits the identity about
\sqrt{n}

times.

It is reasonable to conjecture that the value
r^{3}
can be replaced
by
r^{2}
, as is conjectured (and still open) for the number of
visits to
B(1,r)
for an unconditioned random walk. On the unconditioned
problem the best known to us is
r^{5/2}
, see
[
20
]
. For
the purposes of this paper the exact value of the exponent is not
important.

Proof.

Denote by
M_{n}
the expectation in the lemma, i.e.

M_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}(\max_{x\in G}\mathbb{E}(|\{t<n:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,|\,R_{n})).

Examine for one
x
and one value of
R_{n}
the quantity
\mathbb{E}(|\{t<n:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,|\,R_{n})
.
We wish to write it as
A_{1}+A_{2}+A_{3}+A_{4}
, dividing according
to time. For symmetry reasons we have to be slightly careful. Define

n_{0}=0
,
n_{1}=\lceil n/4\rceil
,
n_{2}=\lceil n/2\rceil
,

n_{3}=n-\lceil n/4\rceil+1
and
n_{4}=n+1
and define

A_{i}\coloneqq A_{i}(x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{t\in\left[n_{i-1},n_{i}\right):R_{t}\in B(x,r)\right\}\right|:R_{n}\right].

Now for every 4 non-negative numbers
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
we
have

(14)

A+B+C+D\leq\max\{A+B+C,B+C+D\}\\
+\;D\mathbbm{1}\{A+B>0\}+A\mathbbm{1}\{C+D>0\}.

We apply this to
A_{1}(x),\dotsc,A_{4}(x)
and maximise over
x
.
For brevity we write
A_{123}(x)=A_{1}(x)+A_{2}(x)+A_{3}(x)
and
similarly for other subsets of
\{1,\dotsc,4\}
. We get

\displaystyle\max_{x}\{A_{1234}(x)\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\eqref{eq:ABCD}}}}{{\leq}}\max_{x}\{\max\{A_{123}(x),A_{234}(x)\}+

\displaystyle\qquad+\;A_{4}(x)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{12}(x)>0\}+A_{1}(x)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{34}(x)>0\}\}\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\max_{x}\{A_{123}(x)\},\max_{x}\{A_{234}(x)\}+

\displaystyle\qquad+\;\max_{x}\{A_{4}(x)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{12}(x)>0\}\}

(15)

\displaystyle\qquad+\max_{x}\{A_{1}(x)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{34}(x)>0\}\}\eqqcolon I+II+III.

To estimate term
I
we claim that

(16)

\max_{x}A_{123}(x)=\max_{x}A_{234}(x).

To see (
16
) note that
A_{123}
is simply

\mathbb{E}(|\{t\in[0,n_{3}-1]:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,|\,R_{n})

and, similarly,
A_{234}
is
for the interval
[\lceil n/4\rceil,n]
. The time reversal of a random
walk starting from
1
and conditioned to end at some
y
is random
walk starting from
y
and conditioned to end at
1
, so
A_{123}(x)=A_{234}(R_{n}^{-1}x)
. This shows
(
16
).

Fix now one
z
such that
\mathbb{P}(R_{n}=z)>0
and let
S
be a random walk of length
n
conditioned on
S_{n}=z
. For every
x_{0}
We may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\{t\in[0,n_{3}-1]:S_{t}\in B(x_{0},r)\}|)=\mathbb{E}\big(

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|\{t\in[0,n_{3}-1]:S_{t}\in B(x_{0},r)\}|\,\big|\,S_{n_{3}}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big(\max_{x}\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(|\{t\in[0,n_{3}-1]:S_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,\big|\,S_{n_{3}}\big)\big).

Maximizing over
x_{0}
(note that the right-hand side does not depend on
x_{0}
) and integrating over
z
gives

(17)

\mathbb{E}(I)\leq M_{n_{3}}.

This terminates the estimate of
I
.

We move to the estimates of
II
and
III
. They are essentially
identical, so for brevity we prove only
II
. For every
x
,

\displaystyle{A_{4}(x)\mathbbm{1}\{A_{12}(x)>0\}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big(|\{(t,s):t\geq n_{3},s<n_{2},R_{t},R_{s}\in B(x,r)\}|\,\big|\,R_{n}\big).

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{3}}^{n_{4}}\sum_{s=0}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{P}(d(R_{t},R_{s})\leq 2r\,\big|\,R_{n}).

Since the right-hand side does not contain
x
, it also bounds
II
.
Integrating over
R_{n}
gives

(18)

\mathbb{E}(II)\leq\sum_{t=n_{3}}^{n_{4}}\sum_{s=0}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{P}(d(R_{t},R_{s})\leq 2r)\leq Cn^{2}\max_{m\geq n/4-1}\mathbb{P}(d(1,R_{m})\leq 2r).

To estimate the right-hand side, we use the results of Lyons, Peres,
Sun and Zheng. By
[
20
, corollary 2.6]
, for every integers

m
and
r
and any two
x,y\in G
,

p_{m}(x,y)<C(m^{-10}r^{20}/|B(2r)|+e^{-cm/r^{2}})

(in
[
20
]
there is an additional parameter
k
, which we
set to
20
). Note that it is at this point that we use that
G

has superpolynomial growth. Inserting this estimate into (
18
)
gives

\mathbb{E}(II)\leq Cn^{-8}r^{20}+Cn^{2}|B(2r)|e^{-cn/r^{2}}.

If
n>C^{\prime}r^{3}
for some
C^{\prime}
sufficient large then
\mathbb{E}(II)\leq C/n
.

We can now wrap up the lemma. If
n\leq C^{\prime}r^{3}
then the claim of
the lemma holds trivially. For any
n>C^{\prime}r^{3}
we have

M_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}(\max_{x}A_{1234}(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\eqref{eq:I II III}}}}{{\leq}}\mathbb{E}(I)+\mathbb{E}(II)+\mathbb{E}(III)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{\leq}}M_{n_{3}}+C/n

where in
(*)
we used (
17
) and the estimate of
II

above (which, as explained, applies verbatim to
III
). Recall that

n_{3}=n-\lceil n/4\rceil+1
. The lemma now follows by a straightforward
induction on
n
.
∎

Lemma 10.2
.

The conclusion of Lemma
10.1

holds for any transient group.

Proof.

Since Lemma
10.1
proves this for
G
with superpolynomial
growth, we need only cover the case that
G
has polynomial growth.
However, for
G
with growth
d
we have the estimates

p_{n}(1,y)\leq Cn^{-d/2}e^{-c|y|^{2}/n}.

This is well known, but for completeness let us explain this estimate. By Gromov’s theorem
[
4
,
13
]
the group
G
is virtually nilpotent. It is not difficult to see that any nilpotent group is virtually torsion free
[
26
, chapter II]
. By Mal'cev’s theorem
[
26
, chapter II]
, a nilpotent torsion-free group is embedded as a cocompact lattice in a connected nilpotent Lie group. By the results of Guivarc’h
[
6
]
there is an integer
d
such that the Lie group has volume growth
d
. Since a Lie group is quasi-isometric to a cocompact lattice in it, and since our cocompact lattice is quasi-isometric to
G
, and since growth is preserved by quasi-isometries, we get that
G
satisfies that
cr^{d}\leq|B(1,r)|\leq Cr^{d}
. Finally the results of Hebisch and Saloff-Coste
[
9
, theorems 2.1 and 4.1]
give the estimate above.

The probability to be at
z
at time
t
and at
y
at time
n>t
can
then be bounded by

Ct^{-d/2}(n-t)^{-d/2}e^{-c|z|^{2}/t-c|y-z|^{2}/(n-t)}.

Summing this estimate over
t\leq n
gives

C\left(\min\{|z|,|y-z|\}\right)^{2-d}n^{-d/2}e^{-c|y|^{2}/n}

(using
d\geq 3
, which follows from our assumption that the group
is transient). Summing over
z\in B(x,r)
gives
r^{2}n^{-d/2}e^{-c|y|^{2}/n}
,
independently of
x
. Thus

\max_{x}\mathbb{E}\big(|\{t<n:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,\big|\,R_{n}=y\big)\leq\frac{Cr^{2}n^{-d/2}e^{-c|y|^{2}/n}}{\mathbb{P}(R_{n}=y)}.

Taking expectation over
R_{n}
gives

\mathbb{E}\big(\max_{x}\mathbb{E}\big(|\{t<n:R_{t}\in B(x,r)\}|\,\big|\,R_{n}\big)\big)\leq Cr^{2}n^{-d/2}\sum_{y}e^{-c|y|^{2}/n}\leq Cr^{2},

where in the last inequality we used the fact that
|B(1,r)|\leq Cr^{d}
. This
proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group,
let
S
be a set of generators, let
w
be a word and let
\gamma

be the path corresponding to
w
and random walks of length
n
with repsect to the generating set
S
. Let
x\in G
and let

r\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\mathbb{E}(|\gamma\cap B(x,r)|)\leq Cr^{3}.

(the constant
C
may depend on
w
).

Proof.

The lemma holds trivially for
G
recurrent, since the only recurrent groups are those having volume growth 1 and 2. Assume therefore that
G
is transient.
Let
l
be the length of
w
and let
a_{i}
be its letters,
so
w=(a_{1}\dotsc a_{l})
. Let
w_{i}
be the word comprising
of the first
i
letters of
w
, for
i\in\{0,\dotsc,l-1\}
.
Let
R^{(1)},\dotsc,R^{(d)}
be the random walks constructing
\gamma
.
Condition on
R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}
. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}\big(|\gamma\cap B(x,r)|\,\big|\,R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\mathbb{E}\big(|w_{i}(R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)})R^{(a_{i+1})}\cap B(x,r)|\,\big|\,R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\mathbb{E}\big(|R^{(a_{i+1})}\cap B(w_{i}(R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)})^{-1}x,r)|\,\big|\,R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\max_{y\in G}\mathbb{E}\big(|R^{(a_{i+1})}\cap B(y,r)|\,\big|\,R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\max_{y\in G}\mathbb{E}\big(|R^{(a_{i+1})}\cap B(y,r)|\,\big|\,R_{n}^{(a_{i+1})}\big).

Taking expectation over
R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d)}
we may estimate
each term by Lemma
10.2
and get

\mathbb{E}(|\gamma\cap B(x,r)|)\leq Clr^{3},

as needed.
∎

11.
Commutators of a power law with itself

Definition 11.1
.

Let
w
be a word on
k
letters, let
G
be finitely generated
group and let
S
be a finite set of generators. Let
\gamma_{n}

be the path corresponding to the word
w
and

,
where
R^{(1)},\dotsc,R^{(d)}
are independent random walks on

of

.

We say that

is

if

\max_{n}\mathbb{P}(g\in\gamma_{n})\leq C\left|g\right|^{-4},

for some
C
independent of
n
and
g
(

may depend on
G
,

S
and

).

We call this property non-Peano because such a path is far from visiting all of
G
.
Clearly

must have at least

dimensional volume growth to be

. But we do not believe this is enough.
Presumably it is possible to construct artificial examples of a

and a “large” group

such that

is Peano on
G
, but
we decided not to pursue this direction here.

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group
and let

be a
2
-generated group. Let
w_{1}
and

be two laws on

and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\,\mathbb{P}(w_{i}(R_{n}^{(1)},\dotsc,R_{n}^{(d_{i})})=1)=p_{i}\qquad i\in\{1,2\}

for some
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
(note that we need here
\liminf
rather than our usual
\limsup
). Assume also that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are non-Peano
on
G
,
S
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there are generators

for

, where

. We use again Fact

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left([w_{1}(\vec{R}^{1}),w_{2}(\vec{R}^{2})]=1\right)

For ease of notation, write

Write
G=B_{L}(u,\left|u\right|/2)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}V

Before moving on to Burnside groups, we need two simple lemmas. One
on paths in lamplighter groups, and another on linear algebra.

Lemma 11.3
.

Let
B
be a
d
-generated group and let
G=\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}\wr B

with the switch-move-switch generators, with respect to lazy simple
random walk on
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}
and the natural walk on
B
.
Let
\gamma
be a geodesic path of length
n
in
G
and let
\beta
be
its projection to
B
. Then

|\{\beta_{0},\dotsc,\beta_{n}\}|\geq\frac{1}{l/2+2}|\gamma_{n}|.

(Recall that a geodesic path is the shortest path between its endpoints).

Proof.

We will show that for any path
\gamma
there exists a path
\gamma^{\prime}

with length
n^{\prime}\leq n
with the same projection to
B
and same endpoint,
such that for all
b
, the projection
\beta^{\prime}
of
\gamma^{\prime}
visits

b
no more than
l/2+2
times. This will show the lemma by
contradiction since if not, then

n^{\prime}\leq|\{\beta^{\prime}_{0},\dotsc,\beta_{n}^{\prime}\}|(l/2+2)=|\{\beta_{0},\dotsc,\beta_{n}\}|(l/2+2)<|\gamma_{n}|=|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}|

which contradicts the definition of
|\gamma_{n}|
as the length
of the shortest path.

To construct
\gamma^{\prime}
we first modify
\gamma
so that at every
visit to every
b
it does a geodesic in
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}

towards
\gamma_{n}(b)
and when it reaches that value it no longer
moves (recall that the walk on
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}
is lazy).
Since at every visit to
b
it can move the value by
2
(once when
entering and once when exiting), and since the diameter of
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}

is
\lfloor l/2\rfloor
, we get that after
\lfloor l/4\rfloor+1

visits to
b
, it has already taken the value
\gamma_{n}(b)
.

Next, since the set of sites visited by
\beta
is connected, one
may find a spanning tree. A simple induction shows that any tree can
be traversed so that each vertex is visited at least once and the overall number of is at most twice the size of the tree (e.g. doing Depth first search). Therefore to get the the desired position and configuration, we
repeat this traversal
\lfloor l/4\rfloor

∎

Lemma 11.4
.

Let
A
be an
m\times m^{\prime}
matrix with
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}

entries, such that no row of
A
is identically zero. Assume that
for some
k
we have that every row of
A
contains no more than

k
non-zero entries, and similarly for all columns of
A
. Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{m^{\prime}}

be independent (not necessarily identically distributed) random
\mathbb{Z}/l\mathbb{Z}

takes no specific value

(with

\mathbb{P}(AX=v)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{\lceil m/k^{2}\rceil}.

Proof.

Consider the first row of
A
(think about rows as equations). Condition
on the value of all
X_{i}
that appear in that row except one, we
see that in order for this equation to be satisfied that last variable
must take a specific value, an event that has probability at most

.

Now, since the first row contains at most
k

since for any such entry that are at most
k
rows containing it,
we see that at most

have any joint variable

.
Continuing this way, the lemma is proved.
∎

12.
Uniform measures on balls

In this section we deal with the measures induced by taking uniform measures on balls. I.e., we choose a finite symmetric generating set
S
containing
1
, and let
\mu_{n}
denote the uniform measure on
S^{n}
. We show that in this case that many laws do not have a strong gap.

Theorem 12.1
.

Let
w
be a non-trivial law such that there is some group of exponential growth satisfying
w
. Then
w
has no strong gap with respect to measures induced by uniform measures on balls.

Proof.

Write
w=w(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
, and let
G
be a group of exponential growth satisfying
w
. Take a finite symmetric generating set
S
of
G
containing
1
, and let
\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{|S^{n}|}>1
be the growth exponent of
G
with respect to
S
. Also, let
H
be a finite group which does not satisfy
w
(it is easy to see that any non-trivial law is not satisfied by a sufficiently large symmetric group).

Consider the group
\Gamma=G\times H
, with generating set
T=(S\times\left\{1\right\})\cup(\left\{1\right\}\times H)
, and let
\mu_{n}
denote the uniform measure on
T^{n}
. Then for any
n\geq 1
and
h\in H
we have

\left|\left\{(g,h)\in T^{n}\mid g\in G\right\}\right|=\begin{cases}|S^{n-1}|,&h\neq 1\\
|S^{n}|,&h=1.\end{cases}

Note that
w((g_{1},h_{1}),\dots,(g_{d},h_{d}))=1
if and only if
w(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})=1
(because
G
satisfies
w
), and thus
w
holds if
h_{1}=\cdots=h_{d}=1
. Therefore, if
(g_{i},h_{i})
are distributed uniformly in a ball of radius
n
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(w((g_{1},h_{1}),\dots,(g_{d},h_{d}))=1)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(h_{1}=\cdots=h_{d}=1)=

\displaystyle=\frac{|S^{n}|^{d}}{\left((|H|-1)|S^{n-1}|+|S^{n}|\right)^{d}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left((|H|-1)\frac{|S^{n-1}|}{|S^{n}|}+1\right)^{d}},

which shows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(w((g_{1},h_{1}),\dots,(g_{d},h_{d}))=1)\geq\frac{1}{\left((|H|-1)\rho^{-1}+1\right)^{d}}.

By replacing
S
with
S^{m}
for large enough
m
we may take
\rho\to\infty
, and the above probability converges to
1
. However, it is clear that
\Gamma
does not satisfy
w
, as required.
∎

13.
Open questions

We conclude this paper with several open questions. For finite groups (or for residually finite groups), the existence of a gap (Question
1.1
) and the positivity (Question
1.2
) are still open.

A particular case of interest is for power laws
x^{m}=1
. When
G
is finite, it is known that there exists a gap for
\mathbb{P}\left(x^{m}=1\textrm{ on }G\right)
depending on
m
and on the number of generators of
G
. However, the dependency on the number of generators is still, as far as we know, unknown:

Question 13.1
.

Does the law
x^{m}=1
satisfy a gap result for finite groups, with no dependency on the number of generators?

This question is open for both the finite case and the infinite case with respect to random walks (though we believe that the two cases might be very different). Note that for
m=3
and
4
Laffey gave a positive answer
[
15
,
17
]
. Further, for
m=p
a prime number, Laffey shows that any non-
p
-groups must have
\mathbb{P}(x^{p}=1)\leq p/(p+1)

[
16
]
. For
p
-groups the situation is more complicated, and Khukhro gave an example of a 5-group where
1>\mathbb{P}(x^{5}=1)\geq 24/25

[
12
]
. See
[
8
]
for a survey of this direction.

As a generalization of several results provided in this paper, we raise the following question:

Question 13.2
.

Let
w_{1}=w_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
and
w_{2}=w_{2}(y_{1},\dots,y_{d^{\prime}})\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be words. Does the law
[w_{1},w_{2}]
satisfy a gap or positivity result?

Recall that our results show that when
w_{1},w_{2}
are balanced, or when
w_{1},w_{2}
are the same word with disjoint variables, then
[w_{1},w_{2}]
does not satisfy a gap or a positivity result; also, if
w_{1}
is a power law, then
[w_{1},w_{2}]
does not satisfy a positivity result.

Our next questions are somewhat “extreme” cases for the gap and positivity of laws. We showed that a law can be satisfied with probability arbitrarily close to
1
on a group, while the group does not satisfy the law (or any law) virtually. One may ask for the following:

Question 13.3
.

Let
w\in\mathrm{F}_{d}
be a word, let
G
be a finitely generated group generated by
S
, and let
R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)}
be independent random walks on
G
with respect to
S
. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(w(R_{n}^{(1)},\dots,R_{n}^{(d)})=1)=1
, is it true that
w=1
holds on
G
?

Regarding positivity,

Question 13.4
.

Can the probability of satisfying a law be
0
for one generating set but positive for another?

Finally, we raise another question, a bit more technical in nature:

Question 13.5
.

Can the
\limsup\neq\liminf
of the probability to satisfy
w
?

This can be arranged, for example, if the random walk is not lazy (consider for example the infinite Dihedral group
