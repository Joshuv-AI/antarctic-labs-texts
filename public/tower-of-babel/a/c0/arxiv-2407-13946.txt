Christoffel transform and multiple orthogonal polynomials
Rostyslav Kozhan 1, Marcus Vaktnäs 2

Abstract
. We investigate multiple orthogonal polynomials associated with the system of measures obtained by applying a Christoffel transform to each of the orthogonality measures. We present an algorithm for computing the transformed recurrence coefficients and determinantal formulas for the transformed multiple orthogonal polynomials of type I and type II. We apply these results to show that zeros of multiple orthogonal polynomials of an Angelesco or an AT system interlace with the zeros of the polynomials corresponding to its one-step Christoffel transform. This allows us to prove a number of interlacing properties satisfied by the multiple orthogonality analogues of classical orthogonal polynomials. For the discrete polynomials, this also produces an estimate on the smallest distance between consecutive zeros. We also identify a connection between the Christoffel transform of orthogonal polynomials and multiple orthogonality systems containing a finitely supported measure. In consequence, the compatibility relations for the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients provide a new algorithm for the computation of the Jacobi coefficients of the one-step or multi-step Christoffel transforms.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mu
be a positive Borel measure on the real line with all the moments
c_{j}:=\int x^{j}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
finite. Denote
P_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k\geq 0\}
, to be the monic orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
with respect to the inner product

(1)

\left\langle f(x),g(x)\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)g(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

These polynomials satisfy the famous three-term recurrence relation

(2)

xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+b_{n}P_{n}(x)+a_{n}P_{n-1}(x)

for some
a_{n}>0
and
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, called the Jacobi coefficients of
\mu
.

Given a point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, a one-step Christoffel transform of
\mu
is a new (potentially signed) measure
\widehat{\mu}
defined by

(3)

\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mu}(x)=\int f(x)({x-z_{0}})\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

More generally, given a polynomial
\Phi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x-z_{j})
we define the multi-step Christoffel transform to be
\widehat{\mu}
given by

(4)

\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{\mu}(x)=\int f(x){\Phi(x)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

which, of course, can be viewed as the one-step transform repeated
m
times for each of the roots of
\Phi(x)
.

It is a natural question to understand the relationship between the orthogonal polynomials
(P_{n}(x))_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
\mu
and
(\widehat{P}_{n}(x))_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
\widehat{\mu}
. In the one-step case (
3
) the following simple relation holds true:

(5)

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{x-z_{0}}\left({P}_{n+1}(x)-\frac{{P}_{n+1}(z_{0})}{{P}_{n}(z_{0})}{P}_{n}(x)\right).\end{split}

More generally, if
\deg\Phi=m
, then
\widehat{P}_{n}
can be expressed in terms of
P_{n},P_{n+1},\ldots,P_{n+m}
using the Christoffel determinantal formula, shown in
[
Christoffel
]
.

Another matter of interest here is the relationship between the Jacobi coefficients of
\widehat{\mu}
and of
\mu
. For the one-step case
\deg\Phi=1
there
are a number of closely related algorithms (among them are the
qd
algorithm of Rutishauser
[
QD
]
, Galant’s
[
Galant
]
, and Gautschi’s
[
Gautschibook
]
)
that allow to compute
\widehat{a}_{n}
’s and
\widehat{b}_{n}
’s recursively from
a_{n}
’s and
b_{n}
’s. There exist explicit algorithms for the quadratic factors,
\deg\Phi=2
, see, e.g.,
[
Gautschibook
, Sect 2.4.3]
. For the general case
\deg\Phi=m
, one typically applies the one-step or two-step algorithms repeatedly.

The Christoffel transform, as well as the closely connected topic of the Darboux transformations, is a very well-studied topic both in pure and applied mathematics, see, e.g.,
[
BaiDer
,
BueMar
,
Chihara
,
Christoffel
,
Mar91
,
SpiZhe
,
Zhe97
]
and
[

,

,

,
Gautschi
,
Gautschibook
,
GolubandKautsky
,
GolKau83
,
KauGol
]
.

Now let us introduce multiple orthogonal polynomials with respect to a system of two measures
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
on
\mathbb{R}
(we work in the more general setting of
r
measures from Section
2
onwards). For
(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, let
P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)
be a non-zero monic polynomial of degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
satisfying

(6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)x^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,n_{1}-1,

(7)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)x^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,n_{2}-1.

P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)
is then called a multiple orthogonal polynomial at the multi-index
(n_{1},n_{2})
. We say that
(n_{1},n_{2})
is normal for
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
if such
P_{n_{1},n_{2}}
exists and is unique.

Assuming sufficiently many indices are normal, these polynomials satisfy
[
NNR
,
Ismail
]
the nearest neighbour recurrence relations (compare with (
2
))

(8)

\displaystyle xP_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)=P_{n_{1}+1,n_{2}}(x)+b_{n_{1},n_{2};1}P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)+a_{n_{1},n_{2};1}P_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}}(x)+a_{n_{1},n_{2};2}P_{n_{1},n_{2}-1}(x),

(9)

\displaystyle xP_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)=P_{n_{1},n_{2}+1}(x)+b_{n_{1},n_{2};2}P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x)+a_{n_{1},n_{2};1}P_{n_{1}-1,n_{2}}(x)+a_{n_{1},n_{2};2}P_{n_{1},n_{2}-1}(x).

Subtracting (
8
) and (
9
) we can also obtain

(10)

P_{n_{1}+1,n_{2}}(x)-P_{n_{1},n_{2}+1}(x)=(b_{n_{1},n_{2};2}-b_{n_{1},n_{2};1})P_{n_{1},n_{2}}(x).

The coefficients
a_{n_{1},n_{2};1},a_{n_{1},n_{2};2},b_{n_{1},n_{2};1},b_{n_{1},n_{2};2}
in (
8
) and (
9
) are called the nearest neighbour coefficients.
These coefficients satisfy a set of partial difference equations

[
NNR
, Eq. (3.6)–(3.8)]

which we will call the compatibility conditions, or CC, for short.
[
ComputingNNR
]
showed that these equations provide an algorithm that allows to recursively compute all the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients
a_{n_{1},n_{2};1},a_{n_{1},n_{2};2},b_{n_{1},n_{2};1},b_{n_{1},n_{2};2}
from the Jacobi coefficients of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
(that is, from
a_{n_{1},0;1},a_{0,n_{2};2},b_{n_{1},0;1},b_{0,n_{2};2}
).

The central idea of our paper is the simple observation that if
\mu_{2}
is supported on
N
distinct points
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, then the multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{n,N}(x)
of the system
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
coincides with
\widehat{P}_{n}(x)\Phi(x)
, where
\Phi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x-z_{j})
and
\widehat{P}_{n}(x)
is the
n
-th orthogonal polynomial of the Christoffel transform
\widehat{\mu}_{1}
(
4
) of
\mu_{1}
corresponding to the polynomial
\Phi(x)
. Indeed,
\widehat{P}_{n}(x)\Phi(x)
is monic, has the right degree, trivially satisfies (
6
)–(
7
), and it only remains to resolve the issue of uniqueness, which we do in Theorem
3.1
.

In particular, the nearest neighbour recurrence relation (
8
) along locations
\big\{(n,N):n\in\mathbb{N}\big\}
reduces to the three-term recurrence relation for
\widehat{\mu}_{1}
(one should observe that
a_{n,N;2}=0
for all
n
, see Theorem
3.5
), and the nearest neighbour coefficients along these locations coincide with the Jacobi coefficients of
\widehat{\mu}_{1}
.

Taking the simplest case
N=1
, one realizes that Christoffel’s formula (
5
) is
just (
10
), while Gautschi’s algorithm
[
Gautschibook
]
for computing the Jacobi coefficients of the Christoffel transform is effectively the CC algorithm of
[
ComputingNNR
]
(after minor modifications related to restricting the coefficients to the strip
\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}
, see Section
3.2
). Furthermore, one can show that the well-known Gauss–Radau quadrature rule for
\mu_{1}
is just the multiple Gauss quadrature rule for
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
with
N=|\operatorname{supp}\mu_{2}|=1
(the Gauss–Lobatto rule corresponds to

).

For any
N
the modified CC algorithm (see Section
3.2
) therefore provides an algorithm for computation of the Jacobi coefficients of the multi-step Christoffel transform. It would be interesting to find out if there is any computational benefit of this algorithm compared to the repeated use of the one-step/two-step Gautschi/Galant algorithm. Such questions are important in numerical mathematics, see, e.g.,
[
BuenoandDopico
,

,

,
Gautschi
,
Gautschibook
,
GolubandKautsky
,
GolKau83
,
KauGol
]
and references therein. We will not pursue this in this paper.

Our main focus is the study of the
multiple
Christoffel transform

(\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mu}_{r})=(\Phi\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\Phi\widehat{\mu}_{r})

of the multiple orthogonality system
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})
for
r\geq 2
.
Such a transform appears naturally when one studies the multiple Gauss quadrature with fixed nodes at the zeros of
\Phi
.

We show how one can use the CC algorithm to compute the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients of
(\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mu}_{r})
(Section
3.2
) and establish the determinantal formula for

(\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mu}_{r})
(see Section
3.1
for type II, and Section

For the special case

the determinantal formulas
are known from the earlier literature: see
[

, Prop 3.2]
for type II, and
[

]
for type I for the case of two measures and multi-indices along the step-line.
During the preparation of the manuscript there appeared
[

]
studying multiple Christoffel transforms using another approach (the Gauss–Borel factorization) for the step-line multi-indices.

In Section
3.7
we demonstrate that CC algorithm can be used to compute

Christoffel transforms, which is the natural setting for the
qd
algorithm of Rutishauser
[
QD
]
and the discrete-time Toda lattices in one (see, e.g.,
[
SpiZhe
]
) and multiple dimensions, see
[
ADMVA
,
Dol
]
and references therein.

In Section
3.8
we classify all possible nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients
\{a_{\bm{n},j},b_{\bm{n},j}\}
that can occur for maximally-normal systems (such systems are called perfect). This was proved for systems with two positive infinitely-supported measures in
[
IntegrableSystems
]
. We provide an alternative simple proof that allows either measures or linear moment functionals which may be finitely or infinitely supported. The main difference is that for finitely supported

’s the

-coefficients must be zero not only on the initial marginal indices (that is, with

) but also on the final ones (with

The next portion of the results (Section

) concerns interlacing of the zeros of multiple orthogonal polynomials

for
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})
and
\widehat{P}_{\bm{n}}
for

. We show that the zeros of

and

interlace for a wide class of systems including all Angelesco and AT systems, and the same result holds for type I polynomials for a class of measures containing all Angelesco systems. In particular, this applies to multiple Laguerre of the first and second kind, Jacobi–Piñeiro, Angelesco–Jacobi, Jacobi–Laguerre, Jacobi–Hermite, Charlier, Meixner of the first and second kind, Krawtchouk, and Hahn (Sections

]

]

The interlacing results for the discrete systems then produces the lower bound 1 for the distance between two consecutive zeros (see Section

), a result that is well-known for the

[

,

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Orthogonal polynomials with respect to moment functionals

We use the notation
\mathbb{N}:=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k\geq 0\}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}:=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k>0\}
.
Let us assume that we are given an arbitrary sequence
\{c_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of complex numbers which will be referred to as the moment sequence. Define the corresponding moment functional
\mu
to be the linear map on the space of all polynomials such that

(11)

\mu[x^{n}]=c_{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Associated to
\mu
we have the bilinear form

(12)

{\left\langle P(x),Q(x)\right\rangle=\mu[P(x)Q(x)].}

In particular, note that

(13)

{\left\langle P(x)R(x),Q(x)\right\rangle=\left\langle P(x),R(x)Q(x)\right\rangle}

for any choice of polynomials
P
,
Q
, and
R
. Orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mu
are non-zero polynomials
P_{n}(x)
such that
\deg{P_{n}}\leq n
and

(14)

{\left\langle P_{n}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,n-1.}

Such polynomials always exist, since solving (
14
) for the first
n+1
Maclaurin coefficients results in a homogeneous system of linear equations with more columns than rows. If we fix the coefficient at
x^{n}
we get a linear system with coefficient matrix

(15)

{M_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{n-1}&c_{n}&\cdots&c_{2n-2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.}

Then we see that
\Delta_{n}=\det{M_{n}}\neq 0
if and only if
P_{n}
is unique up to multiplication by a constant and
\deg{P_{n}}=n
. In this case we always take
P_{n}
to be monic.

Denote
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
to be the set of all
quasi-definite
moment functionals, which are those
\mu
for which
\Delta_{n}\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For such
\mu
the monic orthogonal polynomial
P_{n}
is unique for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
The polynomials satisfy the three-term recurrence relation

(16)

\begin{split}xP_{n}(x)=P_{n+1}(x)+b_{n}P_{n}(x)+a_{n}P_{n-1}(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\end{split}

for some complex numbers
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, called the Jacobi coefficients of
\mu
(in the case
n=0
we formally take
a_{0}=0
and
P_{-1}=0
).

It is well known that
a_{n}\neq 0
for all
n>0
. Conversely, Favard’s Theorem states that any set of
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, with
a_{0}=0
and
a_{n}\neq 0
for
n>0
, generates a sequence of polynomials from (
16
) that are the orthogonal polynomials
with respect to some moment functional
\mu
.

The tridiagonal matrix

(17)

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}b_{0}&1&{0}&\\
a_{1}&b_{1}&1&\ddots\\
{0}&a_{2}&b_{3}&\ddots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right)

will be called the (“monic”) Jacobi matrix associated with
\mu
. It is the matrix of the map
P(x)\mapsto xP(x)
in the basis
\left\{P_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, see (
16
).

Finally, define the Christoffel–Darboux kernel via

(18)

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{{P_{j}(x)}P_{j}(y)}{\left\langle P_{j},P_{j}\right\rangle}.

Then the Christoffel–Darboux identity takes place:

(19)

K_{n}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\left\langle P_{n-1},P_{n-1}\right\rangle}\frac{P_{n}(x)P_{n-1}(y)-P_{n-1}(x)P_{n}(y)}{x-y}.

For more on the basics of orthogonal polynomials with respect to moment functionals, see for example
[
Chihara
]
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
be the set of measures
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
with infinite support and all the moments

(20)

c_{n}=\int x^{n}d\mu(x),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

finite. Such a measure generates a quasi-definite moment functional (
11
) which with a mild abuse of notation we also denote by
\mu
. In particular, (
12
) becomes the usual inner product in
L^{2}(\mu)
. With this convention, we can view
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
as a subset of
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
. The setting
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
corresponds to the standard theory of orthogonal polynomials with
a_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 1
and
b_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
n\geq 0
.

2.2.
\mu
associated with finite (complex) Jacobi matrices

If one takes a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
supported on exactly
N
distinct points
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, then it is known that
\Delta_{n}\neq 0
for
0\leq n\leq N
and
\Delta_{n}=0
for
n>N
. Consequently, only
\{P_{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{N}
are uniquely defined, with
P_{N}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(x-z_{j})
. The three-term recurrence (
16
) holds for
0\leq n\leq N-1
with
a_{n}>0
for
1\leq n\leq N-1
. The corresponding Jacobi matrix
J
in (
17
) is finite of size
N\times N
with
\{b_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
on the diagonal and
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N-1}
on the subdiagonal. Denote the set of such
N
-finitely supported measures by
\mathcal{M}_{N}
.

In what follows we want to allow
complex
finite Jacobi matrices and the associated linear functionals. Therefore we define
\mathcal{L}_{N}
, for each
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, to be the set of all the moment functionals
\mu
for which
\Delta_{n}\neq 0
for
1\leq n\leq N
and
\Delta_{n}=0
for
n>N
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose
\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
for some
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then

(21)

\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=0,\qquad p\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, we have

(22)

\left\langle P(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=0,\qquad p=0,1,\ldots,N-1,

if and only if
P(x)
is divisible by
P_{N}(x)
.

Proof.

Since
\Delta_{N+1}=0
, there has to exist some non-zero
P_{N+1}
solving (
14
) (at
N+1
) for
n=N+1
with degree
\deg{P_{N+1}}<N+1
. This is because
\Delta_{N+1}=0
implies the existence of two linearly independent monic solutions to (
14
) of degree
\leq N+1
, and if they both have degree
N+1
then their difference is also non-zero and solves (
14
) but has degree
<N+1
. We can always scale
P_{N+1}
to be monic.

Since
\Delta_{N}\neq 0
, we must have
P_{N+1}=P_{N}
, as there is only one monic solution at
N
. By the orthogonality relations of
P_{N+1}
we then must have
\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{N}\right\rangle=0
. We proceed to prove by induction that
\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=0
for all
p\geq N
. By
\Delta_{n+1}=0
(assuming
n\geq N
), there is some monic
P_{n}
(solving (
14
) at
n
) with
\deg{P_{n}}=n-k
,
0\leq k\leq n-N
, such that
\left\langle P_{n}(x),x^{n}\right\rangle=0
(since there is some monic
P_{n+1}
with
\deg{P_{n+1}}\leq n
). In particular,
\left\langle P_{n}(x),Q(x)\right\rangle=0
for every polynomial
Q
with
\deg{Q}\leq n
, so we must have
\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{k}P_{n}(x)\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{n}(x),x^{k}P_{N}(x)\right\rangle=0
, by (
13
). Assuming
\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=0
is true for all
p<n
(and here
n>N
), we then end up with
\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{k}P_{n}(x)\right\rangle=0
, since
x^{k}P_{n}(x)=x^{n}+o(x^{n})
, and
\left\langle P_{N}(x),o(x^{n})\right\rangle=0
. This proves (
21
).

Now for the second part, we have
\left\langle P_{N}(x)Q(x),x^{p}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{N}(x),x^{p}Q(x)\right\rangle=0
by (
21
). Conversely, suppose that
P
satisfies (
22
) and write
P=P_{N}q+r
with
\deg r<N
. Since
P_{N}
is orthogonal to any polynomial, we get that
r
also satisfies (
22
). This is only possible if
r\equiv 0
. ∎

Theorem 2.2
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
,
N\leq\infty
, then
\{P_{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{N}
satisfy the three-term recurrence relation (
16
) for

with
a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
for
1\leq n\leq N-1
and
b_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
for
0\leq n\leq N-1
. Conversely, for any non-zero
\left\{a_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{N-1}
and any
\left\{b_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
there exists a functional

Remark 2.3
.

See
[
Gus
, Thm 4]
for a more detailed description of
\mu
from
\mathcal{L}_{N}
: if the
N
roots of

are all distinct then

can be represented as integration against a (complex) measure supported on these roots. If some of the roots overlap then

contains derivative(s) of the Dirac delta function at the corresponding root.

Proof.

The first part of the statement follows from the same arguments as in
[
Chihara
, Ch. 1, Sect. 3–4]
. The second part follows very similarly to the proof of
[
Chihara
, Ch.1, Thm 4.4]
. First define
\mu[1]=c\neq 0
and
\mu[P_{n}(x)]=0
for
n=1,\dots,N-1
, where
P_{n}
are generated by the recurrence coefficients through (
16
). If we also define
\mu[x^{p}P_{N}(x)]=0
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
(see Lemma
2.1
) we can then extend
\mu
uniquely to all polynomials. From the recurrence relation one can prove that
P_{n}
are orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mu
, for
n=0,\dots,N-1
, and also
\left\langle P_{n},x^{n}\right\rangle=a_{n}\dots a_{1}\neq 0
, which implies
\Delta_{n}\neq 0
for
n=1,\dots,N
. From
\langle P_{N}(x),x^{p+N}\rangle=0
we get
\Delta_{n}=0
for each
n>N
, so
\mu
belongs to
\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Since these assumptions on
\mu
were necessary for
\left\{P_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{N}
to be orthogonal with respect to some
\mu\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
, and the recurrence coefficients are uniquely determined by the recurrence, we get the full result.
∎

Finally, we define

(23)

\mathcal{L}=\Big(\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{L}_{N}\Big)\cup\mathcal{L}_{\infty}.

This is the set of moment functionals that we are working with throughout this paper. Note that
\mathcal{L}
includes
\mathcal{M}=\Big(\bigcup_{N=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{N}\Big)\cup\mathcal{M}_{\infty}
, the set of all positive measures of finite or infinite support on
\mathbb{R}
with finite moments.

2.3.
Christoffel transform

For
\mu\in\mathcal{L}
, a one-step Christoffel transform is a functional
\widehat{\mu}
given by

(24)

\widehat{\mu}[x^{n}]=\mu[x^{n}(x-z_{0})],\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, then
\widehat{\mu}
becomes (
3
).

More generally, if
\mu\in\mathcal{L}
and
\Phi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x-z_{j})
is any polynomial, then we define the corresponding Christoffel transform

(25)

\widehat{\mu}[x^{n}]=\mu[x^{n}\Phi(x)],\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

We will also occasionally employ the notation
\Phi\mu
for
\widehat{\mu}
to make the dependence on
\Phi
explicit.
If
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and
\Phi
has real coefficients and does not change sign on the convex hull of
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
, then
\Phi\mu
is also in
\mathcal{M}
and is given as in (
4
).

Some authors choose to work with the normalized version of
\Phi{\mu}
given by

\mu[\Phi(x)]^{-1}\mu[x^{n}\Phi(x)]
, under the assumption
\mu[\Phi(x)]\neq 0
. Note that the monic orthogonal polynomials and their recurrence coefficients for both versions of the Christoffel transform are the same, so this makes no significant difference.

Given any
\mu\in\mathcal{L}
and its Christoffel transform
\widehat{\mu}=\Phi\mu
, we write
\widehat{P}_{n}
for the orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\widehat{\mu}
, and
\widehat{a}_{n}
and
\widehat{b}_{n}
for the Jacobi coefficients of
\widehat{\mu}
. If
\mu,\widehat{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
then the following Christoffel determinantal formula
[
Christoffel
]
holds:

(26)

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{n}(x)={\Phi(x)}^{-1}D_{n}^{-1}\det\begin{pmatrix}P_{n+m}(x)&P_{n+m-1}(x)&\cdots&P_{n}(x)\\
P_{n+m}(z_{1})&P_{n+m-1}(z_{1})&\cdots&P_{n}(z_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
P_{n+m}(z_{m})&P_{n+m-1}(z_{m})&\cdots&P_{n}(z_{m})\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

where
D_{n}
is the normalizing constant

(27)

\begin{split}D_{n}=\det\begin{pmatrix}P_{n+m-1}(z_{1})&P_{n+m-2}(z_{1})&\cdots&P_{n}(z_{1})\\
P_{n+m-1}(z_{2})&P_{n+m-2}(z_{2})&\cdots&P_{n}(z_{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
P_{n+m-1}(z_{m})&P_{n+m-2}(z_{m})&\cdots&P_{n}(z_{m})\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

In the one-step case
m=1
this becomes

(28)

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{x-z_{0}}\left({P}_{n+1}(x)-\frac{{P}_{n+1}(z_{0})}{{P}_{n}(z_{0})}{P}_{n}(x)\right).\end{split}

By the Christoffel–Darboux formula (
19
) we get

(29)

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{n}(x)=\frac{\left\langle P_{n},P_{n}\right\rangle}{P_{n}(z_{0})}K_{n+1}(z_{0},x).\end{split}

Polynomials
K_{n}(z_{0},z)
are sometimes referred to as the kernel polynomials of
\mu
.

(
28
) together with the three-term recurrence (
16
) can be used to generate the recurrence equations (see
[
Gautschibook
]
)

(30)

\begin{split}\widehat{b}_{n}-\delta_{n+1}&=b_{n+1}-\delta_{n},\\
\widehat{a}_{n}-\delta_{n}\widehat{b}_{n}&=a_{n+1}-\delta_{n}b_{n},\\
\delta_{n-1}\widehat{a}_{n}&=\delta_{n}a_{n},\end{split}

with initial conditions
\widehat{a}_{0}=0
and
\delta_{0}=z_{0}-b_{0}
. From this, it is possible to compute the Jacobi coefficients of
\widehat{\mu}
from the Jacobi coefficients of
\mu
, through the following algorithm,

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\delta_{0}:=z_{0}-b_{0};\\
&\widehat{a}_{0}=0;\\
&\widehat{b}_{0}=b_{0}-\frac{a_{1}}{\delta_{0}};\\
&\mbox{for all }n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}:\\
&\qquad\delta_{n}:=\widehat{b}_{n-1}-b_{n}+\delta_{n-1};\\
&\qquad\widehat{a}_{n}=a_{n}\frac{\delta_{n}}{\delta_{n-1}};\\
&\qquad\widehat{b}_{n}=b_{n}+\frac{\widehat{a}_{n}-a_{n+1}}{\delta_{n}}.\end{split}

In Section
3.1
we show how this can be generalized to the multi-step Christoffel transform using multiple orthogonal polynomials.

2.4.
Basics of multiple orthogonal polynomials on the real line (MOPRL)

Let
r\geq 1
and consider a system of functionals
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
. Let us write
\left\langle P(x),Q(x)\right\rangle_{j}
for
\mu_{j}[P(x)Q(x)]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, a type II multiple orthogonal polynomial is a non-zero polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}(x)
such that
\deg{P_{\bm{n}}}\leq\left|\bm{n}\right|:=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}
, and

(32)

\begin{split}\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle_{j}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,n_{j}-1,\qquad j=1,\dots,r.\end{split}

Definition 2.5
.

A type I multiple orthogonal polynomial is a non-zero vector of polynomials
\bm{A_{\bm{n}}}=(A_{\bm{n}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}}^{(r)})
such that
\deg{A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}}\leq n_{j}-1
,
j=1,\dots,r
, and

(33)

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle_{j}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|-2.\end{split}

Note that
A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}=0
when
n_{j}=0
(we take the degree of
0
to be
-\infty
). Hence for
\bm{n}=\bm{0}
there would be no non-zero solutions to (
33
). In this case we take
\bm{A}_{\bm{0}}=\bm{0}
(the
r
-vector of zero polynomials) as the only type I polynomial.

It is easy to show that for any multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\setminus\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There is a unique monic type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
such that
\deg{P_{\bm{n}}}=\left|\bm{n}\right|
;

(ii)

There is a unique type I multiple orthogonal polynomial
(A_{\bm{n}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}}^{(r)})
such that

(34)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}(x),x^{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}\right\rangle_{j}=1.

(iii)

\deg P_{\bm{n}}=\left|\bm{n}\right|
for every non-zero solution of (
32
);

(iv)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}(x),x^{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}\right\rangle_{j}\neq 0
for every non-zero solution of (
33
);

(v)

\det M_{\bm{n}}\neq 0
, where

(35)

M_{\bm{n}}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}^{(1)}&c_{1}^{(1)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}^{(1)}\\
c_{1}^{(1)}&c_{2}^{(1)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|}^{(1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{n_{1}-1}^{(1)}&c_{n_{1}}^{(1)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|+n_{1}-2}^{(1)}\\
\hline\cr&&\vdots\\
\hline\cr c_{0}^{(r)}&c_{1}^{(r)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}^{(r)}\\
c_{1}^{(r)}&c_{2}^{(r)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|}^{(r)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{n_{r}-1}^{(r)}&c_{n_{r}}^{(r)}&\cdots&c_{\left|\bm{n}\right|+n_{r}-2}^{(r)}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
c_{n}^{(j)}
are the moments

\begin{split}c_{n}^{(j)}=\mu_{j}[x^{n}].\end{split}

Definition 2.6
.

If any and therefore each of the above conditions (i)–(v) are satisfied for an index
\bm{n}\neq\bm{0}
then we say that
\bm{n}
is normal. We always take the index
\bm{0}
to be normal.

Whenever
\bm{n}
is normal we will only work with polynomials satisfying (i) and (ii) above, that is,
P_{\bm{n}}
will be monic and
\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}
will satisfy (
34
).

Proposition 2.7
.

If
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{L}_{N_{j}}
with
N_{j}<\infty
, then indices
\bm{n}
with
n_{j}>N_{j}
can never be normal.

Proof.

In the case
r=1
this follows from Lemma
2.1
. In general, if
n_{j}>N_{j}
, we have

\begin{split}\left\langle P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x),x^{n_{j}-1}\right\rangle_{j}=\left\langle P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x),Q(x)\right\rangle_{j}\end{split}

for any degree
n_{j}-1
monic polynomial
Q
, given any choice of type II polynomial
P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}
(for the index
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
). If we choose
Q(x)=x^{n_{j}-1-N_{j}}P_{N_{j}}(x)
, where
P_{N_{j}}
is the degree
N_{j}
orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mu_{j}
, then we get

\begin{split}\left\langle P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x),x^{n_{j}-1}\right\rangle_{j}=\left\langle P_{N_{j}}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)x^{n_{j}-1-N_{j}}\right\rangle_{j}=0\end{split}

by Lemma
2.1
. This means that
P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}
satisfies all the orthogonality relations (
32
) for the index
\bm{n}
, but
\deg{P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}}<\left|\bm{n}\right|
, so
\bm{n}
cannot be normal.
∎

A system
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
in
\mathcal{L}^{r}
or in
\mathcal{M}^{r}
is usually said to be perfect if every
\mathbb{N}^{r}
-index is normal. We modify this notion to the case when
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{L}_{N_{j}}
where
N_{j}
may be finite.
We write
\bm{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\})^{r}
and denote

\mathbb{N}_{\bm{N}}^{r}:=\left\{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}\mid 0\leq n_{j}\leq N_{j}\right\}.

Definition 2.8
.

We say that
(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
is a perfect system if all indices in
\mathbb{N}_{\bm{N}}^{r}
are normal.

One important class of perfect systems is Angelesco systems. Usually they are defined for measures with infinite support, but we extend the notion of Angelesco system to allow measures of finite support.
If
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
we write
\Delta_{j}
for the convex hull of
\operatorname{supp}(\mu_{j})
and
\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}
for the interior of
\Delta_{j}
.

Definition 2.9
.

An Angelesco system is a
\mathcal{M}^{r}
-system such that
\Delta_{k}^{\mathrm{o}}\cap\Delta_{j}^{\mathrm{o}}=\varnothing
and if
\mu_{k}
and
\mu_{j}
are both finitely supported then we also require
\Delta_{k}\cap\Delta_{j}=\varnothing
(
k\neq j
).

With this definition one can show that any Angelesco system is perfect in the sense of Definition
2.8
. The proof goes along the same lines (counting real zeros of odd multiplicity of
P_{\bm{n}}
) as the standard argument for the case
\bm{N}=(\infty,\ldots,\infty)
, see e.g.
[
Ismail
, Thm 23.1.3]
.

2.5.
MOPRL: nearest neighbour recurrence relations

We remind the reader that whenever some index is normal then the type II multiple orthogonal polynomial at that location is always taken to be monic, and the type I polynomial is taken with the normalization (
34
).

It was shown by Van Assche
[
NNR
,
Ismail
]
that multiple orthogonal polynomials of type II and type I satisfy the following set of equations, called the nearest neighbour recurrence relations (NNRR for short). Although it was orinigally stated in
\mathcal{M}_{\infty}^{r}
, the proof is no different in
\mathcal{L}^{r}
.

Theorem 2.10
.

[Van Assche,
[
NNR
]
]

Let
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
. If
\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
are normal, then

(36)

\begin{split}xP_{\bm{n}}(x)=P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}(x)+b_{\bm{n},j}P_{\bm{n}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},i}P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\end{split}

for some constants
a_{\bm{n},1},\dots,a_{\bm{n},r}
and
b_{\bm{n},j}
. Here
a_{\bm{n},l}:=0
whenever
n_{l}=0
.

Remark 2.11
.

The recurrence coefficient
b_{\bm{n},j}
is defined when both
\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
are normal, and it is always unique, given our choice of normalization for the polynomials.
The coefficient
a_{\bm{n},i}
is defined when
\bm{n}
is normal and
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l}
is normal for some
l
.
a_{\bm{n},i}
is unique if
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}
is also normal. Indeed, (
36
) implies

(37)

a_{\bm{n},i}=\frac{\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{n_{i}}\right\rangle_{i}}{\left\langle P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x),x^{n_{i}-1}\right\rangle_{i}}

if
n_{i}>0
. Therefore the constants
a_{\bm{n},i}
are independent of the choice of
j
in (
36
) (i.e., if
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
are normal for several choices of
j
), but may depend on the choice of
P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{1}},\dots,P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{r}}
if any of the indices
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{r}
are not normal.

Remark 2.12
.

In particular, for perfect systems
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
with
N_{j}\leq\infty
, we have that
a_{\bm{n},j}
is defined for all
1\leq j\leq r
and all
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}}
except for
\bm{n}=\bm{N}
. This exception is only relevant if
N_{j}<\infty
for all
j
, of course. Let us adopt the convention
a_{\bm{N},j}:=0
for that case, for reasons that will become more clear later on in the paper. Assuming this, all perfect systems then have well-defined NNRR coefficiens
\{a_{\bm{n},j}\}_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}}}
and
\{b_{\bm{n},j}\}_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}-\bm{e}_{j}}}
for each
j
.

It is well-known that the type I polynomials satisfy similar recurrence relations. Since the explicit proof is hard to locate we provide a proof in Appendix
2.7
, using minimal normality assumptions.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
. If
\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
are normal, then

(38)

\begin{split}x\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}(x)=\bm{A}_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)+b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{\bm{n},i}\bm{A}_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}(x),\end{split}

for some constants
c_{\bm{n},1},\dots,c_{\bm{n},r}
. If
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}
and
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}
are normal then
c_{\bm{n},i}=a_{\bm{n},i}
.

Remark 2.14
.

As above,
c_{\bm{n},i}
are independent of the choice of
j
, but may depend on the choice of the vectors
(A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},1},\dots,A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},r})
when
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}
is not normal (for the details, see the Appendix).

By comparing (
36
) for two different
j
, and doing the same for (
38
), we obtain the following.

Corollary 2.15
.

Assume
\bm{n}
,
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
and
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l}
are normal. Then

(39)

P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l}}-P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}=(b_{\bm{n},j}-b_{\bm{n},l})P_{\bm{n}}.

Similarly, if
\bm{n}
,
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
and
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{l}
are normal, then

(40)

\bm{A}_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{l}}-\bm{A}_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}=(b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}-b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{l},l})\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}

Van Assche showed in
[
NNR
]
that (assuming sufficient normality conditions) the recurrence coefficients satisfy the partial difference equations in the theorem below. Their proof works for
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
without any changes. When we write
\bm{m}\leq\bm{n}
we mean
m_{j}\leq n_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,r
.

Theorem 2.16
.

[Van Assche,
[
NNR
]
]
Let
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
. Assume all indices
\bm{m}\leq\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}+\bm{e}_{l}
is normal. If
j\neq l
then

(41)

\displaystyle b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},l}=b_{\bm{n},j}-b_{\bm{n},l},

(42)

\displaystyle b_{\bm{n},l}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-b_{\bm{n},j}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},l}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},i},

(43)

\displaystyle a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}\left(b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}-b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},l}\right)=a_{\bm{n},j}\left(b_{\bm{n},j}-b_{\bm{n},l}\right).

If
n_{j}=0
or
n_{l}=0
then we only get the first two equations.

We can use (
41
) to rewrite the left hand side of (
42
) as

\begin{split}b_{\bm{n},l}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-b_{\bm{n},j}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},l}&=b_{\bm{n},l}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-b_{\bm{n},j}(b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}+b_{\bm{n},l}-b_{\bm{n},j})\\
&=(b_{\bm{n},l}-b_{\bm{n},j})b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-(b_{\bm{n},l}-b_{\bm{n},j})b_{\bm{n},j}.\end{split}

We write
\delta_{\bm{n},j,l}
for
b_{\bm{n},l}-b_{\bm{n},j}
. Now we can rewrite (
41
)–(
43
) in the alternative form,

(44)

\displaystyle b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}-\delta_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},j,l}=b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},j}-\delta_{\bm{n},j,l},

(45)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},i}-\delta_{\bm{n},j,l}b_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j},i}-\delta_{\bm{n},j,l}b_{\bm{n},j},

(46)

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j,l}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{l},j}=\delta_{\bm{n},j,l}a_{\bm{n},j},

which will be better adapted for our purposes.

Consider a sequence of normal indices
\left\{\bm{n}_{k}\right\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
that forms a path starting at the origin, i.e.,
\bm{n}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{n}_{k+1}=\bm{n}_{k}+\bm{e}_{j_{k}}
for some
j_{k}\in\left\{1,\dots,r\right\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Define the generalized Christoffel–Darboux kernel
\bm{K}_{\bm{n}}(x,y)=(K_{\bm{n}}^{(1)}(x,y),\dots,K_{\bm{n}}^{(r)}(x,y))
via

(47)

\bm{K}_{\bm{n}}(x,y)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}P_{\bm{n}_{k}}(x)\bm{A}_{\bm{n}_{k+1}}(y).

Using the NNRR one can show
[
NNR
]
that the following Christoffel–Darboux formula holds

(48)

(x-y)\bm{K}_{\bm{n}}(x,y)=P_{\bm{n}_{N}}(x)\bm{A}_{\bm{n}_{N}}(y)-\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},j}P_{\bm{n}_{N}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\bm{A}_{\bm{n}_{N}+\bm{e}_{j}}(y),

originally due to
[
DK04
]
.

2.6.
Normality criteria

Here we collect a series of results that allow to establish normality of indices for certain multiple orthogonality systems
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
. While basic, these results may be of interest on their own. We will use these results in Section
3.1
to find necessary and sufficient conditions for
(\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mu}_{r})
to be perfect.

We start with the following simple lemma. One direction is standard, see, e.g.,
[
Ismail
, Cor 23.1.1–23.1.2]
, while the other has appeared in
[
BCVA
]
, as well as
[
mopucrecurrence
]
(for the unit circle), see also
[
KVMOPUC
]
.

Lemma 2.17
.

\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
is normal if and only if

(49)

\begin{split}\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{n_{j}}\right\rangle_{j}\neq 0\end{split}

for every type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
corresponding to the index
\bm{n}
.

Similarly,
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
is normal if and only if
A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}
has degree
n_{j}-1
for every vector of type I multiple orthogonal polynomials
(A_{\bm{n}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}}^{(r)})
.

Proof.

If
\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{n_{j}}\right\rangle_{j}
is zero for some multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
, then
P_{\bm{n}}
satisfies every orthogonality condition for the index
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
, but
P_{\bm{n}}
has degree
\leq\left|\bm{n}\right|
, so
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
cannot be normal. Conversely, if
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
is not normal then (
32
) has a solution
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}
of degree
\leq\left|\bm{n}\right|
.
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}
satisfies the orthogonality conditions (

) for the index
\bm{n}
, so it is a multiple orthogonal polynomial for the index
\bm{n}
. In other words,
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}=P_{\bm{n}}
for some choice of
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}
and

, but then (

) for the index
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}
implies

\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{n_{j}}\right\rangle_{j}=0
.

If there is some

of degree
<n_{j}-1
then we have a non-zero solution to the system (

) for the index
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
, so
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
is not normal since normality condition (iv) is not satisfied. Conversely, if
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}
is not normal then there is a non-zero solution

to

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{(i)}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle_{i}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|-2,\end{split}

but then this is also a solution for the index
\bm{n}
, which means that there is an

of degree
<n_{j}-1
.
∎

In order to define the recurrence coefficients we required some indices to be normal. In turn, the recurrence coefficients can give us information about the normality of neighbouring indices, as we show next.

Lemma 2.18
.

The following holds true for the recurrence coefficients of a system

a)

Let

and

To check perfectness of certain systems the following two results may be useful. Note that direct application of Lemma

Theorem 2.19

(
[

]
)
.

\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{r}
is a perfect system if and only if there is a choice of type II multiple orthogonal polynomials

, such that

{\left\langle P_{\bm{n}}(x),x^{n_{j}}\right\rangle_{j}\neq 0}

Proof.

Necessity of (

Appendix: Proof of Theorem

Lemma 2.22
.

Suppose
\bm{n}
is normal. Then the vectors
\left\{(A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(r)})\right\}_{i=1}^{r}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

Consider the equation

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i}(A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(r)})=0.\end{split}

Since
\bm{n}
is normal
A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{(j)}
has degree
n_{j}
, by Lemma
2.17
, but
A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(j)}
has degree
<n_{j}
when
i\neq j
, so
c_{j}=0
, which proves linear independence.
∎

Remark 2.23
.

The type II equivalent of Lemma
2.22
is the linear independence of
P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e_{1}}},\dots,P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{r}}
, which also follows from Lemma
2.17
(see
[
DK04
,
Ismail
]
).

Proof of Theorem
2.13
.

We have

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x)-A_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{(k)},x^{p}\right\rangle_{k}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|-2.\end{split}

Now choose
d_{\bm{n},j}
such that

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x)-A_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{(k)}-d_{\bm{n},j}A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)},x^{p}\right\rangle_{k}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,\left|\bm{n}\right|-1.\end{split}

The polynomial
B_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}=xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x)-A_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{(k)}-d_{\bm{n},j}A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}
has degree
n_{k}
. Hence the above system of orthogonality relations is homogeneous with matrix
M_{\bm{n}}^{t}
with
r
columns added, where
M_{\bm{n}}
is given by (
15
). This matrix has nullity
r
, since
M_{\bm{n}}^{t}
has nullity
0
, by normality. Hence the solution space is of dimension
r
, and
(B_{\bm{n}}^{(1)},\dots,B_{\bm{n}}^{(r)})=(A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(r)})
are solutions for each
i=1,\dots,r
. By Lemma
2.22
every solution is thus on the form

\begin{split}B_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{\bm{n},i}A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(k)}.\end{split}

In other words we have the recurrence relation

\begin{split}xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x)=A_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{(k)}+d_{\bm{n},j}A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{\bm{n},i}A_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}}^{(k)}.\end{split}

To show that
d_{\bm{n},j}=b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}
, we compare with the recurrence relation

\begin{split}xP_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)=P_{\bm{n}}(x)+b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},i}P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x).\end{split}

From here we can write

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\right\rangle&=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}}(x)\right\rangle\\
&+b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\right\rangle\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},i}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle.\end{split}

The first term vanishes by the orthogonality relations of
P_{\bm{n}}
, and the last term vanishes by the orthogonality relations of
\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}
. The second factor in the middle term is
1
, so we have

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\right\rangle_{k}=b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}.\end{split}

On the other hand, if we instead apply a similar argument using the type I recurrence relation we end up with

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle xA_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\right\rangle_{k}=d_{\bm{n},j},\end{split}

so
d_{\bm{n},j}=b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{j},j}
.

Write
\kappa_{\bm{n},j}
for the degree
n_{j}-1
coefficient of
A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}
, so that
A_{\bm{n}}^{(j)}(x)=\kappa_{\bm{n},j}x^{n_{j}-1}+o(x^{n_{j}-1})
. By comparing the degree
n_{i}
coefficients in (
38
) we see that
\kappa_{\bm{n},i}=c_{\bm{n},i}\kappa_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},i}
. By Lemma
2.17
we know that
\kappa_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},i}\neq 0
, so we get

\begin{split}c_{\bm{n},i}=\frac{\kappa_{\bm{n},i}}{\kappa_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},i}},\end{split}

which shows independence of
j
.

What remains is to show that
c_{\bm{n},i}=a_{\bm{n},i}
. Note that

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle_{k}=1,\end{split}

by the orthogonality relations of
\bm{A}_{\bm{n}}
, assuming
\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}
is normal. By the orthogonality relations of
P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}
we instead get

\begin{split}\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(k)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle_{k}&=\left\langle A_{\bm{n}}^{(i)}(x),P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle_{i}\\
&=\kappa_{\bm{n},i}\left\langle x^{n_{i}-1},P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle_{i}.\end{split}

Similarly, if
\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i}
is normal then

\begin{split}\kappa_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},i}\left\langle x^{n_{i}},P_{\bm{n}}(x)\right\rangle=1,\end{split}

so we get

\begin{split}a_{\bm{n},i}=\frac{\left\langle x^{n_{i}},P_{\bm{n}}(x)\right\rangle}{\left\langle x^{n_{i}-1},P_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i}}(x)\right\rangle}=\frac{\kappa_{\bm{n},i}}{\kappa_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{i},i}}=c_{\bm{n},i},\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

3.
Main results

Let us setup the following notation for the remainder of this paper. We write

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})\in\mathcal{L}^{r},

\displaystyle\bm{\nu}

\displaystyle=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r-1})\in\mathcal{L}^{r-1},

that is,
\bm{\nu}
is
\bm{\mu}
with
\mu_{r}
removed, where
r\geq 2
.

As usual we allow
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{L}_{N_{j}}
with
N_{j}\leq\infty
, and denote
\bm{N}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r})
. We use

\bm{n}
for a multi-index in
\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}}
, and
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{r-1})
for a multi-index in
\mathbb{N}^{r-1}_{\bm{K}}
, where we denote
\bm{K}=(N_{1},\ldots,N_{r-1})
.

Finally, it is clear that the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials for
\bm{\nu}
at a location
\bm{k}
coincide with type II multiple orthogonal polynomials for
\bm{\mu}
at the location
(\bm{k},0)
. So we use the same label
P
for type II polynomials for
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\nu}
interchangeably, as it cannot lead to any ambiguity:
P_{(\bm{k},0)}=P_{\bm{k}}
, and similarly for type I polynomials
A^{(j)}_{(\bm{k},0)}=A^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}
for
1\leq j\leq r-1
.

3.1.
Christoffel Transforms of Type II Polynomials

Let
\mu_{r}\in\mathcal{L}_{m}
with
m=N_{r}<\infty
, and write
\Phi(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x-z_{j})
for the unique monic orthogonal polynomial of degree
m
with respect to
\mu_{r}
.
For example, if all
z_{j}
’s are distinct (which is true if
\mu_{r}\in\mathcal{M}_{m}\subset\mathcal{L}_{m}
), then
\mu_{r}
is of the form

(54)

\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{j}\delta_{z_{j}}

where
w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
for each
j=1,\dots,m
.
But in general
z_{j}
’s may overlap leading to more complicated functionals, see Remark
2.3
.

Consider the Christoffel transforms
\widehat{\mu}_{j}=\Phi\mu_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq r-1
, as in (
25
). We refer to

\widehat{\bm{\nu}}=\Phi\bm{\nu}=(\widehat{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\mu}_{r-1})

as the Christoffel transform of
\bm{\nu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r-1})
. We write
P_{\bm{k}}
for the type II polynomials with respect to
\bm{\nu}
and
\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}
for the type II polynomials with respect to
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
,
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{r-1}_{\bm{K}}
. Let
\{a_{\bm{k},j},b_{\bm{k},j}\}
denote the recurrence coefficients of
\bm{\nu}
, and
\{\widehat{a}_{\bm{k},j},\widehat{b}_{\bm{k},j}\}
denote the recurrence coefficients of
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
. The connection between the systems
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
and
\bm{\mu}=(\bm{\nu},\mu_{r})
is summarized by the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

An index
(\bm{k},m)
is normal for the system
\bm{\mu}=(\bm{\nu},\mu_{r})
if and only if
\bm{k}
is normal for the system
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
. In that case we have
P_{(\bm{k},m)}(x)=\Phi(x)\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)
.

Proof.

Suppose
(\bm{k},m)
is normal. By the orthogonality relations

(55)

\begin{split}\left\langle P_{(\bm{k},m)}(x),x^{p}\right\rangle_{r}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,m-1,\end{split}

we must have
P_{(\bm{k},m)}(x)=\Phi(x)Q(x)
for some polynomial
Q
with
\deg{Q}\leq\left|\bm{k}\right|
, by Lemma
2.1
. For the other inner products we then have

(56)

\begin{split}\left\langle\Phi(x)Q(x),x^{p}\right\rangle_{j}=0,\qquad p=0,1,\dots,k_{j}-1,\qquad j=1,\dots,r-1.\end{split}

Hence
Q
must be a multiple orthogonal polynomial with respect to
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
. Conversely, any multiple orthogonal polynomial
Q=\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}
with respect to
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
satisfies (
56
) by definition. (
55
) is then satisfied with
P_{(\bm{k},m)}(x)
replaced by
\Phi(x)\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)
, by Lemma
2.1
. Now,
(\bm{k},m)
is normal for
(\bm{\nu},\mu_{r})
if and only if every
P_{(\bm{k},m)}
has degree
\left|\bm{k}\right|+m
, if and only if every
\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}
has degree
\left|\bm{k}\right|
, if and only if
\bm{k}
is normal for
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
.
∎

Now we find the determinantal formula, together with a necessary and sufficient condition on the perfectness of
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{r-1}
is a perfect system, and all
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
are distinct.

i)

If
(\bm{k},m)
is normal,
then for any sequence of
\mathbb{N}^{r-1}
-indices
\left\{\bm{s}_{j}\right\}_{j=0}^{m}
with
\bm{s}_{0}=0
and
\left|\bm{s}_{j}\right|=j

\begin{split}\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)=\Phi(x)^{-1}D_{\bm{k}}^{-1}\det\begin{pmatrix}P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m}}(x)&P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m-1}}(x)&\cdots&P_{\bm{k}}(x)\\
P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m}}(z_{1})&P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m-1}}(z_{1})&\cdots&P_{\bm{k}}(z_{1})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m}}(z_{m})&P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{m-1}}(z_{m})&\cdots&P_{\bm{k}}(z_{m})\end{pmatrix},\\
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{r-1}_{\bm{K}}.\end{split}

If

for any
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{r-1}_{\bm{K}}
and any sequence of
\mathbb{N}^{r-1}

\left\{\bm{s}_{j}\right\}_{j=0}^{m}
with
\bm{s}_{0}=0
and
\left|\bm{s}_{j}\right|=j

\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
is perfect.

Proof.

i)
If
D_{\bm{n}}=0
then the columns of

are linearly dependent. This would imply

\begin{split}P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}c_{j}P_{\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{j}}(x)=0,\qquad x=z_{1},\dots,z_{r},\end{split}

for some
(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})\neq(0,\dots,0)
. However, since we have a linear combination of polynomials with different degrees,
P
cannot be identically
0
.
P
satisfies the orthogonality conditions at the index

with respect to

with

since
\bm{k}\leq\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{i}
for every

, and with respect to

by Lemma

since

is divisible by

The determinant in (

\bm{k}\leq\bm{k}+\bm{s}_{i}

with respect to

for

,

it is orthogonal to everything with respect to

. Hence the normality of
(\bm{k},m)
implies that

coincides with the determinant in (

implies (

).

ii)
Conversely, assume
\bm{\nu}
is perfect and

for any choice of path for all

. Fix any path

of multi-indices in
\mathbb{N}^{r-1}_{\bm{K}}
with
\bm{k}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
\bm{k}_{l+1}=\bm{k}_{l}+\bm{e}_{j_{l}}
.
For each
\bm{k}_{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{\bm{K}}^{r-1}

to be the polynomial on the right-hand side of (

for each

is monic of degree

and satisfies all the type II orthogonality conditions for

. Let us show that

satisfy the conditions of Remark

. Indeed, by (

(58)

Remark 3.3
.

If we remove the assumption
\bm{\nu}\in\mathcal{L}_{\infty}^{r-1}
then we get complications when

for all

, since a sequence of normal indices

still works if one replaces

Theorem 3.6

(The CC Algorithm,
[
ComputingNNR
]
)
.

Suppose
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
is perfect. Given the Jacobi coefficients of each
\mu_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, the following algorithm produces all the NNRR coefficients:

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }1\leq j,k\leq r\mbox{ with }j\neq k:

\displaystyle\qquad a_{\bm{n},j}:=0\mbox{ for all }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}}\mbox{ with }n_{j}=0;

\displaystyle\qquad\delta_{\bm{0},j,k}:=b_{\bm{0},k}-b_{\bm{0},j};

\displaystyle\mbox{for all }d\in\mathbb{N}:

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{for all }1\leq j,k\leq r\mbox{ with }j\neq k:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mbox{for all }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{\bm{N}}^{r}\mbox{ with }n_{k}>0,n_{j}>0,|\bm{n}|=d:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad a_{\bm{n},j}=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if }a_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},j}=0,\\
a_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},j}\frac{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},k,j}}{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{j},k,j}}&\mbox{otherwise};\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{for all }1\leq j,k\leq r\mbox{ with }j\neq k:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mbox{for all }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{\bm{N}-\bm{e}_{j}}^{r}\mbox{ with }n_{k}>0,|\bm{n}|=d:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad b_{\bm{n},j}=b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},j}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{e}_{k},i}}{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},k,j}};

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{for all }1\leq j,k\leq r\mbox{ with }j\neq k:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\mbox{ for all }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{\bm{N}-\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{e}_{k}}^{r},|\bm{n}|=d:

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\delta_{\bm{n},j,k}:=b_{\bm{n},k}-b_{\bm{n},j}.

Proof.

Assume all coefficients of indices with size
<d
are computed and consider indices
\bm{n}
with
\left|\bm{n}\right|=d
. Using (
46
) we can compute the coefficients
a_{\bm{n},j}
when
0<n_{j}<N_{j}
by

\begin{split}a_{\bm{n},j}=a_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},j}\frac{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},k,j}}{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{j},k,j}},\end{split}

where
k\neq j
and
n_{k}>0
(if such a
k
does not exist then
\bm{n}=d\bm{e}_{j}
, in which case
a_{\bm{n},j}
is already given). If
n_{j}=0
then we have
a_{\bm{n},j}=0
by definition, and if
n_{j}=N_{j}
then we have
a_{\bm{n},j}=0
by Theorem
3.5
, which agrees with the algorithm.

With every
a_{\bm{n},j}
computed, we can then use (
45
) to compute the coefficients
b_{\bm{n},j}
by

\begin{split}b_{\bm{n},j}=b_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},j}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n},i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}-\bm{e}_{k},i}}{\delta_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{k},k,j}},\end{split}

with
k
chosen such that
k\neq j
and
n_{k}>0
. Hence all the NNRR coefficients can be computed, by induction on
\left|\bm{n}\right|
(in the case
\left|\bm{n}\right|=0
there is nothing to compute).

∎

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\widehat{b}_{\bm{k},j}=b_{\bm{k},j}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\widehat{a}_{\bm{k},i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}a_{\bm{k}+\bm{e}_{j},i}}{\delta_{\bm{k},j}}.

Another example one often wants to consider is the two-step Christoffel transform of
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
with

where

.

might not be perfect however. For example,

\nu
is symmetric with respect to
\operatorname{Re}z_{0}
and

is odd, then

, the degree

, which shows that
(n,1)
is not normal in that case. Instead, let us consider the multiple orthogonality system system
(\nu,\omega)
, where
\omega\in\mathcal{L}_{2}

\omega=w_{0}\delta_{z_{0}}+(1-w_{0})\delta_{\bar{z}_{0}}

must be one of the type II polynomials at index

\displaystyle b_{0}

3.4.
An example on the step-line

In this section we work with a perfect system of two functionals
\bm{\nu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
and write
p_{n}
for the polynomials defined by
p_{2n}=P_{n,n}
and
p_{2n+1}=P_{n+1,n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
\left\{p_{n}\right\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are called the multiple orthogonal polynomials on the step-line. In the previous sections we have worked with recurrence coefficients from the nearest neighbour recurrence relation, but another recurrence relation restricted to the step-line is also commonly used.

We assume indices on the step-line
\left\{(n,n)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\cup\left\{(n+1,n)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are normal. The step-line recurrence relation is then given by

(70)

xp_{n}(x)=p_{n+1}(x)+c_{n}p_{n}(x)+b_{n}p_{n-1}(x)+a_{n}p_{n-2}(x).

If we put
a_{0}=a_{1}=0
and
b_{1}=0
then the recurrence relation holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
(with any choice of
p_{-1}
and
p_{-2}
). To see that (
70
) holds, simply choose
b_{n}
and
c_{n}
such that the polynomial
xp_{n}(x)-p_{n+1}(x)-c_{n}p_{n}(x)-b_{n}p_{n-1}(x)
is of minimal degree, and then verify that it satisfies all the orthogonality conditions for the polynomial
p_{n-2}
.

We put
\Phi(x)=x-z_{0}
and assume that
p_{n}(z_{0})\neq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A slight modification of Theorem
3.10
shows that
\widehat{\nu}=\Phi\nu
has all indices normal on the step-line. The polynomials
\widehat{p}_{n}
on the step-line then satisfy the recurrence relation

(71)

x\widehat{p}_{n}(x)=\widehat{p}_{n+1}(x)+\widehat{c}_{n}\widehat{p}_{n}(x)+\widehat{b}_{n}\widehat{p}_{n-1}(x)+\widehat{a}_{n}\widehat{p}_{n-2}(x).

Let us show how to compute the recurrence coefficients
\widehat{a}_{n}
,
\widehat{b}_{n}
, and
\widehat{c}_{n}
, assuming we are given
a_{n}
,
b_{n}
, and
c_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that
[
ComputingNNR
]
describes how to compute the step-line recurrence coefficients from the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients (see
[
DFK
, Sect 2.2]
for the same problem for an arbitrary increasing path of indices). Here, however, we would like to go directly from the step-line coefficients of
\bm{\nu}
to the step-line coefficients of
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
.

For the system
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\delta_{z_{0}})
we have
P_{n,m,0}(x)=P_{n,m}(x)
and
P_{n,m,1}(x)=(x-z_{0})\widehat{P}_{n,m}(x)
. When
(n,m)
is on the step-line we have the recurrence relations (
70
) and
(
71
)
. We also have the recurrence relation (
39
), which turns into

(72)

p_{n+1}(x)-(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n}(x)=\delta_{n}p_{n}(x).

We proceed by computing
(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n+1}(x)
using (
70
), (
71
), and (
72
), in two different ways (similarly to the proof of Theorem
2.16
in
[
NNR
]
). First, we have

\begin{split}(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n+1}=p_{n+2}-\delta_{n+1}p_{n+1}=xp_{n+1}-(c_{n+1}+\delta_{n+1})p_{n+1}-b_{n+1}p_{n}-a_{n+1}p_{n-1}.\end{split}

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n+1}&=x(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n}-\widehat{c}_{n}(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n}-\widehat{b}_{n}(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n-1}-\widehat{a}_{n}(x-z_{0})\widehat{p}_{n-2}\\
&=x(p_{n+1}-\delta_{n}p_{n})-\widehat{c}_{n}(p_{n+1}-\delta_{n}p_{n})-\widehat{b}_{n}(p_{n}-\delta_{n-1}p_{n-1})-\widehat{a}_{n}(p_{n-1}-\delta_{n-2}p_{n-2})\\
&=xp_{n+1}-\delta_{n}(p_{n+1}+c_{n}p_{n}+b_{n}p_{n-1}+a_{n}p_{n-2})-\widehat{c}_{n}p_{n+1}-(\widehat{b}_{n}-\delta_{n}\widehat{c}_{n})p_{n}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-(\widehat{a}_{n}-\delta_{n-1}\widehat{b}_{n})p_{n-1}+\delta_{n-2}\widehat{a}_{n}p_{n-2}\\
&=xp_{n+1}-(\delta_{n}+\widehat{c}_{n})p_{n+1}-(\delta_{n}c_{n}+\widehat{b}_{n}-\delta_{n}\widehat{c}_{n})p_{n}-(\delta_{n}b_{n}+\widehat{a}_{n}-\delta_{n-1}\widehat{b}_{n})p_{n-1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-(\delta_{n}a_{n}-\delta_{n-2}\widehat{a}_{n})p_{n-2}.\end{split}

By comparing the two results, using liner independence, we get the following result.

Theorem 3.12
.

If all indices on the step-line are normal for
\bm{\nu}
and

We now get the following computation algorithm.

Theorem 3.13
.

Suppose all indices on the step-line are normal for

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\delta_{0}:=z_{0}-c_{0};\\

&\qquad\widehat{c}_{n}=c_{n}+\frac{\widehat{b}_{n}-b_{n+1}}{\delta_{n}}.\end{split}

Proof.

for
n=0
, (

. The rest clearly follows from Theorem

We write

\{b_{\bm{n},j}\}_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}-\bm{e}_{j}}}
to be the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients of some perfect system.

This corresponds to a result central to the paper
[

]
, solved for

, see also
[

. Here we give an alternative simple proof, based on the lemmas in Section

, along with the CC algorithm. The special feature that appears when some of the measures/functionals are finitely supported is property

below.

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
\bm{N}=(N_{j})_{j=1}^{r}
, where

.
Suppose we are given the sets of complex coefficients
\{a_{\bm{n},j}\}_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}}}
and
\{b_{\bm{n},j}\}_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{\bm{N}-\bm{e}_{j}}}
for each

Assume they satisfy the partial difference equations
(
41
), (
42
), (
43
) along with the boundary conditions

a)

a_{\bm{n},j}=0
when
n_{j}=0
,

b)

a_{\bm{n},j}=0
when

.

and the normality conditions

c)

a_{\bm{n},j}\neq 0
when

,

Then there is a unique perfect system

Remark 3.20
.

By induction using (

), we see that d) can be replaced with
b_{\bm{0},j}-b_{\bm{0},k}\neq 0
when
j\neq k
. Alternatively, c) can be replaced with
a_{n\bm{e}_{j},j}\neq 0
for each
0<n<N_{j}
when

.

Remark 3.21
.

If we also assume
a_{n\bm{e}_{j},j}>0
for each
0<n<N_{j}
and some

, then additionally
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{M}_{N_{j}}
.

Proof.

By the Favard Theorem
2.2
, we can get functionals
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r}
such that
a_{n\bm{e}_{j},j}
and
b_{n\bm{e}_{j},j}
are the Jacobi coefficients of
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{L}_{N_{j}}
for each
j=1,\dots,r
, by
c)
. We now want to prove that
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
is perfect and that
a_{\bm{n},j}
and
b_{\bm{n},j}
are the recurrence coefficients of
\bm{\mu}
. We will use induction, to prove that for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
,

1)

Every index
\bm{n}\leq\bm{N}
such that
\left|\bm{n}\right|\leq M
is normal.

2)

The nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients of
\bm{\mu}
, for every index
\bm{n}\leq\bm{N}
such that
\left|\bm{n}\right|\leq M-1
are exactly the recurrence coefficients
\left\{a_{\bm{n},i},b_{\bm{n},i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}
.

This is true when
M=0
and
M=1
, since then we already know which indices of the form
\bm{n}=n_{j}\bm{e}_{j}
are normal, and the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients of
\bm{n}=\bm{0}
are
a_{\bm{0},j}
and
b_{\bm{0},j}
. Now assume 1) and 2) hold for

and suppose
\bm{n}\leq\bm{N}
and
\left|\bm{n}\right|=M+1
. If
\bm{n}=n_{j}\bm{e}_{j}
then we already know that 1) holds. Otherwise we can write
\bm{n}=\bm{m}+\bm{e}_{j}+\bm{e}_{k}
for some index
\bm{m}
and

. Since

we know that the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients for
\bm{m}
are

and

. In particular

is normal by Corollary

. Now the conditions of Theorem

are satisfied and we can apply CC algorithm to compute the nearest neighbour recurrence coefficients of the system
\bm{\mu}
for every

with

∎

3.9.
Zero interlacing for Christoffel transforms: general results

In this section we prove some new interlacing results for multiple orthogonal polynomials of type I and type II. Given two real polynomials
p(x)
and
q(x)
we
write
p(x)\sim q(x)
and
say that the zeros of polynomials
p(x)
and
q(x)
interlace if all the zeros of
p(x)
and
q(x)
are pairwise distinct, real, simple, and between every two consecutive zeros of one of the polynomials there lies exactly one zero of the other polynomial.

Let us call a polynomial real-rooted if all of its zeros are real. Recall (see, e.g.,
[
Ismail
]
) that type II and type I polynomials for any Angelesco system are real-rooted. Type II polynomials for any AT systems are also real-rooted.

The following result is well-known (this is part of the Hermite–Kakeya–Obreschkoff theorem).

Lemma 3.22
.

Suppose
p
and
q
are two polynomials with interlacing zeros
p(x)\sim q(x)
. Then

(85)

\displaystyle p(x)+\alpha q(x)\sim q(x),\mbox{ for any }\alpha\in\mathbb{R},

(86)

\displaystyle p(x)+\alpha q(x)\sim p(x),\mbox{ for any }\alpha\neq 0.

Corollary 3.23
.

Suppose
\bm{\nu}
and
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}=(x-z_{0})\bm{\nu}
are perfect for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
{P}_{\bm{k}+\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\sim{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)
, then
\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}
is real-rooted. Moreover,

(87)

\displaystyle(x-z_{0})\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)\sim P_{{\bm{k}}}(x),

(88)

\displaystyle(x-z_{0})\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}(x)\sim P_{{\bm{k}}+\bm{e}_{j}}(x).

Proof.

Immediate from (
61
) and Lemma
3.22
.
∎

Remark 3.24
.

Let
\bm{\nu}
be any Angelesco or AT system and suppose
z_{0}
is not a zero of any
P_{\bm{k}}(x)
. By Theorem
3.10
,
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
is perfect. Since interlacing for
\bm{\nu}
holds at every multi-index
[
Jacobioperatorontrees
,
Ismail
,
Interlacing
]
, Corollary
3.23
applies for any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and we obtain that every
\widehat{P}_{\bm{k}}
is real-rooted and interlacings (
87
)–(
88
) hold true for all such systems.

In particular, if
z_{0}
belongs to the support of one of the measures, then
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
is no longer a system of positive measures, so its perfectness is not trivial in that case.

Corollary 3.25
.

Suppose
\bm{\nu}
and
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}=(x-z_{0})\bm{\nu}
are perfect for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)\sim\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)
, then
{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)
is real-rooted. Moreover,

(89)

\displaystyle{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)\sim\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x),

(90)

\displaystyle{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)\sim\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{{\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j}}}(x).

Proof.

Use (
40
) and the leftmost equality in (
78
) to get

(91)

\bm{A}_{\bm{k}}-\widehat{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j}}=\delta\widehat{\bm{A}}_{\bm{k}},

where
\delta=\delta_{(\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j},0),m,j}
is non-zero by Lemma
2.18

b)
, and (
41
). If
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
then
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, and Lemma
3.22
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.26
.

If
\bm{\nu}
is Angelesco and
z_{0}
is outside of the convex hull of each
\mu_{j}
, then
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
is also Angelesco. For these systems, the zeros of
\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)
and
\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)
interlace for any index
\bm{k}
and any
j
(see
[
DenYat
,
FMM
]
), so Corollary
3.25
applies. Similarly, if
\bm{\nu}
is Nikishin, then so is
\widehat{\bm{\nu}}
.
\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}-\bm{e}_{j}}(x)
and
\widehat{A}^{(j)}_{\bm{k}}(x)
do interlace but not for all the indices
\bm{n}
(see
[
KVInterlacing
]
for more details), so Corollary
3.25
applies for such indices.

3.10.
Zero interlacing: continuous examples

While Corollaries
3.23
and
3.25
might seem tame and almost obvious, they allow to obtain interesting interlacing results when applied to the multiple analogues of the classical orthogonal polynomials. We collect them in Sections
3.10
and
3.11
.

We remark that recurrences (
61
) and (
91
) (as well as the NNRR for type II and type I polynomials) provide not only interlacing properties but also various relations that the classical MOPRL (as well as classical OPRL, as a special case
r=1
) must satisfy. It is highly likely that these have appeared in the literature in various forms. We focus on interlacing properties only.

3.10.1.
The multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind

Let
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
be the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials corresponding to the system
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}

\alpha_{j}>-1
and
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{k}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for

.

Since
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is an AT system and

(where

is the vector of

3.23
gives

P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{1}}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}+\bm{1}}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and any

.

3.10.4.
The Angelesco–Jacobi polynomials

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
corresponding to the system
\bm{\mu}^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\in\mathcal{M}^{2}

where

(92)

\displaystyle d\mu^{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}_{1}

\displaystyle=(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}|x|^{\gamma}\chi_{[a,0]}(x)\,dx,

3.11.
Zero interlacing: discrete examples

3.11.1.
The multiple Charlier polynomials

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}
corresponding to the discrete system
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{a}}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
given by

\mu_{j}^{\bm{a}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}^{k}}{k!}\delta_{k},

where
a_{j}>0
, and
\alpha_{j}\neq\alpha_{s}
for
j\neq s
.

Recall that
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is an AT system. Now note that
x\bm{\mu}^{\bm{a}}
is supported on
\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k\geq 1\}
and its weight at
\{k\}
is
\frac{a_{j}^{k}}{k!}k=a_{j}\frac{a_{j}^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}
. This means that up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
x\bm{\mu}^{\bm{a}}
shifted by
1
to the left coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{a}}
itself. Corollary
3.23
then gives

(98)

P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad xP_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{a}}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

The self-interlacing property
xP_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}(x)
implies a number of interesting properties, see
[
Fisk
, Sect 8.7]
. In particular, it is easy to see that (
98
) implies that the distance between any two roots of
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{a}}
is at least 1. In Theorem
3.27
below we provide a more general statement.

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{c};\beta}
corresponding to the discrete system
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{c};\beta}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
given by

\mu_{j}^{\bm{c};\beta}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta)_{k}c_{j}^{k}}{k!}\delta_{k},

where
\beta>0
,
0<c_{j}<1
, and
c_{j}\neq c_{s}
for
j\neq s
. Here
(x)_{0}=1
,
(x)_{k}=x(x+1)\ldots(x+k-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol.

Again,
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{c};\beta}
is an AT system, and
x\bm{\mu}^{\bm{c};\beta}
is supported on
\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k\geq 1\}
with the weight at
\{k\}
(

) being
\frac{(\beta)_{k}c_{j}^{k}}{k!}k=\beta c_{j}\frac{(\beta+1)_{k-1}c_{j}^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}
. This means that up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
x\bm{\mu}^{\bm{c};\beta}
shifted by

to the left coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{c};\beta+1}
. Corollary
3.23
then gives

P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{c};\beta+1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{c};\beta}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{c};\beta+1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{c};\beta}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

3.11.3.
The multiple Meixner polynomials of the second kind

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{c;\bm{\beta}}
corresponding to the discrete system
\bm{\mu}^{c;\bm{\beta}}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
given by

\mu_{j}^{c;\bm{\beta}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta_{j})_{k}c^{k}}{k!}\delta_{k},

where
\beta_{j}>0
,
0<c<1
, and
\beta_{j}-\beta_{s}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for

.

\bm{\mu}^{c;\bm{\beta}}
is an AT system, and up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
x\bm{\mu}^{c;\bm{\beta}}
shifted by

to the left coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{c;\bm{\beta}+\bm{1}}

3.23
then gives

P_{\bm{n}}^{c;\bm{\beta}+\bm{1}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{c;\bm{\beta}}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{c;\bm{\beta}+\bm{1}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{c;\bm{\beta}}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

3.11.4.
The multiple Krawtchouk polynomials

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{N;\bm{p}}
corresponding to the discrete finite system
\bm{\mu}^{N;\bm{p}}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
given by

\mu_{j}^{N;\bm{p}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}p_{j}^{k}(1-p_{j})^{N-k}\delta_{k},

where
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
0<p_{j}<1
, and
p_{j}-p_{s}\neq 0
for
j\neq s
.

\bm{\mu}^{N;\bm{p}}
is a discrete AT system (see
[
Discretemoprl
]
), and up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
x\bm{\mu}^{N;\bm{p}}
shifted by
1
to the left coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{N-1;\bm{p}}
. Corollary
3.23
then gives

P_{\bm{n}}^{N-1;\bm{p}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{N;\bm{p}}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{N-1;\bm{p}}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{N;\bm{p}}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
with
|\bm{n}|<N
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

Similarly, up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
(N-x)\bm{\mu}^{N;\bm{p}}
coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{N-1;\bm{p}}
. Corollary
3.23
then gives

P_{\bm{n}}^{N-1;\bm{p}}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{N;\bm{p}}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{N-1;\bm{p}}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{N;\bm{p}}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
with
|\bm{n}|<N
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

3.11.5.
The multiple Hahn polynomials

These are the type II multiple orthogonal polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}
corresponding to the discrete finite system
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}\in\mathcal{M}^{r}
given by

\mu_{j}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(\alpha_{j}+1)_{k}(\beta+1)_{N-k}}{k!(N-k)!}\delta_{k}

where
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\beta>-1
,
\alpha_{j}>-1
, and
\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{s}\neq 0
for
j\neq s
.

Again,
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}
is a discrete AT system (see
[
Discretemoprl
]
), and up to an inconsequential multiplicative normalization,
x\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}
shifted by
1
to the left coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}
, and
(N-x)\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}
coincides with
\bm{\mu}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}
. Corollary
3.23
then gives

(99)

P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)

and

(100)

P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
with
|\bm{n}|<N
and any
1\leq j\leq r
.

3.12.
Minimal distance between roots

In Section
3.11.1
we observed that the minimal distance between consecutive roots (sometimes called the
mesh
) of any type II multiple Charlier polynomial is larger than 1. In the next theorem we generalize this to all the other discrete multiple orthogonal polynomials discussed here. This also serves as a new elementary proof for the case of one measure
r=1
, for which this property has been well known, see
[
ChiSta
,
Lev67
,
KraZar
]
.

Theorem 3.27
.

The minimal distance between roots of any type II multiple Hahn (for
|\bm{n}|<N
), multiple Krawtchouk (for
|\bm{n}|<N
), or multiple Charlier polynomial is larger than 1. The minimal distance between roots of any type II multiple Meixner polynomial of the first or second kind is not smaller than 1.

Proof.

For the Charlier case this is clear from (
98
).

Consider the multiple Hahn case.
Since all the zeros of type II polynomials belong to the interval
[0,N]
(AT systems), then interlacing in (
99
) and (
100
) can be strengthened to
xP_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)
and

(N-x)P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)\sim P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)
.

This implies that below the first zero of
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)
there is no zero of either
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)
nor
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x)
. Then between the first and the second zero of
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)
there is exactly one zero of
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x-1)
and of
P_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N-1}(x)
. This proves that the distance between the first two zeros of
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{j}}^{\bm{\alpha};\beta;N}(x)
is larger than one. An easy inductive argument can be used to complete the proof.

The proof for the multiple Krawtchouk polynomials is identical.

For the Meixner of the first and second kind, recall that they can be obtained from the Hahn polynomials by taking a certain limit with respect to the coefficients
\bm{\alpha},\beta,N
, see
[
BCVA
]
. Since the minimal distance for the Hahn polynomials is
>1
, we get
\geq 1
in the limit.
∎
