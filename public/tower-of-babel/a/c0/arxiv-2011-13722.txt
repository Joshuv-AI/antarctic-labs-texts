Introduction

A long studied problem in combinatorics deals with the partition regularity of Diophantine equations.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
S
be either
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
P\in\mathbb{Z}\left[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right]
. We say that the equation
P\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right)=0
is partition regular on
S
if for every finite partition
C_{1},\dots,C_{r}
of
S
one can find an
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in C_{i}\setminus\{0\}
such that
P\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\right)=0
.

The earliest known result about the partition regularity of equations is due to I. Schur (see
[
18
]
) who established the partition regularity of the equation
x+y=z
on
\mathbb{N}
. Later, Rado generalised Schur’s Theorem and provided a necessary and sufficient condition for a finite system of linear homogeneous Diophantine equations to be partition regular
[
17
]
. For a single linear homogeneous equation, Rado’s result reads as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

(Rado’s Theorem) Let
S
be either
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
. A linear homogeneous equation
c_{1}x_{1}+\dots+c_{m}x_{m}=0
, with integer coefficients, is partition regular on
S
if and only if there is a non-empty
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
.

Motivated by Rado’s Theorem, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

A linear homogeneous polynomial
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}x_{i}
, such that
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, is said to be a Rado polynomial if there is a non empty
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
such that
\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}=0
.
If
P
is Rado, we will also say that the equation
P(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=0
is a Rado equation.

Following the works of F. Galvin and S. Glazer (see
[
12
, Section 5.6]
for historical remarks), ultrafilters on semigroups have been one of the tools used for the study of partition regularity of equations. We identify the set of all ultrafilters on a set
S
to
\beta S
, namely the Čech-Stone compactification of
S
as a discrete space. The general relationship between partition regularity and ultrafilters, that can be made even more precise in an abstract setting (see e.g.
[
12
, Theorem 5.7]
or
[
6
, Proposition 1.8]
), can be formulated for Diophantine equations as follows:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
S
be either
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
P\in\mathbb{Z}\left[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right]
be given. The equation
P\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right)=0
is partition regular on
S
if and only if there is an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}\in\beta S
such that for every
A\in\mathcal{U}
, one can find
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in A
satisfying
P\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\right)=0
.

Motivated by the above result, we introduce a notation that we will often use in what follows.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
S
be either
\mathbb{N}
or
\mathbb{Z}

and

Ultrafilters and their algebra

We assume that the reader knows the basic notions of the algebra on the Čech-Stone compactification of semigroups; in this Section, we only recall some known facts about idempotent ultrafilters and their connection with the partition regularity of equations. As it is well known, idempotent ultrafilters are related with the concept of finite products on semigroups: if

String solutions to linear polynomials

In this section we introduce basic results and notations about the relationships between strings of integers, ultrafilters and partition regularity problems. The relationship is well known in the context of Milliken-Taylor systems, as recalled in the Introduction. Here, however, we are interested in the relationships with single linear equations. Lemma 4.25

Proof.

Let

be the coefficients of

. Let
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|a_{i}c_{j}|\right)+1

Consider

(4.1)

among all

P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)
must be a Rado polynomial, i.e. some non-empty subset of
\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
sums zero.

We will divide the treatment into two parts: first the case in which
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
and, second, the case in which there is a pair inside
\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
that sums to zero.

Case 1:

c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
. If we allow for constant solutions, trivially this equation will have a solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
for all possible choices of
\sigma
. Therefore we restrict here to study the conditions under which such polynomials have an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
. Without loss of generality, we can assume
\gcd(c_{2},c_{3})=1
.

Observation 6.1
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
. Then
P
has an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}((\sigma))
if and only if it has a non-constant solution in
\mathfrak{G}((\sigma))
.

To solve our problem we first notice that, without loss of generality, we can restrict to the case
\sigma=\left(a_{1},a_{2}\right)
. In fact, we have the following:

Lemma 6.32
.

The equation
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
has an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
if and only if there is a
i<n
such that it has a injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(\left(a_{i},a_{i+1}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

Suppose that the equation has a injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
. Then, by Lemma
3.18
, there is a matrix
M
whose columns are coherent with
\sigma
and all pairwise distinct and satisfies
M\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\right)^{T}=0
. Note that, since
M
is coherent with
\sigma
and
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
, the first line of
M
must be
(a_{1},a_{1},a_{1})
; as a consequence, since the solution is injective, for at least one index
1<l\leq n
there is a row
L
in
M
which is the first non constant where
a_{l}
appears, say
L=\left(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\right)
. Again, the condition
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
forces
h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}
to be pairwise different. This means that the previous line should be either
L_{1}=(a_{l-1},a_{l-1},a_{l-1})
or
L_{2}=(a_{l},a_{l},a_{l})
. If the previous line is
L_{1}
, as
M
is coherent with
\sigma
,
L
must be a permutation of
(0,a_{l},a_{l-1})
; in this case, let

M_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{l-1}&a_{l-1}&a_{l-1}\\
h_{1}&h_{2}&h_{3}\\
a_{l}&a_{l}&a_{l}\end{pmatrix}

We have that
M_{1}
is coherent with
(a_{l-1},a_{l})
and that
M_{1}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})^{T}=0
, meaning that
P
has a solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(a_{l-1},a_{l}\right)
. Otherwise, the previous line should be
L_{2}
and, for an analogous reason,
L
must be a permutation of
(0,a_{l},a_{l+1})
; in this case, let

M_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{l}&a_{l}&a_{l}\\
h_{1}&h_{2}&h_{3}\\
a_{l+1}&a_{l+1}&a_{l+1}\end{pmatrix}

We have that
M_{2}
is coherent with
(a_{l},a_{l+1})
and that
M_{2}(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})^{T}=0
, meaning that
P
has a solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(a_{l},a_{l+1}\right)
. In both cases, the columns of the matrices are non-constant which, by Observation
6.1
, guarantees the presence of an injective solution.

Conversely, if the equation has a injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(\left(a_{i},a_{i+1}\right)\right)
, for some
i<n
, then there are distinct
h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\in\{0,a_{i},a_{i+1}\}
such that
P(h_{1},h_{2},h_{3})=0
. For each
j\leq 3
, let
\sigma_{j}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{i},h_{j},a_{i+1},\dots,a_{n})
. Then, each
\sigma_{j}
is coherent with
\sigma
and
P(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3})=0
. By Lemma
3.18
, the equation has a solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
.
∎

The set of solutions of the equation
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
is a linear space generated by the vectors
(1,1,1)
and
\left(0,c_{3},-c_{2}\right)
, so all solutions of the equation have the form

x_{1}=t,\;\;\;x_{2}=t+c_{3}u\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;x_{3}=t-c_{2}u

for some
t,u\in\mathbb{Z}
. This simple observation allows to easily deduce the following:

Lemma 6.33
.

If
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
and
\gcd\left(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\right)=\gcd\left(a_{1},a_{2}\right)=1
, the equation
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
has an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(\left(a_{1},a_{2}\right)\right)
if and only if there are
1\leq i,j\leq 3
such that a permutation of
\left(a_{1},a_{2}\right)
is equal to
\left(c_{i},-c_{j}\right)
.

Proof.

There is an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}\left(\left(a_{1},a_{2}\right)\right)
if and only if there are
t,u\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(t,t+c_{3}u,t-c_{2}u)
is a permutation of
(0,a_{1},a_{2})
. Now we just have to check the possible cases.

If
t-c_{2}u=0
, then either
a_{1}=t=c_{2}u
and
a_{2}=t+c_{3}u=(c_{2}+c_{3})u
or (analogously)
a_{1}=(c_{2}+c_{3})u
and
a_{2}=c_{2}u
; in both cases, as
\gcd(a_{1},a_{2})=1
, we have
u=1
; from the fact that
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
, we have in the first case
a_{1}=c_{2}
and
a_{2}=-c_{1}
, and in the second case
a_{1}=-c_{1}
and
a_{2}=c_{2}
. The cases
t+c_{3}u=0
or
t=0
are analogous.

∎

Putting together Lemmas
6.32
and
6.33
, and observing by linearity that
c_{1}x_{1}+c_{2}x_{2}+c_{3}x_{3}=0
is solvable in
\mathfrak{G}\left(\sigma\right)
if and only if
kc_{1}x_{1}+kc_{2}x_{2}+kc_{3}x_{3}=0
is solvable in
\mathfrak{G}\left(h\sigma\right)
for some
h,k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, we deduce the general characterisation.

Proposition 6.34
.

If
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
and
\sigma=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
, the equation
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
has an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
if and only if there are
1\leq i,j\leq 3
,
h\leq n-1
and
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
r\left(a_{h},a_{h+1}\right)=s\left(c_{i},-c_{j}\right)
.

Example 6.2
.

Let
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=3x_{1}-5x_{2}+2x_{3}
and consider
\sigma=(5,7,-10,-6,13)
. Then
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
has an injective solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
, because
(-10,-6)
is multiple of
(-5,-3)
.

Case 2:
Let us suppose now that there is a pair among
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
and
c_{3}
that sums zero; without loss of generality, we can consider
c_{1}=-c_{2}=c
,
c_{3}=d
and
\gcd(c,d)=1
, namely
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=c\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)+dx_{3}
. We shall show that the only equation satisfying the mentioned conditions solvable in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
, for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
, is Schur’s equation
x_{1}-x_{2}+x_{3}=0
.

Proposition 6.35
.

Consider the polynomial
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=c\left(x_{1}-x_{2}\right)+dx_{3}
, with
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and
\gcd(c,d)=1
. The following facts are equivalent:

1.

there is a non-empty reduced string
\sigma=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)
such that
\sigma\mathcal{U}\models P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
for all additively idempotent ultrafilters
\mathcal{U}\in\beta\mathbb{Z}
;

2.

\sigma\mathcal{U}\models P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
for all additively idempotent ultrafilters
\mathcal{U}\in\beta\mathbb{Z}
and for all non empty reduced strings
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
;

3.

|c|=|d|=1
, i.e.
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)
is Schur’s polynomial
x_{1}+x_{2}-x_{3}
.

Proof.

The implication
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
is a consequence of Theorem
2.11
and the fact that any member of an additively idempotent ultrafilter on
\mathbb{Z}
is an IP-set; the implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is trivial.

To conclude, let us show that
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
. Let
\sigma=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)
, where we assume without loss of generality that
\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=1
, and assume that the equation
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)=0
has a solution in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
. First, let us show that it must be that
|c|=1
. In fact, let
M
be a
k\times 3
, coherent matrix with
\sigma
, such that
M(c,-c,d)=0
; as each
a_{i}
has to appear in the third column of
M
, we always find
u_{i},v_{i}\in\{0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
such that

c\left(u_{i}-v_{i}\right)=da_{i}

(6.1)

As
\gcd(c,d)=1
, the equation (
6.1
) shows that, for each
i\leq n
,
c
divides
a_{i}
. As we assumed
gcd\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)=1
, this forces
|c|=1
.

Now we prove that
|d|\geq 3
cannot occur. Indeed, suppose that
|d|\geq 3
and consider
a=\max\{|a_{i}|\mid i\leq n\}
. Let
i_{0}:=\min\{i\leq n\mid|a_{i}|=a\}
. By changing
\sigma
with
-\sigma
, if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that
a_{i_{0}}=a
. By looking at the appearance of
a_{i_{0}}
in the third column of
M
, one can find
u_{0},v_{0}\in\{0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
such that
u_{0}-v_{0}+da_{i_{0}}=0
. Hence, we have that
|u_{0}-v_{0}|=|da|
, which is absurd, since
|u_{0}-v_{0}|\leq 2a
.

Finally, let us prove that
|d|
cannot be 2. Let us proceed by contradiction; by multiplying
P\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right)
by
-1
, if needed, we can assume that
d=2
. The idea is to show that, in the string
\sigma=\left(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\right)
, whenever
a
appears with index
i\leq n
, then
-a
must appear with an index
j>i
; and, analogously, whenever
-a
appear with an index
j^{\prime}
, then
a
must appear with an index
i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}
. Of course this cannot happen, as it would generate an infinite substring
(a,-a,a,-a,\dots)
of the finite string
\sigma
. To construct this substring, we proceed by recursion on the indices of
\sigma
. Let again
a=\max\{|a_{i}|\mid i\leq n\}
and
i_{0}=\min\{i\leq n\mid|a_{i}|=a\}
.

As before, we may assume that
a_{i_{0}}=a
. Then, there are
u_{0},v_{0}\in\{0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
such that
(u_{0},v_{0},a)
is a line of
M
, say with index
k_{0}\leq k
, which implies that
u_{0}-v_{0}=2a
. Hence, by the maximality of
a
, it must be
u_{0}=a
and
v_{0}=-a
. As
v_{0}\neq 0
and by the minimality of
i_{0}
,

I_{0}=\{i\leq n\mid i>i_{0}\text{ and }a_{i}=-a\}\neq\emptyset.

Let
i_{1}=\min I_{0}
and, by minimality of
i_{0}
, observe that
a_{i_{0}}
cannot appear in the second column of any line of
M
whose index is greater than
k_{0}
.

Now assume that, for a
r\leq n
, we have defined two increasing lists, namely
i_{0},\dots,i_{r}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
k_{0},\dots,k_{r}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, indexing the components of
\sigma
and the lines of the matrix
M
, respectively, in such a way that

1.

for each
j\in\{0,\dots,r\}
,
a_{i_{j}}=(-1)^{j}a
;

2.

for each
j\in\{0,\dots,r\}
,
a_{i_{j}}
appears in the third column of
M
at the line
k_{j}
; and

3.

fixing
j,l\in\{0,\dots,r\}
such that
l\leq j
,
a_{i_{l}}
do not appear in the second column of any line greater than or equal to
k_{j}
.

Thus, there must be
u_{r},v_{r}\in\{0,a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
such that the
k_{r}
-th line of
M
is
(u_{r},v_{r},a_{i_{r}})
, which means that
u_{r}-v_{r}=2a_{i_{r}}
, which can only happen if
u_{r}=a_{i_{r}}
and
v_{r}=-a_{i_{r}}
. By the item (3) above, we see that

I_{r}=\{i\leq n\mid i>i_{r}\text{ and }a_{i}=-a_{i_{r}}\}\neq\emptyset.

Define
i_{r+1}=\min I_{r}
and let
k_{r+1}
be the line of
M
where
a_{i_{r+1}}
appears in the third column. Then, the lists
i_{0},\dots,i_{r+1}
and
k_{0},\dots,k_{r+1}
must be increasing and also satisfy conditions (1), (2) and (3) above. This shows that if
d=2
, there is an infinite substring
(a,-a,a,-a,\dots)
of the finite string
\sigma
, which is absurd.
∎

Notice that, as a straight consequence of Proposition
6.35
, we get the existence of plenty of Rado equations that are not solvable in
\mathfrak{G}(\sigma)
, for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
. Precisely:

Corollary 6.36
.

Let
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\gcd(c,d)=1
. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

there exists a
\sigma\in\mathbb{Z}^{<\omega}
such that, for all additively idempotent ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}\in\beta\mathbb{Z}
,
\sigma\mathcal{U}\models c(x-y)+dz=0
; and

2.

c=d=1.
