Disentangling the Entangled Linkages of Relative Magnetic Helicity
Peter W. Schuck, Mark G. Linton

Abstract
Magnetic helicity, H H , measures magnetic linkages in a volume. The early theoretical development of helicity focused on magnetically closed systems in 𝒱 \mathcal{V} bounded by ∂ 𝒱 {\partial\mathcal{V}} . For magnetically closed systems, 𝒱 ∈ ℝ 3 = 𝒱 + 𝒱 ∗ \mathcal{V}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}}=\mathcal{V}+{\mathcal{V}^{*}} , no magnetic flux threads the boundary, 𝒏 ^ ⋅ 𝑩 | ∂ 𝒱 = 0 \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}|_{\partial\mathcal{V}}=0 . Berger & Field 1984 and Finn & Antonsen 1985 extended the definition of helicity to relative helicity, ℋ \mathcal{H} , for magnetically open systems where magnetic flux may thread the boundary. Berger 1999 ; Berger 2003 expressed this relative helicity as two gauge invariant terms that describe the self helicity of magnetic field that closes inside 𝒱 \mathcal{V} and the mutual helicity between the magnetic field that threads the boundary ∂ 𝒱 {\partial\mathcal{V}} and the magnetic field that closes inside 𝒱 \mathcal{V} . The total magnetic field that permeates 𝒱 \mathcal{V} entangles magnetic fields that are produced by current sources 𝑱 \boldsymbol{J} in 𝒱 \mathcal{V} with magnetic fields that are produced by current sources 𝑱 ∗ \boldsymbol{J}^{*} in 𝒱 ∗ {\mathcal{V}^{*}} . Building on this fact, we extend Berger 2003 ’s expressions for relative magnetic helicity to eight gauge invariant quantities that simultaneously characterize both of these self and mutual helicities and attribute their origins to currents 𝑱 \boldsymbol{J} in 𝒱 \mathcal{V} and/or 𝑱 ∗ \boldsymbol{J}^{*} in 𝒱 ∗ {\mathcal{V}^{*}} , thereby disentangling the domain of origin for these entangled linkages. We arrange these eight terms into novel expressions for internal and external helicity (self) and internal-external helicity (mutual) based on their domain of origin. The implications of these linkages for interpreting magnetic energy is discussed and new boundary observables are proposed for tracking the evolution of the field that threads the boundary.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(30a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=

\displaystyle 0\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=0\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in{\partial\mathcal{V}},

(30b)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=

\displaystyle 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla^{2}\psi\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=0\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(30c)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}\in{\partial\mathcal{V}}.

(30d)

Combining the results in Equations (

), (

)
and (

), and making use of

), (

), (

),
and (

),

\psi\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\psi\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\psi\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(31)

or

\boldsymbol{P}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(32)

and we see that the traditional Neumann potential field described by

\boldsymbol{P}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi
conflates the magnetic field produced by internal current
sources
\boldsymbol{J}
and external current sources
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
as discussed in the
introduction. A similar conflation occurs for the closed field
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}
. The
closed field produced by external currents is

\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,x\right)=\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(33)

and then combining the results in Equations (

),
(

) and (

), and making use of

), (

), and (

),

\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(t,x\right)=\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,x\right)+\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,x\right)=\boldsymbol{B}\left(t,x\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(t,x\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.

(34)

For the external current system
\boldsymbol{J}
to
contribute
to the closed field

\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}
in
\mathcal{V}
is perhaps not surprising given
Figure

. However, the external current system
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in

{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
can produce closed-closed helicity
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}
in
\mathcal{V}
by generating
closed field in
\mathcal{V}
on its own! The presence of current systems that pass
from
\mathcal{V}
to
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
or vice versa also implies that

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\neq 0\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.

(35)

The external current,
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
, in volume,
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
, injects magnetic
vorticity into
\mathcal{V}
.
This is apparent if we consider the curl
of (
29b
)

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(36)

where the observation point
\boldsymbol{x}
is in
\mathcal{V}
not
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
. Using the vector
relationship

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\right)-\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{a},

(37)

this becomes

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]-\frac{1}{c}\,\nabla^{2}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}}.

(38)

The kernel in the second term has the form of a delta distribution
because

\nabla^{2}\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|^{-1}=-4\,\pi\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\delta\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)&\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{V}^{*}},\\
\alpha^{*}\left(x\right)\,\delta\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)&\boldsymbol{x}\in{{\partial\mathcal{V}}^{*}},\\
0&\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.\end{array}\right.

(39)

leading to

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\alpha^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(40)

where

\alpha^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)=1-\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{V}^{*}}\\
\left.\begin{array}[]{ll}1/2&\mbox{smooth surfaces}\\
3/4&\mbox{edges of $\mathcal{V}$}\\
7/8&\mbox{vertices of $\mathcal{V}$}\end{array}\right\}&\boldsymbol{x}\in{{\partial\mathcal{V}}^{*}}\\
0&\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}\end{array}\right.,

(41)

follows from Equation (
6b
) for
\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
and for
the values in braces we have assumed that
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
encloses
\mathcal{V}
.
Passing the divergence under the integral operator, using

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\frac{1}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\frac{1}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|},

(42)

and

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(\phi\,\boldsymbol{a}\right)=\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi+\phi\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{a},

(43)

this simplifies to

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\frac{1}{c}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left[\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}-\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\alpha^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}}.

(44)

The Gauss-Ostrogradsky theorem where
\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}
points into
\mathcal{V}

\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x}\,\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}=-\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{a},

(45)

relates the volume integral of the divergence to a surface integral over the
normal component at the boundary of the volume. Then with the
Gauss-Ostrogradsky theorem, Equation (
44
) becomes

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left[\oint\limits_{{{\partial\mathcal{V}}^{*}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}+\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}^{*}}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]}^{{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}/{\partial t}}=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\alpha^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(46a)

or with
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^{*}=0
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\frac{1}{c}\,\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}}^{-{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}/{\partial t}}=0\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.

(46b)

where in the last expression we have taken the surface integral with
respect to
{\partial\mathcal{V}}
instead of
{{\partial\mathcal{V}}^{*}}
, used
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^{*}=0
as implied
by Ampere’s law (
8
), and assumed that the normal component
of the current is continuous across the boundary

\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{J}-\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)=0
for

\boldsymbol{x}\in{\partial\mathcal{V}}
. Equation (
46b
) has the form of the
Ampère-Maxwell equation

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}{\partial t}=0\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V},

(46c)

where there is no external material current
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in
\mathcal{V}
. Thus, the
magnetic vorticity produced in
\mathcal{V}
is
balanced, but not generated
,
by a time-dependent electric field in
\mathcal{V}
, the so-called ‘displacement
current,’ and both are produced by
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
or on
{\partial\mathcal{V}}
. We
emphasize that the displacement current
\partial\boldsymbol{E}/\partial t

is not
a source of magnetic field.
Closed magnetic field in the sense of

\oint\,\boldsymbol{B}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\cdot{d\ell}\neq 0
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}
, indicates
the presence of magnetic vorticity—and not the exclusive presence of a
local material current
\boldsymbol{J}
. The source of this magnetic vorticity in
\mathcal{V}

may be a non-local current source, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
.

The presence of displacement currents must be reconciled with
Ampére’s law (
8
). Consider

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)
by following the derivation
of (
46a
) mutatis mutandis

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left[\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\,\frac{\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x^{\prime}}\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]}^{{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}/{\partial t}}=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(47a)

or with
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=0
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\overbrace{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}}^{{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}/{\partial t}}=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.

(47b)

which has a material current because

\nabla^{2}\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|^{-1}=-4\,\pi\,\delta\left(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)
is a
delta distribution for

\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
.
This also has the form of the Ampère-Maxwell equation

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{E}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)}{\partial t}=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{V}.

(47c)

Combining Equations (
46a
) and (
47a
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c}\,\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{\nabla}\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\oint\limits_{{\partial\mathcal{V}}}{dS^{\prime}}\,\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\left|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right|}\right]}^{\mbox{Net
Displacement Current}}=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\left[\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\alpha^{*}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right]\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(48a)

the displacement currents cancel and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{B}}=\frac{4\,\pi}{c}\,\boldsymbol{j}\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}},

(48b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}\left(t;\boldsymbol{x}\right)=\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\,\boldsymbol{J}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)+\left[1-\alpha\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right]\,\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\left(t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}}

(48c)

recovers Ampére’s law (
8
) with
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}=0
for

\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathbb{R}^{3}}
. Thus, even when the
net displacement current density is
zero in
\mathcal{V}
,
as implied by Ampère’s law (
8
), there may
be external contributions from
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
to
\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}
and displacement
currents in
\mathcal{V}
.

and

in

)

,

The last missing piece is to compute
\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*};t,\boldsymbol{x}\right)
from

B
. The external relative helicity which
corresponds to the
external
current sources is then

Berger 2003

\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in

\boldsymbol{J}^{*}

in

\boldsymbol{J}

V.3
The Relative Helicity of the Mutual Linkages Between the Internal
\boldsymbol{J}
and External
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}

Above we have established four gauge invariant quantities that describe the
relative helicity of the linkages produced by currents
\boldsymbol{J}
and

\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in
\mathcal{V}
and
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
, respectively:
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}\right)
,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}\right)
,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)
, and

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)
. Four other gauge invariant quantities may be
constructed that describe the relative helicity of the mutual linkages between
fields that have their origin in currents
\boldsymbol{J}
and
\boldsymbol{J}^{*}
in the internal

\mathcal{V}
and external
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
volumes, respectively:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x}\,\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{A}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)-\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)}\cdot\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)}}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)}+\underbrace{2\,\int_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x}\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\cdot\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)}}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)},

(52a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x}\,\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{A}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)-\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)}\cdot\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)}}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)}+\underbrace{2\,\int_{\mathcal{V}}{d^{3}x}\,\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{P}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\cdot\overbrace{\left[\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{BS}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)-\boldsymbol{P}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\right]}^{\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)}}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)}.

(52b)

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)
by
reciprocity, but

\mathcal{H}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\neq\mathcal{H}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)
because

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J},\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\neq\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cl}}^{\mathrm{o}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*},\boldsymbol{J}\right)
. To prove
reciprocity, the difference between the integrands of the first term in
Equations (
52a
) and (
52b
) may be expressed

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)-\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right),

(53a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left[\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}^{*}\right)\boldsymbol{\times}\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{cl}}\left(\boldsymbol{J}\right)\right].

in

and magnetic field in
{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
produced by

\boldsymbol{J}
in
\mathcal{V}

Tracking the evolution of the seven
independent terms will provide insight into how magnetic linkages change
during fundamental stellar processes such as flux emergence, coronal heating,
and eruptive phenomena. However, tracking the evolution of these terms
requires access to dense magnetic field measurement presently only available
in simulations and modeling

. Nonetheless, further
consideration of the helicity transport across boundaries in terms of this
framework may reveal observables that can be computed from photospheric
observations alone
(

. We have also decomposed the magnetic energy in a volume
into terms that describe the origin of the magnetic fields. This
representation results directly in terms that may be computed from surface

techniques

between the photosphere, convection zone, and corona during solar eruptive
phenomena.

The concept of cause and effect from currents to magnetic fields outlined in
this work has broad application to solar physics. Attributing changes in
current systems that lead to changes in magnetic structure has the potential
to reveal causality in ‘sympathetic’ solar eruptions

. Furthermore, combining the attribution of currents in
simulations presented here with new attribution techniques, such as

applicable to the photospheric surface has the potential to unambiguously
connect the photospheric/chromospheric magnetic fingerprints of eruptive
phenomena to coronal current systems, e.g., the photospheric fingerprints of
the formation of the flare current sheet in the corona.

We often ignore the interaction between the external universe

or

{\partial\mathcal{V}}
and the evolution of magnetic fields
in our volume of interest
\mathcal{V}

{\mathcal{V}^{*}}
are
often major players in the evolution of the magnetic field in modeling the

\mathcal{V}

currents provides a deeper and clearer understanding of the evolution of
astrophysical plasmas and MHD simulations.
