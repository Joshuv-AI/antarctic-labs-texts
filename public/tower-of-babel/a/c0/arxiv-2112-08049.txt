Abstract
We discuss a system of two coupled parabolic equations that have degenerate diffusion constants depending on the energy-like variable. The dissipation of the velocity-like variable is fed as a source term into the energy equation leading to conservation of the total energy. The motivation of studying this system comes from Prandtl’s and Kolmogorov’s one and two-equation models for turbulence, where the energy-like variable is the mean turbulent kinetic energy. Because of the degeneracies there are solutions with time-dependent support like in the porous medium equation, which is contained in our system as a special case. The motion of the free boundary may be driven by either self-diffusion of the energy-like variable or by dissipation of the velocity-like variable. The cross-over of these two phenomena is exemplified for the associated planar traveling fronts. We provide existence of suitably defined weak and very weak solutions. After providing a thermodynamically motivated gradient structure we also establish convergence into steady state for bounded domains and provide a conjecture on the asymptotically self-similar behavior of the solutions in ℝ d {\mathbb{R}}^{d} for large times.

Introduction

On a smooth domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we consider the degenerate parabolic system

\displaystyle\dot{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\eta(w)\nabla v\big),

for

\displaystyle(t,x)\in{]0,\infty[}{\times}\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\dot{w}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\kappa(w)\nabla w\big)+\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}

for

\displaystyle(t,x)\in{]0,\infty[}{\times}\Omega,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle 0=\eta(w)\nabla u\cdot{\mathrm{n}},\hskip 10.00002pt0=\kappa(w)\nabla w\cdot{\mathrm{n}}

for

\displaystyle(t,x)\in{]0,\infty[}{\times}\partial\Omega,

(1.1c)

where
v(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
can be considered as a shear velocity and
w(t,x)\geq 0

is an internal energy. Here the functions
w\mapsto\eta(w)
and
w\mapsto\kappa(w)
describe the viscosity law for
v
and the energy-transport coefficient
for
w
. Throughout this work we will mainly restrict to the choice

\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\beta},

(1.2)

where
\alpha,\beta,\eta_{0},\kappa_{0}>0
are given parameters.

The main feature of the model is that the shearing dissipation

\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}
is feeding into the energy equation such that in addition
to the total momentum
{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)
also the total
energy
{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)
are conserved along solutions:

{\mathcal{V}}(v,w):=\int_{\Omega}v(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{E}}(v,w):=\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x

One difficulty of the coupled system is that the viscosity coefficient

\eta(w)
and the energy-transport coefficient
\kappa(w)
can be unbounded, but
this problem will play a minor role in our work. The main emphasis is on the
degeneracies arising from the fact that

\eta(0)=\kappa(0)=0
and that the solutions of our interest have a nontrivial
support. Thus, we are deriving a theory for solutions that have

(v(t,x),w(t,x)=(0,0))
in regions of the
Q_{T}=[0,T]{\times}\Omega
of full
measure. In particular, we are interested in the free boundary arising at the
boundary of the time-dependent support of
w(t,\cdot)
. There is already some
existence theory for related models motivated by turbulence in fluids, see

[
GL∗03
,
LeL07
,
DrN09
]
for stationary models and

[
Nau13
,
MiN15
,
BuM19
,
MiN20
]
for time-dependent
models. However, there it is either assumed that
w^{0}(x)\geq\underline{w}>0
for
all
x\in\Omega
or that
w^{0}(x)=0
for all
x\in\partial\Omega
and

w^{0}(x)>0
a.e. in
\Omega
such that
\int_{\Omega}\log w^{0}(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x>-\infty
, see Remark
5.6
.

We provide a preliminary existence theory for our coupled system in Section

6
which allows for solution with nontrivial support,
\Omega\setminus\mathrm{sppt}(w(t))
has nonempty interior. However, we emphasize that this is
just for completeness and we rather focus on the growth behavior of the
support, i.e. the moving free boundary. Moreover, we emphasize that the
present paper is not a typical paper in applied analysis, but rather a paper in
modeling. Many of the
statements in this paper are in fact conjectures, and only a few results
are formulated rigorously as propositions or theorems. Nevertheless, we believe
that the degenerate coupled system is relevant in applications and open up new
avenues for developing the tools in applied analysis, in particular in the
field of free-boundary problems. The system specific enough to analyze it in
more detail, it is close enough to the porous medium
equation (PME) to lend some of the tools
from there, but it displays a richer structure of nontrivial effects stemming
from the coupling between the two scalar equations.

1.1
) contains the PME, when restricting

:

\dot{w}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\kappa(w)\nabla w\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }{]0,\infty[}{\times}\Omega,\hskip 20.00003pt\kappa(w)\nabla w\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }{]0,\infty[}{\times}\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\binom{\dot{v}}{\dot{w}}={\mathbb{K}}(v,w){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)={\mathbb{K}}(v,w)\binom{0}{\sigma^{\prime}(w)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }

(1.5)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}(v,w)\binom{\zeta}{\xi}=\binom{-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(p_{1}(w)(\nabla\zeta-\xi\nabla v)\big)}{-p_{1}(w)\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta+p_{1}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\xi-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(p_{2}(w)\nabla\xi\big)}

We also compare our model to the plasma model discussed
[
RoH85
,
RoH86
,
HyR86
]
for the mass density

\rho_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\rho\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\big(\rho\theta\big)_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\delta}\phi_{2}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\theta+\theta\rho^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\rho\big),

Section
3
is devoted to steady states and traveling

(v,w)=(v^{0},0)
are steady states, which we call trivial steady

(v,w)=(v_{*},w_{*})=

(\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}},\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})

In Section
3.2
we study planar traveling fronts of the form

\big(v(t,x),w(t,x)\big)=\big(V(z),W(z)\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }z=x_{1}{+}c_{\mathrm{F}}t,

where

is the front speed. It is well-known that the planar fronts play an important
role in the theory of the PME (cf.
[
Váz07
, Sec. 4.3]
), and we
expect a similar role for our coupled system (
1.1
), in particular,
for the understanding of the propagation of the boundary of the support.
Inserting this ansatz into (
1.1
) and assuming
V(z)=W(z)=0
for

z\leq 0
, which simulates a
support propagating with front speed
c_{\mathrm{F}}
, we obtain after integrating each
equations once (see Section
3
for details) the two ODEs

c_{\mathrm{F}}\,V=\eta(W)V^{\prime},\hskip 10.00002ptc_{\mathrm{F}}\,\big(W-\frac{1}{2}V^{2})=\kappa(W)W^{\prime}.

We analyze all solutions of this system, for
the different cases occurring for the choices in (
1.2
). To highlight
one of the results, we consider the case
\eta(w)=w
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w
, i.e.
\alpha=\beta=1
. For

\kappa_{0}\geq 1/2
all traveling fronts have the form

(V(z),W(z))=(0,\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}}\,c_{\mathrm{F}}z)
for

, which
corresponds to the case of the pure PME with
v\equiv 0
. These solutions
still exists for
\kappa_{0}\in{[0,1/2[}
, but now additional, truly coupled
solutions exists:

(V(z),W(z))=\Big(2\sqrt{(1{-}2\kappa_{0})\,c_{\mathrm{F}}z\,}\>,\>2c_{\mathrm{F}}z\Big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }z\geq 0.

For these solutions, the propagation of the support of
w
is not only driven
by self-diffusion as for the PME, but is is driven also by the
generation of
w
via the source term
\eta(w)|\nabla u|^{2}
. This is best seen
in the limit
\kappa_{0}\to 0
, where self-diffusion disappears but
propagation is still possible. In particular we obtain
c_{\mathrm{F}}=\max\{\kappa_{0},1/2\}W^{\prime}(0)
, which again shows that for
\kappa_{0}<1/2
the

In Section
3.3
we conjecture that the typical behavior of

w
near the boundary of its support is given by

with
\gamma=\max\{1/\alpha,1/\beta\}
, which clearly shows that the front
is driven by the
v
-diffusion in case of
\beta>\alpha
. In the critical case

\alpha=\beta
the switch between the two regimes occurs for

\eta_{0}=2\kappa_{0}
.

(

2
The model and its thermodynamical formulation

Here we discuss the basic properties of system (
1.1
),
namely the conservation laws for total linear momentum and the total
energy, the symmetries and scalings, as well as exact similarity solutions.
Section (
2.4
) provides gradient structures which
allows us to show convergence into steady state for the case
\alpha=\beta=12

and
\Omega
bounded, see Theorem
2.3
.

2.1
Conservation laws

We first observe that the divergence structure of the equation for
v
and the
no-flux boundary condition provide
the conservation of the integral over
v
, namely

{\mathcal{V}}(v,w):=\int_{\Omega}v(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

We call this conserved quantity the a momentum because in the thermodynamical
interpretation below
v
should be considered as a velocity, and it should not
be mistaken for a concentration of a diffusing species.

In fact,
w
should be considered as an internal energy such that the
energy density

e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w

plays an important role. It satisfies a conservation law without source
term, namely

\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\kappa(w)\nabla w+\eta(w)v\nabla v\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(w)\nabla e+(\kappa(w){-}\eta(w))\nabla w\big).

(2.1)

Integration over
\Omega
and exploiting the no-flux boundary conditions
gives conservation of the total energy

{\mathcal{E}}(v,w):=\int_{\Omega}\big(\,\frac{1}{2}v(x)^{2}+w(x)\,\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\text{const.}

2.2
Symmetries and scaling properties

The full set symmetries of system (
1.1
) are given for

\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For subsets
\Omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
only those symmetries survive
that are valid for
\Omega
. These symmetries hold for general functions
\eta

and
\kappa
.

Euclidean symmetry:
For all
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
Q\in{\mathrm{O}}(d):=\big\{\,R\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d{\times}d}\>\big|\>R^{\top}R=I\,\big\}
and solutions
(v,w)
of (
1.1
),
the rigidly moved pair

\big(\widehat{v}^{Q,x_{*}},\widehat{w}^{Q,x_{*}}\big)(t,x):=\big(v(t,Qx{+}x_{*}),w(t,Qx{+}x_{*})\big)

is a solution again.

Time and Galilean invariance:
For all
t_{*}\geq 0
and
V_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
the
time and velocity shifted pair

\big(\widetilde{v}^{t_{*},V_{*}},\widetilde{w}^{t_{*},V_{*}}\big)(t,x):=\big(v(t{+}t_{*},x)+V_{*}\,,\;w(t{+}t_{*},x)\big)

is a solution again.

Now we discuss scaling properties. Observing that all terms involve either one
time derivative or two spatial derivatives we have the following invariance of
(
1.1
):

Scaling S1 (parabolic scaling):
Assume
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. If the pair

(v,w)
is a solution of (
1.1
) and
\lambda>0
, then the pair

(v_{\lambda},w_{\lambda})
is a solution as well, where

(v_{\lambda},w_{\lambda})(t,x)=(v,w)(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x).

A more complex symmetry occurs if the viscosity
\eta
and the diffusion
constant
\kappa
are of the same power-law type.

Scaling S2 (nonlinear scaling):
Assume that
\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}

and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
and

\eta_{0},\kappa_{0}\geq 0
. If the pair
(v,w)
is a solution of
(
1.1
) and
\mu,\lambda>0
and
\lambda=1
in the case

\Omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then the pair

\big(v^{\mu,\lambda},w^{\mu,\lambda}\big)
is a solution as well, where

\big(v^{\mu,\lambda},w^{\mu,\lambda}\big)(t,x)=\big(\mu\,v(\mu^{2\alpha}\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x),\mu^{2}w(\mu^{2\alpha}\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x)\big).

Note that the energy density
e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
scales similarly to
w
and
the total conserved quantities satisfy

{\mathcal{V}}(v^{\mu,\lambda},w^{\mu,\lambda})=\frac{\mu}{\lambda^{d}}{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{E}}(v^{\mu,\lambda},w^{\mu,\lambda})=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\lambda^{d}}{\mathcal{E}}(v,w).

The main observation is that the two conserved functionals scale
differently. Hence, it is not possible to have exact similarity solutions with both,

{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)
and
{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)
being finite and different from
0
. As
nontrivial solutions satisfy
{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)>0
the only choice for similarity
solutions is
{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)=0
, see also Section
7
.

2.3
Exact similarity solutions

In general, the scaling symmetries can be used to transform into so-called
scaling variables via

\displaystyle\tau=\log(t{+}t_{*}),\ \ y=(t{+}t_{*})^{-\delta}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt

(2.2)

\displaystyle\big(v(t,x),w(t,x)\big)=\big((t{+}t_{*})^{-\gamma/2}\,\widetilde{v}(\tau,y)\,,\,(t{+}t_{*})^{-\gamma}\,\widetilde{w}(\tau,y)\big).

Parabolic Scaling S1.

According to scaling S1 have to choose
\gamma=0
and

\delta=1/2
and arrive at the transformed parabolic equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{v}-\frac{1}{2}\,y{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(\widetilde{w})\nabla\widetilde{v}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{w}-\frac{1}{2}\,y{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{w}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\kappa(\widetilde{w})\nabla\widetilde{w}\big)+\eta(\widetilde{w})\big|\nabla\widetilde{v}\big|^{2}.

(2.3)

Exact similarity solutions are steady states of this coupled system; however
the existence of nontrivial steady states (i.e. with
\widetilde{v}\not\equiv 0
and

\widetilde{w}\not\equiv 0
) is largely open.

Note that (
2.3
) cannot have steady states with compact support
(or more generally finite energy), because
\widetilde{e}=\widetilde{w}+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{v}^{2}

satisfies
\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{e}-\frac{1}{2}\,y{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\cdots)

and integrating over
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and setting
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tau):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widetilde{e}(\tau,y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
gives
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{e}\;\!\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{d}{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
.

For the case
d=1
one can obtain some results for steady states
(\widetilde{v}(\tau,y),\widetilde{w}(\tau,y))=(V(y),W(y)
for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}
, because the problem reduces to
the ODE system

-\frac{y}{2}V^{\prime}=\big(\eta(W)V^{\prime}\big)^{\prime},\ \ -\frac{y}{2}W^{\prime}=\big(\kappa(W)W^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}+\eta(W)(V^{\prime})^{2}\ \text{ for }y\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(2.4)

We first introduce
c(y):=\eta(W(y))V^{\prime}(y)
such that the first equation reduces
to
c^{\prime}(y)=-\big(y/\eta(W(y))\big)c(y)
. Thus,
c
cannot change sign, which
implies that
V^{\prime}
doesn’t change sign because
\eta(W)\geq 0
. Hence, for any
solution
(V,W)
the function
y\mapsto V(y)
must be monotone. This implies
that for any nontrivial solution
V
cannot lie in
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
for any

p<\infty
. Nevertheless, the function
W
may be integrable (or even have
compact support); then we can integrate the second equation in
(
2.4
) over
{\mathbb{R}}
and find

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta(W(y))\big(V^{\prime}(y)\big)^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}y.

Typical solutions of (
2.4
) will be such that
W
and
V^{\prime}
are
even functions. By Galilean invariance we may assume
V(0)=0
such that
V
is
odd and
E=W+\frac{1}{2}V^{2}
is even again. A special case is given by

\eta\equiv\kappa
, because by (
2.1
) we then have
-\frac{y}{2}\,E^{\prime}=\big(\eta(W)E^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}
which has the unique even solution solution

E\equiv E(0)
.

Theorem 2.1
(Similarity solutions)

Consider the case
\eta\equiv\kappa
. Then, for each pair

(V_{-},V_{+})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and each
E_{0}>\frac{1}{2}\max\{V_{-}^{2},V_{+}^{2}\}
there exists a
unique solution
(V,W)
of (
2.4
) satisfying

V(y)\to V_{\pm}\text{ for }x\to\pm\infty\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptW(y)+\frac{1}{2}V(y)^{2}=E_{0}.

In particular, if
V_{-}\neq V_{+}
, then
y\mapsto V(y)
is strictly monotone and

W(y)>E_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\max\{V_{-}^{2},V_{+}^{2}\}>0
.

Proof.
It suffices to solve the scalar ODE for
V
namely

-\frac{y}{2}V^{\prime}=\big(\eta\big(E_{0}{-}\frac{1}{2}V^{2}\big)V^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}\text{ for
}y\in{\mathbb{R}},\hskip 20.00003ptV(y)\to V_{\pm}\text{ for }y\to\pm\infty.

Then, setting
W(y)=E_{0}-\frac{1}{2}V^{2}
, the pair
(V,W)
solves (
2.4
).

The existence and uniqueness of
V
follows by applying

[
GaM98
, Thm. 3.1]
or
[
MiS21
]
. For this may assume

V_{-}\leq V_{+}
and define
A\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1,\mathrm{Lip}}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
via

\displaystyle A(V)=\int_{V_{-}}^{V}\eta(E_{0}{-}\frac{1}{2}v^{2})\;\!\mathrm{d}v\text{ for }V\in[V_{-},V_{+}]\text{ and }

\displaystyle A^{\prime}(V)=\eta\big(E_{0}{-}\frac{1}{2}V_{\pm})^{2}\big)+(V{-}V_{\pm})\text{ for }\pm V\geq\pm V_{\pm}.

Thus,
A
is uniformly convex with upper and lower
quadratic bound, and the above-mentioned results are applicable. Since the
V
is
monotone its range stays inside
[V_{-},V_{+}]
, hence the choice of
A
outside
of
[V_{-},V_{+}]
is irrelevant.

Unfortunately the previous result does not apply to the degenerate case. For

Example 2.2

\big(V(y),W(y)\big)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\big(y/\sqrt{2}\;,\;B^{2}{-}y^{2}/4\big)&\text{for }|y|\leq 2B,\\

(2.5)

Parabolic Scaling S2.

In the case
\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}
and

\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\alpha}
, it is natural to search for similarity solutions
induced by the
nonlinear scaling S2
. We again can use the transformation
(
2.2
), where we are no longer forced to use
\gamma=0
because
we can exploit the scaling properties of
\eta
and
\kappa
. It suffices to
chose
2\delta+\alpha\gamma=1
to obtain an equation that is autonomous with
respect to
\tau
:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{v}\>-\frac{\gamma}{2}\widetilde{v}\>-\delta\,y\!\;{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{v}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\alpha}\,\nabla\widetilde{v}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ where }2\delta+\alpha\gamma=1,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{w}-\gamma\widetilde{w}-\delta\,y\!\;{\cdot}\nabla\widetilde{w}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\kappa_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\alpha}\!\nabla\widetilde{w}\big)+\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\alpha}\big|\nabla\widetilde{v}\big|^{2}.

Again, the existence for nontrivial steady state solutions is totally open.

However, we can say something for finite-energy solutions. If we look for
solutions respecting energy conservation, i.e.
{\mathcal{E}}(\widetilde{v}(\tau),\widetilde{w}(\tau))={\mathcal{E}}(v(t),w(t))={\mathcal{E}}(v(0),w(0))\in{]0,\infty[}
, then we additionally have
to impose
\gamma=d\delta
. Together with
2\delta+\alpha\gamma=1
we obtain

\delta=\frac{1}{2{+}d\alpha}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma=\frac{d}{2{+}d\alpha}.

Again we can show that there are no nontrivial steady states
(\widetilde{v},\widetilde{w})

with compact support. To see this, we test the steady-state equation for

\widetilde{v}
in (
2.6
) by
|\widetilde{v}|^{-\theta}\widetilde{v}
for

\theta\in{]0,1[}
. Integrating by parts the convective part on the left-hand
side and the divergence term on the right-hand side leads to the relation

\big({-}\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{d\delta}{2{-}\theta}\big)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\widetilde{v}|^{2-\theta}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\alpha}(1{-}\theta)|\widetilde{v}|^{-\theta}|\nabla\widetilde{v}\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}y.

The prefactor on the left-hand side equals
\theta d/(2(2{-}\theta)(2{+}d\alpha))>0
whereas the right-hand side is
non-positive. Thus, we conclude
\widetilde{v}=0
for all compactly supported steady
states. For
\widetilde{v}\equiv 0
the system reduces to the scaled PME and it is
well-known that all similarity solutions are given by
(
1.4
).

In Section
7
we provide some evidence for our conjecture that
all finite energy solutions
(\widetilde{v},\widetilde{w})
of (
2.6
) with

\gamma=d\delta
convergence to the corresponding steady state

(0,W_{E_{0}})
.

2.4
Gradient structure and convergence into steady state

We now show that the coupled system can be generated by a gradient system

({\mathbf{Q}},{\mathcal{S}},{\mathbb{K}})
, where
{\mathbf{Q}}={\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(\Omega)
is the state
space,
{\mathcal{S}}
is an entropy functional, and
{\mathbb{K}}
is the Onsager operator
satisfying
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{K}}^{*}\geq 0
. The latter
defines the dual entropy-production potential
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(v,w;\zeta_{v},\zeta_{w}):=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\mathbb{K}}{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\big\rangle
.
The aim is to show that the coupled system (
1.1
) can be written
in the form

\binom{\dot{v}}{\dot{w}}={\mathbb{K}}(v,w){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)

for a suitable choice of
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathbb{K}}
, see
[
Pel14
,
Mie16
]

for the general theory on gradient systems.
For this, we consider entropies in the form

{\mathcal{S}}(w)=\int_{\Omega}\sigma(w)\;\!\mathrm{d}x
and obtain, along solutions,

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{S}}(w(t))=\int_{\Omega}\Big\{{-}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(w)\kappa(w)|\nabla w|^{2}+\sigma^{\prime}(w)\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\Big\}\;\!\mathrm{d}x=:\mathfrak{P}(v,w).

(2.7)

Thus, we have entropy production whenever
\sigma
is nondecreasing and
concave.

For finding suitable Onsager operators
{\mathbb{K}}
we consider dual
entropy-production potentials in the form

{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(v,w;\zeta_{v},\zeta_{w}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big\{p_{1}(w)\big|\nabla\zeta_{v}{-}\zeta_{w}\nabla v\big|^{2}+p_{2}(w)|\nabla\zeta_{w}|^{2}\Big\}\;\!\mathrm{d}x

with suitable mobilities
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Here
\zeta_{v}
and
\zeta_{w}
are the
variables dual to
v
and
w
, respectively. The conservation laws for
{\mathcal{V}}

and
{\mathcal{E}}
are reflected in the properties

{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(v,w;{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)\equiv 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(v,w;{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(v,w))\equiv 0,

where we use
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)=(1,0)^{\top}
and
{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)=(v,1)^{\top}
.

From the formula of
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
we calculate
{\mathbb{K}}
via
{\mathbb{K}}(u,k)={\mathrm{D}}^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}{\mathcal{P}}^{*}(v,w;{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})
, which results in

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}(v,w)\binom{\zeta_{v}}{\zeta_{w}}

\displaystyle=\binom{-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(p_{1}(w)(\nabla\zeta_{v}{-}\zeta_{w}\nabla v)\big)}{-p_{1}(w)\nabla v\cdot\nabla\zeta_{v}+p_{1}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\varkappa-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(p_{2}(w)\nabla\zeta_{w}\big)}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(p_{1}(w)\nabla\Box\big)&\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\Box p_{1}(w)\nabla v\big)\\
-p_{1}(w)\nabla v\cdot\nabla\Box&\Box p_{1}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(p_{2}(w)\nabla\Box\big)\end{array}\right)\binom{\zeta_{v}}{\zeta_{w}},

where
\Box
indicates the position into which the corresponding component of

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=(\zeta_{v},\zeta_{w})^{\top}
has
to be inserted.

Now, calculating
{\mathbb{K}}(v,w){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)
with

{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)=(0,\sigma^{\prime}(w))^{\top}
we see that we obtain our coupled problem
(
1.1
) if the relations

\eta(w)=\sigma^{\prime}(w)p_{1}(w)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\kappa(w)=-\sigma^{\prime\prime}(w)p_{2}(w)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }w>0

hold. Moreover, we see that the above entropy entropy-production relation
(
2.7
) takes the general form

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{S}}(v(t),w(t))

\displaystyle=\big\langle{\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w),{\mathbb{K}}(v,w){\mathrm{D}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)\big\rangle=2{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\big(v,w;(0,\sigma^{\prime}(w))^{\top}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big(p_{1}(w)\big|0-\sigma^{\prime}(w)\nabla v\big|^{2}+p_{2}(w)\big|\nabla\sigma^{\prime}(w)\big|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\mathfrak{P}(v,w).

The gradient structure for general choices of
\sigma
can be used to obtain a
priori estimates, see Section
5.3
. Moreover, it can be used
to prove convergence into steady state on bounded domains
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For this we observe that taking an increasing and strictly convex

\sigma
given the momentum
V_{0}:={\mathcal{V}}(v(0),w(0))
and the
initial energy
E_{0}:={\mathcal{E}}(u(0),w(0))
there is a unique maximizer of the
entropy
{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)
on all states in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq 0}(\Omega)
satisfying the constraints
{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)=V_{0}
and

{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)=E_{0}
, namely the spatially solutions

\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}V_{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}W_{0}\text{ with }W_{0}:=\big(E_{0}-\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}V_{0}^{2}\big)\geq 0.

By integrating the equation for
w
over
\Omega
and using
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\geq 0
and the no-flux boundary conditions, we easily obtain

0\leq\int_{\Omega}w(0,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}w(t_{1},x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}w(t_{2},x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq W_{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }0<t_{1}<t_{2}<\infty,

(2.8)

where we used energy conservation for the last estimate.

Moreover, under natural conditions all solutions of
\mathfrak{P}(v,w)=0
are given by
the constant function pair
(v,w)\equiv(v^{0},w^{0})
or by pairs of the form

(\widetilde{v}(\cdot),0)
for arbitrary
\widetilde{v}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
. The latter
solutions will be excluded by assuming
\int_{\Omega}w(0,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x>0
.
Hence, in good situations one
can hope for convergence of all solutions into the unique thermal equilibrium
state
(\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}},\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})
depending on the constraint given by
the initial conditions.

The following results provides a first results and explains the main idea in the
simplest case, but we expect that this method generalizes to more general
situations, see
[
MiM18
,
HH∗18
]
for related convergence results
for systems of diffusion equations. It is surprising that the exponential decay
holds globally for our degenerate parabolic system and that the decay rate

\Lambda
is even close to the lower bound for the rate dictated by the
linearization at the steady state.

Theorem 2.3
(Convergence to steady state)

Consider a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and assume that

\exists\,c_{\eta},c_{\kappa}>0\ \forall\,w\geq 0:\hskip 10.00002pt\eta(w)\geq c_{\eta}w^{1/2}\text{ and }\kappa(w)\geq c_{\kappa}w^{1/2}.

(2.9)

Let
\lambda_{N}:=\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}(\Omega)>0
be the first nontrivial eigenvalue of
the Neumann Laplacian on
\Omega
, then all solutions converge exponentially to
a spatially constant equilibrium. More precisely, solutions with

{\mathcal{V}}(v(0))=V_{0}
,
{\mathcal{E}}(v(0),w(0))=E_{0}
, and
w(0,x)\geq c>0
a.e. in

\Omega
satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big|v(t,\cdot){-}\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}}\big|^{2}+\big|\sqrt{w(t,\cdot)}{-}\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}\big|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x

(2.10)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\big|v(0,\cdot){-}\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}}\big|^{2}+\big|\sqrt{w(0,\cdot)}{-}\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}\big|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\text{
for all }t\geq 0,

where
\Lambda=\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}\,\min\{c_{\eta},c_{\kappa}\}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}
.

Proof.
We use the entropy density function
\sigma(w)=\sqrt{w}
and
assume
V_{0}=0
by Galileian invariance. We introduce a relative entropy as
an the auxiliary functional, which depends on the initial values

(v(0),w(0))
via
(\widehat{v}_{0,E_{0}},\widehat{w}_{0,E_{0}})
where

E_{0}={\mathcal{E}}(v(0),w(0))
:

{\mathcal{H}}(v,w)={\mathcal{E}}(v,w)-2\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}{\mathcal{S}}(v,w)+\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}|\Omega|=\int_{\Omega}\!\Big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\big(\sqrt{w}{-}\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}\big)^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

where we heavily used
\sigma(w)=w^{1/2}
such that the linear term
w
inside

{\mathcal{E}}
equals
\sigma(w)^{2}
.
Clearly,
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Liapunov function, and along solutions (
2.7
)
provides the relation

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{H}}(v(t),w(t))=-2\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}\>\mathfrak{P}(v(t),w(t)).

(2.11)

We now show that
\mathfrak{P}
can be estimated from below by
{\mathcal{H}}
as follows. To
simplify notation we introduce
z=\sqrt{w}
and
z_{0}=\sqrt{\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}}
.
First, we can use the explicit form of
\mathfrak{P}
,
\sigma(w)=\sqrt{w}
,

\nabla w=2z\nabla z
,
and the lower estimates for
\eta
and
\kappa
and arrive at

\mathfrak{P}(v,w)\geq\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\,\frac{c_{\eta}}{2}\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}+c_{\kappa}\big|\nabla z\big|^{2}\,\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

Here, we use the assumption
w(0,\cdot)\geq c>0
with implies by Section

5.2
(C2) that
w(t,x)\geq c>0
for all
(t,x)\in{[0,\infty[}{\times}\overline{\Omega}
.

Secondly, setting
\overline{z}:=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}z\;\!\mathrm{d}x>0

we exploit energy conservation
{\mathcal{E}}(v,z^{2})=E_{0}
along solutions and find

E_{0}=|\Omega|\,z_{0}^{2}=|\Omega|\big(\overline{z}^{2}{+}A^{2}\big)\text{ with }|\Omega|A^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+z^{2}-\overline{z}^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+(z{-}\overline{z})^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

Clearly, we have
\overline{z}=\big(z_{0}^{2}{-}A^{2}\big)^{1/2}\in[0,z_{0}]
, and

z_{0}\leq\big(z_{0}^{2}{-}A^{2}\big)^{1/2}+A
yields
0\leq z_{0}-\overline{z}\leq A
.

Thus, for all
(v,z)
satisfying
{\mathcal{E}}(v,z^{2})=E_{0}
we have the following chain
of estimates

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}(v,z^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+(z{-}z_{0})^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+z^{2}-\overline{z}^{2}+(z_{0}{-}\overline{z})^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=|\Omega|A^{2}+|\Omega|(z_{0}{-}\overline{z})^{2}\leq 2|\Omega|A^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big(v^{2}+2(z{-}\overline{z})^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}}\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v|^{2}+2|\nabla z|^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

where we used the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality for the Neumann
Laplacian on
\Omega
and our assumption
\int_{\Omega}v\;\!\mathrm{d}x=V_{0}=0
.

Combining all the estimates we obtain the lower bound

\mathfrak{P}(v,w)\geq\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}}{2}\min\{c_{\eta},c_{\kappa}\}\,{\mathcal{H}}(v,w).

Together with (
2.11
) and the definition of
\Lambda
we find

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{H}}(v(t),w(t))\leq-\Lambda{\mathcal{H}}(v(t),w(t))
, which
implies the desired exponential decay estimate (
2.10
).

We emphasize that the global decay rate
\Lambda
obtained in Theorem

2.3
is optimal up to a possible factor of
2
, because the
linearization of the
coupled system at the steady state
(\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}},\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})
reads

\dot{\widetilde{c}}=\eta(\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\widetilde{c},\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{\widetilde{w}}=\kappa(\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})\Delta_{\mathrm{N}}\widetilde{w},\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{c}\;\!\mathrm{d}x=0=\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{w}\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

Thus, the decay rate
\Lambda
cannot be larger than
2\min\{\eta(\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}),\kappa(\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}})\}\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}(\Omega)
, because
(
2.10
) measures quadratic distances from equilibrium.

Example 2.4
(Worse decay if supports are disjoint)

We want to emphasize that some assumption on the positivity of
w(t,x)
is
necessary in Theorem
2.3
, since otherwise the stated decay
may not hold. As an example consider the case
\Omega={]{-}\ell,\ell[}
for
fixed
\ell\gg 1
,
\eta(w)=\kappa(w)=\frac{3}{2}w^{1/2}
such that

c_{\kappa}=c_{\eta}=3/2
, and initial data
(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)^{2}
:

v^{0}(x)=\mathrm{sign}(x)\big(\max\{0,2|x|{-}\ell\}\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptw^{0}(x)=\Big(\frac{1}{15}\max\big\{1{-}x^{2},0\big\}\Big)^{2}\text{ for
}x\in\Omega.

We set
T_{\ell}=(\ell/2)^{5/2}-1
and observe that for
t\in[0,T_{\ell}]
we
have the explicit solution

v(t,x)=v^{0}(x)\ \text{ and }\ w(t,x)=\frac{1}{(t{+}1)^{2/5}}\Big(\frac{1}{15}\max\big\{1-\frac{x^{2}}{(t{+}1)^{4/5}},0\big\}\Big)^{2}.

Indeed, we have
\mathrm{sppt}(v(t,\cdot))=\overline{\Omega}\setminus{]{-}\ell/2,\ell/2[}

and
\mathrm{sppt}(w(t,\cdot))=[{-}(t{+}1)^{2/5},(t{+}1)^{2/5}]
, such that the
supports are disjoint for
t<T_{\ell}
. Hence, it is easy to see that both
equations are satisfied because of
\eta(w)=0
on the support of
v(t,\cdot)
:
in the equation
v
the dynamics is trivial with
\dot{v}=0
, and in the
equation for
w
we simply have the similarity solution for the PME with

\beta=1/2
, see (
1.4
).

To see that this solution contradicts the expoential decay estimate, it is
sufficient to calculate the involved terms only in there main term in
\ell
,
which we indicate by
\sim\ell^{\alpha}
:

\lambda_{\mathrm{N}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\ell^{2}}\sim\ell^{-2},\hskip 10.00002ptV_{0}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptE_{0}\sim\ell^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{v}_{V_{0},E_{0}}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\widehat{w}_{V_{0},E_{0}}\sim\ell^{2}.

With this we find the exponential decay rate
\Lambda\sim\ell^{-1}
. Because
in (
2.10
) the integrals over
\Omega
on both sides are of order

\ell^{3}
for all
t\in[0,T_{\ell}]
and
T_{\ell}\sim\ell^{5/2}
, we
easily obtain a contradiction because
\Lambda T_{\ell}\sim\ell^{3/2}
such
that
\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda T_{\ell}}\ell^{3}
is smaller than
1
, whereas it
is still of order
\ell^{3}
for the given solution.

Nevertheless, we conjecture that the above decay estimate can be extended to
solutions with
\mathrm{sppt}(w^{0})\subsetneqq\overline{\Omega}
. The point is that
solutions are not unique because of the nonlinearity
w^{1/2}|\nabla v|^{2}
. Constructing solutions by approximating the initial conditions from
above as sketched in Section
6
one may obtain solutions
satisfying
\mathrm{sppt}(w(t,\cdot))\supset\mathrm{sppt}(\nabla v(t,\cdot))
for
all
t>0
, which then satisfy the exponential decay estimate
(
2.10
).

2.5
A related plasma model

In a series of papers starting with
[
RoH85
,
RoH86
,
HyR86
]

a model of the diffusion of the mass density
\rho\geq 0
and the heat
transport for the temperature in a plasma is developed:

\rho_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\rho\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\big(\rho\theta\big)_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\delta}\phi_{2}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\theta+\theta\rho^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(\rho,\theta)\nabla\rho\big),

(2.12)

that conserves mass and energy, namely
\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=M_{0}
and
\int_{\Omega}\rho(t,x)\theta(t,x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=E_{0}
.

Note that the model is such that it allow one to consider a constant
temperature
\theta(t,x)=\theta_{*}
, and it is then sufficient to study the
remaining PME for
\rho
:

\dot{\rho}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\phi_{1}(\rho,\theta_{*})\nabla\rho\big).

(2.13)

Thus, asymptotic self-similar behavior follows if
\phi_{0}(,\theta_{*})\neq 0
. Moreover, if
\phi(\rho,\theta_{*})
is constant, we even have Barenblatt
solutions as in (
1.4
), see
[
RoH86
, Eqn. (5)-(7)]
.

To have better comparison with our model, we can introduce the energy density

e=\rho\theta
and obtain the system,

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\varphi_{1}(\rho,e)\nabla\rho\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt

(2.14)

\displaystyle\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\Big(\rho^{\delta-1}\varphi_{2}(\rho,e)\nabla e+e\big(\rho^{\gamma-1}\varphi_{1}(\rho,e)-\rho^{\delta-2}\varphi_{2}(\rho,e)\big)\nabla\rho\Big),

where
\varphi_{j}(\rho,e)=\phi_{j}(\rho,e/\rho)
. Thus, our case

\eta(w)=\kappa(w)
corresponds here to the case
\rho^{\gamma}\varphi_{1}=\rho^{\delta-1}\varphi_{2}
leading to the special system

\dot{\rho}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\varphi_{1}(\rho,e)\nabla\rho\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\rho^{\gamma}\varphi_{1}(\rho,e)\nabla e\big)

which obviously has solutions with
e(t,x)=\rho(t,x)\theta_{*}
if
\rho

satisfies (
2.13
).

For an existence theory for the model (
2.12
) we refer to

[
BeK90
,
DaG99
]
. However, to the best of the knowledge of the
author a systematic study of the motion of the moving front does not exist.

3
Steady states and traveling fronts

Here we provide a few special solutions that will highlight the coupling
between the two degenerate equations. To obtain a first feeling about the
nontrivial interaction between the two equations we study some simple explicit
solutions, namely steady states and traveling fronts.

3.1
Steady states

Steady states are all the solutions of the coupled degenerate elliptic system

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(w)\nabla v\big),

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt0

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\kappa(w)\nabla w\big)+\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(3.1)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\eta(w)\nabla v\cdot{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\kappa(w)\nabla w\cdot{\mathrm{n}}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

We cause of
\eta(0)=0
it is easy to construct steady states in the form

(v,w)=(V_{\mathrm{st}},0)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where $V_{\mathrm{st}}$ is arbitrary.}

(3.2)

Thus, there is an infinite-dimensional family of
trivial
steady states,
but this family is exceptional. If we assume
w^{0}\not\equiv 0
. i.e.
\int_{\Omega}w^{0}\;\!\mathrm{d}x>0
, we find
{\mathcal{E}}(v(t),w(t))\geq{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})\geq\int_{\Omega}w^{0}\;\!\mathrm{d}x>0
and conclude that these solutions are not relevant
any more.

For bounded domains we have the following uniqueness result for nontrivial
steady states.

Proposition 3.1
(Steady states for
\Omega
bounded)

Assume that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded
Lipschitz domain. Then, all steady states are given either by the trivial ones
in (
3.2
) or by the spatially constant ones, namely

(v,w)\ \equiv\ \big(\frac{1}{|\Omega|}V_{0}\,,\frac{1}{|\Omega|}W_{0}\big),

(3.3)

where
V_{0}
and
W_{0}
are uniquely given by the conserved quantities

{\mathcal{V}}(v,w)=V_{0}
and
{\mathcal{E}}(v,w)=E_{0}=W_{0}+\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}V_{0}^{2}
, see Section

2.4
.

Proof.
We use the pressure function
\widehat{\pi}(x)=\Pi(w(x))
with

\Pi(w)=\int_{0}^{w}\eta(u)\;\!\mathrm{d}u
and observe that for a steady state
(v,w)

the function
\widehat{\pi}
satisfies the linear Neumann problem

-\Delta\widehat{\pi}=\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\ \text{ in }\Omega,\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla\widehat{\pi}\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0\ \text{ on }\partial\Omega.

The classical solvability condition for the Neumann problem requires

\int_{\Omega}\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x=0
. Since the integrand is nonnegative
we conclude
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\widehat{\pi}=\pi_{*}=
const.
Because of the strict monotonicity of
\Pi
we obtain
w=w_{*}=
const. and deduce
either
w_{*}=0
given the trivial steady states (
3.2
) or

w_{*}>0
and
v=v_{*}=
const.

In the case of unbounded
\Omega
we have to allow for solutions with infinite
energy and the result is less complete.

Proposition 3.2
(Steady states for
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
)

Assume
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\in\{1,2\}
.
Then, all steady states (i.e. solutions of (
3.1
)) are given
either by the trivial ones in (
3.2
) or by the spatially
constant ones, namely
(v,w)\ \equiv\ (v_{*},w_{*})=
const.

Proof.
The pressure
\widehat{\pi}
introduced in the previous proof still
satisfies
-\Delta\widehat{\pi}=\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\geq 0
. Moreover, we know

\widehat{\pi}\geq 0
. Hence,
-\widehat{\pi}
is a subharmonic function that is bounded from
above. For
d=1
the function
-\widehat{\pi}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{]{-}\infty,0]}
is convex and
hence can only be bounded if it is constant. For
d=2
we invoke

[
Ran95
, Cor. 2.3.4]
which shows that bounded subharmonic functions
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are constant. In both cases we conclude
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}=0

a.e. in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the result concerning
v
follows.

It is unclear whether the last result is still true in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 3
.

3.2
Traveling fronts

The importance of traveling fronts in the PME arises from the fact that they
can be used as comparison functions and that they serve as models for the local
behavior near the boundary of the support of
w
.
By isotropy of our
system (
1.1
) it is sufficient to study the one-dimensional case
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1}
. We start from the traveling-wave ansatz

v(t,x)=V(x{+}c_{\mathrm{F}}t)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptw(t,x)=W(x{+}c_{\mathrm{F}}t),

where
c_{\mathrm{F}}
is the front speed. We obtain a coupled system of ODEs for

z=x+c_{\mathrm{F}}t
with the unknowns

c_{\mathrm{F}}V^{\prime}=\big(\eta(W)\,V^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptc_{\mathrm{F}}W^{\prime}=\big(\kappa(W)\,W^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}+\eta(W)(V^{\prime})^{2}.

(3.4)

Clearly, the speed
c_{\mathrm{F}}
needs to be determined together with
the nontrivial solution
(V,W)
of (
3.4
). However, we observe
that both right-hand sides in (
3.4
) contain two derivatives
while both left-hand sides contain only one derivative and one factor

c_{\mathrm{F}}
. Hence, we can rescale solutions in such a way that for a solution

(c_{\mathrm{F}},V,W)
also
(\lambda c_{\mathrm{F}},V(\lambda\,\cdot),W(\lambda\,\cdot))
is
a solution for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since the case
c_{\mathrm{F}}=0
leads to steady
states that were investigated already in Section
3.1
,
it suffices to consider the case
c_{\mathrm{F}}=1
, only.

To analyze the solution set of (
3.4
), we integrate the first
equation obtaining the integration constant

v_{*}
and substitute the result for
\eta(W)V^{\prime}
in the second equation. Then,
the second equation can also be integrated with an integration constant
w_{*}
:

V-v_{*}=\eta(W)V^{\prime},\hskip 20.00003ptW-w_{*}=\kappa(W)W^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(V{-}v_{*})^{2}.

(3.5)

The integration constants were chosen such that
(V,W)\equiv(v_{*},w_{*})
is the
only constant solution. The system can be analyzed in the
(V,W)
phase
plane. To see whether (
3.5
) has other solutions that are defined
for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we treat for the three cases
\alpha=\beta
,

\alpha<\beta
, and
\alpha>\beta
separately.

Case
\alpha=\beta
:
For
\eta(w)=w^{\alpha}
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\alpha}
, there is the following
helpful observation: the parabola

{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}:V\mapsto\widehat{w}(V):=w_{*}+\frac{1}{2(1{-}2\kappa_{0})}\,(V{-}v_{*})^{2}

is invariant by the flow of (
3.5
). In the case
\kappa_{0}<1/2

all points starting above

{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}
stay above, which means they cannot reach
W=0
in finite time
and hence exist for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

For
w_{*}>0
, the solutions lying above
{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}
behave as follows
(where
\rho_{*}=w_{*}^{-\alpha}
)

\displaystyle(V(z),W(z))=(v_{*},w_{*})+\big(c_{1}\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{*}z},\frac{c_{1}^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{2\rho_{*}z}}{2(1{-}2\kappa_{0})}+c_{2}\mathrm{e}^{\rho_{*}z/\kappa_{0}}\big)+\text{h.o.t.\ for }z\to-\infty,

\displaystyle(V(z),W(z))=\big(c_{3}z^{\kappa_{0}/\alpha},(\tfrac{\alpha}{\kappa_{0}}\;\!z)^{1/\alpha}\big)+\text{l.o.t.\ for }z\to\infty,

where
c_{1},c_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
c_{2}>0
. The two solutions lying exactly on

{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}:W=\widehat{w}(V)
have a slightly different asymptotics.
Still in the case
\kappa_{0}<1/2
one can show that all solutions starting below of

{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta_{0}}
reach
W=0
in finite time and cannot be extended for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}
, see Figure
3.1
(right picture).

The situation

for

(by Galileian invariance we can set

subsequently). These solutions are in particular interesting, because they

\kappa_{0}

For

As above there are more traveling waves from the solutions lying above the
parabola
{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}
, which is now touching the axis

in the origin

(V,W)=(0,0)

(V(z),W(z))=\big(c(z_{+})^{\kappa_{0}/\alpha},(\tfrac{\alpha}{\kappa_{0}}z_{+})^{1/\alpha}+\tfrac{c^{2}}{2}(z_{+})^{2\kappa_{0}/\alpha}\big)+\text{h.o.t.\hskip 10.22217ptfor }z\to 0^{+},

where

is a parameter for choosing the individual solutions above

{\mathbb{P}}_{\kappa_{0}}
.

Case
\alpha<\beta
:
In the case
\eta(w)=w^{\alpha}
and

\kappa(w)=w^{\beta}
the ODE reads

V^{\prime}=V\,W^{-\alpha},\hskip 10.00002ptW^{\prime}=\big(W{-}\frac{1}{2}V^{2}\big)W^{-\beta},\hskip 20.00003pt(V(0)),W(0))=(0,0).

(3.6)

In the cases with
\alpha\neq\beta

\eta_{0}=\kappa_{0}
without

As usual, there is the trivial solution
with
V\equiv 0
, but all nontrivial solutions of

3.3

Conjectured behavior near boundary of growing supports

Here we collect conjectured consequences of the above established behavior of
traveling fronts. Throughout we assume that we are considering sufficiently
smooth solutions
(v,w)
that have the property that

S(t):=\mathrm{sppt}(v(t))=\mathrm{sppt}(w(t))\Subset\Omega
.

We conjecture that
t\mapsto S(t)
has similarly good properties as the support
of solutions of the PME, see e.g.
[
Váz07
]
. In particular,
t\mapsto S(t)
is non-decreasing and the boundary
\partial S(t)
becomes smooth after a
suitable waiting time. However, in our coupled system the growth of the support
can be steered by different mechanisms depending on the relative size of

\eta(w)
and
\kappa(w)
for
w\ll 1
.

To explain the conjectured behavior in more detail, we consider a point
x_{*}\in\partial S(t_{*})
and assume that
\partial S(t_{*})
is smooth. Without loss of generality
we may assume
x_{*}=0
and that the the outer normal vector to
S(t_{*})
at

x_{*}
is given by
{\mathrm{n}}_{*}=-e_{1}
.

In the PME (
1.3
) with
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\beta}
the typical
behavior (after waiting time) is that
w(t_{*},x)=w_{0}(x_{1})_{+}^{1/\beta}+\text{h.o.t.}
The support
S(t)
is then growing with propagation speed

c_{\mathrm{F}}=\kappa_{0}w_{0}^{\beta}/\beta
.

The behavior for the coupled system depends strongly on the exponents
\alpha

and
\beta
in
\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\beta}
. In
all cases we will address the question of local integrability of

\nabla v^{\gamma}
and
\nabla w^{\gamma}
near the boundary of
S(t)
. These
integrability properties will nicely fit together with the a priori estimates
to be derived below, see (
5.17
) in Proposition

5.5
.

\alpha>\beta
: support is driven by
w
as in PME.
In the
case
\alpha>\beta
and
w\ll 1
the energy-transport coefficient
\kappa(w)
is
much bigger than the viscosity coefficient
\eta(w)
. Hence,
w
will diffuse
fast and
v
will try to keep up by following the growing support. As in the
PME the conjectured behavior (after waiting times) is

(v(t_{*},x),w(t,x))=\big(0,w_{0}\>(x_{1})_{+}^{1/\beta}\big)+\text{h.o.t.},

where
v
vanishes faster than
O(|x|^{m})
for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, see Proposition

3.3
. Again the front speed is solely controlled by
w
alone,
namely
c_{*}=\kappa_{0}w_{0}^{\beta}/\beta
.

The expansion does not give any information about the integrability of
\nabla v^{\gamma}
, however we see that
\nabla w^{\gamma}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(B_{r}((t_{*},0)))
for
\gamma>\beta\,(1{-}1/p)
.

\alpha<\beta
: support is driven by
v
.
Now
\eta(w)\gg\kappa(w)
for
w\ll 1
, hence hence,
v
can easily diffuse to the boundary of
the support and pile up there. We conjecture that the typical behavior (after
waiting times) is given by (
3.7
), namely

(v(t_{*},x),w(t,x))=\big(v_{0}\>(x_{1})_{+}^{1/(2\alpha)},\frac{1}{2}v_{0}^{2}\>(x_{1})_{+}^{1/\alpha}\big)+\text{h.o.t.}.

The corresponding propagation speed is then given by
c_{*}=\eta_{0}v_{0}^{2\alpha-2}/(\alpha 2^{\alpha+1})
.

The reason of this behavior is that in the equation for
w
the energy transport
via
\kappa
can be neglected and the growth of the support is controlled by
the source term
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}
. This leads to a equipartition of
energy near the boundary of the support giving
w\approx\frac{1}{2}v^{2}
, or using

e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
we have
\frac{1}{2}v^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2}e\approx w
.

In this case, we see that
\nabla v^{\gamma}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(B_{r}((t_{*},0)))
for

\gamma>2\alpha\,(1{-}1/p)
and
\nabla w^{\gamma}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(B_{r}((t_{*},0)))
for
\gamma>\alpha\,(1{-}1/p)
.

Critical case
\alpha=\beta
.
We now consider

\eta(w)=w^{\alpha}
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\alpha}
and will see that the both
previous cases appear, because
\kappa(w)/\eta(w)=\kappa_{0}
may be large or
small depending on
\kappa_{0}
.

For
\kappa_{0}>1/2
the support is driven by
w
as in the PME, however, now
v

can follow fast enough. The conjectured behavior is

(v(t_{*},x),w(t_{*},x))=(v_{0}(x_{1})_{+}^{\kappa_{0}/\alpha},w_{0}(x_{1})_{+}^{1/\alpha})+\text{h.o.t.}

Here
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is arbitrary, and the propagation speed
c_{*}=\kappa_{0}w_{0}^{\alpha}/\alpha
depends only on
w_{0}
as for the PME.

For
\kappa_{0}<1/2
the front is driven by a combination of
v
and
w
. The
conjectured expansion takes the form

\big(v(t_{*},x),w(t_{*},x)\big)=\Big(v_{0}\>(x_{1})_{+}^{1/(2\alpha)}\>,\>\frac{v_{0}^{2}}{2(1{-}2\kappa_{0})}\>(x_{1})_{+}^{1/\alpha}\Big)+\text{h.o.t.}

We observe that the leading terms of
v
and
w
are coupled together
by the relation
w\approx v^{2}/(2{-}4\kappa_{0})
. Moreover, the limit

\kappa_{0}\to 0^{+}
is consistent with the equipartition in the case
\alpha<\beta
. The propagation speed is given by

c_{*}=w_{0}^{\alpha}/(2\alpha)
which is different from
c_{*}=\kappa_{0}w_{0}^{\alpha}/(2\alpha)
in the case
\kappa_{0}>1/2
. Thus, the interaction
with the
v
component prevents the deterioration of the wave speed for the
limit
\kappa_{0}\to 0^{+}
.

In both subcases, we see that
\nabla v^{\gamma}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(B_{r}((t_{*},0)))
for

\gamma>2\alpha\,(1{-}1/p)
and
\nabla w^{\gamma}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(B_{r}((t_{*},0)))
for
\gamma>\alpha\,(1{-}1/p)
.

In summary, we find that the behavior of
w
near the boundary of the support
is given by
w(t,x)=w_{0}(x_{1})_{+}^{\gamma}
with
\gamma=\max\{1/\alpha,1/\beta\}
, which clearly shows that the front is driven by the

v
-diffusion in case of
\beta>\alpha
. In the critical case
alpha=\beta

the switch between the two regimes occurs for
\eta_{0}=2\kappa_{0}
.

4

Weak and very weak solutions

In general, we cannot expect to have strong solutions for our degenerate
coupled parabolic system. Hence, we define a suitable notions of weak and very
solutions. The problem is that the degeneracies of the viscosity
\eta
and the
energy-transport coefficient
\kappa
, do not allows us to use parabolic
regularity, which is most easily seen for the trivial solutions

(v(t,x),w(t,x))=(v^{0}(x),0)
that do not regularize at all. Hence, we provide a
proper definition of weak and very weak solutions in Section

4.1
, then discuss a compactly supported explicit solution in
Section
4.2
, and finally show non-uniqueness of very weak
solutions in Section
4.3
.

4.1

Definition of weak and very weak solutions

Moreover, there is an intrinsic problem in passing to the limit in the the
“
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}
” source term
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}
, which typically generates a
nonnegative defect measure. This is particularly difficult because of the
degeneracies
\eta(0)=\kappa(0)
in the viscosity
\eta
and energy-transport
coefficient
\kappa
. To avoid this problem, we use the strategy introduced by
Feireisl and Málek in
[
FeM06
,
BFM09
]
. This means we
replace the “partial energy equation” (
1.1b
) by the equation for
the total energy
e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
as given in (
2.1
). Thus, we
are studying suitably defined weak solutions of the coupled system

\displaystyle\dot{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\!\big(\eta(w)\nabla v\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{t}\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}{+}w\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\!\big(\kappa(w)\nabla w+\eta(w)v\nabla v\big)

(4.1)

completed by no-flux conditions for
v
and
w
at the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

Below we will give two different solution concepts, the first being a classical
weak solution. However, since
\nabla v
only occurs together with
\eta(w)
it
is difficult to obtain
good a priori estimates guaranteeing that a limit
v^{0}
obtained from

v_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(0,T;{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega))
remains in that space. Hence, we define
a second concept called very weak solutions, which can be defined for
v\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(Q_{T})
. For the latter we use the notion of a
weak weighted
gradient
generalizing terms of the form
a\nabla v
, where

a
takes the role of
\eta(w)
and may degenerate. We
will generalize to a mapping
G_{a}v
that is valid under weak assumptions on

v
if
a
is sufficiently well behaved.

Definition 4.1
(Weak weighted gradient)

Let
q\in{]1,\infty[}
,
a\in{\mathrm{W}}^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
v\in{\mathrm{L}}^{q^{*}}(\Omega)
with
1/q+1/q^{*}=1
. We say that
g\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)
with

p\in[1,\infty]
is the

a
-weighted weak gradient of
v
and write
g=G_{a}v
if

\forall\,\Psi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}_{\mathrm{N}}(\overline{\Omega};{\mathbb{R}}^{d}):\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{\Omega}g\cdot\Psi\;\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}v\;\big(\Psi\,{\cdot}\nabla a+a\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Psi\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

(4.2)

where
{\mathrm{C}}^{1}_{\mathrm{N}}(\overline{\Omega};{\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\big\{\,\Psi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}(\overline{\Omega};{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\>\big|\>\Psi\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega\,\big\}
.

As in the classical definition of weak derivatives, we see that
g
is uniquely
defined by the pair
(a,v)\in{\mathrm{W}}^{1,q}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{q^{*}}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, for
v\in{\mathrm{W}}^{1,q^{*}}(\Omega)
we obviously have
G_{a}v=a\nabla v

by applying Gauß’ divergence theorem and using
\Psi\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0
. However,
function for which
\nabla v
does not exist may have a weighted gradient if

a
is canceling the singularity. For instance, on
\Omega={]-1,1[}
we may choose

v:x\mapsto\mathrm{sign}(x)
and
a(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, then
G_{a}v

exists and equals
0
.

The next result shows that the notion of weighted gradients is stable
under limit passages, which will be crucial for constructing weak and very weak
solutions.

Lemma 4.2
(A closedness result for weak weighted gradients)

Let
p,q\in{]1,\infty[}
and consider
a_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{W}}^{1,q}(\Omega)
and
v_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{q^{*}}(\Omega)
such that

a_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup a_{0}\ \text{ in }{\mathrm{W}}^{1,q}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002ptv_{\varepsilon}\to v_{0}\ \text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{q^{*}}(\Omega),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\ g_{\varepsilon}=G_{a_{\varepsilon}}v_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup g_{0}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega).

Then, we have
g_{0}=G_{a_{0}}v_{0}
.

Proof.
We simply consider the defining identity (
4.2
) for
\varepsilon>0
and
observe that we can pass to the limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
in all three terms. On the
right-hand side it is crucial to have strong convergence of
v_{\varepsilon}
.

Indeed, the notion of weak weighted gradients is implicitly defined in

[
GL∗03
, Sec. 3]
and also used in
[
Nau13
, Prop. A]
.

We are now ready to give our two notions of solutions, where in the second
definition we have written out the definition of weak weighted gradients
explicitly to emphasize that the definition does not involve derivatives of
v

and that the test function must be smoother. Moreover, as common in the PME we
use the pressure function

\Pi(w):=\int_{0}^{w}\kappa(s)\;\!\mathrm{d}s\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }s\geq 0.

Definition 4.3
(Weak and very weak solutions)

Given
T\in{]0,\infty]}
and initial conditions

(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega)
we call a pair

(v,w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega))
a
weak solution
of system
(
1.1
) if the following holds:

\displaystyle\nabla v,\ \eta(w)\nabla v,\ \eta(w)v\nabla v,\ \nabla\Pi(w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T};{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\hskip-90.00014pt

(4.3a)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}v^{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\;\!\mathrm{d}x-\iint_{Q_{T}}v\partial_{t}\varphi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\iint_{Q_{T}}\eta(w)\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\text{for all }\varphi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}_{\mathrm{c}}([0,T[{\times}\overline{\Omega}),

(4.3b)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}(v^{0})^{2}{+}w^{0}\big)\xi(0,\cdot)\;\!\mathrm{d}x-\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}{+}w\big)\partial_{t}\xi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\hskip-50.00008pt

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\Big(\nabla\big(\Pi(w)\big)\cdot\nabla\xi+\eta(w)v\nabla v\cdot\nabla\xi\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\hskip-5.0pt

\displaystyle\text{for all
}\xi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{1}_{\mathrm{c}}([0,T[{\times}\overline{\Omega}).

(4.3c)

A pair

(v,w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega))
is called

of
system (
1.1
) if the following holds:

\displaystyle(v{+}v^{2})\eta(w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T}),\ \ (v{+}v^{2})\nabla(\eta(w)),\ \nabla\Pi(w)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T};{\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

(4.4a)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}v^{0}\varphi(0,\cdot)\;\!\mathrm{d}x-\iint_{Q_{T}}v\partial_{t}\varphi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t=\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!v\,\big(\eta(w)\Delta\varphi+\nabla(\eta(w))\cdot\nabla\varphi\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\text{for all }\varphi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}([0,T[{\times}\overline{\Omega})\text{ with }\nabla\varphi\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0,

(4.4b)

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\big(\frac{1}{2}(v^{0})^{2}{+}w^{0}\big)\xi(0,\cdot)\;\!\mathrm{d}x-\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}v^{2}{+}w\big)\partial_{t}\xi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\hskip-50.00008pt

\displaystyle=\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\Big(\!{-}\nabla(\Pi(w))\cdot\nabla\xi+\frac{v^{2}}{2}\big(\nabla(\eta(w))\cdot\nabla\xi+\eta(w)\Delta\xi\big)\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\hskip-50.00008pt

(4.4c)

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\text{for all
}\xi\in{\mathrm{C}}^{2}_{\mathrm{c}}([0,T[{\times}\overline{\Omega})\text{ with }\nabla\xi\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0.

We first observe that functions of the form
(v(t,x),w(t,x))=(v^{0}(x),0)
with

v^{0}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
are very weak solutions. Because of
w\equiv 0
it is
trivial to see that (
4.4
) is satisfied.

We remark that weak solutions are not necessarily very weak solutions, because
the degeneracies do not allow us to transfer the necessary integrabilities
easily.

For both notions of solutions, we have conservation of momentum and energy. To
see this, we simply consider spatially constant test functions

(t,x)=\varphi(t)
and
(t,x)=\xi(t)
. As the spatial gradients of the test
functions vanish, we obtain

{\mathcal{V}}(v^{0})\varphi(0)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{V}}(v(t))\dot{\varphi}(t)\;\!\mathrm{d}t\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})\xi(0)=\int_{0}^{t}{\mathcal{E}}(v(t),w(t))\dot{\xi}(t)\;\!\mathrm{d}t.

By the lemma of Du Bois-Reymond we conclude
{\mathcal{V}}(v(t))={\mathcal{V}}(v^{0})
and

{\mathcal{E}}(v(t),w(t))={\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})
for a.a.
t\in[0,T]
.

Of course, weak or very weak solutions that are sufficiently regular are even
strong solutions.

4.3

Nonuniqueness of very weak solutions

We now consider the pair
(v^{0},w^{0})
that is obtained from
(v^{*},w^{*})
by
keeping
B
and
x_{*}
fixed but taking the limit
t_{*}\to 0^{+}
. Then,

(v^{0},w^{0})
has the initial values
(v^{0}_{0},w^{0}_{0})=(2\sqrt{2}\,B\,\mathbf{1}_{[-x_{*},x_{*}]},0)\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega)
.
Next we observe that

\nabla v=v_{x}
is piecewise constant with values
\pm 1/\sqrt{2t}
in the
intervals
|x{\pm}x_{*}|\leq 2B\sqrt{t}
and
0
otherwise. Hence, we have

\nabla v\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(Q_{T})
for
p\in{[1,3[}
. With
\Pi(w)=w^{2}/2
we find

\nabla\Pi(w)=ww_{x}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{q}(Q_{T})
for all
q\in{[1,2[}
. Using

(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(Q_{t})
we have the conditions (
4.3a
)
and (
4.4a
). Moreover, inserting
(v^{0},w^{0})
into the weak form
(
4.3b
)+(
4.3c
) of the very weak form
(
4.4b
)+(
4.4c
) we can use the explicit formula
for
(v^{0},w^{0})
to undo the integrations by parts and see that
(v^{0},w^{0})
is
indeed a weak solution as well as a very weak solution.

However, there is the trivial second very weak solution, namely

(v(t,\cdot),w(t,\cdot))=(v^{0}_{0},0)=(2\sqrt{2}\,B\,\mathbf{1}_{[-x_{*},x_{*}]},0)
. Thus, we definitely have nonuniqueness in the class of very weak
solutions.

Indeed, we have a two-parameter family of very weak solutions for the initial
conditions
(v^{0}_{0},0)=(2\sqrt{2}\,B\,\mathbf{1}_{[-x_{*},x_{*}]},0)
. The point is that we may keep the solution constant in time for
an arbitrary
t_{+}>0
for
x\geq 0
and then start with a delayed version of

(v^{0},w^{0})
. Moreover, we may choose
t_{-}>0
for starting starting a
delayed version of
(v^{0},w^{0})
for
x\leq 0
. More precisely, we choose
t_{+},t_{-}\in[0,T]
and set

(\widetilde{v}(t,x),\widetilde{w}(t,x))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}(v^{0}_{0}(x),0)&\text{for }x\geq 0\text{ and }t\in[0,t_{+}],\\
(v^{0}(t{-}t_{+},x),w^{0}(t{-}t_{+},x))&\text{for }x\geq 0\text{ and }t\in[t_{+},T],\\
(v^{0}_{0}(x),0)&\text{for }x\leq 0\text{ and }t\in[0,t_{-}],\\
(v^{0}(t{-}t_{-},x),w^{0}(t{-}t_{-},x))&\text{for }x\leq 0\text{ and }t\in[t_{-},T].\end{array}\right.

We emphasize that the different delays do not produce any nonsmoothness,
because we have
(v^{0}(t,0),w^{0}(t,0))=(2\sqrt{2}B,0)
for all

. A
direct calculation shows that
(\widetilde{v},\widetilde{w})
is a very weak solution for all
the choices of

.

4.4

Case
\eta\equiv\kappa
: families of solutions with growing
support

In the case
\kappa\equiv\eta
we have the additional simple equation for
e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
, namely

\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\kappa(w)\nabla e),\hskip 10.00002pt\kappa(w)\nabla e\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0.

Thus, we obtain exactly the same equation as for
v
and may restrict to
a solution class defined via the relation
e=Bv
for some fixed constant
B>0
.
This leads to the relation
w=e-\frac{1}{2}v^{2}=Bv{-}\frac{1}{2}v^{2}
, where we now
have the restrictions
v\in[0,2B]
and
w\in[0,B^{2}/2]
. With this the pair

(v,w)=(v,Bv{-}\frac{1}{2}v^{2})
solves the coupled system (
1.1
) if and
only if
v
solves the scalar equation

\dot{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\widetilde{\kappa}_{B}(v)\nabla v\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\widetilde{\kappa}_{B}(v)\nabla v\cdot{\mathrm{n}}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }\widetilde{\kappa}_{B}(v):=\kappa\big(Bv{-}\tfrac{1}{2}v^{2}\big).

(4.6)

For this scalar equation the general existence theory for the PME (cf.
[
Váz07
]
) can be applied and a huge set of solutions with
compact and growing support for

are known to exist.

For example, we may choose
\eta\equiv\kappa
in the form

\kappa(w)=\eta(w)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}B-\sqrt{B^{2}{-}2w}&\text{for }w\in[0,3B^{2}/8],\\

(4.7)

Then, for
v\in[0,B/2]
we have
\widetilde{\kappa}_{B}(v)=\kappa\big(Bv{-}v^{2}/2\big)=v

and arrive at the classical PME
\dot{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(v\nabla v)=\Delta(v^{2}/2)
, which
has explicit similarity solutions satisfying
v\in[0,B/2]
for sufficiently
large

, see (
1.4
).

5

General a priori estimates

To provide a first existence theory for the coupled system (
1.1
),
we restrict now to
the case that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a smooth and bounded domain. This will
simplify certain compactness arguments. Moreover, throughout this section we
will assume that the solutions
(v,w)
are classical solutions.

To derive general a priori estimate we consider a smooth
function
(v,w)\mapsto\varphi(v,w)
and find for solutions
(v,w)
of
(
1.1
) the relation

\partial_{t}\varphi(v,w)=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(w)\varphi_{v}(v,w)\nabla v+\kappa(w)\varphi_{w}(v,w)\nabla w)\big)+R_{\varphi}(v,w,\nabla v,\nabla w),

(5.1a)

where the remainder
R
is given explicitly via

\displaystyle R_{\varphi}(v,w,\nabla v,\nabla w)

\displaystyle=\eta(w)\,(\varphi_{w}(v,w){-}\varphi_{vv}(v,w))\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}

(5.1b)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt-(\eta(w){+}\kappa(w))\,\varphi_{vw}(v,w)\,\nabla v{\cdot}\nabla w-\kappa(w)\,\varphi_{ww}(v,w)\big|\nabla w\big|^{2}.

Integrating (
5.1a
) and using the boundary condition we find
along solutions

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{\Omega}\varphi(v,w)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}R_{\varphi}(v,w,\nabla v,\nabla w)\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.2)

5.1

Estimates for the
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
norms

Clearly, choosing
\varphi(v,w)=v
or
\varphi(v,w)=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
gives

R_{\varphi}\equiv 0
, which is the conservation of momentum
{\mathcal{V}}
and energy

{\mathcal{E}}
as discussed in Section
2.1
.

Moreover, choosing
\varphi(v,w)=\phi(v)
we obtain

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{\Omega}\phi(v(t,x))\;\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\phi^{\prime\prime}(v)\eta(w)\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x

Hence, for all convex functions
\phi
we obtain that
\int_{\Omega}\phi(v(t,x))\;\!\mathrm{d}x
is nondecreasing in
t
. This implies the decay of all

{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
norms, namely

\forall\,p\in[1,\infty]\ \forall\,t\geq 0:\hskip 10.00002pt\|v(t,\cdot)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|v(0,\cdot)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}.

(5.3)

For the
w\geq 0
we obviously have an a priori bound in
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(\Omega)
via

\int_{\Omega}w(t)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq{\mathcal{E}}(v(t),w(t))={\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0}).

However, because of the
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}
-right-hand side
\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}
it is
difficult to derive a priori bounds for high
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
norms.

The class
\varphi(v,w)=\Phi(w)
is also important and leads to the relation

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(w(t))\;\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\big\{\eta(w)\Phi^{\prime}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}-\kappa(w)\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)|\nabla w|^{2}\big\}\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.4)

Thus, we have growth of the integral functional if
\Phi^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\Phi^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
, e.g. for
\Phi(k)=-k^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Such functionals
include the physically relevant entropies discussed in Section
2.4
.

Remark 5.1
(
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
norms for
\eta\equiv\kappa
)

For the case
\eta\equiv\kappa
we are in a special situation, where we can use

\varphi(u,k)=\phi\big(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}{+}k)
to obtain

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\int_{\Omega}\phi\big(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}{+}k\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\eta(k)\,\phi^{\prime\prime}\big(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}{+}k\big)\>\big|u\nabla u{+}\nabla k\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.5)

Thus, we have decay for all convex
\phi
, whereas concave
\phi
leads to
growth. This estimate is also easily derived from the simple equation
\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(w)\nabla e\big)
, which holds for
\eta\equiv\kappa
.
Together with (
5.3
) we find

\displaystyle\forall\,p\in[1,\infty]\ \forall\,t\geq 0:\hskip 10.00002pt\|w(t)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|e(t)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq\|e_{0}\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|w^{0}\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{2}\|v^{0}\|^{2}_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2p}(\Omega)}.

To see that
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
bounds for
w
can also be derived for cases without

\eta\equiv\kappa
we consider now the situation
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}\eta(w)
.
For this case we can write
R_{\varphi}
as a multiple of
\eta(w)
in the form

R_{\varphi}(v,w,\nabla v,\nabla w)=-\eta(w)\binom{\nabla v}{\nabla w}\cdot{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi}\binom{\nabla v}{\nabla w}\ \text{ with }{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\varphi_{vv}-\varphi_{w}&\frac{1+\kappa_{0}}{2}\varphi_{vw}\\
\frac{1+\kappa_{0}}{2}\varphi_{vw}&\kappa_{0}\varphi_{ww}\end{array}\right).

Thus, it suffices to show that
{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi}(v,w)
is positive
semidefinite for all arguments to obtain decay estimates for
\int_{\Omega}\varphi(v,w)\;\!\mathrm{d}x
.

Lemma 5.2
(Higher norms in Spec. II)

For all integers
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
\kappa_{0}>0

there exists coefficients
a_{j}\geq 0
for
j=1,...,m
such that

\varphi_{m}(v,w)=w^{m}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}w^{m-j}v^{2j}
satisfies

{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi_{m}}(v,w)\geq 0
for all
(v,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}{\times}{[0,\infty[}
.

Proof.
The case
m=1
is solved by
\varphi_{1}(v,w)=w+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}

independently of
\kappa_{0}
. For case
m=2
a direct calculation for

\varphi(v,w)=w^{2}+a_{1}wv^{2}+a_{2}v^{4}
yields

{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi_{2}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(12a_{2}{-}a_{1})v^{2}+(2a_{1}{-}2)w&(1{+}\kappa_{0})a_{1}v\\
(1{+}\kappa_{0})a_{1}v&2\kappa_{0}\end{array}\right),

which is positive semi-definite for all
(v,w)
if and only if
a_{1}\geq 1
and

2\kappa_{0}(12a_{2}{-}a_{1})\geq(1{+}\kappa_{0})^{2}a_{1}^{2}
. Clearly, it suffices to choose

a_{1}=1
and then
a_{2}\geq 1/12+(1{+}\kappa_{0})^{2}/(24\kappa_{0})
to fulfill all
requirements in the case
m=2
.

For general
m\geq 3
we set
e=w+\frac{1}{2}v^{2}
and define

\phi^{m}_{j}(v,w):=e^{m-j}v^{2j}
,

\varphi_{m}(v,w)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}\phi_{j}^{m}(v,w),\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{V}}^{\kappa_{0}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}v^{2}&\frac{1+\kappa_{0}}{2}v\\
\frac{1+\kappa_{0}}{2}v&\kappa_{0}\end{array}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt{\mathbb{E}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}cc@{}}1&0\\
0&0\end{array}\right).

With
\kappa_{*}=(1{-}\kappa_{0})^{2}/(4\kappa_{0})
we obtain
{\mathbb{V}}^{\kappa_{0}}\geq-\kappa_{*}v^{2}{\mathbb{E}}
in the sense of positive definite matrices. Since
{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\phi}

depends linearly, we first calculate the individual terms for
\phi^{m}_{j}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\phi^{m}_{j}}

\displaystyle=(m{-}j)(m{-}j{-}1)e^{m-j-2}v^{2j}{\mathbb{V}}^{\kappa_{0}}+\big((m{-}j)4je^{m-j-1}v^{2j}+2j(2j{-}1)e^{m-j}v^{2j-2}\big){\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle\geq-\kappa_{*}(m{-}j)(m{-}j{-}1)e^{m-j-2}v^{2j+2}{\mathbb{E}}+j(4m{-}2j{-}1)e^{m-j-1}v^{2j}{\mathbb{E}},

where we used
e\geq\frac{1}{2}v^{2}
for the last term in the upper line.
Summing the estimates over
j
and using
b_{j}\geq 0
we obtain the estimate

{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi_{m}}\geq b_{*}(v,e){\mathbb{E}}
with

\displaystyle b_{*}(v,e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}\Big(j(4m{-}2j{-}1)e^{m-j-1}v^{2j}--\kappa_{*}(m{-}j)(m{-}j{-}1)e^{m-j-2}v^{2j+2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(i(4m{-}2i{-}1)b_{i}-\kappa_{*}(m{-}i{+}1)(m{-}i)b_{i-1}\big)e^{m-i-1}v^{2i}.

Clearly,
{\mathbb{A}}^{\kappa_{0}}_{\varphi_{m}}(v,w)\geq 0
for all
(v,w)
, if

b_{*}(v,e)\geq 0
for all
(v,e)
, and this is easily reach by starting with

b_{0}=1
and then choosing iteratively
b_{i}
via
i(4m{-}2i{-}1)b_{i}=\kappa_{*}(m{-}i{+}1)(m{-}i)b_{i-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

The following result follows easily by applying the previous lemma with
(
5.2
) and the fact that
\varphi_{m}
can be estimated from above
and below by
v^{2m}+w^{m}
.

Proposition 5.3
(
{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
bounds for
(v,w)
)

For the case
\kappa\equiv\kappa_{0}\eta
and
p=m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists
a constant
C(\kappa_{0},p)
such that all smooth solutions
(v,w)
of
(
1.1
) satisfy

5.2

Estimates based on comparison principles

As our system is given in terms of two scalar diffusion equations, we can apply
comparison princples when taking care of the interaction between the two
equations.

(C1)
We first observe that
w^{0}(x)\geq 0
immediately implies

w(t,x)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\Omega
. Of course this similarly
holds for
v
, but we do not need a sign condition for
u
.

(C2)
Moreover, if
(v,w)
is a smooth solution of our
system and
\underline{w}
solves the scalar PME

\dot{\underline{w}}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\kappa(\underline{w})\nabla\underline{w}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\underline{w}(0,\cdot)=w^{0},

then we have
w(t,x)\geq\underline{w}(t,x)
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\Omega
, see
[
Váz07
]
for a proof. In particular, if
w^{0}\geq c>0

then
w(t,x)\geq c
for all
(t,x)
.

(C3)
If
v_{*}\leq v^{0}(x)\leq v^{*}
for all
x\in\Omega
, then
v(t,x)\in[v_{*},v^{*}]
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\Omega
.

The next result is more advanced and truly uses the interaction of the two
equations. However, it is restricted to the case
\kappa\equiv\eta
. Under this
assumption, equation (
5.1
) gives

\partial_{t}\varphi=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\eta\nabla\varphi)-\eta\widehat{R}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\widehat{R}=(\varphi_{vv}{-}\varphi_{w})|\nabla v|^{2}+2\varphi_{vw}\nabla v{\cdot}\nabla w+\varphi_{ww}|\nabla w|^{2}.

We are interested in the case
\widehat{R}\geq 0
, for which initial conditions
with
\varphi(v^{0},w^{0})\leq 0
immediately implies
\varphi(v,w)\leq 0
for
all
t\in[0,T]
and
x\in\Omega
.

Proposition 5.4
(Comparison for
\eta\equiv\kappa
)

Consider
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
c\leq 2b
. If
(v,w)
is a classical solution of
our coupled system (
1.1
), then we have

av^{0}+b(v^{0})^{2}+cw^{0}\leq 0\text{ on }\Omega\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002ptav+bv^{2}+cw\leq 0\text{ on }[0,T]{\times}\Omega.

In particular,
|v^{0}|\leq M_{*}w^{0}
on
\Omega
implies
|v(t,x)|\leq M_{*}w(t,x)
for
(t,x)\in[0,T]{\times}\Omega
.

Proof.
We simply observe that the function
\varphi(v,w)=av+bv^{2}+cw
gives
\widehat{R}(v,w)=(2b{-}c)|\nabla v|^{2}
which is non-negative because of

c\leq 2b
. Then, the maximum principle applied to

\zeta(t,x)=\varphi(v(t,x),w(t,x))
shows that
\zeta_{0}\leq 0
implies

\zeta(t,x)\leq 0
, which is the desired result.

The last assertion follows by choosing
(a,b,c)=(1,0,{-}M_{*})
for finding

v\leq M_{*}w
and by choosing
(a,b,c)=(-1,0,{-}M_{*})
for finding

-v\leq M_{*}w
.

5.3

Dissipation estimates

Here we provide space-time estimates for various quantities, which will allow
us to derive suitable compactness for approximating sequences.

We start with the estimate obtained in Section
5.1
for the
choice
\varphi(v,w)=\frac{1}{p}|v|^{p}
with
p>1
. Integration in time gives the
estimate

\iint_{Q_{T}}\eta(w)(p{-}1)|v|^{p-2}\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{p}|v^{0}|^{p}\;\!\mathrm{d}x.

(5.7)

The special case
p=2
shows that it is sufficient to use the initial energy,
namely

\iint_{Q_{T}}\eta(w)\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}|v^{0}|^{p}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0}).

(5.8)

This estimate shows that the right-hand side in the
w
-equation in
(
1.1
) is always in
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T})
, but it will be difficult to
obtain higher integrability.

Choosing
\varphi(v,w)=\Phi(w)
leads to the dissipation relation

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\big(\kappa(w)\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)|\nabla w|^{2}-\eta(w)\Phi^{\prime}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Omega}\!\big\{\Phi(w^{0}){-}\Phi(w(T))\big\}\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

(5.9)

which is obtained by integrating (
5.4
). Here we additionally have
to impose
w(t,x)>0
in the case that
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)\to\infty
for
w\to 0^{+}
.

The last relation can be used in to two different ways to obtain a bound (i) on

\nabla w
and (ii) on
\nabla v
.

(i) Estimates for
\nabla w
:
we observe that we may use
(
5.8
) if
\Phi
satisfies

0=\Phi(0)\leq\Phi(w)\leq C_{\Phi}w,\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq\Phi^{\prime}(w)\leq C_{\Phi},\hskip 10.00002pt\Phi^{\prime\prime}(k)\geq 0

(5.10)

for some
C_{\Phi}>0
. Then, we find

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\kappa(w)\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)|\nabla w|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{\Phi}\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\eta(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t+C_{\Phi}\int_{\Omega}w^{0}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 2C_{\Phi}{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0}).

In particular, one may want to make
\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)
big for
w\approx 0
such that

\kappa(w)\Phi^{\prime\prime}(w)
is large there. Of course, we need
integrability of
\Phi^{\prime\prime}
on
{]0,\infty[}
to allow for
0\leq\Phi^{\prime}(w)\leq C_{*}
.
Hence, generalizing
[
BoG89
,
BGO97
,
MiN20
]
we will use
the family

\Phi(w)=\frac{1}{w^{\delta}(1{+}w)}\ \text{ for }w>0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }\delta\in{]0,1[}.

(5.11)

The case
0<\delta\ll 1
provides good results for large
w
, while

0<1{-}\delta\ll 1
produces good results for small
w
:
Thus, for all
\delta\in{]0,1[}
there exists
C(\delta)>0
such that for all classical solutions

(v,w)
with
w(t,x)>0
on
[0,T]{\times}\overline{\Omega}
we obtain the estimate

\forall\,\delta\in{]0,1[}\ \exists\,C_{\delta}>0:\hskip 10.00002pt\iint_{Q_{T}}\frac{\kappa(w)}{w^{\delta}(1{+}w)}\,\big|\nabla w\big|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{\delta}\,{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0}).\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(5.12)

Alternatively, we may also consider a function
\varphi(v,w)=\Psi(w)
such that

0\geq\Psi(w)\geq-C_{\Psi}(1{+}w),\hskip 10.00002pt\Psi^{\prime}(w)\leq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\Psi^{\prime\prime}(w)>0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }w>0,

(5.13)

where
\Psi(w)=-w^{\gamma}
with
\gamma\in{]0,1[}
is a typical candidate. We
can then drop the term
-\eta(w)\Psi^{\prime}(w)|\nabla v|^{2}\geq 0
and take advantage
of the fact that
\Psi^{\prime\prime}(w)
can be much bigger than
\Phi^{\prime}(w)
for
\Phi

satisfying (
5.10
).
The estimates for
\Psi
and the identity
(
5.9
) show that that positive classical solutions satisfy

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\Big(\Psi^{\prime\prime}(w)\kappa(w)\big|\nabla w\big|^{2}-\Psi^{\prime}(w)\eta(w)\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Omega}\!\big\{\Psi(w^{0})-\Psi(w(T))\big\}\;\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq\int_{\Omega}C_{\Psi}(1+w(T))\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C_{\Psi}\big(|\Omega|+{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})\big),\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(5.14)

where we used that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has a finite Lebesgue volume.

(ii) Estimates for
\nabla v
:

The best estimate for
\nabla v
can be obtained if the function
w\mapsto 1/\eta(w)
is integrable near
w=0
, which is certainly the case for

\eta(w)=w^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in{]0,1[}
. In this case, we define the
negative function
\Psi_{\eta}
via

\Psi_{\eta}(w):=-\int_{0}^{w}\frac{1}{\eta(\varkappa)}\;\!\mathrm{d}\varkappa.

The choice leads to the relation
\Psi_{\eta}^{\prime}(w)=-1/\eta(w)
and
\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{\eta}(w)=\eta^{\prime}(w)/\eta(w)^{2}\geq 0
, such that the conditions (
5.13
) hold
and (
5.14
) leads to the special case

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\Big(\big|\nabla v\big|^{2}+\frac{\eta^{\prime}(w)\kappa(w)}{\eta(w)^{2}}\big|\nabla w\big|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{\Psi_{\eta}}\big(|\Omega|+{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})\big),\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(5.15)

see
[
Nau13
, Eqn. (4.6)]
for an earlier occurrence for the case

\alpha=1/2
.

Another way of estimating
\nabla u
can be derived from (
5.8
)
if we additionally have a pointwise estimate of the form

|v(t,x)|\leq M_{*}w(t,x)
, which was derived in Proposition

5.4
for the special case
\kappa\equiv\eta
. Using the
monotonicity
\eta^{\prime}(w)\geq 0
we have
\eta(|v|/M_{*})\leq\eta(w)
and conclude

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\eta\big(\frac{1}{M_{*}}|v|\big)|\nabla v|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0}).

(5.16)

We summarize the dissipation estimate by restricting to the homogeneous case

\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\beta}
. More general cases
can easily be deduced in the same fashion. We explicitly show the dependence on

|\Omega|
such that unbounded domains like
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
can be treated as
well in cases where no dependence on
|\Omega|
is indicated.

Proposition 5.5
(Dissipation estimates)

Consider the case
\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}
and

\alpha=\beta
and

\kappa_{0}=\eta_{0}
) and

now depending

\delta

\displaystyle\alpha=\beta:\hskip 10.00002pt

Throughout, the constant
C_{*}
is independent of the lower bound
\overline{w}
.

Proof.
The results are obtained by applying the above estimate for
suitable functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Throughout use
w^{\gamma}|\nabla w|^{2}=c_{\gamma}\big|\nabla(w^{1+\gamma/2})\big|^{2}
for the smooth solutions.

Estimate (
5.17a
) follows directly from
(
5.12
).

For (

(
5.14
) with
\Psi(w)=-w^{\delta}
.

Based on the conjectures concerning the typical front behavior, which were

Remark 5.6
(
\int_{\Omega}\log w^{0}\;\!\mathrm{d}x
finite)

In their treatment of Kolmogorov’s two-equation model in

[
BuM19
]
the authors consider the the situation corresponding to

\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w
and
\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w
, i.e.
\alpha=\beta=1
. Moreover, they assume that
w^{0}
is positive almost
everywhere such that
L_{0}:=-\int_{\Omega}\log w^{0}\;\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty
. Choosing
the convex functional
\Psi(w)=-\log w
the dissipation relation
(
5.9
) leads to

\displaystyle\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\Big(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{w}|\nabla w|^{2}+\eta_{0}|\nabla v|^{2}\Big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\log w(T)-\log w^{0}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}w(T)\;\!\mathrm{d}x+L_{0}\leq{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})+L_{0}.

Of course, the assumption
L_{0}<\infty
does not allow to study solutions
with nontrivial support, i.e. it implies
\mathrm{sppt}(w(t))=\overline{\Omega}
for all

t\geq 0
.

6

Existence of solutions

Through this section we use the following standard assumptions:

\displaystyle\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\text{ is bounded with ${\mathrm{C}}^{2}$ boundary},

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\exists\,\alpha,\beta,\eta_{0},\kappa_{0}>0\ \forall\,w\geq 0:\hskip 10.00002pt\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}\text{ and }\kappa(w)=\kappa_{0}w^{\beta}.

(6.1b)

6.1

Three prototypical existence results

For given initial conditions
(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega)
we choose a sequence of smooth initial
data
(v^{0}_{\varepsilon},w^{0}_{\varepsilon})\in{\mathrm{C}}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})^{2}
such that

\displaystyle w^{0}_{\varepsilon}(x)\geq\varepsilon>0\text{ on }\Omega,

(6.2a)

\displaystyle(v^{0}_{\varepsilon},w^{0}_{\varepsilon})\to(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(\Omega),

(6.2b)

\displaystyle v^{0}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptv^{0}_{\varepsilon}\to v^{0}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega).

(6.2c)

Condition (
6.2b
) implies the convergence of the conservation laws

{\mathcal{V}}(v^{0}_{\varepsilon})\to{\mathcal{V}}(v^{0})
and
{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0}_{\varepsilon},w^{0}_{\varepsilon})\to{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})
. Condition (
6.2c
) with
p_{0}\in[1,2]

follows from (
6.2b
), hence, it will only be an extra
condition for
p_{0}>2
.

With this we have a classical parabolic system with positive viscosity such
that local existence of solutions
(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
is classical, see e.g.
[
Ama89
,
Wie92
]
or
[
Lun95
, Cha. 8]
. Of course all
these solutions satisfy our a priori bounds, in particular, we have

w_{\varepsilon}(t,x)\geq\varepsilon
as long as the solution exists, cf. Section

5.2
(C2). This implies that the solution stays as
smooth. Moreover, the structure of the equation for
v
implies the global

{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}
bound
\|v_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|v^{0}_{\varepsilon}\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, see (
5.3
). Finally,
the energy conservation
{\mathcal{E}}(v_{\varepsilon}(t),w_{\varepsilon}(t))={\mathcal{E}}(v^{0}_{\varepsilon},w^{0}_{\varepsilon})
which also implies

\iint_{Q_{T}}\eta(w_{\varepsilon})|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}(v^{0}_{\varepsilon},w^{0}_{\varepsilon})
. Thus, blow-up is not possible and the classical
solutions exist for all time.

The aim is now to show that the solutions
(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})
, or better a
suitable subsequence thereof, converge to a limit

(v,w)
that is a weak or very weak solution of our coupled system. The problem
here is that the limit
w
may have a nontrivial support strictly contained in

\Omega
. As a consequence, an integral bound

\iint_{Q_{T}}\big(w_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}|\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}|^{2}+w_{\varepsilon}^{\delta}|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{*}

(6.3)

does not necessarily imply spatial compactness for

v_{\varepsilon}
. Thus, we will provide two different existence results, in
the case
\eta(w)=\eta_{0}w^{\alpha}
with
\alpha<1
one may obtain the exponent

\delta=0
and a bound for
\nabla v_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
follows.
In the case,
\alpha\geq 1
, we can only treat the case
\eta\equiv\kappa

under the additional assumption
|v^{0}(x)|\leq M_{*}w^{0}(x)
, which allows us
to obtain a bound on
\nabla(v_{\varepsilon})^{1+\delta/2}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
.

Another way of obtaining weak solutions with a positive lower bound
\varepsilon>0

would be to adapt the methods in
[
Nau13
,
BuM19
,
MiN20
]
to the present case. Our approach is different is similar to
the one taken in
[
BeK90
,
DaG99
]
; however, our goal is more
restrictive. There, for the solutions

(\rho,\theta)
of the plasma model (
1.6
) one only asks for

\rho^{\delta/2}\nabla\theta\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathcal{P}})
where
{\mathcal{P}}:=\big\{\,(t,x)\in[0,T]{\times}\Omega\>\big|\>\rho(t,x)>0\,\big\}
.

Throughout the rest of this section we use the following short-hand notations:

(a)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}X\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\exists\,C>0\ \forall\,\varepsilon\in{]0,1[}:\hskip 10.00002ptu_{\varepsilon}\in X\text{ and }\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{X}\leq C,

(b)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{p}{\mathrm{L}}^{q}:={\mathrm{L}}^{p}\big([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{q}(\Omega)\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and similarly }{\mathrm{L}}^{q}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}\text{ or }{\mathrm{L}}^{p}{\mathrm{W}}^{s,q}.

We will use the following standard interpolation in
{\mathrm{L}}^{s}{\mathrm{L}}^{p}
(cf.
[
MiN20
, Lem. 4.2]
):

\displaystyle\forall\,\theta\in[0,1],\ s,s_{1},s_{2},p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]\text{ with }\textstyle\frac{1}{s}=\frac{1{-}\theta}{s_{1}}+\frac{\theta}{s_{2}}\text{ and }\frac{1}{p}=\frac{1{-}\theta}{p_{1}}+\frac{\theta}{p_{2}}

(6.4)

\displaystyle\exists\,C>0\ \forall\,u\in{\mathrm{L}}^{s_{1}}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{1}}\cap{\mathrm{L}}^{s_{2}}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{2}}:\hskip 10.00002pt\|u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{s}{\mathrm{L}}^{p}}\leq C\|u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{s_{1}}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{1}}}^{1-\theta}\|u\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{s_{2}}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{2}}}^{\theta}.

Moreover, we define
2_{d}^{*}
as the optimal exponent in the embedding

{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\subset{\mathrm{L}}^{2_{d}^{*}}(\Omega)
, i.e.
2_{d}^{*}=2d/(d{-}2)
for

d\geq 3
,
2_{1}^{*}=\infty
, and
2_{2}^{*}<\infty
.

We are now ready to state three different existence results. The first result
provides weak solutions and relies on the restriction (
6.5
)
concerning the exponents

\alpha
and
\beta
as well as the integrability power
p_{0}
for
v^{0}
. We
do not expect that condition (
6.5
) is sharp.

Theorem 6.1
(Existence of weak solutions for
\alpha<1
)

Assume that (
6.1
) holds with
\alpha\in{]0,1[}
.
Moreover, consider
p_{0}\geq 2
such that

\frac{\alpha}{\beta+2-2/2_{d}^{*}}+\frac{1}{p_{0}+2-2p_{0}/2_{d}^{*}}<\frac{1}{2}.

(6.5)

Then, for all initial data
(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega)
there exists a weak solution
(v,w)

in the sense of Definition
4.3
. Moreover, this solution
satisfies

\nabla v\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptw\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\beta+2-2/2_{d}^{*}}(Q_{T}).

Condition (
6.5
) is somehow restrictive and shows that small

\alpha
and large
\beta
are desirable. Even assuming
v^{0}\in{\mathrm{C}}(\overline{\Omega})\subset{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, i.e.
p_{0}=\infty
, we still have
to satisfy

2\alpha<\beta+2-\max\{0,1{-}2/d\}.

Because of
\alpha\in{]0,1[}
this is always satisfied for
d\leq 2
, but may
provide a nontrivial lower bound for
d\geq 3
and
\alpha>1/2
. However, the
important case
\alpha=\beta\in{]0,1[}
is always possible, even for finite

p_{0}\in{[2,\infty[}
.

For example, assuming
\alpha=\beta=1/2
(as in Kolmogorov’s one-equation or
Prandtl’s model discussed in Section
8.1
) we obtain, after some
computation, the restriction

p_{0}\geq 2\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptp_{0}>\frac{2d^{2}}{d{+}4}.

For the next result we stay in the case
\alpha\in{]0,1[}
and show that very
weak solutions can be obtained in a previously inaccessible regime. However,
the usage of the weighted gradients as introduced in Definition

4.1
forces us to control
\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T})
whereas the equation provides control on
\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{\beta/2}

only. Thus, the restriction
\beta<2\alpha
will be needed.

Theorem 6.2
(Existence of very weak solutions for
\alpha<1
)

Assume that (
6.1
) holds with

0<\beta<2\alpha<2
.
Moreover, consider
p_{0}\geq 2
such that

\frac{2\alpha+1-2/2_{d}^{*}}{2(\beta{+}2{-}2/2_{d}^{*})}+\frac{1}{p_{0}+2-2p_{0}/2_{d}^{*}}<1.

(6.6)

Then, for all initial data
(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}_{\geq}(\Omega)
there exists a very weak solution
(v,w)

in the sense of Definition
4.3
. Moreover, this solution
satisfies

\nabla v\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptw\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\beta+2-2/2_{d}^{*}}(Q_{T}).

We emphasize that condition (
6.6
) is weaker than
(
6.5
). Indeed, consider the case
p_{0}=\infty
for simplicity, then
(
6.6
) is equivalent to
2\alpha<2\beta+3-2/2_{d}^{*}
, which
is automatically satisfied for
0\leq\beta<2\alpha<2
, because of
2\alpha\leq\alpha+1<2\beta+1<2\beta+3-2/2_{d}^{*}
.

However, for
p_{0}=\infty

(
6.5
) reduces to
2\alpha<\beta+2-2/2_{d}^{*}
which is violated
for
d\geq 3
and suitable
\alpha
and
\beta
, e.g. for

(d,\alpha,\beta)=(4,7/8,1/8)
we have

0<\beta=1/8<2\alpha=7/4<2,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt2\alpha=7/4\not<13/8=2{+}1/8{-}1/2=2{+}\beta{-}2/2_{d}^{*}.

Thus, there are cases where Theorem
6.2
can be applied but
Theorem
6.1
cannot.

Finally we treat the case
\eta\equiv\kappa
which is special in two ways:
first the energy density
e=\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+w
satisfies the simple equation
\dot{e}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta(w)\nabla e\big)
, and second we can pointwise bound
v
by

w
, see Proposition
5.4
. The case
\kappa\equiv\eta=w^{\alpha}
is very special, but it is presently the only case where
\alpha\geq 1
can be handled.

Theorem 6.3
(Existence of very weak solutions for
\eta\equiv\kappa
)

Assume that (
6.1
) holds with
\eta(w)=\kappa(w)=w^{\alpha}
with

\alpha>0
. Moreover, consider
p_{0}\geq 2
such that

p_{0}\Big(1-\frac{2}{2_{d}^{*}}\Big)>\sqrt{8+4\alpha+\alpha^{2}/4}\>-\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(6.7)

Then, for all
M_{*}>0
and all initial data
(v^{0},w^{0})\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}/2}_{\geq}(\Omega)
satisfying

|v^{0}(x)|\leq M_{*}w^{0}(x)\ \text{ for a.a. }x\in\Omega

(6.8)

there exists a very weak solution
(v,w)

in the sense of Definition
4.3
. Moreover, this solution
satisfies

|v(t,x)|\leq M_{*}w(t,x)\ \text{ for a.a. }(t,x)\in Q_{T}.

The right-hand side in (
6.7
) is strictly increasing with
range
{]2,4[}
. Hence, the assumption
p_{0}\geq\max\{4,2d\}
is sufficient for all

\alpha>0
.

The proofs of these three results are the contents of the following three
subsections.

6.2

Limit passage to weak solutions in the case
\alpha<1

Here we provide the proof of existence of weak solutions first, and then show
what has to be changed for obtaining very weak solutions.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.
​​We proceed in three
steps: 1. a priori estimates, 2. compactness, and 3. identification of nonlinear
limits.

Step 1: A priori estimates.

Because of
{\mathcal{E}}(v_{\varepsilon}(t),w_{\varepsilon}(t))=E_{0}

and (
5.17c
) we obviously have
v_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}
.

For
w_{\varepsilon}
we use (
5.17b
) to obtain

\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{(\beta+\delta)/2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
for all

\delta\in{]0,1[}
. Since energy conservation implies

w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}
, we conclude

w_{\varepsilon}^{(\beta+\delta)/2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}
. For bounded Lipschitz domains

\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have the embedding

{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\subset{\mathrm{L}}^{2_{d}^{*}}(\Omega)
with
2_{1}^{*}=\infty
,

2_{2}^{*}<\infty
, and
2_{d}^{*}=2d/(d{-}2)
for
d\geq 3
. We obtain

w_{\varepsilon}^{(\beta+\delta)/2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{L}}^{2_{d}^{*}}
, or equivalently

w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\beta+\delta}{\mathrm{L}}^{q}
for
q=(\beta{+}\delta)2_{d}^{*}/2
.
Using the interpolation (
6.4
) with

w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}
gives

w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{p}(Q_{T})={\mathrm{L}}^{p}{\mathrm{L}}^{p}\text{ with }p=p(\delta):=\beta+\delta+1-2/2_{d}^{*}.

(6.9)

For controlling the gradient of
\Pi(w)=\frac{1}{\beta+1}w^{\beta+1}
in

{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T})={\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}
for some
\eta>0
we use the
simple identity

\nabla(\Pi(w))=w^{\beta}\nabla w=\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}w^{\gamma_{1}}\nabla\big(w^{\gamma_{2}}\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for }\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=\beta+1.

Choosing
\gamma_{2}=(\beta{+}\delta)/2
and using
\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{(\beta+\delta)/2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}
we find

\nabla\Pi(w_{\varepsilon})\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T})
if we have
w_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma_{1}}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{q}{\mathrm{L}}^{q}
or
w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\gamma_{1}q}{\mathrm{L}}^{\gamma_{1}q}
with
1/2+1/q<1
. Hence, it suffices to
show
p(\delta)>2\gamma_{1}(\delta)=:\beta+2-\delta
, which holds for for some

\delta\in{]0,1[}
because of the continuity of

\delta\mapsto(\gamma_{2}(\delta),p(\delta))
on
[0,1]
and
p(1)=\beta+2-2/2_{d}^{*}>2\gamma_{2}(1)=\beta+1
. In summary, we
have established

\exists\,\eta>0:\hskip 20.00003pt\nabla\big(\Pi(w_{\varepsilon})\big)\ \text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}\ {\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T}).

(6.10)

Clearly, the classical dissipation estimate (
5.8
) for the

v
-equation gives

\displaystyle U_{\varepsilon}:=\eta(w_{\varepsilon})^{1/2}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\ \text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}\ {\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})

(6.11)

Similarly, using
w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{L}}^{2/\alpha}\subset{\mathrm{L}}^{2/\alpha}(Q_{T})
and
\eta(w_{\varepsilon})\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\,w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}
yields

\displaystyle\eta(w_{\varepsilon})\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\ \text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}\ {\mathrm{L}}^{2/(1{+}\alpha)}(Q_{T}).

(6.12)

Step 2: Compactness.

As usual we apply an Aubin-Lions-Simon theorem, see

[
Sim87
, Cor. 4]
,
[
Lio69
, Thm. 5.1]
,

[
Rou13
, Lem. 7.7]
and for nonlinear versions see

[
Mou16
, Thm. 1]
.

For this we first create
spatial compactness
,
which is trivial for
v_{\varepsilon}
because of
v_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}
with

{\mathrm{H}}^{1}={\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
compactly embedded into
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega)
.

For
w_{\varepsilon}
we have the difficulty that we only control the derivative of
power of
w
in (
6.10
), namely

w_{\varepsilon}^{1+\beta}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}{\mathrm{W}}^{1,1+\eta}
. However, employing the
fact that the Nemitskii operator
u\mapsto u^{\theta}
maps

{\mathrm{W}}^{s,p}(\Omega)
into
{\mathrm{W}}^{\theta s,p/\theta}(\Omega)
for all

s\in{]0,1[}
and
p\in{]1,\infty[}
, see Proposition

6.4
. This leads to

w_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{(1{+}\eta)(1+\beta)}{\mathrm{W}}^{\sigma,(1{+}\eta)(1+\beta)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all }\sigma\in{\big[0,\tfrac{1}{1{+}\beta}\big[}.

Clearly,
{\mathrm{W}}^{\sigma,(1{+}\eta)(1+\beta)}(\Omega)
is still compactly
embedded into
{\mathrm{L}}^{1{+}\eta}(\Omega)
.

To derive temporal compactness we use the PDEs for
\dot{v}_{\varepsilon}
and
\dot{w}_{\varepsilon}
, see (
1.1
). From (
1.1a
) and
(
6.12
) we obtain

\dot{v}_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{2/(1{+}\alpha)}{\mathrm{W}}_{0}^{-1,2/(1{+}\alpha)}={\mathrm{L}}^{2/(1{+}\alpha)}\big({\mathrm{W}}^{1,2/(1{-}\alpha)}(\Omega)\big)^{*}.

(6.13)

Starting from
\nabla\Pi(w_{\varepsilon})\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T})
and
\eta(w_{\varepsilon})|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T})

the PDE (
1.1b
) for
w_{\varepsilon}
gives

\dot{w}_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}{\mathrm{W}}_{0}^{-1,1+\eta}+{\mathrm{L}}^{1}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}\ \subset\ {\mathrm{L}}^{1}\big({\mathrm{W}}^{1,1+1/\eta}(\Omega)\big)^{*}.

(6.14)

Now applying Banach’s selection principle for weak convergence and
Aubin-Lions-Simon theorem strong convergence we obtain a limit pair
(v,w)
with

v\in{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{w}}([0,T];{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(\Omega))\cap{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptw\in{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{L}}^{1}\cap{\mathrm{L}}^{(1{+}\eta)(1+\beta)}{\mathrm{W}}^{\sigma,(1{+}\eta)(1+\beta)}

and, along a suitable subsequence, the convergences

\displaystyle(v_{\varepsilon}(x),w_{\varepsilon}(x))

\displaystyle\to(v(x),w(x))

a.e. in

\displaystyle\Omega;

(6.15a)

\displaystyle(v_{\varepsilon},w_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\to(v,w)

in

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T}){\times}{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T});

(6.15b)

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup v

in

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1},

(6.15c)

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup w

in

\displaystyle{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\beta}{\mathrm{W}}^{1/(2+\beta),1+\beta}.

(6.15d)

Step 3: Identification of nonlinear limits.

It remains to pass to the limit in the nonlinear terms of the weak formulation,
namely in

\text{(i) }\eta(w_{\varepsilon})\nabla v_{\varepsilon},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{(ii) }\nabla\Pi(w_{\varepsilon}),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{(iii) }\eta(w_{\varepsilon})v_{\varepsilon}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}.

In the trivial nonlinear term
\iint_{Q_{T}}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\partial_{t}\xi\;\!\mathrm{d}x\;\!\mathrm{d}t
in the
left-hand side of (
4.3c
) , the limit passage follows easily from
the strong convergence of
v_{\varepsilon}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
.

Concerning the term (i) we recall the decomposition

w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}U_{\varepsilon}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }U_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}.

By (
6.15b
) we have
w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}\to w^{\alpha/2}
in

{\mathrm{L}}^{2/\alpha}
. One the one hand, we have
U_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup U
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
because of
(
6.11
). On the other hand, the strong convergence of

w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}
and
\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\nabla v
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
implies

U_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup w^{\alpha/2}\nabla v
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2/(1{+}\alpha)}(Q_{T})
. Thus,

U=w^{\alpha/2}\nabla v
and, with the same argument we find

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha/2}U_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup w^{\alpha/2}U=w^{\alpha}\nabla v\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }{\mathrm{L}}^{2/(1+\alpha)}.

(6.16)

For term (ii) we use
\Pi(w)=\frac{1}{1+\beta}w^{1+\beta}
and the a priori
estimates from Step 2, such that along a further subsequence (not relabeled) we
have, for all
q\in{[1,1{+}\beta 2_{d}^{*}/2[}
,

w_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup w\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{q}(Q_{T}),\hskip 10.00002ptw_{\varepsilon}^{1+\beta}\rightharpoonup g\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T}),\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla w_{\varepsilon}^{1+\beta}\rightharpoonup G\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{1+\eta}(Q_{T}).

Together with the strong convergence (
6.15b
) we obtain
w_{\varepsilon}\to w
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{q}({\mathbb{Q}}_{T})
for the same
q
such that
w_{\varepsilon}^{1+\beta}\to w^{1+\beta}
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T})
, which implies

g=w^{1+\beta}
as desired, and
G=\nabla g=\nabla\Pi(w)
follows.

For the term (iii) we may need the extra condition
v^{0}\in{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}(\Omega)
, which implies
v_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}{\mathrm{L}}^{p_{0}}
. Interpolation with
v_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{H}}^{1}\subset{\mathrm{L}}^{2}{\mathrm{L}}^{2_{d}^{*}}
via (
6.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle v_{\varepsilon}\text{\footnotesize\raisebox{-0.87782pt}{$\;\overset{\,\mathrm{bdd}\;}{\in}\,$}}{\mathrm{L}}^{q_{0}}(Q_{T})\text{ with }q_{0}:=p_{0}+2-2p_{0}/2_{d}^{*},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptv_{\varepsilon}\to v\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{q}(Q_{T})\text{ for }q\in{[1,q_{0}[},

where the second statement uses (
6.15b
). Similarly,
(
6.9
) and (
6.15b
) yield

w_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\to w^{\alpha}\text{ in }{\mathrm{L}}^{p/\alpha}(Q_{T})\text{ for }p\in{[1,\beta{+}2{-}2/2_{d}^{*}[}.

Combining these two strong convergences and exploiting the condition
(
6.5
), we conclude
\eta(w_{\varepsilon})v_{\varepsilon}\to\eta(w)v
in

{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(Q_{T})
. Together with the weak convergence
\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\to\nabla v

(cf. (
6.15c
)) we obtain the desired weak convergence for term
(iii), i.e.
\eta(w_{\varepsilon})v_{\varepsilon}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\to\eta(w)v\nabla v
in

{\mathrm{L}}^{1}(Q_{T})
. Thus, the limit passage for all terms on the weak formulation
(
4.3
), which shows that
(v,w)
is indeed a weak solution.

This finishes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.

6.3

Limit passage to very weak solutions for
\alpha<1

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.
​​We follow the same
three steps as in the proof of Theorem
6.1
. Step 1 and
Step 2 are in fact identical, and in Step 3 we have to exploit the idea of
weighted gradients for
\eta(w)\nabla v
, which means that we have to prove
weak convergence in

To substantiate our conjecture we use the scaling properties of the coupled

\beta=\alpha

\displaystyle\tau=\log(t{+}1),\ \ y=(t{+}1)^{-\delta}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\big(v(t,x),w(t,x)\big)=\big((t{+}1)^{-d\theta\delta}\,\widetilde{v}(\tau,y)\,,\,(t{+}1)^{-d\delta}\,\widetilde{w}(\tau,y)\big),

\widetilde{w}
by its limit
w_{\mathrm{st}}^{E_{0}}
.
The surprising fact is that this equation has a unique steady state which is
given by an explicit formula.

Proposition 7.3
(Similarity solutions
(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{st}},\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}})
)

For each pair
(V_{0},E_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}{\times}{]0,\infty[}
there is a unique solution

(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{st}},\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}})
of the system (where
\delta=1/(2{+}d\beta)\,
)

0=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\delta\widehat{v}\,y+\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widehat{v}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt0=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\delta\widetilde{w}\,y+\kappa_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widetilde{w}\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\!\!\widehat{v}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=V_{0},\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\!\!\widetilde{w}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=E_{0}.

This solution is given explicitly in the form

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{st}}(y),\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}}(y)\big)=\Big(a{\mathbb{W}}_{\sigma}(by)\,,\>c{\mathbb{W}}_{1/\beta}(by)\Big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with }\sigma=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\eta_{0}\beta},\hskip 10.00002ptb^{2}=\frac{\beta\delta}{2c^{\beta}\kappa_{0}},

\displaystyle\hskip 120.00018ptV_{0}=\frac{a}{b^{d}}\Theta(\kappa_{0}/(\beta\eta_{0}),d),\hskip 10.00002ptE_{0}=\frac{c}{b^{d}}\Theta(1/\beta,d).

Proof.
The result follows easily with (
7.1b
). Indeed, we
even have
\delta\widehat{v}\,y+\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widehat{v}\equiv 0
and

\delta\widetilde{w}\,y+\kappa_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widetilde{w}\equiv 0
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Of course, justifying the convergence to the steady states

(\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{st}},\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}})
for a suitable solution class
(\widehat{v},\widetilde{w})
of the
full system (
7.4
) is a nontrivial task. This can be seen by
inserting the steady state into the exponentially decaying term which gives the
perturbation

\mathrm{e}^{-d\delta\tau}\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}}^{\alpha}|\nabla\widehat{v}_{\mathrm{st}}|^{2}=\widetilde{c}\,\mathrm{e}^{-d\delta\tau}{\mathbb{W}}_{2\kappa_{0}/(\beta\eta_{0})-1}(by)|y|^{2}.

This term lies in
{\mathrm{L}}^{\infty}(0,T;{\mathrm{L}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
but is singular at
b|y|=1
for
\beta>2\kappa_{0}/\eta_{0}
.

7.3

Conjectured longtime behavior for
\beta\neq\alpha

With the transformation (
7.3
) with

\delta=1/(2{+}d\beta)
we now find the transformed system

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{v}\>

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\delta\,\widetilde{v}\>y+\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\tau}\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widetilde{v}\big)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{with }\gamma=(\beta{-}\alpha)d\delta,

(7.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widetilde{w}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\delta\widetilde{w}\,y+\kappa_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}\nabla\widetilde{w}\big)+\mathrm{e}^{(\gamma-d\delta)\tau}\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}|\nabla\widetilde{v}|^{2}.

(7.9b)

We consider the cases
\alpha>\beta>0
and

\beta>\alpha>0
separately.

Case
\gamma=(\beta{-}\alpha)d\delta>0
:
The chosen scaling
leads to the exponentially growing prefactor for the diffusion in
(
7.9a
). Hence it is dominating the concentration term such
that we expect for large
\tau
that
\nabla v(\tau)
is small. This can also
be seen by testing (
7.9a
) by
\mathrm{e}^{-d\delta\tau}v(\tau)

which leads to

\iint_{Q_{T}}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{(\gamma-d\delta)\tau}\eta_{0}\widetilde{w}^{\beta}|\nabla\widetilde{v}|^{2}\;\!\mathrm{d}y\;\!\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\widetilde{v}(0)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}(R^{d})}^{2}.

Thus, it is reasonable to expect that
\widetilde{v}(t)
converges to
c\,\mathbf{1}_{B_{1/b}(0)}(\cdot)
while
\widetilde{w}
converges to
\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}}
as for the
case
\alpha=\beta
.

A proof of this conjecture will be even more difficult, because
\nabla v(\tau)
must develop a singularity near the boundary of
B_{1/b}(0)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to allow for the convergence
\widetilde{v}(\tau)\to c\,\mathbf{1}_{B_{1/b}(0)}(\cdot)
in
{\mathrm{L}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Case
\gamma=(\beta{-}\alpha)d\delta<0
:
In this case we
expect a different behavior because of the decaying diffusion in the equation
for
\widetilde{v}
. For large
\tau
the dominating term in (
7.9a
) is
the transport term
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\big(\delta\widetilde{v}y\big)
which is simply due to the
similarity rescaling. Hence, for
\tau>\tau_{0}\gg 1
one expects

\widetilde{v}(\tau,y)\approx\mathrm{e}^{d\delta(t-\tau_{0})}\widetilde{v}\big(\tau_{0},\mathrm{e}^{\delta(\tau-\tau_{0})}y\big)
, which in the original physical
coordinates means the same as
v(t,x)\approx v(t_{0},x)
for
t>t_{0}\gg 1
. Thus, it is likely that

\|v(t)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}}^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-d\delta\tau}\|\widetilde{v}(\tau)\|_{{\mathrm{L}}^{2}}^{2}
does not
converge to
0
for
\tau=\log(t{+}1)\to\infty
. Consequently, the
nondecreasing function
\widetilde{E}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widetilde{w}(\tau,y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
is converging
to some
\widetilde{E}_{\infty}\in{[E(0),E_{0}]}
with
E_{0}={\mathcal{E}}(v^{0},w^{0})
. There is
still a chance that
\widetilde{w}
converges to some
\widetilde{w}_{\mathrm{st}}
but now with mass

\widetilde{E}_{\infty}
instead of
E_{0}
.

8

Parabolic systems in turbulence modeling

The author’s motivation for studying the given class of coupled degenerate
parabolic equation comes from turbulence modeling. However, for the sake of a
concise presentation the model was simplified considerably, but still keeping
the main feature of a velocity-type variable with a degenerate viscosity
depending on the energy-like variable, which is now the mean turbulent kinetic energy

k\geq 0
playing the role of
w
. We refer to

[
Lew97
,
GL∗03
,
LeL07
,
DrN09
,
Nau13
,
ChL14
]
for a
mathematical exposition of the ideas behind of such a turbulent modeling and to

[
Wil93
, Ch. 4]
for a fluid mechanical approach.

We first introduce Prandtl’s model for turbulence and then
Kolmogorov’s two-equation model. In both cases, we introduce the full model
with the macroscopically mean velocity
{\mathbf{u}}
satisfying the incompressibility

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathbf{u}}=0
, the pressure
p
, and the turbulent kinetic energy
k\geq 0
. Then, we show that restricting to simple shear flows of the form
{\mathbf{u}}(t,x)=\big(0,...,0,v(t,x_{1},...,x_{d})\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
we obtain a system that has the form of
our coupled system (
1.1
) with some simple extra terms.

8.1

Kolmogorov’s one-equation model = Prandtl’s model

Prandtl’s one-equation model for turbulence was developed from 1925 to 1945,
see
[
Pra25
,
Pra46
]
and the historical remarks
about the development of the model in
[
Nau13
, p. 20]
.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\overline{\nabla}{\mathbf{u}}-\overline{\nabla}p=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\big(\ell\sqrt{k}\,\overline{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{u}})\big)+f,\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}{\mathbf{u}}=0,

\displaystyle\dot{k}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\overline{\nabla}k=\widehat{\kappa}\,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\big(\ell\sqrt{k}\,\overline{\nabla}k\big)+\ell\sqrt{k}\,|\overline{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{u}})|^{2}-a\,\frac{k\sqrt{k}}{\ell},

where
a\geq 0
is a dimensional parameter and
\ell>0
is a constant
characteristic length that may depend on position
(e.g. via the distance to the wall). Moreover,
\overline{\nabla}
,
\overline{{\mathrm{D}}}
, and

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}
stand for the differential operators in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
with
d\in\{1,2\}
.

Looking for shear flows as defined above and assuming that the external force
vanishes, i.e.
f\equiv 0
, and that
k
is independent of
x_{d+1}

we obtain the coupled system

\dot{v}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\!\big(\eta_{0}k^{1/2}\nabla v\big),\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{k}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\!\big(\kappa_{0}k^{1/2}\nabla k\big)+\eta_{0}k^{1/2}|\nabla v|^{2}-\frac{a}{\ell}\,k^{3/2}

(8.1)

with
\eta_{0}=\ell/2
and
\kappa_{0}=\widehat{\kappa}\ell
.
Clearly, when neglecting the term involving
a
, we arrive at our coupled system
in the case
\alpha=\beta=1/2
.

The case
a>0
does not pose any additional problem when constructing
solutions. In particular, the term reduces the total energy in the form

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\mathcal{E}}(v,k)=-\frac{a}{\ell}\int_{\Omega}k^{3/2}\;\!\mathrm{d}x\leq 0
.
Hence, the a priori estimates of this equation remain the same, such that existence
of weak solutions with
k(t,x)\geq\underline{k}>0
was shown already in

[
Nau13
]
. Our existence result for weak solutions in Theorem

6.1
works for
p_{0}=2
because of
\alpha=\beta=1/2
.

The general scaling (
2.2
) leading to (
2.6
)
can still be applied, but to have the same scaling behavior for the terms
\dot{k}
and
k^{3/2}
we have only one choice, namely
\gamma=2
and with

\alpha=1/2
and
2\delta+\alpha\gamma=1
we find
\delta=0
. Hence, for

(\widehat{v},\widehat{w})=\big(\frac{1}{t}v,\frac{1}{t^{2}}w\big)
we
find the rescaled equation

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widehat{v}\>-\ \widehat{v}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\eta_{0}\widehat{k}{}^{1/2}\,\nabla\widehat{v}\big),

(8.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widehat{k}-2\widehat{k}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\kappa_{0}\widehat{k}{}^{1/2}\nabla\widehat{k}\big)+\eta_{0}\widehat{k}{}^{1/2}\big|\nabla\widehat{v}\big|^{2}-\frac{a}{\ell}\,\widehat{k}{}^{3/2}.

We may still consider the conserved quantities and find the relations

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\int_{\Omega}\!\widehat{v}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Omega}\widehat{v}\;\!\mathrm{d}y\ \text{ and }\ \frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{v}^{2}{+}\widehat{k}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}y=2\int_{\Omega}\!\big(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{v}^{2}{+}\widehat{k}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\Omega}\frac{a}{\ell}\,\widehat{k}^{3/2}\;\!\mathrm{d}y.

Thus, we may expect that (
8.2
) has steady states

(v_{\mathrm{st}},k_{\mathrm{st}})
with nontrivial
k_{\mathrm{st}}\geq 0
(but necessarily with
\int_{\Omega}v_{\mathrm{st}}\;\!\mathrm{d}y=0
) that correspond to solutions of (
8.1
) that
decay, namely
(v(t,x),k(t,x))=(\frac{1}{t}v_{\mathrm{st}}(x),\frac{1}{t^{2}}k_{\mathrm{st}}(x))
.

8.2

Kolmogorov’s
k
-
\omega
model

Kolmogorov’s model
[
Kol42
]
(see
[
Spa91
]
for a translation,
sometimes also called Wilcox
k
-
\omega
model because of
[
Wil93
]
)
consists of the Navier-Stokes equation for the velocity
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the
pressure
p
coupled to two scalar equations, namely
for the specific dissipation rate
\omega>0
(or better dissipation per unit
turbulent kinetic energy) and the turbulent energy
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\overline{\nabla}{\mathbf{u}}+\overline{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\overline{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{u}})\big),\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\,{\mathbf{u}}=0,

(8.3a)

\displaystyle\dot{\omega}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\overline{\nabla}\omega

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}\,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\overline{\nabla}\omega\big)-\alpha_{1}\omega^{2},

(8.3b)

\displaystyle\dot{k}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\overline{\nabla}k

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}\,\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{div}}}\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\overline{\nabla}k\big)+\mu_{1}\tfrac{k}{\omega}\,\big|\overline{{\mathrm{D}}}({\mathbf{u}})\big|^{2}-\alpha_{2}k\omega.

(8.3c)

The main point is that all quantities are convected with the fluid
velocity
{\mathbf{v}}
and that all quantities diffuse with “viscosities”
that are proportional to
k/\omega
by the dimensionless factors
\mu_{j}>0
.
There are sink terms in the equations for
\omega
and
k
, namely

-\alpha_{1}\omega^{2}
and
-\alpha_{2}k\omega
with dimensionless nonnegative
constants
\alpha_{j}
.
The nonlinearities in system (
8.3
) are devised in a specific way to
allow for a rich scaling group, see

[
MiN20
, Sec. 2]
.

The existence of weak solutions to the above coupled system is studied in

[
MiN15
,
BuM19
,
MiN20
]
. However, in both of these
papers the construction of the solutions for (
8.3
) strongly relies on
lower bounds on
k(t,x)
. In
[
MiN15
,
MiN20
]
the assumption

k_{0}(x)=k(0,x)\geq k_{*}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
was used, whereas

[
BuM19
]
shows that the weaker assumption
\int_{\Omega}\big|\log k_{0}(x)\big|\;\!\mathrm{d}x<\infty
is sufficient.

Here we are interested in solutions that may have compact
support, i.e.
k(t,x)=0
is allowed on a set of positive measure. In those
regions the regularizing diffusion terms
\mu_{j}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\frac{k}{\omega}\nabla\,\cdot\,)

disappear. Thus, we also restrict to shear flows as above:

\displaystyle\dot{v}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\mu_{1}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\nabla v\big),\hskip 20.00003pt\dot{\omega}=\mu_{2}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\nabla\omega\big)-\omega^{2},

(8.4)

\displaystyle\dot{k}

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\!\big(\tfrac{k}{\omega}\nabla k\big)+\tfrac{\mu_{1}}{2}\,\tfrac{k}{\omega}|\nabla v|^{2}-ak\omega.

The special symmetry of the nonlinearities allows us for more scalings than the
Prandtl equation (
8.1
), because (
8.4
) only
contains dimensionless parameters
\mu_{j}
and
a
whereas
(
8.1
) contains the Prandtl length
\ell
.
We can look for solutions in
the following form (cf. (
2.2
)), where now
\omega
is given by
an explicit solution that is independent of
v
and
k
:

\displaystyle\tau=\log(t{+}1),\hskip 10.00002pty=(t{+}1)^{-\delta}x,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}

\displaystyle\big(v(t,x),\omega(t,x),k(t,x))=\big({(t{+}1)^{-\gamma/2}}\>\widehat{v}(\tau,y),\ \frac{1}{t{+}1},\ {(t{+}1)^{-\gamma}}\>\widehat{k}(\tau,y)\big).

If the exponents
\gamma
and
\delta
are chosen with
\gamma+2\delta=2
we
are lead to the rescaled system

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widehat{v}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\widehat{v}-\delta\,y\cdot\nabla\widehat{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\widehat{k}\nabla\widehat{v}),

(8.5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\widehat{k}-\gamma\,\widehat{k}-\delta\,y\cdot\nabla\widehat{k}

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\widehat{k}\nabla\widehat{k})+\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\widehat{k}|\nabla\widehat{v}|^{2}-\alpha\,\widehat{k}.

(8.5b)

The case
(\gamma,\delta)=(2,0)
can be applied in bounded domains
\Omega
and
it corresponds to the the case in the Prandtl equation. Clearly, we are in the
case
\alpha=\beta=1
with two additional linear terms, namely

\widetilde{v}
and
(2{-}a)\widetilde{k}
. Thus, the existence theory of very weak solutions
in Theorem (
6.3
) only applies in the case
\mu_{1}=2\mu_{2}
.

For the case
\delta>0
we consider
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and the rescaled momentum

V(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}v(y)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
and the
rescaled energy
E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\big(\frac{1}{2}\widehat{v}^{2}{+}\widehat{k}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}y
now satisfies, along solutions
(\widehat{v}(\tau),\widehat{k}(\tau))
, the
linear relations

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}V(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=\big(\frac{\gamma}{2}-d\delta)\big)V(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=(\gamma{-}d\delta)E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})-a\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widehat{k}\;\!\mathrm{d}y.

(8.6)

Because of
2=\gamma+2\delta
the case
(\gamma,\delta)=\big(\frac{2d}{1{+}d},\ \frac{1}{1{+}d}\big)
is special as it leads to

\tbinom{\gamma}{\delta}=\tfrac{1}{1{+}d}\tbinom{2d}{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\Longrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}V(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=0\ \text{ and }\ \frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})=\frac{d}{1{+}d}E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})-a\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\widehat{k}\;\!\mathrm{d}y.

For
a<\frac{d}{1{+}d}
we obtain
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})>0

as long as
E(\widehat{v},\widehat{k})>0
, so no steady state can exist. However, for
a>\frac{d}{1{+}d}
one can expect the existence of a family of state states

(v_{\mathrm{st}},k_{\mathrm{st}})
such that
V(v_{\mathrm{st}},k_{\mathrm{st}})=V_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
