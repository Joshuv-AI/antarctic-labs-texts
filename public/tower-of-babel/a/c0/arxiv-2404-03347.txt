Nonequilibrium properties of autocatalytic networks
Armand Despons

Abstract
Autocatalysis, the ability of a chemical system to make more of itself, is a crucial feature in metabolism and is speculated to have played a decisive role in the origin of life. Nevertheless, how autocatalytic systems behave far from equilibrium remains unexplored. In this work, we elaborate on recent advances regarding the stoichiometric characterization of autocatalytic networks, particularly their absence of mass-like conservation laws, to study how this topological feature influences their nonequilibrium behavior. Building upon the peculiar topology of autocatalytic networks, we derive a decomposition of the chemical fluxes, which highlights the existence of productive modes in their dynamics. These modes produce the autocatalysts in net excess and require the presence of external fuel/waste species to operate. Relying solely on topology, the fluxes decomposition holds under broad conditions and, in particular, do not require steady-state or elementary reactions. Additionally, we show that once externally controlled, the non-conservative forces brought by the external species do not act on these productive modes. This must be considered when one is interested in the thermodynamics of open autocatalytic networks. Specifically, we show that an additional term must be added to the semigrand free-energy. Finally, from the thermodynamical potential, we derive the thermodynamical cost associated with the production of autocatalysts.

Outline of the paper

To derive our results, we build upon the stoichiometric condition of autocatalysis given by Blokhuis
et al.
in Ref.
[
16
]
, recalled in Eq. (
1
), and we introduce the notion of autocatalytic networks.
Specifically, we will consider chemical systems where an autocatalytic network is coupled with external species acting as fuel and waste materials, serving to produce autocatalysts in excess and allow for mass-conservation in the system.
These assumptions are captured by the topology such networks, encoded in their stoichiometric matrices, which follow the block decomposition given in Eq. (
3

Then, in Section
I
, by relying solely on the topology of such networks, we derive their nonequilibrium behavior.
The main result of this section is the decomposition of the chemical fluxes in Eq. (
28

As the analysis is solely based on stoichiometry, this decomposition is independent of the chemical fluxes.
In addition, we show that the physical role of the chemical species exchanged with the environment is fully captured by the topology and depends whether or not they break a conservation law.
Particularly, we demonstrate that, even though both the productive modes and the cycles of the autocatalytic network require influx of external species, they are in fact two drastically different processes.
We will illustrate the main ideas of this section on the network associated with glucose metabolism and its regulation (glycolysis and gluconeogenesis).

In Section
II

Specifically, in this section we derive the nonequilibrium thermodynamical potential of autocatalytic networks.
It is obtained from the semigrand Gibbs free-energy by adding an extra term similar to the potential energy associated with conservative forces in Newtonian mechanics.
The latter represents the conservative forces that the fuel and waste species exert on the reactions when they are converted into an excess of autocatalysts; it is expressed using a gauge transform of the chemical potentials.
The main results of this section are then the expression of the thermodynamical potential in Eq. (
55

60

Finally, we focus on autonomous networks where the cost of sustaining the productive modes and the cycles takes a simple form.

Setup and notations

Autocatalytic CRNs

We consider an autocatalytic network composed of reacting chemical species that change through chemical reactions.
We refer to the chemical species in the autocatalytic network as the
autocatalytic species

\mathcal{Z}

Similarly, the reactions in the autocatalytic network are called the

The net change of species

Such a pathway consists of a linear combination of the autocatalytic reaction,

contains only strictly positive components:

(1)

component-wise.

For simplicity, we will assume in the main text that the rows of

This stronger condition is sufficient (yet not necessary) to satisfy the stoichiometric condition in Eq. (

We relax this assumption in the appendices to address the most general framework of Eq. (

Networks of elementary reactions

In Section

In doing so, for this section, we will further assume that the network is composed of elementary reactions.
In such networks, whenever a reaction exists, its backward counterpart must also exists from microscopic reversibility.

I
Non-equilibrium autocatalytic networks

I.1
Conservation laws

I.1.1
Definition

Conservation laws of a CRN are the row vectors
\boldsymbol{\ell}
that belong to the left nullspace of the full stoichiometric matrix
[
40
,
25
,
27
]
,

\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(11)

Indeed, when the CRN is closed,
L=\boldsymbol{\ell}\cdot[\boldsymbol{s}]
is a conserved quantity,
i.e.

\mathrm{d}_{t}L=0
.
Among the conservation laws, those that have only positive entries,
\ell_{s}\geq 0
for all species
s
, are called
mass-like
conservation laws.
Lavoisier’s law of mass conservation implies that the subset of mass-like conservation laws is never empty.
In addition, there always exists a conservation law such that
\ell_{s}>0
for all species
s
, expressing the conservation of the total mass.
While conservation laws are generally difficult to interpret, moieties form a subset of mass-like conservation laws that can be understood as the molecular fragments exchanged between species during chemical reactions
[
41
]
.
For instance, in biochemical networks, common moieties include carbon conservation and phosphate group conservation (see also Fig.
1
a
).

I.1.2
Moiety matrix

As a result, our stricter condition for autocatalysis Eq. (
2
) amounts to the absence of conservation law in the sub-network.
The absence of conservation laws in
\mathbb{S}
implies that they must be brought by the external species
\mathcal{E}
to ensure mass conservation in the closed system (
\boldsymbol{I}=\boldsymbol{0}
).
We can consider a linear basis of the conservation laws that we index by
i
,
\left\{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{i}\right\}
, .
We then define the matrix
\mathbb{L}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\ell}^{i}\right\}
, where the
i
-th row represents the conservation law
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{i}
.
As a result,
\mathbb{L}
is a full-rank matrix satisfying
\mathbb{L}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Consequently, all the mass-like conservation laws of
\boldsymbol{\nabla}
are either a row of
\mathbb{L}
or can be obtained as a linear combination of the rows of
\mathbb{L}
.

From the block decomposition Eq. (
3
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}+\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot\mathbb{S}=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(12)

Now, as the rows of
\mathbb{L}
are linearly independent, there exists a subset of external species
\mathcal{E}_{p}\subset\mathcal{E}
such that the restriction of
\mathbb{L}
to this subset,
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}
, is (square) non-singular.
Additionally, we denote
\mathcal{E}_{f}\subset\mathcal{E}
as the remaining external species:
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{p}\cup\mathcal{E}_{f}
.
Splitting Eq. (
12
) on these new subsets yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}_{p}}_{\mathcal{R}}+\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}_{f}}_{\mathcal{R}}+\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot\mathbb{S}=\boldsymbol{0},

(13)

and similarly for the additional reactions
\backslash\mathcal{R}
.
Then, from the non-singularity of
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}_{p}}_{\mathcal{R}}=-\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}_{f}}_{\mathcal{R}}-\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot\mathbb{S},

(14)

where we introduce the
moiety matrix
of species
\mathcal{E}_{p}

[
27
]
:

\mathbb{M}=\left(\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{L}=\left(\mathbbm{1}_{|\mathcal{E}_{p}|}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{Z}}\right),

(15)

in which
\mathbbm{1}_{|\mathcal{E}_{p}|}
stands for the
|\mathcal{E}_{p}|\times|\mathcal{E}_{p}|
identity matrix.
The moiety matrix is the row-reduced form of
\mathbb{L}
, which defines another linear basis for the conservation laws:
\mathbb{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Thus, with Eq. (
15
), it becomes explicit that each conservation law (
i.e.
each row of
\mathbb{L}
) is associated with a single species in
\mathcal{E}_{p}
such that setting a non-vanishing external flux on species
e_{p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
(
I^{e_{p}}\neq 0
) breaks its associated conservation law in
\mathbb{L}
.
When the
i
-th row of
\mathbb{L}
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}^{i}
, corresponds to a moiety, its associated species in
\mathcal{E}_{p}
represents the exchanged fragment, and the non-vanishing entries in the
i
-th row of
\mathbb{M}
indicate where this fragment appears in the other species.
However, for conservation laws that cannot be interpreted as an exchanged fragment, the non-vanishing entries in
\mathbb{M}
generalize the notion of a moiety.

I.1.3
Broken conservation laws

Once all external species are coupled to the environment, their physical roles depend on whether they belong to
\mathcal{E}_{p}
or to
\mathcal{E}_{f}
.
Each species in
\mathcal{E}_{p}
breaks its associated conservation law, while species in
\mathcal{E}_{f}
induce flows through the system, effectively connecting external species.
As a result, species
\mathcal{E}_{p}
remove constraints from the system, while species
\mathcal{E}_{f}
enforce non-vanishing fluxes through the reactions.
From this, we can anticipate that if only potential species are present, the system cannot sustain non-vanishing fluxes, which prevents the network from settling into a non-equilibrium steady-state (NESS).

Consequently, species
\mathcal{E}_{p}
are referred to as the (external)
potential
species; imposing a non-vanishing flux on all species in
\mathcal{E}_{p}
breaks the conservation laws in
\mathbb{L}
.
Conversely, species
\mathcal{E}_{f}
are referred to as external
force
species: once coupled to the environment, they are capable of breaking detailed balance, thereby maintaining non-vanishing fluxes through the reactions
[
27
,
42
]
.
Since the sub-network lacks conservation laws, coupling all external species to the environment results in breaking of all conservation laws of the full network.
As a consequence, the matrix
\mathbb{L}
directly provides the broken conservation laws of the open network, which play a pivotal role in the thermodynamics of the open system.

Remark

When the autocatalytic sub-network follows the most general criterion Eq. (
1
) instead of Eq. (

More specifically, the autocatalytic sub-network still possesses conservation laws that are non mass-like,

all of its conservation laws have entries of opposite sign.
In that case, there might also be broken conservation laws in the sub-network originating from the autocatalytic species that are externally controlled.
When this arises, one should split the subset of externally controlled autocatalytic species

into potential species
\mathcal{Y}_{p}
, breaking the conservation laws of the sub-network, and force species
\mathcal{Y}_{f}
.

I.1.4
Elementary modes of production

From the stricter condition for autocatalysis in Eq. (
2
), the rows of
\mathbb{S}
are linearly independent, thus
\mathbb{S}
admits a (non-unique) right-inverse
\mathbb{G}
:

\mathbb{S}\cdot\mathbb{G}=\mathbbm{1}_{|\mathcal{Z}|}.

(16)

The column associated with species
z\in\mathcal{Z}
in
\mathbb{G}
is denoted
\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
. It defines a pathway that produces an excess of one unit of species
z
, leaving the other autocatalytic species unaffected.
In other words,
\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
defines an
elementary mode of production
of species
z
, and, subsequently, the columns of
\mathbb{G}
are the productive modes of the autocatalytic sub-network.
Considering the sum of all the elementary modes,
\boldsymbol{u}=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
, defines a pathway that produces an excess of one unit of
all
the autocatalytic species:

\mathbb{S}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{1}>0.

(17)

As result, our stricter condition for autocatalysis is indeed sufficient to ensure that the stoichiometric criterion in Eq. (
1
) holds.
Finally, as
\mathbb{S}
is integer-valued, the productive modes can be always be rescaled,
\mathbb{G}\to n\mathbb{G}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
, so that all the elementary modes are also integer-valued.
Doing so, the (rescaled) elementary modes produce an excess of
n
units of each autocatalytic species.

Remark

When the autocatalytic sub-network follows the most general criterion of Eq. (
1
), the presence of (non mass-like) conservation laws prevents the definition of an elementary mode for each of the autocatalytic species.
Instead, only a subset
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
of autocatalytic species will be paired with elementary modes, and the production of the other autocatalytic species (
\mathcal{Z}_{p}=\mathcal{Z}-\mathcal{Z}_{f}
) will depend on the production of species in
\mathcal{Z}_{f}
.

Cycles of the full CRN are the column vectors in the right nullspace of the full stoichiometric matrix:

, or
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\mathcal{R}}

\mathbb{S}
.
From the rank nullity theorem, there are
|\ker\penalty\ \mathbb{S}\ |=|\mathcal{R}|-|\mathcal{Z}|
independent cycles in the sub-network.
In what follows, we let
\left\{\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}\right\}_{1\leq\varepsilon\leq|\mathcal{R}|-|\mathcal{Z}|}
be a linear basis of these cycles.
As we did for the conservation laws, we define the matrix

\mathbb{C}=\left\{\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}\right\}_{1\leq\varepsilon\leq|\mathcal{R}|-|\mathcal{Z}|},

whose
\varepsilon

\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
.
Subsequently, the matrix
\mathbb{C}
is full-rank and verifies
\mathbb{S}\cdot\mathbb{C}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

There exists a subset
\mathcal{R}_{1}\subset\mathcal{R}
of autocatalytic reactions such that the restriction
\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
is (square) non-singular, while
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}_{1}
stores the remaining autocatalytic reactions.
By construction,

while
|\mathcal{R}_{2}|=|\mathcal{R}|-|\mathcal{Z}|=|\ker\penalty\ \mathbb{S}\ |
hence, each reaction in the subset
\mathcal{R}_{2}
can be paired with one cycle of the sub-network such that, removing one reaction in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
from the network causes precisely one cycle of
\mathbb{S}
to disappear.
Physically, removing one reaction that belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{2}
removes exactly one cycle from the sub-network (see also Fig.

).
Subsequently, if ones removes all the reactions in
\mathcal{R}_{2}

Finally, the sub-matrix

As the columns of
\mathbb{C}
are linearly independent, this relation implies that
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}

I.2.2
Emergent cycles

We now examine the condition upon which a cycle of
\mathbb{S}
is also a cycle when one considers the external species.
Indeed, a cycle of the sub-network will consume and/or produce external species.
However, when a cycle of the sub-network preserves also the external species one has:

\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathcal{R}_{1}}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(22)

Furthermore, we can use Eq. (
21
) to replace
\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
such that Eq. (
22
) becomes:

\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{c}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(23)

In the left-hand side we recognize the Schur complement that we will denote

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}.

(24)

As a result, the right nullspace of the Schur complement,
\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
, contains all of the cycles of the sub-network that also preserve the external species, and there is
|\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}|
independent such cycles.
When
all
the cycles of
\mathbb{S}
preserve also the external species,

\ker\penalty\ \mathbb{S}=\ker\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}},

(25)

one has
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
From the non-singularity of
\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}
, this occurs if, and only if, the Schur complement vanishes,
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

In contrast, the cycles of the sub-network that are not also cycles of the full-network consume external species without production of autocatalytic species.
In the CRN literature, they are labeled
emergent cycles
as they exist only once all the external species are externally controlled.
In metabolic networks, emergent cycles are also referred to as
futile cycles
because they are useless for producing metabolites but are energy consuming (see next section).

I.3
Dynamics

I.3.1
Linear basis of the elementary fluxes

The right inverse
\mathbb{G}
and the matrix
\mathbb{C}
describe complementary effects for the sub-network: the columns of
\mathbb{G}
are productive modes of the autocatalytic sub-network.
In contrast, the columns of
\mathbb{C}
are pathways preserving the state of the sub-network.
To reflect this, we seek for productive modes that never proceed along the reactions in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
by imposing
\mathbb{G}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
This constraint removes the non-uniqueness of
\mathbb{G}
as the only right-inverse fulfilling this property is

\mathbb{G}=\quad\begin{pNiceArray}.\vskip 5.0pt

(26)

On top of that, we notice that the matrix
\left(\mathbb{G},\,\mathbb{C}\right)
has a non-vanishing determinant:

\begin{vNiceArray}=\dfrac{\mathrm{det}\left[\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\right]}{\mathrm{det}\left[\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right]}\neq 0.

(27)

Hence, the productive modes drawn from
\mathbb{G}
in Eq. (
26
) and the cyclic pathways in
\mathbb{C}
define a linear basis of the flux space.

I.3.2
Decomposition of the fluxes

The chemical fluxes of the autocatalytic reactions,
\boldsymbol{j}
, can be decomposed on the linear basis defined by the columns of
\left(\mathbb{G},\,\mathbb{C}\right)
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}+\mathbb{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varepsilon}J^{\varepsilon}(t)\,\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}+\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{J}^{z}(t)\,\boldsymbol{g}_{z},

(29)

where
J^{\varepsilon}(t)
stands for the macroscopic flux along the cycle
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{J}^{z}(t)
is the macroscopic flux along the elementary mode
\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
.
Furthermore, the constraint on the right inverse implies that the restriction of the fluxes on the subset of reactions
\mathcal{R}_{2}
stems uniquely from the cycles of the sub-network:

\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=\sum_{\varepsilon}J^{\varepsilon}(t)\,\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}.

(30)

Hence, the macroscopic fluxes along the cycles of
\mathbb{S}
are completely determined by the elementary fluxes of reactions in
\mathcal{R}_{2}
:

\boldsymbol{J}=\left(\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}.

(31)

In contrast, the macroscopic fluxes in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
represent the rate at which the autocatalytic species are produced or consumed in the sub-network.
Precisely, if the macroscopic flux associated with
z\in\mathcal{Z}
is positive (resp. negative),
\mathcal{J}^{z}>0
(resp.
\mathcal{J}^{z}<0
), the concentration of species
z
increases (resp. decreases) in the sub-network as its elementary mode,
\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
, is performed forward (resp. backward).

Unlike the macroscopic fluxes along the productive modes,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
, cannot be solely expressed as a function of
\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
.
Indeed, even though the fluxes along the cycles of
\mathbb{S}
can be expressed solely with
\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}

via
Eq. (
30
), part of
\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
is also due to the cycles.
Specifically, from Eq. (
21
),

-\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=-\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}

(32)

is the contribution of the cycles in
\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
.
As a consequence,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}
is obtained by removing this contribution:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}=\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}^{\mathcal{R}_{1}}+\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=\mathbb{S}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}.

(33)

I.3.3
Influx of external species

Injecting the decomposition of the fluxes into the kinetic rate equations of the autocatalytic species provides:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}[\boldsymbol{z}]=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}+\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}}.

(34)

Similarly, by injecting the decomposition of the fluxes
into the rates equations of the external species we obtain:

\mathrm{d}_{t}[\boldsymbol{e}]=\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\penalty\ +\penalty\ \boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(35)

The first term in RHS corresponds to the influx of external species needed to produce (or consume) autocatalytic species using the productive modes, using Eq. (
34
) it can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}=\mathrm{d}_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{e}(\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}}.

(36)

Hence the uptake of external species needed for producing an excess
\Delta\boldsymbol{z}=[\boldsymbol{z}](t)-[\boldsymbol{z}](0)
of autocatalytic species is

\Delta\boldsymbol{e}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{z},

(37)

which depends only on the excess produced.
In contrast, the second term in the RHS of Eq. (
35
) quantifies the uptake of external species along the cycles of the sub-network.
Using Eq. (
21
), it can be written with the Schur complement:

\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}.

(38)

Because the cycles of the sub-network that are also cycle of the full network belong to the right-nullspace of the Schur complement, these cycles do not contribute in Eq. (
38
) where only the emergent/futile cycles remain.
In particular, if
\ker\penalty\ \mathbb{S}=\ker\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}
, all the cycles of the sub-network are also preserving the external species and, in that case, the Schur complement vanishes, and so it is for Eq. (
38
).
Finally, the last two terms in Eq. (
35
) are, respectively, the change of external species along the additional reactions (
\backslash\mathcal{R}
) and the fluxes coupling the external species with the environment.

Hence, Eq. (
36
) highlights that the uptake of external species along the productive modes derived from an exact time derivative.
As a result, it will be independent of the specific pathway taken by the autocatalytic species in the sub-network.
Hence, reminiscent of Newtonian mechanics, we will refer to the influx of external species fueling the production of autocatalytic species as the
conservative
influx.
As we shall see in the next section, this analogy can be pushed further as the chemical forces driving this process can be derived from a potential.
The latter will be essential to study the thermodynamics of an autocatalytic sub-network.
On the contrary, the uptake of external species along the cycles of the sub-network cannot be written as an exact time derivative, and, as a result, they will bring non-conservative forces.

I.3.4
Steady fluxes

From Eq. (
6
), steady-state in the open sub-network is achieved when the concentrations of the autocatalytic species that are not externally coupled,
i.e.
the
\mathcal{X}
species, become constant in time.
This situation occurs when the fluxes verify
\overline{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \mathbb{S}{}^{\,\mathcal{X}}

[
29
,
44
]
.
Clearly, all the cycles of
\mathbb{S}
remain cycles for
\mathbb{S}{}^{\,\mathcal{X}}
and, then, the columns of
\mathbb{C}
defines a family of linearly independent vector which generates a subset of
\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \mathbb{S}{}^{\,\mathcal{X}}
.
Additionally, the elementary modes of the species
\mathcal{Y}
let each of the
\mathcal{X}
species unaffected by definition.
As a result, the columns of
\mathbb{G}_{\mathcal{Y}}
also generate subset of
\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \mathbb{S}{}^{\,\mathcal{X}}
and are linearly independent from the columns of
\mathbb{C}
.
Finally, the rank-nullity theorem implies that
\left(\mathbb{C},\ \mathbb{G}_{\mathcal{Y}}\right)
is a linear basis of
\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \mathbb{S}{}^{\,\mathcal{X}}
.
Then, at steady-state, the decomposition of the fluxes becomes:

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{J}}+\mathbb{G}_{\mathcal{Y}}\cdot\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}}{}^{\,\mathcal{Y}}.

(39)

Removing or adding intermediates species by the means of one-to-one reactions (fueled with an arbitrary number external species as reactants and products) is an important class of coarse-graining that preserves the decomposition of the fluxes.
This procedure is, for example, used to derive the Michaelis-Menten kinetics or the Hill kinetics from elementary steps
[
45
]
.
Aside from adding/removing linear pathways, more advanced reductions preserving steady-state have been recently studied
[
46
]
.
Finally, this decomposition serves as the starting point to establish a circuit theory of open chemical networks, drawing an analogy between CRNs and electronic circuits
[
47
,
48
]
.

I.3.5
Physical role of the species

As a direct consequence of Eq. (
39
), when only the external species are externally controlled (and
\mathcal{Y}=\varnothing
), the steady-fluxes align with the cycles of the sub-network.
Among these cycles, the emergent cycles are the ones consuming the external species and contributing to Eq. (
38
).
As a result, maintaining fluxes along the emergent cycles require to exert non-conservative forces on the sub-network.
Additionally, when only the external species are controlled, only the external force species
\mathcal{E}_{f}
bring such forces in the network.
Hence, we can relate the number of external force species
|\mathcal{E}_{f}|
to the number of emergent cycles in the sub-network (see Appendix C for the proof),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}_{f}|

\displaystyle=|\backslash\mathcal{R}|+|\mathcal{R}_{2}|-|\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}|,

(40)

from which the number of conservation laws brought by the external species follows:

|\mathcal{E}_{p}|=|\mathcal{E}|-|\mathcal{E}_{f}|.

(41)

In other words, Eq. (
40
) states that the physical role of the external force species is (i) to maintain all the additional reactions away from equilibrium and (ii) to sustain non-vanishing fluxes along the

|\mathcal{R}_{2}|-|\mathrm{ker}\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}|

emergent cycles of the sub-network.

Exerting non vanishing external fluxes on the
\mathcal{Y}
species caused
|\mathcal{Y}|
new cycles to exist for the remaining
\mathcal{X}
species.
These are all
emergent
as they do not exist prior to controlling the
\mathcal{Y}
species.
On the other hand, as they do not break any conservation law, the
\mathcal{Y}
species are ”force” species that maintain the flux along their respective elementary mode.
Indeed, to sustain steady production (or consumption) of the autocatalytic species
y
, the latter should be coupled with the environment by the means of a non-vanishing external flux,
I^{y}\neq 0
, such that
\mathcal{J}^{y}(t)\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447pt}\overline{\mathcal{J}}{}^{\,y}=-I^{\,y}
.
Finally, the macroscopic fluxes along the
\mathcal{X}
species tend to vanish as NESS settles in the sub-network,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}{}^{\mathcal{X}}(t)\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{0}
.
Nevertheless, during the transient dynamics, they take non-zero values, and serve as the relaxation modes of the sub-network needed to reach steady-state
[
30
]
.

II
Thermodynamics of the autocatalytic sub-network

II.1
Fundamental non-conservative forces

The total entropy production rate (EPR) quantifies the amount of energy dissipated by the reactions in the system.
It can be expressed as the contribution of the reactions of the sub-network (
\mathcal{R}
) and the additional reactions (
\backslash\mathcal{R}
):

\dot{\Sigma}=\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}+\dot{\Sigma}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}\geq 0.

Starting from the chemical master equation that governs the stochastic trajectories in the concentration space, and taking the large volume limit, ends up in the following form for the dissipation of the dissipation along the reactions of the sub-network in the deterministic regime
[
38
]
:

T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal{R}}(j^{+\rho}-j^{-\rho})\ \ln\left(\dfrac{j^{+\rho}}{j^{-\rho}}\right),

(44)

expressed in units where
RT=1
.
In the dissipation, the log-ratio of the unidirectional fluxes define the chemical forces acting on the reactions,
i.e.
the affinities

\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\equiv\ln\left(\dfrac{j^{+\rho}}{j^{-\rho}}\right)=-\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)_{\rho}=-\Delta G_{\rho},

(45)

which correspond to the opposite of the Gibbs free-energy differences of the reactions.
Using Eq. (
14
) allow us to replace the stoichiometric matrix of the external potential species,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}_{p}}_{\mathcal{R}}
, in Eq. (
45
).
By doing so, we introduce the following gauge transform of the chemical potentials:

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}\cdot\mathbb{M},

(46)

such that the affinities of the reactions can be written as

\mathcal{A}_{\rho}=-\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)_{\rho}.

(47)

As a result, when
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, all the affinities vanish implying that the sub-network have reached detailed balance.
Hence, the entries in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
are the
fundamental non-conservative forces

[
27
,
40
]
associated with each of the chemical species.
As anticipated, by definition, these forces necessarily vanish for the potential species:
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Finally, the dissipation along the autocatalytic reactions can be written as a function of the non-conservative forces of the species:

T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}.

(48)

Similarly, the dissipation along the additional reactions reads

T\dot{\Sigma}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\backslash\mathcal{R}}^{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}.

(49)

II.2
Unconditionally detailed balanced sub-networks

From the decomposition of steady-fluxes Eq. (
39
), when autocatalytic species are exchanged with the environment (
\mathcal{Y}\neq\varnothing
),
macroscopic currents flow along the productive modes of species
\mathcal{Y}
at steady-state, and reaching a detailed balance (equilibrium) in the sub-network is impossible.
In contrast, when
\mathcal{Y}=\varnothing
, the sub-network might still be able to reach equilibrium for arbitrary values of the chemical potentials of external species
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}
.
When this occurs, the sub-network is said to be unconditionally detailed balanced
[
27
]
and the chemical potentials of the free autocatalytic species
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Z}}
, are able to balance arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\text{eq}}\cdot\mathbb{S}=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}.

(50)

As
\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}
is non-singular, the unique candidate for
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\text{eq}}
is

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\text{eq}}=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1}.

(51)

Plugging this solution in the equations for reactions
\mathcal{R}_{2}
provides the feasibility of this candidate, namely:

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}.

(52)

In light of the previous chapter, unconditional detailed balance is achieved if, and only if, the Schur complement vanishes,
i.e.
when

\ker\penalty\ \mathbb{S}=\ker\penalty\ \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}.

Importantly, this condition is independent of the presence of non-conservative forces brought by the external force species,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}_{f}}
, and relies solely on topology.
This is expected from Eq. (
40
) because, when the Schur complement vanishes, species
\mathcal{E}_{f}
serve only to maintain fluxes along the additional reactions
\backslash\mathcal{R}
.

II.3
Thermodynamical potential

The Gibbs free-energy of the closed system (
\boldsymbol{I}=\boldsymbol{0}
) is well-known:

G(\boldsymbol{z},\ \boldsymbol{e})=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}}[e]\left(\mu_{e}-1\right)+\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}[z]\left(\mu_{z}-1\right),

(53)

where the ”
-1
” is the contribution of the solvent.
By setting off the non-vanishing external fluxes on the external species and on species
\mathcal{Y}
, the system starts exchanging moieties with its environment:

\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})=\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot[\boldsymbol{e}]+\mathbb{M}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot[\boldsymbol{z}].

(54)

Hence, in the presence of non-vanishing external fluxes,

\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})=G(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{e}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(55)

corresponds to the thermodynamical potential of the open sub-network which captures its thermodynamics behavior.
The first two terms in the definition of
\mathcal{G}
correspond to the semigrand free energy of an open CRN whcih was derived previously for stochastic
[
40
]
or deterministic open chemical networks
[
25
,
27
,
42
]
.
Yet, the semigrand free-energy fails to predict relaxation towards equilibrium for unconditionally detailed balance sub-networks in the presence of additional reactions.
To fix this, we need to take into account that the non-conservative forces of the external species do not exert along the productive modes of the sub-network because the influx of external species along the productive modes of the sub-network is conservative.
This is implemented by the last therm in Eq. (
55
),
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{e}
.

II.4
Decomposition of the EPR

The time evolution of
\mathcal{G}
can be written as:

\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{G}=-T\dot{\Sigma}-\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{e}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{E}}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{e}\right)+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}}.

(56)

Gathering the terms in the RHS of Eq. (
56
) as follows:

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{driv}}=-\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{e},

(57)

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{E}}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\Delta\boldsymbol{e}\right),

(58)

\displaystyle\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}},

(59)

allows to write the change in free-energy in a thermodynamically appealing way

\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{G}=-T\dot{\Sigma}+\dot{W}_{\text{driv}}+\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{E}}+\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}}.

(60)

In this decomposition,
\dot{W}_{\text{driv}}
represents the driving work rate required to change the equilibrium state in the autocatalytic sub-network by varying the chemical potential of the potential species.
Because the equilibrium state of the sub-network depends on all the external species, the external force species also contribute to the driving work.
The two non-conservative work rates,
\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{E}}
and
\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\penalty\ \mathcal{Z}}
describe the power performed by species
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{Z}
tho maintain to maintain non-vanishing fluxes.
Hopefully, the potential species does not contribute to
\dot{W}_{\text{nc},\ \mathcal{E}}
.

II.5
Autonomous autocatalytic networks

When the driving rate vanishes,

\dot{W}_{\text{driv}}=0,

(61)

the sub-network is said to be autonomous.
This is implemented by fixing the chemical potentials of the external species to a constant value in the system through external particle reservoirs (chemostats).
Autonomy can also be achieved if there exists a timescale separation between the dynamics of the external species and the one of the autocatalytic sub-network such that, in the time scale of the sub-network, the chemical potentials of the external species can be regarded as constant.
In that case, the external fluxes on the external species
\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{E}}
should balance the consumption/production of external species by the chemical reactions in Eq. (
35
).

II.5.1
Thermodynamic cost of production

For autonomous sub-networks, the decomposition of the EPR can be written as:

\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{G}=-T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}+\dot{W}_{\text{cyc}}+\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}.

(62)

In this decomposition,

\dot{W}_{\text{cyc}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\boldsymbol{J},

(63)

accounts for the power needed to sustain the emergent/futile cycles of
\mathbb{S}
.
Furthermore, the total work rate sustaining the cycles can be further decomposed on each cycle
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
of the sub-network.
Indeed, as from Eq. (
47
)
-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
, the
\varepsilon
-th entry in

-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}\cdot\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{C}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\cdot\mathbb{C}

is
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]=-\Delta G[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]
, the overall affinity associated with cycle
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
which is the opposite of its free-energy difference.
As a result, the power maintaining the cycles can be written as

\dot{W}_{\text{cyc}}=\sum_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]\ J^{\varepsilon}=-\sum_{\varepsilon}\Delta G[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]\ J^{\varepsilon},

(64)

such that the
\varepsilon
-th summand corresponds to the specific power allocated to cycle
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
, which vanishes if
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
is also a cycle of the full network because its affinity vanishes in that case.
Consequently, only the emergent/futile cycles will require energy to be maintained while producing no net excess of autocatalytic species.

In addition,

\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}})^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{Z}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}}

(65)

represents the power needed to sustain the production of the autocatalytic species
\mathcal{Z}
along their elementary modes.
Its first term,

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}})^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{\mathcal{Z}},

corresponds to the uptake of external species fueling the elementary modes of production.
As the fluxes decomposition suggested, only the species
\mathcal{Y}
contribute to
\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}
:

\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{g}_{y}]\ I^{y}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Delta G[\boldsymbol{g}_{y}]\ I^{y}

(66)

where

\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{g}_{y}]=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{y}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}^{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{y}-\mathcal{F}_{y}

(67)

is the overall affinity along the elementary mode of species
y
,
\boldsymbol{g}_{y}
, which is the opposite of its Gibbs free-energy difference
\Delta G[\boldsymbol{g}_{y}]
.
Similarly to the cycles, the
y
-th summand in Eq. (
66
) is the specific work rate dedicated to the production of species
y
.

II.5.2
Unconditionally detailed balanced sub-networks

From the EPR decomposition in Eq. (
62
), it is clear that whenever
\dot{W}_{\text{cyc}}=0
and
\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}=0
, the
\mathcal{G}
tends converges to its minimum
\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{G}=-T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}\leq 0
.
These conditions are equivalent to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\,\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=\varnothing.

When this occurs, the sub-network is unconditionally detailed balance.
Furthermore, when these two conditions are fulfilled, the optimum of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to autocatalytic species
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}
is attained when

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{\text{eq}}=-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1},

(68)

which is in accordance with what we found by directly balancing the chemical potentials in Eq. (
51
).
Additionally, the equilibrium state is the global minimum of
\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{z},\,\boldsymbol{e})-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{eq}},\,\boldsymbol{e})

(69)

\displaystyle=\,\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}[z]\log\left(\dfrac{[z]}{[z]^{\text{eq}}}\right)-\left([z]-[z]^{\text{eq}}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{z}\|\boldsymbol{z}^{\text{eq}}\right)\geq 0,

where we recognized the relative entropy between two non-normalized distribution
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{a}\|\boldsymbol{b}\right)=\sum_{i}a_{i}\log(a_{i}/b_{i})-(a_{i}-b_{i})
.
The latter is positive and vanishes if, and only if, the two distributions are the same
[
50
]
.
Hence, the equilibrium state is the global minimum of
\mathcal{G}
, and the convexity of
\mathcal{G}
derives from the convexity of the relative entropy.

II.5.3
Hill-Schnakenberg decomposition

By injecting the decomposition of the elementary fluxes in the EPR,
T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\boldsymbol{j}
, we recover the Hill-Schnakenberg decomposition of the EPR
[
24
,
22
]
:

\displaystyle T\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbb{C}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}

(70)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]\ J^{\varepsilon}+\sum_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{g}_{z}]\ \mathcal{J}^{z}

\displaystyle=T\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{cyc}}\penalty\ +\penalty\ T\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{prod}}

where, as before,
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{g}_{z}]
denotes the overall affinity along the elementary mode
\boldsymbol{g}_{z}
, and
\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]
the overall affinity along the cycle
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
(which vanishes if
\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}
is also cycle for the external species).
Importantly, the dissipation along the cycles of the sub-network,
T\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{cyc}}
, corresponds to
\dot{W}_{\text{cyc}}
in Eq. (
64
).
Subsequently, the power maintaining the emergent/futile cycles of the sub-network ends up dissipated.
On the other hand, the dissipation along the productive modes,
T\dot{\Sigma}_{\text{prod}}
, differs from
\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}
, allowing for work extraction and free-energy change in the sub-network.
Finally, at steady-state,

T\overline{\dot{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{R}}=\sum_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{c}_{\varepsilon}]\ \overline{J}{}^{\,\varepsilon}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{A}[\boldsymbol{g}_{y}]\ \overline{\mathcal{J}}{}^{\,y}.

(71)

Because at steady-state, the production of the autocatalytic species must be balanced by the external fluxes,
\overline{\mathcal{J}}{}^{\,y}=-I^{y}
, one recovers that once NESS is settled, all the power ends up dissipated,

T\overline{\dot{\Sigma}}_{\mathcal{R}}=\overline{\dot{W}}_{\text{cyc}}+\overline{\dot{W}}_{\text{prod}},

(72)

and the free energy is constant in time
\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{G}=0
.

Conclusions

In this work, we have studied the nonequilibrium behavior of autocatalytic networks by relying on the topological features of these systems.
One of the key results of this study is that, while the condition for autocatalysis is topological, it allows for an analysis of the dynamics of autocatalytic networks.
Hence, building on recent advancements in the stoichiometric analysis of autocatalysis, we have developed a framework to describe their dynamics without requiring steady-state conditions or specific reaction kinetics.

By exploiting the hypergraph representation of an autocatalytic networks, we derived a general decomposition of chemical fluxes that is both broad and robust, revealing fundamental insights into their operation far from equilibrium.
Furthermore, the decomposition of chemical fluxes into productive modes and cycles, based solely on the network’s stoichiometric matrix, highlighted two distinct types of behavior: productive fluxes that generate autocatalysts in excess, and cyclic fluxes that preserve the internal state of the network.
This separation, dictated purely by topology, emphasizes the powerful relationship between structure and dynamics in these systems.
In addition, the structure of these networks, particularly through the examination of conservation laws, is sufficient to infer the physical roles played by different chemical species.

A significant aspect of our analysis is the role of external species coupled with the autocatalytic networks.
Indeed, stoichiometric autocatalysis, by definition, violates mass conservation, meaning that the system requires external species, which function as fuel or waste materials, enabling the autocatalytic network to operate.
In the context of biology, these species should be constantly provided to the autocatalytic network and, in addition, external mechanisms must replenish fuel species from the excess of waste.
Even though maintaining these external species bring non-conservative forces, we demonstrated that these forces do not apply on the productive modes of the autocatalytic networks.
This was absent in previous analysis of open chemical networks, in which the non-conservative forces are considered homogeneously among the reactions.
As a result, the condition for unconditional detailed balance in autocatalytic networks is independent on the non-conservative forces brought by the external species.

The thermodynamic properties of autocatalytic networks also exhibit distinct characteristics.
From the nonequilibrium geometry of autocatalytic networks, we derived a correction that must be applied to the semigrand Gibbs free-energy to accurately describe the thermodynamics of autocatalytic networks.
This correction arises to take into account the underlying conservative process of external species sustaining the production of autocatalysts.
From the EPR decomposition of the thermodynamical potential, and the decomposition of the fluxes, we were able to distinguish the cost sustaining the futile cycles of autocatalytic networks from the cost sustaining the production of autocatalysts.
While the former ends up dissipated, the later is able to generate useful work that can be extracted or change the free-energy content in the autocatalytic network.

In summary, our results underscore the central role of topology in governing both the dynamics and thermodynamics of autocatalytic networks.
By linking conservation laws, flux decomposition, and thermodynamical potentials, we have provided a comprehensive framework for understanding these systems in out-of-equilibrium conditions, with implications for both theoretical studies and practical applications in biological and chemical contexts.
Nevertheless, in the metabolism, autocatalysis generally occurs inside a compartment.
In that case, maintaining fluxes along the productive modes will be
induce
a priori
volume growth (or shrink).
In this work, we have neglected this effect, assuming constant volume.
In this regard, Sughiyama
et al.
have recently proposed an innovative framework addressing the chemical thermodynamics of growing system in Ref.
[
46
]
.
However, it was restricted to non-singular stoichiometric matrix
\mathbb{S}
, we hope that the results derived here will help to extend their results in the general case, leaving the extension of this framework for future studies.

Appendix B: Thermodynamics of the sub-network in the general case

B1
Thermodynamical potential

In the general case, the moieties exchanged with environment are

\boldsymbol{m}(\boldsymbol{e},\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot[\boldsymbol{e}]+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{M}}_{\mathcal{Z}}\cdot[\mathcal{Z}].

(B1)

In addition, the fundamental non-conservative forces are

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{M}}

(B2)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p}\equiv(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}},\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Y}_{p}})
represents the chemical potentials of all the potential species
p=\mathcal{E}_{p}\cup\mathcal{Y}_{p}
.
As before, these forces vanish for the potential species:
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{Y}_{p}}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Finally, the thermodynamical potential is given by Eq. (
55

\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})=G(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{e}(\boldsymbol{z}_{f}).

Note that, now, the conservative influx of external species,
\Delta\boldsymbol{e}

\mathcal{Z}_{f}

\Delta\boldsymbol{e}(\boldsymbol{z}_{f})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}}\cdot\mathbb{G}\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol{z}_{f}.

B2
Autonomous sub-networks

When the driving work rate vanishes,
\dot{W}_{\text{driv}}=0

\dot{W}_{\text{prod}}=-\sum_{y_{f}\in\mathcal{Y}_{f}}\Delta G[\boldsymbol{g}_{y_{f}}]\ I^{y_{f}}.

(B3)

As a result, when

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}{}^{\,\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{2}}=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{f}=\varnothing,

\mathcal{G}
relaxes to its minimum.

\mathcal{G}

\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{X}_{f}}^{\text{eq}}=-\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{E}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}+(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{Y}_{p}},\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}^{\text{eq}})\cdot\mathbb{T}\right)\cdot\left(\mathbb{S}_{\mathcal{R}_{1}}\right)^{-1},\\[8.00003pt]
