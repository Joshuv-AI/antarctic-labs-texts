Abstract
. This work extends the idea of matched pairs presented by Majid in [ 11 ] and Takeuchi in [ 16 ] for the context of weak bialgebras and weak Hopf algebras. We introduce, also inspired by partial matched pairs [ 3 ] , the notion of weak matched pairs and establish conditions for a subspace of the smash product be a weak bialgebra/Hopf algebra. Further, some new examples of (co)actions of weak bialgebras over algebras and some results about integral elements are presented.



1.
Introduction

A matched pair of Hopf algebras was introduced in the literature by Singer, and under hypothesis of commutativity and cocommutativity, a new Hopf algebra was obtained
[
15
]
.
Later, Takeuchi also dealt with matched pairs and generalized the previous construction, and obtained a new Hopf algebra which was called bismash product
[
16
]
.
After these works, matched pairs became an interesting way to construct new algebraic structures. For instance, Majid presented several constructions and results that generated new structures of bialgebras/Hopf algebras, by replacing the commutativity and cocommutativity requested by Takeuchi for others compatibility conditions, and described a huge class of noncommutative and noncocommutative Hopf algebras, which have been called bicrossed product of Hopf algebras (for instance, see
[
11
]
).
Since then, the idea of matched pairs was generalized and carry on to several contexts, such as Lie algebras, groupoids and others (for instance, see
[
1
,
2
,
12
,
13
,
17
]
).

In this work, we investigate the construction of weak Hopf algebras through matched pairs.
It is folkloric that (global) actions and coactions of weak Hopf algebras resemble partial actions and coactions of (usual) Hopf algebras
[
4
]
, so that much of we do in this paper is inspired by
partial matched pairs
, particularly in
[
3
]
.

The present work is divided as follows. In Section 2, the concepts of weak bialgebras and weak Hopf algebras plus some useful properties, actions and coactions, and some examples, will be briefly recalled.
Such examples will appear throughout the work.
In Section 3, we introduce the definition of
weak matched pair
as a pair
(H,A)
of two weak bialgebras plus some compatibility conditions (see Definition
3.2
).
It generalizes the classical matched pairs defined by Majid and by Takeuchi (the latter when the bialgebra is a Hopf algebra).
But, different from there, in the weak scenario the product smash
A\#H
is not a (unital) algebra or a (counital) coalgebra.
Then, in Section 4, we deal with this product smash and investigate conditions in the weak matched pair to provide a structure of weak bialgebra/Hopf algebra in a subspace
A\underline{\overline{\#}}H
of
A\#H
.
At last, in Section 5, we present some weak Hopf algebras generated by weak matched pairs, and some results about integral elements.

Throughout, algebraic structures (vector spaces, algebras…) will be all considered over a fixed field
\Bbbk
. Unadorned
\otimes
means
\otimes_{\Bbbk}
.

3.
Weak matched pair

In this section we introduce the definition of a matched pair of weak bialgebras.
It generalizes the classical definitions of matched pairs of bialgebras and Hopf algebras
[

]

Definition 3.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

A pair of bialgebra
(H,A)
is called a

(i)

A
is a left
H
-module algebra with action given by

(ii)

H
is a right

-comodule coalgebra with structure
\rho:H\longrightarrow H\otimes A

\rho(h)=h^{0}\otimes h^{1}

(iii)

\rho(hg)=\rho(h_{1})(g^{0}\otimes(h_{2}\triangleright g^{1}))

(iv)

\Delta(h\triangleright x)=({h_{1}}^{0}\triangleright x_{1})\otimes({h_{1}}^{1}(h_{2}\triangleright x_{2}))

(v)

\varepsilon(h\triangleright x)=\varepsilon(h)\varepsilon(x)
,

(vi)

\rho(1_{H})=1_{H}\otimes 1_{A}
,

for all

is called

{h_{3}}^{0}g^{0}\otimes({h_{1}}^{0}\cdot x_{1})\otimes{h_{1}}^{1}(h_{2}\cdot x_{2}){h_{3}}^{1}(h_{4}\cdot g^{1}),

4.
The weak Hopf algebra
A\underline{\overline{\#}}H

In this section, we analyze under what conditions it is possible to construct a weak bialgebra/Hopf algebra involving the usual smash product and coproduct.

4.1.
The structure of weak bialgebra

Consider
H
and
A
two weak bialgebras.
First, if
A
is a
H
-module algebra, then we can define
A\#H
as
A\otimes H
with the product

(x\#h)(y\#g)=x(h_{1}\cdot y)\#h_{2}g,

for all
x,y\in A
and
h,g\in H
.
It is straightforward to check the associativity of this product.
However, the natural candidate for unity of this product, the element
1_{A}\#1_{H}
, is neither right nor left unity in
A\#H
.
Then, we need to consider a smaller space of it.
Since
(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})=(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})
, for all
(x\#h)\in A\#H
, we denote

A\underline{\#}H=(A\#H)(1_{A}\#1_{H})=\{x(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}\ |\ x\in A,h\in H\}

and
x\underline{\#}h=(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})=x(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}\in A\underline{\#}H
. The next result ensure that
A\underline{\#}H
is an algebra, with unity
1_{A}\underline{\#}1_{H}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
H
and
A
be weak bialgebras, and
A
an
H
-module algebra. Then,
A\underline{\#}H
is an algebra.

Proof.

First we show that
(x\underline{\#}h)(y\underline{\#}g)\in A\underline{\#}H
.
Indeed:

\displaystyle(x\underline{\#}h)(y\underline{\#}g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x({h}_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#{h}_{2})(y({g}_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#{g}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x({h}_{1}\cdot 1_{A})({h}_{2}\cdot y(g_{1}\cdot 1_{A}))\#h_{3}{g}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x({h}_{1}\cdot y)({h}_{2}g_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{3}{g}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x({h}_{1}\cdot y)\#h_{2}g)(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x({h}_{1}\cdot y)\underline{\#}h_{2}g.

Now, for all
(x\#h)\in A\#H
,
(1_{A}\#1_{H})(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)=(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)
and
(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})(1_{A}\#1_{H})=(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})
.
Thus,
1_{A}\underline{\#}1_{H}
is the unity in
A\underline{\#}H
:

\displaystyle(1_{A}\underline{\#}1_{H})(x\underline{\#}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1_{A}\#1_{H})(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\underline{\#}h,

and also

\displaystyle(x\underline{\#}h)(1_{A}\underline{\#}1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})(1_{A}\#1_{H})(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\underline{\#}h.

Therefore,
A\underline{\#}H
is an algebra.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

If
H
is a weak bialgebra cocommutative, then
(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})=(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h)
, for all
(x\#h)\in A\#H
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(x\#h)(1_{A}\#1_{H})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1_{H}\cdot(x(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A}))\#h_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{4.7})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)(\varepsilon_{t}({1_{H}}_{2})h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)(\varepsilon_{t}(\varepsilon_{t}({1_{H}}_{2})h_{1})\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)(\varepsilon_{t}({1_{H}}_{2})\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)({1_{H}}_{2}\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H\ coco}}{{=}}

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)({1_{H}}_{2}\varepsilon_{t}(h_{2})\cdot 1_{A})\#h_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{4.12})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)({1_{H}}_{2}{1_{H}^{\prime}}_{2}\cdot 1_{A})\#{1_{H}^{\prime}}_{1}h

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle H\ coco}}{{=}}

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)({1_{H}}_{2}{1_{H}^{\prime}}_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\#{1_{H}^{\prime}}_{2}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)({1_{H}}_{2}\cdot 1_{A})\#{1_{H}}_{3}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({1_{H}}_{1}\cdot x)\#{1_{H}}_{2}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1_{A}\#1_{H})(x\#h).

Now, if
H
an
A
-comodule coalgebra, we also define
A\#H
as
A\otimes H
with the coproduct

\displaystyle\Delta:A\#H

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle A\#H\otimes A\#H

\displaystyle x\#h

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\Delta(x\#h)=x_{1}\#{h_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}\#h_{2}

and
\varepsilon:A\#H\longrightarrow\Bbbk
given by
\varepsilon(x\#h)=\varepsilon(x)\varepsilon(h),
for all
x\in A
and
h\in H
.
It is straightforward to check that
\Delta
is coassociative, however
\varepsilon
is neither right nor left counit.
Therefore, we denote
\overline{x\#h}=(id\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta(x\#h)=x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}h^{1})\#h^{0}
and consider the subspace

\overline{A\#H}=(id\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta(A\#H)=\{x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}h^{1})\#h^{0}\ |\ x\in A,h\in H\}.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
H
and
A
be weak bialgebras, and
H
an
A
-comodule coalgebra. Then,
\overline{A\#H}
is a coalgebra with coproduct
\Delta
and counit

.

Proof.

Initially, we show that
\Delta(\overline{x\#h})\in\overline{A\#H}\otimes\overline{A\#H}
. Indeed:

\displaystyle\Delta(\overline{x\#h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}h^{1})\#h^{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{3}h^{1})\#{{h^{0}}_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{{h^{0}}_{1}}^{1}\#{{h^{0}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{3}{h_{1}}^{1}{h_{2}}^{1})\#{{h}_{1}}^{00}\otimes x_{2}{{h}_{1}}^{01}\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{3}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{2}{h_{2}}^{1})\#{{h}_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{1}\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{3}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{2})\varepsilon(x_{4}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{3}{h_{2}}^{1})\#{{h}_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{1}\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{1})\#{{h}_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{3}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{2}\varepsilon(x_{4}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{3}{h_{2}}^{1})\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x_{1}\#{h_{1}}^{0}}\otimes\overline{x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}\#{h_{2}}}.

Now, we verify that
\varepsilon
is a counit:

\displaystyle(\varepsilon\otimes id)\Delta(\overline{x\#h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\overline{x_{1}\#{h_{1}}^{0}})\overline{x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}\#{h_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x_{1}{h_{1}}^{01})\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{00})\overline{x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}\#{h_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x_{1}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{1})\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0})\overline{x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{2}\#{h_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0})\overline{x{{h_{1}}^{1}}\#{h_{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0})x_{1}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}_{2}{{h_{2}}^{1}})\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0})x_{1}\varepsilon_{s}({{h_{1}}^{1}})_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}\varepsilon_{s}({{h_{1}}^{1}})_{2}{{h_{2}}^{1}})\#{{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}{1_{A}}_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}\varepsilon_{s}({{h_{1}}^{1}}){1_{A}}_{2}{{h_{2}}^{1}})\#\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0}){{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}{1_{A}}_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}{1_{A}}_{2}\varepsilon_{s}({{h_{1}}^{1}}){{h_{2}}^{1}})\#\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0}){{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}}{{h_{2}}^{1}})\#\varepsilon({h_{1}}^{0}){{h_{2}}^{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\varepsilon(x_{2}{{h_{1}}^{1}})\#\varepsilon({{h}^{0}}_{1}){{h}^{0}}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x\#h},

and also

\displaystyle(id\otimes\varepsilon)\Delta(\overline{x\#h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x_{1}\#{h_{1}}^{0}}\varepsilon(\overline{x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}\#{h_{2}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x_{1}\#{h_{1}}^{0}}\varepsilon(x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}{h_{2}}^{1})\varepsilon({h_{2}}^{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x_{1}\#{h^{0}}_{1}}\varepsilon(x_{2}{h^{1}})\varepsilon({h^{0}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x_{1}\#{h^{0}}}\varepsilon(x_{2}{h^{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\#{h}^{00}\varepsilon(x_{2}{h^{01}})\varepsilon(x_{3}{h^{1}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}\#{h}^{0}\varepsilon(x_{2}{h^{1}}_{1})\varepsilon(x_{3}{h^{1}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{x\#h}.

(\overline{A\#H},\Delta,\varepsilon)
is a coalgebra.
∎

\overline{\overline{x\#h}}=\overline{x\#h}
, for all

, and so
\overline{\overline{A\#H}}=\overline{A\#H}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(H,A)
be an abelian weak matched pair.
Then,
\Delta
is multiplicative in
A\#H
.

Proof.

For all
(x\#h),(y\#g)
in
A\#H
,

\displaystyle\Delta((x\#h)(y\#g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta(x(h_{1}\cdot y)\#h_{2}g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}{(h_{1}\cdot y)}_{1}\#{(h_{2}g_{1})}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{(h_{1}\cdot y)}_{2}{(h_{2}g_{1})}^{1}\#h_{3}g_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{troca}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle x_{1}({h_{1}}^{0}\cdot y_{1})\#{h_{3}}^{0}{g_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}(h_{2}\cdot y_{2}){h_{3}}^{1}(h_{4}\cdot{g_{1}}^{1})\#h_{5}g_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}({h_{1}}^{0}\cdot y_{1})\#{h_{2}}^{0}{g_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}{h_{2}}^{1}(h_{3}\cdot y_{2})(h_{4}\cdot{g_{1}}^{1})\#h_{5}g_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x_{1}({{h_{1}}^{0}}_{1}\cdot y_{1})\#{{h_{1}}^{0}}_{2}{g_{1}}^{0}\otimes x_{2}{h_{1}}^{1}(h_{2}\cdot y_{2})(h_{3}\cdot{g_{1}}^{1})\#h_{4}g_{2}

\displaystyle\Delta(x\#h)\Delta(y\#g).

∎

Now, in order to obtain a structure of weak bialgebra in a subspace of

needs to satisfy some compatible conditions.

Definition 4.5
.

An abelian weak matched pair
(H,A)
is called a
compatible weak matched pair
if, for all

,

(i)

h\cdot 1_{A}=h_{1}\cdot 1_{A1}\varepsilon(h_{2}\cdot 1_{A2})
,

(ii)

h\cdot 1_{A1}\otimes g\cdot 1_{A2}=(h\cdot 1_{A})1_{A1}\otimes(g\cdot 1_{A})1_{A2}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(H,A)
be a compatible weak matched pair. Then, for all
h\in H
,

(i)

h\cdot 1_{A}=(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\varepsilon_{s}(h_{2}\cdot 1_{A})
,

(ii)

\varepsilon_{s}(h\cdot 1_{A})=\varepsilon_{s}(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\varepsilon_{s}(h_{2}\cdot 1_{A})
,

(iii)

h\cdot 1_{A1}\otimes 1_{A2}=(h\cdot 1_{A})1_{A1}\otimes 1_{A2}
,

(iv)

h\cdot 1_{A}=(h\cdot 1_{A1})\varepsilon_{s}(1_{A2})
.

Proof.

We verify each item, as follows.

\underline{(i):}
By items (i) and (ii) of Definition
4.5
, and the definition of
\varepsilon_{s}
,

\displaystyle h\cdot 1_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot 1_{A1}\varepsilon(h_{2}\cdot 1_{A2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})1_{A1}\varepsilon((h_{2}\cdot 1_{A})1_{A2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot 1_{A})\varepsilon_{s}(h_{2}\cdot 1_{A}).

\underline{(ii):}
Just apply
\varepsilon_{s}
on the both sides of the equality in item
(i)
of Definition
4.5
, and use equality (
9
).

\underline{(iii):}

\displaystyle h\cdot 1_{A1}\otimes 1_{A2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\cdot 1_{A1}\otimes 1_{H}\cdot 1_{A2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h\cdot 1_{A})1_{A1}\otimes(1_{H}\cdot 1_{A})1_{A2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h\cdot 1)1_{A1}\otimes 1_{A2}.

\underline{(iv):}
Straightforward from item (ii) of Definition
4.5
.
∎

From now on, we always consider
(H,A)
a compatible weak matched pair.

Proposition 4.7
.

The coproduct
\Delta
is coassociative in
A\underline{\#}H
.

Proof.

Since
A\#H
is coassociative, to prove the coassociativity of
A\underline{\#}H
is enough to show that the coproduct
\Delta
satisfies
\Delta(A\underline{\#}H)\subseteq A\underline{\#}H\otimes A\underline{\#}H
.
Let
x\underline{\#}h\in A\underline{\#}H
,

\displaystyle\Delta(x\underline{\#}h)

\displaystyle=

5.
Examples and properties

In this section we present some examples of weak Hopf algebras
A\underline{\overline{\#}}H
as well as some properties about the theory of integrals.

First, recall that a compatible weak matched pair
(H,A)
gives rise to a weak bialgebra
A\underline{\overline{\#}}H
(see Definitions
3.2
and
4.5
, and Theorem
4.14
).
Furthermore, if
H
and
A
are Hopf algebras and some conditions hold, then
A\underline{\overline{\#}}H
can be endowed with a weak Hopf algebra structure (see Theorem
4.16
).

So, in the following, we show that the examples of weak matched pairs presented in Section
3
are, in fact, compatible matched pairs.
Moreover, since all the weak bialgebras we are dealing with are weak Hopf algebras, we also verify that compatibility conditions for the antipode maps hold, obtaining weak Hopf algebras.

Example 5.1
.

Consider the abelian weak matched pair
(\mathcal{H}^{G},\mathcal{H}^{G})
of Example
3.6
.
Then
\mathcal{H}^{G}\underline{\overline{\#}}\mathcal{H}^{G}
is a weak Hopf algebra.
Moreover, it is not a (usual) Hopf algebra.

Indeed, the two items of Definition
4.5
are clear, then
(\mathcal{H}^{G},\mathcal{H}^{G})
is a compatible matched pair and so
\mathcal{H}^{G}\underline{\overline{\#}}\mathcal{H}^{G}
is a weak bialgebra.
Also, for
h\in\mathcal{H}^{G}
, is a routine computation to conclude that both sides of equality (
21
) are reduced to
\frac{1}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{g,a}hga\otimes g^{-1}\otimes a^{-1}
, and similarly both sides of equality (
22
) are
\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g}hg\otimes g^{-1}.

Hence, by Theorem
4.16
we conclude that
\mathcal{H}^{G}\underline{\overline{\#}}\mathcal{H}^{G}
is a weak Hopf algebra.
Moreover, since
x\underline{\overline{\#}}h=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}xhg\#g^{-1}
, we get on the one hand

\Delta(1\underline{\overline{\#}}1)=1_{1}\underline{\overline{\#}}1_{1}^{\prime}\otimes 1_{2}1_{2}^{\prime}\underline{\overline{\#}}1_{3}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{|G|^{3}}\sum_{g,a,b\in G}ga\#a^{-1}\otimes g^{-1}b\#b^{-1}

and, on the other hand,

1\underline{\overline{\#}}1\otimes 1\underline{\overline{\#}}1=\frac{1}{|G|^{2}}\sum_{g,h\in G}g\#g^{-1}\otimes h\#h^{-1}.

Then,
\Delta(1\underline{\overline{\#}}1)\neq 1\underline{\overline{\#}}1\otimes 1\underline{\overline{\#}}1
and so this weak Hopf algebra is not a (usual) Hopf algebra.
In fact, it can be concluded that
\mathcal{H}^{G}\underline{\overline{\#}}\mathcal{H}^{G}\simeq\mathcal{H}^{\frac{G\times G}{N}}
, where
N=\{(g,g^{-1}),\ |g\ \in G\}.

Finally, let us recall the concept of integrals for weak Hopf algebras and the weak version of the well known Maschke Theorem (see
[
5
]
).

Definition 5.4
.

Consider
H
a weak Hopf algebra. A left (resp. right) integral in
H
is an element
\alpha\in H
such that
h\alpha=\varepsilon_{t}(h)\alpha
(resp.
\alpha h=\alpha\varepsilon_{s}(h))
, for all
h\in H
. We denote
\int_{\ell}^{H}
(resp.
\int_{r}^{H}
) the space of left (resp. right) integrals in
H
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
H
be a finite weak Hopf algebra, then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

H
is semisimple,

(ii)

H
is separable,

(iii)

there exists
\alpha\in\int_{\ell}^{H}
such that
\varepsilon_{t}(\alpha)=1_{H}
.

The following examples and properties were inspired in Hopf algebra theory, in particular, in what was developed in
[
3
]
, regarding partial matched pair.

Example 5.6
.

Consider
\mathcal{H}^{G}
the weak Hopf algebra of Example
2.7
. Since
\varepsilon_{t}(g)=\varepsilon_{s}(g)=g
, for all
g\in G
, we get
\int_{\ell}^{\mathcal{H}^{G}}=\int_{r}^{\mathcal{H}^{G}}=\mathcal{H}^{G}
and so
dim\left(\int_{\ell}^{\mathcal{H}^{G}}\right)=dim\ \mathcal{H}^{G}=|G|
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{H}^{G}
is a semisimple weak Hopf algebra, since

\varepsilon_{t}(1_{\mathcal{H}^{G}})=1_{\mathcal{H}^{G}}
.

Example 5.7
.

Let
H=\mathring{\cup}_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}
be a finite disjoint union of Hopf algebras.
Since, for
h\in H_{i}
,
\varepsilon_{t}(h)=\varepsilon_{i}(h)1_{H_{i}}
, we have
that
\alpha\in H
is a left integral in
H
if and only if
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
, where
\alpha_{i}\in\int_{\ell}^{H_{i}}
.
Moreover

\int_{\ell}^{H}=\Bbbk\left(\mathring{\cup}_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\ell}^{H_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\ell}^{H_{i}}
.
Furthermore,
H
is semisimple if and only if
H_{i}
is semisimple, for some
1\leq i\leq n
.

Example 5.8
.

Let
H
be a Hopf algebra and consider
H^{\prime}
the weak Hopf algebra of the Example
2.9
.
Since,
\varepsilon_{t}(h)=\varepsilon(h)\mathbb{e}
, for all
h\in H
, we conclude
\int_{\ell}^{H^{\prime}}=\Bbbk(\mathbb{1}-\mathbb{e})+\int_{\ell}^{H}
.
Therefore,
H^{\prime}
is semisimple if and only if
H
semisimple.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
(H,A)
be a weak matched pair.
If
\alpha\in\int_{\ell}^{A}
, then
h\cdot\alpha\in\int_{\ell}^{A}
, for all
h\in H
.

Proof.

Consider
\alpha\in\int_{\ell}^{A}
and

, then

\displaystyle x(h\cdot\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})h_{2}\cdot\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot(h_{2}\cdot\alpha))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{lema_2_1_9_Glauber}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle x(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot 1_{A})(h_{2}\cdot\alpha)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{lema_2_1_9_Glauber}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot x)(h_{2}\cdot\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{2})\cdot x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(h_{2}S(h_{3})\cdot x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(h_{2}\cdot(S(h_{3})\cdot x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot((S(h_{2})\cdot x)\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot(\varepsilon_{t}(S(h_{2})\cdot x)\alpha)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{vart_acao}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot(\varepsilon_{t}(S(h_{2})\cdot 1_{A})\varepsilon_{t}(x)\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot(\varepsilon_{t}(S(h_{2})\cdot 1_{A})\varepsilon_{t}(\varepsilon_{t}(x))\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot(\varepsilon_{t}(S(h_{2})\cdot\varepsilon_{t}(x))\alpha)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{def_integral}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle h_{1}\cdot((S(h_{2})\cdot\varepsilon_{t}(x))\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(h_{2}S(h_{3})\cdot\varepsilon_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{2})\cdot\varepsilon_{t}(x))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ref{lema_2_1_9_Glauber}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t}(x)(h_{1}\cdot\alpha)(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{2})\cdot 1_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t}(x)(\varepsilon_{t}(h_{1})\cdot(h_{2}\cdot\alpha))
