Accelerated nonlinear primal-dual hybrid gradient methods with applications to supervised machine learning
Jérôme Darbon jerome_darbon@brown.edu, Gabriel P. Langlois gabriel_provencher_langlois@brown.edu

Abstract
The linear primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) method is a first-order method that splits convex optimization problems with saddle-point structure into smaller subproblems. Unlike those obtained in most splitting methods, these subproblems can generally be solved efficiently because they involve simple operations such as matrix-vector multiplications or proximal mappings that are fast to evaluate numerically. This advantage comes at the price that the linear PDHG method requires precise stepsize parameters for the problem at hand to achieve an optimal convergence rate. Unfortunately, these stepsize parameters are often prohibitively expensive to compute for large-scale optimization problems, such as those in machine learning. This issue makes the otherwise simple linear PDHG method unsuitable for such problems, and it is also shared by most first-order optimization methods as well. To address this issue, we introduce accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods that achieve an optimal convergence rate with stepsize parameters that are simple and efficient to compute. We prove rigorous convergence results, including results for strongly convex or smooth problems posed on infinite-dimensional reflexive Banach spaces. We illustrate the efficiency of our methods on ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -constrained logistic regression and entropy-regularized matrix games. Our numerical experiments show that the nonlinear PDHG methods are considerably faster than competing methods.

Related work

The linear PDHG method was introduced at around the same time by
Pock et al. 2009
and
Esser et al. 2010

Since then, many variants and extensions of the linear PDHG method have been proposed; see

]
, operator, forward-backward, and proximal-gradient splitting

,

,

, and nonlinear

[

]
. Here, we focus on nonlinear PDHG methods.

The extension of the linear PDHG method to the nonlinear setting was first done, to our knowledge, by
Hohage and Homann 2018
to solve non-smooth convex optimization problems posed on Banach spaces. A nonlinear PDHG method for solving such problems using nonlinear proximity operators based on Bregman divergences was later proposed by

Their scheme is not fully nonlinear, however, as it requires one of the Bregman divergence to be a quadratic function. Moreover, their work did not provide accelerated nonlinear schemes for smooth convex problems or smooth and strongly convex problems.

Our contributions

This paper contributes accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods that achieve an optimal rate of convergence in the Nesterov class of optimal first-order methods with stepsize parameters that are simple and efficient to compute. To do so, we extend the theory of accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods initiated in
[
16
]
to solve optimization problems on Banach spaces with nonlinear proximity operators based on Bregman divergences. The main theoretical results and accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods are described in Section
4
. We prove rigorous convergence results, including results strongly convex or smooth problems posed on infinite-dimensional reflexive Banach spaces. In addition, we provide in Section
5
practical implementations of accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods for
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression and zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization, and we perform numerical experiments on these problems in Section
6
to compare the running times of nonlinear PDHG methods to other commonly-used first-order optimization methods. Our numerical experiments show that the nonlinear PDHG methods are considerably faster than competing methods.

The results we present are generally applicable to convex-concave saddle-point optimization problems posed on real reflexive Banach spaces. Setup

4
Accelerated nonlinear primal-dual hybrid gradient methods

In this section, we describe accelerated nonlinear PDHG method (
10
) that are suitable when the functions
g
and
h^{*}
in the saddle-point problem (
4
) have additional structure beyond that stated in assumptions (A1)-(A5). Specifically, we assume either one or both of these statements:

(A6)

There is a positive number
\gamma_{g}
such that the function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto g(\boldsymbol{x})-\gamma_{g}\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex.

(A7)

There is a positive number
\gamma_{h^{*}}
such that the function
\boldsymbol{y^{*}}\mapsto h^{*}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}})-\gamma_{h^{*}}\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}})
is convex.

With assumptions (A1)-(A7), the descent rule (
9
) in Lemma
2.1
is improved: For every
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})\in\mathrm{dom~}g\times\mathrm{dom~}h^{*}
, the output
(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*})
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\tau}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)-\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{g}\tau}{\tau}\right)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})

(16)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*})\right)-\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{h^{*}}\sigma}{\sigma}\right)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*})

\displaystyle+\left\langle\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{*},\bm{A}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\rangle.

The proof of inequality (
16
) is nearly identical to the proof of inequality (
9
), with the only difference that we use the stronger inequality (
58
) (see Fact
A.8
(iii)) on each line of the iteration scheme (
8
) to get

g(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})-g(\boldsymbol{x})\leqslant\frac{1}{\tau}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})\right)-\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{g}\tau}{\tau}\right)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})+\left\langle\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{\tilde{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle.

and

h^{*}(\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*})-h^{*}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}})\leqslant\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*}))\right)-\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{h^{*}}\sigma}{\sigma}\right)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}},\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*})-\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}^{*},\bm{A}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle.

Inequality (
16
) then follows from these two inequalities and the same steps used to prove the descent rule (
9
) in Lemma
2.1
.

Remark 4.1
.

Assumptions (A1)-(A7) imply that
g
is
\gamma_{g}
-strongly convex over
\mathrm{dom~}g\cap\mathrm{dom~}\phi_{\mathcal{X}}
and
h^{*}
is
\gamma_{h^{*}}
-strongly convex over
\mathrm{dom~}h^{*}\cap\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}
. For example,

g(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{\gamma_{g}}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}=\left(g(\boldsymbol{x})-\gamma_{g}\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)+\gamma_{g}\left(\phi_{\mathcal{X}}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\right)

for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}g\cap\mathrm{dom~}\phi_{\mathcal{X}}
, and as the set
\mathrm{dom~}g\cap\mathrm{dom~}\phi_{\mathcal{X}}
is convex and the right hand side is the sum of two convex functions, the left hand side is also convex.

Remark 4.2
.

In light of Remark
4.1
and Fact
A.5
, if assumptions (A1) and (A6) hold, then the primal problem (
5
) has a unique solution. Likewise, if assumptions (A1) and (A7) hold, then the dual problem (
6
) has a unique solution. Finally, if assumptions (A1) and (A6)-(A7) hold, then the Lagrangian (
7
) has a unique saddle point.

The additional terms in (
16
) allow us to create accelerated methods with better convergence rate than the
O(1/K)
rate for estimate (
14
). The first accelerated method, which we describe in Section
4.1
, has a sublinear
O(1/K^{2})
convergence rate and is applicable if assumption (A6) hold. A variant of the first accelerated method, which we describe in Section
4.2
, has a sublinear
O(1/K^{2})
convergence rate and is applicable if assumption (A7) hold. The second accelerated method, which we describe in Section
4.3
, has a linear convergence rate and is applicable if both assumptions (A6) and (A7) hold. We also present another variant of this method in Section
4.4
.

5.1
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression

\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression is a supervised learning algorithm for classification and to identify important features in data sets. Concretely, suppose we receive
m
independent samples
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, each comprising a
d
-dimensional vector of features
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
and a label
b_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
. The
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression problem is then

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\left\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\right\|_{1}\leqslant\lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\left(1+e^{-b_{i}\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{i},\boldsymbol{v}\right\rangle}\right)

(35)

where
\lambda>0
is a tuning parameter. The constraint on the
\ell_{1}
norm regularizes the logistic model; it promotes solutions to have a number of entries that are identically zero
[
34
,
27
,
77
]
. The non-zero entries are identified as the important features, and the zero entries are discarded. The number of non-zero entries itself depends on the value of the tuning parameter
\lambda
.

To derive an appropriate accelerated nonlinear PDHG algorithm for
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression, we will express problem (
35
) as an minimization problem over the unit simplex
\Delta_{2d}
. We can do so because every polytope, including the
\ell_{1}
-ball, can be represented as a convex hull of its vertices in barycentric coordinates
[
38
,
46
]
. Here this means for every
\boldsymbol{v}
inside the
\ell_{1}
-ball of radius
\lambda
, there exists a point
\boldsymbol{x}
in the unit simplex
\Delta_{2d}
for which

\boldsymbol{v}=\lambda(\bm{I}_{d\times d}\mid-\bm{I}_{d\times d})\boldsymbol{x},

(36)

where
(\bm{I}_{d\times d}\mid-\bm{I}_{d\times d})
denotes the horizontal concatenation of the identity matrices
\bm{I}_{d\times d}
and
-\bm{I}_{d\times d}
.

We now apply the change of variables (
36
) to problem (
35
). Let
\bm{B}
denote the
m\times d
matrix
\bm{B}
whose rows are the elements
-b_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
, let
\bm{A}=\lambda(\bm{B}\mid-\bm{B})
, and let
n=2d
. Then problem (
35
) becomes equivalent to

\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\left(1+e^{[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}]_{i}}\right).

(37)

This is the primal problem of interest. Its associated convex-concave saddle-point problem is

\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle-\psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})\right\}

(38)

where
\psi\colon[0,1/m]^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the average negative sum of
m
binary entropy terms,

\psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=\begin{cases}&\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\log\left(m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\right)+(1-m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i})\log\left(1-m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\right)\quad\text{if}\,\boldsymbol{s}\in[0,1/m]^{m},\\
&+\infty,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(39)

The dual problem is

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{\mathrm{vecmax}{(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{*})}-\psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})\right\}

(40)

where
\mathrm{vecmax}{(\boldsymbol{y})}=\max{([\boldsymbol{y}]_{1},\dots,[\boldsymbol{y}]_{m})}
for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Due to the strong concavity of the dual problem (
40
), the convex-concave saddle-point problem (
38
) has at least one saddle point
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{s})\in\Delta_{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
is a global solution to the primal problem (
37
) and
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}
is the unique solution to the dual problem (
40
). They satisfy the optimality conditions

\boldsymbol{x}_{s}\in\partial\mathrm{vecmax}{(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})}\quad\text{and}\quad[\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}]_{i}=\frac{1}{m+me^{-[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}}}\;\text{for}\;i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

(41)

The solution
\boldsymbol{v}_{s}
of the original problem (
35
) follows from
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
and the change of variables formula (
36
). In addition, the first optimality condition in (
41
) can be used to identify the zero entries of
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
as follows
[
68
]
: Let
J(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})
denote the set of indices
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
\mathrm{vecmax}{(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})}=[-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}]_{j}
. Then
[\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{j}=0
whenever
j\not\in J(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})
.

Accelerated nonlinear PDHG method

We propose to solve the
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression problem (
35
) through (
37
) and (
36
) using the accelerated nonlinear PDHG method (
25
) with the following choice of norms and Bregman functions:

\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{1},\quad\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{2},\quad\phi_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{H}_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=\frac{1}{4m}\psi,

where
\mathcal{H}_{n}\colon\Delta_{n}\to(-\infty,0]
denotes the negative entropy function,

\mathcal{H}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}\log([\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}).

The negative entropy function induces the Bregman divergence
D_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}\colon\Delta_{n}\times\mathrm{int}{~\Delta_{n}}\to[0,+\infty)
given by

D_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}\log\left([\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}/[\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}]_{j}\right).

This Bregman divergence is the so-called Kullback–Leibler divergence or relative entropy. The Bregman function
\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}
is, up to a factor of
1/4m
, the average negative sum of
m
binary entropy terms (
39
). It induces the Bregman divergence
D_{\psi/4m}\colon[0,1/m]^{m}\times(0,1/m)^{m}\to[0,+\infty)
given by

D_{\psi/4m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*})=\frac{1}{4m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\log\left(\frac{[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}}{[\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*}]_{i}}\right)+(1-m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i})\log\left(\frac{1-m[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}}{1-m[\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*}]_{i}}\right).

With these choices, assumptions (A1)-(A5) and (A7) hold with
\gamma_{h^{*}}=4m
. In particular, assumption (A5) holds because
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is
1
-strongly convex with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm over the unit simplex
\Delta_{n}
. This fact is a direct consequence of a fundamental result in information theory known as Pinsker’s inequality
[
5
,
22
,
47
,
51
,
61
]
. Moreover, the induced operator norm is the maximum
\ell_{2}
norm of the columns of
\bm{A}
, i.e.,

\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{1,2}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{1}=1}\left\|{\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{2}=\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{ij}^{2}}.

For this algorithm, we set the initial stepsize parameters to be
\theta_{0}=0
,
\tau_{0}>0
and
\sigma_{0}=1/(\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{1,2}^{2}\tau_{0})
. Given
\boldsymbol{x}_{-1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathrm{int}{~\Delta_{n}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{*}\in(0,1/m)^{m}
, the corresponding accelerated nonlinear PDHG algorithm for problem (
37
) consists of the iterations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{-\psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\theta_{k}[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}])\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\sigma_{k}}D_{\psi/4m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k})\right\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\left\{\left\langle\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\tau_{k}}D_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right\}

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=1/\sqrt{1+4m\sigma_{k}},\quad\tau_{k+1}=\tau_{k}/\theta_{k+1},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{k+1}=\theta_{k+1}\sigma_{k}.

The updates
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k+1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
can be both computed explicitly. For the first update, define the auxiliary variable

[\boldsymbol{w}_{k}]_{i}=\log\left(m[\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i}/(1-m[\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i})\right)\;\text{for}\;i\in\{1,\dots m\}.

Then we can update
\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}
in two steps:

\boldsymbol{w}_{k+1}=\left(4m\sigma_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+4m\sigma_{k}\theta_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}\right)+\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)/(1+4m\sigma_{k})

and

[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k+1}]_{i}=\frac{1}{m+me^{-[\boldsymbol{w}_{k+1}]_{i}}}\quad\mathrm{for}\,i\in\left\{1,\dots,m\right\}.

For the second update, a straightforward calculation gives

[\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}]_{j}=\frac{[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}]_{j}e^{-\tau_{k}[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}]_{j}e^{-\tau_{k}[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}}

for
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Hence the iterations are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left(4m\sigma_{k}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+4m\sigma_{k}\theta_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}\right)+\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\right)/(1+4m\sigma_{k})

(42)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{i}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k+1}]_{i}=\frac{1}{m+me^{-[\boldsymbol{w}_{k+1}]_{i}}}\quad\text{for}\;i\in\left\{1,\dots,m\right\}

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}]_{j}e^{-\tau_{k}[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{x}_{k}]_{j}e^{-\tau_{k}[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}}\quad\text{for}\;j\in\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=1/\sqrt{1+4m\sigma_{k}},\quad\tau_{k+1}=\tau_{k}/\theta_{k+1},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{k+1}=\theta_{k+1}\sigma_{k}.

All parameter calculations and updates can be performed in
O(mn)
operations. According to Prop.
4.2
and the optimality conditions (
38
), we have the strong limits

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\to+\infty}[\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i}=\frac{1}{m+me^{-[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}}}\;\text{for}\;i\in\{1,\dots,m\}.

5.2
Zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization

Two-player zero-sum matrix games are a class of saddle-point optimization problems that model one of the basic forms of constrained competitive games
[
12
]
. We focus here on zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization, the latter which models the imperfect knowledge of the payoff matrix
\bm{A}
by the two players
[
54
]
. Let
\Delta_{m}
and
\Delta_{n}
denote the unit simplices on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
\bm{A}
denote an
m\times n
matrix, called the payoff matrix. Zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization are formulated as follow:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\max_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\Delta_{m}}\left\{\lambda\mathcal{H}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})\right\},

(43)

where
\lambda>0
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}\log([\boldsymbol{x}]_{j})
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\log([\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i})
denote the negative entropies of the probability distributions
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
.

The primal and dual problems associated to the entropy regularized zero-sum matrix game (
43
) are given by

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\left\{\lambda\mathcal{H}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}]_{i}/\lambda}\right)\right\}

(44)

and

\max_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\Delta_{n}}\left\{-\lambda\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{j}/\lambda}\right)-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})\right\}

(45)

Due to the strong convexity of the primal problem (
44
) and strong concavity of the dual problem (
45
), the saddle-point problem (
43
) has a unique saddle point
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, which are also the unique solutions to the primal and dual problems above. They satisfy the optimality conditions

-[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}]_{j}=\lambda(1+\log([\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{j}))\quad\text{and}\quad[\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}]_{i}=\frac{e^{[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}/\lambda}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{[\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}/\lambda}}.

(46)

Accelerated nonlinear PDHG method

We propose to solve the zero-sum matrix game with entropy regularization (
43
) using the accelerated PDHG method (
34
) with the following choice of norms and Bregman functions:

\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{1},\quad\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}\implies\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{1},\quad\phi_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{H}_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=\mathcal{H}_{m}.

The Bregman divergences induced by
\phi_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}
are the Kullback–Leibler divergences

D_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}\log\left([\boldsymbol{x}]_{j}/[\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}]_{j}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad D_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}[\boldsymbol{y}^{*}]_{i}\log\left([\boldsymbol{y^{*}}]_{i}/[\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*}]_{i}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{int}{~\Delta_{n}}
,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\Delta_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\bar{y}}^{*}\in\mathrm{int}{~\Delta_{m}}
. With these choices, assumptions (A1)-(A7) hold with the strong convexity parameters
\gamma_{g}=\gamma_{h^{*}}=\lambda
. In particular, assumption (A5) holds because both
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{m}
are
1
-strongly convex with respect to the
l_{1}
norm over their respective unit simplices, due to Pinsker’s inequality
[
5
,
22
,
47
,
51
,
61
]
. Moreover, the induced operator norm is the entry of the payoff matrix
\bm{A}
with largest magnitude:

\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{1,\infty}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{1}=1}\left\|{\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\\
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\end{subarray}}|A_{ij}|.

The stepsize parameters
\theta
,
\tau
, and
\sigma
are accordingly

\theta=1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{1,\infty}^{2}}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{4\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{1,\infty}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}}-1\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\tau=\sigma=\frac{1-\theta}{\lambda\theta}.

Given
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{-1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the corresponding accelerated nonlinear PDHG method for the matrix game (
43
) consists of the iterations

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\Delta_{m}}\left\{-\lambda\mathcal{H}_{m}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\theta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}))\right\rangle-\frac{1}{\sigma}D_{\mathcal{H}_{m}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k})\right\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Delta_{n}}\left\{\lambda\mathcal{H}_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{\tau}D_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right\}.

The updates
\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}_{k+1}
can be both computed explicitly. A straightforward calculation gives the updates

\displaystyle{}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left([\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i}e^{-\tau[\bm{A}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\theta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1})\right)]_{i}}\right)^{1/(1+\lambda\sigma)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left([\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i}e^{-\tau[\bm{A}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\theta(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1})\right)]_{i}}\right)^{1/(1+\lambda\sigma)}}

(47)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left([\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{*}]_{j}e^{-\tau[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}\right)^{1/(1+\lambda\tau)}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left([\boldsymbol{x}_{k}^{*}]_{j}e^{-\tau[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}]_{j}}\right)^{1/(1+\lambda\tau)}}

for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. All parameter calculations and updates can be performed in
O(mn)
operations. According to Prop.
4.4
and the optimality conditions (
46
), we have the strong limits

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\quad\lim_{k\to+\infty}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*},

\lim_{k\to+\infty}-[\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}]_{j}=\lambda(1+\log([\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{j})),\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\to+\infty}[\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}]_{i}=\frac{e^{([\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}/\lambda)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}e^{([\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}]_{i}/\lambda)}}.

7
Discussion

We have introduced new accelerated nonlinear primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) optimization methods to solve efficiently large-scale convex optimization problems with saddle-point structure. We proved rigorous convergence results, including results for strongly convex or smooth problems posed on infinite-dimensional reflexive Banach spaces. The new accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods are particularly useful to solve problems involving a logistic regression model or problems defined on the unit simplex or both. Indeed, for these problems, one may choose to use a Bregman divergence defined in terms of the average negative sum of binary entropy terms or the relative entropy to arrive at a straightforward and efficient optimization method. To illustrate this, we presented practical implementations of accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods for
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression and zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization. Numerical experiments showed that the nonlinear PDHG methods are considerably faster than competing methods.

The new nonlinear PDHG methods are advantageous because they can achieve an optimal convergence rate with stepsize parameters that are simple and efficient to compute. They can be typically computed on the order of
O(mn)
operations where
m
and
n
denote the dimensions to the dual and primal problems at hand. In contrast, most first-order optimization methods, including the linear PDHG method, require on the order of
O(\min{(m^{2}n,mn^{2})})
operations to compute all the parameters required to achieve an optimal convergence rate. This gain in efficiency can be considerable: in our numerical experiments for
\ell_{1}
-constrained logistic regression and zero-sum matrix games with entropy regularization we were able to get a speedup of 5 to 10 compared to other competing optimization methods.

We expect the accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods described in this work to provide efficient methods for solving large-scale supervised machine learning. In particular, these applications to strongly convex and smooth problems defined on the unit simplex, such as
\nu
-support vector machines with squared loss, maximum entropy estimation problems, and boosting and structured prediction problems in machine learning, will be pursued in future work. It would be interesting to extend the accelerated nonlinear PDHG methods described here to the stochastic case for problems that are separable in the dual variable, and to the non-convex case to deal with large-scale non-convex problems, such as those arising in deep learning. These extensions will be pursued in future work as well.

This appendix lists some basic definitions and facts from convex and functional analysis that are used in this work. It is not meant to be exhaustive, and we refer the reader to

In all definitions and facts below, the spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
denote two real reflexive Banach spaces endowed with norms
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}
and
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}
. The interior of a non-empty subset
C
of
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Y}
is denoted by
\mathrm{int}{~C}
. The set of proper, convex and lower semicontinuous functions defined on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are denoted by
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
and
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
. The dual spaces of all continuous linear functionals defined on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are denoted by
\mathcal{X}^{*}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{*}

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
and an element
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}

\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle
gives the value of
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
at
\boldsymbol{x}
. Likewise, for a linear functional
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
and an element
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, the bilinear form
\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}\right\rangle
gives the value of
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
at
\boldsymbol{y}
. The norms associated to
\mathcal{X}^{*}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
are defined as

\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=1}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=1}\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}\right\rangle.

Let
\bm{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
denote a bounded linear operator. Its corresponding adjoint operator
\bm{A}^{*}\colon\mathcal{Y}^{*}\to\mathcal{X}^{*}
is defined so as to satisfy

\left\langle\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle

for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\boldsymbol{y^{*}}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
. The operator norm associated to
\bm{A}
is defined as

\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=1}\left\|{\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}=\left\|{\bm{A}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}=1}\left\|{\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}.

These definitions imply the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality

\left|\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle\right|\leqslant\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}.

.

A subset
C\subset\mathcal{X}
is convex if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in C\times C
and every scalar
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the point
\lambda\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
is contained in
C
.

Definition A.2

(Proper functions)
.

A function
f
defined on
\mathcal{X}
is proper if its domain

\mathrm{dom~}f=\left\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}:f(\boldsymbol{x})<+\infty\right\}

is non-empty and
f(\boldsymbol{x})>-\infty
for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
.

Definition A.3

(Lower semicontinuous functions)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is lower semicontinuous at a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
if for every sequence
\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
in
\mathcal{X}
that converges to
\boldsymbol{x}
,

\liminf_{k\to+\infty}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\geqslant f(\boldsymbol{x}).

We say that
f
is lower semicontinuous if it is lower semicontinuous at every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
.

Definition A.4

(Convex functions)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is convex if its domain
\mathrm{dom~}f
is convex and if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{dom~}f\times\mathrm{dom~}f
and every scalar
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

f(\lambda\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda f(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-\lambda)f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}).

It is strictly convex if the inequality above is strict whenever
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, and it is
m
-strongly convex (with
m>0
) if for every pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{dom~}f\times\mathrm{dom~}f
and every scalar
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

f(\lambda\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda f(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-\lambda)f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\frac{m}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}.

Definition A.5

(Coercive functions)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is coercive if for every sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
in
\mathcal{X}
such that
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=+\infty
,

\lim_{k\to+\infty}f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=+\infty.

A proper function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is supercoercive if for every sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
in
\mathcal{X}
such that
\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=+\infty
,

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}}=+\infty.

Definition A.6

(Weak convergence)
.

A sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
of points in
\mathcal{X}
converges weakly to
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
if for every linear functional
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
,

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle.

Definition A.7

(Differentiability)
.

A proper function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
with
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}f)}\neq\varnothing
is differentiable at a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}f)}
if there exists a linear functional
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\to 0\\
\lambda>0\end{subarray}}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-f(\boldsymbol{x})}{\lambda}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{x^{*}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right\rangle.

This linear functional, when it exists, is unique. It is called the gradient of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
and is denoted by
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Definition A.8

(Subdifferentiability and subgradients)
.

A function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is subdifferentiable at a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
if there exists a linear functional
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
such that for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
,

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-f(\boldsymbol{x})-\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle\geqslant 0.

(48)

In this case,
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is called a subgradient of the function
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
. The set of subgradients at
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is called the subdifferential of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
, and it is denoted by
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})
. The set of points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
at which the subdifferential
\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})
is non-empty is denoted by
\mathrm{dom~}\partial f
.

If
f
is strictly convex, then for
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
the inequality in (
48
) is strict. If
f
is
m
-strongly convex and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial f
, then for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
the subgradients
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})
satisfy the inequality

f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-f(\boldsymbol{x})-\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle\geqslant\frac{m}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}.

(49)

Definition A.9

(Convex conjugates)
.

Let
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
. The convex conjugate
f^{*}\colon\mathcal{X}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
of
f
is defined by

f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle-f(\boldsymbol{x})\right\}.

By definition, the function
f^{*}
is in
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X}^{*})

[
26
, Definition 4.1]
.

Definition A.10

(Saddle points)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper function. A pair of points
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
if for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\boldsymbol{y^{*}}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}
,

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})\leqslant\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})\leqslant\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}).

Definition A.11

(Essential smoothness)
.

A function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is essentially smooth if
\mathrm{dom~}\partial f\neq\varnothing
,
\mathrm{dom~}\partial f=\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}f)}
,
f
is differentiable on
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}f)}
, and
\left\|{\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\to+\infty
for every sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
in
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}f)}
converging to some boundary point of
\mathrm{dom~}f
.

Definition A.12

(Essential strict convexity)
.

A function
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is essentially strictly convex if
f
is strictly convex on every convex subset of
\mathrm{dom~}\partial f
and the subdifferential mapping
\partial f^{*}
is locally bounded on its domain.

Definition A.13

(Bregman divergences)
.

Let
\phi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
with
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}\neq\varnothing
. The Bregman divergence of the function
\phi
is the function
D_{\phi}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}\to[0,+\infty]
defined as

D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\phi(\boldsymbol{x})-\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\max_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\partial\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right\}.

Note that Bregman divergences are sometimes defined differently in the convex analysis literature. Here, we use the definition of
Bauschke et al. 2001
.

Definition A.14

(Bregman proximity operators)
.

Let
f,\phi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
with
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}\neq\varnothing
and let
t>0
. The Bregman
D_{\phi}
-proximal operator
\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\cdot)
is a set-valued mapping defined for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
as

\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{dom~}f\cap\mathrm{dom~}\phi:tf(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})+D_{\phi}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{tf(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\}<+\infty\right\}.

(50)

Facts

Fact A.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and let
\bm{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a bounded linear operator. For every
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y^{*}}),(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}
, the following auxiliary inequality holds:

\left|\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\rangle\right|\leqslant\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}^{2}\right).

(51)

Proof.

From the Cauchy–Schwartz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\alpha}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y^{*}}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}^{2}\right).

∎

Fact A.2

(Weighted averages of a convergent sequence)
.

Let
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}\subset\mathcal{X}
be a sequence converging strongly to some
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, let
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}\subset(0,+\infty)
be a divergent sequence, i.e.,
\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\lambda_{k}=+\infty
, and set
T_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}
. Then

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{k}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right)-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}=0.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists some
K_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\geqslant K_{1}
, we have
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}<\epsilon/2
. Now, let
k\geqslant K_{1}
, take the difference between the weighted average
\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
, take the norm, use the triangle inequality and rearrange to get

\displaystyle\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}\right)}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}-1}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=K_{1}}^{k}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}-1}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=K_{1}}^{k}\lambda_{j}\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}-1}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

The first term on the right hand side of the last line depends on
k
only through the term
T_{k}
. By assumption,
T_{k}\to+\infty
as
k\to+\infty
, and therefore there exists some
K_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
k\geqslant K_{2}
,

\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{K_{1}-1}\lambda_{j}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Taking
k\geqslant\max{(K_{1},K_{2})}
, we find

\left\|{\frac{1}{T_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}<\epsilon.

As
\epsilon
was arbitrary positive number, we can take
\epsilon\to 0
and obtain the desired result.
∎

Fact A.5

(The primal problem and its dual problem)
.

Let
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, let
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, and let
\bm{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a bounded linear operator. Assume the primal (minimization) problem

\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{g(\boldsymbol{x})+h(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x})\right\}

(52)

has at least one solution and assume there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
h
is continuous at
\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}
. Then the dual (maximization) problem

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y^{*}}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}\left\{-g^{*}(-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y^{*}})-h^{*}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}})\right\}

(53)

is finite and has at least one solution. Moreover, if
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})
denotes a pair of solutions to the primal and dual problem then
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})

satisfies the following optimality conditions

-\bm{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}\in\partial g(\boldsymbol{x}_{s})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}\in\partial h(\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{s}).

Proof.

See
[
26
, Theorem 4.1, Theorem 4.2, Equations (4.24)-(4.25)]
for a proof. (Beware, in
[
26
]
the notation used for the solution
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}
is flipped by a minus sign.)
∎

Fact A.6

(Convex-concave saddle point problems)
.

Let
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, let
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{Y})
, let
\bm{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a bounded linear operator, define the function
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})=g(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}},\bm{A}\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle-h^{*}(\boldsymbol{y^{*}}).

Then the pair of points
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
is a solution of the primal problem (
52
) and
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}
is a solution of the dual problem (
53
).

Proof.

See
[
26
, Proposition 3.1, page 57]
.
∎

Fact A.7

(Properties of Bregman divergences)
.

Let
\phi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
with
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}\neq\varnothing
, let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}\phi
, and let
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
. Assume that
\phi
is differentiable on
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
. Then the Bregman divergence
D_{\phi}
of
\phi
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

The Bregman divergence can be written as
D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\phi(\boldsymbol{x})-\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\left\langle\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right\rangle
.

(ii)

The Bregman divergence
D_{\phi}
satisfies the three-point identity

D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=D_{\phi}(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})+D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})+\left\langle\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\nabla\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}),\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle.

(54)

(iii)

If
\phi
is essentially strictly convex, then
D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
.

(iv)

If
\phi
is essentially strictly convex, then the function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
is coercive for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
.

(v)

If
\phi
is supercoercive, then the function
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\mapsto D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
is coercive for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
.

(vi)

If
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is a sequence in
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
converging to a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
, then

\lim_{k\to+\infty}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=0.

(vii)

Assume that
\phi
is essentially smooth. If
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is a sequence in
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
converging to a point
\boldsymbol{x}_{c}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
, then

\lim_{k\to+\infty}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{c}).

(viii)

If
\phi
is
m
-strongly convex with respect to
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}
, then

D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{m}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}.

Proof.

See
[
3
, Lemma 7.3]
for the proof of (i) and (iii)-(vi). Statement (ii) follows from (i) and a straightforward calculation. Statement (vii) follows from (i) and the continuity of both
\phi
and
\nabla\phi
over
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
. Statement (viii) follows from (i) and inequality (
49
).
∎

Fact A.8

(Properties of Bregman proximity operators)
.

Let
f,\phi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{X})
be two functions such that
\mathrm{dom~}f\cap\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}\neq\varnothing
, let
t>0
, and assume
\phi
is essentially smooth and essentially strictly convex. In addition, assume that either
f
is bounded from below or
\phi
is supercoercive. Then the following properties hold:

(i)

The proximal operator
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\mapsto\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
defined in (
50
) is single-valued on its domain
\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
. That is, for every
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
,

\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{tf(\boldsymbol{x})+D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\right\}.

Moreover,
\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\in\mathrm{dom~}\partial f\cap\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
.

(ii)

For every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
, the proximal point
\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
satisfies the characterization

f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))-\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\nabla\phi(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))-\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle\geqslant 0.

(55)

If, in addition, there exists
\gamma_{f}>0
such that the function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto f(\boldsymbol{x})-\gamma_{f}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex, then this characterization can be strengthened to

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\nabla\phi(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))-\nabla\phi(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle

(56)

\displaystyle\geqslant\gamma_{f}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})).

(iii)

For every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{dom~}f
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}{(\mathrm{dom~}\phi)}
,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{t}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))+\frac{1}{t}D_{f}(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(57)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{t}D_{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})).

If, in addition, there exists
\gamma_{f}>0
such that the function
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto f(\boldsymbol{x})-\gamma_{f}\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
is convex, then (
57
) can be strengthened to

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{t}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))+\frac{1}{t}D_{f}(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(58)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{t}+\gamma_{f}\right)D_{f}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})).

Proof.

See
[
4
, Proposition 3.21-3.23, Theorem 3.24, Corollary 3.25]
for the proof of statements (i). Statement (ii) follows directly from
[
26
, Proposition 2.2, page 38]
. To prove inequality (
57
) in (iii), use the characterization (
55
) to write

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{1}{t}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(\mathrm{prox}_{\left(tf,D_{\phi}\right)}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}))+\frac{1}{t}D_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\lim_{l\to+\infty}\Delta_{k_{l}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=\frac{1}{\tau}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{c})+\frac{1}{\sigma}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})-\left\langle\boldsymbol{y^{*}}-\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{c})\right\rangle,

and we conclude, from the completeness property of the real numbers, that

\liminf_{l\to+\infty}\Delta_{k_{l}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})-\Delta_{k_{l+1}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=0.

We therefore deduce the infimum limit

\liminf_{l\to+\infty}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*})\leqslant 0.

The lower semicontinuity property of the functions
g
and
h^{*}
implies

0\geqslant\liminf_{l\to+\infty}\left(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*})\right)\geqslant\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*}),

from which we find

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})\leqslant\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}^{*}).

As the pair of points
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y^{*}})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}
was arbitrary, we conclude that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
is a saddle point of the Lagrangian (
7
).

It remains to prove that the sequence of iterates
\left\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\right\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
converges strongly to the saddle point
(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
. To do so, consider the descent rule (
13
) with the choice of saddle point
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})=(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
. From the saddle-point property
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})\geqslant 0
and inequality (
65
), we have

0\leqslant\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})\leqslant\Delta_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*}).

The sequence of real numbers
\{\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is non-increasing in
l
, and as such, it has a limit. By Lemma
2.1
, Fact
A.7
(vi), and the strong convergence of the subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}},\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*})\}_{l=1}^{+\infty}
to
(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
, we have

\lim_{l\to+\infty}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}})=0,\quad\lim_{l\to+\infty}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*})=0,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\lim_{l\to+\infty}\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}})\right\rangle=0.

We deduce the limit

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})=0.

Now, from this limit and the lower bound (
65
) with
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
, we have

0\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}(1-\sqrt{\tau\sigma}\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}})\left(\frac{1}{\tau}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})+\frac{1}{\sigma}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})\right)\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})=0.

Since
(1-\sqrt{\tau\sigma}\left\|{\bm{A}}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}})>0
and assumption (A5) holds, we deduce the limits

0\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}_{c}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=0

and

0\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})=0.

This proves the strong convergence of the sequence of iterates
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
to the saddle point
(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
. Finally, we deduce from Fact

that the sequence of averages
\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{K},\boldsymbol{Y}_{K}^{*})\}_{K=1}^{+\infty}
converges strongly to the same limit
(\boldsymbol{x}_{c},\boldsymbol{y}_{c}^{*})
. This concludes the proof.

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{K\to+\infty}\left(\frac{1+\gamma_{g}\tau_{0}}{\gamma_{g}}\right)\frac{\sigma_{0}}{\sigma_{K}}\left(\frac{1}{\tau_{0}}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=0.

We further deduce from Fact
A.2
that the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{K}\}_{K=1}^{+\infty}
converges strongly to the same limit
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
.

Suppose now that
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is finite-dimensional. Since the sequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
is bounded and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
converges strongly to
\boldsymbol{x}_{s}
, there is some subsequence
\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{k_{l}},\boldsymbol{y}_{k_{l}}^{*})\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
that converges strongly to a point
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}^{*}
. By Fact
A.2
, the subsequence of averages
\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{k_{l}},\boldsymbol{Y}_{k_{l}}^{*})\}_{k=1}^{+\infty}
also strongly converges to
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})
. A similar argument as the one described two paragraphs before shows then that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{s},\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*})
is a saddle point of the Lagrangian (
7
), and moreover from Fact
A.6
we deduce that
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{*}
is a solution to the dual problem (
6
). This concludes the proof.

Appendix E
Proof of Proposition

We divide the proof into four parts, first deriving an auxiliary result, and then proving in turn the descent rule (

Substitute in

to get

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{y}^{*})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*})

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\tau}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})-(1+\gamma_{g}\tau)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{X}}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})-D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*})-(1+\gamma_{h^{*}}\sigma)D_{\phi_{\mathcal{Y}^{*}}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*})\right)

and substitute in (

\displaystyle\theta\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\theta\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y}_{k}^{*},\bm{A}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k+1})\right\rangle.

Next, use inequality (

Finally, use (
