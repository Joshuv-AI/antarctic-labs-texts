The Differential Geometry of the Orbit Space of Extended Affine Jacobi Group 𝑨 𝟏 \boldsymbol{A_{1}}
Guilherme F. ALMEIDA

Abstract
We define certain extensions of Jacobi groups of A 1 A_{1} , prove an analogue of Chevalley theorem for their invariants, and construct a Dubrovin–Frobenius structure on its orbit space.

Introduction

Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold is a geometric interpretation of a remarkable system of differential equations called WDVV equations
[
6
]
. Since early nineties, there has been a continuous exchange of ideas from fields that are not trivially related to each other, such as topological quantum field theory, non-linear waves, singularity theory, random matrices theory, integrable systems, and Painlevé equations. Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds theory is a bridge between them.

1.1
Orbit space of reflection groups and its extensions

In
[
6
]
, Dubrovin pointed out that WDVV solutions with certain good analytic properties are related with partition functions of TFT. Afterwards, Dubrovin conjectured that WDVV solutions with certain good analytic properties are in one to one correspondence with discrete groups. This conjecture is supported by ideas which come from singularity theory, because in this setting there exists an integrable systems/discrete group correspondence. Furthermore, in minimal models, such as Gepner chiral rings, there exists a correspondence between physical models and discrete groups.
In
[
14
]
, Hertling proved that a particular class of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold, called polynomial Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold, is isomorphic to the orbit space of a finite Coxeter group, which are spaces such that their geometric structure is invariant under the finite Coxeter group.
In
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
24
]
, there are many examples of WDVV solutions that are associated with orbit spaces of natural extensions of finite Coxeter groups, such as
extended affine Weyl groups, and Jacobi groups. Therefore, the construction of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds on orbit space of reflection groups and its extensions is a prospective project of the classification of WDVV solutions. In addition, WDDV solutions arising from orbit spaces may also have some applications in TFT or some combinatorial problem, because previously these relationships were demonstrated in some examples, such as the orbit space of the finite Coxeter group
A_{1}
, and the extended affine Weyl group
A_{1}

[
8
,
11
]
.

1.2
Hurwtiz space/orbit space correspondence

There are several other non-trivial connections that Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds theory can make. For example, Hurwitz spaces is the one of the main sources of examples of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds. Hurwitz spaces
H_{g,n_{0},n_{1},\dots,n_{m}}
are moduli space of covering over
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with a fixed ramification profile. More specifically,
H_{g,n_{0},n_{1},\dots,n_{m}}
is moduli space of pairs

\displaystyle\big\{C_{g},\lambda\colon C_{g}\mapsto\mathbb{CP}^{1}\big\},

where
C_{g}
is a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
and
\lambda
is meromorphic function with poles in

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}(\infty)=\{\infty_{0},\infty_{1},\dots,\infty_{m}\}.

Moreover,
\lambda
has degree
n_{i}+1
near
\infty_{i}
. Hurwitz space, with a choice of a specific Abelian differential, called quasi-momentum or primary differential, give rise to a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold; see section
[
6
,
19
]
for details. In some examples, the Dubrovin–Frobenius structure of Hurwitz spaces are isomorphic to Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds associated with orbit spaces of suitable groups. For instance, the orbit space of the finite Coxeter group
A_{1}
is isomorphic to the Hurwitz space
H_{0,1}
. Furthermore, orbit space of the extended affine Weyl group
\tilde{A}_{1}
and of the Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
are isomorphic to the Hurwitz spaces
H_{0,0,0}
and
H_{1,1}
respectively. Motivated by these examples, we construct the following diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{0,1}\cong\text{orbit space of $A_{1}$}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{0,0,0}\cong\text{orbit space of $\tilde{A}_{1}$}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1,1}\cong\text{orbit space of $\mathcal{J}(A_{1})$}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{1,0,0}\cong\ ?}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 2}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 4}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 3}

From the Hurwitz space side, the vertical lines 2 and 4 mean that we increase the genus by
1
, and the horizontal lines mean that we split one pole of order
2
into two simple poles. From the orbit space side, the vertical line 2 means that we are doing an extension from the finite Coxeter group
A_{1}
to the Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
; the line horizontal line 1 means that we are extending the
orbit space of
A_{1}
to the extended affine Weyl group
\tilde{A}_{1}
. Therefore, one might ask if the line 3 and 4 would imply an orbit space interpretation of the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
. The main goal of this paper is to define a new class of groups such that its orbit space carries the Dubrovin–Frobenius structure of
H_{1,0,0}
. The new group is called extended affine Jacobi group
A_{n}
, and is denoted by
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. This group is an extension of the Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
and of the extended affine Weyl group
\tilde{A}_{1}
.

1.3
Results

The main goal of this paper is to construct the Dubrovin–Frobenius structure of the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
from the data of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. In other words, we derive the WDVV solution associated to the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
without using the correspondent Hurwitz space construction. First of all, recall the definition of WDVV equation:

Definition 1.1
.

The function
F(t)
,
t=\big(t^{1},t^{2},\dots,t^{n}\big)
is a solution of a WDVV equation if its third derivatives

\displaystyle c_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\frac{\partial^{3}F}{\partial t^{\alpha}\partial t^{\beta}\partial t^{\gamma}}

(1.1)

satisfy the following conditions:

1)

\eta_{\alpha\beta}=c_{1\alpha\beta}
is constant nondegenerate matrix;

2)

the function

\displaystyle c_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}=\eta^{\gamma\delta}c_{\alpha\beta\delta}

is structure constant of associative algebra;

3)

F(t)
must be quasi-homogeneous function

\displaystyle F\big(c^{d_{1}}t^{1},\dots,c^{d_{n}}t^{n}\big)=c^{d_{F}}F\big(t^{1},\dots,t^{n}\big)

for any nonzero
c
and for some numbers
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
,
d_{F}
.

Our goal is to extract a WDVV equation from the data of a suitable group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. We define the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. Recall that the group
A_{1}
acts on
\mathbb{C}\ni v_{0}
by reflections

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto-v_{0}.

The group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
is an extension of the group
A_{1}
in the following sense:

Proposition 1.2
.

The group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)\ni(w,t,\gamma)
acts on
\Omega:=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{H}\ni(u,v,\tau)=(u,v_{0},v_{2},\tau)
as follows:

\displaystyle w(u,v,\tau)=(u,wv,\tau),

\displaystyle t(u,v,\tau)=\left(u-\langle\lambda,v\rangle_{\tilde{A}_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle_{\tilde{A}_{1}}\tau,v+\lambda\tau+\mu,\tau\right),

\displaystyle\gamma(u,v,\tau)=\left(u+\frac{c\langle v,v\rangle_{\tilde{A}_{1}}}{2(c\tau+d)},\frac{v}{c\tau+d},\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right),

where
w\in A_{1}
acts by reflection in the first
v_{0}
variables of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\ni v=(v_{0},v_{2})
,

t=(\lambda,\mu)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\qquad\langle v,v\rangle_{\tilde{A}_{1}}=2v_{0}^{2}-2v_{2}^{2}.

See Section
2.1
for details.

In order to define any geometric structure in an orbit space, first it is necessary to define a notion of invariant
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
sections. For this purpose, we generalise the ring of invariant functions used in
[
2
,
3
]
for the group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
, which are called Jacobi forms. This notion was first defined in
[
12
]
by Eichler and Zagier for the group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
, and it was further generalised for the group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
in
[
23
]
by Wirthmuller. Furthermore, an explicit base of generators were derived in
[
2
,
3
]
by Bertola. The Jacobi forms used in this thesis are defined by:

Definition 1.3
.

The weak
\tilde{A}_{1}
-invariant Jacobi forms of weight
k
, order
l
, and index
m
are functions on
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n+2}\oplus\mathbb{H}\ni(u,v_{0},v_{2},\tau)=(u,v,\tau)
which satisfy

\displaystyle\varphi(w(u,v,\tau))=\varphi(u,v,\tau),\qquad\textrm{$A_{1}$-invariant condition},

\displaystyle\varphi(t(u,v,\tau))=\varphi(u,v,\tau),

\displaystyle\varphi(\gamma(u,v,\tau))=(c\tau+d)^{-k}\varphi(u,v,\tau),

\displaystyle E\varphi(u,v,\tau):=-\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\varphi(u,v,\tau)=m\varphi(u,v,\tau),\qquad\textrm{Euler vector field}.

(1.2)

Moreover, the weak
\tilde{A}_{1}
-invariant Jacobi forms are meromorphic in the variable
v_{2}
on a fixed divisor, in contrast with the Jacobi forms of the group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
,which are holomorphic in each variable; see details on Section
2.2
. The ring of weak
\tilde{A}_{1}
-invariant Jacobi forms gives the notion of the Euler vector field; indeed, the vector field defined in the last equation of (
1.2
) measures the degree of the Jacobi forms, which coincides with the index. The differential geometry of the orbit space of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
should be understood as the space such that its sections are written in terms of Jacobi forms. Then, in order for this statement to make sense, we must prove a Chevalley type theorem, which is:

Theorem 1.4
.

The trigraded algebra of Jacobi forms
J_{\bullet,\bullet,\bullet}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}=\bigoplus_{k,l,m}J_{k,l,m}^{\tilde{A}_{1}}
is freely generated by
2
fundamental Jacobi forms
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
over the graded ring
E_{\bullet,\bullet}

\displaystyle J_{\bullet,\bullet,\bullet}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}=E_{\bullet,\bullet}\left[\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}\right],

where

\displaystyle E_{\bullet,\bullet}=J_{\bullet,\bullet,0}

is the ring of coefficients.

More specifically, the ring of function
E_{\bullet,\bullet}
is the space of functions
f(v_{2},\tau)
such that, for fixed
\tau
, the functions
\tau\mapsto f(v_{2},\tau)
is an elliptic function.

Moreover,
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1})
are given by

Corollary 1.5
.

The function

\displaystyle\left[{\rm e}^{z\frac{\partial}{\partial p}}\left({\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}u}\frac{\theta_{1}(v_{0}+v_{2}+p)\theta_{1}(-v_{0}+v_{2}+p)}{\theta_{1}(2v_{2}+p)\theta_{1}^{\prime}(0)}\right)\right]\bigg|_{p=0}=\varphi_{1}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}+\varphi_{0}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}z+O\big(z^{2}\big),

generates the Jacobi forms
\varphi_{0}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}
and
\varphi_{1}^{\mathcal{J}(A_{1})}
,
where

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\big(\hat{\varphi}_{1}^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1})}\big)\bigg|_{p=0}.

This lemma realises the functions
(\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},v_{2},\tau)
as coordinates of the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
.
The unit vector field is chosen to be

\displaystyle e=\frac{\partial}{\partial\varphi_{0}},

(1.3)

because
\varphi_{0}
is the basic generator with maximum weight degree; see the Section
2.2
for details.

The last component we need to construct is the intersection form of the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. The natural candidate to be such a metric is the invariant metric of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
, which given by

\displaystyle g=2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau.

(1.4)

From the data of the intersection form (
1.4
), is possible to derive a second flat metric of the orbit space
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
.
The second metric is given by

\displaystyle\eta^{*}:={\rm Lie}_{e}g^{*},

and it is denoted by the Saito metric due to K. Saito, who was the first to define this metric for the case of finite Coxeter group
[
18
]
. One of the main technical problems of this paper is to prove that the Saito metric
\eta^{*}
is flat. At this point, we can state our main result.

Theorem 1.6
.

A suitable covering of the orbit space
\left(\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{H}\right)/\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
with the intersection form (
1.4
), unit vector field (
1.3
), and Euler vector field given by the last equation of (
1.2
) has a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure. Moreover, a suitable covering of
\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\oplus\mathbb{H}/\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
is isomorphic as Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold to a suitable covering of the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
.

See Section
3.4
for details. In particular, we derive explicitly the WDVV solution associated with the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
, which is given by

\displaystyle F\big(t^{1},t^{2},t^{3},t^{4}\big)=\frac{\rm i}{4\pi}\big(t^{1}\big)^{2}t^{4}-2t^{1}t^{2}t^{3}-\big(t^{2}\big)^{2}\log\left(t^{2}\frac{\theta_{1}^{\prime}\big(0,t^{4}\big)}{\theta_{1}\big(2t^{3},t^{4}\big)}\right),

where

\displaystyle\theta_{1}(v,\tau)=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}{\rm e}^{\pi{\rm i}\tau(n+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}\sin((2n+1)v).

(1.5)

The results of this paper are important because of the following:

1.

The Hurwitz spaces
H_{1,0,0}
are classified by the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
, hence we increase the knowledge of the WDVV/discrete group correspondence. Recently, the case
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
attracted the attention of experts, due to its application in integrable systems
[
5
,
13
,
16
]
.

2.

The orbit space construction of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
can be generalised to the group
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{n})
; see the definition in
[
1
]
. Further, the same can be done to the other classical finite Coxeter groups as
B_{n}
,
D_{n}
. Hence, these orbit spaces could give rise to a new class of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds. Furthermore, the associated integrable hierarchies of this new class of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifolds could have applications in Gromow–Witten theory and combinatorics.

This paper is organised in the following way: In Section
2
, we define extended affine Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
and we prove some results related with its ring of invariant functions. In Section
3

\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
and compute its free-energy. Furthermore, we show that the orbit space of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
is isomorphic, as a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold, to the Hurwitz–Frobenius manifold
\tilde{H}_{1,0,0}

[
6
,
19
]
. See Theorem
3.7
for details.

2
Invariant theory of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)}

The focus of this section is to define a new extension of the finite Coxeter group
A_{1}
such that it contains the affine Weyl group
\tilde{A}_{1}
and the Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
. This new extension will be denoted by Extended affine Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. Further, we prove that, from the data of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
, we can reconstruct the Dubrovin–Frobenius structute of the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
on the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. The advantage of this orbit space construction is the Chevalley Theorem
2.25
, which gives a global interpretation for orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. Furthermore, it attaches the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
to the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
, and this fact might be useful in the general understanding of WDVV/group correspondence. These results sre interesting because the Hurwitz space
H_{1,0,0}
is well know to have a rich Dubrovin–Frobenius structure, called a tri-Hamiltonian structure
[
16
]
and
[
15
]
. This fact realises the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
as suitable ambient space for Dubrovin–Frobenius submanifolds. Furthermore, it shows an interesting relationship relation between the integrable systems of the ambient space and the integrable systems of its Dubrovin–Frobenius submanifolds.

2.1
The group
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)}

The main goal of this section is to motivate and to define the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. In order to do that, it will be necessary to recall the definition of the group
A_{1}
, and some of its extensions. Moreover, its goal is to understand how to derive WDDV solution starting from these groups.

The group
A_{n}
acts on the space
\Omega^{A_{n}}=\big\{(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\colon\sum_{i=0}^{n}v_{i}=0\big\}
by permutations:

\displaystyle(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\mapsto(v_{i_{0}},v_{i_{1}},\dots,v_{i_{n}}).

(2.1)

Let us concentrate on the simplest possible case, i.e.,
n=1
. In this case, the action on
\mathbb{C}\cong\Omega^{A_{1}}
is just:

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto-v_{0}.

The understanding of the orbit space of
A_{1}
requires a Chevalley theorem for the ring of invariants. The Chevalley theorem form the group
A_{n}
says that

Theorem 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let the Coxeter group
A_{n}
which acts on
\Omega^{A_{n}}\ni(v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
as (
2.1
), then

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{n}]^{A_{n}}\cong\mathbb{C}[a_{2},a_{3},\dots,a_{n+1}],

where
a_{i}
are weighted homogeneous polynomials of degree
i
.

In the
A_{1}
case, the ring of invariants is just

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[v_{0}^{2}\big]\cong\mathbb{C}[a_{2}],

then the orbit space of
A_{1}
is just the

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Spec}\left(\mathbb{C}\big[v_{0}^{2}\big]\right).

In the papers
[
6
,
7
]
, it was demonstrated that
\mathbb{C}/A_{1}
has structure of Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold. Furthermore, it is isomorphic to the Hurwitz space
H_{0,1}
, i.e., the space of rational functions with a double pole. The isomorphism can be realized by the following map:

\displaystyle[v_{0}]\mapsto\lambda^{A_{1}}(p,v_{0})=(p-v_{0})(p+v_{0})=p^{2}+a_{2}.

Note that the isomorphism works, because
\lambda^{A_{1}}(p,v_{0})
is invariant under the
A_{1}
-action. Applying the methods developed in
[
6
,
7
]
, one can show that the WDVV solution associated with this orbit space is

\displaystyle F\big(t^{1}\big)=\frac{\big(t^{1}\big)^{3}}{6},

where
t^{1}
is the flat coordinate of the metric
\eta
.

In
[
6
,
10
]
it was also considered the extended affine
A_{1}
that is denoted by
\tilde{A}_{1}
. The action on

\displaystyle\left(L^{A_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}=\left\{(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\colon\sum_{i=0}^{1}v_{i}=0\right\}

is

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto\pm v_{0}+\mu_{0},\qquad v_{2}\mapsto v_{2}+\mu_{2},

where
\mu_{0},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A notion of the invariant ring for the group extended affine
A_{n}
was defined in
[
10
]
, and Dubrovin and Zhang proved that this invariant ring for the case
\tilde{A}_{1}
is isomorphic to

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\big[{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\cos(2\pi{\rm i}v_{0}),{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\big].

Therefore, the orbit space of
\tilde{A}_{1}
is the weight projective variety associated with

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Spec}\left(\mathbb{C}\left[{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\cos(2\pi{\rm i}v_{0}),{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\right]\right).

Further, a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure was built on the orbit space of
\tilde{A}_{1}
with the following WDVV solution:

\displaystyle F\big(t^{1},t^{2}\big)=\frac{\big(t^{1}\big)^{2}t^{2}}{2}+{\rm e}^{t^{2}}.

(2.2)

The orbit space of
\tilde{A}_{1}
is also associated with a Hurwitz space, but the relation is slightly less straightforward. The first step is to consider the following map:

\displaystyle[v_{0},v_{2}]\mapsto\lambda^{\tilde{A}_{1}}(p,v_{0},v_{2})={\rm e}^{p}+{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\cos(2\pi{\rm i}v_{0})+{\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}{\rm e}^{-p}.

The second is to consider the Legendre transformation of
S_{2}
type
[
6
, Appendix B and Chapter 5]
. Consider

\displaystyle b={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}}\cos(2\pi{\rm i}v_{0}),\qquad a={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}v_{2}},

and the following choice of primary differential
{\rm d}\tilde{p}
implicity given by

\displaystyle{\rm d}p=\frac{{\rm d}\tilde{p}}{\tilde{p}-b}.

Then, in these new coordinates
\lambda^{\tilde{A}_{1}}
, is given by

\displaystyle\lambda(\tilde{p},a,b)=\tilde{p}+\frac{a}{\tilde{p}-b}.

Hence, the orbit space of
\tilde{A}_{1}
is isomorphic to the Hurwitz space
H_{0,0,0}
, i.e., space of fractional functions with two simple poles.

The next example of group to be considered is the Jacobi group
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
, which acts on

\displaystyle\Omega^{\mathcal{J}(A_{1})}:=\left(L^{A_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{C}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{H}=\left\{(v_{0},v_{1},u,\tau)\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\oplus\mathbb{H}\colon\sum_{i=0}^{1}v_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}+\tau\mathbb{Z}\right\}

as follows:

A_{1}
-action:

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto-v_{0},\qquad u\mapsto u,\qquad\tau\mapsto\tau.

(2.3)

Translation:

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto v_{0}+\mu_{0}+\lambda_{0}\tau,\qquad u\mapsto u-\lambda_{0}v_{0}-\frac{\lambda_{0}^{2}}{2}\tau,\qquad\tau\mapsto\tau,

(2.4)

where
\mu_{0},\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-action:

\displaystyle v_{0}\mapsto\frac{v_{0}}{c\tau+d},\qquad u\mapsto u-\frac{cv_{0}^{2}}{2(c\tau+d)},\qquad\tau\mapsto\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},

(2.5)

where
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
ad-bc=1
.

The notion of invariant ring of
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
was first defined in
[
12
]
. However, the definitions stated in
[
2
,
3
,
23
]
are more suitable for this purpose.

Definition 2.2
.

The weak
A_{1}
-invariant, Jacobi forms of weight
k
, and index
m
are holomorphic functions on
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{H}\ni(u,v_{0},\tau)

3
Frobenius structure on the orbit space of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)}

In this section, a Dubrovin–Frobenius manifold structure will be constructed on the orbit space of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. More precisely, it will define the data
\big(\Omega^{\mathcal{J}(\tilde{A}_{1}}/\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big),g^{*},e,E\big)
, with the intersection form

, unit vector field

3.1
Intersection form

The first step to be done is to construct the intersection form. It will be shown that such metric can be constructed by using just the data of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. The strategy is to combine the intersection form of the group
\tilde{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})
. Recall that the intersection form of the group
\tilde{A}_{1}

[
6
,
10
]
is

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2},

and the intersection form of
\mathcal{J}(A_{1})

[

,
3
,

is

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}={\rm d}v_{0}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau.

Therefore, the natural candidate to be the intersection form of
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
is

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau.

The following lemma proves that this metric is invariant metric of the group
\mathcal{J}\big(\tilde{A}_{1}\big)
. To be precise, the metric will be invariant under the action of
A_{1}
, and translations, and equivariant under the action of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})

Lemma 3.1
.

The metric

\displaystyle{\rm d}s^{2}=2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau

(3.1)

is invariant under the transformations (
2.9
), (
2.10

Moreover, the transformations (
2.11

, i.e.,

\displaystyle 2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau\mapsto\frac{2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}.

Proof.

Under (
2.9
), (
2.10
), the differentials transform to

\displaystyle{\rm d}v_{0}\mapsto-{\rm d}v_{0},\qquad{\rm d}v_{0}\mapsto{\rm d}v_{0}+\lambda_{0}{\rm d}\tau,\qquad{\rm d}v_{2}\mapsto{\rm d}v_{2}+\lambda_{2}{\rm d}\tau,

\displaystyle{\rm d}u\mapsto{\rm d}u-\lambda_{0}^{2}{\rm d}\tau-2\lambda_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}+\lambda_{2}^{2}{\rm d}\tau+2\lambda_{2}{\rm d}v_{2},\qquad{\rm d}\tau\mapsto{\rm d}\tau.

Hence,

\displaystyle{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}\mapsto dv^{2}_{0},\qquad{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}\mapsto{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}+2\lambda_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}{\rm d}\tau+\lambda_{0}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2},\qquad{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}\mapsto{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2\lambda_{2}{\rm d}v_{2}{\rm d}\tau+\lambda_{2}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2},

\displaystyle 2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau\mapsto 2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau-2\lambda_{0}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2}-4\lambda_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}{\rm d}\tau+2\lambda_{2}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2}+4\lambda_{2}{\rm d}v_{2}{\rm d}\tau.

So,

\displaystyle 2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau\mapsto 2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau.

Let us show that the metric has conformal transformation under the transformations (
2.11

\displaystyle{\rm d}v_{0}\mapsto\frac{{\rm d}v_{0}}{c\tau+d}-\frac{v_{0}{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}},\qquad{\rm d}v_{2}\mapsto\frac{{\rm d}v_{2}}{c\tau+d}-\frac{v_{2}{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}},\qquad{\rm d}\tau\mapsto\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}},

\displaystyle{\rm d}u\mapsto{\rm d}u+\frac{c(2v_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}-2v_{2}{\rm d}v_{2}^{2})}{c\tau+d}-\frac{c(v_{0}^{2}-v_{2}^{2}){\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}.

Then,

\displaystyle{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}\mapsto\frac{{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}-\frac{2v_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{3}}+\frac{v_{0}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2}}{(c\tau+d)^{4}},\qquad{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}\mapsto\frac{{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}-\frac{2v_{2}{\rm d}v_{2}{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{3}}+\frac{v_{2}^{2}{\rm d}\tau^{2}}{(c\tau+d)^{4}},

\displaystyle 2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau\mapsto\frac{2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}+\frac{c(4v_{0}{\rm d}v_{0}-4v_{2}{\rm d}v_{2}){\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{3}}-\frac{c(2v_{0}^{2}-2v_{2}^{2}){\rm d}\tau^{2}}{(c\tau+d)^{4}}.

Then,

\displaystyle 2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau\mapsto\frac{2{\rm d}v_{0}^{2}-2{\rm d}v_{2}^{2}+2{\rm d}u{\rm d}\tau}{(c\tau+d)^{2}}.

∎

3.4

Theorem 3.7
.

Remark 3.9
.

Even thought the Dubrovin–Frobenius structure constructed in a suitable covering of the orbit space of

The constructions start with different hypotheses. Indeed, we derive a WDVV solution in the Hurwitz space framework from the data of the Hurwitz space itself, and through the choice of a suitable primary differential; see

The Hurwitz space construction is based on domain of a solution of a Darboux–Egorrof system. The coordinate system associated with this system of equation is called canonical coordinates.
