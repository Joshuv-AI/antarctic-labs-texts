Robust ab initio solution of the cryo-EM reconstruction problem at low resolution with small data sets
Aaditya V. Rangan, Leslie Greengard

Abstract
Single particle cryo-electron microscopy has become a critical tool in structural biology over the last decade, able to achieve atomic scale resolution in three dimensional models from hundreds of thousands of (noisy) two-dimensional projection views of particles frozen at unknown orientations. This is accomplished by using a suite of software tools to (i) identify particles in large micrographs, (ii) obtain low-resolution reconstructions, (iii) refine those low-resolution structures, and (iv) finally match the obtained electron scattering density to the constituent atoms that make up the macromolecule or macromolecular complex of interest. Here, we focus on the second stage of the reconstruction pipeline: obtaining a low resolution model from picked particle images. Our goal is to create an algorithm that is capable of ab initio reconstruction from small data sets (on the order of a few thousand selected particles). More precisely, we propose an algorithm that is robust, automatic, and fast enough that it can potentially be used to assist in the assessment of particle quality as the data is being generated during the microscopy experiment.



1
Introduction

In single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), a purified
preparation of particles (proteins or macro-molecular complexes)
is frozen in a thin sheet of ice, with each particle held in an unknown
orientation. Using a weak scattering approximation, the image
obtained can be viewed as a noisy projection of the particle’s
electron scattering density, which we denote by
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
with optical aberrations modeled by what
is called a contrast transfer function (CTF)

[
1
,
2
]
. The task at hand is to take the individual
particle images, determine their orientations, and recover a high
resolution characterization of
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
. This is typically accomplished
through the Fourier slice theorem

[
3
,
4
]
, which states that
the two-dimensional Fourier transform of each
projection image is a slice through
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
,
the
three-dimensional
Fourier transform of
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(modulated by the CTF).

The specific slice obtained from a single image
is determined by the orientation of the
individual particle. With many particles at many distinct orientations,

{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is well-sampled and
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
can be obtained through the
inverse Fourier transform. Thus, if the orientations are known,
the reconstruction problem is linear and easily solved.
The orientations, however, are not known, and
the reconstruction problem is both nonlinear and non-convex.
In practice, not only are the orientations unknown, but the particles
must be correctly centered in order to invoke the Fourier slice theorem.
The unknown displacement vector must also be learned for
each particle image.
We postpone a proper discussion of the mathematics to
section
2
and the appendices (Supporting Information).

From an experimental perspective, the initial stages of cryo-EM involve some
hours of imaging, resulting in the collection of multiple micrographs,
each containing a number of particle images.
After sufficiently many micrographs are collected, the next steps
involve particle picking
(identifying individual particles in the micrographs),
and the construction of a low- to medium-resolution molecule for further analysis
and refinement. The latter step typically involves
class averaging - finding particles that appear to have the same orientation
and taking the average in order to improve the signal-to-noise ratio, but some
algorithms proceed from the raw particle images themselves (as we do here).

Despite recent advances in sample preparation, imaging and analysis, the cost of data collection can still be
quite significant, and the availability of microscope time is a limited resource.
In order for the picked-particle images to be useful, they must meet certain criteria: each should contain a single well-centered particle with a minimum of clutter, and the full set must cover a wide variety of viewing angles, sufficient to determine the full three-dimensional structure.
Assuming that these criteria are met, these images (hopefully the least noisy and cleanest projection views)
can then be used in downstream analyses.
If the sample or the images are not of sufficient quality, it may be necessary to terminate the current experiment and repeat with a new sample, hoping to produce higher-quality particle images the next time.

To reduce the time and cost of such experiments, there are many quality-control tasks that would be useful to carry out as early as possible.
These include:

1.

determining whether any picked-particle image contains a reasonably well-centered and isolated molecule.

2.

estimating the viewing angles of the images (and checking that the range of viewing angles is sufficient to reconstruct the molecule).

3.

estimating the displacements/shifts required to more precisely center the images.

4.

estimating the image quality and the number of particles that will be needed to reconstruct the desired molecule at the desired resolution.

5.

estimating the molecular structure itself at low-resolution
(which feeds back to the estimates 1-4)

In current practice, many of these issues are addressed after carrying
out class averaging, which typically requires dozens of particles per class
and tens of thousands of picked particles, although a number of reconstruction algorithms work directly on the raw data.

In this paper, we describe an algorithm which can serve as a complement to more standard strategies.
Our algorithm operates on a relatively small set of picked-particle images (say, a few thousand), and attempts to directly reconstruct
(without class averaging)
a low-resolution version of the imaged molecule, along with estimates of the viewing-angles, shifts and quality of the images involved.
As we demonstrate below, our method is significantly faster, more accurate and more reliable than many existing
de-novo reconstruction algorithms.
We hope that it is robust enough to permit the assessment of data quality as the experiment is being carried out.
Moreover, our algorithm runs quickly and automatically with no ‘tuning’ required, allowing for multiple small pools of approximately 1000 picked-particles to be independently assessed without supervision
(each corresponding to
\sim 30
micrographs).
This would help set the stage for more robust statistical validation techniques such as
bootstrap or jack-knife estimation.

Remark 1

We should emphasize that the
starting point of our experiments here is a collection of picked particle images
(obtained from the EMPIAR database), not
the raw movies or entire micrographs that are sometimes deposited as well.
Particle picking is itself a non-trivial task.
Restricting our attention to molecules for which particles are curated
allows us to focus exclusively on the problem of reconstruction from a small
data set.

At its core, our algorithm is a modification of the simplest expectation-maximization method.
We use the framework of classical iterative refinement - alternating between a reconstruction step and an alignment step.
In the reconstruction step, we use the current estimate of the viewing angles for each particle/image and their displacements to approximate the molecule.
In the alignment step, we use the current estimate of the molecule to better fit the viewing angles and displacements for each image. We will refer to this approach as
alternating minimization
(AM).

It is well-known that standard AM does poorly in this setting; an initialization with randomized viewing angles typically fails to converge to a useful low-resolution approximation of the molecule.
To address these issues and to guide AM to a better structure, we introduce two changes:
(i) we compress and denoise the individual images by computing their
two-dimensional Fourier transform on a polar grid and focusing on carefully selected rings in the transform domain with maximal information content, and (ii) we increase the entropy of the viewing-angle distribution used during the reconstruction step (in a manner to be described in detail).
These modifications both reduce the computational cost and improve the robustness of AM in the low-resolution low-image-number regime.

In the numerical experiments below,
we show that our modified method (
alternating minimization with
entropy maximization and principal modes
or ‘EMPM’)
performs surprisingly well on several published data sets.
Our reconstructions are often on par with the best results one could expect from an ‘oracle-informed’ AM (i.e., standard AM starting with the ‘ground-truth’ viewing-angles and displacements for each image).
Moreover, our results are similar to (or better than) what one might expect from a reconstruction using ‘optimized’ Bayesian inference (i.e., using a ground-truth informed estimated noise level).
Importantly, our strategy is efficient, running about twenty times
faster than standard Bayesian inference or the
de-novo reconstruction in the widely used, open source package Relion

[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
We also make direct comparisons with two other open source
methods: the neural network approach in
cryoDRGN
[
8
]
and the recent low-resolution reconstruction pipeline in Aspire
[
9
]
.
We have not made a direct comparison with the stochastic gradient descent approach in
the GPU-based cryoSPARC package
[
10
]
.

In the remainder of this paper,
we briefly summarize the EMPM method and demonstrate its performance on a
variety of data sets.
The details of our method, as well as some additional motivating examples,
are deferred to the Supporting Information.
It is currently implemented for multi-core CPUs, but we believe it should be
straightforward to implement on GPUs as well, and to integrate into any of the
existing software packages for cryo-EM.

2
Overview of the EMPM iteration

Our general goal is to solve the following problem:
given a set of
N_{A}
picked particle images (2D projections)
from a handful of
micrographs (each with their own CTF),
estimate the unknown viewing-angles
\tau_{j}\in SO(3)
and displacements
\delta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
for each image,
as well as the underlying volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
,
where
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes a point in the Fourier transform domain.
In addition to determining the volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, we would also like
an estimate of the viewing-angle distribution
\mu_{\tau}
and
displacement distribution
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
(taken across the images).
If the estimates of the volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
and the distributions

\mu_{\tau}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
are
accurate estimates of the ground truth, they
will clearly be useful for quality control, determining (for example)
whether the experimental conditions are suitable for larger scale data
collection and subsequent high resolution reconstruction.

The main tool we employ is a form of
alternating minimization
(AM),
which carries out some number of iterations of the following two
step procedure:

Reconstruction:

Given the estimated viewing-angles
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}
and displacements
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}
for
N_{A}
2d picked-particle images
\{{\hat{A}}_{j}\}
, reconstruct the 3D molecular volume
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
(using the Fourier slice theorem and least squares inversion of the Fourier transform).

Alignment:

Given the reconstruction of the 3D molecule

{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
, update the estimates
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}

and
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}
.

In a standard AM scheme, the reconstruction step solves
for
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
using a least-squares procedure, and the
alignment step assigns the
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}
and

\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\}
using some kind of maximum-likelihood
procedure (see sections
A.18
and
A.22
in the Supporting Information).
While there are many more sophisticated strategies for molecular
reconstruction (see, for example,
[
10
,
8
]
),
we focus on AM for
a few reasons:
first and foremost is its simplicity, transparency and ease of automation.
AM has modular steps, few parameters, and is easily
interpretable.

At the same time, however, standard AM does have serious limitations.
First, it can be computationally expensive, particularly if a broad range
of frequencies and/or translations are considered in the alignment step.
Second, AM can fail to converge to the correct solution, especially
during the initial iterations, when the initial guess is far from the
true molecule.

To address these two issues, we propose a modified version of AM
which we refer to as
alternating minimization with entropy-maximization
and principal modes
(EMPM). The two main modifications we introduce are:

Maximum entropy alignment:

We use both the traditional
maximum likelihood alignment strategy, which finds the best estimate
for each image from the current molecular reconstruction, and its

opposite
. That is, we also use a
‘maximum-entropy’ alignment strategy: for each uniformly chosen
viewing angle, we
find the experimental image which best matches the corresponding projection
from the current structure. The subsequent reconstruction step makes use of
this uniform distribution of viewing angles

whether or not it corresponds to the true distribution
.
Use of this counter-intuitive step is important in stabilizing
our low resolution reconstruction from a small set of images
(see section
A.22
in the Supporting Information).

Principal-mode projection:

Each image is transformed onto a polar grid in Fourier space.
The data on each circle in
the transform domain at a fixed modulus
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|
will be referred to as a

Fourier ring
.
Rather than considering all Fourier rings when comparing
2D projections and image data,
we consider only a handful of carefully-chosen combinations of such rings,
which we refer to as
principal modes
.
This yields significant compression of the data
(and computational efficiency) while retaining information useful for
alignment (see section
A.13
in the
Supporting Information).

In the EMPM pipeline, we first estimate the principal-modes using
the images themselves.
Using these ‘empirical’ principal modes, we proceed with AM,
alternating between maximum-likelihood and maximum-entropy alignment.
Once a preliminary volume estimate has been obtained,
we use that estimate to recalculate the principal modes.
We then restart AM using the new principal images and, again,
alternate between maximum-likelihood and maximum-entropy alignment
until we have a final volume estimate.
The algorithm is summarized below (see Supporting Information for a more detailed description):

1.

Use the experimental images
{\hat{A}}_{j}
to calculate
‘empirical’ principal modes
U^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

2.

Compute the
principal images
, defined to be
the projection of the images onto principal modes,

P(U^{{\text{\tiny empir}}},{\hat{A}}_{j})
.

3.

Set random initial viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and set
initial displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
to
0
.

4.

Carry out 32 iterations of EMPM on the principal images.

(a)

Reconstruct
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
using
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

(b)

Use maximum-likelihood alignment to update
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

(c)

Reconstruct
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
using
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

(d)

Use maximum-entropy alignment to update
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

5.

Use
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
to calculate ‘estimated’ principal modes
U^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

6.

Set initial viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}=\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}={\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
.

7.

Carry out 32 iterations of EMPM on the new principal images

P(U^{{\text{\tiny estim}}},{\hat{A}}_{j})
.

(a)

Reconstruct
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
using
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

(b)

Use maximum-likelihood alignment to update
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

(c)

Reconstruct
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
using
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

(d)

Use maximum-entropy alignment to update
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

Note that, each time we update the displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
, we bound the maximum permitted displacement magnitude by
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}
(typically
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}\sim 0.1
in our dimensionless units).
In our studies with small amounts of moderately noisy
experimental data, we have observed that
the EMPM strategy is more effective than
Bayesian inference or maximum-likelihood alignment
alone - even with probabilistic alignment where a distribution of viewing angles is assigned to each image.
Roughly speaking, we believe this is due the fact that the incorrect viewing angles assigned in the
maximum-entropy step are washed out by destructive interference, while the
correctly assigned ones reinforce the true structure.
One simple model problem which illustrates these points is discussed in the Supporting Information (see sections
B
and
C
).
It is worth repeating, however, that we only propose this approach for obtaining a low-resolution initial guess. Existing refinement methods are still crucial for high resolution.

Once we have run our EMPM algorithm, we have the estimated volume
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
as output.
Using
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
as an approximation of
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, we can readily compute other quantities that may be of interest, such as
estimates of the true viewing angle distribution in the data set and other types of correlations.
We will use these quantities in our numerical experiments below to compare the results of EMPM to other methods, such as Relion’s de-novo molecular reconstruction and full Bayesian inference.

2.1
Templates, correlations, and ranks

For each centered molecular reconstruction
{\hat{F}}
, and set of
Euler angles
\tau=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, we define
the inner product
{\cal X}
and normalized inner product
\hat{\cal X}

of the reconstruction with the “ground-truth” model

{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
by

\displaystyle{\cal X}(\tau;{\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\rangle

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{\cal X}(\tau;{\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal X}(\tau;{\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})}{\|{\hat{F}}\|\cdot\|{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\|}\,.

(2)

Here
(\alpha,\beta)
denotes the viewing angle of an image with

\alpha
the azimuthal angle and
\beta
the polar angle.
\gamma
is
the final in-plane rotation (see section
A.6
).

{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\hat{F}}
is the volume obtained from

{\hat{F}}
using the rotation (the element of
SO(3)
) defined by
\tau
.
We define the volumetric correlation
{\cal Z}({\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})
by

{\cal Z}({\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})=\max_{\tau}\hat{\cal X}(\tau;{\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})\text{.}

(3)

This measurement
{\cal Z}({\hat{F}},{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})
represents the optimal
correlation (over all alignments) between the two
volumes
{\hat{F}}
and
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.

In order to compare our results with Relion’s de-novo reconstruction,
we will also need to calculate the ‘masked’ correlation

{\cal Z}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}({{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})
, defined as:

\displaystyle{\cal X}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}(\tau;{{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{{F}}\cdot{{G}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\rangle

(4)

\displaystyle\hat{\cal X}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}(\tau;{{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\cal X}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}(\tau;{{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})}{\|{{F}}\cdot{{G}}\|\cdot\|{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\|}

(5)

\displaystyle{\cal Z}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}({{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau}\hat{\cal X}^{{\text{\tiny mask}}}(\tau;{{F}},{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})\text{,}

(6)

where
{{G}}
is a sharp spherical mask (i.e., the indicator-function of a ball centered at the origin) with a radius determined by the support of
{{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.

We will also need to define inner products and correlations of single
images and volumetric slices. For this, given a Fourier space volume

{\hat{F}}
, we generate noiseless 2D projections (in the Fourier domain)
that incorporate the CTF, which we refer to as
templates
.
We denote these by

{\hat{S}}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;{\hat{F}})
,
where
\tau
is the specific set of Euler angles
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
is the displacement.
When assuming the displacement is zero, we will write these templates
as
{\hat{S}}(\tau;CTF;{\hat{F}})
,
Formally, we have

\displaystyle{\hat{S}}(\tau;CTF;{\hat{F}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left[{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\hat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{.}

(7)

where
CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})
is the given pointwise value of the CTF in Fourier space
and
{\hat{Sl}}
denotes taking the equatorial slice in the Fourier domain.
These CTF-modulated templates are used to calculate the inner products:

\displaystyle{\cal X}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\ {\hat{S}}(\tau;CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})\ ,\ {\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{{\hat{A}}_{j}}\ \rangle\text{,}

(8)

where
{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}})=e^{i{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}}
is the Fourier signature of displacement by
the vector
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
These are used, in turn, to calculate the correlations:

\hat{\cal X}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})=\frac{{\cal X}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})}{\|{\hat{S}}(\tau;CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})\|\cdot\|{\hat{A}}_{j}\|}\text{.}

For convenience, we generally reduce this to a function of the
viewing direction

\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}=\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
, the first two components of

\tau
, alone:

{\cal Z}(\alpha,\beta;{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})=\max_{\gamma,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\hat{\cal X}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}})\text{.}

(9)

The parameters
\gamma
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
are associated with a particular choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
implicitly through the
\operatorname*{arg\,max}
.
When the context is clear, we will often abuse notation and refer to
the correlations in (
9
) by

{\cal Z}(\alpha,\beta;{\hat{A}}_{j})
.

Finally, for each image
{\hat{A}}_{j}
and a set of
N_{S}
templates
defined by the viewing directions
(\alpha_{l},\beta_{l}),\ l=1,\dots,N_{S}
,
we can order the values

{\cal Z}(\alpha_{l},\beta_{l};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}}),\ l=1,\dots,N_{S}.

(10)

This yields what we will refer to as the
template rank
for the image.

Conversely, for each template defined by
(\alpha_{l},\beta_{l})
,
one can order the values

{\cal Z}(\alpha_{l},\beta_{l};{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}}),\ j=1,\ldots,N_{A}.

(11)

This defines the
image rank
for that template.
Using the terminology of linear algebra, we can define the

N_{S}\times N_{A}
matrix

{\cal Z}
with

{\cal Z}_{lj}={\cal Z}(\alpha_{l},\beta_{l};{\hat{A}}_{j}).

Template-ranking then corresponds to sorting the rows of
{\cal Z}
while
image-ranking corresponds to sorting the columns.

3
Results

We will consider a number of examples in this paper, drawn from the
electron-microscopy image archive (EMPIAR) - namely,
trpv1
,
the capsaicin receptor (the
transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1)
[EMPIAR-10005, emd-5778],

rib80s
, the Plasmodium falciparum 80S ribosome bound to emetine
[EMPIAR-10028, emd-2660],

ISWINCP
, the ISWI-nucleosome complex
[EMPIAR-10482, emd-9718],

ps1
, the pre-catalytic spliceosome
[EMPIAR-10180],

MlaFEDB
, a bacterial phospholipid transporter
[EMPIAR-10536, emd-22116],

LetB1
, the lipophilic envelope-spanning tunnel B protein from E. coli
[EMPIAR-10350, emd-20993],

TMEM16F
, a Calcium-activated lipid scramblase and ion channel in
digitonin with calcium bound
[EMPIAR-10278, emd-20244], and

LSUbl16dep
,
L17-Depleted 50S Ribosomal Intermediates, using an average of the published major classes as a reference
[EMPIAR-10076].
To limit the number of picked particles,
we carry out our numerical experiments on
subsets of
N_{A}=1024
images, spanning a small number

N_{\text{\tiny CTF}}
of micrographs.

We set
{K}=48
as the bandlimit for our low-resolution reconstruction, corresponding to a ‘best’ possible resolution of about
20
Å.
With these image pools, we construct a
variety of different ‘reference volumes’ for
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

Ground truth:

First, we center and restrict the published volume to the band-limit
{K}
.
This defines our ‘ground truth’ volume
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

Oracle:

Next, we measure the correlations

{\cal Z}(\tau;{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})
for a sufficiently sampled
set of Euler angles
\tau
and displacements and use maximum-likelihood to align the images to
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
. This assigns ‘ground-truth’
viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
and displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
to
each image. More precisely, we start by calculating
{\cal Z}
using a small
displacement disc of
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{{\text{\tiny local}}}}=0.03
, and then iterate multiple times,
with
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}=0.25
until the correlation between the reconstructed and true
volumes plateaus (this usually takes less than
8
iterations). We then
take the final viewing angles and displacements to define
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
. Given these ground-truth-based viewing angles and displacements, we use a least-squares reconstruction to compute
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny Oracle}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

Oracle-AM (OAM):

Starting with the ground-truth
viewing angles and displacements, as well as

{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny Oracle}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, we apply standard AM,
iterating until the resulting volume converges (this, again, usually
requires less than
8
iterations).
We define this final volume to be
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OAM}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
We expect the volume
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OAM}}}
to be close to the best
reconstruction one could realistically achieve with standard AM.

Oracle-Bayesian (OBI):

Next, we apply Bayesian inference reconstruction
(as described in
[
7
,
11
]
).
This reconstruction depends on (i) the initial starting volume,
(ii) the estimated noise level
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
,
(iii) the resolution
{K}
and range of displacements
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}
considered,
and (iv) the error tolerance used when calculating the integrals required
for estimating the posterior described in
[
7
]
.
We fix
{K}=48
and
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}=0.10
and set the error tolerance to the
same value used in the rest of our numerical experiments
(typically
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}=0.01
).
To create a reference volume associated with the ground truth,
we use
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
as the initial starting volume.
The resulting volume – denoted by

{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}})
– will depend on the noise-estimate
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
.
Note that, when
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}\rightarrow 0
we will have
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}})\rightarrow{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OAM}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, and when
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}\rightarrow\infty
we will have
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}})
converge to a spherically-symmetric distribution.
In our experience the quality of the reconstructed volume depends quite sensitively on the choice of
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
.
To build a good noise-estimate, we scan over several values of
\log(\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}})\in[-5,\ldots,0]
, choosing the
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
for which
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}})
has the highest correlation with
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
after the first iteration.
Fixing
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
to be this optimal value, we define
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):={\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}},\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}})
after iterating to convergence.
Thus,
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny OBI}}}
is close to the best reconstruction possible using Bayesian inference, assuming that the optimal estimated noise-level
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
is known ahead of time.

In addition to the above reference volumes, we also create several
de-novo molecular reconstructions. RDN and EMPM below are
oracle-free, while for Bayesian inference we use the optimal
noise level estimate
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
from
above.

We run the Bayesian inference reconstruction described in
[

11
]
to produce
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny BI}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
As mentioned above, this reconstruction depends on (i) the initial starting volume, (ii) the estimated noise-level
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}
, (iii) the resolution
{K}
and the range of displacements
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}
considered, (iv) the error-tolerance used when computing the integrals in the posterior, and (v) the number of iterations applied.
We fix the noise level
\sigma_{{\text{\tiny BI}}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
to be the value obtained in the Oracle-Bayesian method above.
We fix the resolution at
{K}=48
, set
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{\max}}=0.10
, set the error-tolerance to the same
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}
used in the other methods, and set the number of iterations at
16

For the initial starting volume we assign each of the available images a viewing angle randomly and uniformly, and then use a least-squares
reconstruction to generate a ‘random’ initial starting volume.
While random, this starting volume has roughly the same diameter and bandwidth as the true molecule.
We use the same procedure when initializing our strategy below.

This view of the data sheds light on the differences between maximum-likelihood and maximum-entropy alignment.
Recall that, in standard maximum-likelihood alignment,

sampled template direction.

However, when the estimated and ground-truth template ranks are poorly
correlated (as for

does very poorly, often converging to a volume that is worse than random.

Remark 2

Of note is that the EMPM method works reasonably well even when the particle orientations
are highly non-uniform (see Fig.
15
).
One might expect that the entropy-maximization
step (which forces images to be assigned to each viewing angle) would cause
difficulties. For de novo low- to moderate-resolution reconstructions, however, it
appears that EMPM benefits from the prevention of spurious clustering
that can develop in pure AM iteration.
At higher resolution, we do not recommend the use of EMPM, as
the entropy-maximization step would result in a loss of resolution and is no longer
necessary once a good initial model has been created.

3.1
On-the-fly particle rejection

Focusing on the
ps1

As shown in Figs.

{\cal Z}(\alpha,\beta;{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny EMPM}}})

{\cal Z}(\alpha,\beta;{\hat{A}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})

Motivated by this observation, we define:

{\cal Q}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny EMPM}}}
as a proxy for the ‘true image-quality’
{\cal Q}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.
We see from Figs.

and
11

{\cal Q}_{j}
typically lies in the interval
[0.1,0.4]
.
It is reasonable to conjecture that
those images with a low estimated quality do not align well to the reconstructed molecule and could be discarded
when attempting to reconstruct the molecule.

with the first

dividing these images into

.

The average

for these single-batch runs is
\sim 0.67
(magenta line), with relatively little variation.

to sort the images into octiles (each with
1024

Those with the lowest quality scores define the first octile and
those with the highest quality scores define the eighth octile.
We now perform a reconstruction for each octile separately, with the

Note that the first few octiles produce reconstructions that are quite a bit worse than the typical reconstructions shown in the 1K column.

3.2
Molecules with poor performance on small data sets

While our results can be considered moderately successful for many of the molecules, our reconstructions for
LetB1
,
LSUbl17dep
and
MlaFEDB
using
1024

particles are typically quite mediocre.
For
LetB1
and
LSUbl17dep
, different runs of EMPM yielded
reconstructions that were quite variable.
We suspect that this inconsistency stems from some underlying
heterogeneity in the data set itself (see
[
12
]
).
For
MlaFEDB
, on the other hand, different
trials of EMPM yielded reconstructions
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny EMPM}}}
that were not
close to
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, but were often quite close to one another.
This consistency across trials suggests that the structures observed
in the typical reconstruction might actually exist in the image pool
we are using. A recurring motif in the reconstructions is that of a
cylindrical shape with a disjoint structure off to one side.
In this case, we suspect that the image pool contains artifacts
that are at least
partially responsible for the mediocre performance of EMPM
(as well as BI, RDN, DRG, ASP) for this molecule.

4
Discussion

As demonstrated above, the EMPM iteration (a modification of alternating-minimization) can produce a reasonable low-resolution reconstruction from a
small set of raw images.
As seen in Figs.
3
and
12
,
it is more robust than many of the currently used
de-novo molecular reconstruction techniques,
and at least as accurate as
Bayesian inference with an optimally chosen estimated noise level.
We did not make an exhaustive comparison with all available packages,
since our main purpose here is to provide a new approach which
is
only relevant in the low resolution regime
.
We believe that the maximum entropy step of EMPM accounts
for its success, effectively preventing spurious clustering
of assigned viewing-angles. It should also be noted that the principal mode reduction
described here can be used to accelerate image alignment and volume registration
in more sophisticated and higher resolution reconstruction algorithms.

EMPM also produces useful estimates of image quality and viewing angle
distribution – even when the image pool only contains a few thousand
images. These estimates could play a role in online quality-control:
identifying poor images to be discarded or possibly terminating imaging
sessions that seem to be yielding poor quality data.

Because EMPM is efficient
(our MATLAB implementation requires less than 5% of the computation time of RDN and Bayesian inference), it should make cross-validation more compelling.
Our reconstruction requires about 40 min., RDN requires about 20 hrs, full
Bayesian inference requires 40 hrs, cryoDRGN requires 5 hrs and Aspire (the fastest) requires
15 min. (all on the same laptop).
Strong correlation between volumes reconstructed from different subsets of images can
reasonably be interpreted as an indication that the data set is of high quality.
On a more speculative note, the image curation presented here uses only image-template
correlations. It is possible that image cross-correlations and the cross-correlations
of the residuals of the least-squares reconstruction step could
also be valuable in settings involving discrete or continuous heterogeneity.

Because EMPM does not require any fine-tuning of parameters, it should be
straightforward to incorporate into existing, widely-used, high-quality
software pipelines and we hope to do so
in the near future. The code is available from
[
13
]
.

Supporting Information

Various aspects of the EMPM iteration are described
here in greater detail. We begin with
the discretization of images and volumes
in the Fourier domain, and the techniques used for image alignment
and volumetric reconstruction.
Many of the techniques used require some specification of a ‘global
tolerance’
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}
, which could be considered a
user-specified parameter. In practice, it appears to
be sufficient to set it to
10^{-2}
, corresponding
to two digits of accuracy.
In our experiments, we actually scan over
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}\in[0.001,0.01]
but find that
the quality of the reconstruction does not improve markedly for smaller values of
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}
. In short, EMPM
is not limited by the global tolerance, but presumably
by the experimental error and the structural features of the nonconvex landscape over which we are trying to optimize.

A.1
Manipulating images in the Fourier domain

We use
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
to represent spatial position and frequency, respectively.
In polar-coordinates these vectors can be represented as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\cos\theta,x\sin\theta)

(12)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k\cos\psi,k\sin\psi)\text{,}

(13)

for appropriately chosen angles
\theta
and
\psi
.

Definition 1

The Fourier transform of a two-dimensional function

A\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined as

{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}A({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}~.

(14)

We recover
A
from
{\hat{A}}
using the inverse Fourier transform:

A({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{k}}}\text{.}

(15)

The inner-product between two functions
A,B\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is written as

\langle A,B\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}A({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\dagger}B({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}~,

(16)

where
z^{\dagger}
is the complex conjugate of
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
We will also use Plancherel’s theorem
[
14
]
,

\langle A,B\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\langle{\hat{A}},{\hat{B}}\rangle~,\qquad\forall A,B\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})~\text{.}

(17)

We will ignore the factors of
2\pi
associated with the Fourier transform when they are not relevant.

We represent any given picked-particle image as a function
A\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with values corresponding to the image intensity at each location.
As a consequence of Plancherel’s theorem, any inner-product between
A
and another image (or 2D function)
B
can be calculated in either real or
frequency space, the latter of which is often more convenient when aligning images to projections of a volume
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

Abusing notation slightly, we will refer to
A({\boldsymbol{k}})
and
{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
in polar coordinates as:

\displaystyle A(x,\theta)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{x}})=A(x\cos\theta,x\sin\theta)

(18)

\displaystyle{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\hat{A}}(k\cos\psi,k\sin\psi)\text{.}

(19)

With this notation each
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
for fixed
k
and
\psi\in[0,2\pi)
corresponds to a ‘ring’ in frequency space with radius
k
.

A.2
Rotation and translation

Using the notation above, a rotation
{\cal R}(\gamma)
of an image
A
by angle
\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
can be represented as:

{\cal R}(\gamma)\circ A(x,\theta):=A(x,\theta-\gamma)\text{.}

(20)

Since rotation commutes with the Fourier transform, we have:

\widehat{\left[{\cal R}(\gamma)\circ A\right]}(k,\psi)={\cal R}(\gamma)\circ{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)={\hat{A}}(k,\psi-\gamma)\text{.}

(21)

In this manner, a rotation of any image by
+\gamma
can be represented as an angular-shift of each image-ring by
\psi\rightarrow\psi-\gamma
.

Likewise, a translation
{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}})
of an image
A
by the shift vector
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
can be represented as:

{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}})\circ A({\boldsymbol{x}}):=A({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\text{.}

(22)

In the Fourier domain, this action
can be expressed as

\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(23)

where the translation kernel
\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}})
is

\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}}):=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}}\text{.}

(24)

A.3
Fourier-Bessel coefficients

Over the course of our molecular reconstructions, we calculate many
image-image inner products (see section
2.1
).
To ease the computational burden associated with these calculations,
it is convenient to use the Fourier-Bessel basis

[
15
,
18
,
16
,
17
]
.

To define the Fourier-Bessel coefficients of an image, recall that each image-ring
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
(for fixed
k
) is a
2\pi
-periodic function of
\psi
, which can itself be represented as a Fourier series in
\psi
:

{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{a}(k;q)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}q\psi}\text{,}

(25)

for
q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The Fourier-Bessel coefficients
{a}(k;q)
of the image ring
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
are given by

{a}(k;q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}~.

(26)

These coefficients can be represented in a more traditional fashion by recalling that the Bessel function
J_{q}(kx)
can be written as:

J_{q}(kx)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi+\pi/2)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}~,

(27)

which, when combined with the definition of the Fourier transform,
implies that:

\displaystyle{a}(k;q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi-\theta)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}xdx\mathop{d\theta}~

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(\theta+\pi/2)}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi+\pi/2)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}xdx\mathop{d\theta}~

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(\theta+\pi/2)}J_{q}(kx)xdx\mathop{d\theta}~,

(30)

which is the inner product between the original image (in real space) and a
Bessel function.

Note that, using the Fourier-Bessel representation of an image, the rotation
{\cal R}_{\gamma}
can now be represented as:

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\gamma}{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\hat{A}}(k,\psi-\gamma)

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{a}(k;q)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}q(\psi-\gamma)}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{a}(k;q)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\gamma}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}q\psi}\text{,}

(33)

such that the Fourier-Bessel coefficients of the rotated image ring
{\cal R}_{\gamma}\circ{\hat{A}}(k,\cdot)
are given by the original Fourier-Bessel coefficients
{a}(k;q)
, each multiplied by the phase-factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\gamma}
.
Note that (
33
) easily allows for arbitrary rotations by any
\gamma
, requiring only a pointwise multiplication by the appropriate phase factor.
This observation allows for the efficient alignment of one image to another (see
[
16
,
17
]
).

A.4
Image discretization

We denote by
\Omega(1)
and
\Omega({K})
the ball of radius
1
and
{K}
, respectively, in either real or frequency space:

\Omega(1):=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mbox{~such~that~}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\leq 1\}\text{,}\quad\Omega({K}):=\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mbox{~such~that~}\|{\boldsymbol{k}}\|\leq{K}\}\text{,}

(34)

and we will assume that all the images considered are supported
in
\Omega(1)
. The support constraint

A({\boldsymbol{x}})\subseteq\Omega(1)
implies that
the representation
{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
will have a bandlimit of
1
, so
that
{\hat{A}}
can be accurately reconstructed from its values sampled on a frequency grid with spacing
\mathcal{O}(1)

[
19
]
.

We also assume that any relevant signal within the images has a maximum effective spatial-frequency magnitude of
{K}
; i.e., that the salient features of
{\hat{A}}
are concentrated in
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}
.
Consequently, we expect that the inversion

A({\boldsymbol{x}})\approx\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\iint_{\Omega_{K}}{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(35)

accurately reconstructs
A
to the resolution associated with
{K}

(ignoring high frequency ringing artifacts).
When these assumptions hold we won’t lose much accuracy when applying these transformations in a discrete setting (as discussed below).

Note that the largest
{K}
that we can reasonably expect to attain is related to the number of pixels ‘
N
’ in the original image.
More specifically, once we assume that
A({\boldsymbol{x}})
is supported in
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(1)
, the pixel-spacing will be
{\Delta x}=2/N
, implying the Nyquist spatial-frequency is given by
\pi/{\Delta x}=N\pi/2
.

Since we are using
N_{A}
picked-particle images
A_{j}
to produce a low-resolution estimate of the imaged molecule (denoted
F
below),
we will typically choose
{K}
to be significantly smaller than
N\pi/2
, often in the range of
{K}\sim 50
.
Given the pixel spacing, this corresponds to a low-resolution reconstruction with approximately
20
Åresolution.
Additionally, the fact that
x\leq 1
implies that the Bessel coefficients
{a}(k,q)
will be concentrated in the range
|q|\lesssim k
, meaning that the Bessel coefficients
{a}(k,q)
across all
k\in[0,{K}]
will be concentrated in
q\in[-{Q}/2,+{Q}/2-1]
for
{Q}=\mathcal{O}({K})=\mathcal{O}(N)
.

With the notation above, we can consider an
N\times N
image as a discrete set of pixel-averaged samples within
[-1,+1]^{2}
:

A_{n_{1},n_{2}}=\frac{1}{{\Delta x}^{2}}\int_{n_{1}{\Delta x}}^{(n_{1}+1){\Delta x}}\int_{n_{2}{\Delta x}}^{(n_{2}+1){\Delta x}}A(x_{1},x_{2})\,dx_{2}dx_{1}

(36)

for indices
n_{1},n_{2}\in\{0,\ldots,N-1\}
.
We approximate the Fourier transform
{\hat{A}}
at any
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
via the simple summation:

{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})=({\Delta x})^{2}\sum_{n_{2}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{N-1}A_{n_{1},n_{2}}e^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}}~\text{,}

(37)

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
is the appropriately-chosen pixel-center
{\Delta x}\left(n_{1}+\frac{1}{2},n_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)
.
Because we have assumed that the image is sufficiently well sampled (i.e., that
{\hat{A}}
contains little relevant frequency-content above the Nyquist-frequency
{K}
), we expect the simple sum above to be accurate.

We will typically evaluate
\hat{A}({\boldsymbol{k}})
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}
on a polar-grid, with
k
- and
\psi
-values corresponding to suitable quadrature nodes,
using the non-uniform FFT (NUFFT) to compute
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
at those nodes
(see
[
19
,
17
]
).
The quadrature in
k
corresponds to a set of
{R}
nodes:

k_{1},\ldots,k_{{R}}
and radial weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{{R}}
so that

\int_{0}^{{K}}g(k)kdk\approx\sum_{r=1}^{{R}}g(k_{r})w_{r}

(38)

to high accuracy for a smooth function
g(k)
.
The exact radial quadrature nodes we select are usually those inherited from a volumetric grid (see section
A.6
below).
For convenience, we will sometimes refer to the weight function

w(k)
as some continuous interpolant of the quadrature-weights,
with
w(k_{r})=w_{r}
.
The
{Q}
angular-nodes
\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{{Q}-1}
are equispaced in the periodic interval
[0,2\pi)
, with a spacing of
{\Delta\psi}=2\pi/{Q}
, and
\psi_{q^{\prime}}=q^{\prime}{\Delta\psi}
.
These equispaced
\psi
-nodes allow for spectrally accurate trapezoidal quadrature in the
\psi
-direction, and we approximate the Fourier-Bessel coefficients of each image-ring
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
as follows:

{a}(k,q)\approx\sum_{q^{\prime}=0}^{{Q}-1}{\hat{A}}(k,\psi_{q^{\prime}})\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}q\psi_{q^{\prime}}\right){\Delta\psi}\text{,}

(39)

with the index
q
considered periodically in the interval
[-{Q}/2,\ldots,+{Q}/2-1]
(so that, e.g., the
q
-value of
{Q}-1
corresponds to the
q
-value of
-1
).

A.5
Inner products

The inner-product between an image
A
and a rotated and translated version of image
B
is denoted by:

\displaystyle{\cal X}(\gamma,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};A,B)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\langle{\cal R}(\gamma)\circ A,{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}})\circ B\rangle\text{,}

(40)

and (ignoring factors of
2\pi
) is approximated by:

\displaystyle{\cal X}(\gamma,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\hat{A}},{\hat{B}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\iint_{\Omega({K})}\left[{\cal R}(\gamma)\circ{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]^{\dagger}\cdot\left[\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{B}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(41)

The expression
{\cal X}
can be thought of as a
bandlimited
inner product.

A.6
Volume notation

We use
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
to represent spatial position and frequency, respectively.
In spherical coordinates, the vector
{\boldsymbol{k}}
is represented as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{k}},\text{\ \ with\ \ }\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}=(\cos\phi\sin\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\text{,}

(42)

with polar angle
\theta
and azimuthal angle
\phi
representing the unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
on the surface of the sphere
S^{2}
.

Using a right-handed basis, a rotation about the third axis by angle
\alpha
is represented as:

{\cal R}_{z}(\alpha)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha&0\\
+\sin\alpha&+\cos\alpha&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)\text{,}

(43)

and a rotation about the second axis by angle
\beta
is represented as:

{\cal R}_{y}(\beta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+\cos\beta&0&+\sin\beta\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\beta&0&+\cos\beta\end{array}\right)\text{.}

(44)

A rotation
{\cal R}(\tau)
of a vector
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be represented by the vector of Euler angles
\tau=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
:

{\cal R}(\tau)\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}={\cal R}_{z}(\alpha)\circ{\cal R}_{y}(\beta)\circ{\cal R}_{z}(\gamma)\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(45)

We represent a given volume as a function

F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with values corresponding to the intensity
at each location
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega(1)
.
We will refer to
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
in spherical coordinates as
{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
With this notation, each
{\hat{F}}(k,\cdot)
corresponds to a ‘shell’ in frequency-space with radius
k
.
The rotation of any volume
{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
corresponds to the function
{\hat{F}}(k,{\cal R}(\tau^{-1})\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
, where
\tau^{-1}
corresponds to the vector of Euler angles

\tau^{-1}=\left(-\gamma,-\beta,-\alpha\right)
.

A.7
Spherical harmonics

Using the notation above, we can represent a volume
{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
as:

{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{m=+l}{f}(k;l,m)Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(46)

where
Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
represents the spherical harmonic of degree
l

and order
m
:

Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=Z_{l}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}m\phi}P_{l}^{m}(\cos\theta)\text{,}

(47)

with
P_{l}^{m}
representing the usual (unnormalized) associated Legendre
function, and
Z_{l}^{m}
the normalization constant:

Z_{l}^{m}=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\times\frac{\left(l-|m|\right)!}{\left(l+|m|\right)!}}\text{.}

(48)

The coefficients
{f}(k;l,m)
define the spherical harmonic expansion of the
k
-shell
{\hat{F}}(k,\cdot)
.

Given a rotation
\tau=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
,
we can represent the rotated volume
{\hat{G}}:={\cal R}(\tau){\hat{F}}
as:

{g}_{l}^{m_{1}}=\sum_{m_{2}=-l}^{m_{2}=+l}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{1}\gamma}d_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{l}(\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{2}\alpha}{f}_{l}^{m_{2}}\text{,}

(49)

where
d_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{l}(\beta)
represents the
Wigner d-matrix of degree
l
associated with the second
Euler angle
\beta
.

A.8
Volume discretization

As with our discretization of images, we assume that the volume
F({\boldsymbol{x}})
is supported in
\Omega(1)
, and that the relevant frequency content
is contained in
\Omega({K})
.

We then discretize the radial component of
\Omega({K})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
using a Gauss-Jacobi quadrature for
k
built with a weight-function corresponding to a radial weighting of
k^{2}dk
; the number of radial quadrature nodes
{R}
will be of the order
\mathcal{O}({K})
.
On each of the
k
-shells, we build a quadrature for
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
that discretizes the polar-angle
\beta
using a legendre-quadrature on
[0,\pi]
, and the azimuthal-angle
\alpha
using a uniform periodic grid in
[0,2\pi)
, with associated weights designed for integration on the surface of the sphere; the total number of spherical-shell-quadrature-nodes
{T}(k_{r})
for any particular
k_{r}
need only be
\mathcal{O}(k_{r}^{2})

[
16
]
.

Each of the
k
-shells
{\hat{F}}(k_{r},\cdot)
can be accurately described using spherical harmonics of order
l=\mathcal{O}(k_{r})
.
Thus, the number of spherical-harmonic coefficients required for each shell
{\hat{F}}(k_{r},\cdot)
is
\mathcal{O}(k_{r}^{2})
.
The total number of spherical-harmonic coefficients required to approximate
{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
over
\Omega({K})
is
\mathcal{O}({K}^{3})
, with a maximum degree of
{L}=\mathcal{O}({K})
.
The associated maximum order will be
{M}=1+2{L}
, which is also
\mathcal{O}({K})
.

A.9
Template generation

Given any volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, we can generate templates efficiently using the techniques described in
[
16
]
, which make use of the spherical harmonic representation
{f}(k;l,m)
.
For our purposes, we generate the templates described in Eq.
7
for a collection of viewing directions

(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
, for
t\in\{1,\ldots,{T}\}
.
The particular choice of the third Euler angle (
\gamma_{t}
) for each of
the templates is dictated by the strategy for template generation
in
[
16
]
, and does not play an important role,
since it corresponds to an in-plane rotation of the relevant slice.
We discretize the set of viewing directions
to correspond with the
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
discretization on the largest
k
-shell in
our volumetric (spherical) quadrature grid:
that is,
{T}:={T}(k_{{R}})
.
These viewing directions will be used to enumerate the templates (i.e.,
N_{S}={T}
).
Each of the templates are stored on a polar or Fourier-Bessel grid
identical to those used to store the images.

A.10
Image noise model

We assume that each of the picked-particle images
{\hat{A}}_{j}
is a noisy version of a ‘signal’ corresponding to a 2-dimensional projection of some (unknown) molecular electron-density-function
F^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
:

{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{S}}(\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}};CTF;{\hat{F}})({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(50)

where
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
correspond to the true (but unknown) viewing-angle and displacement associated with image
{\hat{A}}_{j}
.
In this context we say the image’s ‘true viewing direction’ is defined by the first two Euler angles in
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, namely the azimuthal and
polar angles
\alpha_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
and
\beta_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.

We will model the noise in real space as independent and identically
distributed (
iid
), with a variance of
\sigma^{2}
on a unit scale in
two-dimensional real space.
This is a simple model of detector noise

[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
, and does not
take into account more complicated features, such as structural noise
associated with image preparation (see
[
24
]
).
We assume that the image values are the sum
of the signal plus noise:

A_{n_{1},n_{2}}=A({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n_{1},n_{2}})=A^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}+A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}\text{,}

(51)

where the signal and noise are represented by the arrays
A^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
and
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
, respectively.
Because each entry in
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
corresponds to an average over the area-element
{\Delta x}^{2}
associated with a single pixel, we expect the variance of each
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
to scale inversely with
{\Delta x}^{2}
.
That is to say, we’ll assume that each element of the noise array
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
is drawn from the standard normal distribution
{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\Delta x}^{2}}\right)
, with zero mean and a variance of
\sigma^{2}/{\Delta x}^{2}
.
With these assumptions, downsampling the image by averaging neighboring pixels will correspond to an increase in the effective pixel-size and a simultaneous reduction in the variance of the noise associated with each (now larger) pixel.

Given the assumptions above,
{\hat{A}}
will be modeled by:

{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})+{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(52)

where
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
is now complex and
iid
.
Because the noise-term
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
is real, the noise-term
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
will be complex, with the conjugacy constraint that
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}(+{\boldsymbol{k}})={\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})^{\dagger}
.

A.11
Variance Scaling

We define
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\Delta x}^{2}}
as the
variance on a unit scale in two-dimensional frequency space.
We expect that the noise term
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
integrated over any
area element
{\Delta k}^{2}
will have a variance of

\frac{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\Delta k}^{2}}
.
Recalling our polar quadrature described above, we typically sample

{\boldsymbol{k}}
along
{R}
radial quadrature nodes
k_{1},\ldots,k_{{R}}

and
{Q}
angular quadrature nodes
\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{{Q}-1}
,
with radial and angular weights
w_{r}
and
{\Delta\psi}
, respectively.
In this quadrature scheme, each quadrature node

{\boldsymbol{k}}_{rq}=(k_{r},\psi_{q})
is associated with the area element

w_{r}{\Delta\psi}
, which approximates the ‘
kdk
’ integration weight.
Consequently, we expect that the noise term
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}_{rq})

evaluated at
(k_{r},\psi_{q})
on our polar quadrature grid will have
a variance of
\frac{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}
.

Given the radially-dependent variance associated with each degree of freedom in the images, we will typically consider rescaled images
{\hat{A}}(k_{r},\psi_{q})\sqrt{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}
, rather than the raw image values
{\hat{A}}(k_{r},\psi_{q})
.
Fro simplicity, we will often write this as
{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\sqrt{w(k)}
, where we assume that
{\Delta\psi}
is the same for every
k
-value, and
w(k)
is some
functional interpolant of the quadrature weights with
w(k_{r})=w_{r}
.
This rescaling allows us to equate the standard vector 2-norm with the

L^{2}
integral (up to quadrature error):

\sum_{r=1}^{r={R}}\sum_{q=1}^{q={Q}}\|{\hat{A}}(k_{r},\psi_{q})\cdot\sqrt{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}\|^{2}\approx\int_{k=0}^{k={K}}\int_{\psi=0}^{\psi=2\pi}\|{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)\|^{2}kdkd\psi\text{.}

This rescaling will allow our definition of the radial objective function
below to correspond to a standard log-likelihood
(see (
55
) in section
A.14
).
In a similar fashion, the least-squares formulation we will use for
volume reconstruction corresponds to a maximum-likelihood estimate,
with similar properties holding for the inner products used for alignment
(see sections
A.18
and
A.22
below).

A.12
Radial Principal Modes

In these sections, we briefly review the notion of
radial principal modes
for image compression, described in
[
25
]
.
The premise is simple: given an image
{\hat{A}}(k,\psi)
,
not all the frequency rings
{\hat{A}}(k,\cdot)
are equally useful.
By selecting the appropriate linear combinations of frequency rings,
we can compress the images while retaining information useful for alignment.
We choose the coefficients of this linear combination by solving
an eigenvalue problem, which is why we refer to these linear combinations as principal modes.

This strategy has two benefits.
The first is to make the overall computation more efficient; rather than
retaining all
{R}\approx 50
radial quadrature nodes,
we can often compress the majority of relevant information into a smaller
number of principal modes (typically 10-16).
The second benefit is that, by retaining only those principal modes which
are useful for alignment, we explicitly avoid those degrees of freedom
within the original images that are dominated by noise and not useful for
alignment. The same set of modes can be used for
the experimental images and the estimated volumes.
We make use of this compression for both the alignment and
reconstruction steps of our EMPM iteration.

A.13
Principal image rings and principal volume shells

Let us assume that we are given a unit vector

{\boldsymbol{u}}={[u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{{R}}]}^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{{R}}
.
We define the linear combination
[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}](\psi_{q})
as:

\displaystyle[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}](\psi_{q})=\sum_{r=1}^{{R}}u_{r}{\hat{A}}(k_{r},\psi_{q})\sqrt{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}\text{,}

(53)

where the rescaling factor
\sqrt{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}
is designed to equalize the variances associated with the different
k
-values (see section
A.11
).

In a moment, we will choose our
{\boldsymbol{u}}
to be one of the eigenvectors of
an
{R}\times{R}
matrix, and we will refer to the linear combination
[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}](\psi_{q})
as the ‘principal image ring’ associated with

{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Note that, based on our noise model, we assume that the image noise
is
iid
across image-rings.
Consequently, the variance of the noise
[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}](\psi_{q})
is equal to
\sum_{r}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}^{2}{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
, which is equal to
\|{\boldsymbol{u}}\|^{2}{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
.
Because
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is a unit vector, this last expression is simply
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
, which is the same as the variance of any individual term
{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}(k_{r},\psi_{q})
for any particular
k_{r}
.
Moreover, if we consider two orthonormal vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
, then
[{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}](\psi_{q})
and
[{{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}](\psi_{q})
will be independent random variables, each drawn from
{\cal N}(0,{\hat{\sigma}}^{2})
.

The same strategy can be used to define a principal volume shell

[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}](\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
as:

\displaystyle[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}](\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})=\sum_{r=1}^{{R}}u_{r}{\hat{F}}(k_{r},\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})\sqrt{w_{r}{\Delta\psi}}\text{,}

(54)

using the same weights as those used for the principal image rings.
In most situations, we will assume that the angular discretization

{\Delta\psi}
is uniform across all the image rings, allowing us to
ignore the
\sqrt{{\Delta\psi}}
factor in the rescaling-factor above.

A.14
Choice of principal modes

To select the actual principal modes used above,
we construct the following objective function for the vector
{\boldsymbol{u}}
:

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{u}};F,\mu_{\tau},\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}},\mu_{CTF})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\cdots\int\left\|{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{S}}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF,F)-{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\hat{S}}(\tau^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime};CTF^{\prime},F)\right\|^{2}\cdot

(55)

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055ptd\psi d\mu_{\tau}\left(\tau\right)d\mu_{\tau}\left(\tau^{\prime}\right)d\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\right)d\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime}\right)d\mu_{CTF}(CTF)d\mu_{CTF}(CTF^{\prime})\text{,}

where

{\hat{S}}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;F)
is the
usual noiseless image template associated with viewing-angle
\tau
,
displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
and the contrast-transfer-function
CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
The integral in (
55
) is taken over the distributions of viewing angle, displacement and CTF function
(i.e.,
\mu_{\tau}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
and
\mu_{CTF}
, respectively).
Given a volume
F
, as well as distributions
\mu_{\tau}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
and
\mu_{CTF}
, the objective function
C({\boldsymbol{u}})
is (up to an affine transformation) equal to the negative log-likelihood of mistaking one noiseless principal image ring (constructed using principal-vector
{\boldsymbol{u}}
) for a randomly rotated version of another (also constructed using
{\boldsymbol{u}}
).
Those vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}
for which
C({\boldsymbol{u}})
is high correspond to linear combinations of frequencies which are useful for alignment (i.e. for which the typical principal image rings look very different from one another).
Conversely, those vectors
{\boldsymbol{u}}
for which
C({\boldsymbol{u}})
is low correspond to linear combinations of frequencies which are not useful for alignment (i.e., for which the typical principal image rings look quite similar to one another).

The objective function
C({\boldsymbol{u}})
is quadratic in
{\boldsymbol{u}}
, which means that it can be optimized by finding the dominant eigenvector of the Hessian
\boldsymbol{C}:=\partial_{{\boldsymbol{u}}{\boldsymbol{u}}}C
, defined as the
{R}\times{R}
matrix:

C({\boldsymbol{u}})={{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}\cdot\boldsymbol{C}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}\text{.}

Moreover, the corresponding eigenvalue will be equal to the
objective function
C
for that eigenvector.
To actually compute the Hessian
\boldsymbol{C}
in practice,
we use one of two strategies.

A.15
Empirical principal modes

At first, when we have no good estimate of either
F
or the
distributions
\mu_{\tau}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
or
\mu_{CTF}
, we simply
use the images
A_{j}
themselves to form a Monte Carlo estimate of the
integral in (
55
).
This boils down to the following simple approximation:

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{u}})^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{A}^{2}}\sum_{j,j^{\prime}}\iiint\left\|{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\cal R}(\gamma){\hat{A}}_{j}-{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\intercal}{\cal R}(\gamma^{\prime}){\hat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}d\psi d\gamma d\gamma^{\prime}\text{,}

(56)

where we have replaced the noiseless images

{\hat{S}}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;F)
and
{\hat{S}}(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;F)

in the integrand of Eq.
55
with randomly selected images

{\hat{A}}_{j}
and
{\hat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}
from the original image pool, and used the
empirical distribution of the images themselves to stand in for the
unkown
\mu_{\tau}
,
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
and
\mu_{CTF}

(after averaging over the in-plane rotations associated with

\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
).
The associated kernel
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
then reduces to:

\boldsymbol{C}(r,r^{\prime})^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}\propto\sqrt{w_{r}w_{r^{\prime}}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{N_{A}}\sum_{j}\sum_{q}{a}_{j}(k_{r},q)^{\dagger}{a}_{j}(k_{r^{\prime}},q)-\frac{1}{N_{A}^{2}}\left[\sum_{j}{a}(k_{r},0)\right]^{\dagger}\left[\sum_{j}{a}(k_{r^{\prime}},0)\right]\right)\text{.}

(57)

A.16
Estimated principal modes

Once we have a reasonable estimate of the imaged volume
F^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
, we can use it (along with the current estimated distributions
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
) to calculate
C^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
via (
55
).
In practice, we typically assume that
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
is uniform and
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
is an isotropic Gaussian centered at the origin
with a standard deviation estimated using the current
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.
With these assumptions the associated kernel
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
can be computed analytically from the spherical harmonic coefficients
{f}(k;l,m)
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{r,r^{\prime}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle+\sqrt{W_{r}W_{r^{\prime}}}\cdot\left[\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{m=+l}{f}(k_{r};l,m)^{\dagger}{f}(k_{r^{\prime}};l,m)\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{+}(k_{r},k_{r^{\prime}})

(58)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{W_{r}W_{r^{\prime}}}\cdot\left[{f}(k_{r};0,0)^{\dagger}{f}(k_{r^{\prime}};0,0)\right]\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{-}(k_{r})\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{-}(k_{r^{\prime}})\text{,}

(59)

as
{Q}\rightarrow\infty
,
where the CTF-modulated weight
W_{r}
takes the form:

\displaystyle\sqrt{W_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{w_{r}}}{N_{A}}\sum_{j}CTF_{j}(k_{r})\text{,}

(60)

and the terms
\boldsymbol{E}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{-}
denote

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{+}(k_{r},k_{r^{\prime}})=\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{2}}{2}\left[k_{r}^{2}+k_{r^{\prime}}^{2}\right]\right)\cdot\exp\left(k_{r}k_{r^{\prime}}\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{2}\right)\text{,}

(61)

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{-}(k_{r})=\tilde{k}_{r}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\exp\left(-\tilde{k}_{r}^{2}\right)\left({\cal I}_{-1/2}(\tilde{k}_{r}^{2})-{\cal I}_{+1/2}(\tilde{k}_{r}^{2})\right)\text{,}

(62)

where
\tilde{k}_{r}=k_{r}\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}/2
, and
{\cal I}_{q}
refers to the modified Bessel function of first kind of order
q
.

A.17
Multiple principal modes

We select the principal modes
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{H}
to be the dominant
H
eigenvectors of the kernel
\boldsymbol{C}
.
We typically choose
H
such that the
(H+1)
-th eigenvalue
of
\boldsymbol{C}
is less than the global tolerance times the first (dominant)
eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{C}
.
When we set the global tolerance to be
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}\sim 1e-2
in our numerical experiments below, this selection criterion typically corresponds to
selecting
12-16
principal modes from
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\text{\tiny empir}}}
, and slightly fewer (
10-14
) from
\boldsymbol{C}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.

We will refer to the matrix
U
as the collection of
H

principal modes:

U=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}u_{1}&u_{2}&\cdots&u_{H}\end{array}\right]\text{,}

(63)

and refer to the collection of respective principal images as
{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}
:

{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}{u_{1}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}&{u_{2}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}&\cdots&{u_{H}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}\end{array}\right]\text{,}

(64)

\text{such that \ }\left[{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}\right](h,\psi)=\left[{u_{h}}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}\right](\psi)\text{.}

We use the same convention for principal volumes:

{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}{u_{1}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}&{u_{2}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}&\cdots&{u_{H}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}\end{array}\right]\text{,}

(65)

\text{such that \ }\left[{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}\right](h,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})=\left[{u_{h}}^{\intercal}{\hat{F}}\right](\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})\text{.}

We will also refer to the Bessel and spherical harmonic representations
of the principal images and principal volumes as
{U}^{\intercal}{a}
and
{U}^{\intercal}{f}
, respectively.

A.18
Volume reconstruction

Most approaches to estimating molecular volumes in cryo-em involve some kind of reconstruction process which is performed multiple times (over the course of iterative refinement).
For standard AM, the inputs to the reconstruction process are the collection of
N_{A}
picked particle images
{\hat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
and associated CTF-functions
CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, along with estimates of the viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.
The output is the estimate
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

In our case, we will make use of both the standard least-squares
reconstruction, as well as a more computationally efficient
approximate Fourier inversion which we refer to as
‘quadrature-back-propagation’ (section
A.20
below).

A.19
Least-squares reconstruction

The standard strategy for reconstruction typically involves solving a
linear system in a least-squares sense.
In real-space this least-squares system looks like:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}PSD_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\star{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1})\circ\left\{S\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
PSD_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\star{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}})\circ\left\{S\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot F({\boldsymbol{x}})\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})\\
\vdots\\
A_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\\
\end{array}\right]

whereas in the Fourier domain it is written as:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}CTF_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
CTF_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\hat{A}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\vdots\\
{\hat{A}}_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\end{array}\right]

or, more conveniently,

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}CTF_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
CTF_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\vdots\\
{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\end{array}\right]\text{.}

(66)

In this expression, the
N_{A}
viewing angles
\tau_{j}
and
displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}
are assumed to be given as input, as are the
image- or micrograph-specific CTF functions
CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
and
images
{\hat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
The unknown (to be determined) is the volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.

This least-squares problem is standard in maximum-likelihood estimation, and versions of this problem are solved in many software packages, often via conjugate gradient iteration applied to the
normal equations.

As written, the least-squares problem above has a block-structure: different
k
values are independent from one another.
Thus, the problem can be solved by determining
the
k
-shell
{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
from the
k
-rings
{\hat{A}}_{j}(k,\psi)
for one

k
value at a time.
However, the problem stated in (
66
) is slightly misleading,
as the residuals for each of the
k
values are not directly comparable
due to the fact that
different
k
-shells have different variances associated with them
(see section
A.11
).
To account for this, we rewrite (
66
) as:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sqrt{w(k)}\cdot CTF_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{w(k)}\cdot CTF_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sqrt{w(k)}\cdot{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{w(k)}\cdot{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\end{array}\right]\text{,}

(67)

where the function
w(k_{r})
is the radial quadrature weight.
With this formulation, the residual (calculated using the standard vector
2-norm) is (up to a quadrature error) equal to an integral representing
the log-likelihood that the volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
could have produced the
observed images
{\hat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
(under the assumptions about the
noise-model described in section
A.10
).

A.20
Principal-mode least-squares reconstruction

The same general methodology above can be applied to the principal
images directly.
To describe the modified least-squares system, we first define the
low-rank decomposition of the radial component of the CTF-functions.
Let
CTF(k,j)
correspond to the radial component of the CTF-function
CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Using the discretized analog of a functional SVD we represent

CTF(k,j)
by

\displaystyle CTF(k,j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h^{\prime}}u^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(k)\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(j)\text{,}

(68)

which we can truncate at rank
H^{\prime}
in accordance with the global
tolerance.
In the numerical experiments we present below, the image pools typically
span
30
or so micrographs, usually resulting in
H^{\prime}
about
2-3
.

Using this
H^{\prime}
-rank decomposition of the CTF-array
(across the image pool), we can approximately represent the noiseless
principal images as follows:

\displaystyle CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}\right\}({\boldsymbol{k}})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{H^{\prime}}v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(j)\sigma^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}\cdot u^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(\|{\boldsymbol{k}}\|)\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}\right\}({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{.}

(69)

After using the radial principal modes to compress both this approximation and the associated component of the right hand side of (
67
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{H^{\prime}}v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(j)\sigma^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}\cdot{u_{h}}^{\intercal}\left[u^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}\right\}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]={u_{h}}^{\intercal}\left[{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\text{.}

(70)

By defining the CTF-modulated principal volume:

{\hat{G}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}};h^{\prime},{\hat{F}})={U}^{\intercal}\cdot\left[u^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(k)\odot{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\text{,}

we can now write the compressed least-squared problem as:

\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{H^{\prime}}\sigma^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(1)\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(N_{A})\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\hat{G}}({\boldsymbol{k}};h^{\prime},{\hat{F}})\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{U}^{\intercal}\left[{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\\
\vdots\\
{U}^{\intercal}\left[{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]\\
\end{array}\right]\text{,}

(71)

where the radial reweighting of
\sqrt{w(k)}
in (
67
) is accounted for by our definition of
{U}^{\intercal}
.
Note that, when
H^{\prime}=H={R}
the equation above is equivalent to (
67
).
However, when
H^{\prime}<{R}
and
H<{R}
this will correspond to a projected version of (
67
).

Note also that, with this representation, we need not actually solve
for
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Instead, we can use the principal images (on the right hand side) to solve the above problem (in a least-squares sense) for the collection of CTF-modulated principal volumes
{\hat{G}}({\boldsymbol{k}};h^{\prime},{\hat{F}})
.
Solving (
71
) can be less computationally costly than solving (
67
) when the total number of degrees of freedom within the various
{\hat{G}}
is less than the total number of degrees of freedom within
{\hat{F}}
(i.e., when
H\times H^{\prime}<{R}
).
Even when this is not the case, the CTF-modulated principal volumes
{\hat{G}}
(the solution to (
71
)) can still be more accurate (in terms of comparison to the ground truth) than the solution
{\hat{F}}
to (
67
).
We see this improvement most acutely when the principal mode reduction
{U}^{\intercal}
effectively removes noise from the images
{\hat{A}}_{j}
(e.g., if there are many frequency rings which contain little to no information).

We remark that, when dealing with least-squares reconstruction and principal mode reduction, ‘the diagram commutes’.
That is, because the principal mode projection
{U}^{\intercal}
is orthonormal, the principal mode projection
{U}^{\intercal}\left\{CTF\cdot{\hat{F}}\right\}
(i.e., the principal projection of the full volume which solves the original least-squares problem in (
67
)) will be the same as the solution
\sum_{h^{\prime}}v^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}{\hat{G}}
to the principally-projected least-squares problem in (
71
), provided of course that the
H^{\prime}
-rank decomposition of the CTF-array is accurate (e.g., if
H^{\prime}
is taken to
{R}
).
Thus, if
H\times H^{\prime}
is indeed larger than
{R}
, then instead of solving (
71
), we can obtain
{\hat{G}}
more easily by just solving the original least-squares problem for
{\hat{F}}
and then projecting down to the CTF-modulated principal volumes
{\hat{G}}
.

Note also that, due to linearity, the noiseless images associated with the CTF-modulated principal volumes
{\hat{G}}
will approximately equal the noiseless principal images associated with the volume
{\hat{F}}
, with errors controlled by the truncation
H^{\prime}
of the CTF-values.
That is to say, a principal mode compression of (
69
) can be written as:

\displaystyle{U}^{\intercal}\left[CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}\right\}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{H^{\prime}}v^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}(j)\sigma^{\prime}_{h^{\prime}}\cdot\left\{{\hat{S}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{G}}({\boldsymbol{k}};h^{\prime},{\hat{F}})\right\}({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{.}

(72)

This relationship can be used to align the principal images
{U}^{\intercal}{\hat{A}}_{j}
directly to the solution
{\hat{G}}
, without necessarily needing to reconstruct
{\hat{F}}
(see section
A.22
).

Reconstruction via quadrature back propagation (QBP)

In our experience, an accurate approximation of the solution to the least-squares problem above can require a significant amount of computation time when the conjugate gradient method requires many iterations to converge.
As a more efficient (but typically less accurate) alternative, we employ a slightly different strategy.
We refer to this alternative as ‘quadrature-back-propagation’ (QBP).

The basic observation underlying QBP is that, assuming that there was no image noise and no CTF-attenuation, then the volume and images would solve:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\\
\vdots\\
{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{N_{A}})\circ\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\hat{F}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{1}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\vdots\\
{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{N_{A}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\hat{A}}_{N_{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\\
\end{array}\right]

with each template
{\hat{Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}
corresponding to a cross-section of the volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Each of these cross-sections can be viewed as a sample of the overall volume
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, with values given by the appropriate entry on the right-hand-side of the equation above.
With sufficiently many cross-sectional samples, a functional representation of the volume
{\hat{F}}
can be reconstructed via quadrature in the spherical harmonic basis via a spherical harmonic transform. That is, we have the approximate
formula

{f}(k;l,m)\approx\sum_{p}w_{p}\left[Y_{l}^{m}(\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{p})\right]^{\dagger}{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{p})\ \ \forall\ \ l,\|m\|,l^{\prime},\|m^{\prime}\|\leq{L}\text{.}

where
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{p},w_{p}\}
correspond to a quadrature rule on the sphere.
The number of such nodes is of the order

[\mathcal{O}(k)]^{2}
on the spherical
k
-shell.
(Equispaced points in the azimuthal direction and Legendre points in
the polar angle, for example, are spectrally accurate.)
Because the data points will not generally coincide with the necessary
quadrature nodes, we interpolate from the given data to the nodes
in some local neighborhood.
A simple rule would be to take the local average over all data points
that lie within some distance of the node.
Note that this strategy is not iterative and is
typically an order of magnitude (or so) faster than a least-squares
reconstruction.

In the absence of image-noise, the error in the reconstruction of
{f}(k;l,m)
will come from the ‘interpolation error’ associated with interpolating from the cross-sectional sample data to the quadrature nodes.
When the image-noise is nonzero and the interpolation regions
are fixed in size, then the approximation to
{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{p})
will exhibit two sources of error.
In addition to the interpolation error, there is a sampling error
due to the image noise at each cross-sectional sample-point.
In practice, we generally choosing the local neighborhood of the quadrature
nodes to be sufficiently large to contain roughly
\mathcal{O}(10^{1-2})
sample points. This is usually sufficient for two digits of accuracy (coinciding with our usual global error tolerance).

The above strategy can easily be generalized to deal with image-specific CTF-values.
The QBP approach above essentially treats each sample within the local
interpolation region of each quadrature node as a noisy observation of

{\hat{F}}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{p})
.
The average can be thought of as a local maximum-likelihood estimate.
Since different data points might correspond to different CTF functions,
we can simply compute the CTF-weighted local averages instead.
This strategy coincides with the CTF-weighting of Bayesian inference
described in
[
7
,
11
]
.

A.21
Remark regarding further template compression

If the ranks
H
and
H^{\prime}
are both sufficiently small
(e.g.,
2-4
and
1-2
, respectively), then the dimensionality of the
space of CTF-modulated principal templates (as measured via the numerical rank) is very small, and usually quite a bit smaller than the actual number of principal templates (i.e., the number of viewing-directions).
This would allow for additional compression, using a reduced set
of ‘basis’-templates (which span the space of templates) rather than the actual templates themselves.
In our numerical experiments we do not implement this compression because our
H
and
H^{\prime}
are typically too large for it to be efficient.

A.22
Image alignment

Just as for the reconstruction step, image alignment is often performed
multiple times during molecular refinement.
For standard AM, the inputs to the alignment process are the estimated
molecular volume
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
, as well as the picked-particle images
{\hat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
and associated CTF-functions
CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
The outputs are the estimated viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and displacements
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
for the various images.

In our case we will make use of both the standard maximum-likelihood alignment, as well as a more stable ‘maximum-entropy’ alignment (described below).

A.23
Alignment via maximum likelihood

For this, we calculate the quantities
{\cal X}
,
\hat{\cal X}
and
{\cal Z}
,
described in section
2.1
,
for a collection of
{T}=\mathcal{O}({K}^{2})
viewing directions
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
corresponding to our spherical shell quadrature grid
for
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
at
k=k_{{R}}\approx{K}
.
For each of these viewing-directions
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
, we consider a range of
O({K})
values for
\gamma
in
[0,2\pi)
.
For the displacements, we typically limit them to lie in a small disk of
radius
{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{{\text{\tiny local}}}}\sim 0.01
or so, taking advantage of the low numerical rank of the set of translation-operators within this disk

[
17
,
25
]
.

Given the correlations
{\cal Z}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t};{\hat{A}}_{j})
, a common strategy for updating the viewing angles
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
(referred to as ‘maximum-likelihood’ alignment), involves selecting the
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
(and, as a result, the
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
) for each image
j
to maximize
{\cal Z}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
over the
{T}
viewing directions for that particular image
j
.
In the ideal situation where the image formation model is accurate, this choice corresponds to the viewing-angle that is most likely to have produced the particular image
{\hat{A}}_{j}
, given that the molecule is indeed
{\hat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.
In practice we do not define
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
to be the very best
\alpha
and
\beta
for each image, instead randomly selecting an
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
for which
{\cal Z}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
is within the top
5^{\text{th}}
-percentile of its values for that image
[
16
]
.
The results of our numerical-experiments are quite insensitive to this value of
5\%
; any choice from
1\%
to
\sim 10\%
achieves similar performance for maximum-likelihood alternating minimization.

Let us define the function
{\text{\cal Sort}}[\cdot]
which maps lists of real-numbers to lists of positive integers such that, for any vector

\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
, the value of
{\text{\cal Sort}}[\boldsymbol{a}]_{l}
is
the (integer) rank of
a_{l}
(ranging from
1,\ldots,L
as it stands within the entries of
\boldsymbol{a}
).
As noted in section
2.1
,
we can see that the template ranks are obtained by applying
{\text{\cal Sort}}[\cdot]
to each ‘column’ of the array
{\cal Z}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t};{\hat{A}}_{j})
(fixing
j
), while the image ranks are obtained by applying
{\text{\cal Sort}}[\cdot]
to each ‘row’ of the same array (fixing
t
).
The maximum-likelihood alignment described above depends on the template ranks, while the maximum-entropy alignment (described below) depends
on the image ranks.

A.24
Alignment via maximum-entropy

The maximum-likelihood alignment described above can be thought of as a ‘greedy’ algorithm which selects the estimated viewing angles for each image without consideration of the choices made for the other images.
As a result of this greedy strategy, the estimated viewing angle distribution (across all
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
) is not controlled, and can end up becoming quite far from the true viewing-angle distribution (until the
volume used to generate the templates is sufficiently accurate).

To avoid this phenomenon, one can choose the
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
while constraining the distribution of estimated viewing angles in some way.
A simple special case of this strategy is to constrain the distribution of estimated viewing angles to be uniform.
We refer to such an alignment strategy as ‘maximum-entropy’ alignment
(referring to the entropy of
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
).
In practice we implement this maximum-entropy alignment as follows:

1.

Declare all the images ‘unassigned’.

2.

Define a list of
N_{A}
uniformly distributed viewing-directions
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}_{j}=\left(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}\right)
on the sphere.
(This is typically done by cycling through a randomly-permuted list of the
{T}
viewing-angles
(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t})
until
N_{A}
viewing-directions have been selected).

3.

Step through the list of viewing directions.
For each viewing-direction (defined by a particular
\alpha
and
\beta
), assign the image
{\hat{A}}_{j}
that has not previously been assigned and which maximizes
{\cal Z}(\alpha,\beta;{\hat{A}}_{j})
(over the unused image-indices
j
).
When assigning an image
{\hat{A}}_{j}
to a particular viewing direction
\alpha
,
\beta
, we set the in-plane rotation
\gamma_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
implicitly via the
\operatorname*{arg\,max}
(see the definition of
{\cal Z}
in Eq
9
).

Note that the third step of this procedure uses the image ranks for each template (rather than the template ranks for each image).
In some cases the image ranks and template ranks contain similar information (see, e.g., the results for ISWINCP in Fig
2
).
However, it is more typical for the image ranks to be more informative than the template ranks
(see the other case studies in Figs.
2
and
11
).

Intuition might suggest that maximum-entropy alignment is a reasonable
strategy if the true viewing angle distribution is known to be uniform,
and not otherwise.
Nevertheless, as illustrated in the main text, our EMPM iteration still often produces results that are significantly better than maximum-likelihood alignment, even for cryo-EM data where the true viewing angle distribution is rather nonuniform.
We study this behavior in some detail for simplified models
in sections
B
and
C
below.

Appendix B
Multi-Reference Alignment (MRA)

Multi-Reference Alignment (MRA) is an abstraction of
the cryo-EM molecular reconstruction problem, reduced to a two-dimensional
setting.

In this context the image displacements and CTF-modulation are typically ignored, and we assume (i) the imaged molecule itself is translationally invariant in the
x_{3}
-direction, and
(ii) the imaged molecule is only observed from the
x_{3}
-axis.
Because of (i), the function
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
will be determined by
{{F}}(x_{1},x_{2})
, and
{\hat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
will be restricted to the equatorial-plane, as denoted by
{\hat{F}}(k,\psi)
.
Additionally, due to (ii), each ‘image’
{\hat{A}}_{j}
will be a noisy version of
{\hat{F}}(k,\psi)
, subject to an unknown in-plane rotation
\gamma_{j}
:

{\hat{A}}_{j}(k,\psi)=[{\cal R}(\gamma_{j})\circ{\hat{F}}](k,\psi)+\text{noise}\ ={\hat{F}}(k,\psi-\gamma_{j})+\text{noise}.

If we further assume that the true volume
{\hat{F}}
involves only a
single
k
-shell, then each image is restricted to a single
k
-ring,
and can be considered a periodic function on
[0,2\pi]
.

The MRA problem has been well studied

[
26
,
27
,
28
]
,
and can be used to gain insight into the behavior of many
reconstruction strategies. It can be used, for example, to investigate
the sensitivity of Bayesian inference (BI) to mismatches between
the estimated and true noise levels (see Fig
6
).

Appendix C
Multi-Slice Alignment (MSA)

An ‘orthogonal’ sub-problem to MRA, which we refer to as Multi-Slice
Alignment (MSA), can be described as follows.
As in MRA, we ignore displacements and the CTF and assume that the volume is invariant in the
x_{3}
-direction.
However, in MSA, we assume that
the imaged molecule is only observed from the equatorial plane.
With this assumption the images will correspond to projections of
{{F}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
onto one-dimensional
lines in the
x_{1}
-
x_{2}
-plane (i.e., samples of the two-dimensional
Radon transform of
{{F}}(x_{1},x_{2})
).
Thus, retaining the notation
{\hat{A}}_{j}
, each image corresponds to
a one-dimensional slice of
{\hat{F}}(k,\psi)
, expressed as
a function of
k\in\mathbb{R}
for a specific (but unknown)
\psi_{j}
:

{\hat{A}}_{j}(k)={\hat{F}}(|k|,\psi_{j})+\text{noise}\text{\ if\ }k>0\text{,\ or\ \ }{\hat{F}}(|k|,\psi_{j}+\pi)+\text{noise}\text{\ if\ }k<0\text{.}

To simplify the scenario further, we restrict each image to the ray of positive
k
:

{\hat{A}}_{j}(k)={\hat{F}}(k,\psi_{j})+\text{noise}\quad\text{for $k>0$}\text{.}

If we further assume that the true volume
{{F}}
can take on arbitrary complex values, and that
{\hat{F}}
involves only a single
k
-shell, then
{\hat{F}}
corresponds to a closed-curve in
\mathbb{C}
, written as the function
{\hat{G}}(\psi)
, parametrized by
\psi
.
Each image is now simply a point, corresponding to a noisy observation of
{\hat{G}}(\psi)
.
If we assume that the true volume
{\hat{F}}
involves a discrete set of
{R}

k
-values
\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{{R}}\}
, then
{\hat{G}}
will be a closed curve in
\mathbb{C}^{{R}}
(once again parametrized by
\psi
), and each image will be a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{{R}}
corresponding to a noisy observation of
{\hat{G}}(\psi_{j})
.
An example illustrating the MSA problem for
{R}=1
is shown in
Fig
7
.

Numerical experiments:

We believe that MSA can be a useful testbed for studying the
performance of various strategies for cryo-EM reconstruction.
As a simple example, let
{R}\equiv 1
above and define

{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}:[0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
using a fixed number of
Fourier modes:

{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(\psi)=\sum_{q=-{Q}}^{+{Q}}{g}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(q)\cdot\exp(iq\psi)\text{,}

with the shape of
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
determined by the
2{Q}+1

coefficients
{g}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}_{q}
.
In the experiments below, we generate
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
randomly by drawing
each of the
{g}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}_{q}
from a (complex) Gaussian distribution with
variance
1
.

Having drawn a particular
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, we sample this
molecule at
N_{A}
points, producing a collection of
simulated data points
{\hat{A}}_{j}
defined by:

{\hat{A}}_{j}={\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(\psi_{j})+\epsilon_{j}\text{,}

where the
\psi_{j}
are drawn independently and uniformly from
[0,2\pi)
, and each
\epsilon_{j}
is an independent complex random variable drawn from a normal distribution with variance
\sigma
.

Now, given the collection of
N_{A}
images
\{{\hat{A}}_{j}\}
, we can try to reconstruct
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
using the strategies discussed in the main text.
Due to the simplicity of MSA, much of the apparatus described in the main text simplifies dramatically. For example, reconstruction involves a subsampled and/or non-uniform Fourier transform, and alignment involves calculating the distance between each image point and the curve
{\hat{G}}
as a function of
\psi
.

Many features of this toy problem are expected.
For example, when
\sigma=0
the images lie exactly on the
curve
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.
If we begin the alignment process with an initial guess

{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}={\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
for the molecule, then each image will be
aligned exactly to its correct viewing-angle
\psi_{j}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}

(up to a global phase-shift).
Subsequent reconstruction steps will
produce
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}={\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, so long as the number of
observed points
N_{A}
is at least as large as the number of
modes
1+2{Q}
, and the reconstruction operator is well-conditioned
(i.e., if the image-points are not too closely clumped together).
Put more simply, if
\sigma=0
and
N_{A}\geq 1+2{Q}
then,
generically speaking, a maximum-likelihood alternating minimization
starting with
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}={\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
as an initial-function will
be stable.

However, even in this simple scenario (i.e., with
\sigma=0
),
if we were to attempt maximum-likelihood alternating minimization
with a different initial function then we are not guaranteed to
converge to
{\hat{G}}
.
Indeed, if
N_{A}
is not too large, there will typically be many
different functions with the same bandlimit of
{Q}
that pass close
to the observed points
{\hat{A}}_{j}
.
Which of these functions we converge to will depend on the details of
the reconstruction process.
The situation becomes more complicated once we add image noise;
when
\sigma>0
even an initial guess of
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}={\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}

will not necessarily lead to convergence to a function
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}

that is close to
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.

To probe this phenomenon, we perform a set of numerical-experiments.
We initialize our reconstruction with a function of the form:

{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}:=\lambda\cdot{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}+(1-\lambda)\cdot{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny rand}}}\text{,}

where
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny rand}}}
is a random function drawn the same way as (but independent from)
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, and
\lambda
is a parameter describing how close
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}
is to the ground truth.
Given a particular
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny init}}}
, we perform the reconstruction, iterating until the resulting
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
converges.
For each trial we can then measure the error
E
between
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
.

In order to reward trials where
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
and
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
are close to one another, even if they are parametrized differently, we define the error
E
as follows:
First we define
d({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(\psi))
to be the distance between
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(\psi)
(i.e., a point in
\mathbb{C}
) and the curve
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}
.
We then define the error
E({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}})
as the integral of
d^{2}({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}(\psi))
over
\psi
.
Similarly, we define
d({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}(\psi))
to be the distance between
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}(\psi)
and the curve
{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}
, and define the error
E({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}})
as the integral of
d^{2}({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}})
over
\psi
.
Finally, we define the overall error
E=\max(E({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}),E({\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny estim}}}\leftarrow{\hat{G}}^{{\text{\tiny true}}}))
to be the larger of the two.

.

Appendix E
EMPM parameters:

When performing the EMPM reconstructions shown in the main text, we fixed
{K}=48
, corresponding to a maximum resolution of
\sim 20
Å, as is typical for low-resolution estimates. Our EMPM algorithm also requires two other user-specified parameters: the overall tolerance
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}
and the width of the local displacement disk
\delta_{{\text{\tiny local}}}
. As shown in Fig
13
, the typical quality of our results does not change appreciably if these parameters are varied across an order of magnitude. We also run our algorithm for
64
iterations. As shown in Fig
14
, the median quality of our results does not deteriorate appreciably if we reduce the number of iterations by a modest
factor.

As mentioned in the main text, the overall computation time for our algorithm is roughly constant per iteration (i.e., between
30-50
minutes on a desktop workstation for all
64
iterations). In principle, we expect the overall computation time of our algorithm to grow as
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny tol}}}
becomes very small or
\delta_{{\text{\tiny local}}}
becomes very large. The reason we do not see a strong dependence on computation time for the range of parameters shown here is that, within this regime, the array dimensions involved are not so large (i.e.,
\sim 10^{2}-10^{3}
per dimension). As a consequence, roughly half of the overall computational effort is spent on memory movement rather than floating-point operations.
