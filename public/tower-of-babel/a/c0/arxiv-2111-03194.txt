NT @ UW-21-13 Confinement in Two-Dimensional QCD and the Infinitely Long Pion
Colin M. Weller and Gerald A. Miller

Abstract
Three current models of QCD in (1+1) dimensions are examined and extended in light-front coordinates. A pion of high momentum is found to have an infinite extent along its direction of motion.



There has been a recent revival of interest in theories involving two-dimensional(one-space-one time) treatments of QCD
De Téramond and Brodsky 2021
;
Li and Vary 2021
;
Ahmady et al. 2021a
;
Ahmady et al. 2021b
.
This stems from the need to include the effects of non-vanishing quark masses and also to enlarge the number of space-time variables of light-front holographic QCD to four. In the original treatments (see the review
Brodsky et al. 2015
) the chiral limit is used and the longitudinal light-front momentum fraction,
x
, is frozen
Sheckler and Miller 2021
, so that effectively the only degrees of freedom are light-front time and transverse position. The first effort aimed at including the effects of mass was contained in Ref.
Chabysheva and Hiller 2013
. The present epistle aims to unify the earlier treatments and to exhibit the confining aspects of the approaches in three-spatial dimensions using the spatial coordinate,
{\tilde{z}}
, that is canonically conjugate to the variable
x

Miller and Brodsky 2020
.

We begin by briefly summarizing light-front holographic QCD (LFHQCD) following
Brodsky et al. 2015
. Light-front (LF) quantization is a relativistic, frame-independent approach to describing the constituent structure of hadrons. The simple structure of the light-front vacuum allows an unambiguous definition of the partonic content of a hadron in QCD and of hadronic light-front wave functions.
The QCD light-front Hamiltonian is constructed from the energy-momentum tensor of QCD. The spectrum and light-front wave functions of relativistic bound states are obtained from the resulting eigenvalue equation, an infinite set of coupled integral equations for the LF components in a complete basis of noninteracting
n
-particle states. This provides a quantum-mechanical probabilistic interpretation of the structure of hadronic states in terms of their constituents at the same light-front time
x^{+}=x^{0}+x^{3}
.

To a first semiclassical approximation, where quantum loops and quark masses are not included, the relativistic bound-state equation for light hadrons can be reduced to an effective LF Schrödinger equation. In conjugate coordinate space, the relevant dynamical variable is an invariant impact kinematical variable
\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}={\bf b}\sqrt{x(1-x)}
, where
{\bf b}
is the transverse separation of the constituents.
(Boldface notation specifies vectors of the two-dimensional transverse space.)
The complexities of the strong interaction dynamics are then hidden in an effective potential
U(\zeta)
.
Remarkably, the resulting light-front Hamiltonian has a structure that matches exactly the eigenvalue equations in anti-deSitter space
Brodsky et al. 2015
. This offers the possibility to explicitly connect the AdS wave function
\Phi(z)
to the internal constituent structure of hadrons. Moreover, one can obtain the AdS wave equations by starting from the semiclassical approximation to light-front QCD in physical spacetime. This connection yields a relation between the coordinate
z
of AdS space with the impact LF variable
\zeta
, thus giving the holographic variable
z
a precise definition and intuitive meaning in light-front QCD
Brodsky and de Teramond 2009
.

An effective
light-front Schrödinger equation for the quark (
m_{1})
-antiquark (
m_{2})
,
wave function
\psi(x,{\bf k}),

\left[\frac{m_{1}^{2}}{x}+\frac{m_{2}^{2}}{1-x}+\frac{{\bf k}^{2}}{x(1-x)}+V_{\rm eff}\right]\psi=M_{h}^{2}\psi,

(1)

is the transverse relative momentum, the first three terms are the light-front energy of two non-interacting partons,

Note that the kinetic energy term depends only on the two-dimensional vector
{\bf k}/\sqrt{x(1-x)}

Then, using coordinate space, and taking

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{d\zeta^{2}}+\frac{L^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}+U_{\perp}(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})\right)\varphi(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})=M^{2}\varphi(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}),

For the soft-wall model

Using this potential leads to a massless pion (

In Eq. (

Next, we consider the equation of the form
H_{\parallel}\chi_{n}=M_{n}^{2}\chi_{n}

With the fundamental longitudinal wave equation the usual orthonormal equation applies

\displaystyle\int\,dx\chi^{*}_{n}(x)\chi_{m}(x)=\delta_{nm},

Next, consider the matrix element.

\displaystyle\langle n|H|m\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dx\,dy\,\chi^{*}_{n}(x)H(x,y)\chi_{m}(y).

\displaystyle\langle n|H|m\rangle

\displaystyle=\int dx\frac{m^{2}}{x(1-x)}\chi_{n}^{*}(x)\chi_{m}(x)

\displaystyle+\int\,dx\,dy\chi^{*}_{n}(x)V_{||}(x,y)\chi_{m}(y).

(8)

\displaystyle(V_{\rm tH}\,\chi_{n})(x)={g^{2}\over\pi}P\int_{0}^{1}dy{\chi_{n}(x)-\chi_{n}(y)\over(x-y)^{2}},

The function

Taking the Fourier transform of

The ’t Hooft model was extensively studied during the 1970’s; see the review

The third approach

To obtain an expression for the Hamiltonian using the

\displaystyle\langle n|H|m\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dx}{x(1-x)}{\tilde{X}}_{n}(x)\frac{m^{2}}{x(1-x)}X_{m}(x)+\langle n|V_{\parallel}|m\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle n|V_{||}|m\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dx\,dy}{x(1-x)}{\tilde{X}}_{n}(x){\sqrt{x(1-x)}\over\sqrt{y(1-y)}}V(x,y)X_{m}(y).

The effective potential,

The consequence of this lack of hermiticity is that, for example, the resulting wave equation for the t’ Hooft model would be of the form:

\displaystyle M_{n}^{2}X_{n}(x)=

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}}{x(1-x)}X_{n}(x)-\frac{g^{2}}{\pi}\mathcal{P}\int\,dy\frac{(X_{n}(x)-\frac{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}{\sqrt{y(1-y)}}X_{n}(y))}{(x-y)^{2}}.

The non-Hermitian nature of the effective potential seems to represent a difficulty in extending light-front holographic QCD in the conformal limit to include non-zero quark masses and excitation of longitudinal modes.

We now begin to discuss the differences between the two potentials of Eq. (
9

In the chiral limit, the ground state wave function of both

;
Callan et al. 1976

to be simply ( dropping the subscript)
\chi(x)=1

The LFHQCD formalism

\chi(x)=1

The useful identity

\displaystyle M_{\parallel}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\,dx\,\chi(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\,dx\,\chi(x)\left[\frac{m_{1}^{2}}{x}+\frac{m_{2}^{2}}{1-x}\right]

(13)

Given the similarities between the Li-Vary and ’t Hooft models, it is natural to initially expect that
any differences between the models might be thought to be small. This is far from the case. Using coordinate space helps in
understanding that there indeed are significant differences between the potentials.
This is done by evaluating
V_{\rm LV}(x)
in coordinate space.

\displaystyle\langle{\tilde{z}}|V_{\rm LV}(x)|{\tilde{z}}^{\prime}\rangle=\sigma^{2}{\tilde{z}}{\tilde{z}}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}{dx\over 2\pi}e^{ix({\tilde{z}}^{\prime}-{\tilde{z}})}x(1-x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\pi}{\tilde{z}}{\tilde{z}}^{\prime}e^{i\,{({\tilde{z}}^{\prime}-{\tilde{z}})\over 2}}{j_{1}({{\tilde{z}}^{\prime}-{\tilde{z}}\over 2})\over{\tilde{z}}^{\prime}-{\tilde{z}}},

(14)

where

\sigma^{2}{\tilde{z}}^{2}/6
which is in stark contrast to the linear behavior of the potential in the ’t Hooft model. Moreover, there is a significant coordinate-space non-locality in

The most salient difference between the two potentials is in the high-energy spectrum:

M^{2}_{\parallel}({\rm tH)}\approx g^{2}k,

The spectrum corresponding to the Brodsky-de Teramond IMWF model
De Téramond and Brodsky 2021
has not been provided before. The lore is that this model does not come from a wave equation. Here we develop a wave equation of the Sturm-Liouville form that does yield the IMWF and discuss the properties of the solutions.
The first step is asserting that the IMWF is the solution of some wave equation. The presentation is simplified by using
m_{1,2}=m
and also by employing the variable

, with
y^{2}=1/(x(1-x)).
Then using
\phi(y)=e^{-m^{2}y^{2}\over 2\kappa^{2}}

\displaystyle-{d\over dy}f(y){d\phi(y)\over dy}+m^{2}y^{2}\phi(y)=M^{2}\phi(y),

(15)

Using the expression for

The wave function that leads to the ground state IMWF is then given by

\displaystyle-{\kappa^{4}\over m^{2}}\phi^{\prime\prime}+m^{2}y^{2}\phi=M^{2}\phi,

This can be converted to an equation in the variable

\displaystyle-g(x){\kappa^{4}\over m^{2}}{d\over dx}g(x){d\phi(x)\over dx}+{m^{2}\phi(x)\over x(1-x)}={M^{2}}\phi(x),

\kappa^{2}

It is worthwhile to note that the use of current-quark masses in the ’t Hooft model causes the two different models to obtain very different masses of the first excited state.
Ref

The confining aspects of the ’t Hooft model have been well-studied long ago
’t Hooft 1974
;
Callan et al. 1976
, using a momentum-space (
x
) dependence approach based on studying the cancellation of infrared singularities. Another, possibly more intuitive approach, may be obtaining by examining coordinate-space wave functions that depend on the canonically conjugate spatial variable,
{\tilde{z}}
. An intuitive way to think about this variable is that it is the separation between the quark and anti-quark in the direction of motion of a pion moving with high momentum.

Coordinate-space wave functions are obtained using the transformation

\displaystyle\chi({\tilde{z}})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}{dx\over\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ix{\tilde{z}}}\chi(x).

(20)

It is useful to examine the density
\rho({\tilde{z}})\equiv|\chi({\tilde{z}})|^{2}
, a real-valued quantity, for the three models are shown in Fig. 1(a).
These results seem very similar because of the relatively small quark masses of the three models. The densities seem to go to 0 for large absolute values of
{\tilde{z}}.
They do, but there in an interesting feature seen by plotting
{\tilde{z}}^{2}\rho({\tilde{z}})
in Fig. 1(b): the densities fall as
1/{\tilde{z}}^{2}
.

This behavior may be understood by making a asymptotic expansion, obtained by using
e^{i{\tilde{z}}x}=1/(i{\tilde{z}})\partial_{x}e^{i{\tilde{z}}x}
and the feature that

vanishes at the end points. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{|{\tilde{z}}|\to\infty}\chi({\tilde{z}})={i\over{\tilde{z}}}\int_{0}^{1}{dx\over\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{ix{\tilde{z}}}\partial_{x}\chi(x).

(21)

Squaring this quantity leads to the stated

The similarity of the behaviors for small masses suggests that the chiral limit should be examined. In this case,
\chi(x)=1
for all three models. Then a simple closed-form expression for

\displaystyle\rho_{\chi}({\tilde{z}})={2\over\pi}{\sin^{2}{{\tilde{z}}/2}\over{\tilde{z}}^{2}},

\displaystyle\langle\chi|{\tilde{z}}^{2}|\chi\rangle={2\over\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dz\,\sin^{2}({\tilde{z}}/2)=\infty.

(23)

In the chiral limit the pion has an infinite spatial extent, true for all three models.

It is necessary to see how or if this infinite size conflicts with current understanding. First note that the elastic pion form factor of LFHQCD has already been
computed using
\chi(x)=1

e.g.
Ref.

The relevance of the infinite longitudinal extent can be understood in analogy with the familiar Ioffe-time argument
Gribov et al. 1965
;
Ioffe 1969
;
Kovchegov and Levin 2012
;
Braun and Müller 2008
for deep inelastic scattering at small values of Bjorken
x
. The idea is that an incident virtual photon fluctuates into a

pair. The energy difference,
\Delta E
,
between the two states is very small, so that the fluctuation lives for a long time
\Delta t>1/\Delta E
. For virtual photons of high energy, the extent of the fluctuation is
c\Delta t
is very large. Now consider a high-momentum pion incident on a target. The value of
\Delta E
goes to 0 if both the pion and its constituents are massless. Thus the infinite extent of a mass-less pion is part of standard lore.

We summarize. The similarities and differences between the three models of Refs.
De Téramond and Brodsky 2021
;
Li and Vary 2021
;
’t Hooft 1974
are exhibited. Very significant differences in the excitation spectra, at both low and high energies, are obtained, even though all of the ground-state wave functions are the same in the chiral limit. If the ’t Hooft model is used along with current values of light-quark masses, the original spectrum calculations reviewed in Ref.
Brodsky et al. 2015
are preserved. Finally, the pion is shown to have an infinite extent in the longitudinal direction.
