On the monotonicity of a quantum optimal transport cost
Alexander Müller-Hermes

Abstract
. We show that the quantum generalization of the 2 2 -Wasserstein distance proposed by Chakrabarti et al. is not monotone under partial traces. This disproves a recent conjecture by Friedland et al. Finally, we propose a stabilized version of the original definition, which we show to be monotone under the application of general quantum channels.

1.
Introduction

For every dimension
d\in\mathbbm{N}
we define the symmetric subspace

\mathbbm{C}^{d}\vee\mathbbm{C}^{d}=\{|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}~:~\mathbbm{F}_{d}|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle\},

and the antisymmetric subspace

\mathbbm{C}^{d}\wedge\mathbbm{C}^{d}=\{|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}~:~\mathbbm{F}_{d}|\psi\rangle=-|\psi\rangle\},

where we used the flip operator
\mathbbm{F}_{d}:\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}
defined by

\mathbbm{F}|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle=|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle,

and extended linearly. The symmetric subspace and antisymmetric subspace are subspaces of
\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}
and we denote by
\Psym(d)
and
\Pasym(d)
their respective orthogonal projections. In
[
ZYYY22
]
these projectors where used to study quantum versions of classical earth mover’s distances. Along similar lines, a quantum generalization of the classical Wasserstein distance of order
2
with respect to the Hamming distance (see
[
Vil09
]
for details) was proposed in
[
CYL+19
]
:

Definition 1.1
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
the
quantum optimal transport cost
is given by

T(\rho,\sigma)=\min_{\tau_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)}\Tr\left[\tau_{AB}\Pasym(d)\right],

where the minimum is over all quantum states
\tau_{AB}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
with marginals
\tau_{A}=\rho
and
\tau_{B}=\sigma
. The quantity

W(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{T\left(\rho,\sigma\right)},

is called the
quantum Wasserstein semi-distance
of order
2
between
\rho
and
\sigma
.

Many properties of these quantities where discussed in
[
CEFŻ21
,
FECŻ22
]
, and it is conjectured that the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance of order
2
satisfies the triangle inequality, which would make it a genuine distance. It should be pointed out that there are other proposals for quantum generalizations of Wasserstein distances and optimal transport costs including definitions for orders different than
2
. See for example
[
ŻS98
,
CM14
,
GP18
,
DPT21
,
DPMTL21
,
CEFŻ21
]
and references therein. In this article, we will only discuss the quantum optimal transport cost and quantum Wasserstein semi-distance of order
2
from Definition
1.1
. For brevity, we will refer to these quantities as
the
quantum optimal transport cost and
the
quantum Wasserstein semi-distance although we do not claim that these are the “correct” generalizations in any sense. In fact, we will even argue that these definitions have shortcomings, when used as distance measures in a context of processing quantum information.

It was asked in
[
BEŻ22
]
whether the quantum optimal transport cost (and hence the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance) is monotone under the application of quantum channels, i.e., whether the inequality

T(\phi(\rho),\phi(\sigma))\leq T(\rho,\sigma),

holds for all pairs of quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
and any quantum channel
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{d^{\prime}}
. It was observed (see
[
BEŻ22
]
) that this monotonicity holds when
d=d^{\prime}=2
and when
\phi
is a mixed unitary quantum channel. Based on numerical evidence, it was then conjectured in
[
FECŻ22
]
that monotonicity holds in general. Here, we will show that this conjecture is false. The quantum optimal transport cost is
not
monotone under general quantum channels, and in particular it fails to be monotone for arbitrary pairs of quantum states
\rho,\sigma
when
\phi
is a partial trace. This article is structured as follows:

•

In Section
2
we will review some useful properties of the quantum optimal transport cost.

•

In Section
3
we will show that the quantum optimal transport cost is not monotone under partial traces.

•

In Section
4
we will introduce a stabilized version of the quantum optimal transport cost. We will show that this quantity is monotone under the application of quantum channels and that it shares many properties of the original definition.

2.
Properties of the quantum optimal transport cost

To prove properties of the quantum optimal transport cost it will be useful to consider an alternative ordering of the tensor factors when having tensor product input states. We will use the notation

\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}-\mathbbm{F}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{F}_{d_{2}}\right),

to denote the projector onto the antisymmetric subspace
(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}})\wedge(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}})
, but where the tensor factors are reshuffled so that
\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})
acts on
\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}
. We will often use letters ‘A’ and ‘B’ to clarify the situation: We would, for example, denote by
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
the two tensor factors with dimension
d_{1}
, and by
A_{2}
and
B_{2}
the two tensor factors with dimension
d_{2}
. The antisymmetric projector
\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})
is defined with respect to the exchange of the
A
-labeled and the
B
-labeled tensor factors, where we use the ordering
A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}
instead of
A_{1}A_{2}B_{1}B_{2}
.

For quantum states
\rho_{1},\sigma_{1}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\right)
and
\rho_{2},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\right)
we have

T(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})=\min_{\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}}\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right],

where the minimum is over all quantum states

\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\right),

such that the marginals are

\tau_{A_{1}A_{2}}=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},

and

\tau_{B_{1}B_{2}}=\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}.

Finally, it is useful to note the identity

(1)

\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})=\Pasym(d_{1})\otimes\Psym(d_{2})+\Psym(d_{1})\otimes\Pasym(d_{2}).

2.1.
Formulation as a semidefinite problem

Clearly, the quantum optimal transport cost
T(\rho,\sigma)
is a semidefinite program and we can compute its dual as

T^{*}(\rho,\sigma)=\sup_{E,F}\Tr\left[E\rho\right]+\Tr\left[F\sigma\right],

for
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
, where the supremum goes over
E,F\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
such that

(2)

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\leq\Pasym(d).

It is clear that

(3)

T^{*}(\rho,\sigma)\leq T(\rho,\sigma),

since

\Tr\left[E\rho\right]+\Tr\left[F\sigma\right]=\Tr\left[\left(E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\right)\tau_{AB}\right]\leq\Tr\left[\tau_{AB}\Pasym(d)\right],

for any quantum state
\tau_{AB}
with marginals
\tau_{A}=\rho
and
\tau_{B}=\sigma
and any
E,F\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
satisfying (
2
). Moreover, as shown in
[
CYL+19
]
, strong duality holds for the pair of semidefinite programs, and we have

T^{*}(\rho,\sigma)=T(\rho,\sigma),

for all
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
.

2.2.
Monotonicity under tensoring with a state

We start with a theorem proved in
[
FECŻ22
]
showing that the quantum optimal transport cost between two quantum states decreases under tensoring with the another quantum state:

Theorem 2.1

(
[
FECŻ22
]
)
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in D(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}})
and any quantum state
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\right)
we have

T(\rho\otimes\gamma,\sigma\otimes\gamma)\leq T(\rho,\sigma).

For the reader’s convenience we include a proof of this result:

Proof.

The quantum optimal transport cost
T
is jointly convex (see
[
CEFŻ21
, Proposition 2.2]
) and it is enough to prove the inequality for any pure state

\gamma=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\right).

Given a quantum state
\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}})
with marginals
\tau_{A_{1}}=\rho
and
\tau_{B_{1}}=\sigma
, we note that
\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}=\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
is a quantum state with marginals
\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
and
\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
. We find that

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle(\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|,\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)

\displaystyle\leq\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[(\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)(\Pasym(d_{1})\otimes\Psym(d_{2})+\Psym(d_{1})\otimes\Pasym(d_{2}))\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}}\Pasym(d_{1})\right],

since
|\psi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\vee\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}
. This shows that

T(\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|,\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)\leq T(\rho,\sigma),

and the proof is finished.
∎

The main result of our article is to show that the inequality in the previous theorem is, in general, not an equality, and there are quantum states where it is strict. This shows, in particular, that the quantum optimal transport cost (and hence the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance) is not monotone under partial traces.

3.
The quantum optimal transport cost is not monotone

We start with an elementary lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

For
E,F\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}})
the following are equivalent:

(1)

We have

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}+\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\leq\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2}).

(2)

We have both

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}+\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F\leq\Pasym(d_{1}),

and

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}+\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F\leq\Psym(d_{1}).

Proof.

With (
1
) and since

\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}=\Pasym(d_{2})+\Psym(d_{2}),

we find that

\displaystyle\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})-E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}-\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{2}}

\displaystyle=(\Pasym(d_{1})-E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}-\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F)\otimes\Psym(d_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+(\Psym(d_{1})-E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}-\mathds{1}_{d_{1}}\otimes F)\otimes\Pasym(d_{2}).

Since
\Psym(d_{2})
and
\Pasym(d_{2})
are self-adjoint projections onto orthogonal subspaces the statement of the theorem follows.
∎

To show that the quantum optimal transport cost is not monotone under quantum channels, we will need Hermitian matrices
E,F\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
satisfying

(4)

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\leq\Pasym(d),

but also

(5)

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\nleq\Psym(d).

Such examples can be found numerically for
d\geq 4
. For instance, we can choose

E=\begin{pmatrix}-1.37&0&0&0\\
0&0.02&0&0\\
0&0&0.17&0\\
0&0&0&0.26\end{pmatrix}

and

F=\begin{pmatrix}0.1165&-0.02+0.01i&0.03-0.05i&-0.04-0.05i\\
-0.02-0.01i&-0.0935&0.02i&0.16-0.11i\\
0.03+0.05i&-0.02i&-0.2335&0.06+0.11i\\
-0.04+0.05i&0.16+0.11i&0.06-0.11i&-1.1435\end{pmatrix}.

By embedding these examples into matrices of larger dimensions, it is straightforward to show that similar examples exist for any
d\geq 4
. Indeed, for
k\geq 1
and
\alpha\in\mathbbm{R}^{+}
consider the Hermitian matrices
E^{\prime},F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d+k}\right)
given by

E^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}E&0\\
0&-\alpha\mathds{1}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad F^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}F&0\\
0&-\alpha\mathds{1}_{k}\end{pmatrix},

where
E,F\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
are Hermitian matrices satisfying (
4
) and (
5
). If
\alpha
is chosen large enough, then it is straightforward to verify that

E^{\prime}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d+k}+\mathds{1}_{d+k}\otimes F^{\prime}\leq\Pasym(d+k),

and of course we have

E^{\prime}\otimes\mathds{1}_{d+k}+\mathds{1}_{d+k}\otimes F^{\prime}\nleq\Psym(d+k).

While this construction yields examples for any
d\geq 4
, we do not know whether such examples exist for
d=3
. We can now prove our main result:

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
d\geq 4
there are quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
such that

T(\rho,\sigma)>T(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}).

In particular, the quantum optimal transport cost
T
and the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance
W
are not monotone under partial traces over qubit systems.

Proof.

Let
E,F\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
be such that

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\leq\Pasym(d),

but also

E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F\nleq\Psym(d).

Such examples exist by the discussion preceeding the statement of the theorem. Consider the quantum state

\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Pasym(2)}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(2)\right]},

for some pure quantum state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}
satisfying

\langle\psi|(E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F)|\psi\rangle>\langle\psi|\Psym(d)|\psi\rangle.

We set
\rho=\Tr_{B_{1}}\left[|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{A_{1}B_{1}}\right]
and
\sigma=\Tr_{A_{1}}\left[|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{A_{1}B_{1}}\right]
and note that the quantum state
\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}
from above has marginals

\tau_{A_{1}A_{2}}=\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau_{B_{1}B_{2}}=\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}.

We will now show that

T(\rho,\sigma)>T(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}).

For this compute

\displaystyle T(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2})

\displaystyle\leq\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d\otimes 2)\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\left(|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Pasym(2)}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(2)\right]}\right)\Pasym(d\otimes 2)\right]

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|\Psym(d)|\psi\rangle\Tr\left[\frac{\Pasym(2)}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(2)\right]}\right]

\displaystyle<\langle\psi|(E\otimes\mathds{1}_{d}+\mathds{1}_{d}\otimes F)|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[E\rho\right]+\Tr\left[F\sigma\right]

\displaystyle\leq T^{*}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq T(\rho,\sigma),

where the third line follows from (
1
) and the last step follows from (
3
).
∎

To gain a better understanding for how the quantum optimal transport cost can fail to be monotone under partial traces, we can use a symmetrization argument. Recall the
UU
-twirling channel
\Sigma_{d}:\mathcal{M}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{d}
defined as

\Sigma_{d}(X)=\int_{\mathcal{U}_{d}}\left(U\otimes U\right)X\left(U^{\dagger}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)\text{d}U,

where the integral is with respect to the Haar measure on group
\mathcal{U}_{d}
of
d\times d
unitary matrices. Using Schur-Weyl duality
[
Wer89
]
, it can be shown that

(6)

\Sigma_{d}(X)=\Tr\left[X\Psym(d)\right]\frac{\Psym(d)}{\Tr\left[\Psym(d)\right]}+\Tr\left[X\Pasym(d)\right]\frac{\Pasym(d)}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(d)\right]}.

With this identity we can show the following theorem:

Theorem 3.3
.

For quantum states
\rho_{1},\sigma_{1}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}})
and
\rho_{2},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}})
we have

T(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})\geq T\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right).

Proof.

Consider a quantum state
\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}})
such that

\tau_{A_{1}A_{2}}=\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\tau_{B_{1}B_{2}}=\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2},

and such that

T\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)=\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right].

By (
1
) we have

(\textnormal{id}_{d_{1}}\otimes\textnormal{id}_{d_{1}}\otimes\Sigma_{d_{2}})\left(\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right)=\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2}).

Using (
6
) and that the twirl
\Sigma_{d_{2}}
is self-adjoint with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, we find that

\Tr\left[\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right]=\Tr\left[\hat{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right],

where
\hat{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{2}})
is a quantum state of the form

\hat{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}=X_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Psym(d_{2})}{\Tr\left[\Psym(d_{2})\right]}+Y_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Pasym(d_{2})}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(d_{2})\right]},

with positive operators
X_{A_{1}B_{1}},Y_{A_{1}B_{1}}\in\mathcal{B}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{1}}\right)^{+}
such that

X_{A_{1}B_{1}}+Y_{A_{1}B_{1}}=\tau_{A_{1}B_{1}}.

Finally, observe that

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\hat{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[X_{A_{1}B_{1}}\Pasym(d_{1})\right]+\Tr\left[Y_{A_{1}B_{1}}\Psym(d_{1})\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\tilde{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes 2)\right],

for the quantum state

\tilde{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}=X_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Psym(2)}{\Tr\left[\Psym(2)\right]}+Y_{A_{1}B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\Pasym(2)}{\Tr\left[\Pasym(2)\right]}.

Since

\tilde{\tau}_{A_{1}A_{2}}=X_{A_{1}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}+Y_{A_{1}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}=\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},

and

\tilde{\tau}_{B_{1}B_{2}}=X_{B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}+Y_{B_{1}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}=\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},

we have

T\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)=\Tr\left[\hat{\tau}_{A_{1}B_{1}A_{2}B_{2}}\Pasym(d_{1}\otimes d_{2})\right]\geq T\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right).

∎

Theorem
3.3
shows that the violation of monotonicity under partial traces is exhibited strongest when tensoring with the maximally mixed qubit state as in Theorem
3.2
.

4.
Stabilized quantum Wasserstein semi-distance

We have shown that the quantum optimal transport cost (and hence the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance) from above is not monotone under quantum channels and it is natural to ask whether this shortcoming can be fixed by modifying the definition. Let us define the following quantity:

Definition 4.1
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
we define the
stabilized quantum optimal transport cost
as

T_{s}(\rho,\sigma)=\inf_{\gamma}T\left(\rho\otimes\gamma,\sigma\otimes\gamma\right),

where the infimum is over quantum states
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{D}\right)
of any dimensions
D\in\mathbbm{N}
. The
stabilized quantum Wasserstein semi-distance
of order
2
is then defined as

W_{s}(\rho,\sigma)=\sqrt{T_{s}(\rho,\sigma)}.

From Theorem
3.3
and its proof we immediately get a precise characterization of the stabilized quantum optimal transport cost in terms of the original quantum optimal transport cost and as a semidefinite program:

Theorem 4.2
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
we have

\displaystyle T_{s}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{X_{AB},Y_{AB}}\left(\Tr\left[X_{AB}\Psym(d)\right]+\Tr\left[Y_{AB}\Pasym(d)\right]\right),

where the minimum is over positive operators
X_{AB},Y_{AB}\in\left(\mathcal{M}_{d}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{d}\right)^{+}
such that

X_{A}+Y_{A}=\rho,\quad\text{ and }\quad X_{B}+Y_{B}=\sigma.

The stabilized quantum transport cost has many desirable properties:

Lemma 4.3

(Joint convexity)
.

For quantum states
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
and
\lambda\in\left[0,1\right]
we have

\displaystyle T_{s}

\displaystyle(\lambda\rho_{1}+(1-\lambda)\rho_{2},\lambda\sigma_{1}+(1-\lambda)\sigma_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\leq\lambda T_{s}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})+(1-\lambda)T_{s}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2}).

Proof.

This follows easily from the joint convexity of
T
(see
[
CEFŻ21
, Proposition 2.2]
) and Theorem
4.2
.
∎

We also have the following:

Lemma 4.4

(Invariance under tensor products)
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
and any quantum state
\gamma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{D})
we have

T_{s}\left(\rho\otimes\gamma,\sigma\otimes\gamma\right)=T_{s}\left(\rho,\sigma\right).

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
we have

\displaystyle T\left(\rho\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right)\geq T\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right).

But using Theorem
2.1
, we also have

T\left(\rho\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right)\leq T\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right),

Therefore, we have the equality

\displaystyle T_{s}\left(\rho\otimes\gamma,\sigma\otimes\gamma\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(\rho\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\gamma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(\rho\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2},\sigma\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{2}}{2}\right)=T_{s}(\rho,\sigma).

∎

Finally, we note that the following lemma is clear by the definition of
T_{s}
:

Lemma 4.5
.

For quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d})
and any unitary
U\in\mathcal{U}_{d}
we have

T_{s}\left(U\rho U^{\dagger},U\sigma U^{\dagger}\right)=T_{s}\left(\rho,\sigma\right).

We have the following lemma:

Theorem 4.6
.

For any pair of quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
and any quantum channel
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{d^{\prime}}
we have

T_{s}\left(\phi(\rho),\phi(\sigma)\right)\leq T_{s}\left(\rho,\sigma\right).

Proof.

The proof follows a well-known strategy to show monotonicity of quantities under quantum channels (see, e.g.,
[
Wol12
]
). For any quantum channel
\phi:\mathcal{M}_{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{d^{\prime}}
there exists a unitary
U\in\mathcal{U}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}\right)
and a pure quantum state
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}
such that

\phi(X)=\Tr_{E}\left[U(X\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)U^{\dagger}\right],

for every
X\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, where
\Tr_{E}
denotes the trace over the first two tensor factors of
\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d^{\prime}}
. Furthermore note that the completely depolarizing channel
\Delta:\mathcal{M}_{dd^{\prime}}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{dd^{\prime}}
given by

\Delta(X)=\Tr\left[X\right]\frac{\mathds{1}_{dd^{\prime}}}{dd^{\prime}}.

is a mixed unitary channel.

It is easy to see that
T_{s}
is monotone under mixed unitary channels since it is jointly convex and unitarily invariant. For any pair of quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbbm{C}^{d}\right)
we have

\displaystyle T_{s}

\displaystyle\left(\phi(\rho),\phi(\sigma)\right)=T_{s}\left(\frac{\mathds{1}_{dd^{\prime}}}{dd^{\prime}}\otimes\phi(\rho),\frac{\mathds{1}_{dd^{\prime}}}{dd^{\prime}}\otimes\phi(\sigma)\right)

\displaystyle=T_{s}\left((\Delta\otimes\textnormal{id}_{d})\left(U(\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)U^{\dagger}\right),(\Delta\otimes\textnormal{id}_{d})\left(U(\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)U^{\dagger}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq T_{s}\left(U(\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)U^{\dagger},U(\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|)U^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=T_{s}\left(\rho\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|,\sigma\otimes|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right)

\displaystyle=T_{s}\left(\rho,\sigma\right),

where we used Theorem
4.4
in the first equality, the definition of
\Delta
in the second equality, and the fact that
\Delta
is a mixed unitary channel in the inequality. Finally, we used unitary invariance (see Lemma
4.5
) and invariance under tensoring with pure quantum states, which is easily seen from the definition of the quantum optimal transport cost.
∎

5.
Conclusion

We have shown that the quantum optimal transport cost and the quantum Wasserstein semi-distance introduced in
[
CYL+19
]
and studied in
[
CEFŻ21
,
FECŻ22
,
BEŻ22
]
are not monotone under the application of general quantum channels. To fix this shortcoming, we have proposed a stabilized version of the original definition. While this stabilized quantum optimal transport cost is monotone under quantum channels it is still open whether the corresponding stabilized quantum Wasserstein semi-distance satisfies the triangle inequality, which would turn it into a genuine distance. Clearly, this triangle inequality would follow if the triangle inequality for the original definition could be shown.
