Abstract
. Kröncke has shown that the Fubini–Study metric is an unstable generalized stationary solution of Ricci flow [ 10 ] . In this paper, we carry out numerical simulations which indicate that Ricci flow solutions originating at unstable perturbations of the Fubini–Study metric develop local singularities modeled by the blowdown soliton discovered in [ 5 ] .



1.
Introduction

In studying dynamical systems, it is always important to identify and classify fixed points. For Ricci flow, regarded as a dynamical system on the
infinite-dimensional space of Riemannian metrics, one must consider generalized fixed points. These are the
Ricci solitons
, specified by the data
\big(\mathcal{M}^{n},g,\lambda,X\big)
for which

-2\Rc[g]=2\lambda g+\mathcal{L}_{X}g,

where
\Rc[g]
is the Ricci curvature of the metric
g
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{X}g
is the Lie derivative of
g
with respect to the vector field
X
.

A prominent example of a Ricci soliton is the Fubini–Study metric on complex projective space, an Einstein metric corresponding to
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{N},G_{\mathrm{FS}},2(N+1),0\big)
.
1
1

1

N
is the complex dimension, so that the real dimension is
n=2N
.

In §10 of
[
6
]
, Hamilton conjectures that it is a stable fixed point. We refer the reader to Section
4
for an explicit description of the Fubini–Study metric. In Example 2.3 of
[
3
]
, Cao, Hamilton, and Ilmanen note that the Fubini–Study
metric is neutrally linearly stable with respect to the second variation of Perelman’s shrinker entropy, crediting this observation to unpublished work of Goldschmidt. However, in his dissertation, Kröncke computes the third variation of Perelman’s entropy to prove the surprising result that Fubini–Study is in fact unstable. This calculation has appeared as
[
10
]
and has been independently verified in
[
8
]
. The instability is conformal and hence not Kähler.

The instability of the Fubini–Study raises an interesting question. What happens to Ricci flow solutions that start at arbitrarily small but unstable perturbations of
G_{\mathrm{FS}}
? According to the conjectural hierarchy of
4
-dimensional solutions given in
[
3
]
, one expects the Ricci flow of unstable perturbations of
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{2},G_{\mathrm{FS}}\big)
to develop local singularities modeled by the blowdown gradient shrinking soliton
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{-1}
discovered in
[
5
]
. These solutions would start arbitrarily close to (but
not
in) the subset of Kähler metrics and, in finite time, develop local singularities that are asymptotically Kähler (but with the complex structure reversed — see below). A closely related type of singularity formation, an asymptotic approach to the space of Kähler metrics in the blowup limit, has been partially investigated in
[
7
]
. It should be noted that, while the blowdown soliton has been shown to occur as a model of singularity formation on compact manifolds by Máximo
[
11
]
, the solutions considered in that work are Kähler, whereas the unstable perturbations studied here all lie outside the space of Kähler initial data.

In this paper, we describe numerical simulations that provide evidence in favor of the conjecture that there exists an unstable Ricci flow “orbit” starting arbitrarily close to
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{2},G_{\mathrm{FS}}\big)
and ending at the blowdown soliton
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{-1}
in real dimension
n=4
. This is not a true orbit, because convergence to the blowdown soliton happens only locally and after parabolic dilation of the developing singularity. Nonetheless, it provides evidence in favor
of the hierarchy outlined in
[
3
]
. Furthermore, while
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{-1}
is known to be unique among Kähler solitons, our results suggest that there is no
non-Kähler gradient shrinking soliton with the topology of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
blown up at a point, at least none with a density
\Theta
above that of
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
;
cf
.
[
3
]
.

Because unperturbed
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{2},G_{\mathrm{FS}}\big)
is a positively-curved Einstein manifold, under Ricci flow it vanishes in a point. In particular, the area of the distinguished
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
goes to zero at the same time that the diameter of the manifold goes to zero. In each of our numerical simulations, we begin with initial data that are small non-Kähler conformal perturbations
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{2},\tilde{G}\big)
. The conformal factor lies in the nullspace of the second variation of Perelman’s
shrinker entropy
\nu
and is constructed in Section
2
. Our numerics indicate that evolution of this initial data by Ricci–DeTurck flow crushes the

\mathbb{CP}^{1}
fiber before the remainder of the manifold vanishes, that is, before the diameter goes to zero. Performing a parabolic rescaling of the solution fixes the
area of the
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
fiber. Our numerics indicate that the developing singularity is Type-I; that is, it develops at the natural parabolic rate. Because of this, one expects by
[
4
]
to see local convergence of the parabolic blowups to a nonflat gradient shrinking soliton. How do we recognize the soliton numerically?
We do so in two ways.

First, using a measure of local closeness to Kähler derived in Section
7
below, we find that the blowups are becoming asymptotically Kähler near the
distinguished fiber, but with the opposite orientation of the complex structure on unperturbed
\big(\mathbb{CP}^{2},G_{\mathrm{FS}}\big)
. This is significant because we know
by
[
5
]
that the blowdown soliton is the unique gradient shrinking Kähler soliton with the topology of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
blown up at the origin, which corresponds
in the original solution to the distinguished
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

Second, we use the fact that the blowdown soliton is asymptotically conical. By
[
9
]
, there is at most one shrinking gradient soliton
asymptotic to any cone. So, as further verification, we determine that the parabolic blowup is converging numerically to the correct cone near the
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
,
in a neighborhood suitable to the rescaling. Thus, while numerical simulations cannot provide proof, we regard these two observations as compelling evidence in favor of the formation of the
[
5
]
soliton in solutions emerging from unstable perturbations of the Fubini–Study metric.

2.
An unstable conformal perturbation

We seek a conformal factor
\psi
so that
(1+\delta\psi)G_{\mathrm{FS}}
is an unstable perturbation of the Fubini–Study metric for small
\delta>0
.

As shown in
[
8
]
, the function
\psi
must satisfy three conditions:

\Big(\Delta+\frac{1}{\tau}\Big)\psi=0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{CP}^{2}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}V=0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{CP}^{2}}\psi^{3}\,\mathrm{d}V>0.

These are, respectively: the condition that
\psi
lie in the nullspace of the second variation of
Perelman’s entropy
\nu
, a normalization necessary for that functional, and a sufficient condition for the perturbation generated by
\psi
to be unstable.
Here,
1/(2\tau)
is the Einstein constant of the metric, which in our case is
2(N+1)=6
.

Because
\Rc(G_{\mathrm{FS}})=6\,G_{\mathrm{FS}}
and the Laplacian of a rotationally-symmetric function
\psi
is

\Delta\psi=\psi_{\theta\theta}+\big\{3\cot(\theta)-\tan(\theta)\big\}\psi_{\theta},

to specify rotationally symmetric unstable perturbations of the Fubini–Study metric, we seek solutions
\psi
of the second-order linear
ode

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\Delta+12)\psi(\theta)

\displaystyle=\psi^{\prime\prime}(\theta)+\big\{3\cot(\theta)-\tan(\theta)\big\}\psi^{\prime}(\theta)+12\psi(\theta).

Only one fundamental solution of this
ode
is smooth for
\theta\in[0,\pi/2]
. Up to an arbitrary multiplicative constant, it is

\psi=\csc^{2}(\theta)\big\{2-8\cos(2\theta)+6\cos(4\theta)\big\}=1+3\cos(2\theta).

Using
\det(G_{\mathrm{FS}})=\sin^{6}(\theta)\cos^{2}(\theta)
, one readily verifies that this solution satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{CP}^{2}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\big\{1+3\cos(2\theta)\big\}\sin^{3}(\theta)\cos(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=0,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{CP}^{2}}\psi^{3}\,\mathrm{d}V=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\big\{1+3\cos(2\theta)\big\}^{3}\sin^{3}(\theta)\cos(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{2}{5}.

Hence, given
\delta>0
, we choose as initial data

\tilde{G}=\frac{1+\delta\psi}{1+\delta}G_{\mathrm{FS}}=\Big(1+3\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}\cos(2\theta)\Big)G_{\mathrm{FS}}=h\,G_{\mathrm{FS}},

where
h
is defined in (
7
) below, with
\varepsilon=\frac{\delta}{1+\delta}
.

3.
Cohomogeneity-one metrics

On
[0,\pi/2]\times\mathrm{SU}(2)
, we consider cohomogeneity-one metrics of the form

(1)

G=\mathrm{\rho}^{2}\,d\theta^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}^{2}\,\omega^{i}\otimes\omega^{i},

where
\omega^{i}\otimes\omega^{i}
is a Milnor coframe for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
.
The cohomogeneity-one condition means that the fiber over a generic
\theta
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, except for
\theta\in\{0,\pi/2\}
.
We are interested in initial data that are unstable conformal perturbations of the Fubini–Study metric on
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
. Accordingly,
we impose an
Ansatz
of
\mathrm{U}(2)
symmetry in the form

(2)

f_{1}=f\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad f_{2}=f_{3}=g.

From the viewpoint of analysis, it is convenient to fix a gauge and let
s(\theta,t)
represent arclength with respect to
G
. Then
s
and
\theta
satisfy the infinitesimal spatial relation

\mathrm{d}s=\rho\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

In this gauge, the metric takes the form

G=ds^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}f_{i}^{2}\,\omega^{i}\otimes\omega^{i}.

Then the sectional curvatures of
G
are convex linear combinations of

(3a)

\displaystyle\kappa_{12}=\kappa_{31}

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{2}}{g^{4}}-\frac{f_{s}g_{s}}{fg},

(3b)

\displaystyle\kappa_{23}

\displaystyle=\frac{4g^{2}-3f^{2}}{g^{4}}-\frac{g_{s}^{2}}{g^{2}},

(3c)

\displaystyle\kappa_{01}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f_{ss}}{f},

(3d)

\displaystyle\kappa_{02}=\kappa_{03}

\displaystyle=-\frac{g_{ss}}{g},

where
e_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}
and
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
is a Milnor frame for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
.

The variables
(s,t)
do not commute, but this approach yields a strictly parabolic system for Ricci flow on these geometries,

(4a)

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{ss}+2\frac{g_{s}}{g}f_{s}-2\frac{f^{3}}{g^{4}},

(4b)

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=g_{ss}+\left(\frac{f_{s}}{f}+\frac{g_{s}}{g}\right)g_{s}+2\frac{f^{2}-2g^{2}}{g^{3}},

where all time derivatives on the
lhs
are taken at fixed
\theta
. This system has been studied in
[
7
]
. One can recover the behavior of
\rho
under Ricci flow using its evolution equation

\rho_{t}=\left(\frac{f_{ss}}{f}+2\frac{g_{ss}}{g}\right)\rho.

From the viewpoint of numerical simulation, on the other hand, it is preferable to use fixed commuting variables.
Using the fact that any smooth
\zeta(s)
must satisfy

\zeta=\frac{\zeta_{\theta}}{\rho}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\zeta_{ss}=\frac{\zeta_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{\theta}\zeta_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}},

we rewrite system (
4
) in terms of the fixed commuting variables
(\theta,t)
as

(5a)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\theta\theta}}{\rho f}-\frac{\rho_{\theta}f_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}f}+2\frac{g_{\theta\theta}}{\rho g}-2\frac{\rho_{\theta}g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}g},

(5b)

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{\theta}f_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}}+2\frac{f_{\theta}g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}g}-2\frac{f^{3}}{g^{4}},

(5c)

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}-\frac{\rho_{\theta}g_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}}+\left(\frac{f_{\theta}}{f}+\frac{g_{\theta}}{g}\right)\frac{g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}}+2\frac{f^{2}-2g^{2}}{g^{3}}.

The evolution equation (
5a
) for
\rho
prevents this system from being parabolic. To remedy this, we implement DeTurck’s trick below.

4.
Initial data

Our reference (unperturbed) Fubini–Study metric
G_{\mathrm{FS}}
is determined by three functions

\rho_{\mathrm{FS}},f_{\mathrm{FS}},g_{\mathrm{FS}}\colon[0,\pi/2]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, given by

\rho_{\mathrm{FS}}(\theta)=1,\qquad f_{\mathrm{FS}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta)=\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g_{\mathrm{FS}}(\theta)=\sin(\theta).

That is, using (
1
), we have

G_{\mathrm{FS}}=d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\cos^{2}(\theta)\,\omega^{1}\otimes\omega^{1}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\,\omega^{2}\otimes\omega^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)\,\omega^{3}\otimes\omega^{3}.

It is readily verified that these choices have the correct boundary behavior to induce a smooth metric on
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
, with the geometry of an
asymptotically round
4
-ball near
\theta=0
and with a distinguished
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
fiber at
\theta=\pi/2
. Furthermore, it follows easily from (
3
)
that
\Rc[G_{\mathrm{FS}}]=6\,G_{\mathrm{FS}}
.

For use in specifying a Ricci-DeTurck flow below, which shadows the Ricci flow and is parabolic, unlike the Ricci flow itself, we note that the Fubini–Study connection is determined by

(6)

(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{00}^{0}=0,\quad(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{11}^{0}=-\frac{1}{4}\sin(4\theta),\quad(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{22}^{0}=(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{33}^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\sin(2\theta).

Given any
\varepsilon>0
, we recall that the conformal factor determining an unstable perturbation of the Fubini–Study metric is given by

(7)

h(\theta):=\big(1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\big),

as shown in Section
2
.
As initial data for our perturbed Ricci flow, we take
G(0)=\tilde{G}=h\,G_{\mathrm{FS}}
, for which
(\rho,f,g)=(h\rho_{\mathrm{FS}},hf_{\mathrm{FS}},hg_{\mathrm{FS}})
, namely

\displaystyle\rho(\theta)

\displaystyle=\Big(1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\Big),

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big(2\sin(2\theta)+3\varepsilon\sin(4\theta)\Big),

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sin(\theta)\Big(1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\Big)

at time
t=0
. The Levi–Civita connection of
G(0)
is determined by

\displaystyle\Gamma_{00}^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{6\varepsilon\sin(2\theta)}{1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{11}^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(2\theta)\Big(\cos(2\theta)+3\varepsilon\cos(4\theta)\Big)}{2\Big(1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\Big)},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{22}^{0}=\Gamma_{33}^{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\Big(1-6\varepsilon+9\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\Big)}{1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)},

with the unlisted Levi-Civita coefficients vanishing.

5.
Ricci–DeTurck flow

The formulas below that suppress time apply at all
t\geq 0
such that a solution exists.

We consider the
pde
system

(8)

\partial_{t}G=-2\Rc[G]+\mathcal{L}_{V}G

with initial data
G(0)=h\,G_{\mathrm{FS}}
, where our DeTurck vector field
V
is defined by

(9a)

V^{\beta}=G^{\alpha\alpha}\Big(\Gamma_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}\Big),

in which the implicit summation ranges over
\alpha\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.
For the metrics under consideration, this reduces to
V=V^{0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}
, where

(9b)

V^{0}=G^{\alpha\alpha}\Big(\Gamma_{\alpha\alpha}^{0}-(\Gamma_{\mathrm{FS}})_{\alpha\alpha}^{0}\Big).

Remark
.

We note that at
t=0
, one has

V^{0}(\theta,0)=\frac{12\varepsilon\sin(2\theta)}{\Big(1+3\varepsilon\cos(2\theta)\Big)^{3}},

which is smooth for all sufficiently small values of
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

In working with the DeTurck vector field
V=V^{0}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}
and the 1-form
V^{\flat}=V_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
, where
V_{0}=\rho^{2}V^{0}
,
it is convenient to relabel
V^{0}
as the function
v(\theta,t)
defined by

(10)

v=\frac{\rho_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}}+\frac{\tfrac{1}{4}\rho^{2}\sin(4\theta)-ff_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}f^{2}}+\frac{\rho^{2}\sin(2\theta)-2gg_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}g^{2}}.

One finds that
(\mathcal{L}_{V}G)_{\alpha\alpha}=2\nabla_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}
, where the only nonzero components are

\displaystyle\nabla_{0}V_{0}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}v_{\theta}+v\,\rho\rho_{\theta}=\rho(\rho v)_{\theta},

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}V_{1}

\displaystyle=v\,ff_{\theta},

\displaystyle\nabla_{2}V_{2}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{3}V_{3}=v\,gg_{\theta}.

Thus from system (
5
) above, we obtain a manifestly parabolic system corresponding to the Ricci-DeTurck flow,

(11a)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}-3\frac{\rho_{\theta}^{2}}{\rho^{3}}+\Big(\frac{f_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}f}+2\frac{g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}g}+v\Big)\rho_{\theta}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\Big[\frac{\sin(4\theta)}{4f^{2}}\Big]_{\theta}+\Big[\frac{\sin(2\theta)}{g^{2}}\Big]_{\theta}\right)\rho+\frac{f_{\theta}^{2}}{\rho f^{2}}+2\frac{g_{\theta}^{2}}{\rho g^{2}},

(11b)

\displaystyle f_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}+\Big(v-\frac{\rho_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}}\Big)f_{\theta}+2\frac{f_{\theta}g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}g}-2\frac{f^{3}}{g^{4}},

(11c)

\displaystyle g_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{\theta\theta}}{\rho^{2}}+\Big(v-\frac{\rho_{\theta}}{\rho^{3}}\Big)g_{\theta}+\left(\frac{f_{\theta}}{f}+\frac{g_{\theta}}{g}\right)\frac{g_{\theta}}{\rho^{2}}+2\frac{f^{2}-2g^{2}}{g^{3}}.

Below, we convert this into an equivalent form for which suitable boundary behaviors as
\theta\searrow 0
and
\theta\nearrow\pi/2
can be robustly enforced
in numerical simulations.

6.
A system better suited to simulation

Here, we convert system (
11
) into an equivalent one appropriate for numerical simulation of the evolution. The issue we must address is this:
for the evolving functions
(\rho,f,g)
to induce smooth metrics on
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
at each time requires that we enforce the identities

f=0,\quad f_{s}=1,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g=0,\quad g_{s}=1\qquad\mbox{ at }\qquad\theta=0,

and the identities

f=0,\quad f_{s}=-1,\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g>0,\quad g_{s}=0\qquad\mbox{ at }\qquad\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}.

However, we may impose only one Dirichlet or Neumann condition at each boundary for the functions that we evolve numerically. We proceed to resolve this issue in two steps, as follows:

We begin by defining

\tilde{\rho}:=\log\rho,\qquad\tilde{f}:=\log f,\qquad\tilde{g}:=\log g,

and

Q:=\rho^{2}\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\left(\frac{\cos(2\theta)}{f^{2}}+\frac{2}{g^{2}}\right).

Then using (
11
) and diligently doing some algebra, we obtain the system:

(12a)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,\tilde{\rho}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{\theta\theta}-Q\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}-\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}^{2}+\tilde{f}_{\theta}^{2}+2\tilde{g}_{\theta}^{2}+Q_{\theta},

(12b)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,\tilde{f}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}_{\theta\theta}+Q\tilde{f}_{\theta}-2\frac{\rho^{2}}{g^{4}}f^{2},

(12c)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,\tilde{g}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tilde{g}_{\theta\theta}+Q\tilde{g}_{\theta}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{g^{4}}\big(2f^{2}-4g^{2}\big).

Next we define
A,Y,Z
via the relations

\rho=:e^{A+Y+Z},\qquad f=:e^{Y}\cos(\theta)\,g,\qquad g=:e^{A}\sin(\theta),

noting that

Q=2e^{2Z}\left(\cot(2\theta)+e^{2Y}\cot(\theta)\right).

Then we obtain this system:

(13a)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,A_{t}

\displaystyle=A_{\theta\theta}+QA_{\theta}+\csc^{2}(\theta)\left(2e^{4Y+2Z}\cos^{2}(\theta)-4e^{2Y+2Z}-1\right)

\displaystyle\quad+2e^{2Z}\cot(\theta)\left(\cot(2\theta)+e^{2Y}\cot(\theta)\right),

(13b)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,Y_{t}

\displaystyle=Y_{\theta\theta}+QY_{\theta}-2e^{2Z}\tan(\theta)\cot(2\theta)-2e^{2Y+2Z}-\sec^{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle\quad+4e^{2Y+2Z}\csc^{2}(\theta)\left(1-e^{2Y}\cos^{2}(\theta)\right),

(13c)

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\,Z_{t}

\displaystyle=Z_{\theta\theta}-Q(2A_{\theta}+2Y_{\theta}+Z_{\theta})+Q_{\theta}-(A_{\theta}+Y_{\theta}+Z_{\theta})^{2}+3\big(A_{\theta}+\cot(\theta)\big)^{2}

\displaystyle+\big(Y_{\theta}-\tan(\theta)\big)^{2}+2\big(A_{\theta}+\cot(\theta)\big)\big(Y_{\theta}-\tan(\theta)\big)+2e^{2Y+2Z}

\displaystyle+2e^{2Y+2Z}\cot^{2}(\theta)(e^{2Y}-1)+\sec^{2}(\theta)+\csc^{2}(\theta)+2e^{2Z}\cot(2\theta)\big(\tan(\theta)-\cot(\theta)\big).

Now consider what boundary conditions we need to impose smoothness. We need
{A_{\theta}}=0
at
\theta=0
and
\theta=\pi/2
. We need
Y=0
and
{Y_{\theta}}=0
at
\theta=0
and
{Y_{\theta}}=0
at
\theta=\pi/2
. We need
Z=0
and
{Z_{\theta}}=0
at both
\theta=0
and
\theta=\pi/2
. Thus,
A
is the sort of variable we need, but
Y
and
Z
are not. However, defining
B
and
C
by

Y=:B{\sin^{2}}(\theta),\qquad Z=:C{\sin^{2}}(2\theta),

we see that the conditions needed for smoothness can be imposed as long as
{B_{\theta}}={C_{\theta}}=0
at
\theta=\pi/2
.

After some tedious algebra we find that equation (
13b
) yields

(14)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}B

\displaystyle={\rho^{-2}}\biggl[{B_{\theta\theta}}+\left(Q+4\cot(\theta)\right){B_{\theta}}+B\left({{2+2{e^{2Z}}-4{e^{2Y+2Z}}}\over{{\sin^{2}}(\theta)}}\right)

\displaystyle-4B(1+{e^{2Z}}+{e^{2Y+2Z}})+{e^{2Z}}\left({{-2-2{e^{2Y}}+4{e^{4Y}}}\over{{\sin^{2}}(\theta)}}\right)

\displaystyle+4\left({{e^{2Z}-1}\over{{\sin^{2}}(2\theta)}}\right)+4{e^{2Y+2Z}}\left({{1+2Y-{e^{2Y}}}\over{{\sin^{4}}(\theta)}}\right)\biggr].

Similarly some tedious algebra applied to equation (
13c
) yields the following:

(15)

\displaystyle{\partial_{t}}C

\displaystyle={\rho^{-2}}\biggl[{C_{\theta\theta}}+\left(Q+8\cot(2\theta)\right){C_{\theta}}-8C(2+{e^{2Z}}+{e^{2Y+2Z}})

\displaystyle+\left({{1-{e^{2Y+2Z}}}\over{{\sin^{2}}(\theta)}}\right)\left({{2{A_{\theta}}}\over{\sin(2\theta)}}-4C\right)

\displaystyle+\left({{1-{e^{2Z}}}\over{{\sin^{2}}(2\theta)}}\right)\left(-8C+4\cot(2\theta){A_{\theta}}+2\cos(2\theta)\left(\tan(\theta){B_{\theta}}+2B\right)-6\right)

\displaystyle+{e^{2Y+2Z}}\left({{\cosh(2Y)-1}\over{{\sin^{4}}(\theta)}}\right)+8\left(1+{\cos^{2}}(\theta)\right)\left({{1+2Z-{e^{2Z}}}\over{{\sin^{4}}(2\theta)}}\right)+2{{\left({{A_{\theta}}\over{\sin(2\theta)}}\right)}^{2}}

In summary, we evolve the variables

,

and

), (

), and (

), respectively. The Neumann boundary conditions are that
{A_{\theta}},\;{B_{\theta}}
and

vanish at

and

.

7.
The blowdown soliton

We first derive a necessary and sufficient condition for a metric of the form (
1
) to be Kähler, under the symmetry assumptions
f_{1}=f
and
f_{2}=f_{3}=g
.
Calabi observed
[
2
]
that any
\mathrm{U}(2)
-invariant Kähler metric on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\backslash(0,0)
may be written in complex form as

(16)

G_{\mathbb{C}}=\Big\{e^{-r}\varphi\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}+e^{-2r}(\varphi_{r}-\varphi)\,\bar{z}_{\alpha}z_{\beta}\Big\}\,\mathrm{d}z^{\alpha}\otimes\mathrm{d}\bar{z}^{\beta},

with respect to his coordinate
r:=\log(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2})
.
Written in real coordinates, the same metric becomes

(17)

G_{\mathbb{R}}=\varphi_{r}\Big(\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{d}r\otimes\mathrm{d}r+\omega^{1}\otimes\omega^{1}\Big)+\varphi\Big(\omega^{2}\otimes\omega^{2}+\omega^{3}\otimes\omega^{3}\Big),

which shows that
\varphi>0
and
\varphi_{r}>0
are necessary conditions for this to be a Kähler metric.

A comparison of equations (
1
) and (
17
) shows that a coordinate transformation is needed to write a Kähler metric with
respect to arclength
s
, where
\mathrm{d}s=\rho\,\mathrm{d}\theta
. To see this, we observe that if
s
and
r
are related by the
ode

\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}s}=\frac{2}{f},

then equation (
1
) takes the form

(18)

G=f^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{d}r\otimes\mathrm{d}r+\omega^{1}\otimes\omega^{1}\Big)+g^{2}\Big(\omega^{2}\otimes\omega^{2}+\omega^{3}\otimes\omega^{3}\Big),

which matches (
17
) if and only if
f^{2}=\varphi_{r}
and
g^{2}=\varphi
are related by

(19)

f=gg_{s}.

It follows that a cohomogeneity-one metric with the
\mathrm{U}(2)
symmetries we have imposed is Kähler if and only if condition (
19
) holds.
So, we may use the ratio
f/(gg_{s})
to provide a local measurement of the closeness of a metric to the Kähler subspace.

Now, it follows from Lemma 6.1 and equation (27) of
[
5
]
with
\lambda=-1
,
\mu=\sqrt{2}
, and
\nu=0
, that the metric on the blowdown
soliton
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{-1}
is determined by a function
\phi(r)
that solves the separable first-order
ode

(20)

\phi_{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi-(\sqrt{2}-1)-\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\phi^{-1}.

One can solve (
20
) implicitly up to an arbitrary constant
\eta
, obtaining

(21)

e^{r+\eta}=\frac{\phi-1}{\big(\phi+\sqrt{2}-1\big)^{\sqrt{2}-1}}.

As shown in
[
5
]
, the
\mathcal{L}^{2}_{-1}
soliton is complete and exists for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. Using this fact and the positivity of
\phi
and
\phi_{r}
,
it is not difficult to see from (
20
) and (
21
) that
\phi\nearrow\infty

as
r\nearrow\infty
and that the metric is asymptotically conical in the precise sense that

(22)

\frac{\phi_{r}}{\phi}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\qquad\mbox{ as }\qquad\phi\nearrow\infty.

Specifically, if we define
\gamma:=2^{-1/4}
, then the asymptotic cone of the blowdown soliton corresponds to

(23)

f=\gamma^{2}s\quad\mbox{ and }\quad g=\gamma s\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\frac{f^{2}}{g^{2}}=\gamma^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

All sectional curvatures of the cone vanish except
\kappa_{23}=\frac{4(\sqrt{2}-1)}{s^{2}}
.

8.
Results of simulations

The simulations are performed using standard numerical methods for parabolic equations: centered differences for spatial derivatives and Euler’s method for time evolution. We describe the method here in more detail.

Any function
F(t,\theta)
is represented by the values
F^{k}_{i}
that the function takes at points
\theta_{i}
equally spaced with spacing
\Delta\theta
and with times
t_{k}
equally spaced with spacing
\Delta t
. We use standard centered finite differences, so that
F_{\theta}
and
F_{\theta\theta}
are approximated by

(24)

{F_{\theta}}={\frac{{F^{k}_{i+1}}-{F^{k}_{i-1}}}{2\Delta\theta}}

and

(25)

{F_{\theta\theta}}={\frac{{F^{k}_{i+1}}+{F^{k}_{i-1}}-2{F^{k}_{i}}}{{(\Delta\theta)}^{2}}},

respectively.

Time evolution is carried out using the Euler method, so that

(26)

{F^{k+1}_{i}}={F^{k}_{i}}+\Delta t{\partial_{t}}F,

where
{\partial_{t}}F
is the finite difference version of the right hand side of equation (
13a
), (
14
) or (
15
) with the spatial derivatives evaluated using equations (
24
) and (
25
). The standard von Neumann stability analysis of equation (
26
) reveals that the time step must satisfy the Courant condition

(27)

\Delta t<{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}{{({\rho_{\rm min}}\Delta\theta)}^{2}}.

where
\rho_{\rm min}
is the minimum value of
\rho
.

9.
Directions for future work

Our results here provide evidence in favor of the conjecture that unstable perturbations of the Fubini–Study Einstein metric — perturbations which are conformal and not Kähler —
develop finite-time local singularities modeled by the Kähler blowdown soliton
\mathcal{L}_{-1}^{2}
discovered in
[
5
]
, but with the opposite complex structure.

Short of proving the full conjecture, a useful next step would be to derive formal matched asymptotics that describe how parabolic dilations at these singularities approach

\mathcal{L}_{-1}^{2}
, analogous to the matched asymptotics formally derived in
[
1
]
for solutions that approach the Bryant soliton. However, because
\mathcal{L}_{-1}^{2}
is
only known in the implicit form (
21
), a somewhat more approachable next step would be to develop formal matched asymptotics for parabolic dilations that
approach its asymptotic cone
(f,g)=(\gamma^{2}s,\gamma s)
, where
\gamma=2^{-1/4}
, as described in (
23
).
