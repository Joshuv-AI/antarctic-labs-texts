Rankings in directed configuration models with heavy tailed in-degrees
Xing Shi Cai †, Pietro Caputo ††, Guillem Perarnau ∗, Matteo Quattropani ∗∗

Abstract
. We consider the extremal values of the stationary distribution of sparse directed random graphs with given degree sequences and their relation to the extremal values of the in-degree sequence. The graphs are generated by the directed configuration model. Under the assumption of bounded ( 2 + η ) (2+\eta) -moments on the in-degrees and of bounded out-degrees, we obtain tight comparisons between the maximum value of the stationary distribution and the maximum in-degree. Under the further assumption that the order statistics of the in-degrees have a power-law behavior, we show that the extremal values of the stationary distribution also have a power-law behavior with the same index. In the same setting, we prove that these results extend to the PageRank scores of the random digraph, thus confirming a version of the so-called power-law hypothesis . Along the way, we establish several facts about the model, including the mixing time cutoff and the characterization of the typical values of the stationary distribution, which were previously obtained under the assumption of bounded in-degrees.



1.
Introduction

The stationary distribution of the simple random walk on a directed graph (digraph) provides a natural measure
of the ranking of its nodes.
The potentially non-local nature of the stationary distribution in
directed networks makes the analysis of ranking a challenging task,
and it is of interest to relate the ranking statistics to much simpler local statistics such as the
in-degrees of the nodes.
In this paper we compare the maximum values of the stationary distribution to the maximum values of
the in-degrees in the setting of directed configuration models with sparse degree sequences.
We start with a presentation of the model and the main results, and then return to a general
discussion of the problems involved, main motivations and relations to previous work.

1.1.
The model and the assumptions

Let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a set of
n
nodes.
Let
\mathbf{d}_{n}=((d^{-}_{1},d_{1}^{+}),\dots,(d^{-}_{n},d^{+}_{n}))\in\mathbb{N}^{2\times n}
be a bi-degree sequence with

(1.1)

m\coloneqq\sum_{v\in[n]}d^{+}_{v}=\sum_{v\in[n]}d^{-}_{v}.

The directed configuration model is the random directed multigraph (digraph) on
[n]
,

G=G_{n}
, generated as follows:
Assign
d^{-}_{v}

heads
and
d^{+}_{v}

tails
to vertex
v
,
match the
m
heads and the
m
tails with a uniformly random bijection,
and finally add a directed edge
(u,v)
for each tail from
u
that is matched to a head from
v
.

For a node
v\in[n]
,
d_{v}^{-}
and
d_{v}^{+}
are called the in-degree and the out-degree of
v
respectively.
Let
\Delta^{\pm}=\Delta^{\pm}_{n}\coloneqq\max_{v\in[n]}d^{\pm}_{v}
denote the maximum in/out-degree.
Unless otherwise specified, we will
always
assume that the sequence of bi-degree sequences
(\mathbf{d}_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}})
satisfies the following condition:

Assumption 1.1
.

There exist constants
\eta,C>0
and
K\geq 2
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

(i)

minimum out-degree:
\min_{v\in[n]}d_{v}^{+}\geq 2
;

(ii)

bounded maximum out-degree:
\Delta^{+}_{n}\leq K

(iii)

bounded
(2+\eta)
-moment for in-degrees:

(1.2)

\sum_{v\in[n]}(d_{v}^{-})^{2+\eta}\leq Cn.

Note that we do not assume any lower bound on the in-degrees. For the sake of brevity, we will use
\mathbf{d}_{n}
to refer to the sequence
(\mathbf{d}_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}})
.

1.2.
Definitions and notations

We recall some standard definitions and fix some notations.
We write

\mathbf{1}(E)=\mathbf{1}_{E}
for the indicator function of an event
E
.
A sequence of events
(E_{n})_{n\geq 0}
occurs
with high probability
if
\mathbb{P}(E_{n})={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}(E_{n})]\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
We write
X_{n}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}X
whenever a sequence of random variables
X_{n}

converges in probability to the random variable
X
, i.e., when
\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert X-X_{n}\rvert>\varepsilon\right)\to 0
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
We also use
o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
to denote an implicit sequence random variables which converges to
0
in
probability
[
34
]
. To avoid repetitions, it is often understood that our inequalities hold provided that
n
is sufficiently large.

Under
1.1
, the probability that
G
is simple (neither loops nor multiple edges) is bounded away from zero
[
11
,
33
]
. Furthermore, conditional on being simple,
G
has the uniform distribution over simple digraphs on
[n]
with degree sequence
\mathbf{d}_{n}
. Thus, all results in this paper that hold whp can be transferred to uniform simple digraphs.

Under an assumption weaker than
1.1
,
it is known that with high probability
the resulting digraph has a unique strongly connected component
which is globally attractive
[
15
]
; this is false in general if vertices of out-degree at most one are allowed.
In particular, there exists a unique stationary distribution
\pi
characterized by the equations

(1.3)

\pi(x)=\sum_{y\in[n]}\pi(y)P(y,x)\,,\qquad x\in[n],

with the normalization
\sum_{x\in[n]}\pi(x)=1
. Here
P=P_{G}
is the transition matrix of the simple random walk on the multigraph
G
, defined as

(1.4)

P(y,x)=\frac{m(y,x)}{d^{+}_{y}},

where we write
m(y,x)
for the multiplicity of the directed edge
(y,x)
in
G
.

We write
(X_{t},t\geq 0)
for the simple random walk on
G
.
Thus,
P^{t}(v,\cdot)
denotes the distribution of
X_{t}
on
[n]
conditioned on
X_{0}=v
.
If
t\geq 0
is not an integer,
for simplicity we often write
t
instead of
\lfloor t\rfloor
so that for example
P^{t}(v,\cdot)

represents the distribution of the walk after
\lfloor t\rfloor
steps.
A standard measure of the distance to stationarity is
\|P^{t}(v,\cdot)-\pi\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}
,
where the total variation distance between two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
[n]
is defined by

(1.5)

\|\mu-\nu\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}=\max_{A\subset[n]}\,\left\lvert\mu(A)-\nu(A)\right\rvert.

The
mixing time
of the random walk
(X_{t},t\geq 0)

is defined, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, by

(1.6)

T_{{\mathrm{mix}}}(\varepsilon)=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}\;:\;\max_{v\in[n]}\|P^{t}(v,\cdot),\pi\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}<\varepsilon\}.

The
in-degree distribution

\mu_{\rm in}
and
out-degree distribution

\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}
on
[n]
are defined by

(1.7)

\mu_{\rm in}(v)=\frac{d_{v}^{-}}{m}\,,\quad\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}(v)=\frac{d_{v}^{+}}{m},\qquad\forall v\in[n].

Following
[
13
]
, we define the

entropic time
by

(1.8)

T_{{\mathrm{ent}}}=\frac{\log n}{\mathrm{H}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathrm{H}=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mu_{\rm in}(v)\log(d^{+}_{v}).

1.3.
Results

1.3.1.
Mixing time

Our first result concerns the mixing time and the cutoff phenomenon.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1
.
Then, for all
\rho\neq 1
,

(1.9)

\max_{x\in[n]}\left|\|P^{\,\rho\,T_{{\mathrm{ent}}}}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}-\mathbf{1}({\rho<1})\right|\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}0.

In particular, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, with high probability
T_{{\mathrm{mix}}}(\varepsilon)=(1+o(1))T_{{\mathrm{ent}}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

Theorem

1.1
is an extension of the cutoff results from
[
12
]
which were obtained in
the case of bounded degrees
\Delta^{\pm}=O(1)
.
It shows that
T_{{\mathrm{mix}}}(\varepsilon)
is, to leading order,
independent of
\varepsilon
, that is the Markov chain satisfies the
cutoff phenomenon
.
Theorem 2 in
[
12
]
considers also the cutoff window,
namely the behavior of the function
t\mapsto\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}
on the finer
scale
t=T_{{\mathrm{ent}}}+aw_{n}
,
where
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
w_{n}=O(\sqrt{\log n})
,
and showed that it approaches a universal Gaussian shape for all
x\in[n]
.
One can check that the techniques we use here to prove
Theorem

1.1
are sufficient to obtain this refinement in our more general setting.

1.3.2.
Typical values of the stationary distribution

Our second result addresses the convergence
of the empirical distribution

(1.10)

\psi_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\delta_{n\pi(v)},

where
\delta_{a}
is the Dirac distribution centered at
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Note that
\psi_{n}
represents the law of
n\pi(v)
when
v
is picked uniformly at random in
[n]
.
We recall that the
1
-Wasserstein (or Kantorovich-Rubinstein) distance between two probability
measures
\mu
,
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
is defined by

(1.11)

\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f}\left\lvert\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\,\mathrm{d}\nu\right\rvert,

where the supremum runs over of
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq|x-y|
(see, for
example,
[
49
, Chapter 6]
).

Consider the sequence of deterministic
probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\coloneqq[0,\infty)
,
(\mathcal{L}_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
, where for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is defined as the law of the random variable
X_{n}
that satisfies
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=1
, and

(1.12)

X_{n}\overset{d}{=}\frac{n}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\mathcal{I}}^{-}}Z_{k},

where
\mathcal{I}
is a uniformly sampled vertex in
[n]
and
\{Z_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}

are independent and identically distributed random variables satisfying the stochastic fixed point equation

(1.13)

Z_{1}\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{J}}^{+}}\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\mathcal{J}}^{-}}Z_{k},

where
\mathcal{J}
is a random vertex in
[n]
distributed as
\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}
. Existence and uniqueness of solutions to recursive distributional equations of the type
Eq.

1.12
is well known; see for example
[
2
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1
.
We have

(1.14)

\mathcal{W}_{1}\left(\psi_{n},\mathcal{L}_{n}\right)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}0.

1.3.3.
Extremal values of the stationary distribution

Our main concern in this paper will be the behavior of the extremal values
of the stationary distribution. We start with
the maximum

\pi_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{v\in[n]}\pi(v)
and its relation to the maximum in-degree
\Delta^{-}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1
. Then,

(i)

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that, with high probability

(1.15)

\pi_{\max}\leq C\log(n)\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m}.

(ii)

If
\Delta^{-}\to\infty
, then
\forall\varepsilon>0
, with high probability

(1.16)

\pi_{\max}\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m}.

Remark 1.5
.

The bounds
Eq.

1.15
and
Eq.

1.16
on
\pi_{\max}
are essentially optimal under this
generality. Clearly, if the graph is Eulerian, that is if
d_{v}^{+}=d_{v}^{-}
for all
v\in[n]
, then
\pi=\mu_{\rm in}=\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}
and thus
\pi_{\max}=\Delta^{-}/m
. On the other hand,
Theorem 1.6 in
[
18
]
shows the existence of bounded degree sequences for which

\pi_{\max}=\log^{1-o(1)}(n)\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m}
.
We remark that the assumption
\Delta^{-}\to\infty
in
Eq.

1.16
is not really restrictive since by
[
18
]
we already know that
the bound
Eq.

1.16
is always satisfied if
\Delta^{-}=O(1)
.
It is an interesting open question to determine whether the logarithmic term is necessary given that

\Delta^{-}
diverges sufficiently fast.
In
Section

9
, we refine
Eq.

1.15
for a wide class of sequences,
called
extremal
,
proving that in these cases

(1.17)

\pi_{\max}=(1+o(1))\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m}.

In particular, this proves in a strong sense the asymptotic tightness of
Eq.

1.16
.
Moreover, we will see that for such extremal sequences the vertex with the maximum in-degree
coincides with the vertex with maximum stationary value.

1.3.4.
Power-law behavior

We turn to the analysis of the order statistics of the stationary distribution,
in the case where the in-degrees have an approximate power-law behavior.
We will consider the following notion of heavy tails.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{n}
denote the set of empirical distributions of size
n
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,
that is the set of probability measures of the form

(1.18)

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\delta_{x_{v}},

for some fixed vector
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
.
For any
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
and
t\geq 0
, let

(1.19)

\mu_{n}(t,\infty)=\frac{1}{n}\,|\{i\in[n]:\,x_{i}>t\}|

denote the right tail of
\mu_{n}
.

Definition 1.6
.

Given a constant
\kappa>0
, and a sequence of measures
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
,
we say that
\mu_{n}
has
power-law behavior with index
\kappa
if for all
\varepsilon>0
and
for all
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,

(1.20)

n^{-a\kappa-\varepsilon}\leq\mu_{n}(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon},\qquad\mu_{n}(n^{1/\kappa+\varepsilon},\infty)=0,

for all sufficiently large
n
. If the measures
\mu_{n}
are random elements in
\mathcal{M}_{n}
,
we say that
\mu_{n}
has
power-law behavior with index
\kappa
with high probability
,
if for all
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability
Eq.

1.20
holds for any
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
.

Since
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
has minimal mass
1/n
, the upper bounds in
Eq.

1.20
are equivalent to the requirement that
\mu_{n}(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
a>0
.
Notice that if
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)
are independent and identically distributed random variables with probability density
f(t)\propto\min\{1,t^{-1-\kappa}\}
,

t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, for some
\kappa>0
,
then the sequence of random empirical measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
Eq.

1.18
has power-law behavior with
index
\kappa
with high probability (see, e.g.,
[
45
]
).

We apply this notion to our degree sequence.
Let
\phi
be the empirical in-degree distribution; that is, for
k\geq 0
,

(1.21)

\phi(k)=\phi_{n}(k)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{1}(d_{v}^{-}=k).

Both
\phi,\psi
define sequences of distributions
\phi_{n},\psi_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
,
but for simplicity we often drop the subscript
n
from our notation.
The distribution
\phi
has mean value
m/n
,
while
\psi
has mean value
1
for all
n
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1

and assume that its empirical in-degree distribution
\phi
has power-law behavior
with index
\kappa>2
.
Then, with high probability the distribution
\psi
has power-law behavior
with the same index
\kappa
,
that is for all
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability,
\psi(n^{1/\kappa+\varepsilon},\infty)=0
and for all
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,

(1.22)

n^{-a\kappa-\varepsilon}\leq\psi(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}.

Remark 1.8
.

If we only assume
1.1
,
setting
\kappa_{0}=2+\eta
,
where
\eta>0
is such that
Eq.

1.2
holds,
then we will see that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\psi
satisfies the following upper bound with high probability:
for all
a>0
,

(1.23)

\psi(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa_{0}+\varepsilon},

that is the right tail of
\psi
is dominated by a heavy tail with index
\kappa_{0}
.
In some sense, this indicates that among all in-degree distributions with bounded
\kappa_{0}
-moment, the ones with power-law behavior with index
\kappa_{0}
“maximize” the upper tail of the stationary distribution.

Eq.

1.23
will be proved in
Section

7
together with
Theorem

1.7
,
as a consequence of a more general upper bound on
\psi(n^{a},\infty)
.

1.3.5.
PageRank surfer

Next, we discuss the power-law behavior of PageRank.
Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
\lambda
be a probability distribution on
[n]
,
which we refer to as the
teleporting probability

and the
teleporting distribution
respectively. The factor
1-\alpha
is also referred in the literature as the
damping factor
.
Consider the
(\alpha,\lambda)
-PageRank surfer,
that is the Markov chain with transition matrix

(1.24)

P_{\alpha,\lambda}(x,y)=(1-\alpha)P(x,y)+\alpha\lambda(y).

We call
(\alpha,\lambda)
-PageRank score the stationary distribution
\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}
of this Markov chain, which is known to always be unique and to satisfy

(1.25)

\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{k}\lambda P^{k},

see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.
We will further assume that the teleporting distribution
\lambda
is uniform up to multiplicative sub-polynomial factors,
that is, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

(1.26)

n^{-1-\varepsilon}\leq\lambda(x)\leq n^{-1+\varepsilon},

for all sufficiently large
n
, uniformly in
x\in[n]
.

Let
\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
be the empirical distribution in
Eq.

1.18
corresponding to
x_{v}=n\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(v)
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1

and assume that its empirical in-degree distribution
\phi
has
power-law behavior with index
\kappa>2
.
For any constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and probability distribution
\lambda
satisfying
Eq.

1.26
,
with high probability
\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}
has power-law behavior with the same index
\kappa
,
that is, for all
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability,
\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}(n^{1/\kappa+\varepsilon},\infty)=0
and for all
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,

(1.27)

n^{-a\kappa-\varepsilon}\leq\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}.

Remark 1.10
.

We will actually show a stronger result which holds for non-constant
\alpha
. Namely, that the upper bound
\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}
holds
uniformly
for arbitrary sequences
\alpha=\alpha_{n}\in[0,1]
. Indeed, as far as the upper bounds on the stationary distribution are concerned, it turns out that the presence of the parameter
\alpha
can only make our analysis simpler; see
Remark

8.2
. Moreover, the lower bound

\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}(n^{a},\infty)\geq n^{-a\kappa-\varepsilon}
holds under the only assumption that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}<1
; see
Section

8
.

Remark 1.11
.

Concerning the maximum PageRank score we will see that the following bounds hold with high probability for any bi-degree sequence satisfying
1.1
, for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and any probability
\lambda
on
[n]
:

(1.28)

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda_{\min}\,\frac{\Delta^{-}}{\Delta^{+}}\leq\max_{x\in[n]}\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(x)\leq C\log(n)\left(\lambda_{\max}+\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m}\right),

where
C
is an absolute constant,
\lambda_{\min}=\min_{y\in[n]}\lambda(y)
, and
\lambda_{\max}=\max_{x\in[n]}\lambda(x)
. These bounds will be a simple consequence of our main results; see
Remark

8.3
.

1.4.
Motivation and related work

Random walks on random undirected graphs have attracted a lot of attention in the last
decade
[
9
,
10
,
29
,
39
]
.
Contrarily, much less is known in random digraphs.
The non-reversible nature of random walks in directed environments poses the challenge of developing
new techniques to study their properties.

One of the most natural models for random digraphs is the directed configuration model (
dcm
),
which has been introduced in the literature as a directed analogue
of the configuration model
[
21
,
25
,
41
]
.
We refer the interested reader to
[
15
,
25
]
for results
on its component structure and to
[
16
,
18
,
48
]
for the study of its distance profile.
Bordenave, the second author and Salez
[
12
]

recently initiated the study of random walks on the directed configuration model.
Provided that the minimum out-degree is at least
2
and the maximum in-degree and out-degree are bounded,
they showed that the mixing time coincides with the
entropic time
,
defined in
Eq.

1.8
,
exhibits cutoff and has a Gaussian behavior inside the cutoff window.
Moreover, they showed that the stationary distribution of a uniformly random vertex
\mathcal{I}

converges (in the
1
-Wasserstein sense) to the solution
of the stochastic fixed point equation (
sfpe
) displayed in
Eq.

1.12
.
These results are extended to other models of non-reversible sparse random Markov chains in
[
13
]
.
Our results in
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.3
show that the hypothesis on the degree sequence in
[
12
]

can be further relaxed to
1.1
.

One of the questions left open in
[
12
]
is
the determination of the extremal behavior of the stationary values.
The second and fourth authors
[
18
]
showed
that, in the bounded degree setting, the extremal (minimum and maximum) values of the stationary distribution
exhibit logarithmic fluctuations around the average value,
the exponents of the logarithm being essentially determined
by the minimum and maximum in- and out-degrees. In particular, regarding
\pi_{\max}
,
Theorem 1.6 in
[
18
]
shows that if
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
is a bi-degree sequence satisfying

2\leq d_{v}^{\pm}=O(1)
, and such that there are linearly many vertices with degrees

(\Delta^{-},\delta^{+})
, where
\delta^{+}=\min_{v}d_{v}^{+}
, then there exists a constant
C>1
such that with high probability

(1.29)

\frac{n\pi_{\max}}{\log^{1-\kappa_{0}}n}\in[C^{-1},C],

where
\kappa_{0}=\frac{\log\delta^{+}}{\log\Delta^{-}}
.

Theorem

1.4
shows that if we allow the in-degrees to grow with the order of the digraph then

\pi_{\max}
will have much larger fluctuations.

In a similar spirit, the first and third authors
[
17
]

proved that by dropping the condition on the minimum in-degree,
the minimum stationary value may become polynomially smaller than the average,
with the exponent given by the solution of an optimization problem
involving subcritical branching processes
and large deviation rate functions of the bi-degree distribution. Moreover, their results also give an implicit description of the lower tail of
\psi
, complementing
Remark

1.8
.
In both works
[
17
,
18
]
,
controlling the minimum stationary values allows us
to estimate the cover time of a random walk in directed configuration model.

Stationary measures have also been studied for other random digraphs models.
Cooper and Frieze
[
26
]
determined the stationary distribution
of the directed Erdös-Rényi random graph in the strong connectivity regime,
motivated by their systematic study of the cover time in random graph models.
Addario-Berry, Balle and the third author
[
1
]

provided estimations for the extremal values of the stationary distribution
in random out-regular digraphs,
with applications to random deterministic finite automata.

While the analysis of random walks on directed configuration model only started recently, its stationary distribution has in
fact received a lot of attention under the framework of the PageRank algorithm. PageRank was
introduced in
[
43
]
as a ranking measure for the webgraph and is a core element in
Google’s search engine. The PageRank score is simply defined as the stationary distribution of the
PageRank surfer defined in
Eq.

1.24
. We refer to
[
19
]
for
the mixing properties of the PageRank surfer on directed configuration model. Compared to the in-degree ranking, the
PageRank score is less susceptible to assign high priority to spam pages
[
32
]
.
Nevertheless, empirical observations give a high average correlation between in-degrees and
PageRank
[
3
]
. The so-called
power-law hypothesis
ventures a more precise
description for scale-free networks: if the in-degree of a network is power-law distributed, then
its PageRank score also follows a power-law distribution with the same exponent. This has been
experimentally confirmed in several real-world
networks
[
27
,
44
,
46
]
, in the particular case of the webgraph, the
in-degree and PageRank are both approximately power-law distributions with index
\kappa\approx 1.1
. The
effect of the teleporting factor
\alpha
has also been studied in
[
8
]
, observing
that the top
10\%
ranked elements follow a power-law distribution regardless of
\alpha
.

The abundance of empirical evidence has motivated the mathematical analysis of the power-law hypothesis.
A series of papers
[
38
,
50
,
51
]
proposed an idealized stochastic
model proving that the power-law distributions of the in-degree and of the PageRank score of a uniformly random
vertex
\mathcal{I}
only differ by a multiplicative factor.
Chen, Litvak
and Olvera-Cravioto
[
22
,
23
]
initiated the rigorous analysis of PageRank on directed configuration model,
proving that the score of
\mathcal{I}
can be approximated by the PageRank score of the root of certain
infinite random tree, under the assumption that the in- and out-degrees are independently
distributed. In particular, if the in-degree distribution of
\mathcal{I}
is a power-law, so is its
PageRank. Olvera-Cravioto has recently extended these results to degree-degree correlated
distributions
[
42
]
. In particular, the distribution of the PageRank of
\mathcal{I}

weakly converges to the attractive endogenous solution of an stochastic fixed point equation that
generalizes
Eq.

1.12
. The asymptotic properties of the solution imply that upper
tail of the PageRank of
\mathcal{I}
is asymptotically distributed as power-law with the right exponent.

The PageRank has also been studied in other directed random networks such as
inhomogeneous random graph
[
36
,
42
]
and the directed preferential attachment model
[
5
,
7
]
.
Remarkably, the power-law hypothesis is only partially true in directed preferential attachment model: PageRank exhibits a
power-law distribution with different index than the index of the in-degree distribution. An
approach based on local weak convergence was given in
[
31
]
, yielding lower bounds
for the PageRank of a random vertex for any sequence of digraphs that has a local weak limit.

All aforementioned results describe the PageRank score of a vertex

\mathcal{I}
picked uniformly at random in
[n]
, or of a fixed given vertex, as in the case
of the oldest vertex in directed preferential attachment model obtained
in
[
7
]
. However, in most of the applications (such as web indexing), it is of
foremost importance to identify the top ranked elements
[
6
]
.
To our best knowledge,

Theorem

1.9
is the first result that establishes the power-law hypothesis in the
large
deviation
sense, providing the shape of the upper tail of the PageRank distribution in directed configuration model, not
only the upper tail for a typical vertex in the bulk of the digraph.

We refer to
Section

10
for a discussion of open problems and future research directions.

2.
Preliminary results

2.1.
Bounded moments

We will use frequently the following deterministic property of degree sequences
with bounded
2+\eta
moment of in-degrees.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{d}}_{n}
be a bi-degree sequence satisfying
Eq.

1.2
with
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Then

(2.1)

\Delta^{-}=O(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\eta}{6}}).

Moreover, for any
S\subset[n]
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq n^{1-\eta}
,

(2.2)

\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}^{-}=O\Big((\lvert S\rvert n)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\eta^{2}}{12}}\Big).

Proof.

By Hölder’s inequality
[
28
, Theorem 1.5.2]
with
p=2+\eta
,

(2.3)

\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}^{-}\leq\Big(\sum_{v\in S}(d_{v}^{-})^{p}\Big)^{\frac{1}{p}}\lvert S\rvert^{1-\frac{1}{p}}=O\Big(\left(n/|S|\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\Big)\lvert S\rvert=O\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\eta}{6}}\lvert S\rvert^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{6}}\right),

where in the last inequality we used that
\frac{1}{p}\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\eta}{6}
for
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Taking
|S|=1
we obtain
Eq.

2.1
.
Finally, using
\lvert S\rvert\leq n^{1-\eta}
we obtain
Eq.

2.2
from
Eq.

2.3
.
∎

2.2.
Local structure

Let
\mathbf{d}_{n}=((d^{-}_{1},d_{1}^{+}),\dots,(d^{-}_{n},d^{+}_{n}))
be a bi-degree sequence. For each
v\in[n]
, assign a set
E^{-}_{v}
of
d^{-}_{v}
labeled heads, and a set
E^{+}_{v}
of
d^{+}_{v}
labeled tails, and let

E^{\pm}=\cup_{v\in[n]}E_{v}^{\pm}
. Throughout the paper, we will use
f
to denote heads in
E^{-}
and
e
to denote tails in
E^{+}
. Denote by
v_{e}
(or
v_{f}
) the vertex incident to
e
(or
f
).
Every bijection
\omega:E^{+}\to E^{-}
induces a multi-digraph
G=G_{n}(\omega)
with vertex set
[n]
and bi-degree sequence
\mathbf{d}_{n}
by assigning a directed edge to every pair of vertices
(v_{e},v_{f})
such that
\omega(e)=f
.
For simplicity, the multi-digraph
G
will be often referred to as the digraph.

For
h\in\mathbb{N}
, a
path
of length
h
is a sequence of edges

(2.4)

\mathfrak{p}=\left\{(e_{0},f_{1}),\left(e_{1},f_{2}\right),\dots,\left(e_{h-1},f_{h}\right)\right\},

where
e_{j-1}\in E^{+}
,
f_{j}\in E^{-}
,
\omega(e_{j-1})=f_{j}
,
and
v_{f_{j}}=v_{e_{j}}
for all
j\in[h]
.
If
x=v_{e_{0}}
and
y=v_{f_{h}}
,
we say that
\mathfrak{p}
is a path starting at
x
and ending at
y
.
The
weight
of the path
\mathfrak{p}
is the product of the inverse of the out-degrees of all
vertices along
\mathfrak{p}
except the last one, that is

(2.5)

\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})=\prod_{j=0}^{h-1}\frac{1}{d^{+}_{v_{e_{j}}}}.

Let
\mathcal{P}(x,y,h,G)
denote the set of all paths of length
h
starting at
x
and ending at
y
in
the multi-digraph
G
.
A path is called
simple
if it never visits the same vertex more than once.

For any
x\in[n]
and
h\in\mathbb{N}
, the out-neighborhood of
x
of depth
h
,
\mathcal{B}_{x}^{+}(h)
, is the subgraph induced by all paths of length at most
h
starting at
x
. Similarly, for any
y\in[n]
the in-neighborhood of
y
of depth
h
,
\mathcal{B}_{y}^{-}(h)
, is the subgraph induced by all paths of length at most
h
ending at
y
. We often identify
\mathcal{B}_{v}^{\pm}(h)
with its vertex set.
The boundary of
\mathcal{B}_{x}^{+}(h)
, that is the set of vertices
v
such that the shortest path starting at
x
and ending
v
has length
h
, is denoted by
\partial\mathcal{B}_{x}^{+}(h)
. Similarly
\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{-}(h)
represents the set of vertices
v
such that the shortest path starting at
v
and ending at
y
has length
h
.

2.2.1.
Sequential generation.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the digraph
G=G_{n}(\omega)
can be generated by matching tails and heads one at a time as follows.
Given a priority rule
\mathcal{R}
,

(i)

choose an unmatched head
f\in E^{-}
(if any) according to
\mathcal{R}
;

(ii)

choose an unmatched tail
e\in E^{+}
uniformly at random;

(iii)

set
\omega(e)=f
, and proceed.

Observe that the roles of tails and heads can be reversed.

To explore an in-neighborhood
\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)
,
we run the previous procedure with the priority rule given by the breath-first search order.
In other words, at each time we choose a head closest from
y
that has not been matched yet,
and pair it with a uniformly random unmatched tail.
We halt the procedure whenever all unmatched heads are at distance at least
h
from
y
.
Similarly, reversing tails and heads, one can explore out-neighborhoods.

As in many sparse random models, one may expect that the neighborhoods are locally tree-like.
It will be important to see how much they differ from a tree, motivating the following definition.
The
tree-excess
of a multi-digraph
G=(V,E)
is the number of additional edges it has with respect to a tree; that is,

(2.6)

\mathtt{Tx}(G)\coloneqq 1+|E(G)|-|V(G)|.

A step of the generating procedure is called a
collision
if the vertex
v_{f}
of the head
f
such that
\omega(e)=f
had been exposed during one of the previous pairings. Collisions indicate the appearance of additional edges in neighborhoods. In particular, the number of collisions in the breath-first search generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h)
is
\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h))
.

2.2.2.
Coupling with marked Galton-Watson trees.

For
x\in[n]
, let
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}
be the marked random tree with marks
\ell:V(\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y})\to[n]
, where
V(\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y})
is the set of vertices of the tree,
having root
a_{0}
with
\ell(a_{0})=y
and constructed iteratively with the following procedure,
starting with
a=a_{0}
:

(i)

Attach
d_{\ell(a)}^{-}
children to
a
.

(ii)

Assign to each child
b
of
a
independently at random the mark
\ell(b)=z\in[n]
with probability

d^{+}_{z}/m
.

(iii)

Choose the next
a
to be the element in the tree which is one of the closest to the root
among elements whose children have not been exposed.
Terminate if no such element exists; otherwise go to step (i).

Reversing the roles of in-degrees and out-degrees, we construct the random tree
\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
the subtree of
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}
containing the elements at distance at most
h
from the root,
and
\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
the subtree containing those at distance exactly
h
;
similarly for
\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x}(h)
and
\partial\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x}(h)
.
Notice that the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}
is obtained by gluing
d_{y}^{-}
independent copies of
a
Galton-Watson tree
with offspring distribution given by

(2.7)

p^{-}(k)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{v\in[n]}d^{+}_{v}\mathbf{1}_{d^{-}_{v}=k},\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

There is a natural coupling between the generating process of
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}_{v}(h)
and the construction of
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}_{v}(h)
.
We now describe the coupling of
\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)
and
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
.
The corresponding coupling of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h)
and
\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x}(h)
can be obtained by reversing the role of heads and tails.

Clearly, step (ii) in the construction from
Section

2.2.1
can be modified by picking
e
uniformly at random
among all (matched or unmatched) tails in
E^{+}
and rejecting the proposal if the tail was already matched.
The tree can then be generated by iteration of the same sequence of steps with the difference
that at step (ii) we never reject the proposal
and at step (iii) we add a new leaf to the current tree,
with mark
v
if
e\in E_{v}^{+}
,
together with a new set of
d^{-}_{v}
unmatched heads attached to it.

Call
\tau
the first time that a uniform random choice among all tails gives
e\in E^{+}_{v}
for some
mark
v
already in the tree.
By construction, the in-neighborhood and the tree coincide up to time
\tau
.
At the
k
-th iteration, the probability of picking a tail with a mark already used is at most
k\Delta^{+}/m
.
Therefore, by a union bound, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.8)

{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\leq k)\leq\frac{k^{2}\Delta^{+}}{m}.

2.3.
In-neighborhoods

We start with an estimate of the size of the in-neighborhoods and
then proceed with the analysis of the coupling with random trees described above.

For all
\varepsilon>0
define

(2.9)

h_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{\varepsilon\log n}{20\log\Delta^{+}},

and the event

(2.10)

\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\{\forall y\in[n],\,\lvert\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h_{\varepsilon})\rvert\leq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right\}.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon}\right)=1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Fix
y\in[n]
,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
h=h_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}_{y}=\mathcal{B}^{\pm}_{y}(h_{\varepsilon})
.
It is enough to show that, for all
n
large enough

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}|^{2}\right]\leq(d_{y}^{-})^{2}n^{\varepsilon}.

Indeed,
Eq.

2.11
and Markov’s inequality imply

(2.12)

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}|>n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}|^{2}\right]}{n^{1+2\varepsilon}}\leq\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{1+\varepsilon}}.

Therefore, by taking a union bound over
y\in[n]
and applying
Eq.

1.2

(2.13)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{S}^{-}_{\varepsilon})\geq 1-\sum_{y\in[n]}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{1+\varepsilon}}=1-o(1).

To prove

Eq.

2.11
, note that

(2.14)

|\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}|=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbf{1}_{y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}},

and therefore

(2.15)

\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}|^{2}\right]=\sum_{v\in[n]}\sum_{z\in[n]}\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v},\>y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}\right).

Observe that the out-neighborhood
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
has at most
(\Delta^{+})^{h}
edges
and at each step of the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
one has
a probability of matching a head of
y
bounded above
by
\frac{d_{y}^{-}}{m-(\Delta^{+})^{h}}\leq\frac{d_{y}^{-}}{n}
for
n
large enough.
Thus, by a union bound over all steps of the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
, for all
v\in[n]
,

(2.16)

\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}\right)\leq(\Delta^{+})^{h}\frac{d_{y}^{-}}{n}.

Next, for all
z\neq v
,

(2.17)

\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v},\>y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(y,z\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v},\>y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z},\>z\notin\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}\right).

For the event
y,z\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
to occur, one must match a head of
y
and a head of
z
during the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
. Since there are at most
(\Delta^{+})^{h}
steps during the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
, and as in
Eq.

2.16
one has a probability at most
\frac{d_{y}^{-}}{n}
to match a head of
y
at any given step (and
\frac{d_{z}^{-}}{n}
for
z
), a union bound gives

(2.18)

\mathbb{P}\left(y,z\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}\right)\leq(\Delta^{+})^{2h}\frac{d_{z}^{-}d_{y}^{-}}{n^{2}}.

Let us bound the second term in
Eq.

2.17
.
We generate first
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
and then
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}
.
Given the realization of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
, and assuming
y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
and
z\notin\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
,
the event
y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}
can be obtained in two ways: either we match a fresh head of
y
during the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}
, or we match a fresh head of another vertex
w\neq y
which was already discovered
during the generation of
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}
. Note that the first scenario, reasoning as
Eq.

2.16
, has probability at most
(\Delta^{+})^{2h}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}
. To handle the second scenario, define the event

E_{v,z,w}
that during the generation of
B_{v}^{+}
we match a fresh head of
y
and a fresh head of
w
, and then during the generation of
B_{z}^{+}
we match a fresh head of
w
.
This event then satisfies

(2.19)

\mathbb{P}(E_{v,z,w})\leq\frac{d_{y}^{-}\left(\Delta^{+}\right)^{h}}{n}\Big(d_{w}^{-}\frac{\left(\Delta^{+}\right)^{h}}{n}\Big)^{2}=\frac{d_{y}^{-}(d_{w}^{-})^{2}}{n^{3}}\,(\Delta^{+})^{3h}.

Summing over all possible choices of
w

and using

1.1
, we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v},\>y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z},\>z\notin\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v}\right)\leq(\Delta^{+})^{2h}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}+\sum_{w\neq y}\mathbb{P}(E_{v,z,w})

\displaystyle\leq(\Delta^{+})^{2h}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}+(\Delta^{+})^{3h}d_{y}^{-}\sum_{w\neq y}\frac{(d_{w}^{-})^{2}}{n^{3}}\leq\left(\Delta^{+}\right)^{4h}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}.

Combining
Eqs.

2.16
,
2.17
,
2.18
and
2.20
we obtain,

(2.21)

\mathbb{P}\left(y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{v},\>y\in\mathcal{B}^{+}_{z}\right)\leq(\Delta^{+})^{h}\frac{d_{y}^{-}}{n}\mathbf{1}(v=z)+(\Delta^{+})^{2h}\frac{d_{z}^{-}d_{y}^{-}}{n^{2}}+\left(\Delta^{+}\right)^{4h}\frac{(d_{y}^{-})^{2}}{n^{2}}\;.

Inserting the above estimates into
Eq.

2.15
, we obtain
Eq.

2.11
.
∎

In what follows
\mathbb{P}
denotes the probability under the coupling defined in
Section

2.2.2
.
We shall often take the parameter
\varepsilon>0
smaller than some
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\eta)
where
\eta>0
is the parameter appearing in
1.1
. To avoid repetitions, we will simply say that our statements hold for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and
h=h_{\varepsilon}
as in
Eq.

2.9
.
For any
y\in[n]
,

(2.22)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)\neq\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)\right)\leq(d_{y}^{-})^{2}n^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

Moreover, for any
a>0

(2.23)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)\rvert>d_{y}^{-}n^{a}\right)\leq n^{\frac{\varepsilon}{4}-2a}+(d_{y}^{-})^{2}n^{-\frac{1}{3}}.

Moreover, for any
y\in[n]
the coupling of
\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)
and
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
succeeds with high probability and there exists
\eta^{\prime}>0
such that

(2.24)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)\rvert>n^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta^{\prime}}\right)=o(1).

Proof.

Call
\mathcal{C}_{y}=\{\mathcal{B}^{-}_{y}(h)=\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)\}
.
Then, for all
a>0

(2.25)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{C}_{y}^{c}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)|>d_{y}^{-}n^{a}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)|\leq d_{y}^{-}n^{a},\>\mathcal{C}_{y}^{c}\right).

Reasoning as in
Eq.

2.8

(2.26)

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)|\leq d_{y}^{-}n^{a},\>\mathcal{C}_{y}^{c}\right)\leq\frac{\Delta^{+}(d_{y}^{-}n^{a})^{2}}{m}.

Notice that for each
j\geq 1
,

(2.27)

|\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(j)|=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{y}^{-}}Z^{(i)}_{j},

where the
Z^{(i)}_{j}
,
i=1,\dots,d_{y}^{-}
are independent and identically distributed random variables
representing the size of the
j
-th generation of a Galton-Watson process with offspring distribution given by
Eq.

2.7
.
The latter has expected value and variance

(2.28)

\nu=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}^{-}d_{v}^{+}=O(1)\,,\quad\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{v\in[n]}d_{v}^{+}(d_{v}^{-})^{2}-\nu^{2}=O(1),

where the estimates follow from
1.1
.

Setting
W_{j}=\nu^{-j}Z^{(1)}_{j}
, standard martingale computations
(see
[
4
, Chapter I.4]
) show that

(2.29)

\mathbb{E}[Z^{(1)}_{j}]=\nu^{j},\qquad{\rm Var}(Z^{(1)}_{j})=\nu^{2j}{\rm Var}(W_{j})=\nu^{2j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-1}\nu^{-\ell-2}\sigma^{2}\leq C\nu^{2j},

for some constant
C>0
. It follows that

(2.30)

\mathbb{E}[{\lvert\partial\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(j)\rvert}]=d_{y}^{-}\nu^{j},\qquad{\rm Var}(|\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(j)|)=d_{y}^{-}{\rm Var}(Z^{(1)}_{j})\leq C\nu^{2j}d_{y}^{-}.

By Markov’s inequality, uniformly in
j\leq h
, for all
s>0
,

(2.31)

\mathbb{P}\left(|\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(j)|>s\right)\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}[\lvert\partial\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(j)\rvert^{2}]=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,\left((d_{y}^{-}\nu^{j})^{2}+C\nu^{2j}d_{y}^{-}\right)\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}\,2C(d_{y}^{-})^{2}\nu^{2j}.

By a union bound over
j\leq h
and setting
s=d_{y}^{-}n^{a}/h
, it follows that

(2.32)

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)|>d_{y}^{-}n^{a}\right)\leq\frac{2Ch^{3}\nu^{2h}}{n^{2a}}\leq n^{\varepsilon/4-2a}.

for
n
large enough.
By
Eqs.

2.32
,
2.26
and
2.25
and choosing
a=\frac{1}{4}
we conclude that

(2.33)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C}_{y}^{c})\leq(d_{y}^{-})^{2}n^{-1/3}.

The proof of
Eq.

2.23
is an immediate consequence of
Eq.

2.22
and
Eq.

2.32
. Finally, by setting
a=\frac{\eta}{7}
and using
Lemma

2.1
, we obtain that the left-hand-side of
Eqs.

2.26
and
2.32
is
o(1)
for
\varepsilon<\eta
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small and
h=h_{\varepsilon}
. Let
\mathcal{I}
be a uniformly random vertex in
[n]
. Then,

(2.34)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{B}^{-}_{\mathcal{I}}(h)\neq\mathcal{T}^{-}_{\mathcal{I}}(h)\right)\leq n^{-1/4}.

Proof.

The estimate follows from
Lemma

2.3
, summing over
y\in[n]
and using

(2.35)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in[n]}(d_{y}^{-})^{2}n^{-\frac{1}{3}}\leq n^{-\frac{1}{4}},

which holds for all
n
large enough because of
1.1
.
∎

2.4.
Out-neighborhoods

In this section we focus on tree-excesses of out-neighborhoods.
Recall the definition of
h_{\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

2.9
.
For
h\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the event

(2.36)

\mathcal{G}^{+}(h)\coloneqq\cap_{x\in[n]}\{\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h))\leq 1\}.

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

(2.37)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{G}^{+}(2h_{\varepsilon})\right)=1-o(1),

where
h_{\varepsilon}
is defined as in
Eq.

2.9
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in[n]
and
h=h_{\varepsilon}
.
To generate
\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(2h)
,
we match at most
K\coloneqq(\Delta^{+})^{2h}=n^{\varepsilon/10}
tails.
The probability that at any given step we choose a head incident to
an already revealed vertex
is at most

(2.38)

q\coloneqq\frac{K\Delta^{-}}{m-K}.

Therefore,

(2.39)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(2h))\geq 2\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}(K,q)\geq 2)\leq(Kq)^{2},

where
\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}(N,p)
denotes a binomial random variable with parameters
N,p
and we use the simple bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}(N,p)\geq\ell)\leq\binom{N}{\ell}p^{\ell}\leq(Np)^{\ell}
, valid for all
N,\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
p\in[0,1]
.
By
Lemma

2.1
, if
\varepsilon<\frac{\eta}{2}
it follows that
(Kq)^{2}=o(n^{-1})
, and
the conclusion follows by a union bound over
x\in[n]
.
∎

While the previous lemma cannot be improved substantially, the set of vertices with positive tree-excess is small, as the next result shows.
Define

(2.40)

V_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\{x\in[n]:\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h_{\varepsilon}))=0\}\,.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
,

(2.41)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert[n]\setminus V_{\varepsilon}\rvert\leq n^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\geq 1-n^{-\frac{1}{6}}.

Proof.

We may assume that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. As in the proof of
Lemma

2.5
we know that

(2.42)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(h_{\varepsilon}))\geq 1\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}(K,q)\geq 1)\leq Kq,

where now

(2.43)

K=(\Delta^{+})^{h_{\varepsilon}}\,,\quad q=\frac{K\Delta^{-}}{m-K}\,.

Therefore, for any
x\in[n]
,
\mathbb{P}\left(x\notin V_{\varepsilon}\right)\leq Kq
. As a consequence,
\mathbb{E}\left[\lvert[n]\setminus V_{\varepsilon}\rvert\right]\leq nKq\leq K^{2}\Delta^{-}
.
By Markov’s inequality and
Eq.

2.1

(2.44)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lvert[n]\setminus V_{\varepsilon}\rvert\geq n^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)\leq K^{2}\Delta^{-}n^{-\frac{2}{3}}=O\left(n^{\frac{\varepsilon}{10}-\frac{\eta}{6}-\frac{1}{6}}\right).

Since
\varepsilon<\eta
, the lemma follows for large enough
n
.
∎

3.
Random walk

In this section we introduce the random walk on
G
and prove our main results concerning convergence to stationarity.
For a given realization of the digraph
G=G_{n}
and a probability distribution
\mu
on
[n]
,
we will denote by
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}
the
quenched law
of the random walk on
G

with initial distribution
\mu
, i.e.,
the law of the Markov chain on
[n]
with transition matrix
P
as in
Eq.

1.4
.
When
\mu
is concentrated on a single vertex
x\in[n]
, i.e.,
\mu=\delta_{x}
,
we write
\mathbf{P}_{x}
.
We write
X_{t}
for
the position of the random walk at any given time
t\geq 0
.
Notice that, for any
A\subset[n]
,
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}(X_{t}\in A)=\mu P^{t}(A)
is itself a random variable.
When we want to emphasize its dependence on the realization of the
bijection
\omega
which induces the digraph
G
, we write
\mathbf{P}^{\omega}_{\mu}
.
Thanks to
Lemma

2.5
and
1.1
(i), we can immediately infer the following property of the quenched law.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small
and let
V_{\varepsilon}
be defined as in
Eq.

2.40
. For all
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
t\leq h_{\varepsilon}
,

(3.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{x\in[n]}\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin V_{\varepsilon})\leq 2^{-t}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

From
Lemma

2.5
, it is sufficient to prove that the event
\mathcal{G}^{+}(2h_{\varepsilon})
implies
\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\notin V_{\varepsilon})\leq 2^{-t}
for all
x\in[n]
and for all
t\leq h_{\varepsilon}
. Under the event
\mathtt{Tx}(\mathcal{B}^{+}_{x}(2h_{\varepsilon}))\leq 1
there is at most one path of length
t\leq h_{\varepsilon}
from
x
to any point
y\notin V_{\varepsilon}
. Since each path of length
t
has weight at most
2^{-t}
the conclusion follows.
∎

A key object in our analysis is the so-called
annealed law
, obtained by averaging the quenched law over the environment:

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an}}_{x}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{P}_{x}\right]=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\omega}\mathbf{P}^{\omega}_{x}\,,\qquad\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an}}_{\mu}=\sum_{x\in[n]}\mu(x)\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an}}_{x},

where
\omega
ranges over all bijections from
E^{+}
to
E^{-}
.

It will also be important to consider the average over the environment of the quenched law of
K
independent walks. As observed in
[
12
,
13
]
the corresponding annealed law is a very powerful tool in estimating high order moments of random variables such as
\mathbf{P}_{\mu}(X_{t}\in A)
for
A\subset[n]
and
t\geq 0
. More precisely,
for any probability distribution
\mu
on
[n]
, for any subset
A\subset[n]
and for all
t,K\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mu P^{t}(A)\big)^{K}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\mathbf{P}_{\mu}(X_{t}\in A)\big)^{K}\right]=\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{\mu}\left(X_{t}^{(k)}\in A,\>\forall k\in[K]\right),

where the
annealed law

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}^{{\rm an},K}
is the deterministic law of a non-Markovian process

(3.4)

\left\{X_{s}^{(k)}\,,\;s\in\{0,\dots,t\},\;k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\right\},

which can be described as follows. Start with an empty matching. For each
k
, given the first
k-1
walks
(X_{s}^{(\ell)})_{s\leq t,\ell\leq k-1}
, to generate the
k
-th walk
X^{(k)}
,

(i)

start the
k
-th walk at a random vertex
X_{0}^{(k)}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{d}}}\,\mu
.

(ii)

for all
s\in\{0,\dots,t-1\}
: select one of the tails of
X_{s}^{(k)}
uniformly at random, and call it
e
:

•

If
e
was already matched by one of the previous walks, or by
X^{(k)}
itself at a previous step, to some head
f
, then let
X_{s+1}^{(k)}=v_{f}
.

•

If
e
is still unmatched select a uniformly random head,
f
, among the unmatched ones, match it to
e
, and let
X_{s+1}^{(k)}=v_{f}
.

We may view the walks as generating the environment (the digraph) as they activate new matchings (the edges). In particular, for any
K,t\geq 1
, the digraph
G
may be sampled using the edges revealed by the
K
walks up to time
t
and then by completing with a uniform matching of the remaining heads and tails.

3.1.
Law of large numbers

Using the annealed process as a computational tool,
we prove a quenched law of large numbers for the weight of the path determined by the random walk trajectory,
thus extending results in
[
12
]
previously obtained in the case of bounded in-degrees.
In particular, we show that for any
t
of order
\log n
,
with high probability the quenched law of the random walk is concentrated on trajectories
which have weight
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{H}t(1+o(1))}
, where
\mathrm{H}
is the entropy in
Eq.

1.8
.
The proof follows very closely the original argument in
[
12
]
,
while some minor technical difficulties due to the unbounded in-degree setting
are overcome using the
2+\eta
moment condition in
1.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
t=\Theta(\log(n))
and fix a sequence
\theta=\theta_{n}\in(0,1)
. Recall the definitions of path
\mathfrak{p}
in
Eq.

2.4
, of weight
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})
in
Eq.

2.5
and
\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)
. Call

(3.5)

Q_{x,t}(\theta):=\sum_{y\in[n]}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})>\theta}.

If for
\lambda\neq\mathrm{H}
we have

(3.6)

-\frac{\log(\theta)}{t}\longrightarrow\lambda,

then

(3.7)

\max_{x\in[n]}\left|Q_{x,t}(\theta)-\mathbf{1}(\lambda>\mathrm{H})\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Fix sequences
t=\Theta\left(\log(n)\right)
and
\theta=\theta_{n}

such that
Eq.

3.6
holds.
Let
\ell=3\log\log(n)
.
Let us consider the averaged probability

(3.8)

\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in[n]}P^{\ell}(x,y)Q_{y,t}(\theta).

We will show that

\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)
is well approximated by

(3.9)

q_{t}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{t}\frac{1}{D_{k}^{+}}>\theta\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=1}^{t}\log(D_{k}^{+})<-\frac{\log(\theta)}{t}\right),

where
(D_{k}^{+})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an independent and identically distributed sequence of random variables with law

(3.10)

\mathbb{P}(D^{+}_{k}=\ell)=\sum_{v\in[n]}\mu_{\rm in}(v)\mathbf{1}(d_{v}^{+}=\ell)\quad\text{for }\ell\geq 2.

Using
Eq.

1.8
and the law of large numbers for the bounded sequence
\big(\log(D^{+}_{k})\big)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
we have

(3.11)

q_{t}(\theta)\longrightarrow\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\lambda>\mathrm{H},\\
0&\text{if }\lambda<\mathrm{H}.\end{cases}

We first show that
Proposition

3.2
is implied by the following convergence

(3.12)

\max_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\left|\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)-q_{t}(\theta)\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0,

where
V_{\varepsilon}
is defined in
Eq.

2.40
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\eta/2)
, where
\eta\in(0,1)
is as in
1.1
.
Since the weight of a path of length
\ell
is always in
[(\Delta^{+})^{-\ell},2^{-\ell}]
we may estimate

(3.13)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in[n]}Q_{x,t}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in[n]}P^{\ell}(x,[n]\setminus V_{\varepsilon})+\max_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t-2\ell}(\theta 2^{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\ell}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)+\max_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}\left(\theta 2^{\ell}(\Delta^{+})^{-2\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq q_{t}\left(\theta 2^{\ell}(\Delta^{+})^{-2\ell}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where the second line follows from
Lemma

3.1
and the third line from
Eq.

3.12
.
Similarly,

(3.14)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in[n]}Q_{x,t}(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in[n]}P^{\ell}(x,V_{\varepsilon})\min_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t-2\ell}\left(\theta(\Delta^{+})^{2\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-2^{-\ell}-o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\min_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t-2\ell}\left(\theta(\Delta^{+})^{2\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}\left(\theta(\Delta^{+})^{2\ell}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\geq q_{t}\left(\theta(\Delta^{+})^{2\ell}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Moreover, notice that if for some
\theta^{\prime}
it holds
\log(\theta^{\prime})=\log(\theta)+O(\log\log(n))
then

(3.15)

|q_{t}(\theta)-q_{t}(\theta^{\prime})|\to 0.

Thus,
Eqs.

3.11
and
3.12
imply
Proposition

3.2
.

To prove
Eq.

3.12
, we show that
for all
\delta>0

(3.16)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{1}_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\geq q_{t}(\theta)+\delta\right)=o(n^{-1}).

This, together with a union bound over
x\in V_{\varepsilon}
, establishes one half of
Eq.

3.12
; the other half can be obtained in the same fashion replacing

\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)
by
1-\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)
and
q_{t}(\theta)
by
1-q_{t}(\theta)
, which amounts to inverting the inequality signs in the definition of
Q_{x,t}(\theta)
and
q_{t}(\theta)
.

We now prove
Eq.

3.16
.
By Markov’s inequality, for all
K\geq 1
,

(3.17)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{1}_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\geq q_{t}(\theta)+\delta\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\right)^{K}\right]}{(q_{t}(\theta)+\delta)^{K}}.

Hence, it is enough to show that if
K=\lfloor\log^{2}(n)\rfloor
, for all
\delta>0
and
n
large enough one has

(3.18)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\right)^{K}\right]\leq\left(q_{t}(\theta)+\tfrac{\delta}{2}\right)^{K}.

Notice that

(3.19)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{1}_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\bar{Q}_{x,t}(\theta)\right)^{K}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}(B_{K}),

where
\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}
is the law of
K
annealed walks of length
\ell+t
all started at
x
, see
Eq.

3.3
, and
for all
j\leq K
,
B_{j}
is the event that

(i)

the union of the first
j
trajectories up to time
\ell
, that is
(X_{s}^{(1)},\dots,X_{s}^{(j)})_{s\leq\ell}
, forms a directed tree.

(ii)

for each
i\leq j
, the last
t
steps of the
i
-th walk, that is
(X_{s}^{(i)})_{s\in[\ell+1,\ell+t]}
, define a path
\mathfrak{p}
of weight
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})>\theta
.

Since

(3.20)

\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}(B_{K})=\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}(B_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{K}\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}\left(B_{j}\>\middle|\>{}B_{j-1}\right),

it is enough to show that, uniformly in
j\leq K
,

(3.21)

\mathbb{P}^{{\rm an},K}_{x}\left(B_{j}\>\middle|\>{}B_{j-1}\right)\leq q_{t}(\theta)+\frac{\delta}{2}.

In order to check that
Eq.

3.21
holds, we note that, given
B_{j-1}
:

(i)

either the
j
-th walk attains length
\ell
before reaching an unmatched tail: thanks to the tree structure, there are at most
K-1
possible paths of length
\ell
to follow starting at
x
, and each has weight at most
2^{-\ell}
. Thus, the conditional probability of this scenario is less than
K2^{-\ell}=o(1)
.

(ii)

or the
j
-th walk has reached an unmatched tail by time
\ell
: then, the remainder of the path after the first unmatched tail can be coupled with an independent and identically distributed sample from the in-degree distribution on
[n]
at a total-variation cost less than
\frac{\Delta^{-}K(t+\ell)^{2}}{m}
, and the latter is deterministically
o(1)
thanks to
Lemma

2.1
. Indeed, there are at most
t+\ell
heads matched in the generation of the
j
-th walk and the probability that the coupling fails at a given step is bounded uniformly by
\frac{\Delta^{-}K(t+\ell)}{m}
, as all heads can be chosen. Thus, the conditional probability that the walk meets the requirement in that case is at most
q_{t}(\theta)+o(1)
.

∎

3.2.
A weighted out-neighborhood construction

Following an idea introduced in
[
12
,
13
]
, we now consider a further construction of the out-neighborhood of a given vertex that reveals only the directed paths
which have a sufficiently large probability to be followed by the random walk.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1)
. All parameters defined below depend implicitly on
\gamma
. Let

(3.22)

\overline{\mathrm{H}}\coloneqq(1+\gamma)\mathrm{H},

where
\mathrm{H}
is the entropy in
Eq.

1.8
. For any integer
s\geq 1
and every constant
\gamma>0
, let
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)
be the weighted directed graph spanned by the set of paths of length at most
s
, starting from
x
, and having weight
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\geq\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{\mathrm{H}}s}
.
As in
[
13
, Section 4.1]
,
we construct a sequence

(\mathcal{G}^{\ell},\mathcal{T}^{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
, where
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)
with
\ell
edges, constructing
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)
edge by edge, and such that
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}
is a spanning tree of
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}
for each
\ell
. We call
\kappa_{x}=\kappa_{x}(s)
the random number of edges needed to construct the whole digraph
\mathcal{G}^{\kappa_{x}}=\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)
. We now explain the detailed construction of the sequence
(\mathcal{G}^{\ell},\mathcal{T}^{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
. As initialization, let
\mathcal{E}_{0}
be the set of tails of
x
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\emptyset
and let
\mathcal{G}^{0}=\mathcal{T}^{0}
be the digraph containing only
x
and no edges. Then, for all
\ell\geq 1
:

(i)

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell-1}
be the set of
unmatched
tails which are incident to a node in

\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}
. For a tail
e\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell-1}
, define its
cumulative
weight
by

(3.23)

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e)\coloneqq\frac{\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})}{d_{v_{e}}^{+}},

where
\mathfrak{p}
is the unique path from
x
to

v_{e}
in
\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}
.
In words,
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e)
is the probability for the random walk to follow
\mathfrak{p}
and
then the edge containing
e
.

(ii)

Pick
e_{\ell}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell-1}
such that

(a)

v_{e_{\ell}}
is at distance at most
s-1
from
x
,

(b)

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})\geq\mathbf{w}_{\min}\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{\mathrm{H}}s}
,

(c)

e_{\ell}
has maximum cumulative weight among all tails in
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell-1}
which satisfy
(a) and (b), and use some arbitrary rule to break ties if needed.

(iii)

If no such
e_{\ell}
exists, then terminate and let
\kappa_{x}=\ell-1
.

(iv)

Otherwise, pair
e_{\ell}
with a head
f_{\ell}
chosen uniformly at random in
E^{-}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}
and set
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}=\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\cup\{f_{\ell}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{G}^{\ell}
be the resulting partial pairing. If
v_{f_{\ell}}\in V(\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1})
, the set of vertices of
\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1}
, then let
\mathcal{T}^{\ell}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}
; otherwise let

\mathcal{T}^{\ell}=\mathcal{T}^{\ell-1}\cup(e_{\ell},f_{\ell})
. Return to step (i).

When the process terminates, the construction yields
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)\coloneqq\mathcal{G}^{\kappa_{x}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)\coloneqq\mathcal{T}^{\kappa_{x}}
.
By
[
12
, Lemma 11]
, one has the deterministic bound

(3.24)

\mathbf{w}_{\min}\leq\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})\leq\frac{2}{2+\ell},

for all
\ell\leq\kappa_{x}
.
From this, it follows that,

(3.25)

\kappa_{x}(s)\leq\frac{2}{\mathbf{w}_{\min}}=2\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\mathrm{H}}s}.

The main motivation for the above construction is the fact that with high probability the random walk trajectories up to time
s\leq(1-o(1))T_{{\mathrm{ent}}}
are concentrated on the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)
provided the starting point
x
is locally tree-like, as we now explain. Note that the probability that a walk trajectory
(X_{0},\dots,X_{s})
with
X_{0}=x
stays on the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)
may be written as

(3.26)

p(x,s)=\sum_{y\in[n]}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(x,y,s,\mathcal{T}_{x}(s))}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p}),

where
\mathcal{P}(x,y,s,\mathcal{T}_{x}(s))
denotes the set of all paths of length
s
from
x
to
y
in
\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Recall the definition of
V_{\varepsilon}
in
Eq.

2.40
.
For all
\varepsilon>0
,
\gamma>0
, and for any
s\leq(1-\gamma)T_{\mathrm{ent}}
,

(3.27)

\min_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\,p(x,s)\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}1.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{*}=\mathcal{P}(x,y,s,G)\setminus\mathcal{P}(x,y,s,\mathcal{T}_{x}(s))

denote the set of paths starting at
x
and ending at
y
of length
s

which are not on the tree
\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)
.
We need to show that

(3.28)

\max_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\,\sum_{y}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{*}}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}0.

If a path
\mathfrak{p}
is in
\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{*}
, then at least one of the following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

the weight of the path satisfies
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})<\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{\mathrm{H}}s}
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{p}
contains an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{x}(s)\setminus\mathcal{T}_{x}(s)
.

We call
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{1,*}
the set of paths in case (i),
and
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{2,*}
the set of paths in case (ii) but not in case (i).
From the law of large numbers in
Proposition

3.2
we know that

(3.29)

\max_{x\in[n]}\,\sum_{y\in[n]}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{1,*}}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}0.

It remains to show that

(3.30)

\max_{x\in V_{\varepsilon}}\,\sum_{y}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{2,*}}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Define a process
(M_{\ell})_{\ell\geq 0}
by
M_{0}=0
and for
\ell\geq 1

(3.31)

M_{\ell}=M_{\ell-1}+\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})\mathbf{1}(\ell\leq\kappa_{x})\mathbf{1}(v_{f_{\ell}}\in V(\mathcal{G}^{\ell-1})).

Notice that

(3.32)

M_{\kappa_{x}}=\sum_{y\in[n]}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}^{2,*}}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p}).

We will show that
\mathbb{P}(M_{\kappa_{x}}>\delta)=o(n^{-1})
, uniformly in
x\in V_{\varepsilon}
, and for all
\delta>0
. By a union bound over
x\in V_{\varepsilon}
, this implies the desired claim.

Since
x\in V_{\varepsilon}
,
it follows that
\mathcal{G}_{x}(h_{\varepsilon})
is a tree, where
h_{\varepsilon}
is defined as in
Eq.

2.9
.
Therefore, setting
\ell_{\varepsilon}=2^{h_{\varepsilon}}
,
one must have
M_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell<\ell_{\varepsilon}
.
Thus, the desired conclusion follows once we prove

(3.33)

\mathbb{P}\left(M_{\kappa_{x}}-M_{\ell_{\varepsilon}}>\delta\right)=o(n^{-1}).

To prove it we use a martingale version of Bennett’s inequality obtained by Freedman
[
30
]
.

Since
\mathcal{G}_{x}(h_{\varepsilon})
is a tree of size at least
\ell_{\varepsilon}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})
is decreasing in
\ell
,
we have that
M_{\ell}=0
for all
\ell\leq\ell_{\varepsilon}
and, by
Eq.

3.24
, that

(3.34)

0\leq M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1}\leq 2^{-h_{\varepsilon}+1}=o(1),

for all
\ell\geq\ell_{\varepsilon}+1
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
be the filtration associated to
(\mathcal{G}^{\ell},\mathcal{T}^{\ell})
.
Call

(3.35)

g_{\ell}=\sum_{v\in V(\mathcal{G}^{\ell})}d^{-}_{v},

and note that by

Eq.

3.25
, the vertices in
V(\mathcal{G}^{\ell})
are at most
\ell\leq\kappa_{x}\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{\overline{\mathrm{H}}(1-\gamma)T_{\mathrm{ent}}}=2n^{1-\gamma^{2}}
.
Thus, by
Lemma
2.1
we have
g_{\ell}=o(\sqrt{\ell n})
.
Using
Eq.

3.24
,

(3.36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\ell\leq\kappa_{x}}\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})g_{\ell-1}}{m-(\ell-1)}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\ell}}\right);

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{1}_{\ell\leq\kappa_{x}}\frac{\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}(e_{\ell})\right)^{2}g_{\ell-1}}{m-(\ell-1)}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\ell^{3}}}\right).

Adding over all steps until
\kappa_{x}
, we get

(3.37)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa_{x}}\mathbb{E}[M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}]\leq\>o\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa_{x}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell}}=o\left(n^{-\gamma^{2}/2}\right),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa_{x}}\mathbb{E}[(M_{\ell}-M_{\ell-1})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}]\leq o\left(n^{-1/2}\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{\kappa_{x}}\frac{1}{\ell^{3/2}}=o\left(n^{-1/2}\right),

where we used
\sum^{k}_{\ell=1}\ell^{-1/2}=O(\sqrt{k})
,
\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\ell^{-3/2}=O(1)
, and
\kappa_{x}\leq 2n^{1-\gamma^{2}}
.
For
\ell\geq\ell_{\varepsilon}
, define

(3.38)

Z_{\ell+1}=\frac{5}{\delta}(M_{\ell+1}-M_{\ell}-\mathbb{E}[M_{\ell+1}-M_{\ell}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell}]),

which satisfies
|Z_{\ell+1}|\leq 1
by
Eq.

3.34
.
Let
\phi_{u}=\sum_{i=\ell_{\varepsilon}}^{u}Z_{i+1}
. Then
(\phi_{u})_{u\geq\ell_{\varepsilon}}
is a martingale and

M_{\kappa_{x}}=a+\frac{\delta}{5}\phi_{\kappa_{x}}
. When
n
is large enough, we have

a\leq\delta/5
. So

(3.39)

\mathbb{P}\left(M_{\kappa_{x}}\geq\frac{4}{5}\delta\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\phi_{\ell}\geq 3,\;\text{for some}\;\ell\geq\ell_{\varepsilon}\right).

The conditional variance of
\phi_{\ell}
is
b^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}{\rm Var}\left(Z_{i}\>\middle|\>{}\mathcal{F}_{i}\right)\leq(\delta/5)^{-2}b=o(n^{-1/3})
.
Then by
[
30
, Theorem 1.6]
,

(3.40)

\mathbb{P}\left(\phi_{\ell}\geq 3,\text{for some }\ell\geq\ell_{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{3}\left(\frac{b^{\prime}}{3+b^{\prime}}\right)^{3+b^{\prime}}=o\left(n^{-1}\right).\qed

3.3.1.
Proof of the upper bound of

Let us first explain the overall strategy of the proof, which is based on ideas introduced in

,
13
]
.
For each pair of vertices

,

\widetilde{}\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)\subset\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)

and ending at

We will also let

the probability to go from

to

\widetilde{P}^{t}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\widetilde{}\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p}).

Suppose that for some
\delta>0
and some probability distribution
\widetilde{\pi}
on

it holds that

\widetilde{P}^{t}(x,y)\leq(1+\delta)\widetilde{\pi}(y)+\frac{\delta}{n},\qquad\forall x,y\in[n].

For

[x]_{+}=\max\{x,0\}

then

\left[\widetilde{\pi}(y)-P^{t}(x,y)\right]_{+}\leq\big[\widetilde{\pi}(y)-\widetilde{P}^{t}(x,y)\big]_{+}\leq(1+\delta)\widetilde{\pi}(y)+\frac{\delta}{n}-\widetilde{P}^{t}(x,y).

Therefore,

(3.44)

\displaystyle\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\widetilde{\pi}\|_{{\textsc{tv}}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in[n]}|\widetilde{\pi}(y)-P^{t}(x,y)|=\sum_{y\in[n]}\left[\widetilde{\pi}(y)-P^{t}(x,y)\right]_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in[n]}\Big((1+\delta)\widetilde{\pi}(y)+\frac{\delta}{n}-\widetilde{P}^{t}(x,y)\Big)=2\delta+\widetilde{q}(x),

where

is the probability that the walk starting at

follows a path of length

which

\widetilde{q}(x)\coloneqq 1-\sum_{y\in[n]}\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in\widetilde{}\mathcal{P}(x,y,t,G)}\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p}).

Next, we define the set of nice paths.

.

Let

such that

is satisfied. Fix

,

,

Notice that

, and that our choice of parameters

and

such that

as

.

Given

Notice that (ii) implies that for every nice path

\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\leq\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p}_{s+1})\cdot 2^{-h}\leq 2^{-h}n^{-1+2\gamma}\leq n^{-1-\delta^{\prime}},

for some

, where we use the definition of

and
\gamma
, and the fact that

.

With this definition at hand, we show
that, with high probability, starting from any locally-tree-like vertex, the quenched probability to follow a nice path converges to 1.

Proposition 3.5
.

Recall the definition of

in

.
The probability

. Notice that requirement (i) follows, uniformly in

. By our choice of the parameter

, i.e., when

Fix the parameters
\varepsilon,h,s,t

s\leq t
there are
A=o(\sqrt{(Kt+L)n})
unmatched heads incident
to either
S
or to the vertices visited by the first
j-1
walks,
or by the
j
-th walk up to time
s
.
Therefore, conditionally on the first
j-1
walks, the probability that the
j
-th walk ends at
S
is at most

(3.78)

\mathbb{P}_{\rm unif}^{{\rm an},K}\left(B_{j}\>\middle|\>{}B_{j-1}\right)\leq\frac{Kt}{n}+\frac{t\>A}{m-Kt}\leq\sqrt{L/n}\,\log^{5}n=o(n^{-2\delta}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{\rm unif}^{{\rm an},K}
is defined by
Eq.

3.2
with
\mu
uniform over
[n]
.
Indeed, in order to end in
S
the walk needs to visit at some
s\leq t
a vertex which is either in
S
or has already been visited by one of the previous walks. The probability that such an event occurs at the initialization step is bounded by
Kt/n
, while
tA/(m-Kt)
bounds the probability that the event occurs at some later step.
It follows that

(3.79)

\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{t}(S))^{K}]=\mathbb{P}_{\rm unif}^{{\rm an},K}(B_{K})=\mathbb{P}_{\rm unif}^{{\rm an},K}(B_{1})\prod_{j=2}^{K}\mathbb{P}_{\rm unif}^{{\rm an},K}\left(B_{j}\>\middle|\>{}B_{j-1}\right)=o(n^{-2L}).

By Markov’s inequality,

(3.80)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{t}(S)\geq n^{-\delta}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{t}(S))^{K}]}{n^{-L}}=o(n^{-L}).

This implies
Eq.

3.77
.
∎

4.
Bulk behavior

In this section we prove
Theorem

. As we will see, the distribution

approximating the bulk values of the stationary distribution can be characterized as the almost sure limit of an
L^{2}
-bounded martingale.

4.1.
The martingale

Fix
y\in[n]
, an arbitrary
h\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and consider the random tree
\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
constructed in
Section

2.2.2
with marks
\ell(\cdot)
. For
a\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
, define

(4.1)

\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a)\coloneqq d^{-}_{\ell(a)}\prod_{i=1}^{h}\frac{1}{d^{+}_{\ell(a_{i})}},

where
(a_{0},\dots,a_{h}=a)
is the unique path joining
a
with the root
a_{0}
for witch

\ell(a_{0})=y
.
If
a=a_{0}
, then the empty product is interpreted as
1
and we define
\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a_{0})=d^{-}_{y}
in this case.

Define the random process

(4.2)

M_{y}(h)=\sum_{a\in\partial\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)}\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a),\qquad\forall h\geq 0.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{h}
be the sigma algebra generated by the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)
. Then
(M_{y}(h))_{h\geq 0}
is a martingale satisfying
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{y}(h)\right]=d^{-}_{y}
and, uniformly in
h\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\rm Var}(M_{y}(h))=O(d^{-}_{y})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, write
M_{h}=M_{y}(h)
, and note
M_{0}=\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a_{0})=d^{-}_{y}
.
For each
a\in\mathcal{T}^{-}_{y}(h)
, let
N(a)
denote the set of its children.
Then, for all
h\geq 0
,

(4.3)

M_{h+1}-M_{h}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a)}{d_{\ell(a)}^{-}}\left(\sum_{b\in N(a)}\left(\frac{d^{-}_{\ell(b)}}{d^{+}_{\ell(b)}}-1\right)\right).

Let
\mathcal{J}
denote a random vertex in
[n]
distributed as
\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}(x)
defined in
Eq.

1.7
. Using

(4.4)

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d_{\mathcal{J}}^{-}}{d_{\mathcal{J}}^{+}}\right]=\sum_{v\in[n]}\frac{d_{v}^{+}}{m}\frac{d_{v}^{-}}{d_{v}^{+}}=1,

and the fact that the marks in the tree are distributed according to
\mu_{{\mathrm{out}}}
, we obtain

(4.5)

\mathbb{E}[M_{h+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{h}]=M_{h}.

Hence,
(M_{h})_{h\geq 0}
is a martingale with expectation
d_{y}^{-}
. It remains to compute its variance. Let

(4.6)

\Sigma_{h}\coloneqq{\rm Var}(M_{h+1}-M_{h}\mid\mathcal{F}_{h}).

By
Eq.

4.3
,

M_{h+1}-M_{h}
is given by

(4.7)

\sum_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a)}{d_{\ell(a)}^{-}}\,Y_{a},

where, for each
a
,
Y_{a}
is the sum of
d^{-}_{\ell(a)}
independent and identically distributed copies of the random variable

\frac{d^{-}_{\mathcal{J}}}{d^{+}_{\mathcal{J}}}-1
.
Therefore, by conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we obtain

(4.8)

\Sigma_{h}=(A-1)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a)^{2}}{d_{\ell(a)}^{-}},

where, by
1.1
,

(4.9)

A\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{d^{-}_{\mathcal{J}}}{d^{+}_{\mathcal{J}}}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{v\in[n]}\frac{d_{v}^{-}}{m}\left(\frac{d_{v}^{-}}{d_{v}^{+}}\right)^{2}=O(1).

Since

(4.10)

\sum_{b\in\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h+1)}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(b)^{2}}{d_{\ell(b)}^{-}}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{T}_{y}^{-}(h)}\frac{\mathbf{w}_{\mathcal{T}}(a)^{2}}{(d_{\ell(a)}^{-})^{2}}\sum_{b\in N(a)}\frac{d_{\ell(b)}^{-}}{(d_{\ell(b)}^{+})^{2}},

we conclude that

(4.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{h+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{h}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{d^{-}_{\mathcal{J}}}{(d^{+}_{\mathcal{J}})^{2}}\right]\Sigma_{h}.

Using
Eq.

4.4
and the fact that the out-degrees are at least
2
we see that
\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{h+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{h}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\Sigma_{h}
.
Thus, taking the expectation and applying induction on
h
, we have

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{h}\right]\leq 2^{-h}\Sigma_{0},\qquad\forall h\geq 0,

where
\Sigma_{0}={\rm Var}(M_{1})=(A-1)d_{y}^{-}
.
By orthogonality of the martingale increments, and using
Eq.

4.12
, it follows that

(4.13)

{\rm Var}(M_{h})=\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{i}\right]\leq 2(A-1)d_{y}^{-}.

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let

be a uniform random vertex in

Define

by averaging over

4.15

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\Phi_{h}-\Phi_{\infty})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Var}(\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{0})\>\middle|\>{}\mathcal{F}_{0}\right]\sum_{j=h}^{\infty}2^{-j}\leq 2^{-h+1}\>\frac{(A-1)}{\left\langle d\right\rangle},

where

, defined as in
Eq.

n
thanks to
1.1
.
∎

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{x\in[n]}(\{x\in V_{\varepsilon}\}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{x}^{c})\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{x\in[n]}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{x}^{c}\cap\mathcal{Y}_{x}\right)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{Y}^{c}\right)=o(1),

where the first term is bounded by

5.9
and the second by
Lemma

5.2
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.2
.

Let

for sufficiently large

We now turn our attention to the in-neighborhood exploration to bound

.
Let

be a Galton-Watson tree as defined in

Consider the event

(5.17)

y\in[n]
and let us consider the following events which implicitly depend on
y
: for every

\ell\in\{1,\dots,K\}

(6.3)

\displaystyle B_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{X_{t}^{(1)}=\cdots=X_{t}^{(\ell)}=y\},

\displaystyle F_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{D_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{G}\},

\displaystyle H_{\ell}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\forall j\leq h,\>|\partial\mathcal{B}^{-,D_{\ell}}_{y}(j)|\leq 2K\}.

Notice that

(6.4)

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\mu_{t}(y)^{K}]\leq\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(B_{K}\cap F_{K}\cap H_{K})+\mathbb{P}(H_{K}^{c}).

Moreover, letting
B_{0}=F_{0}=H_{0}
be sure events and using the monotonicity of the events defined above,

(6.5)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(B_{K}\cap F_{K}\cap H_{K})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}\left(B_{\ell}\cap F_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}\>\middle|\>B_{\ell-1}\cap F_{\ell-1}\cap H_{\ell-1}\right).

Therefore, in order to prove
Eq.

6.1
it suffices to show that

(6.6)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}\left(B_{\ell}\cap F_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}\>\middle|\>B_{\ell-1}\cap F_{\ell-1}\cap H_{\ell-1}\right)\leq\frac{9K\Delta^{-}}{n},\qquad\forall\ell\in\{1,\dots,K\},

and, moreover,

(6.7)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(H_{K}^{c})=o\left(\left(\frac{\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)^{K}\right).

Plugging
Eqs.

6.6
,
6.7
and
6.5
into
Eq.

6.4
we obtain
Eq.

6.1
.

We start by proving
Eq.

6.7
. For
j\in\{1,\dots,h\}
, set

(6.8)

H_{K,j}=\{|\partial\mathcal{B}^{-,D_{K}}_{y}(j)|\leq 2K\},

so we may write

(6.9)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(H_{K}^{c})=\sum_{j\leq h}\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(H_{K,j}^{c}\cap(\cap_{i<j}H_{K,i})).

Notice that
H_{K,1}^{c}
is the event
that the
K
walks have matched more than
2K
heads of
y
. Hence, for all
n
large enough,

(6.10)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(\mathrm{H}^{c}_{K,1})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}\left(Kt,\frac{\Delta^{-}}{m-Kt}\right)>2K\right)\leq\left(\frac{Kt\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)^{2K}\leq\left(\frac{\Delta^{-}\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{2}K}.

For
j\in\{2,\dots,K\}
, under the event
\cap_{i<j}H_{K,i}
, there are at most
2K\Delta^{-}
heads that can be matched to violate
H_{K,j}
, and therefore

(6.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(H_{K,j}^{c}\cap(\cap_{i<j}H_{K,i}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathop{\mathrm{Bin}}\left(Kt,\frac{2K\Delta^{-}}{m-Kt}\right)>2K\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2K^{2}t\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)^{2K}\leq\left(\frac{\Delta^{-}\log(n)}{n}\right)^{\tfrac{3}{2}K}.

Plugging
Eq.

6.10
and
Eq.

6.11
into
Eq.

6.9
, we obtain
Eq.

6.7
.

We now turn to the proof of
Eq.

6.6
.
We fix a realization
D_{\ell-1}
of the partial matching generated by the first
\ell-1
walks,
and assume that it satisfies
B_{\ell-1}\cap F_{\ell-1}\cap H_{\ell-1}
.
Let
V(D_{\ell,s})
be the set of vertices previously visited by the other walks or by the

\ell
-th walk itself up to time
s-1
for
\ell\geq 1
, together with
y
.
For the event
B_{\ell}
to occur, the
\ell
-th walk must enter at some time
s\in\{0,\dots,t\}

and then traverse only edges in
D_{\ell,s}
from time
s
up to time
t
.
If
s\in\{1,\dots,t\}
,
the event that the
\ell
-th walk enters in
D_{\ell,s}
at time
s
in a given vertex

z\in V(D_{\ell,s})
has probability bounded above by
d_{z}^{-}/(m-t\ell)\leq d_{z}^{-}/n
,
uniformly in the realization of
D_{\ell,s}
.
On the other hand, the probability that the
\ell
-th walk enters in
D_{\ell-1}
at time
s=0
in
z\in D_{\ell-1}
is
1/n
.

Given
D_{\ell,s}=D
and
z\in V(D)
, let
q_{D}(z,z^{\prime},r)
denote the probability that a walk started
at
z
at time
0
arrives in
z^{\prime}
at time
r
by traversing only edges in
D
.
Note that
q_{D}(z,z^{\prime},r)=0
if
z^{\prime}\notin V(D)
,
q_{D}(z,z,0)=1
, and that

(6.12)

q_{D}(z,z^{\prime},r)\leq 2^{-r}|\mathcal{P}\left(z,z^{\prime},r,D\right)|,

since the out-degrees are at least
2
. In conclusion, we can bound uniformly in
D_{\ell-1}

(6.13)

\mathbb{P}^{\mathrm{an}}(B_{\ell}\cap F_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}\>\big\rvert\>D_{\ell-1})\leq\sum_{s=0}^{t}\max_{D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)}\sum_{z\in V(D)}\frac{d^{-}_{z}}{n}\,q_{D}(z,y,t-s),

with the convention that
d_{z}^{-}
must be replaced by
\max\{d^{-}_{z},1\}
when
s=0
, and where we define the set
\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)
of all possible realizations
D
of
D_{\ell,s}
such that

(6.14)

\mathbb{P}^{\rm an}(D_{\ell,s}=D,\>B_{\ell}\cap F_{\ell}\cap H_{\ell}\ |\ D_{\ell-1})>0.

We split the interval
s\in\{0,\dots,t\}

into two parts:

(6.15)

I_{1}=\{0,\dots,t-h\},\qquad I_{2}=\{t-h+1,\dots,t\}.

Observe that if
s\in I_{1}
, for every realization
D
of
D_{\ell,s}
:

(6.16)

q_{D}(z,y,t-s)=\sum_{z^{\prime}\in V(D)}q_{D}(z,z^{\prime},t-s-h)q_{D}(z^{\prime},y,h)\leq\max_{z^{\prime}\in V(D)}2^{-h}|\mathcal{P}(z^{\prime},y,h,D)|.

Thanks to the event
F_{\ell}
,
we know that
|\mathcal{P}(z^{\prime},y,h,D)|\leq 2
for all
z^{\prime}\in V(D)
,
and
D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)
.
Moreover,
|V(D)|\leq(1+Kt)
, and therefore

(6.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in I_{1}}\max_{D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)}\sum_{z\in V(D)}\frac{d^{-}_{z}}{n}\,q_{D}(z,y,t-s)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in I_{1}}\max_{D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)}\frac{|V(D)|\Delta^{-}}{n}2^{-h+1}\leq|I_{1}|\frac{(1+Kt)\Delta^{-}}{n}\,2^{-h+1}=o\left(\frac{\Delta^{-}}{n}\right).

We now turn to bound the sum in
Eq.

6.13
for
s\in I_{2}
. Notice that

(6.18)

\sum_{s\in I_{2}}\max_{D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(s)}\sum_{z\in V(D)}\frac{d^{-}_{z}}{n}q_{D}(z,y,t-s)=\sum_{a=0}^{h-1}\max_{D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(t-a)}\sum_{j=0}^{a}\sum_{z\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{-,D}(j)}\frac{d^{-}_{z}}{n}\,q_{D}(z,y,a).

As the event
F_{\ell}
holds, if
D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(t-a)
, if
a\leq h
, we may estimate

(6.19)

q_{D}(z,y,a)\leq 2^{-a}|\mathcal{P}(z,y,a,D)|\leq 2^{-a+1}.

Therefore, using that
H_{\ell}
holds and
Eq.

6.19
, for all
D\in\mathcal{A}_{\ell}(t-a)
, it follows that

(6.20)

\sum_{j=0}^{a}\sum_{z\in\partial\mathcal{B}_{y}^{-,D}(j)}\frac{d^{-}_{z}}{n}\,q_{D}(z,y,a)\leq\frac{\Delta^{-}}{n}(2Ka+1)2^{-a+1}.

Using
Eq.

6.20
we see that
Eq.

6.18
is bounded above by

8\frac{\Delta^{-}}{n}K
.
Recalling
Eqs.

6.13
and
6.17
, we conclude the
validity of
Eq.

6.6
.
∎

Proof of the upper bound in
Theorem

1.4
.

We show how the desired bound follows by the moment estimates in
Lemma

6.1
and
Corollary

3.7
. Fix
C>0
and consider

(6.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{y\in[n]}\mu_{t}(y)>\frac{20\>C\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{y\in[n]}\mu_{t}(y)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}>\frac{20\>C\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq n\max_{y\in[n]}\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{t}(y)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}>\frac{20\>C\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)+o(1),

where the second inequality follows by a union bound over
y\in[n]
and by
Lemma

2.5
. By Markov’s inequality, for
K=C\log(n)
, and using
Lemma

6.1
,

(6.22)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{t}(y)\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}>\frac{20K\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\mu_{t}(y)^{K}]}{\left(\frac{20K\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)^{K}}\leq 2^{-K}.

Choosing
C>1/\log(2)
we conclude that the probability in
Eq.

6.22
is
o(n^{-1})
.
Since we may take
C
such that
20C<29
,
plugging
Eq.

6.22
into
Eq.

6.21
we obtain, with high probability

(6.23)

\max_{y\in[n]}\mu_{t}(y)<\frac{29\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}.

Define the event
\mathcal{E}=\{\forall y\in[n],\;\pi(y)\leq\mu_{t}(y)+2\mathrm{e}^{-\log^{3/2}(n)}\}
,
with
t=\log^{3}(n)
, and observe that

(6.24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(n\pi_{\max}>30\log(n)\Delta^{-}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(n\pi_{\max}>30\log(n)\Delta^{-};\>\mathcal{E}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{y\in[n]}\mu_{t}(y)>\frac{30\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}-2\mathrm{e}^{-\log^{3/2}(n)}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{y\in[n]}\mu_{t}(y)>\frac{29\log(n)\Delta^{-}}{n}\right)+\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c}).

Corollary

3.7
implies
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}^{c})=o(1)
and the desired conclusion follows from
Eq.

6.23
.
∎

7.
Power-law behavior: Proof of
Theorem

1.7

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.7
.
Recall the definition of
\phi(k)
in
Eq.

1.21
as the proportion of vertices having in-degree
k
.
The lower bound in
Eq.

1.22
is an immediate corollary of the results in
Section

5.3
.
Indeed, from
Corollary

5.6

it follows that with high probability for all
y\in[n]
such that
d_{y}^{-}>n^{a}
,
one has
n\pi(y)>\frac{n^{a}}{2\left\langle d\right\rangle}
,
where
\left\langle d\right\rangle
is the average degree.
Therefore, for all
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,

(7.1)

\psi(n^{a},\infty)\geq\phi(2\left\langle d\right\rangle n^{a},\infty)\geq n^{-a\kappa-\varepsilon}\,,

for all
\varepsilon>0
by the assumed power-law behavior of the degree sequence.

The rest of this section is concerned with the proof of the upper bound in
Theorem

1.7
. As announced in
Remark

1.8
, we actually prove a slightly more general result; see
Theorem

7.3
below.

Definition 7.1
.

A bi-degree sequence is
\kappa
-light
if for all
\varepsilon>0
, for all
a>0
we have

(7.2)

\sum_{k>n^{a}}k\phi(k)\leq n^{-a(\kappa-1)+\varepsilon}.

Proposition 7.2
.

If
\mathbf{d}_{n}
satisfies
1.1
, then
\mathbf{d}_{n}
is
(2+\eta)
-light, where
\eta>0
is such that
Eq.

1.2
holds.
Moreover, if
\mathbf{d}_{n}
has power-law behavior with index
\kappa>2
as in
Eq.

1.20
, then
\mathbf{d}_{n}
is
\kappa
-light.

Proof.

For any
a>0
and using the bounded
(2+\eta)
-moment we have

(7.3)

\sum_{k>n^{a}}k\phi(k)\leq n^{-a(1+\eta)}\sum_{k>n^{a}}k^{2+\eta}\phi(k)=O(n^{-a(1+\eta)}).

This proves the first assertion.

To prove the second one, for any
\varepsilon>0
and using
Eq.

1.20
, we have

(7.4)

\sum_{k\geq\lfloor n^{a}\rfloor}k\phi(k)=\sum_{r=0}^{\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil}\sum_{k=\lfloor n^{a+r\varepsilon}\rfloor}^{\lceil n^{a+(r+1)\varepsilon}\rceil}k\phi(k)\leq\sum_{r=0}^{\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil}\lceil n^{a+(r+1)\varepsilon}\rceil n^{-(a+r\varepsilon)\kappa+\varepsilon}=O(n^{-a(\kappa-1)+2\varepsilon}).

Since
\varepsilon
can be arbitrarily small, this implies the claim.

∎

From the previous facts, we see that the upper bound in
Theorem

1.7
and the claim in
Remark

1.8
both follow from the next result.

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{d}_{n}
satisfies
1.1
and assume that
\mathbf{d}_{n}
is
\kappa
-light for some
\kappa>2
.
Then for all
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability for all
a>0
,

(7.5)

\psi(n^{a},\infty)\leq n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}.

7.1.
The
a
-skeleton

The proof of
Theorem

7.3
is based on the following construction.
For any
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,
the
a
-skeleton

\xi_{a}
is the partial matching of heads and tails defined as follows.
Recall the definition of
V(a)
in
Eq.

5.27
.
For any
z\in V(a)
,
call
W_{a,z}
the set of paths
\mathfrak{p}
starting at
z
whose weight
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})
is at least

(7.6)

\mathbf{w}_{a,z}\coloneqq\frac{n^{a}}{d_{z}^{-}}.

Note that we have not fixed the length of the paths in this definition, and that
W_{a,z}
covers a portion of the out-neighborhood of
z
with depth growing logarithmically as a function of

d_{z}^{-}
.
The
a
-skeleton is defined by

(7.7)

\xi_{a}=\bigcup_{z\in V(a)}W_{a,z}.

We call
V(\xi_{a})
the set of
y\in[n]
such that at least one of the heads or tails of
y
is matched in
\xi_{a}
.
In particular,
V(a)\subset V(\xi_{a})
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

7.3
, for all
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,
\varepsilon>0
,

\xi_{a}
has at most
n^{1-a\kappa+\varepsilon}
edges for
n
large enough. In particular,

(7.8)

|V(\xi_{a})|\leq n^{1-a\kappa+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Fix some
z\in V(a)
and notice that the set
W_{a,z}
can be generated with the weighted out-neighborhood construction given in
Section

3.2
,
with the only difference that here we impose no constraint on the distance to the root
z
,
and the minimal weight
\mathbf{w}_{\min}
is now replaced by
\mathbf{w}_{a,z}
.
Thus, it follows from the argument used in
Eq.

3.25
,
that the number of edges in
W_{a,z}
is at most
2/\mathbf{w}_{a,z}=2n^{-a}d_{z}^{-}
.
Since the degree sequence is
\kappa
-light, the total number of edges in
\xi_{a}
is deterministically bounded by

(7.9)

\sum_{z\in V(a)}2n^{-a}d_{z}^{-}\leq 2n^{-a}\sum_{k>n^{a}}kn\phi(k)\leq n^{1-a\kappa+\varepsilon}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

Let us observe that with high probability if
y\in V(\xi_{a})
then
n\pi(y)\geq\frac{n^{a}}{2\left\langle d\right\rangle}
.
Indeed, if
y\in V(a)
then this is a consequence of
Corollary

5.6
.
If instead
y\in V(\xi_{a})\setminus V(a)
,
then by definition of
a
-skeleton there exists a vertex
z\in V(a)
and a path
\mathfrak{p}
starting at
z

and ending at
y
with
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\geq\mathbf{w}_{a,z}=n^{a}/d_{z}^{-}
.
Thus, if
s
denotes the length of
\mathfrak{p}
, then

(7.10)

n\pi(y)\geq n\pi(z)P^{s}(z,y)\geq n\pi(z)\mathbf{w}_{a,z}\geq n\frac{d_{z}^{-}}{2m}\frac{n^{a}}{d_{z}^{-}}=\frac{n^{a}}{2\left\langle d\right\rangle},

where we used again
Corollary

5.6
to lower bound
\pi(z)
. The heart of the proof of
Theorem

7.3
will consist in showing that for all
y\notin V(\xi_{a})
except for at most

n^{o(1)}
of them one has
n\pi(y)\leq n^{a+o(1)}
.

7.2.
Proof of
Theorem

7.3

Recall that

(7.11)

\psi(n^{a},\infty)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{y\in[n]}\mathbf{1}(\pi(y)>n^{a-1}).

We need to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(7.12)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\exists a\in(0,\infty):\;\psi(n^{a},\infty)>n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}\right)=o(1).

Let
t=\log^{3}(n)
and consider
\mu_{t}
as in
Eq.

3.69
. Define

(7.13)

Z_{a}=\sum_{y\in[n]}\mathbf{1}(\mu_{t}(y)>\tfrac{1}{2}\,n^{a-1}).

The next lemma is the main technical estimate in this section.

Lemma 7.6
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, for any fixed
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
,

(7.14)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_{a}>n^{1-a\kappa+\varepsilon}\right)=o(1).

Before proving
Lemma

7.6
, let us show that this estimate implies
Eq.

7.12
.
We start by proving that

(7.15)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\psi(n^{a},\infty)>0\right)=o(1),

for all
a>1/\kappa
.
The maximum in-degree
\Delta^{-}
is the largest
\ell
such that
\phi(\ell)\geq 1/n
, or equivalently, such that
n\sum_{k\geq\ell}k\phi(k)\geq\ell
. If
\Delta^{-}>n^{\frac{1}{\kappa}}
, by definition of
\kappa
-light degree sequence, for all
\varepsilon>0
we have

(7.16)

\Delta^{-}\leq n\sum_{k>n^{1/\kappa}}k\phi(k)\leq n^{\frac{1}{\kappa}+\varepsilon}.

By the upper bound in
Theorem

1.4
, we have with high probability
n\pi_{\max}\leq\Delta^{-}n^{\varepsilon}\leq n^{\frac{1}{\kappa}+2\varepsilon}
, and therefore
\psi(n^{a},\infty)=0
if
a\geq\frac{1}{\kappa}+2\varepsilon
. Since
\varepsilon
is arbitrarily small, this proves
Eq.

7.15
for all
a>1/\kappa
.

To prove
Eq.

7.12
, thanks to the monotonicity of
a\mapsto\psi(n^{a},\infty)
and
Eq.

7.15
one can replace
\exists a\in(0,\infty)
with
\exists a\in(0,b)
for any fixed
b>1/\kappa
in that statement. For simplicity, we take
b=2/\kappa
.
Moreover, since we have restricted to a bounded interval of exponents
a
, it is actually sufficient to prove that for any
\varepsilon>0

(7.17)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\psi(n^{a},\infty)>n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}\right)=o(1),

for each fixed
a\in(0,2/\kappa)
. Indeed, let
I_{\varepsilon}
denote the set of integers in the interval
[0,4/\varepsilon]
. If
a\in(0,2/\kappa)
, there exists
i\in I_{\varepsilon}
such that
i\varepsilon/2\leq a\kappa\leq(i+1)\varepsilon/2
and

\psi(n^{i\varepsilon/(2\kappa)},\infty)\geq\psi(n^{a},\infty)
. Therefore, if
\psi(n^{a},\infty)>n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}
for some
a\in(0,2/\kappa)
, there must exists
i\in I_{\varepsilon}
such that

(7.18)

\psi(n^{i\varepsilon/(2\kappa)},\infty)>n^{-a\kappa+\varepsilon}\geq n^{-i(\varepsilon/2)+\varepsilon/2}.

Using
Eq.

7.17
, a union bound over the finite set
I_{\varepsilon}
then allows us to conclude
Eq.

7.12
.

Finally we observe that it is sufficient to establish
Eq.

7.17
for all fixed
a\in(0,1/\kappa)
.
Indeed, the case
a>1/\kappa
is covered by
Eq.

7.15
,
and the case
a=1/\kappa
follows by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.

By
Corollary

3.7
,
we know that with high probability
\mu_{t}(y)\geq\pi(y)-1/n
for all
y\in[n]
,
which implies
n\psi(n^{a},\infty)\leq Z_{a}
for all

.
Therefore, in order to prove
Eq.

7.17
it suffices to show
Lemma

7.6
.

7.3.
Proof of
Lemma

7.6

To prove
Eq.

7.14
, we fix
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

applies, and define

,
where

, with, e.g.,

.
The key to our proof will be the following estimate on the moments of

Lemma 7.7
.

Fix

. For all constants
\varepsilon>0
,

,

,

(7.19)

\max_{y\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathbf{1}_{y\notin V(\xi_{a})}\mu_{t}(y)^{K}\right]\leq n^{K(a+\varepsilon-1)},

for all

Proof.

Fix

and

.
We may assume that

,
since otherwise

and the estimate becomes trivially satisfied.

We use the same construction based on the

Lemma

.
With the notation used in that proof,
recall that
D_{\ell}
is the digraph generated by the first
\ell
trajectories
and
D_{\ell,s}
is the union of
D_{\ell-1}

and the edges generated by the
\ell
-th walk up to time

.
For any

,
define

as the union of all paths
\mathfrak{p}
contained in
D_{\ell}
,
starting at
z
, and such that
\mathbf{w}(\mathfrak{p})\geq\mathbf{w}_{a,z}
.
Recall the definition of an event being
D_{\ell}
compatible

8.1.
Lower bound

Let us take

a sequence in

and assume that

(8.1)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{n}\leq 1-\delta,

for some
\delta>0

For

(8.2)

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{k}\lambda P^{k}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda_{\min}\sum_{y\in[n]}P(y,x)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha(1-\alpha)\lambda_{\min}\,\frac{d_{x}^{-}}{\Delta^{+}}\geq\alpha\,\frac{\delta n^{-\varepsilon}}{2\Delta^{+}}\,\frac{d_{x}^{-}}{n}\,,

where
\lambda_{\min}=\min_{y\in[n]}\lambda(y)
, and we have used
1-\alpha\geq\delta/2
,
\lambda_{\min}\geq n^{-1-\varepsilon}
by
Eq.

1.26
,
for all
\varepsilon>0
, and
n
large enough.

On the other hand, by the definition of total variation distance (see

1.5
)
and the monotonicity of distance to equilibrium,
for all

and

\lambda P^{k}(x)\geq\pi(x)-\|\lambda P^{k}-\pi\|_{\textsc{tv}}\geq\pi(x)-\|\lambda P^{t}-\pi\|_{\textsc{tv}}.

Thus, for any

,

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=t}^{\infty}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{k}\lambda P^{k}(x)

Taking

3.7
,
with high probability

Thus, with high probability for all

By the lower bound in

5.6
,
with high probability for all

,

, we have

. If
\alpha\leq n^{-\varepsilon}
then
(1-\alpha)^{t}\geq 1/2
for
n
large enough.
Thus, for all
\varepsilon>0
,
\max\left\{\alpha,(1-\alpha)^{t}\right\}\geq n^{-\varepsilon}
for
n
large enough. We obtain that with high probability

(8.6)

It follows that with high probability

(8.7)

Hence, by the power-law assumption on the in-degree sequence,
we conclude that for all
\varepsilon>0
,
with high probability for all

,

\psi_{\alpha,\lambda}(n^{a},\infty)\geq n^{-a\kappa-(3\kappa+1)\varepsilon}
.

Call

Future research directions

A number of open problems arise from empirical observations. Several papers have identified a consistent disagreement between the largest in-degree nodes and the ones attaining the maximum PageRank score in real-world networks (see, e.g.,
[
24
,
52
]
). Outliers in each ranking exhibit correlation but the top sets tend to disagree. This reinforces the idea that rankings based on stationary values are much more than the simple in-degree ranking and poses the question of determining under which conditions the top in-degree and top score nodes coincide. Notably,
Eq.

1.16
tells us that the maximum stationary value is
never
asymptotically smaller than the maximum in-degree divided by

m
and these two asymptotically coincide for sequences with an outstanding maximum in-degree vertex (see
Proposition

9.1
).

In contrast, in
Fig.

1
we display the results of a simulation done for the directed configuration model with power-law in-degree distribution and constant out-degree, which suggests that asymptotically the two may only differ by a non-trivial multiplicative factor
[

]
.
