Nonparametric Estimation of Matching Efficiency and Mismatch: An Application to Japanese Labor Markets
Suguru Otani

Abstract
I identify significant biases in the traditional Cobb-Douglas function under misspecification of nonadditive, time-varying matching efficiency, and evaluate the finite-sample performance of the nonparametric estimation method of Lange and Papageorgiou (2020) . Additionally, I extend the mismatch index by Şahin et al. (2014) to a nonparametric framework and develop a computational methodology. Applying the method to Japanese labor market data from government surveys and Hello Work, I analyze changes in matching efficiency, elasticities, and mismatch. Both datasets show declining matching efficiency, aligned with decreasing job-finding and vacancy-filling rates. The unemployment elasticity is around 0.5–0.9 in the Hello Work data and lower in the nationally representative aggregate, while the vacancy elasticity is higher in the nationally representative aggregate. Furthermore, I demonstrate that occupational mismatch is more severe than geographic mismatch, with the Cobb-Douglas mismatch index significantly underestimating the true extent of mismatch. Keywords : matching efficiency, matching elasticity, matching function, mismatch JEL code : E24, J61, J62, J64

Introduction

Understanding labor market matching efficiency and mismatch—defined as the misalignment between the distribution of vacant jobs and unemployed workers—is essential for analyzing labor market dynamics. The matching function, which maps hires to the number of job seekers and vacancies while incorporating matching efficiency and search effort, is central to understanding frictional labor markets. However, as
Lange and Papageorgiou (2020)
highlight, a critical limitation of existing studies is their reliance on restrictive functional form assumptions, such as the Cobb-Douglas specification, which often lack empirical and theoretical justification. These assumptions can lead to biased estimates of matching efficiency and elasticity. To address this issue,
Lange and Papageorgiou (2020)
propose a nonparametric approach to identify the matching function and recover matching efficiency without imposing functional form restrictions.

Despite this advancement, the gap between nonparametric and Cobb-Douglas approaches remains, particularly in measuring labor mismatch. This paper aims to bridge this gap by evaluating the finite-sample performance of the proposed estimator, documenting the bias of Cobb-Douglas approaches under misspecification, and extending the mismatch index proposed by
Şahin et al. (2014)
to a nonparametric framework.

Several studies have assessed labor mismatch and its contribution to unemployment fluctuations using the Cobb-Douglas specification.
Şahin et al. (2014)
provide a methodology to quantify mismatch as the deviation between observed labor market outcomes and socially optimal outcomes. However, the Cobb-Douglas specification oversimplifies the mismatch index by canceling out time-varying matching efficiency and elasticities. As a methodological contribution, this paper combines the nonparametric approach with the mismatch index, allowing for nonparametrically estimated matching efficiency and elasticities. Additionally, I demonstrate how to compute the index using the Mathematical Programming with Equilibrium Constraints (MPEC) framework proposed by
Su and Judd (2012)
. To address practitioners’ concerns, I highlight the bias of the traditional Cobb-Douglas matching function and mismatch index and evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed estimator via Monte Carlo simulation and empirical application.

My empirical application combines two complementary Japanese datasets. Dataset 1 (LFS + SET) merges the Labour Force Survey and the Survey on Employment Trends into a nationally representative annual panel at the country, region, and industry levels (country 1972–2024; region and industry 2012–2024). Its strength is broad coverage of observed hiring channels within the Survey on Employment Trends coverage—private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees in SET-covered industries, excluding sectors outside the SET such as government and primary industries—and statistical representativeness within that scope; its limitation is annual frequency and coarse cross-sectional resolution (13 regional blocks, 14 industries). Dataset 2 (Hello Work) draws on the Public Employment Security Office statistics, providing a monthly long-run series (1972–2023 for the aggregate estimation sample) at fine cross-sectional resolution (47 prefectures, 67 job categories), with full-time and part-time decompositions, for a clearly defined platform-level market; its limitation is partial coverage of the labor market, since Hello Work intermediates about 11–22% of total hires nationwide (mean 18.5% over 1991–2024). The matching function estimated on Dataset 1 therefore approximates an aggregate matching function for the Japanese labor market within SET coverage, while the matching function estimated on Dataset 2 represents a platform-level matching function for the Hello Work intermediation system. Hello Work statistics have been the de facto standard data source for Japanese matching function estimation
(
Kano and Ohta 2005
,
Kambayashi and Ueno 2006
,
Sasaki 2008
,
Kambayashi 2013
,
Higashi 2018
,
Kawata 2019
)
, but to my knowledge this is the first paper to complement the Hello Work-based platform matching function with a nationally representative aggregate matching function constructed from LFS + SET.

For Dataset 1, the nationally representative country-year matching efficiency, normalized to 1972, exhibits a long-run downward trend with notable fluctuations. The implied unemployment elasticity is centered around 0.3–0.5, and the vacancy elasticity is correspondingly centered around 0.5–0.7. At the region-year and industry-year level (2012–2024), the nonparametric mismatch index ranges between roughly 0.07 and 0.29 with a mean around 0.20–0.21, consistently exceeding the corresponding Cobb-Douglas indices and confirming the downward bias of the latter even at this more representative aggregate level.

For Dataset 2, the Hello Work matching efficiency, normalized to 1972, also exhibits a declining trend with notable fluctuations, aligning with downward trends in job-finding and vacancy-filling rates. This decline may reflect the increasing role of alternative matching opportunities outside the government-operated Hello Work platform. The implied unemployment elasticity ranges from 0.5 to 0.9, consistent with global findings such as
Petrongolo and Pissarides (2001)
(0.5–0.7) and previous Japanese studies. The vacancy elasticity has an interquartile range of 0.18–0.42 with a mean around 0.29, broadly consistent with estimates by
Lange and Papageorgiou (2020)
(0.15–0.3) and Japanese studies (0.15–0.6). Substantial mismatches are observed at both the prefecture-month and occupation-month levels, with an upward trend over time: the average mismatch across prefectures is about 0.25, while mismatch across occupations averages about 0.52 and ranges from about 0.39 to 0.65—significantly larger than reported by
Shibata (2020)
and
Higashi and Sasaki (2025)
, because their Cobb-Douglas index, by construction, ignores the time variation of matching efficiency and elasticity that the nonparametric estimator captures, thereby attenuating the measured deviation between actual and socially optimal allocations.

Across both datasets, the proposed nonparametric mismatch index reveals the limitations of the Cobb-Douglas mismatch index, demonstrating its downward bias through both Monte Carlo simulations and empirical application. Comparing the geographic and the sectoral or occupational dimensions, sectoral or occupational mismatch is consistently larger than geographic mismatch, with the largest values arising in the Hello Work occupation panel (mean about 0.52 with peaks reaching about 0.65), followed by the Hello Work prefecture panel (mean about 0.25); LFS + SET region and industry mismatches both lie in the 0.1–0.3 range. Part of this gap reflects the granularity of cross-sectional resolution: LFS + SET aggregates to 13 regional blocks and 14 broad industries, which mechanically washes out within-aggregate misallocation, whereas Hello Work resolves 47 prefectures and 67 job categories, which preserves and exposes finer skill- and location-specific frictions. The cross-dataset comparison further shows that the platform-level (Hello Work) matching dynamics are qualitatively informative about wider Japanese labor market trends, but with level differences that warrant caution when extrapolating Hello Work-based magnitudes (e.g. tightness
V/U
or mismatch levels) to the aggregate. These findings underscore the importance of accounting for time-varying matching efficiency and elasticity in labor market analysis, as well as for the cross-sectional resolution at which mismatch is measured.

Nonparametric Aggregate Matching Function

The main objects of interest are matching efficiency and matching elasticities with respect to the number of unemployed workers and vacancies in the labor market.
A matching function based on search models plays a central role in labor economics.

2.2
Nonparametric Mismatch

I construct a nonparametric version of a mismatch index which is a measure of the fraction of hires foregone due to mismatch.
Suppose there are
R
local markets at time
t
with a given number of vacancies, unemployed workers, and aggregate matching efficiencies in the economy.
The number of hires
H_{rt}
in market
r
at time
t
is determined by a matching function
m(A_{rt}U_{rt},V_{rt})
.
Note that, unlike
Şahin et al. (2014)
, I allow any specification of
m
satisfying CRS.
Given matching efficiency
A_{rt}
and the number of vacancies
V_{rt}
, a social planner maximizes the total hires in the
economy by distributing a given number of unemployed workers to each labor market.

Following
Şahin et al. (2014)
, I define mismatch as the deviation of the hires in the data from the efficient allocation chosen by the social planner.
Under the assumptions of homogeneous productivity and job separation rates across labor markets, the optimal allocation of unemployed workers satisfies the following equilibrium conditions:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial m}{\partial U}\left(A_{1t}U_{1t}^{*},V_{1t}\right)=\cdots=\frac{\partial m}{\partial U}\left(A_{Rt}U_{Rt}^{*},V_{Rt}\right)

(1)

where
*
denotes the planner’s allocation.
The equilibrium condition means that, holding the total number of unemployed workers
U_{t}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}U_{rt}
fixed, the planner allocates more unemployed workers to more effective labor markets, that is, markets with more vacancies and higher matching efficiency, until their marginal contribution to hires is equalized across markets.
The main difference from
Şahin et al. (2014)
is that equilibrium condition (
1
) does not have an analytical formula like Cobb-Douglas specification.
In Section
3.2
, I explain how to compute the optimal allocation of unemployed workers numerically.

Using the optimal allocation of unemployed workers
U_{rt}^{*}
, the aggregate actual and optimal number of new hires can be expressed as

\displaystyle H_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}m(A_{rt}U_{rt},V_{rt}),

\displaystyle H_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}m(A_{rt}U_{rt}^{*},V_{rt}).

Using these expressions, I define the mismatch index as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}=1-\frac{H_{t}}{H_{t}^{*}}

(2)

where
\mathcal{M}_{t}
measures the fraction of hires lost in period
t
because of misallocation.
This index accounts for the heterogeneity of matching efficiencies across labor markets. I explicitly refer to the mismatch index (
2
) under the Cobb-Douglas matching function as the Cobb-Douglas mismatch index.

2.3
Why Is the Nonparametric Mismatch Index Superior to the Cobb-Douglas Mismatch?

To illustrate the potential benefit of the nonparametric mismatch index, consider
R
markets observed over
T
years. Estimating the Cobb-Douglas mismatch requires specifying a Cobb-Douglas matching function with additive matching efficiency,
H_{rt}=A_{r}A_{t}U_{rt}^{1-\gamma}V_{rt}^{\gamma}
, with
R+T+1
parameters:
R
market fixed effects (
A_{r}
),
T
year fixed effects (
A_{t}
), and a constant vacancy share parameter (
\gamma
). The equilibrium condition (
1
) can be expressed as:

A_{r}A_{t}\left(\frac{V_{rt}}{U_{rt}^{\star}}\right)^{\gamma}(1-\gamma)=A_{r^{\prime}}A_{t}\left(\frac{V_{r^{\prime}t}}{U_{r^{\prime}t}^{\star}}\right)^{\gamma}(1-\gamma).

This formulation highlights a critical limitation of the Cobb-Douglas mismatch: the time variations in
A_{t}
and the elasticity
\gamma
are canceled out in the equilibrium condition.
In contrast, the proposed nonparametric approach estimates
T\times R
time- and market-specific efficiencies and corresponding time-varying elasticities, thereby preserving the temporal variations of
A_{t}
and elasticity. This allows for greater flexibility and precision in capturing the dynamics of matching efficiency and mismatch across both time and markets.

While the nonparametric approach offers more flexibility, it also requires a larger sample size to ensure precise estimates. The finite-sample performance of this approach is evaluated in Section
4
.

2.4
Extension: Inclusion of Heterogeneity

The baseline mismatch index above follows the benchmark case in
Şahin et al. (2014)
and assumes homogeneous productivity and job separation rates across markets. The nonparametric framework can incorporate these sources of heterogeneity without changing the estimated matching function. Let
z_{rt}
denote market-specific labor productivity and let
\delta_{rt}
denote the market-specific exogenous match destruction rate. The planner’s problem can be generalized by maximizing the weighted value of matches,

\max_{\{U_{rt}^{*}\}_{r=1}^{R}}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{rt}m(A_{rt}U_{rt}^{*},V_{rt})\quad\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{r=1}^{R}U_{rt}^{*}=U_{t},

where
\omega_{rt}
is the value of a match in market
r
. In a static extension,
\omega_{rt}=z_{rt}
. In the dynamic extension of
Şahin et al. (2014)
, a match in a high-destruction market has a shorter expected duration, so a natural weight is proportional to
z_{rt}/[1-\beta(1-\Delta_{t})(1-\delta_{rt})]
, up to the aggregate terms common across markets. The planner therefore equalizes
\omega_{rt}\partial m(A_{rt}U_{rt}^{*},V_{rt})/\partial U
across markets, rather than equalizing the unweighted marginal contribution to hires in (
1
).

Operationally, this extension only changes the objective function and the equilibrium constraints in the MPEC computation. The nonparametric estimates of
A_{rt}
,
m(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the local derivatives can be used as before, while the optimal allocation gives more weight to markets where a new match is more productive or expected to last longer. The corresponding output-weighted mismatch index would be

\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\omega}=1-\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{rt}m(A_{rt}U_{rt},V_{rt})}{\sum_{r=1}^{R}\omega_{rt}m(A_{rt}U_{rt}^{*},V_{rt})}.

Thus, the current index is the special case
\omega_{rt}=1
for all markets. Implementing this richer index would require external measures of market-level productivity and destruction or separation rates, such as region- or occupation-specific wages, value added, or job separation rates. Because sufficiently detailed and consistent measures are not available for the markets in this paper, we view this heterogeneity-adjusted mismatch index as a natural but out-of-scope extension.

Estimation

3.1
Matching Efficiency and Elasticities

Following
Lange and Papageorgiou (2020)
, I begin by estimating
F(A_{0}|U)
across the support of
U
. To this end, I utilize the distribution of hires conditional on unemployed,
U
, and observed vacancies,
V
.
Specifically, I have

\displaystyle F(A_{0}|\psi U_{0})

\displaystyle=G_{H|U,V}(\psi H_{0}|\psi U_{0},\psi V_{0})\quad\text{for any arbitrary scalar }\psi.

\displaystyle F(\psi A_{0}|\lambda U_{0})

\displaystyle=G_{H|U,V}(\psi H_{0}|\lambda U_{0},\psi V_{0})\quad\text{where }\lambda>0\text{ is a scaling factor}

where
F(A_{0}|\psi U_{0})
and
G_{H|U,V}
are conditional distributions.
By varying
(\psi,\lambda)
, I can therefore trace out
F(A|U)
across the entire support of
(A,U)
.

Given finite data, I rely on an estimate of
G_{H|U,V}
for the constructive estimator. Consider an arbitrary point
(H_{\tau},U_{\tau},V_{\tau})
. To obtain
G(H_{\tau}|U_{\tau},V_{\tau})
, I compute the proportion of observations with observed hires below
H_{\tau}
among observations proximate to
(U_{\tau},V_{\tau})
in
(U,V)
-space. In practice, this is done by averaging across all observations in the data, using a kernel to downweight observations far from
(U_{\tau},V_{\tau})
. Consequently, the estimate is given by

F(\psi A_{0}|\lambda U_{0})=G_{H|U,V}(\psi H_{0}|\lambda U_{0},\psi V_{0})

\hat{F}(\psi A_{0}|\lambda U_{0})=\sum 1(H_{t}<\psi H_{0})\kappa(U_{t},V_{t},\lambda U_{0},\psi V_{0})

where
\kappa(\cdot)
denotes normalized bivariate normal kernel weights with baseline bandwidth 0.10.

Having recovered the distribution function
F(A|U)
, I invert
F(A_{t}|U_{t})
to derive
A_{t}
. This is achieved by

A_{t}=F^{-1}(G(H_{t}|U_{t},V_{t})|U_{t})

for all observations
(H_{t},U_{t},V_{t})
in the dataset. Finally, I recover the matching function as

m(A_{t}U_{t},V_{t})=G^{-1}_{H|U,V}(F(A_{t}|U_{t})|U_{t},V_{t}).

To calculate matching elasticities, I run a LASSO regression projecting hires on vacancies, efficiency-weighted unemployment, their interaction, and their squared terms.
The estimates approximate the derivatives of the matching function with respect to vacancies and efficiency-weighted unemployment, yielding the elasticities of the matching function.
I denote the unemployment elasticity by
\varepsilon_{U}\equiv\partial\log m(AU,V)/\partial\log U=\partial\log m(AU,V)/\partial\log(AU)
and the vacancy elasticity by
\varepsilon_{V}\equiv\partial\log m(AU,V)/\partial\log V
.
Under CRS, I report
\varepsilon_{V}=1-\varepsilon_{U}
in the main results, which imposes the adding-up restriction but does not manually censor local estimates that fall outside the unit interval; Appendix
B
reports an illustrative diagnostic of the unconstrained LASSO derivative.
8
8

8

The unemployment elasticity
\frac{d\log m(AU,V)}{d\log U}=\frac{dm(AU,V)}{dU}\frac{U}{H}=\frac{dm(AU,V)}{dAU}\frac{dAU}{dU}\frac{U}{H}=\frac{dm(AU,V)}{dAU}\frac{AU}{H}=\frac{d\log m(AU,V)}{d\log AU}
is obtained from the regression coefficient of
H
on
AU
and multiplying it by
\frac{AU}{H}
. Concretely, I approximate
m
by the second order polynomial
m=\beta_{1}(AU)+\beta_{2}(AU)V+\beta_{3}V+\beta_{4}(AU)^{2}+\beta_{5}V^{2}
and get
\frac{dm(AU,V)}{dAU}=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}V+2\beta_{4}(AU)
and
\frac{d\log m}{d\log U}=\frac{d\log m}{d\log AU}=(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}V+2\beta_{4}(AU))\frac{AU}{H}
. For calculating mismatch, I need
\frac{dm}{dU}=\frac{d\log m(AU,V)}{d\log AU}\frac{H}{U}
at
U=U^{*}
and
H=H^{*}=m(AU^{*},V)
.

3.2
Mismatch

Unlike the original Cobb-Douglas specification of
Şahin et al. (2014)
, equilibrium condition (
1
) does not have an analytical formula.
Instead, I employ the Mathematical Programming with Equilibrium Constraints (MPEC) approach proposed by
Su and Judd (2012)
and
Dubé et al. (2012)
.
The constrained optimization problem in time
t
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle\max_{U_{1t},\cdots,U_{Rt}}

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{R}m\left(A_{rt}U_{rt},V_{rt}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\frac{\partial m}{\partial U}\left(A_{1t}U_{1t}^{*},V_{1t}\right)

\displaystyle=\cdots=\frac{\partial m}{\partial U}\left(A_{Rt}U_{Rt}^{*},V_{Rt}\right),

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{R}U_{rt}.

Maximizing the objective function is equivalent to solving equilibrium conditions (
1
) so that the central planner’s problem represents a nonlinear system of equations with linear equilibrium constraints.
I solve the model using
Ipopt.jl
and
JuMP.jl
, which are standard tools for MPEC problems.
The formulation can incorporate additional equilibrium constraints, such as the government’s cost of reallocating unemployed workers across markets in calibration exercises, but such extensions are outside the scope of this paper.

4
Simulation

To address practitioners’ concerns, I investigate the finite-sample performance of the estimator, extending Section 4 of
Matzkin (2003)
to the aggregate labor market setting. The primary focus is on sample size, robustness to model specification, and sensitivity to endogeneity in estimating time-varying matching efficiency. I simulate 100 datasets with sample sizes
T=50
,
T=100
, and
T=200
, representing aggregate-level data over 50, 100, and 200 months, respectively, and consider correlations between
V
and
A
,
\rho^{AV}=0,0.1,0.2,0.5
. Positive
\rho^{AV}
induces upward bias. A stationary AR(1) process is generated for the inputs as
U_{t}=\rho^{U}U_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}^{U}
,
V_{t}=\rho^{V}V_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}^{V}
, and
A_{t}=\rho^{A}A_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}^{A}
, with
(\rho^{U},\rho^{V},\rho^{A})=(0.2,0.2,0.2)
, and
(\varepsilon_{t}^{U},\varepsilon_{t}^{V},\varepsilon_{t}^{A})
drawn i.i.d. from the standard normal distribution. If
\rho^{AV}>0
,
A_{t}
is recomputed via Cholesky decomposition to achieve the desired correlation with
V_{t}
. Inputs are rescaled to mimic actual data by multiplying
U_{t}
and
V_{t}
by 100 and
A_{t}
by 10, normalizing
A_{t}
such that
A_{1}=100
.

Restricting the analysis to the CRS class, I specify matching function
m(A_{t}U_{t},V_{t})
as a Cobb-Douglas function
((A_{t}U_{t})^{\gamma}V_{t}^{1-\gamma})/10
and a perfect substitute function
((\gamma A_{t}U_{t}+\gamma V_{t})/10)
, where
\gamma=0.3
. The number of matches
H_{t}
is generated using the true
m(A_{t}U_{t},V_{t})
. For nonparametric estimation, a Gaussian kernel with bandwidth
h=0.1
is applied. For comparison, a misspecified Cobb-Douglas model is estimated using OLS, i.e.,
\log(10H_{t})=\hat{A}+\hat{\gamma}\log(U_{t})+(1-\hat{\gamma})\log(V_{t})
, recovering
A=\exp\left(\frac{\hat{A}}{\hat{\gamma}}\right)
as the normalized efficiency term.

Bias and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) of matching efficiency
A_{t}
are calculated at 50 randomly chosen fixed grid points. The averages of these results, across the 50 points and 100 simulations, are reported in Table
2
. The results highlight differences between the nonparametric (NP) and ordinary least squares (OLS) approaches. When the sample size is
T=200
, the NP method demonstrates robust performance with minimal bias (consistently below 1.5%) and relatively low RMSE, even under varying levels of
\rho^{AV}
. By contrast, the OLS approach exhibits substantial bias and significantly larger RMSE across all scenarios due to the misspecification of
A_{t}
as time-invariant. Furthermore, the inaccuracies in
\hat{A}
and
\hat{\gamma}
amplify errors in recovering
A_{t}
via log-transformation. These findings underscore the reliability of the NP method in capturing time-varying matching efficiency, particularly for larger sample sizes, and its modest robustness to endogeneity, contrasting with the limitations of the OLS approach.

5
Data

I use two datasets for complementary goals: (i) nationally representative evidence for external validity and (ii) a clearly defined platform-based market for nonparametric identification of matching dynamics. Dataset 1 (Labour Force Survey combined with the Survey on Employment Trends) covers observed hiring channels within the Survey on Employment Trends coverage---private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees in SET-covered industries, excluding sectors outside the SET such as government and primary industries---but is annual and limited to industry- and region-level analysis, and the matching function estimated on it approximates an aggregate matching function for the Japanese labor market within that scope.

10

10

I am grateful to the Editor, Tito Boeri, for suggesting that I complement the Hello Work-based estimates with a nationally representative aggregate matching function, and to Daiji Kawaguchi and Ryo Kambayashi for guidance on the data availability needed to construct this aggregate from the Labour Force Survey and the Survey on Employment Trends.
Dataset 2 (Hello Work) covers only the Public Employment Security Office channel but provides a long monthly time series with finer cross-sectional resolution and a full-time / part-time decomposition, and the matching function estimated on it is interpreted as a platform-level matching function for the Hello Work intermediation system.

5.1
Dataset 1: Nationally Representative Aggregate Data

For the first dataset, I combine two government surveys to construct annual matching panels at three different resolutions—country, industry, and region: the Survey on Employment Trends (SET,
Koyou Doukou Chousa
), an establishment survey published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), and the Labour Force Survey (LFS,
Roudouryoku Chousa
), a household survey published by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC).
The SET provides hires (
H
) and unfilled vacancies (
V
), and the LFS provides unemployment (
U
); all variables are measured in thousands of persons.
Unlike the Hello Work data in Dataset 2, these series are nationally representative within the SET’s coverage of private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees, and they capture matches realized through observed channels in that coverage rather than only through the Public Employment Security Office.

I define the three matching variables as follows.
For the region and industry panels, let
r
index a cross-sectional market, corresponding to a region or an industry. Hires
H_{rt}
are the annual flow of
hires from non-employment
only (
mishuugyou nyuushokusha
in the SET), explicitly excluding job-to-job transitions, and are taken from the SET table on accessions by prefecture, industry, enterprise size, sex, and job history. Restricting
H
to hires from non-employment is required for stock–flow consistency with the LFS unemployment stock
U_{rt}
(which excludes job changers) and avoids the mechanical
H/U>1
that would otherwise arise from Japan’s high inter-firm mobility; see Appendix
C
for full discussion.
Vacancies
V_{rt}
are the end-of-June stock of unfilled positions, including both full-time and part-time openings, from the SET table on unfilled vacancies by industry, enterprise size, region, and occupation.
Unemployment
U_{rt}
is the annual average of completely unemployed persons from the LFS, classified by industry of previous job for the industry panel, by prefecture for the region panel, and as the national total for the country panel.
Because
V
is an end-of-June stock,
U
is an annual average, and
H
is an annual flow, the three variables are not aligned at a single point in time within a year; this is a standard limitation of annual matching function estimation.

I construct three panels with different geographic and industry resolutions; each panel has a different sample period determined by data availability and classification consistency.

Country panel (year, 1972–2024).

At the national level, I observe
T=53
annual observations spanning 1972–2024. Hires
H_{t}
, vacancies
V_{t}
, unemployment
U_{t}
, and the labor force are taken from the SET and the LFS over the same span. Because the publicly available SET vacancy and hire tabulations do not extend back to 1972 in their currently published form, the country-level series for 1972 to the SET-publication start year are reconstructed from longer-running SET rate series and LFS employee counts; the full reconstruction details are in Appendix
C
.

Industry panel (year
\times
industry, 2012–2024).

The industry panel comprises the 16 major industry groups common to the SET (workplace establishment classification) and the LFS (industry of previous job) over 2012–2024, yielding
N=208
industry-year observations. I use the industry-of-previous-job classification because the LFS does not provide unemployment by destination occupation. At the estimation stage I drop two industries (Mining and Electricity/Gas/Water) for which
U_{rt}
takes too few distinct values to identify the local nonparametric regression, leaving
N=182
effective observations; details are reported in Appendix
C
.

Region panel (year
\times
region, 2012–2024).

The region panel aggregates prefecture-level hires and unemployment to the 13 SET regional blocks—Hokkaido, Tohoku, Kita-Kanto, Minami-Kanto, Hokuriku, Tokai, Kinki, Keihanshin, San’in, San’yo, Shikoku, Kita-Kyushu, and Minami-Kyushu—over 2012–2024, yielding
N=169
region-year observations. The sample starts in 2012 because of the SET’s post-2012 industry classification revision and the availability of LFS prefecture-level unemployment estimates on a consistent basis. Small-cell suppressions in the SET are treated as missing rather than zero, producing an unbalanced panel.

Two coverage caveats are worth noting in the main text.
First, the LFS classifies unemployment by industry of
previous
job, so
U_{rt}
for an industry cell includes workers seeking employment in other industries; the estimated matching function should therefore be read as a relationship that incorporates inter-industry mobility.
Second, the geographic units of
U
(place of residence in the LFS) and
V
(workplace location in the SET) differ, although the use of broad regional blocks rather than prefectures attenuates the resulting commuting-flow measurement error.
Detailed source-table mappings, the JSIC industry crosswalk, and the full set of measurement caveats are reported in Appendix
C
.

5.2
Dataset 2: Hello Work Data with a Clear Market Definition

For the second dataset, I use the Report on Employment Service (
Shokugyo Antei Gyomu Tokei
), published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), which records job seekers, vacancies, and successful placements through the Public Employment Security Office, commonly known as Hello Work. Hello Work is a government-operated employment service that provides job counseling, placement services, and vocational training. Because posted wages in Hello Work vacancies tend to be lower than the national average, and because the service is provided free of charge by the government, Hello Work should also be understood as an employment safety net rather than as a private matching platform. Because these statistics cover the Public Employment Security Office channel rather than all matching channels in Japan, the estimates are interpreted as outcomes in the Hello Work segment. The data are widely used in Japanese matching-function studies
(
Kano and Ohta 2005
,
Kambayashi and Ueno 2006
,
Sasaki 2008
,
Kambayashi 2013
,
Higashi 2018
)
, and
Kawata and Sato (2021)
use recent Hello Work data to measure the welfare effects of the COVID-19 shock on unemployed workers.
11
11

11

https://www.crepe.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/material/crepecl12.html
: Accessed 2024 June 6.
I use three Hello Work panels.

Country panel (month, 1972–2023).

The aggregate country panel covers the full-time + part-time Hello Work market at the monthly level from January 1972 to December 2023, yielding
T=624
observations; the full-time and part-time series each also contain 624 monthly observations. Hires
H_{t}
are successful job placements through Hello Work, vacancies
V_{t}
are active job openings registered at Hello Work, and unemployment
U_{t}
is measured by active job seekers registered at Hello Work. New graduates and vacancies targeting them are excluded; operationally, I use the “general worker” (
ippan
) series in the published tables. In the Hello Work system, part-time workers are defined as workers who work fewer hours than regular employees at the same establishment, so the full-time and part-time decomposition reflects the platform’s administrative classification. This panel is used to estimate the long-run platform-level matching function and to compare its aggregate dynamics with the nationally representative LFS + SET country-year panel. Appendix
A
reports additional analysis using year-level aggregate data covering 1963–2023 and decomposes the monthly Hello Work aggregate into full-time and part-time series.

Prefecture panel (month
\times
prefecture, 2013–2024).

The prefecture panel covers 47 prefectures at the monthly level from January 2013 to April 2024, yielding
N=6{,}392
prefecture-month observations in the nonparametric estimation output. It provides the main geographic cross-sectional panel for the Hello Work market. Hires, vacancies, and job seekers are all measured within the Hello Work system, so the panel has a consistent platform-level market definition across prefectures.

Occupation panel (month
\times
job category, 2012–2023).

The occupation panel covers 67 Hello Work job categories at the monthly level from March 2012 to March 2023, yielding
N=8{,}911
job-category-month observations in the nonparametric estimation output. Unemployed workers are tabulated by registered desired occupation (
yuukou kyuushokusha
), and vacancies are tabulated by occupation-specific openings (
yuukou kyuujin
) under the MHLW occupational classification. This panel is used to measure occupational mismatch within the Hello Work platform. The sample period is constrained by the revision of job classifications before and after 2012, as in
Kawata (2019)
and
Higashi (2018)
. Because occupation classifications and publication formats are not consistently available jointly with prefectures over the full period, I analyze prefecture and occupation panels separately rather than estimating a combined prefecture-by-occupation panel. Finer market definitions are conceptually attractive because they expand the set of across-market reallocations available to the planner; mechanically, mismatch indices tend to be weakly larger as markets become more disaggregated.
Şahin et al. (2014)
make a related choice in their appendix: because CPS unemployment microdata become too thin for fully disaggregated county-by-occupation markets, they use the interaction of two-digit occupations and nine Census divisions at the quarterly frequency to reduce small-cell noise. The same trade-off applies here: occupation-region cells would be useful, but stable measurement requires a worker-side panel that is not publicly available with consistent hires, job seekers, and vacancies over the relevant period.

In summary, the two datasets are complementary rather than substitutable. Dataset 1 (LFS + SET) is nationally representative within the SET’s coverage of private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees in SET-covered industries, excluding sectors outside the SET such as government and primary industries, but it is annual and limited to industry- and region-level resolutions over 2012–2024 (with country totals reconstructed back to 1972). The matching function estimated on Dataset 2 should therefore be read as a platform-level matching function for the Hello Work intermediation system, whereas the matching function estimated on Dataset 1 approximates an aggregate matching function for the Japanese labor market within SET coverage. 6
Empirical Results

I report empirical applications based on two datasets.
The empirical objective is to balance representativeness and market-definition clarity: Dataset 1 provides a nationally representative benchmark, while Dataset 2 identifies matching dynamics in the clearly defined Hello Work segment over a long time horizon. I report aggregate, disaggregated, and mismatch results below, with additional diagnostics in the Online Appendix.

Before presenting the aggregate trend results, I test the assumption that vacancies are independent of matching efficiency, conditional on overall labor supply, i.e.,
V\perp A\mid U
.
Specifically, I use the residuals from a regression of vacancies
V
on the unemployed
U
, and similarly, the residuals of implied matching efficiency
A
on
U
.
For the aggregate data, the correlation between these two residuals is close to zero (0.04), indicating no systematic relationship between them.
For the Hello Work full-time and part-time data, the corresponding correlations are 0.14 and -0.14.
As shown in Figure
13
in the Appendix, visible deviations are driven by a few outliers, so any violation of the independence assumption appears limited.

7
Conclusion

This study highlights significant biases in the traditional Cobb-Douglas function when nonadditive, time-varying matching efficiency is misspecified, and evaluates the finite-sample performance of the nonparametric estimation method proposed by
Lange and Papageorgiou (2020)
. By extending the mismatch index of
Şahin et al. (2014)
to a nonparametric framework and developing a computational methodology, I analyze Japan’s labor market using two complementary datasets: a nationally representative aggregate panel constructed from the Labour Force Survey and the Survey on Employment Trends (LFS + SET, 1972–2024) and the Hello Work platform statistics (1972–2023 for the aggregate monthly estimation sample). Both datasets reveal a declining trend in matching efficiency, consistent with falling job-finding and vacancy-filling rates. Estimated unemployment elasticities are around 0.5 to 0.9 in the Hello Work data and lower in the LFS + SET country-year aggregate, with the vacancy elasticity correspondingly lower in the Hello Work data and higher in the LFS + SET aggregate. The results show that sectoral or occupational mismatch is more pronounced than geographic mismatch in both datasets, with the traditional Cobb-Douglas mismatch index significantly underestimating the extent of mismatch. The cross-dataset comparison further indicates that platform-level (Hello Work) matching dynamics are qualitatively informative about wider Japanese labor market trends, but level differences—driven in part by Hello Work’s partial coverage and finer cross-sectional resolution (47 prefectures, 67 job categories) versus the broader LFS + SET aggregation (13 regional blocks, 14 industries)—warrant caution when extrapolating Hello Work-based magnitudes to the aggregate. These findings underscore the importance of adopting nonparametric approaches to better capture time-varying matching efficiency, elasticity, and mismatch, as well as accounting for the cross-sectional resolution at which mismatch is measured.

Future research could extend this analysis to other private platforms and to individual-level behavior. Recent studies of job-matching intermediaries
(
Kambayashi et al. 2025
)
, on-site spot gig-work platforms
(
Kanayama et al. 2026
,
Cullen and Farronato 2021
)
, off-site gig-work platforms
(
Stanton and Thomas 2025
)
, and scouting platforms
(
Roussille and Scuderi 2025
)
point to settings in which richer microdata could deepen our understanding of labor market dynamics. Another advantage of the proposed computational framework is that the equilibrium constraints can be modified flexibly without changing the nonparametric estimation of the matching function. This feature makes it possible to incorporate richer market heterogeneity, such as productivity differences, market-specific job destruction rates, or policy-relevant reallocation constraints, once sufficiently detailed data become available.

A.2
The Cobb-Douglas Mismatch Index and Matching Function

Under the Cobb-Douglas matching function with additive matching efficiency, i.e.,
H_{rt}=A_{r}A_{t}U_{rt}^{1-\gamma}V_{rt}^{\gamma}
, the equilibrium condition (
1
) can be rewritten as
\frac{V_{rt}}{U_{rt}^{\star}}=\left(\frac{A_{r^{\prime}}}{A_{r}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\frac{V_{r^{\prime}t}}{U_{r^{\prime}t}^{\star}}
. The optimal nationwide number of new hires is then derived as
H_{t}^{*}=A_{t}U_{t}^{1-\gamma}V_{t}^{\gamma}\left[\sum_{r=1}^{R}A_{r}\left(\frac{U_{rt}}{V_{t}}\right)^{1-\gamma}\left(\frac{V_{rt}}{V_{t}}\right)^{\gamma}\right]
. Substituting this into the rewritten equilibrium condition yields
H_{t}^{*}=\bar{A}_{t}A_{t}U_{t}^{1-\gamma}V_{t}^{\gamma}
, where
\bar{A}_{t}=\left[\sum_{r=1}^{R}A_{r}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\left(\frac{V_{rt}}{V_{t}}\right)\right]^{\gamma}
. Using this, the Cobb-Douglas mismatch index takes an analytical form as
\mathcal{M}_{t}=1-\frac{H_{t}}{H_{t}^{*}}=1-\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\frac{A_{r}}{\bar{A}_{t}}\right)\left(\frac{U_{rt}}{U_{t}}\right)^{1-\gamma}\left(\frac{V_{rt}}{V_{t}}\right)^{\gamma}
. When matching efficiencies are identical across labor markets, the Cobb-Douglas mismatch index is equivalent to the conventional mismatch index proposed by
Jackman and Roper (1987)
. The detailed property is shown in
Şahin et al. (2014)
.

The main results impose CRS by reporting

\varepsilon_{U}+\varepsilon_{V}^{\text{direct}}=1

). The main results in the body of the paper instead report

, which mechanically sums to one. The diagnostic is shown only for the Hello Work monthly aggregate, where the large sample size makes the unconstrained LASSO derivative more stable. The post-COVID period may deviate more from the CRS benchmark and should be interpreted with caution.

Appendix C
Data Construction

This appendix provides a detailed description of the construction of the industry-level and region-level panel datasets used in the nationally representative analysis (Dataset 1). All variables are measured in thousands of persons. The raw data (in Japanese), constructed data, and replication code are available on
https://github.com/ohtanilson/hellowork
, making the data construction transparent and open to discussion.

C.1
Data Sources

I combine two government surveys to construct the matching variables
H_{rt}
(hires),
U_{rt}
(unemployment), and
V_{rt}
(vacancies) for cross-sectional market
r
and year
t
, covering 2012–2024. Here,
r
denotes an industry in the industry panel and a region in the region panel.
The raw public data are not provided as a unified dataset but are spread across roughly 60 Excel files with different table layouts, making it difficult to describe every merge and cleaning step fully in prose. The replication code therefore includes the preprocessing scripts used to construct the analysis-ready data, and readers interested in the exact implementation details should refer to those scripts.

Survey on Employment Trends (
Koyou Doukou Chousa
).

Published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), this establishment survey covers private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees. I extract two variables:

•

Hires
(
H
): Number of new hires from non-employment by prefecture
\times
industry, reported as an annual total. Source table: “Number of Accessions by Prefecture, Industry (Major Group), Enterprise Size, Sex, and Job History.”

•

Unfilled vacancies
(
V
): Number of unfilled positions at the end of June (stock), by region
\times
industry. This figure is available only from the first-half survey. Source table: “Number of Unfilled Vacancies by Industry (Major Group), Enterprise Size, Region, and Occupation (Major Group).”

Labour Force Survey (
Roudouryoku Chousa
).

Published by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications (MIC), this household survey provides nationally representative estimates of the unemployed population.

•

Unemployment by industry
(
U
): Annual average of completely unemployed persons classified by industry of previous job (detailed tabulation). Unit: ten thousands (converted to thousands for merging). Available for 21 industry categories, of which 16 match the Survey on Employment Trends classification.

•

Unemployment by prefecture
(
U
): Annual average of completely unemployed persons by prefecture, based on time-series regression model estimates. Unit: thousands. These are model-based estimates because the Labour Force Survey’s sample design does not support direct prefecture-level tabulation with sufficient precision.

C.2
Variable Definitions

Hires (
H
).

H_{rt}
is the annual flow of
new accessions from non-employment
(Japanese:
mishuugyou nyuushokusha
), measured in thousands of persons. I exclude job-to-job transitions because (i) the unemployment stock
U_{rt}
from the LFS does not include job-changers, so pairing
H
with
U
requires a non-employment-only flow to maintain stock–flow conceptual consistency for matching function estimation, and (ii) using total accessions instead inflates
H/U
to mechanically exceed unity in most years given Japan’s high inter-firm mobility. While
H_{rt}
is an annual flow and
V_{rt}
and
U_{rt}
are stocks, this stock–flow pairing is standard in the matching function literature (analogous to JOLTS monthly hires paired with CPS unemployment stocks). However, because the reference periods differ—
H
covers the full calendar year,
V
is observed at end-June, and
U
is an annual average—the timing mismatch may affect the estimates.

Long-run reconstruction of
H
for the country panel (1972–1990).

For the country-year panel covering 1972–2024, the SET breakdown of accessions into “hires from non-employment” and “hires from job change” (Table 15 of the SET annual report) is published only from 1991. For the earlier 1972–1990 segment I therefore recover hires from non-employment as

H^{\text{NE,recovered}}_{t}=H^{\text{total,recovered}}_{t}\times s^{\text{NE}}_{1991},\quad t=1972,\ldots,1990,

where
H^{\text{total,recovered}}_{t}
is the calibrated long-run total-accession series obtained from the published SET hire rate multiplied by the LFS employee count and rescaled to match the SET total-accession level over the 1991–2024 overlap, and
s^{\text{NE}}_{1991}=H^{\text{NE}}_{1991}/H^{\text{total}}_{1991}\approx 0.432
is the boundary-year non-employment share from the SET Table 15 (1991: 2,672/6,189 thousand). I use the boundary-year share rather than the 1991–2024 average (
\approx 0.382
, range 0.34–0.45) to avoid contaminating the pre-1991 segment with later structural shifts in inter-firm mobility. From 1991 onwards,
H^{\text{NE}}_{t}
is taken directly from the published SET Table 15 without rescaling. The
H^{\text{NE,recovered}}_{t}
series is the variable used as
H_{t}
in the country-panel matching function estimation; the original
H^{\text{total,recovered}}_{t}
series and the unscaled SET totals are retained for diagnostic purposes. As a sensitivity check, using the 1991–2024 average non-employment share instead of the boundary-year share changes only the level of the reconstructed pre-1991 hire series and leaves the qualitative aggregate patterns and the paper’s main conclusions unchanged.

Vacancy (
V
).

V_{rt}
is the stock of unfilled vacancies at end-June, taken directly from the Survey on Employment Trends. The measure includes both full-time and part-time unfilled positions. Although the same table reports “part-time workers” (column 16), this figure refers to the number of employed part-time workers, not the part-time component of unfilled vacancies; hence a part-time-excluded vacancy measure cannot be constructed from this source.

Unemployment (
U
).

U_{rt}
is the annual average of completely unemployed persons from the Labour Force Survey. For the industry panel, unemployment is classified by industry of previous job. For the region panel, prefecture-level estimates are aggregated into 13 regional blocks to match the geographic units of the unfilled vacancy data.

Consistency of
H
,
U
, and
V
.

Even with
H
restricted to hires from non-employment,
H_{rt}/U_{rt}
can still exceed unity at the annual frequency because
H
is a 12-month cumulated flow whereas
U
is an annual average stock; over a year, multiple unemployment spells can be turned over from a given average stock. To restore an interpretable “probability per period” scale, I report the monthly-equivalent finding rates
H_{t}/(12U_{t})
and
H_{t}/(12V_{t})
in the country-panel figure, which fall in the 0.05–0.32 and 0.09–0.74 ranges respectively over 1972–2024 and are comparable to the Hello Work monthly ranges (0.04–0.22 and 0.03–0.25). For the industry panel,
H_{rt}>U_{rt}
can still occur in some cells because annual hires are flows and unemployment is an annual average stock.

C.4
Measurement Issues and Caveats

Timing.

The three variables are not measured at the same point in time within a year.
V_{t}
is a stock measured at end-June,
U_{t}
is an annual average, and
H_{t}
is an annual flow. This timing mismatch is a common limitation in matching function estimation with annual data. In my baseline specification, I treat
t
as indexing the calendar year and note that
V_{t}
effectively reflects the mid-year labor market state.

Geographic basis: residence vs. workplace.

The Labour Force Survey records unemployment at the place of residence, whereas the Survey on Employment Trends records hires and vacancies at the establishment location (workplace). In the region panel, this discrepancy is mitigated by the use of broad regional blocks (13 blocks rather than 47 prefectures), which encompass most commuting flows within each block. Nevertheless, cross-region commuting—particularly between Keihanshin and Kinki, or within the Kanto area—may introduce measurement error.

Industry classification: previous job vs. destination job.

The Labour Force Survey classifies unemployed persons by the industry of their
previous
job, not by the industry in which they are seeking employment. If inter-industry mobility is substantial, the measured
U_{rt}
may overstate the effective supply of job seekers in a previous-industry market while understating it in destination industries. This mismatch tends to attenuate the estimated unemployment elasticity of the matching function and may contaminate estimated matching efficiency with industry-switching trends.

Survey coverage.

The Survey on Employment Trends covers private-sector establishments with five or more regular employees across 16 major industries. The Labour Force Survey, being a household survey, is nationally representative and covers all industries and employment types. The estimated matching function therefore applies to the segment of the labor market covered by the Survey on Employment Trends. Industries and establishment sizes outside this coverage are not represented.

Missing values.

Small-cell suppression in the Survey on Employment Trends produces missing values (coded as “
-
” in the original data) for certain industry
\times
prefecture and industry
\times
region combinations. I treat these as missing (NA) rather than replacing them with zero, and estimate on the resulting unbalanced panel. This avoids the systematic downward bias in
H
and
V
that would arise from zero-filling suppressed cells.
