Approximations related to Tempered stable Distributions
Kalyan Barman, Neelesh S Upadhye, Palaniappan Vellaisamy

Abstract
. In this article, we first obtain, for the Kolmogorov distance, an error bound between a tempered stable and a compound Poisson distribution (CPD) and also an error bound between a tempered stable and an α \alpha -stable distribution via Stein’s method. For the smooth Wasserstein distance, an error bound between two tempered stable distributions (TSD) is also derived. As examples, we discuss the approximation of a TSD to normal and variance-gamma distributions (VGD). As corollaries, the corresponding limit theorem follows.

Primary: 62E17, 62E20; Secondary: 60E05, 60E07.

1.
Introduction

Probability approximations is one of the fundamental topics in probability theory, due to its wide range of applications in limit theorems
[
4
,
29
,
31
]
, runs
[
30
]
, stochastic algorithms
[
33
]
, and various other fields. They mainly provide estimates of the distance between the distributions of two random variables (rvs), which measure closeness of the approximations. Hence, estimating the accuracy of the approximation is a crucial task. Recently, Chen
et al.

[
7
,
8
]
, Jin
et al.

[
17
]
, Upadhye and Barman
[
29
]
, Xu
[
34
]
have studied stable approximations via the Stein’s method. The distributional approximations for a family of stable distributions is not straightforward, due to the lack of symmetry and heavy-tailed behavior of stable distributions. One of the major obstacles is that the moments of a stable distribution do not exist, whenever the stability parameter
\alpha\in(0,1]
. To overcome these issues, different approaches and various assumptions are used.

Koponen
[
19
]
first introduced tempered stable distributions (TSD) by tempering the tail of the stable (also called
\alpha
-stable) distributions and making the distribution’s tail lighter. The tails of the TSD are heavier than the normal distribution and thinner than the
\alpha
-stable distribution, see
[
18
]
. Therefore, quantifying the error in approximating
\alpha
-stable and normal distributions to a TSD is of interest. A TSD has mean, variance and exponential moments. Also, the class of TSD includes many well-known sub-families of probability distributions, such as CGMY, KoBol, bilateral-gamma, and variance-gamma distributions, which have applications in several disciplines including financial mathematics, see
[
3
,
6
,
24
,
28
]
.

In this article, we first obtain, for the Kolmogorov distance, an error bound between tempered stable and compound Poisson distributions (CPD). This provides a convergence rate for the tempered stable approximation to a CPD. Next, we obtain the error bounds between tempered stable and
\alpha
-stable distributions via Stein’s method. We obtain also the error bounds between two TSD’s, for smooth Wasserstein distance. As a consequence, we discuss the normal and variance-gamma approximation and the corresponding limit theorems to a TSD.

The organization of this article is as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss some notations and preliminaries that will be useful later. First, we discuss some important properties of TSD and some special and limiting distributions from the TSD family. A brief discussion on Stein’s method is also presented. In Section
3
, we establish a Stein identity and a Stein equation for TSD and solve it via the semigroup approach. The properties of the solution to the Stein equation are discussed. In Section
4
, we discuss bounds for tempered stable approximations for various probability distributions.

2.
The preliminary results

2.1.
Properties of tempered stable distributions

We first define the TSD and discuss some of their the properties. Let
\textbf{I}_{B}(.)
denotes the indicator function of the set
B
.
A rv
X
is said to have TSD (see
[
20
, p.2]
) if its cf is given by

\phi_{ts}(z)=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{ts}(du)\right),~~z\in\mathbb{R},

(2.1)

where the Lévy measure
\nu_{ts}
is

\nu_{ts}(du)=\left(\frac{m_{1}}{u^{1+\alpha_{1}}}e^{-\lambda_{1}u}\mathbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(u)+\frac{m_{2}}{|u|^{1+\alpha_{2}}}e^{-\lambda_{2}|u|}\mathbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\right)du,

(2.2)

with parameters
m_{i},\lambda_{i}\in(0,\infty)
and
\alpha_{i}\in[0,1)
, for
i=1,2
, and we denote it by
\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
. Note that TSD are infinitely divisible and self-decomposable, see
[
20
]
. Also, note that, if
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha\in(0,1),
the Lévy measure in (
2.2
) can be seen as

\displaystyle\nu_{ts}(du)=q(u)\nu_{\alpha}(du),

(2.3)

where

\displaystyle\nu_{\alpha}(du)=\left(\frac{m_{1}}{u^{1+\alpha}}\mathbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}+\frac{m_{2}}{u^{1+\alpha}}\mathbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}\right)du

(2.4)

is the Lévy measure of a
\alpha
-stable distribution (see
[
25
]
) and
q:\mathbb{{R}}\to\mathbb{{R}}_{+}
is a tempering function (see
[
20
]
), given by

\displaystyle q(u)=\bigg(e^{-\lambda_{1}u}\mathbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(u)+e^{-\lambda_{2}|u|}\mathbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\bigg).

(2.5)

The following special and limiting cases of TSD are well known, see
[
20
]

Let
\overset{L}{\to}

m_{i},\lambda_{i}\in(0,\infty)

\alpha_{i}\in[0,1)

i=1,2

(i)

When
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha

When

(2.6)

where

(vii)

The limiting case as
m\to\infty

(m,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda)

\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2})

X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})

z\in\mathbb{{R}}

\displaystyle\Psi(z)=\log\mathbb{E}(e^{izX})=\log\phi_{ts}(z),

(2.7)

where
\phi(z)
is given in (

n

is

\displaystyle C_{n}(X)

\displaystyle:=(-i)^{n}\bigg[\frac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\Psi(z)\bigg]_{z=0}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{n}\nu_{ts}(du)<\infty,~n\geq 1.

(2.8)

In particular (see

\displaystyle C_{1}(X)

2.2.
Key steps of Stein’s method

Let
f^{(n)}
henceforth denotes the
n
-th derivative of
f
with
f^{(0)}=f
and
f^{\prime}=f^{(1)}
. Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be the Schwartz space defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}):=\left\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}|x^{m}f^{(n)}(x)|=0,\text{ for all }m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\},

(2.12)

where
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is the class of infinitely differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}
. Note that the Fourier transform (FT) on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is automorphism. In particular, if
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\widehat{f}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iux}f(x)dx,~~\text{ }u\in\mathbb{R},
then
\widehat{f}(u)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).
Similarly, if
\widehat{f}(u)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and
f(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iux}\widehat{f}(u)du,~~\text{ }x\in\mathbb{R},

then
f(x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, see
[
27
]
.

Next, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{r}=\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}|h\mbox{ is $r$ times differentiable and},\|h^{(k)}\|\leq 1,k=0,1,\ldots,r\},

(2.13)

where
\|h\|=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|h(x)|
. Then, for any two rvs
Y
and
Z
, the smooth Wasserstein distance (see
[
13
]
) is given by

d_{\mathcal{H}_{r}}(Y,Z):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\left|\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\right|,~r\geq 1.

(2.14)

Also, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{W}=\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}|h\mbox{ is $1$-Lipschitz and},\|h\|\leq 1\}.

(2.15)

Then, for any two rvs
Y
and
Z
, the classical Wasserstein distance (see
[
13
]
) is given by

d_{W}(Y,Z):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{W}}\left|\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\right|.

(2.16)

Finally, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{K}

\displaystyle=\left\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}~\big|~h=\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,x]},~x\in\mathbb{{R}}\right\}.

(2.17)

Then, for any two rvs
Y
and
Z
, the Kolmogorov distance (see
[
13
]
) is given by

d_{K}(Y,Z):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\left|\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\right|.

(2.18)

Next, we discuss the steps of Stein’s method. The method is based on the simple fact that, any real-valued random variable (rv)
Z
has probability distribution
F_{Z}
(denoted by
Z\sim F_{Z}
), if and only if

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{A}f(Z)\right)=0,

where
f\in\mathcal{F}
, a class suitable functions. This equivalence is called Stein characterization of
F_{Z}
. This characterization leads us to the Stein equation

Af(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)],

(2.19)

where
h
is a real-valued test function. Replacing
x
with a rv
Y
and taking expectations on both sides of (
2.19
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{A}f(Y)\right]=\mathbb{E}h(Y)-\mathbb{E}h(Z).

(2.20)

This equality (
2.20
) plays a crucial role in Stein’s method. The probability distribution
F_{Z}
is characterized by (
2.19
) such that the problem of bounding the quantity
|\mathbb{E}h(Y)-\mathbb{E}h(Z)|
depends on smoothness of the solution to (
2.19
), and behavior of
Y
. For more details on Stein’s method, we refer to the reader
[
1
,
5
]
and the references therein.

In particular, let
Z
has normal
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
distribution. Then a Stein characterization for
Z
(see
[
26
]
) is

\mathbb{E}\left(\sigma^{2}f^{\prime}(Z)-Zf(Z)\right)=0,

(2.21)

where
f
is any real-valued absolutely continuous function such that
\mathbb{E}|f^{\prime}(Z)|<\infty
. This characterization leads us to the Stein equation

\sigma^{2}f^{\prime}(x)-xf(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}h(Z),

(2.22)

where
h
is a real-valued test function. Replacing
x
with a rv
Z_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{n})
and taking expectations on both sides of (
2.22
) gives

\mathbb{E}\left(\sigma^{2}f^{\prime}(Z_{n})-Z_{n}f(Z_{n})\right)=\mathbb{E}h(Z_{n})-\mathbb{E}h(Z).

(2.23)

Using the smoothness of solution to (
2.22
), it can be shown (see
[
23
, Section 3.6]
) that

\displaystyle d_{W}(Z_{n},Z)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\sigma^{2}\vee\sigma^{2}_{n}}|\sigma^{2}_{n}-\sigma^{2}|.

(2.24)

From (
2.24
), if
\sigma_{n}\to\sigma
, then
d_{W}(Z_{n},Z)=0,
as expected, which implies that
Z_{n}
converges to a normal
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
distribution. We refer the reader to
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
for a number of similar bounds as (
2.24
) for comparison of univariate probability distributions.

3.
Stein’s method for tempered stable distributions

3.1.
A Stein identity for tempered stable distributions

In this section, we obtain a Stein identity for a TSD. First recall that
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}})
denotes the Schwartz space of functions, defined in (
2.12
).

Proposition 3.1
.

A rv
X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
, if and only if

\mathbb{E}\left(Xf(X)-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X+u)\nu_{ts}(du)\right)=0,~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

(3.1)

where
\nu_{ts}
is the associated Lévy measure of TSD, defined in (
2.2
).

Proof.

Taking logarithms on both sides of (
2.1
), and differentiating with respect to
z
, we have

\phi_{ts}^{\prime}(z)=i\phi_{ts}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{ts}(du).

(3.2)

Let
F_{X}
be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
X
. Then,

\phi_{ts}(z)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}F_{X}(dx)~~\implies~~\phi_{ts}^{\prime}(z)=i\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}F_{X}(dx).

(3.3)

Using (
3.3
) in (
3.2
) and rearranging the integrals, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=i\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}F_{X}(dx)-i\phi_{ts}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}F_{X}(dx)-\phi_{ts}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{ts}(du)

(3.4)

The second integral of (
3.4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\phi_{ts}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}e^{izx}F_{X}(dx)\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{iz(u+x)}\nu_{ts}(du)F_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izy}\nu_{ts}(du)F_{X}(d(y-u))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izx}\nu_{ts}(du)F_{X}(d(x-u))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uF_{X}(d(x-u))\nu_{ts}(du).

(3.5)

Substituting (
3.5
) in (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}F_{X}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uF_{X}(d(x-u))\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\left(xF_{X}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uF_{X}(d(x-u))\nu_{ts}(du)\right)

(3.6)

On applying Fourier transform to (
3.6
), multiplying with
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),
and integrating over
\mathbb{R},
we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\left(xF_{X}(dx)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uF_{X}(d(x-u))\nu_{ts}(du)\right)=0.

(3.7)

The second integral of (
3.7
) can be seen as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(x)F_{X}(d(x-u))\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(y+u)F_{X}(dy)\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(x+u)F_{X}(dx)\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X+u)\nu_{ts}(du)\right).

(3.8)

Substituting (
3.8
) in (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Xf(X)-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X+u)\nu_{ts}(du)\right)=0,

which proves (
3.1
).

Assume conversely, (
3.1
) holds for
\nu_{ts}
defined in (
2.2
). For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, let
f(x)=e^{isx}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then (
3.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Xe^{isX}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{is(X+u)}u\nu(du)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}e^{isX}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu(du).

Setting
\phi_{ts}(s)=\mathbb{E}e^{isX}
, then

\phi_{ts}^{\prime}(s)=i\phi_{ts}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu(du).

(3.9)

Integrating out the real and imaginary parts of (
3.9
) leads, for any
z\geq 0
, to

\displaystyle\phi_{ts}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\int_{0}^{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu(du)ds\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{isu}dsu\nu(du)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu(du)\right).

A similar computation for
z\leq 0
completes the derivation of the cf.
∎

We now have the following Corollary for
\alpha
-stable distributions.

Corollary 3.2
.

A rv
X\sim S(m_{1},m_{2},\alpha)
, if and only if

\mathbb{E}\left(Xf(X)-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X+u)\nu_{\alpha}(du)\right)=0,~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

(3.10)

where
\nu_{\alpha}
is the associated Lévy measure of an
\alpha
-stable distribution, given in (
2.4
).

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\alpha
in Theorem
3.1
. Next, taking limits as
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\downarrow 0
in (
3.1
), and then applying the dominated convergence theorem, and noting that
\nu_{ts}\to\nu_{\alpha},
we get (
3.10
).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

(i) Note that we derive the characterizing (Stein) identity (
3.1
) for TSD using the Lévy-Khinchine representation of the cf. Also, observe that several classes of distributions such as variance-gamma, bilateral-gamma, CGMY, and KoBol can be viewed as TSD. Stein identities for these classes of distributions can be easily obtained using (
3.1
).

Recently Arras and Houdré [
[
1
]
, Theorem 3.1 and Section 5] obtained a Stein identity for infinitely divisible distributions with first finite moment via the covariance representation given in
[
16
]
. Note that TSD is a subclass of IDD and TSD has finite mean. Hence, Stein identity for TSD can also be derived using the approach given in
[
1
]
.

3.1.1.
A non-zero bias distribution

In the Stein’s method literature, the zero bias distribution is a powerful tool to obtain bounds, which has been used in several situation. It has been used in conjunction with coupling techniques to produce quantitative results for normal and product normal approximations, see, e.g.,
[
12
]
. The zero bias distribution due to Goldstein and Reinert
[
15
]
is as follows.

Definition 3.4
.

Let X be a rv with
\mathbb{E}(X)=0
, and
Var(X)=\sigma^{2}<\infty
. We say that
X^{*}
has
X
-zero bias distribution if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Xf(X)]=\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[f^{\prime}(X^{*})],

(3.11)

for any differentiable function
f
with
\mathbb{E}(Xf(X))<\infty
.

In the following lemma, we prove existence of a non-zero (extended) bias distribution (see
[
1
, Remark 3.9 (ii)]
) associated with TSD. Before stating our result, let us define

\displaystyle\eta^{+}(u)=\int_{u}^{\infty}y\nu_{ts}(dy),~u>0,\text{ and }\eta^{-}(u)=\int_{-\infty}^{u}(-y)\nu_{ts}(dy),~u<0,

(3.12)

where
\nu_{ts}
is the Lévy measure of the TSD (see (
2.2
)). Let

\eta(u):=\eta^{+}(u)+\eta^{-}(u).

Also let
Y
be a random variable with the density

\displaystyle f_{1}(u)=\frac{\eta(u)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{2}\nu_{ts}(dy)}=\frac{\eta(u)}{Var(X)},~u\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.13)

Then, for
n\geq 1
, the
n
th moment of
Y
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Var(X)}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{n}\eta(u)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n+1)Var(X)}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{n+2}\nu_{ts}(u)du

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{n+2}(X)}{(n+1)C_{2}(X)}.

(3.14)

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
and
Y
(independent of
X
) has the density given in (
3.13
). Then

\text{Cov}(X,f(X))=Var(X)\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(X+Y)\right),

(3.15)

where
g
is an absolutely continuous function with
\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(X+Y)\right)<\infty.

Proof.

Using (
2.8
), for the case
n=1
, in (
3.1
), and rearranging the terms, we get

\text{Cov}(X,f(X))=\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(f(X+u)-f(X))\nu_{ts}(du).

Now

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(X,f(X))=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(f(X+u)-f(X))\nu_{ts}(du)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(X+v)\int_{v}^{\infty}u\nu_{ts}(du)dv

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}f^{\prime}(X+v)\int_{-\infty}^{v}(-u)\nu_{ts}(du)dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(X+v)\left(\eta^{+}(v)\mathbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(v)+\eta^{-}(v)\mathbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(v)\right)dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{ts}(du)\right)\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(X+v)f_{1}(v)dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{ts}(du)\right)\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(X+Y)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Var(X)\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}(X+Y)\right).

This proves the result.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

Note that the covariance identity in (
3.15
) coincides with the one given in
[
1
, Proposition 3.8]
. However, the usefulness of the identity is shown in deriving the error bound of the limiting distributions of TSD (see the proof of Theorem
4.5
).

3.2.
A Stein equation for tempered stable distributions

In this section, we first derive a Stein equation for TSD and then solve it via the semigroup approach. From Proposition
3.1
, for any
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f(x):=-xf(x)+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(x+u)\nu_{ts}(du)

(3.16)

is a Stein operator for TSD. Hence, a Stein equation for
X\sim
TSD
(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},

\lambda_{2})
is given by

\mathcal{A}f(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}(h(X)),

(3.17)

where
h\in\mathcal{H},
a class of test functions. The semigroup approach for solving the Stein equation (
3.17
) is developed by Barbour
[
2
]
, and Arras and Houdré
[
1
]
generalized it for infinitely divisible distributions with the finite first moment. Let
\mathcal{F}=\bar{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{{R}})
henceforth denote the closure of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}})
. Consider the following family of operators
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, as

P_{t}(f)(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(z)e^{izxe^{-t}}\frac{\phi_{ts}(z)}{\phi_{ts}(e^{-t}z)}dz,~~f\in\mathcal{F},

(3.18)

where
\hat{f}
is the FT of
f
, and
\phi_{ts}
is the cf of TSD given in (
2.1
). It is well known that (see
[
25
]
), one can define a cf, for all
z\in\mathbb{R},
and
t\geq 0,
by

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(z):=\frac{\phi_{ts}(z)}{\phi_{ts}(e^{-t}z)}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izu}F_{X_{(t)}}(du),

(3.19)

where
F_{X_{(t)}}
is the CDF of
X_{(t)}
. The property given in (
3.19
) is also known as self-decomposability, see
[
25
]
. Using this property, we get

\displaystyle P_{t}(f)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(z)e^{izxe^{-t}}e^{izu}F_{X_{(t)}}(du)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(z)e^{iz(u+xe^{-t})}F_{X_{(t)}}(du)dz

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(u+xe^{-t})F_{X_{(t)}}(du),

(3.20)

where the last step follows by applying inverse FT.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a family of operators given in (
3.18
). Then

(i)

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
\mathbb{C}_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)

Its generator
T
is given by

T(f)(x)=-xf^{\prime}(x)+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf^{\prime}(x+u)\nu_{ts}(du),~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}).

(3.21)

Following the steps similar to Proposition 3.8 and Lemma 3.10 of
[
29
]
, the proof follows.

Next, we provide a solution to Stein equation (
3.17
).

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
and
h\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
. Then the function
f_{h}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{h}(x):=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dx}P_{t}h(x)dt,

(3.22)

solves (
3.17
).

Proof.

Let

g_{h}(x)=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(P_{t}(h)(x)-\mathbb{E}h(X)\bigg)dt.

Then
g_{h}^{\prime}(x)=f_{h}(x).
Also from (
3.21
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=-xf_{h}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf_{h}(x+u)\nu_{bg}(du)

\displaystyle=Tg_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}TP_{t}(h)(x)dt

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}P_{t}h(x)dt\text{ (see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{nourdin}{}{}, p.68]})}

\displaystyle=P_{0}h(x)-P_{\infty}h(x)

\displaystyle=h(x)-\mathbb{E}h(X)~(\text{by Proposition \ref{PP2:proSem}}).

Hence,
f_{h}
is the solution to (
3.17
). ∎

3.3.
Properties of the solution of the Stein equation

The next step is to estimate the properties of
f_{h}
. In the following theorem, we establish estimates of
f_{h}
, which play a crucial role in the TSD approximation problems. Gaunt
[
10
,
11
]
and D
\ddot{o}
bler
et al.

[
9
]
propose various methods for bounding the solution to the Stein equations that allow them to derive properties of the solution to the Stein equation, in particular for a sub-family of TSD, namely the variance-gamma.

Lemma 3.9
.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
, let
f_{h}
be defined in (
3.22
). Then

(i)

for
r=0,1,2,\ldots,

\displaystyle\|f_{h}^{(r)}\|\leq\frac{1}{r+1}\|h^{(r+1)}\|.

(3.23)

(ii)

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}_{h}(x)-f^{\prime}_{h}(y)\|\leq\frac{\|h^{(3)}\|}{3}\left|x-y\right|.

(3.24)

Proof.

(i) For
h\in\mathcal{H}_{r+1}
,

\displaystyle\|f_{h}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dx}P_{t}h(x)dt\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{(1)}(xe^{-t}+y)F_{X_{(t)}}(dy)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{(1)}\|\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}dt\right|

\displaystyle=\|h^{(1)}\|.

It can be easily seen that
f_{h}
is
r
-times differentiable. Let
r=1
, then

\displaystyle\|f_{h}^{(1)}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{(2)}(xe^{-t}+y)F_{X_{(t)}}(dy)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{(2)}\|\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}dt\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\|h^{(2)}\|}{2}.

Also, by induction, we get

\|f_{h}^{(r)}\|\leq\frac{1}{r+1}\|h^{(r+1)}\|,~r=0,1,2,\ldots.

(ii) For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}_{3}
,

\displaystyle\left|f^{\prime}_{h}(x)-f^{\prime}_{h}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|h^{(2)}(xe^{-t}+z)-h^{(3)}(ye^{-t}+z)\right|F_{X_{(t)}}(dz)dt

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|h^{(3)}\|\left|x-y\right|e^{-t}F_{X_{(t)}}(dz)dt

\displaystyle=\|h^{(3)}\|\left|x-y\right|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-3t}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\|h^{(3)}\|}{3}\left|x-y\right|.

This proves the result.
∎

4.
Bounds for tempered stable approximation

In this section, we present bounds for the tempered stable approximations to various probability distributions. First, we obtain, for the Kolmogorov distance
d_{K}
, the error bounds for a sequence of CPD that converges to a TSD.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
and
X_{n},

n\geq 1
be a compound Poisson rvs with cf

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z):=\exp\bigg(n\left(\phi_{ts}^{\frac{1}{n}}(z)-1\right)\bigg),~z\in\mathbb{{R}},

(4.1)

where
\phi_{ts}(t)
is given in (
2.1
). Then

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{n},X)\leq c\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{2}|C_{j}(X)|\bigg)^{\frac{2}{5}}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{5}},

(4.2)

where
c>0
is independent of
n
and
C_{j}
denotes the
j
th cumulant of
X
.

Proof.

Let
b=\int_{-1}^{1}u\nu_{ts}(du)
, where
\nu_{ts}
is defined in (
2.2
). Then by Equation (2.6) of
[
1
]
,

\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})\overset{d}{=}ID(b,0,\nu_{ts}).

That is,
\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
is an infinitely divisible distribution with the triplet
b,0
and
\nu_{ts}
. Note that TSD are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lévy measure with a bounded density and
\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}<\infty
(see
[
20
, Section 7]
). Recall from Proposition 4.11 of
[
1
]
that, if
X\sim ID(b,0,\nu)
with cf
\phi_{X}
(say), and
X_{n},

n\geq 1
are compound Poisson rvs each of with cf as (
4.1
), then

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{n},X)

\displaystyle\leq c\bigg(|\mathbb{E}(X)|+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu(du)\bigg)^{\frac{2}{p+4}}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p+4}},

(4.3)

where
|\phi_{X}(z)|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}\frac{ds}{|\phi_{X}(s)|}\leq c_{0}|z|^{p},

p\geq 1
.

Now observe that

\displaystyle|\phi_{ts}(z)|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}\frac{ds}{|\phi_{ts}(s)|}

\displaystyle\leq\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}\frac{ds}{|\mathbb{E}(\cos sX)+i\mathbb{E}(\sin sX)|}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}\frac{|\mathbb{E}(e^{\left(-isX\right)})|}{\mathbb{E}^{2}(\cos sX)+\mathbb{E}^{2}(\sin sX)}ds

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}|\mathbb{E}(e^{\left(-isX\right)})|ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\bigg(\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}^{2}(\cos sX)+\mathbb{E}^{2}(\sin sX)}<c_{0}\text{, say}\bigg)

\displaystyle=c_{0}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}|\mathbb{E}(\cos sX-i\sin sX)|ds

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}ds

\displaystyle=c_{0}|z|.

Hence by (
4.3
), for
p=1
, we get

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{n},X)

\displaystyle\leq c\bigg(|\mathbb{E}(X)|+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{ts}(du)\bigg)^{\frac{2}{5}}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{5}}

\displaystyle=c\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{2}|C_{j}(X)|\bigg)^{\frac{2}{5}}\bigg(\frac{1}{n}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{5}},

\text{ since }\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{ts}(du)=C_{2}(X).
This proves the result.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Note that if
n\to\infty
,
d_{K}(X_{n},X)=0
, as expected, and TSD is the limit of CPD.

Our next result yields an error bound for tempered stable approximation to
\alpha
-stable distributions.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
X\sim
TSD
(m_{1},\alpha,\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha,\lambda_{2})
and
X_{\alpha}\sim S(m_{1},m_{2},\alpha)
. Then

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{\alpha},X)\leq C_{1}\lambda_{1}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}+C_{2}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}},

(4.4)

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are independent of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.

Proof.

For
h\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
, from (
3.17
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(X_{\alpha})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X_{\alpha})]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{A}f(X_{\alpha})-\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha})\right],

(4.5)

since

\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha}))=\mathbb{E}\left(-X_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha})+\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\nu_{\alpha}(du)\right)=0,~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}}),

where
\nu_{\alpha}
is the Lévy measure given in (
2.4
) (see (
3.10
)).

Then, from (
4.5
), we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}[h(X_{\alpha})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X)]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(-X_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\nu_{ts}(du)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad-\bigg(-X_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\nu_{\alpha}(du)\bigg)\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\nu_{ts}(du)-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\nu_{\alpha}(du)\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(m_{1}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\frac{e^{-\lambda_{1}u}}{u^{1+\alpha}}du

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+m_{2}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\frac{e^{-\lambda_{2}|u|}}{|u|^{1+\alpha}}du\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\bigg(m_{1}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\frac{du}{u^{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+m_{2}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)\frac{du}{|u|^{1+\alpha}}\bigg)\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[m_{1}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(e^{-\lambda_{1}u}-1)}{u^{1+\alpha}}uf(X_{\alpha}+u)du

\displaystyle\quad\quad-m_{2}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(e^{-\lambda_{2}u}-1)}{u^{1+\alpha}}uf(X_{\alpha}-u)du\bigg]\bigg|.

(4.6)

Now applying triangle and Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities in (
4.6
), we get

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{\alpha},X)

\displaystyle\leq m_{1}\bigg\{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{(e^{-\lambda_{1}u}-1)}{u^{1+\alpha}}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}+u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+m_{2}\bigg\{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{(e^{-\lambda_{2}u}-1)}{u^{1+\alpha}}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg\}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}-u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}m_{1}M^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha)\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}+u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\lambda_{2}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}m_{2}M^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha)\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}-u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(4.7)

where
M(\alpha)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\frac{(e^{-u}-1)}{u^{1+\alpha}}\bigg)^{2}du<\infty
(see
[
14
, p.169]
). Also,
\mathbb{E}(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}+u)du)^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
\mathbb{E}(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}-u)du)^{\frac{1}{2}}
are finite, since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}}).
Now setting

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=m_{1}M^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha)\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}+u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

\displaystyle\text{and }C_{2}

\displaystyle=m_{2}M^{\frac{1}{2}}(\alpha)\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}f^{2}(X_{\alpha}-u)du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty,

in (
4.7
), we get

\displaystyle d_{K}(X_{\alpha},X)\leq C_{1}\lambda_{1}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}+C_{2}\lambda_{2}^{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}},

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are independent of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
. This proves the result. ∎

Next, we state a result that gives the limiting distribution of a sequence of tempered stable random variables.

Lemma 4.4
.

(
[
20
, Proposition 3.1]
)
Let
m_{1},m_{2},m_{i,n},\lambda_{i,n}\in(0,\infty)
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{i,n}\in[0,1)
, for
i=1,2
. Also, let
X_{n}\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1,n},\alpha_{1,n},\lambda_{1,n},m_{2,n},\alpha_{2,n},\lambda_{2,n})
and
X\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})
. If

(m_{1,n},\alpha_{1,n},\lambda_{1,n},m_{2,n},\alpha_{2,n},\lambda_{2,n})\to(m_{1},\alpha_{1},\lambda_{1},m_{2},

\alpha_{2},\lambda_{2})\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then
X_{n}\overset{L}{\to}X.

The following theorem gives the error in the approximation of
X_{n}
to
X
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
X_{n}
and
X
be defined as in Lemma
4.4
. Then

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}(X_{n},X)

\displaystyle\leq\left|C_{1}(X_{n})-C_{1}(X)\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|C_{2}(X_{n})-C_{2}(X)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{6}C_{2}(X)\left|\frac{|C_{3}(X_{n})|}{C_{2}(X_{n})}-\frac{|C_{3}(X)|}{C_{2}(X)}\right|,

(4.8)

where
C_{j}(X),

j=1,2,3
, denotes the
j
-th cumulant of
X
and
d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}
is defined in (
2.14
).

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathcal{H}_{3}
and
f
be the solution to the Stein equation (
3.16
). Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(X_{n})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}f(X_{n})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[-X_{n}f(X_{n})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{n}+u)\nu_{ts}(du)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(-X_{n}+C_{1}(X)\bigg)f(X_{n})+C_{2}(X)f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y)\bigg],

(4.9)

where the last equality follows by (
3.15
), and
Y
has the density given in (
3.13
).

Since
X_{n}\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1,n},\alpha_{1,n},\lambda_{1,n},m_{2,n},\alpha_{2,n},\lambda_{2,n}),
then by Proposition
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[-X_{n}f(X_{n})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(X_{n}+u)\nu_{ts}^{n}(du)\bigg]=0,

(4.10)

where
\nu_{ts}^{n}
is the Lévy measure, given by

\nu_{ts}^{n}(du)=\left(\frac{m_{1,n}}{u^{1+\alpha_{1,n}}}e^{-\lambda_{1,n}u}\mathbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(u)+\frac{m_{2,n}}{|u|^{1+\alpha_{2,n}}}e^{-\lambda_{2,n}|u|}\mathbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\right)du.

Also, by Lemma
3.5
, the identity in (
4.10
) can be seen as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg(-X_{n}+C_{1}(X_{n})\bigg)f(X_{n})+C_{2}(X_{n})f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y_{n})\bigg]=0,

(4.11)

where
Y_{n}
has the density

\displaystyle f_{n}(u)=\frac{[\int_{u}^{\infty}y\nu_{ts}^{n}(dy)]\textbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(u)-[\int_{-\infty}^{u}y\nu_{ts}^{n}(dy)]\textbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)}{C_{2}(X_{n})},~u\in\mathbb{{R}}.

(4.12)

Using (
4.11
) in (
4.9
), we get

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}[h(X_{n})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X)]\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\bigg((-X_{n}+C_{1}(X))f(X_{n})+C_{2}(X)f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y)\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\bigg((-X_{n}+C_{1}(X_{n}))f(X_{n})+C_{2}(X_{n})f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y_{n})\bigg)\bigg]\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\left|C_{1}(X_{n})-C_{1}(X)\right|\|f\|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}\left|C_{2}(X_{n})f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y_{n})-C_{2}(X)f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|C_{1}(X_{n})-C_{1}(X)\right|\|f\|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}\bigg|(C_{2}(X_{n})-C_{2}(X))f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y_{n})\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C_{2}(X)\mathbb{E}\bigg|f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y_{n})-f^{\prime}(X_{n}+Y)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{(1)}\||C_{1}(X_{n})-C_{1}(X)|+\frac{\|h^{(2)}\|}{2}|C_{2}(X_{n})-C_{2}(X)|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+C_{2}(X)\frac{\|h^{(3)}\|}{3}\bigg|\mathbb{E}|Y_{n}|-\mathbb{E}|Y|\bigg|,

(4.13)

where the last inequality follows by applying the estimates given in Lemma
3.9
. From (
4.12
) and (
3.13
), it can be verified that (see (
3.14
))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|Y_{n}|=\frac{|C_{3}(X_{n})|}{2C_{2}(X_{n})}\text{ and }\mathbb{E}|Y|=\frac{|C_{3}(X)|}{2C_{2}(X)}.

(4.14)

Using (
4.14
) in (
4.13
), we get our desired result. ∎

Remark 4.6
.

Next, we discuss two examples. Our first example yields the error in approximating a TSD by a normal distribution.

Example 4.7

(Normal approximation to a TSD)
.

Let
X_{n}\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1,n},\alpha_{1,n},\lambda_{1,n},

m_{2,n},\alpha_{2,n},\lambda_{2,n})
,
X_{m}\sim\text{SVGD}(m,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda)
and
X_{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2})
. Recall from Section
2.1
that,

\text{SVGD}(m,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda)\overset{d}{=}\text{TSD}(m,0,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda,m,0,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda)
. Then, the cf of
\text{SVGD}(m,\sqrt{2m}/\lambda)
is

\displaystyle\phi_{sv}(z)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{z^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2m}\bigg)^{-m},~z\in\mathbb{{R}},

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{sv}(du)\bigg),

(4.16)

where the Lévy measure
\nu_{sv}
is

\displaystyle\nu_{sv}(du)=\bigg(\frac{m}{u}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\lambda}u}\textbf{I}_{(0,\infty)}(u)+\frac{m}{|u|}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\lambda}|u|}\textbf{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\bigg).

Note from (
4.15
) that,

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi_{sv}(z)=e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}z^{2}}{2}}.

That is,
X_{m}\overset{L}{\to}X_{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\lambda^{2}),
as
m\to\infty.
Also, it follows
[
32
, Theorem 7.12]
that, if
X_{m}\overset{L}{\to}X_{\lambda},
as
m\to\infty
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}(X_{n},X_{\lambda})=\lim_{m\to\infty}d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}(X_{n},X_{m}).

(4.17)

Applying Theorem
4.5
to
X=X_{m}
, and taking limit as
m\to\infty
, we get from (
4.17
)

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}(X_{n},X_{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\bigg(\left|C_{1}(X_{n})-C_{1}(X_{m})\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|C_{2}(X_{n})-C_{2}(X_{m})\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{6}C_{2}(X_{m})\left|\frac{|C_{3}(X_{n})|}{C_{2}(X_{n})}-\frac{|C_{3}(X_{m})|}{C_{2}(X_{m})}\right|\bigg)

\displaystyle=|C_{1}(X_{n})|+\frac{1}{2}|C_{2}(X_{n})-\lambda^{2}|+\frac{1}{6}\lambda^{2}\frac{|C_{3}(X_{n})|}{C_{2}(X_{n})},

(4.18)

which gives the error in the approximation between
X_{n}
and
X_{\lambda}
. Note that

\displaystyle C_{1}(X_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(X_{n})=\Gamma(1-\alpha_{1,n})\frac{m_{1,n}}{\lambda_{1,n}^{1-\alpha_{1,n}}}-\Gamma(1-\alpha_{2,n})\frac{m_{2,n}}{\lambda_{2,n}^{1-\alpha_{2,n}}},

\displaystyle C_{2}(X_{n})

\displaystyle=Var(X_{n})=\Gamma(2-\alpha_{1,n})\frac{m_{1,n}}{\lambda_{1,n}^{2-\alpha_{1,n}}}+\Gamma(2-\alpha_{2,n})\frac{m_{2,n}}{\lambda_{2,n}^{1-\alpha_{2,n}}},

\displaystyle\text{and }C_{3}(X_{n})

\displaystyle=\Gamma(3-\alpha_{1,n})\frac{m_{1,n}}{\lambda_{1,n}^{3-\alpha_{1,n}}}-\Gamma(3-\alpha_{2,n})\frac{m_{2,n}}{\lambda_{2,n}^{3-\alpha_{2,n}}}.

When
C_{j}(X_{n})\to 0
, for
j=1,3
and
C_{2}(X_{n})\to\lambda^{2}
, from (
4.18

X_{n}\overset{L}{\to}X_{\lambda}
, as

Example 4.8

(Variance-gamma approximation to a TSD)
.

Let
X_{n}\sim\text{TSD}(m_{1,n},

\alpha_{1,n},\lambda_{1,n},m_{2,n},\alpha_{2,n},\lambda_{2,n})
and
X_{v}\sim
VGD
(m,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. Then

\displaystyle C_{1}(X_{v})

\displaystyle=m\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\bigg),

\displaystyle C_{2}(X_{v})

\displaystyle=m\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{2}}\bigg),

\displaystyle\text{and }C_{3}(X_{v})

\displaystyle=2m\bigg(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{3}}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}^{3}}\bigg).

Now applying Theorem
4.5
to
X=X_{v}
, we get

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H}_{3}}(X_{n},X_{v})

\displaystyle\leq\left|C_{1}(X_{n})-\frac{m(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|C_{2}(X_{n})-\frac{m(\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2})}{\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{6}m\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}}{\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{2}^{2}}\left|\frac{|C_{3}(X_{n})|}{C_{2}(X_{n})}-\frac{2|\lambda_{2}^{3}-\lambda_{1}^{3}|}{\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}(\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2})}\right|,

which gives the error in the approximation between
X_{n}
and
X_{v}
. When
C_{j}(X_{n})\to C_{j}(X_{v})
, for
j=1,2,3,
we have
X_{n}\overset{L}{\to}X_{v}
, as
n\to\infty
.
