Training Zero-Shot Generalizable End-to-End Task-Oriented Dialog System Without Turn-level Dialog Annotations
Adib Mosharrof, A.B. Siddique

Abstract
Task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems enable users to achieve their goals through natural language interactions. Traditionally, these systems have relied on turn-level manually annotated metadata, such as dialogue states and policy annotations, which are expensive, time-consuming, and often inconsistent or error-prone. This dependence limits the potential to leverage vast amounts of readily available conversational data for training TOD systems. Additionally, a critical challenge in TOD system design is determining when and how to access and integrate information from external sources . Current approaches typically expect this information to be provided alongside the dialogue context, rather than learning to identify and retrieve it autonomously. While pre-trained large language models (LLMs) have been used to develop TOD systems, their potential to train such systems without laborious annotations remains largely unexplored . This work employs multi-task instruction fine-tuning to create more efficient and scalable TOD systems that can effectively leverage natural language conversational data without manual annotations, while autonomously managing external information retrieval. Our extensive experimental evaluations, using three diverse TOD datasets and three LLMs of varying sizes, demonstrate that our approach can generalize to new, unseen domains. Notably, our approach outperforms both state-of-the-art models trained on annotated data and billion-scale parameter off-the-shelf ChatGPT models.

Introduction

This work leverages natural language conversational data alone, eliminating the need for extensive manual annotations required by existing works.

Task-oriented dialogue (TOD) systems
Zhang et al. (2020a)
enable users to accomplish a wide range of goals through natural language interactions.
These systems power virtual assistants, customer service chatbots, and various other applications that facilitate tasks such as booking reservations, scheduling appointments, or retrieving information
Williams et al. (2016)
;
Zhang et al. (2019)
. Traditionally, the development of TOD systems has relied heavily on turn-wise manually annotated metadata, including dialogue states and policy annotations
Zhang et al. (2020b)
. This approach, while effective, presents significant challenges to scaling TOD systems to new domains, since this approach cannot exploit vast amounts of readily available raw conversational data for training.
That is, the process of creating these annotations is laborious and costly.
Moreover, the quality of these annotations can be inconsistent and prone to errors
Eric et al. (2020)
;
Zang et al. (2020)
;
Han et al. (2021)
;
Budzianowski and Vulic (2019)
.

Another critical challenge in the design of TOD systems is the effective integration of external information sources.
Many real-world tasks require the system to access and incorporate information beyond the immediate dialogue context, such as querying databases and accessing knowledge bases.
Current approaches often sidestep this challenge by assuming that the necessary external information is provided alongside the dialogue context.
However, this assumption limits the autonomy and adaptability of these systems, as they cannot independently learn when external information is needed and how to retrieve it effectively from which source.

Recent advancements in natural language processing have seen the rise of pre-trained large language models (LLMs)
Vaswani et al. (2017)
;
Devlin et al. (2019)
;
Radford et al. (2019)
, which have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across various language tasks.
While these models have been applied to develop TOD systems
Hosseini-Asl et al. (2020)
;
Yang et al. (2020)
;
Mosharrof et al. (2023b)
;
Budzianowski et al. (2018)
, their potential to train such systems without the need for extensive manual annotations remains largely unexplored.
This untapped potential presents an opportunity to enable the development of end-to-end (E2E) zero-shot generalizable TOD systems.

In this work, we propose to move away from manually annotated turn-wise data and present a novel
N
atural
L
anguage

;

;
Mishra et al. (2021)
and uses only the dialog history and domain schema to create a Zero Shot Generalizable TOD system.
Additionally, our approach integrates autonomous external information retrieval.
Unlike existing systems that require external information to be provided explicitly,
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
learns to identify when additional information is needed and how to retrieve it effectively.
Our approach not only addresses the labor-intensive nature of traditional TOD system development but also opens up new possibilities for utilizing the wealth of unannotated dialogue data available in various domains.

To rigorously evaluate our approach, we conducted extensive experiments using three diverse TOD datasets: SGD
Rastogi et al. (2019)
, KETOD

, and BiToD

.
Our experiments employed three LLMs of varying sizes: GPT-2 Medium (355M parameters)

, and OPT-6.7B (6.7B parameters)

.
For smaller models, we used full-model training, while for larger models, we applied the Low-Rank Adaptation (LORA)

In addition to traditional result reporting metrics (e.g., BLEU, GLEU) for overall response generation, this work also examines performance at the sub-task level: request, inform, and API call capabilities (i.e., the accuracy of method names, parameters, parameter values, and exact full API calls), which provides a fine-grained assessment of the models’ proficiency to fulfill users’ intent while autonomously interacting with external systems.

Our results demonstrate that the proposed approach enables TOD systems to generalize effectively to new, unseen domains.
Notably, our approach outperforms state-of-the-art (SOTA) models that were trained on manually annotated data.
This finding challenges the conventional wisdom that high-quality annotations are necessary for achieving top performance in TOD tasks.
Furthermore, our approach even surpasses the performance of billion-scale parameter off-the-shelf models (e.g., GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-turbo
OpenAI (2023)
) in diverse TOD settings, despite using significantly smaller models.

Most works in all categories require turn-level annotated data to train TOD models, which can be a significant limitation to scale in real world where such data may be scarce or costly.
Moreover, most systems assume that knowledge from external sources will be provided in the dialog context.

Our work, in contrast, focuses on training TOD models using only readily available natural language interactions, while leveraging domain schemas to enable scalability across unseen domains.

3
Methodology

3.1
Problem Formulation

We formulate TOD response generation as a conditional sequence generation task.
We use dialog history and domain schema to facilitate the creation of a zero-shot TOD model that can accommodate interactions from both seen and unseen domains.

Formally, in a multi-domain TOD setting, let
D=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\}
be a set of domains, where each
d_{i}\in D
represents a distinct domain of interest.
For each domain
d_{i}
, there exists a set of intents
I_{d_{i}}
such that
I_{d_{i}}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}I_{d_{j}}
is the set of possible intents associated with the domain
d_{i}
.
Each intent
i_{j}\in I_{d_{i}}
represents a specific user intent in domain
d_{i}
.
For each intent
i_{j}\in I_{d_{i}}
there exists a set of slots
S_{i_{j}}
such that

S_{i_{j}}\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{m}S_{i_{k}}

is the set of possible slots associated with the intent
i_{j}
, where each slot
s_{k}\in S_{i_{j}}
represents a slot relevant to the intent
i_{j}
in the domain
d_{i}
.
So, a domain
d_{i}
can be represented as follows:

d_{i}=\left(I_{d_{i}},\{(i_{j},S_{i_{j}})\mid i_{j}\in I_{d_{i}}\}\right)
.
For example, in the restaurant domain, the intent could be “Book Table” with relevant slots such as “number_of_people”, “date”, and “restaurant_name”.

Similarly, assume we have a dataset of
dialogs between a user and a system.
Each dialog
\mathcal{T}_{i}
consists of up to
T
turns, represented as a sequence:

\mathcal{T}_{i}=\left(\{(u_{t},r_{t})\mid t=1,2,\ldots,T\}\right)
, where
u_{t}
and
r_{t}
represent the user’s and system’s utterance at turn
t
.
Thus, the dialog history
H_{t}
at turn
t
consists of all the user and system utterances up user’s utterance at turn
t
, denoted as:

H_{t}=\{(u_{1},r_{1}),\dots,(u_{t-1},r_{t-1}),u_{t}\}
.
It is important to note that a dialog session may span up to
m
domains:
\mathcal{T}_{i}\sim\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}\subseteq D
, where
m\leq n
.
At turn
t
, the system utterance
r_{t}
can be generated by conditioning on the relevant domain schemas
\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}d_{j}
and dialog history
H_{t}
.

3.2
Optimization of
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}

An autoregressive model (e.g., GPT-2
Radford et al. (2019)
) generates text by predicting the next token given the preceding context.
For a given sequence of tokens
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t-1})
, the model predicts the probability distribution for the next token
x_{t}
as follows:

p(x_{t}\mid x_{1:t-1};\theta)=f_{\theta}(x_{1:t-1})
, where
f_{\theta}
outputs a probability distribution over model’s vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
.
The next token
x_{t}
is sampled from the predicted probability distribution
p(x_{t}\mid x_{1:t-1};\theta)
.
This formulation readily extends to response generation for TOD:

r_{t}\sim p(r_{t}\mid H_{t};\theta)
,
where the system response
r_{t}
for turn
t
is generated token-by-token by an iterative sampling.

In our formulation of
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
, we introduce an additional conditioning variable domain schema
d_{j}\forall_{j}
to encourage zero-shot generalization to unseen domains.
Then, the above formulation can be extended to optimize the model for the overall multi-turn dialog of
T
turns using a dataset of
N
dialogs with the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{NL-TOD}=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(r_{t}\mid H_{t},d_{j};\theta)
.
In practice, dialog history
H_{t}
only consists of the last
k
turns, where
k\leq t
, depending on the model’s context length.

3.3
Multi-task Instruction Fine-tuning

The output of our system can be broadly categorized into two types: natural language responses to the user and API calls to external sources.
Our formulation trains the model for both outputs in an E2E fashion using multi-task instruction fine-tuning.
It is important to note that we do not introduce any special tokens to tell the model which type of response to generate, instead the model learns autonomously when to generate API calls and when to generate a natural language response for the user.
In the following, we provide a brief overview of both types of responses.

3.3.1
Response Generation

The response generation is a natural language response for the user, which can be broken down into multiple sub-categories: request, inform, and general interaction.

Request.

To fulfill user intents, the system may need additional information that the user has not yet provided.
For example, if a user asks to book a flight without specifying the date, the system would ask, “What date would you like to travel?”.

Inform.

The user may also need additional information to make informed decisions, which the system must provide.
This involves retrieving and presenting requested details to the user.
For example, if a user asks, “Does the restaurant have live music?” the system must query the database and provide the requested information to the user.

General Interaction.

The system also needs to engage in general interactions.
This involves managing the flow of the conversation, offering help or suggestions, confirming user inputs, and providing general information that assists the user in navigating through the task.

3.3.2
API Calls

A critical aspect of TOD systems is their ability to communicate with external sources to retrieve additional information needed to complete a task. This category of system output involves making accurate API calls.
Consider a dialog scenario in the travel domain, where a user asks for Flights to New York the next Friday at 10 AM.
Given the dialog context, the system must get a list of flights for recommendation, which requires generating an API call:

APICall(method = FindFlight, parameters = {destination=New York, date=next Friday, time=10AM, origin=CurrentLocation})
.
Using the information returned from the API call, the system can suggest flights to the user.
It is important to note that the API call has several components: method name, list of parameters, and values associated with parameters.

In multi-domain settings, a special case arises when transferring information between domains, where the same data is carried forward but under different parameter names.
For example, a parameter named
destination
in the travel domain might be referred to as
hotel_city
in the subsequent hotel booking domain.
An intelligent TOD model should autonomously perform this mapping.

3.4
Training Details

The dialog history and domain schema are passed through a structured template to form the inputs to the model.
This approach allows the model to generate both types of responses (i.e., natural language response, API call) efficiently by learning from instructions simultaneously.
The template is detailed in Figure
2
in Appendix
A
.

\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
leverages three pre-trained LLMs, namely GPT-2 Medium, Flan-T5 Large, and OPT-6.7B, and further refines them through multi-task instruction fine-tuning
Raffel et al. (2020)
;
Wei et al. (2022)
;
Mishra et al. (2021)
.
Several key optimization techniques were employed to achieve optimal training.
An early stopping mechanism on the evaluation loss was implemented to prevent overfitting with the patience value set to 3.
The training process included 500 warm-up steps, during which the learning rate was gradually increased to stabilize the training process and avoid sudden jumps in the loss function.
The AdamW
Loshchilov and Hutter (2017)
optimizer with a weight decay was used with a learning rate of 0.001.
For GPT-2 Medium, we employed full model training, while for Flan-T5 Large and OPT-6.7B, we employed the Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)
Hu et al. (2021)
and Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation (QLoRA)
Dettmers et al. (2023)
approach.
LoRA facilitates parameter-efficient fine-tuning by injecting trainable low-rank matrices into each layer of the transformer architecture and quantization uses 8-bit integer
Jacob et al. (2018)
as the data type to reduce the memory footprint.

Datasets

We use three diverse TOD datasets: Schema-Guided Dialog (SGD) dataset, Bilingual Task-Oriented Dialog (BiToD) dataset, and Knowledge-Enhanced Task-Oriented Dialog (KETOD) dataset.
We have selected these datasets as these datasets define a schema to describe the domain and the conversational data contains API calls to communicate with external resources. Table

shows detailed statistics about the datasets.

4.2
Evaluation Metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
, we separately assess individual sub-tasks and overall responses.
This approach can provide a more granular understanding of the system’s strengths and areas for improvement.

Response Generation.

We report BLEU-4
Papineni et al. (2002)
scores of overall system responses as well as request and inform sub-task as discussed in Section
3.3
.
We also present the GLEU
Wu et al. (2016)
metric in Appendix
B
.

API Calls.
We introduce a custom metric to assess different components of the API call.
The API format is:

\texttt{APICall}(\texttt{method}=method\_name,\texttt{parameters}=\{(s_{i},v_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\})

The parameters attribute is a list of slot name and slot value pairs, where
s_{i}
represents the slot name and
v_{i}
represents the value of that slot.
We use regular expressions to extract different parts of the API call to calculate the metrics.

•

Invoke Accuracy
measures whether the system can understand when to make an API call.

•

Method Accuracy
checks whether the appropriate method was used in the API call.

•

Param Name Accuracy
assesses whether all the parameter names used to construct the API call are correct.

•

Param Value Accuracy
evaluates whether each parameter value corresponding to a parameter name is correct. It is important to note that this metric will only be considered if the corresponding parameter name is correct.

•

Complete API Call Accuracy
metric checks whether the complete API call (i.e., all components) was generated correctly.

4.3
Evaluation Settings

To better understand the zero-shot generalization ability of
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
, we evaluate the system on dialogs grouped by domain categories.
This evaluation is structured into four domain categories: All, Seen, Unseen, and Mixed.

All Domains.
This category includes dialogs from all domains, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the system’s overall performance.

Seen Domains.
This category contains dialogs from domains that were present in the training data.
Evaluating seen domains demonstrates the supervised learning performance of the system.

Unseen Domains.
This category comprises dialogs from domains that were not included in the training data.
Evaluating unseen domains tests the zero-shot generalizability of the system, demonstrating its ability to handle new, unseen domains without additional training.

Mixed Domains:
This category includes dialogs that span multiple domains, with some domains being seen and others unseen.
This setting serves as an intermediary between supervised and zeo-shot settings.
It tests the system’s ability to manage dialogs where known and unknown domains are intermixed, reflecting more complex real-world scenarios.
We present results for mixed domains in Appendix
B
.

4.4
Baselines

We establish several strong baselines for comparison to evaluate the effectiveness of
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
.
Specifically, we compare
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
with OpenAI’s SOTA models and other SOTA approaches that use turn-level annotated dialog data.

OpenAI’s GPT Models.

To compare with OpenAI’s GPT models, we create a comprehensive prompt for the input dialog.
The prompt is detailed in Figure
3
in Appendix
A
.
The template includes the dialog history, domain schema, and detailed instructions on how to process the dialog, understand the domain schema, and task-specific instructions.
We perform comprehensive comparisons with GPT-3.5-turbo that uses this detailed prompt.
Moreover, we perform a limited-level comparison with the more expensive variant GPT-4-turbo.

SOTA approaches.
We also compare
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
against existing SOTA approaches.
However, it is important to note that current SOTA methods do not report results for the API call task. Therefore, for these comparisons, we focus solely on the response generation task.

•

Soloist
Peng et al. (2021)
introduced an E2E TOD system that employs a transformer-based autoregressive model that generates dialog responses grounded in user goals and real-world knowledge for task completion.

•

T5DST
Lin et al. (2021a)
introduced a slot description enhanced generative approach for zero-shot TOD.

•

FiD-TOD
Zhang et al. (2023)
introduced a cache that contains accessible domains, intents, slots, and APIs. A retrieval module extracts relevant information from the cache and feeds the generative model for E2E TOD generation.

•

ZS-TOD
Mosharrof et al. (2023b)
introduced a zero-shot generalizable E2E TOD model that incorporates domain schema and dialog annotations to generate dialog responses.

•

Combiner
Chen et al. (2022)
introduced a pipeline of a TOD model followed by a response generation model. The pipeline TOD model is built using the SimpleTOD model and generates the belief states and actions, which are fed into a GPT-2-based model for generating the system response.

•

SimpleTODPlus
Chen et al. (2022)
introduced a TOD model based on SimpleTOD.

•

MinTL
Lin et al. (2020)
introduced Levenshtein belief spans (Lev) that model the difference between the current and previous dialog state. A response is generated by conditioning on the dialog context and knowledge base results. The BiToD leaderboard provides implementation of MinTL using mbart, mt5.

6
Conclusion

This work introduces a novel zero-shot generalizable task-oriented dialog (TOD) system,
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
, which reduces reliance on manually annotated turn-level data. Such annotations are often costly, error-prone, and inconsistent. Our approach enables researchers and practitioners to utilize the vast amounts of available conversational data to train TOD systems, eliminating the need for extensive manual labeling.
We consider API calls a core task, as it is crucial for a TOD system to know when and how to interact with external data sources.
To demonstrate the possibility of training TOD systems effectively without turn-level annotations, we employed multi-task instruction fine-tuning and trained three LLMs of varying sizes.
To promote zero-shot generalization, we use domain schema as an additional conditioning variable.
In fine-tuning these models, we experimented with both full-model training and low-rank adaptation techniques.
We conducted extensive experiments using three diverse TOD datasets, analyzing overall response and API generation capabilities, as well as performance on sub-tasks.
Our results show that
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
outperforms OpenAI’s GPT models and existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches that rely on turn-level annotated dialogs.

7
Limitations

\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
has been developed by fine-tuning LLMs such as OPT-6.7B, Flan-T5 Large, and GPT-2 Medium.
These LLMs require significant computational resource requirements to train, particularly OPT-6.7B.
Training and inference with these models can be expensive, limiting their practicality for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Experimental results show that while models perform well on seen domains, their ability to generalize to unseen domains is still a challenge. So the system may struggle with user queries that deviate from the expected task structure.
The LLMs used in the system function as black boxes, making it challenging to interpret the reasoning behind their responses.
This lack of transparency hinders the ability to diagnose and correct erroneous outputs, which is crucial in TOD systems where accuracy is critical. Furthermore, the models may inherit biases present in the training data, leading to biased or unfair responses in certain scenarios.
Although efforts were made to mitigate this issue by fine-tuning using the dialog datasets, completely eliminating biases remains a challenging task. The reliance on pre-trained models introduces limitations related to the coverage of the pre-training data. If the pre-training data lacks specific domain knowledge, the TOD system may underperform in those domains.

The deployment of LLMs in TOD systems raises ethical and privacy concerns, particularly regarding the handling of sensitive user data. Ensuring that the system complies with privacy regulations and ethical standards is an ongoing challenge that requires continuous monitoring and updates.
Similar to other AI technologies, there is a scope for potential misuse of our system.
If
\mathsf{NL}\mbox{-}\mathsf{TOD}
is used with malicious intent or the model is fed inappropriate data, there is a risk of abuse.
We would strongly advise to take necessary precautions and appropriate usage policies.

Addressing the limitations outlined above is crucial for advancing the effectiveness and reliability of TOD systems.
While the usage of pre-trained LLMs offers significant advantages, these models are not without their challenges.
Increasing model interpretability, mitigating biases, and addressing ethical and societal concerns are essential steps toward creating more robust and responsible TOD systems.
