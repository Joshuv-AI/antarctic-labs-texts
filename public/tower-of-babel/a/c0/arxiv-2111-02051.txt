Abstract

Introduction

Let
G
be a simple connected graph.
Suppose
x
and
y
are any two vertices of
G
. The length of the shortest path connecting

x
and
y
in
G

is the natural way to define the distance between
x
and
y
.
This classical distance has certain limitations. For instance, consider two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
such that

(i)

V(G_{1})=V(G_{2})=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.

(ii)

i
and
j
are adjacent in both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.

(iii)

There is only path between
i
and
j
in
G_{1}
and there are multiple paths connecting
i
and
j

in
G_{2}
.

Then the shortest distance between
i
and
j
in both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is
one
.
However, since there are multiple paths connecting
i
and
j
in
G_{2}
, the communication between
i
and
j
in

G_{2}
is better than in
G_{1}
.
This significance is not reflected in the shortest distance.
Several applications require to overcome this limitation.
Instead of the classical distance, the so-called resistance distance
is used widely in many situations like in
electrical networks, chemistry and random walks: see
for example
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
.
If there are multiple paths between two vertices, then the resistance distance is less than
the shortest distance. The resistance matrix is now the matrix with
(i,j)^{\rm th}
entry equal to the resistance distance between

i
and
j
. Resistance matrices are non-singular and the inverse is given by an elegant formula that can be computed directly
from the graph. The main purpose of this paper is to deduce a formula for the inverse of a generalized resistance matrix of a simple digraph
with some special properties.
This new formula generalizes the following known results.

1.1
Inverse of the resistance matrix of a connected graph

Let
G
be a connected graph with
V(G)=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. Let
\delta_{i}
denote the degree of the vertex
i
and
A
be the
adjacency matrix of
G
. Then the Laplacian matrix of
G
is

L=\mathop{\rm Diag}(\delta_{1},\dotsc,\delta_{n})-A
.
Now the resistance between any two vertices
i
and
j
in
G
is

r_{ij}:=l_{ii}^{{\dagger}}+l_{jj}^{\dagger}-2l_{ij}^{\dagger},

(1)

where
l_{ij}^{\dagger}
is the
(i,j)^{\rm th}
entry of the Moore Penrose inverse of
L
.
Define
R:=[r_{ij}]
. Then the inverse of
R
is given by

R^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\frac{1}{\tau^{\prime}R\tau}\tau\tau^{\prime},

(2)

where

\tau:=(\tau_{1},\dotsc,\tau_{n})^{\prime}~~\mbox{and}~~\tau_{i}:=2-\sum_{\{j\in V(G):(i,j)\in E\}}r_{ij}~~~i=1,\dotsc,n.

The proof of (
2
) is given in Theorem 9.1.2 in
[
1
]
.

1.2
Inverse of the distance matrix of a tree

Let
T
be a tree with
V(T)=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
and
r_{ij}
(defined in (
1
)) be
the resistance distance between any two vertices
i
and
j
.
If

is the length of the shortest path connecting
i
and

in
T

then by an induction argument, it can be shown that

. Define

.
Specializing formula (

D^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\frac{(2-\delta_{1},\dotsc,2-\delta_{n})^{\prime}(2-\delta_{1},\dotsc,2-\delta_{n})}{2(n-1)},

where
\delta_{i}
is the degree of the vertex

and
L
is the Laplacian matrix of

.
This formula is obtained by Graham and Lovász in
[

]
.

1.3
Inverse of the distance matrix of a weighted tree

Formula
(\ref{GrahamLovasz})
can be generalized to weighted trees.
We first need to define the Laplacian matrix of a weighted tree.
Consider a tree
G=(V,\Omega)
with

V=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
. To an edge
(i,j)\in\Omega
, we assign a positive real number

w_{ij}
. Define

l_{ij}:=\begin{cases}~~~~~~~~~-\frac{1}{w_{ij}}&(i,j)\in\Omega\\
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~0&i\neq j~\mbox{and}~(i,j)\notin\Omega\\
\sum\limits_{\{k:(i,k)\in\Omega\}}\frac{1}{w_{ik}}&i=j.\end{cases}

Then the Laplacian matrix of
G
is
L:=[l_{ij}]
.
The distance matrix of
G
is the symmetric matrix
D
with
(i,j)^{\rm th}
entry equal to sum of all the weights that lie in the
path connecting
i
and
j
.
In this case, by an induction argument, it can be shown that

LDL+2L=0
and from this identity it is easy to show that

d_{ij}=l_{ii}^{{\dagger}}+l_{jj}^{{\dagger}}-2l_{ij}^{{\dagger}}
, where
l_{ij}^{{\dagger}}
is the
(i,j)^{\rm th}
entry of the Moore Penrose inverse of
L
.
Let
\delta_{i}
be the degree of the vertex
i
.
In this setting, the following inverse formula is obtained in
[
4
]

D^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2}(L+\frac{\tau\tau^{\prime}}{\sum_{i,j}{w_{ij}}}),

(4)

where
\tau
is the vector
(2-\delta_{1},\dotsc,2-\delta_{n})^{\prime}
.

Results obtained

We consider a simple digraph
\mathcal{G}=(V,\mathcal{E})
with

.
An element in
\mathcal{E}
will be denoted by

All edges are assigned a positive definite matrix of some fixed order

These positive definite matrices will be called

Let

.
In this set up, we define the following.

(i)

Laplacian of
\mathcal{G}

If
i
and

are any two distinct vertices in
\mathcal{G}
,
define

L_{ij}:=\begin{cases}-W_{ij}^{-1}&(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\\
~~~~O_{s}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(ii)

. Then, a

(iii)

Balanced vertices:
We say that a vertex

(iv)

Balanced digraphs:
If every vertex of
\mathcal{G}
is balanced, then we shall say that
\mathcal{G}
is balanced.

1.7
Other results

We obtain the following two results after proving Theorem
1
.

•

By numerical computations,
we observe that for any
a,b>0
,

R_{ij}=a^{2}K_{ii}+b^{2}K_{jj}-2abK_{ij}

is positive semidefinite. We do not know how to prove this result in general. However, when the weights in
\mathcal{G}
of Theorem
1
are
positive scalars, we show that
R_{ij}
is always a non-negative real number.

•

Let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices. Suppose
D
and
L
are the distance and Laplacian matrices of
T
. Then, from
(\ref{GrahamLovasz})
it can be deduced that

(D^{-1}-L)^{-1}=\frac{1}{3}D+\frac{1}{3}(\sum_{i,j}w_{ij})\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}.

In particular, this equation says that every entry in

(D^{-1}-L)^{-1}

is non-negative. Suppose
M
is the Laplacian matrix of an arbitrary tree on
n
vertices.
It can be shown that
D^{-1}-M
is non-singular.
We now say that

(D^{-1}-M)^{-1}
is a perturbation of the distance matrix
D
. In
[
4
]
, it is shown that
all perturabations of
D
are non-negative matrices. We now assume that
R
is the resistance matrix of a
connected graph (defined in Section 1.1) on
n
vertices. Now consider the Laplacian matrix
L
of
\mathcal{G}
in Theorem
1
. Suppose
all the weights in
\mathcal{G}
are positive scalars.
It can be shown that
R^{-1}-L
is always non-singular. We now say that
(R^{-1}-L)^{-1}

is a perturabation of
R
.
By performing certain numerical experiments, we observed that similar to the result in
[
4
]
, all perturbations of
R

are non-negative matrices. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a digraph,
L
is not symmetric in general and hence all perturbations of
R
are not symmetric matrices.
Despite this difficulty, by using a argument different from
[
4
]
, we show that all perturbations of
R
are non-negative. This result is proved in the final part of this paper.

Preliminaries

We mention the notation and some basic results that will be used in the paper.

(i)

We reserve
\mathcal{G}
to denote a simple, strongly connected, weighted, and balanced digraph with vertex set
V=\{1,\dotsc,n\}
.
A directed edge from
i
to
j
in
\mathcal{G}
will be denoted by
(i,j)
.
We use
\mathcal{E}
to denote the edge set of
\mathcal{G}
.
The weight of an edge

will be denoted by

. All weights will be symmetric positive definite matrices and have fixed order

.

(ii)

Let

be the set of all real

matrices.
A matrix

in

will be denoted by

, where

is an

s\times s
matrix. We shall say that

is the

block of

. There are
n
blocks in

. The null-space of a matrix

is denoted by
{\rm null}(A)
and the
column space by
\mathop{\rm col}(A)
.

(iii)

The vector of all ones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted by
\mathbf{1}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\otimes I_{s}
will be denoted by

, i.e.

where

appears

to denote the matrix in

with all blocks equal to

. Note that

.

(iv)

The Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
will be denoted by

and its Moore-Penrose by

.
We note that

and

.
The

.
We use

to denote the block diagonal matrix

Let

be the digraph
such that

and

To an edge

.

The Laplacian of

Fix

.
The generalized resistance matrix of

(vi)

Let

be an

matrix.

(a)

We say that

is positive semidefinite if

for all

and positive definite if

for all

.
(To define positive semidefiniteness, we do not assume that

Following

]
, we say that

From the digraph

To an edge

(7)

We now have the weighted graph

. Let

The Laplacian of

\end{cases}

We now have the following proposition. Recall that

Proposition 1
.

L(\widetilde{G})=L+L^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We shall write

Let the
(i,j)^{\rm th}

.
We need to show that

Note that

Partition the set of all edges

and

(9)

Case

(10)

\displaystyle=U^{\prime}\Big(b^{2}UU^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})L+\frac{2ab}{n}UU^{\prime}-2abI_{ns}\Big)\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})U

\displaystyle=(b^{2}n)U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})L\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})U.

By

,

U^{\prime}RL\Delta(L^{\dagger})U=b^{2}n\widetilde{X}^{\prime}L\widetilde{X}.

(30)

We also note that

\displaystyle U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})L^{\prime}RU

\displaystyle=U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})L^{\prime}(a^{2}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})UU^{\prime}+b^{2}UU^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})-2abL^{{\dagger}})U.

(31)

\displaystyle=a^{2}nU^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})L^{\prime}\Delta(L^{{\dagger}})U

\displaystyle=a^{2}n\widetilde{X}^{\prime}L\widetilde{X},

where the second equality follows from
L^{\prime}U=L^{\dagger}U=O_{s}
and the last one from

(\ref{xtilde})
.
Since

R=a^{2}\Delta(L^{\dagger})UU^{\prime}+b^{2}UU^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})-2abL^{\dagger},

we see that

U^{\prime}RU=n(a^{2}+b^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{ii}.

(32)

Substituting
(\ref{eqn18})
,
(\ref{eqn19})
,
(\ref{eqn20})
and
(\ref{eqn21})
in

, we get

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}R\tau=2a^{3}b^{3}\widetilde{X}^{\prime}L\widetilde{X}+\frac{4a^{2}b^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{ii}.

The proof of (vi) is complete.

Since
L
is positive semidefinite,
\widetilde{X}^{\prime}L\widetilde{X}
is positive semidefinite. By Proposition (

), each

is positive definite.
So,
\tau^{\prime}R\tau
is positive definite.
This proves (vii).
The proof is complete.
∎

We prove the inverse formula in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 3
.

R_{a,b}^{-1}=-\frac{1}{2ab}L+\tau(\tau^{\prime}R\tau)^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L).

Proof.

Again, as in the proof of above lemma, we shall use
R_{a,b}
for
R
.
By item (iii) of Lemma
1
,

LR+2abI_{ns}=\tau U^{\prime}.

In view of item (v) of the previous Lemma,

U^{\prime}\tau=2abI_{s}
. So,

LR\tau+2ab\tau=\tau U^{\prime}\tau=2ab\tau.

This implies

LR\tau=O_{s}.

We know that

{\rm null}(L)=\mathop{\rm span}\{(p,\dotsc,p)^{\prime}:p\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\}.

So,

R\tau=UC,

where
C
is a
s\times s
matrix.
Since
\tau^{\prime}R\tau

R\tau
cannot be zero.
Hence,

.
As
\tau^{\prime}U=2abI_{s}

Therefore,

(33)

Since

, from item (iv) of Lemma

, we deduce that

(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)(RL+2abI_{ns})=2ab(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L).

Simplifying the above equation, we get

(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)RL=O_{s}.

(34)

We now claim that

(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)R\neq O_{s}.

If not, then

\tau^{\prime}R\tau-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}R\tau+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})LR\tau=O_{s}.

(35)

By

So,

LR\tau=O_{s}~~\mbox{and}~~L^{\prime}R\tau=O_{s}.

Hence (

\tau^{\prime}R\tau=O_{s}
.
This contradicts that
\tau^{\prime}R\tau
is positive definite.
Hence,

(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)R\neq O_{s}.

Since nullity of
L^{\prime}
is
s
and

, by (

s\times s

such that

We know that
U^{\prime}\tau=2abI_{s}
. So, from the previous equation,

\tilde{C}=\frac{1}{2ab}\tau^{\prime}R\tau.

Thus,

(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)R=\frac{\tau^{\prime}R\tau}{2ab}U^{\prime}.

(36)

We now have

\displaystyle Q:=

\displaystyle\Big(-\frac{1}{2ab}L+\tau(\tau^{\prime}R\tau)^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)\Big)R

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2ab}LR+\tau(\tau^{\prime}R\tau)^{-1}({\tau}^{\prime}-a^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L^{\prime}+b^{2}U^{\prime}\Delta(L^{\dagger})L)R.

Item (iii) of Lemma

Substituting back in (

3.4
Non-negativity of the resistance

From numerical computations, we observe that
R_{ij}=a^{2}l_{ii}^{{\dagger}}+b^{2}l_{jj}^{{\dagger}}-2abl_{ij}^{{\dagger}}
is always
positive semidefinite. But at this stage, we do not know how to prove this. However, when all the weights in
\mathcal{G}
are positive scalars,
we now show that the resistance is always non-negative.
We need the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A=[a_{ij}]
be an
n\times n
matrix with the following properties.

(i)

All the off-diagonal entries are non-positive.

(ii)

A\mathbf{1}=A^{\prime}\mathbf{1}=0
.

(iii)

\mathop{\rm rank}(A+A^{\prime})=n-1
.

(iv)

A
is positive semidefinite.

Let
A^{\dagger}:=[p_{ij}]
be the Moore-Penrose inverse of
A
. Then,

p_{ii}\geq p_{ij}~~\mbox{and}~~p_{ii}\geq p_{ji}~~\forall j.

Proof.

By a permutation similarity argument, without loss of generality, we may assume that
i=1
and
j=n
.
We now show that

p_{11}\geq p_{1n}
and
p_{11}\geq p_{n1}
.
By symmetry of our assumptions, it suffices to show that

p_{11}\geq p_{1n}
.

Let
\mathbf{1}_{n-1}
be the vector of all ones in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
By (ii) we can partition
A
as follows:

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}B&-B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}\\
-\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}B&\mathbf{1}^{\prime}_{n-1}B\mathbf{1}_{n-1}\\
\end{array}\right].

All the row sums of
A+A^{\prime}

are equal to zero. So, all the cofactors of
A+A^{\prime}

are equal. As
\mathop{\rm rank}(A+A^{\prime})=n-1
, we now deduce that the common cofactor of
A+A^{\prime}

is non-zero. In particular,
{\rm det}(B+B^{\prime})\neq 0
. Since
A
is positive semidefinite,

B+B^{\prime}
is positive semidefinite. Because
B+B^{\prime}
is non-singular,
B+B^{\prime}
is positive definite.
So,
B
is positive definite. All the off-diagonal entries of
B
are non-positive. By a well-known theorem
on
\mathbf{Z}
-matrices,
B
is non-singular and all entries of
B^{-1}
are non-negative.
By a direct verification,

A^{\dagger}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}B^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}B^{-1}-\frac{1}{n}B^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}&-\frac{1}{n}B^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}\\
-\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}B^{-1}&0\end{array}\right]+\frac{\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}B^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}}{n^{2}}(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}).

(39)

Put

C=[c_{ij}]:=B^{-1}~~\mbox{and}~~\delta:=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}^{\prime}B^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{n-1}.

Then,
c_{ij}\geq 0~~\forall i,j
and

\begin{gathered}p_{11}=c_{11}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_{j1}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_{1j}+\delta,\\
p_{1n}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}c_{1j}+\delta.\end{gathered}

Now,

p_{11}-p_{1n}=c_{11}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i1}.

By Theorem
2
,

c_{11}\geq c_{j1}~~\forall j=1,\dotsc,n-1.

So,

-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i1}\geq-(\frac{n-1}{n})c_{11}.

Hence,

c_{11}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}c_{i1}\geq c_{11}-\frac{n-1}{n}c_{11}=\frac{1}{n}c_{11}.

Since
c_{11}\geq 0
, we conclude that

p_{11}-p_{1n}\geq 0.

The proof is complete.
∎

Now it can be easily shown that any generalized resistance is non-negative.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose all the weights in
\mathcal{G}
are positive scalars. Let
a,b>0
.
Let
L^{\dagger}=[k_{ij}]
be the Moore-Penrose inverse of the Laplacian of
\mathcal{G}
. Then,

r_{ij}:=a^{2}k_{ii}+b^{2}k_{jj}-2abk_{ij}\geq 0.

Proof.

We note that the Laplacian matrix
L
of
\mathcal{G}
satisfies all the conditions of the previous lemma.
Moreover, by Proposition
3
,
k_{ii}
and
k_{jj}
are positive.
As a consequence of Lemma
2
, we deduce that

{\rm min}(k_{ii},k_{jj})\geq\max(k_{ij},k_{ji}).

Now

a^{2}k_{ii}+b^{2}k_{jj}-2abk_{ij}\geq 0,

follows from the arithmetic mean and geometric mean inequality.
∎

3.5
A perturbation result

We now show that if
R
is the resistance matrix of a connected graph with
n
vertices,
and if
L
is the Laplacian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
with positive scalar weights, then

(R^{-1}-L)^{-1}
has all entries non-negative.

Theorem 5
.

Let
H
be a simple (undirected) connected graph with
n
vertices
and
R
be the resistance matrix of
H
.
Assume that all the weights in
\mathcal{G}
are positive scalars.
Then,
R^{-1}-L
is non-singular and every entry in

(R^{-1}-L)^{-1}
is non-negative.

Proof.

Let
M=[m_{ij}]
be the Moore-Penrose inverse of the Laplacian matrix of
H
.
Then the
(i,j)^{\rm th}
entry
r_{ij}
of
R
is given by

r_{ij}=m_{ii}+m_{jj}-2m_{ij}.

Fix
\alpha\geq 0
. Define
S:=\alpha L
.
We complete the proof by using the following claims.

Claim 1:

R^{-1}-S
is non-singular.

To prove this claim, we can assume that
S=\alpha L
, where
\alpha>0
.
By Proposition
2
,
\mathop{\rm rank}(S)=n-1
,
S+S^{\prime}
is positive semidefinite
and
S^{\prime}\mathbf{1}=S\mathbf{1}=0
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that

(R^{-1}-S)(x)=0.

(40)

Put
u:=R^{-1}x
.
Assuming
u\neq 0
,
we now get a contradiction.
As
\mathbf{1}^{{}^{\prime}}S=0
, it follows that

\mathbf{1}^{\prime}R^{-1}x=0
and hence

u\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
. Writing

R=\mathop{\rm Diag}(M)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathop{\rm Diag}(M)-2M,

we get

\displaystyle u^{\prime}Ru

\displaystyle=u^{\prime}(\mathop{\rm Diag}(M)\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\prime}\mathop{\rm Diag}(M)-2M)u

\displaystyle=-2u^{\prime}Mu.

Since
{\rm null}(M)=\mathop{\rm span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
,
M
is positive definite on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
. So,

u^{\prime}Mu>0
. Hence,

u^{\prime}Ru<0.

(41)

It is easy to see that

u^{\prime}Ru=x^{\prime}R^{-1}x

(42)

By
(\ref{rinverse})
,

x^{\prime}R^{-1}x=x^{\prime}Sx.

Since
S+S^{\prime}
is positive semidefinite,

x^{\prime}Sx\geq 0
. So,

x^{\prime}R^{-1}x\geq 0
. Hence by (
42
),

u^{\prime}Ru\geq 0.

(43)

Thus, we get a contradiction from
(
41
) and (
43
).
Therefore,
u=R^{-1}x=0
. This implies
x=0
.
So,
R^{-1}-S
is non-singular.
The claim is proved.

Claim 2:
If
C
is a
k\times k
proper principal submatrix of
S-R^{-1}
,
then

q^{\prime}Cq>0~~\mbox{for all}~0\neq q\in\mathbb{R}^{k}.

If
A
is an
n\times n
matrix, we shall use the notation

A[i]
to denote the principal submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting the

i^{\rm th}
row and
i^{\rm th}
column of
A
.
Fix
1\leq i\leq n
and
define

B:=S[i]-R^{-1}[i].

Since
R
is negative definite on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}

and the diagonal entries are zero,
R
has exactly one positive eigenvalue.
By an application of interlacing theorem, we see that

-R^{-1}[i]
is positive semidefinite.
Hence

-p^{\prime}R^{-1}[i]p\geq 0~~\mbox{for all}~w\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

(44)

As the row sums and the column sums of
S
are equal to zero, it follows that
all the cofactors of
S
are equal. Because
S+S^{\prime}
is positive semidefinite and
has rank
n-1
, it follows that every proper principal submatrix of
S+S^{\prime}
is positive
definite. So, we have

p^{\prime}S[i]p>0~~\mbox{for all}~0\neq p\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

(45)

By (
44
) and (
45
),

p^{\prime}Bp>0~~\mbox{for all}~~0\neq p\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

The claim is proved.

In particular, we note that all principal minors of

S-R^{-1}
with order less than
n
are positive.

Claim 3:

{\rm det}(S-R^{-1})<0
.

Because
\gamma L-R^{-1}
is non-singular for every
\gamma\geq 0
,

\mbox{sgn}~{\rm det}(\gamma L-R^{-1})=\mbox{sgn}~{\rm det}(-R^{-1}).

Since
-R
has exactly one negative eigenvalue,

{\rm det}(-R^{-1})<0
.
So,

{\rm det}(\gamma L-R^{-1})<0~~\forall\gamma\geq 0.

This proves the claim.

Claim 4:

All principal minors of
(S-R^{-1})^{-1}

are negative.

Put

G:=(S-R^{-1})^{-1}~~\mbox{and}~~H:=S-R^{-1}.

Let

s<n
, and let

\widehat{G}
be a
s\times s
principal submatrix of
G
.
Suppose
\widehat{H}

is the complementary submatrix of

\widehat{G}
in
H
.
By Jacobi identity,

{\rm det}(\widehat{G})=\frac{{\rm det}(\widehat{H})}{{\rm det}(H)}.

By claim
2
and
3
,

\mbox{sgn}({\rm det}\widehat{H})>0~~\mbox{and}~~\mbox{sgn}({\rm det}(H))<0.

So,

{\rm det}(\widehat{G})<0
.
The claim is proved.

We now complete the proof of the theorem.
Given
\beta\geq 0
, let

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{ii}(\beta)&y_{ij}(\beta)\\
y_{ji}(\beta)&y_{jj}(\beta)\end{array}\right]

denote a
2\times 2
principal submatrix of
(\beta L-R^{-1})^{-1}
.
By claim
4
,

y_{ii}(\beta)<0~~\mbox{and}~~y_{jj}(\beta)<0~~\mbox{for all}~\beta\geq 0.

(46)

We now show that

y_{ij}(\beta)<0
for all
\beta\geq 0
.
Since
y_{ij}(0)=-r_{ij}
,
y_{ij}(0)<0
.
If
y_{ij}(\alpha)>0
for some
\alpha>0
, then by continuity,
y_{ij}(\delta)=0

for some
\delta>0
.
Hence by
(\ref{yiibeta})
,

{\rm det}(\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{ii}(\delta)&y_{ij}(\delta)\\
y_{ji}(\delta)&y_{jj}(\delta)\\
\end{array}\right])=y_{ii}(\delta)y_{jj}(\delta)>0.

However by claim
4
,

{\rm det}(\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{ii}(\delta)&y_{ij}(\delta)\\
y_{ji}(\delta)&y_{jj}(\delta)\\
\end{array}\right])<0.

Thus, we have a contradiction. So,
y_{ij}(\alpha)\leq 0
for all
\alpha\geq 0
.
We now conclude that every entry in
(L-R^{-1})^{-1}
is negative. The proof is complete.
∎
