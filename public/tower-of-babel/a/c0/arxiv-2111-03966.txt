Effect of surface porosity of catalytic supports on plasma-assisted catalysis for ammonia synthesis Preprint: AIP/123-QED
S. Jaiswal, Zhe Chen, Sankaran Sundaresan, Bruce E Koel, Ahmed Diallo

Abstract
A fundamental understanding of plasma-catalyst interactions is important for understanding reaction mechanisms, optimizing the catalyst, and increasing the efficiency of plasma-assisted catalytic process for ammonia ( NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} ) synthesis. We report on the effect of the surface porosity of the catalyst support on this reaction carried out in a coaxial dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma reactor. The discharge was created using a variable AC applied voltage at room temperature and near atmospheric pressure (550 Torr). Two catalyst supports were compared: porous silica ( SiO 2 \text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ) ceramic beads and smooth, non-porous soda lime glass beads of almost equal diameter ( ∼ \sim 1.5 mm) were used. N 2 \text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} conversion and the NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} synthesis rate was increased with increasing voltage for both supports, but the energy yield for NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} production increased for the SiO 2 \text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} beads and decreased for the glass beads. All three of these parameters were always higher when using the SiO 2 \text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} beads, which suggests that porosity can be a small advantage for plasma assisted NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} synthesis. Discharge and plasma properties were estimated from Lissajous plots and using calculations with the BOLSIG+ software. The effect of different catalyst supports on the physical properties of the discharge was negligible. High resolution optical emission spectra (OES) were used to explore the evolution of gas phase active species, N 2 + \text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}} , atomic N, electronically excited N 2 \text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , and atomic H (H α , H β ), in the plasma in the presence of both supports. The relative concentration of these species was lower in the case of the porous SiO 2 \text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} beads for all applied voltages, which suggests that surface reactions are more significant than gas phase reactions for the formation of NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} in plasma assisted NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} synthesis.

II
Experimental Setup

Fig.
1
(a) shows a schematic diagram of the experimental setup. The coaxial DBD reactor consisted of a quartz tube (0.4 in. I.D., 0.5 in. O.D.), a 2.25-in. long copper woven wire mesh wrapped around the outside of the quartz tube that served as the grounded electrode, and a 0.125-in. diameter stainless steel rod covered by a 0.25-in. O.D. quartz tube, which served as the high voltage (HV) electrode, running down the center of the larger quartz tube. An AC power source (Information Unlimited, PVM500) with a frequency of 20 kHz was used to produce the DBD plasma.

II.1
Reaction kinetics experiments

Two different types of dielectric spherical beads, which had the same diameter (
\sim
1.5 mm) but had different surface porosities were used in these experiments: (i) porous
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(surface area of 300 m
2
/g) beads, and (ii) non-porous soda lime glass beads. Prior to experiments, the porous
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
beads were dried in-situ at 523 K for 2 h under 50 mL/min
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. The glass beads were dried in-situ at 423 K for 2 h under 50 mL/min
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. All experiments were run at room temperature, with the reactor pressure set to 550 Torr, and no external reactor heating used. The flow rates of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(Airgas, 99.999%) and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
(Praxair, 99.99%) into the reactor were controlled by mass flow controllers (Teledyne Hastings Instruments, 300 Series). The total flow rate was kept at 26.5 mL/min. The
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
produced in the reactor was quantified by using an online gas chromatograph (Agilent, 7890A) with a CP-Volamine column (Agilent, CP-7447) and a thermal conductivity detector.

II.2
Plasma characterization

Signals of applied voltage, current, and the voltage across an external capacitor (1000 pF) connected in series with the reactor were recorded by an oscilloscope (GW Instek, GDS-1054B). A Lissajous plot, which is a plot of charge vs. applied voltage, was constructed from the signals of the capacitor voltage and applied voltage.
Wagner et al. 2003
Discharge power was estimated from the Lissajous plot as the product of frequency and the area enclosed by the plot.
Wagner et al. 2003
We then used BOLSIG+
Hagelaar and Pitchford 2005
to estimate the mean electron temperature of the plasma using the reduced electric field as an input, which was estimated from the Lissajous plot following Butterworth
et al.

Butterworth, Elder, and Allen 2016
and Mei
et al.

Mei et al. 2014
We also estimated the electron number density from the Lissajous plot using a simple technique described by Hong
et al.

Hong et al. 2016b
, in which the electron mobility was obtained by using BOLSIG+.
Hagelaar and Pitchford 2005
Optical emission spectra were recorded using a high resolution (
\leq
0.05 nm) spectrometer (Princeton Instruments SpectraPro
®
HRS-500). The optical arrangement consisted of a multiwavelength (200–1000 nm) collimator (Fig.
1
(b)) that is connected to the spectrometer via a 1-m long optical fiber. The optical setup was located at a distance of
\sim
10 cm from the reactor. A broad spectrum was measured by using a 1200 g/mm grating in the Step and Glue mode that enabled covering a wavelength range from 300 - 1100 nm without compromising the resolution. The spectrometer integration time was set to between 1 - 15 s to compensate for the varying signal from the plasma.

IV
Conclusion

We have investigated the effect of surface porosity of catalytic supports on the reaction rate and energy yield of plasma-assisted catalysis for
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
synthesis. We performed
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
synthesis experiments using a coaxial DBD reactor packed with two different catalyst support materials, i.e., nanoporous
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
beads and non-porous soda lime glass beads, at several applied voltages (10–20 kV) at near atmospheric pressure (550 Torr). Measurements of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
concentrations at the reactor outlet using a GC found that both the observed
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
production rates and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
energy yields were enhanced in the presence of porous
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
beads compared to nonporous glass beads at the respective optimal
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
:
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
feed ratios for the two catalyst support materials.

In conjunction with the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
synthesis measurements, we made operando measurements of Lissajous plots to characterize the plasma by the reduced electric field and electron number density, and measurements of high resolution OES spectra to obtain the relative concentrations of several active species
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, N, and H, in the plasma. OES measurements showed that the concentrations of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, N, and H were lower for plasma in the presence of
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
than glass beads. Despite lower concentrations of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
, N, and H in the gas phase, the DBD reactor packed with
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
beads provided a higher
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
yield than when packed with glass beads. This suggests that the active species in the gas phase were consumed more in the bed of
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
beads than glass beads, which is likely due to the fact that
\text{SiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
provided larger surface area than glass. This shows that surface porosity of the catalyst support material has a significant effect on the plasma properties, reaction mechanism, and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
yield, and that surface reaction pathway contributes most significantly to the overall reaction of plasma-assisted
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
synthesis. Higher surface porosity provides more surface sites for radical adsorption and the subsequent E–R reactions, resulting in higher overall reaction rate of plasma-assisted
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
synthesis.
