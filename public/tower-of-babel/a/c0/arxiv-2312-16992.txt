Non-Split Sharply 2-Transitive Groups of odd positive Characteristic
Marco Amelio, Simon André & Katrin Tent

Abstract
. It is well-known that every sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic 3 3 splits. Here we construct the first examples of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups in odd positive characteristic p p , for sufficiently large primes p p . Furthermore, we show that any group without 2-torsion can be embedded into a non-split sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic p p for all sufficiently large primes p p , yielding 2 ℵ 0 2^{\aleph_{0}} many pairwise non-isomorphic countable non-split sharply 2-transitive groups in any sufficiently large characteristic.

Introduction

†
†

The first and third authors, and the second author until September 2023, were supported by the German Research Foundation (DFG) under Germany’s Excellence Strategy EXC 2044–390685587, Mathematics Münster: Dynamics–Geometry–Structure and by CRC 1442 Geometry: Deformations and Rigidity.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer, and let
G
be a group acting on a set
X
with at least
n
elements. The action is said to be
sharply n-transitive
if for any two
n
-tuples of distinct elements
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
of
X^{n}
there exists a unique element
g\in G
such that
g\cdot x_{i}=y_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. A group
G
is called
sharply
n
-transitive
if there is a set
X
with at least
n
elements on which
G
acts sharply
n
-transitively.

Clearly every group acts sharply 1-transitively on itself by left multiplication. On the other hand, for
n\geq 4
, there are only finitely many sharply
n
-transitive groups, and moreover these groups are necessarily finite and completely classified. Indeed, Jordan proved in
[
Jor72
]
that the only finite sharply
n
-transitive groups for
n\geq 4
are the symmetric groups
S_{n}
and
S_{n+1}
, the alternating group
A_{n+2}
, and the Mathieu groups
M_{11}
and
M_{12}
for the cases
n=4
and
n=5
respectively. Furthermore, Tits proved in
[
Tit52
, Chapitre IV, Théorème I]
that there are no infinite sharply
n
-transitive groups for
n\geq 4
. Zassenhaus gave a complete classification of the finite sharply
n
-transitive groups for the cases
n=2
and
n=3
in
[
Zas35
]
and
[
Zas35a
]
. For
n=2
and
n=3
there do exist also infinite sharply
n
-transitive groups: for a skew-field
K
, the affine group
AGL(1,K)\cong K_{+}\rtimes K^{*}
acts sharply 2-transitively on
K
, and for any (commutative) field
K
, the projective linear group
PGL(2,K)
acts sharply 3-transitively on the projective line. These groups are infinite whenever
K
is infinite.

Until recently, it was an open question whether a sharply 2-transitive group
G
necessarily splits in the form
A\rtimes H
for some non-trivial normal abelian subgroup
A
(in which case we simply say that
G
is
split
). The first examples of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups were exhibited by Rips, Segev and Tent in
[
RST17
]
and by Rips and Tent in
[
RT19
]
. Then, the first examples of infinite simple sharply 2-transitive groups were constructed by André and Tent in
[
AT23
]
and by André and Guirardel in
[
AG22
]
(with additional properties, in particular finite generation).

An important feature associated with a sharply 2-transitive group action is its
characteristic
, which we define as follows. Recall that an element of order 2 in a group is called an
involution
, and a product of two distinct involutions will be called a
translation
. Let
G\curvearrowright X
be a sharply 2-transitive group action. It is easy to see that
G
has involutions, and that involutions form a unique conjugacy class. Moreover, if involutions have fixed points, the action
G\curvearrowright X
is
G
-equivariant to the action of
G
by conjugation on the set of involutions of
G
, and therefore the translations form a conjugacy class. In this case we define the characteristic of
G\curvearrowright X
to be the order of a translation if this order is finite (in which case it is necessarily a prime number
\geq 3
), or as
0
in case this order is infinite. If involutions have no fixed points, we say that the action
G\curvearrowright X
has characteristic
2
. Thus, sharply
2
-transitive group actions in characteristic
2
are fundamentally different from those in other characteristics (note in particular that the examples in
[
RST17
]
show that in characteristic
2
the action by conjugation of
G
on itself need not be
2
-transitive on the class of involutions). Notice that when
K
is a field, the characteristic of
AGL(1,K)\curvearrowright K
coincides with that of
K
in the usual sense. We often say that the group
G
is sharply
2
-transitive of characteristic
p
without referring to the underlying action
G\curvearrowright X
; this is not ambiguous in characteristic
\neq 2
since the underlying action is unique (up to
G
-equivariant bijection).
†
†

Note that a group could
a priori
be simultaneously of characteristic 2 and
p\neq 2
(although there are no known examples).

The examples in
[
RST17
]
(in characteristic
2
) and in
[
RT19
,
AT23
,
AG22
]
(in characteristic
0
) show the class of sharply 2-transitive groups to be wild. By a well-known result due to Kerby (see
[
Ker74
, Theorem 9.5]
) every sharply
2
-transitive group in characterstic
3
splits. On the other hand, the question of the existence of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups in characteristic
p>3
, which we address in this paper for any prime
p
sufficiently large, had remained completely open until now.

The main difficulty in constructing non-split sharply
2
-transitive groups in characteristic
p>3
comes from the fact that the methods used so far in order to construct non-split sharply
2
-transitive groups have proceeded through HNN-extensions, which create translations of infinite order. Therefore, since all the translations have order
p
in a sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic
p>2
, in order to obtain sharply
2
-transitive groups in characteristic
p
, we will have to add new relations of the form
(ij)^{p}=1
for distinct involutions
i,j
. This will be achieved by taking small cancellation quotients similar to the quotients used in the solution of the famous Burnside problem about the existence of infinite finitely generated groups of finite exponent (posed by Burnside in
[
Bur02
]
), in order to guarantee that any translation has order
p
. This makes the construction in odd positive characteristic considerably more complicated than in characteristic 2 and 0. Moreover, it is worth pointing out that the construction of infinite finitely generated groups of even exponent is notoriously more difficult than the construction of infinite finitely generated groups of odd exponent, due to the presence of involutions. As already observed above, sharply 2-transitive groups contain plenty of involutions, and one of the challenges we have to face is to keep these involutions under control when taking small cancellation quotients. We then obtain the following result (see Theorem
2.17
and Corollary
2.18
for stronger versions of this statement, in particular the existence of
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many pairwise non-isomorphic countable non-split sharply 2-transitive groups in any characteristic
p\geq p^{\prime}
).

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a prime number
p^{\prime}
such that any group without 2-torsion embeds into a non-split sharply
2
-transitive group of characteristic
p
, for every prime number
p\geq p^{\prime}
.

The methods used in this article, based on Coulon’s paper
[
Cou16a
]
, will necessarily yield a very large value for
p^{\prime}
. The existence of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups of characteristic
p
for small values of
p\geq 5
remains an open question and seems to bear similarities to the famous open question of the existence of finitely generated infinite groups of small finite exponent.

As a side-remark we note that being a non-split sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic
p\neq 2
is expressible by a first-order sentence in the language of groups. Hence by the Compactness Theorem, we recover the existence of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups of characteristic 0 as a consequence of Theorem
1.1
.
†
†

However, the strategy of the proof in positive characteristic is based on the strategy used in
[
RT19
]
in characteristic 0, with additional work to obtain translations of finite order. So this does not actually yield a new proof in characteristic 0.

Note that
any
group embeds into a non-split sharply sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic 2 and that
any group not containing 2-torsion
can be embedded into a non-split sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic 0, and even in the centralizer of an involution in such a group (see
[
AT23
]
). Theorem
1.1
shows that a similar phenomenon occurs in (large) prime characteristic.

The proof of our main result has its roots in
[
RT19
]
, but a new step has to be added in the iterative construction in order to solve the difficulty mentioned above: given a group action with trivial 2-point stabilizers we inductively consider HNN-extensions to obtain a sharply
2
-transitive action, but the main point of the paper is concerned with the difficulty of taking the appropriate quotients to obtain positive characteristic
p
by killing
p
-powers of products of distinct involutions of infinite order, for some uniform prime
p
.

2.
Preliminaries and outline of the proof

We start by fixing some notation and terminology. Recall that for a group
G
, an
involution
is an element
r\in G
of order two, and a
translation
is a product
rs\in G
of two distinct involutions
r
and
s
. For an element
g\in G
, we write
\lvert g\rvert
for its order. We will use the following convention: for
g,h\in G
, the conjugate of
h
by
g
is
g^{-1}hg
. In this paper, a pair of elements or of subgroups of a group
G
always refers to an ordered pair.

Notation
.

Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq G
an arbitrary subset.

•

We write
I(\mathcal{S})=\{r:r\in\mathcal{S},\ \lvert r\rvert=2\}
for the set of involutions of
\mathcal{S}
.

•

We write
Tr(\mathcal{S})=\{rs:rs\in\mathcal{S},\ r,s\in I(G),\ r\neq s\}
for the set of translations of
\mathcal{S}
.

•

We write
I(\mathcal{S})^{(2)}=\{(r,s):r,s\in I(\mathcal{S}),\ r\neq s\}
for the set of (ordered) pairs of distinct involutions of
\mathcal{S}
.

•

For involutions
r\neq s
of
G
, we write
D_{r,s}=\langle r,s\rangle
(note that this group is dihedral of order
2\lvert rs\rvert
, possibly infinite).

•

We write
O(G)
for the set of natural numbers that are orders of elements of
G
, that is,

O(G)=\{n\in\mathbb{N}\,:\,\exists g\in G\,,\,\lvert g\rvert=n\}.

Note that the sets
I(G)
and
Tr(G)
are invariant under conjugation. Note also that
I(\mathcal{S})=I(G)\cap\mathcal{S}
and
Tr(\mathcal{S})=Tr(G)\cap\mathcal{S}
.

Given a sharply 2-transitive group action
G\curvearrowright X
, it is easy to see that the set
I(G)
is non-empty: for any pair of distinct points
x,y\in X
, there is an element
r
of
G
swapping
x
and
y
. Since
r^{2}
fixes both
x
and
y
, by the uniqueness condition we must have
r^{2}=1
. Similarly, it is easy to see that all involutions are conjugate. In particular, either every involution has a (necessarily unique) fixed point, or no involution has a fixed point.

If
G
acts sharply 2-transitively on a set
X
such that involutions of
G
have fixed points, there is a
G
-equivariant bijection
I(G)\longrightarrow X
given by
r\longmapsto\text{Fix}(r)
, where
G
acts on
I(G)
by conjugation (see e.g. Lemma 3.1 in
[
Ten16
]
).

Hence, if involutions in a sharply 2-transitive group have fixed points, we may assume that the group acts by conjugation on its set of involutions. An immediate consequence of this lemma is the fact that in a sharply 2-transitive group where involutions have fixed points, all translations are conjugate. Recall that in this case we define the characteristic of a sharply 2-transitive group in relation to the order of translations (we refer to the introduction).

Notice that the characteristic of a sharply 2-transitive group is necessarily 0 or a prime number
p
(since otherwise the subgroup generated by a translation
t
of order
m\cdot m^{\prime}
with
m,m^{\prime}>1
would contain a translation of order
m
that cannot be conjugate to
t
) and the case
p=2
occurs if and only if involutions are fixed-point free (since two involutions with different fixed points cannot commute).

From now on, for a prime number
p
we will write
\Fp
for the finite field with
p
elements. Also, for
n\geq 2
we will write
C_{n}
for the cyclic group of order
n
and
D_{n}
for the dihedral group of order
2n
, that is, the group given by the presentation

D_{n}=\langle r,s\ |\ r^{2}=s^{2}=(rs)^{n}=1\rangle\cong C_{n}\rtimes C_{2}.

We now introduce two classes, denoted
\mathcal{ST}(p)
and
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
, which will be used in the construction of groups as in Theorem
1.1
. The conditions for the class of groups
\mathcal{ST}(p)
are comparable to those for the classes considered in
[
RT19
,
AT23
,
AG22
]
, but in order to take small cancellation quotients as explained in the introduction, we will introduce a class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
whose elements are pairs composed of a group
G
and a tree
X
endowed with an action of
G
satisfying a number of technical assumptions that will allow us to apply the results from
[
Cou16a
]
.

Notation
.

Given a subgroup
H
of a group
G
, we write
N_{G}(H)
for the normalizer of
H
in
G
, and
\text{\text{Cen}}(g)
for the centralizer of an element
g
in
G
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group,
(r,s)
a pair in
I(G)^{(2)}
,
p
an odd prime number.

•

We say that
(r,s)
is a pair of
p
-minimal type
if
\lvert rs\rvert=p
and
N_{G}(\langle rs\rangle)=D_{r,s}
.

•

We say that
(r,s)
is a pair of
p
-affine type
if
D_{r,s}
is contained in a subgroup
H
of
G
isomorphic to
\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is not hard to see that
\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
has a unique subgroup
D
isomorphic to
D_{p}
, and that
I(D)=I(\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p}))
. Therefore, the isomorphism between
H
(as in Definition
2.1
) and
\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
induces an isomorphism between
D_{r,s}
and
D
. It follows that
H
normalizes
D_{r,s}
and that
H
acts sharply 2-transitively on
I(D_{r,s})=I(H)
by conjugation. Note in particular that
\lvert rs\rvert=p
. Moreover, if
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
is of
p
-affine type and no nontrivial element of
G
centralizes both
r
and
s
, then in fact
N_{G}(D_{r,s})
coincides with
H
, and thus is isomorphic to
\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
, since the subgroup
H\subseteq N_{G}(D_{r,s})
acts already 2-transitively by conjugation on
I(D_{r,s})
.

Remark 2.3
.

Clearly, if
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
is of
p
-minimal type (respectively, of
p
-affine type), then so is every conjugate of
(r,s)
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
G
be a group,
p
be an odd prime number. We will say that
G
is
almost sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
if it satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

Every translation is either of order
p
or of infinite order, and every pair
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
such that
rs
is of order
p
is either of
p
-minimal type or of
p
-affine type.

(2)

The (normal) subgroup
N=\langle Tr(G)\rangle
contains no involution.

(3)

The set of pairs
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
of
p
-affine type is non-empty and
G
acts transitively on it by conjugation.

(4)

For every pair
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
the subgroup
\text{\text{Cen}}(rs)
is cyclic and generated by a translation.

Remark 2.5
.

If
G
is an almost sharply 2-transitive group of of characteristic
p
, Condition
(3)
implies that
I(G)
is non-empty. Condition
(1)
implies that no two distinct involutions commute (that would yield a translation of order 2). In addition, by Condition
(4)
no non-trivial element of
G
centralizes two distinct involutions: suppose one such
g\in G
centralizes involutions
r\neq s\in I(G)
. Then
g\in\mathrm{Cen}(rs)=\langle h\rangle
for some
h\in G
. If
rs
has order
p
, we can choose
h=rs
and we see that
rgr^{-1}=g^{-1}\neq g
, a contradiction. Otherwise
rs
has infinite order and we have
g^{m}\in\langle rs\rangle\subset\mathrm{Cen}(rs)
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}
, so again
rg^{m}r^{-1}=g^{-m}\neq g^{m}
, a contradiction.

We now introduce the concept of a quasi-malnormal pair of subgroups of a given group, and we relate in Lemma
2.7
this concept to the case where the subgroups in consideration are finite dihedral of order
2p
.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
G
be a group. A subgroup
K
of
G
is said to be
quasi-malnormal
if for all
g\in G\setminus K
we have
\lvert K\cap g^{-1}Kg\rvert\leq 2
. A pair of subgroups
(K,K^{\prime})
of
G
is said to be
quasi-malnormal
if
K
is quasi-malnormal and for all
g\in G
we have
\lvert K\cap g^{-1}K^{\prime}g\rvert\leq 2
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
G
a group. Let
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
be a pair of
p
-minimal type and
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in I(G)^{(2)}
be a pair of
p
-affine type. Then the pair
(D_{r,s},D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}})
is quasi-malnormal.

Proof.

We first prove that
D_{r,s}
is quasi-malnormal. Suppose that there is some
g\in G\backslash D_{r,s}
such that
\lvert D_{r,s}\cap(g^{-1}D_{r,s}g)\rvert>2
. Then, this intersection must have order
p
or
2p
, and in any case both subgroups have the same translations, that is,
\langle rs\rangle=g^{-1}\langle rs\rangle g
. Namely,
g
normalizes
\langle rs\rangle
, contradicting the assumption that
(r,s)
is a pair of
p
-minimal type.

Then, let us prove that
D_{r,s}\cap(g^{-1}D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}g)
has order at most
2
. Assume towards a contradiction that
\lvert D_{r,s}\cap g^{-1}D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}g\rvert>2
. Since a conjugate of a pair of
p
-affine type is still of
p
-affine type, we can assume without loss of generality that
g=1
. Then
\langle rs\rangle=\langle r^{\prime}s^{\prime}\rangle
. But
\langle r^{\prime}s^{\prime}\rangle
is a characteristic subgroup of
D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}
, so the subgroup
H\cong\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})
of
N_{G}(D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}})
also normalizes
\langle r^{\prime}s^{\prime}\rangle=\langle rs\rangle
, contradicting the assumption that
(r,s)
is a pair of
p
-minimal type.∎

Remark 2.8
.

In fact, a pair
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
is of
p
-minimal type if and only if the subgroup
D_{r,s}
is quasi-malnormal. Indeed, if there exists some
g\in N_{G}(\langle rs\rangle)\backslash D_{r,s}
, then
D_{r,s}\cap(g^{-1}D_{r,s}g)
has order at least
p
and thus
D_{r,s}
is not quasi-malnormal.

Now we are ready to introduce the class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
.

Definition 2.9

(Class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
)
.

Let
G
be a group. We will say that
G
belongs to the class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
if it is almost sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
with all translations of order
p
(i.e. no translation has infinite order).

Remark 2.10
.

Clearly
\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
, so in particular this class is non-empty. Furthermore, if
G
is in
\mathcal{ST}(p)
and every pair
(r,s)\in I(G)^{(2)}
is of
p
-affine type, then
G
is sharply
2
-transitive of characteristic
p\neq 2
.

Our construction of non-split sharply 2-transitive groups starts from a group in
\mathcal{ST}(p)
and proceeds inductively by using HNN-extensions to conjugate pairs of
p
-minimal type to pairs of
p
-affine type. Furthermore, we will prove that the sharply 2-transitive groups that we construct contain non-commuting translations. The following theorem implies that these groups will not split.

Theorem 2.11

(see
[
Neu40
]
)
.

Let
G
be a sharply 2-transitive group. Then
G
splits if and only if
Tr(G)\cup\{1\}
is an abelian subgroup (which is normal since the set of translations is invariant under conjugation).

The cornerstone of the inductive process is the following result.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
p
be an odd prime number and let
G
be an almost sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic
p
.

(1)

Let
(r,s),(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in I(G)^{(2)}
with
(r,s)
of
p
-affine type, and
(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
of
p
-minimal type, so
D_{r,s}
and
D_{r^{\prime},s^{\prime}}
are isomorphic to
D_{p}
.
Then the HNN-extension

G^{*}=\langle G,t\ |\ t^{-1}rt=r^{\prime},t^{-1}st=s^{\prime}\rangle

is almost sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
.

(2)

Let
H
be a non-trivial group without
2
-torsion. Then
G^{*}=G*H
is almost sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
.

However, in both cases of Proposition
2.12
, even if
G\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
, we have
G^{*}\notin\mathcal{ST}(p)
since
G^{*}
contains translations of infinite order. Thus, in order to eventually obtain a sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic
p
, in particular a group where all translations have order
p
, we need to take partial periodic quotients (see Theorem
4.28
) of the (normal) subgroup generated by translations. To that purpose, we now introduce a new class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
. Definition
2.13
below involves Theorem
4.28
, which is stated together with the necessary background in Section
4
following work of Coulon (see
[
Cou16a
]
).

Definition 2.13

(Class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
)
.

Let
G
be a group acting by isometries on a (simplicial) tree
X
. Write
N=\langle Tr(G)\rangle
and
Q=Tr(G)
. We say that
(G,X)
belongs to the class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
if
G
is almost sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
and the following conditions are satisfied.

(1’)

The action of
G
on
X
is non-elementary and acylindrical (see Definition
3.28
), and thus it is WPD (see Definition
3.31
).

(2’)

The action is such that
e(N,X)=1
,
r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)\geq 1
,
\nu(N,X)\leq 5
and
A(N,X)=0
(see Subsection
4.1
for the definitions of the parameters), and the prime number
p
is at least
n_{1}
(the constant provided by Theorem
4.28
for these values of the parameters and hyperbolicity constant
\delta=0
).

(3’)

Every translation of infinite order is loxodromic for its action on
X
.

(4’)

A finite subgroup of
G
normalized by a loxodromic element has order at most 2.

We will sometimes say that
G
belongs to the class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
with no mention of the space
X
if this is not necessary. We will prove in Section
5
that a group in class
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
in fact satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
4.28
(that is, that the family
Q
is strongly stable, see Definition
4.26
).

We will show in Section
6
the following refinement of Proposition
2.12
.

Proposition 2.14
.

If
G\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
, then for
G^{*}
as in Proposition
2.12
we have
(G^{*},X)\in\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
where
X
is the Bass-Serre tree associated with the decomposition of
G^{*}
as an HNN-extension or as a free product.

This allows us to take appropriate quotients to return to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
. Namely, we will show in Section
5
the following result.

Proposition 2.15
.

If
(G,X)
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
, then
G
has a quotient
\bar{G}\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
such that the images of pairs of distinct involutions of
p
-affine type (respectively, of
p
-minimal type) will again be of
p
-affine type (respectively, of
p
-minimal type), any subgroup of
G
that is elliptic for the action on
X
projects to an isomorphic image in
\bar{G}
, and
O(G)=O(\bar{G})
.

We will need the following easy observation.

Remark 2.16
.

Notice that if a group
G
is the union of an infinite chain of subgroups
H_{m}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, all of them belonging to the class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
, then so does
G
. Moreover, if for all
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
we have
O(H_{m})=O(H_{m^{\prime}})
, then
O(G)=O(H_{0})
.

Propositions
2.14
and
2.15
are the tools used in the proof our main result, i.e. the following stronger version of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 2.17

(The Embedding Theorem)
.

There exists a prime number
p^{\prime}
with the following property. If
G\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
for a prime number
p\geq p^{\prime}
and if
H
has no 2-torsion, then
G
and
H
embed into a non-split sharply 2-transitive group
\mathbf{G}
of characteristic
p
with
O(\mathbf{G})=O(G)\cup O(H)
and of cardinality equal to the maximum of
\aleph_{0}
and the cardinalities of
G
and
H
.

Proof.

Let
n_{1}
be the constant involved in Definition
2.13
, Condition
(2’)
. Let
p^{\prime}
be the smallest odd prime number such that
p^{\prime}\geq n_{1}
, and let
p\geq p^{\prime}
be a prime number. Let
G\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
and
H
be a group without 2-torsion. By Proposition
2.14
applied to
G^{*}=G*H
we see that
(G^{*},X)\in\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
where
X
is the Bass-Serre tree associated with the free product. Clearly, we have
O(G^{*})=O(G)\cup O(H)
.

Let
G_{0}^{0}=\overline{G^{*}}\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
be the group obtained from
(G^{*},X)
by applying Proposition
2.15
. In particular, we have
O(G^{*})=O(G_{0}^{0})
. Since
G
and
H
are elliptic for their action on the Bass-Serre tree of the free product,
G_{0}^{0}
contains isomorphic images of these groups. Furthermore, we will prove that
G_{0}^{0}
contains non-commuting translations (see Lemma
5.17
).

Now, fix a pair of distinct involutions
(i,j)\in G_{0}^{0}
of
p
-affine type. Enumerate all pairs of
I(G_{0}^{0})^{(2)}
as
(i_{0}^{\lambda},j_{0}^{\lambda}):\lambda<\gamma
. We will construct inductively
G_{0}^{\alpha}
for
\alpha<\gamma
. For successor ordinals, suppose
G_{0}^{\alpha}
has already been constructed, that this group is in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
, that
G_{0}^{0}
embeds into
G_{0}^{\alpha}
, and that we have
O(G)=O(G_{0}^{\alpha})
. Consider the pair
(i_{0}^{\alpha},j_{0}^{\alpha})
. If this pair is of
p
-affine type, we set
G_{0}^{\alpha+1}=G_{0}^{\alpha}
. If the pair is of
p
-minimal type, we set

(G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*}=\langle G_{0}^{\alpha}\,,\,t\ |\ t^{-1}jt=j_{0}^{\alpha}\,,\,t^{-1}it=i_{0}^{\alpha}\rangle.

Note that this is a well-defined HNN-extension since
D_{i,j}
and
D_{i_{0}^{\alpha},j_{0}^{\alpha}}
are isomorphic to
D_{p}
. Clearly, in
(G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*}
the pair
(i_{0}^{\alpha},j_{0}^{\alpha})
is of
p
-affine type.

By Proposition
2.14
, the pair
((G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*},X)
is in
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
, with
X
the Bass-Serre tree of the HNN-extension. Moreover, clearly
G_{0}^{0}
embeds into
(G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*}
, and
O((G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*})=O(G_{0}^{\alpha})
.

With this in mind, we set
G_{0}^{\alpha+1}=\overline{(G_{0}^{\alpha})^{*}}
as in Proposition
2.15
. This group belongs to the class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
, and is such that
O(G_{0}^{\alpha+1})=O(G_{0}^{\alpha})
. Furthermore
G_{0}^{0}
embeds in
G_{0}^{\alpha+1}
(and therefore, so do
G
and
H
) since it has an isomorphic image in the HNN-extension that is elliptic for its action on the Bass-Serre tree
X
. Moreover, every pair
(i_{0}^{\lambda},j_{0}^{\lambda})
of distinct involutions is of
p
-affine type for
\lambda\leq\alpha
.

For a limit ordinal
\alpha
, we set
G_{0}^{\alpha}=\cup_{\beta<\alpha}G_{0}^{\beta}
. By Remark
2.16
this group is in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
,
G_{0}^{0}
embeds into it, and is such that
O(G_{0}^{\alpha})=O(G_{0}^{0})
. Moreover, every pair
(i_{0}^{\lambda},j_{0}^{\lambda})
of distinct involutions is of
p
-affine type for
\lambda<\alpha
.

Put
G_{1}^{0}=\cup_{\beta<\gamma}G_{0}^{\beta}
. With Remark
2.16
in mind, we can deduce similar properties to the ones in the previous paragraph, and in this case, every pair
(r,s)\in I(G_{0}^{0})^{(2)}
is of
p
-affine type. The cardinality of this group is clearly the maximum of
\aleph_{0}
and the cardinalities of
G
and
H
.

Now, build
G^{0}_{k+1}
from
G^{0}_{k}
in a completely analogous way to the construction of
G^{0}_{1}
from
G^{0}_{0}
, just replacing
G^{\alpha}_{0}
by
G^{\alpha}_{k}
,
G^{\alpha+1}_{0}
by
G^{\alpha+1}_{k}
, enumerating the pairs of
I(G_{k}^{0})
as
(i_{k}^{\lambda},j_{k}^{\lambda}):\lambda<\gamma
, and conjugating pairs of
p
-minimal type to the pair
(i,j)
of
p
-affine type. Once again, assume that the group
G_{k}^{\alpha}
is in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
, with
G_{k}^{0}
(and thus also
G_{0}^{0}
) embedding into it, and with
O(G_{k}^{\alpha})=O(G_{0}^{0})
. If we need to take an HNN-extension at that step, Proposition
2.14
ensures that the pair
((G_{k}^{\alpha})^{*},X)
, with
X
the Bass-Serre tree of the extension, belongs to
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
. Moreover, Proposition
2.15
gives that
G_{k}^{\alpha+1}
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
with
O(G_{k}^{\alpha+1})=O(G_{0}^{0})
, and with a completely analogous argument to the case
k=0
we get that
G_{0}^{0}
embeds into this group. Finally, Remark
2.16
guarantees that
G_{k+1}^{0}
is also in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
and that
O(G_{k+1}^{0})=O(G)
. By construction,
G_{0}^{0}
embeds into
G_{k+1}^{0}
and all pairs
(r,s)\in I(G_{k}^{0})^{(2)}
are of
p
-affine type in this group, since they are conjugate to one such pair
(i,j)
. Again, the cardinality of
G_{k+1}^{0}
is the maximum of
\aleph_{0}
and the cardinalities of
G
and
H
.

Set now
\mathbf{G}=\cup_{k<\omega}G_{k}^{0}
. Since every group
G_{k}^{0}
is in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
and is such that
O(G_{k}^{0})=O(G_{0}^{0})
, then by Remark
2.16

\mathbf{G}
is itself in class
\mathcal{ST}(p)
with
O(\mathbf{G})=O(G_{0}^{0})
. Moreover, every pair
(r,s)\in I(\mathbf{G})^{(2)}
is of
p
-affine type: if this pair of involutions appears for the first time in some
G_{m}^{0}
, then our construction ensures that this pair is of
p
-affine type in
G_{m+1}^{0}
. In particular,
\mathbf{G}
is sharply 2-transitive of characteristic
p
. By construction,
G_{0}^{0}
(and thus,
G
and
H
) embeds into
\mathbf{G}
, so it contains non-commuting translations and thus by Theorem
2.11
it is not split. Finally, the cardinality of this group is the maximum of
\aleph_{0}
and the cardinalities of
G
and
H
. Thus,
\mathbf{G}
is a group as claimed by Theorem
2.17
.
∎

Theorem
2.17
also implies the following result.

Corollary 2.18

(The Existence Theorem)
.

There exists a prime number
p^{\prime}
such that for every prime number
p\geq p^{\prime}
there are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many pairwise non-isomorphic countable non-split sharply 2-transitive groups of characteristic
p
.

Proof.

Keeping the same notation as the statement of Theorem
2.17
, put
G=\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\in\mathcal{ST}(p)
. We will show how to choose groups
H
without 2-torsion appropriately so as to obtain
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many non-isomorphic such groups. Denote by
P
the set of prime numbers greater than
p
. There are
\aleph_{0}
many such primes, and therefore there are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many subsets of
P
. For every
S\subseteq P
, write
H_{S}
for the direct sum of the cyclic groups of order
s\in S
. Notice that the prime numbers in
O(H_{S})
are precisely the ones in
S
. Moreover, by the choice of
P
, none of these primes will be in
O(\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p}))
, since
\lvert\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p})\rvert=p(p-1)
, and thus the greatest prime number in
O(\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p}))
is
p
.

Consider now the group
\mathbf{H}_{S}
provided by Theorem
2.17
with
H=H_{S}
. Such a group is a countable non-split sharply 2-transitive group of characteristic
p
with
O(\mathbf{H}_{S})=O(\text{AGL}(1,\mathbb{F}_{p}))\cup O(H_{S})
. In particular, we have
O(\mathbf{H}_{S})\cap P=S
. Therefore, if
S^{\prime}\neq S^{\prime\prime}
are subsets of
P
,
\mathbf{H}_{S^{\prime}}\ncong\mathbf{H}_{S^{\prime\prime}}
(since
O(\mathbf{H}_{S^{\prime}})\neq O(\mathbf{H}_{S^{\prime\prime}})
). Since there are
2^{\aleph_{0}}
many distinct subsets of
P
, this yields the desired conclusion.
∎

The remainder of this article is devoted to recalling the necessary background and developing the tools to complete the proof of Theorem
2.17
, that is, to proving Propositions
2.14
and
2.15
.

3.
Hyperbolic metric spaces and group actions

In this section we recall some concepts from metric geometry. We start by introducing
length metric spaces
and
quasi-geodesics
in Subsection
3.1
(for a more comprehensive overview, see for example
[
BBI22
, Chapter 2]
). In Subsection
3.2
we recall some of the basic ideas of hyperbolic spaces in the sense of Gromov, and in Subsection
3.3
we study the actions by isometries of groups on these spaces. We follow closely the exposition by Coulon in
[
Cou16a
, Sections 2 and 3]
.

From now on, for a metric space
X
and two points
x
and
x^{\prime}
of
X
, we will write
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
(or just
d(x,x^{\prime})
if the metric space is clear from the context) for the distance between
x
and
x^{\prime}
, unless different notation is explicitly defined.

3.1.
Length metric spaces and quasi-geodesics

For a topological space
X
, a
path
in
X
is a continuous map
\gamma:I\longrightarrow X
, where
I
is an interval (possibly consisting of a single point) in
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\gamma:[a,b]\longrightarrow X
be a path. A
partition
of
[a,b]
is a finite collection
Y=\{y_{0},\dots y_{N}\}\subset[a,b]
such that
a=y_{0}\leq y_{1}\leq\dots\leq y_{N}=b
. The
sum of a partition
Y
is defined as

\Sigma(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d(\gamma(y_{i-1}),\gamma(y_{i})).

The length of the path
\gamma
with respect to the metric
d
is denoted by
L_{d}(\gamma)
and defined as

L_{d}(\gamma)=\sup\{\Sigma(Y)\,:\,Y\text{is a partition of}\ [a,b]\}.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space connected by rectifiable paths (i.e. between any two points there is a path of finite length). Then the
intrinsic metric

d_{\ell}
on
X
is defined by putting

d_{\ell}(x,y)=\inf\{L_{d}(\gamma)\,:\,\gamma:[a,b]\longrightarrow X,\,\gamma(a)=x,\,\gamma(b)=y\}.

A metric space
(X,d)
is said to be a
length metric space
(or just a
length space
) if
d_{\ell}=d
. If moreover
(X,d)
has the property that there always exists a path
\gamma
that achieves the infimum in the definition of
d_{\ell}
, then
(X,d)
is called a
geodesic metric space
, and the path
\gamma
is called a
geodesic
.

We now recall the concepts of a
quasi-isometric embedding
and of a
quasi-geodesic
.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\ell,L\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
, and let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two metric spaces. A map
f:X_{1}\longrightarrow X_{2}
is a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if for every two points
x,y\in X_{1}
we have:

\frac{1}{k}d_{X_{2}}(f(x),f(y))-\ell\leq d_{X_{1}}(x,y)\leq kd_{X_{2}}(f(x),f(y))+\ell.

We say that
f
is an
L
-local
(k,\ell)
-quasi-isometric embedding
if its restriction to any subset of diameter at most
L
is a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-isometric embedding.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an interval. A path
\gamma:I\longrightarrow X
that is a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-isometric embedding is called a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic
. If it is an
L
-local
(k,\ell)
-quasi-isometric embedding it is called an
L
-local
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic
.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that for
k=1
and
\ell=0
,
f
is a genuine isometry in Definition
3.3
, and
\gamma
is a genuine geodesic in Definition
3.4
.

Remark 3.6
.

If
(X,d)
is a length space, then by definition of the infimum, for every pair of points
x,y\in X
and every
\ell>0
, there exists a path
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow X
such that
d(x,y)\leq L_{d}(\gamma)\leq d(x,y)+\ell
. After reparametrizing
\gamma
(by arc length) if necessary, we can assume that
a=0
and
b=L_{d}(\gamma)
and thus that
L_{d}(\gamma)=|b-a|
. Hence,
\gamma
is a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic (and thus it is a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic for every
k\geq 1
).

3.2.
Hyperbolic metric spaces

For a metric space
X
, a point
x
in
X
and a subset
Y
of
X
, we will write

d_{X}(x,Y)=\inf_{y\in Y}\{d_{X}(x,y)\}

for the distance between
x
and
Y
.
Also, for a subset
Y
of
X
we will write
\text{diam}(Y)
for the diameter of
Y
, that is,

\text{diam}(Y)=\sup_{y,y^{\prime}\in Y}(d_{X}(y,y^{\prime})).

We will put
B_{X}(x,r)
(or simply
B(x,r)
if the metric space
X
is clear by context) for the ball of radius
r
centered at
x
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
x
,
y
and
z
be three points of
X
. The
Gromov product of
x
and
y
with respect to
z
is

\langle x,y\rangle_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\{d(x,z)+d(y,z)-d(x,y)\}.

A metric space
X
is said to be
\delta
-hyperbolic
(in the sense of Gromov) if for every four points
x,y,z,t\in X
we have

\langle x,z\rangle_{t}\geq\min\{\langle x,y\rangle_{t},\langle y,z\rangle_{t}\}-\delta.

We will say that
X
is
hyperbolic
if it is
\delta
-hyperbolic for some
\delta\geq 0
.

Remark 3.8
.

For simplicity of notation, from now on we assume that the hyperbolicity constant
\delta
is positive. However, notice that this is not a serious restrain: if
X
is
\delta
-hyperbolic for
\delta\geq 0
, then it is
\delta^{\prime}
-hyperbolic for every
\delta^{\prime}\geq\delta
.

If
X
is a
\delta
-hyperbolic geodesic metric space, then every geodesic triangle in
X
is
2\delta
-thin
, that is, for every geodesic triangle in
X
, every side of the triangle is contained in the closed
2\delta
-neighbourhood of the union of the other two sides
[
DK18
, Lemma 11.28]
.

3.2.1.
Quasi-geodesic polygons

We now collect some useful properties of
quasi-geodesic polygons
in length spaces, similar to the
\delta
-thin condition for triangles in geodesic spaces. We follow closely the exposition by Druţu and Kapovich in
[
DK18
, Chapter 11]
.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
x,y,z\in X
. A
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic triangle with vertices
x
,
y
and
z
is the union of the image of three paths
\gamma_{x,y}
,
\gamma_{y,z}
and
\gamma_{z,x}
such that the initial point of
\gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}
is
t
, the endpoint is
t^{\prime}
and each path is a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic.

From now on, we will not distinguish between paths (that are actually maps from an interval of the real line to
X
) from their images in
X
, and we will assume these paths are parametrized by arc length. Notice that if
X
is a length space, we get immediately from Remark
3.6
that for any
x,y,z\in X
and any
\ell>0
, there exists a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic triangle with vertices
x
,
y
and
z
. We will denote one such triangle by
[x,y,z]_{\ell}
and its sides by
[x,y]_{\ell}
,
[y,z]_{\ell}
and
[x,z]_{\ell}
respectively.

In a similar way, we can define a
(k,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic
n
-gon
for
n\geq 4
. As in geodesic spaces, one such
n
-gon is said to be
\alpha
-thin
if every side is contained in the closed
\alpha
-neighbourhood of the other
n-1
sides.

Lemma
3.10
below shows how, even though quasi-geodesics between two points may not be unique in a hyperbolic length space, they cannot go too far away from one another. This result is well-known in the context of geodesic spaces. Since we couldn’t find a proof in the literature for length spaces, we include a proof of this result in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space, let
x,y\in X
. Let
\gamma
be a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic connecting
x
to
y
for
\ell\geq 0
. Let
p\in X
be a point such that

d(x,p)+d(p,y)\leq d(x,y)+2\delta+3\ell.

Then,
d(p,\gamma)\leq 4\delta+8\ell
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space and let
[x,y,p]_{\ell}
be a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic triangle. Then, this triangle is
(4\delta+8\ell)
-thin.

Proof.

Let
z\in[x,y]_{\ell}
, we need to prove that it is
(4\delta+8\ell)
-close to
[y,p]_{\ell}\cup[x,p]_{\ell}
. By
\delta
-hyperbolicity, we have

\langle x,y\rangle_{p}\geq\min(\{\langle x,z\rangle_{p}\,,\,\langle y,z\rangle_{p}\})-\delta.

Without loss of generality, we may consider that

\langle x,y\rangle_{p}\geq\langle x,z\rangle_{p}-\delta,

i.e. that

d(y,p)-d(x,y)\geq d(z,p)-d(x,z)-2\delta.

We will show that
z
is
(4\delta+8l)
-close to
[y,p]_{\ell}
. By combining the last inequality with the fact that, since
z\in[x,y]_{\ell}
we have

d(x,y)-d(x,z)\geq d(y,z)-3\ell,

we obtain

d(y,z)+d(z,p)\leq d(y,p)+3\ell+2\delta.

By Lemma
3.10
, we obtain that
d(z,[y,p]_{\ell})\leq 4\delta+8\ell
.
∎

Notice that the bound shown may not be the optimal one. We immediately obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic length space. Then every
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic
n
-gon is
(4(n-2)\delta+8(n-2)\ell)
-thin.

Corollary
3.12
and the following lemma are proved in a completely analogous way to the case of geodesic
n
-gons in geodesic spaces (see for example
[
DK18
, Section 11.1]
), that is, by triangulating the
n
-gon by
n-3

(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic diagonals joining a given vertex to the non-adjacent vertices of the
n
-gon.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic length space,
[p,q,r,s]_{\ell}
a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic quadrangle with
d(p,q)=d(r,s)
. Then, for any pair of points
x\in[p,q]_{\ell}
and
y\in[r,s]_{\ell}
with
d(p,x)=d(s,y)
we have that

d(x,y)\leq 5\max(\{d(s,p)\,,\,d(q,r)\})+16\delta+38\ell.

3.2.2.
Quasi-convex and strongly quasi-convex subsets

We now define the concepts of a
quasi-convex
subset of a metric space and of a

3.2.3.
The boundary at infinity

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space, and
x\in X
. A sequence
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to
converge to infinity
if
\langle y_{m},y_{m^{\prime}}\rangle_{x}
tends to infinity as

and

tend to infinity. Note that by hyperbolicity this does not depend on the choice of

. The set

of sequences converging to infinity is endowed with a relation

defined as follows: two sequences
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in

are related if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle y_{n},z_{n}\rangle_{x}=+\infty.

Again hyperbolicity gives that this is in fact an equivalence relation.

Definition 3.19
.

Let

be a hyperbolic metric space,
\mathcal{S}
the set of sequences of points of

converging to infinity. The

, denoted as
\partial X
, is the quotient of
\mathcal{S}
by the equivalence relation

.

This definition does not depend on the choice of the base point

since

is hyperbolic. We will write
[(x_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}]
for the equivalence class of the sequence
(x_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
. For a subset

of

, we will write
\partial Y
for the set of elements of

that are limits of sequences of points of

.

By construction, if a group

acts by isometries on a hyperbolic space

.

3.3.
Group actions on hyperbolic spaces

For a group

acting by isometries on a hyperbolic metric space

in
\partial X

[

, Proposition 3.4]

Recall that if

is of one of the following types:

•

elliptic, i.e.
\partial\langle g\rangle
is empty,

•

\partial\langle g\rangle

•

\partial\langle g\rangle

For a loxodromic isometry

They are the only points of

fixed by

(see
[
CDP06
, Chapitre 10, Proposition 6.6]
). Conversely we have the following well-known lemma.

Lemma 3.20
.

[
Cou16a
, Proposition 3.6]

Let

be a group acting by isometries on a hyperbolic metric space

. If

has at least two points, then

contains a loxodromic isometry.

Next we introduce two concepts of translation lengths that can be used, among other things, to give a characterization of loxodromic isometries.

Definition 3.21
.

Let

be a hyperbolic metric space,

an isometry of

of

(or simply

The

of

(or simply

Once again, notice that the definition of the asymptotic translation length does not depend on the choice of

. These two concepts are related as follows:

Lemma 3.22
.

[

, Chapitre 10, Propositions 6.3 and 6.4]

-hyperbolic metric space,

an isometry of

. Then, the quantities

and

satisfy

and

3.3.3.
Acylindrical group actions

Recall that a group is hyperbolic if and only if it acts properly and cocompactly by isometries on a hyperbolic metric space. In recent years, many interesting results were obtained for groups that admit a (non-elementary) action on a hyperbolic metric space by isometries satisfying a weaker condition. One of the two weaker conditions that will be considered in this paper is
acylindricity
. This notion goes back to Sela’s paper
[
Sel97
]
, where it was considered for groups acting on trees. In the context of general metric spaces, the following definition was introduced by Bowditch in
[
Bow08
]
.

Definition 3.28
.

Let
G
be a group acting by isometries on a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space
X
. The action is said to be
acylindrical
if for every
\varepsilon\geq 0
there exist
M,L>0
such that for every
x,y\in X
with
d(x,y)\geq L
we have:

\lvert\{g\in G:d(x,g\cdot x)\leq\varepsilon,\,d(y,g\cdot y)\leq\varepsilon\}\rvert\leq M.

Remark 3.29
.

By
[
DGO17
, Proposition 5.31]
it suffices to check this condition for
\varepsilon=100\delta
. Even though this result is stated for geodesic spaces, this also holds for length spaces (see
[
Cou21
, Proposition 5.6]
).

The following lemma, which is an immediate consequence of
[
Osi16
, Theorem 1.1]
, will play an important role in the proof of our main result.

Lemma 3.30
.

Le
G
be a group acting acylindrically by isometries on a hyperbolic metric space. Then
G
has no parabolic subgroup.

3.3.4.
Weakly properly discontinuous group actions

We now define a weakening of acylindricity, namely
weak proper discontinuity
. This notion was introduced by Bestvina and Fujiwara in
[
BF02
]
.

Definition 3.31
.

Let
G
be a group acting by isometries on a metric space
X
, and let
h\in G
be a loxodromic element. The isometry
h
is said to satisfy the
weak proper discontinuity property
(WPD property) if for every
x\in X
and every
\varepsilon\geq 0
there exist
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
M>0
such that

\lvert\{g\in G:d(x,g\cdot x)\leq\varepsilon,\,d(h^{n}\cdot x,gh^{n}\cdot x)\leq\varepsilon\}\rvert\leq M.

The action is said to be
weakly properly discontinuous
(WPD) if every loxodromic element satisfies the WPD property.

Remark 3.32
.

Clearly, an acylindrical action is also WPD.

The following lemma will be crucial (see
[
Cou16a
, Section 3]
).

Lemma 3.33
.

Let
G
be a group with a WPD action by isometries on a hyperbolic length space
X
. Then every loxodromic subgroup
H
of
G
is contained in a unique maximal loxodromic subgroup of
G
, namely the setwise stabilizer of the pair
\partial H\subset\partial G
, denoted by
M_{G}(H)
. Moreover,
M_{G}(H)
(and thus

) are virtually cyclic.

Recall that an infinite virtually cyclic group

maps either onto
\mathbb{Z}
or onto
D_{\infty}
, with finite kernel (which is the unique maximal normal finite subgroup of
H
). In the first case, we say that
H
is of
cyclic type
, and in the second case we say that
H
is of
dihedral type
. An element of

is called
primitive
if it maps to an element of
\mathbb{Z}
(in the first case) or of
D_{\infty}
(in the second case) that has infinite order and does not admit a proper root. This terminology can be extended to a loxodromic element

of
G
: the element
h
is called
primitive
if it is primitive as an element of the virtually cyclic subgroup
M_{G}(\langle h\rangle)
(equivalently,
h
has minimal asymptotic translation length among the loxodromic elements of

).

The next lemma relates the cylinder of a loxodromic isometry with the characteristic subset of finite subgroups normalized by this element.

Lemma 3.34
.

[
Cou16a
, Lemma 3.33]

Let
G
be a group with a WPD action by isometries on a hyperbolic length space
X
. Let
g
be a loxodromic element of
G
and

a subgroup fixing the set
\{g^{\pm\infty}\}
pointwise. Let

be the maximal normal finite subgroup of

is contained in the

.

4.
Geometric small cancellation

In the 1910s, Dehn proved that for the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus at least two the word problem is solvable. His work involved negative curvature, and was a precursor for small cancellation theory. Small cancellation conditions were formulated explicitely for the first time by Tartakovskii in 1949. Then, small cancellation theory was developed notably by Greendlinger in the early 1960s and by Lyndon and Schupp around the same time to study groups given by group presentations where defining relations have small overlaps with each other. However, the geometric origins of small cancellation theory have gradually been forgotten in favour of combinatorial and topological methods. According to Gromov, “the role of curvature was reduced to a metaphor (algebraists do not trust geometry)”, and he proposed to return to the geometric sources of small cancellation theory. This point of view appears in
[
Gro01
]
and
[
DG08
]
, and was further developed extensively in the work of Coulon.

In this section we introduce and adapt the small cancellation results proved in
[
Cou16a
]
. We start by defining in Subsection
4.1
some
invariants
of a WPD group action on a hyperbolic metric space (for a more comprehensive overview, see
[
Cou16a
, Section 3.5]
). Then, in Subsection
4.2
we introduce the
cone-off
construction (see also
[
Cou16a
, Section 4]
), that will play a central role when we state Theorem
4.15
in Subsection
4.3
and Proposition
4.25
and Theorem
4.28
in Subsection
4.4
, which are the small cancellation results used to prove Proposition
2.15
.

4.1.
Invariants of a Group Action

Throughout this subsection we fix a group
G
with a WPD action by isometries on a hyperbolic space

. We introduce the invariants

(for a subset

of

),

,

and

. They will play the role of the small cancellation parameters in Subsection

.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

be a subset of

. The

of

is

r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)=\inf\{\left[g\right]^{\infty}:g\in Q,\,\,g\,\,\text{loxodromic}\}.

Definition 4.2
.

The invariant

is the least common multiple of
\lvert F\rtimes\text{Aut}(F)\rvert
where

runs over the maximal normal finite subgroup of all maximal loxodromic subgroups of

.

Remark 4.3
.

Notice that if all loxodromic subgroups of

are cyclic, then we will get

.

Definition 4.4
.

The invariant

(or simply

) is the smallest positive integer

satisfying the following property: let

and

be two isometries of

with

,

and

generate an elementary subgroup of

.

The proof of Lemma 6.12 in
[

]
yields the following bound for

for acylindrical actions with positive injectivity radius.

.

Notice that if

4.2.
The Cone-Off Construction

In this subsection we introduce the
cone-off
construction over certain families of subspaces of a metric space, and explain how to extend the action of a group on the metric space to an action on the cone-off. This construction will allow us to iteratively apply the Small Cancellation Theorem introduced in Subsection
4.3
. For the rest of this section, we fix a number
\rho>0
.

Definition 4.8
.

Let
X
be a metric space. The
cone over
X
of radius
\rho
, denoted by
Z_{\rho}(X)
(or, if the value of
\rho
is clear by context, simply
Z(X)
) is the topological quotient of
X\times[0,\rho]
by the equivalence relation identifying all points of the form
(x,0)
,
x\in X
.

The equivalence class of
(x,0)
is called the
apex
of the cone. The cone over
X
is endowed with a metric characterized as follows (see
[
BH13
, Chapter I.5, Proposition 5.9]
). Let
x=(y,r)
and
x^{\prime}=(y^{\prime},r^{\prime})
be two points of
Z(X)
, then

\cosh(d_{Z(X)}(x,x^{\prime}))=\cosh(r)\cosh(r^{\prime})-\sinh(r)\sinh(r^{\prime})\text{cos}\left(\min\left(\pi,\dfrac{d_{X}(y,y^{\prime})}{\sinh(\rho)}\right)\right).

In addition, if
X
is a length space, then so is
Z(X)
. An action by isometries of a group
G
over
X
naturally extends to an action by isometries on
Z(X)
as follows: for
x=(y,r)
in
Z(X)
and
g\in G
, put
g\cdot x=(g\cdot y,r)
. Note that in this case the apex of
Z(X)
is a global fixed point.

We can compare the original metric space
X
with its cone
Z(X)
by defining a
comparison map

\psi:X\longrightarrow Z(X)
such that
x\longmapsto(x,\rho)
.

Now we are ready to introduce the cone-off construction.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
X
be a hyperbolic length space,
\mathcal{Y}
a collection of strongly quasi-convex subsets of
X
. For
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
, denote by
d_{Y}
the metric on
Y
given by the length structure on
Y
induced by the length structure of
X
, by
Z(Y)
the cone over
Y
(endowed with the distance
d_{Y}
) of radius
\rho
and by
\psi_{Y}
the corresponding comparison map.

The
cone-off of radius
\rho
over
X
relative to
\mathcal{Y}
, denoted by
\dot{X}_{\rho}(\mathcal{Y})
(or simply
\dot{X}
if
\rho
and
\mathcal{Y}
are clear by context) is the quotient of the disjoint union of
X
and the
Z(Y)
for all
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
by the equivalence relation that, for all
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
and
y\in Y
, identifies
y
with
\psi_{Y}(y)\in Z(Y)
.

Since the hyperbolic length space
X
embeds into the cone-off
\dot{X}
, we will identify it with its image under this embedding. The cone-off is naturally endowed with a metric given by the length structure on
\dot{X}
induced by the length structures on
X
and
Z(Y)
for all
Y\in\mathcal{Y}

[
Cou16a
, Section 4.2]
.

The following lemma gives conditions under which the cone-off is hyperbolic, with certain control over the hyperbolicity constant. For this purpose, we introduce a parameter that controls the overlap between the elements of
\mathcal{Y}
. We write

\Delta(\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{Y_{1}\neq Y_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\text{diam}\left(Y_{1}^{+5\delta}\cap Y_{2}^{+5\delta}\right)\right).

Following Coulon, we denote by
\boldsymbol{\delta}
the hyperbolicity constant of the hyperbolic plane.

Lemma 4.10
.

[
Cou14
, Proposition 6.4]

There exist positive numbers
\delta_{0}
,
\Delta_{0}
and
\rho_{0}
that satisfy the following property. Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic space with
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a family of strongly quasi-convex subsets of
X
with
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\leq\Delta_{0}
. Let
\rho\geq\rho_{0}
. Then the cone-off
\dot{X}_{\rho}(\mathcal{Y})
is
\dot{\delta}
-hyperbolic, with
\dot{\delta}=900\boldsymbol{\delta}
.

For the remainder of this subsection, we fix a length space
X
and a family
\mathcal{Y}
as in Definition
4.9
.

Lemmas
4.12
and
4.13
provide some insight on how the metric on
\dot{X}
relates to the metric on
X
and to the cones
Z(Y)
. In order to state the first of these results, we introduce the map
\mu:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
characterized by

\cosh(\mu(t))=\cosh^{2}(\rho)-\sinh^{2}(\rho)\text{cos}\left(\min\left(\pi,\dfrac{t}{\sinh(\rho)}\right)\right)

For the remainder of Section

Proof.

We will apply Remark

. The action on

and

as the parameters

and

of Remark

.

Now, let

be such that

-quasi-geodesic segment

First of all, note that Proposition 6.1 in

∎

The main purpose of this section is to prove Proposition

Let

In order to prove Proposition

In order to apply Theorem

Definition 5.1

(Class

(2”)

If

Lemma 5.4

Let

∎

Note that among these four groups, only

Lemma 5.5

Let

Now, let

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\widehat{g}\in\text{Stab}(\widehat{v})
for some

Proposition 5.8

If
(G^{\prime},X^{\prime})\in\mathcal{ST}_{0}^{\prime}(p)

Proof.

(3’)

Since we have proved that the pair

(2”)

t
of the HNN extension, that is, the group
G^{\prime}=G^{*}/\langle t\rangle^{G^{*}}
, and call
\pi^{\star}:G^{*}\twoheadrightarrow G^{\prime}
the quotient map. Notice that
G^{\prime}
can also be obtained as the quotient of
G
by the relations
k=\alpha(k)
, that is
G^{\prime}\cong G/\langle k^{-1}\alpha(k)\,:\,k\in K\rangle^{G}
. We will identify these isomorphic groups, and call
\pi:G\twoheadrightarrow G^{\prime}
the quotient map. Note that the kernel of
\pi
is contained in
N=\langle Tr(G)\rangle
. By assumption
G
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
, thus
N
contains no involution. Hence no involution of
G
is mapped to the identity by
\pi
. Furthermore, the restriction of
\pi^{\star}
to
G
coincides with
\pi
, and every involution of
G^{*}
is conjugate to an involution of
G
. Therefore, no involution of
G^{*}
is mapped to the identity by
\pi^{\star}
. Thus, the image
\pi^{\star}(r_{1}...r_{k})
is a (trivial) product of an odd number of involutions of
G^{\prime}
. Moreover, every preimage of
\pi^{\star}(r_{1}...r_{k})
in
G
will be a product of an odd number of involutions. That is, there is an involution in
N=\langle Tr(G)\rangle
, and we arrive at a contradiction.

(3)
: the set of pairs
(r,s)\in I(G^{*})^{(2)}
of
p
-affine type is non-empty and
G^{*}
acts transitively on it.

First, note that this set is non-empty since
G
is contained in
G^{*}
. Then, if
(r,s)\in I(G^{*})^{(2)}
is of
p
-affine type then
\langle r,s\rangle
is finite, thus it is elliptic in the tree
X
. It follows that
\langle r,s\rangle
is contained in a conjugate of
G
, and the conclusion follows from the fact that
G
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
.

(4)
: for
(r,s)\in I(G^{*})^{(2)}
,
\text{Cen}(rs)
is cyclic and generated by a translation.

If
rs
is elliptic then its centralizer is elliptic in the tree
X
by Condition
(4’)
above, and the conclusion follows from the fact that
G
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
.

If
rs
is loxodromic then it is contained in a (unique) maximal loxodromic subgroup of
G^{*}
, which is isomorphic to
D_{\infty}
(indeed, exactly as in Remark
5.9
, since
G^{*}
satisfies Condition
(1)
and Condition
(4’)
, every loxodromic subgroup of
G^{*}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
or
C_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}
or
D_{\infty}
or
C_{4}\ast_{C_{2}}C_{4}
, and
D_{\infty}
is the only group in this list that contains two distinct involutions), and thus the centralizer of
rs
is cyclic infinite, generated by a translation.

It is left to verify Conditions
(1’)
–
(3’)
of Definition
2.13
of
\mathcal{ST}^{\prime}(p)
.

(1’)
: the action of
G^{*}
on
X
is acylindrical (and non-elementary).

If
G^{*}
is the HNN extension defined in Proposition
2.14
, then by Lemma
2.7
and Lemma
6.1
stabilizers of paths with at least three edges in
X
have order at most two. If
G^{*}=G\ast H
, stabilizers of edges are trivial. In both cases, one easily sees that the action of
G^{*}
on
X
is acylindrical, and the fact that the action is non-elementary is clear.

(2’)
:
e(N,X)=1
,
r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)\geq 1
,
\nu(N,X)\leq 5
and
A(N,X)=0
.

Note that since the space
X
is
0
-hyperbolic, it follows from Lemma
3.22
that
[g]^{\infty}=[g]
for all
g\in G^{*}
, and thus
r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)=\inf(\{[g]:g\in Q,\,g\ \text{loxodromic}\})
. Hence, since
X
is a simplicial tree, we immediately get
r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)\geq 1
(in fact, it can be proved that
r_{\text{inj}}(Q,X)=2
).

Then, let us prove that
\nu(N,X)\leq 5
. The parameters of the definition of an acylindrical action (see Definition
3.28
) corresponding to
\varepsilon=97\delta
can be taken to be
L=3
and
M=2
in the case where
G^{*}
is the HNN extension defined in Proposition
2.14
(since stabilizers of paths with at least three edges in
X
have order at most two), and
L=M=1
in the case where
G^{*}=G\ast H
. In both cases, the bound
\nu(N,X)\leq 5
follows readily from Lemma
4.5
.

To see that
e(N,X)=1
, by Remark
4.3
it is sufficient to prove that every loxodromic subgroup
E
of
N
is cyclic. As in the proof of Condition
(4)
, we have that
E
is isomorphic to one of the following four groups:
\mathbb{Z}
,
C_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
D_{\infty}
or
C_{4}\ast_{C_{2}}C_{4}
. But
N^{*}
has no involution (by Condition
(2)
), and thus
E
is necessarily isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
.

Last, let us prove that
A(N,X)=0
. First notice that, since our space
X
is
0
-hyperbolic, the axis of an element
g
is the set
A_{g}=\{x\in X:d(x,g\cdot x)=[g]\}
. Also, since
\nu=\nu(N,X)
is finite, we are looking at tuples
(g_{0},\dots,g_{\nu})
of elements with
[g_{i}]=0
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,\nu\}
(that is, tuples of elliptic elements) such that they do not generate an elementary subgroup. In this case,
A_{g_{i}}
is just the fixed-point set of
g_{i}
, and
A(g_{0},\dots,g_{\nu})=\text{diam}(A_{g_{0}}\cap\dots\cap A_{g_{\nu}})
is the diameter of the intersection of their fixed-point sets. If all these elliptic elements have a fixed point in common, then every element of
\langle g_{0},\dots,g_{\nu}\rangle
will also fix this point, and therefore, they would generate an elementary subgroup. That is, we only need to consider tuples
(g_{0},\dots,g_{\nu})
of elliptic elements such that
A_{g_{0}}\cap\dots\cap A_{g_{\nu}}=\varnothing
. Thus,
A(g_{0},\dots,g_{\nu})=0
, and therefore,
A(N,X)=0
.

(3’)
: translations of infinite order of
G^{*}
are loxodromic for their action on
X
.

Let
g=rs
be a translation, where
r,s
denote two distinct involutions of
G^{*}
. If
r
and
s
have a common fixed point
v\in X
, then their product
g
fixes
v
as well. Hence
g
is contained in a conjugate of
G
(namely the stabilizer of
v
in
G^{*}
), and thus it has order
p
since
G
belongs to
\mathcal{ST}(p)
. If
r
and
s
do not have a common fixed point, then
g
is loxodromic (and thus it has infinite order).

This finishes the proof of Proposition
2.14
and thus of Theorem
1.1
.∎

Appendix A
Proof of Lemma
3.10

In this appendix we include the proof of Lemma
3.10
. For the sake of completeness, we will also restate this result.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
X
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic metric space,
x,y\in X
. Let
\gamma
be a
(1,\ell)
-quasi-geodesic connecting
x
to
y
for
\ell\geq 0
. Let
p\in X
be a point such that

(1)

d(x,p)+d(p,y)\leq d(x,y)+2\delta+3\ell.

Then,
d(p,\gamma)\leq 4\delta+8\ell
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
d(x,p)\leq d(y,p)
. If we have
d(x,p)\geq d(x,y)
, then we get that
d(y,p)\leq 2\delta+3\ell
and we are done. Similarly, if we assume
d(y,p)\geq d(x,y)
, then we get that
d(x,p)\leq 2\delta+3\ell
.

Now, assume that

\max(\{d(x,p),d(y,p)\})<d(x,y).

Take a point
z\in\gamma
such that

(2)

d(p,y)\leq d(z,y)\leq d(p,y)+\ell.

Since
X
is
\delta
-hyperbolic, we have that

(3)

\langle x,y\rangle_{p}\geq\min\{\langle x,z\rangle_{p}\,,\,\langle y,z\rangle_{p}\}-\delta.

Combining Definition
3.7
with the second inequality of (
2
) we get

(4)

\langle x,z\rangle_{p}-\langle y,z\rangle_{p}=\frac{1}{2}(d(x,p)-d(x,z)-d(y,p)+d(y,z))\leq\frac{1}{2}(d(x,p)-d(x,z))+\frac{\ell}{2}.

Now, inequality (
1
) and the second inequality of (
2
) give

(5)

d(x,p)-2\delta-3l\leq d(x,y)-d(y,p)\leq d(x,y)-d(y,z)+\ell,

and this together with the triangle inequality imply

(6)

d(x,p)-2\delta-3\ell\leq d(x,z)+\ell.

Therefore, inequalities (
4
) and (
6
) yield

(7)

\langle x,z\rangle_{p}-\langle y,z\rangle_{p}\leq\delta+\frac{5}{2}\ell.

From inequalities (
3
) and (
7
) we get

(8)

\langle x,y\rangle_{p}\geq\langle x,z\rangle_{p}-\frac{5}{2}\ell-2\delta.

Inequality (
8
) together with Definition
3.7
yield

d(y,p)-d(x,y)\geq d(z,p)-d(x,z)-5\ell-4\delta,

and thus

(9)

d(y,p)-d(x,y)+d(x,z)\geq d(z,p)-5\ell-4\delta.

Also, since
z\in\gamma
we have that

d(x,z)+d(z,y)\leq d(x,y)+3\ell,

and in consequence,

(10)

d(x,z)-d(x,y)\leq-d(z,y)+3\ell.

Therefore, combining inequalities (
9
) and (
10
) we obtain

(11)

d(y,p)-d(z,y)\geq d(z,p)-8\ell-4\delta.

The first inequality of (
2
) and inequalitiy (
11
) imply that
d(z,p)\leq 8\ell+4\delta
.
∎
