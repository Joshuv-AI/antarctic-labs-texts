1.
Introduction

Let
P(z)
be a polynomial and
S(z)
be a nonzero rational function in the finite complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. Denote
h(z)=S(z)e^{P(z)}
. In this paper, we are concerned with the modulus of the solutions of the first order differential equation

(1.1)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z)=h(z)f(z)+1,\end{split}

which appears in the literature as a modification of the first order differential equation

(1.2)

\begin{split}g^{\prime}(z)=h(z)\left(g(z)-z\right).\end{split}

Equation (
1.2
) becomes equation (
1.1
) if we let
f(z)=z-g(z)
. Equation (
1.2
) arises when applying Newton’s method, namely
g(z)=z-H(z)/H^{\prime}(z)
, to find zeros of entire functions like
H(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\exp(\int_{0}^{t}P_{0}(s)e^{P(s)}ds)dt
, where
P_{0}(z)
is a polynomial. The set of all finite order meromorphic functions satisfying equations of the form in (
1.2
) is the so-called
class N
in complex dynamics
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
. In this case,
P(z)
is a constant
[
22
]
. Equation (
1.1
) also appears in uniqueness theory of meromorphic functions
[
7
,
11
,
19
]
and can be a particular reduction of a second order algebraic differential equation of Hayman
[
15
,
8
,
12
,
20
,
25
]
. By analyzing the modulus of the solution
f(z)
of equation (
1.1
), we are able to provide answers to related problems in these two areas.

From now on we assume that
P(z)
is nonconstant and has degree
n\geq 1
. By re-scaling and translating the variable
z
suitably, we shall assume that
P(z)
takes the form

(1.3)

\begin{split}P(z)=z^{n}+a_{n-2}z^{n-2}+\cdots+a_{0},\end{split}

where
a_{n-2},\cdots,a_{0}
are constants. When
n=1
, this means
P(z)=z
. We also assume that
S(z)=nz^{m}[1+O(z^{-1})]
as
z\to\infty
for some integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. All poles of
S(z)
are located in a finite open disk
D(0,\dot{r})=\{z=x+iy:|z|<\dot{r}\}
for some
\dot{r}>0
. Then the solution
f(z)
of equation (
1.1
) is analytic outside
D(0,\dot{r})
. In particular, if
S(z)
is a polynomial, then the general solutions of equation (
1.1
) are entire functions.

Throughout the paper,
\varepsilon>0
denotes a small constant. Moreover, the notation
O(1)
means the related quantity is bounded while the notation
o(1)
means the related quantity is infinitely small respectively. The main purpose of this paper is to provide a lower bound for
|f(z)|
along certain rays. We first prove the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P(z)
be a polynomial of the form in (
1.3
) and
S(z)
be a nonzero rational function. Let
\theta\in(0,\pi/2n)
be a constant. If
f(z)
is a solution of equation (
1.1
), then there is a sequence
\{r_{k}\}
such that the set
E=\cup_{l=0}^{\infty}[r_{2l},r_{2l+1}]
has infinite logarithmic measure and for all
r\in E
,

(1.4)

\begin{split}|f(re^{i\theta})|\geq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\sqrt[n]{\sin n\theta}}{n}r\exp\left(e^{(1-\varepsilon)r^{n}\cos n\theta}\sin\varepsilon\right).\end{split}

1.1.
The Strategy of the Proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
, which will be given in Section
2
, follows from an observation on the calculation of Nevanlinna’s characteristic
T(r,f)
of the entire function
e^{e^{z}}
in
[
13
, pp. 84–85]
. See also there for the standard notation and basic results of Nevanlinna theory. It is shown there, if
\phi(s)
is a bounded, non-negative and even function for real
s
and that

(1.5)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\phi(t)dt=l[1+o(1)],\quad s\to\infty,\end{split}

then

(1.6)

\begin{split}I(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{r\cos\theta}\phi(r\sin\theta)d\theta=\frac{[l+o(1)]e^{r}}{(2\pi r)^{1/2}},\quad r\to\infty.\end{split}

If we choose
\phi(s)
in (
1.5
) to be
\phi_{1}(s)=\max\{\cos s,0\}
or
\phi_{2}(s)=\max\{-\cos s,0\}
, then the estimate in (
1.6
) holds for both
\phi_{1}(s)
and
\phi_{2}(s)
. Denote the corresponding integrals by
I_{1}(r)
and
I_{2}(r)
, respectively. Since
e^{e^{z}}
has no zeros, by the first main theorem of Nevanlinna we have
\mathbf{I}(r)=I_{1}(r)-I_{2}(r)=m(r,e^{e^{z}})-m(r,e^{-e^{z}})=\log|f(0)|=1
. Basically, for any real constant
\omega
, by the Cauchy integral formula we have
\oint_{|z|=1}e^{-\omega z}z^{-1}dz=2\pi i
, giving the two identities

(1.7)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\omega\cos\theta}\cos(\omega\sin\theta)d\theta&=2\pi,\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\omega\cos\theta}\sin(\omega\sin\theta)d\theta&=0.\end{split}

In general, if the integration interval in (
1.6
) is not
[-\pi,\pi]
, then the corresponding
\mathbf{I}(r)
may not be bounded as
r\to\infty
. To provide a lower bound for
|f(z)|
, we will estimate such kind of
\mathbf{I}(r)
with integration interval being a subinterval of
[0,\pi/2]
in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, as well as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Here we look at the simplest case of equation (
1.1
), that is, the case when
h(z)=e^{z}
. In this case, by integration we may write the solution
f(z)
in (
1.1
) as

(1.8)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{e^{z}}\left(c+\int_{z_{0}}^{z}e^{-e^{t}}dt\right),\end{split}

where
c
is the integration constant and
z_{0}=x_{0}+iy_{0}
is a fixed constant. Here we choose
z_{0}=x_{0}
to be positive real. We see that
\int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}e^{-e^{t}}dt
converges to a finite nonzero constant, say
d_{0}
, along the positive real axis. Since
t^{2}e^{-e^{t}}
is decreasing on
[x,\infty)
for large
x
, we have

(1.9)

\begin{split}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{-e^{t}}dt=\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}e^{-e^{t}}dt-\int_{x}^{\infty}\left(t^{2}e^{-e^{t}}\right)t^{-2}dt=d_{0}+O\left(xe^{-e^{x}}\right),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

It follows that
f(x)=(c+d_{0})e^{e^{x}}+O(x)
as
x\to\infty
along the positive real axis. If
c+d_{0}\not=0
, then
f(z)
has sufficiently large modulus when
x
is large. However, the particular case
c+d_{0}=0
cannot be excluded out.

Let
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
be a constant. Instead of the positive real axis, we consider the ray
\Phi
on which
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
. For a point
z=x+iy\in\Phi
, we have
y=x\tan\theta
. We connect the two points
z_{0}=x_{0}
and
z_{1}=x
by a horizontal line segment
L_{0}
and the two points
z_{1}=x
and
z=x+iy
by a vertical line segment
V
respectively. Let
L
be the joint of
L_{0}
and
V
. For simplicity, denote
\mathbf{r}=e^{x}
. By the Cauchy integral theorem, we may choose the path of integration as
L
for the solution in (
1.8
) and it follows that

(1.10)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{e^{z}}\left[c+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{-e^{s}}ds+i\mathbf{H}\right],\end{split}

where

(1.11)

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}=\int_{0}^{y}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}e^{-i\mathbf{r}\sin t}dt=\mathbf{H}_{1}+i\mathbf{H}_{2}\end{split}

and

(1.12)

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{1}&=\int_{0}^{y}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt,\\
\mathbf{H}_{2}&=\int_{0}^{y}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt.\end{split}

We may write
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a large integer
\mathbf{k}
and a number
\zeta\in[-\pi/2,3\pi/2)
. Then we may write
\mathbf{H}_{1}
and
\mathbf{H}_{2}
as

(1.13)

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{1}=\mathbf{I}_{11}+\mathbf{I}_{12},\quad\mathbf{H}_{2}=\mathbf{I}_{21}+\mathbf{I}_{22},\end{split}

where

(1.14)

\begin{split}\mathbf{I}_{11}&=\int_{0}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt,\\
\mathbf{I}_{12}&=\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt\end{split}

and

(1.15)

\begin{split}\mathbf{I}_{21}&=\int_{0}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt,\\
\mathbf{I}_{22}&=\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt.\end{split}

Note that
\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{-e^{s}}ds=d_{0}+O(xe^{-e^{x}})
as
x\to\infty
by (
1.9
). So we need to estimate
\mathbf{I}_{11}
,
\mathbf{I}_{12}
,
\mathbf{I}_{21}
and
\mathbf{I}_{22}
, respectively. Note that
\cos t
is even and
\sin t
is odd and also that
\cos t
and
\sin t
are both periodic with period
2\pi
.

Consider first
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a number
\zeta\in[-\pi/2+\varepsilon,\pi/2-\varepsilon]
. For
\mathbf{I}_{11}
, by the first identity in (
1.7
), we have

(1.16)

\begin{split}\mathbf{I}_{11}=\mathbf{k}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt=2\mathbf{k}\pi.\end{split}

For
\mathbf{I}_{12}
, we have

(1.17)

\begin{split}\left|\mathbf{I}_{12}\right|=\left|\int_{0}^{\zeta}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt\right|\leq\int_{0}^{|\zeta|}\left|e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)\right|dt\leq\left|\zeta e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}\right|.\end{split}

Obviously,
\mathbf{I}_{12}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. Then by (
1.13
), (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) we see that
|\mathbf{H}_{1}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)y
. Similarly, we may use the second identity in (
1.7
) to show that
\mathbf{I}_{21}=0
and also that
|\mathbf{I}_{22}|\leq|\zeta e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}|
. Thus by (
1.13
) we have
\mathbf{H}_{2}=\mathbf{I}_{22}\to 0
as
x\to\infty
. Then we see from equations in (
1.10
)-(
1.15
) that, for each integration constant
c
, the solution
f(z)
in (
1.8
) satisfies
|f(z)|\geq(1-\varepsilon)(\sin\theta)re^{\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}
for
z=x+iy=re^{i\theta}\in\Phi
such that
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a large integer
\mathbf{k}
and a number
\zeta\in[-\pi/2+\varepsilon,\pi/2-\varepsilon]
.

Consider next
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a number
\zeta\in[\pi/2+\varepsilon,3\pi/2-\varepsilon]
. We still have
\mathbf{I}_{11}=2\mathbf{k}\pi
and
\mathbf{I}_{21}=0
. To estimate
\mathbf{I}_{12}
and
\mathbf{I}_{22}
, we consider the two cases
\zeta\in[\pi/2+\varepsilon,\pi]
and
\zeta\in[\pi,3\pi/2-\varepsilon]
, respectively. When
\zeta\in[\pi/2+\varepsilon,\pi]
, by the same arguments as before, we have

(1.18)

\begin{split}\left|\mathbf{I}_{12}\right|\leq\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta}\left|e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)\right|dt\leq\left|\zeta e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}\right|.\end{split}

On the other hand, when
\zeta\in[\pi,3\pi/2-\varepsilon]
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbf{I}_{12}=\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+2\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt-\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+2\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt,\end{split}

which together with the first identity in (
1.7
) yields

(1.19)

\begin{split}|\mathbf{I}_{12}-2\pi|\leq\left|(2\pi-\zeta)e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}\right|.\end{split}

Similarly, we may use the second identity in (
1.7
) to obtain
|\mathbf{I}_{22}|\leq|\zeta e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}|
or
|\mathbf{I}_{22}|\leq|(2\pi-\zeta)e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}|
. Since
|e^{e^{z}}|=e^{\mathbf{r}\cos\zeta}
and
\cos\zeta<0
and since
\mathbf{I}_{11}=2\mathbf{k}\pi=y-\zeta
and
\mathbf{I}_{21}=0
, by the inequalities in (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) together with similar inequalities for
|\mathbf{I}_{22}|
, we see from equations in (
1.10
)-(
1.15
) that, for each integration constant
c
, the solution
f(z)
in (
1.8
) satisfies
|f(z)|\leq 2\pi
for
z=x+iy\in\Phi
such that
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a large integer
\mathbf{k}
and a number
\zeta\in[\pi/2+\varepsilon,3\pi/2-\varepsilon]
.

On the basis of the previous estimates for
|f(z)|
of
f(z)
in (
1.8
) along the ray
\Phi
, we define a sequence
\{r_{k}\}
in the way that
r_{2l}\sin\theta=2(2l+\mathbf{k})\pi-\pi/2+\varepsilon
if
k=2l
and
r_{2l+1}\sin\theta=2(2l+\mathbf{k})\pi+\pi/2-\varepsilon
. Then the inequality in (
1.4
) holds for all
z=x+iy=re^{i\theta}
such that
r\in E=\cup_{l=0}^{\infty}[r_{2l},r_{2l+1}]
. Since
(r_{2l+1}-r_{2l})\sin\theta=\pi-2\varepsilon
, the set
E=\cup_{l=0}^{\infty}[r_{2l},r_{2l+1}]
has infinite logarithmic measure, i.e.,
\int_{E}dr/r=\infty
.

1.2.
Plan of the Paper

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we prove Theorem
1.1
in the general case of
h(z)
. For a constant
\theta\in(0,\pi/2n)
, we will consider the ray
\Phi
on which
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
. We would like to define the corresponding
\mathbf{H}
as in the simplest case of
h(z)
and estimate the modulus of
\mathbf{H}
. To this end, in Subsection
2.1
we first consider the asymptotic behavior of the function

(1.20)

\begin{split}U(z)=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}S(t)e^{P(t)}dt,\end{split}

where
z_{0}
is fixed and
|z_{0}|=r_{0}>\dot{r}
, and write
U(z)=e^{\mathbf{u}(x,y)+i\mathbf{v}(x,y)}
with two functions
\mathbf{u}(x,y)
and
\mathbf{v}(x,y)
in a carefully chosen region of the plane. In so doing, we are able to write
\mathbf{H}=\int_{\mathbf{y}_{0}}^{y}e^{-e^{\mathbf{u}(x,t)+i\mathbf{v}(x,t)}}dt
for a point
\mathbf{y}_{0}
such that
\mathbf{v}(x,\mathbf{y}_{0})=0
. We note that
\mathbf{H}
is too sensitive to the perturbation of

when

is large and
\mathbf{v}(x,t)<0
. In Subsection

we shall choose a simple smooth curve
\Omega
which is asymptotically close to the ray
z=re^{i\pi/2n}
,

and on which

for a constant
\omega>0
and also choose a family of simple smooth curves

,

on which
\mathbf{v}(x,y)=2k\pi
. Note that each

is perpendicular to
\Omega
. By integrating

along the curve
\Omega
and along each curve

respectively, we use the Cauchy integral theorem to relate the value of
\mathbf{H}
to the values of
\int_{\Omega}e^{-U(t)}dt
and
\int_{L_{k}}e^{-U(t)}dt
. In Subsection
2.3
, we estimate the value of
\int_{L_{k}}e^{-U(t)}dt
and also, using the two identities in (
1.7
), estimate the value of
\int_{\Omega}e^{-U(t)}dt
respectively. In Subsection
2.4
, we obtain estimates for

for

in most parts of
\Phi
and in particular define the desired set

. In Section

we will further look at the modulus

of

in (

) and provide a lower bound for

for

such that
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta
with a large integer
\mathbf{k}
and a number

. Finally, in Section

we illustrate the applications of the results of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem

and, meanwhile, extend the method in the proof of Theorem

to more general case.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
f(z)
be a solution of equation (
1.1
). Then, outside the disc
D(0,\dot{r})
, by integration we may write
f(z)
in the form

(2.1)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{U(z)}\left[c+\int_{z_{0}}^{z}e^{-U(t)}dt\right],\end{split}

where
U(z)
is defined in (
1.20
),
c
is the integration constant and
z_{0}=x_{0}+iy_{0}
. In the following, we always suppose that
x_{0}
is large enough. Our proof will be divided into four parts.

2.1.
Preliminaries

To give a more specialised form for
U(z)
in (
1.20
), in this subsection we first consider the asymptotic behavior of the function

(2.2)

\begin{split}G(z)=e^{-P(z)}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}S(t)e^{P(t)}dt.\end{split}

Denote
\theta_{k}=\frac{2k-1}{2n}\pi
,
k=0,1,\cdots,2n
and let

(2.3)

\begin{split}A_{k}=\left\{z=re^{i\theta}:\quad r\in[r_{0},\infty),\quad\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})\right\}.\end{split}

Note that
\theta_{2n}-\theta_{0}=2\pi
. Then along the ray
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
, if
\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
and
\cos n\theta>0
, then
\log|e^{P(z)}|
is increasing on
[r_{0},\infty)
and
|e^{P(z)}|\geq e^{r^{n}/2}
there; if
\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
and
\cos n\theta<0
, then
\log|e^{P(z)}|
is decreasing on
[r_{0},\infty)
and
|e^{P(z)}|\leq e^{-r^{n}/2}
there. See
[
2
]
and also
[
18
, Lemma 5.14]
. Moreover, as shown by Bank and Langley
[
2
]
(see also
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
), we have the following

Lemma 2.1
.

For the function
G(z)
defined in (
2.2
), there is a rational function
Q(z)
and a large integer
N
such that along the ray
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
and
\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
and
\cos n\theta<0
,

(2.4)

\begin{split}G(re^{i\theta})=Q(re^{i\theta})+c_{1}e^{-P(z)}+O\left(r^{-N}\right),\quad r\to\infty,\end{split}

where
c_{1}=c_{1}(\theta)
is a constant, while along the ray
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
and
\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
and
\cos n\theta>0
,

(2.5)

\begin{split}G(re^{i\theta})=Q(re^{i\theta})+O\left(r^{-N}\right),\quad r\to\infty.\end{split}

In the original proof of Lemma
2.1
in
[
2
]
, the error terms in the two estimates (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) take the form
O(r^{-2})
or
O(r^{-1})
, but their proof gives the error term
O(r^{-N})
. Moreover, by the Phragmén–Lindelöf theorem (see
[
16
, theorem 7.3]
), the constant
c_{1}
in (
2.4
) remains the same for any
\theta
such that
\theta\in(\theta_{k},\theta_{k+1})
and
\cos n\theta<0
.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we also need to consider the asymptotic behavior of
G(z)
along curves that are asymptotically close to the ray
z=re^{i\pi/2n}
. Here and in the following, we always assume that curves appearing are simple and smooth. Let
\omega>0
be a constant. We shall define a slightly larger domain than
A_{0}
in (
2.3
) as

(2.6)

\begin{split}A_{\omega}=\left\{z=re^{i\theta}:\left|\theta\right|\leq\frac{\pi}{2n}+\varepsilon,\quad r\in[r_{0},\infty),\quad\left|e^{P(z)}\right|>\frac{\omega}{2|z|^{2|m-n+1|}}\right\}.\end{split}

We may of course suppose that
z_{0}\in A_{\omega}
. To deal with
|e^{P(z)}|
, it will be convenient to write the polynomial
P(z)
in (
1.3
) as

(2.7)

\begin{split}P(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y).\end{split}

Note that
v(x,y)
is the
harmonic conjugate
of
u(x,y)
. Denote by
\epsilon(x)
any real quantity such that
|\epsilon(x)|=O(x^{-1})
as
x\to\infty
but does not necessarily the same in each appearance. The notation
\epsilon(y)
is understood in the same way. Then we prove the following

Lemma 2.2
.

For the function
G(z)
defined in (
2.2
), there is a rational function
Q(z)
and a large integer
N>2|m-n+1|
such that

(2.8)

\begin{split}G(re^{i\theta})=Q(re^{i\theta})+\left[c_{2}-Q(z_{0})e^{P(z_{0})}\right]e^{-P(z)}+O\left(r^{-N}\right)\end{split}

uniformly as
z=re^{i\theta}\to\infty
in
A_{\omega}
, where
c_{2}=c_{2}(\theta)
is a constant dependent on
\theta
.

Proof.

We define two sequence
\{S_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{Q_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
inductively by

\begin{split}S_{1}=\frac{S}{P^{\prime}},\quad Q_{1}=-S_{1}^{\prime},\quad\cdots,\quad S_{j+1}=\frac{Q_{j}}{P^{\prime}},\quad Q_{j+1}=(-1)^{j}S^{\prime}_{j+1},\quad j\geq 1.\end{split}

Recall that
S(z)=nz^{m}[1+O(z^{-1})]
as
z\to\infty
for some integer
m
. Then, by looking at the degree
n
of
P(z)
and the integer
m
, we see immediately that

(2.9)

\begin{split}\left|Q_{k}(z)\right|\leq B_{k}|z|^{m-kn}\end{split}

holds for a constant
B_{k}>0
provided that
|z|
is sufficiently large, say
|z|\geq r_{k}
. Take now the integral from the right-hand side of equation (
2.2
). Integration by parts results, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, in

\begin{split}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}S(t)e^{P(t)}dt&=\left[S_{1}(t)e^{P(t)}\right]_{z_{0}}^{z}+\int_{z_{0}}^{z}Q_{1}(t)e^{P(t)}dt=\cdots\\
&=\left[(S_{1}(t)+\cdots+S_{k}(t))e^{P(t)}\right]_{z_{0}}^{z}+\int_{z_{0}}^{z}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt.\end{split}

If
Q_{k}(z)\equiv 0
, we have just to define
Q=S_{1}(z)+\cdots+S_{k}(z)
. Otherwise, we select
k
large enough so that
m-kn\leq-(N+1)
for an integer
N
. Then we have to estimate
\int_{z_{0}}^{z}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt
.

Instead of integrating
Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}
along a ray from the point
z_{0}
to
z
, we might as well take a new path of integration as in
[
17
, Lemma 4.1]
, namely, we first integrate
Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}
from the point
z_{0}
to
\infty
along the ray
\Omega_{1}
on which
t=z_{0}+re^{i\pi/n}
and then come back along the ray
\Omega_{2}
on which
t=w+re^{i\pi/n}
from
\infty
to a point
w
such that
|z|=2|w|
and finally move along a curve
\Omega_{3}
from
w
to
z
. By the Cauchy–Riemann equations, we have

\begin{split}P^{\prime}(z)=u_{x}-iu_{y}=nz^{n-1}\left[1+O\left(z^{-1}\right)\right],\end{split}

where
u_{x}
and
u_{y}
are the partial derivatives of
u=u(x,y)
with respect to
x
and
y
respectively. By comparing the real and imaginary parts on both sides of the above equation, we find

\begin{split}u_{x}&=[1+\epsilon(x)]\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})+\epsilon(x)\text{Im}(nz^{n-1}),\\
u_{y}&=\epsilon(x)\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})-[1+\epsilon(x)]\text{Im}(nz^{n-1}).\end{split}

For the point
z=re^{i\theta}\in\Omega_{3}
, we may write
nz^{n-1}=nr^{n-1}e^{i(n-1)\theta}=nr^{n-1}\cos(n-1)\theta+inr^{n-1}\sin(n-1)\theta
. In particular, when
n=1
, by the expression of
P(z)
in (
1.3
) we have
u(x,y)=x
and
v(x,y)=y
and thus
u_{x}=1
and
u_{y}=0
; when
n\geq 2
, we see that
u_{x}>0
and
u_{y}<0
when
|z|
is large. Thus, on the curve
\Omega_{3}
we may always suppose that
e^{P(z)}=e^{u(x,y)}e^{iv(x,y)}
has the maximum modulus at the point
z
. Then, by the previous observation on the asymptotic behavior of
|e^{P(z)}|
, we see that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt=c_{2}(\theta)\end{split}

converges. Note that
c_{2}(\theta)
tends to a fixed constant as
z\to\infty
in
A_{\omega}
. Thus we have

(2.10)

\begin{split}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt=c_{2}(\theta)-\int_{\Omega_{3}}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt.\end{split}

By (
2.9
) and the choice of
k
we see that

(2.11)

\begin{split}\left|Q_{k}(z)e^{P(z)}\right|\leq B_{k}\left|e^{P(z)}\right||z|^{-(N+1)}\end{split}

for all
|z|
sufficiently large. Define again
Q=S_{1}(z)+\cdots+S_{k}(z)
. By the choice of
\Omega_{3}
, we see that the length of the curve
\Omega_{3}
is always of type
O(r)
, uniformly for all
z\in A_{\omega}
. Thus, by (
2.11
), we have

(2.12)

\begin{split}\left|\int_{\Omega_{3}}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt\right|\leq B_{k}\left|e^{P(z)}\right|O\left(|z|^{-N}\right)\leq C_{k}\left|e^{P(z)}\right|r^{-N}\end{split}

for all large
r
and some constant
C_{k}
. Thus we have (
2.8
) by (
2.10
) and (
2.12
) together with the definition of
G(z)
in (
2.2
). This completes the proof.
∎

In the following we will consider the solution in (
2.1
) along a ray
\Phi
on which
z=re^{i\theta}
,
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
and
\theta\in(0,\pi/2n)
or along some curve
\Omega
which is asymptotically close to the ray
z=re^{i\pi/2n}
. Both
\Phi
or
\Omega
are contained in
A_{\omega}
. By Lemma
2.2
, the function
G(z)
defined in (
2.2
) can be written, for
z=re^{i\theta}
in either
\Phi
or
\Omega
, as

\begin{split}G(z)=Q(z)+c_{3}e^{-P(z)}+H(z),\end{split}

where
c_{3}=c_{2}-Q(z_{0})e^{P(z_{0})}
is a constant and

\begin{split}H(z)=e^{-P(z)}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}Q_{k}(t)e^{P(t)}dt\end{split}

satisfies
H(z)=O(r^{-N})
as
z\to\infty
along either
\Phi
or
\Omega
. By the definition
U(z)=e^{P(z)}G(z)
, we may rewrite the integration constant
c
in (
2.1
) as
ce^{-c_{3}}
. For any
\theta
, this redefinition does not affect the estimation of
|f(z)|
in the following. Now, by our assumptions on
P(z)
and
S(z)
, we see from Lemma
2.2
that

(2.13)

\begin{split}G(z)=z^{m-n+1}\left[1+\frac{Q(z)-z^{m-n+1}}{z^{m-n+1}}+\frac{H(z)}{z^{m-n+1}}\right]=z^{m-n+1}\left[1+O\left(z^{-1}\right)\right]\end{split}

along the aforementioned
\Phi
or
\Omega
. By redefining the integration constant
c
in (
2.1
), we may write
U(z)
in (
1.20
) uniformly as

\begin{split}U(z)=e^{P(z)}G(z)=e^{P(z)+R(z)}\end{split}

where
R(z)
is chosen as an analytic branch of the function
\ln G(z)=\ln(Q(z)+H(z))
. Similar as for the expression of
P(z)
in (
2.7
), by (
2.13
) we may also write
R(z)
as

(2.14)

\begin{split}R(z)&=p(x,y)+iq(x,y)\\
&=(m-n+1)\ln\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}+\epsilon(y)+i\left[(m-n+1)\arctan\frac{y}{x}+\epsilon(y)\right].\end{split}

Also,
q(x,y)
is the harmonic conjugate of
p(x,y)
. Denote
\mathbf{u}(x,y)=u(x,y)+p(x,y)
and
\mathbf{v}(x,y)=v(x,y)+q(x,y)
for simplicity. By the definition of
A_{\omega}
in (
2.6
), we may define
U(z)=e^{\mathbf{u}(x,y)+i\mathbf{v}(x,y)}
around a point
z=x+iy\in\Phi
and then extend the definition to the whole
A_{\omega}
by analytic continuation. Thus, in
A_{\omega}
, we may always write
U(z)
as

(2.15)

\begin{split}U(z)=e^{u(x,y)+p(x,y)+i[v(x,y)+q(x,y)]}=e^{\mathbf{u}(x,y)}\cos(\mathbf{v}(x,y))+ie^{\mathbf{u}(x,y)}\sin(\mathbf{v}(x,y)).\end{split}

Below we shall also use the suppressed notation
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(x,y)
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(x,y)
for simplicity.

Suppose that
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
have been defined. We look at
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
more carefully. By (
2.7
) and (
2.14
), along the ray
\Phi
we have

(2.16)

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}=\frac{\cos n\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n}}y^{n}\left[1+\epsilon(y)+O(y^{-n}\ln y)\right]\end{split}

and, along the ray
\Phi
and the curve
\Omega
, we have

(2.17)

\begin{split}\mathbf{v}=\frac{\sin n\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n}}y^{n}\left[1+\epsilon(y)\right]\end{split}

Note that
\theta
is fixed as
z\to\infty
along the curve
\Phi
while
\theta
is not necessarily fixed as
z\to\infty
along the curve
\Omega
. Also note that
\theta\to\pi/2n
as
z\to\infty
along the curve
\Omega
.

Denote by
\mathbf{u}_{y}
and
\mathbf{v}_{y}
the partial derivatives of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
with respect to
y
respectively. We give asymptotic expressions for
\mathbf{v}_{y}
along the ray
\Phi
and the curve
\Omega
. By the two expressions in (
1.20
) and (
2.15
) for
U(z)
, it is elementary to take the first derivative of
U(z)
and obtain

(2.18)

\begin{split}S(z)e^{P(z)}=e^{\mathbf{u}}\left[\mathbf{v}_{y}\cos\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}_{y}\sin\mathbf{v}+i(\mathbf{v}_{y}\sin\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}_{y}\cos\mathbf{v})\right].\end{split}

Moreover, by (
2.13
) we have

(2.19)

\begin{split}S(z)e^{P(z)}=\frac{S(z)}{G(z)}\left[e^{P(z)}G(z)\right]=\left[1+O\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)\right]nz^{n-1}e^{\mathbf{u}+i\mathbf{v}},\quad z\to\infty.\end{split}

Note that
1+O(z^{-1})=1+\epsilon(y)+i\epsilon(y)
. We compare the real and imaginary parts on both sides of the two equations in (
2.18
) and (
2.19
) and find

(2.20)

\begin{split}\mathbf{v}_{y}\cos\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}_{y}\sin\mathbf{v}=\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})&\left\{\left[1+\epsilon(y)\right]\cos\mathbf{v}+\epsilon(y)\sin\mathbf{v}\right\}\\
&-\text{Im}(nz^{n-1})\left\{\left[1+\epsilon(y)\right]\sin\mathbf{v}+\epsilon(y)\cos\mathbf{v}\right\}\end{split}

and

(2.21)

\begin{split}\mathbf{v}_{y}\sin\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{u}_{y}\cos\mathbf{v}=\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})&\left\{\left[1+\epsilon(y)\right]\sin\mathbf{v}+\epsilon(y)\cos\mathbf{v}\right\}\\
&+\text{Im}(nz^{n-1})\left\{\left[1+\epsilon(y)\right]\cos\mathbf{v}+\epsilon(y)\sin\mathbf{v}\right\}.\end{split}

We multiply by
\sin\mathbf{v}
on both sides of (
2.20
) and by
\cos\mathbf{v}
on both sides of (
2.21
) and then make subtraction on both sides of the resulting two equations to obtain

(2.22)

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}_{y}=\epsilon(y)\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})-[1+\epsilon(y)]\text{Im}(nz^{n-1}).\end{split}

Similarly, we multiply by
\cos\mathbf{v}
on both sides of (
2.20
) and by
\sin\mathbf{v}
on both sides of (
2.21
) and then make addition on both sides of the resulting two equations to obtain

(2.23)

\begin{split}\mathbf{v}_{y}=[1+\epsilon(y)]\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})+\epsilon(y)\text{Im}(nz^{n-1}).\end{split}

Along the curve
\Phi
and the curve
\Omega
, we may give explicit expressions for
\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})
and
\text{Im}(nz^{n-1})
as

(2.24)

\begin{split}\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})&=n\frac{\cos(n-1)\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n-1}}y^{n-1},\\
\text{Im}(nz^{n-1})&=n\frac{\sin(n-1)\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n-1}}y^{n-1}.\end{split}

Then from (
2.22
), (
2.23
) and (
2.24
) we see that along the ray
\Phi
, as well as along the curve
\Omega
, we always have

(2.25)

\begin{split}\mathbf{v}_{y}=\text{Re}(nz^{n-1})[1+\epsilon(y)]=\frac{n\cos(n-1)\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n-1}}y^{n-1}[1+\epsilon(y)]\end{split}

and also that

(2.26)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}}{\mathbf{v}_{y}}=\epsilon(y)\end{split}

when
n=1
and

(2.27)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}}{\mathbf{v}_{y}}=-\frac{\sin(n-1)\theta}{\cos(n-1)\theta}[1+\epsilon(y)]\end{split}

when
n\geq 2
. Here we note that, along the ray
\Phi
, in the estimates in (
2.20
)-(
2.27
), we may replace
y
by
y=x\tan\theta
and, in particular, replace the quantities
\epsilon(y)
there by
\epsilon(x)
. Along the curve
\Omega
, we may also do this when
n\geq 2
, but this is not allowed when
n=1
.

For clarity, in the following subsections we shall distinguish the points in
\Phi
and
\Omega
by particularly writing
z=re^{i\vartheta}
for the point
z
in the curve
\Omega
which will be defined later. Moreover, we shall use the notations

(2.28)

\begin{split}\iota&=\frac{\sin(n-1)\theta}{\cos(n-1)\theta},\qquad\iota_{1}=\frac{n\cos(n-1)\theta}{(\sin\theta)^{n-1}},\\
\kappa&=\frac{\sin(n-1)\vartheta}{\cos(n-1)\vartheta},\qquad\kappa_{1}=\frac{n\cos(n-1)\vartheta}{(\sin\vartheta)^{n-1}}\end{split}

in both of the two cases
n=1
and
n\geq 2
. Note that we always have
\iota>0
and
\kappa>0
when

.

2.2.
Definition of the path of integration

From now on, we fix a constant
\theta\in(0,\pi/2n)
. Then we define the path of integration for
f(z)
in (
2.1
) as the ray
\Phi
on which

\begin{split}z=x+iy,\quad y=x\tan\theta,\quad x\in[x_{0},\infty).\end{split}

In addition to
\Phi
, we also define the curves
\Psi_{k}
and
\Omega
as follows. By the definition of
\mathbf{v}
, for each integer
k\geq 0

\Psi_{k}

. Also, for the constant
\omega>0
in

, there is a curve
\Omega

\begin{split}z=x+iy,\quad e^{\mathbf{u}(x,y)}=\omega,\quad x\in[x_{0},\infty).\end{split}

By substituting

,

along the curve

\Phi
,
\Psi_{k}
and
\Omega
all lie entirely in
A_{\omega}
. It is elementary that each
\Psi_{k}
is perpendicular to
\Omega
. By analytic continuation, we may suppose that the curve
\Psi_{k}
with the curve
\Omega
at the unique point

,

For clarity, for a fixed point

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=\mathbf{u}(s,t)

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{v}(s,t)

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}(x,y)
and
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}(x,y)

and

. We connect the point

. By the definition of
\mathbf{v}
together with the expressions in (

) and (

as

along the curve
\Psi_{0}
. We claim that

intersects with each

\mathbf{v}
along
\Phi
and
\Omega

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{s}+\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t}\frac{dt}{ds}=0.\end{split}

\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}

(2.30)

\begin{split}\frac{dt}{ds}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{t}}{\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{t}}.\end{split}

In particular, along the part of the curve
\Psi_{k}
between
\Omega

in the right of

in (

\Psi_{k}
. Thus the ray
\Phi
intersects with each curve
\Psi_{k}
at some unique point and also that

intersects with each curve
\Psi_{k}

for

and some integer
\mathbf{k}
dependent on

.

Here we note that, for the integer
\mathbf{k}
, if

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}-1}
and
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}

(2.31)

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}(x,y)-\mathbf{u}(x,\tilde{y})=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}}{\mathbf{v}_{y}}\left[\mathbf{v}(x,y)-\mathbf{v}(x,\tilde{y}\right]\left[1+\epsilon(x)\right].\end{split}

In fact, denoting
\Delta y=y-\tilde{y}
, by Lagrange’s mean value theorem, there are two constants
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in(0,1)
such that

(2.32)

\begin{split}\mathbf{u}(x,y)-\mathbf{u}(x,\tilde{y})&=\mathbf{u}_{y}(x,y+\delta_{1}\Delta y)\Delta y,\\
\mathbf{v}(x,y)-\mathbf{v}(x,\tilde{y})&=\mathbf{v}_{y}(x,y+\delta_{2}\Delta y)\Delta y.\end{split}

By the two estimates in (
2.26
) and (
2.27
) for
\mathbf{u}_{y}/\mathbf{v}_{y}
, we may write

(2.33)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}(x,y+\delta_{1}\Delta y)}{\mathbf{v}_{y}(x,y+\delta_{2}\Delta y)}=\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}(x,y)}{\mathbf{v}_{y}(x,y)}[1+\epsilon(x)]\end{split}

in both two cases

and
n\geq 2
. Then the relation in (
2.31

2.32
) and (
2.33
). In particular, when
n=1
, by (
2.26
) we have
\mathbf{u}(x,y)-\mathbf{u}(x,\tilde{y})=\epsilon(x)
.

For each
k=0,1,\cdots,\mathbf{k}
, we denote by
L_{k}
the part of
\Psi_{k}
between the point
z_{k}
and the point
\mathbf{z}_{k}
and by
\Omega_{k}
the part of
\Omega
between the two points

and
z_{k}
, respectively. We integrate
e^{-U(z)}
along the curve
L_{k}
and the curve
\Omega_{k}
respectively and denote

(2.34)

\begin{split}\mathbf{F}_{k}=\int_{L_{k}}e^{-U(z)}dz\end{split}

and

(2.35)

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{k}=\int_{\Omega_{k}}e^{-U(z)}dz.\end{split}

In particular, let
L
be the joint of
L_{0}
and
V
. Then, along the curve
L
, by the Cauchy integral theorem, we may write the solution in (
2.1
) as

(2.36)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{U(z)}\left(c+\mathbf{F}_{0}+i\mathbf{H}\right),\end{split}

&=\mathbf{v}_{y}(\tilde{x}_{2l}+\delta_{3}\Delta x,\tilde{y}_{2l}+\delta_{4}\Delta y)\left[1-\frac{1}{\tan\theta}\frac{\mathbf{u}_{y}(\tilde{x}_{2l}+\delta_{3}\Delta x,\tilde{y}_{2l}+\delta_{4}\Delta y)}{\mathbf{v}_{y}(\tilde{x}_{2l}+\delta_{3}\Delta x,\tilde{y}_{2l}+\delta_{4}\Delta y)}\right]\Delta y.\end{split}

in (
2.25

\mathbf{u}_{y}/\mathbf{v}_{y}
in (
2.26
) and (
2.27

2.89
), we obtain

\begin{split}\Delta y=\frac{(\pi-2\varepsilon)(\sin n\theta)^{1-1/n}\tan\theta}{n[\tan\theta+\tan(n-1)\theta]\cos(n-1)\theta}\cdot\frac{1+\epsilon(\tilde{y}_{2l})}{[2(2l+\mathbf{k})\pi-\pi/2+\varepsilon]^{1-1/n}}\end{split}

in both of the two cases

and

. Then the set

has infinite logarithmic measure, i.e.,

z_{2}=x+i(2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi)
by a horizontal line segment
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and then connect the two points
z_{2}=x+i(2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi)
and
z=x+iy
by a vertical line segment
\mathcal{V}_{2}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be the joint of
\mathcal{V}_{1}
,
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
. Then, by the Cauchy integral theorem, along the curve
\mathcal{L}
, we have

(3.31)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{e^{z}}\left[c+i\int_{0}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi}e^{-e^{x_{0}}e^{it}}dt+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{e^{s}}ds+i(\mathbf{H}_{5}+i\mathbf{H}_{6})\right],\end{split}

where

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{5}&=\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi+\eta}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt=\int_{0}^{\eta}e^{\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt,\\
\mathbf{H}_{6}&=\int_{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi}^{2\mathbf{k}\pi+\pi+\eta}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin\left(-\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt=\int_{0}^{\eta}e^{\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin\left(\mathbf{r}\sin t\right)dt.\end{split}

We may also prove Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
3.1
for the simplest case of
h(z)
by estimating the terms in (
3.31
). Denote
\mathbf{r}_{0}=e^{x_{0}}
for simplicity. By comparing the expression in (
3.31
) with the one in (
1.10
) together with the two identities in (
1.7
), we find

(3.32)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\cos(\mathbf{r}\sin t)dt&=\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}_{0}\cos t}\cos(\mathbf{r}_{0}\sin t)dt),\\
\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}\cos t}\sin(\mathbf{r}\sin t)dt&=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\left(e^{e^{s}}-e^{-e^{s}}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-\mathbf{r}_{0}\cos t}\sin(\mathbf{r}_{0}\sin t)dt.\end{split}

Note that
\int_{-\infty}^{x}(e^{e^{s}}-e^{-e^{s}})ds=2\int_{0}^{\mathbf{r}}(\sinh t)t^{-1}dt
converges. By the Cauchy integral theorem, we may let
x_{0}\to-\infty
and thus
\mathbf{r}_{0}\to 0
in (
3.32
).

4.
Concluding remarks

In this paper, by proving Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
3.1
we provide lower bounds for
|f(z)|
of the solution of equation (
1.1
) along certain rays. For a function
f(z)
which is analytic outside the disc
D(0,\dot{r})
, we define the
order
of growth
\sigma(f)
of
f(z)
as

\begin{split}\sigma(f)=\limsup_{|z|\to\infty}\frac{\log\log|f(z)|}{\log|z|}.\end{split}

If
\sigma(f)=\infty
, then we define the
hyper-order

\varsigma(f)
of
f(z)
as

\begin{split}\varsigma(f)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log\log|f(z)|}{\log|z|}.\end{split}

Now, if
f(z)
is a meromorphic solution of equation (
1.1
), then by Theorem
1.1
we have
\varsigma(f)\geq\sigma(h)
. On the other hand, using the Wiman–Valiron theory (see
[
14
]
), it can be shown that
\varsigma(f)\leq\sigma(h)

[
25
]
. Thus
\varsigma(f)=\sigma(h)
. With this fact, below we look at two problems in uniqueness theory of meromorphic functions and an equation of Hayman mentioned in the introduction, respectively.

First, equation (
1.1
) is related to a conjecture posed by Brück
[
7
]
:
Let
f(z)
be an entire function which is not constant. If the hyper-order
\varsigma(f)<\infty
and
\varsigma(f)\not\in\mathbb{N}
, and if
f(z)
and
f^{\prime}(z)
share one value
a
CM, then
f^{\prime}(z)-a=c(f(z)-a)
for some constant
c\in\mathbb{C}
.
Here we say that
f(z)
and
f^{\prime}(z)
share the value
a
CM if
f(z)-a
and
f^{\prime}(z)-a
have the same zeros, counting multiplicities. Brück himself proved that his conjecture is ture if the shared value
a=0
or if
f^{\prime}(z)
has relatively few zeros in the sense of Nevanlinna theory, i.e.
N(r,1/f^{\prime})=o(T(r,f))
,
r\to\infty
. Gundersun and Yang
[
11
]
proved that Brück’s conjecture holds when
f
has finite order.

Under the assumptions of Brück’s conjecture, we have
f^{\prime}(z)-a=e^{\varphi(z)}(f(z)-a)
, where
\varphi(z)
is an entire function. When
a\not=0
, if we let
g(z)=(f(z)-a)/a
, then
g(z)
satisfies the first order differential equation

(4.1)

\begin{split}g^{\prime}(z)=e^{\varphi(z)}g(z)+1.\end{split}

By using the lemma on the logarithmic derivative and the first main theorem of Nevanlinna, we deduce from (
4.1
) that

\begin{split}T(r,e^{\varphi})=m(r,e^{\varphi})&\leq T(r,a/g)+m(r,g^{\prime}/g)+O(1)\\
&\leq T(r,g)+O(\log rT(r,g))+O(1)\leq 2T(r,g),\end{split}

where
r\to\infty
outside a set of finite linear measure. By removing this exceptional set using Borel’s lemma
[
18
, Lemma 1.1.1]
and then applying the Carathéodory inequality (see
[
19
, pp. 66-67]
) to
e^{\varphi(z)}
, we may show that
\varphi(z)
is of finite order. By Theorem
1.1
, if
\varphi(z)
is a nonconstant polynomial, then
f(z)
must be of infinite order and the hyper-order of
f(z)
is equal to the degree of
\varphi(z)
, contradicting with the assumptions of Brück’s conjecture. Thus we have the following

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f(z)
be a nonconstant entire function such that the hyper-order
\varsigma(f)<\infty
and
\varsigma(f)\not\in\mathbb{N}
. If
f(z)
and
f^{\prime}(z)
share the value
a
CM, then
f^{\prime}(z)-a=e^{\varphi(z)}(f(z)-a)
, where
\varphi(z)
is a constant or a transcendental entire function of finite order.

Second, equation (
1.1
) can be a particular reduction of a second order differential equation of Hayman
[
15
]
, i.e.,

(4.2)

f^{\prime\prime}(z)f(z)-f^{\prime}(z)^{2}+\alpha_{1}(z)f^{\prime}(z)f(z)+\alpha_{2}(z)f(z)^{2}=\beta_{1}(z)f(z)+\beta_{2}(z)f^{\prime}(z)+\beta_{3}(z),

where the coefficients
\alpha_{1}(z),\alpha_{2}(z),\beta_{1}(z),\beta_{2}(z),\beta_{3}(z)
are rational functions. The
transcendental
meromorphic solutions
f(z)
of (
4.2
) satisfy a list of first order or second order linear differential equations
[
25
]
. In particular, when
\alpha_{1}(z)
and
\alpha_{2}(z)
both vanish identically, all transcendental meromorphic solutions of equation (
4.2
) are exponential type functions
[
8
,
12
]
. The order of growth of
f(z)
can be determined explicitly, except those satisfying the first order differential equation

(4.3)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z)=h(z)f(z)+\gamma_{1}(z),\end{split}

where
\gamma_{1}(z)
is a rational function and
h(z)
is a meromorphic solution of the first order differential equation

(4.4)

\begin{split}h^{\prime}(z)=\gamma_{2}(z)h(z)+\gamma_{3}(z),\end{split}

where
\gamma_{2}(z),\gamma_{3}(z)
are rational functions
[
20
,
25
]
. We may suppose without loss of generality that
\gamma_{1}(z)
is identically equal to
0
or
1
. If
h(z)
is a rational function, then
\sigma(f)
is an integer; if
h(z)
is transcendental and
\gamma_{1}(z)\equiv 0
, then
\varsigma(f)=\sigma(h)
is an integer
[
25
]
. Now, if
h(z)
is transcendental,
\gamma_{1}(z)\equiv 1
and
\gamma_{3}(z)\equiv 0
, then by Theorem
1.1
we have
\varsigma(f)=\sigma(h)
. It is not known if
\varsigma(f)=\sigma(h)
when
\gamma_{1}(z)\equiv 1
and
\gamma_{3}(z)\not\equiv 0
.

Here we consider the autonomous case of equations (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), i.e.,
f^{\prime}(z)=h(z)f(z)+c_{3}
and
h^{\prime}(z)=c_{1}h(z)+c_{2}
with three constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
. If
c_{1}=0
, then
h(z)
is a constant or a linear polynomial and it follows that
f(z)
has order
1
or
2
. If
c_{1}\not=0
, we may write
h(z)=c_{4}e^{c_{1}z}-c_{2}/c_{1}
for a constant
c_{4}
and thus
f^{\prime}(z)=(c_{4}e^{c_{1}z}-c_{2}/c_{1})f(z)+c_{3}
. If
c_{4}=0
, then
\sigma(f)=1
; if
c_{4}\not=0
and
c_{3}=0
, then
\varsigma(f)=1
. When
c_{3}c_{4}\not=0
, by suitable translation and re-scaling, we may suppose that
c_{3}=c_{4}=1
, i.e.

(4.5)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}(z)=(e^{z}-c_{5})f(z)+1,\end{split}

where
c_{5}
is a constant. If
c_{5}=0
, then by Theorem
1.1
we have
\varsigma(f)=1
. Below we consider the case when
c_{5}\not=0
. We write
c_{5}=a+ib
with two real constants
a,b
such that
|a|+|b|\not=0
.

If
c_{5}
is a positive integer, say
c_{5}=p\geq 1
, then it is easy to check that equation (
4.5
) has a special solution

\begin{split}f_{p}(z)=-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{(p-1)!}{(p-k)!}e^{-kz}.\end{split}

Then all other solutions of equation (
4.5
) take the form
f(z)=f_{p}(z)+g(z)
, where
g(z)
is a nontrivial solution of the equation
g(z)^{\prime}=(e^{z}-c_{5})g(z)
. We see that
g(z)
have hyper-order
\varsigma(g)=1
.

If
c_{5}
is not a positive integer, we shall show that
\varsigma(f)=1
by generalizing the idea in the introduction. For any real constant
\omega
, we consider the complex line integral

(4.6)

\begin{split}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{-\omega z}z^{a+ib-1}dz=:C_{\omega}.\end{split}

In general,
C_{\omega}
is dependent on
\omega
. In particular, when
a+ib
is a negative integer, say
a+ib=-q
, it is elementary that
C_{\omega}=i2\pi(-\omega)^{q}/(q!)
.

Along the curve
L
defined in the introduction, by the Cauchy integral theorem, we may write the solution of the equation
f^{\prime}(z)=(e^{z}-a-ib)f(z)+1
as

(4.7)

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{e^{z}-(a+ib)z}\left[c+\int_{0}^{x}e^{-e^{t}+at+ibt}dt+i\mathbf{H}\right],\end{split}

where
c
is the integration constant and

(4.8)

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}=e^{(a+ib)x}\int_{0}^{y}e^{-e^{x}\cos t-ie^{x}\sin t}e^{(a+ib)it}dt.\end{split}

By the same arguments as in the derivation in (
1.9
), we may show that

(4.9)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-e^{t}+at}e^{ibt}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-e^{t}+at}e^{ibt}dt-\int_{e^{x}}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{a-1}s^{ib}ds=\tilde{d}_{0}+O\left(e^{ax}e^{-e^{x}}\right),\end{split}

where
\tilde{d}_{0}
is a constant. If
c+\tilde{d}_{0}\not=0
, then we see that
\varsigma(f)=1
by choosing
y=0
. If
c+\tilde{d}_{0}=0
, we let
\omega=e^{x}
. We choose
y=2\mathbf{k}\pi
for a positive integer
\mathbf{k}
. Then we compare the integrals in (
4.8
) and (
4.6
) and find that

(4.10)

\begin{split}i\mathbf{H}=e^{(a+ib)x}C_{\omega}\mathbf{k}=i\frac{2\pi(-1)^{q}}{q!}\mathbf{k}\end{split}

when
a+ib=-q
is a negative integer, and

(4.11)

\begin{split}i\mathbf{H}=e^{(a+ib)x}C_{\omega}\sum_{k=1}^{\mathbf{k}}e^{i2(k-1)\pi(a+ib)}=e^{(a+ib)x}C_{\omega}\frac{e^{i2\mathbf{k}\pi(a+ib)}-1}{e^{i2\pi(a+ib)}-1}\end{split}

when
a+ib
is not a nonzero integer. In the latter case, we may choose the integer
\mathbf{k}
so that
\mathbf{k}(a+ib)
is not an integer. When
\omega=e^{x}
is large, we choose an integer
k
so that
k+a>0
and apply the integration by parts
k
times and find

(4.12)

\begin{split}C_{\omega}=\left[\frac{e^{-\omega z}z^{a+ib}}{a+ib}+\cdots+\frac{\omega^{k}e^{-\omega z}z^{a+k+ib}}{(a+ib)\cdots(a+k+ib)}\right]_{e^{i0}}^{e^{2i\pi}}+W(z),\end{split}

where

(4.13)

\begin{split}W(z)=\frac{\omega^{k+1}}{{(a+ib)(a+1+ib)\cdots(a+k+ib)}}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{-\omega z}z^{a+k+ib}dz.\end{split}

For the integral in (
4.13
), by the Cauchy integral theorem, it is easy to show that

(4.14)

\begin{split}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{-\omega z}z^{a+k+ib}dz&=\left[1-e^{i2\pi(a+k+ib)}\right]\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\omega t}t^{a+k+ib}dt.\end{split}

Thus we have

(4.15)

\begin{split}\oint_{|z|=1}e^{-\omega z}z^{a+k+ib}dz&=\frac{1-e^{i2\pi(a+k+ib)}}{\omega^{a+k+1+ib}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{a+k+ib}ds-\int_{\omega}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{a+k+ib}ds\right]\\
&=\frac{1-e^{i2\pi(k+a+ib)}}{\omega^{a+k+1+ib}}\left[\Gamma(a+k+1+ib)-\int_{\omega}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{a+k+ib}ds\right],\end{split}

where
\Gamma(z)
represents the
gamma function of Euler
; see
[
10
, Chapter 15]
. The gamma function of Euler is meromorphic and never vanishes in the plane and, in particular, is analytic in the right half-plane. Then, by the same arguments as in the derivation in (
1.9
), we obtain from (
4.12
)-(
4.15
) that

(4.16)

\begin{split}C_{\omega}=\frac{\Gamma(a+k+1+ib)}{{(a+ib)(a+1+ib)\cdots(a+k+ib)}}\frac{1-e^{i2\pi(a+ib)}}{e^{(a+ib)x}}+O\left(e^{(a+k+1)x}e^{-e^{x}}\right).\end{split}

By (
4.7
)-(
4.11
) and (
4.16
), we see that, for each integration constant
c
, the solution in (
4.7
) satisfies
|f(z)|\geq\xi e^{e^{x}}e^{-ax+b2\mathbf{k}\pi}
for all large
x
and some constant
\xi
dependent on
a+ib
and
\mathbf{k}
. This yields
\varsigma(f)=1
, provided that
a+ib
is not a positive integer. Together with
[
25
, Theorem 2.1]
, this gives a complete description on the order or hyper-order for transcendental entire solutions
f(z)
of equation (
4.2
) in the autonomous case:
If
f(z)
has finite order, then
\sigma(f)=1
or
\sigma(f)=2
and, if
f(z)
has infinite order, then
\varsigma(f)=1
.

Finally, we give some more comments on equation (
4.5
). If
c_{5}=0
, we have equation (
1.1
) and the solution
f(z)
in (
1.8
) takes the form

\begin{split}f(z)=e^{e^{x}\cos y}e^{ie^{x}\sin y}\left[c+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{-e^{t}}dt+i\mathbf{H}\right],\end{split}

where
\mathbf{H}
is defined in (
1.11
). Recall that
\int_{x_{0}}^{x}e^{-e^{t}}dt=d_{0}+O\left(xe^{-e^{x}}\right)
for a constant
d_{0}
. Along the line
\Upsilon_{\mathbf{k},1}
on which
z=x+i(2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta)
such that
\zeta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, the calculations in the introduction actually shows that
|\mathbf{H}-2\mathbf{k}\pi|=|\mathbf{I}_{12}+i\mathbf{I}_{22}|\leq 2|\zeta|e^{-e^{x}\cos\zeta}
. We may choose the integer
\mathbf{k}
so that
c+d_{0}+i2\mathbf{k}\pi
is nonzero and thus
|f(z)|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|c+d_{0}+i2\mathbf{k}\pi|e^{e^{x}\cos\zeta}
. On the other hand, along the line
\Upsilon_{\mathbf{k},2}
on which
z=x+i(2\mathbf{k}\pi+\zeta)
such that
\zeta\in[\pi/2+\varepsilon,3\pi/2-\varepsilon]
, by Theorem
3.1
we have
|f(z)|\geq\xi/2e^{x}\cos(\zeta-\pi)
. This implies that all zeros, except finitely many, of the solution
f(z)
in (
1.8
) in the right half-plane must be around the lines
\Upsilon_{k,3}
on which
z=x+i(2k+1)\pi/2
,
k=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\cdots
. It is an easy work to extend this result to the left half-plane. If
c_{5}\not=0
, then by equation (
4.6
), we have the two identities

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\omega\cos\theta-b\theta}\cos(-\omega\sin\theta+a\theta)d\theta&=C_{\omega,1},\\
\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-\omega\cos\theta-b\theta}\sin(-\omega\sin\theta+a\theta)d\theta&=C_{\omega,2},\end{split}

where

and
C_{\omega,2}
are two real constants. This generalizes the two identities in (
1.7
). With these two identities, we may estimate the modulus
|f(z)|
of the solution
f(z)
of equation (
4.5
) along lines parallel to the real axis as above.

Further, we take the derivatives on both sides of equation (
4.5
) and obtain the second order differential equation
f^{\prime\prime}(z)-(e^{z}-c_{5})f^{\prime}(z)-e^{z}f(z)=0
. If we let

, then

satisfies the second order differential equation

(4.17)

\begin{split}w^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left[-\frac{1}{4}e^{2z}+\frac{1}{2}(c_{5}-1)e^{z}-\frac{1}{4}c_{5}^{2}\right]w(z)=0.\end{split}

The method by Bank and Langley
[
2
]
(see also
[
18
, Chapter 5]
) can be used to detect whether

has relatively few zeros in the sense that the exponent of convergence of zeros of

satisfies

[

,

. These results have applications in describing the oscillating phenomenon of the general second order linear differential equation
[

,
6
,

. We note that equation (

in (
