Abstract
. In this paper, we consider the degenerate semi-linear Schrödinger and Korteweg-deVries equations in one spatial dimension. We construct special solutions of the two models, namely standing wave solutions of NLS and traveling waves, which turn out to have compact support, compactons. We show that the compactons are unique bell-shaped solutions of the corresponding PDE’s and for appropriate variational problems as well. We provide a complete spectral characterization of such waves, for all values of p p . Namely, we show that all waves are spectrally stable for 2 < p ≤ 8 2<p\leq 8 , while a single mode instability occurs for p > 8 p>8 . This extends previous work of Germain, Harrop-Griffits and Marzuola, [ 3 ] , who have previously established orbital stability for some specific waves, in the range p < 8 p<8 .

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we shall be interested in some aspects of the dynamics of dispersive equations, driven by degenerate dispersion. In order to fix ideas, we settle on the one dimensional case on the line, and recall the standard dispersive models. More specifically, for
p>2
, consider the (generalized) Korteweg de Vries equation

(1.1)

u_{t}+u_{xxx}+\partial_{x}(|u|^{p-2}u)=0,\ \ u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

and the non-linear Schrödinger equation

(1.2)

iu_{t}+u_{xx}+|u|^{p-2}u=0,\ \ u:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to{\mathbb{C}}.

The Cauchy problem for these models is well-understood, even in low regularity setting. In particular, and for classical solutions, the solutions conserve energy

E[u(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u^{\prime}|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u|^{p}dx=E[u_{0}]

and mass

M[u(t)]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u|^{2}dx=M[u_{0}].

The solitary waves of these models, namely the standing waves

1.1.
Linearizations about the solitary waves and spectral stability

Take the ansatz
u=\phi(x-\omega t)+e^{\lambda t}v(x-\omega t)

\mathcal{J}\mathcal{H}_{+}v=\lambda v.

where

\mathcal{H}_{+}f=-\phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\phi f)+(\omega-(\phi\phi^{\prime})^{\prime}-(p-1)\phi^{p-2})f=-\phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\phi f)-(p-2)\phi^{p-2}f,

\phi

[-L,L]

For the NLS equation (
1.4

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\

0&\mathcal{H}_{-}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Re v\\
\Im v\end{array}\right)=\lambda\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\Re v\\
\Im v\end{array}\right)

where

is as above and

(1.11)

\mathcal{H}_{-}f=-\phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\phi f)+(\omega+(\phi\phi^{\prime})^{\prime}-\phi^{p-2})f=-\phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\phi f)+2(\omega-\phi^{p-2})f,

again by (

A concise form of (

\mathcal{J}\mathcal{H}\vec{v}=\lambda\vec{v}

where we have introduced

{\mathcal{J}}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\

0&\mathcal{H}_{-}\end{array}\right).

It is now an opportune time to discuss the domains of the linearized operators

may be viewed as self-adjoint extensions of the symmetric operators, associated with the quadratic form, acting on

q_{+}(u,v)=\langle(\phi u)^{\prime},(\phi v)^{\prime}\rangle+\omega\langle u,v\rangle-\langle((\phi\phi^{\prime})^{\prime}+(p-1)\phi^{p-2})u,v\rangle,

and similar for

t o be

The notion of spectral stability is as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{+}
be any self-adjoint extension for the symmetric operator, defined with the form
q_{+}
. We say that the solution

of the generalized KdV problem (
1.3

) does not have non-trivial solutions
(\lambda,v):\Re\lambda>0,v\neq 0
.

Similarly, the solution

of the degenerate NLS equation (
1.4

1.12

(\lambda,\vec{v}):\Re\lambda>0,\vec{v}\neq 0

Note that in this definition, we completely sidestep the important issue for local/global well-posedness of the corresponding Cauchy problems. The local aspect of the theory is discussed in the recent paper

1.2.
Main results

We start with an existence result for the waves
\phi
, which must satisfy the ordinary differential equation (
1.5
). In our construction of the waves, we take advantage of a variational construction, so we introduce our main players. More specifically, consider the following constrained minimization problem

(1.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}N[u]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u\partial_{x}u|^{2}+\omega\int|u|^{2}\to\min\\
\textup{subject to}\ \int|u|^{p}dx=1.\end{array}\right.

Equivalently, the same solutions are achieved via the minimization of the so-called Weinstein functional,

J_{\omega}[u]:=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u\partial_{x}u|^{2}+\omega\int|u|^{2}}{\|u\|_{L^{p}}^{2}}.

Note that formally,

is unconstrained, but one can see that without loss of generality, one may consider only
u:\|u\|_{L^{p}}=1
, which is of course (
1.13
).
The following is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p>2
and
\omega>0
. Then, there exists an unique bell-shaped solution
\phi
of the profile equation (
1.5
), which is a compacton (i.e. it has a compact support). Their half-period
L
and their amplitude are given by (
1.7
). Moreover, such solutions are the unique constrained minimizers of the variational problem (
1.13
).
By construction,
\phi(x-\omega t)
is a traveling wave solution of the degenerate KDV (
1.3

is a standing wave solution of the degenerate NLS, (
1.4

Regarding spectral stability,
\phi(x-\omega t)
and

are spectrally stable solutions (of (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) respectively) if and only if
2<p\leq 8
.

(1)

Recall the issue with possible different self-adjoint extensions of the operators
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
. In particular, the spectral instability results stated above, should be interpreted as follows - for

(2)

Preliminaries

2.1.
Some basics

We use the standard expressions for
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})},1\leq p\leq\infty
as well as the following expression for the Fourier transform and its inverse

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-2\pi ix\xi}dx,\ \ \ f(x)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi ix\xi}d\xi.

Naturally, the Laplacian may be defined through its symbol, that is
\widehat{-\Delta f}(\xi)=4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2}\hat{f}(\xi)
.
Homogeneous and non-homogeneous Sobolev spaces are defined via

\|f\|_{\dot{W}^{s,p}}=\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{p}},\|f\|_{W^{s,p}}=\|(-\Delta)^{\frac{s}{2}}f\|_{L^{p}}+\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

The Sobolev embedding will be useful in the sequel, so we state it here - for each
1<r<q<\infty
,

\|f\|_{L^{q}}\leq C_{r,q}\|f\|_{\dot{W}^{\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{q},r}}.

2.2.
Rearrangements

In this subsection, we discuss the techniques of rearrangements. For a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, let

d_{f}(\alpha):=|\{x\in\mathbb{R}:|f(x)|>\alpha\}|

be its
distribution function
. For every
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi(0)=0
,
one has
the layer cake representation formula

(2.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(|f(x)|)dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi^{\prime}(\alpha)d_{f}(\alpha)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha.

The
non-increasing rearrangement
,
f^{*}
, of the function
f
is the inverse function of
d_{f}
, provided that
\alpha\to d_{f}(\alpha)
is strictly decreasing. In general, we define
f^{*}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

f^{*}(t)=\inf\{s>0:d_{f}(s)\leq t\},\qquad t\geq 0.

Then
f^{*}
is a non-increasing function, and one furthermore has that
d_{f^{*}}(\alpha)=d_{f}(\alpha)
.
Let
f^{\#}(t)=f^{*}(2|t|)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
f^{\#}
provides us with a convenient way of characterizing bell-shapedness, namely, we say that
f
is bell-shaped if and only if
f^{\#}=f
. Note that in particular, bell-shaped functions are non-negative.

Lemma 1
.

(Polya-Szegö inequality)

For
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we have that
f^{\#}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and in fact,

(2.2)

\|f^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\|\partial_{x}f^{\#}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

The next result is the Hardy’s inequality.

Lemma 2
.

Let
a<b
and
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, so that
f(a)=0
. Then,

(2.3)

\int_{a}^{b}\frac{|f(x)|^{2}}{|x-a|^{2}}dx\leq\int_{a}^{b}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx.

Remark:
This is a slightly more general version of the classical statement

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|f(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}dx\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx.

for function
f:f(0)=0
. But it is clear that from the classical version,
one obtains by an approximation argument that

\int_{0}^{b}\frac{|g(x)|^{2}}{|x|^{2}}dx\leq\int_{0}^{b}|g^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx

for every
g:g(0)=0
,
b>0
. It is then clear that the formulation (
2.3
) reduces to this form, by taking
g:g(x)=f(x+a)
.

We follow the setup described in

Consider the Hamiltonian eigenvalue problem of the form

\mathcal{J}\mathcal{H}g=\lambda g,u\in H

where

\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{J})\subset H^{*}\to H

and

\mathcal{H}:H\to H^{*}

We will give a brief introduction of the analysis of the number of unstable eigenvalues of (

\langle\mathcal{H}u,v\rangle
is bounded symmetric bilinear form on

, which gives rise to a self-adjoint operator
(\mathcal{H},D(\mathcal{H}))
Moreover, there exist a decomposition of
H
in
\mathcal{H}
invariant

H=H_{-}\oplus ker\mathcal{H}\oplus H_{+}

where
H_{-},H_{+}
are the negative and positive subspaces respectively. More precisely, upon introducing the self-adjoint projections
P_{-}=\chi_{(-\infty,0)}(\mathcal{H}),P_{+}=\chi_{(0,+\infty)}(\mathcal{H})
, we take
H_{-}=P_{-}[H],H_{+}=P_{+}[H]
. Assume in addition that the Morse index of
\mathcal{H}
, that is
n(\mathcal{H})=dim(H_{-})<\infty
, while there exists
\delta>0
, so that
\langle\mathcal{H}u,u\rangle\geq\delta\|u\|^{2}
, for all
u\in D(\mathcal{H})\cap H_{+}
. For any
\lambda\in\sigma_{p.p.}({\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{H})
, introduce the generalized eigenspace

E_{gen}^{\lambda}:=\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}E_{j}^{\lambda},E_{\lambda}^{j}=\{f\in H:(\mathcal{JH}-\lambda I)^{j}f=0\}

Assume also that the dual space
H^{*}
satisfies

\{\langle g\in H^{*}:\langle g,u\rangle=0,\forall u\in H_{-}\oplus H_{+}\rangle\}\subset D(\mathcal{H}).

Further since,
Ker(\mathcal{H})\subset E_{gen}^{0}
, decompose
E_{gen}^{0}=ker(\mathcal{H})\oplus E_{0}
, where
E_{0}
is finite dimensional with basis say
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
. Let
D
be the matrix with entries

D_{ij}=\langle\mathcal{H}\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle.

Next, we need to introduce the notion of negative Krein signature of a purely imaginary eigenvalue. More specifically, for
i\mu\in\sigma_{p.p.}({\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{H}),\mu>0
, consider
E_{i\mu}:=Ker({\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{H}-i\mu),P_{i\mu}:H\to E_{i\mu}

and
k_{i}^{i\mu}=n(\mathcal{H}|_{E_{i\mu}})=n(P_{i\mu}\mathcal{H}P_{i\mu})
. Finally, we define the total Krein signature

k_{i}^{\leq 0}=\sum\limits_{\mu\neq 0:i\mu\in\sigma_{p.p.}({\mathcal{J}}\mathcal{H})}k_{i}^{i\mu}.

In the most common case, when
i\mu
is a simple eigenvalue, with say an eigenvector
\psi_{\mu}
,
i\mu
is of negative Krein signature exactly when the real quantity
\langle\mathcal{H}\psi_{\mu},\psi_{\mu}\rangle<0
. In such a case

.

By
[
7
]
see also
[
15
]
[
14
]
we have the following Hamiltonian-Krein index formula

(2.5)

k_{Ham.}:=k_{r}+2k_{c}+2k_{i}^{\leq 0}=n(\mathcal{H})-n(D)

where
k_{r}
is the number of real positive eigenvalues of
\mathcal{JH}
(counted with their multiplicities),
k_{c}
is the number of eigenvalues of
\mathcal{JH}
with positive real and imaginary part, and lastly
k_{i}^{\leq 0}
is the total Krein signature introduced above. Note that

implies spectral stability for the model (
2.4
), as

counts all the instabilities, since
k_{Ham}\geq k_{r}+k_{c}
.

In the particular case
n({\mathcal{L}})=1
, the formula (
2.5
) reduces the situation to two cases, namely
k_{Ham}=1
, if
n(D)=0
and
k_{Ham}=0
, if

. We have already discussed that

is a case of stability. If however

, by parity considerations,
it follows that

, while

. In an y event, this implies that the system has a real unstable growing mode. Thus, we have the following useful corollary of (
2.5
).

Corollary 1
.

If

, the the eigenvalue problem (

) is spectrally stable if

, and it has exactly one real unstable mode, if

We start with the proof of Proposition

3.1.
Proof of Proposition
1

We solve the ODE (
1.5
). As long as
\phi\neq 0
, say on an interval
[-L,L]
, we may divide by
\phi
, which then leads us to the ODE

(3.1)

-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x}^{2}(\phi^{2})+\omega-|\phi|^{p-2}=0.

Denoting
Q:=\phi^{2}\geq 0
, this is equivalent to

(3.2)

-\frac{1}{2}Q^{\prime\prime}+\omega-Q^{\frac{p}{2}-1}=0.

Multiplying the equation by
Q^{\prime}
and integrating on the interval
[-L,L]
, and imposing that
Q^{\prime}(-L)=Q^{\prime}(L)=Q(L)=Q(-L)=0
, we reduce the order of the ODE, namely to

(3.3)

(Q^{\prime})^{2}=4\omega Q-\frac{8}{p}Q^{\frac{p}{2}}.

If we further require that
Q
is decreasing on
(0,L)
, i.e.
Q^{\prime}(r)<0,r>0
, we can finally setup the ODE

(3.4)

Q^{\prime}(r)=-2\sqrt{\omega Q(r)-\frac{2}{p}Q^{\frac{p}{2}}(r)},0<r<L.

Note that this immediately implies (
1.6
).
Clearly, (
3.4
) has an unique solution of the required form, namely with
Q(0)=Q_{0}=\left(\frac{p\omega}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{p-2}},Q^{\prime}(0)=0
, if we select

(3.5)

L=\int_{0}^{Q_{0}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\omega Q-\frac{2}{p}Q^{\frac{p}{2}}}}dQ=\frac{p^{\frac{1}{p-2}}}{2^{\frac{p-1}{p-2}}}\omega^{\frac{4-p}{2(p-2)}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{z-z^{\frac{p}{2}}}}dz.

Clearly, such solution is bell-shaped as it satisfies (
3.4
).

Conversely, assume that
\phi
is a bell-shaped solution of (
1.5
), which vanishes at
\pm\infty
. Then,
Q:=\phi^{2}
will satisfy (
3.2
) as argued above, and it will also vanish at
\pm\infty
. We observe now that the support of

may not be infinite, since then, it must be that

+\infty=\int_{0}^{Q(0)}\frac{dQ}{\sqrt{\omega Q-\frac{2}{p}Q^{\frac{p}{2}}}},

which is clearly false as the integral is convergent, due to the mild singularity at both
0
and
Q(0)
. Thus,
Q
(and subsequently
\phi
) is supported on a finite interval
[-L,L]
. Then, it becomes clear that since
Q^{\prime}(L)=0
, it is the case that
Q(0)=Q_{0}=\left(\frac{p\omega}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{p-2}}
and the solution is unique from (

is given by the formula displayed in (

Looking at the form of the functional

(3.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}N_{0}[v]=N[\sqrt{v}]=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v^{\prime}|^{2}+\omega\int|v|\to\min\\
\textup{subject to}\ \int|v|^{p/2}dx=1.\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
N_{0}[v]\geq 0
, so we introduce

m(\omega):=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{S}}J_{\omega}(u)=\inf\limits_{\int|u|^{p}dx=1}N[u]=\inf\limits_{\int|v|^{p/2}dx=1}N_{0}[v].

We now show that (
3.6
) has a solution.

Proposition 2
.

Let
p>2,\omega>0
. Then, the constrained minimization problem (
3.6
) has a bell-shaped solution
\varphi=\varphi^{\#}
.

Proof.

By the Szegö inequality,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v^{\prime}|^{2}\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{x}v^{\#}|^{2}
, while

\int|v|=\int|v^{\#}|,\int|v|^{p/2}=\int|v^{\#}|^{p/2}
, whence it is clear that it suffices to restrict the problem (
3.6
)to bell-shaped entries
v
. Take a minimizing sequence,
v_{n}:\|v_{n}\|_{L^{p/2}}=1
,
v_{n}\in H^{1}\cap L^{1}
,
\lim_{n}\left(\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|v_{n}^{\prime}|^{2}+\omega\int v_{n}\right)=m(\omega)
. It follows that
\sup_{n}\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}<4m(\omega)
,
\sup_{n}\int v_{n}\leq m(\omega)
. By the bell-shapedness, we conclude the point-wise decay
|v_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{1+|x|}
. Thus, by Kolmogorov-Rellich criteria, the set

\{v_{n}:\sup_{n}\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}<\infty,|v_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{C}{1+|x|}\}
is pre-compact in
L^{p/2}(\mathbb{R})
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\lim_{n}\|v_{n}-\varphi\|_{L^{p/2}}=0
and
v_{n}^{\prime}\to\varphi^{\prime}
weakly. Similarly, fixing a compact subset
K\subset\mathbb{R}
, the set
\{v_{n}:\sup_{n}\|v_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}<\infty,\|v_{n}\|_{L^{p/2}}=1\}
is a pre-compact subset in any
C^{\alpha}(K),\alpha<\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, we can without loss of generality assume in addition that
v_{n}
converges uniformly to
\varphi
on the compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. By Fatou’s lemma,
\liminf_{n}\int v_{n}\geq\int\varphi
. Finally, by the lower semi-continuity of the
L^{2}
norm, with respect to the weak topology,
\liminf_{n}\int(\partial_{x}v_{n})^{2}\geq\int(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}
. Putting it all together, we have that
\int\varphi^{p/2}=1
, while

N_{0}[\varphi]\leq\liminf_{n}N_{0}[v_{n}]=m(\omega).

It follows that
N_{0}[\varphi]=m(\omega)
, whence
\varphi
is indeed a constrained minimizer of (
3.6
). In addition,
\varphi
is clearly bell-shaped (as limit of bell-shaped functions).

∎

We will eventually establish that the minimizers
\varphi
of (

Proposition 3
.

The solution
\varphi

where

(3.8)

\displaystyle c(\omega,p)=p^{\frac{3}{p+1}}\omega^{\frac{p+4}{2(p+1)}}\left(2\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{z-z^{\frac{p}{2}}}dz+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{z}{\sqrt{z-z^{\frac{p}{2}}}}dz\right)^{\frac{p-2}{p+1}}.

Finally, there are the following formulas for the behavior of
\varphi
at

(3.9)

\varphi(x)=\omega(L-x)^{2}+O((L-x)^{3}),\ \ \varphi(x)=\omega(x+L)^{2}+O((x+L)^{3}).

(2)

Proof.

∎

achieves its minimum at zero. It follows that

, whence for all test functions

\langle-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\omega-c\varphi^{\frac{p}{2}-1},h\rangle=0,

It follows that on the support of

3.3.
Spectral theory for the linearized operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}

Clearly, by the equivalence between the constrained minimization problems (
1.13
) and (
3.6
), we have that (
1.13
) has a solution
\Phi:=\sqrt{\varphi}
, which is also bell-shaped. We establish further properties of
\Phi
.

Proposition 4
.

The solution
\Phi
of (
1.13
), satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

(3.12)

-\Phi\partial_{x}(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})+\omega\Phi-c(\omega)\Phi^{p-1}=0,\ \ -L<x<L.

In addition, consider the symmetric operator

(3.13)

{\mathcal{L}}_{+}f=-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\Phi f)+\omega f-(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{\prime}f-(p-1)c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}f,

with a base Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Any self-adjoint extension of
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
(also denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
),
has the property
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}|_{\{\Phi^{p-1}\}^{\perp}}\geq 0
.
In particular, (any self-adjoint extension of)
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
has at most one negative eigenvalue.

Remark:
The operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
is associated with the quadratic form

q(u,v)=\langle\partial_{x}(\Phi u),\partial_{x}(\Phi v)\rangle+\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\bar{v}(x)dx-(p-1)c(\omega,p)\langle\Phi^{p-2}u,v\rangle,

acting on functions
u,v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

By the relation (
3.12
), we have that
-(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{\prime}+\omega=c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}
, and so we can rewrite the linearized operator and the corresponding quadratic form, on functions
f:suppf\subset(-L,L)
, as follows

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}f=-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\Phi f)-(p-2)c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}f

(3.15)

\displaystyle q(u,v)=\langle\partial_{x}(\Phi u),\partial_{x}(\Phi v)\rangle-(p-2)c(\omega,p)\langle\Phi^{p-2}u,v\rangle.

Proof.

For
\epsilon:|\epsilon|<<1
and a test function
h:supp\ h\subset(-L,L)
, consider a perturbation
\Phi+\epsilon h
. According to the minimization property of
\Phi
, we must have that the scalar function

f(\epsilon):=N\left[\frac{\Phi+\epsilon h}{\|\Phi+\epsilon h\|_{L^{p}}}\right]=\frac{1}{4\|\Phi+\epsilon h\|_{L^{p}}^{4}}\int(2\Phi\Phi^{\prime}+2\epsilon(\Phi h)^{\prime}+\epsilon^{2}\partial_{x}(h^{2}))^{2}+\frac{\omega}{\|\Phi+\epsilon h\|_{L^{p}}^{2}}\int(\Phi+\epsilon h)^{2}.

has a minimum at
\epsilon=0
. As a consequences of the minimization property,
f^{\prime}(0)=0
, while
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0
. It is however easier to work with expansions in powers of
\epsilon
, instead of differentiating directly in
\epsilon
. We have

\|\Phi+\epsilon h\|_{L^{p}}^{p}=1+p\epsilon\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle+\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\epsilon^{2}\langle\Phi^{p-2},h^{2}\rangle+O(\epsilon^{3}).

To this end, let us do the first order expansion.

\displaystyle f(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime}+\epsilon(\Phi h)^{\prime})^{2}(1-4\epsilon\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle)+\omega\int(\Phi^{2}+2\epsilon\Phi h)(1-2\epsilon\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle)+O(\epsilon^{2})=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(0)+2\epsilon\left(-\int\Phi(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})h+\omega\langle\Phi,h\rangle-\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle\left(2\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}+\omega\int\Phi^{2}\right)\right)+O(\epsilon^{2}).

Hence we have the Euler-Lagrange equation (
3.12
) in weak sense, with
c(\omega)=2\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}+\omega\int\Phi^{2}
, which is the same coefficient that we have encountered in Proposition
3
.

Next, we deal with the second order condition, namely
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0
. To simplify matters, take
h\perp\Phi^{p-1}
, that is
\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle=0
. Looking at the next order, that is the terms containing
\epsilon^{2}
. We obtain

\displaystyle\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}=-(p-1)\langle\Phi^{p-2},h^{2}\rangle\left(2\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}+\omega\int\Phi^{2}\right)+\langle(\Phi h)^{\prime},(\Phi h)^{\prime}\rangle+\int\Phi\Phi^{\prime}\partial_{x}(h^{2})+\omega\int h^{2}.

It is now clear, after integration by parts, that one can write the previous identity in the form

\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}=\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}h,h\rangle.

As this is valid for all
h:\langle\Phi^{p-1},h\rangle=0
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0
, we conclude that
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}|_{\{\Phi^{p-1}\}^{\perp}}\geq 0.

∎

From Proposition
4

n({\mathcal{L}}_{+})\leq 1
. Our next task is to characterize the rest of the spectrum of
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}

\Phi\Phi_{xx}

\Phi^{p-2}.
Multiplying (
3.12

\Phi^{\prime}

(\Phi^{\prime})^{2}=\omega-\frac{2c(\omega)}{p}\Phi^{p-2}.

Another very important relation, which is obtained from (

\Phi\Phi^{\prime\prime}=\frac{2-p}{p}c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}.

We now state a technical result, which connects the degenerate operator

L_{+}:=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\frac{\omega}{4}-c(\omega)\frac{2p^{2}-5p+3}{2p}\Phi^{p-2}(x(t)).

To this end, we borrow an important idea from

Lemma 3
.

The transformation

(3.19)

t(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{dy}{\Phi(y)}:(-L,L)\to\mathbb{R}

is a one-to-one mapping from
(-L,L)

\epsilon>0

, so that

(3.20)

\displaystyle L-C_{\epsilon}e^{(-\sqrt{\omega}+\epsilon)t}<x(t)<L,\ \ t>0

(3.21)

\displaystyle-L<x(t)<-L+C_{\epsilon}e^{(\sqrt{\omega}-\epsilon)t},\ \ t<0.

For the Schrödinger operator
L_{+}
, defined in (

),
there is the relation

(3.22)

{\mathcal{L}}_{+}f=\frac{L_{+}(\sqrt{\Phi}f)}{\sqrt{\Phi}}.

Remark:

Note that due to the asymptotics (

for every

By (

\lim_{x\to L-}\frac{\ln(L-x)}{t(x)}=-\lim_{x\to L-}\frac{\Phi(x)}{L-x}=-\sqrt{\omega},

For the formula (

\displaystyle-\Phi^{\frac{3}{2}}\partial_{x}^{2}[\sqrt{\Phi}f]

∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
be any self-adjoint extension of the symmetric operator introduced in (
3.13
). Then,
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue, say
-\zeta^{2}
, a simple eigenvalue at zero, with
Ker({\mathcal{L}}_{+})=span[\Phi^{\prime}]
, while the rest of the spectrum is away from zero. That is, there exists
\delta>0
, so that

(3.23)

spec({\mathcal{L}}_{+})\setminus\{-\zeta^{2},0\}\subset[\delta,+\infty).

Proof.

Direct inspection shows that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})+\omega\Phi^{\prime}-(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{\prime}\Phi^{\prime}-(p-1)c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}\Phi^{\prime}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})-(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{\prime}\Phi^{\prime}+(\omega\Phi-c(\omega)\Phi^{p-1})^{\prime}=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})-(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{\prime}\Phi^{\prime}+(\Phi\partial_{x}(\Phi\Phi^{\prime}))^{\prime}=0,

whence
\Phi^{\prime}\in Ker({\mathcal{L}}_{+})
. This is of course a consequence of the translational invariance of the profile equation (
3.12
). Also by a direct inspection, and using the equation (
3.12
), we conclude

{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi=-2\omega\Phi-(p-4)c(\omega)\Phi^{p-1}.

Taking dot product with
\Phi
, we obtain,

(3.24)

\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi,\Phi\rangle=-2\omega\int\Phi^{2}-(p-4)c(\omega).

We will now show that such quantity is negative, for all
p>2
, which would establish that
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue. One could use the arguments of Proposition
3
to evaluate the quantity
\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi,\Phi\rangle
explicitly in terms of

. Instead, we provide an easier roundabout argument, which shows
\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi,\Phi\rangle<0
.
To this end, recall that we have already established the relation

c(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\int(\varphi^{\prime})^{2}+\omega\int\varphi=2\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}+\omega\int\Phi^{2}.

We now obtain another identity based on taking dot product of (

\displaystyle c(\omega)=\frac{p\omega}{2}\int\Phi^{2}-\frac{p}{2}\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}.

Combining this with (

\int(\Phi\Phi^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{p-2}{p+4}\omega\int\Phi^{2}dx,

which in turn implies

c(\omega)=\frac{3p\omega}{p+4}\int\Phi^{2}.

Substituting this in (
3.24

\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\Phi,\Phi\rangle=-\omega\frac{(3p^{2}-10p+8)}{p+4}\int\Phi^{2}dx<0,

for

.
Thus,
n({\mathcal{L}}_{+})=1
.

Next, we establish that the eigenvalue at zero is simple and there is a gap between the zero and the non-negative portion of the spectrum. Recall that we work with arbitrary self-adjoint extension of
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}:D({\mathcal{L}}_{+})\subset L^{2}[-L,L]
, given by the quadratic form
q
. Since
q(u,u)\geq 0
for all

, it follows that
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
has only one simple negative eigenvalue, say
-\zeta^{2}
, and
\sigma({\mathcal{L}}_{+})\setminus\{-\zeta^{2}\}\subset[0,\infty)
. For any
\Omega

[0,\infty)

P_{\Omega}:=\chi_{\Omega}({\mathcal{L}}_{+})

3.23

, with
P_{[0,\frac{1}{n})}f_{n}=f_{n},\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}}=1,f_{n}\perp\Phi^{\prime}

\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{+}f_{n},f_{n}\rangle=q(f_{n},f_{n})=\|(\Phi f_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}[-L,L]}^{2}-(p-2)c(\omega)\int_{-L}^{L}\Phi^{p-2}f_{n}^{2}dx\to 0.

Denote

\|g_{n}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}

\limsup_{n}\|g_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}=\limsup_{n}[q(f_{n},f_{n})+(p-2)c(\omega)\int_{-L}^{L}\Phi^{p-2}f_{n}^{2}]\leq C_{p}\|\Phi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{p-2},

From the various convergences and the weak compactness of bounded sets in

3.4.
Spectral theory for the operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}

In order to analyze the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem (

{\mathcal{L}}_{-}
. In the classical theory, such operator is non-negative, with a simple eigenvalue at zero. The same results holds here as well.

We start however with a formula for

in the spirit of (

L_{-}=-\partial_{t}^{2}+\frac{9\omega}{4}-\frac{3(p+1)}{2p}c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}

\sqrt{\Phi}{\mathcal{L}}_{-}f=L_{-}(\sqrt{\Phi}f).

Proposition 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}
be any self-adjoint extension of the symmetric operator

{\mathcal{L}}_{-}f=-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}[\Phi f]+2(\omega-c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2})f,

defined through the quadratic form
q(u,v)=\langle\Phi u,\Phi v\rangle+2\langle(\omega-c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2})u,v\rangle
for
u,v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(-L,L)
.

Then,
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}\geq 0
, where zero is a simple eigenvalue, with
Ker[{\mathcal{L}}_{-}]=span[\Phi]
.

Proof.

A direct inspection shows that

{\mathcal{L}}_{-}[\Phi]=-\Phi\partial_{x}^{2}[\Phi^{2}]+2(\omega-c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2})\Phi=0,

due to the profile equation (
3.12
).

Next, we show that there is the point-wise domination
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}\geq{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
, as in the classical case. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{-}-{\mathcal{L}}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{9\omega}{4}-\frac{3(p+1)}{2p}c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}-\left(\frac{\omega}{4}-\frac{2p^{2}-5p+3}{2p}c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}\right)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\omega-(4-p)c(\omega)\Phi^{p-2}

If
p\geq 4
, this is clearly a positive quantity. In the case
2<p<4
, taking into account that
\Phi\leq\Phi(0)=\left(\frac{p\omega}{2c(\omega)}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-2}}
, we conclude again

{\mathcal{L}}_{-}-{\mathcal{L}}_{+}\geq 2\omega-\frac{(4-p)p\omega}{2}=\frac{\omega}{2}(p-2)^{2}>0

As it was already shown in Proposition
4
, that
{\mathcal{L}}_{+}|_{\{\Phi^{p-1}\}^{\perp}}\geq 0
, it follows that
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}|_{\{\Phi^{p-1}\}^{\perp}}\geq 0
. In addition, such an inequality guarantees that
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}
may have at most a single negative eigenvalue at the bottom of its spectrum. We proceed to rule this out. Recalling
the relationship (
3.27
), it follows that if
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}
has a negative eigenvalue, then the operator
L_{-}
has negative eigenvalue as well. Thus, it remains to rule out negative eigenvalues for

Recall that since
{\mathcal{L}}_{-}[\Phi]=0

has an eigenvalue at zero, with corresponding positive eigenfunction
