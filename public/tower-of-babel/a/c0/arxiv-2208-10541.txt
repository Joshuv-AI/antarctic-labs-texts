On a Bernstein inequality for eigenfunctions
Stefano Decio, Eugenia Malinnikova

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

An eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on a compact smooth Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
is a solution of the equation

(1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}\varphi_{{\lambda}}+{\lambda}\varphi_{{\lambda}}=0.

A pervasive theme in the study of the local properties of eigenfunctions is that
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
should behave like a polynomial of degree approximately
\sqrt{{\lambda}}
. This idea informs the influential conjecture of Yau on the size of the nodal set of eigenfunctions and the works of Donnelly and Fefferman (
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
). In this note we pursue the analogy and obtain fine estimates on the growth of eigenfunctions. In particular, we seek to obtain a version of the classical Bernstein inequality; for a trigonometric polynomial
T_{N}
of degree
N
, the inequality reads

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)}|T^{\prime}_{N}(\theta)|\leq N\sup_{\theta\in(-\pi,\pi)}|T_{N}(\theta)|.

For an algebraic polynomial in
P_{N}
, also of degree
N
, the following inequality, usually called the Markov or Bernstein-Markov inequality, holds:

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in(-1,1)}|P^{\prime}_{N}(x)|\leq N^{2}\sup_{x\in(-1,1)}|P_{N}(x)|.

Note that in general one needs
N^{2}
in the Markov inequality; the Chebyshev polynomials are extremizers of the inequality. Essentially, the Bernstein inequality for trigonometric polynomials is a global inequality, while the Markov inequality is local in nature. For eigenfunctions, the global Bernstein inequality

(2)

\displaystyle\sup_{M}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C_{M}\sqrt{{\lambda}}\sup_{M}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|

holds and is an easy consequence of standard elliptic estimates for PDEs; see for instance
[
18
, Corollary 3.3]
for a proof. Let us remark that a global Bernstein inequality also holds for linear combinations of eigenfunctions (
[
7
]
), but the proof is considerably more involved.

A local version of the Bernstein inequality is substantially more difficult to prove; the study was initiated by Donnelly and Fefferman in
[
6
]
, where they prove that

(3)

\int_{B(x,(1+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}})r)}|\varphi_{\lambda}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{B(x,r)}|\varphi_{\lambda}|^{2},

and deduce the following result

Theorem A
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth compact Riemannian manifold of dimension
d
and let
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
be a solution of (
1
). Then

\displaystyle\sup_{B(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g)\frac{{\lambda}^{\frac{d+2}{2}}}{r}\sup_{B(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|

for any geodesic ball
B(x,r)
.

They also conjecture that the inequality should hold with exponent
1/2
instead of
(d+2)/2
, i.e. that

\displaystyle\sup_{B(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g)\frac{\sqrt{{\lambda}}}{r}\sup_{B(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|

We note that this, if true, would give an inequality that is stronger than the Markov inequality for polynomials, where the square of the degree of the polynomial appears; imprecisely but suggestively, the conjecturally sharp inequality would tell us that an eigenfunction
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
looks like a
harmonic
polynomial of degree
\sqrt{{\lambda}}
. In
[
4
]
Dong obtained a sharper version of Theorem
A
for eigenfunctions on surfaces, using powerful ideas introduced in
[
3
]
, see Section
4
for the details.

The main result of the present article is an improvement of Theorem
A
in any dimension, which, up to logarithmic errors, gives the conjecture of Donnelly and Fefferman at scales up to the wavelength
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
, and resembles more the Markov inequality at larger scales.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact smooth Riemannian manifold, and let
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
be a solution of
\Delta_{g}\varphi_{{\lambda}}+{\lambda}\varphi_{{\lambda}}=0
. Let
B_{g}(x,r)\subset M
be any geodesic ball. Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary. Then the following Bernstein-type inequality holds:

(4)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g,\delta)\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{{\lambda}}\log^{2+\delta}{\lambda}}{r},{\lambda}\log^{2+\delta}{\lambda}\right\}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|

Remark
.

(i)
The constant
C(M,g,\delta)
blows up as
\delta
goes to
0
. If the reader wishes to forget about
\delta
, it is certainly possible to think that
\delta=1
and discard the
\delta
-dependence of the constants.

(ii)
In dimension two, using some geometric techniques of Dong, we can get the sharp inequality up to scale
(\log{\lambda})^{-1}
; see Theorem
3
in Section
4
for the precise statement.

(iii)
We also obtain an
L^{\infty}
version of (
3
), which is effectively equivalent to the Bernstein inequality.

Our proof of Theorem
1
hinges on a version of the Bernstein inequality for solutions of second order elliptic PDEs with smooth enough coefficients. The role of (square root of) the eigenvalue is played in this case by the frequency function (or the doubling index), which serves as a local degree of the solution. We believe the following result is of independent interest, since it shows that a solution to an elliptic PDE with bounded frequency also behaves like a polynomial. By
N_{u}(0,2)
in the statement below we denote the frequency function of
u
in the ball
B(0,2)
, see (
8
) at the beginning of Section
2
for the definition.

Theorem 2
.

let
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))
be a uniformly positive definite matrix satisfying

(i)

\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq A(x)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}
for any
\xi\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
, with
0<\Lambda<\infty
;

(ii)

A(0)=Id
.

Let
u\in W^{1,2}(B(0,2))
be a solution of
\diver(A\nabla u)=0
in
B(0,2)
, and assume
N_{u}(0,2)\leq N
. Suppose that
N>10
and let
L\vcentcolon=N\log^{2+\delta}N
, with
\delta>0
arbitrarily small. Let

be fixed. Then, if for

satisfying

(5)

\displaystyle k\eta>\frac{d}{2}+1

a^{ij}(x)
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
function of

with

\displaystyle\|a^{ij}(x)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq\gamma\text{ for all }i,j,

the following inequalities hold for any

, where

and

:

(6)

\displaystyle\sup_{B(0,(1+1/L)r)}|u|\leq C\sup_{B(0,r)}|u|;

(7)

The constant

\Lambda,\gamma

Remark
.

For the application of Theorem
2
to eigenfunctions we will only need

. But it is worth noting that for the equations considered here we do not need to take such small radii.

Plan of the paper

Section
2
is devoted to the proof of Theorem

, which uses an auxiliary result for solutions of equations with analytic coefficient. Theorem
1
is deduced from Theorem
2
in Section

. In Section

.

Notation

The dependence of the constants on the dimension is pervasive throughout the paper and will more often than not be suppressed. We indicate by
c,C
constants whose value may change from line to line (and even within the same line) but only depends on the manifold

2.
Elliptic PDEs

Donnelly and Fefferman in
[
6
]
prove Theorem
A
directly for eigenfunctions using Carleman inequalities. In this work we take a different route: we view an eigenfunction at scales below the wavelength as a solution to a uniformly elliptic PDE through a well known lifting trick (see Section
3
), and to prove the elliptic result we rely on the monotonicity of the frequency function and some 1-dimensional complex analysis. Let us now describe in detail the proof.

We let
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))
be as in the statement of Theorem
2
, meaning that

(i)

\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq A(x)\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}
for any
\xi\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
.

(ii)

for some
k
such that
k\eta>\frac{d}{2}+1
,
\|a^{ij}(x)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq\gamma
for all
i,j
.

We remind the reader that
0<\eta\leq 1
is the parameter for which we seek to obtain inequalities (
6
) and (
7
) for
r\lesssim N^{-\eta}
. Let now

\displaystyle\mu(x)=\frac{A(x)x\cdot x}{|x|^{2}}

and note that
\Lambda^{-1}\leq\mu(x)\leq\Lambda
. Following
[
8
]
(see also
[
15
]
), for a solution
u
of
\diver(A\nabla u)=0
in
B(0,2)\subset{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
,
u\in W^{1,2}(B(0,2))
, with
A(0)=Id
and a ball
B(x,r)\subset B(0,2)
, we define the frequency function as

(8)

\displaystyle N_{u}(x,r)=\frac{r\int_{B(x,r)}A\nabla u\cdot\nabla u}{2\int_{\partial B(x,r)}\mu|u|^{2}}.

Recall that the key assumption of Theorem
2
is that
N_{u}(0,2)\leq N
.

Throughout the section, we let
B\vcentcolon=B(0,1)
. For the proof of Theorem
2
we will need an auxiliary result about solutions to elliptic PDEs with real-analytic coefficients; we will approximate
u
with such functions. We state and prove the result in the next subsection.

2.1.
A lemma on analytic solutions

We let here
\widetilde{A}
be an elliptic matrix with entries
\widetilde{a}^{ij}
real-analytic in
B
, and ellipticity constant
\Lambda
. More precisely, we require the existence of numbers
K,A
such that

(9)

\displaystyle\max_{i,j}|\nabla^{p}\widetilde{a_{ij}}(x)|\leq Kp!A^{p}\text{ for all }p\geq 0.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a solution
v
of the equation
\diver(\widetilde{A}\nabla v)=0
in
B
, with
\widetilde{A}(0)
satisfying (
9
) and
\widetilde{A}(0)=Id
, and suppose that there is a number
N>10
such that

(a)

N_{v}(B)\leq N
;

(b)

\sup_{B}|v|\leq N^{\kappa}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}
, with
\kappa\geq 0
a dimensional constant.

Let
L\vcentcolon=N\log^{2+\delta}N
, with
\delta>0
a real number. Then there is a constant
c=c(\Lambda,K,A,\kappa,\delta)
such that

(10)

\displaystyle\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|v|\geq c\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|.

Proof.

It is a classical result that a solution to an elliptic equation with coefficients satisfying (
9
) extends to a holomorphic function in a domain in
\mathbf{C}^{d}
. It follows from the proof of Theorem 5.7.1’ in
[
17
]
that for every
y\in B
, there is a complex ball in
\mathbf{C}^{d}
of radius equal to
c(\Lambda,K,A)dist(y,\partial B)
such that
v
extends uniquely to a holomorphic function
F
in that ball, with the supremum of
F
in the complex ball bounded by a multiple of the supremum of
v
in the real ball. Call
\Omega\subset\mathbf{C}^{d}
the domain obtained by the union of all these balls, and call
F
the function holomorphic in
\Omega
that extends
v
. By condition
(b)
we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{\Omega}|F|\leq CN^{\kappa}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

Let
m=\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|v|
, with
L
as in the statement of the Lemma. A consequence of almost monotonicity of the frequency and the bound
(a)
is that
\int_{B(0,r_{2})}|v|^{2}\lesssim(r_{2}/r_{1})^{C(1+N)}\int_{B(0,r_{1})}|v|^{2}
for
r_{1}<r_{2}\leq 1
(see
[
14
]
, Theorem 3.1.3). Then, since
L>N
,

\displaystyle m\geq\sup_{(1-5/N)B}|v|\gtrsim\left(\int_{\partial((1-5/N)B)}|v|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\gtrsim\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

In view of this, we normalize
v
so that
C_{0}m=1
, where
C_{0}
is the constant such that
\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)\Omega}|F|\leq C_{0}m
. We abuse notation and still denote by
v,F
the normalized functions. We have then
\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\leq C
,
\sup_{\Omega}|F|\leq CN^{\kappa}
,
\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)\Omega}|F|\leq 1
and we need to prove that

(11)

\displaystyle\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|\leq C.

Let
x_{0}\in\overline{(1-1/N)B}
be such that
|v(x_{0})|=\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|
. We take the line joining the origin and
x_{0}
and consider the plane given by the complexification of it. Let
\Omega_{0}
be the intersection of
\Omega
with this plane. Up to an isometry we can assume that
x_{0}\in\mathbf{R}
and
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbf{C}
. Let
x_{1}\vcentcolon=(1-1/L)(1-1/N)\in\mathbf{R}
. The domain
\Omega_{0}
contains the domain
R
bounded by the contour
\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}\cup\Gamma_{3}\cup\Gamma_{4}
where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbf{C}:x\geq 0,y\geq 0,z-1=\rho e^{i(\pi-\alpha)}\};

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbf{C}:x\geq 0,y\geq 0,z+1=\rho e^{i\alpha}\};

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbf{C}:x\leq 0,y\leq 0,z+1=\rho e^{-i\alpha}\};

\displaystyle\Gamma_{4}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbf{C}:x\geq 0,y\leq 0,z-1=\rho e^{i(\alpha-\pi)}\}.

for some
0<\alpha<\pi/2
. Note that
\alpha
depends on
\Lambda,K,A
only. Let us define
f\vcentcolon=F_{|_{\Omega_{0}}}
; we have that
\sup_{\Omega_{0}}|f|\leq CN^{\kappa}
and
\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)\Omega_{0}}|f|\leq 1
. Finally, we set
h(z)\vcentcolon=\log|f(z)|
. Note that
h
is a subharmonic function. Consider now the domain

\widetilde{\Omega}\vcentcolon=R\setminus(1-1/L)(1-1/N)R\cap B(x_{1},1-x_{1})

and note that
x_{0}\in\widetilde{\Omega}.
The boundary of
\widetilde{\Omega}
consists of
\Gamma_{-}\cup\Gamma_{+}\cup\Gamma_{0}
,where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{-}=\{x_{1}+\rho e^{i(\pi-\alpha)},0\leq\rho\leq 1-x_{1}\}\cup\{x_{1}+\rho e^{i(\alpha-\pi)},0\leq\rho\leq 1-x_{1}\};

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}=(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{4})\cap B(x_{1},1-x_{1});

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}=\partial B(x_{1},1-x_{1})\cap\left(R\setminus(1-1/L)(1-1/N)R\right).

Below is a picture of the regions described:
\widetilde{\Omega}
is the region bounded by the red curve.

We introduce the harmonic function

\displaystyle\omega(z)\vcentcolon=M\text{Re}\left(\left(\frac{z-x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\right)^{\beta}\right),

defined in
\mathbf{C}\setminus(-\infty,x_{1})
, where
M>0
and
\beta>0
are real numbers to be chosen later. We want to use the maximum principle and bound
h
by
\omega
; to this end, we estimate
\omega
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
. Note that on
\Gamma_{-}\cap\{y\geq 0\}
,
(z-x_{1})^{\beta}=\rho^{\beta}e^{i\beta(\pi-\alpha)}
and hence for any
\beta
such that

\displaystyle\beta(\pi-\alpha)<\frac{\pi}{2}

we have
\text{Re}(z-x_{1})^{\beta}\geq 0
and
\omega(z)\geq 0
. We fix a choice for
\beta
:

(12)

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{1}{2}-\widetilde{\delta}

with
\widetilde{\delta}
a small positive real number to be chosen later. Note that since
\alpha>0
,
\beta
chosen in this way is consistent with the condition above. This choice of
\beta
guarantees that also on
\Gamma_{-}\cap\{y\leq 0\}
we have
\omega(z)\geq 0
. Note that since
\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)\Omega_{0}}|f|\leq 1
, we have that
h\leq 0
on
\Gamma_{-}
, so that
h(z)\leq\omega(z)
for
z\in\Gamma_{-}
.
On
\Gamma_{+}
we have

\min_{z\in\Gamma_{+}}\text{Re}\left(\left(\frac{z-x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\right)^{\beta}\right)=\min_{0\leq\theta\leq\pi-2\alpha}\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+\theta)}\right)^{\beta}\cos\beta\theta\geq(\sin\alpha)^{\beta+1}\geq C_{\alpha}.

Since
h(z)\leq\widetilde{C}+\kappa\log N
on
\Gamma_{+}
, at the very least we require
MC_{\alpha}\geq\widetilde{C}+\kappa\log N
.
Finally, note that on
\Gamma_{0}
,
|z-x_{1}|=1-x_{1}
, and we write
z-x_{1}=(1-x_{1})e^{i\theta}
. On
\Gamma_{0}\cap\{y\geq 0\}
,
\theta\in(\frac{\pi-\alpha}{2},\pi-\alpha)
and by (

)

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(\left(\frac{z-x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\right)^{\beta}\right)=\cos(\beta\theta)\gtrsim\widetilde{\delta}.

The same estimate holds on
\Gamma_{0}\cap\{y\leq 0\}
, hence
\omega(z)\geq M\widetilde{\delta}
on
\Gamma_{0}
.
We choose
M
such that

(13)

\displaystyle M\widetilde{\delta}=C_{1}+C_{2}\kappa\log N

with
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
chosen in such a way that
h(z)\leq\omega(z)
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, so that
h(z)\leq\omega(z)
on
\widetilde{\Omega}
by the maximum principle. In particular,

\displaystyle h(x_{0})\leq\omega(x_{0})=M\text{Re}\left(\left(\frac{x_{0}-x_{1}}{1-x_{1}}\right)^{\beta}\right)=M\left(\frac{(1-1/N)1/L}{1-(1-1/N)(1-1/L)}\right)^{\beta}

\displaystyle\leq M\left(\frac{N}{L}\right)^{\beta}=\left(\frac{C_{1}}{\widetilde{\delta}}+\frac{C_{2}\kappa\log N}{\widetilde{\delta}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2+\delta}N}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\widetilde{\delta}}.

Let us pick
\widetilde{\delta}=\min\{\delta/10,C_{\alpha})
; then
h(x_{0})\leq C_{3}/\delta
, and finally

\displaystyle v(x_{0})=f(x_{0})=\exp(h(x_{0}))\leq\exp\left(\frac{C_{3}}{\delta}\right),

which implies (
11
) and concludes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
2

Note first that (
6
) immediately implies (

) by elementary arguments (see for instance the proof of Lemma
2
in the Appendix, or the beginning of the proof of Proposition

), so we will prove (

Claim 1
.

If

(i)

\max_{\overline{B}}|u|\lesssim N^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(\strokedint_{\partial B}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\max_{\overline{B}}|\nabla u|\lesssim N^{\frac{d}{2}+1}\left(\strokedint_{\partial B}|u|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

We remind the reader that
B

. Let

. By classical local boundedness properties of solutions to elliptic PDEs (see for instance

\displaystyle|u(x)|^{2}\lesssim\strokedint_{B\left(x,\frac{1}{N}\right)}|u|^{2}\lesssim N^{d}\int_{B\left(0,1+\frac{1}{N}\right)}|u|^{2}.

We recall the inequality

\displaystyle|u(x)|^{2}\lesssim N^{d}\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)^{CN}\int_{B}|u|^{2},

which gives

∎

as in the statement of Theorem

, and define

. Let

be the Taylor approximation of order

, denoting with a subscript

satisfies the equation

in

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B}\|A_{r}(x)-\widetilde{A}_{r}(x)\|\leq c(\gamma,d)r^{k}.

Note that the entries of
\widetilde{A}_{r}(x)
satisfy condition (

) for some

depending on
\gamma
. Let now

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\diver(\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v)=0\qquad\>&\text{in}\ B,\\

We also set

. We have that

\displaystyle\diver(\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla h)=\diver((\widetilde{A}_{r}-A_{r})\nabla u_{r}),

and clearly

\displaystyle|h(x)|=\left|\int_{B}\diver(\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla h)(y)G(x,y)dy\right|=\left|\int_{B}\diver((\widetilde{A}_{r}-A_{r})\nabla u_{r})G(x,y)dy\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{B}(\widetilde{A}_{r}-A_{r})\nabla u_{r}(y)\cdot\nabla_{y}G(x,y)dy\right|\leq C(\Lambda)\sup_{B}\|\widetilde{A}_{r}-A_{r}\|\sup_{B}|\nabla u_{r}|.

We now apply (

\displaystyle|h(x)|\leq\varepsilon\|f_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)},

with

\Lambda,\gamma
.

With

Proof.

Note that we have
\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla h=0
, hence

\displaystyle\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v=\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla u_{r}

and we can estimate

\displaystyle\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\lesssim\left(\int_{B}|\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{B}|\nabla u_{r}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{B}A_{r}\nabla u_{r}\cdot\nabla u_{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

so that

\displaystyle\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\lesssim\int_{B}A_{r}\nabla u_{r}\cdot\nabla u_{r},

with implied constants depending on
\Lambda,\gamma
. Since on
\partial B
,
v=u_{r}=f_{r}
, for the frequency we have

\displaystyle N_{v}(B)=\frac{\int_{B}\widetilde{A}_{r}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v}{2\int_{\partial B}\mu_{\widetilde{A}_{r}}|v|^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\int_{B}A_{r}\nabla u_{r}\cdot\nabla u_{r}}{\int_{\partial B}\mu_{A_{r}}|u_{r}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N_{u_{r}}(B)=N_{u}(0,r)\lesssim N,

which settles
(i)
. In the last inequality we used almost monotonicity of the frequency function (
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
). Part
(ii)
follows immediately by the maximum principle and Claim
1
.
∎

We are now ready to finish the proof. Recall that
L=N\log^{2+\delta}N
. By Claim
2
,
v
satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
1
with
\kappa=d/2
, so that
\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|v|\geq c_{0}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|
. Note also that, since
N_{v}(B)\lesssim N
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|\geq\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial((1-1/N)B))}\gtrsim\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}.

Using (
16
) and choosing
a=a(\Lambda,\gamma,\delta)
so that
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(a)
is small enough, we can then estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|\geq\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|v|-\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|h|

\displaystyle\geq c_{0}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|-\varepsilon\|v\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\geq c_{0}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|-\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|v|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\left(\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|-\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|h|\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\left(\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|-\varepsilon\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\left(\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|-C\varepsilon\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(\partial((1-1/N)B))}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}c_{0}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|.

Finally, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{B(0,(1-1/L)(1-1/N)r)}|u|=\sup_{(1-1/L)(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|\geq\tilde{c_{0}}\sup_{(1-1/N)B}|u_{r}|

\displaystyle=\tilde{c_{0}}\sup_{B(0,(1-1/N)r)}|u|,

which is equivalent to (
6
).

3.
The inequality for eigenfunctions

We now have all the tools needed to prove Theorem
1
. We first state a sharper version for balls that have radius smaller than the wavelength. Throughout this section,
(M,g)
will be a compact smooth Riemannian manifold, and
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
an eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
M
,
\Delta_{g}\varphi_{{\lambda}}+{\lambda}\varphi_{{\lambda}}=0
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
L\vcentcolon=\sqrt{{\lambda}}\log^{2+\delta}{\lambda}
with
\delta>0
, and let
r_{0}\colon=\frac{a}{\sqrt{{\lambda}}}
, where
a
is a small constant depending on
(M,g)
and
\delta
. Then for any geodesic ball
B_{g}(x,r)
with
r<r_{0}
we have

(17)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}\left(x,\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g,\delta)\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|;

(18)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g,\delta)\frac{L}{r}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|.

Remark
.

The result says that in balls of radius smaller than the wavelength we have a sharp Bernstein inequality up to logarithms. Inequality (
17
) (with
\sqrt{{\lambda}}
instead of
L
) was conjectured in
[
6
]
, where an
L^{2}
version is proved.

We first show how to obtain Theorem
1
from Proposition
1
, and then prove Proposition
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Take a ball
B_{g}(x,r)
; if
r<r_{0}
, we have nothing to prove. If
r>r_{0}
, let
y\in\overline{B_{g}(x,r)}
be such that
|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}(y)|=\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|
, and consider a ball
b\subset B_{g}(x,r)
of radius
r_{0}
such that
y\in\overline{b}
. Then, by Proposition
1
applied to
b
,

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|=|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}(y)|\lesssim\frac{L}{r_{0}}\sup_{b}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\lesssim{\lambda}\log^{2+\delta}{\lambda}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|,

where the implied constants depend on
(M,g)
and
\delta
only, and the theorem is proved.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Let us first note that (
18
) follows easily from (
17
). In fact, let
y\in\overline{B_{g}(x,r)}
be such that
|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}(y)|=\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|
. An application of standard elliptic estimates followed by (
17
) gives

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}(y)|\lesssim\frac{L}{r}\sup_{B_{g}(y,\frac{r}{L})}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\lesssim\frac{L}{r}\sup_{B_{g}(x,(1+\frac{1}{L})r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\lesssim\frac{L}{r}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|,

which is (
18
).

We will now prove (
17
) as a consequence of (
6
). We consider a system of local coordinates on
M
, and identify the metric
g
with a matrix
(g_{ij})
. We denote by
g^{-1}
the inverse of this matrix, and set
|g|\colon=\det(g_{ij})
. We recall that the Laplace-Beltrami operator has the expression

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\diver(\sqrt{|g|}g^{-1}\nabla(\cdot)),

where the divergence and gradient are in the Euclidean sense. For
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{g}
, consider the function

\displaystyle u(x,t)=\varphi_{{\lambda}}(x)e^{\sqrt{{\lambda}}t}.

Note that
u
is harmonic in
\widetilde{M}\colon=M\times{\mathbf{R}}
with respect to the metric
\widetilde{g}=g\otimes\mathrm{dt}
, which means that in local coordinates
u
satisfies the elliptic equation

\displaystyle\diver(\sqrt{|\widetilde{g}|}\widetilde{g}^{-1}\nabla u)=\diver(A\nabla u)=0.

By results of Donnelly and Fefferman (
[
5
]
),
N_{u}(y,s)\leq C\sqrt{{\lambda}}
for any
s
smaller than the injectivity radius of
M
. Now, (
6
) is essentially (
17
) but in Euclidean balls rather than geodesic balls. Let
\widetilde{x}=(x,0)
; now we consider normal coordinates in
\widetilde{M}
centered at
\widetilde{x}
, and note that in these coordinates
A(\widetilde{x})=Id
. Denoting Euclidean balls with the subscript
e
, we observe that

\displaystyle B_{e}(\widetilde{x},r\sqrt{1-c_{1}r^{2}})\subset B_{\widetilde{g}}(\widetilde{x},r)\subset B_{e}(\widetilde{x},r\sqrt{1+c_{2}r^{2}})

if
r
is small enough. Our choice of
r_{0}
then guarantees that

\displaystyle B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)\subset B_{e}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1+\frac{2}{L}\right)r\right)

and

\displaystyle B_{e}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)\subset B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},r\right)

An application of (
6
) then gives

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)}|u|\leq\sup_{B_{e}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1+\frac{2}{L}\right)r\right)}|u|\lesssim\sup_{B_{e}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)}|u|\lesssim\sup_{B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},r\right)}|u|.

Finally, using once again that
r<a/\sqrt{{\lambda}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}\left(x,\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq\sup_{B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},\left(1+\frac{1}{L}\right)r\right)}|u|\lesssim\sup_{B_{\widetilde{g}}\left(\widetilde{x},r\right)}|u|

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{B_{g}\left(x,r\right)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|e^{\sqrt{{\lambda}}r}\lesssim\sup_{B_{g}\left(x,r\right)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|,

and (
17
) is proved.
∎

4.
A stronger inequality in dimension two

We assume now that the dimension of
M
is two. Following the methods of Dong (
[
4
]
), we will show that there is a better Bernstein inequality than in higher dimension.
Let
\varphi_{\lambda}
be an eigenfunction of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
(M,g)
,
\Delta_{g}\varphi_{\lambda}+{\lambda}\varphi_{\lambda}=0
. The main result of
[
4
]
can be formulated as

(19)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g)\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{{\lambda}}}{r},{\lambda}^{\frac{3}{4}}\right\}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|.

It says that the sharp Bernstein inequality holds for scales up to
{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{4}}
.

We outline the proof given by Dong. Let

\displaystyle q=|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}+\frac{{\lambda}}{2}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}.

Denote by
K
the Gaussian curvature of the surface
(M,g)
. It is shown in
[
3
]
that

(20)

\Delta_{g}\log q\geq-\lambda+2\min\{K,0\}.

Next, fix a point
x_{0}\in M
and in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
define the function

M(x)=\max\{q(y):d_{g}(y,x_{0})\leq d_{g}(x,x_{0})\}.

Using (
20
) and standard properties of the Laplacian, it is not difficult to see that
\Delta_{g}M\geq-c\lambda
, see

[
4
]
for details.
The function
M
depends only on the distance from
x
to
x_{0}
and is non-decreasing. Let
H
be an upper bound for the absolute value of the sectional curvature of
M
. Then the comparison theorem for the Laplace-Beltrami operators, see for example
[
19
, Lemma 7.1.9]
, implies

\displaystyle-c\lambda\leq\Delta_{g}\log M\leq\Delta_{H}\log M,

where
\Delta_{H}
is the Laplace operator on the surface of constant sectional curvature
-H
. The metric on this surface is given by

\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{H}=\mathrm{d}r^{2}+\rho_{0}^{2}(r)\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}

where
\rho_{0}(r)=\frac{\sinh(\sqrt{H}r)}{\sqrt{H}}
. Denoting
\log M(x)=F(r)
for
r=d_{g}(x,x_{0})
, we conclude

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(\rho_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}F(r)\right)\geq-c{\lambda}.

Defining
t(r)=\int^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\rho_{0}(\tau)}
, the inequality becomes:

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}F\geq-c\lambda\rho_{0}^{2}.

The convexity estimate above can be combined with another result of
[
3
]
, which says that

(22)

F(2r)-F(r)\lesssim\sqrt{{\lambda}},

to derive the following inequality

M(r)-M((1-\varepsilon)r)\lesssim(\sqrt{{\lambda}}+{\lambda}r^{2})\varepsilon,

for small enough
r
and any
\varepsilon>0
.
For
r\lesssim{\lambda}^{-1/4}
the first term on the right hand side of the last inequality dominates and (
19
) follows.
Our improvement of (
19
) is based on replacing the inequality (
22
) by a refined version of it for the case
r\gtrsim\lambda^{-1/2}.

The main result of this section is the following

Theorem 3
.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact smooth Riemannian surface, and let
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
be a solution of
\Delta_{g}\varphi_{{\lambda}}+{\lambda}\varphi_{{\lambda}}=0
. Let
B_{g}(x,r)\subset M
any geodesic ball. Then we have:

(23)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq C(M,g)\max\left\{\frac{\sqrt{{\lambda}}}{r},\sqrt{{\lambda}}\log{\lambda}\right\}\sup_{B_{g}(x,r)}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|.

Remark
.

Here we are able to refine Dong’s method to get a sharp inequality for balls of radii up to
(\log{\lambda})^{-1}
. We do not know whether the
\log{\lambda}
is needed or it is a feature of the proof.

Proof.

We fix a geodesic ball
B
and denote by
x_{0}
its center and by
r_{0}
its radius. Our aim is to prove the inequality (
23
) for this ball. We may assume that
r_{0}\gtrsim{\lambda}^{-1/2}
, otherwise the inequality follows from (
19
) or from our Theorem
1
.

As above we set
M(x)=\sup_{y\in B_{r}}q(y)
, where
B_{r}
is a geodesic ball of radius
r=d_{g}(x,x_{0})
centered at
x_{0}
.
Finally, we set
F(r)=\log M(r)
.

Our first aim is to estimate
F\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)-F(r)
. Let
a=1+\frac{1}{r\sqrt{{\lambda}}}=1+\varepsilon
so that
ar=r+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}
. On the wave scale
\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\lambda}}}
the eigenfunction
\varphi_{{\lambda}}
is approximately harmonic and the following elliptic estimates hold:

(24)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{ar}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\lesssim{\lambda}\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2};

(25)

\displaystyle\sup_{B_{ar}}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\lesssim{\lambda}^{2}\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}.

We can then estimate:

\displaystyle\max_{B_{ar}}\left(|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\right)\lesssim{\lambda}^{2}\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}+{\lambda}^{2}\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\lambda}^{2}\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}.

Iterating the estimate (
3
) of Donnelly and Fefferman, one gets

\displaystyle\int_{B_{ar+1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\lesssim\left(a+\frac{1}{r\sqrt{{\lambda}}}\right)^{C\sqrt{\lambda}}\int_{B_{r}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\lesssim\left(a+\frac{1}{r\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{C\sqrt{\lambda}}r^{2}M(x),

where
d_{g}(x,x_{0})=r
.
Combining the last two inequalities and taking the logarithms, we get:

\displaystyle F(ar)-F(r)\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda}\log\left(a+\frac{1}{r\sqrt{{\lambda}}}\right)+\log(\lambda r).

Now we ask that
1/\sqrt{{\lambda}}\lesssim r\lesssim 1/\log{\lambda}
. Then

(26)

\displaystyle F\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)-F(r)=F(ar)-F(r)\lesssim\frac{1}{r}+\log{\lambda}\lesssim\frac{1}{r}.

Note that the estimates up to now hold in any dimension. We will combine (
26
) with a two-dimensional inequality (
21
) of Dong.

Recall that we defined
t(r)
by
t(r)=\int^{r}\frac{d\tau}{\rho_{o}(\tau)}
, where
\rho_{0}(\tau)=\frac{\sinh(\sqrt{H}\tau)}{\sqrt{H}}
is a positive increasing function of
\tau
and for small
\tau
we have
\rho_{0}(\tau)\sim\tau
. Let
t_{1}=t\left(r\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\right)
,
t_{2}=t(r)
, and
t_{3}=t\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)
. The standard mean value theorem and (
21
) then imply

\displaystyle\frac{F\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)-F(r)}{t_{3}-t_{2}}-\frac{F(r)-F\left(r\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\right)}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\gtrsim-\lambda\rho_{0}^{2}\left(r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)(t_{3}-t_{1}).

Now,

\displaystyle t_{2}-t_{1}\sim\int_{r\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)}^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}};

\displaystyle t_{3}-t_{2}\sim\int_{r}^{r+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}r}\sim t_{3}-t_{1}.

Using (
26
) in the previous inequality we thus get:

\displaystyle\frac{F(r)-F\left(r\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\right)}{t_{2}-t_{1}}\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda}r\frac{1}{r}+\lambda r^{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}r}

so that

\displaystyle F(r)-F\left(r\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)\right)\lesssim 1.

The rest of the proof is similar to one in
[
4
]
.
Set
r=r_{0}
and
r_{1}\vcentcolon=r_{0}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)
; we obtained:

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{M}(r)=M(x)\lesssim M(x_{1})=\tilde{M}(r_{1}),

where
d_{g}(x,x_{0})=r_{0}
and
d_{g}(x_{1},x_{0})=r_{1}
.
Remember that the above holds for
\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim r\lesssim(\log\lambda)^{-1}
. For any such
r
, consider
q(x^{*})
for some
x^{*}\in\overline{B_{r_{1}}}
. Then for
\sigma\sim\frac{r}{\sqrt{\lambda}}
we have
B_{\sigma}(x^{*})\subset B_{r}
, and by elliptic estimates

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}(x^{*})|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma^{4}}\int_{B_{\sigma}(x^{*})}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\max_{B_{r}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}\sim\frac{\lambda}{r^{2}}\max_{B_{r}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}.

This gives
\tilde{M}(r_{1})\lesssim\lambda r^{-2}\sup_{B_{r}}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|^{2}
, and finally, by (
27
),

(28)

\displaystyle\sup_{B(x_{0},r_{0})}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\leq\sqrt{\tilde{M}(r_{0})}\lesssim\sqrt{\tilde{M}(r_{1})}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{r_{0}}\sup_{B(x_{0},r_{0})}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|,

which is the sharp Bernstein inequality for
\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim r_{0}\lesssim(\log\lambda)^{-1}
.

For
r_{0}\gtrsim(\log\lambda)^{-1}
, we can take
r^{\prime}\sim(\log\lambda)^{-1}
such that
\sup_{B(x_{0},r_{0})}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|=\sup_{B(p,r^{\prime})}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|
and
B(p,r^{\prime})\subset B(x_{0},r_{0})
. Then applying (
28
) to
B(p,r^{\prime})
we obtain:

\displaystyle\sup_{B(x_{0},r_{0})}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|=\sup_{B(p,r^{\prime})}|\nabla\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{r^{\prime}}\sup_{B(p,r^{\prime})}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|\lesssim{\sqrt{\lambda}\log\lambda}\sup_{B(x_{0},r_{0})}|\varphi_{{\lambda}}|,

which concludes the proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

Appendix

The goal of this section is to prove a quite sharp
L^{p}
version of the Bernstein inequality for harmonic functions in
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
. We start with a Bernstein inequality for harmonic polynomials, which was proved in
[
16
]
. The sharp inequality for the supremum norm in dimensions two and three was studied by Szegö
[
20
]
several decades earlier.

Lemma 2
.

Let
P_{N}
be a harmonic polynomial of degree
N
in
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
and let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Then

(29)

\|\nabla P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}\lesssim\frac{N}{r}\|P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}.

Proof.

The inequality follows from Lemma 4.2 in
[
16
]
, where it is proved that

\|P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(\partial B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r))}\lesssim\|P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(\partial B(0,r))}.

This easily implies

(30)

\|P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r))}\lesssim\|P_{N}\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}.

The Cauchy estimate and properties of subharmonic functions imply

|\nabla P_{N}(x)|\lesssim\frac{N}{r}\strokedint_{B(x,\frac{r}{N})}|P_{N}|.

Then, applying Hölder’s inequality, we get

\int_{B(0,r)}|\nabla P_{N}|^{p}\lesssim\frac{N^{p}}{r^{p}}\int_{B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r)}|P_{N}|^{p}.

Finally, (
30
) gives the required inequality.
∎

We will now extend the Bernstein inequality to harmonic functions. The role of the degree of a polynomial will be played
by the frequency function, which we define as follows:

(31)

N_{h}(x,r)=\frac{r\int_{B(x,r)}|\nabla h|^{2}}{2\int_{\partial B(x,r)}|h|^{2}}

for a function
h\in C(\overline{B(0,r)})
harmonic in
B(x,r)
. Note that for a homogeneous harmonic polynomial
P
we have
N_{P}(0,r)=N
. In general,
N_{h}(x,r)
is a non-decreasing function of
r
and

(32)

\strokedint_{B(0,2r)}|h|^{2}\leq 2^{2N_{h}(0,2r)}\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|^{2}

for any function
h
harmonic in
B(0,R)
with
R>2r
; see for instance
[
12
, Corollary 1.5]
for a proof. We also note that the mean value property for harmonic functions implies that

(33)

\left(\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\sup_{B(0,r)}|h|\lesssim\strokedint_{B(0,2r)}|h|.

The main result of this section is the following Bernstein inequality for harmonic functions:

Theorem 4
.

Let
h
be a harmonic function in
B(0,R)\subset{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
such that
N_{h}(0,\rho)\leq N
for any
\rho<R
and let
r<R/2
. Then

(34)

\|\nabla h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}\lesssim\frac{N}{r}\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))},

for any
p\in[1,\infty]
.

The derivation of Theorem
4
from Lemma
2
can also be found in
[
13
]
. To prove the theorem we approximate a harmonic function with frequency bounded by
N
by a harmonic polynomial of degree
5N
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
a>0
is a fixed constant. There exists
N_{0}=N_{0}(a,d)
such that for any function
h\in C(\overline{B(0,2r)})
harmonic in
B(0,2r)
which satisfies
N_{h}(0,2r)\leq N
for some
N>N_{0}
, there is a harmonic polynomial
P
of degree at most
5N
such that

\sup_{B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r)}|h-P|\leq a\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|.

Proof.

For
x\in B(0,2r)
we write
x=\xi\rho
, where
\rho=|x|
.
We can decompose
h
into spherical harmonics,

h(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}Y_{k}(\xi)\rho^{k},

where
\strokedint_{\partial B(0,1)}Y_{j}Y_{k}=\delta_{jk}
.
Note that

\strokedint_{\partial B(0,\rho)}h^{2}=\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\rho^{2k}
and

N_{h}(0,2r)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}ka_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}}.

We also have

(35)

\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{2k+d}|a_{k}|^{2}r^{2k}.

Consider the decomposition

h(x)=P(x)+Q(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{5N}a_{k}Y_{k}(\xi)\rho^{k}+\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}Y_{k}(\xi)\rho^{k}.

The frequency bound
N_{h}(0,2r)\leq N
implies that

\sum_{k}ka_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq N\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}.

Therefore we have:

5N\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}ka_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq N\sum_{k=0}^{5N}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}+N\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}.

The above inequality and (
35
) give

\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=0}^{5N}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq\frac{10N+d}{4d}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{2k+d}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\\
\leq\frac{10N+d}{4d}\strokedint_{B(0,2r)}|h|^{2}.

Now, applying (
32
) twice and then (
33
) for
B(0,r/2)
, we get

(36)

\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\leq C2^{4N}N\left(\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|\right)^{2},

when
N>N_{0}
.

Our aim is to estimate the norm of
Q=h-P
. Let us first remind the reader that we have the following pointwise bounds for (
L^{2}

normalized) spherical harmonics:

|Y_{k}(\xi)|\leq Ck^{\frac{d-2}{2}}.

This would follow from general results of Hörmander on
L^{\infty}
bounds for eigenfunctions, but since for spherical harmonics the proof is much simpler we briefly outline the argument. Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
denote the space of spherical harmonics of degree
k
, i.e., corresponding to eigenvalue
k(k+d-2)
, and let
Z_{k}(\xi,\eta)
denote the reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, also called the zonal spherical harmonic. Then, denoting by angular brackets the
L^{2}
pairing on
S^{d-1}
, we have

\displaystyle|Y_{k}(\xi)|=|\langle Y_{k}(\eta),Z_{k}(\xi,\eta)\rangle|\leq\|Y_{k}\|_{2}\|Z_{k}(\xi,\cdot)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{dim(\mathcal{H}_{k})}\leq Ck^{\frac{d-2}{2}},

where for the second and third inequalities we refer to
[
1
, Chapter 5]
.

Let
b=1+\frac{1}{N}
. From the estimates on spherical harmonics it follows that, for
x\in B(0,br)
,

|Q(x)|\lesssim\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}|a_{k}|k^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(br)^{k}.

Applying the Cauchy inequality and (
36
), we get

|Q(x)|\lesssim\left(\sum_{5N+1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}(2r)^{2k}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{5N+1}^{\infty}k^{d-2}\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2k}\right)^{1/2}\\
\lesssim N^{\frac{1}{2}}2^{2N}\left(\sum_{5N+1}^{\infty}k^{d-2}\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2k}\right)^{1/2}\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|.

Comparing the series
\sum k^{d-2}(b/2)^{2k}
to a geometric series, we conclude that, for
N>N_{0}
,

\sum_{k=5N+1}^{\infty}k^{d-2}\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^{2k}\leq(5N)^{d-2}2^{-5N}.

This implies

|Q(x)|\lesssim N^{\frac{d-1}{2}}2^{-\frac{N}{2}}\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|.

We complete the proof of the lemma by choosing
N_{0}=N_{0}(a,d)
large enough.
∎

Remark
.

The proof implies the following statement. There is
q<1
such that if
u
is a harmonic function and
N_{u}(0,2r)\leq N
,
N\geq 2
, then there is a harmonic polynomial
P
of degree not exceeding
5N
such that

\sup_{B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r)}|h-P|\leq Cq^{N}\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|.

The celebrated Bernstein-Walsh theorem implies that harmonic functions in a domain
\Omega
can be approximated uniformly by polynomials on a compact subset of
\Omega
and the error of approximation decay exponentially, see for example
[
2
]
. Lemma
3
can be also deduced from this result and inequalities (
32
) and (
33
).

Proof of Theorem
4
.

As in the proof of Lemma
2
it suffices to show that

\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,(1+\frac{1}{N})r))}\leq C\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}.

Let
b=1+\frac{1}{N}
. We apply the above lemma with
a=b^{-d}/2
. It gives
h=P+Q
, where
P
is a harmonic polynomial of degree
d\leq\max\{5N,5N_{0}\}
and
Q
satisfies

\sup_{B(0,br)}|Q|\leq\frac{1}{2b^{d}}\strokedint_{B(0,r)}|h|.

Then, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, by Hölder’s inequality, we have

\|Q\|_{L^{p}(B(0,br))}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,br))}.

Finally, the triangle inequality and (
30
) imply the required estimate:

\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,br))}\leq 2\|P\|_{L^{p}(B(0,br))}\leq CN\|P\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}\leq 2CN\|h\|_{L^{p}(B(0,r))}.

∎
