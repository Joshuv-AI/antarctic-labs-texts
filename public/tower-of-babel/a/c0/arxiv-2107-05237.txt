On the Stable category of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules over Gorenstein rings
Tony J. Puthenpurakal

Abstract
. Let ( A , 𝔪 ) , ( B , 𝔫 ) (A,\mathfrak{m}),(B,\mathfrak{n}) be Gorenstein local rings and let CM ¯ ⁡ ( A ) \operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A) be its stable category of finitely generated maximal Cohen-Macaulay A A -modules. Suppose CM ¯ ⁡ ( A ) ≅ CM ¯ ⁡ ( B ) \operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B) as triangulated categories. Then we show (1) If A A is an abstract complete intersection of codimension c c then so is B B . (2) If A , B A,B are Henselian and not hypersurfaces then dim A = dim B \dim A=\dim B . (3) If A A (and so B B ) are complete hypersurface singularity and multiplicity of A A is at least three then dim A − dim B \dim A-\dim B is even. (4) If A , B A,B are Henselian and A A is an isolated singularity then so is B B . We also give some applications of our results. It should be remarked that if R , S R,S are complete Cohen-Macaulay but not necessarily Gorenstein and if there is an triangle isomorphism between the singularity categories of R R and S S then it is possible that dim R − dim S \dim R-\dim S is odd, see M. Kalck; Adv. Math. 390 (2021), Paper No. 107913.

1.
introduction

Representation theory of Artin Algebras is a well established branch of mathematics. Auslander discovered that many concepts in representation theory of Artin algebras have
natural analogues in the study of maximal Cohen-Macaulay ( = MCM) modules of a commutative Cohen-Macaulay local ring
A
. See
[
35
]
for a nice exposition of these ideas. In this paper we study analogues of a natural concept in theory of Artin algebras in the study of MCM modules over commutative Gorenstein local ring.

Let
R
be a commutative Artin ring and let
\Lambda
be a not-necessarily commutative Artin
R
-algebra. Let
\operatorname{\underline{mod}}(\Lambda)
denote the stable category of
\Gamma
.
The study of equivalences of stable categories of Artin
R
-algebras has a rich history; see
[
1
, Chapter X]
. By following Auslander’s idea we investigate equivalences of the stable
category of MCM modules over commutative Cohen-Macaulay local rings. If
A
is Gorenstein local then the stable category of MCM
A
-modules has a triangulated structure. So as a first
iteration in this program, in this paper we investigate triangle equivalences of stable categories of MCM modules over commutative Gorenstein rings.

In general, stable category of Artin algebras (over a commutative Artin ring) need not be triangulated. So by an equivalence we just mean as equivalence of additive categories over a commutative Artin ring. If
\Gamma
is a self-injective Artin
R
-algebra then
\operatorname{\underline{mod}}(\Gamma)
is triangulated. Furthermore if

f\colon\operatorname{\underline{mod}}(\Gamma)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{mod}}(\Lambda)
is an equivalence of additive categories
then
f
commutes with the shift functor on the corresponding triangulated categories; see
[
1
, 1.12, Chapter X]
. However for the best of our knowledge this isomorphism is
NOT
natural
. So it does not follow that
f
is an equivalence of triangulated categories.

If two finite dimensional self-injective algebra’s over a field are derived equivalent then they are also stably equivalent by a result of Rickard, see
[
31
, 2.2]
. Another result of Rickard’s shows that if two rings are derived equivalent then their center’s are isomorphic, see
[
30
, 9.2]
. Thus derived equivalences
of commutative rings are not interesting.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a commutative Gorenstein local ring with residue field
k
. Let
\operatorname{CM}(A)
denote the full subcategory of finitely generated MCM
A
-modules and let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
denote the stable category of MCM
A
-modules. Recall that objects in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
are same as objects in
\operatorname{CM}(A)
. However the set of morphisms
\underline{\operatorname{Hom}_{A}}(M,N)
between
M
and
N
is
=\operatorname{Hom}_{A}(M,N)/P(M,N)
where
P(M,N)
is the set of
A
-linear maps from
M
to
N
which factor through a finitely generated free module. It is well-known that
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is a triangulated category with translation functor
\Omega^{-1}
, (see
[
9
]
; cf.
2.11
). Here
\Omega(M)
denotes the syzygy of
M
and
\Omega^{-1}(M)
denotes the co-syzygy of
M
. Also recall that an object
M
is zero in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
if and only if it is free considered as an
A
-module. Furthermore
M\cong N
in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
if and only
if there exists finitely generated free modules
F,G
with
M\oplus F\cong N\oplus G
as
A
-modules. If
A
is regular local then all MCM
modules are free. So
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)=0
.

We use Neeman’s book
[
25
]
for notation on triangulated categories. However we will assume that if
\mathcal{C}
is a triangulated category then
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
is a set for any objects
X,Y
of
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
R
be a commutative Noetherian ring. Let
D^{b}(R)
be the bounded derived category of
R
. A complex
X
of
R
-modules is said to be perfect if it is isomorphic in
D^{b}(R)
to a bounded complex of finitely generated projective
R
-modules. The subcategory
D^{b}_{perf}(R)
of perfect complexes is equal to
[R]
the thick hull of
R
considered as a subcategory of
D^{b}(R)
(see
[
9
, 1.2.1]
). The quotient category
D^{b}(R)/[R]
is called the singularity category of
R
and is denoted by
D_{sg}(R)
. Note if
R
is of finite Krull dimension then
D_{sg}(R)=0
if and only if
R
is a regular ring. Buchweitz proved that if
(R,\mathfrak{n})
is a Gorenstein local ring then
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(R)\cong D_{sg}(R)
(see
[
9
, 4.4.1]
).

I

Properties invariant under stable equivalences:

I (a):
We say a local ring
A
is a
geometric complete intersection
if
A=Q/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c})
where
Q
is regular local and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{c}
is a
Q
-regular sequence. We say
A
is an
abstract complete intersection
if the completion
\widehat{A}
is a geometric complete intersection. Geometric complete intersections are abstract complete intersections but the converse is
not
true, see
[
18
, section 2]
.

Our first result is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A,B
be Gorenstein local rings. Assume
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
. If
A
is an abstract complete intersection of co-dimension
c
then
B
is also an abstract complete intersection of co-dimension
c
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an abstract complete intersection of co-dimension
c
. For
1\leq r\leq c
let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(A)
be the thick subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
generated by MCM
A
-modules with complexity
\leq r
(for definition of complexity see
2.6
).
If
A
and
B
are abstract complete intersections with infinite residue fields then it essentially follows from techniques used to prove
1.1
that if
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(A)
is triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq s}(B)
then necessarily
r=s
. However the following result is a bit un-expected:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
,
(B,\mathfrak{n})
be geometric complete intersections of

co-dimension
m,n\geq 2
respectively and with algebraically closed residue fields. Let

r\geq\max\{m/2,n/2\}
and also
r\leq\min\{m,n\}
. If
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(A)
is triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(B)
then necessarily

m=n
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
uses techniques developed by Avramov and Buchweitz in
[
5
]
.
We make the following:

Conjecture 1.3
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
,
(B,\mathfrak{n})
be geometric complete intersections of

co-dimension
m,n
respectively and with algebraically closed residue fields. Let

r\leq\min\{m,n\}
. If
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(A)
is triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}_{\leq r}(B)
then necessarily

m=n
.

I (b):

Our next result shows that in many cases stable equivalence preserves dimension.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m}),(B,\mathfrak{n})
be Henselian Gorenstein with
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
.
Then

(1)

If
A
is not a hypersurface ring then
\dim A=\dim B
.

(2)

If
A
is a hypersurface ring having an MCM
A
-module
M
with
\Omega(M)\ncong M
then
\dim A-\dim B
is even.

Remark 1.5
.

(1)

If
A
is a geometric hypersurface ring with multiplicity
\geq 3
then there exists an MCM
A
-module
M
with
\Omega(M)\ncong M
.
(
Sketch of a proof:
) A geometric hypersurface has an Ulrich module
U
(i.e.,
U
is an MCM
A
-module with multiplicity equal to its number of minimal generators), see
[
20
, 2.4]
. If multiplicity of
A
is
\geq 3
then by
[
26
, Theorem 2]
it follows that
if
U
is any Ulrich
A
-module then
\Omega(U)\ncong U
.

(2)

Knörrer periodicity
[
24
]
gives examples of hypersurface singularities with
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
and
\dim A-\dim B
a non-zero
even number. Note that Knörrer does not show the functor defined in
[
24
, Section 3]
is triangulated. However this is not difficult to prove. We also note that Knörrer periodicity does not preserve multiplicity. So multiplicity is not a stable invariant.

(3)

If
(A,\mathfrak{m})
is an excellent Henselian Gorenstein ring which is an isolated singularity then the functor
-\otimes\widehat{A}\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(\widehat{A})
is an equivalence of triangulated categories. This fact is essentially contained in proof of
Theorem 2.9 in
[
34
]

(also see
[
23
, Remark A.6]
).

(4)

Let
R,S
be complete Cohen-Macaulay but not necessarily Gorenstein and if there is an triangle isomorphism between the singularity categories of
R
and
S
then it is possible that

\dim R-\dim S
is odd, see
[
22
]
. We believe the essential reason this occurs is that if
(R,\mathfrak{n})
is non-Gorenstein then
D_{sg}(R)
might not be Krull-Schmidt. In fact the example in
[
22
, 1.5]
is not Krull-Schmidt.

1.6
.

For a local ring
(A,\mathfrak{m})
let
\operatorname{Spec}^{0}(A)=\operatorname{Spec}{A}\setminus\{\mathfrak{m}\}
denote the punctured spectrum of
A
. Let
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}^{0}(A)
denote the full subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
generated by MCM
A
-modules free on
\operatorname{Spec}^{0}(A)
. We note that if
E
is an MCM
A
-module free on
\operatorname{Spec}^{0}(A)
, all syzygies of
E
are also free on
\operatorname{Spec}^{0}(A)
.
Furthermore
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}^{0}(A)
is a thick subcategory of
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
.
Proof of Theorem
1.4
also shows the following result:

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m}),(B,\mathfrak{n})
be Henselian Gorenstein with triangle equivalence
\Psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
.
Then if
M\in\operatorname{\underline{CM}}^{0}(A)
then
\Psi(M)\in\operatorname{\underline{CM}}^{0}(B)
. In particular if
A
is an isolated singularity then so is
B
.

We now discuss the technique used to prove Theorem
1.4
and Lemma
1.7
. The notion of Auslander-Reiten (AR) triangles was first introduced by Happel in Hom-finite Krull-Schmidt triangulated categories, see
[
16
, 1.4]
. Later in
[
29
, I.2.3]
it is shown that a Hom-finite Krull-Schmidt triangulated category has (right) AR-triangles if and only if right Serre-functor. Our observation is that the notion of AR-triangle is categorical. Note if
A
is Henselian then
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is Krull-Schmidt. So one can define notion of AR-triangle(ending at
M
) when
M
is indecomposable
N\xrightarrow{f}E\xrightarrow{g}M\xrightarrow{h}\Omega^{-1}(N)
in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
(see
6.4
). As our category
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is no-longer Hom-finite it requires a proof that AR-triangles are unique upto isomorphism of triangles in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
; see
6.7
. Finally we prove that there is an AR-triangle ending at
M
if and only if
M\in\operatorname{\underline{CM}}^{0}(A)
. Furthermore
N\cong\Omega^{-d+2}(M)
.
We then show that if
\psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
is a triangle-equivalence and there is an AR-triangle ending in
M
then
\psi
induces naturally an AR-triangle ending in
\psi(M)
.
This proves Lemma
1.7
. Finally we prove Theorem
1.4
using some techniques from
[
27
]
.

II

Applications:

(a)
Essential periodicity of complete intersections:

Recall a module
M
is said to be periodic if
\Omega^{r}(M)\cong M
for some
r\geq 1
.
Note if
A
is Cohen-Macaulay then a periodic module is necessarily MCM
A
-module.
Every abstract complete intersection has a periodic MCM module
M
with
\Omega^{2}(M)\cong M
.
We show:

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an abstract complete intersection of codimension
c
. Let
\mathcal{C}
be some triangulated category with shift functor
\Sigma
. If
\phi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is a non-zero functor of triangulated categories then there exists
X\neq 0
in
\mathcal{C}
with
\Sigma^{2s}(X)\cong X
for some
s\geq 1
. If residue field of
A
is infinite then we may choose
s=1
.

Remark 1.9
.

We note that in Theorem
1.8
the functor
\phi
is
not
necessarily an equivalence. It is simply
non-zero
.

A trivial corollary of Theorem
1.8
is:

Corollary 1.10
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an abstract complete intersection and let
B
be a Gorenstein local ring with no periodic modules. Then any triangulated functor
\Psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
is zero.

(b)
Periodic complexes with finite length cohomology :

Let
\mathbb{F}\colon\cdots\rightarrow F^{i}\xrightarrow{d^{i}}F^{i+1}\rightarrow\cdots
be a co-chain complex of finitely generated free
A
-modules. We say
\mathbb{F}
is periodic if
\mathbb{F}\cong\mathbb{F}(-s)
for some
s>0
. We say
\mathbb{F}
is a minimal complex if
d^{i}(F^{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{m}F^{i+1}
for all
i
. If
M
is a periodic
A
-module (i.e.,
\Omega^{r}(M)\cong M
for some
r\geq 1
) then splicing a minimal resolution of
M
we can construct a minimal periodic acyclic co-chain complex. If we drop the assumption on acyclicty then we may ask whether there exist minimal periodic complexes with finite length cohomology. We prove

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a Noetherian local ring which is a quotient of a regular local ring. Then there exists a minimal periodic (of period
2s
)co-chain complex
\mathbb{F}
of finitely generated free
R
-modules (i.e.,
\mathbb{F}\cong\mathbb{F}[2s])
) such that
H^{i}(\mathbb{F})
has finite length for all
i
. If
R
is regular or if residue field of
R
is infinite we can choose
s=1
. In this case there exists a minimal complex
\mathbb{G}
of finitely generated free modules with
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{G}[2]
and
H^{i}(\mathbb{G})
has finite length for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 1.12
.

(a)

Theorem
1.11
has no content if
\dim R=0
; for in that case we may consider the co-chain complex

\cdots\rightarrow R\rightarrow 0\rightarrow R\rightarrow 0\rightarrow\cdots

(b)

If
\dim R>0
and
\mathbb{F}
is two periodic with finite length cohomology then necessarily
\operatorname{rank}\mathbb{F}^{2i}=\operatorname{rank}\mathbb{F}^{2i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. (see Lemma
7.6
).

(b (i))
2
-periodic co-chain complexes with finite length cohomology over a regular local ring:

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a regular local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
.
As MCM modules over
A
are free we get
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)=0
. By
[
9
, 4.4.1]
it follows that the homotopy category of exact acyclic co-chain complexes of finitely generated free modules over
A
is zero. An initial motivation to prove Theorem
1.11
for us was to prove whether periodic co-chain complexes of finitely generated free modules (with finite length co-homology) exist up to homotopy over regular local rings. However we make the following:

Conjecture 1.13
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a regular local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
. Let
\mathbb{F}
be a non-zero minimal 2-periodic co-chain complex
\mathbb{F}
of finitely generated free
A
-modules such that
H^{i}(\mathbb{F})
has finite length for all
i
.
Then

(1)

\operatorname{rank}\mathbb{F}^{i}\geq d
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

There exists a minimal 2-periodic co-chain complex
\mathbb{G}
of finitely generated free
A
-modules such that
H^{i}(\mathbb{G})
has finite length for all
i
and
\operatorname{rank}\mathbb{G}^{i}=d
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Next we give results which support our conjecture.

(a) We prove (1) for
d=1,2,3,4
.

(b) We show it for
d=1,2,3
.

In general we construct a minimal
2
-periodic co-chain complex
\mathbb{H}
with
\operatorname{rank}\mathbb{H}^{i}=2^{d}
and
H^{i}(\mathbb{H})
has finite length for all
i
.

2.
notation and preliminaries

In this section we introduce some notation and discuss some preliminaries.
In this paper all rings are Noetherian and all modules considered are finitely generated. We let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of non-negative integers. Nothing in this section is a new result.

2.1
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be local with residue field
k
and let
M
be an
A
-module. By
\ell(M)
we denote the length of
M
and by
\mu(M)
we denote the number of its minimal generators. Set
\beta_{i}(M)=\ell(\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_{i}(k,M))=\ell(\operatorname{Ext}_{A}^{i}(M,k))
the
i^{th}
betti number of
M
.

2.2
.

Let
S=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}S_{n}
be a graded algebra over a ring
S_{0}
. Let
M
be a graded
S
-module. If
m
is a homogeneous element of
M
then we set
|m|=
degree of
m
.

We need the following well-known result.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
S=K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}]
be a polynomial ring with
\deg X_{i}=2
for all
i
. Let
M
be a graded
S
-module of dimension
r\geq 1
. Then there exists homogeneous
x
of degree
2s
such that
\ker(M(-|2s|)\xrightarrow{x}M)
has finite length kernel. If
K
is infinite then we may choose
s=1
.

2.4
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a function. Recall
f
is said to be of polynomial type if there exists
p(X)\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
such that
p(n)=f(n)
for all

n\gg 0
. Notice
p(X)
is uniquely determined by
f
and we write it as
p_{f}(X)
. We also write
\deg f=\deg p_{f}(X)
. We make the convention that the zero polynomial has degree
-\infty
.
The following result is easy to prove.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
f
be of polynomial type of degree
r
.

(1)

For
a\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
g(n)=f(n)+f(n+a)
is of polynomial type and
\deg g\leq r
.

(2)

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\geq 0
we have
h(n)=f(an+b)
is of polynomial type and
\deg h\leq r
.

(3)

For
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\geq 0
we have
t(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(ia+b)
is of polynomial type and
\deg t(n)\leq r+1
. ∎

(4)

For
r\geq 0
the function
s(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}i^{r}
is of polynomial type of degree
r+1
.

2.6
.

We need to recall the notion of complexity of a module. This notion was introduced by Avramov in
[
3
]
. More generally we define complexity of a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

\operatorname{cx}f=\inf\left\{b\in\mathbb{N}\left|\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(n))}{n^{b-1}}<\infty\right.\right\}.

Let
\beta_{i}^{A}(M)=\ell(\operatorname{Tor}^{A}_{i}(M,k))
be the
i^{th}
Betti number of
M
over
A
. Let
\beta_{M}
be the function defined as
\beta_{M}(n)=\beta_{i}^{A}(M)
. The complexity of
M
over
A
is defined by

\operatorname{cx}_{A}M=\operatorname{cx}\beta_{M}.

Note that
\operatorname{cx}_{A}M=0
if and only if
\operatorname{projdim}_{A}M<\infty
. Furthermore
\operatorname{cx}_{A}M\leq 1
if and only if
M
has bounded Betti numbers.
If
A
is a local abstract
complete intersection of
\operatorname{codim}c
then
\operatorname{cx}_{A}M\leq c
for any
A
-module
M
; see
[
14
, 4.1]
.

Remark 2.7
.

We note that
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}f(n)/n^{b-1}<\infty
if and only if there exists a polynomial
p(X)\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
with
\deg p(X)=b-1
and positive leading coefficient such that
f(n)\leq p(n)
for all
n\gg 0
.

2.8
.

For
i=1,\ldots,r
let
f_{i}\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be functions.
Set
f(n)=\max\{f_{i}(n)\mid 1\leq i\leq r\}
. Then it is clear that

\operatorname{cx}f\leq\max\{\operatorname{cx}f_{i}\mid 1\leq i\leq r\}.

We will need the following result. Although elementary we give a proof for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be functions such that there exists
n_{0}
and
d
such that

f(n+d)\leq f(n)+g(n)+g(n-1)\quad\text{for all}\ n\geq n_{0}.

If
\operatorname{cx}g\leq r
then
\operatorname{cx}f\leq r+1
.

Proof.

Set
h(n)=g(n)+g(n-1)
for
n\geq 1
and
h(0)=0
. Then clearly
\operatorname{cx}h\leq r
.
We have
f(n+s)\leq f(n)+h(n)
for
n\geq n_{0}
.
For
i=0,\ldots,d-1
set

f_{i}(m)=f(n_{0}+i+dm)

We have

f_{i}(m)\leq f(n_{0}+i)+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}h(n_{0}+i+kj).

Using
2.5
(4) we get
\operatorname{cx}f_{i}\leq r+1
.
It follows that
\operatorname{cx}f\leq r+1
.
∎

2.10
.

Let
N
be an
A
-module. For each non-negative integer
n
, let
\pi^{n}

6.2

Let

Let

6.4
.

The notion of AR-triangles in a triangulated category was introduced by Happel

First note that the notion of split monomorphism and split epimorphism makes sense in any triangulated category. They are called section and retraction respectively in

A triangle

(AR2)

By considering the functor

It is also easy to see that (AR3l) is equivalent to

Remark 6.5

It is shown in

We first prove:

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
with

We note that

satisfies the hypothesis of (1). It follows that
\beta(\mathbb{F})=\beta(\mathbb{F}/x\mathbb{F})\geq 2
. We prove that
\beta(\mathbb{F})=2
is not possible.

Suppose if possible there exists
\mathbb{F}
with
\beta(\mathbb{F})=2
.

By Lemma
7.12
we get that
\operatorname{image}\partial^{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. So
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{image}\partial^{i}=1
for all
i
. As
\ell(H^{i}(\mathbb{F}))<\infty
for all
i
we get that
\operatorname{rank}\ker\partial^{i}=1
for all
i
. Thus
\ker\partial^{i}
is isomorphic to an ideal
I_{i}
in
A
. Note this isomorphism maps
\operatorname{image}\partial^{i-1}
to an ideal
J_{i}\subseteq I_{i}
of
A
. We note that
J_{0}
is generated by
\leq 2
elements. Say
J_{0}=(u,v)
.

Claim-(i)
H^{i}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Suppose if possible
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})=0
. By Lemma
7.12
we get
H^{-1}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
. We have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow I_{-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{-1}\xrightarrow{\partial^{-1}}\mathbb{F}^{0}\rightarrow\operatorname{image}\partial^{0}\rightarrow 0.

By depth Lemma we get
I_{-1}
is free and so
\cong A
. As
J_{-1}
is generated by
2
elements we get that
H^{-1}(\mathbb{F})
is
not
of finite length; a contradiction.
Thus
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
. Similarly
H^{1}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
. As
\mathbb{F}
is
2
-periodic we get
H^{i}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

As
A
is regular local it is in particular a UFD. Suppose
u=au_{1}
and
v=av_{1}
where
u_{1}
and
v_{1}
do not have any common factors.

Claim-(ii)
\mathfrak{m}\notin\operatorname{Ass}A/J_{0}
.

If we prove claim (ii) then result follows from claim (i) as
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})
is a submodule of
A/J_{0}
.
If
u_{1}=1
or
v_{1}=1
then
J_{0}=(a)
and clearly this implies Claim (ii).
So
u_{1}\neq 1
and
v_{1}\neq 1
. Notice
\operatorname{height}(u_{1},v_{1})\geq 2
and so
=2
. Thus
u_{1},v_{1}
is an
A
-regular sequence. If
a=1
then also clearly the result follows.
Thus also
a\neq 1
. For
p,q\in A
we write
p\mid q
if
p
divides
q
and
p\nmid q
otherwise. Choose a prime element
p\in\mathfrak{m}
such that
p\nmid a
. Suppose if possible
\mathfrak{m}\in\operatorname{Ass}A/J_{0}
. Say
\mathfrak{m}=(0\colon\overline{t})
where
t\notin J_{0}
.
So
pt\in J_{0}=(au_{1},av_{1})
. It follows that
a\mid t
. Say
t=at_{1}
. Then check that
\mathfrak{m}t_{1}\subseteq(u_{1},v_{1})
. As
u_{1},v_{1}
is an
A
-regular sequence we get
t_{1}\in(u_{1},v_{1})
. This implies that
t=at_{1}\in J_{0}
which is a contradiction. So claim (ii) holds and as discussed before this proves our result.

(3) Let
x
be a regular parameter. Then
A/(x)
is regular local of dimension
3
. Note
\mathbb{F}/x\mathbb{F}
satisfies the hypothesis of (2). It follows that
\beta(\mathbb{F})=\beta(\mathbb{F}/x\mathbb{F})\geq 3
. We prove that
\beta(\mathbb{F})=3
is not possible.

Suppose if possible there exists
\mathbb{F}
with
\beta(\mathbb{F})=2
.

As all cohomology modules have finite length we have

\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{image}\partial^{i-1}+\operatorname{rank}\ker\partial^{i-1}=\operatorname{rank}\ker\partial^{i}+\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{image}\partial^{i}=3.

By Lemma
7.12
we get that
\operatorname{image}\partial^{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. So without any loss of generality we may assume
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{image}\partial^{-1}=1
. As
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})
has finite length we get
\operatorname{rank}\ker\partial^{0}=1
. Thus
\ker\partial^{0}
is isomorphic to an ideal
I
in
A
. Note this isomorphism maps
\operatorname{image}\partial^{-1}
to an ideal
J\subseteq I
of
A
. We note that
J
is generated by
\leq 3
elements. Say
J=(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})
. As
A
is a UFD we may choose
a
which is the greatest common divisor of
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
. Say
u_{i}=av_{i}
for
i=1,2,3
. Set
K=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
. Then
J=aK
and either
K=A
or
2\leq\operatorname{height}K\leq 3
(the first inequality is easily seen by taking a primary decomposition of
K
).

Claim (iii)
K=A
.

Assume the claim for the time being. Then
J\cong A
is free. So
0ptJ=4
. As
0ptI\geq 2
we get that if
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
then
0ptH^{0}(\mathbb{F})\geq 1
; which is a contradiction. So
H^{0}(\mathbb{F})=0
. So
I=J\cong A
. By Lemma
7.12
we get
H^{1}(\mathbb{F})\neq 0
. We have an exact sequence

0\rightarrow I\rightarrow\mathbb{F}^{0}\rightarrow\operatorname{image}\partial^{0}\rightarrow 0.

Counting depths we have
0pt\operatorname{image}\partial^{0}\geq 3
. Also
0pt\ker\partial^{1}\geq 2
. So

0ptH^{1}(\mathbb{F})\geq 2
which is a contradiction. Thus it suffices to prove Claim (iii).

Proof of Claim (iii):
Suppose if possible
K\neq A
. Let
P
be a minimal prime of
K
. We have
2\leq\operatorname{height}P\leq 3
. Note
\mathbb{F}_{P}
is exact. As
A_{P}
is regular local we get
J_{P}
is free. In particular
J_{P}
is principal. But
J_{P}=(a)_{P}K_{P}
is definitely not principal as
\operatorname{height}K_{P}\geq 2
. So claim (iii) is true and as shown earlier this implies our result.
∎

Remark 7.13
.

We do not know an example of a
2
-periodic complex
\mathbb{F}
over a regular local ring of dimension
4
such that
\beta(\mathbb{F})=4
.

Finally we show:

Theorem 7.14
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a regular local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
. Then there exists a
2
-periodic minimal complex
\mathbb{F}
with finite length cohomology such that

\beta(\mathbb{F})=2^{d}
.

Proof.

Set
(B,\mathfrak{n})=(A[Y]/(Y^{2}),(\mathfrak{m},Y))
and
k=A/\mathfrak{m}=B/\mathfrak{n}
.
Let
\mathbb{G}
be a minimal complete resolution of
\Omega^{d}_{B}(k)
. Then same argument as in proof of Theorem
7.3
shows that
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{G}\otimes_{B}A
is a minimal
2
-periodic complex with finite length cohomology. Thus it suffices to prove
\beta(\mathbb{F})=\beta(\mathbb{G})=2^{d}
. Equivalently it suffices to prove that
\beta_{i}(\Omega^{d}_{B}(k))=2^{d}
for all
i
. As
\Omega^{d}_{B}(k)
does not have a free module as a direct summand (see
[
12
, 1.3]
)
it suffices to show
\mu(\Omega^{d}_{B}(k))=\beta_{d}^{B}(k)=2^{d}
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
be a regular system of parameters of
A
. Then note that
\mathbf{x}
considered in
B
is a regular sequence in
\mathfrak{n}\setminus\mathfrak{n}^{2}
. Set
C=B/\mathbf{x}B
.
By
[
33
, 5.3]
we get

\beta_{d}^{B}(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{d}{i}\beta_{i}^{C}(k).

Now notice
C=k[Y]/(Y^{2})
. Then the minimal resolution of
k
over
C
is given as:

\cdots\rightarrow C\xrightarrow{Y}C\xrightarrow{Y}C\xrightarrow{Y}k\rightarrow 0.

So
\beta_{i}^{C}(k)=1
for all
i
. It follows that
\beta_{d}^{B}(k)=2^{d}
.
∎
