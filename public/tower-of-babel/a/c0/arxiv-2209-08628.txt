Transverse instability of concentric soliton waves
R. Krechetnikov 1

Abstract
Should it be a pebble hitting water surface or an explosion taking place underwater, concentric surface waves inevitably propagate. Except for possibly early times of the impact, finite amplitude concentric water waves emerge from a balance between dispersion or nonlinearity resulting in solitary waves. While stability of plane solitary waves on deep and shallow water has been extensively studied, there are no analogous analyses for concentric solitary waves. On shallow water, the equation governing soliton formation – the nearly concentric Korteweg-de Vries – has been deduced before without surface tension, so we extend the derivation onto the surface tension case. On deep water, the envelope equation is traditionally thought to be the nonlinear Schrödinger type originally derived in the Cartesian coordinates. However, with a systematic derivation in cylindrical coordinates suitable for studying concentric waves we demonstrate that the appropriate envelope equation must be amended with an inverse-square potential, thus leading to a Gross-Pitaevskii equation instead. Properties of both models for deep and shallow water cases are studied in detail, including conservation laws and the base states corresponding to axisymmetric solitary waves. Stability analyses of the latter lead to singular eigenvalue problems, which dictate the use of analytical tools. We identify the conditions resulting in the transverse instability of the concentric solitons revealing crucial differences from their plane counterparts. Of particular interest here are the effects of surface tension and cylindrical geometry on the occurrence of transverse instability.

Introduction

The dissonance between the
Airy 1845

Russell 1844

Boussinesq 1872

Boussinesq 1877

Kadomtsev & Petviashvili 1970
and
Zakharov & Rubenchik 1974
, respectively, but all the subsequent studies have been mostly limited to plane KdV and NLS solitons, i.e. the effects of curvature of the solitary wave as well as of its amplitude decay due to cylindrical geometry have not been systematically addressed; for review of the vast literature on the topic see
Kivshar & Pelinovsky 2000
;

(

;
Grimshaw 2007
;

, this set of problems proved to be understudied as not only instability to transverse perturbations has not been explored, but also the equations governing the solitary wave dynamics in the deep-water limit have not been derived, while in the shallow-water limit the underlying asymptotic assumptions and the structure of the equation and its axisymmetric solutions in the presence of surface tension have not been fully understood. It is the goal of the present study to fill in this gap and, applying the Ursell criterion, to deduce appropriate weakly nonlinear models generating concentric solitary waves in the presence of surface tension and to analyze their transverse instability.

As models for studying the transverse instability of the finite-amplitude concentric carrier waves, we will consider the deep water case resulting in the nearly concentric NLS-type (ncNLS) and the shallow water case resulting in the nearly concentric Korteweg-de Vries (ncKdV) equations, respectively; the term “near concentric” is used to distinguish from the “concentric” limits cNLS and cKdV having no azimuthal dependence. The former (NLS-type), to the author’s knowledge, has not been derived before. Since the systematic derivation is technically involved, to make it more transparent in §
2.1
we guide the reader through the key steps in the derivation of a NLS-type equation in cylindrical geometry including the effects of surface tension, while highlighting the differences in the derivation from the plane case. As for the latter (ncKdV), it has been previously derived by

3.1

Waves on deep water

2.2
On the origin of the potential and its implications

Since the work of
Zakharov 1968
, where 2D NLS was derived, it has been tacitly assumed the Laplacian
\Delta
stays intact when applies the NLS to axisymmetric case
(
Zakharov & Synakh 1976
;
Jones 1988
)
; however, the principle of covariance (coordinate-independence) is applicable only to the fundamental physical laws such as Euler’s equations of fluid motion, not amplitude equations deduced from them under concrete asymptotic assumptions despite their ‘universal’ character.

To understand the origin of the potential term
V(R)\sim\frac{1}{R^{2}}
in (
24
,
28
), let us perform a heuristic derivation of the linear part of the envelope equation in the case of pure gravity-driven waves. To bring in more physical intuition, let us consider the linear part of (
9
) back in dimensional variables:

\displaystyle z\leq\varepsilon\,\eta(t,x)

\displaystyle:\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\phi\equiv\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\theta^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial z^{2}}=0,\\
\nabla\phi\rightarrow 0,\ z\rightarrow-\infty,\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle z=\varepsilon\,\eta(t,x)

\displaystyle:\quad\phi_{z}=\eta_{t},

(30c)

\displaystyle z=\varepsilon\,\eta(t,x)

\displaystyle:\quad\phi_{t}+g\eta=0,

(30d)

the straightforward analysis of which in the axisymmetric case leads to the following form of the solution for the free surface elevation:

\displaystyle\eta(t,r)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\widehat{\eta}_{0}(k)J_{0}(kr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega(k)t}\,k\mathrm{d}k}+\mathrm{c.c.},

(31)

where
\widehat{\eta}_{0}(k)
is the Hankel transform of the initial free surface deflection
\eta_{0}(r)
. The asymptotic expansion of this expression away from the origin,
kr\gg 1
, and in the form of a narrow wavepacket
|\delta k|=|k-k_{0}|\ll k_{0}
near some fixed wavenumber
k_{0}
yields

\eta(t,r)=e^{\mathrm{i}(k_{0}r-\omega_{0}t)}\frac{\varepsilon^{1/2}}{R^{1/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{\eta}_{0}(k_{0},\kappa)\bigg[e^{\mathrm{i}\left(\kappa R-\omega^{\prime}(k_{0})T-\frac{\omega^{\prime\prime}(k_{0})}{2}\kappa^{2}\tau-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\\
+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{R}\right)\bigg]\,\kappa^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\kappa+\mathrm{c.c.},

(32)

that is
\eta(t,r)
is a traveling wave
e^{\mathrm{i}(k_{0}r-\omega_{0}t)}
modulated with an envelope function

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(T,\tau,R)\sim\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\widehat{\eta}_{0}(k_{0},\kappa)e^{\mathrm{i}\left(\kappa R-\omega^{\prime}(k_{0})T-\frac{\omega^{\prime\prime}(k_{0})}{2}\kappa^{2}\tau-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\,\kappa^{1/2}\mathrm{d}\kappa}

(33)

evolving on slow time
T=\varepsilon t
,
\tau=\varepsilon^{2}t
and spatial
R=\varepsilon r
scales, which naturally appear in this narrow wavepacket approximation
\kappa=\delta k/\varepsilon
. Taking the derivatives of (
33
), we get the following factors for the integrand in (
33
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{0T}\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}\kappa\omega^{\prime}(k_{0})}{R^{1/2}},\ &\psi_{0\tau}\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}\kappa^{2}\omega^{\prime\prime}(k_{0})}{2R^{1/2}},\\
\psi_{0R}\sim-\frac{1}{2}R^{-3/2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\kappa}{R^{1/2}},\ \psi_{0RR}&\sim\frac{3}{2}R^{-5/2}-\mathrm{i}\kappa R^{-3/2}-\kappa^{2}R^{-1/2},\end{split}

(34)

where we omitted the sign of integration for brevity, and immediately find that

\displaystyle\psi_{0T}+\frac{\omega^{\prime}(k_{0})}{2R}\psi_{0}+\omega^{\prime}(k_{0})\psi_{0R}=0,

(35)

which is the no-resonance condition (
19
) identified above in the course of the formal analysis, as well as

\displaystyle\psi_{0\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\,\omega^{\prime\prime}(k_{0})}{2}\left(\psi_{0RR}+\frac{1}{R}\psi_{0R}-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}\right)=0,

(36)

which is almost the same as the linear part of (
24
) except for the coefficient in front of the potential, i.e.
-3/4
vs
-1/4
. Notably, with the transformation
\psi_{0}=R^{-1/2}\widetilde{\psi}_{0}(\tau,R)
the above equation reduces to the 1D Schrodinger equation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{0\tau}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\omega^{\prime\prime}(k_{0})}{2}\widetilde{\psi}_{0RR}=0,

(37)

i.e. the effect the potential
-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
plays in (
36
) is to modify the amplitude of the wave as it travels either to or from the origin; this, in turn, explains the appearance of the potential in our system – without it the wave would travel as a “free particle” with unmodified amplitude.

A salient feature of the above heuristic derivation was the assumption that the wavepacket changes its width in the same fashion as in the 1D case. This is evident from the approximation (
32
), which is valid only in the limit
kr\rightarrow\infty
. However, as the behavior of the Bessel function
J_{0}(kr)
entails for large, but finite
kr
, the speed of propagation changes as one gets closer to the origin: this effect leads to the more severe change in the wavepacket width and, in fact, when the corresponding wavelength
\lambda=2\pi/k
becomes shorter than the distance
r
from the origin, is responsible for the formation of a singularity in the form of a spike jet. Therefore, in order to account for a stronger wavepacket width change, the potential must be modified from that of
-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
, and, as we saw from the formal derivation in §
2.1
, the potential indeed becomes stronger (through a modified factor), in the sense that it will lead to a stronger singularity of the solution near the origin compared to
\sim R^{-1/2}
in (
36
) as we will see in §
2.4
.

The resulting envelope equations (
24
,
28
) arise from a balance between nonlinearity and dispersion of the wavepacket, which occurs only at some distance from the origin as the wave amplitude varies with it – this is a crucial difference from the translationally invariant case when one can take the limit of small amplitude solutions and be left with the same linear part; in the case of cylindrical waves this is no longer the case, i.e. the linear part of (
24
), when nonlinearity and dispersion are balanced, does not correspond to (
36
), when nonlinearity is absent. Notably, for both potentials
-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
and
-\frac{3}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
the wave amplitude drops as
R^{-1/2}
, but the behavior near the origin proves to be different (§
2.4
). Finally, the technical reason for the appearance of the
-\frac{3}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
potential instead of
-\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\psi_{0}
is due to the first term in the second-order no-resonance condition (
23
), which entangles both
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{0}
– this effect is absent in the plane (1D and 2D) cases. In any case, the appearance of an inverse-square potential is a generic property of cylindrical envelope wave equations; for example, a derivation of NLS from Maxwell’s equations in nonlinear options gives the factor
-1
at the inverse-square potential. As we saw from (
28
), in the case of waves on deep water this factor changes with surface tension as
\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}/\lambda_{\infty}
.

Our NLS equations (
24
,
28
) with the inverse-square potential belong to the Gross-Pitaevskii type
(
Gross 1961
;
Pitaevskii 1961
)
, originally derived to describe the ground state wavefunction of a quantum system composed of a Bose-Einstein condensate in an external potential and nonlinearity is responsible for the interaction between particles. The interested reader may find a mechanistic interpretation of equation (
28
) in Appendix
A
. Notably, an inverse-square potential also arises, though not in the context of NLS, in the motion of a charged particle in the field of a stationary electric dipole, in quantum mechanics
(
Case 1950
;
Kalf et al. 1975
;
Reed & Simon 1979
)
, molecular physics
(
Camblong et al. 2001
)
, nuclear physics
(
Beane et al. 2001
;
Esteve 2002
)
, black holes
(
Regge & Wheeler 1957
;
Zerilli 1970
;
Moncrief 1974
;
Strominger 1998
;
Claus et al. 1998
;
de Azcárraga et al. 1999
;
Solodukhin 1999
;
Michelson & Strominger 2000
;
Papadopoulos 2000
;
Bellucci et al. 2002
;
Carlip 2002
)
,
in wave propagation on conic manifolds
(
Cheeger & Taylor 1982
)
, and in the theory of combustion
(
Bebernes & Eberly 1989
)
.

Since in our case the potential is
V(R)=-\frac{3}{4R^{2}}
and the Laplacian are of equal strength, the former cannot be neglected and the GP equation retains the NLS scaling symmetry

\displaystyle u(\tau,R)\mapsto\lambda u(\lambda^{2}\tau,\lambda R).

(38)

Because of that it is known to have some peculiar properties such as no ground state, i.e. there is no lower limit on the allowed energies
(
Essin & Griffiths 2006
)
and symmetry breaking anomaly emerges in the process of renormalization
(
Essin & Griffiths 2006
;
Camblong et al. 2000
;
Coon & Holstein 2002
)
. The spatial operator in (
24
) or, more generally, in (
28
):

\displaystyle L=\Delta_{R}+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{\infty}}\frac{1}{R^{2}},

(39)

has as eigenfunctions
L\phi_{\lambda}=\lambda\phi_{\lambda}
either modified Bessel function of real order
I_{\nu}\left(\lambda^{1/2}R\right)
,
K_{\nu}\left(\lambda^{1/2}R\right)
with
\nu^{2}=\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}/\lambda_{\infty}>0
, which are unbounded at infinity and origin, respectively, or of imaginary order
I_{\mathrm{i}\nu}\left(\lambda^{1/2}R\right)
,
K_{\mathrm{i}\nu}\left(\lambda^{1/2}R\right)
with
-\nu^{2}=\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}/\lambda_{\infty}<0
, which have highly oscillatory behavior with the period decreasing near the origin. As we will see in §
2.4
, these observations will have certain implications for the structure of solutions of (
24
,
28
), which could be regular and singular.

Without the potential
V(R)
, the corresponding standard NLS is of critical type since the dimension of the problem is
d=2
, while the order of the nonlinearity
|\psi_{0}|^{2n}
is
n=1
, so that
n\,d=2
. This borderline case separates the subcritical NLS with
n\,d<2
when all solutions exist globally from the supercritical NLS with
n\,d>0
, where singular solutions exist
(
Fibich 2015
)
. Finally, while the standard defocusing NLS has a purely “dispersive” character, i.e. no solitary waves of the type

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\tau,R)=e^{\mathrm{i}\mu\tau}u(R)

(40)

exist and focusing NLS does have ground states (
40
) that are unstable leading to a finite-time blow-up, both focusing and defocusing GP have solutions of the form (
40
) as we will see in §
2.4
. Singular solutions of GP equation are as valuable as the widely studied finite-time singularities peculiar to NLS
(
Glassey 1977
)
– such singularities are indicative of a localized behavior in the original unreduced physical system such as the Euler equations, from which (
28
) is deduced.

Finally, as follows from the derivation in §
2.1
, the deduced GP equations (
24
,
28
) are valid only at asymptotically large distances
R=\mathcal{O}(1)
from the origin. Hence, while the deduced Gross-Pitaevskii equation captures the singularity at the origin, which is naturally expected at the origin as in the spike solutions
(
McAllister et al. 2022
)
, due to limitations its applicability in that region, one should not seek quantitative accuracy in describing the details of the corresponding singularities. Also, the symmetry of (
28
) does not preclude from a possibility of ring-type singularities at a finite distance from the origin, where the GP equation is applicable, which will be shown in §
2.4
. In this context it is worth pointing out that the extensive and controversial research on the rate at which the singularity is approached starting with
Kelley 1965
;
Zakharov & Synakh 1976
(see also overview in
Rypdal & Rasmussen 1986
and
Sulem & Sulem 1999
) is flawed not only because it was unjustifiably assumed that the Laplacian in the 2D NLS deduced in the Cartesian coordinates stays intact when the NLS is applied to an axisymmetric case, but also because the NLS and GP equations in the axisymmetric case are applicable only at sufficiently large distances from the origin. The inapplicability of the NLS model near the blow-up where focusing levels are high (sometimes claimed
(
Fibich 2015
)
necessary to be
\gg 10^{48}
for the self-similar asymptotic rates to be valid) is also obvious as NLS was deduced only for sufficiently small, but finite, amplitudes allowing a balance with the dispersion effects, and the assumptions behind its derivation are no longer valid when the amplitude of the solution becomes incommensurate with the narrow wave-packet assumption.

2.3
Conservation laws, variance, and finite-time singularity

To analyze the conservation laws of the GP equation (
28
), from physical considerations we supply the initial-value problem (IVP) for this equation with the BCs:

\displaystyle R=0:\ \psi_{R}=0;\ R\rightarrow\infty:\ \psi\rightarrow 0.

(41)

Multiplying (
28
) with
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{r}-\mathrm{i}\psi^{i}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\,\overline{\psi}\,\psi_{\tau}+\lambda_{\infty}\overline{\psi}\,\Delta_{R}\psi+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{R^{2}}|\psi|^{2}+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{R^{2}}\overline{\psi}\psi_{\theta\theta}-\chi_{\infty}\,|\psi|^{4}=0,

(42)

and taking the imaginary part, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}+\lambda_{\infty}\left(\psi^{r}\Delta_{R}\psi^{i}-\psi^{i}\Delta_{R}\psi^{r}\right)+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{R^{2}}\left(\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{\theta\theta}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{\theta\theta}\right)=0,

(43)

where we took into account that
\overline{\psi}\,\psi_{\tau}=\psi^{r}\psi^{r}_{\tau}+\psi^{i}\psi^{i}_{\tau}+\mathrm{i}\left(\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{\tau}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{\tau}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}+\mathrm{i}\left(\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{\tau}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{\tau}\right)
,
\overline{\psi}\,\Delta_{R}\psi=\psi^{r}\Delta_{R}\psi^{r}+\psi^{i}\Delta_{R}\psi^{i}+\mathrm{i}\left(\psi^{r}\Delta_{R}\psi^{i}-\psi^{i}\Delta_{R}\psi^{r}\right)
and similar equalities for
\overline{\psi}\,\psi_{\theta\theta}
. Next, since the integral of the second term in (
43
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left[\psi^{r}\left(\psi^{i}_{RR}+\frac{1}{R}\psi^{i}_{R}\right)-\psi^{i}\left(\psi^{r}_{RR}+\frac{1}{R}\psi^{r}_{R}\right)\right]R\,\mathrm{d}R}\\
&=R\left[\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{R}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{R}\right]_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left[\psi^{i}_{R}\left(\psi^{r}R\right)_{R}-\psi^{r}_{R}\left(\psi^{i}R\right)_{R}\right]\,\mathrm{d}R}\\
&+\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left[\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{R}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{R}\right]\,\mathrm{d}R}=0\end{split}

(44)

vanishes in view of the BCs (
41
) as well as the integral of the last term in (
43
):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\left[\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{\theta\theta}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{\theta\theta}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\theta}=\left[\psi^{r}\psi^{i}_{\theta}-\psi^{i}\psi^{r}_{\theta}\right]_{0}^{2\pi}-\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\left[\psi^{r}_{\theta}\psi^{i}_{\theta}-\psi^{i}_{\theta}\psi^{r}_{\theta}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\theta}=0

(45)

due to periodicity in
\theta
, equation (
43
) leads to the conservation of the number of particles (in analogy to quantum mechanics):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int{|\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}=0,

(46)

which is the consequence of the invariance of (
28
) under the phase-shift; the integration over the cylindrical measure
\mathrm{d}\nu
is defined as

\displaystyle\int{\circ\,\mathrm{d}\nu}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\circ\,\mathrm{d}\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{x\mathrm{d}x}.

(47)

Similarly, multiplying (
28
) with
\overline{\psi}_{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\,|\psi_{\tau}|^{2}+\lambda_{\infty}\overline{\psi}_{\tau}\,\Delta_{R}\psi+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{R^{2}}\overline{\psi}_{\tau}\psi+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{R^{2}}\overline{\psi}_{\tau}\psi_{\theta\theta}-\chi_{\infty}\,|\psi|^{2}\overline{\psi}_{\tau}\psi=0,

(48)

and taking the real part of the resulting expression, we get

\lambda_{\infty}\left(\psi^{r}_{\tau}\Delta_{R}\psi^{r}+\psi^{i}_{\tau}\Delta_{R}\psi^{i}\right)+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{R^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}\\
+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{R^{2}}\left(\psi^{r}_{\tau}\psi^{r}_{\theta\theta}+\psi^{i}_{\tau}\psi^{i}_{\theta\theta}\right)-\chi_{\infty}|\psi|^{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}=0,

(49)

where we again took into account that
\psi^{r}\psi^{r}_{\tau}+\psi^{i}\psi^{i}_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left(\psi^{r2}+\psi^{2i}\right)
. Next, integrating by parts

\int_{0}^{\infty}{f_{\tau}\Delta_{R}f\,R\,\mathrm{d}R}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{f_{\tau}\left(f_{RR}+\frac{1}{R}f_{R}\right)\,R\,\mathrm{d}R}=\left.f_{R}f_{\tau}R\right|_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}{f_{R}\left(f_{\tau}\,R\right)_{R}\,\mathrm{d}R}\\
+\int_{0}^{\infty}{f_{R}f_{\tau}\,\mathrm{d}R}=\left.f_{R}f_{\tau}R\right|_{0}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{0}^{\infty}{Rf_{R}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}R},

(50)

and applying this result to
f=\psi^{r}
and
\psi^{i}
with the BCs (
41
), equation (
49
) takes the form of the conservation of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int{\left[-\frac{\lambda_{\infty}}{2}|\psi_{R}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{2R^{2}}|\psi|^{2}-\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{2R^{2}}|\psi_{\theta}|^{2}-\frac{\chi_{\infty}}{4}|\psi|^{4}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\nu}=0;

(51)

here we simplified the last term in (

),
|\psi|^{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}|\psi|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi^{r2}+\psi^{i2})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}(\psi^{r2}+\psi^{i2})=\frac{1}{4}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}(\psi^{r2}+\psi^{i2})^{2}
, and also took into account that
\int_{0}^{2\pi}{f_{\tau}f_{\theta\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}=\left.f_{\tau}f_{\theta}\right|_{\theta=0}^{2\pi}-\int_{0}^{2\pi}{f_{\tau\theta}f_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{f_{\theta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta}
when integrating the third term in (

),
\psi^{r}_{\tau}\psi^{r}_{\theta\theta}+\psi^{i}_{\tau}\psi^{i}_{\theta\theta}
. Hence, the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\int{\left[-\frac{\lambda_{\infty}}{2}|\psi_{R}|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{2R^{2}}|\psi|^{2}-\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{2R^{2}}|\psi_{\theta}|^{2}-\frac{\chi_{\infty}}{4}|\psi|^{4}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\nu}.

(52)

Finally, given the above expression for the Hamiltonian, it can be shown (cf. Appendix
B
) that the evolution of the variance
\mathcal{V}(\tau)=\int{R^{2}|\psi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu}
, also known as the wave power (a variant of the power curve introduced by
Vakhitov & Kolokolov 1973
), obeys

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\lambda_{\infty}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}=-4\,\mathcal{H}+2\pi\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}|\psi(\tau,0)|^{2},

(53)

integrating which yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\tau)=-8\mathcal{H}\tau^{2}+8\pi\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\tau^{\prime}}{|\psi(\tau^{\prime\prime},0)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime\prime}}}+\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)\tau+\mathcal{V}(0).

(54)

Should
\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}=0
as in the case of the standard NLS, then, if the initial conditions are such that
\mathcal{H}>0
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)=-16\mathcal{H}<0
, from the solution of the quadratic equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)\frac{\tau_{*}^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)\tau_{*}+\mathcal{V}(0)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \tau_{*}=\frac{-\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)+\sqrt{\mathcal{V}^{\prime 2}(0)-2\mathcal{V}(0)\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)}}{\mathcal{V}^{\prime\prime}(0)},

(55)

where necessarily
\mathcal{V}(0)>0
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(0)<0
, it follows that there exists a finite time

such that
\mathcal{V}\rightarrow 0
in contradiction to its definition, which shows that it has to be positive. The
H^{1}
-solution must therefore develop a singularity no later than the time

,
|\psi|\rightarrow\infty
,
|\psi_{R}|\rightarrow\infty
at
R\rightarrow 0
. This means that the solution gets out of the

-space, so that the condition of

being positive (when

) does not need to be satisfied any longer. The analogous behavior is known for the standard NLS equations
(

)
. However, the presence of the potential leads to an extra term

in (

is positive-definite, the finite-time singularity still takes the place, while for

It is known that a truly solitary wave occurs only if the phase speed of the carrier wave coincides with the group velocity of the envelope which happens at a certain wavenumber

2.6
Lagrange-Dirichlet stability analysis

While the above spectral analysis provides certain insights into stability, strictly speaking only spectral instability implies linear (and hence nonlinear) instability, while spectral stability does not even imply linear stability
(
Krechetnikov & Marsden 2007
)
. A good visual understanding of the solution stability picture is provided by figure

4a
is structurally unstable as any small perturbation will drive it away from the saddle point. With these considerations in mind, let us look at the stability picture from the Hamiltonian finite-amplitude viewpoint starting with equation (

\displaystyle R=\alpha\,x,\ \psi=\alpha^{-1/2}\beta\,y,\ \tau=\gamma\,t,

(106)

with factors appropriate for

\displaystyle\alpha=\left(-\lambda_{\infty}/\mu\right)^{1/2},\ \beta=\left(-\lambda_{\infty}/\chi_{\infty}\right)^{1/2}\left(-\mu/\lambda_{\infty}\right)^{1/4},\ \gamma=\mu^{-1},

we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}y_{t}-\left(-\frac{\gamma\lambda_{\infty}}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\Delta_{x}y+\frac{\gamma\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{y}{x^{2}}+\frac{\gamma\mu_{\infty}}{\alpha^{2}}\frac{y_{\theta\theta}}{x^{2}}=\frac{\gamma\beta^{2}\chi_{\infty}}{\alpha}|y|^{2}y,

\displaystyle-\frac{\gamma\lambda_{\infty}}{\alpha^{2}}=1,\ \frac{\gamma\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{\alpha^{2}}=-\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{\infty}}=\frac{1}{4}-d,\ \frac{\gamma\mu_{\infty}}{\alpha^{2}}=-\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{\lambda_{\infty}},\ \frac{\gamma\beta^{2}\chi_{\infty}}{\alpha}=1,

(109)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}y_{t}-\Delta_{x}y-\left(d-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{y}{x^{2}}-\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{\lambda_{\infty}}\frac{y_{\theta\theta}}{x^{2}}=|y|^{2}y,\ \text{where}\ d=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\lambda_{\infty}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{\infty}}.

\displaystyle-v_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{x}u+\left(d-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{u}{x^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{\lambda_{\infty}}\frac{u_{\theta\theta}}{x^{2}}+\left(u^{2}+v^{2}\right)u,

(111a)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{x}v+\left(d-\frac{1}{4}\right)\frac{v}{x^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{\infty}}{\lambda_{\infty}}\frac{v_{\theta\theta}}{x^{2}}+\left(u^{2}+v^{2}\right)v,

3.2
Single concentric soliton

The single concentric soliton, transverse instability of which we will be studying, is governed by the

\displaystyle 2H_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}H+\left(\frac{1}{3}-We\right)H_{\xi\xi\xi}+3HH_{\xi}=0,

as opposed to its deep water counterpart (

in the context of ion-acoustic waves in a collisionless plasma, whose numerical simulations showed that solitary waves are characterized by
A\,\lambda^{2}\simeq\mathrm{const}
, where
A
is the amplitude and

further demonstrated that the amplitude dependence on radial position

. In the context of free-surface gravity waves, equation (
135

Miles 1978a
from the Boussinesq equations, though without surface tension effects and
\tau
replaced by
r
; hence, the self-similar solution was studied in that work in the
(r,\xi)
-variables. Numerically, cylindrical solitary waves were also explored by
Chwang & Wu 1976

135
) were constructed, cf.
Calogero & Degasperis 1978
;
Johnson 1979
, with the inverse scattering transform.

On the symmetry side, note that the dilatation group of transformations

\displaystyle\tau\rightarrow\gamma^{-3/2}\,\tau^{\prime},\ \xi\rightarrow\gamma^{-1/2}\,\xi^{\prime},\ H\rightarrow\gamma\,H^{\prime},

(136)

135

\displaystyle H\,\gamma^{-1}=f(\tau\,\gamma^{3/2},\xi\,\gamma^{1/2}).

(137)

\displaystyle H\,\tau^{2/3}=H^{\prime}\,\tau^{\prime 2/3},\ \xi\,\tau^{-1/3}=\xi^{\prime}\,\tau^{\prime-1/3}\equiv\zeta_{0},

\displaystyle H(\tau,\xi)=\tau^{-2/3}F(\zeta),\ \zeta=\tau^{-1/3}\xi,

135

To analyze the transverse instability of the self-similar solution (

\displaystyle\left[2\,\eta_{\tau}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\tau}\eta^{\prime}+\frac{\alpha}{3}\eta_{\xi\xi\xi}^{\prime}+3\left(H\eta^{\prime}\right)_{\xi}\right]_{\xi}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\eta_{\Theta\Theta}^{\prime}=0.

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime}=\tau^{-2/3}h(\tau,\xi),

\displaystyle(\tau,\xi,\Theta)\rightarrow(\widehat{\tau}=\ln{\tau},\zeta=\tau^{-1/3}\xi,\widetilde{\Theta}=\tau^{1/3}\Theta),

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\tau}\partial_{\widehat{\tau}}-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\zeta}{\tau}\partial_{\zeta}+\frac{1}{3}\frac{\widetilde{\Theta}}{\tau}\partial_{\widetilde{\Theta}},\ \partial_{\xi}=\frac{\zeta}{\xi}\partial_{\zeta}=\frac{1}{\tau^{1/3}}\partial_{\zeta},\ \partial_{\Theta}=\tau^{1/3}\,\partial_{\widetilde{\Theta}}.

\displaystyle\left[2\,h_{\widehat{\tau}}-\frac{2}{3}\left(\zeta h_{\zeta}-\widetilde{\Theta}h_{\widetilde{\Theta}}\right)-\frac{1}{3}h+\frac{\alpha}{3}\,h_{\zeta\zeta\zeta}+3\left(Fh\right)_{\zeta}\right]_{\zeta}+h_{\widetilde{\Theta}\widetilde{\Theta}}=0;

\displaystyle h=e^{\lambda\widehat{\tau}}f(\zeta,\widetilde{\Theta}),

(156)

\displaystyle\left[\left(2\,\lambda-\frac{1}{3}\right)f-\frac{2}{3}\left(\widehat{\zeta}f_{\widehat{\zeta}}-\widehat{\Theta}f_{\widehat{\Theta}}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{6}\,f_{\widehat{\zeta}\widehat{\zeta}\widehat{\zeta}}+\left(v^{2}f\right)_{\widehat{\zeta}}\right]_{\widehat{\zeta}}+f_{\widehat{\Theta}\widehat{\Theta}}=0,

(157)

governed by (

(\widehat{\zeta},\widehat{\Theta})

Homsy 1973

Krechetnikov 2017

At the point, however, we may note an important property of (

\widehat{\Theta}
and produce a regularly spaced “spike” structure. This observation holds regardless how we would scale the angular variable with respect to time and, of course, is contrary to standard intuition, but can be seen as a consequence of an effective ‘nonlinearity’ built-in the linear stability problem through the base state-dependent term

manifesting itself in the interaction of two effects: as the single-soliton travels outwards (1) the circular domain is stretching, which inevitably leads to insertion of new wavelengths via the Eckhaus mechanism
(
Knobloch & Krechetnikov 2014
;
Knobloch & Krechetnikov 2015
;
Krechetnikov & Knobloch 2017
)

1

\widehat{\Theta}
structure and non-existence of a single most amplified wavenumber thus demonstrating the crucial differences between the transverse instability of plane and cylindrical solitons. As we saw in §
2
, this phenomenon, however, does not happen in the deep water case, in particular due to the different underlying dispersive relation.

compared to the layer depth

in the case of ncKdV. However, as we will see in the next section, the short-time stability characteristics of ncKdV with application to the self-similar solution (
139
) are very different from the considered here long-time limit conforming to our intuition developed in the near planar case of npKdV – and this difference is due to the essential time-dependence of the base state (
139
).

It is easy to show that
\chi^{1}
essentially follows the time-evolution of
\chi^{0}
albeit with an algebraic function of
\tau
multiplying the exponential
e^{\lambda\tau}
. For example, focusing on the last term on the rhs of (
161b
) responsible for the input of the azimuthal perturbation, we may look for a particular solution of (
161b
) in the form

\displaystyle\chi^{1}=A(\tau)e^{\mu\xi},\ \text{where}\ A(\tau)\sim e^{-\frac{\alpha\mu^{3}}{6}\tau}\int^{\tau}{\frac{k^{2}}{\widetilde{\tau}^{2}}e^{\left(\lambda+\frac{\alpha\mu^{3}}{6}\right)\widetilde{\tau}}\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\tau}}\sim k^{2}\frac{e^{\lambda\tau}}{\tau}\ \text{for}\ \tau\gg 1.

(162)

As consistent with the observation made earlier, there is no preferred wavenumber
n
in the azimuthal direction. The contribution of the base state, i.e. the first term on the rhs of (
161b
), can be computed analogously, after the transformation (
153
) to self-similar variables
(\tau,\xi)\mapsto(\tau,\zeta)
.

3.4
Kadomtsev-Petviashvili type analysis

Finally, let us develop analysis of transverse instability of the self-similar solution (
139
) to the ncKdV equation (
133
) rewritten, for the ease of notation and comparison with the classical analysis of npKdV
(
Kadomtsev & Petviashvili 1970
;
Alexander et al. 1997
)
, in the form

\displaystyle 2\,\eta_{\tau}+\frac{1}{\tau}\eta+3\,\eta\,\eta_{\xi}+\eta_{\xi\xi\xi}+\frac{\beta}{\tau^{2}}\,\partial_{\xi}^{-1}\eta_{\Theta\Theta}=0,

(163)

after moving the surface tension factor
\frac{\alpha}{3}=\frac{1}{3}-We
to the last term in (
163
) via the rescaling of (
133
) with

\displaystyle\tau\rightarrow\gamma\tau,\ \xi\rightarrow\left(\frac{\alpha\,\gamma}{3}\right)^{1/3}\xi,\ \eta_{0}\rightarrow\left(\frac{\alpha}{3\,\gamma^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\eta,\ \Theta\rightarrow\left(\frac{\alpha\,\gamma^{4}}{3\,\beta^{3}}\right)^{1/3}\Theta,

(164)

without intruding new notations for the variables, but dropping index
0
in
\eta_{0}
; note that in the above rescalings the factor
\beta>0
for
\alpha>0
; if, on the other hand,
\alpha<0
, then the factor
\beta<0
. When the solution does not depend on the transverse coordinate
\Theta
, equation (
163
) admits the self-similar solution (
139
):

\displaystyle\eta\,\tau^{2/3}=F(\xi\,\tau^{-1/3})\ \Rightarrow\ \eta=\tau^{-2/3}F(\zeta_{0}),\ \zeta_{0}=\frac{\xi}{\tau^{1/3}}.

(165)

This is the solution the transverse instability of which we will study by perturbing its amplitude and phase in analogy to the analysis of
Kadomtsev & Petviashvili 1970
(see also
Kodama 2018
;
Ablowitz & Segur 1981
for interpretative accounts), who performed stability analysis of 1D plane
\mathrm{sech^{2}}
-soliton (
7
) with the help of the Krylov-Bogoliubov method
(
Bogoliubov & Mitropolsky 1961
)
, translated here onto the stability analysis of a self-similar solution (
165
):

\displaystyle\eta(t,T,\xi,\widetilde{\Theta})=\tau^{-2/3}\left[1+A(T,\widetilde{\Theta})\right]F\left(\frac{\xi+\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}\right);

(166)

here we will assume
A=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
\varphi=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{c})
with time
T=\epsilon^{a}\tau
and slow transverse coordinate
\widetilde{\Theta}=\epsilon^{b}\Theta
; the exponents
a
,
b
, and
c
are to be determined with the requirement that one must have
b>0
for long-wave instability. The time derivative is calculated to become

\eta_{\tau}=-\frac{2}{3}\,\tau^{-5/3}(1+A)\,F(\zeta)+\tau^{-2/3}\,A_{\tau}\,F(\zeta)\\
+\tau^{-2/3}(1+A)\,F^{\prime}(\zeta)\left[-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\zeta}{\tau}+\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{\tau^{1/3}}\right],

(167)

with
\zeta=(\xi+\varphi)/\tau^{1/3}
, while the first derivative w.r.t.
\Theta
reads

\displaystyle\eta_{\Theta}=\tau^{-2/3}\,A_{\Theta}F(\zeta)+\tau^{-2/3}(1+A)\,F^{\prime}(\zeta)\frac{\varphi_{\Theta}}{\tau^{1/3}},

(168)

and the second derivative w.r.t.
\Theta

\eta_{\Theta\Theta}=\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}A_{\Theta\Theta}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+2b+\frac{2}{3}a})}F(\zeta)+\mathop{2\,\tau^{-2/3}\,A_{\Theta}\,\varphi_{\Theta}\,\tau^{-1/3}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{a+1+c+2b})}F^{\prime}(\zeta)+\\
\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}(1+A)\,\varphi_{\Theta}^{2}\,\tau^{-2/3}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\frac{4}{3}a+2c+2b})}F^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)+\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}(1+A)\,\varphi_{\Theta\Theta}\,\tau^{-1/3}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{a+c+2b})}F^{\prime}(\zeta),

(169)

where under each term we show its order of magnitude once time
\tau
and transverse direction
\Theta
derivatives are understood in their modulational counterparts
T
and
\widetilde{\Theta}
, and orders of
A
and
\varphi
are taken into account; the nonlinear terms in the above expression, under appropriate justification, must be omitted in the linear analysis. Since
\eta_{\xi}=\tau^{-1}(1+A)F^{\prime}(\zeta)
,
\eta_{\xi\xi\xi}=\tau^{-5/3}(1+A)F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\zeta)
, and
\partial^{-1}\xi=\tau^{1/3}\partial^{-1}\zeta
, at the leading order we get the equation for a self-similar soliton:

2\left[-\frac{2}{3}\tau^{-5/3}F(\zeta)+\tau^{-2/3}F^{\prime}(\zeta)\left(-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\zeta}{\tau}\right)\right]+\tau^{-5/3}F(\zeta)+\\
3\,\tau^{-2/3}F(\zeta)\,\tau^{-1}F^{\prime}(\zeta)+\tau^{-2/3}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\zeta)\,\tau^{-1}=0,

(170)

or dividing w.r.t.
\tau^{-5/3}
:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}F(\zeta)-\frac{2\,\zeta}{3}F^{\prime}(\zeta)+3\,F(\zeta)\,F^{\prime}(\zeta)+F^{\prime\prime\prime}(\zeta)=0.

(171)

Since in the cylindrical case there is no translational symmetry, we must expand (
171
) about
\zeta_{0}
as the shift of
x
changes the stability properties of the cylindrical soliton. Thus, taking into account that

\displaystyle\zeta F^{\prime}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\left(F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})+F^{\prime\prime}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}\right)\left(\zeta_{0}+\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}\right),

(172a)

\displaystyle F(\zeta)\,F^{\prime}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\left(F(\zeta_{0})+F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}\right)\left(F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})+F^{\prime\prime}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}\right),

(172b)

linearization of equation (
171
) results in (the first-order perturbation):

-\frac{1}{3}F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}-\frac{2}{3}\left[F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})+\zeta_{0}F^{\prime\prime}(\zeta_{0})\right]\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}+\\
3\left[F(\zeta_{0})F^{\prime\prime}(\zeta_{0})+F^{\prime 2}(\zeta_{0})\right]\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}+F^{(iv)}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi}{\tau^{1/3}}=0,

(173)

which must be added (after multiplying by
\tau^{-5/3}
) to the linearization of (
163
):

\displaystyle 2\left[-\frac{2}{3}\tau^{-1}AF(\zeta_{0})+A_{\tau}F(\zeta_{0})-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\zeta_{0}}{\tau}AF^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})+F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{\tau^{1/3}}\right]+\tau^{-1}AF(\zeta_{0})+

(174)

\displaystyle 3\,\tau^{-1}2\,AF(\zeta_{0})F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})+\tau^{-1}AF^{\prime\prime\prime}(\zeta_{0})+\beta\frac{\tau^{1/3}}{\tau^{2}}\partial_{\zeta}\left[A_{\Theta\Theta}F(\zeta_{0})+\varphi_{\Theta\Theta}\tau^{1/3}F^{\prime}(\zeta_{0})\right]=0,

multiplied by
\tau^{-2/3}
, altogether producing

\displaystyle\varphi\,\tau^{-2}

\displaystyle\left\{-\frac{4}{3}F^{\prime}-\frac{2}{3}\left[F^{\prime}+\zeta_{0}F^{\prime\prime}\right]+F^{\prime}+3\left(FF^{\prime\prime}+F^{\prime 2}\right)+F^{(iv)}\right\}

\displaystyle+2

\displaystyle\left\{\underline{-\frac{2}{3}\tau^{-5/3}AF}+\tau^{-2/3}A_{\tau}F+\underline{\tau^{-2/3}AF^{\prime}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\frac{\zeta_{0}}{\tau}\right)}+\tau^{-2/3}F^{\prime}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{\tau^{1/3}}\right\}

(175)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underline{\tau^{-5/3}AF+6\,A\,\tau^{-5/3}FF^{\prime}+\tau^{-5/3}AF^{\prime\prime\prime}}+\beta\tau^{-2}\partial_{\zeta}^{-1}\left[\tau^{-1/3}A_{\Theta\Theta}F+\tau^{-2/3}\varphi_{\Theta\Theta}F^{\prime}\right]=0.

Multiplying (
171
) evaluated at
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
by
A\,\tau^{-5/3}
eliminates the terms underlined in (
175
) simplifying the latter to

\displaystyle\varphi\,\tau^{-2}\left[-\frac{4}{3}F^{\prime}-\frac{2}{3}\left(F^{\prime}+\zeta_{0}F^{\prime\prime}\right)+F^{\prime}+3\left(FF^{\prime\prime}+F^{\prime 2}\right)+F^{(iv)}\right]+

(176)

\displaystyle 2\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}\frac{\varphi_{\tau}}{\tau^{1/3}}F^{\prime}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2a+c})}+2\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}A_{\tau}F}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{5}{3}a})}+\mathop{3\,A\,\tau^{-5/3}FF^{\prime}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{5}{3}a})}+\beta\tau^{-2}\partial_{\zeta}^{-1}\left[\mathop{\tau^{-1/3}A_{\Theta\Theta}F}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1+\frac{7}{3}a+2b})}+\mathop{\tau^{-2/3}\varphi_{\Theta\Theta}F^{\prime}}_{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{\frac{8}{3}a+c+2b})}\right],

where the expression in the first brackets also vanish because equation (

\zeta=\zeta_{0}

4
Conclusions

With the goal to study stability of axisymmetric solitary waves, in the present work we deduced a proper envelope equation for solitary waves on deep water, which proves to include an inverse-square potential and hence be of Gross-Pitaevskii type (
24
,
28
); in the shallow water limit we rederived a ncKdV equation (
133
) by including surface tension effects and under asymptotic assumptions different from what was known before. In the former case, our derivation is set apart from previous studies which postulated that the corresponding NLS for axisymmetric case has the Laplace operator unchanged – our analysis (§§
2.1
,
2.2
) demonstrates that the covariance principle does not apply to envelope equations despite their “universal” character. Given the novelty of the deduced GP equation for deep water waves, we studied its general properties – conservation laws (§
2.3
), Hamiltonian structure (§
2.6
), finite-time-singularity (§
2.3
) – as well as axisymmetric base states along with geometric and mechanistic interpretations of their varieties (§
2.4
, Appendix
A
).

The stability analysis in the deep water case was performed with the help of both spectral (in the limit of long wavelengths, cf. §
2.5
) and Hamiltonian (for general wavelengths, cf. §
2.6
) methods, which complement each other. The challenge of the spectral stability problem (
81
) was its singular nature dictated by the particularities of the base states (§
2.4
), which nevertheless enable analytical approaches. We revealed the crucial differences in stability characteristics between cylindrical and plane solitons: namely, there is a threshold in the Weber number
We_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
above which instability appears in the deep water case as opposed to the nearly plane NLS (
2
), the 1D plane solitons of which are always unstable to transverse perturbations
(
Zakharov & Rubenchik 1974
)
regardless of the value of
We
. Thus, surface tension must be sufficiently strong to induce a transverse instability of a cylindrical soliton on the deep water
4
4

4

A qualitative interpretation one may offer is that in the case of a plane soliton surface tension breaks it similar to a Rayleigh-Plateau instability of a rectilinear liquid column, which takes place for any magnitude of surface tension as long as it is non-zero, while in the case of a cylindrical soliton the Rayleigh-Plateau instability competes with stabilizing effect of the transverse curvature in the plane of the soliton propagation as well as with the time-dependence of the base state.
.

In the shallow water case, we performed an analysis (§
3.4
) in the spirit of
Kadomtsev & Petviashvili 1970
extending it not only to cylindrical geometry but also to self-similar solitons (
139
), with the resulting linear amplitude equation (
184
), which governs perturbation evolution, being highly-nonautonomous and exhibiting transient growth of transverse perturbations regardless of the value of the Weber number in contrast to its plane counterpart, where there is a non-zero critical Weber number
5
5

5

Obviously, the effect of the solid bottom plays a stabilizing role in the case of plane solitons and thus requires strong enough surface tension to induce a transverse instability, i.e. to get into a Rayleigh-Plateau regime. On the other hand, in the case of a cylindrical soliton the effect of the base soliton time-dependence overpowers any other effects thus leading to transient growth of perturbations, which should trigger nonlinear effects before the subsequent linear dynamics would lead to a decay of the perturbation.
. For long times the stability picture is consistent with the intuition that the dynamics should approach that of npKdV (§
3.3
). Also, for general wavenumbers, from the reduction to a 2D eigenvalue problem (
157
) in the self-similar plane, we made an unexpected conclusion that the transverse perturbations must have an irregular structure in the azimuthal
\theta
-direction, cf. §
3.3
. Numerical study of (
157
), however, represents a challenge for future efforts.

While here we explored only the basic properties of the GP and ncKdV solutions, one might expect that similar to the standard (near planar) versions of these equations, the behavior of their solutions is very rich
(
Cai et al. 2002
)
. Including higher-order terms
(
Dysthe 1979
)
or generalization onto finite depth
(
Hasimoto & Ono 1972
)
of the GP equation may offer further insights in the axisymmetric water waves, same as establishing a relation between GP and ncKdV similar to that between NLS and KdV
(
Boyd & Chen 2001
)
as well as considering the near-critical values of the Weber number
We\rightarrow We_{c}=\frac{1}{3}
in the ncKdV equation, which should bring up fifth-order derivatives
(
Green 1983
;
Hunter & Scheurle 1988
)
. Also, in the derivation of ncKdV equation with surface tension (§
3.1
) we neglected the resonance between the linear (carrier) wave speed
c_{0}
and the linear phase speed
\omega(k)/k\approx g^{1/2}(1+We\,k^{2}h^{2})^{1/2}h
, which exhibits itself in the far-field
(
Boyd 1998
;
Grimshaw & Iooss 2003
;
Grimshaw 2005
)
and occurs because the graph
\omega(k)/k
is not monotonic when
0<We<\frac{1}{3}
; for
We>\frac{1}{3}
the graph of
\omega(k)/k
is monotonic and hence the derivation of ncKdV does not require corrections.

Both types of envelope equations – on deep and shallow water – could be amenable to the inverse scattering transform methods, which may serve as yet another method for studying transverse stability as it was done by
Zakharov 1975
for the KdV solitons. While some solutions of the cKdV (
135
) were constructed with inverse scattering transform, cf.
Calogero & Degasperis 1978
;
Johnson 1979
;
Freeman 1980
, the feasibility of the inverse scattering for the NLS with an inverse-square potential has not been fully explored yet, cf.
Murphy 2019
and references therein, even though NLS with potentials could be suitable to inverse scattering transform analysis and represents an active area of research, cf.
Sasaki 2008
;
Fajun & Li 2019
.

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\left[\overline{\psi}_{\tau}\left(R^{2}\psi\right)_{R}-\psi_{\tau}\left(R^{2}\overline{\psi}\right)_{R}\right]\,\mathrm{d}R}

(197)

(199)

Similarly, since

altogether the second integral on the rhs of (

Appendix C
Asymptotics of ground states at infinity

To find next-order corrections, we introduce a phase
\varphi(x)
such that
|\varphi(x)|\ll x
, so that plugging
y(x)=C\cos{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}
into (
62
) produces

-\cos{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}\,(1+\varphi^{\prime})^{2}-\sin{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}\varphi^{\prime\prime}+\cos{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}\\
\frac{d}{x^{2}}\cos{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}+\frac{C^{2}}{x}\cos^{3}{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}=0.

(211)

After cancelling out the leading-order solution
\cos{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}
, the next order terms are

\displaystyle-2\varphi^{\prime}(x)+\frac{C^{2}}{x}\cos^{2}{\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}x}=C^{2}\frac{1+\cos{2\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}}{4x}.

(212)

Since we are studying the asymptotics at infinity, it is convenient to integrate the latter equation from
x
to some large, but finite
x_{\infty}
, which yields

\displaystyle\varphi(x_{\infty})-\varphi(x)=\frac{C^{2}}{4}\left(\ln{x_{\infty}}-\ln{x}\right)+\frac{C^{2}}{4}\left[\Ci(2x_{\infty})-\Ci(2x)\right],

(213)

where the last term follows from

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x_{\infty}}{\frac{\cos{2\left[x+\varphi(x)\right]}}{x}\mathrm{d}x}=\int_{x}^{x_{\infty}}{\frac{\cos{2\widetilde{x}}}{\widetilde{x}-\varphi(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x}}{1+\varphi^{\prime}(x)}}\mathop{\Longrightarrow}_{x\gg 1}\int_{x}^{x_{\infty}}{\frac{\cos{2\widetilde{x}}}{\widetilde{x}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x}}

(214)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{2x_{\infty}}{\frac{\cos{2\widetilde{x}}}{2\widetilde{x}}\mathrm{d}(2\widetilde{x})}=\int_{2x}^{\infty}{\frac{\cos{2\widetilde{x}}}{2\widetilde{x}}\mathrm{d}(2\widetilde{x})}-\int_{2x_{\infty}}^{\infty}{\frac{\cos{2\widetilde{x}}}{2\widetilde{x}}\mathrm{d}(2\widetilde{x})}\equiv-\Ci{(2x)}+\Ci{(2x_{\infty})}.

Letting
\varphi(x_{\infty})=\frac{C^{2}}{4}\ln{x_{\infty}}+\frac{C^{2}}{4}\Ci{(2x_{\infty})}
, we find the phase

\displaystyle\varphi(x)=\frac{C^{2}}{4}\ln{x}+\frac{C^{2}}{4}\Ci{(2x)}=\frac{C^{2}}{4}\ln{x}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),

(215)

where we took into account the asymptotics of the cosine integral for large argument
z
:

\displaystyle\Ci{z}=\frac{1}{z}\sin{z}-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\cos{z}+\ldots.

(216)

Altogether, the solution
y(x)
of (
62
) for large
x
reads

\displaystyle y(x)=C\cos{\left[x+\frac{C^{2}}{4}\ln{x}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\right]},

(217)

which is equation (
71
). To find the next-order correction we may add a new phase function
\psi(x)
to (
71
), i.e.
y(x)=\cos{\left[x+\frac{C^{2}}{4}\ln{x}+\psi(x)\right]}
; straightforward calculations yield

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\frac{C^{2}}{4}\Ci{(2x)}\approx\frac{C^{2}}{4}\left[\frac{1}{2x}\sin{2x}-\frac{1}{4x^{2}}\cos{2x}\right]+\ldots.

(218)
