Introduction

The stochastic multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem is a widely studied online decision-making problem defined as follows. A player is given
n
arms. For each
i\in[n]
, the
i
-th arm is associated with a reward distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
of mean
\mu_{i}
. In each round
t\in[T]
, the player picks one arm
A_{t}
from the
n
arms and then observes and gains a reward drawn from its associated reward distribution. The goal is to maximize the
expected
cumulative reward in
T
rounds, which is equivalent to minimizing the
regret

R(T):=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{A_{t}}\right]
where
\mathtt{arm}^{*}=\mathop{\arg\max}_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i}
is the arm with the largest mean reward. The minimax regret of the MAB problem, namely the worst case regret of the best algorithm, is well-known to be of the order
\Theta(n^{\frac{1}{2}}T^{\frac{1}{2}})
(see e.g.
[
LS20
]
).

A recent line of work focuses on the MAB problems in the streaming model with memory constraint. In this model, the arms arrive one by one in a stream. The player has a memory of size
m
available which is usually less than the number of arms
n
. Only the indices of
m
arms and their corresponding statistics can be stored by the player and arms not in memory cannot be explored. Once an arm is discarded from the memory, all its information will be forgotten. We also allow
P
passes over the stream for any
P>0
. We refer the reader to
Section

2.1
for a formal definition and some discussions on the model.

The memory constraint arises new challenges for the exploration-exploitation trade-off in the classic MAB algorithms. In the single-pass setting, the work of
[
Wan23
]
proved that
\Omega\left(n^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)
regret is necessary if
m\leq\frac{n}{20}
. They also designed an algorithm that achieves
O\left(n^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)
regret using
\Theta\left(\log^{*}n\right)
memory. This is in contrast to the
\Theta\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}}T^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
regret when no memory constraint is present.

The impact of memory constraints can be mitigated by allowing additional passes over the stream. Intuitively, if the number of passes is equal to
T
, then in each round, the player can always wait until the desired arm appears in the stream and explore it. This was formally justified by the results in
[
AKP22
]
. They proved a regret lower bound of
\Omega\left(4^{-P}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)
for
m=o\left(\frac{n}{P^{2}}\right)
and designed an algorithm with
O\left(T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\sqrt{nP\log T}\right)
regret when
m=O(1)
. Their results showed that the effect of the number of passes
P
provides a smooth transition for the dependency on
T
from
T^{\frac{2}{3}}
when
P=1
to
T^{\frac{1}{2}}
when
P
is sufficiently large.

However, the previous results do not reveal the whole picture of the trade-off among regret, memory, and the number of passes. First, the results hold when
m
is small, i.e.,
m\leq c\cdot n
for some universal constant
c<1
. The lower bound in
[
Wan23
]
implies that increasing the memory from
\Theta(\log^{*}n)
to
\frac{n}{20}
does not help in reducing the regret. However, it is not known whether further increase in memory beyond
\frac{n}{20}
would affect the regret, even in the single-pass setting. Secondly, in the multi-pass setting, there still exists a huge gap between the current upper and lower bounds. The correct dependency on
n
and
P
is unknown, and how the memory
m
affects the regret remains unclear. Clarifying these issues has been left as open problems in
[
AKP22
]
as well.

In this paper, we give an almost complete answer to the relationship between the memory
m
, the number of arms
n
and the number of passes
P
in the regret. We design an algorithm with regret at most
\tilde{O}\left((n-m)^{1+\frac{2^{P}-2}{2^{P+1}-1}}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)
for any
n,m
and
P
and complements it with an
\tilde{\Omega}\left((n-m)^{1+\frac{2^{P}-2}{2^{P+1}-1}}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)
lower bound. Therefore, our results are optimal up to a logarithmic factor in
n
and
P
. In particular, our results show that if the memory further increases until
n-m=o(n)
, the regret will decrease, while the dependency on
T
will not be affected.

1.2
Overview of our algorithms and techniques

Our proofs, both upper bounds and lower bounds, reveal some interesting interplay between the memory and the regret which are not well understood before.

Our first observation is that an optimal algorithm should behave differently for large
m
(
m\geq\frac{8n}{9}
, say) and small
m
. To see this, notice that as did in
[
Wan23
]
, when
m
is small, the low regret algorithm is almost equivalent to applying a
best arm identification
(BAI) algorithm in the stream. The task of BAI, as suggested by
[
AW20
]
and
[
JHTX21
]
, can be done optimally with constant memory. Therefore, increasing the size of memory does not help in reducing its complexity. However, minimizing regret is not equivalent to best arm identification in the sense that identifying the best arm is in general harder than playing with low regret. We show that, in the streaming setting the latter is captured by the complexity of the
best arm retention
(BAR) problem, namely to retain a good arm in memory (without necessarily identifying it) at the end of stream. Our results show that the complexity of the BAR problem exhibits a sharp transition phenomenon: when the memory is relatively small, increasing memory size has almost no effect on reducing the difficulty of BAR (and it is in fact equivalent to BAI); when the memory is close to the number of arms, the difficulty of BAR decreases significantly with the increase of the memory, and thus making the streaming MAB problem easier. This explains the
n-m
terms in our regret bounds.

Our algorithm for small
m
also outperforms previous ones in multi-pass setting
[
AKP22
]
. The algorithm in
[
AKP22
]
achieves
O\left(T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\sqrt{nP\log T}\right)
regret using
O(1)
memory via guaranteeing that the best arm is retained in memory with high probability during each pass. Comparing to their algorithm, we employ the explore-then-commit framework
3
3

3

A strategy in the
explore-then-commit framework
first tries to retain the best arm in the memory during the initial rounds (referred to as the exploration phase) and then play arms in the memory using an optimal MAB algorithm in the remaining rounds (referred to as the exploitation phase).
and introduce a new measure for tracking the algorithm’s progress over passes. Specifically, we notice that bounding the
expected mean reward gap between the optimal arm and the best arm retained by the algorithm
in each pass is sufficient to guarantee a low regret in expectation. This saves unnecessary regret costs to attain high probability results, and is the main ingredient for our optimal algorithm, in both small and large memory case. Using this measure, we can shave off the
\log T
factor in
[
AKP22
]
, and give the correct dependency on
n
,
m
and
P
.

On the other hand, our proof of the lower bound essentially reveals that any algorithm with optimal regret must solve the BAR task well at each pass, which aligns with our algorithm. To be specific, we establish a lower bound on the number of rounds for exploration at each pass on some hard instances, which matches the behavior of our algorithm. Such a lower bound reflects the eternal exploration-exploitation trade-off in online learning algorithms. Let us sketch the proof idea.
When there is only one pass, if an algorithm spends too much time exploring each arm, it may incur significant regret if the last arm in the stream happens to be the optimal one. However, if the earlier arms are sampled too few times, via a likelihood argument, we can show that the algorithm may fail the BAR task on some hard instance and may potentially discard the optimal arm among them with significant probability. This results in large regret during the exploitation phase after the stream ends. The advantage of the multi-pass setting appears to avoid this issue. For example, if one more pass is allowed, we can sample fewer times at the initial pass to quickly gain a rough outlook of all the arms. However, we can design a harder instance such that (1) the knowledge obtained from the first pass about the instance is negligible and (2) the second pass may fail the BAR task on this new instance and incur large regret if the number of rounds for explorations at this pass is not large enough. We eventually generalize the above argument for algorithms with arbitrary
P
passes and establish the desired lower bound.

Comparison with lower bounds in
[
AKP22
]

Our approach significantly differs from, and has advantage over that in
[
AKP22
]
. The work
[
AKP22
]
proved a regret lower bound by designing a distribution over hard instances, which incur large regret in expectation for every
deterministic
algorithm and applying Yao’s principle (
[
Yao77
]
). In their construction, the
n
arms are divided into
P+1
disjoint subsets
\left\{\,\mathcal{K}_{j}\,\right\}_{j\in[P+1]}
, with each subset containing a randomly selected arm
\mathcal{I}_{j}\in\mathcal{K}_{j}
that has biased mean reward with a certain probability. At the end of the first pass, they considered the event of all these selected arms being discarded with little valid information gained. If this bad event occurs and the selected arm in
\mathcal{K}_{P+1}
happens to be unbiased, this problem can then be reduced to a problem with
\frac{P}{P+1}\cdot n
arms (with those arms in
\mathcal{K}_{p}
being ignored) in the remaining
P-1
passes. They showed that if the regret is assumed to be small, the probability of these events is at least
\frac{1}{4}
. This ultimately leads to a
\frac{1}{4^{P}}
term in their lower bound by using the inductive method.

Although the lower bound in
[
AKP22
]
is tight in
T
, it is suboptimal in both
P
and
n
for different reasons. Our proof remedy both. At a high level, the suboptimality in
P
mainly arises from the fact the
n
arms are divided into
P+1
parts, where only the first
P-p+2
parts are effectively utilized in the analysis of the
p
-th pass. Instead of relying on Yao’s principle, we directly prove lower bounds for randomized algorithms via a likelihood argument by providing hard instances for each algorithm.
Specifically, we construct a family of hard instances and show that for any
randomized
algorithm, there exists at least one difficult instance within this family. This intuition is further explained in
Section

6
.

The suboptimality in
n
comes from the fact the analysis in
[
AKP22
]
did not consider the dependency of
n
(as well as
m
) in the regret caused by an algorithm. We incorporate this dependency by capturing the hardness of best arm retention problem. A similar idea also appeared in a recent work (
[
AW24
]
) under a different setting. Furthermore, our analysis also breaks the limitation of
m=O\left(\frac{n}{P(P+1)}\right)
in
[
AKP22
]
and provides a full characterization of the memory-regret trade-off.

2.1
Multi-armed bandit in streaming model

2.1.1
Multi-armed bandit

In the problem of multi-armed bandit (MAB), the player is given a set of
n
arms, denoted as
[n]
. A
T
-round decision game will be conducted as follows: in each round
t\in[T]
,

•

the player picks an arm
A_{t}\in[n]
to pull based on the information observed in so far;

•

the environment choose a reward vector
r_{t}\in[0,1]^{n}
;

•

the player observes and gains reward of the chosen arm
r_{t}(A_{t})
.

The reward can be given
stochastically
or
adversarially
. In the stochastic environment considered in this work, there is a fixed distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
of mean
\mu_{i}
for each
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
. In each round,
r_{t}(i)
is independently drawn from this distribution.

The best arm,
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
, refers to the arm with the largest mean reward. The player’s objective is to minimize the difference between the cumulative reward of the best arm and the player’s own cumulative reward. That is, the player aims to design an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to minimize the expected regret

R(T,\mathcal{A})=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{A_{t}}\right].

We also write
R(T,\mathcal{A})
as
R(T)
when
\mathcal{A}
is clear from the context.

There have been many algorithms achieving a regret of
O\left(\sqrt{nT}\right)
for the MAB problem, such as
online stochastic mirror descent
(OSMD) and
follow the regularized leader
(FTRL). The following result is given in
[
LG21
]
.

Proposition 3

(
[
LG21
]
, Theorem 11)
.

The OSMD algorithm with specific parameters on an MAB game satisfies that
R(T)
is bounded by
\sqrt{2\absolutevalue{\+S}T}
where
\mathcal{S}
is the arm set and
T
is the total rounds.

In this work, the algorithm for MAB is treated as a black box. The details of the algorithm pertaining to
Proposition

3
is in
Appendix

C
.

2.1.2
Streaming MAB

Then we formally define the stochastic MAB problem in the
P
-pass streaming model for any integer
P\geq 1
.

The mechanism of streaming MAB

In the streaming MAB problem, the
n
arms arrive in a stream, and available arms are stored in the memory
\mathcal{M}
which has a maximum capacity of
m
arms for some
m\leq n
. The algorithm can only pull the arms stored in memory and store the statistics of those arms in memory. For each arm in the memory, one word
4
4

4

A word is of size
\Theta(\log nT)
bits
is used to store its statistics such as the identity and
empirical mean
of the arm.

In the round
t\in[T]
, the player selects an arm
A_{t}
, observes information and then gains the reward of the chosen arm. In each round, the player either choose an arm from memory directly, or read new arms from stream into memory and then choose one. When the memory is full, the player must discard some arms to make space before incorporating new ones. In other words, in each round
t\in[T]
, the algorithm acts in two stages, which handles arm storage (drop arms in memory, incorporating new arms in stream) and arm sampling (pull an arm in memory, observe and gain rewards) respectively. We emphasize that in one round, the player can drop and read any number of arms (including zero), as long as they are available in the stream, while exactly one pull is allowed.

Once an arm is dropped, all its information, including its identity, is forgotten. A discarded arm will not reappear in the single-pass setting. In this work, we consider a general setting with
P
passes (
P\geq 1
). In each pass, the
n
arms will pass through the stream one time. Therefore, we may expect a discarded arm to appear again in the next pass. The order of arms in different passes can vary. We say a pass ends once the last arm in the pass is read into the memory. This means that the explorations after this point are treated as samples in the next pass.

Streaming stochastic MAB

Our work only focuses on the stochastic arms. The reward of
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
,
r_{t}(i)
, is drawn from a hidden distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
with mean
\mu_{i}
independently in each round. Similar to the MAB problem, let
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
be the arm with the largest reward mean. The player’s objective is to design an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to minimize the regret, which is defined as
R(T,\mathcal{A})=\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{A_{t}}\right]
.
An algorithm is allowed to have its own randomness. We use

Exploration and exploitation phase

We introduce terminologies to describe the behavior of an algorithm for streaming stochastic MAB on a specific instance. We divide the
T

exploration phase
and
exploitation phase
. The rounds
before
the arrival of the last arm in the last pass constitute the phase of exploration, and the remaining rounds form the phase of exploitation.

We further classify the rounds in the exploration phase into
P

p
begins from the arrival of the last arm in pass
p-1
(or from the start of the game if
p=1

p
is read into memory. We will use a random variable
L_{p}
to denote the number of rounds in pass
p
for any

•

For each
p=1,2,\dots,P
, explore
L_{p}
rounds.

•

For the remaining
T-\sum_{p\in[P]}L_{p}
rounds, play arms in the memory (without interacting with the stream since there is no arm left).

More remarks on the memory model

We consider the general memory size
2\leq m<n
. In a less restrictive memory model, where we are not limited to storing the information related to only the arms in memory, it is possible to achieve sublinear regret through explore-then-commit strategies with multiple passes using a single memory slot. However, in the standard setting where oblivion is compulsory, one memory slot must be reserved to pull the arriving arm and another memory slot must be used to store a good arm encountered so far. Otherwise, it is easy to see a regret lower bound linear in
T
for both multi-pass and single-pass case when
m=1
. Hence, we only focus on the situations
m\geq 2
.

It is worth noting that our
lower bounds
actually apply to that less restrictive memory model. In other words, our lower bound holds even if the algorithm is allowed to store any other information, as long as it satisfies the requirement of storing at most
m
arms. This indicates that, when
2\leq m<n
, the main challenge posed by memory constraints is the limitation on the number of arms that can be stored and can be pulled by the algorithm at each round, rather than the restriction on storing additional statistics.

A recent line of work studies the memory-regret trade-off for the learning with expert advice problem (
[
SWXZ22
,
PZ23
,
PR23
]
). Their model differs from ours in three aspects: (1) unlike the stochastic reward in our model, their feedback is adversarial in nature; (2) the entire reward vector is revealed to the player in each round; (3) the player has the freedom to pick any arm in each round, which corresponds to the case that
P=T
in our model. An interesting problem is to combine these two models, considering the adversarial bandit feedback under the streaming model with limited number of passes. We leave this as an open problem for further investigation.

2.1.3
Best arm identification and best arm retention

Best arm identification

There is a
pure exploration
version of MAB, called
best arm identification
(BAI). In the BAI problem, our objective is to find the arm with the maximum reward with high probability. In each round, the player can either choose an arm to observe its reward, or terminate the game and output an arm index.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, an arm is
\varepsilon
-optimal or
\varepsilon
-best if its mean reward
\mu>\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\varepsilon
. For any fixed
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, we say an algorithm is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-probably approximately correct, or
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC for short, if it can identify an
\varepsilon
-optimal arm with probability at least
1-\delta
. The sample complexity of the algorithm is defined as the number of rounds before its termination.

Best arm retention

We also define another pure exploration version of streaming MAB, named as
best arm retention
(BAR). In BAR, the algorithm needs to output a set of arms of size
m<n
. Our objective is to retain the best arm in the output set with high probability. Unlike BAI, the key point of this problem is not to identify the best arm but only to ensure that the arms discarded are all suboptimal. In this work, we mainly care about the expected mean reward gap between the optimal arm in the output set and
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
.

This problem was also mentioned in
[
AW22
]
as the
trapping the best arm
. We will demonstrate the close relationship between BAR and streaming MAB, as well as algorithms for BAR. One can verify that the algorithm in
Appendix

D
is actually a PAC algorithm for BAR and the sample complexity is tight (up to logarithm factors) with certain parameters.

Streaming BAI and streaming BAR

The BAI and BAR problem can also be considered in the streaming setting as well. Streaming BAI and BAR are pure exploration version of streaming MAB. The
n
arms come in a stream and the algorithm can only store
m<n
arms. The memory model of streaming BAI and BAR is the same with that described in
Section

2.1.2
.

3
Upper Bound Analysis for the
m=n-1
Case

In this section, we present our algorithm and its analysis when
m=n-1
. This is a special case of our more general algorithm in
Section

4
. However, this case already showcases our main idea, while both the algorithm and its analysis are much simpler than in the general case.

As mentioned in
Section

1.2
, in each pass, our algorithm is facing a BAR problem, namely to pick an arm to drop in this pass. Our key observation is that the following quantity turns out to be a correct measure for the quality of the BAR solution in our setting:
the expected mean reward of the optimal arm in the memory at the end of each pass
.

Hence, we claim that a good algorithm should guarantee the following two properties in each pass
p\in[P]
:

•

For
p>1
, the regret caused by exploration in pass
p
should be small compared to
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
, where
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
is the best arm in memory at the end of the previous pass;

•

\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{king}_{p}}\right]
is decreasing in
p
, where
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is the optimal arm (among all
n
arms) and
\mathtt{king}_{p}
is the best arm in memory at the end of pass
p
.

In fact, the quantity
\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{king}_{p}}\right]
implicitly serve as a potential function to measure the progress of our algorithm in the analysis.

To fulfill the first requirement, we only need to identify
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
(or an arm comparable to
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
) and discard those arms that perform significantly worse than
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
as early as possible during subsequent sampling. For the second requirement, we can reduce the problem to a version of the BAR problem for which we design an optimal algorithm.

To highlight our main idea, we further simplify the streaming model and strengthen the player by allowing the algorithm to remember the identity of the dropped arms. This capability is not essential, as we will circumvent it using a trick in the general setting. We also assume that
n=o(\sqrt{T})
and
n,T
are sufficiently large to simplify calculations and avoid the tedium of border cases.

3.1
The algorithm

Let
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
denote the optimal arm (the one with the largest empirical mean) among all
n
arms. For any arm with name
\mathtt{a}
, let
\mu_{\mathtt{a}}
be its mean reward. Let
\mathcal{M}_{p}
be the set of arms in memory at the beginning of pass
p
for any
p\in[P]
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{P+1}
denote the arms in memory in the exploitation phase.

Overview of the algorithm

Our main task in each pass is to solve a BAR problem while reducing regret. At the beginning of the first pass, we first read in
n-1
arms. Choose
2
among them arbitrarily and run a
FindBest
subroutine on the two arms to identify the better one, denoted as
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}
. Then we drop the other arm to incorporate the last upcoming arm in stream.

For subsequent passes
p
, the memory is full at the beginning of the pass. We first perform a shorter
FindBest
subroutine on all the arms in memory to find a good arm
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
. Then we choose two other arms together with
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
to perform a longer
FindBest
subroutine. Note that there is only one unvisited arm in stream in this pass. We then drop the worse arm in the second
FindBest
subroutine and read in the remaining arm.

The description of the algorithm

Let
\lambda_{p}=\frac{2^{P-p+1}-1}{2^{P+1}-1}
for any
p\in[P]
. Set the following parameters:

•

the sample times in the first pass
L_{1}=\left\lceil 2^{-2P}\cdot n^{-2\lambda_{1}}T^{1-\lambda_{1}}\right\rceil
;

•

for any
p\in[P]\setminus\left\{\,1\,\right\}
,

\circ

the sample times of the second
FindBest
in the
p
-th pass
L_{p,2}=\left\lceil 2^{-2P+2p-2}\cdot n^{-2\lambda_{p}}T^{1-\lambda_{p}}\right\rceil

\circ

the sample times of the first

in the
p

for

.

The choice of

is to ensure that, the first

is sufficient to figure out the
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
left from the previous pass. The second

is, the smaller mean reward gap between
\mathtt{king}_{p}
and
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
we can get. In our choice, as

becomes larger as well.
This indicates the strategy of our algorithm: explore prudently when we have little information and intensify the exploration as information accumulates (or equivalently, pass number increases).
For example, when

ends, i.e., after Line

or Line

is executed. For an arm with name
\mathtt{a}
, we let

. Then we have the following lemma, which guarantees that our algorithm solves the BAR problem well in each pass.

Lemma 6
.

\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathtt{king}_{p}}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{p}}{n}\right)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is the set of arms in memory at the beginning of pass
p
. If the optimal arm
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is not in
\mathcal{M}_{p}
, according to
Algorithm

2
, it must be in
\mathcal{M}_{p+1}
and therefore
\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p}}=0
. Hence, we only need to consider the case when
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is in memory when we drop arms from
S_{p}
.

When
p=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n-1}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{n}\right),

where the third inequality holds due to Lemma
5
.

For
p>1
,
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is dropped only if
\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2},\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(\spadesuit)}{\leq}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\neq\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\right]\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\neq\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\right]\cdot\sqrt{\frac{6}{L_{p,2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n-2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{6}{L_{p,2}}}=O\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{p}}{n}\right),

where
(\spadesuit)
follows from Lemma
5
.
∎

We remark that it is crucial to include additional
2
arms in the Line
9
of
Algorithm

2
instead of only
1
arm. This allows us to keep
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
in the memory in Line

. By doing so, the premise condition for
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
being discarded is that
\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}
. The probability of this event can be bounded by
O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
, and the saving here is also key to our tight regret bound in terms of
n
.

With the guarantee in
Lemma

6
, we can then bound the expected regret in each pass and deduce a total regret bound of
Algorithm

2
in the following theorem.

Theorem 7

(regret bound for
Algorithm

2
)
.

For any input instance, the expected regret of
Algorithm

2
in pass
p
is
O\left(2^{-P+p}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)
.

Furthermore, the total regret of
Algorithm

2
satisfies

R(T)\leq O\left(n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right).

Proof.

Let
R_{p}
denote the expected regret generated in the
p
-th pass. When
p=1
, we have
R_{1}\leq L_{1}
. When
p>1
, we compute the regret of the two
FindBest
subroutines separately. For the first one, the total regret can be decomposed into two parts: the regret of the OSMD process with respect to
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
, which can be bounded by
\sqrt{2nL_{p,1}}
according to Proposition
3
; and the regret generated due to the gap between
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
and
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
, which can be bounded by
L_{p,1}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p-1}}\right]
.

For the second subroutine, we can do a similar decomposition. The expected regret of this process is no larger than
\sqrt{6L_{p,2}}+L_{p,2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{2n}{L_{p,1}}}+\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p-1}}\right]\right)
, where
\sqrt{\frac{2n}{L_{p,1}}}
is the bound for the gap between the mean of optimal arm in
\mathcal{S}_{p}
and
\mathtt{king}_{p-1}
. This bound is guaranteed by Lemma
5
.

Recall that
L_{1}=O\left(2^{-2P}\cdot n^{-2\lambda_{1}}T^{1-\lambda_{1}}\right)
,
L_{p,1}=\begin{cases}n^{3}\cdot L_{p-1,2},&p>2\\
n^{3}\cdot L_{1},&p=2\end{cases}
and
L_{p,2}=O\left(2^{-2P+2p}\cdot n^{-2\lambda_{p}}T^{1-\lambda_{p}}\right)
for
p>1
where
\lambda_{p}=\frac{2^{P-p+1}-1}{2^{P+1}-1}
. Combining the two subroutines together,

\displaystyle R_{p}

\displaystyle\leq L_{p,1}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p-1}}\right]+\sqrt{2nL_{p,1}}+L_{p,2}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\frac{2n}{L_{p,1}}}+\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p-1}}\right]\right)+\sqrt{6L_{p,2}}\leq O\left(2^{-P+p}\cdot n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right).

For the exploitation phase, the regret can also be decomposed into the part of OSMD and the regret generated by
\Delta_{\mathtt{king}_{P}}
. Similarly, these terms can be bounded by
\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{P}}\right]\cdot T+\sqrt{2nT}
.

In total, we have

\displaystyle R(T)

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}+\sum_{p=2}^{P}O\left(2^{-P+p}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)+\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{P}}\right]\cdot T+\sqrt{2nT}=O\left(n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right).

∎

4
Upper Bound Analysis for the Large Memory Case

In this section we prove
Theorem

1
when
m\geq\frac{8n}{9}
. The algorithm and its analysis are a natural generalization of the
m=n-1
case studied in
Section

3
. Remember that in order to simplify the presentation, in
Algorithm

2
the algorithm is allowed to remember the identity of the dropped arm in each pass. So here we must address the following two issues arising in the generalization:

•

How to choose more than one inferior arms to drop in each pass?

•

How to modify the algorithm so that we do not need to remember the identity of the dropped armed?

4.1
The algorithm

Consider the game with
n
arms,
P
(
1\leq P\leq\log\log T-\log\left(12\log\frac{n}{n-m}\right)
) passes and memory size of
m
(
m\geq\frac{8n}{9}
). Assume
T\geq(n+1)^{2}
. We adopt the notations defined in
Section

3
.
Let
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
denote the optimal arm (with the largest empirical mean). For any arm with name
\mathtt{a}
let
\mu_{\mathtt{a}}
be its mean reward. Let
\mathcal{M}_{p}
be the set of arms in memory at the beginning of pass
p
for any
p\in[P]
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{P+1}
denote the arms in memory in the exploitation phase.

Overview of the algorithm

Similar to
Algorithm

2
, we basically implement a BAR routine to select
n-m
arms to drop at each pass. The idea for BAR is simple, and the intuition is explained in detail with an offline BAR algorithm in
Appendix

D
. However, since in our model then algorithm cannot remember the identity of the dropped arms, we have to implement a streaming-friendly BAR algorithm, which complicates things a bit.

At the beginning of the first pass, the algorithm reads in
m
arms. It randomly selects an arm set
\mathcal{S}_{1}
of size
n-m+1
from the memory to perform a
FindBest
subroutine to identify a good arm, denoted as
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}
. It then discards the
n-m
arms from the set
\mathcal{S}_{1}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}\,\right\}
to make space for the remaining
n-m
arms in the stream.

For the subsequent pass
p
(
p>1
), the memory is full at the beginning of the pass. We first execute a shorter
FindBest
on all the arms in memory to find a good arm,
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
. Then, we randomly discard
\frac{m}{2}
arms from the remaining
m-1
arms. For each arm that arrives next, we put it into memory as long as memory is not full. Note that if we find an arriving arm is already in memory, the newly arriving arm and the one already in memory are essentially the same arm, so they only occupy one position. After including new
\frac{m}{2}
arms in the stream, the memory becomes full. We randomly select
n-m+1
arms from the newly included
\frac{m}{2}
arms. Let
\mathcal{S}_{p}
denote the set of these
n-m+1
arms together with
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
. Then we execute a longer
FindBest
on
\mathcal{S}_{p}
in order to select a good arm
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}
to keep. Then we discard
n-m
arms from
\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1},\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}\,\right\}
. At this point, we know that in the remaining pass, there are only
n-m
arms that are not in current memory. We then sequentially read in these
n-m
arms and end this pass.

The description of the algorithm

Set
\lambda_{p}=\frac{2^{P-p+1}-1}{2^{P+1}-1}
for any
p\in[P]
. Set the following parameters:

•

the sample times in the first pass
L_{1}=\left\lceil 2^{-2P}\cdot(n-m)^{3\lambda_{1}}n^{-2\lambda_{1}}T^{1-\lambda_{1}}\right\rceil
;

•

for any
p\in[P]\setminus\left\{\,1\,\right\}
,

\circ

the sample times of the second
FindBest
in the
p
-th pass
L_{p,2}=\left\lceil 2^{-2P+2p-2}\cdot(n-m)^{3\lambda_{p}}n^{-2\lambda_{p}}T^{1-\lambda_{p}}\right\rceil
;

\circ

the sample times of the first
FindBest
in the
p
-th pass
L_{p,1}=\left\lceil\frac{m^{3}L_{p-1,2}}{(n-m)^{3}}\right\rceil
for
p>2
and
L_{p,1}=\left\lceil\frac{m^{3}L_{1}}{(n-m)^{3}}\right\rceil
for
p=2
.

To achieve tight dependency on
n
and
m
, the parameters for large memory cases inevitably entail some intricacy compared to the simple

case. But in general, the values of these parameters are set for the same purpose as explained in

. The full algorithm is represented in

.

Recall the two issues mentioned at the beginning of this section which stand as the main differences between the

•

How to choose more than one inferior arms to drop?

•

Let us explain how the two issues are addressed in our new algorithm. We resolve the first one by incorporating

arms together with
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
to perform the second

and

For the second problem, note that there is a critical distinction compared to

(

\frac{m}{2}
arms after the first

4.2
The analysis

Let
\varepsilon_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{L_{1}}}
and let
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{L_{p,2}}}
for
p>1
. Let
\mathrm{king}_{p}
be the best arm in memory when pass
p
ends, i.e., after Line
7
or Line
15
is executed. For an arm with name
\mathtt{a}
, we let
\Delta_{\mathtt{a}}=\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{a}}
. The following lemma proves the effect of our algorithm on solving the BAR problem, which is a generalized version of
Lemma

.

Lemma 8
.

\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathtt{king}_{p}}\right]\leq\frac{10(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}}{m}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is the set of arms in memory at the beginning of pass
p
. If the optimal arm
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is not in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
for pass
1
, or
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is not in memory at Line
12
for pass
p>1
, according to
Algorithm

3
, it must be in
\mathcal{M}_{p+1}
and therefore
\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p}}=0
. Hence, we only need to consider the case when
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is in memory when we drop arms from
S_{p}
.

When
p=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-m+1}{m-1}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n-m+1}{m-1}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2(n-m+1)}{L_{1}}}\leq\frac{10(n-m)\varepsilon_{1}}{m},

where the third inequality holds due to Lemma
5
.

For
p>1
, if
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
is in memory when we drop arms from
S_{p}
, it is dropped only if
\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2},\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\max\left\{\,\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}},\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}}\,\right\}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\neq\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\right]\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\neq\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\max\left\{\,\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}},\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}}\,\right\}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2(n-m+1)}{m}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mathtt{arm}^{*}\neq\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\right]\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\max\left\{\,\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}},\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}}\,\right\}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(n-m+1)}{m}\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}}-\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,2}}\;\middle|\;\mathtt{arm}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}\setminus\left\{\,\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}\,\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(\spadesuit)}{\leq}\frac{2(n-m+1)}{m}\sqrt{\frac{2(n-m+2)}{L_{p,2}}}\leq\frac{10(n-m)}{m}\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{L_{p,2}}}\leq\frac{10(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}}{m},

where
(\spadesuit)
follows from Lemma

since Lemma

holds no matter whether
\mathtt{arm}^{*}=\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{p,1}
or not. In total, we have
\mathbf{E}\left[\Delta_{\mathrm{king}_{p}}\right]\leq\frac{10(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}}{m}
for any
p\in[P]
.
∎

Similar to

, and the saving here is also key to our tight regret bound in terms of

and

Now we are ready to prove

when

Theorem 9

(regret bound for

)
.

For any input instance, the expected regret of

Proof.

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{P}O\left(2^{-P+p}(n-m)^{1+\frac{2^{P}-2}{2^{P+1}-1}}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)+O\left((n-m)^{1+\frac{2^{P}-2}{2^{P+1}-1}}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)+\sqrt{2mT}

In this section we present our algorithm and its analysis for the case when the memory

. As previously mentioned, in the regime where

, increasing the memory contributes minimally to solving the BAR problem, whose sample complexity is of the same order as that of the BAI problem. Therefore, when
m
is small, we can directly leverage existing BAI algorithms to construct our algorithm.

Our algorithm still follows the explore-then-commit framework. We aim to identify an
\varepsilon_{p}
optimal arm,
\mathtt{king}_{p}
, after the
p
-th pass, where
\varepsilon_{p}
decreases as
p
increases. During the exploitation phase, we exploit
\mathtt{king}_{P}
. To this end, we first implement a streaming BAI algorithm in each pass. However, we cannot trivially use the BAI algorithm by feeding in every arriving arm since we have to account for the regret caused by the BAI algorithm itself. As a result, we incorporate an admission mechanism for each incoming arm. Only when the arm is not significantly inferior compared to the current best arm, do we allow it to participate in the competition of BAI. This ensures that the BAI process does not result in excessive regret.

6
Informal Elaboration on the Lower Bounds

Before delving into the proof of the regret lower bound, we first provide an informal elaboration on how we obtain lower bounds in
Theorem

2
on the special case that the memory size
m=n-1
. Similar to the case of the upper bound, our treatment for this special case already showcases our main idea for establishing lower bounds. The rigorous proof for the general lower bounds, i.e., the proof of
Theorem

2
, will be presented in the next section.

Recall that we say a pass ends once the last arm in the pass is read into the memory. This means that any exploitation of the arms in the memory after this point is treated as samples in the next pass. For the ease of expression, we make the same assumption as in
Section

3
that the player can remember the identity of discarded arms. This only strengthens our lower bound result.

Note that many ambiguous terms such as “tell apart”, “distinguish” used in the explanation below will be made rigorous in
Section

7
, mainly via a
likelihood argument
.

6.1
Single pass

We start from the simplest case, a lower bound for a single pass, i.e.,
P=1
. We first look at what our
Algorithm

3
does in this case. Generally speaking, since there is only one pass, the algorithm read in the first
m=n-1
arms into the memory, explore them for
L
rounds, and then drop a poor-performing arm to read in the last one. It then uses an optimal MAB algorithm to play for the remaining
T-L
rounds. Clearly there is a trade-off for picking the parameter
L
:

•

In instances where the optimal arm comes at last, the main regret is caused by the first
L
rounds, which can be bounded by
O\left(L\right)
.

•

When the optimal arm comes among the first
m
arms, one has to ensure that the optimal arm is not dropped after the first
L
rounds. This is a BAR task. We showed that
L
samples are sufficient to retain a good arm with expected mean reward gap of
\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}
compared to
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
. The main regret in these instances is due to the failure to retain
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
, which is bounded by
O\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{L}}\cdot(T-L)\right)
.

To balance the two cases, we choose
L\approx\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}
to minimize the worst case regret:

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\min_{L}\max\left\{\,O\left(L\right),O\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{(T-L)}{\sqrt{L}}\right)\,\right\}\approx O\left(\left(T/n\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right).

Then we analyze this strategy from the perspective of lower bounds. We claim that such a strategy with
L\approx\left({T}/{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}
is necessary. Let
\varepsilon\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}
. We choose a number
j\in[n-1]
uniformly at random and construct an instance
H
such that the
n
input arms have Bernoulli rewards with mean
\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2},1
respectively where the
j
-th arm has mean
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
. In other words, in
H
, the first
j-1
arms and the
(j+1)
-th to
(n-1)
-th arms are fair coins, and the
j
-th arm is slightly better while the last arm in the stream is the optimal arm which has mean reward
1
. We construct another instance
H^{\prime}
by replacing the last arm of
H
in stream with a fair coin.

Note that no algorithm can tell apart
H
and
H^{\prime}
without seeing the last arm in the stream. Therefore, if the player samples for more than
L
rounds before the first dropping in expectation, the regret on
H^{\prime}
is
\Omega\left(L\right)\approx\Omega\left(\left({T}/{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)
. However, if the sample time is less than
L\approx\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}
, the probability of failing to distinguish
\mathtt{arm}_{j}
with a fair coin is
\Omega(1)
. Since the biased arm is concealed among
m=n-1
arms on
H^{\prime}
, the probability of failing to retain that arm is approximately
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
. So the total regret generated on
H^{\prime}
is
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\varepsilon(T-L)\right)\approx\Omega\left(\left({T}/{n}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)
. This lower bound analysis in turn prescribes a reasonable behavior to achieve low regret, which is exactly the same with our algorithm.

6.2
Two-pass

We can generalize the above arguments to the
P=2
case. First examine the behavior of an algorithm for the two-pass streaming MAB. The algorithm still works in an explore-drop way. The crucial difference is that when allowed to see the arms for two passes, a clever player should explore less in the first pass to avoid the regret incurred in case that the optimal arm comes at last. Conversely, the player can explore more in the second pass, since after one pass, there is a reasonable probability that the optimal arm is already in the memory.

In general, our algorithm plays
L_{1}
rounds in the first pass, drop one arm to incorporate the last arm in the stream and start the exploration of the second pass. In the second pass, we will explore the
m
arms in memory for
L_{2}
rounds and then drop one among them to read in the remaining arm in the stream. Then the algorithm performs an optimal
(T-L_{1}-L_{2})
-round MAB algorithm with arms in the memory.

For those instances where a significantly better arm comes at last in the first pass, the regret is mainly contributed by the first
L_{1}
rounds, which is bounded by
O\left(L_{1}\right)
.
For other instances, we have proved that a good arm with expected mean reward gap smaller than
\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{p}}}
compared to
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
will be retained at the end of pass
p
(
p=1,2
). There are two possible situations:

•

The main regret is caused by the
L_{2}
rounds of exploration in pass two, which is due to dropping
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
by mistake in pass one. We can bound this by
O\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{L_{1}}}\cdot L_{2}\right)
;

•

The main regret is generated during the exploitation due to dropping
\mathtt{arm}^{*}
by mistake in pass two. This regret is bounded by
O\left(\frac{1}{n\sqrt{L_{2}}}\cdot(T-L_{1}-L_{2})\right)
.

Similar to the single pass case, we will choose
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
to minimize

\max\left\{\,O\left(L_{1}\right),O\left(\frac{L_{2}}{n\sqrt{L_{1}}}\right),O\left(\frac{(T-L_{1}-L_{2})}{n\sqrt{L_{2}}}\right)\,\right\}.

By direct calculation, the optimal choice is
L_{1}\approx{T^{\frac{4}{7}}}/{n^{\frac{6}{7}}}
and
L_{2}\approx{T^{\frac{6}{7}}}/{n^{\frac{2}{7}}}
, resulting in a regret bound of
O\left(T^{\frac{4}{7}}/n^{\frac{6}{7}}\right)
.

Then we argue that this kind of strategy is indeed optimal by providing a family of hard instances. Choose
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{2}
,
\varepsilon_{1}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{1}}}
and
\varepsilon_{2}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{2}}}
. Our hard instances consist of arms with Bernoulli rewards. That is, in each instance the
i
-th arm has rewards drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\mu_{i}
. For

7
Lower Bound Analysis

In this section, we will prove a tight (up to logarithm factors in
n
and
P
) asymptotic regret lower bound for streaming MAB algorithms with number of passes
1\leq P\leq\log{\log T}-\log\left(14\log 8(n-m)\right)
, number of arms
n\geq 3
and arbitrary memory size
m
satisfying
2\leq m\leq n-1
.
In the following, we always assume that the time horizon
T
is sufficiently large.

We pick
\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\dots,\varepsilon_{P}\in(0,1/4]
as parameters for our hard instances.
Our hard instances consist of arms with Bernoulli rewards. That is, in each instance the
i
-th arm has rewards drawn from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter
\mu_{i}
. For
p\in[P]\cup\left\{\,0\,\right\}
and
i\in[n]
, we construct the instance
H_{i}^{(p)}
with
n
arms as: the
i
-th arm has
\mu_{i}=\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{p}
; for every
k\in[n]\setminus\left\{\,i\,\right\}
,
\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{2}
(note that this arm index does not represent the arm order in the stream). Let
H_{0}
be the instance with
n
fair coins.

We also design an order for these instances: For
H_{0}
, we require the arms in each pass to come in an order from arm
1
to arm
n
. For
H_{j}^{(p)}
, the arm order should be the same with
H_{0}
in the first
p
passes (here the order refers to the order of arm identity, or equivalently, arm index). In pass
p+1
(if
p<P
), we require the optimal arm, arm
j
, arrives at the end in stream for
H_{j}^{(p)}
. The order of arms for
H_{j}^{(p)}
in the other passes can be arbitrary.

Additional notations and terminologies

Let us fix some notations and terminologies in the lower bound proof. Let
\Omega
be the set of all possible histories during the
T
-round game. That is, we can regard each
\omega\in\Omega
as a sequence of
{(a_{t},b_{t},m_{t})}
with length
T
, where
a_{t}\in[n]
is the arm that the algorithm pulls at round
t
,
b_{t}
is the corresponding reward and
m_{t}
records the current arms in memory. A streaming algorithm, randomized or deterministic, naturally induces a probability measure with outcomes
\Omega
. So we will casually call
\omega\in\Omega
an outcome and
S\subseteq\Omega
an event. Recall that we say a pass
ends
once the last arm in the pass is read into the memory. This means that any exploitation of the arms in the memory after this point is treated as samples in the next pass. For any
\omega\in\Omega
, let
\omega(1:p)
be the truncated sequence of
\omega
which contains only the history of first
p
passes. We use
\omega(1:P+1)
to denote
\omega
itself.

We emphasize that the algorithm we consider is allowed to be randomized. As a result, in the subsequent analysis, the sources of randomness for all the expectations and probabilities come from both the input instance and the algorithm itself.

Overview of the proof

Our proof is mainly based on the simple yet powerful
likelihood
argument. This technique has been widely used in statistics and in online learning problem to derive lower bounds (e.g.,
[
MT04
]
). For any
\omega\in\Omega
, the likelihood ratio between two input instances
H
and
H^{\prime}
on
\omega
is defined as
\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H}\left[\omega\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H^{\prime}}\left[\omega\right]}
.

Assume
H
and
H^{\prime}
are two instances with Bernoulli arms, and they differ in only one arm. In instance
H
, the mean reward of this arm is
\frac{1}{2}
, while in
H^{\prime}
, it is
\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon
. The likelihood argument states that if an
\omega\in\Omega
has bounded sample times and concentrated sample results, then
\mathbf{Pr}_{H}\left[\omega\right]\approx\mathbf{Pr}_{H^{\prime}}\left[\omega\right]
. This means that any algorithm cannot effectively distinguish between
H
and
H^{\prime}
when seeing this
\omega
.

We observe that it is impossible for an algorithm to keep small regret in both exploration and exploitation phases. Let
r
be the regret lower bound we are aiming to prove in
Theorem

2
. Assume in contradiction that the expected regret of a
P
-pass algorithm is less than
r
on all input instances. We choose
\left\{\,\varepsilon_{p}\,\right\}_{p\in[P]\cup\left\{\,0\,\right\}}
satisfying:
\varepsilon_{1}\approx{\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{r}}}
and
\frac{(n-m)}{\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}\approx{\frac{rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p-1}}}
. Let
L_{p}
be the sample times of the algorithm in pass
p
.

Then we have
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{1}\right]<\tilde{O}\left(\frac{n-m}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
. This is because the algorithm cannot distinguish between
H_{0}
and
H_{j}^{(0)}
before observing the last arm, and excessive exploration in the early stages would result in significant regret on
H^{(0)}_{j}
for any
j\in[n]
. Due to the limitation on
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{1}\right]
, there must be some
j\in[n]
such that with high probability, arm
j
is discarded after being pulled
\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right)
times and its empirical mean is concentrated. From a likelihood argument, arm
j
in
H^{(1)}_{j}
might also be discarded after pass
1
. Then we consider the second pass and can derive that

\mathbf{E}_{H^{(1)}_{j}}\left[L_{2}\;\middle|\;\mbox{arm }j\mbox{ is discarded in pass }1\right]\leq\frac{r}{\varepsilon_{1}\mathbf{Pr}_{H^{(1)}_{j}}\left[\mbox{arm }j\mbox{ is discarded in pass }1\right]}.

Then we apply the likelihood argument again. If the optimal arm of
H^{(1)}_{j}
is dropped in pass
1
, those arms in memory in the early stages of the second pass all have mean reward
\frac{1}{2}
. Note that the samples from the first pass does not provide much assistance in identifying a
\varepsilon_{1}
-optimal arm, so the algorithm cannot differentiate between
H_{0}
and
H^{(1)}_{j}
before seeing the last arm in pass
2
. Therefore,
L_{2}
should also be small on
H_{0}
. In fact, through a more subtle analysis, we show that
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{2}\;\middle|\;S_{1}\right]\leq O\left(\frac{rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{1}}\right)\approx{\frac{n-m}{\varepsilon_{2}^{2}}}
for some high probability event
S_{1}\subseteq\Omega
. Recursively doing this analysis, we can get that with non-zero probability, the total sample times on
H_{0}
should be less than

O\left(\frac{rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{P}}+\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{n-m}{\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}\right).

But this leads to a contradiction since this value is less than
T
when
T
is sufficiently large.

In the following analysis, we will choose
\varepsilon_{1}=\tilde{\Theta}\left(2^{-P}\cdot T^{\frac{-2^{P-1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}(n-m)^{\frac{1-2^{P-1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}n^{\frac{2^{P}-1}{2^{P+1}-1}}\right)
and
\frac{\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\leq 2^{p-k+3}\cdot\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{2^{P-p}-2^{P-k}}{2^{P+1}-1}}
for any
1\leq k<p\leq P
. The precise values will be determined later.

\circ

Here we slightly abuse the notation and let
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]
denote the probability of the event that the history of the first
p
passes is
\omega(1:p)
. The following lemma, which forms the foundation of our analysis, shows that when property
\mathcal{Q}_{p-1}
is satisfied, instance
H_{0}
and
H_{j}^{(p)}
exhibit a strong resemblance if arm
j
is explored for a limited number of times and its cumulative reward is concentrated around
\frac{1}{2}
in pass
p
.

Lemma 15

(likelihood ratio lemma)
.

Assume
T\geq n^{2}
and
P\leq\log{\log T}-\log\left(14\log 8(n-m)\right)
. Let
S_{p-1}
be a set with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p-1}
. Then for any
\omega\in S_{p-1}
, for any
j
such that

•

N_{j}^{(p)}\leq r_{p}
,

•

\max_{1\leq t\leq r_{p}}\absolutevalue{K_j^{(p)}(t)-\frac{t}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{r_{p}}{2}\log 64nP}
,

we have

\frac{e^{-4}}{2}<\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}<2e^{4},

when
T
is sufficiently large.

Proof.

Fix an outcome
\omega\in\Omega
. Recall that
K_{j}^{(p)}(t)
is the cumulative reward of arm
j
during its first
t
samples in the
p
-th pass, and
N_{j}^{(p)}
is the total number of samples of arm
j
in pass
p
. For brevity, we write
K_{j}^{(p)}(N_{j}^{(p)})
as
K_{j}^{(p)}
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{p}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{N_{j}^{(k)}}}{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{K_{j}^{(k)}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(k)}-K_{j}^{(k)}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{p}\frac{1}{\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{K_{j}^{(k)}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(k)}-2K_{j}^{(k)}}}.

From direct calculation, we know that for any
x\in\left[0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
,
1-x\geq e^{-\sqrt{2}x}
. Then for every
k\in[p-1]
, we have

\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{K_{j}^{(k)}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(k)}-2K_{j}^{(k)}}\leq(1-2\varepsilon_{p})^{-2\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{k}}{2}\log(64nP)}}\leq e^{4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{k}}{2}\log(64nP)}}=e^{\frac{\sqrt{8(n-m)}\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}},

where the first inequality follows from the concentration assumption. Recall that we promised to choose
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{P}
satisfying
\frac{\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}\leq 2^{p-k+3}\cdot\left(\frac{T}{n}\right)^{\frac{2^{P-p}-2^{P-k}}{2^{P+1}-1}}
for any
1\leq k<p\leq P
. Since
P\leq\log\log T-\log\left(14\log 8(n-m)\right)
, we have
\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{8(n-m)}\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}}\geq e^{-0.1}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-0.1}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{K_{j}^{(p)}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(p)}-2K_{j}^{(p)}}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-0.1}\cdot\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{2\sqrt{\frac{r_{p}}{2}\log(64nP)}}

\displaystyle>\exp{-4\sqrt{2}\eps_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\eps_p^2 \log(64nP)}\log(64nP)} -0.1}>\frac{e^{-4}}{2}.

On the other hand, for
k<p
,

\displaystyle\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{K_{j}^{(k)}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(k)}-2K_{j}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle\geq(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2})^{\ell_{k}}(1-2\varepsilon_{p})^{2\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{k}}{2}\log(64nP)}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\cdot\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\log(64nP)}-4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}\sqrt{\frac{n-m}{4\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\log(64nP)}\log(64nP)}}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-\frac{2\sqrt{2}(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}-\frac{\sqrt{8(n-m)}\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}}.

Similarly, we have
\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}e^{\frac{2\sqrt{2}(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}^{2}}{\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{8(n-m)}\varepsilon_{p}}{\varepsilon_{k}}}\leq e^{0.11}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}

\displaystyle\leq e^{0.11}\cdot\frac{1}{\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{K_{j}^{(p)}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{N_{j}^{(p)}-2K_{j}^{(p)}}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{0.11}\cdot\left(1-4\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{r_{p}}{2}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}}}\left(1-2\varepsilon_{p}\right)^{-2\sqrt{\frac{r_{p}}{2}\log(64nP)}}

\displaystyle<\exp{4\sqrt{2}\eps_p^2 \cdot\frac{1}{2\eps_p^2 \log(64nP)} + 4\eps_p + 0.11}\cdot\exp{4\sqrt{2}\eps_p\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\eps_p^2 \log(64nP)}\log(64nP)}}

\displaystyle<2e^{4},

where the second inequality follows from the concentration assumption, under which we have
K_{j}^{(p)}\leq\frac{N_{j}^{(p)}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{r_{p}}{2}\log(64nP)}\leq\frac{r_{p}}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}}
. The last inequality holds since
e^{4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}+4\varepsilon_{p}+0.11}<2
when
T
is sufficiently large.
∎

7.2
Analysis for each pass

Intuitively, if an algorithm has small expected regret on any single-pass instance, the sample times in exploration phase should be limited. Otherwise, the regret will explode if the optimal arm appears at the end of the stream. One important observation is that this principle also applies to multi-pass instances to some extent. In this section, we will show the existence of a high probability event with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
, denoted as
S_{p}
, such that
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]
is bounded if the regret is small.

Recall that
r_{p}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}
,
\ell_{p}=\frac{n-m}{2}\cdot r_{p}=\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}
and we say
S_{p}\subseteq\Omega
has property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
if

•

it has bounded length. To be specific, we require for all
k\in[p]
,
L_{k}\leq\ell_{k}
;

•

the sample results are concentrated. Precisely, for all
\omega\in S_{p}
and
k\in[p]
, for all
j\in[n]
, we need

\circ

\max_{1\leq t\leq r_{k}}\absolutevalue{K_j^{(k)}(t)-\frac{t}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{r_{k}}{2}\log 64nP}
and

\circ

\max_{1\leq t\leq\ell_{k}}\absolutevalue{K_j^{(k)}(t)-\frac{t}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{k}}{2}\log 64nP}
;

Then Lemma
16
shows that we can recursively construct
S_{p}
with a probability
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]\geq\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p-1}\right]-\frac{1}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}
as long as
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]
is upper bounded by some specific value.

Lemma 16
.

Assume
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}\cdot\ell_{p}
for some
S_{p-1}\subseteq\Omega
with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p-1}
and
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p-1}\right]\geq 1-\frac{p-1}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}
. There exists
S_{p}\subseteq S_{p-1}
with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
such that
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]\geq 1-\frac{p}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+2}}
.

Proof.

Recall that
K_{j}^{(p)}(t)
is the cumulative reward of arm
j
during its first
t
samples in the
p
-th pass. Let
\tilde{L}=\min\left\{\,\ell_{p},r_{p}\,\right\}
(in fact,
\ell_{p}<r_{p}
only when
m=n-1
). Define the following events:

•

A=\left\{\,L_{p}<\ell_{p}\,\right\}
;

•

C_{j}=\left\{\,\max_{1\leq t\leq\tilde{L}}\absolutevalue{K_j^{(p)}(t)-\frac{t}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\varepsilon_{p}}\,\right\}
for each
j\in[n]
;

•

C^{\prime}_{j}=\left\{\,\max_{1\leq t\leq\ell_{p}}\absolutevalue{K_j^{(p)}(t)-\frac{t}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{p}}{2}\log 64nP}\,\right\}
for each
j\in[n]
.

From the Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[A\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]=1-\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p}>\ell_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]\geq 1-\frac{\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]}{\ell_{p}}\geq 1-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}.

For event
C_{j}
, from Lemma
4
,

\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[C_{j}\right]\geq 1-2\exp{-\frac{2\cdot\frac{1}{2\eps_p^2 }}{\frac{1}{\eps_p^2 \log(64nP)}}}>1-\frac{1}{8nP}

and thus by the union bound,
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{8P}
. For event
C^{\prime}_{j}
, we can similarly get
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C^{\prime}_{j}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{8P}
.

Let
S_{p}=A\cap S_{p-1}\cap\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C^{\prime}_{j}\right)
. Obviously
S_{p}
satisfies
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
. The probability of
S_{p}

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[A\cap S_{p-1}\right]-\left(1-\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C_{j}\right]\right)-\left(1-\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}C^{\prime}_{j}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[A\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p-1}\right]-\frac{1}{4P}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{p-1}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}\right)-\frac{1}{4P}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{p}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+2}}.

∎

The following lemma then gives an upper bound of
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]
for any event
S_{p}\subseteq\Omega
with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
. Recall that
L_{p}(\omega)
is the sample times in pass
p
for any sequence
\omega\in\Omega
. We also use
L_{P+1}(\omega)
to denote the sample times in exploitation phase. Assume
T\geq n^{2}
and
P\leq\log{\log T}-\log\left(14\log 8(n-m)\right)
in the following proof.

Lemma 17
.

Assume the expected regret of an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is at most
r
. Let
S_{p}\subseteq\Omega
be a set with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
(
p\in[P]
). Then there exists a universal constant
c_{1}\geq 1
such that

\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]\leq\frac{c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]},

when
T
is sufficiently large.

Proof.

In each pass,
\mathcal{A}
is required to drop at least
n-m
arms. Recall that
r_{p}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}
and
\ell_{p}=\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}
. Since
L_{p}\leq\ell_{p}
for each
\omega\in S_{p}
, there exists at least
\lceil\frac{n-m}{2}\rceil
arms among the first dropped
n-m
arms satisfying
N_{j}^{(p)}\leq r_{p}
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
denote the set of the first discarded
n-m
arms in pass
p
. To simplify the notation, we use
N_{j}
to represent
N_{j}^{(p)}
when
p
is clear from the context. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}L_{p+1}(\omega)\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbb{I}[\omega\mbox{ satisfies }\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)]\cdot L_{p+1}(\omega)\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j\in[n]}\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mathbb{I}[\omega\mbox{ satisfies }\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)]\cdot L_{p+1}(\omega)\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[\mathbb{I}[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)]\cdot L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j\in[n]}\left(\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]+0\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n-m}\sum_{j\in[n]}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]

Once the event
{j\in\mathcal{J}}
occurs, since either arm
j
comes as the last arm in pass
p+1
for instance
H_{j}^{(p)}
(if
p<P
), or the optimal arm will not appear anymore (if
p=P
), all the arms sampled in pass
p+1
(if
p<P
) or the exploitation phase (if
p=P
) have means of
\frac{1}{2}
.
This indicates that
\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p+1)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p+1)\right]}=\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}
when
{j\in\mathcal{J}}
happens.
For each
\omega
satisfying
S_{p}\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)
, from Lemma
15
,

\frac{e^{-4}}{2}<\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega(1:p)\right]}<2e^{4}.

Define
\Omega_{p}=\left\{\,\omega(1:p)\;\middle|\;\omega\in\Omega\,\right\}
, which is the set of histories of the first
p
passes. Recall that
L_{p}(\omega)
is the sample times in pass
p
for a fixed
\omega\in\Omega
. When the history of only the first
p
passes
\omega_{p}\in\Omega_{p}
is given, we also use
L_{p}(\omega_{p})
to denote the sample times of
\omega_{p}
in pass
p
.
Here we slightly abuse the notation and let
\mathbf{Pr}\left[\omega_{p}\right]
be the probability of the event that the history of the first
p
passes is
\omega_{p}
. It is important to note that once
\omega_{p}
is fixed, whether
\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}
happens is known. We use
\omega_{k}\in\left\{\,\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\,\right\}
to denote that this event happens when the first
k
passes history is
\omega_{k}
for some
k\geq p
. Hence,

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}L_{p+1}(\omega)\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega_{p+1}\in\Omega_{p+1}}L_{p+1}\left(\omega_{p+1}\right)\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega_{p+1}\in\Omega_{p+1}}L_{p+1}\left(\omega_{p+1}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\omega_{p+1}\right]\cdot\mathbb{I}[\omega_{p+1}\in\left\{\,\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\,\right\}]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq 4e^{8}\sum_{\omega_{p+1}\in\Omega_{p+1}}L_{p+1}\left(\omega_{p+1}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\omega_{p+1}\right]\cdot\mathbb{I}[\omega_{p+1}\in\left\{\,\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\,\right\}]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]}

\displaystyle=4e^{8}\mathbf{E}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right],

where the inequality follows from Lemma
15

Note that if arm
j
is discarded in pass
p
, each pull in pass
p+1
(or in the exploitation phase of
p=P
) will cause an expected regret of
\varepsilon_{p}
on
H_{j}^{(p)}
. Since the total regret is bounded by
r
, we can conclude that

\varepsilon_{p}\cdot\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]\mathbf{E}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]\leq r.

Combining above analysis, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8e^{8}}{n-m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{r}{\varepsilon_{p}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]}{\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{j}^{(p)}}\left[\left(j\in\mathcal{J}\right)\cap\left(N_{j}\leq r_{p}\right)\cap S_{p}\right]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16e^{12}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]}

where the second inequality follows from Lemma
15
.
∎

7.3
Regret lower bound

Now we can proceed to establish a lower bound for the regret. Equipped with Lemma
16
and
17
, we can choose
\left\{\,\varepsilon_{p}\,\right\}_{p\in[P]}
satisfying
\frac{c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p-1}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p-1}\right]}=\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}\cdot\ell_{p}
to construct such event
S_{p}
for each pass. Recall that we use
L_{P+1}
to denote the sample complexity of the exploitation phase. This recursive process allows us to ultimately obtain an upper bound on
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{P+1}\;\middle|\;S_{P}\right]
. This will lead to a conflict since
\sum_{p=1}^{P+1}L_{p}
should always equal to
T
.
We then formalize this intuition to establish an asymptotic lower bound on the regret in the following proof of
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

Assume

R(T,\mathcal{A})\leq r=\frac{1}{2^{5}c_{1}}\cdot T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\cdot(n-m)^{1+\frac{(2^{P}-2)}{2^{P+1}-1}}\cdot n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}}\cdot\left(\log(64nP)\right)^{\frac{1-2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}}

on any instances, where
c_{1}
is the constant in Lemma
17
.
Let
\lambda_{p}=\frac{2^{P-p+1}-1}{2^{P+1}-1}
. Choose

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}

\displaystyle=(n-m)^{2-\frac{3}{2^{p}}}(c_{1}n)^{-1+\frac{1}{2^{p-1}}}(r\log(64nP))^{-1+\frac{1}{2^{p}}}\cdot 2^{-t_{p}}

\displaystyle=2^{-t_{p}+5-\frac{5}{2^{p}}}\cdot c_{1}^{\frac{1}{2^{p}}}\cdot T^{\frac{\lambda_{p}-1}{2}}\cdot(n-m)^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3\lambda_{p}}{2}}\cdot n^{\lambda_{p}}\cdot\left(\log(64nP)\right)^{\frac{\lambda_{p}-1}{2}}

(1)

where
t_{p}=P-p+6
.

Then we have
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{1}\right]=\mathbf{E}_{H_{j}^{(0)}}\left[L_{1}\right]\leq 2r
for any
j\in[n]
since the optimal arm of
H_{j}^{(0)}
comes at the end and thus each pull during pass
1
will contribute an expected regret of
\frac{1}{2}
. By our choice of
\varepsilon_{1}
,
2r\leq\frac{1}{2^{P+2}}\cdot\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\log(64nP)}
. Let
S_{0}=\Omega
. From Lemma
16
, we can find
S_{1}\subseteq\Omega
with property
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{1}\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P+1}}
. Then from Lemma
17
,
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{2}\;\middle|\;S_{1}\right]\leq\frac{c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{1}\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{1}\right]}<\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{1}}
.

Note that for
p=[P]\setminus\left\{\,1\,\right\}
, we have
\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p-1}}=\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}\cdot\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}
. Assume
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{p-1}\right]\leq\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p-1}}
for some
p\geq 2
where
S_{p-1}\subseteq\Omega
has property
\mathcal{Q}_{p-1}
and satisfies
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p-1}\right]\geq 1-\frac{p-1}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+3}}
. According to Lemma
16
, we can find
S_{p}\subseteq S_{p-1}
with property
\mathcal{Q}_{p}
and satisfies
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{p}\right]\geq 1-\frac{p}{4P}-\frac{1}{2^{P-p+2}}
. Then from Lemma
17
,
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{p+1}\;\middle|\;S_{p}\right]\leq\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{p}}
.

Therefore, we can prove by induction that
\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[L_{P+1}\;\middle|\;S_{P}\right]\leq\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{P}}
and
\mathbf{Pr}_{H_{0}}\left[S_{P}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}
. However, conditioned on
S_{P}
, we have
L_{p}\leq\ell_{p}
for each
p\in[P]
. This violates the fact
\sum_{p=1}^{P+1}L_{p}=T
since

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{H_{0}}\left[\sum_{p=1}^{P+1}L_{p}\;\middle|\;S_{P}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p=1}^{P}\ell_{p}+\frac{2c_{1}rn}{(n-m)\varepsilon_{P}}

\displaystyle<\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}n^{\frac{2-2^{P+1}}{2^{P+1}-1}+1-\lambda_{P}}\cdot(n-m)^{\frac{2^{P}-2}{2^{P+1}-1}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\lambda_{P}}{2}}\cdot T^{\frac{2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}-\frac{\lambda_{P}-1}{2}}\cdot\left(\log 64nP\right)^{\frac{1-2^{P}}{2^{P+1}-1}-\frac{\lambda_{P}-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{2}+\frac{T}{\sqrt{2}}<T,

where the third inequality holds because
\sum_{p=1}^{P}\frac{n-m}{2\varepsilon_{p}^{2}\log(64nP)}\leq\frac{T}{2}
when
P\leq\log{\log T}-\log\left(14\log 8(n-m)\right)
and
T
is sufficiently large.

∎

A.1
The algorithm

Overview of the algorithm

The algorithm maintains at most
r=m-1
arms,
a^{*}_{1},\dots,a^{*}_{r}
. For each
\ell\in[r]
, during the execution,
a_{\ell}^{*}
is the current best arm of level
\ell
. For each arriving
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
, it starts from the first level and tries to challenge
a_{\ell}^{*}
for increasing
\ell=1,2,\dots
. Basically, the challenge proceeds as follows. At level
\ell
, the algorithm samples the challenger arm for
s_{\ell}
times and compares the empirical mean with that of the current
a_{\ell}^{*}
. If the challenger loses, it will be dropped and the algorithm reads the next arm from the stream. Otherwise, it substitutes
a_{\ell}^{*}
.

Once the challenger finishes the challenge (either being dropped or substituting the previous
a_{\ell}^{*}
), a counter
C_{\ell}
increases by one. As long as the counter meets a threshold
c_{\ell}
, the best arm
a_{\ell}^{*}
will level up and start to challenge
a_{\ell+1}^{*}
, in a way similar to level
\ell
. After this, all our previous statistics at level
\ell
will be reset. The net effect is that, for each
\ell
, every time a group of
c_{\ell}
challengers arrives at the
\ell
-th level, the algorithm sends the winner of these
c_{\ell}
arms to level
\ell+1
. Finally, after processing the whole stream, the algorithm outputs the best of
a_{\ell}^{*}
for
\ell\in[r]
.

The description of the algorithm

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{4}]
. Let
r=m-1
where
m
is the memory size. We assume
2\leq m\leq\log^{*}n+1
. For the convenience of designing streaming MAB algorithm, we allow the input arm set of the BAI algorithm to be some
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
. Let
n^{\prime}=\absolutevalue{\+S}
be the total number of arms in the stream the BAI algorithm needs to handle. It is guaranteed that
n^{\prime}\leq n
, but
n^{\prime}
itself is not known beforehand. Actually, when executing the BAI subroutine in our streaming MAB algorithm,
\mathcal{S}
can be a random set. We would show that property
1
,
2
and
3
in
Proposition

10
holds whatever
\mathcal{S}
is and therefore, without loss of generality, we regard
\mathcal{S}
as deterministic and assume
\mathcal{S}=[n^{\prime}]
when analyzing these properties. We only consider the randomness of
\mathcal{S}
when proving the fourth property.

Set the following parameters:

•

\left\{\,c_{\ell}\,\right\}_{\ell=1}^{r}:c_{\ell}=\lceil\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell)}(n)\rceil
, which is the number of arms needed to arrive at each level before a promotion;

•

A.2.1
Property

In the following discussion, we fix a time during the execution of

and consider a level
L\leq r
such that

. It follows from our algorithm that

has “competed with”

to denote this set of

arms and let
\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}:=\mathop{\arg\max}_{a\in A_{L}}\mu_{a}
, namely the arm in

with the largest mean. The next lemma states that with high probability the mean

is large comparing to

Lemma 19
.

For any
\Delta>0
, it holds that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mu_{a_{L}^{*}}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta\right]\leq c_{L}\cdot\exp{-\frac{s_L\Delta^2}{2}}.

Proof.

Consider the last duration that
C_{L}
increases from
0
to its current value. Our algorithm effectively does the following: It samples each arm in
A_{L}
for
s_{L}
times and let

be the one with the largest empirical mean. As a result, if the event “
\mu_{a_{L}^{*}}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta
” happens, then there must be some arm

with
\mu_{a}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta
who beats
\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}
. This implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\mu_{a_{L}^{*}}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\exists a\in A_{L}\colon\left(\mu_{a}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta\right)\land\left(\hat{\mu}_{a}>\hat{\mu}_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in A_{L}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[\hat{\mu}_{a}>\hat{\mu}_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}\;\middle|\;\mu_{a}\leq\mu_{\mathtt{arm}^{*}_{A_{L}}}-\Delta\right].

The lemma then follows from the Hoeffding’s inequality and the fact that
\absolutevalue{A_L}=C_{L}\leq c_{L}

A.2.2

of

It remains to analyze the regret of the BAI algorithm. We first consider the sample complexity of
Algorithm

5
. Note that the sample complexity only depends on the number of total arms
n^{\prime}
. We can prove the following sample complexity bound of
Algorithm

5
for any
n\geq n^{\prime}
.

Theorem 25

(sample complexity of
Algorithm

5
)
.

For any integer
1\leq r\leq\log^{*}n
, the worst case sample complexity of
Algorithm

5
is no larger than
\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}s_{\ell}\cdot\big\lfloor\frac{n}{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}c_{j}}\big\rfloor+rs_{r}
. This can be further bounded by
O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\mathrm{ilog}^{(r)}(n)\right)\right)
.

Proof.

Note that there are at most
\frac{n}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}
arms that reaches level
\ell
. Then the total sample complexity is no larger than

\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{n\cdot s_{\ell}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}+r\cdot s_{r},

for any
n^{\prime}\leq n
.

Recall that
1\leq r\leq\log^{*}n
,
c_{\ell}=\lceil\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell)}(n)\rceil
and
s_{\ell}=\left\lceil\frac{2^{2\ell+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\frac{2^{\ell+2}c_{\ell}}{\delta}\right)\right\rceil
. When
\ell=1
, we have

n\cdot s_{1}=n\cdot\left\lceil\frac{2^{5}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\frac{2^{3}c_{1}}{{\delta}}\right)\right\rceil=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\log c_{1}\right)\right)=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\mathrm{ilog}^{(r)}(n)\right)\right).

For the term
r\cdot s_{r}
, we can bound it by

r\cdot s_{r}=O\left(\frac{r\cdot 2^{2r+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2^{r+2}n}{\delta}\right)\right)\leq O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Then it remains to deal with
\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n\cdot s_{\ell}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}
. Let
c_{0}=1
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n\cdot s_{\ell}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}\cdot\frac{2^{2\ell+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\frac{2^{\ell+2}}{\delta}+\log\left(\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell)}(n)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n}{c_{\ell-1}c_{\ell-2}}\cdot\frac{2^{2\ell+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\frac{2^{\ell+2}}{\delta}+c_{\ell-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n}{c_{\ell-2}}\cdot\frac{2^{2\ell+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+2\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{n}{c_{\ell-1}c_{\ell-2}}\cdot\frac{2^{2\ell+3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{2^{2\ell}}{\delta^{2\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{4^{\ell+1}}{\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell+2)}(n)}+\frac{8n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\sum_{\ell=2}^{r}\frac{4^{\ell+2}}{\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell+1)}(n)}

where the third inequality is due to
\frac{2^{\ell+2}}{\delta}\leq\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{2\ell}
since
\delta\leq\frac{1}{4}
and the last inequality is due to
\frac{\ell}{c_{\ell-2}}\leq 4
. From direct calculation, we know that
\sum_{\ell=2}^{r+1}\frac{4^{\ell+1}}{\mathrm{ilog}^{(r-\ell+2)}(n)}=O(1)
. Therefore, combining above results, the sample complexity is no larger than

\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{n\cdot s_{\ell}}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}c_{i}}+r\cdot s_{r}=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\mathrm{ilog}^{(r)}(n)\right)\right).

-th arriving arm in

5

)

When

is a random set and for every

Denoted the expected regret with regard to an arm with mean

generated by

as

\displaystyle R_{\textsc{BAI}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}s_{\ell}\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{a^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\mu-\mu_{a^{\prime}})\right]+s_{r}\mathbf{E}\left[\sum_{a^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}_{r+1}}(\mu-\mu_{a^{\prime}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}s_{\ell}\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}c_{j}}\right\rfloor\cdot 2\varepsilon^{\prime}+rs_{r}\cdot 2\varepsilon^{\prime}.

From

, the worst case sample complexity is bounded by

\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}s_{\ell}\cdot\left\lfloor\frac{n}{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}c_{j}}\right\rfloor+rs_{r}=O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\mathrm{ilog}^{(m-1)}(n)\right)\right).

So the total regret generated by the BAI process is

\displaystyle R_{\textsc{BAI}}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{n}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\mathrm{ilog}^{(m-1)}(n)\right)\right)\cdot 2\varepsilon^{\prime}

∎

Technical Proofs

B.1
Proof of Lemma
4

Lemma
4
is a generalization of Hoeffding’s inequality. For completeness and consistency of the paper, we provide its proof here.

Lemma 27

(Hoeffding’s Lemma)
.

Let
X
be a random variable with mean
0
and
a\leq X\leq b
holds almost surely. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\alpha X}\right]\leq e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}}
.

Lemma 28

(Lemma
4
restated)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be
N
independent random variables defined on a common probability space and taking values in
[a,b]
. Assume
\mathbf{E}\left[X_{t}\right]=0
for any
t\in[N]
. Then for any
s>0
,

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^t X_j}\geq s\right]\leq 2\exp{-\frac{2s^2}{N(b-a)^2}}.

Proof.

Let
Z_{0}=1
and
Z_{t}=e^{\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}}
for
t\in[N]
, where
\alpha
is a positive real number to be determined later. Let
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{t}\right)
. From Jensen’s inequality, for any
t\in[N]

\mathbf{E}\left[Z_{t}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=Z_{t-1}\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\alpha X_{t}}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]=Z_{t-1}\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\alpha X_{t}}\right]\geq Z_{t-1}e^{\alpha\mathbf{E}\left[X_{t}\right]}=Z_{t-1}.

Therefore,
\left\{\,Z_{t}\,\right\}_{t\in[N]}
is a submartingale with regard to
\left\{\,\mathcal{F}_{t}\,\right\}_{t\in[N]}
. From the Doob’s submartingale inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}Z_{t}\geq e^{\alpha s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\alpha s}\mathbf{E}\left[Z_{N}\right]=e^{-\alpha s}\prod_{t=1}^{N}\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\alpha X_{t}}\right].

According to the Hoeffding’s lemma (Lemma
27
),
\mathbf{E}\left[e^{\alpha X_{t}}\right]\leq e^{\frac{\alpha^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}}
. Choosing
\alpha=\frac{4s}{N(b-a)^{2}}
, we have

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}\geq s\right]=\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}Z_{t}\geq e^{\alpha s}\right]\leq e^{-\alpha s+\frac{N\alpha^{2}(b-a)^{2}}{8}}\leq e^{-\frac{2s^{2}}{N(b-a)^{2}}}.

(2)

We can similarly derive that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}-\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}\geq s\right]\leq e^{-\frac{2s^{2}}{N(b-a)^{2}}}.

(3)

Combining
Equations

2
and
3
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}\absolutevalue{\sum_{j=1}^t X_j}\geq s\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}\geq s\right]+\mathbf{Pr}\left[\max_{1\leq t\leq N}-\sum_{j=1}^{t}X_{j}\geq s\right]\leq 2e^{-\frac{2s^{2}}{N(b-a)^{2}}}.

∎

B.2
Proof of Lemma
20

Lemma 29

(
Lemma

20
restated)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable. For any
L\in[r]
, if it holds that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\leq c_{L}\cdot\exp{-\frac{s_L\cdot t^2}{2}}
for every
t\geq 0
, then
\mathbf{E}\left[X\right]\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-L}
.

Proof.

Note that for any real-valued random variable
X
, letting
X^{+}=\max\left\{\,X,0\,\right\}
be the positive part of
X
, we have the elementary inequality (see e.g.,
[
Fel71
, Section V.6]
)

\mathbf{E}\left[X\right]\leq\mathbf{E}\left[X^{+}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X^{+}\geq t\right]\differential{t}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[X\right]\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}=\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{L+1}}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}+\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{L+1}}}^{\infty}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}.

Now we bound the two integrals respectively. For the first term, we use the trivial bound that
\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\leq 1
. So

\int_{0}^{\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{L+1}}}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-(L+1)}.

For the second term, observe that

\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\leq c_{L}\cdot\exp{-\frac{s_L\cdot t^2}{2}}\leq c_{L}\left(\frac{1}{2^{L+4}c_{L}}\right)^{\frac{2^{2L+2}t^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\leq 2^{-(L+4)2^{2L+2}(t/\varepsilon)^{2}}.

Therefore, we have

\int_{\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{L+1}}}^{\infty}\mathbf{Pr}\left[X\geq t\right]\differential{t}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}2^{-(L+4)2^{2L+2}(t/\varepsilon)^{2}}\differential{t}\leq\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-(L+1)}.

∎

Let

The algorithm in

where
r_{t}(i)
is the reward of
\mathtt{arm}_{i}
. It is easy to verify that the results in
[
LG21
]
also holds for the reward setting. Let
\eta
be the learning rate and
F:\mathbb{R}^{\absolutevalue{\+S}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\,\infty\,\right\}
be the potential function, where
\mathcal{S}
is the arm set. Without loss of generality, we index the arms in

by

.

Appendix D
More on the BAR algorithm

In
Section

4
,
Algorithm

3
essentially implements a streaming-friendly BAR algorithm in each pass. This BAR algorithm serves two purposes: (1) After the
p
-th pass, it guarantees that an
\varepsilon_{p}
-optimal arm is retained in memory, denoted as
\mathtt{king}_{p}
; (2) it incurs relatively small regret in pass
p
. For a better understanding of this process, we provide an algorithm for BAR problem in
Algorithm

7
in the offline setting, that is, all arms are present in the memory. It is logically equivalent to the routine in
Algorithm

3
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{0}
be two parameters and
m\geq\frac{8n}{9}
. Our BAR algorithm executes the
FindBest
subroutine twice. The first one is short and is performed on all the input arms. The purpose of this
FindBest
subroutine is to identify an
\varepsilon_{0}
-optimal arm, denoted as
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}
. Since the subroutine is short, its regret is small.

The second one, which is longer, is performed on a set
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
that includes
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}
and
n-m+1
randomly selected arms. As a result, the regret caused by this subroutine is with respect to the
\varepsilon_{0}
-optimal arm
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{1}
, so it can be bounded as well. In the second
FindBest
subroutine, we obtain
\mathtt{arm}^{\prime}_{2}
. The objective of the second one is to eliminate
n-m
inferior arms and output a set of size
m
containing an
O\left(\varepsilon_{1}\right)
-optimal arm.

Set
L_{1}=\left\lceil\frac{2n}{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}\right\rceil
and
L_{2}=\left\lceil\frac{2(n-m+2)}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right\rceil
. The algorithm is shown in
Algorithm

7
.

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2nL_{1}}+\sqrt{2(n-m+2)L_{2}}+\varepsilon_{0}\cdot L_{2}

\displaystyle=O\left({\frac{n}{\varepsilon_{0}}}+\frac{(n-m)\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon_{1}^{2}}\right).

Now let us take a closer look at
Algorithm

3
. Essentially, what we want to do is perform two
FindBest
operations on the
m
arms in memory at the beginning of each pass, then discard
n-m
arms, and read in the remaining
n-m
arms. However, in order to adapt to the streaming setting, we cannot do this directly. This is because we cannot determine whether an arriving arm is one of the discarded
n-m
arms or one of the
n-m
arms that we have not encountered since we are only allowed to store the statistics of arms in memory. Therefore, we first discard
\frac{m}{2}
arms and then select
n-m
arms from the newly arrived
\frac{m}{2}
arms to drop. At this point, there remains only
n-m
arms in stream that are not in current memory. This satisfies the requirements of the streaming model while still achieving the desired effectiveness of our BAR algorithm.
