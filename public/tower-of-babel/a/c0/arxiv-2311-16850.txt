General Derivative-Free Optimization Methods under Global and Local Lipschitz Continuity of Gradients
Pham Duy Khanh, Boris S. Mordukhovich, Dat Ba Tran

Abstract




Abstract
.
This paper addresses the study of derivative-free smooth optimization problems, where the gradient information on the objective function is unavailable. Two novel general derivative-free methods are proposed and developed for minimizing such functions with either global or local Lipschitz continuous gradients. The newly developed methods use gradient approximations based on finite differences, where finite difference intervals are automatically adapted to the magnitude of the exact gradients without knowing them exactly. The suggested algorithms achieve fundamental convergence results, including stationarity of accumulation points in general settings as well as global convergence with constructive convergence rates when the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property is imposed. The local convergence of the proposed algorithms to nonisolated local minimizers, along with their local convergence rates, is also analyzed under this property. Numerical experiences involving various convex, nonconvex, noiseless, and noisy functions demonstrate that the new methods exhibit essential advantages over other state-of-the-art methods in derivative-free optimization.

Key words
: derivative-free optimization, finite differences, black-box optimization, noisy optimization, zeroth-order optimization

90C25, 90C26, 90C30, 90C56

Introduction

This paper addresses developing novel
derivative-free methods
to solve unconstrained optimization problems given in the form

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\quad f(x)\quad\text{ subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(1.1)

where
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
is a continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth) function, and where only a
noisy approximation

\phi(x)=f(x)+\xi(x)
of the objective function is computable. It means that the
gradient
information of the objective function is fully
unavailable
, even in the noiseless case when the noise function
\xi(x)
vanishes. These problems have received much attention with a variety of methods being developed over the years. The major developments in this vein are provided by the
Nelder-Mead simplex method

[
31
,
37
]
,
direct search methods

[
17
,
20
]
and
trust-region methods

[
13
]
. More recently, numerous empirical results conducted by Shi et al. in
[
47
]
show that derivative-free optimization methods based on
finite differences
are accurate, efficient, and in some cases superior to other state-of-the-art derivative-free optimization methods established in the literature. Meanwhile, extensive numerical comparisons in Berahas et al.
[
7
]
together with the further analysis by Scheinberg
[
45
]
also tell us that the accuracy of gradients obtained from finite differences is much higher than from
randomized schemes
. These empirical results suggest that the methods using finite differences have much to be recommended, and that the research in this direction should be strongly encouraged.

There are several derivative-free optimization methods in the literature that successfully employ finite differences. Kelley et al.
[
11
,
16
,
25
]
proposed the
implicit filtering
algorithm based on a finite difference approach to deal with
noisy smooth box-constrained
optimization problems. Berahas et al.
[
6
,
8
]
developed finite difference-based line search methods for the minimization of
noisy smooth functions
. The approaches to compute the
finite difference interval
are also studied by Gill et al.
[
15
]
, and recently by Moré and Wild
[
36
]
as well as by Shi et al.
[
46
]
. Although methods of this type are often used in practice to solve derivative-free smooth problems, the
theoretical understanding
of behavior of their iterates in general cases is rather limited in comparison with analysis of classical first-order methods.
Stationarity of accumulation points
is a fundamental convergence property that is achieved by many first-order methods
[
9
,
38
]
and by some derivative-free optimization algorithms using the simplex-based direct method
[
44
, Theorem 3.2]
, line search based on simplex derivatives
[
12
, Chapter 9]
, coordinate search with difference Hessians
[
35
, Theorem 4.2]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, this property has not been established for derivative-free methods employing
finite differences
for general classes of smooth functions (i.e., smooth functions with global or local Lipschitz continuous gradients), even in noiseless cases. Note that the convergence properties of derivative-free methods for noisy functions studied in
[
8
,
6
]
do not ensure them for noiseless functions. The reason is that the usual selection of finite difference intervals based on the noise level becomes zero, being invalid therefore in this case; see, e.g.,
[
6
, Equations (3.3), (3.4)]
. The implicit filtering algorithm
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
, although achieving the stationarity for accumulation points, requires the objective function to be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth with rather strict conditions including the boundedness of level sets and the uniform boundedness of the inverses of Hessians. Furthermore, the convergence of the sequence of iterates to a
nonisolated
stationary point is also ignored in the analysis of aforementioned derivative-free optimization methods while obtained by various first-order methods
[
4
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
under the
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz
(KL) property
[
3
,
30
,
32
]
, which is a rather mild regularity condition satisfied for the vast majority of objective functions in practice. Of course, the local convergence to nonisolated local minimizers for such derivative-free methods is still questionable.

The assumption of
global Lipschitz continuity
of the gradient seems to be omnipresent in derivative-free line search methods; see, e.g.,
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
6
, Assumption A1]
,
[
8
, Assumption 1.1]
. For general derivative-free trust-region methods, Conn et al.
[
13
]
prove global convergence results under the global Lipschitz continuity of the gradient and the Hessian of the objective function. Such properties are also employed in the proximal point method adapted to derivative-free smooth optimization problems by Hare and Lucet
[
18
, Assumption 1]
. We are not familiar with any efficient finite difference-based method considering specifically the class of smooth functions with
locally Lipschitz
continuous gradients, which is much broader than the class of smooth functions with globally Lipschitz continuous gradients and covers, in particular,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth functions. This is in contrast to the exact versions of gradient descent methods, which obtain various convergence properties including the stationarity of accumulation points for the version with backtracking stepsizes on the class of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth functions
[
9
, Proposition 1.2.1]
and the global convergence for the version with sufficiently small stepsizes on the class of definable
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth functions with locally Lipschitz continuous gradients
[
23
]
. This raises the need for the design and analysis of finite difference-based methods concerning the class of smooth functions with locally Lipschitz continuous gradients, which is the best we can hope in this context since the error bounds for finite differences are not available outside this class.

Having in mind the above discussions, we propose in this paper
two novel derivative-free methods
for minimizing smooth functions. A general derivative-free method with
constant stepsize
is designed for smooth functions whose gradients are
globally
Lipschitzian although the exact calculation of the Lipschitz constant is not necessary. A general derivative-free method with
backtracking stepsize
is designed for smooth functions whose gradients are only
locally
Lipschitzian. Such newly developed methods are analyzed rigorously for
noiseless functions
, which particularly have many applications in the design of space trajectories
[
2
]
, reinforcement learning
[
33
]
, and other black-box optimization problems
[
5
,
34
]
. For such problems, the objective functions may in fact well-behave, even being smooth, while the exact gradient information is either unavailable due to the lack of analytical forms of the objective function, or it is computationally expensive to obtain. The main ideas for our methods come mostly from the
inexact reduced gradient methods
(IRGM) introduced and justified in
[
26
]
. Roughly speaking, the newly developed derivative-free methods are designed as inexact gradient descent methods for smooth functions and thus inherit typical convergence properties for first-order methods including stationarity of accumulation points,
global convergence
under the KL property, and constructive convergence rates depending on the KL exponent. Furthermore, we establish
local convergence
to
nonisolated
local minimizers for both proposed methods under the KL property of the objective function.

Regarding
numerical aspects
, our methods borrow the ideas of
gradient approximations
from IRGM
[
26
]
and its nonsmooth extensions
[
27
,
28
]
, where errors are automatically adapted to the magnitude of exact gradients; see, e.g.,
[
26
, Section 6.2]
. Correspondingly, the proposed derivative-free methods generate at each iteration a descent direction based on an approximate gradient associated with the largest finite difference interval. Choosing such an interval in this way allows the methods to omit
rounding errors
as much as possible, which makes them more efficient without knowing the
noise level
in advance. This selection of the finite difference interval is also completely different from the selection used in the implicit filtering algorithm
[
11
,
16
,
25
]
, since the latter forces the finite difference interval to decrease after each iteration while ours does not. Extensive
empirical results
on diverse datasets including convex, nonconvex, noiseless, and noisy functions with different noise levels in different finite-dimensional spaces show that our newly developed methods usually perform better than many other state-of-the-art derivative-free methods in solving smooth problems. For the conciseness of the paper, the convergence analysis and numerical experiments involving methods coupled with quasi-Newton algorithms are not considered here but are deferred to our future research.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents some basic definitions and preliminaries used throughout the entire paper. Section
3
examines two types of approximation for gradients that cover finite differences. The main parts of our work concerning the design and convergence properties of general derivative-free methods under the global and local Lipschitz continuity of the gradient are given in Section
4
and Section
5
, respectively. Local convergence of the proposed methods to nonisolated local minimizers is presented in Section
6
. Numerical experiments comparing the efficiency of the proposed methods with other derivative-free methods for both noisy and noiseless functions are conducted in Section
7
. Concluding remarks on the contribution of this paper together with some open questions and perspectives of our future research are given in Section
8
.

2
Preliminaries

First we recall some basic notions and notation frequently used in the paper. All our considerations are given in the space
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
. For any
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
e_{i}
denote the
i^{\rm th}
basic vector in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}.
As always,
{\rm I\!N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
signifies the collection of natural numbers. For any
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
\varepsilon>0,
let
\mathbb{B}(x,\varepsilon)
and
\overline{\mathbb{B}}(x,\varepsilon)
stand for
the open and closed balls centered at
x
with radius
\varepsilon
, respectively. When
x=0
, these balls are denoted simply by
\varepsilon\mathbb{B}
and
\varepsilon\overline{\mathbb{B}}
.

Recall
that a mapping
G\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}^{m}
is
Lipschitz continuous on a subset

D
of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
if there exists a constant
L>0
such that we have

\displaystyle\left\|G(x)-G(y)\right\|\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|\text{ for all }x,y\in D.

If
D={\rm I\!R}^{n}
, the mapping
G
is said to be
globally Lipschitz continuous
. The
local Lipschitz continuity
of
G
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is understood as the Lipschitz continuity of this mapping on every compact subset of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The latter is equivalent to saying that for any
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
there is a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
G
is Lipschitz continuous on
U
, which is also equivalent to the Lipschitz continuity of
G
on every bounded subset of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. In what follows, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
the class of
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth mappings that have a
locally Lipschitz continuous gradient
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and by
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
the class of
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth mappings that have a
globally Lipschitz continuous gradient with the constant

L>0
on the entire space.

Our convergence analysis of the numerical algorithms developed in the subsequent sections takes advantage of the following important results and notions. The first result taken from
[
21
, Lemma A.11]
presents a simple albeit very useful property of real-valued functions with Lipschitz continuous gradients.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
, and let
x,y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. If
f
is differentiable on the line segment
[x,y]
with its derivative being Lipschitz continuous on this segment with a constant
L>0
, then

\displaystyle\left|f(y)-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}.

(2.1)

The second lemma established in
[
26
, Section 3]
is crucial in the convergence analysis of the general linesearch methods developed in this paper.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
be sequences in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
satisfying the condition

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\left\|d^{k}\right\|<\infty.

(2.2)

If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and if the origin is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
, then there exists an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}\;\mbox{ and }\;d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0.

(2.3)

Next we recall the classical results from
[
14
, Section 8.3.1]
that describe important properties of accumulation points
generated by a sequence satisfying a limit condition introduced by Ostrowski
[
41
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
be a sequence satisfying the Ostrowski condition

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|=0.

(2.4)

Then the following assertions are fulfilled:

(i)
If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the set of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

(ii)
If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then this sequence converges to it.

The version of the
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property
below is taken from Absil et al.
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a differentiable function. We say that
f
satisfies the
KL property
at
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
if there exist a number
\eta>0
, a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
, and a nondecreasing function
\psi:(0,\eta)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that the function
1/\psi
is integrable over
(0,\eta)
and we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq\psi\big(f(x)-f(\bar{x})\big)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in U\;\mbox{ with }\;f(\bar{x})<f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\eta.

(2.5)

Remark 2.5
.

If
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with a neighborhood
U
, it is clear that the same property holds for any
x\in U
where
f(x)=f(\bar{x})
. It has been realized that the KL property is satisfied in broad settings. In particular, it holds at every
nonstationary point
of
f
; see
[
3
, Lemma 2.1 and Remark 3.2(b)]
. Furthermore, it is proved in the seminal paper by Łojasiewicz
[
32
]
that any analytic function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
satisfies the KL property at every point
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)\penalty\ =\penalty\ Mt^{q}
for some
q\in[0,1)
. As demonstrated in
[
26
, Section 2]
, the KL property formulated in Attouch et al.
[
3
]
is stronger than the one in Definition
2.4
. Typical smooth functions that satisfy the KL property from
[
3
]
, and hence the one from Definition
2.4
, are smooth
semialgebraic
functions and also those from the more general class of functions known as
definable in o-minimal structures
; see
[
3
,
4
,
30
]
. The latter property is fulfilled, e.g., in important models arising in deep neural networks, low-rank matrix recovery, principal component analysis, and matrix completion as discussed in
[
10
, Section 6.2]
.

Next we present, based on
[
1
]
, some descent-type conditions ensuring the global convergence of iterates for smooth functions that satisfy the KL property.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function, and let the sequence of iterations
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
satisfy the following conditions:

(H1)

(primary descent condition)
. There exists
\sigma>0
such that for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\sigma\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|.

(H2)

(complementary descent condition)
. For sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, we have

\displaystyle\big[f(x^{k+1})=f(x^{k})\big]\Longrightarrow[x^{k+1}=x^{k}].

If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

When the sequence under consideration is generated by a linesearch method and satisfies some conditions stronger than (H1) and (H2) in
Proposition
2.6
, its convergence rates are established in
[
26
, Proposition 2.4]
under the KL property with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
as given below.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function, and let the sequences
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n},\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}\subset[0,\infty),\left\{d^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
satisfy the iterative condition
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Assume that for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, we have
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
and the estimates

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\beta\tau_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\alpha\left\|d^{k}\right\|,

(2.6)

where
\alpha,\beta
are some positive constants. Suppose in addition that the sequence
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0

(
i.e., there is some
\bar{\tau}>0
such that
\tau_{k}\geq\bar{\tau}
for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N})
, that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, and that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
. Then the following convergence rates are guaranteed:

(i)

If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
.

(ii)

If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then we have the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\right).

Remark 2.8
.

Observe that the two conditions in (
2.6
) together with the boundedness away from
0
of
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
yield assumptions (H1), (H2) in Proposition
2.6
. Indeed, (H1) is verified by the following inequalities:

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\beta\tau_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}=\beta\left\|\tau_{k}d^{k}\right\|\left\|d^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|.

In addition, since
\{\tau_{k}\}
is bounded away from
0
, there is
\bar{\tau}>0
such that
\tau_{k}\geq\bar{\tau}
for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Then for such
k
, the condition
f(x^{k+1})=f(x^{k})
implies that
d^{k}=0
by the first inequality in (
2.6
), and hence
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
by the iterative procedure
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}.
This also verifies (H1).

3
Global and Local Approximations of Gradients

This section is devoted to analyzing several methods for approximating gradients of a smooth function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
by using only information about function values that frequently appears in derivative-free optimization. Methods of this type include, in particular, finite differences
[
40
, Section 9]
, the Gupal estimation
[
19
]
, and gradient estimation via linear interpolation
[
7
]
. We construct two types of approximations that cover all these methods.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function. A mapping
\mathcal{G}:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is :

(i)
A
global approximation
of
\nabla f
if there is a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ for any }\;(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(3.1)

(ii)
A
local approximation
of
\nabla f
if for any bounded set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and any
\Delta>0
, there is
C>0
with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ for any }\;(x,\delta)\in\Omega\times(0,\Delta].

(3.2)

Remark 3.2
.

We have the following observations related to Definition
3.1
:

(i)
If
\mathcal{G}
is a global approximation of
\nabla f
, then it is also a local approximation of
\nabla f
.

(ii)
Assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
and that
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Then we deduce from (
3.2
) with
\Omega=\left\{x\right\}
and any
\Delta>0
the condition

\displaystyle\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\dfrac{\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|}{\delta}<\infty.

(3.3)

Next we recall the two standard types of finite differences taken from
[
40
, Section 9]
, which serve as typical examples of the approximations in Definition
3.1
.

\bullet

Forward finite difference
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,\delta):=\dfrac{1}{\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x)\right)e_{i}\text{ for any }(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(3.4)

\bullet

Central finite difference
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x,\delta):=\dfrac{1}{2\delta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x-\delta e_{i})\right)e_{i}\text{ for any }(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(3.5)

Remark 3.3
.

Let us now recall some results on the error bounds for the two types of finite differences that are mentioned above.

(i)
The global error bound for the forward finite difference (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
40
, Section 8]
) shows that it is a global approximation of
\nabla f
when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
. The local error bound for the forward finite difference is also formulated in
[
40
, Exercise 9.13]
.

(ii)
On the other hand, the global error bound for the central finite difference (see, e.g.
[
7
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
40
, Lemma 9.1]
) requires that
f
is twice continuously differentiable with a Lipschitz continuous Hessian, which is a restrictive assumption.

For completeness, we present a short proof showing that both types of finite differences are global approximations of
\nabla f
when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
and are local approximations of
\nabla f
when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function. Then the following hold:

(i)
Given
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
\delta>0
, if
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{B}}(x,\delta)
the with the constant
L>0
, then both the forward finite difference (
3.4
) and the central finite difference (
3.5
) satisfy the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2}.

(3.6)

(ii)
If
\nabla f
is globally Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L>0
, then both the forward finite difference (
3.4
) and the central finite difference (
3.5
) satisfy the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2}\;\text{ for any }\;(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(3.7)

(iii)
If
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitz continuous, then for any bounded set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and for any
\Delta>0
, there exists a positive number
L
such that both the forward finite difference (
3.4
) and the central finite difference (
3.5
) satisfy the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2}\;\text{ for any }\;(x,\delta)\in\Omega\times(0,\Delta].

(3.8)

[
Proof.]
First we verify (i) for each type of the aforementioned finite difference and then employ (i) to justify (ii) and (iii) for both types.

(i) Take any
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},\delta>0
and assume that
\nabla f
is
Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{B}}(x,\delta)
with the constant
L>0
. Consider first the case where
\mathcal{G}
is given by the forward finite difference (
3.4
). Then for any
i=1,\ldots,n
we get by employing Lemma
2.1
that

\displaystyle\left|f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),x+\delta e_{i}-x\right\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x+\delta e_{i}-x\right\|^{2}=\dfrac{L\delta^{2}}{2},

(3.9)

which is clearly equivalent to

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{1}{\delta}\left(f\left(x+\delta e_{i}\right)-f\left(x\right)\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\right|\leq\dfrac{L\delta}{2}.

Since the latter inequality holds for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{1}{\delta}\left(f(x+\delta e_{i})-f\left(x\right)\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\right)^{2}}\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2},

which therefore verifies estimate (
3.6
).

Assume now that
\mathcal{G}
is given by the central finite difference (
3.5
). Employing Lemma
2.1
gives us for any
i=1,\ldots,n
the two estimates

\displaystyle\left|f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x)-\left\langle\nabla f(x),(x+\delta e_{i})-x\right\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{L\delta^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle\left|f(x)-f(x-\delta e_{i})-\left\langle\nabla f(x),x-(x-\delta e_{i})\right\rangle\right|\leq\dfrac{L\delta^{2}}{2}.

Summing up the above estimates and using the triangle inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|f(x+\delta e_{i})-f(x-\delta e_{i})-2\left\langle\nabla f(x),\delta e_{i}\right\rangle\right|\leq L\delta^{2},

which implies in turn the conditions

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{1}{2\delta}\left(f\left(x+\delta e_{i}\right)-f\left(x-\delta e_{i}\right)\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\right|\leq\dfrac{L\delta}{2}

for all
i=1,\ldots,n
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{1}{2\delta}\left(f(x+\delta e_{i})-f\left(x-\delta e_{i}\right)\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x)\right)^{2}}\leq\dfrac{L\sqrt{n}\delta}{2},

which brings us to (
3.6
) and thus justifies (i).

Assertion (ii) follows directly from (i). To verify (iii), take some
\Delta>0
and a bounded set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and then find
r>0
such that
\Omega\subset r\overline{\mathbb{B}}
. Defining
\Theta:=(r+\Delta)\overline{\mathbb{B}}
, it is clear that
\Theta
is compact. Since
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitzian, it is Lipschitz continuous on
\Theta
with some constant
L>0
. Taking any
(x,\delta)\in\Omega\times(0,\Delta],
we get that
\overline{\mathbb{B}}(x,\delta)\subset\Theta
, and thus
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{B}}(x,\delta)
with the constant
L
. Employing finally (i), assertion (iii) is justified.

The following example shows that when the local Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
is replaced by merely the continuity of
\nabla f
, the finite differences may not be local approximation of
\nabla f
.

Example 1
.

Define the univariate real-valued function
f
by

\displaystyle f(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{x^{3}}&\text{ if }x\geq 0,\\
-\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{-x^{3}}&\text{ if }x<0.\\
\end{cases}

The derivative of
f
is calculated by

\displaystyle\nabla f(x)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{x}&\text{ if }x\geq 0,\\
\sqrt{-x}&\text{ if }x<0\\
\end{cases}

being clearly continuous on
{\rm I\!R}
while not Lipschitz continuous around
0
. If we suppose that
\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)
is the forward finite difference approximation of
\nabla f(x)
from (
3.4
), we get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(0,\delta)=\dfrac{f(\delta)-f(0)}{\delta}=\dfrac{\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\delta^{3}}}{\delta}=\dfrac{2\sqrt{\delta}}{3}\;\text{ for all }\;\delta>0,

which implies that
\mathcal{G}(0,\delta)/\delta\rightarrow\infty
as
\delta\downarrow 0
. It follows from (
3.3
) that
\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)
is not a local approximation of the derivative
\nabla f
. Supposing now that
\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)
is the central finite difference approximation of
\nabla f(x)
, we deduce from (
3.5
) the expression

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(0,\delta)=\dfrac{f(\delta)-f(-\delta)}{2\delta}=\dfrac{4\sqrt{\delta}}{3}\;\text{ for all }\;\delta>0,

which also tells us that
\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)
is not a local approximation of
\nabla f
.

4
General Derivative-Free Methods for
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
Functions

This section addresses the optimization problem (
1.1
) when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
for some
L>0
. In the context of derivative-free optimization, we assume that the only information about the function value
f(x)
is available. This means that
\nabla f(x)
is not exactly computable at every
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and the Lipschitz constant
L
of
\nabla f
is also not available. By employing gradient approximation methods that satisfy the global error bound (
3.1
), we propose here the general
Derivative-Free method with Constant stepsize
(DFC) to solve this problem with providing its convergence analysis. The DFC algorithm is described as follows.

Algorithm 1

(DFC)
.

Step 0
.

Choose a global approximation
\mathcal{G}
of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.1
). Select an initial point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},
an initial sampling radius
\delta_{1}>0,
a constant
C_{1}>0,
a reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
, and scaling factors
\mu>2,r>1,\kappa>0
. Set
k:=1.

Step 1

(approximate gradient)
.

Find
g^{k}
and the smallest nonnegative integer
i_{k}
such that

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k})\text{ and }\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}.

Then set
\delta_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}.

Step 2

(update)
.

If
f\Big(x^{k}-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{k}}g^{k}\Big)\leq f(x^{k})-\dfrac{\kappa(\mu-2)}{2C_{k}\mu}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}
, then
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{k}}g^{k}
and
C_{k+1}:=C_{k}.
Otherwise,
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}
and
C_{k+1}:=rC_{k}.

Remark 4.1
.

Let us now present some observations on Algorithm
1
. The first observation clarifies the existence of
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
in Step 1. Observation (ii) presents the ideas behind the construction of the algorithm. Observation (iii) explains the iteration updates in Step 2 while observation (iv) interprets the term “constant stepsize” in the name of our method.

(i)
The procedure of finding
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
that satisfy Step 1 can be given as follows. Set
i_{k}=0
and set

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\delta_{k}).

(4.1)

While
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq\mu C_{k}\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}
, increase
i_{k}
by
1
and recalculate
g^{k}
under (
4.1
). When
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
, the existence of
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
in Step 1 is guaranteed. Indeed, otherwise we get a sequence
\left\{g^{k}_{i}\right\}
with

\displaystyle g^{k}_{i}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\theta^{i}\delta_{k})\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}_{i}\right\|\leq\mu\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;i\in{\rm I\!N}.

(4.2)

Since
\theta\in(0,1),
the latter means that
g^{k}_{i}\rightarrow 0
as
i\rightarrow\infty.

Since
\mathcal{G}
is a global approximation of
\nabla f
, for
C>0
given in (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}_{i}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\text{ for all }i\in{\rm I\!N}.

Letting
i\rightarrow\infty
with taking into account that
g^{k}_{i}\rightarrow 0
, the latter inequality implies that
\nabla f(x^{k})=0
, which is a contradiction.

(ii)
Since
\mathcal{G}
is a global approximation of
\nabla f
, let
C
be a constant given in (
3.1
). Since
C
is unknown, Algorithm
1
generates the sequence
\left\{C_{k}\right\}
that approximates
C
. Assume that in some iteration
k^{\rm th},
the constant
C_{k}
is slightly larger than
C
. Then Step 1 of Algorithm
1
provides

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\delta_{k+1}\leq C_{k}\delta_{k+1}<\dfrac{1}{\mu}\left\|g^{k}\right\|.

(4.3)

Since
\mu>2
, the inequality above gives us
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\dfrac{1}{\mu}\left\|g^{k}\right\|,
which yields the angle condition
\left\langle g^{k},\nabla f(x^{k})\right\rangle>0
as illustrated in the Figure
1
below.

Figure 1
:
An illustration for the angle condition

Moreover, the result of
[
27
, Theorem 3.2]
about the convergence of general inexact gradient methods also tells us that the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
satisfying the procedure
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\frac{1}{L}g^{k}
and condition (
4.3
) achieves fundamental convergence properties. Although the Lipschitz constant
L
of
\nabla f

is not available
in our context, it is linearly related to
C
for some choice of
\mathcal{G}
; e.g., when
\mathcal{G}
is obtained from finite differences, we get by Proposition
3.4
that
C=\frac{L\sqrt{n}}{2}
. Thus the procedure
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\frac{1}{L}g^{k}
can be replaced by

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=x^{k}-C^{-1}\kappa g^{k}\approx x^{k}-C_{k}^{-1}\kappa g^{k}\;\text{ for some constant }\;\kappa>0.

In the formulation of Algorithm
1
, the constant
\kappa
can be chosen as an arbitrary positive number, which creates more flexibility for our method.

(iii)
The condition
f(x^{k}-C_{k}^{-1}\kappa g^{k})\leq f(x^{k})-\dfrac{\kappa(\mu-2)}{2C_{k}\mu}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}
determines whether
C_{k}
is a good approximation for
C
in the sense that the objective function
f
is sufficiently decreasing when the iterate moves from
x^{k}
to
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-C_{k}^{-1}\kappa g^{k}
. If the condition does not hold, we increase
C_{k}
by setting
C_{k+1}:=rC_{k}
to get a better approximation for
C
and stagnate the iterative sequence by setting
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}
.

(iv)
It will be shown in Proposition
4.2
that there exists a positive number
\bar{C}>0
such that
C_{k}=\bar{C}
for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, which also implies that
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\kappa\bar{C}^{-1}g^{k}
for such
k
. This explains the term “constant stepsize” in the name of our algorithm.

The next proposition verifies that the tail of the sequence
\left\{C_{k}\right\}
generated by Algorithm
1
is constant, which is crucial in deriving the fundamental convergence properties of the algorithm.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\left\{C_{k}\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Assume that
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Then there exists a number
N\in{\rm I\!N}
such that
C_{k+1}=C_{k}
whenever
k\geq N
.

[
Proof.]
Since
\mathcal{G}
is a global approximation of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.1
), there exists
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ for all }\;(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(4.4)

Since
\nabla f
is globally Lipschitz continuous, we also find
L>0
such that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Arguing by contradiction, suppose that the number
N
asserted in the proposition does not exist. By Step 2 of Algorithm
1
, this implies that
C_{k+1}=rC_{k}
for infinitely many
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, and hence
C_{k}\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, there is a number
K\in{\rm I\!N}
such that
C_{K+1}=rC_{K}
and
C_{K}>\max\left\{C,L\kappa\right\}
. Using Step 2 of Algorithm
1
together with the update
C_{K+1}=rC_{K}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle f\Big(x^{K}-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}g^{K}\Big)>f(x^{K})-\dfrac{\kappa(\mu-2)}{2C_{K}\mu}\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}.

(4.5)

Combining
g^{K}=\mathcal{G}(x^{K},\delta_{K+1})
and
\left\|g^{K}\right\|\geq\mu C_{K}\delta_{K+1}
from Step 1 of Algorithm
1
with (
4.4
) and
C_{K}>C
as above, we get the relationships

\displaystyle\left\|g^{K}-\nabla f(x^{K})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{K},\delta_{K+1})-\nabla f(x^{K})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C\delta_{K+1}\leq C_{K}\delta_{K+1}\leq\mu^{-1}\left\|g^{K}\right\|.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the latter gives us

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x^{K}),g^{K}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\nabla f(x^{K})-g^{K},g^{K}\right\rangle+\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\left\|\nabla f(x^{K})-g^{K}\right\|\left\|g^{K}\right\|+\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(1-\mu^{-1})\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}.

Combining this with Lemma
2.1
and taking into account the global Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
with the constant
L
as well as the condition
C_{K}>L\kappa
as above, we get that

\displaystyle f\Big(x^{K}-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}g^{K}\Big)-f(x^{K})

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{K}),g^{K}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}g^{K}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}\Big(1-\dfrac{1}{\mu}\Big)\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{\kappa}{2C_{K}}\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{K}}\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2}\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{\mu}\Big)=-\dfrac{\kappa(\mu-2)}{2C_{K}\mu}\left\|g^{K}\right\|^{2},

which clearly contradicts(
4.5
) and completes the proof of the proposition.

Now we are ready to establish the convergence properties of Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
and assume that
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}.
Then either
f(x^{k})\rightarrow-\infty
, or we have the assertions:

(i)
The sequence
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
converges to
0
.

(ii)
If
f
satisfies the KL property at some accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
.

(iii)
If
f
satisfies the KL property at some accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
, then the following convergence rates are guaranteed for
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
:

\bullet
If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
,
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
, and
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
converge linearly to
\bar{x}
,
0
, and
f(\bar{x})
, respectively.

\bullet
The setting of
q\in(1/2,1)
ensures the estimates

\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\Big(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\Big),\;\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\mathcal{O}\Big(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\Big),\;\text{ and }\;f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x})=\mathcal{O}\Big(k^{-\frac{2-2q}{2q-1}}\Big).

[
Proof.]
Since
\mathcal{G}
is a global approximation of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.1
), there is
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ for all }\;(x,\delta)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times(0,\infty).

(4.6)

By
f\in{\cal C}^{1,1}_{L}
, we find
L>0
such that the gradient mapping
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Taking the number
N\in{\rm I\!N}
from Proposition
4.2
ensures that
C_{k}=C_{N}
for all
k\geq N
. This implies by Step 2 of Algorithm
1
that

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})=f\Big(x^{k}-\dfrac{\kappa}{C_{N}}g^{k}\Big)\leq f(x^{k})-\dfrac{\kappa(\mu-2)}{2C_{N}\mu}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N,

(4.7)

which tells us that
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}_{k\geq N}
is decreasing. If
f(x^{k})\rightarrow-\infty
, there is nothing to prove, so we assume that
f(x^{k})\not\rightarrow-\infty
, which implies that
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
is convergent. As a consequence, we get
f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Then (
4.7
) tells us that
g^{k}\rightarrow 0
. From Step 1 of Algorithm
1
it follows that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\delta_{k+1}=\mu C_{N}\delta_{k+1}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N,

(4.8)

which implies that
\delta_{k+1}\downarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. It further follows from
g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\delta_{k+1})
and (
4.6
) that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\delta_{k+1})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\delta_{k+1}\text{ for all }k\in{\rm I\!N},

(4.9)

which yields
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
and thus justifies (i).

To verify (ii), take any accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and assume that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
. By (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), we obtain that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|&\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})-g^{k}\right\|\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+C\delta_{k+1}\\
&\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+\dfrac{C\left\|g^{k}\right\|}{\mu C_{N}}=\alpha\left\|g^{k}\right\|\text{ for all }k\geq N,\end{array}

where
\alpha:=\frac{\mu C_{N}+C}{\mu C_{N}}
. This together with (
4.7
) yields condition (
2.6
) in Proposition
2.7
. By Remark
2.8
(i), assumptions (H1) and (H2) in Proposition
2.6
hold. Therefore,
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
, which justifies (ii).

To proceed with the proof of assertion (iii) under the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
, we use the iterations
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-C^{-1}\kappa g^{k}
as in Step 2 of Algorithm
1
together with
\|g^{k}\|>0
from Step 1 of Algorithm
1
. This gives us
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
for
k\geq N
. Combining the latter with (
4.7
) and (
4
), we see that all the assumptions in Proposition
2.7
are satisfied. This verifies the convergence rates of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
to
\bar{x}
stated in (iii). Since
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, it follows from (i) that
\bar{x}
is a stationary point of
f
, i.e.,
\nabla f(\bar{x})=0
. Hence the usage of Lemma
2.1
and the decreasing property of
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}_{k\geq N}
yields

\displaystyle 0\leq f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x})\leq\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x}),x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\rangle+\dfrac{{L}}{2}\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|^{2}=\dfrac{{L}}{2}\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|^{2},

which justifies the convergence rates of
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
to
f(\bar{x})
as asserted in (iii).

It remains to verify the convergence rates for
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
. Since
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L>0
, the claimed property follows from the convergence rates for
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
due to

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})-\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|\leq L\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|.

This therefore completes the proof of the theorem.

5
General Derivative-free Methods for
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
Functions

In this section, we propose and justify the novel
Derivative-Free method with Backtracking stepsize
(DFB) to solve the optimization problem (
1.1
). The main result here establishes the
global convergence
with convergence rates of the following algorithm, which employs gradient approximations satisfying the local error bound estimate (
3.2
).

Algorithm 2

(DFB)
.

Step 0

(initialization)
.

Choose a local approximation
\mathcal{G}
of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.2
). Select an initial point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and initial radius
\delta_{1}>0
, a constant
C_{1}>0
, factors
\theta\in(0,1)
,
\mu>2,\eta>1
, linesearch constants
\beta\in(0,1/2),\gamma\in(0,1)
,
\bar{\tau}>0
, and an initial bound
t^{\min}_{1}\in(0,\bar{\tau})
. Choose a sequence of manually controlled errors
\left\{\nu_{k}\right\}\subset[0,\infty)
such that
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Set
k:=1.

Step 1

(approximate gradient)
.

Select
g^{k}
and the smallest nonnegative integer
i_{k}
so that

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k},\nu_{k}\right\})\text{ and }\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}.

(5.1)

Then set
\delta_{k+1}:=\theta^{i_{k}}\theta_{k}
.

Step 2

(linesearch)
.

Set
t_{k}:=\bar{\tau}
. While

\displaystyle f(x^{k}-t_{k}g^{k})>f(x^{k})-\beta t_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\text{ and }t_{k}\geq t_{k}^{\min},

(5.2)

set
t_{k}:=\gamma t_{k}.

Step 3

(stepsize and parameters update)
.

If
t_{k}\geq t^{\min}_{k}
, then set
\tau_{k}:=t_{k}
,
C_{k+1}:=C_{k}
, and
t^{\min}_{k+1}:=t^{\min}_{k}
. Otherwise, set
\tau_{k}:=0,\;C_{k+1}:=\eta C_{k}
and
t^{\min}_{k+1}:=\gamma t^{\min}_{k}
.

Step 4

(iteration update)
.

Set
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\tau_{k}g^{k}.
Increase
k
by
1
and go back to Step 1.

Remark 5.1
.

(i)
Fix any
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. The procedure of finding
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
that satisfies Step 1 of Algorithm
2
can be given as follows. Set
i_{k}:=0
and calculate
g^{k}
as

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}\big(x^{k},\min\{\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k},\nu_{k}\}\big).

(5.3)

While
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq\mu C_{k}\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}
, increase
i_{k}
by
1
and recalculate
g^{k}
by formula (
5.3
). We now show that when
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
, this procedure stops after a finite number of steps giving us
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
as desired. Indeed, assuming the contrary that the procedure does not stop, we get a sequence of
\left\{g^{k}_{i}\right\}
with

\displaystyle g^{k}_{i}=\mathcal{G}\big(x^{k},\min\left\{\theta^{i}\delta_{k},\nu_{k}\right\}\big)\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}_{i}\right\|\leq\mu C_{k}\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;i\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.4)

Since
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
, for any fixed
\Delta>0
the condition (
3.2
) with
\Omega=\left\{x^{k}\right\}
ensures the existence of a positive number
C
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\delta)-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ whenever }\;0<\delta\leq\Delta.

(5.5)

By
\theta\in(0,1)
, there is
N\in{\rm I\!N}
with
\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\leq\Delta
for all
i\geq N
. Combining this with (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) yields

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}_{i}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}_{i}\right\|\leq\mu C_{k}\theta^{i}\delta_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;i\geq N.

Letting
i\rightarrow\infty
, we arrive at
\nabla f(x^{k})=0
, which is a contradiction.

(ii)
It follows directly from the construction of
\delta_{k}
in Step 1 of Algorithm
2
that

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\})\text{ and }\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\delta_{k+1}.

(5.6)

To proceed further with the convergence analysis of Algorithm
2
, we obtain two results of their independent interest. The first one reveals some uniformity of general linesearch procedures with respect to the selections of reference points, stepsizes, and directions.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a function with a locally Lipschitz continuous gradient, and let
\beta\in(0,1/2)
. Then for any nonempty bounded set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, there exists
\bar{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle f(x-tg)\leq f(x)-\beta t\left\|g\right\|^{2}\;\text{ whenever }\;x\in\Omega,\;2\left\|g-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\left\|g\right\|,\;\text{ and }\;t\in(0,\bar{t}].

[
Proof.]
The boundedness of
\Omega
gives us
r>0
such that
\Omega\subset r\overline{\mathbb{B}}
. Using the continuity of
\nabla f
and the compactness of
r\overline{\mathbb{B}}
, we define
r^{\prime}:=\max\left\{\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\;|\;x\in r\overline{\mathbb{B}}\right\}
.
Since
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
, there is
L>0
such that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L
on
\Theta:=(r+2r^{\prime})\overline{\mathbb{B}}
. By
\beta<1/2
, we find
\bar{t}>0
with
\bar{t}<\min\left\{1,L^{-1}(1-2\beta)\right\}
. Now take some
x\in\Omega\subset\Theta
and
g\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
such that
2\left\|g-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\left\|g\right\|
and
t\in(0,\bar{t}]
. The choice of
g
gives us by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\langle\nabla f(x),g\right\rangle&=\left\langle\nabla f(x)-g,g\right\rangle+\left\|g\right\|^{2}\geq-\left\|\nabla f(x)-g\right\|\left\|g\right\|+\left\|g\right\|^{2}\\
&\geq-\frac{1}{2}\left\|g\right\|^{2}+\left\|g\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|g\right\|^{2},\end{array}

(5.7)

and by using the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq\left\|g\right\|-\left\|g-\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|g\right\|.\end{array}

Combining the latter with the choice of
t,\;\bar{t}
,
x\in\Omega\subset r\overline{\mathbb{B}}
and the construction of
r^{\prime}
yields

\displaystyle t\left\|g\right\|\leq\bar{t}\left\|g\right\|\leq 2\bar{t}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq 2\bar{t}r^{\prime}<2r^{\prime},

which ensures that
x-tg\in\Theta
. The convexity of
\Theta
tells us that the entire line segment
[x,x-tg]
lies on
\Theta
. Remembering that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with the constant
L
on
\Theta
, we employ Lemma
2.1
by taking into account that
t\leq\bar{t}<L^{-1}(1-2\beta)
and that (
5.7
). This gives us

\displaystyle f(x-tg)-f(x)

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle x-tg-x,\nabla f(x)\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x-tg-x\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-t\left\langle g,\nabla f(x)\right\rangle+\dfrac{Lt^{2}}{2}\left\|g\right\|^{2}\leq-\frac{t}{2}\left\|g\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{Lt^{2}}{2}\left\|g\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\beta t\left\|g\right\|^{2}+t\left\|g\right\|^{2}\dfrac{2\beta-1+Lt}{2}\leq-\beta t\left\|g\right\|^{2}

and thus completes the proof of the lemma.

Employing the obtained lemma, we derive the next result showing that unless the stationary point is found, Algorithm
2
always makes a progress after a finite number of iterations.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
be the sequences generated by Algorithm
2
, and let
K\in{\rm I\!N}
be such that
\nabla f(x^{K})\neq 0
. Then we can choose a number
N\geq K
so that
\tau_{N}>0
.

[
Proof.]
Assume on the contrary that
\tau_{k}=0
for all
k\geq K
. Steps 3 and 4 of Algorithm
2
give us

\displaystyle t^{\min}_{k+1}=\gamma t^{\min}_{k}\;\text{ and }\;x^{k}=x^{K}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq K.

(5.9)

Therefore,
\nabla f(x^{k})=\nabla f(x^{K})\neq 0
for all
k\geq K,
which implies that
g^{k}
and
i_{k}
in Step 1 of Algorithm
2
exist for all
k\geq K
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
, for any fixed
\Delta>0
condition (
3.2
) with
\Omega=\left\{x^{K}\right\}
ensures the existence of
C>0
with

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{K},\delta)-\nabla f(x^{K})\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ whenever }\;0<\delta\leq\Delta.

(5.10)

It follows from Lemma
5.2
with
\Omega=\left\{x^{K}\right\}
that there is some
\bar{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{K}-tg)\leq f(x^{K})-\beta t\left\|g\right\|^{2}\;\text{ whenever }\;2\left\|g-\nabla f(x^{K})\right\|\leq\left\|g\right\|\;\text{ and }\;t\in(0,\bar{t}].

(5.11)

Using
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0,\;t_{k}^{\min}\downarrow 0
,
\nabla f(x^{K})\neq 0
, and (
5.9
) gives us
N\geq K
for which
\nu_{N}<\min\left\{\Delta,\frac{1}{3C}\left\|\nabla f(x^{K})\right\|\right\}
and
t^{\min}_{N}<\gamma\bar{t}
. Then we get from (
5.10
) with taking into account
x^{N}=x^{K}
that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{N},\min\left\{\delta_{N+1},\nu_{N}\right\})-\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|\leq C\min\left\{\delta_{N+1},\nu_{N}\right\}\leq C\nu_{N}\leq\dfrac{1}{3}\left\|\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|.

Combining this with
g^{N}=\mathcal{G}(x^{N},\min\left\{\delta_{N+1},\nu_{N}\right\})
from (
5.6
) provides the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|g^{N}-\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|\leq\dfrac{1}{3}\left\|\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|

which implies by the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{N}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|-\left\|g^{N}-\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|\geq 2\left\|g^{N}-\nabla f(x^{N})\right\|.

Employing the latter together with (
5.11
) and
x^{N}=x^{K}
yields

\displaystyle f(x^{N}-tg^{N})\leq f(x^{N})-\beta t\left\|g^{N}\right\|^{2}\text{ for all }t\in(0,\bar{t}].

(5.12)

It follows from (
5.12
) and the choice of parameters that

\displaystyle\max\big\{t\;\big|\;f(x^{N}-tg^{N})\leq f(x^{N})-\beta t\left\|g^{N}\right\|^{2},\;t=\bar{\tau},\bar{\tau}\gamma,\bar{\tau}\gamma^{2},\ldots\big\}>\gamma\bar{t}>t_{N}^{\min},

which implies in turn by Step 2 of Algorithm
2
that

\displaystyle t_{N}=\max\big\{t\;|\;f(x^{N}-tg^{N})\leq f(x^{N})-\beta t\left\|g^{N}\right\|^{2},\;t=\bar{\tau},\bar{\tau}\gamma,\bar{\tau}\gamma^{2},\ldots\big\}>t_{N}^{\min}.

By Step 3 of Algorithm
2
, we conclude that
\tau_{N}=t_{N}>0
, a contradiction which completes the proof.

Now we are ready the establish the convergence properties of Algorithm
2
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
2
and assume that
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}.
Then either
f(x^{k})\rightarrow-\infty
, or the following assertions hold:

(i)
Every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
.

(ii)
If the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the set of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

(iii)
If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then this sequence converges to it.

[
Proof.]
First it follows from Steps 2 and 3 of Algorithm
2
that

\displaystyle\beta\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

(5.13)

which tells us that
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
is nonincreasing. If
f(x^{k})\rightarrow-\infty
, there is nothing to prove; so we assume that
f(x^{k})\not\rightarrow-\infty
, which implies by the nonincreasing property of
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
that
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
. Summing up the inequalities in (
5.13
) over
k=1,2,\ldots
with taking into account that
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\tau_{k}g^{k}
from the update in Step 3 of Algorithm
2
gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty\;\text{ and }\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|g^{k}\right\|\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|<\infty.

(5.14)

We divide the proof of (i) into two parts by showing first that the origin is an accumulation point of
\left\{g^{k}\right\}
and then employing Lemma
2.2
to establish the stationarity of all the accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}.

Claim 1
.

The origin
0\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is an accumulation point of the sequence
\left\{g^{k}\right\}
.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that there are numbers
\varepsilon>0
and
K\in{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|\geq\varepsilon\;\text{ for all }k\geq K.

(5.15)

Combining this with (
5.14
) gives us
\tau_{k}\downarrow 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|<\infty
. The latter implies that
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges to some
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. By taking a larger
K
, we can assume that
\tau_{k}<\bar{\tau}
for all
k\geq K
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
such that
\tau_{k}>0
. It follows from Proposition
5.3
that
\mathcal{N}
is infinite. Hence we can take any
k\geq K
with
k\in\mathcal{N}
and get that
\tau_{k}\in(0,\bar{\tau})
. By Step 3 of Algorithm
2
, we deduce that
\tau_{k}=t_{k}\in[t^{\min}_{k},\bar{\tau})
. Fixing some such
k
, we get from the exit condition in Step 2 of Algorithm
2
that

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}\beta\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}<f(x^{k}-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}g^{k})-f(x^{k}).

(5.16)

The classical mean value theorem gives us
\widetilde{x}^{k}\in[x^{k},x^{k}-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}g^{k}]
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{k}-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}g^{k})-f(x^{k})=-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}\left\langle g^{k},\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\right\rangle.

(5.17)

Combining this with (
5.16
) yields

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}\beta\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}<-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}\left\langle g^{k},\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\right\rangle,

which implies by dividing both sides of the inequality by
-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}<0
that

\displaystyle\left\langle g^{k},\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\right\rangle<\beta\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq K,\;k\in\mathcal{N}.

(5.18)

Take some neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}
and
\Delta>0
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.2
), there is
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ whenever }\;0<\delta\leq\Delta\;\text{ and }\;x\in\Omega.

(5.19)

Since
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0
and
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
, by taking a larger
K
we can assume that
\nu_{k}<\Delta
and
x^{k}\in\Omega
for all
k\geq K
. Using this together with (
5.19
) and
g^{k}=\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\})
in (
5.6
), we get that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\}\leq C\nu_{k}.

Combining the latter with
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
,
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, and the continuity of
\nabla f
gives us

\displaystyle g^{k}\rightarrow\nabla f(\bar{x})\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty,

(5.20)

which yields
\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|>0
by (
5.15
). It follows from (
5.20
),
\tau_{k}\downarrow 0
,
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
, and
\widetilde{x}^{k}\in[x^{k},x^{k}-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{k}g^{k}]
for all
k\geq K,k\in\mathcal{N}
that
\widetilde{x}^{k}\overset{\mathcal{N}}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
. Letting
k\overset{\mathcal{N}}{\rightarrow}\infty
in (
5.18
) with taking into account the convergence above and (
5.20
) brings us to the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\beta\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|^{2}.

This contradicts
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|>0
. Thus the origin is an accumulation point of
\left\{g^{k}\right\}
as claimed.

Claim 2
.

Every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
.

Take any accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Using Claim
1
, the second inequality in (
5.14
), and Lemma
2.2
tells us that there is an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}\text{ and }g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0.

Take a neighborhood
\Omega
of
\bar{x}
and
\Delta>0
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.2
), there exists
C>0
for which

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ whenever }\;0<\delta\leq\Delta\;\text{ and }\;x\in\Omega.

(5.21)

Since
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0
and
x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
, we can select
K\in{\rm I\!N}
so that
\nu_{k}\leq\Delta
and
x^{k}\in\Omega
for all
k\geq K,k\in J
. This ensures together with (
5.21
) that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq C\nu_{k}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq K,\;k\in J.

Employing
g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
and
\nu_{k}\downarrow 0
as above, we deduce that
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
, and hence
\nabla f(\bar{x})=0.
Therefore,
\bar{x}
is a stationary point of
f,
which justifies (i).

Now we verify (ii) and (iii) simultaneously. It follows from (
5.14
) and
\tau_{k}\leq 1
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
by the choice of
\tau_{k}
in Step 3 of Algorithm
2
that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}^{2}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\bar{\tau}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty,

which implies that
\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\rightarrow 0
. Then both assertions (ii) and (iii) follow from Lemma
2.3
.

The next result establishes the global convergence with convergence rates of the iterates
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
in Algorithm
2
under the KL property and the boundedness of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. We have already discussed the KL property in Remark
2.5
. The boundedness of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is also a standard assumption that appears in many works on gradient descent methods; see, e.g.,
[
4
, Theorem 4.1]
,
[

, Theorem 1]
, and
[
29
, Assumption 7]
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence of iterates generated by Algorithm
2
. Assuming that
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
and that
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded yields the assertions:

(i)
If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

(ii)
If in addition to
(i)
, the KL property at
\bar{x}
is satisfied with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0,q\in(0,1)
, then the following convergence rates are guaranteed:

\bullet
If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
.

\bullet
If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then we have the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\big(k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\big).

[
Proof.]
Let
\Omega:=\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, and let
\Delta>0
. Since
\mathcal{G}
is a local approximation of
\nabla f
satisfying condition (
3.2
), there exists a positive number
C
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{G}(x,\delta)-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq C\delta\;\text{ whenever }\;x\in\Omega\;\text{ and }\;0<\delta\leq\Delta.

(5.22)

Select
K\in{\rm I\!N}
so that
\nu_{k}<\Delta
for all
k\geq K
, which implies by (
5.22
) and the choice of
g^{k}
in Step 1 of Algorithm
2
the relationships

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{G}(x^{k},\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\})-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C\min\left\{\delta_{k+1},\nu_{k}\right\}\leq C\delta_{k+1}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq K.

(5.23)

We split the proof of the result into two parts by showing first that the sequences
\left\{C_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{t_{k}^{\min}\right\}
are constant after a finite number of iterations and verifying then the convergence of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
in (i) with the rates in (ii) by using Propositions
2.6
and
2.7
.

Claim 1
.

There exists
k_{0}\in{\rm I\!N}
such that
C_{k}=C_{k_{0}}
and
t^{\min}_{k}=t^{\min}_{k_{0}}
for all
k\geq k_{0}.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose that such a number
k_{0}
does not exist. By the construction of
\left\{C_{k}\right\}
and
\left\{t^{\min}_{k}\right\}
in Step 3 of Algorithm
2
, we deduce that
C_{k}\uparrow\infty
and
t^{\min}_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Since
\Omega
is bounded, Lemma
5.2
allows us to find
\bar{t}\in(0,1)
for which

\displaystyle f(x-tg)\leq f(x)-\beta t\left\|g\right\|^{2}\;\text{ whenever }\;x\in\Omega,\;\left\|g-\nabla f(x)\right\|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|g\right\|,\;\text{ and }\;t\in(0,\bar{t}].

(5.24)

Using the aforementioned properties of
\{C_{k}\}
and

\{t^{\min}_{k}\}
, we get
N\geq K
such that
C_{k}>C
and
t^{\min}_{k}<\gamma\bar{t}
for all
k\geq N
. Fix such a number
k
and then combine the condition
\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\delta_{k+1}
from (
5.1
) with
C_{k}>C
,
\mu>2
, and (
5
). This gives us

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\delta_{k+1}\geq\mu C\delta_{k+1}\geq 2\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|,

which implies together with
x^{k}\in\Omega
and (
5.24
) the estimate

\displaystyle f(x^{k}-tg^{k})\leq f(x^{k})-\beta t\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\text{ for all }t\in(0,\bar{t}]

and thus tells us that
t_{k}>\gamma\bar{t}>t_{k}^{\min}
. Employing Step 3 of Algorithm
2
yields
t_{k+1}^{\min}=t_{k}^{\min}
. Since the latter holds whenever
k\geq N
, we conclude that the equality
t_{k}^{\min}=t_{N}^{\min}
is satisfied for all
k\geq N
. This contradicts the condition
t^{\min}_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
and hence justifies the claimed assertion.

Claim 2
.

All the assertions in
(i)
and
(ii)
are fulfilled.

From Step 2 and Step 3 of Algorithm
2
, we deduce that

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\beta\tau_{k}\left\|g^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.25)

Defining
N:=\max\left\{K,k_{0}\right\}
with
k_{0}
taken from Claim
1
gives us the equalities

\displaystyle C_{k}=C_{N}\;\text{ and }\;t_{k}^{\min}=t_{N}^{\min}\;\text{ whenever }\;k\geq N.

(5.26)

Combining
C_{k}=C_{N}
with (
5
) and
\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\delta_{k+1}
from (
5.1
) ensures that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+C\delta_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+\dfrac{C}{\mu C_{N}}\left\|g^{k}\right\|=\alpha\left\|g^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N,

(5.27)

where
\alpha:=1+\frac{C}{\mu C_{N}}
. In addition, we have

in (

\displaystyle\tau_{k}=t_{k}\geq t_{k}^{\min}=t_{N}^{\min}\;\text{ as }\;k\geq N

(5.28)

confirming the boundedness of
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
from below. If the KL property of

holds at the accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, it follows from Remark
2.8
(i), (
5.25
), (
5
), and (
5.28
) that assumptions (H1) and (H2) in Proposition

hold. Thus
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as

, which verifies (i).

Assume finally that the KL property at
\bar{x}
is satisfied with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q},\;M>0
, and

. The iterative procedure

in Step 4 of Algorithm

together with (

from Step 1 therein tells us that

Combining this with (

5

6
Local Convergence of General Derivative-Free Methods to Nonisolated Local Minimizers

In this section, we develop appropriate versions of both Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
and obtain efficient conditions that guarantee the
local convergence
with constructive convergence rates of these algorithms to
nonisolated local minimizers
.

The following general algorithm of this type covers local versions of both Algorithms
1
and
2
.

Algorithm 3

(General derivative-free (GDF) method)
.

Step 0
.

Choose a local approximation
\mathcal{G}
of
\nabla f
under condition (
3.2
). Select an initial point

an initial sampling radius
\delta_{1}>0,
a sequence
\left\{C_{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}_{+},
a reduction factor
\theta\in(0,1)
and a scaling factor
\mu>2
. Choose a sequence of manually controlled sampling radii
\left\{\nu_{k}\right\}\subset[0,\infty)
. Set
k:=1
.

Step 1

(approximate gradient)
.

Find
g^{k}
and the smallest nonnegative integer

such that

\displaystyle g^{k}=\mathcal{G}\big(x^{k},\min\left\{\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k},\nu_{k}\right\}\big)\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}\right\|>\mu C_{k}\theta^{i_{k}}\delta_{k}.

Then set

Step 2

\tau_{k}\geq 0
and set

Go back to Step 1.

Similarly to Remark

Assuming that

The Lipschitz continuity of

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})-f(x^{k})

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

Remark 6.2
.

Note that the condition
\nabla f(x^{k})\neq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
in Theorem
6.1
is a standard assumption in the convergence analysis of derivative-free optimization methods since there is no tool available to determine whether
\nabla f(x^{k})
equals zero or not. Similar assumptions can also be found at
[
12
, Section 4]
and
[
19
, Corollary 3.3]
. Recently, Josz et al.
[
24
]
presented a local convergence analysis for
exact momentum methods
with
constant stepsizes
, specifically focusing on
semi-algebraic
functions with gradients being
locally Lipschitzian everywhere
. It is necessary to emphasize that this analysis does not encompass the obtained convergence properties in Theorem
6.1
. Specifically, our work focuses on
derivative-free methods
with
variable stepsizes
when applied to
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth functions satisfying the KL property. These functions have gradients that are
locally Lipschitzian
but only in the vicinity of
local minimizers
. Observe also that the local convergence result above does not follow from the one in
[
4
, Theorem 2.10]
. The latter relies on global conditions (H1) and (H2), along with the local growth condition (H4), which are not presumed in our derivative-free context and may not be satisfied within the scope of our given assumptions.

The following two consequences of Theorem
6.1
address the local convergence to local minimizers of Algorithm
1
and Algorithm
2
.

[

8
Concluding Remarks

This paper proposes two general derivative-free optimization methods for minimizing smooth functions. The proposed methods achieve stationary accumulation points, and under the KL property of the objective function, the global convergence with constructive convergence rates. The two newly developed algorithms cover derivative-free methods based on finite differences for
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}_{L}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
functions, where the finite difference intervals are chosen as large as possible while being automatically adapted with the exact gradients without knowing them. Our methods do not force finite difference intervals to decrease after each iteration, which omits rounding errors as much as possible and thus leads to better numerical performances in solving general convex, nonconvex, noiseless, and noisy derivative-free smooth problems in finite-dimensional spaces. In addition to the aforementioned globally convergent algorithms, we design their local versions, which locally converge to nonisolated local minimizers.

Our future research includes convergence analysis of the newly developed algorithms coupled with quasi-Newton methods for noisy smooth functions. We also intend to establish efficient conditions to ensure local and global convergence to local minimizers of iterative sequences generated by derivative-free methods for problems of nonsmooth constrained optimization.
