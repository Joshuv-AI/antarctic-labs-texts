Searching for Isotropic Stochastic Gravitational-Wave Background in the International Pulsar Timing Array Second Data Release
Zu-Cheng Chen, Yu-Mei Wu, Qing-Guo Huang

Abstract
We search for isotropic stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) in the International Pulsar Timing Array second data release. By modeling the SGWB as a power-law, we find very strong Bayesian evidence for a common-spectrum process, and further this process has scalar transverse (ST) correlations allowed in general metric theory of gravity as the Bayes factor in favor of the ST-correlated process versus the spatially uncorrelated common-spectrum process is 30 ± 2 30\pm 2 . The median and the 90 % 90\% equal-tail amplitudes of ST mode are 𝒜 ST = 1.29 − 0.44 + 0.51 × 10 − 15 \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ST}}=1.29^{+0.51}_{-0.44}\times 10^{-15} , or equivalently the energy density parameter per logarithm frequency is Ω GW ST = 2.31 − 1.30 + 2.19 × 10 − 9 \Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{ST}}=2.31^{+2.19}_{-1.30}\times 10^{-9} , at frequency of 1/year. However, we do not find any statistically significant evidence for the tensor transverse (TT) mode and then place the 95 % 95\% upper limits as 𝒜 TT < 3.95 × 10 − 15 \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TT}}<3.95\times 10^{-15} , or equivalently Ω GW TT < 2.16 × 10 − 9 \Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{TT}}<2.16\times 10^{-9} , at frequency of 1/year.



I
Introduction

After the direct detection of gravitational waves (GWs) from a binary black hole
(
Abbott et al. 2016
)
and a binary neutron star
(
Abbott et al. 2017
)
mergers by LIGO-Virgo, other types of GW sources are yet to be identified. Of particular interest is the stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) produced by the superposition of a large number of independent GW signals from compact binary coalescences. While the ground-based interferometers are sensitive to GWs from Hz to kHz, a pulsar timing array
(
Sazhin 1978
;
Detweiler 1979
;
Foster & Backer 1990
, PTA;)
, which regularly monitors the time of arrivals (TOAs) of radio pulses from an array of stable millisecond pulsars, offers a unique and powerful probe to correlated signals at low frequencies from nHz to
\mu
Hz. The SGWB sources for PTAs could come from the inspiral of supermassive black hole binaries (SMBHBs)
(
Jaffe & Backer 2003
;
Sesana et al. 2008
;
Sesana et al. 2009
)
, the first-order phase transition
(
Witten 1984
;
Hogan 1986
)
, and the scalar-induced GWs
(
Saito & Yokoyama 2009
;
Yuan et al. 2019a
;
Yuan et al. 2019b
)
, etc. It is expected that the SGWB from SMBHBs will be the first GW signal to be detected with PTAs
(
Rosado et al. 2015
)
.

There are three major PTAs with accumulated pulsar-timing data of more than a decade, namely the European Pulsar Timing Array
(
Kramer & Champion 2013
, EPTA;)
, the North American Nanoherz Observatory for Gravitational Waves
(
McLaughlin 2013
, NANOGrav;)
, and the Parkes Pulsar Timing Array
(
Manchester et al. 2013
, PPTA;)
. These collaborations support the International Pulsar Timing Array
(
Hobbs et al. 2010
;
Manchester 2013
, IPTA;)
. Over the last decade, PTAs have accumulated increasingly sensitive data sets, and the null-detection of GWs with PTAs has successfully constrained various astrophysical scenarios, such as cosmic strings
(
Lentati et al. 2015
;
Arzoumanian et al. 2018
;
Yonemaru et al. 2020
)
, SGWBs from SMBHBs with power-law spectra
(
Lentati et al. 2015
;
Shannon et al. 2015
;
Arzoumanian et al. 2018
)
, and primordial black holes
(
Chen et al. 2020
)
, etc. It is widely expected that the inkling of an SGWB will first manifest as the emergence of a spatially uncorrelated common-spectrum process (UCP) among all pulsars, and culminate in the appearance of the spatial correlations that unambiguously signify the detection of an SGWB.

In a recent analysis, the NANOGrav collaboration found strong evidence for a stochastic common-spectrum process modeled by a power-law spectrum in their 12.5-yr data set
(
Arzoumanian et al. 2020
)
. However, there was no statistically significant evidence for the tensor transverse (TT) spatial correlations which are deemed to be necessary to claim an SGWB detection consistent with general relativity.
Chen et al. 2021
then reanalyzed the NANOGrav 12.5-yr data set and found strong Bayesian evidence that the common-spectrum process reported by NANOGrav collaboration has the scalar transverse (ST) spatial correlations which can originate from a general metric theory of gravity. Later on, the PPTA collaboration analyzed their second data release (DR2) and also found a common-spectrum process but without significant evidence for, or against, the TT spatial correlations
(
Goncharov et al. 2021
)
. Furthermore,
Wu et al. 2021
searched for the non-tensorial polarizations in the PPTA DR2, and found no significant evidence supporting the existence of alternative polarizations, thus constraining the amplitude of each polarization mode. Note that the
95\%
upper limit on the amplitude of the ST mode from
Wu et al. 2021
is consistent with the result from
Chen et al. 2021
.

The IPTA collaboration has published their second data release comprising of 65 pulsars in total with a timespan as long as about three decades
(
Perera et al. 2019
)
. The IPTA DR2 is an invaluable complement to the NANOGrav 12.5-yr data set and PPTA DR2 in the sense of more pulsars included and longer timespan. In this letter, we aim to search for the SGWB signal modeled as a power-law spectrum in the IPTA DR2 with a particular interest in exploring if the ST spatial correlations are present in the data set or not.

II
The Data Set and Methodology

The IPTA DR2
(
Perera et al. 2019
)
was created by combining published data from individual PTA data releases, including EPTA DR1
(
Desvignes et al. 2016
)
, NANOGrav 9-yr data set
(
Arzoumanian et al. 2015
)
, and PPTA DR1
(
Manchester et al. 2013
)
. It consists of 65 pulsars and provides a better sky coverage compared to the IPTA DR1
(
Verbiest et al. 2016
)
. There are two data combination versions in the IPTA DR2, namely VersionA and VersionB. The two versions are different in modeling the dispersion measure (DM) variation and handling the noise properties of pulsars
(
Perera et al. 2019
)
. In particular, the noise parameters of the pulsars in VersionB are re-estimated based on the IPTA data combination, while VersionA uses the previously constrained values from other PTA data sets
(
Perera et al. 2019
)
. It has been shown that the overall time-dependent DM variations modeled by these two methods are largely consistent with each other
(
Perera et al. 2019
)
. In this work, we use the VersionB data release by choosing only pulsars with a timing baseline greater than three years in our analyses and excluding the pulsar J1939+2134 because of its complicated DM variation and timing noise
(
Kaspi et al. 1994
;
Manchester et al. 2013
;
Lentati et al. 2016
)
. Therefore, all results in this work are based on 52 pulsars that meet the requirements.

The GWs will manifest as the unexplained residuals in the pulsar TOAs after subtracting a deterministic timing model that accounts for the pulsar spin behavior and the geometric effects due to the motion of the pulsar and the Earth
(
Sazhin 1978
;
Detweiler 1979
)
. For two pulsars
a
and
b
, the cross-power spectral density of the timing residuals induced by an SGWB at frequency
f
is
(
Lee et al. 2008
;
Chamberlin & Siemens 2012
;
Gair et al. 2015
)

S_{ab}^{P}(f)=\frac{h_{c,P}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}f^{3}}\Gamma^{P}_{ab}(f),

(1)

where
h_{c}^{P}(f)
is the characteristic strain of the polarization mode
P
. In this work, we only search for the TT and ST polarization modes. The TT mode is the usual tensor transverse mode containing the “
+
” and “
\times
” polarizations, and the ST mode is the spin-0 breathing polarization. The overlap functions
\Gamma_{ab}(f)
for the
\mathrm{TT}
and
\mathrm{ST}
polarizations can be approximated by
(
Hellings & Downs 1983
;
Lee et al. 2008
)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta_{ab})+\frac{3}{2}k_{ab}\left(\ln k_{ab}-\frac{1}{6}\right),

(2)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\mathrm{ST}}_{ab}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\left(3+4\delta_{ab}+\cos\xi_{ab}\right),

(3)

where
\delta_{ab}
is the Kronecker delta symbol,
\xi_{ab}
is the angle between pulsars
a
and
b
, and
k_{ab}\equiv(1-\cos\xi_{ab})/2
. The TT overlap function
\Gamma^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
is also known as the Hellings & Downs
(
Hellings & Downs 1983
)
or quadrupolar correlations. Note that the TT and ST overlap functions are related to each other by
\Gamma^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}=\Gamma^{\mathrm{ST}}_{ab}+(3/2)k_{ab}\ln k_{ab}
. Here, we also consider a parameterized overlap function

\Gamma_{ab}(f)=\frac{1}{8}\left(3+4\delta_{ab}+\cos\xi_{ab}\right)+\frac{\alpha}{2}k_{ab}\ln k_{ab},

(4)

which reduces to
\Gamma^{\mathrm{ST}}_{ab}
when
\alpha=0
, and
\Gamma^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
when
\alpha=3
.

We take the characteristic strain
h_{c}^{P}(f)
to be a power-law form, which can originate from the SGWB produced by a population of inspiraling SMBHBs
(
Jaffe & Backer 2003
;
Sesana et al. 2008
;
Sesana et al. 2009
)
. Assuming the binaries are in circular orbits and the orbital decay is dominated by the GW emission, the cross-power spectral density can be approximately estimated by
(
Cornish et al. 2018
)

S_{ab}^{P}(f)=\Gamma^{P}_{ab}\frac{\mathcal{A}_{P}^{2}}{12\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{yr}}}\right)^{-\gamma_{P}}f_{\mathrm{yr}}^{-3},

(5)

where
\mathcal{A}_{P}
is the GW amplitude of the polarization mode
P
, and
f_{\mathrm{yr}}=1/\mathrm{year}
. The power-law index
\gamma_{P}
is
13/3
for the TT polarization, and
5
for the ST polarization. The dimensionless GW energy density parameter per logarithm frequency normalized by the critical energy density for the polarization mode
P
is related to
\mathcal{A}_{P}
by
(
Thrane & Romano 2013
)

\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{P}(f)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}f^{2}h_{c,P}^{2}=\frac{2\pi^{2}f_{\mathrm{yr}}^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}}\mathcal{A}_{P}^{2}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\mathrm{yr}}}\right)^{5-\gamma_{P}},

(6)

where
H_{0}=67.4\,\mathrm{km}\sec^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
is the Hubble constant taken from Planck 2018
(
Aghanim et al. 2020
)
.

We now briefly describe the noise model used in our analyses. After subtracting the timing model from the TOAs, the timing residuals
\delta\bm{t}
of each single pulsar are contributed from a number of sources by
(
Lentati et al. 2016
, see
e.g.
)

\delta\bm{t}=M\bm{\epsilon}+\delta\bm{t}_{\text{SN}}+\delta\bm{t}_{\text{DM}}+\delta\bm{t}_{\text{yrDM}}+\delta\bm{t}_{\text{WN}}+\delta\bm{t}_{\text{CP}}.

(7)

The first term
M\bm{\epsilon}
accounts for the inaccuracies in the subtraction of timing model
(
Chamberlin et al. 2015
, see
e.g.
)
, in which
M
is the timing model design matrix obtained from
TEMPO2

(
Hobbs et al. 2006
;
Edwards et al. 2006
)
through
libstempo
1
1

1

https://vallis.github.io/libstempo
interface, and
\bm{\epsilon}
is a vector denoting small offsets for the parameters of timing model. The second term
\delta\bm{t}_{\text{SN}}
is the stochastic contribution from the red spin noise (SN) intrinsic to each pulsar and is modeled by a power law with
30
frequency components. The third term
\delta\bm{t}_{\text{DM}}
denotes the stochastic contribution from the DM noise which is also modeled by a power law with
30
frequency components. Unlike the SN, the DM noise is dependent upon the radio frequency whose information is added to the Fourier basis components. The fourth term
\delta\bm{t}_{\text{yrDM}}
is the stochastic contribution caused by annual DM variation described by a deterministic yearly sinusoid
(
Lentati et al. 2016
, see
e.g.
)
. The fifth term
\delta\bm{t}_{\text{WN}}
represents the stochastic contribution due to the white noise (WN), including a scale parameter on the TOA uncertainties (EFAC), an added variance (EQUAD), and a per-epoch variance (ECORR) for each backend/receiver system
(
Arzoumanian et al. 2016
, see
e.g.
)
. We include separate EFACs and EQUADs for all the backend/receiver-dependent PTA data sets, and separate ECORRs for the backend/receiver-dependent NANOGrav data sets. The last term
\delta\bm{t}_{\text{CP}}
is the stochastic contribution due to the common-spectrum process (such as an SGWB) with the cross-power spectral density given by Eq. (
5
). In the analyses, we use
10
frequency components roughly starting from
1.09\times 10^{-9}
Hz to
1.09\times 10^{-8}
Hz for the common-spectrum processes. For pulsar J1713+0747, we also include a chromatic exponential dip to model the sudden change in dispersion when the signal passes through the interstellar medium during propagation
(
Lentati et al. 2016
)
.

III
Results and discussion

Our analyses are mainly based on the Bayesian inference in which the Bayes factor
is used to quantify the model selection scores. The Bayes factor is defined as

\mathcal{BF}\equiv\frac{\rm{Pr}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M}_{2})}{\rm{Pr}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M}_{1})},

(8)

where
\rm{Pr}(\mathcal{D}|\mathcal{M})
denotes the probability that the data
\mathcal{D}
are produced under the assumption of model
\mathcal{M}
. Model
\mathcal{M}_{2}
is preferred if the
\mathcal{BF}
is sufficiently large. An interpretation of the
\mathcal{BF}
in model comparison given by
Kass & Raftery 1995
can be found in Table
2
.

3
, we summarize the Bayes factors of various models. The
\ln\mathcal{BF}
of the UCP model with
\gamma_{\mathrm{UCP}}=13/3
versus the noise only (NO) model without any common-spectrum process is
10.5
, indicating significant Bayesian evidence for a common-spectrum process in the IPTA DR2. When allowing the power-index to vary, the
\gamma_{\mathrm{UCP}}
shows a relatively broad distribution as can be seen in Fig.
1

90\%
equal-tailed credible intervals are
\log_{10}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{UCP}}=-14.26^{+0.41}_{-0.47}
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{UCP}}=3.76^{+0.96}_{-0.95}
.

Furthermore, we consider an ST+TT model where both the ST and TT spatial correlations are taken into account simultaneously. The
\ln\mathcal{BF}
of the ST+TT model versus the UCP (with
\gamma_{\mathrm{UCP}}=13/3
) model is
3.63
, indicating no significant evidence for a second common-spectrum process with TT correlations on top of the ST-correlated process. The posterior distributions of the ST and TT amplitudes obtained from the ST+TT model are shown in Fig.
3
, with the amplitude of ST mode from this model is
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ST}}=1.11^{+0.63}_{-1.10}\times 10^{-15}
or equivalently
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{ST}}=1.71^{+2.48}_{-1.70}\times 10^{-9}
, at frequency of 1/year. Note that this result is consistent with the one obtained from the ST model and that reported in
Chen et al. 2021
where
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{ST}}=1.54^{+1.20}_{-0.71}\times 10^{-9}
from the NANOGrav 12.5-yr data set.

To evaluate the potential deviation of the ST correlations in IPTA DR2, we also consider a model in which the overlap function is parameterized by the
\alpha
parameter as Eq. (
4
). Specifically,
\alpha=0
corresponds to the ST correlations, while
\alpha=3
corresponds to the TT correlations. The marginalized posteriors for the
\alpha
parameter are shown in Fig.
4
, indicating the IPTA DR2 can be very well described by the ST correlations, but TT correlations are excluded by the
90\%
credible regions.

Based on the above discussions, we conclude that there is strong Bayesian evidence for the ST correlations, but no significant evidence for the TT correlations in the IPTA DR2. Therefore, we place
95\%
upper limits on the amplitude of TT polarization mode as
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TT}}\lesssim 3.95\times 10^{-15}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TT}}\lesssim 3.27\times 10^{-15}
, or equivalently, the
95\%
upper limits for the energy density parameter per logarithm frequency are
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{TT}}\lesssim 2.16\times 10^{-8}
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{GW}}^{\mathrm{TT}}\lesssim 1.48\times 10^{-8}
, from the TT and ST+TT models, respectively.
