The structure of END( 𝓣 𝒏 \mathcal{T}_{n} )
Victoria Gould, Ambroise Grau, Marianne Johnson

Abstract
. The full transformation semigroups 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} , where n ∈ ℕ n\in\mathbb{N} , consisting of all maps from a set of cardinality n n to itself, are arguably the most important family of finite semigroups. This article investigates the endomorphism monoid End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) of 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} . The determination of the elements of End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) is due Schein and Teclezghi. Surprisingly, the algebraic structure of End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) has not been further explored. We describe Green’s relations and extended Green’s relations on End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) , and the generalised regularity properties of these monoids. In particular, we prove that ℋ = ℒ ⊆ ℛ = 𝒟 = 𝒥 \mathop{\mathscr{H}}=\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathscr{R}}=\mathop{\mathscr{D}}=\mathop{\mathscr{J}} (with equality if and only if n = 1 n=1 ); the idempotents of End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) form a band (which is equal to End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) if and only if n = 1 n=1 ) and also the regular elements of End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) form a subsemigroup (which is equal to End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) if and only if n ≤ 2 n\leq 2 ). Further, the regular elements of End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) are precisely the idempotents together with all endomorphisms of rank greater than 3 3 . We also provide a presentation for End ⁡ ( 𝒯 n ) \mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}) with respect to a minimal generating set.

1.
Introduction

The full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{n}
on a finite set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
is an important object in algebra. It is therefore natural to study its endomorphism monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. The elements of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
were described by Schein and Teclezghi
[
21
]
, and we use this description as the starting point for our investigation of the algebraic structure and properties of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Our initial task is to partition
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
in several ways, depending on the
rank
of an element (the cardinality of its image) and
type
of an element (a quality of the image that determines the behaviour of
the element in products). This partitioning is then made use of throughout the text.

One of the first questions to ask about a monoid
S
is whether it is
regular
, that is, for any
a\in S
there is a
b\in S
such that

a=aba
. Regularity is intimately connected with the position and nature of the
idempotents
in the monoid, where

e\in S
is
idempotent
if
e=e^{2}
. We show that for
n\geq 3
the monoid
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is not regular. On the other hand the set of idempotents
of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is very special in that it is a
band
, that is, a semigroup of idempotents. Moreover it is a special kind of band, namely a

left regular band
.

We next investigate minimal generating sets of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, and provide a presentation for
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
in terms of these generators.

A major tool used in understanding monoids is that of
Green’s relations

\mathop{\mathscr{R}},\mathop{\mathscr{L}},\mathop{\mathscr{H}},\mathop{\mathscr{D}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
, which measure mutual divisibility properties between elements by considering the principal (one-sided) ideals that they generate. Specifically, for elements
a,b
of a monoid
S
we have

a\mathop{\mathscr{R}}b
if and only if
aS=bS
; this is equivalent to
a=bs
and
b=at
for some
s,t\in S
. The relation
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
is defined dually, and

\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
is defined by
a\mathop{\mathscr{J}}b
if and only if
SaS=SbS
. Finally,

\mathop{\mathscr{H}}=\mathop{\mathscr{R}}\wedge\mathop{\mathscr{L}}=\mathop{\mathscr{R}}\cap\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{D}}=\mathop{\mathscr{R}}\vee\mathop{\mathscr{L}}=\mathop{\mathscr{R}}\circ\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
with
\mathop{\mathscr{D}}=\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
for a finite semigroup; the latter two assertions are theorems - see, for example,
[
11
]
. We characterise Green’s relations on
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. In particular, we show that
\mathop{\mathscr{H}}=\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathscr{R}}=\mathop{\mathscr{D}}=\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
, and for
n\neq 4
the relation
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
is trivial outside of the group of units. We then use the description of
\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
to fully determine the ideals of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
.

Green’s relations provide an alternative characterisation of regularity, specifically, a monoid is regular if every
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
-class (or, equivalently, every
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
-class) contains an idempotent.
We have commented that
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is not regular, however, we show that it satisfies the weaker property of being left abundant, where a monoid is
left abundant
if every class of the extended Green’s relation
\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}}
contains an idempotent.
This follows from the fact that the relation
\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}}
on
\mathcal{E}_{n}
precisely captures the relation of having the same rank. We postpone giving the definitions of the extended Green’s relations
\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{H}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{D}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{J}^{*}}
until later in the text, but suffice to say here they are relations of mutual cancellativity. They first arose in the work of Pastijn
[
19
]

and McAlister
[
17
]
. As observed in
[
6
]
, it follows from
[
12
]
that a monoid is left abundant precisely when every monogenic right act is projective. We determine the
*
-relations

\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{H}^{*}},\mathop{\mathscr{D}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{J}^{*}}
on
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, from which we observe that

\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
does not satisfy the dual property of being right abundant. To complete the picture of the extended Green’s relations we consider the
\sim
-relations

\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}},\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}},\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{H}}},\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}}
which extend the corresponding
*
-relations. They are formulated using idempotent left identities: again, we postpone their definition until later. The
\sim
-relations
first appear in
[
5
]
and have subsequently proved to be useful in a number of ways. For example, they are inherent in the characterisation of left restriction semigroups
[
10
]
and in determining varieties containing quasi-varieties of
abundant semigroups.

The main focus of our work will be on the general behaviour of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
which emerges for
n\geq 5
. The cases
n\leq 4
exhibit several degenerate or exceptional behaviours: for instance, it is immediate that
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{1})=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{1})
is the trivial group, and
we will see that
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{4})
is unique in that it contains endomorphisms of rank
7
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we remind the reader of the description of the elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
from
[
21
]
.
We prove several fundamental results concerning the non-automorphisms in
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
and give the partitions promised above.
Sections
3
–
6
consider the structure of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
for
n\geq 5
: in Section
3
we describe some properties of the idempotents and determine the regular elements; in Section
4
we provide a minimal generating set and describe a presentation
for
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
in terms of these generators; in Section
5
we give a description of Green’s relations and use this to determine the ideal structure of

\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
; we consider the extended Green’s relations on
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
in Section
6
. To complete the picture, in Section
7
, we analyse the structure of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
for
n\leq 4
. Finally, in Section
8
, we indicate how the ideas in this article could be developed and extended.

Notation and conventions:
Throughout the paper we write
\mathcal{S}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}
to denote the symmetric group and
\mathcal{A}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
the alternating group, that is, the subgroup of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of all even permutations. By a slight abuse of notation, we suppress the dependence on
n
and write simply
\mathrm{id}
to denote the identity element of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. For an element
g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and
s\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we denote by
s^{g}
the product
g^{-1}sg
. For
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
we also write
(i\,j)
for the transposition swapping
i
and
j
in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, and
c_{i}
for the constant map with image
i
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. For
n\neq 4
, the alternating group is the only non-trivial proper normal subgroup of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, whilst for
n=4
there is one additional normal subgroup
\mathcal{K}=\{\mathrm{id},(1\,2)(3\,4),(1\,3)(2\,4),(1\,4)(2\,3)\}
. A straightforward calculation reveals that for any
t\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
there is a unique element of
\mathcal{K}t
which fixes
4
.
To facilitate readability, we take the convention that elements of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
will be written using Roman letters, while endomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
will be written using Greek letters. We will also use the short-hand notation
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
and
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
for the endomorphism monoid and automorphism group of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. The identity element of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
(and hence also
\mathcal{G}_{n}
) is the trivial automorphism, which will be denoted by
\varepsilon
(again, suppressing the dependence on
n
where convenient).

We attempt to keep the exposition as self-contained as possible, but refer the reader to
[
11
]
for further details of semigroup notions we employ.

2.
Singular endomorphisms

In this section we record several useful properties of the endomorphisms in
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, which we refer to as
singular endomorphisms
. We begin by recalling the characterisation of the semigroup endomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
(that is, endomorphisms preserving the binary operation, but not necessarily the identity) due to Schein and Teclezghi
[
21
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
21
]
)
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and define maps
\psi_{g}
and
\phi_{t,e}
for any
s\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
by:

s\psi_{g}=s^{g}\quad\text{and}\quad s\phi_{t,e}=\begin{cases}t&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n},\\
t^{2}&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\\
e&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}.\end{cases}

Then the automorphisms and endomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are described as follows.

(1)

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\psi_{g}:g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\}
.

(2)

For
n\neq 4
,

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathcal{G}_{n}\cup\{\phi_{t,e}:t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\,t^{3}=t,\;te=et=e^{2}=e\}.

(3)

For
n=4

\mathcal{E}_{4}=\mathcal{G}_{4}\cup\{\phi_{t,e}:t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{4},\,t^{3}=t,\;te=et=e^{2}=e\}\cup\{\sigma^{g}:g\in\mathcal{S}_{4}\},

where
s\sigma=c_{4}
if
s\in\mathcal{T}_{4}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{4}
and
s\sigma=p_{s}
if
s\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
, where
p_{s}
denotes the unique element in the coset of
\mathcal{K}s

which fixes
4
, and for all
s\in\mathcal{T}_{4}
and all
g\in\mathcal{S}_{4}

s\sigma^{g}=(s\sigma)^{g}
.

We remark that in the case
n=4
in Theorem
2.1
there is no bias towards 4; any apparent bias is dealt with by the process of conjugation. Note also that
\sigma=\sigma^{\mathrm{id}}
and

\psi_{\mathrm{id}}=\varepsilon
.

It is easy to see that for any
g,h\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
we have that
\psi_{g}\psi_{h}=\psi_{gh}
. Further if
\psi_{g}=\psi_{h}
then an easy computation gives that for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we have that

c_{ig}=c_{i}\psi_{g}=c_{i}\psi_{h}=c_{ih}
, so that
ig=ih
and, since
i
was arbitrary,
g=h
. It follows that
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is isomorphic to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
we write
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
to denote the image of
\alpha
, and define the
rank
of
\alpha
to be the cardinality of
\mathrm{im\,}\alpha
.
Clearly the image of each automorphism
\psi_{g}
is the whole of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and hence has rank
\lvert\mathcal{T}_{n}\rvert=n^{n}
. The remaining endomorphisms, that is, the singular endomorphisms, have rank strictly less than
n^{n}
. From the above theorem the rank of each singular endomorphism is either
1,2,3,
or
7
.
For, the image of each endomorphism of the form
\phi_{t,e}
is the set
\left\{t,t^{2},e\right\}
, which can have up to three distinct elements. In the case where
n=4
an easy computation gives that

\mathrm{im\,}\sigma^{g}=\{t^{g}:t\in\mathcal{S}_{4},4t=4\}\cup\{c_{4g}\},
has precisely
7
elements.

2.1.
Endomorphisms of rank at most 3

In order to encapsulate the conditions of the elements
t
and
e
so that
\phi_{t,e}
is an endomorphism, we let

U_{n}=\left\{t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\mid t^{3}=t\text{ with }te=et=e^{2}=e\text{ for some }e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\right\}.

We say that
(t,e)
form a
permissible pair
, if
\phi_{t,e}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
, and we denote by
P_{n}
the set of all permissible pairs, that is,

P_{n}=\left\{(t,e)\mid t\in U_{n},\,te=et=e^{2}=e\right\}.

Before considering the endomorphisms in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
further, we give some important properties of the sets
U_{n}
and
P_{n}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The set
U_{n}
consists of all elements
t=t^{3}
satisfying
kt=k
for at least one
1\leq k\leq n
. Moreover, for
t\in U_{n}
the number of permissible pairs with first component equal to
t
is

\sum_{r=1}^{|J|}\binom{|J|}{r}r^{|I|+|J|-r}

where
J=\{k:kt=k\}
and
I
is maximal such that
t
restricts to a fixed point free permutation on
I\cup It
.

Proof.

Let
t\in U_{n}
. By definition we have
t^{3}=t
. Note that if there exists
e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
such that
et=e
, then then for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
we must have
ket=ke
, giving that all elements in the image of
e
are fixed by
t
.
Conversely, suppose that
t^{3}=t
and
kt=k
. Then it is easy to see that
c_{k}t=tc_{k}=c_{k}=c_{k}^{2}
, and hence
t\in U_{n}
.

Note that if
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies
t^{3}=t
then for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
there exist
i_{k},j_{k}
such that
kt=j_{k}
,
kt^{2}=j_{k}t=i_{k}
and
kt^{3}=i_{k}t=j_{k}t^{2}=kt
.
If
j_{k}=k
then also
i_{k}=k
. It follows that
J=\{k:i_{k}=j_{k}=k\}
,
K=\{k:k\neq j_{k}=i_{k}\}
,
L=\{k:k=i_{k}\neq j_{k}\}
and
M=\{k:k\neq j_{k}\neq i_{k}\neq k\}
, partition the domain

\{1,\cdots,n\}
of
t
. It is clear from these definitions that
t
restricts to the identity on
J
,
Kt\subseteq J
and
Mt\subseteq L
. Moreover,
L
is maximal such that
t
restricts to a fixed-point free permutation of order
2
on
L
; let us partition
L=I\cup It
.

satisfying
t^{3}=t
, we may partition the domain of
t
as:

Noting that
l\in L
if and only if
lt\in L
, it is clear that
L
may be further partitioned into two sets of equal
size,
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{s}\}
and
It=\{i_{1}t,\ldots,i_{s}t\}
. Thus we may write each
t
such that
t^{3}=t
in the form:

\displaystyle t=\begin{pmatrix}\cdots j\cdots&\cdots k\cdots&\cdots i_{r}\cdots&\cdots i_{r}t\cdots&\cdots m\cdots\\
\cdots j\cdots&\cdots kt\cdots&\cdots i_{r}t\cdots&\cdots i_{r}\cdots&\cdots mt\cdots\end{pmatrix}\!,\mbox{ with }kt\in J\mbox{ and }mt\in I\cup It,

where here
j
,
k
and
m
denote arbitrary elements of the sets
J
,
K
and
M
respectively. We claim that the elements
e
satisfying
e=e^{2}=te=et
are precisely those of the form:

\displaystyle e=\begin{pmatrix}\cdots j\cdots&\cdots k\cdots&\cdots i_{r}\cdots&\cdots i_{r}t\cdots&\cdots m\cdots\\
\cdots jf\cdots&\cdots ktf\cdots&\cdots i_{r}f\cdots&\cdots i_{r}f\cdots&\cdots mtf^{\prime}\cdots\end{pmatrix}\!,

where
f
is any function
f:J\cup I\rightarrow R
fixing a non-empty subset
R\subseteq J
pointwise, and
f^{\prime}:I\cup It\rightarrow R
is defined by
i_{r}tf^{\prime}=i_{r}f^{\prime}=i_{r}f
.
(For example, given the transformation

t=t^{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&3&2&1&2\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,
this description yields that

e=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&1&1&1&1\\
\end{pmatrix}
is the unique idempotent satisfying
te=et=e
. Indeed, if
t
fixes exactly one element then we have no choice but to take
R=J
, and since this is a singleton set this leaves no choice for the function
f
.)

Now to prove the claim.
If
t\in U_{n}
, then by the first paragraph of the proof we may assume that
|J|>0
. It is then straightforward to describe the elements
e
which satisfy
et=te=e=e^{2}
. The condition that
e
is idempotent implies that
e
fixes each element of its image. Further, if
R
is a subset of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then there is a bijection between
functions
f:\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus R\rightarrow R
and idempotents with image equal to
R
. Note that (as observed above) the condition
et=e
implies that the image of
e
is contained in
J
. Let
R
be a non-empty subset of
J
. We claim that every function
f:(J\setminus R)\cup I\rightarrow R
extends uniquely to the whole of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
to give an idempotent
e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with image
R
satisfying the constraints
et=te=e
.

We have observed that every idempotent must fix its image. Thus, if
e
is to be an idempotent with image
R
extending
f
, we have no choice on how
e
must act on
J\cup I
. Now the condition
te=e
forces
ke=kte
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}=J\cup K\cup I\cup It\cup M
. If
k\in K
then
kt\in J
and
ke=kte=(kt)f
. If
k\in It
, then
kt\in I
and the condition
e=te
forces
ke=kte=(kt)f
.
If
k\in M
then
kt\in I\cup It
and hence either
kt\in I
and
ke=kte=(kt)f
, or

kt\in It
. In the latter case, certainly
kt^{2}\in I
, so that
kt=kt^{3}=i_{k}t
for
i_{k}=kt^{2}\in I
, and then
ke=kte=i_{k}f
.
Since the sets
J\cup K\cup I\cup It\cup M
partition the domain of
t
, this shows that there is exactly one way to extend
f
to an idempotent
e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with image
R
satisfying
et=te=e
.

For a fixed
t
, the number of idempotents such that
(t,e)\in P_{n}
is therefore found by summing the total number of functions
f:(J\setminus R)\cup I\rightarrow R
as
R
ranges over non-empty subsets of
J
. That is, for
t\in U_{n}
with partition as given above we have that the number of idempotents
e
satisfying
(t,e)\in P_{n}
is

\sum_{r=1}^{|J|}\binom{|J|}{r}r^{|I|+|J|-r}
.
∎

We now gather together some routine but useful facts concerning the set
P_{n}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

(1)

If
t
is idempotent, then
t\in U_{n}
and
(t,t)\in P_{n}
.

(2)

If
(t,e)\in P_{n}
with
t^{2}=e
, then
t=e
.

(3)

We have
t\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\cap U_{n}
if and only if
t^{2}=\mathrm{id}
. Consequently,
t\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\cap U_{n}
is a product of an odd number [resp. even number] no more than
(n-1)/2
of disjoint transpositions if
t\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n}
[resp. if
t\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
].

(4)

We have
\left(t^{g}\right)^{k}=t^{g}
if and only if
t^{k}=t
. Additionally,
t^{g}=\mathrm{id}
if and only if
t=\mathrm{id}
.

(5)

If
(t,e)\in P_{n}
, then
(t^{g},e^{g})\in P_{n}
.

(6)

If
(t,e)\in P_{n}
and
e=\mathrm{id}
, then
t=\mathrm{id}
.

(7)

If
(t,e)\in P_{n}
, then
t^{2}
is idempotent and
(t^{2},e)\in P_{n}
.

(8)

If
(t,e)\in P_{4}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
, then
(t\sigma^{g},e\sigma^{g})\in P_{4}
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
be an idempotent. Then clearly
t^{3}=t=t^{2}
, and hence
(t,t)\in P_{n}
.

(2)

Let
(t,e)\in P_{n}
be such that
t^{2}=e
. Then we immediately obtain that
t=t^{3}=tt^{2}=te=e
.

(3)

If
t\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, from
t^{3}=t
we get that
t^{2}=tt^{-1}=\mathrm{id}
, while
t\notin\mathcal{S}_{n}
directly implies that
t^{2}\neq\mathrm{id}
. The second part follows from the fact that a permutation of order
2
is a product of disjoint transpositions, and that
t\in U_{n}
must fix at least one element of
\left\{1,\dots,n\right\}
by Lemma
2.2
.

(4)

If
t^{k}=t
, then we have that
(t^{g})^{k}=(g^{-1}tg)^{k}=g^{-1}t^{k}g=g^{-1}tg=t^{g}
.
Conversely, if
(t^{g})^{k}=t^{g}
, then we have
g^{-1}t^{k}g=g^{-1}tg
which immediately gives us that
t^{k}=t
.
Finally,
g^{-1}tg=t^{g}=\mathrm{id}
if and only if
t=g\,\mathrm{id}\,g^{-1}=\mathrm{id}
as required.

(5)

Let
(t,e)\in P_{n}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Since
t^{3}=t
, by the previous argument, we get that
(t^{g})^{3}=t^{g}
.
Also,
t^{g}e^{g}=g^{-1}teg=g^{-1}eg=e^{g}
and similarly
e^{g}t^{g}=e^{g}=(e^{g})^{2}
, which shows that
(t^{g},e^{g})\in P_{n}
.

(6)

Let
(t,e)\in P_{n}
so that
te=e
. Then, if
e=\mathrm{id}
we get that
t=t\,\mathrm{id}=\mathrm{id}
, as required.

(7)

Let
(t,e)\in P_{n}
. Then from
t^{3}=t
we directly obtain that
(t^{2})^{2}=t^{3}t=t^{2}
and thus
t^{2}
is an idempotent and lies in
U_{n}
by point
(1)
.
Since
te=et=e
, we have that
t^{2}e=t(te)=te=e
, and similarly
et^{2}=e
.
Hence
t^{2}e=et^{2}=e=e^{2}
, which shows that
(t^{2},e)\in P_{n}
.

(8)

Let
(t,e)\in P_{4}
and
g\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
. We first show that
(t\sigma,e\sigma)\in P_{4}
and then apply (5) (recalling the definition of
\sigma^{g}
) to obtain the full result. Notice that if
t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}
, then
t\sigma=e\sigma=c_{4}
, while if
t=e=\mathrm{id}
, then
t\sigma=e\sigma=\mathrm{id}
, which by part (1) shows in both cases that
(t\sigma,e\sigma)\in P_{4}
. Otherwise,
t\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
and
e\neq\mathrm{id}
, and then
t\sigma=p_{t}
and
e\sigma=c_{4}
. Since
\sigma
is an endomorphism, we get that

(t\sigma)^{3}=t^{3}\sigma=t\sigma=p_{t}=t\sigma
. Moreover, as
p_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
fixes
4
by definition, it follows that
p_{t}c_{4}=c_{4}p_{t}=c_{4}=c_{4}^{2}
and we thus have
(t\sigma,e\sigma)=(p_{t},c_{4})\in P_{4}
.∎

We now give some characteristics of the endomorphisms of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
with rank at most three that will play an important part in the discussions to come.
Elements of the proofs of the following two results given explicitly can be found scattered throughout the proof of Theorem
2.1
in
[
21
]
.
They are only included here for convenience, and to prepare the reader for similar arguments to come.

We refer in Corollary
2.4
to a semilattice ordering on idempotents, which we now explain. For any semigroup
S
we order the idempotents
E=E(S)
of
S
by

e\leq f
if
ef=fe=e
. This is easily seen to be a partial order. If
Y
is a commutative subsemigroup of
E
, then we refer to
Y
as a

semilattice
. The reason for employing this terminology being that the order
\leq
restricted to
Y
is a

semilattice ordering
in the sense that for any
e,f\in Y
the product
ef
is the meet of
e
and
f
under the partial order.

Corollary 2.4
.

(1)

An endomorphism
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
has rank
1
if and only if
\alpha=\phi_{e,e}
for some
e^{2}=e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the endomorphisms of rank
1
and the one-element subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

(2)

An endomorphism
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
has rank
2
if and only if
\alpha=\phi_{t,e}
for some
(t,e)\in P_{n}
with
t^{2}=t\neq e
. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the endomorphisms of rank
2
and the two-element semilattices
\left\{t,e\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
e<t
.

(3)

An endomorphism
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
has rank
3
if and only if
\alpha=\phi_{t,e}
for some
(t,e)\in P_{n}
with
t\neq t^{2}\neq e
. There is a one-to one correspondence between the endomorphisms of rank
3
and the three-element subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
consisting of a two-element subgroup
\left\{t,t^{2}\right\}
having identity element
t^{2}
, together with an adjoined zero
e
.

(4)

The map
\phi_{t,\mathrm{id}}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
if and only if
t=\mathrm{id}
.

(5)

If
\phi_{u,f}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
for some
u,f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, then
\phi_{u^{2},f}
,
\phi_{f,f}
and
\phi_{{u}^{2},{u}^{2}}
are also in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
be an element of rank at most three. Thus
\alpha=\phi_{t,e}
for some
(t,e)\in P_{n}
and it follows from the definition of
\phi_{t,e}
that
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{t,e}=\left\{t,t^{2},e\right\}
.

For part (1) it is clear that
\alpha
has rank
1
if and only if
t=t^{2}=e
. In this case, since
e
is idempotent it is clear that the image of
\phi_{e,e}
is the trivial semigroup
\{e\}
. Conversely, for each idempotent
e

there is a unique endomorphism
\phi_{e,e}
of rank
1
with image
\{e\}
.

For part (2), we note that it follows from Lemma
2.3
part (2) that
\phi_{t,e}
having rank
2
is equivalent to the condition that
t=t^{2}\neq e
(we have seen in Lemma
2.3
part (2) above that it is not possible to simultaneously have
t\neq t^{2}
and
t^{2}=e
, and since
e
is idempotent it is also not possible to simultaneously have
t=e
and
t^{2}\neq t
). In this case, the image of
\phi_{t,e}
is
\{e,t\}
and the relations
e=e^{2}=te=et
and
t=t^{2}
yield that this is a two element semilattice with
e<t
. Conversely, for each pair of distinct comparable idempotents
t,e
with
t>e
there is a unique endomorphism
\phi_{t,e}
of rank
2
with image
\{t,e\}
. For part (3), it is clear that
\phi_{t,e}
has rank
3
if and only if
t\neq t^{2}\neq e
. In this case
\phi_{t,e}
has image
\{e,t,t^{2}\}
and using the fact that
t^{2}
is idempotent and
(t^{2},e)\in P_{n}
by Lemma
2.3
part
(7)
, we obtain that
\left\{t,t^{2},e\right\}
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, where the idempotent
t^{2}
acts identically on the left and right of
t
, and the idempotent
e
acts as a left and right zero on all three elements. Conversely, for each two-element subgroup
\{t,t^{2}\}
where
t^{2}
is idempotent, and each idempotent
e
such that

\{t,t^{2},e\}
is a group with a zero
e
adjoined, we have a unique endomorphism
\phi_{t,e}
of rank
3
with image
\{t,t^{2},e\}
. This proves parts (1)–(3). To see that (4) holds note that for the map
\phi_{t,\mathrm{id}}
to be in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we require
(t,\mathrm{id})\in P_{n}
, which forces
t=\mathrm{id}
by
(6)
of Lemma
2.3
, and then
\phi_{t,\mathrm{id}}=\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
.
Finally, for part (5), we know that
\phi_{u,f}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
if and only if
(u,f)\in P_{n}
. But then using parts
(7)
and
(1)
of Lemma
2.3
we have that
u^{2}
and

are idempotents, and we therefore have that
u^{2},f\in U_{n}
as well as
(u^{2},f)\in P_{n}
.
Hence
\phi_{u^{2},f}
,
\phi_{f,f}
and
\phi_{{u}^{2},{u}^{2}}
satisfy all conditions to be endomorphisms.
∎

The next result is surprising in that singular elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
of rank no greater than 3 (so, all singular elements in the case
n\geq 5
) are entirely determined by their images. This has significant consequences later when we consider Green’s relations.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\phi_{t,e},\phi_{u,f}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
Then
\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{u,f}
if and only if
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{t,e}=\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{u,f}
if and only if
t=u
and
e=f
.
Further, if
n=4
and
g,h\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
then
\sigma^{g}=\sigma^{h}
if and only if
g=h
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{t,e}=\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{u,f}
and consider the description of the images corresponding to the possible ranks of these maps as given in Corollary
2.4
.
Clearly if
\phi_{t,e}
and
\phi_{u,f}
have rank
1
, then
t=e=u=f
.
Suppose that they have rank
2
so that
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{t,e}=\left\{t,e\right\}
and
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{u,f}=\left\{u,f\right\}
where
t
,
e
,
u
and
f
are all idempotents.
Then, since their images are two element semilattices with
e<t
and
f<u
, we get that
e=f
and
t=u
.
Finally, if
\phi_{t,e}
and
\phi_{u,f}
have rank
3
, then
\left\{t,t^{2},e\right\}=\left\{u,u^{2},f\right\}
where
t
and
u
are the only non idempotent elements, and
e
and
f
are the zeros, respectively, which together forces
t=u
and
e=f
.
In all cases, we have shown that if
\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{t,e}=\mathrm{im\,}\phi_{u,f}
then
t=u
and
e=f
, and therefore
\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{u,f}
. This finishes the argument for the first statement.

If
\sigma^{g}=\sigma^{h}
in
\mathcal{E}_{4}
, then by consideration of the images of these maps we find
4g=4h
and
t^{g}=t^{h}
for all
t\in\mathcal{S}_{4}
that fix
4
. The action on the transpositions

(1\,2)
and
(1\,3)
forces

. The converse direction is clear.
∎

We also describe below the explicit multiplication of elements in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
for

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
g,h\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
(t,e),(u,f)\in P_{n}
. Then we have the following compositions in
\mathcal{E}_{n}

(1)

\psi_{g}\psi_{h}=\psi_{gh}
;

(2)

\psi_{g}\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{t,e}

(3)

\phi_{t,e}\psi_{g}=\phi_{t^{g},e^{g}}

(4)

\phi_{t,e}\phi_{u,f}=\begin{cases}\phi_{u,f}&\mbox{if $t\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n}$ and }e\neq\mathrm{id},\\
\phi_{u^{2},f}&\mbox{if $t\in\mathcal{A}_{n}$ and }e\neq\mathrm{id},\\
\phi_{f,f}&\mbox{if $t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}$ and }e\neq\mathrm{id},\\
\phi_{{u}^{2},{u}^{2}}&\mbox{if }t=e=\mathrm{id}.\end{cases}

Proof.

All of the products are straightforward computations using the definition in Theorem
2.1
; we only detail that for (4).
It is nonetheless worth noting that the map on the right-hand side of product (3) is well-defined and indeed belongs to
\mathcal{E}_{n}
by point
(5)
of Lemma
2.3
.

So consider
(t,e),(u,f)\in P_{n}
so that

.
Then:

s\phi_{t,e}\phi_{u,f}=\begin{cases}t\phi_{u,f}&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{n},\\
t^{2}\phi_{u,f}&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{A}_{n},\\
e\phi_{u,f}&\mbox{if }s\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}.\end{cases}

Recall from Lemma
2.3
part
(6)
that if
e=\mathrm{id}
then we must also have

. Clearly, if

, then we have that

so that

in this case. Thus in all remaining cases we may assume that

and hence

.
If
t\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}

t^{2},e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{n}
so that
t\phi_{u,f}=t^{2}\phi_{u,f}=e\phi_{u,f}=f
.
Therefore we get that

In the case where

Lemma 2.9

3.
Idempotents and regularity

Throughout this section we assume that

\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\phi_{t,e}:(t,e)\in P_{n}\}=\left\{\phi_{t,e}\mid t,e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\mbox{ with }t^{3}=t\text{ and }te=et=e^{2}=e\right\}.

3.1.
The left action of the endomorphism monoid on the singular part

For

Definition 3.1
.

Let

. Then we define
\alpha^{+}
,
\alpha^{-}
and

as:

•

\alpha^{+}=\gamma\alpha
for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
of even type;

•

\alpha^{-}=\gamma\alpha
for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}

•

\alpha^{0}=\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\alpha
(that is,
\gamma\alpha
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
of trivial type).

Additionally, for

and

to be the set
\left\{\alpha^{\dagger}\colon\alpha\in X\right\}

Remark 3.2
.

Under this definition, we can now see that if

for any

Additionally, if we write

\gamma\alpha=\begin{cases}\alpha=\phi_{t,e}&\mbox{if $\gamma$ has group type or odd type,}\\
\alpha^{+}=\phi_{t^{2},e}&\mbox{if $\gamma$ has even type,}\\
\alpha^{-}=\phi_{e,e}&\mbox{if $\gamma$ has non-permutation type,}\\
\alpha^{0}=\phi_{{t}^{2},{t}^{2}}&\mbox{if $\gamma$ has trivial type}.\end{cases}

We now show how each of the sets involved in the decomposition of

behave under the operations mapping
\alpha
to
\alpha^{+}
,
\alpha^{-}
or
\alpha^{0}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}

(1)

1={\rm rank}(\alpha^{0})={\rm rank}(\alpha^{-})\leq{\rm rank}(\alpha^{+})\leq{\rm rank}(\alpha)\leq 3

(2)

\alpha^{+}=\alpha
if and only if

if and only if

(3)

\alpha^{-}=\alpha
if and only if
\alpha^{0}=\alpha
if and only if
{\rm rank}(\alpha)=1
if and only if

;

(4)

E_{3}^{+}\cup A^{+}\subseteq E_{2}=E_{2}^{+}
,

,  and

(5)

(\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n})^{-}=(\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n})^{0}=E_{1}

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\phi_{t,e}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
for some

.
Throughout this proof, we use the description of
\alpha^{+}
,
\alpha^{-}
and

Part (1) follows immediately from Remark
3.2

3.2.
Idempotents and regular elements

The set of idempotents of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, which we denote by
E=E(\mathcal{E}_{n})
, has very nice properties. In particular,
E
is a band. Before stating Proposition
3.4
, we give a little semigroup terminology and background.
If we have a semilattice
Y
, then a semigroup
S
decomposed into disjoint subsemigroups
S_{i}
,
i\in Y
, is a
semilattice
Y
of the subsemigroups
S_{i}
if
S_{i}S_{j}\subseteq S_{i\wedge j}
for all
i,j\in I
.
It is a fact (see, for example,
[
11
, Theorem 4.4.1]
) that any band is a semilattice
Y
of rectangular bands

D_{i}
,
i\in I
, where a band is
rectangular
if it satisfies the identity
x=xyx
. A
right zero (resp. left identity)
for a semigroup
S
is an element

e
such that
ae=e
(resp.
ea=a
) for all
a\in S
. A semigroup consisting entirely of right zeroes (equivalently, entirely of left identities) is called a
right-zero semigroup
; clearly any such semigroup is a band, in fact, a special kind of rectangular band. In what follows, we use the term
chain
for a linearly ordered set
(which is, of course, a semilattice).

Proposition 3.4
.

(1)

Each element of
E_{3}
is a left identity of
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

(2)

Each element of
E_{1}
is a right zero of
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

(3)

The minimal ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is
E_{1}
.

(4)

The set of all singular idempotents
E_{3}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{1}
forms a left ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

(5)

The set of all idempotents
E=\{\varepsilon\}\cup E_{3}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{1}
is a band, and forms a chain of right-zero semigroups.

Proof.

Throughout, we bear in mind that
\varepsilon
is the identity of the monoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

The fact that elements of
E_{3}
are left identities for
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
is immediate from
Corollary
2.6
, as is the fact that elements of
E_{1}
are right zeroes for
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Consideration of ranks immediately gives that
E_{1}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Since
E_{1}
is a right-zero semigroup it has no proper ideals, hence is the minimal ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. This verifies
(1)–(3).

For (4), let
\beta=\phi_{t,e}\in E_{3}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{1}
. Using Remark
3.2
and Corollary
2.6
, we know that for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}

we have that

\alpha\beta\in\{\beta,\beta^{+},\beta^{-},\beta^{0}\}=\{\phi_{t,e},\phi_{t^{2},e},\phi_{e,e},\phi_{t^{2},t^{2}}\}.

If
\beta\in E_{3}\cup E_{2}
then
t^{2}=\mathrm{id}
; if
\beta\in E_{1}
then
t=t^{2}=e
. It follows
that
\alpha\beta\in E\setminus\left\{\varepsilon\right\}
and hence
E_{3}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{1}
is a left ideal. Moreover, for
\alpha,\beta\in E_{k}
where
k=1,2,3
it is straightforward to check that
\alpha\beta=\beta
and hence each of these sets forms a right-zero semigroup and indeed
E
is a band. The only thing remaining to check to see that (5) holds is that

E_{2}E_{3}\subseteq E_{2}
, and this is a now familiar calculation (also inherent in the above).
∎

We have shown that the idempotents of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
form a
left regular band
, that is, a band that satisfies the identity
xyx=yx
.

We now have all the tools necessary to describe the regular elements of

Proposition 3.5
.

The set of all regular elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is
\mathcal{G}_{n}\cup E(\mathcal{E}_{n})

\mathcal{E}_{n}

\mathcal{E}_{n}

Proof.

Clearly if
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
is an automorphism or an idempotent, then it is a regular element.
The fact that
\mathcal{G}_{n}\cup E(\mathcal{E}_{n})
forms a (proper) subsemigroup is immediate from Lemma
2.11

and Proposition
3.4
.

Conversely, let
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
be a regular element of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
so that
\alpha\gamma\alpha=\alpha
where
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Suppose for contradiction that
\alpha
is not idempotent (that is,
\alpha\in A\cup B\cup C
) then it follows from Corollary
2.6

\gamma\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
Note that

must be an idempotent left identity for

and hence of the same rank as

. If

, then
\alpha
has rank

; but since

and

has even or non-permutation type,
the rank of

is no greater than

. Similarly, if

has rank

.
The result follows.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We will see in Section

that the

is regular are

.

4.
Generators and presentations

Throughout this section we assume that
n\geq 5
. We demonstrate a minimal set of generators for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
and, with respect to this set of generators, a presentation for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
Recall that for
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the orbit of
\alpha
is
\alpha\mathcal{G}_{n}
. All elements of the same orbit have the same rank, and so we shall speak of orbits of a given rank. We shall say that the orbit of
\alpha
is

if
\alpha
cannot be generated by elements of strictly greater rank.

Remark 4.1
.

Clearly the orbit of each rank
3
element, that is, each element in
E_{3}\cup A\cup B
, is essential. Meanwhile, there is a unique essential orbit of rank
1
, namely the orbit of
\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
. Indeed,
any
\phi_{e,e}
, where
e\neq\mathrm{id}
may be decomposed as a product
\gamma\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
where
\gamma\in B
and
\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}\in E_{2}
each have rank greater than
1
, and so the orbit is not essential. The orbit of
\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
is simply
\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Then
\Sigma
is a generating set for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
if and only if
\Sigma
contains a generating set for the group
\mathcal{G}_{n}
together with at least one element of each essential orbit. The minimal size of a generating set is therefore
3+r_{3}(n)+r_{2}(n)
, where
r_{3}(n)
denotes the number of essential orbits (equivalently, orbits) of rank
3
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
and
r_{2}(n)
denotes the number of essential orbits of rank
2
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\Sigma
is a generating set. It follows immediately from the multiplication given in Corollary
2.6
that
\Sigma
must contain a generating set for
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Clearly
\mathcal{G}_{n}
forms a single essential orbit, and
\Sigma
contains at least one element of this orbit. By Remark
4.1
we have that
\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\}
is the unique essential orbit of rank
1
, and since it is clear from Corollary
2.6
that
\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
cannot be decomposed into a product of elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\}
it follows that any generating set must contain

\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
.

Assume that the rank of
\alpha\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
is
2
or
3
, and the orbit of
\alpha
is essential. Since
\Sigma
is a generating set we must have that
\alpha=\theta_{1}\cdots\theta_{m}
for some
m
where
\theta_{i}\in\Sigma
. Since
\alpha
is singular, it is clear that at least one of the generators
\theta_{i}
is singular. Moreover, since elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
act as left identities on the singular elements, we may assume without loss of generality that
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{l}
are all singular and
\theta_{l+1},\ldots,\theta_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
. Let
\gamma=\theta_{1}\cdots\theta_{l-1}
so that
\gamma\theta_{l}
is in the orbit of
\alpha
and in particular has the same rank as
\alpha
. We aim to show that the generator
\theta_{l}
is in the orbit of
\alpha
too.

If
\alpha
has rank
3
, then it follows from the facts that
3=|\mathrm{im\,}\alpha|=|\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\theta_{l}|\leq|\mathrm{im\,}\theta_{l}|\leq 3
and
\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\theta_{l}\subseteq\theta_{l}
that

\mathrm{im\,}\gamma\theta_{l}=\mathrm{im\,}\theta_{l}
and by Lemma
2.5
we have
\gamma\theta_{l}=\theta_{l}
.

Suppose now that
\alpha
and hence
\gamma\theta_{l}
have rank
2
.
If
\theta_{l}
has rank
3
then as
\gamma\theta_{l}
has rank
2
we must have that
\gamma\theta_{l}=\gamma^{\prime}\theta_{l}
for any representative
\gamma^{\prime}
of an essential orbit lying in
A
. But this would contradict our assumption that the orbit of
\alpha
is essential. Thus

\theta_{l}
has rank
2
and as above we have
\gamma\theta_{l}=\theta_{l}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\Sigma
is any set with the given properties. By Remark
4.1
we have that
\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\in\Sigma
. It is clear that we can generate all elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
(since we have a generating set for this finite group), and hence also all elements of the essential orbits (by definition). Since each rank
3
orbit is essential this shows that we may generate all elements of rank at least
3
, which in turn allows us to generate all elements of rank
2
(each element in a non-essential orbit by definition being obtained as products of elements of order greater than
2
). Finally, for each idempotent
e\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
e\neq\mathrm{id}
we have that
\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
is an endomorphism of rank
2
, and so for all
\gamma\in B
of rank
3
we have that
\gamma\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}=\phi_{e,e}
, which shows that all elements of rank
1
can be generated.

Since
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}_{n}
it is clear that a minimal generating set for
\mathcal{G}_{n}
has two elements. Since all rank
3
orbits are essential, whilst
\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}\}
is the unique rank
1
essential orbit it then follows that a minimal generating set has
2+r_{3}(n)+r_{2}(n)+1
elements.
∎

We now give a sketch of how one might enumerate a minimal generating set for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Clearly this amounts to finding a formula for

r_{2}(n)
and
r_{3}(n)
.
Each singular element corresponds to a pair
(t,e)\in P_{n}
and hence to a labelled coloured directed graph (one colour denoting the action of
t
and one of
e
), with specific properties.
Hence, each
orbit
of a singular element corresponds to an
unlabelled
coloured directed graph - and it must have precisely the following properties:

•

There are
n
unlabelled vertices.

•

All edges are coloured either blue (corresponding to the action of
t
and its conjugates) or red (corresponding to the action of
e
and its conjugates).

•

There is exactly one red edge and exactly one blue edge leaving each vertex.

•

Looking only at the blue edges, recalling the constraint
t^{3}=t
only, one can split the graph into components of the following two forms:

–

a vertex with a loop, possibly with edges coming into it and

–

a two-cycle, possibly with edges coming in to the vertices.

•

Looking now at the red edges, recalling the constraints
te=e=et=e^{2}
, the red edges map all vertices to a subset of the blue-loop-vertices in such a way that:

–

there is a red-loop at each vertex in the subset and

–

all vertices of a blue component are mapped by a red edge to the same red-loop-vertex.

Counting the total number of orbits therefore amounts to counting the number of (non-isomorphic) unlabelled coloured directed graphs of this kind. Rank
3
orbits correspond to the graphs containing at least one two-cycle. Rank
2
orbits correspond to graphs where there is no two-cycle but the red and blue edges do not completely coincide.

Having found minimal generating sets for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we now give a presentation using those generators. First, we recall some details concerning presentations for monoids: we must quotient free monoids by congruences.

Let
X
be an alphabet and denote by

the free monoid on
X
.
The elements of

are
words
over
X
, that is, finite products of elements of
X
, which may be referred to as
letters
.
We allow the empty product which we will denote by
1
. Then
X^{*}
becomes a monoid under concatenation of words, with
1
being the identity.
A
congruence
on
X^{*}
is an equivalence relation that is compatible with the binary operation: if
\rho
is such a congruence then

X^{*}/\rho:=\{[w]:w\in X^{*}\}
becomes a monoid under
[v][w]=[vw]
with identity
[1]
. If
R\subseteq X^{*}\times X^{*}
, we denote by
R^{\sharp}
the congruence on
X^{*}
generated by
R
, that is, the smallest congruence on
X^{*}
containing
R
. It is easy to see that
R^{\sharp}
is the reflexive transitive closure of the set

\{(xvy,xwy):x,y\in X^{*},(v,w)\in R\mbox{ or }(w,v)\in R\}
. We say a monoid
S
has
monoid

presentation

\langle X:R\rangle
if
{S\cong X^{*}/R^{\sharp}}
or, equivalently, if there is an epimorphism
\theta:X^{*}\to S
with kernel
R^{\sharp}=\ker\theta
. Here the congruence
\ker\theta

is defined by

\ker\theta:=\{(v,w):v\theta=w\theta\}
. If
\theta
is such an epimorphism, we say
S
has
presentation
\langle X:R\rangle
with respect to
\theta
. A relation
(w_{1},w_{2})\in R
will usually be displayed as an equation:
w_{1}=w_{2}
.

Now let
\Pi\cup\Sigma
be a (minimal) generating set for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
consisting of a generating set
\Pi
for
\mathcal{G}_{n}
together with a set

containing exactly one element

of each remaining essential orbit. We define

We choose and fix a single element of

of odd (even, non-permutation, trivial) type and denote it by

(
p^{ev},p^{np},p^{tr}
) (supressing here the subscripts, and where

).
Note that, by definition,

has rank
3
. Since each rank
3
orbit is essential, it is clear that we may also select
p^{ev}
and
p^{np}
so that their images under

have rank
3
. With abuse of terminology, we may refer
to

having (odd/even/non-permutation/trivial)

(where

) if

does. It follows from Remark
4.1
that

is the unique generator of rank
1
.

Let
X=X_{\Pi}\cup X_{\Sigma}
and
\theta:X^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{n}
be defined by

p_{t,e}\theta=\phi_{t,e}\mbox{ and }q_{g}\theta=\psi_{g}.

Let
R_{\Pi}
consist of
any set of relations that yield
\langle X_{\Pi}:R_{\Pi}\rangle
is a presentation of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. We do not concern ourselves here with the ‘best’ form of
R_{\Pi}
, as this is a well-trodden route. We aim to describe a set of relations
R_{\Pi\cup\Sigma}
on
X
that, when taken together with the relations
R_{\Pi}
, yields a presentation for
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. In what follows, the underlying assumptions are that
q_{g}
varies over
X_{\Pi}
and
p_{t,e}
varies over
X_{\Sigma}
. Define
F_{\Pi}(n)
to be the smallest number
such that any element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
has a representation as a word of length at most

in the generators
X_{\Pi}
. Let
R_{\Pi\cup\Sigma}
consist of the following relations, where
m\leq F_{\Pi}(n)
:

(R1)

q_{g}p_{t,e}=p_{t,e}

(R2)

p_{t,e}q_{g_{1}}\ldots q_{g_{m}}=p_{t,e}
where
\phi_{t,e}\psi_{g_{1}}\ldots\psi_{g_{m}}=\phi_{t,e}

(R3)

p_{t_{1},e_{1}}p_{t_{2},e_{2}}q_{g_{1}}\ldots q_{g_{m}}=p_{u_{1},f_{1}}p_{u_{2},f_{2}}
where

\phi_{t_{1},e_{1}}\phi_{t_{2},e_{2}}\psi_{g_{1}}\cdots\psi_{g_{m}}=\phi_{u_{1},f_{1}}\phi_{u_{2},f_{2}}

(R4)

p_{t,e}p_{u,f}=p_{t^{\prime},e^{\prime}}p_{u,f}
where
p_{t,e}
and

have the same type

(R5)

p^{od}p_{t,e}=p_{t,e}

(R6)

p^{ev}p_{t,e}=p_{t,e}
where
p_{t,e}
has rank
2

(R7)

p^{ev}p^{ev}p_{t,e}=p^{ev}p_{t,e}

(R8)

p^{np}p^{ev}p_{t,e}=p^{np}p_{t,e}
,
p^{ev}p^{np}p_{t,e}=p^{np}p_{t,e}
and
p^{np}p^{np}p_{t,e}=p^{np}p_{t,e}

(R9)

p^{ev}p^{tr}=p^{tr}
,
p^{np}p^{tr}=p^{tr}
and
p^{tr}p^{tr}=p^{tr}

(R10)

p^{tr}p^{np}p_{t,e}=p^{np}p_{t,e}
and

where

has odd or even type.

We now let

Theorem 4.3
.

The monoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
has presentation
\langle X:R\rangle
with respect to
\theta
.

Proof.

The morphism
\theta
takes
X
to a set of generators of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, so is an epimorphism. Let us denote

by
\sim
. Corollary

gives that

; it remains to show the converse.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
w\in X^{*}
and suppose that
w
contains at least one letter from
X_{\Sigma}
. Then

w\sim p_{t,e}q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}\mbox{ or }w\sim p_{t_{1},e_{1}}p_{t_{2},e_{2}}q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}

for some
p_{t,e},p_{t_{1},e_{1}},p_{t_{2},e_{2}}\in X_{\Sigma}
and
q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}\in X_{\Pi}
, for some

.

Proof.

By (R1) we have that
w\,\sim\,p_{t_{1},e_{1}}\ldots p_{t_{k},e_{k}}q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}

where
m\geq 0
. Either

, so that the first case holds.
Otherwise, using (R4) and (R5) we have that

p_{t_{1},e_{1}}\ldots p_{t_{k},e_{k}}\,\sim\,p^{1}\ldots p^{k-1}p_{t_{k},e_{k}}
where

for

. Finally, (R7)–(R8) give that the second case holds.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
w\in X^{*}
and suppose that
w
contains at least one letter from
X_{\Sigma}
. Then the orbit of
w\theta
is essential if and only if

w\sim p_{t,e}q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}

for some
p_{t,e}\in X_{\Sigma}
and
q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}\in X_{\Pi}
, for some
m\geq 0
.

Proof.

If
w\sim p_{t,e}q_{g_{1}}\cdots q_{g_{m}}
then it is clear the orbit of
w\theta
is essential. Conversely, suppose that
the orbit of
w\theta
is essential and the second case of Lemma
4.4
holds. Then

w\sim p_{t_{1},e_{1}}p_{t_{2},e_{2}}q_{h_{1}}\cdots q_{h_{k}}
. We proceed by examining the rank of
w\theta
to show that
p_{t_{1},e_{1}}p_{t_{2},e_{2}}\,\sim\,p_{t,e}
for some
p_{t,e}
.
Note that it follows from (R4) together with the fact that
p^{od}\theta,p^{ev}\theta
and
p^{np}\theta
are assumed to have rank
3
that we may assume without loss of generality that either
p_{t_{1},e_{1}}\theta
has rank
3
or else
p_{t_{1},e_{1}}=p^{tr}
.

If the rank of
w\theta
is
3
, then this forces

to be of odd type, and

p_{t_{1},e_{1}}p_{t_{2},e_{2}}\,\sim\,p_{t_{2},e_{2}}
.

If the rank of
w\theta
is
2
, then either
\phi_{t_{1},e_{1}}
is of odd type, and we proceed as above. Or,
\phi_{t_{1},e_{1}}
is of even type and

\phi_{t_{2},e_{2}}
has rank
2
or
3
. If

has rank

, then
\phi_{t_{1},e_{1}}\phi_{t_{2},e_{2}}=\phi_{t_{2},e_{2}}
and also by (R4) and
(R6) we have

. Otherwise,
\phi_{t_{2},e_{2}}
has rank
3
, and since by assumption
\phi_{t_{1},e_{1}}
has rank
3
, this contradicts the fact that

lies in an essential orbit.

If
w\theta
has rank

We shall consider all combinations of types for elements

is of trivial type, then

5.
Green’s relations and ideal structure

Throughout this section we again assume that

\phi_{t,e}
each having rank at most three. Here we turn our attention to Green’s relations
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
,
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
,
\mathop{\mathscr{D}}
,
\mathop{\mathscr{H}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{J}}

We now recall their precise definitions. The original sources for the

\displaystyle\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}}\beta\iff

These relations come with the appropriate generalisation of the inclusions

Let

\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
-below

Remark 6.7
.

Unlike the situation for
\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}}
and
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}
, we find that
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
is a strictly smaller relation than
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}
.
Indeed, in general, the relation
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
on a semigroup
S
is well-known to be a right congruence. However, it is easy to see that
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}
is
not
a right congruence. Indeed, taking
\alpha\in A
,
\beta\in B
and
\phi_{t,e}\in E_{3}
we find that
\alpha\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}\beta
whilst
\alpha\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}}
and
\beta\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{e,e}
are distinct idempotents, and hence not
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}
-related.

In what follows, for each
(t,e)\in P_{n}
we write
\mathrm{Fix}(t,e)=\left\{g\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\colon t^{g}=t\text{ and }e^{g}=e\right\}
.
Notice in particular that
\mathrm{Fix}(\mathrm{id},e)=\mathrm{Fix}(e,e)
for all
e^{2}=e
and
\mathrm{Fix}(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})=\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
The next lemma is immediate.

Lemma 6.8
.

For any
\phi_{t,e}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
\psi_{g}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
we have that

\phi_{t,e}\psi_{g}=\phi_{t,e}
if and only if
g\in\mathrm{Fix}(t,e)
.

We can now give the description of
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
.

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Then
\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}\beta
if and only if one of the following occurs:

•

\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
;

•

\alpha,\beta\in E_{3}\cup E_{2}\cup E_{1}
and
\alpha=\beta
; or

•

\alpha,\beta\in A\cup B\cup C
are such that
\alpha=\phi_{t,e}
,
\beta=\phi_{u,f}
have the same type and
\mathrm{Fix}(t,e)=\mathrm{Fix}(u,f)
.

Proof.

Since
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}\subseteq\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}
, we have that all elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
are
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related, and that idempotents of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
distinct from
\varepsilon
form their own
\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}}
-class by Proposition
6.6
and thus they also form their own
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-class.
Therefore, it only remains to show that elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
cannot be
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related to non-regular elements (that is, elements of
A\cup B\cup C
) and that two non-regular elements are
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related if and only if they have the same type and are fixed by the same automorphisms of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

To see first that no elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
can be
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related to elements of
A\cup B\cup C
, consider
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
and
\beta\in A\cup B\cup C
, and let
t=(1\,2),\,e=c_{3}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
Since
t^{2}=\mathrm{id}
and
(t,e)\in P_{n}
we have
\phi_{t,e},\phi_{\mathrm{id},e},\phi_{e,e}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
Clearly
\alpha\phi_{t,e},\alpha\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
and
\alpha\phi_{e,e}
are all distinct.
If
\beta\in A
, then we have that
\beta\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}=\beta\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
, while if
\beta\in B\cup C
we have
\beta\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{e,e}=\beta\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
.
Therefore elements of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
cannot be
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related to elements of
A\cup B\cup C
.

From now on, we assume that
\alpha,\beta
are non-regular (i.e. contained in
A\cup B\cup C
), and that
\alpha=\phi_{v,k}
and
\beta=\phi_{u,f}
for some
(v,k),(u,f)\in P_{n}
.

Suppose that
\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}\beta
and consider the maps
\phi_{t,e}
,
\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}
and
\phi_{e,e}
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
as above.
To see that
\alpha
and
\beta
must have the same type, notice that if
\alpha\in A
and
\beta\in B\cup C
, then
\alpha\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{\mathrm{id},e}\neq\phi_{e,e}=\alpha\phi_{e,e}
while
\beta\phi_{t,e}=\phi_{e,e}=\beta\phi_{e,e}
which contradicts the fact that
\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}\beta
.
Thus either
\alpha,\beta\in A
, or
\alpha,\beta\in B\cup C
which shows that
\mathop{\mathscr{L}^{*}}
-related maps must be of the same type.
Lemma
6.8
gives that
\mathrm{Fix}(v,k)=\mathrm{Fix}(u,f)
.

Conversely, assume that
\alpha
and
\beta
have the same type and that
\mathrm{Fix}(v,k)=\mathrm{Fix}(u,f)
.
By Lemma
2.13
we have that
\alpha\eta=\beta\eta
for all
\eta\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
Suppose now that
\psi_{g},\psi_{h}
in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
. If
\alpha\psi_{g}=\alpha\psi_{h}
, then
\alpha\psi_{gh^{-1}}=\alpha
.
It follows that

and so
\beta\psi_{g}=\beta\psi_{h}
.
Finally, it is easy to see that if
\alpha\in A\cup B\cup C

and

, then
as the rank of

\alpha

is strictly less than the rank of
\alpha

\alpha\gamma=\alpha\delta
.
Using the symmetry in the arguments used above concludes the proof.
∎

7.
The structure of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
for
n\leq 4

In order to have clean statements with uniform proofs, in the previous sections we focussed on the case where
n\geq 5
. To complete the picture, in this section we describe the structure of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
in the cases where
n\leq 4
. We note that the decomposition in terms of rank and type given in Lemma
2.9
can also be used to describe the structure of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
in these small cases, however, some of the sets turn out to be empty. Indeed,
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{G}_{1}=\{\varepsilon\}
is a trivial group, and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
decomposes as a disjoint union
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\mathcal{G}_{2}\cup E_{2}(2)\cup E_{1}(2)
where
\mathcal{G}_{2}=\{\varepsilon,\psi_{(1\,2)}\}
is the automorphism group, whilst
E_{2}(2)=\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},c_{1}},\phi_{\mathrm{id},c_{2}}\}
and
E_{1}(2)=\{\phi_{\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id}},\phi_{c_{1},c_{1}},\phi_{c_{2},c_{2}}\}
are the idempotents of rank
2
and
1
respectively. We note in particular that these two semigroups (consisting of group elements and idempotents only) are regular. For
n=3
, the endomorphism monoid
\mathcal{E}_{3}
decomposes as a disjoint union
\mathcal{E}_{3}=\mathcal{G}_{3}\cup E_{3}(3)\cup E_{2}(3)\cup C(3)\cup E_{1}(3)
. The elements of
C(3)
are not regular (note that the reasoning given in the proof of Proposition
3.5
also applies here). For the case
n=4
, recall that the endomorphism monoid
\mathcal{E}_{4}=\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{4})
was described in Lemma
2.9
as
\mathcal{E}_{4}=\mathcal{G}_{4}\cup D(4)\cup E_{3}(4)\cup B(4)\cup E_{2}(4)\cup C(4)\cup E_{1}(4)
where each set is non-empty and
D(4)
contains idempotents of rank
7
, namely the elements in the set
E_{7}(4)=\left\{\sigma^{g}\colon g\in\mathcal{K}\right\}
.

In spite of these differences, certain properties turn out to be common to all endomorphism monoids
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. Since their proofs are often akin to those presented in Sections
3
–
6
and these results could be obtained by direct enumeration using a computer program such as GAP, we will only highlight the main points that differ or require attention. A detailed version of these proofs can be found in the thesis of the second author
[
9
]
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
n
be a natural number. In the endomorphism monoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, the following statements hold:

(1)

the set of all idempotents is a band, and forms a rank-ordered chain of
right zero semigroups;

(2)

the set of idempotents of rank
1
is the minimal ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
;

(3)

\mathop{\mathscr{H}}=\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathscr{R}}=\mathop{\mathscr{D}}=\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
;

(4)

\mathop{\mathscr{R}^{*}}=\mathop{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}
and the classes are the sets of elements with the same rank.

Proof.

These facts have already been explicitly proven for
n\geq 5
in the previous sections, and it is straightforward to check that the details go through in just the same way for all
n\neq 4
. Indeed, for all
n\neq 4
, statements (1) and (2) follow in exactly the same way as in the proof of Proposition
3.4
, whilst it is readily verified that the characterisation of the
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
relations given in Section
5
(
\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\beta
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
or
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
; and

\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{R}}\beta
if and only if
\alpha,\beta\in E_{k}
for some

or
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
or
\alpha\mathcal{G}_{n}=\beta\mathcal{G}_{n}
) also hold in these cases, from which statement (3) follows (since
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
). Part (4) is trivial in the case where
n=1,2
(since these semigroups are regular) and can be proved in an entirely similar manner in the case
n=3
, using the fact that there is an idempotent of each type.

The case
n=4
is a little different, since there are extra endomorphisms to consider. We use extensively the multiplication of elements described in Corollary
2.6
and Lemma
2.7
.

For parts (1) and (2): It is clear from the multiplication in Corollary
2.6

that
E_{1}(4)<E_{2}(4)<E_{3}(4)<\{\varepsilon\}
is a chain of right-zero semigroups. That
E_{1}(4)
is the minimal ideal can be seen by consideration of ranks. Notice that if
\alpha=\sigma^{g}\in E_{7}(4)
, then
p_{g}=\mathrm{id}
and
\alpha\sigma^{h}=\sigma^{h}
, which means that elements of
E_{7}(4)
are left identities for elements of

, and hence in particular that
E_{7}(4)
is also a right-zero semigroup. To complete the proof it suffices to verify that
E_{7}(4)E_{k}(4)\subseteq E_{k}(4)
and
E_{k}(4)E_{7}(4)\subseteq E_{k}(4)
for all

, which follows immediately from Lemma
2.7
.

For part (3): It is clear from the multiplication in Corollary
2.6
and Lemma
2.7
that
D(4)
and
\mathcal{E}_{4}\setminus D(4)
are subsemigroups of
\mathcal{E}_{4}
. Elements of
D(4)
cannot be in the same
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
- or
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
-class as elements of

since they do not have the same rank.
Moreover, from the multiplication one finds that two elements of
\mathcal{E}_{4}\setminus D(4)
will be
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
(respectively,
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
) related in
\mathcal{E}_{4}
if and only if they are
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
(respectively,
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
) related in
\mathcal{E}_{4}\setminus D(4)
, and one can describe the classes here in a similar manner to the case
n\neq 4
. Thus it suffices to describe how

splits into
\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
and
\mathop{\mathscr{R}}
classes. Since
\mathrm{im\,}\sigma^{g}=\left\{t^{g}\colon t\in\mathcal{S}_{4},4t=4\right\}\cup\left\{c_{4g}\right\}
, it follows that
\mathrm{im\,}\sigma^{g}=\mathrm{im\,}\sigma^{h}
if and only if

. Thus
\alpha\mathop{\mathscr{L}}\beta
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
or
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{G}_{4}

8.
Related problems

As mentioned in the Introduction, this article is an opener in the discussion of the structure of endomorphism monoids of full transformation semigroups of various natural kinds. We present here a number of questions and possible further directions worthy of investigation.

Minimal presentations and counting problems

The presentation constructed in Section
4
uses a minimal generating set, whose size has a nice combinatorial interpretation in terms of directed coloured graphs of certain types. Using the machinery of generating functions, it seems possible that one could give (recurrence) formulae for
r_{3}(n)
(the number of orbits of rank
3
) and
r_{2}(n)
(the number of essential orbits of rank
2
), and hence give a formula for the size
m(n)
of a minimal generating set of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
. A natural question in this regard is how
m(n)
grows asymptotically, in particular as compared with the size of the endomorphism monoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
itself. We note that some similar counting problems (concerning solutions to the system of equations
X^{2}=X
,
Y^{2}=Y
,
XY=YX
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
) have been considered in
[
13
]
.

Whilst our generating set is minimal, we make no claim, however, as to the minimality of our
presentation
. Indeed, the number of relations of the form (R2) and (R3) and the length of the words involved in these relation is dependent upon the chosen presentation
\langle X_{\Pi}:R_{\Pi}\rangle
of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Specifically, it is dependent upon
F_{\Pi}(n)
, that is, the smallest number
such that any element of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
has a representation as a word of length at most
F_{\Pi}(n)
in the generators
X_{\Pi}
.

Other questions concerning presentations

We have considered a presentation for the monoid
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, based on a minimal set of generators. If we allowed ourselves to chose a generator from each orbit, then our relations would naturally simplify. Previous articles have considered presentations for the singular part of
\mathcal{T}_{n}

[
2
,
4
]
. We could equally well consider (now semigroup) generators and presentations for
\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Other semigroups of transformations

We have focussed on
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
as
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is perhaps the most natural semigroup of
finite
transformations.The automorphisms and endomorphisms of
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}_{n}
have already been described by different authors (see
[
8
, Chap. 7]
), where
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}_{n}
are, respectively, the
semigroups of partial (partial one-one) maps of a finite set, under composition of (partial) maps.
What can be said of the structure of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{PT}_{n})
,
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{I}_{n})
or
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
?

Moving away from transformation semigroups, one could consider related semigroups, such as Brauer monoids and partition monoids (see, for example,
[
3
]
), where here the endomorphisms have been determined
[
18
]
.

We have started with an unordered set

\{1,\ldots,n\}
and defined
\mathcal{T}_{n}
to be all maps from this set to itself. There are ordered analogues of

\mathcal{T}_{n}
obtained by considering order preserving maps of

\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The study of their endomorphisms has begun in
[
15
,
16
]
, and it would be interesting to develop these ideas further to describe the structure of the endomorphism monoids.

In another direction, one can drop the constraint of finiteness. Specifically, what of the endomorphism monoid of the full transformation monoid (and related semigroups) on an
infinite
set, such as
\mathbb{N}
?

Semigroup vs. monoid endomophisms

For any monoid
S
we may consider the monoid of monoid endomorphisms (that is, semigroup endomorphisms also fixing the identity) or the monoid of semigroup endomorphisms. Here we have considered the latter for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, but it would also be possible to adapt our results to consider the former.

Endomorphisms of endomorphism monoids of other algebras

We may regard regard
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as the monoid of endomorphisms of an algebra with no operations. This is a degenerate case of a free algebra. One could consider endomorphisms of other endomorphism monoids of free algebra, perhaps starting with their automorphisms. A useful reference here is
[
1
]
.

Iteration

The structure of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is quite rich: it forms a chain of
\mathcal{D}=\mathop{\mathscr{J}}
-classes, which are non-trivial, and it has elements of every rank.
It is, of course, the semigroup of endomorphisms of an algebra with no operations. By contrast, the structure of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is rather thin - for example,

\mathop{\mathscr{L}}
is trivial outside of the group of units. Will this make the structure of
\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
richer? In general, what can one say about the sequence of monoids

\mathcal{T}_{n},\;\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n}),\;\mathrm{End}(\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{T}_{n})),\ldots?
