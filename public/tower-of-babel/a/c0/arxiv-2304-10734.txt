Beta Jacobi ensembles and associated Jacobi polynomials, II
Fumihiko Nakano, Hoang Dung Trinh, Khanh Duy Trinh

Abstract
In a high temperature regime, it was shown in Trinh–Trinh ( J. Stat. Phys. 185 (1), Paper No. 4, 15 (2021)) that the empirical distribution of beta Jacobi ensembles converges to a limiting probability measure which is related to Model III of associated Jacobi polynomials. In this paper, we establish Gaussian fluctuations around the limit whose statement involves orthogonal polynomials. For the proofs, we refine a moment method at the process level which has been used to deal with Gaussian beta ensembles and beta Laguerre ensembles. Keywords: Beta Jacobi ensembles ; high temperature regime ; associated Jacobi polynomials ; duals of Jacobi orthogonal polynomials AMS Subject Classification: Primary 60B20 ; Secondary 60H05



1
Introduction

Beta Jacobi ensembles are a family of probability distributions on
[0,1]^{N}
with joint probability density functions of the form

\frac{1}{Z}\times\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|^{\beta}\prod_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{a}(1-\lambda_{l})^{b},\quad(0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}\leq 1),

(1)

where
N
is the system size,
Z
is the normalizing constant and
\beta>0
and
a,b>-1
are parameters. They can be realized as the joint distribution of the eigenvalues of random tridiagonal matrices
[
12
]
. In a high temperature regime where
N\to\infty
with
\beta N\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
, and
a,b
are fixed, (with
c
being a constant), the empirical distribution

L_{N,\beta}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}

converges weakly to a probability measure
\nu_{c}
(depending on
a
and
b
as well) which is the probability measure of a new model (Model III) of associated Jacobi polynomials, almost surely
[
18
]
. Here
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the Dirac measure at
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since all probability measures
L_{N,\beta}
are supported on
[0,1]
, the above convergence of random probability measures is equivalent to the following: for any polynomial test function
p
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta N\to 2c
,

\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}p(\lambda_{i})\to\langle\nu_{c},p\rangle,\quad\text{almost surely.}

Here
\langle\mu,f\rangle
denotes the integral
\int fd\mu
, for an integrable function
f
with respect to a probability measure
\mu
. The above almost sure convergence is referred to as the law of large numbers (LLN) for polynomial test functions.

The aim of this paper is to study fluctuations around the limit. We establish central limit theorems (CLT) for
\{\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle\}
with polynomial test functions
p
. Moreover, we obtain multivariate CLTs involving orthogonal polynomials with respect to the measure
x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)dx
. Let us introduce the result in detail. Denote by
\nu_{c}^{*}
the probability measure with density
\nu_{c}^{*}(x)
proportional to
x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)
, that is,

\nu_{c}^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{c}^{*}}x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x),\quad x\in(0,1),\quad(Z_{c}^{*}=\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle).

Let
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be orthonormal polynomials with respect to
\nu_{c}^{*}
which are defined by the following three term recurrence relation (see Sect.
4
)

\begin{cases}\tilde{p}_{0}(x)=1,\quad b_{1}^{*}\tilde{p}_{1}(x)=x-a_{1}^{*},\\
b_{n+1}^{*}\tilde{p}_{n+1}(x)=x\tilde{p}_{n}(x)-a_{n+1}^{*}\tilde{p}_{n}(x)-b_{n}^{*}\tilde{p}_{n-1}(x),\quad n=1,2,\dots,\end{cases}

(2)

where
a_{n}^{*}=\lambda_{n-1}^{*}+\mu_{n-1}^{*}
and
b_{n}^{*}=\sqrt{\mu_{n-1}^{*}\lambda_{n}^{*}}
, with

\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}^{*}=\frac{n+c+a+1}{2n+2c+a+b+2}\frac{n+c+a+b+2}{2n+2c+a+b+3},\\
\mu_{n}^{*}=\frac{n+c+1}{2n+2c+a+b+3}\frac{n+c+b+2}{2n+2c+a+b+4},\end{cases}\quad n\geq 0.

Let
\tilde{P}_{n}
be a primitive of
\tilde{p}_{n}
, that is,
\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}=\tilde{p}_{n}
. Then our main result on Gaussian fluctuations around the limit is stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a,b>-1/2
and
c>0
be given. Then the following hold.

(i)

For
n\geq 0
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
,

\sqrt{N}\left(\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle\right)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{n}}^{2}),\quad\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{n}}^{2}=\frac{Z_{c}^{*}}{(n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2)}.

(3)

Here ‘
\overset{d}{\to}
’ denotes the convergence in distribution,
{\mathcal{N}}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
denotes the Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, and

Z_{c}^{*}=\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle=\frac{(c+a+1)(c+b+1)(c+a+b+2)}{(2c+a+b+2)^{2}(2c+a+b+3)}.

(ii)

For any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\big\{\sqrt{N}\big(\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle\big)\big\}_{n=0}^{M}
jointly converge in distribution to independent Gaussian random variables.

Note that the condition
a,b>-1/2
is assumed when dealing with beta Jacobi processes (see Sect. 3). The above result is expected to hold for
a,b>-1
. Note also that the convergence (
3
) holds under the condition that
\beta=2c/N
. We still get an analogous result when
\beta N\to 2c
by replacing
\langle\nu_{c},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle
by
\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle]
, that is, as
\beta N\to 2c
,

\sqrt{N}\left(\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle-\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle]\right)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{n}}^{2}).

We conclude the introduction with the following remark.
Theorem
2.3
below implies that
for any
k,l\geq 1
, the limit

\sigma_{k,l}=\lim_{N\to\infty;\beta=2c/N}N\Cov[\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{k}\rangle,\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{l}\rangle]

exists and that for any
M\geq 1
,

\sqrt{N}\left(\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{k}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle\right)_{k=1}^{M}\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}_{M}(0,\Sigma),\quad\Sigma=(\sigma_{k,l})_{k,l=1}^{M},

where
{\mathcal{N}}_{M}(0,\Sigma)
denotes the
M
-dimensional Gaussian distribution with mean zero and covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Besides, the limiting covariance
\sigma_{k,l}
satisfies the following relation (Eq. (5.23) in
[
9
]
)

\sigma_{k,l}=\frac{1}{k+2c+a+b+1}\left(l(u_{l}-u_{k+l})-b\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sigma_{i,l}-2c\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}u_{i}\sigma_{k-i,l}\right).

Here
u_{k}=\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle
is the
k
th moment of the limiting measure
\nu_{c}
. There is also a recursive formula for
\{u_{k}\}
as given in equation (
19
). Now for each
n
, we can assume without loss of generality that the constant term in a polynomial
\tilde{P}_{n}
is zero. Given
M\geq 1
, let
C
be an
M\times M
coefficients matrix of
\{\tilde{P}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{M-1}
, that is,

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{0}\\
\tilde{P}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{P}_{M-1}\end{pmatrix}=C\begin{pmatrix}x\\
x^{2}\\
\vdots\\
x^{M}\end{pmatrix}.

It is clear that
C
is a lower triangular matrix. Then Theorem
1.1
(ii) implies that

\diag(\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{0}}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{M-1}}^{2})=C\Sigma C^{\top},

(4)

where
\diag(a_{1},\dots,a_{M})
denotes the diagonal matrix with
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{M}
in the diagonal, and
C^{\top}
denotes the transpose of
C
.
It would be an interesting problem to study properties of the limiting variance matrix
\Sigma
like the above decomposition which is related to the LDL decomposition directly from the recursive formula for
\sigma_{k,l}
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sect. 2, we establish the LLN and CLTs for polynomial test functions by using the random tridiagonal matrix model. Sect. 3 proves a dynamic version of Theorem
1.1
for stationary beta Jacobi processes. Several properties of associated Jacobi polynomials needed in arguments in Sect. 3 are introduced in Sect. 4. Finally, the proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in Sect. 5.

2
Limit theorems for the empirical distribution of beta Jacobi ensembles

In this section, we establish the convergence to a limit and Gaussian fluctuations around the limit of the empirical distribution by using the tridiagonal random matrix model. We begin with introducing the model.
Denote by
\Beta(a,b)
the beta distribution with parameters
a,b>0
.
Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
and
q_{1},\dots,q_{N-1}
be independent random variables having beta distributions with parameters

\displaystyle p_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\Beta((N-n)\tfrac{\beta}{2}+a+1,(N-n)\tfrac{\beta}{2}+b+1),

\displaystyle q_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\Beta((N-n)\tfrac{\beta}{2},(N-n-1)\tfrac{\beta}{2}+a+b+2),

where
a,b>-1
and
\beta>0
.
Define

\displaystyle s_{n}

\displaystyle={p_{n}(1-q_{n-1})},\quad n=1,\dots,N,\quad(q_{0}=0),

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle={q_{n}(1-p_{n})},\quad n=1,\dots,N-1,

and form a (random symmetric) tridiagonal matrix

J_{N,\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{s_{1}}\\
\sqrt{t_{1}}&\sqrt{s_{2}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\sqrt{t_{N-1}}&\sqrt{s_{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{s_{1}}&\sqrt{t_{1}}\\
&\sqrt{s_{2}}&\sqrt{t_{2}}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&\sqrt{s_{N}}\end{pmatrix}.

Then the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}
of
J_{N,\beta}
are distributed according to the beta Jacobi ensemble (
1
)
[
12
]
.

For given
a,b>-1
and
c>0
, we form the following random (infinite) tridiagonal matrix

J^{(\infty)}_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{s_{1}^{(\infty)}}\\
\sqrt{t_{1}^{(\infty)}}&\sqrt{s_{2}^{(\infty)}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{s_{1}^{(\infty)}}&\sqrt{t_{1}^{(\infty)}}\\
&\sqrt{s_{2}^{(\infty)}}&\sqrt{t_{2}^{(\infty)}}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{pmatrix},

where

\begin{cases}s_{n}^{(\infty)}={p_{n}^{(\infty)}(1-q_{n-1}^{(\infty)})},\\
t_{n}^{(\infty)}={q_{n}^{(\infty)}(1-p_{n}^{(\infty)})},\end{cases}\quad n=1,2,\dots,\quad(q_{0}^{(\infty)}=0),

with
\{p_{n}^{(\infty)}\}
and
\{q_{n}^{(\infty)}\}
being two sequences of i.i.d. (independent identically distributed) random variables independent of each other with the common distributions
\Beta(c+a+1,c+b+1)
and
\Beta(c,c+a+b+2)
, respectively. The matrix
J^{(\infty)}_{c}
is the limit in distribution of
J_{N,\beta}
as
N\to\infty
with
N\beta\to 2c
in the sense that for each fixed
i
,

J_{N,\beta}(i,i)\overset{d}{\to}J^{(\infty)}_{c}(i,i),\quad J_{N,\beta}(i,i\pm 1)\overset{d}{\to}J^{(\infty)}_{c}(i,i\pm 1).

The joint convergence also holds.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta}
be the spectral measure of
J_{N,\beta}
, the unique probability measure satisfying

\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta},x^{n}\rangle=(J_{N,\beta})^{n}(1,1),\quad n=0,1,\dots.

Relations between the spectral measure of tridiagonal matrices and orthogonal polynomials will be discussed in Sect.
4
.
It turns out that
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta}
has the following expression

{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\delta_{\lambda_{i}},

where
w_{i}=v_{i}(1)^{2}
with
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
normalized eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
J_{N,\beta}
. Moreover, it is known that
(w_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are independent of the eigenvalues and have the Dirichlet distribution with parameters
(\beta/2,\dots,\beta/2)
(see
[
12
]
or Chapter 3 in
[
8
]
). Consequently, for any test function
f
,

\Ex[\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta},f\rangle]=\Ex\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}f(\lambda_{i})\bigg]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Ex[w_{i}]\Ex[f(\lambda_{i})]=\frac{1}{N}\Ex\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\lambda_{i})\bigg]=\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},f\rangle].

In particular, when
f=x^{k}
, the above relation implies that

\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{k}\rangle]=\Ex\bigg[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(i,i)\bigg]=\Ex[(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)].

(5)

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{c}
be the spectral measure of
J^{(\infty)}_{c}
, the unique probability measure satisfying

\langle{\mathcal{S}}_{c},x^{n}\rangle=(J^{(\infty)}_{c})^{n}(1,1),\quad n=0,1,\dots.

It is unique because entries of
J^{(\infty)}_{c}
are bounded (see Sect.
4
). Let
\nu_{c}
be the mean of
{\mathcal{S}}_{c}
. The convergence in distribution of
\{p_{n}\}
,
\{q_{n}\}
together with their independence implies that as
\beta N\to 2c
,

\Ex[(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)]\to\Ex[(J^{(\infty)}_{c})^{k}(1,1)]=\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle.

(6)

When
\beta=2c/N
, we estimate the rate of convergence as follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, in the regime where
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

|\Ex[(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)]-\Ex[(J^{(\infty)}_{c})^{k}(1,1)]|\leq\frac{C}{N}.

Consequently,
for any polynomial
p
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
,

\sqrt{N}\big(\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]-\langle\nu_{c},p\rangle\big)\to 0.

(7)

We need the following result to prove the above lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

(i)

Assume that
X\sim\Beta(a,b)
has the beta distribution with parameters
a,b>0
. Then for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\Ex[X^{k}]=\prod_{r=1}^{k}\frac{a+r}{a+b+r}.

(ii)

For given
a,b>0,0<\varepsilon<a\wedge b
and
K\in\{1,2,\dots\}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
Y\sim\Beta(a+\delta_{a},b+\delta_{b})
with
|\delta_{a}|,|\delta_{b}|<\varepsilon
, and for any
k\in\{1,2,\dots,K\}
,

|\Ex[Y^{k}]-\Ex[X^{k}]|\leq C\varepsilon.

Here
a\wedge b=\min\{a,b\}
.

Proof.

(i) is a fundamental result on beta distributions. (ii) is deduced from (i) by using the mean value theorem for functions of two variables.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

For given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it is clear that when
N\geq k
,
(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)
is a polynomial of
\{s_{i},t_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell_{k}}
, and thus, is a polynomial of
\{p_{i},q_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\ell_{k}}
, where
\ell_{k}
depends only on
k
. A general term in that polynomial is of the form

const\times\prod_{i=1}^{\ell_{k}}p_{i}^{a_{i}}q_{i}^{b_{i}},

with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
and
\sum_{i}(a_{i}+b_{i})\leq 2k
. By the independence of
\{p_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{q_{i}\}_{i}
, its mean value is a product of moments of beta distributions.
Now, we make use of Lemma
2.2
(ii) to deduce that in the regime where
\beta=2c/N
,

|\Ex[(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)]-\Ex[(J^{(\infty)}_{c})^{k}(1,1)]|\leq\frac{C}{N},

for some constant
C>0
depending on
k,a,b
and
c
. The convergence (
7
) is a direct consequence of the above estimate. The proof is complete.
∎

Here is the main result in this section.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
a,b>-1
and
c>0
be fixed. Then the following hold.

(LLN)

As
N\to\infty
with
\beta N\to 2c
, the empirical distribution
L_{N,\beta}
converges weakly to
\nu_{c}
, almost surely. Equivalently, for any polynomial
p
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta N\to 2c
,

\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle\to\langle\nu_{c},p\rangle,\quad a.s..

(8)

(CLT)

For any polynomial
p
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
,

N\Var[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]\to\sigma_{p}^{2},\quad\sqrt{N}\big(\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},p\rangle\big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{p}^{2}),

(9)

where
\sigma_{p}^{2}\geq 0
is a constant. Equivalently, there are jointly Gaussian random variables
\{\zeta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that for any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\left(\sqrt{N}(\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},x^{n}\rangle)\right)_{n=1}^{M}\overset{d}{\to}(\zeta_{n})_{n=1}^{M}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{M}(0,\Sigma),

(10)

where
\Sigma=(\sigma_{k,l})_{k,l=1}^{M}
is the limiting covariance matrix,

\sigma_{k,l}=\lim_{N\to\infty;\beta=2c/N}N\Cov[\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{k}\rangle,\langle L_{N,\beta},x^{l}\rangle].

Proof.

(LLN) For any polynomial
p
, the convergence of the mean value of
\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle
follows directly from equation (
6
). The almost sure convergence of the form

\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle-\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]\to 0,\quad a.s.,

can be deduced by using the random matrix model
J_{N,\beta}
(see
[
18
]
). Thus, we get the first part proved.

(CLT) We only present key ideas because arguments are almost the same as those used in
[
14
]
for the Gaussian case. First, it is clear that
\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle
is a polynomial of
\{p_{n},q_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
. If the two sequences
\{p_{n}\}
and
\{q_{n}\}
were i.i.d. and independent of each other, an application of the martingale difference central limit theorem with a filtration constructed from
p_{n}
’s and
q_{n}
’s leads to the following CLT (cf.
[
14
, §2.1]
)

N\Var[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]\to\sigma_{p}^{2},\quad\sqrt{N}\big(\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle-\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]\big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{p}^{2}),\quad(\sigma_{p}^{2}\geq 0).

(11)

Second, in the regime where
\beta=2c/N
, the two sequences
\{p_{n}\}
and
\{q_{n}\}
are locally approximated by i.i.d. sequences, and thus, arguments as used in
[
14
, §2.2]
for the Gaussian case also work here to yield the CLT (
11
). Third, the mean value
\Ex[\langle L_{N,\beta},p\rangle]
can by replaced by
\langle\nu_{c},p\rangle
in (
11
) by Lemma
2.1
. Finally, the equivalent statement for moments is standard, which completes the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
∎

3
Limit theorems for the empirical measure process of beta Jacobi processes

3.1
Beta Jacobi processes

Let
a,b>-1/2
and
\beta>0
. Consider the following system of stochastic differential equations (SDE)

\displaystyle d\phi_{i}

\displaystyle={\sqrt{2}}db_{i}+\frac{2a+1}{2}\cot\frac{\phi_{i}}{2}dt-\frac{2b+1}{2}\tan\frac{\phi_{i}}{2}dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j:j\neq i}^{N}\left(\cot(\frac{\phi_{i}}{2}+\frac{\phi_{j}}{2})+\cot(\frac{\phi_{i}}{2}-\frac{\phi_{j}}{2})\right)dt,\quad(i=1,2,\dots,N),

(12)

with initial conditions
\phi_{i}(0)=y_{i},(i=1,2,\dots,N)
, where
{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
, the closure of

{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}:0<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{N}<{\pi}\right\}.

Here
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}=\{b_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are independent standard Brownian motions. The system of SDEs (
12
) has a unique strong solution under the constraint that
(\phi_{1}(t),\dots,\phi_{N}(t))\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
for all
t\in[0,\infty)
c.f.
[
6
]
. To deal with the singularity of the drift term at the boundary of
{\mathcal{C}}
, the theory of multivariate SDEs developed in
[
3
,
4
]
has been used.

Define a function
\Psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
as

\displaystyle\Psi({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=(2a+1)\sum_{i}\Big(-\log\sin\frac{x_{i}}{2}\Big)+(2b+1)\sum_{i}\Big(-\log\cos\frac{x_{i}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\sum_{i<j}\Big\{-\log\sin\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{2}+\frac{x_{j}}{2}\Big)-\log\sin\Big(\frac{x_{j}}{2}-\frac{x_{i}}{2}\Big)\Big\},\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathcal{C}},

and
\Psi({\boldsymbol{x}})=\infty
, otherwise. Note that under the condition
a,b>-1/2
and
\beta>0
, the function
\Psi
is convex with the domain
{\mathcal{C}}
. For
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, it is clear that

\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{i}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=-\frac{2a+1}{2}\cot\frac{x_{i}}{2}+\frac{2b+1}{2}\tan\frac{x_{i}}{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j\neq i}\left\{\cot\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{2}+\frac{x_{j}}{2}\Big)+\cot\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{2}-\frac{x_{j}}{2}\Big)\right\},

and thus, the system of SDEs (
12
) for
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}=(\phi_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{N}
can be expressed as

d{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}={\sqrt{2}}d{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}-\nabla\Psi({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})dt.

(13)

Here
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}=(b_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{N}
is a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, and
\nabla\Psi=(\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{i}})_{i=1}^{N}
. It is worth mentioning that the above expression makes it easy to use the theory of multivariate SDEs since the subderivative of a convex function is a maximal monotone operator.

Beta Jacobi processes are then defined via a change of variables
\lambda_{i}=(\sin\frac{\phi_{i}}{2})^{2},i=1,\dots,N
. They satisfy the following system of SDEs

\begin{cases}d\lambda_{i}=\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}db_{i}+\left(a+1-(a+b+2)\lambda_{i}+\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\frac{2\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\right)dt,\\
\lambda_{i}(0)=x_{i},\end{cases}\quad i=1,\dots,N.

(14)

Recall that
a,b>-1/2
and
\beta>0
.

For any
\varphi(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\in C_{0}^{2}((0,\infty)\times{\mathcal{C}})
, the space of continuous functions with continuous derivatives up to second order and with compact support, using Itô’s formula, we get that for any
T>0
,

\displaystyle f(T,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{T})

\displaystyle=f(0,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{i}f(t,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})\sqrt{2}db_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\partial_{t}f(t,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}f(t,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})\partial_{i}\Psi({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}^{2}f(t,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t})\right)dt.

Here
\partial_{t}
(resp.
\partial_{i}
) denotes the partial derivative with respect to
t
(resp.
x_{i}
).
Since
f
has compact support,
f(0,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{0})=0
and
f(T,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{T})=0
when
T
is large enough. Taking the mean values of both sides in the above formula with noting that the diffusion part is a martingale, we get that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\partial_{t}f(t,{\boldsymbol{z}})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}f(t,{\boldsymbol{z}})\partial_{i}\Psi({\boldsymbol{z}})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}^{2}f(t,{\boldsymbol{z}})\right)d\nu_{t}^{[{\boldsymbol{y}}]}(d{\boldsymbol{z}})dt=0,

where
\nu_{t}^{[{\boldsymbol{y}}]}
is the law of the unique strong solution
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
with initial condition
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{y}}\in\bar{\mathcal{C}}
. In other words, the law
\nu_{t}^{[{\boldsymbol{y}}]}
satisfies the following Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial t}=\mathrm{div\,}(\nabla\Psi\mu)+\Delta\,\mu,\quad\mu=(\mu_{t}(dx))_{t>0}

(15)

in a distributional sense with initial condition
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}
.

The function
\Psi
is in fact
K
-convex with

K=\frac{a+b+1}{2}>0,

that is, the function
\Psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})-\frac{K}{2}(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{N}^{2})
is convex, which can be verified by a direct calculation.
Now, from the theory of gradient flows, we deduce that
\nu_{t}^{[{\boldsymbol{y}}]}
is the gradient flow of a
K
-geodesically convex functional in the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with finite second moment endowed with the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
. Refer to the monograph
[
1
]
, especially to Subsection 11.2.1 therein for terminologies. Moreover, the probability measure
\mu_{inv}
on
{\mathcal{C}}
or on
\bar{\mathcal{C}}
with density proportional to
e^{-\Psi({\boldsymbol{x}})}
is the unique invariant measure of the Fokker–Planck equation (
15
) or of the system of SDEs (
12
). Then the
K
-convexity implies the following long time behavior (see
[
1
, §11.2]
)

W_{2}(\nu_{t}^{[{\boldsymbol{y}}]},\mu_{inv})\leq e^{-Kt}W_{2}(\delta_{{\boldsymbol{y}}},\mu_{inv}).

(16)

Note that the push-forward measure of
\mu_{inv}
under the transformation
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\sin^{2}\frac{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}{2}
, is the beta Jacobi ensemble (
1
). Thus, a beta Jacobi process, starting from any initial condition, converges in distribution to the corresponding beta Jacobi ensemble as
t\to\infty
.

3.2
Law of large numbers for the empirical measure process

From now on, we consider stationary beta Jacobi processes. More precisely, we consider the processes
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
solving the system of SDEs (
14
) with initial data
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
being random,
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
measurable, distributed according the Jacobi beta ensemble (
1
), where
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t}=(b_{i}(t))_{i=1}^{N}
is a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
with respect to (w.r.t.) the filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. In other words, the Brownian motion is independent of the initial data.

The law of large numbers for the empirical measure process

\mu_{t}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(t)}

has been established in
[
18
]
by using a moment method for processes.
Let us briefly introduce the result here. Recall the assumption on the parameters that
a,b>-1/2,c>0
and
\beta=2c/N
. For
f\in C^{2}([0,1])
, using Itô’s formula, we deduce that

\displaystyle d\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})db_{i}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})\left(a+1-(a+b+2)\lambda_{i}+\frac{c}{N}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\frac{2\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}\right)dt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{i})dt,

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle

\displaystyle=dM_{t}^{(N)}+\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},(a+1)f^{\prime}(x)-(a+b+2)xf^{\prime}(x)+x(1-x)f^{\prime\prime}(x)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\quad+c\iint\frac{x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)-y(1-y)f^{\prime}(y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)dt

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c}{N}\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\{x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)\}^{\prime}\rangle dt,

(17)

where

M_{t}^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(1-\lambda_{i})}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i})db_{i}

is a martingale with the quadratic variation

[M^{(N)}]_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu_{s}^{(N)},2x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)^{2}\rangle ds.

It is worth noting that the above identity holds when
\{\lambda_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are distinct, which holds for almost every
t
.

For
k=1,2,\dots,
let

S_{k}^{(N)}(t)=\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}(t)^{k}

be the
k
th moment process of
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
. Then equation (
17
) with
f=x^{k}
can be expressed in the following integral form

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle+M_{k}^{(N)}(t)-k(2c+a+b+k+1)\int_{0}^{t}S_{k}^{(N)}(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+k(a+k)\int_{0}^{t}S_{k-1}^{(N)}(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+ck\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}S_{i}^{(N)}(s)S_{k-1-i}^{(N)}(s)ds-ck\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}S_{j}^{(N)}(s)S_{k-j}^{(N)}(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c}{N}\int_{0}^{t}(k^{2}S_{k-1}^{(N)}(s)-k(k+1)S_{k}^{(N)}(s))ds.

(18)

Here
M_{k}^{(N)}
denotes the corresponding martingale part. Equation (
18
) is an initial value ordinary differential equation (ODE) of
\int_{0}^{t}S_{k}^{(N)}(s)ds
, and thus, we can express
S_{k}^{(N)}(t)
as a functional of
\{S_{l}^{(N)}(t)\}_{0\leq l\leq k-1}
and
M_{k}^{(N)}(t)
by using the following fundamental result stated without proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that a continuous function
X(t)
satisfies the equation

X(t)=x_{0}-\gamma\int_{0}^{t}X(s)ds+F(t),\quad(t\geq 0),

where
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\gamma
is a constant and
F(t)
is a continuous function with
F(0)=0
. Then

X(t)=x_{0}e^{-\gamma t}+F(t)-\gamma e^{-\gamma t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma s}F(s)ds,\quad(t\geq 0).

For fixed
T>0
, let
{\mathbb{X}}
be the space
C([0,T])=C([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
of continuous functions on
[0,T]
endowed with the supremum norm. Then
S_{k}^{(N)}
and
M_{k}^{(N)}
are random elements on
{\mathbb{X}}
. We can use induction in
k
to show that
S_{k}^{(N)}
converges in probability to a deterministic limit as
{\mathbb{X}}
-valued random elements. Indeed, by Doob’s martingale inequality, the martingale part
M_{k}^{(N)}
is easily shown to converge in probability to zero in
{\mathbb{X}}
. The initial value
\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle
converges in probability to
\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle
by Theorem
2.3
(LLN). Then, by induction, we deduce that
(S_{k}^{(N)}(t))_{0\leq t\leq T}
converges in probability to a deterministic limit
m_{k}(t)
in
{\mathbb{X}}
. By this approach, the limit
m_{k}(t)
, for
k\geq 1
, satisfies the following ODE

\displaystyle m_{k}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-k(2c+a+b+k+1)m_{k}(t)+k(a+k)m_{k-1}(t)

\displaystyle\quad+ck\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}m_{i}(t)m_{k-1-i}(t)-ck\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}m_{j}(t)m_{k-j}(t),

with initial value
m_{k}(0)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle=\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle.

Here
m_{0}(t)\equiv 1
.

Recall that
u_{k}=\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle
denotes the
k
th moment of
\nu_{c}
. Note that by the stationary property, for any fixed
t
,
S_{k}^{(N)}(t)
converges to
u_{k}
in probability (by Theorem
2.3
(LLN)). Therefore, the limit
m_{k}(t)\equiv u_{k}
is a constant function. Consequently, the moments
\{u_{k}\}
satisfy the following relation

u_{k}=\frac{1}{2c+a+b+k+1}\bigg((a+k)u_{k-1}+c\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}u_{i}u_{k-1-i}-c\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}u_{k-j}\bigg),\quad(k\geq 1).

(19)

In summary, for stationary beta Jacobi point processes, the following law of large numbers at the process level holds.

Theorem 3.2
.

As
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
, the
k
th moment process
S_{k}^{(N)}
, as random element in
C([0,T])
, converges in probability to a constant function
u_{k}=\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle
. Consequently, for any polynomial
f
, as
N\to\infty
with
\beta=2c/N
,

\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]),

(20)

meaning that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\to\infty;\\
\beta=2c/N\end{subarray}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle|\geq\varepsilon\right)=0.

3.3
Central limit theorem for moment processes

In this subsection, we refine a moment method developed in
[
13
]
. The work in
[
13
]
deals with systems of SDEs with trivial initial condition while we consider here stationary processes.
Let

\tilde{S}_{k}^{(N)}(t)=\sqrt{N}\left(S_{k}^{(N)}(t)-u_{k}\right).

Then for each
k
, we deduce from equation (
18
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{k}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle-u_{k})+\sqrt{N}M_{k}^{(N)}(t)-k(2c+a+b+k+1)\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{S}_{k}^{(N)}(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+k(a+k)\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{S}_{k-1}^{(N)}(s)ds

\displaystyle\quad+ck\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(\sqrt{N}S_{i}^{(N)}(s)S_{k-1-i}^{(N)}(s)-\sqrt{N}u_{i}u_{k-1-i}\Big)ds

\displaystyle\quad-ck\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\Big(\sqrt{N}S_{j}^{(N)}(s)S_{k-j}^{(N)}(s)-\sqrt{N}u_{j}u_{k-j}\Big)ds

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}(k^{2}S_{k-1}^{(N)}(s)-k(k+1)S_{k}^{(N)}(s))ds.

(21)

Here we have used the relation (
19
) for
\{u_{k}\}
.
Although the last term depends on
S_{k}^{(N)}
, it is considered as an error term because it vanishes in the limit when
N\to\infty
.

Note that
\mu_{0}^{(N)}
has the same distribution as the empirical distribution of the corresponding Jacobi beta ensemble. Thus Theorem
2.3
(CLT) tells us that
\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle-u_{k})\}_{k\geq 1}
jointly converge in distribution to jointly Gaussian random variables
\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, meaning that for every
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\big\{\sqrt{N}\big(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},x^{k}\rangle\big)\big\}_{k=1}^{M}\overset{d}{\to}\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{M}.

We then define the limit with respect to any polynomial test function as a linear combination of
\{\zeta_{k}\}
, that is, for
f=\sum_{k=0}^{M}a_{k}x^{k}
,

\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle)\overset{d}{\to}\sum_{k=1}^{M}a_{k}\zeta_{k}=:\langle\zeta,f\rangle.

Our next aim is to show that the martingale parts
\{\sqrt{N}M_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
, as random elements in
C([0,T])
, jointly converge in distribution to Gaussian processes
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
chosen to be independent of
\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
. Moreover, the initial values and the martingale parts jointly converge to
\zeta
’s and
\eta
’s. We use the same notation
\overset{d}{\to}
to denote the convergence in distribution of
C([0,T])
-valued random elements.

For a polynomial
f
, let

\Phi^{(f;N)}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\lambda_{i}(s)(1-\lambda_{i}(s))}f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}(s))db_{i}(s).

Then
\Phi^{(f;N)}
is a continuous martingale with
\Phi^{(f;N)}(0)=0
, a.s.. It is clear that for polynomials
f
and
g
, the cross-variation of
\Phi^{(f;N)}(t)
and
\Phi^{(g;N)}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle[\Phi^{(f;N)},\Phi^{(g;N)}]_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}2\lambda_{i}(s)(1-\lambda_{i}(s))f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}(s))g^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}(s))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle\mu^{(N)}_{s},2x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)\rangle ds.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
be polynomials of degree at least
1
. Then
\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}\}_{k=1}^{n}
jointly converge to Gaussian processes
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
of mean zero and covariance

\Ex[\eta_{k}(s)\eta_{l}(t)]=\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)f_{k}^{\prime}(x)f_{l}^{\prime}(x)\rangle\times(s\wedge t).

Here recall that
s\wedge t=\min\{s,t\}
.

Proof.

For any
k
and
l
, as
N\to\infty
, Theorem
3.2
implies that

[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)},\Phi^{(f_{l};N)}]_{t}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)f_{k}^{\prime}(x)f_{l}^{\prime}(x)\rangle\times t,\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]).

In particular, the convergence in probability holds for each
t>0
. Moreover, for each fixed
t
, the sequence
[\Phi^{(f;N)},\Phi^{(g;N)}]_{t}
is uniformly bounded because
\mu_{t}^{(N)}
is supported in
[0,1]
. It follows that
[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)},\Phi^{(f_{l};N)}]_{t}
converges in
L^{q}
for any
q\in[1,\infty)
. Then the desired convergence is a consequence of a general CLT in
[
15
]
for local martingales. The proof is complete.
∎

Let
\{\langle\eta,f\rangle\}
be a family of Gaussian processes indexed by polynomials
f
of mean zero and covariance

\Ex[\langle\eta,f\rangle(s)\langle\eta,g\rangle(t)]=\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)\rangle\times(s\wedge t),

(22)

which is independent of
\{\langle\zeta,f\rangle\}
. In particular, for each polynomial
f
, the Gaussian process
\langle\eta,f\rangle/\sqrt{\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)f^{\prime}(x)^{2}\rangle}
is a standard Brownian motion.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
be polynomials of degree at least
1
. Then

\Big\{\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},f_{k}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f_{k}\rangle)\}_{k=1}^{n},\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}\}_{k=1}^{n}\Big\}\overset{d}{\to}\Big\{\{\langle\zeta,f_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n},\{\langle\eta,f_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n}\Big\}.

We need some preliminaries before proving this lemma. Let
M_{t}
be a continuous martingale w.r.t. the filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t};0\leq t<\infty\}
satisfying
M_{0}=0
, a.s., and
[M]_{\infty}=\infty
, a.s.. Let

T_{t}=\inf\{s:[M]_{s}>t\}.

It is known that (see Theorem V.1.6 in
[
16
]
)

B_{t}=M_{T_{t}}

is a standard Brownian motion w.r.t. the filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{T_{t}};0\leq t<\infty\}
and

M_{t}=B_{[M]_{t}}.

In particular, the Brownian motion
B_{t}
is independent of
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For each
N
, assume that
M_{t}^{(N)}
is a continuous martingales with
M_{t}^{(N)}=0,
a.s. and
[M^{(N)}]_{\infty}=\infty,
a.s.. Assume further that as
N\to\infty
,

[M^{(N)}]_{t}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}m_{t},\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]),

where
m_{t}\in C([0,T])
is non-random.
Let
B^{(N)}_{t}
be the standard Brownian motion associated with the martingale
M^{(N)}_{t}
defined as above. Define a process
A^{(N)}_{t}
as

A^{(N)}_{t}=B^{(N)}_{m_{t}},\quad t\in[0,T].

Then as
N\to\infty
,

M^{(N)}_{t}-A^{(N)}_{t}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0,\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]).

Proof.

For given
\varepsilon>0
, we estimate the probability

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{(N)}_{t}-A^{(N)}_{t}|\geq\varepsilon\right)

as follows. For any
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{(N)}_{t}-A^{(N)}_{t}|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|B^{(N)}_{[M^{(N)}]_{t}}-B^{(N)}_{m_{t}}|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|B^{(N)}_{[M^{(N)}]_{t}}-B^{(N)}_{m_{t}}|\geq\varepsilon,\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|[M^{(N)}]_{t}-m_{t}|<\delta\right)

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|[M^{(N)}]_{t}-m_{t}|\geq\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq m_{T}}\sup_{s^{\prime}\geq 0,|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|B^{(N)}_{s^{\prime}}-B^{(N)}_{s}|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|[M^{(N)}]_{t}-m_{t}|\geq\delta\right).

(23)

By letting
N\to\infty
, we obtain that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{(N)}_{t}-A^{(N)}_{t}|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq m_{T}}\sup_{s^{\prime}\geq 0,|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|B^{(N)}_{s^{\prime}}-B^{(N)}_{s}|\geq\varepsilon\right).

Note that the right hand side of the above equation does not depend on
N
. It remains to show that it converges to zero as
\delta\to 0+
. Now let
B_{t}
be a given standard Brownian motion. Since with probability one, its path is continuous, it follows that as
\delta\to 0+
,

\sup_{0\leq s\leq m_{T}}\sup_{s^{\prime}\geq 0,|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|B_{s^{\prime}}-B_{s}|\to 0,\text{a.s..}

Consequently, for any given
\varepsilon>0
, as
\delta\to 0+
,

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq m_{T}}\sup_{s^{\prime}\geq 0,|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|B^{(N)}_{s^{\prime}}-B^{(N)}_{s}|\geq\varepsilon\right)={\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq s\leq m_{T}}\sup_{s^{\prime}\geq 0,|s-s^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|B_{s^{\prime}}-B_{s}|\geq\varepsilon\right)\to 0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

. For each martingale
\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}
, since
f^{\prime}_{k}
is a non-zero polynomial, it follows that with probability one,

[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}]_{\infty}=\infty.

Recall that

[\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}]_{t}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)(f_{k}^{\prime}(x))^{2}\rangle\times t,\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]).

Thus,
\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}
satisfies the assumptions in Lemma
3.5
.
Denote by
A^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}
the corresponding process introduced in Lemma
3.5
. Then as proved in Lemma
3.5
,

\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}-A^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}0,\quad\text{in}\quad C([0,T]).

(24)

Consequently, the processes
\{A^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
jointly converge in distribution to the same limits
\{\langle\eta,f_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n}
as
\{\Phi^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
do. In addition, the processes
\{A^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are independent of the initial data
\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},f_{k}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f_{k}\rangle)\}_{k=1}^{n}
. Thus, the following joint convergence holds,

\Big\{\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},f_{k}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f_{k}\rangle)\}_{k=1}^{n},\{A^{(f_{k};N)}_{t}\}_{k=1}^{n}\Big\}\overset{d}{\to}\Big\{\{\langle\zeta,f_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n},\{\langle\eta,f_{k}\rangle\}_{k=1}^{n}\Big\}.

Finally, we can replace
A
’s by
\Phi
’s because of (
24
). The proof is complete.
∎

Equation (
21
), together with the above result on the joint convergence of initial data and martingale parts implies the following result.

Theorem 3.6
.

There are Gaussian processes
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
such that for each
n=1,2,\dots,
as
N\to\infty
,

\tilde{S}_{n}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\to}\xi_{n},

as
C([0,T])
-valued random elements.
The joint convergence also holds. Consequently, there is a family of Gaussian processes
\langle\xi,f\rangle
indexed by polynomials
f
constructed from
\zeta
’s and
\eta
’s such that for any polynomial
f

\sqrt{N}\left(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle\right)\overset{d}{\to}\langle\xi,f\rangle.

Theorem (
3.6
) can be proved by using induction with the help of Lemma
3.7
below. We skip its proof and the proof of Lemma
3.7
because arguments are almost the same as those used in
[
13
]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that
\tilde{S}_{n_{1}}^{(N)}
and
\tilde{S}_{n_{2}}^{(N)}
jointly converge in distribution to
\xi_{n_{1}}
and
\xi_{n_{2}}
as
C([0,T])
-valued random elements. Then

\sqrt{N}(S_{n_{1}}^{(N)}S_{n_{2}}^{(N)}-u_{n_{1}}u_{n_{2}})\overset{d}{\to}u_{n_{1}}\xi_{n_{2}}+u_{n_{2}}\xi_{n_{1}}.

In fact, the family
\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle)\}
, martingale parts
\{\Phi^{(f;N)}\}
and initial data
\{\sqrt{N}(\langle\mu_{0}^{(N)},f\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},f\rangle)\}
jointly converge in distribution to
\xi
’s,
\eta
’s and
\zeta
’s. Let
P
be a polynomial and
p=P^{\prime}
. For convenience, we rewrite the formula (
17
) with
P=f
as follows

\displaystyle d\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},P\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}d\Phi^{(P;N)}(t)+\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},(a+1)p(x)-(a+b+2)xp(x)+x(1-x)p^{\prime}(x)\rangle dt

\displaystyle\quad+c\iint\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)dt

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{c}{N}\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},\{x(1-x)p(x)\}^{\prime}\rangle dt.

(25)

Since
p
is a polynomial, it is clear that the integrand of the above double integral can be written as a linear combination of
x^{k}y^{l}
, that is,

\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}=\sum_{\text{finite}}c_{k,l}x^{k}y^{l}.

Thus, that double integral is a linear combination of
\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{k}\rangle\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},x^{l}\rangle
. Consequently, its limit as
N\to\infty
exists

\displaystyle\iint\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(x)d\mu_{t}^{(N)}(y)

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}\iint\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(x)d\nu_{c}(y),\quad\text{in $C([0,T])$}.

Now write equation (
25
) in the integral form and then let
N\to\infty
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle\nu_{c},P\rangle^{\prime}(=0)

\displaystyle=\langle\nu_{c},(a+1)p(x)-(a+b+2)xp(x)+x(1-x)p^{\prime}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+c\iint\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(x)d\nu_{c}(y).

Using this identity, again we write the equation (
25
) in the integral form, subtract each term by the corresponding limit when
N\to\infty
, multiply it by
\sqrt{N}
and then let
N\to\infty
, we arrive at the following relation

\langle\xi,P\rangle(t)=\langle\zeta,P\rangle+\langle\eta,P\rangle(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,{\mathcal{L}}(p)\right\rangle(s)ds,

(26)

where
{\mathcal{L}}
is a transformation which maps a polynomial
p(x)
to a polynomial

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(p)(x)

\displaystyle:=2c\int\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+(a+1)p(x)-(a+b+2)xp(x)+x(1-x)p^{\prime}(x).

(27)

Here we have used Lemma
3.7
to deal with the double integral term.

It follows from the covariance formula (
22
) that when we take primitives
\{P_{n}\}
of orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
with respect to the measure
2x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)dx
, the limiting Gaussian processes
\{\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle\}
are independent.

In Sect.
4
, the sequence of orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
(w.r.t.
2x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)dx
) is taken by using the three term recurrence relation (
42
).
Let
P_{n}
be a primitive of
p_{n}
. Then Proposition
4.4
shows that

{\mathcal{L}}(p_{n})=-\gamma_{n}P_{n}+const,\quad\gamma_{n}=(n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2).

Thus the relation (
26
) becomes

\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t)=\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle+\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle(t)-\gamma_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,P_{n}\right\rangle(s)ds.

Solving this ODE with respect to
\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle\xi,P_{n}\right\rangle(s)ds
(see Lemma
3.1
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle e^{-\gamma_{n}t}+\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle(t)-\gamma_{n}e^{-\gamma_{n}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma_{n}s}\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle(s)ds

\displaystyle=\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle e^{-\gamma_{n}t}+e^{-\gamma_{n}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma_{n}s}d\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle(s).

(28)

Here the last integral is a stochastic integral with respect to the Brownian motion
\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle
. We arrive at the main result in this section.

Theorem 3.8
.

The following hold.

(i)

For each
n
, the limiting Gaussian process
\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle
has the same distribution with the following process

\zeta e^{-\gamma_{n}t}+\alpha_{n}e^{-\gamma_{n}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma_{n}s}dw(s),\quad(\alpha_{n}^{2}=\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)p_{n}(x)^{2}\rangle),

where
w
is a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}
independent of a Gaussian random variable
\zeta\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}}{2\gamma_{n}})
.

(ii)

The limiting Gaussian processes
\{\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle\}_{n\geq 0}
are independent.
In other words,
\big\{\sqrt{N}\big(\langle\mu_{t}^{(N)},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},P_{n}\rangle\big)\big\}_{n\geq 0}
jointly converge to independent Gaussian processes.

Remark 3.9
.

Let
\nu_{c}^{*}(x)dx=(Z_{c}^{*})^{-1}x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)dx
be a probability measure, where
Z_{c}^{*}=\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle
is the normalizing constant. Then
\{p_{n}\}
are orthogonal with respect to
\nu_{c}^{*}
. Moreover, with notations in Sect.
4
,
\alpha_{n}^{2}
has the following explicit expression

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\nu_{c},2x(1-x)p_{n}(x)^{2}\rangle={2}{\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle}\langle\nu_{c}^{*},p_{n}(x)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{*}_{i}\mu^{*}_{i-1}.

Proof.

(i) Recall that the covariance formula (
22
) implies that
\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle/\alpha_{n}
is a standard Brownian motion. Thus, it suffices to identify the variance of
\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle
. We first calculate the variance of
\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t)
from the formula (
28
) as

\displaystyle\Ex[(\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\Ex[\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle^{2}]e^{-2\gamma_{n}t}+\alpha_{n}^{2}e^{-2\gamma_{n}t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\gamma_{n}s}ds

\displaystyle=\Ex[\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle^{2}]e^{-2\gamma_{n}t}+\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}}{2\gamma_{n}}-\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}}{2\gamma_{n}}e^{-2\gamma_{n}t}.

In addition the stationary property implies that the variance of
\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t)
does not depend on
t
. It then follows that

\Ex[\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle^{2}]=\frac{\alpha_{n}^{2}}{2\gamma_{n}}.

(ii) We show that
\Cov(\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle,\langle\zeta,P_{m}\rangle)=0
, for
n\neq m
. Indeed, for
n\neq m
, it follows from the relation (
28
) for
\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle
and
\langle\xi,P_{m}\rangle
that

\Cov(\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t),\langle\xi,P_{m}\rangle(t))=\Cov(\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle,\langle\zeta,P_{m}\rangle)e^{-(\gamma_{n}+\gamma_{m})t}.

Here we have used the fact that
\langle\eta,P_{m}\rangle
and
\langle\eta,P_{n}\rangle
are independent Gaussian processes independent of
\{\langle\zeta,P_{m}\rangle,\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle\}
. Again, the stationary property implies that

\Cov(\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle,\langle\zeta,P_{m}\rangle)=\Cov(\langle\xi,P_{n}\rangle(t),\langle\xi,P_{m}\rangle(t))\equiv 0.

The covariances are zero for
n\neq m
implies that
\{\langle\zeta,P_{n}\rangle\}
are independent because they are jointly Gaussian random variables. The desired result follows immediately, The proof is complete.
∎

4
Associated Jacobi polynomials

4.1
Orthogonal polynomials

Let us introduce some concepts and results without proofs on orthogonal polynomials. We refer readers to
[
5
]
or
[
17
]
for proofs and more explanations.. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
having all finite moments, that is,

\int x^{n}d\mu(x)<\infty,\quad\text{for all $n=1,2,\dots.$}

Assume for instance that
\mu
is supported on an infinite set. Then the monomials
\{1,x,x^{2},\dots\}
are linearly independent in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mu)
. Let

p_{n}=x^{n}+\text{lower order terms},\quad(n=0,1,2,\dots)

be the sequence of monic polynomials resulting from the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization process of the monomials with respect to the usual inner product in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mu)
. Then
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfy the three term recurrence relation

\begin{cases}p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}=x-a_{1},\\
p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-a_{n+1}p_{n}-b_{n}^{2}p_{n-1},\quad n\geq 1,\end{cases}

(29)

for real numbers
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and positive numbers
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. The (infinite) tridiagonal matrix
J
formed from the two sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}

J=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

(30)

is called the Jacobi matrix of
\mu
. A symmetric tridiagonal matrix itself is called a Jacobi matrix.

Conversely, given two sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of real numbers and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of positive numbers, or given an (infinite) Jacobi matrix
J
of the form (
30
), there is a probability measure
\mu
satisfying

\langle\mu,x^{n}\rangle=J^{n}(1,1),\quad n=0,1,2,\dots.

(31)

Then the sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
defined by the three term recurrence relation (
29
) are orthogonal with respect to
\mu

\int p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)d\mu(x)=\begin{cases}b_{1}^{2}\cdots b_{n}^{2},&n=m,\\
0,&n\neq m.\end{cases}

Consequently, the polynomials
\tilde{p}_{n}=p_{n}/(b_{1}\cdots b_{n})
are orthonormal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mu)
. Note that the sequence
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}
satisfies the following relation

\begin{cases}\tilde{p}_{0}=1,\quad b_{1}\tilde{p}_{1}=x-a_{1},\\
b_{b+1}\tilde{p}_{n+1}=x\tilde{p}_{n}-a_{n+1}\tilde{p}_{n}-b_{n}\tilde{p}_{n-1},\quad n\geq 1.\end{cases}

(32)

A probability measure
\mu
satisfying the moment condition (
31
) exists but may not be unique in general. In case of uniqueness, it is called the spectral measure of the Jacobi matrix
J
. A useful sufficient condition for the uniqueness is given by (see Corollary 3.8.9 in
[
17
]
)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{b_{n}}=\infty.

In particular, the uniqueness holds when the sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is bounded.

The finite case is much more straightforward. Let

J=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&b_{N-1}&a_{N}\par\end{pmatrix},\quad(a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}},b_{i}>0),

be a Jacobi matrix of size
N
. Then
J
has
N
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
. Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{N}
be the corresponding normalized eigenvectors. In this case, the spectral measure
\mu
of
J
which satisfies the moment relation (
31
) has the following form

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{N}|v_{i}(1)|^{2}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}.

The three term relation is defined up to
N
,

\begin{cases}p_{0}=1,\quad p_{1}=x-a_{1},\\
p_{n+1}=xp_{n}-a_{n+1}p_{n}-b_{n}^{2}p_{n-1},\quad n=1,\dots,N-1,\end{cases}

with
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
orthogonal polynomials and
p_{N}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x-\lambda_{i})=0
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\mu)
.

4.2
Beta Jacobi ensembles and duals of Jacobi polynomials

In this subsection, we consider the limiting behavior of beta Jacobi ensembles in a low temperature regime, that is, the regime where
N
is fixed,
(\beta,a,b)=(2\kappa,A\kappa,B\kappa)
with
\kappa\to\infty
, for fixed
A,B>0
. Recall that
L_{N,\beta}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta}
denote the empirical distribution and the spectral measure of the random tridiagonal matrix
J_{N,\beta}
.

In this regime, it follows from the asymptotic behavior of beta distributions that

J_{N,\beta}\to T_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{c_{1}}\\
\sqrt{d_{1}}&\sqrt{c_{2}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&\sqrt{d_{N-1}}&\sqrt{c_{N}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{c_{1}}&\sqrt{d_{1}}\\
&\sqrt{c_{2}}&\sqrt{d_{2}}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&\sqrt{c_{N}}\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-N-A}{2-2N-A-B},

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-N-A}{2n-2N-A-B}\frac{n-N-A-B}{2n-2N-A-B-1},\quad n\geq 2,

\displaystyle d_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-N}{2n-2N-A-B+1}\frac{n-N-B}{2n-2N-A-B},\quad n\geq 1.

Namely, each entry of
J_{N,\beta}
converges in probability and in
L^{q}
, for any
q\in[1,\infty)
, to the corresponding entry of
T_{N}
. Since the size
N
is fixed, it follows that for any
k=1,2,\dots,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(i,i)\to\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_{N})^{k}(i,i),\quad(J_{N,\beta})^{k}(1,1)\to(T_{N})^{k}(1,1),

(33)

in probability and in
L^{q}
for any
q\in[1,\infty)
. Recall from the identity (
5
) that the two random variables in the above expression have the same mean value.
Consequently, the two limits are identical

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(T_{N})^{k}(i,i)=(T_{N})^{k}(1,1),\quad\text{for all $k=1,2,\dots$.}

Let
z_{1}<z_{2}<\cdots<z_{N}
be the eigenvalues of
T_{N}
. The above relation implies that the spectral measure
\mu_{N}
of
T_{N}
coincides with the empirical measure
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{i}}
. Here the spectral measure
\mu_{N}
is the unique probability measure satisfying

\langle\mu_{N},x^{k}\rangle=(T_{N})^{k}(1,1),\quad k=0,1,2,\dots.

Then the convergence of moments (
33
) implies that both
L_{N,\beta}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{N,\beta}
converge weakly to
\mu_{N}
, in probability.

Beta Jacobi ensembles at low temperature can also be studied by analyzing the joint density
[
11
]
. Note that beta Jacobi ensembles considered in
[
11
]
are ensembles of points in the interval
[-1,1]
. Their results will be translated to fit our setting as follows. We rewrite the joint density in the following form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\times\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}|^{2\kappa}\prod_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{A\kappa}(1-\lambda_{l})^{B\kappa}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\times\bigg(\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})\prod_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{\frac{A}{2}}(1-\lambda_{l})^{\frac{B}{2}}\bigg)^{2\kappa},\quad(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\bar{W_{C}},

(34)

where
\bar{W_{C}}
is the closure of

{W_{C}}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}:0<x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N}<1\}.

Let the function
\phi\colon{W_{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined as

\phi(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})=\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})\prod_{l=1}^{N}\lambda_{l}^{\frac{A}{2}}(1-\lambda_{l})^{\frac{B}{2}},\quad(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{N})\in\bar{W_{C}}.

For
A,B>0
, one can show that
\phi
has a unique maximum in
\bar{W_{C}}
at an inner point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in{W_{C}}
. Let
X_{\kappa}=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})
be a random vector distributed according to the beta Jacobi ensemble (
34
). Then by a well-known Laplace method (see
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
), as
\kappa\to\infty
,

X_{\kappa}\overset{{\mathbb{P}}}{\to}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N}).

Consequently, the empirical distribution
L_{N,\beta}
converges weakly to
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{y_{i}}
, in probability. As a byproduct, we deduce that
(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
.

In addition, from a classical theory of Jacobi polynomials, the points
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{N}\}
are the zeros of the
N
th Jacobi polynomials
P_{N}^{(A-1,B-1)}
with parameters
A-1
and
B-1
. Here Jacobi polynomials
\{P_{n}^{(A-1,B-1)}\}_{n\geq 0}
are monic polynomials orthogonal with respect to the beta distribution with parameters
A,B>0
, or the probability measure with density proportional to
x^{A-1}(1-x)^{B-1},x\in(0,1)
. It is also known that the Jacobi matrix of the beta distribution with parameters
A,B>0
is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}\\
\sqrt{\mu_{1}}&\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\lambda_{0}}&\sqrt{\mu_{1}}\\
&\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}&\sqrt{\mu_{2}}\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\qquad\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}=\frac{n+A}{2n+A+B}\frac{n+A+B-1}{2n+A+B-1},&n\geq 0,\\
\mu_{n}=\frac{n}{2n+A+B-1}\frac{n+B-1}{2n+A+B-2},&n\geq 1,\\
a_{1}=\lambda_{0},&\\
a_{n}=\lambda_{n}+\mu_{n},&n\geq 2,\\
b_{n}^{2}=\lambda_{n-1}\mu_{n},&n\geq 1.\end{cases}

Using such Jacobi matrix, Jacobi polynomials
\{P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
can be defined by

\displaystyle P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{0}=1,\quad P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{1}=x-a_{1},

\displaystyle P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n+1}=xP^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n}-a_{n+1}P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n}-b_{n}^{2}P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n-1},\quad n\geq 1.

Let
H_{[N]}
be the top left submatrix of size
N\times N
of the semi-infinite matrix
H
,

H_{[N]}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&b_{N-1}&a_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

Then it follows from the three term recurrence relation that

P_{N}^{(A-1,B-1)}(x)=\det(x-H_{[N]}).

Thus, the zeros
(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
of
P_{N}^{(A-1,B-1)}
are the eigenvalues of
H_{[N]}
.

Now let
T^{*}_{N}
be the matrix obtained by reversing the parameters in
H_{[N]}
, that is,

T^{*}_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{*}&b_{1}^{*}\\
b_{1}^{*}&a_{2}^{*}&b_{2}^{*}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&b_{N-1}^{*}&a_{N}^{*}\end{pmatrix},\quad a_{i}^{*}=a_{N-i+1},b_{i}^{*}=b_{N-i}.

We define orthogonal polynomials
\{Q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
with respect to
T^{*}_{N}
as

\displaystyle Q_{0}=1,\quad Q_{1}=x-a_{N},

\displaystyle Q_{n+1}=xQ_{n}-a_{N-n}Q_{n}-b_{N-n}^{2}Q_{n-1},\quad n=1,\dots,N-2.

The polynomials
\{Q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
are called the duals of Jacobi polynomials
\{P^{(A-1,B-1)}_{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
. On the one hand, they are orthogonal with respect to the spectral measure of
T_{N}^{*}
. On the other hand, they are orthogonal w.r.t. the probability measure

\mu^{*}_{N}=\frac{1}{Z^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}(1-y_{i})\delta_{y_{i}}.

where
Z^{*}
is a normalizing constant
[
19
]
(see also
[
2
]
). Thus, the measure
\mu^{*}_{N}
is the spectral measure of
T_{N}^{*}
. Then we derive the following relation between
T_{N}
and
T_{N}^{*}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
n=0,1,2,\dots,
it holds that

(T^{*}_{N})^{n}(1,1)=\frac{(T_{N})^{n+1}(1,1)-(T_{N})^{n+2}(1,1)}{T_{N}(1,1)-(T_{N})^{2}(1,1)}.

(35)

Proof.

Recall that the measure

\mu_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{i}}

is the spectral measure of
T_{N}
, and the measure

\mu_{N}^{*}=\frac{1}{Z^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}(1-z_{i})\delta_{z_{i}}

is the spectral measure of
T_{N}^{*}
. (Because
(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
.) The normalizing constant
Z^{*}
is given by

Z^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}(1-z_{i})=N\langle\mu_{N},x-x^{2}\rangle.

From the definition of spectral measures, we see that for any
n=0,1,\dots,
,

(T_{N}^{*})^{n}(1,1)=\langle\mu_{N}^{*},x^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{Z^{*}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}(1-z_{i})z_{i}^{n}=\frac{\langle\mu_{N},x^{n+1}-x^{n+2}\rangle}{\langle\mu_{N},x-x^{2}\rangle},

and any
k=0,1,\dots
,

\langle\mu_{N},x^{k}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}z_{i}^{k}=(T_{N})^{k}(1,1).

From those, the relation (
35
) immediately follows. The proof is complete.
∎

4.3
The limiting measure in a high temperature regime

We first recall arguments in
[
18
]
which were used to derive the Jacobi matrix of the limiting measure
\nu_{c}
in the regime where
\beta N\to 2c\in(0,\infty)
.
Let
m_{k}(N,\kappa,a,b)
be the expected value of the
k
th moment of the empirical distribution
L_{N,\beta}
, that is,

m_{k}(N,\kappa,a,b)=\Ex[\langle L_{N},x^{k}\rangle]=\Ex\left[\frac{1}{N}\tr((J_{N,2\kappa})^{k})\right]=\Ex[(J_{N,2\kappa})^{k}(1,1)].

Here
\kappa=\beta/2
.
Then from formulas for moments of the beta distribution, we see that
m_{k}
can be defined for any
N,\kappa,a
and
b
. The function
m_{k}
satisfies the following duality relation

m_{k}(N,\kappa,a,b)=m_{k}(-\kappa N,1/\kappa,-a/\kappa,-b/\kappa),

(36)

(see
[
7
, Appendix A]
and
[
10
, Eq. (4.15)]
).

Next, we form an infinite matrix
J_{c}
by changing
N\leftrightarrow-c,A\leftrightarrow-a,B\leftrightarrow-b
in the matrix
T_{N}
, that is,

J_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\lambda}_{0}&\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}_{0}\mu_{1}}\\
\sqrt{\hat{\lambda}_{0}\mu_{1}}&\lambda_{1}+\mu_{1}&\sqrt{\lambda_{1}\mu_{2}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

where

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{c+a+1}{2c+a+b+2},

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+c+a+1}{2n+2c+a+b+2}\frac{n+c+a+b+1}{2n+2c+a+b+1},\quad n\geq 0,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+c}{2n+2c+a+b+1}\frac{n+c+b}{2n+2c+a+b},\quad n\geq 0.

Then the duality relation implies that the limit of the empirical distribution
L_{N,\beta}
in the regime where
\beta N\to 2c
coincides with the spectral measure of
J_{c}
. In other words, the limiting measure
\nu_{c}
is the spectral measure of
J_{c}
.
The density of
\nu_{c}
was explicitly calculated in
[
18
]
. It is viewed as Model III of associated Jacobi polynomials.

In the same manner, we now define an infinite Jacobi matrix
J_{c}^{*}
by changing
N\leftrightarrow-c,A\leftrightarrow-a,B\leftrightarrow-b
in the matrix
T_{N}^{*}
, that is,

J^{*}_{c}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{0}^{*}+\mu_{0}^{*}&\sqrt{\mu_{0}^{*}\lambda_{1}^{*}}\\
\sqrt{\mu_{0}^{*}\lambda_{1}^{*}}&\lambda_{1}^{*}+\mu_{1}^{*}&\sqrt{\mu_{1}^{*}\lambda_{2}^{*}}\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

(37)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+c+a+1}{2n+2c+a+b+2}\frac{n+c+a+b+2}{2n+2c+a+b+3},

(38)

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{n+c+1}{2n+2c+a+b+3}\frac{n+c+b+2}{2n+2c+a+b+4},\quad n\geq 0.

(39)

Lemma 4.2
.

The matrix
J^{*}_{c}
is the Jacobi matrix of the probability measure

\nu^{*}_{c}=(Z_{c}^{*})^{-1}x(1-x)\nu_{c}(x)dx,\quad\text{with}\quad Z_{c}^{*}=\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle=\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1}).

(40)

Proof.

The relation (
35
) in Lemma
4.1
implies that for any
n=0,1,\dots,

(J^{*}_{c})^{n}(1,1)=\frac{(J_{c})^{n+1}(1,1)-(J_{c})^{n+2}(1,1)}{J_{c}(1,1)-(J_{c})^{2}(1,1)}.

Thus, the measure
\nu^{*}_{c}
defined in (
40
) satisfies

\langle\nu^{*}_{c},x^{n}\rangle=\frac{\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)x^{n}\rangle}{\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle}=\frac{(J_{c})^{n+1}(1,1)-(J_{c})^{n+2}(1,1)}{J_{c}(1,1)-(J_{c})^{2}(1,1)}=(J^{*}_{c})^{n}(1,1),\quad n=0,1,\dots.

Therefore,
\nu^{*}_{c}
is the spectral measure of
J_{c}^{*}
. The lemma is proved.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

By a direct calculation, we see that the Jacobi matrix
J_{c}^{*}
coincides with the Jacobi matrix of Model I of associated Jacobi polynomials (see the matrix
J_{I}
in
[
18
, §4]
) with parameters
(c,a+1,b+1)
. This fact is similar to what happens in the Laguerre case (see
[
13
]
).

Recall that the transformation
{\mathcal{L}}
is defined on polynomials as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(p)(x)

\displaystyle:=2c\int\frac{x(1-x)p(x)-y(1-y)p(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+(a+1)p(x)-(a+b+2)xp(x)+x(1-x)p^{\prime}(x).

(41)

Let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\nu_{c}^{*}
which are defined by using the Jacobi matrix
J^{*}_{c}
as

\begin{cases}p_{0}(x)=1,\quad p_{1}(x)=x-a_{1}^{*},\\
p_{n+1}(x)=xp_{n}(x)-a_{n+1}^{*}p_{n}(x)-(b_{n}^{*})^{2}p_{n-1}(x),\quad n=1,2,\dots,\end{cases}

(42)

where
a_{n}^{*}=\lambda_{n-1}^{*}+\mu_{n-1}^{*}
and
b_{n}^{*}=\sqrt{\mu_{n-1}^{*}\lambda_{n}^{*}}
.
Results on the Gaussian case and the Laguerre case suggest the following.

Proposition 4.4
.

It holds that

{\mathcal{L}}(p_{n})=-\gamma_{n}P_{n}+const,\quad\gamma_{n}=(n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2),

(43)

where
P_{n}
is a primitive of
p_{n}
, that is,
P_{n}^{\prime}=p_{n}
.

Proof.

Define

q_{n}(x)=\int\frac{p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu^{*}_{c}(y).

Then
\{q_{n}\}
satisfy the same three term recurrence relation as
\{p_{n}\}
but with different initial conditions (
q_{0}=0,q_{1}=1
). We observe that

\displaystyle 2c\int\frac{x(1-x)p_{n}(x)-y(1-y)p_{n}(y)}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y)

\displaystyle=2c\int\frac{(x(1-x)-y(1-y))p_{n}(x)+y(1-y)(p_{n}(x)-p_{n}(y))}{x-y}d\nu_{c}(y)

\displaystyle=2c(1-x-\hat{\lambda}_{0})p_{n}(x)+2c\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1})q_{n}(x).

Using this, the desired identity (
43
) is equivalent to the following by taking the derivative

\displaystyle(2c(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0})+a+2)p_{n}^{\prime}+2c\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1})q_{n}^{\prime}-(2c+a+b+4)xp_{n}^{\prime}+x(1-x)p_{n}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle\quad((n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2)-(2c+a+b+2))p_{n}=0.

We claim that this relation can be shown by induction using recurrence relations for
\{p_{n}\}
and
\{q_{n}\}
. The arguments are direct but rather long, so that we omit the detail.
∎

5
Proof of the main result

Theorem
1.1
is in fact a direct consequence of Theorem
3.8
. Note that Theorem
3.8
states results on orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
while Theorem
1.1
states results on orthonormal polynomials
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}
. Theorem
3.8
implies that

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N,\beta},P_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},P_{n}\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{P_{n}}^{2}),

where

\sigma_{P_{n}}^{2}=\frac{\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)p_{n}^{2}\rangle}{(n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2)},

because
L_{N,\beta}
and
\mu_{0}^{(N)}
have the same distribution. From the relation between
\nu_{c}
and
\nu_{c}^{*}
, we see that

\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)p_{n}^{2}\rangle=\langle\nu_{c},x(1-x)\rangle\langle\nu_{c}^{*},p_{n}^{2}\rangle=\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1})\langle\nu_{c}^{*},p_{n}^{2}\rangle.

Note that
\tilde{p}_{n}=p_{n}/(\langle\nu_{c}^{*},p_{n}^{2}\rangle)^{1/2}
. Thus, if we take
\tilde{P}_{n}=P_{n}/(\langle\nu_{c}^{*},p_{n}^{2}\rangle)^{1/2}
, we get that

\sqrt{N}\Big(\langle L_{N,\beta},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle-\langle\nu_{c},\tilde{P}_{n}\rangle\Big)\overset{d}{\to}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{n}}^{2}),\quad\sigma_{\tilde{P}_{n}}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\lambda}_{0}(1-\hat{\lambda}_{0}-\mu_{1})}{(n+1)(n+2c+a+b+2)}.

The first statement in Theorem
1.1
follows immediately by substituting the formulae of
\hat{\lambda}_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
. The second statement in Theorem
1.1
was already included in the proof of Theorem
3.8
. The proof is complete. ∎
