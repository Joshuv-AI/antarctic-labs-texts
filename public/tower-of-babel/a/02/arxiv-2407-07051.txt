Abstract
For a fixed graph property Φ \Phi and integer k ≥ 1 k\geq 1 , consider the problem of counting the induced k k -vertex subgraphs satisfying Φ \Phi in an input graph G G . This problem can be solved by brute-force in time O ⁡ ( n k ) O(n^{k}) . Under ETH, we prove several lower bounds on the optimal exponent in this running time: • If Φ \Phi is edge-monotone (i.e., closed under deleting edges), then ETH rules out n o ⁡ ( k ) n^{o(k)} time algorithms for this problem. This strengthens a recent lower bound by Döring, Marx and Wellnitz [STOC 2024]. Our result also holds for counting modulo fixed primes. • If at most ( 2 − ε ) ( k 2 ) (2-\varepsilon)^{\binom{k}{2}} graphs on k k vertices satisfy Φ \Phi , for some ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , then ETH also rules out an exponent of o ⁡ ( k ) o(k) . This holds even when the graphs in Φ \Phi have arbitrary individual weights, generalizing previous results for hereditary properties by Focke and Roth [SIAM J. Comput. 2024]. • If Φ \Phi is non-trivial and excludes β Φ \beta_{\Phi} edge-densities, then the optimal exponent under ETH is Ω ⁡ ( β Φ ) \Omega(\beta_{\Phi}) . This holds even when the graphs in Φ \Phi have arbitrary individual weights, generalizing previous results by Roth, Schmitt and Wellnitz [SIAM J. Comput. 2024]. In all cases, we also obtain #W[1] -hardness if k k is part of the input and considered as the parameter. We also obtain lower bounds on the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension. As opposed to the nontrivial techniques from combinatorics, group theory, and simplicial topology used before, our results follow from a relatively straightforward “algebraization” of the problem in terms of polynomials, combined with applications of simple algebraic facts, which can also be interpreted in terms of Fourier analysis. Most of the #W[1] -hardness results known in the area are subsumed by our paper, and our hardness results often hold in a more general setting.



1
Introduction

Counting small patterns in graphs is of fundamental importance in computer science, with practical applications in network analysis and bioinformatics
[
40
]
.
From a theoretical perspective, pattern counting problems can be formalized in the following ways:

1.

For a
fixed pattern

H
, consider the function
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
that, on input a graph
G
, outputs the number
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to G)
of induced
H
-copies in
G
. An example is the number of triangles in
G
.
Each such function can be computed in polynomial time, and we wish to use hardness assumptions such as the Exponential-Time Hypothesis (ETH)
[
30
]
to bound the exponent of this running time, depending on
H
.

2.

We may also view both
H
and
G
as input when computing
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to G)
and then consider
k\coloneqq|V(H)|
as a parameter, in the sense of
parameterized complexity

[
16
,
24
]
.
Here, we seek fixed-parameter tractable algorithms with running time
f(k)\cdot n^{O(1)}
for computable functions
f
.
Similar to the classical notion of
#P
-hardness, the notion of
#W[1]
-hardness in parameterized complexity rules out such algorithms.

In the fixed-pattern perspective, it is known that ETH implies a constant
\alpha>0
such that, for every
k
-vertex graph
H
, the number
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
cannot be computed in time
O(n^{\alpha\cdot k})

[
8
,
21
]
.
Note that this lower bound holds for every pattern
H
.

From the parameterized perspective, the problem of counting induced
H
-copies in graphs
G
with parameter
|V(H)|
is
#W[1]
-hard, since it subsumes the canonical
#W[1]
-hard problem of counting
k
-cliques
[
24
]
.
For a more refined picture, we consider the problems
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\mathcal{H})
for fixed recursively enumerable graph classes
\mathcal{H}
:
Here, the input is a graph
H\in\mathcal{H}
and a graph
G
, the output is
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to G)
, and the parameter is
|V(H)|
. Every such problem is trivially polynomial-time solvable if
\mathcal{H}
is finite; otherwise it is
#W[1]
-complete
[
9
]
.

1.1
Counting Induced Subgraphs Satisfying a Fixed Property

Rather than counting induced
H
-copies for fixed
H
, one may be interested in fixing a
set

\Phi
of
k
-vertex graphs and counting induced subgraphs of
G
that are isomorphic to some graph
H\in\Phi
.
We denote this value by
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)
.
The systematic study of such counting problems was initiated by Jerrum and Meeks
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
:
Given a fixed graph property
\Phi
, they define the parameterized problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
that asks, on input
(G,k)
, to determine the number
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to G)
of induced
k
-vertex subgraphs of
G
that satisfy
\Phi
.
Here, the
slice

\Phi^{(k)}
denotes the restriction of
\Phi
to graphs on exactly
k
vertices, while
\Phi
itself may contain graphs of arbitrary sizes.

It has been shown that
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is either fixed-parameter tractable or
#W[1]
-hard, for every computable property
\Phi

[
12
]
, with an explicit tractability criterion.
However, this criterion is indirect, meaning it is often highly nontrivial to determine whether a given property
\Phi
meets it.
In particular, the criterion has not helped in identifying nontrivial tractable properties or ruling out their existence.
To elaborate, if all but finitely many slices
\Phi^{(k)}
are trivial (we also say
k
-trivial) in the sense that they are empty or contain all
k
-vertex graphs, then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is easy to compute.
Such properties
\Phi
are called
meager
; for all other properties, it was conjectured that
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard
[
20
,
25
,
54
]
; see also
[
51
]
for a recent survey.

It was shown very recently that this conjecture does not hold in the full generality stated
[
13
]
.
Nevertheless, the conjecture has been verified for several very natural classes of properties; see
[
21
, Section 1.1]
.
We list some of these known results; whenever we say in the following that “ETH implies an exponent of
\Omega(f(k))
”, we mean that ETH implies a constant
\alpha>0
such that
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to G)
cannot be computed in time
O(n^{\alpha\cdot f(k)})
on
n
-vertex graphs, for an infinite set of integers
k
. In the following, we assume
\Phi
to be computable.

1.

If
\Phi
is closed under deleting edges (i.e., edge-monotone)
[
21
]
or closed under deleting vertices (i.e., hereditary)
[
25
]
, then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard. This strengthens an earlier result that required
\Phi
to satisfy both closure properties
[
54
]
.
It has also been shown that ETH implies an exponent of
\Omega(\sqrt{\log k}/\log\log k)
for edge-monotone properties, and an exponent of
\Omega(k)
for hereditary properties.

2.

Given
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\beta_{\Phi}(k)
be the number of edge-counts avoided by
\Phi
on
k
-vertex graphs.
If there is an infinite subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
on which
\Phi
is slice-wise nontrivial and
\beta_{\Phi}
grows like
\omega(k)
, then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard
[
54
]
.
Moreover, ETH implies an exponent of
\Omega(\beta_{\Phi}(k)/\sqrt{\log\beta_{\Phi}(k)})
.

3.

If
\Phi(H)
depends only on
|E(H)|
, then the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard and ETH implies an exponent of
\Omega(k/\sqrt{\log k})

[
54
]
.

4.

If there are infinitely many prime powers
t
such that
\Phi
holds for exactly one of the graphs
K_{t,t}
(the complete bipartite graph) or
I_{2t}
(the edge-less graph),
then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard and ETH implies an exponent of
\Omega(k)

[
20
]
.

In this paper, we strengthen most of the above hardness results.
However, we consider our main contribution to be the introduction of simple algebraic techniques to the study of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
, which was previously mostly approached through sophisticated techniques from group theory. Indeed, we derive many of the above results as relatively straightforward consequences of known results such as the Schwartz-Zippel lemma.

1.2
The Known Technique: Using the Sub-Expansion

Following other recent works, we approach
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
by interpreting its
k
-vertex slices
\Phi^{(k)}
as linear combinations of (not necessarily induced)
subgraph
counts.
Let us fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and abbreviate
f(\star)=\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
.
Then

f(\star)=\underbrace{\sum_{H\in\Phi^{(k)}}\alpha_{H}\cdot\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)}_{\text{ind-expansion of }f(\star)},

(1)

with
H
ranging over unlabeled
k
-vertex graphs, where we have
\alpha_{H}=1
for all
H\in\Phi^{(k)}
and
\alpha_{H}=0
otherwise.
Note that
\Phi^{(k)}
is finite; we could generally allow linear combinations involving arbitrary finite sets of graphs.

By known formulas
[
38
, (5.17)]
, there exists a set
\mathcal{H}
of unlabeled
k
-vertex graphs, together with non-zero coefficients
\beta_{H}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
H\in\mathcal{H}
, such that

f(\star)=\underbrace{\sum_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\beta_{H}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)}_{\text{sub-expansion of }f(\star)}.

(2)

We call the linear combinations on the right-hand side of (
1
) and (
2
) the
ind-expansion
and
sub-expansion
of
f(\star)
, respectively.
These expansions are specified by the involved patterns and their (non-zero) coefficients.
Both expansions are unique for a given function
f
, as the involved functions
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
and
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)
are linearly independent for simple unlabeled graphs, see, e.g.,
[
38
, Corollary 5.45]
.

Complexity Monotonicity.

Linear combinations of
k
-vertex subgraph pattern counts as above enjoy a useful “complexity monotonicity”: Under certain conditions, the presence of a single hard function
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)
in the linear combination can render the entire linear combination hard.
This phenomenon was already observed and exploited for
homomorphism
counts
[
12
]
, for which it holds without further conditions.
For subgraph counts, a sufficient condition for such a “complexity monotonicity” is that all patterns have the same number
k
of vertices, and moreover not only
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)
is hard, but even its
colorful
variant is hard.
(In this variant, given a vertex-colored graph
G
, we count subgraph copies of
H
that are colorful, i.e., which contain exactly one vertex of each color in
G
.
The colorful variant admits a parameterized reduction to the uncolored variant, but the converse does not hold, e.g., for matchings.)

We can thus understand the complexity of counting induced subgraphs with property
\Phi
by answering two questions: First, for which
H
is counting colorful
H
-subgraphs hard? Second, which patterns
H
appear in the sub-expansion (
2
) derived from
\Phi
?
Similar approaches were taken in
[
20
,
21
,
54
]
.
We discuss these two questions in the following.

Which Subgraphs are Hard to Count?

The complexity of counting subgraphs is well-understood in both the uncolored and colorful settings
[
15
,
12
]
.
First, let us observe that counting
H
-subgraphs may be easier than counting induced
H
-subgraphs, e.g., when
H
is edgeless.

In the
uncolored
setting, it is known that the subgraph variant
\#\textnormal{{ParSub}}(\mathcal{H})
of the induced subgraph counting problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\mathcal{H})
is polynomial-time solvable if the maximum vertex-cover number among graphs in
\mathcal{H}
is finite;
otherwise it is
#W[1]
-hard and almost tight lower bounds are known under ETH
[
15
,
12
]
.

The
colorful
variant admits additional tractable patterns: Standard techniques yield an
O(n^{\tw(H)+1})
time algorithm for counting colorful
H
-subgraphs in vertex-colored
n
-vertex graphs, where
\tw(H)
is the
treewidth
of
H
, which measures how similar
H
is to a tree for algorithmic purposes.
For recursively enumerable
\mathcal{H}
of bounded treewidth,
the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
of counting colorful
H
-subgraphs in
G
is therefore polynomial-time solvable, where both
H\in\mathcal{H}
and
G
are inputs.
If the treewidth of
\mathcal{H}
is unbounded, then the problem is
#W[1]
-hard, see
[
15
, Theorem 3.4]
or
[
10
, Theorem 5.6]
.
Moreover, ETH implies a constant
\beta>0
such that for every graph
H
with treewidth
t
, the number of colorful
H
-subgraph copies in
n
-vertex graphs cannot be computed in time
O(n^{\beta\cdot t/\log t})

[
39
]
.

In this paper, we rely on large edge-density of
H
as a sufficient proxy condition to guarantee large treewidth of
H
and thus hardness of counting colorful
H
-subgraph copies.
Indeed, it is well-known that graphs
H
of treewidth
t
have at most
t\cdot|V(H)|
edges.
Moreover, the following lower bound under ETH was recently shown.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

Assuming ETH, there is a constant
\beta>0
such that, for every pattern
H
with
k
vertices and
\ell
edges, colorful
H
-subgraph copies in
n
-vertex cannot be counted in
O(n^{\beta\cdot\ell/k})
time.

A similar version of this theorem holds with regard to
#W[1]
-hardness.
We stress that large edge-density is only a
sufficient
criterion for hardness.
Indeed, expanders provide an example of patterns
H
that have degree
3
and treewidth
\Omega(|V(H)|)
.

Which Patterns Appear in the Sub-Expansion?

Theorem
1.1
with the complexity monotonicity of subgraph counts allow us to show hardness of
f(\star)=\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
by finding
k
-vertex patterns
H
with
\omega(k)
edges and non-zero coefficients in the sub-expansion (
2
) of
f(\star)
.

As a first step, it can be shown that the coefficient
\beta_{H}
of
H
in the sub-expansion equals a certain combinatorial quantity, the
alternating enumerator
.
We define this quantity as

\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\sum_{S\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\Phi(H[S]),

where
H[S]
is the subgraph obtained from
H
by keeping all vertices and only the edges in
S
.
Our definition includes an extra factor of
(-1)^{|E(H)|}
compared to previous works
[
20
,
50
,
52
]
.

However, showing that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
turns out to be nontrivial:
With some exceptions
[
25
,
54
]
, most approaches towards this task this can be traced back to a seminal paper
[
52
]
that interprets edge-monotone properties
\Phi
defined on
k
-vertex graphs as abstract simplicial complexes.
Up to sign,
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})
then is the (reduced)
Euler characteristic
of the simplicial complex: Viewing
t
-edge graphs as dimension-
t
facets of the complex corresponding to
\Phi
, we obtain that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})
is the difference between the counts of odd-dimension and even-dimension facets, similar to Euler’s formula for plane graphs.
By analyzing group actions on simplicial complexes that preserve the Euler characteristic modulo primes, it is then shown that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})\neq 0
for several properties
\Phi
, e.g., for edge-monotone properties
\Phi
that are false on all odd cycles.
In subsequent works
[
20
,
21
,
53
]
, transitive group actions on graphs are used to derive more general results, also for related pattern counting problems, culminating in the recent result on edge-monotone properties
[
21
]
mentioned above.

1.3
Our Approach: Understanding Sub-Expansions via Polynomials

To summarize the method described in the previous section, we have a graph parameter

f(\star)=\sum_{H}\alpha_{H}\cdot\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)=\sum_{H}\beta_{H}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)

involving
k
-vertex graphs
H
in the sums. Both representations are unique, and the coefficients
\beta_{H}
are given by the
\alpha_{H}
via alternating enumerators.
As outlined above,
f(\star)
is hard if there is a graph
H
with
\beta_{H}\neq 0
and
\omega(k)
edges.
Our goal is to find such dense graphs
H
.

To attain this goal, we propose the following new strategy: First, we generalize graph parameters
f(\star)
of the above form to multilinear polynomials.
Related polynomials were already studied before in different contexts in algebraic complexity theory
[
5
,
17
,
36
]
.
Then, we observe that the degree of the polynomial corresponding to
f
is precisely the maximum edge-count
|E(H)|
among graphs
H
with
\beta_{H}\neq 0
.
Finally, we establish degree lower bounds on the polynomials using various algebraic techniques.

Polynomials for Graph Invariants.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
K_{t}
be the complete graph on
t
vertices, and let
x_{uv}
for
uv\in E(K_{t})
be indeterminates.
We define the
subgraph polynomial

\mathrm{Sub}_{H,t}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq K_{t}\\
F\cong H\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{uv\in E(F)}x_{uv},

(3)

where
F
ranges over all subgraphs of
K_{t}
isomorphic to
H
.
This can be interpreted as a weighted
H
-subgraph count in
K_{t}
.
We also define the
induced subgraph polynomial

\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,t}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq K_{t}\\
F\cong H\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{uv\in E(F)}x_{uv}\prod_{uv\in\binom{V(F)}{2}\setminus E(F)}(1-x_{uv}).

(4)

These polynomials generalize subgraph and induced subgraph counts:
Given a graph
G
with
V(G)=[t]
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and writing
m=\binom{t}{2}
, let
\boldsymbol{a}_{G}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
be the characteristic vector of
E(G)
as a subset of
\binom{[t]}{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sub}_{H,t}(\boldsymbol{a}_{G})

\displaystyle=\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G),

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,t}(\boldsymbol{a}_{G})

\displaystyle=\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to G).

(6)

Since multilinear polynomials are determined by their evaluations on
0
-
1
-inputs, the polynomials
\mathrm{Sub}_{H,t}
and
\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,t}
are the unique multilinear polynomials that agree, on
t
-vertex graphs, with subgraph counts from
H
or induced subgraph counts from
H
.
In other words, not only do these polynomials allow us to recover (induced) subgraph counts on
t
-vertex graphs at
0
-
1
-inputs, the polynomials are even
determined
by these counts.

This holds more generally for linear combinations of such counts:
As in (
2
), let
f(\star)=\sum_{F}\beta_{F}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\to\,\star\,)
be a linear combination of subgraph counts.
Then the linear combination of polynomials
q_{f,t}=\sum_{F}\beta_{F}\cdot\mathrm{Sub}_{F,t}
is the unique polynomial that agrees with
f
on
t
-vertex graphs. We call
q_{f,t}
the
polynomial representation

of
f
on
t
-vertex graphs.

Large Degree Implies Hardness.

For all graphs
H
and integers
t\geq|V(H)|
, the degree of the subgraph polynomial
\mathrm{Sub}_{H,t}
is
|E(H)|
.
This follows immediately from the monomial expansion (
3
).
More generally, for every linear combination
f(\star)=\sum_{F}\beta_{F}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\to\,\star\,)
, the degree of the polynomial
q_{f,t}=\sum_{F}\beta_{F}\cdot\mathrm{Sub}_{F,t}
is at most the maximum of
|E(F)|
over all graphs
F
with
\beta_{F}\neq 0
. (In fact, provided that
t\geq k
, the degree is even
equal
to this maximum. This follows from the linear independence of the polynomials, but it is not required in our arguments.)

This simple fact turns out to be very useful:
Given a graph parameter
f(\star)
that is a finite linear combination of subgraph counts, if the degree of its polynomial representation
is at least
d
for some
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then the sub-expansion of
f(\star)
must contain a graph
F
with at least
d
edges.
In particular, if
d\in\omega(k)
, then the strategy described in the beginning of this subsection comes to fruition and we obtain the desired hardness results.

As an example, reasoning along this strategy shows that
f(\star)=\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
is hard for
every

k
-vertex graph
H
, since its unique representation as a multilinear polynomial (
4
) clearly has degree
\binom{k}{2}
. This means that the sub-expansion of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
contains a graph on
\binom{k}{2}
edges, which is necessarily
K_{k}
.

Linear combinations of the functions
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(H\to\,\star\,)
for different
H
may however incur cancellations that make
K_{k}
or even all dense graphs disappear from the sub-expansion.
We use various techniques to prove lower bounds on the degrees of polynomials
q_{f,t}
associated with general graph parameters
f(\star)
, and thus lower-bound the number of edges among the graphs in their sub-expansion. These techniques include several elementary facts from algebra, e.g., a multilinear version of the Schwartz-Zippel lemma
[
57
,
6
]
, and the rich toolkit of
Fourier analysis

[
34
,
46
]
.

1.4
Our Results

As outlined above, we can study the complexity of linear combinations
f(\star)
of
k
-vertex pattern subgraph counts by showing that their polynomial representations
have large degree.
In the following, we discuss concrete results on the complexity of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
obtained this way.

Sparse and Hereditary Properties.

We first show hardness of properties that hold only with probability
\exp(-\Omega(k^{2}))
on
k
-vertex graphs.
This implies known results on hereditary properties
[
25
]
as a special case.
Moreover, our result holds even for “weighted properties”:
In the following, a
graph invariant

\Phi
is an isomorphism-invariant function from graphs to a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
We call
\Phi
a
k
-vertex graph invariant
if it is supported only on
k
-vertex graphs and define

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)=\sum_{F}\Phi(F)\cdot\#\mathrm{IndSub}(F\to G).

The Schwartz-Zippel lemma
[
18
,
55
,
58
]
can be used to show that every non-zero function
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{F}}
of small support necessarily has large degree:
If
1\leq|\supp(f)|\leq 2^{m-\ell}
for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then the degree of the polynomial representation of
f
over
{\mathbb{F}}
is at least
\ell
.
Using this, we show that the subgraph basis representation of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\star)
contains a graph
H
with
\ell
edges, rendering this problem hard if
\ell
is large.
In particular, when
\ell\geq\delta\cdot k^{2}
for a constant
\delta>0
, we obtain a graph
H
with
\delta\cdot k^{2}
edges, which implies tight lower bounds under ETH.
Since the Schwartz-Zippel lemma works over arbitrary fields, our results also apply to modular counting.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
0<\varepsilon<1
there is
\delta>0
such that, for every
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
with

1\leq|\supp(\Phi)|\leq(2-\varepsilon)^{\binom{k}{2}}
,
no algorithm computes
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k})
on
n
-vertex graphs
G
unless ETH fails.

Moreover, for every computable graph invariant
\Phi
such that

1\leq|\supp(\Phi^{(k)})|\leq(2-\varepsilon)^{\binom{k}{2}}

holds for infinitely many slices
\Phi^{(k)}
, the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.
This holds in particular when
\Phi
is a hereditary property that is not meager.

For every fixed prime
p
, the lower bounds hold even when the value
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)
is only to be computed modulo
p
, replacing ETH by the randomized ETH and
#W[1]
by
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
.

The claimed consequences for hereditary properties follow from the fact that, for fixed graphs
F
and large enough
k
, every non-empty property excluding induced
F
-subgraphs contains at most

or a self-contained proof in Appendix

Edge-Monotone Properties.

Let
\Phi
be a nontrivial

-vertex graph property that is monotone, i.e., closed under the deletion of edges.

1

1

In the literature, this condition is often called “edge-monotone” (see e.g.,

By adapting known results on the Fourier degrees of monotone functions with symmetry conditions

Theorem 1.3
.

There is
\delta>0
such that, for every

For every fixed prime

For

Special cases of such properties were studied before

Theorem 1.4
.

There is
\delta>0
such that, for every
k
-vertex graph property
\Phi

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)

Moreover, for every computable graph property
\Phi

\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)

Properties Avoiding many Hamming Weights.

We also consider properties
\Phi
that avoid a superlinear number of Hamming weights, see
[
31
,
54
]
.
Given a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
, we define its

as

{\sf hw}(\Phi)\coloneqq\{|E(H)|\mid H\in\Phi\}
,
i.e., the set of all edge-counts among the graphs in
\Phi
.
We then define a value
\beta_{\Phi}
that equals

when
\Phi
is empty and
\binom{k}{2}+1-|{\sf hw}(\Phi)|
otherwise.
For a general graph invariant
\Phi
and

, we define
\beta_{\Phi}(k)\coloneqq\beta_{\Phi^{(k)}}
.
By a symmetrization argument previously used in the study of Boolean functions
[

]
, we obtain:

Theorem 1.5
.

There is
\delta>0
such that, for every
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
,
no algorithm computes
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot\beta_{\Phi}/k})
on
n
-vertex graphs
G
unless ETH fails.
Moreover,
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard for computable graph invariants
\Phi
with
\beta_{\Phi}(k)\in\omega(k)
.

Invariants with Small Image.

Next, we consider a useful tool for
k
-vertex graph invariants
\Phi
with a sufficiently small image.
We define
\iota_{\Phi}\coloneqq|\mathrm{im}(\Phi)|-1
, where
\mathrm{im}(\Phi)
denotes the set of all
a\in{\mathbb{F}}

attained as
\Phi(G)
for some graph
G
.
Given an arbitrary mapping
\tau\colon\mathrm{im}(\Phi)\to{\mathbb{F}}
, we can “rename” the images of
\Phi
using
\tau
to obtain a new invariant
\Phi\circ\tau
defined via
(\Phi\circ\tau)(G)\coloneqq\tau(\Phi(G))
.

Suppose we can choose
\tau
in such a way that the polynomial representation
of
\Phi\circ\tau
has large degree
d
.
Since
\tau
can be represented by a (univariate) polynomial of degree at most
\iota_{\Phi}
using standard interpolation arguments, we can conclude that the polynomial representation
of
\Phi
has degree at least
d/\iota_{\Phi}
.
In other words, if
\iota_{\Phi}
is small and
\Phi\circ\tau
has large degree
d
, then the large degree
d
must stem from
\Phi
itself, not from the composition by
\tau
.
In particular, if
\iota_{\Phi}
is sufficiently small (compared to
d
), this implies that
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

We apply this method to show
#W[1]
-hardness for monotone graph invariants
\Phi
whose images have sublinear size, i.e.,
|\mathrm{im}(\Phi^{(k)})|=o(k)
.
This strengthens a result from
[
22
]
, which obtains hardness only if
|\mathrm{im}(\Phi^{(k)})|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\sqrt{k}
.

The All-Even Property.

Finally, we consider the curious property
\Phi_{\sf even}
containing exactly those graphs whose vertices all have even degree.
This property turns out to be interesting for several reasons. Firstly, it encodes a vector space over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, the cycle space of
K_{k}
, and we can show that precisely the bipartite graphs on
k
vertices appear in its subgraph expansion.
This implies that
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
is
#W[1]
-hard, with tight lower bounds under ETH.

Theorem 1.6
.

There is
\delta>0
such that no algorithm computes
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}\to G)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k})
on
n
-vertex graphs
G
unless ETH fails.
Moreover,
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
is
#W[1]
-hard.

Secondly, the fact that only bipartite graphs appear in the sub-expansion of

also yields non-trivial
upper bounds
on its complexity.
This in turn also implies

for other properties, as we can take pointwise products with

to filter out unwanted graphs from a property
\Phi
and translate degree lower bounds and complexity implications for the filtered version back to

for details.

Our techniques allow us to analyze the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of graph parameters.
The

The

such that

for which
f(G)\neq f(G^{\prime})
.
This complexity measure captures inexpressibility in certain logics and lower bounds for symmetric computational models.

We bound the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
for various
k
-vertex graph invariants
\Phi
:
For example, for monotone
k
-vertex graph properties
\Phi
, the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
is at least
k/4
, and the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)

.

Graphs

We use standard graph notation.

We write

G

Also, we use

A graph
H

of another graph
G

H\subseteq G
, if

and

.
For a graph
G

to denote the

of

by

.
Similarly, for

, we write

to denote the
(edge-)induced subgraph
of
G
by
S
.
It is usually clear from the context whether we are taking a vertex- or an edge-induced subgraph.

For
k\geq 1
, we write

for the complete graph with

V(K_{k})=[k]
and

.
For

, we write

to denote the complete bipartite graph with

and

.
We remark that we explicitly define the graph
K_{k}
to have vertex set
[k]
.
In particular, for
S\subseteq\binom{[k]}{2}
, we commonly use

to refer to the graph with vertex set
[k]
and edge set
S
.
If the index
k
is clear from context, we sometimes omit it and simply write

.

A
graph invariant

\Phi
(over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
) maps each graph
G
to a value
\Phi(G)\in{\mathbb{F}}
such that
\Phi(G)=\Phi(G^{\prime})
for all isomorphic graphs
G,G^{\prime}
.
In this work, we mostly consider graph invariants over the rationals
{\mathbb{Q}}
and over the
p

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for a prime
p
.
Unless stated otherwise, graph invariants are defined over the rationals.
A graph invariant is a

if
\Phi(G)\in\{0,1\}
for all graphs
G
.
For a graph
G
and a graph property
\Phi
, we write

\Phi(G)=1
.
For

, a
k
-vertex graph invariant/property

\Phi

\Phi(G)=0
if
|V(G)|\neq k
.
The
k
-th slice

\Phi^{(k)}
of a graph invariant
\Phi
is the

-vertex graph invariant that agrees with
\Phi
on
k
-vertex graphs (and is
0
otherwise).
For
k\geq 1
, a graph invariant
\Phi
(over
{\mathbb{F}}
) is

if there is some

such that

for all
k
-vertex graphs

.
A graph invariant
\Phi

k

.

For a graph invariant
\Phi
, we define

Observe that, if

is a graph property, then

.

2.2
Parameterized Complexity

We give a brief introduction to parameterized counting complexity and refer the reader to the textbooks
[
16
,
24
]
and theses
[
10
,
50
]
for additional background.

A
parameterized counting problem
consists of a function
P\colon\Sigma^{*}\to{\mathbb{Q}}
and a
parameterization

\kappa\colon\Sigma^{*}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
For an instance
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, the number
\kappa(x)
is called the
parameter
of
x
.
The problem
(P,\kappa)
is called
fixed-parameter tractable (fpt)
if there is a computable function
f
, a constant
c\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 1}
and a (deterministic) algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that, given an instance
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, computes
P(x)
in time
f(\kappa(x))\cdot|x|^{c}
.

A
parameterized Turing reduction
from
(P,\kappa)
to
(P^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
is a deterministic fpt-algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
with oracle access to
P^{\prime}
that, given an instance
x
, computes
P(x)
and only makes oracle calls to instances
y
that satisfy
\kappa^{\prime}(y)\leq g(\kappa(x))
for some computable function
g\colon{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
The problem
\#\textnormal{{Clique}}
takes as input a graph
G
and an integer
k\geq 1
and asks to determine the number of
k
-cliques in
G
.
The number
k
is the parameter of the instance
(G,k)
.
A parameterized counting problem
(P,\kappa)
is
#W[1]
-hard
if there is a parameterized Turing reduction from
\#\textnormal{{Clique}}
to
(P,\kappa)
.
A
parsimonious
parameterized reduction is a parameterized Turing reduction that makes exactly one oracle call
y
and then outputs the oracle response on
y
without further processing.

For a graph invariant
\Phi
, the parameterized counting problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
takes as input a graph
G
and an integer
k\geq 1
(the parameter) and outputs
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to G)
.
When studying the complexity of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
, it will mostly suffice to restrict our attention to the
k
-th slice
of the problem, for fixed integers
k\geq 1
, where the input consists only of a graph
G
.
This motivates us to define the following problem:
Given
k\geq 1
and a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
,
the problem
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
takes as input a graph
G
and outputs
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)
.
For a graph invariant
\Phi
, we also write
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi,k)
instead of
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi^{(k)})
.
Compared to
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
, this problem is polynomial-time solvable for every fixed
\Phi
and
k
.

Modular Counting.

Given a prime
p
, we write
\#_{p}
to denote the modular counting version of a problem.
For example,
\#_{p}\textnormal{{Clique}}
denotes the problem of counting
k
-cliques modulo
p
.
We say a problem
(P,\kappa)
is
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
-hard if there is a parameterized Turing reduction from
\#_{p}\textnormal{{Clique}}
to
(P,\kappa)
.
Note that a parsimonious parameterized reduction from
\#\textnormal{{Clique}}
to the non-modular counting version
(P^{\prime},\kappa)
of

implies such a reduction.

If
\Phi
is a graph invariant over

, then
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for every graph

, which leads to the computational problems
\#_{p}\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
and

.
Observe that, if

is a property, we can meaningfully consider the modular counting versions

for every prime

.

2.3
Representing Boolean Functions

We represent Boolean functions
f
(that in turn represent graph parameters) as polynomials
p
and lower-bound the degrees of these polynomials to obtain complexity-theoretic lower bounds.
In the following, we present the relevant theory for general Boolean functions; the specifics for graph parameters are discussed in Section
3
.

Polynomial Representation.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be a field.
Every Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{F}}
of arity
m
can be uniquely represented as a multilinear polynomial in
{\mathbb{F}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
via

q_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}f(\boldsymbol{a})\prod_{i\in[m]:a_{i}=1}x_{i}\prod_{i\in[m]:a_{i}=0}(1-x_{i}),

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
.
We call
q_{f}
the
polynomial representation of
f
.
For
S\subseteq[m]
, let us denote the monomial corresponding to
S
as

x^{S}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in S}x_{i},

with the convention that
x^{\emptyset}=1
.
By expanding (
7
) and collecting terms, we obtain unique coefficients
f^{\downarrow}(S)\in{\mathbb{F}}
for every
S\subseteq[m]
such that

q_{f}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}f^{\downarrow}(S)\cdot x^{S}.

(8)

This notation is inspired by our notation for the alternating enumerator; see Definition
3.1
and Remark
3.2
for more on the origin of this notation.

We write
\deg_{{\mathbb{F}}}(f)
to denote the degree of the polynomial
q_{f}
, i.e.,

\deg_{{\mathbb{F}}}(f)=\max\{|S|\mid S\subseteq[m]\text{ with }f^{\downarrow}(S)\neq 0\}.

If
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
, we simply omit the index and write
\deg(f)
, and we write
\deg_{p}(f)
if
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
We are often dealing with functions
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
which can be interpreted over any field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
In this situation, observe that
\deg_{p}(f)\leq\deg(f)
.

We regularly need to restrict Boolean functions to subcubes.
Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{F}}
be a Boolean function of arity
m
and let
J\subseteq[m]
.
We write
\overline{J}\coloneqq[m]\setminus J
for the complement of
J
in
[m]
.
Also let
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{j})_{j\in\overline{J}}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{J}}
.
Then we define the
restriction of
f
to
J
using
\boldsymbol{z}
, denoted by
f_{J|\boldsymbol{z}}\colon\{0,1\}^{J}\to{\mathbb{F}}
, to be the subfunction of
f
given by fixing the coordinates in
\overline{J}
to the values in
\boldsymbol{z}
.
Slightly abusing notation, for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\{0,1\}^{J}
, we write
(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
for the composite tuple in
\{0,1\}^{m}
, even tough
\boldsymbol{y}
and
\boldsymbol{z}
are not actually concatenated.
In this notation, we have
f_{J|\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y})=f(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
.
Then

f_{J|\boldsymbol{z}}^{\downarrow}(S)=\sum_{T\subseteq\overline{J}}f^{\downarrow}(S\cup T)\prod_{j\in T}z_{j}

(9)

for all
S\subseteq J
.
In particular,
\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(f)\geq\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(f_{J|\boldsymbol{z}})
.

Fourier Representation.

Boolean functions can also be represented by their
Fourier expansion
.
In an earlier version of the paper, several proofs relied on this representation, while the current version instead works only in the representation over
\{0,1\}^{m}
described above.
Nevertheless, we briefly discuss the Fourier representation here, because it can provide an alternative perspective on some of our results, and it originally led us to their discovery. Pragmatic readers and readers familiar with Fourier analysis can safely skip this part.

For simplicity, we restrict our attention to functions that map into
{\mathbb{R}}
.
In Fourier analysis, we consider the input space of Boolean functions to be
\{-1,1\}^{m}
, and the reader is encouraged to associate
-1
with
True
and
1
with
False
.
Let
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
As before, there is a unique multilinear polynomial in
{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}]
representing
f
, i.e., there are unique so-called
Fourier coefficients

\widehat{f}(S)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for
S\subseteq[m]
with

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}\widehat{f}(S)\cdot x^{S}.

(10)

The polynomial representation of
f
is called the
Fourier expansion of
f
.
We refer to the textbook
[
46
]
for extensive background on the Fourier expansion of Boolean functions.
We write

\displaystyle\supp(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}\mid f(\boldsymbol{x})\neq 0\},

\displaystyle\supp(\widehat{f})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{S\subseteq[m]\mid\widehat{f}(S)\neq 0\}.

By the
uncertainty principle
, one of these two sets is always large; see
[
46
, Exercise 3.15]
.

Lemma 2.3

(Uncertainty Principle)
.

If
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is not the zero function, then

|\supp(f)|\cdot|\supp(\widehat{f})|\geq 2^{m}.

The
Fourier degree

\deg(f)
of
f
is the degree of the Fourier expansion, i.e.,

\deg(f)\coloneqq\max\{|S|\mid S\in\supp(\widehat{f})\}.

It is possible to change between the
\{0,1\}
-based and
\{-1,1\}
-based representations by a simple affine shift of variables. More concretely, to every function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we can canonically associate a function
f^{*}\colon\{-1,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}

by setting
f^{*}(\boldsymbol{a}^{*})\coloneqq f(\boldsymbol{a})
, where
\boldsymbol{a}
is obtained from
\boldsymbol{a}^{*}
by replacing every
1
by
0
, and every
-1
by
1
.
We have that

f^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}\widehat{f^{*}}(S)x^{S}=q_{f}\left(-\frac{1}{2}x_{1}+\frac{1}{2},\dots,-\frac{1}{2}x_{m}+\frac{1}{2}\right).

(11)

As the right-hand side equals
q_{f}
up to an affine shift of variables, we obtain
\deg(f)=\deg(f^{*})
.
Even stronger,
q_{f}
and the polynomial representing
f^{*}
have the same inclusion-wise maximal monomials.

3
General Approach for Hardness Proofs

While previous works (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
21
,
52
]
) analyze the alternating enumerator directly to obtain hardness results, we observe a close connection to the coefficients of the polynomial representation of
f_{\Phi}
, i.e., the Boolean function associated with
\Phi
.
This allows us to use algebraic techniques to obtain hardness results for
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
for various different types of graph invariants
\Phi
.

3.1
Polynomial Representations and Alternating Enumerators

Given a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
, we can represent
\Phi
canonically as a Boolean function.
For ease of notation, we write
K\coloneqq K_{k}
for the complete graph on vertex set
[k]
in the remainder of this section.
We associate to
\Phi
the Boolean function
f_{\Phi}\colon\{0,1\}^{E(K)}\to{\mathbb{F}}
of arity
\binom{k}{2}
, where

f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a})\coloneqq\Phi(K[\boldsymbol{a}])

with
K[\boldsymbol{a}]\coloneqq K[S_{\boldsymbol{a}}]
and
S_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\{e\in E(K)\mid a_{e}=1\}
.

In the following, we write
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{e})_{e\in E(K)}
.
As outlined in Section
2.3
, there is a unique multilinear polynomial in
{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{x}]
that agrees with
f_{\Phi}
, the
polynomial representation of
\Phi
, given by

q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{E(K)}}f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a})\prod_{e\in E(K)\colon a_{e}=1}x_{e}\prod_{e\in E(K)\colon a_{e}=0}(1-x_{e}).

(12)

Since
\Phi
is a graph invariant, it attains the same value on graphs with the same isomorphism type. This means we can collect terms in (
12
) and change notation, to obtain

q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{unlabeled }H\\
\text{on $k$ vertices}\end{subarray}}\Phi(H)\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq K\\
F\cong H\end{subarray}}\prod_{e\in E(F)}x_{e}\prod_{e\notin E(F)}(1-x_{e})}_{=\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,k}(\boldsymbol{x})}\,,

(13)

with
\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,k}
from (
4
).
As we only require the polynomials
\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,k}
rather than
\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,t}
for general
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we abbreviate
\mathrm{IndSub}_{H}\coloneqq\mathrm{IndSub}_{H,k}
.
Similarly, we abbreviate
\mathrm{Sub}_{H}\coloneqq\mathrm{Sub}_{H,k}
, where the latter was defined in (
3
).

We wish to understand the polynomial representation of
\Phi
in more detail and connect it to graph-theoretic notions. First, recall the following combinatorial definition:

Definition 3.1
.

The
alternating enumerator
of a graph invariant
\Phi
on a graph
H
is

\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\sum_{S\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|S|}\,\Phi(H[S]).

Remark 3.2
.

In earlier works
[
20
,
21
,
52
]
, the alternating enumerator is defined without the factor
(-1)^{|E(H)|}
.
These works aim at showing that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
for certain graphs
H
; such statements are not affected by the absence of the factor
(-1)^{|E(H)|}
.
To obtain more straightforward statements in Lemma
3.3
and Corollaries
3.5
and
3.6
, we prefer the variant defined above.

Additionally, earlier works usually write
\widehat{\Phi}(H)
to denote the alternating enumerator.
We avoid this notation, since the hat-notation
\widehat{f}
is commonly used in the analysis of Boolean functions to denote Fourier coefficients (see, e.g.,
[
46
]
).
Instead, our notation follows
[
38
]
.

By expanding the right-hand side of (
12
) similar to
(
3
)
, we see that the polynomial representation of
\Phi
has the alternating enumerators as coefficients in its monomial expansion.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Phi
be a graph invariant on
k
-vertex graphs.
Then

q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq E(K)}\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S])\cdot x^{S},

(14)

i.e.,
f_{\Phi}^{\downarrow}(S)=\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S])
for every
S\subseteq E(K)
.

Proof.

For
E\subseteq E(K)
let
q_{E}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\prod_{e\in E}x_{e}\prod_{e\in E(K)\setminus E}(1-x_{e})
.
Then

q_{E}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq E(K)\setminus E}(-1)^{|S|}x^{E\cup S}=\sum_{S\supseteq E}(-1)^{|S\setminus E|}x^{S}.

It follows that the coefficient of
x^{S}
is equal to

\sum_{E\subseteq S}(-1)^{|S\setminus E|}\cdot\Phi(K[E])=(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\sum_{E\subseteq S}(-1)^{E}\cdot\Phi(K[E])=\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S]).

∎

As a direct corollary, a large degree
\deg(q_{\Phi})=\deg(f_{\Phi})
of
f_{\Phi}
implies the existence of graphs
H
with non-zero alternating enumerator and many edges.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
k\geq 1
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant.
Then
\deg(q_{\Phi})\geq\ell
if and only if there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
|E(H)|\geq\ell
and
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
.

By collecting terms corresponding to isomorphic graphs, the polynomial
q_{\Phi}
can be expressed as a linear combination of the subgraph polynomials defined in (
3
).

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\Phi
be a graph invariant on
k
-vertex graphs.
Then

q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{unlabeled }H\\
\text{on $k$ vertices}\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\mathrm{Sub}_{H}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(15)

Proof.

If
\Phi
is a graph invariant, then also
\Phi^{\downarrow}
is a graph invariant by definition.
Therefore, we have
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S])=\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S^{\prime}])
if
K[S]\cong K[S^{\prime}]
.
This allows us to collect terms corresponding to isomorphic graphs in (
14
) to obtain

q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq E(K)}\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S])\cdot x^{S}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{unlabeled }H\\
\text{on $k$ vertices}\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq E(K)\\
K[S]\cong H\end{subarray}}x^{S}}_{=\mathrm{Sub}_{H}(\boldsymbol{x})},

which proves the corollary.
∎

In particular, (
13
) and (
14
) together imply that
\Phi(F)=\sum_{H}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to F)
for every
k
-vertex graph
F
.
We show below that this holds even when
|V(F)|\neq k
.
Related correspondences have been shown combinatorially, see
[
20
, Lemma 8]
or
[
52
, Theorem 1]
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Given a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
, for
k\geq 1
, we have

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\star)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{unlabeled }H\\
\text{on $k$ vertices}\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\star).

(16)

Proof.

Given an
n
-vertex graph
G
, we can write
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)=\sum_{X}\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G[X])
, where
X
ranges over all
k
-vertex subsets of
V(G)
.
Since
G[X]
is a
k
-vertex graph, we have
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G[X])=\Phi(G[X])=\sum_{H}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G[X])
.
Changing the order of summation, we obtain

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)=\sum_{H}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\sum_{X}\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G[X])=\sum_{H}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G).

∎

3.2
Non-Vanishing Alternating Enumerators Imply Hardness

To show hardness of the problem
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
and its variants, we follow previous works
[
20
,
21
,
52
]
and identify graphs of unbounded treewidth with non-zero alternating enumerator:
For a graph
H
, we write
\tw(H)
for the treewidth of
H
(see, e.g.,
[
16
, Chapter 7]
) and say that an (infinite) sequence of graphs
(H_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has
unbounded treewidth
if, for every

, there is some
k\geq 1
such that
\tw(H_{k})\geq t
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
(H_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence of graphs of unbounded treewidth.

(a)

If
\Phi
is a computable graph invariant with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H_{k})\neq 0
for all
k\geq 0
, then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

(b)

If
p
is prime and
\Phi
is a computable graph invariant over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H_{k})\neq 0\bmod p
for all
k\geq 0
, then
\#_{p}\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
-hard.

The next theorem gives us more fine-grained lower bounds based on ETH and rETH.

Theorem 3.8
.

There is a universal constant
\alpha_{\textsc{ind}}>0
and an integer
N_{0}\geq 1
such that for all numbers
k,\ell\geq 1
, the following holds:

(a)

If
\Phi
is a
k
-vertex graph invariant and there exists a graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
E(H)\geq k\cdot\ell\geq N_{0}
, then
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
cannot be solved in time
O(n^{\alpha_{\textsc{ind}}\cdot\ell})
unless ETH fails.

(b)

If
p
is prime,
\Phi
is a
k
-vertex graph invariant over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
and there exists a graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0\bmod p
and
E(H)\geq k\cdot\ell\geq N_{0}
, then
\#_{p}\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
cannot be solved in time
O(n^{\alpha_{\textsc{ind}}\cdot\ell})
unless rETH fails.

Both theorems can be proved by (variants of) known arguments
[
10
,
20
,
21
,
52
]
via Corollary
3.6
.
Still, we provide full proofs in Appendix
A
.

Finally, let us also note the following algorithmic result.
Let
G
be a graph.
A
vertex cover
of
G
is a set
C\subseteq V(G)
such that
e\cap C\neq\emptyset
for every edge
e\in E(G)
.
The
vertex cover number
of
G
, denoted by
\vc(G)
, is the minimal size of a vertex cover of
G
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\Phi
be a computable graph invariant and let
t(k)
denote the maximal vertex cover number of a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
.
Then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
can be solved in time
f(k)\cdot n^{t(k)+1}
for some computable function
f
.

The theorem is a consequence of
[
12
, Theorem 1.1]
and Corollary
3.6
; a proof is again given in Appendix
A
.

4
Hardness Results

Section
3.2
shows that graphs
H
with many edges and non-zero alternating enumerators
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)
imply hardness of
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
.
By Corollary
3.4
, such graphs
H
exist if the polynomial representation of
\Phi
has large degree.
This allows us to exploit tools from the analysis of Boolean functions to find such graphs
H
.
Thus, combining the generic hardness results with algebraic techniques, we obtain hardness results for specific problems
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
and
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
.

4.1
Moderately Sparse Invariants

As a first application, we consider graph invariants with moderately small support.
For a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
, we write

\supp(\Phi)\coloneqq\Big\{K_{k}[S]\penalty\ \Big|\penalty\ S\in\binom{[k]}{2},\Phi(K_{k}[S])\neq 0\Big\}.

Observe that
\supp(\Phi)
contains exactly the graphs
G
with vertex set
[k]
for which
\Phi(G)\neq 0
.
For an arbitrary graph invariant
\Phi
(which is not only defined on
k
-vertex graphs) we also write
\supp_{k}(\Phi)\coloneqq\supp(\Phi^{(k)})
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{F}}
be a field.
Let
k\geq 1
,
0\leq\ell\leq\binom{k}{2}
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over
{\mathbb{F}}
such that

1\leq|\supp(\Phi)|\leq 2^{\binom{k}{2}-\ell}.

Then there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
|E(H)|\geq\ell
.

We stress that Theorem
4.1
holds over arbitrary fields
{\mathbb{F}}
.
The proof relies on the following lemma on Boolean functions, which can be viewed as a variant of the Schwartz-Zippel lemma for multlinear polynomials, and which was shown, e.g., in
[
57
, Lemma 2.2]
and
[
6
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{F}}
be a Boolean function that is not the zero function.
Then

|\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\mid f(\boldsymbol{a})\neq 0\}|\geq 2^{m-d}

where
d\coloneqq\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(f)
.

Proof.

Let
q_{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}[\boldsymbol{x}]
be the polynomial representation of
f
in indeterminates
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
and let
S\subseteq[m]
be a set of size
d
such that
f^{\downarrow}(S)\neq 0
, i.e., the monomial
x^{S}
has a non-zero coefficient in
q_{f}
.
Without loss of generality assume that
S=\{1,\dots,d\}
.
For every
\boldsymbol{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{S}}
, consider the polynomial

q_{\boldsymbol{c}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=q_{f}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},c_{d+1},\dots,c_{m})

obtained from
q_{f}
by substituting
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{d+1},\dots,c_{m})
into
x_{d+1},\ldots,x_{m}
.
Note that
q_{\boldsymbol{c}}
is not the zero polynomial, since the coefficient of
x^{S}
remains unchanged.
Because the multilinear polynomial
q_{\boldsymbol{c}}
is determined by its evaluations on all tuples in
\{0,1\}^{S}
, there is some
\boldsymbol{b}\in\{0,1\}^{S}
such that
q_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\boldsymbol{b})\neq 0
.

So overall, for every
\boldsymbol{c}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{S}}
there is some
\boldsymbol{b}\in\{0,1\}^{S}
such that
f(\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{c})=q_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\boldsymbol{b})\neq 0
.
It follows that there are at least
|\{0,1\}^{\overline{S}}|=2^{m-d}
vectors
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
with
f(\boldsymbol{a})\neq 0
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
0\leq\ell\leq\binom{k}{2}
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over
{\mathbb{F}}
such that

1\leq|\supp(\Phi)|\leq 2^{m-\ell}

where
m\coloneqq\binom{k}{2}
.
Since
|\supp(\Phi)|\geq 1
we get that
f_{\Phi}\colon\{0,1\}^{E(K_{k})}\to{\mathbb{F}}
is not the zero function.
Let
d\coloneqq\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(f_{\Phi})
.
Then, by Lemma
4.2
, we get that

2^{m-d}\leq|\{\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{E(K_{k})}\mid f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a})\neq 0\}|=|\supp(\Phi)|\leq 2^{m-\ell}.

It follows that
d\geq\ell
.
By Corollary
3.4
, we obtain a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
|E(H)|\geq\ell
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

An earlier version of the paper showed a slightly weaker form of Theorem
4.1
for
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{R}}
via the uncertainty principle (see Lemma

Indeed, if

is small, then the Fourier representation of

has large support, which implies its degree is also large.

(a)

.

) that bounds the number of graphs that do not contain

4.2
Monotone Properties

Toward the proof of Theorem

Let

p

.

Recall that a Boolean functions

for uniquely determined coefficients
f^{\downarrow}(S)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for

.
We write

to denote the degree of

(over the field

).

Also, we write
\vec{0}=(0,\dots,0)
and

to denote the all-zero and all-one vector of the appropriate length, respectively (the length of the vector is always clear from context).

Lemma 4.8
.

Let

be a function over

where

is prime and

Also suppose

Proof.

First observe that
f(\vec{0})=q_{f}(\vec{0})=f^{\downarrow}(\emptyset)

f(\vec{1})=q_{f}(\vec{1})=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}f^{\downarrow}(S).

We need to argue that

The following two claims form the key ingredients.

Claim 4.9
.

For every
S\subseteq[m]
and
\sigma\in\Aut(f)
, we have
f^{\downarrow}(S)=f^{\downarrow}(S^{\sigma})
, where
S^{\sigma}=\{\sigma(i)\mid i\in S\}

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\Aut(f)
.
Consider the polynomial

q_{f}^{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}f^{\downarrow}(S^{\sigma})\cdot x^{S}.

We have that

q_{f}(\boldsymbol{a})=f(\boldsymbol{a})=f(\boldsymbol{a}^{\sigma})=q_{f}^{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{a})

for all

.
Since the polynomial representation of

which implies that

for every

.
∎

Claim 4.10

For every
\emptyset\subsetneq S\subsetneq[m]

.

Proof.

Let

.

|S|\cdot|S^{\Aut(f)}|=m\cdot|\{S^{\prime}\in S^{\Aut(f)}\mid 1\in S^{\prime}\}|.

(17)

First observe that this implies the claim, since
|S|<m

by

We partition the power set

4.3
Fully Symmetric Properties

We say that a
k
-vertex graph property
\Phi
is
fully symmetric
if
\Phi(G)=\Phi(G^{\prime})
for all
k
-vertex graphs
G,G^{\prime}
with
|E(G)|=|E(G^{\prime})|
.
In other words,
\Phi
is fully symmetric if it only depends on the number of edges.
As usual, we say a graph property
\Phi
is
fully symmetric on
k
-vertex graphs
if
\Phi^{(k)}
is fully symmetric.
After a series of partial results on the hardness of fully symmetric properties
[
31
,
33
,
52
]
, it was shown in
[
54
]
that
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
is hard for every non-trivial, fully symmetric
k
-vertex graph property.
In the following, we give an alternative proof of hardness that is arguably simpler.

Our proof relies on the following theorem from
[
56
]
.
A Boolean function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
fully symmetric
if
f(\boldsymbol{a})=f(\boldsymbol{a}^{\sigma})
for every
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
and every
\sigma\in S_{m}
, i.e.,
f(\boldsymbol{a})
only depends on the number of
1
-entries in
\boldsymbol{a}
.
Observe that a property
\Phi
on
k
-vertex graphs is fully symmetric if and only if
f_{\Phi}
is fully symmetric.

(
[
56
, Theorem 2.8]
)
.

Let

We note that
[

.
Then
g_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{a})=f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{z})=f_{\Phi}((\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{z})^{\pi})=f_{\Phi}((\boldsymbol{a}^{\sigma},\boldsymbol{z}))=g_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{a}^{\sigma})
where the second equality holds since
f_{\Phi}
is fully symmetric.

So it remains to argue that there is some
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{J}}
such that
g_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is non-constant.
Suppose towards a contradiction that
g_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is constant for every
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{J}}
.
This means that for every
\boldsymbol{z}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{J}}
there is some
\alpha(\boldsymbol{z})\in\{0,1\}
such that
g_{\boldsymbol{z}}
always evaluates to
\alpha(\boldsymbol{z})
.
Since
f_{\Phi}
is non-constant, there are
\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{\overline{J}}
such that
\alpha(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})\neq\alpha(\boldsymbol{z}_{2})
.
Additionally, we may assume that
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{2}
only differ in one entry.
But now there are
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{J}
such that
(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{1})
and
(\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})
have the same number of
1
-entries since
m\geq 1
.
So
f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{1})=\alpha(\boldsymbol{z}_{1})\neq\alpha(\boldsymbol{z}_{2})=f_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})
.
But this is a contradiction since
f_{\Phi}
is fully symmetric.
∎

So
\deg(g_{\boldsymbol{z}})=m
by Theorem
4.16
which implies that
g_{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\downarrow}(J)\neq 0
.
By Equation (
9
) there is some set
T\subseteq\overline{J}
such that
f_{\Phi}^{\downarrow}(J\cup T)\neq 0
.
So
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K_{k}[J\cup T])\neq 0
by Lemma
3.3
.
To finish the proof observe that
K_{\ell}
is a subgraph of
H\coloneqq K_{k}[J\cup T]
since
E(K_{\ell})\subseteq J
.
∎

Note that, by Bertrand’s Postulate, it is always possible to choose
\ell\geq\frac{k}{2}
.
In fact, using the estimate on the maximal gap between consecutive prime numbers
[
4
]
discussed above, a simple calculation shows that it is possible to choose
\ell\geq k-O(k^{0.05})
.
So by combining Theorem
4.17
with Theorems
3.7
(a)
and
3.8
(a)
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 4.19

(Theorem
1.4
restated)
.

There are
N_{0},\delta>0
such that the following holds.
Let
k\geq N_{0}
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph property that is fully symmetric and not
k
-trivial.
Then no algorithm solves
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k})
unless ETH fails.

Moreover, for every computable graph property
\Phi
that is fully symmetric on
k
-vertex graphs and not
k
-trivial for infinitely many
k
, the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

Remark 4.20
.

We note that previous techniques by Roth et al.
[
54
]
only rule out an algorithm solving
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k/\sqrt{\log k}})
assuming ETH.
However, combining the previous techniques with Theorem
1.1
, which was shown only very recently
[
14
]
, a lower bound of
O(n^{\delta\cdot k})
under ETH would also follow.

On the other hand, our arguments remain valid even without the tighter lower bounds provided by Theorem
1.1
, since we obtain a
k
-vertex graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
such that
H
is not only dense, but it even contains an
\ell
-clique for
\ell\geq k/2
.
Under different complexity assumptions than ETH, this can yield stronger lower bounds; see Section
6
.

4.4
Invariants with Few Distinct Hamming Weights

In this section, we use the polynomial representation of graph invariants to derive lower bounds for
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
when
\Phi
avoids a superlinear number of Hamming weights.
In particular, our theorem allows us to recover
[
31
, Theorem 3.4]
on hardness for
\Phi
with
o(k^{2})
distinct Hamming weights, and the more general
[
54
, Main Theorem 5]
, which applies whenever
\omega(k)
Hamming weights are avoided.
Our result even holds for graph invariants rather than properties, provided that they map into a field of characteristic zero.

Given a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
, define

{\sf hw}(\Phi)\coloneqq\{|E(H)|\mid\Phi(H)\neq 0\}

to be the set of all edge-counts (i.e, Hamming weights, when viewed as bitstrings) among the support of
\Phi
.
Throughout this section, let
m\coloneqq\binom{k}{2}
.
We define a value that counts the avoided Hamming weights,
except when
\Phi
is empty, as

\beta_{\Phi}\coloneqq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }|{\sf hw}(\Phi)|=0,\\
m+1-|{\sf hw}(\Phi)|&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For example, if
\Phi
is trivial, then
\beta_{\Phi}=0
. If
\Phi
is the planarity property of
k
-vertex graphs, then
\beta_{\Phi}=m-3k+6
.
For a general graph invariant
\Phi
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define
\beta_{\Phi}(k)\coloneqq\beta_{\Phi^{(k)}}
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
of characteristic
0
that is not
k
-trivial.
Then there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
|E(H)|\geq\beta_{\Phi}/2
.

If additionally
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{Q}}
and
\Phi(F)\geq 0
for all
k
-vertex graphs
F
, then
|E(H)|\geq\beta_{\Phi}
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
To prepare the proof of our lower bound, we define an invariant
\overline{\Phi}
from
\Phi
by setting
\overline{\Phi}(H)\coloneqq\Phi(\overline{H})
, where
\overline{H}
is the complement graph of
H
.

Fact 4.22
.

If
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
, then
\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(q_{\Phi})=\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(q_{\overline{\Phi}})
.

Proof.

Let
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
q_{\overline{\Phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the polynomial representations of
\Phi
and
\overline{\Phi}
, respectively.
From the definition, we directly obtain that
q_{\Phi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=q_{\overline{\Phi}}(1-x_{1},\ldots,1-x_{m})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
.
It follows that
\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(q_{\Phi})=\deg_{\mathbb{F}}(q_{\overline{\Phi}})
.
∎

The following simple fact will also be useful:

Fact 4.23
.

If
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
which is not
k
-trivial, then there is a graph
F
with
|E(F)|\leq\beta_{\Phi}/2
such that
\Phi(F)\neq 0
or
\overline{\Phi}(F)\neq 0
.

Proof.

The invariant
\Phi
cannot avoid all of the weights
0,\dots,\lfloor\beta_{\Phi}/2\rfloor
and
m,m-1,\dots,m-\lfloor\beta_{\Phi}/2\rfloor
(since those are at least
\beta_{\Phi}+1
weights).
In the first case, there is a graph
F
with
|E(F)|\leq\beta_{\Phi}/2
such that
\Phi(F)\neq 0
.
Otherwise, there is a graph
H
with
|E(H)|\geq m-\beta_{\Phi}/2
such that
\Phi(H)\neq 0
.
We set
F\coloneqq\overline{H}
which satisfies
|E(F)|\leq\beta_{\Phi}/2
and
\overline{\Phi}(F)\neq 0
.
∎

We are ready for the main proof.

Proof of Theorem
4.21
.

We prove the first statement and then describe the modifications required for the second statement.
Let
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
be the polynomial representation of
\Phi
in variables
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
; slightly abusing notation, we identify the variables
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
with
(x_{e})_{e\in E(K_{k})}
by fixing an arbitrary numbering of the edges of
K_{k}
.
By Corollary
3.4
and Fact
4.22
, it suffices to show that one of
q_{\Phi}
or
q_{\overline{\Phi}}
has degree at least
\beta_{\Phi}/2
.

Let
F
be a graph with minimal number of edges
r\coloneqq|E(F)|
such that
\Phi(F)\neq 0
.
We may assume without loss of generality that
r\leq\beta_{\Phi}/2
, since otherwise we can replace
\Phi
by
\overline{\Phi}
in the remainder of the argument, by Fact
4.23
.

Let
S\coloneqq E(F)
.
We define the polynomial

q(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot x^{S}\penalty\ =\penalty\ q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\prod_{e\in S}x_{e}.

Clearly
\deg(q)\leq\deg(q_{\Phi})+r
.
On every input
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
of Hamming weight
w\notin{\sf hw}(\Phi)
, we have
q(\boldsymbol{a})=0
, since then
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a})=\Phi(K_{k}[\boldsymbol{a}])=0
.
Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
has Hamming weight
r
, we have

q(\boldsymbol{a})=\begin{cases}\Phi(F)&\text{if }K_{k}[\boldsymbol{a}]=F,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(18)

We show
\deg(q)\geq\beta_{\Phi}
, which implies
\deg(q_{\Phi})\geq\beta_{\Phi}-r\geq\beta_{\Phi}/2
and proves the lemma.
Towards this end, we define a symmetric polynomial from
q
via

q^{\mathrm{sym}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=\sum_{\pi\in S_{m}}q(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(m)}).

Clearly,
\deg(q^{\mathrm{sym}})\leq\deg(q)
.
Moreover,
q^{\mathrm{sym}}
can be “compressed” to a univariate polynomial: By
[
34
, Theorem 2.2]
(see also
[
45
,
41
]
), there exists a univariate polynomial
\tilde{q}
with
\deg(\tilde{q})\leq\deg(q^{\mathrm{sym}})
and
\tilde{q}(\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{m})=q^{\mathrm{sym}}(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m})
for all
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}\in\{0,1\}
.

Claim 4.24
.

\deg(\tilde{q})\geq\beta_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

The polynomial
\tilde{q}(x)
has at least
\beta_{\Phi}
roots, since
\tilde{q}(b)=0
for all
b\in\{0,\dots,m\}\setminus{\sf hw}(\Phi)
.
This implies
\deg(\tilde{q})\geq\beta_{\Phi}
if
\tilde{q}\not\equiv 0
.
To show
\tilde{q}\not\equiv 0
, observe that only
\Phi(F)
contributes to
\tilde{q}(r)
, that is,

\tilde{q}(r)\penalty\ =\penalty\ r!(m-r)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\text{ of}\\
\text{Hamming weight }r\end{subarray}}q(\boldsymbol{a})\penalty\ =\penalty\ r!(m-r)!\cdot\Phi(F)\penalty\ \neq\penalty\ 0,

where the second equality follows from (
18
).
Since
\tilde{q}(r)\neq 0
(where we use that
{\mathbb{F}}
has characteristic
0
) we indeed have
\tilde{q}\not\equiv 0
.
∎

The lemma now follows directly from Corollary
3.4
and the inequalities

\deg(q_{\Phi})+\beta_{\Phi}/2\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ \deg(q)\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ \deg(q^{\mathrm{sym}})\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ \deg(\tilde{q})\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ \beta_{\Phi}.

For the second claim, we set
q(\boldsymbol{x})=q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
, so
\deg(q)=\deg(q_{\Phi})
holds without the additive term
r
. We proceed as before, ignoring (
18
), and show
\tilde{q}\not\equiv 0
in Claim
4.24
by observing that
\tilde{q}(r)=r!(m-r)!\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}}q(\boldsymbol{a})>0
, since all terms in the sum are nonnegative and (at least) the terms corresponding to
F
are positive.
∎

By combining Theorem
4.21
with Theorems
3.7
(a)
and
3.8
(a)
, we obtain the following.
For functions
f,g\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{Q}}
, we say that
f\in\omega_{\mathrm{inf}}(g)
if there is an infinite sequence
n_{0},n_{1},n_{2},\ldots
such that
f(n_{i})\in\omega(g(n_{i}))
. Note that
f(n_{i})
and
g(n_{i})
take
i
as input.
Recall that
\beta_{\Phi}(k)=\beta_{\Phi^{(k)}}
, where
\Phi^{(k)}
is the
k
-th slice of
\Phi
.

Corollary 4.25

(Theorem
1.5
restated)
.

There are
N_{0},\delta>0
such that the following holds.
Let
k\geq N_{0}
and
0<d\leq k/2
, and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant such that

\beta_{\Phi}\geq d\cdot k.

Then no algorithm solves
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot d})
unless ETH fails.

Moreover, for every computable graph invariant
\Phi
such that

\beta_{\Phi}(k)\in\omega_{\mathrm{inf}}(k),

the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

We note that the lower bound in Corollary
4.25
is essentially tight:
For every
4\leq d\leq k/2
, there is a
k
-vertex property
\Phi
with
\beta_{\Phi}\approx d\cdot k
such that
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
can be solved in time
O(n^{d})
; see
[
13
, Remark 3.6]
.

4.5
Invariants with Small Image

We also derive hardness results for graph invariants with sufficiently small image.
Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over
{\mathbb{F}}
. We write
\mathrm{im}(\Phi)
for its image and define

\iota_{\Phi}\coloneqq|\mathrm{im}(\Phi)|-1.

For a general graph invariant
\Phi
, we define
\iota_{\Phi}(k)\coloneqq\iota_{\Phi^{(k)}}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Let
\tau\colon\mathrm{im}(\Phi)\to{\mathbb{F}}
be a function that, intuitively speaking, relabels the outputs of
\Phi
.
Formally, we define the graph invariant
\Phi\circ\tau
via

(\Phi\circ\tau)(G)\coloneqq\tau(\Phi(G)).

Lemma 4.26
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant over
{\mathbb{F}}
that is not
k
-trivial and let
\tau\colon\mathrm{im}(\Phi)\to{\mathbb{F}}
.
Define
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\circ\tau
and let
F
be a
k
-vertex graph such that
\Psi^{\downarrow}(F)\neq 0
.
Then there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and

|E(H)|\geq\frac{|E(F)|}{\iota_{\Phi}}.

Proof.

Let
m\coloneqq\binom{k}{2}
.
Let
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
be the polynomial representations of
\Phi
and
\Psi
.

Claim 4.27
.

\deg(q_{\Psi})\leq\deg(q_{\Phi})\cdot\iota_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

Using interpolation, there is a univariate polynomial
p(x)
of degree at most
\iota_{\Phi}
such that
p(a)=\tau(a)
for all
a\in\mathrm{im}(\Phi)
.
Now, consider the polynomial
q_{\Psi}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})=p(q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x}))
.
Clearly,
\deg(q_{\Psi}^{\prime})\leq\deg(q_{\Phi})\cdot\iota_{\Phi}
.
Also,
q_{\Psi}^{\prime}
represents
\Psi
over
\{0,1\}^{m}
.

Given a polynomial
r(\boldsymbol{x})
, let
r_{\textsf{red}}
denote the polynomial obtained by replacing
x_{i}^{d}
with
x_{i}
in every monomial.
We clearly have
r(\boldsymbol{a})=r_{\textsf{red}}(\boldsymbol{a})
for all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
.
Since every multilinear polynomial in
m
indeterminates is determined by its evaluations on all values
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, we obtain
q_{\Psi}=(q_{\Psi}^{\prime})_{\textsf{red}}
.
In particular,
\deg(q_{\Psi})\leq\deg(q_{\Psi}^{\prime})
.
∎

Now, the lemma immediately follows from Corollary
3.4
and Claim
4.27
.
Indeed,
|E(F)|\leq\deg(q_{\Psi})
by Corollary
3.4
.
So
\deg(q_{\Phi})\geq|E(F)|/\iota_{\Phi}
by Claim
4.27
.
Using Corollary
3.4
again, we obtain a
k
-vertex graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
|E(H)|\geq\deg(q_{\Phi})\geq|E(F)|/\iota_{\Phi}
.
∎

To demonstrate the applicability of Lemma
4.26
, let us consider the example of monotone graph invariants with small images.
This setting has first been studied in
[
22
]
, and we can use Lemma
4.26
to obtain improved lower bounds.
Let
k\geq 1
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant.
We say that
\Phi
is
monotone
if
\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq\Phi(G)
for every
k
-vertex graph
G
and every edge-induced subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
.
For an arbitrary invariant
\Phi
, we say that
\Phi
is
monotone on
k
-vertex graphs
if
\Phi^{(k)}
is monotone.

Corollary 4.28
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant that is monotone and not
k
-trivial.
Then there is some
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and

|E(H)|\geq\frac{k^{2}}{16\cdot\iota_{\Phi}}.

Proof.

Let
I_{k}
denote the edge-less
k
-vertex graph and let
a\coloneqq\Phi(I_{k})
.
Let
\tau\colon\mathrm{im}(\Phi)\to{\mathbb{Q}}
be defined via
\tau(a)=1
and
\tau(b)=0
for all
a\neq b\in\mathrm{im}(\Phi)
.
Then
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\circ\tau
is a
k
-vertex graph property that is monotone and not
k
-trivial.
By Theorem
4.7
there is a
k
-vertex graph
F
such that
\Psi^{\downarrow}(F)\neq 0
and
|E(F)|\geq k^{2}/16
.
So, by Lemma
4.26
, there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and

|E(H)|\geq\frac{|E(F)|}{\iota_{\Phi}}\geq\frac{k^{2}}{16\cdot\iota_{\Phi}}.\qed

By combining Corollary
4.28
with Theorems
3.7
(a)
and
3.8
(a)
, we obtain the following result.
We say a graph invariant
\Phi
is
infinitely often monotone with sublinear image
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Phi^{(k)}
is monotone and not
k
-trivial, and
\iota_{\Phi}(k)\leq\varepsilon\cdot k
.

Corollary 4.29
.

There are
N_{0},\delta>0
such that the following holds.
Let
k\geq N_{0}
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant that is monotone and not
k
-trivial.
Then no algorithm solves
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k/\iota_{\Phi}})
unless ETH fails.

Moreover, for every computable graph invariant
\Phi
that is infinitely often monotone with sublinear image the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

We note that Corollary
4.29
improves over
[
22
, Main Theorem 2]
which only obtains
#W[1]
-hardness if
\iota_{\Phi}(k)\leq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\sqrt{k}
.

5
All Degrees are Even

In this section we consider the
all-even
property
\Phi_{\sf even}
defined via

\Phi_{\sf even}(G)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\deg_{G}(v)\equiv 0\bmod 2\text{ for all }v\in V(G)\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

i.e.,
\Phi_{\sf even}
contains exactly those graphs where all vertices have even degree.
This property turns out to be interesting since
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)=0
for most graphs
H
.
While
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
is still
#W[1]
-hard, this property allows us to also derive non-trivial upper bounds on the complexity.
Moreover, the fact that
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)=0
for most graphs
H
surprisingly allows us to obtain hardness of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
for other properties
\Phi
.

5.1
Complexity of the All-Even Property

The starting point for our analysis is the observation that each slice
\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}
forms a vector space over the
2
-element field
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
Indeed,
\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}
contains exactly those graphs that are in the
cycle space
of
K_{k}
.
This allows us to use basic tools from linear algebra to determine exactly which graphs
H
satisfy
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The graphs
H
satisfying
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
are precisely the bipartite graphs.

Proof.

Let
H
be a bipartite graph.
For every
S\subseteq E(H)
with
\Phi_{\sf even}(H[S])=1
we have
|S|
even, so every term in

(-1)^{|E(H)|}\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)=\sum_{S\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}(H[S])

is non-negative.
Since the term
\Phi_{\sf even}(H[\emptyset])=1
is strictly positive, we get
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
.

Next, let
H
be a non-bipartite graph and let
C^{*}\subseteq E(H)
be the edges of an odd cycle in
H
.
Consider the cycle space of
H
, i.e., the set of all subgraphs in which all vertices have even degree:

{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq\{S\subseteq E(H)\mid H[S]\in\Phi_{\sf even}\}.

As noted above,
{\mathcal{C}}
can be viewed as a subspace of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{E(H)}
where each
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
is identified with its corresponding indicator vector
v_{C}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{E(H)}
.
Slightly abusing notation, we identify
C
with
v_{C}
.

Claim 5.2
.

There is a basis
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{d}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}
such that
|C_{i}|
is odd for every
i\in[d]
.

Proof.

Clearly, there is a basis
{\mathcal{B}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
C^{*}\in{\mathcal{B}}
.
But then

{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\coloneqq\{C\mid C\in{\mathcal{B}},|C|\text{ is odd}\}\cup\{C\oplus C^{*}\mid C\in{\mathcal{B}},|C|\text{ is even}\}

is also a basis of
{\mathcal{C}}
, where
C\oplus C^{*}
denotes the symmetric difference of
C
and
C^{*}
(i.e., the corresponding vectors over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
are added).
Every element of
{\mathcal{B}}^{*}
has odd cardinality, as desired.
∎

Now let
\{C_{1},\dots,C_{d}\}
be the basis constructed in the claim.
Since
|C_{i}|
is odd for every
i\in[d]
, we get that

|\bigoplus_{i\in M}C_{i}|\equiv|M|\mod 2

for every
M\subseteq[d]
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\cdot\sum_{S\subseteq E(H)}(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}(H[S])

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\cdot\sum_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}(-1)^{|C|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\cdot\sum_{M\subseteq[d]}(-1)^{|\bigoplus_{i\in M}C_{i}|}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{|E(H)|}\cdot\sum_{M\subseteq[d]}(-1)^{|M|}

\displaystyle=0

where the last equality follows from the Binomial Theorem.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

A weaker version of Theorem
5.1
can also be obtained via the Fourier expansion.
Let
k\geq 1
and let
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}
.
The set
\supp(f_{\Psi})
forms a subspace of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{m}
for
m\coloneqq\binom{k}{2}
.
In this case, the support
\supp(\widehat{f}_{\Psi})
in the Fourier expansion is known to be the complement space; see
[
46
, Proposition 3.11]
.
Since
\supp(f_{\Psi})
corresponds to the
cycle space
of
K_{k}
, we obtain that
\supp(\widehat{f}_{\Psi})
corresponds to the
cut space
of
K_{k}
, i.e., the complete bipartite graphs on
k
vertices.
Using (
11
), it is possible to conclude that

1.

\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
for all complete bipartite graphs
H
, and

2.

if
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
then
H
is bipartite.

Our Theorem
5.1
shows that
all
bipartite graphs
H
are in the support of
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)
, not just the complete bipartite graphs.

By combining Theorem
4.17
with Theorems
3.7
(a)
and
3.8
(a)
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

There are
N_{0},\delta>0
such that for every
k\geq N_{0}
there is no algorithm that solves
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)})
in time
O(n^{\delta\cdot k})
unless ETH fails.
Moreover,
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
is
#W[1]
-hard.

In contrast to the results in Section
4
, Theorem
5.1
also allows us to obtain a non-trivial upper bound on the complexity of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
.
This follows from Theorem
3.9
, by observing that each bipartite graph with
k
vertices has vertex cover number at most
\lfloor k/2\rfloor
.

Corollary 5.5
.

The problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi_{\sf even})
can be solved in time
f(k)\cdot n^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}
for some computable function

.

Observe that the algorithm from the last corollary achieves roughly a quadratic speed-up over the brute-force algorithm, which runs in time
f(k)\cdot n^{k}
for some computable function
f
.

5.2
Showing Hardness via the All-Even Property

Next, we use Theorem
5.1
to show hardness of
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
for other graph invariants.
Indeed, Theorem
5.1
shows that the all-even property is “relatively easy” in the sense that only few graphs have non-zero alternating enumerator.
Surprisingly, this fact allows us to prove hardness results for other graph invariants.

More precisely, to obtain hardness results for
\#\textnormal{{IndSub}}(\Phi)
for a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
, the key idea is to take a point-wise product with the all-even property.
Formally, the
point-wise product
of two graph invariants
\Phi,\Gamma
is the graph invariant
\Psi=\Phi\cdot\Gamma
defined via
\Psi(G)=\Phi(G)\cdot\Gamma(G)
.

In our applications, consider a
k
-vertex graph invariant
\Phi
and the pointwise product
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}
.
If
\Psi^{\downarrow}(H^{*})\neq 0
for some
k
-vertex graph
H^{*}
that is “far away” from being bipartite, we can show that there is some
k
-vertex graph
H
with many edges such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
(see Lemma
5.6
).
This allows us to lift hardness results from
\Psi
to the invariant
\Phi
.

In the following, we demonstrate this approach on two examples.
In all cases, the starting point is the following lemma that expresses an alternating enumerator for a pointwise product via the alternating enumerators of its factors.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\Phi,\Gamma
be
k
-vertex graph invariants and
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\Gamma
.
Then, for every graph
H
,

\Psi^{\downarrow}(H)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq E(H)\colon\\
S_{1}\cup S_{2}=E(H)\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\downarrow}(H[S_{1}])\cdot\Gamma^{\downarrow}(H[S_{2}]).

Proof.

Let
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
be the polynomial representations of
\Phi,\Gamma
and
\Psi
, respectively.
Given a polynomial
r(\boldsymbol{x})
, let
r_{\textsf{red}}(\boldsymbol{x})
denote the multilinear polynomial obtained by replacing
x_{i}^{d}
with
x_{i}
in every monomial.
We clearly have
r(\boldsymbol{a})=r_{\textsf{red}}(\boldsymbol{a})
for all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
.
Since every multilinear polynomial in
m
indeterminates is determined by its evaluations on all values
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, we obtain
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=(q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x}))_{\textsf{red}}
, since
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{a})=q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{a})\cdot q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{a})
for all
\boldsymbol{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
.
We now consider the coefficients of
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Let
K=K_{k}
.
In the product
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x})
, monomials
x^{S}
represent multisets
S
of edges from
E(H)
with multiplicity at most
2
per edge.
By Lemma
3.3
, the coefficients of
x^{S}
in
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x})
are
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S])
and
\Gamma^{\downarrow}(K[S])
, respectively.
By collecting monomials after polynomial multiplication, the coefficient of
x^{S}
in
q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x})
equals

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq S\colon\\
S_{1}\cup S_{2}=S\end{subarray}}\Phi^{\downarrow}(K[S_{1}])\cdot\Gamma^{\downarrow}(K[S_{2}]),

(19)

where the union
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
is interpreted as a multiset union, i.e., counting copies.
Collecting monomials after substitution, it follows that the coefficient
c_{S}
of
x^{S}
in
q_{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=(q_{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot q_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{x}))_{\textsf{red}}
for a
set
(rather than multiset)
S\subseteq E(H)
also equals
(
19
), but with
S_{1}\cup S_{2}
interpreted as a set union, i.e., not counting copies.
∎

As the first example, we consider properties
\Phi
whose intersection with
\Phi_{\sf even}
is non-empty and contains only bipartite graphs.
In this situation, setting
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}
, we can show that
\Psi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})\neq 0
.
Using Lemma
5.6
and Theorem
5.1
, we can argue that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
for some graph
H
with
\Omega(k^{2})
edges.

Theorem 5.7
.

For every
k
-vertex graph property
\Phi
whose intersection with
\Phi_{\sf even}
is non-empty and contains only bipartite graphs, there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
K_{\ell}
is a subgraph of
H
for some
\ell\geq\frac{k}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}
.
Note that
\Psi
is also a graph property that corresponds to the intersection of
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\sf even}
.
Then every
k
-vertex graph
H\in\Psi
has an even number of edges.
So

\Psi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})=\sum_{S\subseteq E(K_{k})}(-1)^{|S|}\cdot\Psi(K_{k}[S])=\sum_{S\subseteq E(K_{k})}\Psi(K_{k}[S])

denotes the number of
k
-vertex graphs in
\Psi
.
Since there is at least one such graph, we conclude that
\Psi^{\downarrow}(K_{k})\neq 0
.

By Lemma
5.6
there are sets
S,S_{\sf even}\subseteq E(K_{k})
such that
S\cup S_{\sf even}=E(K_{k})
and
\Phi^{\downarrow}(K_{k}[S])\neq 0
and
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(K_{k}[S_{\sf even}])\neq 0
.
We set
H\coloneqq K_{k}[S]
.
Since
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(K_{k}[S_{\sf even}])\neq 0
, we conclude that
K_{k}[S_{\sf even}]
is bipartite by Theorem
5.1
.
Let
V,W
denote a bipartition of
K_{k}[S_{\sf even}]
.
Then
\binom{V}{2}\subseteq S
and
\binom{W}{2}\subseteq S
since
S\cup S_{\sf even}=E(K_{k})
.
In particular,
H
contains a clique of size at least
\frac{k}{2}
.
∎

As the second example, we consider invariants
\Phi
whose intersection with
\Phi_{\sf even}
is non-empty and contains only few graphs.
A similar approach gives the following variant of Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
k\geq 1
,
0\leq\ell\leq\binom{k}{2}-\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{2}
and let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant such that

1\leq|\supp(\Phi\cdot\Phi_{\sf even})|\leq 2^{\binom{k}{2}-\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{2}-\ell}.

Then there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
|E(H)|\geq\ell
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi\coloneqq\Phi\cdot\Phi_{\sf even}
.
By Theorem
4.1
, there is a
k
-vertex graph
F
such that
\Psi^{\downarrow}(F)\neq 0
and
|E(F)|\geq\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{2}+\ell
.

By Lemma
5.6
there are sets
S,S_{\sf even}\subseteq E(F)
such that
S\cup S_{\sf even}=E(F)
and
\Phi^{\downarrow}(F[S])\neq 0
and
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(F[S_{\sf even}])\neq 0
.
We set
H\coloneqq F[S]
.
Since
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(F[S_{\sf even}])\neq 0
, we conclude that
F[S_{\sf even}]
is bipartite by Theorem

.
In particular,
|S_{\sf even}|\leq\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)^{2}
.
Because
S\cup S_{\sf even}=E(F)
and

|E(H)|=|S|\geq\ell
.
∎

6
Weisfeiler-Leman Dimension

Besides hardness results for induced subgraph counts, our techniques also allow us to bound or even precisely determine the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of such graph invariants.
The
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm (
k
-WL) is a standard heuristic for testing isomorphism of graphs, but it also has surprising connections to various other areas of computer science, including finite model theory and machine learning
[
28
,
29
,
35
,
43
]
.
We omit a detailed description of the WL algorithm here and refer the interested reader to
[
28
,
29
,
35
]
.
Indeed, the following characterization shown by Dvorák
[
23
]
is sufficient for our purposes.

A homomorphism from a graph
F
to a graph
G
is a mapping
\varphi\colon V(F)\to V(G)
such that
\varphi(v)\varphi(w)\in E(G)
for every edge
vw\in E(F)
.
We write
\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G)
to denote the number of homomorphisms from
F
to
G
.
Then two graphs
G,G^{\prime}
are
distinguished
by
k
-WL if
\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G)\neq\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G^{\prime})
for some graph
F
with
\tw(F)\leq k
.

The
Weisfeiler-Leman dimension
of a graph invariant
f
is the minimal
k\geq 1
such that
k
-WL distinguishes between all graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
for which
f(G)\neq f(G^{\prime})
.
For the graph invariants considered in this paper, such a number
k
indeed exists.
Due to the connections of WL to various other areas of computer science, the WL dimension has become a widely studied measure (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
26
,
27
,
37
,
44
]
).
Our results yield bounds on the WL dimension of
f(\star)=\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
for various graph invariants
\Phi
.

More precisely, combining results from
[
12
]
and
[
37
,
44
]
, we establish a connection between the WL dimension and the alternating enumerator of certain graphs with respect to
\Phi
.
We start by covering the necessary tools from
[
12
]
and
[
37
,
44
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

A graph invariant
\Psi
is a
graph motif parameter
if there is a finite collection of graphs
{\mathcal{L}}
and coefficients
\alpha_{F}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
F\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that

\Psi(G)=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{L}}}\alpha_{F}\cdot\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G)

for all graphs
G
.

The WL dimension of graph motif parameters has been determined in
[
37
,
44
]
.

Theorem 6.2

(
[
37
,
44
]
)
.

Let
\Psi
be a
graph motif parameter
and suppose there are a finite collection of pairwise non-isomorphic graphs
{\mathcal{L}}
and coefficients
\alpha_{F}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
for all
F\in{\mathcal{L}}
such that

\Psi(G)=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{L}}}\alpha_{F}\cdot\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G)

for all graphs
G
.
Then the WL dimension of
\Psi
is equal to
\max_{F\in{\mathcal{L}}}\tw(F)
.

Also, it has been shown in
[
12
]
that, for every graph invariant
\Phi
on
k
-vertex graphs, the function
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
is a graph motif parameter.
In fact,
[
12
]
even provides explicit formulas to determine the set
{\mathcal{L}}
and the coefficients
\alpha_{F}
for all graphs
F\in{\mathcal{L}}
.
For the purpose of this paper, the following weaker version is sufficient.

For a graph
H
consider a partition
\rho
of
V(H)
such that each block
B\in\rho
is an independent set.
Then we define
H/\rho
as the graph obtained from
H
by contracting each block to a single vertex.
Formally, we set
V(H/\rho)=\rho
and
E(H/\rho)=\{B_{1}B_{2}\mid\exists v_{1}\in B_{1},v_{2}\in B_{2}\colon v_{1}v_{2}\in E(H)\}
.
We say a graph
F
is a
quotient
of
H
, denoted by
F\preceq H
, if
F
is isomorphic to
H/\rho
for some partition
\rho
of
V(H)
such that each block
B\in\rho
is an independent set.

Lemma 6.3

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant.
Then there is a finite collection of pairwise non-isomorphic graphs
{\mathcal{L}}
and coefficients
\alpha_{F}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(I)

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to G)=\sum_{F\in{\mathcal{L}}}\alpha_{F}\cdot\#\mathrm{Hom}(F\to G)
for all graphs
G
,

(II)

F\in{\mathcal{L}}
for all graphs such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(F)\neq 0
, and

(III)

if
F\in{\mathcal{L}}
, then there is some
k
-vertex graph
H
such that
F\preceq H
and
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
.

The next lemma is an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
\Phi
be a
k
-vertex graph invariant.
Suppose there is a
k
-vertex graph
H
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and
\tw(H)\geq\ell
.
Then the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
is at least
\ell
.

We can combine Lemma
6.4
with Theorems
4.1
,
4.7
,
4.17
,
4.21
,
5.1
,
5.7
and
5.8
as well as Corollary
4.28
to obtain bounds on the WL dimension of certain graph invariants.
Let us only explicitly mention the following consequence, which follows from Theorem
4.7
by setting
p=2
and gives a lower bound that differs from the trivial upper bound of
k-1
only by the constant factor
4
.

Corollary 6.5
.

Let
k\geq 1
and suppose
\Phi
is a
k
-vertex graph property that is monotone and not
k
-trivial.
Then the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
is strictly greater than
\frac{k}{4}
.

For
\Phi_{\sf even}
, we can even determine the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
exactly.

Theorem 6.6
.

For every
k\geq 2
the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
is equal to
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
.

The proof relies on the following simple lemma.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
H
be a graph and
F
a quotient of
H
.
Then
\tw(F)\leq\vc(F)\leq\vc(H)
.

Proof.

The first inequality is folklore.
For the second inequality, let
\rho
be a partition of
H
into independent sets, and let
C
be a vertex cover of
H
.
Then
C/\rho\coloneqq\{B\in\rho\mid B\cap C\neq\emptyset\}
is a vertex cover of
H/\rho
.
So
\vc(H/\rho)\leq\vc(H)
.
Since
F
is a quotient of
H
, the second inequality holds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.6
.

Let
B\coloneqq K_{\left\lfloor k/2\right\rfloor,\left\lceil k/2\right\rceil}
.
We have
\tw(B)=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
and
by Theorem
5.1
, we have
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(B)\neq 0
.
So the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
is at least
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
by Lemma
6.4
.

For the other direction, let
H
be a graph such that
\Phi_{\sf even}^{\downarrow}(H)\neq 0
and let
F
be a quotient of
H
.
Then
H
is bipartite by Theorem
5.1
which implies that
\tw(F)\leq\vc(H)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
by Lemma
6.7
.
This implies that the WL dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi_{\sf even}^{(k)}\to\,\star\,)
is at most
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\right\rfloor
by Theorem
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
(III)
.
∎

7
Conclusion

We show that relatively straightforward algebraic techniques can yield strong complexity results for the problem of counting induced subgraphs satisfying a fixed property
\Phi
.
Our mostly self-contained paper supersedes significant results for this problem obtained over the last decade via different techniques.
Nevertheless, several open problems remain.

Most importantly, we are still lacking a complete understanding for which properties
\Phi
the parameterized problem
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.
Recently, together with Döring
[
13
]
, we identified several non-meager graph properties
\Psi
for which
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is polynomial-time solvable, refuting an earlier conjecture on the complexity of
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)

[
20
,
25
,
54
]
.
This led to the following updated conjecture:

Conjecture 7.1

(
[
13
, Conjecture 4.2]
)
.

Let
\Phi
be a computable graph property and let
(H_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence of graphs of unbounded vertex cover number such that
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H_{k})\neq 0
for all
k\geq 1
.
Then
\#\textnormal{{ParIndSub}}(\Phi)
is
#W[1]
-hard.

A version of this conjecture for graph
invariants
rather than properties is known to fail.

Moreover, it would be interesting to obtain tighter lower bounds for the Weisfeiler-Leman dimension of
\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi\to\,\star\,)
for
k
-vertex graph invariants
\Phi
.
Such bounds translate into tighter inexpressibility results in logics and computational lower bounds for symmetric circuits.

A.1
Vertex-Colored Graphs

In the appendix, we require formal definitions regarding colored graphs that are not relevant for the main part.
A
vertex-colored graph
with colors
C
is an undirected graph
G=(V,E,c)
that is given together with a
coloring

c\colon V(G)\to C
.
Write
G^{\circ}=(V,E)
for the uncolored graph underlying
G
.
We call
G

colorful
if
c
is a bijection, and we call
G

canonically colored
if
C=V(G)
and
c
is the identity function.

For
i\in C
, we write
V_{i}(G)
for the vertices of color
i
.
For
i,j\in C
, we write
E_{ij}(G)
for the edges in
G
with one endpoint of color
i
and another of color
j
.
For
X\subseteq C
and
Y\subseteq\binom{C}{2}
, let
G_{\setminus X,Y}
be the colored graph obtained from
G
by deleting all vertices with colors from
X
and all edges whose endpoints have a color pair from
Y
, i.e,

G_{\setminus X,Y}=\left(V\setminus\bigcup_{i\in X}V_{i},\ E\setminus\bigcup_{ij\in Y}E_{ij}\right).

Given vertex-colored graphs
H
and
G
, we write
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)
for the number of subgraphs
F
of
G
that are isomorphic to
H
by an isomorphism that respects colors; for colored graphs
F
and
H
with colorings
c_{F}
and
c_{H}
, this is an isomorphism
\pi\colon V(F)\to V(H)
with
c_{F}(v)=c_{H}(\pi(v))
for all
v\in V(H)
.
We write
F\cong F^{\prime}
to denote that two (colored or uncolored) graphs
F,F^{\prime}
are isomorphic.

Given a canonically colored graph
H
, a graph
G
on the same set of vertex-colors is
H
-colored
if
E_{ij}(G)=\emptyset
for all
ij\notin E(H)
.
The following fact is immediate.

Fact A.1
.

For vertex-colored graphs
H
and
G
with canonically colored
H
, we have

\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)=\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G_{\setminus\emptyset,\overline{E(H)}}).

Thus, when considering
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)
for graphs
H
and
G
with canonically colored
H
, we may assume
G
to be
H
-colored without loss of generality.

A.2
Isolating Colorful Subgraphs from Linear Combinations

As in similar works
[
10
,
1
]
, we use the inclusion-exclusion principle for reductions between subgraph counting problems. More specifically, inclusion-exclusion allows us to count subgraphs not contained in any
bad
sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{s}
(e.g., avoiding a color they should contain) by determining the cardinalities of intersections of bad sets. We use a version with additive weights that appears, e.g., in elementary probability theory.

Lemma A.2

(see, e.g.,
[
42
, Lemma 1.3]
)
.

Let
\Omega
be a finite set with weights
w:\Omega\to{\mathbb{F}}
for a field
{\mathbb{F}}
.
Given a subset
F\subseteq\Omega
, define its weight as
w(F)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in F}w(x)
.
Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{s}\subseteq\Omega
.
For
X\subseteq[s]
, write
B_{X}\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in X}B_{i}
with the convention
B_{\emptyset}=\Omega
.
Then

w\left(\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}B_{i}\right)=\sum_{X\subseteq[s]}(-1)^{|X|}\,w(B_{X}).

Using this, we can compute
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)
for vertex-colored graphs
H
and
G
, with colorful
H
, provided access to a linear combination of
uncolored
subgraph counts
f(\star)
that contains
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to\,\star\,)
with non-zero coefficient.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\mathbb{F}}
be a field.
For coefficients
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\in{\mathbb{F}}

and pairwise non-isomorphic uncolored graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{s}
, each on exactly
k
vertices, consider the graph invariant

f(\star)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H_{i}\to\,\star\,).

(20)

Let
H
be a canonically colored graph with
H^{\circ}\cong H_{b}
for some
b\in[s]
, and let
G
be an
H
-colored graph.
Then we have

\alpha_{b}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq V(H)\\
Y\subseteq E(H)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|X|+|Y|}f(G_{\setminus X,Y}^{\circ}).

Note that this lemma handles two separate issues for us: First, it allows us to evaluate one
individual
subgraph count
\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)

with an oracle for evaluating a linear combination of subgraph counts.
Moreover, it allows us to reduce subgraph counts from
colorful
graphs to evaluations of
f
on uncolored graphs.
As it turns out, these two issues are best handled together; this was recently done similarly for linear combinations of homomorphism counts
[
11
]
.

Proof.

For
i\in[s]
, denote the set of all
H_{i}
-subgraph copies in
G^{\circ}
as

\Omega_{i}\coloneqq\{F\subseteq G^{\circ}\mid F\cong H_{i}\}.

These sets are pairwise disjoint, since the graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{s}
are pairwise non-isomorphic.
Let
\Omega\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=1}^{s}\Omega_{i}
and endow
\Omega
with a weight function
w:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
by setting
w(F)=\alpha_{i}
if
F\in\Omega_{i}
.
Then the sum in (
20
) can be rephrased as
w(\Omega)
.

Our aim is to isolate the graphs
F\in\Omega
that are isomorphic to
H^{\circ}
.
We achieve this by using the weighted inclusion-exclusion principle to enforce two conditions on graphs
F\in\Omega
to be counted in terms of “bad subsets” of
\Omega
:

•

F
should be vertex-colorful under the coloring of
G
. This is equivalent to requiring
V_{i}(F)\neq\emptyset
for all colors
i\in[k]
,
since
F
has exactly
k
vertices by the prerequisites of the lemma. We capture this by defining, for color
i\in[k]
, the bad set
B_{i}:=\{F\in\Omega\mid V_{i}(F)=\emptyset\}

of subgraphs
F
that miss color
i
.

•

F
should have
E_{ij}(F)\neq\emptyset
for all
ij\in E(H)
.
For
ij\in E(H)
, we therefore define the bad set
B_{ij}:=\{F\in\Omega\mid E_{ij}(F)=\emptyset\}

violating this condition for
ij
.

Write
A\subseteq\Omega
for the set of “good” graphs
F\in\Omega
, i.e.,

A=\Omega\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i\in[k]}B_{i}\ \cup\bigcup_{ij\in E(H)}B_{ij}\right).

Given
F\in\Omega
, we write
F^{c}
for the colored graph obtained by inheriting the colors from
G
.
We claim that
A^{c}\coloneqq\{F^{c}\mid F\in A\}
is precisely the set of color-respecting
H
-copies in
G
, which implies that
w(A)=\alpha_{b}\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)
as required.

To see this claim, observe that the following statements hold for every graph
F^{c}
with
F\in A
:

•

F^{c}
is
colorful
: At least one vertex from each color class of
G
is contained in
F
, because
F\notin B_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
, and
F
has
k
vertices in total.

•

Because
G
is
H
-colored and
F^{c}
is a colorful subgraph of
G
, it follows that
F^{c}\cong H^{\prime}
for a
subgraph

H^{\prime}\subseteq H
.

•

Moreover, since
F\notin B_{ij}
for all
ij\in E(H)
, we have
E_{ij}(F)\neq\emptyset
for
ij\in E(H)
. It follows that
F^{c}
is isomorphic to a
supergraph
of
H
.

Since
F^{c}
is colorful and isomorphic to both a subgraph and a supergraph of
H
, it follows that
F^{c}
is actually isomorphic to
H
.
This shows that
A
contains precisely the colorful
H
-copies in
G
and proves the claim by Lemma
A.2
.
∎

A.3
Complexity Lower Bounds by Isolating Subgraphs

We are ready to use Lemma
A.3
to obtain complexity lower bounds.
First, the following useful fact allows us to restrict alternating enumerators to slices.

Fact A.4
.

For every graph invariant
\Phi
and

, it holds that
(\Phi^{(k)})^{\downarrow}(H)=\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)
for all
k
-vertex graphs
H
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Definition
3.1
, since
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H)
depends only on the values of
\Phi
on
k
-vertex graphs.
∎

We base our hardness results on the following parameterized problem:
For a fixed class of uncolored graphs
\mathcal{H}
, the problem
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
asks, on input an uncolored graph
H\in\mathcal{H}
and a vertex-colored graph

\#\mathrm{Sub}(H^{\textnormal{{can}}}\to G)
, where

denotes the canonically colored version of
H
.

.
Under different names, it has featured in a similar role as a reduction source throughout parameterized counting complexity, e.g., in
[

,

,

]
, and it is known to be hard if

has unbounded treewidth and satisfies very mild constructibility conditions, e.g., recursive enumerability.

Theorem A.5
.

If
\mathcal{H}=(H_{t})_{t\geq 1}
is a recursively enumerable sequence of graphs of unbounded treewidth,
then
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
is
#W[1]
-hard and
\#_{p}\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
is
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
-hard for every prime
p
.

Proof.

For integer
\ell\geq 1
, let
\boxplus_{\ell}
denote the uncolored
\ell\times\ell
square grid, and let
\mathcal{H}_{\boxplus}=\{\boxplus_{\ell}\mid\ell\geq 1\}
denote the class of grids.
The problem
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H}_{\boxplus})
is
#W[1]
-hard by a parsimonious parameterized reduction from counting
k
-cliques (cf.
[
10
, Lemma 5.7]
), so
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H}_{\boxplus})
and
\#_{p}\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H}_{\boxplus})
for primes

are
#W[1]
-hard and
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
-hard, respectively.

The excluded grid theorem
[
49
,
7
]
implies that, for every

, there exists an index

such that
\boxplus_{\ell}
is a minor of
H_{t(\ell)}
.
Consider two recursively enumerable graph classes

and

such that, for every graph

, there is a graph

containing

A parsimonious parameterized reduction from

, Lemma 5.8]
.
Taking

and
\mathcal{H}
as above, it follows that
\#\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
is
#W[1]
-hard and
\#_{p}\textnormal{{ParColSub}}(\mathcal{H})
is
\textnormal{{Mod}}_{p}\textnormal{{W[1]}}
-hard for every prime
p
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

We reduce

to

via Theorem
A.5
and Lemma
A.3
, which carry over to the modular setting.
Towards this, let
\Phi
be a computable graph invariant. We describe a procedure that constructs a graph sequence
\mathcal{H}=(H_{t})_{t\geq 1}
with
\Phi^{\downarrow}(H_{t})\neq 0
and
\tw(H_{t})\geq t
for all

:
Maintain a variable

for the largest treewidth seen so far, and for increasing

, enumerate all graphs on vertex set

Whenever this process reaches a graph

with

and treewidth

(where both conditions on

for all

By the assumed sequence of unbounded treewidth in the theorem statement, this sequence is infinite, and by construction,

is recursively enumerable.

Theorem
A.5
shows that

is

-hard. This problem in turn reduces to

via Lemma

Consider an instance

for

.
We wish to determine

.
Using Fact

, we may assume that

By Corollary

\#\mathrm{IndSub}(\Phi^{(k)}\to\star)=\sum_{F}(\Phi^{(k)})^{\downarrow}(F)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(F\to\star)=:f(\star),

where

ranges over all
k
-vertex graphs.
Invoking Lemma

(\Phi^{(k)})^{\downarrow}(H)\cdot\#\mathrm{Sub}(H\to G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\subseteq V(H)\\
Y\subseteq E(H)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|X|+|Y|}f(G_{\setminus X,Y}^{\circ}).

Since

For the second statement, note that

Thus, the above approach also yields a parameterized reduction from

∎

Appendix B
Few Graphs Avoid a Fixed Induced Graph

A stronger bound on the asymptotic number of induced
F
-free graphs for fixed
F
is known
[
47
]
, but it requires a significantly more sophisticated proof. For completeness, we give an elementary proof of a weaker upper bound.
In the following, an
induced
F
-copy
in
G
is an induced subgraph of
G
isomorphic to
F
.

Theorem B.1
.

For every fixed graph
F
, there is a constant
\varepsilon>0
such that, for
k
large enough, at most
(2-\varepsilon)^{\binom{k}{2}}
labeled graphs on vertex set
[k]
do not contain an induced
F
-copy.

Proof.

We show that, for every fixed
F
, there is a constant
c>0
such that, for
k
large enough, a random graph
H\sim\mathcal{G}(k,1/2)
contains an induced
F
-copy with probability at least
1-\exp(-ck^{2})
.
This implies the theorem, since then at most
\exp(-ck^{2})\cdot 2^{\binom{k}{2}}\leq(2-\varepsilon)^{\binom{k}{2}}
graphs for constant
\varepsilon>0
have
no
induced
F
-copy.

In our inductive proof, we show a stronger statement: Let
A_{k,F,t}
be the event that a random graph
H\sim\mathcal{G}(k,1/2)
contains
at least
t
vertex-disjoint induced
F
-copies, and let

p(k,b,t)\coloneqq\min_{b\text{-vertex graph }F}\mathrm{Pr}(A_{k,F,t}).

Note that
p(k,1,t)=1
for all
t\leq k
. In the main part of the proof, we show:

Claim B.2
.

There are constants
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\ldots
such that, for even
b
and large enough even
k
,

p(k,b,t)\geq p(k/2,b/2,2t)^{2}\cdot\left[1-\exp(-\beta_{b}\cdot t^{2})\right].

(24)

Before proving this, let us first show how the theorem follows from (
24
) by induction:
First, we can assume by padding that
k
and
b
are powers of two.
Then we have

\displaystyle p(k,b,1)

\displaystyle\geq p(k,b,k/b^{2})

\displaystyle\geq p(k/2,b/2,2k/b^{2})^{2}\cdot\left[1-\exp(-\beta_{b}\cdot k^{2}/b^{4})\right]\geq\ldots

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{s=0}^{\log_{2}b-1}\left[1-\exp(-\beta_{b/2^{s}}\cdot 4^{s}\cdot k^{2}/b^{4})\right]^{2^{s}}\geq 1-\exp(-0.99\cdot\beta_{b}\cdot k^{2}/b^{4}).

Here, the first inequality is trivial, as
k/b^{2}
disjoint
F
-copies in particular give
one

F
-copy.
The next inequalities are inductive applications of (
24
), terminating at
p(k/b,1,k/b)=1
.
The last inequality follows from standard bounds and the fact that

for all

, so the factor

grows slowest in the overall product over

.

In the remainder of the proof, we show Claim
B.2
.
Let

and let
F
be a
b
-vertex graph minimizing
\mathrm{Pr}(A_{k,F,t})
.
Partition

equitably into

and
F_{R}
, and partition

equitably into

and
H_{R}
, by choosing the first half of vertices in the respective graphs.
With probability at least

, there are
2t
vertex-disjoint induced

-copies

in
H_{L}
, and analogous copies

in
H_{R}
.
Note that these two events are independent.

Construct an auxiliary bipartite graph

on vertices
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{2t}
and

corresponding to the copies of

and
F_{R}
in

.
The edge

is present in

induces an

-copy in

.

Claim B.3
.

The distribution of

follows a

random graph model with
\alpha k
vertices and independent edge probability

.

Proof.

Indeed,

occurs with constant probability

, depending only on

. Moreover, these events are mutually independent:
Whether

.
The cross-edges

∎

Next, observe that every

.
A simple union bound allows us to show that such a matching is very likely to occur.

Claim B.4

There is a constant
