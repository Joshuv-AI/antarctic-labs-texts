There and Back Again: Learning to Simulate Radar Data for Real-World Applications
Rob Weston, Oiwi Parker Jones, Ingmar Posner

Abstract
Simulating realistic radar data has the potential to significantly accelerate the development of data-driven approaches to radar processing. However, it is fraught with difficulty due to the notoriously complex image formation process. Here we propose to learn a radar sensor model capable of synthesising faithful radar observations based on simulated elevation maps. In particular, we adopt an adversarial approach to learning a forward sensor model from unaligned radar examples. In addition, modelling the backward model encourages the output to remain aligned to the world state through a cyclical consistency criterion. The backward model is further constrained to predict elevation maps from real radar data that are grounded by partial measurements obtained from corresponding lidar scans. Both models are trained in a joint optimisation. We demonstrate the efficacy of our approach by evaluating a down-stream segmentation model trained purely on simulated data in a real-world deployment. This achieves performance within four percentage points of the same model trained entirely on real data.



I
Introduction

The long sensing range of radar and its resilience to adverse environmental conditions make it an attractive complement to lidar and vision for robotics and autonomous driving applications. However, radar is notoriously challenging to interpret; multi-path phenomena, limited resolution, and pernicious noise artefacts arising throughout the complex and imperfect measurement pipeline pose significant challenges to radar-based perception systems. In recent years, data-driven approaches have made significant strides to overcome these challenges across a range of tasks in robotics

Akin to progress in the use of other sensing modalities, simulation has the potential to significantly accelerate the development and deployment of radar-based methods by reducing the need for human annotation and automating the data-gathering process. The importance of learning from simulated data can be seen across a wide range of tasks in vision and lidar

; and it is echoed in the rapid development of multiple autonomous driving simulators capable of simulating complex worlds, designed to facilitate these approaches

Inspired by the impact simulation has brought to the development of sophisticated vision and lidar systems, the overarching goal of our work is to enable the training of data-driven models for radar interpretation in simulation. To successfully train models in simulation, we therefore consider learning a radar sensor model that is able to faithfully simulate radar observations, such that the domain gap experienced by a down-stream system when trained on either real or synthetic data is minimal. In particular, we aim to interface with existing simulators already capable of synthesising complex scenes and adopted widely by the community. In meeting this requirement, we consider simulating radar observations given a layout of the world, supplied to our model in the form of an elevation map. Noise processes arising throughout the radar sensing pipeline give radar an inherently stochastic nature; to accurately replicate this, we adopt a probabilistic approach, using a
deep implicit model

[
14
]
to capture a distribution over possible radar sensor measurements.

Adopting an adversarial paradigm, we train our sensor model to explicitly generate realistic radar observations from
simulated
height maps using unaligned real-world radar data. As shown in Figure
1
, alongside the forward sensor model we also learn the backward model to infer the elevation state of the real world from radar. This allows us to further constrain training by enforcing cyclical consistency
[
15
]
between the forward and backward processes. Alongside this we ground the predictions of our backward model in the real world by enforcing alignment with partial height measurements generated automatically from lidar.

Through our approach, we demonstrate the feasibility of training radar models in simulation to the best of our knowledge for the first time. In doing so, our approach achieves performance within
4\%
compared to that of the same model trained in the real world.

III
Deep Radar Simulation

III-A

Problem Formulation

Let
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
denote a real radar observation generated from real world state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}),\quad\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})

(1)

where
p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
is the real radar sensor model. Specifically, we consider radar observations of the form
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Phi\times R}
where
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}_{ij}
gives the power returned from the world at polar-coordinate
(\mathrm{\phi}_{i},\mathrm{r}_{j})\in\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\Phi}\times\{\mathrm{r}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{R}
.

We aim to learn a radar sensor model
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
capable of generating feasible radar observations
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
from a
simulated
state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{w}})
. We model the world state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{\Phi}\times\mathrm{R}}
, with the same dimensions as
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
, where
\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{i,j}
gives the elevation of the world location
(\phi_{i},r_{i})
. In addition to containing the necessary information to generate
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
(such as the shape, size, and position of objects in the scene), this representation allows us to easily interface with a preexisting simulation environment, such as CARLA
[
11
]
. Whilst
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
is easily simulated it is more challenging to measure in the real world. We assume that we are able to obtain
partial
measurements
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}=m(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
using lidar, where due to the limited range and number of beams of lidar many locations
(\phi_{i},r_{i})
are without an elevation measurement. We therefore have at our disposal real world observations
\mathtt{R}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*})_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
and simulated observations
\mathtt{S}=\{\bm{\mathrm{w}}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
with which to train our approach.

III-B

Stochastic Simulation Using Deep Implicit Models

For the same world state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}
, aleatoric sensor noise results in radar sensor measurements
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}_{t}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
and
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}_{t+1}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
that are inherently stochastic. To capture the stochasticity in the mapping from
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
to
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
– mimicking the true sensing process – we introduce a latent variable
\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
; using a neural network
g_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}},\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}})
with parameters
\theta_{\mathrm{x}}
, for a fixed
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
, we are able to sample multiple possible
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
by sampling
\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
. This allows us to implicitly capture a distribution
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
over possible radar observations sampling
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
as

\bm{\mathrm{x}}=g_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}},\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}};\theta_{\mathrm{x}})\quad\text{with}\quad\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}}\sim p_{\mathrm{\epsilon}}(\bm{\mathrm{\epsilon}})\text{ .}

(2)

Through this approach we are able to leverage the representational power of a deep neural network
g_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}
to learn the complex mapping from world state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
to radar sensor observation
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
, whilst simultaneously implicitly learning how to characterise the uncertainty in the sensing process.

III-C

Learning only from Real Observations

If we could observe real-world observation-state pairs
\mathtt{R}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
we might train
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
by minimising

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})}[\ell(\theta_{\mathrm{x}};\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})]\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\ell(\theta_{\mathrm{x}};\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}_{n},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}_{n})

(3)

where
\ell(\theta_{\mathrm{x}};\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
is a regression loss (eg. MAE or MSE) between the real
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
and simulated observations
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
. However, only partial partial measurements of the world state
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}=m(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
are available. In addition, by training our model in this way,

is only trained on real state observations
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\in\mathtt{R}
and a significant domain gap between
\bm{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathtt{S}
and
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\in\mathtt{R}
is still likely to persist. We posit that models that explicitly incorporate simulated state observations
\bm{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathtt{S}
into the training loop are more likely to generate feasible radar observations in simulation. We now propose how this might be achieved.

III-F

Learning from Partial Lidar Measurements

Another benefit of learning the backward model
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{w}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*})
is that it allows us to learn from partial measurements
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\sim p(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})
when they are available. This is achieved through an alignment consistency objective,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{w}}(\theta_{\mathrm{w}})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})}[\ell(\theta_{\mathrm{w}};\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*})]

(12)

where
\ell(\theta_{\mathrm{w}};\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*})=\|(\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}-g_{\theta_{\mathrm{w}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{\kappa}}))\odot\mathbb{I}(\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*})\|_{1}

is the joint distribution over real observation-state pairs with
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
an element-wise indicator function returning
1

exists or

Considering a combined training objective,

\mathcal{L}(\theta_{\mathrm{x}},\theta_{\mathrm{w}})=\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{gw}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{w}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cx}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{x}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cw}}\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{w}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{aw}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{w}}

and

– explicitly encouraging

to generate realistic radar observations in simulation, training on
\bm{\mathrm{w}}\in\mathtt{S}
, whilst also learning from aligned pairs
\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}\in\mathtt{R}
when measurements

IV-A

Self-Supervised Dataset Generation

In section
III-A

\mathtt{R}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*})_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}

IV-A
1
Generating
\mathtt{R}

We generate
\mathtt{R}
from the Oxford Radar RobotCar Dataset
[
41
,
42
]
. We partition the dataset into train and test sets with the training set being composed of
29
10km loops (generating
222420
observations) with
3
loops being reserved for testing (
23460
observations), resulting in an approximate
90:10
split. Each
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
corresponds to the output of a Navtech CTS350x FMCW radar rotating about its vertical axis, down-sampled to a
0.35\text{m}
resolution and scaled to give
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}\in[-1,1]^{400\times 471}
. This corresponds to a
360^{\circ}
field-of-view with maximum observable height

. We construct partial height map measurements

by combining the output of two HDL32E Velodyne Lidars: as a result of differing sensing frequencies (radar at

and lidar at

is matched to multiple lidar scans to maintain accurate labelling. The lidar pointclouds are filtered to coincide with the radar’s horizontal field-of-view

, before being binned onto a polar grid and labelling each grid cell with the maximum height of any point falling within it. Each
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}
is then scaled from the interval
[-2.2\text{m},5.2\text{m}]
to the interval
[-1,1]
with any grid cell without a label assigned the value

. Due to the limited number of lasers and range of each lidar, each
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}
is only a partial measurement
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}=m(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*})

IV-C

Evaluation

IV-C
1
Radar Simulation

One of the central motivations for our approach was to develop a radar sensor model
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
to train new models
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
in simulation that generalise to the real world. With this in mind, to assess the realism of radar observations
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
, we train a model
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
in simulation minimising
\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})}[\ell(\bm{\mathrm{y}},y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))]
with respect to
\alpha
where
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
is a loss between the actual and predicted target. We then assess the realism of
p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
as,
\mathbb{E}_{p(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})}\left[m(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*},y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\theta_{\mathrm{x}}))\right]
evaluating the performance of the trained model
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}|\theta_{\mathrm{x}})
in the real world with metric
m(\cdot,\cdot)
. Specifically, we consider training a model to segment the world into occupied, free, and unknown space. To successfully train
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
this task requires
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{x}}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}|\bm{\mathrm{w}})
to replicate realistic noise artefacts (which
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
must learn to overcome) whilst ensuring the mapping from world state
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
to radar observation
\bm{\mathrm{x}}
is as faithful as possible to the real-world mapping
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}
to
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*}
.

We train and evaluate the segmentation model using datasets
\mathtt{S}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})\}_{n=1}^{N}
and
\mathtt{R}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{N}
, generated from the sets held out in sections
IV-A1
and
IV-A2
. In both cases labels are generated automatically from either partial
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}
or full
\bm{\mathrm{w}}
measurements of the world state in a similar approach to
[
1
]
: after extracting the ground plane, any cell with a height measurement is labelled as occupied, all cells before the first return are labelled as free, and anything else is labelled unknown. For all segmentation networks
y_{\alpha}
, a U-Net architecture
[
48
]
is used with
6
levels, doubling the features and halving spatial resolution at each level, starting with
8
features at the input and allowing information to flow from encoder to decoder using skip connections. Each model is trained with a batch size of
8
using the Adam
[
47
]
optimizer (learning rate
1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}
) to minimise the cross-entropy loss, with an additional weighting of
50
applied to the occupied class to account for class imbalance. The model attaining the highest IoU over
4
epochs of training (tested on a
10\%
hold out dataset after each epoch) is used for evaluation – usually the first or second.

In keeping with
[
49
]
and as proposed in the Pascal Voc Challenge
[
50
]
each model is evaluated using the mean Intersection over Union metric
[
51
]
(mIoU)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta_{\mathrm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c}\left[\frac{\mathrm{TP}(c)}{\mathrm{FP}(c)+\mathrm{FN}(c)+\mathrm{TP}(c)}\right]

(14)

where the true positives
\mathrm{TP}
, false positives
\mathrm{FP}
and false negatives
\mathrm{FN}
are determined for each class (occupied and free) comparing
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}_{i,j}
and
y_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})_{i,j}
at each index
(i,j)
across the entire dataset
\mathtt{R}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})\}_{n=1}^{N}
. Any cell that is predicted unknown but labelled as free or occupied is counted as a false negative.

IV-C
2
Evaluating the Backward Model

The heights predicted by our backward model
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{w}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*})
are evaluated using absolute mean error between
\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}\sim p_{\theta_{\mathrm{w}}}(\bm{\mathrm{w}}^{*}|\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{*})
and partial state measurements
\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}
over the test set
\mathtt{R}^{\prime}
from section
IV-A1

\bm{\mathrm{m}}^{*}_{i,j}
exist. As a result of the class imbalance between height labels corresponding to the ground plane and targets in the scene, we evaluate this metric over occupied and free space independently, presenting both alongside their average. All height evaluations were run for four models trained with different random seeds, from which we computed averages and error bounds presented as standard deviations.

VI
Conclusion

This work demonstrates that it is possible to develop a data-driven approach to radar-simulation capable of training downstream systems that are readily deployable in the real world. By simulating radar sensor observations from elevation maps we are able to interface with existing simulators already capable of synthesising complex real-world scenes. We adopt an inherently stochastic and data-driven approach, capturing a mapping from state to radar sensor model (alongside sensor noise). We learn our approach from real radar measurements, simulated elevation maps, and partial elevation measurements generated in lidar. To encourage our model to simulate realistic radar observations, we adopt an adversarial approach model the backward mapping to further constrain learning through cyclical consistency losses and partial alignment to real-world elevation maps.

Using our approach to train a segmentation system in simulation, we find that when deployed in the real world, the system is able to operate with a mIoU of
0.664
performing comparably to a model trained in the real world only. To the best of our knowledge this is the first time that the feasibility of training models in simulation has been demonstrated in radar. The backward model learnt as part of the same training setup can be used to infer the height state of the world with an accuracy of
23\text{cm}
, using only partial elevation measurements, whilst generalising to regions of space for which no labels exist.

Whilst our model is able to successfully train segmentation models in simulation that partition the world into occupied, free, and unknown space, early experiments (on a limited test set) found that partitioning occupied space into finer grained classes was significantly more challenging. This constitutes an interesting area for future research.
