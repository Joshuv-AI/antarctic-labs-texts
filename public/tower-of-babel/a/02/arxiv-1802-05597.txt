Theoretical aspects of microscale acoustofluidics
Henrik Bruus

Abstract
Short biography: Henrik Bruus received his PhD degree in physics from the Niels Bohr Institute, University of Copenhagen in 1990, and then worked as postdoc at Nordic Institute of Theoretical Physics 1990-92, Yale University 1992-94 and CNRS Grenoble 1994-96. He returned to the Niels Bohr Institute as associate professor 1997-2001, before moving to the Technical University of Denmark in 2001. There, he became full professor of lab-chip systems in 2005 and of theoretical physics in 2012. His current research interests comprise micro/nanofluidics, acoustofluidics, electrokinetics, the physics of on-chip cell manipulation, the motion of sugar in living plants, and topology-optimized microflows. He has (co)au- thored 134 journal papers on condensed matter physics and microfluidics, 180 conference papers, and 2 monographs, the latest being ”Theoretical Microfluidics”, Oxford University Press (2008). Abstract . In this contribution, I summarize some of the recent results within theory and simulation of microscale acoustofluidic systems that I have obtained in collaboration with my students and international colleagues. The main emphasis is on three dynamical effects induced by external ultrasound fields acting on aqueous solutions and particle suspensions: The acoustic radiation force acting on suspended micro- and nanoparticles, the acoustic streaming appearing in the fluid, and the newly discovered acoustic body force acting on inhomogeneous solutions.



I
Introduction

Microscale acoustofluidic devices are used increasingly in biology, environmental and forensic sciences, and clinical diagnostics as reviewed in the Lab Chip Tutorial Series
Bruus et al. 2011
, reprinted by the Royal Society of Chemistry in the book
Microscale Acoustofluidics

Laurell and Lenshof 2014
.

Following the standard method described in textbooks such as Lighthill
Lighthill 2002
, Pierce
Pierce 1991
, and Landau & Lifshitz
Landau and Lifshitz 1993
, we formulate the linear acoustics in fluids and elastic solids, as well as the non-linear acoustic dynamics in fluids using first-and second-order perturbation theory in the notation established in the textbook by Bruus
Bruus 2008
. The basic fields in both fluids and solids are the density
\rho
, the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
, the internal energy density
\varepsilon
, and the temperature
T
. These fields are supplemented in the fluids by the pressure
p
and the velocity
\bm{v}
, and in the solids by the displacement
\bm{u}
.

II
Governing equations

For fluids, advection causes non-linearities, and the governing equations are formulated in terms of the fluxes of the mass density
\rho
, the momentum density
\rho\bm{v}
, and the energy density
\rho\big(\varepsilon+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}v^{2}\big)
under the simplifying assumption of no net body forces and no heat sources,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\big[-\!\rho\bm{v}\big],

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\big[\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}-\rho\bm{v}\bm{v}\big],

(1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\rho\varepsilon\!+\!\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}\rho v^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\big[k_{\mathrm{th}}\bm{\nabla}T+\!\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}-\!\rho(\varepsilon+\mbox{$\frac{1}{2}$}v^{2})\bm{v}\big],

(1c)

where the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}
for fluids is

\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{f}}=-\Big[p\!+\!\big(\mbox{$\frac{2}{3}$}\eta_{0}-\eta_{\mathrm{B}}\big)\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{v}\Big]\bm{1}+\eta_{0}\!\Big[\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{v})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\Big].

(2)

Here, the material parameters are the shear viscosity
\eta_{0}
, the bulk viscosity
\eta_{\mathrm{B}}
, and the thermal conductivity
k_{\mathrm{th}}
, while
\bm{1}
is the unit tensor and ’T’ means tensor transpose.

For elastic solids with an isobaric thermal expansion coefficient
{\alpha_{p}}
, an isothermal compressibility
\kappa_{T}
, and a specific heat ratio
\gamma
, the governing equations are linear due to the lack of advection,

\displaystyle\rho\partial^{\,2}_{t}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{s}}-\frac{{\alpha_{p}}}{\kappa_{T}}\>\bm{\nabla}T,

(3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}T

\displaystyle=\gamma D_{\mathrm{th}}\nabla^{2}T-\frac{\gamma-1}{{\alpha_{p}}}\partial_{t}\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{u},

(3b)

where the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{s}}
for solids is given in terms of the longitudinal and transverse sound speeds
c_{\mathrm{L}}
and
c_{\mathrm{T}}
, respectively,

\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{s}}=\rho\Big[c^{2}_{\mathrm{L}}-2c^{2}_{\mathrm{T}}\Big](\bm{\nabla}\!\cdot\!\bm{u})\bm{1}+\rho c^{2}_{\mathrm{T}}\!\Big[\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\Big].

(4)

III
Acoustic perturbation theory

We consider a system initially at rest, and which has the density
\rho_{0}
and velocity
\bm{v}=\bm{0}
. We assume that the system is perturbed by imposing an harmonically oscillating displacement with a small position-dependent amplitude
\bm{d}
on parts of the boundary. As a result, harmonic variations
\rho_{1}
in the density and
\bm{v}_{1}
in the velocity are induced. For a medium with the speed of sound
c_{0}
, the magnitude of the perturbation can then be characterized by the dimensionless acoustic Mach number Ma,

\text{Ma}=\frac{v_{1}}{c_{0}}=\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{0}}\ll 1.

(5)

Typically in microscale acoustofluidics
\text{Ma}\approx 10^{-4}
, so a perturbation expansion of any field
f
in order of Ma makes sense,
f=f_{0}+f_{1}+f_{2}+\ldots
, where
f_{n}\propto\text{Ma}^{n}
. We expand the non-linear fluid equations to second order, while the linear solid equations terminate at first order,

\begin{array}[]{rll}T&=T_{0}+T_{1}+T_{2},&\quad\bm{u}=\bm{0}+\bm{u}_{1},\\[2.84526pt]
\rho&=\rho_{0}+\rho_{1}+\rho_{2},&\quad\bm{v}=\bm{0}+\bm{v}_{1}+\bm{v}_{2},\\[4.2679pt]
p&=p_{0}+p_{1}+p_{2}.&\end{array}

(6)

III.1
The first-order equations

Using the complex-valued representation
\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}
of harmonic time dependence with angular frequency
\omega
, the actuation of the system is modeled by prescribing the motion of its exterior boundaries at positions
\bm{r}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{d}(\bm{r}_{0})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}},

\displaystyle\;\text{ on solid boundaries},

(7a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\bm{d}(\bm{r}_{0})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}},

\displaystyle\;\text{ on fluid boundaries}.

(7b)

All first-order fields thus have the form
g_{1}=g_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}
, which changes the time derivative into a simple complex-valued factor,
\partial_{t}\rightarrow-\mathrm{i}\omega
, and consequently, all first-order terms contain the harmonic phase-factor
\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}
. This factor is divided out leaving the time-independent, complex-valued amplitude fields
g_{1}(\bm{r})
.

For fluids in the isentropic case, we have the simplifying relations
p_{1}=c^{\,2}_{0}\rho_{1}
and
T_{1}=\frac{{\alpha_{p}}T_{0}}{c_{\mathrm{p}}\rho_{0}}\>p_{1}
, and the governing equations for the first-order fields become

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega p_{1}

\displaystyle=-\rho_{0}c^{\,2}_{0}\>\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1},

(8a)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\rho_{0}\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1f}},

(8b)

where the first-order stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1f}}
is Eq. (
2
) with all fields substituted by first-order fields. Similarly for the solids, we obtain the simplified first-order equation of motion,

-\omega^{2}\rho_{0}\bm{u}_{1}=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1s}}.

(9)

At internal fluid-solid interfaces with the surface normal vector
\bm{n}
, we demand continuity of the velocity and stress fields,

\left.\begin{array}[]{rl}\bm{v}_{1}=&-\mathrm{i}\omega\bm{u}_{1}\\[2.84526pt]
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1f}}\!\cdot\!\bm{n}=&\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1s}}\!\cdot\!\bm{n}\end{array}\!\!\right\}\!,\text{internal fluid-solid interfaces}.

(10)

III.2
The time-averaged second-order equations

The basic acoustofluidic dynamics takes place in fluids on a ms-time scale, much slower than the ultrasonic
µs
-time scale. It is therefore described by the time-averaged response over one oscillation period, denoted by angled brackets
\langle\ldots\rangle
in the following. The terms of lowest order contributing to this time average are second-order terms. Moreover, since the time-average of the time derivative
\big\langle\partial_{t}g\big\rangle
of any field
g
in time-periodic systems vanish,
\langle\partial_{t}g\rangle=0
, the second-order governing equations of fluids become

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\Big[\rho_{0}\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle+\big\langle\rho_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=0,

(11a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\Big[\big\langle\bm{\sigma}_{2}\big\rangle-\rho_{0}\big\langle\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=\bm{0}.

(11b)

In terms of the momentum flux density tensor

\bm{\Pi}=\rho\bm{v}\bm{v}-\bm{\sigma},

(12)

the second-order equation (
11b
) becomes

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big\rangle=\bm{0}.

(13)

On the boundary, the velocity is oscillating harmonically, so there
\big\langle\bm{v}\big\rangle=\bm{0}
. On the other hand, a boundary point with the time-averaged position
\bm{r}_{0}
has the time-dependent position
\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{0}+\bm{u}_{1}
, so by a second-order Taylor expansion
\big\langle\bm{v}(\bm{r})\big\rangle=\big\langle\bm{v}(\bm{r}_{0})+\bm{u}_{1}(\bm{r}_{0})\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}(\bm{r}_{0})\big\rangle
. Thus on the boundary
\bm{0}=\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle+\big\langle\bm{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle
, and using
\bm{u}_{1}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega}\bm{v}_{1}
from Eq. (
10
), the boundary condition for
\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle
becomes

\big\langle\bm{v}_{2}\big\rangle=-\frac{1}{\omega}\big\langle(\mathrm{i}\bm{v}_{1})\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle,\text{ on fluid boundaries}.

(14)

IV
The radiation force

In a series of papers, we have studied the acoustic radiation force
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
on a spherical particle of radius
a
suspended in an inviscid
Bruus 2012
, viscous
Settnes and Bruus 2012
, and thermoviscous fluid
Karlsen and Bruus 2015
with kinematic viscosity
\nu_{0}
and thermal diffusivity
D_{\mathrm{th}}
. When exposed to an incident acoustic wave of angular frequency
\omega
and wavenumber
k
, a viscous and thermal boundary layer of width
\delta_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{2\nu_{0}/\omega}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{t}}=\sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{th}}/\omega}
, respectively, develop at the particle surface. In the long-wave limit
a,\delta_{\mathrm{s}},\delta_{\mathrm{t}}\ll 1/k
, we can express
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
as the time-averaged momentum flux through any static surface
\partial\Omega_{0}
encompassing the particle,

\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}=-\left\langle\oint_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\!\!\bm{\Pi}_{2}\cdot\bm{n}\>\mathrm{d}a\right\rangle=-\int_{\Omega_{0}}\!\!\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big\rangle\>\mathrm{d}V.

(15)

In spite of Eq. (
13
), the expression for
\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}
is not zero because of delta-function singularities which appear in
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big\rangle
due to the scattering of the incident acoustic wave on the particle. When properly evaluating these singularities we find
Settnes and Bruus 2012
,

\displaystyle\bm{F}^{\mathrm{rad}}\!=-\frac{4}{3}\pi a^{3}\!\Big[\kappa_{0}\big\langle(f_{0}p_{1}^{\mathrm{in}})\!\bm{\nabla}p_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\big\rangle\big]-\frac{3}{2}\rho_{0}\big\langle(f_{1}\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{in}})\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}\big\rangle\Big],

(16)

where
p_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\bm{v}_{1}^{\mathrm{in}}
are the first-order pressure and velocity fields of the incident acoustic wave evaluated at the particle position,
\kappa_{0}
is the isentropic compressibility of the fluid, while
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are the so-called mono- and dipole scattering coefficients, respectively. The latter are involved functions of the material parameters as shown in Ref.
Karlsen and Bruus 2015
. For a fluid particle (a droplet),
f_{0}
becomes

\displaystyle f_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}=1-\tilde{\kappa}_{s}+3(\gamma-1)\left(1-\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}_{p}}{\tilde{\rho}_{0}\>\tilde{c}_{p}}\right)^{2}H(x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime}),

(17a)

\displaystyle H(x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime})=\dfrac{1}{x_{\mathrm{t}}^{2}}\left[\dfrac{1}{1-\mathrm{i}x_{\mathrm{t}}}-\dfrac{1}{\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{th}}}\dfrac{\tan x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime}}{\tan x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime}-x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime}}\right]^{-1},

(17b)

where the tilde represent the droplet-to-medium ratio of the various material parameters, and where
H(x_{\mathrm{t}},x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime})
is a function of the particle radius
a
through the non-dimensionalized thermal wavenumber
x_{\mathrm{t}}\approx(1+\mathrm{i})a/\delta_{\mathrm{t}}
for the fluid medium, and likewise for
x_{\mathrm{t}}^{\prime}
of the droplet.

Similarly,
f_{1}
becomes

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\mathrm{fl}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\left(\tilde{\rho_{0}}-1\right)\left(1+F(x_{\mathrm{s}},x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime})-G(x_{\mathrm{s}})\right)}{\left(2\tilde{\rho_{0}}+1\right)\big[1+F(x_{\mathrm{s}},x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime})\big]-3G(x_{\mathrm{s}})},

(18a)

\displaystyle G(x_{\mathrm{s}})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{3}{x_{\mathrm{s}}}\left(\dfrac{1}{x_{\mathrm{s}}}-\mathrm{i}\right),

(18b)

\displaystyle F(x_{\mathrm{s}},x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-\mathrm{i}x_{\mathrm{s}}}{2(1-\tilde{\eta}_{0})+\dfrac{\tilde{\eta}_{0}x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime 2}(\tan x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime})}{(3-x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime 2})\tan x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-3x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}}},

(18c)

where
x_{\mathrm{s}}=(1+\mathrm{i})a/\delta_{\mathrm{s}}
is the non-dimensionalized shear-viscous wavenumber for the fluid medium, and likewise for
x_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}
of the droplet.

A full experimental verification of our theoretical expression for the acoustic radiation force has not yet been carried out. However, we have successfully obtained partial experimental verifications
Barnkob et al. 2010
;
Augustsson et al. 2011
;
Barnkob et al. 2012a
.

V
Acoustic streaming in homogeneous fluids

In a series papers, we have studied theoretically and numerically the acoustic streaming in hard-walled acoustofluidic devices
Muller et al. 2012
;
Muller et al. 2013
;
Muller and Bruus 2014
;
Antfolk et al. 2014
;
Muller and Bruus 2015
. All types of time-averaged acoustic flows are driven by an acoustic body force
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
, which is given in terms of the non-zero divergence of the acoustic part
\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{\mathrm{ac}}\big\rangle
of the momentum-flux-density tensor
\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big\rangle
. We define
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
as
Karlsen et al. 2016

\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{\mathrm{ac}}\big\rangle,

(19a)

where
\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{\mathrm{ac}}\big\rangle
is the part of
\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{2}\big\rangle
that contains all the time-averaged products of first-order acoustic fields,

\displaystyle\big\langle\bm{\Pi}_{\mathrm{ac}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle p_{11}\big\rangle\mathbf{I}+\big\langle\rho_{0}\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle,\quad\quad\mathrm{with}

(19b)

\displaystyle\big\langle p_{11}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{0}|p_{1}|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}|\bm{v}_{1}|^{2}\big\rangle.

(19c)

In this expression,
\big\langle p_{11}\big\rangle
is a local time-averaged acoustic pressure. Using the body force
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
, we rewrite Eq. (
11b
) as an equation for a body-force-driven Stokes flow,

\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\sigma}_{2}\big\rangle=\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}.\\

(20)

For a planar rigid wall and a homogeneous fluid, we recover the well-known Rayleigh streaming, with pairs of vortices and anti-vortices each with a spatial extension of
\frac{1}{4}\lambda\times\frac{1}{4}\lambda

Muller et al. 2012
. In the same work we added tracer particles and studied the transition in particle dynamics from being dominated by the radiation force to be dominated by the Stokes drag force from the acoustic streaming. We determined the critical radius
a_{0}
for the cross-over between these two regimes to be around
1~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
for polystyrene particles in water at 2 MHz
Muller et al. 2012
;
Barnkob et al. 2012b
.

In subsequent studies of rectangular microchannels, we compared the theoretical predictions of our streaming model with experimental observations for the standard case of a horizontal standing half-wave acoustic resonance. In Ref.
Barnkob et al. 2012b
we verified experimentally the model for the cross-over from radiation-force to streaming-drag dominated particle dynamics, and in Ref.
Muller et al. 2013
we found good agreement between experimental, analytical, and numerical determinations of streaming-dominated particle paths in all three spatial dimensions. Finally in Ref.
Antfolk et al. 2014
, we found good agreement between theory and experiments for the handling of sub-
µm
particles in the special case of single-vortex streaming obtained by phase-specific oscillation of both pairs of opposite walls in a rectangular channel.

VI
Acoustic body-force handling of inhomogeneous solutions

Recently, it was discovered experimentally that inhomogeneities in density
\rho_{0}
, compressibility
\kappa_{0}
, and viscosity
\eta_{0}
of a fluid, introduced by a solute concentration
s
, can be relocated and stabilized into field-dependent configurations by use of acoustic fields
Deshmukh et al. 2014
;
Augustsson et al. 2016
. Based on the separation of the fast
µs
-time-scale
t
of acoustics and the slow
ms
-time-scale
\tau
of hydrodynamics, we subsequently developed the following theory
Karlsen et al. 2016
for the acoustic body force responsible for the observed phenomena.

The density
\rho
is written as the sum of the slowly evolving hydrodynamic density
\rho_{0}
and the quickly oscillating density perturbation
\rho_{1}
,

\rho=\rho_{0}(\bm{r},\tau)+\rho_{1}(\bm{r},\tau)\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}},

(21)

where
\rho_{1}
is associated with the acoustic pressure and velocity fields
p_{1}
and
\bm{v}_{1}
. Now, the two equations for
p_{1}
and
\rho_{1}
cannot be combined into one as in Eq. (
8
), so
inhomogeneous acoustics are given by three equations,

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\kappa_{0}p_{1}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1},

(22a)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\rho_{0}\kappa_{0}p_{1}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\omega\rho_{1}+\bm{v}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\rho_{0},

(22b)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\omega\rho_{0}\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{1f}}.

(22c)

The fluid inhomogeneity is caused by a solute concentration field
s=s(\bm{r},\tau)
, which is being transported on the slow timescale
\tau
. The density
\rho_{0}\big(s(\bm{r},\tau)\big)
, compressibility
\kappa_{0}\big(s(\bm{r},\tau)\big)
, and viscosity
\eta_{0}\big(s(\bm{r},\tau)\big)
are all functions of the solute concentration, and consequently the hydrodynamics is governed by the Stokes equation with body forces (acoustics and gravity) for the velocity field
\bm{v}
, the continuity equation for
\rho_{0}
, and the advection-diffusion equation of the solute
s
,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}(\rho_{0}\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}+\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}+\rho_{0}\bm{g},

(23a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho_{0}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big(\rho_{0}\bm{v}\big),

(23b)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}s

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big[-D\bm{\nabla}s+\bm{v}s\big].

(23c)

Usual streaming in the homogeneous viscous case
Muller et al. 2012
;
Muller et al. 2013
is obtained using
s=0
and
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
of the form

\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{hom}}=-\rho_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle.

(24)

The recently discovered slow-time-scale relocation of solute inhomogeneities into stable field-dependent configurations is obtained in the inhomogeneous inviscid case, for which
s\neq 0
and and
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
reduces to
Karlsen et al. 2016
;
Karlsen and Bruus 2017

\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{invisc}}=-\frac{1}{4}|p_{1}|^{2}\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{0}(s)-\frac{1}{4}|\bm{v}_{1}|^{2}\bm{\nabla}\rho_{0}(s).

(25)

In the general inhomogeneous viscous case, which we are currently studying, the streaming flow co-develop with the solute relocation using
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}
from Eq. (
19
),

\bm{f}_{\mathrm{ac}}=-\bm{\nabla}\big\langle p_{11}(s)\big\rangle-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\rho_{0}(s)\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle.

(26)
