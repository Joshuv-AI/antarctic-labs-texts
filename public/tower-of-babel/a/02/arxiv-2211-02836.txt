The generalized inverses of the quaternion tensor via the T-product
Hongwei Jin, Peifeng Zhou, Hongjie Jiang, Xiaoji Liu

Abstract
In this article, specific definitions of the Moore-Penrose inverse, Drazin inverse of the quaternion tensor and the inverse along two quaternion tensors are introduced under the T-product. Some characterizations, representations and properties of the defined inverses are investigated. Moreover, algorithms are established for computing the Moore-Penrose inverse, Drazin inverse of the quaternion tensor and the inverse along two quaternion tensors, respectively. Keywords : Quaternion tensors; Moore-Penrose inverse; Drazin inverse; Inverse along two quaternion tensors AMS classification: 15A18, 15A69.

H^{n},H^{m\times n},H_{r}^{m\times n}

.
\textbf{a}^{H}
,

and

are the conjugate transpose of

,

, respectively.

Now, we present the T-product defined in the work
[

,

]
.

Let
\textbf{a}\in{{H}}^{n}
. Recall that if
\textbf{a}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&...&a_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{H}
, then

\ccc(\textbf{a})=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}&a_{n}&\cdots&a_{2}\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
a_{n}&a_{n-1}&\cdots&a_{1}\\
\end{bmatrix}

is a circulant quaternion matrix. Similarly, if
A_{1},...,A_{n}
are
n_{1}\times n_{2}
quaternion matrices, then

\ccc(A_{1},...,A_{n})=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&A_{n}&\cdots&A_{2}\\
A_{2}&A_{1}&\cdots&A_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
A_{n}&A_{n-1}&\cdots&A_{1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Define
\unfold(\cdot)
to take an
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}
quaternion tensor and return an
n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}
block quaternion matrix in the following way:

\unfold(\mathcal{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{{A}}_{1}\\
\overline{{A}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where
\overline{{A}}_{1}=\mathcal{A}(:,:,1)
,…,
\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}}=\mathcal{A}(:,:,n_{3})
,
\mathcal{A}(:,:,i)
,
i=1,2,...,n_{3}
are the frontal slices of
\mathcal{A}
.
\fold(\cdot)
is the inverse operation, which takes
an
n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}
block quaternion matrix and returns an
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}
quaternion tensor. In additon, we have

\fold(\unfold(\mathcal{A}))=\mathcal{A}.

Now, one can create a quaternion tensor in a block circulant pattern, where each block is a quaternion matrix:

\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A}))=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{{A}}_{1}&\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}}&\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}-1}&\cdots&\overline{{A}}_{2}\\
\overline{{A}}_{2}&\overline{{A}}_{1}&\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}}&\cdots&\overline{{A}}_{3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}}&\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}-1}&\overline{{A}}_{n_{3}-2}&\cdots&\overline{{A}}_{1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(2.1)

Next, we will use the Kronecker product, symbolized as
\otimes
. Let
F_{n_{i}}
be the
n_{i}\times n_{i}

discrete Fourier transform (DFT) matrix, i.e.,

F_{n_{i}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{i}}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&1&1&1&\cdots&1\\
1&\omega&\omega^{2}&\omega^{3}&\cdots&\omega^{(n_{i}-1)}\\
1&\omega^{2}&\omega^{4}&\omega^{6}&\cdots&\omega^{2(n_{i}-1)}\\
1&\omega^{3}&\omega^{6}&\omega^{9}&\cdots&\omega^{3(n_{i}-1)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&\omega^{(n_{i}-1)}&\omega^{2(n_{i}-1)}&\omega^{3(n_{i}-1)}&\cdots&\omega^{(n_{i}-1)(n_{i}-1)}\\
\end{array}\right],

where
\omega=e^{2\pi i/n_{i}}
. Define
F=F_{n_{3}}
. Then there exist quaternion matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n_{3}}\in{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
such that

(F\otimes I_{n_{1}})\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A}))(F^{H}\otimes I_{n_{2}})=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&A_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}.

(2.2)

Let us define the function
\DFT(\cdot)
that implements the above procedure, i.e,

\textbf{DFT}(\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A})))=(F\otimes I_{n_{1}})\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A}))(F^{H}\otimes I_{n_{2}}).

Also, let
\text{uncirc}(\cdot)
and
\textbf{IDFT}(\cdot)
be the inverse operations of
\text{circ}(\cdot)
and
\textbf{DFT}(\cdot)
, respectively.

The formula (

) allows us to define the T-product of two quaternion tensors.

Definition 2.1

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

Then the

is the
n_{1}\times l\times n_{3}
quaternion tensor defined as

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}=\fold(\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A}))\unfold(\mathcal{B})).

(2.3)

in MATLAB pseudocode is provided which is inspired by

\mathcal{A}
and

quaternion tensor
\mathcal{B}

Output:

quaternion tensor
\mathcal{C}

1.

i=3
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}=\fft(\mathcal{A},[~],i)
,

2.

for

,

Algorithm 2.1

Compute the T-product of two quaternion tensors

Denote

\textbf{DFT}(\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A})))=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&A_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{A}.

By Algorithm
2.1
, we have the following fact:

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}\Longleftrightarrow\widehat{A}\cdot\widehat{B}=\widehat{C},

(2.4)

where
\widehat{B}
and

can be obtained by using the same manner of

.

Now, it is easy to check the following basic properties of the T-product.

Lemma 2.1

If
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
are quaternion tensors of adequate size, then the following statements are true:

(i)

\mathcal{A}*(\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{C}
;

(ii)

(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})*\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{C}
;

(iii)

(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})*\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}*(\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{C})
.

Definition 2.2

[
36
]

The
n\times n\times n_{3}

identity tensor

\mathcal{I}
is
the tensor such that the first frontal
slice
\mathcal{I}(:,:,1)
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and all other frontal slices
\mathcal{I}(:,:,k)
,
k=2,...,n_{3}
are zero matrices.

The following result can be checked by using (
2.4
).

Lemma 2.2

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
and
\mathcal{I}
be an
n\times n\times n_{3}
order-
3
identity tensor. Then,

\mathcal{I}*\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{A}.

Proof:

Let

\textbf{DFT}(\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{I})))=\begin{bmatrix}I_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&I_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&I_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{I}\ \text{and}\ \textbf{DFT}(\ccc(\unfold(\mathcal{A})))=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&A_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&A_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}=\widehat{A}.

Since
\widehat{I}\cdot\widehat{{A}}=\widehat{A}
, using (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\widehat{I}\cdot\widehat{{A}}=\widehat{A}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}*\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}.

Similarly,
\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{A}
.
\Box

Definition 2.3

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
. If there exists an tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
such that

\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{I}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathcal{B}*\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{I},

then
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
invertible
. Moreover,
\mathcal{B}
is the
inverse
of
\mathcal{A}
, which is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
.

In fact, the inverse of an invertible quaternion tensor is unique.

Lemma 2.3

If
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is invertible, then its inverse tensor is unique.

Proof:

Suppose
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
are both the inverse of
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is invertible, one has

\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{B}_{1}*(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}_{2})=(\mathcal{B}_{1}*\mathcal{A})*\mathcal{B}_{2}=\mathcal{I}*\mathcal{B}_{2}=\mathcal{B}_{2},

where we used Lemma
2.1
(c), Lemma
2.2
and Definition
2.3
.
Hence, the inverse tensor of
\mathcal{A}
is unique.

\Box

The conjugate transpose of quaternion tensors can be defined as follows.

Definition 2.4

If
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, then the
conjugate transpose
of
\mathcal{A}
, which is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{H}
, is the
n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}
quaternion tensor obtained by conjugate transposing each
\mathcal{A}(:,:,i)
, for
i=1,2,\ldots,n_{3}
and then reversing the order of the
\mathcal{A}(:,:,i)
from

, i.e.,

\mathcal{A}^{H}(:,:,1)=(\mathcal{A}(:,:,1))^{H},

\mathcal{A}^{H}(:,:,i)=(\mathcal{A}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i))^{H},\ i=2,\ldots,n_{3}.

(2.5)

Lemma 2.4

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
are two quaternion tensors such that
\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{B}^{H}*\mathcal{A}^{H}
are
defined. Then,

(i)

(\mathcal{A}^{H})^{H}=\mathcal{A}
.

(ii)

(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{A}^{H}+\mathcal{B}^{H}
.

(iii)

(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{B}^{H}*\mathcal{A}^{H}
.

Proof:

(i) By (
2.5
), we have

(\mathcal{A}^{H})^{H}(:,:,1)=((\mathcal{A}(:,:,1))^{H})^{H}=\mathcal{A}(:,:,1).

(\mathcal{A}^{H})^{H}(:,:,i)=((\mathcal{A}(:,:,i))^{H})^{H}=\mathcal{A}(:,:,i),\ i=2,\ldots,n_{3}.

Hence,
(\mathcal{A}^{H})^{H}=\mathcal{A}
.

(ii) Again using (
2.5
), one has

(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{H}(:,:,1)=(\mathcal{A}(:,:,1)+\mathcal{B}(:,:,1))^{H}=(\mathcal{A}(:,:,1))^{H}+(\mathcal{B}(:,:,1))^{H}=\mathcal{A}^{H}(:,:,1)+\mathcal{B}^{H}(:,:,1).

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{H}(:,:,i)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i)+\mathcal{B}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i))^{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i))^{H}+(\mathcal{B}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i))^{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{H}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i)+\mathcal{B}^{H}(:,:,n_{3}+2-i),

where
i=2,\ldots,n_{3}.
Hence,
(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{A}^{H}+\mathcal{B}^{H}
.

(iii) Suppose
(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{C}^{H}
, by (

), we have

(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{C}^{H}\Rightarrow(\widehat{A}\cdot\widehat{B})^{H}=\widehat{C}^{H}\Rightarrow\widehat{C}^{H}=\widehat{B}^{H}\cdot\widehat{A}^{H}\Rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{H}=\mathcal{B}^{H}*\mathcal{A}^{H}.

Hence,
(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B})^{H}=\mathcal{B}^{H}*\mathcal{A}^{H}
.
\Box

The Moore-Penrose Inverse of Quaternion Tensors

The T-product of two tensors presented in Definition
2.1
allows us to obtain the Moore-Penrose inverse of an arbitrary quaternion tensor
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 3.1

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}

(1)\,\,\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}*\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}\qquad(2)\,\,\mathcal{X}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}\qquad(3)\,\,(\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X})^{H}=\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{X}\qquad(4)\,\,(\mathcal{X}*\mathcal{A})^{H}=\mathcal{X}*\mathcal{A},

(3.1)

then
\mathcal{X}
is called the
Moore-Penrose inverse
of the quaternion tensor
\mathcal{A}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}
.

For any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, denote
\mathcal{A}{\{i,j,\ldots,k\}}

the set of all
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
which satisfy equations (
i
), (
j
),
\ldots
, (
k
) of
(\ref{mp})
. In this case,
\mathcal{X}
is a
\{i,j,\ldots,k\}
-inverse.

If
\mathcal{A}
is invertible, it is clear that
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{A}^{-1}
trivially satisfies the four equations.

Theorem 3.1

[
1
]
(Quaternion matrix SVD) Let
A\in{H^{m\times n}}

be of rank
r
. Then there exist
unitary quaternion matrices
U\in{H^{m\times m}}
and
V\in{H^{n\times n}}

such that

A=U\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{r}&0\\
0&0\\
\end{bmatrix}V^{H},

where
\Sigma_{r}=diag(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{r})
and the
\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
are real positive singular values of

.

Theorem 3.2

(Quaternion tensor SVD) Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
. Then there exist

\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
and
\mathcal{V}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

such that

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{S}*\mathcal{V}^{H},

where
\mathcal{S}
is an

F
-diagonal tensor.

Proof:
Let

\displaystyle\textbf{DFT}(\text{circ}(\textbf{Unfold}(\mathcal{A})))=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&A_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}.

Next compute the SVD of each
A_{i}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,n_{3}
as
A_{i}=U_{i}\Sigma_{i}V^{H}_{i}
. Thus, we have

\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&A_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&U_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\Sigma_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V^{H}_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&V^{H}_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(3.2)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{Fold}(\text{uncirc}(\textbf{IDFT}(\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&A_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix})))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{Fold}(\text{uncirc}(\textbf{IDFT}(\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&U_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix})))*\textbf{Fold}(\text{uncirc}(\textbf{IDFT}(\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&\Sigma_{n_{3}}\\

\displaystyle\textbf{Fold}(\text{uncirc}(\textbf{IDFT}(\begin{bmatrix}V^{H}_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&V^{H}_{n_{3}}\\
\end{bmatrix})))

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}*\mathcal{S}*\mathcal{V}^{H}.

This completes the proof.

Theorem 3.3

The Moore-Penrose inverse of an arbitrary quaternion tensor
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
exists and is unique.

Proof:
Let

\displaystyle\textbf{DFT}(\text{circ}(\textbf{Unfold}(\mathcal{A})))=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&A_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\textbf{DFT}(\text{circ}(\textbf{Unfold}(\mathcal{X})))=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&X_{n_{3}}\end{bmatrix}.

The Inverse Along Two Quaternion Tensors

In this section, we will do some researches on the right/left inverse along two quaternion tensors.

Definition 6.1

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
and

\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times l\times n_{3}}
,

\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{H}^{k\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
. If there exist tensors

\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
,

\mathcal{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{l\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
and

\mathcal{Y}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times k\times n_{3}}
such that

\mathcal{Z}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B},\qquad\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{C},\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}*\mathcal{C},

(6.1)

then
\mathcal{Z}
is called the
right inverse along
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}

and is denoted by
{\mathcal{A}_{r}}^{\parallel\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}}
. In particular,
when
\mathcal{A}\in{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
,
\mathcal{Z}\in{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
is called the right inverse along the quaternion matrix
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 6.2

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
and

\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{H}^{l\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
,

\mathcal{E}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times k\times n_{3}}
. If there exist tensors

\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
,

\mathcal{X}_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times l\times n_{3}}
and

\mathcal{Y}_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{k\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
such that

\mathcal{D}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{D},\qquad\mathcal{Z}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E},\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}_{2}*\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{E}*\mathcal{Y}_{2},

(6.2)

then
\mathcal{Z}
is called the
left inverse along
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{E}

and is denoted by
{\mathcal{A}_{l}}^{\parallel\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}}
. In particular,
when
\mathcal{A}\in{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
,
\mathcal{Z}\in{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}}
is called the left inverse along the quaternion matrix
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}
.

In fact, the right/left inverse along two quaternion tensors if exists, is unique.

Theorem 6.1

Let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
. Then the following statements are true.

(i)
If
\mathcal{A}
is right invertible along
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
, then it is unique.

(ii)
If
\mathcal{A}
is left invertible along
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}
, then it is unique.

Proof:
(i) Let
\mathcal{Z}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{2}\in\mathbb{H}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
be two right inverses of
\mathcal{A}
along

\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
. Exist tensors
\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2},\mathcal{Y}_{1},\mathcal{Y}_{2}
of adequate size such that

\mathcal{Z}_{i}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B},\qquad\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\mathcal{C},\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{i}=\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{X}_{i}=\mathcal{Y}_{i}*\mathcal{C},

for
i=1,2
. Now we have

\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{X}_{1}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\mathcal{Y}_{2}*\mathcal{C}*\mathcal{A}*\mathcal{Z}_{1}=\mathcal{Y}_{2}*\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{Z}_{2}.

The proof is finished.

(ii) Similar to (i).
\Box

The outer inverse

Suppose that statement (i) holds and let

,

is equivalent to
