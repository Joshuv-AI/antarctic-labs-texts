A Measure-Theoretic Characterization of Tight Language Models
Clara Meister Jason Eisner Ryan Cotterell

Abstract
Language modeling, a central task in natural language processing, involves estimating a probability distribution over strings. In most cases, the estimated distribution sums to 1 over all finite strings. However, in some pathological cases, probability mass can “leak” onto the set of infinite sequences. In order to characterize the notion of leakage more precisely, this paper offers a measure-theoretic treatment of language modeling. We prove that many popular language model families are in fact tight, meaning that they will not leak in this sense. We also generalize characterizations of tightness proposed in previous works.

Properly treating a distribution over this sample space requires a modicum of measure theory.

3

Indeed, our treatment resolves an imprecision present in the literature.
For instance,

To clarify the situation, we review the measure-theoretic treatment of distributions over

We then make use of a termination symbol

In a tight language model, this random variable has probability 1 of being a string and hence finite.

Beyond offering a measure-theoretic formalization, our paper also demonstrates how tightness relates to the Borel–Cantelli lemmata, simplifying a recent result by
Meister et al. 2022

To conclude our paper, we analyze several language modeling architectures and give conditions on their tightness.
We demonstrate that

-gram language models—and more generally, language models defined by stochastic finite-state automata—can be non-tight, and we give a simple necessary and sufficient condition for tightness in terms of the inverse of the automaton’s transition matrix.
This builds on a known result due to

We also discuss when neural language models become non-tight.
We prove that Transformer-based language models

However, we also exhibit a recurrent neural network (RNN) language model with a ReLU activation

As a byproduct, we also generalize and strengthen the results given by

3
The Language Model Measure

In this section, we rigorously characterize the kind of distribution induced by an ASM. As mentioned earlier, an ASM can lose probability mass to the set of infinite sequences,
\Sigma^{\infty}
. However,
\Sigma^{\infty}
, unlike
\Sigma^{*}
, is uncountable, and it is due to this fact that we need to work explicitly with the measure-theoretic formulation of probability.

3.1
Measure-Theoretic Background

The goal of measure-theoretic probability is to assign probability to subsets
of an
outcome space

\Omega
. For instance, in
2.6
,
\Omega=\{\texttt{H},\texttt{T}\}^{\infty}
.
However, in the course of the study of measure theory, it has become clear that for many common
\Omega
, it is impossible to assign probabilities in a way that satisfies a set of reasonable desiderata.
7
7

7

Measure theory texts commonly discuss such desiderata and the dilemmas that comes with them. See, e.g., Chapter 7 in
Tao 2016
, Chapter 3 in
Royden 1988
or Chapter 3 in
Billingsley 1995
. We also give an example in
Theorem

3.5
.

Consequently, the standard approach to probability theory resorts
to only assigning probability to certain “nice” subsets of
\Omega
, which are referred to as
events
or
measurable subsets
, as in the theory of integration or functional analysis.
The set of measurable subsets is commonly denoted as
\mathcal{F}
(
Definition

3.1
), and a probability measure
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
is the function that assigns a probability to each measurable subset.
As it turns out, the following simple and reasonable requirements imposed on
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathbb{P}
are enough to rigorously discuss probability
Kolmogorov 1933
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
be the powerset of
\Omega
.
Then
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
is called a
\bm{\sigma}
-algebra
(or
\sigma
-field)
over
\Omega
if the following conditions hold:

1.

\Omega\in\mathcal{F}
,

2.

if
E\in\mathcal{F}
, then its complement
E^{\mathsf{c}}\in\mathcal{F}
,

3.

if
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
is a finite or infinite sequence of sets in
\mathcal{F}
, then
\bigcup_{n}E_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra over
\Omega
, we call the tuple
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
a
measurable space
.

A measurable space guarantees that operations on countably many sets are always valid, and hence permits the following definition.

Definition 3.2
.

A
probability measure

\mathbb{P}
over a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
is a function
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
such that

1.

\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1
,

2.

if
E_{1},E_{2},\dots
is a finite or infinite sequence of disjoint sets in
\mathcal{F}
, then
\mathbb{P}(\bigcup_{n}E_{n})=\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(E_{n})
.

In this case we call
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})

. Note that it assigns measure only to the sets in
\mathcal{F}
; other sets are said to be
non-measurable
.

As we saw in

Hence, we hope to regard the ASM as defining a probability space over

As mentioned in

.

4
Characterizing Tightness

Beyond the measure-theoretic formalization, a goal of this paper is to provide an exact characterization of tightness in ASMs.
The results presented in this section generalize Lemma 3.2 in

First, we consider the event

Thus, using the random variable

\displaystyle P(X\in\Sigma^{\infty})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(\Sigma^{\infty}))

\displaystyle=\textstyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}A^{\mathsf{c}}_{k}\right).

A sequence model

Note that the definition of

4.1
A Lower Bound Result

We have characterized tightness in terms of the probability of a specific event

Lemma 4.2

If
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n}\mid\cap_{m=1}^{n-1}A_{m}^{\mathsf{c}}\right)=\infty

.

See

∎

If

D.2

4.2
The Borel–Cantelli Lemmata

It turns out that

admits a converse statement in which we can prove a similar property of

by assuming that the model is tight.
To prove this result, we will use a fundamental inequality from probability theory—the Borel–Cantelli lemmata.
The Borel–Cantelli lemmata are useful for our purposes because they relate the probability measure of sets of the form
\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}
or
\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}
to a series

We will only state the lemmata here without supplying their proofs;

14

14

See §2.3 in
Durrett 2019
or §4 in

however, we point out that

Concretely, given a sequence of events

We formally state the first and second Borel–Cantelli lemmata below.

Section

D.3
.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents a measure-theoretic treatment of language modeling and its tightness.
Practical implications of our results include determining when sampling from an autoregressive sequence model is guaranteed to terminate and whether MCMC algorithms over such models will mix to the correct distribution.

To this end, we first defined various components of language modeling in measure-theoretic terminology.
This in turn allows us to understand the portion of probability mass allocated to infinite-length strings.
Importantly, this presentation formalizes a definition of sequence modeling under which the probability of producing an infinite-length sequence is non-zero; while today’s models are often capable of producing such strings, previously there was no rigorous treatment of this case.

Indeed, such a definition is useful when considering a number of neural architectures (e.g., a simple RNN as in
Elman 1990
) and language generation systems (e.g., the distribution induced by nucleus sampling;
Holtzman et al. 2020
).
In particular, we showed that perhaps the most commonly-used NLP architecture, the Transformer language model, is indeed a language model—a tight distribution over finite strings—a property that had been called into question by previous work.

Limitations

Our discussion in this paper leaves out the consideration of computability of measures over languages.
Specifically, we note that there exist works on computable measure theory developed in the context of theoretical computer science
(
de Leeuw et al. 1956
)
and probabilistic programming languages
(
Roy 2011
)
.
Additional machinery needs to be developed in order for a proper treatment and we leave this for future work.

Another notable limitation is that we exclusively focused on the autoregressive production of language.
Importantly, our formalism might not be compatible with other models of language production such as those induced by a PCFG.

Finally, our proofs of
Theorems

5.9
and
5.10
exploit the strictly positive property of the softmax function. Importantly, they do not apply to models with sparse distributions
Martins and Astudillo 2016
;
Peters et al. 2019
;
Martins 2021
.

Ethics

There are no ethical implications of this paper to the best knowledge of the authors.

K_{1}=\overline{B_{M}(0)}
, we have shown that, for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, the attention block maps
K_{0}^{t}
into
K_{1}^{t}
.

Note that we
cannot
use

5.7
here because the attention block defines a different function on
\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
for each
t
, and
Theorem

5.7
only implies that there exists a separate
length-dependent
output compact set
K_{t}\subset\mathbb{R}^{t\times d}
for each
t
, which is different from this lemma’s statement.

The feedforward function is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and therefore, by
Theorem

5.7
, maps its input compact set
K_{1}
to an output compact set, which we call
K_{2}
.

Finally, residual connections and layer norms are also continuous functions acting on each of the input vectors, and hence by the same reasoning would also preserve compactness.

Now we can use induction and show that there exist compact sets
K_{3},K_{4},\dots,K_{2n-1},K_{2n}
where

contains the output set of the final layer. Set
K^{\prime}=K_{2n}
and we have proven the statement.
∎

Proof.

Let
X_{t}(\bm{\omega})
be the random variable that is equal to the
t
th
token in an outcome
\bm{\omega}\in\Omega
.
Also let
\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}
be the hidden representation of the RNN after processing some finite list of tokens
\bm{x}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
.
Further, let
\bm{u}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the output embedding of
x\in\overline{\Sigma}
,
Then for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\bm{x}\in\Sigma^{t}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=\textsc{eos}\mid X_{\leq t}=\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\bm{u}_{\textsc{eos}}^{\top}\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}}{\sum_{y\in\overline{\Sigma}}\exp\bm{u}_{y}^{\top}\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}}

(63a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{\sum_{y\in\overline{\Sigma}}\exp\bm{u}_{y}^{\top}\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}}\,/\,{\exp\bm{u}_{\textsc{eos}}^{\top}\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}}}

(63b)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{y\in\Sigma}\exp(\bm{u}_{y}-\bm{u}_{\textsc{eos}})^{\top}\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}}

(63c)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+\sum_{y\in\Sigma}\exp\left(\|\bm{u}_{y}-\bm{u}_{\textsc{eos}}\|_{2}\|\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}\|_{2}\right)}

(Cauchy–Schwarz)

(63d)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+\sum_{y\in\Sigma}\exp(k\|\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}\|_{2})}

(63e)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\,\|\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}\|_{2})}

(63f)

Now define
\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{def}}}{{=}}}\sup_{\bm{x}\in\Sigma^{t}}\|\bm{h}_{\bm{x}}\|_{2}
.
We then have that
\forall t\in\mathbb{N}
and
\forall\bm{x}\in\Sigma^{t}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{t+1}=\textsc{eos}\mid X_{\leq t}=\bm{x})\geq\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2})}

(64)

Now, by
Proposition

4.3
, we have that if
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\,\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2})}
diverges, then the language model is tight.
We will show that this condition holds if
\exists N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall t\geq N
,
k\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\log t
.

First, note that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\frac{1+|\Sigma|t}{1}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}+|\Sigma|=|\Sigma|\in(0,\infty)
.
Hence, by the limit comparison test, since
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t}
diverges, this means
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|t}
must also diverge.

Now, suppose there exists
N
such that that
k\,\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\log t
for all
t\geq N
.
This implies that for
t\geq N
we have
\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2})}\geq\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|t}
, which combined with the above and the comparison test, implies that

\sum_{t=N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2})}
diverges.
This in turn means that
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+|\Sigma|\exp(k\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2})}
diverges.

Hence, if
k\,\|\widehat{\bm{h}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\log t
for all sufficiently large
t
(that is, for all
t\geq N
), then the RNN ASM is tight and thus defines a language model.

∎
