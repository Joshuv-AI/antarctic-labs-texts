Shape sensitivity of a 2D Fluid-Structure Interaction problem between a viscous incompressible fluid and an incompressible elastic structure.
V. Calisti, I. Lucardesi, J-F. Scheid

Abstract
. We study the shape differentiability of a general functional depending on the solution of a bidimensional stationary Stokes-Elasticity system, with respect to the reference domain of the elastic structure immersed in a viscous fluid. The differentiability with respect to reference elastic domain variations are considered under shape perturbations with diffeomorphisms. The shape-derivative is then calculated with the use of the velocity method. This derivative involves the material derivatives of the solution of this Fluid-Structure Interaction problem. The adjoint method is used to obtain a simplified expression for the shape derivative.



Keywords.

Fluid-structure system, Stokes and elasticity equations, Shape optimisation, Shape sensitivity.

1.
Introduction

Fluid Structure Interaction (FSI) problems model physical systems in which a solid body (rigid or deformable) interacts with a fluid (internal or external to the body).
In this work, we consider an elastic body in plain strain, clamped to a rigid support in its interior and immersed in a viscous incompressible fluid. The system is infinite in the anti-plane dimension.
From the mathematical point of view, we consider a system of bi-dimensional stationary PDEs which involves on one hand the Stokes equations for the fluid flow and, on the other hand, an incompressible linearized elastic equations for the deformation of the structure. These two sub-systems are coupled through a boundary condition on the interface between the solid and the fluid, by imposing the forces continuity across the interface.

In this paper, we are interested in a shape optimization issue for this fluid-structure interaction problem. We aim to study the shape sensibility with respect to the reference domain
\Omega_{0}
of the elastic body, also called the reference configuration (i.e. the domain at rest, before deformation) of a given shape functional. This functional depends on the elastic reference domain
\Omega_{0}
as well as on the corresponding solution of the full PDEs system. We point out that in this context, we do not directly control the shape of the deformed elastic body which actually interacts with the fluid.

The goal of this paper is to show the differentiability of a broad family of shape functionals (e.g. energy functional, drag functional,…) in which the shape is the reference configuration
\Omega_{0}
of the elastic body and also to calculate the associated shape derivatives.
The differentiability is tackled with respect to the reference configuration
\Omega_{0}
by considering a class of perturbations of
\Omega_{0}
by diffeomorphisms.
We also provide formulas for the associated shape derivatives.
These derivatives would be useful in a numerical shape optimisation procedure (as e.g. steepest descent methods) to determine an optimal elastic reference domain that minimizes a given shape functional
(see e.g.

[
2
,
33
,
24
,
44
]
).

Early important contributions can be found in

In the early 2000, mathematicians

The second issue in FSI problems is to find

2.
A two-dimensional FSI model with a shape optimisation problem

In this section, we first present the FSI model under study and then the related shape optimisation problem that will be addressed in this paper.
The FSI model couples the Stokes equations with the elasticity equation and follows essentially
[
25
]
and
[
38
]
. The difference with respect to the literature is the assumption of linear incompressible elasticity for the structure, which results in a divergence free condition for the structure’s displacement.

2.2.
The Fluid-Structure Interaction model

We consider a two-dimensional elastic body (the structure) immersed in an incompressible viscous fluid and clamped from a part of its boundary, while applying volume forces to both fluid and elastic parts.
This results in the deformation of the free boundary of the elastic body, which is the interface where the interaction between the elastic body and the fluid takes place (see Figure

The fluid and the structure are subject to volume forces which result in a deformation of the elastic part. In our analysis, we assume that the system is at equilibrium, in particular, the time variable will not appear in the model.

The deformed elastic body, denoted by
\Omega_{S}
, is described in Lagrangian coordinates, that is, through a function defined in the reference configuration:

\Omega_{S}:=T(\mathrm{w})(\Omega_{0}),

with

(11)

T(\mathrm{w}):\Omega_{0}\to D\setminus\overline{\omega},\quad T(\mathrm{w})=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+\mathrm{w},

where
\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
is the identity in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\mathrm{w}
is the
elastic displacement field
in
\Omega_{0}
.
Accordingly, the deformed fluid-structure interface is

(12)

\Gamma_{FS}:=T(\mathrm{w})(\Gamma_{0})=(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+\mathrm{w})(\Gamma_{0}).

On the other hand, the fluid is described in Eulerian coordinates, namely through functions defined in the region surrounding the deformed elastic body

(13)

\Omega_{F}:=D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{S}\cup\omega}.

The functions describing the fluid are the
velocity field

\mathrm{u}:\Omega_{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the
pressure field

\mathrm{p}:\Omega_{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

In the following paragraphs, we will specify the PDEs governing the two phases of the system, and their interaction.

2.2.1.
Fluid equations

In the framework of incompressibility, the velocity field
u
and the pressure field
p
are governed by Stokes equations:

(14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})&=f&&\text{ in }\Omega_{F},\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\ \mathrm{u}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{F},\\
\mathrm{u}&=0&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{F}.\end{aligned}\right.

In the system,
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the applied force, defined in the whole space, whereas
\varsigma
is the
Cauchy stress tensor
, defined by

(15)

\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p}):=2\nu\nabla^{s}\mathrm{u}-\mathrm{p}\mathrm{I},

with
\nu>0
the viscosity of the fluid. We recall that the superscript
s
stands for the symmetrization operator (see (
6
)).

2.2.2.
Structure equations

We suppose that the elastic body is attached to the rigid support
\omega
via its boundary
\Gamma_{\omega}
. This assumption results in a Dirichlet boundary condition for the elastic displacement
\mathrm{w}
:

(16)

\mathrm{w}=0\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{\omega}.

A given volume force
g
is applied to the structure in
\Omega_{0}
and the elastic displacement
\mathrm{w}
satisfies the elasticity equation

(17)

-\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\sigma(\mathrm{w})=g\ \text{ in }\Omega_{0},

where
\sigma
is the
linearized stress tensor
(also called the second Piola-Kirchoff stress tensor) or simply
stress tensor

(18)

\sigma(\mathrm{w}):=2\mu\nabla^{s}\mathrm{w}+\lambda(\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\mathrm{w})\mathrm{I}.

Here
\lambda
and
\mu
are the so-called Lamé coefficients (see e.g.
[
16
]
).
Furthermore, we impose the equilibrium of the surface forces on the free boundary
\Gamma_{0}
which reads as

(19)

\int_{\Gamma_{0}}\sigma(\mathrm{w})n_{0}\cdot(v\circ(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+\mathrm{w}))\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{0}=\int_{\Gamma_{FS}}\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})n_{FS}\cdot v\ \mathrm{d}\Gamma_{FS},

for all function
v
defined on
\Omega_{F}
. In the above relation,
\Gamma_{FS}
is defined in (
12
) and denotes the boundary between the fluid domain
\Omega_{F}
and the deformed elastic body
\Omega_{S}
, whereas
\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{0}
and
\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{FS}
are the length elements of the boundaries
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{FS}
respectively,
and finally
n_{0}
and
n_{FS}
are the outer unit normal vectors to
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{FS}
respectively.
Recalling that
\Gamma_{FS}
is the image of
\Gamma_{0}
via
T(\mathrm{w})=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+\mathrm{w}
, cf. (
11
)-(
12
), we infer (see e.g.
[
16
]
) that

(20)

n_{FS}\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{FS}=[\det(\nabla(T(\mathrm{w})))\nabla(T(\mathrm{w}))^{-\mathsf{T}}n_{0}]\mathrm{d}\Gamma_{0}.

Thus, using
T(\mathrm{w})
for a change of variables in (
19
) together with (
20
), we get
the following boundary condition

(21)

\sigma(\mathrm{w})n_{0}=(\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})\circ T)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)n_{0}\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},

where

(22)

\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)=\det(\nabla T)(\nabla T)^{-T}

is the cofactor matrix of the jacobian matrix of
T:=T(\mathrm{w})
.

In this paper, we consider the special case of linear incompressible elasticity for the structure, by imposing the following equation for the displacement:

(23)

\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\mathrm{w}=0.

We introduce a Lagrange multiplier function
\mathrm{s}
associated to the incompressibility constraint (
23
). Then, the structure equation (
17
) together with the continuity condition of forces (
21
) become for
(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{s})
:

(24)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\sigma(\mathrm{w})+\nabla\mathrm{s}&=g&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0},\\
\left(\sigma(\mathrm{w})-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{I}\right)n_{0}&=(\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})\circ T)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)n_{0}&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

2.2.3.
Full FSI coupled system

Using the fact that both the velocity
\mathrm{u}
and the displacement
\mathrm{w}
are divergence free,
the FSI system for
(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})
and
(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{s})
that we consider in this paper is the following:

(25)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\nabla\mathrm{u})+\nabla\mathrm{p}&=f&&\text{ in }\Omega_{F},\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\ \mathrm{u}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{F},\\
\mathrm{u}&=0&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{F},\end{aligned}\right.

and

(26)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\nabla\mathrm{w})+\nabla\mathrm{s}&=g&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0}&&\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\mathrm{w}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0}&&\\
\mathrm{w}&=0&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{\omega}&&\\
\left(\mu\nabla\mathrm{w}-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{I}\right)n_{0}&=((\nu\nabla\mathrm{u}-\mathrm{p}\mathrm{I})\circ T)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)n_{0}&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}.&&\end{aligned}\right.

Remark 1
.

We point out that if we consider the general linear elastic equation (
17
) in place of the special incompressible case given by (
23
) and (
24
), we have to add an area preserved constraint for the displacement
\mathrm{w}
, due to the incompressibility of the fluid. This extra constraint reads as:

(27)

\lvert\Omega_{S}\rvert=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\det(\mathrm{I}+\nabla\mathrm{w})\,dx=\lvert\Omega_{0}\rvert,

where
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the Lebesgue measure for domains.
We have that

(28)

\det\left(\mathrm{I}+\nabla\mathrm{w}\right)=1+\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\mathrm{w})+\det(\nabla\mathrm{w})=1+\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\mathrm{w})+O\left(\lVert\nabla\mathrm{w}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\right).

So, under the condition that
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\mathrm{w}=0
and neglecting the second order terms in (
28
), we obtain that the area constraint (
27
) is satisfied.

We can observe that the coupling of the FSI problem (
25
)-(
26
) is twofold:

•

the structure displacement
\mathrm{w}
affects and defines the domain
\Omega_{F}
on which the fluid equations are posed and where the velocity
\mathrm{u}
and the pressure
\mathrm{p}
are defined,

•

the velocity
\mathrm{u}
and the pressure
\mathrm{p}
of the fluid give rise to a surface force which influences the calculation of the displacement
\mathrm{w}
.

One of the main difficulties lies in the fact that there are two kinds of variables under consideration. On the one hand, the FSI problem involves Eulerian variables with the fluid velocity

\mathrm{u}
and pressure
\mathrm{p}
, and on the other hand, the elastic displacement
\mathrm{w}
and the multiplier
\mathrm{s}
are Lagrangian variables.

Moreover, the domain
\Omega_{F}
on
which the fluid equations are written is unknown.
To overcome these difficulties, we need to transport the fluid equations into a reference domain matching with the elastic reference domain
\Omega_{0}
.

2.3.
Fixed domain formulation of the FSI problem

In order to tackle the FSI problem (
25
)-(
26
), we
transpose the fluid equations (
25
) posed on the fluid domain
\Omega_{F}
onto the fixed domain
\Omega_{0}^{c}
defined by

(29)

\Omega_{0}^{c}:=D\setminus\overline{\Omega_{0}\cup\omega}.

Thus, we need a
C^{1}
-diffeomorphism which maps
\Omega_{0}^{c}
to
\Omega_{F}
.
To this aim, we consider an extension of the map
T
, initially defined on
\Omega_{0}
in (
11
), to the whole box
D
. With a slight abuse of notation, we use the same letter
T
and we set

(30)

T(\mathrm{w})=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+\mathop{P}\nolimits(\mathrm{w}),

where
\mathrm{w}
is a displacement field defined in the initial elastic body domain
\Omega_{0}
, and
\mathop{P}\nolimits
is an extension operator from
\Omega_{0}
to
D
, such that
\mathop{P}\nolimits(\mathrm{w})
is defined in
D
and
T(\mathrm{w})
is one to one in
D
.
This allows us to consider the fluid domain
\Omega_{F}
defined as:

(31)

\Omega_{F}=T(\mathrm{w})(\Omega_{0}^{c}),

where
\Omega_{0}^{c}
is defined in (
29
) (see also Figure
1
). We will go through this extension procedure in details later on, to give a rigorous definition of
T
.

In the same way as in
[
25
]
,
we can define the transported velocity and pressure fields

(32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{v}:=\mathrm{u}\circ T(\mathrm{w}),

(33)

\displaystyle\mathrm{q}:=\mathrm{p}\circ T(\mathrm{w}).

With these new variables, we can write
the fluid equations transported onto the reference domain
\Omega^{c}_{0}
, (e.g., by using the variational formulation as in
[
12
]
, Section 3.2.2),
and the complete FSI problem reads as:

(34)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits((\nabla\mathrm{v})F(\mathrm{w}))+G(\mathrm{w})\nabla\mathrm{q}&=(f\circ T(\mathrm{w}))J(\mathrm{w})&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0}^{c},\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(G(\mathrm{w})^{\top}\mathrm{v})&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0}^{c},\\
\mathrm{v}&=0&&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{0}^{c},\\
-\mu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\nabla\mathrm{w})+\nabla\mathrm{s}&=g&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0},\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits\mathrm{w}&=0&&\text{ in }\Omega_{0},\\
\mathrm{w}&=0&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{\omega},\\
(\mu\nabla\mathrm{w}-\mathrm{s}\mathrm{I})n_{0}&=\nu(\nabla\mathrm{v})F(\mathrm{w})n_{0}&&\\
&\hskip 30.1388pt-\mathrm{q}G(\mathrm{w})n_{0}&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where we have set

(35)

\displaystyle F(\mathrm{w})

\displaystyle:=(\nabla T(\mathrm{w}))^{-1}\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T(\mathrm{w})),

(36)

\displaystyle G(\mathrm{w})

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T(\mathrm{w})),

(37)

\displaystyle J(\mathrm{w})

\displaystyle:=\det(\nabla T(\mathrm{w})).

The boundary condition on
\Gamma_{0}
appearing in (
34
) comes from the computation of the surface force applied on the structure, given in (
21
) by
(\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})\circ T)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)n_{0}
, in terms of the new variables
\mathrm{v}
and
\mathrm{q}
:

(38)

(\varsigma(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{p})\circ T)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)n_{0}=(\nu(\nabla\mathrm{v})F(\mathrm{w})-\mathrm{q}G(\mathrm{w}))n_{0}.

We point out that the FSI problem (
34
) is a sort of hybrid model compared to
[
25
]
, coming from the linearization of the equilibrium equation of the structure (that is to say the Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor) and the area constraint (

). This has been done in order to simplify the shape optimisation analysis performed in this paper.
Moreover, we do not have linearized the terms arising from the fluid equations change of variables, i.e.,
J(\mathrm{w})
,
G(\mathrm{w})
, and
F(\mathrm{w})
, because we want to compute shape derivatives by keeping as much information as possible, for possible further applications and calculation purposes for a general system.

2.4.
Optimisation of the FSI system

The shape sensitivity analysis of the FSI model (
34
) carried out in this article, is motivated by a shape optimisation problem. This problem consists in
seeking for an optimal shape of the elastic reference domain
\Omega_{0}
that minimizes a functional depending on the solution of the FSI system associated to
\Omega_{0}
. The shape optimisation problem we consider is of the following form:

(39)

\min_{\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0}),

where
\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})
is a quite general shape functional depending on the initial elastic domain defined by

(40)

\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})=\int_{\Omega_{0}}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))\,\mathrm{d}Y+\int_{\Omega_{F}}j_{F}(x,\mathrm{u}(x),\nabla\mathrm{u}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
j_{F}
and
j_{S}
are smooth functions depending respectively on
\mathrm{u}=\mathrm{v}\circ T(\mathrm{w})^{-1}
and
\mathrm{w}
.
The fields
\mathrm{v}
and
\mathrm{w}
are the velocity and the displacement solutions of the FSI problem (
34
) posed on
\Omega_{0}\cup\Omega_{0}^{c}
.
The domain
\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
belongs to a class
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
of smooth domains admissible for the FSI problem.
For example, we can consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}:=\{A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},\

\displaystyle A=B\setminus\overline{\omega}\text{ with }B\text{ smooth,}

\displaystyle\text{ simply connected},\omega\subset B\subset D\text{ and }|A|=|\Omega_{0}|\}.

In this paper, we do not go as far as to solve the complete optimisation problem (
39
).
We will restrict our study to the shape sensitivity analysis of the FSI model (
34
).

3.
Existence and uniqueness result for the FSI problem

In this section, we establish an existence and uniqueness result written in Theorem
1
for the FSI problem (
34
).
In
[
25
]
, an existence result is obtained for the Navier-Stokes equations coupled with a St Venant-Kirchhoff material in
3
D.
For volume forces regular and small enough, the author finds a solution, not necessarily unique, to the FSI problem by applying a fixed point procedure.
For our purpose, the uniqueness of the solution is required to address the associated optimisation problem and its shape sensitivity analysis.
For the same reason,
we need higher regularity results for the data and the solutions of Problem (
61
).
The existence and uniqueness result for our semi-linearized model is obtained
by adapting what is done in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
D
,
\Omega_{0}
and
\omega
be domains of the form (
9
)-(
10
) with boundary components
\partial D
and
\Gamma_{\omega}
of class

Fixed point procedure.

3.3.
Resolution of the fluid problem

Problem (

Let us introduce the null mean-value pressure space

(63)

L_{0}^{2}(\Omega_{0}^{c})=\left\{q\in L^{2}(\Omega_{0}^{c})\ \Big|\ \int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}q\mathrm{d}x=0\right\}.

The following result extends the standard, and well-known, Stokes existence and uniqueness result for the fluid problem (

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, having a
C^{3}

\partial\Omega
.
Let
f_{F}\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{2}

be such that

(64)

\int_{\Omega}h_{F}\mathrm{d}x=0,

and
\bm{\mathrm{A}},\bm{\mathrm{B}}\in(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}
be two matrix fields.
We assume that there exists
\psi\in(H^{3}(\Omega))^{2}
such that

(65)

\bm{\mathrm{B}}=\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits\nabla\psi.

There exists a positive constant

such that, if

(66)

\lVert\mathrm{I}-\bm{\mathrm{A}}\rVert_{(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{pert}},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lVert\mathrm{I}-\bm{\mathrm{B}}\rVert_{(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{pert}},

(v,p)\in(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{3}(\Omega))^{2}\times(L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega))

(67)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits((\nabla v)\bm{\mathrm{A}})+\bm{\mathrm{B}}\nabla p&=f_{F}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\

and there exists a positive constant
C_{\mathrm{F}}
such that

(68)

\lVert v\rVert_{3,2,\Omega}+\lVert p\rVert_{2,2,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathrm{F}}(\lVert f_{F}\rVert_{1,2,\Omega}+\lVert h_{F}\rVert_{2,2,\Omega}).

We refer to
[
25
]
where the proof of this result is entirely given.
The demonstration relies on a fixed point argument – leading to conditions (
66

(see e.g.
[
11
]

For a complete proof of well-posedness and regularity for Stokes problem, we may refer to
[

3.4.
Resolution of the structure problem

Now we solve the structure problem (
61
).
Under the assumptions we have made, the elastic material is incompressible, and the state equation is linear, so that it can be described by Stokes-like equations.
The only difference with the behaviour of the fluid is that we do not impose a non-slip boundary condition on
\Gamma_{0}
for the structure displacement
\mathrm{w}
.
Instead of that, the equilibrium of the surface forces leads to a
stress boundary condition
on
\Gamma_{0}
.

Usually, the Dirichlet condition for the Stokes problem implies that we have a solution for which the pressure field is defined up to a constant (which is often chosen such that the pressure has a zero mean value), whereas pure Neumann or pure stress condition brings to a solution for which the velocity field is defined up to a constant.
In the case of a mixed boundary condition, i.e with Dirichlet condition on a part of the boundary and stress condition on the rest of the boundary, we will note that the velocity together with the pressure are completely determined, and no zero mean value has to be imposed for the pressure.

Given a bounded connected open set
\mathcal{O}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with boundary
\Gamma
and given a connected open subset
\omega\subset\subset\mathcal{O}
with boundary
\Gamma_{\omega}
, we define the annular domain

(69)

\Omega:=\mathcal{O}\setminus\overline{\omega},

with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma\cup\Gamma_{\omega}
.
For such a domain, we introduce the subspace of
H^{1}
functions, vanishing on the boundary component
\Gamma_{\omega}
: we set

(70)

H^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{\omega}}(\Omega):=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\mid u=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\omega}\}.

We give an existence, uniqueness and regularity result for the solution to the structure problem when the stress boundary condition on
\Gamma
is given.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain of the form (
69
) with boundary components
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{\omega}
of class
C^{3,1}
,
and let
(g,h_{S},f_{b})\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{2}\times H^{2}(\Omega)\times(H^{3/2}(\Gamma))^{2}
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{A}}
,
\bm{\mathrm{B}}\in(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}
such that there exists
\psi\in(H^{3}(\Omega))^{2}
satisfying

(71)

\bm{\mathrm{B}}=\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits\nabla\psi.

There exists a positive constant
C_{\mathrm{pert},2}
such that, if

(72)

\lVert\mathrm{I}-\bm{\mathrm{A}}\rVert_{(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{pert},2},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lVert\mathrm{I}-\bm{\mathrm{B}}\rVert_{(H^{2}(\Omega))^{2\times 2}}\leq C_{\mathrm{pert},2},

then there exists a unique pair
(w,s)
in
(H^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{\omega}}(\Omega)\cap H^{3}(\Omega))^{2}\times H^{2}(\Omega)
solution of the problem:

(73)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\mu\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits((\nabla w)\bm{\mathrm{A}})+\bm{\mathrm{B}}\nabla s&=g&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\top}w)&=h_{S}&&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
w&=0&&\text{ on }\Gamma_{\omega},\\
(\mu(\nabla w)\bm{\mathrm{A}}-s\bm{\mathrm{B}})n&=f_{b}&&\text{ on }\Gamma,\end{aligned}\right.

where

is the outward normal vector to
\Gamma

Furthermore, there exists a positive constant
C_{\mathrm{s}}
depending only on
\Omega
such that

(74)

\lVert w\rVert_{3,2,\Omega}+\lVert s\rVert_{2,2,\Omega}\leq C_{\mathrm{s}}(\lVert g\rVert_{1,2,\Omega}+\lVert h_{S}\rVert_{2,2,\Omega}+\lVert f_{b}\rVert_{H^{3/2}(\Gamma)}).

Problem (
73

In the case where

, a proof of the first part of Theorem
3

From there, Theorem

4.
Velocity method and shape differentiability of the FSI system

After having proved the existence of solutions of the FSI system for a prescribed reference configuration, we now address the so called
shape sensitivity analysis
: we analyse the behavior of the solutions with respect to infinitesimal perturbations of the reference configuration. The section is organized as follows: we start, in §
4.1
, by introducing the classical velocity method. Then, in §
4.2
and §
4.3
, we perform some preliminary computations, preparatory to the main result of this section: the shape differentiability of the solutions of the FSI problem, stated in §
4.5
, Theorem
4
.

4.1.
Presentation of the method

We are interested in the study of the behavior of a shape functional
\mathcal{J}(\Omega)
with respect to infinitesimal variations of its argument, the set
\Omega
.
This topic, referred to as
shape derivative
or
shape sensitivity analysis
, is now a standard tool in shape optimization. See, e.g.,
[
39
, Chapter 2]
,
[
29
, Section 5.1]
, or
[
2
, Chapter 6]
.

Let us present the classical approach: the
velocity method
.
Given an admissible domain
\Omega_{0}
for
\mathcal{J}
, we consider a 1-parameter family of shapes
(\Omega_{0,t})_{t}
of the form

(109)

\Omega_{0,t}:=\Phi_{t}(\Omega_{0}),

where
(\Phi_{t})_{t}
is family of diffeomorphisms, chosen with the following properties:

-

at
t=0
there holds
\Phi_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
;

-

the map
t\mapsto\Phi_{t}
is of class
C^{1}
;

-

each diffeomorphism
\Phi_{t}
preserves the imposed geometrical constraints on
\Omega_{0}
, so that every
\Omega_{0,t}
is admissible for
\mathcal{J}
.

If the function
t\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0,t})
is differentiable at
t=0
, then it admits the following development in
t
:

\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0,t})=\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})+t\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})+o(t).

The coefficient
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})
of
t
is the so-called shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}
at
\Omega_{0}
with respect to the deformations
(\Phi_{t})_{t}
.

In the literature, it is classical to take diffeomorphisms of the form

\Phi_{t}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+tV,

for a suitable vector field
V
, representing the velocity (when
t
is seen as the time) of
\Phi_{t}
at
t=0
.

In order to write the expression of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})
, it is useful to introduce the notion of
material derivative
of a family of functions
(\varphi_{t})_{t}

defined on the family of transformed domains
(\Omega_{0,t})_{t\geq 0}
given by (
109
).
By definition,
\varphi_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}
are all defined in the fixed domain
\Omega_{0}
. If the map
t\mapsto\varphi_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}
is differentiable at
t=0
, we define the material derivative
\dot{\varphi}
of
\varphi_{t}
at
t=0
as the coefficient of
t
in the expansion

\varphi_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}=\varphi_{0}+t\dot{\varphi}+o(t).

Note that
\varphi_{0}
and
\dot{\varphi}
do not depend on
t
.

\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})\hskip-2.15277pt=\hskip-2.15277pt\mathcal{J}_{S}(\Omega_{0})+\mathcal{J}_{F}(\Omega_{0})\hskip-2.15277pt=\hskip-2.15277pt\int_{\Omega_{0}}\hskip-2.15277ptj_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))\,\mathrm{d}Y+\int_{\Omega_{F}}\hskip-2.15277ptj_{F}(x,\mathrm{u}(x),\nabla\mathrm{u}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
j_{S}
and
j_{F}
are differentiable functions.
As we have done in the previous Section, we consider a 1-parameter family of shapes
\Omega_{0,t}
defined in (
112
).

Performing the shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}
with respect to the deformation chosen amounts to compute the derivative of
t\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0,t})
at
t=0
.

The shape functional evaluated on the domain
\Omega_{0,t}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0,t})=\mathcal{J}_{S}(\Omega_{0,t})+\mathcal{J}_{F}(\Omega_{0,t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0,t}}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}_{t}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}_{t}(Y))\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{F,t}}j_{F}(x,\mathrm{u}_{t}(x),\nabla\mathrm{u}_{t}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.

where
(\mathrm{w}_{t},\mathrm{u}_{t})
are the solution fields of the FSI problem (
120
),(
121
).

Let us first compute the derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{S}(\Omega_{0,t})
.
After transporting the integral from
\Omega_{0,t}
to
\Omega_{0}
, we obtain

(190)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{S}(\Omega_{0,t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}j_{S}\left(\Phi_{t}(Y),\mathrm{w}_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(Y),(\nabla\mathrm{w}_{t})\circ\Phi_{t}(Y)\right)\det(\nabla\Phi_{t})\,\mathrm{d}Y.

Thus the shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{S}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{S}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits V\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{1}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))V\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{2}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))\dot{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}Y

(191)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{3}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w}(Y),\nabla\mathrm{w}(Y))(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{w}}-\nabla\mathrm{w}\nabla V)\,\mathrm{d}Y,

where
\dot{\mathrm{w}}
is the material derivative of
\mathrm{w}_{t}
at
t=0
, defined by

(192)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{w}}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{w}^{t})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{w}_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}),

and
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
,
D_{3}
stand for the differential on each argument of
j_{S}
.
In (
5.1
), we have used the relation

(193)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}\det(\nabla\Phi_{t})=\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits V

with the definition (
110
) of
\Phi_{t}
(see (
245
) in Appendix
6
).

Secondly we consider the shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{F}
with respect to
t
.
We perform a change of variable
x=T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)
, in order to rewrite the integrals from
\Omega_{F,t}
to
\Omega_{0}^{c}
.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{F}(\Omega_{0,t})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}\Big(j_{F}(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X),\mathrm{u}_{t}\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X),(\nabla\mathrm{u}_{t})\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X))

(194)

\displaystyle\hskip 184.9429pt\det(\nabla(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}X.

We calculate the shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}_{F}
, setting

(195)

\mathrm{v}=\mathrm{u}\circ T

where
T=T_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+R\gamma(\mathrm{w})
.
This gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{F}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\nabla\dot{T})\,\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{1}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\dot{T}\det(\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{2}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\dot{\mathrm{v}}\det(\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}X

(196)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{3}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\left(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{v}}-\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\nabla\dot{T}\right)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}X,

where we denote by

\dot{\mathrm{v}}
the material derivative of
\mathrm{v}
defined by

(197)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{v}^{t})=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{v}_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}),

and by

\dot{T}
the material derivative of
T_{t}
defined by

(198)

\displaystyle\dot{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}\left(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}\right).

From the definitions of
T_{t}
in (
117
) and of
\dot{T}
, we have

(199)

\dot{T}=V+\mathcal{R}\gamma(\dot{\mathrm{w}}).

The term
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\nabla\dot{T})
in (
196
) comes from the differentiation of
\det(\nabla(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)))
in (
5.1
).
The terms
\dot{T}
and
\dot{\mathrm{v}}
in (
196
) are respectively the results of the differentiation through the chain rule of the terms
T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)
and
\mathrm{u}_{t}\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)
in (
5.1
).
For the last term
(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{v}}-\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\nabla\dot{T})\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}
in (
196
) deriving from
(\nabla\mathrm{u}_{t})\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)
in (
5.1
), we can write

\displaystyle(\nabla\mathrm{u}_{t})\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}(X)

\displaystyle=(\nabla(\mathrm{u}_{t}\circ T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}))(X)(\nabla(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}))^{-1}(X),

(200)

\displaystyle=(\nabla(\mathrm{v}_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}))(X)(\nabla(T_{t}\circ\Phi_{t}))^{-1}(X),

with
\mathrm{v}_{t}=\mathrm{u}_{t}\circ T_{t}
(see (
118
)).
From there, we can write in the following proposition the formula of the shape derivative
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})
of the abstract shape functional
\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})

defined by (
189
).

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be the shape functional defined by (
189
), where
j_{S}
and
j_{F}
are differentiable functions.
Let
V
be a velocity field belonging to the space
\Theta
introduced in (
111
).
Then, the shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}
in the direction
V
computed at
\Omega_{0}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w},\nabla\mathrm{w})\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits V\,\mathrm{d}Y+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{1}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w},\nabla\mathrm{w})V\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{2}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w},\nabla\mathrm{w})\dot{\mathrm{w}}\,\mathrm{d}Y+\int_{\Omega_{0}}D_{3}j_{S}(Y,\mathrm{w},\nabla\mathrm{w})(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{w}}-\nabla\mathrm{w}\nabla V)\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\nabla\dot{T})\,\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{1}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\dot{T}\det(\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{2}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\dot{\mathrm{v}}\det(\nabla T)\,\mathrm{d}X

(201)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}D_{3}j_{F}(T,\mathrm{v},\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1})\left(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{v}}-\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\nabla\dot{T}\right)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}X.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathrm{u}
and
\mathrm{w}
be the velocity and displacement solutions of the FSI problem associated to
\Omega_{0}
.
Let us consider the following energy shape functional

(202)

\mathcal{J}_{E}(\Omega_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{F}}\lvert\nabla^{s}_{x}(\mathrm{u})\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\lvert\nabla^{s}_{Y}(\mathrm{w})\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}Y,

where
\nabla^{s}
is defined in (
6
), and the norm of a matrix is defined in (
7
).

The shape derivative of
\mathcal{J}
in direction
V
evaluated at
\Omega_{0}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{E}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\nabla^{s}\mathrm{w}:\left(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{w}}-\nabla\mathrm{w}\nabla V\right)\,\mathrm{d}Y+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\lvert\nabla^{s}\mathrm{w}\rvert^{2}\mathop{\rm div}\nolimits(V)\,\mathrm{d}Y

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}\left[\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\right]^{s}:\left(\nabla\dot{\mathrm{v}}-\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\nabla\dot{T}\right)\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\mathrm{d}X

(203)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{c}}\left\lvert\left[\nabla\mathrm{v}(\nabla T)^{-1}\right]^{s}\right\rvert^{2}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\mathop{\rm cof}\nolimits(\nabla T)^{\top}\nabla\dot{T})\,\mathrm{d}X.

Notice that the expression (
201
) of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
depends on the material derivatives
\dot{\mathrm{v}}
and
\dot{\mathrm{w}}
of the velocity and of the displacement.
These material derivatives can be computed as solutions of boundary value problems which depend on the direction
V
(see
[
12
, Section 3.4.4]
). For a practical use of the shape derivative – within a shape optimization algorithm for example – it is suitable to find an expression which does not depend on
\dot{\mathrm{v}}
and
\dot{\mathrm{w}}
.
For this, we apply in the next section the classical
adjoint method
allowing for a simplified expression of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
.

5.2.
Adjoint method, or Céa’s method

The
adjoint method
, or
Céa’s method
, was introduced in
[
14
]
. It allows to guess straightforwardly the
adjoint states
we need to introduce in order to simplify the expression of the shape derivative.
After the introduction to this method, we apply it to the FSI problem.
We refer to
[
2
, Section 6.4.3]
for a more detailed presentation of this method.

5.2.1.
Presentation of the method

Let
\Omega_{0}
be an admissible domain of the fluid-structure problem, standing for the elastic material (see Figure
1
). We denote by
\Omega
any smooth perturbation of
\Omega_{0}
.
For example we can consider
\Omega=(\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}+tV)(\Omega_{0})
, for
V\in\Theta

where
\Theta
is defined in (
111
) and for
t>0
.
First we define the following functional space:

(204)

\mathbf{Q}:=(H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{c}_{0}))^{2}\times L^{2}_{0}(\Omega^{c}_{0})\times(H^{1}_{0,\Gamma_{\omega}}(\Omega_{0}))^{2}\times L^{2}(\Omega_{0}).

We will denote by
\mathcal{X}_{0}=(\mathrm{v},\mathrm{q},\mathrm{w},\mathrm{s})
the solution of the fluid-structure problem with initial datum
\Omega_{0}
, and by
\mathcal{Y}=(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{s})
a test quadruplet.
Let us rewrite the FSI problem with these notations.
Let
A:\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable map, bilinear on
\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{Q}

and
L:\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}\times\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbf{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable map, linear on
\mathbf{Q}
, where
\mathcal{U}_{ad}
is a class of admissible domains for the FSI problem.
Finally, let
\mathfrak{F}:\mathbf{Q}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be a non linear differentiable map. We consider the solution
\mathcal{X}_{\Omega}
of the following problem:

(205)

\text{Find }\mathcal{X}_{\Omega}\in\mathbf{Q}\text{ such that: }\hskip 4.30554ptA\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{X}_{\Omega},\mathcal{Y}\big)=L\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{\Omega}),\mathcal{Y}\big),\hskip 4.30554pt\forall\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbf{Q}.

Now we define a shape functional of the form:

(206)

\mathcal{J}(\Omega)=j(\Omega,\mathcal{X}_{\Omega}).\quad

This suggests the definition of the following Lagrangian for all
\Omega
and
\forall\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbf{Q}
:

(207)

\mathcal{L}\big(\Omega,\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\big)=j(\Omega,\mathcal{X})+A\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\big)-L\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}),\mathcal{Y}\big).

By definition we have
\forall\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbf{Q}
:

(208)

\mathcal{L}\big(\Omega,\mathcal{X}_{\Omega},\mathcal{Y}\big)=j(\Omega,\mathcal{X}_{\Omega})

Thus the shape derivative of
j
is

(209)

\displaystyle j^{\prime}(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}\big)+\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathcal{X}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}\big),\dot{\mathcal{X}_{0}}\right\rangle,

where
\dot{\mathcal{X}_{0}}=\partial_{\Omega}\mathcal{X}_{0}
is the material derivative of
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.

If the following problem admits a solution

(210)

\text{Find }\mathcal{Y}_{0}\in\mathbf{Q}\text{ such that: }\quad\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathcal{X}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big),\mathcal{Z}\right\rangle=0\quad\forall\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbf{Q},

then
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
is called the
adjoint solution
, or
adjoint state
.

In general the existence of
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
has to be proved. However, in the following we do not care about this aspect and we write (formally) the expression of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}
, assuming the well-posedness of (
210
). We finally have

(211)

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})=j^{\prime}(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0})=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big).

We can see that the shape derivative
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})
in expression (
211
) does not depend on the material derivative
\dot{\mathcal{X}_{0}}
, unlike expression (
209
).
Let us give a slightly more detailed expression of
\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})
.
We develop problem (
210
):

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathcal{X}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}\big),\mathcal{Z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial j}{\partial\mathcal{X}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0}\big),\mathcal{Z}\right\rangle+A\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{Y}\big)

(212)

\displaystyle\hskip-73.97733pt+\left\langle\frac{\partial A}{\partial\mathfrak{F}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}\big)\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Z}\right\rangle-\left\langle\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathfrak{F}}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Y}\big)\mathfrak{F}^{\prime}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Z}\right\rangle,

where we have used the fact that
A
is linear with respect to
\mathcal{Z}
, and we develop the shape derivative

(213)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial j}{\partial\Omega}(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0})+\frac{\partial A}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big).

Finally we can write the shape derivative involving the adjoint state
\mathcal{Y}_{0}
as follows:

(214)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\prime}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial j}{\partial\Omega}(\Omega_{0},\mathcal{X}_{0})+\frac{\partial A}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\Omega}\big(\Omega_{0},\mathfrak{F}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),\mathcal{Y}_{0}\big).

Let us apply this method to the FSI problem.

5.2.2.
Shape functional and its related Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Omega_{0})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t,(v,q

\displaystyle,w,s),(\mathfrak{v},\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{s}))=

5.2.3.
Derivatives of the Lagrangian

In order to obtain the adjoint problems, we need to derive the Lagrangian

,
