Subsampling Suffices for Adaptive Data Analysis
Guy Blanc

Abstract
Ensuring that analyses performed on a dataset are representative of the entire population is one of the central problems in statistics. Most classical techniques assume that the dataset is independent of the analyst’s query and break down in the common setting where a dataset is reused for multiple, adaptively chosen, queries. This problem of adaptive data analysis was formalized in the seminal works of Dwork et al. (STOC, 2015) and Hardt and Ullman (FOCS, 2014). We identify a remarkably simple set of assumptions under which the queries will continue to be representative even when chosen adaptively: The only requirements are that each query takes as input a random subsample and outputs few bits. This result shows that the noise inherent in subsampling is sufficient to guarantee that query responses generalize. The simplicity of this subsampling-based framework allows it to model a variety of real-world scenarios not covered by prior work. In addition to its simplicity, we demonstrate the utility of this framework by designing mechanisms for two foundational tasks, statistical queries and median finding. In particular, our mechanism for answering the broadly applicable class of statistical queries is both extremely simple and state of the art in many parameter regimes.

Data is a scarce and valuable resource. As a result, data analysts often reuse the same dataset to answer multiple queries. Hardt and Ullman

Much prior work has focused on the design of

\varphi

This work is motivated by a simple question.

How can we guarantee that the query responses will have low bias, even without an explicit mechanism?

The purpose of asking this question is twofold. First, it models a reality in which data analysts often do not explicitly use mechanisms to obfuscate query results before looking at them. Second, if the assumptions we make on the queries are sufficiently easy to explain to an analyst, they are actionable, as the analyst can keep these assumptions in mind when deciding how to analyze data.

Our approach.
We show that as long as each query takes as input a random subsample of the dataset and outputs to a small range, the results will have low bias. Quantitatively, our results depend
only
on the size of the subsample and the number of possible outputs the query has. These quantities are intuitive and easy to explain to a data analyst who may be interested in ensuring their results have low bias. Furthermore many algorithms, such as stochastic gradient descent, already subsample their data, in which case we guarantee generalization for free.

One interpretation of this framework is that it eliminates the need to design a noise distribution for each task. Prior works design mechanisms to bound the bias by adding an appropriate amount of noise to the true result before returning it (e.g. by adding a mean-
0
Gaussian). Our work shows that the noise inherent in subsampling suffices. It also extends to tasks where it is difficult to design an appropriate noise distribution – for example, when the output of each query is categorical rather than numerical.

As easy corollaries of this subsampling approach, we give simple mechanisms for two foundational tasks, statistical queries and median finding, demonstrating the power of this framework. In particular, our mechanism for answering the broad and influential class of
statistical queries
(SQs)
[
Kea98
,
FGR+17
]
achieves state of the art accuracy in many parameter regimes. In addition to their broad applicability, statistical queries have been the standard bearer by which we assess the utility of approaches for adaptive data analysis since the early works of
[
DFH+15c
,
BNS+16
]
. Our SQ mechanism had advantages beyond its accuracy: It runs in sublinear time, and its extreme simplicity renders it broadly applicable in non-standard setting.

1.1
Main results

We show that an analyst can ask an adaptive sequence of
subsampling queries
without incurring large bias.

Definition 1.1

(Subsampling query)
.

For any sample
S\in X^{n}
and function
\varphi:X^{w}\to Y
, the
subsampling query

\varphi
is answered by drawing
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w}
uniformly without replacement from
S
, and then providing the response
\bm{y}=\varphi(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w})
.

In an abuse of notation, we use
\varphi(S)
to denote the distribution of
\bm{y}
defined above. Similarly, for any distribution
\mathcal{D}
supported on
X
, we use
\varphi(\mathcal{D})
to denote the distribution of
\bm{y}^{\prime}=\varphi(\bm{x}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w}^{\prime})
when
\bm{x}_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w}^{\prime}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}
.

We allow the analyst’s choice of queries to be
adaptive
: Formally, at each time step
t\in[T]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,T\}
, the analyst selects a query
\varphi_{t}:X^{w_{t}}\to Y_{t}
which may depend on the previous responses
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1}
and then receives the response
\bm{y}_{t}\sim\varphi_{t}(S)
. We begin with an informal result bounding the sample size needed to ensure the results have low bias.

Theorem 1

(Subsampling responses have low bias)
.

Suppose an analyst asks an adaptive sequence of
T
subsampling queries, each mapping
X^{w}
to
Y
, to a sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
. As long as

n\geq\Omega(w\sqrt{T|Y|}),

with high probability, all of the queries will have low bias.

Compare
Theorem

1
to a naive approach which takes a fresh batch of
w
samples for each query. Subsampling has a quadratically better dependence on the number of queries asked,
T
, than that naive approach which requires
n\geq wT
. Based on the lower bounds in
[
HU14
,
SU15b
]
, this quadratic improvement is optimal.

Informally, a subsampling query
\varphi
has low bias if the distributions
\varphi(\bm{S})
and
\varphi(\mathcal{D})
are “close.” We’ll formalize and generalize
Theorem

1
in
Section

2.5
. That generalization will, for example, allow the analyst to choose a different domain size and range for each query.

The main ingredient in the proof of
Theorem

1
is a bound on the mutual information between the sample and the query responses.

Theorem 2

(Subsampling queries reveal little information)
.

Suppose an analyst asks an adaptive sequence of
T
subsampling queries to a sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
where the
t^{\text{th}}
query takes as input a subsample of size
\bm{w}_{t}
and outputs to a range of size
\bm{r}_{t}
. Then, the mutual information between the query responses and the sample is at most

I(\bm{S};(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}))\leq\frac{4\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{w}_{t}(\bm{r}_{t}-1)\right]}{n}

where
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}
are the query responses and the expectation is over the analyst’s choices for the subsampling queries.

1.2.1
A mechanism for statistical queries

1.2.2
A mechanism for finding approximate medians

We also consider a generalization of statistical queries, each of which map
w
inputs to some set
R\subseteq\mathds{R}
. For such queries, we give a mechanism for determining an
approximate median
of the distribution
\varphi(\mathcal{D})

Definition 1.2

(Approximate median)
.

For a distribution
\mathcal{E}
with support in
\mathds{R}
, we say a value
y
is an
approximate median of
\mathcal{E}

\min\left(\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{E}}[\bm{x}\leq y],\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{E}}[\bm{x}\geq y]\right)\geq 0.4.

\varphi^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{w})=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}\in[w]}[\varphi(\bm{x}_{i})].

Since
\varphi^{\prime}

\varphi^{\prime}
has a smaller variance, Chebyshev’s inequality implies that any approximate median of
\varphi^{\prime}
will be within

\mu_{\varphi}(\mathcal{D})
. As a result, the mechanism of

2
can give similar accuracy results as guaranteed in
Theorem

3

1.3
Other related work

Subsampling has been thoroughly explored in the context of privacy amplification (see e.g.
[
BBG18
,
ZW19
]
or the book chapter
[
Ste22
]
): if
\mathcal{A}
is a differentially private algorithm, running
\mathcal{A}
on a random subset of the data gives an algorithm with even better privacy parameters. Given the previous applications of differential privacy to adaptive data analysis, this seems like a natural starting point for our work. However, such an approach is not sufficient to analyze subsampling queries. Indeed, subsampling queries do not necessarily satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy with sufficiently good parameters to give useful bounds on the bias.

Fish, Reyzin, and Rubinstein explored the use of subsampling to speed up classical mechanisms for adaptive data analysis
[
FRR20
]
. For example, their mechanism for answering a statistical query
\varphi
, computes
\varphi
on a random subsample of the data
and
adds Laplacian noise to that result. This allows them to retain the accuracy guarantees of prior mechanisms that added Laplacian noise
[
BNS+16
]
while also running in sublinear time. In contrast, our work shows that subsampling alone is sufficient, and achieves sample size bounds that improve upon prior work.

Technical overview

For the vast majority of mechanisms for adaptive data analysis, differential privacy (DP) is used to prove correctness. This is done in three steps.

1.

Ensure that each answer the mechanisms provides is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. This is typically accomplished by adding noise as appropriate.

2.

Show that DP composes nicely, even when the queries are chosen adaptively.

3.

Prove, via a transfer theorem, that any differentially private mechanism also generalizes.

Steps 2 and 3 are, by now, quite generic. See, for example,
[
DRV10
]
for composition and
[
BNS+16
]
for a transfer theorem. Therefore, when designing a new mechanism, one “only” needs to verify that the mechanism satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP for appropriate
\varepsilon,\delta
. Specifically, if the aim is to answer
T
many queries, we roughly need
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}
and
\delta\leq\frac{1}{T}
.

Unfortunately, this approach only gives trivial bounds for subsampling queries. Consider the simplest type of subsampling query that takes as input a single point and outputs one bit of information about it (meaning
\varphi:X^{1}\to\{0,1\}
). The output of the subsampling query
\varphi(S)
on sample
S\in X^{n}
is distributed according to a Bernoulli with
p=\frac{k}{n}
, where
k
is the number of points in
S
on which
\varphi
outputs
1
. Therefore, there are
S,S^{\prime}\in X^{n}
differing in only one point for which
\varphi(S)\sim\mathrm{Ber}(0)
and
\varphi(S^{\prime})\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\frac{1}{n})
. As a result, this subsampling query is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP only if
\delta\geq\frac{1}{n}
, regardless of how
\varepsilon
is set. Such weak parameters only allow for adaptively answering
\approx n
queries.

In contrast,
Theorem

1
allows for
\approx n^{2}
of these simple queries, which is optimal. To achieve this quadratic improvement over the DP-based bounds, we need a different approach.

2.1
Sketch of
Theorem

1
for the simplest subsampling queries

We begin by sketching a proof of our main result in the simplest setting. Specifically, we’ll show that if an analyst asks
\tilde{O}(n^{2})
subsampling queries, each mapping
X^{1}
to
\{0,1\}
, the last query asked will have low bias. This sketch follows the same three steps described in
Section

2
, except we replace “differential privacy” with
average leave-one-out KL stability
.

Definition 2.1

(Average leave-one-out KL stability
[
FS18
]
)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
is
\varepsilon
-
ALOOKL stable
if there is a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:X^{n-1}\to Y
such that, for all samples
S\in X^{n}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([n])}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{-{\bm{i}}})}\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon,

where
d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\cdot}\middle\|{\cdot}\right)
is the KL-divergence and
S_{-i}
refers to the
S
with the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate removed.

With this definition in hand, we seek to show the following three claims.

1.

For any
\varphi:X^{1}\to\{0,1\}
, the randomized algorithm mapping
S\in X^{n}
to
\varphi(S)
is
\tilde{O}(1/n^{2})
-ALOOKL stable.

2.

ALOOKL stability composes linearly and adaptively: Applied to our setting, this means that any analyst that adaptively asks
T
queries each of which are
\varepsilon
-ALOOKL stable and then chooses a test as a function of the query responses, the algorithm mapping the sample to the test is
(T\varepsilon)
-ALOOKL stable.

3.

ALOOKL stability bounds bias: Applied to our setting, let
\mathcal{M}
be an ALOOKL stable randomized algorithm that takes as input a sample set and outputs some test
\psi:X^{1}\to[0,1]
. Then, if we sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and a test function
{\bm{\psi}}\sim\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})
, the expectation of
{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{S})
is close to the expectation of
{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{x})
where
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
is a fresh sample independent of
{\bm{\psi}}
.

The second claim, composition, was proven by Feldman and Steinke in their work introducing ALOOKL stability
[
FS18
]
. We therefore only need to sketch the first and third claims.

Simple subsampling queries are ALOOKL stable.
Fix some subsampling query
\varphi:X^{1}\to\{0,1\}
and let
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:X^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be the randomized algorithm that takes in a sample
S
and outputs
\varphi(S)
. The first step in bounding the ALOOKL stability of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
is designing the randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}:X^{n-1}\to\{0,1\}
. The randomized algorithm,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
, will do the following: With probability
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
, it outputs a uniform bit in
\{0,1\}
. Otherwise, it runs the subsampling query
\varphi
on its input (i.e. outputs
\varphi(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}
chosen uniformly from its input).

Rather than a full analysis proving the stability of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
, we only sketch two extreme and representative inputs.

1.

Consider an input
S\in X^{n}
for which
\varphi
outputs
1
on roughly
n/2
of the
x\in S
. In this case,
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}(S)\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p)
for
p\approx 1/2
. Furthermore, regardless of how we remove a point from
S
, we’ll have that
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}(S_{-i})\sim\mathrm{Ber}(p^{\prime})
for
|p^{\prime}-p|\leq O(1/n)
. The desired bound on ALOOKL stability follows from the observation that
d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\middle\|{\mathrm{Ber}(p+O(1/n))}\right)\leq O(1/n^{2})
as long as
p
is close to
1/2
.

2.

For the other case, consider an input
S\in X^{n}
for which
\varphi
outputs
1
on exactly one
x\in S
and outputs
0
on all
n-1
other points in
S
. On this input,
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}(S)\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1/n)
. Then, with probability
\frac{1}{n}
over
\bm{i}
,
S_{-\bm{i}}
will contain only points on which
\varphi
evaluates to
0
, in which case,
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}^{\prime}(S_{-\bm{i}})\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1/(2n^{2}))
. With the remaining
1-\frac{1}{n}
probability over
\bm{i}
,
S_{-\bm{i}}
outputs

with probability
\frac{1}{n}+O(1/n^{2})
. We can then bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([n])}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}^{\prime}(S_{-{\bm{i}}})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\frac{1}{n}\cdot d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(1/n)}\middle\|{\mathrm{Ber}(1/n^{2})}\right)+(1-1/n)\cdot d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(1/n)}\middle\|{\mathrm{Ber}(1/n+O(1/n^{2}))}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot\tilde{O}(1/n)+(1-1/n)\cdot O(1/n^{2})=\tilde{O}(1/n^{2}).

This second case exemplifies why ALOOKL stability works better than differential privacy in this setting. If
S
contains exactly one point on which
\varphi
evaluates to one, then when
S_{-\bm{i}}
happens to remove that special point, the cost is quite high. However, this cost is modulated by the fact that the special point is only removed with probability
1/n
. In contrast, since DP chooses
S^{\prime}
to be a worst-case neighbor, this high cost is not modulated.

ALOOKL stability bounds bias.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a ALOOKL stable randomized algorithm that takes as input a sample set
S\in X^{n}
and outputs a test function
\varphi:X^{1}\to[0,1]
. Consider the following three experiments.

1.

In the first experiment, we draw
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
,
{\bm{\varphi}}\sim\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})
, and
\bm{i}\sim[n]
. Then, we output
{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{S}_{\bm{i}})
.

2.

In the second experiment, we similarly draw
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
{\bm{\varphi}}\sim\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})
. This time, we draw
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}
we output
{\bm{\varphi}}(\bm{x})
.

3.

In the third experiment, we similarly draw
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
\bm{i}\sim[n]
. This time, we draw
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-\bm{i}})
and output
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{\bm{i}})
. Here,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is the randomized algorithm guaranteed to exist by
Definition

2.1
.

The goal is to show that the first and second experiments have similar expectations. We do this by using the third experiment as a bridge. The first and third experiments are close by the guarantee of ALOOKL stability – in particular, even conditioned on the choice of
\bm{S}
and
\bm{i}
(which fixes
\bm{S}_{\bm{i}}
), the distributions of
{\bm{\varphi}}
and
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}
will be close.

The second and third experiments are close because, in both cases, the input to the test (
\bm{x}
for the second experiment and
\bm{S}_{\bm{i}}
for the third experiment) is distributed according to
\mathcal{D}
and independent of the test used (
{\bm{\varphi}}
in the second experiment and
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}
in the third). For the second experiment, this independence is immediate from the experiment setup. For the third experiment, it holds because the drawing of
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}
only depends on
\bm{S}_{-\bm{i}}
which is independent of
\bm{S}_{\bm{i}}
. Therefore, the only difference between the second and third experiments is in the marginal distributions of
{\bm{\varphi}}
and
{\bm{\varphi}}^{\prime}
, and this difference is once again guaranteed to be small by the ALOOKL stability condition.

2.2
The remainder of this technical overview

Generalizing the ideas from
Section

2.1
to subsampling queries that take as input more than one point requires a fair bit of work. Instead, we give a technically simpler and quantitatively stronger proof that relies on a natural generalization of ALOOKL stability, which we call
average leave-many-out KL stability
(ALMOKL stability). We describe this new form of stability and analyze the ALMOKL stability of subsampling queries in
Section

2.3
. This analysis involves a new generalization of Hoeffding’s celebrated reduction theorem
[
Hoe63
]
, described in
Section

2.3.2
, which may be of independent interest.

Rather than directly bound bias using ALMOKL stability, we use mutual information as a technical intermediate. Specifically, in
Section

2.4
, we describe a proof of the following. If the analyst adaptively asks a series of adaptively chosen queries to a sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
, then the mutual information between
\bm{S}
and all the query responses is bounded by the sum of the ALMOKL stabilities of the queries. Mutual information is well-known to bound bias in a variety of contexts. In
Section

2.5
, we use that well-known connection to give formal statements of our bias bounds (formalizing
Theorem

1
).

As is typical of generalization results based on mutual information, our guarantees in
Section

2.5
only bound the
expected
bias. While one can use Markov’s inequality to get an explicit dependence on the failure probability, that dependence will be polynomial in the inverse failure probability. In
Section

2.6
, we describe how to improve that dependence (in some settings) to logarithmic in the inverse failure probability. This involves a generalization of a direct product theorem of Shaltiel
[
Sha04
]
which may also be of independent interest.

2.3
The average leave-many-out KL stability of subsampling queries

We introduce the following notion of
algorithmic stability
.

Definition 2.2

(Average leave-many-out KL stability)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
is
ALMOKL stable
with parameters
(m,\varepsilon)
if there is a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:X^{n-m}\to Y
such that, for all samples
S\in X^{n}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon

where
d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\cdot}\middle\|{\cdot}\right)
is the KL-divergence,
\bm{J}
is drawn uniformly from all size-
(n-m)
subsets of
[n]
, and
S_{J}
indicates the size-
(n-m)
subset of
S
specified by the indices within
J
.

This definition generalizes ALOOKL (
Definition

2.1
) stability, which corresponds to ALMOKL stability with
m=1
. At first glance, this more general definition seems harder to satisfy because
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
must predict the behavior of
\mathcal{M}(S)
when a large fraction of points from
S
are removed, rather than when just a single point is removed. However, our analysis also allows for a larger KL divergence in the case where
m
is large – specifically, we will later see the goal is to minimize the ratio
\varepsilon/m
. Therefore, by setting
m
larger, we can afford a weaker bound on
\varepsilon
. Indeed, for subsampling queries, we find using
m\approx n/2
gives a stronger result
and
simpler analysis than
m=1
.

To use ALMOKL stability in our analysis, we must bound the stability of subsampling queries.

Lemma 2.3

(Subsampling queries are ALMOKL stable)
.

For any function
\varphi:X^{w}\to Y
and
m\leq n
, the subsampling query
\varphi
is
(m,\varepsilon)
-ALMOKL stable for

\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{w(|Y|-1)}{n-m+1}.

As discussed earlier, the ratio
\varepsilon/m
will play a crucial role in our analysis. By setting
m=\lceil n/2\rceil
, this ratio becomes
O(w|Y|/n^{2})
, which is enough to prove our results.

2.3.1
Bounding the stability of simple subsampling queries

We sketch a proof of
Lemma

2.3
in the simplest setting where
w=1
,
Y=\{0,1\}
, and
m=n/2
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:X^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be the randomized algorithm mapping
S
to
\varphi(S)
. Then, the randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}^{\prime}:X^{n/2}\to\{0,1\}
will do the following: With probability
\frac{1}{n}

\{0,1\}
. Otherwise, it runs the subsampling query
\varphi
on its input (i.e. outputs
\varphi(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}
chosen uniformly from its input). Note this is essentially the same construction of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}^{\prime}
used in
Section

2.1
, except in this case, we output a random bit with probability
\frac{1}{n}
rather than
\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

We aim to show, for any
S\in X^{n}
, that

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\leq O\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

Consider two cases:

1.

If
\varphi
evaluates to
0
on
every

x\in S
, then
\mathcal{M}(S)\sim\mathrm{Ber}(0)
and regardless of what
J
is chosen,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{J})\sim\mathrm{Ber}(1/2n)
. In this case,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{\bm{J}})}\right)\right]=d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(0)}\middle\|{\mathrm{Ber}(1/2n)}\right)=O\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

A symmetric argument holds if
\varphi
evaluates to
1
on every
x\in S
.

2.

Otherwise, let
p
be the fraction on
x\in S
on which
\varphi
evaluates to
1
(in this case,
p\in[1/n,1-1/n]
). Then, for
\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}
, let
\bm{q}
be the probability that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{\bm{J}})
evaluates to
1
. We observe that
\left|\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{q}]-p\right|\leq 1/n
and that
\operatorname{{Var}}[\bm{q}]=O(p(1-p)/n)
. Using a second-order approximation of the KL divergence,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathrm{Ber}(p)}\middle\|{\mathrm{Ber}(\bm{q})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\frac{(p-\bm{q})^{2}}{2p(1-p)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{q}]-p)^{2}}{2p(1-p)}+\frac{\operatorname{{Var}}[\bm{q}]}{2p(1-p)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1/n^{2}}{2p(1-p)}+\frac{O(\frac{p(1-p)}{n})}{2p(1-p)}=O\left(\textstyle\frac{1}{n}\right).

2.3.2
Handling larger subsampling queries using the concentration of U-statistics

To prove

Roughly speaking, the stability of queries using subsamples of size-

Since the ALMOKL stability of subsampling queries with

\approx w/n
.
5
5

5

To show the statement in

Theorem 5

(Hoeffding’s reduction theorem
[
Hoe63
]
)
.

Consider any finite population

and integer
m\leq n
. Let
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[m]}\bm{x}_{i}\right)\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[m]}\bm{y}_{i}\right)\right].

Theorem

5
give a simple approach for proving concentration inequalities on the sum of random variables sampled without replacement. Since

is convex, it implies that the moment generating function of

\sum_{i}\bm{y}_{i}

\sum_{i}\bm{y}_{i}

\sum_{i}\bm{x}_{i}

Our generalize of Hoeffding’s reduction theorem uses a few pieces of notation: For any

and its compliment respectively. The term
I(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}};\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})
measures how much knowing half the elements of
\bm{S}
affects the distribution of the other half of elements and is natural measure of “productness.” Fortunately, we find that learning the output of a ALMOKL stable randomized algorithm cannot change the half-conditional entropy of the sample by much.

Lemma 2.6

(Bounding expected drop in half-conditional entropy)
.

For any randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
and random variable
\bm{S}
supported on
X^{n}
(including non-product distributions),

H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S})-H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S}\mid\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))\leq\mathrm{stab}_{n/2}(\mathcal{M}).

In addition to
Lemma

2.6
, we need one last ingredient.

Lemma 2.7

(Half-conditional entropy bounds mutual information)
.

For any random variables
\bm{S},\bm{y}
where the distribution of
\bm{S}
is product on
X^{n}
,

I(\bm{S};\bm{y})\leq 2\cdot\left(H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S})-H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})\right).

Given these ingredients, the proof of
Theorem

7
in the special case of
m=n/2
is simple. By repeatedly applying
Lemma

2.6
to each query response,

H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S})-H_{\mathrm{half}}(\bm{S}\mid(\bm{y},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}))\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\sum_{t\in T}\mathrm{stab}_{n/2}\left(\mathcal{M}^{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1}}\right)\right].

The desired bound then follows from
Lemma

2.7
. We note that this application of
Lemma

2.7
does require the
starting
distribution of
\bm{S}
to be product, but we can apply it even if the intermediate distributions of
\bm{S}
conditioned on some of the responses is non-product. This is because
Lemma

2.6
does
not
require productness, so we can bound the drop in
m
-conditional entropy of the entire sequence of adaptively chosen queries.

In
Section

5
, we generalize these ideas to other choices of
m
. Feldman and Steinke also show how to bound the mutual information using ALOOKL stability, corresponding to
Theorem

7
in the case of
m=1

[
FS18
]
. Our techniques are similar to theirs, appropriately generalized for
m>1
. Our main contributions to this notion of stability is the idea to generalize the definition to the
m>1
case as well as a unique presentation, particularly the introduction of half-conditional entropy.

2.6
A self-reduction for boosting success probability

As is typical of mutual information-based bounds,

only guarantees low bias in expectation. When

Theorem 10

(Improved dependence on failure probability)
.

In the setting of

\bm{w}_{t}=1
for all
t\in[T]
and
\sum_{t\in[T]}|\bm{Y}_{t}|\leq b
almost surely, then, for any failure probability
\delta>0

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sup_{\psi\in\bm{\Psi}}\mathrm{error}(\psi,\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\geq O\left(\ln(m/\delta)\cdot\left(\frac{b}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)\right]\leq\delta.

The case of
w=1
is particularly important for two reasons.

1.

It is sufficient for our application of answering statistical queries, a widely-applicable query class, given in
Section

1.2.1
. Indeed, one way to characterize statistical queries are precisely those queries
\varphi:X^{n}\to[0,1]
for which an unbiased and bounded estimator of
\mu_{\varphi}(S)
can be computed given a single
\bm{x}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)

2.

One way to answer a query with

is to cast a sample of
n

\mathcal{D}
as a sample of
\lfloor n/w\rfloor
points each drawn from
\mathcal{D}^{w}
. By doing so, each query

10
would directly hold without the restriction that

The high probability guarantee of

Lemma 2.10

(Informal version of

8.2
)
.

In the context of

9

The starting point of

S\in X^{N\coloneqq nk}
, the answer of a query
\bm{y}=\varphi(S)
where
\varphi:X^{1}\to Y
is equivalent to the answer
\bm{y}^{\prime}=\varphi(S_{\bm{i}})
where

are equal-sized partitions of

\bm{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}([k])

An illustration of why, for

The hypothesis of

However, because the analyst can choose which query to ask a group

KL divergence, entropy, mutual information and their properties

Definition 4.1

(Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence)
.

For distributions
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{E}
supported on the same domain, the
KL divergence
between
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
is defined as,

d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}}\middle\|{\mathcal{E}}\right)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{D}(\bm{x})}{\mathcal{E}(\bm{x})}\right)\right].

Definition 4.2

(Entropy and conditional entropy)
.

The entropy of a random variable
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}
is defined as

H(\bm{x})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}_{x}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{x})}\right)\right].

For a second random variable
\bm{y}
with marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{y}
, the conditional entropy is defined,

H(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{y}}\left[H(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}=\bm{y}^{\prime})\right].

One of the many convenient properties of entropy is that it is
additive
.

Fact 4.3

(Additivity of entropy)
.

For any random variables
\bm{x},\bm{y}
,

H((\bm{x},\bm{y}))=H(\bm{x})+H(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})=H(\bm{y})+H(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y}).

Definition 4.4

(Mutual information)
.

For random variables
\bm{x},\bm{y}
jointly distributed according to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{x}
and
\mathcal{D}_{y}
be the marginal distributions of
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
respectively. The mutual information between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
is defined as

I(\bm{x};\bm{y})\coloneqq H(\bm{x})-H(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y})=H(\bm{y})-H(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})=I(\bm{y};\bm{x}),

which is equivalent to the following

I(\bm{x};\bm{y})=d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}}\middle\|{\mathcal{D}_{x}\times\mathcal{D}_{y}}\right)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}_{x\mid\bm{y}}}\middle\|{\mathcal{D}_{x}}\right)\right].

where
\mathcal{D}_{x\mid y}
is the marginal distribution of
\bm{x}
conditioned on
\bm{y}=y
.

A useful fact about KL divergence and mutual information is that they are
nonnegative
.

Fact 4.5

(Non-negativity of KL divergence and mutual information)
.

For any distributions
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{E}
on the same domain,

d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}}\middle\|{\mathcal{E}}\right)\geq 0.

As a consequence, for any random variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}

I(\bm{x};\bm{y})\geq 0.

As an easy corollary, we obtain the following.

Corollary 4.6

(Conditioning cannot increase entropy)
.

For any random variables
\bm{x},\bm{y}
,

H(\bm{x}\mid\bm{y})\leq H(\bm{x}).

We’ll also use conditional mutual information.

Definition 4.7

(Conditional mutual information)

hold for deterministic analysts, they also hold for randomized analysts.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a randomized analyst. We can think of it as a mixture of deterministic analysts:
\mathcal{A}
first draws
\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{Z}
and then executes the deterministic strategy
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{z}}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}(\cdot,\bm{z})
. We begin with the reduction for
Theorems

1
,
8
and
9
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\text{Error with $\mathcal{A}$ as the analyst}]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}}[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\text{Error with $\mathcal{A}_{\bm{z}}$ as the analyst}]]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\text{Error with $\mathcal{A}_{z}$ as the analyst}].

The left-hand side of
Equation

6
when
\mathcal{A}
is the analyst is the expectation of the same quantity when
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{z}}
is the analyst. Therefore, if it is small for all deterministic analysts, it is also small for all randomized analysts.

For
Theorem

10
, the same argument holds where ”Error with
\mathcal{A}
as the analyst” is replaced by ”Failure probability with
\mathcal{A}
as the analyst.” Finally, for
Theorem

2
which bounds the mutual information of
\bm{S}
and
\bm{y}
, let
\bm{y}_{z}
be the distribution of
\bm{y}
conditioned on the analyst being
\mathcal{A}_{z}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}}\left[I(\bm{S};\bm{y}_{\bm{z}})\right]-I(\bm{S};\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\left(H(\bm{S})-\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}}\left[H(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y}_{\bm{z}})\right]\right)-\left(H(\bm{S})-H(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})\right)

\displaystyle=H(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})-H(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y},\bm{z})

\displaystyle=I((\bm{S}\mid\bm{y});\bm{z})\geq 0.

(
4.5
)

Therefore, upper bounding the mutual information for deterministic analysts in

suffices to upper bound it for randomized analysts as well.
∎

4.1.2
Deterministic vs randomized subsampling queries

The analyst may be interested in asking a subsampling query
\varphi:X^{w}\to Y
that itself incorporates randomness. In this case, we can model
\varphi
as a deterministic function
\varphi^{\prime}
which also takes in a source of randomness, i.e.
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{w})\coloneqq\varphi^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{w},\bm{z})
where
\bm{z}
is the source of randomness. However, in each of our theorems, we can without loss of generality assume that the query functions are deterministic by using the random sample as a source of randomness. If the original sample is
\mathcal{D}
we consider the augmented distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\mathcal{Z}_{2}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{Z}_{T}
where each
\mathcal{Z}_{t}
will be a source of randomness for the
t^{\text{th}}
query. We then create a
deterministic
query
\psi_{t}:(X\times Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{T})^{w}\to Y
that evaluates

\psi_{t}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{w})\coloneqq\varphi^{\prime}((a_{1})_{x},\ldots,(a_{w})_{x},(a_{1})_{z_{t}})

where for
a\in X\times Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{T}
, the notation
a_{x}
takes the first component of
a
and
a_{z_{t}}
takes the
(t+1)^{\text{th}}
component of
a
. Using
\psi_{t}
with the distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
will generate the exact same distribution of responses as
\varphi_{t}
with the distribution
\mathcal{D}
, despite the former being a deterministic function and the latter a randomized function.

5
Average leave-many-out KL stability and its connection to mutual information

The starting point of our analysis is a new notion of
algorithmic stability
. We require the algorithm’s output to be stable even if a large portion of the dataset is removed. This definition generalizes
[
FS18
]
’s notion of “Average leave-one-out KL stability,” which is equivalent to the below with
m=1
.

Definition 5.1

(Average leave-many-out KL stability,
Definition

2.2
restated)
.

A randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
is
ALMOKL stable
with parameters
(m,\varepsilon)
if there is a randomized algorithm
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:X^{n-m}\to Y
such that, for all samples
S\in X^{n}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon.

We’ll furthermore use the notation
\mathrm{stab}_{m}(\mathcal{M})
to refer to the infimum over
\varepsilon
such that
\mathcal{M}
is
(m,\varepsilon)
-ALMOKL stable.

The utility of ALMOKL stability is that we can use it to upper bound the mutual information between a sample and a series of adaptively chosen randomized algorithms.

Theorem 12

(Using ALMOKL stability to upper bound mutual information, restatement of
Theorem

7
)
.

Let
\bm{S}
drawn from a product distribution over
X^{n}
and, for each
t\in[T]
, draw
\bm{y}_{t}\sim\mathcal{M}^{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1}}(\bm{S})
. Then, for any
m\in[n]
,

I(\bm{S};(\bm{y},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}))\leq\frac{n}{m}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\sum_{t\in T}\mathrm{stab}_{m}\left(\mathcal{M}^{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1}}\right)\right].

The proof of
Theorem

12
is broken into two pieces. First, we bound the drop in
m
-conditional entropy in terms of ALMOKL stability. Note that the half-conditional entropy defined in
Equation

4
corresponds to the
m=n/2
case below.

Definition 5.2

(
m
-conditional entropy)
.

For any random variable
\bm{S}
supported on
X^{n}
and
m\in[n]
, the
m
-conditional entropy is defined as

H_{m}(\bm{S})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})\right].

Furthermore, for any
\bm{y}
with marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{y}
, we use

H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}_{y}}[H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y}=\bm{y}^{\prime})].

Lemma 5.3

(ALMOKL stability bounds the drop in
m
-conditional entropy, generalization of
Lemma

2.6
)
.

For any
(m,\varepsilon
)-ALMOKL stable
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
and random variable
\bm{S}
supported on
X^{n}
(including non-product distributions),

H_{m}(\bm{S})-H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))\leq\varepsilon.

The second ingredient of
Theorem

12
is using the drop in
m
-conditional entropy to upper bound mutual information.

Lemma 5.4

(
m
-conditional entropy bounds mutual information, generalization of
Lemma

2.7
)
.

For any random variables
\bm{S},\bm{y}
where the distribution of
\bm{S}
is product on
X^{n}
and
m\leq n
,

I(\bm{S};\bm{y})\leq\frac{n}{m}\cdot\left(H_{m}(\bm{S})-H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})\right).

Before proving each of these two lemmas individually, we’ll show how they imply
Theorem

12
.

Proof of
Theorem

12
given
Lemmas

5.3
and
5.4
.

We first use
Lemma

5.3
bound the drop in
m
-conditional entropy of
\bm{S}
by conditioning on
\bm{y}\coloneqq(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T})
.

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[H_{m}(\bm{S})-H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\sum_{t\in T}H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1})-H_{m}(\bm{S}\mid\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\sum_{t\in T}\mathrm{stab}_{m}\left(\mathcal{M}^{\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{t-1}}\right)\right].

The desired result follows from
Lemma

5.4
.
∎

5.1
Bounding the drop in
m
-conditional entropy using ALMOKL stability

In this subsection, we prove
Lemma

5.3
. This proof uses many of the ideas from Feldman and Steinke’s proof of a similar result in the special case where
m=1

[
FS18
]
. Just like them, we use the following fact that states the “center” of a collection of probability, as measured by minimizing the average KL divergence to each distribution, is exactly the mean of those distributions.

Fact 5.5

(
[
BMD+05
]
Proposition 1 &
[
FSG08
]
Theorem II.1)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{D}_{a}\}
be a family of distributions, each supported on
X
, and indexed by
a\in A
and
\mathcal{A}
be a distribution supported on
A
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{a}\sim\mathcal{A}}[\mathcal{D}_{\bm{a}}]
denote the convex combinations of the distributions
\{\mathcal{D}_{a}\}
with mixture weights given by
\mathcal{A}
. Then, for any
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
supported on
X
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{a}\sim\mathcal{A}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}_{\bm{a}}}\middle\|{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}}\right)\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{a}\sim\mathcal{A}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{D}_{\bm{a}}}\middle\|{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\right)\right].

5.5
actually holds for any
Bregman divergence
, which, in particular, includes KL divergence.

Proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Expanding the definitions, our goal is to show that for any
\bm{S}
distributed on
X^{n}
,
m\in[n]
, and randomized algorithms
\mathcal{M}:X^{n}\to Y
,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}:X^{n-m}\to Y
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})-H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}},\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))\right]\leq\sup_{S\in X^{n}}\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(S)}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(S_{-\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\right).

Rather than picking a worst-case
S
, we’ll prove the above holds on average over the distribution of
\bm{S}
:

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})-H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}},\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})}\right)\right]\right]

We’ll show that the above inequality holds for each
J\in\binom{[n]}{m}
individually, which implies the desired result by averaging over the choices of
J
. Throughout this proof, we’ll use
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\cdot]
as an operator that takes as input a random variable and outputs the probability a new draw of that random variable takes on that value (for example,
H(\bm{x})=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}}[\ln(1/\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{x}])]
). Let
\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})
. Then,

\displaystyle H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})-H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}},\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S},\bm{y}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{1}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}]}\right)-\ln\left(\frac{1}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J},\bm{y}]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S},\bm{y}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J},\bm{y}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}]}\right)\right]

Note that, for any random variables
\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c}
,

\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{b},\bm{c}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{c}]}=\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{b},\bm{c}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{c}]}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{b}\mid\bm{c}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{b}\mid\bm{c}]}=\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a},\bm{b}\mid\bm{c}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{a}\mid\bm{c}]\cdot\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{b}\mid\bm{c}]}=\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{b}\mid\bm{a},\bm{c}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{b}\mid\bm{c}]}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})-H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}},\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S},\bm{y}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S})}\middle\|{(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}_{-J}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J},\bm{S}_{J})}\middle\|{(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J})}\right)\right]\right].

Here, we utilize
5.5
. Fix any choice of
\bm{S}_{-J}=S_{-J}
. The distribution of
(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}=S_{J})
is exactly the convex combination of
\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{J},S_{-J}
where each
\bm{S}_{J}
is sampled conditioned on
\bm{S}_{-J}
. Therefore, it is the distribution minimizing the above KL divergence. As a result, we can replace it with any other distribution, namely
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-J})
, without decreasing the KL divergence.

\displaystyle H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}})-H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-\bm{J}},\mathcal{M}(\bm{S}))

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}_{-J}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}_{J}\mid\bm{S}_{-J}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({(\bm{y}\mid\bm{S}_{-J},\bm{S}_{J})}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-J}}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\left({\mathcal{M}(\bm{S})}\middle\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(\bm{S}_{-J}}\right)\right].\qed

Bounding mutual information using

and

\bm{S}

For any choice of

\displaystyle=H(\bm{S}_{-J})+H(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}\mid\bm{S}_{-J})

6
Bounding the stability of subsampling queries

The goal of this section is prove the following result.

Lemma 6.1
.

For any function
\varphi:X^{w}\to Y
and
m\leq n
, the randomized algorithm which maps
S\in X^{n}
to
\varphi(S)
is
(m,\varepsilon)
-ALMOKL stable for

\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{|Y|-1}{\lfloor\frac{n-m}{w}\rfloor+1}\leq\frac{w(|Y|-1)}{n-m+1}.

Before proving
Lemma

6.1
, we show how it gives a bound on the mutual information between the sample and query responses.

Theorem 13

(
Theorem

2
restated)
.

For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
over domain
X
and analyst that makes a series of adaptive queries
{\bm{\varphi}}_{1}:X^{\bm{w}_{1}}\to\bm{Y}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\varphi}}_{T}:X^{\bm{w}_{T}}\to\bm{Y}_{T}
to a sample
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and receives responses
\bm{y}\coloneqq(\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T})

I(\bm{S};\bm{y})\leq\frac{4\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{\varphi}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{\varphi}}_{T}}\left[\sum_{t\in T}\bm{w}_{t}|\bm{Y}_{t}|\right]}{n}.

Proof of
Theorem

13
assuming
Lemma

6.1
.

We set
m\coloneqq\lceil n/2\rceil
. By
Lemma

6.1
, the
m
-ALMOKL stability of a subsampling query
\varphi:X^{w}\to Y
is
\frac{w(|Y|-1)}{n-m+1}\leq\frac{2w(|Y|-1)}{n}
. The desired result follows from
Theorem

12
and that
n/m\leq 2
.
∎

To prove
Lemma

6.1
, we need to design a randomized
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
for which,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\varphi(S)}\big\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})}\big)\right]\leq\varepsilon.

A natural choice for
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
is to make it perform the subsampling query
\varphi
, meaning it outputs
y
with probability
\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w}\sim(S_{J})^{(w)}}[\varphi(\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{w})=y]
. Unfortunately, this natural choice can lead to an infinite KL-divergence, even in the simplest case where

. If
S
has only contains a single point that evaluates to
y
, then with nonzero probability
S_{\bm{J}}
will contain
no
points that evaluate to
y
. In that case, the support of
\varphi(S)
will be strictly smaller than that of
\varphi(S)
, leading to an infinite KL divergence.

To avoid this, we smooth
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
to ensure it has a nonzero probability of outputting every point in
Y
. Specifically, we set,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{J})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathrm{Unif}(Y)&\text{wp }\alpha\\
\varphi(S_{J})&\text{wp }1-\alpha\end{cases}

\displaystyle\text{where},\quad\alpha\coloneqq\frac{|Y|}{|Y|+\lfloor\frac{n-m}{w}\rfloor}.

(8)

Our choice of
\alpha
is purely for technical convenience. As we will see in the proof of
Lemma

6.1
that with this choice of
\alpha
we are able to use the following inverse moment bound for the binomial distribution.

Fact 6.2

(Inverse expectation of a binomial random variable)
.

For any
n\in\mathds{N}
and
p\in(0,1)
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{k}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)}\left[\frac{1}{\bm{k}+1}\right]=\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1}}{p(n+1)}\leq\frac{1}{p(n+1)}.

Proof.

We expand the desired expectation,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\frac{1}{\bm{k}+1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\binom{n}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k+1}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(n+1)}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k+1}p^{k+1}(1-p)^{(n+1)-(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(n+1)}\cdot\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n+1,p)}[\bm{k}^{\prime}\neq 0]

\displaystyle=\frac{1-(1-p)^{n+1}}{p(n+1)}.\qed

Other choices of
\alpha
in
Equation

8
would correspond to different inverse moments of the binomial, which require a more involved analysis than the above simple fact (see e.g.
[
CS72
]
). Our proof of
Lemma

6.1
uses our generalization of Hoeffding’s reduction theorem,
Theorem

6
, which we prove in
Section

6.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

6.1
.

We use the mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}
defined in
Equation

8
. Our task is to bound, for any sample
S\in X^{n}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\varphi(S)}\big\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})}\big)\right]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\varphi(S)=\bm{y}]}{\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})=\bm{y}]}\right)\right]\right].

For each
y\in Y
and
J\in\binom{[n]}{n-m}
, we’ll define
q_{y}(S_{J})\coloneqq\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\varphi(S_{J})=y\right]
. Then, for

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{J})=y\right]=\frac{1}{\ell+|Y|}+\frac{\ell}{\ell+|Y|}\cdot q_{y}(S_{J})=\frac{\ell\cdot q_{y}(S_{J})+1}{\ell+|Y|}.

We’ll also use
p_{y}(S)
as shorthand for
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\varphi(S)=y]
. With this notation,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\varphi(S)}\big\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})}\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)}{\frac{\ell\cdot q_{\bm{y}}(S_{\bm{J}})+1}{\ell+|Y|}}\right)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{\ell\cdot q_{\bm{y}}(S_{\bm{J}})+1}\right)\right]\right].

Here, we apply
Theorem

6
. For any
y\in Y
, let
\varphi_{y}:X^{w}\to[0,1]
be function
T\mapsto\mathds{1}[\varphi(T)=y]
which indicates whether the subsampling query returns
y
. We choose
f_{y}(t)=\ln(\frac{p_{y}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{\ell\cdot t+1})
as the convex function. Then,
Theorem

6
says,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{\ell\cdot q_{\bm{y}}(S_{\bm{J}})+1}\right)\right]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[f_{y}\left(\mu_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})\right)\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{\ell}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}S^{(w)}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i\in[k]}\varphi_{y}({\bm{T}}_{i})\right)\right]

Based on the definition of
p_{y}(S)
, we have that
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\varphi_{y}({\bm{T}}_{i})=1]=p_{y}(S)
. Therefore, we can continue to bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{n-m}}\left[d_{\mathrm{KL}}\big({\varphi(S)}\big\|{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\ell,p_{\bm{y}}(S))}\left[\ln\left(\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{\bm{z}+1}\right)\right]\right]

(
Theorem

6
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\ell,p_{\bm{y}}(S))}\left[\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{\bm{z}+1}-1\right]\right]

(
\ln x\leq x-1
)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\varphi(S)}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(\ell,p_{\bm{y}}(S))}\left[\frac{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+|Y|)}{p_{\bm{y}}(S)(\ell+1)}-1\right]\right]

(

)

\displaystyle=\frac{|Y|-1}{\ell+1}

which is exactly the desired bound based on our definition of

6.1
Improving Hoeffding’s reduction theorem

In this section, we prove
Theorem

6
.
Recall that, for any
S\in X^{n}
and
w\leq n
, we use
{\bm{T}}\sim S^{(w)}
to indicate that
{\bm{T}}
contains
w
element from
S
sampled uniformly without replacement, and, for any
\varphi:X^{w}\to\mathds{R}
, we use
\mu_{\varphi}(S)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}\sim S^{(w)}}[\varphi({\bm{T}})]
.

Theorem 14

(
Theorem

6
restated)
.

Consider any integers
w\leq m\leq n
, finite population
S\in X^{n}
, and
\varphi:X^{w}\to\mathds{R}
. For any convex
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}\sim\binom{[n]}{m}}\left[f\left(\mu_{\varphi}(S_{\bm{J}})\right)\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{k}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}S^{(w)}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}\varphi({\bm{T}}_{i})\right)\right]

where
k=\lfloor m/w\rfloor
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, and to ease notation, we will assume that
X=[n]
. Otherwise, for
X=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we could consider the kernel
\psi(i_{1},\ldots,i_{w})\coloneqq\varphi(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{w}})
, because the U-statistic with kernel
\varphi
and population
X
has the same distribution as that with kernel
\psi
and population
[n]
.

We draw
{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{k}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}[n]^{(w)}
and note that at most
wk\leq m
unique elements are contained in
{\bm{T}}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup{\bm{T}}_{k}
. Then, we draw
\bm{J}
uniform from
\binom{[n]}{m}
conditioned on all the those unique elements being contained in
\bm{J}
. By symmetry, we observe the following:

1.

Marginally (i.e.
not
conditioning on the values of
{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{k}
), the distribution of
\bm{J}
is uniform over
\binom{[n]}{m}
.

2.

Conditioning on any choice of
\bm{J}=J
, the distribution of
{\bm{T}}_{i}
is uniform over
J^{(w)}
for every
i\in[k]
.

We proceed to bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\mu_{\varphi}(X_{\bm{J}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{J})\right)\right]

(WLOG,
X=[n]
)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}^{\prime}\sim\bm{J}^{(w)}}[\varphi({\bm{T}}^{\prime})]\right)\right]

(Definition of
\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{J})
)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}_{i}\mid\bm{J}}[\varphi({\bm{T}}_{i})]\right)\right]\quad\text{for any $i\in[k]$}

(
{\bm{T}}_{i}\mid\bm{J}
is uniform over
\bm{J}^{(w)}
)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}\mid\bm{J}}\left[\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}\varphi({\bm{T}}_{i})\right]\right)\right]

(Linearity of expectation)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{J}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}\mid\bm{J}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}\varphi({\bm{T}}_{i})\right)\right]\right]

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}}\left[f\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}\varphi({\bm{T}}_{i})\right)\right],

(Law of total expectation)

which, since we drew
{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{k}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}[n]^{(w)}
, is exactly the desired result.

∎

7
From small mutual information to small bias

In this section, we connect the mutual information bound of
Theorem

2
to generalization error, completing the proof of
Theorems

1
,
8
and
9
. Recall our definition of

for a test query.

Definition 7.1

(
Definition

2.9
, restated)
.

For any
\psi:X^{w}\to[0,1]
and distribution
\mathcal{D}
over

, we define

\mathrm{error}(\psi,S,\mathcal{D})\coloneqq\frac{1}{w}\cdot\min\left(\Delta,\frac{\Delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)

Theorem 15

(Mutual information bounds bias)
.

For any
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}
and
\bm{y}\in Y
, as well as
\Psi^{(\bm{y})}
a set of test queries each mapping

chosen as a function of

Our proof will use the following fact.

Fact 7.2

(
[
FS18
]
)
.

For any random variables
\bm{S}\in X
and
{\bm{\psi}}:X\to\mathds{R}\in\Psi
, as well as
\lambda>0
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S},\bm{y}}\left[{\bm{\psi}}(\bm{S})\right]\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(I(\bm{S};{\bm{\psi}})+\sup_{\psi\in\Psi}\ln\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\psi(\bm{S})\right)\right]\right)\right).

Corollary 7.3
.

For any random variables
\bm{x}\in X
and
{\bm{f}}:X\to\mathds{R}\in F
satisfying, for all
t\geq 0
and
f\in F
,
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}}[f(\bm{x})\geq t]\leq e^{-t}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[{\bm{f}}(\bm{x})]\leq 2(I(\bm{x};{\bm{f}})+\ln 2).

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
f\in F
. We first bound the moment-generating function of
f(\bm{x})
.

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\exp(f(\bm{x})/2)]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{{Pr}}[\exp(f(\bm{x})/2)\geq t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{1}^{\infty}\operatorname{{Pr}}[f(\bm{x})\geq 2\ln t]dt

\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}dt=2.

The desired result follows from
7.2
with
\lambda=1/2
.
∎

Bernstein’s inequality
will give the sorts of high probability bounds needed to apply

Fact 7.4

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

For any iid mean-zero random variables
\bm{a}_{1},\ldots,\bm{a}_{n}
with variance
\sigma^{2}
and satisfying, almost surely, that
|\bm{a}_{i}|\leq 1
, let
\bm{A}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\bm{a}_{i}
. Then,

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[|\bm{A}|\geq\Delta\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^{2}n}{2(\sigma^{2}+\frac{\Delta}{3})}\right).

(9)

For our setting, a black-box application of Bernstein’s inequality is not sufficient. We wish to prove concentration of a random variable that is
not
necessarily the sum of iid random variables. Fortunately, the proof of Bernstein’s inequality only uses a bound on the moment generating function of
\bm{A}
: It proceeds by applying Markov’s inequality to the random variable
e^{\lambda\bm{A}}
for an appropriate choice of
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
. As a result, the following also holds.

Fact 7.5

(Generalization of Bernstein’s inequality)
.

Let
\bm{B}
be any random variable satisfying, for all
\lambda\in\mathds{R}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[e^{\lambda\bm{B}}]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[e^{\lambda\bm{A}}],

where
\bm{A}
is as in
7.4
. Then, the bound of
Equation

9
also holds with
\bm{B}
in place of
\bm{A}
.

We’ll use the following to produce a bound in our setting.

Proposition 7.6
.

For any
\psi:X^{w}\to\mathds{R}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
X
, and convex function
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}
, set
k=\lfloor n/w\rfloor

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[f\left(\mu_{\psi}(\bm{S})\right)\right]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{{\bm{T}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{T}}_{k}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{D}^{w}}\left[f\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{i}\sim[k]}\left[\psi({\bm{T}}_{i})]\right]\right)\right].

is “almost” a simple corollary of

Proof.

Finally, we bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[f\left(\mu_{\psi}(\bm{S})\right)\right]

9.1
The statistical-query mechanism: Proof of
Theorem

3

The proof of
Theorem

3
combines two bounds: First, with high probability, all statistical queries asked have low bias, meaning
\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{S})
and
\mu_{\varphi}(\mathcal{D})
are close. Second, with high probability, the answer the mechanism gives when receiving a statistical query
\varphi
is close to
\mu_{\varphi}(\bm{S})
. These two results are combined with the following proposition.

Proposition 9.1

(Triangle-like inequality)
.

For any
\tau>0
and
a,b,c\in[0,1]
satisfying

|b-a|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right)\quad\quad\quad\quad|c-b|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{b(1-b)}\right)

it also holds that

|c-a|\leq 3\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right).

Proof.

First, consider the case where
|c-b|\leq\tau^{2}
. Then,

|c-a|\leq|a-b|+|c-b|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right)+\tau^{2}\leq 2\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}\right).

In the other case,

\displaystyle|c-b|

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sqrt{b(1-b)}

\displaystyle=\tau\sqrt{(a+(b-a))(1-a-(b-a))}

\displaystyle=\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)+(b-a)(1-2a)-(b-a)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)+|b-a|}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}+\tau\sqrt{|b-a|}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}+\frac{\tau^{2}+|b-a|}{2}

(
2xy\leq x^{2}+y^{2}
for any
x,y\in\mathds{R}
)

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}+\frac{2\max(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)})}{2}

(
|b-a|\leq\max(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)})
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\max(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{a(1-a)}).\qed

The desired result follows from the standard triangle inequality.

The difference between
\bm{y}_{t}
and
\varphi_{t}(\bm{S})
is bounded by the following form of Chernoff bounds.

Fact 9.2

(Chernoff bounds)
.

Let
\bm{y}
be the mean of
k
independent random variables each supported on
\{0,1\}
. If

k=O\left(\ln(1/\delta)/\tau^{2}\right),

then, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\bm{y}-p|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{p(1-p)}\right)

where
p\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{y}]

Applying the above and a union bound over the
T
queries, we arrive at the following.

Corollary 9.3
.

In the setting of
Theorem

3
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
t\in[T]
,

|\varphi_{t}(\bm{S})-\bm{y}_{t}|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{\varphi_{t}(\bm{S})(1-\varphi_{t}(\bm{S}))}\right).

We are ready to prove that our SQ mechanism is accurate with high probability. This proof will apply the “monitor” technique of Bassily, Nissim, Smith, Steinke, Stemmer, and Ullman
[
BNS+16
]
to set the test query to the SQ with the “worst” response. To select this worst query, the analyst must have some knowledge of the distribution as they must measure the deviation between
\bm{y}_{t}
and
\mu_{\varphi_{t}}(\mathcal{D})
.

At first glance, this is strange: If the analyst knew
\mathcal{D}
, they would already know
\mu_{\varphi_{t}}(\mathcal{D})
to perfect accuracy and therefore have no need to use our mechanism. The key is that, while we assume the analyst knows
\mathcal{D}
as part of the analysis, we use this to conclude that the mechanism’s responses,
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}
, have good accuracy. Therefore, even if the analyst did not know
\mathcal{D}
, they could use the mechanism’s responses to estimate the answer to their queries. We’ll also mention that, from the perspective of applying
Theorem

10
, we are free to assume the analyst knows
\mathcal{D}
as the guarantee of
Theorem

10
holds for all distributions and analysts.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

After asking the adaptive sequence of queries
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{T}
and receiving responses
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}
, the analyst sets the test query to

\psi\coloneqq\varphi_{t^{\star}}\quad\text{where}\quad t^{\star}\coloneqq\argmax_{t\in[T]}\frac{|\bm{y}_{t}-\mu_{\varphi_{t}}(\mathcal{D})|}{\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\operatorname{{std}}_{\varphi_{t}}(\mathcal{D})\right)}.

It is sufficient to show that
Equation

2
holds for
t=t^{\star}
as, based on how we defined
t^{\star}
, it then holds for all
t\in[T]
.

In order to execute the mechanism in
Figure

1
, the analyst makes a total of
Tk
subsampling queries, one for each vote generated, each with
w=1
and a range
|Y|=|\{0,1\}|=2
. As a consequence of
Theorem

10
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\mathrm{error}(\varphi_{t^{\star}},\bm{S},\mathcal{D})\leq O\left(\ln(1/\delta)\cdot\left(\frac{Tk}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)=O\left(\ln(1/\delta)\cdot\left(\frac{\tau^{2}}{\ln(1/\delta)}\right)\right)=\tau^{2}.

The above implies that

|\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\bm{S})-\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\mathcal{D})|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{\operatorname{{Var}}_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\mathcal{D})}\right)\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\operatorname{{std}}_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}(\mathcal{D})}\right).

We furthermore know by
Corollary

9.3
that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\bm{S})-\bm{y}_{t^{\star}}|\leq\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\sqrt{\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\bm{S})(1-\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\bm{S}))}\right).

Note that it is important that
Corollary

9.3
union bounded over all of
t=1,\ldots,T
because it is therefore guaranteed to apply for
t^{\star}
. We couldn’t have simply bounded the above for
t^{\star}
by directly applying a Chernoff bound to it, as the selection process for
t^{\star}
depends on the values of
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{T}
and is more likely to choose a
\bm{y}_{t}
that deviates greatly from its mean.

Finally, union bounding both events that each occur with probability
1-\delta
and applying
Proposition

9.1
, we have that with probability at least
1-2\delta
,

|\mu_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}}(\mathcal{D})-\bm{y}_{t^{\star}}|\leq 3\max\left(\tau^{2},\tau\operatorname{{std}}_{\varphi_{t^{\star}}(\mathcal{D})}\right).

The desired result follows by renaming
\delta^{\prime}\coloneqq\delta/2
and
\tau^{\prime}\coloneqq\tau/3
.
∎

9.2
The median-finding mechanism: Proof of
Theorem

4

In this section, we prove
Theorem

4
. The first step will be to quantify when a sample
S
is representative of the distribution
\mathcal{D}
for one particular query
\varphi
.

Definition 9.4

(Good and bad samples for a query)
.

For a query
\varphi:X^{w}\to R\subseteq\mathds{R}
and distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
X
, we say that a sample
S\in X^{n}
is
good
for query
\varphi
if

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\varphi(S)}[\bm{z}<r_{\min}]\leq 0.45\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\varphi(S)}[\bm{z}>r_{\max}]\leq 0.45,

where
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
are the smallest and largest approximate medians of
\varphi(\mathcal{D})
respectively. If
S
is not
good
for query
\varphi
, we refer to it as
bad
for query
\varphi
.

The proof of
Theorem

4
is broken into two steps. First, we show that if, for all
t\in[T]
only a small fraction (
\leq 0.02k)
of the groups
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
are bad for
\varphi
, then the mechanism will succeed with high probability. Second, we show that with high probability, only a small fraction of the groups are bad.

Claim 9.5

(If most groups are good, the mechanism is correct with high probability)
.

For any distribution
\mathcal{D}
, groups
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
, and query
\varphi:X^{w}\to R
, let
\bm{y}
be the value output by the mechanism in
Figure

2
. If at most
0.02k
of the groups
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
are bad for
\varphi
, then

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}\text{ is not an approximate median of }\varphi(\mathcal{D})]\leq\log_{2}|R|\cdot e^{-\Omega(k)}.

Proof.

Let
r_{\min}
and
r_{\max}
be the smallest and largest approximate medians of
\varphi(\mathcal{D})
respectively. For
\bm{y}
to not be an approximate median, there must have been some threshold
r
tested during the binary search in which one of the following occurred.

1.

The mechanism determined that
\bm{y}\geq r
when
r>r_{\max}
.

2.

The mechanism determined that
\bm{y}<r
when
r\leq r_{\min}
.

The binary search only tests
\log_{2}|R|
values of
r
, so it suffices to show that for each such test, the probability one of the above two cases happens is at most
e^{-\Omega(k)}
.

Fix any choice of
r
. If
r>r_{\max}
, we wish to argue that it is unlikely the mechanism determines that
\bm{y}\geq r
, or equivalently, it is unlikely that at least
k/2
of the votes
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{k}
are set to
1
. If
i\in[k]
is a good group, using
Definition

9.4
,

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{v}_{i}=1]=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\varphi(S_{i})}[\bm{z}\geq r]\leq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\varphi(S_{i})}[\bm{z}>r_{\max}]\leq 0.45.

For the bad groups, we can pessimistically bound
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{v}_{i}=1]\leq 1
. Then, using the assumption that at most
0.02k
groups are bad, we have that

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{i\in[k]}\bm{v}_{i}\right]\leq 0.98k\cdot 0.45+0.02k\cdot 1\leq 0.47k.

Using the independence of
\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{k}
and a Chernoff bound, the probability at least
k/2
votes are
1
is
e^{-\Omega(k)}
, as desired.

The other case, in which
r\leq r_{\min}
, is symmetric. Here, we need to argue it is unlikely the mechanism determines that
\bm{y}<r
, which means it is unlikely that at least
k/2
votes are
0
. Using the same logic as in the prior case, the expected number of
0
votes is at most
0.47k
and the same Chernoff bound applies.
∎
