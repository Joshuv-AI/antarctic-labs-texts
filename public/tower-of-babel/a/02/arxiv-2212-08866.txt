Geometric decomposition of flows generated by rough path differential equations
Pedro Catuogno Lourival Lima Paulo Ruffino

Abstract
Whenever an Itô-Wentsel type of formula holds for composition of flows of a certain differential dynamics, there exists locally a decomposition of the corresponding flow according to complementary distributions (or foliations, in the case of integrability of these distributions). Many examples have been proved in distinct context of dynamics: Stratonovich stochastic equations, Lévy driven noise, low regularity α \alpha -Hölder control functions ( α ∈ ( 1 / 2 , 1 ] \alpha\in(1/2,1] ), see e.g. [ 6 ] , [ 7 ] , [ 21 ] , [ 20 ] . Here we present the proof of this categorical property: we illustrate with the α \alpha -Hölder rough path, α ∈ ( 1 / 3 , 1 / 2 ] \alpha\in(1/3,1/2] using the Itô-Wentsel formula in this context proved in [ 5 ] . Different from the previous approaches, here however, instead of using an intrinsic rough path calculus on manifolds, the manifold has to be embedded in an Euclidean space. A cascade decomposition is also shown when we have multiple lower dimensional directions which span the whole space. As application, the linear case is treated in details: the cascade decomposition provides a row factorization of all matrices which allow real logarithm.



Key words:
flow of diffeomorphisms generated by rough path, decomposition of flows, complementary distributions, complementary foliations.

60L20, 37H05 37C10 (60L90).

Rough path integration and Itô-Wentzel Formula

In this section, we recall basic definitions and properties of rough path theory, essentially only the necessary to develop our theory of decomposition of flows, so that, for details and further equally interesting properties we recommend, among many others,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
1
]
and references therein. The crucial property we are going to use in order to obtain the decompostion of rough generated flows is the Itô-Wentzel type formula in this context. Another simplification in this rather short and direct overview on the preliminaries of the theory is the fact that all linear spaces involved here shall be Euclidean spaces. In fact, these finite dimensional spaces are enough to perform our decomposition via embedding differentiable manifolds in a sufficiently large dimensional space.

The interval of time we are considering here is always
[0,T]
, with
0<T<\infty
. The parameter
\alpha
is always in
(1/3,1/2]
and the domain of the tensor product component is the ‘simplex’ given by
\Delta_{T}:=\{(s,t):0\leq s\leq t\leq T\}
.

Definition 2.1

An
\alpha
-Hölder rough path is a pair of functions
\textbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
, such that
X:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous and
\mathbb{X}:\Delta_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
2\alpha
-Hölder continuous path, such that the following (Chen) relation holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{st}=\mathbb{X}_{su}+\mathbb{X}_{ut}+X_{su}\otimes X_{ut},

(1)

for all
0\leq s\leq u\leq t
, where
X_{st}=X_{t}-X_{s}
.

The motivating example for the theory is lift of the canonical Brownian motion via the Lévy area, i.e.
\textbf{B}=(B,\mathbb{B})
where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and

\mathbb{B}_{st}:=\int_{s}^{t}B_{sr}\otimes dB_{r}:=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{B}^{ij}_{st}\ e_{i}\otimes e_{j}

where
\{e_{i}:1,\ldots,d\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\mathbb{B}^{ij}_{st}=\int_{s}^{t}B_{sr}^{i}\ dB^{j}_{r}.

(2)

We denote the transpose in the tensor product
\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
(a\otimes b)^{*}=(b\otimes a)
. Among the space of rough paths with first coordinate
X;[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we are interested in those which satisfy the following:

Definition 2.2

Let
\textbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
, be a rough path. We say that
X
is geometric if:

X_{st}\otimes X_{st}=2\ \mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{X}_{st}),

where,
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathbb{X}_{st}):=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{X}_{st}+\mathbb{X}_{st}^{*})
is the symmetric part of
\mathbb{X}
.

For example, the rough path given by the lift of the Brownian motion
\textbf{B}=(B,\mathbb{B})
defined above is geometric if the integral of equation (
2
) is taken in the sense of Stratonovich, see e.g.
[
10
]
. The importance of the next definition is that
controlled paths
are the natural integrands with respect to rough paths:

Definition 2.3

Fix
X:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous path. Let
Y:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and
Y^{\prime}:[0,T]\rightarrow L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
be also
\alpha
-Hölder paths. A pair
(Y,Y^{\prime})
is called a controlled path with respect to
X
if

(s,t)\in\Delta_{T}\longmapsto R^{Y}_{st}:=Y_{st}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\,X_{st}

is
2\alpha
-Hölder continuous. The path
Y^{\prime}
is called a Gubinelli derivative of
Y
with respect to
X
.

The prototype of a controlled path with respect to
X
is given by
Y=F(X)
, where
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow L(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
is a differentiable function and
Y^{\prime}=DF(X)
.
Next Theorem gives us the definition and the main property of the rough path integral:

Theorem 2.4

Let
\textbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
be an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
(Y,Y^{\prime})
be a controlled path with respect to
X
, with values in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\cong L(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Then the following limit

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}\ d\mathbf{X}_{r}:=\lim_{|\pi|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\pi}\Big(Y_{u}\,X_{uv}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\ \mathbb{X}_{uv}\Big)

exists, where
\pi
are partitions of
[s,t]
and the limit is taken when the mesh of
\pi
goes to zero.

This limit is called the rough integral of the controlled path
(Y,Y^{\prime})
with respect to the rough path
\mathbf{X}
. Mind that
Y^{\prime}
has values in
L(\mathbb{R}^{d};L(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}))\cong L(\mathbb{R}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
. An important property of this rough integration is the following estimate for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
,

\Big|\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}dX_{r}-Y_{s}X_{st}-Y^{\prime}_{s}\mathbb{X}_{st}\Big|\leq C\Big\{\big|{R}^{Y}\big|_{2\alpha}\,\big|X\big|_{\alpha}+\big|Y^{\prime}\big|_{\alpha}\big|\mathbb{X}\big|_{2\alpha}\Big\}|t-s|^{3\alpha},

where
C
is a constant which depends only on
\alpha
, and the norms on the right hand side are the standard
\alpha
-Hölder norms, see e.g.
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
. The key point in the link between stochastic analysis and rough path theory is the following: if
(Y,Y^{\prime})
is an adapted stochastic process which is
\alpha
-Hölder controlled with respect to
B
then

\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,d\mathbf{B}_{s}=\int_{0}^{T}Y_{s}\,dB_{s},

where the integral on the right hand side is Itô (Stratonovich) integration if in equation (
2
) we have Itô (Stratonovich, respectively) integral. See e.g.
[
10
, Prop. 5.1 and Cor. 5.2]
.

The integration of controlled path with respect to another controlled path is a fundamental tool in the rough path calculus. Let
(Y,Y^{\prime})
and
(Z,Z^{\prime})
be two controlled path with respect to a rough path
X
. Then the limit

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}Y_{r}\ dZ_{r}:=\lim_{|\pi|\to 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\pi}\Big(Y_{u}\,Z_{uv}+Y^{\prime}_{u}\ Z^{\prime}_{u}\mathbb{X}_{uv}\Big),

exists, where
\pi
are partitions of
[s,t]
and the limit is taken when the mesh of
\pi
goes to zero. Mind that here we have that
Y
is a controlled path with values in
L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
and
Z
has values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

In this article we consider the flow of local diffeomorphisms of the dynamics generated by a rough differential equation (RDE). More precisely, let
\mathbf{X}
be an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow L(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m})
be a differentiable map (cf.
d
vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
). Consider the RDE given by:

dx_{t}=F(x_{t})\ d\mathbf{X},

(3)

where the solution is taken in the sense of the integration above. We denote by
\varphi_{t}
the solution flow of (local) diffeomorphisms. For existence and uniqueness of solution given and initial conditions and existence of flow of (local) diffeomorphisms see e.g.
[
1
]
,
[
10
, Chap. 8]
. We remark that via the link between stochastic and rough path theory, the calculation of the solutions in both systems are analogous, i.e. if
y_{t}
satisfies

y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(y_{s})\,d\mathbf{B}_{s}

for each
\omega
(a.s.) then

y_{t}=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}F(y_{s})\,dB_{s},

where the integration above can be either in Itô or Stratonovich sense. And reciprocally, see e.g.
[
10
, Thm 9.1]
.

We finish this section with the generalized Itô formula and the corollary we are going to use in the next section:

Theorem 2.5
(Itô-Wentzel formula)

Let
\textbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
be an
\alpha
-Hölder geometric rough path.

Let

x\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\longmapsto(h(\cdot,x),h^{\prime}(\cdot,x))
be a continuous family of controlled rough path with respect to
X
with values in
L(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{m})
. We assume that

1.

h(\cdot,x)
is continuous and twice differentiable in
x
.

2.

D_{x}h(\cdot,x)
is continuous and differentiable in
x
.

3.

For each
t\in[0,T]
,
h^{\prime}(t,x)
has continuous derivative in
x
.

4.

x\mapsto(D_{x}h(\cdot,x),(D_{x}h)^{\prime}(\cdot,x))
is a continuous family of controlled rough path with values in
L(\mathbb{R}^{\ell};L(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}))
.

Let

g(t,x)=g(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}h(s,x)d\mathbf{X}_{s}.

Assume that
g:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is continuous and twice differentiable in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and the functions
(t,x)\mapsto Dg(t,x)
and
(t,x)\mapsto D^{2}g(t,x)
are continuous.
Then for
(Z,Z^{\prime})
a controlled rough path w.r.t.
X
we have that:

\displaystyle g(t,Z_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(0,Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}h(r,Z_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}+\int_{0}^{t}Dg(r,Z_{r})dZ_{r}

(4)

Cf. Kunita’s Itô generalized formula in
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
. For a proof in the rough path context, see
[
5
]
. Two direct corollaries come from this generalized Itô formula. The first one is the formula for changing coordinates (classical Itô formula):

Corollary 2.6
(Change of coordinates)

For geometric rough path and the same notations of Theorem
2.5
. If
h\equiv 0
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a differentiable function then

g(Z_{t})=g(Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}Dg(Z_{r})\,dZ_{r}

In particular is
Z_{t}
is a solution of equation (
3
), then

g(Z_{t})=g(Z_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}DgF(Z_{r})\,d\mathbf{X}_{r}.

Moreover, if
g
is a diffeomorphism, then it is a conjugacy between
Z_{t}
and
g(Z_{t})
.

Proof:
It is straightforward.

The second corollary is crucial for the decomposition of flows which appears in the next Section:

Corollary 2.7

Let
Y
and
Z
be the flows associated with the RDE driven by a geometric
\alpha
-Hölder rough path
\mathbf{X}
:

dY=G(Y)d\mathbf{X}~\ \ \ \ \mbox{ and }~\ \ \ \ dZ=H(Z)d\mathbf{X}

respectively.
Then the composition of the flows
V=Y\circ Z
satisfies the RDE:

dV=G(V)d\mathbf{X}+Y_{*}H(V)d\mathbf{X}.

Proof:
We apply the Itô-Wentzell formula (
4
).
By assumption,

Y_{t}(x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}G(Y_{s}(x))d\mathbf{X}_{s}.

Applying the Itô-Wentzell formula, the substitution rule and the expression for the Gubinelli derivative of the solution of a rough differential equation we obtain

\displaystyle V_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+\int_{0}^{t}G(Y_{r}(Z_{r}))d\mathbf{X}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}D_{x}Y_{r}(Z_{r})dZ_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+\int_{0}^{t}G(V_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}+\int_{0}^{t}D_{x}Y_{r}(Z_{r})H(Z_{r})d\mathbf{X}_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+\int_{0}^{t}(G(V_{r})+(Y_{r})_{*}H(V_{r}))d\mathbf{X}_{r}.

\Box

3
Decomposition of Flows according to complementary distributions

Let
M
be a (compact) connected
m
-dimensional differentiable manifold. Like all the others geometrical objects, differentiability here means smoothness.
To fit the rough path theory presented in the previous sections into rough dynamics in
M
, we shall, firstly, consider
M
as a somehow nice subset (surface) of an Euclidean space. Indeed, there always exists an immersion
i:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
for
N
large enough: either applying the classical Whitney theorem in general, or Nash isometric theorem, if
M
is Riemannian. We shall freely identify
M
with
i(M)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, its image by the immersion.

A version of the support theorem holds for RDE in the following sense. In equation (
3
) if the image
\mathrm{Im}\{F(x)\}\subseteq T_{x}M
for all
x\in M
, then the solution flow

\varphi_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
preserves
M
, i.e. the solutions starting in
M
lies in
M
for all lifetime of the solution, yet:
\varphi_{t}(x)\in M
for all
x\in M
and
t<T\wedge\tau
, where
\tau
is the explosion time of the solution. In fact, this is not difficult to prove. Given an initial conditions
x_{0}\in M
let
s_{0}:=\sup\{0\leq s\leq T\wedge\tau:\varphi_{t}(x_{0})\in M\}
. One just have to prove that the interval
[0,s_{0}]
is an open set in
[0,T\wedge\tau]
. Take a local coordinate around
\varphi_{s_{0}}(x_{0})\in M
such that locally
M
is mapped horizontally in
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-m}
. In a neighbouhood of
\varphi_{s_{0}}(x_{0})
, the vertical component in
\mathbb{R}^{N-d}
of the integrand function
F
vanishes. Hence, also in a neighborhood of
s_{0}
, with respect to this coordinates, the solution
\varphi_{s}(x_{0})
is also horizontal, i.e.
\varphi_{s}(x_{0})\in M
. Hence
s_{0}=T\wedge\tau
, i.e. the solution stays always on
M
.

Consider a geometric rough path
\mathbf{X}=(X,\mathbb{X})
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
F:M\rightarrow L(\mathbb{R}^{d},TM)
be a differentiable mapping (‘
d
vector fields’) such that
F(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow T_{x}M
for all
x\in M
. A RDE on
M
written as

dx_{t}=F(x_{t})\ d\mathbf{X},

(5)

is interpreted as a RDE in the Euclidean space where
M
is immersed and
F
extended smoothly in a neighbourhood of
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The existence of flow in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the support theorem in the rough context allow us to talk about a flow
\varphi_{t}
of local diffeomorphisms in
M
. In the case of a non-autonomous
F(t,x_{t})
, the solution flow is denoted by
\varphi_{s,t}
.

Let
\mbox{Diff}(M)
be the infinite dimensional Lie group of smooth diffeomorphisms of
M
generated by flows of smooth vector fields on
M
(understood as extended smoothly vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
). The Lie algebra associated to
\mbox{Diff}(M)
is the infinite dimensional space of smooth vector fields on
M
, see e.g. Neeb
[
22
]
, Omori
[
24
]
, among others. In this Lie group, the exponential map
\exp\{tY\}\in\mbox{Diff}(M)
is the associated flow of global diffeomorphisms generated by the smooth vector field
Y
. In this context, given an element
\varphi\in\mbox{Diff}(M)
the derivative of the right translation is given by
R_{\varphi*}Y=Y(\varphi)
for any smooth vector
Y
; the derivative of left translation
L_{\varphi*}Y=D\varphi(Y)
, and the adjoint operator is given by
\mathrm{Ad}(\varphi)Y=\varphi_{*}(Y(\varphi^{-1}))
.

This Lie group structure is convenient for obtaining the equations of each component of the decomposition we are interested in. In particular, a solution flow
\varphi_{t}
of the RDE (
5
) can be written as the solution of a right invariant RDE in the Lie group of diffeomorphisms
\mbox{Diff}(M)
:

d\varphi_{t}=R_{\varphi_{t*}}Y\,d\textbf{X}_{r}.

(6)

Interesting problems arise when one decomposes a (flow of) diffeomorphism
\varphi\in\mbox{Diff}(M)
, into composition of conveniently prescribed components, in the sense that each component provides a geometrical or dynamical information. This kind of decomposition appears in the literature, for example, in Bismut
[
4
]
, Kunita
[
14
]
and many others. In particular, it is also relevant when each component of the decomposition belongs to prescribed subgroups of
\mbox{Diff}(M)
, see e.g Melo et al
[
21
]
, Catuogno et al
[
7
]
, Iwasawa and non-linear Iwasawa decomposition
[
8
]
, Ming Liao
[
18
]
among many others.

In this section, we explore in the context of rough path calculus the existence of a geometrical decomposition as treated in
[
7
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
6
]
, i.e. in the following sense: Suppose that locally
M
is endowed with a pair of differentiable distributions: i.e., every point
x\in M
has a neighbourhood
U
and differentiable mappings
\Delta^{1}:U\rightarrow Gr_{k}(M)
and
\Delta^{2}:U\rightarrow Gr_{{m-k}}(M)
respectively, where

Gr_{p}(M)=\bigcup_{x\in M}Gr_{p}(T_{x}M)

is the Grasmannian bundle of
p
-dimensional subspaces over
M
, with
1\leq p\leq m
. We assume that
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
are complementary in the sense that
\displaystyle{\Delta^{1}(x)\oplus\Delta^{2}(x)=T_{x}M}
, for all
x\in U
. With this notation we define the subgroup of
\mbox{Diff}(M)
which is generated by a certain distribution
\Delta
by:

\mbox{Diff}(\Delta,M)=\mbox{cl}\bigl\{\exp(t_{1}X_{1})\ldots\exp(t_{n}X_{n}),\mbox{ with }X_{i}\in\Delta,t_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}.

Note that if a distribution
\Delta
is involutive, then each element of the group
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta,M)
preserves the leaves of the corresponding foliation.

Extending the distributions to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
We freely treat these complementary distribution on
M
as intrinsic to
M
since the complementary distributions
\Delta^{1}\oplus\Delta^{2}=T_{x}M
has locally differentiable extensions to neighbourhoods of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
: in fact, if
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
are l.i smooth vector fields which span
\Delta^{1}
, then, there exists locally a neighborhood in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
where their extensions span to an extended distribution of
\Delta^{1}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Here, we are abusing notation when writing the distributions on
M
and in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the same symbol. With this construction, locally the extended
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
will also be transversal. One only has to increase the dimension of
\Delta^{2}
, say, by the span of vector fields transversal to
M
to get completeness in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, i.e.
\Delta^{1}\oplus\Delta^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
. When necessary to distinguish the
(N-k)
-dimensional
\Delta^{2}
extended distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the original
(m-k)
-dimensional
\Delta^{2}
which is only defined on
M
, the last one will be denoted by
\Delta^{2}_{M}
.

In particular, in this Section we focus on the subgroups
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
and
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{2},M)
. The main result of this paper (Theorem
3.2
) establishes a local decomposition of the solution flow
\varphi_{t}
of equation (
5
) into two components: a curve (solution of an autonomous RDE) in
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
composed with a solutions of a non-autonomous RDE in
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{2},M)
.

Definition 3.1

A diffeomorphism
\eta\in\mbox{Diff}(M)
preserves transversality of
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
in a neighbourhood
U\subset M
if
\eta_{*}\Delta^{2}\left(\eta^{-1}(x)\right)\cap\Delta^{1}(x)=\{0\}
, for all
x\in U
.

By continuity, given any pair of complementary distributions and any point
p\in M
, there always exists a neighbourhood of the identity
Id\in\mbox{Diff}(M)
and a neighbourhood
U
of
p
where all diffeomorphisms in this neighbourhood preserve transversality. Moreover, if the distribution
\Delta^{1}
is involutive then all elements in
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
preserves tranversality of
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
: in fact, the derivative
\eta_{*}
above is a linear isomorphism which sends tangent spaces of the associated foliation to tangent spaces of the same leaf.

Next theorem states the main result of this section:

Theorem 3.2
(Decomposition of flows of RDE)

Locally, up to an explosion time
\tau\in[0,T]
, the solution flow
\varphi_{t}
of the RDE (
5
) is decomposable as

\varphi_{t}=\eta_{t}\circ\psi_{t},

where
\eta_{t}
is solution of an (autonomous) RDE in the Lie group
\emph{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
and
\psi_{t}
is
solution of a non-autonomous RDE in
\emph{Diff}(\Delta^{2},M)
.

Proof:

Given
p\in M
, initially extend the distributions
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
to a neighbourhood
U
of
p
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
as described above.
Take
\eta\in\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},\mathbb{R}^{N})
sufficiently close to the identity such that
\eta
preserves tranversality in
U
, i.e.
\mathrm{Ad}(\eta)\Delta^{2}
and
\Delta^{1}
are complementary. Mind that
\Delta^{1}
might not be involutive, but at points on
M
, the Lie bracket
[\Delta^{1},\Delta^{1}]\subseteq\Delta^{1}\oplus\Delta^{2}
. It means that diffeomrophisms in
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},\mathbb{R}^{N})
restricts to (local) diffeomorphisms on
M
, i.e. in another notation
\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},\mathbb{R}^{N})|_{M}=\mbox{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
.

The integrand mapping
F
of the RDE (
5
), after being extended to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, can be decomposed uniquely as

\displaystyle F(x)=h(x)+V(\eta,x),

(7)

where
h(x)\in\Delta^{1}(x)
and
V(\eta,x)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\eta)\Delta^{2}(x)
, for all
x\in U
. Note that for points
x\in M
,
V(\eta,x)\in\Delta^{2}|_{M}
, since
F
itself lives in
\Delta^{1}\oplus\Delta^{2}|_{M}
and
\eta_{*}
preserves the tangent bundle
TM
. The first component of the decomposition in the statement of the theorem,
\eta_{t}
is taken as the solution of the following RDE in the group
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)
:

\displaystyle d\eta_{t}=R_{\eta_{t*}}\,h\,d\mathbf{X}_{t},

(8)

with initial condition
\eta_{0}=Id
, the identity.
Even though the equation above is described in terms of a right translation, it is not a right invariant equation since
h
in general depends on
\eta_{t}
. The second component of the decomposition of
\varphi_{t}
is obtained using that
\psi_{t}=\eta^{-1}_{t}\circ\varphi_{t}
. Since the families of diffeomorphisms
\varphi_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
preserves
M
(i.e. are diffeomorphisms in
M
when restrict to this space), then
\psi_{t}
also preserves
M
. Applying Corollary
2.7
, it follows that:

\displaystyle d\eta^{-1}_{t}=-L_{\eta^{-1}_{t*}}\,h\,d\mathbf{X}_{t},

where
L_{\eta^{-1}_{t*}}
is the derivative of the left translation at the identity by
\eta^{-1}_{t}
. Finally, we find a equation for
\psi_{t}
applying Corollary
2.7
again:

\displaystyle d\psi_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\eta^{-1}_{t}h\,\eta_{t}\,\psi_{t}-\eta^{-1}_{t}X\,\eta_{t}\,\psi_{t})\,d\mathbf{X}_{t}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}(\eta^{-1}_{t})V(\eta_{t})\ d\mathbf{X}_{t}.

Note that
V(\eta,\cdot)
does not necessarily belong to
\Delta^{2}
, but rather
d\psi_{t}\in\Delta^{2}
since
d\psi_{t}\in\mathrm{Ad}(\eta^{-1})\mathrm{Ad}(\eta)\Delta^{2}=\Delta^{2}
. Then
\psi_{t}
is the
\Delta^{2}
-component of the original flow
\varphi_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The intrinsic result for flows on
M
itself is obtained by restricting the (flow of) diffeomorphisms to
M
, since both
\eta_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
preserve
M
.

\Box

Corollary 3.3

If the distributions
\Delta^{1}
and
\Delta^{2}
are integrable, then the decomposition of Theorem
3.2
is unique.

Proof:
In fact, in this case
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1},M)\cap\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2},M)=\{Id\}
.

\Box

4
Cascade decomposition and the linear case

When the state space
M
is endowed with many compatible pairs of complementary distributions in its tangent bundle (in a sense which will be described below) Theorem
3.2
can be applied recursively to obtain further geometrical applications of the decomposition. In this Section we deal with this case to obtain a sequence of dynamically meaningful corollaries. In particular, we calculate explicitly the decomposition established in these corollaries for the linear RDE. We prove in particular that the main geometrical obstruction for the decomposition, namely the explosion time, does not appear in this case if one chooses appropriate linear (affine) foliations.

We start with the assumption that the base manifold
M
has more than one pair of complementary distributions or foliations. More than that, initially, we assume that there exists a pair of
complementary flag bundles
in the following sense: There exists a sequence of complementary non trivial distributions, i.e. for all
x\in M
we have
\Delta^{1}_{1}(x)\oplus\Delta^{2}_{1}(x)=\Delta^{1}_{2}\oplus\Delta^{2}_{2}=\ldots=\Delta^{1}_{k}\oplus\Delta^{2}_{k}=T_{x}M
, such that the first component is an increasing sequence of tangent subspaces
\Delta^{1}_{1}\subset\Delta^{1}_{2}\ldots\subset\Delta_{k}^{1}\subset T_{x}M
and the second component is a decreasing sequence of subspaces
T_{x}M\supset\Delta^{2}_{1}\supset\Delta^{2}_{2}\ldots\supset\Delta_{k}^{2}
.

This structure of nested distribution
F^{1}=(\Delta^{1}_{1},\ldots,\Delta^{1}_{k})
and
F^{2}=(\Delta^{2}_{k},\ldots,\Delta^{2}_{1})
are called
flag bundle
over the manifold
M
. Each fibre is a flag manifold, i.e. a homogeneous space
Sl(n,\mathbb{R})/P
, where
P
is a parabolic subgroup, see e.g. the classical book
[
12
]
or more recent applications in dynamics in the survey
[
25
]
. If
\dim\Delta^{1}_{i}-\dim\Delta^{1}_{i-1}=1
for all
i=2,\ldots,k
then each fibre is called a
maximal flag manifold
, in this case
k=m-1
, where
m
is the dimension of the manifold
M
. Our first corollary establishes a cascade of decomposition in this geometrical context. A version of this result has already appeared in
[
7
]
in the context of stochastic systems. We include it here again, in rough path context with a simpler proof.

Corollary 4.1

Given complementary flag bundles
F^{1}=(\Delta^{1}_{1},\ldots,\Delta^{1}_{k})
and
F^{2}=(\Delta^{2}_{k},\ldots,\Delta^{2}_{1})
there exists (locally) a decomposition of the original flow, up to an explosion time:

\varphi_{t}=\eta^{1}_{t}\circ\ldots\eta^{i}_{t}\circ\eta^{i+1}_{t}\circ\ldots\eta^{k}_{t}\circ\psi_{t},

(10)

with the property that for all
i=1,\ldots k
we have that the first
i
-th components

(\eta^{1}_{t}\circ\ldots\eta^{i}_{t})
solves a rough differential equation in the Lie group
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})
and the last
(k-i+1)
components
(\eta^{i+1}_{t}\circ\ldots\eta^{k}_{t}\circ\psi_{t})
solves a (non autonomous) rough equation in the Lie group
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i})
, with the convention that if
i=k
then
\eta^{i+1}_{t}=Id
.

The decomposition is unique if the distributions of the flag bundles are all involutive.

Proof:

By Theorem
3.2
, for each pair of complementary distributions
\Delta^{1}_{i}\oplus\Delta^{2}_{i}
there exists a corresponding decomposition

\varphi_{t}=\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i}\circ\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{i},

with
\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})
and
\widetilde{\psi_{t}}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i})
, for
i=1,\ldots k
.

For the first and the last elements in the right hand side of formula (
10
) of the statement take
\eta_{t}^{1}=\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{1}
and
\psi_{t}=\widetilde{\psi_{t}}^{k}
. And for
i=2,\ldots k
, define

\eta^{i}_{t}:=(\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i-1})^{-1}\circ\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i}.

The result follows immediately by the properties of the decomposition established by Theorem
3.2
and telescope cancellation.

\Box

A particular situation of complementary flag bundles occurs if the subspaces of the flags are both prescribed by a common sequence of distributions. Namely, suppose we have a sequence of distribution
\Delta_{1},\ldots\Delta_{k}
such that for each
x\in M
we have
that
\Delta_{1}\oplus\ldots\Delta_{k}=T_{x}M
.
In this case, there exists a special choice of complementary flag bundles
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
F_{1}
is given by subspaces
\Delta^{1}_{i}=\Delta_{1}\oplus\ldots\Delta_{i}
and
F_{2}
is constructed with the subspaces
\Delta^{2}_{i}=\Delta_{i+1}\oplus\ldots\Delta_{k}
, for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
.. In this case the last component
\psi_{t}
in equation (
10
) disappears and the decomposition assumes the following form:

Corollary 4.2

Consider a sequence of
k
complementary nondegenerate distributions in a manifold
M
, i.e.
\Delta_{1}\oplus\ldots\Delta_{k}=T_{x}M
for all
x\in M
. Then a flow generated by the RDE (
5
) can be locally decomposed, up to an explosion time as:

\varphi_{t}=\xi^{1}_{t}\circ\ldots\xi^{i}_{t}\circ\xi^{i+1}_{t}\circ\ldots\xi^{k}_{t},

such that for each
i=1,\ldots k
we have that the first
i
-th components

(\xi^{1}_{t}\circ\ldots\xi^{i}_{t})
solves a rough differential equation in the Lie group
\displaystyle{\mathrm{Diff}(\oplus_{j=1}^{i}\Delta_{j})}
and the last
(k-i)
components
(\xi^{i+1}_{t}\circ\ldots\xi^{k}_{t})
solves a (non autonomous) rough equation in the Lie group
\displaystyle{\mathrm{Diff}(\oplus_{j=i+1}^{k}\Delta_{j})}
.

Proof:
It follows directly from Corollary
4.1
: just define the flags
F^{1}
and
F^{2}
by the following increasing/decreasing non trivial subspaces:

\Delta^{1}_{i}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{i}\Delta_{j}

and

\Delta^{2}_{i}=\bigoplus_{j=i+1}^{k}\Delta_{j}.

Mind that, here, the flags
F^{1}
and
F^{2}

are, each one, determined by
(k-1)
non trivial subspaces.

\Box

In general we have that the subgroups of diffeomorphisms
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})\subseteq\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i+1})
and
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i+1})\supseteq\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i})
for all
i=1,\ldots,(k-1)
. In the case of flags with only involutive distributions, these inclusions are strict, which allows a more refined result:

Corollary 4.3

Consider a sequence of
k
complementary involutive distributions (foliations) in a manifold
M
, i.e. the tangent subspace of the
k
foliations
\Delta_{1}\oplus\ldots\Delta_{k}=T_{x}M
for all
x\in M
. Then the flow generated by the RDE (
5
) can be locally decomposed, up to an explosion time, as:

\varphi_{t}=\xi^{1}_{t}\circ\ldots\xi^{k}_{t},

(11)

such that for each
i=1,\ldots k
we have that

\xi^{i}_{t}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta_{i})
. The decomposition is unique.

Proof:

Consider the same flags generated by subspaces
\Delta^{1}_{i}
and
\Delta^{2}_{i}
as in Corollary
4.2
above. Again, by Theorem
3.2
, for each pair of complementary distributions
\Delta^{1}_{i}\oplus\Delta^{2}_{i}
, with
i=1,\ldots,k
, there exists a corresponding decomposition of the same original flow of equation (
5
), that is:

\varphi_{t}=\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{1}\circ\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{1}=\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{2}\circ\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{2}=\ldots\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{k}\circ\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{k}.

(12)

with
\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})
and
\widetilde{\psi_{t}}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i})
, for
i=1,\ldots k
.

The first and the last components in the equation (
11
) of the statement are taken as
\xi_{t}^{1}=\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{1}
and
\xi^{k}_{t}=\widetilde{\psi_{t}}^{k}
. Obviously
\xi^{1}_{t}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta_{1})
and
\xi^{k}_{t}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta_{k})
. For
i=2,\ldots k
, define

\xi^{i}_{t}:=(\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i-1})^{-1}\circ\widetilde{\eta_{t}}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i}).

Equation (
12
) says that it is also equivalent to define
\xi^{i}_{t}=(\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{i})^{-1}\circ\widetilde{\Psi_{t}}^{i-1}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i-1})
. One concludes that
\xi^{i}_{t}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})\cap\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i-1})
. Since all the distributions are associated to foliations, diffeomorphisms in the intersection
\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{1}_{i})\cap\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta^{2}_{i-1})
can only be generated by flows associated to vector fields on the foliation associated to the involutive distribution
\Delta^{1}_{i}\cap\Delta^{2}_{i-1}=\Delta_{i}
, which implies that
\xi_{t}^{i}\in\mathrm{Diff}(\Delta_{i})
.

\Box

Example 1:
In general, let
\phi=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{m}):U\subset M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a local coordinate of
M
. Then
\phi
establishes in
U
, an open set,
m
1-dimensional complementary foliations and corresponding distributions which fulfill the geometrical hypothesis of the previous corollaries. Hence, given the flow of (local)
diffeomorphisms
\varphi_{t}
, up to an explosion time, we can decompose locally

\varphi_{t}=\xi^{1}_{t}\circ\xi_{t}^{2}\ldots\circ\xi_{t}^{m}

where each diffeomorphism
\xi^{i}_{t}

preserves the
j
-th coordinates for all
j\neq i
. The
composition
(\xi^{1}_{t}\circ\xi_{t}^{2}\ldots\circ\xi^{i})

is a solution of a rough differential equation in the groups of diffeomorphisms which preserves
coordinates
\phi_{i+1},\phi_{i+2},\ldots,\phi_{m}
. On the other hand,
the composition
(\xi^{i+1}_{t}\circ\ldots\circ\xi^{m})

preserves coordinates
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{i}
. The decomposition is unique.

\Box

4.1
Linear case

Consider the case of an autonomous linear RDE in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

d\,x(t)=A\,x(t)\ d\mathbf{X}_{t},

(13)

where
A
is an
m\times m
real matrix. Let
\varphi_{t}
be the associated linear flow in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. As for the geometrical structure, let
F_{1}\oplus F_{2}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
be two complementary vector subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. By translations, they generate the complementary affine foliations

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}_{1}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}F_{1}+x}\ \ \ \ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \ \ \ \ \mathcal{F}_{2}=\bigcup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}F_{2}+x.

In this Section these foliations are the focus of our attention. For the Cartesian decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, with
\ell=(m-k)
, the corresponding affine foliations generated by
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
are called the the
Cartesian foliation
. The concern about pair of affine foliations for linear systems comes from the following:

Lemma 4.4

Complementary foliations
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
are affine if and only if for all linear flow
\varphi_{t}
, the local decomposition of Theorem
3.2

\varphi_{t}=\eta_{t}\circ\psi_{t}

is such that the components
\eta_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
are also linear. In this case, the domain of the decomposition is
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof:
Since any pair of complementary affine foliation can be obtained by changing bases of the Euclidean space, without lost of generality we can prove only the case of Cartesian foliations
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We have to prove that
\eta_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
are linear. In fact, in this case, we write the linear solution flow as

\varphi_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(F_{1}(t)\Big)_{k\times k}&\Big(F_{2}(t)\Big)_{k\times\ell}\\
&\\
\Big(F_{3}(t)\Big)_{\ell\times k}&\Big(F_{4}(t)\Big)_{\ell\times\ell}\end{array}\right).

Since
\eta_{t}
can not change the last
\ell
coordinates, and
\psi_{t}
can not change the first
k
coordinates, necessarily
\psi_{t}
is linear and invertible (for
t
close enough to zero) of the form:

\psi_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(Id\Big)_{k\times k}&\Big(0\Big)_{k\times\ell}\\
&\\
\Big(F_{3}(t)\Big)_{\ell\times k}&\Big(F_{4}(t)\Big)_{\ell\times\ell}\end{array}\right).

(14)

Which implies that
\eta_{t}=\varphi_{t}\circ\psi_{t}^{-1}
is also linear. Conversely, suppose that
\eta_{t}
and
\psi_{t}
are both linear. Then
\psi_{t}
must have the form (
14
) and similarly, the first component must have the form:

\eta_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(G_{1}(t)\Big)_{k\times k}&\Big(G_{2}(t)\Big)_{k\times\ell}\\
&\\
\Big(0\Big)_{\ell\times k}&\Big(Id\Big)_{\ell\times\ell}\end{array}\right).

(15)

We have to prove that
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
are affine foliations. In fact, for each point
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, we prove that the leaves of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
are affine subspaces passing through
(x_{1},x_{2})
, each of them parallel to
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\{0\}
and
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
respectively. Assume initially that
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
is non zero. Write the matrix of coefficients as

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(A_{1}\Big)_{k\times k}&\Big(A_{2}\Big)\\
\Big(A_{3}\Big)&\Big(A_{4}\Big)_{\ell\times\ell}\end{array}\right)

(16)

Take zero entries for
A_{1},A_{2}
and
A_{3}
. For an
\alpha
-Hölder rough path
X:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the action of the second component is reduced to
\psi_{t}(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1},\exp(X_{t}\,A_{4})x_{2})
which, by definition, carries
(x_{1},x_{2})
along the foliation
\mathcal{F}_{2}
. Since
X_{t}
,
A_{4}
are arbitrary and
x_{2}\neq 0
then
\exp(X_{t}\,A_{4})x_{2}
can reach any point in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
at finite time. This proves that the leaves of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
passing through any
(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{2}\neq 0
is the affine subspace generated by
\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. The similar argument shows that the leaves of
\mathcal{F}_{1}
passing through any
(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{1}\neq 0
is the affine subspace generated by
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\{0\}
.

There are several ways to conclude the statement for points
(x_{1},x_{2})
with
x_{1}=0
or
x_{2}=0
. One of the simplest is to use a local foliated coordinate system at each of these points and use continuity of the leaves.

\Box

Next proposition shows that the explosion of Example 2 does not appear in generalized eigenvectors related to real eigenvalues. In such a way that in higher dimensions, isolating subspaces of dimension at most 2 (for nonreal eigenvalues), we have decomposition without explosion. In other words, there always exists a basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the decomposition of equation (
17
) holds for all time

if we allow 2-dimensional foliations related to non real eigenvalues. More precisely:

Proposition 4.5

Given a canonical Jordan basis of the matrix of coefficients (with 1 above the diagonal), the following cascade decomposition of the linear flow
\varphi_{t}
has no explosion with respect to this basis:

\varphi_{t}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{[}*&*\ldots*&*&*]_{d_{1}\times n}\\
&&&\\
&I_{d_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\\
&&&I_{d_{k}}\\
\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}I_{d_{1}}&&&\\
&&&\\
\mbox{{\bf[}}*&*&*&*\mbox{\bf{]}}_{d_{2}\times n}\\
&I_{d_{3}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&I_{d_{k}}\\
\end{array}\right)\ldots\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}I_{d_{1}}&&&\\
&&&\\
&\ddots&&\\
&&&\\
&&I_{d_{k-1}}&\\
\mbox{\bf{[}}*&*\ldots*&*&*\mbox{\bf{]}}_{d_{k}\times n}\\
\end{array}\right),

where
d_{i}=1
if the corresponding eigenvalues is real,
d_{i}=2
otherwise.

The positive integer
k
above counts the number of real eigenvalues plus the number of pairs of conjugate eigenvalues counting multiplicity, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}=m
.

Proof:
We use the notations established in equations (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
) for the components
\psi_{t}
,
\eta_{t}
and the matrix of coefficients

, with respect to a single Cartesian decomposition. The differential equations of
\psi_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
are given in general by equations (

. In the particular case of a linear vector field and Cartesian foliations we have that the submatrices of

and

satisfies:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}dG_{1}(t)=\big[A_{1}\ G_{1}(t)-G_{2}(t)\ A_{3}\ G_{1}(t)\big]\,dX_{t},&\\
&\\
dG_{2}(t)=\big[A_{1}G_{2}(t)+A_{2}-G_{2}(t)\,A_{4}-G_{2}(t)\,A_{3}\,G_{2}(t)\big]\,dX_{t},&\\
&\\
dF_{3}(t)=\big[A_{3}\,G_{1}(t)+A_{3}\,G_{2}(t)\,F_{3}(t)+A_{4}F_{3}(t)\big]\,dX_{t},&\\
&\\
dF_{4}(t)=\big[A_{3}\,G_{2}(t)\,F_{4}(t)+A_{4}\,F_{4}(t)\big]\,dX_{t}.&\end{array}

For details of this calculation see
[

.

The canonical Jordan form of matrix

give us exactly

\mathbb{R}^{m}=\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
with
