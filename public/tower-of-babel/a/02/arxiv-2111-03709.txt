Positivity-Preserving Lax-Wendroff Discontinuous Galerkin Schemes for Quadrature-Based Moment-Closure Approximations of Kinetic Models Thanks: This research was partially funded by NSF Grants DMS–1620128 and DMS–2012699.
Erica R. Johnson, James A. Rossmanith, Christine Vaughan

Abstract
The quadrature-based method of moments (QMOM) offers a promising class of approximation techniques for reducing kinetic equations to fluid equations that are valid beyond thermodynamic equilibrium. In this work, we study a particular five-moment variant of QMOM known as HyQMOM and establish that this system is moment-invertible over a convex region in solution space. We then develop a high-order discontinuous Galerkin (DG) scheme for solving the resulting fluid system. The scheme is based on a predictor-corrector approach, where the prediction is a localized space-time DG scheme. The nonlinear algebraic system in this prediction is solved using a Picard iteration. The correction is a straightforward explicit update based on the time-integral of the evolution equation, where the space-time prediction replaces all instances of the exact solution. In the absence of limiters, the high-order scheme does not guarantee that solutions remain in the convex set over which HyQMOM is moment-realizable. To overcome this, we introduce novel limiters that rigorously guarantee that the computed solution does not leave the convex set of realizable solutions, thus guaranteeing the hyperbolicity of the system. We develop positivity-preserving limiters in both the prediction and correction steps and an oscillation limiter that damps unphysical oscillations near shocks. We also develop a novel extension of this scheme to include a BGK collision operator; the proposed method is shown to be asymptotic-preserving in the high-collision limit. The HyQMOM and the HyQMOM-BGK solvers are verified on several test cases, demonstrating high-order accuracy on smooth problems and shock-capturing capability on problems with shocks. The asymptotic-preserving property of the HyQMOM-BGK solver is also numerically verified.

Introduction

Kinetic Boltzmann equations model the non-equilibrium dynamics of a wide variety of fluids, including gases, multiphase flows, and plasma.
These equations have the following general form:

f_{,t}+{\underline{v}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{x}}}f+{\underline{\mathcal{F}}}\cdot{\underline{\nabla}}_{{\underline{v}}}f=\mathbb{C}(f),

(1)

where
f\left(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}}\right):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{V}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the distribution function that describes the state of the fluid,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is time,
{\underline{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is the spatial coordinate, and
{\underline{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is the velocity coordinate.
Additionally,
{\underline{\mathcal{F}}}\left(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{V}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{V}
is the forcing term that could include lift, drag, gravity, and other forces acting on the particles, and

\mathbb{C}(f):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the collision term that describes direct particle-particle interactions.

Kinetic models of the form (
1
) offer two desirable
features: (1) the evolution equations have a relatively simple form (i.e.,
advection in phase space); and (2) the models are capable of accurately
describing a large class of physical phenomena that are important
in application problems. However, the main difficulty with kinetic models is that
their solutions live in high-dimensional phase space, which means that high
fidelity numerical computations are very expensive.

1.1
Fluid models and the moment-closure problem

One approach for reducing the computational complexity of kinetic models
is to replace them with so-called
fluid models
, which means that instead of
evolving the distribution function directly, one evolves a finite set of
moments
of the distribution function.
For example the
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
moment of the distribution function,
f
, is defined as follows:

{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{1}^{\ell_{1}}v_{2}^{\ell_{2}}v_{3}^{\ell_{3}}f\left(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}}\right)\,dv_{1}\,dv_{2}\,dv_{3},

(2)

where
{\underline{v}}=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
. If moments of the kinetic equation (
1
) are computed, we arrive at
three-dimensional evolution equations of the following form:

{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}),t}+{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1}+1,\ell_{2},\ell_{3}),x_{1}}+{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}+1,\ell_{3}),x_{2}}+{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}+1),x_{3}}=0,

(3)

where for simplicity, we have set the forcing and
collision operator to zero:
{\underline{\mathcal{F}}}\equiv{\underline{0}}
and
\mathbb{C}\equiv 0
.

The key benefit of considering a finite set of evolution equations of the form (
3
) over the fully kinetic
equation (
1
) is the reduction in the number of independent variables from
1+D+V\leq 7

to
1+D\leq 4
. However, we observe from (
3
) the key challenge in fluid model approximations of the kinetic equation: to evolve the moment
{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})}
, we need to know
higher-order moments:
{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1}+1,\ell_{2},\ell_{3})}
,
{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}+1,\ell_{3})}
, and

{\mathsf{M}}_{(\ell_{1}+1,\ell_{2},\ell_{3}+1)}
.
This issue is known simply as the
moment-closure problem
. And in particular, to obtain a closed fluid system,
one needs to somehow approximate the highest moments in the system. Furthermore, different choices lead to systems
of differential (or integro-differential) equations with vastly different mathematical properties.

1.2
Moment-closure methods

A standard approach for developing moment-closure approximation for (
1
) is to assume
a specific ansatz for the distribution function:

f(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}})\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\ell}\left({\underline{v}},\beta_{\ell}\left(t,{\underline{x}}\right)\right)\quad\text{or}\quad f(t,{\underline{x}},{\underline{v}})\approx\prod_{\ell=1}^{M}\psi_{\ell}\left({\underline{v}},\beta_{\ell}\left(t,{\underline{x}}\right)\right).

(4)

The first systematic attempt at developing a moment-closure approach is due to the seminal
work of Grad
article:grad49
, in which he proposed a moment-closure
that assumed the distribution was a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution multiplied by a polynomial in
{\underline{v}}
.
Since Grad’s work, a vast body of literature has developed on various moment closures, including
modern modifications of Grad’s closure (e.g.,
article:CaFaLi13
;
article:CaFaLi14
;
article:Koellermeier2021
;
article:Koellermeier2014
;
article:KoTo17
),
maximum entropy
article:Dreyer87
;
article:Lev96
;
article:MuRu93
and its numerous variants
(e.g.,
article:AbdBru2016
;
article:BohmTorr2019
), and quadrature-based moment closures
(e.g.,
article:desjardins2008
;
article:Fox08
;
article:fox09
;
article:FoxLauVie2018
;
article:marchisio2005
;
article:PaDeFo2019
).
A full review of all methods is well beyond the scope of the current
work, but can be found in the paper of Torrilhon
article:Torrilhon2009
and the references therein.

1.3
Scope of this work

In this work, we study a particular five-moment moment-closure known as the hyperbolic quadrature-based moment closure (HyQMOM), originally due to Fox, Laurent, and Vié
article:FoxLauVie2018
. Our focus here is only on the
one-dimensional version of kinetic equation (
1
) (i.e., 1D1V). We begin in §
2
with a brief review of the moment-closure problem and a few strategies for producing fluid approximations with desirable mathematical properties.
In §
3
we provide a brief review of the classical quadrature-based moment closure (QMOM), show its shortcomings, and then establish that the HyQMOM system is moment-invertible over a convex set in solution space.
In §
4
we introduce a novel high-order Lax-Wendroff discontinuous Galerkin scheme for solving the HyQMOM fluid system. The scheme is based on a predictor-corrector approach, where the prediction step is based on a localized space-time discontinuous Galerkin scheme. The nonlinear algebraic system that arises in this prediction step is solved using a Picard iteration. The correction is a straightforward explicit update based on the time-integral of the evolution equation, where the space-time prediction replaces all instances of the exact solution. In the absence of additional limiters, the proposed high-order scheme does not guarantee that the numerical solution remains in the convex set over which HyQMOM is moment-realizable. To overcome this challenge, we introduce in §
5
novel limiters that rigorously guarantee that the computed solution does not leave the convex set over which moment-invertible and hyperbolicity of the fluid system is guaranteed. We develop positivity-preserving limiters in both the prediction and correction steps and an oscillation limiter that damps unphysical oscillations near shocks. In §
6
we perform convergence tests to verify the order of accuracy of the scheme and test the scheme on Riemann data to demonstrate the shock-capturing and robustness of the method. In §
7
we develop an asymptotic-preserving
article:Jin12
extension of the proposed scheme that allows us to solve a five-moment fluid model with a Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK)
article:BGK54
collision operator. Finally, in §
8
we perform convergence tests to verify the order of accuracy of the scheme and verify the method on Riemann data with different Knudsen numbers. Conclusions are provided in §
9
.

2
Brief review of moment-realizability

For the remainder of the present work, we focus exclusively on the one-dimensional
version of (
1
). In particular, in this section,
we focus on the transport portion of the kinetic equation, and thus
restrict ourselves to the 1D1V collisionless Boltzmann equation (aka Vlasov equation):

f_{,t}+vf_{,x}=0,

(5)

where
f(t,x,v):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the probability distribution function
that describes the state of the fluid.

The moments of
f
are defined as follows:

{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{\ell}\,f\,dv,\quad\text{for}\quad\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

(6)

A simple calculation reveals that
for each
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, the moments satisfy the following equation:

{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell,t}+{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell+1,x}=0.

(7)

The key difficulty is that the evolution of the
\ell^{\text{th}}
moment
depends on the
(\ell+1)^{\text{st}}
moment, meaning that
the moment expansion does not produce a closed system.
Therefore, the key challenge for developing fluid approximations of kinetic
models is the following question: How does one close the
moment hierarchy?

Definition 1
(Univariate moment-closure problem)

Let
S\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Given only the first
S+1
moments of a univariate distribution function
f(v)
:

{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v^{\ell}f(v)\,dv,\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=0,1,\ldots,S,

(8)

find an approximation of the next moment,
{\mathsf{M}}_{S+1}
,
in terms of the given moments.

The basic strategy in most moment-closure approaches is as follows:
(1) start with a finite set of moments (e.g.,
\ell=0,1,\ldots,S
);
(2) assume a form of the distribution function with several free parameters (typically, the number of free parameters is the same as the number
of moments that will be tracked); (3) determine the free parameters in the assumed
distribution function so that its moments match all of the known moments (this
part is called
moment-inversion
); and (4)
compute the next moment of the assumed distribution function, which
is then used to provide the flux in the evolution equation for
{\mathsf{M}}_{S}
:

{\mathsf{M}}_{S,t}+\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\mathsf{M}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{S+1,x}=0,

(9)

where
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\mathsf{M}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{S+1}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\mathsf{M}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{S+1}\left({\mathsf{M}}_{0},{\mathsf{M}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathsf{M}}_{S}\right)
.

2.1
Existence: Moment-realizability

Before considering specific strategies for approximating the missing moment,
{\mathsf{M}}_{S+1}
, it is worthwhile to discuss the general existence problem first. We begin by defining some important quantities relevant throughout this work, namely the mass density, macroscopic velocity, pressure, heat flux, and modified kurtosis:

\begin{gathered}\rho:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,dv,\qquad u:=\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vf\,dv,\qquad p:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(v-u)^{2}f\,dv,\\
h:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(v-u)^{3}f\,dv,\qquad\text{and}\qquad k:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(v-u)^{4}f\,dv-\left(\frac{p^{3}+\rho q^{2}}{\rho\,p}\right),\end{gathered}

(10)

where we assume that
\rho,p>0
. These
primitive variables
are directly linked to the moments
{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}
:

\begin{gathered}\rho={\mathsf{M}}_{0},\qquad u=\frac{{\mathsf{M}}_{1}}{{\mathsf{M}}_{0}},\qquad p={\mathsf{M}}_{2}-\frac{{\mathsf{M}}_{1}^{2}}{{\mathsf{M}}_{0}},\qquad h={\mathsf{M}}_{3}-\frac{3{\mathsf{M}}_{1}{\mathsf{M}}_{2}}{{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}+\frac{2{\mathsf{M}}_{1}^{3}}{{\mathsf{M}}_{0}^{2}},\\
\text{and}\qquad k=\frac{{\mathsf{M}}_{2}^{3}-2{\mathsf{M}}_{1}{\mathsf{M}}_{2}{\mathsf{M}}_{3}+{\mathsf{M}}_{0}{\mathsf{M}}_{3}^{2}+{\mathsf{M}}_{1}^{2}{\mathsf{M}}_{4}-{\mathsf{M}}_{0}{\mathsf{M}}_{2}{\mathsf{M}}_{4}}{{\mathsf{M}}_{1}^{2}-{\mathsf{M}}_{0}{\mathsf{M}}_{2}}.\end{gathered}

(11)

Using these we define the
normalized velocity variable
,
s
, and the
normalized moments
:

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}
:

s:=\frac{v-u}{\sqrt{T}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}:=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{\rho}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s^{j}\,f\left(v(s)\right)\,ds=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s^{j}\,\widetilde{f}\left(s\right)\,ds,

(12)

where
T=p/\rho
is the temperature.
The moments and the normalized moments are related as follows:

\displaystyle{{\mathsf{M}}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\rho\,\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\begin{pmatrix}\ell\\
j\end{pmatrix}T^{\frac{j}{2}}\,u^{\ell-j}\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}={\rho}^{-1}\,T^{-\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\begin{pmatrix}j\\
\ell\end{pmatrix}\left(-u\right)^{j-\ell}\,{{\mathsf{M}}}_{\ell}.

(13)

By construction, the normalized moments have the following property:

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{0}=1,\qquad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}=0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{2}=1.

(14)

Definition 2
(Realizable moments)

The following rescaled moments:

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{0}=1,\quad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}=0,\quad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{2}=1,\quad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3},\quad\ldots,\quad\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{S},

where
S\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 3}
and
\bigl|\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}\bigr|<\infty

\forall j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
,
are called
realizable
if there exists a probability density function,
\widetilde{f}(s):\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, such that

\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{j}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s^{j}\,\widetilde{f}(s)\,ds\qquad\text{for}\quad j=0,1,\ldots,S.

This leads to an obvious question: for a given
S\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 3}
, under what conditions is the set of moments,
\{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{0}=1,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}=0,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{2}=1,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3},\ldots,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{S}\}
, realizable? This question is the celebrated
truncated Hamburger moment problem
(e.g., see Chapter 9 of

book:schmudgen2017
) for which we can state the following result. Note that we state only the case where
S
is even, although a similar result also exists when
S
is odd
book:schmudgen2017
.

Theorem 2.1
(Truncated Hamburger moment problem (adapted from
Theorem 9.27 of
book:schmudgen2017
))

Let
S\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>3}
be an even integer. The set of moments:

\{\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{0}=1,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{1}=0,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{2}=1,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3},\ldots,\,\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{S}\}

is realizable if all of the Hankel determinants for
m=0,1,\ldots,S/2

are positive:

D_{m}:=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&1&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m}\\
0&1&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{4}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+1}\\
1&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{4}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{5}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+2}\\
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{3}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{4}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{5}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{6}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+1}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+2}&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{m+3}&\cdots&\widetilde{\mathsf{M}}_{2m}\end{vmatrix}>0.

2.3
Techniques for moment-closure

There are many different moment closure methods for approximating the final moment,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu{\mathsf{M}}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{S+1}
, and each method has its own merits and challenges. A full review of all methods is well beyond the scope of the current
work, but can be found in the paper of Torrilhon
article:Torrilhon2009
and the references therein.
In this work, we settle for a brief summary of three broad classes of the most commonly used closures.

Grad closure:

This approach was originally developed
by Grad
article:grad49
in 1949, but
variants with improved hyperbolicity properties have been introduced more recently
article:CaFaLi13
;
article:CaFaLi14
;
article:Koellermeier2021
;
article:Koellermeier2014
;
article:KoTo17
. The basic idea is that the distribution function is approximated as a Maxwellian multiplied by a polynomial in
v
:

\widetilde{f}(s)\sim\frac{e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}\left[\rho+\sum_{\ell=3}^{S}T^{-\frac{\ell}{2}}\beta_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}\left(s\right)\right],

(19)

where
\psi_{\ell}\left(s\right)
is the Hermite polynomial of degree
\ell
and
{\underline{\beta}}
are coefficients chosen so that
\widetilde{f}(s)
matches the first

S+1
input moments. A related approach widely used in applications is the R13 model, which regularizes the 13-moment Grad closure through additional terms from the Chapman-Enskog expansion; an excellent review of the R13 model can be found in Torrilhon
article:Torrilhon2016
.

Maximum entropy closure:

The maximum entropy closure
article:Dreyer87
;
article:Lev96
;
article:MuRu93
and its numerous variants
(e.g., see
article:AbdBru2016
;
article:BohmTorr2019
) formulate the
moment-inversion problem as an optimization problem to maximize the entropy
under some assumed form of the distribution function. In the original formulation, the distribution function is approximated as an exponential of a polynomial:

\widetilde{f}(s)\sim e^{\,{\underline{\beta}}\cdot{\underline{{\Phi}}}(s)}\qquad\text{where}\qquad{\underline{\Phi}}(s)=[1,s,s^{2},\dots]^{T},

(20)

and the coefficients,
{\underline{\beta}}
, are chosen so that
\widetilde{f}(s)
matches the first
S+1
input moments.

Quadrature-based moment closures:

In the quadrature-based method of moments (QMOM)
(e.g.,
article:desjardins2008
;
article:Fox08
;
article:fox09
;
article:FoxLauVie2018
;
article:marchisio2005
;
article:PaDeFo2019
),
the distribution function is
represented as a sum of Dirac delta functions. This closure will be discussed in detail in §
3
.

3
HyQMOM: Hyperbolic quadrature-based moment closure

In this section, we review the quadrature-based moment-closure (QMOM) approach and describe in detail the five-moment hyperbolic regularization of QMOM (HyQMOM), which is the main focus of the current work.

3.1
Classical QMOM Approach

The classical quadrature-based moment-closure (QMOM) approach is widely used in modeling multiphase flows; key developments in this methodology have been
developed over the course of the past several years, e.g., see
article:ChKaMa12
;
article:desjardins2008
;
book:Fox2003
;
article:Fox08
;
article:fox09
;
article:marchisio2005
;
article:Vikas11
.

The key idea is to assume that the distribution is a sum of Dirac delta functions whose locations (abscissas) and strengths (weights) are free parameters:

\displaystyle{f}(t,x,v)\approx f^{\star}(t,x,v):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\delta\left(v-\mu_{j}\right),

(21)

where
\delta(v)
is the Dirac delta and the quadrature
weights,
\omega_{j}
, and abscissas,
\mu_{j}
, are all functions
of
t
and
x
. This approach is reminiscent of other discrete velocity models such as the Broadwell model
article:broad64a
;
article:broad64b
;
article:platkowski88
; however, a key difference is that the discrete velocities,
\mu_{\ell}
, change with the solution.

The moment inversion problem requires us to find
(\omega_{j},\mu_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,N
by matching the
first
2N
moments of
f(t,x,v)
:

{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{\ell}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=0,1,\ldots,2N-1.

(22)

The closure then comes from taking the next moment as follows:

{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{2N}.

(23)

3.1.1
Example:
N=2
case

As a simple example, let us consider the
N=2
case.
The first four moments of
f^{\star}
in (
21
) with
N=2
are

{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}=\omega_{1}\mu^{\ell}_{1}+\omega_{2}\mu^{\ell}_{2}\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=0,1,2,3.

(24)

The moment inversion problem is then this: given
({\mathsf{M}}_{0},{\mathsf{M}}_{1},{\mathsf{M}}_{2},{\mathsf{M}}_{3})
, find the parameters
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

such that (
24
) is satisfied.

This inversion problem is equivalent to finding the quadrature points and weights
for the following weighted Gaussian quadrature rule:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(v)\,f(v)\,dv\,\approx\,{\omega}_{1}\,g\left({\mu}_{1}\right)+{\omega}_{2}\,g\left({\mu}_{2}\right),

(25)

where
f(v)
is a probability density function with moments
({\mathsf{M}}_{0},{\mathsf{M}}_{1},{\mathsf{M}}_{2},{\mathsf{M}}_{3})
.
If we attempt to make this quadrature
rule exact with
g(v)=1
,
v
,
v^{2}
, and
v^{3}
, we again arrive at (
24
).

To find the correct Gaussian quadrature rule, we invoke results from classical numerical analysis and look for polynomials of degree up to two that are orthogonal in the weighted
L^{2}(-\infty,\infty)
inner product:
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{f}
.
Such polynomials are easily obtained via Gram-Schmidt, and indeed the relevant
one here is the quadratic polynomial:

\displaystyle\psi_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=p^{2}-p\rho\left(v-u\right)^{2}+\rho h\left(v-u\right),

(26)

where
\rho
,
u
,
p
, and
h
are defined by
(
10
)–(
11
).
The abscissas are the two distinct real roots of
{\psi}_{2}(v)

and the weights can easily be obtained by enforcing (
24
):

\displaystyle{\mu}_{1},\,{\mu}_{2}=u+\frac{h}{2p}\mp\sqrt{\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}},\quad{\omega}_{1},\,\omega_{2}=\frac{\rho}{2}\left[1\pm\frac{\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}}}\right].

(27)

3.1.2
Weak hyperbolicity and linear degeneracy of QMOM for all
N\geq 1

While the above-described process can be used for any
N\geq 1
, it turns out that the resulting
fluid equations are always only
weakly hyperbolic
. Furthermore, all of the waves in the Riemann
problem solution are
linearly degenerate
. We state these facts in the form
of
theorem

A.1
in
appendix

A
; for completeness, we also provide a full
proof of this theorem in
appendix

A
. The theorem results are well-known in the QMOM literature
(e.g., see Chalons et al.
article:ChKaMa12
), although
previously, no theorem had been presented to rigorously show both the weak hyperbolicity and linear degeneracy of all the waves
for all
N\geq 1
.

3.2
Pressure Regularized QMOM

To overcome the weak hyperbolicity present in classical QMOM, Chalons, Fox, and Massot
article:Chalons10
proposed to replace the delta function ansatz
(
21
) with a multi-Gaussian ansatz of the form:

\displaystyle{f}(t,x,v)\approx f^{\star}(t,x,v):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\exp\left[-\frac{\left(v-\mu_{j}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma}\right],

(28)

where the free parameters are now the quadrature weights,
\omega_{k}
,
the abscissas,
\mu_{k}
, and the additional parameter
\sigma
.
A similar approach using B-splines was also considered by Cheng and Rossmanith
article:ChRo13
. The additional parameter
\sigma
allows this closure to match an additional moment (i.e., a total of
2N+1
moments can now be matched), but more importantly, it provides a pressure regularization that restores strong hyperbolicity.
Unfortunately, this closure exhibits a singularity in the limit of thermodynamic
equilibrium, since in that limit, all the quadrature points collapse to the macroscopic velocity:

\sigma\rightarrow T,\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{N}\omega_{j}=\rho,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu_{j}\rightarrow u\quad\forall\,j\in\left[1,N\right].

(29)

This type of singularity is also evident in other closures, most notably the
maximum entropy
closure
article:Junk98
.

3.3
HyQMOM: Density Regularized QMOM

As an alternative to the above-described pressure regularized QMOM, Fox et al.
article:FoxLauVie2018
;
article:PaDeFo2019
developed a density regularized
version, which they refer to as HyQMOM (hyperbolic quadrature-based method of moments). This approach was also studied by Johnson
thesis:Johnson2017
and Wiersma
thesis:Wiersma2019
.
The five-moment HyQMOM system is the subject of the current work, and we briefly review it in this section.

The idea of HyQMOM is as follows: approximate the distribution as a sum of delta functions (as in classical QMOM), but place one (or more) of these delta functions at known locations.
This converts the quadrature rule from classical Gaussian quadrature to something akin to Gauss-Radau quadrature.
The version of this idea relevant to the current work
is the case of three delta functions:

f\approx f^{\star}=\omega_{1}\delta(v-\mu_{1})+\omega_{2}\delta(v-u)+\omega_{3}\delta(v-\mu_{3}),

(30)

where two delta distributions are at unknown locations
\mu_{1},\mu_{3}
and the last delta distribution is fixed at the velocity,
u
. Each of the distributions is weighted by
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{3}
. This results in the following
moment-inversion problem:

\begin{split}\omega_{1}+\omega_{3}&=\widetilde{\rho},\\
\omega_{1}\mu_{1}+\omega_{3}\mu_{3}&=\widetilde{\rho}u,\\
\omega_{1}\mu_{1}^{2}+\omega_{3}\mu_{3}^{2}&=\widetilde{\rho}u^{2}+p,\\
\omega_{1}\mu_{1}^{3}+\omega_{3}\mu_{3}^{3}&=\widetilde{\rho}u^{3}+3pu+h,\end{split}

(31)

and

\widetilde{\rho}:=\rho-\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}\left(\frac{\mu_{1}}{u}\right)^{4}+\omega_{3}\left(\frac{\mu_{3}}{u}\right)^{4}-\frac{6\rho p^{2}u^{2}+4\rho phu+p^{3}+\rho h^{2}+\rho pk}{\rho pu^{4}}.

(32)

System (
31
) can be solved in the same way that the
N=2
classical QMOM system was solved, namely by constructing a quadratic polynomial:

\psi_{2}(v)=p^{2}-p\widetilde{\rho}\left(v-u\right)^{2}+h\widetilde{\rho}\left(v-u\right),

(33)

which is just (
26
) with
\rho\rightarrow\widetilde{\rho}
. The roots of (
33
) provide
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{3}
,
and the corresponding weights,
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{3}
, can easily
be computed from (
31
) once
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{3}
are known:

\displaystyle{\mu}_{1},\,{\mu}_{3}=u+\frac{h}{2p}\mp\sqrt{\frac{p}{\widetilde{\rho}}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}},\quad{\omega}_{1},\,\omega_{3}=\frac{\widetilde{\rho}}{2}\left[1\pm\frac{\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{p}{\widetilde{\rho}}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}}}\right].

(34)

Finally, we can obtain
\omega_{2}
and fully solve the moment inversion problem
by inserting the above expressions for
\omega_{1}
,

\omega_{3}
,
\mu_{1}
, and
\mu_{3}
into (
32
):

\widetilde{\rho}=\rho-\omega_{2}=\frac{p^{3}}{p\left(k+\frac{p^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{h^{2}}{p}\right)-h^{2}}.

(35)

Putting all of these results together yields:

\begin{split}{\mathsf{M}}_{5}^{\star}=\,\rho u^{5}+10pu^{3}+10hu^{2}+5ru+\frac{2hr}{p}-\frac{h^{3}}{p^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad r:=\frac{p^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{h^{2}}{p}+k.\end{split}

(36)

From this, we can now assemble the full fluid approximation implied by the
5-moment HyQMOM approximation (
30
).

The 5-moment HyQMOM approximation can be written either in conservative or

{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}=\,{\underline{0}}\,\qquad\text{or}\qquad{\underline{\alpha}}_{,t}+{\underline{\underline{B}}}\left({\underline{\alpha}}\right)\,{\underline{\alpha}}_{,x}=\,{\underline{0}}\,,

Proposition 1
(Hyperbolicity of HyQMOM)

The HyQMOM fluid model as expressed either in conservative or primitive form
(
37
)–(
39
)
is strictly hyperbolic for all
\rho
,
p
,
k

>0
.
Note that
\rho
,
p
,
k

>0
defines a convex set in the solution space:
{\underline{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
.
The resulting wave structure includes one linearly degenerate wave and four nonlinear waves.

Note that the essential contents of this theorem were already known in Fox, Laurent, and Vié
article:FoxLauVie2018
,
but never presented as a formal proposition and proved. Therefore, we include the proof here.

Proof

The eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix,
{\underline{\underline{B}}}\left({\underline{\alpha}}\right)
, in (
39
)
can be computed explicitly:

\begin{gathered}\lambda_{1}=u+\frac{h}{2p}-\sqrt{a+b},\quad\lambda_{2}=u+\frac{h}{2p}-\sqrt{a-b},\quad\lambda_{3}=u,\\
\lambda_{4}=u+\frac{h}{2p}+\sqrt{a-b},\quad\lambda_{5}=u+\frac{h}{2p}+\sqrt{a+b},\end{gathered}

(41)

where

a=\frac{p}{\rho}+\frac{k}{p}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b=\sqrt{\frac{k^{2}}{p^{2}}+\frac{k}{\rho}}.

(42)

One can show via simple calculations that for all
\rho
,
p
,
k

>0
:

\displaystyle a>b>0,\quad\sqrt{a+b}>\frac{|h|}{2p},\quad\text{and}\quad\sqrt{a-b}>\frac{|h|}{2p}.

(43)

Therefore, all five eigenvalues shown in
(
41
)–(
42
) are real and distinct
for all
\rho
,
p
,
k

>0
, which is sufficient to show that system (
37
)–(
39
)
is strictly hyperbolic.

The eigenvectors of the flux Jacobian (
40
) can be written
as follows:

{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}}}=\left[1,\lambda_{\ell},\lambda_{\ell}^{2},\lambda_{\ell}^{3},\lambda_{\ell}^{4}\right]^{T}\qquad\text{for}\qquad\ell=1,2,3,4,5.

(44)

To determine whether the corresponding waves are linearly degenerate or not, we need to compute the quantities:

\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}}}=\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{\alpha}}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial{\underline{q}}}{\partial{\underline{\alpha}}}\right)^{-1}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}}},\quad\forall\ell=1,2,3,4,5,

(45)

where

\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}:=\left[\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial q_{1}},\,\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial q_{2}},\,\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial q_{3}},\,\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial q_{4}},\,\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial q_{5}}\right],

(46)

and

\frac{\partial{\underline{q}}}{\partial{\underline{\alpha}}}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]1&&0&&0&&0&&0\\
u&&\rho&&0&&0&&0\\
u^{2}&&2\rho u&&1&&0&&0\\
u^{3}&&3\left(p+\rho u^{2}\right)&&3u&&1&&0\\
\frac{\rho^{2}u^{4}-p^{2}}{\rho^{2}}&&4\left(h+3pu+\rho u^{3}\right)&&\frac{6\rho p^{2}u^{2}+2p^{3}-\rho h^{2}}{\rho p^{2}}&&\frac{2h+4pu}{p}&&1\end{bmatrix}.

(47)

We note that one of the waves is linearly degenerate while the remaining
are nonlinear:

\frac{\partial\lambda_{3}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{3}}}\equiv 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}_{\ell}}}\not\equiv 0\quad\text{for}\quad\ell=1,2,4,5.

(48)

\blacksquare

4
Locally-implicit Lax-Wendroff discontinuous Galerkin

We consider generic one-dimensional conservation laws of the form:

{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}={\underline{0}},

(49)

where
t
is time,
x
is space,
q(t,x):\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{eqn}}}
is the vector of conserved variables,
{M_{\text{eqn}}}
is the number
of equations, and

{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right):\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{eqn}}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{eqn}}}
is the
flux function. We assume that this system is hyperbolic, meaning that the
flux Jacobian:

{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,{\underline{q}}}:\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{eqn}}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{eqn}}}\times{M_{\text{eqn}}}},

(50)

has real eigenvalues and a complete set of eigenvectors over some convex
region
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{eqn}}}}
in solution space inside of which we are interested in solving the equation.

The Lax-Wendroff method
article:LxW60
is a time
discretization for
hyperbolic conservation laws based on
the the Cauchy-Kovalevskaya

article:Kovaleskaya1875
procedure to convert temporal derivatives into spatial derivatives.
We begin with a Taylor series in time:

{\underline{q}}(t+\Delta t,x)={\underline{q}}(t,x)+\Delta t{\underline{q}}_{,t}(t,x)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}{\underline{q}}_{,t,t}(t,x)+\ldots,

(51)

and then replace all time derivatives by spatial derivatives:

{\underline{q}}_{,t}=-{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x},\quad{\underline{q}}_{,t,t}=-{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,t,x}=-\left[{\underline{\underline{f^{\prime}}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)\,{\underline{q}}_{,t}\right]_{,x}=\left[{\underline{\underline{f^{\prime}}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right){\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}\right]_{,x},\quad\ldots,

(52)

which results in the following:

{\underline{q}}(t+\Delta t,x)={\underline{q}}-\Delta t{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}\left[{\underline{\underline{f^{\prime}}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right){\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}\right]_{,x}+\ldots,

(53)

where on the right-hand side, we have suppressed the evaluation at
(t,x)
.
The final step is to truncate the Taylor series at some finite number of terms,
and then replace all spatial derivatives by some discrete spatial derivative operators.
The above Lax-Wendroff formalism
article:LxW60
has been used in conjunction with
with a variety of spatial discretizations, including finite volume
article:Le97
,
weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO)
article:TitaToro02
, and discontinuous Galerkin
article:Qiu05
operators.

In this work, we are concerned with the discontinuous Galerkin version
of Lax-Wendroff
article:Qiu05
; and in particular, we make use
of the reformulation of Gassner et al.
article:GasDumHinMun2011
of the Lax-Wendroff discontinuous Galerkin (LxW-DG) scheme
in terms of a locally-implicit prediction step, followed by an explicit correction step.
The key advantage of this formulation is that we do not need to explicitly compute the
partial derivatives as shown in (
52
); and instead, the locally-implicit
solver automatically produces discrete versions of these derivatives.
The next challenge is to efficiently solve the nonlinear algebraic equations arising from the
locally-implicit prediction step; we solve these equations by following Gassner et al.
article:GasDumHinMun2011
and making use of a Picard fixed point iteration. We will follow the notational conventions of Guthrey and Rossmanith
article:GuRo2017
developed for locally-implicit and regionally-implicit LxW-DG schemes.
Note that the predictor-correct method is equivalent to the Lax-Wendroff DG method for linear constant-coefficient hyperbolic systems. For nonlinear systems, the predictor-correct method differs slightly from Lax-Wendroff DG in that the Picard iteration inside the prediction step approximates the direct computation of the nonlinear Taylor series expansion.

4.1
Discontinuous Galerkin finite elements

To discretize equation (
49
) in space, we use the discontinuous Galerkin (DG) finite element method, which was first introduced by Reed and Hill
article:ReedHill73
. It was fully developed for time-dependent hyperbolic conservation laws in a series of papers by Bernardo Cockburn, Chi-Wang Shu, and collaborators (see
article:CoShu98
and references therein for details).

We define the broken finite element space

\mathcal{W}^{\Delta x}:=\left\{w^{\Delta x}\in[L^{\infty}(\Omega)]^{M_{\text{eqn}}}:w^{\Delta x}|_{\mathcal{T}_{i}}\in[\mathbb{P}(M_{\text{deg}})]^{M_{\text{eqn}}}\quad\forall\mathcal{T}_{i}\right\},

(54)

where
\Delta x=(x_{\text{high}}-x_{\text{low}})/M_{\text{elem}}
is a uniform grid spacing with
M_{\text{elem}}
being the number of elements. Additionally,
M_{\text{eqn}}
is the number of conserved variables,

\mathbb{P}(M_{\text{deg}})
is the set of all polynomials of degree at most
M_{\text{deg}}
, and the computational mesh is described by non-overlapping elements of width
\Delta x
centered at the points
x_{i}
:

\mathcal{T}_{i}=\left[x_{i}-\frac{\Delta x}{2},\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right]\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for }i=1,\dots,M_{\text{elem}}.

(55)

On each element, we define the local spatial variable,
\xi
:

x=x_{i}+\left(\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right)\xi\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\in[-1,1].

(56)

On each element, we approximate the solution by a finite expansion
in terms of the following orthonormal Legendre polynomial basis functions:

{\underline{\Phi}}=\left(1,\,\sqrt{3}\xi,\frac{\sqrt{5}}{2}\left(3\xi^{2}-1\right),\,\frac{\sqrt{7}}{2}\left(5\xi^{3}-3\xi\right),\,\frac{\sqrt{9}}{8}\left(35\xi^{4}-30\xi^{2}+3\right),\,\dots\right),

(57)

with the orthonormality property:

\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}{\underline{\Phi}}\,{\underline{\Phi}}^{T}\,d\xi={\underline{\underline{\,\,{\mathbb{I}}\,\,}}},

(58)

where
{\underline{\underline{\,\,{\mathbb{I}}\,\,}}}
is the identity matrix.
On each element at time
t=t^{n}
, we approximate the solution as follows:

{\underline{q}}^{h}\left(t^{n},x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\xi\right):={\underline{\Phi}}(\xi)^{T}{\underline{\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}}\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\in[-1,1],

(59)

where

\displaystyle{\underline{\Phi}}(\xi):[-1,1]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{C}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\underline{\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{C}}}\times{M_{\text{eqn}}}}.

(60)

The number of basis functions in 1D is
{M_{\text{C}}}={M_{\text{deg}}}+1
, and the order of accuracy is
{M_{\text{O}}}={M_{\text{deg}}}+1
.

4.2
Prediction step

The prediction step is completely local to each element, and therefore, the prediction step is inconsistent with the underlying conservation law.
This inconsistency allows us to freely choose updated variables; we use the primitive variables for this step:
{\underline{\alpha}}=(\rho,u,p,h,k)
.

The prediction step is local on each space-element
\left[t^{n},t^{n+1}\right]\times{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
, where
t^{n+1}=t^{n}+\Delta t
. Let
t=t^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(1+\tau)
, for
\tau\in[-1,1]
and

{\underline{\alpha}}_{,\tau}={\underline{\Theta}}({\underline{\alpha}}):=-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\,{\underline{\underline{B}}}\left({\underline{\alpha}}\right){\underline{\alpha}}_{,\xi},

(61)

where
{\underline{\underline{B}}}({\underline{\alpha}})
is the (primitive variable) flux Jacobian matrix
defined by (
39
). We introduce a space-time Legendre basis on each element:

\Psi_{\ell}(\tau,\xi)=\Phi_{\ell_{1}}(\tau)\Phi_{\ell_{2}}(\xi),\quad\text{for}\quad\ell_{1}=1,\ldots,{M_{\text{O}}},\quad\ell_{2}=1,\ldots,{M_{\text{O}}}+1-\ell_{1},

(62)

where

\ell={M_{\text{O}}}\left(\ell_{1}-1\right)-\frac{\left(\ell_{1}-1\right)\left(\ell_{1}-2\right)}{2}+\ell_{2}\quad\text{such that}\quad\ell=1,\ldots,{M_{\text{P}}},

(63)

and
{M_{\text{P}}}={M_{\text{O}}}({M_{\text{O}}}+1)/2
is the number of space-time basis functions. These space-time basis functions are
orthonormal on
[-1,1]^{2}
:

\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\underline{\Psi}}\,{\underline{\Psi}}^{T}\,d\tau\,d\xi={\underline{\underline{\,\,\mathbb{I}\,\,}}}.

(64)

We write the predicted solution as follows:

{\underline{\alpha}}^{\text{ST}}\left(t^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(1+\tau),\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\,\xi\right):={\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau,\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i}}}},\qquad{\underline{\underline{W_{i}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{P}}}\times{M_{\text{eqn}}}},

(65)

for
(\tau,\xi)\in[-1,1]^{2}
, where
{\underline{\underline{W}}}
represents the matrix of unknown coefficients.

We proceed by multiplying (
61
) by the test function
{\underline{\Psi}}
, then integrating over
(\tau,\xi)\in[-1,1]^{2}
:

\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\alpha}_{m,\tau}\,{\underline{\Psi}}\,d\tau\,d\xi=\frac{1}{4}\,\iint_{-1}^{1}{\Theta}_{m}({\underline{\alpha}})\,{\underline{\Psi}}\,d\tau\,d\xi,

(66)

and applying integration-by-parts in
\tau
only:

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)\,{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)\,d\xi-\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)\,{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)\,d\xi\\
-\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\alpha}_{m}\,{\underline{\Psi}}_{,\tau}\,d\tau\,d\xi=\frac{1}{4}\,\iint_{-1}^{1}{\Theta}_{m}({\underline{\alpha}})\,{\underline{\Psi}}\,d\tau\,d\xi,\end{gathered}

(67)

where the choice of values for
{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}
at
\tau=1
and
\tau=-1
still remains to be made.
Before making this choice, however, let us reverse integrate-by-parts, such that the newly introduced boundary
terms are always the internal values of the current space-time element:

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigl[{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)-{\alpha}_{m}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)\Bigr]\,{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)\,d\xi\\
-\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\Bigl[{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)-{\alpha}_{m}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)\Bigr]\,{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)\,d\xi\\
+\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\underline{\Psi}}\,{\alpha}_{m,\tau}\,d\tau\,d\xi=\frac{1}{4}\,\iint_{-1}^{1}{\Theta}_{m}({\underline{\alpha}})\,{\underline{\Psi}}\,d\tau\,d\xi.\end{gathered}

Now we plug-in the following values for
{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}
at
\tau=1
and
\tau=-1
:

{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)={\alpha}_{m}\left(\tau=1,\xi\right)\quad\text{and}\quad{\alpha}^{\star}_{m}\left(\tau=-1,\xi\right)={\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi\right)^{T}\,{\underline{A^{n}_{i\,(:,m)}}},

(68)

as well as the following values for
{\alpha}_{m}
on the interior of the space-time element:

{\alpha}_{m}\left(\tau,\xi\right)={\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau,\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i(:,m)}}}.

(69)

The result is

\begin{split}{\underline{\underline{L}}}\,\,{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i\,(:,m)}}}&=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\Theta}_{m}\left({\underline{\Psi}}^{T}{\underline{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i}}}}\right){\underline{\Psi}}\,d\tau\,d\xi\\
&+\left[\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\,{\underline{{\Psi}}}\left(-1,\xi\right)\,{\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi\right)^{T}\,d\xi\right]\,{\underline{A^{n}_{i\,(:,m)}}},\end{split}

(70)

for each equation
m=1,\ldots,{M_{\text{eqn}}}
,
where

\displaystyle{\underline{\underline{L}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{-1}^{1}{\underline{\Psi}}\,{\underline{\Psi}}^{T}_{,\tau}\,d\tau\,d\xi+\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}{\underline{\Psi}}_{|_{\tau=-1}}{\underline{\Psi}}_{|_{\tau=-1}}^{T}\,d\xi,

5
HyQMOM limiters

The high-order numerical method as described in §
4
does not guarantee that density, pressure, and modified kurtosis remain positive throughout a time-step:

\rho^{n}>0,\quad p^{n}>0,\quad k^{n}>0\quad\centernot\Longrightarrow\quad\rho^{n+1}>0,\quad p^{n+1}>0,\quad k^{n+1}>0.

(80)

Recall that positivity of these quantities is needed to guarantee the moment-realizability of the moment-closure and strict hyperbolicity of the resulting evolution equations.
If we want positivity over a time step, we will need to introduce
positivity-preserving limiters
. Additionally, if we want to control unphysical oscillations near large gradients, shocks, and rarefactions, we will also need
non-oscillatory limiters
. In this section, we derive all of these limiters. In particular, we first need to establish that a simple first-order scheme is positivity-preserving under some appropriate time-step restriction; this is done in §
5.1
. Using this result we derive a suite of limiters that ensure positivity: §
5.2
(positivity at select points in the prediction step), §
5.3
(positivity of the average density, pressure, and modified kurtosis in each element in the correction step), and §
5.4
(positivity at select points in the correction step). We then develop an unphysical oscillation limiter in §
5.5
.

5.1
Positivity of the Rusanov scheme

Before considering positivity limiters for the high-order method, we must
first establish that simple first-order schemes, in this case, we consider the Rusanov (aka local Lax-Friedrichs) scheme
article:Ru61
, are positivity-preserving under some appropriate time-step restriction. This is established in the theorem below, which is an extension of the result of Zhang and Shu
article:ZhangShu2011
for the compressible Euler equations.

Theorem 5.1

Let
{\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}
be some
approximation of the element averages of the conserved variables
on element
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
at time
t^{n}
,
and let
{\underline{Q_{i}^{n+1}}}
be the element averages produced by the Rusanov scheme
article:Ru61

at time

. Then

\quad\rho_{i}^{n}>0,\,\,\,\,p_{i}^{n}>0,\,\,\,\,k_{i}^{n}>0\,\,\,\,\,\forall i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho^{n+1}_{i}>0,\,\,\,\,p^{n+1}_{i}>0,\,\,\,\,k^{n+1}_{i}>0\,\,\,\,\,\forall i,

(81)

under the CFL condition:

\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\max_{i}\left({\lambda_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)<1,

(82)

where

{\lambda_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}}=\max\left\{{\lambda_{\max}}\left({\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}\right),\,{\lambda_{\max}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i\pm 1}}}\right),\,{\lambda_{\max}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left({\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}+{\underline{Q^{n}_{i\pm 1}}}\right)\right)\right\},

the flux Jacobian (

Proof

Recall that the Rusanov scheme can be written as

{\underline{Q_{i}^{n+1}}}={\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left({\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}}-{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}}\right),

(84)

where the numerical fluxes are given by

{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\underline{f}}({\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}})+{\underline{f}}({\underline{Q_{i\pm 1}^{n}}})\right]-\frac{1}{2}{\lambda_{i\pm\frac{1}{2}}}\left({\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}-{\underline{Q^{n}_{i\pm 1}}}\right),

(85)

where the flux function,
{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)

We can rewrite the above expression into the following numerical update:

\begin{split}{\underline{Q_{i}^{n+1}}}&=\left[1-\frac{\Delta t}{2\Delta x}\left({\lambda_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}+{\lambda_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\right]{\underline{Q_{i}^{n}}}+\left[\frac{\Delta t{\lambda_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}}{2\Delta x}\right]{\underline{M^{+}_{i}}}+\left[\frac{\Delta t{\lambda_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}}{2\Delta x}\right]{\underline{M^{-}_{i}}},\end{split}

where

\displaystyle{\underline{M^{+}_{i}}}={\underline{Q_{i+1}^{n}}}-\left({\lambda_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{-1}{{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q_{i+1}^{n}}}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad{\underline{M^{-}_{i}}}={\underline{Q_{i-1}^{n}}}+\left({\lambda_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{-1}{{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q_{i-1}^{n}}}\right)}.

(89)

Now let

Furthermore, we note that density, pressure, and modified kurtosis (

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\left({\underline{M^{+}_{i}}}\right)>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\left({\underline{M^{-}_{i}}}\right)>0,

(91)

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}\left({\underline{q}}\right):=q_{1},\quad{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{q}}\right):=q_{3}-\frac{q_{2}^{2}}{q_{1}},\\

(92)

1.

Density:
We take
\alpha\equiv\rho

{\mathcal{C}}_{\rho}\left({\underline{Q}}\pm\ \lambda^{-1}{\underline{f}}({\underline{Q}})\right)=\frac{\left(\lambda\pm u\right)\rho}{\lambda}.

(93)

Positivity of (

2.

Pressure:
We take
\alpha\equiv p

{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q}}\pm{\lambda^{-1}}{\underline{f}}({\underline{Q}})\right)=\frac{p\rho\left(\lambda\pm u\right)^{2}+h\rho\left(u\pm\lambda\right)-p^{2}}{\lambda\left(\lambda\pm u\right)\rho},

\begin{split}z_{1}=\mp\left(u+\frac{h}{2p}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}},\quad z_{2}=\mp\left(u+\frac{h}{2p}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

(i.e.,
\lambda
is always to the right of the roots) and

(i.e., the quadratic is concave up).

3.

We take
\alpha\equiv k

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q}}\pm{\lambda^{-1}}{\underline{f}}({\underline{Q}})\right)=\left(\frac{k(\lambda\pm u)}{\lambda}\right)\left(\frac{p\rho\left(\lambda\pm u\right)^{2}+h\rho\left(u\pm\lambda\right)-p^{2}-k\rho}{p\rho\left(\lambda\pm u\right)^{2}+h\rho\left(u\pm\lambda\right)-p^{2}}\right),

93
) and (
94
). The roots of the quadratic are:

\begin{split}z_{1}=\mp\left(u+\frac{h}{2p}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{k}{p}+\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}},\quad z_{2}=\mp\left(u+\frac{h}{2p}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{k}{p}+\frac{p}{\rho}+\left(\frac{h}{2p}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

\lambda>\max\left\{z_{1},\,z_{2}\right\}
(i.e.,
\lambda
is always to the right of the roots) and

(i.e., the quadratic is concave up).

\blacksquare

We have now shown that the first-order method will maintain positivity from one time step to the next under an appropriate CFL condition. However, higher-order methods will not automatically guarantee positivity; we address this issue in the subsequent subsections.

5.2
Limiter I: Positivity-at-points in the prediction step

The prediction step, as described in (
73
) requires numerical quadrature in space-time in each Picard iteration. Furthermore, once the
predicted solution has been computed, it will again be integrated in
space-time in the correction step (i.e., see (
76
)–(
79
)). To guarantee that all of the
numerical quadratures in both the prediction and correction steps only use positive values of density, pressure, and modified kurtosis, we introduce a prediction-step positivity limiter.

Let the 1D Gauss-Legendre points internal to each element, augmented by the element
end-points (i.e., the location of the element faces), be defined as follows:

{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}:=\Bigl\{-1,1\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{\text{roots of the ${M_{\text{O}}^{\text{th}}}$ degree Legendre polynomial}\Bigr\},

(96)

where
{M_{\text{O}}}
is the desired order of accuracy. Note that
{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}

contains a total of
{M_{\text{O}}}+2
points.
We note that all of the quadratures in the prediction update (
73
)
and correction update (
76
)–(
79
)
only depends on the predicted solution at a small number of quadrature points,
which are fully contained in the Cartesian product of
{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
with itself:

{\mathbb{X}}^{2}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}:={\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}\otimes{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}.

(97)

Therefore,
{\mathbb{X}}^{2}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}

contains a total of

Our goal is thus to enforce positivity at all the space-time points

Following the strategy developed by
Zhang and Shu
article:ZhangShu11
for the Runge-Kutta discontinuous Galerkin scheme, we apply the following procedure, which is applied element-by-element.

Step 1.

On the current space-time element defined over

(t,x)\in\left[t^{n},\,t^{n}+\Delta t\right]\times\left[x_{i}-\frac{\Delta x}{2},\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right],

the solution is given by (

minimum density, pressure, and modified kurtosis of this solution
over the points
(\tau,\xi)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}

\alpha_{i}^{(m)}:=\min_{(\tau,\xi)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{2}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau,\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i\,(:,m)}}}\right\},

(98)

for
m=1
(density),
m=3
(pressure), and
m=5
(modified kurtosis).

Step 2.

Rewrite the solution as

\begin{split}{\underline{\alpha}}^{\text{ST}}\left(t^{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(1+\tau),\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\,\xi;\,\theta\right):=(1-\theta)\,{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i\,(1,:)}}}+\theta\,{\underline{\Psi}}\left(\tau,\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i}}}},\end{split}

(99)

where
\theta\in[0,1]
, such that
\theta=1
recovers the original solution
(
65

\theta=0
results in reducing the entire solution
on to its space-time average. We now choose the largest possible
\theta\in[0,1]

so that (

m=1\,(\text{density}),\,3\,(\text{pressure}),\,5\,(\text{modified kurtosis})
at all the space-time points in
{\mathbb{X}}^{2}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
.
This is achieved by taking

\theta=\min_{m\in\left\{1,3,5\right\}}\min\left\{1,\frac{{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i\,(1,m)}}-\varepsilon}{{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i\,(1,m)}}-\alpha_{i}^{(m)}}\right\},

(100)

where
\varepsilon>0
is a preselected small constant (e.g., in this work
we select

5.3
Limiter II: Positivity-in-the-mean in the correction step

One of the key challenges in the correction step, as described by
(
76
)–(
79
)
is to make sure that the element
averages of the density, pressure, and modified kurtosis remain positive
at the end of the time-step:

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\rho\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}^{n+1}>0
,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mup\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}^{n+1}>0
, and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muk\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}^{n+1}>0
,
where the bar over each variable refers to the element average.
The prediction step limiter described in the previous subsection, §
5.2
, helps with this positivity-in-the-mean but cannot guarantee it. Furthermore, without positivity-in-the-mean, we cannot achieve positivity of the higher-order polynomial inside the element (i.e., if the polynomial average is negative, a significant portion of the polynomial must be negative inside the element). To overcome this challenge, we extend the approach developed by Moe et al.
article:MoRoSe17
, which, for the element averages, blends the high-order
update described by (
76
)–(
79
)
with a first-order Rusanov scheme. We have already proved in Theorem
5.1
that the Rusanov scheme is guaranteed to preserve positivity.

We begin by defining the Rusanov
article:Ru61
(aka local Lax-Friedrichs)
update based on the element averages at
t=t^{n}
:

{\underline{Q_{i}^{\text{Rus}}}}\,:=\,{\underline{Q^{n}_{i(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{\text{Rus}}}}-{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{\text{Rus}}}}\,\right),

(101)

where the numerical flux is given by

{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{\text{Rus}}}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i(1,:)}}}\right)+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i-1(1,:)}}}\right)\right]-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|{\lambda_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}\Bigr|\left({\underline{Q^{n}_{i(1,:)}}}-{\underline{Q^{n}_{i-1(1,:)}}}\right),

(102)

and
\Bigl|{\lambda_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}\Bigr|
is a local bound of the flux Jacobian spectral radius.
Recall that
{\underline{Q_{i}^{\text{Rus}}}}
is guaranteed to have positive
density, pressure, and modified kurtosis under a time-step restriction (see Theorem
5.1
).

Next, we write the update for the element averages of the high-order
method in terms of the low-order update (
101

{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,:)}}}={\underline{Q^{\text{Rus}}_{i\,(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\,\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\,{\underline{\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}}}-\theta_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\,{\underline{\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}}\,\right),

(103)

where the difference between the high and low-order fluxes is given by

{\underline{\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}}:={\underline{{\mathcal{F}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}}-{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}^{\text{Rus}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}}},

(104)

and
\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\in[0,1]
measures the amount of flux limiting, where
\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=0

represents maximal limiting (i.e., no high-order flux contributions) and

\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=1
represents no limiting (i.e., no low-order flux contributions).

The strategy for the positivity-in-the-mean limiter is then to find the
maximum
\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\in[0,1]
such that
\forall i

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\left({\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,:)}}}\right)>0,

(105)

for
\alpha\equiv\rho
(density),
\alpha\equiv p
(pressure),
and
\alpha\equiv k
(modified kurtosis), as defined in (
92

The strategy for achieving this is outlined below and
is applied element-by-element. The process begins
by initializing
\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}=1

\forall i
.

Step 1: (density)

Define

\Gamma:=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\left({Q^{\text{Rus}}_{i(1)}-\varepsilon}\right).

(106)

Set
\Lambda_{\text{left}}=\Lambda_{\text{right}}=1
(i.e., full high-order flux), but modify these if there is any potential for the density to decrease below
zero.

Case 1
.

If
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}(1)}<0
and
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}(1)}<0
, then

\Lambda_{\text{left}}=\Lambda_{\text{right}}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\Gamma}{\left|\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}(1)}\right|+\left|\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}(1)}\right|}\right\}.

(107)

Case 2
.

If
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}(1)}<0
and
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}(1)}>0
then

\Lambda_{\text{left}}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\Gamma}{\left|\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}(1)}\right|}\right\}.

(108)

Case 3
.

If
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}(1)}<0
and
\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}(1)}>0
then

\Lambda_{\text{right}}=\min\left\{1,\frac{\Gamma}{\left|\Delta{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}(1)}\right|}\right\}.

(109)

Step 2: (pressure)

Compute the Rusanov pressure (which is guaranteed to be positive):

p^{\text{Rus}}:={\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q^{\text{Rus}}_{i}}}\right).

(110)

Set
\mu_{11}=\mu_{10}=\mu_{01}=1
, but modify these if there is any potential for the pressure to decrease below zero.

Part 2A.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\Lambda_{\text{right}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}-\Lambda_{\text{left}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}\right),\quad p^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(111)

If
p^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then we set
\mu_{11}=\left({p^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon}\right)\bigl/\left(p^{\text{Rus}}-p^{\star}\right)
.

Part 2B.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{\text{left}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}},\quad p^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(112)

If
p^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then
\mu_{10}=\left({p^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon}\right)/\left(p^{\text{Rus}}-p^{\star}\right)
.

Part 2C.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{\text{right}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}},\quad p^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(113)

If
p^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then
\mu_{01}=\left(p^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon\right)/\left(p^{\text{Rus}}-p^{\star}\right)
.

Part 2D.

Set

\mu=\min\bigl\{\mu_{11},\mu_{10},\mu_{01}\bigr\},\quad\Lambda_{\text{left}}\leftarrow\mu\,\Lambda_{\text{left}},\quad\Lambda_{\text{right}}\leftarrow\mu\,\Lambda_{\text{right}}.

(114)

Step 3: (modified kurtosis)

Compute the Rusanov modified kurtosis (which is guaranteed to be positive):

k^{\text{Rus}}:={\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q^{\text{Rus}}_{i}}}\right).

(115)

Set
\mu_{11}=\mu_{10}=\mu_{01}=1
, but modify these if there is any potential for the pressure to decrease below zero.

Part 3A.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(\Lambda_{\text{right}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}-\Lambda_{\text{left}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}\right),\quad k^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(116)

If
k^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then we set
\mu_{11}=\left({k^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon}\right)\bigl/\left(k^{\text{Rus}}-k^{\star}\right)
.

Part 3B.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}+\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{\text{left}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}},\quad k^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(117)

If
k^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then
\mu_{10}=\left({k^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon}\right)/\left(k^{\text{Rus}}-k^{\star}\right)
.

Part 3C.

Set

{\underline{Q^{\star}}}={\underline{Q^{n}_{i\,(1,:)}}}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\Lambda_{\text{right}}\,{\underline{{\mathcal{F}}_{i+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}},\quad k^{\star}={\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q^{\star}}}\right).

(118)

If
k^{\star}<\varepsilon
, then
\mu_{01}=\left(p^{\text{Rus}}-\varepsilon\right)/\left(k^{\text{Rus}}-k^{\star}\right)
.

Part 3D.

Set

\mu=\min\bigl\{\mu_{11},\mu_{10},\mu_{01}\bigr\},\quad\Lambda_{\text{left}}\leftarrow\mu\,\Lambda_{\text{left}},\quad\Lambda_{\text{right}}\leftarrow\mu\,\Lambda_{\text{right}}.

(119)

Step 4:

Set

\theta_{i-\frac{1}{2}}\leftarrow\min\left\{\theta_{i-\frac{1}{2}},\,\Lambda_{\text{left}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}}\leftarrow\min\left\{\theta_{i+\frac{1}{2}},\,\Lambda_{\text{right}}\right\}.

(120)

In all of the above formulas, we select in this work:
\varepsilon=10^{-14}
.

5.4
Limiter III: Positivity-at-points in the correction step

Once we have ensured that the element averages are positive, we then look to enforce positivity of the corrected solution at spatial quadrature points:
\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
as defined by (
96
). Following the ideas developed by
Zhang and Shu
article:ZhangShu11
for the Runge-Kutta discontinuous Galerkin scheme, we aim to find the maximum
\theta\in[0,1]
such that

{\underline{q}}^{h}\left(t^{n+1},\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\,\xi;\theta\right):=\left(1-\theta\right)\,{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,:)}}}+\theta\,{\underline{\Phi}}(\xi)^{T}\,{\underline{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i}}}}

(121)

is positive at all points
\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
for every space
element
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
.
As in the prediction step limiter from §
5.2
,
\theta=0
means that the solution is limited fully down to its element average, while
\theta=1
means that no limiting is needed and the full high-order approximation can be used.
We apply the following procedure element-by-element.

Step 1.

On the current element defined over

x\in\left[x_{i}-\frac{\Delta x}{2},\,x_{i}+\frac{\Delta x}{2}\right],

the solution is given by (
59
). Find the
minimum density
\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
(see (
96
)) and compute the corresponding damping parameter
(
\theta
):

\rho_{i}^{\text{min}}:=\min_{\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{{\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,1)}}}\right\},\quad\theta=\min\left\{1,\frac{{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,1)}}-\varepsilon}{{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,1)}}-\rho_{i}^{{\text{min}}}}\right\}.

(122)

Finally, rescale the higher-order coefficients using the above
calculated damping parameter (
\theta
):

{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\leftarrow\theta\,{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\quad\forall\ell=2,\ldots,{M_{\text{C}}}.

(123)

Step 2.

Now that the density is positive
\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
, we repeat
Step 1
for the pressure. That is,
we find the average pressure and the minimum pressure
\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
:

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}:={\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,:)}}}\right),\quad p_{i}^{\text{min}}:=\min_{\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i}}}}\right)\right\},

(124)

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{p}\left({\underline{q}}\right)
is defined in (
92
).
From here, we compute the corresponding damping parameter and rescale the higher-order coefficients:

\theta=\min\left\{1,\frac{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}-\varepsilon}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muP\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}-p_{i}^{{\text{min}}}}\right\},\quad{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\leftarrow\theta\,{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\quad\forall\ell=2,\ldots,{M_{\text{C}}}.

(125)

Step 3.

Now that both density and pressure are positive
\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
, we repeat
Step 2
for the modified kurtosis. That is, we find the average modified kurtosis and the minimum modified kurtosis
\forall\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
:

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muK\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}:={\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(1,:)}}}\right),\quad k_{i}^{\text{min}}:=\min_{\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi\right)^{T}{\underline{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i}}}}\right)\right\},

(126)

where
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\left({\underline{q}}\right)
is defined in (
92
).
From here, we compute the corresponding damping parameter and rescale the higher-order coefficients:

\theta=\min\left\{1,\frac{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muK\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}-\varepsilon}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muK\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i}-k_{i}^{{\text{min}}}}\right\},\quad{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\leftarrow\theta\,{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(:,\ell)}}}\quad\forall\ell=2,\ldots,{M_{\text{C}}}.

(127)

In all the formulas presented above we use
\varepsilon=10^{-14}
.

5.5
Limiter IV: Unphysical oscillation limiter

The previously described limiters guarantee positivity for
\rho
,
p
, and
k
, but there still may be unphysical oscillations near shocks, rarefactions, or large gradients. We augment the method with one more limiter to eliminate these oscillations: a variant of the strategy developed in Moe et al.
article:Moe15
. This limiter is applied once per time step and can remove unphysical oscillations without overly diffusing the numerical solution.
We apply the following procedure.

Step 1.

Loop over each element
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
and compute the minimum and maximum values of all of the following variables:
w^{\ell}\in\left\{\rho,u,p,h,r\right\}
:

w^{\ell}_{M_{i}}:=\max_{\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{w^{\ell}\left({\underline{q^{\Delta x}}}(\xi)\right)\Bigl|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}\right\},\quad w^{\ell}_{m_{i}}:=\min_{\xi\in{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}}\left\{w^{\ell}\left({\underline{q^{\Delta x}}}(\xi)\right)\Bigl|_{{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}\right\},

(128)

for all
\ell=1,2,3,4,5
. Here
{\mathbb{X}}_{{M_{\text{O}}}}
is taken to be the
{M_{\text{O}}}
roots of the
{M_{\text{O}}}^{\text{th}}

Legendre polynomial (i.e., Gauss-Legendre points) plus the element ends points (see

eq.

96
).

Step 2.

Compute upper and
lower bounds over all neighborhoods,
N_{{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}:=\left\{{\mathcal{T}}_{i-1},{\mathcal{T}}_{i},\,{\mathcal{T}}_{i+1}\right\}
:

\begin{split}{M^{\ell}_{i}}&={\max\left\{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\ell}_{i}+{\mathcal{A}}_{0}h^{1.5},\,\displaystyle\max_{j\in N_{{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}}\left\{w^{\ell}_{M_{j}}\right\}\right\}},\\
{m^{\ell}_{i}}&={\min\left\{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\ell}_{i}-{\mathcal{A}}_{0}h^{1.5},\,\displaystyle\min_{j\in N_{{\mathcal{T}}_{i}}}\left\{w^{\ell}_{m_{j}}\right\}\right\}},\end{split}

(129)

where
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muw\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\ell}_{i}
are the element averages of each variable, and
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}h^{1.5}
is used to offset these averages
to recover high-order accuracy for smooth solutions in the limit
h\rightarrow 0
(see Moe et al.
article:Moe15
for more details).

Step 3.

On each element
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
, compute the largest damping parameters between
[0,1]
that guarantee that the high-order solution in
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}

does not violate the maximum and minimum bounds defined by (
129
):

\theta=\min\left\{1,\,\mu\cdot\min_{\ell}\left\{\frac{M^{\ell}_{i}-\bar{w}^{\ell}_{i}}{w^{\ell}_{M_{i}}-\bar{w}^{\ell}_{i}}\right\},\,\mu\cdot\min_{\ell}\left\{\frac{m^{\ell}_{i}-\bar{w}^{\ell}_{i}}{w^{\ell}_{m_{i}}-\bar{w}^{\ell}_{i}}\right\}\right\},

(130)

where the factor
\mu=10/11
is introduced to slightly increase the aggressiveness of the limiter (again, see Moe et al.
article:Moe15
for more details).

Step 4.

On each element
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
, limit the conserved variables:

{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(\ell,:)}}}\leftarrow\theta\,{\underline{Q^{n+1}_{i\,(\ell,:)}}}\quad\forall\ell=2,\ldots,{M_{\text{C}}}.

(131)

6
Collisionless HyQMOM numerical examples

In this section, we apply the proposed scheme and the corresponding limiters to several test cases.
In §
6.1
, we verify the claimed orders of accuracy on a smooth exact solution. In §
6.2
and §
6.3
we apply the scheme to
shock tube
initial data. These results demonstrate the ability of the
non-oscillatory limiter to control unphysical oscillations.
Finally, in §
6.4
, we fully validate the positivity limiters by applying the scheme to piecewise constant initial data that lead to the formation of a vacuum.
This example demonstrates the ability of the positivity limiters to prevent negative states in density, pressure, and modified kurtosis, both on the element average and on the solution values internal to the element.
In all the cases presented in §
6.2
,
§
6.3
, and §
6.4
, we compare the high-order scheme against a highly-resolved first-order Rusanov scheme that is guaranteed to be positivity-preserving without the need for any limiters.

(134)

exact solution.

{M_{\text{O}}}+1

{M_{\text{O}}}

(135)

20

(136)

20

{\underline{q}}^{\star}
is the exact solution. Gaussian quadrature rules have been tabulated in many books and websites; we obtained our data from
web:gauss_quad

135

7
Extension to HyQMOM-BGK

Up to this point, we have only considered the HyQMOM approximation applied to the Vlasov model
eq.

5
; this allowed us to study the mathematical structure of HyQMOM and
to develop accurate high-order methods and limiters. On the other hand, the practicality of HyQMOM is not for solving
collisionless kinetic models since, in this regime, it would be far better to directly solve the Vlasov equation with Lagrangian or semi-Lagrangian approaches. Instead, the true benefit of the HyQMOM approximation is in the approximation of kinetic systems near thermodynamic equilibrium – a regime we study in this section.

In this section, we extend the previously developed numerical method to HyQMOM with a BGK collision operator. Importantly, we develop this extension so that the resulting HyQMOM solver adheres to the following two key design parameters:

1.

The method should remain high-order accurate irrespective of the Knudsen number:
\varepsilon>0
.

2.

For fixed mesh parameters (i.e., fixed
\Delta x
and
\Delta t
), the method should remain stable in the singular
limit:
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. This property is often referred to as the
asymptotic-preserving
(AP) property, and a variety of schemes with this property can be found in the literature (e.g.,
see
article:Ben08
;
article:Caflish1997
;
article:Coron1991
;
article:Gabetta1997
;
article:Jin1995
;
article:Jin99
;
article:Jin12
;
article:Jin1996
;
article:Pieraccini2007
;
article:Xiong2017
).

The specific approach we detail in this section is novel and directly relies on the prediction and correction format of the
method developed in
section

4
.

7.1
1D1V Boltzmann-BGK equation

Consider the 1D1V Boltzmann-BGK equation
article:BGK54
:

f_{,t}+vf_{,x}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left({\mathcal{M}}-f\right),

(141)

where
\varepsilon>0
is the Knudsen number, which is a non-dimensional ratio
of the particle mean-free path to a characteristic length scale,
and
{\mathcal{M}}(t,x,v):\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

is the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution:

{\mathcal{M}}(t,x,v):=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{(v-u)^{2}}{2T}}.

(142)

In this expression,
\rho
is density,
p
is pressure, and
T=p/\rho
is temperature (e.g., see
definitions (
10
)). For
\varepsilon\gg 1
, and for a fixed
t
and
x
,
the collision operator is weak, and the solution behaves similarly to Vlasov equation (
5
). For
\varepsilon\ll 1
, and for a fixed
t
and
x
, the BGK collision operator forces
f
towards the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution (i.e., thermodynamic equilibrium):

f(t,x,v)\rightarrow{\mathcal{M}}(t,x,v)+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon).

(143)

7.2
HyQMOM-BGK and the asymptotic-preserving property

Relevant in this work are the first five moments of (
141
) with the
HyQMOM moment-closure (
36
):

\displaystyle{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{f}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)_{,x}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,{\underline{S^{\text{cons}}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right),\qquad{\underline{\alpha}}_{,t}+{\underline{\underline{B}}}\left({\underline{\alpha}}\right)\,{\underline{\alpha}}_{,x}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,{\underline{S^{\text{prim}}}}\left({\underline{\alpha}}\right),

(144)

where only the fourth and fifth components of the source terms are nonzero:

S^{\text{cons}}_{4}=S^{\text{prim}}_{4}=-h,\quad S^{\text{cons}}_{5}=-k+\frac{2p^{2}}{\rho}-\frac{4puh+h^{2}}{p},\quad S^{\text{prim}}_{5}=-k+\frac{2p^{2}}{\rho}+\frac{h^{2}}{p}.

(145)

In
eq.

144
we are using definitions (
36
),
(
38
), and (
39
). For
\varepsilon\ll 1
, and for a fixed
t
and
x
, the BGK collision operator forces the heat flux,
h
, and modified kurtosis,
k
, towards their Maxwell-Boltzmann values:

h(t,x)=0+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)\qquad\text{and}\qquad k(t,x)=\frac{2p(t,x)^{2}}{\rho(t,x)}+{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon).

(146)

In particular, in the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
limit, solutions of the HyQMOM-BGK system converge to solutions
of the 1D compressible Euler equations at a convergence
rate of
{\mathcal{O}}\left(\varepsilon\right)
:

\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\end{bmatrix}_{,t}+\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
\rho u^{3}+3pu\end{bmatrix}_{,x}={\underline{0}},

(147)

where
h\equiv 0
and
k\equiv\frac{2p}{\rho}
. Furthermore,
by including the next order term in the Chapman-Enskog expansion, one can show that solutions to HyQMOM-BGK converge
to solutions of the 1D Navier-Stokes
equations at a convergence rate of

{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{2})

article:Bardos1991
:

\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\end{bmatrix}_{,t}+\begin{bmatrix}\rho u\\
\rho u^{2}+p\\
\rho u^{3}+3pu\end{bmatrix}_{,x}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon pT_{,x}\end{bmatrix}_{,x},

(148)

where
T=\frac{p}{\rho}
and

q\equiv-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon pT_{,x}
.

Definition 4
(Asymptotic-preserving (AP) property

)

Let
{\underline{q^{(\Delta t,\Delta x)}}}(t,x;\varepsilon)
be an approximation to the exact solution of

eq.

144
–
eq.

145
as
computed by a numerical method with mesh parameters
\Delta t,\Delta x>0
. We assume that for a fixed
\varepsilon>0
, this
method is convergent to the exact solution of
eq.

144
–
eq.

145
.
This numerical method is said to be asymptotic-preserving (AP) provided that the vanishing mesh parameter limit,
\Delta t,\Delta x\rightarrow 0^{+}
, and the vanishing
Knudsen number limit,
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
, commute:

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[\lim_{\Delta t,\Delta x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\,{\underline{q^{(\Delta t,\Delta x)}}}(t,x;\varepsilon)\right]=\lim_{\Delta t,\Delta x\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\,{\underline{q^{(\Delta t,\Delta x)}}}(t,x;\varepsilon)\right].

Practically, this means that an AP scheme remains stable and accurate for a fixed mesh,
\Delta t
, and
\Delta x
,
for all
\varepsilon>0
, including in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

The goal of this section is to develop an extension of the Lax-Wendroff DG scheme
developed in
section

4
and
section

for the HyQMOM-BGK system

eq.

–
eq.

that behaves, on the discrete

eq.

147
and
eq.

148
in the singular limit

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
The key innovation in this work is that we make use of the prediction-correction formulation of Lax-Wendroff DG
to incorporate the collision operator.

7.3
Prediction

To describe the HyQMOM-BGK prediction step, it is first useful
to define the following matrices that allow us to map Legendre coefficients to nodal space-time Gauss-Legendre quadrature points
and back again:

\displaystyle C^{1}_{(a,b)}

\displaystyle:=\Psi_{b}\left(\tau_{a},\,\xi_{a}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle{\underline{\underline{C^{1}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{O}}^{2}\times{M_{\text{P}}}},

(149)

\displaystyle C^{2}_{(b,a)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\omega_{a}}{4}\,\Psi_{b}\left(\tau_{a},\,\xi_{a}\right),\qquad

\displaystyle{\underline{\underline{C^{2}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{P}}}\times M_{\text{O}}^{2}},

(150)

where
\omega_{a}
and
\left(\tau_{a},\,\xi_{a}\right)
for
a=1,\ldots,M_{\text{O}}^{2}

are tensor product Gauss-Legendre weights and abscissas. These
two matrices satisfy

{\underline{\underline{C^{2}}}}\,{\underline{\underline{C^{1}}}}={\underline{\underline{\,\,{\mathbb{I}}\,\,}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{P}}}\times{M_{\text{P}}}}.

(151)

HyQMOM consists of five evolution equations, and the first three are unaffected by the collision operator; therefore, the update inside the Picard iteration for the three collision invariants (i.e., density, macroscopic velocity, and pressure) remains the same as in the collisionless case:

eq.

73
.
On the other hand, the update for the heat flux,
h
, has a non-zero BGK contribution; however, the BGK term is linear in the heat flux
(see
eqs.

144
and
145
), which allows for simple treatment. The strategy we pursue here is to include the BGK source term in the implicit portion of the Picard update to remain uniformly stable in
\varepsilon>0
. After simple algebra, we arrive at the following update for the heat flux,
h
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\,{\underline{\underline{I}}}+\varepsilon\,{\underline{\underline{L}}}\,\right)\,{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}(j)}_{i\,(:,4)}}}=\,&\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{a=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\sum_{b=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\,\omega_{a}\,\omega_{b}\,{\underline{{\Psi}}}\left(\mu_{b},\,\mu_{a}\right)\,{\Theta}_{(a,b,4)}\\
+&\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{b=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\omega_{b}\,{\underline{{\Psi}}}\left(-1,\xi_{b}\right)\,{\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi_{b}\right)^{T}{\underline{A^{n}_{i\,(:,4)}}},\end{split}

(152)

where we are using the short-hand:

{\Theta}_{(a,b,m)}:={\Theta}_{m}\left({\underline{\Psi}}\left(\mu_{b},\mu_{a}\right)^{T}{\underline{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}(j-1)}_{i}}}}\right).

(153)

The update for the final primitive variable,
k
(modified kurtosis),
requires more work. The source term shown in
eqs.

144
and
145
is linear in
k
, but it also includes nonlinear terms from three previously updated quantities:
\rho
,
p
, and
h
. To construct these nonlinear quantities, we first apply the mapping from Legendre to nodal values via

eq.

149
, and then evaluate the nonlinear portion of the source:

\displaystyle m=1,3,4:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\underline{\widehat{W}_{(:,m)}}}={\underline{\underline{C^{1}}}}\,{\underline{W_{i(:,m)}^{n+\frac{1}{2}(j)}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{O}}^{2}},

(154)

\displaystyle a=1,\ldots,M_{\text{O}}^{2}:

\displaystyle\left\{\rho_{a},p_{a},h_{a}\right\}=\widehat{W}_{(a,\{1,3,4\})},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{\,(j)}_{ia}=\frac{2p_{a}^{2}}{\rho_{a}}+\frac{h_{a}^{2}}{p_{a}}.

From here, the update for the modified kurtosis inside the Picard iteration looks very similar to the update for heat flux (see
eq.

152
), but with the additional nonlinear terms computed from
eq.

154
, which now need to be mapped back to Legendre coefficients via
eq.

150
.
After some simple algebra, the update takes the following form:

\begin{split}\left(\,\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\underline{\underline{I}}}+\varepsilon\,{\underline{\underline{L}}}\,\right)\,{\underline{W^{n+\frac{1}{2}(j)}_{i\,(:,5)}}}=\,&\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{a=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\sum_{b=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\,\omega_{a}\,\omega_{b}\,{\underline{{\Psi}}}\left(\mu_{b},\,\mu_{a}\right)\,{\Theta}_{(a,b,5)}\\
+&\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\sum_{b=1}^{{M_{\text{O}}}}\omega_{b}\,{\underline{{\Psi}}}\left(-1,\xi_{b}\right)\,{\underline{\Phi}}\left(\xi_{b}\right)^{T}{\underline{A^{n}_{i\,(:,5)}}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\,{\underline{\underline{C^{2}}}}\,\,{\underline{\widehat{\mathcal{S}}^{(j)}_{i}}}.\end{split}

(155)

7.4
Post-prediction BGK source evaluation

Once the Picard iterations are complete and all five primitive variables have been predicted, there is one final computation that must be completed to prepare us for the correction step: we need to evaluate and project the BGK source term,
{\underline{S^{\text{cons}}}}
, from
eq.

145
. This is done similar to
eq.

154
by first mapping the predicted solution from Legendre to nodal values via

eq.

149
,
then evaluating the source components at nodal values, and finally mapping back to Legendre coefficients via
eq.

\displaystyle m=1,\ldots,5:

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\underline{\widehat{W}_{(:,m)}}}={\underline{\underline{C^{1}}}}\,{\underline{W_{i(:,m)}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{\text{O}}^{2}},

(156)

\displaystyle a=1,\ldots,M_{\text{O}}^{2}:

\displaystyle\left\{\rho_{a},\,u_{a},\,p_{a},\,h_{a},\,k_{a}\right\}=\widehat{W}_{(a,1:5)},

\displaystyle\widehat{\Delta{\mathcal{M}}}_{(a,4)}=-h_{a},\,\,\,\,\,\widehat{\Delta{\mathcal{M}}}_{(a,5)}=-k_{a}+\frac{2p_{a}^{2}}{\rho_{a}}-\frac{4p_{a}u_{a}h_{a}+h_{a}^{2}}{p_{a}},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\underline{\Delta{\mathcal{M}}_{i(:,m)}}}={\underline{\underline{C^{2}}}}\,{\underline{\widehat{\Delta{\mathcal{M}}}_{(:,m)}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{{M_{\text{P}}}}.

In the above expressions, we use the notation

to signify that these BGK source terms are, in fact, measuring the deviations in the heat flux,

9
Conclusions

In this work, we considered a particular moment closure called HyQMOM (the hyperbolic quadrature-based method of moments), which was originally introduced by Fox, Laurent, Vie
article:FoxLauVie2018
and further studied by Johnson
thesis:Johnson2017
and Wiersma
thesis:Wiersma2019
.
Quadrature-based method of moments (QMOM), including the HyQMOM variant, are a promising class of approximation techniques for reducing kinetic equations to fluid equations that are valid beyond thermodynamic equilibrium.
In particular, the goal of the present work was to develop high-order discontinuous Galerkin schemes and corresponding limiters that control both unphysical oscillations and eliminate positivity violations.

The numerical scheme developed is based on the Lax-Wendroff discontinuous Galerkin scheme
introduced by Qiu, Dumbser, and Shu
article:Qiu05
, with the predictor-corrector interpretation developed by Gassner et al.
article:GasDumHinMun2011
, and further
refinements developed by Felton et al.
article:REU2018
.
The resulting numerical method is performed in two phases at each time step.

Prediction step.

The equation and numerical solution are written in the primitive variables in this phase. In the space-time DG approximation, which is applied to each element, integration-by-parts is only performed on the time variable. The result is a system of local nonlinear equations on each element. These equations are solved using a Picard iteration, which provides a sufficiently accurate solution after
{M_{\text{O}}}-1
iterations, where
{M_{\text{O}}}
is the order of accuracy of the method.

Correction step.

The correction is a straightforward explicit update based on the time-integral of the evolution equation in conservation form, where the space-time prediction replaces all instances of the exact solution.

Several limiters were applied to the scheme to guarantee positivity and achieve solutions without unphysical oscillations.

Limiter I: Prediction step positivity limiter.

This limiter is completely local and minimally damps high-order corrections to the primitive variables to get pointwise positivity of the predicted density, pressure, and modified kurtosis on all space-time quadrature points (Gauss-Legendre + edges). The limiter is applied once after each Picard iteration, meaning it is applied a total of
{M_{\text{O}}}-1
times per time step.

Limiter II: Correction step positivity limiter on cell average.

This limiter is applied once per time step and blends high-order numerical fluxes with positivity preserving low-order fluxes in such a way as to preserve the positivity of the corrected element averages of density, pressure, and modified kurtosis. This limiter is applied once per time step.

Limiter III: Correction step positivity limiter on quadrature points.

This limiter is similar to Limiter I and involves minimally damping the high-order corrections to preserve the positivity of the corrected density, pressure, and modified kurtosis, on all spatial quadrature points (Gauss-Legendre + edges). This limiter is applied once per time step at the end of the step.

Limiter IV: Oscillation Limiter.

This limiter damps the solution if the primitive solution variables on the current element significantly exceed the primitive solution variables on neighboring
elements. This limiter is applied once per time step at the end of the time step.

In the collisionless regime, the proposed high-order method and the limiting strategy were tested on both smooth and Riemann problems. The smooth solution was used to perform convergence tests that demonstrated the expected orders of accuracy. The Riemann data tests clearly showed that the limiters were successful in damping unphysical oscillations without adversely diffusing the solution and preserving the positivity of the relevant variables.

Once the collisionless method is fully developed, we propose a version of the scheme for HyQMOM with a BGK collision operator. We carefully show how to handle the collision operator in both the prediction and collision steps to achieve an asymptotic-preserving (AP) property in the
high-collision limit. Several numerical examples are provided to validate the scheme both for smooth solutions and Riemann initial data.
The asymptotic-preserving property is validated for smooth solutions and Riemann initial data.

Future work will focus on extending this work to higher dimensions; perhaps using the conditional moment strategy of

article:FoxLauVie2018
;
article:PaDeFo2019
, or some other higher-dimensional extension.

\displaystyle Q_{2N-1}(sv)=\sum_{{j}=0}^{2N-1}b_{{j}}s^{j}v^{j}=b_{0}+\left(b_{1}s\right)v+\cdots+\left(b_{2N-1}s^{2N-1}\right)v^{2N-1}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle Q_{2N-1}(s)=\sum_{{j}=0}^{2N-1}b_{{j}}{\mathcal{R}}_{j}(s)={\underline{b}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}}}(s),

which when combined with (
186
) gives the desired result.

\blacksquare

Theorem A.1
(Weak hyperbolicity and linear degeneracy of QMOM)

The classical quadrature-based moment (QMOM) closure for a fixed
N\in{\mathbb{N}}_{\geq 1}
, denoted
by
eq.

21
, leads to a system of partial
differential equations that has the following quasilinear form:

{\underline{q}}_{,t}+{\underline{\underline{A}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)\,{\underline{q}}_{,x},\qquad\text{where}\quad{\underline{q}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathsf{M}}_{0},\,{\mathsf{M}}_{1},\,\cdots,\,{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-2},\,{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}\end{bmatrix},

(189)

where the flux Jacobian matrix is given by

{\underline{\underline{A}}}\left({\underline{q}}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]0&1&&&\\
&0&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&&0&1\\
\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}&\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N-2}}&\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\end{bmatrix},

(190)

where
{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}
is given by (
22
).
System (
189
) and (
190
)
is weakly hyperbolic for any integer
N\geq 1
with the following properties:

1.

The eigenvalues of (
190
)
are
\lambda_{\ell}=\mu_{\ell}
for
{\ell}=1,2,3,\ldots,N
, where
\mu_{\ell}
are the abscissas in (
21
);

2.

Every eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity exactly two;

3.

Every eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity exactly one; and

4.

Every wave in the system is linearly degenerate:
\frac{\partial\lambda_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}=0
for
{\ell}=1,2,3,\ldots,N
, where
\left(\lambda_{\ell},{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}\right)
is an eigenvalue-eigenvector pair of flux Jacobian (
190
).

Proof

The key to understanding the eigenvalues of flux Jacobian (
190
)
is to understand the last row. To this end, consider:

{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}=\sum_{{j}=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{2N}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}=\sum_{{j}=1}^{N}\left[\mu_{j}^{2N}\frac{\partial\omega_{j}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}+2N\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{2N-1}\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}\right].

(191)

To make sense of this we need to obtain expressions for the partial derivatives of the
quadrature weights and abscissas with respect to the moments. To this end, we compute
the related quantities:

\displaystyle{\mathsf{M}}_{s}=\sum_{{j}=1}^{N}\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{s}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{s}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}=\sum_{{j}=1}^{N}\left[\mu_{j}^{s}\frac{\partial\omega_{j}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}+s\omega_{j}\mu_{j}^{s-1}\frac{\partial\mu_{j}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}}\right]=\delta^{s}_{\ell},

(192)

where
s,\ell=0,1,\ldots,2N-1
and
\delta^{s}_{\ell}
is the Kronecker delta, which
arises due to the fact that
{\mathsf{M}}_{\ell}
and
{\mathsf{M}}_{s}
are independent variables if
s\neq\ell
.
The expression in (
192
) can be written in matrix form to obtain the
following result:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]\frac{\partial\omega_{1}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\omega_{1}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\omega_{N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial\omega_{N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\\
\omega_{1}\frac{\partial\mu_{1}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}&\cdots&\omega_{1}\frac{\partial\mu_{1}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\omega_{N}\frac{\partial\mu_{N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}&\cdots&\omega_{N}\frac{\partial\mu_{N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]1&\mu_{1}&\mu_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\mu_{1}^{2N-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&\mu_{N}&\mu_{N}^{2}&\cdots&\mu_{N}^{2N-1}\\
0&1&2\mu_{1}&\cdots&(2N-1)\mu_{1}^{2N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&1&2\mu_{N}&\cdots&(2N-1)\mu_{N}^{2N-2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}.

(193)

Using this result in (
191
) produces expressions for the
last row of flux Jacobian (
190
):

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{0}}\\
\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{1}}\\
\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-2}}\\
\frac{\partial{\mathsf{M}}^{\star}_{2N}}{\partial{\mathsf{M}}_{2N-1}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]1&\mu_{1}&\cdots&\mu_{1}^{2N-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&\mu_{N}&\cdots&\mu_{N}^{2N-1}\\
0&1&\cdots&(2N-1)\mu_{1}^{2N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&1&\cdots&(2N-1)\mu_{N}^{2N-2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]\mu_{1}^{2N}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{N}^{2N}\\
2N\mu_{1}^{2N-1}\\
\vdots\\
2N\mu_{N}^{2N-1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}[1.5]a_{0}\\
\vdots\\
a_{N-1}\\
a_{N}\\
\vdots\\
a_{2N-1}\end{bmatrix},

(194)

where the last equality follows from Lemma
2
and
a_{j}
for
j=0,1,\ldots,2N-1
are
the coefficients of the Hermite interpolating polynomial defined through
(
175
) and (
176
).

Next we attempt to directly compute the eigenvalues of the flux Jacobian:

\left|{\underline{\underline{A}}}-v{\underline{\underline{\mathbb{I}}}}\right|=\begin{vmatrix}[1.5]-v&\quad 1\\
&\quad\ddots&\quad\ddots\\
&&-v&1\\
a_{0}&\quad\cdots&\quad a_{2N-2}&\quad\left(a_{2N-1}-v\right)\end{vmatrix}=v^{2N}-\sum_{{j}=0}^{2N-1}a_{j}v^{{j}}.

(195)

Using a classical result from Hermite polynomial interpolation, we can write
the right-most term in the above expression as follows
(e.g., see Theorem 6.4 on page 190 of Süli and Mayer
book:suli2003
):

\left|{\underline{\underline{A}}}-v{\underline{\underline{\mathbb{I}}}}\right|=\left(v-\mu_{1}\right)^{2}\left(v-\mu_{2}\right)^{2}\cdots\left(v-\mu_{N}\right)^{2}.

(196)

This proves the first two claims of the theorem: (1) the eigenvalues are the
quadrature abscissas, and (2) each eigenvalue has algebraic multiplicity exactly two.

Next we look at the eigenvectors. For example, the
{\ell}^{\text{th}}
eigenvector
for each
{\ell}=1,2,\ldots,N
satisfies the relationship:

\left({\underline{\underline{A}}}-\mu_{\ell}\,{\underline{\underline{\mathbb{I}}}}\right){\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}={\underline{0}}.

(197)

By inspection, we see that
{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}\not\equiv{\underline{0}}
if and only if
the first component of
{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}
is not zero. Without loss of generality the
first component is taken to be unity, and then by inspection we note that
the only eigenvector associated to eigenvalue
v=\mu_{\ell}
must be

{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}=\left(1,\,\mu_{\ell},\,\mu_{\ell}^{2},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{\ell}^{2N-1}\right)^{T}.

(198)

This proves the third claim of the theorem: (3)
each eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity exactly one. Since the geometric multiplicity for
each eigenvalue is strictly less than the algebraic multiplicity, system (
189
) and (
190
) is weakly hyperbolic for any integer
N\geq 1
.

The final claim in the theorem is that each wave is linearly degenerate. Proving this requires
us to investigate the following dot product

\frac{\partial\mu_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}.

(199)

Invoking Lemmas (
3
) and (
4
) shows that
for every
{\ell}=1,2,\ldots,N
:

\frac{\partial\mu_{\ell}}{\partial{\underline{q}}}\cdot{\underline{{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}}}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\mu_{\ell}}\left\{\frac{\left(s-\mu_{\ell}\right)}{\omega_{\ell}}\left[{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq{\ell}\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(s-\mu_{j}\right)^{2}}\middle/{\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq{\ell}\end{subarray}}^{N}\left(\mu_{\ell}-\mu_{j}\right)^{2}}\right]\right\}=0,

(200)

which proves that every wave for
{\ell}=1,2,\ldots,N
is linearly degenerate.

\blacksquare

Lemma 5
(Convexity property)

Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\left({\underline{Q}}\right):\mathbb{R}^{5}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a convex function. Then
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

a\geq 0,\quad b\geq 0,\quad 1-a-b\geq 0,

(201)

the following inequality holds:

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl((1-a-b){\underline{P}}+a{\underline{Q}}+b{\underline{R}}\Bigr)\leq(1-a-b){\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{P}}\Bigr)+a{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{Q}}\Bigr)+b{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{R}}\Bigr)\quad\forall{\underline{P}},{\underline{Q}},{\underline{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}.

(202)

Proof

By definition, the function
{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}
is convex
if and only if
the following is true for all
\theta\in[0,1]
:

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl(\bigl(1-\theta\bigr){\underline{P}}+\theta{\underline{Q}}\Bigr)\leq\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\,{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{P}}\Bigr)+\theta\,{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{Q}}\Bigr)\quad\forall{\underline{P}},{\underline{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}.

(203)

Consider the convex function applied to a sum of three vectors of the following form:

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl((1-a-b){\underline{P}}+a{\underline{Q}}+b{\underline{R}}\Bigr)\quad\text{where}\quad a,b,(1-a-b)\geq 0.

(204)

We can temporarily define the following vector:

{\underline{S}}:=\biggl(\frac{a}{a+b}\biggr)\,{\underline{Q}}+\biggl(\frac{b}{a+b}\biggr)\,{\underline{R}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a{\underline{Q}}+b{\underline{R}}=(a+b){\underline{S}},

(205)

such that

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl((1-a-b){\underline{P}}+a{\underline{Q}}+b{\underline{R}}\Bigr)={\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl((1-a-b){\underline{P}}+(a+b){\underline{S}}\Bigr).

(206)

Invoking the convexity definition (
203
) with
\theta=a+b
, which by assumption satisfies

\theta\in[0,1]
, we have that

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl((1-a-b){\underline{P}}+a{\underline{Q}}+b{\underline{R}}\Bigr)\leq(1-a-b){\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{P}}\Bigr)+(a+b){\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Biggl(\biggl(\frac{a}{a+b}\biggr){\underline{Q}}+\biggl(\frac{b}{a+b}\biggr){\underline{R}}\Biggr).

(207)

We then again invoke definition (
203
), this time with
\theta=\frac{b}{a+b}
, which also satisfies

\theta\in[0,1]
, to get that

{\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Biggl(\biggl(\frac{a}{a+b}\biggr){\underline{Q}}+\biggl(\frac{b}{a+b}\biggr){\underline{R}}\Biggr)\leq\biggl(\frac{a}{a+b}\biggr){\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{Q}}\Bigr)+\biggl(\frac{b}{a+b}\biggr){\mathcal{C}}_{\alpha}\Bigl({\underline{R}}\Bigr).

(208)

Combining the last two inequalities, (
207
) and (
208
), results in desired inequality: (
202
).

\blacksquare
