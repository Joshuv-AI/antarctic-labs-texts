Universal bifurcations to explosive synchronization for networks of coupled oscillators with higher-order interactions
Lauren D. Smith, Penghao Liu

Abstract
We determine critical parameter sets for transitions from gradual to explosive synchronization in coupled oscillator networks with higher-order coupling using self-consistency analysis. We obtain analytic bifurcation values for generic symmetric natural frequency distributions. We show that non-synchronized, drifting, oscillators are non-negligible, and play a crucial role in bifurcation. As such, the entire natural frequency distribution must be accounted for, rather than just the shape at the center. We verify our results for Lorentzian and Gaussian distributed natural frequencies.

Here we determine critical parameter sets for general natural frequency distributions at which the onset of synchronization bifurcates from a gradual, smooth, transition, to an abrupt, explosive, transition. We do this using a self-consistency approach akin to that of Kuramoto’s

As such, the entirety of the frequency distribution must be accounted for. We verify that our methodology recovers known formulae for Lorentzian distributed frequencies, and uncover analytic bifurcation values for Gaussian distributed frequencies.

Having derived the self-consistency equations, we use them to determine bifurcations in the type of synchronization at onset. We use the general principle described in Kuehn & Bick

In summary, we have found critical synchronization transitions for generic symmetric natural frequency distributions via a self-consistency approach. Our analysis shows that drifters play a crucial role in controlling synchronization bifurcations, and so the whole natural frequency distribution must be accounted for. This is unlike most other self-consistency analyses, in which the drifters can be neglected, and, hence, bifurcations are determined exclusively by the shape of the distribution at its center. We have shown the efficacy of our methodology for both Lorentzian and Gaussian natural frequency distributions.

Our methodology can be readily extended to a broad class of possible higher-order interactions, with the only requirement being that the mean-field dynamics can be written in the form (
4
) for some
\gamma
that encodes the relevant Daido order parameters and their weightings.

Appendix A: Quantifying the effect of drifters

Repeating equation (
10
) from the main text, the effect of drifters on the order parameter
r_{k}
is

r_{k}^{d}=\int_{|\omega|>\gamma}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(k\theta)g(\omega)\rho(\theta,\omega)d\theta d\omega.

We define

\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(k\theta)\rho(\theta,\omega)d\theta,

(23)

and will show that this is an odd function if
k
is odd and an even function if
k
is even. It then follows that

\displaystyle r_{k}^{d}

\displaystyle=\int_{|\omega|>\gamma}\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)g(\omega)d\omega

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&k\in\text{odd},\\
2\int_{\gamma}^{\infty}\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)g(\omega)d\omega,&k\in\text{even},\end{cases}

(24)

since
g(\omega)
is assumed to be even. Returning to
\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)
, the integral can be split as

\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)=\int_{-\pi}^{0}\cos(k\theta)\rho(\theta,\omega)d\theta+\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos(k\theta)\rho(\theta,\omega)d\theta.

(25)

Focusing on the second integral, after a change of variables
\phi=\theta-\pi
we obtain

\int_{-\pi}^{0}\cos(k(\phi+\pi))\rho(\phi+\pi,\omega)d\phi=\int_{-\pi}^{0}(-1)^{k}\cos(k\phi)\rho(\phi,-\omega)d\phi,

(26)

where we have used the symmetry
\rho(\theta+\pi,-\omega)=\rho(\theta,\omega)
. Substituting back into (
25
) yields

\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)=\int_{-\pi}^{0}\cos(k\theta)\left(\rho(\theta,\omega)+(-1)^{k}\rho(\theta,-\omega)\right)d\theta,

(27)

from which it is clear that
\tilde{I}_{k}(\omega)
is odd if
k
is odd and even if
k
is even. We note that (
11
) from the main text follows directly from (
24
) for
k=2
after a change of variables
\eta=\omega/\gamma
.

We now derive in full detail the Taylor series expansion (
15
) of
r_{2}^{d}
about
\gamma=0
. We will write the Taylor series as
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)=c_{0}+c_{1}\gamma+c_{2}\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})
. First, since
\int_{1}^{\infty}I_{\theta}(\eta)d\eta=-1/3
converges, if
g(\omega)
is bounded (as it generically is), then using the dominated convergence theorem it follows that

\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\int_{1}^{\infty}I_{\theta}(\eta)g(\gamma\eta)d\eta=\int_{1}^{\infty}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}I_{\theta}(\eta)g(\gamma\eta)d\eta=-\frac{g(0)}{3}.

(28)

Hence,

c_{0}=\lim_{\gamma\to 0}r_{2}^{d}=\lim_{\gamma\to 0}2\gamma\left(-\frac{g(0)}{3}\right)=0,

(29)

and so the constant term in the Taylor series is zero. For the linear term in the Taylor series, we can write

c_{1}=\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\frac{r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)}{\gamma}=2\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\int_{1}^{\infty}I_{\theta}(\eta)g(\gamma\eta)d\eta=-\frac{2g(0)}{3},

(30)

where we have again used (
28
). For the quadratic term in the series, we have

c_{2}=\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\frac{\gamma\frac{dr_{2}^{d}}{d\gamma}-r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)}{\gamma^{2}}.

(31)

From Leibniz rule we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dr_{2}^{d}}{d\gamma}

\displaystyle=2\int_{1}^{\infty}I_{\theta}(\eta)g(\gamma\eta)d\eta+2\gamma\int_{1}^{\infty}\eta I_{\theta}(\eta)g^{\prime}(\gamma\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)}{\gamma}+2\gamma\int_{1}^{\infty}\eta I_{\theta}(\eta)g^{\prime}(\gamma\eta)d\eta.

(32)

Substituting (
32
) into (
31
) yields

c_{2}=2\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\int_{1}^{\infty}\eta I_{\theta}(\eta)g^{\prime}(\gamma\eta)d\eta.

(33)

We note that for Lorentzian and Gaussian frequency distributions,
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)
can be computed directly, and its Taylor series computed subsequently, as an alternative to using (
33
).

Appendix B: An explicit example showing that the entire frequency distribution must be accounted for

Here we use an explicit example to show that unlike the onset of synchronization in the Kuramoto model, which depends only on the properties of
g(\omega)
at
\omega=0
, the onset of synchronization in the higher-order model (
1
) is determined by the entirety of
g(\omega)
. This originates from the inability to interchange the limit and integral in (
33
), and can be physically interpreted as quantifying the effect of drifters, which we have shown to be non-negligible in the case
K_{2}\neq 0
.

We compare two frequency distributions that are identical in the range
\omega\in(-b,b)
, but have different tails. The first distribution is a standard Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
, with PDF
g_{1}(\omega)
. We show in the main text that the critical values for
g_{1}(\omega)
are
K_{c}=2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}
,
c_{2}=1/(4\sigma^{2})
and
K_{2,3}^{*}=2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi^{3}}
. As a second distribution, we consider the hybrid PDF

g_{2}(\omega)=\begin{cases}g_{1}(\omega),&|\omega|<b,\\
f(\omega)/\alpha,&|\omega|>b,\end{cases}

(34)

where
g_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is the same as our first distribution,
f(\omega)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})
is a second Gaussian distribution with a larger variance, and

\alpha=\frac{\erfc\left(D\right)}{\erfc\left(B\right)}

(35)

is a renormalization constant ensuring that
\int g_{2}d\omega=1
, where
B=b/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)
and
D=b/(\sqrt{2}s)
. An example of such a distribution
g_{2}(\omega)
is shown in Fig.
3
using
\sigma^{2}=0.1
,
s=1
and
b=\sqrt{2}\sigma\erfc^{-1}(1/2)\approx 0.2133
such that
\int_{-b}^{b}g_{1}(\omega)d\omega=1/2
.

We compute
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)
and its Taylor series about
\gamma=0
explicitly, using (
24
). Since we are interested in the limit
\gamma\to 0
, we compute
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)
only for
\gamma<b
. For the center part of the distribution, with
|\omega|<b
and
g_{2}=g_{1}
, we obtain

2\int_{\gamma}^{b}\tilde{I}_{2}(\omega)g_{1}(\omega)d\omega=\frac{1}{A^{2}}\left(\left(A^{2}-1\right)\left(\erf(B)-\erf(A)\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(2Be^{-B^{2}}+e^{-A^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\pi}-2A-2\Gamma(3/2,B^{2}-A^{2})\right)\right)\right),

(36)

where
A=\gamma/(\sqrt{2}\sigma)
, as in (
20
)-(
21
) and
\Gamma(a,z)
denotes the incomplete Gamma function.
The associated Taylor series expansion is

-\frac{2g_{1}(0)}{3}\gamma+\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{e^{-B^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}B}+\erf(B)\right)\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})

(37)

Here the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)
term is identical to that of the standard Gaussian distribution
g_{1}(\omega)
(cf. (
15
)).
For the tails, with
|\omega|>b
and
g_{2}=f/\alpha
, we obtain

2\int_{b}^{\infty}\tilde{I}_{2}(\omega)\frac{f(\omega)}{\alpha}d\omega=\frac{\erfc(B)}{C^{2}\erfc(D)}\left(\left(C^{2}-1\right)\erfc(D)+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(e^{-C^{2}}\Gamma(3/2,D^{2}-C^{2})-De^{-D^{2}}\right)\right),

(38)

where
C=\gamma/(\sqrt{2}s)
.
The corresponding Taylor series expansion for the tails is

\frac{\erfc(B)\left(2D\,\Gamma(3/2,D^{2})-e^{-D^{2}}\left(1+2D^{2}\right)\right)}{4\sqrt{\pi}s^{2}D\erfc(D)}\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{4})

(39)

We note that, as expected, the tails do not contribute to the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)
term, and only contribute to the higher order terms.

Therefore,
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)
for the hybrid distribution
g_{2}(\omega)
is given by the sum of (
36
) and (
38
). These equations can in principle be solved to find the solution branches for
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. As discussed in the main text, criticality of the pitchfork bifurcation at
K_{1}=K_{c}
can be determined from the Taylor series expansion of
r_{2}^{d}(\gamma)
about
\gamma=0
, which, for
g_{2}
, is given by the sum of (
37
) and (
39
). This Taylor series should be compared to the Taylor series corresponding to the standard Gaussian
g_{1}(\omega)
, which is given by

-\frac{2g_{1}(0)}{3}\gamma+\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3}).

(40)

We see that the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)
term is the same for both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, and depends only on the value of
g_{1}(\omega)
at
\omega=0
, i.e., it is independent of the tails of the distributions. On the other hand, the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
terms are markedly different, which will yield different bifurcation values from gradual to explosive synchronization according to (
18
). Letting
c_{2}^{(1)}=1/(4\sigma^{2})
denote the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
term corresponding to
g_{1}(\omega)
, and
c_{2}^{(2)}
denote the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
term corresponding to
g_{2}(\omega)
(summing (
37
) and (
39

s>\sigma

\sigma^{2}=0.1

c_{2}^{(2)}/c_{2}^{(1)}
takes on a range of values greater than
