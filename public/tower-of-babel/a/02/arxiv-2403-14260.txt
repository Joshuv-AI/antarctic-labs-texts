1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study the complexity of the
model checking problem

{\mathsf{MC}}
for
inquisitive propositional logic

\mathsf{InqB}
and for
inquisitive modal logic

\mathsf{InqM}
, that is, the problem of deciding whether a given finite structure for the logic satisfies a given formula.
1
1

1

In the terminology introduced by Vardi in
[
8
]
, this is the so-called
combined complexity
for the model checking problem.

In particular, we prove that both problems are
{\mathsf{AP}}
-complete.

Inquisitive logics
are a family of formalisms that extend classical and non-classical logic to encompass
questions
.
Traditionally, logic is concerned with
statements
, that is, expressions completely determined by their
truth-conditions
.
The standard approaches to semantics are based on this assumption and are usually formulated in terms of truth-assignment (e.g.,
Boolean valuations
in propositional logic).
However, this semantical approach does not allow us to study expressions whose meaning is
not
determined in terms of truth-conditions, such as
questions
.

In order to interpret questions, which are not true or false, the semantics of
Inquisitive logic
is based on a relation called
support
between and
information states
and a sentence.
This allows to give a uniform semantic account for statement and questions alike:
an information state
supports
a statement if it
implies
the statement;
and it
supports
a question if it
resolves
the question.

The particular instances of inquisitive logic we consider in this paper are
\mathsf{InqB}
and
\mathsf{InqM}
, extending propositional logic and modal logic respectively with the question-forming operators.
In these systems information states are formalized as
sets of truth-assignments
, which gives us a much richer semantical structure than in the classical setting.
The main question we tackle in this paper is:
how hard is it to
decide if a formula of
\mathsf{InqB}
(resp.,
\mathsf{InqM}
) is supported by an information state in a given model
?

The problem of deciding whether a formula
\phi
of a logic
L
is satisfied by a model
\mathcal{M}
is referred to as the
model checking problem for
L
(in symbols
{\mathsf{MC}}(L)
).
The computational complexity of this problem is known for several logics (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
for an overview of the classical results).
In recent years, the problem has been addressed also
for a class of logics called
team logics
which, like our inquisitive systems, are interpreted relative to sets of assignments (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
]
and
[
9
, Ch. 7]
).

Although there is a close connection between these logics and inquisitive logics, it was still an open question what are the complexities of
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
and
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
.
2
2

2

Both model checking problems have been investigated in
[
10
]
(unpublished), building up on previous work by Yang
[
6
]
.

In this paper we give a reduction of the
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete problem
true quantified Boolean formulas

{\mathsf{TQBF}}
(see, e.g.,
[
7
, Ch. 8]
) to
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
, thus settling that both
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
and
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
are
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete
.

The paper is structured as follows:
In Section
2
we present the basic notions of inquisitive logic used throughout the paper.
In Section
3
we introduce suitable encodings of
\mathsf{InqB}
and
\mathsf{InqM}
structures and formalize the model checking problems
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
and
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
.
Moreover, in the same section we present an algorithm for
alternating Turing machines
to solve
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
and use it to show that
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
—and a fortiori also
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqBQ})
—is in the class
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
.
In Section
4
we present and study a polynomial-space reduction of the
{\mathsf{TQBF}}
problem to the
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
, thus showing that the problem is also
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard, and we thus infer that
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
and
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
are both
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete.
We conclude in Section
5
with some remarks and directions for future work.

As an extended introduction on the topic of inquisitive logic we suggest

2.1
Inquisitive propositional and modal logic

Henceforth, we indicate with
\mathrm{AP}
a fixed set of atomic propositions.

Definition 1

(Syntax of
\mathsf{InqB}
and
\mathsf{InqM}
)
.

The set of formulas of
\mathsf{InqM}
—also called
inquisitive modal formulas
—is defined by the following grammar:

\phi\;::=\;\bot\;|\;p\;|\;\phi\land\phi\;|\;\phi\ivee\phi\;|\;\phi\to\phi\;|\;\Box\phi\;|\;\boxplus\phi

where
p\in\mathrm{AP}
.
The set of
inquisitive (propositional) formulas

\mathsf{InqB}
is the set of
\mathsf{InqM}
formulas not containing the symbols
\Box
and
\boxplus
.

So the language of inquisitive logic is the standard language of modal logic extended with
\ivee
and
\boxplus
.
The operator
\ivee
is called
inquisitive disjunction
, while
\boxplus
is the
window modality
.
Additionally, we introduce the following standard shorthands:

\neg\phi:=\phi\to\bot\hskip 50.00008pt\phi\vee\psi:=\neg(\neg\phi\land\neg\psi)

The role of
\ivee
is to enhance the language of modal logic with
alternative questions
.
For example, the formula
p\ivee\neg p
stands for the
natural language question
“does
p
hold (or not)?”.
Instead, the derived operator
\vee
corresponds to the usual disjunction from propositional logic:
for example, the expression
p\vee\neg p
is intuitively interpreted as the
sentence
“
p
holds or
p
does not hold”.
We will show the formal difference in the interpretation of these two formulas after introducing the semantics of the logic.

The operator
\boxplus
plays the role of an
inquisitive modality
, sensitive to the inquisitive structures of the models.
For example, in the context of epistemic logic (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
) where the operator
\Box
is interpreted as
knowledge
of an abstract agent (e.g.,
\Box p
usually stands for “the agent knows that
p
”), the operator
\boxplus
is roughly interpreted as a
wondering
operator, encoding what the agent wonders about (e.g.,
\boxplus?p
stands for “the agent wonders whether
p
is the case”).

As pointed out in Section
1
, the presence of questions requires to move from a semantics based on
truth-assignments
to one based on
information states
.
To formalize this intuition, the logic employs special semantic structures, called
information models
.

Definition 2

(Information models)
.

An
information model for
\mathsf{InqB}
is a tuple of the form
\mathcal{M}=\left\langle W,V\right\rangle
where
W
is a non-empty set (the
worlds
of the model) and
V:\mathrm{AP}\to\mathcal{P}(W)
is a function called the
valuation
of the model.

An
information model for
\mathsf{InqM}
is a tuple of the form
\mathcal{M}=\left\langle W,V,\Sigma\right\rangle
where
W
and
V
are as before, and
\Sigma:W\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(W))
is a function called an
inquisitive state map
, satisfying the following conditions for every
w\in W
:

1.

\Sigma(w)\neq\emptyset
;

2.

If
s\in\Sigma(w)
and
t\subseteq s
, then
t\in\Sigma
.

We refer to the second condition as
downward closure of
\Sigma(w)
.

Each world

is naturally associated with a
Boolean valuation
over
\mathrm{AP}
, defined as

V_{w}(p)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }w\in V(p)\\

So we can think of each world as providing a
complete description
of the current
state of affairs
.
Under this interpretation, we can represent an information state as a
set of worlds
, that is, all the worlds
compatible

So for example, the information that “
p

1

\{\,w\in W\,|\,V_{w}(p)=1\,\}=V(p)

In line with this intuition, henceforth we refer to a subset
s\subseteq W
as an
information state
of the model.
The semantics of the logic is then defined relative to a model
and
an information state.

Definition 3

(Semantics of
\mathsf{InqM}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be an information and
s
an information state of the model.
We define the semantic relation
\vDash
of
\mathsf{InqM}
by the following inductive clauses:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{M},s\vDash\bot&\iff&s=\emptyset\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash p&\iff&s\subseteq V(p)\;\;\iff\;\;\forall w\in s.\;V_{w}(p)=1\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\land\psi&\iff&\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\text{ and }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\psi\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\ivee\psi&\iff&\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\text{ or }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\psi\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\to\psi&\iff&\text{For all $t\subseteq s$, if }\mathcal{M},t\vDash\phi\text{ then }\mathcal{M},t\vDash\psi\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\Box\phi&\iff&\text{For all $w\in s$, }\mathcal{M},\bigcup\Sigma(w)\vDash\phi\\
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\boxplus\phi&\iff&\text{For all $t\in\Sigma[s]$, }\mathcal{M},t\vDash\phi\end{array}

If
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi
we say that
s

supports

\phi
.

Intuitively, an information state
s
supports a formula
\phi
if the information represented by
s

implies the statement represented by
\phi
or
resolves the question represented by
\phi
.
For example, consider the formula
p\ivee\neg p
representing the question “Does
p
hold?”.
The support conditions for this formula are:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{M},s\vDash p\ivee\neg p&\iff\mathcal{M},s\vDash p\;\text{or}\;\mathcal{M},s\vDash\neg p\\
&\iff\left(\forall w\in s.\;V_{w}(p)=1\right)\;\text{or}\;\left(\forall w\in s.\;V_{w}(p)=0\right)\end{array}

So a state
s
supports the formula
p\ivee\neg p
if
either
all the worlds in
s
agree on
p
being true
or
all the worlds in
s
agree on
p
being false.
That is, if we have enough information to
either
affirm that
p
holds
or
affirm that
p
does not hold.

We conclude the Section with a proposition, exhibiting two fundamental properties of the semantics.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\phi
be a formula of the language and
\mathcal{M}
be a model.

(Empty state)

\mathcal{M},\emptyset\vDash\phi
.

(Persistency)

For every
s\subseteq t\subseteq W
, if
\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi
then
\mathcal{M},t\vDash\phi
.

3
Encoding and model checking algorithm

Henceforth, we limit ourselves to work with finite sets of atomic propositions and with finite models (i.e., with models with finitely many worlds).
Throughout the section, we will use the following ad-hoc notations for ease of presentation:

•

The vocabulary consists of the atomic propositions
\mathrm{AP}=\{p_{0},\dots,p_{l-1}\}
, thus
l
is the number of atomic propositions;

•

The set of worlds is
W=\{w_{0},\dots,w_{n-1}\}
, thus
n
is the number of worlds in the model;

•

The state map is
\Sigma(w_{i})=\{S_{0}^{i},\dots,S_{k_{i}-1}^{i}\}
for every
w_{i}\in W
;

•

We define
m
as the “size” of the function
\Sigma
, that is, the value

m\;=\;|\Sigma(w_{0})|+\dots+|\Sigma(w_{n-1})|\;=\;k_{0}+\dots+k_{n-1}

Consider an information model.
We define a natural way to encode this model by storing the propositional valuation in
n+1
binary strings as follows:

•

The first string is
\delta
of length
nl
.
The
(li+j)^{\text{th}}
bit of the string is
\delta_{li+j}=V_{w_{i}}(p_{j})
.
This representation allows to check conditions of the form
w_{i}\in V(p_{j})
in time linear in
nl
.

•

The other strings are
\epsilon_{0},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1}
.
The string
\epsilon_{k}
has length
(n+1)k_{i}+1
and contains in order a digit
\mathtt{0}
, a binary representation of length
n
of
S^{i}_{0}
, a digit
\mathtt{0}
, a binary representation of length
n
of
S^{i}_{1}
…and ends with a digit
\mathtt{1}
.
This representation allows to check conditions of the form
w_{i}\in\bigcup\Sigma[s]
in time linear in
mn
.

An example of encoding is given in Figure
2
.
Notice that to encode an information model for
\mathsf{InqB}
we do not need to store the strings
\epsilon_{0},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1}
, but just the string
\delta
.

For example, the bit
\mathtt{1}

\mathtt{0}

w_{0}\notin V(p_{2})

We use a similar representation for
information states
, encoded as strings of length
n

1

w_{i}
is part of the state.
So, for example, the state
\{w_{0},w_{2}\}

\mathtt{101}

\mathcal{M},s\vDash p_{j}

and

\mathcal{O}((n(l+m))^{2})

This and others bounds we indicate in this section are far from optimal, but this will not affect our analysis of the complexity of the model checking problem.

With a slight abuse of notation, we use the symbols
\mathcal{M}
,
s
and
\phi
to indicate both the formal objects introduced and their binary encodings.
We define the
model checking problem for
\mathsf{InqM}
as the decision problem

{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})\;:=\;\{\;\left\langle\mathcal{M},s,\phi\right\rangle\;|\;\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a model for $\mathsf{InqM}$ and }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\;\}

We also introduce the corresponding anti-satisfaction problem

{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqM})\;:=\;\{\;\left\langle\mathcal{M},s,\phi\right\rangle\;|\;\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a model for $\mathsf{InqM}$ and }\mathcal{M},s\nvDash\phi\;\}

Similarly, we introduce the corresponding problems for
\mathsf{InqB}
.

\displaystyle{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})\;:=\;\{\;\left\langle\mathcal{M},s,\phi\right\rangle\;|\;\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a model for $\mathsf{InqB}$ and }\mathcal{M},s\vDash\phi\;\}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqB})\;:=\;\{\;\left\langle\mathcal{M},s,\phi\right\rangle\;|\;\text{$\mathcal{M}$ is a model for $\mathsf{InqB}$ and }\mathcal{M},s\nvDash\phi\;\}

We are interested in studying the complexity of these decision problems, and in particular we claim that all the problems have complexity
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
.
Notice that the problem
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
(resp.,
{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqM})
) is strictly more complex than the problem
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
(resp.,
{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqB})
), so for our claim we just need to prove that the former is a
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
problem and the latter is a
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard problem.

These algorithms are a variation of the algorithms presented in

4.3
The translation

We have all the elements to define our encoding of an instance of
{\mathsf{TQBF}}
into an instance of
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
, that is, an information model, a state and an
inquisitive formula
.
We firstly focus on the most complex part:
defining the inquisitive formula.

For ease of read, we use the symbols
\zeta,\xi,\eta
to indicate propositional formulas (without quantifiers, so that an arbitrary QBF is of the form
Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
) and the symbols
\phi,\psi,\chi
to indicate inquisitive formulas.
Moreover, we assume that propositional formulas contain only propositional variables from the set
\mathrm{AP}_{l}=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{l-1}\}
.
Finally, we assume that all propositional formulas are in
negation normal form
, that is, for negations only appear in front of atomic propositions.
7
7

7

Translating a formula in negation normal form is an operation linear in time, thus this assumption does not affect our complexity analysis.

We give the translation in two steps:
firstly, we define by recursion
two
translations of
propositional formulas
into inquisitive formulas with an associated polarity, a
positive translation

\zeta^{p}
and a
negative translation

\zeta^{n}
.
Then we provide, again by mutual recursion, two translations for quantified Boolean formulas with an associated polarity,
(Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta)^{P}
and
(Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta)^{N}
.

The translations
\zeta^{p}
and
\zeta^{n}
are defined by the following clauses:

\begin{array}[]{l@{\hspace{.2em}}l @{\hspace{4em}} l@{\hspace{.2em}}l}(x_{i})^{p}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=q_{i}\to p_{i}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&(x_{i})^{n}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=q_{i}\to\neg p_{i}\\
(\neg x_{i})^{p}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=q_{i}\to\neg p_{i}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&(\neg x_{i})^{n}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=q_{i}\to p_{i}\\
(\zeta\land\xi)^{p}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=\zeta^{p}\land\xi^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&(\zeta\land\xi)^{n}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=\zeta^{n}\ivee\xi^{n}\\
(\zeta\vee\xi)^{p}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=\zeta^{p}\ivee\xi^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&(\zeta\vee\xi)^{n}\hfil\hskip 1.99997pt&:=\zeta^{n}\land\xi^{n}\end{array}

This translation allows us to encode the semantics of
\zeta
in terms of switching states instead of Boolean valuations.
In particular, the formula
\zeta
is satisfied under a certain Boolean valuation
\sigma
iff
\zeta^{p}
is supported by the corresponding switching
s_{\sigma}
(recall Definition
8
).

Lemma 11
.

Let
\sigma:\mathrm{AP}_{l}\to\{0,1\}
be a Boolean valuation and let
s_{\sigma}
be the corresponding switching of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
.
Then for every propositional formula
\zeta
we have that:

\begin{array}[]{l @{\qquad\text{iff}\qquad} l @{\qquad\text{iff}\qquad} l}\sigma(\zeta)=1&s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{p}&s_{\sigma}\nvDash\zeta^{n}\\
\sigma(\zeta)=0&s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{n}&s_{\sigma}\nvDash\zeta^{p}\end{array}

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the structure of the formula
\zeta
.
We firstly prove that
\sigma(\zeta)=1\iff s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{p}
and
\sigma(\zeta)=0\iff s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{n}
.

•

If
\zeta=x_{i}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l@{\;\iff\;}l @{\hspace{4em}} l@{\;\iff\;}l}\sigma(x_{i})=1&s_{\sigma}\vDash r_{i}\to p_{i}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&\sigma(x_{i})=0&s_{\sigma}\vDash r_{i}\to\neg p_{i}\\
&s_{\sigma}\vDash(x_{i})^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&s_{\sigma}\vDash(x_{i})^{n}\end{array}

•

If
\zeta=\neg x_{i}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l@{\;\iff\;}l @{\hspace{4em}} l@{\;\iff\;}l}\sigma(\neg x_{i})=1&\sigma(x_{i})=0\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&\sigma(\neg x_{i})=0&\sigma(x_{i})=1\\
&s_{\sigma}\vDash r_{i}\to\neg p_{i}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&s_{\sigma}\vDash r_{i}\to p_{i}\\
&s_{\sigma}\vDash(\neg x_{i})^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&s_{\sigma}\vDash(\neg x_{i})^{n}\end{array}

•

If
\zeta=\eta\land\xi
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l @{\hspace{4em}} l@{}l}&\sigma(\eta\land\xi)=1\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\sigma(\eta\land\xi)=0\\
&\;\iff\;\sigma(\eta)=\sigma(\xi)=1\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;\sigma(\eta)=0\;\text{or}\;\sigma(\xi)=0\\
&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash\eta^{p}\land\xi^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash\eta^{n}\;\text{or}\;s_{\sigma}\vDash\xi^{n}\\
&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash(\eta\land\xi)^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash(\eta\land\xi)^{n}\end{array}

•

If
\zeta=\eta\vee\xi
, we have

\begin{array}[]{l@{}l @{\hspace{4em}} l@{}l}&\sigma(\eta\vee\xi)=1\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\sigma(\eta\vee\xi)=0\\
&\;\iff\;\sigma(\eta)=1\;\text{or}\;\sigma(\xi)=1\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;\sigma(\eta)=\sigma(\xi)=0\\
&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash\eta^{p}\ivee\xi^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash\eta^{n}\land\xi^{n}\\
&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash(\eta\vee\xi)^{p}\hfil\hskip 40.00006pt&&\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\vDash(\eta\vee\xi)^{n}\end{array}

Secondly, we notice that:

\displaystyle s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{p}\;\iff\;\sigma(\zeta)=1\;\iff\;\sigma(\zeta)\neq 0\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\nvDash\zeta^{n}

\displaystyle s_{\sigma}\vDash\zeta^{n}\;\iff\;\sigma(\zeta)=0\;\iff\;\sigma(\zeta)\neq 1\;\iff\;s_{\sigma}\nvDash\zeta^{p}

This concludes the proof.

∎

Now we shift our attention to quantified Boolean formulas, and in particular to the formulas
\theta_{k}:=Q_{k}x_{k}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
.
Notice that the lower the value
k
, the more quantifiers appear in the formula
\theta_{k}
, with the limit case being
\theta_{l}:=\zeta
.

As for propositional formulas we define two translations by mutual recursion.
The two translations are indicated with
\theta_{k}^{P}
and
\theta_{k}^{N}
.
These translations involve the formulas
D_{i}
and
S_{i}
introduced in Subsection
4.2
.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\zeta^{P}&=&\zeta^{p}\\
\zeta^{N}&=&\zeta^{n}\\[1.99997pt]
\theta_{k-1}^{P}&=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}&\text{if}\;Q_{k-1}=\forall\\
\left(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N}\right)\to S_{k}&\text{if}\;Q_{k-1}=\exists\end{array}\right.\\[10.00002pt]
\theta_{k-1}^{N}&=&\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P})\to S_{k}&\text{if}\;Q_{k-1}=\forall\\
D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N}&\text{if}\;Q_{k-1}=\exists\end{array}\right.\end{array}

Similarly to the translations
\zeta^{p}
and
\zeta^{n}
, we can prove a preservation result for these translations.

Lemma 12
.

Consider the quantified Boolean formula
\theta_{k}=Q_{k}x_{k}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
with variables in
\mathrm{AP}_{l}
.
Let
\sigma:\mathrm{AP}_{k}\to\{0,1\}
be a Boolean valuation and
s_{\sigma}
the corresponding
k
-switching of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
.
We have that:

\begin{array}[]{l c l}\sigma(\theta_{k})=1&\text{iff}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\theta_{k})^{P}\\
\sigma(\theta_{k})=0&\text{iff}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\theta_{k})^{N}\end{array}

(6)

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on the number of quantifiers preceding
\zeta
.
The base case is

\begin{array}[]{l c l}\sigma(\zeta)=1&\text{iff}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\zeta)^{P}\\
\sigma(\zeta)=0&\text{iff}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\zeta)^{N}\end{array}

and this is the statement of Lemma
11
.

As for the inductive step, suppose the equivalences to hold for formulas with
l-k-1
quantifiers.
We want to show that the equivalences hold also for formulas with
l-k
quantifiers.
So consider the formula
\theta_{k-1}=Q_{k-1}x_{k-1}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
.
As a useful shorthand, we will write
Q_{k-1}x_{k-1}\theta_{k}
for the formula
\theta_{k-1}
, making explicit reference to the relation between the two formulas.

Let
\sigma:\mathrm{AP}_{k-1}\to\{0,1\}
and
s_{\sigma}
be as in the statement of the lemma.
We have two cases two consider, depending on whether
Q_{k-1}=\forall
or
Q_{k-1}=\exists
.

•

Suppose
Q_{k-1}=\forall
and let us firstly focus on the first equivalence of
6
.
We have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}\\
\iff&\text{For every $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, if }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\text{ then }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash\theta_{k}^{P}\end{array}

By persistency of the logic (Proposition
4
), we just need to check the condition for the
maximal
substates of
s_{\sigma}
satisfying
D_{k-1}
.
By Lemma
9
these maximal substates are
t^{+}=s_{\sigma}\setminus\{w_{k-1}^{-}\}
and
t^{-}=s_{\sigma}\setminus\{w_{k-1}^{+}\}
, which are both
k
-switchings and substates of
s_{\sigma}
.
These switchings correspond respectively to the Boolean valuations
\tau^{+},\tau^{-}:\mathrm{AP}_{k}\to\{0,1\}
defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\tau^{+}(x_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma(x_{i})&\text{if }i<k\\
1&\text{if }i=k\end{array}\right.\\[10.00002pt]
\tau^{-}(x_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma(x_{i})&\text{if }i<k\\
0&\text{if }i=k\end{array}\right.\end{array}

By inductive hypothesis, we have

\begin{array}[]{l l l}\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{+}\vDash\theta_{k}^{P}&\iff&\tau^{+}(\theta_{k})=1\\
\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{-}\vDash\theta_{k}^{P}&\iff&\tau^{-}(\theta_{k})=1\end{array}

So in conclusion we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\\
\iff&\tau^{+}(\theta_{k})=\tau^{-}(\theta_{k})=1\\
\iff&\sigma(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})=\sigma(\theta_{k-1})=1\end{array}

As for the second equivalence of
6
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{N}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P})\to S_{k}\\
\iff&\text{For every $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, if }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}\text{ then }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash S_{k}\end{array}

By Lemma
10
, the only substate of
s_{\sigma}
not satisfying
S_{k}
is
s_{\sigma}
itself.
So the chain of equivalences above can be extended as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{N}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\nvDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}\\
\iff&\text{For some $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\text{ and }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\nvDash\theta_{k}^{P}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\iff}}&\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{+}\nvDash\theta_{k}^{P}\text{ or }\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{-}\nvDash\theta_{k}^{P}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast\ast}}{{\iff}}&\tau^{+}(\theta_{k})=0\text{ or }\tau^{-}(\theta_{k})=0\\
\iff&\sigma(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})=\sigma(\theta_{k-1})=0\end{array}

where the equivalence
(\ast)
follows by persistency and the equivalence
(\ast\ast)
follows by two applications of the inductive hypothesis applied to the
k
-switchings
t^{+}
and
t^{-}
respectively.

•

Suppose
Q_{k}=\exists
.
Most of the arguments and passages follow closely the structure of the previous case, so we will omit comments to most of them.

Let us firstly focus on the first equivalence of
6
.
We have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N})\to S_{k}\\
\iff&\text{For every $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, if }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N}\text{ then }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash S_{k}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\nvDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\iff&\text{For some $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\text{ and }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\nvDash\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{+}\nvDash\theta_{k}^{N}\text{ or }\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{-}\nvDash\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\iff}}&\tau^{+}(\theta_{k})=1\text{ or }\tau^{-}(\theta_{k})=1\\
\iff&\sigma(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})=\sigma(\theta_{k-1})=1\end{array}

where the equivalence
(\ast)
follows by inductive hypothesis.

As for the second equivalence of
6
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{N}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},s_{\sigma}\vDash D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\iff&\text{For every $t\subseteq s_{\sigma}$, if }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash D_{k-1}\text{ then }\mathcal{S}_{l},t\vDash\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\iff&\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{+}\vDash\theta_{k}^{N}\text{ and }\mathcal{S}_{l},t^{-}\vDash\theta_{k}^{N}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\iff}}&\tau^{+}(\theta_{k})=\tau^{-}(\theta_{k})=0\\
\iff&\sigma(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})=\sigma(\theta_{k-1})=0\end{array}

where the equivalence
(\ast)
follows from inductive hypothesis.

And this concludes the proof of the inductive step.
Thus the result follows by induction.

∎

As a direct corollary of the previous lemma we obtain the desired result:
we can reduce an instance of
{\mathsf{TQBF}}
to an instance of
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
.

Corollary 13
.

Let
\theta:=Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
be a closed quantified Boolean formula.
Then
\theta
is valid iff
\mathcal{S}_{l},W_{l}\vDash\theta^{P}
.
In other terms:

\theta\in{\mathsf{TQBF}}\quad\text{iff}\quad\left\langle\mathcal{S}_{l},W_{l},\theta^{P}\right\rangle\in{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})

Proof.

Recall that the set
W_{l}
of all the worlds of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
is a
0
-switching, and that the corresponding valuation is the empty function.
By Lemma
12
(applied for
k=0
) we have that
\theta
is valid (i.e., satisfied by the empty function) iff
\mathcal{S}_{l},W_{l}\vDash\theta^{P}
.
∎

4.4
Complexity

It remains to show that the reduction of
{\mathsf{TQBF}}
to
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
presented in Corollary
13
is polynomially bounded in size.
This entails that
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
is a
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard problem, since so is
{\mathsf{TQBF}}
.

Let
\theta
be the formula
Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
with
\left|\zeta\right|=h
, and thus
\left|\theta\right|=\Theta(l+h)
.
Using the encoding of information models presented in Section
3
, the size of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
is
\left|\{p_{0},\dots,p_{l-1},q_{0},\dots,q_{l-1}\}\right|\cdot\left|W_{l}\right|=2l^{2}
, which is indeed polynomially bounded by
l+h
.
And the same goes for the encoding of the information state
W_{l}
, which is of size
2l
.

As for the size of
\theta^{P}
, the computations are slightly more involved, so we proceed one step at a time.
Since there are several summations and an inductive proof involved, the
\mathcal{O}
notation may easily lead to errors.
For this reason, we firstly present a semi-formal analysis of the complexity involving
\mathcal{O}
s in the current section, and we give a more thorough analysis in Appendix
A
.

Firstly, we find bounds for the size of the special formulas presented in Subsection
4.2
.
Recall that we indicate with
\left|\phi\right|
the size of the encoding of a formula
\phi
.

•

\left|C^{+}_{k}\right|=\left|\;q_{k}\land p_{k}\;\right|=\mathcal{O}(\log(l))
and
\left|C^{-}_{k}\right|=\left|\;q_{k}\land\neg p_{k}\;\right|=\mathcal{O}(\log(l))
.

•

\left|\neg C_{k}^{+}\land\neg C_{k}^{-}\right|=\left|\neg C_{k}^{+}\ivee\neg C_{k}^{-}\right|=\mathcal{O}(\log(l))
.

•

\left|S_{0}\right|=\mathcal{O}(l)+\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\left|\neg C^{+}_{i}\ivee\neg C^{-}_{i}\right|=\mathcal{O}(l)+\mathcal{O}(l\log(l))=\mathcal{O}(l\log(l))
.

•

For
k>0
we have
\left|S_{k}\right|=\mathcal{O}(l)+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left|\neg C_{i}^{+}\land\neg C_{i}^{-}\right|+\sum_{i=k}^{l-1}\left|\neg C_{i}^{+}\ivee\neg C_{i}^{-}\right|=\mathcal{O}(l)+\mathcal{O}(k\log(l))+\mathcal{O}((l-k)\log(l))=\mathcal{O}(l\log(l))
.

•

\left|D_{k}\right|=\left|\;q_{k}\to(p_{k}\ivee\neg p_{k})\;\right|=\mathcal{O}(\log(l))
.

•

A straightforward inductive argument on the construction of
\zeta^{P}
(resp.,
\zeta^{N}
) shows that
\left|\zeta^{P}\right|=\mathcal{O}(h)
(resp.,
\left|\zeta^{N}\right|=\mathcal{O}(h)
).

And now we prove by (descending) induction on
k
that
\left|\theta_{k}^{P}\right|
and
\left|\theta_{k}^{N}\right|
are both in
\mathcal{O}((l-k-1)l\log(l)+h)
.
The base step of the induction
k=l
(i.e., when there are no quantifiers involved and the formulas are
\zeta^{P}
and
\zeta^{N}
respectively) was already considered.
So assume that
\left|\theta_{k}^{P}\right|
and
\left|\theta_{k}^{N}\right|
are both in
\mathcal{O}((l-k-1)l\log(l)+h)
.
We prove only that
\left|\theta_{k-1}^{P}\right|
is in
\mathcal{O}((l-k)l\log(l)+h)
, since the other inequality follows similar computations.
We consider two separate cases, depending on whether
Q_{k-1}=\forall
or
Q_{k-1}=\exists
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left|(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\right|&=\left|D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}(\log(l))+\mathcal{O}((l-k-1)l\log(l)+h)\\
&=\mathcal{O}((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left|(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\right|&=\left|(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N})\to S_{k}\right|\\
&=\mathcal{O}(l\log(l))+\mathcal{O}((l-k-1)l\log(l)+h)+\mathcal{O}(l\log(l))\\
&=\mathcal{O}((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\end{array}

And this concludes the inductive proof.
The salient results we thus obtain are the following.

Theorem 14
.

For
\theta=Q_{0}x_{0}\dots Q_{l-1}x_{l-1}\zeta
a closed quantified Boolean formula, the size of
\theta^{P}
is in
\mathcal{O}(l^{2}\log(l)+\left|\zeta\right|)
.
In particular, the size of the translation is polynomially bounded by the size of the original formula.

Corollary 15
.

The problem
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
is
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard.

As anticipated, this was the last ingredient needed to show that the four problems considered are
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete.

Theorem 16
.

The problems
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
,
{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqB})
,
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
and
{\mathsf{\overline{MC}}}(\mathsf{InqM})
are
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete.

5
Conclusions

In this paper we formalized and studied the model checking problems
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
and
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
for inquisitive propositional logic and inquisitive modal logic, and studied their complexity.
In Section
3
we provided an algorithm to solve the problem
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqM})
and proved its correctness.
This allowed us to show that the complexity of the problem is in the class
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
.
In Section
4
we provided a polynomial-space reduction of the
true quantified Boolean formula
problem to
{\mathsf{MC}}(\mathsf{InqB})
, thus showing that the problem is also
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-hard.
Putting these results together, we inferred that both problems are
{\mathsf{PSPACE}}
-complete.

This paper is the first investigation of computational complexity issues for inquisitive logics.
A venue for further investigation is whether the proof presented adapts to other inquisitive logics, such as
inquisitive epistemic logic

[
1
, Ch. 7]
,
inquisitive intuitionistic logic

[
2
]
or first-order versions of the system such as
\mathsf{InqBQ}

[
1
, Ch. 4]
.

Explicit analysis

\left|D_{k}\right|=\left|\;q_{k}\to(p_{k}\ivee\neg p_{k})\;\right|\leq c_{9}\log(l)

A straightforward inductive argument on the construction of

Secondly, we provide an explicit analysis of the size of

Q_{k-1}=\exists
.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\left|(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\right|&=\left|D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{P}\right|\\
&\leq c_{10}\log(l)+c((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\\
&\leq c\log(l)+c(l-k)l\log(l)+ch&\text{(if $c\geq c_{10}$)}\\
&\leq c(\;(l-(k-1))l\log(l)+h\;)\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lll}\left|(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{P}\right|&=\left|(D_{k-1}\to\theta_{k}^{N})\to S_{k}\right|\\
&\leq c_{11}l\log(l)+c((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\\
&\leq cl\log(l)+c(l-k)l\log(l)+ch&\text{(if $c\geq c_{11}$)}\\
&\leq c(\;(l-(k-1))l\log(l)+h\;)\end{array}

Secondly we prove the inequality
\left|\theta_{k-1}^{N}\right|\leq c((l-(k-1))l\log(l)+h)
.
Also in this case we distinguish the cases
Q_{k-1}=\forall
and
Q_{k-1}=\exists
.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\left|(\forall x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{N}\right|&=\left|(D_{k}\to\theta_{k}^{P})\to S_{k}\right|\\
&\leq c_{12}l\log(l)+c((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\\
&\leq cl\log(l)+c(l-k)l\log(l)+ch&\text{(if $c\geq c_{12}$)}\\
&\leq c(\;(l-(k-1))l\log(l)+h\;)\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lll}\left|(\exists x_{k-1}\theta_{k})^{N}\right|&=\left|D_{k}\to\theta_{k}^{N}\right|\\
&\leq c_{13}\log(l)+c((l-k)l\log(l)+h)\\
&\leq c\log(l)+c(l-k)l\log(l)+ch&\text{(if $c\geq c_{13}$)}\\
&\leq c(\;(l-(k-1))l\log(l)+h\;)\end{array}

So by choosing
c:=\max(c_{10},c_{11},c_{12},c_{13})
we meet all the conditions necessary for the inequality to hold.
This concludes our inductive proof, and shows that both
\left|\theta_{k}^{P}\right|
and
\left|\theta_{k}^{N}\right|
are in
\mathcal{O}(((l-k)l\log(l)+h))
, as desired.
