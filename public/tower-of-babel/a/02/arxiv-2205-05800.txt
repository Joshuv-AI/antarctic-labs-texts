Stochastic first-order methods for average-reward Markov decision processes


Abstract
We study average-reward Markov decision processes (AMDPs) and develop novel first-order methods with strong theoretical guarantees for both policy optimization and policy evaluation. Compared with intensive research efforts in finite sample analysis of policy gradient methods for discounted MDPs, existing studies on policy gradient methods for AMDPs mostly focus on regret bounds under restrictive assumptions, and they often lack guarantees on the overall sample complexities. Towards this end, we develop an average-reward stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD) method for solving AMDPs with and without regularizers and provide convergence guarantees in terms of the long-term average reward. For policy evaluation, existing on-policy methods suffer from sub-optimal convergence rates as well as failure in handling insufficiently random policies due to the lack of exploration in the action space. To remedy these issues, we develop a variance-reduced temporal difference (VRTD) method with linear function approximation for randomized policies along with optimal convergence guarantees, and design an exploratory VRTD method that resolves the exploration issue and provides comparable convergence guarantees. By combining the policy evaluation and policy optimization parts, we establish sample complexity results for solving AMDPs under both generative and Markovian noise models. It is worth noting that when linear function approximation is utilized, our algorithm only needs to update in the low-dimensional parameter space and thus can handle MDPs with large state and action spaces.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) problems are generally formulated as Markov decision processes (MDPs). At each time step, the agent observes the current state and subsequently takes an action, which leads to an instantaneous reward or cost as well as the transition to the next state according to an unknown transition kernel. The eventual goal of the agent is to learn a policy to optimize the reward accrued (or cost paid) over time. MDPs consist of two prominent classes, including the discounted MDPs (DMDPs), which introduce a discount factor to the measure of accumulated reward/cost, and the average-reward MDPs (AMDPs), which measure the success of the system in terms of its steady-state performance. Although DMDP has been studied extensively, it usually leads to poor long-term performance when a system operates for an extended time period. The average-reward criterion used in AMDPs can alleviate this issue, hence has been widely used in many different applications ranging from engineering to natural and social sciences; see, e.g.,
Kober et al. 2013
;
Xu et al. 2014
for surveys of applications.

Since the transition kernel is unknown, one important objective in RL studies is to understand its sample complexity,
which characterizes how many samples are required to obtain a nearly-optimal policy up to a given accuracy.
Finite sample analyses are often
carried out under different observation models.
The two most well-known observation models considered by the literature are the so-called
generative model
(“i.i.d.” model) and the

Markovian model
(“single-trajectory” model).

In the
generative
model, we have access to an oracle that returns the next state when queried at any state-action pair, typically seen in agent training with simulators.
On the other hand, the
Markovian
setup does not permit system restarts at arbitrary state-action pairs; once initiated, the trajectory must be followed sequentially. This latter situation often occurs following the deployment of an agent’s policy.

To facilitate the analysis, these observation models are also augmented with various assumptions on the behavior of the underlying MDPs (e.g., mixing and ergodicity).
In comparison with significant progress recently made on
the finite sample analysis of DMDPs,
theoretical studies on the finite sample analysis of AMDPs, especially under the Markovian noise model, are rather limited.

Under the Markovian noise model, we distinguish two types of RL methods, namely on-policy learning
(
Konda and Tsitsiklis 1999
)
and off-policy learning
(
Degris et al. 2012
)
. In on-policy learning, the samples can be collected based on the currently generated policy, while for off-policy learning the samples are collected based on a behavior policy. Both settings have their areas of applications in practice. The off-policy learning encourages the agent to explore the action space by using a sufficiently random, but possibly highly non-optimal policy
(
Wan et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021a
)
, while on-policy learning enables continuous policy improvement in-situ with instantaneous rewards generated by nearly-optimal policies.
One significant challenge associated with on-policy learning exists
in the exploration for the action space. In particular, as the policy converges towards the optimal policy (either a deterministic or randomized optimal policy), non-optimal actions are rarely explored given their diminishing probabilities in the policy at hand, thus making the policy evaluation task increasingly difficult.
Consequently, existing on-policy learning methods require restrictive assumptions that each policy generated by the algorithm is random enough, meaning that the policy will take each action with a probability bounded away from zero. This assumption is unrealistic since in general the optimal policy does not possess this structure, so the goal of policy optimization will hardly be achieved. Up to our knowledge, in spite of intensive research effort of on-policy learning
(
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1999
;
Yu and Bertsekas 2009
;
Zhang et al. 2021b
;
Mou et al. 2022
)
, one seemly unresolved problem in RL is whether one can design sampling-efficient on-policy learning algorithms for insufficiently random policies to remove the aforementioned problematic assumption (see Remark 1 of
Lan 2022
).

It is well-known that one can formulate AMDPs
as a linear programming (LP) problem (see, e.g.,
Puterman 2014
).
As a result, one can utilize the rich theory in LP and specialize some advanced algorithms for solving AMDPs. In particular, to deal with the unknown transition kernels, current research has been focused on utilizing general stochastic primal-dual saddle point optimization methods (see Chapter 4 of
Lan 2020
), and some important progresses along this research direction have been made in
Wang 2017
;
Jin and Sidford 2020
.
In spite of the elegance of the stochastic LP approach, there exist a few significant limitations associated with this approach.
Firstly, due to the high dimensionality of the state space, the size of LP can be huge. One common practice is to use function approximation with linear kernel or neural networks for the value functions. However, the LP approach cannot deal with function approximations.
Secondly, in MDP and RL, one usually needs to incorporate certain nonlinear objectives and/or constraints. One prominent example is a regularization given by Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, which can be used to model the difference between the desirable policy and a reference policy. The LP approach will fail to handle
these nonlinear components.
Thirdly, in the current stochastic LP approaches for RL, one needs to
have access to a generative model to simulate the probability of moving to the next state for any state-action pair.
As a consequence, this type of approach cannot be applied to the Markovian noise setting.

Some classic dynamic programming methods, especially value iteration, have been adapted for AMDPs with unknown transition kernels

(
Abounadi et al. 2001
;
Gosavi 2004
;
Wan et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021b
)
. These methods are often called value-based methods or Q-learning in the RL literature. Even though value-based methods are extensively studied in the DMDP literature and optimal finite sample guarantees are established
(
Wainwright 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
)
, the only known finite sample analysis of Q-learning for AMDPs up to our knowledge is
Zhang et al. 2021b
. However,
Zhang et al. 2021b
only focused on the generative model, and the overall sample complexity is significantly worse than LP methods
(
Wang 2017
;
Jin and Sidford 2020
)
. Moreover, the value-based approach can fail in handling linear function approximation
(
Boyan and Moore 1994
;
Baird 1995
;
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1996
)
or policy regularization. In addition, Q-learning is also an off-policy method and cannot be applied to in-situ improvements.
It is also noteworthy that a recent work of
Jin and Sidford 2021
established a reduction method that solves an AMDP by solving an associated DMDP. Using this reduction technique, while the dependence on the problem parameters, e.g., mixing time, is improved, the dependence on the accuracy measure is sub-optimal, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon^{3})
. After we released the initial version of this work, a few more recent works
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zhang and Xie 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Zurek and Chen 2023
)
further improved the reduction argument, and showed that the optimal sample complexities can be achieved in
Wang et al. 2023
;
Zurek and Chen 2023
. However, these optimal results require solving the associated DMDP with model-based approaches, thus failing to handle the policy regularization and function approximation.

In this paper, we focus on the study of a new class of
first-order methods for solving AMDPs that involves
both
policy evaluation
and
policy improvement
steps.
The framework is also known as the
actor-critic
method in the RL literature, which generalizes the classic policy iteration method. Specifically, the actor aims to perform the policy update while the critic is dedicated to evaluating the value associated with the currently generated policy, which constitutes first-order information of the nonlinear policy optimization problem.
In comparison with the LP method and value-based method, these policy-based first-order methods offer remarkable flexibility for function approximation as well as policy regularization. Meanwhile, the model-free framework of policy-based methods naturally accommodates both generative and Markovian noise settings.
Recently, there has been considerable interest in the development and analysis of first-order methods for DMDPs. In particular, sublinear convergence guarantees of policy gradient method and its variants are discussed in
Agarwal et al. 2021
and
Shani et al. 2020
.

Cen et al. 2021
showed that
natural policy gradient (NPG) converges linearly for entropy-regularized DMDPs.

Lan 2022
proposed the policy mirror descent (PMD) method which achieved linear convergence for DMDPs, and
\widetilde{\cal O}(1/\epsilon)
(resp.,
\widetilde{\cal O}(1/\epsilon^{2})
) sample complexity for regularized (resp., unregularized) DMDPs. Further studies for these types of methods can be found in
Khodadadian et al. 2021
;
Zhan et al. 2021
;
Lan et al. 2022
;
Xiao 2022
;
Li et al. 2022
. Nevertheless, theoretical understanding of policy-based methods for AMDPs remains very limited. It is noteworthy that
Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2019
;
Lazic et al. 2021
proposed a policy-based method named POLITEX for AMDPs, but their analysis requires restrictive assumptions on the transition kernel and they only established regret bounds without sample complexities (see early related work
Bartlett and Tewari 2012
;
Auer et al. 2008
;
Ouyang et al. 2017
).

1.1
Main contributions

In this work, we make three distinct contributions.

•

Policy mirror descent for AMDPs (Actor)
: Motivated by the stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD) algorithm for solving DMDPs
(
Lan 2022
)
, we develop the average-reward SPMD method. We consider the linear function approximation in the SPMD updates and reveal an update rule in the low-dimensional space when equipping SPMD with KL-divergence.
Specifically, our algorithm only needs to update in the low-dimensional parameter space and thus can handle MDPs with large state and action spaces.

In the convergence analysis, we handle the expected error and bias error from the critic step separately, leading to sharpened convergence results. The SPMD method achieves
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})
convergence rate for unregularized AMDPs, and an improved
\mathcal{O}(1/\omega T)
convergence rate (where
\omega
stands for the strongly convex modulus of the regularizer) for strongly convex regularized AMDPs.

•

Policy evaluation for AMDPs (Critic)
: We first propose a simple multiple trajectory method for policy evaluation in the generative model,
along with sample complexities for both bias and expected squared error of the estimators. For the on-policy evaluation under Markovian noise, we develop an average-reward variant of the variance-reduced temporal difference (VRTD) algorithm
(
Khamaru et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
)
with linear function approximation, which achieves
\mathcal{O}(t_{\text{mix}}^{3}\log(1/\epsilon))
sample complexity for the bias of the estimators, as well as an instance-dependent sample complexity for expected error of the estimators. The latter complexity improved the one in
Zhang et al. 2021b
by at least a factor of
\mathcal{O}(t_{\text{mix}}^{2})
. Meanwhile, we develop a novel exploratory variance-reduced temporal difference (EVRTD) that resolves the exploration issue in the action space when the policy is not random enough. The idea of the EVRTD algorithm naturally extends to DMDPs.

•

Sample complexity
:
We establish the overall sample complexity under both the generative model and Markovian noise model. Under the mixing unichain setting with a generative model, by implementing the SPMD algorithm with multiple trajectory method as the critic, we achieve
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{3}|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{\epsilon^{2}}\big)
overall sample complexity. As for the ergodic setting with the Markovian observation model, we implement SPMD with VRTD/EVRTD as the critic, which achieves

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
sample complexity. For regularized AMDPs, this bound is improved to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\tfrac{1}{\omega^{2}\epsilon})
for obtaining an
\epsilon
-optimal policy in terms of distance to the optimal solution.
To the best of our knowledge, these sample complexities are new in the literature on solving average-reward RL problems with policy-based (first-order) methods.

2
Background and problem setting

In this section, we introduce some preliminaries for (regularized) AMDPs and the concrete observation models that we study.

2.1
Average-reward Markov decision processes

An AMDP is described by a tuple
\mathcal{M}:=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathsf{P},c)
, where
\mathcal{S}
denotes a finite state space,
\mathcal{A}
denotes a finite action space,
\mathsf{P}
is the transition kernel, and
c
is the cost function. At each time step, the agent takes an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
at the current state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then the system moves to some state
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
with probability
\mathsf{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
, while the agent pays the instantaneous cost
c(s,a)
(or receives
the
-c(s,a)
instantaneous reward). The goal of the agent is to determine a policy which minimizes the long-term cost. A randomized stationary policy of an MDP is a mapping
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
that maps a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
to a fixed distribution over actions. We denote
P_{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
and
P^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|)\times(|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|)}
as the state transition matrix and state-action transition matrix induced by policy
\pi
, respectively, where
P_{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\mathsf{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
and
P^{\pi}((s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}))=\mathsf{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})
. Under a given policy
\pi
, the (regularized) long-run average cost/reward for state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is defined as

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}(s):=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{T}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\big[\textstyle{\sum}_{t=0}^{T-1}(c(s_{t},a_{t})+h^{\pi}(s_{t}))\big|s_{0}=s\big].

(2.1)

Here,
h^{\pi}
is a closed convex function with respect to the policy
\pi
, i.e., there exists
\omega\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle h^{\pi}(s)-[h^{\pi^{\prime}}(s)+\langle\nabla h^{\pi^{\prime}}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\rangle]\geq\omega D_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\pi}(s),

(2.2)

where
\nabla h^{\pi^{\prime}}
denotes the subgradient of
h
at
\pi^{\prime}
and
D_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\pi}(s)
is the Bregman distance between
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
. In this paper, we fix
D_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\pi}(s)
to be the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, i.e.,
D_{\pi^{\prime}}^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\log\frac{\pi(a|s)}{\pi^{\prime}(a|s)}
.
It should be noted that our algorithmic framework allows us to use other distance generating functions, e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{p}^{2}
for some
p>1
. Clearly, if
h^{\pi}=0
, then Eq. (
2.1
) reduces to the classical unregularized average cost function, while if
h^{\pi}(s)=\omega D_{\pi_{0}}^{\pi}(s)
for
\omega>0
then Eq. (
2.1
) defines the average cost of the entropy-regularized MDP.

In this work, we consider the
unichain
setting, where the induced Markov chain consists of a single recurrent class plus a possibly empty set of transient states for any deterministic stationary policy. We further restrict our attention to
mixing
AMDPs, which satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

An AMDP instance is mixing if for any feasible policy
\pi
, there exists a stationary distribution
\nu^{\pi}
such that for any distribution
q\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
, the induced Markov chain has mixing time bounded by
t_{\text{mix}}<\infty
, where
t_{\text{mix}}
is defined as

\displaystyle t_{\text{mix}}:=\max_{\pi}\big[\arg\min_{t\geq 1}\big\{\max_{q\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}}||((P_{\pi})^{t})^{\top}q-\nu^{\pi}||_{1}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\big\}\big].

This is a widely used regularity condition for AMDPs (see, e.g.,
Wang 2017
;
Jin and Sidford 2020
), which can be ensured by adding the
aperiodic
assumption to the unichain setting.
As a result, for any feasible policy
\pi
, the average reward function does not depend on the initial state (see Section 8 of
Puterman 2014
), and we can define a scalar which satisfies for all
s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\rho^{\pi}(s)=\rho^{\pi}:=\langle\nu^{\pi},c^{\pi}+h^{\pi}\rangle,

where
\nu^{\pi}
is the stationary distribution of the states induced by the policy
\pi
, and
c^{\pi}
is the expected cost function induced by policy
\pi
, i.e.,
c^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}c(s,a)\pi(a|s)
. Given that one can view
\rho^{\pi}
as a function of
\pi
, we will use the notation
\rho^{\pi}
and
\rho(\pi)
interchangeably. Since the average-reward function only captures the “steady-state” behavior of the underlying policy, the literature utilizes the following differential functions to capture the “transient” behavior of the underlying policy.
Specifically, the
basic differential value function
(also called bias function in
Puterman 2014
) is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{V}^{\pi}(s):={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\left[\textstyle{\sum}_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(c\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right)+h^{\pi}(s_{t})-\rho^{\pi}\right)\big|s_{0}=s\right],

and the
basic differential action-value function (or basic differential Q-function)
is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{\pi}(s,a):={\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\left[\textstyle{\sum}_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(c\left(s_{t},a_{t}\right)+h^{\pi}(s_{t})-\rho^{\pi}\right)\big|s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right].

Moreover, we define the sets of
differential value functions
and
differential action-value functions (differential Q-functions)
as the solution sets of the following Bellman equations, respectively,

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=c^{\pi}+h^{\pi}-\rho^{\pi}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{S}|}+P_{\pi}V

(2.3)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=c+\widetilde{h}^{\pi}-\rho^{\pi}\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}+P^{\pi}Q,

(2.4)

where
\widetilde{h}^{\pi}(s,a)=h^{\pi}(s)
. Under Assumption
1
, the solution of Eq. (
2.3
) (resp., Eq. (
2.4
)) is unique up to an additive constant, which takes the form of
\{\bar{V}^{\pi}+b\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{S}|}|b\in\mathbb{R}\}
(resp.,
\{\bar{Q}^{\pi}+b\mathbf{1}_{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}|b\in\mathbb{R}\}
).

Finally, our goal in AMDP is to find an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
that minimizes the long-run average cost. Consequently,
the concerned
policy optimization
problem can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\min_{\pi}\quad\rho(\pi)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|},~\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(2.5)

2.2
Observation models

It is typically assumed in RL that there is sample access to the stochastic transition kernel and cost function. As mentioned in Section
1
, this study differentiates between two fundamental observation models in RL: the generative model (also known as the “i.i.d.” model) and the Markovian noise model (also known as the “single trajectory” model).

For the generative model, we utilize an oracle that, when queried with a state-action pair
(s,a)
, provides the subsequent state
s^{\prime}
and the immediate cost
c(s,a)
. If the cost function is noiseless, this scenario implicitly implies a complete understanding of the cost function.

In the Markovian noise model, we assume that all observed samples stem from a single trajectory of a Markov chain induced by the policy
\pi
under evaluation. Specifically, we are presented with a sequence of state-action pairs
\{(s_{0},a_{0}),(s_{1},a_{1}),...\}
that are generated according to the policy
\pi
and the transition kernel
\mathsf{P}
.
Since solving problem (
2.5
) requires sample access to all the states in
\mathcal{S}
, we introduce the following natural assumption for the Markovian noise model. Clearly, this assumption is stronger than Assumption
1
since it indicates that the stationary distribution of any feasible policy is strictly positive.

Assumption 2
.

For any feasible policy
\pi
, the Markov chian induced by policy
\pi
is irreducible and aperiodic.

Given the stochastic nature of the RL setting, a key problem for us is
policy evaluation
, which involves estimating the long-run average reward/cost and the differential value function or differential action-value function for a specific policy under various observation models. By utilizing policy evaluation techniques, we are able to develop effective first-order algorithms to address the policy optimization problem (
2.5
).

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
3
, we develop the stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD) method for solving AMDPs.
In Section
4
, we propose efficient methods to solve the policy evaluation problem under both types of observation models and establish the overall sample complexity of the SPMD method. In Section
5
, we provide numerical experiments to corroborate our theoretical guarantees.

3
Actor: Stochastic policy mirror descent for AMDPs

Our goal in this section is to develop a computationally efficient first-order method to solve the AMDP problem (
2.5
) in the stochastic setting. To start with, we first explore the first-order information of the average reward/cost function and establish a variational inequality (VI) formulation of the problem.

The following simple lemma provides the gradient of the average-reward function and the proof follows from routine differentiation.

Lemma 1

(Gradient of average reward/cost)
.

For any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial\rho(\pi)}{\partial\pi(a|s)}=\nu^{\pi}(s)\left(\bar{Q}^{\pi}(s,a)+\nabla h^{\pi}(s,a)\right).

where
\nabla h^{\pi}(s,\cdot)
denotes the gradient of
h^{\pi}(s)
w.r.t. policy
\pi
.

In view of lemma
1
, the optimality condition of (
2.5
) suggests us to solve the following VI

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{s\in\nu^{*}}\big[\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi^{*}},\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi}(s)-h^{\pi^{*}}(s)\big]\geq 0.

(3.1)

This VI can be solved efficiently since it satisfies certain generalized monotonicity condition
(
Facchinei and Pang 2003
;
Dang and Lan 2015
;
Kotsalis et al. 2020
)
, thanks to the following performance difference lemma (generalized from
Kakade and Langford 2002
;
Zhang and Ross 2021
).

Lemma 2

(Performance difference lemma)
.

Given any two policies
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
, we have

\rho(\pi^{\prime})-\rho(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\nu^{\pi^{\prime}}}\big[\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi}(s,\cdot),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)-\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi^{\prime}}(s)-h^{\pi}(s)\big].

(3.2)

As a consequence of Lemma
2
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\rho(\pi^{*})-\rho(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\nu^{*}}\big[\left\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi}\left(s,\cdot\right),\pi^{*}\left(\cdot|s\right)-\pi\left(\cdot|s\right)\right\rangle+h^{\pi^{*}}(s)-h^{\pi}(s)\big].

Since
\rho(\pi)-\rho(\pi^{*})\geq 0
for any feasible policy
\pi
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\nu^{*}}\big[\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi}\left(s,\cdot\right),\pi\left(\cdot|s\right)-\pi^{*}\left(\cdot|s\right)\rangle+h^{\pi}(s)-h^{\pi^{*}}(s)\big]=\rho(\pi)-\rho(\pi^{*})\geq 0,

(3.3)

which implies the VI in Ineq. (
3.1
) satisfies the generalized monotonicity condition.
We will design efficient stochastic optimization algorithms and provide theoretical guarantees for solving this VI in the following several subsections.

3.1
Stochastic policy mirror descent for AMDPs

In order to reduce the optimality gap

\rho(\pi)-\rho(\pi^{*})
,
we intend to minimize the left-hand side of the identity in (
3.3
), which inspires us to introdue the following iterative prox-mapping step:

\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)=\underset{p(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}{\arg\min}\left\{\lambda_{k}\left[\left\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}(s,\cdot),p(\cdot|s)\right\rangle+h^{p}(s)\right]+D_{\pi_{k}}^{p}(s)\right\},\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(3.4)

Here, the proximal term
D_{\pi_{k}}^{p}
is added
so that
\pi_{k+1}
does not move too far away from
\pi_{k}
.
Note however that in the RL setting the exact basic differential Q-function
\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}
is unavailable, thus we replace it with its stochastic estimator calculated with samples

, denoted as
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}

3:

with samples

,

\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}=\text{critic}(\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}).

(3.5)

\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)=\underset{p(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}{\arg\min}\left\{\lambda_{k}\left[\langle\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot),p(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{p}(s)\right]+D_{\pi_{k}}^{p}(s)\right\},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

To ensure the convergence of the SPMD method, we make the following assumptions on the evaluation error. In Section
4
, we will propose concrete policy evaluation approaches with the desired accuracy guarantees.

Assumption 3
.

Define
\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k}}:=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{k}}\left[\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}\right]
. There exist some
\varsigma,\sigma,\kappa\geq 0
such that for any
k\geq 0

\displaystyle\|{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k}}-\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}}\|_{\text{sp}}

\displaystyle\leq\varsigma,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{k}}\big[\|{\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}-\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}}\|_{\text{sp}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{k}}\big[\|{\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}}\|_{\text{sp}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}.

(3.9)

Note that we define the error measures in the span norm because the update rule of Algorithm
1
is invariant with additive all-one vector
\mathbf{1}
on
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}
.
We will show in Section
4
that the bias term
\varsigma
can be reduced much faster than the expected error term
\sigma^{2}
, which enables us to sharpen the analysis to obtain more efficient convergence guarantees than one would obtain without distinguishing the bias and variance terms. For convenience, we denote the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}_{s}:=\sigma(\zeta_{0},...,\zeta_{s})
and

\delta_{k}:=\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}-\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}.

3.2
SPMD with linear function approximation

In many reinforcement learning applications, the state and action spaces (especially the state space) can be very large, making the original SPMD method, as well as the policy evaluation algorithms, computationally inefficient and memory-intensive.
To address this issue, we discuss the incorporation of a linear function approximation scheme for the basic differential Q-functions
\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}
within the SPMD method in this subsection.

In particular, one can choose

\mathbb{S}:=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}\},

for
d
linearly independent basis vectors
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}
. For each state action pair
s\in\mathcal{S},~a\in\mathcal{A}
, we let
\psi(s,a):=[\psi_{1}(s,a),\psi_{2}(s,a),...,\psi_{d}(s,a)]^{\top}
denote its feature vector. For simplicity, we define
\Psi:=[\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)\times d}
. It should be noted that we fix the feature matrix
\Psi
for all SPMD iterations, and use
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote the low-dimensional estimator for evaluating policy
\pi_{k}
, i.e.,
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}=\Psi\theta_{k}
.

Due to the linear function approximation, we will introduce an approximation error when
\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}\notin\mathbb{S}
. Consequently, the bias term
\varsigma
defined in (
3.7
) cannot be controlled towards
0
. However, in the following sections, we will show that the bias will not be accumulated while running the SPMD method; instead, it can converge to the optimal solution up to the upper bound
\varsigma
for the bias.
By contrast, some alternative algorithms, such as the stochastic Q-learning methods, could diverge with simple function approximation due to the absence of contraction properties for the Bellman optimality equation with function approximation, see
Baird 1995
;
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1996
.

Moreover, we do not need to enumerate all state-action pairs for the policy updating
step (
3.6
) in the SPMD method.
By utilizing the optimality condition of the prox-mapping updates (
3.6
) of SPMD under entropy regularization, we obtain the following SPMD update rule for the parameters
\widetilde{\theta}_{k}
in the lower-dimensional space.

Lemma 3
.

Assume
h^{\pi}(s):=\omega\cdot\textstyle{\sum}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\log\pi(a|s)
for some
\omega\geq 0
, and
\pi_{0}(a|s)=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
for any
s\in\mathcal{A},a\in\mathcal{A}
. Consider the linear parametrization
\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}=\Psi\theta_{k}
. Then step (
3.6
) in Algorithm
1
can be equivalently written in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{0}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{k+1}=-\tfrac{\lambda_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}\omega}\theta_{k}+\tfrac{1}{1+\lambda_{k}\omega}\widetilde{\theta}_{k},\quad k\geq 0.

(3.10)

Proof.

Proof.
By the definition of
D_{\pi}^{\pi^{\prime}}
and
h^{\pi}
, we can equivalently written Eq. (
3.6
) as

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)

\displaystyle=\underset{p(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}{\arg\min}\left\{\lambda_{k}\langle\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot)-\omega\log\pi_{k}(\cdot|s),p(\cdot|s)\rangle+(1+\lambda_{k}\omega)\langle\log p(\cdot|s),p(\cdot|s)\rangle\right\},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\underset{p(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}{\arg\min}\left\{\lambda_{k}\langle(\Psi\theta_{k})(s,\cdot)-\omega\log\pi_{k}(\cdot|s),p(\cdot|s)\rangle+(1+\lambda_{k}\omega)\langle\log p(\cdot|s),p(\cdot|s)\rangle\right\},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

Then by checking the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, we obtain that

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)\propto\exp\left(-\tfrac{\lambda_{k}}{1+\lambda_{k}\omega}(\Psi\theta_{k})(s,\cdot)+\tfrac{1}{1+\lambda_{k}\omega}\log\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)\right).

Then by using the above equation recursively and noticing that
\pi_{0}(a|s)=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}
, it is easy to see that

\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)\propto\exp\big((\Psi\widetilde{\theta}_{k+1})(s,\cdot)\big),

where
\widetilde{\theta}_{k+1}
is defined in Eqs. (
3.10
).
∎

From Eq. (
3.10
), the SPMD updates can be fully implemented in the low-dimensional subspace
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if the linear parametrization of the differential-Q-function
\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}
, namely
\theta_{k}
, is given in each SPMD iteration.

To implement the SPMD method, we need to combine the above update rule with a policy evaluation subroutine that can handle linear function approximation. Note that in order to evaluate the policy
\pi_{k}
under the on-policy setting, we need to generate the Markov trajectory following policy
\pi_{k}
. Nevertheless, there is no need to calculate the whole policy
\pi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}
and save it in memory. Instead, we only need to calculate the policy
\pi(\cdot|s)
following

\displaystyle\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)\propto\exp\big((\Psi\widetilde{\theta}_{k+1})(s,\cdot)\big),

(3.11)

once reaching a new state
s
, which only requires a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{A}|\times d)
. This structure makes the algorithm implementable when the state space is super large or even infinite. In Section
4.2
, We will propose some efficient on-policy evaluation approaches with linear function approximation.

3.3
SPMD for unregularized AMDPs

In this subsection, we start proving the convergence guarantees for SPMD.
The following lemma characterizes the convergence behavior for each iteration of Algorithm
1
. Recall the definition of the convexity modulus
\omega\geq 0
in Ineq. (

Lemma 4

Assume Assumption
3

Proof.

Proof.
First, by the optimality condition of (

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\left(\langle\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot),\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)-\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi_{k+1}}(s)-h^{\pi^{*}}(s)\right)+D_{\pi_{k}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(s)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{k}\left(\langle\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot),\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)-\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi_{k}}(s)-h^{\pi^{*}}(s)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\lambda_{k}\left(\langle\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot),\pi_{k+1}(\cdot|s)-\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi_{k+1}}(s)-h^{\pi_{k}}(s)\right)+D_{\pi_{k}}^{\pi_{k+1}}(s)\right]

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\left(\langle\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}(s,\cdot),\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)-\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi_{k}}(s)-h^{\pi^{*}}(s)\right)

\displaystyle\leq D_{\pi_{k}}^{\pi^{*}}(s)-\left(1+\lambda_{k}\omega\right)D_{\pi_{k+1}}^{\pi^{*}}(s)+\tfrac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|{\widetilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k}}-\bar{Q}^{\pi_{k}}}\|_{\text{sp}}\|\pi_{k}(\cdot|s)-\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\|_{1}+\tfrac{\lambda_{k}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bar{\mathcal{Q}}^{\pi_{k},\zeta_{k}}(s,\cdot)}\|_{\text{sp}}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]}{4}+\lambda_{k}^{2}L^{2}

\omega=0
and
L=0
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
\omega=0
,

3

(3.14)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\rho(\pi_{r})-\rho(\pi^{*})]\leq\tfrac{\kappa\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{A}|}}{\sqrt{K}}+\varsigma.

(3.15)

Proof.

Proof.
By taking the telescope sum of
k=0,..,K-1

3.4

In this subsection, we consider using the SPMD method to solve strongly convex regularized MDPs, i.e.,
\omega>0

We first consider the case when

Proof.

Proof.

In Section

4.2
Policy evaluation in Markovian noise model

In the Markovian noise model, we assume that Assumption
2
(ergodicity assumption) holds to ensure sample access to all the states.
As a consequence, we have the following lemma which characterizes the mixing time property under Assumption
2
(See
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis 1995
).

Lemma 7
.

There exist constants
C_{P}>0
and
\varrho\in(0,1)
such that for any policy
\pi
, we have
\nu^{\pi}>0
and

\max_{s\in S}\|(P_{\pi})^{t}_{(s,\cdot)}-\nu^{\pi}\|_{1}\leq C_{P}\cdot\varrho^{t},~\text{ for all }\;t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Assumption
2
and Lemma
7
guarantee that the mixing time defined in Assumption
1
is bounded as
t_{\text{mix}}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\tfrac{\log(C_{P})}{\log(1/\varrho)}\right).

Under this assumption, we aim to solve the following Bellman equation for a fixed policy
\pi
:

\displaystyle Q=P^{\pi}Q+c-\rho^{\pi}\mathbf{1},\quad Q\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}.

(4.4)

It is worth noting
the issue of lacking exploration in the action space
when solving the above Bellman equation. We need access to samples of all state-action pairs in
\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
to compute an accurate solution of (
4.4
). Clearly, under the ergodicity assumption (Assumption
2
), we have enough explorations over the state space for all feasible policies. However, when the policy
\pi
is not a sufficiently random policy (common in cases where the optimal policy is a greedy policy), then the action space may not be sufficiently explored. More precisely, when there exists
s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\pi(a|s)
is very close or equal to
0
, we do not have enough samples for this state-action pair, posing challenges in learning the corresponding differential Q-function.

To address the aforementioned issue, we will first focus on the simple case where the policy is sufficiently random (Section
4.2.1
). Then in Section
4.2.2
, we propose a novel exploration framework to remedy the lack of exploration issue encountered when the policy is not sufficiently random. Notice that our new exploration framework guarantees the same optimal sample complexity in
\epsilon
for both sufficiently random and insufficiently random policies, which is achieved for the first time in both AMDP and DMDP literature.

4.2.1
Evaluation of sufficiently random policy

In this subsection, we consider the simple case such that

\displaystyle There~exists~a~\underline{\pi}>0~such~that~\pi(s|a)\geq\underline{\pi}~for~all~s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}.

(4.5)

When analyzing the sampling scheme of the Markovian noise model, we define a natural weighting diagonal matrix

D^{\pi}:=\diag\big([D^{\pi}_{1};...;D^{\pi}_{|\mathcal{S}|}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)\times(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)},

where
D^{\pi}_{s}:=\nu^{\pi}(s)\cdot\diag(\pi(\cdot|s))\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
.
Clearly, the diagonal elements of
D^{\pi}
are the steady-state distribution of all state-action pairs in the Markov chain induced by the policy
\pi
, and we have
D^{\pi}\succ 0
due to Assumption
2
and the condition
\pi(s|a)\geq\underline{\pi}
. For brevity of notation, in the remaining part of Section
4.2.1
, we let
P:=P^{\pi},D:=D^{\pi},\nu:=\nu^{\pi}
and
\rho^{*}:=\rho^{\pi}
.

To deal with large state and action spaces, we recall the linear function approximation introduced in Section
3.2
. Specifically, we choose
\mathbb{S}:=\text{span}\{\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}\}
for
d
linearly independent basis vectors
\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
. For each state action pair
s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
, we let
\psi(s,a):=[\psi_{1}(s,a),\psi_{2}(s,a),...,\psi_{d}(s,a)]^{\top}
denote its feature vector. Additionally, we assume that
\|\psi(s,a)\|_{2}\leq 1
for each state-action pair, which can be ensured through feature normalization. Let
\Pi_{\mathbb{S},D}
denote the projection onto the subspace
\mathbb{S}
with respect to the
\|\cdot\|_{D}
-norm. Then instead of solving Eq. (
4.4
), we consider solving the following projected Bellman equation:

\displaystyle\bar{Q}=\Pi_{\mathcal{S},D}(P\bar{Q}+c-\rho^{*}\mathbf{1}).

This is equivalent to find
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\Psi^{\top}D\Psi\theta^{*}=\Psi^{\top}DP\Psi\theta^{*}+\Psi^{\top}D(c-\rho^{*}\mathbf{1}),

(4.6)

where
\Psi:=[\psi_{1},...,\psi_{d}]
.
Next, we define a set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}
that have identical value on the support of
\pi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|}|x(s,a)=1~\text{if}~\pi(a|s)>0\}.

For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathbb{S}=\phi
, which is equivalent to
\mathbf{1}\notin\mathbb{S}
in this subsection since
\pi
is strictly positive. This condition can be easily verified by choosing the features appropriately. Then by Theorem 1 of
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1999

4.6
) has a unique solution. Furthermore, we define

\displaystyle\bar{Q}^{*}=\bar{Q}^{\pi}+\widehat{b}\mathbf{1}\quad\text{where}\quad\widehat{b}:=\arg\min_{b\in\mathbb{R}}\{\|\bar{Q}^{\pi}+b\mathbf{1}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}\}.

(4.7)

In words,
\bar{Q}^{*}
is the projection of
\Psi\theta^{*}
onto the set of the differential Q-functions defined in Eq. (
2.4
).

It is convenient in the analysis to have access to an orthonormal basis spanning the projected space
\mathbb{S}
. Define the matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as
B_{i,j}:=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{D}
for each
i,j{\@index{\@indexphrase{[d]}}}
.
Note that
B\succ 0
due to the linear independence of
\psi_{i}
’s and
D\succ 0
.

Let

\Phi:=[\phi_{1},\phi_{2},...,\phi_{d}]=\Psi B^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

By construction, the vectors
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}

We are now prepared to introduce the basic ideas of the Variance-reduced Temporal Difference (VRTD) Method (Algorithm
3
) for solving (
4.22
). First, it incorporates the idea of the variance-reduced TD learning for solving DMDPs from
Li et al. 2021
. This algorithm runs in epochs and utilizes the current best estimator
\widetilde{\theta}
to recenter the updates inside each epoch, allowing us to capture the “correct” stochastic error of the problem.
Second, the data generated by the Markovian noise model is highly correlated, which induces challenges in the algorithmic analysis. To overcome the difficulties, we incorporate the idea of sample skipping from
Kotsalis et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2021
to reduce the bias induced by correlated data. The spirit of this sample skipping idea can be traced back to the classical work by
Yu 1994
.
Third, in contrast to the DMDP environment, policy evaluation within AMDPs entails two objectives—
\rho^{*}
for average reward/cost and
\theta^{*}
for the parameterized differential Q-function. It is worth noting that estimating
\rho^{*}
demands significantly fewer samples compared to estimating
\theta^{*}
. This distinction arises from the nature of the tasks. Estimating
\rho^{*}
involves assessing a policy’s steady-state behavior, whereas estimating
\theta^{*}
necessitates acquiring a deeper understanding of the transition kernel.
In view of this observation, unlike the existing TD approaches that updates both objectives simultaneously
(
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1999
;
Zhang et al. 2021b
)
, our VRTD algorithm first collects samples to estimate
\rho^{*}
at the beginning of each epoch and then performs (variance-reduced) stochastic approximation to update
\theta
. Consequently, we are able to significantly improve the sample complexity of the TD algorithms introduced by
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1999
and analyzed by
Zhang et al. 2021b
.

The following theorem characterizes the convergence of VRTD in terms of expected squared
\ell_{D}
-norm error, which is a natural convergence criterion (see, e.g.,
Bhandari et al. 2018
;
Mou et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
for DMDPs) for policy evaluation in the RL literature. The proof of this result can be found in Appendix
A.5
.

Theorem 4
.

Fix the total number of epochs
K
and positive integers
N
and
N^{\prime}
. Assume for each epoch
k\in[K]
, the parameters
\eta
,
T
,
N_{k}
and
N^{\prime}_{k}
satisfy

\eta\leq\tfrac{1-\beta}{845},~~T\geq\tfrac{64}{\mu(1-\beta)\eta},~~N_{k}\geq\max\big\{\tfrac{1160}{\mu(1-\beta)^{2}},(\tfrac{3}{4})^{K-k}N\big\},~~\text{and}~~N_{k}^{\prime}\geq(\tfrac{3}{4})^{K-k}N^{\prime}.

(4.16)

Set the output of epoch
k
to be
\widehat{\theta}_{k}:=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}}{T}
. If
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are chosen to satisfy

\displaystyle\varrho^{\tau}\leq\tfrac{\sqrt{\mu}\eta}{C_{M}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varrho^{\tau^{\prime}}\leq\min\{\tfrac{\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\nu(s)}{C_{P}},\tfrac{1}{4C_{\max}+1}\},

(4.17)

then we have

{\mathbb{E}}[(\widehat{\rho}_{K}-\rho^{*})^{2}]\leq\tfrac{5\bar{c}^{2}}{N^{\prime}},

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\Psi\widehat{\theta}_{K}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2^{K}}\|\Psi\theta^{0}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{D}+\tfrac{3W_{1}}{N}+\tfrac{108\bar{c}^{2}}{N^{\prime}(1-\beta)^{2}\mu}+\tfrac{3\bar{c}^{2}}{4N^{\prime}},

(4.18)

where
W_{1}:=22\mathrm{trace}\{(I_{d}-M)^{-1}\bar{\Sigma}(I_{d}-M)^{-\top}\}+\tfrac{4}{\mu(1-\beta)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|(\bar{Q}^{*}-\Psi\theta^{*})\|_{D}^{2}]
and

\bar{\Sigma}:=\cov\big[B^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\langle\psi(s,a)-\psi(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\theta^{*}\rangle-c(s,a)\big)\psi(s,a)\big],

for
s\sim\nu,a\sim\pi(\cdot|s),s^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{P}(\cdot|s,a),a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime}).

Noting that the error caused by inexact feature approximation is unavoidable, we set the stopping criterion of VRTD as finding a solution
\widehat{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to achieve the accuracy
{\mathbb{E}}\|\Psi\widehat{\theta}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\|\Psi\theta^{*}-\bar{Q}^{*}\|_{D}^{2}+\epsilon
. From the statement of Theorem
4

\mathcal{O}\{\log(\|\Psi\theta^{0}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|^{2}_{D}/\epsilon)\}
and the total number of samples is
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(\tau T+\tau^{\prime}(N_{k}+N_{k}^{\prime})\big)
, which is of the order

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\big\{\tfrac{t_{\text{mix}}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\mu}\log(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})+\tfrac{t_{\text{mix}}\bar{c}^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\mu\epsilon}+\underbrace{\tfrac{t_{\text{mix}}\cdot\mathrm{trace}\{(I_{d}-M)^{-1}\bar{\Sigma}(I_{d}-M)^{-\top}\}}{\epsilon}}_{\text{dominant stochastic error}}\big\}.

We would like to provide some comments on the dominant term in the complexity discussed above. This term represents an instance-dependent characterization of the stochastic error associated with solving the projected Bellman equation (
4.6
) while having access to samples from the transition kernel. A direct extension of Proposition 1 of
Li et al. 2021
demonstrates that this term matches the asymptotic instance-dependent lower bound on the stochastic error for solving Eq. (
4.6
) with i.i.d. samples up to an additional mixing time factor. For fair comparison to the existing literature, we apply a direct upper bound
\mathcal{O}\big\{\frac{\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}}{(1-\beta)^{2}\mu}\big\}

on
\mathrm{trace}\{(I_{d}-M)^{-1}\bar{\Sigma}(I_{d}-M)^{-\top}\}
, as well as the upper bound
\mathcal{O}(t_{\text{mix}})
on

, thus arriving at the sample complexity

\mathcal{O}\big\{\tfrac{t_{\text{mix}}^{3}}{\mu}\log(\tfrac{1}{\epsilon})+\tfrac{t_{\text{mix}}^{3}(\bar{c}^{2}+\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2})}{\mu\epsilon}\big\}.

To the best of our knowledge, the only work that had established finite sample complexity for solving the AMDP policy evaluation problem under Markovian noise is
Zhang et al. 2021b
. They analyzed the TD(
\lambda
) method proposed in
Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1999
, where the algorithm simultaneously updates estimators of both objectives, i.e., average reward/cost
\rho^{\pi}
and differential value function
\bar{V}^{\pi}
. Compared to their guarantees, our sample complexity outperforms by a factor of
\mathcal{O}((1-\beta)^{-2})
or equivalently
\mathcal{O}(t_{\text{mix}}^{2})
. In addition, their convergence analysis was conducted in
\ell_{2}
-norm, which is not a natural metric for the underlying problem, thus leading to worse dependences on other problem parameters, e.g., dimension of the transition kernel. It is noteworthy that recently
Mou et al. 2022
proposed a variance-reduced stochastic approximation approach that solves the AMDP policy evaluation problem in span semi-norm under the generative model. However, their results do not directly extend to the Markovian noise setting. Meanwhile, it is hard to obtain convergence guarantees in terms of the “bias” by using their metric and analysis techniques.

Next, we establish a bias bound in Theorem
5
,
whose proof is deferred to Appendix

(4.19)

Set the output of epoch

to be
\widehat{\theta}_{k}:=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}}{T}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\|\Psi{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{\theta}_{K}]-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2^{K}}\|\Psi\theta^{0}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}^{2}+\big(b_{1}C_{\max}^{2}\varrho^{2\tau^{\prime}}+\tfrac{b_{2}C_{\max}\varrho^{\tau}}{(1-\beta)\sqrt{\mu}}\big)\big(\|\Psi\theta^{0}-\Psi\theta^{*}\|_{D}^{2}+\tfrac{W_{1}}{N}+\bar{c}^{2}\big),

According to Theorem

4.2.2
Evaluation of insufficiently random policies

In this section, we remove the assumption on sufficiently random policies, i.e., (
4.5
). Precisely, we aim to evaluate a policy
\pi
where
\pi(a|s)
can be
0
or arbitrarily small for any state-action pair.

As mentioned before, a possible lack of exploration in this setting poses significant challenges for on-policy evaluation. A classical method to remedy this issue is through off-policy learning, where the agent chooses actions according to a behavior policy that encourages exploration. However, in this manner, the agent is not able to collect rewards according to the policy of interest, thus losing the advantages of on-policy reinforcement learning. On the other hand, one may also argue that we can directly perturb the policy to encourage more exploration. However, this is still an off-policy method, and the differential Q-function of the perturbed policy can be very different from that of the original policy.

In this section, we seek an on-policy method to remedy this exploration issue. More specifically, we propose an exploratory variance-reduced temporal difference (EVRTD) algorithm that alternates between the policy of interest
\pi
and a perturbed policy, denoted as
\widetilde{\pi}
.
The EVRTD algorithm for insufficiently random policy
\pi
has a similar structure to Algorithm
3
, with differences only lying in the sample collection steps. In particular, the EVRTD algorithm replaces steps 5 and 7 in Algorithm
3
with the following ones. Here again, we set
P:=P^{\pi}
,
\nu:=\nu^{\pi}
and
\rho^{*}:=\rho^{\pi}
for brevity of notation.

Step
5^{\prime}
: Run the Markov trajectory induced by policy
\pi
for
\tau^{\prime}
steps and obtain the current state
s
. Then generate an action
a\sim\widetilde{\pi}(\cdot|s)
, the next state
s^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{P}(\cdot|s,a)
, the next action
a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})
, and collect the tuple
\{(s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),c(s,a)\}
. Repeat for
N_{k}
times, and index the samples as
\xi_{i}^{k}(\tau^{\prime})=\{(s_{i},a_{i}),(s_{i}^{\prime},a_{i}^{\prime}),c(s_{i},a_{i})\}
.
Calculate
\widehat{g}(\widetilde{\theta},\widetilde{\rho})=\tfrac{1}{N_{k}}\textstyle{\sum}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\widetilde{g}(\widetilde{\theta},\widetilde{\rho},\xi_{i}^{k}(\tau^{\prime})).

Step
7^{\prime}
: Run the Markov trajectory induced by policy
\pi
for
\tau

s_{t}
. Then generate an action
a_{t}\sim\widetilde{\pi}(\cdot|s_{t})
, the next state
s_{t}^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})
, the next action
a_{t}^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}^{\prime})
, and collect the tuple
\{(s_{t},a_{t}),(s_{t}^{\prime},a_{t}^{\prime}),c(s_{t},a_{t})\}

Clearly, the EVRTD algorithm is still an on-policy algorithm since, in most time steps, the agent collects rewards/costs following the policy of interest
\pi
. We now explain some intrinsic ideas of the EVRTD algorithm by focusing on one sample tuple
\{(s,a),(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),c(s,a)\}

\tau

\tau^{\prime}

\nu

\pi

a\sim\widetilde{\pi}(\cdot|s)
,
s^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{P}(\cdot|s,a)
and
a^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\Psi^{\top}\widetilde{D}\Psi\theta^{*}=\Psi^{\top}\widetilde{D}P\Psi\theta^{*}+\Psi^{\top}\widetilde{D}(c-\rho^{*}\mathbf{1}),

(4.22)

where
\widetilde{D}

\widetilde{D}:=\diag\big([\widetilde{D}_{1};...;\widetilde{D}_{|\mathcal{S}|}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{(|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|)\times(|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|)},

\widetilde{D}_{s}:=\nu^{\pi}(s)\cdot\diag(\widetilde{\pi}(\cdot|s))

4.22
) can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\bar{Q}=\Pi_{\mathbb{S},\widetilde{D}}(P\bar{Q}+c-\rho^{*}\mathbf{1}).

It is easy to see that instead of solving the Bellman equation induced by a perturbed policy, we are still solving a projected version of the average-reward Bellman equation (
4.4
) induced by the original policy
\pi
. This projected Bellman equation completely reduces to (
4.4

\exists b\in\mathbb{R},~\text{s.t.}~\bar{Q}^{\pi}+b\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{S}

\widetilde{D}\succ 0
when the underlying Markov chain is ergodic and the policy
\widetilde{\pi}(a|s)>0
for all
(s,a)
pairs. With a slight abuse of notation, in this subsection we define the matrix

by letting
B_{i,j}:=\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle_{\widetilde{D}}
for each
i,j{\@index{\@indexphrase{[d]}}}

\displaystyle\Phi:=[\phi_{1},\phi_{2},...,\phi_{d}]=\Psi B^{-\frac{1}{2}},\quad\mu:=\lambda_{\min}(B),\quad\widetilde{M}:=\Phi^{\top}\widetilde{D}P\Phi,

(4.23)

and

\widetilde{\Sigma}:=\cov\big[B^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\langle\psi(s,a)-\psi(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\theta^{*}\rangle-c(s,a)\big)\psi(s,a)\big],

for

We modify the definition of the deterministic operator correspondingly

\displaystyle g(\theta,\rho)=\Psi^{\top}\widetilde{D}(\Psi\theta-P\Psi\theta-c+\rho\mathbf{1}).

(4.24)

Now switch our attention to the construction of the perturbed policy
\widetilde{\pi}
. We first fix a constant
0<\underline{\pi}<1

\mathcal{A}_{s}:=\{a\in\mathcal{A}|\pi(a|s)>{\underline{\pi}/2}\}

\displaystyle\forall s\in\mathcal{S},~~\widetilde{\pi}(a|s):=\begin{cases}\underline{\pi},&\text{if}~a\notin\mathcal{A}_{s}\\
\pi(a|s)\cdot\tfrac{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{s}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)},&\text{if}~a\in\mathcal{A}_{s}\end{cases}.

(4.25)

We have that
\tfrac{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{s}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)}=\tfrac{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}}{1-\sum_{a^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{A}_{s}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)}\leq\tfrac{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}}{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}/2}\leq 1

\underline{\pi}\leq\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|-\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\mathcal{A}_{s}|},
we have
\tfrac{1-(|\mathcal{A}|-|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)\underline{\pi}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{s}}\pi(a^{\prime}|s)}\geq 0
. Therefore, we ensure that
\widetilde{\pi}(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
. It should be noted that the EVRTD method reduces to the VRTD method (Algorithm
3
) when
\mathcal{A}_{s}=\mathcal{A}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Lemma 9
.

Assume Assumption
2
holds. Recalling the definition of
1-\beta
in (

\displaystyle 1-\beta>0.

(4.26)

Assume

\displaystyle\underline{\pi}\leq\tfrac{(1-\beta)\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\nu(s)}{(|\mathcal{A}|-\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|\mathcal{A}_{s}|)(8+(1-\beta)\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\nu(s))},

(4.27)

\widetilde{\pi}

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{S},\|x\|_{\widetilde{D}}=1}x^{\top}\widetilde{D}(I-P)x\geq\min\big\{\tfrac{27\alpha(\underline{\pi})(1-\beta)}{32},\tfrac{1-\alpha(\underline{\pi})(1-\beta)}{8}\big\},

(4.28)

Proposition 3
.

After replacing

\min\big\{\tfrac{27\alpha(\underline{\pi})(1-\beta)}{32},\tfrac{1-\alpha(\underline{\pi})(1-\beta)}{8}\big\}

In conclusion, we develop the EVRTD algorithm, where the agent follows the original policy

The EVRTD algorithm recovers the convergence guarantees of VRTD in a weighted

4.2.3
Sample complexities for SPMD in Markovian noise

In this subsection, we provide the sample complexities for using the SPMD method with EVRTD as a critic to solve AMDPs in Markovian noise. We consider both unregularized AMDPs and regularized AMDPs.

To establish the convergence guarantees, we require some additional notation. For a fixed policy
\pi
, we let
\bar{\theta}_{\pi}
denote the solution of the projected Bellman equation (

) induced by policy

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we focus on the design and analysis of policy evaluation and optimization algorithms for AMDPs. For policy optimization, we develop an average-reward stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD) method along with convergence analysis for both unregularized and strongly convex regularized AMDPs. For policy evaluation, we develop the multiple trajectory method and the VRTD method for the generative model and Markovian noise model, respectively. Moreover, we develop a novel EVRTD method that resolves the issue of lack of exploration in the action space. Finally, we establish
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-2})
overall sample complexity for unregularized AMDPs and
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\epsilon^{-1})
sample complexity for solving strongly convex regularized AMDPs.
