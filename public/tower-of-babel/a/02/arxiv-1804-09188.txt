Work Statistics, Loschmidt Echo and Information Scrambling in Chaotic Quantum Systems
Aurélia Chenu, Javier Molina-Vilaplana, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
Characterizing the work statistics of driven complex quantum systems is generally challenging because of the exponential growth with the system size of the number of transitions involved between different energy levels. We consider the quantum work distribution associated with the driving of chaotic quantum systems described by random matrix Hamiltonians and characterize exactly the work statistics associated with a sudden quench for arbitrary temperature and system size. Knowledge of the work statistics yields the Loschmidt echo dynamics of an entangled state between two copies of the system of interest, the thermofield double state. This echo dynamics is dictated by the spectral form factor. We discuss its relation to frame potentials and its use to assess information scrambling.



Quantum thermodynamics has become a research field in bloom, building on the dialogue between technological progress and foundations of physics
[
1
,
2
]
. In the presence of thermal and quantum fluctuations, familiar concepts from traditional thermodynamics at the macroscale, such as heat and work, become stochastic variables. Their analysis has been a fertile ground of inquiry, leading to the discovery of fluctuation theorems
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
and time-work uncertainty relations
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

In the quantum domain, even for isolated systems governed by unitary dynamics, work is not considered to be an observable.
The definition of work requires two projective measurements, one at the beginning and another at the end of the physical process under consideration
[
6
,
11
]
. Measuring the work statistics done by or on a driven system requires analyzing the transitions between its energy levels at the beginning and at the end of the process. In simple quantum systems, this is an amenable task and pioneering experiments have measured the quantum work statistics, e.g., in coupled qubits in a NMR setting
[
12
]
, a driven quantum oscillator describing the dynamics of a trapped ion
[
13
]
, and ultracold atoms
[
14
]
.

For complex quantum systems, determining the work probability density function associated with a given process is intrinsically challenging, due to the exponential growth in the number of transitions with the dimension of the Hilbert space.
Progress in this direction has been achieved avoiding their explicit computation. To this end, it has proved useful to recast the characteristic function of the work distribution function as a Loschmidt echo amplitude
[
15
]
.
This interpretation has facilitated the study of quantum work statistics in driven many-body spin systems
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
and quantum fluids
[
18
]
.

Among complex systems, those exhibiting quantum chaos constitute a paradigmatic reference example. Following pioneering work by Wigner and Dyson, quantum chaotic systems are often defined as systems whose spectral properties are well described by random-matrix Hamiltonian ensembles
[
19
,
20
]
. Random-matrix Hamiltonians can actually characterize a variety of physical systems ranging from atomic nuclei to spin chains. Quantum chaos plays as well a prominent role in black hole physics, as it is widely believed that quantum correlations in these systems are scrambled at a maximum rate saturating an analogue of the Lyapunov exponent
[
21
]
. It should be noted that, very recently, preliminary steps have been taken to study work statistics in quantum chaotic systems
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

In addition, black hole physics has motivated recent progress to characterize information scrambling, i.e., the spreading of initially localized quantum information across different degrees of freedom in many-body systems. Approaches to this end include the study of the decay dynamics of certain entangled states (the thermofield double state, associated with the purification of a thermal density matrix)
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, related analytically-continued partition functions
[
28
,
29
]
, out-of-time-order correlators
[
21
]
and frame potentials
[
30
]
, some of which have been related to work statistics
[
24
,
31
,
32
]
.

In this work, we characterize the work statistics when a quantum chaotic system is driven between two different chaotic Hamiltonians. We show that the characteristic function of the work probability distribution of a thermal state is equivalent to the Loschmidt echo of an entangled state. Using this relation, we show that the work statistics can be used to elucidate the dynamics responsible for information scrambling in chaotic quantum systems. The connection between work statistics and information scrambling is further discussed by relating the Loschmidt echo to the spectral form factor and frame potentials.

1
Quantum work statistics in chaotic quantum systems

Work probability distribution function.—

Let us consider a driven, isolated system described by the instantaneous Hamiltonian at time
s

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{s}=\sum_{n}E_{n}^{s}|n_{s}\rangle\langle n_{s}|\,,

(1)

where
E_{n}^{s}
denotes the real energy eigenvalue for the eigenstate
|n_{s}\rangle
.
We focus on the time-evolution of a system initially prepared in a mixed state
\hat{\rho}
at time
s=0
and driven to a final Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\tau}
at time
s=\tau
. The corresponding time-evolution operator is

\displaystyle\hat{U}(\tau)={\cal T}\exp\left[-i\int_{0}^{\tau}ds\hat{H}_{s}\right]\,,

(2)

where the time-ordering operator
{\cal T}
is required whenever the instantaneous Hamiltonians at two different times of evolution
0\leq(s,s^{\prime})\leq\tau
do not commute.
The associated work probability distribution function (work pdf, hereafter) is given by
[
6
,
11
]

p_{\tau}(W):=\sum_{n,m}p^{0}_{n}\;p^{\tau}_{m|n}\delta\left[W-\left(E_{m}^{\tau}-E_{n}^{0}\right)\right]\,.

(3)

Here, a first projective energy measurement leads to the probability
p^{0}_{n}=\langle n_{0}|\hat{\rho}|n_{0}\rangle
to find the initial state
\hat{\rho}
in the
n
-th eigenmode of the initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
.
Following the evolution associated with
\hat{U}(\tau)
, a second projective energy measurement is used to determine the transition probability from
|n_{0}\rangle
to
|m_{\tau}\rangle
given by
p^{\tau}_{m|n}=|\langle m_{\tau}|\hat{U}(\tau)|n_{0}\rangle|^{2}
. The work associated with such a transition is set by the energy difference between the associated eigenvalues,
W=E_{m}^{\tau}-E_{n}^{0}
. The definition of the work pdf in (
3
) encompasses all possible transitions with the corresponding probabilities. As already advanced in the introduction, the need for two projective-energy measurements prevents the introduction of a Hermitian work operator
[
11
]
. However, it is possible to describe work in terms of a positive-operator value measure (POVM)
[
33
,
34
]
.

Work in chaotic quantum systems.—

Quantum chaotic systems are described by random matrix Hamiltonians that have been extensively studied following the seminal works by Wigner and Dyson
[
19
,
20
]
.
We here focus on the quantum work statistics associated with the driving of a system between two chaotic Hamiltonians, each independently sampled from the Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE), i.e., described by random
N\times N
Hermitian matrices invariant under conjugation by unitaries. Here,
N
is the dimension of the Hilbert space that can be related to the size of a
N_{p}
particle system (for qubits,
N=2^{N_{p}}
).

We shall consider the initial state to be described by the canonical density matrix of a thermal state at inverse temperature
\beta=(k_{B}T)^{-1}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm th}=\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}}{Z(\beta)}\,,

(4)

where
Z(\beta)={\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E_{n}^{0}}
is the partition function of the initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
.
This density matrix, being diagonal in the energy eigenbasis of the initial Hamiltonian, is left unchanged by the first projective energy measurement.
The occupation probability of the
n
-initial eigenmode is thus given by the Boltzmann factor,
p_{n}^{0}=\exp(-\beta E_{n}^{0})/Z(\beta)
where
E_{n}^{0}
is the
n
-th eigenstate of the initial Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
, that is sampled from GUE. The Hamiltonian at the end of the evolution
\hat{H}_{\tau}
is also sampled from the GUE. As a result, we are led to consider the average work pdf
\langle p_{\tau}(W)\rangle
that includes a double GUE average over
\hat{H}_{0}
and
\hat{H}_{\tau}
,

\langle p_{\tau}(W)\rangle=\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\sum_{n,m}p^{0}_{n}\;p^{\tau}_{m|n}\delta\left[W\!-\left(E_{m}^{\tau}-E_{n}^{0}\right)\right]\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle.

(5)

For its study, we shall use the average of an arbitrary operator
\hat{O}
over the probability density function
\rho(\hat{H})
from which realizations of the chaotic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
are sampled in GUE. This average can be written as
[
19
,
23
]

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d\hat{H}\rho(\hat{H})\,\hat{O}=\int DE\,\hat{O}(E)\,,

(6)

where, in terms of the eigenvalues
E_{n}
of
\hat{H}
, the GUE measure
DE
has been defined to include the eigenvalue Vandermonde determinant
\Delta(E)=\prod_{k>j}(E_{k}-E_{j})\,
and all the Gaussian terms in the pdf. Therefore, the normalization condition gives the scaling of eigenvalues with the system size and reads
\int DE=C_{N}\,\int\prod_{k}dE_{k}|\Delta(E)|^{2}e^{-\sum_{j}E_{j}^{2}}=1
, the constant
C_{N}
being given in Ref.
[
19
,
20
]
.

As we have seen, the measurement of work singles out the energy eigenbasis as a preferred basis. In the following, we shall be able to express all the ensemble averages using the density of states
\rho(E)=\sum_{n}\,\delta(E-E_{n})
. When the Hilbert space dimension
N
is large, the averaged
\langle\rho(E)\rangle
is well described by Wigner’s semicircle law
[
19
]
.
A common approach in random matrix theory to account for a finite Hilbert space dimension
N
relies on perturbative expansions in
1/N
. However, in the GUE, the exact
\langle\rho(E)\rangle
for any finite
N
is simply read
[
19
]
,

\displaystyle\langle\rho(E)\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\varphi_{j}(E)^{2}\,,

(7)

where the
\varphi_{j}(E)={(2^{j}j!\sqrt{\pi})^{-1/2}}e^{-\frac{E^{2}}{2}}\mathcal{H}_{j}(E)
are given in terms of the Hermite polynomials
\mathcal{H}_{j}
.
Knowledge of the density of states will allow us to characterize the work statistics in systems of arbitrary size. To this end, we shall also need the connected two-level correlation function
[
19
]
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\rho_{c}^{(2)}(E,E^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\rho(E)\rho(E^{\prime})\rangle-\langle\rho(E)\rangle\langle\rho(E^{\prime})\rangle

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\varphi_{j}(E)\varphi_{j}(E^{\prime})\right)^{2}\,,

that accounts for the presence of correlations between different energy levels.

Thus, the results we obtain in this manuscript are nonperturbative in
N
and account exactly for the finiteness of the Hilbert space dimension,
which can be varied from small to arbitrarily large values. This is of particular importance to describe the quantum work statistics of finite quantum
chaotic systems as well as to explore the connections between work fluctuations and information scrambling.

1.1
Characteristic function and work distribution

The characteristic function of the work pdf is defined as its Fourier transform

\displaystyle\chi(t,\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dWp_{\tau}(W)\,e^{iWt}\,.

(9)

Using the definition of
p_{\tau}(W)
, the explicit form of the characteristic function can be written as

\displaystyle\chi(t,\tau)=\sum_{n}p_{n}^{0}\langle n_{0}|e^{it\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}}e^{-it\hat{H}_{0}}|n_{0}\rangle\,,

(10)

which suggests an interpretation of the variable
t
as a second time of evolution – different from the physical time
s
– with a quench from the initial Hamiltonian to the effective Hamiltonian
[
15
,
22
,
35
,
24
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\tau)\hat{H}_{\tau}\hat{U}(\tau)\,,

(11)

defined in terms of the time-evolution operator
\hat{U}(\tau)
in Eq. (
2
).

In what follows, we aim at deriving an exact analytical solution of the characteristic function valid at finite
N
. We shall focus on the average of this quantity for Hamiltonians sampled from the GUE.
Specifically, for an initial thermal state
\hat{\rho}_{\rm th}
, the characteristic function of the work distribution associated with a sudden quench protocol between two GUE Hamiltonians can be written as

\left\langle\chi(t,\tau)\right\rangle=\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\tau}\hat{H}_{\tau}}\,e^{-\sigma_{0}\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,,

(12)

with
\sigma_{0}=\beta+it,\,\sigma_{\tau}=-it
, and where the brackets denote a double average over the initial and final GUE ensembles.

Using the spectral resolution of the initial and final Hamiltonians, we find

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi(t,\tau)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}\times

(13)

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\sum_{n,m}|\langle m_{\tau}|n_{0}\rangle|^{2}e^{-\sigma_{\tau}E_{m}^{\tau}}e^{-\sigma_{0}E_{n}^{0}}\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,.

Note that in going from (
12
) to (
13
), the average of the fraction has been approximated as the ratio of
the numerator and denominator averages. This kind of approximation is known as annealing in the context of random matrix theory. The resulting ‘annealed’ expression of the characteristic function is required for an analytical treatment, and we verify numerically that it is a good approximation.
Introducing the density of states
\rho(E)=\sum_{n}\,\delta(E-E_{n})

and using the average of the transition probability over the GUE
\left\langle p^{\tau}_{m|n}\right\rangle=1/N

[
23
]
, we find

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi(t,\tau)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\,\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}\iint dE_{0}\,dE_{\tau}\,\langle\rho(E_{0})\rangle\,\langle\rho(E_{\tau})\rangle\,

(14)

\displaystyle\times e^{-\sigma_{\tau}E_{\tau}}\,e^{-\sigma_{0}E_{0}}\,.

A compact form can be derived by defining the Laplace transform of the density of states averaged over a GUE,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\sigma)=\int dE\,\langle\rho(E)\rangle\,e^{-\sigma E}\,.

(15)

It is worth pointing out that the related real Fourier transform of the density of states is used as a tool to measure statistical level properties of systems with a complex energy spectrum
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

The explicit evaluation of (
15
) for finite
N
requires the expression for the GUE density of states (
7
),
and leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\sigma)=e^{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}}L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)=\langle Z(\sigma)\rangle\,,

(16)

where
L_{n}^{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n+\alpha}{n-i}(-x)^{i}/i!
denotes the generalized Laguerre polynomial. Physically,
\mathcal{I}(\sigma)
corresponds to the average of the analytically continued partition function
[
27
]
.
The GUE averaged partition function is readily obtained as,

\displaystyle\langle Z(\beta)\rangle=\mathcal{I}(\beta)\xrightarrow[\beta\rightarrow 0]{}L^{1}_{N-1}(0)=N\,,

(17)

where we verify that in the infinite-temperature limit the partition function measures the Hilbert space dimension
N
.
Thus, we obtain the following compact and explicit expression for the characteristic function

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi(t,\tau)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\frac{\langle Z(\sigma_{0})\rangle\langle Z(\sigma_{\tau})\rangle}{\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}e^{\frac{1}{2}(\beta^{2}-t^{2})}e^{i\frac{\beta t}{2}}\frac{L^{1}_{N-1}(-\frac{(\beta+it)^{2}}{2})L^{1}_{N-1}(\frac{t^{2}}{2})}{L^{1}_{N-1}(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2})}\,.

Note that the characteristics function in the limit of large
N
has also been studied in
[
40
]
.

In the infinite temperature limit, it simplifies to
\left\langle\chi(t,\tau)\right\rangle=e^{-t^{2}/2}[L^{1}_{N-1}(t^{2}/2)]^{2}/N^{2}
and can be written in the form of a Gaussian multiplied by a polynomial of even powers up to
2(N-1)
.
A log-log representation exhibits a number of dips that corresponds to the positive roots. The number of dips is thus
(N-1)
, before a final monotonic decay. Figure
1
presents the analytical results for different temperatures and Hilbert space dimensions. It illustrates the importance of the closed-form expression (
18
) over the numerical simulations that exhibit poor convergence properties.

From the characteristic function in Eq. (
18
), the average work probability distribution can be obtained by inverting the Fourier transform (
9
). The work pdf is found to be given by the auto-convolution of the average density of states
\langle\rho(E)\rangle
with an exponential weight,

\langle p(W)\rangle=\frac{1}{N\mathcal{I}(\beta)}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}dE\langle\rho(E)\rangle\langle\rho(E{+}W)\rangle e^{{-}\beta E}\,.

(19)

We present the average work pdf in Figure
2
, and verify that results obtained from the numerical integration of the above equation coincide with those obtained from direct sampling over many realizations of the GUE. In the infinite temperature limit, the even parity of the density of states
\langle\rho(E)\rangle=\langle\rho(-E)\rangle
carries over the work pdf,
\langle p(W)\rangle=\langle p(-W)\rangle
. As a result, any odd moment of the distribution
\langle W^{n}\rangle=\int dWp(W)W^{n}
, including the mean work (
n=1
), vanishes identically in this limit. Away from it, the work pdf is described by a bell-shaped function whose mean shifts from zero to higher positive values as the temperature of the initial canonical state is decreased.

) (circles), and the numerical solution averaging over 50000 realizations of the GUE (solid lines), at infinite temperature (red) and

. The dimension of the Hilbert space is (a)

.

1.2
Mean work in a random quench

The first moment of the work pdf, the mean work, is of both practical and fundamental interest. It controls the performance of quantum thermodynamic devices such as heat engines and refrigerators. In addition, it can be used to assess irreversibility and inner friction of quantum thermodynamic processes
[
41
,
42
]
.

The mean of the work pdf is directly given by the difference between the initial and final mean energies,

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\hat{H}_{\tau}\rangle-\langle\hat{H}_{0}\rangle

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\!\left\langle{\rm Tr}\left[(\hat{H}_{\tau}-\hat{H}_{0})\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}}{{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)}\right]\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,,

which involves a double GUE average over
\hat{H}_{0}
and
\hat{H}_{\tau}
.
The mean final energy vanishes identically, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{H}_{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\!\left\langle{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{H}_{\tau}\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}}{{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)}\right]\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle=0\,,

(21)

because of the spectral symmetry of the final ensemble.
The mean work is thus set exclusively by the initial mean energy and reads

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle=-\,\langle\hat{H}_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}\frac{d\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}{d\beta}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{2}+\beta\,\frac{L_{N-2}^{2}(-\beta^{2}/2)}{L_{N-1}^{1}(-\beta^{2}/2)}\,.

For high temperature (
\beta\ll 1
), the exact asymptotic behavior is thus

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle\xrightarrow[\beta\rightarrow 0]{}\frac{N}{2}\beta-\frac{N^{2}-1}{24}\beta^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{5})\,,

(23)

verifying the fact that the mean work vanishes exactly in the infinite temperature limit and increases as the temperature is decreased, as seen in Figure
2
.
The mean work is lower-bounded by the low-temperature limit,

\displaystyle\langle W\rangle\xrightarrow[\beta\rightarrow\infty]{}\frac{\beta}{2}\,,

(24)

as in this regime the second term in the r.h.s. of Eq. (
22
) vanishes.
The behavior of the mean work for chaotic systems as a function of the inverse temperature is presented in Figure
3
for different dimensions of the Hilbert space and illustrates the accuracy of the high temperature asymptotes.

Comparing the analytical results with the numerical simulations, we find discrepancies between the ‘annealed’ and exact expression at low temperatures.
This can be understood by the shape of the distribution of the GUE Hamiltonians that, as the temperature lowers, has an increasing non-zero tail that eventually requires more sampling—in contrast with the large
N
limit where the semi-circle distribution ensures a finite sampling domain. Physically, the work, set by (the negative of) the ensemble initial energy, should be bounded at low temperature. This is clearly not the case in the analytical asymptote in Eq. (
24
), which shows the limitations of the annealed approximation made in Eq. (
13
) in the low temperature regime, needed for the pursued analytical treatment.

). The star symbols indicate the conditions for which the detailed characteristic function and work pdf are given in Figs.

1.3
Variance of the work pdf in a random quench

The stochastic nature of work in the presence of quantum thermal fluctuations becomes manifest when considering fluctuations away from the mean
\langle W\rangle
.
We focus on the variance that naturally characterizes the width of the work pdf. As we shall see, it also governs the decay of the Loschmidt echo at early times of evolution
[
24
]
and constitutes the leading contribution to the irreversible entropy in linear response theory
[
43
]
.

An explicit computation shows that the second moment of the work pdf simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle W^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dWp(W)\,W^{2}\right\rangle

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\!\left\langle{\rm Tr}\!\left((\hat{H}_{\tau}^{2}+\hat{H}_{0}^{2})\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}}{{\rm Tr}(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}})}\right)\!\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,,

as a result of the aforementioned symmetry in the final ensemble, that yields

\langle\!\langle\hat{H}_{\tau}\hat{H}_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=0
.
The GUE-averaged expectation value of the second moment for the initial Hamiltonian can be derived from the partition function in Eq. (
17
),

\left\langle{\rm Tr}\left(\hat{H}_{0}^{2}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\right\rangle=\frac{d^{2}\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}{d\beta^{2}}\,.

(26)

By contrast, the second moment of the final Hamiltonian requires averaging over both the initial and final state ensembles, specifically

\displaystyle\left\langle\!\!\left\langle{\rm Tr}\left(\hat{H}_{\tau}^{2}\,e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\sum_{m,n}|\langle m_{\tau}|n_{0}\rangle|^{2}\!\left(E^{\tau}_{m}\right)^{2}\!e^{-\beta E^{0}_{n}}\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p_{m|n}^{\tau}\rangle\left\langle\sum_{m}\left(E^{\tau}_{m}\right)^{2}\right\rangle\left\langle\sum_{n}e^{-\beta E^{0}_{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left.\frac{d^{2}\mathcal{I}(\beta)}{d\beta^{2}}\right|_{\beta=0}\langle Z(\beta)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\langle Z(\beta)\rangle\,.

(27)

Equations (
25
)-(
1.3
), along with the definitions (
16
) and (
17
), give the exact, non-perturbative expression for the work fluctuations as

\displaystyle\langle W^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{2}+\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}\frac{d^{2}\langle Z(\beta)\rangle}{d\beta^{2}}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N{+}1}{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}+(1{+}\beta^{2})\frac{L_{N-2}^{2}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}{L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle+\beta^{2}\frac{L_{N-3}^{3}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}{L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}\,.

Knowledge of the first two moments of the distribution, (
22
) and (
28
), readily yields the work variance

\displaystyle\sigma_{W}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle W^{2}\rangle-\langle W\rangle^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N+1}{2}+\frac{L_{N-2}^{2}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}{L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle+\beta^{2}\left[\frac{L_{N-3}^{3}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}{L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}-\left(\frac{L_{N-2}^{2}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}{L_{N-1}^{1}\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right)}\right)^{2}\right]\,.

At infinite temperature, the work variance saturates at the Hilbert space dimension
N
following the asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle\sigma_{W}^{2}\xrightarrow[\beta\rightarrow 0]{}N-\frac{N^{2}-1}{8}\beta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{4})\,.

(30)

In the low temperature regime, the work variance is still finite and saturates at the value of
(N+1)/2
beyond
\beta\gtrapprox 1.5
.
Both limits are presented in Figure
4
, where the monotonic decay of
\sigma_{W}^{2}
is shown as function of
\beta
for different values of the Hilbert space dimension.
Here, the analytical and numerical results for the annealed approximation match the exact simulation, as the error observed on the average energy (see Figure
3
) is exactly compensated by that on the energy second moment also used to obtain the variance.

in the low-temperature regime. The analytical results (solid lines) are in excellent agreement with the numerical simulations for both the annealed (circles) and the exact (dotted lines) expressions.

2
Loschmidt echo in Chaotic Systems and information scrambling

The study of the work statistics can be recast into a dynamical problem, since the characteristic function of the work pdf can be related to a Loschmidt echo.
A Loschmidt echo quantifies the degree to which a given dynamics can be reversed
[
44
,
45
]
.
The connection between work statistics and Loschmidt echoes was first established for a system initialized in an energy eigenstate and driven by a sudden quench
[
15
]
. Following works extended this relation to arbitrary evolutions by interpreting the characteristic function as a Loschmidt echo amplitude where the backward evolution is generated by an effective Hamiltonian
[
22
,
35
]
. More recently, we have shown that such a connection holds as well for an arbitrary initial state, whether it is pure or not
[
24
]
. In turn, the ability to recast work statistics in terms of a dynamical problem (a Loschmidt echo) for any arbitrary initial states results is an important connection between work statistics and information scrambling.

To appreciate this, we first consider a general initial state
\hat{\rho}
, which may include coherences in the initial energy eigenbasis. Following the first energy measurement associated with the projectors
\hat{P}^{0}_{n}=|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}|
, the post-measurement state becomes
\sum_{n}\hat{P}^{0}_{n}\hat{\rho}\hat{P}^{0}_{n}=\sum_{n}p^{0}_{n}\,|n_{0}\rangle\langle n_{0}|
. This mixed state can be purified by embedding it in an extended Hilbert space, e.g.,
\mathcal{H}_{L}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{R}
, with
\mathcal{H}_{L}=\mathcal{H}_{R}=\mathcal{H}
. The purified state is then given by an entangled state between two copies of the system

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{n}\sqrt{p^{0}_{n}}\,\,|n_{0}\rangle_{L}\otimes|n_{0}\rangle_{R}\,.

(31)

The characteristic function associated with the evolution
\hat{U}(\tau)
in Eq. (
2
) can then be expressed as the survival amplitude
\langle\Psi_{0}|\Psi_{t}\rangle
of the purified state (
31
) when one of the copies of the system evolves under the sudden quench

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}\,\Theta(-t)-\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}\,\Theta(t)\,,

(32)

this is, a sudden quench between the initial Hamiltonian and the effective Hamiltonian
-\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}
defined in Eq. (
11
),
\Theta(t)
denoting the Heaviside function. Specifically, the equivalence becomes explicit by composition of the unitary evolution operators, and reads
[
24
]

\displaystyle\chi(t,\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|\Psi_{t}\rangle

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{0}|e^{+it\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}}e^{-it\hat{H}_{0}}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{R}|\Psi_{0}\rangle\,,

where we emphasize that the quench evolution acts exclusively on one of the copies, e.g., the left one. Thus, the characteristic function in Eq. (
33
) also encodes the dynamics of the Loschmidt echo amplitude of the pure state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
in which the left copy evolves first forward in time under the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
and then backward under the effective Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}
, leaving the right copy unchanged.

We stress here that the connection
\chi(t,\tau)\equiv\langle\Psi_{0}|\Psi_{t}\rangle
is valid for any driving protocol and for any purified state
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
, including those arising from mixed states at finite temperature
[
24
]
. One may thus consider the initial state to be the canonical thermal state, as in Eq. (
4
).
In this particular case, the corresponding purified state is the so-called thermofield double state
[
46
]
,

\displaystyle|{\Phi}_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z(\beta)}}\!\sum_{n}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\hat{H}_{0}\,\otimes\,\mathbb{1}_{R}}\,|n_{0}\rangle_{L}\otimes|n_{0}\rangle_{R}\,.

Its time-evolution is then described by

|\Phi_{t}\rangle=e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hat{H}(t^{\prime})\,\otimes\,\mathbb{1}_{R}}\,|\Phi_{0}\rangle\,,

(35)

with
\hat{H}(t^{\prime})
given in Eq. (
32
). Its survival probability amplitude is equivalent to the characteristic function of the corresponding work statistics, namely

\displaystyle\chi(t,\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{0}\,|\,\Phi_{t}\rangle

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z(\beta)}\,{\rm Tr}\left(e^{+it\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}}e^{-(\beta+it)\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\,.

In this interpretation, the thermofield double state (
2
) is not invariant under time-evolution and the equivalence established above gives the Loschmidt echo
\mathcal{L}(t)
as the fidelity between the initial thermofield double state
|\Phi_{0}\rangle
and its time-evolution, also known as the survival probability:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t)=|\langle\Phi_{0}\,|\,\Phi_{t}\rangle|^{2}\,=\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dWp_{\tau}(W)\,e^{iWt}\right|^{2}\,.

Said differently, the Loschmidt echo of the thermofield double state can be obtained from the Fourier transform of the work pdf.
This observation leads to a direct connection between quantum thermodynamics – the analysis of work statistics – and information scrambling, as characterized by
\mathcal{L}(t)
.

Information scrambling was first discussed in black hole physics
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
. In particular, within the AdS/CFT correspondence, an eternal black hole, or so-called Einstein-Rosen bridge, is dual to the thermofield double state, and described by a state with a highly localized entanglement distribution between the degrees of freedom of the two non-interacting copies of a CFT
[
51
]
. The notion of information scrambling refers to the redistribution of any quantum information, initially localized in a subsystem, over the entire system under unitary time evolution. Thus, in the AdS/CFT framework, it is natural to study the spreading of information in black holes via the survival probability of a thermofield double state, that is, via the fidelity between the initial state and its unitary time evolution, as in Eq. (
36
)
[
25
,
26
,
28
,
29
,
27
]
. The time-evolved state
|\Phi_{t}\rangle
in Eq. (
35
) can be understood, in the gravitational picture, as an Einstein-Rosen bridge which interior has grown
[
52
,
53
]
. This notion of information scrambling has proved useful in a more general setting, in which initially localized information in a subsystem spreads and gets scrambled in the degrees of freedom of a many-body system. In the following, we characterize this dynamics in chaotic quantum systems represented by the Gaussian unitary ensemble.

2.1
Loschmidt Echo in GUE

The Loschmidt echo, defined in Eq. (
2
), averaged over the GUE follows from the characteristic function in Eq. (
12

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\!\Big\langle\frac{1}{{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\right)^{2}}{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\tau}\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}}e^{-\sigma_{0}\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\quad

(38)

\displaystyle\times{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\tau}^{*}\hat{H}_{\tau}^{\rm eff}}e^{-\sigma_{0}^{*}\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\Big\rangle\!\!\Big\rangle\,.

Using the property of the GUE invariance under conjugation by a unitary, the double average reduces to two independent GUE averages, one over
\hat{H}_{0}
and one over
\hat{H}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)^{2}\rangle}\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\left|{\rm Tr}\left(e^{-\sigma_{\tau}\hat{H}_{\tau}}e^{-\sigma_{0}\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\right|^{2}\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,.

Its explicit form can be conveniently written in terms of the partition function and the GUE spectral form factor defined as

\displaystyle g(\beta,t)\equiv\left\langle Z(\beta+it)Z(\beta-it)\right\rangle\,.

(40)

As shown in Appendix
A
, it is found that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)^{2}\rangle}\,\frac{1}{N^{2}{-}1}\Big(g(0,t)g(\beta,t)

(41)

\displaystyle+N\langle Z(2\beta)\rangle-\frac{1}{N}\langle Z(2\beta)\rangle g(0,t)-\frac{1}{N}g(\beta,t)N\Big)\,.

The dependence of the Loschmidt echo on the spectral form factor illustrates the importance of correlations between energy eigenvalues.
In the infinite-temperature regime (
\beta=0
), this result simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\frac{1}{N^{2}{-}1}\Big(g(0,t)^{2}{+}N^{2}{-}2g(0,t)\Big)\,.

The Loschmidt echo can thus be fully characterized from the GUE spectral form factor (

2.3
Loschmidt echo dynamics

With the exact analytical expressions at hand, we proceed to characterize the dynamics of the Loschmidt echo.
The Loschmidt echo is shown in Figure
5
in two different panels corresponding to infinite temperature (
\beta=0
) and a finite value (
\beta=1

In both cases, the decay dynamics is shown for different values of the Hilbert space dimension, and one can distinguish a sequence of three stages. The Loschmidt echo first decays monotonically until reaching a minimum value. The second stage is characterized by a broad dip in which the Loschmidt echo stabilizes around
1/N^{2}
and gradually starts to grow.
Subsequently, a saturation of the Loschmidt echo occurs around an approximately constant value.

The short time behavior of the Loschmidt echo was first given in our recent work
[
24

and is governed by work fluctuations – the variance of the work pdf – according to

\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\big\langle e^{-t^{2}\sigma_{W}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})}\big\rangle\geq e^{-t^{2}\langle\sigma_{W}^{2}\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})}\,,

(46)

where we have used Jensen’s inequality.
For
\beta\penalty\ =\penalty\ 0

N
, leading to a Gaussian decay that is simply given by

, as verified in Figure

As for the long-time asymptotics, it is best analyzed in terms of the spectral form factor. The latter can be written, using Eq. (

\displaystyle g(\beta,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle Z(2\beta)\rangle

\displaystyle+\left\langle\sum_{n}\sum_{n^{\prime}\neq n}e^{-\beta(E_{n}+E_{n^{\prime}})}e^{-it(E_{n}-E_{n^{\prime}})}\right\rangle\,.

At long times, the complex exponential exhibits rapid oscillations and only the first term survives under a short time average that enters in the feature of a plateau.

\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\frac{\langle Z(2\beta)\rangle}{\langle Z(\beta)^{2}\rangle}\frac{2}{(N+1)}

2.4
Loschmidt Echo, frame potentials and scrambling

As discussed in Section
2
, quantum work statistics can be used to assess information scrambling, as the generating function of the work pdf is equivalent to the Loschmidt echo amplitude of a thermofield double state.
Scrambling amounts to the effective randomization of the state of a quantum system subject to unitary time evolution. Depending on the dynamics, scrambling produces different degrees of randomness. There is a wide variety of approaches to pursue its study, including the use of survival probabilities, analytically-continued partition functions, and out-of-time-order correlators, among other examples.

In black hole physics, the common reference is the Haar/Page information scrambling, which consists in drawing Haar unitaries from the Haar ensemble. This yields a complete randomization of a quantum state, making it impossible to recover information about the initial state by carrying out only local measurements on the system. In this context, it is relevant to analyze the extent to which a concrete ensemble of unitaries can match Haar scrambling.

Frame potentials have been argued to act in this context as natural probes of the randomness created by a concrete time evolution, by quantifying an ensemble ability to reproduce moments of the Haar random unitary ensemble
[
30
]
.
The
k
-th frame potential with respect to an ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\mathcal{E}}=\int_{\hat{A},\hat{B}\,\in\mathcal{E}}D\hat{A}\,D\hat{B}\left|\,{\rm Tr}\,\hat{A}^{\dagger}\,\hat{B}\,\right|^{2k}\,.

(49)

For any ensemble,
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\mathcal{E}}\geq\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\rm Haar}
,
where the Haar ensemble frame potential is
\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\rm Haar}=k!
.

In our case, the relevant ensemble consists of the unitary time evolutions at a fixed time
t
with Hamiltonians drawn from the GUE and reads

\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\rm GUE}=\big\{e^{-i\hat{H}t},\penalty\ {\rm for}\penalty\ \hat{H}\in{\rm GUE}\big\}\,.

Remarkably, we note that the
k=1
frame potential with respect to the GUE is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)=\left\langle\!\!\left\langle\left|\,{\rm Tr}\,\left(e^{it\hat{H}_{\tau}}e^{-it\hat{H}_{0}}\right)\,\right|^{2}\right\rangle\!\!\right\rangle\,.

(50)

This is directly related to the expression of the GUE-averaged Loschmidt echo in Eq. (
2.1
) at infinite temperature as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\,\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)\quad{\rm for}\quad\beta=0\,.

(51)

This correspondence thus shows that whenever
\mathcal{L}(t)\approx 1/N^{2}\,
, the frame potential
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)\approx 1
, indicating that the GUE time evolution achieves Haar randomness.
At very early times
g(0,0)=N^{2}
, and the dominant term
g(0,t)^{2}
yields
\mathcal{L}(t)\approx 1
and
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)\approx N^{2}
, showing that the GUE time evolution is far from achieving Haar randomness. At very late times,
g(0,t\to\infty)\approx N
, which implies that
\mathcal{L}(t)\approx 2/N^{2}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)\approx 2
. Between these two limits, there is a timescale for which the spectral form factor is about
\sqrt{N}
, yielding
\mathcal{L}(t)\approx 1/N^{2}
as far as
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)\approx N^{2}/(N^{2}-1)\approx 1
, and signaling a GUE-mediated Haar scrambling. The relation between
\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle
and
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\rm GUE}(t)
can be extended to other correlation functions, as discussed in Appendix
C
.

However, the relation shown in Eq. (
51
) is restricted to the infinite temperature limit, since the first frame potential for GUE Hamiltonians
[
30
]

\mathcal{F}_{{\rm GUE}}^{(1)}(\beta,t)=\frac{1}{N^{2}{-}1}\Big(g(\beta/2,t)^{2}+N^{2}-2g(\beta/2,t)\Big)\,,

(52)

cannot be directly related to the expression for
\mathcal{L}(t)
in Eq. (
41
) at finite temperature
\beta>0
.

3
Summary and Discussion

The work associated with driving a quantum mechanical system can be determined via two-projective energy measurements and is
characterized by a probability distribution, reflecting the stochastic nature of this quantity.
Analyzing the work statistics of complex systems is generally challenging due to the number of transitions generated during the driving between the energy levels of the initial and final Hamiltonians of the system. Indeed, the latter blows up exponentially as the size of the system is increased.

In this manuscript, we have reported the exact work statistics of quantum chaotic systems of arbitrary size that are described by random matrix Hamiltonians.
In particular, we have characterized the mean work and work fluctuations resulting from driving a canonical thermal state by a sudden quench. We have further shown that the generating function of the work pdf can be interpreted as the Loschmidt echo of the purified thermal state, the thermofield double state. The dynamics of this Loschmidt echo accounts for the reversibility of quenches between chaotic Hamiltonians.
This dynamics also describes the scrambling of information of the thermofield double state, which acquires a natural interpretation in AdS/CFT being dual to an eternal black hole. The connection between work statistics and information scrambling has been established by mapping the work pdf to the Loschmidt echo, that can be expressed in terms of the spectral form factor is related to the frame potential.

Our work establishes an unexpected and firm connection between different fields, including quantum thermodynamics, the emergence of irreversibility, and information scrambling.
This connection is amenable to experimental tests in a variety of quantum platforms. A prominent example is offered by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments, in which measurements of work statistics
[
12
]
, Loschmidt echo dynamics in chaotic systems
[
54
]
and information scrambling
[
55
]
have been reported. Alternative setups include trapped ions
[
13
,
14
,
56
]
and superconducting qubits
[
57
]
, where digital quantum simulation techniques
[
58
,
59
,
60
]
can be used to explore quantum chaos and systems described by AdS/CFT that exhibit scrambling
[
61
]
.
Chaotic systems could also be implemented in controllable many-body systems by applying random pulses to create an evolution with a Haar-uniform distribution
[
62
]
.

Furthermore, our results should find broad applications in quantum thermodynamics of many-body chaotic systems.
Quantum many-body systems can be used as a working medium in thermodynamic devices including heat engines and refrigerators
[
63
,
64
,
65
]

and it is natural to explore the role of quantum chaos in this context.

we obtain the following expression

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)^{2}\rangle}\,\frac{1}{N^{2}{-}1}\bigg[

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!Z(\sigma_{\tau})Z(\sigma_{\tau}^{*})\Big\rangle\,\Big\langle\!Z(\sigma_{0})Z(\sigma_{0}^{*})\Big\rangle+\Big\langle Z\big(2\sigma_{0}^{R}\big)\Big\rangle\,\Big\langle Z\big(2\sigma_{\tau}^{R}\big)\Big\rangle

(59)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}\Big\langle Z\big(2\sigma_{0}^{R}\big)\Big\rangle\Big\langle Z(\sigma_{\tau})Z(\sigma_{\tau}^{*})\Big\rangle-\frac{1}{N}\Big\langle Z(\sigma_{0})Z(\sigma_{0}^{*})\Big\rangle\Big\langle Z\big(2\sigma_{\tau}^{R}\big)\Big\rangle\bigg]\,,

where
\sigma^{R}
denotes the real part
{\rm Re}(\sigma)
.
This expression can be conveniently written in terms of the spectral form factor as shown in Eq. (
41
) of the main text.

Alternative derivation.—
The average of the Loschmidt echo over the GUE can be obtained via an alternative route. As any Hermitian Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
can be diagonalized by a unitary similarity transformation, it is possible to express the GUE average in Eq. (
6
) as an average over the eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{E}
and
N(N-1)
angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}
associated with the unitary
[
23
]
,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{O}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\hat{H}\,\rho(\hat{H})\,\hat{O}=\int d\boldsymbol{E}d\boldsymbol{\theta}\rho_{\boldsymbol{E}}(\boldsymbol{E})\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\hat{O}(\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,,

(60)

where
\rho_{\boldsymbol{E}}(\boldsymbol{E})
and
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
are the corresponding distributions given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\boldsymbol{E}}(\boldsymbol{E})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{N(N-1)/2}}{\pi^{N/2}\prod_{n=1}^{N}n!}\prod_{k>j}(E_{k}-E_{j})^{2}e^{-\sum_{k}E_{k}^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N(N-1)}}\,.

(61)

Since the energy eigenvalues
\boldsymbol{E}
and the angles
\boldsymbol{\theta}
are statistically independent from each other, the double average in Eq. (
2.1
) factorizes as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{\langle Z(\beta)^{2}\rangle}\,\sum_{n,n^{\prime}}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\left\langle p_{m|n}^{\tau}p_{m^{\prime}|n^{\prime}}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}\left\langle e^{-\sigma_{\tau}E_{m}^{\tau}-\sigma_{\tau}^{*}E_{m^{\prime}}^{\tau}}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{E}^{\tau}}\!\left\langle e^{-\sigma_{0}E_{n}^{0}-\sigma_{0}^{*}E_{n^{\prime}}^{0}}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{E}^{0}}\,,

(62)

where we have made use of the transition probabilities, as in Eq. (
13
).
While the average of the transition probabilities generates a uniform distribution, known as
\left\langle p_{m|n}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}=1/N

[
23
]
, the average of two transition probabilities is quite more involved and has not yet been reported.
The difficulty in evaluating this expression comes from the fact that the second transition is not independent from the first one, creating a complex combinational problem. Inspired by the approach in the previous derivation, we here find its explicit form using integration over the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(N)
.

Let us first note the explicit the expression, dropping the indices on the eigenvalues for clarity,

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{m|n}^{\tau}p_{m^{\prime}|n^{\prime}}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\langle m|U(\tau)|n\rangle\langle n|U^{\dagger}(\tau)|m\rangle\langle m^{\prime}|U(\tau)|n^{\prime}\rangle\langle n^{\prime}|U^{\dagger}(\tau)|m^{\prime}\rangle\!\Big\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle U_{m,n}(\tau)U^{\dagger}_{n,m}(\tau)U_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}(\tau)U^{\dagger}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\tau)\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}\,.

Because the GUE measure is invariant under unitary conjugation, we can equivalently evaluate this average over angles as an integral over the unitary group
\mathcal{U}(N)
, i.e.

\int_{\mathcal{U}(N)}dUU_{m,n}U^{\dagger}_{n,m}U_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime}}U^{\dagger}_{n^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\,.

(64)

Recognizing here the second moment of the Haar integral, the evaluation of this expression can be done using the Weingarten functions as detailed above and yields

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{m|n}^{\tau}p_{m^{\prime}|n^{\prime}}^{\tau}\right\rangle_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\tau}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}-1}\Big(1+\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{N}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{N}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\Big)\,.

(65)

This expression can be used to compute the exact average Loschmidt echo Eq. (
41
), upon substituting it into Eq. (
62
) and evaluating the energy averages.

Appendix B
Spectral form factor

We derive here an explicit form of the spectral form factor that provides an alternative form to the result in
[
27
]
, with a corrected sign and expression. Starting with the definition of the connected form factor in (
44
), we expand the square value in Eq. (
8
) and write

\displaystyle g_{c}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\int dEdE^{\prime}\varphi_{n}(E)\varphi_{m}(E)e^{-\sigma E}\varphi_{m}(E^{\prime})\varphi_{n}(E^{\prime})e^{-\sigma^{*}E^{\prime}}

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n,m=0}^{N-1}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)}{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\mathcal{I}_{m,n}(\sigma^{*})}{2^{m}m!\sqrt{\pi}}\,,

where the second line has been obtained using the definition of
\varphi_{n}(E)
given in the main text and introducing the integral over the Hermite polynomials

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)=\int dEe^{-E^{2}-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{n}(E)\mathcal{H}_{m}(E)\,.

(67)

Note the symmetrical role played by
(n,m)
in this function.
To find an explicit form, we follow
[
27
]
and, for
n\leq m
, use the identity
\int_{\infty}dEe^{-E^{2}}\mathcal{H}_{n}(E)f(E)=\int_{\infty}dEe^{-E^{2}}D^{n}f(E)
for
f(E)=e^{-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{m}(E)
to write

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)=\int dEe^{-E^{2}}D^{n}(e^{-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{m}(E))\,.

(68)

The differential
D^{n}=(d/dE)^{n}
can be expanded in a binomial form,
D^{n}(e^{-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{m}(E))=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}D^{k}(e^{-\sigma E})D^{n-k}(\mathcal{H}_{m}(E))
, and leads, using the derivatives of the Hermite polynomials
D^{j}(\mathcal{H}_{m}(E))=2^{j}m!/(m-j)!\mathcal{H}_{m-j}(E)
, to a sum of single-Hermite integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{m!}{(m-n+k)!}2^{n-k}(-\sigma)^{k}\int dEe^{-E^{2}-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{m-n+k}(E)\,.

(69)

Using the same identity with
f(E)=e^{-\sigma E}
, the last integral can be evaluated as
[
27
]

\displaystyle\int dEe^{-E^{2}-\sigma E}\mathcal{H}_{m-n+k}(E)=\mathcal{I}_{m-n+k}(\sigma)=\sqrt{\pi}(-\sigma)^{m-n+k}e^{\sigma^{2}/4}\,.

(70)

We further note the simplification of the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{x^{k}}{(m-n+k)!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{m!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{m}{n-k}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}=\frac{n!}{m!}L_{n}^{m-n}(-x)

(71)

in terms of the generalized Laguerre polynomials.
After symmetrization and defining
\tilde{n}\equiv{\rm min}(n,m)
, this gives Eq. (
69
) as

\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)=\sqrt{\pi}e^{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}}(-\sigma)^{|m-n|}\,2^{\tilde{n}}\,\tilde{n}!\,L_{\tilde{n}}^{|m-n|}(-\sigma^{2}/2)\,.

(72)

Using this expression and the fact that
\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)\mathcal{I}_{m,n}(\sigma^{*})=|\mathcal{I}_{n,m}(\sigma)|^{2}
, we obtain an analytical expression for the connected form factor, Eq. (
66
), given in Eq. (
45
) of the main text.

Appendix C
Loschmidt echo and correlation functions

Finally, following
[
69
]
we briefly comment on a connection between the GUE-averaged Loschmidt echo and correlation functions at infinite temperature. We focus on a particular time-ordered
2k
-point correlation function

{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{A}_{1}\,\hat{B}_{1}(t)\cdots\hat{A}_{k}\,\hat{B}_{k}(t)\,\hat{\rho}\right\}\,,

(73)

where
\hat{A}_{1},\cdots,\hat{A}_{k}
and
\hat{B}_{1},\cdots,\hat{B}_{k}
are Pauli operators acting on a system of
N_{p}
qubits and the dimension of the Hilbert space of the system is
N=2^{N_{p}}
. This can be understood as a correlator evaluated at infinite temperature (
\beta=0
), i.e, for the completely mixed state
\hat{\rho}=\mathbb{1}/N
.
Here
\hat{B}_{j}(t)\equiv\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)\,\hat{B}_{j}\,\hat{U}(t)
, with the unitary
\hat{U}(t)\in\mathcal{E}_{t}^{\rm GUE}
.
The average of these kind of out-of-time ordered correlation functions for an ensemble of unitary operators such as
\mathcal{E}_{t}^{GUE}
can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{GUE}}\,d\hat{U}\,{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{A}_{1}\,\hat{B}_{1}(t)\cdots\hat{A}_{k}\,\hat{B}_{k}(t)\,\hat{\rho}\right\}\,.

(74)

These ensemble averaged correlators were shown to be related to the
k-
th frame potential through
[
69
]

\displaystyle\frac{N^{4k}}{N^{2(k+1)}}\,\mathcal{F}^{(k)}_{\rm GUE}=\sum_{\left\{\hat{A}\right\}\,\left\{\hat{B}\right\}}\left|{\int_{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{GUE}}}d\hat{U}\,{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{A}_{1}\,\hat{B}_{1}(t)\cdots\hat{A}_{k}\,\hat{B}_{k}(t)\,\hat{\rho}\right\}\right|^{2}\,,

(75)

where the sum is over the
N^{4k}
possible Pauli operators
\hat{A}_{1},\cdots,\hat{A}_{k}\,,\hat{B}_{1},\cdots,\hat{B}_{k}
. Thus, considering the case for
k=1

and the correspondence established in Eq. (
51
), it is possible to express the GUE-averaged Loschmidt echo in terms of an averaged 2-point function

\left\langle\mathcal{L}(t)\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\!\!\!\sum_{\left\{\hat{A}\right\}\,\left\{\hat{B}\right\}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{E}_{t}^{GUE}}\,d\hat{U}\,{\rm Tr}\left\{\hat{A}\,\hat{B}(t)\,\hat{\rho}\right\}\right|^{2}\,,

(76)

both measuring through their decrease, the degree of randomness induced by GUE-time evolution.
