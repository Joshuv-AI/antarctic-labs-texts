Third quantization of open quantum systems: new dissipative symmetries and connections to phase-space and Keldysh field theory formulations
Alexander McDonald, Aashish A. Clerk

Abstract
The connections between standard theoretical tools used to study open quantum systems can sometimes seem opaque. Whether it is a Lindblad master equation, the equation of motion for the Wigner function or a dissipative Keldysh action, features evident in one formalism are often masked in another. Here, we reformulate the technique of third quantization in a way that explicitly connects all three methods. We first show that our formulation reveals a fundamental dissipative symmetry present in all quadratic bosonic or fermionic Lindbladians. This symmetry can then be used to easily diagonalize these models, and provides a intuitive way to demonstrate the separation of dissipation and fluctations in linear systems. For bosons, we then show that the Wigner function and the characteristic function can be thought of as “wavefunctions” of the density matrix in the eigenbasis of the third-quantized superoperators we introduce. The field-theory representation of the time-evolution operator in this basis is then the Keldysh path integral. To highlight the utility of our approach, we apply our version of third quantization to a dissipative non-linear oscillator, and use it to obtain new exact results.



I
Introduction

Open quantum systems are generically much more difficult to characterize than their closed-system counterparts. Quadratic systems of bosons or fermions coupled to Markovian baths are arguably an exception: computing average values and correlation functions is straightforward using either the master equation, Keldysh field theory or the Heisenberg-Langevin equations. However, it is often useful to understand the full structure of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Lindbladian.
This information can help answer questions that are difficult to address using Keldysh or Langevin equations, e.g. the full time evolution of an arbitrarily complicated non-Gaussian initial state. In a set of seminal works, Prosen
Prosen 2008
and Prosen and Seligman
Prosen and Seligman 2010
introduced the technique of third quantization, which allows one to obtain the spectral decomposition of quadratic multi-mode Lindbladians in a manner that is analogous to diagonalizing a second-quantized quadratic Hamiltonian. For all its virtues and widespread use in the literature
Lieu et al. 2020
;
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Costa et al. 2022
;
Talkington and Claassen 2022
;
Ribeiro and Prosen 2019
;
Nakanishi and Sasamoto 2022
;
Yang et al. 2022
;
Alaeian and Buča 2022
;
Shibata and Katsura 2019
, the method as presented does not provide an intuitive picture of open quantum system dynamics. Further, it is not a priori clear how it is related to more standard methods. A more physically-transparent formulation of third quantization which addresses these issues could thus help make it an even more powerful tool.

In this work we present an alternate reformulation of third quantization which satisfies both criteria simultaneously. This is achieved by introducing a new set of canonical superoperators, defined in Eq. (
50
) for bosons and Eq. (
74
) for fermions. In this basis, one directly identifies the two pieces of data that determine any quadratic Lindbladian: a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian which acts as an effective dynamical matrix, and a second matrix which encodes the noise. This separation of dissipation and fluctuations further reveals a fundamental symmetry of all such models. We show that this symmetry can be used to effectively gauge away the fluctuation terms in the master equation using a novel similarity transformation implemented at the superoperator level. This provides a simple quantum-oriented way to demonstrate that
the eigenvalues of the Lindbladian are independent of noise in linear systems
.

The superoperators we introduce also make the connection between the Lindbladian and the Keldysh formalism evident. We make this notion exact by formulating a path integral representation of third quantization over a finite time contour. For bosons, we go further by demonstrating that third quantization is naturally linked to phase-space representations of the density matrix. Namely, we show that the Wigner function and its characteristic function can be thought of as wavefunctions in the basis of what is essentially the third-quantized equivalent of position and momentum eigenkets. We use this insight to study a dissipative interacting model, a thermally-damped Kerr cavity. Our approach lets us
analytically describe the time-evolution of cavity’s Wigner function, starting from an

II
Diagonalizing a Liouvillian using third quantization

II.1
Setup

While the diagonalization method we discuss can be applied to arbitrary multi-mode bosonic quadratic Lindbladians, for clarity we discuss these ideas in the simplest possible setting: a harmonic oscillator coupled to a thermal Markovian bath.
Our starting point is the equation of motion for the density matrix (with
\hbar=1
throughout)

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}]+i\kappa(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle+i\kappa\bar{n}_{\rm th}\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}\equiv\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}

(1)

where
\hat{a}
is the bosonic annihilation operator of the oscillator,
\omega_{0}
is its frequency,
\kappa
its decay rate and
\bar{n}_{\rm th}
is the thermal occupation of the bath. Here, we have defined the usual dissipator as
\mathcal{D}[\hat{X}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{X}\hat{\rho}\hat{X}^{\dagger}-\{\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{X},\hat{\rho}\}/2
and
\mathcal{L}
as the Liouvillian superoperator. Our goal is to diagonalize
\mathcal{L}
. It is convenient to think of operators as being elements of a Hilbert space
\hat{\rho}\to|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle
with the usual Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
Albert 2018

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{X}|\hat{Y}\rangle\rangle\equiv\Tr\left(\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{Y}\right)

(2)

for arbitrary operators
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
1
1

1

Technically, for the infinite-dimensional spaces under consideration, both
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
must be trace class to be part of this Hilbert space of operators. This is cumbersome, since operators of interest such as
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
do not satisfy this property. We do not concern ourselves with these technical details. We will thus use the language and notation that one uses for a normal Hilbert space.
.

Using this notation, superoperators become operators acting on this new space and we thus write them with hats. To differentiate them from operators acting on the Hilbert space of wavefunctions, we write all superoperators using a bold typeface
\mathcal{L}\to\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}.
The inner product Eq. (
2
) then allows us to define the adjoint of any superoperator. For instance the adjoint Liouvillian, which controls the time evolution of observables, is in standard second quantized form given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\hat{Y}=\omega_{0}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{Y}]-i\kappa(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{a}]\hat{Y}-i\kappa\bar{n}_{\rm th}\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\hat{Y}

(3)

with
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{X}]\hat{Y}=\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{Y}\hat{X}-\{\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{X},\hat{Y}\}/2
the adjoint dissipator. As the Linbladian is non-Hermitian
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}\neq\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}^{\dagger}
, to diagonalize it we must find both its right and left eigenvectors. That is, we seek operators and complex numbers which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}|\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle=|\mathcal{L}\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=E_{\mu,\nu}|\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle

(4a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}|\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=\langle\langle\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}|

\displaystyle=E_{\mu,\nu}\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}|

(4b)

where, with some foresight, we have labelled the eigenvectors by two non-negative integers
\mu,\nu\geq 0
.

Although the physical interpretation of the right and left eigenvectors may not be straightforward, the eigenvalues have a simple intuitive meaning. The real part of
E_{\mu,\nu}
plays the role of energy differences between eigenstates, whereas the imaginary component dictates the rate of decay. For the linear system of interest however, just as in the classical case, the characteristic oscillation and decay rates are independent of temperature. This simply reflects the fact that a linear system’s response to noise is always independent of the noise itself, something that is clear e.g. by using Langevin equations to describe our system. We thus conclude that the eigenvalues of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
must not depend on
\bar{n}_{\rm th}
.
A crucial question though still remains: how do we rigorously identify these fluctuations and reach this conclusion directly from the master equation?

II.2
Classical and Quantum Superoperators

We argue that this can be straightforwardly done by introducing appropriate third-quantized superoperators defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{a},\hat{\rho}\}\rangle\rangle,\hskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{a},\hat{\rho}]\rangle\rangle

(5a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\rangle\rangle,\hskip 8.5359pt

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}]\rangle\rangle

(5b)

with which we refer to as classical and quantum superoperators. The naming convention is in analogy with Keldysh field theory, which is justified by writing the Lindbladian in this basis of superoperators

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=

\displaystyle(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle-i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}.

(6)

This form directly mimics the structure of the Keldysh action for this same model
Kamenev 2011
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
; we make this connection more explicit in Sec
IV
. Just as in the field theory description, the coefficients of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
serve as an effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, whereas the term
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
should be thought of as the fluctuations that must accompany the dissipation
Clerk et al. 2010
. Although
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
are the conjugate of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
, they are not the creation operators of true bosonic modes since
[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}]=0
. Thus, we cannot interpret
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
as number operators.

Rather, despite being non-Hermitian, we should think of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
as operators whose eigenvalues correspond to a number of quanta in a mode. This follows since the only non-vanishing commutation relation are

\displaystyle[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger},-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}]=\bm{\hat{1}},

(7)

with
\bm{\hat{1}}
the identity superoperator. Given their status as number operators, to determine the eigenvectors of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
we must first find the “vacuums” of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
. These are simply the parity and identity operator

\displaystyle\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\equiv 2e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle=\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle=0

(8a)

\displaystyle\hat{0}_{\rm q}\equiv\hat{1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}=0

(8b)

which follows since
e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}
and
\hat{1}
respectively anti-commute and commute with both
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
. Using Schur’s lemma, these vacuums are unique. In complete analogy with a simple harmonic oscillator, we can obtain the simultaneous eigenvectors of these number operators by repeatedly applying the appropriate creation operators on the vacuums. Thus, we find that

\displaystyle|\hat{r}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger})^{\mu}(-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q})^{\nu}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle

(9a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{l}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}|,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl})^{\mu}(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger})^{\nu}

(9b)

are the right and left eigenvectors of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
with eigenvalue
\mu
and
-\nu
respectively.

The above eigenvectors are unfortunately not eigenvectors of the Liouvillian: while the first two terms in Eq. (
6
) are proportional to our generalized number operators, the last term is not. We next show that there is a simply way to deal with this.

II.3
Gauging away the noise

Recall that we have already reasoned that fluctuations in this linear system should never affect the eigenvalues of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
, only its eigenvectors. We should thus be able to remove the temperature-dependent noise term
-i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
entirely using a similarity transformation. This can indeed be achieved by defining

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\equiv e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}},

(10)

and using the commutation relations Eq. (
7
) along with the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff identity to verify that

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}^{-1}\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}=(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}.

(11)

Since this superoperator and
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
are isospectral, we confirm that as promised, the temperature does not effect the eigenvalues of the Lindbladian.
Further, the transformed Lindbladian is now just a sum of our generalized number operators, and hence is diagonalized by the eigenvectors introduced in Eqs. (
9a
)-(
9b
). Note that the transformed Lindbladian is not in Lindblad form due to the presence of a negative rate

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{-1}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{V}\hat{\rho}=\omega_{0}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}]+i\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}-\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}\right),

(12)

which is however irrelevant for diagonalization purposes
2
2

2

One should be careful when working with the inverse of
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
, since as we we show in Sec.
III
it is unbounded. One can always write down expression which involve only
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
however, e.g.
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}|\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\rangle\rangle=\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\left[(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger})\right]|\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\rangle\rangle

.

We have thus made a non-trivial mapping at the level of master equations from a noisy damped harmonic oscillator to its fluctuation-free equivalent. Our ability to remove the noise term exactly is a consequence of the usual separation of dynamics and fluctuations in linear systems, yet here there are quantum consequences, seeing as how the eigenmodes of the Liouvillian have a Fock state structure.

We pause to note that one could imagine adding a quadratic term to our Liouvillian that would break our symmetry, i.e. a term proportional to
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
. The similarity transformation
e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}
would no longer bring
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
to a diagonal form, since the former commutes with the quantum superoperators but not the classical ones. Such a term is however forbidden by the trace-preserving nature of the dynamics
\Tr(\mathcal{L} \hrho)=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle=0
, which a classical-classical term would violate. At a quantum level, being able to eliminate the noise can be understood as a special kind of symmetry; the combination of linearity and conservation of probability.

This structure and an analogous transformation to Eq. (
10
) will also be present in the multi-mode bosonic case considered in Sec.
V
. A comparable symmetry, transformation and conclusion will also be presented for fermionic master equations in Sec.
VI
. In non-linear quantum systems, which are of course also probability-conserving, fluctuations can impact the dynamics as we show explicitly in Sec.

II.4
Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and non-Hermitian quasiparticles

With Eq. (
11
) already in diagonal form, the similarity transformation allows us to simply relate the eigenvectors
\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu},\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}
of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
to
\hat{r}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu},\hat{l}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}
, those of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
. They are given by

\displaystyle|\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}|\hat{r}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger})^{\mu}(-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q})^{\nu}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle,

(13a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}|

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\hat{l}^{\prime}_{\mu,\nu}|e^{(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}\langle\langle{\hat{0}_{\rm q}}|(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}))^{\mu}(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}))^{\nu}

(13b)

with corresponding eigenvalues

\displaystyle E_{\mu,\nu}=(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\mu-(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\nu.

(14)

Here we have identified
\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}
as the unique zero-eigenvalue eigenvector of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
, i.e. the steady state of dissipative dynamics. We have also defined

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})\equiv\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}^{-1}=\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}

(15a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})\equiv\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}^{-1}=\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}

(15b)

where note that since
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
is not unitary,
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})
is not the conjugate of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})
despite the notation. This is however of no concern, seeing as the similarity transformations preserve all commutation relations, e.g.

\displaystyle[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}),\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}),-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}]=\bm{\hat{1}}.

(16)

We can thus identify
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}),\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}),\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
as the correct independent “non-Hermitian quasiparticle” superoperators which create and destroy excitations on top of the their vacuum with a definite energy and decay rate. Although the vacuum of the quantum superoperators is the identity, the right vacuum of these new classical superoperators is the steady state

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=0,

(17)

which is of course explicitly temperature-dependent.

The superoperators
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}),\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})
can also be interpreted as having displaced the classical superoperators by fluctuations. This can be made more precise by noting that
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
does not affect average values of the creation or annihilation operators
\sqrt{2}\langle\hat{a}(t)\rangle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle

=\sqrt{2}e^{-i\omega_{0}t-\frac{\kappa}{2}t}\langle\hat{a}(0)\rangle
, consistent with the zero-mean nature of the noise. Higher-order correlation functions like average particle number however are affected by fluctuations, which is why the original noise-free classical superoperators cannot be used to build up the left eigenvectors. For instance

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\bar{n}_{\rm th})|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-\kappa t}\left[\langle\{\hat{a}(0),\hat{a}^{\dagger}(0)\}\rangle-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\right]

(18)

has the simple exponential decay characteristic of a left eigenvector whereas its noise-free equivalent

\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle=\langle\{\hat{a}(t),\hat{a}^{\dagger}(t)\}\rangle=e^{-\kappa t}\langle\{\hat{a}(0),\hat{a}^{\dagger}(0)\}\rangle

+(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)(1-e^{-\kappa t})
does not.

II.5
Remaining unsatisfactory issues

This completes the diagonalization of a single thermally-damped oscillator (something that can be easily generalized to the multi-mode case, as we show below). There are however two unsatisfactory features of the procedure presented above. First, the similarity transformation
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
at this stage does not seem to possess a simple physical interpretation. For instance, it is well-known that the steady state is a Gaussian state, with an average particle number of
\bar{n}_{\rm th}
. In contrast, our procedure simply asserts that it is equivalent to
e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle
. Is there a direct way to see the connection here, that is

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\bar{n}_{\rm th}}{\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}\right)^{n}|n\rangle\langle n|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}2e^{-(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+\frac{1}{2})[\hat{a}^{\dagger},[\hat{a},\cdot]]}e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}.

(19)

It is at this stage not clear how we should think about this result or even explicitly demonstrate this equality. Second, the right eigenvectors are given by nested commutators of
\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{a}
and the steady state; their explicit second-quantized form is tedious to obtain and largely uninformative. Take for example the right eigenvector describing population decay with a characteristic decay rate of
2\kappa
,

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{2,2}=\frac{1}{8\bar{n}_{\rm th}^{2}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)^{2}}\left(\hat{n}^{2}-(4\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\hat{n}+2\bar{n}_{\rm th}^{2}\right)\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}

(20)

with
\hat{n}\equiv\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
. At first glance, there is no obvious intuitive way to think about such an eigenvector, let alone a simple connection to a 2-excitation Fock state.

III
Equivalence between third-quantization and phase-space formalism

While seemingly devoid of physical content, we shall now demonstrate that both the similarity transformation
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
in Eq. (
10
) and eigenvectors
\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}
,
\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}
of Eq. (
13
) have transparent phase-space representations. The continuous nature of phase space arises by asking a natural question: what are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the classical and quantum superoperators? As we now show, the answer to this question leads to the main results of this section: the similarity transformation
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
is equivalent to a phase-space convolution with a Gaussian of width
2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1
and the eigenvectors of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}

III.1
Classical and quantum eigenvectors

Thankfully, the eigenvectors of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
are already familiar from quantum optics. For any complex number
\alpha
and
\eta
let us define corresponding displaced parity and displacement operators
Bishop and Vourdas 1994
respectively

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle\equiv 2\hat{D}(\sqrt{2}\alpha)e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}},

(21a)

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{D}(\sqrt{2}\eta)

(21b)

with
\hat{D}(\alpha)\equiv e^{\alpha\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{a}}
. Using the defining property of the displacement operator
\hat{a}\hat{D}(\alpha)=\hat{D}(\alpha)(\hat{a}+\alpha)
and parity operator
\hat{a}e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}=-e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\hat{a}
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle,\>\>\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle=\alpha^{*}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle,

(22a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\eta|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle,\>\>\>\>\>\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle=\eta^{*}|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle.

(22b)

The spectrum of each of our third-quantization superoperators is thus the whole complex plane.

The corresponding eigenvectors are reminiscent of standard single-mode coherent states, an analogy we can make even stronger by writing

\displaystyle|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle,

(23a)

\displaystyle|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\eta\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}-\eta^{*}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}}|\hat{0}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

(23b)

in the same way that coherent states are displaced vacuum. Unlike coherent states however, which are not eigenvectors of
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
,
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
share the same eigenvectors, as do
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
. This a consequence of
[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}]=0
. For the same reason,
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}
and
\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}
are orthogonal to other eigenstates of the same kind:

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\beta}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\hat{\beta}_{\rm cl}\right)=2\pi\delta^{2}(\alpha-\beta),

(24a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|\hat{\xi}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\hat{\xi}_{\rm q}\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}\delta^{2}(\eta-\xi)

(24b)

where the trace was computed using coherent states. In this sense, these operators are more reminiscent to position and momentum eigenkets.
Just like their first-quantized counterparts, they can then be used to form a resolution of the identity

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{1}}=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|=\int\frac{2d^{2}\eta}{\pi}|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|

(25)

where
d^{2}\beta\equiv d\text{Re}\beta\,d\text{Im}\beta
for any complex
\beta
. It follows that we can write the density matrix as

\displaystyle|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle=\int\frac{2d^{2}\eta}{\pi}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

(26)

with

\displaystyle W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle=2\Tr\left(e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\sqrt{2}\alpha)\hat{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle=2\Tr\left(e^{i\pi\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}})\hat{\rho}\hat{D}(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{2}}),\right)

(27a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle=\Tr\left(\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\sqrt{2}\eta)\hat{\rho}\right).

(27b)

Remarkably, these “wavefunctions” of the density matrix in these bases are well-known objects:
W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)
and
\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)
are precisely the Wigner function of
\hat{\rho}
and its characteristic function respectively
Royer 1977
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2010
.

To recover the result that
W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)
and
\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)
are Fourier transforms of each other, we compute the overlap

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle=\Tr\left(\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\right)=e^{\eta\alpha^{*}-\eta^{*}\alpha},

(28)

use the definitions Eqs. (
27a
)-(
27b
) and the resolution of the identify Eq. (
25
) to obtain

\displaystyle W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{2d^{2}\eta}{\pi}e^{\eta\alpha^{*}-\eta^{*}\alpha}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta),

(29a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}e^{\eta^{*}\alpha-\eta\alpha^{*}}W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha).

(29b)

Much like position and momentum eigenkets corresponds to a perfectly spatially-localized or delocalized particle in standard quantum mechanics, the eigenkets
|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle
and
|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle
can be thought of as states in phase space which are perfectly localized at
\alpha
or delocalized with wavevector
\eta
. That
\alpha
and
\eta
are Fourier transform pairs is also consistent with
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
being conjugate to one another. This can be further demonstrated by using Eqs. (
23a
)-(
23b
) to write

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\eta^{*}}\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|,\>\>\>\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}=-\partial_{\alpha}\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|

(30a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha^{*}}\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm q}|,\>\>\>\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}=-\partial_{\eta}\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|.

(30b)

Given any third-quantized Lindbladian, we can then use Eqs. (
27a
)-(
27b
) and Eqs. (
30a
)-(
30b
) to readily find the equation of motion for either the Wigner or the characteristic function. These rules are summarized in Table
1
. For example, we can recover the standard equivalent equations of motion
Gardiner and Zoller 2010

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\left[(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}-i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\right]|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

(31a)

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\updownarrow

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left[-(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\partial_{\alpha}\alpha+(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\partial_{\alpha^{*}}\alpha^{*}+i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha^{*}}\right]W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

(31b)

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\updownarrow

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\left[(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})\eta^{*}\partial_{\eta^{*}}-(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\eta\partial_{\eta}-i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\eta^{*}\eta\right]\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta).

(31c)

From now on, we will write the classical and quantum superoperators and their action on these eigenvectors as equivalent, e.g.
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}=\alpha=\partial_{\eta^{*}}
.

III.2
Interpretation of
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
and phase-space eigenvectors of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}

It is worth stressing that by using the resolution of the identity Eq. (
25
), one can obtain a phase-space representation of an arbitrary operator
W_{\hat{X}}(\alpha)\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|\hat{X}\rangle\rangle=2\Tr(e^{i \pi\ha^\dagger\ha}\hat{D}(\sqrt{2}\alpha)\hat{X})
. This is nothing but the Wigner-Weyl transform
Curtright et al. 2014
, yet here it appears much more naturally using the language of standard quantum mechanics. This allows us to easily describe how the similarity transformation
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
which eliminated the noise acts on an arbitrary density matrix, something which would be extremely challenging using standard formulations and tools of quantum mechanics in phase space, e.g. phase-point operators
Fano 1957
;
Wootters 1987
.

Being diagonal in the quantum basis,
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}=e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}
simply acts by multiplication on the characteristic function
\Lambda_{\mathcal{V}\hat{\rho}}(\eta)=e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)|\eta|^{2}}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\eta)
. We can then obtain the Wigner function by Fourier transforming using Eq. (
29a
)

\displaystyle W_{\mathcal{V}\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)=\int\frac{d^{2}\beta}{(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\pi}e^{-\frac{|\alpha-\beta|^{2}}{2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}}W_{\hat{\rho}}(\beta).

(32)

As promised,
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
is phase-space convolution with a Gaussian of width
(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)
, spreading out and delocalizing any distribution. This is also consistent with the similarity transformation
e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}
removing the noise, or equivalently the diffusion term in the Fokker-Planck equation Eq. (
31b
), which has an identical delocalization effect. Note that if
\bar{n}_{\rm th}=0
, then this is precisely the Glauber-Sudarshan
P
-function of the oscillator, which is insensitive to vacuum noise
Gardiner and Zoller 2010
.

Turning our attention to the right eigenvectors of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
, we find that they have a compact form in phase space

\displaystyle W_{\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}\langle\langle\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}|(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger})^{\mu}(-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q})^{\nu}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\mu+\nu}}{\sqrt{\mu!\nu!}}\partial_{\alpha}^{\mu}\partial_{\alpha^{*}}^{\nu}W_{\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}}(\alpha).

(33)

Focusing on the
\mu=\nu
case for simplicity and comparing with the Wigner function of Fock state projectors
|\mu\rangle\langle\mu|
we obtain

\displaystyle W_{\hat{r}_{\mu,\mu}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2(-1)^{\mu}e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}}}{(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)^{\mu+1}}L_{\mu}\left(\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}\right),

(34a)

\displaystyle W_{|\mu\rangle\langle\mu|}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=2(-1)^{\mu}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}L_{\mu}(2|\alpha|^{2})

(34b)

where
L_{\mu}
is the
\mu
-th Laguerre polynomial. Unlike the explicit second-quantized form, which from Eq. (
13a
) requires computing nested commuators of
\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}
, in this representation
\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}
bear a striking similarity to Fock states. Although they can be made almost identical by re-scaling the phase-space variable
W_{\hat{r}_{\mu,\mu}}(\alpha)\to W_{\hat{r}_{\mu,\mu}}(\sqrt{2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1}\alpha
), which would make the Gaussian factors match, the argument of the Laguerre polynomial would still differ by a factor of
2
. This property also holds when comparing arbitrary right eigenmodes
\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}
and outer products of Fock states
|\mu\rangle\langle\nu|
which we show in Appendix
A
. There we also give the phase-space representations of the left eigenvectors.

Using the equivalent representations of the master equation Eq. (
31
)), we conclude that the phase-space representations of
|\hat{r}_{\mu,\nu}\rangle\rangle
and
\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\mu,\nu}|
are also right and left eigenvectors of the classical Fokker-Planck differential operator. The existence of quantized Laguerre-polynomial eigenfunctions is a well-known property of this classical damped oscillator problem
Risken 1996
, but the corresponding quantum analog arguably is not. The quantized nature of these modes in a classical or quantum setting is easily understood for this linear problem: any observable should only oscillate and decay at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency and decay rate respectively. For instance,
\langle(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{\nu}\hat{a}^{\mu}\rangle
and its classical counterpart includes the harmonics
\omega_{0}(\mu-\nu)
and decay
\frac{\kappa}{2}(\mu+\nu)
.

Finally, note that these eigenvectors are still correct even when there is no dissipation
\bar{n}_{\rm th}\to 0,\kappa\to 0
. This might seem surprising at first, as in this limit
W_{\hat{r}_{\mu,\mu}}(\alpha)\neq W_{|\mu\rangle\langle\mu|}(\alpha)
. This discrepancy is easily understood. Without decay, each eigenvalue
E_{\mu,\nu}=\omega_{0}(\mu-\nu)
is infinitely degenerate, which is most obvious by writing
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=\omega_{0}(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger})=\omega_{0}(-\partial_{\alpha}\alpha+\partial_{\alpha^{*}}\alpha^{*})=\omega_{0}i\partial_{\phi}
where
\phi=\arg\alpha
. There is thus no unique way to diagonalize
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
; in this limit our approach picks out one linearly-independent set of eigenvectors out of the infinitely-many available ones.

IV
Correspondence between third quantization and Keldysh field theory

Despite the Lindbladian Eq. (
6
) being reminiscent of the dissipative Keldysh action of the same model, the connection between these approaches we have been suggesting are at this stage not rigorous. In this section, we make the correspondence concrete by showing that Keldysh field theory emerges directly from the structure of the third-quantized Lindbladian expressed in the basis of classical and quantum superoperators.
Our approach here complements standard derivations of the Keldysh action for open systems
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Kamenev 2011
. Unlike these treatments, our focus is not the derivation of a partition function for the calculation of correlation functions, but rather on obtaining the time evolution of an arbitrary initial state. In this sense, this section introduces an alternate formulation of the Feynman-Vernon influence functional approach
Feynman and Vernon 1963
and subsequent closely-related descriptions
Sharan 1979
;
Marinov 1991
.

IV.1
Path integral for a thermally-damped harmonic oscillator

The Keldysh path integral emerges here by asking how the Wigner or characteristic function evolves in time.
Indeed, since
e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}
is the time evolution superoperator, we can use the resolution of the identity Eq. (
25
) formed using the classical and quantum operators to write

\displaystyle|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}W_{\hat{\rho}(0)}(\alpha)|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{2d^{2}\eta}{\pi}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\eta)|\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}\rangle\rangle

(35)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}K(\eta,\alpha;t)W_{\hat{\rho}(0)}(\alpha),

(36a)

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle.

(36b)

The matrix element
K(\eta,\alpha;t)
, also referred to as the kernel, thus serves as the mixed phase-space propagator, and in principle contains the same information as the spectral decomposition of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
.

Using a resolution of the identity

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{1}}=\int\frac{d^{2}a_{{\rm cl},j}d^{2}a_{{\rm q},j}}{\pi^{2}}e^{a_{{\rm q},j}a_{{\rm cl},j}^{*}-a_{{\rm q},j}^{*}a_{{\rm cl},j}}|\hat{a}_{{\rm cl},j}\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\hat{a}_{{\rm q},j}|,

(37)

which follows directly from Eq. (
25
) and Eq. (
28
), along with the defining property of the classical and quantum eigenvectors, we can obtain a path-integral description of Eq. (
36b
) using the familiar prescription
Altland and Simons 2010
. The central object will be a functional very similar to the standard Keldysh action of a thermally-damped harmonic oscillator. There are however important differences that emerge. We emphasize these here, leaving the technical and mostly-familiar details in Appendix
B
.

Once these details have been taken into account, we are left with a path integral representation of the kernel

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]e^{iS_{0}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\left(\mathbbm{a}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\mathbb{J}(t^{\prime})-\mathbb{J}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\mathbbm{a}(t^{\prime})\right)}

(38)

where
\mathcal{D}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]
is the usual functional integration measure, the action takes the form

\displaystyle S_{0}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\begin{pmatrix}a^{*}_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})&a^{*}_{\rm q}(t^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{G}^{-1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\begin{pmatrix}a_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime\prime})\\
a_{\rm q}(t^{\prime\prime})\end{pmatrix},

(39)

and the inverse of the matrix Green’s function reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{-1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\begin{pmatrix}0&i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-(\omega_{0}+i\frac{\kappa}{2})\\
i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})&i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\end{pmatrix}.

(40)

Throughout this work, we will denote vectors and matrices with a
blackboard bold font to distinguish them from operators
and superoperators. Here we have defined the row vectors
\mathbbm{a}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\equiv\left(a^{*}_{\rm{cl}}(t^{\prime}),a^{*}_{\rm{q}}(t^{\prime})\right)
and the source term

\displaystyle\mathbb{J}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\equiv\left(-\eta^{*}\delta(t^{\prime}-t),\alpha^{*}\delta(t^{\prime})\right).

(41)

Note that in most path integrals, from Eq. (
36b
), one would normally impose boundary conditions on the fields such as
a_{\rm cl}(0)=\alpha
,
a_{\rm q}(t)=\eta
; this is incorrect here as it implies that
\mathbb{G}^{-1}
has a zero-eigenvalue eigenvector. We stress that one must instead keep track of the boundary term through the source
\mathbb{J}
and work directly the with the discrete representation of the Green’s function as we do in Appendix
B
.

There we show that, although
\mathbb{G}^{-1}
is symbolically the same regardless of the length of the time contour, the matrix Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\equiv\begin{pmatrix}G^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&G^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\\
G^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&0\end{pmatrix}

(42)

is sensitive to such changes. While the retarded
G^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})
and advanced
G^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})
Green’s function remains the same regardless of the contour, the Keldysh Green’s
G^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})
function does not

\displaystyle G^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=-i\Theta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})e^{-i(\omega_{0}-i\frac{\kappa}{2})(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})},

(43a)

\displaystyle G^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=(G^{R}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime}))^{*},

(43b)

\displaystyle G^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=-i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\int_{0}^{t}dTG^{R}(t^{\prime},T)G^{A}(T,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=-i(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)e^{-i\omega_{0}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\left(e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}|t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}|}-e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}(t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime})}\right)

(43c)

with
\Theta(t)
the Heaviside step function. Crucially, the discrete-time path integral makes it clear that the correct choice of the Heaviside step function at zero is
\Theta(0)=1
.

The path integral can be computed in the usual manner when one has a quadratic action and a source term. Making the displacement

\displaystyle\mathbbm{a}(t^{\prime})\to\mathbbm{a}(t^{\prime})+i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{G}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{J}(t^{\prime\prime})

(44)

and, using
\int\mathcal{D}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]e^{iS_{0}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]}=\det(i \mat{G})=1
as in the usual Keldysh formalism, we obtain

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)=e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\mathbb{J}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})\mathbb{G}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\mathbb{J}(t^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)(1-e^{-\kappa t})|\eta|^{2}+e^{-\frac{\kappa}{2}t}\left(\eta^{*}\alpha e^{-i\omega_{0}t}-\rm{c.c.}\right)}.

(45)

As expected due to the quadratic nature of the Lindbladian, the propagator is a Gaussian function of
\alpha
and
\eta
. Equation (
45
) can independently be verified to be correct by noting that it must satisfy Eq. (
31c
) along with the initial condition
K(\eta,\alpha;0)=e^{\eta^{*}\alpha-\rm{c.c.}}
.

IV.2
Correlation functions and timer evolution of the Wigner function

For the sake of completeness, in this subsection we demonstrate how, with knowledge of
K(\eta,\alpha;t)
and the initial Wigner function
W_{\hat{\rho}(0)}(\alpha)
, one can straightforwardly obtain both arbitrary correlation functions and the time-evolved Wigner function
W_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\alpha)
. Since the characteristic function
\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\eta)
is the generator of symmetrically-ordered correlation functions, we can simply use Eq. (
36a
) to obtain

\displaystyle(\sqrt{2})^{\mu+\nu}\langle\{\hat{a}^{\mu}(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{\nu}\}_{\rm sym}\rangle=(\partial_{\eta^{*}})^{\mu}(-\partial_{\eta})^{\nu}\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\eta)|_{\eta=\eta^{*}=0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}\Big[(\partial_{\eta^{*}})^{\mu}(-\partial_{\eta})^{\nu}K(\eta,\alpha;t)_{\eta=\eta^{*}=0}\Big]W_{\hat{\rho}(0)}(\alpha),

(46)

which is valid for any Lindbladian, and not simply the damped oscillator considered here.

Using the resolution of the identity Eq. (
25
) we can obtain the classical-classical phase-space propagator

\displaystyle\Xi(\beta,\alpha;t)\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\beta}_{\rm cl}|e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle=\int\frac{2d^{2}\eta}{\pi}e^{\eta\beta^{*}-\eta^{*}\beta}K(\eta,\alpha;t)

(47)

which we can then use to write

\displaystyle W_{\hat{\rho}(t)}(\beta)=\int\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{2\pi}\Xi(\beta,\alpha;t)W_{\hat{\rho}(0)}(\alpha).

(48)

Once again this is valid for an arbitrary Lindbladian, but can be done here exactly due to the Gaussian nature of the problem.

IV.3
Path integral for arbitrary Lindbladians

Although in this example
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
was quadratic in creation and annihilation superoperators, it is evident that a path integral representation of the propagator can be obtained for arbitrary Lindbladians. Replacing all
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}
by their respective fields in the action however is only valid once all quantum superoperators have been placed to the left of the classical ones. This is due to the form of the resolution of the identity Eq. (
25
). This convention is different than the one used in the standard coherent-state path integral, where one must ensure that the Lindlabidan is normal-ordered
Kamenev 2011
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
. No differences emerge in this linear setting, but the two different prescriptions will produce two different Keldysh actions for an interacting problem. We expand on this point in Sec.
VII
when we study a dissipative Kerr oscillator.

V
Quadratic Bosonic Lindbladians

V.1
Setup

We now generalize the previous results to an arbitrary quadratic multi-mode bosonic Lindbladian. The model now under consideration is a Lindblad master equation of the form

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}=\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{H}_{nm}[\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}[\mathbb{K}_{nm}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}+{\rm{h.c.}},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{b}\mathcal{D}\left[\sum_{m}\left(l_{bm}\hat{a}_{m}+p^{*}_{bm}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\right)\right]\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{H}_{nm}[\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{L}_{nm}\left(\hat{a}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}\}\right)+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{P}_{nm}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{m}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}[\mathbb{K}_{nm}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}+{\rm h.c.},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{C}_{nm}\left(\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\right)+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{C}^{*}_{nm}\left(\hat{a}_{n}\hat{\rho}\hat{a}_{m}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a}_{m}\hat{a}_{n},\hat{\rho}\}\right)\equiv\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}

(49)

where
n
and
m
label independent bosonic modes
[\hat{a}_{m},\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{n,m}
. Further
\mathbb{H}_{nm}
and
\mathbb{K}_{nm}
are, respectively, arbitrary Hermitian and complex symmetric matrices which describes the coherent particle-conserving and non-conserving process of the isolated system. The coefficients
l_{bm}
,
p^{*}_{bm}
characterize the coupling to a set of dissipative baths indexed by
b
and, consequently, the positive semi-definite Hermitan matrices
\mathbb{L}_{nm}=\sum_{b}l^{*}_{bn}l_{bm}
and
\mathbb{P}_{nm}=\sum_{b}p^{*}_{bn}p_{bm}
capture the phase-insensitive loss and pumping respectively. The complex matrix
\mathbb{C}_{nm}=\sum_{b}l^{*}_{bn}p^{*}_{bm}
describes coherences between these processes. Its phase-sensitivity indicates that not all quadratures are equivalent.

V.2
Multi-mode classical and quantum eigenvectors

Mimicking the procedure of the single-oscillator case, for each mode
m
we define a set of classical and quantum creation and annihilation superoperators

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}\}\rangle\rangle,

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{a}_{m},\hat{\rho}]\rangle\rangle,

(50a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m},\hat{\rho}\}\rangle\rangle,

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}]\rangle\rangle

(50b)

whose only non-vanishing commutators are

\displaystyle[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m},\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}^{\dagger},-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}]=\delta_{nm}\bm{\hat{1}}.

(51)

A tedious but straightforward calculation gives the third-quantization representation of the Lindbladian

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}+(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}^{\dagger}\right]-i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{N}_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{K}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}^{\dagger}+(\mathbb{K}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}\right]-i\sum_{n,m}\left[\mathbb{Q}_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}^{\dagger}+\mathbb{Q}_{nm}^{*}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\right].

(52)

Here we have defined the effective Hamiltonian and two-photon pumping matrices

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\equiv\mathbb{H}-\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathbb{L}-\mathbb{G}\right),\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{K}_{\rm eff}\equiv\mathbb{K}-\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{C}^{T}\right)

(53)

in addition to the phase-insensitive and phase-sensitive noise matrices

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}\equiv\mathbb{L}+\mathbb{G},\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{Q}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{C}^{T}).

(54)

The form of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
directly parallels the single-mode example. The quantum-classical terms encodes the dynamics, the quantum-quantum terms the noise, and there is no classical-classical term due to conservation of probability.

The presence of the matrices
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{C}
breaks a global
U(1)
symmetry
\hat{a}_{m}\to e^{i\theta}\hat{a}_{m},\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}\to e^{-i\theta}\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}
and, just like in the closed-system case, implies that we have to work with a Nambu structure. For clarity, we first set
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}=0
and avoid the additional technical details that comes with doubling the size of the matrix one has to diagonalize. At the end of this section we briefly discuss how the presence of these terms changes the diagonalziation procedure.

V.3
Correlation matrix

To justify referring to
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
and
\mathbb{N}
as the dynamical and noise matrices respecticely, let us compute the equations of motion for the symmetrically-ordered correlation matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{mn}(t)\equiv\langle\{\hat{a}_{m}(t),\hat{a}_{n}^{\dagger}(t)\}\rangle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle

(55)

which is given by

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\mathbb{S}(t)=\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{S}(t)-\mathbb{S}(t)\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}+i\mathbb{N}.

(56)

As promised,
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
serves as the dynamical matrix which determines the fluctuation-free evolution of the system. It is also the dynamical matrix that would appear in the equation of motion for the average values of
\hat{a}_{m}(t)
. The noise matrix
\mathbb{N}
only affects the correlations and occupation of the modes. Using the Heisenberg-Langevin equations, it can be shown that
\mathbb{N}
corresponds to the correlation matrix of a set of fluctuating noisy forces acting on the oscillators. Further, note that the dissipation cannot be larger than the noise
i(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}-\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})\leq\mathbb{N}
, which ensures that the Heisenberg uncertainty principle is satisfied
Clerk et al. 2010
. Finally,
we remind the reader that the identification of noise is always contingent on the ordering prescription used to define the corelation matrix. For example, the equations of motion for the normal-ordered covariance matrix takes the same form as that of
\mathbb{S}
but with modified noise terms:
\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{P}
.

Let us assume that all eigenvalues of
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
have negative imaginary part, ensuring the existence of a unique steady state. The formal solution to Eq. (
56
) then reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}}\mathbb{S}(0)e^{i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}t}+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}(t-t^{\prime})}\mathbb{N}e^{i\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff}(t-t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}t}\Bigl(\mathbb{S}(0)-\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}\Bigr)e^{i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}t}+\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}.

(57)

where the steady state correlation matrix can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\>e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}t}\mathbb{N}e^{i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}t}

(58a)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathbb{G}^{R}(\omega)\>\mathbb{N}\>\mathbb{G}^{A}(\omega)

(58b)

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{\sigma,\tau}|\Psi^{R}_{\sigma}\rangle\frac{\langle\Psi^{L}_{\sigma}|\mathbb{N}|\Psi^{L}_{\tau}\rangle}{E_{\sigma}-E_{\tau}^{*}}\langle\Psi^{R}_{\tau}|.

(58c)

Here,

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{R}(\omega)\equiv\frac{1}{\omega\mathbbm{1}-\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}}=(\mathbb{G}^{A}(\omega))^{\dagger}

(59)

are the frequency-space retarded and advanced Green’s function respectively whereas
\sigma
and
\tau
label the bi-orthonormal eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}|\Psi^{R}_{\sigma}\rangle=E_{\sigma}|\Psi^{R}_{\sigma}\rangle,\>

\displaystyle\>\langle\Psi^{L}_{\sigma}|\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}=\langle\Psi^{L}_{\sigma}|E_{\sigma},

(60a)

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{L}_{\sigma}|\Psi^{R}_{\tau}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{\sigma\tau}.

(60b)

It is worth stressing that while Eq. (
58c
) assumes
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
can be diagonalized, Eqs. (
58a
)-(
58b
) are always valid. Thus, even when the dynamical matrix
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
is defective or equivalently at an exceptional point
Bergholtz et al. 2021
;
Ashida et al. 2020
, the steady state is well defined.

V.4
Gauging away noise, eigenvectors and eigenvalues

Returning to the question of how to diagonalize
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
, recall that in the single-mode case
linearity and the lack of a classical-classical term is ultimately what let us easily diagonalize the Lindbladian.
As we now show, a similar approach works in the multi-mode case. We can again find a multi-mode similarity transformation that effectively gauges away the fluctuations, leaving a transformed Liouvillian that can be written in terms of generalized number operators. The generalization of
e^{-(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}}
to several oscillators is simply

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\equiv\exp\left(-\sum_{n,m}(\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\right).

(61)

The Liouvillian in the new frame defined by
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
is, using the Baker-Cambell-Hausdorff identity and Eq. (
51
),

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}^{-1}\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}+(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}^{\dagger}\right]-\sum_{n,m}\left(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}-\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}+i\mathbb{N}\right)_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}+(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},n}^{\dagger}\right]

(62)

where we have used
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}-\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}+i\mathbb{N}=0
which follows from Eq. (
56
). In this new basis, we are then left with two commuting third-quantized non-Hermitian Hamiltonians. Using the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues we used to diagonalize
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
, these two terms can easily be brought to diagonal form (i.e. written as a sum of commuting generalized number operators). Moving back to the original frame, we finally arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=\sum_{\sigma}\left[E_{\sigma}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)+E_{\sigma}^{*}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\right]

(63)

where the relevant quasiparticle superoperators are

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}(\Psi^{L}_{m}(\sigma))^{*}\left(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}+\sum_{n}(\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss})_{mn}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}\right),

(64a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}\Psi^{R}_{m}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}^{\dagger},

(64b)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\Psi^{L}_{m}(\sigma)\left(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}^{\dagger}-\sum_{n}(\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss})_{nm}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},n}^{\dagger}\right),

(64c)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}(\Psi^{R}_{m}(\sigma))^{*}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{q},m}

(64d)

with
\Psi^{R}_{m}(\sigma)\equiv\langle m|\Psi^{R}_{\sigma}\rangle
and
(\Psi^{L}_{m}(\sigma))^{*}\equiv\langle\Psi^{L}_{\sigma}|m\rangle
. Although
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma)
is not the conjugate of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)
, this is irrelevant since, using the bi-orthonormality of the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
, the only non-vanishing commutators amongst the set Eqs. (
64a
)-(
64d
) are

\displaystyle[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma),\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime})]=[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma),-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma^{\prime})]=\delta_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\bm{\hat{1}}.

(65)

We can therefore think of
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)
and
-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma)
as non-Hermitian number operators. The right and left vacuums of both number operators is simply the steady state and identity operator respectively

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}(\sigma)|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=0

(66a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma)=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{\rm q}|\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma)=0

(66b)

with
\hat{0}_{\rm q}\equiv\hat{1}
. The steady state
|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}|\hat{0}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle
is Gaussian with two-point correlation matrix determined by
\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}
. Here
\hat{0}_{\rm cl}=2^{M}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}
is the total parity operator with
\hat{N}=\sum_{m}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{a}_{m}
, which serves as the joint vacuum of the classical superoperators, and
M
is the total number of modes.

In complete analogy to a second-quantized quadratic Hamiltonian, we can now write the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
as

\displaystyle|\hat{r}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{\sigma}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{\sigma}!\nu_{\sigma}!}}\left[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\right]^{\mu_{\sigma}}\left[-\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}(\sigma)\right]^{\nu_{\sigma}}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle

(67a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}|

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{q}|\prod_{\sigma}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{\sigma}!\nu_{\sigma}!}}\left[\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)\right]^{\mu_{\sigma}}[\bm{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}_{\rm cl}(\sigma)]^{\nu_{\sigma}}

(67b)

with corresponding eigenvalues

\displaystyle E_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}=\sum_{\sigma}\left(E_{\sigma}\mu_{\sigma}-E_{\sigma}^{*}\nu_{\sigma}\right).

(68)

Here,
\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}
are lists of non-negative integers
\mu_{\sigma},\nu_{\sigma}
which characterize the number of “particles”
\mu_{\sigma}
or “holes”
\nu_{\sigma}
in mode
\sigma
. The right and left eigenvectors are normalized such that the bi-orthonormality condition
\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\vec{\mu^{\prime}},\vec{\nu^{\prime}}}|\hat{r}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\rangle\rangle=\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\mu}^{\prime}}\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{\nu}^{\prime}}
is satisfied.

V.5
Multi-mode Wigner function and path integral

The equivalence between third quantization, phase-space representations of the density matrix and Keldysh field theory presented in Secs.
II
-
IV
can be extended to this multi-mode setting in an obvious manner. For any set of complex numbers
\alpha_{m}
and
\eta_{m}
the multi-mode displaced parity and displacement operator

\displaystyle\vec{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm cl}

\displaystyle\equiv 2^{M}e^{\sum_{m}\left(\alpha_{m}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{m}^{*}\hat{a}_{m}\right)}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}

(69a)

\displaystyle\vec{\hat{\eta}}_{\rm q}

\displaystyle\equiv e^{\sum_{m}\left(\alpha_{m}\hat{a}_{m}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{m}^{*}\hat{a}_{m}\right)}

(69b)

are the eigenvectors of the classical and quantum superoperators respectively. The multi-mode Wigner function and characteristic function are given by

\displaystyle W_{\hat{\rho}}(\vec{\alpha})

\displaystyle\equiv\Tr\left(\vec{\hat{\alpha}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\right),

(70a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\vec{\eta})

\displaystyle\equiv\Tr\left(\vec{\hat{\eta}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\right).

(70b)

from which we can obtain a simple physical interpretation of the similarity transformation used to eliminate the noise. Using the basis which diagonalizes the Hermitian covariance matrix
\mathbb{S}_{\rm ss}
, that is the set of orthogonal modes
\hat{a}_{\lambda}
satisfying
\langle\{\hat{a}_{\lambda},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\lambda^{\prime}}\}\rangle_{\rm ss}=(2\bar{n}_{\lambda}+1)\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
, we have
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}=\exp\left(-\sum_{\lambda}(2\bar{n}_{\lambda}+1)\bm{\hat{a}}^{\dagger}_{{\rm q},\lambda}\bm{\hat{a}}_{{\rm q},\lambda}\right)
. Given the result of Sec.
III
we conclude that
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
is a convolution with a multi-mode Gaussian in phase spaces along a set of orthogonal axes determined by the modes
\hat{a}_{\lambda}
whose width depends on the average number of quanta in that mode in steady state.

The equations of motion for
W_{\hat{\rho}}(\vec{\alpha})
and
\Lambda_{\hat{\rho}}(\vec{\eta})
can be easily read off from the third-quantized form of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
in Eq. (
52
) and the multi-mode generalization of the rules in Table
1
e.g.
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\text{cl},m}=\alpha_{m}=\partial_{\eta^{*}_{m}}
. We do not reproduce them here for the sake of compactness. We can also find the propagator
K(\vec{\eta},\vec{\alpha};t)\equiv\langle\langle\hat{\eta}_{\rm q}|e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}|\hat{\alpha}_{\rm cl}\rangle\rangle
for this differential equation using the Keldysh path integral. The multi-mode matrix Green’s function for the finite-time contour of length
t
is nothing but the generalization of Eq. (
42
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{G}^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&\mathbb{G}^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\\
\mathbb{G}^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-i\int_{0}^{t}dT\mathbb{G}^{R}(t^{\prime},T)\>\mathbb{N}\>\mathbb{G}^{A}(T,t^{\prime\prime})&-i\Theta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\\
i\Theta(t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime})e^{i\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff}(t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime})}&0\end{pmatrix}

(71)

and consequently the propagator is nothing but the multi-mode generalization of Eq. (
45
)

\displaystyle K(\vec{\eta},\vec{\alpha};t)=\exp\left(-i\mathbb{J}_{\rm int}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{G}^{K}(t,t)&\mathbb{G}^{R}(t,0)\\
\mathbb{G}^{A}(0,t)&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{J}_{\rm int}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\mathbb{J}_{\rm int}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}t^{\prime}}\>\mathbb{N}\>e^{i\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff}t^{\prime}}&e^{-i\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}t}\\
-e^{i\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff}t}&0\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{J}_{\rm int}\right)

(72)

where here we have defined the integrated source term
\mathbb{J}_{\rm int}^{\dagger}\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\mathbb{J}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})
.

V.6
Symmetry-breaking terms

In the presence of
U(1)
symmetry-breaking terms in the Lindbladian Eq. (
52
) due to non-zero
\mathbb{K}
and
\mathbb{C}
, the strategy would be analogous to the one presented above. One would now have non-vanishing anomalous steady-state covariance matrix elements
\langle\{\hat{a}_{m},\hat{a}_{n}\}\rangle_{\rm ss}
, which would now enter the similarity transformation used to remove the noise terms. What remains is a
4M\times 4M
non-Hermitian Hamiltonian. Just as in the closed-system case, one would have to perform an appropriate Bogoliubov transformation when defining third-quantized quasi-particle superoperators. The propagator would also take essentially the same form, the only difference being that the matrix Green’s function would be of size
4M\times 4M
.

VI
Quadratic fermiomic Lindbladians

VI.1
Setup

In this section we will diagonalize an arbitrary fermionic Lindbladian of the form

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{H}_{nm}[\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m},\hat{\rho}]+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}[\mathbb{K}_{nm}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}+{\rm h.c.},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{b}\mathcal{D}\left[\sum_{m}\left(l_{bm}\hat{c}_{m}+p^{*}_{bm}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{H}_{nm}[\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{L}_{nm}\left(\hat{c}_{m}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m},\hat{\rho}\}\right)+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{P}_{nm}\left(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{m}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{c}_{m}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}[\mathbb{K}_{nm}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}+{\rm h.c.},\hat{\rho}]+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{C}_{nm}\left(\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}\}\right)+i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{C}^{*}_{nm}\left(\hat{c}_{n}\hat{\rho}\hat{c}_{m}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{c}_{m}\hat{c}_{n},\hat{\rho}\}\right)\equiv\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}.

(73)

where
m
and
n
label independent fermionic degrees of freedom
\{\hat{c}_{m},\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{nm}
. The matrices
\mathbb{H}
,
\mathbb{L}
,
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{C}
have the same form and interpretation as in the bosonic case studied in Sec.
V
. The only difference is that the complex matrix
\mathbb{K}
now describes the pairing of fermions, and is thus necessarily anti-symmetric.

VI.2
Creation and annihilation superoperators

In analogy with the bosonic case Eq. (
50
), we would like to define a set of fermionic superoperators which satisfy canonical anti-commutation relations. The issue however is that superoperators acting on the left and the right of the density matrix commute with one another; this would seem to preclude introducing superoperators with canonical fermionic anti-commutation relations. The solution is to introduce the parity operator
e^{i\pi\hat{N}}
in the definition of these modes, where
\hat{N}=\sum_{m}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m}\hat{c}_{m}
is the total number operator. Defining

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{c}_{m},\hat{\rho}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}\}\rangle\rangle,\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{c}_{m},\hat{\rho}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}]\rangle\rangle

(74a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle,

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|[\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger},\hat{\rho}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}]\rangle\rangle,\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|\{\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{m},\hat{\rho}e^{i\pi\hat{N}}\}\rangle\rangle

(74b)

one verifies that
\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}^{\dagger}
and
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}^{\dagger}
are the Hermitian conjugates of
\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}
and
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}
as per the definition of the inner product Eq. (
2
). The only non-vanishing set of anti-commutators are

\displaystyle\{\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m},\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\}=\{\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m},\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{nm}\bm{\hat{1}}.

(75)

Thus,
\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}
and
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}
can be interpreted as genuine annihilation operators of fermionic modes.

From the definitions Eqs (
74a
)-(
74b
), the total parity and identity operator serve as the unique joint vacuum for one type of superoperator, while simultaneously serving as the completely-filled state of the other, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}\equiv e^{i\pi\hat{N}}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}^{\dagger}|\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}\rangle\rangle=\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}|\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}\rangle\rangle=0,

(76a)

\displaystyle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\equiv\hat{1}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle=\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}^{\dagger}|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle=0.

(76b)

To be explicit,
|\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}\rangle\rangle
defines a state where all type-1 quasiparticle states are filled, and all type-2 quasiparticle states are empty;
|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle
is interpreted in a similar fashion.
Note that these effective states are not normalized to unity but rather
\langle\langle\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}|\hat{1}_{1},\hat{0}_{2}\rangle\rangle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle=2^{M}
where
M
is the total number of modes.

We stress that these superoperators and states differ from Prosen
Prosen 2008
, who instead introduced Majorana superoperators instead of fermionic creation and annihilation superoperators. Our choice allows us to make the connection to the Keldysh formalism, as is apparent when writing the Lindbladian in this basis

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}-(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}\right]-i\sum_{n,m}\mathbb{N}_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{K}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}-(\mathbb{K}_{\rm eff})^{*}_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}\right]-i\sum_{n,m}\left[\mathbb{Q}_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}^{\dagger}-\mathbb{Q}^{*}_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}\right]

(77)

where for fermions the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonians and pairing matrices are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\equiv\mathbb{H}-\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathbb{L}+\mathbb{P}\right),\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{K}_{\rm eff}\equiv\mathbb{K}-\frac{i}{2}\left(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{C}^{T}\right)

(78)

with the noise matrices taking the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{N}\equiv\mathbb{L}-\mathbb{P},\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{Q}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{C}^{T}\right).

(79)

Setting
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}=0
for simplicity, we confirm that the nomenclature is justified by defining the anti-symmetrized covariance matrix

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{mn}(t)\equiv\langle[\hat{c}_{m}(t),\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}(t)]\rangle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}|\hat{\rho}(t)\rangle\rangle

(80)

and computing its equation of motion

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\mathbb{A}(t)=\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{A}(t)-\mathbb{A}(t)\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff}+i\mathbb{N}.

(81)

Note that here the dissipation must be larger than the fluctuations
i(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}-\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})\geq\mathbb{N}
, whereas the opposite is true for bosons. This ensures that the Pauli exclusion principle is satisfied. The solution and physical content of Eq. (
81
) was already discussed in Sec.
V
. Our only goal here was to stress that each term in the Lindbladian has a physical interpretation; it describes either dynamics or noise.

VI.3
Gauging away the noise

Just as in the bosonic case, there are terms in the third-quantized representation of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
such as
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}
which are not allowed due to conservation of probability
\langle\langle\hat{1}|\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=0
. We can use this to move to a frame where there are no fluctuations, interpreting the separation of dissipation and noise in this linear system as the underlying symmetry which allows us find such a basis. Defining the anti-symmetrized steady-state covariance matrix
(\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss})_{mn}\equiv\langle[\hat{c}_{m},\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{n}]\rangle_{\rm ss}
which satisfies
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss}-\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss}\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}+i\mathbb{N}=0
and the superoperator

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}\equiv\exp\left(-\sum_{n,m}(\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}\right)

(82)

we can use the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formlua and the anti-commutations relations Eq. (
75
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}^{-1}\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}-(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}\right]-\sum_{n,m}\left(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss}-\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss}\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}^{\dagger}+i\mathbb{N}\right)_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\left[(\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}-(\mathbb{H}^{\dagger}_{\rm eff})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}^{\dagger}\right].

(83)

We can now use the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of
\mathbb{H}_{\rm eff}
to bring this superoperator to diagonal form (i.e. written as a sum of commuting generalized number operators). Moving back to the original frame, we have

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=\sum_{\sigma}\left[E_{\sigma}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma)-E_{\sigma}^{*}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)\right]

(84)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}(\Psi^{L}_{m}(\sigma))^{*}\left(\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}+\sum_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss})_{mn}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,n}\right)

(85a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}\Psi^{R}_{n}(\sigma)\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,m}^{\dagger}

(85b)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}\Psi_{m}^{L}(\sigma)\left(\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}-\sum_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{\rm ss})_{nm}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1,n}^{\dagger}\right)

(85c)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{m}(\Psi_{m}^{R}(\sigma))^{*}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2,m}.

(85d)

Although
\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma)
and
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)
are not the conjugate of
\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma)
and
\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}(\sigma)
, the canonical anti-commutation relations are still preserved, e.g.,

\displaystyle\{\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma),\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime})\}=\{\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}(\sigma),\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma^{\prime})\}=\delta_{\sigma\tau}\bm{\hat{1}}.

(86)

However, as a result of the non-unitary nature of
\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}
, the left and right vacuum and completely filled state of these new superoperators are not the same

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}(\sigma)=0,

(87a)

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma)|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=0.

(87b)

where we can write
|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle=2^{-M}\mathbfcal{\hat{V}}|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle
. In direct analogy with a standard Hermitian Hamiltonian, the eigenvectors of
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
can then be written as

\displaystyle|\hat{r}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\prod_{\sigma}[\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}(\sigma)]^{\mu_{\sigma}}\left[\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}(\sigma)\right]^{\nu_{\sigma}}|\hat{\rho}_{\rm ss}\rangle\rangle

(88a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}|

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|\prod_{\sigma}[\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}(\sigma)]^{\mu_{\sigma}}[\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}(\sigma)]^{\nu_{\sigma}}

(88b)

with corresponding eigenvalues

\displaystyle E_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}=\sum_{\sigma}\left(E_{\sigma}\mu_{\sigma}-E_{\sigma}^{*}\nu_{\sigma}\right).

(89)

Here,
\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}
are lists with entries
\mu_{\sigma},\nu_{\sigma}=\{0,1\}
and the eigenvectors satisfy
\langle\langle\hat{l}_{\vec{\mu^{\prime}},\vec{\nu^{\prime}}}|\hat{r}_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\nu}}\rangle\rangle=\delta_{\vec{\mu},\vec{\mu}^{\prime}}\delta_{\vec{\nu},\vec{\nu}^{\prime}}
.

The inclusion of
U(1)
symmetry-breaking terms adds no formal degree of complexity; one simply has to diagonalize a larger matrix. The underlying physics, that is the separation of dynamics and fluctuations, still allows one to eliminate the noise using an appropriate similarity transformation analogous to Eq. (
82
).

VI.4
Third quantization and Keldysh field theory for fermions

Just as we did in the bosonic case, we now develop a path integral representation of the dynamics starting from the third-quantized representation of the Lindbladian in Eq. (
77
). For simplicity, let us assume that we have a single fermionic mode, such that the third-quantized Lindbladian takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}=

\displaystyle(\epsilon_{0}-i\frac{\gamma}{2})\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}-(\epsilon_{0}+i\frac{\gamma}{2})\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}

(90)

\displaystyle-i\gamma(1-2\bar{n})\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}

(91)

where
\gamma
is the decay rate and
\bar{n}=\langle\hat{c}^{\dagger}\hat{c}\rangle_{\rm ss}
the average number of particles in the steady state. Although here the Hamiltonian loss and pumping matrices have been simply replaced by numbers,
\mathbb{H}\to\epsilon_{0}

\mathbb{L}\to\gamma(1-\bar{n}),\mathbb{P}\to\gamma\bar{n}
, the generalization to several modes is straightforward.

The first step in formulating any fermionic path integral is to introduce the fermionic equivalent of coherent states, for which one must work with the usual Grassmann numbers
Altland and Simons 2010
. We thus introduce four Grassmann numbers
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\bar{\psi}_{1},\bar{\psi}_{2}
which anticommute with each other and all superoperators. Recalling that we have identified the identity operator as the vacuum of type-1 quasiparticlces and the occupied state of type-2 quasiparticles, see Eq. (
76
), we then define

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{e^{-\psi_{1}\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}-\bar{\psi}_{2}\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}}}{2}|\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}\rangle\rangle,

(92a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\psi|

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\langle\hat{0}_{1},\hat{1}_{2}|e^{-\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}\bar{\psi}_{1}-\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}\psi_{2}}

(92b)

which satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}|\psi\rangle\rangle

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}|\psi\rangle\rangle,\hskip 5.69046pt\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle\rangle=\bar{\psi}_{2}|\psi\rangle\rangle

(93a)

\displaystyle\langle\langle\psi|\bm{\hat{c}}_{1}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\langle\langle\psi|\bar{\psi}_{1},\hskip 5.69046pt\langle\langle\psi|\bm{\hat{c}}_{2}=\langle\langle\psi|\psi_{2}

(93b)

and

\displaystyle\langle\langle\psi^{\prime}|\psi\rangle\rangle=e^{\bar{\psi}^{\prime}_{1}\psi_{1}+\psi^{\prime}_{2}\bar{\psi}_{2}}.

(94)

One can then verify that these coherent states can be used as a resolution of the identity

\displaystyle\int d\bar{\psi}_{1}d\psi_{1}d\psi_{2}d\bar{\psi}_{2}\,e^{-\bar{\psi}_{1}\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\bar{\psi}_{2}}|\psi\rangle\rangle\langle\langle\psi|=\bm{\hat{1}}

(95)

from which one can construst a Grassmann-valued “wavefunction” which, in analogy with the Wigner function for bosons, we denote
W_{\hat{\rho}}(\psi)\equiv\langle\langle\psi|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle
. It follows that the matrix element

\displaystyle K(\psi^{\prime},\psi;t)\equiv\langle\langle\psi^{\prime}|e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}|\psi\rangle\rangle

(96)

contains all information about the dynamics.

A continuous-time version of this propagator can be build using essentially the same procedure presented in Sec.
IV
. Leaving all details to Appendix
C
, we arrive at

\displaystyle K(\psi^{\prime},\psi;t)=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}[\psi_{1},\psi_{2}]e^{iS_{0}[\psi_{1},\psi_{2}]+\bar{\psi}^{\prime}_{1}\psi_{1}(t)-\bar{\psi}_{2}(t)\psi_{2}^{\prime}+\bar{\psi}_{1}(0)\psi_{1}-\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi_{2}(0)}

(97)

where
\mathcal{D}[\psi_{1},\psi_{2}]
is the standard functional measure, and the action is

\displaystyle S_{0}[\psi_{1},\psi_{2}]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}dt^{\prime\prime}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{\psi}_{1}(t^{\prime})&\bar{\psi}_{2}(t^{\prime})\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{G}^{-1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(t^{\prime\prime})\\
\psi_{2}(t^{\prime\prime})\end{pmatrix},

(98)

where the matrix Green’s function now reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}^{-1}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=\delta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})\begin{pmatrix}i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-(\epsilon_{0}-i\frac{\gamma}{2})&i\gamma(1-2\bar{n})\\
0&i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-(\epsilon_{0}+i\frac{\gamma}{2})\end{pmatrix}.

(99)

The matrix Green’s function is then

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\equiv\begin{pmatrix}G^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})&G^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\\
0&G^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\end{pmatrix}

(100)

where

\displaystyle G^{R}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=-i\Theta(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})e^{-i(\epsilon_{0}-i\frac{\gamma}{2})(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}

(101a)

\displaystyle G^{A}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=(G^{R}(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime}))^{*}

(101b)

\displaystyle G^{K}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})=-i\kappa(1-2\bar{n})\int_{0}^{t}dTG^{R}(t^{\prime},T)G^{A}(T,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=-i(1-2\bar{n})e^{-i\epsilon_{0}(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})}\left(e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}|t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}|}-e^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}(t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime})}\right).

(101c)

Mimicking the bosonic case, we can make the appropriate translation to the Grassmann variables to eliminate the source term and integrate over the quadratic action which gives unity (see Appendix
C
for details). We then obtain

\displaystyle K(\psi^{\prime},\psi;t)=e^{-(1-2\bar{n})(1-e^{-\gamma t})\bar{\psi}_{1}^{\prime}\psi_{2}^{\prime}+e^{(-i\epsilon_{0}-\frac{\kappa}{2})t}\bar{\psi}^{\prime}_{1}\psi_{1}-e^{(i\epsilon_{0}-\frac{\kappa}{2})t}\bar{\psi}_{2}\psi^{\prime}_{2}}

(102)

From Eq. (
92
), by taking derivatives of the Grassmann variables we can use this propagator to obtain expectation values of observables, e.g.
\partial_{\bar{\psi}^{\prime}_{1}}\langle\langle\psi^{\prime}|e^{-i\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}t}|\hat{\rho}(0)\rangle\rangle|_{\psi^{\prime}=0}=\sqrt{2}\langle\hat{c}(t)\rangle
. Of course, we could have obtained this answer easily in this single-mode example; the application of this path integral is more useful when there are several fermionic modes.

VII
Application: dissipative non-linear cavity

We now provide an example of how our formalism can be extremely useful even in situations where there are true interactions, and the Lindbladian is not simply quadratic. We consider a paradigmatic problem: a single bosonic mode with a Kerr (or Hubbard) type nonlinearity, and subject to thermal single-photon dissipation. The master equation for this system is

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=[\omega_{0}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{U}{2}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a},\hat{\rho}]

\displaystyle+i\kappa(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}+i\kappa\bar{n}_{\rm th}\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}\equiv\mathcal{L}\hat{\rho}

(103)

with
U
the strength of the Kerr non-linearity. Since
\mathbfcal{\hat{L}}
is now evidently quartic in creation and annihilation superoperators, it would seem as though describing this problem exactly is not feasible.

Surprisingly, well-known analytic expressions for this system exist, derived using a variety of different techniques (see e.g.
Dykman 1973
;
Chaturvedi and Srinivasan 1991a
;
Chaturvedi and Srinivasan 1991b
;
Milburn 1986
;
Milburn and Holmes 1986
;
Daniel and Milburn 1989
;
Peinová and Luk 1990
). While elegant, these solutions typically provide little direct intuition or insight into the underlying physics. Further, using them for practical calculation is often complicated (e.g. Ref.
Stobińska et al. 2008
for instance chose to study Eq. (
103
) numerically as opposed to exploiting existing analytic expressions). A more recent work Ref.
McDonald and Clerk 2022
demonstrated that the solvability of this interacting model is related to a special symmetry. However, the results of that work still do not provide a simple prescription for calculating observable quantities, especially if one is interested in a phase-space picture of the dynamics.

In this section, we will use the equivalence between third-quantization and the Keldysh path integral to show that there exists simple physical picture of the interplay between the non-linearity and damping in this system. We call this “self-dephasing”: the fluctuating photon number in the oscillator causes it to dephase itself, i.e. degrade Fock space coherences. Further, using a novel gauge transformation within the path integral, this intuition will lead to simple expressions for physical observables of interest. Namely, we will demonstrate that we can obtain the time evolution of the Wigner function for an arbitrary initial state. We note that we will also implicitely make use of the results in Secs.
III
-
IV
extensively.

VII.1
Propagator via Keldysh path integral

In our formulation of third quantization, the nonlinear term in Eq. (
103
) takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{U}{2}\left(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\right)\left(\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}-2\right)|\hat{\rho}\rangle\rangle

(104)

which, using Table
1
, in phase space is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{U}{2}\left(-\partial_{\alpha}\alpha+\partial_{\alpha^{*}}\alpha^{*}\right)\left(|\alpha|^{2}-\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha^{*}}-2\right)W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{U}{2}i\partial_{\phi}\left(|\alpha|^{2}-\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha^{*}}-2\right)W_{\hat{\rho}}(\alpha)

(105)

where
\phi=\arg\alpha
. The first term in the bracket tells us that the frequency of the mode is amplitude-dependent, whereas the second stems from the quantum noise associated with inherent uncertainty associated with the amplitude
|\alpha|^{2}
. The last term is due the ordering convention: phase-space averages weighted by the Wigner function are equivalent to symmetrically-ordered operator averages
Gardiner and Zoller 2010
. Our Hamiltonian on the other-hand is normal-ordered, hence this extra factor.

A path integral representation of the mixed phase-space kernel
K(\eta,\alpha;t)
can immediately be read off from Eq. (
6
) and Eq. (
105
),

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)=\langle e^{\eta^{*}a_{\rm cl}(t)-\eta a^{*}_{\rm cl}(t)+a_{\rm q}^{*}(0)\alpha-a_{\rm q}(0)\alpha^{*}}\rangle_{S}

(106)

where the average is with respect to the Keldysh action

\displaystyle S[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}a_{\rm cl}^{*}&a_{\rm q}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-\left(\omega_{0}-U+\frac{U}{2}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})+i\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\\
i\partial_{t^{\prime}}-\left(\omega_{0}-U+\frac{U}{2}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})-i\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)&i\kappa(2\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{\rm cl}\\
a_{\rm q}\end{pmatrix}

(107)

.

Here we have at times dropped the temporal argument for notational compactness. We have also defined
n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})\equiv a_{{\rm cl}}^{*}(t^{\prime})a_{{\rm cl}}(t^{\prime})+a_{{\rm q}}^{*}(t^{\prime})a_{{\rm q}}(t^{\prime})
. The nomenclature is justified by noting that this term comes from the contribution
\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm cl}+\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}^{\dagger}\bm{\hat{a}}_{\rm q}=\{\hat{n}_{\rm sym},\cdot\}
in third quantization where
\hat{n}_{\rm sym}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}\}
is the symmetrized number operator. Despite the name, our formulation still takes into account all quantum fluctuations. Only by dropping the quantum noise term
a_{{\rm cl}}^{*}(t^{\prime})a_{{\rm cl}}(t^{\prime})+a_{{\rm q}}^{*}(t^{\prime})a_{{\rm q}}(t^{\prime})\to a_{{\rm cl}}^{*}(t^{\prime})a_{{\rm cl}}(t^{\prime})
would the dynamics be rendered classical, since the phase-space equation of motion would be equivalent to that of a classical Fokker-Planck equation, see Eq. (
105
).

It is worth emphasizing that Eq. (
107
) is the usual Keldysh action for this model written in a non-standard way in that we have singled-out
n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})
. This way of writing the action is useful however since it makes it evident that the Kerr non-linearity serves as an amplitude-dependent frequency. By making the following gauge transformation within the path integral

\displaystyle a_{\rm cl/q}(t^{\prime})\to e^{iUt^{\prime}-i\frac{U}{2}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime\prime})}a_{\rm cl/q}(t^{\prime}),

(108)

we effectively move to a frame where the number-dependent phase accumulated by the fields has been taken into account.

This transformation has two effects: it both removes the non-linearity in the action
S\to S_{0}
and introduces a multiplicative phase factor attached to
a_{\rm cl}(t)

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)

\displaystyle=\langle\exp\left(\eta^{*}e^{iUt-i\frac{U}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})}a_{\rm cl}(t)+\alpha a_{\rm q}^{*}(0)-\rm{c.c.}\right)\rangle_{S_{0}}.

(109)

Although a seemingly a more unwieldy expression than our starting one Eq. (
106
), we now show how this can not can not only be straightforwardly evaluated but has a simple physical interpretation.

VII.2
Self-dephasing through amplitude fluctuations

To remove the phase factor
e^{-i\frac{U}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})}
from the exponential in Eq. (
109
), we can use the delta function representation of an angular variable
\delta(\theta-\theta^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-il(\theta-\theta^{\prime})}
and write

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}e^{il\theta+iUlt}\langle\exp\left(-i\frac{Ul}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})+\left[\eta^{*}e^{-i\theta}a_{\rm cl}(t)+\alpha a_{q}^{*}(0)-\rm{c.c.}\right]\right)\rangle_{S_{0}}

(110a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}e^{il\theta+iUlt}\langle\exp\left(\eta^{*}e^{-i\theta}a_{\rm cl}(t)+\alpha a_{q}^{*}(0)-\rm{c.c.}\right)\rangle_{S_{\frac{Ul}{2}}}

(110b)

In going from Eq. (
110a
) to Eq.(
110b
), we have lumped the phase proportional to the integral of
n_{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})
in a new
quadratic
action defined by

\displaystyle S_{\frac{Ul}{2}}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]\equiv S_{0}[a_{\rm cl},a_{\rm q}]-\frac{Ul}{2}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}(a_{\rm cl}^{*}a_{\rm cl}+a_{\rm q}^{*}a_{\rm q}).

(111)

As shown in Appendix
VII
, the resulting Gaussian integral for each
l
can then be performed and we arrive at

\displaystyle K(\eta,\alpha;t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i(\omega_{0}-U)lt+\frac{\kappa}{2}t}A_{l}(t)e^{-B_{{\rm{q}},l}(t)|\eta|^{2}}e^{-B_{{\rm{cl}},l}(t)|\alpha|^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}e^{il\theta+(\eta^{*}\alpha e^{-i\theta}-{\rm{c.c.}})A_{l}(t)}

(112)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i(\omega_{0}-U)lt+\frac{\kappa}{2}t}A_{l}(t)e^{-B_{{\rm q},l}(t)|\eta|^{2}-B_{{\rm cl},l}(t)|\alpha|^{2}}e^{-il(\varphi-\phi)}\mathcal{J}_{l}\Bigl(2|\eta||\alpha|A_{l}(t)\Bigr)

Time-dependent Wigner function

Our third-quantization fueled approach for deriving exact expressions for this nonlinear dissipative cavity problem is useful even beyond calculating specific time-dependent averages or correlators. 48

VIII
Conclusion

By starting with a different set of superoperators for both bosons Eq. (
50
) and fermions Eq. (
74
), we have reinterpreted the standard approach to third quantization introduced by Prosen
Prosen 2008
and Prosen and Seligman
Prosen and Seligman 2010
. These new superoperators allows us to naturally relate third quantization, Keldysh field theory and the phase-space formulation of quantum mechanics. Further, our approach let us straightforwardly identify a symmetry of all quadratic Lindbladians, which can then be used to effectively gauge away fluctuations in these models. This leads to a simple and intuitive diagonalization procedure, and provides a quantum-inspired way of demonstrate that dynamics are not affected by noise in linear systems. Finally, we have shown that our formalism provides a simple picture of the dynamics in a paradigmatic dissipative non-linear cavity model, which can be used to provide straightforward exact expressions.

In future work, it would be worth exploring if third quantization can be extended to spins in a useful manner. Further, it is interesting to ask whether the set of non-Hermitian quasiparticles which were used to diagonalize the Lindbladian can be used to build a mean-field theory of interacting open quantum systems.
