Electrodynamics of an oscillating particle without cheating PART I : In vacuo. PART II : Near a dispersive bulk
M. Garcia-Vergara, G. Demésy, A Nicolet, F. Zolla

Abstract
In this paper, the electromagnetic radiation from an oscillating particle placed in the vicinity of an object of size comparable to the wavelength is studied. Although this problem may seem academic at first sight, the details of the calculations are presented throughout without any detail left under the carpet. A polyharmonic decomposition of the radiation sources allows the diffraction problem to be fully characterised while satisfying energy conservation. Finally, the source expressions obtained are suitable for use in a numerical code. A 3D illustration using finite elements is provided.



1
Introduction

This paper consists in two main parts: In the first one we will address the problem of the electromagnetic (EM) field generated by an oscillating particle
in vacuo
and the second one is devoted to its interaction near to a bulk made of some dispersive material. Albeit the apparently pure academic nature of this problem, many applications to this case of configurations can be found. Starting from the study of antennas made by Hertz and Sommerfeld
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
to the more recently study of quantum nanoemitters and its interaction with nano spheres
[
4
,
5
]
. For this kind of phenomena, it is common to use an approximation to describe the field generated by an oscillating charged particle, the most common of these approximations for the far field is to use a
dipole approximation
as described in
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
2
]
. However, it is important to remark here that
the dipole approximation is only valid when dealing with the far field
.

It is then necessary to find a better way to describe the field generated by an oscillating charge, the immediate approach is to use the
Liénard-Wiechert
fields, which describe in quite a compact form the fields produced by a moving charge. The problem with this solution is that is not tractable from the practical (
i.e.
numerical) point of view as we will show in Section
3.2.1
. Other approaches have been to consider the moving charge as a delta distribution that can be approximated by a Taylor’s series
[
9
]
or a harmonic expansion
[
10
]
. Here we propose a different way for obtaining the harmonic representation of the fields which explicitly depends on time and space. Finally, once sources are obtained in harmonic form we use them as an incident field of the diffraction problem to obtain the diffracted field by a sphere by using the Finite Elements Method.

2
Mathematical description of the problem

The mathematical description of the interaction of an EM field with a time dispersive bulk is given by the Maxwell equations:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\textit{$\xi$}$}}}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}},

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=\partial_{t}\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}+\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=\rho,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=0

(4)

where
\textit{$\xi$}=\{I,II\}
represents the restriction of the fields,
I
corresponds to the total field
outside the bulk
and
II
to the field
inside the bulk
. The constitutive relations are then
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}
(
i.e.
the dispersive bulk has no magnetization) and
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=\epsilon_{0}(\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$}}*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}})
, where

\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$}}(t)=\begin{cases}2\pi\delta(t)&\textrm{ if }\xi=I,\\
2\pi\delta(t)+\chi(t)&\textrm{ if }\xi=II,\end{cases}

(5)

and
\chi(t)
is the causal electric susceptibility with its support within the bulk. The convolution is the one corresponding to the Fourier Transform convention in Appendix A. Thus, our new system of equations in terms of the (
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\xi}},\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\xi}}
) fields reads:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}(\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$ }}*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}})+\mu_{0}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot(\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$}}*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}})=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}=0.

(9)

The sources
\rho
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
represent the charge density and current corresponding to an oscillating particle and their explicit expression will be given in Section
4
. The fields generated by these sources
in vacuo
are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}+\mu_{0}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}},

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=0

(13)

where the constitutive relations
\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=\epsilon_{0}\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}=\mu_{0}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
,
\xi=\{I,II\}
have been used. Let us remark here that (
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
) is the incident field of our diffraction problem and that it is defined evrywhere in space.
The next step is to consider a new set of fields defined as:
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1}:=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}-\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1}:=\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}-\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}
which, in the present linear case, satisfy the system of so called diffracted fields:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}(\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$}}*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1})+\mu_{0}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{II}}^{0},

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot(\epsilon_{r,\textit{$\xi$}}*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1})=\frac{\rho^{0}_{II}}{\epsilon_{0}},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\textit{$\xi$}}}^{1}=0,

(17)

whose sources are defined as:

\rho^{0}_{II}:=-\epsilon_{0}\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot([\epsilon_{II}-2\pi\delta]*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}),

(18)

and

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{II}}^{0}:=\epsilon_{0}\partial_{t}([\epsilon_{II}-2\pi\delta]*\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}^{0}),

(19)

and from these definitions it is easy to see that they satisfy charge conservation. Notice that the support of these new sources is within the bulk and depends on the electromagnetic field generated by the oscillating particle. However obtaining a handy expression of such field requires a very careful crafting as it will be shown in the next section.

3
The search for the EM Field generated by an oscillating charge
in vacuo

The problem of obtaining the electromagnetic field generated by a charged oscillating particle is a very important problem
per se
. This section will start with the very general approach of finding the (
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}
) fields generated by an arbitrary pair of charge and current distributions (
\rho
,
\jmath
). Usually these solutions are given by the so-called
Jefimenko’s equations
. However, it will be shown how by following a similar method as in Ref.
[
11
]
for the far field approximations, it is possible to express the electric field mostly in terms of the current density. Next we will discuss some of the efforts that have been made in order to represent the EM field created by and oscillating charge.

3.1
The EM Field generated by an arbitrary charge distribution

First, we consider the system of equations (
10
-
13
) (in the sequel the superscript
0
denoting the incident field radiated by the oscillating particle in freespace has been removed to alleviate notations). By considering the
Lorentz gauge
, one can obtain the electric and magnetic fields (
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}
) using the expressions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}=-\partial_{t}\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{\nabla}\phi,

(20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{}}

(21)

where
\phi
and
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{}}
are the retarded potentials
[
7
,
6
]

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(23)

with the retarded time
t_{r}=t-\frac{R}{c}
and
R=|\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}|
with
\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}
. The next step consists in plugging the retarded potentials Eqs. (
22
,
23
) into Eqs. (
20
,
21
) and then take the appropriate derivatives. Nevertheless, this implies to take time derivatives with respect to a function which is in terms of the retarded time
t_{r}
. In order to avoid this difficulty and carry our calculations further, we propose to use the Fourier transform in time (See Annexe
A
,
\hat{f}
denoting the Fourier transform of
f
) and then consider only the spatial derivatives. Starting by transforming the potential
\phi
one has:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{R}\mathcal{F}_{t\rightarrow\omega}\{\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t-R/c)\}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{R}e^{iRk_{0}}\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(24)

where
k_{0}=\frac{\omega}{c}
. In a similar way the vector potential in the frequency domains reads:

\mathbf{\hat{A}}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{+iRk_{0}}\frac{\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(25)

Before proceeding, the following identities are necessary:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}R=\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}\frac{1}{R}=-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\mathbf{\nabla}R=-\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{3}},

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}e^{iRk_{0}}=ik_{0}e^{iRk_{0}}\mathbf{\nabla}R=ik_{0}e^{iRk_{0}}\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R},

(28)

and combining equations (
27
) and (
28
) we get:

\mathbf{\nabla}\bigg(\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\bigg)=\bigg(ik_{0}-\frac{1}{R}\bigg)\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}.

(29)

Equipped with these tools, it is quite easy to obtain
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}
by simply taking the curl of Eq. (
25
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{B}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\nabla}\times\bigg(\frac{e^{+iRk_{0}}}{R}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\nabla}\bigg(\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\bigg)\times\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(ik_{0}-\frac{1}{R}\bigg)\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\times\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(32)

And by taking the inverse Fourier transform, we arrive to the expression:

\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{int}}+\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}},

(33)

where
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{int}}
is the intermediate field and
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}}
is the radiated field which are given by the integrals:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\partial_{t}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(35)

In order to obtain the electric field, it is necessary to consider the Fourier transform of Eq. (
20
):

\mathbf{\hat{E}}=+i\omega\mathbf{\hat{A}}-\mathbf{\nabla}\hat{\phi}.

(36)

The first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
36
) is quite easy to calculate,

i\omega\mathbf{\hat{A}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}ik_{0}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\omega,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime})\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(37)

The second term is much more tricky and its derivation goes as follows:

\displaystyle-\mathbf{\nabla}\hat{\phi}=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\nabla}\bigg(\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\bigg)\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\nabla}\bigg(\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\bigg)\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}ik_{0}\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{3}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(38)

Up to this point most textbooks
e.g.
Refs.
[
6
,
7
,
12
]
simply take the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
38
) and give the electric field in terms of the derivative of the density of charges and obtain the so called
Jefimenko’s equations

[
12
,
7
]
. Despite the straightforward nature of this derivation, we are going to carry the calculations further. The interest of doing so will be shown later. For our purposes, the second integral in Eq. (
38
) is already in optimum form, and we will focus our attention on the integral defined within a bounded volume
\Omega
.

{\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\Omega}:=\int_{\Omega}ik_{0}\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{2}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(39)

Notice that the integral we are looking for, is the limit case when
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Next, we will make use of the continuity equation in the frequency domain:

i\omega\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\omega)=\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}

(40)

and by defining the function:

F(R):=\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{cR^{2}},

(41)

the integral
{\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\Omega}
in Eq. (
39
) can be written in a more compact way (omitting the
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}
,
R
and
\omega
dependencies) as:

{\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\Omega}:=\int_{\Omega}F\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(42)

It is very important to remark here that
the divergence is being taken with respect to the primed coordinates (hence the prime superscript in
\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}
)
. Due to the fact that we are working with Cartesian coordinates, it is possible to write

\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}=\sum_{\eta=x,y,z}(\eta-\eta^{\prime}){\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\eta},

(43)

where
(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{x}},\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}},\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}})
denote the cartesian unit vectors, and then:

{\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\Omega}=\sum_{\eta=x,y,z}\int_{\Omega}(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}{\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\text{$\eta$}}=\sum_{\eta=x,y,z}I_{\Omega,\eta}{\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{}}}_{\text{$\eta$}}.

(44)

Each one of these
\eta
integrals can be evaluated by means of the identity
(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}=\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\cdot\big[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\big]-\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\big[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\big]
and the Green-Ostrogradsky’s theorem
[
13
,
14
]
as follows:

I_{\Omega,\eta}=\int_{\partial\Omega}(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{out}}\big|_{\partial\Omega}\,\mathrm{d}S^{\prime}-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\big[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\big]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(45)

Taking the limit
\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
and keeping in mind that the boundary term vanishes as
\frac{1}{R}
we have:

I_{\mathbb{R}^{3},\eta}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\big[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\big]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(46)

The gradient (with respect to the primed coordinates) can be computed explicitly:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}\big[(\eta-\eta^{\prime})F\big]=

\displaystyle F\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}(\eta-\eta^{\prime})+(\eta-\eta^{\prime})\mathbf{\nabla}^{\prime}F

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-F\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{\text{$\eta$}}}-(\eta-\eta^{\prime})\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\bigg(\frac{ik_{0}}{R}-\frac{2}{R^{2}}\bigg)F

(48)

and then

\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{\mathbb{R}^{3},\text{$\eta$}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}F\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{\text{$\eta$}}}+(\eta-\eta^{\prime})\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\bigg(\frac{ik_{0}}{R}-\frac{2}{R^{2}}\bigg)F\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(49)

Plugging Eq. (
49
) into Eq. (
44
) we get:

\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg[\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}+\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\bigg(\frac{ik_{0}}{R}-\frac{2}{R^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]F\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(50)

Before taking the inverse Fourier transform, we will try to express the term between square brackets (which we will call
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
) in a more illuminating way. First, we rearrange
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
as:

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\frac{2}{R^{2}}+ik_{0}\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R}.

(51)

Now we consider the vector identity
(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})-R^{2}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}

[
14
]
, and from this we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R}=\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R}+R\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}},

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}-2\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R^{2}}=-\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{2}}-\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R^{2}}.

(53)

Then
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
can be seen as:

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}=-\bigg[\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{2}}+\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R^{2}}\bigg]+ik_{0}\bigg[\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R}+R\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\bigg].

(54)

Substituting this result into Eq. (
50
) and then plugging that new integral into Eq. (
38
) we finally arrive to the expression:

\displaystyle-\mathbf{\nabla}\hat{\phi}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\hat{\rho}\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R^{3}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}ik_{0}\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{3}}e^{iRk_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg[\frac{(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{4}}+\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R^{4}}\bigg]e^{iRk_{0}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}ik_{0}\mathbf{\hat{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}}(\omega,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime})\frac{e^{iRk_{0}}}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}

(55)

From Eq. (
37
) we recognize the last integral as
-i\omega\mathbf{\hat{A}}
and then, after taking the inverse Fourier transform, we get:

\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{c}}+\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{int}}+\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}},

(56)

where
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{c}}
is the Coulomb field,
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{int}}
the intermediate field and
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}}
the radiated field given by the integrals:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})}{R^{3}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{int}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg[\frac{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{4}}+\frac{(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\cdot\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})}{R^{4}}\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{(\partial_{t}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(59)

At this point the reader may be wondering the reason for why we have made all these extra steps when the Jefimenko’s equations already provide an explicit expression for the electric field. This is because when dealing with the Jefimenko’s equations, the magnetic field is terms of the electric current
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
and the electric field is in terms of the distribution of charge
\rho

[
12
,
7
]
. This point of view albeit intuitively is very clear, makes it difficult to compare the terms corresponding to the radiation field. By making the manipulations described above, we have ensured the fact that the intermediate and radiated electric and magnetic fields are all expressed in terms of the electric current solely. The utility of this approach will be shown later.

3.2
The different approximations to the oscillating source problem

3.2.1
The Liénard-Wiechert’s Field approach

The academic problem of describing the
\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}
fields generated by a charged particle that moves along a given trajectory
\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{}}(t)
, which are called the
Liénard-Wiechert
fields, has been studied in many books
[
6
,
7
,
10
,
15
,
16
,
11
]
. The basic idea is to consider a charge density
\rho
and an electric current
\mathbf{j}_{\mathrm{}}
given by:

\displaystyle\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)=q\delta(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{}}(t)),

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)=q\delta(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{}}(t))\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{}}(t),

(61)

where
\delta
is a Delta distribution and
\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{}}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{}}}{dt}

3.2.3

4
Harmonic decomposition of the sources

As we saw in the previous section, the
Liénard-Wiechert
fields are not the best way to obtain the fields produced by an oscillating particle. The main problem is that the source terms depend on the trajectory that describes the charge. For this reason it will be convenient to find a way to decompose the source terms in a polyharmonic way. This idea has been previously considered by Landau
[
10
]
and, as evident from equations (
3.2.3
) and (
3.2.3
), also by Raimond
[
9
]
, albeit indirectly. However, as demonstrated in the previous section, the expressions derived from Landau’s and Raimond’s ideas are challenging to implement in practice. Thus, a new approach for describing the sources is necessary.

In this section we propose another way inspired in quantum mechanics, which can be summarized as:
The superposition of waves spread in a certain domain can be seen as a particle
. Physically, this means that a very localized source can be seen as the interference of a certain kind of waves. Mathematically speaking, we are looking to find a sequence such that, we can have convergence in the sense of distributions to a Dirac delta
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. It is worth noting that a similar concept is employed in references
[
22
,
23
]
for the case of a charged particle in uniform motion.

4.1
Two Fourier expansions for the sources

Let us start our analysis by having the charge density
\rho
and its current density
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
simply given by:

\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)=q\delta(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\bot}})\otimes\varrho(z,t),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\bot}}=(x,y)

(88)

with
\varrho(z,t):=\delta(z-a\cos(\w_0 t))
and

\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)=q\delta(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\bot}})\otimes\jmath(z,t)\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}},

(89)

with
\jmath(z,t):=-a\omega_{0}\sin(\w_0 t)\varrho(z,t)
out of charge conservation. Notice that
\varrho
and
\jmath
are not multiplied by the charge. It then turns out that the charge density is not harmonic despite the harmonic motion of the particle. In other words, no complex function
\underline{\varrho}(z)
can be found in such a way that
\varrho(z,t)=\real\mathrm{e}\{\underline{\varrho}(z)e^{i\omega t}\}
.
This is quite important to remark because in references as
[
6
,
17
]
this is the starting point when representing and oscillating dipole
.
Nevertheless it is apropos to notice that the distribution
\varrho(z,\cdot)
is a
\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{0}}
-periodic distribution. Thus,
\varrho
as well as
\jmath(z,t)
can be expanded as a Fourier series
(See Annex
B
) :

\varrho(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varrho_{l}^{F}(z,t),\quad\jmath(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\jmath_{l}^{F}(z,t),

(90)

with

\varrho_{l}^{F}(z,t)=w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}

(91)

and

\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{F}(z,t)=-a\omega_{0}\sin(\w_0 t)w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t},

(92)

where
T_{l}
are the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind
[
24
]
and
w(z)
is the weight function

w(z):=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{a^{2}-z^{2}}}\chi_{[-a,a]}(z)\,

(93)

with
\chi_{[-a,a]}(z)
a characteristic function.

4.2
Continuity equation for the harmonic components of the sources

In the previous paragraph, an expansion for
\rho
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
are linked by the so-called charge conservation, namely:
\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}+\accentset{\mbox{\large.}}{\rho}=0
. Notice that for moving point particles this equation has to be understood in the sense of distributions
[
13
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
. What about the different components
\varrho_{l}^{F}
and
\jmath_{l}^{F}
? In other words, is there any transference of energy, between the different waves oscillating with the different frequencies at stake
\omega_{0}
,
2\omega_{0}
, etc
\dots
? To answer this question, we have to care much more about the notation referring this
l
.
While
\varrho_{l}^{F}
oscillates with frequency
l\omega_{0}
, the scalar function
\jmath_{l}^{F}
is a mix of two oscillations with different frequencies namely
(l-1)\omega_{0}
and
(l+1)\omega_{0}
due to the presence of
\sin(\w_0 t)
term in Eq. (
92
).
Making use of the complex representation of
\sin(\w_0 t)
it follows:

\jmath(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{F}(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{a\omega_{0}}{2i}w(z)T_{l}(\frac{z}{a})[e^{i(l-1)\omega_{0}t}-e^{i(l+1)\omega_{0}t}].

(94)

As we can see, this representation is not in a convenient form, instead we would like to see each term in the series oscillating at frequency
l\omega_{0}
(where
l
is a dummy index), namely:

\jmath(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\jmath_{l}^{T}(z)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}.

(95)

This can be easily achieved after renaming indices (
l+1\rightarrow l
and
l-1\rightarrow l
) for the corresponding terms
e^{i(l-1)\omega_{0}t}
and
e^{i(l+1)\omega_{0}t}
which allows to obtain:

\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{T}(z):=\frac{a\omega_{0}}{2i}[\textit{$\xi$}_{l+1}(z)-\textit{$\xi$}_{l-1}(z)],

(96)

where
\textit{$\xi$}_{l}(z)=w(z)T_{l}(\frac{z}{a})
. Analogously for
\rho(z,t)
we get:

\rho(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varrho_{l}^{F}(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varrho_{l}^{T}(z)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\textit{$\xi$}_{l}(z)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}.

(97)

The arcane meaning of the superscripts
T
and
F
is therefore clear:
T
(resp.
F
) means true (resp. false) in the sense that each spatial coefficient corresponds to only one
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Correspondingly the conservation of charge can be now formulated for each multiple of the frequency
\omega_{0}
.
By the definition of
\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)
The conservation of charge implies:

\partial_{t}\varrho(z,t)+\partial_{z}\jmath(z,t)=0,

(98)

and plugging the harmonic expansions of
\varrho(z,t)
and
\jmath(z,t)
one gets:

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg[il\omega_{0}\textit{$\xi$}_{l}(z)+\frac{a\omega_{0}}{2i}\partial_{z}(\textit{$\xi$}_{l+1}(z)-\textit{$\xi$}_{l-1}(z))\bigg]e^{+il\omega_{0}t}=0.

(99)

Given the fact that
e^{+il\omega_{0}t}
with
l\in\mathbb{Z}
is a basis
[
19
,
20
]
for our polyharmonic decomposition, all the terms between square brackets are equal to zero. Thus the following identity is obtained:

l\textit{$\xi$}_{l}(z)=\frac{a}{2}\partial_{z}(\textit{$\xi$}_{l+1}(z)-\textit{$\xi$}_{l-1}(z)).

(100)

And the conservation of charge for each
l
-th term in the expressions Eq. (
95
) and Eq. (
97
) follows. Upon demonstrating that there is no transfer among the distinct harmonic components of the charge density
\rho(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)
and the current density
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}},t)
, we may proceed to employ these Fourier expansions to derive the electric and magnetic induction fields for an oscillating charged particle.This will be show in the following section.

5

The main consequence of the harmonic decomposition of the sources and the conservation of charge term by term is that the electric and magnetic induction fields, respectively

-

) and (

-

\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-\mathbf{x^{\prime}}_{\mathrm{}}
,

the retarded time. It is important to remark here that

5.1
The Fourier expansion of the Fields

6
Far Field radiated power

As it was mentioned before, the main approach towards the study of the EM field generated by an oscillating charge requires to consider the
far field approximation
. In this section we will demonstrate how, by using the polyharmonic expression from previous sections, it is possible to retrive the same results as reported in the literature
[
6
]
.

6.1
The polyharmonic representation of the EM far field approximations

As we know, the radiated electric and magnetic induction fields are given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}}=q\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi c}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\partial_{t}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{}}}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},

(120)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}}=q\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{(\partial_{t}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r})\times\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{}}}{R}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}.

(121)

where
\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{}}=\frac{\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{}}}{R}
. Using the fact that the electric current is defined as
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},t_{r}):=q\delta(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\bot}})\otimes\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z,t_{r})\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}
, the fields read:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}}=q\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi c}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\jmath}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\tilde{t}_{r})\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\mathbf{\tilde{n}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\tilde{R}}dz^{\prime},

(122)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}}=q\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\partial_{t}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime},\tilde{t}_{r})\times\mathbf{\tilde{n}}_{\mathrm{}})\times\mathbf{\tilde{n}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\tilde{R}}dz^{\prime}.

(123)

and as we did before
\mathbf{\tilde{R}}_{\mathrm{}}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}-z^{\prime}\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}
,
\mathbf{\tilde{n}}_{\mathrm{}}=\frac{\mathbf{\tilde{R}}_{\mathrm{}}}{\tilde{R}}
, and
\tilde{t}_{r}=t-\frac{\tilde{R}}{c}
. Now, in order to compute the fields radiated
at infinitum
we make the following approximations for when
|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}^{\prime}|\ll|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{n}}_{\mathrm{}}\simeq\frac{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}=\boldsymbol{\nu},

(124)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{R}}\simeq\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|},

(125)

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{r}\simeq t-\frac{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}{c}+\bigg(\frac{z}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z^{\prime}}{c}\bigg)=\tau_{r}.

(126)

By plugging these approximations into equations (
122
) and (
123
), one arrives to the expressions:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{rad}}\simeq\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=

\displaystyle q\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi c|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z^{\prime},\tau_{r})dz^{\prime}\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu},

(127)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{rad}}\simeq\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=

\displaystyle q\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}c^{2}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z^{\prime},\tau_{r})dz^{\prime}(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})\times\boldsymbol{\nu},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(128)

From this last equation it is clear that we can focus our attention just in
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}
. Therefore, the next step is to consider its polyharmonic representation. Remembering the definition of
\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z^{\prime},\tau_{r})
in equation (
95
) and
\tau_{r}
in (
126
) we have that

\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{il\omega_{0}t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l},

(129)

with the spatial coefficient
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}
defined as:

\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}:=il\omega_{0}\frac{q\mu_{0}}{4\pi c|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{T}(z^{\prime})\exp[ilk_{0}\frac{z}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}z^{\prime}\bigg]dz^{\prime}(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}).

(130)

Here it is important to emphasize that
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{0}=0
. Thus the results obtained in this section are for
l\neq 0
.
Notice that the integral term resembles to a spatial Fourier transform that goes from
z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}
with the new variable

\eta_{l}:=lk_{0}\frac{z}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}.

(131)

Thus

\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}=il\omega_{0}\frac{q\mu_{0}}{4\pi c|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}2\pi\mathcal{F}_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}}\big\{\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{T}(z^{\prime})\big\}(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}).

(132)

This Fourier transform can be easily computed by using the definition in Eq.(
96
):

2\pi\mathcal{F}_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}}\big\{\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{T}(z^{\prime})\big\}=2\pi\bigg[\mathcal{F}_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}}\big\{\textit{$\xi$}_{l+1}(z^{\prime})-\mathcal{F}_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}}\big\{\textit{$\xi$}_{l-1}(z^{\prime})\big\}\bigg]\frac{a\omega_{0}}{2i}

(133)

Fortunately, the Fourier transform of
\textit{$\xi$}_{l}
is given in equation (
206
) and after using identity (
242
) one gets:

2\pi\mathcal{F}_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow\eta_{l}}\big\{\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{T}(z^{\prime})\big\}=ai^{l}l\omega_{0}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a},

(134)

which implies that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}

\displaystyle=i^{l+1}\frac{q\mu_{0}}{4\pi c}(l\omega_{0})^{2}\frac{a}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}),

(135)

\displaystyle=i^{l+1}\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}c(lk_{0})^{2}qa\frac{e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}(\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu}),

(136)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}c(lk_{0})^{2}\frac{e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}(\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}})\bigg(i^{l-1}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\bigg),

(137)

where we have defined the dipole moment vector
\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}=qa\mathbf{e}_{\mathrm{z}}
. Notice that this last expression is very similar to the one in
[
6
]
for the
magnetic dipole field
in the radiation zone.
Calling

A_{l}=i^{l-1}\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}c(lk_{0})^{2}e^{-ilk_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|},

(138)

we get:

\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}=A_{l}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nu}\times\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}})}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|},

(139)

and its square norm is given by:

\displaystyle|\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{l}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{z}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2},

(140)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|A_{l}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{a\eta_{l}}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2},

(141)

where it has been used the fact that:
\eta_{l}=lk_{0}\frac{z}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|}
. This last result will be used in the study of the radiated power.

6.2
Radiated power

The expression for the far field radiated power for an oscillating will be derived. First for the relativistic case and later for the non relativistic one.

6.2.1
Relativistic case

We know from the definition of the Poynting vector and (
6.1
) that:

\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{\infty}}\times\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}(\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}\times\boldsymbol{\nu})\times\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}=\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}|\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}|^{2}\boldsymbol{\nu}.

(142)

Remembering the Fourier expansion of
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}
in Eq. (
129
) we have that:

|\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
l\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\neq 0\end{subarray}}e^{i(l-m)\omega_{0}t}\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}^{l}\overline{\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{}}}^{m}_{\infty}.

(143)

Now, we consider the time-averaged Poynting vector, given the fact that
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{\infty}}
is
\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{0}}
-periodic we integrate Eq. (
142
) from
-\frac{\pi}{\omega_{0}}
to
\frac{\pi}{\omega_{0}}
to have:

\innerproduct{\Field[\infty]{S}}{\Field[\infty]{S}}=\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}\innerproduct{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}\boldsymbol{\nu},

(144)

where the quantities between brackets are time averaged. By the orthogonality of the complex exponential we have:

\innerproduct{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
l\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{|A_{l}|^{2}}{|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{}}|^{2}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{a\eta_{l}}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2}.

(145)

For obtaining the power it is customary to calculate the flux of the averaged Poynting vector through a spherical surface of radius
R_{0}
(it could be any surface encompassing the EM sources, but the spherical surface is the one that allows the simplest calculations). That is:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\innerproduct{\Field[\infty]{S}}{\Field[\infty]{S}}R_{0}^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\innerproduct{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}{|\Field[\infty]{B}|^2}R_{0}^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi,

(146)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
l\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{2\pi c}{\mu_{0}}|A_{l}|^{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(\eta_{l}a)}{\eta_{l}a}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{a\eta_{l}}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(147)

Next, we perform the change of variable:

u=a\eta_{l}=alk_{0}\frac{z}{R_{0}}=alk_{0}\cos\theta

(148)

then the differential
du
reads

\mathrm{d}u=-alk_{0}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

(149)

After these calculations, the flux of the averaged Poynting vector reads:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\innerproduct{\Field[\infty]{S}}{\Field[\infty]{S}}R_{0}^{2}\sin\theta d\theta d\phi=

(150)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\in\mathbb{Z}\\
l\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{2\pi c}{\mu_{0}alk_{0}}|A_{l}|^{2}\int_{-alk_{0}}^{alk_{0}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(u)}{u}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{u}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2}du.

Calling:

D_{l}:=\frac{c(2\pi)^{2}}{\omega_{0}\mu_{0}alk_{0}}|A_{l}|^{2}=\frac{2\pi c}{\mu_{0}alk_{0}}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}c(lk_{0})^{2}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{Z_{0}c^{2}(lk_{0})^{4}}{8\pi(alk_{0})},

(151)

and because
D_{-l}=-D_{l}
one has that the averaged energy flux can be seen as:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\innerproduct{\Field[\infty]{S}}{\Field[\infty]{S}}R_{0}^{2}\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}2D_{l}\int_{-alk_{0}}^{alk_{0}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(u)}{u}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{u}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}u|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2},

(152)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}P_{l}

(153)

with
P_{l}
the
l
-th contribution given by:

\displaystyle P_{l}

\displaystyle=4D_{l}\int_{0}^{alk_{0}}\bigg(\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}(u)}{u}\bigg)^{2}\bigg[1-\bigg(\frac{u}{alk_{0}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}u|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2},\qquad l\geq 1,

(154)

\displaystyle=4D_{l}\bigg[\int_{0}^{alk_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{l}^{2}(u)}{u^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}u-\frac{1}{(alk_{0})^{2}}\int_{0}^{alk_{0}}\mathrm{J}_{l}^{2}(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\bigg]|\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{z}}|^{2}

(155)

It is common sense that for the

The

For large

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow 0<ak_{0}\cos\theta<1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow v:=a\omega_{0}<c

With

9
Conclusion

In this
chapter
work the problem of an oscillating particle near to a nano-sphere has been studied by using the diffracted field formalism. The first problem to tackle was the description of the electromagnetic field generated by an oscillating particle
in vacuo
. Albeit this problem can be solved in principle by the Liénard-Wiechert fields, the resulting equations are not useful in practice. In order to overcome this difficulty we propose a harmonic decomposition of the sources
\rho
and
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\jmath}}_{\mathrm{}}
which will allow us to find the harmonic representation of the electromagnetic fields. Next, we make an analysis of the radiated fields for the relativistic and non relativistic cases. Finally, by using the finite Elements Method, we solve the full problem that involves the interaction between the oscillating charge and the dispersive nano sphere. The solutions obtained have been validated by an energy balance.

Appendix B

In this Annexe we show to obtain the poly harmonic representation of the sources.

For this, the distribution
\varrho(z,t)
has to be considered as a
\delta
-distribution of a function
f(t):=z-a\cos(\w_0 t)
. Using the expansion given in
[
24
,
6
]
, we obtain:

\varrho(z,t)=\delta(f(t))=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\dot{f}(t_{l})}\delta(t-t_{l}),

(182)

where
t_{l}
are the zeros of the function
f(t)
,
i.e.

\cos(\w_0 t)=\frac{z}{a}.

(183)

Given the fact that the absolute value of the cosine is bounded by 1, there are only two solutions within the range
[-a,a]
. Then, by considering the principal branch of the arc cosine function and denoting
t_{0}(z):=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}}\mathrm{arccos}(\frac{z}{a})
, these zeros are

\displaystyle t_{l}^{+}(z)

\displaystyle=t_{0}(z)+2l\pi/\omega_{0},

(184)

\displaystyle t_{l}^{-}(z)

\displaystyle=-t_{0}(z)+2l\pi/\omega_{0}.

(185)

In addition, it turns out that for every
t_{l}
(
t^{+}_{l}
or
t^{-}_{l}
)
|\dot{f}(t)|=\omega_{0}\sqrt{a^{2}-z^{2}}
and the
\delta
-distribution reads:

\varrho(z,t)=\delta(f(t))=\frac{1}{\omega_{0}\sqrt{a^{2}-z^{2}}}(W_{+}(t)+W_{-}(t))\chi_{[-a,a]}(z),

(186)

where
W_{\pm}(t,z)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(t-t^{\pm}(z))
. These last two series of distributions (Dirac’s combs) are expandable in a Fourier series as follows
[
35
]
:

W_{\pm}(t,z)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta\bigg(t\mp t_{0}(z)-\frac{2\pi l}{\omega_{0}}\bigg)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Phi^{\pm}_{m}e^{i\frac{m\pi}{T_{0}}t},

(187)

with
T_{0}=\frac{\pi}{\omega_{0}}
. Making the change of variables
t=\tau\pm t_{0}(z)
one gets:

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta(\tau-2lT_{0})=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\Phi^{\pm}_{m}e^{i\frac{m\pi}{T_{0}}(\tau\pm t_{0}(z))}.

(188)

Multiplying this equation by
e^{-i\frac{n\pi}{T_{0}}(\tau\pm t_{0}(z))}
, taking the integral from
-T_{0}
to
T_{0}
and performing the change of variables
\sigma=\tau-2lT_{0}
, equation (
188
) can be rewritten as:

\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{-(2l-1)T_{0}}^{-(2l+1)T_{0}}\delta(\sigma)e^{-im\frac{\pi\sigma}{T_{0}}}e^{\mp im\frac{t_{0}(z)}{T_{0}}}d\sigma=2T_{0}\Phi^{\pm}_{m}.

(189)

or in a more illuminating way

e^{\mp im\frac{t_{0}(z)}{T_{0}}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\delta(\sigma)e^{-im\frac{\pi\sigma}{T_{0}}}d\sigma=2T_{0}\Phi^{\pm}_{m},

(190)

which implies

\Phi^{\pm}_{m}=\frac{1}{2T_{0}}e^{\mp im\frac{t_{0}(z)}{T_{0}}}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}e^{\mp im\omega_{0}t_{0}(z)}.

(191)

Then

W_{\pm}(t,z)=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\mp im\omega_{0}t_{0}(z)}e^{im\omega_{0}t},

(192)

and naturally (recovering the
l
index)

\displaystyle W_{+}(t,z)+W_{-}(t,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg[e^{il\omega_{0}t_{0}(z)}+e^{-il\omega_{0}t_{0}(z)}\bigg]e^{im\omega_{0}t},

(193)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}}{\pi}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\cos(l \w_0 t_0(z))e^{il\omega_{0}t}.

(194)

Remembering the definition of
t_{0}
one gets:

\cos(l \w_0 t_0)=\cos\bigg(l \arccos\bigg(\frac{z}{a} \bigg) \bigg)=T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg),

(195)

where
T_{l}
are the Chebyshev polynomials of first kind. Therefore

W_{+}(t,z)+W_{-}(t,z)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}

(196)

Plugging this equation into Eq. (
186
) and defining the function

w(z):=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{a^{2}-z^{2}}}\chi_{[-a,a]}(z),

(197)

the function
\varrho
reads

\varrho(z,t)=\sum_{l\mathbb{Z}}w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}.

(198)

As a consequence of this, the function
\boldsymbol{\jmath}
is given by

\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z,t)=-a\omega_{0}\sin(\w_0 t)\sum_{l\mathbb{Z}}w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}.

(199)

In short,
\varrho
and
\boldsymbol{\jmath}
are in the following form

\varrho(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\varrho_{l}^{F}(z,t),\quad\boldsymbol{\jmath}(z,t)=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{F}(z,t),

(200)

with

\varrho_{l}^{F}(z,t)=w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t},

(201)

\boldsymbol{\jmath}_{l}^{F}(z,t)=-a\omega_{0}\sin(\w_0 t)w(z)T_{l}\bigg(\frac{z}{a}\bigg)e^{+il\omega_{0}t}.

(202)

On the other hand, the Fourier transform
\varrho(z,t):=\delta(z-a\cos(\w_0t))
can be derived by considering the convention:

\displaystyle\hat{\varrho}(k,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{z\rightarrow k}\{\delta(z-a\cos(\w_0t))\}
