Handling Hard Affine SDP Shape Constraints in RKHSs
Pierre-Cyril Aubin-Frankowski pierre-cyril.aubin@inria.fr, Zoltán Szabó z.szabo@lse.ac.uk

Abstract
Shape constraints, such as non-negativity, monotonicity, convexity or supermodularity, play a key role in various applications of machine learning and statistics. However, incorporating this side information into predictive models in a hard way (for example at all points of an interval) for rich function classes is a notoriously challenging problem. We propose a unified and modular convex optimization framework, relying on second-order cone (SOC) tightening, to encode hard affine SDP constraints on function derivatives, for models belonging to vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (vRKHSs). The modular nature of the proposed approach allows to simultaneously handle multiple shape constraints, and to tighten an infinite number of constraints into finitely many. We prove the convergence of the proposed scheme and that of its adaptive variant, leveraging geometric properties of vRKHSs. Due to the covering-based construction of the tightening, the method is particularly well-suited to tasks with small to moderate input dimensions. The efficiency of the approach is illustrated in the context of shape optimization, safety-critical control, robotics and econometrics.

Introduction

The design of flexible predictive models is among the most fundamental problems of machine learning. However, in several applications one is faced with a limited number of samples due to the difficulty or the cost of data acquisition.
A well-established way to tackle this serious bottleneck and to improve sample-efficiency corresponds to incorporating qualitative priors on the shape of the model, such as non-negativity, monotonicity, convexity or supermodularity, collectively known as shape constraints
(
Guntuboyina and Sen 2018
)
. This side information can originate from both physical and theoretical constraints on the model such as “stay within boundaries” in path-planning or “be nonnegative and integrate to one” in density estimation.

Various scientific fields, including econometrics, statistics, biology, game theory or finance, impose shape constraints on their hypothesis classes. For instance, economic theory dictates increasing and concave utility functions, decreasing demand functions, or monotone link functions
(
Johnson and Jiang 2018
;
Chetverikov et al. 2018
)
.
In statistics, applying a monotonicity assumption on the regression function (for instance in isotonic regression;
Han et al. 2019
) dates back at least to
Brunk 1955
; the non-negativity requirement also arises naturally when learning the intensity function of Poisson processes and the triggering function of Hawkes processes
(
Yang et al. 2019
)
. Density estimation entails non-negativity which can be paired with other constraints
(
Royset and Wets 2015
)
, whereas, in quantile regression, conditional quantile functions grow w.r.t. the quantile level
(
Koenker 2005
)
.
In biology, monotone regression is particularly well-suited to dose-response studies
(
Hu et al. 2005
)
and to identification of genome interactions
(
Luss et al. 2012
)
. Inventory problems, game theory and pricing models commonly rely on the assumption of supermodularity
(
Topkis 1998
;
Simchi-Levi et al. 2014
)
. In financial applications, call option prices should be increasing in volatility, monotone and convex in the underlying stock price
(
Aït-Sahalia and Duarte 2003
)
. In control theory, shape constraints are known as state constraints, and rank among the most difficult topics of the field
(
Hartl et al. 1995
;
Aubin-Frankowski 2021
)
.

A large and important class of these shape requirements takes the form of an affine SDP (positive semidefinite) inequality over the derivatives of
\mathbf{f}\in\mathscr{F}
where
\mathscr{F}
is a hypothesis class (a set of candidate predictive models). Particularly, these constraints are requested to hold
pointwise
at
all elements
of a set
\mathscr{K}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}_{P\times P}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\diag(\mathbf{b})+\mathbf{D}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})\quad\forall\,\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{K}

(1)

for some bias
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{P}
and differential operator
\mathbf{D}
(e.g., the Hessian). The fundamental challenge one faces when optimizing an objective
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{f})
over
\mathscr{F}
is that in most relevant cases the set
\mathscr{K}
has non-finite cardinality, and hence there is an infinite number of constraints to satisfy. For instance, in constrained path-planning,
\mathscr{K}
corresponds to a time interval and the goal is to avoid collisions at all times.

In the statistics community, the main emphasis has been on designing consistent estimators and on studying their rates
(
Han and Wellner 2016
;
Chen and Samworth 2016
;
Freyberger and Reeves 2018
;
Lim 2020
;
Deng and Zhang 2020
;
Kur et al. 2020
)
. While these asymptotic results are of significant theoretical interest, imposing shape priors is generally beneficial in the small-sample regime. Since optimization with an infinite number of constraints (
1
) is
computationally intractable
, one has to either relax or tighten the problem.

Relaxing
corresponds to approaches for which the constraint (
1
) is not guaranteed to be satisfied. For instance, one can choose to enforce the constraint only at a finite number of points
(
Takeuchi et al. 2006
;
Blundell et al. 2012
;
Agrell 2019
)
by replacing
\mathscr{K}
with a discretization
\{\mathbf{x}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}\subsetneq\mathscr{K}
in (
1
). An alternative approach for relaxing is to add soft penalties to the objective
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{f})

(
Sangnier et al. 2016
;
Koppel et al. 2019
;
Brault et al. 2019
)
.
Tightening
on the contrary restricts the search space of functions to a smaller and more amenable subset
\mathscr{F}_{0}\subsetneq\mathscr{F}
. This principle can be implemented by encoding the requirement (
1
) into
\mathscr{F}_{0}
through algebraic techniques. The approach is feasible for restrictive finite-dimensional
\mathscr{F}_{0}
such as subsets of polynomials
(
Hall 2018
;
Curmei and Hall 2021
)
or polynomial splines
(
Turlach 2005
;
Papp and Alizadeh 2014
;
Pya and Wood 2015
;
Wu and Sickles 2018
;
Meyer 2018
)
. These limitations motivate the design of novel shape-constrained optimization techniques which avoid (i) restricted function classes, (ii) limited out-of-sample guarantees and (iii) the lack of modularity in terms of the shape constraints imposed.

In this work the class of functions
\mathscr{F}
is assumed to be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathscr{F}:=\mathscr{F}_{K}
(RKHS;
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
;
Saitoh and Sawano 2016
; also referred to as abstract splines;
Wahba 1990
;
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2004
;
Wang 2011
). There are multiple advantages in selecting this family of functions. First, kernel methods rely inherently on pointwise evaluation
(
Aronszajn 1950
)
which are well-suited to handle the pointwise constraints (
1
). In particular, the associated reproducing property (which also holds for derivatives;
Zhou 2008
) allows one to rephrase the inequality constraints (
1
) in
\mathscr{F}_{K}
using a geometric perspective, as it will be elaborated in Section
3.1
. Moreover, RKHSs can be rich enough to approximate various function classes (including the space of continuous bounded functions, a property known as universality;
Steinwart 2001
;
Micchelli et al. 2006
;
Sriperumbudur et al. 2011
;
Simon-Gabriel and Schölkopf 2018
).
In addition, the models
\mathbf{f}\in\mathscr{F}_{K}
obtained through kernel regression share the regularity of the underlying kernel
(
Steinwart and Christmann 2008
)
, allowing one to incorporate additional prior knowledge through the choice of
K
. Furthermore, vector-valued RKHSs (vRKHS;
Micchelli and Pontil 2005
;
Brouard et al. 2011
;
Kadri et al. 2016
;
Bouche et al. 2021
;
Huusari and Kadri 2021
) induced by operator-valued kernels can efficiently encode dependency between output coordinates
(see
Álvarez et al. 2012
, for an exhaustive review)
. These vRKHSs have similar spectral decomposition
(
Vito et al. 2013
)
and universal approximation properties
(
Carmeli et al. 2010
)
as their real-valued counterpart. Finally, despite the infinite-dimensional nature of most vRKHSs of interest, kernel methods often remain computationally tractable thanks to representer theorems
(
Schölkopf et al. 2001
;
Zhou 2008
)
. However, classical representer theorems only hold for a finite number of evaluations both in the objective and in the constraints. This is one of the points we address through our approach based on finite compact coverings.

Other kernel approaches to deal with pointwise constraints were recently investigated based on kernel sum-of-squares turning the inequalities (
1
) into equalities to nonparametric nonnegative functions, requiring SDP optimization. These approaches either tighten a single non-negativity constraint over the whole space (
\mathscr{K}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
Marteau-Ferey et al. 2020
) or relax a convexity constraint on a compact set
(
Muzellec et al. 2022
, Section 5)
. Our flexible framework can be seen as complementary to the latter since we propose a feasible tightening of constraints on a compact set, and thus achieve a certificate of optimality. We also have significantly fewer assumptions on the constraint set and on the kernel. Indeed, in
Muzellec et al. 2022
the compact set considered is a finite union of Euclidean balls with the same radius and the kernel is essentially of Sobolev type.

In this paper, we thus propose a unified and modular convex optimization framework for kernel machines relying on SOC tightening to encode hard affine SDP constraints on function derivatives. Our framework is suited for a large number of settings and applications owing to the ubiquity of shape constraints. To our best knowledge, this is the first approach with similar properties. Due to the covering-based construction, the method is particularly well-suited to the setting of small to moderate input dimensions, but can face curse-of-dimensionality issues in larger dimensions.

This article extends the results of
Aubin-Frankowski and Szabó 2020
by (i) considering matrix-valued rather than real-valued kernels, (ii) generalizing the shape requirements studied from real-valued to affine SDP constraints, (iii) proposing an adaptive covering scheme and showing its convergence, and (iv) providing applications complementary to the previous focus on joint quantile regression. The present article also encompasses two prior domain-specific applications with
\mathscr{X}=[0,T]
: convoy trajectory reconstruction
(
Aubin-Frankowski et al. 2020
)
and linear quadratic optimal control
(
Aubin-Frankowski 2021
)
.

Problem Formulation

In this section we formulate our problem after recalling the definition of vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (vRKHS).

vRKHS:
A function
K:\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Q\times Q}
is called a matrix-valued kernel on
\mathscr{X}
if

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=K(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})^{\top}
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{X}
and
\sum_{i,j\in[N]}\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}K(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\mathbf{v}_{j}\geq 0
for all

N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}_{n\in[N]}\subset\mathscr{X}
and
\{\mathbf{v}_{n}\}_{n\in[N]}\subset\mathbb{R}^{Q}
. For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{X}
, let
K(\cdot,\mathbf{x})
be the mapping
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{X}\mapsto K\left(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times Q}
. Let
\mathscr{F}_{K}
denote the vRKHS associated to the kernel
K
; we use the shorthand
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{K}:=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathscr{F}_{K}}
and
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{K}:=\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{\mathscr{F}_{K}}
for the norm and the inner product on
\mathscr{F}_{K}
. The Hilbert space
\mathscr{F}_{K}
consists of
\mathscr{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{Q}
functions for which (i)
K(\cdot,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{c}\in\mathscr{F}_{K}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathscr{X}
and

\mathbf{z}_{\text{low}}\in\mathscr{F}_{K}
, then it could be treated as a bias
\mathbf{f}_{0,i}=\mathbf{z}_{\text{low}}
(resp.
\mathbf{z}_{\text{high}}
).
While this would reduce the number of shape constraints from

to
I=2
, our current choice allows us to investigate the efficiency of the proposed approach in a complementary setting. Indeed, in contrast to the considered shape optimization task with one shape constraint (
I=1
) on a large

which is refined by the soap bubble algorithm, the path-planning task involves

constraints on an already refined grid.

6.3
Experiment-3: Estimation of Robotic Arm Position

In our
third experiment
we consider a robotic arm with
N_{S}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
segments moving in a two-dimensional plane for which we want to estimate the “tool tip pose”, i.e. its 2D-position and its 1D-orientation, depending on the length of the links
[L_{i}]_{i\in\left[N_{S}\right]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{S}}
and angle of the joints
[\theta_{i}]_{i\in\left[N_{S}\right]}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{S}}
. This means that the input is
\mathbf{x}=[L_{1};\dots,L_{N_{S}};\theta_{1};\dots;\theta_{N_{S}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=2N_{S}
. We follow the experimental protocol of
Agrell 2019
where the author considered a
4
-dimensional input (
N_{S}=2
) and we also extend it to
6
-dimensional input (
N_{S}=3
). Since
Agrell 2019
focuses on Gaussian processes (a Bayesian estimate), we chose to compare our method against the closer, frequentist and recent, kernel sum-of-squares technique (kSoS;
Muzellec et al. 2022
). In this experiment the underlying “tool tip pose” function—which we are aiming to estimate—takes the form

\mathbf{f}^{ref}(\mathbf{x})=\Bigg[\sum_{i\in\left[N_{S}\right]}L_{i}\underbrace{\cos\Bigg(2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{i}\theta_{j}\Bigg)}_{=:c_{i}^{1}(\mathbf{x})};\sum_{i\in\left[N_{S}\right]}L_{i}\underbrace{\sin\Bigg(2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{i}\theta_{j}\Bigg)}_{=:c_{i}^{2}(\mathbf{x})};\sin\Bigg(2\pi\sum_{j=1}^{N_{S}}\theta_{j}\Bigg)\Bigg]\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(34)

The observations
S=(\mathbf{x}_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n})_{n\in[N]}
(
N=40
) are noisy measurements of the functional relation (
34
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n}=\mathbf{f}^{ref}(\mathbf{x}_{n})+\bm{\epsilon}_{n}

with
(\bm{\epsilon}_{n})_{n\in[N]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}_{3},0.2^{2}\mathbf{I}_{3}\right)
, and inputs
\mathbf{x}_{n}
generated according to Latin hypercube sampling of
\mathscr{X}=[0,1]^{d}
. As the output values are clearly not independent, we approximate the
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{y}
relation using a vRKHS associated to a decomposable matrix-valued kernel
K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
, where
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
is the covariance matrix of the outputs of (
34
) estimated over
1000
samples. The kernel
k
was chosen to be the Gaussian:

\displaystyle k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=e^{-(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}\diag\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)_{i\in[d]}\right)(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}.

The objective function is a regularized empirical mean square error

\mathcal{L}_{S}(\mathbf{f})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[N]}\|\mathbf{y}_{n}-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{n})\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{f}\|_{K}^{2}.

(35)

Following
Agrell 2019
, we add some extra side information, assuming we know whether or not the arm will move further away from the x-axis or y-axis when changing the link lengths, given any joint configuration. The considered constraints are thus

\displaystyle(\partial_{i}f^{ref}_{l}(\mathbf{x}))_{l}(\partial_{i}f_{l}(\mathbf{x}))_{l}\geq 0\text{ for $l\in[2]$, $i\in\left[N_{S}\right]$, }

expressing that the estimate and true derivatives point in the same direction component-wise. By (
34
), the linearity w.r.t.
L_{i}
entails that
\partial_{i}f^{ref}_{l}(\mathbf{x})=c_{i}^{l}(\mathbf{x})
. Consequently we consider five constraints: the original one (
36
); its relaxation through discretization (
37
); two SOC tightenings, obtained through a ball covering (

), and ball and hyperplanes (
39
), with notations consistent with those of
Section

3.1
and
Section

6.1
; and finally a kSoS approximation (
40
) as per
Muzellec et al. 2022
with an extra positive semidefinite matrix-valued variable
\mathbf{A}=[a_{m_{1},m_{2}}]_{m_{1},m_{2}\in[M]}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
. These constraints are as follows:

\displaystyle c_{i}^{l}(\mathbf{x})\partial_{i}f_{l}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\geq 0,\,

\displaystyle\forall\,x\in\mathscr{X},

(36)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})\partial_{i}f_{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})

\displaystyle\geq 0,\,

\displaystyle\forall\,m\in[M],

(37)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})\partial_{i}f_{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{i}\|\mathbf{f}\|_{K},\,

\displaystyle\forall\,m\in[M],

(38)

\displaystyle\exists\,\xi_{m}\geq 0,\,\,\xi_{m}\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{l}

\displaystyle\geq r_{i}^{l}\|\mathbf{f}-\xi_{m}\partial_{i,2}K(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{m})\mathbf{e}_{l}\|_{K},\,

\displaystyle\forall\,m\in[M],

(39)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})\partial_{i}f_{l}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}\in[M]}a_{m_{1},m_{2}}k_{SoS}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m_{1}})k_{SoS}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m_{2}}),\,

\displaystyle\forall\,m\in[M].

(40)

We consider anchor points
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Our

Conclusions

In this paper we focused on the problem of incorporating hard affine SDP shape constraints on function derivatives into optimization problems over vector-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. We proposed a unified and modular second-order cone (SOC) based convex optimization framework to tackle this task. We designed and analysed two complementary approaches to derive SOC-based tightenings; they build upon a convex separation theorem in RKHSs (Theorem
2
) and on an upper bound of the modulus of continuity (Theorem
3
). We established the existence and certificate of optimality of the tightenings (Theorem
5
) alongside with a convergence guarantee (Proposition
7
) in terms of the refinement of the underlying covering. In addition, we proposed the soap bubble algorithm which guarantees hard shape constraints while adaptively refining the covering, and proved its convergence (Theorem
9
). The efficiency of the approach was demonstrated in four applications (Section
6
): in the context of shape optimization, safety-critical control, robotics and econometrics.

Lemma 10

Lemma 11

(Infimum over half-spaces)
.

Let
\mathscr{F}
be a Hilbert space,
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{v}\in\mathscr{F}

\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}

(Lemma
11

•

\xi\geq 0

\displaystyle-\infty

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{<}}\hskip-4.26773pt\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in H^{-}_{\mathscr{F}}(\mathbf{v},\rho)}\left<\mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\right>_{\mathscr{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\hskip-2.84544pt\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left<-\xi\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u},\frac{\rho}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}}\mathbf{v}-\tau\mathbf{v}\right>_{\mathscr{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}-\xi\rho+\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\xi\tau\|\mathbf{v}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{F}},

•

\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle-\infty

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{<}}\hskip-5.40608pt\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in H^{-}_{\mathscr{F}}(\mathbf{v},\rho)}\left<\mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\right>_{\mathscr{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\hskip-2.84544pt\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left<-\xi\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u},\frac{\rho}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathscr{F}}^{2}}\mathbf{v}-\tau\mathbf{u}\right>_{\mathscr{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\hskip-2.84544pt-\xi\rho+\inf_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}-\tau\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{F}}.

\xi\geq 0

\mathbf{w}\in H^{-}_{\mathscr{F}}(\mathbf{v},0)
