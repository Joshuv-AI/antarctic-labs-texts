Krylov Subspace Methods for Quantum Dynamics with Time-Dependent Generators
Kazutaka Takahashi, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
Krylov subspace methods in quantum dynamics identify the minimal subspace in which a process unfolds. To date, their use is restricted to time evolutions governed by time-independent generators. We introduce a generalization valid for driven quantum systems governed by a time-dependent Hamiltonian that maps the evolution to a diffusion problem in a one-dimensional lattice with nearest-neighbor hopping probabilities that are inhomogeneous and time dependent. This representation is used to establish a novel class of fundamental limits to the quantum speed of evolution and operator growth. We also discuss generalizations of the algorithm, adapted to discretized time evolutions and periodic Hamiltonians, with applications to many-body systems.

Krylov construction for time-dependent generators
—We treat
the Schrödinger equation,

i\partial_{t}|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat{H}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle
,
governing the time evolution of the state vector

|\psi(t)\rangle
under the time-dependent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
.
When the latter is time-independent, the state space is spanned by

\{\hat{H}^{n}|\psi(0)\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
,
known as the Krylov space.
An orthonormal basis can be constructed by the standard Gram–Schmidt procedure.
This procedure brings the Hamiltonian to a tridiagonal form.

An efficient way to explore the time-dependent case can be found from the so-called

(t,t^{\prime})
formalism
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
.
We introduce two different times,
s
and
t
, to define

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=e^{-is(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|\phi(t)\rangle,

(1)

where
|\phi(t)\rangle
is an arbitrary vector with the constraint

|\phi(0)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
.
The actual time-evolved state is obtained as
|\psi(t)\rangle=|\psi(t,t)\rangle
.
The operator
\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}
is sometimes used
in the context of Floquet theory
[
38
,
39
]
.
This representation motivates us to use the space

\{(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})^{n}|\phi(t)\rangle\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
If we consider extending the Hilbert space to a larger one,

\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}
is treated as a static
Hamiltonian
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
40
,
41
]
.
Here, we keep the space unchanged and
the
(t,t^{\prime})
formalism is not exploited in the following discussions.

For a normalized basis
|K_{0}(t)\rangle
with
|K_{0}(0)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
,
we produce a series of time-dependent basis vectors by

\displaystyle|K_{k+1}(t)\rangle b_{k+1}(t)=(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})|K_{k}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle-|K_{k}(t)\rangle a_{k}(t)-|K_{k-1}(t)\rangle b_{k}(t),

(2)

for
k=0,1,\dots,d-2
with
|K_{-1}(t)\rangle b_{0}(t)=0
.
Each Lanczos coefficient is time dependent and is determined so that the orthonormal relation

\langle K_{m}(t)|K_{n}(t)\rangle=\delta_{m,n}
holds.
We obtain

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=\langle K_{k}(t)|(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})|K_{k}(t)\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\langle K_{k-1}(t)|(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})|K_{k}(t)\rangle.

(4)

Here, each of
\{a_{k}(t)\}_{k=0}^{d-1}

is real and the phase of
|K_{k}(t)\rangle

is chosen so that each of
\{b_{k}(t)\}_{k=1}^{d-1}
becomes non-negative.
The recurrence process halts when the possible basis vectors are exhausted,
and the Krylov dimension
d
is defined as the total number of the basis vectors.
The state vector is represented in the Krylov basis by the coherent quantum superposition

|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\varphi_{k}(t)
.
The transformed state

|\varphi(t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k\rangle\varphi_{k}(t)=(\varphi_{0}(t),\varphi_{1}(t),\dots,\varphi_{d-1}(t))^{\mathrm{T}}
,
satisfies
i\partial_{t}|\varphi(t)\rangle=\hat{\cal L}(t)|\varphi(t)\rangle

with the initial condition
|\varphi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle
.
The generator
\hat{\cal L}(t)
is written in a tridiagonal form as

\hat{\cal L}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}a_{k}(t)|k\rangle\langle k|+\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}b_{k}(t)(|k\rangle\langle k-1|+|k-1\rangle\langle k|)
.
Thus, the time evolution of the state can be described in terms of
a single-particle hopping in a finite or semi-infinite chain,
as in the time-independent case
[
2
,
3
]
.
The diagonal element
a_{k}(t)
represents the on-site potential at site
k
and
the off diagonal
b_{k}(t)
represents the hopping amplitude between
k-1
and
k
.

The Krylov algorithm for the state
involves diagonal components of the tridiagonal matrix.
They can be eliminated by the phase transformation

|\tilde{K}_{k}(t)\rangle=|K_{k}(t)\rangle e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}ds\,a_{k}(s)}
.
Then, the tridiagonal matrix has complex elements

\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\tilde{b}_{k}^{\phantom{*}}(t)|k\rangle\langle k-1|+\tilde{b}_{k}^{*}(t)|k-1\rangle\langle k|)

with
\tilde{b}_{k}(t)=b_{k}(t)e^{i\int_{0}^{t}ds\,[a_{k}(s)-a_{k-1}(s)]}
.
A different possibility is to use the density operator

\hat{\rho}(t)=|\psi(t)\rangle\langle\psi(t)|

[
37
]
.

The Krylov expansion is dependent on the choice of the initial basis.
As in the time-independent case,
it is natural to take
|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
.
The first component
\varphi_{0}(t)=\langle\psi(0)|\psi(t)\rangle
gives the survival amplitude.
An alternative choice for the time-dependent case is
the instantaneous ground state of the Hamiltonian,
|\epsilon_{0}(t)\rangle
,
under the condition that the initial state is in the ground state.
Then,
|\varphi_{0}(t)|^{2}=|\langle\epsilon_{0}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle|^{2}
represents
the fidelity measuring how good the adiabatic approximation is.
The introduction of the time-dependent basis leads to the notion of parallel transport
in Krylov space, associated with the generator
\hat{A}(t)
satisfying

i\partial_{t}|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\hat{A}(t)|K_{k}(t)\rangle
.
Our framework is then equivalent to the standard Krylov algorithm for

\hat{H}(t)-\hat{A}(t)
defined locally in
t
.
The Hamiltonian is decomposed into two parts, which is reminiscent of shortcuts to
adiabaticity
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]

where the counterdiabatic term, also known as adiabatic gauge potential,
is used as a gauge field for parallel transport.

Quantum speed and propagation limits in Krylov space
—A key advantage of
the Krylov expansion is that, in any system,
it renders the state evolution as a propagation in a one-dimensional space.
The degree of the propagation can be measured by
the spread complexity
[
4
,
8
]

\displaystyle K(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}k|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}.

(5)

It can be regarded as the expectation value of
the Krylov operator
\hat{\cal K}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}k|k\rangle\langle k|
.
At early times of evolution,
K(t)
increases from the initial value
K(0)=0
,
and the growth rate is characterized by the Lanczos coefficients.
We can use the Robertson uncertainty relation to obtain
the dispersion bound
[
9
]
as

|\partial_{t}K(t)|\leq 2\Delta L(t)\Delta K(t)

where
\Delta L(t)=[\langle\varphi(t)|\hat{\cal L}^{2}(t)|\varphi(t)\rangle-\langle\varphi(t)|\hat{\cal L}(t)|\varphi(t)\rangle^{2}]^{1/2}

is the variance of
\hat{\cal L}(t)
with respect to
|\varphi(t)\rangle
,
and
\Delta K(t)
is similarly defined for
\hat{\cal K}
.
In contrast to the time-independent case
[
9
]
,

\Delta L(t)
is not equal to
b_{1}
.

Since we can define the Krylov operator, we can derive the operator
quantum speed limit for the Heisenberg operator
[
42
]
.
Here, we are interested in a speed limit for
the time-evolved state rather than for the operator.
In the standard formulation, one considers as a distance
the Fubini–Study angle
\Theta_{\mathrm{FS}}(t)=\arccos|\langle\psi(0)|\psi(t)\rangle|

[
43
]
.
The minimum time for sweeping
\Theta_{\mathrm{FS}}(t)
is then lower bounded by
the time-average energy variance
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
.
To characterize the spreading, we generalize the Fubini–Study angle as

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}(t)=\mathrm{arccos}\,\sqrt{P_{n}(t)},

(6)

where
n=0,1,\dots,d-1
and
P_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}
is the probability
for the time-evolved state to remain within the first
n+1
sites of the Krylov chain.
We have generally

0=\Theta_{d-1}(t)\leq\Theta_{d-2}(t)\leq\dots\leq\Theta_{0}(t)=\Theta_{\mathrm{FS}}(t)\leq\pi/2
.
The spread complexity is written as
K(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\sin^{2}\Theta_{k}(t)
.

By using similar techniques to those in the derivation of the celebrated Mandelstam–Tamm
time-energy uncertainty relation
[
43
,
47
]
,
we can derive upper bounds to
\Theta_{n}(t)
.
The time derivative of
\Theta_{n}=\Theta_{n}(t)
leads to

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}\Theta_{n}|\leq b_{n+1}(t)\sqrt{1-\frac{\sin^{2}\Theta_{n+1}}{\sin^{2}\Theta_{n}}}\sqrt{1-\frac{\cos^{2}\Theta_{n-1}}{\cos^{2}\Theta_{n}}},

(7)

where
n=0,1,\dots,d-2
and
\cos^{2}\Theta_{-1}=0
at
n=0

[
37
]
.
The equality holds when the even components
\varphi_{0}(t),\varphi_{2}(t),\dots

are real and the odd components
\varphi_{1}(t),\varphi_{3}(t),\dots
are purely imaginary.
This is achieved when
\hat{\cal L}(t)
has sublattice symmetry, that is,
the diagonal components of
\hat{\cal L}(t)
are equal to zero.
In the Krylov lattice, the condition
a_{k}(t)=0
indicates a vanishing on-site potential.
Then, no potential disturbs the wave function, and its spreading is maximized.

We can further simplify the right-hand side of Eq. (
7
) by bounding it from above as

|\partial_{t}\Theta_{n}(t)|\leq b_{n+1}(t)
.
This is a generalization of the Mandelstam–Tamm inequality
for the nonescape probability
P_{n}(t)
.
Setting
n=0
gives the Mandelstam–Tamm inequality
for the survival probability
|\langle K_{0}(t)|\psi(t)\rangle|^{2}

adapted to any reference states
[
48
]
.
If we choose
|K_{0}(t)\rangle
properly,
the upper bound
b_{1}(t)
represents the energy variance with respect to
|K_{0}(t)\rangle
.

In the general case with
n\neq 0
,
the bound cannot be tight except in the special case
of a localized state
|\varphi_{k}(t)|\sim\delta_{k,n}
.
By using
\sin\Theta_{n+1}(t)\geq 0
and
\cos\Theta_{n}(t)\leq 1
,
we obtain tighter inequalities

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}\Theta_{n}(t)|\leq b_{n+1}(t)\sin\Theta_{n-1}(t)\leq b_{n+1}(t)\Theta_{n-1}(t).

(8)

The last inequality can be iterated to obtain

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}ds\,b_{n+1}(s)\int_{0}^{s}ds_{n}\,b_{n}(s_{n})\dots\int_{0}^{s_{2}}ds_{1}b_{1}(s_{1}).

(9)

When
b_{k}(t)
takes a form
b_{k}(t)=b(t)c_{k}
, we can write

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}(t)\leq\exp\left[-(n+1)\ln\left(\frac{n+1}{v_{n+1}\tau(t)}\right)\right],

(10)

for
n\gg 1
.
Here,
v_{n+1}=(c_{1}c_{2}\dots c_{n+1})^{1/(n+1)}
represents the speed of propagation
and
\tau(t)=\int_{0}^{t}ds\,b(s)
represents an effective time scale.
The relation
v_{n}\tau(t)\sim n
sets the scale of the propagation limit,
which is reminiscent of the Lieb–Robinson bound
[
49
,
50
]
.
The derived inequalities are also applicable to systems
with time-independent Hamiltonians.

Examples with closed complexity algebra
—When the Hamiltonian has
a tridiagonal form in a natural basis, and the initial state is
chosen properly, the original basis is equivalent to the Krylov basis up to a phase.
Paradigmatic examples are given by the following three systems
[
37
]
:
(i) single spin
\hat{H}(t)=\bm{h}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}
,
(ii) harmonic oscillator with translation

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}[\hat{x}-x_{0}(t)]^{2}
,
(iii) harmonic oscillator with dilation

\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m}{2}\omega^{2}(t)\hat{x}^{2}
.
Also, when we choose the instantaneous ground state as the initial basis,
each of the Krylov basis elements is given by an instantaneous energy eigenstate.
This is due to the property that the time-derivative operator
in the instantaneous eigenstate basis is written in a tridiagonal form
[
37
]
.
The time derivative of the eigenstate is closely related to the counterdiabatic
term
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
Generally, when the initial Krylov basis is chosen as an instantaneous eigenstate

|\epsilon_{n}(t)\rangle
,
the first Lanczos coefficient
b_{1}(t)
is given by the variance of the counterdiabatic term

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=i\sum_{n}(1-|\epsilon_{n}(t)\rangle\langle\epsilon_{n}(t)|)|\partial_{t}\epsilon_{n}(t)\rangle\langle\epsilon_{n}(t)|
.

Remarkably, in each case (i)–(iii),
the diagonal Lanczos coefficient
a_{k}(t)
is linear in
k
and
the off diagonal part
b_{k}(t)
takes a form
b_{k}(t)=b(t)c_{k}
,
where the time-independent part
c_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle c_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt{k(d-k)}&\mbox{for (i)},\\
\sqrt{k}&\mbox{for (ii)},\\
\sqrt{2k(2k-1)}&\mbox{for (iii)}.\end{array}\right.

The linear form of
a_{k}(t)
reflects the property that
these systems have constant eigenvalue spacing.
The time scale of the propagation limit determined from Eq. (
10
) is

\tau=O(n^{1/2})
for (i) at
n\ll d
and (ii), and
\tau=O(n^{0})

for (i) at
n\sim d
and for (iii)
[
37
]
.

In the time-independent case, the forms of
c_{k}
have been discussed as examples that give
closed algebra of the Krylov operator
\hat{\cal K}

[
7
,
9
]
.
When we consider the phase-transformed basis
|\tilde{K}_{k}(t)\rangle
,
we have
[\hat{\cal K},\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)]=i\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)
and

[\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t),\hat{\cal K}]=i\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)
,
where
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)=-i\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(\tilde{b}_{k}^{\phantom{*}}(t)|k\rangle\langle k-1|-\tilde{b}_{k}^{*}(t)|k-1\rangle\langle k|)

is interpreted as the current operator.
The commutator of
\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)
and
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)
generally gives
a diagonal matrix.
The algebra is closed for the above examples.
In each case, we find a form

[\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t),\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)]=-i(\alpha(t)\hat{\cal K}+\gamma(t))
.
We have
\alpha\leq 0
for (i),
\alpha=0
for (ii), and
\alpha\geq 0
for (iii).

In the time-independent case of these systems, the Heisenberg representation of
\hat{\cal K}
is
spanned by the identity operator,
\hat{\cal K}
, and
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}
, and
leads to the saturation of the operator quantum speed,
maximizing the operator growth rate limit
[
42
]
.
For the time-dependent case, the evolving state is extended to a higher dimensional space
and no saturation occurs
[
37
]
.

Discrete time evolution and the Arnoldi iteration
—Discrete-time evolution
arises in unitary quantum circuits.
At the
k
th step, the quantum state
|\psi^{k}\rangle
is propagated by the action of
the unitary
\hat{U}^{k}
, as
|\psi^{k+1}\rangle=\hat{U}^{k}|\psi^{k}\rangle
.
For non-Hermitian matrices, the Krylov basis set is constructed by
the Arnoldi iteration procedure
[
3
]
.
Its applications range from open quantum systems
[
19
]
to
periodic systems
[
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
]
, and
unitary circuit dynamics
[
56
]
.
The algorithm is adapted to the time-dependent case as

\displaystyle|K_{n+1}^{k+1}\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{n}^{k}|^{2}}=\hat{U}^{k}|K_{n}^{k}\rangle-|f_{n}^{k+1}\rangle z_{n}^{k},

(15)

\displaystyle|f_{n}^{k+1}\rangle=-|f_{n-1}^{k+1}\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{n-1}^{k}|^{2}}+|K_{n}^{k+1}\rangle(z_{n-1}^{k})^{*},

(16)

with
n=0,1,\dots,d-2
and
z_{-1}^{k}=1
at
n=0
.
The
n
th order Krylov basis
|K_{n}^{k}\rangle
is defined at
n\leq k

and satisfies the orthonormal condition at each
k
.
We also use an auxiliary vector
|f_{n}^{k}\rangle
and
a complex number
z_{n}^{k}
with
|z_{n}^{k}|\leq 1
.
The operation
\hat{U}^{k}|K_{n}^{k}\rangle
produces
the one-step forwarded basis
|K_{n+1}^{k+1}\rangle
, instead of
|K_{n+1}^{k}\rangle
.
By expanding the time-evolved state as

|\psi^{k}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\min(k,d-1)}|K_{n}^{k}\rangle\langle n|\varphi^{k}\rangle
,
we obtain the form
|\varphi^{k+1}\rangle=\hat{\cal U}^{k}|\varphi^{k}\rangle

with
|\varphi^{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
.
The matrix
\hat{\cal U}^{k}
takes an upper Hessenberg form.
Each component, Arnoldi coefficient, is written by using
z_{n}^{k}

[
37
]
.

Periodically-driven systems
—When the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
has
a period
T=2\pi/\Omega
,
we can apply the Floquet theorem
[
30
,
31
]
.
The Hilbert space is extended to the Floquet–Hilbert, or Sambe
space
[
58
]
, which changes the definition of the inner product to
include the time average over the period
[
37
]
.
The Krylov algorithm in Eq. (
2
) is applied to give

|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\langle k|e^{-is\hat{\cal L}}|0\rangle
.
In this case, the tridiagonal matrix
\hat{\cal L}
is time independent.
The basis vectors are not orthogonal with each other in the usual sense,
and the Krylov dimension is infinite even for systems in finite Hilbert space.
The time evolution by
\hat{\cal L}
corresponds to that by the Floquet Hamiltonian,
and that of the Krylov basis represents the micromotion.

We treat as an example the Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick model
[
59
]
.
The Krylov analysis for the time-independent case has been studied
for operators
[
60
]
as well as for states
[
61
,
62
]
.
For the spin operator with
S=N/2
, we treat the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=-\frac{2J}{N}(\hat{S}^{z})^{2}-2h\hat{S}^{x}\sin\Omega t,

(18)

with the initial condition
\hat{S}^{z}|\psi(0)\rangle=\frac{N}{2}|\psi(0)\rangle
.
We focus on a slow driving regime
\Omega\ll J
where
the standard Floquet picture is not applicable
[
63
]
.
The static properties at
N\to\infty
are described by the fixed-point analysis.
For
h<J
,
|\psi(0)\rangle
corresponds to the stable ferromagnetic point.
It turns into an unstable point for
h>J
, and a stable paramagnetic point appears.

In Fig.
3
, we show the Lanczos coefficients.
When the Krylov step
k
is smaller than
N/2
, the half of the Hilbert space dimension,
the Lanczos coefficients are close to those at the limit
\Omega\to 0
.
To observe how the spreading occurs in the Krylov space,
we numerically calculate
the population in the
z
-spin eigenstates
|\mu\rangle
with

\hat{S}^{z}|\mu\rangle=\left(\frac{N}{2}-\mu\right)|\mu\rangle

as
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,|\langle\mu|K_{k}(t)\rangle|^{2}
,
and the population in each Fourier component

\left|\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,e^{im\Omega t}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\right|^{2}
, respectively.
For
k<N/2
, the spreading is in the direction of the Hilbert space
and the spreading in the Fourier space is suppressed.
When the step reaches the point
k=N/2
,

b_{k}
at
\Omega\to 0
decreases,
which enhances the spreading in the Fourier space.
We find that
a_{k}
is almost constant and
b_{k}
shows a slow linear growth.
We also find a qualitative difference between the results at
h<J
and those at
h>J
.
For
h>J
and
k<N/2
, the Lanczos coefficients rapidly converge to the static limit
and are insensitive to the specific values of
h
.

Summary
—We have generalized the Krylov subspace
method for time evolutions of quantum states involving a time-dependent generator. Using it, the quantum state evolution is mapped to a one-dimensional hopping problem, as in the time-independent case. This description motivates generalized distance measures of spreading in Krylov space, for which we report universal bounds that are naturally related to quantum speed limits and Lieb–Robinson bounds.
We show how our method can be flexibly adapted to
treat discrete-time evolution and periodic driving,
making it possible to analyze the many-body dynamics and quantum complexity of driven and controlled quantum processes. Our formulation for nonautonomous differential equations is rather general, and beyond quantum science,
it should find broad applications
in nonequilibrium phenomena, dynamical systems, and various fields of mathematics.

Supplemental Material

I
(t,t^{\prime})
formalism and Krylov method

In the
(t,t^{\prime})
formalism, we define a two-time dependent state as in Eq. (
1
).
The operator
\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}
is written by the unitary time-evolution operator

\hat{U}(t)
for the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}=-\hat{U}(t)i\partial_{t}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t).

(S1)

Then, we can write for
|\phi(t)\rangle
with

|\phi(0)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
as

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=e^{-is(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|\phi(t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)e^{-s\partial_{t}}\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)|\phi(t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t-s)|\phi(t-s)\rangle.

(S2)

Setting
s=t
, we find
|\psi(t,t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle=|\psi(t)\rangle
.

A different way to show that the operator
e^{-is(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})}
is
equivalent to the time evolution operator is to use the Lie–Trotter product formula as

\displaystyle e^{-is(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\left(e^{-i\frac{s}{M}\hat{H}(t)}e^{-\frac{s}{M}\partial_{t}}\right)^{M}

(S3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}e^{-i\frac{s}{M}\hat{H}(t)}e^{-i\frac{s}{M}\hat{H}(t-s/M)}e^{-i\frac{s}{M}\hat{H}(t-2s/M)}\cdots e^{-i\frac{s}{M}\hat{H}(t-(M-1)s/M)}e^{-s\partial_{t}}.

The last expression at
s=t
takes the form of the time-ordered product,
except the last factor.
The last factor brings
|\phi(t)\rangle
back to
|\phi(0)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
.
Thus, we can find the equivalence.

The two-time dependent state
|\psi(s,t)\rangle
is more complicated than
the original state
|\psi(t)\rangle
.
This can be understood by using an orthonormal complete set
\{|n\rangle\}_{n=0,1,\dots}
as

\displaystyle e^{-is(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|0\rangle=\hat{U}(t)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t-s)|0\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}|n(t)\rangle\langle n(t-s)|0\rangle,

(S4)

where
|n(t)\rangle=\hat{U}(t)|n\rangle
.
This state generally contains contributions from all of
n=0,1,\dots

and is simplified to
|0(t)\rangle
at
s=t
.

The time-evolution law of
|\psi(s,t)\rangle
is written as

\displaystyle i\partial_{s}|\psi(s,t)\rangle=(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})|\psi(s,t)\rangle.

(S5)

This equation can be solved if we can find the eigenstates and
eigenvalues of
\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}
.
It is a formidable task and we can apply the Krylov algorithm to tridiagonalize the operator.
By using the Krylov basis introduced in Eq. (
2
), we define

\displaystyle\hat{F}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\langle k|=(|K_{0}(t)\rangle,|K_{1}(t)\rangle,\dots,|K_{d-1}(t)\rangle).

(S6)

When the dimension of the Hilbert space is denoted by
N
,

\hat{F}(t)
is a
N\times d
matrix and satisfies

\hat{F}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{F}(t)=\hat{I}_{d}
, where
\hat{I}_{d}
is
the identity operator in the
d
dimensional space.
We note that
\hat{F}(t)\hat{F}^{\dagger}(t)
is not necessarily equal to
the identity operator in the Hilbert space.
Then, the definition of the basis vector leads to the relation

\displaystyle(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})\hat{F}(t)=\hat{F}(t)(\hat{\cal L}(t)-i\partial_{t}).

(S7)

Expanding the state in the Krylov basis is equivalent to the transformation

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\hat{F}(t)|\varphi(s,t)\rangle.

(S8)

The transformed vector
|\varphi(s,t)\rangle
has the size of the Krylov dimension.
It satisfies

\displaystyle i\partial_{s}|\varphi(s,t)\rangle=(\hat{\cal L}(t)-i\partial_{t})|\varphi(s,t)\rangle,

(S9)

with the initial condition
|\varphi(0,t)\rangle=|0\rangle
.
The two-time state is written as

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\hat{F}(t)e^{-is(\hat{\cal L}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|0\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\langle k|e^{-is(\hat{\cal L}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|0\rangle.

(S10)

One of the prominent features of the Krylov method is that
some physical quantities are described only by the Lanczos coefficients.
A typical example that is known for the time-independent Hamiltonian
is the survival amplitude
[
4
]
.
This property still holds in the time-dependent case, as we see from the relation

\langle K_{0}(t)|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\langle 0|e^{-is(\hat{\cal L}(t)-i\partial_{t})}|0\rangle
.
The right-hand side is represented by the Lanczos coefficients and their derivatives.
We can find differential equations for the Lanczos coefficients
if we know the overlap
\langle K_{0}(t)|\psi(s,t)\rangle
.

II
Krylov method for the density operator

In the standard applications of the Krylov subspace method to quantum dynamics,
it is common to resort to the Heisenberg picture and
describe the time evolution of an operator rather,
as opposed to describing the time evolution of the quantum state.
The Heisenberg representation of a quantum operator
\hat{X}

evolving under a time-independent Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is given by

e^{i\hat{H}t}\hat{X}e^{-i\hat{H}t}
.
In the time-dependent case, we cannot apply the same method
as the time-evolution operator requires time-ordering
given the noncommutative property
[\hat{H}(t),\hat{H}(t^{\prime})]\neq 0
.

However, treating the density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
is still possible.
The density operator satisfies the von Neumann equation,

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}(t)=[\hat{H}(t),\hat{\rho}(t)].

(S11)

We can use the recurrence relation

\displaystyle b_{k+1}(t)\hat{O}_{k+1}(t)=(\hat{\cal L}_{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})\hat{O}_{k}(t)-b_{k}(t)\hat{O}_{k-1}(t),

(S12)

where
\hat{\cal L}_{H}(t)(\cdot)=[\hat{H}(t),(\cdot)]
.
The Lanczos coefficients are given by

b_{k}(t)=(\hat{O}_{k-1}(t),(\hat{\cal L}_{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})\hat{O}_{k}(t))

with a properly-defined inner product
(\hat{A},\hat{B})
for an arbitrary set of
operators
\hat{A}
and
\hat{B}
.

When the density operator is expanded as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\varphi_{k}(t)\hat{O}_{k}(t),

(S13)

the vector
|\varphi(t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k\rangle\varphi_{k}(t)

satisfies
i\partial_{t}|\varphi(t)\rangle=\hat{\cal L}(t)|\varphi(t)\rangle
with

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}b_{k}(t)(|k\rangle\langle k-1|+|k-1\rangle\langle k|)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}0&b_{1}(t)&0&&&\\
b_{1}(t)&0&b_{2}(t)&&&\\
0&b_{2}(t)&0&&&\\
&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&0&b_{d-1}(t)\\
&&&&b_{d-1}(t)&0\end{array}\right).

The diagonal components are absent in the present case, and the coefficient

b_{k}(t)
is different from the corresponding one
b_{k}(t)
for the state
|\psi(t)\rangle
.
We further note that
\hat{O}_{k}(t)
is neither Hermitian nor positive definite.
Since the density operator satisfies the normalization condition

\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\rho}(t)=1
, we have several additional conditions, such as

\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\varphi_{k}(t)\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{O}_{k}(t)=1
.

III
Krylov speed limit inequality

To derive Eq. (
7
), we start by evaluating the time derivative of the angle
defined in Eq. (
6
), which yields the identity relation

\displaystyle-2\partial_{t}\Theta_{n}(t)\sin\Theta_{n}(t)\cos\Theta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\langle\varphi(t)|k\rangle\langle k|\hat{\cal L}(t)|\varphi(t)\rangle-\langle\varphi(t)|\hat{\cal L}(t)|k\rangle\langle k|\varphi(t)\rangle\right)

(S21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ib_{n+1}(t)\left(\langle\varphi(t)|n\rangle\langle n+1|\varphi(t)\rangle-\langle\varphi(t)|n+1\rangle\langle n|\varphi(t)\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2b_{n+1}(t)\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi_{n}(t)\varphi_{n+1}(t),

and the inequality

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}\Theta_{n}(t)|\sin\Theta_{n}(t)\cos\Theta_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle b_{n+1}(t)|\varphi_{n}(t)||\varphi_{n+1}(t)|

(S22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{n+1}(t)\sqrt{\sin^{2}\Theta_{n}(t)-\sin^{2}\Theta_{n+1}(t)}\sqrt{\cos^{2}\Theta_{n}(t)-\cos^{2}\Theta_{n-1}(t)}.

The equality holds when
\varphi_{n}(t)\varphi_{n+1}(t)
is pure imaginary.
This condition is satisfied when
a_{k}(t)=0
.
In that case, the equation for
\varphi_{n}(t)
reads

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\varphi_{n}(t)=b_{n}(t)\varphi_{n-1}(t)+b_{n+1}(t)\varphi_{n+1}(t).

(S23)

This equation can be solved with the initial condition

\varphi_{n}(0)=\delta_{n,0}
. The even components
\varphi_{0}(t),\varphi_{2}(t),\dots
are real
and the odd components
\varphi_{1}(t),\varphi_{3}(t),\dots
are pure imaginary.
Thus, we obtain Eq. (
7
).

IV
Solvable examples

IV.1
Single spin

We treat the single spin system, generally written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=h(t)\bm{n}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}.

(S24)

\hat{\bm{S}}=(\hat{S}^{x},\hat{S}^{y},\hat{S}^{z})

represents the spin operator with
\hat{\bm{S}}^{2}=S(S+1)
.
The applied external field is characterized by the absolute value

h(t)
and the direction

\bm{n}(t)=(\sin\theta(t)\cos\varphi(t),\sin\theta(t)\sin\varphi(t),\cos\theta(t))
.
The Hilbert space is generally spanned by the eigenstates of
\hat{S}^{z}
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{z}|m\rangle=m|m\rangle,

(S25)

with
m=-S,-S+1,\dots,S
.
We set
\hat{H}(0)=h(0)\hat{S}^{z}
and the initial state as
|\psi(0)\rangle=|S\rangle
.

IV.1.1
Fixed
z
-basis

In the fixed
z
-basis, the Hamiltonian is represented
in a tridiagonal form, which means that we can naturally obtain a Krylov basis.
When the initial basis is set as
|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|S\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t})|K_{0}(t)\rangle=\hat{H}(t)|S\rangle=Sh(t)\cos\theta(t)|S\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2S}h(t)\sin\theta(t)e^{i\varphi(t)}|S-1\rangle.

(S26)

This gives

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)=Sh(t)\cos\theta(t),

(S27)

\displaystyle b_{1}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{2S}}{2}h(t)\sin\theta(t),

(S28)

\displaystyle|K_{1}(t)\rangle=e^{i\varphi(t)}|S-1\rangle.

(S29)

In a similar way, we can calculate the higher-order contributions.
The point in the present example is that
the Hamiltonian is written in terms of three kinds of operators and
we can generally write its action in a given state
|m\rangle
as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)|m\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h\left[S^{z}\cos\theta+\frac{1}{2}\left(S^{+}e^{-i\varphi}+S^{-}e^{i\varphi}\right)\right]|m\rangle

(S30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle hm\cos\theta|m\rangle+\frac{h}{2}e^{-i\varphi}\sqrt{(S-m)(S+m+1)}|m+1\rangle+\frac{h}{2}e^{i\varphi}\sqrt{(S+m)(S-m+1)}|m-1\rangle.

The state vector
|m\rangle
is time-independent, and taking the time derivative
to the state vector does not give any contribution.
As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=e^{ik\varphi(t)}|S-k\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots,2S),

(S31)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=(S-k)h(t)\cos\theta(t)+k\dot{\varphi}(t)\qquad(k=0,1,\dots,2S),

(S32)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{2}h(t)\sin\theta(t)\sqrt{k(d-k)}\qquad(k=1,\dots,2S),

(S33)

where the dot denotes the time derivative.
The Krylov dimension is equal to the Hilbert space dimension, i.e.,
d=2S+1
.

IV.1.2
Instantaneous eigenstate basis

The instantaneous eigenstates of the Hamiltonian are
given by the eigenstates of rotated-
\hat{S}^{z}
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{R}(t)\hat{S}^{z}\hat{R}^{\dagger}(t)|m(t)\rangle=m|m(t)\rangle,

(S34)

with
m=-S,-S+1,\dots,S
.
The rotation operator
\hat{R}(t)
is written as

\displaystyle\hat{R}(t)=\exp\left(-i\theta(t)\hat{\bm{S}}\cdot\bm{e}_{\varphi}(t)\right),

(S35)

with
\bm{e}_{\varphi}(t)=(-\sin\varphi(t),\cos\varphi(t),0)
.

We set the initial Krylov basis as

\displaystyle|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|S(t)\rangle.

(S36)

We apply
\hat{H}(t)-i\partial_{t}
to this basis.
The action of the Hamiltonian only gives an eigenvalue, and
we need to consider the time derivative of the instantaneous eigenstates.
The time derivative is closely related to the counterdiabatic
term
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
28
,
29
]
.
Generally, for possible instantaneous eigenstates
|m(t)\rangle
, we can write

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}|m(t)\rangle=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)|m(t)\rangle+|m(t)\rangle i\langle m(t)|\dot{m}(t)\rangle.

(S37)

In the present case, the counterdiabatic term
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)
is given by
[
25
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=\bm{n}(t)\times\dot{\bm{n}}(t)\cdot\hat{\bm{S}},

(S38)

and we have

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}|m(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}(\dot{\theta}(t)-i\dot{\varphi}(t)\sin\theta(t))e^{-i\varphi(t)}\sqrt{(S-m)(S+m+1)}|m+1(t)\rangle

(S39)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}(\dot{\theta}(t)+i\dot{\varphi}(t)\sin\theta(t))e^{i\varphi(t)}\sqrt{(S+m)(S-m+1)}|m-1(t)\rangle-m\dot{\varphi}(t)(1-\cos\theta(t))|m(t)\rangle.

Comparing this to the recurrence relation of the Krylov basis, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=(-i)^{k}e^{ik(\varphi(t)+\varphi_{0}(t))}|S-k(t)\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots,2S),

(S40)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=(S-k)[h(t)+\dot{\varphi}(t)(1-\cos\theta(t))]+k(\dot{\varphi}(t)+\dot{\phi}_{0}(t))\qquad(k=0,1,\dots,2S),

(S41)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dot{\theta}^{2}(t)+\dot{\varphi}^{2}(t)\sin^{2}\theta(t)}\sqrt{k(d-k)}\qquad(k=1,\dots,2S),

(S42)

where
d=2S+1
represents the Krylov dimension and

\displaystyle e^{i\phi_{0}(t)}=\frac{\dot{\theta}(t)+i\dot{\varphi}(t)\sin\theta(t)}{\sqrt{\dot{\theta}^{2}(t)+\dot{\varphi}^{2}(t)\sin^{2}\theta(t)}}.

(S43)

IV.2
Harmonic oscillator with translation

We consider a one-dimensional single-particle in a harmonic oscillator potential.
The center of the potential is changed as a function of time
keeping the potential shape invariant.
The Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}(\hat{x}-x_{0}(t))^{2}.

(S44)

We set
x_{0}(0)=0
and choose the initial state as the ground state of
\hat{H}(0)
.

IV.2.1
Fixed eigenstate basis

The Hamiltonian is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega-\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}\omega x_{0}(t)(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{0}^{2}(t).

(S45)

Here, we define the lowering operator

\displaystyle\hat{a}=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}\hat{x}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\hat{p},

(S46)

and the raising operator
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
.
They give
\hat{a}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1\rangle
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle

for the number state
|n\rangle
satisfying
\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}|n\rangle=n|n\rangle
.

The number state basis
\{|n\rangle\}
allows us to write
the Hamiltonian in a tridiagonalized form.
When we set
|K_{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\left(-\frac{\dot{x}_{0}(t)}{|\dot{x}_{0}(t)|}\right)^{k}|k\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S47)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x_{0}^{2}(t)\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S48)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}\omega|x_{0}(t)|\sqrt{k}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots).

(S49)

IV.2.2
Instantaneous eigenstate basis

The Hamiltonian can also be written with respect to
the operator at each time defined as

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}(\hat{x}-x_{0}(t))+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega}}\hat{p}.

(S50)

We obtain

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{a}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega,

(S51)

and the instantaneous eigenstates are given by
|n(t)\rangle

with
n=0,1,\dots
, where

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t)|n(t)\rangle=\sqrt{n}|n-1(t)\rangle,

(S52)

\displaystyle\hat{a}^{\dagger}(t)|n(t)\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1(t)\rangle.

(S53)

The time derivative of the instantaneous eigenstate is written as in Eq. (
S37
).
The counterdiabatic term in the present case is given by
[
27
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=\dot{x}_{0}(t)\hat{p}=-i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}\dot{x}_{0}(t)(\hat{a}(t)-\hat{a}^{\dagger}(t)).

(S54)

This has a tridiagonal form in the instantaneous eigenstate basis.
When we set
|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|0(t)\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\left(-i\frac{\dot{x}_{0}(t)}{|\dot{x}_{0}(t)|}\right)^{k}|k(t)\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S55)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S56)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2}}|\dot{x}_{0}(t)|\sqrt{k}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots).

(S57)

IV.3
Harmonic oscillator with dilation

Let us again consider a Harmonic oscillator system.
We set
x_{0}(t)=0
and make the frequency
\omega
a time-dependent function.
The Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m}{2}\omega^{2}(t)\hat{x}^{2}.

(S58)

The initial state is set as the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian.

IV.3.1
Fixed eigenstate basis

We write the Hamiltonian in terms of the annihilation operator at
t=0
:

\displaystyle\hat{a}=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega(0)}{2}}\hat{x}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega(0)}}\hat{p}.

(S59)

We obtain

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\frac{\omega^{2}(t)+\omega^{2}(0)}{2\omega(0)}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{\omega^{2}(t)-\omega^{2}(0)}{4\omega(0)}\left((\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}+\hat{a}^{2}\right).

(S60)

Since the last term lowers or raises the state by two,
the Krylov basis is given by
|2k\rangle
.
When we set
|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|0\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\left(\frac{\omega^{2}(t)-\omega^{2}(0)}{|\omega^{2}(t)-\omega^{2}(0)|}\right)^{k}|2k\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S61)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=\left(2k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\omega^{2}(t)+\omega^{2}(0)}{2\omega(0)}\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S62)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\frac{|\omega^{2}(t)-\omega^{2}(0)|}{4\omega(0)}\sqrt{2k(2k-1)}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots).

(S63)

IV.3.2
Instantaneous eigenstate basis

The Hamiltonian is written with respect to the operator

\displaystyle\hat{a}(t)=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega(t)}{2}}\hat{x}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2m\omega(t)}}\hat{p}.

(S64)

The counterdiabatic term is given by
[
27
]

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{CD}}(t)=i\frac{\dot{\omega}(t)}{4\omega(t)}(\hat{a}^{2}(t)-(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{2}(t)).

(S65)

When we set
|K_{0}(t)\rangle=|0(t)\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\left(i\frac{\dot{\omega}(t)}{|\dot{\omega}(t)|}\right)^{k}|2k(t)\rangle\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S66)

\displaystyle a_{k}(t)=\left(2k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\omega(t)\qquad(k=0,1,\dots),

(S67)

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=\frac{|\dot{\omega}(t)|}{4\omega(t)}\sqrt{2k(2k-1)}\qquad(k=1,2,\dots).

(S68)

IV.4
An example of speed limit

As an exactly solvable model, we treat the Hamiltonian of the
harmonic oscillator potential in Eq. (
S44
) with

\displaystyle x_{0}(t)=vt.

(S69)

As a Krylov basis set, we use the instantaneous set
in Eqs. (
S55
), (
S56
), and (
S57
).
The Lanczos coefficients are independent of
t
and we can find the exact form
of
\varphi_{k}(t)
.

Since the Hamiltonian is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=e^{-ivt\hat{p}}\left(\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}^{2}\right)e^{ivt\hat{p}},

(S70)

the equation for
|\psi(t)\rangle
can be solved by moving to the rotating frame.
We can write

\displaystyle|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-ivt\hat{p}}e^{-i\hat{H}_{v}t}|0\rangle,

(S71)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{v}=\frac{1}{2m}(\hat{p}-mv)^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}^{2}-\frac{mv^{2}}{2}=e^{-imv\hat{x}}\left(\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{2}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}\hat{x}^{2}-\frac{mv^{2}}{2}\right)e^{imv\hat{x}}.

(S72)

When
v>0
, the Krylov basis is written as

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=(-i)^{k}|k(t)\rangle=(-i)^{k}e^{-ivt\hat{p}}|k\rangle.

(S73)

Then, the overlap is written as

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(t)=i^{k}e^{imv^{2}t/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-i(n+1/2)\omega t}\langle k|e^{imv\hat{x}}|n\rangle\langle n|e^{-imv\hat{x}}|0\rangle.

(S74)

The matrix element of
e^{imv\hat{x}}
with respect to the eigenstate basis
can be calculated by using the coherent state.
We obtain

\displaystyle\langle k|e^{imv\hat{x}}|n\rangle=e^{-\gamma^{2}/2}\sum_{l=0}^{\min(n,k)}\frac{\sqrt{n!k!}}{l!(n-l)!(k-l)!}(i\gamma)^{n+k-2l},

(S75)

where

\displaystyle\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{mv^{2}}{2\omega}}.

(S76)

We substitute this expression to Eq. (
S74
).
The sums over
n
and
l
can be performed by inverting their order as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{\min(n,k)}(\dots)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(\dots)+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(\dots)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{n=l}^{\infty}(\dots).

(S77)

We obtain

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}\left[-\gamma(1-e^{-i\omega t})\right]^{k}e^{-\gamma^{2}(1-e^{-i\omega t})}e^{i(\gamma^{2}-1/2)\omega t}.

(S78)

The probability for the state
|\psi(t)\rangle
at the
k
th order of the Krylov basis
is given by

\displaystyle|\varphi_{k}(t)|^{2}=\frac{1}{k!}\left(2\gamma\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)^{2k}\exp\left[-\left(2\gamma\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)^{2}\right]\sim\left(\frac{2\gamma\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}}{\sqrt{k}}\right)^{2k}\exp\left[-\left(2\gamma\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)^{2}\right].

(S79)

The last expression is applied for
k\gg 1
.
This allows us to estimate the propagation limit.
The time scale is set by

\displaystyle 2\gamma\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}=\sqrt{k}.

(S80)

The general propagation limit discussed in the main text is written as

\displaystyle\Theta_{n}(t)\leq\exp\left[-n\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\gamma\omega t}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\gamma\omega t=\sqrt{n}.

(S83)

This is consistent with Eq. (

The equality in Eq. (

In the present case, we have

V
Complexity algebra

V.1
Closed algebra

For a given set of Lanczos coefficients, we can discuss
the complexity algebra.
By using the phase transformation mentioned in the main body,
we change the generator
\hat{\cal L}(t)
as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&b_{1}(t)e^{i\delta_{1}(t)}&0&\\
b_{1}(t)e^{-i\delta_{1}(t)}&0&b_{2}(t)e^{i\delta_{2}(t)}&\\
0&b_{2}(t)e^{-i\delta_{2}(t)}&0&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right),

where
\delta_{k}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}ds\,(a_{k}(s)-a_{k-1}(s))
.
The corresponding current operator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)=-i\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&-b_{1}(t)e^{i\delta_{1}(t)}&0&\\
b_{1}e^{-i\delta_{1}(t)}&0&-b_{2}(t)e^{i\delta_{2}(t)}&\\
0&b_{2}(t)e^{-i\delta_{2}(t)}&0&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right).

As we show in the main body, these operators are related to each other by
the commutator with the complexity operator

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&\\
0&1&0&\\
0&0&2&\\
&&&\ddots\end{array}\right).

The spread complexity is written as
K(t)=\langle\varphi(t)|\hat{\cal K}|\varphi(t)\rangle
.
We have

\displaystyle[\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}},\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)]=i\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t),

(S99)

\displaystyle[\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t),\hat{\cal K}]=i\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t).

(S100)

These relations hold generally.
Furthermore, in the three systems studied in the previous sections,
the commutator of
\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}
and
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}
takes the form

\displaystyle[\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t),\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)]=-i(\alpha(t)\hat{\cal K}+\gamma(t)).

(S101)

These commutation relations are not enough to describe the time evolution of
the spread complexity as knowledge of the time dependence of the Lanczos coefficients
is additionally required.
The equation is simplified when we impose

\displaystyle b_{k}(t)=b(t)c_{k},

(S102)

\displaystyle\delta_{1}(t)=\delta_{2}(t)=\delta_{3}(t)=\cdots=:\delta(t).

(S103)

These conditions are satisfied in all the examples described in the previous section.
For example, for the single spin Hamiltonian in the instantaneous eigenstate basis, we can write

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=-\left(\dot{\theta}^{2}(t)+\dot{\varphi}^{2}(t)\sin^{2}\theta(t)\right),

(S104)

\displaystyle\gamma(t)=-S\alpha(t),

(S105)

\displaystyle b(t)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-\alpha(t)},

(S106)

\displaystyle c_{k}=\sqrt{k(d-k)},

(S107)

\displaystyle\dot{\delta}(t)=h(t)-\dot{\varphi}(t)\cos\theta(t)-\dot{\phi}_{0}(t).

(S108)

In the remaining part of the present section, we treat this case.

V.2
Time evolution of Heisenberg operators

We consider the Heisenberg representation of three operators

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t),

(S109)

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t),

(S110)

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\cal K}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t),

(S111)

where
\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t)
is the unitary time-evolution operator satisfying

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t)=\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t),

(S112)

with
\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(0)=1
.
The spread complexity is written as
K(t)=\langle\varphi(t)|\hat{\cal K}|\varphi(t)\rangle=\langle 0|\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)|0\rangle
.
We apply the time derivative operator to these operators.
Due to the above-mentioned structure of the Lanczos coefficients,

\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)
is obtained from the time derivative of

\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)
and vice versa:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)=\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t)+\dot{\delta}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t),

(S113)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)=\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}(t)-\dot{\delta}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}(t).

(S114)

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}&\dot{\delta}(t)&0\\
-\dot{\delta}(t)&\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}&\alpha(t)\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\end{array}\right)+\gamma(t)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\\
0\end{array}\right).

The current is written by the time derivative of the Krylov complexity as

\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)
.
This relation comes from the commutation relation in Eq. (
S99
) and holds generally.

We consider the simplest case for the single spin system

\displaystyle\theta(t)=\omega t,\quad\varphi(t)=0,\quad\dot{h}(t)=0.

(S128)

The differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&h&0\\
-h&0&-\omega^{2}\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\\
\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)\end{array}\right)+S\omega^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\\
0\end{array}\right),

is solved analytically as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{h\left[1-\cos\left(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t\right)\right]}{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}\hat{\cal L}(0)+\frac{\sin\left(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t\right)}{\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}}\hat{\cal J}(0)

(S142)

\displaystyle+\frac{h^{2}+\omega^{2}\cos\left(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t\right)}{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}\hat{\cal K}+S\frac{\omega^{2}\left[1-\cos\left(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t\right)\right]}{h^{2}+\omega^{2}},

and

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=\partial_{t}\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t),

(S143)

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal L}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)=h(\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{\mathrm{H}}(t)-\hat{\cal K})+\hat{\cal L}(0).

(S144)

The spread complexity is given by

\displaystyle K(t)=S\frac{\omega^{2}\left[1-\cos\left(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t\right)\right]}{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}.

(S145)

V.3
Operator quantum speed limit

In
[
42
]
,
the operator quantum speed limit was derived.
When the formula is applied to the complexity operator
\hat{\cal K}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{arccos}\,\left(\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t)}{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{2}}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{t}ds\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal J}}}^{2}(s)}{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{2}}},

(S146)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}=\hat{\cal K}-\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\cal K}.

(S147)

This speed limit relation makes sense when
d
is finite.
In the single spin case, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{arccos}\,\left(\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}^{\dagger}(t)\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}\hat{\tilde{{\cal U}}}(t)}{\mathrm{Tr}\,\hat{\tilde{{\cal K}}}^{2}}\right)\leq\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\sqrt{\dot{\theta}^{2}(s)+\dot{\varphi}^{2}(s)\sin^{2}\theta(s)}.

(S148)

In the time-dependent case, the equality condition is not satisfied.
For example, when we use Eq. (
S128
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{arccos}\,\left(\frac{h^{2}+\omega^{2}\cos(\sqrt{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}t)}{h^{2}+\omega^{2}}\right)\leq\omega t.

(S149)

The equality is satisfied only in the trivial case
h=0
.

VI
Krylov method for discrete-time systems

VI.1
Time evolution operator

In the discrete-time case, the Krylov basis is produced by the Arnoldi
iteration procedure.
Then, by using the Arnoldi coefficients,
we can consider the time evolution of the transformed state as

|\varphi^{k}\rangle=\hat{\cal U}^{k-1}|\varphi^{k-1}\rangle
,
with
|\varphi^{0}\rangle=|0\rangle
.
The Arnoldi procedure is shown in the main body.
Here, we show how to construct
\hat{\cal U}^{k}
in the iteration procedure.

We decompose the operator as

\displaystyle\hat{\cal U}^{k-1}=\sum_{l=0}^{\mathrm{min}\,(k-1,d-1)}|u_{l}^{k-1}\rangle\langle l|,

(S150)

to write

\displaystyle|\varphi^{k}\rangle=\hat{\cal U}^{k-1}|\varphi^{k-1}\rangle=\sum_{l=0}^{\mathrm{min}\,(k-1,d-1)}|u_{l}^{k-1}\rangle\varphi_{l}^{k-1}.

(S151)

We produce the first vector from the Arnoldi procedure, as

\displaystyle|u_{0}^{k-1}\rangle=|0\rangle z_{0}^{k-1}+|1\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{0}^{k-1}|^{2}}.

(S152)

Then, a perpendicular vector is defined as an auxiliary vector:

\displaystyle|v_{0}^{k-1}\rangle=-|0\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{0}^{k-1}|^{2}}+|1\rangle(z_{0}^{k-1})^{*}.

(S153)

We repeat the same procedure with

\displaystyle|u_{l}^{k-1}\rangle=|v_{l-1}^{k-1}\rangle z_{l}^{k-1}+|l+1\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{l}^{k-1}|^{2}},

(S154)

\displaystyle|v_{l}^{k-1}\rangle=-|v_{l-1}^{k-1}\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{l}^{k-1}|^{2}}+|l+1\rangle(z_{l}^{k-1})^{*},

(S155)

for
l=1,2,\dots,d-2
.
Finally, for
l=d-1
, we use

\displaystyle|u_{d-1}^{k-1}\rangle=|v_{d-2}^{k-1}\rangle.

(S156)

By completing this procedure, we can obtain
all components of
\hat{\cal U}^{k-1}
.

The first three vectors are written explicitly as

\displaystyle\hat{\cal U}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}z_{0}&-\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}z_{1}&\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|z_{1}|^{2}}z_{2}&\cdots\\
\sqrt{1-|z_{0}|^{2}}&z_{0}^{*}z_{1}&-z_{0}^{*}\sqrt{1-|z_{1}|^{2}}z_{2}&\cdots\\
0&\sqrt{1-|z_{1}|^{2}}&z_{1}^{*}z_{2}&\cdots\\
0&0&\sqrt{1-|z_{2}|^{2}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right),

which satisfies
\hat{\cal U}^{\dagger}\hat{\cal U}=1
.

VI.2
One-dimensional quantum Ising model

We apply the discrete-time formalism to the one-dimensional
quantum Ising model in Eq. (
17
).
It is well known that the model is integrable, and it can be written as a free-fermion
Hamiltonian, as described, e.g., in
[
57
]
.
The system is shown to be equivalent to the sum of independent two-level systems.
We assume
N
is even and impose the periodic boundary condition
\hat{Z}_{N+1}=\hat{Z}_{1}
.
When the discrete-time is denoted as
t=-t_{Q}+k\Delta t
with
k=0,1,\dots,M
,
the final time is given by
t_{Q}=M\Delta t/2
.
The effective Hamiltonian is written as

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{k}=\sum_{n=1}^{N/2}\left(-\epsilon_{n}^{k}\hat{Z}^{n}+v_{n}^{k}\hat{X}^{n}\right),

(S163)

where
\hat{Z}^{n}
and
\hat{X}^{n}
are Pauli operators at each site
n
, and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{n}^{k}=2h\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{2k}{M}\right)^{2}+4\left(1-\frac{k}{M}\right)\frac{k}{M}\sin^{2}\frac{p_{n}}{2}},

(S164)

\displaystyle v_{n}^{k}=-\frac{h^{2}\sin p_{n}}{t_{Q}(\epsilon_{n}^{k})^{2}},

(S165)

\displaystyle p_{n}=\frac{\pi}{2N}(2n-1).

(S166)

The initial state is set as
|\psi(0)\rangle=\otimes_{n}|0\rangle_{n}
.
At
k=M/2
and
N\to\infty
, we find
\epsilon_{n}^{k}\to 0
,
which denotes the quantum phase transition for the corresponding static system.

We apply the unitary time-evolution operator in the Arnoldi iteration procedure at each step.
It is written as

\displaystyle\hat{U}^{k}=\prod_{p=1}^{N/2}\left[\cos(h_{n}^{k}\Delta t)-i\left(\hat{Z}^{n}\frac{\epsilon_{n}^{k}}{h_{n}^{k}}+\hat{X}^{n}\frac{v_{n}^{k}}{h_{n}^{k}}\right)\sin(h_{n}^{k}\Delta t)\right],

(S167)

where
h_{n}^{k}=[(\epsilon_{n}^{k})^{2}+(v_{n}^{k})^{2}]^{1/2}
.

VI.3
Full orthogonalization procedure

The Arnoldi iteration procedure described in the main body involves large numerical errors
and we use the full orthogonalization
[
6
]
.
For a given initial basis
|K_{0}^{k}\rangle
at each time step
k
and
a given set of the Krylov basis
|K_{n}^{k-1}\rangle
(
n=0,1,\dots,k-1
) at
k-1
,
we produce the set at the next step
k
as

\displaystyle|K_{n}^{k}\rangle\sqrt{1-|z_{n-1}^{k-1}|^{2}}=\hat{U}^{k}|K_{n-1}^{k-1}\rangle-\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|K_{m}^{k}\rangle\langle K_{m}^{k}|\hat{U}^{k}|K_{n-1}^{k-1}\rangle\qquad(n=1,2,\dots,k).

(S168)

The time-evolved state
|\psi^{k}\rangle
is calculated independently as

|\psi^{k}\rangle=\hat{U}^{k-1}|\psi^{k-1}\rangle
,
and we take the overlap
\langle n|\varphi^{k}\rangle=\langle K_{n}^{k}|\psi^{k}\rangle

to calculate the spread complexity.

VII
Krylov method for periodic systems

VII.1
Time evolution in extended space

We consider a periodic Hamiltonian satisfying
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}(t+T)
.
It can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\hat{H}(t)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{H}_{m}e^{-im\Omega t},

(S169)

where
\Omega=2\pi/T
.
Although
|\psi(t)\rangle
is not necessarily a periodic function,

|\psi(s,t)\rangle
in Eq. (
1
) is periodic in
t
if we choose
|\phi(t)\rangle

as a periodic state, and we can write

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi_{m}(s)\rangle e^{-im\Omega t}.

(S170)

The initial condition
|\psi(0,t)\rangle=|\phi(t)\rangle
gives us the relation

\displaystyle|\phi(t)\rangle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi_{m}(0)\rangle e^{-im\Omega t}.

(S171)

Furthermore, setting
t=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\psi(0)\rangle=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi_{m}(0)\rangle.

(S172)

A possible simplest choice is

\displaystyle|\psi_{m}(0)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle\delta_{m,0},

(S173)

which corresponds to choosing
|\phi(t)\rangle=|\psi(0)\rangle
.

By using the periodicity with respect to
t
, we consider the Fourier decomposition
in Eq. (
S170
).
The transformed state satisfies

\displaystyle i\partial_{s}|\psi_{m}(s)\rangle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(\hat{H}_{m-n}-\delta_{m,n}m\Omega)|\psi_{n}(s)\rangle.

(S174)

According to the standard procedure, we extend the Hilbert space to
the Hilbert–Floquet, or Sambe, space.
We define the state vector in the extended space as

\displaystyle|\Psi(s)\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{m}|\psi_{m}(s)\rangle\otimes|m\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
|\psi_{1}(s)\rangle\\
|\psi_{0}(s)\rangle\\
|\psi_{-1}(s)\rangle\\
\vdots\end{array}\right),

and the inner product
\langle\!\langle\Psi^{\prime}(s)|\Psi(s)\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{m}\langle\psi_{m}^{\prime}(s)|\psi_{m}(s)\rangle
is naturally defined from this representation.
The Schrödinger equation reads

\displaystyle i\partial_{s}|\Psi(s)\rangle\!\rangle=(\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega)|\Psi(s)\rangle\!\rangle,

(S181)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\ddots&\hat{H}_{0}-\Omega&\hat{H}_{1}&\hat{H}_{2}&\\
\ddots&\hat{H}_{-1}&\hat{H}_{0}&\hat{H}_{1}&\ddots\\
&\hat{H}_{-2}&\hat{H}_{-1}&\hat{H}_{0}+\Omega&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right).

In this infinite Hamiltonian matrix, the
(m,n)
-block is given by

\hat{H}_{m-n}-\delta_{m,n}m\Omega
.
The initial condition corresponding to Eq. (
S173
) is given by

\displaystyle|\Psi(0)\rangle\!\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
0\\
|\psi(0)\rangle\\
0\\
\vdots\end{array}\right).

Since the effective Hamiltonian in the extended space is independent of
s
,
we can apply the standard Krylov method for time-independent generators.
We set the initial basis as the initial state:

\displaystyle|K_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=|\Psi(0)\rangle\!\rangle.

(S194)

Then, higher-order basis elements are constructed through the recurrence relation

\displaystyle|K_{k+1}\rangle\!\rangle b_{k+1}=(\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega)|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle-|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle a_{k}-|K_{k-1}\rangle\!\rangle b_{k},

(S195)

where

a_{k}=\langle\!\langle K_{k}|(\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega)|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle
and

b_{k}=\langle\!\langle K_{k-1}|(\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega)|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle
.

Suppose that we can find the Krylov basis and that the number of the basis elements,
the Krylov dimension, is given by
d
.
Then, using the matrix

\hat{F}=(|K_{0}\rangle\!\rangle,|K_{1}\rangle\!\rangle,\dots,|K_{d-1}\rangle\!\rangle)
,
the effective Hamiltonian is transformed as

\displaystyle(\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega)\hat{F}=\hat{F}\hat{\cal L},

(S196)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}a_{0}&b_{1}&0&&&\\
b_{1}&a_{1}&b_{2}&&&\\
0&b_{2}&a_{2}&&&\\
&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&a_{d-2}&b_{d-1}\\
&&&&b_{d-1}&a_{d-1}\end{array}\right),

and the state is written as

\displaystyle|\Psi(s)\rangle\!\rangle=\hat{F}|\Phi(s)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle\Phi_{k}(s).

(S204)

Note that
|\Phi(s)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k\rangle\Phi_{k}(s)
follows
the Schrödinger equation with the generator
\hat{\cal L}
and is written as

\displaystyle|\Phi(s)\rangle=e^{-is\hat{\cal L}}|0\rangle,

(S205)

with the initial condition

\displaystyle|0\rangle=|\Phi(0)\rangle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{array}\right).

Now, we go back to the original Hilbert space. The state

|\psi(s,t)\rangle
is generally written as

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\hat{\cal I}e^{-i\hat{\cal M}\Omega t}\hat{F}e^{-i\hat{\cal L}s}|0\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\hat{\cal I}e^{-i\hat{\cal M}\Omega t}|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle\langle k|e^{-is\hat{\cal L}}|0\rangle,

(S211)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\cal I}=(\hat{I}_{N},\hat{I}_{N},\dots).

(S212)

When the state is written in the standard Krylov form

\displaystyle|\psi(s,t)\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\Phi_{k}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|K_{k}(t)\rangle\langle k|e^{-is\hat{\cal L}}|0\rangle,

(S213)

the basis
|K_{k}(t)\rangle
is defined as

\displaystyle|K_{k}(t)\rangle=\sum_{m}e^{-im\Omega t}\langle m|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle,

(S214)

and satisfies the orthonormal relation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}dt\,\langle K_{k}(t)|K_{l}(t)\rangle=\delta_{k,l}.

(S215)

This condition corresponds to extending the Hilbert space.
States
|\psi\rangle e^{-im\Omega t}
with
m=0,\pm 1,\dots
are distinguished with each other.

VII.2
Lipkin–Meshkov–Glick model

As an example, we consider the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
18
).
The effective Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\cal H}-\hat{\cal M}\Omega=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\ddots&\hat{H}_{0}-\Omega&-i\hat{P}&0&\\
\ddots&i\hat{P}&\hat{H}_{0}&-i\hat{P}&\ddots\\
&0&i\hat{P}&\hat{H}_{0}+\Omega&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}=-\frac{2J}{N}(\hat{H})^{2},

(S222)

\displaystyle\hat{P}=h\hat{S}^{x}.

(S223)

The explicit form of the Hamiltonian is denoted by using a fixed basis.
We use the
z
-basis set
\{|\mu\rangle\}_{\mu=0,1,\dots,N}
satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{S}^{z}|\mu\rangle=\left(\frac{N}{2}-\mu\right)|\mu\rangle.

(S224)

When the initial state is given by
|0\rangle
, we choose the first Krylov basis as

\displaystyle|K_{0}\rangle\!\rangle=|\mu=0\rangle\otimes|m=0\rangle.

(S225)

We expand each basis as

\displaystyle|K_{k}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{\mu=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}|\mu\rangle\otimes|m\rangle f_{k}(\mu,m).

(S226)

Then, the recurrence relation is written as

\displaystyle b_{k+1}f_{k+1}(\mu,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{NJ}{2}\left(1-\frac{2\mu}{N}\right)^{2}-m\Omega-a_{k}\right]f_{k}(\mu,m)-b_{k}f_{k-1}(\mu,m)

(S227)

\displaystyle-\frac{ih}{2}\sqrt{\mu(N+1-\mu)}\left[f_{k}(\mu-1,m+1)-f_{k}(\mu-1,m-1)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{ih}{2}\sqrt{(\mu+1)(N-\mu)}\left[f_{k}(\mu+1,m+1)-f_{k}(\mu+1,m-1)\right].

The Lanczos coefficients are determined from the orthonormality condition

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=0}^{N}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{k}^{*}(\mu,m)f_{l}(\mu,m)=\delta_{k,l}.

(S228)

When the modulated field is changed from
\sin\Omega t
to
\cos\Omega t
in Eq. (
18
),
the recurrence relation is slightly modified as

\displaystyle b_{k+1}f_{k+1}(\mu,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{NJ}{2}\left(1-\frac{2\mu}{N}\right)^{2}-m\Omega-a_{k}\right]f_{k}(\mu,m)-b_{k}f_{k-1}(\mu,m)

(S229)

\displaystyle-\frac{h}{2}\sqrt{\mu(N+1-\mu)}\left[f_{k}(\mu-1,m+1)+f_{k}(\mu-1,m-1)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{h}{2}\sqrt{(\mu+1)(N-\mu)}\left[f_{k}(\mu+1,m+1)+f_{k}(\mu+1,m-1)\right].

We confirm that the result of the Lanczos coefficients is basically unchanged.
