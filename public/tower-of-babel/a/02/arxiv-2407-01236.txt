The reverse mathematics of the pigeonhole hierarchy
Quentin Le Houérou, Ludovic Levy Patey, Ahmed Mimouni

Abstract
The infinite pigeonhole principle for k k colors ( 𝖱𝖳 k 1 \mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k} ) states, for every k k -partition A 0 ⊔ ⋯ ⊔ A k − 1 = ℕ A_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{k-1}=\mathbb{N} , the existence of an infinite subset H ⊆ A i H\subseteq A_{i} for some i < k i<k . This seemingly trivial combinatorial principle constitutes the basis of Ramsey’s theory, and plays a very important role in computability and proof theory. In this article, we study the infinite pigeonhole principle at various levels of the arithmetical hierarchy from both a computability-theoretic and reverse mathematical viewpoint. We prove that the hierarchy of pigeonhole principles induced by restricting instances to levels of the arithmetic hierarchy is strict over 𝖱𝖢𝖠 0 \mathsf{RCA}_{0} using an elaborate iterated jump control construction, and study its first-order consequences. This is part of a large meta-mathematical program studying the computational content of combinatorial theorems.

Introduction

The infinite pigeonhole principle for
k
colors (
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
) states, for every
k
-partition
A_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup A_{k-1}=\mathbb{N}
, the existence of an infinite subset
H\subseteq A_{i}
for some
i<k
. In particular, for
k=2
, it is equivalent to the existence, for every set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, of an infinite set
H\subseteq A
or
H\subseteq\overline{A}
. This statement is the basis of Ramsey’s theorem, and more generally of Ramsey’s theory. Recall that Ramsey’s theorem for
n
-tuples states, for every
k
-coloring of
[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
, the existence of an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that the coloring is monochromatic on
[H]^{n}
. Here,
[H]^{n}
denotes the set of all subsets of
H
of size
n
. The classical proof of Ramsey’s theorem consists of reducing a
k
-coloring of
[\mathbb{N}]^{n+1}
to a
k
-coloring of
[\mathbb{N}]^{n}
by
\omega
many successive applications of the pigeonhole principle. In the base case, Ramsey’s theorem for singletons is nothing but
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
.

In reverse mathematics, the pigeonhole principle plays an important role, both for theoretical and technical reasons. Reverse mathematics is a foundational program at the intersection of computability theory and proof theory, whose goal is to find the optimal axioms to prove theorems from “ordinary mathematics”. The study of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs received a particular attention as its strength escaped the empirical structural observation of reverse mathematics
[
23
]
. As it turns out, the computability-theoretic study of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs is closely related to the study of the pigeonhole principle. Technically, all the computability-theoretic and proof-theoretic subtleties of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs are already present in the study of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}

[
4
,
14
,
28
,
29
]
.

In this article, we study the pigeonhole principle viewed both as a statement in second-order arithmetic and as a mathematical problem, formulated in terms of
instances
and
solutions
. An instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
is a set
A
, and a solution is an infinite subset
H\subseteq A
or
H\subseteq\overline{A}
. We study a hierarchy of pigeonhole principles based on the complexity of their instances, and use the frameworks of computability theory and reverse mathematics to give lower and upper bounds to the complexity of a solution. We extend several known results to higher levels of the arithmetic hierarchy, using a unifying framework of iterated jump control for the pigeonhole principle initially developed by Monin and Patey
[
32
,
34
]
. This answers multiple open questions from Benham et al.
[
2
]
. In particular, we prove that every
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
instance of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}
admits a solution whose jump is computable in any PA degree over
\emptyset^{\prime}
. This result is obtained by an asymmetric construction whose dividing line is new in computability theory. This can be put in contrast with weak König’s lemma, where the restriction to
\Delta^{0}_{1}
trees is strictly weaker than the restriction to
\Sigma^{0}_{2}

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, and whose restriction to
\Delta^{0}_{n}

\Sigma^{0}_{n}

1.1
Reverse mathematics

Reverse mathematics is a foundational program started by Harvey Friedman
[
17
,
18
]
.This program can be considered as a partial realization of Hilbert’s program
[
40
]
, and as an answer to the crisis of foundations. It uses the framework of subsystems of second-order arithmetic, with a base theory,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, capturing “computable mathematics”. More precisely, here is a formal description of its axioms.

Robinson arithmetic

\mathsf{Q}
(Peano arithmetic without induction) is composed of the following axioms:

(1)

x+1\neq 0

(2)

x=0\vee\exists y\ (x=y+1)

(3)

x+1=y+1\rightarrow x=y

(4)

x+0=x

(5)

x+(y+1)=(x+y)+1

(6)

x\times 0=0

(7)

x\times(y+1)=(x\times y)+x

(8)

x<y\leftrightarrow\exists z\ (z\neq 0\wedge x+z=y)

A second-order formula is
arithmetic
if it contains only first-order quantifiers (with second-order parameters allowed). Arithmetic formulas admit a natural classification based on the number of alternations of their quantifiers in prenex normal form. A formula is
\Sigma^{0}_{0}
(or
\Pi^{0}_{0}
) if it contains only bounded first-order quantifiers, that is, quantifiers of the form
\forall x\leq t
or
\exists x\leq t
where
x
is a first-order variable and
t
is an arithmetic term. A formula is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
if it is of the form
\exists x_{0}\forall x_{1}\dots Qx_{n-1}\varphi(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
, where
\varphi
is a
\Sigma^{0}_{0}
-formula and
Q
is
\forall
if
n
is even, and
\exists
otherwise.
\Pi^{0}_{n}
formulas are defined accordingly, starting with a universal first-order quantifier. A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
or
\Pi^{0}_{n}
if it is definable by a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
or
\Pi^{0}_{n}
formula, respectively. It is
\Delta^{0}_{n}
if it is simultaneously
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
and
\Pi^{0}_{n}
. By a theorem from Gödel,
\Delta^{0}_{1}
sets are exactly the computable ones.
The
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme
is defined for every
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(x)
and every
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-formula
\psi(x)
as

\forall x(\varphi(x)\leftrightarrow\psi(x))\to\exists Z\forall x(x\in Z\leftrightarrow\varphi(x))

The left-hand part of the implication ensures that the predicate defined by
\varphi(x)
is
\Delta^{0}_{1}
, while the right-hand part is the classical comprehension axiom for
\varphi(x)
.
Based on the correspondence between
\Delta^{0}_{1}
and computable sets, the
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme restricts the comprehension scheme to sets which can be obtained computably from their parameters.

Last, the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-induction scheme is defined for every
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(x)
as

[\varphi(0)\wedge\forall x(\varphi(x)\rightarrow\varphi(x+1))]\to\forall y\varphi(y)

In general, the induction scheme for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas is equivalent to a bounded version of the
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-comprehension scheme, that is, the existence of every initial segment of the
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-set (see Hájek and Pudlák
[
21
]
). Restricting the induction therefore corresponds to restricting the complexity of the finite sets of the model.

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
is composed of Robinson arithmetic, together with the
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme, and the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-induction scheme.

Models of second order arithmetic are of the form
\mathcal{M}=(M,S,+,\times,<,0,1)
, where
M
is the first-order part, representing the integers in the model, and
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}(M)
is the second-order part, representing the sets of integers. An
\omega
-model
is a structure
\mathcal{M}=(M,S,+,\times,<,0,1)
whose first-order part
M
consists of the standard integers
\omega
, together with the standard operations
+
,
\times
and the natural order
<
. Thus,
\omega
-models are fully specified by their second-order part
S
. In particular,
\omega
-models of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
admit a nice characterization in terms of Turing ideals. A
Turing ideal

S\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is a non-empty collection of sets which is downward-closed under Turing reducibility, and closed under the effective join
X\oplus Y=\{2n:n\in X\}\cup\{2n+1:n\in Y\}
.
In particular,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
admits a minimal
\omega
-model (with respect to inclusion) whose second-order part is exactly the collection of all computable sets.

Among models of second-order arithmetic,
\omega
-models are of particular interest due to their connection with classical computability theory, and most proofs of non-implications over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
consist of building a Turing ideal satisfying the left-hand side but not the right-hand side. This will also be the case in this article.

Since the beginning of reverse mathematics, many theorems from the core of mathematics have been studied, and some empirically observed structural phenomena emerged: there exist four main subsystems of second-order arithmetic, linearly ordered by logical strength, such that the vast majority of mathematics is either equivalent to one of these systems over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, or already provable over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
. See Simpson
[
41
]
or Dzhafarov and Mummert
[
15
]
for an introduction to reverse mathematics and these main systems. Most of the theorems studied in reverse mathematics are statements of the form
(\forall X)[\phi(X)\rightarrow(\exists Y)\psi(X,Y)]
. Any such statement can be seen as a problem
\mathsf{P}
, whose instances are sets
X
such that
\phi(X)
, and whose solutions to
X
are any set
Y
such that
\psi(X,Y)
holds. These statements are called
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problems
.

1.2
Hierarchy of pigeonhole principles

The structure property observed in reverse mathematics, and known as the “Big Five”, admits a few counter-examples, mostly coming from Ramsey theory. Historically, the first natural theorem escaping this phenomenon is Ramsey’s theorem for pairs (
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
), which was proven not to be even linearly ordered with the above-mentioned main subsystems. The computability-theoretic and proof-theoretic study of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs raised many long-standing open questions, each of them requiring the development of new techniques and breakthroughs in computability theory
[
39
,
4
,
28
,
33
]
. The computability-theoretic study of combinatorial theorems from Ramsey theory is still currently the most active branch of research in reverse mathematics. See Hirschfeldt
[
23
]
for an introduction to the reverse mathematics of combinatorial principles.

Beyond the clear combinatorial link between Ramsey’s theorem for pairs and the pigeonhole principle, the formal computability-theoretic relation between these two principles was emphasized by the decomposition of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
into a cohesiveness principle (
\mathsf{COH}
) and the pigeonhole principle for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
instances (
{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
).

1.3
Strictness of the hierarchy

The main contributions of this article are the strictness of the hierarchy of pigeonhole principles over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
,
and conservation theorems at various levels of the induction hierarchy. Separating a problem
\mathsf{P}
from another problem
\mathsf{Q}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
usually consists in finding an invariant computability-theoretic weakness property such that every weak instance of
\mathsf{P}
admits a weak solution, while there is a weak instance of
\mathsf{Q}
with no weak solution. Then, a simple iterated construction yields an
\omega
-model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{P}
which is not a model of
\mathsf{Q}
.

The most natural weakness properties are the levels of the arithmetic hierarchy, but these are not invariant, as if a set
X
is
\Delta^{0}_{2}(Y)

\Delta^{0}_{2}

is not in general
\Delta^{0}_{2}

X
is of
low

X^{\prime}\leq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}

n

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\nvdash{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
for every
n\geq 2
. Thus, the hierarchy of pigeonhole principles is strict, answering a question of Benham et al.
[
2
]
. The separation being witnessed by
\omega
-models, these separations also hold when adding the full induction scheme to
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
.

.

Over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}

{\Sigma^{0}_{1}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}<{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}<{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}<{\Delta^{0}_{3}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}<\dots

1.4
Conservation theorems

The
first-order part
of a second-order theory
T
is the set of its first-order theorems.
Characterizing the first-order part of ordinary second-order theorems is an important subject of study in reverse mathematics and is closely related to Hilbert’s finitistic reductionism program
[
40
]
.

The first-order parts of the main subsystems studied in reverse mathematics are well-understood.
In particular, the first-order part of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
corresponds to

\mathsf{Q}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n}
, where
\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n}
denotes the induction scheme restricted to
\Sigma_{n}
-formulas, that is, with no second-order parameter allowed
[
18
]
.

A good way to calibrate the first-order part of second-order theories is to reduce it to existing benchmark theories through conservation. Given a family of sentences
\Gamma
, a theory
T_{2}
is
\Gamma
-conservative
over
T_{1}
if every
\Gamma
-sentence provable over
T_{2}
is provable over
T_{1}
. A formula is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
if it starts with a universal second-order quantifier, followed by an arithmetic formula. If a theory
T_{2}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
T_{1}
, then the first-order part of
T_{2}
is included in the first-order part of
T_{1}
.

Characterizing the first-order part of
{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is one of the most important questions in reverse mathematics. Chong, Lempp and Yang
[
5
]
proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\to\mathsf{I}\Delta^{0}_{2}
. On the other hand, Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
4
]
proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}+{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
and Chong, Slaman and Yang
[
7
]
proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\nvdash\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
. Thus, the first-order part of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is above
\mathsf{Q}+\mathsf{I}\Delta_{2}
and strictly below
\mathsf{Q}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{2}
.

Benham et al.
[
2
]
asked whether
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}+{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
. We answer positively at every level of the hierarchy.

Main Theorem 1.10
.

Fix
n\geq 2
. Then
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
.

It follows that the Ginsburg-Sands theorem for
T_{1}
-spaces is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
.
On the other hand, it remains open whether
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\vdash\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
for any
n\geq 2

or even whether
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Delta^{0}_{n}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Delta^{0}_{n}
.

1.5
Notation

We assume the reader is familiar with notations from classical computability theory. See Cooper
[
8
]
or Soare
[
43
]
for a reference. In particular,
\leq_{T}
denotes Turing reducibility, and
\Phi_{0},\Phi_{1},\dots
is a fixed enumeration of all the Turing functionals.
We write
\Phi_{e}^{A}(x)\!\!\downarrow
to say that the
e
th program with oracle
A
halts on input
x
, and
\Phi_{e}^{A}(x)\!\!\uparrow
otherwise.

Integers
. We let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of non-negative integers. When working in models of weak arithmetic, we distinguish the formal set
\mathbb{N}
representing the integers in the model, from the set
\omega
of standard integers, which corresponds to the integers in the meta-theory. In particular,
\omega
is always a (proper or not) initial segment of
\mathbb{N}
.

Binary strings
. We let
2^{<\mathbb{N}}
denote the set of all finite binary strings, and
2^{\mathbb{N}}
denote the class of all infinite binary sequences. Note that
2^{\mathbb{N}}
is in bijection with
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
and both are usually identified.
Finite binary strings are written with greek letters
\sigma,\tau,\mu,\dots
. The length of a binary string
\sigma
is written
|\sigma|
, and the concatenation of two binary strings
\sigma
and
\tau
is written
\sigma\cdot\tau
. Given an infinite binary sequence
X\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
X\upharpoonright_{n}
for its initial segment of length
n
. A string
\sigma
is a prefix of a string
\tau
, written
\sigma\preceq\tau
, if there is some
\mu
such that
\sigma\cdot\mu=\tau
.

Finite sets
. Finite binary strings, seen as finite characteristic functions, are often identified with finite sets, that is,
\sigma
is identified with
\{n<|\sigma|:\sigma(n)=1\}
. Based on this correspondence, we extend the set-theoretic notations to binary strings, and let for example
\sigma\cup\rho
denote the binary string of length
\max(|\sigma|,|\rho|)
, and such that
(\sigma\cup\rho)(n)=1
if and only if
\sigma(n)=1
or
\rho(n)=1
.
In particular, one shall distinguish the cardinality
\operatorname{card}\sigma=\operatorname{card}\{n<|\sigma|:\sigma(n)=1\}
of a string seen as a set, from its length
|\sigma|
.

Trees
. A
binary tree
is a set
T\subseteq 2^{<\mathbb{N}}
closed under prefix. A
path
through a binary tree
T
is an infinite binary sequence
X\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
X\upharpoonright_{n}\in T
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We let
[T]
denote the class of all paths through
T
. Every closed class in the Cantor space can be written of the form
[T]
for a binary tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\mathbb{N}}
. A class
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}(X)
if it is of the form
[T]
for an
X
-co-c.e. tree
T
, or equivalently an
X
-computable tree
T
, or even a primitive
X
-recursive tree
T
. A set
P
is of
PA degree
over
X
if for every
X
-computable infinite binary tree
T\subseteq 2^{<\mathbb{N}}
,
P
computes an infinite path. Note that if
P
is of PA degree over
X
, then
P
computes
X
.

Formulas
.
A formula is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
(resp.
\Pi^{0}_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
) if it is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
(resp.
\Pi^{0}_{n}
) with parameters
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
. Here,
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
are either free set variables, or actual set parameters, in which case we implicitly consider an augmented language.
Given a family of sets
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
, a formula is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(\mathcal{M})
(resp.
\Pi^{0}_{n}(\mathcal{M})
) if it is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
(resp.
\Pi^{0}_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
) for some
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Ideals
.
Recall that a non-empty family of sets
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is a
Turing ideal
if for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
Y\leq_{T}X
, then
Y\in\mathcal{M}
, and for every
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
,
X\oplus Y\in\mathcal{M}
. A Turing ideal
\mathcal{M}
is a
Scott ideal
if furthermore, for every infinite binary tree
T\in\mathcal{M}
, there is an infinite path
P\in[T]\cap\mathcal{M}
.
A countable Turing ideal
\mathcal{M}=\{Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots\}
is
coded
by a set
M\subseteq\mathbb{N}
if
M=\bigoplus_{n}Z_{n}=\{\langle x,n\rangle:x\in Z_{n}\}
. Here,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
denotes the usual Cantor bijection. An
M
-index
of an element
X\in\mathcal{M}
is an integer
n
such that
X=Z_{n}
. A
Scott code
of a Scott ideal
\mathcal{M}
is a code
M
such that the operations
(m,n)\mapsto p
such that
Z_{p}=Z_{m}\oplus Z_{n}
, and
(e,n)\mapsto p
such that
Z_{p}
is a completion of
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{n}}
, are computable. A collection of sets
\mathcal{M}
is
topped
by a set
X
if it is of the form
\{Z\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:Z\leq_{T}X\}
. Note that every topped collection is a Turing ideal, and that no Scott ideal is topped.

\omega
-structure
. An
\omega
-structure
\mathcal{M}=(\omega,S)
is fully specified by its second-order part
S
. Thus, we identify both notions. In particular, we say that
\mathcal{M}
is topped if so is
S
. As mentioned,
\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
if and only if
S
is a Turing ideal.

Mathias forcing
. A
Mathias condition
is an ordered pair
(\sigma,X)
, where
\sigma
is a finite binary string, and
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is an infinite set with
\min X>|\sigma|
. A condition
(\tau,Y)

extends
another condition
(\sigma,X)
if
\sigma\preceq\tau
,
Y\subseteq X
, and
\tau\setminus\sigma\subseteq X
. The set
X
of a Mathias condition
(\sigma,X)
is considered as a
reservoir
of elements which are allowed to be later added to the initial segment
\sigma
. By abuse of notation, when
\min X\leq|\sigma|
, we shall write
(\sigma,X)
to denote the Mathias condition
(\sigma,X\setminus\{0,\dots,|\sigma|\})
.

Organization of the paper

In

PA degrees play an essential role in computability theory, and are often involved as intermediary objects to obtain a good iterated jump control construction. Let
weak König’s lemma
(
\mathsf{WKL}
) be the problem whose instances are infinite binary trees and whose solutions are paths. In

, we prove that

-hyperimmune, they will remain

-hyperimmune, where

is the generic object. This both serves as a gentle example to iterated-jump control and a preliminary construction necessary to prove our main theorems.

Solutions to combinatorial theorems from Ramsey theory are often constructed using variants of Mathias forcing. However, Mathias forcing does not behave well with respect to iterated-jump control. In
Section

4
, we introduce the fundamental concepts of largeness, partition regularity, minimal and cohesive class, which enable to define a refinement of Mathias forcing with the appropriate iterated-jump control. Then, in

In

, we define two notions of forcing, namely, main forcing and witness forcing, to build low

and weakly low

Last, in

2
Iterated jump control and forcing question

The main theorems of this article are proven by effective forcing, with an iterated jump control.
This technique consists of making the constructed set inherit computability-theoretic weaknesses of the ground model by translating arithmetical properties of the generic set into absolute arithmetical formulas of the same complexity.

In what follows, we shall consider an arbitrary notion of forcing
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
, together with an
interpretation function

[\cdot]:\mathbb{P}\to\mathcal{P}(2^{\mathbb{N}})
such that if
d\leq c
, then
[d]\subseteq[c]
.
Intuitively, a condition
c
is an approximation of the constructed set, and its interpretation is the class of all candidate sets which satisfy the approximation. If
d\leq c
, then the approximation
d
is more precise than
c
, thus
[d]\subseteq[c]
. In all the notions of forcing we shall consider, the interpretation will be a closed class in the Cantor space, and for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
, the class
\bigcap_{c\in\mathcal{F}}[c]
will be a singleton
\{G_{\mathcal{F}}\}
. A condition
c

forces
a formula
\varphi(G)
if
\varphi(G_{\mathcal{F}})
holds for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
containing
c
.

The computability-theoretic weaknesses of the generic set are closely related to the existence of a so-called forcing question with a good definitional complexity.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a family of formulas. A
forcing question
for
\Gamma
-formulas is a relation
\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\times\Gamma
such that for every
c\in\mathbb{P}
and
\varphi(G)\in\Gamma
,

1.

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G)
, then there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\varphi(G)
;

2.

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\varphi(G)
, then there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\neg\varphi(G)
.

Given a formula
\varphi(G)
, the set
\mathbb{P}
can be divided into three categories: the conditions forcing
\varphi(G)
, the conditions forcing
\neg\varphi(G)
, and the conditions forcing neither of those.
A forcing question can be thought of as a dividing line within the third category. There are therefore two canonical relations satisfying
Definition

2.1
:

•

c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}_{0}\varphi(G)
if there is some
d\leq c
forcing
\varphi(G)
. This relation will give the same answers to the third category and the first one.

•

c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}_{1}\varphi(G)
if there is no
d\leq c
forcing
\neg\varphi(G)
. This relation will give the same answers to the third category and the second one.

In some cases, however, there exist intermediary forcing questions with a better definitional complexity.

Note that a forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formulas induces a forcing question for
\Pi^{0}_{n}
formulas by negating the relation, thus we shall only consider forcing questions for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formulas. The notion of forcing question was introduced by Monin and Patey
[
32
, Section 2]
who proved two abstract theorems. We recall them for the sake of completeness.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a family of formulas. A forcing question is
\Gamma
-preserving
if for every
c\in\mathbb{P}

and every formula
\varphi(G,x)\in\Gamma
, the relation
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G,x)
is in
\Gamma
uniformly in
x
, that is, the predicate
P(x):=c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G,x)
is a
\Gamma
-formula.

The first abstract theorem concerns the preservation of the arithmetic hierarchy. It is used to prove cone avoidance, or its iterated versions.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
be a notion of forcing with a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving forcing question.
For every non-
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
set
C
and every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
,
C
is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G_{\mathcal{F}})
.

Proof.

For every
e\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{e}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
be the set of all conditions forcing
W_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}\neq C
.
We claim that
\mathcal{D}_{e}
is dense. Indeed, given
c\in\mathbb{P}
, consider the following set:

U=\{x\in\mathbb{N}:c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}x\in W_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}\}

Since the forcing question is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving, the set
U
is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, thus
U\neq C
.
Let
x\in U\Delta C=(U\setminus C)\cup(C\setminus U)
. Suppose first that
x\in U\setminus C
. By Property 1 of the forcing question, there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
x\in W_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}
.
Suppose now that
x\in C\setminus U
. By Property 2 of the forcing question, there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
x\not\in W_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}
.
In both cases, the extension
d\leq c
forces
W_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}\neq C
, so the set
\mathcal{D}_{e}
is dense. This proves our claim. Thus, for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}
is
\{\mathcal{D}_{e}:e\in\mathbb{N}\}
-generic, hence
C
is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n}(G_{\mathcal{F}})
.
∎

Many forcing questions, when answering positively a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
question, can actually find a finite set of witnesses for the outermost existential quantifier. This can be seen as a form of compactness.

Definition 2.4
.

A forcing question is
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-compact
if for every
c\in\mathbb{P}
and every
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formula
\varphi(G,x)
, if
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\exists x\varphi(G,x)
, then there is some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x<k)\varphi(G,x)
.

The existence of a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-compact forcing question is closely related to the ability to compute fast-growing functions. Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
A
-hyperimmune
if it is not dominated by any
A
-computable function.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
be a notion of forcing with a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-compact,
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving forcing question.
For every
\emptyset^{(n-1)}
-hyperimmune function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
,
f
is
G_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n-1)}
-hyperimmune.

Proof.

For every
e\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{D}_{e}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
be the set of all conditions forcing
\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}
not to dominate
f
. More precisely,
\mathcal{D}_{e}
is the set of all conditions forcing either
\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}
to be partial, or
\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow<f(x)
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim that
\mathcal{D}_{e}
is dense.
Suppose first that
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}
. Then by Property 2 of the forcing question, there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\uparrow
, hence to be partial.
Suppose now that for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
,
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\exists v\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow=v
. By
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-compactness of the forcing question, for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, there is some bound
k_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists v<k_{x})\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow=v
. Let
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be the function which on input
x
, looks for some
k_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists v<k_{x})\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow=v
, and outputs
k_{x}
. Such a function is total by hypothesis, and
\emptyset^{(n-1)}
-computable by
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preservation of the forcing question. Since
f
is
\emptyset^{(n-1)}
-hyperimmune,
h(x)<f(x)
for some
x\in\mathbb{N}
. By Property 1 of the forcing question, there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
(\exists v<k_{x})\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow=v
. Since
f(x)\geq k_{x}
,
d
forces
\Phi_{e}^{G^{(n-1)}}(x)\!\!\downarrow<f(x)
.
This proves our claim. Thus, for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}
is
\{\mathcal{D}_{e}:e\in\mathbb{N}\}
-generic, hence
f
is
G_{\mathcal{F}}^{(n-1)}
-hyperimmune.
∎

Additional structural properties on the forcing question, such as the ability to find simultaneous answers to independent questions, yield PA or DNC avoidance, as in Monin and Patey
[
33
]
.

The forcing question plays an important role in conservation theorems as well. Indeed, given a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problem
\mathsf{P}
, proving that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}+\mathsf{P}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
consists in starting with a countable model
\mathcal{M}=(M,S)
of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, and given an instance
X\in S
of
\mathsf{P}
, constructing a solution
G\subseteq M
such that
\mathcal{M}\cup\{G\}\models\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{M}\cup\{G\}=(M,S\cup\{G\})
.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a notion of forcing
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
and some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a forcing question is
(\Sigma^{0}_{n},\Pi^{0}_{n})
-merging

if for every
c\in\mathbb{P}
and every pair of
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
formulas
\varphi(G),\psi(G)
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G)

but
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\psi(G)
, then there is an extension
d\leq c
forcing
\varphi(G)\wedge\neg\psi(G)
.

The existence of a
(\Sigma^{0}_{n},\Pi^{0}_{n})
-merging forcing question enables to preserve
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-induction.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(M,S)\models\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
be a countable model and let
(\mathbb{P},\leq)
be a notion of forcing with a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving
(\Sigma^{0}_{n},\Pi^{0}_{n})
-merging forcing question. For every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{M}\cup\{G_{\mathcal{F}}\}\models\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
.

Proof.

For every
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formula
\varphi(x,G)
, let
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
be the set of all conditions forcing either
\forall x\varphi(x,G)
,
or
\neg\varphi(0,G)
, or
\varphi(a-1,G)\wedge\neg\varphi(a,G)
, for some
a\in M
with
a>0
. We claim that

Let

The most natural way to define a forcing question consists in defining

to hold if there exists some

forcing
\varphi(G)
. There exists an inductive syntactic definition of the forcing relation, and when the partial order is computable, this definition yields a

-preserving forcing question. However, in most cases, the partial order is not computable, and one uses a custom

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\Gamma

for

3
Forcing with trees

PA degrees play an essential role in the computability-theoretic analysis of the pigeonhole principle. Indeed, the notion of forcing used to build solutions to the pigeonhole principle is a variant of Mathias forcing whose reservoirs belong to a Scott ideal. Therefore, to prove an avoidance or preservation property for the pigeonhole principle, one must first prove a basis theorem for
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes with respect to the same property.

PA degrees admit several characterizations and therefore form a robust notion. A function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
diagonally non-
X
-computable
(
X
-DNC) if
f(e)\neq\Phi_{e}^{X}(e)
for every
e\in\mathbb{N}
. A set is of PA degree over
X
if and only if it computes a diagonally non-
X
-computable
\{0,1\}
-valued function, or equivalently if, given an enumeration
\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\dots
of all
\Pi^{0}_{1}(X)
formulas, it computes a function
g:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\{0,1\}
such that
(\forall e_{0},e_{1})[(\psi_{e_{0}}\vee\psi_{e_{1}})\rightarrow\psi_{e_{g(e_{0},e_{1})}}]
. Furthermore, there exists an
X
-computable infinite binary tree whose paths are exactly the
\{0,1\}
-valued
X
-DNC functions, so there exists a maximally difficult tree whose paths are all of PA degree. It follows that any computability-theoretic result about PA degrees can be stated equivalently over PA degrees or over members of
\Pi^{0}_{1}
classes.

For our purpose, we will need to prove the existence of PA degrees which preserve multiple hyperimmunities relative to various levels of the arithmetic hierarchy. For example, if
f
is hyperimmune and
g
is
\emptyset^{\prime}
-hyperimmune, one wants to prove the existence of a set
P
of PA degree such that

is
P
-hyperimmune and
g
is
P^{\prime}
-hyperimmune. There exist, among others, two well-known basis theorems which serve a large majority of the purposes: the low and the computably dominated basis theorem
[
24
]
. A set
X
is of
computably dominated
degree if every total
X
-computable function is dominated by a total computable function, or equivalently if it does not compute any hyperimmune function.

Theorem 3.1

(Jockusch and Soare
[
24
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
be a non-empty
\Pi^{0}_{1}
class.

1.

There exists a member
X\in\mathcal{C}
of low degree (
X^{\prime}\leq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
).

2.

There exists a member
X\in\mathcal{C}
of computably dominated degree.

Given
f
and
g
as in the discussion above (before
Theorem

3.1
), by the computably dominated basis theorem, there exists a set
P
of PA and computably dominated degree. In particular,
f
is
P
-hyperimmune. On the other hand, by the low basis theorem, there is a set
Q
of PA and low degree, so since
g
is
\emptyset^{\prime}
-hyperimmune and
Q^{\prime}\leq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
,
g
is
Q^{\prime}
-hyperimmune. The difficulty is to preserve both hyperimmunities simultaneously, as every low degree is hyperimmune, so no set can be simultaneously of low and computably dominated degree.

In order to prove the preservation of multiple hyperimmunities simultaneously, we use a notion of forcing with primitive recursive trees introduced by Wang
[
46
]
, who showed the existence of a forcing question with good definability properties at every level. For the sake of completeness, we state the properties of his notion of forcing, and use it to prove the existence of PA degrees which preserve multiple hyperimmunities simultaneously.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the notion of forcing whose

are infinite primitive recursive trees, partially ordered by the inclusion relation. We write

for the inclusion and say that
S

extends

T
.

A non-empty class

\Pi^{0}_{1}

. Using a delaying trick, for every co-c.e. tree

(see
[

).
It follows that there is a primitive recursive tree whose paths are exactly the
\{0,1\}
-valued DNC functions.

The partial order

does not have the right definitional complexity already for deciding

-properties of the generic object. We shall therefore define a custom forcing relation for
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
and
\Pi^{0}_{1}
formulas. At higher levels, the complexity of the partial order is absorbed by the complexity of the forced formula, and therefore one can use the standard inductive definition of the forcing relation.

Definition 3.3
.

We define inductively a forcing relation for arithmetic formulas as follows:

For
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{0}^{0}

•

For

\Pi_{n}^{0}

•

The following lemma corresponds to
[

Let

[

(Scott

4
Largeness and partition regularity

Solutions to problems from Ramsey theory are often constructed using variants of Mathias forcing, that is, with conditions consisting of a finite stem and an infinite reservoir. Even in the case of computable Mathias forcing, where the reservoirs are computable, the
\Sigma^{0}_{2}(G)
and
\Pi^{0}_{2}(G)
properties of the generic object
G
are generally more complex than the
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
and
\Pi^{0}_{2}
properties of the ground model. In particular, every sufficiently generic set for computable Mathias forcing is of high degree. Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
dominant
if it eventually dominates every computable function. By Martin’s domination theorem
[
30
]
, a degree is high if and only if it computes a dominant function. The
principal function
of an infinite set
X=\{x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots\}
is the function
p_{X}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by
n\mapsto x_{n}
.

Proposition 4.1

(Folklore)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sufficiently generic filter for computable Mathias forcing.
Then the principal function of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is dominant.

Proof.

Given a total computable function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and a computable Mathias condition
(\sigma,X)
, one can computably thin out the reservoir
X
to obtain a computable reservoir
Y
such that
p_{\sigma\cup Y}
eventually dominates
g
.
The condition
(\sigma,Y)
is an extension of
(\sigma,X)
forcing the principal function of
G_{\mathcal{F}}
to dominate
g
.
∎

A condition can be seen as an invariant property that is preserved along the construction of an infinite object: given a mathematical approximation satisfying some structural properties, one can apply one step of the construction, and obtain another mathematical approximation satisfying the same structural properties.

In the construction of solutions to the pigeonhole principle, Mathias forcing over Scott ideals is an appropriate invariant for a good first-jump control, but it is an over-generalization preventing from having a good second-jump control: the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{2}(G)
properties is a density statement about an infinite collection of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(G)
properties. It requires guaranteeing some
positive
information about the future, while a reservoir forces some
negative
information, as it restricts the candidate integers that can be added to the generic set. One must therefore use a “reservoir of reservoirs”, which will restrict the possible choices of reservoirs, hence will restrict the future negative information, which is a way of forcing positive information.

This “reservoir of reservoirs” must still allow the necessary operations on the reservoirs to ensure a good first-jump control. Looking at the combinatorics of a first-jump control of the pigeonhole principle, the only operations on the reservoirs are finite truncation, and splitting based on a 2-partition. This naturally yields the notion of partition regular class.
Partition regularity is a generalization of the notion of infinity.

Definition 4.2
.

A class
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is
partition regular
if :

•

\mathcal{A}
is non-empty,

•

for all
X\in\mathcal{A}
, if
X\subseteq Y
, then
Y\in\mathcal{A}
,

•

for every
X\in\mathcal{A}
, for every
2
-cover
Y_{0}\cup Y_{1}\supseteq X
, there exists
i<2
such that
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.

By iterating the splitting, if
\mathcal{A}
is partition regular, then for every integer
k
, for every
X\in\mathcal{A}
, and every
k
-cover
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{k}
of
X
, there exists some
i\leq k
such that
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.
By the infinite pigeonhole principle, the class of all infinite sets is partition regular. We will be interested in partition regular classes having only infinite sets. These classes are called
non-trivial
. Equivalently, a partition regular class is non-trivial if every set has at least 2 elements.
For a set

, let
\mathcal{L}_{X}
be the
\Pi_{2}^{0}(X)
partition regular class containing all the sets having an infinite intersection with

.

Given a partition regular class
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
, one can construct solutions to the pigeonhole principle with a good first-jump control, using a variant of Mathias forcing whose reservoirs belong to
\mathcal{A}
. Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
4
]
and Dorais
[
10
]
first used variants of Mathias forcing with reservoirs in partition regular classes to build generic sets of non-high degree. The technique was then developed by Monin and Patey
[
32
,
34
,
33
,
35
]
to prove several basis theorems about the pigeonhole principle.

4.1
Large classes

One should expect from a notion of largeness that it is closed upwards under inclusion. The collection of all partition regular classes is not closed upwards: for example, letting

be any infinite and co-infinite set,
\mathcal{L}_{X}
is partition regular, but
\mathcal{L}_{X}\cup\{\overline{X}\}
is not. The following notion of largeness is more convenient to work with, and closely related to partition regularity.

Definition 4.3
.

A class
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is
large
if :

•

for all
X\in\mathcal{A}
, if
X\subseteq Y
, then
Y\in\mathcal{A}
,

•

for every integer
k
, for every
k
-cover
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{k}
of
\mathbb{N}
, there exists

such that
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.

The notion of largeness was introduced and studied by Monin and Patey
[

]
. They proved that a class is large if and only if it contains a partition regular subclass. Furthermore, every large class contains a maximal partition regular subclass for inclusion, which admits an explicit syntactic definition.

Definition 4.4

Given a large class

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})=\{X\in\mathcal{A}:\forall k\forall X_{0}\cup\dots\cup X_{k-1}\supseteq X\penalty\ \exists i<k\penalty\ X_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\}

is large, then

is the maximal partition regular subclass of

(
[
32
]
)

Let

The contrapositive of

is not large. We shall be interested only in G
δ

By an immediate relativization, we let

From now on, fix a Scott ideal

and the basic operations on the
M
-indices are computable).
Given a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
, we let

\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcap_{(e,i)\in C}\mathcal{U}_{e}^{Z_{i}}

Thanks to
Lemma

4.5
, largeness of an arbitrary intersection of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
classes can be reduced to checking largeness of a finite intersection of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
classes, which is a
\Pi^{0}_{2}
statement. The following lemma gives the relativized complexity of the general statement:

Lemma 4.6

(
[
34
]
)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
be a set, the statement “
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is large” is
\Pi_{1}^{0}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
.

Proof sketch.

By
Lemma

4.5
and by compactness, this statement can be rephrased as “for every
k
and every finite subset
E
of
C
, there exists some
n
such that for every
k
-partition of
\{0,\dots,n\}
, one of its parts belongs to
\bigcap_{(i,e)\in E}\mathcal{U}_{e}^{Z_{i}}
.”
∎

Given a large
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
class
\mathcal{U}
, its largest partition regular subclass
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})
is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
. Still by
Lemma

4.5
, the largest partition regular subclass of a large
\Pi^{0}_{2}
class is again
\Pi^{0}_{2}
. One can therefore switch from largeness to partition regularity with no additional cost:

Lemma 4.7

(
[
34
]
)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
be a set, then
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}})=\bigcap_{F\subseteq_{\mathtt{fin}}C}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{F}^{\mathcal{M}})
is
\Pi_{1}^{0}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
and there exists a set
D
computable uniformly in
C
such that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}})=\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

4.2
\mathcal{M}
-minimal classes

As mentioned above, the notion of forcing for constructing solutions to the pigeonhole principle with a good first-jump control is a variant of Mathias forcing whose conditions belong to a Scott ideal. To obtain a good second-jump control, one must restrict the reservoirs to some well-chosen partition regular class.

Given the computability-theoretic nature of the
\Sigma^{0}_{2}(G)
and
\Pi^{0}_{2}(G)
statements that need to be forced, the appropriate partition regular class does not admit a nice explicit combinatorial definition. One can either decide to start with the simplest partition regular class of all the infinite sets, and refine this class over the construction by considering partition regular subclasses which will ensure stronger positive information about the reservoirs, or build once and for all the most restrictive partition regular class, in other words, the partition regular class which will maintain as much positive information about the reservoirs as possible. We adopt the latter approach.

Seeing a partition regular class as a “reservoir of reservoirs”, if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
are two partition regular classes,
\mathcal{A}
will impose more restrictions on the possible choice of reservoirs than
\mathcal{B}
. Considering that a reservoir forces negative information about the set,
\mathcal{A}
will force more positive information than
\mathcal{B}
. Therefore, minimal partition regular classes will ensure as much positive information as possible.

Definition 4.8
.

A large class
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathcal{M}
-minimal
if for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
e\in\mathbb{N}
, either
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}
or
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}
is not large.

Every large class containing a partition regular subclass, every
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large class of the form
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is also partition regular.
There is a natural greedy algorithm to build a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is non-trivial and
\mathcal{M}
-minimal.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Scott ideal with Scott code
M
and let
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
be a set of indices such that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{D}
is large and contains only infinite sets. Then
(D\oplus M^{\prime})^{\prime}
computes a set
C\supseteq D
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is
\mathcal{M}
-minimal.

Proof.

By the padding lemma, there is a total computable function
g:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every
e,s\in\mathbb{N}
and every set
X
,
\mathcal{U}^{X}_{g(e,s)}=\mathcal{U}^{X}_{e}
and
g(e,s)>s
. By uniformity of the properties of a Scott code, there is another total computable function
h:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every
e,s\in\mathbb{N}
and every Scott code
M
,
h(e,s)
and
e
are both
M
-indices of the same set, and
h(e,s)>s
.

We build a
(D\oplus M^{\prime})^{\prime}
-computable sequence of
D
-computable sets
C_{0}\subseteq C_{1}\subseteq\dots
such that, letting
C=\bigcup_{s}C_{s}
,
\mathcal{U}^{M}_{C}
is
\mathcal{M}
-minimal and for every
s
,
C\upharpoonright s=C_{s}\upharpoonright s
.
Start with
C_{0}=D
.
Then, given a set
C_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}
is large, and a pair
(e,i)
, define
C_{s+1}=C_{s}\cup\{(g(e,s),h(i,s))\}
if
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{Z_{i}}
is large, and
C_{s+1}=C_{s}
otherwise. The set
C=\bigcup_{s}C_{s}
is the desired set. Note that by choice of
g
and
h
, in the former case,
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s+1}}^{\mathcal{M}}=\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{Z_{i}}
.
By
Lemma

4.6
, the statement “
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{Z_{i}}
is large” is
\Pi^{0}_{1}(C_{s}\oplus M^{\prime})
, so it can be decided
(D\oplus M^{\prime})^{\prime}
-computably since
C_{s}\leq_{T}D
. The use of
g
and
h
ensures that
C_{s+1}\upharpoonright s=C_{s}\upharpoonright s
.
∎

One can apply
Lemma

4.9
with
D=\{(e_{s},i):s\in\mathbb{N}\}
where
\mathcal{U}_{e_{s}}^{Z_{i}}=\{Y\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:\operatorname{card}Y\geq s\}
to obtain a set
C\leq_{T}M^{\prime\prime}
such that
\mathcal{U}^{M}_{C}
is
\mathcal{M}
-minimal.
However, being
M^{\prime\prime}
-computable is too complex for our purpose. Thankfully, one does not need to explicitly have access to the set of indices of the
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class, but only to be able to check that a class is compatible with it. This yields the notion of
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class.

4.3
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive classes

In the previous algorithm for constructing an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class, the order in which one considers the pairs
(e,i)
matters. Indeed, if
\mathcal{A}
is large and
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
are two large subclasses, then
\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{C}
is not necessarily large. Therefore, there exist many
\mathcal{M}
-minimal classes, depending on the ordering of the pairs. The notion of
\mathcal{M}
-cohesiveness is a way of choosing an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class without explicitly giving its set of indices.

Definition 4.10
.

A large class
\mathcal{A}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive
if for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
, either
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
or
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
.

It follows from the definition that for every infinite set
X\in\mathcal{M}
, if
X\in\mathcal{A}
, then
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
.
Indeed, otherwise, by
\mathcal{M}
-cohesiveness,
X\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
, yielding a contradiction.
The cohesiveness terminology comes from the cohesiveness principle (
\mathsf{COH}
), which states for every infinite sequence of sets
R_{0},R_{1},\dots
, the existence of an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, either
H\subseteq^{*}R_{n}
or
H\subseteq^{*}\overline{R}_{n}
. Such a set
H
is said to be
cohesive
for the sequence. There exists an immediate correspondence between the cohesiveness principle and the existence of
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive classes. Indeed, given an infinite set
H
which is cohesive for the sequence
\mathcal{M}=\{Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots\}
,
the class
\mathcal{L}_{H}
is partition regular and
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive.

The following lemma shows that an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class already contains the information of an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class, in the sense that in the greedy algorithm to build an
\mathcal{M}
-minimal class from an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive one, the ordering on the pairs does not matter.

Lemma 4.11

(
[
34
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
be an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class. Let
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
be such that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
are both large. Then so is
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{E}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

It follows that every
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class admits a unique
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large subclass.

Lemma 4.12

(
[
34
]
)
.

For every
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a unique
\mathcal{M}
-minimal large subclass:

\langle\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\rangle=\bigcap_{e\in\mathbb{N},X\in\mathcal{M}}\{\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}:\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X}\textit{is large}\}

Contrary to
\mathcal{M}
-minimal classes, one can build a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive computably in any PA degree over
M^{\prime}
. There are two possible constructions: either using the correspondence with the cohesiveness principle, knowing that any PA degree over
M^{\prime}
computes the jump of an infinite set
H\subseteq\mathbb{N}
cohesive of
\mathcal{M}=\{Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots\}
, and computing in
H^{\prime}
the set
C
, or directly building the set
C
by deciding, given a set
C_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
and a set
Z_{i}
, whether
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{Z_{i}}
or
\mathcal{U}_{C_{s}}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{Z}_{i}}
is large. We prove it formally with the latter approach.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Scott ideal with Scott code
M
and let
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2}
be a set of indices such that
\mathcal{U}^{M}_{D}
is large and contains only infinite sets. Then any PA degree over
D\oplus M^{\prime}
computes a set
C\supseteq D
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive.

Proof.

Fix
P
a PA degree over
D\oplus M^{\prime}
. Recall that
P
is able to choose, among two
\Pi^{0}_{1}(D\oplus M^{\prime})
formulas such that at least one is true, a valid one.

First, consider two
M
-computable enumerations of sets
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{U}_{E_{n}}^{Z_{n}}=\mathcal{L}_{Z_{n}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{F_{n}}^{Z_{n}}=\mathcal{L}_{\overline{Z_{n}}}
. By the padding lemma, one can suppose that
\min E_{n},\min F_{n}\geq n
. The set
C
will be defined as
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}
for
C_{0}\subseteq C_{1}\subseteq\dots
a
P
-computable sequence of
M\oplus D
-computable sets satisfying:

•

C_{0}=D
,

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}
is large for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

C_{k}\upharpoonright k=C\upharpoonright k
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus
C
will be
P
-computable.

Let
C_{0}=D
, then, by assumption,
\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}}
is large.

Assume
C_{k}
has been defined for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, as
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}
is large, one of the two following
\Pi_{1}^{0}(D\oplus M^{\prime})
statements must hold:
``\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}\ \cap\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k}}\mbox{is large}^{\prime\prime}
or
``\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}\ \cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{Z_{k}}}\mbox{is large}^{\prime\prime}
. Hence,
P
is able to choose one that is true. If
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}\ \cap\mathcal{L}_{Z_{k}}
is large, let
C_{k+1}=C_{k}\cup E_{k}
, and if
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}}\ \cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{Z_{k}}}
is large, let
C_{k+1}=C_{k}\cup F_{k}
. By our assumption that
\min E_{n},\min F_{n}\geq n
for all
n
, the value of
C_{k}\upharpoonright k
will be left unchanged in the rest of the construction.
∎

The above construction of
C
carries the information, given an element
X\in\mathcal{M}
, whether
X\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
or not (or equivalently whether
X\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
or not, or again whether
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
or not). The following lemmas shows that this information can be recovered by any such set
C
independently of this construction, with the help of
M^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
be an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class.
C\oplus M^{\prime}
computes a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to 2
such that for every
a\in\mathbb{N}
,
f(a)=1
if and only if
Z_{a}\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

By
\mathcal{M}
-cohesiveness,
Z_{a}\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{Z_{a}}
. The statement
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{Z_{a}}
is
\Pi^{0}_{1}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
(as it is equivalent to
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{Z_{a}}
large). This statement is also
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
, since it is equivalent to
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{Z_{a}}}
not large, hence it is
\Delta^{0}_{1}(C\oplus M^{\prime})
and therefore decidable by
C\oplus M^{\prime}
.
∎

The following lemma shows that, in some sense, the construction above of an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class can be done without loss of generality.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
be an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive class, then
C\oplus M^{\prime}
is of PA degree over
X^{\prime}
for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathcal{M}
. Fix an
X
-c.e. enumeration
E_{0}\subseteq E_{1}\subseteq\dots
of
X^{\prime}
.
Given
e\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Y_{e}
be the set of all
n
such that
\Phi_{e}^{E_{n}}(e)[n]\!\!\downarrow=1
. Note that if
\Phi_{e}^{X^{\prime}}(e)\!\!\downarrow=1
then
Y_{e}
is cofinite, and if
\Phi_{e}^{X^{\prime}}(e)\!\!\downarrow=0
then
Y_{e}
is finite. It follows that if
\Phi_{e}^{X^{\prime}}(e)\!\!\downarrow
, then
Y_{e}\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if
\Phi_{e}^{X^{\prime}}(e)=1
. Let
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be the computable function which to
e
associates an
M
-index for
Y_{e}
and let
f:\mathbb{N}\to 2
be the
C\oplus M^{\prime}
-computable function of
Lemma

4.14
. Then
e\mapsto 1-f(g(e))
is a
C\oplus M^{\prime}
-computable

-valued

-DNC function, hence
C\oplus M^{\prime}
is of PA degree over
X^{\prime}
.
∎

Note that in the case where
M
is of low degree, then
C\oplus M^{\prime}
is of PA degree over
M^{\prime}
.
It is not clear at first sight that the notions of
\mathcal{M}
-cohesiveness and
\mathcal{M}
-minimality do not coincide, at least for classes of the form
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
. The following proposition shows that the two notions are always distinct. It is not of direct use for the remainder of this article, but of independent interest.

Proposition 4.16
.

For every countable Turing ideal
\mathcal{M}
, there exists a set
C
such that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive but not
\mathcal{M}
-minimal.

Proof.

Consider the following
\Pi_{2}^{0}
class
\mathcal{P}=\{X:(\forall n)(\exists x)|[x,2^{x})\cap X|\geq n\}
.

Claim 1. The class
\mathcal{P}
is partition regular.
First,
\mathbb{N}\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
X_{0}\cup X_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
for some sets
X_{0},X_{1}
, if
X_{0},X_{1}\notin\mathcal{P}
, then for all
i<2
, there exists some
n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x
, and
|[x,2^{x})\cap X_{i}|<n_{i}
. Thus, for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
,
|[x,2^{x})\cap(X_{0}\cup X_{1})|<n_{0}+n_{1}
, contradicting our assumption that
X_{0}\cup X_{1}\in\mathcal{P}
. This proves our claim.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
be an
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive partition regular subclass of
\mathcal{P}
, which exists as
\mathcal{P}
is
\Pi^{0}_{2}
.
Let
Z_{0},Z_{1},\dots
be the list of all sets
X
in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
and
let
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}=\bigcap_{n}\mathcal{L}_{Z_{n}}
.
By
\mathcal{M}
-cohesiveness of
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}
, for every
X\in\mathcal{M}
, either
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X}
or
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
, so
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive. Moreover,
\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
.

Claim 2.
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\not\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{D}^{\mathcal{M}}

Let
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots\}
defined as follows: let
x_{n}
be the smallest element of
\bigcap_{k\leq n}Z_{k}
bigger than
2^{x_{n-1}}
(or bigger than
0
if
n=0
). Note that
\bigcap_{k\leq n}Z_{k}
is infinite, since
\{\overline{Z}_{k}:k\leq n\}\cup\{\bigcap_{k\leq n}Z_{k}\}
is a cover of
\mathbb{N}
and none of
\overline{Z}_{k}
belongs to
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
, so
\bigcap_{k\leq n}Z_{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{N}}
. By construction
X
cannot be an element of
\mathcal{P}
as
|X\cap[x,2^{x})|\leq 1
for every
x
, and
X\in\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
as
X\cap Z_{k}
is infinite for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
\mathcal{U}_{C}^{\mathcal{M}}
is
\mathcal{M}
-cohesive, but not
\mathcal{M}
-minimal.

∎

4.4
Scott towers, largeness towers

In order to obtain notions of forcing with a good iterated jump control, we shall often work with hierarchies of Scott ideals with good computational properties.

Definition 4.17
.

Fix
n\geq 0
. A
Scott tower
of height
n
is a sequence of Scott ideals
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}

with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n}
, respectively, such that for every
i<n
,
M_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
.

Intuitively, a Mathias-like notion of forcing with a good iterated-jump control will decide
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
-properties with a question with parameters in
\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

Remark 4.18
.

Note that for every Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
and
i<n
, there exists a computable function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

translating
M_{i}
-indices into
M_{i+1}
-indices, that is, such that for every
a\in\mathbb{N}
,
g(a)
is an
M_{i+1}
-index of the set of
M_{i}
-index
a
. Indeed, there is a computable function
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\Phi^{M_{i}}_{h(a)}
is a set of
M_{i}
-index
a
for every
a\in\mathbb{N}
. For every
a\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Z_{a}
be the set of
M_{i+1}
-index
a
. By our definition of a Scott code, there exists a total computable function
q:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
Z_{q(e,a)}
is a completion of
\Phi_{e}^{Z_{a}}
. Let
b
be an
M_{i+1}
-index of
M_{i}
and let
g(a)=q(h(a),b)
. By definition,
Z_{g(a)}
is a completion of
\Phi^{M_{i}}_{h(a)}
, hence is the set of
M_{i}
-index
a
.

The following proposition will be useful throughout this article.

Proposition 4.19
.

Fix
n\geq 0
. There exists a Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n}
, such that for every
i\leq n
,
M_{i}
is low over
\emptyset^{(i)}
, that is,
(M_{i}\oplus\emptyset^{(i)})^{\prime}\leq_{T}\emptyset^{(i+1)}
.

Proof.

For every set
X
, consider the
\Pi^{0}_{1}(X)
class
\mathcal{C}(X)
defined in
Proposition

3.11
.
By a relativized version of the low basis theorem
[
24
]
, for every
i\leq n
, there is a Scott ideal
\mathcal{M}_{i}
containing
\emptyset^{(i)}
with Scott code
M_{i}\in\mathcal{C}(\emptyset^{(i)})
of degree low over
\emptyset^{(i)}
. Then, for every
i<n
,
M_{i}^{\prime}\equiv_{T}\emptyset^{(i+1)}
, and thus
M_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
.
∎

Given a Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
, we shall define some sets
C_{0},\dots,C_{n-1}
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}_{C_{i}}
is
\mathcal{M}_{i}
-cohesive, and work with notions of forcing with multiple reservoirs, such that the reservoir
X_{i}
at level
i
belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{i}\cap\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}_{C_{i}}
. The combinatorics of the notions of forcing will require in particular that the various cohesive classes are compatible, that is,
\bigcap_{i}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}_{C_{i}}
is large. This is not the case in general, as given a bi-infinite set
X
,
\mathcal{L}_{X}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
are partition regular, but
\mathcal{L}_{X}\cap\mathcal{L}_{\overline{X}}
is not even large. We will therefore need to ensure structurally this compatibility between the cohesive classes. This yields the notion of largeness tower.

Definition 4.20
.

Fix
n\geq 0
. A
largeness tower
of height
n
is a Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}

together with a sequence of sets
C_{0},\dots,C_{n-1}
such that for every
i<n
:

1.

\mathcal{U}_{C_{i}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}
is an
\mathcal{M}_{i}
-cohesive large class containing only infinite sets ;

2.

C_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
;

3.

\mathcal{U}_{C_{i+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i+1}}\subseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{i}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}\rangle
if
i<n-1
.

The following proposition shows that the compatibility requirements between the various notions of largeness
do not impose any constraints on the notion of Scott tower, and thus that the two constructions can done separately.

Proposition 4.21

(Monin and Patey
[
34
]
)
.

Every Scott tower can be completed into a largeness tower.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
be a Scott tower with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n}
.
The Scott ideal
\mathcal{M}_{1}
contains
M_{0}^{\prime}
hence also contains a set
X_{0}
that is PA over
M_{0}^{\prime}
. By
Lemma

4.13
,
X_{0}
computes a set
C_{0}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
is an
\mathcal{M}_{0}
-cohesive large class. Note that
C_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
since a Scott ideal is stable by Turing reduction.

Assume
C_{k}
has already been defined for some
k<n-1
. In particular,
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}
is
\mathcal{M}_{k}
-cohesive, so by
Lemma

4.9
,
(C_{k}\oplus M_{k}^{\prime})^{\prime}
computes a set
D_{k+1}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{D_{k+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}=\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\rangle
. By
Remark

4.18
, there is a set
E_{k+1}\leq_{T}D_{k+1}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{E_{k+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k+1}}=\mathcal{U}_{D_{k+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}
. Note that
C_{k}\oplus M_{k}^{\prime}\in M_{k+1}
, so
E_{k+1}\leq_{T}D_{k+1}\leq_{T}M_{k+1}^{\prime}
. By definition of a largeness tower,

so there exists a set
X_{k+1}\in\mathcal{M}_{k+2}
of PA degree over

. By

4.13
,
X_{k+1}
computes a set

such that the class
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k+1}}
is an
\mathcal{M}_{k+1}
-cohesive class. In particular,

.
∎

5
Core forcing

Monin and Patey
[
34
]
designed a notion of forcing which will serve as the common combinatorial core to all the notions of forcing introduced in this article. We define it and state its main lemmas and re-prove them for the sake of completeness.
For the remainder of this section, fix
n\geq 0
, and let
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n+1}
be Scott ideals with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n+1}
, respectively, and let
C_{0},\dots,C_{n}
be forming a largeness tower, that is, for every
i\leq n
:

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{i}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}
is an
\mathcal{M}_{i}
-cohesive large class containing only infinite sets ;

•

C_{i},M_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{i+1}
;

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{i+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i+1}}\subseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{i}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}\rangle
if
i<n
.

This hierarchy is defined abstractly because of its multiple uses in
Section

6
and
Section

7
.

Following the intuition on
\mathcal{M}
-minimal partition regular classes,
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\rangle
is a collection of reservoirs with a maximum amount of positive information, that is, satisfying a maximum amount of
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
properties. More generally,
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
satisfies a maximum amount of
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
properties. Moreover, this hierarchy of minimal classes is ordered under inclusion, so that any reservoir in
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
will satisfy simultaneously all these
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
properties for
k\leq n
. The core forcing is a refinement of Mathias forcing in which the reservoirs are required to maintain as much positive information as possible.

Definition 5.1

(Condition)
.

For any set
A\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, let
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
be the notion of forcing whose conditions are Mathias conditions
(\sigma,X_{n})
such that

•

\sigma\subseteq A
;

•

X_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\cap\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
.

Note that for every set
A
, either
A
or
\overline{A}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, hence either
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
or
\mathbb{P}^{\overline{A}}_{n}
is a valid notion of forcing, and it might be the case for both. Actually, by Monin and Patey
[
35
, Proposition 2.7]
, the measure of sets
A
such that both
A
and
\overline{A}
belong to
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
is 1.

The definition of a condition slightly differs from the original notion
[
34
]
by two aspects: First, the reservoir is required to belong to
\mathcal{M}_{n}
, while the original definition did not impose any computability-theoretic constraint on it. Second, the stem
\sigma
is required to be a subset of
A
. None of these variations will affect the combinatorial properties of the notion of forcing, but they will be very useful for the study of
{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
and
{\Delta^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
.

Definition 5.2
.

The set
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
is partially ordered using the Mathias extension relation, that is, a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\tau,Y_{n})

extends
a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n})
(and we write
(\tau,Y_{n})\leq(\sigma,X_{n})
) if
Y_{n}\subseteq X_{n}
and
\sigma\preceq\tau\subseteq\sigma\cup X_{n}
.

Given a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n})
, requiring that the stem
\sigma
is included in
A
might be an issue, since the reservoir
X_{n}
might have empty intersection with
A
. The following lemma shows that not only
A\cap X_{n}
is infinite, but
X_{n}
and
X_{n}\cap A
also satisfy the same large
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
properties forced by
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
for every
k\leq n
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(\sigma,X_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
be a condition. Then
A\cap X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
.

Proof.

By partition regularity of
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, since
A\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
and
A=(A\cap X_{n})\cup(A\cap\overline{X_{n}})
, either
A\cap X_{n}
or
A\cap\overline{X_{n}}
belongs to
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
. Since
X_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\cap\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
and
\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}
is
\mathcal{M}_{n}
-cohesive,
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X_{n}}
and therefore,
A\cap\overline{X_{n}}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, hence
A\cap X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
.
∎

Every filter
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
induces a set
G_{\mathcal{F}}
whose characteristic function is the limit of
\{\sigma:(\sigma,X_{n})\in\mathcal{F}\}
, that is,
x\in G_{\mathcal{F}}
is
\sigma(x)=1
for some
(\sigma,X_{n})\in\mathcal{F}
.
It is often convenient to see a forcing condition
c\in\mathcal{F}
as an approximation of the constructed object
G_{\mathcal{F}}
. The following notion of cylinder gives the class of “candidate” objects associated to a condition.

Definition 5.4

(Cylinder)
.

The
cylinder
under a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n})
is the class

[\sigma,X_{n}]=\{G:\sigma\subseteq G\subseteq\sigma\cup(X_{n}\cap A)\}

Given a filter
\mathcal{F}
, using the “candidate” interpretation of a cylinder, one should expect the resulting object
G_{\mathcal{F}}
to belong to the cylinder of each condition of
\mathcal{F}
. We shall see in
Proposition

5.12
that this is the case.
Moreover, still following this intuition, if a condition
d
extends another condition
c
, then
d
is a more precise approximation than
c
, so there should be less candidate objects associated to
d
than to
c
. This is indeed the case: if
(\tau,Y_{n})\leq(\sigma,X_{n})
, then
[\tau,Y_{n}]\subseteq[\sigma,X_{n}]
.

Monin and Patey
[
34
]
designed the following forcing question for the core forcing. In order to obtain a forcing question with sufficiently good definitional properties, the question does not directly involve the reservoir of the condition, but over-approximates it by asking whether the collection of reservoirs with the desired property is large. This results in a forcing question depending only on the stem of the condition. Despite this over-approximation, the resulting question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
, but rather
\Pi^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n+1})
. Because of this, we shall refine this notion of forcing in the later sections to obtain better definability properties.

Definition 5.5

(Forcing question,
[
34
, Definition 3.3]
)
.

Let
\sigma\in 2^{<\mathbb{M}}
be a finite string.

•

For
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{0}^{0}
formula, let
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
hold if:

\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)(\exists x)\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)\}\textit{ is large}.

•

For
1\leq k\leq n
and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula, we define inductively the relation
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
to hold if:

\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\cap\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)(\exists x)\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)\}\textit{ is large}.

As mentioned above, the forcing question does not have the appropriate definability property.

Lemma 5.6
.

The statement
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\phi(G)
is
\Pi_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{k+1})
if
\phi(G)
is a
\Sigma_{k+1}^{0}
formula for
k\leq n
.

Proof.

By an immediate induction using
Lemma

4.6
.
∎

The forcing question is defined by induction over the syntactical definition of a formula. It is therefore sensitive to the presentation of a property. In particular, logically equivalent formulas might yield a different answer through the forcing question.

Every notion of forcing induces a forcing relation by letting
c
force
\varphi(G)
if and only if
\varphi(G_{\mathcal{F}})
for every sufficiently generic filter
\mathcal{F}
containing
c
. However, in many situations, it is more convenient to work with a custom syntactical forcing relation.

Definition 5.7

(Forcing relation,
[
34
, Definition 3.5]
)
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{A}
-condition, we define the forcing relation
\Vdash
for
\Sigma_{k}^{0}
and
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formulas for every
0<k\leq n+1
as follows: For
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{0}^{0}
formula:

•

c\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
if
\phi(\sigma,a)
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

c\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
if
(\forall\rho\subseteq X_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\neg\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)
.

For
0<k\leq n
and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula:

•

c\Vdash(\exists x)\phi_{e}(G,x)
if
c\Vdash\phi(G,a)
for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
;

•

c\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
if
(\forall\rho\subseteq X_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
.

Intuitively, a
\Pi^{0}_{k+1}
-formula
(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
can be seen as a countable collection
\{\neg\phi(G,x):x\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
\Sigma^{0}_{k}
-formulas. Assuming that the forcing question meets its requirements, the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{k+1}
formulas is a density statement, saying that for every
\Sigma^{0}_{k}
-formula
\neg\phi(G,x)
, whatever the extension of the stem
\sigma
into
\sigma\cup\rho
, since
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
, there is an extension forcing
\neg\phi(G,x)
. Thus, if
\mathcal{F}
is a sufficiently generic filter containing a condition
c=(\sigma,X_{n})
such that
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
, then for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, there will be a condition in
\mathcal{F}
forcing
\neg\phi(G,x)
.

Also note that the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{k}
formulas might seem too strong, as one would expect to require
\rho
to range over
X_{n}\cap A
instead of
X_{n}
. We shall see, thanks to

, that it is still dense to force either a formula or its negation.
Together with
Lemmas

5.8
and
5.13
, this shows that the forcing relation satisfies the axioms of
Definition

2.8
.

Lemma 5.8
.

The forcing relations are closed downwards.

Proof.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n})
and
d=(\tau,Y_{n})
be two
\mathbb{P}_{n}^{A}
-conditions, such that
d\leq c
.
Let
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi^{0}_{0}
-formula.

•

If
c\vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then
\phi(\sigma,a)
holds for some
a\in\mathbb{N}
hence
\phi(\tau,a)
holds and thus
d\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

•

If
c\vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)\varphi(G)
, then
(\forall\rho\subseteq X_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\neg\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)
, hence in particular, letting
\mu
be such that
\sigma\cdot\mu=\tau
,
(\forall\rho\subseteq Y_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\neg\phi(\sigma\cup\mu\cup\rho,x)
, hence

.

Now, let
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi^{0}_{k}
-formula for

•

If
c\vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then
c\Vdash\phi(G,a)
for some

. By induction hypothesis,

, and thus

.

•

If
c\vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
, then
(\forall\rho\subseteq X_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
, hence in particular, letting
\mu
be such that
\sigma\cdot\mu=\tau
,
(\forall\rho\subseteq Y_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\mu\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
, hence

∎

In order to prove iterated lowness basis theorems, one will need to construct generic filters effectively. For this, it is necessary to fix a finite representation of
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition to talk about effectivity.
Recall that an
M_{n}
-index
of a set

is an integer

such that
X=Z_{a}
. Note that

-indices are not unique.

Definition 5.9
.

An
index
of a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n})
is a pair
\langle\sigma,a\rangle
where

is an
M_{n}
-index of

.

The following lemma states that the forcing question meets its specifications, that is, each case is witnessed by an extension forcing the answer.

Lemma 5.10

(
[
34
, Lemma 3.8]
)
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition and
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for
k\leq n
.

•

If
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

•

If
\sigma\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
.

Deciding which case holds requires
M_{k+1}^{\prime}
, then given the answers to the forcing question, finding an index of the extension can be done uniformly in an index of
c
using
A\oplus M_{n+1}
.

Proof.

Suppose
k=0
.
Let
\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
denote
\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)(\exists x)\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)\}
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then the class
\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
is large. By definition of the
\langle\cdot\rangle
operator, this yields that
\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
.

By
Lemma

5.3
,
A\cap X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, hence there exists some
\rho\subseteq A\cap X_{n}
such that

holds. The
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition

is such that
d\leq c
and
d\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then the class
\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
is not large.
This yields the existence of a finite set

such that
\mathcal{U}_{F}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
is not large. As such, there is some

and some

-partition

of

such that for all

,

. Consider the

is a Scott ideal, there is such a partition in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Consider such a partition
Z_{0},\dots Z_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
is partition regular, and as
X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
, there exists

such that

, and thus
Z_{i}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle
. Moreover, since
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{F}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}
, we have that
Z_{i}\not\in\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
, and thus

. The
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition

is such that

and

.

Suppose
0<k\leq n
.
Let
\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
denote
\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)(\exists x)\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)\}
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then the class
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)
is large. By definition of the
\langle\cdot\rangle
operator, this yields that
\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\cap\mathcal{U}(\sigma,\phi)\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{k}}^{\mathcal{M}_{k}}\rangle\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle

5.3
,
A\cap X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n}}\rangle

.

•

This is proved the exact same way as in the

.

Regarding the complexity of finding such

, the main difficulty is finding, given an

-partition

in

, some

such that

, this can be done uniformly in

∎

Genericity is usually defined with respect to a collection of dense sets of conditions. We define a hierarchy of notions of genericity depending on the formulas which are decided by the filter.

Definition 5.11

(Generic filter)
.

We say that a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition
c

decides
a formula
\varphi(G)
if either
c\Vdash\varphi(G)
, or
c\Vdash\neg\varphi(G)
. A filter
\mathcal{F}

decides
a formula
\varphi(G)
if it contains a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-condition deciding
\varphi(G)
.

A filter
\mathcal{F}
is
k
-generic
for
1\leq k\leq n+1
if it decides every
\Sigma^{0}_{1},\dots,\Sigma^{0}_{k}
formulas.

Thanks to
Lemma

5.10
, for every
k\leq n
and every
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
-formula
\varphi(G)
, it is dense to either force
\varphi(G)
or force
\neg\varphi(G)
. It follows that every sufficiently generic filter is
(n+1)
-generic. Given a filter
\mathcal{F}
, we write
[\mathcal{F}]
for
\bigcap_{(\sigma,X_{n})\in\mathcal{F}}[\sigma,X_{n}]
.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
1
-generic filter. There exists a unique set
G_{\mathcal{F}}
belonging to
[\mathcal{F}]
. Furthermore,
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is infinite.

Proof.

For every condition

Let

By

As mentioned, the semantic definition of the forcing relation states that a condition

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a

, then for every

, there exists some

. Furthermore,

will hold.

Proof.

Proceed by strong induction on

:

Let

. Let
\mathcal{F}
be
k

\Sigma_{k}^{0}

. By

, there exists some

. Assume for contradiction that

•

and all

6
Iterated lowness basis

The goal of this section is to prove the following iterated basis theorem, which, besides its intrinsic interest, will serve to separate

.

\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}

Proof.

For

By the low basis theorem relativized to

(
[

]

∎

The restriction of

.

Proof.

By Downey, Hirschfeldt, Lempp and Solomon

Consider a chain

The remainder of the section is dedicated to the proof of
Main Theorem

1.6
. Fix
n\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
be Scott ideals with Scott codes

, respectively, and let

•

\mathcal{U}_{C_{i}}^{\mathcal{M}_{i}}

•

By partition regularity of

We now present our two notions of forcing, called

6.1
Main forcing

Throughout this section, fix a
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
set

.

Definition 6.3

(Condition)
.

Let
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
be the
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
notion of forcing.

The
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing coincides with the
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-forcing as a partial order, but one will exploit the
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}

to define a
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-preserving forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas. Recall that

-forcing admits a forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas with a bad definitional complexity.

The forcing relation for

-forcing inherits the forcing relation from
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-forcing. However,
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-forcing does not define any forcing relation for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
and
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas.

Definition 6.4

(Forcing relation)
.

Let

for

For

•

if

for some

;

•

if for every
\rho\subseteq X_{n-1}\cap A
and every

,
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,a)
.

As expected, the forcing relations for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
and

2.8
. The following lemma extends

5.8
to
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
and

Lemma 6.5
.

The forcing relations are closed downwards.

Proof.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1})
and

be two
\mathbb{M}_{n}^{A}
-conditions, such that

. Let
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi^{0}_{n}
-formula.

•

If

for some

. By

,

and for all

,

. This yields in particular, letting

A forcing question is

Definition 6.6

(Forcing question for

Let

The following lemma states that the forcing question at the last level meets its specifications, that is, each answer is witnessed by an extension forcing it.

.

\mathcal{U}_{C_{0}}^{\mathcal{M}_{0}}\cap\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)\phi(\tau\cup\rho,a)\}

holds, contradicting

•

If

, then, write

\Pi_{n-1}^{0}

and all

,

holds hence
\tau\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists y)\psi(G,a,y)
holds and the following class is large (and therefore includes

\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\cap\{X:(\exists\rho\subseteq X)(\exists y)\tau\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\psi(\tau\cup\rho,a,y)\}

As

, there exists some

and some

Therefore,

holds. If

holds by definition of the forcing relation for

formulas, hence

will hold. If

, then, writing
\phi(G,x)
as

for some
\Pi_{n-1}^{0}

\psi(G,x,y)
yields that

, hence

(either by

∎

.

Proof.

, since

, with

(n+1)
-generic filter

such that the corresponding set

a computable list of all the

Let

and assume

If

for some

is
(n+1)

, hence

.
∎

enables to reprove the theorem from Monin and Patey

about

(Monin and Patey

6.2
Witness forcing

Throughout this section, fix a
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
set
A
such that
\overline{A}\not\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. The set
A
in this section must be thought of as the complement of the set
A
in
Section

6.1
. Contrary to the previous notions of forcing, witness forcing conditions need a second reservoir of higher complexity.

Definition 6.12

(Condition)
.

Let
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
be the notion of forcing whose conditions are tuples
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
where:

•

\sigma\subseteq A
;

•

X_{n-1}\supseteq X_{n}
;

•

\overline{X_{n}}\not\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
;

•

X_{n-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
and
X_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
.

Given a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
,
X_{n}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
by partition regularity of the class, and

X_{n-1}\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
by its upward-closure. Therefore,
c\upharpoonright{\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}}=(\sigma,X_{n-1})
is a valid
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-condition.
One can think of a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
either as a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1})
, or as a Mathias condition
(\sigma,X_{n})
. The notion of extension follows from both approaches:

Definition 6.13
.

A
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\tau,Y_{n-1},Y_{n})

extends
a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},\allowbreak X_{n})

if
Y_{n-1}\subseteq X_{n-1}
,
Y_{n}\subseteq X_{n}
and
\sigma\preceq\tau\subseteq\sigma\cup X_{n}
.

The notion of cylinder is naturally defined as follows:

Definition 6.14
.

The
cylinder
under a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
is the class

[\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}]=\{G:\sigma\subseteq G\subseteq\sigma\cup(X_{n}\cap A)\}.

The notion of index of a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition is defined accordingly:

Definition 6.15
.

An
index
of a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
is a tuple
\langle\sigma,a,b\rangle
where
a
is an
M_{n-1}
-index of
X_{n-1}
and
b
is an
M_{n}
-index of
X_{n}
.

Despite having a second reservoir,
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-forcing inherits abstractly many properties from
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-forcing. The following commutation lemma shows that any density property over
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-forcing yields a density property over
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-forcing. It follows in particular that
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-forcing inherits the forcing relation for
\Sigma_{k}^{0}
-formulas for
k\leq n
and that
Lemma

5.10
also holds with
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-conditions.

Lemma 6.16
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition and
(\tau,Y_{n-1})\leq c\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
be a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-extension. Then there is a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-extension
d\leq c
such that
d\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}=(\tau,Y_{n-1})
. Furthermore, an index for
d
can be found computably uniformly in an index for
c
and
(\tau,Y_{n-1})
.

Proof.

Since
c
is a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition,
\overline{X_{n}}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. Then,
(\tau,Y_{n-1})
being a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-condition, we have
Y_{n-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\cap\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
, thus
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{Y_{n-1}}
and
\overline{Y_{n-1}}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
.
Combining those two results yields that
\overline{X_{n}}\cup\overline{Y_{n-1}}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
by partition regularity of
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
.
Hence,
d=(\tau,Y_{n-1},X_{n}\cap Y_{n-1})
is a valid
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition. It is clear that
d\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}=(\tau,Y_{n-1})
and that
d\leq c
.

By
Remark

4.18
, an
M_{n}
-index for the set
Y_{n-1}
can be found uniformly computably using an
M_{n-1}
index for
Y_{n-1}
. Thus, by our assumptions on the encodings of Scott ideals, an index for
X_{n}\cap Y_{n-1}
can be found uniformly computably using an
M_{n}
-index for the set
X_{n}
and an
M_{n-1}
-index for the set
Y_{n-1}
. Therefore, an index for
d
can be found uniformly computably in an index for
c
and
(\tau,Y_{n-1})
.
∎

The following forcing relation for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
and
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
formulas is closer to the one from
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n}
-forcing than
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing, in that
\tau
ranges over
X_{n}
instead of
X_{n}\cap A
. Contrary to
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing, the complexity of the forcing relation for
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas does not play any role in the constructions.

Definition 6.17

(Forcing relation)
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition, we define the forcing relation
\Vdash
for
\Sigma_{n+1}^{0}
and
\Pi_{n+1}^{0}
formulas as follows: for
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{n}^{0}
formula,

•

c\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
if there exists some
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\Vdash\phi(G,a)
.

•

c\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
if
(\forall\tau\subseteq X_{n})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
.

As mentioned earlier in
Section

6
, if
\overline{A}\not\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
, then this is witnessed by a large
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
class
\mathcal{U}\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
such that
\overline{A}\not\in\mathcal{U}
. This witness plays an important role in the design of a forcing question with good definitional properties. We therefore define the notion formally and state the complexity of finding such a witness.

Definition 6.18
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition. A
witness
for
c
is a
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
class
\mathcal{U}\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
such that
\overline{X_{n}}\cup\overline{A}\not\in\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 6.19
.

For every
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
, there exists some witness
\mathcal{U}
.
An index for such a class
\mathcal{U}
can uniformly be found using
M_{n}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By partition regularity of
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
,
\overline{X_{n}}\cup\overline{A}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
, otherwise either
\overline{X_{n}}
or
\overline{A}
would be in
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
which is impossible by definition of a condition.

The class
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
being an intersection of
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
large classes, there exists one of those
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
large class
\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathcal{U}\supseteq\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
and such that
\overline{X_{n}}\cup\overline{A}\not\in\mathcal{U}
.

To find such a witness, one first
M_{n}^{\prime}
-computes a set
D_{n-1}\supseteq C_{n-1}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{D_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}=\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. Then, for every
(e,i)\in D_{n-1}
, ask whether for every
\rho\subseteq\overline{X_{n}}\cup\overline{A}
,
\rho\not\in W_{e}^{Z_{i}^{n-1}}
(where
Z_{i}^{n-1}
is the
i
-th element of
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
). This can be done
M_{n}^{\prime}
-computably as
\overline{A}
is
\Sigma_{n+1}^{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{n}
contains
\emptyset^{(n)}
and
\overline{X_{n}}
. One must eventually find such a pair
(e,i)
for which the answer is positive.
∎

We now define forcing questions for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
and
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas in order to preserve hyperimmunities. Contrary to
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing, the set
A
is
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
, hence one cannot ask for some
\rho\subseteq X_{n-1}\cap A
to satisfy some property, as the corresponding question would be too complex. We will therefore use an over-approximation by quantifying universally over all sets. Thankfully, given a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
c
and a witness
\mathcal{U}
, one can restrict the over-approximation to all sets
B
such that
\overline{B}\not\in\mathcal{U}
, as this is the case for
X_{n-1}\cap A
. This refined over-approximation has two benefits: (1) it is still compact, hence yields a forcing question with the appropriate definitional complexity, and (2) for every such set
B
, since
\overline{B}
does not belong to a large class, then
B
must contain many elements, hence one can always ask for a subset of
B
.

Definition 6.20

(Forcing question)
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for
k=n-1
or
k=n
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
-class and define
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
to hold if for every
B\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\overline{B}\not\in\mathcal{U}
, there is a finite
\tau\subseteq B\cap X_{k}
and some
x\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\phi(\sigma\cup\tau,x)
holds if
k=0
or
\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
holds if
k>0
.

The following lemma shows that the forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
and
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
formulas has the appropriate definitional complexity.

Lemma 6.21
.

Let
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for
k=n-1
or
k=n
and let
\mathcal{U}
be a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
-class. For every
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition
c
, the relation
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
.

Proof.

By compactness, the statement
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
is equivalent to the following statement when
k>0
(the case
k=0
is similar), with
\overline{\beta}
representing the bitwise complement of
\beta
:

(\exists\ell)(\forall\beta\in 2^{\ell})([\overline{\beta}]\subseteq\mathcal{U}\vee(\exists\tau\subseteq_{\mathtt{fin}}\beta\cap X_{k})(\exists x)\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x))

The statement
[\overline{\beta}]\subseteq\mathcal{U}
is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
as
\mathcal{U}
is a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
-class. The statement
\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
is
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
by
Lemma

5.6
.
∎

The following lemma states, as usual, that the forcing question meets its specifications.

Lemma 6.22
.

Let
c
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition,
\mathcal{U}
be a witness for
c
and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for
k=n-1
or
k=n
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, then there exists
d\leq c
such that
d\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
.

Deciding whether
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
holds or not, and finding the appropriate extension can be done uniformly in an index of
\mathcal{U}
and

using
M_{n}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Say
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
and let
\phi(G,x)
be a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for
k=n-1
or
k=n
. There are two cases:

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
. By definition of a witness,
\overline{A\cap X_{n}}\notin\mathcal{U}
, hence there exists a finite
\tau\subseteq_{\mathtt{fin}}A\cap X_{n}
and some

such that

. By

, there exists an extension
(\rho,Y_{n-1})\leq(\sigma\cup\tau,X_{n-1}\setminus\{0,\dots,|\tau|\})
forcing
\phi(G,a)
. Therefore,
d=(\rho,Y_{n-1},X_{n}\cap Y_{n-1})
extends
c
and forces
(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

•

If
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
. The following class is
\Pi_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
and non-empty:

\mathcal{C}=\{B\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}:\overline{B}\notin\mathcal{U}\wedge(\forall\tau\subseteq_{\mathtt{fin}}B\cap X_{k})(\forall x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)\}

Therefore, as
\mathcal{M}_{k}
is a Scott ideal, there exists some

. Since

,
\overline{B}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. As
\overline{B}
and
\overline{X_{k}}
are not in
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
,
\overline{B\cap X_{k}}\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. Hence, for

,

is a valid condition extending

such that

and for
k=n
,
d=(\sigma,X_{n-1},B\cap X_{n})
is a valid condition extending
c
such that
d\Vdash(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
.

By

6.21
, the relation
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
, hence can be decided using

. In the first case,
\tau
,

and the extension

can be found

-computably, hence

-computably since

is
\Pi^{0}_{n+1}
and
\emptyset^{(n)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
.
In the second case, an
M_{k}
-index of a tree

such that

can be found computably, and and since

is a Scott code, an
M_{k}
-index of
B
can be found computably in the
M_{k}
-index of
T
. Thus an index of the extension witnessing the second case is found computably in an index of
c
.
∎

Given a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-filter
\mathcal{F}
, we write
[\mathcal{F}]
for
\bigcap_{(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})\in\mathcal{F}}[\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}]

and
\mathcal{F}\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}=\{c\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}:c\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Note that
\mathcal{F}\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
is a
\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}
-filter and that
[\mathcal{F}]\subseteq[\mathcal{F}\upharpoonright\mathbb{P}^{A}_{n-1}]
.
The following proposition is the counterpart of
Proposition

5.12
for the witness forcing.

Proposition 6.23
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
1
-generic
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-filter. There exists a unique set
G_{\mathcal{F}}
belonging to
[\mathcal{F}]
. Furthermore,
G_{\mathcal{F}}
is infinite.

Proof.

For every condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
in
\mathcal{F}
, the cylinder
[\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}]
is a non-empty closed subset of
2^{\mathbb{N}}
. Assume by contradiction that
[\mathcal{F}]=\emptyset
, then as
2^{\mathbb{N}}
is compact, there exists a finite subfamily
E
of
\mathcal{F}
such that

\bigcap_{(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})\in E}[\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}]=\emptyset

By compatibility of a filter, there is a condition
c
extending every member of this family, which yields that the cylinder under
c
is empty, contradiction. Therefore,
[\mathcal{F}]
is non-empty.

For every condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
in
\mathcal{F}
,
[\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}]\subseteq[\sigma,X_{n-1}]
,
hence by
Proposition

5.12
,
[\mathcal{F}]
is a singleton
G_{\mathcal{F}}
, which is infinite as a set.
∎

Proposition 6.24
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a
n
-generic
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-filter. If there exists some
c\in\mathcal{F}
such that
c\Vdash(\forall x)\phi(G,x)
for some
\Sigma_{n}^{0}
formula
\phi(G,x)
, then for every

, there exists some
d\in\mathcal{F}
such that
d\Vdash\phi(G,a)
. Furthermore,
(\forall x)\phi(G_{\mathcal{F}},x)
will hold.

Proof.

A similar proof as the one of
Proposition

5.13
can be used to prove this result, by discarding the reservoir
X_{n-1}
of a condition
(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
.
∎

We are now ready to prove our second abstract construction. When considering a set
M_{n}
such that

, it states the existence of an infinite subset of
A
of low
n+1
degree. Note that
Proposition

6.25
is the same statement as

6.10
.

Proposition 6.25
.

There exists an infinite subset
H\subseteq A
such that
H^{(n+1)}\leq_{T}M_{n}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of
Proposition

6.10
:

An
(n+1)
-generic
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-filter
\mathcal{F}
can be build effectively in

using

6.19
to find suitable over-approximations

of the class

, and using

6.22
and

to build extensions deciding every

.

By

6.23
, there exists an infinite

-computable set

. Furthermore, by

and

, every property forced by

∎

6.3
Applications

First-jump control is often considered as simpler than second-jump control. This is why the former technique preferable, when available. In their seminar paper, Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
4
]
constructed solutions to
{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
using both techniques, to show that it admits weakly low and a low
2
basis.
Very recently, Benham et al.
[
2
]
showed that the second-jump control of Cholak, Jockusch and Slaman
[
4
]
could be extended to
{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
to produce low
2
solutions and asked whether it was also the case for first-jump control.

We now prove that
{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
admits solutions both with
(n+1)
th jump control and
n
th jump control,
to prove a low
n+1
basis and a weakly low
n
basis, respectively. We first start with a direct
(n+1)
th jump control construction.

Theorem 6.26
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
B
be a
\Sigma_{n+1}^{0}
set, then there exists an infinite set
H
of low
n+1
degree such that
H\subseteq B
or
H\subseteq\overline{B}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.19
, there is a Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n}
of height
n
with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n}
, such that for every
i\leq n
,
M_{i}
is of low degree over
\emptyset^{(i)}
.
By
Proposition

4.21
, it can be enriched with some sets
C_{0},\dots,C_{n-1}
to form a largeness tower of height
n
. There are two cases:

Case 1:
B\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. Then
Proposition

6.10
yields a set
H
such that
H\subseteq B
and
H^{(n+1)}\leq_{T}M_{n}^{\prime}
.

Case 2:
B\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n+1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n+1}}\rangle

Proposition

6.25
applied on the set
\overline{B}
yields a set

such that
H^{(n+1)}\leq_{T}M_{n}^{\prime}
.

Since

is low over

,

and
H
is low
n+1
.
∎

We now turn to the
n
th jump control construction to prove that
{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
admits a weakly low
n
basis. Due to the asymmetry of

sets, contrary to

1.6

Main Theorem
1.6
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. For every
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
set
B
and every set
P
of PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n)}
, there is an infinite set
H\subseteq B
or

such that
H^{(n)}\leq_{T}P
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.19
, there is a Scott tower
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
of height
n-1
with Scott codes
M_{0},\dots,M_{n-1}
, such that for every
i<n
,
M_{i}
is of low degree over
\emptyset^{(i)}
.
Moreover, by
Proposition

3.11
,
P
computes a Scott code
M_{n}
of a Scott ideal
\mathcal{M}_{n}
containing
\emptyset^{(n)}
. Thus,
\mathcal{M}_{0},\dots,\mathcal{M}_{n-1},\mathcal{M}_{n}
forms a Scott tower of height
n
.
By
Proposition

4.21
, it can be enriched with some sets
C_{0},\dots,C_{n-1}
to form a largeness tower of height
n
. There are two cases:

Case 1
:
B\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
.

Lemma 6.27
.

Let
c
be a
\mathbb{P}^{B}_{n-1}
-condition and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for some
k<n
. An index of an extension deciding
(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
can be found
P
-computably uniformly in an index of
c
and
\phi
.

Proof.

Say
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1})
.
Let
\mathcal{V}=\{Z:(\exists\rho\subseteq Z)(\exists x\in\mathbb{N})\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)\}
if
k>0
and otherwise, if
k=0
, let
\mathcal{V}=\{Z:(\exists\rho\subseteq Z)(\exists x\in\mathbb{N})\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)\}
.
We claim that one of the following two statements is true:

(1.a)

there is some
\rho\subseteq X_{n-1}\cap B
and some
x\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
holds if
k>0
or
\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)
if
k=0
;

(1.b)

the class
\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\cap\mathcal{V}
is not large.

Indeed, if (1.b) fails, then by
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
-cohesiveness of
\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}
,
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{V}
. Since
X_{n-1}\cap B\in\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
, there exists some
\rho\subseteq X_{n-1}\cap B
and some
a\in\mathbb{N}
such that either
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,a)
holds if
k>0
or
\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,a)
holds if
k=0
, so we are in case (1.a).

Note that the first statement is
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
as
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{k})
by
Lemma

5.6
, and the second statement is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n})
by
Lemma

4.6
, so since
P\geq_{T}M_{n}
, both events are
P
-c.e. One can then wait
P
-computably for one of the two facts to be witnessed.

In Case 1.a, by
Lemma

5.10
, there exists an extension
d\leq(\sigma\cup\rho,X_{n-1}\setminus\{0,\dots,|\rho|\})\leq c
forcing
(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
.

In Case 1.b, by
Lemma

4.5
, there is a finite set
F\subseteq_{\mathtt{fin}}C_{n-1}
, such that
\mathcal{U}_{F}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\cap\mathcal{V}
is not large. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{P}_{k}
be the
\Pi^{0}_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
class of all
k
-partitions
Y_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Y_{k-1}=\mathbb{N}
such that for every
i<k
, either
Y_{i}\not\in\mathcal{U}_{F}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}
or
Y_{i}\not\in\mathcal{V}
. By assumption, there is some
k
such that
\mathcal{P}_{k}\neq\emptyset
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
is a Scott ideal, there is a
k
-partition
Y_{0}\sqcup\dots\sqcup Y_{k-1}=\mathbb{N}
in
\mathcal{P}_{k}\cap\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
. By construction and
Lemma

4.14
, since the
k
-partition belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
,
C_{n-1}\oplus M_{n-1}^{\prime}
can computably find some
i<k
such that
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}_{C_{n-1}}
, uniformly in an
M_{n-1}
-index of the
k
-partition. By
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
-cohesiveness of
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}_{C_{n-1}}
, since
X_{n-1},Y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}
and
X_{n-1},Y_{i}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}_{C_{n-1}}
, then
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}_{C_{n-1}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{X_{n-1}}\cap\mathcal{L}_{Y_{i}}
, so

. In particular,
Y_{i}\in\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}_{F}
so
Y_{i}\not\in\mathcal{V}
. It follows that
(\sigma,X_{n-1}\cap Y_{i})
forces
(\forall x)\neg\phi(G,x)
.
∎

Using
Lemma

6.27
, we can construct, computably in
P
, a sequence
c_{0}\geq c_{1}\geq\dots
of
\mathbb{P}^{B}_{n-1}
conditions such that the set

is a

-generic filter. By

5.12
, the corresponding set

is infinite,

computable and satisfies

. Furthermore, by
Proposition

5.13
, every property forced will hold for

, hence

.

Case 2
:
B\notin\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
. Then, there exists a
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
class
\mathcal{U}
such that
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and

.

Let

be the
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
set of chains associated with
\mathcal{U}
. Since

, for every

,

.

Lemma 6.28
.

Let
c
be a
\mathbb{P}^{\overline{B}}_{n-1}
-condition and
\phi(G,x)
a
\Pi_{k}^{0}
formula for some

. An index of an extension deciding

can be found
P
-computably uniformly in an index of
c
and
\phi
.

Proof.

Say
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1})
.
Consider
\mathcal{V}
the same class as in
Lemma

6.27
and let
W_{\mathcal{V}}
be the
\Sigma_{1}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{n-1})
set of chains associated with
\mathcal{V}
.

We claim that one of the following two cases holds:

(2.a)

There is some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\rho_{0},\dots,\rho_{\ell-1}\in W_{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\rho_{i}\subseteq X_{n-1}
for all

and for every
\mu\in\rho_{0}\times\dots\times\rho_{\ell-1}
(seen as finite sets of integers), there exists some
\rho\subseteq\mu
and some

such that
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,x)
holds if
k>0
or
\phi(\sigma\cup\rho,x)
holds if
k=0
;

(2.b)

\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\cap\mathcal{V}
is not large.

Indeed, if
(2.b)
fails, then by
Lemma

4.11
,
\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\cap\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{U}
is large. It follows that
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{V}\cap\mathcal{U}
. In particular, as

and
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle
is partition regular, there exists a depth
p
such that for every partition
Y_{0}\cup Y_{1}=\{0,\dots,p-1\}\cap X_{n-1}
, there exists some side
i<2
and some
\rho\in W_{\mathcal{U}}
and
\rho^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{V}}
such that
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\subseteq Y_{i}
. Fix
\rho_{0},\dots,\rho_{\ell-1}
be all such
\rho\in W_{\mathcal{U}}
. We claim that this family satisfies
(2.a)
. Indeed, let
\mu\in\rho_{0}\times\dots\times\rho_{\ell-1}
, and consider the partition
\mu\cup(\overline{\mu}\cap X_{n-1}\cap\{0,\dots,d-1\})=X_{n-1}\cap\{0,\dots,d-1\}
, by definition of the
(\rho_{k})_{k<\ell}
, there exists some
k<\ell
and some
\rho^{\prime}\in W_{\mathcal{V}}
such that
\rho_{k},\rho^{\prime}\subseteq\mu
or
\rho_{k},\rho^{\prime}\subseteq\overline{\mu}
. As
\mu\in\rho_{0}\times\dots\times\rho_{\ell-1}
, it is not possible for

to be a subset of
\overline{\mu}
for any

. Hence, we are in the first case and, as

, we have found some
\rho^{\prime}\subseteq\mu

such that

.

Note that the first case is

and the second is

. One can then

-computably wait for one of the two facts to be witnessed.

In Case 2.a, since

, there exists some

, there exists an extension

can find one satisfying the

.

In Case 2.b, as in Case 1.b, there is a set

such that the extension

forces

and such an extension can be found computably in

.

∎

Similarly, we can
P
computably construct an infinite set

such that

using

. Thus, the theorem is proved.

∎

Corollary 6.29
.

Let
n\geq 1
and let
A
be a
\Sigma_{n+1}^{0}
set, then there exists an infinite set
H
of low
n+1
degree such that
H\subseteq A
or
H\subseteq\overline{A}
.

Recall that the Ginsburg-Sands theorem
[
19
]
restricted to

-spaces states that every infinite topological space has an infinite subspace homeomorphic to either the discrete or the cofinite topology on
\mathbb{N}
. This theorem was studied by Benham et al.
[
2
]
, who proved that it is equivalent over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
to
\mathsf{COH}+{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
. Let
\mathsf{GST}_{1}
be the Ginsburg-Sands theorem for
T_{1}
-spaces. Benham et al.
[
2
]
asked whether
\mathsf{GST}_{1}
admits a weakly low basis. We answer positively through the following theorems:

Proposition 6.30
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Let
\mathsf{P}_{0},\dots,\mathsf{P}_{k-1}
be
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-problems admitting a weakly low

Then for every set
Q
of PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n)}
, there is an
\omega
-model
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{P}_{0}+\dots+\mathsf{P}_{k-1}

such that for every set

,

.

Proof.

Fix
Q
of PA degree over
\emptyset^{(n)}
. By Scott
[
38
]
,

computes a Scott code of a Scott ideal
\mathcal{N}
containing
\emptyset^{(n)}
. Since
\mathsf{P}_{0},\dots,\mathsf{P}_{k-1}
admit a weakly low
n

\mathcal{M}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}\subseteq\dots
of countable

such that

•

is the

-model whose second-order part are the computable sets;

•

\mathcal{M}_{i}
is topped by a set

such that

•

For every

and every instance

of

in

, there is some

such that
\mathcal{M}_{j}

contains a
\mathsf{P}_{s}
-solution to

.

Then

is an

-model of

such that for every set

,

, hence

.
∎

Corollary 6.31
.

Fix

. Let
\mathsf{P}_{0},\dots,\mathsf{P}_{k-1}
and

be
\Pi^{1}_{2}

.

Let

be a computable instance of

and let

be of PA degree over

.
By

, there is an

-model

of

,

. In particular,

, so there is a

-solution

to

. In particular,

.

Note that

also holds when the implication

.
The following corollary states that

Corollary 6.32
.

For every set

7.1
Disjunctive forcing

The disjunctive notion of forcing is a variant of Mathias forcing commonly used in the reverse mathematics of Ramsey-type theorems, due to the intrinsic disjunctive nature of the statements. Fix a
\Delta^{0}_{n+1}
set
A
such that
A
and
\overline{A}
both belong to
\langle\mathcal{U}_{C_{n-1}}^{\mathcal{M}_{n-1}}\rangle

We shall see thanks to

that a disjunctive condition is nothing but two main forcing conditions sharing a reservoir. Thus, disjunctive forcing inherits many properties of the main forcing, and in particular, each side admits a non-disjunctive forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
with the good definitional properties. In particular, if

is

-hyperimmune, then every sufficiently generic filter

will yield two infinite sets

such that

will be both
G_{0}^{(n)}
-hyperimmune and

-hyperimmune. The only purpose of this disjunctive notion of forcing is then to design a disjunctive forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas with the good definitional properties. Then, if

is

-hyperimmune, it will be either

-hyperimmune, or
G_{1}^{(n-1)}
-hyperimmune.

Definition 7.3

(Condition)
.

Let

Let

(Forcing question)

7.2
Compact forcing questions

As explained in
Section

2
, there is a close relationship between preservation of hyperimmunities and the existence of compact forcing questions with good definitional properties.
For the purpose of
Main Theorem

1.8
, one needs to use notions of forcing whose forcing questions for
\Sigma_{k+1}^{0}
-formulas
are
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
-preserving and
\Sigma^{0}_{k+1}
-compact for
k\in\{n-1,n\}
.
The proof of
Main Theorem

1.8
uses the disjunctive forcing (
\mathbb{D}^{A}_{n}
) if
A
and
\overline{A}
both belong to the appropriate partition regular class, and the witness forcing (
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
or
\mathbb{W}^{\overline{A}}_{n}
) if either fails. The disjunctive forcing inherits its forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas from the main forcing (
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
and
\mathbb{M}^{\overline{A}}_{n}
) and defines a new disjunctive forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas. The witness forcing defines new forcing questions for both
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas and
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas.

In this section, we study the forcing questions of the main forcing, the disjunctive forcing, and the witness forcing, and prove that
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas, the disjunctive
\mathbb{D}^{A}_{n}
-forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas, and the
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-forcing questions for both
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formulas and
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas are all compact.

Remark 7.9
.

\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-compactness of a forcing question states that if
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
for some
\Pi^{0}_{n}
-formula
\phi
, then there is some bound
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x\leq k)\phi(G,x)
. Traditionally, bounded quantification is treated as syntactic sugar, where
(\exists x\leq y)\phi(x)
is translated into
(\exists x)[x\leq y\wedge\phi(x)]
and
(\forall x\leq y)\phi(x)
becomes
(\forall x)[x\leq y\rightarrow\phi(x)]
. However, due to the lack of robustness of the forcing question, it might be that two logically equivalent formulas do not yield the same answer.

To circumvent this issue, in the case of of
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-compactness, bounded existentials will be treated natively, requiring to define the forcing question for formulas of the form
(\exists x\leq k)\phi(G,x)
where
\phi
is
\Pi^{0}_{n}
. The definition of
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x\leq k)\phi(G,x)
will be very similar to that of
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
, except that the existential quantifier for
x
will be bounded by
k
accordingly. The lemma stating that the forcing question meets its specifications remains true when working with bounded formulas.

We start with the main forcing.

Lemma 7.10
.

In
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing, the
\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}
relation for
\Sigma_{n+1}^{0}
formulas is compact, i.e., if
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
for some
\Pi_{n}^{0}

\phi(G,x)
, then there exists some bound
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x\leq\ell)\phi(G,x)
.

Proof.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1})
and assume
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}(\exists x)\phi(G,x)
holds for some
\Pi_{n}^{0}

\phi(G,x)
. There exists some
a\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\rho\subseteq X_{n-1}\cap A
such that
\sigma\cup\rho\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\phi(G,a)
, therefore

.
∎

.

8
Conservation theorems

We now turn to the last main application of the previous notions of forcing: conservation theorems.
The goal of this section is to prove the following main theorem:

Main Theorem

.

Let

.
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is

The techniques will be very similar to the standard realm, but working with a formalized version of the notions of forcing in models of weak arithmetic. We start with a short introduction to the techniques of

-conservation over

8.1
Conservation over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}

The standard model-theoretic approach for proving that a theory
T_{2}
is
\Gamma
-conservative over a theory

is the following:

•

First, assume by contrapositive that
T_{1}
does not prove some formula

•

Using Gödel completeness theorem, there exists some model
\mathcal{M}
of
T_{1}+\neg\phi
.

•

From this model, construct another model
\mathcal{N}
, this time of
T_{2}+\neg\phi
.

•

Therefore,
T_{2}
does not prove
\phi
either.

The heart of these proofs lies in the construction of the model of

. In this article,
\Gamma
will be the set of all the
\Pi_{1}^{1}
-formulas. This allows to easily ensure that the model constructed will be a model of
\neg\phi
: this came free if the model constructed is an

-extension of the initial model.

Definition 8.1
.

A model
\mathcal{N}=(N,T,+^{\mathcal{N}},\times^{\mathcal{N}},<^{\mathcal{N}},0^{\mathcal{N}},1^{\mathcal{N}})

3

3

8.2
Merging forcing questions

As mentioned in
Section

2
, preservation of
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-induction is closely related to the existence of a
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-preserving,
(\Sigma^{0}_{n},\Pi^{0}_{n})
-merging forcing question. A forcing question is
left
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-extremal
if for every condition
c
and every
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formula
\varphi(G)
,
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G)
if and only if
c
forces
\varphi(G)
. It is
right
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-extremal
if for every condition
c
and every
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-formula
\varphi(G)
,
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}\varphi(G)
if and only if
c
does not force
\neg\varphi(G)
.
Any left or right
\Sigma^{0}_{n}
-extremal forcing question is
(\Sigma^{0}_{n},\Pi^{0}_{n})
-merging.

The goal being to prove that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
, one needs to consider the merging properties of the forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas for the main and witness forcing.
The forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas in the
\mathbb{M}^{A}_{n}
-forcing is right
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-extremal, hence is
(\Sigma^{0}_{n+1},\Pi^{0}_{n+1})
-merging. On the other hand, the forcing question for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-formulas in the
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-forcing is not
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
-extremal. The following lemma shows that it is however somehow
(\Sigma^{0}_{n+1},\Pi^{0}_{n+1})
-merging, by considering the appropriate witnesses.

Definition 8.8
.

Given a class
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
be the class

\{X:(\forall X_{0}\cup X_{1}\supseteq X)(\exists i<2)X_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

Note that if the class
\mathcal{A}
is open, then so is
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
.
Moreover, there is a computable function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{U}_{g(e)}^{X}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U}_{e}^{X})
for every index
e\in\mathbb{N}
and every oracle
X
. Also note that if
\mathcal{A}
is large, then so is
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
. Indeed,
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{A})
.

Lemma 8.9
.

Let
c=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n})
be a
\mathbb{W}^{A}_{n}
-condition and let
\mathcal{U}
be a witness for
c
.
Let
\varphi(G),\psi(G)
be two
\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
formulas such that

c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U})}\varphi(G)
and
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}\psi(G)
. Then there exists an extension
d\leq c

such that
d\Vdash\varphi(G)
and
d\Vdash\neg\psi(G)
.

Proof.

Since
c\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}\psi(G)
, the proof of
Lemma

6.22
gives us a set
B\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
such that
\overline{B}\notin\mathcal{U}
and such that
e=(\sigma,X_{n-1},X_{n}\cap B)
is a valid condition extending
c
and forcing
\neg\psi(G)
.

Write
\varphi(G)=(\exists x)\theta(G,x)
for some
\Pi_{n}^{0}
formula
\theta(G,x)
. We claim that
e\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}\varphi(G)
: let
D\in 2^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\overline{D}\notin\mathcal{U}
. For every such
D
,
\overline{D}\cup\overline{B}\notin\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U})
(otherwise, either
\overline{D}
or
\overline{B}
would be in
\mathcal{U}
), hence, as
c\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U})}\varphi(G)
, there exist some finite
\tau\subseteq D\cap(X_{n}\cap B)
and some
x\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sigma\cup\tau\operatorname{{?}{\nvdash}}\neg\theta(G,x)
. Therefore,
e\operatorname{{?}{\vdash}}^{\mathcal{U}}\varphi(G)
and by
Lemma

6.22
, there exists some extension
d\leq e
forcing
\varphi(G)
.
∎

Proposition 8.15
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{n+2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is not
\Pi_{1}^{1}
conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}

Proof.

Given a family of formulas
\Gamma
, let
\mathsf{C}\Gamma
be the statement that no
\Gamma
-formula defines a total injection with bounded range. This scheme was introduced by Seetapun et Slaman
[
39
]
.
The principle
\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\not\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
, see Groszek and Slaman
[
20
]

2.

\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2}\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
. Indeed, by
[
21
, Theorem 2.23]
,
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2}
is equivalent to the pigeonhole principle for
\Sigma^{0}_{n+2}
(
\mathsf{PHP}(\Sigma^{0}_{n+2})
) which immediately implies
\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
.

3.

\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\wedge\neg\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
, see Kołodziejczyk et al.
[
26
]

The first item yields that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\not\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
, the first-order part of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
being
\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{n+1}
, hence
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\not\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
.
The two others items yield that
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2}\vee(\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\wedge\neg\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1})\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
, hence
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\wedge(\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\to\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2})\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
. By Chong, Lempp and Yang
[
5
]
,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{n+2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\vdash\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\to\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2}
, so
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}+{\Delta^{0}_{n+2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\vdash\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\wedge(\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}\to\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+2})
, hence
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}+{\Delta^{0}_{n+2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}\vdash\mathsf{C}\Sigma_{n+2}
.
Therefore,
{\Delta^{0}_{n+2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is not
\Pi_{1}^{1}
conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{n+1}
.
∎

9
Open questions

Many questions remain open concerning the pigeonhole hierarchy in reverse mathematics. Due to its connections with Ramsey’s theorem for pairs,
{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
was significantly more studied than the other levels. It was proven not to imply
\mathsf{COH}
over non-standard models by Chong, Slaman and Yang
[
6
]
, and more recently over
\omega
-models by Monin and Patey
[
33
]
. The question is open for higher levels of the hierarchy:

Question 9.1
.

Does
{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
imply
\mathsf{COH}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
?

The first-order part of
{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
received a lot of attention. It is known to follow strictly from
\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
and to imply
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
. However, the following question is one of the most important questions of modern reverse mathematics:

Question 9.2
.

Is
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}

\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
?

The answer is conjectured to be negative. By
Proposition

8.15
,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{3}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is not
\Pi_{1}^{1}
conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
, but it might still be the case for
{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
.

Question 9.3
.

Is
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}

\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
?

In
Section

8
, we proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{n}
, for
n\geq 2
. It is however still unknown whether any sufficiently high level of the pigeonhole hierarchy even implies
\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
.

Question 9.4
.

Does
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
imply
\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
?

The well-foundedness principle
\mathsf{WF}(\alpha)
states that there is no infinite decreasing sequence of ordinals smaller than
\alpha
. In particular,
\mathsf{WF}(\omega^{\omega})
admits several characterizations, among which the statement of the totality of Ackermann’s function (see Kreuzer and Yokoyama
[
27
]
). The principle
\mathsf{WF}(\omega^{\omega})
is known to follow strictly from
\mathsf{I}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
and be incomparable with
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
(see Simpson
[
42
]
).
Patey and Kokoyama
[
36
]
proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Delta^{0}_{2}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
is
\forall\Pi^{0}_{3}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, hence does not imply
\mathsf{WF}(\omega^{\omega})
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
.

Question 9.5
.

Does
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+{\Sigma^{0}_{n}}\mbox{-}\mathsf{Subset}
imply
\mathsf{WF}(\omega^{\omega})
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
?

A positive answer to
Question

9.4
would yield a positive answer to
Question

9.5
, while a positive answer to
Question

9.5
for
n=2
would yield a negative answer to
Question

9.3
.
