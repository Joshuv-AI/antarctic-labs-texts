The Schinzel-Szekeres function
Andreas Weingartner

Abstract
. We derive asymptotic estimates for distribution functions related to the Schinzel-Szekeres function. These results are then used in three different applications: the longest simple path in the divisor graph, a problem of Erdős about a sum of reciprocals, and the small sieve of Erdős and Ruzsa.



1.
Introduction

The Schinzel-Szekeres function is defined as

F(n)=\begin{cases}1&\quad(n=1)\\
\max\{d\,P^{-}(d):d|n,\ d>1\}&\quad(n\geq 2),\end{cases}

where
P^{-}(d)
denotes the smallest prime factor of
d>1
and
P^{-}(1):=\infty
.
This function appears implicitly in
[
11
]
.
It plays a role in various applications,
such as a problem of Erdős considered by Schinzel and Szekeres
[
11
]
,
the small sieve of Erdős and Ruzsa
[
6
]
,
the distribution of divisors
[
7
,
12
,
16
]
, and the length of the longest simple path in the divisor graph
[
8
,
13
]
.
For the corresponding counting function

A(x)=|\{n\geq 1:F(n)\leq x\}|,

Schinzel and Szekeres
[
11
]
showed that
A(x)=o(x)
as
x\to\infty
.
Ruzsa
[
6
]
found that there is a constant
c>0
such that
A(x)\leq\frac{x}{\log^{c}x}
.
Tenenbaum
[
12
,
13
]
improved this to
A(x)=\frac{x}{\log x}(\log\log x)^{O(1)}

and Saias obtained
A(x)\asymp\frac{x}{\log x}
.

Theorem 1
.

For
x\geq 2
, we have

A(x)=\frac{ax}{\log x}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right)\right),

where the constant
a=1.53796...
is given by

a=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma}}\left(1-\gamma+\int_{1}^{\infty}A(t)g(t)\frac{dt}{t^{2}}\right),

\gamma
is Euler’s constant and

g(t)=\biggl(\sum_{p\leq t}\frac{\log p}{p-1}+\gamma-\log t\biggr)\prod_{p\leq t}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

1.1.
The longest simple path in the divisor graph

The divisor graph of order
n
consists of vertices
1,2,\ldots,n
and an edge between vertices
j,k
if and only if
j|k
or
k|j
.
Let
f(n)
be the maximal number of vertices in a simple path of the divisor graph of order
n
.
Improving on earlier work by Pollington
[
3
]
, Pomerance
[
4
]
and Tenenbaum
[
13
]
, Saias
[
8
,
10
]
showed that
f(n)\asymp\frac{n}{\log n}
and
f(n)\geq A(n/2)>0.306\frac{n}{\log n}
for
n\geq n_{0}
.
With Theorem
1
, we obtain the following lower bound.

Corollary 1
.

For all sufficiently large
n
,

f(n)>0.76898\frac{n}{\log n}.

1.2.
A problem of Erdős

Erdős considered the question of how large
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n}
can be for a set
\mathcal{S}
of integers up to
x

with the property

(1)

m,n\in\mathcal{S}\text{ and }n\neq m\ \Rightarrow\ \lcm(m,n)>x.

Define

R(x)=\max_{\mathcal{S}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n},

where the maximum is taken over all sets of integers
\mathcal{S}\subset\{2,3,\ldots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}
that have the
\lcm
property (
1
).

Erdős showed that
R(x)<2
and conjectured
[
11
]
that
R(x)<1+o(1)
as
x\to\infty
. Lehman
[
2
]
improved this to
R(x)<\frac{7}{6}+\frac{1}{6x}
.
It seems that there are only two known cases where
R(x)>1
, namely
R(5)=\frac{31}{30}
from
\mathcal{S}=\{2,3,5\}
,
and
R(11)=\frac{4699}{4620}
from
\mathcal{S}=\{3,4,5,7,11\}
.
Schinzel and Szekeres
[
11
]
showed that
R(x)\leq\frac{31}{30}
, with equality being attained only at
x=5
.
Moreover, they proved that
R(x)<c+o(1)
, where
c=1.017262...
.
They
[
11
, Theorem 3]
also established the lower bound
R(x)>1-o(1)
, by way of
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{B}(x)
,
where

\mathcal{B}(x)=\{n\leq x:F(n)>x\text{ and }F(d)\leq x\text{ for all }d|n,d<n\}.

It is not difficult to see
[
11
, Proof of Thm. 3]
that
\mathcal{B}(x)
has the
\lcm
property (
1
).

Theorem 2
.

For
x\geq 2
, we have

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}(x)}\frac{1}{n}=1-\frac{\delta}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\right),

where
\delta:=a+\gamma-1-\beta=0.560...
, the constant
a
is as in Theorem
1
and
\beta
is given by (
44
).
More precisely,
0.560374<\delta<0.560579
.

Theorem
2
allows us to
improve the lower bound
R(x)>1-o(1)
of Schinzel and Szekeres to

R(x)>1-\frac{\delta+o(1)}{\log x},\quad\delta=0.560...

We can improve this further by making a small change to
\mathcal{B}(x)
, based on the two known instances where
R(x)>1
.
Let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(x)
be the set obtained from
\mathcal{B}(x)
, by replacing every
n\in(\frac{x}{6},\frac{x}{5}]\cap\mathcal{B}(x)

by
\{2n,3n,5n\}
, and every
n\in(\frac{x}{12},\frac{x}{11}]\cap\mathcal{B}(x)
by
\{3n,4n,5n,7n,11n\}
.
Then
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(x)\subset[1,x]
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(x)
has the
\lcm
property (
1
).

Theorem 3
.

For
x\geq 2
, we have

R(x)\geq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(x)}\frac{1}{n}=1-\frac{\kappa}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\right),

where
\kappa=0.543...
is given by (
48
). More precisely,
0.543595<\kappa<0.543804
.

It is clear that any other examples of
R(x)>1
, besides
x=5,11
, if they exist,
would lead to further small improvements. In the absence of such examples, it seems plausible
that Theorem
3
is best possible.

Question 1
.

Is it true that

R(x)=1-\frac{\kappa+o(1)}{\log x}
?

1.3.
The small sieve of Erdős and Ruzsa

Here the question is how small we can make the number of unsieved integers up to
x
,
after removing multiples of a set
\mathcal{S}
with the property

(2)

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n}\leq 1,\quad 1\notin\mathcal{S}.

Define

H(x)=\min_{\mathcal{S}}|\{n\leq x:s\nmid n\text{ for all }s\in\mathcal{S}\}|,

where the minimum is over all sets
\mathcal{S}
satisfying (
2
).
Ruzsa
[
6
]
showed that
\frac{x}{\log x}\ll H(x)<\frac{x}{(\log x)^{c}}
for some constant
c>0
.
Tenenbaum
[
12
]
improved the upper bound to
H(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log x}(\log\log x)^{2}
, and Saias
[
8
]

established
H(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log x}
via the inequality

H(x)\leq\max(A(x),B(x)+\sqrt{x})
, where
B(x)=|\mathcal{B}(x)|
.

Theorem
2
shows that
\mathcal{B}(x)
satisfies (
2
), for all sufficiently large
x
.
After removing multiples of members of
\mathcal{B}(x)
, the unsieved integers up to
x
are exactly the members of

\mathcal{A}(x)=\{n\geq 1:F(n)\leq x\}.

Thus

H(x)\leq A(x)=\frac{(a+o(1))x}{\log x}<\frac{1.53797x}{\log x},

by Theorem
1
, for all sufficiently large
x
.
The term
-\frac{\delta}{\log x}
in Theorem
2
allows us to improve on this upper bound.
For
\frac{1}{x}\leq\tau\leq 1
, define

\mathcal{B}_{\tau}(x)=\mathcal{B}(\tau x)\cup\{p\in(\tau x,x]:p\text{ prime}\},

as in Ruzsa
[
6
, Proof of the upper bound in Theorem I]
.
After removing multiples of members of
\mathcal{B}_{\tau}(x)
, the unsieved integers up to
x
are exactly the members of

\mathcal{A}(\tau x)
. It follows that

H(x)\leq H^{*}(x):=\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(x)}A(\tau x),

where

\mathcal{T}(x)=\Bigl\{\tau\geq 1/x:\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{\tau}(x)}\frac{1}{n}\leq 1\Bigr\}.

With Theorems
1
and
2
, we will establish an estimate for
H^{*}(x)
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
a
and
\delta
be as in Theorems
1
and
2
.
For
x\geq 2
, we have

H(x)\leq H^{*}(x)=\frac{ae^{-\delta}x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\right),

where
ae^{-\delta}\approx 0.878
. More precisely,
0.877992<ae^{-\delta}<0.878171
.

Corollary 2
.

For all sufficiently large
x
, we have

H(x)<\frac{0.879x}{\log x}.

For
2\leq x\leq 40
, we find that
H^{*}(x)-H(x)\in\{0,1\}
.
It seems plausible that
H(x)\sim H^{*}(x)
as
x\to\infty
.

Question 2
.

Is it true that

H(x)\sim\frac{ae^{-\delta}x}{\log x}

as
x\to\infty
?

Ruzsa
[
6
]
also considered the more general problem of estimating

H(x,z)=\min_{\mathcal{S}}|\{n\leq x:s\nmid n\text{ for all }s\in\mathcal{S}\}|,

where the minimum now is over all sets
\mathcal{S}
that satisfy

(3)

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n}\leq z,\quad 1\notin\mathcal{S}.

Let

H^{*}(x,z):=\min_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}(x,z)}A(\tau x),

where

\mathcal{T}(x,z)=\Bigl\{\tau\geq 1/x:\sum_{n\in\mathcal{B}_{\tau}(x)}\frac{1}{n}\leq z\Bigr\}.

Theorem 5
.

Let
a
and
\delta
be as in Theorems
1
and
2
.
Let
\mu>-\delta
be constant. For
x\geq 2
, we have

H\left(x,1+\frac{\mu}{\log x}\right)\leq H^{*}\left(x,1+\frac{\mu}{\log x}\right)=\frac{ae^{-\delta-\mu}x}{\log x}+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/2}}\right).

Let
Z>1
be fixed. Uniformly for
1\leq z\leq Z
,
x\geq 2
,

H\left(x,z\right)\asymp\frac{x^{\exp(1-z)}}{\log x}.

The last estimate improves Ruzsa’s result
[
6
, Theorem I]

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log H(x,z)}{\log x}=e^{1-z}.

1.4.
Integers with dense divisors

The significance of
F(n)
to the distribution of divisors comes from Tenenbaum’s identity
[
12
, Lemma 2.2]

\frac{F(n)}{n}=\max_{1\leq i<k}\frac{d_{i+1}}{d_{i}},

where
1=d_{1}<d_{2}<\cdots<d_{k}=n
is the increasing sequence of divisors of
n
.
Thus

D(x,y):=|\{n\leq x:F(n)/n\leq y\}|

counts the number of integers up to
x
whose sequence of divisors grows by factors of at most
y
.
It may appear that the asymptotic estimates for
D(x,y)
in
[
16
]
should suffice to derive Theorem
1
, but we have not been able to do so.
Instead, we derive Theorem
1
from new estimates for the more general

D(x,y,z):=|\{n\leq x:F(n)/n\leq y,\ P^{-}(n)>z\}|,

which has been considered previously by Saias
[
9
]
and the author
[
14
]
.

In Section
2
, we state these new estimates for
D(x,y,z)
, which are proved in sections
3
through
5
,
with methods developed in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
for the study of
D(x,y)
.
As a consequence, in Section
6
, we are able to derive results, not only for
A(x)
, but for the more general

A(x,y,z):=|\{n\leq x:F(n)\leq xy,P^{-}(n)>z\}|,

which are needed for the proofs of Theorems
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
.
In Section
7
we give an algorithm for computing the constant factor in the asymptotic for
A(x,y,z)
.
We prove Theorem
2
in Section
8
and Theorem
3
in Section
9
.
Section
10
contains the proofs of Theorems
4
and
5
.

2.
Estimates for
D(x,y,z)
and
A(x,y,z)

Define

u=\frac{\log x}{\log y},\quad v=\frac{\log x}{\log z},\quad r=\frac{u}{v}=\frac{\log z}{\log y}.

In
[
14
, Theorem 1]
we found that

(4)

D(x,y,z)=\frac{xd(u,v)}{\log z}+\frac{y}{\log y}-\frac{z}{\log z}+O\left(\frac{x}{\log^{2}z}\right),\quad(x\geq y\geq z\geq 3/2),

where the function
d(u,v)
is defined by a certain difference-differential equation
[
14
, Eq. 3]
.
This definition of
d(u,v)
is based on the Buchstab identity

(5)

D(x,y,z)=1+\sum_{z<p\leq y}D(x/p,py,p-0),

which follows from grouping the integers counted in
D(x,y,z)
according to their smallest prime factor
p
.
In
[
15
,
16
]
we used a different kind of functional equation (i.e. Lemma
1
with
z=1
) to show that

D(x,y)=x\eta(y)d(u)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right)\right)\quad(x\geq y\geq 1),

where
\eta(y)=1+O(1/\log y)
and

d(u)
is given by
d(u)=0
for
u<0
and

(6)

d(u)=1-\int_{0}^{\frac{u-1}{2}}\frac{d(t)}{t+1}\ \omega\left(\frac{u-t}{t+1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\qquad(u\geq 0).

Here and throughout,
\omega(u)
denotes Buchstab’s function.
Equation (
6
) can be solved with Laplace transforms to obtain
[
15
, Theorem 1]

(7)

d(u)=\frac{C}{u+1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)\right),\quad C=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\gamma}}=2.280291...

The following theorems generalize these results to
d(u,v)
and
D(x,y,z)
.

Theorem 6
.

For
v\geq u\geq 1
we have

\begin{split}d(u,v)&=e^{-\gamma}(1-u/v)\,d(u)\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{uv}\right)\right)=\frac{Ce^{-\gamma}(1-u/v)}{u+1}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)\right).\end{split}

We will use the following notation throughout. Let

\Pi(z):=\prod_{p\leq z}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right),\qquad\Sigma(z):=\sum_{p\leq z}\frac{\log p}{p-1},

and

(8)

\mathcal{E}(z):=\sup_{t\geq z}\left|\Sigma(t)+\gamma-\log t\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}\exp\left\{-(\log z)^{3/5-\varepsilon}\right\},

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, by the prime number theorem.

Theorem 7
.

For
x\geq y\geq z\geq 3/2
,

(9)

D(x,y,z)=xd(u,v)e^{\gamma}\Pi(z)+\frac{x\beta_{y,z}}{\log xy}+\frac{y}{\log y}-\frac{z}{\log z}+O\left(\frac{x\log y}{\log^{2}x\log z}\right),

where
\beta_{y,z}=c_{y,z}-C\Pi(z)\log(y/z)
and
c_{y,z}
is as in Theorem
8
. We have

(10)

|\beta_{y,z}|\leq C\Pi(z)(\mathcal{E}(z)+\mathcal{E}(y)).

Note that Theorem
7
implies (
4
).
Let
\chi(n)
be the characteristic function of the set

(11)

\mathcal{D}:=\{n\in\mathbb{N}:F(n)/n\leq y,P^{-}(n)>z\}.

Let
\pi(y,z)
be the number of primes

satisfying
z<p\leq y
, that is

\pi(y,z):=\sum_{z<p\leq y}1.

Theorem 8
.

For
x\geq y\geq z\geq 3/2
and

we have

(12)

and, for

3.
Proof of Theorem
6

Let

\Phi(x,z)=|\{n\leq x:P^{-}(n)>z\}|.

Note that for
n\geq 2
with prime factorization
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
, where
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots\leq p_{k}
, we have

\frac{F(n)}{n}\leq y\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad p_{j+1}\leq y\prod_{1\leq i\leq j}p_{i}\quad(0\leq j<k).

Lemma 1
.

For
x\geq 1
,
y\geq z\geq 1
we have

\Phi(x,z)=\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\Phi(x/n,yn).

Proof.

Every
m
counted in
\Phi(x,z)
factors uniquely as
m=nr
, where
n\in\mathcal{D}
and
P^{-}(r)>ny
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

For
x\geq 1
,
y\geq z\geq 1
we have

D(x,y,z)-1=\Phi(x,z)-\Phi(x,y)-\sum_{z<n\leq\sqrt{x/y}}\chi(n)\bigl(\Phi(x/n,yn)-1\bigr)

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
1
, since
1\in\mathcal{D}
,
2\leq n\leq z\Rightarrow n\notin\mathcal{D}
, and
n>\sqrt{x/y}\Rightarrow\Phi(x/n,yn)=1
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For
x\geq 1
,
z\geq 3/2
, we have

\Phi(x,z)-1=x\Pi(z)+\frac{x(w(v)-e^{-\gamma})}{\log z}-\left.\frac{z}{\log z}\right|_{x\geq z}+O\left(\frac{xe^{-v/3}}{\log^{2}z}\right),

where
v=\frac{\log x}{\log z}
and
w(v)
is Buchstab’s function.

Proof.

This is Lemma 6 of
[
19
]
.
∎

We write
d_{r}(u):=d(u,u/r)
for
0<r\leq 1
and
d_{0}(u):=e^{-\gamma}d(u)
.

Lemma 4
.

For
u\geq 0
,

must belong to
\mathcal{S}
, with at most
y/\log x
exceptions. Thus

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}\atop n>y}\frac{1}{n}\geq\sum_{y<p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}-\frac{y}{\log x}\cdot\frac{1}{y}=\log\frac{\log x}{\log y}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right).

As before, we must have
y\geq x^{\eta}
for a suitable
\eta>0
, or else the last sum is too large.
Let
U(y,\mathcal{S}):=|\{n\leq y:s\in\mathcal{S}\Rightarrow s\nmid n\}|.
Then

U(y,\mathcal{S})+\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\lfloor y/n\rfloor\geq\lfloor y\rfloor
. Thus

\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}\atop n\leq y}\frac{1}{n}\geq 1-\frac{1+U(y,\mathcal{S})}{y}\geq 1-\frac{1+H(x,z)}{y}\geq 1-O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right).

Thus,

z\geq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n}\geq 1+\log\frac{\log x}{\log y}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right),

that is

y\gg x^{e^{1-z}},

which is the desired lower bound.
