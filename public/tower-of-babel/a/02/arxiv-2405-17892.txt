Data-Driven Predictive Control and MPC: Do we achieve optimality?
A.S. Anand, S. Sawant, D. Reinhardt, S. Gros

Abstract
In this paper, we explore the interplay between Predictive Control and closed-loop optimality, spanning from Model Predictive Control to Data-Driven Predictive Control. Predictive Control in general relies on some form of prediction scheme on the real system trajectories. However, these predictions may not accurately capture the real system dynamics, for e.g., due to stochasticity, resulting in sub-optimal control policies. This lack of optimality is a critical issue in case of problems with economic objectives. We address this by providing sufficient conditions on the underlying prediction scheme such that a Predictive Controller can achieve closed-loop optimality. However, these conditions do not readily extend to Data-Driven Predictive Control. In this context of closed-loop optimality, we conclude that the factor distinguishing the approaches within Data-Driven Predictive Control is if they can be cast as a sequential decision-making process or not, rather than the dichotomy of model-based vs. model-free. Furthermore, we show that the conventional approach of improving the prediction accuracy from data may not guarantee optimality.



1
Introduction

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a popular and mature method for the control of complex systems. A core principle in MPC is that the predictions supporting an MPC scheme are based on simulations using an internal model of the real system
(
Rawlings et al., 2017
)
. This model can be based on a state-space description of the system or purely on input-output sequences. In addition, it can be based on first principles (white box), partially data-driven (gray box), or purely data-driven (black box)
(
Mesbah et al., 2022
)
. The accuracy of the MPC predictions can be crucial if the closed-loop performance of the policy produced by the MPC scheme is important
(
Darby and Nikolaou, 2012
)
. This can be a challenge when the real system is stochastic.

The introduction of Machine Learning (ML) into MPC has been intensively studied in the recent literature
(
Maiworm et al., 2021
;
Mesbah et al., 2022
)
. The hope is arguably in part to alleviate the issues related to inaccurate models
(
Hewing et al., 2020
)
. A classic approach is to use ML techniques to improve the MPC model using real-world data, typically via ridge regression
(
Wu et al., 2019
)
. A core issue identified in these approaches is that they do not tie model improvement to the closed-loop performance of the MPC scheme. Rather, they construct a model that best fit the data, hoping that this will translate into improved closed-loop performance. However, this assumption is not well supported in theory and not always verified in practice
(
Gros and Zanon, 2019
;
Piga et al., 2019
)
.

In this paper, we propose sufficient conditions for the MPC model such that the MPC policy achieves optimal closed-loop performance. We then propose an analysis of these conditions, which provides strong insights into when a model that best fits the data in the expected value sense can lead to optimal closed-loop performance. We observe that these proposed conditions are intimately linked to the fact that MPC operates via sequential decision-making. As a result, they apply to all forms of MPC as long as the predictions are based on internal model simulations.

In contrast to MPC, Data-Driven Predictive Control (DDPC) does not build its predictions by simulating a model
(
Krishnan and Pasqualetti, 2021
)
. Instead, it builds predictions directly from past input-output data collected from the real system
(
Yang and Li, 2015
;
Coulson et al., 2019
;
Reinhardt and Gros, 2023
)
. Bypassing the construction of a model to support the predictions is sometimes presented as beneficial on the basis that such direct data-driven predictions are then tailored to the optimization objectives
(
Coulson et al., 2019
)
. However, this position is not well supported in theory nor in practice
(
Reinhardt and Gros, 2023
;
Mattsson and Schön, 2023
;
Fiedler and Lucia, 2021
)
.

More importantly, we observe that DDPC does not, in general, operate via sequential decision-making. In fact, the predictions built in DDPC do not necessarily achieve causality
(
Sader et al., 2023
)
. A crucial consequence of these observations is that the conditions for closed-loop optimality of MPC proposed in this paper do not readily extend to DDPC. This sets DDPC strikingly at odds with other Predictive Control approaches. However, we argue that in the context of closed-loop performance, the distinction between model-based vs. model-free Predictive Control is less relevant than the distinction between sequential decision-making or not.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Sec.
2
introduces the necessary background knowledge, Sec.
3
presents the sufficient condition for closed-loop optimality of an MPC scheme. Sec.
4
discusses its implication in the context of DDPC, and a discussion of the findings and conclusion are presented in Sec.
5
.

2
Background

2.1
Markov Decision Processes

In this paper, we will adopt the definition of closed-loop optimality in the context of discrete infinite-horizon discounted Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
Sutton and Barto (2018)
. The developments proposed in this paper can be readily extended to broader definitions, but we will remain within this framework for the sake of brevity. MDPs assume the system dynamics of the form,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\sim\rho\left(\,.\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\,\right)

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\in\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\in\mathbb{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
are a pair of state and action (or input), respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\in\mathbb{S}
is the successor state.
\rho
is a conditional probability density or, more generally, a conditional probability measure. For a given stage cost
\ell\,:\,\mathbb{S}\,\times\,\mathbb{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the closed-loop performance of a policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}\,:\,\mathbb{S}\,\rightarrow\,\mathbb{A}
is given by:

\displaystyle J\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\right)\,\right|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\right)\right],

(2)

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor modeling the probabilistic lifetime of the system and ensuring the boundedness of the infinite-horizon problem. The expectation
\mathbb{E}
is over the Markov Chain resulting from (
1
) in closed-loop with policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}
. Solving the MDP consists in determining an optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
by minimizing (
2
), i.e.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}\in\Pi}\,J\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}\right).

(3)

In this paper, we assume that (
1
),
\ell
and
\Pi
are such that (
2
) is integrable. Under these assumptions, the solution to the MDP is characterized by the Bellman equations,

\displaystyle Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)+\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[V^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\right)\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right]\,,

(4a)

\displaystyle V^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,\,Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)\,,

(4b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{arg}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,\,Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)\,.

(4c)

where
V\,\text{and }Q
represents value and action-value functions respectively, and
V^{\star}\,\text{and }Q^{\star}
are their optimal values. In the MDP framework, constraints on the state and action can be enforced by assigning infinite values to the stage cost
\ell
for constraint violation. More specifically, if a stage cost
L\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)
is to be minimized by the optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
while respecting a set of constraints
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)\leq 0
, then

\displaystyle\ell\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)=L\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)+\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&\mathrm{if}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)\leq 0\\
\infty&\mathrm{if}&\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)>0\end{array}\right.

is used. We finally observe that equations (
4
) are generally very difficult to solve due to the curse of dimensionality of Dynamic Programming.

2.2
Model Predictive Control

An MPC scheme
(
Rawlings et al., 2017
)
solves the following optimization problem, for a given state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
at time
k
:

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\right)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}

\displaystyle\quad\gamma^{N}T\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\gamma^{k}L\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)\,,

(7b)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{h}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)\leq 0\,,

(7c)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N}\in\mathbb{T}\,.

(7d)

Here
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
is the MPC prediction model, which can be in a classic state-space form, but it can also be an input-output prediction model if

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{i}\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{i-p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i-l}\end{array}\right]^{\top},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}_{k-p},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k-1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k-l}\end{array}\right]^{\top}.

for some
p,l
large enough to make
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
a Markov state, and where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{y}}
are measured outputs of the system. The terminal set
\mathbb{T}
and the terminal cost
T
are typically used to account for the fact that problem (
7
) is solved over a finite horizon
N
, while the MDP is open-ended. We assume throughout this paper that,
V^{\mathrm{MPC}}=\infty
when (
7
) is infeasible. In this paper, we follow the MPC formulation with discounting (
7
), but all the results presented in this paper are valid for the undiscounted case (
\gamma=1
). Note that the choice
\gamma=1
common in MPC places some specific requirements on the stage cost
\ell
for (
2
) to be well-posed.

The solution of (
7
) delivers an input sequence
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\star}_{0,\ldots,N-1}
and the corresponding state sequence
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}_{0,\ldots,N}
that the system is predicted to follow, according to the MPC model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
. Because the model is typically inaccurate, this prediction is usually inexact. To address this, MPC (
7
) is solved at every discrete time
k
, using the latest state of the system
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
. Importantly, only the first input of the sequence, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\star}_{0}
is applied to the real system. Therefore, the MPC scheme (
7
) produces a policy

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\right)=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\star}_{0}

(9)

that assigns for every feasible state
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k}
a corresponding action
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}^{\star}_{0}
implicitly defined by (
7
). In this paper, we aim to clarify how an MPC solves the corresponding MDP (
2
)-(
3
) and discuss sufficient conditions that the underlying predictive mechanism of the MPC scheme should satisfy to achieve
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
.

3
MPC as a solution for MDPs

The goal of this section is to propose formal conditions under which the MPC policy (
9
) achieves closed-loop optimality, i.e. minimizes (
2
) in the presence of stochasticity and model error.

3.1
MPC as an action-value function

In order to form conditions for the closed-loop optimality of MPC, we will detail how an MPC (
9
) delivers an action-value function
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)
and the conditions under which it corresponds to the optimal action-value function
Q^{\star}
of the MDP. We propose the following definition of
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)
:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}\right):=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}

(
7a
)

(10a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\eqref{eq:MPC0:Dyn}-\eqref{eq:MPC0:Boundaries}

(10b)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k}.

(10c)

We observe that (
10
) is identical to MPC (
7
), to the exception of constraint (
10c
), which assigns the initial control input of the MPC. This definition of
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}
is valid in the sense of fundamental Bellman relationships between optimal action-value functions, value functions, and policies, i.e.

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\phantom{\mathrm{arg}}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right),

(11a)

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{arg}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)

(11b)

hold by construction. This implies that MPC scheme (
7
) solves the MDP if the condition:

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)=Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right),\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}

(12)

holds, thereby delivering an optimal policy, the optimal value function, and the optimal action-value function of the MDP. In this case, MPC can provide a full representation of the MDP solution. Condition (
12
) can be relaxed such that the MPC policy matches the optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\pi}}^{\star}
. Then
Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}
needs to match
Q^{\star}
only in the sense of

\displaystyle\mathrm{arg}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)=\mathrm{arg}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\,Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right),\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}.

(13)

Note that (
12
) implies (
13
), but the converse is not true, hence making (
13
) less restrictive than (
12
). While less restrictive, (
13
) falls short of making the MPC scheme a fully satisfying solution of the MDP. Indeed, while an MPC scheme satisfying (
13
) would deliver the optimal policy, it would not predict the expected cost associated to a given state-action pair correctly. It is important to note that (
12
) can hold locally in the actions
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
for all states
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}
, and then deliver the optimal policy, the optimal value function and locally the correct action-value function. Hence, arguably, (
12
) holding locally in
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}
is–for all practical purposes–as valuable as if it holds globally.

An additional case of interest is if (
12
) is relaxed to be valid up to a constant, i.e.

\displaystyle Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)+Q_{0}=Q^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right),\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}

(14)

for some constant
Q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. If the MPC scheme satisfies condition (
14
), then it delivers the optimal policy and the optimal value functions up to a constant. As we will elaborate in the next sections, this case is important to understand the connection between MPC and MDPs. Note that condition (
14
) can be converted to condition (
12
) by simply adding a constant to the MPC costs (
7a
) and (
10a
).

3.2
Conditions for MPC optimality

This section outlines the necessary conditions for an MPC scheme to fulfill (
14
), thereby delivering an optimal policy, along with a correct representation of the MDP solution. We provide the key condition in the following Theorem.

Theorem 1

If the MPC model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
satisfies the equality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\right)\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\,\right]-V^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)\right)=V_{0}

(15)

for a constant
V_{0}
, and
T=V^{\star}
is used in MPC (
10
) then (

) holds.

{pf}

Consider the MPC (

) and modified stage cost

, and terminal cost,
T(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}})=V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}})

and the associated action value function

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{MPC}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}\,\gamma^{N}V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\gamma^{i}\hat{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}))

(16a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\eqref{eq:MPC:Qmodel:const},\eqref{eq:MPC:Qmodel:Aconst}

(16b)

Using (
15
), we get,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\mathrm{MPC}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})=Q^{\mathrm{MPC}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})+gV_{0},\quad g\in\mathbb{R}

(17)

where

is some constant.
(

) can be further expanded as,

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:MPC:Qmodel2:cost}=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}\,\gamma^{N}V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{N})+\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\gamma^{i}\Big(L(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})

\displaystyle+\gamma(\mathbb{E}[V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i+1})|\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})]-V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i})))\Big)\,.

(18)

Using a telescopic sum, we get,

\eqref{eq:MPC:Qmodel2:cost}=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}}Q^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{k},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}_{k})+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\gamma^{i}A^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)\right]

wherein,

denotes advantage function, defined as:

\displaystyle A^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}Q^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})-V^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}})&\text{ if }\left|Q^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}})\right|<\infty\\

According to Bellman equalities,

therefore, (

and we get,

(22)

where

, which concludes the proof.

The immediate observation on Theorem
1
is that it establishes a sufficient condition on the MPC model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
to satisfy such that the MPC scheme is closed-loop optimal. This condition interlaces the optimal value function of the MDP,
V^{\star}
, and the prediction model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
in a non-trivial way. Before further investigating the implications of Theorem
1
, it is noteworthy that condition (
15
) is
unlike
the conventional criteria used in SYSID and ML for constructing a prediction model that best fits the data. This suggests that an MPC scheme using such conventional criteria to build
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
does not necessarily yield an optimal policy. We will further detail that in the next section.

In the context of MPC, regression-based approaches are generally trying to estimate a model
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
as an expectation over the system state transitions as follows,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right],\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\,.

(23)

Therefore, we will focus the rest of our discussions in the context of the family of models seeking to achieve (
23
). In addition to their relevance within MPC, this choice is motivated by the fact that this family of models, in combination with Theorem
1
, produces interesting results regarding the closed-loop optimality of MPC. The subsequent sections will explore this in detail.

Another interesting remark on the condition (

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{f}}
, which predicts in a one-step-ahead manner in MPC, and not to the entire prediction
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}_{1,\ldots,N}
that is inherent in MPC decisions. This observation is closely related to the sequential decision-making nature of MPC, as attested in the proof of Theorem
1
. We will discus this remark later in the paper.

3.3
Local optimality of MPC with expected-value model

In this section, we will investigate the consequence of Theorem
1
for models achieving (

). If model

is constructed such that it achieves (
23
), then condition (

) becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V^{\star}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\right)\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\,\right]-V^{\star}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}}_{+}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}\right]\right)=V_{0},\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\mathrm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}},

5
Discussion & Conclusions

In this paper, we have discussed the closed-loop optimality of Predictive Control schemes, as viewed in the framework of Markov Decision Processes, i.e. when the system to be controlled is stochastic. The question holds particular relevance for
economic
problems, where closed-loop performance is of primary importance, in contrast to
tracking
problems, where it can be secondary.

Our investigation identified sufficient conditions that allow a Predictive Control scheme to attain closed-loop optimality. These conditions (Theorem
1
) challenge the conventional approach of improving the prediction accuracy to achieve the best closed-loop performance for a Predictive Control scheme. We then provide an analysis that reconciles this conventional approach with our conditions, but this is limited to smooth tracking (or dissipative) problems and only valid locally around the (stochastic) steady state of the problem. For non-smooth or non-dissipative problems, the best-fitting model does not necessarily allow the Predictive Control scheme to deliver an optimal policy, even locally.

A key observation from our investigation across different Predictive Control schemes is that the proposed sufficient conditions are only applicable if the Predictive Control scheme can be viewed as a sequential decision-making process. This requires that the predictions are based on an underlying one-step ahead simulation, regardless of whether it is performed explicitly or not. We have labeled this property
self-consistency
.

While MPC is self-consistent by construction, the same does not hold true for DDPC in its direct form. Hence, the optimality conditions we have identified are not automatically applicable to DDPC. Therefore, to answer whether DDPC achieves optimality, we do not know unless it adheres to the restrictive structure proposed in (
4
). However, our observations suggest that–in the context of closed-loop performance–the most important distinction between different Predictive Control methods is not between model-based and model-free approaches but rather between approaches that are self-consistent or not.

Finally, we note that the observations on local closed-loop optimality of MPC pertain to models seeking expected-value predictions (
23
). While expected-value prediction is the objective of several SYSID and ML approaches, other forms of predictions, such as Maximum Likelihood Estimation, Quantile Regression, and Stochastic Predictions, are also used. However, we do not expect these approaches to have strong properties with respect to Theorem
1
. However, we leave it for further work, a deeper investigation of various prediction techniques in the context of Theorem
1
.
