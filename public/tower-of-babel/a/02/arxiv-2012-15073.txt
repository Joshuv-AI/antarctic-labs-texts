Abstract

1.
Introduction

The representation of positive integers by
m
-gonal numbers
which were defined as the number of dots to constitute a regular
m
-gon in BC 2-th Century has been studied for a long time.
The famous Fermat’s conjecture which states that every positive integer may be written as a sum of at most
m

m
-gonal numbers date back to 17-th Century.
His conjecture was resolved by Lagrange and Gauss for
m=4
and
m=3
in 1770 and 1796, respectively.
And finally Cauchy completed its proof for general
m\geq 3
in 1813.

One may easily derive that the number of dots to constitute a regular
m
-gon with
x
dots for each side has a formula

(1.1)

P_{m}(x)=\frac{m-2}{2}x^{2}-\frac{m-4}{2}x.

We particularly call the
m
-gonal number
P_{m}(x)
in (
1.1
) as the
x
-th
m
-gonal number
.
Although the original definition for
m
-gonal number admits only positive integer for variable
x
in (
1.1
),
one may naturally generalize the
m
-gonal number by admitting the
x
in (
1.1
) zero and negative integer too.
As a general version of Fermat’s Conjecture, the author and collaborators
[
1
]
determined the optimal
\ell_{m}
satisfying every positive integer is written as a sum of
\ell_{m}
generalized
m
-gonal numbers for every
m\geq 3
except
7
and
9
as

\ell_{m}=\begin{cases}m-4&\text{ if }m\geq 10\\
3&\text{ if }m\in\{3,5,6\}\\
4&\text{ if }m\in\{4,8\}.\\
\end{cases}

We call a sum of weighted
m
-gonal numbers

(1.2)

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})

as
m
-gonal form
where
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
For an
m
-gonal form
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
and a positive integer
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if the diophantine equation

(1.3)

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=N

has an integer solution
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, then we say that
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})

represents
N
.
If
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
represents every positive integer, then we say that
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
is
universal
.
To determine the universality of
m
-gonal form is one of the significant problems in the field of number theory and has a long history.
Many mathematicians have been made an effort to classify universal
m
-gonal forms for some specific
m
’s.
Liouville classified all universal ternary triangular forms and Ramanujan classified all universal quaternary square forms.
Ramanujan claimed that there are 55 universal quateranry square forms, but one of them has been distinguised that is not universal.
Willerding claimed there are 124 quaternary non-diagonal universal quadratic form except Ramanujan’s list of diagonal universal quadratic form.
But Willerding’s list also has been corrected later as the total number of quaternary universal quadratic forms are exactly 214 up to isometry.

In general, to determine the universality of a form is not easy.
On the other hand, in 1993, Conway and Schneeberger announced a stunning result on determining the universality of quadratic form.
Their result well known as
15-Theorem
states that the representability of positive integers up to only
15
characterize the universality of quadratic form.
The
15
-Theorem
make readily check the universality of arbitrary quadratic form.
Actually, Willerding’s mistake was corrected in virtue of the
15
-Theorem
.
In
[
3
]
, Kane and Liu claimed such a fineteness theorem holds for any
m
-gonal form for
m\geq 3
.
On the other words, they proved that for any
m\geq 3
, there exists (unique and minimal)
\gamma_{m}
for which if an
m
-gonal form
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
represents every positive integer up to
\gamma_{m}
, then
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
is universal.
Since the smallest generalized
m
-gonal number is
m-3
except
0
and
1
, we may obviously have
m-4\leq\gamma_{m}
, i.e.,
\gamma_{m}
is asymptotically increasing value as
m
increases.
They
[
3
]
also questioned about the growth of
\gamma_{m}
and showed that

m-4\leq\gamma_{m}\ll m^{7+\epsilon}.

As an improvement of the result, Kim and the author
[
4
]
provided the optimal growth of
\gamma_{m}
on
m
which is exactly linear on
m
by proving the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

m-4\leq\gamma_{m}\leq C(m-2)

for any
m\geq 3
.

Proof.

See
[
1
]
.
∎

In effect,
\gamma_{m}
is strictly bigger than
m-4
.
In other words, the representability of every positive integer up to
m-4
by an
m
-gonal form is essential for its universality but does not characterize its universality.

Then how far is it to an
m
-gonal form which represents every positive integer up to
m-4
from an universal form?
The following theorem (which is the main goal in this paper) may suggest the answer.

Theorem 1.2
.

(1)

For
m=6
or
m\geq 12
, let an
m
-gonal form
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})
represents every positive integer up to
m-4
.
Then there is
a_{n+1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})

is universal.

(2)

For
m\in\{5,7,8,9,10,11\}
,
let an
m
-gonal form
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})
represents every positive integer up to
m-4
.
Then there are
a_{n+1},a_{n+2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})+a_{n+2}P_{m}(x_{n+2})

is universal.

Theorem
1.2
says that for any
m
-gonal form which is escalated up to
m-4
, only one more term would be enough to be universal for
m\geq 12
.
Throughout this paper, we prove Theorem
1.2
by using the arithmetic theory of quadratic form.
Especially, we concentrate the solvability of the diophantine systems

(1.4)

\begin{cases}a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}^{2}=2A+B\\
a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}x_{n}=B\end{cases}

over
{\mathbb{Z}}
to verify the representability of a positive integer by an
m
-gonal form.
Note that if (
1.4
) is solvable, then

\displaystyle A(m-2)+B

\displaystyle=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-2)}{2}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}x_{k}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}x_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}x_{k}

would have an integer solution, i.e.,
A(m-2)+B
is represented by the
m
-gonal form
a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})
.
On the other hand, we may observe that the diophantine system (
1.4
) has an integer solution
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
if and only if the binary quadratic
{\mathbb{Z}}
-lattice
[a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n},B,2A+B]
is represented by the diagonal quadratic
{\mathbb{Z}}
-lattice
\left<a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\right>
as follows

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}&B\\
B&2A+B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&\cdots&x_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&a_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&a_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}\\
1&x_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
1&x_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Throughout this paper, we use the above arguments.

Before we move on, we organize some languages and notations which are adopted in this paper.
A
{\mathbb{Z}}
(or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
)-lattice
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
(or
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
) module

on

with

(or

).
We often identify

with its
Gram matrix
\begin{pmatrix}(B(x_{i},x_{j}))\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n}
where

is the symmetric bilinear form
B
corresponding to
Q
.
When the Gram matrix
\begin{pmatrix}(B(x_{i},x_{j}))\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n}
is diagonal, i.e.,

for any

, we simply write
\begin{pmatrix}(B(x_{i},x_{j}))\end{pmatrix}_{n\times n}
as
\left<Q(x_{1}),\cdots,Q(x_{n})\right>
.
When

, we use the notation
[Q(x_{1}),B(x_{1},x_{2}),Q(x_{2})]
instead of
\begin{pmatrix}(B(x_{i},x_{j}))\end{pmatrix}_{2\times 2}
.
On the other words,
[Q(\mathbf{x}_{1}),B(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}),Q(\mathbf{x}_{2})]
identify with the binary quadratic
{\mathbb{Z}}
-lattice

In order to avoid confusing of the readers, I would like to note that someone may use the different notation

for

For simple notation, we write

Any unexplained notation and terminology can be found in

Proposition 2.1
.

Let an
m
-gonal form
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})
with
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
represents every positive integer up to
m-4

If an
m
-gonal form

represents the consecutive
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{i}+1
integers

, then the
m
-gonal form

G_{m}(\mathbf{y})+a_{i+1}P_{m}(x_{i+1})+\cdots+aP_{n}(x_{n})

represents the consecutive
m-3
integers
N,\cdots,N+(m-4)
.

Proof.

From assumption, for
0\leq r\leq m-4
, we may take
\mathbf{x}(r)\in\{0,1\}^{n}

for which

F_{m}(\mathbf{x}(r))=r.

And then for
\mathbf{y}(r)
with

G_{m}(\mathbf{y}(r))=N+a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1}(r))+\cdots+a_{i}P_{m}(x_{i}(r)),

we have that

G_{m}(\mathbf{y}(r))+a_{i+1}P_{m}(x_{i+1}(r))+\cdots+aP_{n}(x_{n}(r))=N+r.

∎

Corollary 2.2
.

For an
m
-gonal form

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})=a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{n}P_{m}(x_{n})

with
a_{1}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}
which represents every positive integer up to
m-4
and an unary

-gonal form

if

a_{1}P_{m}(x_{1})+\cdots+a_{i}P_{m}(x_{i})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})

(for some

) represents every positive integer of the form of

A(m-2)+B,\ A(m-2)+(B+1),\ \cdots,\ A(m-2)+(B+a_{1}+\cdots+a_{i}+1)

for a fixed
B\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, then the
m
-gonal form

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})

is universal.

Proof.

By using Proposition
2.1
with

G_{m}(\mathbf{y})=a_{1}P_{m}(y_{1})+\cdots+a_{i}P_{m}(y_{i})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(y_{n+1})

and

N=A(m-2)+B+1,

we obtain that

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})

represents every positive integer in

[A(m-2)+(B+1),A(m-2)+B+(m-3)],

yielding that
F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+a_{n+1}P_{m}(x_{n+1})
represents every positive integer in
[A(m-2)+B,A(m-2)+B+(m-3)]
.
Since
\{B,B+1,\cdots,B+(m-3)\}
is a complete system of residues modulo
m-2
, it completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let an

-gonal form

When

,

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+2P_{m}(x_{n+1})+3P_{m}(x_{n+2})

(2)
When

F_{m}(\mathbf{x})+P_{m}(x_{n+1})+3P_{m}(x_{n+2})

Remark 3.3
.

(1)

Theorem
1.2

(2)
In order for an

m
-gonal form would also be greater than or equal to

The author determined the minimal rank for universal

]

See

On the other hand, Theorem
