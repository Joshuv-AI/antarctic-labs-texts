Squarefrees are Gaussian in short intervals
Ofir Gorodetsky, Alexander P. Mangerel, Brad Rodgers

Abstract
. We show that counts of squarefree integers up to X X in short intervals of size H H tend to a Gaussian distribution as long as H → ∞ H\rightarrow\infty and H = X o ⁡ ( 1 ) H=X^{o(1)} . This answers a question posed by R.R. Hall in 1989. More generally we prove a variant of Donsker’s theorem, showing that these counts scale to a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter 1 / 4 1/4 . In fact we are able to prove these results hold in general for collections of B B -free integers as long as the sieving set B B satisfies a very mild regularity property, for Hurst parameter varying with the set B B .



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Statistics of counts of squarefrees

Let
S\subset\mathbb{N}
be the set of squarefree natural numbers (that is natural numbers without a repeated prime factor; by convention we include
1\in S
). We write

N_{S}(x)=|\{n\leq x:\;n\in S\}|

for the number of squarefrees no more than
x
. It is well known that
N_{S}(x)\sim x/\zeta(2)
, thus the squarefrees have asymptotic density
1/\zeta(2)=6/\pi^{2}
. Our purpose in this note is to investigate their distribution at a finer scale. In particular we will investigate the distribution of squarefrees in a random interval
(n,n+H]
, where
n
is an integer chosen uniformly at random from
1
to
X
, with
X\rightarrow\infty
.

If
H
is fixed and does not grow with
X
, at most
O(1)
squarefrees can lie in such an interval. Their distribution as
X\rightarrow\infty
is slightly complicated but completely understood; it may be described by Hardy–Littlewood type correlations which can be derived from elementary sieve theory (see
[
32
,
31
]
). Or, more abstractly, the distribution of squarefrees in an interval of size
O(1)
can be described by a non-weakly mixing stationary ergodic process (see
[
7
,
43
]
).

For
H
tending to infinity with
X
matters become at once simpler and more difficult; simpler because some of the irregularities in the distribution just described are smoothed out at this scale, but more difficult in that natural conjectures become more difficult to prove.

Let

N_{S}(n,H)=N_{S}(n+H)-N_{S}(n)

be the count of squarefrees in the interval
(n,n+H]
. R.R. Hall
[
16
,
17
]
was the first to investigate the distribution of this count when
H
grows with
X
. In
[
16
, Corollary 1]
, Hall proved that the variance of the number of squarefrees is of order
\sqrt{H}
if
H
is not too large with respect to
X
. More exactly, as
X\rightarrow\infty
we have

(1.1)

\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\left(N_{S}(n,H)-\frac{H}{\zeta(2)}\right)^{2}\sim A\sqrt{H},\qquad A=\frac{\zeta\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)}{\pi}\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\left(1-\frac{3}{p^{2}}+\frac{2}{p^{3}}\right)

as long as
H\to\infty
with
H\leq X^{2/9-\varepsilon}
.

Keating and Rudnick
[
25
]
studied this problem in a function field setting, connecting it with Random Matrix Theory, and suggested based on this that (
1.1
) will hold for
H\leq X^{1-\varepsilon}
. The best known result is
[
13
]
, where it is shown that (
1.1
) holds for
H\leq X^{6/11-\varepsilon}
unconditionally and
H\leq X^{2/3-\varepsilon}
on the Lindelöf Hypothesis. In fact in
[
13
]
it is shown that even an upper bound of order
\sqrt{H}
for
H\leq X^{1-\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
would already imply the Riemann Hypothesis.

Because
N_{S}(n,H)
is on average of order
H
, one might naively have expected the variance to also be of order
H
. That the variance is of order
\sqrt{H}
speaks to the fact that the squarefrees are a rather rigid sequence. This can be discerned even visually in comparison, for instance, to the primes (Figure
1
) and we will return to give a more exact description of it in Section
1.3
.

In
[
17
]
Hall

M_{k}(X,H)=\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\left(N_{S}(n,H)-\frac{H}{\zeta(2)}\right)^{k},

Various authors have asked whether this can be refined, with the most recent result,

Our first main result confirms this conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
1\leq H\leq X
, as
X\rightarrow\infty
,

M_{k}(X,H)=\mu_{k}(A^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{\frac{1}{4}})^{k}+O_{k}(H^{\frac{k}{4}-\frac{c}{k}}+H^{k}X^{-\frac{1}{3}+o(1)}),

for every positive integer
k
, where
\mu_{k}=1\cdot 3\cdots(k-1)
if
k
is even and
\mu_{k}=0
if
k
is odd. Here
c>0
is an explicit constant.

Thus if
H\rightarrow\infty
and
H\leq X^{4/(9k)-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
, we have

M_{k}(X,H)=(\mu_{k}+o(1))(A^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{\frac{1}{4}})^{k}\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty.

Note that the main term is the
k
-th moment of a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
A\sqrt{H}
.

If
H=X^{o(1)}
, then for any fixed
k
we have that
H
satisfies
H\leq X^{4/(9k)-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
for sufficiently large
X
. Hence by the moment method (see
[
4
, Section 30]
), we obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H=H(X)
satisfy

H\rightarrow\infty,\text{ yet }\,\frac{\log H}{\log X}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty.

Then, for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{X}\left|\left\{n\leq X:\frac{N_{S}(n,H)-\frac{H}{\zeta(2)}}{A^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{\frac{1}{4}}}\geq z\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt.

That is, the centered, normalized counts tend in distribution to a Gaussian random variable.

Gaussian limit theorems are known for the sums over short intervals of several important arithmetic functions (for instance divisor functions
d_{k}
(see
[
28

) and the sums-of-squares representation function
r
(see
[
20
]
)), and Gaussian limit theorems for counts of primes in short intervals are known under the assumption of strong versions of the Hardy–Littlewood conjectures
[
33
]
, but Theorem
1.2
seems to be the first instance of an
unconditional
proof of Gaussian behavior for short interval counts of a non-trivial, natural number theoretic sequence.

Hall in
[
17
]
asked also about the order of magnitude of the absolute moments

M_{\lambda}^{+}(X;H)=\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\left|N_{S}(n,H)-\frac{H}{\zeta(2)}\right|^{\lambda},

and as a standard corollary of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
we obtain an asymptotic formula for these.

Corollary 1.3
.

For fixed
\lambda>0
, let
H=H(X)
satisfy

H\rightarrow\infty,\text{ yet }\,\,\frac{\log H}{\log X}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty.

Then

M_{\lambda}^{+}(X,H)\sim\frac{2^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\right)(A\sqrt{H})^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}.

Proof.

We give a quick derivation in the language of probability. By
[
4
, Theorem 25.12]
and the subsequent corollary, if
Y_{j}
is a sequence of random variables tending in distribution to a random variable
Y
and
\sup_{j}\mathbb{E}\,|Y_{j}|^{1+\varepsilon}<+\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
\mathbb{E}\,Y_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\,Y
. Having chosen the function
H=H(X)
, for each
X
let
n\in[1,X]
be chosen randomly and uniformly and define the random variables
\Delta_{X}=|(N_{S}(n,H)-H/\zeta(2))/(A^{1/2}H^{1/4})|^{\lambda}
. Then as
X\rightarrow\infty
, we have that
\Delta_{X}
tends in distribution to
|G|^{\lambda}
for
G
a standard normal random variable, by Theorem
1.2
. Moreover, Theorem
1.1
implies for any even integer
2\ell>\lambda
that
\limsup_{X\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{X}|^{2\ell}<+\infty
. Thus the result follows by computing
\mathbb{E}\,|G|^{\lambda}
via calculus.
∎

Remark 1.4
.

For a given result relating to the behavior of an arithmetic function in short intervals, it is natural to consider the analogous problem in a short arithmetic progression. For example, in analogy to the quantity
N_{S}(n,H)
for
n\in[1,x]
and
H=H(x)
slowly growing, one might consider the quantity

N_{S}(x;q,a):=|\{n\leq x:n\in S,n\equiv a\text{ mod }{q}\}|,

where
1\leq q\leq x
is chosen so large that
x/q
(which corresponds in this context to
H
) is only slowly growing, and
a\text{ mod }{q}
is a specified residue class. When
a
is coprime to
q
the expected size of
N_{S}(x;q,a)
is

\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\frac{x}{q}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right),

and one can define the analogous moments

\tilde{M}_{k}(x;q):=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sideset{}{{}^{\ast}}{\sum}_{a\text{ mod }{q}}\left(N_{S}(x;q,a)-\frac{x}{q\zeta(2)}\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\right)^{k}\text{ for $k\geq 2$}.

As noted by Nunes
[
37
, Sections 1.2, 3.2]
, one may reduce the estimation of
\tilde{M}_{k}(x;q)
to a quantity that is very similar to what is obtained in the course of estimating
M_{k}(x,H)
, making the analysis of the problem for arithmetic progressions nearly identical to that of short intervals. As such, one could very similarly obtain an arithmetic progression analogue of the Gaussian limit theorem Theorem
1.2
, if desired. However, unlike the short interval problem the problem in progressions does not seem to admit a nice interpretation in the language of fractional Brownian motion (see Section
1.3
). We have therefore chosen to focus on the short interval problem in this paper in order to avoid making this paper even longer.

1.2.
B-frees

It is natural to write our proofs in the more general setting of
B
-free numbers. We recall their definition shortly, but first we fix some notation.

For a sequence
J
of natural numbers we will write
\boldsymbol{1}_{J}
for the indicator function of
J
, and

N_{J}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\boldsymbol{1}_{J}(n)

for the count of elements of
J
no more than
x
.

Definition 1.5
.

We say that a sequence
J
is of
index

\alpha\in[0,1]
if
N_{J}(x)=x^{\alpha+o(1)}
.

Definition 1.6
.

A measurable function
L
defined, finite, positive, and measurable on
[K,\infty)
for some
K\geq 0
is said to be
slowly varying
if for all
a>0
,

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{L(ax)}{L(x)}=1.

A sequence
J\subset\mathbb{N}
is said to be
regularly varying
if
N_{J}(x)\sim x^{\alpha}L(x)
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
and some slowly varying function
L
.

For instance the function
L(x)=(\log x)^{j}
is slowly varying, for any
j\in\mathbb{R}
. For any slowly varying function
L
it is necessary that
L(x)=x^{o(1)}
, but this condition is not sufficient. Clearly in the definition above
\alpha
will be the index of the regularly varying sequence
J
. Further information on regularly varying sequences can be found in
[
41
, Chapter 4.1]

Fix a non-empty subset
B\subset\mathbb{N}_{>1}
of pairwise coprime integers with
\sum_{b\in B}1/b<\infty
; we call such a set a
sieving set
. We say that a positive integer is
B
-free
if it is indivisible by every element of
B
.
For instance if
B=\{p^{2}:\;p\text{ prime}\}
,
B
-frees are nothing but squarefrees. Another studied example is
B=\{p^{m}:\;p\text{ prime}\}
for some
m\geq 3
, for which
B
-frees are the
m
-th-power free numbers, i.e., integers indivisible by an
m
-th power of a prime. The notion of
B
-frees was introduced by Erdős
[
12
]
, who was motivated by Roth’s work
[
42
]
on gaps between squarefrees. (See also
[
6
,
22
]
for the closely related notion of
convergent sieves
. We also mention that
[
22
]
upper bounded the quantity
\tilde{M}_{2}(x;q)
mentioned in Remark
1.4
.)

We write
\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
for the indicator function of
B
-free integers, and
N_{B\text{-free}}(x)
for the number of
B
-free integers
n\leq x
. For the sets
B
we are considering here, it is known (see e.g.,
[
10
, Theorem 4.1]
) that

(1.3)

N_{B\text{-free}}(x)\sim\mathcal{M}_{B}x,\quad\text{for}\quad\mathcal{M}_{B}=\prod_{b\in B}\left(1-\frac{1}{b}\right)\in(0,1),

so that
B
-frees have asymptotic density
\mathcal{M}_{B}
.

We write
\langle B\rangle
for the multiplicative semigroup generated by
B
, that is the set of positive integers that can be written as a product of (possibly repeated) elements of
B
. By convention
1\in\langle B\rangle
, as
1
arises from the empty product. (For instance if
B=\{p^{2}:\;p\text{ prime}\}
, then
\langle B\rangle=\{n^{2}:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.)

We introduce an arithmetic function
\mu_{B}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
, analogous to the Möbius function
\mu
, defined by

\mu_{B}(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }n\notin\langle B\rangle\text{ or if there exists }b\in B\text{ with }b^{2}\mid n,\\
(-1)^{k}&\text{if }n=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{k},\,b_{1}<b_{2}<\ldots<b_{k},\,b_{j}\in B.\end{cases}

Observe that
\mu_{B}
and
\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}
are multiplicative and relate via the multiplicative convolution

(1.4)

\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}=\mu_{B}\ast\mathbf{1}.

We denote by
[B]\subset\langle B\rangle
the subset of
\langle B\rangle
of elements
n
satisfying
\mu_{B}^{2}(n)=1
, i.e., those
n\in\langle B\rangle
such that no
b\in B
divides
n
twice. (For instance, if
B=\{p^{2}:\;p\;\textrm{prime}\}
, then we have
[B]=\{n^{2}:\;n\;\textrm{squarefree}\}
.) We will often use without mention the fact that both
[B]
and
\langle B\rangle
are closed under
\gcd
and
\mathrm{lcm}
, or equivalently, they are sublattices of the positive integers with respect to these two operations.

1.2.1.
Variance and Moments

Let
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)=N_{B\text{-free}}(n+H)-N_{B\text{-free}}(n)
be the count of
B
-frees in an interval
(n,n+H]
. We consider the moments

M_{k,B}(X,H)=\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\left(N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)-\mathcal{M}_{B}H\right)^{k}.

Proposition 1.7
.

If the sequence
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for each fixed positive integer
k
,

C_{k,B}(H):=\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}M_{k,B}(X,H),

exists and moreover for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

(1.5)

\displaystyle M_{k,B}(X,H)=C_{k,B}(H)+O_{k}(H^{k}X^{\tfrac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}+\varepsilon}+1).

We will describe an explicit formula for
C_{k,B}(H)
later.

When
k=2
we have the following general result for the variance, building on ideas of Hausman and Shapiro
[
19
]
and Montgomery and Vaughan
[
34
]
,

Proposition 1.8
.

If the sequence
\langle B\rangle
is of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

C_{2,B}(H)=H^{\alpha+o(1)}
.

If
\langle B\rangle
is in addition a regularly varying sequence then we prove an asymptotic formula for
C_{2,B}(H)
.

Proposition 1.9
.

If the sequence
\langle B\rangle
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

C_{2,B}(H)\sim A_{\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H),

where

(1.6)

A_{\alpha}:=\zeta(2-\alpha)\gamma(\alpha)\prod_{b\in B}\left(1-\frac{2}{b}+\frac{2}{b^{1+\alpha}}-\frac{1}{b^{2\alpha}}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\gamma(\alpha):=\frac{(2\pi)^{\alpha}}{\pi^{2}}\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\Gamma(1-\alpha).

This generalizes a result of Avdeeva
[
3
]
which requires more robust assumptions about
\langle B\rangle
. It also gives a new proof for (
1.1
) that does not use contour integration and is essentially elementary.

In fact, we do not need an asymptotic formula for the variance to prove that the moments are Gaussian.

Theorem 1.10
.

If
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

(1.7)

C_{k,B}(H)=\mu_{k}C_{2,B}(H)^{\frac{k}{2}}+O_{k}(H^{\frac{k\alpha}{2}-\frac{c}{k}})

for every positive integer
k
. Here
c
is an absolute constant depending only on
\alpha
.

It is evident that Proposition
1.8
and Theorem
1.10
recover the moment estimate Theorem
1.1
for squarefrees. Moreover, for the same reasons as given for the central limit theorem there, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
H=H(X)
satisfy

H\rightarrow\infty,\text{ yet }\,\,\frac{\log H}{\log X}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty.

If the sequence
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{X}\left|\left\{n\leq X:\frac{N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)-\mathcal{M}_{B}H}{\sqrt{C_{2,B}(H)}}\geq z\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}dt.

Remark 1.12
.

Combining Theorem
1.10
and Proposition
1.7
, we see that for each
k
, the moments
M_{k,B}(X,H)
will be asymptotically Gaussian as long as
H,X\rightarrow\infty
with

H\leq X^{\frac{c_{\alpha}}{k}-\varepsilon},\text{ where }c_{\alpha}=\frac{1-\alpha}{(1+\alpha)(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})}.

In Section
6.2
we give a further application of Theorem
1.10
to estimates for the frequency of long gaps between consecutive
B
-free numbers, improving results of Plaksin
[
39
]
and Matomäki
[
29
]
.

1.3.
Fractional Brownian motion

We have mentioned that the squarefrees and more generally
B
-frees in a random interval
(n,n+H]
with
n\leq X
are governed by a stationary ergodic process if
H
remains fixed. The number of
B
-frees in such an interval is
\mathcal{M}_{B}H
on average. This process has measure-theoretic entropy which becomes smaller the larger
H
is chosen to be, and thus does not seem very ‘random’. This may be compared to primes in short intervals
(n,n+\lfloor\lambda\log X\rfloor]
, which contain on average
\lambda
primes. In such intervals primes are conjectured to be distributed as a Poisson point process, and thus appear very ‘random.’

Nonetheless a glance at Figure
1
comparing squarefrees to primes – along with consideration of the central limit theorems we have just discussed – reveals that at a scale of
H\rightarrow\infty
,
B
-frees still retain some degree of randomness. It turns out that there is a natural framework to describe the ‘random’ behavior of
B
-frees at this scale (analogous to the Poisson process for primes above) and this is fractional Brownian motion.

We give here a short introduction to fractional Brownian motion, as we believe this perspective sheds substantial light on the distribution of
B
-frees; however, the remainder of the paper is arranged so that a reader only interested in the central limit theorems of the previous sections can avoid this material.

Definition 1.13
.

A random process
\{Z(t):t\in[0,1]\}
is said to be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\gamma\in(0,1)
if
Z
is a continuous-time Gaussian process which satisfies
Z(0)=0
and also satisfies
\mathbb{E}\,Z(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
and has covariance function

(1.8)

\mathbb{E}\,Z(t)Z(s)=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2\gamma}+s^{2\gamma}-|t-s|^{2\gamma}\right),

for all
t,s\in[0,1].

Using
Z(0)=0
, it is easy to see the covariance condition (
1.8
) is equivalent to

(1.9)

\mathbb{E}\,|Z(t)-Z(s)|^{2}=|t-s|^{2\gamma}.

For a proof that such a stochastic process exists and is uniquely defined by this definition see e.g.
[
36
]
.

Classical Brownian motion is a fractional Brownian motion with a Hurst parameter of
\gamma=1/2
. If
\gamma>1/2
, increments of the process are positively correlated, with a rise likely to be followed by another rise, while if
\gamma<1/2
, increments of the process are negatively correlated.

Donsker’s theorem is a classical result in probability theory showing that a random walk with independent increments scales to Brownian motion (see
[
5
, Section 8]
). We prove an analogue of Donsker’s theorem for counts of
B
-frees using the following set up. We select a random starting point
n\leq X
at uniform and define the random variables
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
in terms of
n
by

\xi_{k}=\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(n+k)-\mathcal{M}_{B}=\begin{cases}1-\mathcal{M}_{B}&\text{if $n+k$ is $B$-free,}\\
-\mathcal{M}_{B}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Set

(1.10)

Q(\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\tau\rfloor}\xi_{k}+\{\tau\}\xi_{\lfloor\tau\rfloor+1},

where
\{\tau\}
denotes the fractional part.

For integer
\tau
this is a random walk which increases on
B
-frees and decreases otherwise, and for non-integer
\tau
, the function
Q(\tau)
linearly interpolates between values; thus
Q(\tau)
is continuous.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
H=H(X)
satisfy

H\rightarrow\infty,\text{ yet }\,\,\frac{\log H}{\log X}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty,

and choose a random integer
n\in[1,X]
at uniform. Suppose that
\langle B\rangle
is a regularly varying sequence of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define the function

(1.11)

W_{X}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)}}Q(t\cdot H),

where
A_{\alpha}
is defined by (
1.6
). Then, as a random element of
C[0,1]
, the function
W_{X}
converges in distribution to a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\alpha/2
as
X\rightarrow\infty
.

1.5.
The structure of the proof

There is a heuristic way to understand the Gaussian variation of
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)
. Note that

\displaystyle N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)=\sum_{n<vd\leq n+H}\mu_{B}(d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[B]\\
d\leq X+H\end{subarray}}\mu_{B}(d)\Big(\Big\lfloor\frac{n+H}{d}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\frac{n}{d}\Big\rfloor\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[B]\\
d\leq X+H\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{\mu_{B}(d)}{d}H+\mu_{B}(d)\Big(\Big\{\frac{n}{d}\Big\}-\Big\{\frac{n+H}{d}\Big\}\Big)\Big).

The contribution of the first summand is close to the value
\mathcal{M}_{B}H
around which
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)
oscillates. On the other hand, the functions
n\mapsto\{(n+H)/d\}-\{n/d\}
are mean-zero functions of period
d
and thus are linear combinations of terms
e(n\ell/d)
for
1\leq\ell\leq d-1
. Upon reducing the fractions
\ell/d
by the maximal divisor
b|(\ell,d)
with
b\in\langle B\rangle
, we see that
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)-\mathcal{M}_{B}H
is approximated by a linear combination of terms
e(n\ell/d)
for
(\ell,d)

B
-free and
d\in[B]
.

Heuristically, if
X
is large and
n\in[1,X]
is chosen uniformly at random, one may expect the terms
\{e(n\ell/d):\;(\ell,d)\text{ $B$-free}\}
to behave like a collection of independent random variables, and this would imply the Gaussian oscillation of
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)
.

Nonetheless we do not quite have independence; instead, roughly speaking, one may use the same Fourier decomposition to relate the
k
-th moment
M_{k,B}(X,H)
to (weighted) counts of solutions to the equation

(1.12)

\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{\ell_{k}}{d_{k}}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1,

where
\|\ell_{i}/d_{i}\|<1/H
for all
i
. Indeed, the realization that
M_{k}(X,H)
for the squarefrees are related to these counts appears already in
[
17
]
.

It can be seen that Gaussian behavior will then follow from most solutions to the above equation being diagonal, meaning there is some pairing
\ell_{i}/d_{i}\equiv-\ell_{j}/d_{j}\text{ mod }1
for all
i
, and this is what we demonstrate. Our main tool is the Fundamental Lemma and its extensions (see Lemmas
2.6
,
4.1
, and
5.5
), developed by Montgomery–Vaughan
[
34
]
and later used by Montgomery–Soundararajan
[
33
]
to prove a conditional central limit theorem for primes in short intervals.

However, this strategy if used by itself is not sufficient to prove a central limit theorem; the Fundamental Lemma was already known to Hall who used it to obtain his upper bound
M_{k}(X;H)\ll H^{(k-1)/2}
. The reason this strategy does not work as it did for Montgomery–Soundararajan is that
M_{k,B}(X;H)
has size roughly
H^{\alpha k/2}
; for primes the
k
-th moment of a short interval count is (conditionally) much larger. Thus in order to recover a main term, our error terms must be shown to be substantially smaller than for the primes.

We obtain an upper bound for the number of off-diagonal solutions to (
1.12
) by bringing in two ideas in addition to those used by Montgomery–Soundararajan. The first is due to Nunes, who showed in the recent paper
[
37
]
that solutions to (
1.12
) make a contribution to
M_{k}(X;H)
only when
d_{i}
is larger than
H^{1-o(1)}
for all
i
and the least common multiple of the
d_{i}
is larger than
H^{k/2-o(1)}
; this argument appears in Section
5.1
. The second is an observation that bounding a term
T_{4}
which appears in a variant of the Fundamental Lemma (Lemma
5.5
) requires a more delicate treatment than that which appears in
[
34
, Lemma 8]
. However, it can be accomplished in our context by counting solutions to a certain congruence equation, which in turn can be estimated using an averaging argument relying crucially on the Pólya–Vinogradov inequality; this is done in Section
5.2
. See Remark
5.10
for further discussion of how the more direct, but less quantitatively precise, approach of
[
34
, Lemma 8]
is insufficient in our context.

In order to prove that counts of
B
-frees scale to a fractional Brownian motion it is not sufficient to consider the flat counts
N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)
, but instead we must consider the weighted counts

\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\Big(\frac{u-n}{H}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(u)

where
\varphi
belongs to a class of functions that includes step-functions. A proof of a central limit theorem for flat counts remains essentially unchanged as long as
\varphi
is of bounded variation and compactly supported in
[0,\infty)
. Convergence to a fractional Brownian motion as a corollary of this central limit theorem is discussed in Section
7
.

Lemma 2.2

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\varphi

\Phi_{H}(t)\ll F_{H}(t),

Lemma 2.5

is

\ll H^{o(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in[B]\\
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]\leq z\end{subarray}}\frac{[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]}{X}\leq\frac{H^{o(1)}}{X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in[B]\\
d\leq z\end{subarray}}\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},k}(d)d=\frac{H^{o(1)}z^{1+\alpha+o(1)}}{X}.

Thus (
2.11
) is

(2.12)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in[B]\\
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]\leq z\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\cdots\mu_{B}(d_{k})}{d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\ell_{j}<d_{j}\,\forall j\\
\sum\ell_{i}/d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}\Big)\cdots\Phi_{H}\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}\Big)+O\left(\frac{H^{o(1)}z^{1+\alpha+o(1)}}{X}\right).

We now complete the sum above. Directly applying Lemma
2.6
(and appealing to Lemma
2.3
and the second part of Lemma
2.2
), we see that the corresponding sum over tuples

\boldsymbol{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\text{ with }[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]>z

is

(2.13)

\ll H^{\frac{k}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in[B]\\
[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]>z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{[d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}]}\ll H^{\frac{k}{2}}z^{\alpha-1+o(1)}.

Hence from (
2.10
), (
2.12
), (
2.13
),

M_{k}(X,H;\varphi)=C_{k}^{\ast}(H;\varphi)+O(H^{k}z^{\alpha-1+o(1)}X^{o(1)}+H^{k}X^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}+o(1)}+H^{o(1)}z^{1+\alpha+o(1)}X^{-1}),

where

(2.14)

C_{k}^{\ast}(H;\varphi)=\sum_{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}\in[B]}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\cdots\mu_{B}(d_{k})}{d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\ell_{j}<d_{j}\,\forall j\\
\sum\ell_{i}/d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}\Big)\cdots\Phi_{H}\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}\Big).

(Note that the absolute convergence of this sum is implied by the above derivation.) Setting
z=X^{(1-\varepsilon)/(\alpha+1)}
we obtain the desired error term.

It remains to demonstrate
C_{k}^{\ast}(H;\varphi)=C_{k}(H;\varphi)
. To do this, note that if
\sigma_{i}=\ell_{i}/d_{i}
, with
0<\ell_{i}<d_{i}
, we can find a maximal
e_{i}\in[B]
such that
\ell_{i}=e_{i}l_{i}^{\prime}
,
d_{i}=e_{i}d_{i}^{\prime}
, and so that
(l_{i}^{\prime},d_{i}^{\prime})
is
B
-free. Moreover, writing
\sigma_{i}=l_{i}^{\prime}/d_{i}^{\prime}
does not affect the condition
\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\ell_{j}<d_{j}\forall j\\
\sum\ell_{i}/d_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{\ell_{1}}{d_{1}}\right)\cdots\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{\ell_{k}}{d_{k}}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1}\mid d_{1}\\
e_{1}\in[B]\end{subarray}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{k}\mid d_{k}\\
e_{k}\in[B]\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\sigma_{k}^{\prime}\\
\sigma_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(d_{j}/e_{j})\,\forall j\\
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}+\cdots+\sigma_{k}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}(\sigma_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\Phi_{H}(\sigma_{k}^{\prime}).

Note also that as
\ell_{i}\neq 0
,
d_{i}/e_{i}\neq 1
. Therefore, setting
r_{j}:=d_{j}/e_{j}
in each of the above sums, we have

\displaystyle C_{k}^{\ast}(H;\varphi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\\
r_{j}\in[B]\,\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(r_{1})\cdots\mu_{B}(r_{k})}{r_{1}\cdots r_{k}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\geq 1\\
e_{j}\in[B]\,\forall j\\
(e_{j},r_{j})\text{ is $B$-free}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(e_{1})\cdots\mu_{B}(e_{k})}{e_{1}\cdots e_{k}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}\\
\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r_{j})\\
\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}(\sigma_{1})\cdots\Phi_{H}(\sigma_{k}).

The sums over
e_{j}
factor as

\prod_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\nmid r_{j}\\
b\in B\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{b}\right),

and we see
C_{k}^{\ast}(H;\varphi)=C_{k}(H;\varphi)
as required.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

From (
2.14
), reindexing
\ell_{1}^{\prime}=\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}^{\prime}=d_{2}-\ell_{2}
, we have the following useful alternative expression for
C_{2}(H;\varphi)
:

(2.15)

C_{2}(H;\varphi)=\sum_{d_{1},d_{2}\in[B]}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\mu_{B}(d_{2})}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\ell_{1}^{\prime}<d_{1}\\
0<\ell_{2}^{\prime}<d_{2}\\
\ell_{1}^{\prime}/d_{1}=\ell_{2}^{\prime}/d_{2}\end{subarray}}\Phi_{H}\Big(\frac{\ell_{1}^{\prime}}{d_{1}}\Big)\Phi_{H}\Big(-\frac{\ell_{2}^{\prime}}{d_{2}}\Big).

The absolute convergence of this sum is implied by the above argument.

Remark 2.8
.

When
\varphi=\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]}
the corresponding expression for
C_{2}(H;\varphi)
may also be calculated using correlation formulae for
\mu_{B}
. Indeed, upon expanding the
k
-th power in the definition of
M_{k}(X,H;\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]}
and swapping orders of summation, we find

M_{k}(X,H;\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]})=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\binom{k}{j}(-\mathcal{M}_{B}H)^{k-j}\sum_{0\leq h_{1},\ldots,h_{j}\leq H}\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(n+h_{1})\cdots\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(n+h_{j}).

When
B=\{p^{2}:p\text{ prime}\}
, Hall
[
17
, Lemma 2]
used this approach to compute the main terms
C_{k}(H;\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]})
. This was generalized straightforwardly to the setting with
B=\{p^{m}:p\text{ prime}\}
for
m\geq 3
by Nunes
[
37
, Lemma 2.2]
and can be generalized to
B
-frees as well.

3.
Estimates for variance

3.1.
Preliminary results on the index of
B
,
[B]
, and
\langle B\rangle

In this section we will estimate the variance
C_{2}(H)
in various ways. We first prove some preliminary results relating the index of
\langle B\rangle
to
[B]
and
B
itself.

Lemma 3.1
.

For a sieving set
B
, the sequence
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha
if and only if
[B]
has index
\alpha.

Proof.

Suppose
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha
. We first show that
[B]
has index
\alpha
also. Plainly,

N_{[B]}(x)\leq N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)=x^{\alpha+o(1)}.

On the other hand,

(3.1)

\sum_{b\leq x,b\in\langle B\rangle}N_{[B]}\left(\frac{x}{b^{2}}\right)=N_{\langle B\rangle}(x).

Introducing a parameter
y=x^{\varepsilon}
, we upper-bound the left-hand sum as follows. For
b\geq y
with
b\in\langle B\rangle
we apply
N_{[B]}(x/b^{2})\leq N_{\langle B\rangle}(x/b^{2})
, and for
b<y
we use monotonicity in the form
N_{[B]}(x/b^{2})\leq N_{[B]}(x)
. Thus,

N_{\langle B\rangle}(y)N_{[B]}(x)\geq N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)-\sum_{b\geq y,b\in\langle B\rangle}N_{\langle B\rangle}\left(\frac{x}{b^{2}}\right)\geq x^{\alpha+o(1)},

so
N_{[B]}(x)\geq x^{(1-\varepsilon)\alpha+o_{\varepsilon}(1)}
. Since this holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, we obtain the lower bound needed for the claim.

In the converse direction, if
[B]
has index
\alpha
, then

N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)\geq N_{[B]}(x)=x^{\alpha+o(1)}.

To prove an upper bound for
N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)
, use (
3.1
) and note the left-hand side will be
\ll x^{\alpha+o(1)}
since
\sum_{b\in\langle B\rangle}b^{-2\alpha}<\infty
.
∎

Other authors have proved results in this area based on assumptions regarding the index of the set
B
(for instance
[
15
]
). Though we will not require it in what follows, for completeness’ sake we note the following implication.

Proposition 3.2
.

For a sieving set
B
, if
B
has index
\alpha
, then
\langle B\rangle
also has index
\alpha.

Proof.

As
B\subseteq\langle B\rangle
, it suffices to prove an upper bound on
N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)
. For
n\in\langle B\rangle
, let
\omega_{B}(n):=\#\{b\in B:b\mid n\}
and define

N_{[B]}^{(k)}:=\{n\leq x:\,n\in[B],\,\omega_{B}(n)\leq k\}.

We will first show for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

(3.2)

N_{[B]}^{(k)}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{k}x^{\alpha+\varepsilon}.

For notational reasons let
B^{\dagger}:=B\cup\{1\}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. It is plain there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that

\sum_{b\leq x,\;b\in B^{\dagger}}\frac{1}{b^{\alpha+\varepsilon}}\leq C_{\varepsilon},\quad\text{and}\quad N_{B^{\dagger}}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}x^{\alpha+\varepsilon}.

This gives (
3.2
) for
k=1
. But (
3.2
) then follows inductively for all
k
from the above bounds and

N_{[B]}^{(k)}(x)\leq\sum_{b\leq x,\,b\in B^{\dagger}}N_{[B]}^{(k-1)}\left(\frac{x}{b}\right).

Now note
\omega_{B}(n)\leq\omega(n)=o(\log x)
for all
n\leq x
(see
[
35
, Theorem 2.10]
). Hence for all
x
there exists
k=o(\log x)
such that

N_{[B]}(x)=N_{[B]}^{(k)}(x)\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{k}x^{\alpha+\varepsilon}=x^{\alpha+\varepsilon+o(1)}.

As
\varepsilon
is arbitrary this implies

N_{[B]}(x)\ll x^{\alpha+o(1)}.

Using (
3.1
) as before this implies
N_{\langle B\rangle}(x)\ll x^{\alpha+o(1)}
as desired.
∎

It seems likely that the converse to Proposition
3.2
is false, but we do not pursue this here.

3.2.
Variance for
\langle B\rangle
with index
\alpha

We now show that the exponent of the variance is determined by the index of
\langle B\rangle
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
\varphi
is of bounded variation, supported on a compact subset of
[0,\infty)
. Assume moreover that
\varphi
is non-vanishing on some open interval. If
\langle B\rangle
is of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then

C_{2}(H;\varphi)=H^{\alpha+o(1)}.

Proof.

We have

C_{2}(H;\varphi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r>1\\
r\in[B]\end{subarray}}g(r)^{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}.

For an upper bound we apply Lemma
2.3
and the second part of Lemma
2.2
to see that

(3.3)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}\ll r\min\{r,H\}.

As
g(r)\ll 1/r
we have

C_{2}(H;\varphi)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r>1\\
r\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\cdot r\min\{r,H\}\ll H^{\alpha+o(1)}.

Suppose
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is supported on an interval
[0,c]
. Before embarking on the proof of the lower bound, we make the following observation. Since
\varphi
is of bounded variation we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left|\Phi_{H}(0)-H\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq m\leq cH}\left|\varphi\left(\frac{m}{H}\right)-H\int_{m/H}^{(m+1)/H}\varphi(t)dt\right|

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq H\int_{0}^{1/H}\sum_{0\leq m\leq cH}\left|\varphi\left(\frac{m}{H}\right)-\varphi\left(\frac{m}{H}+u\right)\right|du\ll 1.

We now split the proof of the lower bound into two cases, depending on whether or not
\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)dt=0
.

Case 1:
\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)dt=0
. From (
3.4
) we obtain
\Phi_{H}(0)=O(1)
. Now, for convenience set
\nu_{H}(r)=0
if
r\notin[B]
, and otherwise put

\nu_{H}(r):=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\text{ mod }{r}\\
(a,r)\text{ is $B$-free}\end{subarray}}\left|\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)\right|^{2}.

Using the convolution formula
\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}=\mathbf{1}\ast\mu_{B}
, for each
r\in[B]
we obtain

\nu_{H}(r)=\sum_{a\text{ mod }{r}}(\mathbf{1}\ast\mu_{B})((a,r))\left|\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)\right|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}md=r\\
m,d\in[B]\end{subarray}}\mu_{B}(d)\sum_{b\text{ mod }{m}}\left|\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{b}{m}\right)\right|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}md=r\\
m,d\in[B]\end{subarray}}\mu_{B}(d)\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}.

Moreover, if
m\geq 10cH
then by Plancherel’s theorem on
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
, we obtain

(3.6)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\mid(n_{1}-n_{2})\end{subarray}}\varphi\left(\frac{n_{1}}{H}\right)\varphi\left(\frac{n_{2}}{H}\right)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\left(\frac{n}{H}\right)^{2}\gg H,

where in the last step we used the fact that
\varphi
is non-vanishing on some open interval.

Since
g(r)^{2}\asymp r^{-2}
for
r\in[B]
, we find that

\displaystyle C_{2}(H;\varphi)

\displaystyle\gg\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r>1\end{subarray}}\frac{\nu_{H}(r)}{r^{2}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}md>1\\
m,d\in[B],(d,m)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(d)}{d^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 1\\
m\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geq 1\\
(d,m)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(d)}{d^{2}}-|\Phi_{H}(0)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 1\\
m\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
b\nmid m\end{subarray}}(1-b^{-2})-O(1).

Since

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\in B\\
b\nmid m\end{subarray}}(1-b^{-2})\geq\prod_{b\in B}(1-b^{-2})>0

uniformly in
m
, we may use positivity to restrict to
m\geq 10cH
and apply (
3.6
), getting

C_{2}(H;\varphi)\gg\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 10cH\\
m\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}(m)}|\Phi_{H}(\sigma)|^{2}\right)\gg H\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 10cH\\
m\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\gg H\cdot H^{\alpha-1+o(1)}=H^{\alpha+o(1)},

where in the second to last step we used the fact that
[B]
has index
\alpha
by Lemma
3.1
. This proves the lower bound in this case.

Case 2:
\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)dt\neq 0
. In this case, we again use positivity to restrict the sum in
C_{2}(H;\varphi)
as

C_{2}(H;\varphi)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r>1\\
r\in[B]\end{subarray}}g(r)^{2}\left|\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right|^{2}.

Let
K\geq 10
be a large constant. Then for
r\geq KcH
, we have
e(j/r)=1+O(1/K)
uniformly for
1\leq j\leq cH
, so from (
3.4
) we get

\displaystyle\left|\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\Big|\sum_{1\leq j\leq cH}\varphi\left(\frac{j}{H}\right)\Big|-O\left(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{1\leq j\leq cH}\left|\varphi\left(\frac{j}{H}\right)\right|\right)=|\Phi_{H}(0)|-O\left(\frac{H}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\geq H\left(\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(t)dt\right|-O\left(\frac{1}{K}\right)\right)-O(1)\gg H,

if
K
is large enough.

Since
g(r)^{2}\gg r^{-2}
for
r\in[B]
, we have

C_{2}(H;\varphi)\gg H^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r>KcH\\
r\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\gg H^{\alpha+o(1)},

since, again by Lemma
3.1
,
[B]
has index
\alpha
. The claim is thus proved in this case as well.
∎

Obviously this implies Proposition
1.8
where
\varphi=\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]}
.

3.3.
Variance for regularly varying
\langle B\rangle

If
\langle B\rangle
is a regularly varying sequence we can say more; in this case we will show the asymptotic formula of Proposition
1.9
.

We begin with a useful expression for
C_{2}(H)
. Throughout this subsection we use the notation

V(t)=\frac{\sin\pi t}{\pi t}.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

(3.7)

C_{2}(H)=2H^{2}\sum_{d\in[B]}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\nmid d\\
b\in B\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}\Big)\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\left|V\left(\frac{H\lambda}{d}\right)\right|^{2}.

Proof.

We begin with the expression in Remark
2.7
for
C_{2}(H;\varphi)
. If we specialize to the case
\varphi=\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]}
, then
\Phi_{H}(t)=E_{H}(t)
. If we use the identity

E_{H}(t)=e\left(\frac{H+1}{2}t\right)\frac{\sin(\pi Ht)}{\sin(\pi t)},

we see that (
2.14
) gives

C_{2}(H)=\sum_{d_{1},d_{2}\in[B]}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\mu_{B}(d_{2})}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<\ell_{1}^{\prime}<d_{1}\\
0<\ell_{2}^{\prime}<d_{2}\\
\ell_{1}^{\prime}/d_{1}=\ell_{2}^{\prime}/d_{2}\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{\sin(\pi H\ell_{1}^{\prime}/d_{1})}{\sin(\pi\ell_{1}^{\prime}/d_{1})}\Big)\Big(\frac{\sin(\pi H\ell_{2}^{\prime}/d_{2})}{\sin(\pi\ell_{2}^{\prime}/d_{2})}\Big).

But in this sum
\ell_{1}^{\prime}/d_{1}=\ell_{2}^{\prime}/d_{2}
and using

\frac{1}{(\sin\pi x)^{2}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(\pi(x+k))^{2}},

we obtain

C_{2}(H)=H^{2}\sum_{d_{1},d_{2}\in[B]}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\mu_{B}(d_{2})}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\neq 0\\
\lambda_{1}/d_{1}=\lambda_{2}/d_{2}\end{subarray}}V\left(\frac{H\lambda_{1}}{d_{1}}\right)V\left(\frac{H\lambda_{2}}{d_{2}}\right)\\
=2H^{2}\sum_{d_{1},d_{2}\in[B]}\frac{\mu_{B}(d_{1})\mu_{B}(d_{2})}{d_{1}d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 1\\
\lambda_{1}/d_{1}=\lambda_{2}/d_{2}\end{subarray}}V\left(\frac{H\lambda_{1}}{d_{1}}\right)V\left(\frac{H\lambda_{2}}{d_{2}}\right).

Write
d=(d_{1},d_{2})
and
d_{1}=\nu_{1}d
,
d_{2}=\nu_{2}d
and parameterize solutions to
\lambda_{1}/d_{1}=\lambda_{2}/d_{2}
by
\lambda_{1}=\lambda\nu_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}=\lambda\nu_{2}
. Then the above expression for
C_{2}(H)
simplifies to

C_{2}(H)=2H^{2}\sum_{d\in[B]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in[B]\\
(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu_{B}(d\nu_{1})\mu_{B}(d\nu_{2})}{d^{2}\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}\sum_{\lambda>0}V\left(\frac{H\lambda}{d}\right)^{2},

which in turn simplifies to (
3.7
).
∎

We will use the following result of Pólya to estimate the above sum.

Proposition 3.5

(Pólya)
.

If
N_{R}(x)
is the counting function of a sequence
R
which regularly varies with index
\alpha
, and if
f\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function that is Riemann integrable over every finite interval
[a,b]\subset[0,\infty)
which satisfies

|f(x)|\ll\begin{cases}x^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}&\textrm{as}\;x\rightarrow 0,\\
x^{-\alpha-\varepsilon}&\textrm{as}\;x\rightarrow\infty,\end{cases}

for some
\varepsilon>0
, then

\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N_{R}(X)}\sum_{r\in R}f\left(\frac{r}{X}\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)d(t^{\alpha}).

Proof.

This can be found in Pólya and Szegő’s book
[
41
, Problem No. 159 in Part II, Chapter 4 of Volume I]
; see also Pólya’s paper
[
40
]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.9
.

We define

\mathcal{F}(s)=\sum_{d\in[B]}\frac{1}{d^{s}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}b\nmid d\\
b\in B\end{subarray}}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}\Big)=\prod_{b\in B}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}+\frac{1}{b^{s}}\Big),

and

\mathcal{G}(s)=\sum_{r\in\langle B\rangle}\frac{1}{r^{s}}=\prod_{b\in B}\Big(1-\frac{1}{b^{s}}\Big)^{-1}.

For both
\mathcal{F}(s)
and
\mathcal{G}(s)
the above sum and Euler product converge absolutely for
\Re\,s>\alpha
. Note that for
\Re\,s>\alpha
we have

(3.8)

\mathcal{F}(s)=\mathcal{G}(s)\mathcal{U}(s),

where

\mathcal{U}(s)=\prod_{b\in B}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}+\frac{2}{b^{1+s}}-\frac{1}{b^{2s}}\Big)=\sum_{c\geq 1}\frac{u_{c}}{c^{s}},

where the coefficients
u_{c}
are defined by this relation. (The coefficients
u_{c}
will be supported on
\langle B\rangle
but this fact will not be important.)

Note the Euler product defining
\mathcal{U}(s)
converges absolutely for
\Re\,s>\alpha/2
, and therefore the Dirichlet series also converges absolutely in this region. Hence it follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{t<c\leq 2t}|u_{c}|\ll t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon},

with the implications

(3.9)

\sum_{c\leq t}|u_{c}|\ll t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon},\hskip 20.0pt\sum_{c\geq t}\frac{|u_{c}|}{c}\ll t^{\frac{\alpha}{2}-1+\varepsilon},

which will be important later.

By using the Dirichlet convolution implicit in (
3.8
), we can write

C_{2}(H)=2\sum_{r\in\langle B\rangle}\sum_{c}\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}u_{c}\cdot\Big(\frac{H}{rc}\Big)^{2}\Big|V\Big(\frac{H\lambda}{rc}\Big)\Big|^{2}=2\sum_{r\in\langle B\rangle}f\left(\frac{r}{H}\right),

for

f(x)=\sum_{c}\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\frac{u_{c}}{(xc)^{2}}\Big|V\Big(\frac{\lambda}{xc}\Big)\Big|^{2}.

But from the bound
V(\xi)^{2}\ll\min(1,1/\xi^{2})
, we have

f(x)\ll\frac{1}{x^{2}}\sum_{c}\frac{|u_{c}|}{c^{2}}\Big(\sum_{\lambda\leq cx}1+\sum_{\lambda>cx}\frac{x^{2}c^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\Big)\ll\sum_{c<1/x}|u_{c}|+\frac{1}{x}\sum_{c\geq 1/x}\frac{|u_{c}|}{c}\ll\begin{cases}\frac{1}{x}&\textrm{if}\;x>1,\\
\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon}&\textrm{if}\;x\leq 1.\end{cases}

The last estimate follows because if
x>1
the first term vanishes while the second is bounded trivially, while if
x\leq 1
both terms satisfy the claimed estimate by (
3.9
).

Therefore one may check that Pólya’s proposition may be applied and

(3.10)

\begin{split}C_{2}(H)&\sim 2N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\alpha t^{\alpha-1}\,dt\\
&=2N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)\sum_{c}\sum_{\lambda\geq 1}\frac{u_{c}}{c^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}V\Big(\frac{\lambda}{tc}\Big)^{2}\alpha t^{\alpha-1}\,dt,\end{split}

where rearrangement of sums and integrals is justified in the second line of (
3.10
) by absolute convergence. By a change of variables
\tau=\lambda/tc
, the last line can be simplified to

2N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)\zeta(2-\alpha)\prod_{b\in B}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}+\frac{2}{b^{1+\alpha}}-\frac{1}{b^{2\alpha}}\Big)\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{1-\alpha}V(\tau)^{2}\,d\tau.

since the sums over
\lambda
and
c
can then be simplified as
\zeta(2-\alpha)
and
\mathcal{U}(\alpha)
respectively. But (see
[
14
, formula 3.823]
)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{1-\alpha}V(\tau)^{2}\,d\tau=-2^{\alpha-1}\pi^{\alpha-2}\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\Gamma(-\alpha),

which recovers the constant (
1.6
) claimed in the proposition.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

We will not require it, but with a bit more work one can show that if
\langle B\rangle
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
\varphi
is of bounded variation with compact support,

C_{2}(H;\varphi)\sim A_{\varphi,\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H),

where

A_{\varphi,\alpha}=2\alpha\zeta(2-\alpha)\prod_{b\in B}\Big(1-\frac{2}{b}+\frac{2}{b^{1+\alpha}}-\frac{1}{b^{2\alpha}}\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau^{1-\alpha}|\hat{\varphi}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau.

4.
Diagonal terms

In this section we show how the approximation of
C_{k}(H;\varphi)
by Gaussian moments arises from terms in which
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
are
paired
and
non-repeated
in the sum defining this quantity. In the next section we will show that the remaining terms are negligible.

We say that the tuple
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
is
paired
if
k
is even and we may partition
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
into
k/2
disjoint pairs
\{(a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k/2}
with
r_{a_{i}}=r_{b_{i}}
and
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
. We say that
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
is
repeated
if
r_{i_{1}}=r_{i_{2}}=r_{i_{3}}
for some
i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}
. For
k=2
all the terms in
C_{2}(H;\varphi)
must be paired.

We adopt the abbreviations
r=[r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}]
for the
\mathrm{lcm}
and
\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
for the vector of the
r_{i}
. Given integers
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{k}>1
belonging to
[B]
, let

S_{H}(\mathbf{r}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r_{i})\,(1\leq i\leq k)\\
\sum\rho_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}F_{H}(\rho_{i}).

Our approach in this section largely follows Montgomery and Soundararajan’s proof of
[
33
, Theorem 1]
. We will use the following variant of the Fundamental Lemma; the result is a generalization of
[
33
, Lemma 2]
, which corresponds to
B
being the set of primes. The original proof
3
3

3

In
[
33
, p. 597]
, the second occurrence of
r_{i}
in the first equation should not be there.
works as is.

Lemma 4.1

(Montgomery and Soundararajan)
.

Let
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}
be integers with
q_{i}>1
and
q_{i}\in[B]
. Let
G
be a complex-valued function defined on
(0,1)
, and suppose that
G_{0}
is a non-decreasing function on
[B]
such that

(4.1)

\sum_{a=1}^{q-1}\left|G\left(\frac{a}{q}\right)\right|^{2}\leq qG_{0}(q)

for all
1\neq q\in[B]
. Then

(4.2)

\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\\
0<a_{i}<q_{i}\\
\sum a_{i}/q_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}G\left(\frac{a_{i}}{q_{i}}\right)\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{[q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}]}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(q_{i}G_{0}(q_{i})^{\frac{1}{2}}).

The next lemma separates repeated or non-paired
r
from what we will show is the main contribution to
C_{k}(H;\varphi)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
, and suppose
\varphi
is of bounded variation and supported in a compact subset of
[0,\infty)
. If
k
is odd we have

(4.3)

C_{k}(H;\varphi)\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r}).

If
k
is even we have

(4.4)

C_{k}(H;\varphi)=\mu_{k}\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k/2}>1}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}g^{2}(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{k/2}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k/2}b_{i}/r_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i})+O\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r})\right)

where

J_{H}(b,n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
(a,n)\,\text{ is $B$-free}\\
(b-a,n)\text{ is $B$-free}\end{subarray}}^{n}\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{a}{n}\right)\Phi_{H}\left(\frac{b-a}{n}\right).

Proof.

We first consider odd
k
. There are no vectors
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})
that are both paired and non-repeated. The proof of this case is concluded by recalling that
g(n)
is dominated by
\mu_{B}^{2}(n)/n
and
\Phi_{H}
by
F_{H}
.

We now consider even
k
. By the triangle inequality,

(4.5)

C_{k}(H;\varphi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is paired}\\
\text{and non-repeated}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}g(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r_{i})\,(1\leq i\leq k)\\
\rho_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{k}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{H}(\rho_{i})+O\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r})\right).

There are
(k-1)(k-3)\cdots 1=\mu_{k}
ways in which the pairing in the first sum in (
4.5
) can occur. We take the pairing to be
r_{i}=r_{k/2+i}
without loss of generality. We further write
\rho_{i}=a_{i}/r_{i}
and set
b_{i}
to be the unique integer in
[1,r_{i}]
congruent to
a_{i}+a_{k/2+i}
modulo
r_{i}
. Hence

(4.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is paired}\\
\text{and non-repeated}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}g(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(r_{i})\,(1\leq i\leq k)\\
\rho_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{k}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Phi_{H}(\rho_{i})

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{k/2}>1\\
\text{distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}g^{2}(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{k/2}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k/2}b_{i}/r_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i})

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k/2}>1}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}g^{2}(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{k/2}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k/2}b_{i}/r_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i})

(4.8)

\displaystyle\qquad+O\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r})\right).

This finishes the proof.
∎

We now show that paired and non-repeated terms above can be reduced to powers of the variance.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
k\geq 4
be even. Suppose
\varphi
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma

3.3
.
If the sequence
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha
then

\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{k/2}>1}\prod_{i=1}^{k/2}g^{2}(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{k/2}\\
1\leq b_{i}\leq r_{i}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k/2}b_{i}/r_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i})=C_{2}(H;\varphi)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+O\left(H^{-\alpha+o(1)}\right)\right).

Proof.

Let
j
be the number of values of
i
for which
b_{i}\neq r_{i}
, so that
b_{i}=r_{i}
for the remaining
k/2-j
values of
i
. Since there are
\binom{k/2}{j}
ways of choosing the
j
indices, we see that the left-hand side is

(4.9)

\sum_{j=0}^{k/2}\binom{\frac{k}{2}}{j}C_{2}(H;\varphi)^{\frac{k}{2}-j}W_{j}(H)

where
W_{0}\equiv 1
and

W_{j}(H)=\sum_{r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}>1}\prod_{i=1}^{j}g^{2}(r_{i})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{j}\\
0<b_{i}<r_{i}\\
\sum b_{i}/r_{i}\equiv 0\text{ mod }1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i}).

Recall
C_{2}(H;\varphi)=H^{\alpha+o(1)}
by Lemma
3.3
. The term
j=0
will contribute the main term, so it remains to bound the other terms. It is also clear that
j=1
contributes
0
as
b_{1}/r_{1}\in(0,1)
.
To finish the proof it suffices to show that
W_{j}(H)=O(H^{\alpha(j-1)+o(1)})
for
j\geq 2
.

Montgomery and Soundararajan
[
33
, equation (34)]
showed that

(4.10)

\sum_{b=1}^{n-1}|J_{H}(b,n)|^{2}\ll n^{3}H

for
B
being the set of primes and
\varphi=\boldsymbol{1}_{(0,1]}
. Their argument works as is for general
B
and general
\varphi
. Indeed their proof proceeds by dropping the conditions
(a,n)=1
and
(b-a,n)=1
from the sum defining
J
, applying the estimate
\Phi_{H}\ll F_{H}
(in the general case this is Lemma
2.3
), and straightforwardly estimating the sum of positive terms that result, so their bound applies to our sum as well.

Due to (
4.10
) we may apply Lemma
4.1
with
G_{0}(n)=CHn^{2}
to find that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b_{1},\ldots,b_{j}\\
0<b_{i}<r_{i}\\
\sum b_{i}/r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}J_{H}(b_{i},r_{i})\ll\frac{1}{[r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}]}\prod_{i=1}^{j}(r_{i}^{2}H^{\frac{1}{2}}).

Additionally, as
|\Phi_{H}(x)|\ll F_{H}(x)\leq 1/\|x\|
, we have the simple bound

\sum_{b=1}^{n-1}|J_{H}(b,n)|\ll n^{2}\sum_{a=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\min\{a,n-a\}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}b=1\\
b\neq a\end{subarray}}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\min\{|b-a|,n-|b-a|\}}\ll n^{2}(\log n)^{2}

and hence, for any value of
Z\geq 1
,

(4.11)

\displaystyle W_{j}(H)

\displaystyle\ll H^{\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}\in[B]\\
[r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}]>Z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{[r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}]}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}\in[B]\\
[r_{1},\ldots,r_{j}]\leq Z\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{j}(\log r_{i})^{2}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq H^{\frac{j}{2}}\sum_{r>Z}\frac{\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},j}(r)}{r}+(\log Z)^{2j}\sum_{r\leq Z}\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},j}(r)

(4.13)

\displaystyle\leq H^{\frac{j}{2}}Z^{\alpha-1+o(1)}+Z^{\alpha+o(1)}.

We take
Z=H^{j/2}
to find that the left-hand side is

\ll H^{\frac{j\alpha}{2}+o(1)}\ll H^{\alpha(j-1)+o(1)}

as
j\geq 2
.
∎

Taken together, Lemma
4.2
and Proposition
4.3
show that paired and non-repeated terms in
C_{k}(H;\varphi)
are enough to recover the claimed main term.

\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>N\\
r>M\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r})\ll H^{\frac{k-1}{2}+o(1)}\left(N^{\frac{1}{2}}+H^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{4(k-1)}}+\mathcal{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}

where

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\\
(s,t)=1,\,s,t\in[B],\,t>s\end{subarray}}s^{-\alpha-3}t^{-\alpha-2}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a:\,(a,t)=1\\
N<ast\leq H^{2}\\
a\in[B]\end{subarray}}a^{-\alpha-1}F_{H}\left(\frac{\|as\tau\|}{as}\right)^{2}\bigg)^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{r}
be a vector which is either repeated or non-paired with
r>M
and such that
r_{i}>N
for all
i
. It suffices to bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>N\\
r>M\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is non-paired}\\
\text{and non-repeated}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>N\\
r>M\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S_{H}(\mathbf{r}).

If
\mathbf{r}
is repeated with
r_{i_{1}}=r_{i_{2}}=r_{i_{3}}
, then we apply
Lemma
5.5
with
r_{i_{1}},r_{i_{2}}
in place of
r_{1},r_{2}
, obtaining

S_{H}(\mathbf{r})\ll r_{1}r_{2}\cdots r_{k}r^{-1}H^{\frac{k}{2}}(T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}+T_{4}).

(The parameter
T
is taken to be
H/N
.) To study the
T_{i}
s, recall that
d=(r_{i_{1}},r_{i_{2}})=r_{i_{1}}
factors as
d=st
, where

s\mid\prod_{i\neq i_{1},i_{2}}r_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\big(t,\prod_{i\neq i_{1},i_{2}}r_{i}\big)=1.

In our case,
t=1
and
s=d=r_{i_{1}}>N
. We have

T_{1}=\frac{\log H}{(\frac{H}{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad T_{2}=d^{-\frac{1}{4}}<N^{-\frac{1}{4}},\quad T_{3}=s^{-\frac{1}{2}}=d^{-\frac{1}{2}}<N^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad T_{4}=0,

so the total contribution of the
T_{i}
’s in the repeated case is at most

(5.11)

\ll H^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(N^{-\frac{1}{4}}+\frac{N^{\frac{1}{2}}\log H}{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>N\\
r_{i}\in[B],\,r>M\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r}\ll H^{\frac{k-1}{2}+o(1)}N^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{r>M}\frac{\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},k}(r)}{r},

which is absorbed in the error term since the series over
r
is
\ll M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{r}
is non-paired and also non-repeated. The contribution of
\mathbf{r}
is
0
if there is a prime
p
dividing only one of the
r_{i}
(as then
S_{H}(\mathbf{r})=0
), so we assume that each prime divisor of
r
divides at least two of the
r_{i}
. This implies that
r_{i}\mid\prod_{j\neq i}r_{j}
. As in
[
34
, p. 323]
, this implies
r_{i}\leq\prod_{j\neq i}(r_{j},r_{i})
and so for each
i
there exists

such that

(r_{j},r_{i})\geq r_{i}^{\frac{1}{k-1}}>N^{\frac{1}{k-1}}.

We claim that there is at least one pair
r_{i},r_{j}
with
i\neq j
,
(r_{j},r_{i})>N^{1/(k-1)}
and
r_{i}\neq r_{j}
. Indeed, if there is no such pair, it means that each value in the multiset
\{r_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\}
appears at least twice. We also know that each value appears at most twice, as we are in the non-repeated case. Hence each value appears twice, contradicting the fact that we are in the non-paired case.

Hence necessarily
r_{i_{1}}\neq r_{i_{2}}
with
(r_{i_{1}},r_{i_{2}})>N^{1/(k-1)}
for some
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
. We again apply
Lemma
5.5
with
r_{i_{1}},r_{i_{2}}
in place of
r_{1},r_{2}
, and
T=H/N
. By definition,
T_{3}=0
. As we have
T_{1}=(\log H)/(H/N)^{1/2}
and
T_{2}=d^{-1/4}<N^{-1/(4(k-1))}
, we get that the total contribution of
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
in the non-paired case is at most

(5.12)

\ll H^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(N^{-\frac{1}{4(k-1)}}+\frac{(\log H)N^{\frac{1}{2}}}{H^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\sum_{r>M}\frac{\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},k}(r)}{r},

which is also absorbed in the error term.

We now treat the contribution of
T_{4}
when
\mathbf{r}
is non-paired and non-repeated, and show that it is at most
H^{k/2-1+o(1)}M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}\mathcal{E}^{1/2}
, so is absorbed as well.

Assuming always that Lemma
5.5
is applied with the first two elements of
\mathbf{r}
(at the cost of a constant of size
k^{2}
from permuting the indices), and recalling that if
\mathbf{r}
is non-paired and non-repeated we may assume
(r_{1},r_{2})>N^{1/(k-1)}
,
we see that
T_{4}
contributes at most

(5.13)

\ll H^{\frac{k}{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{T_{4}(\mathbf{r})}{r}\leq H^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{T_{4}^{2}}{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{1}{r}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

by Cauchy–Schwarz, where

X=\{\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}_{>1}^{k}:N<r_{1},r_{2}\leq H^{2},\,N^{\frac{1}{k-1}}<d\leq\frac{H}{\sqrt{N}},t>d^{\frac{1}{2}},\,r_{i}>N,\,r>M,\,r_{i}\in[B]\}

and
d=(r_{1},r_{2})
,
s=(d,r_{3}\cdots r_{k})
,
t=d/s
. (Note that the condition
t>d^{1/2}
is the same as
t>s
.)
The second sum is at most

(5.14)

\sum_{r>M}\frac{\tau_{B,\mathrm{lcm},k}(r)}{r}\ll M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}.

To study the first sum, we write
r
as
r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}stw
where
r^{\prime}_{i}=r_{i}/d
and
w=r/(r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)
. Note that
r^{\prime}_{1}
,
r^{\prime}_{2}
,
s
and
t
are pairwise coprime so
w
must be an integer. Instead of summing over
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
we sum over
r^{\prime}_{1}
,
r^{\prime}_{2}
,
s
and
t
(so
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are determined). Given
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
w
we have
r
; given
r
,
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
there are at most
\tau^{k-2}(r)
possibilities for
\mathbf{r}
with these values of
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
r
, since each
r_{i}
(
3\leq i\leq k
) divides
r=wr^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st
. Here
\tau
is the usual divisor function. Hence we have

(5.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{T_{4}^{2}}{r}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2},s,t\in[B]\text{ coprime},\,t>s\\
N<r^{\prime}_{i}st\leq H^{2}\,(i=1,2)\\
N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st}T_{4}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w>M/(r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)\\
w\in[B]\\
(w,s)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\tau^{k-2}(wr^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)}{w}

(5.16)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2},s,t\in[B]\text{ coprime},\,t>s\\
N<r^{\prime}_{i}st\leq H^{2}\,(i=1,2)\\
N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{\tau^{k-2}(r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)}{r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st}T_{4}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w>M/(r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)\\
w\in[B]\end{subarray}}\frac{\tau^{k-2}(w)}{w}.

Because
[B]
has index
\alpha
and
\tau(n)=n^{o(1)}
, the innermost sum above is

\ll\left(\frac{M}{r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st}\right)^{\alpha-1+o(1)},

so that

(5.17)

\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{T_{4}^{2}}{r}\leq M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}H^{o(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2},s,t\in[B]\text{ coprime},\,t>s\\
N<r^{\prime}_{i}st\leq H^{2}\,(i=1,2)\\
N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{T_{4}^{2}}{(r^{\prime}_{1}r^{\prime}_{2}st)^{\alpha}}.

Plugging the definition of
T_{4}
in the last equation, and first summing over
s,t
and only later over
r_{i}^{\prime}
we obtain

(5.18)

\sum_{\mathbf{r}\in X}\frac{T_{4}^{2}}{r}\ll M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}H^{o(1)-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\\
(s,t)=1,\,s,t\in[B],\,t>s\end{subarray}}s^{-\alpha-3}t^{-\alpha-2}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a:\,(a,t)=1\\
N<ast\leq H^{2}\\
a\in[B]\end{subarray}}a^{-\alpha-1}F_{H}\left(\frac{\|as\tau\|}{as}\right)^{2}\bigg)^{2}.

Plugging this bound in (
5.13
), we end up with
H^{k/2-1+o(1)}M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}\mathcal{E}^{1/2}
.
∎

In order to get a good upper bound on
\mathcal{E}
we must estimate the frequency with which
\|\|as\tau\|/(as)\|
is smaller than
1/H
. We will do this by reduction to congruence conditions and we will bound sums over such congruence conditions using the following simple consequence of the Pólya–Vinogradov inequality. (For Pólya–Vinogradov see e.g.
[
21
, Theorem 12.5]
).

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\chi
be a Dirichlet character modulo
q
and suppose
A\gg q
. We have

\left|\sum_{1\leq|i|\leq q/2}\chi(i)F_{H}\left(\frac{i}{A}\right)^{2}\right|\ll\begin{cases}AH&\text{if }\chi\text{ is principal,}\\
\min\left\{AH,H^{2}\sqrt{q}\log q\right\}&\text{if }\chi\text{ is non-principal.}\end{cases}

Proof.

By the definition of
F_{H}
,

(5.19)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{1\leq|i|\leq q/2}\chi(i)F_{H}\left(\frac{i}{A}\right)^{2}\right|\ll H^{2}\left|\sum_{1\leq|i|\leq A/H}\chi(i)\right|+A^{2}\left|\sum_{|i|>A/H}\frac{\chi(i)}{i^{2}}\right|.

The bound for principal characters is evident, so we now consider non-principal ones. The sum over smaller
|i|
satisfies the required bound by combining the Pólya–Vinogradov inequality

\sum_{1\leq|i|\leq n}\chi(i)=O(\sqrt{q}\log q)

with the trivial bound
|\chi|\leq 1
. To bound the sum over larger
i
, we can either use the trivial bound
\ll H/A
, or else again appeal to Pólya–Vinogradov together with partial summation as follows:

\left|\sum_{i>N}\frac{\chi(b)}{b^{2}}\right|\ll\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left|\sum_{i\leq N}\chi(i)\right|+\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{\left|\sum_{n\leq y}\chi(n)\right|}{y^{3}}dy\ll\frac{\sqrt{q}\log q}{N^{2}},

where
N=A/H
.
∎

In what follows we use the notation
a\sim x
to mean
x<a\leq 2x
. The following proposition will be used shortly.

Proposition 5.8
.

Suppose
\langle B\rangle
is of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
s,t\in[B]
be coprime positive integers with
t\leq H
. Let
r
be a
B
-free divisor of
t
. Given
A\leq H^{2}
let

G_{r,v}(\tau_{1}):=A^{-(\alpha+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|i|\leq t/(2r)\\
(i,t/r)=1\end{subarray}}F_{H}\left(\frac{i}{2Ast/r}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,\,a\in[B],\,(a,t)=1\\
a\equiv is^{-1}(\tau_{1}/r)^{-1}\text{ mod }t/r\end{subarray}}1

for
\tau_{1}
with
(t,\tau_{1})=r
. We have

(5.20)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}G_{r,v}(\tau_{1})^{2}\ll\frac{H^{2}}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\left(\left(\frac{st}{r}\right)^{2}A^{o(1)}+A^{-2(\alpha+1)}\min\left\{H^{2}\frac{t^{1+o(1)}}{r},\left(\frac{Ast}{r}\right)^{2}\right\}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }t/r}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,\,a\in[B]\\
(a,t)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(a)\right|^{2}\right).

Proof.

Expanding the square we have

(5.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}G_{r,v}(\tau_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle\ll A^{-2(\alpha+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|i_{1}|,|i_{2}|\leq t/(2r)\\
(i_{1}i_{2},t/r)=1\end{subarray}}F_{H}\left(\frac{i_{1}}{2Ast/r}\right)^{2}F_{H}\left(\frac{i_{2}}{2Ast/r}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim A,\,a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\\
(a_{1}a_{2},t)=1\\
a_{1}\equiv i_{1}s^{-1}(\tau_{1}/r)^{-1}\text{ mod }t/r\\
a_{2}\equiv i_{2}s^{-1}(\tau_{1}/r)^{-1}\text{ mod }t/r\end{subarray}}1.

The two congruences in the innermost sum imply

(5.22)

a_{1}i_{2}\equiv a_{2}i_{1}\text{ mod }\frac{t}{r},

and we may replace the inner double sum over
\tau_{1}
,
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
with the sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim A,\,a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\\
(a_{1}a_{2},t)=1\\
a_{1}i_{2}\equiv a_{2}i_{1}\text{ mod }t/r\end{subarray}}1.

We can detect (
5.22
) using orthogonality of characters, obtaining

(5.23)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}G_{r,v}(\tau_{1})^{2}\ll

\displaystyle\frac{A^{-2(\alpha+1)}}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }t/r}\left(\sum_{1\leq|i_{1}|\leq t/(2r)}\chi(i_{1})F_{H}\left(\frac{i_{1}r}{2Ast}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}\sim A,\,a_{1}\in[B]\\
(a_{1},t)=1\end{subarray}}\overline{\chi}(a_{1})\right)

(5.24)

\displaystyle\times\left(\sum_{1\leq|i_{2}|\leq t/r}\overline{\chi}(i_{2})F\left(\frac{i_{2}r}{2Ast}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{2}\sim A,\,a_{2}\in[B]\\
(a_{2},t)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(a_{2})\right)

(5.25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }t/r}A^{-2(\alpha+1)}\left|\sum_{1\leq|i|\leq t/(2r)}\chi(i)F_{H}\left(\frac{ir}{2Ast}\right)^{2}\right|^{2}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,\,a\in[B]\\
(a,t)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(a)\right|^{2}.

By Lemma
5.7
, the contribution of the principal character
\chi=\chi_{0}
is

\ll\frac{A^{o(1)}}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\left(\frac{Hst}{r}\right)^{2},

which gives the first term in the required bound. We now consider the non-principal characters. Applying the pointwise bound for the sum of
F
twisted by
\chi
as given in Lemma
5.7
, we see that they contribute

\ll H^{2}A^{-2(\alpha+1)}\min\left\{\frac{H^{2}t}{r}\log^{2}t,\left(\frac{Ast}{r}\right)^{2}\right\}\frac{1}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\sum_{\chi_{0}\neq\chi\text{ mod }t/r}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,\,a\in[B]\\
(a,t)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(a)\right|^{2}.

This gives the second contribution to the bound, and we are done.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
N
be in the range
H\geq N\geq H^{8/9}
. Suppose that

\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha\in(0,1)
. In the notation of Proposition
5.6
, we have

\mathcal{E}\ll H^{2+o(1)}\left(\left(\frac{H}{N^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)^{\alpha}+N^{-\frac{\alpha}{k-1}}\right).

Proof.

We dyadically decompose the inner sum over
a
in the definition of
\mathcal{E}
.
Given coprime integers
s,t\in[B]
with
t>1
and
\tau=\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)
, and setting
r:=(t,\tau_{1})
for convenience, we have

(5.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim U,\,a\in[B]\\
(a,t)=1\end{subarray}}F_{H}\left(\frac{\|as\tau\|}{as}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq t/(2r)\\
(i,t/r)=1\end{subarray}}F_{H}\left(\frac{i}{2Ust/r}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim U,\,a\in[B],\,(a,t)=1\\
a\equiv\pm is^{-1}(\tau_{1}/r)^{-1}\text{ mod }t/r\end{subarray}}1

(5.27)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|i|\leq t/(2r)\\
(i,t/r)=1\end{subarray}}F_{H}\left(\frac{i}{2Ust/r}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim U,\,a\in[B],\,(a,t)=1\\
a\equiv is^{-1}(\tau_{1}/r)^{-1}\text{ mod }t/r\end{subarray}}1

for any positive integer
U
, as we now explain.

First, we may replace

F_{H}\left(\frac{\|as\tau\|}{as}\right)\quad\text{ with }\quad F_{H}\left(\frac{\|as\tau\|}{2Us}\right)

as in general if
0<x,y\leq 1/2
satisfy
y\ll x
then
F_{H}(x)\ll F_{H}(y)
. The denominator of the fraction
\|as\tau\|
in reduced form is exactly
t/(t,\tau_{1})
, since both
s
and
a
are coprime with
t
.
We write the left-hand side of (
5.26
) as a sum over the possible values of
\|as\tau\|
, and need to count the number of times a given value is obtained, that is, count solutions to
\|as\tau\|=i/(t/r)
, which is an equation that determines
a
modulo
t/r
up to a sign, yielding the new inner sum over
a
.

Putting this in the definition of
\mathcal{E}
and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we thus obtain,

(5.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\ll H^{o(1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{1/(k-1)}\leq st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\\
(s,t)=1,\,s,t\in[B],\,t>s\end{subarray}}s^{-\alpha-3}t^{-\alpha-2}\sum_{r\mid t}\sum_{2^{v}\in(N/(2st),H^{2}/(st))}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}G^{2}_{r,v}.

Now, by orthogonality we have

\frac{1}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}\sum_{\chi\text{ mod }{t/r}}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim 2^{v}\\
a\in[B]\end{subarray}}\chi(a)\bigg|^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim 2^{v}\\
a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\\
(a_{1}a_{2},t/r)=1\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{a_{1}\equiv a_{2}\text{ mod }{t/r}}.

Thus, using this in the conclusion of Proposition
5.8
with
A=2^{v}
, we get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau_{1}/t\in\mathcal{R}_{B}(t)\\
(t,\tau_{1})=r\end{subarray}}G^{2}_{r,v}\ll H^{2}\left(\frac{s^{2}\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)^{2}}{\phi\left(\frac{t}{r}\right)}H^{o(1)}+2^{-2v}\min\left\{\frac{H^{2}t}{r},\left(\frac{2^{v}st}{r}\right)^{2}\right\}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2\alpha v}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim 2^{v}\\
a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{a_{1}\equiv a_{2}\text{ mod }{t/r}}\right).

Inserting this estimate back into our upper bound for
\mathcal{E}
, we obtain a bound

\mathcal{E}\ll H^{2+o(1)}(\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2}+\mathcal{E}_{3}),

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{1/(k-1)}<st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\\
(s,t)=1,\,t>s,\,s,t\in[B]\end{subarray}}(ts)^{-\alpha-1}\sum_{r\mid t}r^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{1/(k-1)}<st\leq H/\sqrt{N}\\
(s,t)=1,\,t>s,\,s,t\in[B]\end{subarray}}s^{-\alpha-3}t^{-\alpha-2}\sum_{r\mid t}\sum_{N/(2st)<2^{v}\leq H^{2}/(st)}2^{-2(\alpha+1)v}\min\left\{\frac{H^{2}t}{r},\left(\frac{2^{v}st}{r}\right)^{2}\right\}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim 2^{v}\\
a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\\
a_{1}=a_{2}\end{subarray}}1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{r\leq H/\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in[B]\\
s\leq H/(r\sqrt{N})\end{subarray}}s^{-\alpha-3}\sum_{N^{3/2}/(2H)<2^{v}\leq H^{2}/N^{1/(k-1)}}2^{-2(\alpha+1)v}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1},a_{2}\sim 2^{v}\\
a_{1},a_{2}\in[B]\\
a_{1}\neq a_{2}\end{subarray}}\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell:\,r\ell\in[B]\\

where the variable

Remark 5.10
.

It is worth highlighting the main novelty of our argument over the work in
[
34
, Lemma 8]
, specifically the treatment of the expressions
G_{r,v}(\tau_{1})
in the notation of Proposition
5.8
.

For simplicity, assume that
r=1
and, fixing
v
, write
A=2^{v}
. In the context of
[
34
]
we may replace
[B]
with the set of divisors
t
of the (squarefree) modulus
q
, in which case one may estimate the inner sum in
G_{1,v}(\tau_{1})
using the simple bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,\,a\mid q\\
(a,t)=1\\
a\equiv b\text{ mod }{t}\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A\\
a\equiv b\text{ mod }{t}\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{A}{t}+1,

uniformly
over both reduced residues
b\text{ mod }{t}
, and over
t
. This follows from the equidistribution in residue classes of integers in an interval. While somewhat crude, this estimate suffices to produce the required power savings in
H
in Montgomery and Vaughan’s analogue of
\mathcal{E}
, as found in Proposition
5.6
.

In contrast, when
B
is a sparse set of index
\alpha<1
we cannot reasonably hope for such equidistribution in residue classes in general. Even if, say, the optimistic bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\sim A,a\in[B]\\
(a,t)=1\\
a\equiv b\text{ mod }{t}\end{subarray}}1\ll(At)^{o(1)}\left(\frac{A^{\alpha}}{t}+1\right)

held uniformly in
b\in(\mathbb{Z}/t\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
, tracing through the remainder of the proof of
[
34
, Lemma 8]
we would find that the corresponding savings obtained is of the shape
H^{1/2-\alpha+o(1)}
, and therefore only provides power savings for
\alpha>1/2
. To deal with the most general situation (i.e., potentially with
\alpha\leq 1/2
and no guarantee of equidistribution) we cannot simply rely on pointwise counts for elements of
[B]
in residue classes. The proof of Proposition
5.9
demonstrates that power savings in
H
may be obtained upon
averaging
in both the residue class
b\text{ mod }{t}
and the modulus
t
.

6.
The central limit theorem for general weights
\varphi

6.1.
A proof of the central limit theorem

We assemble the estimates of the previous sections to show that
C_{k}(H;\varphi)
and therefore
M_{k}(X,H;\varphi)
exhibit Gaussian behavior.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose
\varphi
is of bounded variation and supported in a compact subset of
[0,\infty)
. Assume moreover that
\varphi
is non-vanishing on some open interval. If
\langle B\rangle
has index
\alpha\in(0,1)
then

C_{k}(H;\varphi)=C_{2}(H;\varphi)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(\mu_{k}+O(H^{-\frac{c}{k}})\right)

for every positive integer
k
. Here
c
is an absolute constant depending only on
\alpha
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
and Proposition
4.3
we have

(6.1)

C_{k}(H;\varphi)=\mu_{k}C_{2}(H;\varphi)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(1+O\left(H^{-\alpha+o(1)}\right)\right)+O\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S(\mathbf{r})\bigg).

Let
H^{8/9}\leq N\leq H
and
M>1
. By Lemmas
5.3
–
5.4
and Proposition
5.6
we have

(6.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\text{ is repeated}\\
\text{or non-paired}\\
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}>1\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\mu_{B}^{2}(r_{i})}{r_{i}}S(\mathbf{r})\leq M^{\alpha+o(1)}+H^{\frac{k-1}{2}+o(1)}\left(N^{\frac{1}{2}}+H^{\frac{1}{2}}N^{-\frac{1}{4(k-1)}}+\mathcal{E}^{\frac{1}{2}}H^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)M^{\alpha-1+o(1)}.

We apply Proposition
5.9
to bound
\mathcal{E}
, so that the right-hand side is

\ll M^{\alpha+o(1)}\left(1+\frac{H^{\frac{k}{2}+o(1)}}{M}\left(\left(\frac{N}{H}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\frac{H}{N^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}+N^{-\frac{\min\{\alpha/2,1/4\}}{k-1}}\right)\right).

We now choose
N=H^{1-c_{1}}
and
M=H^{k/2-c_{2}/k}
where
c_{i}
are sufficiently small with respect to
\alpha
, and recall
C_{2}(H;\varphi)=H^{\alpha+o(1)}
by Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Obviously this implies Theorem
1.10
and thus the central limit theorem, Theorem
1.11
for flat counts in short intervals. In fact more generally, combining Theorem
6.1
with Proposition
2.1
we see that as long as
H\leq X^{c_{\alpha}/k-\varepsilon}
, we have

(6.3)

M_{k}(X,H;\varphi)=C_{2}(H;\varphi)^{\frac{k}{2}}\left(\mu_{k}+O(H^{-\frac{c}{k}})\right).

By the moment method this implies that weighted counts also satisfy a central limit theorem.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
H=H(X)
satisfy

H\rightarrow\infty,\text{ yet }\,\,\frac{\log H}{\log X}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }X\rightarrow\infty,

and choose a random integer
n\in[1,X]
at uniform. Suppose that
\langle B\rangle
is a regularly varying sequence of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varphi
is a real-valued function of bounded variation and supported in a compact subset of
[0,\infty)
and non-vanishing on some open interval. Then the random variable

\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{2}(H;\varphi)}}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\Big(\frac{u-n}{H}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(u)-\mathcal{M}_{B}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\Big(\frac{h}{H}\Big)\right)

tends to the standard normal distribution
N_{\mathbb{R}}(0,1)
as
X\rightarrow\infty
.

6.2.
An application to long gaps

Estimate (
6.3
) allows us to obtain strong information about the frequency of long gaps between consecutive
B
-free integers. Given
1\leq H\leq X
, let

\mathcal{G}(X,H):=\{n\leq X:N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)=0\}.

Thus,
|\mathcal{G}(X,H)|
records the number of length
H
intervals with an endpoint
n\in[1,X]
that contains no
B
-free numbers.

Improving on work of Plaksin
[
39
]
, Matomäki
[
29
]
used a sieve-theoretic method to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

|\mathcal{G}(X,H)|\ll XH^{-1+\varepsilon}\text{ for $1\leq H\leq X^{\frac{1}{6}-\varepsilon}$}

(with no upper bound constraint on the range of
H
if
B
consists only of primes). As a consequence of our
k
th-moment bounds, we can prove the following.

Corollary 6.3
.

If
\langle B\rangle
is of index
\alpha\in(0,1)
then for any
k\geq 1
and
1\leq H\leq X^{c_{\alpha}/k-\varepsilon}
we have
|\mathcal{G}(X,H)|\ll_{k}XH^{-(2-\alpha)k}
.

Since we have
(2-\alpha)k\geq 1
whenever
k\geq 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, our result improves on that of Matomäki in some range of
H
. Note that, for instance, if
k=2
then
c_{\alpha}/k\geq 1/6
whenever
0<\alpha<(7-\sqrt{33})/2=0.6277...
, and our range contains hers, at least if
B
does not consist only of primes.

Proof.

If
n\in\mathcal{G}(X,H)
then we of course have

\left(N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)-\mathcal{M}_{B}H\right)^{2k}=(\mathcal{M}_{B}H)^{2k}.

Combined with Proposition
1.8
and Theorem
1.10
, this implies that if
H\leq X^{c_{\alpha}/k-\varepsilon}
then

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{G}(X,H)|}{X}(\mathcal{M}_{B}H)^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n\leq X}\left(N_{B\text{-free}}(n,H)-\mathcal{M}_{B}H\right)^{2k}=C_{2k,B}(H)+o(H^{k\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mu_{2k}C_{2}(H)^{k}+o(H^{k\alpha})\ll_{k}H^{k\alpha}.

We deduce immediately that
|\mathcal{G}(X,H)|\ll_{k}XH^{k\alpha-2k}=XH^{-k(2-\alpha)}
, as claimed.
∎

7.
Fractional Brownian motion

7.1.
Convergence in
C[0,1]

We now prove Theorem
1.14
, showing that a random walk on the
B
-frees tends to a fractional Brownian motion. There is ready-made machinery to demonstrate that a sequence of random elements of
C[0,1]
, like
W_{X}(t)
in (
1.11
), tend in distribution to a limiting element and we cite the relevant results here. (For a motivated exposition on convergence of random functions in
C[0,1]
, see for instance
[
5
]
.)

Theorem 7.1
.

If
Y,Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
are random elements of
C[0,1]
, then
Y_{n}
converges in distribution to
Y
as
n\rightarrow\infty
as long as both of the following conditions are met:

(i)

(convergence of finite-dimensional distributions) for any
k\geq 1
and
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in[0,1]^{k}
, we have convergence in distribution of the random vector

(Y_{n}(t_{1}),\ldots,Y_{n}(t_{k}))\rightarrow(Y(t_{1}),\ldots,Y(t_{k})),

(ii)

(tightness) the sequence of random elements
Y_{n}
of
C[0,1]
is tight.
4
4

4

That is for all
\varepsilon>0
, there is a compact subset
K
of
C[0,1]
such that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}\notin K)<\varepsilon
for all sufficiently large
n
. For
C[0,1]
this is equivalent to the condition that
Y_{n}(0)
is a tight family of real-valued random variables and
\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(\omega(Y_{n},\delta)\geq\varepsilon)=0
, where the modulus of continuity of a function
f\in C[0,1]
is given by
\omega(f,\delta)=\sup_{|s-t|\leq\delta}|f(s)-f(t)|;
see
[
5
, Theorem 7.3]
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of
[
23
, Lemma 16.2 and Theorem 16.3]
.
∎

We also have the following device for proving tightness.

Theorem 7.2

(Kolmogorov–Chentsov)
.

Using the notation of the previous theorem, if
Y_{n}(0)=0
for all
n
and if there are an absolute constant
C
and constants
a,b>0
such that

\sup_{n}\,\mathbb{E}\,|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|^{a}\leq C|s-t|^{1+b},

for all
s,t\in[0,1]
, then the sequence of random elements
Y_{n}
of
C[0,1]
is tight.

Proof.

This is a special case of
[
23
, Corollary 16.9]
.
∎

7.2.
The
B
-free random walk

We apply these results to the random functions
W_{X}(t)
with
X\rightarrow\infty
. We need one last lemma regarding regularly varying sequences.

Lemma 7.3
.

If we have a sequence of natural numbers
J
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(0,1)
then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\frac{N_{J}(tH)}{N_{J}(H)}\ll_{\varepsilon}t^{\alpha-\varepsilon},

for all
t\leq 1
and
H
larger than the first element of
J
.

Proof.

Obviously the result is true if
tH<1
, so suppose
tH\geq 1
.

If
J\subset\mathbb{N}
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then there is some slowly varying
L
such that
N_{J}(x)\ll x^{\alpha}L(x)
for all
x
and
N_{J}(x)\gg x^{\alpha}L(x)
for all
x
larger than the first element of
J
. For convenience we will take
L(x)
to be defined for all
x\geq 1
with

\inf_{x\in[1,K]}L(x)>0\text{ for any }K>0.

(The reader should check that this may be done.)

It then follows from Karamata’s representation of slowly varying functions (see
[
26
, consequence (2.5) of Theorem 2.2 in p. 180]
) that if
t\leq 1
,

\frac{L(tH)}{L(H)}\leq 2t^{-\varepsilon},

for
tH
and
H
sufficiently large depending on
\varepsilon
. By compactness then

\frac{L(tH)}{L(H)}\ll_{\varepsilon}t^{-\varepsilon}

for
tH,H\geq 1
, which implies the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.14
.

Let
\{Z(t):\,t\in[0,1]\}
be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
\alpha/2
. By Theorem
7.1
we need only demonstrate (i) the finite dimensional distributions of
W_{X}(t)
tend to those of
Z(t)
and (ii) tightness for the family
W_{X}
.

Let us treat (i) first. Note for each
t\in[0,1]
we have
\mathbb{E}\,W_{X}(t)\rightarrow 0
as
X\rightarrow\infty
(which agrees of course with
\mathbb{E}\,Z(t)=0
). Moreover, for
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s>t
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,|W_{X}(s)-W_{X}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)}\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n=\lfloor tH\rfloor}^{X+\lfloor sH\rfloor}|N_{B\text{-free}}(n,\lfloor(s-t)H\rfloor)-\mathcal{M}_{B}\lfloor(s-t)H\rfloor+O(1)|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{N_{\langle B\rangle}((s-t)H)}{N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)}\sim(s-t)^{\alpha},

by Proposition
1.9
. As
W_{X}(0)=0
, this allows one to deduce that
W_{X}(t)
has the same limiting covariance function as
Z(t)
.

Thus we will have the finite dimensional distributions of
W_{X}(t)
tend to those of
Z(t)
if we show for any
k\geq 1
and any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
that the vector
(W_{X}(t_{1}),\ldots,W_{X}(t_{k}))
tends to a Gaussian vector. By the Cramér–Wold device
[
4
, Theorem 29.4]
this will be true if for any fixed real numbers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
the random variable

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\theta_{j}W_{X}(t_{j})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A_{\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\theta_{j}Q(t_{j}\cdot H)

tends to a real valued Gaussian distribution. But

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\theta_{j}Q(t_{j}\cdot H)=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\Big(\frac{u-n}{H}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{B\text{-free}}(u)-\mathcal{M}_{B}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi\Big(\frac{h}{H}\Big)+O(1),\quad\textrm{for}\quad\varphi=\sum\theta_{j}\mathbf{1}_{(0,t_{j}]},

and so the Gaussian behavior follows from Theorem
6.2
.

We now demonstrate (ii). Note that for any positive integer
\nu
and
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,|W_{X}(s)-W_{X}(t)|^{2\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(A_{\alpha}N_{\langle B\rangle}(H))^{\nu}}\frac{1}{X}\sum_{n=\lfloor tH\rfloor}^{X+\lfloor sH\rfloor}|N_{B\text{-free}}(n,\lfloor(s-t)H\rfloor)-\mathcal{M}_{B}\lfloor(s-t)H\rfloor+O(1)|^{2\nu}

\displaystyle\ll_{\nu}\frac{(N_{\langle B\rangle}((s-t)H)+1)^{\nu}}{N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)^{\nu}}\ll_{\nu,\varepsilon}|s-t|^{(\alpha-\varepsilon)\nu},

as long as
X
(and thus
H
) is sufficiently large so that
N_{\langle B\rangle}(H)
is non-zero, using Lemma
7.3
in the last step.
Hence choosing
\varepsilon
smaller than
\alpha
and
\nu
large enough that
(\alpha-\varepsilon)\nu>1
, condition (ii) follows from the Kolmogorov–Chentsov Theorem. This completes the proof.
