Multilinear Kernel Regression and Imputation via Manifold Learning
Duc Thien Nguyen, Konstantinos Slavakis 1

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel nonparametric framework for data imputation, coined multilinear kernel regression and imputation via the manifold assumption (MultiL-KRIM). Motivated by manifold learning, MultiL-KRIM models data features as a point cloud located in or close to a user-unknown smooth manifold embedded in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. Unlike typical manifold-learning routes, which seek low-dimensional patterns via regularizers based on graph-Laplacian matrices, MultiL-KRIM builds instead on the intuitive concept of tangent spaces to manifolds and incorporates collaboration among point-cloud neighbors (regressors) directly into the data-modeling term of the loss function. Multiple kernel functions are allowed to offer robustness and rich approximation properties, while multiple matrix factors offer low-rank modeling, integrate dimensionality reduction, and streamline computations with no need of training data. Two important application domains showcase the functionality of MultiL-KRIM: time-varying-graph-signal (TVGS) recovery, and reconstruction of highly accelerated dynamic-magnetic-resonance-imaging (dMRI) data. Extensive numerical tests on real and synthetic data demonstrate MultiL-KRIM’s remarkable speedups over its predecessors, and outperformance over prevalent “shallow” data-imputation techniques, with a more intuitive and explainable pipeline than deep-image-prior methods.

Introduction

Missing data occur frequently across different fields, such
as recommender systems
[
1
]
, remote
sensing
[
2
]
, sensor
networks
[
3
]
, and accelerated medical
imaging
[
4
,
5
]
,
inflicting bias and deficiency on downstream data-analysis
tasks. Amidst a wide range of strategies for data
imputation,
imputation-by-regression
is one of
the most popular frameworks, where measured data act as
regressors while missing data are imputed by the fitted
regression model and measured data
[
6
]
.

Imputation of missing data is typically viewed as a
matrix/tensor completion problem, since (dynamic) images, or
spatio-temporal data of sensor networks are usually arranged
into matrices/tensors. Popular data-imputation approaches
are compressed sensing, e.g.,
[
7
]
, and
low-rank methods which are realized via a decomposition of
the data matrix into low-rank matrix
factors
[
8
,
9
]
. On the
other hand, dictionary learning
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]

factorizes the data matrix into an over-complete (“fat”)
matrix and a sparse one. Sequential multi-layer
factorization of the data matrix has been also
reported
[
14
]
. Non-linear
activation function can be also applied to those layers,
resulting into deep matrix
factorizations
[
15
,
16
]
, similarly
to deep neural networks. Tensors can be reshaped
(“flattened”) into matrices to enjoy the tools of matrix
completion. However, there exist concepts of multilinear
rank inherent to tensors, such as the CP and Tucker rank,
yielding CP and Tucker tensor
decompositions
[
17
]
. Recently, tensor
networks offer generalized tensor
decompositions
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. All of the aforementioned methods fall
under the umbrella of parametric modeling, where the
characteristic is that model dimensions, that is, inner
dimensions of the matrix/tensor factorizations, do not scale
with the number of the observed data.

Another recently popular path of parametric modeling is deep
learning (DeepL), e.g.,
[
21
,
22
]
. Nevertheless, not only is DeepL usually
training-data hungry and computationally heavy, but concerns
were also raised in
[
23
]
about
instabilities in medical-image reconstruction. Deep image
priors (DIP)
[
24
]
and untrained neural
networks offer user-defined priors to alleviate the need for
massive training data, and have been used in signal
recovery
[
25
,
26
]
and
accelerated medical imaging
[
27
,
28
]
.

In contrast to the previous parametric methods,
nonparametric regression operates without any priors and
statistical assumptions on the data in an effort to reduce
as much as possible the bias inflicted on data modeling by
the user
[
29
]
. The price to be paid
for this distribution-free approach is that the dimensions
of the nonparametric-regression model scales with the number
of observed data. Within nonparametric-regression methods,
the kernel-based ones stand out thanks to their highly
intuitive and explainable functional forms to approximate
involved non-linear
functions
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
. The crux is to use a non-linear feature
mapping
\varphi(\cdot)
to map data from their input space
to a high-, even infinite-dimensional feature space, where
linear operations are used to reflect non-linear operations
in the original input space. If the feature space is chosen
to be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)

\mathscr{H}
, with well-known and versatile properties in
approximation theory
[
35
]
, inner
products in
\mathscr{H}
can be calculated via simple
functional evaluations in the input space (“kernel
trick”)
[
36
]
.

Aiming at learning methods for high-dimensional data,
manifold learning (ManL) offers a framework where
data/features are considered to be points in or close to a
low-dimensional (non-linear) smooth manifold
\mathscr{M}

embedded in a high-dimensional ambient
space
[
37
]
. ManL affords many degrees of
freedom in data modeling because it adopts a minimal number
of assumptions; no specifics of
\mathscr{M}
are presumed
other than its smoothness. ManL identifies low-dimensional
data patterns to effect dimensionality reduction. More
specifically, “local approaches,” including local linear
embedding (LLE)
[
38
]
and Laplacian
eigenmaps
[
39
]
, effect dimensionality
reduction by preserving local proximity among data, while
“global approaches,” such as
ISOMAP
[
40
]
, preserve geodesic
distances between all pairs of points. While local
approaches may fail to capture long-range dependencies in
the point-cloud, global ones are usually computationally
costly. The standard ManL route for data imputation is to
use a graph Laplacian matrix to model the interdependencies
among the rows/columns of the data
matrix
[
41
,
42
]
. This
approach has been widely used in recommender
systems
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
to construct users/items
interaction. Due to the focus on “locality” by the graph Laplacian
matrix, the computed interpolation functions appear to be
non-continuous at sampled points, especially in cases of
severe under-sampling
[
46
]
.

Different from parametric modeling and motivated by ManL,
this paper develops a generalization, abbreviated hereafter

MultiLinear Kernel Regression and Imputation via the
Manifold assumption (MultiL-KRIM),
of the nonparametric
kernel-based approach of
[
47
,
48
]
. Features are assumed to form a
point-cloud which lies in or close to a smooth manifold

\mathscr{M}
embedded in an RKHS
\mathscr{H}
. Unlike
popular decomposition-based methods, such as
low-rank-
[
49
,
50
]
,
tensor-based
[
51
,
52
]
and
dictionary learning
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
,
which promote a “blind decomposition” of the data
matrix/tensor, and unlike standard ManL routes which are
based on Laplacian-matrix-based regularization of a
regression loss
[
41
,
42
]
, MultiL-KRIM explores
collaborative-filtering and tangent-space modeling ideas to
extract and at the same time incorporate latent feature
geometry
directly
into data-matrix
decompositions.
MultiL-KRIM needs no training data to operate, belongs to
the family of nonparametric regression to reduce its
dependence on the probability distribution of the data as

29
]
, and offers an
explainable and simple geometric learning paradigm, unlike
the majority of DeepL schemes which are based on perplexed

Unlike its bilinear predecessors

47
]
and

48

pre-steps

flexible matrix factors for higher computational efficiency

To showcase the functionality of MultiL-KRIM, two imputation

(i)

the recently popular problem of time-varying
graph-signal (TVGS) recovery (
Section

IV
), and

(ii)

signal reconstruction in dynamic magnetic resonance
imaging (dMRI) under severe under-sampling

Section

V
). This manuscript extends the short
paper
[
53
]
, which considers the dMRI

The extensive numerical tests in
Sections

IV
and
V

illustrate that MultiL-KRIM outperforms various
state-of-the-art “shallow” parametric and nonparametric
methods.

The rest of the paper is organized as
follows.

II
The Imputation-by-Regression Problem and Prior Art

Data are arranged in an
N
-mode tensor
\mathbfscr{Y}
of
size
I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\cdots\times I_{N}
, where

\{I_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{N}_{*}
(
\mathbb{N}_{*}
is the
set of all positive integers). The observed data take values
from the set of all complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, while
entries of
\mathbfscr{Y}
which correspond to missing data
are set to be equal to
+\infty
. In other words,

\mathbfscr{Y}\in(\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\})^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\cdots\times I_{N}}
.

Tensor
\mathbfscr{Y}
can be

unfolded/matricized/flattened
along mode
k
into
a matrix
\mathbf{Y}_{(k)}

[
17
]
. For
simplicity, assume that
\mathbfscr{Y}
is unfolded along
mode
N
, and

\mathbf{Y}\coloneqq\mathbf{Y}_{(N)}\in(\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\})^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
, where

I_{0}\coloneqq I_{1}I_{2}\cdots I_{N-1}
. Let the
(i,t)
th
entry of
\mathbf{Y}
be denoted by

y_{it}\coloneqq[\mathbf{Y}]_{it}
. Denote also the rows and
columns of
\mathbf{Y}
as

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I_{0}}
and

\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{I_{N}}
,
respectively; that is,

\mathbf{Y}=[\mathbf{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{I_{0}}]^{\intercal}=[\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{I_{N}}]
,
where
\intercal
denotes vector/matrix transposition. For
rigorous discussions, let the index set of observed entries

\Omega\coloneqq\{(i,t)\in\{1,\ldots,I_{0}\}\times\{1,\ldots,I_{N}\}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}y_{it}\neq+\infty\}
,
and define the linear
sampling mapping

\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}\colon(\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\})^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}\colon\mathbf{Y}\mapsto\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})
, which
operates entry-wisely as follows:

[\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})]_{it}\coloneqq[\mathbf{Y}]_{it}
, if
(i,t)\in\Omega
, while

[\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})]_{it}\coloneqq 0
, if

(i,t)\notin\Omega
.

To cover a wide range of application domains, it is assumed
that data
\mathbf{Y}
, observed in a input-data domain

\mathscr{D}_{y}\subset(\mathbb{C}\cup\{+\infty\})^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
, possess also a representation over another
domain
\mathscr{D}_{x}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
,
and that the “link” between
\mathscr{D}_{y}
and

\mathscr{D}_{x}
is a transform mapping

\mathscr{T}\colon\mathscr{D}_{x}\to\mathscr{D}_{y}
, which
is usually considered to be invertible. In other words, it
is assumed that there exists an

\mathbf{X}\in\mathscr{D}_{x}\subset\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
such that (s.t.)
\mathscr{T}(\mathbf{X})
agrees with

\mathbf{Y}\in\mathscr{D}_{y}
on its observed entries at
positions
\Omega
. For example, in the context of

Section

V
,
\mathscr{D}_{y}
is the k-space domain and

\mathscr{D}_{x}
is the image one, with
\mathscr{T}
being
the two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform (DFT)

\mathscr{F}

[
5
]
. In cases where there
is no need for the extra domain
\mathscr{D}_{x}
(see

Section

IV
), then

\mathscr{D}_{x}\coloneqq\mathscr{D}_{y}
, and
\mathscr{T}

is set to be the identity mapping
\Id
.

Typically, imputation-by-regression is achieved by solving the inverse problem

\displaystyle\min\nolimits_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}}{}

\displaystyle{}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X})+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X}){}

(1a)

s.to

\displaystyle{}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}\mathscr{T}(\mathbf{X})

(1b)

and other constraints,

where
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
is a loss that incorporates the
user-defined data model into the design, and

\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
is the regularizer used to impose
structural priors to the data model. Moreover,
(
1b
) is the “data-consistency”
constraint. A popular strategy for low-rank models is to
have data consistency appear as the “soft” loss

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X})\coloneqq\lVert\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})-\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}\mathscr{T}(\mathbf{X})\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}

in (
1a
), where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}

stands for the Frobenius norm of a matrix, together with the
nuclear-norm regularizer

\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X})\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{X}\rVert_{*}

[
7
]
, instead of using
the “hard” constraint
in (
1b
). Although such a path can be
also addressed by the proposed MultiL-KRIM, the present
manuscript keeps the “hard” constraint
in (
1b
) to allow for

\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
obtain simpler, “cleaner,” and more
familiar expressions than those after involving the operator

\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}
in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.

A popular low-rank data model is expressed by the loss

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})=\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}
, where

\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are added as auxiliary variables
to the inverse problem (
1
), with

\mathbf{U}
having more rows than
columns
[
8
,
9
]
. In
contrast, if
\mathbf{U}
is over-complete, i.e., more columns
than rows, and
\mathbf{V}
is sparse, the previous loss
yields dictionary learning
[
11
]
. More
generally, the loss

\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{U}_{1}\mathbf{U}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{U}_{Q}\mathbf{V}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}

\mathbf{X}
,

\{\mathbf{U}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
and
\mathbf{V}
are variables to

[
14

\mathbfscr{Y}

tensor-decomposition frameworks such as Tucker and CP

[
17
]
, or the more recent

[
18

19

20
]
, replace the standard matrix ones. All of
the aforementioned parametric factorization schemes are
“blind,” in a sense that all matrix/tensor factors are

\mathcal{R}(\cdot)

The nonparametric basis pursuit
(NBP)

, respectively. A very short

A

(supplementary file). The motivation

2

,

\varphi_{\mathcal{Z}}
and

\varphi_{\mathcal{Y}}

Appendix

A
), and

is a

\mathbf{X}

\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{C}
in (

) gather

\{c_{kt^{\prime}}\}
, respectively. Similarly to

]

(KGL)
[
54
]
assumes the loss

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{G})=\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Y}}\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}
, which yields (
2
) by
decomposing
\mathbf{G}
as the product
\mathbf{BC}
of
low-rank matrices. Moreover, kernel regression over graphs
(KRG)
[
55
]
adopts the loss

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H})=\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Y}}\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}
,
which yields NBP and KGL by decomposing

into

\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{BC}
and

\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{G}
, respectively. To avoid
alternating minimization for solving for the bilinear

2
),
[
33
]
proposed the
loss

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X},\bm{\gamma})=\lVert\tovec(\mathbf{X})-\mathbf{K}_{\otimes}\bm{\gamma}\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}
, where
\mathbf{K}_{\otimes}

and

is the vectorization of

\mathbf{X}
. Although
[
33
]
offers an
efficient closed-form solution, its data modeling lacks

[

]
and motivated by

[

]
,
[

]

introduces

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{H},\mathbf{F},\mathbf{Q})=\lVert\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}_{F}^{2}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}+\lVert\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{Y}}\rVert^{2}_{\textnormal{F}}

Notice that kernel matrices

. Moreover, all approximating functions

and

in NBP, KGL, and KRG are only

IV
Application: Time-Varying Graph-Signal Recovery

This section begins with an overview of the problem of
time-varying graph-signal (TVGS) recovery, followed by a
short review of several state-of-the-art prior works in

Section

IV-A
.
Section

IV-B
formulates
and provides solutions to a special instance of the
MultiL-KRIM’s inverse
problem (
11
). Finally,

Section

IV-C
compares empirically MultiL-KRIM with
several state-of-the-art methods, and investigates several

IV-A

TVGS recovery

Graph signal processing (GSP) is an emerging field at the
intersection of graph theory and signal
processing
[
58
,
59
]
. A graph is
an abstract mathematical object equipped with nodes/vertices
and edges, where nodes correspond to entities such as
sensors of a physical network or data points, and edges
represent the connections/relationships between these
entities. In GSP, each node is annotated with a
(time-varying) signal, so that all signals gathered across
all nodes constitute the so-called graph signal.

To be more specific, a graph is denoted by

G=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where

\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,I_{0}\}
represents
the set of
I_{0}
nodes, and

\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
is
the set of edges. The topology of the graph is described by
the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{0}}
,
whose
(i,j)
th entry
w_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the edge
weight between nodes
i
and
j
. In this study,
G
is
considered to be undirected, thus,
\mathbf{W}
is
symmetric. The graph Laplacian
I_{0}\times I_{0}
matrix is
defined as

\mathbf{L}\coloneqq\diag(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{1}_{I_{0}})-\mathbf{W}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{I_{0}}
denotes the
I_{0}\times 1

all-one vector, and
\diag(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{1}_{I_{0}})
stands
for the
I_{0}\times I_{0}
diagonal matrix whose diagonal
entries are the corresponding entries of the
I_{0}\times 1

vector
\mathbf{W}\mathbf{1}_{I_{0}}
. The graph signal is
denoted by

\mathbf{Y}=[\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{I_{N}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
, where the columns

\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{I_{N}}
are the
graph-signal “snapshots” at
I_{N}
time points, while rows

\mathcal{Z}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{I_{0}}
of

\mathbf{Y}
are the time profiles of the
I_{0}
signals which
annotate the nodes of the graph.

The analysis of graph signals is pivotal in fields such as
recommender systems
[
60
]
, power
grids
[
61
]
,
sensor
[
62
]
,
urban
[
63
]
, and vehicular
networks
[
64
]
. Due to reasons like
users’ privacy, sensors fault, or resources conservation,
such application domains often encounter the problem of
missing data. Naturally, there have been numerous studies to
address the problem, most of which leverage low-rank and
smoothness assumptions on
\mathbf{Y}
. Typically, it is
assumed that
\mathbf{Y}
is low rank due to dependencies
among time points and/or nodes
[
65
]
. On the
other hand, there are studies which adopt the graph
Laplacian operator
\mathbf{L}

[
58
]
to
impose graph-wide (a.k.a. spatial) smoothness, i.e.,
neighboring nodes share similar signal
attributes
[
66
,
67
]
. For
example, the regularization function
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
of
both KGL and KRG (see
Section

II
)
comprises a classical Tikhonov regularizer and an

\mathbf{L}
-based quadratic form. Instead, NBP uses only
Tikhonov regularization for
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
. Although
KGL and KRG jointly recover
\mathbf{Y}
and the underlying
graph topology
\mathbf{W}
or
\mathbf{L}
, since this study
emphasizes on TVGS recovery,
\mathbf{L}
is assumed to be
given and fixed in the sequel. Another kernel-based
regression method can be also found
in
[
68
]
, where kernel ridge regression is
used to develop a kernel Kalman-filtering (KKF) algorithm
for TVGS recovery, with an
\mathbf{L}
-based regularizer

\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
. Furthermore,
[
69
,
70
]
introduce differential smoothness and
impose low-rank structure by nuclear-norm
regularizers. Low-rank-cognizant matrix factorizations have
been also studied extensively
[
71
,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
]
.

By using the one-step difference operator

\mathbf{\Delta}

[
71
]
,
study
[
69
]
introduces the
spatio-temporal-smoothness regularizer

\tr(\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Delta})
in the place of

\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
in (
1
), where

\tr(\cdot)
stands for the trace operator. Specifically,

\mathbf{\Delta}
is an
I_{N}\times(I_{N}-1)
matrix, s.t.
[\mathbf{\Delta}]_{i,i}=-1,[\mathbf{\Delta}]_{i+1,i}=1
for all
i=1,\ldots,I_{N}-1
, while other entries are
zeros. Subsequently,
[
70
]
considered an
additional low-rank constraint in the form of a nuclear-norm
regularizer, which was shown to outperform other popular
regularizers such as spatial

\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{LX})
and temporal

\lVert\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Delta}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}

smoothness
[
76
]
. On the other hand,

[
77
]
introduces the following
Sobolev-smoothness regularizer

\tr(\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{L}+\epsilon\mathbf{I}_{I_{0}})^{\beta}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Delta})
, where
\epsilon,\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
are
user-defined parameters. For convenience, denote

\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\coloneqq(\mathbf{L}+\epsilon\mathbf{I}_{I_{0}})^{\beta}
. Works
[
69
,
70
,
77
]
use a “soft”
data-consistency loss, i.e., the quadratic loss

(1/2)\lVert\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})-\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}
is
used instead of the “hard” constraint

\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})
.

The extensive numerical tests of the present study have
shown that the Sobolev-smoothness regularizer is currently
offering state-of-the-art performance in TVGS
recovery. Consequently, to ensure fairness during numerical
tests,
all
competing methods, as well as the
proposed MultiL-KRIM, will be equipped hereafter with the
Sobolev-smoothness regularizer.

The main

a trade-off between NRMSE and runtime, but it declines

VI
Conclusions

This paper introduced MultiL-KRIM, a nonparametric
(kernel-based) data-imputation-by-regression framework. Two
important application domains were considered for
validation: time-varying-graph-signal (TVGS) recovery, and
reconstruction of highly accelerated
dynamic-magnetic-resonance-imaging (dMRI) data. Numerical
tests on TVGS recovery demonstrated MultiL-KRIM’s
outperformance over state-of-the-art “shallow”
methods. Furthermore, via tests on dMRI data, MultiL-KRIM
was shown to match the performance of its predecessor
KRIM
[
48
]
with significant reduction in
computational time. MultiL-KRIM outperformed
state-of-the-art “shallow” methods also on dMRI data, and
although it did not reach the accuracy of the
deep-image-prior-based TDDIP
[
27
]
, it offered
a more explainable and computationally tractable
path. Prospectively, to further enhance MultiL-KRIM’s
performance, current research effort includes also its
“deep” variations.

Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

A feature mapping
\varphi:\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\to\mathscr{H}

maps a vector
\mathbfit{l}\in\mathbb{C}^{\nu}
to

\varphi(\mathbfit{l})
in a feature space
\mathscr{H}
.
To provide structured solutions, it is assumed that

\mathscr{H}
is a complex-valued reproducing kernel Hilbert
space (RKHS), associated with a reproducing kernel

\kappa(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\times\mathbb{C}^{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}
, with rich attributes in approximation
theory
[
35
,
36
,
105
,
106
,
107
]
. In the RKHS setting, the feature
mapping becomes

\varphi(\mathbfit{l})\coloneqq\kappa(\mathbfit{l},\cdot)\in\mathscr{H}
,

. Most well-known
kernels for RKHSs are:

(i)

The linear kernel

\kappa_{\text{L}}(\mathbfit{l},\mathbfit{l}^{\prime})\coloneqq\mathbfit{l}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbfit{l}^{\prime}
,
where
\mathsf{H}
denotes complex conjugate vector/matrix
transposition;

(ii)

the Gaussian kernel

\kappa_{\text{G};\gamma}(\mathbfit{l},\mathbfit{l}^{\prime})\coloneqq\text{exp}[-\gamma(\mathbfit{l}-\overline{\mathbfit{l}}^{\prime})^{\intercal}(\mathbfit{l}-\overline{\mathbfit{l}}^{\prime})]

[
105
,
106
]
,
where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, superscript
\intercal

stands for vector/matrix transposition, and the overline
symbol denotes entry-wise complex conju- gation of a
scalar/vector/matrix; and

(iii)

the polynomial kernel

\kappa_{\text{P};(c,r)}(\mathbfit{l},\mathbfit{l}^{\prime})\coloneqq(\mathbfit{l}^{{\mathsf{H}}}\mathbfit{l}^{\prime}+c)^{r}
,
where
r\in\mathbb{N}_{*}
,

.

This section details the solutions to the TVGS
sub-tasks (
14a
) and
(
14b
). Sub-task (
14c
)
involves a composite convex loss function with affine

[
79

C-A

Solving (
14a
)

Given the index set
\Omega
, define the affine constraint

\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})\}
, and notice
that (
14a
) takes the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n+1/2)}=\arg\min\nolimits_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}}\mathcal{L}_{X}(\mathbf{X})\,,

(18)

where the differentiable strongly convex loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle{}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert{\mathbf{X}}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda_{L}}{2}\tr(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle+\tfrac{\tau_{X}}{2}\lVert\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}\rVert_{\textnormal{F}}^{2}\,,

and

\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{1}^{(n)}\cdots\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{Q}^{(n)}\mathbfcal{K}\hat{\mathbfcal{B}}^{(n)}
. For convenience in notations, let

\mathbf{X}_{*}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n+1/2)}
.

Problem (
18
) is a convex minimization task
with affine constraints, so it can be solved iteratively by
the efficient
[
79
]
. Nevertheless,
(
18
) possesses also a closed-form
solution. To formulate that closed-form solution, define the
sampling matrix

\mathbf{S}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
as
follows:
[\mathbf{S}_{\Omega}]_{ij}\coloneqq 1
, if

(i,j)\in\Omega
, while

[\mathbf{S}_{\Omega}]_{ij}\coloneqq 0
, if

(i,j)\notin\Omega
. Then,

\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{S}_{\Omega}\odot\mathbf{X}
, where
\odot
stands for the Hadamard
product. Furthermore, define the complement set

\Omega^{\complement}\coloneqq\{(i,j)\in\{1,\ldots,I_{0}\}\times\{1,\ldots,I_{N}\}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}(i,j)\notin\Omega\}
. It can be verified that any

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
can be written as

\mathbf{X}=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})+\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{S}_{\Omega}\odot\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\odot\mathbf{X}
. Moreover,
let

\tovec\colon\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}I_{N}}
denote the standard vectorization
operator which turns matrices into vectors, and let its
inverse mapping be denoted by

\tovec^{-1}\colon\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}I_{N}}\to\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}
. Let also the “vectorization” of
\Omega

as the one-dimensional index set

\tovec\Omega\coloneqq\{i\in\{1,\ldots,I_{0}I_{N}\}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}[\tovec(\mathbf{S}_{\Omega})]_{i}=1\}
. Also, for
any
I_{0}I_{N}\times 1
vector
\mathbf{v}
, let

\mathbf{v}|_{\tovec\Omega}
be the

\lvert\tovec\Omega\rvert\times 1
sub-vector of

\mathbf{v}
formed by the entries of
\mathbf{v}
at indices

\tovec\Omega
.

According to
[
108
, Prop. 26.13]
, the desired
solution satisfies

\mathbf{X}_{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\Leftrightarrow\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X}_{*})
and

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{X}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\in(\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}-\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y}))^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{0}=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X})\}^{\perp}=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{I_{0}\times I_{N}}\nonscript\,|\allowbreak\nonscript\,\mathopen{}\mathbf{0}=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X})\}
, where

\perp
stands for the orthogonal complement of a linear
subspace. Hence,

\displaystyle\hskip-20.0pt\nabla\mathcal{L}_{X}(\mathbf{X}_{*})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\mathbf{X}_{*}+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathbf{X}_{*}\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}-\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\mathbf{X}_{*}+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}[\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})+\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X}_{*})]\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle-\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}-\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\mathbf{X}_{*}+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}-\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}\,.

The requirement
\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\nabla\mathcal{L}_{X}(\mathbf{X}_{*}))=\mathbf{0}
yields

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\odot\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})+\lambda_{L}\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\odot\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{L}\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\odot\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}\odot(\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}+\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)})\,,

and after vectorizing by
\tovec(\cdot)
,

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\diag(\tovec\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}})\cdot\tovec\left(\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\right)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{L}\diag(\tovec\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}})\cdot(\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta})\tovec\left(\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\right)

\displaystyle{}={}-\lambda_{L}\diag(\tovec\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}})\tovec(\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle\hphantom{ {} = {} }+\diag(\tovec\mathbf{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}})\tovec(\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}+\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)})\,,

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. Consequently,
by keeping only the entries at indices

\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})
,

\displaystyle(1+\tau_{X})\tovec\left(\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\right)|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{L}(\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})\times\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}\cdot\tovec\left(\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\right)|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}

\displaystyle{}={}-\lambda_{L}\tovec(\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}

\displaystyle\hphantom{ {} = {} }+\tovec(\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}+\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}\,,

where

(\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})\times\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}
is the submatrix extracted from

\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}
by keeping only the rows and
columns indicated by
\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})
. It can
be readily verified that

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\tovec\bigl(\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\bigr)|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\Bigl((1

\displaystyle+\tau_{X})\mathbf{I}_{|\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})\rvert}+\lambda_{L}(\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal}\otimes\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})\times\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}\Bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\cdot\Bigl(

\displaystyle-\lambda_{L}\tovec(\mathbf{L}_{\epsilon}^{\beta}\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})\mathbf{\Delta}\mathbf{\Delta}^{\intercal})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}

\displaystyle+\tovec(\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{(n)}+\tau_{X}\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)})|_{\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})}\Bigr)\,.

(19)

Recovering
\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})

from (
19
) is a straightforward task: take
an all-zero
I_{0}I_{N}\times 1
vector, replace its zero
entries at positions
\tovec(\Omega^{\complement})
by the
corresponding entries of (
19
), and then
apply
\tovec^{-1}(\cdot)
to recover

\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})
. Finally,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n+1/2)}=\mathbf{X}_{*}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X}_{*})+\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})

\displaystyle=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{Y})+\mathscr{S}_{\Omega^{\complement}}(\mathbf{X}_{*})\,.

Solving (
14b
)

For compact notations, let

\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{Q+1}^{(n)}\coloneqq\mathbfcal{K}
and

\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{Q+2}^{(n)}\coloneqq\hat{\mathbfcal{B}}^{(n)}
. For any
q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}
,
define

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{q}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod\nolimits_{q^{\prime}=1}^{q-1}\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{q^{\prime}}^{(n)}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{q}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\prod\nolimits_{q^{\prime}=q+1}^{Q+2}\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{q^{\prime}}^{(n)}\,,

while
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{1}^{(n)}\coloneqq\mathbf{I}_{I_{0}}
and

\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{1}^{(n)}\coloneqq\prod_{q^{\prime}=2}^{Q+2}\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{q^{\prime}}^{(n)}
, so that
the gradient of the loss in (
14b
) takes the
form

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{D}(\mathbfcal{D}_{q})

\displaystyle{}={}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{q}^{(n)}\mathbfcal{D}_{q}\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{q}^{(n)}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{2}\mathbfcal{D}_{q}+\tau_{D}(\mathbfcal{D}_{q}-\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{q}^{(n)})\,.

The solution
\hat{\mathbfcal{D}}_{q}^{(n+1/2)}

to (
14b
) takes a closed form, and the way
to obtain it follows the steps of
Section

C-A
,
where the affine constraint to amount for the block-diagonal
form of
\mathbfcal{D}_{q}
, for
q\in\{2,\ldots,Q\}
,
becomes

\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}\coloneqq\{\mathbfcal{D}_{q}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{q-1}M\times d_{q}M}{\mathbin{}\mid\mathbin{}}\mathbf{0}=\mathscr{S}_{\Omega}(\mathbfcal{D}_{q})\}
, with the index
set
\Omega
comprising all pairs of indices positioned off
the diagonal blocks. In the case of
\mathbfcal{D}_{1}
, no
affine constraint (no block-diagonal structure) is necessary
to solve (
14b
). Solving

\nabla\mathcal{L}_{D}(\mathbfcal{D}_{1})=\mathbf{0}
for

Consider a convex function
