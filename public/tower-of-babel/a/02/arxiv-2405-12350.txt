A framework for extraction and transformation of documents
Cristian Riveros, Markus L. Schmid, Nicole Schweikardt

Abstract
We present a theoretical framework for the extraction and transformation of text documents. We propose to use a two-phase process where the first phase extracts span-tuples from a document, and the second phase maps the content of the span-tuples into new documents. We base the extraction phase on the framework of document spanners and the transformation phase on the theory of polyregular functions, the class of regular string-to-string functions with polynomial growth. For supporting practical extract-transform scenarios, we propose an extension of document spanners described by regex formulas from span-tuples to so-called multispan-tuples, where variables are mapped to sets of spans. We prove that this extension, called regex multispanners, has the same desirable properties as standard spanners described by regex formulas. In our framework, an Extract-Transform (ET) program is given by a regex multispanner followed by a polyregular function. In this paper, we study the expressibility and evaluation problem of ET programs when the transformation function is linear, called linear ET programs. We show that linear ET programs are equally expressive as non-deterministic streaming string transducers under bag semantics. Moreover, we show that linear ET programs are closed under composition. Finally, we present an enumeration algorithm for evaluating every linear ET program over a document with linear time preprocessing and constant delay.

Although IE is a crucial task for data management, document extraction is usually followed by a transformation into new documents. This forms the main task of the so-called ETL technologies

Hence, although document spanners provided the formal ground of ruled-based IE, these grounds are incomplete without understanding the following transformation process.

In this paper, we study the extraction and transformation of text documents through the lens of document spanners. We propose to use a two-phase process where the first phase extracts span-tuples from a document, and the second phase maps the content of the span-tuples into new documents. The main idea here is to use the framework of document spanners for the extraction phase, completing it with an transformation phase using the theory of polyregular functions.

These examples show that typical string transduction tasks can often be naturally divided into the job of locating the relevant parts of our input, followed by their manipulation (e. g., reordering, deleting, copying, etc.), and our framework is particularly tailored to this situation. Moreover, we can easily construct new ET programs by combining specifications for an extract task (i. e., a multispanner) with specifications for a transform task (a string-to-string function) of existing ET programs. For example, the same spanner that finds relevant text blocks, like names, email addresses, URLs, etc. can be combined with different string-to-string functions that pick and arrange some of these text blocks in a certain format. Or, for cleaning textual data, we use a string-to-string function that deletes all the annotated factors, and we combine this with different spanners for finding the factors to be deleted.

As also pointed out by our examples, it is a desired feature that the output of an ET program can contain duplicates. Let us motivate this by a more practical example. Assume that the extraction phase marks the addresses in a list of customer orders, while the transformation phase then transforms these addresses into a format suitable for printing on the parcels to be shipped to the customers. In the likely situation that there are different orders by the same person, the extraction phase produces different markings that will all be transformed into the same address label. Such duplicates, however, are important, since we actually want to obtain an address label for each distinct order (i. e., distinct marking), even if these addresses are the same (although the orders are not). This means that the output sets of our ET programs should actually be bags.

Our main conceptual contribution is a sound formalisation of our ET framework, which includes the extension of the classical spanner framework to multispanners (which we believe are interesting in their own right). Our main algorithmic result is about
linear ET programs
, i. e., the subclass where the multispanner of the extraction can be described by a regular expression (similar to the ones used above) and the transformation function is a polyregular function with linear growth (e.g., the program
(E_{1},T_{1})
). We show that we can enumerate the output documents of linear ET programs with linear preprocessing and output-linear delay (in data complexity). Furthermore, we show that ET programs are closed under composition.
This implies
that we can enumerate the output of a composition of programs efficiently.
As an important tool for our enumeration algorithm, we show that linear ET programs have the same expressive power as nondeterministic streaming string transducers (NSST). This also gives an important insight with respect to the expressive power of linear ET programs.

Further related work

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work on extending document spanners with transformation.

String transductions
[
10
,
34
,
12
]
–
a classical model
in computer science – have recently gained renewed
attention with the theory of polyregular functions
[
2
]
. Although we can see an ET program as a single transduction (cf., Section
4
and the equivalence with NSSTs), this work has several novel contributions compared to
classical string transductions. Firstly, our framework models the process by two declarative phases (which is natural from a data management perspective), contrary to string transductions that model the task as a single process. Secondly, our work uses bag semantics, which requires revisiting some results on transduction (see Section
6
). Moreover, efficient enumeration algorithms for (non-deterministic) linear polyregular functions have not been studied before.

There are systems for transforming documents into documents (e.g.,
[
29
]
). For example, they used a combination of regular expressions with replace operators
[
30
]
or parsing followed by a transduction over the parsing tree
[
29
]
. Indeed, in practice, regular expressions
support some special commands for transforming data (also called substitutions). Our study has a theoretical emphasis on information extraction and transformation, starting from the framework of document spanners. To our knowledge, previous systems neither use the expressive power of document spanners nor polyregular functions.

Let us first give some standard notations. By

denotes its length (in particular,
|\eword|=0
). A word

is a
factor
(or
subword

if there are

with
w=u_{1}vu_{2}
;
v
is a
prefix
or
suffix
of
w
, if
u_{1}=\eword
or
u_{2}=\eword
, respectively. For every

, let

denote the symbol at position
i
of

. We use DFAs and NFAs (deterministic and nondeterministic finite automata, resp.) as commonly defined.

Multispans and multispanners

For a document
\mathbf{D}\in\Sigma^{*}
and for every
i,j\in[|\mathbf{D}|{+}1]
with
i\leq j
,
[i,j\rangle
is a
span of
\mathbf{D}
and its

\mathbf{D}
from symbol
i

(called an

\mathbf{D}[1,|\mathbf{D}|{+}1\rangle=\mathbf{D}
. By

, we denote the set of spans of

, and by

\{[i,j\rangle\mid i,j\in\mathbb{N},\ i\leq j\}

Two spans

A

is a (possibly empty) set of pairwise disjoint spans.

Let

be a finite set of variables. A

(over a document
\mathbf{D}
and variables

)

]

such that, for every

Note that every span-tuple

can be considered as a special case of a multispan-tuple where

.
For simplicity, we usually denote multispan-tuples in tuple-notation, for which we assume an order

, then multispan

to

to

Note that

and

and

A

(over a document

and variables

and

.
Given a finite alphabet

a

(over
\Sigma
and

) is a function that maps every document

to a multispan-relation over

and

Intuitively, any multiref-word

The advantage of the notion of

a document and some multispan-tuple

Therefore,

A

(
over terminal alphabet
\Sigma
and variables

) is a set of multiref-words over

and

.
Any multiref-language

over
\Sigma
and

defined as follows. For every

:
{\lsem{}{L}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})\ =\ \ \{\textsf{t}_{w}\mid w\in L\text{ \ and \ }\mathbf{D}_{w}=\mathbf{D}\}
.

Analogously to classical spanners
(cf.
[

,
27
,

,

,

),

define the class of

as those multispanners

for some regular multiref-language

. Moreover, as
done in
[

for classical spanners, we

use a class of regular expressions to define a subclass of regular multispanners that shall play a central role for our extraction and transformation framework.

Regex multispanners

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet and let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite set of variables. We now define

multispanner-expressions
(
over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{X}
). Roughly speaking, these expresssions are a
particular class of regular expressions for defining
sets of
multiref-words and therefore multispanners.
More
specifically,
a multispanner-expression

(over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{X}
) satisfies the syntax:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}R&:=&\eword\ \mid\ a\in\Sigma\mid\ (R\cdot R)\ \mid\ (R+R)\ \mid\ R^{*}\mid\ \textsf{x}\{R\}\end{array}

for every
\textsf{x}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}
such that

does not appear in

Such
a multispanner-expression

naturally defines a set of multiref-words
\mathcal{L}(R)
as follows:
\mathcal{L}(\eword)=\{\eword\}
,
\mathcal{L}(a)=\{a\}
,
\mathcal{L}(R\cdot R^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(R)\cdot\mathcal{L}(R^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{L}(R+R^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(R)\cup\mathcal{L}(R^{\prime})
,
\mathcal{L}(R^{*})=\mathcal{L}(R)^{*}
, and
\mathcal{L}(\textsf{x}\{R\})\ =\ \ \{\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\}\cdot\>\mathcal{L}(R)\cdot\{\closemark_{\textsf{x}}\}

where, for every

Thus, we can
define the

{\lsem{}{R}\rsem}={\lsem{}{\mathcal{L}(R)}\rsem}
.
Furthermore, we say that a multispanner

if

for some multispanner-expression

R\ :=\ \ (\eword+\Sigma^{*}\mathtt{a}^{+})\cdot\textsf{x}\big\{(\textsf{y}\{\mathtt{b}^{+}\}\cdot\textsf{y}\{\mathtt{a}^{+}\})^{*}\cdot\textsf{y}\{\mathtt{b}^{+}\}\big\}\cdot(\mathtt{a}^{+}\Sigma^{*}+\eword)

Comparison with classical spanners

Multispanners are designed to naturally extend the classical model of spanners from
[
22
]
to the setting where variables are mapped to sets of spans instead of single spans. Let us discuss a few particularities of our definitions.

We first note that since classical span-tuples, span-relations and spanners (in the sense of
[
22
]
) can be interpreted as special multispan-tuples, multispan-relations and multispanners, respectively, our framework properly extends the classical spanner framework.

A multispan-tuple
t
allows
[i,j\rangle\in t(\textsf{x})
and
[k,l\rangle\in t(\textsf{y})
with
i\leq k<j\leq l
and
\textsf{x}\neq\textsf{y}
(and this is also the case for classical span-tuples). However,

[i,j\rangle,[k,l\rangle\in t(\textsf{x})
with
i\leq k<j\leq l
is not possible for multispan-tuples, since then representing
[i,j\rangle
and
[k,l\rangle
by parentheses
\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\ldots\closemark_{\textsf{x}}
in the document cannot be distinguished from representing
[i,l\rangle
and
[k,j\rangle
. Furthermore,
for distinct
s,s^{\prime}\in t(\textsf{x})
we require
that
s
and
s^{\prime}
are disjoint, which is motivated by the fact that without this restriction, the subsequence of all
\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}
and
\closemark_{\textsf{x}}
occurrences could be an arbitrary well-formed parenthesised expression (instead of a sequence
(\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}})^{k}
); thus, recognising whether a given string over
\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}

is a proper multiref-word cannot be done by an NFA, as is the case for classical spanners.

Our main motivation for multispanners is that they can express information extraction tasks that are of interest in the context of our extract and transform framework (and that cannot be represented by classical spanners in a convenient way). However, there are other interesting properties of multispanners not related to their application in our extract and transform framework, which deserve further investigation. For example, if
L
and
L^{\prime}
are multiref-languages (i. e.,
{\lsem{}{L}\rsem}
and
{\lsem{}{L^{\prime}}\rsem}
are multispanners), then
L\cup L
,
L\cdot L^{\prime}
and
L^{*}
are also multiref-languages (and therefore
{\lsem{}{L\cup L^{\prime}}\rsem}
,
{\lsem{}{L\cdot L}\rsem}
and
{\lsem{}{L^{*}}\rsem}
are also multispanners). For classical spanners, this is only true for the union. Consequently, multispanners show some robustness not provided by classical spanners.

3
Extract transform framework

Let us first present some more notation. As usual,
f:A\to B
denotes a function from
A
to
B
. When
f
is partial, we write
f:A\rightharpoonup B
and use
f(a)=\nondef
to indicate that
f
is undefined for element
a\in A
. Moreover,
\textsf{dom}(f)=\{a\in A\mid f(a)\neq\bot\}
denotes the domain of
f
. Every partial function
T:A^{*}\rightharpoonup B^{*}
is called a
string-to-string function
with input alphabet
A
and output alphabet
B
.

The main idea
of our extraction and transformation framework is to use
a document spanner
to extract information
and then use a string-to-string function to convert this information into new documents.
For this purpose, it is necessary to convert the output of a spanner (i.e., span-tuples) into the input of a
string-to-string function
(i.e., strings). In the following, we present
a unique way
to encode multispan-tuples into multiref-words, which will serve as our intermediate object between extraction and transformation.

A unique multiref-word representation

The representation of documents and multispan-tuples by multiref-words allows us to define multispanners by multiref-languages.
But this
representation is not unique, which is inconvenient if we want to use it for
encoding
multispan-tuples as strings.
As a remedy, we adopt the following approach: We
represent a document
\mathbf{D}\in\Sigma^{*}
and multispan-tuple
t
as a
multiref-word
w
such that factors of
w

that belong to
\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}^{*}
(i.e., factors between two letters of
\mathbf{D}
)
have the form:

(\closemark_{\textsf{x}}\altop\eword)(\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}}\altop\eword)(\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\altop\eword)(\closemark_{\textsf{y}}\altop\eword)(\prescript{}{\textsf{y}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{y}}\altop\eword)(\prescript{}{\textsf{y}}{\openmark}\altop\eword)\ldots\,.

Specifically, let
\mathbf{D}\in\Sigma^{*}
be a document and
t
a multispan-tuple over
\mathbf{D}
and variables
\mathcal{X}
. For a variable
\textsf{x}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}
and
i\in[|\mathbf{D}|+1]
, define
t[i,x]\ =\ \ (\closemark_{\textsf{x}})^{c}(\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}})^{e}(\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark})^{o}
,
where
c,e,o\in\{0,1\}

with
c=1
iff
[j,i\rangle\in t(\textsf{x})
for some
j\neq i
;
e=1
iff
[i,i\rangle\in t(\textsf{x})
; and
o=1
iff
[i,j\rangle\in t(\textsf{x})
for some
j\neq i
.
E. g., for the tuple
t
in Example
2.1
, we have that
t[3,\textsf{x}]=\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}
and
t[4,\textsf{y}]=\closemark_{\textsf{y}}\prescript{}{\textsf{y}}{\openmark}
.

Let
\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}=\{\textsf{x}_{1},\textsf{x}_{2},\ldots,\textsf{x}_{m}\}
with
\textsf{x}_{1}\preceq\textsf{x}_{2}\preceq\ldots\preceq\textsf{x}_{m}
, where
\preceq
is some fixed linear order on
\mathcal{X}
.
For every
i\in[|\mathbf{D}|+1]
, we define
t[i]\ :=\ \ t[i,\textsf{x}_{1}]\cdot t[i,\textsf{x}_{2}]\cdot\ldots\cdot t[i,\textsf{x}_{m}]
.
We can then define the encoding of
t
and
\mathbf{D}
as the multiref-word:

\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})\ =\ \ t[1]\cdot\mathbf{D}[1]\cdot t[2]\cdot\mathbf{D}[2]\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathbf{D}[|\mathbf{D}|]\cdot t[|\mathbf{D}|{+}1].

Note that
\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})
is
a multiref-word,
\mathbf{D}_{\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})}=\mathbf{D}
and
\textsf{t}_{\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})}=t
.
Thus,
\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})
is a correct and unique encoding for
t
and
\mathbf{D}
as a multiref-word.
Next, we use this encoding to define
extract-transform programs
.

Extract-transform programs

An
extract-transform program

(for short:
ET program
)
is a pair
(E,T)
such that
E
is a multispanner over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{X}
, and
T
is a string-to-string function with input alphabet
\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}
and some output alphabet
\Omega
.
The semantics
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}

of an ET program
(E,T)
is defined as follows. For an input document

\mathbf{D}
, we first apply
E
on
\mathbf{D}
, which produces a
multispan-relation
E(\mathbf{D})
. Then, for every multispan-tuple
t\in E(\mathbf{D})
, we apply
T
on the multiref-word
\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})
producing
a new document in the output. Note that while
\mathtt{enc}(\cdot,\cdot)

is injective, the function
T
might not be, which means that for
t,t^{\prime}\in E(\mathbf{D})
with
t\neq t^{\prime}
we might have
T(\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D}))=T(\mathtt{enc}(t^{\prime},\mathbf{D}))
. This leads to duplicates, but, as explained in
the introduction, we keep them, since we want each distinct
t\in E(\mathbf{D})
of the extraction phase to correspond to a transformed
document in the output. Hence, the output set is a
bag
(also
known as
multiset
).
As common in the literature, we use the notation

\{\!\!\{\ldots\}\!\!\}
for bags (we give a detailed definition of bags
in Appendix, Section
A
).
Formally, for every
\mathbf{D}\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})\ =\ \{\!\!\{T(\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D}))\mid t\in E(\mathbf{D})\}\!\!\}
.

Polyregular ET programs

In the introduction, we have already discussed several examples of ET programs on an intuitive level. The multispanners of these examples were explicitly stated as multispanner expressions. The transformation functions were given as ad-hoc specifications, but they were all instances of
so-called
polyregular functions
. A polyregular function is a string-to-string function that is regular and has
a growth at most polynomial
in the size of the output string. Formally,
T
is polyregular if it can be defined by a
pebble transducer
:
a two-way transducer enhanced with a fixed number of pebbles (i.e., pointers) that the machine can drop or remove over the input word in a nested fashion and can read when it passes over them (see
[
20
]
for a formal definition). Polyregular functions
form a robust class that has found several characterizations,
among them for-transducers, MSO interpretations,
and
others
[
12
]
. Furthermore, polyregular functions are closed under composition and have good theoretical properties
(see
[
12
]
for an overview).
For this reason, we introduce the following definition.

A
polyregular ET program
is an ET program
(E,T)
where
E
is a regex multispanner and
T
is a polyregular function. All the ET programs mentioned in the introduction were examples of polyregular ET programs. In the example ET program
(E_{2},T_{2})
discussed there, we need the following string-to-string function
T_{2}
in the transformation phase:

\displaystyle u\>\#\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}u_{\textsf{x}}\closemark_{\textsf{x}};\prescript{}{\textsf{y}}{\openmark}u_{\textsf{y}}\closemark_{\textsf{y}};\prescript{}{\textsf{z}}{\openmark}v_{\textsf{z},1}\closemark_{\textsf{z}};\prescript{}{\textsf{z}}{\openmark}v_{\textsf{z},2}\closemark_{\textsf{z}}\ldots\prescript{}{\textsf{z}}{\openmark}v_{\textsf{z},k}\closemark_{\textsf{z}}\>u^{\prime}\>\mapsto

\displaystyle u_{\textsf{y}}\mbox{\kern 0.59998pt\vrule height=1.29167pt}\vbox{\hrule width=3.00003pt}\hbox{\vrule height=1.29167pt}u_{\textsf{x}}:v_{\textsf{z},1}\#u_{\textsf{y}}\mbox{\kern 0.59998pt\vrule height=1.29167pt}\vbox{\hrule width=3.00003pt}\hbox{\vrule height=1.29167pt}u_{\textsf{x}}:v_{\textsf{z},2}\#\ldots\#u_{\textsf{y}}\mbox{\kern 0.59998pt\vrule height=1.29167pt}\vbox{\hrule width=3.00003pt}\hbox{\vrule height=1.29167pt}u_{\textsf{x}}:v_{\textsf{z},k}\#\,,

where
u,u^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
,
u_{\textsf{x}},u_{\textsf{y}},v_{\textsf{z},1},v_{\textsf{z},2},\ldots,v_{\textsf{z},k}\in\widehat{\Sigma}^{*}
.
It can be verified that this is indeed a polyregular function, by defining a pebble transducer or a for-transducer for it, which, due to space constraints, is done in the Appendix (see Section
B
).

Let us explain why the class of polyregular functions is a good choice for our setting. As mentioned before, polyregular functions can be seen as the class of MSO string-to-string functions, i.e., they have the same expressive power as MSO interpretations. Furthermore, polyregular functions are a robust and well-established class with several representations and good closure properties. Polyregular functions also capture most of the transformations that occur in practice. For example, practical regex use substitutions for transforming strings
[
44
,
45
]
. Interestingly, one can define all these operators by combining regex multispanners and polyregular functions. There are even substitutions in regex that define non-linear polyregular functions (e.g., see the operator
$'
in [1,2]). Thus, polyregular functions are a natural choice. In the following we will focus on a fragment called
linear ET programs
.

Linear ET programs

We restrict attention to string-to-string functions of linear growth.
Specifically, we say that a string-to-string function
T
has
linear growth
if there exists a constant
c
, such that
|T(w)|\leq c\cdot|w|
for every string
w
.
A
linear ET program
is a polyregular ET program
(E,T)
where
T
has linear growth.

The class of linear polyregular functions forms a proper and well-behaved subclass of polyregular functions, which
allows for several equivalent representations like two-way
transducers, MSO transductions, or deterministic streaming string
transducers
[
12
,
3
]

(this last formalism will be crucial for the rest of this paper).
The string-to-string function
T_{1}
(see Page
1
)
that we used in the first example discussed in the introduction is an example of a linear polyregular function, and we shall see a deterministic streaming string transducer for it later on.

Nondeterministic streaming string transducers

We
extend DSSTs
(cf. Section
3
) to the nondeterministic case and bag semantics. Let us explain the model by pointing out its differences to DSSTs.
2
2

2

Note that nondeterministic streaming string transducers with
set semantics
instead of bag semantic have already been introduced in
[
4
]
.

A
nondeterministic streaming string transducer
(NSST) is a tuple
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\Omega,\mathcal{R},\Delta,I,F)
,
where
Q
,
\Sigma
,
\Omega
,
\mathcal{R}
and
F
have the same meaning as for DSSTs. The partial function
I:Q\rightharpoonup\textsf{Val}(\mathcal{R},\Omega)
plays the role of the initial state, i. e., a state
q
is a possible initial state if
I(q)
is defined, and in this case
I(q)
is an initial valuation of the registers.
Moreover,
\Delta
is not a
(partial)
function from
Q\times\Omega
to
\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R},\Omega)\times Q
anymore, but a
bag
of transitions,
i.e.,
\Delta
is a bag of elements in
Q\times\Sigma\times\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R},\Omega)\times Q
.

The semantics of the model are as expected. A run over a string
a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
is a sequence of configurations
(q_{i},\nu_{i})
of the form:

\rho\ :=\ (q_{0},\nu_{0})\xrightarrow{a_{1}/\sigma_{1}}(q_{1},\nu_{1})\xrightarrow{a_{2}/\sigma_{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{n}/\sigma_{n}}(q_{n},\nu_{n})

such that
I(q_{0})
is defined and
\nu_{0}=I(q_{0})
,
(q_{i},a_{i+1},\sigma_{i+1},q_{i+1})\in\Delta
and
\nu_{i+1}=\nu_{i}\circ\sigma_{i+1}
for every
i<n
. As for DSSTs,
\rho
is an
accepting run
if
\nu_{n}(F(q_{n}))
is defined, and the output of an accepting run
\rho
is the string
\textsf{out}(\rho)=\nu_{n}(F(q_{n}))
. We define
\textsf{Runs}_{\mathcal{T}}(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})
as the bag of all accepting runs of
\mathcal{T}
over
a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
.

Finally, we define the semantics of an NSST
\mathcal{T}
over a string
w\in\Sigma^{*}
as the
bag:

{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}(w)\ =\ \{\!\!\{\,\textsf{out}(\rho)\,\mid\,\rho\in\textsf{Runs}_{\mathcal{T}}(w)\,\}\!\!\}.

Equivalence of linear ET programs and NSSTs

We show that
linear ET programs and NSSTs are equivalent. This is a fundamental insight with respect to the expressive power of ET programs. Moreover, the fact that the two-stage model of linear ET programs can be described by a single NSST will be important for our enumeration algorithm
(Section
5
) and their composition (Section
6
).

We first discuss how linear ET programs can be transformed into NSSTs.
Every multiref-word over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{X}
describes a document
\mathbf{D}_{w}
and a multispan-tuple
\textsf{t}_{w}
.
Hence,
we can extend the encoding
\mathtt{enc}(\cdot)
to multiref-words by setting
\mathtt{enc}(w)=\mathtt{enc}(\textsf{t}_{w},\mathbf{D}_{w})
. Intuitively speaking, applying the function
\mathtt{enc}(\cdot)
on a multiref-word
w

simply means that every maximal factor
u\in\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}^{*}
of
w
is re-ordered according to the order
\preceq
on
\mathcal{X}
, and superfluous matching brackets
\prescript{}{\textsf{x}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}}
are removed (since several of those in the same maximal factor over
\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}
would describe the same empty span several times). We define
\mathtt{enc}(L)=\{\mathtt{enc}(w)\mid w\in L\}
for multiref-languages
L
.

Let us consider a linear ET program
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}
, i. e.,
E
is a regex multispanner represented by some multispanner-expression

r
, and
T
is a linear polyregular function represented by some DSST
\mathcal{T}
. The high-level idea of the proof is to construct an NSST
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
that simulates a DFA
M
for
\mathtt{enc}(\mathcal{L}(r))
and the DSST
\mathcal{T}
in parallel. More precisely, we read an input
\mathbf{D}\in\Sigma^{*}
, but between reading a symbol
\mathbf{D}[i]
and the next symbol
\mathbf{D}[i+1]
, we pretend to read a sequence of symbols from
\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}
with
\mathcal{T}
and
M
at the same time. Thus, we virtually read some multiref-word
w
with the property
\mathbf{D}_{w}=\mathbf{D}
. We need the DSST
\mathcal{T}
for producing an output on that multiref-word, and we need the DFA
M
to make sure that the virtual multiref-word has the form
\mathtt{enc}(t,\mathbf{D})
for some
t\in E(\mathbf{D})
.

One may wonder why we want
M
to be a DFA rather than an NFA. The reason is:
Having to deal with bag semantics (rather than just set semantics) makes things more complicated.
In particular, this means that we need
M
to be deterministic to have a one-to-one correspondence between the accepting runs of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
and the accepting runs of
M
on the corresponding multiref-word.
Otherwise,
if
M
was an NFA,
the different possible accepting paths of
M
on the same multiref-word would translate into different accepting paths of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
which would cause erroneous duplicates in
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
.

We can transform
r
into a DFA
M
for
\mathtt{enc}(\mathcal{L}(r))
by standard automata constructions, but the fact that
M
needs to be deterministic means that the construction is
(one-fold)
exponential in
|r|
, and the fact that it has to accept
\mathtt{enc}(\mathcal{L}(r))
and not just
\mathcal{L}(r)
means that the construction is also
(one-fold)
exponential in
|\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}|
.
In summary, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Given a regex multispanner
E
over
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{X}
(represented by a multispanner-expression
r
), and a linear polyregular string-to-string function
T
with input alphabet
\Sigma\cup\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}
(represented by a DSST with
h
states),
we can construct an NSST
\mathcal{T}
with
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}={\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}
in time
\bigo(2^{4|r|+9|\mathcal{X}|}|\Sigma|^{3}|\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}|^{2}h^{2})
.

Let us now move on to representing general NSSTs by ET programs. When an NSST
\mathcal{T}
is in a state
p
and reads a symbol
b\in\Sigma
, then the number of possible nondeterministic choices it can make is the sum over all the multiplicities of elements
(p,b,\sigma,q)\in\Delta
(recall that
\Delta
is the bag of transitions). We shall call this number the
nondeterministic branching factor of
p
and
b
.
The

nondeterministic branching factor
of
\mathcal{T}
is the maximum over all the nondeterministic branching factors of
p
and
b
for all
(p,b)\in Q\times\Sigma
.

The only obstacle in the simulation of an NSST by a DSST is that the latter cannot deal with the nondeterministic choices. However, in linear ET programs, a DSST gets an annotated version of the actual document
\mathbf{D}
as input, i. e., a multiref-word
w
with
\mathbf{D}_{w}=\mathbf{D}
. Consequently, the DSST could interpret the additional information given by
w
in the form of the symbols from
\Gamma_{\mbox{$\mathcal{X}$}}
as information that determines which nondeterministic choices it should make. More formally, we can
construct a regex multispanner that, for every
i\in[1,2,\ldots,|\mathbf{D}|]
, nondeterministically chooses some
\textsf{x}_{\ell}\in\{\textsf{x}_{1},\textsf{x}_{2},\ldots,\textsf{x}_{m}\}
, where
m
is the NSST’s nondeterministic branching factor, and puts the empty span
[i,i\rangle
into
t(\textsf{x})
. On the level of multiref-words, this simply means that every symbol is preceded by
\prescript{}{\textsf{x}_{\ell}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}_{\ell}}
for some
\ell
. Such an occurrence of
\prescript{}{\textsf{x}_{\ell}}{\openmark}\closemark_{\textsf{x}_{\ell}}
can then be interpreted by the DSST as an instruction to select the
\ell^{\text{th}}
nondeterministic choice when processing the next symbol from
\Sigma
.
In summary, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Given an NSST
\mathcal{T}
with
n
states, input alphabet
\Sigma
and nondeterministic branching factor
m
,
we can construct a regex multispanner
E
over
\Sigma
and
\{\textsf{x}_{1},\textsf{x}_{2},\ldots,\textsf{x}_{\max\{n,m\}}\}
and a linear polyregular function
T
with
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}={\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}
. Moreover,
E
is represented by a multispanner-expression
r
with
|r|=\bigo(|\Sigma|+\max\{n,m\})
,
T
is represented by a DSST
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with
\bigo(\max\{n,m\}\cdot n)
states, and both
r
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
can be constructed in time
\bigo(|\mathcal{T}|+n\cdot\max\{n,m\}+|\Sigma|)
.

Let us conclude this section by mentioning that the statement of Theorem
4.1
is also true, if
E
is a multispanner represented by an NFA (this stronger version is proven in the appendix).

5
Evaluation of linear ET programs

In this section, we present the main technical result of the paper, regarding the evaluation of linear ET programs. Specifically, we consider the following enumeration problem
(let
E
be a regex multispanner and
T
a linear polyregular function).

Problem:

\textsc{EnumLinearET}[(E,T)]

Input:

A document
\mathbf{D}

Output:

Enumerate
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})

Notice that
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
is a bag; thus, the task is to produce an enumeration that contains each element of the bag exactly once, e. g.,
(a,a,b,c,a)
is a possible enumeration of
\{\!\!\{b,a,a,a,c\}\!\!\}
.

As usual,
we measure the running time in
data complexity
, namely, we assume that
E
and
T
are fixed. Given this assumption, we can assume that
E
is given as a multispanner-expression, and
T
as a DSST. Otherwise, we can convert
E
and
T
to satisfy this requirement.

For this problem, we strive for an
enumeration algorithm with linear preprocessing and output-linear delay
, i. e., in a
preprocessing phase
it receives the input and produces some data structure
DS
which encodes the expected output, and in the following
enumeration phase
it produces a sequential enumeration
w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}
of the results from
DS
. Moreover, the time for the preprocessing phase is
\bigo(|\mathbf{D}|)
, the time for producing
w_{1}

is less than
c\cdot|w_{1}|
, and the time between producing
w_{i-1}
and
w_{i}
is less than
c\cdot|w_{i}|
, for some fixed constant
c
that does not depend on the input. As it is common
[
43
]
,
we assume the computational model of
Random Access Machines
(RAM) with uniform cost measure and addition and subtraction as basic operations
[
1
]
.
We obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

\textsc{{EnumLinearET}}[(E,T)]
admits an enumeration algorithm with linear preprocessing time and output-linear delay.

Due to space restrictions, all the details and the analysis of the enumeration algorithm are deferred to the Appendix.
In the following, we
highlight
the main technical challenges of this algorithm.

The first step of the algorithm is to apply Theorem
4.1
and convert the pair
(E,T)
into an NSST
\mathcal{T}_{E,T}
. This takes time exponential in
|E|
;
nevertheless, it does not depend on
|\mathbf{D}|
and so we can consider it as constant time. Therefore, our enumeration algorithm aims for computing
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{E,T}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
with linear time preprocessing and output-linear delay.

For evaluating
\mathcal{T}_{E,T}
over
\mathbf{D}
, the first challenge that we need to overcome is that its runs could maintain registers with content that is not used at the end of the run. For an illustration, consider the following NSST (note that
I(q)
is the valuation
X:=\eword
and
Y:=\eword
, and
F(p)=X
and
F(r)=Y
):
q
\begin{array}[]{l}X:=\eword\\
Y:=\eword\end{array}\!\!\!
a\ \ \begin{array}[]{|l}X:=aX\\
Y:=Y\end{array}
b\ \ \begin{array}[]{|l}X:=X\\
Y:=bY\end{array}
p
X
r
Y
a\ \ \begin{array}[]{|l}X:=aX\\
Y:=Y\end{array}
b\ \ \begin{array}[]{|l}X:=X\\
Y:=bY\end{array}

For each input word
w
with
n

a
-symbols and
m

b
-symbols, the NSST outputs
a^{n}
if
w
ends with
a
and
b^{m}
if
w
ends with
b
. Consequently, every run on a word that ends with
a
produces “garbage” in register
Y
, since the content of this register is not used for the output (and analogously with register
X
for inputs that end with
b
).
This behavior of storing “garbage” will be problematic for our enumeration approach, given that the delay depends on the (potentially useless) contents of the registers.

Given the above discussion, we formalize
the notion of “garbage”
as follows.
Consider an NSST
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\Omega,\mathcal{R},\Delta,I,F)
.
For
u\in(\mathcal{R}\cup\Omega)^{*}
, let
\textsf{reg}(u)
be the set of all registers
X\in\mathcal{R}
that appear in
u
.
For

\sigma:\mathcal{R}\rightharpoonup(\mathcal{R}\cup\Omega)^{*}
let
\textsf{reg}(\sigma)=\bigcup_{X\in\textsf{dom}(\sigma)}\textsf{reg}(\sigma(X))
, namely,

\textsf{reg}(\sigma)
is
the set of all registers used by
\sigma
.
We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
garbage-free
if, and only if, for every string
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}
and every accepting run
\rho
of the form:

\rho\ :=\ (q_{0},\nu_{0})\xrightarrow{a_{1}/\sigma_{1}}(q_{1},\nu_{1})\xrightarrow{a_{2}/\sigma_{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{n}/\sigma_{n}}(q_{n},\nu_{n})

it holds that
\textsf{dom}(\nu_{i})=\textsf{reg}(\sigma_{i+1})
for every
i<n
, and
\textsf{dom}(\nu_{n})=\textsf{reg}(F(q_{n}))
. In other words, the registers
\textsf{dom}(\nu_{i})
that we have filled with content so far coincide with the registers
\textsf{reg}(\sigma_{i+1})
that we use on the right hand sides of the next assignment.

The first challenge is to show how to make NSSTs garbage-free.

Proposition 5.2
.

For every NSST
\mathcal{T}
, there exists a garbage-free NSST
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
such
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}(w)={\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\rsem}(w)
for every string
w
.

The construction of Proposition
5.2
causes a blow-up that is exponential in the number of registers, since we turn an NSST with
|Q|
states into an NSST with
|Q|\times 2^{|\mathcal{R}|}
states. Of course, if we start with a garbage-free NSST, this blow-up can be avoided. Interestingly, we can show that one can check the garbage-free property in polynomial time.

Proposition 5.3
.

Given an NSST
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,\Omega,\mathcal{R},\Delta,I,F)
, we can decide in time
\bigo(|\Delta|\cdot|\mathcal{R}|)
whether
\mathcal{T}
is garbage-free.

The second challenge is maintaining the set of outputs compactly for enumerating them. The main problem here is that the output of a run is not produced linearly as a single string (as is the case for classical spanners
[
9
,
6
]
) but instead in parallel on different registers that are combined in some order
at the end of the input. To solve this, we follow the approach in
[
32
,
33
]
and present an extension of
Enumerable Compact Sets
(ECS), a data structure that stores sets of strings compactly and retrieves them with output-linear delay. We modify ECS for storing sets of valuations and we call it
ECS with assignments
. For storing valuations, we use assignments in the internal nodes of the data structure, which allows us to encode the runs’ output and keep the content of different registers synchronous.

Although we can eliminate the garbage from an NSST
(cf., Proposition
5.2
),
the machine can still swap and move registers during a run, producing assignments that permute the registers’ content but do not contribute to a final output. We call these assignments
relabelings
. Thus, the last challenge is taking care of relabeling assignments.
Similarly as in
[
33
]
(i.e., with shifts), we treat them as special objects and
compact
them in the data structure whenever possible.
These extensions require
to revisit ECS and to provide
new ways for extending or
unifying
sets of valuations by also taking care of relabeling assignments.

6
Composition of linear ET programs

A valuable property of ET programs is that
they receive
documents as input and produce documents as outputs.
In this line, it is natural to think on reusing the output of one ET program
(E_{1},T_{1})
to feed a second ET program
(E_{2},T_{2})
, namely, to compose them. Formally, we define the
composition

(E_{1}\cdot T_{1})\circ(E_{2}\cdot T_{2})
as a function
{\lsem{}{(E_{1}\cdot T_{1})\circ(E_{2}\cdot T_{2})}\rsem}
that maps documents to a bag of documents such that for every document
\mathbf{D}
:

{\lsem{}{(E_{1}\cdot T_{1})\circ(E_{2}\cdot T_{2})}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})\ =\ \bigcup_{\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\in{\lsem{}{E_{1}\cdot T_{1}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})}{\lsem{}{E_{2}\cdot T_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime}).

Note that we use here the union of bags, which is defined in the natural way (a formal definition is given in the Appendix, Section
A
).

For extraction and transformation of information, it is useful to evaluate the composition efficiently. One naive approach
is to evaluate
{\lsem{}{E_{1}\cdot T_{1}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})

(e.g., by using the evaluation algorithm of Section
5
in case that
(E_{1},T_{1})
is a
linear
ET program),
and for every output
\mathbf{D}^{\prime}
in
{\lsem{}{E_{1}\cdot T_{1}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
compute
{\lsem{}{E_{2}\cdot T_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime})
, gathering all the outputs together.
Of course, this could be time consuming, since
|{\lsem{}{E_{1}\cdot T_{1}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})|
could be exponential in
|\mathbf{D}|
in the worst case.

Towards solving the previous algorithmic problem, in the next result we show that every composition of NSSTs can be defined by an NSST. Formally, let us denote the input and output alphabet of some NSST
\mathcal{T}
by
\Sigma(\mathcal{T})
and
\Omega(\mathcal{T})
, respectively. Given two NSSTs
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
such that
\Omega(\mathcal{T}_{1})\subseteq\Sigma(\mathcal{T}_{2})
, we define the composition
\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}
as the function from documents to bags of documents:
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})=\ \bigcup_{\mathbf{D}^{\prime}\in{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})}{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D}^{\prime})
.
We obtain the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

For every pair of NSSTs
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
such that
\Omega(\mathcal{T}_{1})\subseteq\Sigma(\mathcal{T}_{2})
, there exists an NSST
\mathcal{T}
such that
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}={\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}
. The construction of
\mathcal{T}
is effective, and
|\mathcal{T}|=\bigo(2^{\mathsf{poly(|\mathcal{T}_{1}|,|\mathcal{T}_{2}|)}})
.

The statement of Theorem
6.1
for set semantics rather than bag semantics was obtained
by
[
4
,
5
]
.
The novelty of
Theorem
6.1
is that we extend
the result to bag semantics; namely, we need to maintain the multiplicities of the final outputs correctly. The proof revisits (and simplifies) the construction in
[
4
,
5
]
: we simulate
\mathcal{T}_{1}
over
\mathbf{D}
while
\mathcal{T}_{2}
runs over the
registers’
content of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, compactly representing subruns of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
by using
pairs
of states and assignment summaries
[
3
]
. The extension requires two changes for working under bag semantics. First, similar to the evaluation of NSSTs, garbage on
\mathcal{T}_{1}
-registers could generate additional runs for
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, producing outputs with wrong multiplicities.
Therefore, our
first step is to remove this garbage
by
constructing equivalent garbage-free NSSTs by using Propositon
5.2
.
Second, the construction in
[
4
]
guesses subruns of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
for each register content and each starting state. In the end, we will use one of these guesses, and then unused subruns could modify the output multiplicity. Therefore, we simplify the construction in
[
4
]
by guessing a single subrun for each register content, using the non-determinism to discard wrong guesses. Interestingly, this simplification overcomes the error pointed out by Joost Engelfriet in the construction of
[
4
]
, which was solved recently in
[
5
]
. Our construction does not need the machinery of
[
5
]
; thus, an adaptation of our construction for NSSTs (with set semantics) can be considered as a simplified version of the proof in
[
4
,
5
]
.
We conclude by mentioning that as an alternative proof of Theorem
6.1
we could move to MSO transductions
[
19
]
, extend the logic with a bag semantics, and then do the composition at a logical level; however, this strategy would lead to a non-elementary blow-up.

By combining Theorem
4.1
,
6.1
and
4.2
, we get the following corollary regarding the expressiveness of linear ET programs and their composition.

Corollary 6.2
.

For every pair of linear ET programs
(E_{1},T_{1})
and
(E_{2},T_{2})
, there exists
a linear
ET program
(E,T)
such that
{\lsem{}{E\cdot T}\rsem}\ \ =\ \ {\lsem{}{(E_{1}\cdot T_{1})\circ(E_{2}\cdot T_{2})}\rsem}
.

We conclude
this section by
the following corollary regarding the evaluation of
the
composition of
linear
ET programs, obtained by combining Theorem
6.1
and
5.1
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Given linear ET programs
(E_{1},T_{1}),\ldots,(E_{k},T_{k})
we can evaluate the composition:

{\lsem{}{(E_{1}\cdot T_{1})\circ\ldots\circ(E_{k}\cdot T_{k})}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})

with linear time preprocessing and output-linear delay in data complexity.

7
Future Work

There are two obvious further research questions directly motivated by our results. Firstly, the framework of multispanners deserves further investigation. In particular, we wish to find out which results and properties of classical spanners directly carry over to multispanners, and in which regards these two models differ. Secondly, while we provide a sound formalisation of the class of polyregular ET programs (and have argued that it covers relevant extract and transform tasks), our
technical
results focus on the subclass of linear ET programs.
Our future work will focus on proving similar results for the full class of polyregular ET programs.

APPENDIX

Appendix A
Definitions for bags

Let us provide some formal details on bag semantics that will be useful in our formal proofs.

A
bag

B
is a surjective function
B:I\rightarrow U
where
I
is a finite set of identifiers (or ids) and
U
is the underlying set of the bag. Given any bag
B
, we refer to these components as
I(B)
and
U(B)
, respectively. For example, a bag
B=\{\!\!\{a,a,b\}\!\!\}
(where
a
is repeated twice) can be represented with a surjective function
B_{0}=\{0\mapsto a,1\mapsto a,2\mapsto b\}
where
I(B_{0})=\{0,1,2\}
and
U(B_{0})=\{a,b\}
. In general, we will use the standard notation for bags
\{\!\!\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1}\}\!\!\}
to denote the bag
B
whose identifiers are
I(B)=\{0,\ldots,n{-}1\}
and
B(i)=a_{i}
for each
i\in I(B)
.
When we refer to the
elements of
B
we mean
I(B)
, namely, considering every object of
B
by its identity (i.e., its identifier).

For every
a
, we define the multiplicity of
a
in
B
as
\textsf{mult}_{B}(a)=|\{i\mid B(i)=a\}|
, namely, the number of occurrences of
a
in
B
. Furthermore, we define
|B|=|I(B)|
. We say that a bag
B
is
contained
in the bag
B^{\prime}
, and write
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
, if
\textsf{mult}_{B}(a)\leq\textsf{mult}_{B^{\prime}}(a)
for every
a
. We also say that two bags
B
and
B^{\prime}
are
equal
, and write
B=B^{\prime}
, if
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
.
Given a set
A
, we say that
B
is a bag of
A
if

We observe that, by induction, this means that

1.

There is a transitions

2.

There are transitions

.

There is a transition
(p,a,\sigma,q)\in\Delta

is defined and
\textsf{dom}(\sigma)\neq\textsf{reg}(F(q))
.

For every state

, and we say that

if there are no two incoming transitions
(q,a,\sigma_{1},p),(r,b,\sigma_{2},p)\in\Delta
with

.

If there is a state that is not reg-consistent or not dom-consistent, then one of the three properties from above is satisfied. Indeed, let us assume that

is not reg-consistent, which means that there are
(p,a,\sigma_{1},q),(p,b,\sigma_{2},r)\in\Delta

or
\textsf{dom}(I(p))\neq\textsf{reg}(\sigma_{2})
, which means that the first property is satisfied. If
I(p)
is not defined, then, since

is trim, there must be at least one incoming transition

. Thus,

or
\textsf{dom}(\sigma_{3})\neq\textsf{reg}(\sigma_{2})

\textsf{dom}(\sigma_{2})\neq\textsf{reg}(F(p))

. Thus,

\textsf{dom}(\sigma_{2})\neq\textsf{reg}(\sigma_{3})

If all states are both reg-consistent and dom-consistent, then every state

(i. e., the set

for any transition

, or undefined if

We can check whether every state is reg- and dom-consistent as follows. We iterate through all transitions

to

\mathsf{regv}(p)
and
\mathsf{domv}(p)
for every

\bigo(|\Delta||\mathcal{R}|)
.

If all the states are indeed dom- and reg-consistent, then the only way of how one of the three properties from above can be satisfied is that for some

we have that

is defined and
\textsf{dom}(I(p))\neq\mathsf{regv}(p)
(note that, by assumption,
\mathsf{regv}(p)
must be defined), or that

\textsf{reg}(F(p))\neq\mathsf{domv}(p)

must be defined), or that both

\mathsf{domv}(p)

Since we have already computed the values
\mathsf{regv}(p)
and
\mathsf{domv}(p)
for every

\bigo(|Q||\mathcal{R}|)

Hence, we can check whether

\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
are garbage-free.

Given sets
A
and
B
, we define the set
\textsf{Asg}^{*}(A,B)\subseteq\textsf{Asg}(A,B)
of all copyless assignments that are copyless both in
A
and
B
, simultaneously; namely, every element in
A\cup B
appears at most once in the image of
\sigma
for every
\sigma\in\textsf{Asg}^{*}(A,B)
. Note that
\textsf{Asg}^{*}(A,B)
is a finite set. In the sequel, we will use
\zeta
to denote an assignment in
\textsf{Asg}^{*}(A,B)
.

We define the NSST
\mathcal{T}
that will work as the composition of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
such that:

\mathcal{T}\ =\ (Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\mathcal{R},\Delta,I,F)

where
Q=Q_{1}\times\{f\colon\mathcal{R}_{1}\rightharpoonup(Q_{2}\times\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})\times Q_{2})\}
. Intuitively, the states of
\mathcal{T}
simulates a run of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
in the first component. In the second component,
\mathcal{T}
remember a
subrun

f(X)=(q_{1},\sigma,q_{2})
over the content of register
X\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
from
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, where
q_{1}\in Q_{2}
is the starting state,
q_{2}\in Q_{2}
is the ending state, and
\sigma
is a
summary
of the registers content of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
. To define the other components of
\mathcal{T}
(i.e.,
\Delta
,
I
, and
F
) we need several technical definitions that we introduce next.

Summaries

We start by formalizing the notion of summary, introduced in
[
3
]
. Given a word
w\in(\mathcal{R}_{2}\cup\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})^{*}
we define the
summary of
w
by
\mathcal{R}
as the pair
(\bar{w},\zeta)
such that, if
w
is of the form:

w=u_{1}X_{1}u_{2}X_{2}\ldots X_{k}u_{k+1}

for some
k\geq 0
such that
X_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
and
u_{i}\in(\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})^{*}
, then
\bar{w}=Y_{1}X_{1}Y_{2}X_{2}\ldots X_{k}Y_{k+1}
for some pairwise distinct registers
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k+1}\in\mathcal{R}
and
\zeta\in\textsf{Asg}(\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k+1}\},\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})
satisfies that
\zeta(Y_{i})=u_{i}
for every
i\leq k+1
. The idea of a summary
(\bar{w},\zeta)
is to replace each maximal subword
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k+1}\in(\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})^{*}
from
w
with distinct registers
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k+1}
, represented by
\bar{w}
, and store them into the registers
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k+1}
, represented by
\zeta
. In particular, one can check that
\zeta(\bar{w})=w
and that
\zeta
is always copyless.

We can generalize the notion of summary from words to assignments as follows. Let
\sigma\in\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})
be an assignment and assume that
\textsf{dom}(\sigma)=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}\}
.
The summary of
\sigma
by
\mathcal{R}
is a pair
(\bar{\sigma},\zeta)
such that, if
(\bar{w}_{1},\zeta_{1}),\ldots,(\bar{w}_{\ell},\zeta_{\ell})
are summaries of
\sigma(X_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(X_{\ell})
, respectively, with
\textsf{dom}(\zeta_{i})\cap\textsf{dom}(\zeta_{i^{\prime}})=\emptyset
for every
i\neq i^{\prime}
, then
\bar{\sigma}(X_{j})=\bar{w}_{j}
for every
j\leq\ell
and
\zeta=\zeta_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\zeta_{\ell}
. Intuitively, we are summarizing every
\sigma(X_{i})
together into a single assignment
\bar{\sigma}
and
\zeta
is the disjoint union of
\zeta_{i}
.
Finally, the summary of a tuple
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
with each
\sigma_{i}\in\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})
is a pair
((\bar{\sigma}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\sigma}_{n}),\zeta_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\zeta_{n})
such that, for every
i\leq n
,
(\bar{\sigma}_{i},\zeta_{i})
is a summary of
\sigma_{i}
and
\textsf{dom}(\zeta_{i})\cap\textsf{dom}(\zeta_{j})=\emptyset
for every
j\neq i
.
Notice that
\zeta
is an assignment in
\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R},\Gamma)
and it is always copyless, since we always pick disjoint set of registers for each component.

Semigroup of subruns

The following semigroup will be useful for composing subruns and checking if the composition is valid. Consider the semigroup with domain
\mathcal{S}=(Q_{2}\times\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\Gamma\cup\mathcal{R})\times Q_{2})\cup\{\bot\}
where
\bot
is a new fresh element. Then we define the operation
\circledast
of the semigroup such that:

(q_{1},\sigma,q_{2})\circledast(q_{3},\sigma^{\prime},q_{4})\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(q_{1},\sigma\circ\sigma^{\prime},q_{4})&\text{if }q_{2}=q_{3}\\
\bot&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

Further,
s_{1}\circledast s_{2}=\bot
whenever
s_{1}=\bot
or
s_{2}=\bot
.
One can easily check that
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
forms a semigroup, given that
\circledast
is well-defined in
\mathcal{S}
(i.e., the result is always an element in
\mathcal{S}
), and is associative.
Below, we will use the semigroup
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
to easily apply and check that subruns match. Indeed, one can check that
s_{1}\circledast s_{2}=s_{3}
and
s_{3}\neq\bot
, then
s_{3}
is the subrun representing
s_{1}
followed by
s_{2}
. Instead, if
s_{1}\circledast s_{2}=\bot
then either
s_{1}
or
s_{2}
are not valid subruns, or
s_{1}
followed by
s_{2}
does not form a subrun.

For using the semigroup
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
, the notion of homomorphism between semigroups will be also relevant. Let
A
be any finite alphabet. An
homomorphism
from
(A^{*},\cdot)
to
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
is a function
H:A^{*}\to\mathcal{S}
such that
H(w_{1}\cdot w_{2})=H(w_{1})\circledast H(w_{2})
. In the following, we will usually define homomorphisms as a function
H:A\to\mathcal{S}
, that extends naturally to a homomorphism
H:A^{*}\to\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that
H(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})=H(a_{1})\circledast\ldots\circledast H(a_{n})
.

Assignment indexing

The last technical ingredient is a notation for indexing all letters from an assignment in
\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R}_{1},\Omega)
.
Formally, for
c\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\Omega
, let
\textsf{index}(c,X,i)
be a function such that
\textsf{index}(c,X,i)=(c,X,i)
if
c\in\Omega
and
\textsf{index}(c,X,i)=c
, otherwise.
Then we define
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
as an assignment in
\textsf{Asg}(\mathcal{R}_{1},\Omega\times\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathbb{N})
such that
\textsf{dom}(\sigma_{\textsf{index}})=\textsf{dom}(\sigma)
and, if
\sigma(X)=c_{1}\ldots c_{n}
, then:

\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(X)=\textsf{index}(c_{1},X,1)\ldots\textsf{index}(c_{n},X,n).

For the special case that
\sigma(X)=\epsilon
, we define
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(X)=(\epsilon,X,1)
.
In other words,
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
works as
\sigma
where each letter in
\sigma(X)
is indexed by
X
and its position. For example, below we show an assignment
\sigma
and its corresponding
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
.

\begin{array}[]{ll}\begin{array}[]{rl}\sigma:&X:=baXab\\
&Y:=Xb\\
&Z:=\epsilon\end{array}&\ \ \ \ \ \begin{array}[]{rl}\sigma_{\textsf{index}}:&X:=(b,X,1)(a,X,2)Y(a,X,4)(b,X,5)\\
&Y:=X(b,Y,2)\\
&Z:=(\epsilon,Z,1)\end{array}\end{array}

Finally, define
\textsf{index}[\sigma]
to be all letters of the form
(c,X,i)
that appear in
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
. We call
(c,X,i)
an
index
of
\sigma
.

It will be convenient to extend the notation
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
to words. Specifically, for a word
w=c_{1}\ldots c_{n}\in(R_{1}\cup\Omega)^{*}
we define
w_{\textsf{index}}=\textsf{index}(c_{1},1)\ldots\textsf{index}(c_{n},n)
where
\textsf{index}(c,i)=(c,i)
if
c\in\Omega
, and
\textsf{index}(c,i)=c
, otherwise. If
w=\epsilon
, we define
w_{\textsf{index}}=(\epsilon,1)
. For the word case, note that we omit the
X
component in the index, given that it is unnecessary.

The purpose of
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}
is to identify each letter
a
(or
\epsilon
) in
\sigma
and to assign a special code
(a,X,i)
. The set
\textsf{index}[\sigma]
will allow us to collect all these codes for deciding the transitions that we will use when the NSST
\mathcal{T}_{1}
takes a transition. This will be more clear in the next subsection, when we finally define the transition relation of
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition of
\Delta

We have all the ingredients to define the components
\Delta
,
I
, and
F
of the NSST
\mathcal{T}
. For defining
\Delta
, we repeat the following steps, until all possible decisions are covered.

1.

Take a state
(p,f)\in Q
(recall that
f:\mathcal{R}_{1}\rightharpoonup Q_{2}\times\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})\times Q_{2}
).

2.

Take a transition
(p,a,\sigma,q)\in\Delta_{1}
.

3.

Let
\textsf{index}[\sigma]=\{(a_{1},X_{1},i_{1}),\ldots,(a_{\ell},X_{\ell},i_{\ell})\}
be all indexes of
\sigma
.

4.

Choose transitions
(q_{1},a_{1},\sigma_{1},q_{1}^{\prime}),\ldots,(q_{\ell},a_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell},q_{\ell}^{\prime})\in\Delta_{2}
.
For the special case that
a_{i}=\epsilon
for some
i\leq\ell
, choose a pair of the form
(q_{i},\epsilon,\sigma_{\textsf{id}},q_{i})
where
q_{i}\in Q_{2}
and
\sigma_{\textsf{id}}
is the identity function over
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

5.

Define the homomorphism
H:\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\textsf{index}[\sigma]\to\mathcal{S}
from words to the semigroup
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
such that

H(X)=f(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
H((a_{j},X_{j},i_{j}))=(q_{j},\sigma_{j},q_{j}^{\prime})
for every
j\leq\ell
.

6.

If
H(\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(X))\neq\bot
for every
X\in\textsf{dom}(\sigma_{\textsf{index}})
, then continue.

7.

Let
\textsf{dom}(\sigma)=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
and let
H(\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(Y_{i}))=(p_{i},\pi_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
for every
i\leq m
.

8.

Construct the summary
((\bar{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\pi}_{m}),\zeta)
of
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m})
.

9.

Define the partial function
f^{\prime}
such that
\textsf{dom}(f^{\prime})=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
and
f^{\prime}(Y_{i})=(p_{i},\bar{\pi}_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
.

10.

Add the transition
((p,f),a,\zeta,(q,f^{\prime}))
to
\Delta
.

For understanding the previous construction, recall that
(p,f)\in Q
represents that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is in state
p
and, for every register
X
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
has a subrun
f(X)
over the content of
X
(step (1)). Then, if we move
\mathcal{T}_{1}
by using
(p,a,\sigma,q)\in\Delta_{1}
(step (2)), then we need to update the moves of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, namely, update
f
with
\sigma
and
\Delta_{2}
. The moves of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
for registers in
\sigma
are encoded in
f
, but we still have to decide how to move with letters in
\sigma
. For this reason, we choose one transition
(q_{j},a_{j},\sigma_{j},q_{j}^{\prime})
for each letter
a_{j}\in\Omega
appearing in
\sigma
, represented by the index
(a_{j},X_{j},i_{j})
(step (3) and (4)). Thus, for simulating
\mathcal{T}_{2}
over
\sigma
, we define the homomorphism
H
, which for every register content
X
uses
f(X)
and for every letter indexed as
(a_{j},X_{j},i_{j})
uses
(q_{j},\sigma_{j},q_{j}^{\prime})
(step (5)). To check that these guesses of the subruns and the transitions are correct, we check that the homomorphism
H
applied to each content
\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(X)
is different from
\bot
(step (6)). If this is the case, it means that the subruns and guessed transitions coincide, and we proceed to construct the new transition in
\Delta
(steps (7) to (10)). Towards this goal, we summarize the subrun
H(\sigma_{\textsf{index}}(Y_{i}))=(p_{i},\pi_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
and store it in
f^{\prime}
as
f^{\prime}(Y_{i})=(p_{i},\bar{\pi}_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
(steps (7) to (9)). The summaries produce the copyless assignment
\zeta
that stores the partial valuations of the summaries in
\mathcal{R}
-registers. Finally, we define the transition
((p,f),a,\zeta,(q,f^{\prime}))\in\Delta
, which encodes the whole process above.

It is important to note that
\zeta
in the transition
((p,f),a,\zeta,(q,f^{\prime}))\in\Delta
is a copyless assignment. Indeed, given that
H
works as a homomorphism and there is a single appearance of each register
X
in
\sigma
, then each register of
\mathcal{R}
appears at most once over all assignments
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m}
. Therefore, the construction here always produces copyless assignments.

Definition of
I

For defining the initial partial function
I
, we proceed similar to
\Delta
:

1.

Take a state
p\in Q_{1}
such that
I_{1}(p)=\nu
.

2.

Let
\textsf{index}[\nu]=\{(a_{1},X_{1},i_{1}),\ldots(a_{\ell},X_{\ell},i_{\ell})\}
be the indexes of
\nu
.

3.

Choose transitions
(q_{1},a_{1},\sigma_{1},q_{1}^{\prime}),\ldots,(q_{\ell},a_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell},q_{\ell}^{\prime})\in\Delta_{2}
.
For the special case that
a_{i}=\epsilon
for some
i\leq\ell
, choose a pair of the form
(q_{i},\epsilon,\sigma_{\textsf{id}},q_{i})
where
q_{i}\in Q_{2}
and
\sigma_{\textsf{id}}
is the identity function over
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

4.

Define the homomorphism
H:\textsf{index}[\nu]\to\mathcal{S}
from words to the semigroup
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
such that
H((a_{j},X_{j},i_{j}))=(q_{j},\sigma_{j},q_{j}^{\prime})
for every
j\leq\ell
.

5.

If
H(\nu_{\textsf{index}}(X))\neq\bot
for every
X\in\textsf{dom}(\nu_{\textsf{index}})
, then continue.

6.

Let
\textsf{dom}(\nu)=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
and let
H(\nu_{\textsf{index}}(Y_{i}))=(p_{i},\pi_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
for every
i\leq m
.

7.

Construct the summary
((\bar{\pi}_{1},\ldots,\bar{\pi}_{m}),\zeta)
of
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m})
.

8.

Define the partial function
f
such that
\textsf{dom}(f)=\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\}
and
f(Y_{i})=(p_{i},\bar{\pi}_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})
.

9.

Define
I((p,f))=\zeta
.

The definition of
I
follows the same approach as for the transition relation
\Delta
. Intuitively, if we start with a state
p
such that
I_{1}(p)
is defined and equal to
\nu
, then we run
\mathcal{T}_{2}
over the content in
\nu
. Given that
\nu
is an assignment, the definition follows the same ideas as for
\Delta
, by switching
\sigma
with
\nu
.

Definition of
F

The construction of the final function
F
is slightly different to
\Delta
and
I
, although we present it in a similar fashion.

1.

Take a state
(p,f)\in Q
such that
F_{1}(p)
is defined.

2.

Let
\textsf{index}[F_{1}(p)]=\{(a_{1},i_{1}),\ldots(a_{\ell},i_{\ell})\}
be the indexes of
F_{1}(p)
.

3.

Choose transitions
(q_{1},a_{1},\sigma_{1},q_{1}^{\prime}),\ldots,(q_{\ell},a_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell},q_{\ell}^{\prime})\in\Delta_{2}
(not necessarily different).
For the special case that
F_{1}(p)=\epsilon
and
\textsf{index}[F_{1}(p)]=\{(\epsilon,1)\}
, choose one tuple of the form
(r,\epsilon,\sigma_{\textsf{id}},r)
for some
r\in Q_{2}
and
\sigma_{\textsf{id}}
is the identity function over
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

4.

Define the homomorphism
H:\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\textsf{index}[F_{1}(p)]\to\mathcal{S}
from words to the semigroup
(\mathcal{S},\circledast)
such that

H(X)=f(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
H((a_{j},i_{j}))=(q_{j},\sigma_{j},q_{j}^{\prime})
for every
j\leq\ell
.

5.

If
H(F_{1}(p))=(p_{0},\pi,p_{f})
and both
I_{2}(p_{0})
and
F_{2}(p_{f})
are defined, then define:

F((p,f))=[I_{2}(p_{0})]\big(\pi(F_{2}(p_{f}))\big).

Here, the goal is to reconstruct the final output of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, given that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
reaches a final state where
F_{1}(p)
is defined (step (1)). Then, similar to
\Delta
, we choose the transitions for the letters in
F_{1}(p)
(steps (2) and (3)), and construct the homomorphism
H
to simulate
\mathcal{T}_{2}
over
F_{1}(p)
(step (4)). If
H(F_{1}(p))=(p_{0},\pi,p_{f})
, this means that the subruns and transitions of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
match. However, we have to still check that
p_{0}
is an initial state and
p_{f}
is a final state. If this is the case, then we can reconstruct the output of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
with the expression
[I_{2}(p_{0})]\big(\pi(F_{2}(p_{f}))\big)
(step (5)).

Correctness

This concludes the construction of
\mathcal{T}
. To show that
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})={\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
for every document
\mathbf{D}
, the proofs goes by making a bijection between accepting runs
\rho
of
\mathcal{T}
over
\mathbf{D}
and pair of runs
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
such that
\rho_{1}
is an accepting run of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
over
\mathbf{D}
and
\rho_{2}
is an accepting run of
\mathcal{T}_{2}
over the output of
\rho_{1}
.
For defining this bijection
F
, we can follow the construction above and, for every run
\rho
of
\mathcal{T}
over
\mathcal{D}
, construct a unique pair
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
by induction. For showing that
F
is injective, the garbage-free assumption over
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
helps, given that all the content tracked by
\mathcal{T}
is useful, and then always produce a unique pair
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
. For proving that
F
is surjective, from a pair of runs
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
we can construct the run
\rho
over
\mathbf{D}
by following the construction above.
Given that there is a bijection between
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
and
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}(\mathbf{D})
for every
\mathbf{D}
, we conclude that
{\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}}\rsem}={\lsem{}{\mathcal{T}_{1}\circ\mathcal{T}_{2}}\rsem}
.

Size of
\mathcal{T}

To bound the size of the NSST
\mathcal{T}
, let
|Q_{1}|=k
,
|\mathcal{R}_{1}|=\ell
,
|Q_{2}|=m
, and
|\mathcal{R}_{2}|=n
. Recall that
Q=Q_{1}\times\mathcal{F}
where:

\mathcal{F}\ =\ \{f:\mathcal{R}_{1}\rightharpoonup Q_{2}\times\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})\times Q_{2}\}.

First, we bound the number of register in
\mathcal{R}
that we need to do the summaries (i.e.,
|\mathcal{R}|
). For this, we need to bound the number of states to do a summary of one
f\in\mathcal{F}
. For one assignment
\sigma\in\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})
, we will need at most
(n+1)^{2}
registers. Given that we have at most
\ell
of these assignments in
f
, then we need at most
\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2}
registers in
\mathcal{R}
, namely,
|\mathcal{R}|\leq\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2}
. To simplify the following calculations, let
N=\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2}
.

Next, we bound the size of
\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})
. For any
\sigma\in\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})
and any
X\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
, we can put at most
(n+N+1)!
different strings at the entry
\sigma(X)
. Therefore,
|\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})|\leq((n+N+1)!)^{n}
.

To finish, we bound the size of
Q_{1}\times\mathcal{F}
. Given that
\mathcal{F}
are partial functions we have that the number of possible elements in the image is:

|Q_{2}\times\textsf{Asg}^{*}(\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{R})\times Q_{2}|\leq m^{2}\cdot((n+N+1)!)^{n}

Then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq(m^{2}\cdot((n+N+1)!)^{n})^{\ell}
. We conclude that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|Q|&\leq&|Q_{1}|\cdot|\mathcal{F}|\\
&\leq&k\cdot(m^{2}\cdot((n+N+1)!)^{n})^{\ell}\\
&\leq&k\cdot m^{2\ell}\cdot((n+N+1)!)^{n\ell}\\
&\leq&k\cdot m^{2\ell}\cdot((n+\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2}+1)!)^{n\ell}\\
&\leq&2^{\log(k)+2\ell\cdot\log(m)+n\ell\cdot(\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2})\cdot\log(\ell\cdot(n+1)^{2})}\end{array}

where for the last equation we are using that
x!\leq x^{x}
for
x
big enough. Then the number of states of
\mathcal{T}
is in
O\big(2^{g(|\mathcal{T}_{1}|,|\mathcal{T}_{2}|)}\big)
for some polynomial
g(x,y)
. Note that one can similarly bound the number of transitions in
O\big(2^{g(|\mathcal{T}_{1}|,|\mathcal{T}_{2}|)}\big)
.
∎
