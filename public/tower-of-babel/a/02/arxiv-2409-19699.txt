Efficient Verification of Stabilizer Code Subspaces with Local Measurements
Congcong Zheng, Xutao Yu, Zaichen Zhang, Ping Xu, Kun Wang

Abstract
We address the task of verifying whether a quantum computer, designed to be protected by a specific stabilizer code, correctly encodes the corresponding logical qubits. To achieve this, we develop a general framework for subspace verification and explore several stabilizer code subspaces of practical significance. First, we present two efficient verification strategies for general stabilizer code subspaces, utilizing measurements of their stabilizer generators and stabilizer groups, respectively. Then, building on the observation that certain tests can be conducted in parallel when the subspace exhibits specific structural properties, we propose a coloring strategy tailored to graph code subspaces and an XZ strategy tailored to Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) code subspaces. Compared to stabilizer-based strategies, these new strategies require significantly fewer measurement settings and consume fewer state copies, approaching near-global optimality. Notably, all the strategies employ a limited number of Pauli measurements, are non-adaptive, and work on mixed states, enabling efficient experimental certification of both logical qubits and logical operations in noisy quantum computers. This work contributes to the first systematic study of efficient verification of stabilizer code subspaces with local measurements.

II
General framework of subspace verification

Let
\mathcal{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\rm span}\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}
be the subspace spanned
by a set of orthonormal states
\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}_{j}
,
where
|{\psi_{j}}\rangle
lies in an
n
-partite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\otimes_{\ell=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
.
By definition,
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V})
be the set of density operators acting on
\mathcal{V}

and
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{j}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|
be the projector onto
\mathcal{V}
.
It should be noted that
\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V})
if and only if
\tr[\Pi\sigma]=1
,
as proved in Appendix
A
.
We can now formally define the quantum subspace verification task:
Given a quantum computer
\mathcal{D}
designed to produce states in
\mathcal{V}
and
N
copies of states
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N}
generated by
\mathcal{D}
,
the objective is to distinguish between the following two cases:
(i)
Good
: for all
i\in[N]
,
\tr[\Pi\sigma_{i}]=1
;
(ii)
Bad
: for all
i\in[N]
,
\tr[\Pi\sigma_{i}]\leq 1-\epsilon
for some fixed
\epsilon
.
In the following, we discuss how to complete this task.

Suppose that we have access to a set of POVM elements
\mathcal{M}
.
Define a probability mass
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{M}\to[0,1]
,
\sum\mu(M)=1
.
For each state preparation, we pick a POVM element
M\in\mathcal{M}
with probability
\mu(M)

and consider the corresponding two-outcomes POVMs
\{M,\mathbbm{1}-M\}
,
where
M
has output “pass” and
\mathbbm{1}-M
has output “fail”. We call
M
a
test operator
.
The expected probability of a randomly generated quantum state
\sigma
passing the test reads

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\right\}=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mu(M)\tr[M\sigma]=\tr[\Omega\sigma],

(1)

where the
verification operator
of this strategy is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mu(M)M.

(2)

To satisfy the requirement of the verification task,
we impose two conditions on the verification operator
\Omega
:
perfect completeness
condition and
soundness
condition.
The perfect completeness condition requires that

\forall\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V}),\tr[\Omega\sigma]=1
.
Intuitively, this condition guarantees that states in the target subspace
\mathcal{V}
can always pass the test.
This condition can be equivalently characterized using the projector
\Pi
associated with
\mathcal{V}
as

\tr[\Omega\Pi]=\rank(\Pi)
, where
\rank(X)
denotes the rank of the operator
X
.
Detailed arguments and proofs is given in Appendix
A
.
Then, let’s consider the soundness condition.
We show that the worst-case passing probability
p(\Omega)
, defined as

\displaystyle p(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Pi\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\Pr\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\},

(3)

in the
Bad
case is uniquely
determined by the largest eigenvalue of the projected effective verification operator,
as elucidated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

It holds that

\displaystyle p(\Omega)=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Pi\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\tr[\Omega\sigma]=1-(1-\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega}))\epsilon,

(4)

where
\widehat{\Omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbbm{1}-\Pi)\Omega(\mathbbm{1}-\Pi)
is the
projected effective verification operator
and
\lambda_{\max}(X)
denotes the maximal eigenvalue of the Hermitian operator
X
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A
.
Therefore, the probability of accepting the
Bad
case is bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\text{``accept''}|\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{N}\right\}\leq(1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon)^{N},

(5)

where
\nu(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega})
is the
spectral gap
.
To achieve a confidence level
1-\delta
, it suffices to take

\displaystyle N(\Omega)\geq\frac{1}{\nu(\Omega)}\times\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

(6)

state copies.
This inequality provides a guideline for constructing efficient verification strategies by maximizing
\nu(\Omega)
.

As evident from Eqs. (
2
) and (
6
),
the sample complexity of a verification strategy
\Omega
depends heavily on the set of available measurements,
\mathcal{M}
.
Consider the extreme case where the strategies are constructed from measurements without any restrictions, referred to as
global
or
entangled measurements
, in contrast to the
local measurements
that will be investigated.
The complexity of this globally optimal strategy serves as a reasonable benchmark for
verification strategies with restricted measurements.
Define the test POVM
\{\Omega_{g},\mathbbm{1}-\Omega_{g}\}
with
\Omega_{g}=\Pi
,
which satisfies
\nu(\Omega_{g})=1
.
Therefore, it suffices to take
N\geq 1/\epsilon\ln 1/\delta
state copies to achieve a confidence level
1-\delta
.
However, it is important to note that the globally optimal verification strategy necessitates the use of entangled measurements if the target subspace is entangled (in which case there is at least one entangled basis state).
Implementing entangled measurements is experimentally challenging.
In the following, we discuss subspace verification under local and non-adaptive measurement restrictions, where each POVM element
M
is a local projector
and fixed
a prior
, rather than being chosen based on prior measurement settings and/or their outcomes.
Strategies under these restrictions are much more experimentally feasible.
Notably, we show that these severely restrictions incur only a
constant-factor
penalty compared to the globally optimal strategy.

III
Stabilizer subspace verification

Let
\{I,X,Y,Z\}
be the single-qubit Pauli operators.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denotes the Pauli group on
n
qubits,
consisting of
n
-fold tensor products of
I,X,Y,Z
with the overall factors
\pm 1
or
\pm i
.
An
[\![n,k,d]\!]
stabilizer code is defined by a commutative group
\mathcal{S}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}
acting on the state space of
n
physical qubits
[
24
,
42
]
.
The group
\mathcal{S}_{k}
has
n-k
independent generators, labeled as
\mathcal{G}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{n-k}\}
.
A stabilizer code
\mathcal{S}_{k}
induces a stabilizer subspace
\mathcal{V}
, defined to be the
+1
eigenspace of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
and can be interpreted
as the state space of
k
logical qubits
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
Thus, verifying the stabilizer subspace is equivalent to verifying logical qubits
[
24
]
,
a critical task as we are marching on the era of fault-tolerant quantum computing.
In the following, we provide two efficient verification strategies for
verifying a general stabilizer subspace
\mathcal{V}
induced by
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.
These strategies utilize only Pauli measurements, which have two outcomes,
+1
or
-1
, indicating whether the state lies in the positive or negative eigenspace of the corresponding Pauli operator.

Strategy I:
This strategy involves choosing a stabilizer operator
P
from
\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}

uniformly at random and measure the state with
P
.
If the measurement outcome is
+1
, the state passes this test; otherwise, it fails.
Mathematically, the verification operator of this strategy is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm I}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}P^{+},

(7)

where
P^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(P+\mathbbm{1})/2
is the projector onto the positive eigenspace of the stabilizer operator
P
.
We prove in Appendix
B
that

\Omega_{\rm I}
is a valid verification strategy for
\mathcal{V}
,
the spectral gap of
\Omega_{\rm I}
is
\nu(\Omega_{\rm I})=2^{n-k-1}/(2^{n-k}-1)
,
and uniform random sampling is optimal when only the set of measurements
\mathcal{S}_{k}
is available.
Therefore, to achieve a confidence level
1-\delta
, it suffices to take

\displaystyle N(\Omega_{\rm I})=\frac{2^{n-k}-1}{2^{n-k-1}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\approx 2\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}

(8)

state copies, which is independent of the number of logical qubits
k
and physical qubits
n
.
Moreover, this strategy necessitates at most twice as many copies as the globally optimal verification.
However,
Strategy I
requires implementing
2^{n-k}-1
different Pauli measurement settings,
which increases exponentially with the number of generators
n-k
.
This drawback motivates the second strategy, which involves exponentially fewer measurement settings.

Strategy II:

This strategy chooses a stabilizer generator
S
from
\mathcal{G}_{k}
uniformly at random
for each state and performs the corresponding measurement.
The state passes only if the measurement outcome is
+1
.
Mathematically, the verification operator of this strategy is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm II}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k}}S^{+},

(9)

where
S^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(S+\mathbbm{1})/2
is the projector onto the positive eigenspace of the stabilizer generator
S
.
In Appendix
B
, we prove that

\Omega_{\rm II}
is a valid verification strategy for
\mathcal{V}
,
the spectral gap of
\Omega_{\rm II}
is
\nu(\Omega_{\rm II})=1/(n-k)
,
and uniform random sampling is optimal when only the set of measurements
\mathcal{G}_{k}
is available.
Therefore, to achieve a confidence level of
1-\delta
, it suffices to choose

\displaystyle N(\Omega_{\rm II})=(n-k)\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}.

(10)

Strategy II
requires exponentially fewer measurement settings compared to
Strategy I

while consuming only
(n-k)/2
times more state copies than
Strategy I
.
The results of these two strategies are consistent with those in
[
14
]
,
which investigated the special case
k=0
, corresponding solely to an entangled state.
Although these two strategies work for all stabilizer subspaces, more efficient strategies warrant further investigation.
In the following sections, we explore two large classes of stabilizer codes that are of practical interests
and propose subspace verification strategies requiring fewer measurement settings and consuming fewer state samples.

IV
Graph subspace verification

A stabilizer code can be represented as a graph code
using local unitary transformations
[
43
]
,
and graph codes are of primary importance in photonic quantum technologies
[
44
]
.
An
[\![n,k,d]\!]
graph code
\mathscr{G}=(G,W)
is determined by an undirected graph
G=(V,E)

and an Abelian group
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\bm{w}_{1},\cdots,\bm{w}_{k}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, where
V\equiv[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
The graph
G
induces a
graph state

|{G}\rangle
,
which is an
n
-qubit stabilizer state determined by the stabilizer generators
\mathcal{G}_{G}=\{S_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
where
S_{i}=X_{i}\prod_{(i,j)\in E}Z_{j}
, and
X_{i}
means
X
in the
i
-th qubit.
The corresponding
graph subspace

\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
is spanned by an orthogonal basis
\{G_{\bm{w}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bm{w}\in W\}
, where

\displaystyle|{G_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z^{\bm{w}}|{G}\rangle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{w_{i}}|{G}\rangle.

(11)

A graph code can also be represented in graphical form, with each
\bm{w}
visualized as a logical qubit, as shown in Fig.
2
.
In the following, we propose an efficient verification strategy for
\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
in two steps.
First, we construct a new graph structure of the graph code
\mathscr{G}
consisting of
n-k
vertices.
Then, we introduce a coloring strategy for the graph code based on the new graph structure,
inspired by hypergraph state verification strategies
[
45
]
.
For the sake of easy understanding, we focus on the single logical qubit case, i.e.,
k=1
,
with the analysis for multi-logical qubits case detailed in Appendix
C
.

IV.1
Step 1: Design a new graph

Since
k=1
, there is only one element
\bm{w}\neq\bm{0}\in W
.
Let
{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{l\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bm{x}_{l}\neq 0\}\subseteq[n]
for
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.
We design a new graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
that induces
n-1
stabilizer generators of the graph code
\mathscr{G}
via
\bm{w}
.
The vertices in
V^{\prime}
and their associated stabilizer generators are constructed as follows:

•

If
|{\rm supp}(\bm{w})|=1
,
then
V^{\prime}=V\setminus{\rm supp}(\bm{w})
and
\forall a\in V^{\prime}
,
S_{a}^{\prime}=S_{a}
.

•

Else, we first set
V^{\prime}=V\setminus{\rm supp}(\bm{w})
and
\forall a\in V^{\prime}
,
S_{a}^{\prime}=S_{a}
.
Then, we sort the vertices in
{\rm supp}(\bm{w})
in the ascending order and group them pair-wisely.
For example,
{\rm supp}(\bm{w})=\{1,2,5\}
yields
\{\{1,2\},\{2,5\}\}
.
Finally, we treat each pair
\{a,\bar{a}\}
as a new vertex and add it to
V^{\prime}
.
The associated stabilizer
S^{\prime}_{\{a,\bar{a}\}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=S_{a}S_{\bar{a}}
.

By construction,
|V^{\prime}|=n-1
and vertices in
V^{\prime}
are directly related to vertices in
V
.
We show in Appendix
C
that such
constructed stabilizers are indeed stabilizer generators of
\mathscr{G}
.
Note that the construction of stabilizers here is different from the
standard stabilizers construction of graph states.

Define
\delta_{a,b}=1
if
(a,b)\in E
else
\delta_{a,b}=0
.
The set of edges
E^{\prime}
is constructed as follows: For arbitrary
v_{1},v_{2}\in V^{\prime}
,

1.

If
v_{1}=a
and
v_{2}=b
,
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E^{\prime}
if
\delta_{a,b}=1
;

2.

If
v_{1}=a
and
v_{2}=(b,\bar{b})
,
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E^{\prime}
if
\delta_{a,b}=1
or
\delta_{a,\bar{b}}=1
;

3.

If
v_{1}=\{a,\bar{a}\}
and
v_{2}=\{b,\bar{b}\}
,
(v_{1},v_{2})\not\in E^{\prime}
if at least one of the following two conditions holds:

(a)

v_{1}\cap v_{2}=\emptyset
and
\delta_{a,b}=\delta_{a,\bar{b}}=\delta_{\bar{a},b}=\delta_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}
;

(b)

v_{1}\cup v_{2}=\{a,b,c\}
and
\delta_{a,b}=\delta_{a,c}=\delta_{b,c}
.

These rules are visualized in Appendix
C
.
Note that the edges in
E^{\prime}
do not contribute to the construction of stabilizers but
determine the construction of the verification strategy.

To deepen understanding of the construction, which is a bit complicated,
we present two representative examples of graph codes
[
46
]
in Fig.
2
,
explicitly detailing the original graphs, the new graphs, and the relationships between these graphs.

IV.2
Step 2: Construct a coloring strategy

Based on the new graph
G^{\prime}
,
we propose the following coloring verification strategy for the graph subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
.
Let
\mathscr{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{m}\}
be an independence cover of
G^{\prime}

composed of
m
nonempty independent sets
[
47
,
45
]
.
For each
A_{\ell}\in\mathscr{A}
, we construct a test operator
P_{\ell}
in the following way:
For each
v\in A_{\ell}
,

1.

If
v=a
, perform
X
measurement on the
a
-th qubit;

2.

If
v=(a,\bar{a})
and
\delta_{a,\bar{a}}=1
, perform
Y
measurements on
a
-th
and
\bar{a}
-th qubits; If
v=(a,\bar{a})
and
\delta_{a,\bar{a}}=0
,
perform
X
measurements on these two qubits.

3.

Perform
Z
measurement on other not involved qubits.

The state passes the test only if the measurement result of
S^{\prime}_{a}
is
+1
for all
a\in A_{\ell}
.
As shown in Appendix
C
, the test operator takes the form
P_{\ell}=\prod_{a\in A_{\ell}}(\mathbbm{1}+S^{\prime}_{a})/2
.
The verification strategy is constructed by sampling from the set
\{P_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
uniformly at random and
the verification operator is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathscr{G}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}P_{\ell}.

(12)

In Appendix
C
, we prove that

\Omega_{\mathscr{G}}
is a valid verification strategy for the graph code
\mathscr{G}
,
the spectral gap satisfies
\nu(\Omega_{\mathscr{G}})=1/m
, and
uniform random sampling is optimal when only the set of measurements
\{P_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{m}
is available.
Consequently, to achieve a confidence level of
1-\delta
, it suffices to take

\displaystyle N(\Omega_{\mathscr{G}})=m\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}.

(13)

This result is consistent with
[
45
]
, which investigated hypergraph state verification.
Recall that
Strategy II
requires
n-1
measurement settings to verify this graph subspace.
Since
m\leq n-1
by the definition of the independence cover, the new strategy
\Omega_{\mathscr{G}}

can verify the graph subspace using even fewer measurement settings and state copies,
underscoring the superiority of the coloring strategy.
Surprisingly, as demonstrated in Appendix
C
,
this advantage persists in multi-logical qubit cases,
which can be efficiently verified using a coloring strategy with
m\leq n-k
measurement settings.
This superiority is rooted in the fact that many test operators in
Strategy I
and
Strategy II
can be conducted in parallel,
thanks to the graph structure. This observation motivates an efficient protocol for the CSS subspace considered below.

VI
Conclusions

We have established a general framework for verifying entangled subspaces,
enabling efficient certification of whether an unknown state belongs to a specific subspace.
Then, we focus on the entangled subspaces spanned by stabilizer codes,
which constitute the fundamental cornerstone for the era of fault-tolerant quantum computing.
The obtained results are summarized in Table
1
.
For a general
[\![n,k,d]\!]
stabilizer code, we have proposed two efficient strategies to verify the corresponding stabilizer subspace.

Strategy I
is derived from the full stabilizer group, requires
2^{n-k}-1
measurement settings, and consumes
2/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
state copies.

Strategy II
is constructed solely from the stabilizer generators, requires
n-k
measurement settings, and consumes
(n-k)/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
states copies.
To further improve the verification efficiency, we have investigated two special types of stabilizer codes, namely graph codes and CSS codes, which are of practical interests.
For an
[\![n,k,d]\!]
graph code, we have introduced a new graph structure of the graph code and propose a coloring strategy for verifying the corresponding graph subspace.
This strategy uses
m\leq n-k
measurement settings and requires
m/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
state copies.
For an
[\![n,k,d]\!]
CSS code, we have proposed an XZ strategy that uses only
2
measurement settings and consumes
2/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
state copies, regardless of the code size.
This strategy achieves
nearly
the same performance as the globally optimal strategy in terms of both the required number of measurement settings and state copies.
For the special dual-containing codes, we have proposed an even more efficient strategy that uses
just
3
measurement settings and consumes
1.5/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
state copies.
This work contributes the first systematic study of efficient verification of stabilizer code subspaces with local measurements,
enabling experimentally efficient certification of both logical qubits and logical operations in noisy quantum computers.

Several key questions regarding quantum subspace verification remain unresolved,
with one of the most prominent being the design and proof of
optimal
verification strategies
using local measurements. In existing approaches, the quantum states are typically destroyed during the measurement process, rendering them unusable for subsequent tasks. Thus, identifying verification strategies that employ quantum nondemolition measurements,
which preserve the post-measurement states, is both intriguing and significant
[
51
]
.

Appendix A
General framework of subspace verification

Consider a quantum computer
\mathcal{D}
that produces
N
copies of
n
-qubit states, denoted

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\cdots,\sigma_{N}
.
The task of quantum state verification seeks to answer the question:

“Are the states
\sigma_{i}
generated by
\mathcal{D}
equal to a fixed state
|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
?”

Similarly, the quantum subspace verification aims to answer the question:

“Are the states
\sigma_{i}
generated by
\mathcal{D}
contained within a specific subspace
\mathcal{V}
?”

In the following, we set up a formal framework for general subspace verification strategies.

A.1
Task of subspace verification

To mathematically verify whether a state
\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V})
,
where
\mathcal{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\rm span}\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}_{j}
,
we define the projector
\Pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|
and provide the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})
be a density operator. It holds that

\tr[\Pi\sigma]=1
if and only if
\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V})
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

The
necessity
is obvious.
If
\sigma\in\text{span}\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{jl}\sigma_{jl}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{l}}|\quad\Rightarrow\quad\tr[\Pi\sigma]=\sum_{j}\langle{\psi_{j}}|\sigma|{\psi_{j}}\rangle=1.

(18)

Now we turn to show the
sufficiency
.
For an arbitrary matrix
\sigma
, it can be written as

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|{\phi_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\phi_{i}}|,\quad\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1,\quad\lambda_{i}\geq 0.

(19)

For each eigenstate
|{\phi_{i}}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle|{\phi_{i}}\rangle=\sin\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle+\cos\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}\rangle,

(20)

where
\sum_{j}|\langle\psi_{j}|\Psi_{i}\rangle|^{2}=1
, and
|{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}\rangle
is orthogonal to
|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle
.
Then,
\sigma
can also be written as

\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\left(\sin^{2}\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{i}}|+\sin\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}|+\sin\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{i}}|+\cos^{2}\theta_{i}|{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{i}^{\bot}}|\right).

(21)

With the trace constraint, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\sin^{2}\theta_{i}=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sin\theta_{i}=1,\;\forall\;\theta_{i},\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sigma=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}|{\Psi_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{i}}|,

(22)

which hints that
\sigma\in{\rm span}\{|{\psi_{i}}\rangle\}
.
∎

With the help of the above lemma, we can now formally define the quantum subspace verification
task—Given a quantum device
\mathcal{D}
that is designed to produce states in
\mathcal{V}
, distinguish between the following two cases:

1.

Good
: for all
i\in[N]
,
\tr[\Pi\sigma_{i}]=1
;

2.

Bad
: for all
i\in[N]
,
\tr[\Pi\sigma_{i}]\leq 1-\epsilon
for some fixed
\epsilon
.

A.2
The conditions for verification operator

To complete this distinguish task, we consider randomly pick a POVM element
M\in\mathcal{M}
with some probability and consider the corresponding two-outcomes POVMs
\{M,\mathbbm{1}-M\}
,
where
M
has output “pass” and
\mathbbm{1}-M
has output “fail”.
Moreover, we define a probability mass
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{M}\to[0,1]
,
\sum\mu(M)=1
.
The probability of a generated quantum state
\sigma
passing the test can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\right\}=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mu(M)\tr[M\sigma]=\tr[\Omega\sigma],

(23)

where the
verification operator
of this protocol is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\mu(M)M.

(24)

To satisfy the requirement of the verification task,
we impose two conditions on the verification operator
\Omega
:

1.

Perfect completeness
condition:
states within the target subspace
\mathcal{V}
will always pass the test, that is,

\displaystyle\tr[\Omega\sigma]=1,\quad\forall\;\sigma\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V}).

(25)

2.

Soundness
condition: states in the
Bad
case can be rejected with high probability.

Perfect completeness condition

The perfect completeness condition can be equivalently characterized using the projector
\Pi
associated with
\mathcal{V}
as follows.

Lemma 3
.

The perfect completeness condition can be equivalently characterized as

\displaystyle\tr[\Omega\Pi]=\rank(\Pi),

(26)

where
\rank(\Pi)
is the rank of the projector.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

With perfect completeness condition, there exist a set of orthogonal bases
\{|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\}_{l}
in the complementary subspace of the target subspace,
such that
\Omega
can be written as

\displaystyle\Omega=\Pi+\sum\omega_{l}|{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}|,

(27)

otherwise
\forall\;\sigma\in{\rm span}\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\},\tr[\Omega\sigma]=1
does not hold.
We define the projected effective verification operator as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbbm{1}-\Pi)\Omega(\mathbbm{1}-\Pi)=\sum\omega_{l}|{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}|.

(28)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\tr[\Omega\Pi]=\tr[\Pi^{2}]+\tr[\widehat{\Omega}\Pi]=\tr[\Pi]=\rank(\Pi).

(29)

∎

Soundness condition

Next, we consider the soundness condition.
We demonstrate that the worst-case passing probability,
p(\Omega)
, defined as

\displaystyle p(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Pi\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\Pr\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\},

(30)

in the
Bad
case is uniquely determined by the largest eigenvalue of the projected effective verification operator.
Specifically,

\displaystyle p(\Omega)=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Pi\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\tr[\Omega\sigma]=1-(1-\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega}))\epsilon.

(31)

We provide the proof as follows.

Proof of the Theorem
1
.

For a fixed set
\{|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\}
,
an arbitrary quantum state
\sigma
with
\tr[\Pi\sigma]=r
can always be written as

\displaystyle\sigma=r\Psi+(1-r)\Psi^{\bot}+\sum_{jl}\left(c_{jl}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}|+c_{jl}^{*}|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|\right),

(32)

where
\Psi
and
\Psi^{\bot}
are the states in the
{\rm span}\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}
and
{\rm span}\{|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\}
, respectively.
Then, such a state will pass the test with probability

\displaystyle\Pr\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\}

\displaystyle=\tr[\Omega\sigma]

(33)

\displaystyle=r\tr[\Omega\Psi]+(1-r)\tr[\widehat{\Omega}\Psi^{\bot}]

(34)

\displaystyle\leq r+(1-r)\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega}).

(35)

The above inequality becomes an equality if

\displaystyle\Psi^{\bot}=|{\psi^{\bot}_{\max}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{\max}}|,

(36)

where
|{\psi_{\max}^{\bot}}\rangle
is the eigenstate of
\widehat{\Omega}
corresponding to the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega})
.
Thus, for a fixed
\Omega
,

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Pi\sigma]=r}\Pr\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\}=r+(1-r)\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega}),

(37)

which is achieved by any density matrix of the form

\displaystyle\sigma=r\Psi+(1-r)|{\psi_{\max}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\max}^{\bot}}|+\sum_{jl}\left(c_{jl}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}|+c_{jl}^{*}|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|\right).

(38)

Note that the pure state
\sigma=|{\phi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|
for

\displaystyle|{\phi}\rangle=\sqrt{r}|{\psi^{\prime}}\rangle+\sqrt{1-r}|{\psi^{\bot}_{\max}}\rangle,

(39)

where
|{\psi^{\prime}}\rangle
is the linear combination of vectors
|{\psi_{j}}\rangle
, is of this form.
Therefore, we can only consider pure states in the following analysis.

Now, for a fixed
\bar{\epsilon}\geq\epsilon>0
, we define a state
\sigma=|{\phi_{\bar{\epsilon}}}\rangle\!\langle{\phi_{\bar{\epsilon}}}|
with

|{\phi_{\bar{\epsilon}}}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\bar{\epsilon}}|{\phi}\rangle+\sqrt{\bar{\epsilon}}|{\phi^{\bot}}\rangle
,
where
|{\phi}\rangle
is the linear combination of vectors
\{|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\}_{j}

and
\langle\phi|\phi^{\bot}\rangle=0
.
Then, we define that the worst-case passing probability as

\displaystyle p(\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Omega\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\Pr\{\text{``pass''}|\sigma\}

(40)

\displaystyle=\max_{\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\tr[\Omega\sigma]\leq 1-\epsilon}\tr[\Omega\sigma]

(41)

\displaystyle=\max_{\bar{\epsilon}\geq\epsilon,|{\phi^{\bot}}\rangle}1-\bar{\epsilon}+\bar{\epsilon}\langle{\phi^{\bot}}|\widehat{\Omega}|{\phi^{\bot}}\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle=1-(1-\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\Omega}))\epsilon.

(43)

∎

Therefore, the probability of accepting the
Bad
case is bounded as follows,

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\text{``accept''}|\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{N}\right\}\leq(1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon)^{N}.

(44)

We want this probability to be bounded from above by
\delta>0
, that is

\displaystyle(1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon)^{N}\leq\delta.

(45)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{1}{\ln(1-\nu(\Omega)\epsilon)^{-1}}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\approx\frac{1}{\nu(\Omega)}\times\frac{1}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}.

(46)

This inequality provides a guideline for constructing efficient verification by maximizing
\nu(\Omega)
so that we can use less state copies.

Appendix B
Proof of stabilizer subspace verification

In this section, we prove the two verification strategies of stabilizer subspace.
For the subspace
\mathcal{V}
determined by
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, we define a set of orthogonal bases in
\mathcal{V}
as

\displaystyle\{|{\psi_{1}}\rangle,\cdots,|{\psi_{2^{k}}}\rangle\}.

(47)

The set of stabilizer operators is defined as
\mathcal{S}_{k}
, and the projector onto
\mathcal{V}
can be defined in the following two ways,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}P=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|.

(48)

With Eq. (
27
), we know that a feasible verification strategy
\Omega
must be in the following form:

\displaystyle\Omega=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\sum_{l=1}^{2^{n}-2^{k}}\omega_{l}|{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}|

(49)

where
\{|{\psi_{1}^{\bot}}\rangle,\cdots,|{\psi_{2^{n}-2^{k}}^{\bot}}\rangle\}
is a set of orthogonal bases in complementary subspace of
\mathcal{V}
.
Additionally, the spectral gap of
\Omega
is

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega)=1-\max_{l}\;\omega_{l}.

(50)

B.1
Proof of Strategy I

Now, we begin our proof.
Firstly, we show that
\Omega_{\rm I}
defined is a valid verification strategy and compute the corresponding spectral gap.
For each
P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}
, we have

\displaystyle P=P^{+}-P^{-},\quad P^{+}+P^{-}=\mathbbm{1},

(51)

where
P^{+}\;(P^{-})
is the projector onto the positive (negative) eigenspace of
P
.
With the above decomposition, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}\left(P^{+}-P^{-}\right)+\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\mathbbm{1}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}\left(2P^{+}-\mathbbm{1}\right)+\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\mathbbm{1}

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k-1}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}P^{+}-\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{n-k-1}}\right)\mathbbm{1}.

(54)

Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}P^{+}

\displaystyle=2^{n-k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\left(2^{n-k-1}-1\right)\mathbbm{1}

(55)

\displaystyle=2^{n-k-1}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\left(2^{n-k-1}-1\right)\left(\sum_{j}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\sum_{l}|{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}|\right)

(56)

\displaystyle=(2^{n-k}-1)\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\left(2^{n-k-1}-1\right)\sum_{l}|{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi^{\bot}_{l}}|.

(57)

Finally, we derive the desired equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n-k}-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}P^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k}}|{\psi_{j}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{j}}|+\frac{2^{n-k-1}-1}{2^{n-k}-1}\sum_{l}|{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{l}^{\bot}}|,

(58)

which hints the
\Omega_{\rm I}
defined in Eq. (
7
) is feasible and

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{\rm I})=1-\frac{2^{n-k-1}-1}{2^{n-k}-1}=\frac{2^{n-k-1}}{2^{n-k}-1}.

(59)

Then, we prove that the uniform sample distribution is the optimal one.
Define the probability of measurement
P
to be sampled as
\mu(P)
,
P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}
.
The corresponding verification operator is

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm I},\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}}\mu(P)P^{+}.

(60)

We have
\tr[\Omega_{{\rm I},\mu}]=2^{n-1}
.
With Eq. (
49
), we know that

\displaystyle\tr[\Omega_{{\rm I},\mu}]=2^{k}+\sum_{l}\omega_{l}=2^{n-1}.

(61)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\max_{l}\omega_{l}\geq\frac{2^{n-1}-2^{k}}{2^{n}-2^{k}}=\frac{2^{n-k-1}-1}{2^{n-k}-1},

(62)

the equality holds when
\mu(P)=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}-1}
for all
P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\{\mathbbm{1}\}
, i.e.,
\Omega_{{\rm I},\mu}=\Omega_{\rm I}
.

B.2
Proof of Strategy II

Here, we define a verification strategy with probability distribution
\mu
as

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k}}\mu(S)S^{+},

(63)

i.e., each measurement
S\in\mathcal{G}_{k}
is sampled with probability
\mu(S)
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\tr[\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}\Pi_{V}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k},P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\mu(S)\tr[PS^{+}]

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k},P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\mu(S)\left(\tr[P]+\tr[PS]\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k+1}}\left[\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k},P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\mu(S)\tr[P]+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k},P\in\mathcal{S}_{k}}\tr[PS]\right]

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n-k+1}}\left[2^{n}+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{k}}\mu(S)\cdot 2^{n}\right]

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n+1}}{2^{n-k+1}}=2^{k}=\rank(\Pi_{V}).

(68)

Thus,
\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}
must satisfy perfect completeness condition defined in Lemma
3
.
Subsequently, we analyze the spectral gap of
\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}
.
We define a set of stabilizer generators with size
n
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\underbrace{S_{1},\cdots,S_{n-k}}_{\mathcal{G}_{k}},S_{n-k+1},\cdots,S_{n}\}.

(69)

Then, we can construct a set of orthogonal bases
|{C_{w}}\rangle
with
n
-bit strings
\{\bm{w}\}
, where

\displaystyle|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\!\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{\bm{w}_{j}}S_{j}}{2},\quad\bm{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}.

(70)

Obviously,
|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle
is also a stabilizer state for all
\bm{w}

[
18
]
.
And there is a subset
W\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, for all
\bm{w}\in W
,
|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\in\mathcal{V}
.
In other word, for a fixed
\bm{w}\in W
, the first
n-k
bits of it are all zeros.
Then, we can define arbitrary state in
\mathcal{V}^{\bot}
as

\displaystyle|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{w}|{C_{w}}\rangle,\quad\sum_{\bm{w}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}=1,

(71)

and we have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|S_{i}^{+}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

(72)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\sum_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{\bm{w}}^{*}\alpha_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|S_{i}^{+}|{C_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}}\rangle

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\sum_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{\bm{w}}^{*}\alpha_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\delta_{\bm{w}\bm{w}^{\prime}}\epsilon_{i,\bm{w}^{\prime}}

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\epsilon_{i,\bm{w}},

(75)

where
W^{\bot}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\setminus W
,
S_{i}^{+}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle=\epsilon_{i,\bm{w}}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle
, and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i,\bm{w}}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $i$-th bit of $\bm{w}$ is 0}\\
0\quad\text{else}\end{cases}.

(76)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\Psi^{\bot}}\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{\Psi^{\bot}}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\epsilon_{i,\bm{w}}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu(S_{i})\epsilon_{i,w}

(78)

\displaystyle=1-\min_{S_{i}}\mu(S_{i}),

(79)

with the fact that for the first
k
bits of
\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}
, there are at most
n-k-1
bits equal to
0
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\Psi^{\bot}}\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm II},\mu}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle\geq 1-\frac{1}{n-k},

(80)

the equality holds when
\mu(S_{i})=\frac{1}{n-k}
for all
i\in[n-k]
.
Therefore,
\Omega_{\rm II}
defined in Eq. (
9
) is the optimal one, and we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{\rm II})=\frac{1}{n-k}.

(81)

Appendix C
Proof of graph subspace verification

A graph subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
is defined by a graph
G=(V,E)
and an Abelian group
W=\langle\bm{w}_{1},\cdots,\bm{w}_{k}\rangle
,
such that the dimension of
\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
is
2^{k}
.
The graph subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\mathscr{G}}
has a set of orthogonal bases
\{G_{\bm{w}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\bm{w}\in W\}
, where

\displaystyle|{G_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z^{\bm{w}}|{G}\rangle=\prod_{i}Z_{i}^{w_{i}}|{G}\rangle.

(82)

Each
\bm{w}\in W
represents a logical qubit and connected to the
a
-th physical qubit if
a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{w})
.
In the following, we will demonstrate that the problem of graph subspace verification can be reduced to the problem of coloring a graph with
n-k
vertices.

C.1
The evolution of generators

Recall that a graph
G
with
n
vertices has
n
stabilizer generators.
However, with the introduction of
W
, the number of stabilizer generators is reduced to
n-k
.
This raises the question of which generators will be deleted or modified.

With the constraints imposed by
W
,
the set of generators must take the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathscr{G}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{S^{\bm{y}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\forall\;i\in[k],|{\rm supp}(\bm{y})\cap{\rm supp}(\bm{w}_{i})|\text{ is even }\}

(83)

where

\displaystyle S^{\bm{y}}=\prod_{a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y})}S_{a}.

(84)

Specially, let
\{S^{\bm{0}}\}=\emptyset
.
A set of operator
\{S^{\bm{y}}\}
is said to be independent if
\{\bm{y}\}
is a set of linear independent bases.
We can then divide the stabilizer generators of the graph code
\mathscr{G}
into the following two parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\mathscr{G}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{\overline{W}}\cup\mathcal{G}_{W}.

(85)

The first part,
\mathcal{G}_{\overline{W}}
, can be expressed in two equivalent forms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\overline{W}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}

\displaystyle=\{S^{\bm{y}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\forall\;i\in[k],{\rm supp}(\bm{y})\cap{\rm supp}(\bm{w}_{i})=\emptyset\text{ and }|{\rm supp}(\bm{y})|=1\}

(86)

or

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\overline{W}}=\{S_{a}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a\in\overline{W}\},

(87)

where
\overline{W}=\{a\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a\not\in{\rm supp}(\bm{w}_{i}),\;\forall\;i\in[k]\}
,
representing the set of physical qubits that are not connected to any logical qubits. Therefore, the set
\mathcal{G}_{\overline{W}}
can be directly obtained from
G
and
W
.
Next, we focus on constructing
\mathcal{G}_{W}
, which will allow us to fully determine the set of generators for the graph code
\mathscr{G}
.

C.2
Graph structure of the graph code

In this subsection, we analyze the graph structure of the graph code.
We define a new graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
, where
|V^{\prime}|=n-k
, and each vertex represents a stabilizer generator, as defined in Eq. (
83
).
We now need to determine how to connect these vertices, labeled as
\{\bm{y}\}
.
To clarify our reconnection rules, we first explain the physical meaning of an edge in the graph.

For a graph state
|{G}\rangle
and its corresponding graph
G
,

a
-th and
b
-th qubits are connected in
G
(i.e.,
(a,b)\in E
),
if and only if the corresponding stabilizer generators
S_{a}
and
S_{b}
anti-commute on these two qubits.
Similarly, in the graph
G^{\prime}
,
(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
if and only if
S^{\bm{y}}
and
S^{\bm{y}^{\prime}}
anti-commute on the related qubits. This can be verified as follows:

1.

For
a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y})
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
, define the function

\displaystyle f(a,\bm{y^{\prime}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\{b\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y^{\prime}})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(a,b)\in E\}|.

(98)

This function counts the number of
b\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y^{\prime}})
that are connected to
a
in
G
.

2.

(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})\in E^{\prime}
if one of the following conditions holds:

(a)

\exists\;a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y})\cap{\rm supp}(\bm{y}^{\prime})
such that

is odd;

(b)

\exists\;a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y})
but
\not\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y}^{\prime})
such that
f(a,\bm{y}^{\prime})
is odd;

(c)

\exists\;a\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y}^{\prime})
but
\not\in{\rm supp}(\bm{y})
such that
f(a,\bm{y})
is odd.

Specially, for the
k=1
case, the rules of connection can be summarized as follows.
Define
\delta_{a,b}=1
if
(a,b)\in E
; otherwise
\delta_{a,b}=0
.
The edges in
G^{\prime}
are then determined as follows,

1.

If
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{1})=\{a\}
and
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{2})=\{b\}
,
(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})\in E^{\prime}
if
\delta_{a,b}=1
;

2.

If
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{1})=\{a\}
and
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{2})=\{b,\bar{b}\}
,
(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})\in E^{\prime}
if
\delta_{a,b}=1
or
\delta_{a,\bar{b}}=1
;

3.

If
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{1})=\{a,\bar{a}\}
and
{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{2})=\{b,\bar{b}\}
,
(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})\not\in E^{\prime}
if at least one of the following two conditions holds:

(a)

and
\delta_{a,b}=\delta_{a,\bar{b}}=\delta_{\bar{a},b}=\delta_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}
;

(b)

{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{1})\cup{\rm supp}(\bm{y}_{2})=\{a,b,c\}
and
\delta_{a,b}=\delta_{a,c}=\delta_{b,c}
.

C.3
Coloring strategy

With the above analysis, the graph subspace
\mathcal{V}
can be described using a new graph with
n-k

Inspired by the coloring strategy initially applied to hypergraph states
[
45
]
, we propose a verification strategy for this graph subspace, which we term the
coloring strategy
. This strategy is based on the coloring problem of the graph.
Let
\mathscr{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{m}\}
be an independence cover of
G^{\prime}
, composed of
m
nonempty independent sets, such that

\bigcup_{\ell}A_{\ell}=\{\bm{y}\}
and
A_{\ell}\cap A_{\iota}=\emptyset
for all
\ell\neq\iota
.
For each set
A_{\ell}
, we construct a test operator
P_{\ell}
as follows:

1.

For
\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}
and

,
if
f(a,\bm{y})
is even, perform an
X
measurement on the
a
-th qubit;
otherwise, perform a
Y
measurement on the
a
-th qubit,
where

is defined in Eq. (

).

2.

For other qubits, perform
Z
measurements.

3.

The state passes only if the measurement result of
S^{\bm{y}}
is
+1
for all
\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}
.

Therefore, the corresponding test operator is defined as

\displaystyle P_{\ell}=\prod_{\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S^{\bm{y}}}{2}.

(99)

Suppose the test
P_{\ell}
is performed with probability
\mu_{\ell}
.
Our coloring strategy is characterized by the weighted independence cover
(\mathscr{A},\mu)
.
The corresponding verification operator is given by

\displaystyle\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu)=\sum_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}P_{\ell}.

C.4
Efficiency of the coloring strategy

To analyze the efficiency of the coloring strategy, we first introduce the following lemma, which will be instrumental in the subsequent analysis.

Lemma 4
.

Given a set of stabilizer generators
\{S_{1},\cdots,S_{m}\}
with
[S_{i},S_{j}]=0
for all
i,j\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}+\prod_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}\right)=2\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right).

(101)

Proof.

For
m=1
case, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{1}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{1}\right)=2\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{1}\right).

(102)

Thus, this lemma holds when
m=1
.
Now, assume that this lemma holds when
m=k-1
, we want to prove that it is hold for
m=k
case. Then, for
m=k
case, we have

\displaystyle\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}+\prod_{i=1}^{k}S_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{k}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}+\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}S_{i}\cdot S_{k}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{k}\right)S_{k}\left(S_{k}+\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}S_{i}\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left(S_{k}+\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}S_{i}\right)\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{k}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right)\right]\left[\left(\mathbbm{1}+\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}S_{i}\right)+\left(S_{k}-\mathbbm{1}\right)\right]\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{k}\right)

(106)

\displaystyle=2\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}\right),

(107)

with the fact that
(S_{k}-\mathbbm{1})(\mathbbm{1}+S_{k})=0
.
Therefore, the lemma holds for
m=k
case.
∎

Similar to previous discussions, we first prove the condition for perfect completeness.
For all
\ell\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\tr\left[\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}P_{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\bm{y}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S^{\bm{y}}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S^{\bm{y}}}{2}\right)\right]

(108)

\displaystyle=\tr[\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}]=\rank(\Pi_{\mathcal{V}}),

(109)

where we use Lemma
4
.
Then, we consider the spectral gap of
\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu)
defined in Eq. (
100
).
We define a set of stabilizer generators

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\{\underbrace{K_{1},\cdots,K_{n-k}}_{\text{generators of }\mathscr{G},\{S^{\bm{y}}\}},K_{n-k+1},\cdots,K_{n}\}.

(110)

Based on these generators, we can define a set of orthogonal states
\{|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\}
, where

\displaystyle|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|=\prod_{i}^{n}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{\bm{x}_{i}}K_{i}}{2}.

(111)

The state
|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\!\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|\in\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{V})
if and only if
{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\cap[n-k]=\emptyset
.
Define

\displaystyle A_{\ell}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{i\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S^{\bm{y}}=K_{i},\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}\}\text{ with }\bigcup_{\ell}A_{\ell}^{\prime}=[n-k]\text{ and }A_{\ell}\cap A_{\iota}=\emptyset\text{ for all $\ell\neq\iota$}.

(112)

For a fixed
P_{\ell}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|P_{\ell}|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{\bm{x}_{i}}K_{i}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{y}\in A_{\ell}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S^{\bm{y}}}{2}\right)\right]

(113)

\displaystyle=\tr\left[\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{\bm{x}_{i}}K_{i}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in A_{\ell}^{\prime}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+K_{i}}{2}\right)\right]

(114)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if ${\rm supp}(\bm{x})\subseteq\bar{A}_{\ell}^{\prime}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(115)

where
\bar{A}_{\ell}^{\prime}=[n]\setminus A_{\ell}^{\prime}
.
Therefore, for a fixed
\bm{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu)|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle=\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|\sum_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}P_{\ell}|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle=\sum_{\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\subseteq\bar{A}_{\ell}^{\prime}}\mu_{\ell}.

(116)

Define arbitrary pure state in
\mathcal{V}^{\bot}
as

\displaystyle|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\cap[n-k]\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{\bm{x}}|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle,\quad\sum|\alpha_{\bm{x}}|^{2}=1,

(117)

then the spectral gap can be represented as

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu))

\displaystyle=1-\max_{|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle}\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu)|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

(118)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{\{\alpha_{\bm{x}}\}}\left(\sum_{\bm{x}}|\alpha_{\bm{x}}|^{2}\langle{G_{\bm{x}}}|\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu)|{G_{\bm{x}}}\rangle\right)

(119)

\displaystyle=1-\max_{\bm{x}}\left(\sum_{\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\subseteq\bar{A}_{\ell}}\mu_{\ell}\right).

(120)

For the above maximization problem, it suffices to consider the case in which
|{\rm supp}(\bm{x})\cap[n-k]|=1
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu))=1-\max_{\ell}\left(1-\mu_{\ell}\right)=\min_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}.

(121)

With the previous analysis, we want to maximize the spectral gap,

\displaystyle\max_{\mu}\nu(\Omega(\mathscr{A},\mu))=\max_{\mu}\min_{\ell}\mu_{\ell}=\frac{1}{m},

(122)

when
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\cdots=\mu_{m}=\frac{1}{m}
.
Therefore, we can define

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathscr{G}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell}P_{\ell},\quad\nu(\Omega_{\mathscr{G}})=\frac{1}{m}.

(123)

Appendix D
Proof of CSS subspace verification

A CSS code can be described by two matrices

H_{X}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k_{X}\times n}
and
H_{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{k_{Z}\times n}
,
where
H_{X}H_{Z}^{T}=\bm{0}

[
50
]
.
There are
k_{X}+k_{Z}
stabilizer generators:
X^{\bm{c}_{X}}
and
Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}
,
where
P^{\bm{c}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}P^{c_{i}}
,

\bm{c}_{X}
and
\bm{c_{Z}}
are the rows of
H_{X}
and
H_{Z}
respectively.
Such a CSS code can determine a CSS subspace
\mathcal{V}_{\rm CSS}
with dimension
2^{k}
with
k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=n-(k_{X}+k_{Z})
, which projector is

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm CSS}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}\right).

(136)

The corresponding verification operator is defined as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}+\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}\right).

(137)

Firstly, we prove the perfect completeness condition defined in Lemma
3
as follows,

\displaystyle\tr[\Pi_{\rm CSS}\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}+\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}\right)\right]

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tr\left[\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}\right)\left(\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}\right)\right]=\rank(\Pi_{\rm CSS}).

(139)

Then, we analyze the spectral gap of
\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}
.
We define a set of stabilizer generators with size
n
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}=\{\underbrace{S_{1},\cdots,S_{k_{X}}}_{\{X^{\bm{c}_{X}}\}_{\bm{c}_{X}}},\underbrace{S_{k_{X}+1},\cdots,S_{n-k}}_{\{Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}\}_{\bm{c}_{Z}}},S_{n-k+1},\cdots,S_{n}\}.

(140)

Then, we can define a set of orthogonal bases
\{|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\}_{\bm{w}}
, where

\displaystyle|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\!\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{w_{j}}S_{j}}{2},\quad\bm{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}.

(141)

And there is a subset
W\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
, for all
\bm{w}\in W
,
|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle\in\mathcal{V}_{\rm CSS}
.
In other word, for a fixed
\bm{w}\in W
, the first
k
bits of it are all zeros.
Then, we can define arbitrary state in
\mathcal{V}^{\bot}_{\rm CSS}
as

\displaystyle|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle=\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{w}|{C_{w}}\rangle,\quad\sum_{\bm{w}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}=1,

(142)

where
W^{\bot}=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\setminus W
, and we have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{\bm{w}}^{*}\alpha_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}|{C_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}}\rangle

(143)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{\bm{w}}^{*}\alpha_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\left(\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|\prod_{i=1}^{k_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}}{2}|{C_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}}\rangle+\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|\prod_{i=k_{X}+1}^{n-k}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}}{2}|{C_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}}\rangle\right)

(144)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left(\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}\right),

(145)

where

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}}{2}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle,\quad\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $\bm{w}_{i}=0$ for all $i=1,\cdots,k_{X}$}\\
0\quad\text{else}\end{cases},

(146)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=k_{X}+1}^{k}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}}{2}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle,\quad\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $\bm{w}_{i}=0$ for all $i=k_{X}+1,\cdots,n-k$}\\
0\quad\text{else}\end{cases}.

(147)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left(\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2},

(148)

with the fact that for all
\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}
,
\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}\leq 1
.
Additionally, note that the above equality is achievable.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ})=1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}.

(149)

Then, we prove that the uniform distribution is the optimal one as follows.
We define that

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm CSS},\mu}^{\rm XZ}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu(X)\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}+\mu(Z)\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}.

(150)

We have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm CSS},\mu}^{\rm XZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left[\mu(X)\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\mu(Z)\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}\right]

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\mu(X),\mu(Z)\}.

(152)

The above equality is achievable.
With the definition of spectral gap, we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{{\rm CSS},\mu}^{\rm XZ})

\displaystyle=1-\max_{|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle}\;\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm CSS},\mu}^{\rm XZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

(153)

\displaystyle=1-\max\{\mu(X),\mu(Z)\}.

(154)

Therefore, we obtain the optimal performance of verification strategy when
\mu(X)=\mu(Z)=1/2
, i.e., sampling uniformly.

D.1
Example: toric subspace verification

A typical CSS code is the
toric code
, which is described by a
L\times L
torus and each edge represents a qubit
[
28
]
.
The corresponding stabilizer generators can be divided into two groups:

1.

those associated with each lattice vertex
v
, with
X
acting on every qubit associated with an edge attached to the given vertex;

2.

those associated with each plaquette
p
of the lattice, with
Z
acting on each qubit represented by an edge surrounding the plaquette.

Mathematically, they can be written as

\displaystyle S_{v}=\prod_{i\in v}X_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{p}=\prod_{i\in p}Z_{i}.

(155)

The corresponding subspace is termed as
toric subspace

\mathcal{V}_{\rm Toric}
.
To verify this subspace, we can directly apply
\Omega_{\rm CSS}^{\rm XZ}

using
2
measurement settings and consuming
2/\epsilon\ln(1/\delta)
state copies.
Concretely, the verification operator reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm Toric}^{\rm XZ}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\prod_{v}S_{v}^{+}+\prod_{p}S_{p}^{+}\right),

(156)

where
S_{v}^{+}
(
S_{p}^{+}
) is the projector onto the positive eigenspace of stabilizer generator
S_{v}
(
S_{p}
).

D.2
Proof of the dual-containing subspace verification

Here, we consider the dual-containing code,
which is a special CSS code with
H_{X}=H_{Z}
.
We label the corresponding dual-containing subspace as
\mathcal{V}_{\rm DC}
with the projector

\displaystyle\Pi_{\rm DC}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{\bm{c}}\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}\right),

(157)

where
\bm{c}
are the rows of
H_{X}
.
The verification operator reads

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\prod_{\bm{c}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}}{2}+\prod_{\bm{c}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}+\prod_{\bm{c}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}\right).

(158)

With Lemma
4
, we have

\displaystyle\tr\left[\prod_{\bm{c}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}\prod_{\bm{c}}\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\rank(\Pi_{\rm DC})

(159)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\tr[\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ}\Pi_{\rm DC}]

\displaystyle=\rank(\Pi_{\rm DC}).

(160)

Then, we compute the spectral gap as follows.
Similarly, we can also define define arbitrary state in
\mathcal{V}^{\bot}_{\rm DC}
as
|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle
and have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime}\in W^{\bot}}\alpha_{\bm{w}}^{*}\alpha_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}\langle{C_{\bm{w}}}|\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ}|{C_{\bm{w}^{\prime}}}\rangle

(161)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left(\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}\right),

(162)

where

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+S_{i}S_{i+k_{X}}}{2}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}|{C_{\bm{w}}}\rangle,\quad\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{w}_{i+k_{X}}$ for all $i=1,\cdots,k_{X}$}\\
0\quad\text{else}\end{cases}.

(163)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left(\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}\right)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3},

(164)

with the fact that for all
\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}
,
\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}+\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}\leq 1
.
Additionally, note that the above equality is achievable.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{\rm DC}^{\rm XYZ})=1-\frac{1}{3}=\frac{2}{3}.

(165)

Then, we can also prove that the uniform distribution is the optimal one as follows.
We define that

\displaystyle\Omega_{{\rm DC},\mu}^{\rm XYZ}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu(X)\prod_{\bm{c}_{X}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}_{X}}}{2}+\mu(Z)\prod_{\bm{c}_{Z}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z^{\bm{c}_{Z}}}{2}+\mu(Y)\prod_{\bm{c}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X^{\bm{c}}Z^{\bm{c}}}{2}.

(166)

We have

\displaystyle\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm DC},\mu}^{\rm XYZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{w}\in W^{\bot}}|\alpha_{\bm{w}}|^{2}\left[\mu(X)\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{x}+\mu(Z)\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{z}+\mu(Y)\epsilon_{\bm{w}}^{y}\right]

(167)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\mu(X),\mu(Z),\mu(Y)\}.

(168)

The above equality is achievable.
With the definition of spectral gap, we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega_{{\rm DC},\mu}^{\rm XYZ})

\displaystyle=1-\max_{|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle}\;\langle{\Psi^{\bot}}|\Omega_{{\rm DC},\mu}^{\rm XYZ}|{\Psi^{\bot}}\rangle

(169)

\displaystyle=1-\max\{\mu(X),\mu(Z),\mu(Y)\}.

(170)

Therefore, we obtain the optimal performance of verification strategy when
\mu(X)=\mu(Z)=\mu(Y)=1/3
, i.e., sampling uniformly.
