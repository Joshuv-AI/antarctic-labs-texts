Fractional Diffusion limit of a kinetic equation with Diffusive boundary conditions in a bounded interval
L. Cesbron, A. Mellet, M. Puel

Abstract
We investigate the fractional diffusion approximation of a kinetic equation set in a bounded interval with diffusive reflection conditions at the boundary. In an appropriate singular limit corresponding to small Knudsen number and long time asymptotic, we show that the asymptotic density function is the unique solution of a fractional diffusion equation with Neumann boundary condition. This analysis completes a previous work by the same authors in which a limiting fractional diffusion equation was identified on the half-space, but the uniqueness of the solution (which is necessary to prove the convergence of the whole sequence) could not be established.

Introduction

1.1
The linear Boltzmann equation with diffusive boundary conditions

In this paper, we investigate the fractional diffusion approximation of a linear kinetic equation set on a bounded domain with diffusive boundary conditions in dimension
1
.
Our starting point is the following kinetic equation, which models the evolution of a particle distribution function
f(t,x,v)\geq 0
depending on the time
t>0
, the position
x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
and the velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}
:

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+v\,\partial_{x}f=\nu_{0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,w)\,dw\,F(v)-f\right)&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\\[8.0pt]
f(0,x,v)=f_{in}(x,v)&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1)

The left hand side of (
1
) models the free transport of particles, whereas the operator in the right hand side models the diffusive and mass preserving interactions between the particles and the background.
For simplicity, we consider here the linear Boltzmann operator with constant collision frequency
\nu_{0}>0
and equilibrium function
F(v)
.
Importantly, the function
F(v)
is taken to be a given heavy-tail distribution function satisfying, for some
s\in(1/2,1)
and
\gamma>0
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &F\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\int F(v)\,{\rm{d}}v=1,\quad F(v)=F(|v|)\geq 0\\
&\Big|F(v)-\frac{\gamma}{|v|^{1+2s}}\Big|\leq\frac{C}{|v|^{1+4s}}\qquad\mbox{ for all }|v|\geq 1.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

Importantly, we consider here the case where
\Omega
is a bounded interval and we take (without loss of generality)
\Omega=(0,1)
. We denote
\Gamma_{\pm}=\{(x,v)\in\partial\Omega\,;\,\pm n(x)\cdot v>0\}
(note that
\partial\Omega=\{0,1\}
and
n(0)=-1
,
n(1)=1
)
and define the traces
\gamma_{\pm}f=f|_{\Gamma_{\pm}}
.
With these notations, we consider the following diffusive reflection conditions on
\partial\Omega
:

\gamma_{-}f(t,x,v)=\mathcal{B}[\gamma_{+}f](t,x,v)\qquad\forall(x,v)\in\Sigma_{-}

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}
is the following scattering operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[\gamma_{+}f^{\varepsilon}](t,x,v)=c_{0}F(v)\int_{w\cdot n(x)>0}\gamma_{+}f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)|w\cdot n(x)|\,{\rm{d}}w

(4)

with
c_{0}
the normalizing constant:

\displaystyle c_{0}:=\left(\int_{w\cdot n(x)>0}F(w)|w\cdot n(x)|\,{\rm{d}}w\right)^{-1}.

(5)

The use of diffusive reflection conditions at the boundary is classical in kinetic theory. We are assuming that the
boundary operator
\mathcal{B}
involves the same equilibrium function
F
as the bulk collision operator in order to avoid the need of boundary layer analysis. Note that we consider
s>1/2
in order for the constant
c_{0}
to be well-defined.

The diffusion approximation of such an equation is obtained by investigating the long time, small mean-free-path asymptotic behavior of
f
. To this end we introduce the Knudsen number
\varepsilon\ll 1
and the following rescaling of (
1
)-(
3
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\varepsilon^{2s}\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon v\partial_{x}f^{\varepsilon}=\nu_{0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)\,dwF(v)-f^{\varepsilon}\right)&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\\
&f^{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)=f_{in}(x,v)&\mbox{ in }\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\\[5.0pt]
&\gamma_{-}f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\mathcal{B}[\gamma_{+}f^{\varepsilon}](t,x,v)&\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Gamma_{-}\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

We see that the particular choice of power of
\varepsilon
in front of the time derivative in (
6
) depends on the equilibrium
F
. When
\Omega
is the whole line
\mathbb{R}
it has been proved (see for instance
[
10
,
8
,
2
,
3
]
and references therein) that as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
,
f^{\varepsilon}
converges to a function
f^{0}(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)F(v)

where
\rho(t,x)
is the weak solution of a fractional diffusion equation
\partial_{t}\rho+\kappa\big(-\Delta\big)^{s}\rho=0
.

There is now a very significant literature devoted to the fractional diffusion approximation of kinetic equations. But the role of boundary conditions in these limits has only recently started to be investigated.
The case of Dirichlet boundary condition was studied in
[
1
]

and the case of specular reflection conditions was investigated by the first author in
[
5
,
6
]
.
In
[
7
]
, we considered the case of diffusive reflection conditions (
3
)
in dimension
n\geq 1
when
\Omega
is the half space
\{x_{n}>0\}
.
However, while this previous work clearly identified the limiting Neumann fractional diffusion equation in
\Omega
(see Section
1.3
below), we did not prove that the limiting density was the unique weak solution of that equation (given, for instance, by Hille-Yoshida’s theorem). We only established that it satisfies the equation in a weaker sense, for which uniqueness is not clear. As a result, we also did not prove the convergence of the whole sequence
f^{\varepsilon}
.

The goal of this paper is to fill this gap in the simpler one-dimensional framework by proving that the limiting density is the unique weak solution of a Neumann fractional diffusion equation.
We achieve this by sharpening the assumptions on the test functions used to derive the limiting equation.
In addition, this paper provides the first result of this type in a bounded domain.
Finally, we point out that while we focus here on the one-dimensional case, the proofs provide a roadmap for handling this problem in higher dimensions and in general convex domains.

1.2
Weak solutions of (
6
)

We now recall the standard definition of weak solutions for the kinetic equation with diffuse boundary condition.
First, we note that for any test function
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R})
, smooth solutions of (
6
) satisfy:

\displaystyle-\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v-\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f_{in}(x,v)\phi(0,x,v)\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\varepsilon^{1-2s}\iint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Gamma_{+}}\gamma_{+}f^{\varepsilon}\left(\gamma_{+}\phi-\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi]\right)|v\cdot n(x)|\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}\sigma(x)\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\varepsilon^{-2s}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\big[f^{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon v\partial_{x}\phi-\nu_{0}\phi\right)+\nu_{0}\rho^{\varepsilon}F(v)\phi\big]\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi](t,x)=c_{0}\int_{w\cdot n(x)<0}\gamma_{-}\phi(t,x,w)F(w)|w\cdot n(x)|\,{\rm{d}}w.

(7)

and
\rho^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\,{\rm{d}}v
. Note that
\mathcal{B}^{*}
does not depend on
v

because of the simple form of diffuse reflection operator we consider here (constant cross-section).
We then have:

Definition 1.1
.

We say that
f^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
is a weak solution to (
6
) if for every test function
\phi
such that
\phi
,
\partial_{t}\phi
and
v\partial_{x}\phi
are in
L^{2}_{F}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
and
\phi
satisfies the dual boundary condition

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}\phi=\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi]\quad\mbox{ on }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}

we have

\displaystyle\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v+\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f_{in}(x,v)\phi(0,x,v)\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

(8)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{-2s}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\big[f^{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon v\partial_{x}\phi-\nu_{0}\phi\right)+\nu_{0}\rho^{\varepsilon}F(v)\phi\big]\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

Here and in the rest of the paper, we used the notation

L^{2}_{F^{-1}}((0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N})=\left\{f(t,x,v)\,;\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|f(t,x,v)|^{2}\frac{1}{F(v)}\,dv\,dx\,dt<\infty\right\}

and a similar definition for
L^{2}_{F}((0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

The existence of a weak solution in the sense of this definition is discussed, for instance, in
[
4
,
9
]
.

1.3
The asymptotic diffusion equation

In this section, we recall previous results (in particular our result of
[
7
]
) and introduce the asymptotic model.

As already mentioned above, it is now classical that when
\Omega
is the whole line
\mathbb{R}
(or more generally
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
),
f^{\varepsilon}
converges to a function

\rho(t,x)F(v)

where
\rho(t,x)
is the weak solution of a fractional diffusion equation
\partial_{t}\rho+\kappa\big(-\Delta\big)^{s}\rho=0
.
When
\Omega
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the diffusion equation must be supplemented by boundary condition. Studying the asymptotic limit of this kinetic equation provides us with the framework to find out physically relevant boundary conditions for fractional diffusion equations. We recall that in the classical diffusion approximation (e.g. when
F
is a Maxwellian distribution) the limiting equation is the diffusion equation with Neumann boundary conditions.

In
[
7
]
, we study the problem (
6
) in dimension
n\geq 1
when
\Omega
is the upper half plane. We show that the asymptotic operator (which we denote by
(-\Delta)_{N}
since it corresponds to Neumann boundary conditions) is given by

(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u(x):=-\frac{c_{n,s}}{2s}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(y)\cdot\frac{y-x}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,

with
c_{n,s}=\frac{2^{2s}\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+s\right)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma\left(-s\right)|}
(the constant is chosen here so that when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we recover
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}=(-\Delta)^{s}
)
which can also be written in divergence form as

(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u(x)=-\mathrm{div}\,D_{N}^{2s-1}[u],\qquad D_{N}^{2s-1}[u](x):=\frac{c_{n,s}}{2s(2s-1)}\int_{\Omega}(y-x)\cdot\nabla u(y)\frac{y-x}{|y-x|^{n+2s}}\,dy.

With these notations, the main result of
[
7
]
is:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
7
]
,Theorem 1.1)
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (
2
) with
s\in(1/2,1)
and let
\Omega
be the upper half space

\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,;\,x_{n}>0\}.

Assume that
f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
is a weak solution of (
6
) in
(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

There exists a subsequence
f^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
which converges weakly in
L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}))
to the function
\rho(t,x)F(v)
where
\rho(t,x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega}

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\Big(\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)+\kappa(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi](t,x)\Big)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x+\int_{\Omega}\rho_{in}(x)\psi(0,x)\,{\rm{d}}x=0

(9)

for all test function
\psi\in C^{1}(0,\infty;H^{2}(\Omega))
, such that
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi]\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)
and
D^{2s-1}[\psi]\cdot n=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

By using the integration by parts formula (see Proposition 3.4 in
[
7
]
):

\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,D^{2s-1}[\varphi]\psi\,{\rm{d}}x-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,\mathrm{div}\,D^{2s-1}[\psi]\,{\rm{d}}x=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\psi D^{2s-1}[\varphi]\cdot n-\varphi D^{2s-1}[\psi]\cdot n\right]\,{\rm{d}}S(x)

(10)

we see that (
9
) is a natural weak formulation for the parabolic boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\rho-\kappa\,\mathrm{div}\,D_{N}^{2s-1}[\rho]=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega\\
&D_{N}^{2s-1}[\rho]\cdot n=0\qquad\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega\\
&\rho(0,x)=\rho_{in}(x)\qquad\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

Using Hille-Yoshida’s theorem, we prove in
[
7
]
that (
11
) is well posed:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
7
]
,Theorem 1.2)
.

For all
\rho_{in}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\kappa>0
, the evolution problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}\rho-\kappa\,\mathrm{div}\,D_{N}^{2s-1}[\rho]=0&\mbox{ in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{in}(x)&\mbox{ in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(12)

has a unique solution
\rho\in C^{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}((0,\infty);D((-\Delta)_{N}^{s}))
where

D((-\Delta)_{N}^{s})=\{u\in H^{s}(\Omega)\,;\,(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\quad D^{2s-1}[u]\cdot n=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\}.

We recall that the space
H^{s}(\Omega)
is defined by

H^{s}(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,;\,\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}y<\infty\right\}

and is equipped with the norm:

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}=\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\,{\rm{d}}x+\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}y.

Unfortunately, it is not clear that the characterization of
\rho(t,x)
given by Theorem

implies that
\rho
is the unique solution of (
1.2
)
provided by Theorem
1.2
.
Indeed, while we can show that the solution of Theorem

satisfies (

) as in Theorem

, it does not appear that this formulation is strong enough to yield uniqueness.
The problem is that the condition

The aim of the present paper is to show (in dimension

can be proved for a different set of test function

1.4
Main results of the paper

To state our main result, we introduce the space of test function (for
\beta>0
):

X^{\beta}=\left\{\psi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty);C^{s}(\overline{\Omega}))\,;\,(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}))\right\}

(we do not indicate the dependence of these spaces on

since

is fixed throughout the paper).
We can now state the main theorem of this paper:

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (
2
) with
s\in(1/2,1)
and assume that the initial condition satisfies, for some constant
C\geq 0
:

0\leq f_{in}(x,v)\leq CF(v),\qquad f_{in}\in L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R})).

Let
f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
be a weak solution of (
6
) in
(0,\infty)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}

in the sense of Definition

.
Then the function
f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
converges weakly in
L^{\infty}((0,\infty);L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}))
, as

goes to
0
, to the function
\rho(t,x)F(v)
where
\rho(t,x)
is the unique function satisfying

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega}{\iint}

\displaystyle\rho(t,x)\Big(\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)-\kappa(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi](t,x)\Big)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x+\underset{\Omega}{\int}\rho_{in}(x)\psi(0,x)\,{\rm{d}}x=0

(14)

for all test function
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
for some
\beta>0
and
\kappa=c_{1,s}^{-1}\gamma\nu_{0}^{1-2s}\Gamma(2s+1)
.

Importantly, the uniqueness of the limiting density

is a new result, which implies that the whole sequence

(and not just a subsequence) converges.

because we required stronger conditions on the test function

).

Proposition 1.1
.

Given
\beta>0
and for all
\rho_{in}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique function
\rho(t,x)\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))

for all test function

This solution is also the unique weak solution of (

and therefore satisfies

\rho\in C^{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}((0,\infty);D((-\Delta)_{N}^{s}))

In the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we make use of the fact that the condition
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\psi\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega}))
– which is a natural condition to get the uniqueness of Proposition

) (see in particular the proof of Lemma

).

While we believe that these Hölder regularity estimates hold in any dimension, we focus on this paper on the one-dimensional case because, as explained below, the operator
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}
can be written in term of the usual fractional Laplace operator in one dimension, and existing regularity theory
[
11
]
can then be used.
Extending our result to higher dimension would require the development of a regularity theory for the Neumann boundary value problem (
13
) in higher dimension.

We conclude this section by explaining what makes the one dimensional case so much nicer to work with:
Given a (continuous) function
u
defined in
\overline{\Omega}
, we introduce the continuous extension of
u
by constant:

\widetilde{u}(x)=\begin{cases}u(0)&\mbox{ if }x\leq 0\\
u(x)&\mbox{ if }0\leq x\leq 1\\
u(1)&\mbox{ if }x\geq 1.\end{cases}

(15)

We then have:

\displaystyle(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{1,s}}{2s}\int_{\Omega}u^{\prime}(y)\frac{y-x}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{c_{1,s}}{2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{u}^{\prime}(y)\frac{y-x}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,dy

\displaystyle=c_{1,s}P.V.\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\widetilde{u}(x)-\widetilde{u}(y)}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}y.

that is

(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u(x)=(-\Delta)^{s}\widetilde{u}(x)\qquad\qquad\mbox{ for all $x\in\Omega$}.

(16)

In particular, we note that if

is the solution of (

) provided by Theorem

satisfies

\begin{cases}\lambda\widetilde{u}+(-\Delta)^{s}\widetilde{u}=g&\mbox{ in }\Omega=(0,1)\\

and the regularity theory for the fractional Dirichlet boundary value problem developed for example in

can be used to study the regularity of

.
When

, this gives

.

Note finally that we can also write

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{1,s}}{2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}[\widetilde{u}(y)-u(x)]\frac{y-x}{|y-x|^{1+2s}}\,dy.

(17)

15
). Then the function

\displaystyle\phi(x,v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\widetilde{\psi}(x+\varepsilon zv)\,dz

solves (
19
). Furthermore,
\phi
satisfies
\gamma_{+}\phi(x,v)=\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi](x)
for
(x,v)\in\Gamma_{+}
(and thus solves (
18
)) if and only if

D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](0)=D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](1)=0

(20)

where the operator
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}
is defined by (
21
) below.

Proof.

We easily check that
\phi(x,v)
given by Proposition
2.1
solves (
19
).
Indeed, we have:

\varepsilon v\partial_{x}\phi=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\frac{d}{dz}\left[\widetilde{\psi}(x+\varepsilon zv)\right]\,dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}^{2}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\widetilde{\psi}(x+\varepsilon zv)\,dz-\nu^{0}\psi(x)=\nu_{0}(\phi-\psi)

and if
(x,v)\in\Gamma_{+}
, for instance if
x=0
and
v<0
, then

\phi(0,v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\widetilde{\psi}(\varepsilon zv)\,dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\psi(0)\,dz=\psi(0).

Next,
we note that
\phi
satisfies
\gamma_{+}\phi(x,v)=\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi](x)
on
\Gamma_{+}

if and only if
\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}(\phi-\psi)]=0
on
\Gamma_{+}
, which, using (
7
) and the fact that
\phi(x,v)-\psi(x)=0
on
\Gamma_{+}
, is equivalent to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}vF(v)[\phi(x,v)-\psi(x)]\,dv=0\qquad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

The result then follows by introducing the operator

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{1-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}vF(v)[\phi(x,v)-\psi(x)]\,dv

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{1-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}vF(v)[\widetilde{\psi}(x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi(x)]\,dz\,dv.

(21)

∎

Since we want to use the function
\phi(x,v)
as a test function in (
8
), we need
\phi
to satisfy the condition
\gamma_{+}\phi(x,v)=\mathcal{B}^{*}[\gamma_{-}\phi](x)
.
We cannot require a given function
\psi
to satisfy (
20
) since this condition depends on
\varepsilon
.
But we can approximate a given test function
\psi

by a function
\psi^{\varepsilon}
satisfying (
20
).
To that end, we consider a smooth function
\chi
satisfying

\displaystyle\chi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad\chi(0)=1,\quad\mathrm{supp}\,(\chi)\subset[0,1/2),\quad 0\leq\chi(x)\leq 1

(22)

(these conditions guarantee that
D_{N}^{2s-1}[\chi](0)\neq D_{N}^{2s-1}[\chi](1)
).
We then have

Proposition 2.2
.

Given
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(\overline{\Omega})
we defined
\psi^{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle\psi^{\varepsilon}(x)=\psi(x)+\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}\chi(x)+\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}\chi(1-x)\qquad\forall x\in\Omega

(23)

with

\left\{\begin{aligned} \lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\frac{-D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](0)D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](0)+D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](1)D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](1)}{(D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](0))^{2}-(D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](1))^{2}},\\[8.0pt]
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}=\frac{-D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](0)D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](1)+D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](1)D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi](0)}{(D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](0))^{2}-(D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](1))^{2}}\end{aligned}\right.

(24)

where
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}
is defined by (
21
). Then

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi_{\varepsilon}](0)=D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi_{\varepsilon}](1)=0

(25)

Proof.

We note that

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi(1-x)](0)

\displaystyle=D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\chi](1)

so the linearity of the operator
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}
and the choice of
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}
and
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}
implies
(
25
).
Note that we will prove later that
D_{\varepsilon}^{2s-1}[\chi](x)
converges to
\gamma_{0}\frac{2s}{c_{1,s}}D_{N}^{2s-1}[\chi](x)
(see Lemma
2.2
). So the fact that
D_{N}^{2s-1}[\chi](0)\neq D_{N}^{2s-1}[\chi](1)
and Lemma
2.2
imply that the denominator in (
24
) does not vanish for
\varepsilon
small enough.

∎

We now have all the tools needed to set up the proof of our main result:
for a given test function
\psi(t,x)
in
\mathcal{D}([0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
, we consider
\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)
given by Proposition
2.2
. Then the function

\phi^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\widetilde{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)\,dz

(26)

solves (
18
) (see Proposition
2.1
) and by taking
\phi^{\varepsilon}
as a test function in
(
8
), we find:

\displaystyle\iiint_{Q}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v+\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f_{in}(x,v)\phi^{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\varepsilon^{-2s}\iiint_{Q}\big[f^{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon v\partial_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}-\nu_{0}\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)+\nu_{0}\rho_{\varepsilon}F(v)\phi^{\varepsilon}\big]\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\varepsilon^{-2s}\iiint_{Q}\big[-\nu_{0}f^{\varepsilon}\psi^{\varepsilon}+\nu_{0}\rho_{\varepsilon}F(v)\phi^{\varepsilon}\big]\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\varepsilon^{-2s}\iiint_{Q}\rho_{\varepsilon}F(v)\nu_{0}\big[\phi^{\varepsilon}-\psi^{\varepsilon}\big]\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}](x)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x.

(27)

where we used the fact that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}\,dv=\rho^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{\varepsilon}F(v)\,dv

and we defined the following operator (for any test function
\psi
and with
\phi
defined by Proposition
2.1
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle:=\varepsilon^{-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu_{0}F(v)\big[\phi(x,v)-\psi(x)\big]\,dv

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}^{2}e^{-\nu_{0}z}F(v)[\widetilde{\psi}(x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi(x)]\,dz\,dv.

(28)

The proof of Theorem
1.4
now consists in passing to the limit in (
27
), which requires, in particular, to show that for appropriate
\psi
, the function
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}]
converges (strongly in
L^{1}
) to
\kappa(-\Delta)^{s}[\widetilde{\psi}]=\kappa(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}[\psi]
.

In the section below, we first derive simpler formulas for
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}
and
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}
. These formulas will then be used to prove the needed convergence results.

2.2
Reformulation of the operators
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}
and
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}

After a simple change of variable, we find the following formula for the operator
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi]
, defined by (
28
):

\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)=\int_{\Omega}F_{1}^{\varepsilon}(y-x)[\widetilde{\psi}(y)-\psi(x)]dy

with

F_{1}(v)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{2}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{-1}F\left(\tau^{-1}v\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau,\quad F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1+2s}}F_{1}\left(\frac{v}{\varepsilon}\right).

(29)

Similarly, the operator
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi]
introduced in (
21
) can be written as:

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}[\psi]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(y-x)F_{0}^{\varepsilon}(y-x)[\widetilde{\psi}(y)-\psi(x)]\,dy

(30)

with

F_{0}(v)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{-2}F\left(\tau^{-1}v\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau,\quad F^{\varepsilon}_{0}(v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1+2s}}F_{0}\left(\frac{v}{\varepsilon}\right).

(31)

The introduction of the functions
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
allow us to eliminate the variable
z
from the definition of
D^{2s-1}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}
.
Of course, their behavior for large
v
is related to that of
F
. More precisely, we have the following Lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that the distributions
F_{0}
and
F_{1}
given by (
31
) and (
29
) satisfy

\displaystyle F_{0}(z)\leq C\min\left(\frac{1}{|z|^{1+2s}},\frac{1}{|z|}\right),\qquad F_{1}(z)\leq C\min\left(\frac{1}{|z|^{1+2s}},|\ln(z)|\right),\mbox{ for all $z\in\mathbb{R}$}

and

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}(z)-\frac{\gamma_{i}}{|z|^{1+2s}}\right|\leq\frac{C}{|z|^{1+4s}}\qquad\mbox{ for all }|z|\geq 1

where
\gamma_{i}=\gamma\nu_{0}^{1-2s}\Gamma(2s+i)
, with
\gamma
the constant of
F
in (
2
).

Proof.

We first note that, for
i=0,1
we can write
F_{i}
as

\displaystyle F_{i}(z)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}F\left(\frac{z}{\tau}\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau.

For the first estimate, using (
2
) we write on the one hand

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{z}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}F\left(\frac{z}{\tau}\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{z}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}\left(\frac{\tau^{1+2s}}{|z|^{1+2s}}+\frac{\tau^{1+4s}}{|z|^{1+4s}}\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{|z|^{1+2s}}\min\left(1,|z|^{2s+i}\right)+\frac{C}{|z|^{1+4s}}\min\left(1,|z|^{4s+i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\min\left(\frac{1}{|z|^{1+2s}},\frac{1}{|z|^{1-i}}\right)

and on the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}F\left(\frac{z}{\tau}\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}\,{\rm{d}}\tau

with

\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}\,{\rm{d}}\tau=\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{-2}\,{\rm{d}}\tau\leq C\max\left(e^{-\nu_{0}z},\frac{1}{z}\right)\leq\frac{C}{z}\quad\mbox{ for $i=0$}

\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}\,{\rm{d}}\tau=\int_{z}^{+\infty}\nu_{0}^{2}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{-1}\,{\rm{d}}\tau\leq C\max\left(e^{-\nu_{0}z},\ln(z)\right)\leq C\ln(z)\quad\mbox{ for $i=1$}

The first estimates follow.

To prove the second estimates, we use the formula
\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}\tau^{2s+i-1}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\,{\rm{d}}\tau=\nu_{0}^{1-2s}\Gamma(2s+i)
to get:

\displaystyle\left|F_{i}(z)-\frac{\gamma_{i}}{|z|^{1+2s}}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\left|\tau^{i-2}F\left(\frac{z}{\tau}\right)-\frac{\gamma\tau^{2s+i-1}}{|z|^{1+2s}}\right|\,{\rm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\leq\int_{0}^{z}\nu_{0}^{1+i}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{4s+i-1}\frac{C}{|z|^{1+4s}}\,{\rm{d}}\tau+\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\nu_{0}^{1+i}\left(\tau^{i-2}\|F\|_{L^{\infty}}+\frac{\gamma\tau^{2s+i-1}}{|z|^{1+2s}}\right)\,{\rm{d}}\tau

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\leq\frac{C}{|z|^{1+4s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{4s+i-1}\,{\rm{d}}\tau+C\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{i-2}\,{\rm{d}}\tau+\frac{C}{|z|^{1+2s}}\int_{z}^{\infty}e^{-\nu_{0}\tau}\tau^{2s+i-1}\,{\rm{d}}\tau

and the result follows.

∎

2.3
Convergence of the operator
D_{\varepsilon}^{2s-1}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}

In order to pass to the limit in (
27
), we need to show that
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}]
converges strongly in
L^{1}
when
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
. The key result of this section is the following proposition:

Proposition 2.3
.

Given
\psi\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})

such that
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi]\in C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
\beta>0
and satisfying
D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi]=0
on
\partial\Omega
, let
\psi^{\varepsilon}
be defined as in (
23
).
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}]\to\mathcal{L}[\psi]:=-\kappa(-\Delta)^{s}[\widetilde{\psi}]\mbox{ strongly in }L^{1}(\Omega)

with
\kappa=c_{1,s}^{-1}\gamma_{1}
.

This result implies in particular the convergence of
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)]
for all
t
whenever
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
.
Its proof will follow from the following two lemmas:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\psi\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\alpha>2s-1
, then

\displaystyle D_{\varepsilon}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)\rightarrow\gamma_{0}\frac{2s}{c_{1,s}}D_{N}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)\mbox{ uniformly in }\overline{\Omega}.

(32)

In particular, if
\psi
satisfies
D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi]=0
on
\partial\Omega
then the constants defined by (
24
) satisfy

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}=0.

and

Lemma 2.3
.

Assume that
\psi\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})
and
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi]\in C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
\beta>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi]\to\mathcal{L}[\psi]=-\kappa(-\Delta)^{s}[\widetilde{\psi}]\mbox{ strongly in }L^{1}(\Omega)

with
\kappa=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{c_{1,s}}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

In view of (
23
), we have

\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}](x)=\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)+\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\chi]+\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\chi(1-\cdot)](x).

Lemma
2.3
implies the convergence of
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi]
,
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\chi]
and
\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\chi(1-\cdot)]
in
L^{1}
and Lemma
2.2
gives
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}=0.

The result follows.
∎

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of the two lemma.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

We write

D_{\varepsilon}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)-\gamma_{0}\frac{2s}{c_{1,s}}D_{N}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}[\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\psi(x)]y\left[F_{0}^{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\right]\,dy.

Lemma
2.1
gives the following bounds:

|y|F^{\varepsilon}_{0}\left(y\right)\leq\frac{C}{|y|^{2s}}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\left||y|F^{\varepsilon}_{0}\left(y\right)-\frac{\gamma_{0}}{|y|^{2s}}\right|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2s}}{|y|^{4s}}\quad\forall y>\varepsilon.

We thus have, using the
C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
regularity of
\psi
, with
\alpha>2s-1
:

\displaystyle\left|D_{\varepsilon}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)-\gamma_{0}\frac{2s}{c_{1,s}}D_{N}^{2s-1}[\psi](x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon}\frac{|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\psi(x)|}{|y|^{2s}}\,dy+C\varepsilon^{2s}\int_{|y|\geq\varepsilon}\frac{|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\psi(x)|}{|y|^{4s}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq C\varepsilon^{1+\alpha-2s}+C\varepsilon^{2s}(1+\varepsilon^{1+\alpha-4s})

\displaystyle\leq C[\varepsilon^{2s}+\varepsilon^{1+\alpha-2s}].

∎

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

As noted in the introduction, a crucial observation in this proof is the fact that the condition
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi]\in C^{\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
implies some Hölder regularity for
\psi
.
Indeed, since
(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}[\psi]=(-\Delta)^{s}[\widetilde{\psi}]
, we can use the regularity theory developed in
[
11
]
to get the following estimate (we use here the notation of
[
11
]
for the Hölder norms):

\|\psi\|_{\beta+2s}^{(-s)}\leq C(\|\psi\|_{C^{s}}+\|(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}[\psi]\|_{\beta}^{(s)})

where

\|\psi\|_{\beta+2s}^{(-s)}:=\sup_{\Omega}d_{x}^{-s}u(x)+\sup_{\Omega}d_{x}^{1-s}u^{\prime}(x)+\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}}d_{x,y}^{\beta+s}\frac{|\psi^{\prime}(x)-\psi^{\prime}(y)|}{|x-y|^{\beta+2s-1}}.

and

\|g\|_{\beta}^{(s)}:=\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}}d_{x,y}^{\beta+s}\frac{|g(x)-g(y)|}{|x-y|^{\beta}}.

with

d_{x}=\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega),\qquad d_{x,y}=\min(d_{x},d_{y}).

We deduce that for any
\psi
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
2.3
, we have

\sup_{\Omega}d_{x}^{-s}u(x)+\sup_{\Omega}d_{x}^{1-s}u^{\prime}(x)+\sup_{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}}d_{x,y}^{\beta+s}\frac{|\psi^{\prime}(x)-\psi^{\prime}(y)|}{|x-y|^{\beta+2s-1}}\leq C.

(33)

We now recall that

\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)\big)F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)\,{\rm{d}}y

where Lemma
2.1
gives (recall that
F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)=\varepsilon^{-1-2s}F_{1}(y/\varepsilon)
):

\displaystyle F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)\leq\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\quad\left|F_{1}^{\varepsilon}(y)-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\right|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon^{2s}}{|y|^{1+4s}}\quad\forall|y|>\varepsilon.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
(to be chosen later), we thus have (for
x\in\Omega)
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)-\mathcal{L}[\psi](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)\big)\left[F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\right]\,{\rm{d}}y

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big[\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y\big]\left[F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\right]\,{\rm{d}}y

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{|y|\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big[\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)\big]\left[F^{\varepsilon}_{1}(y)-\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|y|^{1+2s}}\right]\,{\rm{d}}y

which yields

|\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)-\mathcal{L}[\psi](x)|\leq\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y\big|\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}y+\int_{|y|\geq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)\big|\frac{C\varepsilon^{2s}}{|y|^{1+4s}}\,{\rm{d}}y.

The second term is clearly bounded by
\|\psi\|_{\infty}\varepsilon^{2s-4s\alpha}
, so we can write

\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)-\mathcal{L}[\psi](x)|\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y\big|\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx+\|\psi\|_{\infty}\varepsilon^{2s(1-2\alpha)}

and we write the integral in the right hand side as
J^{\varepsilon}_{1}+J^{\varepsilon}_{2}
with

J_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y\big|\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}1_{(x+y)\notin\Omega}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx

J_{2}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y\big|\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}1_{(x+y)\in\Omega}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx.

In order to bound
J_{1}^{\varepsilon}
, we note that if
(x+y)\notin\Omega
then
|y|\geq d_{x}
. Using (
33
), we deduce

\displaystyle J_{1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{d_{x}\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\int_{d_{x}\leq|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big[|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)|+|\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)|y\big]\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}1_{(x+y)\notin\Omega}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{d_{x}\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\int_{d_{x}<|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\big[d_{x}^{s}+d_{x}^{s-1}|y|\big]\frac{C}{|y|^{1+2s}}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{d_{x}\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}d_{x}^{-s}\,dx\leq C\varepsilon^{\alpha(1-s)}

For
J_{2}^{\varepsilon}
, we first notice that when
x+y\in\Omega
we have (using (
33
)):

|\widetilde{\psi}(x+y)-\widetilde{\psi}(x)-\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(x)y|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(x+\tau y)y-\psi^{\prime}(x)y\,\,{\rm{d}}\tau\right|\leq C\frac{y^{\beta+2s}}{d_{x,x+y}^{\beta+s}}

and so

\displaystyle J_{2}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\Omega}\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}\frac{y^{\beta-1}}{d_{x,x+y}^{\beta+s}}1_{(x+y)\in\Omega}\,{\rm{d}}y\,dx\leq C\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}y^{\beta-1}\int_{\Omega}d_{x,x+y}^{-\beta-s}1_{(x+y)\in\Omega}\,dx\,{\rm{d}}y

As long as
\beta+s<1
, we have
\int_{\Omega}d_{x,x+y}^{-\beta-s}1_{(x+y)\in\Omega}\,dx<\infty
and so

J_{2}^{\varepsilon}\leq C\int_{|y|\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}}y^{\beta-1}\,{\rm{d}}y\leq C\varepsilon^{\beta\alpha}

We have thus proved (provided
0<\beta<1-s
):

\int_{\Omega}|\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi](x)-\mathcal{L}[\psi](x)|\,dx\leq C[\varepsilon^{2s(1-2\alpha)}+\varepsilon^{\beta\alpha}+C\varepsilon^{\alpha(1-s)}]

and the result follows by taking
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
.
∎

2.4
Convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}

Finally, in order to pass to the limit in the remaining terms in (
27
), we need the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}
:

Lemma 2.4
.

Consider
\psi\in C^{0}_{c}([0,\infty);C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}))
with
\alpha>2s-1
such that
D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi]=0
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty)
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\varepsilon}-\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}F(v)\,{\rm{d}}v\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}t=0.

If
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,\infty);C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}))
, with
\alpha>2s-1
then

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}-\partial_{t}\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}F(v)\,{\rm{d}}v\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}t=0

Proof.

First, we note that

|\phi^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}|\widetilde{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\,dz

and so (with
T
such that
\psi(t)=0
for
t\geq T
):

\displaystyle\iiint_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}|\phi^{\varepsilon}-\psi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}F(v)\,{\rm{d}}v\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\iiint_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}|\widetilde{\psi}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}F(v)\,dz\,{\rm{d}}v\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\iint_{(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\,dxF(v)\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\ \,dz\,{\rm{d}}v\,{\rm{d}}t

Then, we note that (recall that
\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{0},\lambda^{\varepsilon}_{1}\to 0
by Lemma

):

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\psi}^{\varepsilon}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\,dx=0\quad\mbox{ for all }t,v,z

and

\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\psi}(t,x+\varepsilon zv)-\psi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\,dxF(v)\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\leq\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}F(v)\nu_{0}e^{-\nu_{0}z}\in L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})

Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem implies the result.

The second limit is proved similarly (note that
t
is a parameter).
∎

2.5
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We are now ready to prove our main result.

A priori estimates.

We have the following classical lemma:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f_{in}
be in
L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R})
. The weak solution
f^{\varepsilon}
of (
6
)
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{N}))
and
satisfies, up to a subsequence

f^{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\rho(t,x)F(v)\quad\text{ weakly in }L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}_{F^{-1}}(\Omega\times\mathbb{R}))

(34)

where
\rho(t,x)
is the weak limit of
\rho^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\varepsilon}\,{\rm{d}}v
.
Assume furthermore that
f_{in}(x,v)\leq CF(v)
for some constant
C
.
Then
f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\leq CF(v)
and

\rho^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\rightharpoonup\rho(t,x)\quad L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R})\star-\mbox{weak.}

(35)

Proof.

We do not prove the first part of the lemma which is classical (see for instance Lemma 2.1 in
[
7
]
).

For the second part, we note that when
f_{in}(x,v)\leq CF(v)
, the function
(t,x,v)\mapsto CF(v)-f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)
is a solution of (
6
) with non-negative initial data and thus is thus non-negative for all time. This implies
f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\leq CF(v)
and so
\rho^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\leq C.

∎

Convergence to a solution of the asymptotic problem.

Given a test function
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
,
we can now pass to the limit in the weak formulation (
27
), which we recall here:

\iiint_{Q}f^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v+\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}f_{in}(x,v)\phi^{\varepsilon}(0,x,v)\,{\rm{d}}x\,{\rm{d}}v=-\iint_{\Omega\times\mathbb{R}}\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}^{\varepsilon}[\psi^{\varepsilon}](x)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x.

For any subsequence along which (
34
) holds, Proposition
2.3
, Lemma
2.4
(both of which apply since
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
) and (
35
) allow us to take the limit, proving that the limiting density
\rho(t,x)
satisfies (
14
).

The fact that the whole sequence converges then follows from the uniqueness of the limit
\rho
given by Proposition
1.1
which we prove below.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

2.6
Proof of Proposition
1.1

Proof of Proposition
1.1
.

To prove the existence, we simply have to show that the weak solution
u(t,x)
provided by Theorem
1.2
satisfies (
14
) for appropriate test functions.
We recall that
u\in C^{0}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{1}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))\cap C^{0}((0,\infty);D((-\Delta)_{N}^{s}))

and
that in dimension one, the condition
(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u\in C^{0}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))
implies that
(t,x)\mapsto D_{N}^{2s-1}[u](t,x)
is continuous (so the Neumann condition is satisfied in the classical sense).
Using integration by parts and (
10
), it is easy to check that
u
satisfies

\underset{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega}{\iiint}u\big(\partial_{t}\psi-\kappa\,(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\psi\big)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x+\underset{\Omega}{\iint}\rho_{in}(x)\psi(0,x)\,{\rm{d}}x=0

for all test function
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
.

Let now
\rho_{1}(t,x)
and
\rho_{2}(t,x)
be two functions satisfying (
14
) for appropriate test functions.
The function
\bar{\rho}=\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
satisfies

\underset{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\Omega}{\iiint}\bar{\rho}(t,x)\Big(\partial_{t}\psi-\kappa\,(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\psi\Big)(t,x)\,{\rm{d}}t\,{\rm{d}}x=0

(36)

for all test function
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
.
Given a smooth test function
g\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
and
\lambda>0
, we let
\phi(x)
be the weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda\phi+\kappa\,(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\phi=g&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
D_{N}^{2s-1}[\phi](x)\cdot n(x)=0&\mbox{ for all }x\in\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

given by Theorem
1.3
and we define

\psi(t,x)=e^{-\lambda t}\phi(x).

We need to check that we can take this function
\psi
as test function in (
36
):

First, the maximum principle (for the Neumann boundary value problem) implies that
\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}
.
Next, we note that the extension
\tilde{\phi}
solves

(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{\phi}=g-\lambda\phi
in
\Omega
with
\tilde{\phi}
constant in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega
.
Standard regularity theory for the fractional Dirichlet boundary value problem (Proposition
1.2
) implies that
\tilde{\phi}\in C^{s}(\mathbb{R})
and so
\phi\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})
.
In turns, this implies that

(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}\phi=(-\Delta)^{s}\tilde{\phi}\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})
. It is now easy to see that
\psi\in X^{\beta}_{0}
.

Using the fact that
\partial_{t}\psi(t,x)-\kappa(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}[\psi](t,x)=-e^{-\lambda t}g(x)
, it follows from (
36
) that

\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\rho}(t,x)\,e^{-\lambda t}\,dtg(x)\,dx=0.

Since this holds for all
g\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, we deduce

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\rho}(t,x)\,e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=0\quad\mbox{ in $\Omega$, for all }\lambda>0

and taking the inverse Laplace transform implies that
\bar{\rho}(t,x)=0
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega
.

∎

3
Optimal regularity for the elliptic problem: Proof of Proposition
1.2

In this section, we are interested in the optimal regularity of the solutions to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u+(-\Delta)_{N}^{s}u=g&\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
&D_{N}^{2s-1}[u](x)=0&\quad\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

(37)

with
\Omega=(0,1)
and
g\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. As stated before, the extension
\tilde{u}
solves
\tilde{u}+\big(-\Delta\big)^{s}\tilde{u}=g
and is constant outside
\Omega
so the regularity theory for the fractional Dirichlet boundary value problems ensures that
u\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})
. We will show that this regularity is optimal by constructing a solution of (
37
) which behaves like
\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{s}
close to the boundary.

First, we recall that
v:x\mapsto\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}
is an explicit solution to

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(-\Delta)^{s}v=1\quad&\mbox{ in }\Omega\\
&v=0&\mbox{ in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

with
\Omega=(0,1)
and the proper choice of constant
\kappa_{s}>0
, see e.g.
[
11
]
.
Of course, this function does not satisfies
D^{2s-1}_{N}[v]=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

We thus consider two smooth functions
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{1}
with compact support in
(1/4,3/4)
and such that for
i\in\{1,2\}
and
x\in\partial\Omega
:
D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{i}](x)\neq 0
and

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](0)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](1)-D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](1)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](0)\neq 0.

We then define the function
u
as

\displaystyle u(x)=\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}+\lambda_{0}\psi_{0}(x)+\lambda_{1}\psi_{1}(x)

(38)

where
\kappa
is a positive constant and the
\lambda_{i}
are defined, similarly to Proposition
2.2
, as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \lambda_{0}=\frac{D^{2s-1}_{N}[\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}](0)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](1)+D^{2s-1}_{N}[\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}](1)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](0)}{D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](0)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](1)-D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](1)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](0)},\\
\lambda_{1}=\frac{D^{2s-1}_{N}[\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}](1)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](0)+D^{2s-1}_{N}[\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}](0)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](1)}{D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](0)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](1)-D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{0}](1)D^{2s-1}_{N}[\psi_{1}](0)}.\end{aligned}\right.

Note that
x\to x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}\in C^{s}(\overline{\Omega})
hence
D^{2s-1}_{N}[\kappa_{s}x_{+}^{s}(1-x)_{+}^{s}]
is continuous on
\overline{\Omega}
and the boundary values exist. This choice of
\lambda_{i}
and the linearity of
D^{2s-1}_{N}
implies naturally

\displaystyle D^{2s-1}_{N}[u](x)=0,\qquad\forall x\in\partial\Omega.

Finally,
u
is a solution of (
37
) with right hand side

\displaystyle g(x)=u+1+\lambda_{0}(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}\psi_{0}+\lambda_{1}(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}\psi_{1}

and by assumption on the support of
\psi_{i}
we have
(-\Delta)^{s}_{N}\psi_{i}=(-\Delta)^{s}\widetilde{\psi}_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and in particular
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

We have thus built a solution
u
of (
37
) with
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, which behaves like
\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{s}
when
x\to 0
and
x\to 1
, which completes the proof.
