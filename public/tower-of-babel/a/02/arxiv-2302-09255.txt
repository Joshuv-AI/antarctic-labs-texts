Abstract
High covariate dimensionality is increasingly occurrent in model estimation, and existing techniques to address this issue typically require sparsity or discrete heterogeneity of the unobservable parameter vector. However, neither restriction may be supported by economic theory in some empirical contexts, leading to severe bias and misleading inference. The clustering-based grouped parameter estimator (GPE) introduced in this paper drops both restrictions and maintains the natural one that the parameter support be bounded. GPE exhibits robust large sample properties under standard conditions and accommodates both sparse and non-sparse parameters whose support can be bounded away from zero. Extensive Monte Carlo simulations demonstrate the excellent performance of GPE in terms of bias reduction and size control compared to competing estimators. An empirical application of GPE to estimating price and income elasticities of demand for gasoline highlights its practical utility. Keywords: high-dimension, non-sparsity, parameter heterogeneity, clustering, approximation error JEL classification: C01, C55



Introduction
Let
y
be a scalar outcome and
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a vector of covariates. Consider the standard linear model

(0.1)

\displaystyle y={\bm{x}}\bm{\beta}_{o}+\varepsilon,

where the random noise
\varepsilon

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|{\bm{x}}]=0
almost surely (
a.s.
). To estimate
\bm{\beta}_{o}
with a finite sample of size

p<n
. However, when
p
is large relative to
n
, OLS gives highly imprecise estimates when
p<n
or is infeasible when
p>n
. Unfortunately, the problem of high dimensionality in model estimation is increasingly becoming common in empirical practice.

A popular approach to tackling the problem of high dimensionality is to assume sparsity where only a few covariates are relevant and determine them using a regularisation technique, e.g., LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996a
)
. Several sparsity-dependent estimators have been proposed in the literature, e.g., the post-LASSO estimators of
Belloni et al. 2014a
;
Belloni et al. 2017a
. The Dantzig selector
(
Candes & Tao 2007a
;
Bickel et al. 2009a
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2022c
)
constitutes another important category of sparsity-dependent estimators. The rationale behind the aforementioned methods is that the penalty promotes sparsity in the estimate. Thus, a single parameter value, typically zero, can be assigned to a large subset of the covariates while parameters of the remaining covariates are “freely” estimated. As
Li & Müller 2021a
points out, (approximate) sparsity in social science applications is not
a priori
obvious. Moreover, sparsity is not invariant to linear reparametrisations of covariates
(
Li & Müller 2021a
;
Giannone et al. 2021a
)
.
1
1

1

For example, sparsity in the rotated space of principal components, e.g, the first principal component only, corresponds to density in the un-transformed space of covariates
(
Giannone et al. 2021a
, 2412)
.

Clustering is another useful technique used to deal with high dimensionality. Discrete heterogeneity, i.e., finite number of support points in
\bm{\beta}_{o}
, and well-separated groups which are commonly assumed in clustering-based estimators, improve upon the sparsity assumption, see, e.g.,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
;
Ke et al. 2015a
;
Sarafidis & Wansbeek 2012a
;
Su et al. 2016a
;
Cheng et al. 2021a
. While the aforementioned methods improve upon sparsity-dependent methods by achieving group-wise heterogeneity, they typically assume discrete heterogeneity of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
(characterised by a finite number of latent-types) in panel data settings with a large time dimension.
2
2

2

To have a sense of the improvement, note that a clustering-based estimator is consistent under the discrete-non-sparse configuration with
\bm{\beta}_{o}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\prime}
using at least one group of covariates while sparsity-dependent estimators fail.
Notable exceptions include
Bonhomme et al. 2022a
which allows continuous heterogeneity and
Ke et al. 2015a
which considers a cross-sectional setting but imposes discrete heterogeneity. Besides some game-theoretic settings, discrete heterogeneity does not appear to enjoy much support from, e.g., economic theory
(
Hahn & Moon 2010a
)
. The grouped parameter estimator (GPE) is fundamentally different from the aforementioned clustering-based estimators. (1) In this paper, each element of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
corresponds to a different covariate. (2) Clustering is a pure approximation tool as discrete heterogeneity is
not assumed
and the resulting approximation error is controlled via a consistent choice of the number of groups. (3) The goal is to estimate potentially high-dimensional models, and not to learn discrete groupings of units from panel data. Thus, this paper is correctly viewed as complementing clustering-based estimators by not assuming discrete heterogeneity, characterising the resulting approximation error, and choosing the number of groups such that the estimator is asymptotically normal in the presence of approximation error.

This paper bears some similarity with the recent paper of
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
as both treat the parameter as a product of two matrix-valued parameters. However, the focus of
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
differs substantially from ours. (1) Their parameter is high dimensional
(n\times p)
matrix-valued while
\bm{\beta}_{0}
is
p\times 1
. Thus, we do not need to impose a rank condition on
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. The product representation in this paper comes from assigning
\bm{\beta}_{0}
(up to an approximation bias) to
k\leq p
groups. (2) It is not obvious how a standard textbook linear model such as (
0.1
) with cross-sectional data can be cast in their proposed framework since their outcome, covariate matrix, parameter, and random noise is each
n\times p
matrix-valued – see
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
. (3) They necessarily require
p\rightarrow\infty
while this paper does not. (4) Unlike this paper, Assumption 4.3 of
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
requires a bounded support of
{\bm{x}}
with substantial restrictions on its variation – see
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
.

Since
\bm{\beta}_{o}
is never observed in practice, wrongly assumed restrictions can result in misleading inference. For this reason, there is an emerging need in the literature for methods that are agnostic of and robust to different degrees of (non)-sparsity and heterogeneity of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
. The Grouped Parameter Estimator (GPE) introduced in this paper is developed using clustering as an approximation tool under the natural condition that the support of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
be bounded. Relative to existing estimators, GPE is robust to pairwise configurations of (1) sparsity, approximate sparsity, or non-sparsity and (2) continuous, mixed, or discrete heterogeneity of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
. Therefore, GPE eliminates the burden of assuming specific configurations of the
unobservable

\bm{\beta}_{o}
, e.g., sparsity, and the attendant consequences for inference. A simple data-driven selection rule is proposed in this paper to determine the number of groups in order to control approximation error. Under mild conditions, the large sample properties of GPE are established under both fixed and increasing
p
asymptotics.

1
The Grouped Parameter Estimator

The Grouped Parameter Estimator (GPE) exploits proximity between elements of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
in order to decrease the size of the approximating model from
p
to
k
where
k\in\{\underline{k}_{p},\ldots,\bar{k}_{p}\}
and
1\leq\underline{k}_{p}\leq\bar{k}_{p}\leq p
. For instance, if
\beta_{oj}\approx\beta_{oj^{\prime}}\approx\beta_{oj^{\prime\prime}}
“sufficiently”, one can assign a common parameter to the corresponding
(j,j^{\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
’th covariates in
{\bm{x}}
.
3
3

3

Technically, “sufficiently” as used here requires that the resulting approximation error not dominate the estimation error.
This implies that for
p\geq 2
, one can consider partitioning
\bm{\beta}_{o}
into
k
groups. Owing to the linear index
{\bm{x}}\bm{\beta}_{o}
in (
0.1
), grouping parameters translates into grouping covariates by design. Covariates in each group are aggregated and assigned their respective group parameters
\{\delta_{ol},1\leq l\leq k\}
. In essence, the idea behind GPE is to fit (
0.1
) using
k
instead of
p
parameters. GPE simplifies to the OLS estimator when
k=p
.

1.1
A population-level treatment

Let
{\bm{m}}_{o}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
be a binary matrix that represents the optimal assignment (in the
k
-means clustering sense) of elements in
\bm{\beta}_{o}
to
k
groups, where
\mathcal{M}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
denotes a space of
p\times k
binary matrices. The
(j,l)
’th entry of
{\bm{m}}_{o}
equals
1
if
\beta_{oj}
belongs to group
l
and zero otherwise. Since each row of
{\bm{m}}_{o}
contains a single one and
k-1
zeros,
{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}{\bm{m}}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is a diagonal matrix with group sizes on the diagonal and
\mathrm{tr}({\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}{\bm{m}}_{o})=p
, where
\mathrm{tr}(A)
denotes the trace of matrix
A
. Also, because each element of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
belongs to only one group and empty groups are not allowed, the inner product of any two columns of
{\bm{m}}_{o}
is zero.

For a fixed
k\in\{\underline{k}_{p},\bar{k}_{p}\}
,
\bm{\beta}_{o}
is decomposed as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{o}={\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}+\bm{b}_{k},

where
\bm{b}_{k}:=\bm{\beta}_{o}-{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}
is a non-stochastic
approximation bias
. The GPE estimand, viz.
{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}
solves the following clustering problem:

(1.2)

\min_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\ \bm{\delta}\in\Delta}\lVert{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}-\bm{\beta}_{o}\rVert^{2}

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the Euclidean norm when applied to a vector and
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We introduce the following standard assumption in order to characterise the bound on the approximation bias
\bm{b}_{k}
.

Assumption 1.1
.

\bm{\beta}\in[-C_{b},C_{b}]^{p}
for some positive constant
C_{b}<\infty
that does not depend on
p
.

1.1
is a standard assumption in the literature, which is used to bound the approximation bias
\bm{b}_{k}
– cf.
Su et al. 2016a
,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
,
Cheng et al. 2021a
, and
Bonhomme et al. 2022a
.

The GPE estimand
{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}
is unique since it is invariant to the relabelling/permutation of groups. Moreover, neither
{\bm{m}}_{o}
nor
\bm{\delta}_{o}
is of independent interest in this paper. We, nonetheless, follow the literature, e.g.,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
;
Cheng et al. 2021a
, in defining
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
up to permutations that leave
{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}_{o}
unchanged. To avoid overly dominant or empty groups that may induce ill-conditioning in the matrix
A_{m}:={\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]{\bm{m}}
for all
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, we maintain the following condition throughout the paper.

Condition 1.1
.

Group sizes induced by all
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
are bounded above by some constant
M
and away from zero uniformly in
p
.

Group sizes induced by the group assignment matrix
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
constitute the diagonal elements of
{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bm{m}}
. Since the columns of
{\bm{m}}
are orthogonal, group sizes are also the eigenvalues of
{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bm{m}}
. Thus,
1.1
implies that
||{\bm{m}}||\lesssim 1
for all
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
where
||\cdot||
denotes the spectral norm when applied to matrices. In practice,
1.1
can
always be guaranteed
by splitting up overly dominant groups and ensuring each group has at least one element.
1.1
hence requires that the number of groups increases if
p
is increasing. This also means that the lower and upper bounds on
k
(
\underline{k}_{p}
and
\bar{k}_{p}
), which are
p
-indexed, ought to increase under increasing-
p
asymptotics. To alleviate concerns of scaling as covariates are aggregated into groups, we implicitly assume throughout the paper that
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}]=0
.

For any two sequences of non-negative numbers
\{a_{n}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{b_{n}:n\geq 1\}
,
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
means
a_{n}\leq cb_{n}
for some finite
c>0
and
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
means
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
. Also, let
|\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\beta}_{o})|
denote the number of support points of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
. The following result derives the bound on
||\bm{b}_{k}||
in terms of
p
and
k
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
1.1
hold, then
||\bm{b}_{k}||^{2}\leq 4C_{b}^{2}M^{2}\frac{p}{k^{2}}
if
k<|\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\beta}_{o})|
and zero otherwise.

Proposition

1.1
shows

is decreasing in
k
but increasing in
p
. Thus, the more granular the partitions of
\bm{\beta}_{0}
, the smaller the approximation error.
Proposition

1.1
is a univariate
non-asymptotic
and
non-stochastic
analogue of the stochastic bound on the
k
-means clustering approximation bias in
Graf & Luschgy 2002a
– cf.
Bonhomme et al. 2022a
.

Remark 1.1
.

Proposition

1.1
is a worst-case bound under continuous heterogeneity and increasing
p
asymptotics.
4
4

4

||\bm{b}_{k}||^{2}\asymp k^{-2}
if

Under discrete or mixed heterogeneity of

with

is zero when

Next, we motivate GPE from a regression point of view. Define the following least squares criterion:

(1.3)

Q_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}):=\mathbb{E}[(y-{\bm{x}}{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})^{2}]/p.

Given
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
,
\bm{\delta}_{o}({\bm{m}}):=({\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}y]
is a deterministic function of
{\bm{m}}
that minimises
Q_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})
with respect to
\bm{\delta}\in\Delta
holding
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
fixed. Define
\mathcal{S}_{p}:=\{\uptau\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:||\uptau||=1\}
as the space of vectors with unit length. The following standard assumptions are imposed in order to characterise the identification of the GPE estimand
{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}
.

Assumption 1.2
.

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|{\bm{x}}]=0\ a.s.

Assumption 1.3
.

There exist constants
B>0
and

such that uniformly in
n
,
\mathbb{E}[(|\varepsilon|^{d})|{\bm{x}}]\leq B\ a.s.
,
\mathbb{E}[|{\bm{x}}\uptau_{p}|^{d}]\leq B
, and
B^{-1}\leq\mathbb{E}[|{\bm{x}}\uptau_{p}|^{2}]
for all
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
.

1.2
is a standard exogeneity condition that precludes neither non-Gaussianity nor arbitrary heteroskedasticity of the model error
\varepsilon
.
1.3
requires that the conditional variance be bounded, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}|{\bm{x}}]\leq B\ a.s.
The conditional moment restriction,
\mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon|^{d}|{\bm{x}}]\leq B\ a.s.
, is fairly weak, cf.
Sarafidis & Wansbeek 2012a

\varepsilon
in
Ke et al. 2015a
but slightly stronger than Condition SM(i) of
Belloni et al. 2012c
. Let
\rho_{\mathrm{\max}}(A)
denote the largest eigenvalue of the square matrix
A
.

Remark 1.2
.

1.3
implies that the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]
are bounded above and away from zero uniformly in

since for any
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
,
\mathbb{E}[|{\bm{x}}\uptau_{p}|^{2}]=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]\uptau_{p}
and

B^{-1}\leq\underline{\uptau}_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]\underline{\uptau}_{p}=:\rho_{\mathrm{\min}}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}])\leq\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]\uptau_{p}\leq\rho_{\mathrm{\max}}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]):=\bar{\uptau}_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]\bar{\uptau}_{p}\leq B

where
\underline{\uptau}_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
and
\bar{\uptau}_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
denote the eigenvectors associated with the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]

In view of
Remark

1.2
,
1.3
rules out perfect or very high collinearity in
{\bm{x}}
– cf.
Belloni et al. 2015a
and
Belloni et al. 2012c

is positive-definite for all
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
and
k\in\{\underline{k}_{p},\bar{k}_{p}\}

By the decomposition (
1.1

in (
1.3
) consists of terms involving
\bm{b}_{k}
and terms not dependent on
\bm{b}_{k}

(1.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}):=({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}-{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o})^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}-{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o})/p+\sigma^{2}/p

\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}]
.
\widetilde{Q}_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})

. Thus, we can characterise the identification of the GPE estimand

up to the bias term

.

Theorem 1.1
.

Under
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.1
,
Q_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})=\widetilde{Q}_{o}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})+O(||\bm{b}_{k}||/\sqrt{p})
and

is uniquely minimised at

.

By
Theorem

1.1
, the identification of
{\bm{m}}_{o}\bm{\delta}_{o}
holds exactly if
||\bm{b}_{k}||=0
or approximately if

.

Proposition 1.2
.

Suppose
1.2
and
1.3
hold, then

(a) the minimiser of

solves the parameter clustering problem:

\displaystyle\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\ \bm{\delta}\in\Delta}||{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}-\bm{\beta}_{o}||_{\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]}^{2}
and

(b)
\displaystyle||\bm{b}_{k}||^{2}\asymp\min_{{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\ \bm{\delta}\in\Delta}||{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}-\bm{\beta}_{o}||_{\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]}^{2}

Proposition

1.2
Computation

Let
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}),1\leq i\leq n\}
be a random sample of
({\bm{x}},y)
and
\mathbb{E}_{n}[\cdot]
denote the sample mean. Define
Q_{n}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}):=\mathbb{E}_{n}[(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})^{2}]/p
as the sample version of (
1.3
). GPE is given by
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}:=\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}
, where

(1.5)

\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}=\argmin_{\{{\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}:\ {\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k},\bm{\delta}\in\Delta\}}Q_{n}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta}).

Unlike in the population, a two-step estimation of
\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}
and
\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}
cannot be followed in finite samples because (1)
\bm{\beta}_{o}
is unknown; (2)
\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}
and
\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}
are interdependent and cannot be computed separately; and (3) the computational cost of an exhaustive search is prohibitive, especially for large
p
. In light of the foregoing, we propose the following iterative algorithm for GPE.

Algorithm 1
.

(a)

Fix
k\leq\min\{p,n-2\}
, initialise
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{(0)}
, compute
\{\hat{{\bm{m}}}^{(0)},\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}^{(0)}\}
, and set counter
s=1
.

(b)

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, compute
\hat{\beta}_{n,j}^{(s)}
and update the
j
’th row of
\hat{{\bm{m}}}^{(s)}
.

(c)

Compute
\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}^{(s)}=\argmin_{\bm{\delta}\in\Delta}Q_{n}(\hat{{\bm{m}}}^{(s)}\bm{\delta})
.

(d)

Check for numerical convergence, else set
s\leftarrow s+1
and return to Step
(b)
.

k\leq(n-2)
ensures there is at least one degree of freedom for estimation.
\hat{\beta}_{n,j}^{(s)}
in Step
(b)
is the OLS slope estimate of
y-{\bm{x}}_{-j}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n,-j}^{(s-1)}
regressed on the
j
’th covariate
x_{j}
where
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n,-j}^{(s-1)}:=(\hat{\beta}_{n,1}^{(s)},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{n,j-1}^{(s)},\hat{\beta}_{n,j+1}^{(s-1)},\ldots,\hat{\beta}_{n,p}^{(s-1)})^{\prime}
and
{\bm{x}}_{-j}^{\prime}
is a
(p-1)\times 1
vector formed from
{\bm{x}}
by removing the
j
’th covariate. Updating the
j
’th row of
\hat{{\bm{m}}}^{(s)}
involves assigning
\hat{\beta}_{n,j}^{(s)}
to the nearest element (in absolute value) in
\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}^{(s-1)}
, and updating the group mean. This is the step that differs from, e.g.,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
; it is a faster yet (asymptotically) equivalent step thanks to
Proposition

1.2
(b). Assigning
\hat{\beta}_{n,j}^{(s)}
to the nearest group in Step
Item

(b)
achieves the same goal up to the same order of approximation bias as obtaining the group assignment of
x_{j}
which minimises the objective function. The latter involves computing the objective function
p-1
times while the former only requires computing
k-1
scalar absolute differences.

Remark 1.3
.

Theorem

1.1
shows that the objective function is a weighted within-group sum of squared deviations up to an approximation bias term. One observes from
Proposition

1.2
that unweighted group assignment saves much computational cost as it avoids recomputing the objective function at the group assignment step of
Algorithm

1
. Unlike typical clustering-based regression algorithms, e.g.,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
,
{\bm{x}}
can be non-binary, and panel data are not
sine qua non
.

It is well known in the literature that the solution to iterative algorithms like
Algorithm

1
is sensitive to starting values. Hence, it is essential to have reliable starting schemes.
Our starting values are obtained from subgroup analyses based on the regression model as proposed by
Ma & Huang 2017a
, except that in our case, we apply the concave penalty function to the pairwise differences of the model parameters instead of the intercepts. We proceed to solve the constrained optimisation problem using a modified alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm of
Boyd et al. 2011c
– see
Appendix

S.7
for details. Following the literature, e.g.,
Bonhomme et al. 2022a
, our asymptotic theory focuses on the global minimum (indexed by
k
) while abstracting away from optimisation error.

Remark 1.4
.

A researcher may sometimes be interested in the partial effects of a finite set of covariates and may not want to group them. Such a restriction is easily incorporated in
{\bm{m}}
by adding columns corresponding to singleton covariate groups whose parameters are updated in Step
Item

(c)
while group assignments are held fixed in Step

(b)
across iterations. The intercept is handled in this way.

2.1
Convergence of

We consider a sequence of models indexed by
n
with observed data
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}),1\leq i\leq n\}
,
y_{i}=y_{i,n}
,
{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{x}}_{i,n}
, and
p=p_{n}
. The large sample properties of GPE are developed under the following additional assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

Observed data
\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}),1\leq i\leq n\}
are independent and identically distributed
(iid)
for each
n
.

Assumption 2.2
.

p\log(p)=o(n)
.

The dependence of the sampling process on
n
in
2.1
allows
p
to grow with
n
while allowing for fixed-
p
asymptotics as well.
2.2
characterises the rate at which
p
is allowed to grow with
n
. As,
2.2
is an asymptotic rate condition, it does not rule out
p>n
in finite samples.

Remark 2.1
.

The rate condition in
2.2
required for GPE is more restrictive relative to LASSO’s
\log(p)=o(n^{1/3})
, which allows up to an exponential growth of
p
in
n
– see e.g.,
Belloni et al. 2012c
;
Belloni et al. 2014a
;
Farrell 2015a
. Feature-screening methods, e.g.,
Li et al. 2012a
;
Shao & Zhang 2014a
used in a first stage to weed out irrelevant covariates -- as do sparsity-dependent methods -- can complement the GPE in handling ultra-high dimensional models.
5
5

5

For concerns of space and scope, such a pursuit is left for future work.

By
Remark

2.1
, the slower growth rate of
p
in
n
allowed by GPE, unlike (post)-LASSO estimators, is the price to pay for not explicitly exploiting sparsity (if it holds) which eliminates irrelevant covariates in a first step.

The following provides a characterisation of the convergence rate of
||\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}-{\bm{m}}_{o}||
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
1.1
,
1.2
,
2.1
and

hold, then
||\hat{{\bm{m}}}_{n}-{\bm{m}}_{o}||=O_{p}(||\bm{b}_{k}||^{2}/p)+O_{p}(n^{-1})
.

From
Theorem

above, one observes that the convergence of

to

depends on
||\bm{b}_{k}||
,

, and

.

2.2
Consistency and Asymptotic Normality

For a given
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, the corresponding estimator is

\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}({\bm{m}})=({\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}y_{i}].

The closed-form expression of
\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}({\bm{m}})
is useful in concentrating out
\bm{\delta}
from the objective function
Q_{n}({\bm{m}}\bm{\delta})
.
Define
\hat{A}_{m}:={\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}
as the sample analogue of
A_{m}
. A consistency condition is imposed on
\hat{A}_{m_{o}}
in the following assumption.

Assumption 2.3
.

||\hat{A}_{m_{o}}-A_{m_{o}}||=o_{p}(1)
.

2.3
is a high-level assumption – cf.
Tropp 2012c
,
Chen & Christensen 2015c
, and
Belloni et al. 2015a
.
Lemma

S.3.1
in the Online Appendix verifies
2.3
under sufficient conditions that allow an unbounded support of
{\bm{x}}
and possibly increasing
p
. Also, observe that for a given
k
,
2.3
is imposed on
{\bm{m}}_{o}
and not the entirety of
\mathcal{M}_{k}
.

Using the decomposition of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}_{o}
into the estimation error
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
and the approximation error
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
, namely
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}_{o}=\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{k}+\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{k}:={\bm{m}}_{o}\hat{A}_{m_{o}}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]\ \text{ and }\ \widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}:=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-{\bm{m}}_{o}\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{n}({\bm{m}}_{o})+(\mathrm{I}_{p}+{\bm{m}}_{o}\hat{A}_{m_{o}}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}])\bm{b}_{k},

we characterise the convergence rate of GPE
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}
and its asymptotic normality for any
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
. Define
\sigma_{\theta}:=(\uptau_{p}^{\prime}{\bm{m}}_{o}A_{m_{o}}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}\varepsilon^{2}]{\bm{m}}_{o}A_{m_{o}}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}\uptau_{p})^{1/2}
where dependence of
\sigma_{\theta}
on
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
is suppressed for notational ease.

Theorem 2.2

(Consistency and Asymptotic Normality)
.

Suppose
1.3
,
1.3
,
1.1
,
2.3
,
2.1
,
1.2
and
2.2
hold, then for all
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
, (a)
\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}=O_{p}(||\bm{b}_{k}||)+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
; (b)
\uptau_{p}^{\prime}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}_{o}-\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
; and (c)
\sigma_{\theta}^{-1}\sqrt{n}\uptau_{p}^{\prime}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}-\bm{\beta}_{o}-\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k})\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

2.3
Selection rule

The underlying principle of GPE is that (
0.1
) be estimable with fewer than
p
parameters and thus conserve degrees of freedom. Although
Proposition

1.1
could be used to specify a selection rule of
k
that ensures that the approximation error is controlled, such a choice rule would lack flexibility and be poorly adapted to the configuration of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
. For instance, under discrete or mixed heterogeneity of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
, a small
k
should suffice to control approximation error. Further, simulations under a continuous-non-sparse configuration of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
in
Section

3
suggest that a small number of groups
k
suffices to control approximation error. Therefore, instead of using a large value of
k
to drive approximation bias almost to zero at the cost of imprecise estimates and poor inference, we choose the smallest
k
in a data-driven way to control approximation error – see
Bonhomme et al. 2022a
for a selection rule based on a similar concept.

We select the number of groups per the rule

\hat{k}_{n}:=\min_{k\geq 1}\{k:n\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)\leq C\}

where

\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k):=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{n}[(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{k})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{n}[(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{k+1})^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}_{n}[(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{k+1})^{2}]},

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{k}
is GPE with
k
groups (for notational emphasis), and
C>0
is a user-defined constant.
6
6

6

We recommend using
C=2.7
– see
Appendix

S.4
for details.
As
\mathbb{E}_{n}[(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{n}^{k})^{2}]
is non-increasing in
k
,
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)
is non-negative.
7
7

7

To see why, it suffices to create a
(k+1)
’th singleton group using the covariate whose parameter estimate generates the largest within-group absolute deviation.
Also,
\hat{k}_{n}
is scale-invariant since
\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)
is a ratio. The above selection rule bears an interesting similarity to model selection criteria such as the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) – see, e.g.,
Sarafidis & Weber 2015a
. Unlike the BIC which has a penalty term to avoid over-fitting, setting
C
to a constant and choosing
\hat{k}_{n}
as the minimum admissible
k
guards against over-fitting in our case. The following result shows that this simple selection rule is consistent, i.e., it selects
k
such that the approximation error
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
in
Theorem

2.2
does not dominate the estimation error
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{k}
. In view of
Proposition

1.1
and
Theorem

2.2
(a), let
\{a_{k}:k\geq 1\}
be a decreasing sequence of positive numbers such that
||\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}||=O_{p}(n^{-1/a_{k}})+o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
1.3
,
1.3
,
1.2
,
1.1
,
2.1
and
2.2
hold, then (a)
n\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)=O_{p}(1)
if
a_{k}\leq 2
; and (b)
\displaystyle n\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)\rightarrow\infty
as
n\rightarrow\infty
otherwise.

An important implication of
Theorem

2.3
is that
n\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)=O_{p}(1)
implies
||\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}||=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.
k=\hat{k}_{n}
is chosen to ensure inference is not contaminated by the approximation error. Thus,
C>0
is set to a suitable constant under
a_{k}<2
, so that the approximation error is dominated by the estimation error, i.e.,
||\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}||=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
.
8
8

8

C=2.7
is the
90
’th percentile of the
\chi_{1}^{2}
limiting distribution of
n\widehat{\varphi}_{n}(k)
under two technical conditions and
||\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}||=o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
– see
Appendix

S.4
.

It is instructive to draw parallels between
k
and its approximate analogue in the post-LASSO estimator of
Belloni et al. 2014a
(pLASSO hereafter), namely the sparsity index
s
, which is the bound on the number of covariates in a sparse model whose corresponding elements in
\bm{\beta}_{o}
are non-zero.

Remark 2.2
.

In addition to requiring that zero be an atom of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
, pLASSO imposes the rate condition
s^{2}(\log(p\vee n))^{2}/n=o(1)
. As GPE remains consistent even when
\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\beta}_{o})
is bounded away from zero and
\hat{k}_{n}<|\mathrm{supp}(\bm{\beta}_{o})|
is allowed as long as approximation error is controlled, pLASSO is more restrictive in this sense. Unlike, e.g., pLASSO – see
Belloni et al. 2014a
– which attains valid inference by assuming the approximation error is bounded, an important element to GPE, in light of
Proposition

1.1
and
Theorem

2.3
, is that
k
is chosen to control approximation error.

The choice of
k
is user-determined in a data-driven way whereas the pLASSO rate condition on
s
is an assumption imposed on the unobservable
\bm{\beta}_{o}
.
Remarks

2.1
and
2.2
highlight the complementarity and trade-off between sparsity-dependent estimators and GPE in handling high covariate dimensionality. The consistent choice
k_{n}
controls the approximation error and avoids
unverifiable
assumptions on the approximation error. In contrast, panel-data clustering-based methods, e.g.,
Bonhomme & Manresa 2015c
;
Ke et al. 2015a
;
Sarafidis & Weber 2015a
assume zero approximation error subject to correctly choosing
k
, and regularisation-based methods such as
Belloni et al. 2014a
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2023a
impose order conditions on the approximation error.

5
Conclusion

As the specific configuration of a high-dimensional
\bm{\beta}_{o}
cannot be known for sure in several empirical settings, there is a growing need for reliable, robust, and flexibly-adapted estimators. Although sparsity-dependent estimators such as pLASSO constitute the workhorse of econometric and applied work in high-dimensional settings and their theoretical properties are now well understood, their reliance on sparsity remains a major drawback. Existing clustering-based estimators are largely limited to fixed effects models with large
T
panel data and do typically impose discrete heterogeneity. The aforementioned assumptions are restrictive and hard to verify or justify theoretically as they are imposed on the unobservable
\bm{\beta}_{o}
.

This paper makes a useful addition to the practitioner’s toolkit of high-dimensional model estimation methods. The proposed GPE eliminates the burden on the practitioner of imposing typically unverifiable sparsity or discrete heterogeneity conditions on
\bm{\beta}_{o}
while maintaining the natural requirement that
\bm{\beta}_{o}
be defined on a bounded support. GPE is thus agnostic of and robust to different configurations of
\bm{\beta}_{o}
– it accommodates various degrees of (non)-sparsity and heterogeneity. Thanks to a simple data-driven selection rule, GPE achieves parsimony and adaptability to different empirically relevant configurations.

This paper develops and applies GPE to the linear model with exogenous covariates in an
i.i.d.
setting. To enhance GPE’s usefulness to the practitioner, some important extensions remain. It will be interesting to consider GPE under endogeneity with possibly many (weak) instruments, clustered or weakly dependent data, and non-linear models with both smooth and non-smooth objective functions.

Supporting Lemmata

Lemmata contained in this section are used in the proofs of the theoretical results in the main text.

B.1
Convergence rate: non-stochastic
k
-means clustering

Consider the
k
-means clustering problem for a non-stochastic vector
\mathscr{B}_{p}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The problem involves partitioning
\mathscr{B}_{p}
into
k
groups
\bm{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
,
k\geq 1
, in order to minimise the sum of within-group sums of squared deviations:

\{g_{1}^{*},\ldots,g_{p}^{*}\}=\displaystyle\argmin_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}))^{2}

where
\tilde{b}(l)
is the mean of elements
b_{j}
in group
S_{l}:=\{b_{j}:g_{j}=l\}
, and
g_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let
\{S_{l}^{*},1\leq l\leq k\}
denote the sets of optimal group assignments and
|S_{l}|
denote the cardinality of
S_{l}
. The following lemma establishes a worst-case bound on the maximum absolute within-group deviation and the average of within-group squared deviations in terms of
k
.

Lemma B.1
.

Suppose
\mathscr{B}_{p}\in[-C_{b},C_{b}]^{p}
, i.e., it has a bounded support in
\mathbb{R}
uniformly in
p
,
\displaystyle\{g_{1}^{*},\ldots,g_{p}^{*}\}:=\argmin_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}))^{2}
are the optimal group assignments where
g_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, group sizes are bounded above by
M<\infty
and away from zero uniformly in
p
, and
k<|\mathrm{supp}(\mathscr{B}_{p})|
, i.e.,
k
is less than the number of support points of
\mathscr{B}_{p}
, then (a)
\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}^{*})|\leq 2C_{b}M/k
and (b)
\displaystyle\min_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}))^{2}\leq 4C_{b}^{2}M^{2}/k^{2}
.

Proof:

Uniformly in
p
,
\displaystyle\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|b_{j}|\leq C_{b}
by the boundedness of the support of
\mathscr{B}_{p}
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}))^{2}&=\min_{\bm{S}}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{b\in S_{l}}(b-\tilde{b}(l))^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{b\in S_{l}^{*}}(b-\tilde{b}(l))^{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\frac{|S_{l}^{*}|}{p}\max_{b\in S_{l}^{*}}|b-\tilde{b}(l)|^{2}\\
&=:\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}\tilde{r}_{l}^{2}\end{split}

where
\displaystyle\{S_{1}^{*},\ldots,S_{k}^{*}\}=\argmin_{\bm{S}}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\sum_{b\in S_{l}}(b-\tilde{b}(l))^{2}
,
\ \tilde{p}_{l}:=\frac{|S_{l}^{*}|}{p}
, and
\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{l}:=\max_{b\in S_{l}^{*}}|b-\tilde{b}(l)|
is the
l
’th group’s maximum within-group deviation from the group mean.
\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}=1
such that
\tilde{p}_{l}>0
for each
l=1,\ldots,k
as there are no empty groups.

Part (a):
Denote the diameter of group
S_{l}^{*}
by
\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(S_{l}^{*})=\max_{b_{1},b_{2}\in S_{l}^{*}}|b_{1}-b_{2}|
. At the minimised sum of within-group sums of squared deviations, the sum of group diameters cannot exceed the length of the support, i.e.,
\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathrm{diam}(S_{l}^{*})\leq 2C_{b}
because groups do not overlap – see, e.g.,
Fisher 1958a
on the contiguity of least squares partitions. Since maximum within-group deviation from the group mean cannot exceed the diameter of the group, i.e.,
\displaystyle\max_{1\leq l\leq k}\big(\tilde{r}_{l}-\mathrm{diam}(S_{l}^{*})\big)\leq 0
, it follows that
\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{r}_{l}\leq 2C_{b}
.

Define a parameter vector
\bm{r}:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
, then

\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}\tilde{r}_{l}^{2}\leq\max_{\bm{r}}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}^{2}\text{ subject to }\sum_{l=1}^{k}r_{l}\leq 2C_{b}.

The Lagrangian corresponding to the constrained maximisation problem above is given by

\mathcal{L}(\bm{r},\lambda)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}^{2}-\lambda(\sum_{l=1}^{k}r_{l}-2C_{b})

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the Lagrange multiplier. Taking partial derivatives gives

(B.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{r})}{\partial r_{l}}

\displaystyle:=2\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}-\lambda,\ l=1,\ldots,k\text{ and}

(B.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\bm{r})}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle:=-(\sum_{l=1}^{k}r_{l}-2C_{b}).

The case of
\lambda=0
implies the maximiser of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{r})
is
r_{l}^{*}=0
for each
l=1,\ldots,k
from (
B.1
) but this rather minimises the objective function. This case is hence ruled out. For
\lambda>0
, (
B.2
) gives
\sum_{l=1}^{k}r_{l}-2C_{b}=0
which is equivalently expressed as
r_{k}=2C_{b}-\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}r_{l}
. Setting the set of equations (
B.1
) to zero results in
2\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}=\lambda
for each
l=1,\ldots,k
and
\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}=\tilde{p}_{k}r_{k}
for each
l=1,\ldots,k-1
with
r_{k}=2C_{b}-\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}r_{l^{\prime}}
. Substituting
r_{k}=2C_{b}-\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}r_{l^{\prime}}
into the
k-1
equations gives
\tilde{p}_{l}r_{l}=\tilde{p}_{k}(2C_{b}-\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}r_{l^{\prime}}),\ l=1,\ldots,k-1
. Dividing through by
\tilde{p}_{k}
and rearranging terms gives
\bm{A}\bm{r}_{-k}=2\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}C_{b}
where

\bm{A}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1+\frac{\tilde{p}_{1}}{\tilde{p}_{k}}&1&\ldots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ldots&\vdots\\
1&1&\ldots&1+\frac{\tilde{p}_{k-1}}{\tilde{p}_{k}}\\
\end{array}\right)

,
\bm{r}_{-k}=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{k-1})^{\prime}
, and
\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}
is a
(k-1)\times 1
vector of ones.
\bm{A}\bm{r}_{-k}=2\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}C_{b}
is solved by
\bm{r}_{-k}^{*}:=2\bm{A}^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}C_{b}
.

The next step is to obtain the explicit expression for
\bm{A}^{-1}
. Note that
\bm{A}=\bm{D}+\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{k-1}^{\prime}
where
\bm{D}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\{D_{ll}:=\frac{\tilde{p}_{l}}{\tilde{p}_{k}}
,
1\leq l\leq k-1\}
. Applying the formula of
Sherman & Morrison 1949a
, see e.g.,
Bartlett 1951a
,

(\bm{A}^{-1})_{ll^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{l}}-\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}^{2}}{q\tilde{p}_{l}^{2}}&\text{ if }l=l^{\prime}\\
-\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}^{2}}{q\tilde{p}_{l}\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}}&\text{ if }l\not=l^{\prime}\end{cases}

where
q=1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{\ell}}
. It hence follows that for each
l\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
,

\displaystyle r_{l}^{*}

\displaystyle=2C_{b}\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}(\bm{A}^{-1})_{ll^{\prime}}=2C_{b}\Big(\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{l}}-\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}^{2}}{q\tilde{p}_{l}^{2}}-\sum_{l^{\prime}\not=l}^{k-1}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}^{2}}{q\tilde{p}_{l}\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}}\Big)=2C_{b}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{q\tilde{p}_{l}}\Big(q-\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=2C_{b}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{(1+\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}})\tilde{p}_{l}}=2C_{b}\frac{1}{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k}\frac{\tilde{p}_{l}}{\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}}}=2C_{b}\frac{1}{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k}\frac{|S_{l}^{*}|}{|S_{l^{\prime}}^{*}|}}\leq 2C_{b}M/k

under the condition that group sizes
\{|S_{l}^{*}|,1\leq l\leq k\}
are bounded above by
M
and away from zero. Also, observe that
r_{k}^{*}=2C_{b}-\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}r_{l}^{*}=2C_{b}\frac{1}{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k}\frac{\tilde{p}_{k}}{\tilde{p}_{l^{\prime}}}}=2C_{b}\frac{1}{\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{k}\frac{|S_{k}^{*}|}{|S_{l^{\prime}}^{*}|}}\leq 2C_{b}M/k
. Thus,
r_{l}^{*}\leq 2C_{b}M/k
for all
l\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and that concludes the proof of the first part.

Part (b):
From part (a), note that
\displaystyle\min_{\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}}\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(b_{j}-\tilde{b}(g_{j}))^{2}\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}\tilde{r}_{l}^{2}\leq\max_{1\leq l\leq k}\tilde{r}_{l}^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}=\max_{1\leq l\leq k}\tilde{r}_{l}^{2}\leq 4C_{b}^{2}M^{2}/k^{2}
recalling that
\sum_{l=1}^{k}\tilde{p}_{l}=1
.

∎

B.2
Triangular array CLT

Define the triangular array
\{S_{in},1\leq i\leq n,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
where
S_{in}=\tilde{S}_{in}/s_{n}
,
\tilde{S}_{in}=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}
,
s_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}]\uptau_{p}
, and
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
.

Lemma B.2
.

Under
2.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{in}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Proof:

First,
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{in}]=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}|{\bm{x}}_{i}]]=0
by
1.2
and the law of iterated expectations (LIE) for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Second, under independent sampling (
2.1
),
\mathbb{E}[(\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{in})^{2}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[S_{in}^{2}]=s_{n}^{2}/s_{n}^{2}=1
. Third, it suffices to verify the Lyapunov condition
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|S_{in}|^{d}]=0
in order to complete the proof.

By the LIE and
1.3
,

(B.3)

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{in}^{2}]=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}\varepsilon_{i}^{2}]\uptau_{p}=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}|{\bm{x}}_{i}]]\uptau_{p}\leq B\rho_{\mathrm{\max}}(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}])\lesssim 1

for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This implies
s_{n}=O(n^{1/2})
. Since
\mathbb{E}[|\uptau_{p}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}|^{d}]\leq B
by
1.3
, it follows from the LIE and
1.3
that
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{S}_{in}|^{d}]=\mathbb{E}[|\uptau_{p}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}|^{d}\mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon_{i}|^{d}|{\bm{x}}_{i}]]\leq B^{2}<\infty
. Thus,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{S}_{in}|^{d}]=O(n)
. Combining the foregoing,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|S_{in}|^{d}]=s_{n}^{-d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{S}_{in}|^{d}]=O(n^{-(d/2-1)})=o(1)
since
d>2
(
1.3
). The conclusion follows from
Davidson 2021a
.

∎

B.3
Auxiliary Result I

The following lemma is used in the proof of
Theorem

2.1
. Let
\hat{\varepsilon}_{m_{o},i}
and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}
denote the
i
’th residual from regressing
\mathcal{E}
on
{\bf X}{\bm{m}}
and
{\bf X}{\bm{m}}_{o}
, respectively.

Lemma B.3
.

Fix
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}
, then under
1.2
,
1.3
and
2.1
,
(a)
\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{m_{o},i}^{2}-\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}^{2})\Big|\geq 1\Big)\leq 2kB
; and
(b)
\mathbb{P}(|\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}]|\geq 1)\leq 2B^{2}/n
for any
\uptau_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{p}
.

Proof:

Observe that
{\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}
is a projection matrix with
k
eigenvalues equal to one and the rest
n-k
equal to zero
(
Hansen 2022a
, Theorem 3.3)
. Let
h_{il}^{(m)}
be
i
’th element of the eigenvector associated with the
l
’th eigenvalue of
{\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}
.

Part (a):
Recall
\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}=\varepsilon_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]
. By the foregoing and the eigen-decomposition,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{m_{o},i}^{2}-\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}^{2})

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varepsilon_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]-n\mathbb{E}_{n}[\varepsilon_{i}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}_{o}\hat{A}_{m_{o}}^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}}_{o}({\bm{m}}_{o}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}}_{o})^{-1}{\bm{m}}_{o}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{il}^{(m)}\varepsilon_{i}\big)^{2}-\sum_{l=1}^{k}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{il}^{(m_{o})}\varepsilon_{i}\big)^{2}.

By the triangle inequality and the arguments similar to those of
Lemma

B.6
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\varepsilon}_{m_{o},i}^{2}-\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}^{2})\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{il}^{(m)}\varepsilon_{i}\big)^{2}\Big]+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{il}^{(m_{o})}\varepsilon_{i}\big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq 2kB

under
2.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
. The conclusion of this part follows from the Markov inequality.

Part (b):

\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}]=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]-\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]
, hence
(\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}])^{2}\leq 2(\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}])^{2}+2(\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}])^{2}
by the
c_{r}
-inequality. From (
B.3
),
\mathrm{var}[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]]\leq n^{-1}B^{2}
under
2.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
. Next, define
\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}}):={\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}{\bf X}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{\uptau}_{p}:={\bf X}\uptau_{p}/||{\bf X}\uptau_{p}||
.

\displaystyle\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\uptau_{p}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\tilde{\uptau}_{p}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}}({\bm{m}}^{\prime}{\bf X}^{\prime}{\bf X}{\bm{m}})^{-1}{\bm{m}}{\bf X}^{\prime}\mathcal{E}\cdot||{\bf X}\uptau_{p}||

\displaystyle=:n^{-1}\tilde{\uptau}_{p}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})\mathcal{E}\cdot||{\bf X}\uptau_{p}||.

Thus by the LIE,
1.2
,
2.1
and
1.3
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{var}[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]{\bm{m}}\hat{A}_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]]

\displaystyle=n^{-2}\mathbb{E}\big[||{\bf X}\uptau_{p}||^{2}\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}|{\bf X}]\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})\tilde{\uptau}_{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-2}B\mathbb{E}\big[||{\bf X}\uptau_{p}||^{2}\tilde{\uptau}_{p}^{\prime}\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})\tilde{\uptau}_{p}\big]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}B\mathbb{E}\big[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]\uptau_{p}\big]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}B\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]\uptau_{p}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}B^{2}.

The first inequality uses the
almost sure
bound on the conditional variance (
1.3
) and the idempotence property of
\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})
. The second inequality follows because
1
is the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}_{n}({\bm{m}})
. The second equality holds by identical distribution (
2.1
), and the last inequality follows from
1.3
.

Taken together,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}]|\geq 1)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{var}[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\hat{\varepsilon}_{m,i}]]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathrm{var}[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]]+2\mathrm{var}\big[\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}]{\bm{m}}A_{m}^{-1}{\bm{m}}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2B^{2}/n

by the
c_{r}
- and Chebyshev’s inequalities.
∎

\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}y_{i}]=\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}{\bm{x}}_{i}]\bm{\beta}_{o}+\uptau_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{n}[{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\prime}\varepsilon_{i}]=O_{p}(\sqrt{p})+O_{p}(n^{-1/2})=O_{p}(\sqrt{p})

),
1.3
,
1.2
,
1.1
and
2.1
.

First, since
||\hat{A}_{m_{o}}^{-1}||=O_{p}(1)=||\hat{A}_{\hat{m}_{n}}^{-1}||
under
1.3
and that no group is empty, it follows from (
B.5
),
1.3
, and that group sizes are uniformly bounded above for all
{\bm{m}}\in\mathcal{M}_{k}

Appendix S.7
Starting schemes of
Algorithm

1

Instead of employing random starting schemes, our experiments show a faster and better convergence when informative preliminary estimates are used as starting values. Specifically, we use a model-based subgroup analysis in which a penalty function is applied to the pairwise differences of the model parameters. A modified alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm of
Boyd et al. 2011c
is used to solve the constrained optimisation to generate the starting values for
Algorithm

1
. To find the initial subgroups, we minimise the objective function

(S.71)

\displaystyle\cfrac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\bm{\beta})^{2}+\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq n}p_{\gamma}(\eta_{jj^{\prime}},\lambda)\text{ subject to }\beta_{j}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}=\eta_{jj^{\prime}},

where
p_{\gamma}(\eta_{jj^{\prime}},\lambda)
is a minimax concave penalty (MCP) and
\eta_{jj^{\prime}}
is a splitting variable. For
t>0
and
\gamma>1
, we define

\displaystyle p_{\gamma}(t,\lambda)=\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\left(1-\cfrac{u}{\gamma\lambda}\right)_{+}du,

where
(a)_{+}=\max\{0,a\}
and
\gamma
controls the level of concavity. As
\gamma\to\infty
, MCP converges to the
\ell_{1}
penalty. Therefore, like LASSO, MCP initially applies the same rate of penalisation and then smoothly relaxes the rate down to zero as the absolute value of the input increases. Hence, MCP avoids unnecessary shrinkage of large coefficients.

We solve
S.71
through the augmented Lagrangian

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{\omega}(\bm{\beta},\bm{\eta},\bm{\nu})

\displaystyle=\cfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-{\bm{x}}_{i}\bm{\beta})^{2}+\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq p}p_{\gamma}(\eta_{jj^{\prime}},\lambda)+\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq p}\nu_{jj^{\prime}}(\beta_{j}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}-\eta_{jj^{\prime}})

(S.72)

\displaystyle+\cfrac{\omega}{2}\sum_{1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq p}\lVert\beta_{j}-\beta_{j^{\prime}}-\eta_{jj^{\prime}}\rVert^{2},

where
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the dual variable and

is a tuning parameter. Let
\bm{\eta}=\{\eta_{jj^{\prime}},1\leq j<j^{\prime}\leq p\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

,

•

Step 1: Set the starting values
