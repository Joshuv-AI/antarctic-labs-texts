Learning quantum Hamiltonians at any temperature in polynomial time
Ainesh Bakshi, Allen Liu, Ankur Moitra, Ewin Tang

Abstract
We study the problem of learning a local quantum Hamiltonian H H given copies of its Gibbs state ρ = e − β ​ H / tr ⁡ ( e − β ​ H ) \rho=e^{-\beta H}/\tr(e^{-\beta H}) at a known inverse temperature β > 0 \beta>0 . Anshu, Arunachalam, Kuwahara, and Soleimanifar [ AAKS20 ] gave an algorithm to learn a Hamiltonian on n n qubits to precision ε \varepsilon with only polynomially many copies of the Gibbs state, but which takes exponential time. Giving a computationally efficient algorithm has been a major open problem [ Alh22 , AA23 ] , with prior work only resolving this in the limited cases of high temperature [ HKT22 ] or commuting terms [ AAKS21 ] . We fully resolve this problem, giving a polynomial time algorithm for learning H H to precision ε \varepsilon from polynomially many copies of the Gibbs state at any constant β > 0 \beta>0 . Our main technical contribution is a new flat polynomial approximation to the exponential function, and a translation between multi-variate scalar polynomials and nested commutators. This enables us to formulate Hamiltonian learning as a polynomial system. We then show that solving a low-degree sum-of-squares relaxation of this polynomial system suffices to accurately learn the Hamiltonian.

Our results

Surprisingly, we provide a positive resolution to

Our main result is a computationally efficient algorithm for Hamiltonian learning that works at all temperatures.
This is a fortunate development since, if learning were truly computationally hard in the low-temperature regime, then we could not understand the behavior of analog quantum simulators in precisely the regimes where they outperform classical simulators

As a consequence our main result, we also resolve

(On temperature)
.

For our algorithm, we only need to know an upper bound on

As noted by prior work

]
, Hamiltonian learning is a generalization of the classical and well-studied problem of learning undirected graphical models, specifically parameter learning of these models.
This classical problem requires

5

5

It is an interesting open question to improve our doubly exponential dependence to singly exponential.

Analogies with the classical setting turn out to be of limited use, since the non-commutativity and non-locality inherent in the quantum setting rules out generalizations of classical ideas.
However, with the classical setting we can identify barriers to designing a time-efficient algorithm.

A key challenge of time-efficient Hamiltonian learning is that we cannot work directly with the partition function.
The only previous approach to low temperature
[
AAKS20
]
only used its copies of
\rho
to estimate
\tr(E_{a}\rho)
for all
a\in[m]
.
It is known in the classical literature that taking just these estimates and using them to compute the parameters
\lambda_{a}
is as hard as computing the partition function
[
Mon15
]
.
To avoid this barrier, we take a richer set of expectations
\tr(P\rho)
that allows us to reduce learning to a tractable, but fairly involved, optimization problem instead.
Along the way we develop several new tools of independent interest, and ultimately give a semi-definite programming algorithm based on the sum-of-squares hierarchy.
Consequently, we show that sophisticated modern tools in optimization theory lead to a surprising resolution of the Hamiltonian learning problem.

1.2
Technical overview

The recipe for quantum Hamiltonian learning introduced by Anshu, Arunachalam, Kuwahara, and Soleimanifar
[
AAKS20
]
is based on matching the local marginals of the Gibbs state
\rho
, which we can estimate with copies of
\rho
.
Specifically, for two Hamiltonians
H=\sum\lambda_{a}E_{a}
and
H^{\prime}=\sum\lambda_{a}^{\prime}E_{a}
with respective Gibbs states

\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\tr(e^{-\beta H})}\mbox{ and }\rho^{\prime}=\frac{e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}}{\tr(e^{-\beta H^{\prime}})},

they show that
H=H^{\prime}
(and so
\lambda_{a}=\lambda_{a}^{\prime}
for all
a\in[m]
) if and only if
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are identical on local marginals i.e.
\tr\left\lparen E_{a}\rho\right\rparen=\tr\left\lparen E_{a}\rho^{\prime}\right\rparen
for all
a\in[m]

[
AAKS20
, Proposition 4]
.
This does not imply a bound on sample complexity, because with copies of
\rho
, we can only compute
\tr(E_{a}\rho)
approximately, with noise introduced from sampling error.
The key structural result of
[
AAKS20
]
is that this equivalence can be made robust, so that if
H^{\prime}
only approximately matches marginals, then the corresponding coefficients
\lambda_{a}^{\prime}
approximately match the true coefficients
\lambda_{a}
.

However, the last step of this algorithm is to invert the map
\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a\in[m]}\mapsto\{\tr(E_{a}\rho)\}_{a\in[m]}
, which is a computationally hard problem.
Formally, for a classical Hamiltonian,
6
6

6

A classical Hamiltonian is a Hamiltonian that is diagonal, i.e. its terms are tensor products of the identity and
\sigma_{z}
(
Definition

2.1
).
For a classical Hamiltonian, the state
\rho
is a sample from the Gibbs distribution, and
\tr(E_{a}\rho)
is a
\mathfrak{K}
-point correlation function.

\tr\left\lparen E_{a}\rho\right\rparen
are
sufficient statistics
of a graphical model and it is known that estimating the parameters of a graphical model from these sufficient statistics is computationally intractable
[
Mon15
]
.
This doesn’t mean that the problem is hopeless, but rather that to find a tractable algorithm, we should be looking for the opportunity to use a richer family of statistics.

Designing a new system of constraints.

We interpret the previous argument as defining and then solving a constraint system in the set of unknowns,

\{\lambda_{a}^{\prime}\}_{a\in[m]}
. The structural result in
[
AAKS20
]
shows that an approximate solution to this system will be close to the true parameters
\lambda_{a}
.
However, this system is computationally hard to solve. Our starting point is to define a larger set of constraints which
\{\lambda_{a}\}_{a\in[m]}
must satisfy, which can be verified by measuring expectations of observables slightly less local than the terms
\{E_{a}\}_{a\in[m]}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{\text{local}}
be the set of Pauli matrices whose support is
K
-local for some large constant
K
.
We begin with the following system of constraints:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\forall a\in[m]&-1\leqslant\lambda_{a}^{\prime}&\leqslant 1\\
&&H^{\prime}&=\sum_{a\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}\cdot E_{a}\\
&\forall P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{local}},&\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H^{\prime}}Pe^{\beta H^{\prime}}\rho\right\rparen&=\tr\left\lparen PQ\rho\right\rparen\\
\end{aligned}\right\},

(1)

The constraints above are indeed satisfied for the true parameters (
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
) since by assumption
\lvert\lambda_{a}\rvert\leqslant 1
for all
a\in[m]
and moreover

\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\rho\right\rparen=\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{\tr(e^{-\beta H})}\right\rparen=\tr\left\lparen PQ\rho\right\rparen

which follows from the cyclic property of the trace.
Two main challenges remain: Must a solution to this system be close to the true coefficients?
And how can we efficiently solve the system?
Eventually we will derive a convex relaxation for it that is based on

(A)

replacing the last constraint in
Eq.

1
which involves the matrix exponential with low degree polynomial constraints on the indeterminates (
\Set{\lambda_{a}^{\prime}}_{a\in[m]}
) instead and

(B)

showing that any choice of
\lambda^{\prime}
that satisfy the constraints must also approximately match the true coefficients
\lambda
.

In general solving systems of polynomial equations is computationally hard, but because our analysis in (B) will be based on sum-of-squares proofs, there is by now standard machinery for turning it into an efficient algorithm (see
Section

2.5.1
for detailed explanation).

Identifying an equivalence between nested commutators and polynomials.

Working towards the goal of replacing the term
\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H^{\prime}}Pe^{\beta H^{\prime}}\rho\right\rparen
with a low-degree polynomial in the variables
\lambda^{\prime}
, we begin by recalling the Hadamard formula:
7
7

7

This can be derived from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula,

\exp(A)\exp(B)=\exp\Big\lparen A+B+\frac{1}{2}[A,B]+\frac{1}{12}\lparen[A,[A,B]]+[B,[B,A]]\rparen+\dots\Big\rparen.

e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}Pe^{\beta H^{\prime}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\beta^{\ell}\frac{[H^{\prime},P]_{\ell}}{\ell!},

(2)

where
[H^{\prime},P]_{\ell}=[H^{\prime},[H^{\prime},\ldots,[H^{\prime},P]\ldots]]
is the
\ell

d

\tr\left\lparen Q\left\lparen\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\beta^{\ell}\frac{[H^{\prime},P]_{\ell}}{\ell!}\right\rparen\rho\right\rparen

is a low-degree polynomial in the variables
\lambda^{\prime}
. For instance, observe for the order-
2

\begin{split}\tr\Big\lparen\Big[\sum_{a\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}E_{a},P\Big]_{2}\rho\Big\rparen&=\tr\Big\lparen\Big[\sum_{a\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}E_{a},\sum_{a\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}[E_{a},P]\Big]\rho\Big\rparen\\
&=\sum_{a,b\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}\lambda_{b}^{\prime}\tr\left\lparen\left[E_{a},[E_{b},P]\right]\rho\right\rparen,\end{split}

which is a degree-
2
polynomial in the
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
indeterminates.
However, the series in
Eq.

2
only converges quickly when
\beta

[

]
, so we cannot use it.
8
8

8

This expansion does converge after
\beta\lVert H\rVert
terms, but our running time is exponential in the degree, so this would be far too large.

Nevertheless, from this observation we can develop a general formalism for constructing polynomial approximations of evolutions of operators.
We observe that in the eigenbasis of
H^{\prime}
,

[H^{\prime},P]_{\ell}=P\circ\Set{\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen^{\ell}}_{ij},

where
\Set{\sigma_{i}}_{i\in[N]}
are the eigenvalues of

and

denotes the Hadamard product (

2.6

Similarly,

e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}Pe^{\beta H^{\prime}}=P\circ\Set{e^{-\beta(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})}}_{ij},

and thus we can focus our attention on designing polynomials that approximate the scalar quantity
e^{-\beta\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen}
with low-degree polynomials in
\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen
. Further, any degree-
d

p(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}c_{\ell}z^{\ell}
can be extended to commutators as follows:

p(H^{\prime}\mid P)=P\circ\Set{p\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen}_{ij}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}c_{\ell}[H^{\prime},P]_{\ell}.

This allows us to translate between matrix series expansions involving nested commutators and univariate polynomials.
We note that for technical reasons we need to extend our equivalence to nested commutators with two distinct operators
X,Y
appearing in an arbitrary order, such as
[X,[Y,[X,\ldots]\ldots],E_{a_{1}}]
and bi-variate polynomials
p\left\lparen x,y\right\rparen
(
Section

3

The translation between bi-variate polynomials and nested commutators incurs additive error depending on

due to re-ordering of the

and

). In our full algorithm (

Focusing on the scalar polynomial approximation to the exponential function, we now formalize the notion of approximation that we require.

Constructing a new, flat approximation to the exponential.

Recall that we want a polynomial such that, working in the eigenbasis of
H^{\prime}
,

p(H^{\prime}\mid P)=P\circ\Set{p\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen}_{ij}\approx P\circ\Set{e^{-\beta(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})}}_{ij}=e^{-\beta H^{\prime}}Pe^{\beta H^{\prime}},

(3)

where “
\approx
” denotes an unusual notion of approximation which, for the purposes of this discussion, we can consider to mean that the matrices are close in some norm.
The Taylor series approximation to the exponential would be
Eq.

2
, which we established is too high degree.

Our key insight is that we choose a better polynomial approximation.
We begin by observing that an operator with small support is approximately band-diagonal in the basis of eigenvectors of
H
, which is a property of local terms proved by Arad, Kuwahara, and Landau
[
AKL16
]
.
We state a weak version of this here: let
P
be a Pauli operator with support size
\mathcal{O}\lparen 1\rparen
, and let
H=\sum_{i}d_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{\dagger}
be an eigendecomposition of
H
.
Then, considering
P
in the eigenbasis of
H
,

\lvert P_{ij}\rvert=\lvert v_{i}^{\dagger}Pv_{j}\rvert\leqslant e^{-\Omega\lparen\lvert d_{i}-d_{j}\rvert\rparen}.

(
Corollary

2.16
)

A consequence of this is that entries of
P\circ\Set{p\left\lparen\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}\right\rparen-e^{-\beta(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})}}_{ij}
, which denote the error of the polynomial approximation, are
weighted inverse exponentially in
\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}
.
Therefore, our polynomial approximation need not be equally good for all
\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}
; rather, our approximation should be
\varepsilon
-good in a small range but is allowed to diverge at a sufficiently slow exponential rate outside that range.
We call this a
flat
approximation.
In particular, given parameters
\beta\geqslant 0
,
0<\varepsilon,\eta<1
, we construct
p
such that

\begin{cases}\left\lvert p(z)-e^{-\beta z}\right\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon&\hskip 14.45377pt\textrm{if }z\in[-1,1]\\
\left\lvert p(z)\right\rvert\leqslant\max\left\lparen 1,e^{-\beta z}\right\rparen\cdot e^{\eta\beta\lvert z\rvert}&\hskip 14.45377pt\textrm{if }z\notin[-1,1]\end{cases}

(4)

The key difficulty in satisfying the above constraints is satisfying
|p(z)|\leqslant e^{\eta\beta|z|}
for
z\geqslant\beta
, as standard approximations like Taylor series truncations and Chebyshev series truncations fail this condition (
Remark

4.3
).
In
Section

4
, we explicitly construct a degree-
\left\lparen 2^{\mathcal{O}(1/\eta)}\cdot\left\lparen\beta+\log(1/(\varepsilon\eta))\right\rparen\right\rparen
polynomial that satisfies
Eq.

4
.
This construction is inspired by the iterative “peeling” of the exponential used in proofs of Lieb-Robinson bounds
[
LR72
,
Has10
]
.
We can write

e^{-\beta z}=\underbrace{e^{-\beta_{c}z}\cdots e^{-\beta_{c}z}}_{\beta/\beta_{c}}

for a fixed small constant
\beta_{c}
and then truncate the Taylor series expansion of
e^{-\beta_{c}z}
at different scales for all of the
\beta/\beta_{c}=O(\beta)
copies in the product so that the tails of the different truncations don’t “interfere".

We show that when
p
is a flat approximation of the above form for some sufficiently small
\eta
, then
Qp(H|P)\rho
is a good approximation to
Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\rho
.
In other words, the polynomial approximation is good when
H^{\prime}=H
and we right multiply by
\rho
; this is crucial for the polynomial system that we set up next to be feasible.

Formulating a polynomial system.

We now have all the tools to describe a polynomial system that captures the Hamiltonian learning problem. The constraint system we describe in this section is an informal treatment of the system that appears in
Section

6
, and avoids several technical details.
We show that using our flat approximation to the exponential, we can obtain a polynomial
p
such that

\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\rho\right\rparen\approx\tr\left\lparen Qp\left\lparen H\mid P\right\rparen\rho\right\rparen.

Then, we can re-write
Eq.

1
as the following polynomial constraint system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\forall a\in[m]&-1&\leqslant\lambda_{a}^{\prime}\leqslant 1\\
&&H^{\prime}&=\sum_{a\in[m]}\lambda_{a}^{\prime}E_{a}\\
&\forall P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{local}},&|\tr\left\lparen Qp\left\lparen H^{\prime}\mid P\right\rparen\rho\right\rparen&-\tr\left\lparen PQ\rho\right\rparen|\leqslant\varepsilon\end{aligned}\right\},

(5)

and observe that the last constraint encodes a relaxation of the last constraint in

1

H^{\prime}=H
.
Further, all of the constraints are indeed succinctly representable as low-degree polynomials in the indeterminates,
\Set{\lambda_{a}^{\prime}}_{a\in[m]}
, as discussed earlier.
Finally, the coefficients, such as
\tr\left\lparen[E_{a},[E_{b},P]]\right\rparen
, are expectations of the Gibbs state against a slightly larger set of local observables, which are the richer class of test functions we desired.
We can obtain estimates of these expectations through quantum measurements (
Section

5
).
Computing these estimates is the only quantum part of our algorithm, and the rest of the algorithm is entirely classical.

Feasibility of the polynomial system.

Recall that to show that the polynomial system in (
5
) is feasible, we need to argue that

\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\rho\right\rparen\approx\tr\left\lparen Qp\left\lparen H\mid P\right\rparen\rho\right\rparen

for all
P,Q
. Working in the eigenbasis of
H
, let its eigenvalues be
\Set{\sigma_{i}}_{i\in[2^{n}]}
. The key tool that we leverage is from
[
AKL16
]
(see
Corollary

2.16
) which roughly states that any local term
E
must be approximately diagonal in the eigenbasis of
H
, with off-diagonal entries decaying as
|E_{ij}|\leqslant e^{-\Omega(|\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}|)}
. Thus, we can decompose the matrices
Q,P
into two parts – parts indexed by
i,j
where
|\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}|\leqslant\beta
and parts indexed by
i,j
where
|\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}|\geqslant\beta
. Then we use the fact that
p(x)
is a good approximation to
e^{-x}
on
[-\beta,\beta]
to prove that the error on the first part is small. We then appeal to the exponential decay of the off-diagonals to argue that the contribution from the second part in both
\tr\left\lparen Qe^{-\beta H}Pe^{\beta H}\rho\right\rparen
and
tr\left\lparen Qp\left\lparen H\mid P\right\rparen\rho\right\rparen
is small. Our flat approximation to the exponential is designed to ensure that it does not overwhelm the exponential decay in the off-diagonal entries in
P,Q
in any regime.

Efficiently optimizing polynomial systems.

Now that we know that our polynomial system is feasible, we consider a convex relaxation of this system. In particular, we consider a degree-
d
sum-of-squares relaxation, which can be efficiently optimized by expressing it as a semi-definite program (see
Section

2.5.1
for details), with
d=\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\mathcal{O}(\beta)}
. Since we have
m
variables and
2^{\mathcal{O}(\beta)}
constraints, and each constraint is a degree-
d
polynomial, we can solve the degree-
2d
sum-of-squares relaxation of
Eq.

5

in
m^{\left\lparen\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{\mathcal{O}(\beta)}\right\rparen}
time.
The main challenge in analyzing the sum-of-squares relaxation is to show that we can
round
it to estimates
\Set{\tilde{\lambda}^{\prime}_{a}}_{a\in[m]}
such that they are close to the true parameters.
Here, we adopt the so-called
proofs-to-algorithms
philosophy, where we instead work with the dual object to the sum-of-squares relaxation, namely sum-of-squares proofs (see
[
Bar
,
FKP+19
]
, and references therein). This perspective states that if the true parameters are identifiable only using the sum-of-squares proof system, then we immediately obtain an efficient algorithm and we show that we can easily and accurately
round
the solution.

We then provide a proof of identifiability, i.e. for all
a\in[m]
, the inequality
\left\lparen\lambda_{a}^{\prime}-\lambda_{a}\right\rparen\leqslant\varepsilon
can be derived using the system of polynomial constraints and other basic inequalities that admit sum-of-squares proofs (we refer the reader to
Section

9
for a detailed exposition). At a high level, the proof works by arguing that when
H^{\prime}-H
is large, there are witnesses
P,Q
such that

|\tr(Qp(H^{\prime}|P)\rho)-\tr(Qp(H|P)\rho)|

is large. Since we know that
H
is a feasible solution, this would imply that
H^{\prime}
cannot be a feasible solution so any feasible solution must have
H^{\prime}-H
be small. The construction of the witnesses relies on an additional property of the polynomial
p
that we construct, namely that it is strongly monotone (in some appropriate quantitative sense).

For the identifiability proof, we crucially use an additional important property of local Hamiltonians.
It deals with the quantity
\tr(A^{2}\rho)
, where
A=\sum_{b}\sigma_{b}P_{b}
is a Hermitian linear combination of Pauli matrices with small support.
Thinking of
\rho
as a distribution,
\tr(A^{2}\rho)
is a second moment term with respect to
\rho
; we can prove this is not much smaller than
\tr(A^{2}I/\dim)=\sum_{b}\sigma_{b}^{2}
, the second moment against the uniform distribution: for some constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\tr(A^{2}\rho)\geqslant c^{\mathcal{O}\lparen\beta\rparen}\max_{b}\sigma_{b}^{2}.

(
Theorem

2.13
)

Intuitively, this shows that
\rho
is not close to zero in any local direction.
This was first shown by
[
AAKS20
]
for quasi-local operators; we adjust their proof to hold for just local operators and give a tighter bound.
We show that we can obtain a slightly weaker statement of this form in the sum-of-squares proof system by formulating it as a quadratic inequality.
This inequality can be used to remove the dependence on
\rho
in expressions appearing in the proof; for example, it is used to relate
\tr([H,H^{\prime}]^{2}\rho)
to the size of
[H,H^{\prime}]
itself.

Finally, we observe that our identifiability proof does not use the full power of a degree-
2d
sum-of-squares relaxation and therefore, it should suffice to solve a significantly smaller semi-definite program. We show that we can execute our proof of identifiability by only appealing to a sparse subset of monomials of degree at most
2d
and invoke a linearization theorem by Steurer and Teigel
[
ST21
]
to obtain a final running time of
\poly(m)\cdot\left\lparen 1/\varepsilon\right\rparen^{2^{\mathcal{O}(\beta)}}
, as desired.

1.3
Further related work

Hamiltonian learning.

Hamiltonian learning is a broad topic studied both in experimental and theoretical contexts.
This work fits into a large body of algorithmic research about learning properties of quantum states modeling physical systems
[
Cra+10
,
AA23
]
.
Here, we point to a few lines of related work in this field.

Hamiltonian learning often focuses on the real-time evolution setting, where one can allow the system to evolve with respect to
H
, applying the unitary
e^{-\mathrm{i}Ht}

[
SLP11
,
WGFC14
,
HTFS23
]
.
Some algorithms consider taking time derivatives (i.e. taking
t\to 0
), which are similar to small-
\beta
algorithms in the Gibbs state setting
[
ZYLB21
,
HKT22
,
GCC22
]
.
There is some research on learning from (zero-temperature) ground states
[
QR19
]
, but the algorithmic work is limited because the ground state of a Hamiltonian need not determine the Hamiltonian.
We study the finite temperature case, which is both the typical temperature at which experiments are run and, in the
\beta\to\infty
limit, a rich approximation to the much less computationally tractable ground state
[
Alh22
,
GHLS15
]
.

Though our algorithm is not practical, we use constraint systems that bear some similarity to the “correlation matrix” linear constraint systems analyzed heuristically and experimentally in prior work
[
BAL19
,
QR19
]
.
In fact, our constraint system contains these constraints for technical reasons.
Our work places these works on a rigorous basis, as we prove that, though the linear constraint systems might not uniquely identify the true Hamiltonian, adding more, similar constraints eventually fully constrains the Hamiltonian.

Bounding correlations in Gibbs states.

Though classical Gibbs states have extremely good locality properties, these become much weaker in the quantum setting.
A series of works aims at bounding the non-locality in quantum Gibbs states with various different measures and in various different regimes
[
KB19
,
KKB20
,
KAA21
]
, often with the goal of concluding that simulating or learning these systems can be done time-efficiently.
It is an interesting open problem whether one can extract a new kind of “locality” statement from our algorithm, to understand how general our approach is for learning quantum systems.
Our polynomial approximation is inspired by proofs of the Lieb–Robinson bound
[
LR72
,
Has10
]
, and can be viewed as a “low-degree” form of this bound.
This could be of independent interest.

Parameter learning of graphical models.

There is a rich body of work on the problem of learning graphical models.
Our setting is that of learning Markov random fields; the literature on this topic focuses on the task of
structure learning
, which in our setting corresponds to learning the terms
\{E_{a}\}_{a\in[m]}
, given the guarantee that they form an (unknown) dual interaction graph with bounded degree
[
BMS13
,
Bre15
,
HKM17
,
KM17
]
.
The problem we consider, learning the parameters with known terms, is easy in the classical setting
[
HKT22
, Appendix B]
, because classical Gibbs states satisfy the Hammersley–Clifford theorem
[
HC71
]
, also known as the Markov property.
A consequence of the Markov property is that estimating a parameter on a
\mathfrak{K}
-body term can be done by computing conditional marginals on the support of this term.

It is not clear how to generalize this argument to the quantum setting, since the Markov property does not hold for low-temperature quantum Hamiltonians, even approximately
[
KKB20
]
.

The sum-of-squares meta-algorithm.

The sum-of-squares hierarchy has been used to analyze several problems in quantum information, including best state separation
[
DPS02
,
BCY11
,
BBHKSZ12
,
BKS17
]
, optimizing fermionic Hamiltonians
[
HO22
,
Has23
]
, and a quantum analogue of max-cut
[
PT21
,
WCEHK23
]
. Additionally, the proofs-to-algorithms perspective, introduced in
[
BKS15
,
Bar
]
, has been extensively used to design efficient algorithms for several estimation and learning tasks. In particular, this perspective has led to efficient algorithms for robust learning
[
HL18
,
KSS18
,
KKM18
,
BDHKKK20
,
BP21
,
LM21
,
BDJKKV22

and list-decodable learning
[

,

,
BK21
]
.

2.1
Linear algebra

We work in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{N}
corresponding to a system of
n
qubits,
\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
, so that
N=2^{n}
.
For a matrix
A
, we use
A^{\dagger}
to denote its conjugate transpose and
\lVert A\rVert
to denote its operator norm; for a vector
v
, we use
\lVert v\rVert
to denote its Euclidean norm.
We will work with this Hilbert space, often considering it in the basis of (tensor products of) Pauli matrices.

Definition 2.1

(Pauli matrices)
.

The Pauli matrices are the following
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices.

\sigma_{I}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

These matrices are unitary and (consequently) involutory.
Further,
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{z}
,
\sigma_{y}\sigma_{z}=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{x}
, and
\sigma_{z}\sigma_{x}=\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}
, so the product of Pauli matrices is a Pauli matrix, possibly up to a factor of
\{\mathrm{i},-1,-\mathrm{i}\}
.
The non-identity Pauli matrices are traceless.
We also consider tensor products of Pauli matrices,
P_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{n}
where
P_{i}\in\{\sigma_{I},\sigma_{\textup{x}},\sigma_{\textup{y}},\sigma_{\textup{z}}\}
for all
i\in[n]
.
The set of such products of Pauli matrices, which we denote
\mathcal{P}
, form an orthogonal basis for the vector space of
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
(complex) Hermitian matrices under the trace inner product.
The product of two elements of
\mathcal{P}
is an element of
\mathcal{P}
, possibly up to a factor of
\{\mathrm{i},-1,-\mathrm{i}\}
.

Definition 2.2

(Support of an operator)
.

For an operator
P\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
on a system of
n
qubits, its
support
,
\supp(P)\subset[n]
is the subset of qubits that
P
acts non-trivially on.
That is,
\supp(P)
is the minimal set of qubits such that
P
can be written as
P=O_{\supp(P)}\otimes I_{[n]\setminus\supp(P)}
for some operator
O
.

So, for example, the support of a tensor product of Paulis,
P_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes P_{n}
are the set of
i\in[n]
such that
P_{i}\neq\sigma_{I}
.
A central object we consider is (nested) commutators of operators.

Definition 2.3

(Commutator)
.

Given operators
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, the
commutator
of
A
and
B
is defined as
[A,B]=AB-BA
.
The
nested commutator
of order
\ell
is defined recursively as
[A,B]_{k}=[A,[A,B]_{k-1}]
, with
[A,B]_{1}=[A,B]
.

Pauli matrices behave straightforwardly under commutation: the commutator of two Pauli matrices is another Pauli matrix up to a scalar (see
Lemma

2.10
).

Finally, we define the following notation to extract the piece of an operator that acts on a particular qubit.

Definition 2.4

(Localizing an operator)
.

For an operator
O\in\mathbbm{C}^{N\times N}
, define

\displaystyle O_{(i)}=O-\tr_{i}(O)\otimes\frac{I_{i}}{2},=O-\int d\mu_{i}(U)U^{\dagger}OU,

where
I_{i}
denotes the identity operator on the
i
th qubit,
\tr_{i}
denotes the partial trace operation with respect to the
i
th qubit, and
\mu_{i}
denotes the Haar measure over the set of unitaries only supported on qubit
i
.
In other words,
[\cdot]_{(i)}:(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})^{\otimes n}\to(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})^{\otimes n}
is the linear map on operators that is the identity on every qubit but
i
, and on the
i
th qubit maps
M\mapsto M-\frac{1}{2}\tr(M)I
for
M\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
.

For a tensor product of Pauli matrices,
P\in\mathcal{P}
,
P_{(i)}
is
P

and
0
otherwise.
So, for a linear combination of Paulis,
A=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\lambda_{P}P
, applying this map restricts the sum to Pauli matrices that interact with qubit

A_{(i)}=\sum_{P:i\in\supp(P)}\lambda_{P}P

(6)

So,
\lvert\supp(A_{(i)})\rvert\leqslant({\mathfrak{d}}+1)\mathfrak{K}
.

Definition 2.5

(Projector onto eigenspaces)
.

For a Hermitian matrix
X
and an interval
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
\Pi_{\mathcal{I}}^{(X)}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto the subspace spanned by eigenvectors of
X
with eigenvalues in
\mathcal{I}
.

We sometimes work in bases where matrices are diagonal, in which case Hadamard products become useful.

Definition 2.6

(Hadamard product)
.

For
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, their
Hadamard product
, denoted
A\circ B

[A\circ B]_{ij}=A_{ij}B_{ij}
.

2.2
Hamiltonians of interacting systems

We begin by defining a Hamiltonian, which encodes the interaction forces between quantum particles in a physical system.

Definition 2.7

(Hamiltonian)
.

A
Hamiltonian
is an operator
H\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
that we consider as a linear combination of local
terms

E_{a}
with associated
coefficients

\lambda_{a}

H=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\lambda_{a}E_{a}

For normalization, we assume
\lVert E_{a}\rVert\leqslant 1
and
\lvert\lambda_{a}\rvert\leqslant 1
.
This Hamiltonian is
\mathfrak{K}
-local
if every term
E_{a}
satsifies
\lvert\supp(E_{a})\rvert\leqslant\mathfrak{K}
.

We will only consider Hamiltonians whose terms

are distinct, traceless product of Pauli matrices.
Other kinds of local Hamiltonians can be reduced to this setting by expanding out local terms, which are Hermitian matrices, into the basis of products of Pauli matrices.
This preserves the locality of the Hamiltonian and only inflates the number of terms by a factor exponential in the locality, which we think of as constant.
The assumption that
E_{a}
are traceless is without loss of generality, since adding a multiple of the identity to the Hamiltonian does not affect its corresponding Gibbs state.

Definition 2.8

(Low-intersection Hamiltonian

HKT22

For a
\mathfrak{K}
-local Hamiltonian

on a system of
n

if and only if

\displaystyle\supp(E_{a})\cap\supp(E_{b})\neq\varnothing.

Let
{\mathfrak{d}}
denote the maximum degree of this graph.
We call
H

low-intersection

[

]

Throughout, we assume that our Hamiltonian is

This class is more general than geometrically local Hamiltonians, as it can extend to qubits where locality is dictated by an expander graph.

We will be considering operators other than the terms, so we define a notion of locality with respect to the dual interaction graph that generalizes to such operators.
Concretely, what we need about

2.3
Properties of local operators on quantum systems

For a Hamiltonian describing a quantum system, we consider getting access to the associated state attained by thermalizing it at a particular inverse temperature
\beta
.
This is known as a Gibbs state.

Definition 2.11

(Gibbs state)
.

The
Gibbs state
of the Hamiltonian
H=\sum\lambda_{a}E_{a}
at inverse temperature

is given by

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{\exp(-\beta H)}{\Tr\exp(-\beta H)}=\exp\biggl(-\beta\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}E_{a}\biggr)\biggr/\Tr\exp\biggl(-\beta\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}E_{a}\biggr).

(7)

A useful piece of intuition is to think of
\rho
as a distribution over its eigenspaces with probability proportional to the eigenvalue.
In that sense, we can think about expectations and variances against this distribution.

Definition 2.12

(Expectation against the Gibbs state)
.

For a Gibbs state
\rho
of a Hamiltonian
H

A\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
, we define
\langle A\rangle=\tr(A\rho)
.

A key result in the prior work of Anshu, Arunachalam, Kuwahara, and Soleimanifar gives a lower bound on the variance of a local operator with respect to the energy distribution defined by the Gibbs state.
The authors prove this for the more general class of quasi-local operators, but we only need it for local operators, for which the result can be tightened.
We give this tighter version below; its proof is in

Theorem 2.13

(
[

Let

\mathfrak{K}
-local Hamiltonian with dual interaction graph
{\mathfrak{G}}

{\mathfrak{d}}
.
Let

be a
\mathfrak{K}
’-local operator where

-1\leqslant\sigma_{b}\leqslant 1
and whose dual interaction graph has max degree
{\mathfrak{d}}^{\prime}
.

\displaystyle\langle A^{2}\rangle=\tr(A^{2}\rho)\geqslant\max_{i\in[n]}\Big\lparen c\tr(A_{(i)}^{2}/N)\Big\rparen^{6+c^{\prime}\beta}.

Here,

After some manipulation we can conclude that, if a local operator has small variance with respect to

Some may recognize this as the quantum analogue of the statement that bounded-degree graphical models have local marginals bounded away from zero (see e.g.

Corollary 2.14

\tr(X^{2}\rho)\geqslant\exp(-c_{\mathfrak{K},{\mathfrak{d}},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime},{\mathfrak{d}}^{\prime}}-c_{\mathfrak{K},{\mathfrak{d}},\mathfrak{K}^{\prime},{\mathfrak{d}}^{\prime}}^{\prime}\beta)\max_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}\sigma_{Q}^{2}

Let

\displaystyle\tr(X^{2}\rho)

2.4

One key property about commutators

We use a cluster expansion argument to bound these terms.

Definition 2.17

Let

\ell

Lemma 2.18

2.5
Sums-of-squares polynomials

We will need some preliminaries about polynomials and the sum-of-squares (SoS) framework. First, we introduce a notion of polynomials with sum-of-squares representations with bounded coefficients. We maintain coefficient bounds because later, certain sampling and approximation errors are multiplied by these coefficients. The general SoS framework is not concerned with such tight bounds on the coefficients and thus we need to make a few definition outside of the general framework.

Definition 2.21

(Sum-of-squares polynomial)
.

A polynomial

is a

.

We sometimes abbreviate sum-of-squares as SoS.

Definition 2.22

(Bounded polynomial)
.

We say a polynomial

if

(a)

for each monomial of degree

in

We say

is a

-bounded sum-of-squares polynomial

is a sum-of-squares polynomial,

-bounded.

(Composition of bounded SoS polynomials)

Pseudo-Distributions.

A discrete probability distribution over
\mathbbm{R}^{m}
is defined by its probability mass function,
D\colon\mathbbm{R}^{m}\to\mathbbm{R}
, which must satisfy
\sum_{x\in\mathrm{supp}(D)}D(x)=1
and
D\geqslant 0
.
We extend this definition by relaxing the non-negativity constraint to merely requiring that
D
passes certain low-degree non-negativity tests.
We call the resulting object a pseudo-distribution.

Definition 2.24

(Pseudo-distribution)
.

A
degree-
\ell
pseudo-distribution
is a finitely-supported function
D:\mathbbm{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
such that
\sum_{x}D(x)=1
and
\sum_{x}D(x)p(x)^{2}\geqslant 0
for every polynomial
p
of degree at most
\ell/2
, where the summation is over all
x
in the support of
D
.

Next, we define the related notion of pseudo-expectation.

Definition 2.25

(Pseudo-expectation)
.

The
pseudo-expectation
of a function
f
on
\mathbbm{R}^{m}
with respect to a pseudo-distribution
\mu
, denoted by
\widetilde{\E}_{#1}\,\left[f(x)\right]
, is defined as

\widetilde{\E}_{#1}\,\left[f(x)\right]=\sum_{x}\mu(x)f(x).

We use the notation
\widetilde{\E}_{#1}\,\left[(1,x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})^{\otimes\ell}\right]
to denote the degree-
\ell
moment tensor of the pseudo-distribution
\mu
.
In particular, each entry in the moment tensor corresponds to the pseudo-expectation of a monomial of degree at most
\ell
in
x
.

Definition 2.26

(Constrained pseudo-distributions)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\Set{p_{1}\geqslant 0,p_{2}\geqslant 0,\dots,p_{r}\geqslant 0}
be a system of

m

Let

be a degree-
\ell
pseudo-distribution over
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We say that
\mu

satisfies

\mathcal{A}
at degree

if for every subset
\mathcal{S}\subset[r]
and every sum-of-squares polynomial

such that

\widetilde{\E}_{#1}\,\left[q\prod_{i\in\mathcal{S}}p_{i}\right]\geqslant 0
.
Further, we say that
\mu

approximately satisfies

if the above inequalities are satisfied up to additive error

Up to minor technical details, our algorithm is to set up a polynomial constraint system and then call

That is, for a constraint

\widetilde{\E}_{#1}\,[p]\geqslant 0

Basic sum-of-squares proofs.

Now, we recall some basic facts about sum-of-squares proofs.
First, any univariate polynomial inequality admits a sum-of-squares proof over the reals.

Fact 2.34

(Univariate polynomial inequalities admit SoS proofs
[
Lau09
]
)
.

Let
p
be a polynomial of degree
d
.
If
p(x)\geqslant 0
for all
x\geqslant 0
, we have
\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{d}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\Set{p(x)\geqslant 0}
.
If
p(x)\geqslant 0
for all
x\in[a,b]
, then
\Set{x\geqslant a,x\leqslant b}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{d}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\Set{p(x)\geqslant 0}
.

Second, if
p\geqslant 0
and
p
is a quadratic, then this admits a sum-of-squares proof.

Fact 2.35

(Quadratic polynomial inequalities admit SoS proofs)
.

Let
p
be a polynomial in the indeterminates
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
such that
p
has degree
2
and
p\geqslant 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then
\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.53467pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2}$}}}\hskip-8.53467pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.53467pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x}$}}}$}}}\Set{p(x)\geqslant 0}
.

Proof.

Let
M
be the unique
(m+1)\times(m+1)
Hermitian matrix such that, for
v(x)=(1,x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{\dagger}
,

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=v(x)^{\dagger}Mv(x)\,.

The inequality
p\geqslant 0
implies that
M
is PSD: consider a vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m+1})\in\mathbbm{R}^{m+1}
.
If
v_{1}\neq 0
, then
v^{\dagger}Mv=p(w)\geqslant 0
for
w_{1}=v_{2}/v_{1},\dots,w_{m}=v_{m+1}/v_{1}
.
If
v_{1}=0
, then
v^{\dagger}Mv=\lim_{c\to\infty}p(c\cdot w)\geqslant 0
for
w_{1}=v_{2},\dots,w_{m}=v_{m+1}
.
This shows that
M
must be PSD, so we can write
M=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}u_{i}u_{i}^{\dagger}
for some vectors
u_{i}\in\mathbbm{R}^{m+1}
. Thus,

p(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=v(x)^{\dagger}Mv(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\langle u_{i},v(x)\rangle^{2}

which is a degree-
2
SoS polynomial and we are done.
∎

Fact 2.36

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.25113pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.25113pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[8.25113pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2}$}}}\hskip-8.25113pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.25113pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{v}$}}}$}}}\Big[v^{\dagger}Av\leqslant\lVert A\rVert\lVert v\rVert^{2}\Big].

Fact 2.37

(Almost triangle inequality)
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{r}
be indeterminates. Then

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{41.92969pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-41.92969pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[41.92969pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2t}$}}}\hskip-41.92969pt\raisebox{3.36111pt}{\makebox[41.92969pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{r}}$}}}$}}}\Set{\Big\lparen\sum_{i\leqslant r}f_{i}\Big\rparen^{2t}\leqslant r^{2t-1}\Big\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}^{2t}\Big\rparen}.

Fact 2.38

(SoS Hölder’s inequality)
.

Let
w_{1},\ldots w_{n}
be indeterminates and let
f_{1},\ldots f_{n}
be polynomials of degree
d
in the variables
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{m}
.
Let
k
be a power of 2.
Then

\Set{w_{i}^{2}=w_{i},\forall i\in[n]}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{16.77197pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-16.77197pt\raisebox{-6.82222pt}{\makebox[16.77197pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2kd}$}}}\hskip-16.77197pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[16.77197pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{x,w}$}}}$}}}\Set{\Big\lparen\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}f_{i}\Big\rparen^{k}\leqslant\Big\lparen\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\Big\rparen^{k-1}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}^{k}\Big\rparen}.

Fact 2.39

(Almost square-root)
.

Given a scalar indeterminate
v
,
\Set{v^{2}\leqslant 1}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{8.25113pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.25113pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[8.25113pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2}$}}}\hskip-8.25113pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[8.25113pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{v}$}}}$}}}\Set{-1\leqslant v\leqslant 1}
.

Proof.

We know that
\Set{\left\lparen 1-v\right\rparen^{2}=1+v^{2}-2v\geqslant 0}
and
\Set{\left\lparen 1+v\right\rparen^{2}=1+v^{2}+2v\geqslant 0}
.
By assumption,
\Set{1-v^{2}\geqslant 0}
.
So, from the addition rule we have
\Set{2+2v\geqslant 0}
and
\Set{2-2v\geqslant 0}
.
Rearranging yields the claim.
∎

3
Translating between polynomials and nested commutators

In this section, we relate nested commutators of matrices to polynomials of their associated eigenvalues.
We begin with the following basic observation mentioned in the technical overview.

Lemma 3.1

(Nested commutators to eigen-polynomials)
.

For matrices
A,B\in\mathbbm{C}^{n\times n}
, in the basis where
A
is diagonal with entries
A_{ii}=\alpha_{i}
,
AB=B\circ\{\alpha_{i}\}_{ij}
and
BA=B\circ\{\alpha_{j}\}_{ij}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle[A,B]_{k}=B\circ\{(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{k}\}_{ij}.

Further, by linearity, for a polynomial
q(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}x^{k}
,

\displaystyle\sum c_{k}[A,B]_{k}=B\circ\{q(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})\}_{ij}

In light of the above, we make the following definition associating a polynomial to an expression involving commutators of matrices.

Definition 3.2

(Univariate “commutator polynomials”)
.

For a polynomial
p(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\dots+a_{d}x^{d}
, given square matrices
X,A
of the same size, define

p(X|A)=a_{0}A+a_{1}[X,A]_{1}+\dots+a_{d}[X,A]_{d}\,.

We will need a generalization of the above that associates bivariate polynomials
p(x,y)
to expressions with nested commutators involving two matrices
X,Y
.
We will primarily be interested in the case where
X,Y
commute or are close to commuting.
We begin by generalizing the nested commutator.

Definition 3.3

(Bivariate nested commutators)
.

Let
S\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
and
X,Y,A\in\mathbbm{C}^{N\times N}
be matrices. Consider a sequence
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{\ell}
of length
\ell
where each
Z_{i}\in\{X,Y\}
and
Z_{i}=X
if and only if the
i
th entry of
S
is
0
. We define

[(X,Y)_{S},A]=[Z_{1},[Z_{2},[\dots[Z_{\ell},A]\dots]]]\,.

For a monomial
x^{i}y^{j}
, we wish to associate it to a nested commutator
[(X,Y)_{S},A]
where the number of
0
’s and
1
’s in
S
is
i
and
j
, respectively.
There are many different such commutators, reflecting that
X
and
Y
need not commute.
We will show that when
X,Y
are close to commuting, the nested commutators are also close, so the ordering in
S
does not matter.
When
\lvert S\rvert=2
, this follows from the identity below.

Fact 3.4

(Jacobi identity)
.

We have the identity
[X,[Y,A]]-[Y,[X,A]]=[[X,Y],A]
.

We extend this to higher-order commutators.

Lemma 3.5

(Reordering bivariate nested commutators)
.

For any two sequences
S,S^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\ell}
with the same number of
0
’s and
1
’s, let
t\leqslant\ell^{2}
be the number of adjacent swaps needed to transform
S
to
S^{\prime}
.
Then there are some coefficients
c_{1},\dots,c_{t}\in\{-1,1\}
, and sequences
S_{1},T_{1},\dots,S_{t},T_{t}
where
\len(S_{i})+\len(\lvert T_{i}\rvert)=\ell-2
such that

[(X,Y)_{S},A]-[(X,Y)_{S^{\prime}},A]=\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}\left[(X,Y)_{S_{i}},\left[[X,Y],\left[(X,Y)_{T_{i}},A\right]\right]\right].

Proof.

Consider when
S,S^{\prime}
differ exactly by a single swap of two adjacent elements. In this case, by Fact
3.4
, the difference on the LHS is equal to exactly one term of the form

\left[(X,Y)_{S_{i}},\left[[X,Y],\left[(X,Y)_{T_{i}},A\right]\right]\right]

where
S_{i}
is the prefix up to the point where
S,S^{\prime}
differ and
T_{i}
is the suffix. Now we can repeatedly apply this to swap adjacent elements of
S
until it matches
S^{\prime}
. Each of the residual terms is of the form given on the RHS so we are done.
∎

Now we define the correspondence between bivariate polynomials and bivariate nested commutators by arbitrarily choosing an order for the
S
associated to every monomial.

Definition 3.6

(Bivariate “commutator polynomials”)
.

Given a polynomial of degree
d
in two variables
x,y
,
p(x,y)=\sum_{i+j\leqslant d}a_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
, we define its associated matrix commutator polynomial for matrices
X,Y,A\in\mathbbm{C}^{n\times n}
as

p(X,Y|A)=\sum_{i+j\leqslant d}a_{ij}[X,[Y,A]_{j}]_{i}

The key property that we will need is that certain polynomial identities (in the original bivariate polynomials) are essentially preserved under this translation.
We begin by showing this for monomials.
The following fact shows a relation between a commutator polynomial on
A
to a commutator polynomial on
B
.

Fact 3.7
.

For any Hermitian matrix
X
and matrices
A,B,\rho
, we have the identity

\tr\left\lparen[X,A]B^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen A[X,B]^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen=-\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}[X,\rho]\right\rparen\,.

Proof.

\displaystyle\tr\left\lparen[X,A]B^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen A[X,B]^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\tr\left\lparen XAB^{\dagger}\rho-AXB^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}X\rho-AXB^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\tr\left\lparen XAB^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}X\rho\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}\rho X\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}X\rho\right\rparen

\displaystyle=-\tr\left\lparen AB^{\dagger}[X,\rho]\right\rparen\qed

This can be extended to general monomials.

Lemma 3.8

(Commutator monomial equivalences)
.

Let
p(x,y)=x^{i_{1}}y^{i_{2}}
,
q(x,y)=x^{j_{1}}y^{j_{2}}
, and
r(x,y)=p(x,y)q(x,y)
.
Let
d=\deg(r)
.
Then for some
\ell\leqslant d^{2}
, we can write

\displaystyle\tr\Big\lparen p(X,Y|A)q(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\Big\rparen-\tr\Big\lparen A\cdot r(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\Big\rparen=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Z_{i},

where every
Z_{i}
is one of the following three types of errors:

1.

\pm\tr\left(\left[(X,Y)_{S},A\right]\left[(X,Y)_{T},B\right]^{\dagger}[X,\rho]\right)
, where
\len(S)+\len(T)=d-1
;

2.

\pm\tr\left(\left[(X,Y)_{S},A\right]\left[(X,Y)_{T},B\right]^{\dagger}[Y,\rho]\right)
, where
\len(S)+\len(T)=d-1
;

3.

\pm\tr\left(A\left[(X,Y)_{S},\left[[X,Y],[(X,Y)_{T},B]\right]\right]^{\dagger}\rho\right)
, where
\len(S)+\len(T)=d-2
.

Proof.

Our goal is to express

\displaystyle\tr\left\lparen[X,[Y,A]_{i_{2}}]_{i_{1}}[X,[Y,B]_{j_{2}}]_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\Big\lparen A[X,[Y,B]_{i_{2}+j_{2}}]_{i_{1}+j_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho\Big\rparen

as a sum of errors.
Observe that
3.7
allows us to remove one copy of
X
or
Y
from the commutator in front of
A
and move it onto the commutator in front of
B
at the cost of an error term of type
1
or
2
.
Thus, we can repeatedly apply
3.7
to move all of the
X
’s and
Y
’s in the commutator in front of
A
onto the commutator in front of
B
and write

\tr\left\lparen[X,[Y,A]_{i_{2}}]_{i_{1}}[X,[Y,B]_{j_{2}}]_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen-\tr\left\lparen A[Y,[X,[Y,B]_{j_{2}}]_{j_{1}+i_{1}}]_{i_{2}}^{\dagger}\rho\right\rparen

as a sum of
i_{1}
terms of type
1
and
i_{2}
terms of type
2
.
Next, we can apply
Lemma

3.5
to reorder the sequence of
X
and
Y
on the commutator in front of
B
at the cost of error terms of type
3
. This allows us to write

\tr\Big\lparen A[Y,[X,[Y,B]_{j_{2}}]_{j_{1}+i_{1}}]_{i_{2}}^{\dagger}\rho\Big\rparen-\tr\Big\lparen A[X,[Y,B]_{i_{2}+j_{2}}]_{i_{1}+j_{2}}^{\dagger}\rho\Big\rparen

as a sum of
i_{2}(j_{1}+i_{1})
terms of type
3
.
Together, this gives the desired bound.
∎

Theorem 3.9

(Polynomial identities to nested commutator identities)
.

Consider a formal polynomial identity in two variables

p_{1}(x,y)q_{1}(x,y)+\dots+p_{k}(x,y)q_{k}(x,y)=0

where each of the polynomials
p_{i},q_{i}
is
(d,C)
-bounded. Let
X,Y\in\mathbbm{C}^{N\times N}
be Hermitian matrices and
\rho,A,B\in\mathbbm{C}^{N\times N}
be arbitrary matrices.
Then we can write

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left(p_{1}(X,Y|A)q_{1}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}+\dots+p_{k}(X,Y|A)q_{k}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\right)\rho\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}Z_{i},

\frac{\pm 1}{(\len(S)+\len(T))!}\tr\left(\left[(X,Y)_{S},A\right]\left[(X,Y)_{T},B\right]^{\dagger}[Y,\rho]\right)

\frac{\pm 1}{(\len(S)+\len(T))!}\tr\left(A\left[(X,Y)_{S},\left[[X,Y],[(X,Y)_{T},B]\right]\right]^{\dagger}\rho\right)

Proof.

Let
r_{\ell}(x,y)=p_{\ell}(x,y)q_{\ell}(x,y)
.
By the assumed formal polynomial identity,

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left(Ar_{1}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}+\dots+Ar_{k}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\right)\rho\right)=0.

So, it suffices to express each product

\displaystyle\tr\left(p_{\ell}(X,Y|A)q_{\ell}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\right)-\tr\left(Ar_{\ell}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\right)

as a linear combination of errors.
Let
p_{\ell}=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}a_{\ell,i_{1},i_{2}}x^{i_{1}}y^{i_{2}}
and
q_{\ell}=\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}}b_{\ell,j_{1},j_{2}}x^{j_{1}}y^{j_{2}}

\displaystyle\tr\left(p_{\ell}(X,Y|A)q_{\ell}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\right)-\tr\left(Ar_{\ell}(X,Y|B)^{\dagger}\rho\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}}a_{\ell,i_{1},i_{2}}\cdot b_{\ell,j_{1},j_{2}}\Big\lparen\tr([X,[Y,A]_{i_{2}}]_{i_{1}}\cdot[X,[Y,B]_{j_{2}}]_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho)-\tr(A\cdot[X,[Y,B]_{i_{2}+j_{2}}]_{i_{1}+j_{1}}^{\dagger}\rho)\Big\rparen\,.

Lemma

3.8
shows how to expand summand into error terms; note that the degree of the product is

There are at most

error terms per summand, and there are

summands per product

.
This gives a total bound on the number of error terms as

as desired.
All that is left is to bound the size of the coefficients.
For an error term of any type associated to a particular summand, the coefficient is
a_{\ell,i_{1},i_{2}}b_{\ell,j_{1},j_{2}}
.
Let

be the sequences associated to the error term.
Because

and

are

-bounded, we can bound

\displaystyle\lvert a_{\ell,i_{1},i_{2}}b_{\ell,j_{1},j_{2}}\rvert\leqslant\frac{C^{2}}{(i_{1}+i_{2})!(j_{1}+j_{2})!}\leqslant\frac{C^{2}2^{2d}}{(i_{1}+i_{2}+j_{1}+j_{2})!}\leqslant\frac{C^{2}2^{2d}}{(\len(S)+\len(T))!}.

The last inequality holds because, in all error types,

4
Polynomial approximations of the exponential

Our goal for this section is to construct a polynomial approximation to

that satisfies the properties necessary for our algorithm.
In particular, we need a polynomial that approximates

on an interval
[-\kappa,\kappa]
, but with the additional property that the polynomial does not grow too quickly outside the area of approximation.

Definition 4.1

(Flat approximation of the exponential)
.

Given
\varepsilon,\eta\in(0,1)
and

The canonical polynomial approximation of

Definition 4.2

Let

.

The Taylor series truncation

This is because the truncation blows up too quickly on the negative tail:

The same issue occurs with conventional approximations of the exponential, like truncations of the Chebyshev series and QSVT-style “bounded” approximations of the Chebyshev series

While standard techniques for approximating the exponential do not suffice, we construct a modified polynomial that is a flat approximation to the exponential.

5
Accessing Gibbs states: the quantum piece

We now describe how our algorithm uses its copies of
\rho
.
This is the only location in the algorithm where we access
\rho
; the rest of the algorithm is entirely classical.
By making measurements on copies of
\rho
, we can estimate expectations of observables
\tr(X\rho)
for various choices of the matrix

. These estimates will then be used in the learning algorithm.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X=UDU^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
be a unitarily diagonalizable matrix, and suppose we are given copies of a quantum state with density matrix
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
.
Then we can estimate
\tr(X\rho)
to
\varepsilon\lVert X\rVert
error with probability
\geqslant 1-\delta
using
\mathcal{O}\lparen\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\rparen
copies of
\rho
.
The running time is the number of samples times the cost of applying
U^{\dagger}
to a quantum state and measuring in the computational basis.

Proof.

Consider taking
\rho
and applying
U^{\dagger}
to form
U^{\dagger}\rho U
, then measuring in the computational basis.
We see the outcome
i
with probability
\bra{u_{i}}\rho\ket{u_{i}}
, where
u_{i}
is the
i
th column of
U
.
Let
z
be the random variable attained by performing this measurement and taking
z=D_{i,i}
; then
\E[z]=\sum_{i}D_{i,i}\bra{u_{i}}\rho\ket{u_{i}}=\tr(X\rho)
, and
z
is always bounded by
\max_{i}\lvert D_{i,i}\rvert=\lVert X\rVert
.
By a Chernoff bound we conclude that averaging
\mathcal{O}\lparen\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\rparen
independent copies of this estimator gives the desired error bound.
∎

We wish to estimate
\tr(X\rho)
for many different Pauli observables

, so we could simply run

for each one.
However, we can use a classical shadows-like procedure
[

]
to estimate them all at once.

Lemma 5.2

(Computing expectations of observables of Gibbs states)
.

Let
H=\sum_{a=1}^{m}\lambda_{a}E_{a}
be a
\mathfrak{K}
-local Hamiltonian on
n
qubits whose dual interaction graph
{\mathfrak{G}}
has maximum degree
{\mathfrak{d}}
.
Consider the set of
\ell

-local Paulis,

.
There is a quantum algorithm that, with probability
\geqslant 1-\delta

to
\varepsilon

This algorithm uses

samples,
\mathcal{O}\lparen nt\rparen
quantum gates, and
\poly(t,n,\lvert P_{\ell}\rvert)

Proof.

Consider the following procedure, where we take as input a copy of
\rho

Take

(23)

\Set{\mathcal{C}_{\lambda^{\prime}}}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{11.44789pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\lambda^{\prime}}$}}}$}}}\Biggl\{\tr\left\lparen[H,H^{\prime}](H^{\prime}\rho-\rho H^{\prime})\right\rparen\geqslant-m^{2}(10^{\mathfrak{K}}{\mathfrak{d}})^{5}\varepsilon_{0}\Biggr\},

Lemma 8.2

(Lower bounding the commutator at arbitrary temperature)
.

Given
0<\beta
,
H^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in[m]}\lambda_{i}^{\prime}E_{i}
,
H=\sum_{i\in[m]}\lambda_{i}E_{i}
and
\rho=e^{-\beta H}

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{11.44789pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\lambda^{\prime}}$}}}$}}}\Set{\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta\lVert H\rVert}\tr\left\lparen(\mathrm{i}[H^{\prime},H])^{2}\rho\right\rparen\leqslant\tr\left\lparen[H^{\prime},H]\left\lparen H^{\prime}\rho-\rho H^{\prime}\right\rparen\right\rparen}.

Proof.

Consider the basis where
H
is diagonal and let its eigenvalues be
\sigma_{i}
. Let
Z=\tr(e^{-\beta H})
. Then,

\begin{split}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{11.44789pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\lambda^{\prime}}$}}}$}}}\Biggl\{\tr\lparen[H^{\prime},H](H^{\prime}\rho-\rho H^{\prime})\rparen&=\tr\lparen(H^{\prime}H-HH^{\prime})(H^{\prime}\rho-\rho H^{\prime})\rparen\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\tr\lparen(H^{\prime}\circ\{\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i}\}_{ij})(H^{\prime}\circ\{e^{-\beta\sigma_{j}}-e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\}_{ij})\rparen\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}H_{ij}^{\prime}H_{ji}^{\prime}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})(e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}-e^{-\beta\sigma_{j}})\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})(e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}-e^{-\beta\sigma_{j}})\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})(1-e^{-\beta(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})})e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\Biggr\}\end{split}

(30)

By a similar argument,

\begin{split}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{11.44789pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\lambda^{\prime}}$}}}$}}}\Biggl\{\tr\left\lparen(\mathrm{i}[H^{\prime},H])^{2}\rho\right\rparen&=\frac{1}{Z}\tr\lparen(H^{\prime}\circ\{\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i}\}_{ij})(H^{\prime}\circ\{(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})e^{-\beta\sigma_{j}}\}_{ij})\rparen\\
&=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})^{2}e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\Biggr\}\,.\end{split}

(31)

We observe that, since
e^{x}\geqslant 1+x
,
e^{-x}\leqslant\frac{1}{1+x}
, and so

\displaystyle x(1-e^{-\beta x})\geqslant\lvert x\rvert(1-e^{-\beta\lvert x\rvert})\geqslant\frac{\beta x^{2}}{1+\lvert\beta x\rvert}.

Applying this inequality with
x=\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i}
, which are constants in the sum-of-squares proof system, and substituting back into (
30
), we have,

\begin{split}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{11.44789pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{-1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-11.44789pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[11.44789pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\lambda^{\prime}}$}}}$}}}\Biggl\{\tr\lparen[H^{\prime},H](H^{\prime}\rho-\rho H^{\prime})\rparen&=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})(1-e^{-\beta(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})})e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\\
&\geqslant\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})^{2}\frac{\beta}{1+\lvert\beta\rvert\lvert\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i}\rvert}e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\\
&\geqslant\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta\lVert H\rVert}\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}^{\prime}\rvert^{2}(\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i})^{2}e^{-\beta\sigma_{i}}\\
&=\frac{\beta}{1+2\beta\lVert H\rVert}\tr\left\lparen(\mathrm{i}[H^{\prime},H])^{2}\rho\right\rparen\Biggr\},\end{split}

(32)

where the second inequality follows from
\lvert\sigma_{j}-\sigma_{i}\rvert\leqslant 2\lVert H\rVert
and the last equality follows from (
31
).

∎
