Abstract
. This survey revolves around the question how the roots of a monic polynomial (resp. the spectral decomposition of a linear operator), whose coefficients depend in a smooth way on parameters, depend on those parameters. The parameter dependence of the polynomials (resp. operators) ranges from real analytic over C ∞ C^{\infty} to differentiable of finite order with often drastically different regularity results for the roots (resp. eigenvalues and eigenvectors). Another interesting point is the difference between the perturbation theory of hyperbolic polynomials (where, by definition, all roots are real) and that of general complex polynomials. The subject, which started with Rellich’s work in the 1930s, enjoyed sustained interest through time that intensified in the last two decades, bringing some definitive optimal results. Throughout we try to explain the main proof ideas; Rellich’s theorem and Bronshtein’s theorem on hyperbolic polynomials are presented with full proofs. The survey is written for readers interested in singularity theory but also for those who intend to apply the results in other fields.

1.
Introduction

The question how the spectral decomposition of a linear operator in Hilbert space depends on parameters of the operator
is a natural and important one. Its investigation – stimulated without doubt by the development of quantum mechanics –
began with Rellich’s paper
[
99
]
published in 1937
(the first in a series which culminated in Rellich’s book
[
100
]
).
In finite dimensions, the problem reduces to the questions about the regularity of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
families of matrices and, in consequence, one is led to ask how regular the roots of a monic polynomial depending on parameters
can be chosen as functions of these parameters.

In this survey, we will concentrate on the finite dimensional setting of matrices and polynomials; occasionally we will provide
references for generalizations to operators in infinite dimensional Hilbert space.

Real analytic perturbations of normal matrices and hyperbolic polynomials

In
[
99
]
, Rellich was concerned with analytic families of linear operators, in particular, he proved that
real analytic curves of Hermitian matrices admit real analytic eigenvalues and a real analytic frame of orthonormal eigenvectors.
To this end, he showed that a real analytic curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials admits a real analytic choice of its roots.
A monic polynomial
P_{a}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}
with real coefficients is called
hyperbolic

if all
d
roots
of
P_{a}
(with multiplicities) are real.

Rellich also realized that the analytic dependence of the spectral decomposition breaks down as soon as the Hermitian matrix
depends on two or more real parameters. The analytic multiparameter case was taken up and continued
by Kurdyka and Păunescu
[
64
]
: there exists an analytic modification of the parameter space such that, after this
modification, the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors (or the roots in the case of hyperbolic polynomials)
admit real analytic parameterizations, locally.
Actually, these results hold in the more general setting of normal matrices, as observed by Rainer
[
94
]
,
where the eigenvalues are generally complex valued. We discuss the analytic perturbation theory in Section
2
.

Singularity theory methods

Many results of perturbation theory can be proven using singularity theory.
Puiseux’s theorem is an important ingredient of Rellich’s proof and the multiparameter case is proven using its multivariable counterpart,
the Abhyankar–Jung theorem. In Section
2
we present a different proof of Rellich’s theorem on perturbation of hyperbolic polynomials,
based on a nowadays standard singularity theory tool, the splitting, which we also recall briefly.
Similar methods are used in the multiparameter case and in the study of the perturbation of normal matrices,
see Parusiński and Rond
[
88
]
,
where the splitting is replaced by a version of Hensel’s lemma for normal matrices, or in the problem of lifting over invariants of group
representations,
where it is replaced by the slice theorem (see Theorem
5.9
). We describe how
this method is used in several places of the survey.

In the multiparameter case, the resolution of singularities is used to make ideals principal generated by normal crossings.
In most cases, this is the ideal generated by the discriminant of
P_{a}
, or of the square-free reduction
(P_{a})_{\operatorname{red}}
, so that one can use then the Abhyankar–Jung theorem.
Interestingly, the resolution of singularities is used as well to show that the continuous roots of
P_{a}
, with
a=a(t)

depending smoothly on
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
are locally absolutely continuous,
see Section
4.3
and Parusiński and Rainer
[
83
]
.

In the case of
C^{\infty}
coefficients (or coefficients of finite differentiability),
besides splitting, another important ingredient is Glaeser’s inequality and its generalizations.
We recall some of its manifestations in
Section
3
. The higher order Glaeser inequalities appear in
Section
4
.

C^{\infty}
perturbations of normal matrices and hyperbolic polynomials

Another field of application which greatly stimulated the research on the regularity problem for roots of polynomials is the theory of PDEs –
most notably the Cauchy problem for hyperbolic PDOs.
With this goal in mind, Bronshtein
[
21
]
proved that any continuous root of a monic hyperbolic polynomial
P_{a}
of degree

d
with coefficients
C^{d-1,1}
functions
a_{j}:U\to\mathbb{R}
, where
U
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
is locally Lipschitz. (Glaeser’s inequality gives Bronshtein’s result in the simplest nontrivial case.)
Note that ordering the roots
P_{a}
increasingly yields a continuous system of the roots.
This result is sharp. If
n=1
and the coefficients are
C^{d}
(resp.
C^{2d}
), then there is a
C^{1}
(resp. twice differentiable)
choice of the roots, but there are examples of nonnegative
C^{\infty}
functions
f
such that
Z^{2}=f
has no
C^{1,\alpha}
solution
for any
\alpha>0
. We give a full proof of Bronshtein’s theorem with uniform bounds for the Lipschitz constant in Section
3.2

(it is based on Parusiński and Rainer
[
81
]
).

The step from analytic to
C^{\infty}
coefficients entails a big drop of regularity for the roots, because it allows for oscillation.
The same is true for the eigenvalues of Hermitian and, more generally, normal matrices; for the eigenvectors it is even worse:
there are
C^{\infty}
curves of symmetric
2\times 2
matrices that do not admit a continuous system of eigenvectors.
On the other hand, the conditions that guarantee locally Lipschitz or
C^{1}
eigenvalues of Hermitian (even normal) matrices
are much weaker than for hyperbolic polynomials: for instance, a
C^{0,1}
(resp.
C^{1}
) curve of Hermitian (or, more generally, normal) matrices admits a
system of
C^{0,1}
(resp.
C^{1}
) eigenvalues. We explore several explanations for this phenomenon: one of them is that
the determinant is a Gårding hyperbolic polynomial with respect to the identity matrix on the real vector space of

d\times d
Hermitian matrices (see Section
3.4
and Section
3.5
).
This also gives that
C^{1,1}
curves of Hermitian matrices admit eigenvalues that are locally of Sobolev class
W^{2,1}

(which is optimal in view of the Sobolev inequality).

There are different approaches (discussed in Section
3.3
) to ascertain better
C^{p}
regularity of the roots and the eigenvalues
by imposing stronger assumptions.
For instance, given any integer
p\geq 2
, forcing
f\geq 0
defined on
\mathbb{R}
and sufficiently many of its derivatives to vanish on all local minima of
f

guarantees that the equation
Z^{2}=f
has a
C^{p}
solution.
Or, one can define a regularity class
\mathcal{F}^{\beta}
,
\beta>0
, of nonnegative
C^{\beta}
functions on
\mathbb{R}
, incorporating flatness of
f
near its zeros
in a certain sense,
such that
f^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{F}^{\alpha\beta}
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\beta}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
In the case of a curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials or normal matrices,
sufficient conditions for the existence of
C^{p}
roots or eigenvalues can be given in terms of the differentiability of the coefficients and the
finite order of contact of the roots or eigenvalues. If the coefficients are
C^{\infty}
and definable in any (not necessarily polynomially bounded)
o-minimal expansion of the real field,
then the roots and eigenvalues can be chosen
C^{\infty}
and definable; this reaffirms that oscillation is to blame for the loss of regularity.

Perturbations of polynomials. General (nonhyperbolic) case

Other problems from PDEs (cf.
[
106
]
) and geometric analysis demand to abandon the hyperbolicity assumption:
how regular can the roots of a general (nonhyperbolic) monic polynomial
P_{a}
with smooth coefficients
a_{j}:U\to\mathbb{C}
be?
This problem was solved only recently by Ghisi and Gobbino
[
37
]
in the radical case and
by Parusiński and Rainer
[
83
,
84
,
85
]
in the general case.
We present in Section
4
two approaches to this problem: one is based on formulas
for the roots of the universal monic polynomial of degree
d
, obtained by resolution of singularities, the other one is elementary and yields the optimal Sobolev regularity result.
It states that the roots of a curve of monic complex polynomials
P_{a}
of degree
d

with coefficients of class
C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I})
, where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a bounded open interval, admit an absolutely continuous parameterization whose derivative is in
L^{p}(I)
for each
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
.
Actually, each continuous root has this property, in particular, it belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,p}(I)
for each
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
.
Moreover, there are uniform bounds for the Sobolev norm of the roots in terms of the
C^{d-1,1}
norm of the coefficients. This result is optimal.

A multiparameter version for continuous roots follows by standard arguments, provided there exists a continuous root (perhaps on some subset).
But in general there are obstructions for continuous roots, due to monodromy.
It turns out that the possibly discontinuous roots still can be represented by functions of bounded variation, cf. Section
4.4.1
.
The proof uses the formulas for the roots of the universal monic polynomial alluded to above.

Lifting from the orbit space

The problem of finding roots with optimal regularity of monic (hyperbolic and nonhyperbolic) polynomials
has a representation-theoretic interpretation: it is equivalent to a lifting problem over the orbit map
\sigma
of the
standard representation of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, respectively, where the components of
\sigma

are the elementary symmetric functions. This point of view can be generalized considerably: we will discuss in Section
5
the lifting problem
for real orthogonal representations of compact Lie groups and for complex rational representations of
linearly reductive groups (the representation spaces will always be finite dimensional).
In this general setting, the lifting occurs over a map
\sigma
consisting of basic invariants, that is a finite collection of generators of the algebra of invariant
polynomials.
It gives another heuristic explanation why the eigenvalues of symmetric matrices are better behaved than the roots of monic hyperbolic
polynomials: only a “partial lifting” is necessary (see Section
5.4.1
).

Applications

In the last section, Section
6
, we give two applications of the results for general (nonhyperbolic) polynomials.
The first concerns the zero set of
C^{\infty}
functions
f
.
If
f
vanishes to some finite order
\gamma
at a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
then locally near
x_{0}
the zero set of
f
is given by the real roots of a monic polynomial
P_{a}
of degree
\gamma
,
by Malgrange’s preparation theorem.
We present several consequences of the optimal Sobolev regularity of the roots of
P_{a}
,
most of them observed by Beck, Becker-Kahn, and Hanin
[
10
]
.
Often one knows in advance the vanishing order of a function so that this setup applies,
e.g., for solutions of second order elliptic equations, Laplace eigenfunctions or finite linear combinations of such.

The second application is taken from the recent paper
[
5
]
by Antonini, Cavalletti, and Lerario.
This paper brings forward a reinterpretation and an extension of the regularity problem for the roots of polynomials
to the study of the Wasserstein distance on the space of
d
-degree hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
This space is embedded in the space of measures on the projective space
and thus the optimal transport problem between hypersurfaces is studied.
An inner Wasserstein distance is obtained which turns the projective space of homogeneous polynomials in
a complete geodesic space.

In the appendix, we collect definitions and basics on function spaces used in the survey (Section
7
) and
we present some topological and geometric properties of the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of a fixed degree (Section
8
).
There are several open problems that we included all over the article.

2.
Perturbation theory with real analytic coefficients

2.1.
Rellich’s theorems

In 1937 F. Rellich proved the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(Rellich
[
99
, Satz I]
,
[
100
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
A(t)
be a family of
d\times d
Hermitian matrices whose coefficients are real analytic in
t\in I
,
where
I
is an open neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
. Then, maybe in a smaller neighborhood of
0
, the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
A(t)
can be chosen real analytic in
t
.

More precisely, there exist real valued functions

\lambda_{i}(t)
and complex valued
d
-vectors
v_{i}(t)
,
i=1,\ldots,d
,
depending analytically on
t
, such that for
t
in an open neighborhood
I^{\prime}
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
:

(1)

v_{1}(t),\ldots,v_{d}(t)
is an orthonormal frame in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
;

(2)

A(t)v_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}(t)v_{i}(t)
.

Note that the statement of this theorem is local.
It was originally stated and proven in terms of convergent power series but clearly, by unique analytic continuation,
it implies a global statement on an arbitrary open interval
I
and with
I^{\prime}=I
. For proving the above theorem, Rellich shows a result that we rephrase as follows.

Theorem 2.2

(Rellich
[
99
]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
and let

P_{a}(t)(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}(t)Z^{d-j},\quad t\in I,

be a monic hyperbolic polynomial with real analytic coefficients
a_{j}
.
Then there exist real analytic functions
\lambda_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
,
defined maybe in a smaller neighborhood
I^{\prime}
of
0
in
\mathbb{R}
,
which parameterize the roots of
P_{a}
, i.e.,

P_{a}(t)(Z)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(Z-\lambda_{i}(t)),\quad t\in I^{\prime}.

Actually, Rellich states this result for complex analytic functions that have real values for real
t
,
see Rellich
[
99
, Hilfssatz II]
. Again, for real
t
, it is easy to see that it holds globally, that is for an arbitrary
I
and with
I^{\prime}=I
.

Rellich’s proof of Theorem
2.2
is based on Puiseux’s theorem. Its idea goes as follows. Let
\lambda_{i}(t)
be the complex roots of
P_{a}(t)
. By Puiseux’s theorem, there is
l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\eta_{i}(s)=\lambda_{i}(s^{l})
are all complex analytic, but also, by the assumption, every
\eta_{i}(s)
is real whenever
s^{l}
is real. But this means that
\eta_{i}
, as a power series, depends only on
s^{l}
. Indeed, suppose this is not true and let
c_{k}s^{k}
be the lowest degree term in the expansion of
\eta_{i}
such that
l
does not divide
k
.
Then
\eta_{i}
can not be real for all
s
such that
s^{l}
is real.
(Rellich gives a slightly different argument still based on Puiseux’s theorem in
[
100
, pages 30-31]
.)

Then to show Theorem
2.1
Rellich, for each analytic eigenvalue
\lambda(t)
of
A(t)
, solves the equation
A(t)v=\lambda(t)v
using formulas involving the minors of the matrix
A(t)
. We refer the reader to Rellich’s paper for this elementary argument.

Remark 2.3
.

It was observed by Rainer
[
94
]
that Theorem
2.1
holds as well for normal matrices.
This shows, surprisingly, that it is not the hyperbolicity of the characteristic polynomial of
A(t)
that is
important but the fact that we can diagonalize the matrices in orthonormal bases, but see also Example
2.16
.
This was made even more transparent in the proof for normal matrices given in
[
88
]
,
where analytic diagonalizibility is showed directly without proving first the analyticity of the eigenvalues. See also Section

.

2.2.
A proof of Theorem
2.2

We present here a different proof based on the splitting principle.

2.2.1.
Tschirnhausen form

Every monic real polynomial

P_{a}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}

of degree
d
can be identified with the point
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that
P_{a}
is in
Tschirnhausen form
if
a_{1}=0
.
Every
P_{a}
can be put in Tschirnhausen form by the substitution

P_{\tilde{a}}(Z)=P_{a}(Z-\tfrac{a_{1}}{d})=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j}.

We call it
Tschirnhausen transformation
.
Note that

(1)

\tilde{a}_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}C_{i}a_{i}a_{1}^{j-i},\quad 2\leq j\leq d,

where the
C_{i}
are universal constants and
a_{0}=1
.
For a polynomial
P_{\tilde{a}}
in Tschirnhausen form we have

-2\tilde{a}_{2}=\lambda_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}^{2},

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
are the roots of
P_{\tilde{a}}
(with multiplicities),
consequently, for a hyperbolic polynomial,

\tilde{a}_{2}\leq 0.

Recall that the coefficients (up to their sign) are the elementary symmetric polynomials in the roots, by Vieta’s formulas.

Lemma 2.4
.

The coefficients of a hyperbolic polynomial
P_{\tilde{a}}
in Tschirnhausen form satisfy

(2)

|\tilde{a}_{j}|^{1/j}\leq\sqrt{2}\,|\tilde{a}_{2}|^{1/2},\quad j=1,\ldots,d.

Proof.

Let
s_{k}:=\lambda_{1}^{k}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}^{k}
,
k\geq 1
, be the Newton polynomials in
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
.
We claim that

(3)

|s_{k}|^{1/k}\leq|s_{2}|^{1/2},\quad k=2,\ldots,d.

Setting
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
, we
have, for
k\geq 2
,

|s_{k}|^{1/k}\leq\|\lambda\|_{k}\leq\|\lambda\|_{2}=|s_{2}|^{1/2},

by a well-known relation between the
p
-norms.
The Newton identities between the Newton polynomials and the elementary symmetric polynomials
\sigma_{k}
,

j\sigma_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(-1)^{i-1}\sigma_{j-i}s_{i},\quad d\geq j\geq 1,

imply

|\tilde{a}_{j}|\leq\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i=2}^{j}|\tilde{a}_{j-i}||s_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i=2}^{j}|\tilde{a}_{j-i}||s_{2}|^{i/2}.

By (
3
), it is now
easy to conclude the statement using induction.
∎

2.2.2.
Splitting

The following well-known lemma can be found for instance in
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
P_{a}=P_{b}P_{c}
, where
P_{b}
and
P_{c}
are monic real polynomials without common (complex) root.
Then
we have
P=P_{b(P)}P_{c(P)}
for analytic mappings
P\mapsto b(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{b}}
and
P\mapsto c(P)\in\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{c}}
,
defined for
P
near
P_{a}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\deg P_{a}}
, with the given initial values.

Proof.

The product
P_{a}=P_{a}P_{c}
defines on the coefficients a polynomial map
\varphi
such that
a=\varphi(b,c)
.
Its Jacobian determinant
\det\operatorname{Jac}_{\varphi}(b,c)
equals the resultant of
P_{b}
and
P_{c}
which is nonzero, by assumption.
Thus
\varphi
can be inverted locally, by the inverse function theorem.
∎

We denote by
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
.
Let
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
be the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
in Tschirnhausen form
and let
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
be the subspace of polynomials
P_{\tilde{a}}
with
\tilde{a}_{2}=-1
.
By Lemma
2.4
,
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
is compact.
Every polynomial in
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
can be written as a product of two monic real polynomials of positive degree without common root
(indeed, the sum of the roots vanishes while the sum of their squares is
2
).

Let us now prove Theorem
2.2
.
The case
\tilde{a}_{2}=0
is trivial, since then all
\tilde{a}_{j}=0
. Thus let
P_{\tilde{a}}\in\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)
be such that
\tilde{a}_{2}\neq 0
. Since
-\tilde{a}_{2}(t)
is positive,
it is of the form
-\tilde{a}_{2}(t)=t^{2k}u(t)
,
u(0)>0
. Choose one of the
two analytic roots of
-\tilde{a}_{2}(t)
,

\pm t^{k}u^{\frac{1}{2}}(t)
, and denote it by
\theta(t)
.
Then the polynomial

Q_{\underline{a}}(Z):=\theta^{-d}P_{\tilde{a}}(\theta Z)=Z^{d}-Z^{d-2}+\sum_{j=3}^{d}\theta^{-j}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j}

belongs to
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}^{0}(d)
.
By Lemma
2.5
,
we have

Q_{\underline{a}}=Q_{\underline{b}}Q_{\underline{c}},

on some open ball
B(P_{\tilde{a}},r)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that
\deg Q_{\underline{b}}<d
,
\deg Q_{\underline{c}}<d
, and

\underline{b}_{i}=\psi_{i}(\theta^{-3}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\theta^{-d}\tilde{a}_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,\deg Q_{\underline{b}},

where
\psi_{i}
are real analytic functions; likewise for
\underline{c}_{i}
.
Note that, by Lemma
2.4
,
\theta^{-i}\tilde{a}_{i}
are real analytic and hence so are
b_{i}
and
c_{i}
.
If
Q_{\underline{a}}
is hyperbolic, then also
Q_{\underline{b}}
and
Q_{\underline{c}}
are hyperbolic.
This induces a splitting

(4)

\displaystyle P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c},\quad\text{ on }B(P_{\tilde{a}},r),

where

b_{i}=\theta^{i}\psi_{i}(\theta^{-3}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\theta^{-d}\tilde{a}_{d}),\quad i=1,\dots,\deg P_{b},

are real analytic functions of
t
and likewise for
c_{i}
.
To conclude we proceed by induction on
\deg P_{a}
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

At first the above proof looks more complicated than
Rellich’s original argument. This is because
Rellich uses Puiseux’s theorem, that can be proven itself using the splitting.
But the splitting allows to work with coefficients
a_{i}
that are not necessarily real analytic, but

C^{p}
or
C^{\infty}
for instance, and that cannot be easily complexified. It can also be used in the
multiparameter case.

It is natural to ask what happens if the entries of a Hermitian matrix are
C^{\infty}
functions of
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, in general, there is a drastic drop of regularity for the eigenvalues (this will be discussed in detail in Section
3.5
and Section
3.7
),
while the eigenvectors may not even admit a continuous choice, as seen in the following example, see also Example
3.48
.

Example 2.7

(
[
99
, §2.2]
)
.

The
C^{\infty}
curve of symmetric matrices

A(t):=e^{-1/t^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\cos t^{-1}&\sin t^{-1}\\
\sin t^{-1}&-\cos t^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\neq 0,

and
A(0):=0

has the
C^{\infty}
eigenvalues
\pm e^{-1/t^{2}}
, but the normalized eigenvectors cannot be continuous at
t=0
.

Under the additional assumption that no two continuous eigenvalues meet to infinite order of flatness, Rellich’s theorem
2.1

still holds in the
C^{\infty}
category; see
[
2
, Theorem 7.6]
and
[
94
]
for normal matrices.

2.3.
Multiparameter case

Both Rellich’s theorems fail in the multiparameter case.

Example 2.8

(Rellich
[
99
, §2, 1]
)
.

The eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix

A(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}1+2x_{1}&x_{1}+x_{2}\\
x_{1}+x_{2}&1+2x_{2}\end{pmatrix},

\lambda_{\pm}=1+x_{1}+x_{2}\pm\sqrt{2(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})}
, are not analytic in any neighborhood of
(0,0)
.

In
[
64
]
K. Kurdyka and L. Păunescu proposed multiparameter versions of Rellich’s theorems. The following is a multiparameter generalization of Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
64
, Theorems 4.1 and 5.8]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and

P_{a}(x)(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}(x)Z^{d-j},\quad x\in U,

be a monic hyperbolic polynomial with real analytic coefficients
a_{j}
.
Then there exist locally Lipschitz functions
\lambda_{i}:U\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
,
which parameterize the roots of
P_{a}
.

Moreover, there exists a modification
\sigma:W\to U
(by a locally finite composition of
blowings-up with global smooth centers) such that the roots of
P_{a}(\sigma(w))(Z)
can be parameterized, locally on
W
, by real analytic functions.

Since all roots of
P_{a}(x)
are real, we can order them

\lambda_{1}(x)\leq\lambda_{2}(x)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{d}(x)

and the first claim of the above theorem implies that all
such
\lambda_{i}
are locally Lipschitz.

Example 2.10

(
[
64
, Example 5.9]
)
.

Consider
P_{a}(x)(Z)=Z^{2}-(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})
. The zero set of
P_{a}
is the double cone.
Then both the upper and the lower nappes are graphs of Lipschitz functions. But note that if we restrict them to a line through the origin
these restrictions are not real analytic. We have to interchange them while passing through the origin to get analytic roots
(that exist by
Rellich’s theorem).

If we blow up the origin in the real plane, then the roots become locally, but not globally, real analytic. Indeed,
in the chart
x_{1}=w_{1},x_{2}=w_{1}w_{2}
the roots are
\pm w_{1}\sqrt{1+w_{2}^{2}}
.
Going along the exceptional divisor,
the center circle of the Möbius band, changes the sign of the root, i.e., interchanges them.

To show the second claim of Theorem
2.9
, Kurdyka and Păunescu use
resolution of singularities, splitting, and the Abhyankar–Jung theorem.

2.3.1.
Abhyankar–Jung theorem

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field and let

(5)

\displaystyle P(Z)=Z^{d}+a_{1}(X)Z^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}(X)\in\mathbb{K}[[X]][Z]

be a monic polynomial whose coefficients are formal power series in

X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
.
Such a polynomial
P
is called
quasi-ordinary
if the discriminant
\discr_{P}(X)

equals
X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}U(X)
, with
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
U(0)\neq 0
.

Theorem 2.11

(Abhyankar–Jung theorem)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and let

P\in\mathbb{K}[[X]][Z]
be a quasi-ordinary polynomial
such that the discriminant of
P
is of the form

\discr_{P}(X)=X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}U(X)
, where

U(0)\neq 0
, and
r\leq n
.
Then there is
q\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
such that
P(Z)
has its roots in

\mathbb{K}[[X_{1}^{\frac{1}{q}},...,X_{r}^{\frac{1}{q}},X_{r+1},...,X_{n}]]
.

The Abhyankar–Jung theorem can be understood as a multiparameter version of Puiseux’s theorem.
It has first been proven by Jung in 1908 for
n=2
and
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, cf.
[
51
]
.
The first complete proof of Theorem
2.11
appeared in
[
1
]
.

The Abhyankar–Jung theorem still holds if
P
has multiple factors
(so its discriminant is identically equal to zero) but the discriminant of the square-free reduction
P_{\operatorname{red}}

of
P
is of the form
X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{r}^{\alpha_{r}}U(X)
.

The Abhyankar–Jung theorem can be proven using splitting, as it is done in
[
87
]
.
But in order to apply the splitting one needs the following result that is much more difficult to prove, see

[
87
, Theorem 1.1 and Remark 1.4]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a (not necessarily algebraically closed) field of characteristic zero and let

P\in\mathbb{K}[[X]][Z]
be a quasi-ordinary polynomial in Tschirnhausen form (i.e.
a_{1}=0
).
Then the ideal
(a_{i}^{d!/i}(X))_{i=2,\ldots,d}
is generated by one of
a_{i}^{d!/i}(X)
and this generator equals a monomial in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
times a unit.

2.3.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.9

We sketch a proof of this theorem.

The first claim of the theorem follows from Bronshtein’s theorem, see Theorem
3.2
below. The proof given in
[
64
]
is different and based on real analytic geometry arguments.

To show the second claim one first applies Hironaka’s resolution of singularites
to make the discriminant

\discr_{P_{\operatorname{red}}}
of
P_{\operatorname{red}}

normal crossings. For this it suffices, by
[
49
, Main Theorem II”(N), page 158]
, to apply a modification given by the composition of a locally finite number of global blowings-up with smooth centers. Therefore,
one may suppose that
P_{\operatorname{red}}
is quasi-ordinary. To conclude Kurdyka and Păunescu use the Abhyankar–Jung theorem applied to a hyperbolic quasi-ordinary polynomial, see
[
64
, Proposition 5.4]
for details.
Instead, one may use here directly Theorem
2.12
and splitting. Firstly, after applying the Tschirnhausen transformation we may suppose
a_{1}=0
.
Secondly, by Lemma
2.4
, it is
a_{2}^{d!/2}
that generates the ideal

(a_{i}^{d!/i})_{i=2,\ldots,d}
. Denote by
\theta(x)
one of the two analytic square roots of
-a_{2}(x)
,
that is, if
-a_{2}(x)=u(x)\prod x_{k}^{2\alpha_{k}}
, then
\theta(x)=\pm u^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)\prod x_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
.
Then, one concludes exactly as in the proof of Rellich’s theorem by splitting the polynomial
P
and proceeding by induction on its degree.
∎

2.4.
Perturbation of normal matrices

Kurdyka and Păunescu showed multiparameter counterparts of Theorem
2.1

for Hermitian matrices.

Theorem 2.13

(
[
64
, Theorem 6.2 and Remark 6.3]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
A(x)
,
x\in U
, be an analytic family of Hermitian matrices.
Then, there exists a locally finite composition of blowings-up with smooth global
centers
\sigma:W\to U
, such that locally on
W
the corresponding family

A\circ\sigma
admits a simultaneous analytic diagonalization.

They also gave a version for real antisymmetric matrices,
[
64
, Theorem 7.2]
.
This was extended to normal matrices by Rainer
[
94
]
by showing that the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of normal matrices depend analytically on the parameters after a modification of the parameter space. Note that
these results generalize the one parameter case because there are no nontrivial modifications of one dimensional nonsingular spaces.

Recall that for a square matrix
A
we denote by
\Delta_{A}
the discriminant of the reduced (i.e., square-free) form
(\charp_{A})_{\operatorname{red}}
of its characteristic polynomial
\charp_{A}
.
The normal crossings assumption for
\Delta_{A}
is sufficient for the local simultaneous reduction of families of normal matrices as it is showed by Parusiński and Rond in
[
88
]
.
The following three algebraic theorems, stated in terms of formal power series
\mathbb{K}[[X]]
,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}
, were proven in
[
88
]
.
They hold as well for several subrings of
\mathbb{K}[[X]]
including the algebraic power series
\mathbb{K}\langle X\rangle
and the
convergent power series
\mathbb{K}\{X\}
, see Remark
2.20
.

Theorem 2.14

(
[
88
, Theorem 2.5]
)
.

Let
A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
be normal and suppose that

\Delta_{A}(X)=X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}u(X)

with
u(0)\neq 0
.
Then there is a unitary matrix

U(X)\in\operatorname{U}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
such that

U(X)^{-1}A(X)U(X)=D(X),

where
D(X)
is a diagonal matrix with entries in
\mathbb{C}[[X]]
.

Theorem 2.15

(
[
88
, Theorem 2.9]
)
.

Let
A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{R}[[X]])
be normal and suppose that
\Delta_{A}(X)=X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}u(X)

with
u(0)\neq 0
. Then there exists an orthogonal matrix

O(X)\in\operatorname{O}_{d}(\mathbb{R}[[X]])
such that
O(X)^{-1}A(X)O(X)
is block diagonal with the blocks of size

1
and
2
. The blocks of size
2

are
2\times 2
matrices of the form

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(X)&b(X)\\
-b(X)&a(X)\end{array}\right)

for some
a(X)
,
b(X)\in\mathbb{R}[[X]]
.

Note that it follows
that if
A(X)
is symmetric, resp. antisymmetric, then
O(X)^{-1}A(X)O(X)
is symmetric (i.e., diagonal), resp. antisymmetric.

Example 2.16

(Rainer
[
94
, Example 8.3]
)
.

The eigenvalues of the one parameter diagonalizable, but not normal, matrix

A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t&0&0\\
0&0&t^{2}\\
0&t&0\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

are
t,\pm t^{3/2}
for
t\geq 0
and
t,\pm i|t|^{3/2}
for
t<0
.

The above example shows that the eigenvalues of a real analytic family of diagonalizable matrices are not necessarily analytic (even for one parameter families).
As the following theorem shows, for arbitrary analytic families of matrices, not necessarily square, the counterparts of the above two theorems hold for the singular value decomposition.

Let
A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
A=VDU^{-1}

for some unitary matrices
V\in\operatorname{U}_{m}(\mathbb{C})
,

U\in\operatorname{U}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and a (rectangular) diagonal matrix
D
with real nonnegative coefficients. The
diagonal elements of
D
are the nonnegative square roots of the
eigenvalues of
A^{*}A
or, equivalently, of
AA^{*}
. They are called
singular values
of
A
.
If
A
is real, then
V
and
U
can be chosen orthogonal.

Theorem 2.17

(
[
88
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.3]
)
.

Let
A=A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
,
d\leq m
, and suppose that
\Delta_{A^{*}A}(X)=X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}u(X)

with
u(0)\neq 0
. Then there are unitary matrices
V\in\operatorname{U}_{m}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
,

U\in\operatorname{U}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
such that

D(X)=V(X)^{-1}A(X)U(X)

is (rectangular) diagonal.

If
A=A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,d}(\mathbb{R}[[X]])
, then
U
and
V
can be chosen real (that is orthogonal) so that
V(X)^{-1}A(X)U(X)
is
block (rectangular) diagonal with blocks as in Theorem
2.15
.

Suppose in addition that the last nonzero coefficient of

\Delta_{A^{*}A}(X)
is of the form
X_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\beta_{n}}h(X)

with
h(0)\neq 0
. Then, both in the real and the complex case,
we may require that
V(X)^{-1}A(X)U(X)
is (rectangular) diagonal with the entries on the diagonal in
\mathbb{R}[[X]]
, and,
moreover, that those entries that are nonzero are of the form
X^{\alpha}a(X)
, with
a(0)>0
, and that the exponents
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
are well-ordered.

In general, even if
d=m=1
, one needs the extra assumption on the last nonzero coefficient of

\Delta_{A^{*}A}
, see
[
88
, Example 3.2]
, though this assumption is automatically satisfied if
n=1
.
It is in general not possible to have all the entries of
D(X)
non-negative.

2.4.1.
Remarks on the proofs

In
[
64
]
the authors first use Theorem
2.9
in order to get, after a modification of
the parameter space, the eigenvalues real analytic.
Then they solve linear equations describing the eigenspaces corresponding to the irreducible factors of the characteristic polynomial.
This requires to monomialize the ideal defined by all the minors of the associated matrices which usually necessitates
further blowings-up.
A similar approach is adapted in
[
94
]
.
First the eigenvalues are made analytic by blowings-up and then further blowings-up are necessary, for instance to make the coefficients of matrices and their differences normal crossing.

In
[
40
]
Grandjean shows results similar to those of

[
64
]
and
[
94
]
but by a different approach. He does
not treat the eigenvalues first, but considers directly
the eigenspaces defined on the complement of the discriminant locus. We refer the reader to
[
40
]
for details.

The proofs in
[
88
]
also consider the eigenvalues and eigenspaces at the same time and
only the assumption that the discriminant of the reduced characteristic polynomial is normal crossings is necessary.
These proofs adapt a strategy similar to the algorithm of the proof of the
Abhyankar–Jung theorem of
[
87
]
and of the proof of Rellich’s theorem
2.2
that we presented in
Section
2.2
. Given a normal matrix
A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
, first one subtracts from
A(X)
the identity matrix times
\operatorname{Tr}A
, so one can assume
\operatorname{Tr}A=0
. This corresponds to the Tschirnhausen transformation. Then
the following proposition replaces Theorem
2.12
.

Proposition 2.18

(
[
88
, Proposition 2.7]
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
2.14
are satisfied and that, moreover,
A=(a_{ij}(X))
is
nonzero and
\operatorname{Tr}A=0
. Then the ideal
(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,d}
of
\mathbb{C}[[X]]
is generated by one of

a_{ij}
and this generator equals a monomial in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
times a unit.

Note that all the coefficients
a_{ij}
of
A
have the same weight,
in contrast to Theorem
2.12
, where suitable powers of the coefficients of
P
must be considered.

Finally, the following version of Hensel’s lemma for normal matrices replaces the splitting lemma, Lemma
2.5
.
This result is a strengthened version of Cohn’s version of Hensel’s lemma, see
[
24
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.19

(
[
88
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

Let
A(X)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
be a normal matrix. Assume that

A(0)=\begin{pmatrix}B^{o}_{1}&0\\
0&B^{o}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

with
B^{o}_{i}\in\operatorname{Mat}_{d_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
,
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
, such that the characteristic polynomials of
B^{o}_{1}
and

B^{o}_{2}
are coprime.
Then there is a unitary matrix
U\in\operatorname{U}_{d}(\mathbb{C}[[X]])
,
U(0)=\mathbb{I}
, such that

(8)

\displaystyle U^{-1}AU=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}&0\\
0&B_{2}\end{pmatrix},

and
B_{i}(0)=B^{o}_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

Remark 2.20
.

Theorems
2.14
(for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
),
2.15
(for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
), and
2.17
(for

remain valid if we replace

,

(2)

\mathbb{K}\{\!\!\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\!\!\}

, …,

\mathbb{K}\{\!\!\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\!\!\}\cap(X_{i})\mathbb{K}[[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]]=(X_{i})\mathbb{K}\{\!\!\{X\}\!\!\}

.

]
).

(
[
88
, Theorem 5.3]
)
.

Let
M
be a manifold defined in one of the following categories:

(1)

real analytic;

(2)

real Nash;

(3)

quasianalytic (under the asssumptions (3.1) – (3.6) of
[

]

;

if

.

Here one has to work with real local coordinates, since a complex change of coordinates does not commute with

Example 2.22

(

The eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix

and
(1,x_{2}/x_{1})
and
(1,-x_{1}/x_{2})
are the corresponding eigenvectors.
The discriminant of the
characteristic polynomial
(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{2}
is not normal crossings over the reals. It is so over the
complex numbers,
(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{2}(x_{1}-ix_{2})^{2}
, but in the new system of coordinates
(x_{1}+ix_{2},x_{1}-ix_{2})

the matrix is not normal any longer.
To diagonalize this family simultanously one has to blow-up the origin.

Remark 2.23

Many of the results presented for matrices extend (in appropriate form) to parameterized families of
unbounded normal or selfadjoint operators
in Hilbert space with common domain of definition and compact resolvent. See e.g.
[
2
]
,
[
94
]
, and references therein.

3.
Differentiable roots of hyperbolic polynomials

3.1.
Bronshtein’s theorem

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and

P_{a}(t)(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}(t)Z^{d-j},\quad t\in I,

a monic hyperbolic polynomial.
It is not hard to see (e.g.,
[
2
, Theorem 4.3]
and Remark
3.12
below) that if
a_{j}\in C^{d}(I)
,
1\leq j\leq d
,
then there exist differentiable functions
\lambda_{i}:I\to\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq i\leq d
,
which parameterize the roots of
P_{a}
, i.e.,

P_{a}(t)(Z)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(Z-\lambda_{i}(t)),\quad t\in I.

Actually, it suffices to assume that the coefficients are of class
C^{p}
if
p
is the maximal multiplicity of the roots.

Bronshtein
[
21
]
proved that the roots
\lambda_{i}
have locally bounded derivative:

Theorem 3.1

(Bronshtein’s theorem I,
[
21
]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval and
Y
a compact Hausdorff topological space.
Let
P_{a}(t,y)
,
(t,y)\in I\times Y
, be a monic hyperbolic polynomial of degree
d

such that the multiplicity of its roots does not exceed
p
and

\partial^{k}_{t}a_{j}(t,y)
,
1\leq j\leq d
,
0\leq k\leq p
, are continuous functions on
I\times Y
.
Then, for each compact
K\subseteq I\times Y
,
there is a constant
C=C(K)>0
such that the roots of
P
can be represented by functions
\lambda_{i}(t,y)

that are differentiable in
t
and
satisfy

|\partial_{t}\lambda_{i}(t,y)|\leq C,\quad(t,y)\in K,\,1\leq i\leq d.

Bronshtein’s original proof is not easy to penetrate (cf. Remark
3.12
).
A much simpler proof was given by Parusiński and Rainer
[
81
]
.
It also gives uniform bounds for the Lipschitz constant of the roots in terms of the coefficients.

Theorem 3.2

(Bronshtein’s theorem II,
[
81
]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Let
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, be a monic hyperbolic polynomial of degree
d
with coefficients
a_{j}\in C^{d-1,1}(I)
,
1\leq j\leq d
.
Then any continuous root
\lambda\in C^{0}(I)
of
P_{a}
is locally Lipschitz
and, for any pair of relatively compact open intervals
I_{0}\Subset I_{1}\Subset I
,

(9)

\operatorname{Lip}_{I_{0}}(\lambda)\leq C\,\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|a_{j}\|^{1/j}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I}_{1})},

with
C=C(d)\,\max\{\delta^{-1},1\}
, where
\delta:=\operatorname{dist}(I_{0},\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{1})
.

The last claim of the above theorem, the form of the constant
C
, follows from the proof
of
[
81
, Theorem 2.1]
, see also Lemma
3.23
below.
Note that the Lipschitz constant may blow up if we approach the boundary of
I
as for

P_{a}(t)(Z)=Z^{d}-t
and
I=(0,1)
. We remark also that a system of continuous roots always exists, for hyperbolic polynomials one may order them for instance:

\lambda_{1}(t)\leq\lambda_{2}(t)\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{d}(t)
.

Remark 3.3
.

If
p
is the maximal multiplicity of the roots of
P_{a}
, then it is enough that

,
1\leq j\leq d
,
for any continuous root
\lambda
to be locally Lipschitz on
I
. The bound for the Lipschitz constant (
9
) of
\lambda
has to be modified;
it will also depend on a quantity that measures the “uniformity” of having multiplicity at most

, namely,

B:=\sup_{t\in I}\frac{\lambda_{d}(t)-\lambda_{1}(t)}{\min_{1\leq i\leq d-p}(\lambda_{i+p}(t)-\lambda_{i}(t))},

where
\lambda_{1}(t)\leq\lambda_{2}(t)\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{d}(t)
,
t\in I
, is an increasing enumeration of the roots.
See
[
81
, Section 4.7]
for details.

A multiparameter version follows immediately (because a function defined on a box that is Lipschitz with respect to each variable is Lipschitz).

This was first observed by Colombini, Orrú, and Pernazza
[

]

[

Theorem 3.9

(
[
81
, Theorem 2.4]
)
.

Let
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, be a
C^{p}
curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials, where
p
is
the maximal multiplicity of the roots.
Then:

(1)

Any continuous root
\lambda
of
P_{a}
has left- and right-sided derivatives
\lambda^{\prime\pm}
at each
t\in I
.

(2)

\lambda^{\prime-}
and

are continuous in the following sense: for every

\lim_{t\to t_{0}^{-}}\lambda^{\prime\pm}(t)=\lambda^{\prime-}(t_{0})

and

.

(3)

There exist differentiable systems of the roots, any differentiable root is

.

3.9

Remark 3.12
.

Bronshtein’s original proof of Theorem
3.1
rests on a polynomial equation for the derivatives of the roots of
P_{a}
, see (
14
) below.
Suppose that
\lambda_{0}
is a
q
-fold root of
P_{a}(t_{0})
and consider
the polynomial

(12)

P_{a}(t)(Z+\lambda_{0})=A_{0}(t)Z^{q}+A_{1}(t)Z^{q-1}+\cdots+A_{q}(t)+R(t,Z),

where
R(t,Z)
is a polynomial in
Z
divisible by
Z^{q+1}
(possibly identically zero).
Note that

A_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{(q-j)!}\partial_{Z}^{q-j}P_{a}(t)(\lambda_{0})
and
set
a_{j}^{i}:=\partial_{t}^{i}A_{j}(t_{0})
. Then
a_{0}^{0}\neq 0
, while
a_{j}^{0}=0
for

, since
\lambda_{0}
is a
q
-fold
root of
P_{a}(t_{0})
. Using that
P_{a}
is hyperbolic
and that hyperbolicity is preserved by differentiation with respect to

, Bronshtein shows that
a_{j}^{i}=0
for
0\leq i\leq j-1
and
1\leq j\leq q
,
or equivalently, that
A_{j}(t)=(t-t_{0})^{j}\hat{A}_{j}(t)
, where

is a continuous function.

Consequently, the change of variables

in (

leads to

\displaystyle(t-t_{0})^{-q}P_{a}(t)((t-t_{0})W+\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=a_{0}^{0}\,W^{q}+\tfrac{1}{1!}a_{1}^{1}\,W^{q-1}+\cdots+\tfrac{1}{q!}a_{q}^{q}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k=0}^{d}B_{k}(t)W^{d-k},

where

are continuous functions with
B_{k}(t_{0})=0
.
It follows that

a_{0}^{0}\,W^{q}+\tfrac{1}{1!}a_{1}^{1}\,W^{q-1}+\cdots+\tfrac{1}{q!}a_{q}^{q}=0.

Now, for contradiction, Bronshtein

\lambda(t_{n},y_{n})\to\lambda(t_{0},y_{0})

,

Then

3.2.2.

A classical inequality used by Glaeser in

For

and

denote the open interval centered at

I(t_{0},r):=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:|t-t_{0}|<r\}.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open bounded interval.
Let

satisfy
f\geq 0
or
f\leq 0
on
I

Let

and assume that

,

, and

Then

|f^{\prime}(t_{0})|\leq(M+M^{-1}\operatorname{Lip}_{I_{0}}(f^{\prime}))|f(t_{0})|^{1/2}.

If additionally

Note that if

Proof.

Suppose that

; otherwise consider

Thus

The assertion follows from setting

∎

3.2.3.
Interpolation estimates

Lemma 3.15
.

Let

Lemma 3.16
.

Let

, where

3.3.
Sufficient conditions for
C^{p}
roots

Let us analyze what causes the loss of regularity and give several sufficient conditions for better regularity of the roots.

3.3.1.
The effect of positive local minima

A finer analysis of Example
3.10
(1)
indicates that the small positive local minima of the function
f

prevent the solutions of
Z^{2}=f
from being
C^{1,\alpha}
.
This is confirmed by the following result of Bony, Broglia, Colombini, and Pernazza
[
18
]
.

Theorem 3.24

(
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be of class
C^{4}
.
Then
Z^{2}=f
has a
C^{2}
solution if and only if
there exists a continuous function
\gamma
vanishing on
\{t\in\mathbb{R}:f^{(j)}(t)=0,\,0\leq j\leq 4\}

such that for each local minimum
t_{0}
of
f
with
f(t_{0})>0

we have
f^{\prime\prime}(t_{0})\leq\gamma(t_{0})f(t_{0})^{1/2}
.

Bony, Colombini, and Pernazza
[
19
]
extended this result.
Forcing
f
and sufficiently many of its derivatives to vanish on all local minima of
f
,
turns out to be sufficient for the existence of
C^{p}
square roots:

Theorem 3.25

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be of class
C^{2p}
,
p\geq 2
, and
assume that
f
vanishes along with its derivatives up to order
2p-4
at all its local minima.
Then
Z^{2}=f
has a
C^{p}
solution.
A solution with a derivative of order
p+1
everywhere exists, provided that,
under the same assumption on the minima,
f
is
C^{2p+2}
.

3.3.2.
A regularity class for taking radicals

A different approach is due to Ray and Schmidt-Hieber
[
98
]
.
They define a regularity class
\mathcal{F}^{\beta}
,
\beta>0
,
for nonnegative functions
f:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
which behaves nicely
with respect to radicals.
For
\beta>0
we let
C^{\beta}
be a short notation for the Hölder class
C^{m,\alpha}
, where
m
is the largest integer strictly smaller than
\beta
and
\alpha=\beta-m
.
For
f\in C^{\beta}([0,1])
set

|f|_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}:=\max_{1\leq j<\beta}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{|f^{(j)}(t)|^{\beta}}{|f(t)|^{\beta-j}}\Big)^{1/j}.

It measures the flatness of
f
near its zeros. In particular, if
|f|_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}<\infty
then that
f

vanishes at some point entails that also all its derivatives up to order
<\beta
vanish.
Consider

\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1]):=\{f\in C^{\beta}([0,1]):f\geq 0,\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}<\infty\},

where

\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}:=\|f\|_{C^{\beta}([0,1])}+|f|_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}.

Note that
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])
may possess infinitely many nonzero local mimima.

Theorem 3.26

(
[
98
]
)
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta>0
. For all
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])
, we have

\|f^{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{F}^{\alpha\beta}([0,1])}\leq C(\alpha,\beta)\,\|f\|^{\alpha}_{\mathcal{F}^{\beta}([0,1])}.

In particular,
f^{\alpha}\in C^{\alpha\beta}([0,1])
.

Moreover,
bounds on the wavelet coefficients of
f^{\alpha}
are derived in
[
98
]

which give additional information on the local regularity of
f^{\alpha}
.

3.3.3.
Finite order of contact

In the case of a curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, of degree
d
,
sufficient coefficients for the existence of
C^{p}
roots can be given in terms
of the differentiability of the coefficients and the finite order of contact of the roots;
see Rainer
[
92
,
95
]
.

In order to present these results we need some terminology.
The polynomial

\Delta_{s}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}):=\sum_{i_{1}<\ldots<i_{s}}(X_{i_{1}}-X_{i_{2}})^{2}\cdots(X_{i_{1}}-X_{i_{s}})^{2}\cdots(X_{i_{s-1}}-X_{i_{s}})^{2}

is symmetric and so it is a (unique) polynomial in the elementary symmetric functions:

\Delta_{s}=\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}).

Thus
t\mapsto\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(P_{a}(t))
, where we identify
P_{a}
with its vector of coefficients,
is well-defined.
We say that
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, is
normally nonflat
if
for each
t_{0}\in I
the following condition is satisfied.
Let
s
be the maximal integer such that the germ at
t_{0}
of
t\mapsto\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(P_{a}(t))

is not zero. Then

m_{t_{0}}(\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(P_{a})):=\sup\{m\in\mathbb{N}:(t-t_{0})^{-m}\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(P_{a}(t))\text{ is continuous near }t_{0}\}<\infty.

We call this quantity the
multiplicity of
\tilde{\Delta}_{s}(P_{a})
at
t_{0}
;
obviously we can analogously define the multiplicity of any continuous univariate function.
It is not hard to see that
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, is normally nonflat
if and only if for any continuous system
\lambda_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq d
, of its roots

m_{t_{0}}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})=\infty
implies
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
near
t_{0}
.

Let
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, be a normally nonflat curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials.
In
[
92
,
95
]
, numbers
\gamma(P_{a}),\Gamma(P_{a})\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,
\gamma(P_{a})\leq\Gamma(P_{a})
,
are defined, in terms of a splitting algorithm for
P_{a}
,
that encode the conditions for
C^{p}
solvability.
We refer the reader to
[
92
]
and
[
95
]
for the details of the definition.

Theorem 3.27

(
[
92
,
95
]
)
.

Let
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, be a normally nonflat curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
.
If
p\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, has
C^{p+\Gamma(P_{a})}
coefficients,
then it admits a
C^{p+\gamma(P_{a})}
system of its roots.

Note that for
p=\infty
the theorem yields the result of Alekseevsky, Kriegl, Losik, and Michor
[
2
]

that, under the normal nonflatness assumption,
C^{\infty}
systems of the roots exist if the coefficients are
C^{\infty}
.

3.3.4.
Definability: no oscillation

Let us state one more result. It shows that, actually, it is not the infinite contact
between the roots that is to blame
for the loss of regularity, but oscillation. Of course, smooth coefficients can admit infinite oscillation
only in conjuction with infinite flatness.
The result is formulated in the framework of o-minimal expansions
\mathscr{S}
of the real field.
That means that
\mathscr{S}
is a family
\mathscr{S}=(\mathscr{S}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, where
\mathscr{S}_{n}
is a collection of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

•

\mathscr{S}_{n}
is a boolean algebra with respect to the usual set-theoretic operations,

•

\mathscr{S}_{n}
contains all semialgebraic subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

•

\mathscr{S}
is stable by cartesian products and linear projections,

•

each
S\in\mathscr{S}_{1}
has only finitely many connected components.

The
\mathscr{S}
-definable sets
are those sets
S
such that there exists
n\geq 1
with
S\in\mathscr{S}_{n}
.
A map is
\mathscr{S}
-definable if its graph is
\mathscr{S}
-definable.

Theorem 3.28

(
[
92
, Theorem 4.12]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be an arbitrary o-minimal expansion of the real field.
Let
P_{a}(t)
,
t\in I
, be a curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials
with
C^{\infty}
-coefficients that are
\mathscr{S}
-definable.
Then there exists a
C^{\infty}
-system of the roots of
P_{a}
consisting of

\mathscr{S}
-definable functions.

By Miller’s dichotomy theorem
[
77
]
, for a fixed o-minimal expansion
\mathscr{S}
,
either for every
\mathscr{S}
-definable function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
there is
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(t)=O(t^{N})
as
t\to\infty
,
or the global exponential function
\exp:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathscr{S}
-definable.
In the latter case, there are infinitely flat
C^{\infty}
functions in
\mathscr{S}
.
On the other hand,
\mathscr{S}
cannot contain oscillating functions.

3.4.
Gårding hyperbolic polynomials

There are various ways to generalize univariate hyperbolic polynomials to multivariate polynomials.
Of particular interest are the
Gårding hyperbolic polynomials
and the closely related
real stable polynomials
;
they have numerous applications in PDEs, combinatorics, optimization, functional analysis, probability, etc.

3.4.1.
Gårding hyperbolic and real stable polynomials

A homogeneous polynomial
f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
of degree
d
is said to be

Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

if
f(v)\neq 0
and
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the univariate polynomial
f(x-Tv)\in\mathbb{R}[T]
is hyperbolic, i.e., has all roots real.
Geometrically, this means that any
affine line with direction
v
meets the real hypersurface
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:f(x)=0\}
in
d
points (with multiplicities).

This notion was introduced by Gårding
[
35
]
in the 1950s. He showed that
f
being Gårding
hyperbolic with respect to a direction
v

is a necessary and sufficient condition for local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem with principal symbol
f

and initial data on a hyperplane with normal vector
v
.
Gårding hyperbolic polynomials have found many applications ever since, for instance, in Gurvits’ proof
[
42
]
of the van der Waerden conjecture.

An important example is the determinant on the real vector space of
d\times d

Hermitian matrices: it is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to the identity matrix
\mathbb{I}
.

A polynomial
f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
is called
stable

if
f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\neq 0
for all
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
with
\mathrm{Im}(z_{j})>0
,
1\leq j\leq n
.
If all coefficients of a stable polynomial are real, it is called
real stable
.
Real stable polynomials played a crucial role in the recent proof of the Kadison–Singer conjecture
[
73
]
.

These notions are closely related.

Proposition 3.29

(
[
20
, Proposition 1.1]
)
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
be of degree
d
and let
f_{H}\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n},W]
be the unique
homogeneous polynomial of degree
d
such that

f_{H}(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n},1)=f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}).

Then
f
is real stable if and only if
f_{H}
is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
every vector
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n},0)
with
v_{i}>0
for all
i
.

Interesting examples of real stable polynomials
are generated as follows (see
[
20
, Proposition 1.12]
):
Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
be positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices and
B
a (complex) Hermitian
d\times d
matrix. Then the
polynomial

f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})=\det\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}A_{j}+B\Big)

is either real stable or identically zero.
Conversely, any real stable polynomial in two variables
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
can be written as
\pm\det(Z_{1}A_{1}+Z_{2}A_{2}+B)
,
where
A_{j}
are positive semidefinite and
B
is symmetric, see
[
20
, Theorem 1.13]
.
The latter result is based on the Lax conjecture
[
66
]
for Gårding hyperbolic polynomials
which is true: as noted in

[
67
]

it follows from
[
46
]
and
[
113
]
.

Remark 3.30
.

The Lax conjecture states
that a homogeneous polynomial
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is Gårding hyperbolic of degree
d
with respect to the direction
(1,0,0)
with
f(1,0,0)=1

if and only if there exist real symmetric
d\times d
matrices
A
and
B
such that

(26)

f(X,Y,Z)=\det(X\mathbb{I}+YA+ZB).

A representation of type (
26
) is in general not possible for Gårding hyperbolic polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 4
.
Indeed, the dimension of the space of Gårding hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to a fixed direction
is
\binom{n+d-1}{d}
while the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
of the form

(27)

\det(X_{1}\mathbb{I}+X_{2}A_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}A_{n}),

where
A_{i}
are real symmetric
d\times d
matrices, has dimension at most
(n-1)\cdot\binom{d+1}{2}
.
A particular homogeneous polynomial of degree
2
which is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
(1,0,\ldots,0)
but cannot be represented in the form
(
27
)
is the Lorentzian polynomial
f(X)=X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}-\cdots-X_{n}^{2}
for
n\geq 4
.
Cf.
[
67
, p. 2498]
.

See the
survey article
[
9
]
for background on Gårding hyperbolic polynomials and
more ways to generated examples.

3.4.2.
Characteristic roots

Let
f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d

which is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to a direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We may factorize

f(x+Tv)=f(v)\prod_{j=1}^{d}(T+\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(x)),

where

\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1}(x)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}(x)

are the decreasingly ordered roots of
f(x-Tv)\in\mathbb{R}[T]
.
We call

\lambda^{\downarrow}=(\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

the
characteristic map
of
f
with respect to
v
and
\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}

(in no particular order) the
characteristic roots
.
The polynomial
f
is recovered by

f(x)=f(v)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(x)
.
Then, for all
j=1,\ldots,d
,
r\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda^{\downarrow}_{j}(rx+sv)&=r\lambda^{\downarrow}_{j}(x)+s,\\
\lambda^{\downarrow}_{j}(-x)&=-\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d+1-j}(x).\end{split}

Gårding
[
36
, Theorem 2]
proved that
\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}
is concave which, by (
28
), is equivalent to

\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1}
being convex.
In view of (
28
) it follows that the largest root
\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1}
is sublinear (i.e., positively homogeneous and subadditive).
The connected component
C_{f}
of the set
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{f=0\}
which contains
v
is an open convex cone, one has

C_{f}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}(x)>0\}
,
and
f
is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to each
w\in C_{f}
.

Following
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
, we can produce new Gårding hyperbolic polynomials from the given
f
:
if
g
is a homogeneous symmetric polynomial of degree
e
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

which is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
(1,1,\ldots,1)
and has characteristic map
\mu^{\downarrow}
,
then
g\circ\lambda^{\downarrow}
is Gårding hyperbolic of degree
e
with respect to
v
and its characteristic map is
\mu^{\downarrow}\circ\lambda^{\downarrow}
.

Let us apply this to the homogeneous symmetric polynomial

g_{k}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}\\
|I|=k\end{subarray}}\sum_{i\in I}Y_{i}

of degree
\binom{d}{k}
which
is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
(1,1,\ldots,1)
and has the characteristic roots

\mu_{I}(Y)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in I}Y_{i}
,
where
I
ranges over the subsets of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
k
elements.
On the set
\{Y_{1}\geq Y_{2}\geq\cdots\geq Y_{d}\}

we have
\mu_{I}(Y)\geq\mu_{J}(Y)
if and only if
\sum_{i\in I}i\leq\sum_{j\in J}j
, in particular,

\mu_{\{1,\ldots,k\}}
is the largest characteristic root.
Then
g_{k}\circ\lambda^{\downarrow}
is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to
v
and has
the largest characteristic root
\mu_{\{1,\ldots,k\}}\circ\lambda^{\downarrow}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{\downarrow}
.
From Gårding’s result we may conclude that, for all
k=1,\ldots,d
, the sum of the
k
largest roots

\sigma_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{\downarrow}

is a sublinear function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Any finite sublinear function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is globally Lipschitz; see
[
101
, Corollary 10.5.1]
.
So the functions
\sigma_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, are convex and globally Lipschitz.
We have proved the following proposition.

Proposition 3.31

(
[
96
, Proposition 4.1]
)
.

Let
f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d

which is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to a direction
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The characteristic map
\lambda^{\downarrow}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is globally Lipschitz and difference-convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A real valued function on a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
difference-convex

if it can be written as the
difference of two continuous convex functions (cf.
[
7
]
and
[
48
]
).
The class
DC
of difference-convex functions arises as the smallest
vector space containing all the continuous convex functions on the given set.
If
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, then (cf.
[
112
, Theorem 11]

and
[
48
, Section II]
)

C^{1,1}(U)\subseteq DC_{\operatorname{loc}}(U)\subseteq C^{0,1}(U).

On the other hand, the first order partial derivatives of a difference-convex function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}

have bounded variation, i.e., the weak second order partial derivatives of
f
are signed Radon measures (cf. Section
7.4
).
This follows from Dudley’s result
[
33
]
that a Schwartz distribution is a convex function if and only if its second derivative
is a nonnegative matrix valued Radon measure.
In dimension one, a real valued function
f
on a compact interval is difference-convex if and only if

f
is absolutely continuous and
f^{\prime}
has bounded variation.
Note also that
DC(\mathbb{R}^{n})=DC_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, by
[
44
]
.

Using the regularity properties of difference-convex functions, we may further investigate
the regularity of the roots of Gårding hyperbolic polynomials.

Theorem 3.32

(
[
96
, Lemma 4.5 and Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Let
f(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
d

which is Gårding hyperbolic with respect to some direction in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then:

(1)

If
x:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is of class
C^{1}
,
then there exists a differentiable system
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
of the roots of
f
along
x

(i.e.,
\lambda(t)
and
\lambda^{\downarrow}(x(t))
coincide as unordered
d
-tuples for all
t
).

(2)

Let
x\in DC(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n})
(for instance,
x\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{n})
). Then
any differentiable system
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
of the roots of
f
along
x
is actually of class

\lambda\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap DC(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3)

The result is uniform in the following sense:
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
U
be an open neighborhood of the closure of
I^{1+k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+k}
.
Suppose that
x:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is such that

•

x
is locally DC on
U
,

•

x(\cdot,r)\in C^{1}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{2,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
.

Assume that, for each
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
, a
C^{1}
system
\lambda(\cdot,r)
of the roots of
f
along
x(\cdot,r)
is fixed.
Then the family

\lambda(\cdot,r)
, for
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
, is bounded in
C^{1}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there is a nonnegative

L^{1}
function
m:I^{k}\to[0,\infty)

such that

\|\lambda(\cdot,r)\|_{W^{2,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq m(r),\quad\text{ for a.e.\ }r\in I^{k}.

Remark 3.33
.

It is shown in
[
45
, Theorem 4.2]
that for any Gårding hyperbolic polynomial
f
with respect to
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of degree
d
,

x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
w\in C_{f}

there is a system
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the roots of
f
along
t\mapsto x_{0}+tw
which is
real analytic and such that each
\lambda_{j}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly increasing and surjective. The inverses of the
\lambda_{j}

form a system of the characteristic roots of the Gårding hyperbolic polynomial
f
with respect to
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
along
t\mapsto x_{0}+tv
.
The proof is based on the homogeneity properties (
28
) and the description of
C_{f}
as the set, where
\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}

are positive.

3.5.
Eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices

Let us apply the above findings to the Gårding hyperbolic polynomial
\det
(with respect to the identity matrix
\mathbb{I}
)
on the real vector space
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
of complex
d\times d
Hermitian matrices.
Its characteristic map
\lambda^{\downarrow}=(\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}):\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

assigns to a Hermitian matrix
A
its eigenvalues in decreasing order

\lambda^{\downarrow}_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{\downarrow}_{d}(A).

As a special case of Proposition
3.31
, we get:

Corollary 3.34

(
[
96
, Corollary 6.1]
)
.

The characteristic map

\lambda^{\downarrow}:\operatorname{Herm}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is globally Lipschitz and difference-convex on
\operatorname{Herm}(d)
.
The sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\downarrow}_{i}
, for
k=1,\ldots,d
, of the
k
largest eigenvalues is sublinear.

The following result is a consequence of
Theorem
3.32
.

Theorem 3.35

(
[
96
, Theorem 6.2]
)
.

Let
A:\mathbb{R}\to\operatorname{Herm}(d)
be a curve of
d\times d
Hermitian matrices.
Then:

(1)

If
A
is of class
C^{1}
, then there exists a differentiable system

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
of the eigenvalues of
A
.

(2)

If
A
is of class
DC\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}
on
\mathbb{R}
(for instance, if it is of class
C^{1,1}
),
then any differentiable system
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
of the eigenvalues of
A
is actually of class

\lambda\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap DC(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(3)

The result in
2
is uniform in the following sense:
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
U
be an open neighborhood of the closure of
I^{1+k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{1+k}
.
Suppose that
A:U\to\operatorname{Herm}(d)
is such that

•

A
is locally DC on
U
,

•

A(\cdot,r)
is of class
C^{1}\cap W^{2,1}
on
\overline{I}
for all
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
.

Assume that, for each
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
, a
C^{1}
system
\lambda(\cdot,r)
of the eigenvalues of
A(\cdot,r)
is fixed.
Then the family

\lambda(\cdot,r)
, for
r\in\overline{I}^{k}
, is bounded in
C^{1}(\overline{I},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there is a nonnegative

L^{1}
function

such that

\|\lambda(\cdot,r)\|_{W^{2,1}(I,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq m(r),\quad\text{ for a.e.\ }r\in I^{k}.

The conclusion of this theorem is best-possible among all Sobolev spaces
W^{k,p}
. Indeed, by the Sobolev inequality,

W^{k,p}
regularity with
k+p>3
would imply
C^{1,\alpha}
regularity with some
\alpha>0
, contradicting the following counterexample.

Example 3.36

(
[
61
]
)
.

There is a
C^{\infty}
curve
A(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, of real symmetric
2\times 2
matrices and a convergent sequence
t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}

such that

A(t_{n}+t)=\begin{pmatrix}2^{-n^{2}}&2^{-n}t\\
2^{-n}t&-2^{-n^{2}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{ for }|t|\leq n^{-2},\,n\geq 1.

There is no
C^{1,\alpha}
-system of the eigenvalues of

for any
\alpha>0
.

A little more work leads to the following stronger version of (1) in the above theorem.

Theorem 3.37
.

If
A:\mathbb{R}\to\operatorname{Herm}(d)
is of class
C^{1}
, then there exists a
C^{1}
system

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
of the eigenvalues of
A
.

This is due to Rellich
[

]
in the case of symmetric matrices (see also Weyl
[
115
]
), and it was proved
for normal matrices in Rainer
[
94
]
; see Theorem
3.43
below.

Note that, conversely, Theorem
3.35
implies Theorem
3.32
if
n\leq 3
; cf. Remark
3.30
.

3.6.
Singular values

Theorem
3.35
contains information on the regularity of singular values.
Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
be any complex
m\times n
matrix and let

\sigma^{\downarrow}_{1}(A)\geq\sigma^{\downarrow}_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma^{\downarrow}_{n}(A)\geq 0

be the singular values of
A
in decreasing order, i.e., the nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues of
A^{*}A
.
If
\operatorname{rank}A=\ell
, then
\sigma^{\downarrow}_{\ell+1}(A)=\cdots=\sigma^{\downarrow}_{n}(A)=0
.
Thus we set
\ell:=\min\{m,n\}
and consider only
\sigma^{\downarrow}_{j}(A)
, for
j=1,\ldots,\ell
.
We may consider the
\sigma^{\downarrow}_{j}
as functions on the vector space
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.

Without loss of generality assume that
m\leq n
and let
\tilde{A}
be the
n\times n
matrix resulting from
A
by
adding
n-m
rows consisting of zeros. Then the eigenvalues of the Hermitian matrix

(29)

\begin{pmatrix}0&\tilde{A}\\
\tilde{A}^{*}&0\end{pmatrix}

are precisely

\sigma^{\downarrow}_{1}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma^{\downarrow}_{n}(A)\geq-\sigma^{\downarrow}_{n}(A)\geq\cdots\geq-\sigma^{\downarrow}_{1}(A).

By Corollary
3.34
, for all
k=1,\ldots,\ell
, the sum

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma^{\downarrow}_{j}

of the
k
largest singular values is a sublinear function on
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
viewed as a real vector space. In fact, the sums

A\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma^{\downarrow}_{j}(A)
are the so-called
Ky Fan norms
.

Corollary 3.38
.

The mapping

\sigma^{\downarrow}=(\sigma^{\downarrow}_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{\downarrow}_{\ell}):\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, where
\ell=\min\{m,n\}
,
is globally Lipschitz and difference-convex on
\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.

Theorem 3.39
.

Let
A:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
be a curve of
m\times n
complex matrices
of class
C^{1}\cap DC\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}
.
If
\operatorname{rank}A(t)=\min\{m,n\}=:\ell
for all
t
, then
there exists a system
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{\ell})
of the singular values of
A

such that
\sigma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})\cap DC(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})\cap W^{2,1}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
.
This result is uniform in the sense explained in Theorem
3.35
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3.35
to the Hermitian matrix (
29
).
The condition on the rank of
A
guarantees that
the nontrivial singular values of
A
are always strictly positive and hence there exists a
C^{1}
system of them,
since there exists a
C^{1}
system of the eigenvalues of (
29
).
∎

The rank condition is necessary; for instance, the singular value of the symmetric
1\times 1
matrix
A(t)=(t)
is
|t|

which does not admit a
C^{1}
parameterization.

3.7.
Eigenvalues of normal matrices

We have seen that the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices have similar regularity properties as the roots of
hyperbolic polynomials. Actually, they are slightly stronger under much weaker assumptions, see Theorem
3.35
,
since the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices are the characteristic roots of the determinant which is
Gårding hyperbolic with respect to the identity matrix.

Interestingly, the eigenvalues of the wider class of normal complex matrices have similar strong regularity properties.

Theorem 3.40

(
[
12
]
,
[
11
, VII.4.1]
)
.

Let
A,B
be normal complex
d\times d
matrices and
let
\lambda_{j}(A)
and

\lambda_{j}(B)
,
1\leq j\leq d
, denote the respective eigenvalues.
Then

\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|\lambda_{j}(A)-\lambda_{\sigma(j)}(B)|\leq C\,\|A-B\|

for a universal constant
C
with
1<C<3
,
where
\operatorname{S}_{d}
is the symmetric group and
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm.

This result shows that the unordered
d
-tuple of eigenvalues
[\lambda_{1}(A),\ldots,\lambda_{d}(A)]
is
Lipschitz continuous as a function of the normal matrix
A
.
On the left-hand side we have the Wasserstein metric
W_{\infty}
(up to a constant factor); see Example
6.8
.
In general, the single eigenvalues do not admit a
continuous parameterization, as shown by the following example.

Example 3.41
.

Each choice of the eigenvalues of the normal matrix

A(z):=\begin{pmatrix}0&z\\
|z|&0\end{pmatrix},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

must be discontinuous in a neighborhood of
0
.

Continuous systems of the eigenvalues always exist for continuous families of Hermitian matrices (e.g., by ordering the eigenvalues
increasingly or decreasingly) or for continuous real one-parameter families of normal matrices (cf.
[
52
, II.5.2]
).
The following theorem should be compared to Bronshtein’s theorem
3.2
.

Theorem 3.42

(
[
94
, Proposition 6.3]
)
.

Let
A(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a
C^{0,1}
curve of normal complex matrices.
Then any continuous system of the eigenvalues of
A
is
C^{0,1}
.

Let us sketch the main ideas in the proof.
Fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
and an eigenvalue
z
of
A(s)
. Let
m
be the multiplicity of
z
.
For
t
near
s
, there are
m
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{m}(t)
, close to
z

and
\lambda_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq m
, are continuous functions.
For each
t
, we may choose an orthonormal system of eigenvectors
v_{1}(t),\ldots,v_{m}(t)
corresponding to
\lambda_{1}(t),\ldots,\lambda_{m}(t)
.
For each
t
and each sequence
t_{k}\to t
, we have, after passing to a subsequence,
v_{j}(t_{k})\to w_{j}(t)
,
where
w_{1}(t),\ldots,w_{m}(t)
form an orthonormal system of eigenvectors of
A(t)
(restricted to the direct sum of eigenspaces of the
\lambda_{j}(t)
,
1\leq j\leq m
).
Then

\frac{A(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}{t_{k}-t}v_{j}(t_{k})+\frac{A(t_{k})-A(t)}{t_{k}-t}v_{j}(t_{k})=\frac{\lambda_{j}(t_{k})-\lambda_{j}(t)}{t_{k}-t}v_{j}(t_{k}).

By taking the inner product with
w_{j}(t)
and passing to the limit, one can show that
for any continuous system
\lambda_{j}
of the eigenvalues of
A

the derivatives
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}(t)
exist whenever
A^{\prime}(t)
exists,
they form the set of eigenvalues of
A^{\prime}(t)
and satisfy

(30)

\lambda_{j}^{\prime}(t)=\langle A^{\prime}(t)w_{j}(t)\mid w_{j}(t)\rangle,

possibly after applying a suitable permutation of the eigenvalues on one side of
t
.
Now it suffices to check that
\lambda_{j}
is locally absolutely continuous.
Then, by means of (
30
), we see that
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}
exists almost everywhere and is locally bounded.
We may conclude that
\lambda_{j}
is
C^{0,1}
.

With the help of the formula (
30
),
one can show that there are
C^{1}
systems of the eigenvalues provided that
t\mapsto A(t)
is
C^{1}
.

Theorem 3.43

(
[
94
, Proposition 6.11]
)
.

Let
A(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a
C^{1}
curve of normal complex matrices.
Then there is a
C^{1}
system of the eigenvalues of
A
.
If
t\mapsto A(t)
is
C^{2}
, then there is a
C^{1}
system of the eigenvalues of
A

which is twice differentiable everywhere.

Note that this is best possible, by Example
3.36
.
The next example shows that the assumption that
A
is normal cannot be omitted.

Example 3.44

(
[
52
, II.5.9]
)
.

Let
\alpha>1
and
\beta>2
. Then

A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}|t|^{\alpha}&|t|^{\alpha}-|t|^{\beta}\big(2+\sin\frac{1}{|t|}\big)\\
-|t|^{\alpha}&-|t|^{\alpha}\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\quad A(0)=0,

forms a
C^{1}
curve of diagonalizable (but not normal) matrices.
The eigenvalues of

are given by

\lambda_{\pm}(t)=\pm|t|^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}\Big(2+\sin\frac{1}{|t|}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\},\quad\lambda_{\pm}(0)=0.

The derivatives
\lambda_{\pm}^{\prime}
exist everywhere, but they are discontinuous at
0
if
\alpha+\beta\leq 4
and even unbounded near

if
\alpha+\beta<4
.

For multiparameter families of normal matrices we have the following.

Corollary 3.45

(
[
94
, Theorem 6.19]
)
.

Let
A(x)
,
x\in U
, be a
C^{0,1}
family of normal complex matrices on an open subset

and let
\lambda:V\to\mathbb{C}
be a continuous eigenvalue of

.

.

The first statement follows from Theorem

and the fact that

]
).
For the second assertion, we note that the formula (

In the spirit of Theorem

, it is possible to give sufficient conditions for the existence

C^{p}
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of curves of normal matrices in terms of the differentiability of the entries and
the order of contact of the eigenvalues.

Theorem 3.46

(
[
95
, Theorem 7.8]
)
.

Let
A(t)
,
t\in I
, be a curve of normal complex matrices.
Suppose that the characteristic polynomial is normally nonflat.
There exists
\Theta(A)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

, has

.
If the entries of the normal matrix

Theorem 3.47

(
[
95
, Theorem 8.1]
)
.

Let
A(t)
,
t\in I
, be a definable curve of normal complex matrices.
There exists an integer

,

, has

That there is no hope for continuous eigenvectors in this setup (even if there is no oscillation) is seen by the following example.

Example 3.48

(
[

)
.

Let

4.
Regularity of the roots in the general (nonhyperbolic) case

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and let

(31)

P_{a}(t)(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}(t)Z^{d-j},\quad t\in I,

be a monic polynomial with complex valued functions
a_{j}:I\to\mathbb{C}
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, as coefficients.
It is not hard to see that if the coefficients
a_{j}
are continuous then
P_{a}
always admits a continuous system of the roots
(e.g.
[
52
, II.5.2]
).
In the absence of hyperbolicity assumptions, the roots of
P
cannot be chosen Lipschitz even in the simplest radical case:
a_{d}=t
and
a_{j}\equiv 0
for
j\leq d-1
.

Motivated by the analysis of certain systems of pseudo-differential equations, Spagnolo
[
106
]
posed
the question of absolute continuity of the roots of
P_{a}
.
Using explicit formulas for the roots, he gave the positive answer for the polynomials of degree 2 and 3 in
[
105
]
.

The positive answer to this question in the general case was given in Parusiński and Rainer
[
83
,
84
]

with two different proofs.
They both use in a substantial way the work of Ghisi and Gobbino
[
37
]

on the radical case, where the optimal regularity conditions were given. The paper
[
84
]
also contains the optimal Sobolev regularity of the roots in the general case, see below for precise statements.

4.1.
The case of radicals

The first result towards absolute continuity of the roots
is probably Lemma 1 in Colombini, Jannelli, and Spagnolo
[
25
]
which states that
for a real valued nonnegative function
f
of class
C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{I})
on a bounded open interval
I
, with

k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
0<\alpha\leq 1
, the real radical
f^{1/(k+\alpha)}
is absolutely continuous on
I
and satisfies

\|(f^{\frac{1}{k+\alpha}})^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(I)}^{k+\alpha}\leq C(k,\alpha,I)\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{I})}.

Tarama
[
108
]
extended this lemma to real valued functions (not necessarily nonnegative).
A better summability for the weak partial derivatives of
f^{1/(k+1)}
was obtained by Colombini and Lerner
[
26
]
for nonnegative
C^{k+1}
functions
f
of several real variables.

The case of radicals was settled by Ghisi and Gobbino
[
37
]
by
finding their optimal regularity.

Theorem 4.1

(Ghisi and Gobbino
[
37
, Theorem 2.2]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and let
f
be a real valued continuous function such that there exists

g\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{I})
so that
|f|^{k+\alpha{}}=|g|
on
I
. Then

f^{\prime}\in L_{w}^{p}(I)
, where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{k+\alpha}=1
, and

(32)

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}\|_{p,w,I}\leq C(k)\max\Big\{[\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,I}(g^{(k)})]^{\frac{1}{k+\alpha}}|I|^{\frac{1}{p}},\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}^{\frac{1}{k+\alpha}}\Big\};

in particular,
f
belongs to the Sobolev space
W^{1,q}(I)
for every
q\in[1,p)
.

Here
L^{p}_{w}(I)
denotes the weak Lebesgue space equipped with the quasinorm

\|f\|_{p,w,I}:=\sup_{r\geq 0}\big\{r\cdot\mathcal{L}^{1}(\{t\in I:|f(t)|>r\})^{\frac{1}{p}}\big\}
,
where
\mathcal{L}^{1}
is the one dimensional Lebesgue measure.
By
\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,I}(g^{(k)})
we mean the
\alpha
-Hölder constant of
g^{(k)}
on
I
, and
|I|=\mathcal{L}^{1}(I)
is the length of the interval
I
, see also Section
7
.

Ghisi and Gobbino also provided examples that show that the assumptions as well as the conclusion in their theorem are
best possible.

•

Take
g(t)=t
and
f(t)=|t|^{\frac{1}{k+\alpha}}
on the interval
I=(-1,1)
.
Then
f^{\prime}\not\in L^{p}(I)
for
p
defined by
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{k+\alpha}=1
.
So the summability of
f^{\prime}
in Theorem
4.1
is optimal. See
[
37
, Example 4.3]
.

•

In
[
37
, Example 4.4]
, a
C^{\infty}
function
g:I\to\mathbb{R}
on an interval
I=(0,r)
with the following properties is constructed:

–

g
belongs to
C^{k,\beta}(\overline{I})
for every
\beta<\alpha
but not for
\beta=\alpha
.

–

f:=|g|^{\frac{1}{k+\alpha}}
has unbounded variation on
I
and hence
f^{\prime}\not\in L^{1}(I)
.

So the differentiability assumption on
g
in Theorem
4.1
is optimal.

Higher order Glaeser inequalities
[
37
, Theorems 2.1 and 3.2]
play an important role in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
We recall a slightly more general statement in the next subsection, see Corollary
4.4
.

4.2.
Optimal Sobolev regularity of the roots

Theorem 4.2

(
[
84
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and let
P_{a}

be a monic polynomial (
31
) with complex valued
coefficients
a_{j}\in C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I})
,
j=1,\ldots,d
.
Let
\lambda\in C^{0}(I)
be a continuous root of
P_{a}
.
Then
\lambda
is absolutely continuous on
I
and belongs to the Sobolev space

W^{1,p}(I)
for every
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
. The derivative
\lambda^{\prime}
satisfies

(33)

\displaystyle\|\lambda^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(I)}

\displaystyle\leq C(d,p)\max\{1,|I|^{1/p}\}\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|a_{j}\|^{1/j}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I})},

where the constant
C(d,p)
depends only on
d
and
p
.

The above result can be understood as a complex analogue of Bronshtein’s theorem, see Theorem
3.2
.
Its proof given in
[
84
]
follows the strategy of the proof of
Bronshtein’s theorem as explained in Section
3.2
, though many details are
significantly more complex. The universal splitting
P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c}
allows the induction
on the degree of
P_{a}
.
After the Tschirnhausen transformation
P_{b}\leadsto P_{\tilde{b}}
the new polynomials

P_{\tilde{b}}
split again near points
t_{1}\in I
,
where not all
\tilde{b}_{i}
vanish. The central idea of the underlying induction is to show an inequality
between the combined coefficients of
P_{\tilde{a}}
and
P_{\tilde{b}}
and a similar expression for

P_{\tilde{b}}
and the polynomial issued from the splitting of
P_{\tilde{b}}
, that is
[
84
, Formula (18)]
.
The proof of this technical step is based on the radical case, Theorem
4.1
, and on the following interpolation lemma.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
84
, Lemma 4]
)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, and
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
If
f\in C^{m,\alpha}(\overline{I})
, then for all

and

(34)

\displaystyle|f^{(s)}(t)|\leq C|I|^{-s}\bigl(V_{I}(f)+V_{I}(f)^{(m+\alpha-s)/(m+\alpha)}(\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,I}(f^{(m)}))^{s/(m+\alpha)}|I|^{s}\bigr),

for a universal constant
C
depending only on
m
and
\alpha
.

Here

. As a corollary we get the following generalizations of
the higher order Glaeser inequalities of
[
37
]
.

Corollary 4.4

(
[
84
, Corollary 3]
)
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
Let
I=(t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta)

and

(35)

\displaystyle|f^{(s)}(t_{0})|\leq C|I|^{-s}\bigl(|f(t_{0})|+|f(t_{0})|^{(m+\alpha-s)/(m+\alpha)}(\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,I}(f^{(m)}))^{s/(m+\alpha)}|I|^{s}\bigr),

for a universal constant
C
depending only on

and

.

Note that the bound (

is not invariant under rescaling of the interval

in contrast to (

4.2.1.
Multivalued Sobolev functions

In
[
4
]
Almgren developed a theory of
d
-valued Sobolev functions.
A
d
-valued function is a mapping with values in the set
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
of unordered
d
-tuples of points in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let us denote by
[x]=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
the unordered
d
-tuple consisting of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
; then

[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]=[x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(d)}]
for each permutation
\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
.
The set
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m})=\{[x]=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]:x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\}
forms a
complete metric space when endowed with the metric

d([x],[y]):=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}-y_{\sigma(i)}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

This is (up to a constant factor) the Wasserstein metric
W_{2}
, see Example
6.8
.
Almgren proved that
there is an integer
N=N(d,m)
, a positive constant
C=C(d,m)
, and an
injective Lipschitz mapping
\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with Lipschitz constant

\operatorname{Lip}(\Delta)\leq 1
and
\operatorname{Lip}(\Delta|^{-1}_{\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))})\leq C
; moreover, there is a
Lipschitz retraction of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
onto
\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))
.

One can use this bi-Lipschitz embedding to define Sobolev spaces of
d
-valued functions:
for open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
define

W^{1,p}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m})):=\{f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{m}):\Delta\circ f\in W^{1,p}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})\}.

For an intrinsic definition, see
[
30
, Definition 0.5 and Theorem 2.4]
.

Let us identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
.
Theorem
4.2
implies a sufficient condition for a
d
-valued function
U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
to belong to the
Sobolev spaces
W^{1,p}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
for every
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
; see Theorem
4.7
below.

We shall use the following terminology. By a
parameterization
of a
d
-valued function
f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
we mean a
function
\varphi:U\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
f(x)=[\varphi(x)]=[\varphi_{1}(x),\ldots,\varphi_{d}(x)]
for all
x\in U
.
Let
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be defined by
\pi(z):=[z]
; it is a Lipschitz mapping with Lipschitz constant
\operatorname{Lip}(\pi)=1
.
Then a parameterization of
f
amounts to a lifting
\varphi
of
f
over
\pi
, i.e.,

f=\pi\circ\varphi
.
The elementary symmetric polynomials induce a bijective mapping

a:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

a_{j}([z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]):=(-1)^{j}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{j}}z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{j}},\quad 1\leq j\leq d.

In other words, the monic complex polynomials of degree
d
are in one-to-one correspondence
with their unordered
d
-tuples of roots.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
84
, Theorem 6]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
let
f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be continuous.
If
a\circ f\in C^{d-1,1}(U,\mathbb{C}^{d})
, then
f\in W^{1,p}(V,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
for each relatively compact open
V\Subset U

and each
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
.
Moreover,

\|\nabla(\Delta\circ f)\|_{L^{p}(V)}\leq C(d,n,p,\mathcal{K},\Delta)\big(1+\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\|a_{j}\circ f\|^{1/j}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{W})}\big),

where
\mathcal{K}
is any finite cover of
\overline{V}
by open boxes
\prod_{i=1}^{d}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})

contained in
U
and
W=\bigcup\mathcal{K}
.

In particular, the roots of a polynomial
P_{a}
of degree
d
with coefficients

a_{j}\in C^{d-1,1}(U)
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, form a
d
-valued function

\lambda:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
which belongs to
W^{1,p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
for each
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
;
in fact, it is well-known that
\lambda:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is
continuous (cf.
[
52
]
or
[
91
, Theorem 1.3.1]

4.4.1.
Bounded variation of the roots

There is nevertheless an important difference between the one parameter and the multiparameter case.
Recall that by a theorem of Kato
[
52
, II.5.2]

a continuous family of complex monic polynomials depending on one real parameter always admits a continuous choice of roots. This is
no longer true if the dimension of the parameter space is at least two. In that case, monodromy may
prevent the existence of continuous roots. For instance, the radical
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}\ni(x+iy)\mapsto(x+iy)^{1/d}
,
d>1
,
does not admit continuous parameterizations near the origin.

Functions of bounded variation (
BV
) are integrable functions whose distributional derivative is a vector valued finite Radon measure (cf. Section
7.4
).
They form an algebra of discontinuous functions.
Due to their ability to deal with discontinuities, they are widely used in the applied sciences,
see e.g.
[
53
]
. It is shown by Parusiński and Rainer in
[
85
]
that
the roots of a polynomial (
38
) with coefficients in a differentiability class of
sufficiently high order can be represented by functions of bounded variation.

Theorem 4.11

(
[
85
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For all integers
d,n\geq 2
there exists an integer
k=k(d,n)\geq\max\{d,n\}
such that the following holds.
Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let (
38
)
be a monic polynomial of degree
d
with complex valued coefficients

a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in C^{k-1,1}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{C}^{d})
.

Then the roots of
P_{a}
admit a parameterization
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})
by
special functions of bounded variation (

) on
\Omega
such that

(40)

\|\lambda\|_{BV(\Omega)}\leq C(d,n,\Omega)\,\max\big\{1,\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\big\}\max\big\{1,\|a\|_{C^{k-1,1}(\overline{\Omega})}\big\}.

There is a finite collection of
C^{k-1}
hypersurfaces

in

such that
\lambda
is continuous in the complement of

.
Any hypersurface

is closed in an open subset of

but possibly not in

The main difficulty of the problem is to make a good choice of the discontinuity set

of the roots.
On the complement of

, the roots are of optimal Sobolev class

for all

[

In general, it is not possible to choose the discontinuity set

of finite codimension one Hausdorff measure

[

Thus, in order to have bounded variation it is crucial that the jump height of a selection of a root is
integrable (with respect to

) along its discontinuity set, see also the end of Section
6.1
.

The proof is based on the radical case, see
[
85
, Theorem 1.4]
, which is significantly simpler,
and on the formulas (

) for the roots of the universal polynomial

,

, obtained in

[
83
]
.
The result of Ghisi and Gobbino
[

is used in several places.
Interestingly, the method of
[

seems to be better suited for the control of
the discontinuities and integrability along them than a more elementary method of
[
84
]
.

5.
Lifting maps over invariants of group representations

5.1.
A reformulation of the regularity problem for hyperbolic polynomials

The regularity problem for the roots of hyperbolic polynomials can be seen as a special lifting problem:
let the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
act on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates.
The algebra of invariant polynomials
\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{R}^{d}]^{\operatorname{S}_{d}}
is generated by the elementary symmetric functions

\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{i}}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{i}},\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

Each point in the image of the map
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

represents, in view of Vieta’s formulas, a monic hyperbolic polynomial
P_{a}
of degree
d
, in a unique way.
The fiber of
\sigma
over this point is the orbit
\operatorname{S}_{d}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})=\{(\lambda_{\tau(1)},\ldots,\lambda_{\tau(d)}):\tau\in\operatorname{S}_{d}\}
, where

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
are the roots of
P_{a}
(with multiplicities). Thus
\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.

In this picture, a family of monic hyperbolic polynomials
P_{a}(x)
,
x\in U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is a map

p:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with image contained in
\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a system of roots for the family
P_{a}

is a lifting
\overline{p}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
p
over
\sigma
, i.e.,
\sigma\circ\overline{p}=p
.
The regularity problem can be rephrased as follows:
Given that
p
has some regularity, how regular can a lifting of
p
be chosen?

\textstyle{\mathbb{R}^{d}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sigma}
\textstyle{U\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{p}}
\scriptstyle{p}
\textstyle{\sigma(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

This setup can be generalized considerably and we will see that Bronshtein’s theorem
3.2
and many other results
on the perturbation theory of hyperbolic polynomials hold in greater generality.

We shall also discuss a generalization of the nonhyperbolic case along the same lines in Section
5.5

and subsequent sections.

5.2.
Orthogonal representations of compact Lie groups

Let
G
be a compact Lie group and let
\rho:G\rightarrow\operatorname{O}(V)
be an
orthogonal representation in a real finite dimensional Euclidean
vector space
V
with inner product
\langle\cdot\mid\cdot\rangle
. We will often just write
G\circlearrowleft V
.
In particular,
G
may be a finite group.
By a classical theorem of Hilbert and Nagata,
the algebra
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}
of invariant polynomials on
V

is finitely generated.
So let
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a system of homogeneous generators
of
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}
and call it a system of
basic invariants
.

A system of basic invariants
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

is called
minimal
if there is no polynomial relation of the
form
\sigma_{i}=P(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\sigma_{i}},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
, or equivalently,

\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
induces a basis of the real vector space
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}_{+}/(\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}_{+})^{2}
,
where
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}_{+}=\{f\in\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}:f(0)=0\}
; cf.
[
31
, Section 3.6]
.
The elements in a minimal system of basic invariants may not be unique, but their number and their degrees

d_{i}:=\deg\sigma_{i}
are unique. Let us set

d:=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}d_{i}

and consider the
orbit map

\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}):V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The image
\sigma(V)
is a semialgebraic set in the categorical quotient

V/\!\!/G:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:P(y)=0~\mbox{for all}~P\in\mathscr{I}\}
,
where
\mathscr{I}
is the ideal of relations between
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}
.
Since
G
is compact,
\sigma
is proper and separates orbits of
G
, and
it thus induces a homeomorphism between the orbit space
V/G
and
\sigma(V)
.

Note that, by the differentiable slice theorem,
V/G
is a local model for the orbit space of differentiable
G
-manifolds.

The given data, i.e., the representation

G\circlearrowleft V
, a fixed system of basic invariants
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with degrees
d_{i}=\deg\sigma_{i}
,
and the corresponding
orbit map
\sigma
, will be abbreviated by the

(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
.

In this setting, we may consider the following lifting problem:
let

,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, be a map with some regularity (
C^{\omega}
,
C^{\infty}
,
C^{p}
, etc.)
and image contained in
\sigma(V)
; for brevity we will write

, where
\mathscr{C}
is the respective
regularity class.
How regular can a lifting
\overline{f}:U\to V
of

be?

\textstyle{V\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\sigma}
\textstyle{U\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\overline{f}}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{\sigma(V)}

Note that any two systems of basic invariants differ by a polynomial diffeomorphism.
Thus this question is independent of the choice of
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and hence of
\sigma
.

5.2.1.

The results known for this lifting problem generalize the results for hyperbolic polynomials.
The role of the polynomial degree (as an order of differentiablity of the coefficients needed to guarantee

5.3.
The main tools

Let us briefly discuss the main tools used for the lifting problem
and identify their counterpart in the study of hyperbolic polynomials.

5.3.1.
Removing fixed points

Let
V^{G}:=\{v\in V:Gv=v\}
be the linear subspace of fixed points
and let
V^{\prime}
be its orthogonal complement in
V
.
Then
V=V^{G}\oplus V^{\prime}
,
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}=\mathbb{R}[V^{G}]\otimes\mathbb{R}[V^{\prime}]^{G}
, and
V/G=V^{G}\times V^{\prime}/G
.
Thus it suffices to study the lifting problem for
G\circlearrowleft V^{\prime}
or equivalently to assume
V^{G}=\{0\}
.
This corresponds to the Tschirnhausen transformation (see Section
2.2.1
); indeed,

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\operatorname{S}_{d}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{d}\}
and

(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\prime}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}+x_{2}+\cdots+x_{d}=0\}
.

5.3.2.
Dominant invariant

We may assume that
v\mapsto\langle v\mid v\rangle=\|v\|^{2}
belongs to the system of basic invariants
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
say it is
\sigma_{1}
. This does not change

; in fact,
V^{G}=\{0\}
implies

.
Then
\sigma_{1}
is dominant in the sense that

|\sigma_{i}|^{1/d_{i}}\leq C(\sigma)\,|\sigma_{1}|^{1/d_{1}},\quad 1\leq i\leq n,

by homogeneity. This corresponds to the dominance of the second coefficient of monic hyperbolic polynomials in Tschirnhausen form (see Lemma
2.4
).

5.3.3.
The slice theorem

For

, let

be the normal subspace of the orbit

at

.
It carries a natural action

of the

The crossed product (or associated bundle)

5.3.4.
Orbit type stratification

The conjugacy class

By

5.4.3.
Polynomials with symmetries

In
[
71

the lifting problem was used to improve upon the regularity problem for hyperbolic polynomials with symmetries.
The idea is the following.
A curve of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree

is a curve

in the semialgebraic set

where
\sigma
consists of the elementary symmetric functions

.
If

lies in some proper semialgebraic subset of
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)

has to fulfill more constraints and thus,
in general, the conditions that guarantee the existence of differentiable

\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
then

lies in

. The symmetries of the roots are represented by the induced action

of

The solution of the lifting problem can be brought to bear, if the restrictions
\sigma_{i}|_{V}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, generate
\mathbb{R}[V]^{G}
.
Indeed, the orbit type stratification of
G\circlearrowleft V
is generally coarser than the restriction to
V
of the orbit type stratification of
\operatorname{S}_{d}\circlearrowleft\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

5.5.
A reformulation of the regularity problem for general polynomials

If we let the symmetric group

5.6.

An algebraic group

By Hilbert’s finiteness theorem, for rational representations

In a

The map

. In fact,
for every point

On the other hand, if

is a geometric quotient

We write again

(d) The constant is also of the form (
44
)
if the curve
c
satisfies
c^{(j)}(\alpha)=c^{(j)}(\beta)=0
for all
j=1,\ldots,d-1
.

Note that, in view of the counterexamples for the regularity of the roots of polynomials,
the results of Theorem
5.12
(as most of the subsequent results) are optimal.

The lifting of mappings defined in open domains of dimension
m>1
essentially
admits the same regularity as for curves, provided that continuous lifting is possible.
But, in general, there are topological obstructions for continuous lifting.

Theorem 5.14

(
[
86
, Theorem 1.4]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a complex finite dimensional representation of a finite group
G
.
Let
f\in C^{d-1,1}(\overline{\Omega},\sigma(V))
,
where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open bounded box
\Omega=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.
Then each continuous lifting
\overline{f}:U\to V
of
f
over
\sigma

defined on an open subset
U\subseteq\Omega

belongs to
W^{1,p}(U,V)

and satisfies

(45)

\|\nabla\overline{f}\|_{L^{p}(U)}\leq C(G\circlearrowleft V,\Omega,m,p)\,\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|f_{j}\|^{1/d_{j}}_{C^{d-1,1}(\overline{\Omega})}

for all
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
.

The case
U=\Omega
is not excluded.
It is clear that Theorem
5.14
implies a version of the statement, where

\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is any bounded open set,

U\Subset\Omega
is relatively compact open in
\Omega
,
and the constant also depends on
U
(or more precisely on a cover of
U
by boxes contained in
\Omega
).
Concerning a global result we have the following.

Remark 5.15
.

If
G\circlearrowleft V
is coregular, then Theorem
5.14
holds as stated for any bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
; cf.
[
86
, Remark 1.5]
.

If there is no continuous lifting, it is natural to ask how bad the discontinuities can be:

Open Problem 5.16
.

When continuous lifting is impossible,
do there exist liftings of bounded variation?

See Theorem
5.23
for a partial answer.

In the general case, for an infinite linearly reductive group
G
, it is not clear if a continuous curve in
\sigma(V)
admits a continuous lifting to
V
. The
notion of
stability
in geometric invariant theory provides a remedy.
A point
v\in V
is called
stable
if the orbit
Gv
is closed and the isotropy group

G_{v}
is finite.
The subset
V^{s}\subseteq V
of stable points is
G
-invariant and open in
V
,
and its image
\sigma(V^{s})
is open in
V{/\!\!/}G\cong\sigma(V)
(cf.
[
78
, Proposition 5.15]
).
The restriction
\sigma:V^{s}\to\sigma(V^{s})
of the map
\sigma
provides a one-to-one correspondence
between points in
\sigma(V^{s})\cong V^{s}/G
and
G
-orbits in
V^{s}
, that is
V^{s}/G
is a geometric quotient.
A continuous curve
c:I\to\sigma(V^{s})
has a continuous lifting
\overline{c}:I\to V^{s}
,
cf.
[
86
, Lemma 1.6]
.
Theorem
5.12
has the following corollary.

Corollary 5.17

(
[
86
, Theorem 1.7]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational complex finite dimensional representation of a linearly reductive group
G
.
Let
c\in C^{d-1,1}([\alpha,\beta],\sigma(V^{s}))
be a curve defined on a compact interval
[\alpha,\beta]
with

c([\alpha,\beta])\subseteq\sigma(V^{s})
.
Then there exists an absolutely continuous lifting

\overline{c}:[\alpha,\beta]\to V^{s}
of
c
over
\sigma
which
belongs to
W^{1,p}([\alpha,\beta],V^{s})

with

(46)

\|\overline{c}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}([\alpha,\beta])}\leq C(G\circlearrowleft V,[\alpha,\beta],p)\,\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|c_{j}\|^{1/d_{j}}_{C^{d-1,1}([\alpha,\beta])}

for all
1\leq p<d/(d-1)
.

For a mapping
f
defined on a compact subset
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
f(K)\subseteq\sigma(V^{s})
the situation is
more complicated:
we can apply Theorem
5.14
to the slice representations at any point
v\in V^{s}
,
but it is not clear if these local (and partial) lifts can be glued in a continuous fashion.

More can be said for polar representations (which include e.g. the adjoint actions).
We encountered polar representations already in Section
5.2
in the real setup.
Let us briefly discuss them in the framework of rational representations
V
of linearly reductive groups
G
;
we follow
[
29
]
.
Let
v\in V
be such that the orbit
Gv
is closed and consider the linear subspace

\Sigma_{v}=\{x\in V:\mathfrak{g}x\subseteq\mathfrak{g}v\}
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
.
All orbits that intersect
\Sigma_{v}
are closed, whence
\dim\Sigma_{v}\leq\dim V{/\!\!/}G
.
The representation
G\circlearrowleft V
is said to be
polar
if there exists
v\in V
with closed orbit
Gv

and
\dim\Sigma_{v}=\dim V{/\!\!/}G
. Then
\Sigma_{v}
is called a
section
or
Cartan subspace
of
V
.
Any two sections are
G
-conjugate. Let us fix a section
\Sigma
.
All closed orbits in
V
intersect
\Sigma
.
The
Weyl group

W:=N_{G}(\Sigma)/Z_{G}(\Sigma)
, where
N_{G}(\Sigma)=\{g\in G:g\Sigma=\Sigma\}
is the
normalizer and
Z_{G}(\Sigma)=\{g\in G:gx=x\text{ for all }x\in\Sigma\}
is the centralizer of
\Sigma
in
G
,
is finite and the intersection of any closed
G
-orbit in
V
with
\Sigma
is precisely
one
W
-orbit.
The ring
\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}
is isomorphic via restriction to the ring
\mathbb{C}[\Sigma]^{W}
.
If
G
is connected, then
W
is a pseudoreflection group and hence
\mathbb{C}[V]^{G}\cong\mathbb{C}[\Sigma]^{W}
is a polynomial ring,
by the Shephard–Todd–Chevalley theorem.

Corollary 5.18

(
[
86
, Theorem 1.8]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a polar representation of a linearly reductive group
G
. Then:

(1)

Each curve

has an absolutely continuous lifting
\overline{c}:(\alpha,\beta)\to V
of

over

which belongs to

for all

and satisfies (

).

(2)

Let

,
where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an open bounded box
\Omega=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.
Each continuous lifting
\overline{f}
defined on an open subset

with values in a section
\Sigma

is of class
W^{1,p}
on

for all

and satisfies (

).

(3)

If

is connected, then the constant in (

)
and

This follows from applying Theorem
5.12
and Theorem

to

Open Problem 5.19

To what extent are the above results true for general (nonpolar) representation?

5.6.2.
Analytic lifting

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a complex finite dimensional representation of a finite group
G
.
Then the orbit space coincides with
the categorical quotient
V{/\!\!/}G
which is a normal affine variety.
Thus the orbit space has the natural structure of a complex analytic
set and there are several types of morphisms into
V{/\!\!/}G
, like
regular, rational, or holomorphic.
In
[
58
]
those regular, holomorphic, or formal maps into
V{/\!\!/}G

that admit a
regular, holomorphic, or formal lifting to
V
are characterized.
The conditions are formulated in terms of
the orbit type stratification of the orbit space
which, in this case (compare with Section
5.3.4
), is finer than its stratification as affine variety
and with the use of spaces of jets at
0\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
of morphisms
\mathbb{C}^{m}\to V{/\!\!/}G

of finite and infinite order.

Now let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational representation of a linearly reductive group
G
.
Suppose that
c:\mathbb{R}\to V{/\!\!/}G\cong\sigma(V)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
is real analytic.
In contrast to the real case, there does not always exist a real analytic lifting of
c
over
\sigma
.
But there is the following generalization of Puiseux’s theorem.

Theorem 5.20

(
[
70
, Theorem 3.3 and 3.4]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational representation of a linearly reductive group
G
. Then:

(1)

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval,
t_{0}\in I
, and
c\in C^{\omega}(I,\sigma(V))
.
Then there exists a positive integer
N

such that, locally near
t_{0}
,

t\mapsto c(t_{0}\pm(t-t_{0})^{N})
admits a real analytic lifting
\overline{c}_{\pm}
to
V
.

(2)

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open and connected,
z_{0}\in U
, and
c\in\mathcal{H}(U,\sigma(V))
, i.e.,
c
is holomorphic.
Then there exists a positive integer
N

such that, locally near
z_{0}
,

z\mapsto c(z_{0}+(z-z_{0})^{N})
admits a holomorphic lifting
\overline{c}
to
V
.

In analogy to Section
5.2.2
,
one defines that
c
is normally nonflat at
t_{0}
, see
[
70
, Section 2.5]
;
roughly speaking, it means that

c
does not meet lower dimensional strata of the orbit type stratification of
V{/\!\!/}G

with infinite order of flatness at
t_{0}
.
Then in (1) we may take
c\in C^{\infty}(I,\sigma(V))
if
c
is normally nonflat at
t_{0}

and conclude that
\overline{c}_{\pm}
is of class
C^{\infty}
. Or we may work with suitable quasianalytic subclasses of
C^{\infty}
.

As a consequence, we may conclude that real analytic curves in
\sigma(V)
have liftings that are locally absolutely continuous.

Theorem 5.21

(
[
70
, Theorem 5.4]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational representation of a linearly reductive group
G
.
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open interval.
Each
c\in C^{\omega}(I,\sigma(V))
admits a lifting
\overline{c}:I\to V
that is locally absolutely continuous.

Again this remains true for smooth curves in
\sigma(V)
that are normally nonflat.

For maps in several variables into
\sigma(V)
there is an analogue of Theorem
5.8
, where, however,
local blowings-up
and
local power substitutions must be used.
A
local power substitution
is the composite of the inclusion of a coordinate chart
U

and a map
W\to U
given in local coordinates by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\mapsto((-1)^{\epsilon_{1}}x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}},\ldots,(-1)^{\epsilon_{m}}x_{m}^{\gamma_{m}}),

for some integers
\gamma_{i}\geq 1
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

Theorem 5.22

(
[
93
, Theorem 4.6]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational representation of a linearly reductive group
G
.
Let
M
be a real analytic manifold and
f\in C^{\omega}(M,\sigma(V))
.
For each compact
K\subseteq M
,
there exist a neighborhood
U
of
K
in
M

and a finite covering
\{\pi_{j}:U_{j}\to U\}

of
U
, where each
\pi_{j}
is a composite of finitely many
maps each of which is either a local blowing-up with smooth center
or a local power substitution,
such that, for all
j
,
the map
f\circ\pi_{j}
has a real analytic lifting on
U_{j}
.

The theorem remains true for suitable quasianalytic subclasses of
C^{\infty}

as well as for holomorphic maps. In the latter case,
a local power substitution is simply a map
of the form

(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\mapsto(z_{1}^{\gamma_{1}},\ldots,z_{m}^{\gamma_{m}})
.

It is possible to extract information about the existence of weak liftings
of
f
(without modification of its domain). For instance, there is the following result
which gives a partial answer to Open Problem
5.16
.

Theorem 5.23

(
[
93
, Theorem 6.11]
)
.

Let
(G\circlearrowleft V,d,\sigma)
be a rational representation of a linearly reductive group
G
.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
f\in C^{\omega}(U,\sigma(V))
.
For each compact
K\subseteq U
,
there exist a neighborhood

of
K
in

of

Actually,

5.7.
Some remarks on the proofs

We focus on the proof of Theorem
5.12
.
The foundation of the proof is that the result holds for finite rotation groups
C_{d}\cong\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
acting
in the standard way on
\mathbb{C}
,
where
\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{C}]^{C_{d}}
is generated by
z\mapsto z^{d}
and a lifting of a map
f
is a solution of the radical equation

z^{d}=f
. This follows from Theorem
4.1
.
Among all representations of finite groups
G
of a fixed order
|G|
,
it is the one
with the worst loss of
regularity, since in general
d\leq|G|
, by Noether’s degree bound, and equality can only happen for
cyclic groups.

The strategy in the general case is similar to the one described in Section
5.3
.
Evidently, one may reduce to the case that the linear subspace

V^{G}
of invariant vectors is trivial. Then
Luna’s slice theorem (see
[
72
]
or
[
102
, Theorem 5.3]
) allows us to reduce the problem locally to the
slice representation
G_{v}\circlearrowleft N_{v}
of the isotropy group
G_{v}
on
N_{v}
, where

T_{v}V\cong T_{v}(Gv)\oplus N_{v}
is a
G_{v}
-splitting. Since in our case
G
is finite, we have
N_{v}\cong V
.
The assumption
V^{G}=\{0\}
entails that for all
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
the isotropy group
G_{v}

is a proper subgroup of
G
which suggests to use induction on the group order.
For this induction scheme to work it is important that the slice reduction is uniform in the sense that it
does not depend on the parameter
t
of the curve
c
in
\sigma(V)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The lack of a dominant invariant polynomial (in contrast to the real case)
forces one to choose at points
t_{0}
with
c(t_{0})\neq 0
a
dominant
component
c_{k}
of
c
such that

|c_{k}^{1/d_{k}}(t_{0})|=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|c_{j}^{1/d_{j}}(t_{0})|

and to work on small neighborhoods of
t_{0}
with this distinguished component
c_{k}
.

The better bound in the case that
G\circlearrowleft V
is coregular (cf. Remark
5.13
; see also Remark
5.15
and Corollary
5.18
(3))
is achieved by an application of Whitney’s extension theorem to
extend the curve
c
to a larger interval on which it can be suitably modified without changing it on the original interval
(\alpha,\beta)
.
Coregularity guarantees that the extended curve still lies in
\sigma(V)
(because
\sigma(V)=\mathbb{C}^{n}
).

6.
Applications

6.1.
Zero sets of smooth functions

It is well-known that every closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the zero set
Z_{f}
of some
real valued
C^{\infty}
function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. So in general
Z_{f}
can be
arbitrarily irregular. On the other hand, constraints for the derivatives of
f

entail regularity properties of
Z_{f}
; think of the implicit function theorem or
of the solutions of elliptic and parabolic PDEs.

Let us assume here that our function
f
vanishes to some finite order
\gamma
at a
point
x_{0}
. Then Malgrange’s preparation theorem puts one in the position to study

Z_{f}
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
, by using the regularity theorems for the roots of polynomials,
that is Theorem
4.2
or Theorem
4.9
. Indeed,
in suitable local coordinates at
x_{0}
, we have
f=p\cdot u
, where

p
is a monic polynomial in one of the coordinates with
C^{\infty}
coefficients in the other
coordinates and
u
is a
C^{\infty}
unit.

This setting is widely applicable,
for instance, to solutions of second order elliptic equations,
Laplace eigenfunctions and finite linear combinations of such (
[
32
]
,
[
68
]
,
[
50
]
), etc.
Often
Z_{f}
is called the
nodal set
of
f
, but we will stick to the term

zero set
.

The following result is taken from Bär
[
8
]
.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
8
, Lemma 3]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{\infty}
function that vanishes to order
\gamma
at
x_{0}
.
Then there exist
r_{0}>0
and an affine hyperplane
H
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

Z_{f}\cap B(x_{0},r_{0})
is contained in the union of countably many graphs of
C^{\infty}
functions
from
H\cap B(x_{0},r_{0})
to
H^{\bot}
.
Moreover, for all
r<r_{0}
,

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{f}\cap B(x_{0},r))\leq n2^{n-1}\gamma\,r^{n-1}.

Note that Yomdin
[
116
]
obtained a similar result;
he assumed that the partial derivatives of some order
j
of
f
are “small” in a certain
precise sense and concluded that
f
behaves in many regards like a polynomial of degree
j-1
,
in particular, it has vanishing order at most
j-1
.

The following theorem is a consequence of Theorem
4.2
or Theorem
4.9
(together with Malgrange’s preparation theorem),
due to Beck, Becker-Kahn, and Hanin
[
10
]
.

Theorem 6.2

(
[
10
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and let
\Theta
be a compact smooth manifold (possibly with boundary).
Let
f\in C^{\infty}(U\times\Theta)
and write
f^{\theta}:=f(\cdot,\theta)
for
\theta\in\Theta
.
Suppose that
f^{\theta_{0}}
has finite vanishing order
\gamma
at
x_{0}\in U
.
Then there exist
p>1
,
r_{0}>0
, a neighborhood
V
of
\theta_{0}
in
\Theta
, an affine hyperplane

H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and functions
f_{i}^{\theta}\in W^{1,p}(H,H^{\bot})
,
1\leq i\leq\gamma
,
\theta\in V
,
with

\sup_{1\leq i\leq\gamma,\,\theta\in V}\|f_{i}^{\theta}\|_{W^{1,p}}<\infty

such that

Z_{f^{\theta}}\cap B(x_{0},r_{0})\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\gamma}\Gamma_{f_{i}^{\theta}},\quad\theta\in V,

where
\Gamma_{f^{\theta}_{i}}
is the graph of
f^{\theta}_{i}
.

Remark 6.3
.

Theorem
6.2
was proved in
[
10
]
based on Theorem
4.9
.
Using the optimal result, Theorem
4.2
, in the proof,
one can get more precise information on
p
: the statement is true for all
1\leq p<\gamma/(\gamma-1)
.

By the same strategy, one gets similar parameterization results in the setting studied by Yomdin
[
116
]

(where the partial derivatives of some order are small)
as well as for
C^{\infty}
functions all of whose derivatives are suitably controlled by a weight sequence
(e.g. functions in a quasianalytic Denjoy–Carleman class),
see Rainer
[
97
]
. In the latter case, the vanishing order is bounded (globally on a convex body)
by a quantity computed from the weight sequence
which has similarities with the polynomial degree.

Note that, if one is interested only in the properties of the zero set of a function near a
point of finite vanishing order, then there is some freedom in the choice of the local coordinates
(i.e., the hyperplane
H
in Theorem
6.2
).

Open Problem 6.4
.

Is is possible to improve the above results by choosing the local system of coordinates wisely?

As a consequence of Theorem
6.1
or Theorem
6.2
, one gets
a nonconcentration estimate
[
10
, Proposition 4]
: suppose that the vanishing order of
f^{\theta}
is at most
\gamma
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
For compact
K\subseteq U
and
m
-rectifiable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some
m\leq n-1
,
there exists
r_{0}>0
such that

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{f^{\theta}}\cap E_{r}^{\prime}\cap K)\leq C(n)\,r^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{n}(E_{r}),\quad r\leq r_{0},

for all
\theta\in\Theta
and all images
E^{\prime}
of Lipschitz maps
E\to K
with Lipschitz constant
\leq 1
,
where
E_{r},E_{r}^{\prime}
denote the
r
-neighborhoods of
E,E^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This entails the following continuity result.
Let
\operatorname{Sing}_{f}:=\{x\in Z_{f}:\nabla f(x)=0\}
.

Corollary 6.5

(
[
10
, Corollary 2]
)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
f\in C^{\infty}(U\times[0,1])

such that
f^{1}:=f(\cdot,1)
has
finite vanishing order on
U
.
Then for any compact
K\subseteq U
with
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(K\cap\operatorname{Sing}_{f})=0

we have

\lim_{\theta\to 1}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{f^{\theta}}\cap K)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{f^{1}}\cap K).

In particular, the authors of
[
10
]
conclude a continuity result for the zero sets of
the heat flow on a compact smooth Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
: if
u:M\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}

solves

(\partial_{t}+\Delta_{g})u(x,t)=0

with initial data
u^{0}\in L^{2}(M)
, then

\lim_{t\to 1}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{u^{t}})=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(Z_{\psi}),

where
\psi
is the first nonzero eigenspace projection of
u^{0}
and the time variable
underwent a change from
t
to
\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan(t)
.

Another application of Theorem
6.2
presented in
[
10
]

is that, given
C^{\infty}
functions
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}
on a compact Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
of dimension
n

such that for all
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}\setminus\{0\}

the vanishing order of
\theta_{0}f_{0}+\cdots+\theta_{p}f_{p}
is at most
\gamma
on
M
,
the map

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{p}\ni[\theta_{0}:\cdots:\theta_{p}]\mapsto\partial\{\theta_{0}f_{0}+\cdots+\theta_{p}f_{p}<0\}

into the space of modulo 2 flat
(n-1)
-cycles in
M

is an admissible
p
-sweepout (see
[
10
]
for the definition). This was proposed by Marques and Neves in
[
74
]
,
where they settled a conjecture of Yau on the existence of infinitely many minimal surfaces in
the setting of positive Ricci curvature.
This proof uses an argument involving the growth in
p
of the min-max width
\omega_{p}(M)
which is defined
in terms of admissible
p
-sweepouts and is a nonlinear version of the spectrum of the Laplacian
(cf. Gromov
[
41
]
).
In fact, by Guth
[
43
]
, one has

\omega_{p}(M)\leq C\,p^{1/n},

which is reproved in
[
10
, Theorem 3]
as a corollary of Theorem
6.2
.

Let us finish this section with a few consequences of the results for radicals, i.e.  Theorem
4.1
,
due to Ghisi and Gobbino
[
37
]
and the co-area formula.
These facts play an essential role in the proof of the existence of
BV
roots, Theorem
4.11
, in
[
85
]
.
They concern the behavior of differentiable functions near their zero set and
the level sets of their sign; they are interesting in their own right.
Note that there is no assumption on the vanishing order.

Theorem 6.6

(
[
85
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and set
s=k+\alpha
.
Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and

f\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1})
,
f\not\equiv 0
.
Then there is a constant
C=C(n,\ell,k,\alpha,\Omega)
such that
for all
0<\epsilon\leq 1
and all small
\delta>0
we have

\big|\big\{y\in(0,\delta):y^{1/s}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(|f|^{-1}(y))\geq\epsilon^{-1}C\,\|f\|^{1/s}_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\big\}\big|\leq\epsilon\delta.

In particular,
if
A\subseteq[0,\infty)
has full measure near
0
, i.e.,

|A\cap[0,\epsilon)|=\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
,
then there is a sequence
A\ni y_{j}\to 0
with

\sup_{j}\Big(y_{j}^{1/s}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(|f|^{-1}(y_{j}))\Big)\leq C\,\|f\|^{1/s}_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

That means that the
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-measures of the level sets
\{|f|=y_{j}\}
do not grow faster than
y_{j}^{-1/s}

as
y_{j}\to 0
.

The
sign
of
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
is the map
\operatorname{sgn}(f):\Omega\setminus f^{-1}(0)\to\mathbb{S}^{\ell}
given by

\operatorname{sgn}(f):=\frac{f}{|f|}.

Theorem 6.7

(
[
85
, Theorem 3.3]
)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and set
s=k+\alpha
.
Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain
and
f\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1})
, where
n\geq\ell\geq 1
.
Then there is a constant
C=C(n,\ell,k,\alpha,\Omega)
such that
for each small
\epsilon>0

\mathcal{H}^{\ell}\Big(\Big\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{\ell}:\int_{\operatorname{sgn}(f)^{-1}(y)}|f|^{\ell/s}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}\geq\epsilon^{-1}\,C\,\|f\|^{\ell/s}_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\Big\}\Big)\leq\epsilon.

In particular, we have
\ell/s
-integrability with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}
of
f
along almost every level set of the sign of
f
:

\int_{\operatorname{sgn}(f)^{-1}(y)}|f|^{\ell/s}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}<\infty,\quad\text{ for $\mathcal{H}^{\ell}$-a.e. }y\in\mathbb{S}^{\ell}.

Moreover,
for every relatively compact open
K\Subset\Omega\setminus f^{-1}(0)
,

\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}\big(K\cap\operatorname{sgn}(f)^{-1}(y)\big)<\infty,\quad\text{ for $\mathcal{H}^{\ell}$-a.e. }y\in\mathbb{S}^{\ell}.

6.2.
Extension to the optimal transport between algebraic hypersurfaces

A recent paper
[
5
]
, by Antonini, Cavalletti, and Lerario, contains an interesting reinterpretation and
extension of the regularity problem of the roots, Section
4
,
to the study of the Wasserstein distance on the space of
d
-degree hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth compact Riemannian manifold and let
\mathscr{P}(M)
denote the space of Borel probability measures on
M
. Fix
q\geq 1
. For any
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathscr{P}(M)
,
the
q
-Wasserstein distance
is defined by

W_{q}(\mu_{0},\mu_{1}):=\left(\inf_{\xi\in\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\int_{M\times M}\mathsf{d}_{g}(x,y)^{q}\,\xi(dxdy)\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where
\mathsf{d}_{g}
is the geodesic distance on
M
and
\Pi(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})

is the set of transport plans between
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, i.e.,
the set of probability measures
\xi
on
M\times M
with marginals
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}

(the push-forward measures of
\xi
with respect to the natural projections).
Intuitively, this distance minimizes the transport cost from
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
over all such plans.

Denote by
H_{n,d}
the space of complex homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
n+1
variables.
Let
P_{n,d}:=\mathbb{P}(H_{n,d})
be the projectivization. Note that
P_{n,d}
can be identified with

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{N},N=\binom{n+d}{d}-1
. Let
Z_{P}
denote the zero set of
P\in H_{n,d}
.
This set depends only on the class of
P
in
P_{n,d}
.
Therefore, to simplify the notation, we will consider the zero sets
Z_{P}
of
P\in P_{n,d}
, abusing the terminology slightly.

Let us associate to each
P\in P_{n,d}
a measure
\mu(P)\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})

as follows.
If
Z_{P}
is nonsingular, then
we take the restriction to
{Z_{P}}
of the Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{2n-2}
(with respect to the Fubini–Study metric of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
), normalized:

\mu(P):=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(Z_{P})}\,\mathcal{H}^{2n-2}\llcorner{Z_{P}},

where
\mathrm{vol}(Z_{P}):=(\mathcal{H}^{2n-2}\llcorner{Z_{P}})({Z_{P}})
.
(Note that all nonsingular hypersurfaces of degree
d
have the same volume

\mathrm{vol}(Z_{P})=d\,\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n-1})
so that the normalization factor does not depend on
P
.)

Denote by
\Delta_{n,d}\subseteq P_{n,d}
the discriminant locus, i.e., the set of polynomials
P\in P_{n,d}
such that

Z_{P}
is singular. It is a hypersurface in
P_{n,d}=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{N}
given by

\Delta_{n,d}:=\left\{P\in P_{n,d}:\exists z\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\},\,P(z)=\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_{0}}(z)=\cdots=\frac{\partial P}{\partial z_{n}}(z)=0\right\},

Using integral geometry (a Cauchy–Crofton kind of argument) one can extend the definition of
\mu(P)
to singular hypersurfaces,
that is for
P\in\Delta_{n,d}
, as follows, see
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
,

\int_{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}}f\,d\mu(P):=\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{G}(1,n)}\left(\sum_{z\in Z_{P}\cap\ell}m_{z}(P|_{\ell})f(z)\right)\mathrm{vol}_{\mathbb{G}(1,n)}(d\ell),\quad f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}),

where
{\mathbb{G}(1,n)}
stands for the set of projective lines in projective
n
-space,
which is equivalent to the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(2,n+1)
,
and
m_{z}(P|_{\ell})
is the multiplicity of
z
as a zero of
P|_{\ell}
.
Then for every
q\geq 1
, the map
\mu:P_{n,d}\to\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})

is continuous and injective.
(Here by
\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})
we mean
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})
with the Wasserstein distance
W_{q}
.)

Example 6.8

(
n=1
)
.

For simplicity,
we prefer to work with a polynomial of one complex variable

P\in\mathbb{C}[z]
. The passage to homogeneous polynomials of two complex variables
z_{0},z_{1}

is classical be means of homogenization, see e.g.
[
5
, Remark 3.2]
.

Consider the space of monic polynomials of degree
d
in one complex variable,

P_{a}(z)=z^{d}+a_{1}z^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d-1}z+a_{d}
,
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
To each polynomial
P=P_{a}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
one associates a probability measure

\mu(P)\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
defined by

\mu(P):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P(z)=0}m_{z}(P)\cdot\delta_{z},

where
m_{z}(P)\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the multiplicity of
z
as a zero of
P
and
\delta_{z}
is the Dirac measure. This defines a map

\mu:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C}).

Geometrically, the image of
\mu
can be identified with the quotient of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
under the action
of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
.
The
q
-th Wasserstein metric on
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

W_{q}([x],[y])=\Big({\frac{1}{d}}\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}-y_{\sigma(j)}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q},

where
[x]=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
and
[y]=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d}]
are unordered
d
-tuples. It is geodesically convex and
coincides with the restriction of the
q
-Wasserstein metric of
\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.

Contrary to the case
n=1
, in general, the image of
\mu
is not geodesically convex for the
W_{q}
-metric,
that is the geodesic joining
\mu(P_{0})
to
\mu(P_{1})
in
\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})
will in general not stay on
\mu(P_{n,d})
.
To overcome this issue it is proposed in
[
5
]
to consider
the inner
W_{q}
-distance, adopting the approach of
J.-D. Benamou and Y. Brenier. For this one needs the notion of a
q
-absolutely continuous curve in this setup.
A curve
\mu_{t}:I\to\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n})
belongs to
AC^{q}(I,\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}))
and is called
q
-absolutely continuous

if there is
f\in L^{q}(I)
such that

W_{q}(\mu_{s},\mu_{t})\leq\int_{s}^{t}f(\tau)\,d\tau,\quad\text{for all }s\leq t.

In that case, for
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-a.e.
t\in I
the limit
\lim_{h\to 0}W_{q}(\mu_{t+h},\mu_{t})/|h|
exists,
is denoted by
|\dot{\mu}_{t}|_{q}
, and called
metric speed
.
Then the
inner
W_{q}
-distance on
P_{n,d}
is defined by

W_{q}^{\textrm{in}}(P_{0},P_{1}):=\inf_{\mu_{t}:=\mu(\gamma(t))}\Big(\int_{I}|\dot{\mu}_{t}|_{q}^{q}\,dt\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where the infimum is taken over all
q
-absolutely continuous curves
\gamma:I=[0,1]\to P_{n,d}

joining
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
.

It is shown in Section 5.2 of
[
5
]
, see Theorem 5.13 and Remark 6.10 therein,
that the metric space
(P_{n,d},W_{q}^{\textrm{in}})
is complete and geodesic.
Moreover, away of the discriminant locus
\Delta_{n,d}
the
W_{q}^{\textrm{in}}
distance and the Fubini–Study distance

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{FS}}
on
P_{n,d}
defined via the identification
P_{n,d}=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{N}
are related as follows.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and denote

P_{n,d}(\epsilon):=\{p\in P_{n,d}:\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{FS}}(p,\Delta_{n,d})\geq\epsilon\}
.
Then the identity map

\mathrm{id}:(P_{n,d}(\epsilon),\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{FS}})\to(P_{n,d}(\epsilon),W_{q}^{\mathrm{in}})

is Lipschitz. In particular,
P_{n,d}(\epsilon)
is compact in the
W_{q}^{\mathrm{in}}
–topology.

In the proof, continuous semialgebraic curves are used.
By Puiseux’s theorem, they can be reparametrized, near their singular points, to get
C^{m}
regularity, for arbitrary finite
m
.

Another important question is the compactness of
(P_{n,d},W_{q}^{\mathrm{in}})
.
This problem was only partially solved in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 6.10

(
[
5
, Theorem 6.11]
)
.

There is
e(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for every
1\leq q<e(n)d/(e(n)d-1)
,
the complete and separable geodesic space
(P_{n,d},W_{q}^{\operatorname{in}})
is compact.

Theorem
4.2
is used in the proof of the above theorem to provide
q
-absolutely continuous curves as follows.
This is fairly straightforward for
n=1
because, any curve of polynomials

P_{a}(t)(z)=z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}(t)z^{d-j},\quad t\in[0,1],

with
a_{j}\in C^{d-1,1}([0,1])
, defines, after homogenization, an element of
AC^{q}([0,1];\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}))
for every
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
. Thus one may take
e(1)=1
.

Thanks to the uniformity of the bound of Theorem
4.2
,
the general case can be reduced to the case
n=1
by restriction to all affine lines parameterized by
{\mathbb{G}(1,n)}

and then integration over this Grassmannian, provided we can avoid the lines entirely included in
Z_{P}
.
This is always possible for generic
P
if
d>2n-3
,
[
5
, Theorem 6.3]
, but not in general.
Therefore it is proposed in
[
5
]
to use the integration over the group of unitary transformations of a
rational curve of sufficiently high degree (instead of a line).
It follows from a recent result of B. Lehmann, E. Riedl and S. Tanimoto that for a given curve of polynomials
P_{t}\in P_{n,d}

there is a rational curve of degree
e(n)
,
given by an explicit formula, such that none of its unitary transformations can be entirely included in
Z_{P_{t}}
.
For all details of the proof we refer the reader to
[
5
]
.

Open Problem 6.11
.

For which
q\geq 1
is the space
(P_{n,d},W_{q}^{\operatorname{in}})
compact?
In particular, is it always compact for
q=2
?

7.
Appendix A. Function spaces

7.1.
Hölder spaces

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open.
We denote by
C^{0}(\Omega)
the space of continuous complex valued functions on
\Omega
.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\cup\{\infty\}
,

C^{k}(\Omega)
is the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable functions,

C^{k}(\Omega)=\{f\in\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}:\partial^{\alpha}f\in C^{0}(\Omega)\text{ for }0\leq|\alpha|\leq k\},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

and

C^{\infty}(\Omega)=\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{N}}C^{k}(\Omega).

The space of real analytic functions on
\Omega
is denoted by
C^{\omega}(\Omega)
.
If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
is the space of holomorphic functions on
\Omega
.

If the open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded, we write
C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
for the space of
all
f\in C^{k}(\Omega)
such that
\partial^{\alpha}f
,
0\leq|\alpha|\leq k
, has a continuous extension to the closure

\overline{\Omega}
. Then
C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})
endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})}:=\sup_{|\alpha|\leq k}\sup_{x\in\Omega}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|

is a Banach space.
The spaces
C^{k}(\Omega)
,
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
C^{\omega}(\Omega)
, and
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)

carry their natural locally convex topologies, where
\Omega
is not necessarily bounded;
note that
C^{k}(\Omega)
,
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
are Fréchet spaces.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
and let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded.
A function is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous in
\Omega
if

\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,\Omega}(f):=\sup_{x,y\in\Omega,\,x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}<\infty.

It is
Lipschitz continuous in
\Omega
if it is
1
-Hölder continuous in
\Omega
; in that case, we write

\operatorname{Lip}_{\Omega}(f):=\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{1,\Omega}(f)<\infty.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, we have the scale of
Hölder(–Lipschitz) spaces

C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):=\{f\in C^{k}(\overline{\Omega}):\partial^{\beta}f\text{ is $\alpha$-H\"{o}lder continuous for }|\beta|=k\}

and we equip them with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}:=\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})}+\sup_{|\beta|=k}\operatorname{H\ddot{o}ld}_{\alpha,\Omega}(\partial^{\beta}f).

Then
C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
is a Banach space.
We write
C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)
(where
\Omega
is not necessarily bounded) for the space of all functions
f\in C^{k}(\Omega)
such that

\|f|_{\Omega^{\prime}}\|_{C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}^{\prime})}<\infty
for all relatively compact
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega

with its natural Fréchet topology.

7.2.
Lebesgue spaces and weak Lebesgue spaces

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
The
Lebesgue space

L^{p}(\Omega)
is the space of measurable complex valued functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
that are
p
-integrable
with respect to the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{n}
; actually, the elements of
L^{p}(\Omega)
are equivalence classes of functions
that coincide
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere.
If not stated otherwise, “measurable” always means “Lebesgue measurable”.
The
L^{p}
-norms make the Lebesgue spaces to Banach spaces:

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle:=\Big(\int_{\Omega}|f(x)|^{p}dx\Big)^{1/p},\quad 1\leq p<\infty,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|,\quad p=\infty.

We denote by
L^{p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\Omega)
the space of measurable functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\|f\|_{L^{p}(K)}<\infty
for each compact subset
K\subseteq\Omega
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded. For
1\leq p<\infty
, the

weak
L^{p}
-space
is the space of all measurable functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\|f\|_{p,w,\Omega}:=\sup_{r>0}\big(r\cdot\mathcal{L}^{n}(\{x\in\Omega:|f(x)|>r\})^{1/p}\big)<\infty;

again, we identify functions that coincide
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-almost everywhere.
Note that
\|\cdot\|_{p,w,\Omega}
is only a quasinorm: the triangle inequality fails, but for
f_{j}\in L^{p}_{w}(\Omega)
one still
has

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}\Big\|_{p,w,\Omega}\leq m\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|f_{j}\|_{p,w,\Omega}.

The spaces
L^{p}_{w}(\Omega)
are complete with respect to the quasinorms
\|\cdot\|_{p,w,\Omega}
, i.e., they are quasi-Banach spaces.
If
p>1
, then there exists a norm equivalent to
\|\cdot\|_{p,w,\Omega}
which makes
L^{p}_{w}(\Omega)
into a Banach space.
For
1\leq q<p<\infty
, we have

\|f\|_{q,w,\Omega}\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\leq\Big(\frac{p}{p-q}\Big)^{1/q}\mathcal{L}^{n}(\Omega)^{1/q-1/p}\,\|f\|_{p,w,\Omega}

and thus there are continuous strict inclusions
L^{p}_{w}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{q}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{q}_{w}(\Omega)
.
The
L^{p}_{w}
-quasinorm is
\sigma
-subadditive (i.e.,
\|f\|^{p}_{p,w,\Omega}\leq\sum_{j}\|f\|^{p}_{p,w,\Omega_{j}}
for
countable open covers
\Omega=\bigcup_{j}\Omega_{j}
) but not
\sigma
-additive.

7.3.
Sobolev spaces

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, we consider the
Sobolev space

W^{k,p}(\Omega):=\{f\in L^{p}(\Omega):\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{p}(\Omega)\text{ for }|\alpha|\leq k\},

where
\partial^{\alpha}f
are distributional derivatives, with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Then
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
are Banach spaces.
We denote by
W^{k,p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\Omega)
the space of functions
f\in L^{p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\Omega)
such that
\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{p}_{\operatorname{loc}}(\Omega)

for
|\alpha|\leq k
.

Let
k=1
and suppose
f\in L^{p}(\Omega)
.
Then
f\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
if and only if
f
has a representative
\overline{f}
that is absolutely continuous on almost all line segments in

\Omega
parallel to the coordinate axes and whose classical derivatives
\partial_{i}\overline{f}
belong to
L^{p}(\Omega)
.
In particular, for a bounded interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
W^{1,1}(I)
can be identified with the space
AC(I)
of absolutely continuous
functions on
I
.
We recall that, by definition,
f\in AC(I)
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}|f(b_{j})-f(a_{j})|\leq\epsilon

for every finite number of disjoint intervals
(a_{j},b_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,k
, satisfying
[a_{j},b_{j}]\subseteq I
and

\sum_{j=1}^{k}(b_{j}-a_{j})\leq\delta
.

In the case that
p=\infty
and that
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain,

f\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
if and only if
f
has a representative that is Lipschitz continuous on
\Omega
.

7.4.
Functions of bounded variation

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open.
A real valued
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is a
function of bounded variation in
\Omega

if the distributional derivative of
f
is representable
by a finite Radon measure in
\Omega
, i.e., for all
C^{\infty}
functions
\varphi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support in
\Omega
and all
1\leq i\leq n
,

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\partial_{i}\varphi(x)\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\,dD_{i}f,

for some
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued Radon measure
Df=(D_{1}f,\ldots,D_{n}f)
on
\Omega
.
By definition, a complex valued function is of bounded variation in
\Omega
if its real and imaginary parts are.
The space
BV(\Omega)
of all functions of bounded variation in
\Omega

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{BV(\Omega)}:=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+|Df|(\Omega),

where
|Df|(\Omega)
is the total variation measure, is a Banach space.

The Sobolev space
W^{1,1}(\Omega)
is strictly contained in
BV(\Omega)
(for
f\in W^{1,1}(\Omega)
we have
Df=\nabla f\,\mathcal{L}^{n}
).

Functions of bounded variation may have discontinuities.
Let
f\in BV(\Omega)
. Then the Lebesgue decomposition with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{n}
induces a decomposition

Df=D^{a}f+D^{j}f+D^{c}f,

where
D^{a}f=\nabla f\,\mathcal{L}^{n}
is the
absolutely continuous part
,

D^{j}f
is the
jump part
(given by integration of the jump height against the restriction of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
to the set of approximate jump points
J_{f}
,
i.e.,
D^{j}f=((f^{+}-f^{-})\otimes\nu_{f})\,(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\llcorner J_{f})
, where
\nu_{f}
is the direction of the jump),
and
D^{c}f
is the
Cantor part
.

The closed subspace
SBV(\Omega):=\{f\in BV(\Omega):D^{c}f=0\}
of
BV(\Omega)
is called the space of

special functions of bounded variation
.
We have continuous strict inclusions
W^{1,1}(\Omega)\subseteq SBV(\Omega)\subseteq BV(\Omega)
.

8.
Appendix B. The space of hyperbolic polynomials

The fact that the regularity of the roots of hyperbolic polynomials is essentially better than that of the roots of general polynomials
is related to the geometry of the space
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d

and the constraints a sufficiently differentiable curve of hyperbolic polynomials is subdued to at the boundary of
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.
In this section, we collect some interesting topological and geometric properties of
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.

The following results are due to Kostov
[
54
]
; see also Arnol’d
[
6
]
and Givental’
[
38
]
.
Let
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})
be a probability vector, i.e.,
p_{i}>0
and
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{d}=1
.
The
Vandermonde mapping

W=W(p):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

W_{j}(x):=p_{1}x_{1}^{j}+\cdots+p_{d}x_{d}^{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq d.

If
p_{i}=1/d
for
1\leq j\leq d
, then
W_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq d
, are the Newton polynomials (up to a constant factor) which
generate the algebra of symmetric polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
consequently the image
W(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
in this case.

The set
K:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq\cdots\leq x_{d}\}
is a fundamental domain for the
action of the symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates.
Every set of the form
\{x\in K:W_{j}(x)=c_{j},\,1\leq j\leq k\}
is called a

(d-k)
-dimensional Vandermonde manifold
.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
54
]
)
.

Every Vandermonde manifold is either contractible or empty.
The map
W:K\to W(K)
is a homeomorphism.
Every set of the form
\{y\in W(K):y_{j}=c_{j},\,1\leq j\leq k\}

is either contractible or empty.

As a consequence,
W^{k}:=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{k})
,
k\leq d
, is a homeomorphism of the closure of every
k
-dimensional stratum of the polyhedron
K

onto its image; indeed, the restriction of
W^{k}
to such a stratum is the
k
-dimensional Vandermonde mapping for some probability vector.

Let
K_{0}:=\{x\in K:W_{1}(x)=0,\,W_{2}(x)\leq 1\}
.

Theorem 8.2

(
[
54
]
)
.

The sets
W(K_{0})
and
W^{k}(K_{0})
,
1\leq k\leq d-1
, are quasiconvex,
i.e., there is a constant
C>0
such that any two points can be joint by a piecewise smooth curve in the set
of length not exceeding
C
times the Euclidean distance of the two points.

In particular, the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
in Tschirnhausen form with
|\tilde{a}_{2}|\leq 1

is quasiconvex. We refer to
[
54
]
for additional information on the stratification of
W(K)
.

The semialgebraic set
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be described by explicit inequalities.
Let
s_{j}=x_{1}^{j}+\cdots+x_{d}^{j}
,
j\geq 0
, be the Newton polynomials and consider the Bezoutiant matrix

B:=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}&s_{1}&\cdots&s_{d-1}\\
s_{1}&s_{2}&\cdots&s_{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{d-1}&s_{d}&\cdots&s_{2d-2}\end{pmatrix}.

We have
B=\tilde{B}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
for a unique matrix valued polynomial
\tilde{B}
,
where the
\sigma_{i}
are the elementary symmetric functions. The following result is Hermite’s
[
47
]
and Sylvester’s
[
107
]

version of Sturm’s theorem;
the indicated references give a modern account and a far reaching generalization.

Theorem 8.3

(
[
89
,
90
]
)
.

The space
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
can be identified with
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\tilde{B}(y)\geq 0\}
.
The rank of
\tilde{B}
equals the number of distinct roots and its signature equals the number of distinct real roots.

Since a real symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite if and only if the determinants of the principal minors are
nonnegative, this description yields explicit inequalities that determine
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
.

Recall that
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(d)=\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\cap\{y_{1}=0\}
is the space of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d
in Tschirnhausen form.
Then
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(1)
is the origin,
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(2)
is
(-\infty,0]
,
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(3)
is the closure of the inside of a cusp,
and
\operatorname{Hyp}_{T}(4)
is the closure of the inside of the swallowtail.

In
[
75
]
Meguerditchian
studies the “escape” from the set
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
: roughly speaking, for a polynomial
P
in
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
an integer
s_{P}
, which depends only on the
multiplicity vector of
P
, is introduced
such that one can decide whether
P+Q
belongs to
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
or not
in terms of how the degree of
Q
relates to
s_{P}
.

By Nuij
[
79
]
, for
P\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
also
P+sP^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
A generalization of this result is due to Kurdyka and Păunescu
[
65
]
.
