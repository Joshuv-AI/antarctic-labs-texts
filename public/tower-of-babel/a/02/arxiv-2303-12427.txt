A Representation Transformation of Parametric Feynman Integrals
Wen Chen

Abstract
A transformation on homogeneous polynomials is proposed, which is further applied to parametric Feynman integrals. The two representations related through this transformation are dual to each other. It naturally leads to dualities of Landau equations and linear integral relations between the two representations. For integrals with momentum-space correspondences, the dual representation is equivalent to the Baikov representation.

II
Polynomial transformation

We consider the following transformation on a homogeneous polynomial
\mathcal{F}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n+1})
.

\displaystyle u_{i}\equiv\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial x_{i}},

(II.1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(u)\equiv\left.\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}}\right|_{x=x(u)}.

(II.1b)

We assume that the degree of
\mathcal{F}
is
L+1
. Then by virtue of the homogeneity of
\mathcal{F}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}u_{i}x_{i}=L+1.

(II.2)

Taking the derivatives of both sides with respect to
u_{i}
, we get

x_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}u_{j}\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial u_{i}}=0.

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial u_{i}}=\mathcal{F}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}}\right)=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{F}}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial u_{i}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}u_{j}\frac{\partial x_{j}}{\partial u_{i}}.\end{split}

A combination of the above two equations leads to

x_{i}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial u_{i}}.

(II.3)

Thus we see that the transformation defined by
eq.

II.1
is reciprocal.

Generally speaking, the transformation defined by
eq.

II.1a
may not be reversible. Even if it is, the obtained function
\mathcal{G}(u)
may not be a rational function. However, for the
\mathcal{F}
polynomial of a loop integral,
\mathcal{G}
is indeed a rational function, as will be clear in the subsequent sections.

III
Representation transformation

As was shown in refs.
Chen 2020a
;
Chen 2021
;
Chen 2020b
, a Feynman integral can be parametrized by scalar integrals of the following form.

\begin{split}&I_{x}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\frac{\Gamma(-\lambda_{0})}{\prod_{i=m+1}^{n+1}\Gamma(\lambda_{i}+1)}\\
&\times\int\mathrm{d}\Pi_{x}^{(n+1)}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}-1}\prod_{i=m+1}^{n+1}x_{i}^{\lambda_{i}}~.\end{split}

(III.1)

Here, the integration measure
\mathrm{d}\Pi_{x}^{(n+1)}
is defined by
\mathrm{d}\Pi_{x}^{(n+1)}=\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}~\delta(1-\mathcal{E}^{(1)}(x))
, with
\mathcal{E}^{(i)}(x)
a positive definite homogeneous function of
x
of degree
i
. The region of integration for
x_{i}
is
[0,~\infty)
if
i>m
and
(-\infty,~\infty)
if
i\leqslant m
.
\mathcal{F}
is a homogeneous polynomial of the Feynman parameters
x_{i}
. For a parametric integral with a momentum space correspondence, the
\mathcal{F}
polynomial is related to the well-known Symanzik polynomials
U
and
F
through
\mathcal{F}=F+Ux_{n+1}
, and
x_{i}
with
i\leq m
correspond to cut propagators
Chen 2020b
. For normal loop integrals,
\lambda_{0}
is related to the space time dimension
d
through
\lambda_{0}=-\frac{d}{2}
, but here it is just a parameter.

We introduce the function
Chen 2020b

w_{\lambda}(x)\equiv e^{\frac{\lambda+1}{2}i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v~\frac{1}{(v+i0^{+})^{\lambda+1}}e^{-ivx}~.

(III.2)

We have

\displaystyle w_{-1}(x)=

\displaystyle~2\pi\delta(x)~,

\displaystyle w_{\lambda}(x)=

\displaystyle~2\pi\theta(x)\frac{x^{\lambda}}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)},~\lambda\geq 0~.

The Fourier transform of
w_{\lambda}(x)
gives

\frac{1}{x^{\lambda+1}}=e^{\frac{\lambda+1}{2}i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{2\pi}~w_{\lambda}(v)e^{-ivx},~\mathrm{Im}\{x\}<0~.

(III.3)

Obviously we can write
\frac{1}{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}xx^{\lambda}
as
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}xw_{\lambda}(x)
. By expressing
w_{\lambda}(x)
(or
x^{-\lambda-1}
) in terms of
v^{-\lambda-1}
[or
w_{\lambda}(v)
] by using eqs. (
III.2
and
III.3
), the integral
I_{x}
in eq. (
III.1
) becomes

\begin{split}&I_{x}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\\
=&(-2i\pi)^{-n-1}\Gamma(-\lambda_{0})\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\right)\\
&\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}v_{i}\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{\lambda_{i}}(v_{i})\prod_{i=m+1}^{n+1}v_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}-1}\\
&\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{x}^{(n+1)}\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}\exp\left(-i\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}x_{i}\right)\\
=&(-2i\pi)^{-n-1}\Gamma(-\lambda_{0})\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\right)\\
&\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{v}^{(n+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{\lambda_{i}}(v_{i})\prod_{i=m+1}^{n+1}v_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}-1}\\
&\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}~\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}\exp\left(-i\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}x_{i}\right)~,\end{split}

(III.4)

where
\mathrm{d}\Pi_{v}^{(n+1)}\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}v_{i}~\delta(1-\mathcal{E}^{(1)}(v))
. The last step of this equation can be proven by inserting an integral
\int\mathrm{d}s\delta(s-\mathcal{E}^{(1)}(v))
, rescaling
x
and
v
by
x_{i}\to\frac{x_{i}}{s}
and
v_{i}\to sv_{i}
, and finally integrating out
s
.

We consider a special class of integrals of which the
\mathcal{F}
polynomials are complete. Here, by complete, we mean that there are
n
linearly independent matrices
\Lambda_{i,jk}
such that

\sum_{j,k=1}^{n+1}\Lambda_{i,jk}u_{j}x_{k}=0,~i=1,2,\dots,n~.

(III.5)

We further assume that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{i,j(n+1)}=

\displaystyle~0,~j\neq n+1~,

(III.6a)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda_{i,jj}=

\displaystyle~n_{0}\Lambda_{i,(n+1)(n+1)}~,

(III.6b)

with

a constant. As can be seen in

B
, a family of loop integrals with a complete set of propagators do satisfy these conditions. That is, the completeness of propagators implies the completeness of the corresponding
\mathcal{F}
polynomial. The first condition of eqs. (
III.6
) is a consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{F}
is linear in
x_{n+1}
. For a complete homogeneous
\mathcal{F}
polynomial satisfying the conditions in eqs. (
III.6
), it can be proven that (see
appendix

A
)

\begin{split}&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}~\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}\exp\left(i\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}x_{i}\right)\\
=&~(-2i\pi)^{n+1}e^{\frac{i}{2}\pi(L+1)\lambda_{0}}C_{\mathcal{F}}v_{n+1}^{-n_{0}-1}\mathcal{G}(v)^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}~,\end{split}

(III.7)

where
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}\equiv-\lambda_{0}-\frac{n-n_{0}}{L+1}
and
C_{\mathcal{F}}
is a (
v

III.7
) into eq. (
III.4
) gives

\begin{split}&I_{x}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\\
=&\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}\left[(L+1)\lambda_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\lambda_{i}\right]\right)\\
&\times C_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\Gamma(-\lambda_{0})}{\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})}~I_{u}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})~,\end{split}

(III.8)

where
\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}=\lambda_{n+1}+n_{0}+1
, and
\lambda_{i}^{\prime}=\lambda_{i},~i\neq 0,n+1
. The integral
I_{u}
is defined by

\displaystyle I_{u}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})

(III.9)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle~\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\Pi_{u}^{(n+1)}\mathcal{G}(u)^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}w_{\lambda_{i}^{\prime}}(u_{i})\prod_{i=m+1}^{n+1}u_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1}~.

Thus, we have proven that
I_{x}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
equals
I_{u}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})
up to some constants, and there is an elegant duality between these two representations, which is summarized in

\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}}

x=\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}}{\partial u}

\frac{1}{u^{\lambda+1}}=e^{-\frac{\lambda+1}{2}i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{2\pi}~w_{\lambda}(x)e^{iux}

~w_{\lambda}(x)=e^{\frac{\lambda+1}{2}i\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u~\frac{1}{u^{\lambda+1}}e^{-iux}

IV
The Baikov representation

We consider a loop integral with propagators

v_{i}=\sum_{j,k}A_{i,jk}l_{j}\cdot l_{k}+2\sum_{j,k}B_{i,jk}l_{j}\cdot p_{k}+C_{i},\quad i=1,~2,\dots,n~,

(IV.1)

where
l
are the loop momenta and
p
are the external momenta. For simplicity, we assume that the Gram determinant of the external momenta
p
does not vanish, and all the propagators are complete and linearly independent. Here, by complete, we mean that
v_{i}
form a complete basis such that all the scalar products
l_{i}\cdot l_{j}
and
l_{i}\cdot p_{j}
can be expressed in terms of
v_{i}
. For future convenience, we collectively denote
l
and
p
by
q
. Scalar integrals can be parametrized by integrals of the form
Baikov 1996
;
Baikov 1997

\begin{split}&J(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=\\
&\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}v_{n}~P^{-\lambda_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(L+E+1)}\prod_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}-1}~,\end{split}

(IV.2)

where
E
is the number of the external momenta
p
. Here we have omitted some irrelevant prefactors for brevity. Notice that we use such a convention that the indices of the propagators are
-\lambda_{i}-1
rather than the more conventional
-\lambda_{i}
. The polynomial
P(v)
is proportional to the Gram determinant of the momenta
q
. That is,

P(v)\equiv\frac{\det(q_{i}\cdot q_{j})}{\det(p_{i}\cdot p_{j})}~.

(IV.3)

In this section, we will prove that
J(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
is equivalent to the integral
I_{u}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})
in
eq.

III.9
up to some constants.

Taking
\mathcal{E}^{(1)}(u)=|u_{n+1}|
and
m=0
in the integral
I_{u}
, we get

\displaystyle I_{u}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}u_{i}~\left[\mathcal{G}_{+}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}+\mathcal{G}_{-}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}e^{-i\pi(\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}+1)}\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}u_{i}~\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle\times\left[\mathcal{G}_{-}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}e^{i\pi(\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}+1)}+\mathcal{G}_{-}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}e^{-i\pi(\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}+1)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\cos\left(\pi(\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}+1)\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}u_{i}~\mathcal{G}_{-}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-4i\pi}{\Gamma(1+\lambda_{0}^{\prime})}\cos\left(\pi\lambda_{n+1}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}u_{i}~\theta\left(-\mathcal{G}_{-}\right)\left(-\mathcal{G}_{-}\right)^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{-\lambda_{i}^{\prime}-1}~,

where
\mathcal{G}_{+}\equiv\left.\mathcal{G}\right|_{u_{n+1}=1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{-}\equiv\left.\mathcal{G}\right|_{u_{n+1}=-1}
. Due to the positivity of
|u_{n+1}|
, the integral receives contributions from both
u_{n+1}=1
and
u_{n+1}=-1
. The second step of this equation is carried out by replacing the integration variables with
x_{i}\to-x_{i}
for the first term in the square bracket. In the last step, we have closed the contour around the branch cut. As a result, only the discontinuity of
\mathcal{G}_{-}^{\lambda_{0}^{\prime}}
contributes to the integral. The discontinuity arises from the region where
\mathcal{G}_{-}<0
, which gives rise to the theta function
\theta\left(-\mathcal{G}_{-}\right)
. Notice that we use such a convention that
\mathrm{Im}\{\mathcal{F}\}<0
. Thus
\mathrm{Im}\{\mathcal{G}\}>0
. In the last step we have expressed
\sin(\pi\lambda_{0}^{\prime})
in terms of
\frac{-\pi}{\Gamma(-\lambda_{0}^{\prime})\Gamma(1+\lambda_{0}^{\prime})}
. Since
n_{0}=n-\frac{1}{2}(L+1)(L+E+1)
(see
appendix

B
), we have
\lambda_{0}^{\prime}=-\lambda_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(L+E+1)
. Comparing the above integral with the Baikov integral in
eq.

IV.2
, we see that these two integrals are equivalent if

\begin{split}P(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})=&-\mathcal{G}_{-}\\
=&-\mathcal{G}(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n},-1)~.\end{split}

(IV.4)

Immediately, we will see that this is indeed true.

We define

\displaystyle A_{ij}\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k,ij}x_{k}~,

\displaystyle B_{ij}\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k,ij}x_{k}~,

\displaystyle C\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}x_{i}~.

Then we have

\mathcal{F}=\sum_{i,j,k,l}p_{k}\cdot p_{l}\tilde{A}_{ij}B_{ik}B_{jl}+(x_{n+1}-C)U~,

where

\displaystyle U\equiv

\displaystyle\det(A_{ij})~,

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{ij}\equiv

\displaystyle\left(A^{-1}\right)_{ij}U~.

Let
a_{ij}
,
b_{ij}
,
\alpha_{ij,k}
, and

be those defined in ref.

\displaystyle\sum_{l}a_{il}B_{l,jk}=0,

(IV.6a)

\displaystyle\sum_{l}b_{il}A_{l,jk}=0,

(IV.6b)

\displaystyle\sum_{kl}\alpha_{ij,k}a_{kl}A_{l,mn}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta^{im}\delta^{jn}+\delta^{in}\delta^{jm}\right),

(IV.7)

where

is the inverse of the Gram matrix

, and

On the other hand,

can be understood as a block matrix. Then we have

\displaystyle P=~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det(p_{i}\cdot p_{j})}\det\begin{pmatrix}l_{i}\cdot l_{j}&l_{i}\cdot p_{j}\\

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l}\beta_{ij,k}b_{kl}(v_{l}-C_{l})\equiv-B^{\prime\prime}_{ij}=-\left.B^{\prime}_{ij}(v)\right|_{v_{n+1}=-1}~,

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l}\alpha_{ij,k}a_{kl}(v_{l}-C_{l})~.

Thus

\begin{split}P=&\det\left(\sum_{kl}\alpha_{ij,k}a_{kl}(v_{l}-C_{l})\right.\\
&\left.-\sum_{mn}g_{mn}B^{\prime\prime}_{im}B^{\prime\prime}_{jn}\right)~.\end{split}

(IV.8)

V
Discussion

A reversible transformation on homogeneous polynomials is proposed. The transformation is further applied to parametric Feynman integrals, since a parametric integral is characterized by a homogeneous polynomial. An exact duality between the two representations related through this transformation is proven, as summarized in
table

1
. Furthermore, it is proven that the dual representation is equivalent to the Baikov representation.

The transformation and the corresponding duality can be widely applied to the studies of Feynman integrals, including integral reduction and Laundau analysis. Specifically,
eq.

III.5
establishes a correspondence between a syzygy equation in the Baikov representation
Larsen and Zhang 2016
;
Böhm et al. 2018
and a parametric annihilator in the parametric representation
Lee 2014
;
Bitoun et al. 2019
. Additionally, the transformation in
II.1
establishes a duality between the first (second) Landau equation in the parametric representation and the second (first) Landau equation in the Baikov representation. It is known that the infrared singularities of Feynman integrals can be identified with solutions of Landau equations. The method of regions
Beneke and Smirnov 1998
;
Smirnov 1999
;
Smirnov 2002
;
Pak and Smirnov 2011
;
Jantzen 2011
provides a systematic method to isolate the infrared structures of Feynman integrals. While endpoint singularities (in the parametric space) can be identified using a geometric method
Pak and Smirnov 2011
;
Gardi et al. 2023
;
Ma 2024
, this method can not directly be applied to more general regions, including threshold regions
Beneke and Smirnov 1998
;
Jantzen et al. 2012
;
Ananthanarayan et al. 2019
;
Gardi et al. 2024
. The latter are related to solutions of the second Landau equations which are away from the origin. By virtue of the duality found in this letter, it is expected that this kind of regions can be identified by applying the geometric method to the dual representation. This is confirmed through some one-loop integrals, including those considered in refs.
Beneke and Smirnov 1998
;
Jantzen et al. 2012
. The application of this method to multiloop integrals is complicated by the presence of irreducible scalar products, which deserves further investigation.

I_{0}(f)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}~\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}f(x)\exp\left(-i\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}x_{i}\right)~,

with

a polynomial in

. Then, by virtue of eq. (
A.1
), we have

\displaystyle-(L+1)\lambda_{0}\Lambda_{i,(n+1)(n+1)}I_{0}(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}I_{0}(u_{j}x_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{d}x_{i}~\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}^{\lambda_{0}}}{\partial x_{j}}x_{k}\exp\left(-i\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}v_{i}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jj}I_{0}(1)+i\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}v_{j}I_{0}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-n_{0})\Lambda_{i,(n+1)(n+1)}I_{0}(1)-\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}v_{j}\frac{\partial I_{0}(1)}{\partial v_{k}}~.

That is

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}v_{j}\frac{\partial I_{0}(1)}{\partial v_{k}}=

\displaystyle\left[n-n_{0}+(L+1)\lambda_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\times\Lambda_{i,(n+1)(n+1)}I_{0}(1)~.

On the other hand, eq. (
A.1
) is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}v_{j}\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}(v)}{\partial v_{k}}=-(L+1)\Lambda_{i,(n+1)(n+1)}\mathcal{G}~.

Thus we get

\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,jk}v_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial v_{k}}\left[\mathcal{G}(v)^{\lambda_{0}+\frac{n-n_{0}}{L+1}}I_{0}(1)\right]=0~.

By assumption, the matrix
\Lambda^{\prime}_{ij}(v)\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\Lambda^{\prime}_{i,kj}v_{k}

\begin{split}I_{0}(1)=&(-2i\pi)^{n+1}e^{\frac{i}{2}\pi(L+1)\lambda_{0}}C_{\mathcal{F}}\\

(A.2)

where
