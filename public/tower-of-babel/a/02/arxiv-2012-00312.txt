Density operator approach to turbulent flows in plasma and atmospheric fluids Preprint: 17th Russian Gravitational Conference - International Conference on Gravitation, Cosmology and Astrophysics (RUSGRAV-17) Preprint: Universe 6 , 216 (2020) [ DOI: 10.3390/universe6110216 ]
Konstantin G. Zloshchastiev

Abstract
We formulate a statistical wave-mechanical approach to describe dissipation and instabilities in two-dimensional turbulent flows of magnetized plasmas and atmospheric fluids, such as drift and Rossby waves. This is made possible by the existence of Hilbert space, associated with the electric potential of plasma or stream function of atmospheric fluid. We therefore regard such turbulent flows as macroscopic wave-mechanical phenomena, driven by the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian operator we derive, whose anti-Hermitian component is attributed to an effect of the environment. Introducing a wave-mechanical density operator for the statistical ensembles of waves, we formulate master equations and define observables: such as the enstrophy and energy of both the waves and zonal flow as statistical averages. We establish that our open system can generally follow two types of time evolution, depending on whether the environment hinders or assists the system’s stability and integrity. We also consider a phase-space formulation of the theory, including the geometrical-optic limit and beyond, and study the conservation laws of physical observables. It is thus shown that the approach predicts various mechanisms of energy and enstrophy exchange between drift waves and zonal flow, which were hitherto overlooked in models based on wave kinetic equations.

II.1
Hasegawa-Mima equation for plasma

We assume a conventional geophysical

\mathbf{x}=(x,y)
are coordinates on a two-dimensional plane,

and the
y
-axis points in the
direction of the local gradient of the plasma density (in atmospheric fluids,

Let us derive a generalized Hasegawa-Mima equation
in the case of plasma being entrapped
in the magnetic field, which is directed along
z
-axis:

\mathbf{B}=B\,{\bf e}_{z},

(1)

where
B=B_{0}
is a constant magnitude
and

{\bf e}_{z}
is a unit vector normal to the plane.
Plasma is assumed to be confined to a slab, which is perpendicular to the magnetic field, so that perturbations of density and potential can propagate only in the
(x,y)
plane.
It is inhomogeneous, with density gradient pointing along
x
-axis.

From now on, we assume the cold ions approximation
T_{i}\ll T_{e}
.
Electron and ion components’ temperatures are assumed to be constant, therefore
the electron density is

n_{e}=n_{0}\exp{(e\tilde{\phi}/T_{e})}
,
where

\phi=\phi_{0}+\tilde{\phi}
being electric potential,

n_{0}
is unperturbed density and
T_{e}
is the electron temperature.
If
e\tilde{\phi}\ll T_{e}
,
then
n_{e}\approx n_{0}(1+e\tilde{\phi}/T_{e})
.
Keeping perturbations of electron density up to a first order only,
n_{e}=n_{0}+\tilde{n}_{e}
, we obtain

\frac{\tilde{n}_{e}}{n_{0}}=\frac{e\tilde{\phi}}{T_{e}},

(2)

therefore, in the formula
\phi=\phi_{0}+\tilde{\phi}
the first term vanishes.

In a slab plasma with
v_{z}\ll v_{\perp}
, with the electrostatic field being
\mathbf{E}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi
,
where
\boldsymbol{\nabla}=\mathbf{e}_{x}\partial_{x}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\partial_{y}
, the equation of motion for an ion is

m_{i}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}=q\left(-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi+\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\times\mathbf{B}\right),

(3)

where
m_{i}
and
q
are ion’s mass and charge, respectively.
Applying a perturbation theory,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v_{\perp}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(0)}+\lambda\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(2)}+...,

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(r)+\lambda\tilde{\phi}(r,t)+...,

we can write Eq. (
3
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(0)}+\lambda\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(2)}+...\right)=\frac{1}{B}\left[-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(\lambda\tilde{\phi}(r,t)+...\right)+\left(\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(0)}+\lambda\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(1)}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{v_{\perp}}^{(2)}+...\right)\times\mathbf{B}\right]\!,~~~

(4)

where
\omega_{ci}=qB/m_{i}
is the ion’s cyclotron frequency.
To a zeroth order of perturbation theory, we thus obtain the equation:

\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}}\frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(0)}}{dt}=\frac{1}{B}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(0)}\times\mathbf{B}\right),

(5)

whose solution describes rotary motion, so that we have

\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(0)}=0.

In the first order, one can write

\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}}\frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(1)}}{dt}=-\frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}-\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(1)}\times\mathbf{B}\right),

(6)

whose solution is given by

\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(1)}=-\frac{1}{B}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z}-\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B}\frac{D}{Dt}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi},

(7)

where
we
denoted the convective derivative as

\frac{D}{Dt}\equiv\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{B}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla},

where by
\partial_{t}
we denote a partial time derivative.
Ignoring higher-order corrections,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{\perp}=\mathbf{v}_{\perp}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{B}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z}

(8)

\displaystyle-\left[\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right]\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}.~~

By substituting
n_{i}=n_{0}+\tilde{n}_{i}
into the continuity equation for the ion component’s fluid,

\frac{\partial n_{i}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(n_{i}\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\right)=0,

(9)

and using the quasi-neutrality condition
\tilde{n}_{i}=\tilde{n}_{e}
,
we obtain the following equation

\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\tilde{n}_{e}}{n_{0}}\right)+\mathbf{v}_{\perp}\cdot\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}n_{0}}{n_{0}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{n}_{e}}{n_{0}}\right)+\left(1+\frac{\tilde{n}_{e}}{n_{0}}\right)\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{\perp}=0,

(10)

which transforms, using Eqs. (
2
) and (
8
),
and neglecting terms which are higher than the first order,
into the equation

\partial_{t}\frac{e\tilde{\phi}}{T_{e}}-\frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}n_{0}}{n_{0}}+\frac{e\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}}{T_{e}}\right)-\left(1+\frac{e\tilde{\phi}}{T_{e}}\right)\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left\{\frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z}\right)+\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B}\left[\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{B}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right]\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\right\}=0.

(11)

In this equation,
the term
(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}\times{\bf e}_{z})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}

vanishes due to orthogonality,
while the terms with

(e\tilde{\phi}/T_{e})\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot
can be neglected as being higher-order small.
Multiplying Eq. (
9

-T_{e}/e
and recalling an expression for the ion’s cyclotron (gyro)radius,

R_{s}=\frac{c_{s}}{\omega_{ci}}=\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}}\sqrt{\frac{T_{e}}{m_{i}}},

where

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left[\left(\rho^{2}_{s}\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}-1\right)\tilde{\phi}\right]+\left({\bf e}_{z}\times\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\tilde{\phi}}{B}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\Big[\big(\rho^{2}_{s}\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}-1\big)\tilde{\phi}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{T_{e}}{|e|B}\frac{\partial\ln n_{0}}{\partial y}\frac{\partial\tilde{\phi}}{\partial x}=0.~~~~

(13)

In terms of dimensionless values

\acute{t}=\omega_{ci}t

(\acute{x},\acute{y})=(x/R_{s},y/R_{s})
and
\acute{\phi}=e\tilde{\phi}/T_{e}

\frac{\partial}{\partial\acute{t}}\Big[(\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}-1)\acute{\phi}\Big]+({\bf e}_{z}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\acute{\phi})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\big[(\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}-1)]\acute{\phi}\big]-\frac{\partial\ln n_{0}}{\partial\acute{x}}\frac{\partial\acute{\phi}}{\partial\acute{x}}=0,

where

Q(\mathbf{x},t)
is an added external forces’ and dissipation term,

Placing both plasma and atmospheric flow cases on the same footing,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int dy\,\overline{w}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int dy\,U_{1}^{2},

III
Wave-mechanical analogy in zonal flows

Building on the ideas presented in works

;
p16
;

,
let us formulate a formal mapping of Eq. (
30
) to
an effective Schrödinger-like equation which is somewhat
analogous to a quantum-mechanical description.
For reasons, which will be later specified, around Eq. (
58
),
we shall refer to this analogy as
wave-mechanical
.

Judging by the form of Eq. (
30
), one can expect that its solutions
form the Hilbert space of normalizable smooth functions, whose inner product is defined as
an integral over the whole
(x,y)
plane,
\int d^{2}x
.
Postponing discussion of the physical implications of this until the end
of this section,
we introduce, in Dirac’s bra-ket notations,
a state vector
|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle
,
such that
in the coordinate representation one obtains

\tilde{{\phi}}(\mathbf{x},t)=\langle\mathbf{x}|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle
, similarly for
\tilde{\xi}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

Using the resolution of identity,

\int d^{2}x|\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{x}|=\hat{I}
,
we obtain

\langle\tilde{{\phi}}|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle\equiv\aleph=\int\langle\tilde{{\phi}}|\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{x}|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle d^{2}x=\int\tilde{{\phi}}^{2}d^{2}x,

(37)

where
\aleph(t)
is a norm of a state vector
|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle
. This state vector is almost what we are looking for, but the proper Schrödinger analogy requires
a
normalized
state vector (ray), which spans a projective Hilbert space.
We thus introduce

|\Psi\rangle\equiv\aleph^{-1/2}|\tilde{{\phi}}\rangle,

(38)

so that

\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=1
.

Furthermore,
using an operator of spatial translations in the
(x,y)
plane,

\hat{\mathbf{p}}=-i\boldsymbol{\nabla}=-i(\mathbf{e}_{x}\hat{p}_{x}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\hat{p}_{y})

\hat{p}^{2}=\hat{\textbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\textbf{p}}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}
,

\hat{p}^{2}_{D}=\hat{p}^{2}+L_{D}^{-2}\Leftrightarrow 1-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}

and
\hat{U}\equiv U(\hat{y},t)
.
In terms of these operators, Eq. (
30
) can be rewritten in the Schrödinger-like form:

i\partial_{t}\langle\textbf{x}|\Psi\rangle=\hat{H}\langle\textbf{x}|\Psi\rangle+i\aleph^{-1/2}\langle\textbf{x}|\tilde{\xi}\rangle,

(39)

or, in a general basis

i\partial_{t}|\Psi\rangle=\hat{H}|\Psi\rangle+i\aleph^{-1/2}|\tilde{\xi}\rangle,

(40)

where the Hamiltonian operator is given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mho}+\hat{p}_{D}^{-2}\hat{U}_{2}-\beta\hat{p}_{D}^{-2}\right)\hat{p}_{x}-i\mu_{\text{dw}}\hat{I},

(41)

where
we
introduced a similarity transform of

\displaystyle\hat{\mho}=\hat{p}_{D}^{-2}\hat{U}\hat{p}_{D}^{2},

IV
Density operator formalism

In this section, we describe statistical approach based on the density operator, which is analogous to the von Neumann approach in quantum mechanics of mixed states, i.e., probabilistic mixtures of pure states
sz13
;
sz14
;
sz14cor
;
ser15w
;
z15
;
sz15
.
In the matrix representation of a density operator, pure states are described by diagonal elements of a density matrix, whereas mixed states - by off-diagonal components thereof.
It is the latter which are regarded in quantum theory as an essentially quantum-mechanical effect, which does not usually have a classical counterpart.

When it comes to
NH systems,
the density operator formalism is more general than the state-vector one, because
it allows the description of not only pure states, or their superpositions, but also mixed states.
This generalization is especially important, because dissipative effects are known to evolve some pure states
into mixed ones
z15
.
In other words, restricting ourselves to state vectors
ab initio
would pose an implicit assumption of forbidding the transition
from pure to mixed states.
This assumption does not seem to be realistic when it comes to open systems.

Besides,
if one were dealing with an equation for state vectors driven by a non-Hermitian Hamilton operator, such as Eq. (
40
),
then one would arrive at complex eigenvalues of energy, which seem somewhat contradictory to the notion of energy
per se
.
On the contrary, in a density operator approach, no complex-valued energies occur:
the role of anti-Hermitian part of the Hamiltonian is to describe the time evolution of energy eigenvalues of its Hermitian part.

IV.1
Master equation: Non-sustainable evolution

Let us regard the operator
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
as a special case of the
reduced

\hat{W}

From Eq. (

IV.2
Master equation: Sustainable evolution

Dynamical systems, which can be described by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, can follow two types
of evolution;
which can be referred as non-sustainable and sustainable, by analogy with some photobiological systems,
where this difference becomes striking

The former type is the one
described by the density
\hat{W}
obeying the master equation (

The sustainable type is the one described by the normalized density operator

\hat{\rho}=\hat{W}/\text{Tr}(\hat{W}),\quad

(72)

so that
\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho})=1

This density would be a solution of the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i[\hat{H}_{+},\hat{\rho}]-\left\{\hat{\Gamma}_{0},\hat{\rho}\right\}+2\,\text{Tr}(\hat{\Gamma}_{0}\hat{\rho})\,\hat{\rho},

(73)

Fortunately, for practical computations, this equation can be transformed into a linear equation of
type (
65

using the ansatz (
72

For the sustainable type of evolution,
physical observables are defined as

\displaystyle\langle\hat{A}\rangle_{\rho}\equiv\text{Tr}(\hat{A}\hat{\rho}),

(74)

for a given observable’s operator
\hat{A}
,
and similarly for correlation functions
sz14cor

Using the ansatz (

which is often convenient for optimizing computations.

Furthermore,
recalling the mean-value origin of the last term
in Eq. (

\hat{W}
must be replaced with
\hat{\rho}
in the equation for the function

according to remarks preceding Eq. (

Similarly to Eqs. (

)-(
70

one can define “normalized” enstrophy and entropy as

\displaystyle\grave{{\cal Z}}_{\text{dw}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{p}^{4}_{D}\rangle_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\hat{p}^{4}_{D}\hat{\rho}),

(77)

\displaystyle\grave{{\cal Z}}_{\text{zf}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int dy\,U_{1}^{2},

(78)

\displaystyle\grave{{\cal E}}_{\text{dw}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\hat{p}^{2}_{D}\rangle_{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}(\hat{p}^{2}_{D}\,\hat{\rho}),

(79)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int dy\,U^{2},

(80)

While these definitions look very similar to their non-sustainable evolution

To summarize Sections

apparatus itself, as well as any pre- and post-selection measurement protocols.
For example, in quantum optics, the calibrating and resetting of

IV.3
Time evolution of averages

Master equations are differential equations for operators, which makes solving them a technically challenging task.
However, some physical information can be extracted without actually finding a density operator explicitly.
One of methods of such an extraction are equations for averages, which can be derived from a master equation.

Let us consider the non-sustainable type of NH evolution,
described by Eqs. (

Multiplying Eq. (

For example:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\hat{I}\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle-2\langle\hat{\Gamma}\rangle_{W},

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\hat{p}^{2}_{D}\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle-i\langle[\hat{p}^{2}_{D},\hat{H}_{+}]\rangle_{W}-\langle\{\hat{p}^{2}_{D},\hat{\Gamma}\}\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle-i\langle[\hat{p}^{2}_{D},\hat{H}]\rangle_{W}-2\langle\hat{\Gamma}\hat{p}^{2}_{D}\rangle_{W},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle[\hat{p}^{4}_{D},\hat{H}_{+}]\rangle_{W}-\langle\{\hat{p}^{4}_{D},\hat{\Gamma}\}\rangle_{W}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle[\hat{p}^{4}_{D},\hat{H}_{+}]\rangle_{W}-\langle\hat{p}^{2}_{D}[\hat{p}^{2}_{D},\hat{\Gamma}]\rangle_{W}

Otherwise, one has to apply approximations or iteration methods to the full set.

V
Phase-space formulation: Non-sustainable evolution

Master equations of the types described in the previous section are differential equations for operators.
This creates a formidable technical problem when it comes to solving them.
As a result, there are a number of methods which allow us to solve them,
each with a different degree of accuracy or completeness.
Solving the equations for averages is one of the approaches - it allows us
to compute averages
as solutions of a set of differential equations for functions, instead of
dealing with operators.
However, in the case of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, the number of such equations is not
necessarily finite or reducible to something simple,
but dependent on other symmetries of the problem.
The second method, which is to be discussed in this section,
is based on mapping between
functions in the quantum phase space formulation and Hilbert space operators in the Schrödinger representation

wbook
;
w32
;
gr46
;
hos84
, which was adapted for density operators for various NH systems
cv07
;
gs11
;
bc13
;
bcl15
,
including zonal-flow models specifically
wo09
;
p16
;
rd16
.

There also exists a subtle point when it comes to the completeness of the phase space description of density operators for NH systems including zonal-flow ones.
As mentioned at the beginning of Section
IV
,
it is important to preserve and extract, as fully as possible,
an essentially non-classical piece of information
about time evolution of a probabilistic mixture of states of NH-driven systems
z15
.
This is straightforward within the framework of the Hilbert space matrix formulation of a density operator
from Section
IV
, where the structure is clear: this information is encoded mostly in the off-diagonal elements of a density matrix, as we know it from quantum mechanics of mixed states.
It is far less obvious how one can separate “off-diagonal” effects from “diagonal” ones in the phase space formulation of a density operator, in presence of additional
approximations which are technically inevitable to obtain definite results.

V.1

VII
Conclusion

In this paper,
we have presented a statistical mechanical approach to describing dissipative phenomena in zonal flows
of plasmas and atmospheric fluids, such as drift waves and Rossby waves,
which is based on Landau-von Neumann’s density operator in a theory of open quantum systems.
This became possible due to an occurrence of Hilbert space associated with an
electric potential
or stream function, which is regarded as the fundamental Hilbert space of the theory.
This results in a formal mapping between flow and wave equations,
which allows us to describe zonal flows and associated phenomena as macroscopic wave-mechanical effects.
As a consequence of this mapping,
flow equations can be rewritten as Schrödinger-like equations, with two important differences:
for dimensionality purposes,
one uses an effective Planck constant whose value is not necessarily equal to the quantum-mechanical Planck constant,
and the resulting Hamiltonian operator is not necessarily Hermitian.

The second feature requires us to treat the entire theory
from within the framework of the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian approach,
where anti-Hermitian parts of Hamiltonian operators usually
describe an effect of the environment.
Fortunately, such an approach has been already developed and applied to various open systems.
According to this formalism, one has to generalize from state vectors to density operators,
because pure states do not necessarily stay pure during the time evolution in the presence of dissipation
and noise.
Moreover,
the
density operator approach prevents the occurrence of complex-valued energies.
In this approach, energies are always real-valued, and refer to the subsystem itself,
whereas any anti-Hermitian components describe the effects of
the environment upon this subsystem (decay rates,
et cetera
).

Thus, after deriving a Hamiltonian operator and evolution equations for state vectors
in our DW/RW models,
we made a transition from state vectors to density operators,
introduced NH master equations and defined observables;
including the enstrophy and energy of both the waves and zonal flow.
The upshot is that
two types
of density operator’s evolution exist,
these can be referred to as non-sustainable and sustainable, by analogy with some photobiological systems
where they can be visualized.

The non-sustainable type is the one described by the non-normalized density operator.
During such evolution, an open (sub)system experiences drain or loss of its degrees of freedom,
which can result in its total decay or critical instability.
While this can indeed be the case in some systems,
it is not a compulsory feature of all open systems.

The sustainable type is the one described by the density operator
which is normalized at all-times.
This automatically removes the problem of probability’s gain or loss in NH systems,
thus enhancing their stability.
An example of such stability would be an environment-assisted stability in photobiological systems.
Consequently, various observables, including those related to enstrophy and energy,
behave in a way different from the non-sustainable case.

Because we are dealing with reduced density operators;
a selection of one or another type of NH evolution, or even the switch between them at some point in time, is a
process external
to the subsystem itself.
In fact, this process is neither unitary nor continuous.
It can be considered a special case of the effect induced by the environment,
which can also include the measuring
apparatus itself.

Furthermore, to establish a method for solving master equations for a given DW/RW model,
we considered a phase-space formulation of the theory.
We introduced relevant Weyl-Wigner transforms
and rewrote evolution equations and observables in the Moyal form.
We also studied a leading-order approximation of the Wigner approach,
for both types of evolution,
which is analogous to the eikonal or geometrical approximation in optics or WKB approximation in quantum mechanics.

As it turns out,
the statistical-mechanical density operator formalism
shows itself
to be an approach to dissipative phenomena related to zonal flows,
which has clear foundations and notions emanating from quantum mechanics.
It also allows us
to take into account the wave-mechanical effects in the above-mentioned systems,
thus producing more realistic descriptions thereof.
As such, the formalism can also be extended
to other dissipative systems in plasma and atmospheric physics,
which allow the wave-mechanical analogy and non-Hermitian Hamiltonian operator description.

Appendix A
DERIVATION OF VORTICITY

In the case of effectively two-dimensional systems,
one can define vorticity as a projection of
the vorticity pseudovector on the third axis.
We therefore assume

w\equiv\mathbf{w}\cdot{\bf e}_{z}=(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{v_{\perp}})\cdot{\bf e}_{z},

(160)

where

\mathbf{w}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{v_{\perp}}

is the vorticity pseudovector,
and

\mathbf{v_{\perp}}

is the
ion’s velocity in the
(x,y)
plane.
Using Eq. (
8
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{(1)}+\mathbf{w}^{(2)}+\mathbf{w}^{(3)},

(161)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{B}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi\times{\bf e}_{z}),

(162)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi=0,

(163)

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B^{2}}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\left\{[(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi\times{\bf e}_{z})\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}]\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi\right\}.

(164)

In the components’ notations,
we can write these expressions as

\displaystyle(\mathbf{w}^{(1)})_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{B}\epsilon_{mkl}\epsilon_{mj3}\partial_{l}\partial_{j}\phi=\frac{1}{B}\delta_{k3}\partial_{m}\partial_{m}\phi,

(165)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{w}^{(3)})_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B^{2}}\epsilon_{kmn}\epsilon_{ij3}\partial_{i}\partial_{n}\phi\;\partial_{m}\partial_{j}\phi

(166)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{ci}B^{2}}\delta_{k3}(\partial_{m}\partial_{n}\phi\;\partial_{m}\partial_{n}\phi-\partial_{n}\partial_{n}\phi\;\partial_{m}\partial_{m}\phi),

where
we used the following properties of the Levi-Civita symbol in three dimensions:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{imn}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{jm}\delta_{kn}-\delta_{jn}\delta_{km},

(167)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{lmn}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{il}(\delta_{jm}\delta_{kn}-\delta_{jn}\delta_{km})

(168)

\displaystyle-\delta_{im}(\delta_{jl}\delta_{kn}-\delta_{jn}\delta_{kl})

\displaystyle+\delta_{in}(\delta_{jl}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}).

Appendix B: WIGNER-WEYL FORMALISM

The Weyl symbol
A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})
for any given operator
\hat{A}
is defined as

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{n}s\exp{\!(-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{s})}\,\langle\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{s/2}|\hat{A}|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{s/2}\rangle.

(169)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\textbf{x},\textbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int d^{n}p\exp{[-i\textbf{p}\cdot(\textbf{x}^{\prime}-\textbf{x})]}\,A\left(\frac{\textbf{x}^{\prime}+\textbf{x}}{2},\textbf{p}\right),

(170)

in particular

\mathcal{A}(\textbf{x},\textbf{x})=\int\frac{d^{n}p}{(2\pi)^{n}}A(\textbf{x},\textbf{p}).

(171)

Completeness condition

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d^{n}p|\mathbf{p}\rangle\langle\mathbf{p}|=1.

(172)

Delta function

\delta(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d^{n}p\exp{\!(i\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x})}\,.

(173)

Inner product of two different variables

\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{p}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\!(i\textbf{p}\cdot\textbf{x})}\,.

(174)

Moyal product rule:
for any
\hat{C}=\hat{A}\hat{B}
, the corresponding Weyl symbols satisfy

C(\textbf{x},\textbf{p})=A(\textbf{x},\textbf{p})\star B(\textbf{x},\textbf{p}),

(175)

where
\star
is the Moyal product
defined as

A(\textbf{x},\textbf{p})\star B(\textbf{x},\textbf{p})\equiv A(\textbf{x},\textbf{p})\exp{\!(i\hat{\mathfrak{L}}/2)}\,B(\textbf{x},\textbf{p}),

(176)

where

\hat{\mathfrak{L}}\equiv\overleftarrow{\partial_{\textbf{x}}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\partial_{\textbf{p}}}-\overleftarrow{\partial_{\textbf{p}}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\partial_{\textbf{x}}}
is the Janus operator,
and

A\hat{\mathfrak{L}}B\equiv\left\{A,B\right\}_{\text{c}}=\partial_{\textbf{x}}A\,\partial_{\textbf{p}}B-\partial_{\textbf{p}}A\,\partial_{\textbf{x}}B

(177)

is the canonical Poisson bracket.

The Moyal product is associative

A\star B\star C\equiv(A\star B)\star C=A\star(B\star C),

(178)

and becomes an ordinary product inside the phase space integrals

\int d^{n}xd^{n}pA\star B=\int d^{n}xd^{n}pAB,

(179)

provided integrands vanish at integration boundaries.
Using the Moyal product,
it is convenient to define the Moyal or sine bracket

\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{A,B\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}\equiv-i(A\star B-B\star A)=2A\sin(\hat{\mathfrak{L}}/2)B,

(180)

which is the Wigner map of the commutator,

\left[\hat{A},\hat{B}\right]\mapsto i\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{\mathfrak{W}(\hat{A}),\mathfrak{W}(\hat{B})\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}=i\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{A,B\}\!\boldsymbol{\}},

(181)

as well as the symmetric Moyal or Groenewold-Baker’s cosine bracket

\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}\equiv A\star B+B\star A=2A\cos(\hat{\mathfrak{L}}/2)B,

(182)

which is the Wigner map of the anti-commutator

\left\{\hat{A},\hat{B}\right\}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{\!\{\mathfrak{W}(\hat{A}),\mathfrak{W}(\hat{B})\}\!\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}=\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}\!\boldsymbol{\}},

(183)

where
\mathfrak{W}(\hat{A})
denotes the Weyl transform of
\hat{A}
.

When assuming an eikonal or leading-order WKB approximation,
these brackets have the following properties

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{A,B\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle A\hat{\mathfrak{L}}B=\left\{A,B\right\}_{\text{c}}+{\cal O}(\hbar_{\text{eff}}^{2}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\{}\!\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}\!\boldsymbol{\}}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle 2AB+{\cal O}(\hbar_{\text{eff}}^{2}),

(184)

where the effective Planck constant is defined in Eq. (
58
).

Finally, let us give some useful formulae for our case.
If configuration space is two-dimensional and phase-space functions
A
and
B
do not depend on a coordinate
x
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{A,B\right\}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{y}A\,\partial_{p_{y}}B-\partial_{p_{y}}A\,\partial_{y}B,

(185)

\displaystyle f\left\{A,B\right\}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-B\,\partial_{p_{y}}f\,\partial_{y}A

(186)

\displaystyle+{\cal D}(\partial_{p_{y}},\partial_{y}),\ \forall\,f=f(\mathbf{p}),

where

{\cal D}(\partial)

are total derivative terms with respect to arguments listed in braces.
Such terms can be omitted when working
inside reduced phase space integrals:
\int d^{2}p\,dy\,{\cal D}(\partial)=0
,
assuming that physical values vanish at phase space borders.
For instance,
for the functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{x}U+p_{x}U_{2}/p_{D}^{2}-\beta p_{x}/p_{D}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2p_{x}p_{y}U_{1}/p_{D}^{2}-p_{x}p_{y}U_{3}/p_{D}^{4},

we obtain the following

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p^{2n}_{D}\left(\left\{\mathcal{H},\overline{W}\right\}_{\text{c}}-2\mathcal{G}\overline{W}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=2\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p_{x}p_{y}p^{2(n-1)}_{D}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times\left[(1-n)\left(U_{1}+\frac{U_{3}}{p^{2}_{D}}\right)+U_{1}\right]\overline{W},~~~~~~

(187)

where

hence

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p^{4}_{D}\left(\left\{\mathcal{H},\overline{W}\right\}_{\text{c}}-2\mathcal{G}\overline{W}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=-2\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p_{x}p_{y}U_{3}\overline{W},~~~

(188)

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p^{2}_{D}\left(\left\{\mathcal{H},\overline{W}\right\}_{\text{c}}-2\mathcal{G}\overline{W}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=2\int\frac{d^{2}p}{(2\pi)^{2}}dy\,p_{x}p_{y}U_{1}\overline{W},

(189)

because neither
