Generalized single-valued hyperlogarithms
Oliver Schnetz

Abstract
. Single-valued hyperlogarithms are generalized to include primitives of differential forms d ​ z / ( a ​ z ​ z ¯ + b ​ z + c ​ z ¯ + d ) \mathrm{d}z/(az{\overline{z}}+bz+c{\overline{z}}+d) , a , b , c , d ∈ ℂ a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{C}} , where z ¯ {\overline{z}} is the complex conjugate of the variable z ∈ ℂ z\in{\mathbb{C}} . The construction of these generalized single-valued hyperlogarithms (GSVHs) relies on a commutative hexagon which allows one to express primitives as anti-primitives. The article provides a proved constructive theory of GSVHs.



1.
Introduction

Single-valued hyperlogarithms on the punctured complex plane have a plethora of applications in mathematics. A classical example is the Bloch-Wigner dilogarithm
(see
[
24
]
and the references therein),

(1)

D(z)=\mathrm{Im}\,(\mathrm{Li}\,_{2}(z)+\log(1-z)\log|z|),

where
\mathrm{Li}\,_{2}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z^{k}/k^{2}
is the (multivalued) dilogarithm. There exists a natural class of single-valued functions which generalizes the Block-Wigner dilogarithm
to hyperlogarithms of higher weights. This class of single-valued hyperlogarithms is well studied and understood (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
17
]
).

In recent years, certain calculations in perturbative Quantum Field Theory (pQFT) led to single-valued functions on the punctured complex plane (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
17
]
).
It quickly became clear that the class of single-valued hyperlogarithms is too restricted to describe many of these functions (even if they are of hyperlogarithmic nature)

[
11
,
17
,
18
]
.

While single-valued hyperlogarithms are iterated integrals of differential forms with poles in
{\mathbb{C}}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z-a},\quad a\in{\mathbb{C}},

there is demand for including differential forms with denominators which are bilinear in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
(the complex conjugate of
z
),

(2)

\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{az{\overline{z}}+bz+c{\overline{z}}+d},\quad a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{C}}.

One expects that (iterated) single-valued primitives of these differential forms exist if the zero locus of the denominator is empty in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
By integrating
\mathrm{d}z/(z+1/{\overline{z}})
, e.g., we get
\log(z{\overline{z}}+1)
which is single-valued on
{\mathbb{C}}
(Example
68
).

This setup, however, still is too specific. In practice, one often has denominators which vanish on a curve in
{\mathbb{C}}
. A frequent case is the denominator
z-{\overline{z}}
which vanishes on the
real axis. In general, these differential forms have no single-valued primitives on the punctured complex plane. The logarithm
\log(z-{\overline{z}})
inherits the singular real axis from the
differential form.

Nonetheless, it may happen that a numerator cancels the singularity of the denominator to render the function single-valued on the punctured complex plane.
Because
D(z)
in (
1
) vanishes on the real line, one examples of this type is (see Example
71
, Section
8.3
, and
[
11
]
)

(3)

\frac{D(z)}{z-{\overline{z}}}.

Another example of a function with lifted singularity is (Example
69
)

(4)

\frac{\log(z{\overline{z}})}{z-1/{\overline{z}}}.

Both functions have single-valued primitives on the (punctured) complex plane. Generalized single-valued hyperlogarithms (GSVHs) are the most general class of single-valued functions
which emerge from iteratively integrating differential forms (
2
), see Definition
44
.
In this article we present a theory of these functions. We prove the existence of single-valued primitives (and other fundamental properties of GSVHs) in Theorem
74

using the commutativity of the hexagon in Figure
1
. The proof is constructive (Section
7
) and implemented in the Maple package
HyperlogProcedures

[
19
]
(which also contains application to pQFT).

In detail, the paper is organized as follows. In Sections
2
and
3
we set up the notation and compile some results on iterated integrals and hyperlogarithms.
In Section
4
we generalize to hyperlogarithms in two variables
z
and
{\overline{z}}
which can either be considered independent or complex conjugates.
We fix a quadratically closed number field
{\mathbb{F}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
which is closed under complex conjugation,
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}={\mathbb{F}}
, and construct the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector-space
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}
of
(possibly multivalued) generalized hyperlogarithms with a finite singular set
z\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})\subset\{-(c{\overline{z}}+d)/(a{\overline{z}}+b),\;a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{F}}\}
which emerges from iteratively integrating
differential forms of type (
2
). In Section
5
we define the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma_{0}}
of single-valued functions with point-like singularities in a finite set

\Sigma_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
(which – at this point – is not related to
\Sigma({\overline{z}})
). Our notion of single-valuedness does not only demand the absence of monodromies but the (stronger) existence of
single-valued log-Laurent expansions

\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{m,{\overline{m}}\geq M_{a}}c_{\ell,m,{\overline{m}}}^{a}[\log(z-a)({\overline{z}}-{\overline{a}})]^{\ell}(z-a)^{m}({\overline{z}}-{\overline{a}})^{\overline{m}},\quad M_{a}\in{\mathbb{Z}},

at all
a\in\Sigma
and at infinity (Definition
35
; these expansions are natural in the context of pQFT
[
1
,
17
,
18
]
).

Generalized single-valued hyperlogarithms are defined in Section
6
as the intersection of both spaces

\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}\cap\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}=\bigcup_{\Sigma_{0}\subset{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma_{0}}

is the union of
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma_{0}}
over all finite singular loci
\Sigma_{0}
.

Likewise,
\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
is the union over all
\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}

\partial_{\overline{z}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}

which – by Theorem

\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. The functions (

2.
Iterated integrals

Let

(6)

\Sigma=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{N}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}

be a finite set of points in
{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\Sigma^{\ast}
be the set of words with letters in
\Sigma
, with
e\in\Sigma^{\ast}
being the empty word.
Let
|w|
be the length of the word
w
and
\widetilde{w}
be
w
in reversed order. With the shuffle product

(7)

ua\,\hbox{\rus x}\,vb=(u\,\hbox{\rus x}\,vb)a+(ua\,\hbox{\rus x}\,v)b,\quad\text{for }u,v\in\Sigma^{\ast},\;a,b\in\Sigma,

and
e\,\hbox{\rus x}\,u=u=u\,\hbox{\rus x}\,e
the
{\mathbb{Z}}
-span
\langle\Sigma^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
becomes a commutative ring.

For any word
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{n}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
and any path
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma
with
a_{0}=\gamma(0)
and
a_{n+1}=\gamma(1)
we define the iterated integral
[
12
]

(8)

I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})=\int_{0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<1}\gamma^{\ast}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{1}}{t_{1}-a_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\gamma^{\ast}\frac{\mathrm{d}t_{n}}{t_{n}-a_{n}},

where
\gamma^{\ast}\omega
is the pullback of the differential form
\omega
by
\gamma
.

Because (in this one-dimensional case) the integrand is closed, it is clear that the iterated integral is a homotopy invariant. It neither depends on the parametrization
of the curve
\gamma
nor on the shape of the path
\gamma
as long as the endpoints are fixed and no singularities are crossed.
It is convenient to generalize to the potentially singular case where the endpoints
a_{0},a_{n+1}
are in
\Sigma
.
In this case the value of the iterated integral may depend on the direction in which the endpoints are approached (see, e.g., Section 2.2 in
[
17
]
for an elementary approach to iterated
integrals). In the case of hyperlogarithms (Section
3
) the initial point is 0 and the endpoint is the variable
z
. One typically choses the initial point to be approached from the
positive real axis (this, e.g., gives
I(0,0,z)=\log z
). An equivalent alternative is to shuffle-regularize iterated integrals, see Section
3.2
.

In the case of single-valued functions (Section
5
) the regularization convention for
\gamma
is insignificant as long as it is used consistently. In
[
19
]
the path
\gamma
is
always a straight line between the endpoints (this gives
I(0,0,z)=\log|z|
).

In general, iterated integrals are very convenient to handle. An (incomplete) list of identities (all of which are easy to verify) is
[
17
]

I0:

I(a_{0},a_{1})=1
by definition.

I1:

I(a_{0},w,a_{n+1})
is independent of the parametrization of
\gamma
.

I2:

I(a_{0},w,a_{n+1})
is a homotopy invariant.

I3:

I(a_{0},w,a_{0})=0
for the constant path
\gamma=a_{0}
and
|w|\geq 1
.

I4:

I(a_{0},w,a_{n+1})=(-1)^{|w|}I(a_{n+1},\widetilde{w},a_{0})
if the path
\gamma
is reversed.

I5:

For any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{k},x)I(x,a_{k+1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})

by path composition
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}
with
\gamma_{1}(1)=x=\gamma_{2}(0)
.

I6:

For any
x\in{\mathbb{C}}
,

I(a_{0},u,x)I(a_{0},v,x)=I(a_{0},u\,\hbox{\rus x}\,v,x)

by shuffling the
t_{i}
in the integration domain of (
8
).

I7:

For any
A\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
,
B\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
a_{0}\neq a_{1}
,
a_{n}\neq a_{n+1}
,

I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})=I(Aa_{0}+B,(Aa_{1}+B)\ldots(Aa_{n}+B),Aa_{n+1}+B)

by substituting
t_{i}\mapsto(t_{i}-B)/A
in (
8
).

I8:

For letters
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n+1}
which depend on a variable
x
,

\partial_{x}I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\partial_{x}\log\frac{a_{k+1}-a_{k}}{a_{k}-a_{k-1}}\right)I(a_{0},a_{1}\ldots a_{k-1}a_{k+1}\ldots a_{n},a_{n+1})

(where
\partial_{x}\log 0=0
) by using integration by parts in (
8
).

Note that in
I7
the alphabet
\Sigma
changes to
A\Sigma+B
. There exists an analogous formula for any Möbius transformation
t\mapsto\frac{at+b}{ct+d}

[
17
]
.
Singular iterated integrals are more subtle: The transformation
t\mapsto t/A
formally maps
I(0,0,1)=0
to
I(0,0,A)=\log(A)
.
If
A=1
, however,
I7
holds for all iterated integrals. Likewise, identities
I0
–
I6
and
I8
also hold for singular iterated integrals.

There also exists a formula for the Galois coaction on iterated integrals
[
6
,
15
]
. Although the existence of a coaction is central in the theory of iterated integrals,
we will not use it in this article.

3.
Hyperlogarithms

3.1.
General theory

In this section we mostly follow the first sections of
[
5
]
. For completeness we give (somewhat independent) proofs for the results in this section.

Consider the ring of regular functions on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma
,

(9)

\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}={\mathbb{C}}[z,((z-a)^{-1})_{a\in\Sigma}].

(One may think of the generator
z
as being related to a puncture at infinity.) For any word
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
we define the multivalued analytic hyperlogarithm
L_{w}(z)
as iterated integral,

(10)

L_{w}(z)=I(0^{+},w,z).

The
L_{w}(z)
span the space of hyperlogarithms over
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
,

\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}=\langle L_{w}(z),\;w\in\Sigma^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}.

The homotopy dependence of
I(0^{+},w,z)
reflects the multivaluedness of
L_{w}(z)
. We get from
I8

(11)

\partial_{z}L_{wa}(z)=\frac{1}{z-a}L_{w}(z)

with (regualrized) limits
L_{w}(0)=0
. If
0\in\Sigma
, we get for a string
0^{\{n\}}
of
n
letters 0

(12)

L_{0^{\{n\}}}(z)=\frac{1}{n!}\log^{n}(z).

If the path of the iterated integral does not encircle singularities then
L_{w}(z)
can be expressed as multiple sum.
With
w=b_{1}0^{\{n_{1}-1\}}b_{2}0^{\{n_{2}-1\}}\ldots b_{r}0^{\{n_{r}-1\}}
(i.e.
b_{1}=a_{1}
,
b_{2}=a_{n_{1}+1}
, …) we get

(13)

L_{w}(z)=(-1)^{r}\sum_{0<k_{1}<k_{2}<\ldots<k_{r}}\frac{(\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}})^{k_{1}}(\frac{b_{3}}{b_{2}})^{k_{2}}\cdots(\frac{z}{b_{r}})^{k_{r}}}{k_{1}^{n_{1}}k_{2}^{n_{2}}\cdots k_{r}^{n_{r}}}\quad\text{for small }|z|.

We refer to
|w|
as the weight of the hyperlogarithm
L_{w}
. The number
r
of non-zero letters in
w
is the depth of
L_{w}
(and of
w
).
It will follow from Theorem
4
that weight and depth of hyperlogarithms are well-defined.

Let
{\mathbb{C}}\langle\Sigma\rangle
be the
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector-space freely generated by
\Sigma^{\ast}
.
Following F. Brown in
[
4
,
5
]
we use the formal algebra (note that in a computer implementation one would use this formal setup)

\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}:={\mathbb{C}}\langle\Sigma\rangle\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}

as model of hyperlogarithms. We consider
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
as free
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
module where multiplication is on the
right
1
1

1

In
[
5
]
multiplication is on the left. Because of the connection to motivic Galois theory where the Galois group conveniently coacts to the right we
employ a left to right notation.
.

We consider hyperlogarithms as (analytic) realizations of the map

(14)

\mathrm{eval}\,:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}&\rightarrow&\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}\\
w\otimes\phi(z)&\mapsto&\phi(z)L_{w}(z).\end{array}

The
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
module
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
has a trivial Hopf-algebra structure with shuffle multiplication (
7
) and deconcatenation as
coproduct
2
2

2

The Galois coaction structure has no connection to this Hopf-algebra structure.
.
The antipode is given by
w\mapsto(-1)^{|w|}\widetilde{w}
which, by
I4
, corresponds to path reversal.

By
I6
eval maps the shuffle product to the pointwise product of complex functions,

(15)

\mathrm{eval}\,[(u\,\hbox{\rus x}\,v)\otimes\phi(z)]=\phi(z)L_{u}(z)L_{v}(z),\quad\text{for }u,v\in\Sigma^{\ast},\;\phi\in\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma},

which lifts eval to a homomorphism between the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebras
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
.

Words
0^{\{k\}}aw
,
a\neq 0
,
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
, which begin in 0 have a unique representation (see Lemma 3 in
[
20
]
)

(16)

0^{\{k\}}aw=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}0^{\{k-i\}}\,\hbox{\rus x}\,a[0^{\{i\}}\,\hbox{\rus x}\,w].

With (
12
), (
13
), and (
15
) this defines integral and sum representations for hyperlogarithms of words which begin with 0,

(17)

L_{0^{\{k\}}aw}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\frac{(\log z)^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}L_{a[0^{\{i\}}\,\hbox{\rus x}\,w]}(z),

where the hyperlogarithms are extended to
\Sigma^{\ast}
by linearity. One can use (
12
), (
13
), (
17
) or (
11
) and
L_{w}(0)=0
as alternative definitions of
hyperlogarithms.

We make
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
a differential algebra by defining a derivative
\partial_{z}
.
For the empty word
e
we set

\partial_{z}(e\otimes\phi)=e\otimes\partial_{z}\phi(z).

For all other words we define

(18)

\partial_{z}(wa\otimes\phi)=wa\otimes\partial_{z}\phi(z)+w\otimes\frac{\phi(z)}{z-a}.

For any
a\in\Sigma
we define left and right derivatives—with respect to
x
, see (
7
)—
\delta_{a}^{\mathrm{l}}
and
\delta_{a}^{\mathrm{r}}
on
\Sigma^{\ast}
by

(19)

\delta_{a}^{\mathrm{l}}w=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}u&\text{if }w=au,\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad\delta_{a}^{\mathrm{r}}w=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}v&\text{if }w=va,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We get

(20)

\partial_{z}=\text{id}\otimes\partial_{z}+\sum_{a\in\Sigma}\delta_{a}^{\mathrm{r}}\otimes\frac{1}{z-a}.

The above formula implies that
\partial_{z}
is a derivation on
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
. We find that eval is a homomorphism of differential algebras.

We also define a residue
\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}
for
a\in{\mathbb{C}}
acting on
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
,

\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}(w\otimes\phi)=w\otimes\mathrm{res}\,_{a}\phi,

where
\mathrm{res}\,_{a}
on the right hand side is the residue at
a
in
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
.

Because
\mathrm{res}\,_{a}\partial_{z}=0
we find that
\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}\circ\partial_{z}
decreases the weights of the words in
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
.
The residue in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
needs a further evaluation at

,

\mathrm{res}\,_{a}=\mathrm{eval}\,_{a}\circ\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}:=\mathrm{eval}\,|_{z=a}\circ\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}.

Without restriction we assume that any
x=\sum_{w}w\otimes\phi_{w}\in\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
is a finite sum over distinct words
w
with non-zero canceled fractions
\phi_{w}
.
Any such representation is unique. The maximum weight (length) of words in
x
induces a strict partial order on
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
.
It is a standard technique in our proofs to show that a minimal counter-example does not exist. To use this technique efficiently it is convenient to refine the order induced by the weight.

Definition 2
.

Let
x=\sum_{w}w\otimes\phi_{w}\in\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
as above with weight
|x|=\max\{|w|\}
. Let
N_{x}=\#\{w,|w|=|x|\}
be the number of words in the maximum weight part of
x
.
The strict partial order ‘
\prec
’ on
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
is defined by

(21)

x\prec y\Leftrightarrow|x|<|y|\text{ or }(|x|=|y|\text{ and }N_{x}<N_{y}).

Note that
\partial_{z}f\not\succ f
for all
f\in\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
.

Lemma 3
.

The kernel of
\partial_{z}
in
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
is
e\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
be of minimum weight
n
such that
\partial_{z}x=0
. Modulo lower weights
\partial_{z}
only acts on the right hand side of the tensor product.
For
\partial_{z}x=0
we hence need that
\partial_{z}
trivializes the fractions of the maximum weight part of
x
. Hence, these fractions are constant.
If the weight of
x
is at least 1 then the maximum weight part of
x
is of the form
\sum_{wa}wa\otimes c_{wa}
with
0\neq c_{wa}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}

of weight
n-1
. We get
0=\mathrm{res}\,_{a}^{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}}\partial_{z}x=\sum_{w}w\otimes c_{wa}
modulo terms of weight
\leq n-2
. Hence all
c_{wa}=0
and the claim follows by contradiction.
∎

We get the following structure theorem for hyperlogarithms.

Theorem 4

(F. Brown 2004
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a finite set.

(1)

The map
eval is an isomorphism. In particular,
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
is a free
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
module.

(2)

The sequence

0\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{z}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}\longrightarrow 0

is exact. I.e. the kernel of
\partial_{z}
in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
is
{\mathbb{C}}
and every
f\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
has a primitive
F\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
with
\partial_{z}F=f
.

(3)

\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
is differentially simple. I.e. for every
0\neq f\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
there exists a differential operator
D
such that
Df=1
.

Proof.

We know that eval is a surjective differential homomorphism. To prove (1) we show that there exists no
0\neq x\in\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
with
\mathrm{eval}\,x=0
.
Because the order
\prec
is discrete we may assume that
x
is minimal with respect to
\prec
and all
\Sigma
. Let
w\otimes\phi
be a term in
x
of maximum weight.
Because
\{\phi=0\}
is a finite set in
{\mathbb{C}}
we may assume without restriction that
\{\phi=0\}\subseteq\Sigma
. We have
\phi^{-1}\in\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
.
Because the term
w\otimes 1
in
(e\otimes\phi^{-1})x
loses weight upon differentiation we have
\partial_{z}(e\otimes\phi^{-1})x\prec x
.
Hence
\partial_{z}(e\otimes\phi^{-1})x=0
by minimality of
x
. From Lemma
3
we get
(e\otimes\phi^{-1})x\in e\otimes{\mathbb{C}}
. Hence
x=e\otimes c\phi
for some
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
From
\mathrm{eval}\,x=c\phi=0
we get
c=0
which is a contradiction.

By statement (1) and Lemma
3
the kernel of
\partial_{z}
is
{\mathbb{C}}
. To prove (2) it hence suffices to show that every
f\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
has a primitive.
We do this by induction over the weight
n\in\{-\infty,0,1,\ldots\}
of
f
. A primitive of
0
is 0.
For
f\neq 0
we may assume by linearity using partial fraction decomposition that
f(z)=(z-a)^{k}L_{w}(z)
for
w\in\Sigma^{\ast}
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
A primitive of
f(z)=(z-a)^{-1}L_{w}(z)
is
L_{wa}(z)\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
. If
k\neq-1
, integration by parts yields

\int(z-a)^{k}L_{w}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{(z-a)^{k+1}}{k+1}L_{w}(z)-\frac{1}{k+1}\int(z-a)^{k+1}\partial_{z}L_{w}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

With (
11
) the right hand side exists in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
by induction.

We also prove (3) by induction over the weight
n
of
f
. Let
0\neq f\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
and let
Q
be the common denominator of all coefficients of terms with weight
n
in
f
.
Then
Qf
has only polynomial coefficients in the maximum weight piece. Let
m
be the maximum degree of these polynomial. With
D_{0}=\partial_{z}^{m}Q

we find that
D_{0}f
has weight
n
but
\partial_{z}D_{0}f
has weight
\leq n-1
. If
D_{0}f=c\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
then
D=D_{0}/c
. Otherwise we obtain from statement (2) that
\partial_{z}D_{0}f\neq 0
.
By induction there exists a differential operator
D_{1}
such that
D=D_{1}\partial_{z}D_{0}
fulfills
Df=1
.
∎

3.5.
A commutative hexagon

We have defined two derivations on
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
, namely
\partial_{z}
and
m_{a}
.
Because hyperlogarithms are analytic the derivative
\partial_{\overline{z}}
with respect to the complex conjugate of
z
is zero. Later, when we define GSVHs,
we sacrifice analyticity for single-valuedness. This means that
m_{a}
becomes trivial whereas
\partial_{\overline{z}}
becomes non-trivial.
The main tool for the construction of GSVHs will be a commutative hexagon which has a (simpler) analogue in the analytic case with
\partial_{\overline{z}}
replaced by
m_{a}
. We present this
analogue as introduction to GSVHs. An application of the commutative hexagon for hyperlogarithms is integration in the motivic
f
-alphabet
[
23
]
.

Before we prove commutativity of the hexagon we need to define a generic infinitesimal monodromy (covering all points
a\in\Sigma
). To this end we extend

\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
to
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
where the first factor keeps track of the puncture
a
.
By

we embed
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
into
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
.
(A model for
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
is
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
which we do not use here.) We define

(40)

m:\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}&\longrightarrow&\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}\\
w\otimes f(z)&\mapsto&\sum_{a\in\Sigma}wa\otimes m_{a}f(z).\end{array}

The derivative
\partial_{z}
only acts on the right factor of
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}
.

.

is
\Sigma^{\ast}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}
. It is clear that

.
Assume that there exists an

with

and weight

(in the right factor). We uniquely write

which is a contradiction.

We prove the existence of

by induction over the weight

of

4.
Generalized hyperlogarithms

Let
{\mathbb{F}}=\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
be a quadratically closed number field, i.e.

(45)

x\in{\mathbb{F}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}\Rightarrow\overline{x},\pm\sqrt{x}\in{\mathbb{F}}.

The smallest field
{\mathbb{F}}
are the constructible numbers, the largest field is
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
.

Remark 12
.

For applications in massless pQFT the fields
{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{i})
and
{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{e}^{\pi\mathrm{i}/3})
possibly suffice
[
10
,
20
,
21
]
.
We expect that pQFT uses only a small subspace of the GSVHs defined in Section
6
.

To describe single-valued hyperlogarithms on the punctured complex plane we need to give up analyticity.
Because the differentials
\partial_{z}
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}
commute, in most situations one has the option to either consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as complex conjugates
or as independent variables. Depending on the context we shift from one picture to the other. We use double arguments
z,{\overline{z}}
in functions if we consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as independent variables.

Remark 13
.

With minimal modifications it is possible to develop a very similar theory of GSVHs where
z
and
{\overline{z}}
are replaced by any linear combination of
z
and
{\overline{z}}

(or more general functions of
z,{\overline{z}}
). One may, e.g., use real and imaginary parts of
z
. For the (rather special) Gegenbauer method in
[
1
,
17
]

one needs to use the modulus and the argument of
z
. In many situations, however, the variables
z
and
{\overline{z}}
are most efficient.
In pQFT, e.g., one has to invert the Laplacian
\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}
which factorizes in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
.

In contrast to the concept of single-valuedness (Section
5
), the definition of generalized hyperlogarithms is of technical nature.
In pQFT there exists no fundamental property that requires generalized hyperlogarithms. They are merely the most general functions that are well understood and can be handled efficiently.
For small graphs most graphical functions can be expressed in terms of generalized hyperlogarithms
[
1
,
17
]
.

4.1.
Fractional linear transformations

Let

(46)

\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}=\Big\{\beta(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{F}}\Big\}

be the set of fractional linear transformations (FLTs) with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}}
.
The FLT
\beta
is invertible if
ad-bc\neq 0
. Then
\beta
is an automorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
(a Möbius transformation). Because

\partial_{z}\frac{az+b}{cz+d}=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^{2}},

ad-bc=0
is equivalent to constant
\beta
. For any
\beta(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)
with
ad-bc\neq 0
the inverse of
\beta
is (we do
not
use
\beta^{-1}
for the reciprocal of
\beta
)

\beta^{-1}(z)=\frac{dz-b}{-cz+a}.

Lemma 14
.

For any variables
z,{\overline{z}}
and
\beta(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
with
ad-bc\neq 0
we have

\partial_{z}\log({\overline{z}}-\beta(z))=\frac{1}{z-\beta^{-1}({\overline{z}})}-\frac{1}{z+d/c},

where the second term on the right hand side is absent if
c=0
.

Proof.

Because
ad-bc\neq 0
either
a\neq 0
or
c\neq 0
. We get

{\overline{z}}-\beta(z)=\frac{(c{\overline{z}}-a)(z-\beta^{-1}({\overline{z}}))}{cz+d}.

Taking
\partial_{z}\log
gives the result.
∎

In general, the product of two FLTs is not a sum of FLTs (it may have squares). Here, we need the converse property that the difference of two FLTs is the product of two FLTs.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
.

(1)

There exist
\beta_{3},\beta_{4}\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
such that

\beta_{1}(z)-\beta_{2}(z)=\beta_{3}(z)\cdot\beta_{4}(z)

(2)

We have

\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{z}\log(z-\beta_{1}({\overline{z}}))&\partial_{\overline{z}}\log(z-\beta_{1}({\overline{z}}))\\
\partial_{z}\log(z-\beta_{2}({\overline{z}}))&\partial_{\overline{z}}\log(z-\beta_{2}({\overline{z}}))\end{array}\right)=[\partial_{\overline{z}}\log(\beta_{1}({\overline{z}})-\beta_{2}({\overline{z}}))]\left[\partial_{z}\log\frac{z-\beta_{1}({\overline{z}})}{z-\beta_{2}({\overline{z}})}\right].

Proof.

For
a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},d_{1},a_{2},b_{2},c_{2},d_{2}\in{\mathbb{F}}
we have

\frac{a_{1}z+b_{1}}{c_{1}z+d_{1}}-\frac{a_{2}z+b_{2}}{c_{2}z+d_{2}}=\frac{(a_{1}c_{2}-a_{2}c_{1})z^{2}+(a_{1}d_{2}-a_{2}d_{1}+b_{1}c_{2}-b_{2}c_{1})z+b_{1}d_{2}-b_{2}d_{1}}{(c_{1}z+d_{1})(c_{2}z+d_{2})}.

Because
{\mathbb{F}}
is quadratically closed, the numerator factorizes in
{\mathbb{F}}
and the product may be expressed as
\beta_{3}(z)\beta_{4}(z)

for a suitable choice of
\beta_{3}(z)
and
\beta_{4}(z)
. Statement (2) is an explicit calculation.
∎

Statement (1) is the sole reason for our restriction to quadratically closed fields.

4.2.
Iterated integrals with parameters

Let

\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}=\bigcup_{\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{F}}}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}.

Likewise
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
is the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra of hyperlogarithms in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}
with constant coefficients in
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Any iterated integral with letters in
{\mathbb{C}}(z)
is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. A bootstrap algorithm for the conversion to constant letters is given by differentiating the iterated integral with
respect to
z
using
I8
. This lowers the weight of the iterated integral. By induction the derivative is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. A priori, the primitive is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Using
I8
it is not hard to see that the primitive is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. This algorithm was suggested by F. Brown
[
2
,
3
]
and later implemented by E. Panzer
[
16
]
.

To emphasize the dependence on the variable
z
(or
{\overline{z}}
) we will give
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
an argument.
So,
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\Sigma}(z)
denotes the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra of hyperlogarithms in
z
and
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}(z)
is the set of FLTs over
{\mathbb{F}}
in
z
.

Lemma 16
.

We consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as independent variables. Let
f(z,{\overline{z}})=L_{\beta_{1}(z)\ldots\beta_{n}(z)}({\overline{z}})
be a hyperlogarithm in
{\overline{z}}
with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}(z)
.
For any
a\in{\mathbb{F}}
we get
\lim_{z\to a}f(z,{\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
, where the explicit result may depend on the direction in which
z
approaches
a
.

Proof.

We use induction over
n
. For
n=0
we have
f=1
and the claim is trivial. If
n\geq 1
then
f(z,0)=0
and

\partial_{\overline{z}}f(z,{\overline{z}})=\frac{1}{{\overline{z}}-\beta_{n}(z)}L_{\beta_{1}(z)\ldots\beta_{n-1}(z)}({\overline{z}}).

By Brown’s algorithm, the hyperlogarithm on the right hand side can be converted into a hyperlogarithm in
z
(with
{\overline{z}}
-dependent letters).
By Lemma
6
it admits a log expansion at
z=a
. By induction the constant term is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
. The factor
({\overline{z}}-\beta_{n}(z))^{-1}
has an expansion at
z=a

with constant term
({\overline{z}}-\beta_{n}(a))^{-1}
if
\beta_{n}(a)\neq\infty
. In this case
\beta_{n}(a)\in{\mathbb{F}}
and integration with respect to
{\overline{z}}
gives
f\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
plus terms that
vanish in the limit
z\to a
. If
\beta_{n}(a)=\infty
, the constant term vanishes and
\lim_{z\to a}f(z,{\overline{z}})=0
.
∎

Example 17
.

Consider
f(z,{\overline{z}})=L_{z-a}({\overline{z}})
for
a\in{\mathbb{F}}
. We get the regularized limit (see Section
3.2
)

\lim_{z\to a}f(z,{\overline{z}})=\lim_{z\to a}\log(1-{\overline{z}}/(z-a))=\lim_{z\to a}\log(-{\overline{z}}/(z-a))=\log({\overline{z}})+c=L_{0}({\overline{z}})+c

for some constant
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
whose value depends on the direction in which
z
approaches
a
.

Remark 18
.

Note that due to possible singularities, taking limits in hyperlogarithms cannot be performed by substitution (in general). As an example consider

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to 0}L_{z0}({\overline{z}})=\lim_{z\to 0}L_{10}({\overline{z}}/z)=-\lim_{z\to 0}\mathrm{Li}\,_{2}({\overline{z}}/z)=\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{[\log(z/{\overline{z}})\pm\mathrm{i}\pi]^{2}}{2}-\zeta(2)=\frac{[\log({\overline{z}})\mp\mathrm{i}\pi]^{2}}{2}-\zeta(2)

\displaystyle\neq\;L_{00}({\overline{z}})=\frac{(\log{\overline{z}})^{2}}{2},

where the sign ambiguity is related to monodromy. The subtle step in Brown’s algorithm is to accurately take the limits
z\to 0
that provide the integration constants
[
16
]
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
f(z,{\overline{z}})=L_{\beta_{1}(z)\ldots\beta_{n}(z)}({\overline{z}})
be a hyperlogarithm in
{\overline{z}}
with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}(z)
. Then

f
is a linear combination of hyperlogarithms in
z
with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
and coefficients in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
.

Proof.

We consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as independent variables and use induction over
n
(following Brown’s algorithm). For
n=0
the claim is trivial.

By differentiation with respect to
z
we get from
I8
a sum of hyperlogarithms in
{\overline{z}}
of weight
n-1
with factors
\pm\partial_{z}\log(\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k})

for
k=0,\ldots,n
(where
\beta_{0}=0
and
\beta_{n+1}={\overline{z}}
). By induction the hyperlogarithms are linear combinations of hyperlogarithms in
z
with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})

and coefficients in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
.

With Lemma
15
(1) we express
\beta_{k+1}-\beta_{k}
for
k<n
as product of two FLTs over
{\mathbb{F}}
.
Upon differentiation the log becomes a sum of simple poles in
z
at values in
{\mathbb{F}}
.

The term
k=n
is only non-zero if
\beta_{n}
is not constant. In this case we get from Lemma
14
that the coefficient is a sum of simple poles with
values in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
.

By (
11
) both cases have primitives which are linear combinations of hyperlogarithms in
z
with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
.
These hyperlogarithms vanish in the limit
z\to 0
. To determine
f
we need to add the integration constant (in
z
) which is
\lim_{z\to 0}f(z,{\overline{z}})
.
By Lemma
16
this limit is in
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})

∎

Example 20
.

Consider
f(z,{\overline{z}})=L_{z-a}({\overline{z}})
,
a\in{\mathbb{F}}
, from Example
17
. We have

f(z,{\overline{z}})=\log\Big(1-\frac{{\overline{z}}}{z-a}\Big)=\log\Big[\Big(1-\frac{z}{{\overline{z}}+a}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{{\overline{z}}}{a}\Big)\Big(1-\frac{z}{a}\Big)^{-1}\Big]=L_{{\overline{z}}+a}(z)+L_{-a}({\overline{z}})-L_{a}(z).

Differentiation with respect to
z
gives
\partial_{z}f(z,{\overline{z}})=(z-{\overline{z}}-a)^{-1}-(z-a)^{-1}
. Upon integration we get the first and the third term. The second term is
\lim_{z\to 0}f(z,{\overline{z}})
.

To obtain an explicit result one can use the path convention at the beginning of Section
3.4
. Otherwise the result depends on the sheets where the hyperlogarithms are evaluated.
This will become insignificant when we pass to single-valued functions.

4.3.
Definition of generalized hyperlogarithms

Let
{\overline{z}}
be the complex conjugate of
z
. Let

\Sigma({\overline{z}})=\{\beta_{1}({\overline{z}}),\beta_{2}({\overline{z}}),\ldots,\beta_{N}({\overline{z}})\}\subset\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})

be a finite set of FLTs over
{\mathbb{F}}
in the variable
{\overline{z}}
with subset of constants
\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}
.
We define the ring of regular functions on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma({\overline{z}}):={\mathbb{C}}\backslash\{z=\beta_{i}({\overline{z}}),i=1,\ldots,N\}
as the ring generated
by
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma({\overline{z}})}(z)
and
\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}}}({\overline{z}})
, see (
9
),

(47)

\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})={\mathbb{C}}[z,(z-\beta({\overline{z}}))^{-1}_{\beta\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})},{\overline{z}},({\overline{z}}-{\overline{b}})^{-1}_{b\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}}].

We also define

(48)

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z)=\bigcup_{\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{F}}}\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})=\bigcup_{\Sigma({\overline{z}})\subset\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})}\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}}).

Example 21
.

Reciprocals of bilinear forms in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
are in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})
. For
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
a\neq 0
,

\frac{1}{az{\overline{z}}+bz+c{\overline{z}}+d}=\frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{{\overline{z}}+b/a}\cdot\frac{1}{z+(c{\overline{z}}+d)/(a{\overline{z}}+b)}\in\mathcal{O}_{\{-\overline{b}/{\overline{a}},-(c{\overline{z}}+d)/(a{\overline{z}}+b)\}}(z,{\overline{z}}).

Remark 22
.

By partial fraction decomposition first in
z
and then (the coefficients) in
{\overline{z}}
every
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})
has a unique representation as

f(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{\beta({\overline{z}})\in\Sigma({\overline{z}}),b\in{\mathbb{F}},m,{\overline{m}}}c^{\beta,b}_{m,{\overline{m}}}(z-\beta({\overline{z}}))^{m}({\overline{z}}-\overline{b})^{\overline{m}},

where the sum is finite with
m,{\overline{m}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and

if
\beta\neq 0,b\neq 0
, respectively. Because partial fraction decomposition may generate (spurious) singularities in
{\overline{z}}

we cannot assume that
b\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}
. By Lemma
15
(1), however, we have
b\in{\mathbb{F}}
.

Definition 23
.

Let (
\Sigma^{\ast}
is the sets of words in
\Sigma
)

(49)

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}=\langle L_{v}({\overline{z}})L_{w({\overline{z}})}(z),v\in\overline{\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}}^{\ast},w({\overline{z}})\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})}

be the space of generalized hyperlogarithms on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma({\overline{z}})
. We use the notation
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\Sigma}
for the set of generalized hyperlogarithms with

{\mathbb{C}}
. The sets
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
and

By Theorem

(1) there exists a unique representation of any

as a linear combination of hyperlogarithms

L_{v}({\overline{z}})L_{w({\overline{z}})}(z)
with non-zero canceled fractions as coefficients. The weight of

is the maximum total weight
|v|+|w({\overline{z}})|
in this representation.

We suppress the
{\overline{z}}
dependence of

in subscripts. In

4.4.
Properties of generalized hyperlogarithms

In analogy to Theorem
4
and Proposition
8
we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 24
.

Let
\Sigma({\overline{z}})\subset\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
be finite.

(1)

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
is a free
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})
-module which is closed under multiplication. In particular,
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}
is a
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra.

(2)

The sequence

0\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})\longrightarrow\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\partial_{z}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}\longrightarrow 0

is exact. I.e. the kernel of
\partial_{z}
in
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
is
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
and every
f\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
has a primitive
F\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}

with
\partial_{z}F=f
.

(3)

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
is differentially simple. I.e. for every
0\neq f\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
there exists a differential operator
D
such that
Df=1
.

(4)

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
are stable under complex conjugation,

(50)

\overline{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}},\quad\overline{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}}=\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}.

(5)

\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
are stable under transformations in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
: For any

,
g\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
, and
\beta\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
we have

(51)

f(\beta(z))\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}\quad\text{and}\quad g(\beta(z))\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}.

Proof.

We consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as independent complex variables. For (1) we apply Theorem
4
(1) to the hyperlogarithms in
z
.
The coefficients are hyperlogarithms in
{\overline{z}}
on which we apply Theorem
4
(1) again. This proves that
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
is free.
With (
15
) it is clear that
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
is closed under multiplication.

We fix
{\overline{z}}
. By Theorem
4
(2) the kernel of
\partial_{z}
on

are functions which do not depend on
z
. By statement (1) the space of these functions
is
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
. To prove the existence of primitives in
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
we use the explicit inductive construction in the proof of Theorem
4
(2).
To do so we need partial fraction decomposition with respect to

in
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})
which can be achieved by repeatedly using the formula

\frac{1}{(z-\beta_{i}({\overline{z}}))(z-\beta_{j}({\overline{z}}))}=\frac{1}{(\beta_{i}({\overline{z}})-\beta_{j}({\overline{z}}))(z-\beta_{i}({\overline{z}}))}-\frac{1}{(\beta_{i}({\overline{z}})-\beta_{j}({\overline{z}}))(z-\beta_{j}({\overline{z}}))}.

By Lemma
15
(1) it is clear that both terms on the right hand side are in
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(z,{\overline{z}})
for some
\Sigma^{\prime}({\overline{z}})\supseteq\Sigma({\overline{z}})

which may contain new (constant) singularities due to zeros of
\beta_{i}({\overline{z}})-\beta_{j}({\overline{z}})
.

For (3) we follow the steps in the proof of Theorem
4
(3).
Now, multiplication with the common denominator
Q
ensures that the maximum weight part of
Qf
is a polynomial in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
with maximum degree
(m,{\overline{m}})
in
(z,{\overline{z}})
.
We define
D_{0}=\partial_{z}^{m}\partial_{\overline{z}}^{\overline{m}}Q
and find that
D_{0}f
has weight
n

\partial_{z}D_{0}f
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}D_{0}f
have weight
\leq n-1
.
If

then
D=D_{0}/c
. Otherwise
\partial_{z}D_{0}f\neq 0
or
\partial_{\overline{z}}D_{0}f\neq 0
. If
\partial_{z}D_{0}f\neq 0
(or
\partial_{\overline{z}}D_{0}f\neq 0
)
then (by induction) there exists a differential operator
D_{1}
such that
D=D_{1}\partial_{z}D_{0}
(or
D=D_{1}\partial_{\overline{z}}D_{0}
) fulfills
Df=1
.

For (4) we observe that (see Example
21
) the complex conjugate of
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})
can be written as
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma^{\prime}}(z,{\overline{z}})
for some new set
\Sigma^{\prime}({\overline{z}})
.
So, it suffices to prove the second identity in (
50
). The complex conjugate of

is
L_{\overline{v}}(z)L_{\overline{w}(z)}({\overline{z}})
.
The first factor is in
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. By Proposition

with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
and

\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
. Hence, also the second factor is in
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
. By statement (1) the product is in
\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

To show (5) we first observe that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})
is stable under transformations in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
. A generator

.
The result has a denominator which is bilinear in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
. By Example

, it is in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})
.

By linearity it suffices to show that

for

,

, see (

We use Proposition

\beta_{1}\ldots\beta_{n}\mapsto w\circ\overline{\beta}\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}^{\ast}

∎

4.5.
Singular decomposition

Any function
f\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
has a log-Laurent expansion at
z=0
whose coefficients have log-Laurent expansions at
{\overline{z}}=0
, see (
24
). In general, with every order in

z
the pole order in
{\overline{z}}
increases. It is also possible to first expand
f
in
{\overline{z}}
and the coefficients in
z
. In this case one may get increasingly negative orders in
z
.
For GSVHs we are interested in functions where both expansions are equal. This is equivalent to the existence of a global limit for pole orders in
z
and
{\overline{z}}
.

Definition 28
.

A function
f
admits a (simultaneous) log-Laurent expansion at
z={\overline{z}}=0
if

(52)

f(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{\ell,{\overline{\ell}}=0}^{L}\sum_{m,{\overline{m}}=M}^{\infty}c_{\ell,{\overline{\ell}},m,{\overline{m}}}(\log z)^{\ell}(\log{\overline{z}})^{\overline{\ell}}z^{m}{\overline{z}}^{\overline{m}},\quad 0\leq L,M\in{\mathbb{Z}},\;c_{\ell,{\overline{\ell}},m,{\overline{m}}}\in{\mathbb{C}},

in a neighborhood of
z={\overline{z}}=0
. The
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra of functions with log-Laurent expansions at
0
is
\mathcal{R}^{0}
. If
L=M=0
we say that
f
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic at 0, see Definition
35
.

One may consider
\mathcal{R}^{0}
as space of regular functions at 0.
Note that the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{0}
does not depend on the sheets on which the logarithms are evaluated. By (
24
) we get
\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}(z)\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}({\overline{z}})\subset\mathcal{R}^{0}
.

Example 29
.

The function
\log(z-{\overline{z}})=L_{\overline{z}}(z)+L_{0}({\overline{z}})\pm\mathrm{i}\pi
is not in
\mathcal{R}^{0}
.

In this section we derive a unique decomposition of a function
f\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\Sigma}
into a regular and a singular part such that
f
is regular at 0 if and only if its singular part vanishes.
We need the decomposition to prove Theorem
52
which, in turn, is essential to prove the main theorem for GSVHs, Theorem
74
.

Lemma 30
.

Let
w({\overline{z}})
be a word with letters in
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
and
\delta_{0}^{\mathrm{l}}w({\overline{z}})=0
. Then (the empty sum is zero)

(53)

L_{0^{\{L\}}w({\overline{z}})}(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\Big(g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})\log^{\ell}(z)+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{w=u\beta v}{\beta\neq 0=\beta(0)}}f_{\ell}^{\beta v}({\overline{z}})L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})v({\overline{z}})}(z)\Big),

where
u,v\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}^{\ast}
and
\beta\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
. The functions
g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
are
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic at
0
with
g_{\ell}(0,{\overline{z}})=0
.
The functions
f_{\ell}^{\beta v}({\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
are anti-holomorphic at
{\overline{z}}=0
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction over the weight
n
of
w({\overline{z}})
. The case
n=0
is trivial by (
12
).

Assume that
n\geq 1
and
w({\overline{z}})=x({\overline{z}})\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})
with
x\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}^{\ast}
and
\beta_{n}\in\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}
. By induction we obtain

(54)

\partial_{z}L_{0^{\{L\}}x({\overline{z}})\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})}(z)=\frac{1}{z-\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})}\Big(\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})\log^{\ell}(z)+\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x=u\beta v}{\beta\neq 0=\beta(0)}}f_{\ell}^{\beta v}({\overline{z}})L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})v({\overline{z}})}(z)\Big).

If
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})=0
we use integration by parts
\ell+1
times in the first term of the sum over
\ell
, iteratively integrating
g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})/z

(which is
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic at
0
because
g_{\ell}(0,{\overline{z}})=0
) and differentiating
\log^{\ell}(z)
. With (
3.5
) we obtain a set of new functions

\tilde{g}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
with
\tilde{g}_{\ell}(0,{\overline{z}})=0
.
Integration of the second term merely adds a letter 0 to
v({\overline{z}})
. The integration constant is zero because both sides of (
53
) vanish at
z=0

(and any value of
{\overline{z}}
in the neighborhood of 0). This gives the result for
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})=0
.

If
\beta_{n}(0)\neq 0
then
1/(z-\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}))
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic at
0
and the result follows like in the case
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})=0
.

We are left with the case
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})\neq 0=\beta_{n}(0)
which implies
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})=a{\overline{z}}/(b{\overline{z}}+1)
for some constants
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
a\neq 0
. In (
54
) we write

(55)

g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})=[g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})-g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})]+g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}}).

Because
g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic,
g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})
is anti-holomorphic at
{\overline{z}}=0
. By
g_{\ell}\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
we have

g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})=\sum_{v,w({\overline{z}})}c_{v,w({\overline{z}})}L_{v}({\overline{z}})L_{w({\overline{z}})}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})),\quad c_{v,w({\overline{z}})}\in{\mathbb{C}}.

With Brown’s algorithm and Lemma
15
(1) we obtain
L_{w({\overline{z}})}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}))\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
.
Therefore
g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
. This implies
g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})-g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

By the theory of complex functions (or by explicit calculation) we see that

g_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})-g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})=[z-\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})]h_{\ell}(z,{\overline{z}})

with functions
h_{\ell}
which are
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic at
0
. We follow the argument of the case
\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})=0
to see that integrating the first term of (
55
)
gives rise to a set of new functions
\tilde{g}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
with
\tilde{g}_{\ell}(0,{\overline{z}})=0
.
Integrating the second term of (
55
) gives
\ell!g_{\ell}(\beta_{n}({\overline{z}}),{\overline{z}})L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta_{n}({\overline{z}})}(z)
which is the term
v=e
in the second sum of (
53
).

The terms

are trivially obtained from integrating the second sum in (
54
).
∎

Let
\mathcal{R}^{0}({\overline{z}})
be the space of anti-analytic functions in
\mathcal{R}^{0}
. Complementary to Definition
28
we define singular functions at 0.

Definition 31
.

For any finite set

we define the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}
of singular functions at 0 as the

has a unique representation as

(56)

f(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\beta v}{\beta\neq 0=\beta(0)}}f_{\ell}^{\beta v}({\overline{z}})L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})v({\overline{z}})}(z),\quad\text{with }L\geq 0,\;\beta\in\Sigma,\;v\in\Sigma^{\ast},\;f_{\ell}^{\beta v}({\overline{z}})\in\mathcal{R}^{0}({\overline{z}}).

We consider the
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})

\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}

Note that a function

The module
\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}

(because
\partial_{z}\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\subset\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})

whereas

\partial_{z}\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}\ni\partial_{z}L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})}(z)=L_{0^{\{\ell\}}}(z)/(z-\beta({\overline{z}}))\notin\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}
.

Proposition 32
.

For any finite set
\Sigma({\overline{z}})\subset\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{F}}({\overline{z}})
we have

(57)

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}=\{0\}.

Proof.

Consider
f(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{w}f_{w}(z,{\overline{z}})L_{w({\overline{z}})}(z)\in\mathcal{X}:=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
, where
w({\overline{z}})=0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})v({\overline{z}})

with
\beta({\overline{z}})\neq 0=\beta(0)
(by
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}
) and
f_{w}\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
(by
f\in\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
).
We assume that
0\neq f\in\mathcal{X}
is a minimal counter-example with respect to ‘
\prec
’ in Definition
2
for the weight
|w({\overline{z}})|
.

We consider
\partial_{z}f
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}f
which are in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}
. The differential operators act on
f_{w}(z,{\overline{z}})
and on
L_{w({\overline{z}})}(z)
.
In the latter case we get from
I8
a sum of hyperlogarithms with one letter of
w({\overline{z}})
removed times factors in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})
.
By Lemma
30
the hyperlogarithms split into terms in
\mathcal{R}^{0}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
and terms in
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.
We subtract all terms of the first type from
\partial_{z}f
(or
\partial_{\overline{z}}f
) to obtain an expression in
\mathcal{R}^{0}\cap\mathcal{G}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
which also is in
\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}
.
The expression is in
\mathcal{X}
and it has the coefficients
\partial_{z}f_{w}
(or
\partial_{\overline{z}}f_{w}
) in the highest weight part.

For any polynomial
0\neq P\in{\mathbb{C}}[z,{\overline{z}}]
we get
0\neq Pf\in\mathcal{X}
.
From a given function in
\mathcal{X}
we can hence construct new functions in
\mathcal{X}
where the coefficients of highest weight are multiplied with polynomials and repeatedly differentiated
with
\partial_{z}
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}
. From (the proof of) Theorem
24
(3) we construct a differential operator
D
with
Df_{v}(z,{\overline{z}})=1
for a fixed

v({\overline{z}})
of maximum weight. We may hence assume
f_{v}=1
without restriction.

By induction, the differential operators
\partial_{z}
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}
map
f
to polynomials in
\log(z)
with coefficients in
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{H}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}({\overline{z}})
,
see (
53
). Integrating
\partial_{z}f
provides hyperlogarithms with words
0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})
. Because
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{S}^{0}_{\Sigma}
we get

f(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\beta(\zz)\in\Sigma(\zz)}{\beta\neq 0=\beta(0)}}f_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta}(z,{\overline{z}})L_{0^{\{\ell\}}\beta({\overline{z}})}(z).

Because
\partial_{z}f
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}f
are polynomials in
\log(z)
we get
f_{0^{\{L\}}\beta}(z,{\overline{z}})=:f_{\beta}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. We assume that
L
is minimal, i.e. at least one

f_{\beta}\neq 0
.

We multiply
f\in\mathcal{O}_{\Sigma}(z,{\overline{z}})\mathcal{R}^{0}
with the common denominator
Q(z,{\overline{z}})=\sum_{k+{\overline{k}}\leq d}c_{k,{\overline{k}}}z^{k}{\overline{z}}^{\overline{k}}
,

, so that
Qf\in\mathcal{R}^{0}
.
Using (
17

in the log-Laurent expansion of

,

c_{L}(z,{\overline{z}})=\frac{Q(z,{\overline{z}})}{L!}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{\beta(\zz)\in\Sigma(\zz)}{\beta\neq 0=\beta(0)}}f_{\beta}L_{\beta({\overline{z}})}(z).

The FLTs
\beta({\overline{z}})
are of the form
a{\overline{z}}/(b{\overline{z}}+1)
with
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}
,
a\neq 0
. We expand

and
L_{\beta({\overline{z}})}(z)=\log(1-z(b{\overline{z}}+1)/a{\overline{z}})
first in
z
and then in
{\overline{z}}
to obtain

c_{L}(z,{\overline{z}})=-\frac{1}{L!}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k,\kk\geq 0}{k+\kk\leq d}}\sum_{k_{1}=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}\sum_{\beta({\overline{z}})=\frac{a{\overline{z}}}{b{\overline{z}}+1}\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})}c_{k,{\overline{k}}}f_{\beta}z^{k+k_{1}}{\overline{z}}^{{\overline{k}}-k_{1}+i}\frac{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{k_1}{i}b^{i}}{k_{1}a^{k_{1}}}.

Large values of

give poles in
{\overline{z}}
. We read off the

coefficient of

,

(58)

c_{L,m,{\overline{m}}-m}=-\frac{1}{L!}\sum_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k,\kk\geq 0}{k+\kk\leq\min\{d,\mm\}}}\sum_{\beta({\overline{z}})=\frac{a{\overline{z}}}{b{\overline{z}}+1}\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})}c_{k,{\overline{k}}}f_{\beta}\frac{\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m-k}{\mm-k-\kk}b^{{\overline{m}}-k-{\overline{k}}}}{(m-k)a^{m-k}},\quad\text{if }m>{\overline{m}}.

Because
Qf\in\mathcal{R}^{0}
we get
c_{L,m,{\overline{m}}-m}=0
for fixed
{\overline{m}}
and sufficiently large
m
. This leads to a system of linear equations for
c_{k,{\overline{k}}}f_{\beta}
.
With an infinite number of values for
m
we may consider
m
as a variable (by interpolation). For
k+{\overline{k}}={\overline{m}}
the summand has poles

.
For all other values of

the summands are holomorphic in
m
. By linear independence every pole term has to vanish separately. If
{\overline{m}}\leq d
,

\sum_{\beta({\overline{z}})=\frac{a{\overline{z}}}{b{\overline{z}}+1}\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})}c_{k,{\overline{m}}-k}f_{\beta}\frac{1}{(m-k)a^{m-k}}\quad\text{for all }k=0,\ldots,{\overline{m}}.

5.
Single-valued functions

In the previous section we defined functions which may have one-dimensional singular loci in
{\mathbb{C}}
(such as
\log(z-{\overline{z}})
).
For single-valuedness we now consider a finite set
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{C}}
of point-like singularities.

In the context of pQFT, single-valuedness is a fundamental property of all graphical functions (
[
17
]
with a proof in
[
14
]
).
Graphical functions have single-valued log-Laurent expansions at their singular points
0
,
1
, and

(conjectured in
[

,
17
]
with a partial proof in
[
1
]

Definition 35
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite set of points in
{\mathbb{C}}
. A function
f
on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma
has a single-valued log-Laurent expansion at
a\in\Sigma
if

(59)

f(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{a}}\sum_{m,{\overline{m}}=M_{a}}^{\infty}c_{\ell,m,{\overline{m}}}^{a}[\log(z-a)({\overline{z}}-{\overline{a}})]^{\ell}(z-a)^{m}({\overline{z}}-{\overline{a}})^{\overline{m}}

in some neighborhood of
a
. A function
f
has a single-valued log-Laurent expansion at
\infty
if

(60)

f(z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{\infty}}\sum_{m,{\overline{m}}=-\infty}^{M_{\infty}}c_{\ell,m,{\overline{m}}}^{\infty}[\log z{\overline{z}}]^{\ell}z^{m}{\overline{z}}^{\overline{m}}

in some neighborhood of
\infty
. We say that
f
is
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic (i.e.
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued real-analytic) at
a\in{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
if
L_{a}=M_{a}=0
.

The space of
{\mathbb{C}}
-analytic functions on
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma
with single-valued log-Laurent expansions at
\Sigma\cup\{\infty\}
is
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma}
. We also define

\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}=\bigcup_{\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{C}}}\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma}.

Remark 36
.

(1)

If
f
has a single-valued log-Laurent expansion at
a
then
f
has trivial monodromy at
a
.

(2)

Because single-valuedness is defined locally it is clear that
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma}
is a bi-differential
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra.

(3)

In general, one expects that every
f\in\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma}
has a primitive in
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\Sigma}
. Because integration has a non-local character, it is not easy to construct these
primitives in general.

(4)

The space
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
is invariant under complex conjugation,
\overline{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

(5)

The space
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
is invariant under linear transformations
z\mapsto az+b
for
a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}
and under the inversion
z\mapsto 1/z
. These transformations generate
\mathrm{FLT}\,_{\mathbb{C}}
;
for any

and

we have

.

7.
Single-valued integration

The commutative hexagon in Figure
1
establishes a bootstrap algorithm for the construction of GSVHs.
The crucial step is the single-valued integration in
\partial_{z}\partial_{\overline{z}}\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{F}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on the bottom right sector.
After using integration by parts there is always a weight-drop in this integration. The subtraction of residues with
\pi_{\partial_{z}}
needs to be compensated
by adding single-valued logarithms. With the strategy of Examples
69
and
70
we get the following inductive formulae for the single-valued integral
of a function
f\in\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}_{\Sigma}^{\mathbb{C}}
,
\Sigma({\overline{z}})\subset\mathrm{FLT}\,({\overline{z}})
, in the
z,{\overline{z}}
basis of (
49
) (in terms of the multivalued integrals

\int_{0}\mathrm{d}z
and
\int_{0}\mathrm{d}{\overline{z}}
, see (
3.5
)),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{sv}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}\pi_{\partial_{z}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z+\int_{0}\Big(\pi_{\partial_{\overline{z}}}\int_{\mathrm{sv}}\partial_{\overline{z}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\Big)\Big|_{z=0}\mathrm{d}{\overline{z}}+\sum_{a\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}}(\mathrm{res}\,_{a}f)\mathcal{L}_{a}(z),

(83)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{sv}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}{\overline{z}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}\Big(\pi_{\partial_{\overline{z}}}f(z)\Big)\Big|_{z=0}\mathrm{d}{\overline{z}}+\int_{0}\pi_{\partial_{z}}\int_{\mathrm{sv}}\partial_{z}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}{\overline{z}}\,\mathrm{d}z+\sum_{a\in\Sigma({\overline{z}})\cap{\mathbb{F}}}(\overline{\mathrm{res}\,}_{a}f)\mathcal{L}_{a}(z),

where we consider
z
and
{\overline{z}}
as independent variables. Both integrations in (
7
) preserve the
z,{\overline{z}}
basis.

7.1.
Extension of GSVHs

Equation (
7
) was used for first calculations with GSVHs in a predecessor of
[
19
]
. Because the algorithm was programmed in Maple which is not
ideal for handling large expressions it turned out that the following single-valued representation is more efficient.

We use (

in Example
