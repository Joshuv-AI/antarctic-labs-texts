\Gamma_{1}^{\rightarrow}(x)
) denote the set of vertices adjacent to (resp. from) the vertex
x
. In another words,

\Gamma_{1}^{\rightarrow}(x)=\{z\mid(x,z)\in{\cal E}(\Gamma)\}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(x)=\{z\mid(z,x)\in{\cal E}(\Gamma)\}.

Two small comments about the above notation: (i) drawing directed edge from
x
to
z
, we have
x\rightarrow z
, which yields the idea of using the notation
\Gamma_{1}^{\rightarrow}(x)
; (ii) drawing directed edge from
z
to
x
, we have
x\leftarrow z
(or
z\rightarrow x
), which yields the idea of using the notation
\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(x)
. The elements of
\Gamma_{1}^{\rightarrow}(x)
are called
neighbors
of
x
. Instead of a set of vertices, we can consider a set of arcs: for a vertex
y
, let
\delta_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)
(resp.
\delta_{1}^{\rightarrow}(y)
) denote the set of arcs adjacent to (resp. from) the vertex
y
. The number
|\delta_{1}^{\rightarrow}(y)|
is called the
out-degree of
y
and is equal to the number of edges leaving
y
. The number
|\delta_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)|
is called the
in-degree of
y
and is equal to the number of edges going to
y
. A digraph
\Gamma
is
k
-regular
(of valency
k
) if
|\delta^{\rightarrow}_{1}(y)|=|\delta_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)|=k
for all
y\in X
. We call
\Gamma

simple
, if
\Gamma
contains neither loops nor multiple edges. In this paper we will always assume that our digraph is simple and strongly connected.

Let
\Gamma=(X,{\cal E})
denote a digraph. For any two vertices
x,y\in X
, a
directed walk
of length
h
from
x
to
y
is a sequence
[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{h}]

(x_{i}\in X,\,0\leq i\leq h)
such that
x_{0}=x
,
x_{h}=y
, and
x_{i}
is adjacent to
x_{i+1}
(i.e.
x_{i+1}\in\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{1}(x_{i})
) for
0\leq i\leq h-1
. We say that
\Gamma
is
strongly connected
if for any
x,y\in X
there is a directed walk from
x
to
y
. A
closed directed walk
is a directed walk from a vertex to itself. A
directed path
is a directed walk such that all vertices of the directed walk are distinct. A
cycle
is a closed directed path. The
girth
of
\Gamma
is the length of a shortest cycle in
\Gamma
.

For any
x,y\in X
, the
distance
from
x
to
y
(or between
x
and
y
), denoted by
\partial(x,y)
, is the length of a shortest directed path from
x
to
y
. The
diameter

D=D(\Gamma)
of a strongly connected digraph
\Gamma
is defined to be

D=\max\{\partial(y,z)\,|\,y,z\in X\}.

For a vertex
x\in X
and any non-negative integer
i
not exceeding
D
, let
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
(or
\Gamma_{i}(x)
) denote the subset of vertices in
X
that are at distance
i
from
x
, i.e.,

\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)=\{z\in X\mid\partial(x,z)=i\}.

We also define the set
\Gamma^{\leftarrow}_{i}(x)
as
\Gamma^{\leftarrow}_{i}(x)=\{z\in X\mid\partial(z,x)=i\}
. Let
\Gamma_{-1}(x)=\Gamma_{D+1}(x):=\emptyset
. The
eccentricity
of
x
, denoted by
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(x)
, is the maximum distance between
x
and any other vertex of
\Gamma
. Note that the diameter of
\Gamma
equals
\max\{\varepsilon(x)\mid x\in X\}
.

All undirected graphs in this paper can be understood as digraphs in which an undirected edge between two vertices
x
and
y
represents two arcs, an arc from
x
to
y
, and an arc from
y
to
x
. In diagrams instead of drawing two arcs we draw one undirected edge between vertices
x
and
y
. For a basic introduction to the theory of undirected graphs we refer to
[
20
, Section 2]
. With the word
graph
we refer to a finite simple directed or undirected graph.

2.1
Equitable and distance-faithful partition

A
partition
of a digraph
\Gamma
is a collection
\{{\mathcal{P}}_{0},{\mathcal{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{P}}_{s}\}
of nonempty subsets of the vertex set
X
, such that
X=\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=0}^{s}{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{j}=\emptyset
for all
i,j

(0\leq i,j\leq s,\,i\neq j)
. An
equitable partition
of a digraph
\Gamma
is a partition
\{{\mathcal{P}}_{0},{\mathcal{P}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{P}}_{s}\}
of its vertex set, such that for all integers
i,j

(0\leq i,j\leq s)
the following two conditions hold.

(i)

The number
d^{\rightarrow}_{ij}
of neighbors which a vertex in the cell
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
has in the cell
{\mathcal{P}}_{j}
is independent of the choice of the vertex in
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
(i.e., for every
y\in{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
we have
|\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{1}(y)\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{j}|=d^{\rightarrow}_{ij}
).

(ii)

The number
d^{\leftarrow}_{ij}
of vertices from the cell
{\mathcal{P}}_{j}
which are adjacent to a vertex in
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
is independent of the choice of the vertex in
{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
(i.e., for every
y\in{\mathcal{P}}_{i}
we have
|\sum_{z\in{\mathcal{P}}_{j}}|\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{1}(z)\cap\{y\}|=d^{\leftarrow}_{ij}=|\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{j}|
).

We call the numbers
d^{\rightarrow}_{ij}
,
d^{\leftarrow}_{ij}

(0\leq i,j\leq s)
the
corresponding parameters
.

A
distance partition around

x
of a digraph
\Gamma
with vertex set
X
is a partition
\{\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{0}(x)=\{x\},\Gamma^{\rightarrow}(x),\ldots,\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{\varepsilon(x)}(x)\}
of
X
where
\varepsilon(x)
is eccentricity of
x
. An
x
-distance-faithful partition

\{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=\{x\},{\mathcal{P}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathcal{P}}_{s}\}
(with
s\geq\varepsilon
) is a refinement of the distance partition around
x
(here refinement means that some of
\Gamma_{i}(x)
can be equal to a union of some
{\mathcal{P}}_{h}
’s).

The
distance-
i
diagram
of an equitable partition
\Pi=\{\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{0}(x)=\{x\},\Gamma^{\rightarrow}(x),\ldots,\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{\varepsilon(x)}(x)\}
(around
x
) of a graph
\Gamma
is a collection of circles indexed by the sets of
\Pi
with directed edges between some of them. If there is no directed edge between
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
and
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)
, then it means that there is no directed edge
yz
for any
y\in\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
and
z\in\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)
. If there is a directed edge between
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
and
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)
, then a number on the line (from
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
to
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)
) near the circle
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
denotes the corresponding parameter
d^{\rightarrow}_{ij}
. A number above or below a circle
\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{i}(x)
denotes the corresponding parameter
d^{\rightarrow}_{ii}(=d^{\leftarrow}_{ii})
. A similar explanation holds for the corresponding parameter
d^{\leftarrow}_{ij}
(see Figures
1
for an example).

We say that the combinatorial structure of the distance-
i
diagram is
the same
around every vertex if for every vertex
x
there exists an
x
-distance-faithful equitable partition with
D
cells of same cardinalities and (same) corresponding parameters do not depend on the choice of
x
.

2.2
Elementary algebraic graph theory and the standard basis

In this section, we recall some definitions and basic concepts from algebraic graph theory.

The
adjacency matrix

A\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
of a digraph
\Gamma
(with vertex set
X
) is indexed by the vertices from
X
, and is defined in the following way

\mbox{$(A)_{yz}=$ the number of arcs from $y$ to $z$}\qquad(y,z\in X)

(note that
(A)_{yz}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{+}_{0}
). Moreover, if we allow loops, the diagonal entries of
A
can be different from zero. If
\Gamma
is simple graph (i.e., if the adjacency matrix
A
is a
01
-matrix), then the
yz
-entry of the power
A^{\ell}

(\ell\in{\mathbb{N}})
corresponds to the number of
\ell
-walks from the vertex
y
to the vertex
z
in
\Gamma
(see Lemma
2.1
). As we already mention, in this paper we will always work with simple and strongly connected graphs.

Lemma 2.1

Let
\Gamma
denote a simple strongly connected digraph with vertex set
X
, diameter
D
and adjacency matrix
A
. The number of walks of length
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
in
\Gamma
from
x
to
y
is equal to
(x,y)
-entry of the matrix
A^{\ell}
.

Proof.

Routine.

The distance-
i
matrix
A_{i}
of a digraph
\Gamma
with diameter
D
and vertex set
X
is defined by

(A_{i})_{zy}=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&\mbox{ if $\partial(z,y)=i$},\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise.~~~~}\end{matrix}\right.\qquad(z,y\in X,~0\leq i\leq D).

In particular,
A_{0}=I
and
A_{1}=A
. These matrices play a key role in the study of distance-regularity. A matrix
A\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
is said to be a
reducible
when there exists a permutation matrix
P
such that
P^{\top}AP=\left(\begin{matrix}X&Y\\
{\boldsymbol{O}}&Z\end{matrix}\right)
, where
X
and
Z
are both square, and
{\boldsymbol{O}}
is a zero matrix. Otherwise,
A
is said to be
irreducible
.

Lemma 2.2
(see, for example,
[
40
, Section 8.3]
)

A digraph
\Gamma
with adjacency matrix
A
is strongly connected if and only if
A
is an irreducible matrix.

Theorem 2.3
(Perron–Frobenius Theorem)

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
denote a simple strongly connected digraph with spectrum
\spec(\Gamma)
. If
\theta=\max_{\lambda\in\spec(\Gamma)}|\lambda|
, then the following hold.

(i)

\theta\in\spec(\Gamma)
.

(ii)

The algebraic multiplicity of
\theta
is equal to
1
.

(iii)

There exists an eigenvector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
with all positive entries, such that
A{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\theta{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
.

Sometimes it is useful to normalize a vector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
from
(iii)
in such a way that the smallest entry is equal to
1
. Such a vector
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is called a Perron–Frobenius eigenvector.

Proof.

Routine using Lemma
2.2
. (See, for example,
[
40
, Section 8.3]
.)

A matrix
A\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
is called
normal
if it commutes with its adjoint, i.e. if
A\overline{A}^{\top}=\overline{A}^{\top}A
.

Theorem 2.4
(see, for example,
[
2
, Chapter 7]
)

Let
A\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
denote a matrix over
{\mathbb{C}}
, with rows and columns indexed by
X
. Then, the following are equivalent.

(i)

A
is normal.

(ii)

{\mathbb{C}}^{|X|}
has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of
A
.

(iii)

A
is a diagonalizable matrix.

(iv)

The algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda
is equal to the geometric multiplicity of
\lambda
, for every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
.

Two matrices
A,B\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
are said to be
simultaneously diagonalizable
if there is a nonsingular
S\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
such that
S^{-1}AS
and
S^{-1}BS
are both diagonal.

Lemma 2.5
(
[
28
, Theorem 1.3.12]
)

Two diagonalizable matrices are simultaneously diagonalizable if and only if they commute.

Theorem 2.6

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
denote a space of commutative normal matrices. Then, there exists a unitary matrix
U\in\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
which diagonalizes
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

Immediate from Theorem
2.4
, Lemma
2.5
and
[
28
, Subsection 1.3]
.

Let
\circ
denote the elementwise–Hadamard product of matrices. We call two
01
-matrices
B
,
C

disjoint
if
B\circ C={\boldsymbol{O}}
. A basis
\{F_{0},F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}\}
of some subalgebra
{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mbox{\rm Mat}_{X}({\mathbb{C}})
satisfying all the following properties is known as the
standard basis
of
{\mathcal{C}}
: (i)
{\cal F}=\{F_{0},F_{1}(=F),\ldots,F_{d}\}
is a set of mutually disjoint
(0,1)
-matrices; (ii) the sum of some (respectively all) of these matrices gives
I
(respectively
J
); (iii) for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
, the transpose of
F_{i}
belongs to
{\cal F}
; (iv) the vector space spanned by
{\cal F}
is closed under both ordinary and Hadamard multiplication; and (v) each
F_{i}
is a polynomial (not necessarily of degree
i
) in
F
.

Theorem 2.7
(
[
27
, Theorem 1]
)

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
denote a digraph with adjacency matrix
A
and vertex set
X
. Let
\jj
denote the all-ones vector of order
|X|
and
J
all-ones matrix of order
|X|
(i.e., the matrix every column of which is
\jj
). Then, the following hold.

(i)

There exists a polynomial
h(t)
such that
J=h(A)
if and only if
\Gamma
is strongly connected and
A\jj=A^{\top}\jj=k\jj
, for some positive integer
k
.

(ii)

The unique polynomial of least degree satisfying
J=h(A)
is
|X|s(t)/s(k)
, where
(t-k)s(t)
is the minimal polynomial of
A
and
k
is the positive integer such that
A\jj=k\jj
.

Lemma 2.8

Let
A
denote a complex matrix. Then,
A
is normal if and only if
\overline{A}^{\top}=p(A)
for some polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[t]
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Assume that
\overline{A}^{\top}=p(A)
for some polynomial
p\in{\mathbb{C}}[t]
. Then,
A\overline{A}^{\top}=Ap(A)=p(A)A=\overline{A}^{\top}A
, and thus
A
is a normal matrix.

(\Rightarrow)
Assume that
A
is a normal matrix, and let
\spec(A)=\{[\lambda_{0}]^{m(\lambda_{0})},\ldots,[\lambda_{d}]^{m(\lambda_{d})}\}
denote the spectrum of
A
. For each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}

(0\leq i\leq d)
let
U_{i}
denote the matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the eigenspace
\ker(A-\lambda_{i}I)
, and note that
\dim(\ker(A-\lambda_{i}I))=m(\lambda_{i})
. Abbreviate
m_{i}=m(\lambda_{i})

(0\leq i\leq d)
. Pick
i

(0\leq i\leq d)
, and note that

AU_{i}=\lambda_{i}U_{i}.

If
U=[U_{1}|U_{2}|...|U_{d}]
, then

A=U\Lambda\overline{U}^{\top},\qquad\mbox{ where }\qquad\Lambda=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{0}I_{m_{0}}&0&...&0\\
0&\lambda_{1}I_{m_{1}}&...&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&~&\vdots\\
0&0&...&\lambda_{d}I_{m_{d}}\end{matrix}\right].

Now, it is routine to see that

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=U\Lambda\overline{U}^{\top}=[U_{0}|U_{1}|\ldots|U_{d}]\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{0}I_{m_{0}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\lambda_{1}I_{m_{1}}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&\lambda_{d}I_{m_{d}}\\
\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}\underline{\overline{U_{0}}^{\top}}\\
\underline{\overline{U_{1}}^{\top}}\\
\underline{\,\,\,\vdots\,\,\,}\\
\overline{U_{d}}^{\top}\\
\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=[\lambda_{0}U_{0}|\lambda_{1}U_{1}|\cdots|\lambda_{d}U_{d}]\left[\begin{matrix}\underline{\overline{U_{0}}^{\top}}\\
\underline{\overline{U_{1}}^{\top}}\\
\underline{\,\,\,\vdots\,\,\,}\\
\overline{U_{d}}^{\top}\\
\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}U_{0}\overline{U_{0}}^{\top}+\lambda_{1}U_{1}\overline{U_{1}}^{\top}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}U_{d}\overline{U_{d}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}E_{0}+\lambda_{1}E_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}E_{d}\qquad(\mbox{where }E_{i}:=U_{i}\overline{U_{i}}^{\top}).

Since
\overline{E_{i}}^{\top}=E_{i}
, it follows that
\overline{A}^{\top}=\overline{\lambda_{0}}E_{0}+\overline{\lambda_{1}}E_{1}+\cdots+\overline{\lambda_{d}}E_{d}
. Note that
\{E_{0},E_{1},\ldots,E_{d}\}
is a basis of
{\mathcal{A}}=\Span\{A^{0},A^{1},\ldots,A^{d}\}
. The result follows.

2.3
Commutative association schemes

In Section
1
, we have already provided the definition of a commutative
d
-class association scheme
{\mathfrak{X}}=\{X,\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}\}
together with definitions of relations
\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}
, relation matrices
\{B_{i}\}_{i=0}^{d}
and Bose–Mesner algebra
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathfrak{X}}
. The meaning of a “matrix generates
{\mathfrak{X}}
” has been given in Section

as well. Note that relation matrices

form a standard basis of the Bose-Mesner algebra

if every element of the Bose–Mesner algebra

of

can be writen as a polynomial in

is adjacency matrix of the (di)graph

is

with respect to

and there exists an ordering
(B_{0},B_{1},\ldots,B_{d})
and polynomials

of degree

(0\leq j\leq d)
. In Section

, we show that if

is a

-polynomial ordering of

then

is a distance-regular (di)graph (see Proposition

). Recall that the association scheme

is

(with respect to

) if it is generated by a relation

for some

(we recommend articles
[
41
,
56
]
for interesting results in that direction).

Proposition 2.9

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
denote a digraph with adjacency matrix
A
and vertex set
X
. Assume that
A
generates the Bose–Mesner algebra
{\mathcal{M}}
of a commutative
d
-class association scheme, and let
\{B_{0},B_{1},\ldots,B_{d}\}
denote the standard basis of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Then, the following hold.

(i)

For any
i

(0\leq i\leq d)
and

, if
(B_{i})_{zy}=(B_{i})_{uv}=1
then
\partial(z,y)=\partial(u,v)
.

(ii)

Every distance-
i
matrix
A_{i}
of
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
belongs to
{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,
A_{i}\in{\mathcal{M}}

(0\leq i\leq D)
.

Proof.

Since
A
generates the Bose–Mesner algebra
{\mathcal{M}}
, and
J\in{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a polynomial
p(t)
such that
J=p(A)
. This implies that
\Gamma
is regular and strongly connected (see Theorem
2.7
).

(i) For every
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists complex scalars
\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i}

(0\leq i\leq d)
such that
A^{\ell}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i}B_{i}
. Recall that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}B_{i}=J
and
B_{i}\circ B_{j}=\delta_{ij}B_{i}

(0\leq i,j\leq d)
. This yields that, for any

and
i

(0\leq i\leq d)
, if
(B_{i})_{zy}\neq 0
and
(B_{i})_{uv}\neq 0
then
(A^{\ell})_{zy}=(A^{\ell})_{uv}=\alpha^{(\ell)}_{i}
, i.e., the number of walks of length

from

to

is equal to the the number of walks of length
\ell
from

to

(see Lemma

). Moreover,
(A^{\ell})_{zy}=(A^{\ell})_{uv}
holds for any
\ell

(\ell\in{\mathbb{N}})
. To prove the claim, we use the proof by a contradiction, similar as in
[
16
, Lemma 2.3]
where the author has an undirected graph. Assume that
\partial(z,y)>\partial(u,v)=m
. Then,
(A^{m})_{uv}\neq 0
and
(A^{m})_{zy}=0
, a contradiction.
The result follows.

(ii) From the proof of (i) above it follows that, if
y,z\in X
are two arbitrary vertices such that
\partial(z,y)=i
, then there exists
B_{j}
(for some
0\leq j\leq d
) such that
(B_{j})_{zy}=1
. Recall also that
(A_{i})_{zy}=1
. In fact, for such a choice of

and any nonzero

-entry of

, we have
\partial(u,v)=i
. This yields

A_{i}=\sum_{j:A_{i}\circ B_{j}\neq{\boldsymbol{O}}}B_{j}\qquad(0\leq i\leq D).

The result follows.

d^{\rightarrow}_{ij}:=|\Gamma_{1}^{\rightarrow}(y)\cap\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)|\quad\mbox{ and }\quad d^{\leftarrow}_{ij}:=|\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)\cap\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{j}(x)|\qquad(0\leq i,j\leq D)

depend on the index

to

and

Lemma 4.5

With reference to
Notation
2.10
, let
\Gamma
denote a distance-regular digraph. Pick
i

(0\leq i\leq D)
,
x\in X
,

, and let

. Then, for every

,
\partial(z,x)=h
.

Proof.

We use mathematical induction on
h
.

Basis of induction.
Case

yields
i=0
, and the result follows. Note that
h\neq 0
implies
i\neq 0
. Let
h=1
, i.e., pick
y\in\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)
and assume that
\partial(y,x)=1
. Since the distance-
i
partition is equitable, every vertex from
\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)
has exactly

neighbours in
\Gamma_{0}^{\rightarrow}(x)=\{x\}
. To be more precise,
d_{i0}^{\rightarrow}=|\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{1}(z)\cap\Gamma_{0}^{\rightarrow}(x)|=1
for any

. The result follows.

Step of induction.
Assume now that, for any

(

, where

), the following claim holds:

if
y\in\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)
and
\partial(y,x)=h
, then for every
z\in\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)

\partial(z,x)=h
holds.

In the next few lines, we prove that our claim holds also for

y\in\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)
and
\partial(y,x)=m
. Since
\partial(y,x)=m
, there exists a shortest path
[y,w_{1},\ldots,w_{m-1},w_{m}:=x]
of length

such that

and

(1\leq j\leq m)

\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{\ell_{j}}(x)\neq\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)

(1\leq j\leq m-1)

(1\leq j\leq m)
. Note that

does not have any neighbours in

(otherwise the distance from

to

will be less than

which has a neighbour in

(otherwise

would have a neighbour in
\bigcup_{j=2}^{m}\Gamma_{\ell_{j}}

. We got that the distance from an arbitrary

Proposition 4.6

Let
\Gamma
denote a distance-regular digraph. For any

Our proof is by construction. Note that

.

Since every vertex has the same eccentricity

, there exists

, for every

,
\partial(z,x)=1
. This yields
\Gamma_{i_{1}}^{\rightarrow}(x)\subseteq\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(x)
.
Next, pick
y_{2}\in X
such that
\partial(y_{2},x)=2

y_{2}\in\Gamma_{i_{2}}^{\rightarrow}(x)

, for every

We continue in this way next

By construction, the elements of the set

The result follows.

Lemma 5.1

Let
\Gamma=\Gamma(A)
denote a distance-regular digraph, and
\{A_{0},A:=A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{D}\}
denote the distance-

(0\leq i,h\leq D)

A_{i}A=\sum_{h=0}^{D}p^{h}_{i1}A_{h}.

Proof.

Note that

(A_{i}A)_{xy}=|\Gamma_{i}^{\rightarrow}(x)\cap\Gamma_{1}^{\leftarrow}(y)|\qquad(x,y\in X).

Since
\Gamma
is a distance-regular digraph, by definition the distance-

The result follows.

Proposition 5.2

Let

Spectral excess theorem for distance-regular digraphs

In this section, we prove spectral excess theorem for distance-regular digraphs. A version of such a theorem can be also found in
[
44
, Theorem 4.6]

Lemma 13.1

With reference to
Definition
12.1
, let
\Gamma
denote a
k
-regular digraph with
d+1
distinct eigenvalues, spectrally maximum diameter
D=d
, adjacency algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, and predistance polynomials
\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{D}\}
. Then,
\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}|\Gamma_{D}^{\rightarrow}(x)|\leq p_{D}(k)
, and equality holds if and only if
A_{D}=p_{D}(A)
.

Proof.
Our proof is within the same lines as the proof of
[
17
, Lemma 1]
, where the authors deal with undirected graph.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=0}^{D}
denote distance-
i
matrices of
\Gamma
. Consider the vector space
{\cal T}={\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{D}}
, where
{\mathcal{A}}=\Span\{A^{0},A^{1},\ldots,A^{d}\}
and
{\mathcal{D}}=\Span\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{D}\}
, together with the inner product (
15
). Note that
{\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{D}}
above is the ordinary sum of the vector spaces, not the direct sum. Consider the orthogonal projection
{\cal T}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}
, onto the vector space
{\mathcal{A}}
, denoted by
S\mapsto\proj_{{\mathcal{A}}}(S)
, and note that
\{p_{i}(A)\}_{i=0}^{D}
is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{A}}
(with respect to the inner product (
15
)). Since
\sum_{i=0}^{D}A_{i}=J
,
\sum_{i=0}^{D}p_{i}(A)=J
(see Lemma
12.2
),
p_{i}
is of degree
i

(0\leq i\leq D)
and
(A^{i})_{xy}=0
if
\partial(x,y)>i
, the orthogonal projection of
A_{D}

(A_{D}\neq{\boldsymbol{O}})
is

\displaystyle\proj_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A_{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{D}\frac{\langle A_{D},p_{j}(A)\rangle}{\|p_{j}\|^{2}}p_{j}(A)=\frac{\langle A_{D},p_{D}(A)\rangle}{\|p_{D}\|^{2}}p_{D}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle A_{D},J\rangle}{\|p_{D}\|^{2}}p_{D}(A)=\frac{\|A_{D}\|^{2}}{p_{D}(k)}p_{D}(A).

(16)

Recall that
p_{D}(k)=\|p_{D}\|^{2}>0
and note

\|\proj_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A_{D})\|^{2}=\left\langle\frac{\|A_{D}\|^{2}}{p_{D}(k)}p_{D}(A),\frac{\|A_{D}\|^{2}}{p_{D}(k)}p_{D}(A)\right\rangle=\frac{\|A_{D}\|^{4}}{p_{D}(k)}.

Now consider the equality
A_{D}=\proj_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A_{D})+N
, where
N\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\bot}
. The Pythagorean theorem and (
16
) yield

0\leq\|N\|^{2}=\|A_{D}\|^{2}-\|\proj_{{\mathcal{A}}}(A_{D})\|^{2}=\|A_{D}\|^{2}\left(1-\frac{\|A_{D}\|^{2}}{{p_{D}(k)}}\right).

Now we have
\|A_{D}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{|X|}\trace(A_{D}\overline{A}^{\top}_{D})=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{z\in X}(A_{D}\circ A_{D})_{xz}=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{x\in X}|\Gamma^{\rightarrow}_{D}(x)|
, and the inequality follows. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
N={\boldsymbol{O}}
, i.e.,
A_{D}=p_{D}(A)
.

Lemma 13.2

Let
A
denote a normal matrix with spectrum
\spec(A)=\{[\lambda_{0}]^{m(\lambda_{0})},\ldots,[\lambda_{d}]^{m(\lambda_{d})}\}
. With reference to the inner product (
15
),

\langle p,q\rangle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{j=0}^{d}m(\lambda_{j})p(\lambda_{j})\overline{q(\lambda_{j})}.

Proof.

For each eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}

(0\leq i\leq d)
let
U_{i}
denote the matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the eigenspace
\ker(A-\lambda_{i}I)
, and note that
\dim(\ker(A-\lambda_{i}I))=m(\lambda_{i})
. Abbreviate
m_{i}=m(\lambda_{i})

(0\leq i\leq d)
. In the proof of Lemma
2.8
we show that

A=\lambda_{0}E_{0}+\lambda_{1}E_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}E_{d}\qquad(\mbox{where }E_{i}:=U_{i}\overline{U_{i}}^{\top}).

Since
E_{i}E_{j}=\delta_{ij}E_{i}
,
\overline{E_{i}}^{\top}=E_{i}
and
\trace(E_{i})=m_{i}

(0\leq i\leq d)
, for any polynomials
p,q\in{\mathbb{C}}_{d}[t]
we have

p(A)=p(\lambda_{0})E_{0}+\cdots+p(\lambda_{d})E_{d},\qquad q(A)=q(\lambda_{0})E_{0}+\cdots+q(\lambda_{d})E_{d}

and

\displaystyle\langle p,q\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{X}\trace(p(A)\overline{q(A)}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{X}\trace\big(p(\lambda_{0})\overline{q(\lambda_{0})}E_{0}+\cdots+p(\lambda_{d})\overline{q(\lambda_{d})}E_{d}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{j=0}^{d}m_{j}p(\lambda_{j})\overline{q(\lambda_{j})}.

The result follows.

Lemma 13.3

With reference to
Definition
12.1
, let
A
denote a normal matrix with spectrum
\spec(A)=\{[\lambda_{0}]^{m(\lambda_{0})},\ldots,[\lambda_{d}]^{m(\lambda_{d})}\}
such that
A\jj=\lambda_{0}\jj
, and let
\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}\}
denote a set of predistance polynomials. Set
\pi_{i}=\prod_{j=0(j\not=i)}^{d}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})

(0\leq i\leq d)
. Then

p_{d}(\lambda_{0})=\frac{|X|}{|\pi_{0}|^{2}(m(\lambda_{0}))^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{1}{m(\lambda_{j})|\pi_{j}|^{2}}\right)^{-1}

Proof.

Our proof use similar technique that can be fond in
[
7
]
. Define polynomials
Z_{i}(t)

(1\leq i\leq d)
of degree
d-1
in the following way

Z_{i}(t)=\prod\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j=1}}{{j\not=i}}}^{d}(t-\lambda_{j}).

Note that

Z_{i}(\lambda_{\ell})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\pi_{0}}{\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{i}},&\mbox{if }\ell=0,\\
0,&\mbox{if }\ell\neq 0\mbox{ or }\ell\neq i,\\
\frac{\pi_{i}}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{0}},&\mbox{if }\ell=i\end{array}\right.\qquad(1\leq i\leq d),

which implies

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle p_{d},Z_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}m(\lambda_{\ell})p_{d}(\lambda_{\ell})\overline{Z_{i}(\lambda_{\ell})}\qquad(\mbox{see Lemma~\ref{qd}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\left(m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0})\overline{Z_{i}(\lambda_{0})}+m(\lambda_{i})p_{d}(\lambda_{i})\overline{Z_{i}(\lambda_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\left(m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0})\frac{\overline{\pi_{0}}}{\overline{\lambda_{0}}-\overline{\lambda_{i}}}+m(\lambda_{i})p_{d}(\lambda_{i})\frac{\overline{\pi_{i}}}{\overline{\lambda_{i}}-\overline{\lambda_{0}}}\right).

The last line from above yields

m(\lambda_{i})p_{d}(\lambda_{i})\overline{\pi_{i}}=m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0})\overline{\pi_{0}}\qquad(1\leq i\leq d).

(17)

Taking conjugate of (
17
), we also have
m(\lambda_{i})\overline{p_{d}(\lambda_{i})}{\pi_{i}}=m(\lambda_{0})\overline{p_{d}(\lambda_{0})}{\pi_{0}}
.
In the end, by (
17
), we have

\displaystyle p_{d}(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\|p_{d}\|^{2}}_{>0}=\langle p_{d},p_{d}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{j=0}^{d}m(\lambda_{j})p_{d}(\lambda_{j})\overline{p_{d}(\lambda_{j})}\qquad(\mbox{see Lemma~\ref{qd}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0})\underbrace{\overline{p_{d}(\lambda_{0})}}_{=p_{d}(\lambda_{0})}+\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{j=1}^{d}m(\lambda_{j})p_{d}(\lambda_{j}){p_{d}(\lambda_{j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|X|}m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0}){p_{d}(\lambda_{0})}+\frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{j=1}^{d}m(\lambda_{j})\frac{m(\lambda_{0})p_{d}(\lambda_{0})\overline{\pi_{0}}}{m(\lambda_{j})\overline{\pi_{j}}}\frac{m(\lambda_{0})\overline{p_{d}(\lambda_{0})}{\pi_{0}}}{m(\lambda_{j}){\pi_{j}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p_{d}(\lambda_{0}))^{2}}{|X|}m(\lambda_{0})+\frac{(p_{d}(\lambda_{0}))^{2}}{|X|}|\pi_{0}|^{2}(m(\lambda_{0}))^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{m(\lambda_{j})|\pi_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p_{d}(\lambda_{0}))^{2}}{|X|}|\pi_{0}|^{2}(m(\lambda_{0}))^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{d}\frac{1}{m(\lambda_{j})|\pi_{j}|^{2}}.

The result follows.
