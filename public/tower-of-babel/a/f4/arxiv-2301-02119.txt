A tensor bidiagonalization method for higher-order singular value decomposition with applications
A. El Hachimi, K. Jbilou, A. Ratnani, L. Reichel

Abstract
The need to know a few singular triplets associated with the largest singular values of third-order tensors arises in data compression and extraction. This paper describes a new method for their computation using the t-product. Methods for determining a couple of singular triplets associated with the smallest singular values also are presented. The proposed methods generalize available restarted Lanczos bidiagonalization methods for computing a few of the largest or smallest singular triplets of a matrix. The methods of this paper use Ritz and harmonic Ritz lateral slices to determine accurate approximations of the largest and smallest singular triplets, respectively. Computed examples show applications to data compression and face recognition.



keywords

tensors, t-product, partial tensor bidiagonalization, restarted tensor bidiagonalization,
singular value decomposition, face recognition.

Introduction

The last 20 years has seen an immense growth of the amount of data that is collected for
analysis, but it is a challenging problem to extract useful information from available
data. This difficulty arises, e.g., in machine learning, data mining, and deep learning;
see, e.g., Arnold et al.
[
1
]
. The extraction of useful information from data
that is represented by a
matrix
often is facilitated by the singular value
decomposition of the matrix. Typically, only a few of the largest singular triplets, i.e.,
the largest singular values and associated right and left singular vectors, are required
to extract useful information from the matrix. A restarted Lanczos bidiagonalization
method for computing accurate approximations of these singular triplets is described in

[
5
]
, and R code written by Bryan W. Lewis is available at

[
6
]
.

In many recent applications the given data are represented by a multidimensional array.
These arrays, known as
tensors
, are natural generalizations of matrices. Several
approaches to define tensor-tensor products and tensor-matrix products are described in
the literature, including the
n
-mode product
[
9
,
25
]
, the t-product

[
22
,
31
]
, and the c-product
[
21
,
30
]
. Generalizations of
the singular value decomposition (SVD) to tensors are described in
[
25
]
using
the
n
-mode product (the so-called HOSVD), and in
[
21
,
22
]

using the tensor c-product and
t-product. The need to compute the SVD or a partial SVD of a tensor arises in a variety
of applications, including image restoration, tensor completion
[
10
]
,
robust tensor principal
component analysis
[
13
]
, tensor compression
[
3
]
, and
recognition of color faces
[
17
,
18
]
. These
applications require knowledge of the largest singular values and associated lateral
tensor singular slices.

It is the purpose of the this paper to introduce a new restarted tensor Lanczos
bidiagonalization method for third-order tensors using the t-product for approximating
a few of the largest singular values and associated lateral tensor singular slices. This
method generalizes the approach described in
[
5
]
from matrices to
tensors. We remark that the Lanczos bidiagonalization method (also known as the
Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization method) for third-order tensors using the t-product has
been described in
[
15
,
16
,
22
,
32
]
; however, this bidiagonalization method
differs from the one of the present paper.

In
[
5
]
the authors also describe a restarted Lanczos bidiagonalization method
for the computation of a few of the smallest singular values and associated singular
vectors of a large matrix by determining harmonic Ritz values is presented. This paper
presents an analogous scheme for third-order tensors.

The organization of this paper is as follows. Section
2
recalls some properties
of the t-product and Section
3
reviews tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization of
third-order tensors using the t-product. Restarted tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization
methods are presented for the approximation of a few of the largest singular values and
associated lateral tensor singular slices by computing lateral tensor Ritz slices, as well
as for approximating a few of the smallest singular values and associated lateral tensor
singular slices by evaluating harmonic lateral tensor Ritz slices. Section
4

discusses multidimensional principal component analysis using a partial tensor HOSVD with
application to face recognition, and Section
5
presents a few computed examples.
Concluding remarks and possible extensions can be found in Section
6
.

This section reviews results by Kilmer et al.

notation employed there and by Kolda and Bader
[
25
]
. A third-order
tensor is an array
\mathscr{A}=[a_{ijk}]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
. Matrices and
vectors are tensors of order two and one, respectively. A
slice
or

a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}

indices. Using MATLAB notation,
\mathscr{A}(i,:,:)
,
\mathscr{A}(:,j,:)
, and

\mathscr{A}(:,:,k)
denote the
i
th horizontal, the
j
th lateral, and the
k
th frontal
slices of
\mathscr{A}
, respectively. The lateral slice
\mathscr{A}(:,j,:)
also is
denoted by
\vec{\mathscr{A}}_{j}
, and the frontal slice
\mathscr{A}(:,:,k)
is an

\ell\times p
matrix that is sometimes denoted by
\mathscr{A}^{(k)}
. A
fiber
of a third
order tensor
\mathscr{A}
is defined by fixing any two of the three indices. The fiber

\mathscr{A}(i,j,:)
is called a
tube
of
\mathscr{A}
. We will use capital
calligraphic letters
\mathscr{A}
to denote third-order tensors, capital letters
A
to
identify matrices, bold face lower case letters
\bm{a}
to denote tubes, and lower case
letters
a
stand for scalars. Further,
\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}

denotes the space of third-order tensors of size
\ell\times p\times n
,

\mathbb{K}^{\ell}_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times 1\times n}
stands for the space of lateral slices of size

\ell\times n
, and
\mathbb{K}_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1\times n}
denotes the space of tubes with
n

entries. For a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
with frontal slices

\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, we define:

•

The block circulant matrix associated with
\mathscr{A}

{\tt bcirc}(\mathscr{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&\mathscr{A}^{(n)}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(2)}\\
\mathscr{A}^{(2)}&\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(3)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathscr{A}^{(n)}&\mathscr{A}^{(n-1)}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(1)}\\
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell n\times pn}.

(1)

•

The operator
{\tt unfold}
applied to
{\mathscr{A}}
gives the matrix made up of
its frontal slices,

{\tt unfold}(\mathscr{A})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}\\
\mathscr{A}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathscr{A}^{(n)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell n\times p}.

We also will need the inverse operator
{\tt fold}
such that

{\tt fold}\left(\tt unfold\left(\mathscr{A}\right)\right)=\mathscr{A}

•

The block diagonal matrix associated with

{\tt bdiag}\left(\mathscr{A}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&&&\\
&\mathscr{A}^{(2)}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\

Definition 1
.

(
[
23
]
)
Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times q}_{n}
and
\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{K}^{q\times p}_{n}
be
third-order tensors. The t-product of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
is defined by

\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}:={\tt fold}\left({\tt bcirc}(\mathscr{A})\,{\tt unfold}(\mathscr{B})\right)\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}.

The block circulant matrix (
1
) can be block-diagonalized by using the discrete
Fourier transform (DFT) as follows:

{\tt bcirc}(\mathscr{A})=\left(F_{n}^{H}\otimes I_{\ell}\right){\tt bdiag}(\widehat{\mathscr{A}})\left(F_{n}\otimes I_{p}\right),

where
F_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is the discrete Fourier matrix,
F_{n}^{H}
denotes its
conjugate transpose,
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
stands for the Fourier transform of

\mathscr{A}
along each tube,
I_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times\ell}
denotes the identity
matrix, and
\otimes
is the Kronecker product. The matrix
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
can be
computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm; see
[
23
]
for details.
Using MATLAB notations, we have

\widehat{\mathscr{A}}={\tt fft}(\mathscr{A},[\,],3).

The inverse operation can be evaluated in MATLAB with the command

\mathscr{A}={\tt ifft}(\widehat{\mathscr{A}},[\,],3).

Hence, the t-product
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}
can be evaluated as

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}\widehat{\mathscr{B}}^{(i)},\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,n,

(2)

where
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}
,
\widehat{\mathscr{B}}^{(i)}
, and

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{(i)}
are the
i
th frontal slices of the tensors

\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
,
\widehat{\mathscr{B}}
, and
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}
,
respectively.

As already pointed out by Kilmer et al.
[
22
]
, one can use symmetry
properties of the DFT when applied to real data to reduce the computational effort when
evaluating the t-product with the FFT. This is described by the following result, which
can be found, e.g., in
[

]
.

Lemma 1
.

Given a real vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the associated DFT vector
\widehat{v}=F_{n}v
satisfies

\widehat{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\quad{\tt conj}\left(\widehat{v}_{i}\right)=\widehat{v}_{n-i+2},\quad i=2,3,\ldots,\left[\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right],

where
{\tt conj}
denotes the complex conjugation operator and

\left[\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right]
denotes the integer part of
\dfrac{n+1}{2}
.

It follows that for a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
, we have

\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p},\quad{\tt conj}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}\right)=\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(n-i+2)},\quad i=2,3,\ldots,\left[\dfrac{n+1}{2}\right].

This shows that the t-product of two third-order tensors can be determined by evaluating
just about half the number of products involved in (
2

The following definition is concerned with the t-product of a third-order tensor and a
tube.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
. Then

\mathscr{C}:=\mathscr{A}\star\bm{b}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
is obtained by applying the
inverse DFT along each tube of
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}
, where each frontal slice is
determined by the standard matrix product between each frame of
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}

and
\widehat{\bm{b}}
, i.e.,

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}\widehat{\bm{b}}^{(i)}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\widehat{\bm{b}}^{(i)}\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n.

A third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
can be written as

\mathscr{A}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{A}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{A}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{A}}_{p}\right],

thus, for the tensors
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times q}_{n}
and

\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{K}^{q\times p}_{n}
, the t-product
\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}
can be
expressed as

\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}=\left[\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{B}}_{1},\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{B}}_{2},\ldots,\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{B}}_{p}\right],

where

\mathscr{A}\star\overrightarrow{\mathscr{B}}_{i}=\overrightarrow{\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}\right)}_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,p.

The Frobenius norm of a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
is given by

\left\|\mathscr{A}\right\|_{F}:=\sqrt{\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}=1}^{\ell,p,n}a_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}^{2}},

and the inner product of two third-order tensors of the same size

\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
is defined as

\langle\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\rangle:=\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}=1}^{\ell,p,n}a_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}b_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}.

We have the relations

\left\|\mathscr{A}\right\|_{F}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{A}}\right\|_{F},\qquad\langle\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{n}\langle\widehat{\mathscr{A}},\widehat{\mathscr{B}}\rangle.

We recall for later use the definitions of some special tensors and operations:

•

The identity tensor
\mathscr{I}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times\ell}_{n}
is the tensor whose
first frontal slice is the identity matrix and all other slices have zero entries only.

•

The transpose of a real third-order tensor,
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
,
denoted by
\mathscr{A}^{H}\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times\ell}_{n}
, is the tensor obtained by first
transposing each one of the frontal slices of
\mathscr{A}
, and then reversing the order
of the transposed frontal slices
2
through
n
; see
[
23
]
. Let the third-order
tensors
{\mathscr{A}}
and
\mathscr{B}
be such that the products

{\mathscr{A}}\star\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{B}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}^{H}
are defined. Then,
similarly to the matrix transpose, the tensor transpose satisfies

({\mathscr{A}}\star\mathscr{B})^{H}=\mathscr{B}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}^{H}
.

•

A tensor
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times\ell}_{n}
is said to be orthogonal if
and only if

\mathscr{Q}^{H}\star\mathscr{Q}=\mathscr{Q}\star\mathscr{Q}^{H}=\mathscr{I}_{\ell}.

•

A square third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times\ell}_{n}
is invertible if
there is a third-order tensor
\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times\ell}_{n}
such that

\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{I}_{\ell},\quad\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{I}_{\ell}.

In this case
\mathscr{B}
is said to be the inverse of
\mathscr{A}
, and is denoted by

\mathscr{A}^{-1}
.

.

The tensor singular value decomposition (t-SVD) associated with the t-product, introduced
by Kilmer and Martin
[

described in the next theorem.

Theorem 4
.

(
[
23
]
)
Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
be a third-order tensor. Then it can be
represented as the t-product of three third-order tensors,

\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{S}\star\mathscr{V}^{H},

(3)

where
\mathscr{U}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times\ell}_{n}
and
\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times p}_{n}
are
orthogonal tensors, and
\mathscr{S}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}

\mathscr{S}
is a diagonal matrix.

\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{S}\star\mathscr{V}^{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{\ell,p\}}\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\star\bm{s}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}^{H},

The next result generalizes the Eckart-Young theorem for matrices to third-order tensors.
It is important in the context of data compression.

Theorem 6
.

(

3
Tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization for computing the largest and smallest
singular triplets

This section describes the Lanczos bidiagonalization process for tensors using the
t-product, and discusses how approximations of the largest and smallest singular triplets
of a large third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
can be computed.

3.1
The tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization algorithm

The Lanczos bidiagonalization process was introduced for matrices by Golub and Kahan

[
14
]
and therefore sometimes is referred to as the Golub-Kahan
bidiagonalization process. For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p}
, this process is closely
related to symmetric Lanczos process applied to the real symmetric matrices
AA^{T}
and
A^{T}A
,
or alternatively to the symmetric matrix

\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
A^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Lanczos bidiagonalization algorithms have been applied to solve numerous problems such as
large-scale least squares problem
[
28
]
, the approximation of the largest or
smallest singular triplets of a large matrix

[
5
,
19
,
24
]
, and in
Tikhonov regularization of large linear discrete ill-posed problems; see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
.
We note that the bidiagonalization method described in

[
28
]
and applied in
[
11
,
12
]
reduces a large matrix
A
to a small lower
bidiagonal matrix, while in
[
5
]
the matrix
A
is reduced to a
small upper bidiagonal matrix. We will review the latter approach.

Application of
m\ll\min\{\ell,p\}
steps of the Lanczos bidiagonalization process to the
matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p}
with the initial unit vector
p_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
generically
produces two matrices

P_{m}=\left[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m},\quad Q_{m}=\left[q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times m}.

The columns of
P_{m}
and
Q_{m}
form orthonormal bases for the Krylov subspaces

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(A^{T}A,p_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt span}\{p_{1},A^{T}Ap_{1},\left(A^{T}A\right)^{2}p_{1},\ldots,\left(A^{T}A\right)^{m-1}p_{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(AA^{T},q_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt span}\{q_{1},AA^{T}q_{1},\left(AA^{T}\right)^{2}q_{1},\ldots,\left(AA^{T}\right)^{m-1}q_{1}\},

respectively, where
q_{1}=Ap_{1}/\|Ap_{1}\|_{2}
. A matrix interpretation of the recursion
relations of the Lanczos process gives the matrix relations

\displaystyle AP_{m}=

\displaystyle Q_{m}B_{m},

(8)

\displaystyle A^{T}Q_{m}=

\displaystyle P_{m}B_{m}^{T}+\beta_{m}p_{m+1}e_{m}^{T},

(9)

where
e_{m}=[0,\ldots,0,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\beta_{m}\geq 0
is a scalar, and
p_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
The matrix
B_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is upper bidiagonal and satisfies
B_{m}=Q_{m}^{T}AP_{m}
.

When considering bidiagonalization of a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}
using the
t-product, the scalars and the columns of the matrices
P_{m}
and
Q_{m}
in the matrix
decompositions (
8
) and (
9
) become tubes and lateral slices,
respectively, in
the decompositions determined by the tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization process. The
application of
m
steps of tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization to the third-order
tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
generically computes two tensors

\mathscr{P}_{m}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{2},\dots,\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times m}_{n}\mbox{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathscr{Q}_{m}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{2},\dots,\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times m}_{n},

whose lateral slices form bases for the tensor Krylov subspaces

\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A},\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\right)
and

\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{A}^{H},\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1}\right)
,
respectively. They are defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A},\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt span}\{\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1},\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}\right)\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1},\ldots,\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}\right)^{m-1}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{A}^{H},\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tt span}\{\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1},\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{A}^{H}\right)\star\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1},\ldots,\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{A}^{H}\right)^{m-1}\star\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1}\},

where
\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\in\mathbb{K}^{p}_{n}
is a lateral slice of unit norm, and the
lateral slice
\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell}_{n}
is of unit norm and proportional to

\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}
. Algorithm

bidiagonalization algorithm.

3.2
Approximating singular tubes and singular lateral slices

We describe an approach to approximate the largest or smallest singular triplets
(singular tubes and associated left and right lateral singular slices) of a large tensor

\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
using restarted partial tensor Lanczos
bidiagonalization. Since the tensor
\mathscr{A}
is large, computing its
k
largest or
smallest singular triplets by determining the t-SVD of
\mathscr{A}
is very expensive.
The idea is to approximate the extreme singular triplets of the tensor
\mathscr{A}
by
determining the extreme singular triplets the bidiagonal tensor
\mathscr{B}_{m}
, where
m

is small. Let
\{\bm{s}_{i},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}\}
,
1\leq i\leq m
,
denote the singular triplets of
\mathscr{B}_{m}
. They satisfy

\mathscr{B}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}=\bm{s}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\mbox{\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathscr{B}^{H}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}=\bm{s}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}.

The
k\leq m
largest singular triplets of
\mathscr{A}
are approximated by the triplets

\{\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\}
defined by

\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\bm{s}_{i},\quad\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\mathscr{Q}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i},\quad\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\mathscr{P}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,k.

(14)

For
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{P}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{m}\star\mathscr{B}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{m}\star\bm{s}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{Q}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\star\bm{s}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{Q}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathscr{P}_{m}\star\mathscr{B}_{m}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\right)\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}.

To accept
\{\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\}
as an approximate singular triplet of

\mathscr{A}
, the remainder term

\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}
should be small
enough. We can bound the remainder term according to

\displaystyle\left\|\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}}\right){\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\left(\vec{\mathscr{E}}^{H}_{m}\right)}\right){\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\vec{{\mathscr{U}}_{i}}}\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left\|{\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}}\right)\right\|_{F}\left\|{\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\left(\vec{\mathscr{E}}^{H}_{m}\right)}\right){\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\vec{{\mathscr{U}}_{i}}}\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|{\tt bdiag}\left(\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\right)\right\|_{F}\left\|{\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\left(\vec{\mathscr{E}}^{H}_{m}\right)}\right){\tt bdiag}\left(\widehat{\vec{{\mathscr{U}}_{i}}}\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\beta}_{m}\right\|_{F}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left|\widehat{\left(\vec{\mathscr{E}}^{H}_{m}\right)}^{(s)}\widehat{\vec{{\mathscr{U}}_{i}}}^{(s)}\right|.

Analogously as in
[
5
]
, we require for
1\leq s\leq n
that

\left|\widehat{\left(\vec{\mathscr{E}}^{H}_{m}\right)}^{(s)}\widehat{\vec{{\mathscr{U}}_{i}}}^{(s)}\right|\leq\delta^{\prime}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(s)}\right\|=\delta^{\prime}\left(\bm{s}^{\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(s)}}_{1,m}\right)=\delta\left(\bm{s}^{\widehat{\mathscr{A}}}_{1,m}\right)^{(s)},

for a user-chosen parameter
\delta^{\prime}>0
, where

\left(\bm{s}^{\widehat{\mathscr{A}}}_{j,m}\right)^{(s)}
denotes the
s
th element of the

j
th approximate singular tube of
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
. We obtain from eq.
(
4
) that

\left\|\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\right\|_{F}\leq\delta^{\prime}\left\|\bm{\beta}_{m}\right\|_{F}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\left(\bm{s}^{\widehat{\mathscr{A}}}_{1}\right)^{(s)}=n\delta^{\prime}\left\|\bm{\beta}_{m}\right\|_{F}\left(\bm{s}^{\mathscr{A}}_{1}\right)^{(1)}=n\delta^{\prime\prime}\left(\bm{s}^{\mathscr{A}}_{1}\right)^{(1)},

where
\delta^{\prime\prime}=\delta^{\prime}\left\|\bm{\beta}_{m}\right\|_{F}
. The computed approximate
singular triplets
\{\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\}
,
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, of
\mathscr{A}
are accepted as
singular triplets of
\mathscr{A}
if

\left\|\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\right\|_{F}\leq\delta\left(\bm{s}^{\mathscr{A}}_{1,m}\right)^{(1)},\quad i=1,2,\ldots k,

(16)

for some user-specified parameter
\delta>0
.

To keep the storage requirement fairly small for large-scale problems, we would like the
number of steps
m
of the tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization process to be small. However,
when
m
is small, it may not be possible to approximate the desired singular triplets
sufficiently accurately using the available Krylov subspaces

\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A},\vec{\mathscr{Q}}_{1}\right)
and

\mathscr{K}_{m}\left(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{A}^{H},\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{1}\right)
. A remedy
for this situation is to restart the tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization process. The idea
is to repeatedly update the initial lateral slices used for the tensor Lanczos
bidiagonalization process, and in this way determine a sequence of increasingly more
appropriate Krylov subspaces, until the
k
desired singular triplets have been found
with required accuracy. We remark that restarting techniques have been used for computing a few desired
singular triplets or eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs of a large matrix, where properties of
Ritz vectors, harmonic Ritz vectors, and refined Ritz vectors have been exploited; see,
e.g.,
[
5
,
19
,
20
,
35
,
36
]
for details.

3.3
Augmentation by Ritz lateral slices

Assume that we would like to approximate the
k
largest singular triplets of

\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
. To this end, we carry out
m>k
steps of
tensor Lanczos bidiagonalization as described in the previous subsection. The approximate
right singular lateral slice
\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
is a Ritz lateral slice of
\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}
associated with the Ritz tube

\left(\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right)^{2}=\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
for
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, and we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\right)\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\left(\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right)^{2}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}.

In what follows we will show some results that will help us to approximate the largest
or smallest singular triplets of a third-order tensor. The idea behind these results
is to find equations that are analogous to (
10
) and (
11
), and such that
the reduced tensor will contain the
k
approximate singular tubes among its first
k

elements on the diagonal, and the right projection tensor will contain the
k
right Ritz
lateral slices among its first
k
lateral slices, and the left projection tensor will
contain the
k
left Ritz lateral slices among its first
k
lateral slices. The following
theorem will be helpful.

Theorem 9
.

Assume that
m
steps of Algorithm
5
have been applied to the third-order tensor

\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times p}_{n}
, and suppose that
\bm{\beta}_{m}
in
(
11
) is nonvanishing. Then for
k<m
, we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}^{H}+\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{k+1}^{H},

(18)

where
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times(k+1)}_{n}
and

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times(k+1)}_{n}
have orthonormal lateral slices,
and the first
k
lateral slices of
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{m}
are the first
k
Ritz
lateral slices of
\mathscr{A}
,
\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}_{n}

is an upper triangular tensor,
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}\in\mathbb{K}^{p}_{n}
is a lateral
slice that is orthogonal to
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}
,

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
, and
\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{k+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{k+1}_{n}
is the
canonical element under the t-product.

Proof.

Let the Ritz lateral slices
\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
for
1\leq i\leq k

be associated with the
k
Ritz tubes of
\mathscr{A}
. Introduce the tensor

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times(k+1)}_{n},

(19)

where
\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}
is given by (
12
). Then, using the fact that

\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\right].

Orthogonalizing the term
\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}
against

\{\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\}_{i=1:k}
gives

\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{\rho}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k},

(21)

where
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k}
is orthogonal to

\{\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\}_{i=1:k}
, and the
\bm{\rho}_{i}
for

i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
are given by

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{i}=\left(\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right)^{H}\star\left(\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right)^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}\right)^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{m}^{H}\star\left(\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}^{H}\right)\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{m}\star\left<\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i},\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}\right>,

because
\bm{\beta}_{m}=\bm{\beta}_{m}^{H}
.

Let
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k}=\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\star\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+1}
be a normalization of
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k}
,
and introduce the tensors

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times(k+1)}_{n}

(22)

and

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\textbf{0}&\ldots&\textbf{0}&\bm{\rho}_{1}\\
\textbf{0}&\bm{s}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\ldots&\textbf{0}&\bm{\rho}_{2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\textbf{0}&\ldots&\textbf{0}&\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\bm{\rho}_{k}\\
\textbf{0}&\ldots&\ldots&\textbf{0}&\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}_{n}.

(23)

Then, from (
20
) and (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{\rho}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k}\right]

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}.

On the other hand, as

\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}=\left[\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\ldots,\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}},\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right],

using (
15
), we get

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{R}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\star\bm{\beta}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\star\bm{\rho}_{i}^{H}.

Since

\left<\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}^{\prime}_{k},\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right>=\left(\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}^{\prime}_{k}\right)^{H}\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\bm{s}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\left(\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}^{\prime}_{k}\right)^{H}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}=\textbf{0},

the tensor
\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}
is orthogonal to

\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
. Moreover, in view of that

\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
is orthogonal to
\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}
, we
obtain

\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}=\bm{\gamma}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}+\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1},

(25)

where
\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1}
is orthogonal to
\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}
as well as to

\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}
. Due to the orthogonality of

\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k}
(or
\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}
)
to
\left\{\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right\}_{i=1:k}
, the parameter

\bm{\gamma}
in (
25
) is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}=\left<\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1},\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1},\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bm{\rho}_{i}\star\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k},\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left<\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}}}_{k},\overrightarrow{\widetilde{\mathscr{R}^{\prime}}}_{k}\right>=\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+1}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\star\bm{\rho}_{1}^{H},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\star\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}+\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}\star\bm{\rho}_{k}^{H},\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+1}\star\vec{\mathscr{P}}_{m+1}+\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1}\right]

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}^{H}+\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{k+1}^{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}^{H}+\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{k+1}^{H},

where
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}
and
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}
are
determined by the normalization of
\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1}
, i.e.,

\vec{\mathscr{F}}_{k+1}=\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}
, because

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}^{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{s}_{2,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\ldots&\bm{0}&\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{\rho}_{1}^{H}&\bm{\rho}_{2}^{H}&\ldots&\bm{\rho}_{k}^{H}&\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{K}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}_{n}.

The orthogonality of
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}

now follows from the orthogonality of the sequences

\left\{\vec{\mathscr{V}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right\}_{i=1:k}
and

\left\{\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{i,m}^{\mathscr{A}}\right\}_{i=1:k}
, respectively, given by (
14
).
∎

In the preceding theorem we assumed
\bm{\beta}_{m}
to be nonvanishing. If, instead,

\bm{\beta}_{m}
vanishes, then the singular tubes of
\mathscr{B}_{m}
are singular tubes of

\mathscr{A}
, and the left and right singular lateral slices of
\mathscr{A}
can be
determined from those of
\mathscr{B}_{m}
. Similarly, if
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}
in
(
25

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+1}
are
singular tubes of
\mathscr{A}
, and the singular lateral slices of
\mathscr{A}
can be
determined from
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}
.

If the
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}
is nonvanishing, then we append new lateral slices to

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}
repeatedly until
iteration
m-k
. This is the subject of the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Assume that
m
steps of Algorithm
5
have been applied to
\mathscr{A}
and that

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{m},

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{m}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{m}^{H}+\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{m}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{m+1}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H},

where
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times m}_{n}
and

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times m}_{n}
have orthonormal lateral slices,

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{m\times m}_{n}
is an upper triangular,

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}_{n}
,
\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{m+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{p}_{n}
is

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{m}
, and
\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{m}_{n}
is the

and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{m}
are the same as those of the

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}

Let the tensors
\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}
and
\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}

defined in (
24
) and (

), respectively, be represented by

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1}=\left[\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{1},\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+1}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times(k+1)}_{n}

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}=\left[\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{1},\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+1}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times(k+1)}_{n},

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+2}
be given by

\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+2}=\left[\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+1},\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{p\times(k+2)}_{n}.

By normalizing the quantity
\left(\mathscr{I}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}^{H}\right)\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}
, we obtain the lateral slice

\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+2}
such that

\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+2}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+2}=\left(\mathscr{I}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}^{H}\right)\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{k+2}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathscr{I}_{\ell}-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}^{H}\right)\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}-\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+2}

\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+2}=\left[\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+1},\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+2}\right]\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell\times(k+2)}_{n}

\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+2}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{s}_{1,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\textbf{0}&\ldots&\textbf{0}&\bm{\rho}_{1}&\textbf{0}\\

\mathscr{A}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+2}=\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}_{k+2}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{k+2}.

\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+3}

\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k+2}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}}_{k+3}=\left(\mathscr{I}-\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+2}\star\widetilde{\mathscr{P}}_{k+2}^{H}\right)\star\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\vec{\widetilde{\mathscr{Q}}}_{k+2}

and

&&\bm{s}_{k,m}^{\mathscr{A}}&\bm{\rho}_{k}&&&\\

If we would like to compute the smallest singular triplets of

3.4
Augmentation by harmonic Ritz lateral slices

When the smallest singular values of a matrix
A
are clustered, their computation
by the restarted Lanczos bidiagonalization method as described above may require many
iterations. In this situation it may be beneficial to instead compute approximations
of the smallest singular values of
A
by seeking to determine approximations of the
largest singular values of the matrix
\left(A^{T}A\right)^{-1}
without explicitly
computing the matrix
\left(A^{T}A\right)^{-1}
. This was done for the matrix case by using
computing harmonic Ritz vectors; see
[
5
,
27
]
.
Harmonic Ritz vectors furnish approximations of
eigenvectors of
A^{T}A
associated with the corresponding harmonic Ritz values.

In the case of tensors, harmonic Ritz lateral slices furnish approximations of eigenvectors of

\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}
associated with harmonic Ritz tubes of

\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}
. The harmonic Ritz tubes
\widecheck{\bm{\theta}}_{j}
of

\mathscr{A}^{H}\star\mathscr{A}
associated with the partial tensor tridiagonalization
defined in (
13
) are the eigentubes of the generalized eigenvalue
problem

\left(\left(\mathscr{B}_{m}^{H}\star\mathscr{B}_{m}\right)^{2}+\bm{\alpha}_{m}^{2}\star\bm{\beta}_{m}^{2}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}\star\vec{\mathscr{E}}_{m}^{H}\right)\star\vec{\widecheck{\mathscr{\omega}}}_{j}=\widecheck{\bm{\theta}}_{j}\star\mathscr{B}_{m}^{H}\star\mathscr{B}_{m}\star\vec{\widecheck{\mathscr{\omega}}}_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq m.

Multidimensional principal component analysis for facial recognition

Principal component analysis (PCA) is used in numerous areas of science and engineering,
such as in data denoising, image classification, and facial recognition. Some approaches to
color image classification
involve conversion of color images to grayscale images to reduce the computational burden,
because color images are represented by tensors, while gray scale images can be
represented by matrices; see
[
2
,
29
]
. However, this conversion
entails loss of information. A color image in RGB format can be represented by a
third-order tensor. This section discusses the application of PCA to third-order
tensors.

PCA when applied to gray-scale face recognition computes a set of characteristics
(eigenfaces) corresponding to the main components of the initial set of training images.
Recognition is done by projecting the training images into the eigenface subspace, in
which an image of a person is classified by comparing it with other available images in
the eigenface subspace. The main advantages of this procedure are its
simplicity, speed, and insensitivity to small changes on the faces.

When applying PCA to third-order tensors using the t-product, tubes, lateral slices, and
third-order tensors are analogues of scalars, vectors, and matrices in the eigenface
technique for classifying grayscale images. Using this identification, PCA for third-order
tensors that represent color images is structurally very similar to PCA for matrices that
represent grayscale images. The latter is described in
[
17
]
.

Let
N
training color images
I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{N}
of size
\ell\times p\times n
be
available. They are represented by the third-order tensors

\mathscr{I}_{1},\mathscr{I}_{2},\ldots,\mathscr{I}_{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times p\times n}
. The
procedure of recognizing color facial images using third-order tensors is as follows:

1.

For each image
I_{i}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,N
, we determine a lateral slice

\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell p\times 1\times n}
by vectorizing each frontal slice,
i.e.,
\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(s)}={\tt vec}(\mathscr{I}_{i}^{(s)})
for
s=1,2,\ldots,n
. We
then construct a tensor, whose frontal slices are given by
\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{i}
, i.e.,

\mathscr{X}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{N}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell p\times N\times n}.

2.

Compute the mean of the frontal slices of
\mathscr{X}
, i.e.,

\vec{\mathscr{M}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\dfrac{\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{i}}{N},

and let

\overline{\mathscr{X}}=[\vec{\overline{\mathscr{X}}}_{1},\vec{\overline{\mathscr{X}}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\overline{\mathscr{X}}}_{N}],\qquad\vec{\overline{\mathscr{X}}}_{i}=\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{i}-\vec{\mathscr{M}}.

3.

Determine the first
k
left singular vectors of
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
. We denote them
by
\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k}
. Construct the projection subspace

\mathbb{U}_{k}={\tt span}\left\{\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{A}}_{k}\right\}

(43)

and let

\mathscr{U}_{k}=\left[\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{1},\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\mathscr{U}}_{k}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell p\times k\times n}.

4.

Project each face
I_{i}
onto the subspace (
43
) to obtain

\mathscr{U}_{k}^{H}\star\vec{\overline{\mathscr{X}}}_{i}
. A test image
I_{0}
also is
projected onto the same space to get

\mathscr{U}_{k}^{H}\star\left(\vec{\mathscr{X}}_{0}-\vec{\mathscr{M}}\right)
. Finally,
determine the closest image to the test image by computing the minimal distance
between the projected test image and all the projected training images.

The main difference between methods that use PCA for facial recognition is the way that
the first (dominant) left singular vectors of
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
are computed.
In the present paper, we use our proposed method to compute the
dominant singular triplets that are used in PCA. The following algorithm summarises
the different steps in our approach.

6
Conclusion and extensions

This paper presents two new methods for approximating the largest or smallest singular
triplets of large third-order tensors using the t-product. We use restarted Lanczos
bidiagonalization for third-order tensors to develop the Ritz augmentation method to
determine the largest or smallest singular triplets. Moreover, we propose the
harmonic Ritz augmentation method to compute the smallest singular triplets. These methods
are applied to data compression and face recognition.
