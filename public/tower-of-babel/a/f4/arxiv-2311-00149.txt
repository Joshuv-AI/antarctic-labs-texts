A Knowledge Compilation Take on Binary Polynomial Optimization
Florent Capelli, Alberto Del Pia, Silvia Di Gregorio

Abstract
The Binary Polynomial Optimization (BPO) problem is defined as the problem of maximizing a given polynomial function over all binary points. The main contribution of this paper is to draw a novel connection between BPO and the field of Knowledge Compilation. This connection allows us to unify and significantly extend the state-of-the-art for BPO, both in terms of tractable classes, and in terms of existence of extended formulations. In particular, for instances of BPO with hypergraphs that are either β \beta -acyclic or with bounded incidence treewidth, we obtain strongly polynomial algorithms for BPO, and extended formulations of polynomial size for the corresponding multilinear polytopes. The generality of our technique allows us to obtain the same type of results for extensions of BPO, where we enforce extended cardinality constraints on the set of binary points, and where variables are replaced by literals. We also obtain strongly polynomial algorithms for the variant of the above problems where we seek k k best feasible solutions, instead of only one optimal solution. Computational results show that the resulting algorithms can be significantly faster than current state-of-the-art.



Key words: Binary Polynomial Optimization; Knowledge Compilation; Boolean functions; strongly polynomial algorithms; Extended formulations

Introduction

In
Binary Polynomial Optimization (BPO),
we are given a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, a
profit
function
p\colon E\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
, and our goal is to find some
x
that attains

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{x}\quad&\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}x(v)\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&x\in\{0,1\}^{V}.\end{split}

(BPO)

BPO is strongly NP-hard, as even the case where all the monomials have degree equal to 2 has this property
[
30
]
.
Nevertheless, its generality and wide range of applications attract a significant amount of interest in the optimization community.
A few examples of applications can be found in classic operations research, computer vision, communication engineering, and theoretical physics
[
3
,
6
,
47
,
40
,
33
,
44
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, three main tractable classes of BPO have been identified so far, which depend on the structure of the hypergraph
H
.
These are instances such that:

(i)

The primal treewidth of
H
, denoted by
\mathsf{ptw}(H)
, is bounded by
\log(\poly(|V|,|E|))
;

(ii)

H
is
\beta
-acyclic;

(iii)

H
is a cycle hypergraph.

The techniques used to obtain these tractability results are based either on combinatorial techniques, or on a polyhedral approach.
Combinatorial approaches directly exploit the structure of the problem, and generally result in strongly polynomial algorithms.
Algorithms of this type have been obtained in
[
17
]
for class (i), and in

[
21
,
22
]
for class (ii).
On the other hand, polyhedral approaches result in linear programming reformulations, which can be used to solve the problem in polynomial time
[
36
]
or strongly polynomial time
[
49
]
, and to obtain strong relaxations for more general problems with tractable substructures.
Polyhedral approaches are based on
the introduction of variables
x(e)
, for every
e\in E
, which leads to the reformulation of BPO as

\displaystyle\max_{x}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}p(e)x(e)

s.t.

\displaystyle x(e)=\prod_{v\in e}x(v)

\displaystyle x\in\{0,1\}^{V\cup E}.

The feasible region of the above optimization problem is

\mathcal{MS}_{H}=\Big\{x\in\{0,1\}^{V\cup E}:x(e)=\prod_{v\in e}x(v),\;\forall e\in E\Big\},

and is called the

multilinear set
of
H

[
23
]
.
The convex hull of
\mathcal{MS}_{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
, and is called the
multilinear polytope
of
H
.
Since the objective function is linear, if a linear inequalities description of
\mathcal{MP}_{H}

is readily available,
we can solve the original BPO problem via linear programming.
In fact, to solve the original BPO problem via linear programming, it suffices to have access to an
extended formulation
of
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
, which is a linear inequalities description of a higher-dimensional polytope whose projection in the original space is
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
.
Extended formulations of polynomial size for
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
can be found in
[
50
,
39
,
5
]
for class (i), in
[
26
]
, for class (ii), and in
[
20
]
for class (iii).
The result in
[
26
]
extends previous extended formulations for Berge-acylic instances
[
24
,
10
]
, for
\gamma
-acyclic instances
[
24
]
, and for kite-free
\beta
-acyclic instances
[
25
]
.

In this paper we provide a novel connection between BPO and the field of
knowledge compilation,
which allows us to unify and significantly extend most known tractability results for BPO, including all the ones mentioned above.
We establish this connection on two different levels: (1) in terms of polynomial time algorithms, and (2) in terms of extended formulations of polynomial size.

The first contribution is to draw a connection between
BPO
and the problem of performing
algebraic model counting over the
(\max,+)
semiring
on some Boolean function
[
37
]
.
We associate a Boolean function and a weight function to each BPO such that optimal solutions of the original BPO are in one to one correspondence with the optimal solutions of the Boolean function.
This Boolean function can be encoded as a Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) formula whose underlying structure is very close to the structure of the original BPO problem, allowing us to leverage many known tractability results on CNF formulas to BPO such as bounded treewidth CNF formulas
[
45
]
or
\beta
-acyclic formulas
[
13
]
(see
[
12
, Chapter 3]
for a survey).
More interestingly, the tractability of algebraic model counting for CNF formulas is sometimes proven in two steps: first, one computes a small data structure which represents every satisfying assignment of the CNF formula in a factorized yet tractable form, as in
[
8
]
, and for which we can find the optimal value in optimal time
[
37
,
7
]
.
This data structure can be seen as a syntactically restricted Boolean circuit, known as
Decomposable Negation Normal Form circuits,
DNNF for short, which have extensively been studied in the field of Knowledge Compilation
[
19
]
.
Our connection actually shows that all feasible points of a tractable BPO instance and their value can be represented by a small DNNF, which can be efficiently constructed from the BPO instance itself.
This enables us to transfer other known tractability results to the setting of BPO.
For example, by working directly on the Boolean circuit, we can not only prove that broad classes of BPO can be solved in strongly polynomial time, but also that we can solve BPO instances in these classes together with cardinality constraints or find the
k
best solutions.

The second contribution is to draw a connection between
extended formulations for BPO
and DNNF.
We show that if a multilinear set
\mathcal{MS}_{H}
can be encoded via a DNNF circuit
C
, then we can extract from
C
an extended formulation of the multilinear polytope
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
of roughly the same size of
C
. Our extended formulation is very simple, and in particular all coefficients are
0,\pm 1
.
It also enjoys a nice theoretical property, since the system is
totally dual integral
(TDI).
This connection allows us to obtain extended formulations of polynomial size for all the classes of BPO that we show can be solved in strongly polynomial time with the approach detailed above.
Besides the wide applicability of our result, our method inherits the flexibility of DNNF circuits.
As an example, for each multilinear set for which we obtain a polynomial-size extended formulation of the corresponding multilinear polytope, we directly obtain a polynomial-size extended formulation corresponding to the subset of the multilinear set obtained by enforcing cardinality constraints.As a result, we provide a unifying approach for a large number of polynomial-size extended formulations, and at the same time significantly extend them.

In the remainder of the introduction we present our results in further detail.
We remark that these results constitute just the tip of the iceberg of what our novel connection can achieve.
We begin by stating our first result for BPO, and we refer the reader to
Section

3
for the definitions of
\beta
-acyclicity and of incidence treewidth of a hypergraph
H
, which we denote by
\mathsf{itw}(H)
.

Theorem 1

(Tractability of BPO)
.

There is a strongly polynomial time algorithm to solve the BPO problem, provided that
H
is
\beta
-acyclic, or the incidence treewidth of
H
is bounded by
\log(\poly(|V|,|E|))
.
Furthermore, under the same assumptions, there exists a polynomial-size extended formulation for
\mathcal{MP}_{H}
.

Theorem

1
implies and unifies the known tractable classes (i), (ii), (iii).
To see that (i) is implied, it suffices to note that
\mathsf{ptw}(H)\geq 2\mathsf{itw}(H)
.
We observe that this bound can be very loose, as in fact
the hypergraph with only one edge containing all
n
vertices has
\mathsf{ptw}(H)=n-1
and
\mathsf{itw}(H)=1
.
Hence,
Theorem

1
greatly extends (i).

The generality of our approach allows us to significantly extend
Theorem

1
in several directions.
The first extension enables us to consider a constrained version of BPO, with a more general feasible region.
Namely, we consider feasible regions consisting of binary points that satisfy
extended cardinality constraints
of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}x(v)\in S,

(1)

for some
S\subseteq[n]
where
n=|V|,

and where the set
S
is given as part of the input.
Note that extended cardinality constraints are quite general and extend several classes of well-known inequalities, like cardinality constraints and modulo constraints.
To the best of our knowledge, there are only two classes of constrained BPO that are known to be tractable.
In these two classes, the feasible region consists of
(a) binary points satisfying cardinality constraints
l\leq\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}\leq u
, and
E
is nested
[
14
]
or
|E|=2

[
15
]
;
or (b) binary points satisfying polynomial constraints

\sum_{f\subseteq e_{i}}p^{i}(f)\prod_{v\in f}x(v)\geq 0,\qquad i=1,\dots,m,

where, for
i=1,\dots,m
,
e_{i}\subseteq V
and
p^{i}\colon 2^{e_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
,
and it is assumed that the primal treewidth of
(V,E\cup\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\})
is bounded by
\log(\poly(|V|,|E|,m))

[
50
,
39
,
5
]
.

Our second extension concerns the objective function of the problem.
Namely, we consider the objective function obtained from the one of BPO by replacing variables with literals:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}\sigma_{e}(v),

where, for each
e\in E
,
\sigma_{e}
is a mapping (given in input) with
\sigma_{e}(v)\in\{x(v),\ 1-x(v)\}
.
We refer to this class of problems as
Binary Polynomial Optimization with Literals,
abbreviated BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
.
This optimization problem is at the heart of the area of research known as
pseudo-Boolean optimization
in the literature, and we refer the reader to
[
6
]
for a thorough survey.
In the unconstrained case, i.e., the feasible region consists of
\{0,1\}^{V}
, it is known that BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
can be solved in polynomial time if
H
is
\beta
-acyclic
[
34
]
, and an extended formulation of polynomial size for the convex hull of the associated
pseudo-Boolean set
is given in
[
27
]
.
This result,
as well as the tractability of (a) above,
is implied by our most general statement, the extension of
Theorem

1
, given below.

Theorem 2

(Tractability of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
)
.

There is a strongly polynomial time algorithm to solve BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
with extended cardinality constraints, provided that
H
is
\beta
-acyclic, or
the incidence treewidth of
H
is bounded by
\log(\poly(|V|,|E|))
.
Furthermore, under the same assumptions, there exists a polynomial-size extended formulation for the convex hull of the points in the pseudo-Boolean set that satisfy the extended cardinality constraints.

Our third and final extension allows us to consider a different overarching goal.
In fact, we consider the problem of finding
k
best feasible solutions to the optimization problem, rather than only one optimal solution.
For all classes of BPO and BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
considered in
Theorems

1
and
2
, we obtain
polynomial time algorithms to find
k
best feasible solutions. While this result also follows by combining our extended formulations with Theorem 4 in
[
2
]
, our approach yields a direct and practical algorithm that does not rely on extended formulations.

A key feature of our approach is that it also leads to practically efficient algorithms for BPO.
Preliminary computational results show that the resulting algorithms can be significantly faster than current state-of-the-art on some structured instances.

2
Binary Polynomial Optimization and Boolean Maximization Problem

In this section, we detail a connection between solving a BPO problem and finding optimal solutions of a Boolean function for a given weight function.

Binary Polynomial Optimization Problem.

Our first goal is to redefine Binary Polynomial Optimization problems using hypergraphs.
A
hypergraph

H
is a pair
(V,E)
, where
V
is a finite set of
vertices

V
and
E\subseteq 2^{V}
is a set of
edges.

Given a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and a profit function
p\colon E\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
, the
Binary Polynomial Optimization
problem for
(H,p)
is defined as finding an
optimal solution

x^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
to the following maximization problem:
\max_{x\in\{0,1\}^{V}}P_{(H,p)}(x)
where
P_{(H,p)}
is the polynomial defined as
P_{(H,p)}(x)=\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}x(v)
.
We denote this problem as
\mathsf{BPO}(H,p)
.
The value
P_{(H,p)}(x^{*})
is called the
optimal value
of
\mathsf{BPO}(H,p)
.

Boolean Maximization Problem.

A closely related notion is the notion of
Boolean Maximization Problem
. Let
X
be a finite set of variables. We denote by
\{0,1\}^{X}
the set of
assignments
on variables
X
to Boolean values, that is, the set of mappings
\tau\colon X\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. A
Boolean function
on variables
X
is a subset of assignments
f\subseteq\{0,1\}^{X}
. An assignment
\tau\in f
is said to be a
satisfying assignment
of
f
.
A weight function
w
on variables
X
is a mapping
w:X\times\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
. Given
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{X}
, we let
w(\tau)=\sum_{x\in X}w(x,\tau(x))
. Given a Boolean function
f
on variables
X
, we let
w(f)=\max_{\tau\in f}w(\tau)
. That is

w(f)=\max_{\tau\in f}\sum_{x\in X}w(x,\tau(x)).

This value can be seen as a form of Algebraic Model Counting on the
(\mathbb{Q}\cup\{-\infty\},\max,+,-\infty,0)
semiring, which is most of the time
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to compute but for which we know some tractable classes
[
37
]
.
Given a Boolean function
f
and a weight function
w
, the
Boolean Maximization Problem

(f,w)
is defined as the problem of computing an optimal solution
\tau^{*}\in f
, that is, an assignment
\tau^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{X}
such that
w(\tau^{*})=w(f)
.

BPO as Boolean Maximization Problem.

There is a pretty straightforward connections between the BPO problem and the Boolean Maximization Problem, which is based on the notion of multilinear sets introduced by Del Pia and Khajavirad in
[
23
]
.
Given a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, the
multilinear set
f_{H}
of
H
is the Boolean function on variables
X\cup Y
where
X=\{x_{v}\}_{v\in V}
and
Y=\{y_{e}\}_{e\in E}
such that
\tau\in f_{H}
if and only if for every
e\in E
,
\tau(y_{e})=\prod_{v\in e}\tau(x_{v})
.

Moreover, given a profit function
p\colon E\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
, we define a weight function
w_{p}\colon X\cup Y\times\{0,1\}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
as:

•

for every
v\in V
and
b\in\{0,1\}
,
w_{p}(x_{v},b)=0
,

•

for every
e\in E
and
b\in\{0,1\}
,
w_{p}(y_{e},b)=b\times p(e)
.

In the next lemma, we show that the BPO instance
(H,p)
is equivalent to the Boolean Maximization Problem
(f_{H},w_{p})
, in the sense that any
\tau\in f_{H}
can naturally be mapped to a feasible point
x_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
whose value is the weight of
\tau
. More formally:

Lemma 1
.

For every
\tau\in f_{H}
, we have
P_{(H,p)}(x_{\tau})=w_{p}(\tau)
where the mapping
x_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
is defined as
x_{\tau}(v)=\tau(x_{v})
for every
v\in V
.

Proof.

Recall that by definition,
\tau\in f_{H}
if and only if
\tau(y_{e})=\prod_{v\in e}\tau(x_{v})
. We have:

\displaystyle w_{p}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}w_{p}(x_{v},\tau(x_{v}))+\sum_{e\in E}w_{p}(y_{e},\tau(y_{e}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}0+\sum_{e\in E}\tau(y_{e})p(e)\text{ by definition of $w_{p}$}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}\tau(x_{v})\text{ since $\tau\in f_{H}$}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}x_{\tau}(x_{v})\text{ by definition of $x_{\tau}$}

\displaystyle=P_{(H,p)}(x_{\tau}).

∎

Theorem 3
.

Let
(H,p)
be an instance of BPO. The set of optimal solutions of
\mathsf{BPO}(H,p)
is equal to
\{x_{\tau^{*}}\mid\tau^{*}\text{ is an optimal solution of }(f_{H},w_{p})\}
.

Proof.

First of all, observe that given
x\in\{0,1\}^{V}
, there exists a unique
\tau\in f_{H}
such that
x=x_{\tau}
. Indeed, we define
\tau(x_{v})=x(v)
and
\tau(y_{e})=\prod_{v\in e}x(v)
. It is readily verified that
\tau\in f_{H}
and that
x=x_{\tau}
. Let
x^{*}
be an optimal solution of
\mathsf{BPO}(H,p)
and let
\tau
be such that
x^{*}=x_{\tau}
. We claim that
\tau
is an optimal solution of
(f_{H},w_{p})
. Indeed, let
\tau^{\prime}\in f_{H}
. By
Lemma

1
,
w_{p}(\tau^{\prime})=P_{(H,p)}(x_{\tau^{\prime}})
and
P_{(H,p)}(x_{\tau^{\prime}})\leq P_{(H,p)}(x^{*})
since
x^{*}
is optimal. Moreover,
P_{(H,p)}(x^{*})=w_{p}(\tau)
by
Lemma

1
again. Hence
w_{p}(\tau^{\prime})\leq w_{p}(\tau)
. In other words,
\tau
is an optimal solution of
(f_{H},w_{p})
.
Now let
\tau^{*}
be an optimal solution of
(f_{H},w_{p})
. By a symmetric reasoning, we show that
x_{\tau^{*}}
is an optimal solution of
\mathsf{BPO}(H,p)
. Indeed, for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{V}
, if
\tau\in f_{H}
is such that
x=x_{\tau}
; by
Lemma

1
, we have
P_{(H,p)}(x)=w_{p}(x_{\tau})\leq w_{p}(\tau^{*})=P_{(H,p)}(x_{\tau^{*}})
.
∎

This connection is at the heart of our main technique for solving BPO problems in this paper: if
H
is such that
w_{p}(f_{H})
is known to be tractable, then we can use
Theorem

3
to leverage these
results on Boolean function directly to BPO. We study in the next section some Boolean function for which the Boolean Maximization Problem is tractable.

3
Encoding BPO as CNF formulas

In the previous section, we have established a relation between a BPO problem
(H,p)
and the Boolean Maximization Problem problem
(f_{H},w_{p})
of finding an optimal solution to the Boolean function
f_{H}
for the weight function
p
.
This connection is merely a rewriting of the original problem and, without any detail on how
f_{H}
is encoded, it does not provide any insight on the complexity of solving BPO.
In this section, we explore encodings of
f_{H}
in Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF), which is a very generic way of encoding Boolean functions.
This is also the usual input of SAT solvers. We compare the structure of
H
and the structure of our CNF encodings. In
Section

4
, we will use this structure to discover tractable classes of BPO.

CNF formulas.

Given a set of variables
X
, a literal over
X
is an element of the form
x
or
\neg x
for some
x\in X
. Given an assignment
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{X}
, we naturally extend it to literals by defining
\tau(\neg x)=1-\tau(x)
. A
clause

C
over
X
is a set of literals over
X
. We denote by
\mathsf{var}(C)
the set of variables appearing in
C
. An assignment
\tau
satisfies a clause
C
if and only if there exists a literal
\ell\in C
such that
\tau(\ell)=1
. A
Conjunctive Normal Form formula
F
over
X
, CNF formula for short, is a set of clauses over
X
. We denote by
\mathsf{var}(F)
the set of variables appearing in
F
. An assignment
\tau
satisfies
F
if and only if it satisfies every clause in
F
. Hence a CNF formula over
X
naturally induces a Boolean function over
X
, which consists of the set of satisfying assignments of
F
.

A first encoding.

In this section, we fix a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
and a profit function
p
.
From
Theorem

3
, one can think of the BPO instance
(H,p)
as a Boolean function that expresses the structure of the polynomial objective function through constraints of the form
y_{e}=\prod_{v\in e}x_{v}
, for all
e\in E
.
From a logical point of view, it corresponds to the logical equivalence
y_{e}\iff\bigwedge_{v\in e}x_{v}
, for all
e\in E
. In fact, given
e\in E
,
y_{e}=1
if and only if all the variables present in that specific monomial represented by
e
have value equal to 1, i.e.,
x_{v}=1
for all
v\in e
.
Let us now consider some
e\in E
, and see more in detail how the two implications of the above if and only if are expressed both from an integer optimization point of view and from a more logical point of view.

On the one hand, we must encode that if
x_{v}=1
for all
v\in e
, then
y_{e}=1
.
In the integer optimization approach, the typical single constraint that is added in this case is
\sum_{v\in e}x_{v}-y_{e}\leq|e|-1
.
The most straightforward way of encoding this as a CNF formula is as
y_{e}\vee\bigvee_{v\in e}\neg x_{v}
.
In fact, if
x_{v}=1
for all
v\in e
then, for the formula to be satisfied, we need to have
y_{e}=1
.

On the other hand, we also want to model that if
y_{e}=1
then each
v\in e
must satisfy
x_{v}=1
too.
In the optimization community this is usually done by adding the constraints
y_{e}\leq x_{v}
for every
v\in e
. The easiest way to do with a CNF formula is by adding the clause
\neg y_{e}\vee x_{v}
for each
v\in e
. If
y_{e}=1
, then the clause can only be satisfied if
x_{v}=1
.

The above discussion motivates the following definition:
F_{H}
is the CNF formula on variables
X\cup Y
having the following clauses for every
e\in E
:

•

R_{e}=y_{e}\vee\bigvee_{v\in e}\neg x_{v}
,

•

and for every
v\in e
,
L_{e,v}=\neg y_{e}\vee x_{v}
.

Example 1
.

Let us consider the multilinear expression
-3x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+4x_{4}x_{5}+5x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}x_{6}
.
The typical hypergraph that represents it is
G=(V,E)
with
V=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5},v_{6}\}
and
E
containing only three edges
\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
,
\{v_{4},v_{5}\}
,
\{v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5},v_{6}\}
.

3.1
A CNF encoding preserving treewidth

We now turn our attention to proving that some structure of hypergraph
H
can be found in the CNF encoding
F_{H}
.
One of the most studied structures that is known to give interesting tractability both for CNF formulas and for BPO is the case of bounded treewidth hypergraphs. This class has a long history in the SAT solving community, where the tractability of SAT on CNF having bounded treewidth has been established in several independent work such as
[
1
,
31
]
, later generalized in
[
48
]
. In the BPO community, several work establish the tractability for bounded treewidth
[
50
,
39
,
5
]
.

Treewidth.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. A
tree decomposition
\mathcal{T}=(T,(B_{t})_{t\in V_{T}})
is a tree
T=(V_{T},E_{T})
together with a labeling
B
of its vertices, such that the two following properties hold:

•

Covering
: For every
e\in E
, there exists
t\in V_{T}
such that
e\subseteq B_{t}
.

•

Connectedness
: For every
v\in V
,
\{t\in V_{T}\mid v\in B_{t}\}
is connected in
T
.

An element
B_{t}
of
B
is called a
bag of
\mathcal{T}
. The
treewidth of
\mathcal{T}
is defined as
\mathsf{tw}(G,\mathcal{T})=\max_{t\in B_{t}}|B_{t}|-1
, that is, the size of the biggest bag minus
1
. The
treewidth of
G
is defined to be the smallest possible width over every tree decomposition of
G
, that is,
\mathsf{tw}(G)=\min_{\mathcal{T}}\mathsf{tw}(G,\mathcal{T})
.

Two notions of treewidth can be defined for hypergraphs depending on the underlying graph considered.
Recall that A
graph
G=(V,E)
is a hypergraph such that for every
e\in E
,
|e|=2
.
Given a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we associate two graphs to it. The
primal graph
\mathsf{G_{prim}}(H)
of
H
is defined as the graph whose vertex set is
V
and edge set is
\{\{u,v\}\mid u\neq v,\exists e\in E,\ u,v\in e\}
. Intuitively, the primal graph is obtained by replacing every edge of
H
by a clique. The
incidence graph
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
of
H
is defined as the bipartite graph whose vertex set is
V\cup E
and the edge set is
\{\{v,e\}\mid v\in V,e\in E,v\in e\}
. Similarly, the
primal graph
\mathsf{G_{prim}}(F)
of a CNF formula
F
is the graph whose vertices are the variables of
F
and such that there is an edge between variables
x
and
y
if and only if there is a clause
C\in F
such that
x,y\in\mathsf{var}(C)
. The
incidence graph
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(F)
of
F
is the bipartite graph on vertices
\mathsf{var}(F)\cup F
such that there is an edge between a variable
x
and a clause
C
of
F
if and only if
x\in\mathsf{var}(C)
.

Given a hypergraph
H
, its
primal treewidth

\mathsf{ptw}(H)
is defined as the treewidth of its primal graph, that is
\mathsf{ptw}(H)=\mathsf{tw}(\mathsf{G_{prim}}(H))
, while its
incidence treewidth

\mathsf{itw}(H)
is defined as the treewidth of its incidence graph, that is
\mathsf{itw}(H)=\mathsf{tw}(\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H))
.
The
primal treewidth of
F
is defined to be
\mathsf{tw}(\mathsf{G_{prim}}(F))
.
The
incidence treewidth
of
F
is defined to be
\mathsf{tw}(\mathsf{G_{inc}}(F))
.

We now prove a relation between the incidence treewidth of
F_{H}
and of the incidence treewidth of
H
:

Lemma 3
.

For every
H=(V,E)
, we have
\mathsf{itw}(F_{H})\leq 2\cdot(1+\mathsf{itw}(H))
.

Proof.

The proof is based on the following observation. Let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be the graph obtained from
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
as follows: we introduce for every edge
e\in E
a vertex
y_{e}
that is connected to
e
and to every
v\in e
. Clearly,
e
and
y_{e}
have the same neighborhood in
G^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
can be obtained by contracting
e
and
y_{e}
for every
e\in E
. Such a transformation is known as a
modules contraction
in graph theory and one can easily prove that the treewidth of
G^{\prime}
is at most
2(1+\mathsf{itw}(H))
(see
Lemma

14
in
Appendix

A
for a full proof). Now, one can get
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(F_{H})
by renaming every vertex
v
of
G^{\prime}
into
x_{v}
and every vertex
e
into
R_{e}
and by splitting the edge
\{y_{e},x_{v}\}
of
G^{\prime}
with a node
L_{e,v}
. Hence
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(F_{H})
is obtained from
G^{\prime}
by splitting edges, an operation that preserves treewidth (see
Lemma

13
in
Appendix

A
for details). In other words,
\mathsf{itw}(F_{H})\leq\mathsf{tw}(G^{\prime})\leq 2(1+\mathsf{itw}(H))
.
∎

We observe here that bounded incidence treewidth also captures a class of hypergraphs that is known to be tractable for BPO: the class of cycle hypergraphs
[
20
]
. We define a
cycle hypergraph
as a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
such that
E=\{e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}\}
and
e_{i}\cap e_{j}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
j=i+1\mod n
. We remark that this definition is slightly more general than the one in
[
20
]
. While the primal treewidth of a cycle hypergraph can be arbitrarily large (since an edge of size
k
induces a
k
-clique in the primal graph and hence treewidth larger than
k
), the incidence treewidth of cycle hypergraphs is
2
.

Lemma 4
.

For every cycle hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we have
\mathsf{itw}(H)\leq 2
.

Proof.

We give a tree decomposition
T
for
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
of width
2
. We start by introducing
n
bags
B_{0},\dots,B_{n-1}
connected by a path such that
B_{0}=\{e_{0},e_{1}\}
and
B_{i}=\{e_{0},e_{i},e_{i+1}\}
for
1\leq i<n-1
and
B_{n-1}=\{e_{0},e_{n-1}\}
.
We connect to
B_{i}
one bag
\{e_{i},e_{i+1\mod n},v\}
for every
v\in e_{i}\cap e_{(i+1)\mod n}
and one bag
\{e_{i},v\}
for every
v\in e_{i}\setminus(e_{i-1\mod n}\cup e_{i+1\mod n})
.
We claim that this tree decomposition, which clearly has width
2
, is a tree decomposition of
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
. Indeed, every edge of
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
is of the form
(e_{i},v)
. Moreover, since
H
is a cycle hypergraph:

•

Either
v\in e_{i-1\mod n}
in which case the edge
(e_{i},v)
is covered by the bag
\{e_{i-1\mod n},e_{i},v\}
connected to
B_{i-1}
.

•

Either
v\in e_{i+1\mod n}
in which case the edge
(e_{i},v)
is covered by the bag
\{e_{i+1\mod n},e_{i},v\}
connected to
B_{i}
.

•

Or
v\in e_{i}\setminus(e_{i-1\mod n}\cup e_{i+1\mod n})
in which case the edge
(e_{i},v)
is covered by the bag
\{e_{i},v\}
connected to
B_{i}
.

Hence every edge of
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
is covered by
T
.
It remains to show that the connectedness requirement holds.
Let
w
be a vertex of
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(H)
. Either
w
is an edge
e_{i}
of
H
or it is a vertex of
H
. Let us start from examining the case where
w
is an edge of
H
.
If
i=0
, the subgraph induced by
w

is connected since
e_{0}
appears in every
B_{i}
and every other bag is connected to some
B_{i}
.
If
w=e_{i}
, for
i>0
, then
e_{i}
appears only in
B_{i}
,
B_{i+1}
or in bags connected to
B_{i}
or
B_{i+1}
. Hence the subgraph of
T
induced by
e_{i}
is connected. Now
w
may be a vertex
v\in V
. It is easy to see however that
v
appears in at most one bag of the tree decomposition and is hence connected.
∎

3.2
A CNF encoding preserving
\beta
-acyclicity

Several generalizations of graph acyclicity have been introduced for hypergraphs
[
29
]
, but in this paper we will be interested in the notion of
\beta
-acyclicity. A good overview of notions of acyclicities for hypergraphs can be found in
[
9
]
. They are particularly interesting since both CNF and BPO are tractable on
\beta
-acyclic hypergraphs.

Let
H=(V,E)
be a hypergraph.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we denote by
E(v)=\{e\in E\mid v\in e\}
the set of edges containing
v
.
A
nest point of
H
is a vertex
v\in V
such that
E(v)
is ordered by inclusion, that is, for any
e,f\in E(v)
either
e\subseteq f
or
f\subseteq e
.
H
is
\beta
-acyclic if there exists an ordering
(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})
of
V
such that for every
i\leq n
,
v_{i}
is a nest point of
H\setminus\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1}\}
, where
H\setminus W
is the hypergraph with vertex set
V\setminus W
and edge set
\{e\setminus W\mid e\in E\}
. Such ordering is called a
\beta
-elimination order
.
A hypergraph is
\beta
-acyclic
if and only if it has a
\beta
-elimination order.
For a CNF formula, the
hypergraph
H(F)
of
F
is the hypergraph whose vertices are
\mathsf{var}(F)
and edges are
\mathsf{var}(C)
for every
C\in F
.
1
1

1

We observe that the incidence graph of a CNF formula is not the same as the incidence graph of its hypergraph. Indeed, if a CNF formula has two clauses
C,D
with
\mathsf{var}(C)=\mathsf{var}(D)
, then they will give the same hyperedge in
H
while they will be distinguished in the incidence graph of
F
. However, one can observe that
C
and
D
are
modules
in
\mathsf{G_{inc}}(F)
.
A CNF formula is said to be
\beta
-acyclic if and only if
H(F)
is
\beta
-acyclic.

It has been recently proven that the BPO problem is tractable when its underlying hypergraph is
\beta
-acyclic

,
22
]
. This result is akin to known tractability for
\beta
-acyclic CNF formulas
[
13
]
. To connect both results, it is tempting to use the CNF encoding
F_{H}
from previous section. However, one can construct a
\beta
-acyclic hypergraph

where

is not
\beta
-acyclic. This can actually be seen on the example from
Figure

\beta
-acyclic since

We can nevertheless design a CNF encoding of

for

be a CNF on variables

.

For every hypergraph

and order

.

This encoding now preserves

Lemma 6
.

Let

be a

-acyclic hypergraph and

-elimination order for

. Then

Proof.

We claim that
(y_{e_{1}},\dots,y_{e_{m}},x_{v_{1}},\dots,x_{v_{n}})
is a
\beta
-elimination order of

, where

with

y_{e}
is exactly in

and

for every

. The fact that

Hence, one can eliminate

-elimination order of

4
Solving BPO using Knowledge Compilation

The complexity of finding an optimal solution for a Boolean Maximization Problem
(f,w)
given a Boolean function
f
and a weight function
w
depends on the way
f
is represented in the input.
If
f
is given in the input as a CNF formula, then deciding
w(f)=0
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard, since even deciding whether
f
is satisfiable is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. Now, if
f
is given on the input as a truth table, that is, as the list of its satisfying assignments, then it is straightforward to compute
w(f)
in polynomial time by just computing explicitly
w(\tau)
for every
\tau\in f
and return the largest value. This algorithm is polynomial in the size of the truth table, hence, computing
w(f)
in this setting is tractable.
However, one can easily observe that this algorithm is polynomial time only because the input was given in a very explicit and non-succinct manner.
The domain of Knowledge Compilation
[
35
]
has focused on the study of the representations of Boolean functions, together with their properties, and, more generally, on analysing the tradeoff between the succinctness and the tractability of representations of Boolean functions.
An overview presenting these representations and what is tractable on them can be found in the survey by Darwiche and Marquis
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the
deterministic Decomposable Negation Normal Form
representation, d-DNNF for short, a representation introduced in
[
18
]
. This data structure has two advantages for our purposes.
On the one hand, given a Boolean function
f
represented as a d-DNNF
C
and a weight function
w
, one can find an optimal solution for
(f,w)
in polynomial time in the size of
C

[
7
,
37
]
.
On the other hand, d-DNNF can succinctly encode CNF formulas having bounded incidence treewidth or
\beta
-acyclic CNF formulas
[
13
,
8
]
.

A
Negation Normal Form circuit
(NNF circuit for short)
C
on variables
X
is a labeled directed acyclic graph (DAG) such that:

•

C
has a specific node
v
called the output of
C
and denoted by
\mathsf{out}(C)
.

•

Every node of
G
of in-degree
0
is called an
input of
C
and is labeled by either
0
,
1
or a literal
\ell
over
X
.

•

Every other node of
G
is labeled by either
\wedge
or
\vee
. Let
v
be such a node and
w
be such that there is an oriented edge from
w
to
v
in
C
. We say that
w
is
an input of
v
and we denote by
\mathsf{inputs}(v)
the set of inputs of
v
.

Given a node
v
of
C
, we denote by
\mathsf{var}(v)
the subset of
X
containing the variables
y
such that there is path from an input labeled by
y
or
\neg y
to
v
. Each node of
C
computes a Boolean function
f_{v}\subseteq 2^{\mathsf{var}(v)}
inductively defined as:

•

If
v
is an input labeled by
0
(respectively
1
), then
f_{v}=\emptyset
(respectively
f_{v}=2^{\emptyset}
)

•

If
v
is an input labeled by
x
(respectively
\neg x
) then
f_{v}
is the Boolean function over
\{x\}
that contains exactly the one assignment mapping
x
to
1
(respectively to
0
)

•

If
v
is labeled by
\vee
then
\tau\in f_{v}
if and only if there exists
w\in\mathsf{inputs}(v)
such that
\tau|_{\mathsf{var}(w)}\in f_{w}
. In other words,

Theorem 4
.

Let
(f,w)
be a Boolean Maximization Problem and

a d-DNNF such that

. We can find an optimal solution
\tau^{*}\in f
for
w
in strongly polynomial time, i.e., with
O(\poly(|C|))
arithmetic operations on numbers of size
\poly(size(w))
.

Proof (sketch).

The idea is to compute inductively, for each node
v
of
C
, an optimal solution
\tau_{v}^{*}
of the Boolean Maximization Problem problem
(f_{v},w)
, that is, a solution
\tau_{v}^{*}\in f_{v}
such that
w(\tau_{v}^{*})
is maximal.
We recall that
w(\tau_{v}^{*})
is equal to
\sum_{x\in\mathsf{var}(v)}w(\tau_{v}^{*},\tau_{v}^{*}(x))
.
If
v
is a leaf labeled by literal
\ell
, then
f_{v}
contains exactly one assignment, which is hence optimal by definition.

Now let
v
be an internal node of
C
and let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
be its input. Assume that for each
v_{i}
, an optimal solution
\tau_{v_{i}}^{*}
of
(f_{v_{i}},w)
has been precomputed. If
v
is a
\wedge
-node, then we have that
f_{v}=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{k}f_{v_{i}}
, by the fact that the circuit is decomposable. It can be easily verified that
\tau^{*}_{v}:=\bigtimes_{i=1}^{k}\tau^{*}_{v_{i}}
is an optimal solution of
f_{v}
which proves the induction step in this case.
Otherwise,
v
must be a
\vee
-node and in this case,
f_{v}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}f_{v_{i}}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{var}(v)\setminus\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}
. An optimal solution
\sigma_{i}^{*}
for
\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{var}(v)\setminus\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}
can be obtained by taking
\sigma_{i}^{*}(x)=b\in\{0,1\}
if

for every

. In this case
\tau^{*}_{v_{i}}\times\sigma^{*}_{i}
is an optimal solution of
f_{v_{i}}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{var}(v)\setminus\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}
. Finally, observe that an optimal solution of
f_{v}
has to be an optimal solution for
f_{v_{i}}\times\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{var}(v)\setminus\mathsf{var}(v_{i})}
for some

\tau_{v}^{*}=\text{argmax}_{i=1}^{k}w(\tau_{v_{i}}^{*}\times\sigma_{i}^{*})
is an optimal solution of
f_{v}
.

At each gate, we have to compare at most
O(|C|)
numbers, each of size
\poly(\size(w))
, to decide which optimal solution to take. Since we are doing this at most once per gate, we have an algorithm that computes an optimal solution of
f=f_{C}
with
\poly(|C|)
arithmetic operations on number of size at most
\poly(\size(w))
.
∎

5
Beyond BPO

In this section, we show the versatility of our circuit-based approach by showing how we can use it to solve optimization problems beyond BPO.

5.1
Adding Cardinality Constraints

We start by focusing on solving BPO problems that are combined, for example, with cardinality or modulo constraints, that is, a BPO problem with additional constraints on the value of
\sum_{v\in V}x(v)
.
In this section, we will be interested in the
extended cardinality constraints
that we define as constraints of the form
\sum_{v\in V}x(v)\in S
, for some
S\subseteq[n]
where
n=|V|
.

Our approach is based on a transformation of the d-DNNF so that they only accept assignments whose number of variables set to
1
is constrained. The key result is the following one:

Theorem 9
.

Let
C
be a d-DNNF on variables
Z
and let
X\subseteq Z
with
p=|X|
. One can construct a d-DNNF
C^{\prime}
in time
O(\poly(|C|))
and of size
O(\poly(|C|))
such that there exists nodes
r_{0},\dots,r_{p}
in

with

where
B_{i}(X,Z)=\{\tau\in\{0,1\}^{Z}\mid\sum_{x\in X\cap Z}\tau(x)=i\}
for

Proof.

Recall that each node
v
of
C
computes a Boolean function
f_{v}
on
\mathsf{var}(v)
inductively defined in
Section

2
. The main idea of the proof is to construct

by introducing
p+1

for every node
v
of
C
and plug them together so that
f_{v^{(i)}}
computes
f_{v}\cap B_{i}(X,\mathsf{var}(v))
.
Observe that if
|\mathsf{var}(v)\cap X|<p

|\mathsf{var}(v)\cap X|<j\leq p
,

, so we do not really need to introduce a copy

. However, in order to minimize the number of cases to consider in the induction, we introduce
p

v
, regardless of

.

To make the transformation easier to perform, we first assume that
C
has been normalized so that for every
\vee
-node

and input

[

\wedge

We now construct from

a new circuit

of size at most

v
of
C
, there exists

in

such that

computes

. The construction of

is done by induction on the size of

.

We start with the base case where
C
contains exactly one node, which is then necessarely an input
v

is labeled by
0
,
1
,
y
or
\neg y

or by
\neg x

then we add an input node
v^{(0)}
with the same label as

0

. If
v
is an input node of
C
labeled by

, we introduce an input labeled by
x
as

and let
v^{(0)},v^{(2)},\dots,v^{(p)}
be
0

v
is an input, then

is the same as

for every

. Moreover,
|C^{\prime}|\leq p=3p^{2}|C|
in this case, which respects the induction hypothesis.

We now proceed to the inductive step. Let
v
be node of
C
that has outdegree
0
and let
C_{0}
be the circuit obtained by removing

from
C
. By induction, we can construct a circuit

of size at most
3p^{2}|C_{0}|=3p^{2}|C|-3p^{2}
, such that for every node

C_{0}
– that is for every node of
C
that is different from

, there exists
w^{(0)},\dots,w^{(p)}
in
C_{0}^{\prime}
such that
w^{(i)}
computes

. Observe that

or in

. Hence, to construct

v^{(0)},\dots,v^{(p)}
in
C_{0}^{\prime}
such that
v^{(i)}
computes
f_{v}\cap B_{i}(X,\mathsf{var}(v))
. We now explain how to do it by adding at most

We start with the case where

It is readily verified that
f_{v^{(i)}}=\bigcup_{(a,b)\mid a+b=i}f_{w_{1}^{(a)}}\times f_{w_{2}^{(b)}}

By induction, this is equal to
\bigcup_{(a,b)\mid a+b=i}(f_{w_{1}}\cap B_{a}(X,\mathsf{var}(w_{1})))\times(f_{w_{2}}\cap B_{b}(X,\mathsf{var}(w_{2}))

Moreover,

Now let

be the circuit obtained from

We explain it for the

In particular, BPO problems with cardinality or modulo constraints are tractable on

Finally, we observe that the construction from

5.2
Top-
k
for BPO

We now turn our attention to the problem of computing
k

k
.
This task is natural when there is more than one optimal solution and one wants to explore them in order to find a solution that is more suitable to the user’s needs than the one found by the solver. Formally, a top-
k
set for a BPO instance
(H,p)
is a set
K=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}\}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{V}
of size
k
such that
P_{(H,p)}(\sigma_{1})\geq\dots\geq P_{(H,p)}(\sigma_{k})
and such that for every
\sigma\notin K

P_{(H,p)}(\sigma)\leq P_{(H,p)}(\sigma_{k})
.
We observe that a top-
k
set for a BPO instance may not be unique, as two distinct
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
may have the same value under
P_{(H,p)}
. The top-
k
problem asks for outputting a top-
k
set given a BPO instance
(H,p)
and

on input.

We similarly define the top-
k
problem for a Boolean Maximization Problem

. It turns out that finding a top-

Using the weight preserving isomorphism from

5.3
Solving Binary Polynomial Optimization with Literals

The relation between BPO and Boolean Maximization Problem from
Section

4
can naturally be extended to Binary Polynomial Optimization with Literals, abbreviated BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
. In BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
, the goal is to maximize a polynomial over binary values given as a sum of monomials over literals, that is, each monomial is of the form
p(e)\prod_{v\in e}\sigma_{e}(v)
where
\sigma_{e}(v)\in\{x(v),1-x(v)\}
. An instance of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
is completely characterized by
(H,p,\sigma)
where
H=(V,E)
is a multihypergraph — that is, a hypergraph where
E
is a multiset,
p\colon E\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
is a profit function, and
\sigma=(\sigma_{e})_{e\in E}
is a family of mappings for
e\in E
that associates
v\in e
to an element in
\{x(v),1-x(v)\}
. We denote by
P_{\mathcal{H}}
the polynomial
\sum_{e\in E}p(e)\prod_{v\in e}\sigma_{e}(v)
.

Given an instance of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}

\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
, we define the Boolean function
f_{\mathcal{H}}
on variables
X=(x_{v})_{v\in V}
and
Y=(y_{e})_{e\in E}
as follows:
\tau\in\{0,1\}^{X\cup Y}
is in
f_{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if
\tau(y_{e})=\prod_{v\in e,\sigma_{e}(v)=x(v)}\tau(x_{v})\prod_{v\in e,\sigma_{e}(v)=1-x(v)}1-\tau(x_{v})
. As before, we can naturally associate a Boolean Maximization Problem instance to
\mathcal{H}
. Indeed, we define
w_{\mathcal{H}}
as before to be the weighting function defined as
w_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{v})=0
for every
v\in V
and
w_{\mathcal{H}}(y_{e})=p(e)
for every
e\in E
. Again, there is a weight preserving isomorphism between
f_{\mathcal{H}}
and
\{0,1\}^{V}
, mirroring
Lemma

1
and
Theorem

3
:

Lemma 7
.

For every
\tau\in f_{\mathcal{H}}
, we have
P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{\tau})=w_{p}(\tau)
where the mapping
x_{\tau}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
is defined as
x_{\tau}(v)=\tau(x_{v})
for every
v\in V
.

Theorem 13
.

For every instance
\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
, the set of optimal solutions of
\mathcal{H}
is equal to
\{x_{\tau^{*}}\mid\tau^{*}\text{ is an optimal solution of $(f_{\mathcal{H}},w_{p})$}\}
.

Now we use this connection and a CNF encoding of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
to compute the optimal value of structured instances of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
. As before,
f_{\mathcal{H}}
is equivalently defined as the following Boolean formula:
\bigwedge_{e\in E}y_{e}\Leftrightarrow\big(\bigwedge_{v\in e,\sigma_{e}(v)=x(v)}x_{v}\wedge\bigwedge_{v\in e,\sigma_{e}(v)=1-x(v)}\neg x_{v}\big)
which can be rewritten as
\bigwedge_{e\in E}y_{e}\Leftrightarrow\bigwedge_{v\in e}\sigma_{e}(x_{v})
where we abuse the notation
\sigma_{e}
by defining
\sigma_{e}(x_{v})=x_{v}
if
\sigma_{e}(v)=x(v)
and
\neg x_{v}
otherwise. Again,
F_{\mathcal{H}}
can be encoded as the conjunction of:

•

R^{\prime}_{e}=y_{e}\vee\bigvee_{v\in e}\neg\sigma_{e}(x_{v})
for every
e\in E
(where we replace
\neg\neg x
by
x
),

•

L^{\prime}_{e,v}=\neg y_{e}\vee\sigma_{e}(x_{v})
for every
e\in E
and
v\in e
.

Given a multihypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we define its incidence graph as for hypergraph but we have one vertex for each occurence of
e\in E
in the multiset
E
. The incidence treewidth
\mathsf{itw}(H)
of
H
is defined to be the incidence treewidth of the incidence graph of
H
. The proof of
Lemma

3
also works for multihypergraphs:

Lemma 8
.

For every instance
\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
, we have that the satisfying assignment of
F_{\mathcal{H}}
are exactly
f_{\mathcal{H}}
. Moreover,
\mathsf{itw}(F_{\mathcal{H}})\leq 2\cdot(1+\mathsf{itw}(H))
.

Similarly as before, we need an alternate encoding to handle
\beta
-acyclic instances. If
\prec
is an order on
V
, we define
F_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prec}
as the conjunction of:

•

R^{\prime}_{e}=y_{e}\vee\bigvee_{v\in e}\neg\sigma_{e}(x_{v})
for every
e\in E
(where we replace
\neg\neg x
by
x
),

•

L^{\prime}_{e,v,\prec}=\neg y_{e}\vee\sigma_{e}(x_{v})\vee\bigvee_{w\in e,v\prec w}\neg\sigma_{e}(x_{w})
for every
e\in E
and
v\in e
(where we replace
\neg\neg x
by
x
).

A multihypergraph
H=(V,E)
is said to be
\beta
-acyclic if it admits a
\beta
-elimination order. One can actually check that this is equivalent to the fact that the hypergraph

obtained by simply transforming

into a set is
\beta
-acyclic. As before, we have:

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
be an instance of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
and
\prec
be a
\beta
-elimination order for

. The satisfying assignments of

are exactly
f_{\mathcal{H}}
and
F_{\mathcal{H}}^{\prec}
is
\beta
-acyclic.

Hence
Lemmas

8
and
9
combined with
Theorems

5
and
6
allows to show that:

Theorem 14
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
be an instance of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
. If
H
is
\beta
-acyclic (resp. has incidence treewidth
t
), one can construct a d-DNNF computing
f_{\mathcal{H}}
in time
\poly(|V|,|E|)
(resp.
2^{O(t)}\poly(|V|,|E|)
) of size
\poly(|V|,|E|)
(resp.
2^{O(t)}\poly(|V|,|E|)
).

Theorem

14
with the isomorphism from
Lemma

7
allows to show the tractability of BPO

on
\beta
-acyclic and bounded incidence treewidth instances. We can actually go one step further and incorporate the techniques from

5.1
and
5.2
to get the following tractability result, which is a formalized version of the computational complexity result presented in

2
:

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=(H,\sigma,p)
with
H=(V,E)
be an instance of BPO
{}_{\text{L}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
S\subseteq[n]
where
n=|V|
. We can solve the following optimization problem
(OPT)
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{top}^{k}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{V}}P_{\mathcal{H}}(x)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}x(v)\in S

in time
\poly(k,|V|,|E|)
(resp.
2^{O(t)}\poly(k,|V|,|E|)
) if
H
is
\beta
-acyclic (resp.
H
has incidence treewidth
t
).

Proof.

We give the proof for the
\beta
-acyclic case, the bounded incidence treewidth case is completely symmetrical. We start by constructing a d-DNNF computing
f_{\mathcal{H}}
using
Theorem

14
and transform it so that it computes
f^{\prime}=f_{\mathcal{H}}\cap B_{S}(X,X\cup Y)
using
Corollary

1
. We use
Theorem

11
to compute a top-
k
set
K=\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{k}\}
for
(f^{\prime},w_{\mathcal{H}})
and we return
L=\{x_{\tau_{1}},\dots,x_{\tau_{k}}\}
where
x_{\tau}
is the isomorphism from
Lemma

7
. The whole computation is in time
\poly(k,|V|,|E|)
.

It remains to prove that
L
is indeed a solution of the optimization problem from the statement. We claim that
x_{\tau_{i}}
is a feasible point of
(OPT)
. By
Lemma

7
,
\tau_{i}(x_{v})=x_{\tau_{i}}(v)
, hence
\sum_{v\in V}x_{\tau_{i}}(v)=\sum_{v\in V}\tau_{i}(x_{v})\in S
since
\tau_{i}\in B_{S}(X,X\cup Y)
.

Now, we prove that
L
is a top-
k
set for
(OPT)
. By
Lemma

7
, we have
w_{\mathcal{H}}(\tau_{i})=P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{\tau_{i}})
. Hence
P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{\tau_{1}})\geq\dots\geq P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{\tau_{k}})
. Moreover, for any feasible point
x\notin L
of
(OPT)
, we let
\tau\in f_{\mathcal{H}}
be the unique assignment of
f_{\mathcal{H}}
such that
x=x_{\tau}
. Observe that since
x
verifies
\sum_{v\in V}\tau(x_{v})=\sum_{v\in V}x(v)\in S
, we have
\tau\in f_{H}\cap B_{S}(X,X\cup Y)
. Moreover,
\tau\notin K
since
x\notin L
. Hence we have that
w_{\mathcal{H}}(\tau)\leq w_{\mathcal{H}}(\tau_{k})
since
K
is a top-
k
set for
f_{H}\cap B_{S}(X,X\cup Y)
. By
Lemma

7
again,
w_{\mathcal{H}}(\tau)=P_{\mathcal{H}}(x)\leq w_{\mathcal{H}}(\tau_{k})=P_{\mathcal{H}}(x_{k})
, which establishes that
L
is a top-
k
set for
(OPT)
.
∎

6
Extended formulations from DNNF

Another way of efficiently solving BPO is by providing an extended formulation of polynomial size of its multilinear polytope: one can then solve the optimization problem efficiently with linear programming
[
36
,
49
]
.
Interestingly, for most known tractable classes of BPO, polynomial size extended formulations are known
[
50
,
39
,
5
,
24
,
20
,
26
]
.
In this section, we give a unifying and generalizing view of these results by proving that if the multilinear set
f_{H}
of
H
is represented as a (smooth) DNNF
C
, then there is an extended formulation of
\conv(f_{H})
of size
O(|C|)
.
In particular, this result provides extended formulations of polynomial size corresponding to all the classes of problems described in
Section

5
.

Given a set of vectors
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{V}
, the
convex hull of
P
, denoted by
\conv(P)
is defined as
\{\sum_{p\in P}\alpha_{p}p\mid\forall p\in P,\alpha_{p}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{p\in P}\alpha_{p}=1\}
. If
C
is a DNNF, we define the
convex hull of
C
to be
\conv(f_{C}^{-1}(1))
which we will denote by
\conv(C)
for short. We prove the following:

Theorem 16
.

For every smooth DNNF
C
, there exists a matrix
A_{C},G_{C}
such that
\conv(C)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}:\exists y\in\mathbb{R}^{W}s.t.A_{C}x+G_{C}y\leq b\}
. Moreover,
A_{C},G_{C}
and
b
have
O(|C|)
rows and their coefficients are in
\{-1,0,1\}
and a sparse representation can be computed in
O(|C|)
given
C
.

Extended formulations have historically been known to follow from dynamic programming algorithms. In a way, our result is similar since DNNF can be seen as some trace of a dynamic programming algorithm. Actually, Martin, Rardin and Campbell gave a very general framework in
[
42
]
that could be used on DNNF directly but the translation between both formalism is more tedious than providing an independant proof.

In this section, we hence fix DNNF
C
and normalize it as follows, wlog: we first assume that
C
is smooth since every DNNF
C^{\prime}
can be smoothed into a DNNF of size at most
|C|\times|\mathsf{var}(C)|
. Remember that a DNNF is smooth whenever for every
\vee
-gate
g
and
g^{\prime}
a child of
g
, we have
\mathsf{var}(g)=\mathsf{var}(g^{\prime})
. We also assume that the output gate of
C
, denoted by
o
, is a
\vee
-gate with no outgoing edges and that there is a path from every gate
g
of
C
to
o
. This can be ensured by iteratively removing every gate which does not satisfy this property. Finally, wlog, we assume that for every literal
\ell
, there is at most one input of
C
labeled by
\ell
. This can be easily ensured by merging several edges labeled by
\ell
. We denote by
\mathsf{edges}_{\ell}(C)
the edges going out of the only input labeled by
\ell
. We associate a system of linear inequality constraints to every NNF circuit
C
as follows. We will use the following variables:

•

A variable
x_{v}
for each
v\in V
,

•

A variable
y_{e}
for each
e\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
.

In the rest of this section, we use the following notations: for a gate
g
of
C
, we denote by
\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)
the set of edges going in
g
and by
\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)
the set of edges going out of
g
. We simlarly denote by
\in[g]
the set of inputs of
g
, that is, the gates
h
such that
(h,g)
is an edge of
C
and by
\mathsf{out}(g)
the set of outputs of
g
, that is, the gates
h
such that
(g,h)
is an edge of
C
.

We define
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
as the following system of linear constraints in
y
variables:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(o)}y_{e}

\displaystyle=1,

(2a)

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}y_{e}-\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}

\displaystyle=0

for every
\vee
-gate
g\neq o
,

(2b)

\displaystyle y_{e}-\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}

\displaystyle=0

for every
\wedge
-gate
g
and
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)

(2c)

\displaystyle y_{e}

\displaystyle\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for every $e\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$}.

(2d)

We then define
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
as the polyhedron:

\mathcal{P}_{y}(C):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}:\text{$y$ satisfies $\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)$}\},

Now let
\mathcal{S}_{x,y}(C)
be the system of linear constraints in
x,y
variables obtained by augmenting
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
with the following constraints linking
x
and
y
variables:

\displaystyle x_{v}-\sum_{e\in\mathsf{edges}_{v}(C)}y_{e}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ for every }v\in V,

(3)

Let
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
be the polyhedron:

\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}:\text{$(x,y)$ satisfies $\mathcal{S}_{x,y}(C)$}\}.

Theorem

16
directly follows from
Lemma

10
which establishes a connection between the projection of integral solutions of
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
onto
x
variables and
f_{C}^{-1}(1)
and
Lemma

11
which shows that
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
is integral.

We start by connection
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
and
f_{C}^{-1}(1)
together:

Lemma 10
.

The projection of
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
onto
(x_{v})_{v\in V}
coincides with
f_{C}^{-1}(1)
.

Certificates.

The proof of
Lemma

10
is based on the notion of certificates. Intuitively, we prove that the solution of
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
can be seen as some kind of fractional relaxation of the notion of certificate. A certificate is a witness in the circuit that a Boolean assignment is a solution. Formally, a
certificate of
C
is a subset
T
of gates of
C
such that the output of
C
is in
T
and such that:

•

for every
\vee
-gate
g
of
T
, exactly one input of
g
is in
T
,

•

for every
\wedge
-gate of
g
of
T
, every input of
g
is in
T
,

•

for every
g
in
T
that is not the output of
C
,
g
is the input of at least one gate in
T
.

We denote by
\cert(C)
the set of certificate of
C
. In DNNF, it is not hard to see that certificates are trees directed toward the output of the circuit, that is, for each gate
g
in
T
, there is at least one
g^{\prime}
in
T
such that
(g,g^{\prime})
is an edge. Moreover, they are related to solutions in
C
as follows (see
[
12
, Section 6.1.1]
for details). If
C
is a smooth DNNF, one can easily check that for every certificate
T
and every variable
v\in V
, there is exactly one input gate labeled with a literal involving variable

(that is, either there is exactly one input labeled
v
or exactly one input labeled

). For a certificate
T
, we denote by
\tau_{T}
the assignment defined as
\tau_{T}(v)=1
if an input labeled by

is in

and
\tau_{T}(v)=0
if an input labeled by

is in

. We have that

is the set of solutions of

.

Proof of

.

We start by proving that the projection of
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
onto

coincides with
f_{C}^{-1}(1)
. To do so, we will show that
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
naturally corresponds to

.
More formally, given

, we let
(\mathbf{y}^{T},\mathbf{x}^{T})
to be the point such that for every

if

and

y_{e}^{T}=0
otherwise. Moreover, we let
x_{v}^{T}=\tau_{T}(v)
. We claim that a point
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
is in
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
if and only if there exists a certificate

such that
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{y}^{T},\mathbf{x}^{T})
. Given a certificate
T
, showing that
(\mathbf{y}^{T},\mathbf{x}^{T})\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
is a simple check that every constraint in
\mathcal{S}_{x,y}(C)
are satisfied. All constraints are straightforward to check but the one from

3
. Let
v\in V
.
By definition,
x_{v}^{T}=\tau_{T}(v)
, that is,
x_{v}^{T}
is set to

if and only if an input gate labeled with literal

appears in

and
0
otherwise. First, assume that literal
v
does not appear in
T
. Then
y^{T}_{e}=0
for every

and
\tau_{T}(v)=0
by definition. Hence
x^{T}_{v}=\tau_{T}(v)=\sum_{e\in\mathsf{edges}_{v}(C)}y^{T}_{e}
.
Now if literal

appears in
T
, we have to show that there is exactly one edge

such that

. There is obviously at least one by definition of certificates. The uniqueness follows from the fact that certificates are trees directed to the output of the DNNF.

For the other way around, consider
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}
. First, observe that for every gate
g
and edge

, by (
2b
) if

is a
\vee
-gate or by (

) if

y_{e}\leq\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}.

(4)

We now prove that for every gate

\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}\in\{0,1\}
. This can be proven by induction on the depth of

, that is, the length of the longest path from

to

. If the depth of

is

, it means that

is an edge of

and is the only edge going out of

. Indeed, if there is another edge
(g,g^{\prime})
in
\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)
, then since there is a path from

to

, there would be a path from

to

, contradicting the fact that

has depth

. In other words,
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}=y_{e}
. Now by (

),
y_{e}\leq 1
which implies
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}=y_{e}\in\{0,1\}
since

is an integer. Now assume that the induction hypothesis holds for every gate of depth at most

be a gate of depth

. Clearly,

has depth at most
d
, hence the induction hypothesis holds. In other words,
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g^{\prime})}y_{f}\in\{0,1\}
. But,
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g^{\prime})
, hence by (

), we have
y_{e}\leq\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g^{\prime})}y_{f}
. In other words,
y_{e}\in\{0,1\}
. It remains to show that there is at most one edge

. By (
4
) and by induction, if a gate
g^{\prime}
with depth at most
d
has an incoming edge
f
with
y_{f}=1
, then it has an outgoing edge
f^{\prime}
with
y_{f^{\prime}}=1
. Hence, from
g
, we can define two paths going toward the output
o
, one starting with
e
and the other with
e^{\prime}
, and both following only edges
f
with
y_{f}=1
. Both paths have to meet at some gate
g^{\prime\prime}
, with two incoming edges
e_{1}=(g_{1},g^{\prime\prime})
and
e_{2}=(g_{2},g^{\prime\prime})
with
y_{e_{1}}=y_{e_{2}}=1
. Now observe that
g^{\prime\prime}
cannot be a
\wedge
-gate, otherwise both input of
g^{\prime\prime}
would share a variable (through the subcircuit rooted in
g
), contradicting the decomposability assumption. Hence
g^{\prime\prime}
is a
\vee
-gate having of depth at most
d
and having two incoming edges with
y_{e_{1}}=y_{e_{2}}=1
. But by (
2b
), it would imply
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g^{\prime\prime})}y_{f}\geq 2
, which contradicts the induction hypothesis if
g^{\prime\prime}
is not the output of
C
. If
g^{\prime\prime}=o
, it violates (
2a
).
Hence
g
has at most one outgoing edge
e
with
y_{e}=1
which establishes the induction at depth
d+1
.

We have established that for every
g
,
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}\in\{0,1\}
. One direct consequence is that
y_{e}\in\{0,1\}
for every edge
e
and
x_{v}\in\{0,1\}
for every
v\in V
by (
3
). Now, consider the subset
T
of gates in
C
defined as
g\in T
if there is some edge
e
of
C
containing
g
and such that
y_{e}=1
. We prove that
T
is a certificate of
C
which would directly establish that
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{y}^{T},\mathbf{x}^{T})
. It is enough to check that every condition of certificate are respected by
T
. First, let
g
be a
\vee
-gate in
T
. By definition, it is in at least one edge
e
with
y_{e}=1
. By (
2b
),
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}y_{f}=\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}
and by what precedes the RHS of this equality must be equal to
1
. In other words, there is exactly one ingoing edge
e_{1}
in
g
and exactly one outgoing edge
e_{2}
from
g
such that
y_{e_{1}}=y_{e_{2}}=1
. This proves the first condition of certificate.
Now, if
g
is a
\wedge
-gate, again, at most one edge
e
containing
g
is such that
y_{e}=1
by definition of
T
. From what precedes, we hence must have
\sum_{f\in\mathsf{out_{E}}(g)}y_{f}=1
. Hence, by (
2c
), every ingoing edge
e
of
g
is such that
y_{e}=1
. It remains to show that the third condition holds. Assume toward a contradiction that there is a gate
g
such that there is an edge
e=(g^{\prime},g)
with
y_{e}=1
but such that for every outgoing edge
e^{\prime}=(g,g^{\prime\prime})
,
y_{e^{\prime}}=0
. It directly contradicts (
4
). Hence the third condition of certificate is respected which concludes the proof.
∎

We now show that
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
is integral.

Lemma 11
.

The polyhedron
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
is integral.

We prove
Lemma

11
by proving that
P_{y}(C)
is integral. This will be enough thanks to the following observation:

Lemma 12
.

Let
C
be a NNF circuit.
If
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
is integral, then so is
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be a vertex of
\mathcal{P}_{x,y}(C)
.
Then there exist
n_{x}+n_{y}
linearly independent constraints among
\mathcal{S}_{x,y}(C)
such that
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is the unique vector that satisfies these
n_{x}+n_{y}
constraints at equality.
Note that constraints (
3
) are the only ones containing variables
x
, and each such constraint contains exactly one
x
variable with nonzero coefficient.
Thus,
n_{x}
of the linearly independent constraints defining
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
must be (
3
).
The remaining
n_{y}
linearly independent constraints defining
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
must be in
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
, and these constraints only involve
y
variables.
Hence,
\mathbf{y}
is a vertex of
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
.
∎

To show that
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
is an integral polyhedron we use total dual integrality.
A rational system of linear inequalities
Ay=b
,
y\geq 0
is
totally dual integral
, abbreviated
TDI,
if the minimum in the LP-duality equation

\max\{c^{\mathsf{T}}y:Ay=b,\ y\geq 0\}=\min\{z^{\mathsf{T}}b:zA\geq c\}

has an integral optimum solution
z
for each integral vector
c
for which the minimum is finite.

Theorem 17
.

Let
C
be a DNNF circuit.
Then, the system
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
is TDI and the polyhedron
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
is integral.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that the system
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
is TDI.
In fact, since the right-hand-side is integral, it then follows from
[
28
]
(see also Corollary 22.1c in
[
46
]
) that
\mathcal{P}_{y}(C)
is integral.
Hence, in the remainder of the proof we show that the system
\mathcal{S}_{y}(C)
is TDI.

Dual problem.

Let
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathsf{edges}(C)}
.
To write the dual of the LP problem
\max\{c^{\mathsf{T}}y:y\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{S}_{y}\}
, we associate variable
z_{o}
to constraint (
2a
),
variables
z_{g}
, for every
\vee
-gate
g
, to constraints (
2b
),
variables
z_{g,e}
, for every
\wedge
-gate
g
and
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)
, to constraints (
2c
).
It will be useful to define, for every gate
g
,
\mathsf{z}(g)
as the set of
z
variables corresponding to
g
.
Note that sets
\mathsf{z}(g)
, for every gate
g
, partition all the
z
variables.
Furthermore, if
g
is an input gate we have
\mathsf{z}(g)=\emptyset
, if
g
is a
\vee
-gate we have
\mathsf{z}(g)=\{z_{g}\}
, and if
g
is a
\wedge
-gate we have
\mathsf{z}(g)=\{z_{g,e}:e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)\}
.
The dual of the LP problem
\max\{c^{\mathsf{T}}y:y\text{ satisfies }\mathcal{S}_{y}\}
is

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle z_{o}

s.t.

\displaystyle z_{h}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g
input,
h

\vee
-gate,

\displaystyle z_{h,e}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g
input,
h

\wedge
-gate,

\displaystyle-z_{g}+z_{h}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g

\vee
-gate,
h

\vee
-gate,

\displaystyle-z_{g}+z_{h,e}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g

\vee
-gate,
h

\wedge
-gate,

\displaystyle-\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z_{g,f}+z_{h}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g

\wedge
-gate,
h

\vee
-gate,

\displaystyle-\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z_{g,f}+z_{h,e}

\displaystyle\geq c_{e}

\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
with
g

\wedge
-gate,
h

\wedge
-gate.

Algorithm.

Next, we give an algorithm that, as we will show later, constructs an optimal solution to the dual which is integral.
The algorithm recursively assigns values to the variables starting from the inputs, and proceeding towards the output.
One variable is assigned its value only when all variables of its inputs have already been assigned.
More precisely, a variable in
\mathsf{z}(h)
is assigned its value only when all variables
\mathsf{z}(g)
, for each
g\in\mathsf{in}(h)
have already been assigned.

At the very beginning, the algorithm considers the gates
h
such that
\mathsf{in}(h)
contains only input gates, since
g
is an input gate if and only if
\mathsf{z}(g)=\emptyset
.
As a warm up, we describe the algorithm in this simpler case.
If
h
is a
\vee
-gate, there can be several constraints in the dual lower-bounding
z_{h}
:

\displaystyle z_{h}\geq c_{e}

\forall e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)
.

Thus we assign
z^{*}_{h}:=\max\{c_{e}:e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)\}
.
If
h
is a
\wedge
-gate, there is exactly one constraint in the dual lower-bounding each variable
z_{h,e}
, for
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)
:

\displaystyle z_{h,e}\geq c_{e}.

Thus we assign
z^{*}_{h,e}:=c_{e}
for each
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)
.

Next, we describe the general iteration of the algorithm.
Let
h
be a gate such that, for each
g\in\mathsf{in}(h)
, the variables in
\mathsf{z}(g)
have already been assigned.
Consider first the case where
h
is a
\vee
-gate.
The constraints in the dual lower-bounding
z_{h}
are:

\displaystyle\begin{split}z_{h}\geq c_{e}&\qquad\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ input,}\\
z_{h}\geq c_{e}+z_{g}&\qquad\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ $\vee$-gate,}\\
z_{h}\geq c_{e}+\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z_{g,f}&\qquad\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ $\wedge$-gate.}\end{split}

(5)

Note that all variables on the right-hand of the above constraints have already been assigned by the algorithm.
Thus we assign
z^{*}_{h}
as follows:

\displaystyle z^{*}_{h}:=\max\begin{Bmatrix}[l]c_{e}&\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ input,}\\
c_{e}+z^{*}_{g}&\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ $\vee$-gate,}\\
c_{e}+\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z^{*}_{g,e}&\text{$\forall e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)$ with $g$ $\wedge$-gate}\end{Bmatrix}.

(6)

Next, consider the case where
h
is a
\wedge
-gate, and let
e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)
.
There is exactly one constraint in the dual lower-bounding
z_{h,e}
, which is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}z_{h,e}\geq c_{e}&\qquad\text{if $g$ input,}\\
z_{h,e}\geq c_{e}+z_{g}&\qquad\text{if $g$ $\vee$-gate,}\\
z_{h,e}\geq c_{e}+\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z_{g,f}&\qquad\text{if $g$ $\wedge$-gate.}\end{split}

(7)

Also in this case, all variables on the right-hand of the above constraints have already been assigned by the algorithm.
Thus we assign
z^{*}_{h,e}
as follows:

\displaystyle z^{*}_{h,e}:=\begin{cases}c_{e}&\qquad\text{if $g$ input,}\\
c_{e}+z^{*}_{g}&\qquad\text{if $g$ $\vee$-gate,}\\
c_{e}+\sum_{f\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(g)}z^{*}_{f,e}&\qquad\text{if $g$ $\wedge$-gate}.\end{cases}

(8)

This concludes the description of the algorthm. Note that, since
c
is integral,
z^{*}
is integral.

Termination.

Our algorithm assigns a value to each variable.
This follows from the structure of the directed graph
C
, since
C
is acyclic and the inputs of
C
are precisely the gates of
C
that have no ingoing edge. Moreover
C
is a connected graph since we assumed there is a path from every gate
g
to the output
o
.

Feasibility.

It is simple to see from the definition of our algorithm that the solution
z^{*}
constructed is feasible to the dual.
In fact, in each step, the component of
z^{*}
considered is defined in a way that it satisfies all inequalities lower-bounding it.
On top of that, each constraint in the dual is considered at some point in the algorithm.

Optimality.

To prove that the solution
z^{*}
constructed by the algorithm is optimal to the dual, we show that any
z
feasible for the dual must have objective value greater than or equal to that of
z^{*}
, which is
z^{*}_{o}
.
To do so, we give a procedure that selects a number of constraints of the dual.
Summing together the selected constraints yields the inequality
z_{o}\geq z^{*}_{o}
.
We remark that the procedure is strongly connected to the concept of certificates.

The procedure is recursive and starts from the gate
o
, which in particular is a
\vee
gate. We give the general recursive construction for a gate
h
.

•

If
h
is an input gate, there is nothing to do.

•

If
h
is a
\vee
gate, we select one constraint among (
5
) that achieves the maximum in (
6
).
This essentially amounts to selecting an edge
e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
, and therefore a new gate
g
.
We then apply recursively the construction to the gate
g
.

•

If
h
is a
\wedge
gate, we select, for every
e\in\mathsf{in_{E}}(h)
, the one constraint (
7
).
This essentially amounts to selecting all edges
e=(g,h)\in\mathsf{edges}(C)
, and therefore all new gates
g\in\mathsf{in}(h)
.
We then apply recursively the construction to all the gates
g\in\mathsf{in}(h)
.

It is simple to check that summing all the inequalities selected by the above recursive procedure yields
z_{o}\geq z^{*}_{o}
.
∎

.

A common way of showing integrality is by showing that the matrix of the system is totally unimodular. This method would fail in our case however and this is why we resorted to TDI. Figure

Appendix A
Graph Theoretical Lemmas

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
. The
edge subdivision of
G
along
E^{\prime}
is a graph
sub(G,E^{\prime})
obtained from
G
by splitting every edge of
E^{\prime}
in two. More formally,
sub(G,E^{\prime})=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime\prime})
where
V^{\prime}=V\cup\{v_{e}\mid e\in E^{\prime}\}
and
E^{\prime\prime}=(E\setminus E^{\prime})\cup\{\{v,v_{e}\}\mid e\in E^{\prime},v\in e\}
. One can easily see that splitting edges does not change the treewidth of
G
:

Lemma 13
.

For every graph
G=(V,E)
,
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
and
G^{\prime}=sub(G,E^{\prime})
. We have
\mathsf{tw}(G^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{tw}(G)
.

Proof.

If
G
is a tree, then it is clear that
G^{\prime}
is also a tree and automatically we have that
\mathsf{tw}(G)=\mathsf{tw}(G^{\prime})=1
.
Otherwise let
T
be a tree decomposition of
G
of width
k>1
. We transform it into a decomposition
T^{\prime}
of
G^{\prime}
as follows: for every

e=\{u,v\}\in E^{\prime}
, let
B_{e}
be a bag of
T
such that
e\subseteq B_{e}
(note that it exists by definition of tree decomposition).
In
T^{\prime}
, we attach a new bag
B=\{u,v,v_{e}\}
to
B(e)
, for all
e\in E^{\prime}
.
This is clearly a tree decomposition of
G^{\prime}
and its width is the same as the width of
T
since we only added bags of size
3
and the treewidth of
T
is bigger than
1
.

∎

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
For
u\in V
, the
neighborhood of
u
is defined as
N_{G}(u)=\bigcup_{e\in E,u\in e}e
.
A
module
M\subseteq V
of
G
is a set of vertices such that for every
u,v\in M
,
N_{G}(u)\setminus M=N_{G}(v)\setminus M
, that is, every vertex of
M
has the same neighborhood outside of
M
. We denote by
N^{*}_{G}(M)
this set. A
partition of
G
into modules
is a partition
\mathcal{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{k})
of
V
where
M_{i}
is a module of
V
(possibly of size
1
). The
modular contraction of
G
wrt
\mathcal{M}
, denoted by
G/\mathcal{M}
, is the graph whose vertices are
\mathcal{M}
and such that there is an edge between
M_{i}
and
M_{j}
if and only if
N^{*}_{G}(M)\cap M^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
(observe that in this case
M^{\prime}\subseteq N^{*}_{G}(M)
and
M\subseteq N^{*}_{G}(M^{\prime})
). While it is known that the treewidth of a modular contraction of
G
might decrease it arbitrarily
[
43
]
, we can bound it as follows:

Lemma 14
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\dots,M_{k}\}
a partition of
G
into modules and
m=\max_{i=1}^{k}|M_{i}|
. Then
\mathsf{tw}(G)\leq m\times(1+\mathsf{tw}(G/\mathcal{M}))-1
.

Proof.

We prove it by transforming any tree decomposition
T
of
G/\mathcal{M}
of width
k
into a tree decomposition of
G
of width at most
(k+1)m-1
. To do that, we construct
T^{\prime}
by replacing every occurrence of
M_{i}
in
T
by the vertices of
M_{i}
. If
B
is a bag of
T
, we denote by
B^{\prime}
its corresponding bag in
T^{\prime}
. Each bag
B^{\prime}
hence contains at most
(\max|M_{i}|)\cdot(k+1)=m(k+1)
and the bound on the width of
T^{\prime}
follows. It remains to prove that
T^{\prime}
is indeed a tree decomposition of
G
.

We start by proving that every edge of
G
is covered in
T^{\prime}
. Let
\{u,v\}
be an edge of
G
and
M_{u},M_{v}\in\mathcal{M}
be such that
u\in M_{u}
and
v\in M_{v}
. If
M_{u}=M_{v}
then edge
\{u,v\}
is covered by
B^{\prime}
in
T^{\prime}
for any bag
B
of
T
that contains
M_{u}
. Otherwise, since
\{u,v\}
is an edge of
G
, we have
u\in N^{*}_{G}(M_{v})
. Hence
\{M_{u},M_{v}\}
is an edge
G/\mathcal{M}
. Hence there is a bag
B
of
T
such that
M_{u},M_{v}\in B
. Hence edge
\{u,v\}
is covered in
T^{\prime}
by
B^{\prime}
.

Finally, we prove that every vertex of
G
is connected in
T^{\prime}
. Indeed, let
u
be a vertex of
G
such that
u\in B_{1}^{\prime}\cap B_{2}^{\prime}
. It means that there exists
M_{1}\in B_{1}
and
M_{2}\in B_{2}
such that
u\in M_{1}
and
u\in M_{2}
. Now, by definition,
u
is in exactly one module, that is,
M_{1}=M_{2}=M
. Hence, by connectedness of
T
,
M
is in every bag on the path from
B_{1}
to
B_{2}
in
T
which means that
u
is in every bag on the path from
B_{1}^{\prime}
to
B_{2}^{\prime}
.
∎
