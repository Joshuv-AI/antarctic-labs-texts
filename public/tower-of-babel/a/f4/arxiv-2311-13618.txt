1.
Introduction

In the last 1980s, Bank and Laine
[
2
,
3
]
initiated the study on the complex oscillation of the second order differential equation

(1.1)

f^{\prime\prime}+Af=0,

where
A
is an entire function, under the framework of Nevanlinna theory. See
[
10
]
for the standard notation and fundamental results of Nevanlinna theory.
Recall that, for a nonconstant entire function
f
, the
order

\rho(f)
of
f
is defined as

\rho(f)=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\log|f(z)|}{\log|z|}

and the
exponent of convergence

\lambda(f)
of zeros of
f
is defined as

\lambda(f)=\inf\left\{\lambda:\sum_{z\not=0:f(z)=0}m(z)|z|^{-\lambda}<\infty\right\}=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log n(r,0,f)}{\log r},

where
m(z)
is the multiplicity of the zero
z
and
n(r,0,f)=\sum_{|z|\leq r}m(z)
denotes the number of zeros of
f
in the disc
\{z:|z|\leq r\}
. It is elementary that all nontrivial solutions of equation (
1.1
) are entire functions. When
A
is a transcendental entire function of finite order, an application of the lemma on the logarithmic derivative
[
10
, Section 2.2]
easily yields that all nontrivial solutions of (
1.1
) satisfy
\rho(f)=\infty
. However, there are examples of
A
(say
A(z)=e^{z}-q^{2}/16
for an odd integer
q
) such that the product
E=f_{1}f_{2}
of two linearly independent solutions of (
1.1
) satisfies
\lambda(E)<\infty
or even
\lambda(E)=0

[
12
, Theorem 5.22]
. Generally, we have the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
E
be the product of two linearly independent solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of the differential equation (
1.1
). Then

(1)

if
\rho(A)\not\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\lambda(E)\geq\rho(A)
;

(2)

if
\rho(A)\leq 1/2
, then
\lambda(E)=\infty
.

These results were proved by Bank and Laine in
[
2
,
3
]
, except for the case
\rho(A)=1/2
in (2) which was prove by Shen
[
16
]
and Rossi
[
15
]
independently. Shen
[
16
]
and Rossi
[
15
]
actually showed that if
1/2\leq\rho(A)<1
, then the conclusion
\lambda(E)\geq\rho(A)
can be improved to satisfy the inequality

(1.2)

\frac{1}{\rho(A)}+\frac{1}{\lambda(E)}\leq 2.

A problem left by their work–known as
the Bank–Laine conjecture
–is whether
\lambda(E)=\infty
whenever
\rho(A)\not\in\mathbb{N}
. This conjecture has attracted much interest; see the surveys
[
8
,
13
]
and references therein. Bergweiler and Eremenko
[
4
,
5
]
disproved this conjecture by using the method of quasiconformal surgery to construct, for each
\rho\in(1/2,\infty)
, entire function
A
such that
\rho(A)=\rho
and the differential equation (
1.1
) has two linearly independent solutions
f_{1},f_{2}
such that
\lambda(E)=\rho(E)<\infty
and one of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
has no zeros. We refer to
[
1
,
14
]
for the basic results of quasiconformal mapping theory.

An entire function
E
is called a
Bank–Laine function
if
E(z)=0
implies that
E^{\prime}(z)\in\{-1,1\}
. In particular,
E
is called
special
if
E(z)=0
implies that
E^{\prime}(z)=1
. For two linearly independent solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of (
1.1
), if the
Wronskian determinant

W(f_{1},f_{2})=f_{1}f_{2}^{\prime}-f_{1}^{\prime}f_{2}=1
, then we say that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are
normalized
. A Bank–Laine function
E
can be written as
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of the second order differential equation (
1.1
); see
[
12
, Proposition 6.4]
. Most study on the Bank–Laine conjecture has concentrated on constructing Bank–Laine functions with certain properties. All Bank–Laine functions have order at least
1
by Theorem
1.1
and the inequality (
1.2
). Bergweiler and Eremenko’s construction in
[
4
]
shows for the first time that there exists a special Bank–Laine function
E
such that
\rho(E)
is not an integer order or half-integer order.

For two linearly independent solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of equation (
1.1
), it is well known that the ratio
F=f_{2}/f_{1}
satisfies the
Schwarzian differential equation

(1.3)

\begin{split}S(F):=\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}\right)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}\right)^{2}=\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}\right)^{2}=2A,\end{split}

where the expression
S(F)
is called the
Schwarzian derivative
of
F
. Then
F
is a locally univalent meromorphic function and the Schwarzian derivative is invariant under the linear fractional transformation of
F
. This implies a one-to-one correspondence between the equivalence class of locally univalent meromorphic functions and entire function. On the other hand, a normalized pair
(f_{1},f_{2})
of solutions of (
1.1
) can be recovered from
F
by the formulas
f_{1}^{2}=1/F^{\prime}
and
f_{2}^{2}=F^{2}/F^{\prime}
. The quotient
F/F^{\prime}=f_{1}f_{2}=E
is then a Bank–Laine function and all Bank–Laine functions arise in this way. Zeros of a Bank–Laine function
E
are zeros and poles of
F
respectively, and
E
is special if and only if
F
is entire. By gluing different locally univalent meromorphic functions together using quasiconformal surgery, Bergweiler and Eremenko then recover the desired
F
and also the corresponding coefficient
A
from equation (
1.3
). In
[
19
]
, the present author completed Bergweiler and Eremenko’s construction in
[
5
]
and, in particular, prove the following

Theorem 1.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\lambda\in(1,\infty)
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. Then there exists a Bank–Laine function
E
such that
\lambda(E)=\rho(E)=\lambda
and
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two entire functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
such that
\lambda(f_{1})=\delta\lambda
and
\lambda(f_{2})=\lambda
.

The construction in
[
19
]
have started from a family of locally univalent meromorphic functions
F=f_{2}/f_{1}
with
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
being two normalized solutions such that
\lambda(f_{i})<\infty
,
i=1,2
, of the second order differential equation

f^{\prime\prime}-\left[\frac{1}{4}e^{2z}+\left(\frac{k_{2}-k_{1}}{2}\right)e^{z}+\frac{(k_{1}+k_{2}+1)^{2}}{4}\right]f=0,

where
k_{1},k_{2}
are two nonnegative integers (see also
[
6
,
21
,
20
,
18
]
). This equation admits two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
in the form

\begin{split}f_{1}=\kappa_{1}e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}a_{i}e^{iz}\right)e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{-\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}f_{2}=\kappa_{2}e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k_{2}}b_{j}e^{jz}\right)e^{-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}+1}{2}z}e^{\frac{1}{2}e^{z}}\end{split}

with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}a_{i}=\frac{k_{2}!}{(i+k_{2})!}a_{0},&\quad i=1,\cdots,k_{1},\\
b_{j}=\frac{(-1)^{j}k_{1}!}{(j+k_{1})!}b_{0},&\quad j=1,\cdots,k_{2}\end{array}\right.

and
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
are two constants such that
a_{k_{1}}b_{k_{2}}=\frac{k_{1}!k_{2}!}{[(k_{1}+k_{2})!]^{2}}a_{0}b_{0}=1
. Let

\begin{split}F=\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{k_{2}}b_{j}e^{jz}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}a_{i}e^{iz}}\right)e^{e^{z}}.\end{split}

We choose
a_{0}=b_{0}
. Let
A_{0}=B_{0}=1
and
A_{i}=a_{i}/a_{0}
and
B_{j}=b_{j}/b_{0}
. For every pair
(m,2n)
of two nonnegative integers
m
and
n
, we consider the rational function

(1.4)

\begin{split}R_{m,n}(z)=\frac{P_{n}(z)}{Q_{m}(z)}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}z^{j}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}z^{i}}.\end{split}

Thus we may write

\begin{split}h_{m,n}(z)=R_{m,n}(z)e^{z}=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}z^{j}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}z^{i}}\right)e^{z}.\end{split}

It follows that
A_{m}B_{2n}=\frac{m!(2n)!}{[(m+2n)!]^{2}}=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}
.
Then the meromorphic (entire when
m=0
) function

(1.5)

\begin{split}g_{m,n}(z)=h_{m,n}(e^{z})=\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n}B_{j}e^{jz}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iz}}\right)e^{e^{z}}\end{split}

satisfies

(1.6)

\begin{split}g^{\prime}_{m,n}(z)=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}\frac{e^{e^{z}+(m+2n+1)z}}{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}A_{i}e^{iz}\right)^{2}}.\end{split}

Since
A_{j}
are all positive numbers,
g^{\prime}_{m,n}(z)\not=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
g_{m,n}
is increasing on
\mathbb{R}
, and satisfies
g_{m,n}(x)\to 1
as
x\to-\infty
as well as
g_{m,n}(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
. By the recursive formulas for
A_{i}
, we see that if
n\geq 1
, then
A_{1}+A_{2}+\cdots+A_{m}<1
for all
m\geq 1
, which implies that
g_{m,n}
has no poles on the imaginary axis. If
n=0
and
m\geq 1
, it is possible that
g_{m,n}
has some poles on the imaginary axis. Bergweiler and Eremenko’s glued the functions
g_{m,n}
in (
1.5
) with
m=0
and two or infinitely many different even numbers
2n
. Our construction in
[
19
]
considered the generic choice of
m
and
n
. For the underlying ideas behind the gluing, see
[
5
, Section 2]
.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, for each number
\rho\in(1/2,1)
, we let
\gamma=1/(2\rho-1)
. In
[
19
]
, the author actually proved that, for every
\delta\in[0,1]
, there exists an entire function
A
of order
\rho(A)=\rho
such that the differential equation (
1.1
) has two linearly independent solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
such that
E=f_{1}f_{2}
satisfies
\lambda(E)=\rho\gamma
and
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
satisfy

\lambda(f_{1})=\delta\rho\gamma
and
\lambda(f_{2})=\rho\gamma
, respectively. In particular, when
\delta=0
the function
f_{1}
can be chosen to have no zeros so that the associated Bank–Laine function
E
is special. Theorem
1.2
completes the construction in
[
5
, Corollary 1.1]
. As pointed out in
[
19
]
, we can extend the results to the case that
\rho\in(n/2,n)
for any positive integer
n
by following exactly the same process as in
[
5
, Section 5]
, thus completing the construction in
[
5
, Corollary 1.2]
; see also the comments in Subsection
3.5
and Subsection
4.3
. Unfortunately, the proof of Theorem
1.2
in
[
19
]
is not complete because the proof for the two lemmas in
[
19
, Lemma 2.1 and Lemma 2.2]
contains gaps in both of the two cases
\gamma=1
and
\gamma>1
. One of the purposes of this paper is to fill these gaps. The case
\gamma=1
will be included in Theorem
1.4
below and the corrected versions of the two lemmas will be provided in Section
4
.

We note that, when
\delta=1
and
\gamma>1
, the zeros of the function
f_{1}
constructed in Theorem
1.2
actually satisfies

(1.7)

c_{2}(\log r)^{-2}r^{\lambda}\leq n(r,0,f_{1})\leq c_{1}(\log r)^{-2}r^{\lambda}

for two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
and all large
r
. Thus
f_{1}
has less number of zeros than
f_{2}
. It seems that the method in
[
19
]
does not allow to remove the term
(\log r)^{-2}
in (
1.7
). For two positive quantities
S_{1}(r)
and
S_{2}(r)
, we say that
S_{1}(r)\asymp S_{2}(r)
, provided that there are two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that
S_{1}(r)
and
S_{2}(r)
satisfy
c_{2}S_{2}(r)\leq S_{1}(r)\leq c_{1}S_{2}(r)
for all large
r
. As a complement to Theorem
1.2
, we shall first consider gluing two locally univalent meromorphic functions
g_{m,n}
in (
1.5
) along the real axis as in
[
4
]
and prove the following

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
M
and
N
be two positive integers such that
M\not=N
. Then there exists an entire function
A
of order

\rho=1+\frac{\log^{2}(M/N)}{4\pi^{2}}

such that the associated Bank–Laine function
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of (
1.1
) satisfies
\lambda(E)=\rho(E)=\rho
and the number of zeros of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
satisfy
n(r,0,f_{1})\asymp n(r,0,f_{2})
.

By the proof of Theorem
1.3
, we know that the zeros of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
actually satisfy
n(r,0,f_{i})\asymp r^{1/\text{Re}(\mu)}(\log r)
,
i=1,2
. Moreover, if we assume that
M=N
, then the term
\log r
here can be removed. We remark that we can choose
M
and
N
suitably so that
f_{1}
has no zeros and thus the corresponding Bank–Laine function
E
is special. We may even choose
M
and
N
suitably so that
f_{2}
has no zeros.

By looking at the results in Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, the existence of Bank–Laine functions in the case
\rho(A)
is an integer is still not clear. Langley
[
11
]
pointed out, using
Lagrange interpolation
, we can make Bank–Laine functions
E=Pe^{Q}
with
P,Q
being polynomials. However, it is not known if there exist Bank–Laine functions
E
such that
\lambda(E)=\lambda<\rho(E)=n
for any preassigned number
\lambda
and positive integer
n
. Moreover, all the constructions now yield Bank–Laine functions such that
\rho(E)=\rho(A)
, but it is not known if there exist Bank–Laine functions such that
\lambda(E)>\rho(A)
. It seems that the method of quasiconformal surgery in
[
5
, Subsection 5.3]
is invalid when dealing with these two cases, as will be explained in Section
3
. In this paper we shall describe a new method of quasiconformal surgery for constructions of such kind of Bank–Laine functions. Concerning the first question, we shall prove the following

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integer and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in[0,n]
be two numbers such that
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
. Then there exists an entire function
A
of order
n
such that the associated Bank–Laine function
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of (
1.1
) satisfies
\lambda(f_{1})=\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda(f_{2})=\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda(E)=\lambda_{2}\leq\rho(E)=n
.

In Theorem
1.4
, the number of zeros of
f_{1}
actually satisfies
n(r,0,f_{1})\asymp(\log r)^{2}
when
\lambda_{1}=0
or
n(r,0,f_{1})\asymp r^{\lambda_{1}}
when
0<\lambda_{1}\leq n
. Then number of zeros of
f_{2}
has similar asymptotic behaviors. By slightly modifying the proof of Theorem
1.4
,
f_{1}
or
f_{2}
can be chosen to have only finitely many zeros in the plane, as pointed out in Subsection
3.1
.

As in
[
19
, Theorem 3]
, we can describe the asymptotic behaviors of the Bank–Laine function
E
in Theorem
1.3
and also the corresponding coefficient function
A
of the second order differential equation (
1.1
) in the case
n=1
. We have the following

Theorem 1.5
.

When
n=1
, the functions
A
and
E
in Theorem
1.4
can be chosen so that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\log|A(re^{i\theta})|\sim\log\frac{1}{|E(re^{i\theta})|}\sim\frac{1}{l}r\cos(\theta)\quad\text{for}\quad|\theta|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\pi}{2},\end{split}

where
l
is an integer such that
l=1
when
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}<1
and
l=3
when
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}=1
and
l=4
when
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
, while

\begin{split}\log|E(-re^{i\theta})|\sim\frac{1}{l}r\cos(\theta)\quad\text{for}\quad|\theta|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\pi}{2}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}|A(-re^{i\theta})|\sim\frac{1}{4l^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad|\theta|\leq(1-\varepsilon)\frac{\pi}{2}\end{split}

uniformly as
r\to\infty
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

The proof for Theorem
1.5
is only a slight modification of that in
[
19
, Theorem 3]
for the case
\rho=\sigma=1
and thus will be omitted. The proof is based on the fact that the Schwarzian derivative
S(F)
can be factored as
S(F)=B(F/F^{\prime})/2
, where

\begin{split}B(E):=-2\frac{E^{\prime\prime}}{E}+\left(\frac{E^{\prime}}{E}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{E^{2}}.\end{split}

See Bank and Laine
[
2
,
3
]
. The general solution of the differential equation
B(E)=4A
, that is, of the equation

(1.8)

\begin{split}4A=-2\frac{E^{\prime\prime}}{E}+\left(\frac{E^{\prime}}{E}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{E^{2}}\end{split}

is a product of two normalized solutions of (
1.1
). The results in
[
19
, Theorem 3]
shows that the functions
A
and
E
constructed in Theorem
1.3
are actually of completely regular growth (in the sense of Levin and Pfluger). This is obvious for
E
and
A
when
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}<n
. In fact, by the classical Hadamard factorization theorem,
Theorem
1.5
together with its extension to the general case in Subsection
3.4
implies that
E(z)=H(z)e^{p(z)}
, where
H
is the canonical product formed by the zeros of
E
and

is a polynomial of degree

of the form
p(z)=z^{n}+\cdots
.

While the Bank–Laine functions
E=f_{1}f_{2}
constructed in previous three theorems all satisfy
\rho(E)<\infty
, by the linear independence of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, the product

with a nonzero constant

is also a Bank–Laine function and satisfies
\lambda(E)=\infty
. However, we shall prove the following

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\rho\in(1/2,\infty)
and
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
. Then there exists an entire function
A
of order
\rho
such that the associated Bank–Laine function

for two normalized solutions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
of (

) satisfies

and
\log n(r,0,f_{2})\asymp r^{\rho}
. Moreover, the Bank–Laine function

never satisfies
\lambda(E)<\infty
for any other solution

linearly independent from

.

To prove Theorem
1.6
, we not only glue the functions

as in the proof of Theorem

but also glue the two functions

along horizontal lines for certain pair

of two nonnegative integers

and

. If we fix

for one pair

of two nonnegative integers

and

By the properties of

described in the introduction, we see that there exists an increasing diffeomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.1)

g_{m_{2},n_{2}}(x)=(g_{m_{1},n_{1}}\circ\phi)(x)

for all

. Denote

\begin{split}K=\frac{M}{N}=\frac{m_{2}+2n_{2}+1}{m_{1}+2n_{1}+1}.\end{split}

Though

in (

) has some poles, we may use the arguments as in
[
4
]
to show

Lemma 2.1
.

The asymptotic behaviors of the diffeomorphism
\phi
satisfy:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x+O(e^{-x/2}),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \phi^{\prime}(x)=1+O(e^{-x/2}),\quad\quad x\to\infty,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Kx+c+O(e^{-\delta|x|/2}),\ \ \ \ \phi^{\prime}(x)=K+O(e^{-\delta|x|/2}),\quad x\to-\infty,

with

\begin{split}c=\frac{1}{N}\log\left[\frac{\binom{m_{1}+2n_{1}}{m_{1}}}{\binom{m_{2}+2n_{2}}{m_{2}}}\frac{N!}{M!}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\delta=\frac{1}{2}\min\{1,K\}.\end{split}

Proof.

In order to prove (
2.2
), we note that

\begin{split}\log g_{m_{2},n_{2}}(x)=e^{x}+O(x)=e^{x}(1+O(xe^{-x})),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

The equation
g_{m_{2},n_{2}}(x)=(g_{m_{1},n_{1}}\circ\phi)(x)
easily implies that
2x/3\leq\phi(x)\leq 2x
for large
x
. Thus we also have

\begin{split}\log g_{m_{1},n_{1}}(\phi(x))&=e^{\phi(x)}(1+O(\phi(x)e^{-\phi(x)}))=e^{\phi(x)}(1+O(\phi(x)e^{-2x/3})),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

Combining the last two equations we obtain

\begin{split}e^{\phi(x)-x}=1+O(xe^{-2x/3}),\quad x\to\infty,\end{split}

from which the first statement in (
2.2
) easily follows. For the second statement in (
2.2
) we use

(2.4)

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}=\frac{g^{\prime}_{m_{2},n_{2}}}{g_{m_{2},n_{2}}}\frac{g_{m_{1},n_{1}}\circ\phi}{g^{\prime}_{m_{1},n_{1}}\circ\phi}=e^{Mx-N\phi(x)}\frac{A_{m_{2}}B_{2n_{2}}}{A_{m_{1}}B_{2n_{1}}}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{m_{1}}A_{i}e^{i\phi(x)}}{\sum_{i=0}^{m_{2}}A_{i}e^{ix}}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{2n_{1}}B_{j}e^{j\phi(x)}}{\sum_{j=0}^{2n_{2}}B_{j}e^{jx}},\end{split}

so that, together with the first statement in (
2.2
),

(2.5)

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}&=e^{Mx-N\phi(x)+(m_{1}+2n_{1})\phi(x)-(m_{2}+2n_{2})x}\frac{1+O(e^{-\phi(x)})}{1+O(e^{-x})}\frac{1+O(e^{-\phi(x)})}{1+O(e^{-x})}\\
&=e^{O(e^{-x/2})}(1+O(e^{-\phi(x)})+O(e^{-x}))=1+O(e^{-x/2}),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

In order to prove (
2.3
) we note from (

) and (

) that

\begin{split}h^{\prime}_{m,n}(x)=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}\frac{e^{x}x^{m+2n}}{Q_{m}(x)^{2}}.\end{split}

Since

is analytic on

, then by taking the derivatives of

successively we find that
h^{\prime}_{m,n}(0)=\cdots=h^{(m+2n)}_{m,n}(0)=0
. Thus by the formula for the error term of a Taylor series we have

\begin{split}h_{m,n}(x)=R_{m,n}(x)e^{x}=1+\frac{x^{m+2n+1}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n+1)!}+O(x^{m+2n+2}),\quad x\to 0.\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}g_{m,n}(x)&=1+\frac{e^{(m+2n+1)x}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n+1)!}+O(e^{(m+2n+2)x})\\
&=1+\frac{e^{(m+2n+1)x}}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n+1)!}(1+O(e^{x})),\quad x\to-\infty.\end{split}

The equation
g_{m_{2},n_{2}}(x)=g_{m_{1},n_{1}}(\phi(x))
now yields

\begin{split}\frac{\binom{m_{2}+2n_{2}}{m_{2}}}{\binom{m_{1}+2n_{1}}{m_{1}}}\frac{M!}{N!}e^{N\phi(x)-Mx}=1+O(e^{x})+O(e^{\phi(x)}),\quad x\to-\infty\end{split}

and hence

\begin{split}\phi(x)=\frac{M}{N}x+\frac{1}{N}\log\left[\frac{\binom{m_{2}+2n_{2}}{m_{2}}}{\binom{m_{1}+2n_{1}}{m_{1}}}\frac{M!}{N!}\right]+O(e^{x})+O(e^{\phi(x)}),\quad x\to-\infty,\end{split}

which gives the first statement in (
2.3
). For the second statement in (
2.3
), recall that
A_{m}B_{2n}=\frac{m!(2n)!}{[(m+2n)!]^{2}}=\frac{1}{\binom{m+2n}{m}(m+2n)!}
. Similarly as for the estimates in (
2.5
), we use (
2.4
) together with the first statement in (
2.3
) to obtain

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}(x)&=\frac{A_{m_{2}}B_{2n_{2}}}{A_{m_{1}}B_{2n_{1}}}e^{Mx-N\phi(x)}\frac{1+O(e^{\phi(x)})}{1+O(e^{x})}\frac{1+O(e^{\phi(x)})}{1+O(e^{x})}\\
&=\frac{A_{m_{2}}B_{2n_{2}}}{A_{m_{1}}B_{2n_{1}}}e^{Mx-N(Kx+c+O(e^{-\delta|x|/2}))}(1+O(e^{\phi(x)})+O(e^{x}))\\
&=\frac{A_{m_{2}}B_{2n_{2}}}{A_{m_{1}}B_{2n_{1}}}e^{-Nc+O(e^{-\delta|x|/2})}(1+O(e^{\phi(x)})+O(e^{x}))=K+O(e^{-\delta|x|/2}).\end{split}

g_{m_{k},n_{k}}\left(\frac{x+iy-2i\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}}{N_{k}}\right),&x\leq 0.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Note that the two expressions in the definition of
\tilde{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}
in (
3.20
) coincide on the imaginary axis in each of three cases (I), (II) and (III).

Now we begin to glue the functions
\tilde{g}_{m,n}
defined in (
3.18
) along the horizontal lines. We shall always assume that
(m_{k})
and
(n_{k})
are the two sequences chosen in either of the three cases. In either case, the choice of the integer
l
guarantees that the resulting functions
U
,
V
and
G
are analytic in almost all parts of the plane.

We begin by constructing the map
U
. We shall use the explicit expressions for
\tilde{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}
in (
3.20
). Recall from equation (
3.18
) that
\tilde{g}_{m,n}=g_{m,n}\circ\chi_{m,n}
for an affine mapping
\chi_{m,n}
in the right half-plane. Instead of
\tilde{g}_{m,n}
, we consider the map

\begin{split}u_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m,n}(z)=g_{m,n}(\chi_{m,n}(z)+s_{m,n}).\end{split}

Note that
u_{m,n}
is increasing on the real line and maps
[0,\infty)
onto
[2,\infty)
. For the two chosen sequences
(m_{k})
and
(n_{k})
, we write
N_{k}=m_{k}+2n_{k}+1
. Put
U(z)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}
in the half-strip

(3.21)

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}=\{x+iy:x>0,\quad 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\}.\end{split}

The function
U
may be discontinuous along the horizontal lines
\mathcal{L}^{+}_{k-1}=\{z=x+iy:x>0,y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\}
and
\mathcal{L}^{+}_{k}=\{z=x+iy:x>0,y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\}
if
m_{k}=1
or
n_{k}=1
. In order to obtain a continuous function we consider the function
\psi_{k}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
defined by
u_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}(x)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x))
. The function
\psi_{k}
is closely related to the function
\phi
considered in (
3.13
). In fact, denoting by
\phi_{k}
the function
\phi
corresponding to
(m_{k},2n_{k})
and
(m_{k+1},2n_{k+1})
, we have

(3.22)

\begin{split}\psi_{k}(x)=l\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x}{l}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)-ls_{m_{k},n_{k}}.\end{split}

We then define
U:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C}
by interpolating between
u_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
u_{m_{k},n_{k}}
as follows: if
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
, say
y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}+2\pi N_{k}t
where
0\leq t<1
, then we put

\begin{split}U(x+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)).\end{split}

The function
U
defined in this way is continuous along the horizontal lines
\mathcal{L}^{+}_{k}
for any
k
in the right-half plane.

We now define a function
V
in the left half-plane. In order to do so, we define

\begin{split}v_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z<0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v_{m,n}(z)=g_{m,n}\left(\chi_{m,n}(z)+s_{m,n}\right).\end{split}

Note that
v_{m,n}
maps
(-\infty,0]
monotonically onto
(1,2]
. For the two sequences
(m_{k})
and
(n_{k})
as before, this time we would like to define
V(z)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)
in the half-strip

(3.23)

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{-}=\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\},\end{split}

but again this function would be discontinuous along the horizontal lines
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{k-1}=\{z=x+iy:x<0,y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\}
and
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{k}=\{z=x+iy:x<0,y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\}
if
m_{k}=1
or
n_{k}=1
. In order to obtain a continuous function we again interpolate between
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
v_{m_{k},n_{k}}
. Similarly as before we consider the map
\psi_{k}:(-\infty,0]\to(-\infty,0]
defined by
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}(x)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x))
. More precisely, denoting by
\phi_{k}
the function
\phi
corresponding to
(m_{k},2n_{k})
and
(m_{k+1},2n_{k+1})
, we have

(3.24)

\begin{split}\psi_{k}(x)=N_{k}\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)-N_{k}s_{m_{k},n_{k}}.\end{split}

Then we define
V:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\leq 0\}\to\mathbb{C}
by interpolating between
v_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and
v_{m_{k},n_{k}}
as follows: if
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
, say
y=2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}+2\pi N_{k}t
where
0\leq t<1
, then we put

\begin{split}V(x+iy)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\psi_{k}(x)+iy)=v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)).\end{split}

This map
V
is continuous along the horizontal lines
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{k}
for any
k
in the left half-plane.

Note that
U
and
V
coincide on the imaginary axis. With the definitions of the maps
U
and
V
, we define the map

(3.25)

\begin{split}G(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}U(z),&\text{if}\quad|\arg z|\leq\frac{\pi}{2};\\
V(z),&\text{if}\quad|\arg(-z)|\leq\frac{\pi}{2},\end{array}\right.\end{split}

which is continuous along the horizontal lines
\mathcal{L}^{+}_{k}
in the right half-plane and
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{k}
in the left half-plane for any integer
k
respectively. In the section below, we will show that the map
G
defined in (
3.25
) is indeed quasiregular in the plane and also satisfies the hypothesis of the Teichmüller–Wittich–Belinskii theorem
[
14
, § V.6]
.

3.3.
Estimation of the dilatation

Now we begin to estimate the complex dilatation of the functions
U
,
V
and
G
defined in previous section. For a quasiregular map
f
, let

\begin{split}\mu_{f}(z)=\frac{f_{\bar{z}}(z)}{f_{z}(z)}\ \ \text{and}\ \ K_{f}(z)=\frac{1+|\mu_{f}(z)|}{1-|\mu_{f}(z)|}.\end{split}

In order to apply the Teichmüller–Wittich–Belinskii theorem, we have to estimate
K_{G}(z)-1
. We note that

\begin{split}K_{G}(z)-1=\frac{2|\mu_{G}(z)|}{1-|\mu_{G}(z)|}=\frac{2|\mu_{G}(z)|(1+|\mu_{G}(z)|)}{1-|\mu_{G}(z)|^{2}}\leq\frac{4|\mu_{G}(z)|}{1-|\mu_{G}(z)|^{2}}.\end{split}

By the explicit expressions of
\tilde{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}
in (
3.20
), the arguments for estimating the dilation of
G
will be similar in either of the three cases (I), (II) and (III). Below we shall first consider the first case (I).

We begin by estimating
K_{U}-1
in the right half-plane

H^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z>0\}
.
Let
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
so that
0\leq t=(y-2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1})/(2\pi N_{k})<1
. Then
U(z)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z))
with

(3.26)

\begin{split}q(x+iy)=x+iy+t(\psi_{k}(x)-x)=x+iy+\frac{y-2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}}{2\pi N_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x)-x),\end{split}

where
\psi_{k}(x)
takes the form in (
3.22
). Thus

(3.27)

\begin{split}q_{z}(z)=1+a(z)-ib(z)\ \ \text{and}\ \ q_{\bar{z}}=a(z)+ib(z)\end{split}

with

(3.28)

\begin{split}a(x+iy)=\frac{t}{2}(\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)-1)\ \ \text{and}\ \ b(x+iy)=\frac{1}{4\pi N_{k}}(\psi_{k}(x)-x).\end{split}

Note that if
a(z)<0
, then

\begin{split}a(z)=\frac{t}{2}(\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1)\geq\frac{1}{2}(\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1)\end{split}

and thus
1+2a(z)\geq\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z)>0
. Then we have
1+2a(z)\geq\min\{1,\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z)\}>0
as well as
1+a(z)>0
in any case.

We deduce from (
3.26
) that

\begin{split}|\mu_{U}(z)|^{2}=|\mu_{q}(z)|^{2}=\frac{a(z)^{2}+b(z)^{2}}{(1+a(z))^{2}+b(z)^{2}}\end{split}

and thus

\begin{split}K_{U}(z)-1&\leq\frac{4|\mu_{q}(z)|}{1-|\mu_{q}(z)|^{2}}\leq\frac{4\sqrt{(1+a(z))^{2}+b(z)^{2}}\sqrt{a(z)^{2}+b(z)^{2}}}{1+2a(z)}\\
&\leq\frac{4(1+a(z)+|b(z)|)(a(z)+|b(z)|)}{1+2a(z)}\leq\frac{4(1+r(x))r(x)}{\min\{1,\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)\}},\end{split}

where

(3.29)

\begin{split}r(x)=|\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)-1|+|\psi_{k}(x)-x|.\end{split}

In order to estimate
K_{U}(z)-1
for
z\in\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
defined in (
3.21
), we recall from Lemma
2.1
that

(3.30)

\begin{split}|\phi_{k}(x)-x|=o(1),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

By the definition of
\psi_{k}(x)
in (
3.22
) we have

\psi_{k}(x)-x=l\phi_{k}(x/l+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}})-l(x/l+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}})+l(s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}-s_{m_{k},n_{k}})
and, together with the estimate in (
3.30
), thus

\begin{split}|\psi_{k}(x)-x|=O(1),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

Further, by Lemma
2.1
we also have

\begin{split}|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1|=o(1),\quad x\to\infty.\end{split}

The last two equations imply that the functions
r(x)
defined by (
3.29
) satisfies
r(x)=O(1)
for
z\in\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
. Moreover, we note that the affine mapping
\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}:H^{+}\to H^{+}
defined in (
3.18
) satisfies
\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=x/l+iy/N_{k}
and so
|\mu_{\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}}|=(N_{k}-l)/(N_{k}+l)<1
. Thus
U
is quasiregular in the right half-plane
H^{+}
and

\begin{split}K_{U}(z)-1=O(1),\quad x\to\infty\end{split}

for
z\in\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
. We denote

\begin{split}S_{1,k_{i}}=\{x+iy:x>0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}},\ m_{k_{i}}=1\}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}S_{2,k_{j}}=\{x+iy:x>0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}},\ n_{k_{j}}=1\}.\end{split}

Let
\varepsilon>0
be small. Define
\gamma
as follows: let
\gamma=\varepsilon
when
\lambda_{2}=0
and
\gamma=\lambda_{2}
when
\lambda_{2}>0
. Then, together with the estimates in (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we have

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{i}}}\frac{dxdy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq\int_{S_{1,k_{i}}}\frac{dxdy}{x^{2}+4\pi^{2}(k_{i}-1)^{2}}\leq 2\pi N_{k_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{2}+4\pi^{2}\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}-1}^{2}}=\frac{\pi N_{k_{i}}}{2\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}-1}}.\end{split}

By the choice of the two sequence of
(m_{k})
and
(n_{k})
we easily see that

(3.31)

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim lk\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty.\end{split}

This yields

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{i}}}\frac{dxdy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq\frac{\pi}{c(2\pi i)^{1/\gamma}}\end{split}

for
i\geq 1
and some positive constant
c
. Thus

(3.32)

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{i}}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy\leq\frac{c_{1}}{(2\pi i)^{1/\gamma}}\end{split}

for all
i\geq 1
and some constant
c_{1}
. Similarly, we also have

(3.33)

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{i}-1}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy\leq\frac{c_{2}}{(2\pi i)^{1/\gamma}}\end{split}

for all
i\geq 1
and some constant
c_{2}
. Moreover, together with the estimates in (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we can also deduce that

(3.34)

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{j}}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy\leq\frac{c_{3}}{(2\pi j)^{1/\gamma}}\end{split}

for all
j\geq 1
and some constant
c_{3}
, and that

(3.35)

\begin{split}\int_{S_{1,k_{j}-1}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy\leq\frac{c_{4}}{(2\pi j)^{1/\gamma}}\end{split}

for all
j\geq 1
and some constant
c_{4}
. Note that
U
is meromorphic in
H^{+}
apart from the half-strips
S_{1,k_{i}-1}
,
S_{1,k_{i}}
,
S_{1,k_{j}-1}
and
S_{1,k_{j}}
. Then the last four inequalities (
3.32
)-(
3.35
) imply that

(3.36)

\begin{split}\int_{z\in H^{+}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\Pi^{+}_{k}}\frac{K_{U}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy<\infty.\end{split}

The estimate of
K_{V}(z)-1
in the left half-plane

H^{-}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z<0\}

is similar. Now we have
V(z)=V_{m_{k},n_{k}}(q(z))
where
q(z)
has the same form as in (
3.26
). Moreover, (
3.27
) and (
3.28
) both hold for the same
a(x+iy)
and
b(x+iy)
, but
\psi_{k}(x)
taking the form in (
3.24
). Instead of (
3.28
) we obtain

\begin{split}K_{V}(z)-1&\leq\frac{4(1+r(x))r(x)}{\min\{1,\psi_{k}^{\prime}(x)\}}\end{split}

with

(3.37)

\begin{split}r(x)=\left|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1\right|+\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left|\psi_{k}(x)-x\right|.\end{split}

Now, by the definition of
\psi_{k}(x)
in (
3.24
), we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left|\psi_{k}(x)-x\right|=\left|\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)-\frac{x}{N_{k}}-s_{m_{k},n_{k}}\right|\\
=&\ \left|\phi_{k}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)-\frac{N_{k+1}}{N_{k}}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)+\frac{N_{k+1}}{N_{k}}s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}-s_{m_{k},n_{k}}\right|\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\left|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1\right|=\frac{N_{k}}{N_{k+1}}\left|\phi^{\prime}_{k}\left(\frac{x}{N_{k+1}}+s_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}\right)-\frac{N_{k+1}}{N_{k}}\right|.\end{split}

We thus have by Lemma
2.1
that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{N_{k}}\left|\psi_{k}(x)-x\right|=O(1),\quad x\to-\infty\end{split}

and also that

\begin{split}&\left|\psi^{\prime}_{k}(x)-1\right|=o(1),\quad x\to-\infty.\end{split}

The last two inequalities imply that the function
r(x)
defined in (
3.37
) satisfies
r(x)=O(1)
for
z\in\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{-}
defined in (
3.23
).

\begin{split}T_{1,k_{i}}=\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{i}},\ m_{k_{i}}=1\}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}T_{2,k_{j}}=\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k_{j}},\ n_{k_{j}}=1\}.\end{split}

Then by the same arguments as the estimate for
K_{U}-1
in the right-half plane, we also conclude that
V
is quasiregular in the left half-plane and that

\begin{split}K_{V}(z)-1=O(1),\quad x\to-\infty\end{split}

for
z\in T_{1,k_{i}}
,
z\in T_{1,k_{i}+1}
,
z\in T_{1,k_{j}}
and
z\in T_{1,k_{j}+1}
. This finally implies that

(3.38)

\begin{split}\int_{z\in H^{-}}\frac{K_{V}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\Pi_{k}^{-}}\frac{K_{V}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy<\infty.\end{split}

The two inequalities in (
3.36
) and (
3.38
) shows that, if
r>0
, then

(3.39)

\begin{split}\int_{|z|>r}\frac{K_{G}(z)-1}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dxdy<\infty.\end{split}

For the case (II), since
l=3
and since the sequence
(n_{k})
are chosen in the way that
n_{k}=1
for all
k\geq 4
, we also have we have
N_{k}\leq 4
for all
k\geq 4
and we only need to estimate the complex dilatation of
U
in the stripes
S_{1,k_{i}-1}
and
S_{1,k_{i}}
of
\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
and the dilation of
V
in the stirps
T_{1,k_{i}-1}
and
T_{1,k_{i}}
of
\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{-}
as well as the complex dilatation of
U
in the stripes
\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
for the case
k=1,2,3
to conclude that the inequality in (
3.39
) also holds.

For the case (III), since
l=4
and since the two sequences
(m_{k})
and
(n_{k})
are chosen in the way that
m_{k}=n_{k}=1
for all
k\geq 4
, we have
N_{k}=4
for all
k\geq 4
. Thus we only need to estimate the complex dilatation of
U
in the stripes
\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}
for the case
k=1,2,3
to conclude that the inequality in (
3.39
) also holds.

Therefore, in either of the three cases (I), (II) and (III), the function
G
defined in (
3.25
) satisfies the hypothesis of the Teichmüller–Wittich–Belinskii theorem.
This theorem, together with the existence theorem for quasiconformal mappings, yields that there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\tau:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and a meromorphic function
F
such that

(3.40)

\begin{split}G(z)=F(\tau(z))\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(z)\sim z\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.\end{split}

Then
F=G\circ\tau^{-1}
is a locally univalent meromorphic function. Define
E=F/F^{\prime}
. Then
E
is a Bank–Laine function. Clearly,
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two normalized solutions of the second order differential equation (
1.1
) with an entire coefficient
A
.

3.4.
Proof for the case
n=1

We first prove Theorem
1.4
for the case
n=1
. We shall consider the three cases (I), (II) and (III) together. We will use similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
to estimate the numbers of poles and zeros of
F
respectively and also to determine the orders of
E
and
A
.
Also, for two positive quantities
S_{1}(r)
and
S_{2}(r)
, we use the notation
S_{1}(r)\asymp S_{2}(r)
, provided that there are two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that
c_{2}S_{2}(r)\leq S_{1}(r)\leq c_{1}S_{2}(r)
for all large
r
.

We first estimate the number of poles of
F
. Let
r>0
and choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}<r\leq 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
. Note that
\mathcal{N}_{k}\sim lk
as
k\to\infty
by (
3.31
). By the construction of
(m_{k})
together with the definition of
\chi_{m,n}
in (
3.18
), we have

(3.41)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\leq n(r,\infty,U)\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i},&\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\leq n(r,\infty,V)\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}.&\end{array}\right.

By the choice of
k_{i}
in (
3.6
) or (
3.7
) in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we easily see that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\sim(2\pi)^{-1}(\log(2\pi k))^{2}
as
k\to\infty
when
\lambda_{1}=0
and that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\sim(2\pi)^{\lambda_{1}-1}k^{\lambda_{1}}
as
k\to\infty
when
0<\lambda_{1}\leq 1
. Together with these two relations, the two double inequalities in (
3.41
) imply that
n(r,\infty,G)\asymp(\log r)^{2}
when
\lambda_{1}=0
and that
n(r,\infty,G)\asymp r^{\lambda_{1}}
when
0<\lambda_{1}\leq 1
. Then the relations in (
3.40
) imply that
n(r,\infty,F)\asymp(\log r)^{2}
when
\lambda_{1}=0
and that
n(r,\infty,F)\asymp r^{\lambda_{1}}
when
0<\lambda_{1}\leq 1
. Thus
\lambda(f_{1})=\lambda_{1}
in all three cases.

The estimate for the number of zeros of
F
is similar. Again, let
r>0
and choose
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}<r\leq 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}
. By the construction of the sequence
(n_{k})
together with the definition of
\chi_{m,n}
in (
3.18
), we have

(3.42)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2\sum_{j=1}^{k}n_{j}\leq n(r,0,U)\leq 4\sum_{i=j}^{k}n_{j},&\\

4.2.

We begin to define a quasiregular map using the functions

Let

when

and

(4.20)

when

and on each strip

\check{\Pi}_{-k}=\{z=x+iy:\quad 2\pi(-k)\leq y\leq 2\pi(-k+1)\}

of the lower half-plane we will consider the functions

(4.21)

\bar{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=g_{m_{k},n_{k}}\left(x-i\frac{y-2\pi N_{1-k}}{k}\right).

In view of the explicit expressions of

We begin by constructing the map
U
. Instead of

defined in (

), we consider the map

\begin{split}u_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m,n}(z)=\bar{g}_{m,n}(z+\bar{s}_{m,n}).\end{split}

Note that
\bar{g}_{m,n}
is increasing on the real line and maps
[0,\infty)
onto
[2,\infty)
. Put
U(z)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)
in the half-strip

\begin{split}\hat{\Pi}_{k}^{+}=\{x+iy:x>0,\quad 2\pi(k-1)<y<2\pi k\}.\end{split}

The function
U
will be discontinuous along the horizontal lines
L^{+}_{k-1}=\{z=x+iy:x>0,y=2\pi(k-1)\}
and
L^{+}_{k}=\{z=x+iy:x>0,y=2\pi k\}
. In order to obtain a continuous function we consider the function
\bar{\psi}_{k}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)

. The function

is closely related to the function
\bar{\phi}
considered in Lemma

and Lemma
4.6
and also the function
\bar{\phi}_{2}
defined in (

\bar{\phi}_{k}
the function
\bar{\phi}
corresponding to

and

\begin{split}\bar{\psi}_{k}(x)=\bar{\phi}_{k}(x+\bar{s}_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}})-\bar{s}_{m_{k},n_{k}}.\end{split}

We then define
U:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C}
by interpolating between
u_{m_{k+1},n_{k+1}}
and

as follows: if

, say

, then we put

\begin{split}U(x+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}((1-t)x+t\bar{\psi}_{k}(x)+iy)=u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(x+iy+t(\bar{\psi}_{k}(x)-x)).\end{split}

We now define a function
V
in the left half-plane. In order to do so, we define

\begin{split}v_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z<0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v_{m,n}(z)=\bar{g}_{m,n}\left(\frac{z}{m+2n+1}+\bar{s}_{m,n}\right).\end{split}

and
(n_{k})

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{-}=\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\},\end{split}

but again this function would be discontinuous along the horizontal lines

and
(n_{k})

\begin{split}H^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\end{split}

for

. We thus have to define

in the strip
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\text{Re}\ z<1\}
. For

we define

in this strip by interpolation; that is, we put

\begin{split}Q(x\pm iy)=x\pm i((1-x)g(y)+xy)\quad\text{if}\quad 0<x<1\quad\text{and}\quad y\geq 1.\end{split}

In the remaining part of the strip we define
Q
by

\begin{split}Q(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}z|z|^{\gamma-1},&\text{if}\quad 0<|z|<1;\\
z,&\text{if}\quad|z|>1,\quad\text{but}\quad 0<\text{Re}\ z<1\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq|\text{Im}\ z|\leq 1.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Note that

for

m_{1}=n_{1}=0

, and thus

for

do indeed coincide for

satisfies

\begin{split}W(\pm iy)=U(\pm ig(y))=V(\pm iy^{\gamma})\quad\text{for}\quad y\geq 0.\end{split}

Let now

. We choose the constant

and put

. The hypothesis that

\begin{split}G(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W(z^{\rho}),&\text{if}\quad|\arg z|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\rho};\\
V(-(-z)^{\sigma}),&\text{if}\quad|\arg(-z)|\leq\frac{\pi}{2\sigma},\end{array}\right.\end{split}

which is continuous on the imaginary axis and on the horizontal lines

in the right half-plane and
\mathcal{L}^{-}_{k}
in the left half-plane for any integer

respectively. Here, for

, we denote by

the principal branch of the power which is defined in
\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
.

4.3.
Completion of the proof

With the definition of
G
in (
4.22
) in previous section, here we first prove Theorem
1.6
under the assumption that
\rho\in(1/2,1)
. Now, we can follow exactly the same process as that in
[
5
, Section 3.3]
to estimate the dilation of
G
and thus prove that
G
is indeed quasiregular in the plane and also that
G
satisfies the hypothesis of the Teichmüller–Wittich–Belinskii theorem.
This theorem, together with the existence theorem for quasiconformal mappings, yields that there exists a quasiconformal homeomorphism
\tau:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
and a meromorphic function
F
such that

(4.23)

\begin{split}G(z)=F(\tau(z))\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(z)\sim z\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.\end{split}

Then
F=G\circ\tau^{-1}
is a locally univalent meromorphic function. Define
E=F/F^{\prime}
. Then
E
is a Bank–Laine function. Clearly,
E=f_{1}f_{2}
for two normalized solutions of the second order differential equation (
1.1
) with an entire coefficient
A
.

Then we use similar arguments as in the proof of Theorem
1.3
to estimate the numbers of poles and zeros of
F
respectively and also to determine the orders of
E
and
A
. Again, two positive quantities
S_{1}(r)
and
S_{2}(r)
are written as
S_{1}(r)\asymp S_{2}(r)
if there are two positive constants
d_{1}
and
d_{2}

The definitions for

),

\begin{split}u_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m,n}(z)=\bar{g}_{m,n}(\chi_{m,n}(z)+\bar{s}_{m,n}),\end{split}

where

Note that

onto

For each two chosen sequences
(m_{k})

(n_{k})
, we write

in the half-strip

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{+}=\{x+iy:x>0,\quad 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}<y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\}.\end{split}

Then we consider the map

\begin{split}u_{m_{k},n_{k}}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=\bar{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)+\bar{s}_{m_{k},n_{k}})\end{split}

when

and

\begin{split}u_{m_{k},n_{k}}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\geq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad u_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=\frac{\bar{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)+\bar{s}_{m_{k},n_{k}})+1}{2}\end{split}

when

. Consider the function

Then we may interpolate in the same way as in Subsection
3.2
to obtain a map

which is continuous along the horizontal lines

in the left half-plane, we define

\begin{split}v_{m,n}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z<0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v_{m,n}(z)=\bar{g}_{m,n}\left(\chi_{m,n}(z)+\bar{s}_{m,n}\right),\end{split}

(m_{k})

be the two sequences as before. This time we would like to define

\begin{split}\hat{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{k}^{-}=\{x+iy:x<0,\ 2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k-1}\leq y<2\pi\mathcal{N}_{k}\}.\end{split}

\begin{split}v_{m_{k},n_{k}}:\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\text{Re}\ z\leq 0\}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)=\bar{g}_{m_{k},n_{k}}(\chi_{m_{k},n_{k}}(z)+\bar{s}_{m_{k},n_{k}})\end{split}

\begin{split}G(z)=F(\tau(z))\quad\text{and}\quad\tau(z)\sim z\quad\text{as}\quad z\to\infty.\end{split}

We now estimate the numbers of poles and zeros of
