Rings with u n − 1 u^{n}-1 nilpotent for each unit u u
Peter Danchev, Arash Javan, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. We continue the study in-depth of the so-called n n -UU rings for any n ≥ 1 n\geq 1 , that were defined by the first-named author in Toyama Math. J. (2017) as those rings R R for which u n − 1 u^{n}-1 is always a nilpotent for every unit u ∈ R u\in R . Specifically, for any n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , we prove that a ring is strongly n n -nil-clean if, and only if, it is simultaneously strongly π \pi -regular and an ( n − 1 ) (n-1) -UU ring. This somewhat extends results due to Diesl in J. Algebra (2013), Abyzov in Sib. Math. J. (2019) and Cui-Danchev in J. Algebra Appl. (2020). Moreover, our results somewhat improves the ones obtained by Koşan et al. in Hacettepe J. Math. Stat. (2020).

We start with the following useful technicality.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
R
is a ring of prime characteristic
p
such that

m-1|p-1
. Then,
R
is an
(m-1)
-UU ring if, and only if, every invertible element of the ring R is
m
-strongly nil clean.

Proof.

Let
a\in U(R)
. Since
R
is an
(m-1)
-UU ring, we have
b=1-a^{m-1}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
; and since
m-1|p-1
, we have
b^{\prime}=1-a^{p-1}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Therefore, there exist positive integers
k,k^{\prime}
such that

(ab)^{k}=a^{k}b^{k}=b^{k}a^{k}=0=b^{\prime k^{\prime}}a^{k}=a^{k}b^{\prime k^{\prime}}=(ab^{\prime})^{k^{\prime}}.

Also, we can choose a number
n
large enough so that
p^{n}>k,k^{\prime}
. Since the characteristic of
R
is the prime number
p
, it follows that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=(a^{m-1}+b)^{p^{n}}=(a^{m-1})^{p^{n}}+b^{p^{n}}=(a^{p^{n}})^{m-1}+b^{p^{n}},

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=(a^{p-1}+b^{\prime})^{p^{n}}=(a^{p-1})^{p^{n}}+b^{\prime p^{n}}=(a^{p^{n}})^{p-1}+b^{\prime p^{n}}.

Furthermore, suppose
e=a^{p^{n}}
,
f=b^{p^{n}}
and
f^{\prime}=b^{\prime p^{n}}
. Then,
e^{m-1}+f=1=e^{p-1}+f^{\prime}
and also
ef=(ab)^{p^{n}}=0=(ab^{\prime})^{p^{n}}=ef^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=e(e^{m-1}+f)=e^{p}+ef=e^{m},

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=e(e^{p-1}+f^{\prime})=e^{p}+ef^{\prime}=e^{p}.

Likewise, on the other side, we have

(a-e)^{p^{n}}=a^{p^{n}}-e^{p^{n}}=e-e^{p^{n}}=e-e=0,

so
a-e\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Therefore,
a=e+(a-e)
, as required.
∎

As a valuable consequence, we extract the following.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
R
is a ring of prime characteristic
p
. Then,
R
is a
\pi
-UU ring if, and only if, every invertible element of
R
is periodic.

Proof.

For every
a\in U(R)
, there exists a positive integer
k
such that
b=a^{k}-1\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Let us assume that
b^{s}=0
. We may take an integer
n
large enough such that
p^{n}\geq s
. Thus, we obtain that

a^{kp^{n}}=(1+b)^{p^{n}}=1+b^{p^{n}}=1,

as needed.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
F
is a field. If
F
is
n
-UU, then
F
is finite and
(|F|-1)|n
.

Proof.

Let
f(x)=1-x^{n}\in F[x]
. Since
F
is a field, the polynomial
f(x)
has at most
n
roots in
F^{\ast}
. So, if we suppose
A
to be the set of all roots of
f
in
F^{\ast}
, we will have
F^{\ast}=A
. Therefore,
|F^{\ast}|=|A|<n
.

On the other hand, as
F^{\ast}
is a cyclic group, there exists
a\in F^{\ast}
such that
F^{\ast}=\langle a\rangle
. Since
a^{n}=1
, we get
o(a)|n
, and hence
n=o(a)q=|F^{\ast}|q
. Consequently,
|F^{\ast}||n
. Finally,
(|F|-1)|n
, as stated.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
D
be a division ring and
n\geq 2
. If
D
is
n
-UU, then
D
is a finite field and
(|D|-1)|n

Proof.

Certainly,

. So, for any

, we have

, whence
a=a^{n+1}
. Furthermore, appealing to the famous Jacobson’s theorem (cf.

]
), we detect that

must be commutative, and thus a field, as expected.

The second part follows at once from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Lemma 2.22

(a) An arbitrary subring

(b) The necessity follows directly from (a).

For sufficiency, if all

2.2.
Matrix rings and generalizations

Let
A,B
be two rings and let
M,N
be the
(A,B)
-bimodule and
(B,A)
-bimodule, respectively. Also, we consider the bilinear maps
\phi:M\otimes_{B}N\to A
and
\psi:N\otimes_{A}M\to B
that apply to the following properties

Id_{M}\otimes_{B}\psi=\phi\otimes_{A}Id_{M},\quad Id_{N}\otimes_{A}\phi=\psi\otimes_{B}Id_{N}.

For
m\in M
and
n\in N
, we define
mn:=\phi(m\otimes n)
and
nm:=\psi(n\otimes m)
.
Thus, the 4-tuple

R=\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}

becomes to an associative ring equipped with the obvious matrix operations, which is called a
Morita context ring
. Denote the two-sided ideals
Im\phi
and
Im\psi
to
MN
and
NM
, respectively, that are called the
trace ideals
of the Morita context (compare with
[
2
]
as well).

We are managed to prove the following assertion.

Theorem 2.31
.

Suppose
n
is a positive integer such that either
n
is odd or
n=2^{k},6,10
. Let
R=\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}

be a Morita context ring such that
MN
and
NM
are nilpotent ideals of
A
and
B
, respectively. Then,
R
is
n
-UU if, and only if, both
A
and
B
are
n
-UU rings.

Proof.

As both
A
and
B
are subrings of
R
, in accordance with Lemma
2.22
(a) they are
n
-UU ring.

If now
A,B
are two
n
-UU rings, then by virtue of Corollary
2.30
we have that
J(A)
and
J(B)
are both nil-ideals and also that
A/J(A)
and
B/J(B)
are both
n
-UU rings. On the other side, referring to
[
10
, Theorem 13(1)]
, we compute

J(R)=\begin{pmatrix}J(A)&M\\
N&J(B)\end{pmatrix},

and

R/J(R)\cong A/J(A)\times B/J(B).

Since Lemma
2.22
(b) assures that the direct product of
n
-UU rings is again
n
-UU, the ring
R/J(R)
is also
n
-UU. Furthermore, according to Corollary
2.30
, it is sufficient to show that
J(R)
is a nil-ideal. To that purpose, suppose

S=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&m\\
n&a_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in J(R)

such that
a_{1}^{n}=a_{2}^{n}=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, one finds that

S^{n}\in\begin{pmatrix}MN&M\\
N&NM\end{pmatrix},

and hence, we easily obtain by induction on

that

\begin{pmatrix}MN&M\\
N&NM\end{pmatrix}^{2k}=\begin{pmatrix}(MN)^{k}&(MN)^{k}M\\
(NM)^{k}N&(NM)^{k}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,
J(R)
is nil, as asserted.
∎

Given a ring
R
and a central element
s
of
R
, the
4
-tuple

\begin{pmatrix}R&R\\
R&R\end{pmatrix}

becomes a ring with addition defined componentwise and with multiplication defined by

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&x_{1}\\
y_{1}&b_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&x_{2}\\
y_{2}&b_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}+sx_{1}y_{2}&a_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}b_{2}\\
y_{1}a_{2}+b_{1}y_{2}&sy_{1}x_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

This ring is denoted by
K_{s}(R)
. A Morita context

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}

with
A=B=M=N=R
is called a generalized matrix ring over
R
. It was observed in
[
19
]
that a ring
S
is a generalized matrix ring over
R
if, and only if,
S=K_{s}(R)
for some
s\in C(R)
, the center of
R
. Here
MN=NM=sR
, so that
MN\subseteq J(A)\Longleftrightarrow s\in J(R)
,
NM\subseteq J(B)\Longleftrightarrow s\in J(R)
, and
MN,NM
are nilpotents
\Longleftrightarrow s
is a nilpotent.

Thus, Theorem
2.31
yields the following two direct consequences.

n
-UU ring.

Proposition 2.35
.

The power series ring
R[[t]]
is not
n
-UU for any ring
R
.

Proof.

Referring to
[
20
, Exersice 6.5]
, we know that

J(R[[x]])=\{a+xf(x)|x\in J(R),f(x)\in R[[x]]\}.

So,
1+x\in U(R)
. But
(1+x)^{n}
is not an unipotent for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as required.
∎

According to
[
12
]
and
[
8
]
, a commutative ring
R
is a UU (resp.
\pi
-UU) ring if, and only if, the ring of polynomials
R[t]
is also a UU (resp.
\pi
-UU) ring. Now, we aim to extend these results to
n
-UU rings. The prime radical
N(R)
of a ring R is defined as the intersection of all prime ideals of
R
. It is known that
N(R)
is equal to the lower nil-radical
{\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R)
of
R
. A ring
R
is called
2
-primal if
N(R)
is the same as the set (nil-radical)
{\rm Nil}(R)
.

For an endomorphism
\alpha
of
R
, the ring R is called
\alpha
-compatible if, for any elements
a
and
b
in
R
, the equality
ab=0
holds if, and only if,
a\alpha(b)=0
. This definition is given in
[
15
]
. In this case, the map
\alpha
must be injective.

We, thus, have all the ingredients necessary to prove the following assertion.

Proposition 2.36
.

Let
n
be either odd, or
n=2^{k}
, or
6
, or
10
, and
R
be a
2
-primal ring with
\alpha
an endomorphism of
R
such that
R
is
\alpha
-compatible. The following are equivalent:

(i)

R[t,\alpha]
is an
n
-UU ring.

(ii)

R
is an
n
-UU ring.

(iii)

J(R)={\rm Nil}(R)
and
U^{n}(R/J(R))={\bar{1}}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). It is obvious.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Since
R
is a
2
-primal ring, we deduce
{\rm Nil}(R)={\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R)\subseteq J(R)
. On the other hand, since
R
is an
n
-UU ring, one must be that
J(R)\subseteq{\rm Nil}(R)
. Thus,
{\rm Nil}(R)=J(R)
, whence the quotient
R/J(R)
is reduced, which gives
U^{n}(R/J(R))={\bar{1}}
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i). Since
J(R)={\rm Nil}(R)
, the factor
R/J(R)
is reduced and we get
\alpha(J(R))\subseteq J(R)
. However,
\overline{\alpha}:R/J(R)\to R/J(R)
, defined by
\overline{\alpha}(a+J(R))=\alpha(a)+J(R)
, is an endomorphism of
R/J(R)
. Moreover, by
[
23
, Lemma 2.4]
, the ring
R/J(R)
is
\alpha
-compatible. So, by
[
4
, Corollary 2.12]
, we derive

U(R/J(R)[x,\alpha])=U(R/J(R)).

Thus,
R/J(R)[x,\alpha]
is an
n
-UU ring.

Furthermore, as

R/J(R)[x,\alpha]\cong R[x,\alpha]/J(R)[x,\alpha],

it follows that
R[x,\alpha]/J(R)[x,\alpha]
is an
n
-UU ring. But, utilizing
[
4
, Lemma 2.2]
, we know that
{\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R[x,\alpha])={\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R)[x,\alpha]
. So, the ideal

J(R)[x,\alpha]={\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R)[x,\alpha]={\rm Nil}_{\ast}(R[x,\alpha])

is nil, as pursued.
∎

As a valuable consequence, we extract:

Corollary 2.37
.

Let
n
be either odd, or
n=2^{k}
, or
6
, or
10
. Then, a
2
-primal ring
R
is an
n
-UU ring if, and only if,
R[t]
is an
n
-UU ring, if and only if,
J(R)={\rm Nil}(R)
and
U^{n}(R/J(R))={\bar{1}}
.

We now need the following claim.

Lemma 2.38
.

Let
D
be a division ring such that
|D|>2
. Suppose that
n\in\mathbb{N}
is a constant, and
f(x)=1-x^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
. If there exists
d\in D\backslash\left\{0\right\}
such that
f(d)\neq 0
, then
{\rm M}_{m}(D)
is not an
n
-UU ring for any
m\geq 2
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists
d\in D\backslash\left\{0\right\}
such that
f(d)\neq 0
. Writing

A:=\begin{pmatrix}f(d)&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

then one inspects that

I-A=\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}^{n},

where

\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in U(R).

So, this manifestly implies that the ring
{\rm M}_{m}(D)
is
not
an
n
-UU ring, as asserted.
∎

We now establish the following two reduction statements.

Lemma 2.39
.

If
R
is a ring and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(m,n)=d
, then
R
is a
d
-UU ring, provided
R
is simultaneously
m
-UU and
n
-UU.

Proof.

Given
a\in U(R)
, there exist
q,p\in{\rm Nil}(R)
such that
a^{n}=1+q
and
a^{m}=1+p
. Since
(m,n)=d
, there exist
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
km+k^{\prime}n=d
. Exploiting Proposition
2.17
, there exist
q^{\prime},p^{\prime}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
such that
a^{k^{\prime}n}=1+q^{\prime}
and
a^{km}=1+p^{\prime}
. Consequently,

a^{-km}(1-a^{d})=a^{-km}-a^{k^{\prime}n}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Since
a\in U(R)
, we have
1-a^{d}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, as required.
∎

As an automatic consequence, we obtain:

Corollary 2.40
.

If
R
is a ring and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(m,n)=1
, then
R
is an UU ring, provided
R
is both
m
-UU and
n
-UU.

2.3.
Group rings

Firstly, we would like to recollect some background material on group rings that we will use in the sequel. In fact, if
R
is a ring and
G
is a group, the symbol
RG
denotes the group ring of the group
G
over
R
. The ring homomorphism
\omega:RG\to R
, defined by

\sum r_{g}g\to\sum r_{g},

is said to be
the augmentation map
, and
ker(\omega)
is called
the augmentation ideal
of the group ring
RG
and is denoted by
\Delta(RG)
. That is,

\Delta(RG)=\{\sum r_{g}g\in RG:\sum r_{g}=0\}.

Next, let
\pi
be a non-empty set of prime numbers. We shall say that an integer
n
is
a
\pi
-number
if all its prime divisors lie within the set
\pi
. Let
G
be a group and let
g\in G
be an element of finite order, say
{\rm o}(g)
. Then, the element
g
is called
a
\pi
-element
if
{\rm o}(g)
is a
\pi
-number. Likewise,
G
is said to be
a
\pi
-torsion group
if every element of
G
is a
\pi
-element. Specifically, if
\pi=\{\ p\}
, then
G
is the standard
p
-group. Therefore, in a
p
-group, the order of every element is a power of the prime number
p
. If, however,
G
does not possess
\pi
-torsion elements other than the identity, then
G
is known as a torsion-free
\pi
-group. Moreover,
G
is termed
locally finite
if every finitely generated subgroup of
G
is finite. It is clear that every finite group is locally finite, but the converse fails.

Our purpose here is to expand
[
11
, Theorem 2.1]
pertaining to the criterion of (
not
necessarily commutative) UU group rings over locally finite groups. Before doing that, we need the following pivotal statements.

Theorem 2.41
.

[
12
, Theorem 3.2]

Let
R
be a ring with
{\rm char}(R)=m<\infty
. If
a\in R
is such that
a^{s+1}=0
for some integer
s\geq 0
, then
(1-a)^{m^{s}}=1
.

We have now accumulated all the information necessary to prove the following major for this subsection result.

Theorem 2.42
.

Let
R
be a ring with
{\rm char}(R)=m
and let
G
be a group. Also, for positive integers
m>1
,
n\geq 1
, assume
\pi
is the set of prime divisors of
m
and
n
. If
RG
is an
n
-UU ring, then
R
is an
n
-UU ring and
G
is a
\pi
-torsion group.

Proof.

Since
R\subseteq RG
may be viewed as a natural embedding, in view of Proposition
2.22
,
R
is an
n
-UU ring. Now, if
g\in G
, then
g\in U(RG)
, and since
RG
is an
n
-UU ring we have
g^{n}=1-q
, where
q\in{\rm Nil}(RG)
. Assuming that
q^{s+1}=0
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
, then Theorem
2.41
gives that
(1-q)^{m^{s}}=1
, whence

g^{nm^{s}}=(g^{n})^{m^{s}}=(1-q)^{m^{s}}=1,

as required.
∎

Proposition 2.43
.

Let
R
be an
n
-UU ring such that
p\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, where
p
is a prime number. Also, suppose that
G
is a locally finite
p
-group. Then,
RG
is an
n
-UU ring.

Proof.

According to
[
7
, Proposition 16]
, the ideal
\Delta(RG)
is nil. Furthermore, by combining the isomorphism
R\cong RG/\Delta(RG)
and Lemma
2.29
, the assertion is concluded.
∎

As three useful consequences, we yield:

Corollary 2.44
.

Let
R
be an
n
-UU ring, where
n
is odd, and also suppose that
G
is a locally finite
2
-group. Then,
RG
is an
n
-UU ring.

Corollary 2.45
.

[
11
, Theorem 2.1]

Let
G
be a group and
R
a ring.

(i)

If
RG
is a UU ring, then
R
is a UU ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

(ii)

If
G
is locally finite, then
RG
is a UU ring if, and only if,
R
is a UU ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Employing
[
12
, Theorem 2.6(1)]
, one knows that
2\in{\rm Nil}(R)
and
{\rm char}(R)=2^{t}
where
t\geq 0
. Therefore, by setting
n=1
in Theorem
2.42
and Proposition
2.43
, the proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 2.46
.

Let
R
be a ring with
{\rm char}(R)=p^{k}
, where
p
is a prime number, and let
G
be a torsion-free
\pi
-group, where
\pi
is the set of prime divisors of
n
. If
RG
is an
n
-UU ring, then
R
is an
n
-UU ring and
G
is a
p
-group.

Proof.

According to Theorem
2.42
,
R
is an
n
-UU ring and
G
is a
(\{p\}\cup\pi)
-torsion group, where
\pi
is the set of prime divisors of
n
.

Assume
\pi=\{q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k}\}
, where, for every
1\leq i\leq k
,
q_{i}
is a prime number. Supposing
g\in G
, then

{\rm o}(g)=q_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots q_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}p^{\beta}.

However,

{\rm o}(g^{p^{\beta}})=q_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}...q_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}.

Consequently, the element
g^{p^{\beta}}
is a
\pi
-element. And since
G
is a torsion-free
\pi
-group, it must be that
g^{p^{\beta}}=1
, that is,
G
is a
p
-group, as stated.
∎

As a consequence, we extract the following.

Corollary 2.47
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free
\pi
-group, where
\pi
is the set of prime divisors of
n
and
n
is odd. If
RG
is an
n
-UU ring, then
R
is an
n
-UU ring and
G
is a
2
-group.
