1.
Introduction

A properly embedded connected hypersurface
S
in a complete connected Riemannian manifold
M
is called homogeneous if the subgroup of isometries of
M
preserving
S
acts transitively on
S
. A proper isometric action of a connected Lie group
G
on
M
is said to be of cohomogeneity one if it has a codimension one orbit, and two such actions are called orbit equivalent if there is an isometry of
M
mapping the orbits of one action onto the orbits of the other. Nonsingular orbits of cohomogeneity-one actions are homogeneous hypersurfaces and, conversely, every homogeneous hypersurface arises in this way. Since a cohomogeneity-one action is uniquely determined by any of its orbits, the problem of classification of homogeneous hypersurfaces up to isometric congruence is equivalent to that of cohomogeneity-one actions up to orbit equivalence. Such actions have been in the limelight because they can be used to construct various geometric structures on manifolds, like Einstein metrics or metrics with special holonomy. In the context of Riemannian symmetric spaces, they also reveal new connections between geometric and algebraic properties of the space as well as provide the chief example of polar and hyperpolar actions.

The classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on irreducible symmetric spaces of compact type was obtained by Kollross (see
[
15
]
and further references there). The reducible case is still open. In the noncompact rank-one situation, the classification was obtained by Berndt, Tamaru, and Brück for real and complex hyperbolic spaces and the Cayley hyperbolic plane (
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
) and by Díaz-Ramos, Domínguez-Vázquez, and Rodríguez-Vázquez for quaternionic hyperbolic spaces (
[
11
]
). The higher rank noncompact case, however, presents a significantly more difficult problem. It is a standard fact about cohomogeneity-one actions on Hadamard manifolds that any such action has at most one singular orbit (and no exceptional orbits). Berndt and Tamaru classified cohomogeneity-one actions on irreducible symmetric spaces of noncompact type that have either no singular orbits (
[
6
]
) or a totally geodesic singular orbit (
[
7
]
). They later went on to prove that any cohomogeneity-one action on such a space
M
that has a non-totally-geodesic singular orbit must arise from one of two new methods proposed in
[
9
]
: the canonical extension and the nilpotent construction. The canonical extension method allows one to obtain cohomogeneity-one actions on
M
from those on certain totally geodesic submanifolds of
M
called boundary components. These submanifolds are noncompact symmetric spaces of lower rank, which, in theory, allows one to proceed by induction on rank. The nilpotent construction method reduces the search for cohomogeneity-one actions to a certain problem in representation theory of reductive Lie groups. This method is arguably more involved and has so far produced only two new examples not arising through any other technique. It is noteworthy that both of them are actions on exceptional symmetric spaces related to the exceptional Lie group
G_{2}
.

Using the above results, Berndt and Tamaru obtained the explicit classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on symmetric spaces
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
G_{2}^{2}/\mathrm{SO}(4)
(
[
9
]
). Later, Berndt and Domínguez-Vázquez refined the nilpotent construction method, which allowed them to obtain the classification on
G_{2}(\mathbb{C})/G_{2},\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(3),
and
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{R}^{n+4})=\mathrm{SO}^{0}(n+2,2)/\mathrm{SO}(n+2)\mathrm{SO}(2),\,n\geqslant 1
(
[
5
]
).

The purpose of the present article is to coalesce ideas from these two papers as well as develop some new computational techniques to obtain the explicit classification of cohomogeneity-one actions – and thus homogeneous hypersurfaces – on several noncompact symmetric spaces of rank 2 that have not been considered yet, namely on
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{H})/\mathrm{Sp}(3),\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(5),
and
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+4})=\mathrm{SU}(n+2,2)/\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n+2)\mathrm{U}(2)),n\geqslant 1
. The first of them is the quaternionic analog of the spaces
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(3)
that have already been dealt with. In order to handle this space, we have to use the classification of so-called protohomogeneous subspaces of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
that was recently obtained in
[
11
]
. The space
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+4})
is the complex analog of the real noncompact Grassmannian of two-planes studied by Berndt and Domínguez-Vázquez. It is also the noncompact dual of the ordinary Grassmannian of complex two-planes, a space whose differential geometry was studied in depth by Berndt in
[
1
]
. We adapt some results from that paper to deal with the congruence problem for actions on
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+4})
. Finally,
\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(5)
is a space with root system of type
B_{2}
dual to the compact symmetric space
\mathrm{Spin}(5)
. The nilpotent construction problem is not so onerous for this space, so we handle it using rather elementary methods and no auxiliary results. The main result of the paper is that the nilpotent construction method yields no new actions on these spaces. This provides further evidence to the hypothesis made by Berndt and Tamaru in
[
9
]
that the nilpotent construction only yields new actions for exceptional symmetric spaces, and perhaps only those related to
G_{2}
.

There are still a few noncompact symmetric spaces of rank 2 for which the classification of cohomogeneity-one actions remains open, namely
E_{6}^{-26}/F_{4}
(type
A_{2}
),
\mathrm{Sp}(2,2)/\mathrm{Sp}(2)\mathrm{Sp}(2)
(type
C_{2}
),
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{H}^{n+4}),E_{6}^{-14}/\mathrm{Spin}(10)\mathrm{U}(1),
and
\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{H})/\mathrm{U}(5)
(all three of type
(BC)_{2}
). Unsurprisingly, the main obstacle to the classification for all of them is the complexity of the nilpotent construction problem. For example, for
E_{6}^{-26}/F_{4}
it involves dealing with the 16-dimensional spin representation of
\mathrm{Spin}(9)
, while for
\mathrm{Sp}(2,2)/\mathrm{Sp}(2)\mathrm{Sp}(2)
one has a 10-dimensional representation of the Lorentz group
\mathrm{SO}^{0}(5,1)
. Undoubtedly, novel conceptual methods are needed if one hopes to solve the classification problem for all symmetric spaces of noncompact type.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews the terminology, notation, and previous results needed to understand the rest of the paper. We also introduce some new terminology helpful for studying the nilpotent construction problem (Subsection
2.3
). Apart from that, we correct a small conceptual error that occurred in
[
9
]
and a few other publications of its authors (Subsection
2.4
) and prove a useful result for noncompact rank-
1
symmetric spaces allowing one to represent cohomogeneity-one actions with a singular orbit on them in a uniform manner (Subsection
2.5
). Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
are each dedicated to one of the three spaces in question.

(1)

(2)

Theorem

2.3.
Generalizing the nilpotent construction problem.

Since the nilpotent construction is so involved and presents one of the main hindrances to the classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on irreducible symmetric spaces of noncompact type, it is worth looking at it from a slightly more general perspective and introducing some new terminology to talk about it more easily.

Let
G
be a reductive Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{{g}}
, a maximal compact subgroup
K\subseteq G
, and a Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{{g}}=\mathfrak{{k}}\oplus\mathfrak{{p}}
(we are using the notation from
[
14
, Section VII.2]
in this subsection). Let
V
be a finite-dimensional real representation of
G
. Fix some
K
-invariant inner product on
V
and let
\mathfrak{{v}}\subseteq V
be a subspace with
\dim\mathfrak{{v}}\geqslant 2
. Inspired by
[
11
]
, we call
\mathfrak{{v}}

protohomogeneous
if there exists a subgroup of
K
preserving
\mathfrak{{v}}
and acting transitively on the unit sphere in
\mathfrak{{v}}
. We call
\mathfrak{{v}}

admissible
if the projection of
N_{\mathfrak{{g}}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
to
\mathfrak{{p}}
along
\mathfrak{{k}}
is onto, i.e. its image equals the whole
\mathfrak{{p}}
. The problem, which we call
the generalized nilpotent construction problem
, is to classify subspaces of
V
that are both protohomogeneous and admissible up to the action of
K
. One readily sees how this generalizes the nilpotent construction problem by taking
G=M_{j}
(or
L_{j}
),
K=K_{j},\hskip 1.5pt\mathfrak{{g}}=\mathfrak{{m}}_{j}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{j}\oplus\mathfrak{{b}}_{j}
(or
\mathfrak{{l}}_{j}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{j}\oplus(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{j})
), and
V=\mathfrak{{n}}_{j}^{1}
. Admissibility and protohomogeneity become assumptions (1) and (2) of the nilpotent construction, respectively. Note that every subspace of
V
is trivially admissible when
G
is compact.

This problem is solved for a few representations. For instance, Berndt and Brück handled the cases
(G,V)=(\mathrm{SO}(n),\mathbb{R}^{n}),\hskip 1.0pt(\mathrm{U}(n),\mathbb{C}^{n}),\hskip 1.0pt(\mathrm{Spin}(7),\text{the spin representation}\;\mathbb{R}^{8})
in
[
2
]
, while Díaz-Ramos et al. dealt with
(G,V)=(\mathrm{Sp}(n)\mathrm{Sp}(1),\mathbb{H}^{n})
in
[
11
]
. See also
[
5
]
and
[
9
]
for some examples with
G
noncompact. The latter of these two papers contains also a general result on the nilpotent construction problem that facilitates the search for admissible and protohomogeneous subspaces for certain noncompact symmetric spaces and choices of
j
(see Proposition 5 there), and it may, in theory, be possible to extend this result to the generalized nilpotent construction problem. When dealing with the nilpotent construction on some rank-2 spaces later in the paper, we will solve this problem for a few more representations. We want to stress, however, that almost all attempts to solve the problem have so far been ad-hoc, and it does not seem feasible to proceed like this in the future. The more complex the representation of
G
on
V
is, the more difficult the problem becomes (for instance, it took the authors of
[
11
]
an entire paper to solve the problem for
\mathrm{Sp}(n)\mathrm{Sp}(1)\curvearrowright\mathbb{H}^{n}
!). We believe that a more holistic and general approach to the problem is necessary if one wants to solve the nilpotent construction problem for all symmetric spaces of noncompact type.

It is stated in the above works that

Proof.

\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}

\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}
. We conclude that
X\in Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j})
, so
Z_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{j}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})=Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j})
. Similar arguments show that
Z_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{j}}(\bigoplus_{\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha})=Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j})
.

Now we deal with the compact part of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}
. According to Corollary
2.2.1
, it coincides with
Z_{\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})
. We claim that
Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})
decomposes as an orthogonal direct sum
\mathfrak{{z}}_{j}\oplus Z_{\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})
. Indeed, it follows immediately from the fact that
Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})=Z_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{j}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})
and that
\mathfrak{{m}}_{j}=\mathfrak{{z}}_{j}\oplus^{\perp}\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}
. The assertion in the proposition about the compact part follows.

Now we have to show that the noncompact part of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}
is
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
and that it is orthogonal to the compact part. First, we show that
Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})\perp\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
. Indeed, any
X
from the centralizer is already orthogonal to
\bigoplus_{\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}
, and if
Y,Z
lie in the latter, we have
\langle\hskip 1.00006pt{X}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt{[Y,Z]}\hskip 1.00006pt\rangle\hskip 0.20004pt=-B(X,[Y,Z])=-B([X,Y],Z)=0
. Since
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
is spanned as a vector space by
\bigoplus_{\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}
and
[\bigoplus_{\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha},\bigoplus_{\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}]
,
X
is orthogonal to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
. Next, assume
X\in\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
is orthogonal to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
. We want to show that it lies in
Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})
, that is, commutes with all
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}
’s,
\upalpha\in\Upsigma_{j}
. Given
Y\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}
,
Z=[X,Y]\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}
, and
||Z||^{2}=-B([X,Y],\uptheta Z)=-B(X,[Y,\uptheta Z])=\langle\hskip 1.00006pt{X}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt{[Y,\uptheta Z]}\hskip 1.00006pt\rangle\hskip 0.20004pt=0
, since
[Y,\uptheta Z]
lies in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
. We deduce that
\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}=(Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})\ominus\mathfrak{{z}}_{j})\oplus^{\perp}\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
.

The fact that
\widetilde{\mathfrak{{g}}}_{j}
is actually an ideal of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{j}
is obvious because it has a complementary ideal
Z_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{b}}_{j})\ominus\mathfrak{{z}}_{j}
with which it commutes.
∎

2.5.
Rank-one case.

Here we prove a small technical result pertaining to cohomogeneity-one actions on rank-1 noncompact symmetric spaces, i.e. on hyperbolic spaces over finite-dimensional real normed division algebras. It will allow us to describe actions on rank-2 spaces canonically extended from their boundary components in a relatively nice and uniform way. Note that if
M
is a noncompact symmetric space of rank
1
, we have
\Uplambda=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
and
\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
, so
M_{1}
is compact and every subspace of
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}^{1}
is automatically admissible.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
M
be a symmetric space of noncompact type and rank
1
.

(1)

If
\mathfrak{{v}}\subseteq\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}^{1}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}
is a protohomogeneous subspace, then
\mathfrak{{h}}_{\mathfrak{{v}}}=N_{\mathfrak{{k}}}(\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}})\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{g}}
whose corresponding connected Lie subgroup is closed, acts on
M
with cohomogeneity one, and in fact has the same orbits as
H_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}
.

(2)

Let
V\subseteq\mathfrak{{p}}
be a Lie triple system corresponding to a totally geodesic orbit
S
of some cohomogeneity-one action on
M
, assume
\mathfrak{{a}}\subseteq V
, and let
\mathfrak{{h}}
stand for the preimage of
V
under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 6.3941pt\hskip-6.3941pt\hbox{$\xrightarrow{\phantom{\sim}}$}\hskip-6.3941pt\hskip-4.76384pt\raisebox{0.64444pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\!\sim\,$}}\hskip-4.76384pt\hskip 6.3941pt}}\mathfrak{{p}}
. Then
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}}(\mathfrak{{h}})\oplus\mathfrak{{h}}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{g}}
whose corresponding connected Lie subgroup is closed, acts on
M
with cohomogeneity one, and has
S
as its orbit.

Proof.

For the first part, note that
\mathfrak{{h}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}=N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}}(\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}})\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}
, so it is contained in
\mathfrak{{h}}_{\mathfrak{{v}}}
(which is clearly a Lie subalgebra) and has the same projection in
\mathfrak{{p}}
as
\mathfrak{{h}}_{\mathfrak{{v}}}
. Since we already know that
H_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}
acts with cohomogeneity one, it follows that its orbits coincide with those of the Lie subgroup corresponding to
\mathfrak{{h}}_{\mathfrak{{v}}}
. The latter is easily seen to be
N^{0}_{K}(AN_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}})AN_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}}
, which is closed by the global Iwasawa decomposition.

For the second part, our assumption implies
\mathfrak{{a}}\subseteq\mathfrak{{h}}
, so we can write
\mathfrak{{h}}=\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{h}}_{n}
, where
\mathfrak{{h}}_{n}=\mathfrak{{h}}\cap\mathfrak{{n}}
. Assume for a moment that we know that
\mathfrak{{h}}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}
and write
H
for its connected Lie subgroup. Clearly,
\mathfrak{{h}}_{n}
is then also a Lie subalgebra. Its corresponding Lie subgroup
H_{n}
of
N
is closed because the exponential map of
N
is a diffeomorphism. We see that
H=AH_{n}
and hence it is also a closed subgroup. Under our assumption,
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}}(\mathfrak{{h}})\oplus\mathfrak{{h}}
is trivially a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{g}}
and its Lie subgroup is
N_{K}^{0}(H)H
, which is closed. Finally,
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}}(\mathfrak{{h}})=N_{\mathfrak{{k}}}(V)
, and the fact that
S
is an orbit of some cohomogeneity-one action implies that the slice representation of
N^{0}_{K}(H)H
at
o
is of cohomogeneity one, hence so is the action of
N^{0}_{K}(H)H
on
M
.

It follows that we only need to prove that
\mathfrak{{h}}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}
and its corresponding Lie subgroup
H
has
S
as an orbit (a priori, we only know that its orbit through
o
is of the same dimension and touches
S
at
o
tangentially), which we are going to do in a rather ad-hoc fashion. Consider first the special case when our totally geodesic orbit is

(2.1)

S^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}H^{k}\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1),\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{R}H^{n},\\
\mathbb{C}H^{k}\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1)\;\text{or}\;\mathbb{R}H^{n},\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{C}H^{n},\\
\mathbb{H}H^{k}\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1)\;\text{or}\;\mathbb{C}H^{n},\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{H}H^{n},\\
\mathbb{O}H^{1}\;\text{or}\;\mathbb{H}H^{2},\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{O}H^{2},\end{cases}

where all these totally geodesic submanifolds are embedded into the corresponding hyperbolic space in a standard way. We are going to write
\mathfrak{{h}}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{{h}}^{\prime}_{n}
instead of
\mathfrak{{h}}
and
\mathfrak{{h}}_{n}
here to avoid notational confusion later on. Note that
S^{\prime}
is an orbit of the Lie subgroup

G^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{SO}^{0}(k,1)\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1),\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{R}H^{n},\\
\mathrm{SU}(k,1)\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1)\;\text{or}\;\mathrm{SO}^{0}(n,1),\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{C}H^{n},\\
\mathrm{Sp}(k,1)\;(0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1)\;\text{or}\;\mathrm{SU}(n,1),\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{H}H^{n},\\
\mathrm{SO}^{0}(8,1)\;\text{or}\;\mathrm{Sp}(2,1),\quad&\text{if}\;M=\mathbb{O}H^{2},\end{cases}

also embedded into the corresponding
G
in a standard way. But
G^{\prime}
is
\Uptheta
-stable and
V
coincides with
\mathfrak{{p}}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{{g}}^{\prime}\cap\mathfrak{{p}}
, where
\mathfrak{{g}}^{\prime}=\mathrm{Lie}\hskip 0.59998pt(G^{\prime})
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{{a}}\subseteq\mathfrak{{p}}^{\prime}
is a maximal abelian subspace, while
\Upsigma^{\prime}\cap\Upsigma^{+}
is a choice of positive restricted roots for
\mathfrak{{g}}^{\prime}
. It all implies that
\mathfrak{{h}}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{h}}^{\prime}_{n}
coincides with
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}^{\prime}
, where
\mathfrak{{n}}^{\prime}
is the sum of positive root subspaces of
\mathfrak{{g}}^{\prime}
. But the latter sum is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}
. What is more, since
S^{\prime}
is an orbit of
G^{\prime}
, it is also an orbit of
H^{\prime}=AH^{\prime}_{n}=AN^{\prime}\subseteq G^{\prime}
, so
S^{\prime}
chosen in such a special way satisfies the assertions of (2).

Totally geodesic submanifolds in (
2.1
) exhaust the list of totally geodesic orbits of cohomogeneity-one actions on hyperbolic spaces up to isometric congruence (see
[
2
, Theorem 1]
). In other words, given an arbitrary
S
as in part (2) of the proposition, it is congruent to some
S^{\prime}
as in (
2.1
), for which the assertions of (2) are satisfied. Now,
S^{\prime}
is a symmetric space of rank 1, so
I^{0}(S^{\prime})
acts homogeneously and isotropically on
S^{\prime}
. Moreover, every element of
I^{0}(S^{\prime})
can be extended to an isometry of
M
. This is because
S^{\prime}
is a semisimple and totally geodesic complete submanifold of
M
(see the argument in
[
7
, Proposition 3.2]
or
[
12
, Theorem V.4.1(i)]
). It follows that we can take our congruence
k\in I(M)
between
S
and
S^{\prime}
to preserve
o
and
\mathfrak{{a}}
and even fix
\mathfrak{{a}}
pointwise, i.e. to lie in
Z_{\widetilde{K}}(\mathfrak{{a}})
, where
\widetilde{K}
is the isotropy subgroup of
I(M)
at
o
. But such
k
preserves both
\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}
and
\mathfrak{{p}}
and is a Lie algebra automorphism of the former. Since it maps
V
onto
V^{\prime}
, it must map
\mathfrak{{h}}
onto
\mathfrak{{h}}^{\prime}
, so
\mathfrak{{h}}
is a Lie subalgebra. Moreover,
S^{\prime}
is an orbit of
H^{\prime}
, which means that
S
must be an orbit of
H
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.
Classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{H})/\mathrm{Sp}(3)

In this section we classify, up to orbit equivalence, cohomogeneity-one actions on the noncompact symmetric space
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{H})/\mathrm{Sp}(3)
.

The symmetric space

is irreducible of rank 2 and dimension 14, and its root system is

\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}(3)
and

[
5
]
, respectively. Let
\Uplambda=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1},\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
. Then
\Upsigma^{+}=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1},\upalpha_{2},\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
and all the roots have multiplicity 4. The Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{{sp}}(1)^{\oplus 3}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

be a closed connected subgroup acting on

(1)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup

where

.

(2)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup

of
G

\mathfrak{{h}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus(\mathfrak{{n}}\ominus\ell_{1}),

where
\ell_{1}
is any one-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}
. Its orbits form a Riemannian foliation on
M
and there is exactly one minimal orbit.

(3)

The action of the subgroup
L_{1}=L_{1}^{0}
of
G
. It has a 6-dimensional totally geodesic singular orbit
F_{1}\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{5}\times\mathbb{E}
.

(4)

The action of the subgroup
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})
of
G
embedded in a standard way. It has an 8-dimensional totally geodesic singular orbit isometric to
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(3)
.

(5)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup
H^{\Uplambda}_{1,k},k\in\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{0,1,2,3}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
, of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{{h}}_{1,k}^{\Uplambda}=N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{w}})\oplus\mathfrak{{w}}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1},

. Any element of
\mathfrak{{m}}_{2}
normalizing such a subspace must have
p_{12},p_{21}\in\mathbb{C}
, so the image of the projection of
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{2}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
to
\mathfrak{{p}}
is smaller than
\mathfrak{{b}}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{{v}}
is not admissible.

Finally, let
\mathfrak{{v}}
be of quaternionic Kähler angle
(0,\upvarphi,\upvarphi),\upvarphi\in(0,\frac{\uppi}{2})
. According to
[
2
]
(see the discussion before Theorem 5 there), we can take
\mathfrak{{v}}
to be spanned by
E_{23},iE_{23},j\cos\upvarphi E_{23}+j\sin\upvarphi E_{13},
and
k\cos\upvarphi E_{23}+k\sin\upvarphi E_{13}
. In a similar fashion to what we had in case 1, by solving the system of linear equations defining
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{2}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
, one gets – among other things – the same two linear dependencies
\hskip 0.70007pt\mathrm{Re}\hskip 0.70007pt(p_{12})=0
and
\hskip 0.70007pt\mathrm{Re}\hskip 0.70007pt(p_{21})=2\hskip 0.70007pt\mathrm{Re}\hskip 0.70007pt(p_{11})\cot\upvarphi
. Therefore, the projection of
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{2}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
in
\mathfrak{{p}}
is yet again contained in
\mathfrak{{b}}_{2}\cap\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\hskip 0.70007pt\mathrm{Re}\hskip 0.70007pt(p_{11})\cot\upvarphi-\hskip 0.70007pt\mathrm{Re}\hskip 0.70007pt(p_{12})=0}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
and
\mathfrak{{v}}
fails to be admissible. This finishes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4.
Classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on
\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(5)

In this section we classify, up to orbit equivalence, cohomogeneity-one actions on the noncompact dual of the compact Lie group
\mathrm{Spin}(5)
.

The symmetric space
\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(5)
is irreducible of rank 2 and dimension 10. Its root system is
B_{2}
and can be identified with the root system of the complex simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{{so}}(5,\mathbb{C})
. Consequently, all the root spaces have complex dimension 1. Let
\Uplambda=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1},\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
, where
\upalpha_{1}
is the longest root. Then
\Upsigma^{+}=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1},\upalpha_{2},\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2},\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
. We also have
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}=i\mathfrak{{a}}=\mathbb{R}iH_{\upalpha_{1}}\oplus^{\perp}\mathbb{R}iH^{2}=\mathbb{R}iH^{1}\oplus^{\perp}\mathbb{R}iH_{\upalpha_{2}}\simeq\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)
, where
i
the complex structure of
\mathfrak{{so}}(5,\mathbb{C})
. The fact that
\upalpha_{1}
and
\upalpha_{2}
have different lengths implies that the corresponding boundary components
B_{1}\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{3}
and
B_{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{3}
have different sectional curvatures (
-||\upalpha_{1}||^{2}
and
-||\upalpha_{2}||^{2}
, respectively) and thus are not congruent (which reflects the asymmetry of the Dynkin diagram
B_{2}
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
H\subset G=\mathrm{SO}(5,\mathbb{C})
be a closed connected subgroup acting on
M
with cohomogeneity one. Then its action is orbit equivalent to exactly one of the following:

(1)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup
H_{\ell}
of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{{h}}_{\ell}=(\mathfrak{{a}}\ominus\ell)\oplus\mathfrak{{n}},

where
\ell
is a one-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathfrak{{a}}
. Its orbits are all isometrically congruent to each other and form a Riemannian foliation on
M
.

(2)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup
H_{i},i\in\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{1,2}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\},
of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{{h}}_{i}=\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus(\mathfrak{{n}}\ominus\ell_{i}),

where
\ell_{i}
is any one-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{i}}
. Its orbits form a Riemannian foliation on
M
and there is exactly one minimal orbit.

(3)

The action of the subgroup
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})
of
G
embedded in a standard way. It has a
6
-dimensional totally geodesic singular orbit isometric to
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(4)\simeq\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}(2)\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{3}\times\mathbb{R}H^{3}
.

(4)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup
H^{\Uplambda}_{j,0},j\in\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{1,2}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
, of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{{h}}_{j,0}^{\Uplambda}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{j}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{j}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{j}.

This action has a minimal singular orbit of codimension
3
and can be obtained by canonical extension of the cohomogeneity-one action on
B_{j}
with a single point as a singular orbit.

(5)

The action of the connected Lie subgroup
H^{\Uplambda}_{j,1},j\in\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{1,2}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
, of
G
with Lie algebra

\mathfrak{{h}}_{j,1}^{\Uplambda}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{j},

This action has a minimal singular orbit of codimension
2
and can be obtained by canonical extension of the cohomogeneity-one action on
B_{j}
with a geodesic as a singular orbit.

Proof.

We consider different cases of Theorem
2.1
. If the orbits of
H
form a foliation, we get the actions in (1) and (2). The action in (3) is the only one that has a totally geodesic singular orbit according to
[
7
]
. The stabilizer of
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})
at
o
is
\mathrm{SO}(4)
, whose slice representation at
o
is equivalent to the tautological one (we have
(\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})\hskip-1.2pt\cdot\hskip-0.5pto)^{\perp}\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{4}
), hence
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})
does indeed act with cohomogeneity one. It is also worth noting that we can take

\mathfrak{{so}}(4,\mathbb{C})=\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\upalpha\in\Upsigma\\
\text{long root}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha}.

The long roots in
B_{2}
form a root system
A_{1}\sqcup A_{1}
sitting inside
B_{2}
, and the hyperbolic spaces in the decomposition
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SO}(4)\simeq\mathbb{R}H^{3}\times\mathbb{R}H^{3}
have the same curvature.

Now we determine the actions arising via the canonical extension method. Each boundary component
B_{j}
is isometric to the real hyperbolic space
\mathbb{R}H^{3}
(with different curvatures depending on
j
), which has precisely two cohomogeneity-one actions with a singular orbit up to strong orbit equivalence. One of them is the action of the (restricted) isotropy subgroup of
\mathbb{R}H^{3}
, which has a single point as a singular orbit and whose canonical extension is described in (4). The other one has a geodesic as a singular orbit and, by Proposition
2.5
and the observation made at the end of Subsection
2.4
, is given by the connected Lie subgroup corresponding to
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{j}}(\mathfrak{{a}}^{j})\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{j}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{j}
. Its canonical extension is given in (5). Observe that the actions in (4) and (5) are not orbit equivalent to each other because the normal spaces of their singular orbits are tangent to the corresponding boundary components and thus have different sectional curvatures.

Now we proceed to the main part, namely to the nilpotent construction. Since the Dynkin diagram
B_{2}
is asymmetric, we have to consider two cases.

Nilpotent construction with

j=2
. In this case we have:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{l}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{g}}_{-\upalpha_{1}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{2}\simeq\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\simeq\mathfrak{{gl}}(2,\mathbb{C}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{k}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{k}}_{\upalpha_{1}}=(\mathfrak{{g}}_{2}\cap\mathfrak{{k}})\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{2}\simeq\mathfrak{{su}}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\simeq\mathfrak{{u}}(2).

The adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{2}
on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
is a nontrivial complex representation, hence it is equivalent to the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{a}}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{2}=\mathbb{C}H^{2}
on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
is the standard complex representation by scalars because
H^{2}
acts on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
by multiplication by
\langle\hskip 1.00006pt{\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 1.00006pt,\hskip 1.00006pt{H^{2}}\hskip 1.00006pt\rangle\hskip 0.20004pt=1
. It follows that the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{l}}_{2}
(respectively,
\mathfrak{{k}}_{2}
) on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
is equivalent to the tautological representation of
\mathfrak{{gl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
(respectively,
\mathfrak{{u}}(2)
) on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Since
\mathfrak{{l}}_{2}
is a
\uptheta
-stable Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{g}}
, we may assume
\uptheta
corresponds to the standard Cartan involution on
\mathfrak{{gl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
(minus the adjoint of a matrix). Therefore, the problem of finding admissible and protohomogeneous subspaces of the
L^{0}_{2}
-module

\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
is equivalent to the analogous problem for the tautological representation of
\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

Let
\mathfrak{{v}}\subseteq\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
be a linear subspace of dimension at least 2. According to Lemma 1 and Proposition 7 in
[
2
]
,
\mathfrak{{v}}
is protohomogeneous if and only if it has constant Kähler angle. In particular, it must be even-dimensional. If
\mathfrak{{v}}=\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}^{1}
, then it is trivially admissible and we have
\mathfrak{{h}}_{2,\mathfrak{{v}}}=\mathfrak{{l}}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}=\mathfrak{{so}}(4,\mathbb{C})\ominus\mathfrak{{g}}_{-\upalpha_{1}-2\upalpha_{2}}
. Since we know that
H_{2,\mathfrak{{v}}}
acts with cohomogeneity one, it follows that it has the same orbits as
\mathrm{SO}(4,\mathbb{C})
, so its action has already been taken into account in (3). Now suppose
\mathfrak{{v}}
has real dimension 2. It was shown in
[
5
, Theorem 6]
that such a subspace is admissible if and only if its Kähler angle is zero, i.e. if it is a complex subspace. Up to the action of
K_{2}^{0}\simeq\mathrm{U}(2)
, we may assume
\mathfrak{{v}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}
. We have

\mathfrak{{h}}_{2,\mathfrak{{v}}}=\mathfrak{{l}}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{-\upalpha_{1}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}.

If we look back at (5), we see that
\mathfrak{{h}}_{1,1}^{\Uplambda}
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{{h}}_{2,\mathfrak{{v}}}
. Since
H_{1,1}^{\Uplambda}
acts with cohomogeneity one, its orbits coincide with the orbits of
H_{2,\mathfrak{{v}}}
. Altogether, we see that the nilpotent construction method with
j=2
does not give rise to any new actions.

Nilpotent construction with

j=1
. In this case we have:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}^{1}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{3},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{l}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{g}}_{-\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\simeq\mathfrak{{gl}}(2,\mathbb{C}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{k}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{k}}_{\upalpha_{2}}=(\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}\cap\mathfrak{{k}})\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{{su}}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\simeq\mathfrak{{u}}(2).

We fix an isomorphism between
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
and
\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}
by sending the standard basis
e,f,h
of the former to
X,\uptheta X,h_{\upalpha_{2}}\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}
, where
X\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}},\uptheta X\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{-\upalpha_{2}}
, and
h_{\upalpha_{2}}=\frac{2}{||\upalpha_{2}||^{2}}H_{\upalpha_{2}}
. Observe that
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
is an
\upalpha_{2}
-string, so it is an irreducible complex representation of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Since it is 3-dimensional, it is isomorphic to the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Now,
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
is simple, hence it is also simple over
\mathbb{R}
, which means that the representation of
\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}
on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
is irreducible as a real representation. An alternative description of this representation is the second symmetric power of the tautological representation of
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
. The adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{a}}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{1}=\mathbb{C}H^{1}
on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}^{1}
is the standard complex representation by scalars because
H^{1}
acts on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
by multiplication by
\langle\hskip 1.00006pt{\upalpha_{1}}\hskip 1.00006pt,\hskip 1.00006pt{H^{1}}\hskip 1.00006pt\rangle\hskip 0.20004pt=1
. Consequently, we can describe the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{{l}}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathbb{C}
(respectively,
\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}\simeq\mathfrak{{su}}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)
) on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
as the exterior tensor product
\uprho_{3}\otimes\upsigma
, where
\uprho_{3}
is the irreducible 3-dimensional complex representation of
\mathfrak{{sl}}(2,\mathbb{C})
(respectively, of
\mathfrak{{su}}(2)
) and
\upsigma
is the tautological representation of
\mathbb{C}
(respectively,
\mathfrak{{u}}(1)
) on
\mathbb{C}
.

Now we need to determine – up to
K_{1}^{0}\simeq\mathrm{U}(2)
– all subspaces
\mathfrak{{v}}
of
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
which are both protohomogeneous and admissible. We begin by borrowing an argument from
[
5
, Proposition 6]
. The case
\mathfrak{{v}}=\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
can be excluded straight away since
\mathrm{U}(2)
cannot act transitively on the 5-sphere. According to
[
5
, Proposition 5]
, we may assume that
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})=\uptheta N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{v}}})
is contained in
(\mathfrak{{g}}_{1}\cap(\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}))\oplus\mathfrak{{z}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}
. In that case,
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
is contained in
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\simeq\mathfrak{{u}}(1)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)
. But then
\mathfrak{{v}}
must be 2-dimensional, for
K_{0}^{0}\simeq\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{U}(1)
cannot act transitively on any sphere of dimension greater than one. We will show that – under the assumption
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})\subseteq\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}
– the only option for
\mathfrak{{v}}
is
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}
.

Take nonzero vectors
e_{j}\in\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+j\upalpha_{2}},j\in\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{0,1,2}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\},
such that
e_{j}=\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(X)e_{j-1}
. Let
v=\sum_{j=0}^{2}z_{j}e_{j}\in\mathfrak{{v}}
be a nonzero vector. A generic element of
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}
can be written as

Y=(a+ib)X+ch_{\upalpha_{2}}+i(dh_{\upalpha_{2}}+eH^{1}),

where
a,b,c,d,
and
e
are some real numbers. If
\mathfrak{{v}}
lies in
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}
, then
a
and
b
must be zero for
Y
to normalize
\mathfrak{{v}}
, which means that the projection of
N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
in
\mathfrak{{p}}
will be smaller than
\mathfrak{{b}}_{1}
in that case. Hence, we may assume that
z_{2}\neq 0
and, for the sake of contradiction, that either
z_{0}
or
z_{1}
is also nonzero. Observe that
\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(h_{\upalpha_{2}})
acts diagonally on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}
with eigenvalues
-2,0,
and
2
, whereas
\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(H^{1})
acts as the identity on the whole
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
. Consequently,
i(dh_{\upalpha_{2}}+eH^{1})
acts on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
diagonally with eigenvalues
i(e-2d),ie,i(e+2d)
. It implies that
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})\subseteq\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
must be one-dimensional (otherwise it would be the whole
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{{v}}
would have to be of dimension at least 3). In this situation,
N_{\mathfrak{{k}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{v}})
is spanned by some
i(d_{0}h_{\upalpha_{2}}+e_{0}H^{1})
and
\mathfrak{{v}}=\mathbb{R}\hskip 0.2ptv\hskip 0.5pt\oplus\hskip 0.5pt\mathbb{R}\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(i(d_{0}h_{\upalpha_{2}}+e_{0}H^{1}))\hskip 0.4ptv
. In order for
\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(Y)
to normalize
\mathfrak{{v}}
,
\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(Y)\hskip 0.5ptv
must be a linear combination of
v
and
\mathrm{ad}\hskip 0.59998pt(i(d_{0}h_{\upalpha_{2}}+e_{0}H^{1}))v
, which boils down to the following system of equations:

\begin{cases}(-2c+i(e-2d))z_{1}=\uplambda z_{1}+i\upmu(e_{0}-2d_{0})z_{1},\\
(a+ib)z_{1}+iez_{2}=\uplambda z_{2}+i\upmu e_{0}z_{2},\\
(a+ib)z_{2}+(2c+i(e+2d))z_{3}=\uplambda z_{3}+i\upmu(e_{0}+2d_{0})z_{3}.\end{cases}

First assume that
z_{1}\neq 0
. The first equation then implies
\uplambda=-2c
, which, when substituted into the second one, gives

a+ib=\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}(-2c+i(\upmu e_{0}-e)).

By writing
\dfrac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}=f+ig
, we obtain

\begin{cases}a=-2fc-g(\upmu e_{0}-e),\\
b=-2gc+f(\upmu e_{0}-e).\end{cases}

Whatever
f
and
g
are, we get a linear dependency on
a,b,e
, which means that
\mathfrak{{v}}
cannot be admissible. In case
z_{1}=0
but
z_{2}\neq 0
, the second equation of our system implies
\uplambda=0
, which transforms the third equation into

a+ib=\frac{z_{3}}{z_{2}}(-2c+i(\upmu(e_{0}+2d_{0})-e-2d)).

In a similar way, we get a linear dependency on
a,b,e
.

The upshot of the above argument is that, up to
K_{1}^{0}
,
\mathfrak{{v}}
must be equal to
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}
. Observe that this is indeed an admissible and protohomogeneous subspace. Since the representation of
\mathrm{SU}(2)\subset K_{1}^{0}
on
\mathfrak{{n}}_{1}
is equivalent to the 3-dimensional complex irreducible representation of
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, there exists an element of
K_{1}^{0}
that maps
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}
onto
\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}
(in terms of the representation on the space of quadratic polynomials, we can take the special unitary matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&\hskip 4.09723pt0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, which induces
y^{2}\mapsto x^{2}
). We have:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}},

\displaystyle N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}})

\displaystyle=\uptheta N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}})=\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}^{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{{h}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}}

\displaystyle=N_{\mathfrak{{m}}_{1}}(\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}})\oplus\mathfrak{{a}}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}}\oplus\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}=\mathfrak{{g}}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{{n}}_{2}.

But looking at the actions given in (5), one sees that
\mathfrak{{h}}_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}}\hskip-2.5pt=\hskip 0.5pt\mathfrak{{h}}_{2,1}^{\Uplambda}
, which means that the actions of
H_{2,1}^{\Uplambda}
and
H_{1,\mathfrak{{g}}_{\upalpha_{1}}}
have the same orbits. We conclude that the nilpotent construction method does not give any new actions for
M
.
∎

5.
Classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on the noncompact complex two-plane Grassmannians

In this section we classify, up to orbit equivalence, cohomogeneity-one actions on the noncompact complex Grassmann manifolds
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{C}^{n+4})=\mathrm{SU}(n+2,2)/\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n+2)\mathrm{U}(2)),n\geqslant 1
.

The symmetric space
M=\mathrm{SU}(n+2,2)/\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n+2)\mathrm{U}(2))
is irreducible of rank 2 and dimension
4n+8
, and its root system is
BC_{2}
. This is the complex analog of the symmetric space
\mathrm{Gr}^{*}(2,\mathbb{R}^{n+4})=\mathrm{SO}^{0}(n+2,2)/\mathrm{SO}(n+2)\mathrm{SO}(2)
of type
B_{2}
, the classification of cohomogeneity-one actions on which, as we mentioned earlier, was obtained by Berndt and Domínguez-Vázquez in
[
5
]
. If we choose simple roots
\upalpha_{1}
and
\upalpha_{2}
such that
2\upalpha_{2}
is also a root, then we have
\Upsigma^{+}=\hskip-0.8pt\left\{\hskip 0.29999pt{\upalpha_{1},\upalpha_{2},2\upalpha_{2},\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2},\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2},2\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}}\hskip 0.29999pt\right\}
, where
\upalpha_{1}
and
\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}
have multiplicity 2,
\upalpha_{2}
and
\upalpha_{1}+\upalpha_{2}
have multiplicity
2n
, and
2\upalpha_{2}
and
2\upalpha_{1}+2\upalpha_{2}
have multiplicity 1. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{{k}}_{0}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{{u}}(n)\oplus\mathfrak{{u}}(1)
. Observe that
M
is a Hermitian symmetric space and it also has a quaternion-Kähler structure. Let
I
and
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathrm{End}(TM)
denote the almost complex and quaternion-Kähler structures of
M
, respectively (the latter is a rank-3 subbundle of the endomorphism bundle of
TM
). We will use the interplay between them to distinguish between non-equivalent cohomogeneity-one actions on
M
. The boundary components
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are isometric to
\mathbb{C}H^{n+1}
and
\mathbb{R}H^{3}
, respectively. Therefore, for the first time, we are encountering a noncompact rank-2 symmetric space containing a boundary component not isometric to a real hyperbolic space. The reason why this is special is because, unlike real hyperbolic spaces, complex hyperbolic spaces have a nondiscrete moduli space of cohomogeneity-one actions with a singular orbit, so the canonical extension method, when applied to
B_{1}
, will produce a one-parameter family of cohomogeneity-one actions with a singular orbit on
M
. In order to be able to formulate the theorem, we need to know what the almost complex structure
I
on
M
looks like in terms of the restricted root space decomposition of
\mathfrak{{g}}
. With respect to
I_{o}
,

We already know what the complex structure of

\mathfrak{{k}}=\left\{\left.\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\begin{array}[]{c c c}&&\\
