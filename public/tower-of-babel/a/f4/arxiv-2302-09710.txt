Normal ordering associated with λ \lambda -Stirling numbers in λ \lambda -Shift algebra Thanks: * is corresponding author
Taekyun Kim 1,∗, Dae San Kim 2

Abstract
. The Stirling numbers of the second kind are related to normal orderings in the Weyl algebra, while the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind are related to normal orderings in the shift algebra. Kim-Kim introduced a λ \lambda -analogue of the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind and that of the r r -Stirling numbers of the first kind. In this paper, we introduce a λ \lambda -analogue of the shift algebra (called λ \lambda -shift algebra) and investigate normal orderings in the λ \lambda -shift algebra. From the normal orderings in the λ \lambda -shift algebra, we derive some identities about the λ \lambda -analogue of the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind .

Introduction

The Stirling number of the first kind
S_{1}(n,k)
is defined in such a way that the unsigned Stirling number of the first kind
{n\brack k}=(-1)^{n-k}S_{1}(n,k)
enumerates the number of permutations of the set
[n]=\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}
which are products of
r
disjoint cycles.

The unsigned
r
-Stirling number of the first kind
{n\brack k}_{r}
is the number of permutations of
[n]
with exactly
k
disjoint cycles in such a way that the numbers
1,2,\dots,r
are in distinct cycles.

In
[
16
]
, introduced are a
\lambda
-analogue of the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind
{n\brack k}_{\lambda}
and that of the unsigned
r
-Stirling numbers of the first kind
{n\brack k}_{r,\lambda}
respectively as a
\lambda
-analogue of
{n\brack k}
and that of
{n\brack k}_{r}
,   (see (
8
), (
9
)).

The Stirling numbers of the second kind appear as the coefficients in normal orderings in the Weyl algebra (see (
10
), (
11
)), while the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind appear as those in normal orderings in the shift algebra
S
(see (
12
), (
13
)).

The aim of this paper is to introduce the
\lambda

S_{\lambda}
(for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

\lambda

\lambda

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the
\lambda
-falling factorial sequence is defined by

(1)

\begin{split}(x)_{0,\lambda}=1,\ \ (x)_{n,\lambda}=x(x-\lambda)\cdots(x-(n-1)\lambda),\quad(n\geq 1),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{4,11}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

In particular, the falling factorial sequence is given by

(2)

\begin{split}(x)_{0}=1,\ \ (x)_{n}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-(n-1)),\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

Note that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}(x)_{n,\lambda}=(x)_{n}
.

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, the
\lambda
-rising factorial sequence is defined by

(3)

\begin{split}\langle x\rangle_{0,\lambda}=1,\,\,\langle x\rangle_{n,\lambda}=x(x+\lambda)\cdots(x+(n-1)\lambda),\quad(n\geq 1),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{4,11}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

Especially, the rising factorial sequence is given by

(4)

\begin{split}\langle x\rangle_{0}=1,\,\,\langle x\rangle_{n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+(n-1)),\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

Observe that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\langle x\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\langle x\rangle_{n}
.

With the notation in (
2
), the Stirling numbers of the first kind are defined by

(5)

\begin{split}(x)_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{1}(n,k)x^{k},\quad(n\geq 0),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{5,6,19,23}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

In addition, the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind are given by
{n\brack k}=(-1)^{n-k}S_{1}(n,k),\,\,(n,\ k\geq 0)
.

The Stirling numbers of the second kind are defined by

(6)

\begin{split}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brace k}(x)_{k},\quad(n\geq 0).\end{split}

Recently, with the notation in (
1
) the
\lambda
-Stirling numbers of the first kind, which are
\lambda
-analogues of the Stirling numbers of the first kind, are defined by

(7)

\begin{split}(x)_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{1,\lambda}(n,k)x^{k},\quad(n\geq 0),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{11}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

In addition, with the notation in (
3
) the unsigned
\lambda
-Stirling numbers of the first kind are defined by

(8)

\begin{split}\langle x\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k},\quad(n\geq 0),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{11}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

Note that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}S_{1,\lambda}(n,k)=S_{1}(n,k)\,\,(\mathrm{see}\eqref{eq05}),\,\,\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}={n\brack k}
.

For
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the
\lambda
-
r
-Stirling numbers of the first kind, which are
\lambda
-analogues of the
r
-Stirling numbers of the first kind, are defined by

(9)

\begin{split}\langle x+r\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r,\lambda}x^{k},\quad(n\geq 0),\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{11}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

Note that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r,\lambda}={n+r\brack k+r}
, where
{n+r\brack k+r}
are the
r
-Stirling numbers of the first kind which are introduced by Broder (
see
[
3
]
) and given by (see (
4
))

\begin{split}\langle x+r\rangle_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r}x^{k},\quad(n\geq 0).\end{split}

The Weyl algebra is the unital algebra generated by letters
a
and
a^{\dagger}
satisfying the commutation

(10)

\begin{split}aa^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}a=1,\quad(\text{see [1, 2, 7-21]}).\end{split}

Katriel proved that the normal ordering in Weyl algebra is given by (see (
6
))

(11)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brace k}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{k},\quad(\text{see [7-9]}).\end{split}

From the definition of the Stirling numbers of the second kind and (
11
), we note that

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger})^{n}a^{n}=(a^{\dagger}a)_{n}=a^{\dagger}a(a^{\dagger}a-1)\cdots(a^{\dagger}a-n+1),\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

The shift algebra
S
is defined as the complex unital algebra generated by
a
and
a^{\dagger}
satisfying the commutation relation

(12)

\begin{split}aa^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}a=a,\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{20}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

A word in
S
is said to be in normal ordered form if all letters
a
stand to the right of all letters
a^{\dagger}
.

From (
12
), we note that the normal ordering in the shift algebra
S
is given by (see (
11
))

(13)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n},\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{20}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we consider a
\lambda
-analogue of the shift algebra
S
which is defined as the complex unital algebra generated by
a
and
a^{\dagger}
satisfying the commutation relation (see (
12
))

(14)

\begin{split}aa^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}a=\lambda a.\end{split}

The
\lambda
-analogue of the shift algebra
S
is called the
\lambda
-shift algebra and denoted by
S_{\lambda}
.

2.
\lambda
-analogues of normal ordering in the
\lambda
-shift algebra.

Let
S_{\lambda}
be the
\lambda
-shift algebra defined in (
14
).
A word in
S_{\lambda}
is said to be in normal ordered form if all letters
a
stand to the right of all letters
a^{\dagger}
.

In
S_{\lambda}
, by (
14
), we get

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{2}&=(a^{\dagger}a)(a^{\dagger}a)=a^{\dagger}(aa^{\dagger})a=a^{\dagger}(\lambda a+a^{\dagger}a)a\\
&=a^{\dagger}(\lambda+a^{\dagger})a^{2}=\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle_{2,\lambda}a^{2},\end{split}

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{3}&=(a^{\dagger}a)(a^{\dagger}a)(a^{\dagger}a)=a^{\dagger}(aa^{\dagger})(aa^{\dagger})a\\
&=a^{\dagger}(\lambda+a^{\dagger})a(\lambda+a^{\dagger})a^{2}=a^{\dagger}(\lambda+a^{\dagger})(a\lambda+aa^{\dagger})a^{2}\\
&=a^{\dagger}(\lambda+a^{\dagger})(2\lambda a+a^{\dagger}a)a^{2}=a^{\dagger}(a^{\dagger}+\lambda)(a^{\dagger}+2\lambda)a^{3}\\
&=\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle_{3,\lambda}a^{3}.\end{split}

Continuing this process, we have

(15)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle_{n,\lambda}a^{n},\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

Thus, by (
8
) and (
15
), we get (see (
7
), (
11
), (
13
))

(16)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
16
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

In
S_{\lambda}
, the unsigned
\lambda
-Stirling numbers of the first kind appear as the coefficients of
(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}
in normal ordered form, as it is given by

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}.\end{split}

For
r\geq 0
, by (
14
), we get

\begin{split}((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{2}&=((a^{\dagger}+r)a)((a^{\dagger}+r)a)=(a^{\dagger}+r)(aa^{\dagger}+ra)a\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+r)(a^{\dagger}a+\lambda a+ra)a=(a^{\dagger}+r)(a^{\dagger}+r+\lambda)a^{2}\\
&=\langle a^{\dagger}+r\rangle_{2,\lambda}a^{2},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{3}&=((a^{\dagger}+r)a)((a^{\dagger}+r)a)((a^{\dagger}+r)a)\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+r)a(a^{\dagger}+r)(\lambda+r+a^{\dagger})a^{2}\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+r)(\lambda+r+a^{\dagger})a(\lambda+r+a^{\dagger})a^{2}\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+r)(a^{\dagger}+r+\lambda)(\lambda a+ra+aa^{\dagger})a^{2}\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+r)(a^{\dagger}+r+\lambda)(a^{\dagger}+r+2\lambda)a^{3}=\langle a^{\dagger}+r\rangle_{3,\lambda}a^{3}.\end{split}

Continuing this process, we have

(17)

\begin{split}((a^{\dagger}+r))a^{n}=\langle a^{\dagger}+r\rangle_{n,\lambda}a^{n},\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

From (
9
) and (
17
), we get

(18)

\begin{split}((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{n}&=\langle a^{\dagger}+r\rangle_{n,\lambda}a^{n}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r,\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
18
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
r
be a nonnegative integer. In
S_{\lambda}
, the
\lambda
-
r
-Stirling numbers of the first kind appear as the coefficients of
((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{n}
in normal ordered form, as it is given by

\begin{split}((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r,\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}.\end{split}

From (
14
), we note that

(19)

\begin{split}a^{m}a^{\dagger}&=a^{m-1}(aa^{\dagger})=a^{m-1}(a^{\dagger}+\lambda)a\\
&=(a^{m-1}a^{\dagger})a+\lambda a^{m}=a^{m-2}(a^{\dagger}a+\lambda a)a+\lambda a^{m}\\
&=(a^{m-2}a^{\dagger})a^{2}+2\lambda a^{m}=\cdots=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)a^{m},\end{split}

and

(20)

\begin{split}a(a^{\dagger})^{n}&=(aa^{\dagger})(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}=(\lambda+a^{\dagger})a(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}\\
&=(\lambda+a^{\dagger})(aa^{\dagger})(a^{\dagger})^{n-2}=(\lambda+a^{\dagger})^{2}a(a^{\dagger})^{n-2}=\cdots\\
&=(\lambda+a^{\dagger})^{n-1}aa^{\dagger}=(\lambda+a^{\dagger})^{n}a.\end{split}

By (
19
), we get

(21)

\begin{split}a^{m}(a^{\dagger})^{n}&=(a^{m}a^{\dagger})(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)a^{m}(a^{\dagger})^{n-1}\\
&=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)(a^{m}a^{\dagger})(a^{\dagger})^{n-2}=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)a^{m}(a^{\dagger})^{n-2}\\
&=\cdots=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)^{n}a^{m}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
19
), (
20
) and (
21
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\lambda\neq 0
, we have in
S_{\lambda}
the normal orderings given by

\begin{split}&a^{m}a^{\dagger}=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)a^{m},\quad a(a^{\dagger})^{n}=(a^{\dagger}+\lambda)^{n}a,\\
&a^{m}(a^{\dagger})^{n}=(a^{\dagger}+m\lambda)^{n}a^{m}.\end{split}

Now, we observe from Theorem 3 that

(22)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n+1}&=(a^{\dagger}a)(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=a^{\dagger}a\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}\\
&=a^{\dagger}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}a(a^{\dagger})^{k}a^{n}=a^{\dagger}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}(\lambda+a^{\dagger})^{k}aa^{n}\\
\ &=a^{\dagger}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{k-j}(a^{\dagger})^{j}a^{n+1}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{k=j}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\binom{k}{j}\lambda^{k-j}(a^{\dagger})^{j+1}a^{n+1}\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}\bigg(\sum_{k=j-1}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\binom{k}{j-1}\lambda^{k+1-j}\bigg)(a^{\dagger})^{j}a^{n+1}.\end{split}

On the other hand, by Theorem 1, we get

(23)

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}{n+1\brack j}_{\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{j}a^{n+1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}{n+1\brack j}_{\lambda}(a^{\dagger})^{j}a^{n+1}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
22
) and (
23
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Let
n,j\in\mathbb{Z}
with
n\geq 0
and
j\geq 1
. In
S_{\lambda}
, the unsigned
\lambda
-Stirling numbers of the first kind satisfy the following recurrence relation:

\begin{split}{n+1\brack j}_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=j-1}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\binom{k}{j-1}\lambda^{k+1-j}={n\brack j-1}_{\lambda}+\sum_{k=j}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\binom{k}{j-1}\lambda^{k+1-j}.\end{split}

For
n\geq 1
, by (
15
) and (
17
), we have the
\lambda
-analogues of Boole’s relations in the
\lambda
-Shift algebra given by

\begin{split}(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\langle a^{\dagger}\rangle_{n,\lambda}a^{n},\quad((a^{\dagger}+r)a)^{n}=\langle a^{\dagger}+r\rangle_{n,\lambda}a^{n}.\end{split}

Now, we define the
\lambda
-analogues of
n!
as (see (
8
))

(24)

\begin{split}(0)_{\lambda}!=1,\quad(n)_{\lambda}!=\langle 1\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda},\quad(n\geq 1).\end{split}

Note that
\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}(n)_{\lambda}!=n!
.

From (
8
) and (
9
), we note that

(25)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{k!}\bigg(-\frac{\log(1-\lambda t)}{\lambda}\bigg)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\end{split}

and

(26)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{k!}\bigg(-\frac{\log(1-\lambda t)}{\lambda}\bigg)^{k}\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\lambda t}\bigg)^{\frac{r}{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}{n+r\brack k+r}_{r,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!},\end{split}

where
k
is nonnegative integer.

Thus, by (
25
) and (
26
), we get

(27)

\begin{split}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}{n+1\brack k+1}_{1,\lambda}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}&=\bigg(\frac{1}{1-\lambda t}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\frac{1}{k!}\bigg(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\log(1-\lambda t)\bigg)^{k}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}}{l!}\ t^{l}\sum_{m=k}^{\infty}{m\brack k}_{\lambda}\frac{t^{m}}{m!}\\
&=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}{n-l\brack k}_{\lambda}\bigg)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}.\end{split}

Comparing the coefficients on both sides of (
27
), we get

(28)

\begin{split}{n+1\brack k+1}_{1,\lambda}=\sum_{l=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}{n-l\brack k}_{\lambda}.\end{split}

From (
9
) and (
28
), we note that

(29)

\begin{split}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n+1\brack k+1}_{1,\lambda}m^{k}\lambda^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{l=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}{n-l\brack k}_{\lambda}m^{k}\lambda^{k}\\
&=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{n-l}{n-l\brack k}_{\lambda}m^{k}\lambda^{k}\bigg)\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}\langle m\lambda\rangle_{n-l,\lambda}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
29
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

\begin{split}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}\langle 1\rangle_{l,\lambda}\langle m\lambda\rangle_{n-l,\lambda}.\end{split}

We note that

\begin{split}\langle 1+m\rangle_{n}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\binom{n}{l}l!\langle m\rangle_{n-l}.\end{split}

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the
\lambda
-shift operator
\delta_{\lambda}^{\alpha}
by

(30)

\begin{split}\delta_{\lambda}^{\alpha}f(x)=f(x+\lambda\alpha).\end{split}

Then we see that

\begin{split}\delta_{\lambda}x-x\delta_{\lambda}=\lambda\delta_{\lambda},\end{split}

where
\delta_{\lambda}=\delta_{\lambda}^{1}
, and
x
denotes the ‘multiplication by
x
’ operator.

In the
\lambda
-shift algebra
S_{\lambda}
, a concrete representation is given by the operators
a^{\dagger}\mapsto x
and
a\mapsto\delta_{\lambda}
.
From Theorems 1 and 3, we note that

\begin{split}\delta_{\lambda}^{r}x^{s}=(x+r\lambda)^{s}\delta_{\lambda}^{r},\end{split}

and

(31)

\begin{split}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k}\delta_{\lambda}^{n},\quad(n\geq 0).\end{split}

Now, we observe from (
30
) and (
31
) that

(32)

\begin{split}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}e^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k}\delta_{\lambda}^{n}e^{x}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k}e^{(n\lambda+x)}.\end{split}

By (
8
) and (
32
), we get

(33)

\begin{split}e^{-(n\lambda+x)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}e^{x}=\langle x\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k}.\end{split}

In particular, for
x=1
, we have (see (
24
))

(34)

\begin{split}e^{-(n\lambda+x)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}e^{x}\Big\rvert_{x=1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}=(n)_{\lambda}!.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
33
) and (
34
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, in
S_{\lambda}
, we have

\begin{split}e^{-(x+n\lambda)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}e^{x}=\langle x\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{k}.\end{split}

In particular, for
x=1
, we get

\begin{split}e^{-(x+n\lambda)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}e^{x}\Big\rvert_{x=1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}=(n)_{\lambda}!.\end{split}

From Theorem 6, we note that

(35)

\begin{split}e^{-(x+(m+n)\lambda)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{m+n}e^{x}\Big\rvert_{x=1}=(m+n)_{\lambda}!.\end{split}

On the other hand, by (
31
), we get

(36)

\begin{split}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{m+n}&=(x\delta_{\lambda})^{m}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{j}\delta_{\lambda}^{m}x^{k}\delta_{\lambda}^{n}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{j}(x+m\lambda)^{k}\delta_{\lambda}^{m+n}.\end{split}

From (
36
), we have

(37)

\begin{split}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{m+n}e^{x}&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{j}(x+m\lambda)^{k}\delta_{\lambda}^{m+n}e^{x}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{j}(x+m\lambda)^{k}e^{x+(m+n)\lambda}.\end{split}

Thus, by (
37
), we get

(38)

\begin{split}e^{-(x+(m+n)\lambda)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{m+n}e^{x}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}x^{j}(x+m\lambda)^{k}.\end{split}

From (
35
) and (
38
), we have

(39)

\begin{split}(m+n)_{\lambda}!&=e^{-(x+(m+n)\lambda)}(x\delta_{\lambda})^{n+m}e^{x}\Big\rvert_{x=1}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\brack k}_{\lambda}(1+m\lambda)^{k}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}.\end{split}

By Theorem 5 and (
39
), we get

(40)

\begin{split}(m+n)_{\lambda}!&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\langle m\lambda\rangle_{n-k,\lambda}\langle 1\rangle_{k,\lambda}.\end{split}

Therefore, by (
40
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 7
.

For
m,n\geq 0
, we have

\begin{split}(m+n)_{\lambda}!=\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\binom{n}{k}\langle m\lambda\rangle_{n-k,\lambda}\langle 1\rangle_{k,\lambda}.\end{split}

Remark 8
.

(a) Taking the limit as
\lambda\rightarrow 1
, we see from Theorem 7 that

\begin{split}(m+n)!=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1}(m+n)_{\lambda}!=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{m\brack j}\binom{n}{k}\langle m\rangle_{n-k}k!,\quad(\text{see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{16}{}{}]}}).\end{split}

This was discovered by Mező in
[
17
]
which is dual to Spivey’s identity (see
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
).

(b) As the identities in Theorem 7 are obviously symmetric in
m
and
n
, we get the following symmetric identities:

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=0}^{m}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\langle 1+m\lambda\rangle_{n,\lambda}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}{n\brack j}_{\lambda}\langle 1+n\lambda\rangle_{m,\lambda},\\
&\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{m\brack j}_{\lambda}\binom{n}{k}\langle m\lambda\rangle_{n-k,\lambda}\langle 1\rangle_{k,\lambda}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{m}{n\brack j}_{\lambda}\binom{m}{k}\langle n\lambda\rangle_{m-k,\lambda}\langle 1\rangle_{k,\lambda}.\end{split}
