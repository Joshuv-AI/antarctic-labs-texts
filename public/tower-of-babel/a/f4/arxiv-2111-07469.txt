Abstract
. In this paper we study the Cauchy problem for diffusion equations associated to a class of strongly hypoelliptic pseudo-differential operators on graded Lie groups. To do so, we develop a global complex functional calculus on graded Lie groups in order to analyse the corresponding energy estimates. One of the main aspects of this complex functional calculus is that for the ( ρ , δ ) (\rho,\delta) -Euclidean Hörmander classes we recover the standard functional calculus developed by Seeley [ 38 ] . In consequence the Gårding inequality that we prove for arbitrary graded Lie groups absorbs the historical 1953’s inequality due to Gårding [ 26 ] .



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a graded Lie group. In this work we study the well-posedness for the Cauchy problem

(\textnormal{IVP}):\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)v+f,&\text{ }v\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
v(0)=u_{0},&\text{ }\end{cases}

(1.1)

associated to a continuous time-dependent family of strongly hypoelliptic pseudo-differential operators
K(t)=K(t,x,D),
and within the scale of suitable Sobolev spaces
[
24
]
.

This general framework allows us to consider relevant examples in the setting of nilpotent Lie groups (e.g. the Heisenberg group and any stratified Lie group). Indeed, here we consider the situation for a very general class of pseudo-differential operators
K(t)
in the global Hörmander classes on graded Lie groups as developed in
[
22
,
23
]
allowing
K(t)
to be a hypoelliptic operator of arbitrary order that cannot be treated with the usual Hörmander classes defined by localisations (or also with more classical classes). We recall that several classes of diffusion equations in the temporally homogeneous case can be studied within the setting of the semigroup theory, see e.g. Taylor
[
40
, Chapter IV]
.

One of the new aspects in the analysis of (
1.1
) is the construction of analytic functions of global pseudo-differential operators on graded groups, providing a modern extension of the work of Seeley
[
38
]
to the nilpotent setting, and of the global functional calculus on compact Lie groups as developed by the third author and Wirth in
[
37
]
.

Graded Lie groups are the family of nilpotent Lie groups characterised by the existence of Rockland operators in view of the Helffer and Nurrigat solution
[
29
]
of the Rockland conjecture. For instance, our approach allows us to study (in particular) the Heat equation associated with the following operators:

•

K=-\sum_{j}X_{j}^{2},
that is, the positive sub-Laplacian on a stratified group,

•

any positive Rockland operator on a graded Lie group, and any fractional power
\mathcal{R}^{a}
and
(1+\mathcal{R})^{a}
of
\mathcal{R}
and
1+\mathcal{R},
respectively, defined by the spectral calculus,

•

a long variety of hypoelliptic pseudo-differential operators on arbitrary graded Lie groups (see Theorem
5.2
for details).

Observe that in the Euclidean case,
the analysis of the regularity for parabolic equations has a long tradition. For instance, a good account on the local structure of solutions and other properties can be found in
[
16
]
. On the other hand, fractional diffusion equations have a wide variety of applications e.g. fluid mechanics
[
8
,
35
]
, mathematical finance
[
9
]
and fractional dynamics
[
39
,
33
,
34
]
. The fractional Laplacian and its different generalisations have been of intensive research interest in the last decades, e.g.
[
25
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
6
]
.

We observe that in the Euclidean setting in the case of the Laplacian
\mathcal{R}=-\Delta
, that is a Rockland operator of order 2, the heat equation associated to the fractional powers
(-\Delta)^{a}
and its generalisations are related with diffusion problems
[
8
,
35
]
, and the relevance of its analysis is justified by its applications in probability, finance, mathematical physics and differential geometry (see
[
28
]
and references therein).

It is worth mentioning that the study of the diffusion equation for the operator
(-\Delta)^{a}
has been undertaken from several perspectives. Indeed, as it was pointed out in
[
27
]
, roughly speaking: (1) there is a probabilistic approach, based on the fact that it is the infinitesimal generator of a Lévy process, (2) there is an approach by potential theoretic methods searching for similarities with the usual Laplacian
\Delta
, and (3) there is an approach in terms of its Fourier representation using that the fractional Laplacian is (modulo an error term) a pseudo-differential operator
[
30
]
. The first two methods have filled the major part of contributions in recent years; they deal mainly with real functions and real integral operators (Calderón-Zygmund singular integral operators with real kernels).

The third method gave some early results in the sixties and seventies, indeed, from the pseudo-differential calculus in
[
32
,
30
]
and its generalisations, but has only been reintroduced fairly recently for this particular type of operators. In this work, we will use the pseudo-differential model to study the diffusion equation, e.g. for
\mathcal{R}^{a}
and
(1+\mathcal{R})^{a},

a>0.
Indeed, still the kernels of these pseudo-differential operators become kernels of Calderón-Zygmund type
[
23
]
.

Our analysis allows us to tackle fractional powers of the propagator term. Some of the main ideas to study parabolic equations in this work correspond to an adaptation of the methods used for the classical classes of pseudo-differential operators as presented for instance in
[
40
]
and from the recent works
[
12
,
13
]
in the setting of the Weyl-Hörmander calculus.

In order to study the well-posedness for the initial value problem (IVP for short) (
1.1
), it is necessary to extend the functional calculus of Seeley
[
38
]
in the framework of the global Hörmander classes of
[
23
]
. Indeed, to exploit the strongly-ellipticity condition on
K(t),
and to study the corresponding energy estimates we use a Gårding type inequality
[
21
]
, whose proof lies in the construction of the analytic functional calculus. We want to emphasize that this global functional calculus extends in the context of graded Lie groups the functional calculus on compact Lie groups developed in
[
37
]
and
[
5
, Sections 7 and 8]
for elliptic and subelliptic Hörmander classes of operators, respectively. In the case of
G=\mathbb{R}^{n},
and
\mathcal{R}=-\Delta,
we recover the historical 1953’s inequality due to Gårding
[
26
]
.

We observe that the inclusion of the spectral calculus of Rockland operators (which is defined by their spectral measures) into the global Hörmander classes on graded Lie groups was consistently developed in
[
23
]
, so, in this paper we address the problem of the inclusion of analytic functions of general symbols in these classes. As an application of this complex functional calculus of operators we obtain a proof of the Gårding inequality and we obtain the well-posedness of (
1.1
). Naturally, the initial data are considered on the Sobolev spaces associated to Rockland operators, as consistently developed in
[
24
]
.

As for versions of the Gårding inequality and the sharp Gårding inequality on compact Lie groups with a free of local coordinate systems approach we refer the reader to
[
5
, Section 8.2]
and
[
3
]
. Gårding type inequalities in the context of the non-harmonic analysis on general smooth manifolds can be found in
[
4
]
.

This paper is organised as follows:

•

In Section

]
.

•

In Sections

In Subsection

•

Finally, the analysis for the diffusion problem (

of Section

2.
Preliminaries and global quantization on graded Lie groups

The notation and terminology herein on the analysis of homogeneous Lie groups are mostly taken from Folland and Stein
[
19
]
. For the theory of pseudo-differential operators we will follow the framework developed in
[
23
]
through the notion of (operator-valued) global symbols. If
H_{1},H_{2}
are Hilbert spaces,
\mathscr{B}(H_{1},H_{2})
denotes the
C^{*}
-algebra of bounded linear operators from
H_{1}
to
H_{2},
and also we will write
\mathscr{B}(H_{1})=\mathscr{B}(H_{1},H_{2}).

2.1.
Homogeneous and graded Lie groups

Let
G
be a homogeneous Lie group. This means that
G
is a connected and simply connected Lie group whose Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is endowed with a family of dilations
D_{r},

r>0,
which are automorphisms on
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying the following two conditions:

•

For every
r>0,

D_{r}
is a map of the form

D_{r}=\textnormal{Exp}(\ln(r)A)

for some diagonalisable linear operator
A\equiv\textnormal{diag}[\nu_{1},\cdots,\nu_{n}]
on
\mathfrak{g}.

•

\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{g},
and
r>0,

[D_{r}X,D_{r}Y]=D_{r}[X,Y].

We call the eigenvalues of
A,

\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\cdots,\nu_{n},
the dilation’s weights or weights of
G
. The homogeneous dimension of a homogeneous Lie group
G
is given by

Q=\textnormal{{Tr}}(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}.

The family of dilations
D_{r}
of
\mathfrak{g}
induces a family of maps on
G
defined via,

D_{r}:=\exp\circ D_{r}\circ\exp^{-1},\,\,r>0,

where
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow G
is the exponential mapping associated to the Lie group
G.
We refer to the family
D_{r},

r>0,
as dilations on the group. If we write
rx=D_{r}(x),

x\in G,

r>0,
then a relation between the homogeneous structure of
G
and the Haar measure
dx
on
G
is given by

\int\limits_{G}(f\circ D_{r})(x)dx=r^{-Q}\int\limits_{G}f(x)dx.

A Lie group is graded if its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
may be decomposed as the direct sum of subspaces
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{s}
such that
[\mathfrak{g}_{i},\mathfrak{g}_{j}]\subset\mathfrak{g}_{i+j},
and
\mathfrak{g}_{i+j}=\{0\}
if
i+j>s.
Examples of such groups are the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
and more generally any stratified groups where the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is generated by
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Here,
n
is the topological dimension of
G,

n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s},
where
n_{k}=\mbox{dim}\mathfrak{g}_{k}.

A Lie algebra admitting a family of dilations is nilpotent, and hence so is its associated
connected, simply connected Lie group. The converse does not hold, i.e., not every
nilpotent Lie group is homogeneous (see Dyer
[
17
]
) although they exhaust a large class (see Johnson
[
31
, page 294]
). Indeed, the main class of Lie groups under our consideration is that of graded Lie groups. A graded Lie group
G
is a homogeneous Lie group equipped with a family of weights
\nu_{j},
all of them positive rational numbers. Let us observe that if
\nu_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{b_{i}}
with
a_{i},b_{i}
integer numbers, and
b
is the least common multiple of the
b_{i}^{\prime}s,
the family of dilations

\mathbb{D}_{r}=\textnormal{Exp}(\ln(r^{b})A):\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g},

has integer weights,
\nu_{i}=\frac{a_{i}b}{b_{i}}.
So, in this paper we always assume that the weights
\nu_{j},
defining the family of dilations are non-negative integer numbers which allow us to assume that the homogeneous dimension
Q
is a non-negative integer number. This is a natural context for the study of Rockland operators (see Remark 4.1.4 of
[
23
]
).

2.2.
Fourier analysis on nilpotent Lie groups

Let
G
be a simply connected nilpotent Lie group.
Let us assume that
\pi
is a continuous, unitary and irreducible representation of
G,
this means that,

•

\pi\in\textnormal{Hom}(G,\textnormal{U}(H_{\pi})),
for some separable Hilbert space
H_{\pi},
i.e.
\pi(gg^{\prime})=\pi(g)\pi(g^{\prime})
and for the adjoint of
\pi(g),

\pi(g)^{*}=\pi(g^{-1}),
for every
g,g^{\prime}\in G.

•

The map
(g,v)\mapsto\pi(g)v,
from
G\times H_{\pi}
into
H_{\pi}
is continuous.

•

For every
g\in G,
and
W_{\pi}\subset H_{\pi},
if
\pi(g)W_{\pi}\subset W_{\pi},
then
W_{\pi}=H_{\pi}
or
W_{\pi}=\emptyset.

Let
\textnormal{Rep}(G)
be the family of unitary, continuous and irreducible representations of
G.
The relation,

\pi_{1}\sim\pi_{2}\textnormal{ if and only if, there exists }A\in\mathscr{B}(H_{\pi_{1}},H_{\pi_{2}}),\textnormal{ such that }A\pi_{1}(g)A^{-1}=\pi_{2}(g),

for every
g\in G,
is an equivalence relation and the unitary dual of
G,
denoted by
\widehat{G}
is defined via

\widehat{G}:={\textnormal{Rep}(G)}/{\sim}.
Let us denote by
d\pi
the Plancherel measure on
\widehat{G}.

The Fourier transform of
f\in\mathscr{S}(G),
(this means that
f\circ\textnormal{exp}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathfrak{g})
, with
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{\dim(G)}
) at
\pi\in\widehat{G},
is defined by

\widehat{f}(\pi)=\int\limits_{G}f(g)\pi(g)^{*}dg:H_{\pi}\rightarrow H_{\pi},\textnormal{ and }\mathscr{F}_{G}:\mathscr{S}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{S}(\widehat{G}):=\mathscr{F}_{G}(\mathscr{S}(G)).

If we identify one representation
\pi
with its equivalence class,
[\pi]=\{\pi^{\prime}:\pi\sim\pi^{\prime}\}
, for every
\pi\in\widehat{G},
the Kirillov trace character
\Theta_{\pi}
defined by

(\Theta_{\pi},f):=\textnormal{{Tr}}(\widehat{f}(\pi)),

is a tempered distribution on
\mathscr{S}(G).
In particular, the identity

f(e_{G})=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\textnormal{{Tr}}(\widehat{f}(\pi))d\pi,

implies the Fourier inversion formula
f=\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}(\widehat{f}),
where

(\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}\sigma)(g):=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\textnormal{{Tr}}[\pi(g)\sigma(\pi)]d\pi,\,\,g\in G,\,\,\,\,\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}:\mathscr{S}(\widehat{G})\rightarrow\mathscr{S}(G),

is the inverse Fourier transform. In this context, the Plancherel theorem takes the form
\|f\|_{L^{2}(G)}=\|\widehat{f}\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{G})}
, where
L^{2}(\widehat{G}):=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}H_{\pi}\otimes H_{\pi}^{*}d\pi,
is the Hilbert space endowed with the norm:
\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{G})}=(\int_{\widehat{G}}\|\sigma(\pi)\|_{\textnormal{HS}}^{2}d\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

2.3.
Homogeneous linear operators and Rockland operators

A linear operator
T:C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
is homogeneous of degree
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
if for every
r>0
the equality

T(f\circ D_{r})=r^{\nu}(Tf)\circ D_{r}

holds for every
f\in\mathscr{D}(G).

If for every representation
\pi\in\widehat{G},

\pi:G\rightarrow U({H}_{\pi}),
we denote by
{H}_{\pi}^{\infty}
the set of smooth vectors, that is, the space of elements
v\in{H}_{\pi}
such that the function
x\mapsto\pi(x)v,

x\in\widehat{G}
is smooth, a Rockland operator is a left-invariant differential operator
\mathcal{R}
which is homogeneous of positive degree
\nu=\nu_{\mathcal{R}}
and such that, for every unitary irreducible non-trivial representation
\pi\in\widehat{G},

\pi({R})
is injective on
{H}_{\pi}^{\infty};

\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}(\pi)=\pi(\mathcal{R})
is the symbol associated to
\mathcal{R}.
It coincides with the infinitesimal representation of
\mathcal{R}
as an element of the universal enveloping algebra. It can be shown that a Lie group
G
is graded if and only if there exists a differential Rockland operator on
G.
If the Rockland operator is formally self-adjoint, then
\mathcal{R}
and
\pi(\mathcal{R})
admit self-adjoint extensions on
L^{2}(G)
and
{H}_{\pi},
respectively. Now if we preserve the same notation for their self-adjoint
extensions and we denote by
E
and
E_{\pi}
their respective spectral measures, we will denote by

f(\mathcal{R})=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\lambda)dE(\lambda),\,\,\,\textnormal{and}\,\,\,\pi(f(\mathcal{R}))\equiv f(\pi(\mathcal{R}))=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\lambda)dE_{\pi}(\lambda),

the functions defined by the functional calculus.
In general, we will reserve the notation
{E_{A}(\lambda)}_{0\leq\lambda<\infty}
for the spectral measure associated with a positive and self-adjoint operator
A
on a Hilbert space
H.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},
and for an arbitrary family
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},
of left-invariant vector-fields we will use the notation

[\alpha]:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nu_{j}\alpha_{j},

(2.1)

for the homogeneity degree of the operator
X^{\alpha}:=X_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},
whose order is
|\alpha|:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}.

2.4.
Symbols and quantization of pseudo-differential operators

In order to present a consistent definition of pseudo-differential operators as developed in
[
23
]
(see the quantisation formula (
2.4
)), we need a suitable class of spaces on the unitary dual
\widehat{G}
acting in a suitable way with the set of smooth vectors
H_{\pi}^{\infty},
on every representation space
H_{\pi}.
Let us now recall the main notions.

Definition 2.1

(Sobolev spaces on smooth vectors)
.

Let
\pi_{1}\in\textnormal{Rep}(G),
and
a\in\mathbb{R}.
We denote by
H_{\pi_{1}}^{a},
the Hilbert space obtained as the completion of
H_{\pi_{1}}^{\infty}
with respect to the norm

\|v\|_{H_{\pi_{1}}^{a}}=\|\pi_{1}(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{a}{\nu}}v\|_{H_{\pi_{1}}},

where
\mathcal{R}
is a positive Rockland operator on
G
of homogeneous degree
\nu>0.

In order to introduce the general notion of symbol as the one developed in
[
23
]
, we will use a suitable notion of operator-valued symbols acting on smooth vectors. We introduce it as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

A
\widehat{G}
-field of operators
\sigma=\{\sigma(\pi):\pi\in\widehat{G}\}
defined on smooth vectors is defined on the Sobolev space
{H}_{\pi}^{a}
when for each representation
\pi_{1}\in\textnormal{Rep}(G),
the operator
\sigma(\pi_{1})
is bounded from
H^{a}_{\pi_{1}}
into
H_{\pi_{1}}
in the sense that

\sup_{\|v\|_{H_{\pi_{1}}^{a}}=1}\|\sigma(\pi_{1})v\|<\infty.

We will consider those
\widehat{G}
-fields of operators with ranges in Sobolev spaces on smooth vectors. We recall that the Sobolev space
L^{2}_{a}(G)
is defined by the norm (see
[
23
, Chapter 4]
)

\|f\|_{L^{2}_{a}(G)}=\|(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{a}{\nu}}f\|_{L^{2}(G)},

(2.2)

for
a\in\mathbb{R}.

Definition 2.3
.

A
\widehat{G}
-field of operators defined on smooth vectors with range in the Sobolev space
H_{\pi}^{a}
is a family of classes of operators
\sigma=\{\sigma(\pi):\pi\in\widehat{G}\}
where

\sigma(\pi):=\{\sigma(\pi_{1}):H^{\infty}_{\pi_{1}}\rightarrow H_{\pi}^{a},\,\,\pi_{1}\in\pi\},

for every
\pi\in\widehat{G}
viewed as a subset of
\textnormal{Rep}(G),
satisfying for every two elements
\sigma(\pi_{1})
and
\sigma(\pi_{2})
in
\sigma(\pi):

\textnormal{If }\pi_{1}\sim\pi_{2}\textnormal{ then }\sigma(\pi_{1})\sim\sigma(\pi_{2}).

The following notion will be useful in order to use the general theory of non-commutative integration (see e.g. Dixmier
[
15
]
).

Definition 2.4
.

A
\widehat{G}
-field of operators defined on smooth vectors with range in the Sobolev space
H_{\pi}^{a}
is measurable when for some (and hence for any)
\pi_{1}\in\pi
and any vector
v_{\pi_{1}}\in H_{\pi_{1}}^{\infty},
as
\pi\in\widehat{G},
the resulting field
\{\sigma(\pi)v_{\pi}:\pi\in\widehat{G}\},

is
d\pi
-measurable and

\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\|v_{\pi}\|^{2}_{H_{\pi}^{a}}d\pi=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\|\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{a}{\nu}}v_{\pi}\|^{2}_{H_{\pi}}d\pi<\infty.

Remark 2.5
.

We always assume that a
\widehat{G}
-field of operators defined on smooth vectors with range in the Sobolev space
H_{\pi}^{a}
is
d\pi
-measurable.

The
\widehat{G}
-fields of operators associated to Rockland operators can be defined as follows.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
L^{2}_{a}(\widehat{G})
denote the space of fields of operators
\sigma
with range in
H_{\pi}^{a},
that is,

\sigma=\{\sigma(\pi):H_{\pi}^{\infty}\rightarrow H_{\pi}^{a}\},\textnormal{ with }\{\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{a}{\nu}}\sigma(\pi):\pi\in\widehat{G}\}\in L^{2}(\widehat{G}),

for one (and hence for any) Rockland operator of homogeneous degree
\nu.
We also denote

\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}_{a}(\widehat{G})}:=\|\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{a}{\nu}}\sigma(\pi)\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{G})}.

By using the notation above, we will introduce a family of function spaces required to define
\widehat{G}
-fields of operators (that will be used to define the symbol of a pseudo-differential operator, see Definition
2.8
).

Definition 2.7

(The spaces
\mathscr{L}_{L}(L^{2}_{a}(G),L^{2}_{b}(G)),

\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(G)
and
L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})
)
.

•

The space
\mathscr{L}_{L}(L^{2}_{a}(G),L^{2}_{b}(G))
consists of all left-invariant linear operators
T
such that
T:L^{2}_{a}(G)\rightarrow L^{2}_{b}(G)
extends to a bounded operator.

•

The space
\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(G)
is the family of all right convolution kernels of elements in
\mathscr{L}_{L}(L^{2}_{a}(G),L^{2}_{b}(G)),
i.e.
k=T\delta\in\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(G)
if and only if
T\in\mathscr{L}_{L}(L^{2}_{a}(G),L^{2}_{b}(G)).

•

We also define the space
L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})
by the following condition:
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})
if

\|\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{b}{\nu}}\sigma(\pi)\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{-\frac{a}{\nu}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{G})}:=\sup_{\pi\in\widehat{G}}\|\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{b}{\nu}}\sigma(\pi)\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{-\frac{a}{\nu}}\|_{\mathscr{B}(H_{\pi})}<\infty.

In this case
T_{\sigma}:L^{2}_{a}(G)\rightarrow L^{2}_{b}(G)
extends to a bounded operator with

\|\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})}=\|T_{\sigma}\|_{\mathscr{L}(L^{2}_{a}(G),L^{2}_{b}(G))},

and
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})
if and only if
k:=\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{a,b}(G).

With the previous definitions, we will introduce the type of symbols that we will use in this work and under which the quantization formula makes sense.

Definition 2.8

(Symbols and right-convolution kernels)
.

A symbol is a field of operators
\{\sigma(x,\pi):H_{\pi}^{\infty}\rightarrow H_{\pi}^{\infty},\,\,\pi\in\widehat{G}\},
depending on
x\in G,
such that

\sigma(x,\cdot)=\{\sigma(x,\pi):H_{\pi}^{\infty}\rightarrow H_{\pi}^{\infty},\,\,\pi\in\widehat{G}\}\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G})

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}.
The right-convolution kernel
k\in C^{\infty}(G,\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(G))
associated with
\sigma
is defined, via the inverse Fourier transform on the group by

x\mapsto k(x)\equiv k_{x}:=\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}(\sigma(x,\cdot)):G\rightarrow\mathscr{S}^{\prime}(G).

Definition
2.8
in this section allows us to establish the following theorem, which gives sense to the quantization of pseudo-differential operators in the graded setting (see Theorem 5.1.39 of
[
23
]
).

Theorem 2.9
.

Let us consider a symbol
\sigma
and its associated right-convolution kernel
k.
For every
f\in\mathscr{S}(G),
let us define the operator
A
acting on
\mathscr{S}(G),
via

Af(x)=(f\ast k_{x})(x),\,\,\,\,\,x\in G.

(2.3)

Then
Af\in C^{\infty},
and

Af(x)=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\textnormal{{Tr}}(\pi(x)\sigma(x,\pi)\widehat{f}(\pi))d\pi.

(2.4)

Theorem
2.9
motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.10
.

A continuous linear operator
A:C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
with Schwartz kernel

2.6.
Global Hörmander classes of pseudo-differential operators

The main tool in the construction of global Hörmander classes is the notion of difference operators.
Indeed, for every smooth function
q\in C^{\infty}(G)
and
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(G),
where
a,b\in\mathbb{R},
the difference operator
\Delta_{q}
acts on
\sigma
according to the formula (see Definition 5.2.1 of
[
23
]
),

\Delta_{q}\sigma(\pi)\equiv[\Delta_{q}\sigma](\pi):=\mathscr{F}_{G}(qf)(\pi),\,\textnormal{ for a.e. }\pi\in\widehat{G},\textnormal{ where }f:=\mathscr{F}_{G}^{-1}\sigma\,\,.

We will reserve the notation
\Delta^{\alpha}
for the difference operators defined by the functions
q_{\alpha}(x):=x^{\alpha},
while we denote by
\tilde{\Delta}^{\alpha}
the difference operators associated with the functions
\tilde{q}_{\alpha}(x)=(x^{-1})^{\alpha}
. In particular, we have the Leibnitz rule,

\Delta^{\alpha}(\sigma\tau)=\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\alpha}c_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\Delta^{\alpha_{1}}(\sigma)\Delta^{\alpha_{2}}(\tau),\,\,\,\sigma,\tau\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G}).

(2.11)

For our further analysis we will use the following property of the difference operators
\Delta^{\alpha},
(see e.g.
[
20
, page 20]
),

\Delta^{\alpha}(\sigma_{r\cdot})(\pi)=r^{[\alpha]}(\Delta^{\alpha}\sigma)(r\cdot\pi),\,\,\,r>0\,\,\,\pi\in\widehat{G},

(2.12)

where we have denoted

\sigma_{r\cdot}:=\{\sigma(r\cdot\pi):\pi\in\widehat{G}\},\,\,r\cdot\pi(x):=\pi(D_{r}(x)),\,\,x\in G.

(2.13)

In terms of difference operators, the global Hörmander classes introduced in
[
23
]
can be introduced as follows.
Let
0\leq\delta,\rho\leq 1,
and let
\mathcal{R}
be a positive Rockland operator of homogeneous degree
\nu>0.
If
m\in\mathbb{R},
we say that the symbol
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{a,b}(\widehat{G}),
where
a,b\in\mathbb{R},
belongs to the
(\rho,\delta)
-Hörmander class of order
m,

S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
if for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R},
the following conditions

p_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,m}(\sigma)=\operatornamewithlimits{ess\,sup}_{(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}}\|\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]-m-\gamma}{\nu}}[X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}\sigma(x,\pi)]\pi(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{\gamma}{\nu}}\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty,

(2.14)

hold true for all
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}.
The resulting class
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
does not depend on the choice of the Rockland operator
\mathcal{R}.
In particular (see Theorem 5.5.20 of
[
23
]
), the following facts are equivalent:

(A)

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},\forall\gamma\in\mathbb{R},

p_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,m}(\sigma)<\infty;

(B)

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},

p_{\alpha,\beta,0,m}(\sigma)<\infty;

(C)

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},

p_{\alpha,\beta,m+\delta[\beta]-\rho[\alpha],m}(\sigma)<\infty;

(D)

\sigma\in{S}^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G});

and in view of Theorem 13.16 of
[
5
]
, the conditions (A), (B), (C) and (D) are equivalent to the following one:

(E)

\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n},\exists\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R},

p_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma_{0},m}(\sigma)<\infty.

We will denote,

\|\sigma\|_{k\,,S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}}:=\max_{[\alpha]+[\beta]\leq k}\{p_{\alpha,\beta,0,m}(\sigma)\}.

(2.15)

Remark 2.13
.

In the abelian case
G=\mathbb{R}^{n},
endowed with its natural structure of abelian group, and
\mathcal{R}=-\Delta_{x},

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
with
\Delta_{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}
being the usual Laplace operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
the classes defined via (
2.14
), agree with the well known Hörmander classes on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see e.g. Hörmander
[
30
, Vol. 3]
). In this case the difference operators are the partial derivatives on
\mathbb{R}^{n},
(see Remark 5.2.13 and Example 5.2.6 of
[
23
]
).

For an arbitrary graded Lie group, the Hörmander classes
S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

m\in\mathbb{R},
provide a symbolic calculus closed under compositions, adjoints, and existence of parametrices. The following theorem summarises the composition and the adjoint rules for global operators as well as the Calderón-Vaillancourth theorem. The notion of ellipticity as developed in
[
23
]
and the existence of parametrices will be presented in Section

.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
and let us denote
\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}:=\textnormal{Op}({S}^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})),
for every
m\in\mathbb{R}.
Then,

(i)

The mapping
A\mapsto A^{*}:\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}\rightarrow\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}
is a continuous linear mapping between Fréchet spaces and the symbol of
A^{*},

\sigma_{A^{*}}(x,\pi)\equiv\widehat{A^{*}}(x,\pi)
satisfies the asymptotic expansion,

\widehat{A^{*}}(x,\pi)\sim\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{\infty}\Delta^{\alpha}X_{x}^{\alpha}(\widehat{A}(x,\pi)^{*}).

This means that, for every
N\in\mathbb{N},
and all
\ell\in\mathbb{N},

\footnotesize{\Delta^{\alpha_{\ell}}X_{x}^{\beta}\left(\widehat{A^{*}}(x,\pi)-\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N}\Delta^{\alpha}X_{x}^{\alpha}(\widehat{A}(x,\pi)^{*})\right)\in{S}^{m-(\rho-\delta)(N+1)-\rho\ell+\delta[\beta]}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})},

where
[\alpha_{\ell}]=\ell.

(ii)

The mapping
(A_{1},A_{2})\mapsto A_{1}\circ A_{2}:\Psi^{m_{1}}_{\rho,\delta}\times\Psi^{m_{2}}_{\rho,\delta}\rightarrow\Psi^{m_{3}}_{\rho,\delta}
is a continuous bilinear mapping between Fréchet spaces, and the symbol of
A=A_{1}\circ A_{2},
satisfies the asymptotic expansion,

\sigma_{A}(x,\pi)\sim\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{\infty}(\Delta^{\alpha}\widehat{A}_{1}(x,\pi))(X_{x}^{\alpha}\widehat{A}_{2}(x,\pi)),

this means that, for every
N\in\mathbb{N},
and all
\ell\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\Delta^{\alpha_{\ell}}X_{x}^{\beta}\left(\sigma_{A}(x,\pi)-\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant N}(\Delta^{\alpha}\widehat{A}_{1}(x,\pi))(X_{x}^{\alpha}\widehat{A}_{2}(x,\pi))\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\in{S}^{m_{1}+m_{2}-(\rho-\delta)(N+1)-\rho\ell+\delta[\beta]}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

for every
\alpha_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
with
[\alpha_{\ell}]=\ell.

(iii)

For
0\leqslant\delta\leq\rho\leqslant 1,

\delta\neq 1,
let us consider a continuous linear operator
A:C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
with symbol
\sigma\in{S}^{0}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
. Then
A
extends to a bounded operator from
L^{2}(G)
to
L^{2}(G).

3.
Parameter ellipticity on an analytic curve

In this section we start the construction of our global functional calculus. Firstly, we require the notion of ellipticity with respect to a parameter
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

We will use

\mathcal{M}:=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{1}{\nu}},

(3.1)

for the Riesz potential of order one associated to the positive Rockland operator
\mathcal{R}
of homogeneous order
\nu>0.
For instance, for any
s\in\mathbb{R},
the field of vectors

\{\pi(\mathcal{M})^{s}:=(1+\pi(\mathcal{R}))^{\frac{s}{\nu}}:\pi\in\widehat{G}\},

(3.2)

is the global symbol of the operator
\mathcal{M}^{s}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(1+\mathcal{\lambda})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}dE(\lambda),

where
\{E(\lambda)\}_{\lambda>0}
denotes the spectral measure of
\mathcal{R}.
The sequence
\{\nu_{jj}(\pi)\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
denotes the spectrum of the operator
\pi(\mathcal{R}),
and by the spectral mapping theorem the sequence
\{(1+\nu_{jj}(\pi))^{\frac{1}{\nu}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
determines the spectrum of the operator
\pi(\mathcal{M}),

\pi\in\widehat{G}.

To develop the pseudo-differential functional calculus we need a more broad notion of ellipticity. By following the approach in
[
37
]
, we present in our pseudo-differential context the notion of parameter ellipticity.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
m>0,
and let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1.

Let
\Lambda=\{\gamma(t):t\in I\}
be an analytic curve in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, where
I=[a,b],

-\infty<a\leqslant b<\infty,

I=[a,\infty),

I=(-\infty,b]
or
I=(-\infty,\infty).
If
I
is a finite interval we assume that
\Lambda
is a closed curve. For simplicity, if
I
is an infinite interval we assume that
\Lambda
is homotopy equivalent to the line
\Lambda_{i\mathbb{R}}:=\{iy:-\infty<y<\infty\}.
Let
a=a(x,\pi)\in{S}^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).
Assume also that

{\boxed{R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)^{-1}:=a(x,\pi)-\lambda\textnormal{ is invertible for every }(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G},andall\lambda\in\Lambda.}}

We say that
a
is parameter elliptic with respect to
\Lambda,
if

\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sup_{(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty.

Remark 3.2
.

Observe that for
a=b=0,

I=\{0\},
and for the trivial curve
\gamma(t)=0,
that
a
is parameter elliptic with respect to
\Lambda=\{0\},
is equivalent to say that
a
is elliptic. The following theorem describes the resolvent operator
R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)
of a parameter elliptic symbol
a.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
m>0,
and let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1.
If
a
is parameter elliptic with respect to
\Lambda,
the following estimate

\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sup_{(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty,

holds true for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Proof.

We will split the proof in the cases
|\lambda|\leqslant 1,
and
|\lambda|>1,
where
\lambda\in\Lambda.
It is possible however that one of these two cases could be trivial in the sense that
\Lambda_{1}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:|\lambda|\leqslant 1\}
or
\Lambda_{1}^{c}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:|\lambda|>1\}
could be empty sets. In such a case the proof is self-contained in the situation that we will consider where we assume that
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{1}^{c}
are not trivial sets. For
|\lambda|\leqslant 1,
observe that

\displaystyle\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m(k+1)+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m(k+1)}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m(k+1)+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}.

We have

\displaystyle\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m(k+1)}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-1}+I)^{m(k+1)}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\leqslant\||\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-1}+I\|^{m(k+1)}_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{|\lambda|\in[0,1]}\sup_{1\leqslant j\leqslant\infty}(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}(1+\nu_{jj}(\pi))^{-\frac{1}{\nu}}+1))^{k(m+1)}

\displaystyle=O(1).

On the other hand, we can prove that

\displaystyle\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m(k+1)+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}=O(1).

For
k=1,

\partial_{\lambda}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)=R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)^{2}.
This can be deduced from the Leibniz rule, indeed,

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{\lambda}(R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)(a(x,\pi)-\lambda))=(\partial_{\lambda}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi))(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)+R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)(-1)

implies that

\displaystyle-\partial_{\lambda}(R_{\lambda}(x,\pi))(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)=R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)(-1).

Because
(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)=R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)^{-1}
the identity for the first derivative of
R_{\lambda},

\partial_{\lambda}R_{\lambda}
follows. So, from the chain rule we obtain that the term of higher order expanding the derivative
\partial_{\lambda}^{k}R_{\lambda}
is
R_{\lambda}^{k+1}.
Because
R_{\lambda}\in S^{-m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
the pseudo-differential calculus implies that
R_{\lambda}^{k+1}\in S^{-m(k+1)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).
This fact, and the compactness of
\Lambda_{1}\subset\mathbb{C},
provide us the uniform estimate

\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}}\sup_{(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m(k+1)+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty.

Now, we will analyse the situation for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}^{c}.
We will use induction over
k
in order to prove that

\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}^{c}}\sup_{(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty.

For
k=0
notice that

\displaystyle\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}},

and denoting
\theta=\frac{1}{|\lambda|},

\omega=\frac{\lambda}{|\lambda|},
we have

\displaystyle\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{\lambda}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}R_{\lambda}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}|\lambda|^{-1}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(1+|\lambda|^{-\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(1+\theta^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(1+\theta^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|(1+\theta^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}.

Because
(1+\theta^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\in S^{0}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
we have that the operator norm
\|(1+\theta^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\|_{\textnormal{op}}
is uniformly bounded in
\theta\in[0,1].
The same argument can be applied to the operator norm

\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}},

by using that
(\theta\times a(x,\pi)-\omega)^{-1}\in S^{-m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
with
\theta\in[0,1]
and
\omega
being an element of the complex circle. The case
k\geqslant 1
for
\lambda\in\Lambda_{1}^{c}
can be proved in an analogous way.
∎

4.
Global complex functional calculus on graded Lie groups

In this section we develop the global functional calculus for pseudo-differential operators. The calculus will be applied to obtaining a pseudo-differential Gårding ineqiality and for studying the Dixmier trace of pseudo-differential operators.

4.1.
Functions of symbols vs functions of operators

Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
be a parameter elliptic symbol of order
m>0
with respect to the sector
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}.
For
A=\textnormal{Op}(a),
let us define the operator
F(A)
by the (Dunford-Riesz) complex functional calculus

F(A)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)(A-zI)^{-1}dz,

(4.1)

where

(CI)

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\Lambda\cup\{z:|z|\leqslant\varepsilon\},

\varepsilon>0,
and
\Gamma=\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\subset\textnormal{Resolv}(A)
is a positively oriented curve in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.

(CII)

F
is an holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda_{\varepsilon},
and continuous on its closure.

(CIII)

We will assume decay of
F
along
\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
in order that the operator (
4.1
) will be densely defined on
C^{\infty}(G)
in the strong sense of the topology on
L^{2}(G).

Now, we will compute the symbols for operators defined by this complex functional calculus.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
be a parameter elliptic symbol of order
m>0
with respect to the sector
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}.
Let
F(A):C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
be the operator defined by the analytical functional calculus as in (
4.1
). Under the assumptions
(CI)
,
(CII)
, and
(CIII)
, the symbol of
F(A),

\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)
is given by

\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)dz

where
\mathcal{R}_{z}=(A-zI)^{-1}
denotes the resolvent of
A,
and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)\in S^{-m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
is its symbol.

Proof.

From Corollary
3.5
, we have that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)\in S^{-m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).
Now, observe that

\displaystyle\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)=\pi(x)^{*}F(A)\pi(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)\pi(x)^{*}(A-zI)^{-1}\pi(x)dz.

We finish the proof by observing that
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)=\pi(x)^{*}(A-zI)^{-1}\pi(x),
for every
z\in\textnormal{Resolv}(A).

∎

Assumption (CIII) will be clarified in the following theorem where we show that the pseudo-differential calculus is stable under the action of the complex functional calculus.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
m>0,
and let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1.
Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
be a parameter elliptic symbol with respect to
\Lambda.
Let us assume that
F
satisfies the estimate
|F(\lambda)|\leqslant C|\lambda|^{s}
uniformly in
\lambda,
for some
s<0.
Then the symbol of
F(A),

\sigma_{F(A)}\in S^{ms}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
admits an asymptotic expansion of the form

\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)\sim\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\sigma_{{B}_{N}}(x,\pi),\,\,\,(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G},

(4.2)

where
\sigma_{{B}_{N}}(x,\pi)\in{S}^{ms-(\rho-\delta)N}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
and

\sigma_{{B}_{0}}(x,\pi)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}dz\in{S}^{ms}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

Moreover,

\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)\equiv-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)a^{-\#}(x,\pi,\lambda)dz\textnormal{ mod }{S}^{-\infty}(G\times\widehat{G}),

where
a^{-\#}(x,\pi,\lambda)
is the symbol of the parametrix to
A-\lambda I,
in Corollary
3.4
.

Proof.

First, we need to prove that the condition
|F(\lambda)|\leqslant C|\lambda|^{s}
uniformly in
\lambda,
for some
s<0,
is enough in order to guarantee that

\sigma_{{B}_{0}}(x,\pi):=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}dz,

is a well defined operator-symbol.
From Theorem
3.3
we deduce that
(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}
satisfies the estimate

\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m(k+1)}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}\partial_{z}^{k}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty.

Observe that

\displaystyle\|(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{1\leqslant j\leqslant\infty}(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+(1+\nu_{jj}(\pi))^{\frac{1}{\nu}})^{-m}

\displaystyle\leqslant|z|^{-1},

and the condition
s<0
implies

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}dz\right|\lesssim\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}|z|^{-1+s}|dz|<\infty,

uniformly in
(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}.
In order to check that
\sigma_{B_{0}}\in{S}^{ms}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
let us analyse the cases
-1<s<0
and
s\leqslant-1
separately. So, let us analyse first the situation of
-1<s<0.
We observe that

\displaystyle\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}\sigma_{B_{0}}(x,\pi)\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C}{2\pi}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}|z|^{s}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}|dz|.

Now, we will estimate the operator norm inside of the integral. Indeed, the identity

\displaystyle\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}=

\displaystyle\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

implies that

\displaystyle\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\lesssim\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

where we have used that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}\sup_{(x,\pi)}\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}<\infty.

Consequently, by using that
s<0,
we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{C}{2\pi}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}|z|^{s}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{ms+\rho[\alpha]-\delta[\beta]}X_{x}^{\beta}\Delta^{\alpha}(a(x,\pi)-z)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}|dz|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{C}{2\pi}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}|z|^{s}\|(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-ms}\|_{\textnormal{op}}|dz|

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2\pi}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}|z|^{s}\sup_{1\leqslant j\leqslant\infty}(|z|^{\frac{1}{m}}+(1+\nu_{jj}(\pi))^{\frac{1}{\nu}})^{-m}(1+\nu_{jj}(\pi))^{-\frac{ms}{\nu}}|dz|.

In order to study the convergence of the last contour integral we only need to check the convergence of
\int_{1}^{\infty}r^{s}(r^{\frac{1}{m}}+\varkappa)^{-m}\varkappa^{-ms}dr,
where
\varkappa>1
in a parameter. The change of variable
r=\varkappa^{m}t
implies that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}r^{s}(r^{\frac{1}{m}}+\varkappa)^{-m}\varkappa^{-ms}dr

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\varkappa^{-m}}^{\infty}\varkappa^{ms}t^{s}(\varkappa t^{\frac{1}{m}}+\varkappa)^{-m}\varkappa^{-ms}\varkappa^{m}dt=\int\limits_{\varkappa^{-m}}^{\infty}t^{s}(t^{\frac{1}{m}}+1)^{-m}dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\limits_{\varkappa^{-m}}^{1}t^{s}dt+\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}t^{-1+s}<\infty.

Indeed, for
t\rightarrow\infty,

t^{s}(t^{\frac{1}{m}}+1)^{-m}\lesssim t^{-1+s}
and we conclude the estimate because
\int\limits_{1}^{\infty}t^{-1+s^{\prime}}dt<\infty,
for all
s^{\prime}<0.
On the other hand, the condition
-1<s<0
implies that

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\varkappa^{-m}}^{1}t^{s}dt=\frac{1}{1+s}-\frac{\varkappa^{-m(1+s)}}{1+s}=O(1).

In the case where
s\leqslant-1,
we can find an analytic function
\tilde{G}(z)
such that it is a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Lambda_{\varepsilon},
and continuous on its closure and additionally satisfying that
F(\lambda)=\tilde{G}(\lambda)^{1+[-s]}.
1
1

1

[-s]
denotes the integer part of
-s.
In this case,
\tilde{G}(A)
defined by the complex functional calculus

\tilde{G}(A)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{G}(z)(A-zI)^{-1}dz,

(4.3)

has symbol belonging to
{S}^{\frac{sm}{1+[-s]}}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
because
\tilde{G}
satisfies the estimate
|G(\lambda)|\leqslant C|\lambda|^{\frac{s}{1+[-s]}},
with
-1<\frac{s}{1+[-s]}<0.

By observing that

\displaystyle\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)dz=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{G}(z)^{1+[-s]}\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)dz

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\tilde{G}(A)^{1+[-s]}}(x,\pi),

and computing the symbol
\sigma_{\tilde{G}(A)^{1+[-s]}}(x,\pi)
by iterating
1+[-s]
-times the asymptotic formula for the composition in the pseudo-differential calculus, we can see that the term with higher order in such expansion is
\sigma_{\tilde{G}(A)}(x,\pi)^{1+[-s]}\in{S}^{ms}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).
Consequently we have proved that
\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)\in{S}^{ms}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

This completes the proof for the first part of the theorem.
For the second part of the proof, let us denote by
a^{-\#}(x,\pi,\lambda)
the symbol of the parametrix to
A-\lambda I,
in Corollary
3.4
. Let
P_{\lambda}=\textnormal{Op}(a^{-\#}(\cdot,\cdot,\lambda)).
Because
\lambda\in\textnormal{Resolv}(A)
for
\lambda\in\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon},

(A-\lambda)^{-1}-P_{\lambda}
is an smoothing operator. Consequently, from Lemma
4.1
we deduce that

\displaystyle\sigma_{F(A)}(x,\pi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)dz

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)a^{-\#}(x,\pi,z)dz-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)(\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{z}(x,\pi)-a^{-\#}(x,\pi,z))dz

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{\partial\Lambda_{\varepsilon}}F(z)a^{-\#}(x,\pi,z)dz\textnormal{ mod }{S}^{-\infty}(G\times\widehat{G}).

The asymptotic expansion (
4.2
) comes from the construction of the parametrix in the pseudo-differential calculus (see Proposition
2.16
).
∎

4.2.
Gårding inequality

In the setting of graded groups the global version of the Gårding inequality remains valid. Indeed, in
[
21
]
it was proved under the assumption that the symbol of the operator commutes with the symbol of
\mathcal{R}
. In this section we remove this commutativity condition making use of the complex functional calculus for the Hörmander classes allowing the Gårding inequality to be valid in a more general context. To do so, we need some preliminary results.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1.
Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
be an elliptic symbol where
m\geqslant 0
and let us assume that
a(x,\pi)
is positive definite for a.e.
(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G}
. Then
a
is parameter-elliptic with respect to
\mathbb{R}_{-}:=\{z=x+i0:x<0\}\subset\mathbb{C}.
Furthermore, for any number
s\in\mathbb{C},

\widehat{B}(x,\pi)\equiv a(x,\pi)^{s}:=\exp(s\log(a(x,\pi))),\,\,(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G},

defines a symbol
\widehat{B}(x,\pi)\in S^{m\times\textnormal{Re}(s)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

Proof.

Any operator
a(x,\pi),

\pi\in\widehat{G},
is normal, so that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
we have

\displaystyle\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\asymp\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\|(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)(a(x,\pi)-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}.

Let us note that the condition
m\geq 0,
implies that
\|\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\leq 1.
So, if
|\lambda|\leq 1/2,
then
|\lambda|\|\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\leq 1/2,
which implies that for
|\lambda|\leq 1/2,

1-\lambda\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m}
is invertible on
H_{\pi},
its inverse is a bounded operator, and from
the first von-Neumann identity

\|(1-\lambda\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{-m})^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}\leq(1-|\lambda|\|\pi(\mathcal{M})\|_{\textnormal{op}})^{-m})^{-1}\leq 2.

Now, fixing again
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{-}
observe that from the compactness of
[0,1/2]
we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1/2}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\asymp\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1/2}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\asymp\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1/2}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\asymp\sup_{0\leqslant\lambda\leqslant 1/2}\|(I-\lambda\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m})^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 1/2}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}+\pi(\mathcal{M}))^{m}(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}-\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 1/2}\|(|\lambda|^{\frac{1}{m}}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-1}+I)^{m}(I-\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 1/2}\|(\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-1}+|\lambda|^{-\frac{1}{m}}I)^{m}|\lambda|(I-\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}\lambda)^{-1}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 1/2}\|\pi(\mathcal{M})^{-m}|\lambda|(-\lambda)^{-1}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}\|_{\textnormal{op}}

\displaystyle=1.

So, we have proved that
a
is parameter-elliptic with respect to
\mathbb{R}_{-}.
To prove that
\widehat{B}(x,\pi)\in S^{m\times\textnormal{Re}(s)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
we can observe that for
\textnormal{Re}(s)<0,
we can apply Theorem
4.2
. If
\textnormal{Re}(s)\geqslant 0,
we can find
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\textnormal{Re}(s)-k<0
and consequently from the spectral calculus of operators we deduce that
a(x,\pi)^{\textnormal{Re}(s)-k}\in S^{m\times(\textnormal{Re}(s)-k)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).
So, from the calculus we conclude that

a(x,\pi)^{s}=a(x,\pi)^{s-k}a(x,\pi)^{k}\in S^{m\times\textnormal{Re}(s)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

Thus the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1.
Let
a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),
be an elliptic symbol where
m\geqslant 0
and let us assume that
a
is positive definite. Then

\widehat{B}(x,\pi)\equiv a(x,\pi)^{\frac{1}{2}}:=\exp(\frac{1}{2}\log(a(x,\pi)))\in S^{\frac{m}{2}}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

Now, let us assume that for some positive real number
C_{0}>0,
the symbol

A(x,\pi):=\frac{1}{2}(a(x,\pi)+a(x,\pi)^{*}),\,(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G},\,\,a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

is elliptic and satisfies

\displaystyle A(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},

in the operator sense, that is,
A(x,\pi)-C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}\geqslant 0.
Then, for
C_{1}\in(0,{C_{0}})
we have that

\displaystyle A(x,\pi)-C_{1}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}\geqslant\left({C_{0}}-C_{1}\right)\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}>0.

If
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
from Corollary
4.4
, we have that

\displaystyle q(x,\pi):=(A(x,\pi)-C_{1}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m})^{\frac{1}{2}}\in S^{\frac{m}{2}}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

From the symbolic calculus we obtain

\displaystyle q(x,\pi)q(x,\pi)^{*}=A(x,\pi)-C_{1}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m}+r(x,\pi),\,\,r(x,\pi)\in S^{m-(\rho-\delta)}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}).

Now, let us assume that
u\in C^{\infty}(G).
Then we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(a(x,D)u,u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}((a(x,D)+\textnormal{op}(a^{*}))u,u)=(A(x,D)u,u)

\displaystyle=C_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{m}u,u)+(q(x,D)q(x,D)^{*}u,u)+(r(x,D)u,u)

\displaystyle=C_{1}(\mathcal{M}_{m}u,u)+(q(x,D)^{*}u,q(x,D)^{*}u)-(r(x,D)u,u)

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-(r(x,D)u,u)

\displaystyle=C_{1}\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-(\mathcal{M}^{-\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}r(x,D)u,\mathcal{M}^{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}u).

Observe that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}^{-\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}r(x,D)u,\mathcal{M}^{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathcal{M}^{-\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}r(x,D)u\|_{L^{2}(G)}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}

\displaystyle=\|r(x,D)u\|_{L^{2}_{-\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)},

where in the last line we have used the Sobolev boundedness of
r(x,D)
from
L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)
into
L^{2}_{-\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G).
Consequently, we deduce the lower bound

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(a(x,D)u,u)\geqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-C\|u\|_{L^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}^{2}.

If we temporarily assume that for every
\varepsilon>0,
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0,
such that

\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m-(\rho-\delta)}{2}}(G)}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

(4.4)

for
0<\varepsilon<C_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(a(x,D)u,u)\geqslant(C_{1}-\varepsilon)\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-C_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}.

So, with the exception of the proof of (
4.4
) we have deduced the following estimate which is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 4.5

(Gårding inequality for graded Lie groups)
.

For
0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
let
a(x,D):C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
be an operator with symbol
a\in{S}^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
,
m\in\mathbb{R}
. Let us assume that for some positive real number
C_{0}>0,
the real part of
a(x,\pi),
that is

A(x,\pi):=\frac{1}{2}(a(x,\pi)+a(x,\pi)^{*}),\,(x,\pi)\in G\times\widehat{G},\,\,a\in S^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

is elliptic and satisfies

\displaystyle A(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},\quad\pi\in\widehat{G},

in the operator sense. Then, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0,
such that the lower bound

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(a(x,D)u,u)\geqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-C_{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

(4.5)

holds true for every
u\in C^{\infty}(G).

In view of the analysis above, for the proof of Theorem
4.5
we only need to prove (
4.4
). However we will deduce it from the following more general lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let us assume that
s\geqslant t\geqslant 0
or that
s,t<0.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0,
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{t}(G)}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{s}(G)}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

(4.6)

holds true for every
u\in C^{\infty}(G).

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0.
Then, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(1+\nu_{ii}(\pi))^{2t/\nu}-\varepsilon(1+\nu_{ii}(\pi))^{2s/\nu}\leqslant C_{\varepsilon},

uniformly in
\pi\in\widehat{G}.
Then (
4.6
) follows from the Plancherel theorem. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{t}{2}}(G)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}(1+\nu_{ii}(\pi))^{2t/\nu}|\widehat{u}_{ij}(\pi)|^{2}d\pi

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\limits_{\widehat{G}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}(\varepsilon(1+\nu_{ii}(\pi))^{2s/\nu}+C_{\varepsilon})|\widehat{u}_{ij}(\pi)|^{2}d\pi

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{s}(G)}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
a(x,D):C^{\infty}(G)\rightarrow\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(G)
be an operator with symbol
a\in{S}^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G})
,
m\in\mathbb{R}
. Let us assume that for some
C_{0}>0,
the symbol
a(x,\pi)
is elliptic and satisfies

\displaystyle a(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},

in the operator sense. Then, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0,
such that the lower bound

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(a(x,D)u,u)\geqslant C_{1}\|u\|_{{L}^{2}_{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-C_{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

(4.7)

holds true for every
u\in C^{\infty}(G).

5.
Analysis of diffusion problems on graded groups

The complex
pseudo-differential functional calculus here developed for the global Hörmander classes on graded Lie groups will be applied to the well-posedness of parabolic and hyperbolic problems.
More precisely, if
T>0
we will study the regularity, existence and uniqueness for the following Cauchy problem

(\textnormal{IVP}):\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)v+f,&\text{ }v\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
v(0)=u_{0},&\text{ }\end{cases}

(5.1)

where the initial data
u_{0}\in H^{s}(G),

K(t):=K(t,x,D)\in\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

f\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{s}(G)),

m>0,

s\in\mathbb{R},
and a suitable positivity condition is imposed on
K.
Indeed, we consider the operator

\textnormal{Re}(K(t)):=\frac{1}{2}(K(t)+K(t)^{*}),\,\,0\leqslant t\leqslant T,

to be elliptic, and in order to use the Gårding inequality (see Theorem
4.5
), we will assume that there is a positive real number
C_{0}>0,
such that for any
t,

\displaystyle\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},\quad\pi\in\widehat{G},

in the operator sense.
We recall that for any
s\in\mathbb{R},
the Sobolev space
H^{s}(G)\equiv L^{2}_{s}(G)
is defined as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{0}(G)
with respect to the norm

\|u\|_{H^{s}(G)}\equiv\|u\|_{L^{2}_{s}(G)}:=\|(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}u\|_{L^{2}(G)}.

Under such assumptions we will prove the existence and uniqueness of a solution
v\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)).
We will start with the following energy estimate.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
K(t)=K(t,x,D)\in\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
be a pseudo-differential operator of order
m>0,
such that:

•

\textnormal{Re}(K(t))
is elliptic for every
t\in[0,T]
with
T>0.

•

The symbol
\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)
of
K(t)
satisfies the operator inequality

\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},\pi\in\widehat{G},

for some
C_{0}>0.

If
v\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)),
then there exist
C,C^{\prime}>0,
such that

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|v(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|(\partial_{t}-K(\tau))v(\tau)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}d\tau\right),

(5.2)

holds for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T.
Moreover, we also have the estimate

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|v(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|(\partial_{t}-K(\tau)^{*})v(\tau)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}d\tau\right).

(5.3)

Proof.

Let
v\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)).
The hypothesis

v\in C([0,T],H^{\frac{m}{2}}(G))

will be used in order to apply the Gårding inequality. This fact will be useful later because we will use the Gårding inequality applied to the operator
\textnormal{Re}(-K(t)).
So,

v\in\textnormal{Dom}(\partial_{\tau}-K(\tau))
for every
0\leqslant\tau\leqslant T.
In view of the embedding
H^{m/2}\hookrightarrow L^{2}(G),
we also have that
v\in C([0,T],L^{2}(G)).
Let us define
f(\tau):=Q(\tau)v(\tau),
with
Q(\tau):=(\partial_{\tau}-K(\tau)),
for every
0\leqslant\tau\leqslant T.
Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left(v(t),v(t)\right)_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{dv(t)}{dt},v(t)\right)_{L^{2}(G)}+\left(v(t),\frac{dv(t)}{dt}\right)_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\left(K(t)v(t)+f(t),v(t)\right)_{L^{2}(G)}+\left(v(t),K(t)v(t)+f(t)\right)_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=\left((K(t)+K(t)^{*})v(t),v(t)\right)_{L^{2}(G)}+2\textnormal{Re}(f(t),v(t))_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle=2\textnormal{Re}(K(t)v(t),v(t))_{L^{2}(G)}+2\textnormal{Re}(f(t),v(t))_{L^{2}(G)}.

Now, from the Gårding inequality,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Re}(-K(t)v(t),v(t))\geqslant C_{1}\|v(t)\|_{H^{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}-C_{2}\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2},

(5.4)

and the parallelogram law, we have

\displaystyle 2\textnormal{Re}(f(t),v(t))_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\textnormal{Re}(f(t),v(t))_{L^{2}(G)}+\|f(t)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}+\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f(t)+v(t)\|^{2}\leqslant\|f(t)+v(t)\|^{2}+\|f(t)-v(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+2\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)},

and consequently

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(C_{2}\|v(t)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}-C_{1}\|v(t)\|_{H^{\frac{m}{2}}(G)}\right)+2\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+2\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}.

So, we have proved that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\lesssim\|f(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}.

By using Gronwall’s Lemma we obtain the energy estimate

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|v(0)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|f(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}d\tau\right),

(5.5)

for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T,
and
T>0.

To finish the proof, we can change the analysis above with
v(T-\cdot)
instead of
v(\cdot),

f(T-\cdot)
instead of
f(\cdot)
and
Q^{*}=-\partial_{t}-K(t)^{*},
(or equivalently
Q=\partial_{t}-K(t)
) instead of
Q^{*}=-\partial_{t}+K(t)^{*}
(or equivalently
Q=\partial_{t}-K(t)
) using that
\textnormal{Re}(K(T-t)^{*})=\textnormal{Re}(K(T-t))
to deduce that

\displaystyle\|v(T-t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(C\|v(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|(-\partial_{t}+K(T-t)^{*})v(T-\tau)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle=\left(C\|v(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|(-\partial_{t}-K(t)^{*})v(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}ds\right).

So, we conclude the proof.
∎

Now, we will prove the existence and uniqueness for (
5.1
) when the initial data are taken in
L^{2}(G).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
K(t)=K(t,x,D)\in\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
be a pseudo-differential operator of order
m>0,
such that:

•

\textnormal{Re}(K(t))
is elliptic for every
t\in[0,T]
with
T>0.

•

The symbol
\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)
of
K(t)
satisfies the operator inequality

\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},\pi\in\widehat{G},

for some
C_{0}>0.

Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(G)
, and let
f\in L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)).
Then there exists a unique
v\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G))
solving (
5.1
). Moreover,
v
satisfies the energy estimate

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))}\right),

(5.6)

for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

Proof.

We have proved the
a priori
estimate in Proposition
5.1
for

v\in X_{m}:=C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)).

Essentially the condition
v\in C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G))
was used when applying the Gårding inequality in (
5.4
). Now, we will use the Hanh-Banach theorem to deduce that the solution to (
5.1
) indeed, lives in
X_{m}.
So, let us denote
Q=\partial_{t}-K(t),
and let us introduce the spaces

\displaystyle E:=\{\phi\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)):\phi(T)=0\},

and
Q^{*}E:=\{Q^{*}\phi:\phi\in E\}.
Let us define the linear form
\beta\in(Q^{*}E)^{\prime}
by

\beta(Q^{*}\phi):=\int\limits_{0}^{T}(f(\tau),\phi(\tau))d\tau+\frac{1}{i}(u_{0},\phi(0)).

From (
5.3
) we deduce for every
\phi\in E
that

\displaystyle\|\phi(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(C\|\phi(T)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|(\partial_{t}-K(\tau)^{*})\phi(\tau)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}d\tau\right).

So, we have

\displaystyle|\beta(Q^{*}\phi)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\limits_{0}^{T}\|f(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(G)}\|\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(G)}d\tau+\|u_{0}\|_{{L^{2}(G)}}\|\phi(0)\|_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))}+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(G)}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(G)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{T}(\|f\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)}+\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(G)})\|Q^{*}\phi(\tau)\|_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))},

which shows that
\beta
is a bounded functional on
\mathscr{T}:=Q^{*}E\cap L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)),
with
\mathscr{T}
endowed with the topology induced by the norm of
L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)).
By using the Hahn-Banach extension theorem, we can extends
\beta
to a bounded functional
\tilde{\beta}
on
L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)),
and by using the Riesz representation theorem, there exists
v\in(L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))^{\prime}=L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)),
such that

\tilde{\beta}(\psi)=(v,\psi),\quad\psi\in L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G)).

In particular, for
\psi=Q^{*}\phi\in\mathscr{T},
we have

\tilde{\beta}(Q^{*}\phi)={\beta}(Q^{*}\phi)=(v,Q^{*}\phi),

Because, we can identify
C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T),\mathscr{D}(G))
as a subspace of
E

C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T),C^{\infty}(G))\subset E=\{\phi\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G)):\phi(T)=0\},

we have the identity

\displaystyle(f,\phi)=\int\limits_{0}^{T}(f(\tau),\phi(\tau))d\tau=\int\limits_{0}^{T}(f(\tau),\phi(\tau))d\tau+\frac{1}{i}(u_{0},\phi(0))=(v,Q^{*}\phi),

for every
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T),C^{\infty}(G)).
So, this implies that
v\in\textnormal{Dom}(Q^{**}).
Because
Q^{**}=Q,
we have that

\displaystyle(v,Q^{*}\phi)=(Qv,\phi)=(f,\phi),\,\,\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T),C^{\infty}(G)),

which implies that
Qv=f.
A routine argument of integration by parts shows that
v(0)=u_{0}.
Now, in order to show the uniqueness of
v,
let us assume that
u\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G))
is a solution of the problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)u+f,&\text{ }u\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
u(0)=u_{0}.&\text{ }\end{cases}

Then
\omega:=v-u\in C^{1}([0,T],L^{2}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2}(G))
solves the problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)\omega,&\text{ }\omega\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
\omega(0)=0,&\text{ }\end{cases}

and from Proposition
5.1
,
\|\omega(t)\|_{L^{2}(G)}=0,
for all
0\leqslant t\leqslant T,
and consequently, from the continuity in
t
of the functions we have that
v(t,x)=u(t,x)
for all
t\in[0,T]
and a.e.
x\in G.

∎

Now, we extend Theorem
5.2
to the case where the initial data are considered in general Sobolev spaces (we consider the additional hypothesis that
K(t)
commutes with
\mathcal{R}
).

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
K(t)=K(t,x,D)\in\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

0\leqslant\delta<\rho\leqslant 1,
be a pseudo-differential operator of order
m>0,
such that:

•

\textnormal{Re}(K(t))
is elliptic for every
t\in[0,T]
with
T>0.

•

The symbol
\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)
of
K(t)
satisfies the operator inequality

\sigma_{K(t)}(x,\pi)\geqslant C_{0}\pi(\mathcal{M})^{m},\pi\in\widehat{G},

for some
C_{0}>0.

•

K(t)
commutes with the Rockland operator
\mathcal{R}.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R},

u_{0}\in H^{s}(G)
, and let
f\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{s}(G)).
Then there exists a unique
v\in C^{1}([0,T],H^{s}(G))\cap C([0,T],H^{m/2+s}(G))
solving (
5.1
). Moreover,
v
satisfies the energy estimate

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s}(G)}+C^{\prime}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T],H^{s}(G))}\right),

(5.7)

for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T.

Proof.

Observe that if
v
is a solution for the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)v+f,&\text{ }v\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
v(0)=u_{0},&\text{ }\end{cases}

where the initial data
u_{0}\in H^{s}(G),

K(t):=K(t,x,D)\in\Psi^{m}_{\rho,\delta}(G\times\widehat{G}),

f\in L^{2}([0,T],H^{s}(G)),
then using the commutativity of
K(t)
with
\mathcal{R},
(and hence with its spectral calculus) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}v

\displaystyle=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}K(t,x,D)v+(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}f

\displaystyle=K(t,x,D)(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}v+(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}f,

showing that
v^{\prime}:=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}v\in L^{2}(G)
and
f^{\prime}:=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}f\in L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))
satisfy the initial boundary value problem

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial v^{\prime}}{\partial t}=K(t,x,D)v^{\prime}+f^{\prime},&\text{ }v^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times G),\\
v^{\prime}(0)=u^{\prime}_{0}:=(1+\mathcal{R})^{\frac{s}{\nu}}u_{0}\in L^{2}(G).&\text{ }\end{cases}

So, by applying Proposition
5.2
we get the estimate

\|v^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|u^{\prime}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}+C^{\prime}\|f^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(G))}\right),

(5.8)

for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T.
Note that by applying Proposition
5.2
we have that
v^{\prime}\in C([0,T],H^{m}(G))
which implies that
v\in C([0,T],H^{m+s}(G)).
Finally, observe that (
5.8
) is equivalent to the following energy inequality

\|v(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}(G)}\leqslant\left(C\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s}(G)}+C^{\prime}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{s}([0,T],L^{2}(G))}\right),

(5.9)

for every
0\leqslant t\leqslant T,
proving Theorem
5.3
.
∎
