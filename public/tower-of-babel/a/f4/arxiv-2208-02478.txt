The Bad Locus in the moduli of Super Riemann Surfaces with Ramond punctures
Ron Donagi, Nadia Ott

Abstract
. The bad locus in the moduli of super Riemann surfaces with Ramond punctures parametrizes those super Riemann surfaces that have more than the expected number of independent closed holomorphic 1-forms. There is a super period map that depends on certain discrete choices. For each such choice, the period map blows up along a divisor that contains the bad locus. Our main result is that away from the bad locus, at least one of these period maps remains finite. In other words, we identify the bad locus as the intersection of the blowup divisors. The proof abstracts the situation into a question in linear algebra, which we then solve. We also give some bounds on the dimension of the bad locus.

2.
Periods of super Riemann surfaces with Ramond punctures

A genus g super Riemann surface
X
with
2r
Ramond punctures determines a curve
C
of the same genus
g
with a divisor
D=\Sigma_{i=1}^{2r}x_{i}
consisting of
2r
distinct labeled points and a twisted spin structure, i.e. a line bundle
L
satisfying

(1)

L^{\otimes 2}=K_{C}(D).

Riemann-Roch gives

(2)

\chi(L)=h^{0}(L)-h^{0}(L(-D))=r.

For
r>0
an easy general position argument shows that generically
h^{0}(L)=r
.
This means that the set of super Riemann surfaces with
h^{0}(L)=r
can be identified with an open subset of the moduli space

(3)

M:=\{(C,D,L)\ |\ C\ \text{a curve of genus}\ g,D\in C^{2r},\ L\in{\text{Pic}}^{g+r-1}(C)\ {\text{satisfies \eqref{spin}}}\}.

Following Witten
[
W15
]
, we refer to the complement

B:=\{(C,D,L)|h^{0}(L)\geq r+1\}\subset M.

of this open subset as the “bad locus”.

The super vector space
\Omega
of closed 1-forms on
X
has dimension
g|h^{0}(L)
.
(Our sign convention is that when
X
is split, the forms pulled back from the reduced curve
C
are considered even, e.g, for local coordinates
(z,\theta)
on
X
,
dz
is even and
d\theta
is odd. Both this and the opposite convention occur in the literature.)
In the split case, the even closed forms are pulled back from
H^{0}(C,K_{C})
while the odd closed forms are exact, of the form
da(z)\theta
where
a=a(z)
is a section of the twisted spin bundle
L
, cf.
[
W15
]
, section 5.1, or our appendix
B.2
.
So generically
\Omega
is of dimension
g|r
.

We recall Witten’s superperiod map

P:\Omega\to\Lambda=\Lambda_{0}\oplus\Lambda_{-}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{2g|2r}.

This depends on some discrete choices.
The
2g
even periods, just as in the classical theory, depend on the choice of a symplectic basis
(a_{1},\dots,a_{g};b_{1},\dots,b_{g})
of
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
.
Since the residue gives a natural trivialization of the fiber of
K_{C}(D)
at each puncture
x_{i}\in D
,
equation (
1
) specifies a trivialization of the square
(L|{x_{i}})^{2}
of the fiber of
L
at
x_{i}
. By an
orientation

\psi_{i}
of the puncture we mean a square root of this, i.e. a trivialization of
L|{x_{i}}
that squares to the above trivialization of
K_{C}(D)
. The
2r
odd periods depend on a choice of orientation at each of the
2r
punctures.

These discrete choices determine identifications

\Lambda_{0}=H^{1}(C,{\mathbb{C}})

and

\Pi\Lambda_{1}=H^{0}(L/L(-D))=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2r}L_{x_{i}}.

The even part of the superperiod map is then restriction from
X
to
C
followed by integration on the symplectic basis
(a_{1},\dots,a_{g};b_{1},\dots,b_{g})
. The odd part is just the restriction from
C
to
D
:

H^{0}(C,L)\to H^{0}(D,L)=H^{0}(L/L(-D)),

cf. Appendix
B.2
.

One bad thing about the “bad” locus
B
is that this superperiod map
P
is not injective there: On
X\in B
there are (odd) closed
1
-forms with all periods equal to zero, as can be seen from the long exact sequence

(4)

0\to H^{0}(L(-D))\to H^{0}(L)\overset{P_{1}}{\to}H^{0}(L/L(-D))\to H^{1}(L(-D))\to\dots

where
h^{0}(C,L(-D))\geq 1
.

The target of the superperiod map is period superspace
\Lambda\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{2g|2r}
. This comes equipped with a super symplectic pairing
Q
(symplectic on
{\mathbb{C}}^{2g}
, orthogonal on
{\mathbb{C}}^{0|2r}
) which we may think of as a super version of the usual intersection pairing on
H^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. In the obvious notation,

Q=\Sigma_{k=1}^{g}a_{k}\wedge b_{k}-\Sigma_{i=1}^{2r}(\psi_{i})^{2},

where we recall that the orientations
\psi_{i}
form a basis for
\Lambda_{1}
.
The period map can be described by a
(g|r)\times(2g|2r)
matrix
\mathcal{P}
. In order to obtain a
(g|r)\times(g|r)
period matrix, we need to split period space as a sum of two complementary Q-Lagrangian subspaces,
\Lambda=\Lambda_{A}\oplus\Lambda_{B}
. For the even part we use the span of
(a_{1},\dots,a_{g})
for
\Lambda_{A,0}

and of
(b_{1},\dots,b_{g})
for
\Lambda_{B,0}
.
For the odd part we fix a pairing
\nu
of the
2r
punctures, e.g. we could pair
x_{2i-1}
with
x_{2i}
. We then take

\Lambda_{A,1}
as the span of
x_{2i-1}+\sqrt{-1}x_{2i}
and

\Lambda_{B,1}
as the span of
x_{2i-1}-\sqrt{-1}x_{2i}
. The period map then decomposes:
P=(P_{A},P_{B})
, with corresponding decomposition of the matrix,
\mathcal{P}=(\mathcal{P}_{A},\mathcal{P}_{B})
. The
(g|r)\times(g|r)
period matrix is then
\mathcal{P}_{A}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{B}
. This is defined only where

\mathcal{P}_{A}
is invertible. It depends on:

•

the data
X=(C,D,L)
underlying the super Riemann surface with labeled Ramond punctures,

•

the symplectic basis
(a_{1},\dots,a_{g};b_{1},\dots,b_{g})
of
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and

•

the orientations
\psi_{i}
.

Sometimes we may want to replace the specific pairing (
x_{2i-1}
with
x_{2i}
) by an arbitrary pairing
\nu
and to emphasize the dependence on this choice.
A pairing
\nu
is specified by relabeling the
2r
points as:

x_{ij},\ \ \ i=1,\dots,r,\ \ \ j\in\pm:=\{+,-\}.

By gluing the points
(x_{i+},x_{i-})
in each pair we obtain a nodal curve
\overline{C}
(of arithmetic genus
g+r
, with
r
nodes).
Specifying the pairing of the points is equivalent to specifying the normalization map
\nu:C\to\overline{C}
.

Having specified the pairing
\nu
,
a
sign choice

\sigma
is a specification of a spin bundle, aka theta characteristic,
on the nodal curve
\overline{C}
,
i.e
,
a line bundle
L_{\sigma}
on the nodal curve
\overline{C}
satisfying

(5)

L_{\sigma}^{\otimes 2}=K_{\overline{C}}.

In particular,

L:=\nu^{*}L_{\sigma}
is a twisted spin structure on
C
satisfying (
1
).
Equivalently, given
L
,
\sigma
specifies for each
i=1,\dots,r
an isomorphism

(6)

\sigma_{i}:L_{x_{i+}}\to L_{x_{i-}}

whose square:

\sigma_{i}^{\otimes 2}:K_{C}(D)_{|{x_{i+}}}=L_{{x_{i+}}}^{\otimes 2}\to L_{{x_{i-}}}^{\otimes 2}=K_{C}(D)_{|{x_{i-}}}

is identified via residues with multiplication by
-1
,

\sigma_{i}^{\otimes 2}=-1:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}.

(
L_{\sigma}
clearly determines a
\sigma
as in (
6
). Conversely, given
\sigma
as in (
6
), we recover
L_{\sigma}
as the subsheaf of
L
consisting of sections
s
of
L
satisfying

(7)

s(x_{i-})=-\sqrt{-1}\sigma_{i}s(x_{i+})

for all
1\leq i\leq r
.
In particular,
h^{0}(\overline{C},L_{\sigma})>0
if
L
has a
global
section satisfying the gluing equations (
7
) for all
1\leq i\leq r
. )

Recall that an orientation
\psi
is a trivialization of
L|_{D}
compatible via residues with (
1
). In our current notation this amounts to trivializations
\psi_{ij}
of each
L_{x_{ij}}
. Such a
\psi
combined with formula (
6
) therefore determines a

\sigma=\sigma(\psi)
. Changing
\psi
at both
x_{i+}
and
x_{i-}
leaves
\sigma
unchanged. On a given
X
there are
2^{2r}

\psi
’s but only
2^{r}

\sigma
’s.

Recall that the odd parts

P_{1}:\Omega_{1}\to\Lambda_{1},\ P_{A,1}:\Omega_{1}\to\Lambda_{A,1}

of the superperiod map depend on the orientation
\psi
. The kernels, however, depend only on
\sigma=\sigma(\psi)
. Indeed, if we identify
\Omega_{1}
with
H^{0}(C,L)
, we find the identifications :

(8)

Ker(P_{1})=H^{0}(L(-D))\subset Ker(P_{A,1})=H^{0}(\overline{C},L_{\sigma})\subset H^{0}(C,L).

We will write
\mathcal{P}^{\nu,\sigma}
when we want to emphasize that the matrix
\mathcal{P}
representing the period map depends on a choice of pairing
\nu
and sign
\sigma
.

3.
Moduli spaces and the main theorem

We are interested in describing the locus in moduli space where the period matrix is not defined, i.e., the locus where
\mathcal{P}_{A}
is singular for all possible signs
\sigma
and for some or all pairings
\nu
. To do this, we need to consider some covers of the moduli space
M
of (
3
).
Let

M^{\prime}:=\{\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L\}

be the (bosonic) moduli space parametrizing the data of a curve
C
with labeled divisor
D
, a pairing
\nu
on the points of
D
, and a twisted spin bundle
L
satisfying (
1
). There is a forgetful map

\pi:M^{\prime}\to M.

There is a further
2^{r}
-sheeted cover

\pi^{\prime}:M^{\prime\prime}\to M^{\prime}

where

M^{\prime\prime}:=\{\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L_{\sigma}\}

parametrizes curves
C
with labeled divisor
D
, a pairing of the points of
D
, and a choice of a spin bundle
L_{\sigma}
satisfying (
5
).

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote (the component of degree

curve over the Deligne-Mumford compactification of the moduli space
M_{g+r}
of curves of genus

\mathcal{P}:=\{C^{\prime},L^{\prime}\ |\ C^{\prime}\ {\text{a stable curve of genus}}\ g+r,L^{\prime}\in{\text{Pic}}^{g+r-1}(C^{\prime})\}.

In
\mathcal{P}
there is a natural divisor
\mathcal{P}_{e}
parametrizing pairs
\{C^{\prime},L^{\prime}\}
where
L^{\prime}
is an effective line bundle:
h^{0}(L^{\prime})>0
.
There is a natural map of
M^{\prime\prime}
to
\mathcal{P}
sending

\{\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L_{\sigma}\}

to

\{\overline{C},L_{\sigma}\}.

We let
Y^{\prime\prime}
denote the inverse image of the divisor
\mathcal{P}_{e}
:

Y^{\prime\prime}=Y^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}:=\{(\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L_{\sigma})\ |\ L_{\sigma}^{\otimes 2}=K_{\overline{C}},\ h^{0}(L_{\sigma})\geq 1\}\subset M^{\prime\prime}.

This is a divisor in
M^{\prime\prime}
. If
(C,D,L,\nu,\sigma)\in Y^{\prime\prime}
then there exists a global section of
L
satisfying (
7
) for all
1\leq i\leq r
, i.e., there exists a global section
s
whose odd A-periods all vanish.
So
P_{A,1}^{\nu,\sigma}
has a non-trivial kernel and the matrix
\mathcal{P}_{A,1}^{\nu,\sigma}
is singular.

As shown in Appendix A of
[
W15
]
, the period matrix (viewed as a map on
M^{\prime\prime}
) blows up (has a pole) along this divisor.
This divisor
Y^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}
does not depend on a choice of sign
\sigma
; the subscript
\sigma
simply reminds us that it is a divisor in
M^{\prime\prime}
, whose fiber over a point of
M^{\prime}
is indexed by the
\sigma
’s.) The reason that
\sigma
occurs here rather than the full orientation
\psi
was explained at the end of the previous section:
Y^{\prime\prime}
parametrizes points where the (odd part of the) superperiod map fails to be injective. According to (
8
), this depends only on
\sigma
rather than
\psi
.

Descending to
M^{\prime}
, we have the closed locus

Y^{\prime}:=\{(\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L)\ |\ L^{\otimes 2}=K_{C}(D),\ h^{0}(L_{\sigma})\geq 1\ \forall\sigma\}=\{y\in M^{\prime}\ |\ \pi^{\prime-1}(y)\subset Y^{\prime\prime}\}\subset M^{\prime}

parametrizing super Riemann surfaces with a given pairing of their
2r
Ramond punctures
D
such that the period matrix blows up for
every
sign choice
\sigma
, i.e.,
(C,D,L,\nu)\in Y^{\prime}
if
\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\nu,\sigma}
is singular for all
\sigma
. So the complement of
Y^{\prime}
is the open subset where the period matrix, for at least one sign choice
\sigma
, makes sense.
Somewhat informally we write
Y^{\prime}=\cap_{\sigma}Y^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}
. (Informal because
Y^{\prime\prime}\subset M^{\prime\prime}
while
Y^{\prime}\subset M^{\prime}
. )

Descending further to
M
, we have the closed locus

Y:=\{(C,D,L)|(\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L)\in Y^{\prime}\ \forall\nu\}=\{y\in M\ |\ \pi^{-1}(y)\subset Y^{\prime}\}\subset M,

where
(C,D,L)\in Y
if
\mathcal{P}_{A}^{\nu,\sigma}
is singular for all pairings
\nu
and signs
\sigma
,
and also the a priori larger locus

\tilde{Y}:=\{(C,D,L)|(\nu:C\to\overline{C},D,L)\in Y^{\prime}\ {\text{for some}}\ \nu\}=\pi(Y^{\prime})\subset M.

The distinction is that
(C,D,L)
is in
\tilde{Y}
if
(C,D,L,\nu)\in Y^{\prime}
for some pairing
\nu
, while in order to be in

this must hold for all
\nu
.

Lemma 1
.

B\subseteq Y\subseteq\tilde{Y}

Proof.

If
(C,D,L)\in B
, then
h^{0}(L(-D))\geq 1
and so there exists a global section of
L
satisfying the gluing equation (
7
) for all
1\leq i\leq r
and for all pairings
\nu
and signs
\sigma
.
∎

Our main result can now be stated as follows:

Theorem 2
.

B=Y=\tilde{Y}
.

4.
Linear algebra

Consider a set of
2r
abstract points

p_{ij},i=1,\dots,r,\ j\in\pm:=\{+1,-1\}.

By a section, or
r
-section, we mean a function

\sigma:\{1,\dots,r\}\to\pm,

or equivalently the set of
r
points

p_{i,\sigma(i)},\ (i=1,\dots,r).

Our study of Ramond punctures leads to the following:

Conjecture 3
.

Let
V
be a vector space of dimension
\geq r
containing
r
pairs of points
p_{ij},i=1,\dots,r,\ j\in\pm
that span
V
. The following conditions (*), (**) are equivalent:

(*) For each of the
2^{r}
sections
\sigma
, the
r
points

p_{i,\sigma(i)},\ (i=1,\dots,r)

are linearly dependent.

(**) There is a subset

I\subset\{1,\dots,r\}

of some cardinality
k<r
such that the
2k
points

p_{ij},\ i\in I,\ j\in\pm

span a subspace of dimension
<k
.

In section
A
of the appendix we prove

Theorem 4
.

The conjecture holds when
V
is
r
dimensional.

We will need only this case. The full Conjecture has been proved in
[
D22
]
.

5.
From Ramond punctures to linear algebra

In this section we reduce Theorem
2
to the linear algebra result, Theorem
4
.

We begin by proving that condition (*) in Conjecture
3
and Theorem
4
characterizes the locus
\tilde{Y}
. Let
V
be the vector space

V:=(H^{0}(L)/H^{0}(L-D))^{*}

of linear functions on
H^{0}(L)
that vanish on
H^{0}(L-D)
.
(Equivalently, it is the dual of the image of the odd superperiod map
P_{1}
.)
By Riemann-Roch, its dimension is
r
.
We are interested mostly in the case that
h^{0}(L)=r
, so
H^{0}(L-D)=0
and
V=H^{0}(L)^{*}
.
For each
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
and
j\in\pm
,
fix an orientation
\psi_{ij}
, i.e. a trivialization of the fiber
L_{|x_{ij}}
of
L
at
x_{ij}
, compatible with the residue trivialization of
L^{\otimes 2}=K_{C}(D)
.
Evaluation at
x_{ij}
then becomes a vector
q_{ij}\in V
.
We let
p_{ij}:=q_{i+}+j\sqrt{-1}q_{i-}
, or explicitly:

p_{i+}:=q_{i+}+\sqrt{-1}q_{i-},\ \ \ p_{i-}:=q_{i+}-\sqrt{-1}q_{i-}.

The collection of
2r
vectors
q_{ij}
clearly spans
V
, and therefore so do the
p_{ij}
. A sign choice
\sigma
picks, for each
i
, a vector
p_{i\sigma(i)}
which is one of
p_{i+},p_{i-}
.
The condition for being in
Y^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}
is that the
r
points
p_{i\sigma(i)},\ \ i=1,\dots,r
should be contained in a hyperplane in
V
.
Condition (*) of Theorem
4
therefore characterizes
\tilde{Y}
.

Next we describe a certain locus
W
in moduli space which we will show is equal to both
B
(Lemma
7
) and
\tilde{Y}
(Lemma
6
), and thereby we prove Theorem
2
.
For every subset

I\subset\{1,\dots,r\},

the divisor

D_{I}:=\Sigma_{i\in I}(x_{i+}+x_{i-})

which is of degree
2k
where
k:=\#I
, determines the locus:

W_{I}:=\{(C,D,L)|h^{0}(L(-D_{I}))\geq r+1-k\}\subset M.

For example, the bad locus
B
equals
W_{\emptyset}
. Now let

W:=\cup_{I}W_{I}.

Note 5
.

These loci obey a duality. Let
k:=\#I
and let
I^{\prime}
denote the complement of
I
. We have

h^{0}(L(-D_{I}))=\chi(L(-D_{I}))+h^{1}(L(-D_{I}))=r-2k+h^{0}(K\otimes L^{-1}(D-D_{I^{\prime}}))=r-2k+h^{0}(L-D_{I^{\prime}}))

so
W_{I^{\prime}}=W_{I}
.

Lemma 6
.

Condition (**) of Conjecture
3
and Theorem
4
characterizes
W
. In particular,
W=\tilde{Y}
.

Proof.

Let
k:=\#I
.
The condition that the
2k
points
p_{ij},\ i\in I,\ j\in\pm
are contained in a subspace of dimension
k-1
implies that we are in
W_{I}
.
The converse fails in general, because
h^{0}(L)
can be
>r
if we are at a point of
B
.
However, if we are at a point of
\tilde{Y}
that is not in
B=W_{\emptyset}
, then
h^{0}(L)=r
,
H^{0}(L-D)=0
and
V=H^{0}(L)^{*}
, and then the condition that the
2k
points
p_{ij},\ i\in I,\ j\in\pm
are contained in a subspace of dimension
k-1
is equivalent to being in
W_{I}
.
So Condition (**) of Theorem
4
characterizes
W
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2
is now reduced to the following result:

Lemma 7
.

We have the inclusion and equality:

B=W_{\emptyset}=\cup_{I}W_{I}=W\subset Y.

In particular,
B=Y=\tilde{Y}
.

Proof.

A point of
W_{I}
is given by
(C,D,L)
such that
h^{0}(L(-D_{I}))\geq r+1-\#I
.
For elements of
H^{0}(L(-D_{I}))
,
the conditions for being in some
Y^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}
are clearly satisfied automatically at points of
I
since such an element vanishes along the divisor

,
so we only need to impose

conditions at the remaining points in

,
ending with
h^{0}(L_{\sigma})\geq 1
. Thus
W=\cup_{I}W_{I}\subset Y
.

On the other hand, each
W_{I}
is contained in
B
.
Indeed, say we have a point of
W_{I}
that is not in
B
.
Then
h^{0}(L)=r
and the divisor
D_{I}
imposes at most

, where we assume

.
But the equality

implies that the divisor

imposes at most

.
So
h^{0}(L-D)\geq r-(k-1)-(r-k-1)=2
, contradicting (
2

6.
Dimension counts

Among general line bundles
L
of degree
g-1+r
on a curve
C
of genus
g
, for
0\leq r<g
, the locus where
h^{0}(L)>r
has codimension
r+1
by Brill-Noether theory
[
BN
,
ACGH
]
. One might therefore naively expect that the bad locus
B
should have codimension
r+1
in
M
. The residue theorem implies that this expectation is wrong: it is off by at least 1. In this section we show that there are no further corrections:

Theorem 8
.

•

In the unpunctured case
r=0
, the moduli space
M=M^{+}\cup M^{-}
is reducible. The bad locus
B
consists of all of
M^{-}
plus the irreducible “vanishing thetanull” divisor in
M^{+}
.

•

For
1\leq r<g
, every component of
B
has codimension
\leq r
and there is a component of codimension exactly
r
.

•

For
r\geq g
the bad locus is empty.

Proof.

The case
r=0
is well known:
M^{-}
is the moduli of odd spin bundles
L
, where
h^{0}(L)
is odd and in particular
h^{0}(L)\geq 1
.

M^{+}
is the moduli of even spin bundles
L
, for which
h^{0}(L)=0
generically and
h^{0}(L)
is even and
\geq 2
along the irreducible divisor of vanishing thetanulls
[
A71
,
M71
]
.

For
r\geq g
, the degree of a twisted spin bundle
L
is
\geq 2g-1
, so
h^{1}(L)=0
and
h^{0}(L)=r
, so the bad locus is empty.

Assume
1\leq r<g
. To see that the codimension is at most
r
(and thus off by 1 from the Brill-Noether expectation), it is convenient to switch to the
2r
-sheeted cover

\tilde{M}:=\{(C,D,L,p)\ |\ (C,D,L)\in M\ {\text{and}}\ p\in D\}

and to
\tilde{B}
, the inverse image there of
B
.
Consider the map

f:\tilde{M}\to{\text{Pic}}^{g-r}(\mathcal{C}/M),

sending

(C,D,L,p)\mapsto N:=L(p-D).

Here
\mathcal{C}
is the universal curve over
M
. In the Picard, the effective locus

G^{0}_{g-r}:=\{N\ |\ h^{0}(N)>0\}

has codimension
r
. We claim that

\tilde{B}=f^{-1}(G^{0}_{g-r}),

which implies that the codimension is at most
r
.

Indeed in one direction,

L\in\tilde{B}\iff h^{0}(L)>r\iff h^{0}(L(-D))>0\implies h^{0}(N)>0.

But conversely, a non-zero section
s\in H^{0}(N)
gives a meromorphic 1-form

s^{2}\in H^{0}(K(-(D-p))(p))

with a possible pole only at
p
. By the residue theorem, it actually cannot have a pole there, so
s
must be a non-zero section in
H^{0}(N-p)=H^{0}(L-D)
.

To conclude, we need to exhibit a component of codimension
r
. Let
A
be a generic odd theta characteristic:

A^{\otimes 2}=K,h^{0}(A)=1.

Let
a\in{\text{Sym}}^{g-1}(C)
be the unique divisor of a non-zero section of
A
, and let
d\in{\text{Sym}}^{r}(C)
be the sum of any
r
of these points. (Recall we are assuming
r\leq g-1
.) Let
p\in d
be one of these points. Generically such
p
is not in
a-d
, which we will assume.
Take
D:=2d,L:=A(d)
. We claim that
f(\tilde{M})
is transversal to
G^{0}_{g-r}
at
f(C,D,L)
, so the component of
\tilde{B}
through
(C,D,L)
has codimension
r
.

This is seen by comparing the tangent spaces to
f(\tilde{M})
and
G^{0}_{g-r}
at

f(C,D,L)=N:=L(p-D)=A(d+p-2d)=A(-(d-p)).

The tangent there to
G^{0}_{g-r}
is spanned by the
g-r
tangent lines to the Abel-Jacobi image of
C
in its Jacobian at the points of
a-d+p
, or projectively by the corresponding
g-r
points of the canonical image of
C
in canonical space.
The tangent to
f(\tilde{M})
is likewise spanned by the
2r
tangent lines to the Abel-Jacobi (or: canonical) image of
C
at the points of
D=2d
. (Since our
D
is non-reduced, this really means the
r
osculating planes to
C
at the points of
d
.) This follows from the relation

N^{\otimes 2}=K(-(D-p)+p),

which implies that the differential of
f
at each of these points is
\pm\frac{1}{2}
times the corresponding differential of Abel-Jacobi, so the spans are the same.

The transversality is therefore equivalent to saying that the divisor

2d+(a-d+p)=a+d+p

is not contained in any canonical divisor. From our assumption that
h^{0}(A)=1
it follows that the unique canonical divisor containing
a
equals
2a
. This does not contain
a+d+p
because of our assumption that
p\notin a-d
.

∎
