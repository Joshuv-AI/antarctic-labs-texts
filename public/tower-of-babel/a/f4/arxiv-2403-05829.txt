Measuring Robustness in Cyber-Physical Systems under Sensor Attacks Note: This is an extended and revised version of a preliminary paper appeared in the proceedings of the 26th ACM International Conference on Hybrid Systems: Computation and Control (HSCC’23) [ 1 ] .
Jian Xiang, Ruggero Lanotte, Simone Tini, Stephen Chong, Massimo Merro

Abstract
This paper contributes a formal framework for quantitative analysis of bounded sensor attacks on cyber-physical systems, using the formalism of differential dynamic logic. Given a precondition and postcondition of a system, we formalize two quantitative safety notions, quantitative forward and backward safety, which respectively express (1) how strong the strongest postcondition of the system is with respect to the specified postcondition, and (2) how strong the specified precondition is with respect to the weakest precondition of the system needed to ensure the specified postcondition holds. We introduce two notions, forward and backward robustness, to characterize the robustness of a system against sensor attacks as the loss of safety. To reason about robustness, we introduce two simulation distances, forward and backward simulation distances, which are defined based on the behavioral distances between the original system and the system with compromised sensors. Forward and backward distances, respectively, characterize upper bounds of the degree of forward and backward safety loss caused by the sensor attacks. We verify the two simulation distances by expressing them as modalities, i.e., formulas of differential dynamic logic, and develop an ad-hoc proof system to reason with such formulas. We showcase our formal notions and reasoning techniques on two non-trivial case studies: an autonomous vehicle that needs to avoid collision and a water tank system.

Introduction

Cyber-Physical Systems (CPSs) are integrations of networking and distributed computing systems with physical processes, where feedback loops allow physical processes to affect computations and vice versa.
A peculiar class of attacks in such systems is the so-called
physics-based attacks
: attacks targeting the physical devices (sensors and actuators)
of CPSs

, such as DoS or integrity attacks on sensors, may lead to crashing the system under attack

, or allow an adversary to control the system

.

The importance of ensuring the

.
Existing work often treats satisfaction of safety as a boolean predicate: either a system satisfies a desired safety property or it does not.
However, a simple yes/no answer doesn’t fit the setting of CPSs, which interact with continuous and quantitative entities, such as measurements of the controlled physical process.
For example, under the same road conditions, a vehicle with a shorter braking distance toward an obstacle is considered safer than a vehicle with a longer braking distance, even if both of them can brake in time. Thus, when working with CPSs, a
quantitative
notion of safety can be much more informative than standard safety.

However, knowing the degree of safety of a CPS is not enough to analyze the effect of attacks targeting its sensors. For example, a vehicle with a very short braking distance may not be able to tolerate certain attacks on the

obstacle detection system
, resulting in unsafe runtime behaviors.
Here, it is important to understand the
robustness
of a system’s safety under sensor attacks, that is, how the safety may change because of sensor attacks.
For example, consider a vehicle equipped with a self-braking system whose
safety requirement
is to brake from the speed of 100 km/h when an obstacle is detected 40 meters away.
And suppose the vehicle, at that speed, starts braking when the obstacle is detected 60 meters away.
Assume that an adversary is able to perturb the readings of the distance to an obstacle by 10 meters without being detected. Then, the vehicle is still safe as it starts braking, at the speed of 100 km/h, when the obstacle is 50 meters away; 10 meters more than the safety requirements.
The degree of safety loss is a clear indicator of the vehicle’s robustness against such an attack.

In this work, we define two notions of
quantitative safety
for CPSs and use them to analyze a system’s robustness under sensor attacks. Our threat model assumes
bounded sensor attacks
, that is, attacks that may compromise a subset of sensors and offset their readings by some degree.
We do not model or discover the mechanisms by which attackers manipulate sensor values; we simply assume they are able to do so. We also assume every system has a known
precondition
and
postcondition
. The precondition specifies the initial conditions and environment when the system starts operating, and the postcondition specifies the desired condition that the system should always satisfy for it to be safe.

The first notion is
forward quantitative safety
, which estimates the room for maneuver to ensure that the system remains safe after any execution starting from a state satisfying the precondition. It basically estimates how strong the strongest postcondition is with respect to the desired safety postcondition. Said in other words, given a precondition, forward safety provides a quantification of the margins on possible strengthening of the safety postcondition with respect to the strongest postcondition. Technically, it is defined as the
shortest
distance between the set of states satisfying the strongest postcondition and the set of unsafe states.
The larger this distance is, the further away the system’s reachable states are from unsafe states, and thus the safer the system is.
Built upon forward safety, we introduce

forward robustness
, which characterizes the impact of a given sensor attack as a ratio: the degree of forward safety of the compromised system over the degree of forward safety of the original system. Intuitively, the closer the ratio gets to 1, the more robust the original system is against the attack. A ratio of 1 means the attack doesn’t weaken the safety guarantee at all.

The second notion is
backward quantitative safety
, which provides a degree of safety by estimating the room for maneuver to ensure that the system remains safe with respect to a given postcondition by weakening the precondition. It basically estimates how strong the specified precondition is with respect to the weakest precondition needed to ensure the safety of the system after its execution. Said in other words, given a safety postcondition, backward safety provides a quantification of the precondition with respect to the weakest precondition.
Technically, it is defined as the
shortest
distance between the set of states satisfying the precondition
and the set of “bad” initial states that may lead the system to unsafe states.
The larger this distance is, the further away the system’s states that satisfy the precondition are from “bad” initial states, and thus the safer the system is.
Built upon backward safety, we introduce
backward robustness
that characterizes the impact of a sensor attack as a ratio: the degree of backward safety of the compromised system over the original system. Similar to forward robustness, the closer the ratio gets to 1, the more robust the original system is against the attack.

The two robustness notions together give system designers a good way to understand and compare different design candidates by focusing either on preconditions or on postconditions. For example, if a system is likely to suffer from sensor attacks, a designer may simply choose a candidate design with better degrees of robustness.
If one degree of robustness (e.g., forward robustness) is identical or similar among different designs, the designers may use the other (e.g., backward robustness) to compare the designs.

To reason about forward (and backward) robustness, we introduce a forward (and backward) simulation distance to, respectively, provide an upper bound of the loss of forward (and backward) safety caused by sensor attacks. The simulation distances are defined based on the behavioral distances
[
18
]
between the original system and the system with compromised sensors. In particular, the forward simulation distance characterizes the
forward distance
between the two systems by quantifying the distance between their reachable states, given the same set of initial states. Thus, the forward distance between the original and the compromised system returns an upper bound on the admissible perturbations introduced by a sensor attack on the safety of the behaviors originating from a desired precondition.
Analogously, the backward simulation distance characterizes the
backward distance
between the two systems by quantifying the distance between their sets of safe initial states, i.e., those states that never lead the system to an unsafe state, given the same set of safe final states. Thus, the backward distance between the original and the compromised system returns an upper bound on the admissible perturbations introduced by a sensor attack on the initial states leading to possible violations of safety, given a desired postcondition.
We prove that the forward (and backward) simulation distance represents a sound proof technique for calculating upper bounds of forward (and backward) robustness as it returns upper bounds of the loss of forward (and backward) safety caused by sensor attacks.

2.3
Distance Metrics

To conduct quantitative analysis, we define a notion of
distance between states
, using the
Euclidean distance

\rho:\textsc{Sta}\times\textsc{Sta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\rho(\omega,\nu)=\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{V}}(\omega(x)-\nu(x))^{2}}

(1)

Notice that
\rho
is a
metric
, namely, it satisfies the following properties:

\rho(\omega,\nu)=0
if and only if
\omega=\nu
;

\rho(\omega,\nu)=\rho(\nu,\omega)
; and

\rho(\omega,\nu)\leq\rho(\omega,\mu)+\rho(\mu,\nu)
for
\omega,\nu,\mu\in\textsc{Sta}
.

For a state
\omega
and a real
\epsilon>0
, the ball of ray
\epsilon
centered in
\omega
is the set of states

\textbf{B}(\omega,\epsilon)=\{\nu~|~\rho(\omega,\nu)\leq\epsilon\}
.

We adopt existing notions
[
22
,
23
]
to specify the distance between a state and a set of states:

•

The distance between a state
\omega
and a set of states
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\textsc{Sta}
is the
shortest
distance between
\omega
and all states in
\mathcal{S}
, that is,

\bm{\mathsf{dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})=\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\rho(\omega,\nu)~|~\nu\in\mathcal{S}\}

•

The
depth
of
\omega
in
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\textsc{Sta}
is the
shortest
distance between
\omega
and the
boundary
of
\mathcal{S}
, that is,

\bm{\mathsf{depth}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})=\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\rho(\omega,\nu)~|~\nu\in(\textsc{Sta}\setminus\mathcal{S})\}

•

The
signed distance
between
\omega
and a set of states
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\textsc{Sta}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathsf{depth}}(\omega,\mathcal{S}),&\text{if }\omega\in\mathcal{S}\\
-\bm{\mathsf{dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S}),&\text{if }\omega\not\in\mathcal{S}\\
\end{cases}

Note that in the first case the signed distance is a positive real number, while in the second case the signed distance is negative. Thus,
\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})>0
implies that
\textbf{B}(\omega,\epsilon)\subseteq\mathcal{S}
for all
\epsilon<\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})
, whereas
\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})<0
implies that
\textbf{B}(\omega,\epsilon)\subseteq(\textsc{Sta}\setminus\mathcal{S})
for all
\epsilon<-\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})
.

\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})=0
is not very informative.

Here, we assume
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}~\emptyset=\infty
and
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}~\mathbb{R}=-\infty
, and we let operator
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}
in the set of
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty,-\infty\}
, thus every set has an infimum.

3
Quantitative Safety

The Boolean notion of safety in
d
\mathcal{L}
, e.g.,
\textsc{safe}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
, does not provide any quantitative information on how “good” (i.e., safe) the system is. In this section, we introduce two quantitative notions of safety. The two notions are the foundation of defining forward and backward robustness. They are, respectively, built on the
strongest postcondition
and
weakest precondition
in the setting of
d
\mathcal{L}
. In defining quantitative safety, we use hybrid program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
to model a system of interest, formula
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
as the precondition of the system, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
as the postcondition.

3.1
Extended
d
\mathcal{L}

To help define quantitative safety, we extend
d
\mathcal{L}
with another syntactic structure:
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}
, which intuitively represents the
strongest postcondition
after the execution of the program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
in a state satisfying the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
. Its formal definition is the following:

\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}\rrbracket=\{~\nu~|~\exists\omega\text{~such that~}\omega\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}\rrbracket\text{~and~}(\omega,\nu)\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rrbracket~\}

Its dual is the modality of necessity
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
, which represents the
weakest precondition
to ensure that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
is satisfied after any execution of program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
. Its formal definition was shown above in Figure
2
.

3.2
Forward Quantitative Safety

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\cup}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}?{temp_{s}>100}}\,;\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{delta:=-0.5}}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1})}

\displaystyle\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\cup}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}?{temp_{s}\leq 100}}\,;\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{delta:=1}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1})}

A quantitative variation to the Boolean notion of safety, e.g.,
\textsc{safe}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
, is
forward quantitative safety
, which provides a degree of safety by estimating the room of maneuver to ensure that the system remains in the safety region after any admissible execution. It basically estimates how strong the strongest postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}
(obtained by the execution of program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
in the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
) is
with respect to the postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
.
In other words, this degree of safety gives an indication of the margins on possible strengthening of the postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
.

Definition 2
(Forward quantitative safety)

Given a real
u\in\mathbb{R}
and formulas
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
, a hybrid program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
is forward
u
-safe for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
, denoted
{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}
, if

u=\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\nu,\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}\rrbracket)~|~\nu\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}\rrbracket\}

Given a system
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
and a precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
, the real number
u

measures the
shortest
distance between the set of states satisfying the strongest postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}
and the set of unsafe states.
If
u
is positive, then all reachable states by the system
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
from initial states satisfying the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
stay safe. The bigger
u
is, the safer the system is. Conversely, if
u
is negative, then some reachable states violate the safety condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
. If
u
is 0, then the system cannot be considered safe as its safety may depend on very small perturbations of the system’s variables
[
22
]
.

Then, we have
{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}
, where

, for
u=4

So,
u
is our “degree of safety” with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
: the system will always satisfy the postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{p}\leq 105}

Now, consider a different postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi_{post}^{\prime}}\equiv temp_{p}<=101}

{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi_{post}^{\prime}})}

\textsc{safe}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi_{post}^{\prime\prime}})
is false. This shows that when the degree of safety is 0 we cannot assess the safety of the system.

3.3
Backward Quantitative Safety

Another quantitative safety notion is
backward quantitative safety
, which estimates how strong the precondition is with respect to the required initial condition for the system to be safe.
It provides quantitative
information on how “good” (i.e., strong) the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
is with respect to the weakest precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
, while ensuring safety (i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
) after executions of the system
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
.
In other words, this degree of safety gives an indication of the margins on a possible weakening of the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
.
It is defined as the
shortest
distance from states that satisfy the precondition to any “bad” initial states that can lead the system to unsafe states.

Definition 3
(Backward quantitative safety)

Given a real
r\in\mathbb{R}
and formulas
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
, a hybrid program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
is backward
r
-safe for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
, denoted
\textsc{B-safe}_{r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
, if

r=\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}\rrbracket)~|~\omega\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}\rrbracket\}

Here, if
r
is positive then any execution of the system that starts from initial states in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
shall always stay safe. The bigger
r
is, the safer the system is. On the contrary, if
r
is negative, then some initial states in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
can lead the system’s execution to an unsafe state. Similar to the forward quantitative safety, if
r
is 0 the system cannot be considered safe.

Assuming the precondition (
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{p}=100}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{p}<=105}

\textsc{B-safe}_{r}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
, for

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{p}<=105}
.
Then
r=5
is our “degree of safety” with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
: we have a room of maneuver of
5
on the precondition to ensure
the postcondition after the execution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
.

The Boolean version of safety,

{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}

, forward quantitative safety, i.e.,
{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}

always
holds for some

, since the infimum always exists (even for unsafe systems whose

4
Quantitative Robustness

This section first introduces the
threat model
on sensor attacks and then provides two notions of robustness to measure the resilience of a CPS, in terms of forward and backward safety, respectively, with respect to a specific sensor attack.

4.1
Bounded Sensor Attacks

Existing work
[
17
]
considers a threat model of sensor attacks that the attackers can arbitrarily manipulate the sensor readings, e.g.,
compromised temperature sensor is modeled by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{temp_{s}:=*}}
.
The threat model is too coarse and strong, in particular, when the system under attacks is equipped with some sort of IDS (for instance, anomaly detection IDSs
[
2
]
) that the attacker would like to evade.

4.2
Quantitative Robustness

With the definitions of quantitative safety, we can characterize the robustness of a system against sensor attacks as the loss of safety. In particular, the robustness notions are defined by comparing the degree of safety of the original system and the system whose sensors have been compromised.
We introduce two notions of quantitative robustness, forward and backward robustness, which are built on the notions of forward and backward safety, respectively.

Forward Robustness

The first robustness notion,
forward robustness
, measures, intuitively, how much an attack affects the system’s reachable states if the system starts with the expected precondition.
Forward robustness characterizes the impact of a sensor attack as a ratio: the degree of safety of the compromised system over the degree of safety of the original system.

Definition 5
(Quantitative forward robustness)

Given a hybrid program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
, a set of sensors
S_{A}\subseteq\textsc{Var}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P})
, an offset function
o:S_{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
, real numbers
u,u_{1},\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, and properties
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
,
we say that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
is forward
\delta
-robust under
o
-bounded
S_{A}
-attacks
,
written

\textsc{F-robust}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}},S_{A},o,\delta)
, if

•

{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}
, with
u>0

•

{\textsc{F-safe}_{u_{1}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}

•

\delta=u_{1}/u
.

As expected, forward robustness applies only to systems that are safe when not exposed to sensor attacks, i.e.,
u>0
.
The value of ratio
\delta
indicates the system’s robustness under the sensor attack. Note that by Theorem
1
, we know that
u_{1}\leq u
. We can analyze
\delta
via the following cases:

•

\delta=1
: the attack doesn’t affect the system’s forward safety.

•

0<\delta<1
:
then
0<u_{1}<u
. Given initial states where the precondition holds, reachable states of both the original system and the compromised system stay safe.
The value of (
1-\delta
) quantifies the
percentage of forward safety that is lost
due to the attack. The closer
\delta
is to
1
, the more robust the system is.

•

\delta\leq 0
: then
u>0
and
u_{1}\leq 0
.
Executions of the original system stay safe, but the attack may be able to “break” the system: some of its executions under attack may run into unsafe states. The lower the value of
\delta
, the more effective the attack can be.
If
\delta=0
the attacked system can no longer be considered safe.

Consider again the cooling system described before. We already know from Example

that

5
Reasoning about Robustness with Simulation Distances

Using Definition
5
(and
6
), we can compute forward (and backward) robustness of a system in terms of the forward (and backward) safety of the system, before and after a bounded sensor attack.
However, the computation of forward and backward safety may be difficult, as they consider all admissible values to compute the infimum. This is particularly difficult for a system with compromised sensors, due to the complications caused by the offset function.

In this section, we introduce two
simulation distances
between hybrid programs, called
forward simulation distance
(or
forward distance
) and
backward simulation distance
(or
backward distance
). They quantify the behavioral distance between the original system and the compromised
one, according to a forward and backward flavor, respectively. These distances allow us to compute an upper bound on the loss of forward (and backward) safety.
The computed upper bounds are not necessarily tight bounds, but they are easier to reason with and can be verified with existing tools or via the proof systems proposed in Section
6
.

To define forward (and backward) simulation distance between two programs, we extend the notion of distance between states, i.e.,
\rho(\omega,\nu)
given in Equation
1
, to support computing distance on a set
\mathcal{H}
of variables
[
17
]
.
Intuitively, variables in
\mathcal{H}
are the ones that are relevant to the specified precondition and postcondition. And thus computing distance over these variables gives us the quantitative distance of interest. Consider the cooling system example, we are interested in the behavioral distance between the original program and the compromised one with respect to the variable
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{p}}
, rather than
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}temp_{s}}
.

We introduce a new notion of distance between states with respect to a set
\mathcal{H}
of variables, as follows:

Definition 7

For a set of variables
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{V}
, two states
\omega
and
\nu
are at

\mathcal{H}

-distance
d
, written

\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\omega,\nu)=d
, if

\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\omega(x)-\nu(x)\right)^{2}}=d
.
We write
\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}_{\scalebox{0.7}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})
to denote

\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})
where
\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\omega,\nu)
is used instead of
\rho(\omega,\nu)
.
Then,
\bm{\mathsf{depth}}_{\scalebox{0.7}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\omega,\mathcal{S})
is defined in the same manner.

The following proposition shows that computing the forward and backward safety (using
\rho(\omega,\nu)
) can be reduced to a computation using
\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\omega,\nu)
with the appropriate variable sets
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,
\textsc{Var}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}})
or
\textsc{Var}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
.

Proposition 3

For
u,u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, and formula
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
, if

•

u
=
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}(\omega,\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}\rrbracket)~|~\omega\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}\rrbracket\}

•

u_{1}
=
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}_{\scalebox{0.7}{{Var}(${\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}$)}}(\omega,\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}\rrbracket)~|~\omega\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}\rrbracket\}

•

u_{2}
=
\bm{\mathsf{inf}}\{\bm{\mathsf{Dist}}_{\scalebox{0.7}{{Var}(${\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}$)}}(\omega,\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}\rrbracket)~|~\omega\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}\rrbracket\}

Intuitively, the proposition holds because the infimum value of

is essentially decided by the distance calculated with respect to the relevant variables in

5.1

We introduce the notion of

Intuitively, programs
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{1}}

Here, for programs
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}

5.2
Backward Simulation Distance

Symmetrically, we introduce
backward simulation distance
to reason with upper bounds of loss of backward safety caused by sensor attacks. Intuitively, programs
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{2}}

d
if for the same postcondition,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{2}}
can mimic the behaviors of

d

Expressing and Verifying Simulation Distances

The content of this section is organized as follows. With the help of a use case on autonomous vehicles,
we first demonstrate how to encode both forward and backward simulation distances as

\mathcal{L}

6.1
Encoding Simulation Distances as
d
\mathcal{L}
Formulas

The forward and backward simulation distance are defined upon distance between states that respectively satisfy two formulas. For example, the forward distance (Definition
8
) is computed on states satisfying, respectively,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{1}}\rangle}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{2}}\rangle}
. Moreover, both distances are formalized in a “forall exists” manner.
Therefore, a direct way to verify them, is to compute the relevant two formulas, and then verify the distance between states that satisfy the two formulas.

Based on this insight, the following formula can be instantiated with different formulas to verify both simulation distances:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}\land(\overline{y}=\overline{x}))\rightarrow\exists\overline{x}.~({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}\land{(\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\overline{y},\overline{x})\leq d))}}

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
are formulas specifying, respectively, conditions of the compromised system and the genuine system. They share the same set of variables.
Here
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{x}}
are variables used by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{y}}
are a list of
fresh
variables whose dimension is the same as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{x}}
. Variables in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{y}}
are (implicitly) universally quantified. The fresh variables are used to store values of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{x}}
that satisfy the first formula.
The notation
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\overline{y},\overline{x})}
computes the distance between two vectors of variables with respect to the set
\mathcal{H}
:

\sqrt{\sum_{\overline{x}(i)\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\overline{x}(i)-\overline{y}(i)\right)^{2}}
, where
\overline{x}(i)
and
\overline{y}(i)
represent, respectively, the
i
th element in vector
\overline{x}
and
\overline{y}
.

The encoding can be used to verify forward distance by letting
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
, respectively, be the formula
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\rangle}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}
.

6.3
A Modality-based Approach

In the case study above, we have pre-computed the strongest postcondition and weakest precondition for the compromised system and the genuine system. However, such a computation may be difficult for certain systems. To alleviate this problem, we introduce another approach for encoding and reasoning with simulation distances using modalities.

Encoding Simulation Distances with Modalities

An alternative way to encode the two simulation distances is through modalities (c.f. Section
2.1
) which directly express program executions.
In the case of forward distance, i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\sqsubseteq^{\scriptsize\rm{F}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},\mathcal{H},d}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
, we can express it as the following
d
\mathcal{L}
formula:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}\land{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\rangle(\overline{y}=\overline{x})})}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rightarrow}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\exists\overline{x}.~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}\land{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle(\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\overline{y},\overline{x})\leq d)}})

The left-hand-side formula encodes “for each state that can be reached from precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
after an execution of the compromised program”. The fresh variables of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{y}}
are used to record the reachable states.
The right-hand-side formula encodes “there is an execution of the genuine program under precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
such that the distance between the corresponding final
states is bound by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}d}
.”

We can similarly express the backward distance

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\sqsubseteq^{\scriptsize\rm{B}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}},\mathcal{H},d}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}((\overline{y}=\overline{x})\,\land\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\rangle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\neg{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}}})}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\rightarrow}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}(\exists\overline{x}.~(\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\leq d)\,\land\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\neg{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}}})}

The left-hand-side formula encodes “for each initial state that can lead the compromised system to unsafe states”. The fresh variables of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\overline{y}}
are used to record the initial states.
The right-hand-side formula encodes “there is an initial state that can lead the genuine program to unsafe states such that the distance between the two initial states is bound by
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}d}
".

D-modality

The modality-based encodings above closely follow the definitions of forward and backward distances. However, they may be cumbersome and difficult to reason with due to the quantifiers.
To address this problem, we introduce a more efficient modality, denoted D-modality, to readily encode both forward and backward distances. We develop a proof system to reason with this new modality in an effective manner.
The proof system contains a set of proof rules which can be derived from existing
d
\mathcal{L}
axioms and proof rules
[
20
,
21
]
.

The D-modality is designed to capture the forall exists relationship between the compromised program and the genuine program. To capture such a relationship, the modality needs to refer to variables in both programs. Since the compromised and genuine programs often refer to the same set of variables, the D-modality renames variables used by the genuine program into a fresh set of variables, and then captures the relationship between the two programs using both the original and renamed variables.

To rename variables we use renaming functions.

Encoding Forward Distances using the D-modality

In the following, we show how we can use the D-modality
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o)_{\xi}}\rangle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
to encode the forward distance.
The following theorem introduces the D-modality based encoding of the forward distance and states its soundness.
Intuitively, if both genuine and compromised programs start under a precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
then
for any reachable state of the compromised program, there exists a reachable state of the genuine program satisfying
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
, which ensures an upper bound of distance between the two reachable states.
The formulas
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
satisfy certain requirements to match the definition of the forward distance. Formally speaking,

Theorem 5
(D-modality based Encoding of Forward Distance)

For a hybrid program
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
, a set of variables
S_{A}\subseteq\textsc{Var}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P})
, an offset function
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}o}
, formulas
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
, a renaming function
\xi
, a set of variables
\mathcal{H}
and
d\in\mathbb{R}
,
if we have

•

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\,\rightarrow\,}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o)_{\xi}}\rangle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}

•

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\,\rightarrow\,}\exists{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi(\textsc{Var}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}))}.\,({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}\land{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}})})

•

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\,\rightarrow\,}\rho^{\xi}_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}\leq d
, for
\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}^{{\xi}}=\sqrt{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{H}}\left(x-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi(x)}\right)^{2}}

then

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}\sqsubseteq^{\scriptsize\rm{F}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},\mathcal{H},d}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
.

Let us go in some detail of the three requirements of the theorem.
Here, the formula
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
captures the relationship between the initial states of the two programs
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P})}
.
The first requirement expresses the following: if an execution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\textsc{attacked}(}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},S_{A},o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0})}
starting from an initial state satisfying
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}

reaches a state where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
holds, then there exists an execution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P})}
starting from the same condition that reaches a state where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
holds as well.
The second requirement says that each state in
\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}\rrbracket
is in relation with at least one state in
\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}})}\rrbracket
under the condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\psi}
.
The first two requirements together capture the description “for each state
\nu_{1}\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{1}}\rangle}\rrbracket

\nu_{2}\in\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P_{2}}\rangle}\rrbracket

The third requirement specifies that the postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}
ensures an upper bound of distance between reachable states of the two programs. It matches the description “
\rho_{\scalebox{0.5}{$\mathcal{H}$}}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\leq d
” in the definition of the forward distance.

The detailed proof of this theorem can be found in

Intuitively, if both genuine and compromised programs start from a precondition

8
Related Work

Robustness of CPSs

Our work is a quantitative generalization of Xiang et at.
[
17
]
, in the setting of hybrid programs and
d
\mathcal{L}
.
In that paper, the authors propose two notions of robustness for CPSs:
robustness of safety
, when (unbound) sensor attacks are unable to affect the system under attack, and
robustness of high-integrity state
, when high-integrity parts of the system cannot be compromised. In the current paper, we generalize the first of the two relations.

Fränzle et al.
[
28
]
classify the notions of robustness for CPSs as follows:
(i) input/output robustness;
(ii) robustness with respect to system parameters;
(iii) robustness in real-time system implementation;
(iv) robustness due to unpredictable environment;
(v) robustness to faults.
The notion of robustness considered in this paper falls in category (iv), where the attacks are the source of environment’s unpredictability.
Other works study robustness properties for CPSs
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
. Some of them focus on robustness against attacks
[
29
,
30
]
, even adopting quantitative reasonings
[
31
,
32
]
.

Our notion of forward robustness shares similarities with some existing notions of robustness, such as invariance
[
33
]
and input-to-state stability
[
34
]
. These notions concern if a system stays in a safe region when small changes happen to initial conditions, while forward robustness concerns if a system stays in a safe region when under attack. Although it might be possible to reformulate existing notions of robustness to characterize our forward robustness, our formulation focuses on modeling attacks which makes it easier to analyze their impact.

The current work extends and generalizes the paper by Chong et al.
[
1
]
.
In particular, in Section
6
, we first demonstrate how it is possible to encode both forward and backward simulation distances via modalities.
Then, we propose a new modality, called D-modality, to encode both forward and backward distances (Theorem
5
and Theorem
6
). Finally, we
define a proof system to help reasoning on D-modality and prove its soundness (Proposition
5
).
The proof system has been applied in a new case study on the field of water tank (Section
7
). Last but not least, unlike the conference version
[
1
]
,
the current paper provides in the appendix the full formal proofs of all results.

Signal Temporal Logic
(STL)
[
35
]
is a specification formalism for expressing real-time temporal
safety
and performance properties, such as
robustness
, of CPSs.
Ferrère et al.
[
36
]
study a quantitative extension of STL that classifies signals as inputs and outputs to specify the system-under-test as an input/output relation instead of a set of correct execution traces. The idea behind their approach is quite similar to that followed in our forward robustness, as they express families of admissible patterns of both the model inputs and the model preconditions that guarantee the desired behavior of the model output. Mohammadinejad et al.
[
37
]
adopt a dual approach, similar to that followed in our backward robustness. Given an output requirement they propose an algorithm to mine an environment assumption, consisting of a large subset of input signals for which the corresponding output signals satisfy the output requirement.

Formal Analysis of Sensor Attacks

Lanotte et al.
[
38
,
3
,
39
]
propose process-calculus approaches to model and analyze the impact of
physics-based attacks, as sensor attacks in CPSs. Their threat models consider attacks that may manipulate both sensor readings and control commands. Their model of physics is discrete and they focus on crucial timing aspects of attacks, such as beginning and duration. Bernardeschi et al.
[
40
]
introduce a framework to analyze the effects of attacks on sensors and actuators. Controllers of systems are specified using the formalism PVS
[
41
]
. The physics is described by other modeling tools.
Their threat model is similar to ours: the effect of an attack is a set of assignments to the variables defined in the controller. Simulation is used to analyze the effects of attacks.
Huang et al.
[
30
]
proposed a risk assessment method that uses a Bayesian network to model the attack propagation process and infers the probabilities of sensors and actuators to be compromised. These probabilities are fed into a stochastic hybrid system model to predict the evolution of the physical process. Then, the security risk is quantified by evaluating the system availability with the model.

9
Conclusion

A formal framework for
quantitative
analysis of bounded sensor attacks on CPSs is introduced.
Given a precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
and postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
of a system
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
, we formalize two safety notions,
quantitative forward safety
,
{\textsc{F-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})}
, and
quantitative backward safety
,
\textsc{B-safe}_{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}})
, where
u\in\mathbb{R}
respectively express: (1) how strong the strongest postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\langle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}\rangle}
is with respect to the postcondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
,
and (2) how strong the precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}}
is with respect to the weakest precondition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}[}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}]}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
. The bigger
u
is, the safer the system is. On the contrary, if
u
is negative, then some reachable states violate the safety condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}}
. If
u
is 0, then the system cannot be considered safe.
We introduce
forward and backward robustness
,
\textsc{F-robust}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}},S_{A},o,\delta)
and
\textsc{B-robust}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{pre}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\phi}_{post}},S_{A},o,\delta)
respectively, to quantify the robustness
\delta
, with
\delta\leq 1
, for a system
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}P}
against
bounded sensor attacks
, as the ratio between the safety of the attacked system and the degree of safety of the original system; here, the value of (
1-\delta
) quantifies the
percentage of safety that is lost
due to the attack. The closer
\delta
is to
1
, the more robust the system is. To reason about the notions of robustness, we introduce two simulation distances,
forward and backward simulation distance
, defined based on the behavior distances between the original system and the compromised system, to characterize upper bounds of the degree of forward and backward safety loss caused by the sensor attacks.
To reason with forward and backward simulation distances, we propose a new
modality
and a
proof system
to reason with the modality. The proposed modality can be used to encode both forward and backward simulation distances in an intuitive and concise way.
We formally prove the soundness of the encodings and its associated proof system.
We showcase our formal notions on two non-trivial examples: an autonomous vehicle that needs to avoid collision and a water tank system.

Future work

As observed in
[
42
,
38
,
3
]
,
timing
is a critical issue when attacking CPSs. We aim at generalizing our threat model to deal with more sophisticated time-sensitive sensor attacks, where the attacker may specify (possibly periodic) attack windows in which offsets might be potentially different in each window, depending on the system state. This might be necessary to implement stealthy attacks working around adaptive IDSs.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}x}{{}^{\prime}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\theta}}}
, a solution of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\xi({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}x}{{}^{\prime}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\theta}})}
can reach a state so

holds, by evolving for the same duration as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}x}{{}^{\prime}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\theta}}}
. The evolution constraint
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t\leq\epsilon}
won’t affect as both dynamics evolve for the same duration.
