Martingale deep neural network for very high-dimensional stochastic optimal controls Thanks: Date: September 23, 2026 (First version available on arXiv:2408.14395 as preprint on Aug. 26, 2024).
Wei Cai, Shuixin Fang, Wenzhong Zhang, Tao Zhou

Abstract
We propose a martingale deep learning method for very high-dimensional stochastic optimal control problems (SOCPs) via their associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations and the verification theory for the optimal control. The method decomposes the problem into two coupled components: a martingale formulation for the value function and an integral optimality condition for the feedback control. For the value function, we extend DeepMartNet by introducing a pilot process for state-space exploration and system processes for martingale property enforcement. This yields a derivative-free formulation that avoids computing PDE solution’s derivatives and online simulations of full trajectories, while enabling parallel loss evaluation in both time and space. Also, the martingale condition is imposed in a weak Galerkin form realized with adversarial learning, which avoids direct computation of conditional expectations. The control is learned by minimizing an integral residual of the optimality condition over the explored state space, thereby avoiding state-space point-wise optimization over the control set. Numerical results demonstrate accurate and efficient performance for SOCPs with complex patterns in value function and controls in dimensions up to 10,000.



keywords

Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, stochastic optimal control, deep learning, adversarial networks, martingale formulation.

Introduction

Stochastic optimal control problems (SOCPs) concern the control of dynamical systems subject to random perturbations, with the goal of optimizing a prescribed performance criterion
[
53
]
.
These problems arise widely in decision-making and systems under uncertainty, with applications in engineering, economics, biology, and rare event simulation
[
22
,
61
]
; see, e.g.,
[
69
,
46
,
67
]
.
Their analysis and numerical solution have long been central topics in applied mathematics.

The theory of SOCPs has evolved along two pioneering and complementary directions:
Pontryagin’s maximum principle
[
12
,
41
,
11
,
8
,
52
]
, which provides necessary conditions for optimal controls via adjoint processes
[
66
, p. 118]
, and Bellman’s dynamic programming
[
5
,
6
,
9
,
7
]
, which characterizes optimal controls through stochastic verification theorems
[
78
]
and reduces the problem to solving the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation
[
66
, p. 163]
.
After decades of development, these two perspectives were connected with the theory of backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs),
a concept originally introduced by Bismut
[
10
]
as adjoint equations in the stochastic maximum principle (SMP), and later established as a systematic theory by Pardoux and Peng
[
50
]
.
Nowadays, the theory of SOCPs continues to be an active area of research, with ongoing advances in analysis, probability, and in more general problem settings
[
51
,
13
,
63
,
19
,
31
,
38
]
.

Numerical methods for SOCPs play a central role in bridging theoretical developments and practical applications.
However, designing efficient solvers remains highly challenging in high-dimensional settings, which frequently arise in applications involving large state and control spaces.
It is well-known that conventional numerical methods
[
59
,
49
,
56
,
3
,
14
,
60
,
72
,
74
,
73
,
25
,
24
,
64
]
suffer from the curse of dimensionality (CoD)
[
6
]
: their computational complexity grows exponentially with the problem dimension, making them impractical for high-dimensional SOCPs.

In recent years, the incorporation of deep learning into numerical methods has led to substantial progress in tackling the CoD, yielding practical solvers for high-dimensional SOCPs.
Existing deep learning methods for SOCPs can be broadly categorized as follows:
(a) SMP approach: the SMP characterizes the optimal control through a coupled forward-backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs) system together with a maximum condition
[
66
, p. 118]
, and this system is then solved by deep learning; see, e.g.,
[
15
,
36
,
34
,
37
]
.
(b) PDE approach: the dynamic programming principle reduces the SOCP to solving the associated HJB equation
[
66
, p. 163]
, which is then tackled by deep-learning-based PDE solvers, e.g.,
[
54
,
26
,
4
,
62
,
21
,
20
,
1
,
57
]
.
(c) Hybrid approach: SMP- and PDE-based approaches are combined to improve state-space exploration and learning efficiency; see, e.g.,
[
44
,
48
]
.
(d) Reinforcement learning approach: the SOCP is reformulated as a Markov decision process and solved using policy-iteration methods; see, e.g.,
[
75
,
39
,
40
,
35
,
2
,
76
,
77
]
.

Despite the notable success of deep learning approach, existing methods still face substantial challenges in very high-dimensional settings, for example when the state dimension
d
or control dimension
m
is in the order of thousands or more.
These challenges arise mainly from the following three sources:
(i) HJB equations involve Hessian matrices with
d\times d
entries, and PDE- and hybrid-based approaches typically evaluate these Hessians through automatic differentiation of deep neural networks, which become prohibitively costly in computation and memory as the dimension increases.
This bottleneck has motivated a line of recent works on high-dimensional PDEs
[
30
,
33
,
32
,
58
,
43
,
42
,
70
]
.
(ii) SMP- and reinforcement-learning-based approaches avoid explicit Hessian evaluation through reformulation, but they require simulation of sample paths of the state process.
These simulations are sequential in time and must be repeatedly updated as the control changes during training, which substantially limits the parallelizability of the algorithms.
(iii) Both the SMP and HJB formulations involve an embedded optimization problem for the optimal control, whose minimizer generally does not admit a closed-form expression.
As a result, pointwise optimization is required at every time-space point, which further exacerbates the CoD problem.

In this work, we develop a new deep learning method for very high-dimensional SOCPs through their associated HJB equations and the
verification theory for the optimal control.
Our method aims to overcome the above-mentioned difficulties by combining the following ideas:

1.

Following the approach of reinforcement learning, we split the HJB equation into two coupled subproblems: one for the value function and one for the optimal control.
For the value-function subproblem, we extend DeepMartNet
[
17
,
18
]
to reformulate the equation as a martingale problem, therefore avoiding all derivative computations in the original PDE.

2.

To improve the parallelizability of the martingale formulation, we introduce two types of stochastic processes: a pilot process to explore the state space and system processes to encode the martingale property of the value-function subproblem.
The pilot process is decoupled from training and can be generated offline.
The system processes can be simulated independently at different time steps, enabling mini-batch sampling and parallel loss evaluation in both time and space.

3.

Following the idea of SOC-MartNet
[
15
]
, we enforce the martingale formulation with a Galerkin method, which is realized with adversarial learning techniques
[
68
]
.
This avoids computation of conditional expectations in the martingale formulation, and further improves the efficiency of the method.

4.

For the optimal-control subproblem, we introduce a neural network to learn the optimal control.
Its loss is designed to minimize the integrated residual of the optimality condition over the state space explored by the pilot process.
This approach eliminates the need for pointwise optimization at every time-space point.

Combining these ideas yields a martingale deep learning method for SOCPs and HJB equations.
Numerical results show that the proposed method efficiently solves problems, including cases in which the optimal control does not admit an explicit expression, in dimensions up to
10^{4}
.

Section

Section
4

Some concluding remarks are given in Section

.

2
Problem setup and preliminary

2.1
Problem setup

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space equipped with a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
generated by the standard Brownian motion
B:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where
T>0
is a determined terminal time.
The state equation is given by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mu\big(s,X_{s},u(s,X_{s})\big)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma\big(s,X_{s},u(s,X_{s})\big)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T],

(1)

where
X_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable with a prescribed distribution;
the stochastic integral with respect to
B_{s}
is understood in the Itô sense;

\mu:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
are given functions representing the drift and diffusion coefficients, respectively;

U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given control domain;

X:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called the state process corresponding to the state-feedback control
u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to U
, which belongs to the admissible set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
defined by

\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}:=\left\{u:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to U:u\text{ is Borel measurable}\right\}.

(2)

Here the Borel measurability of
u
is required to ensure the validity of the relevant stochastic integrals and expectations in the subsequent analysis.
To avoid technicalities, we assume that for any
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
, the SDE (
1
) admits a unique strong solution
X
.
Sufficient conditions for this assumption can be found in, e.g.,
[
66
, Chapter 1, Theorem 6.17]
.

The performance of a control
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
is measured by the cost functional

J(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}c\big(s,X_{s},u(s,X_{s})\big)\,\mathrm{d}s+g(X_{T})\right],

(3)

where
c:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are given functions representing the running cost and the terminal cost, respectively.
The SOCP considered by this paper is to find an optimal control
u^{*}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
minimizing the cost functional, i.e.,

J(u^{*})=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}J(u).

(4)

2.2
Dynamic programming principle

To lay the theoretical foundation for our method, we briefly review the dynamic programming principle in stochastic control; see, e.g.,
[
66
, Chapter 4]
and
[
53
, Chapter 3]
for comprehensive treatments.

The dynamic programming principle is based on the value function defined by

v(t,x):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{t}^{T}c\big(s,X_{s},u(s,X_{s})\big)\,\mathrm{d}s+g(X_{T})\;\Big|\;X_{t}=x\right]

(5)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where
X
is the state process governed by (
1
) under control
u
.
By
[
66
, p. 182, Proposition 3.5; p. 190, Theorem 5.2]
, the value function
v
is the classical or viscosity solution to the following HJB equation:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\inf_{\kappa\in U}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}v(t,x)+c(t,x,\kappa)\right\}=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&v(T,x)=g(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}
is a differential operator defined by

\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}:=\mu^{\top}(t,x,\kappa)\partial_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}[\sigma\sigma^{\top}\left(t,x,\kappa\right)\partial_{xx}^{2}],\quad(t,x,\kappa)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U.

(7)

The operators
\partial_{x}:=\nabla_{x}
and
\partial_{xx}^{2}:=\nabla_{x}\nabla_{x}^{\top}
denote the gradient and Hessian, respectively, and
\operatorname{Tr}[\cdot]
denotes the trace of a matrix.

Under the regularity condition
v\in C^{1,2}
, i.e.,
v(t,x)
is once continuously differentiable in
t\in[0,T)
and twice continuously differentiable in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the verification theorem
[
66
, Chapter 5, Theorem 5.1]
implies that the optimal control
u^{*}
can be given by the following feedback form:

u^{*}(t,x)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}v(t,x)+c\big(t,x,\kappa\big)\right\},\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(8)

Thus, the SOCP (
4
) reduces to solving the HJB equation (
6
) for
v
and then computing
u^{*}
from (
8
).
This paper aims to develop an efficient implementation of this procedure for high-dimensional SOCPs.

3
Martingale deep learning

To proceed, we substitute (
8
) into (
6
), reformulating the HJB equation as the following two-step procedure for determining the value function
v
and the optimal control
u
:

\displaystyle\;\;\text{find}\;\;v\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle\bigl(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u}\bigr)v(t,x)+c\big(t,x,u(t,x)\big)=0,

(9)

\displaystyle\;\;\text{find}\;\;u\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}\left\{\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}v(t,x)+c\big(t,x,\kappa\big)\right\},

(10)

where
(t,x)
ranges over
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
;
v
satisfies the terminal condition in (
6
) a priori;
\mathcal{L}^{u}
is defined by (
7
) with
\kappa=u(t,x)
.

In the remainder of this section, we develop a deep learning method to solve
(u,v)
simultaneously from (
9
) and (
10
).
The primary difficulty is to evaluate the derivatives in
\mathcal{L}^{\kappa}v
, which involves the Hessian
\partial_{xx}^{2}v
with
d\times d
entries.
A second difficulty is the minimization in (
10
), which generally lacks a closed-form minimizer; a direct approach requires pointwise optimization at every
(t,x)
.
These two difficulties incur substantial computational costs in high dimensions, constituting a primary source of the CoD.
We address them with a martingale approach that is derivative-free, highly parallel, and free of pointwise minimization.

3.1
Stochastic processes for martingale formulation

Let
\pi_{N}
be a uniform time partition of
[0,T]
defined by

\pi_{N}:0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T,\quad t_{n}:=n\Delta t,\quad\Delta t:=T/N

(11)

with
N
the number of time steps.
Define the Brownian increments by

\Delta B_{n+1}:=B_{t_{n+1}}-B_{t_{n}},\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

where the subscript
n+1
in
\Delta B_{n+1}
is used to indicate that it is
\mathcal{F}_{t_{n+1}}
-measurable.

Let
u_{\alpha}
and
v_{\theta}
denote neural networks parameterized by
\alpha
and
\theta
, respectively, used to approximate the optimal control
u
and the value function
v
as defined in (
9
) and (
10
).
The following two types of stochastic processes are the key ingredients for our method:

1.

(Pilot process)

Let
\hat{X}:\pi_{N}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a discrete pilot process (indicated by
\hat{}
) used to explore the state space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, generated by the following Euler–Maruyama scheme:

\hat{X}_{n+1}:=\hat{X}_{n}+\hat{\mu}_{n}\Delta t+\hat{\sigma}_{n}\Delta B_{n+1},\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(12)

where
\hat{\mu}_{n}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{n}
are the drift and diffusion coefficients evaluated with a pilot control
\hat{u}
in place of
u
, i.e.,

\hat{\mu}_{n}:=\mu\big(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},\hat{u}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\big),\quad\hat{\sigma}_{n}:=\sigma\big(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},\hat{u}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\big).

(13)

For parallelization purposes, the pilot path
(\hat{X}_{n})_{n=0}^{N}
need only be updated occasionally during training, with
\hat{u}
taken as a frozen snapshot of
u_{\alpha}
.

2.

(System process)

For
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, define the system process as a random jump from
\hat{X}_{n}
to a new location
X_{n+1}
at time
t_{n+1}
by

X_{n+1}:=\hat{X}_{n}+\mu_{n}\Delta t+\sigma_{n}\Delta B_{n+1},\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(14)

where
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
are the drift and diffusion coefficients evaluated using the current control network
u_{\alpha}
, i.e.,

\mu_{n}:=\mu\big(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},u_{\alpha}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\big),\quad\sigma_{n}:=\sigma\big(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},u_{\alpha}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\big).

(15)

The process
(X_{n+1})_{n=0}^{N-1}
encodes the dynamics of
\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}
, which will be used to derive a martingale formulation for (
9
) in
section

3.2
.

The pilot and system processes have distinct computational features that align with their roles in the method.
The pilot process
\hat{X}
must be simulated sequentially in time; however, since its main purpose is to explore the state space, it can be generated offline and updated only occasionally during training.
By contrast, the system process
X
depends on the current control network
u_{\alpha}
and therefore must be updated at every training iteration to reflect the dynamics of
\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}
.
Nevertheless, once the pilot path is generated, the system paths
(X_{n+1})_{n=0}^{N-1}
can be computed independently across all time steps, which enables parallelization in the temporal direction.

Figure

1
.

Remark 1
(Controlled SDEs for space exploration).

The pilot process
\hat{X}
in (
12
) explores the state space by mimicking the controlled dynamics in (
1
).
This strategy helps to focus the training on regions most relevant to the control problem.
As shown in
[
48
,
44
]
, such an exploration strategy can significantly improve the performance of SOCP solvers, especially for problems with complex dynamics.

3.2
Martingale formulation for value function

We begin by deriving a martingale formulation for the value-function subproblem (
9
).
The main idea is to parallelize the original DeepMartNet
[
17
,
18
]
using the pilot and system processes introduced above.

The random jump from
\hat{X}_{n}
to
X_{n+1}
in (
14
) can be interpreted as the evolution of the process
\hat{X}_{n,t}:=\hat{X}_{n}+\mu_{n}(t-t_{n})+\sigma_{n}(B_{t}-B_{t_{n}})
as
t
increases from
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
.
This evolution admits an infinitesimal generator
\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}
, where the coefficients in
\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}
are evaluated at
(t,x)=(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})
and held fixed during the evolution from
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
.
Then the Dynkin formula
[
79
, Theorem 7.4.1]
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[v_{\theta}(t_{n+1},X_{n+1})\,\big|\,\hat{X}_{n}]-v_{\theta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}}\big(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}\big|_{(t,x)=(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})}\big)v_{\theta}(s,\hat{X}_{n,s})\,\mathrm{d}s\,\Big|\,\hat{X}_{n}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}})v_{\theta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

where the remainder term is valid under the regularity condition
v_{\theta}\in C^{1,2}
.

To relate the above formula to the HJB equation (
9
), we add the running cost term
c(t,x,u_{\alpha}(t,x))\Delta t
, evaluated at
(t,x)=(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})
, to both sides, which yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1};u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})\,\big|\,\hat{X}_{n}\big]

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\{(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}})v_{\theta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})+c\big(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},u_{\alpha}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\big)\big\}\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}),

where
\mathcal{M}
is a deterministic function defined by

\mathcal{M}\left(t,x,x^{\prime};u,v\right):=v\left(t+\Delta t,x^{\prime}\right)-v(t,x)+c(t,x,u(t,x))\Delta t

(18)

for
(t,x,x^{\prime})\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Neglecting the remainder term
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
, the second line represents exactly the residual of (
9
) evaluated at
(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})
.
Therefore, the term in the first line should vanish, which leads to the following martingale formulation for (
9
): train
v_{\theta}
to satisfy

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1};u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})\,\big|\,\hat{X}_{n}\big]=0,\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

(19)

where
X_{n+1}
is defined in (
14
).

The formulation in (
19
) is an alternative characterization of the PDE (
9
), which does not involve any derivatives of
v_{\theta}
.
However, a direct enforcement of (
19
) with
v_{\theta}
requires a two-level Monte Carlo procedure:
first sampling the pilot path
\hat{X}
, and then, for each realized
\hat{X}_{n}
, approximating the conditional expectation in (
19
) by generating multiple samples of
X_{n+1}
conditioned on
\hat{X}_{n}
.
This nested Monte Carlo approach is computationally intensive.

To address this issue, we adopt the idea in
[
68
,
15
]
and enforce (
19
) in a weak sense using a Galerkin method.
Let
\rho_{\eta}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
be a neural network parameterized by
\eta
, which serves as a test function in the Galerkin method.
Then (
19
) can be enforced in the weak sense by requiring
v_{\theta}
to satisfy

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1};u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})\rho_{\eta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\right]=0\;\;\text{for any}\;\;\eta,

where the probability measure in the expectation serves as the weight for the Galerkin method.
The above condition can be equivalently written as the following minimax problem:

\min_{\theta}\max_{\eta}\big|L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta})\big|^{2},

(20)

where the loss function
L
is defined by

L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta}):=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1};u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})\rho_{\eta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})\right].

(21)

Here
\mathcal{M}
is given by (
18
);
\hat{X}_{n}
and
X_{n+1}
given by (
12
) and (
14
), respectively.
The minimax problem in (
20
) can be solved via the adversarial training procedure proposed in
[
68
]
; see
section

3.4
for details.

Remark 2
(Efficiency gain from weak formulation).

Compared with enforcing (
19
) directly, which would require a two-level Monte Carlo procedure,
the weak formulation (
20
) reduces the loss evaluation in (
21
) to a single-level procedure.
Specifically, since the expectation in (
21
) is taken with respect to the joint distribution of
(\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1})
, it can be approximated by generating independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) pairs of
(\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1})
, instead of generating multiple samples of
X_{n+1}
for each realized
\hat{X}_{n}
.
This substantially lowers the computational cost.
A mini-batch version of (
21
) in such a single-level manner is given in
eq.

32
for practical implementation.

Remark 3
(Trick of vector-valued test function).

Recall that
\rho_{\eta}
is constructed as an
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued test function.
Accordingly, the loss
L
defined in (
21
) is also
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-valued, and
|\cdot|
in (
20
) denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
Compared with the scalar-valued case
r=1
, choosing vector-valued test functions, e.g.,
r\geq 600
, substantially improves the stability of the adversarial training for (
20
), as evidenced in the numerical results in
[
15
,
16
]
.
A specific structure of
\rho_{\eta}
is given in (
28
).

Remark 4
(Connection to derivative-free methods for PDEs).

The equality in (
16
) is a key step of the proposed method, as it eliminates the differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}
through a martingale differencing argument.
Similar ideas have also been explored in
[
29
,
65
,
16
]
for solving high-dimensional parabolic PDEs.

3.3
Integral formulation for feedback control

We next derive an integral form of (
10
) for learning the optimal feedback control
u
.
The main idea is to use (
17
) to remove the differential operator
\mathcal{L}^{u}
and transform the pointwise minimization in (
10
) into an integral minimization over the state explored by
\hat{X}
.

Note that (
10
) can be equivalently written as

u(t,x)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}\left\{(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{\kappa})v(t,x)+c\left(t,x,\kappa\right)\right\},

(22)

since the term
\partial_{t}v(t,x)
is independent of
\kappa
and thus does not affect the minimizer
; that is, we seek an optimal feedback control
u
under the current value function
v
.
With
P_{\hat{X}_{n}}(\cdot)
denoting the probability density function of
\hat{X}_{n}
, the minimization condition (
22
) implies

\displaystyle u=

\displaystyle\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{\bar{u}})v(t_{n},x)+c(t_{n},x,\bar{u}(t_{n},x))\right\}P_{\hat{X}_{n}}(x)\Delta t\,\mathrm{d}x

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{\bar{u}})v(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})+c(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},\bar{u}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n}))\right]\Delta t,

where the second line is obtained by expressing the integral in the first line as an expectation with respect to
\hat{X}_{n}
.

By the second line of (
23
) with
(u,v)
replaced by the neural networks
(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})
,
and then using (
17
) to express the residual term in the second line of (
23
),
we obtain the following integral formulation for learning the optimal feedback control:

u_{\alpha}=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1};\bar{u},v_{\theta})\right],

(24)

where we have neglected the remainder term
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
in (
17
).
Using the loss function
L
defined in (
21
), and constructing
u_{\alpha}
so that its values lie in
U
as described in (
26
), the minimization problem (
24
) can be solved by training
u_{\alpha}
via

\min_{\alpha}L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},1),

(25)

where
L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},1)
is given by (
21
) with
\rho_{\eta}
replaced by the constant function equal to
1
.
As shown in (
25
), the integral minimization in (
24
) eliminates the time-state space point-wise minimization in (
22
) and reduces the problem to a standard neural network training task.

By alternating the training of
v_{\theta}
via (
20
) and
u_{\alpha}
via (
25
), we obtain a unified martingale formulation for solving the SOCP (
4
).
This formulation is simple, since both subproblems are based on the same loss function
L
in (
21
), differing only in the choice of test function.
This unified structure simplifies implementation and improves computational efficiency by allowing intermediate quantities to be shared across the two subproblems.

Remark 5
(Sufficiency of (
25
) for optimal feedback control).

While (
23
) is derived as a necessary condition for (
22
), it also implies the pointwise optimality in sense of

u(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\kappa\in U}\big\{(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{\kappa})v(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n})+c(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n},\kappa)\big\},\quad n=0,1,\cdots,N-1,

almost surely.
This guarantees that
u
acts as the optimal feedback control in the time-space regions explored by
\hat{X}
with positive probability.
Consequently, by neglecting the
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2})
remainder in (
17
),
the minimization problem in (
25
) serves as a sufficient approximation of (
23
) for learning the optimal control over the state space explored by
\hat{X}
, provided
\Delta t
is sufficiently small.

3.4
Network architecture

Now we specify the architectures of the networks
u_{\alpha}
,
v_{\theta}
, and
\rho_{\eta}
used in the training procedures (
20
) and (
25
).
To avoid technical complications, we assume that
u_{\alpha}
is constructed such that its output always lies within the control domain
U
.
This can be achieved by applying an appropriate output activation function to enforce the range, or, if
U
is convex, by projecting the output of a neural network onto
U
:

u_{\alpha}(t,x)=\mathop{\arg\min}_{y\in U}\big|\psi_{\alpha}(t,x)-y\big|,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(26)

where
\psi_{\alpha}
is a neural network with parameters
\alpha
.
For regular
U
, such as box constraints of the form
U=\prod_{i=1}^{m}[a_{i},b_{i}]
with
-\infty<a_{i}\leq b_{i}<+\infty
, this projection can be computed efficiently.

For the network
v_{\theta}
, we assume that the terminal condition in (
6
) is satisfied by construction.
This can be achieved by embedding the terminal condition
v(T,x)=g(x)
directly into the architecture of
v_{\theta}
, e.g.,

v_{\theta}(t,x)=\begin{cases}g(x),&\text{if }t=T,\\
\phi_{\theta}(t,x),&\text{if }t<T\end{cases}

(27)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\phi_{\theta}:[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a neural network parameterized by
\theta
.
The discontinuity of
v_{\theta}
at
t=T
does not cause any issues in training,
since the network is only evaluated at the discrete time steps in
\pi_{N}
.

The adversarial network
\rho_{\eta}
in (
21
) plays the role of test functions in classical Galerkin methods for solving PDEs.
By our experimental results,
\rho_{\eta}
is not necessarily very deep; instead, it can be a shallow network with enough output dimensionality.
Following the multiscale neural network ideas in
[
45
]
,
we consider a typical
\rho_{\eta}
given by

\rho_{\eta}(t,x)=\sin\left(\Lambda\left(W_{1}t+W_{2}x+b\right)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{r},

(28)

where
\eta:=(W_{1},W_{2},b)\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the trainable parameter;
\Lambda(\cdot)
is a scale layer defined by

\Lambda(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{r})=\left(c_{1}y_{1},c_{2}y_{2},\cdots,c_{r}y_{r}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\quad c_{i}:=1+\frac{i-1}{r-1}(c-1)

(29)

for
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, where
c>0
is a non-trainable hyperparameter;

\sin(\cdot)
is the activation function applied to the outputs of
\Lambda
in an element-wise manner.

3.5
Learning algorithm

The learning algorithm is obtained by approximating the objectives in (
20
) and (
25
) with mini-batch estimates of the expectation in (
21
).
Specifically, for each
n=0,1,\cdots,N-1
, let
\{(\hat{X}_{n}^{m},X_{n+1}^{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
be i.i.d. samples of
(\hat{X}_{n},X_{n+1})
generated by (
12
) and (
14
).
Then, by the definition of
L
in (
21
), the objectives in (
20
) and (
25
) can be approximated by the following unbiased mini-batch estimators:

\displaystyle\left|L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta})\right|^{2}\approx\;

\displaystyle L^{\top}(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta};\mathbb{A}_{1})L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta};\mathbb{A}_{2}),

(30)

\displaystyle L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta})\approx\;

\displaystyle L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta};\mathbb{A}_{1}\cup\mathbb{A}_{2}),

(31)

\displaystyle L(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta},\rho_{\eta};\mathbb{A}_{i}):=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left|\mathbb{A}_{i}\right|}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{A}_{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{M}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n}^{m},X_{n+1}^{m};u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})\rho_{\eta}(t_{n},\hat{X}_{n}^{m}),

(32)

where
\left|\mathbb{A}_{i}\right|
denotes the cardinality of
the index sets
\mathbb{A}_{i}
for
i=1,2
, sampled from
\{1,2,\cdots,M\}
, such that
\mathbb{A}_{1}\cap\mathbb{A}_{2}=\emptyset
.
The disjointness of
\mathbb{A}_{1}
and
\mathbb{A}_{2}
ensures that the mini-batch estimator in (
30
) is unbiased.

In summary, to solve
(u,v)
from (
9
) and (
10
), we train
(u_{\alpha},v_{\theta})
by enforcing (
20
) and (
25
).
Specifically, we update
v_{\theta}
and
u_{\alpha}
by gradient descent to minimize the objectives in (
20
) and (
25
), respectively, and update the test network
\rho_{\eta}
by gradient ascent to maximize (
20
).
In implementation, the objectives and their stochastic gradients are approximated using the mini-batch estimators in (
30
)–(
31
).
For efficiency, the pilot paths
\hat{X}_{n}^{m}
are updated only occasionally during training.

Algorithm

1
summarizes the complete training procedure.

Remark 6
(Extension to quasilinear parabolic PDEs).

Observe that the subproblem (
9
) reduces to a quasilinear parabolic PDE of
v
when the control
u
is replaced by the solution
v
itself; namely,

\bigl(\partial_{t}+\mathcal{L}^{v}\bigr)v(t,x)+c\big(t,x,v(t,x)\big)=0,\quad(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(33)

with terminal condition the same as in (
6
).
This observation shows that
Algorithm

1
extends naturally to the quasilinear parabolic PDE (
33
).
In practice, one removes Line
9
and replaces each remaining occurrence of
u_{\alpha}
with
v_{\theta}
.
The resulting output
v_{\theta}
then provides an approximation of the solution
v
to (
33
).

3.6
Discussions on advantages

The martingale deep learning method presented in
Algorithm

1
builds upon our earlier work on DeepMartNet
[
17
,
18
]
and SOC-MartNet
[
15
]
, developed for solving PDEs and SOCPs, respectively.
The differences between the proposed method and existing works are summarized as follows:

•

Compared with DeepMartNet
[
17
,
18
]
and
other SDE-based methods such as
[
36
,
34
,
37
,
75
,
39
,
40
,
35
,
2
,
76
,
77
]
, the proposed method avoids simulating full trajectories as explained in
Figure

1
(a).
In those existing methods, path simulation is sequential in time.
Moreover, because the simulated paths depend on the control neural networks being trained, they must be regenerated whenever the parameters of the networks are updated.
This online and temporally sequential path simulation substantially limits the computational efficiency, thus limiting the capability of these methods for very high-dimensional problems.

•

Compared with the original SOC-MartNet
[
15
]
, the proposed method features a totally derivative-free formulation.
Although SOC-MartNet already avoids online path simulation, it still depends on automatic differentiation to evaluate derivatives of
v_{\theta}
.
The proposed method in this paper introduces the random jumps
\hat{X}_{n}\mapsto X_{n+1}
in (
14
) to represent the dynamics of
\mathcal{L}^{u_{\alpha}}v_{\theta}
, thereby eliminating the need for explicit derivative evaluations.

•

Compared with PDE solvers, e.g.,
[
54
,
4
,
62
,
23
,
26
,
27
,
55
,
71
,
47
,
28
]
, applied directly to HJB equations,
our method avoids time and state-space pointwise minimization over the control variable when learning the optimal feedback control.
Instead, the control is learned via the integral formulation in (
24
), similar to standard neural network training by loss minimization. This avoids the complex optimization steps required in SMP and HJB formulations.

In summary, the proposed method is derivative-free, avoids sequential path simulation, and eliminates time and state-space pointwise minimization.
These features yield substantial improvements in computational efficiency, especially for very high-dimensional problems, as demonstrated in
Section

4
.

4.5
HJB equation without explicit
\boldsymbol{\inf_{u}H}

Now we consider a SOCP whose complexity arises in the Hamiltonian, for which the associated HJB equation reads:

\partial_{t}v+\inf_{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{(b+\kappa)^{\top}\partial_{x}v+\frac{1}{2}\left|\kappa\right|^{2}+\epsilon\sin(\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}\kappa)\right\}+\frac{1}{8}\Delta_{x}v=0

(45)

for
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\epsilon
is a scalar parameter specified later, and
b
is a
\mathbb{R}^{d}

given by

b_{i}=\sin(t+i+x_{i+1}-1),\quad x_{d+1}:=x_{1},\quad i=1,2,\cdots,d,

The terminal condition is

v(T,x)=g(x):=\ln\Big(1+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(x_{i}^{2}+0.5\sin(10x_{i})\right)\Big),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad T=1.

This HJB equation arises from the following SOCP:

\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}}J(u),\quad J(u):=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|u(t,X_{t}^{u})\right|^{2}+\epsilon\sin(\boldsymbol{1}_{d}^{\top}u(t,X_{t}^{u}))\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathbb{E}\left[g(X_{T}^{u})\right],

where
X^{u}:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the state process defined by

X_{t}^{u}=X_{0}^{u}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(b+u\right)(s,X_{s}^{u})\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\in[0,T].

(46)

The admissible control set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{ad}}
consists of all Borel measurable functions from
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that the SDE (
46
) admits a unique strong solution.

When
\epsilon=0
, the value function
v(t,x)
admits a closed-form solution given by (
42
), where
X_{t}^{0}
is replaced by the solution of (
46
) with
u\equiv 0
.
To compute the reference solution, we approximate the expectation in (
42
) by Monte Carlo, generating
10^{6}
samples of
X_{T}^{0}
via the Euler–Maruyama scheme applied to (
46
).
For
\epsilon\neq 0
, the infimum in the HJB equation
eq.

45
does not admit a closed-form solution due to the presence of the sine term.
Therefore, this example serves as a test case for evaluating the effectiveness of our method on general problems.
In our experiments, we consider a decreasing sequence of the perturbation parameter
\epsilon=1,1/2,1/4,1/8,0
with
d=10^{4}

As
\epsilon
decreases, the solution of (

\epsilon=0

The numerical results are presented in

As

v_{\theta}

These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method for SOCPs whose Hamiltonian

\inf_{u}H

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a highly parallel, derivative-free martingale deep learning method for high-dimensional SOCPs through their associated HJB equations and the verification theory for the optimal control.
The method reformulates the HJB equation into two coupled subproblems: a martingale problem for the value function and an integral optimality condition for the feedback control.
As a result, the loss function avoids evaluating the gradient and Hessian of the value function.
To enable parallel computation, we introduce a pilot process for state-space exploration and system processes for martingale enforcement, allowing the loss to be evaluated in parallel across both time and space.
The martingale condition is imposed in a weak Galerkin form via adversarial learning, which further improves training efficiency and robustness.
The optimal feedback control is learned from the integral optimality condition, thereby avoiding pointwise minimization for the optimal control.
As a result, an efficient training algorithm is proposed to solve the value function and the optimal control simultaneously for high-dimensional SOCPs.

Numerical experiments on a range of high-dimensional problems demonstrate that the proposed method is both accurate and efficient in dimensions up to
10^{4}
, including cases in which the optimal control does not admit an explicit closed-form expression.
The results also indicate that network expressivity can become a practical bottleneck for extremely high-dimensional problems with complicated solution profiles.
Future work will focus on the theoretical analysis of the proposed scheme, and the development of more efficient network architectures to further enhance the performance of the method for large-scale problems.
