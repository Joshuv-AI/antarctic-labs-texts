Regularized Identification with Internal Positivity Side-Information
Mohammad Khosravi, Roy S. Smith

Abstract
In this paper, we present an impulse response identification scheme that incorporates the internal positivity side-information of the system. The realization theory of positive systems establishes specific criteria for the existence of a positive realization for a given transfer function. These transfer function criteria are translated to a set of suitable conditions on the shape and structure of the impulse responses of positive systems. Utilizing these conditions, the impulse response estimation problem is formulated as a constrained optimization in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space equipped with a stable kernel, and suitable constraints are imposed to encode the internal positivity side-information. The optimization problem is infinite-dimensional with an infinite number of constraints. An equivalent finite-dimensional convex optimization in the form of a convex quadratic program is derived. The resulting equivalent reformulation makes the proposed approach suitable for numerical simulation and practical implementation. A Monte Carlo numerical experiment evaluates the impact of incorporating the internal positivity side-information in the proposed identification scheme. The effectiveness of the proposed method is demonstrated using data from a heating system experiment.



I
Introduction

In various dynamical systems, the characteristic variables are constrained to be non-negative or bounded, either by the nature of their definition or according to the physics of the underlying system. For instance, charges in RC-circuits, temperatures and thermal energies in buildings, mass flows in compartmental systems, the population of certain species of animals or bacteria, the concentration of pathogens, level of traffic and congestion in networks and roads, prices of stocks and goods, the pressure of fluids, and many other quantities of interest are always non-negative

In the broad sense, a system described only by such non-negative variables is called a

Depending on our perspective, i.e., whether the positivity feature is considered as an input-output property or a state-space characteristic, we have two central notions of positivity in the system theory literature
[
5
]
: internal positivity and external positivity, where the main focus of the literature is on the former one. In externally positive systems, the non-negativity of the input signal implies the same feature for the output signal. Meanwhile, in internally positive systems, the state trajectory and output signal are non-negative when the initial state and input signal are non-negative.

Positive systems have received extensive attention in the past decades owing to being omnipresent in various fields of science and their wide range of applications
[

,

. Luenberger pioneered the system theoretic approach to positive systems with his seminal work
[

in the 1980s. Since then various subjects of system theory aspects have been tackled, e.g., realization theory
[

, controllability and reachability
[

, observability and observer design
[

, robust stability

Concerning the identification problem, there are two aspects when the underlying system is known to be internally positive. First, respecting the positivity property can be an essential issue in some applications, such as implementing model predictive control. Accordingly, any accurate mathematical modeling approach is expected to include this feature and construct an internally positive system. The second aspect is informed system identification
[

,
21
,

,

and concerns utilizing the internal positivity side-information and integrating features of this knowledge in the model to improve the estimation accuracy. Indeed, disregarding the positivity information can lead to models, which are not physically interpretable and explainable, or behaviors in contradiction with our expectations

. While positive systems have been extensively researched from various viewpoints

[
26

[
24

Since internally positive systems are also externally positive, side-information on internal positivity implies external positivity. Accordingly, from the perspective of informed system identification, one can consider external positivity as partial information to be integrated into the model. To this end, one may employ the external positive system identification methods
[

In this approach, the covariance of the prior distribution is specified by the stable kernels, while the mean is designed arbitrarily as an exponentially decaying FIR. Note that in the FIR identification approaches with an external positivity constraint

,

[
39
]
. Together with the realization theory of positive systems
[
5
]
, the kernel-based framework can provide a suitable foundation for impulse response identification of positive systems.

This paper extends our previous work
[
21
]
and presents an identification method that integrates the internal positivity side-information in the estimated impulse response. From the realization theory of positive systems
[
5
]
, we know that the impulse response of an internally positive system has a specific form, i.e., it has a dominant non-negative part, where the corresponding transfer function has structured poles, and a residual part. This specific form can be translated to a set of structural constraints on the impulse response. Accordingly, the estimation problem is expressed in the form of a constrained optimization in a stable reproducing kernel Hilbert space, where suitable constraints are imposed to encode the internal positivity side-information. Though this problem is initially formulated in an infinite-dimensional space and with an infinite number of constraints, we derive an equivalent finite-dimensional convex optimization in the form of a convex quadratic program. We evaluate the impact of incorporating the internal positivity side-information and assess the performance of the proposed identification scheme through a Monte Carlo numerical experiment. The efficacy of the proposed positive system identification technique is confirmed using data from a thermal dynamics experiment.

IV
Mathematical Formulation for Internally Positive System Identification

In the realization theory of positive systems,
sufficient conditions are introduced under which the transfer function of a system admits a so-called positive realization.
We employ these conditions together with the notion of stable reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) for bridging to the impulse response identification of positive systems.

&\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{Hankel}({\mathrm{h}}))<\infty,\\
&{a}\geq{a}_{\min},\end{array}

(12)

where
{a}_{\min}>0
is a given lower-bound for
{a}
to ensure that
{a}>0
, and
\lambda>0
is the regularization weight.
Note that, similarly to the standard problem formulation in the literature on the kernel-based impulse response identification
[
33
]
, the objective function in (
12
) is an empirical loss function regularized with the RKHS norm of
{\mathrm{h}}
.
This ensures the stability of
{\mathrm{h}}
and also allows incorporating other features such as exponential decay and smoothness
[
39
]
.
The next theorem says that the solution of (
12
) leads to an internally positive estimation of impulse response
{\mathrm{g}}
.
Before proceeding to the theorem, we need to introduce an assumption.

Assumption 1
.

There exist
C\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
\rho_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathrm{d}$}}}\in(0,\rho)
such that we have
|\mathbbm{k}(t,t)|\leq C\rho_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathrm{d}$}}}^{2t}
, for any
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

Theorem 9
.

Let Assumption
1
hold,
{a}
and
{\mathrm{h}}=(h_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a solution pair for (
12
), and, the impulse response
{\mathrm{g}}=(g_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
be defined as
g_{t}=h_{t}+{a}\rho^{t}
, for any
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Then,
{\mathrm{g}}
is internally positive.

Proof.

Let
G
be the transfer function which corresponds to
{\mathrm{g}}
.
Due to (
12
), the rank of Hankel operator
\mathrm{Hankel}({\mathrm{h}})
is finite. Subsequently, according to Theorem
2
, the transfer function corresponding to
{\mathrm{h}}
,
H
, has finite order.
On the other hand, we know that

G(z)=\frac{{a}z^{-1}}{1-\rho z^{-1}}+H(z).

(13)

Therefor, the order of
G
is finite. Accordingly, due to Theorem
2
, we know that

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{Hankel}({\mathrm{g}}))<\infty
, i.e.,
{\mathrm{g}}
satisfies (
4
).
Furthermore, according to the first constraint in (
12
), one can see that
{\mathrm{g}}
is a non-negative impulse response and (
5
) holds for
{\mathrm{g}}
.
Moreover, from the reproducing property of kernel, we know that
h_{t}={\langle{\mathbbm{k}_{t},{\mathrm{h}}}\rangle}
and
\|\mathbbm{k}_{t}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2}={\langle{\mathbbm{k}_{t},\mathbbm{k}_{t}}\rangle}=\mathbbm{k}(t,t)
, for any
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
. Consequently, due to Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and Assumption
1
, we have

\begin{split}\!\!\!\!\!|h_{t}|=|{\langle{\mathbbm{k}_{t},{\mathrm{h}}}\rangle}|&\leq\|\mathbbm{k}_{t}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\\
&\qquad\ =\mathbbm{k}(t,t)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\rho_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathrm{d}$}}}^{t}.\end{split}

(14)

for any
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Following this, one can see that

\begin{split}0&\leq\operatornamewithlimits{liminf}\limits_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}h_{t}\leq\operatornamewithlimits{limsup}\limits_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}h_{t}\leq\operatornamewithlimits{limsup}\limits_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}|h_{t}|\\
&\leq\operatornamewithlimits{limsup}\limits_{t\to\infty}C^{\frac{1}{2}}\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\rho_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathrm{d}$}}}^{t}\rho^{-t}=0,\end{split}

(15)

where the last equality is due to
\rho_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\mathrm{d}$}}}\in(0,\rho)
.
Hence,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}h_{t}
is well-defined and we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}h_{t}=0
.
Subsequently, due to the definition of
{\mathrm{g}}
, it follows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\rho^{-t}g_{t}={a}
and
{\mathrm{g}}
satisfies (
9
).
Therefore,
{\mathrm{g}}
belongs to
{\mathscr{P}}_{\!\rho}
, and consequently, due to Corollary
6
,
{\mathrm{g}}
is internally positive.
∎

Remark 1
.

For
a
,
{\mathrm{h}}
and
{\mathrm{g}}
introduced in Theorem
9
, we have

{\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}(a,{\mathrm{h}})=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}\big(y_{t_{i}}-{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{g}})\big)^{2},

(16)

i.e., in the cost function of (
12
), the first term is the sum of squared errors for the impulse response fitting when the dominant pole
\rho
is known.

V
Towards a Tractable Solution

In this section, we investigate optimization problem (
12
) which was introduced for impulse response identification with internal positivity.
This optimization problem is in an infinite-dimensional space with an infinite number of constraints.
In the following, we analyze this problem and provide a tractable approach for deriving its solution.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
be the Hilbert space
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
which is endowed with inner product
{\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\times{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined as following

{\langle{({a}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{1}),({a}_{2},{\mathrm{h}}_{2})}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}={a}_{1}{a}_{2}+{\langle{{\mathrm{h}}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{2}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}},

(17)

for any
{a}_{1},{a}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathrm{h}}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
.
Also, let
{\mathscr{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
be the set of finite Hankel rank impulse responses in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, i.e.,

{\mathscr{F}}=\big\{{\mathrm{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\!\ \big|\!\ \mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{Hankel}({\mathrm{h}}))<\infty\big\}.

(18)

We define function
{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as

{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})={\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\delta_{{\mathscr{F}}}({\mathrm{h}})+\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2},

(19)

where
{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is the following set

{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}:=\Big\{\big({a},(h_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\!\big)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\!\ \Big|\!\ h_{s}+{a}\rho^{s}\geq 0,{a}\geq{a}_{\min}\Big\},

(20)

for
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
From the definition of
{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}
, it follows easily that the
optimization problem (
12
) is equivalent to

\inf_{({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\ \!{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}).

(21)

For
({a},{\mathrm{h}})=({a}_{\min},\mathbf{0})
, where
\mathbf{0}
denotes the zero vector in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, one can easily see that

{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}({a}_{\min},\mathbf{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_{t_{i}}-{a}_{\min}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})\big)^{2}<\infty.

(22)

Since, for any
({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, we have
{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})\geq 0
, it follows that
(
21
) is bounded.
However, this arguments does not guarantee the existence of solution for
(
21
).
In the following, we show that under mild conditions the optimization problem (
21
) admits a solution when the kernel
\mathbbm{k}
meets certain criteria.
Let function

{\mathcal{J}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be defined as following

{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})={\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2},

(23)

and consider the optimization problem

\inf_{({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\ \!{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}).

(24)

One can easily see that
{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}={\mathcal{J}}+\delta_{{\mathscr{F}}}
, which implies that

{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})\leq{\mathcal{J}}_{\!{\mathscr{F}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\qquad\forall\!\ ({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}.

(25)

Consequently, if (
24
) has a solution
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
such that the operator
\mathrm{Hankel}({\mathrm{h}}^{*})
is finite-rank, then
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
is a solution for (
21
) as well. In other words, the identification problem with internal positivity side-information introduced in (
12
) admits a solution.
Hence, we need to study the solution behavior of (
24
).
To this end, we require several technical assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

There exists
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
such that
{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})\neq 0
.

Note that if Assumption
2
does not hold, then, for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
, we have
{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})=0
, which means that the dominant pole is not excited by the input signal
{\mathrm{u}}
. Accordingly, this assumption essentially says that the input signal excites the dominant pole.

Assumption 3
.

There exists
\underline{t}\leq 0
such that
u_{t}=0
, for
t<\underline{t}
.

Assumption
3
is a technical assumption and introduced mainly for the sake of our mathematical arguments, i.e., to guarantee the continuity of operator
{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}:{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
. Indeed, this assumption holds in realistic situations such as when the system is initially at rest.
Based on these assumptions, we can show the existence and uniqueness for the solution of (
24
).

Theorem 10
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
3
, optimization problem (
24
) admits a unique solution, i.e., there exists
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
such that

{\mathcal{J}}({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})<{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}),

(26)

for any
({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\backslash\big\{({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})\big\}
.

Proof.

Let set
{\mathscr{R}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
be defined as
{{\mathscr{R}}}=\bigcap_{s=0}^{\infty}{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}
. Accordingly, one can see that
\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}}=\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}
, and hence, we have

{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})={\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2}+\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}).

(27)

With respect to each
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, define set
{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
as

{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}:=\Big\{\big({a},(h_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\!\big)\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\!\ \Big|\!\ h_{s}+{a}\rho^{s}\geq 0\Big\}.

(28)

For any
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, due to the reproducing property of the kernel, we have

\begin{split}h_{s}+{a}\rho^{s}&={\langle{{\mathrm{h}},\mathbbm{k}_{s}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}+{a}\rho^{s}={\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),(\rho^{s},\mathbbm{k}_{s})}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}.\end{split}

(29)

Therefore, we know that
{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is a half-space, and hence, it is a non-empty, closed and convex subset of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, for all
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Note that
[{a}_{\min},\infty)\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is a non-empty, closed and convex subsets of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
.
One can see that

{{\mathscr{R}}}=\big(\operatornamewithlimits{\text{\scalebox{1.25}{$\cap$}}}\limits_{s=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}\big)\!\ \cap\!\ \big([{a}_{\min},\infty)\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\big),

(30)

and also, we know that
({a}_{\min},\mathbf{0})
belongs to
[{a}_{\min},\infty)\times{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}_{s}
, for each
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Therefore,
{{\mathscr{R}}}
is a non-empty, closed and convex subset of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
. Consequently, it follows that

\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
is a proper, convex and lower semi-continuous function, where we have
{\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}({a}_{\min},\mathbf{0})=0}
.
With respect to each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
, define
\varphi_{i}
as

\varphi_{i}:=\sum_{s=0}^{t_{i}-\underline{t}}u_{t_{i}-s}\mathbbm{k}_{s}=u_{t_{i}}\mathbbm{k}_{0}+u_{t_{i}-1}\mathbbm{k}_{1}+\ldots+u_{\underline{t}}\mathbbm{k}_{t_{i}-\underline{t}}.

(31)

Since

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is a linear space which contains the sections of the kernel, we know that
\varphi_{i}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
.
Moreover, from Assumption
3
, reproducing property of kernel and the linearity property of inner product, it follows that

\begin{split}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{h}})&=\sum_{s=0}^{t_{i}-\underline{t}}{\langle{{\mathrm{h}},\mathbbm{k}_{s}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}u_{t_{i}-s}\\
&=\langle{\mathrm{h}},\sum_{s=0}^{t_{i}-\underline{t}}\mathbbm{k}_{s}u_{t_{i}-s}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}={\langle{{\mathrm{h}},\varphi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}.\end{split}

(32)

Accordingly, we have

{a}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}+{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{h}})={a}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}+{\langle{{\mathrm{h}},\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}={\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}},

(33)

where
\psi_{i}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
is the vector defined as
\psi_{i}=({\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho}),\varphi_{i})
, for
i=0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1
.
Therefore, one can see that

{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})\!=\!\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}\!\big(y_{t_{i}}\!-\!{\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\big)^{2}+\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2}\!+\!\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}),

Accordingly, since
\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}
is proper, convex and lower semi-continuous, and also, due to the fact that

{\mathcal{J}}({a}_{\min},\mathbf{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_{t_{i}}-{a}_{\min}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})\big)^{2}<\infty,

(34)

we know that
{\mathcal{J}}
is a proper, convex and lower semi-continuous function.
This implies that (
24
) has a solution
[
49
]
.
Define bilinear operator
{\mathrm{Q}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\times{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\begin{split}{\mathrm{Q}}\big(&({a}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{1}),({a}_{2},{\mathrm{h}}_{2})\big)\\
=&\!\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}{\langle{({a}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{1}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}{\langle{\psi_{i},({a}_{2},{\mathrm{h}}_{2})}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\!+\!\lambda{\langle{{\mathrm{h}}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{2}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}.\end{split}

(35)

For any
({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, one can easily that

{\mathrm{Q}}\big(({a},{\mathrm{h}}),({a},{\mathrm{h}})\big)=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}{\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2}\!+\!\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2}\geq 0.

(36)

Moreover, since
\lambda
is a positive real scalar, if
{\mathrm{Q}}\big(({a},{\mathrm{h}}),({a},{\mathrm{h}})\big)=0
, then, we need to have
{\mathrm{h}}=0
and
{\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}=0
, for all
i=0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1
, which implies that
{a}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})=0
.
Subsequently, due to Assumption
2
, we have
{a}=0
, i.e.,
({a},{\mathrm{h}})=(0,\mathbf{0})
. Based on this argument, we know that
{\mathrm{Q}}
is a positive definite bilinear operator.
Therefore, the function
f:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined as

f({\mathrm{v}})={\mathrm{Q}}({\mathrm{v}},{\mathrm{v}})
, for all
{\mathrm{v}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
, is strictly convex
[
49
]
.
Note that we have

{\mathcal{J}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})=f({a},{\mathrm{h}})-2{\mathrm{L}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}y_{t_{i}}^{2}+\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}({a},{\mathrm{h}}),

(37)

where
{\mathrm{L}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is the bounded linear operator defined as

{\mathrm{L}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}y_{t_{i}}{\langle{({a},{\mathrm{h}}),\psi_{i}}\rangle}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}.

(38)

Therefore, since
f
is strictly convex,
{\mathrm{L}}
is linear, and,
\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}}
is convex, we know that
{\mathcal{J}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a strictly convex function, and consequently, the the solution of optimization problem (
24
) is unique
[
49
]
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Due to Theorem
10
,
we know that the convex program (
24
) has a unique solution
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
.
Meanwhile, one should note that (
24
) is an infinite dimensional optimization problem with infinite number of constraints. Thus, obtaining the solution
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
is not straightforward.
On the other hand, since
{\mathrm{h}}^{*}
belongs to the set of stable responses
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
dominated by
{\mathrm{f}}_{\rho}
, one may intuitively expect that

{\mathrm{h}}^{*}\in{{\mathscr{R}}}_{m}
, for large enough
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
In other words, the solution to the optimization problem (
24
) is determined by a finite number of constraints and the remaining constraints are unnecessary.
In order to formalize this idea, let function

{\mathcal{J}}_{m}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be defined as

{\mathcal{J}}_{m}({a},{\mathrm{h}})={\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\sum_{s=0}^{m}\delta_{{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}}({a},{\mathrm{h}})+\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2},

(39)

and, consider the following program

\inf_{({a},{\mathrm{h}})\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}\ \!{\mathcal{J}}_{m}({a},{\mathrm{h}}).

(40)

Note that (
40
) is equivalent to

\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{a}\in{\mathbb{R}},{\mathrm{h}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}&\!\!\!\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}\!\big(y_{t_{i}}-{a}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})-{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{h}})\big)^{2}\!\!+\!\lambda\|{\mathrm{h}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2},\\
\text{s.t.}&\!\!\!h_{t}+{a}\rho^{t}\geq 0,\quad\forall\!\ t\in\{0,1,\ldots,m\},\\
&\!\!\!{a}\geq{a}_{\min}.\end{array}

(41)

The next theorem guarantees the existence and uniqueness of solution for optimization problem (
40
), or equivalently, for program (
41
).

Theorem 11
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
10
,
for each
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, problem (
40
) admits unique solution
({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})
.

Proof.

Define set
{\mathscr{P}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathbbm{k}}
as
{\mathscr{P}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}}=\bigcap_{s=0}^{m}{{\mathscr{R}}}_{s}
.
By replacing
{\mathscr{P}}
with
{\mathscr{P}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}}
in the proof of Theorem
10
and then repeating the same steps, the claim follows.
∎

Once the existence and uniqueness for the solution of (
40
) are established by Theorem
11
, a reasonable concern is
the asymptotic behavior of
({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})
, especially with respect to
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
.
The next theorem reveals this link saying that the solution
({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})
coincides with
({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
when
m
is large enough.

Theorem 12
.

Let Assumptions
2
and
3
hold. Then, the following statements hold:

i)

Under Assumption
1
, there is a non-negative integer
m
such that
{a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}}={a}^{*}
and
{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}}={\mathrm{h}}^{*}
.

ii)
For a non-negative integer
m
, one has
({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})=({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
if and only if
{\mathcal{J}}({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})<\infty
.

iii)

If
({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})=({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
, for a non-negative integer
m
, then
{a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}\overline{m}\mathrm{)}$}}}}={a}^{*}
and
{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}\overline{m}\mathrm{)}$}}}}={\mathrm{h}}^{*}
, for every
\overline{m}\geq m
.

Proof.

Part i) For any
{a}\geq{a}_{\min}
and any
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, we have

{\mathcal{J}}_{m}({a},\mathbf{0})={\mathcal{J}}({a},\mathbf{0})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_{t_{i}}-{a}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{f}}_{\rho})\big)^{2}\in[0,\infty).

(42)

Let
{a}_{0}
be

{\mathrm{argmin}}_{{a}\geq{a}_{\min}}{\mathcal{J}}({a},\mathbf{0})
,
and, define
C_{0}
as
C_{0}:={\mathcal{J}}({a}_{0},\mathbf{0})
, which is equal to
{\mathcal{J}}_{m}({a}_{0},\mathbf{0})
, for any
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
If
C_{0}=0
, then one can easily see that
({a}_{0},\mathbf{0})
is a solution for optimization problems (
24
) and (
40
), where, because of their uniqueness,
({a}_{0},\mathbf{0})=({a}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}},{\mathrm{h}}^{{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathrm{(}m\mathrm{)}$}}}})=({a}^{*},{\mathrm{h}}^{*})
.
Now, we consider the case
C_{0}>0
.
Define
m_{0}
as following

Nevertheless, compared to (

In the remainder of this section, we fill this gap by employing the notion of finite Hankel rank kernels.

Definition 4
.

We call the Mercer kernel
\mathbbm{k}:{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
a
finite Hankel rank
if, for each
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, the section of kernel at
s
is a finite Hankel rank impulse response, i.e.,

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{Hankel}(\mathbbm{k}_{s}))<\infty,\quad\forall\!\ s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

(69)

The commonly employed stable kernels in the literature
[
33
,
52
]
are

Tuned/Correlated
(TC),

Diagonal/Correlated
(TC), and

Stable Spline
(TC),
which are respectively denoted by
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
and
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
,
and defined as follows:

•

Tuned/Correlated (TC) kernel:

\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}(s,t)=\beta^{\max(s,t)},\quad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},

(70)

where
\beta\in[0,1)
is the hyperparameter of
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,

•

Diagonal/Correlated (DC) kernel:

\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}(s,t)=\beta^{\frac{s+t}{2}}\gamma^{|s-t|},\quad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},

(71)

where
\beta\in[0,1)
and
\gamma\in[-1,1]
are the hyperparameters of
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
, and,

•

Stable Spline (SS) kernel:

\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}(s,t)=\frac{\beta^{s+t+\max(s,t)}}{2}-\frac{\beta^{3\max(s,t)}}{6},\quad\forall s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},

(72)

where
\rho\in[0,1)
is the hyperparameter of
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
.

As shown by the next theorem, these common kernels are finite Hankel rank.

Theorem 17
.

Every finite support kernel,

\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
,
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
and
\mathbbm{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
is finite Hankel rank.

Proof.

See Appendix
A-C
.
∎

VII
Further Internal Positivity Side-Information and Extensions

In Section
IV
, we employed positive system realization theory to formulate the identification problem with internal positivity side-information.
The resulting optimization problem, (
12
), is formulated using the fact that the transfer function of system
{\mathcal{S}}
is in the following form

G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=F^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=\frac{a}{1-\rho z^{-1}}+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z).

(82)

The transfer function
G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
has a dominant part
F^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z):=a(1-\rho z^{-1})^{-1}
with
\rho\in(0,1)
, and a suppressed part
H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
.
Given that the impulse response of the system satisfies specific properties,
this formulation can be further extended to the following cases

G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=F^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=\frac{N^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z^{-1})}{(1-\rho z^{-1})^{n}}+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z),

(83)

and

G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=F^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=\frac{N^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z^{-1})}{1-\rho^{n}z^{-n}}+H^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z),

(84)

where
N^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
is a polynomial with degree less than
n
, i.e.,
N^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{n-1}[z^{-1}]
.
Note that according to
[
7
, Theorem 9]
, the transfer function of a positive system with non-zero spectral radius is in form of (
83
) or (
84
), which are generalized forms of (
82
).
These cases correspond to the situations where, in addition to internal positivity, we have further information on the dominant part of the impulse response of the system.
In this section, we discuss these extensions.

VII-A

We first discuss the extension which corresponds to (
83
), i.e., the transfer function has a unique dominant pole with multiplicity
n
where

The mathematical proofs are omitted since they are similar to the ones given in Section

With respect to each

VII-C

Zero Spectral Radius

According to
[
7
, Theorem 8]
, when the transfer function
G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
has zero spectral radius,
r(G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}})=0
, the impulse response
{\mathrm{g}}^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
is internally positive if
{\mathrm{g}}^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
is non-negative.
Based on the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 8]
, we know that
{\mathrm{g}}^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
belongs to
c_{00}
, the space of finitely non-zero impulse responses, i.e., there exists
n_{{\mathrm{g}}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
such that
G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}(z)=\sum_{t=0}^{n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1}g_{t}z^{-t}
and
g_{t}=0
, for all
t\geq n_{{\mathrm{g}}}
.
Comparing to the other cases of internal positivity side-information, the case of zero spectral radius provides the weakest information.
Indeed, this knowledge only says the
{\mathrm{g}}^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}}
is a non-negative and finitely non-zero sequence and provides no further information about the behavior of the impulse response of system
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Based on the discussion above, the identification problem with the internal positivity side-information and the extra knowledge
r(G^{{\scalebox{.5}{$({\mathcal{S}})$}}})=0
can be easily formulated as following

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}&\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}\big(y_{t_{i}}-{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{g}})\big)^{2}+\lambda\!\ \|{\mathrm{g}}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbbm{k}}}^{2},\\
\text{s.t.}&g_{t}\geq 0,\quad\forall t=0,1,\ldots,n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1,\\
\end{array}

(100)

where
\mathbbm{k}
is a kernel zero on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{2}\backslash\{0,\ldots,n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1\}^{2}
.
One can show that for
{\mathrm{g}}^{*}=(g_{t}^{*})_{t=0}^{\infty}
, the solution of (
100
), and for
t=0,1,\ldots,n_{{\mathrm{g}}}
, we have

g_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}x_{i}{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}(\mathbbm{k}_{t})+\sum_{s=0}^{n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1}x_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}+s}\mathbbm{k}_{s}(t),

(101)

where
{\mathrm{x}}=[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}+n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!
is the solution of following
convex quadratic program

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{\mathrm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}+n_{{\mathrm{g}}}}}&\big\|{\mathrm{y}}-\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{O}}\!\!&\!\!{\mathrm{L}}\end{bmatrix}{\mathrm{x}}\big\|^{2}+\lambda{\mathrm{x}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{O}}\!\!&\!\!{\mathrm{L}}\\
{\mathrm{L}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\!\!&\!\!{\mathrm{K}}\\
\end{bmatrix}{\mathrm{x}},\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{L}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\!\!&\!\!{\mathrm{K}}\\
\end{bmatrix}{\mathrm{x}}\geq 0.\end{array}

(102)

Also, one can solve (
100
) directly for
[g_{0},\ldots,g_{n_{{\mathrm{g}}}-1}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\mathrm{g}}}}
at the cost of a matrix inversion which can be both imprecise and computationally demanding, especially when

is large.

VIII
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we provide numerical and experimental examples to verify the efficacy and performance of the proposed method for impulse response identification with internal positivity side-information.
The first example concerns the impact of incorporating internal positivity side-information on the estimation quality and providing a comparative analysis for the proposed identification scheme through a Monte Carlo analysis. The second example concerns the efficacy of the proposed identification scheme on a set of data collected from an experimental heating system.

Discussion

We have several observations from the Monte Carlo numerical experiment.
As shown in Figure
1
, all methods are outperformed by the proposed identification scheme, which maximally incorporates the internal positivity side-information.
Indeed, the side-information helps excluding spurious model candidates and subsequently increases the accuracy of the estimation. The bias-variance results presented in Table
I
confirm this fact.
While each of these methods estimates a non-negative impulse response and partially integrates positivity side-information, we can see that the level of integrated side-information is
less than that of the proposed method.
The proposed approach incorporates this information in the model
maximally.
Comparing methods B and C, one can see the former one is a two-step procedure where the estimation is performed in the first step and non-negativity of the impulse response is obtained in the second step, while the latter approach is a single-step procedure that considers impulse response non-negativity during the estimation.
On the other hand, according to the fitting results shown in Figure
1
, C performs better than method B. For methods D and E, we have similar arguments.
This observation highlights the importance of jointly considering the positivity with the impulse response estimation, as done by the proposed method.
Method A knows the actual order of the system. However, according to the results presented in Figure
1
and Table
I
, one can see that positivity is a more advantageous and stronger side-information for impulse response estimation, especially when it is incorporated with its maximum strength like in the proposed scheme.
Finally, one can see that the kernel-based methods D, E, F, and G have better estimation performance comparing to the methods A, B, and C, which is expected

IX
Conclusion

In this paper, we have considered the problem of impulse response identification when side-information is available on the internal positivity of the system. We have employed the realization theory of positive systems to introduce the identification scheme in which the positivity side-information is integrated into the identified model. The resulting formulation is in the form of a constrained optimization over a reproducing kernel Hilbert space endowed with a stable kernel where the constraints are suitably designed to incorporate the positivity side-information in the solution. We have borrowed techniques and tools from optimization theory in normed spaces to derive an equivalent finite-dimensional convex quadratic program. This gives a computationally tractable identification scheme that incorporates the internal positivity side-information and has the well-known advantageous features of kernel-based methods. We have performed a Monte Carlo numerical experiment to compare the performance of proposed approach with FIR identification methods considering only the external positivity feature. This has empirically studied the impact of integrating positivity side-information in terms of estimation bias, variance, and mean squared error. The results show that the proposed identification approach, which integrates internal positivity, outperforms the schemes considering only external positivity. We have observed that incorporating internal positivity side-information reduces the estimation bias and variance. This observation is expected since FIR external positivity implies the weakest form of information about an internally positive system and fails to exploit the complete information of internal positivity. We have further evaluated the effectiveness of the proposed identification scheme using data from a heating system experiment.
