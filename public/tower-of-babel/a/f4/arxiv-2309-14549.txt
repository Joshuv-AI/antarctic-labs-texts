Counting Rational Points on the Stacky Sym 2 ⁡ ℙ 1 \Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
John Yin

Abstract
We prove the weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture formulated in [ 6 ] for the stack Sym 2 ⁡ ℙ 1 := ( ℙ 1 × ℙ 1 ) / S 2 \Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}:=({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1})/S_{2} , where the S 2 S_{2} action just permutes the two coordinates. In particular, we show that the diagonal Δ ⊂ Sym 2 ⁡ ℙ 1 \Delta\subset\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1} is an accumulating substack.

Introduction

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}
be the stack
\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}:=({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1})/S_{2}
, where
S_{2}
is the symmetric group with two elements, acting via permutation of coordinates. In this paper, we will prove the weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture formulated in
[
6
]
for
{\mathfrak{X}}
. There is also a stacky Batyrev-Manin conjecture formulated in
[
5
]
, which further predicts the existence of a leading constant. We do not prove this stronger conjecture, though our proof does not give any indication that the leading constant does not exist. Other point counts with respect to the stacky height defined in
[
6
]
has been done.
[
11
]
establishes asymptotic bounds on point counts of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
with
3
stacky points
\{0,1,\infty\}
and the residue gerbes all equal to
\mu_{2}
.
[
12
]
establishes point counts of weighted projective spaces.
[
2
]
establishes asymptotic bounds on point counts on certain modular curves.

We perform this count by considering the data that
{\mathfrak{X}}
parametrizes. That is, for any field
k
, the
k
-points of
{\mathfrak{X}}
correspond to a quadratic étale extension, along with an element in that extension. Most of the time, the element in the extension determines the quadratic étale extension, unless that element is in
k
. Thus, counting algebraic numbers of degree
2
over
k
essentially correspond to counting the
k
-points of
{\mathfrak{X}}
. Counting algebraic numbers of a fixed degree and bounded absolute height over
{\mathbb{Q}}
was first done in
[
9
]
. The authors approach this problem by counting the polynomials of a fixed degree whose roots have bounded absolute height, instead of the algebraic numbers themselves. Such polynomials have coefficients varying in a “nice” region, and they are able to apply the principle of Lipschitz to obtain bounds on the number of such polynomials.

In this paper, we will carry out essentially the same count, except with respect to the stacky height in
[
6
]
. The main difficulty is that while Masser and Vaaler dealt with only the absolute height of an algebraic number, the stacky height involves both the absolute height and the discriminant of the number field the algebraic number generates. Thus, parametrizing by polynomials does not lead to nice regions, as the discriminant of the number field generated by a polynomial is a very discontinuous function in terms of the coefficients of the polynomial. As a result, we have to employ different methods, resulting in worse asymptotic bounds than those in
[
9
]
. However, these bounds turn out to be enough to prove the weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture in the context of
{\mathfrak{X}}=\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

Furthermore, in the setting of Batyrev-Manin conjecture, which deals with point counts on a Fano variety
X
, there is a notion of “accumulating subvariety”
C\subset X
. It turns out that it could happen that “most” of the points of bounded height lie on this
C
, despite the fact that it is of a lower dimension. In these cases, the Batyrev-Manin conjecture makes predictions of points on
X\setminus C
. In our case, we will show that the diagonal
\Delta\subset\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is an “accumulating substack.” We will show that point counts on this substack are on the order of
B^{2}
in Theorem
3.3
, using just elementary number theory. Points off this substack correspond to points in the quadratic étale extension
{\mathbb{Q}}^{2}
, which we call “split points,” or an algebraic number in a quadratic number field, which we call “non-split points.” We will show that the number of split, non-diagonal points is on the order of
B\log B
in Theorem
3.4
. Finally, we will show that the number of non-split, non-diagonal points corresponding to quadratic field extensions is
\Theta(B\log B)
in Theorem
3.5
. Theorem
3.4
also just uses elementary number theory, while Theorem
3.5
is the hardest to prove, and is the main goal of this paper.

The approach we to tackle our main theorem is by separating the non-split, non-diagonal points of
\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
we count into two pieces. The non-split points of
\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
now truly do correspond to algebraic numbers
\alpha
of degree
2
, and so correspond further to just quadratic polynomials up to scaling by taking their minimal polynomial. Furthermore, the heights of elements of
\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
, expressed in terms of their minimal polynomials
ax^{2}+bx+c
representing them, is approximately
H(a:b:c)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}
, where
H(a:b:c)
is the usual height on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and
\sqf
denotes the squarefree part. Thus, the two pieces we separate into are a piece containing quadratic polynomials
f
where
|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|
is small, and a piece where
|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|
is big. In the situation that
|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|
is small, we use L-functions to parametrize quadratic polynomials which generate a quadratic extension
k
with fixed discriminant, then apply a Tauberian theorem. In the situation that
|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|
is big, we switch perspectives and notice that
b^{2}-4ac
is divisible by a small square, and count quadratic polynomials of bounded height whose discriminant is divisible by a small square.

The methods in this paper does not immediately allow one to prove weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture for higher
\Sym^{n}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. This problem is in general difficult. An element of
\Sym^{n}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
is a pair
(k,\alpha)
, where
k
is an ètale extension of degree
n
, and
\alpha
is an element of
k
. The height of such a pair
(k,\alpha)
is
H_{\abs}(\alpha)^{2n}\sqrt{|\Delta_{k}|},
where
H_{\abs}
is the absolute height and
\Delta_{k}
is the absolute discriminant of
k
. By
[
13
]
, there exists a primitive element
\alpha_{k}
of height bounded by
O(|\Delta_{k}|^{1/2n})
in
k
. So, the height of the pairs
(k,\alpha_{k})
would be
O(|\Delta_{k}|^{3/2})
. Now, the weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture in this case would predict that the number of such pairs of height bounded by
B
is in particular bounded by
B\log B
, and so there must be at most
B\log B
extensions of degree
n
whose discriminant is bounded by
B^{2/3}
. In other words, the number of extensions of bounded degree and discriminant
O(B^{3/2}\log B)
, independent of
n
. Thus, the weak form of the generalized Batyrev-Manin-Malle conjecture for
\Sym^{n}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is much stronger than the state of the art bounds
[
8
]
on the number of
number fields
of fixed degree
n
and bounded discriminant
B
, which is currently
O(B^{c\log(n)^{2}})
.

3
Overview

Let
{\mathfrak{X}}:=\Sym^{2}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}=({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1})/S_{2}
, where
S_{2}
acts on the two coordinates by permuting them. We will study point counts on this stack.

Definition 3.1
.

A rational point
x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}})
corresponds to a
S_{2}
-equivariant map
f:\Spec(K)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, where
K
is a degree
2
etale extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. If
K={\mathbb{Q}}\oplus{\mathbb{Q}}
, then we call
x
a
split
point. Otherwise, we call
x

non-split
. Furthermore, if the map
\Spec(K)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
factors through
\Spec(K)\to\Spec({\mathbb{Q}})
, we call
x
a
diagonal
point.

We may describe non-split points
x
by the
S_{2}
-equivariant map
f:\Spec(K)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
, corresponding to an ordered pair
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(K)\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(K)
, which are equivariant under the two actions

1.

(\alpha,\beta)\to(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\beta})

2.

and
(\alpha,\beta)\to(\beta,\alpha)
.

In conclusion, the datum of an non-split point is equal to the datum of a single
\alpha\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(K)
. The same reasoning applies for split points, except given
(a_{0},a_{1})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
, the notation
\overline{(a_{0},a_{1})}
is just
(a_{1},a_{0})
. In this case we obtain that the datum of a split point is equal to the datum of an ordered pair
(a_{0},a_{1})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Let
K
be a global field. For
x\in{\mathfrak{X}}(K)
, we define
h(x)
to be the height associated to the tangent bundle of
{\mathfrak{X}}
. By the calculations in
[
6
, Section 3.6]
, we obtain that given a point
x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}})
,

h(x)=\begin{cases}2h_{\abs}(a)+2h_{\abs}(b)&x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}})\text{ split, associated with }(a,b)\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})\\
4h_{\abs}(\alpha)+\frac{1}{2}\log|\Delta_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]}|&x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}})\text{ non-split, associated with }\alpha\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(K)\end{cases},

where
h_{\abs}
is the logarithmic absolute Weil height.

Now define
H_{\abs}(x)=e^{h_{\abs}(x)}
to be the exponential Weil height, and also define
H(x)=e^{h(x)}
. Our goal is to count
{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}})
with respect to
H(x)
. Counting split and diagonal points is easy, involving only some elementary number theory, and we may obtain the exact asymptotic. However, counting non-split points is more difficult and instead we only obtain an asymptotic upper and lower bound with the same magnitude.

We begin with the main statement of the paper, which shows that the diagonal is an accumulating subset.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assuming GRH, we have that

\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ diagonal},H(x)\leq B\}=\Theta(B^{2}),

and

\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ off-diagonal},H(x)\leq B\}=\Theta(B\log B).

Proof.

This is just the combination of Theorems
3.3
,
3.4
, and
3.5
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
c=\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(x)}{x^{8}}
. Then,

\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ diagonal},H(x)\leq B\}=2cB^{2}+O(B)

Proof.

Let
x
be a diagonal point. Then,
x
is a map
x:\Spec(K)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
whose image is a rational point of the form
(r,r)
, where
r\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}({\mathbb{Q}})
(here
K
may be
{\mathbb{Q}}\times{\mathbb{Q}}
). Also let
r=a/b
, where
a,b
in lowest form. Then, the height
H(x)
is
H_{\abs}(r)^{4}\sqrt{|\Delta_{K}|}
. Thus, we must count the following set

\{(r,K):[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]=2,\max(a,b)^{4}|\Delta_{K}|^{1/2}\leq B\}.

This count is

\sum_{x=1}^{B^{1/4}}2\phi(x)\#\{K:[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]=2,|\Delta_{K}|\leq B^{2}/x^{8}\}.

It is well known that

\#\{K:[K:{\mathbb{Q}}]=2,|\Delta_{K}|\leq B^{2}/x^{8}\}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\frac{B^{2}}{x^{8}}+O(\frac{B}{x^{4}}).

Thus, we need to find

\sum_{x=1}^{B^{1/4}}2\phi(x)\frac{B^{2}}{x^{8}}+O(\sum_{x=1}^{B^{1/4}}2\phi(x)\frac{B}{x^{4}})=A+O(E).

First we estimate
A
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{B^{1/4}}\frac{\phi(x)}{x^{8}}

\displaystyle=c-\sum_{x=B^{1/4}}^{\infty}\frac{\phi(x)}{x^{8}}

\displaystyle=c+O(\sum_{x=B^{1/4}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{7}})

\displaystyle=c+O(B^{-3/2})

Thus,
A=2cB^{2}+O(\sqrt{B})
.

Now we estimate
E
.

E=\sum_{x=1}^{B^{1/4}}2\phi(x)\frac{B}{x^{4}}=O(B).

Thus,
A+E=2cB^{2}+O(B)
, as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ split and nondiagonal},H(x)\leq B\}=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)^{2}}B\log B+O(B)

Proof.

Here, we are counting pairs of rational points
(r,s)
so that
H_{\abs}(r)^{2}H_{\abs}(s)^{2}\leq B
. Writing
r=a/b
and
s=c/d
where
a/b
and
c/d
are fractions in lowest terms, we see that we need to count

\{(a,b,c,d):(a,b)=1,(c,d)=1,\max(a,b)^{2}\max(c,d)^{2}\leq B\}.

This count is

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{B}/x}\#\{(a,b):(a,b)=1,

\displaystyle\max(a,b)=x\}\#\{(c,d):(c,d)=1,\max(c,d)=y\}

\displaystyle=4\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{B}/x}\phi(x)\phi(y)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\phi(x)\sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{B}/x}\phi(y).

The fact that

\sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{B}/x}\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}(\sqrt{B}/x)^{2}+O((\sqrt{B}/x)\log(\sqrt{B}/x))

is standard. Thus, we get

4\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\phi(x)\left(\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{x}\right)^{2}+O\left(\frac{\sqrt{B}}{x}\log(\sqrt{B}/x)\right)\right)

=\frac{2B}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\frac{\phi(x)}{x^{2}}+O(\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\phi(x)\frac{\sqrt{B}\log(\sqrt{B}/x)}{x})=A+O(E),

(3.1)

where we denote
A
to be the first term and
E
to be the second term.

First we estimate
A
. Using summation by parts, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\frac{\phi(x)}{x^{2}}

\displaystyle=O(\frac{\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\phi(x)}{\sqrt{B}^{2}})+\sum_{k=1}^{\sqrt{B}-1}(\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{1}{(k+1)^{2}})\sum_{x=1}^{k}\phi(k)

\displaystyle=O(1)+\sum_{k=1}^{\sqrt{B}-1}\frac{2k+1}{k^{2}(k+1)^{2}}(\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}k^{2}+O(k\log k))

\displaystyle=O(1)+\frac{1}{\zeta(2)}\sum_{k=1}^{\sqrt{B}-1}\frac{1}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\zeta(2)}\log(B)+O(1)

Thus,
A=\frac{1}{\zeta(2)^{2}}B\log B+O(B).
This is our main term. We conclude by estimating
E
, which will be an error term.

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\phi(x)\frac{\sqrt{B}\log(\sqrt{B}/x)}{x}

\displaystyle<\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\sqrt{B}\log(\sqrt{B}/x)

\displaystyle=B\log(\sqrt{B})-\sqrt{B}\sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{B}}\log(x)

\displaystyle=B\log(\sqrt{B})-\sqrt{B}(\sqrt{B}\log(\sqrt{B})+O(\sqrt{B}))

\displaystyle=O(B)

∎

The rest of the paper will be dedicated to proving the following theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assuming GRH,

\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ non-split and nondiagonal},H(x)\leq B\}=\Theta(B\log B).

Proof.

We have by definition that an non-split point is given by an element of a quadratic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and an element of a quadratic extension may be, up to conjugates, identified with its minimal polynomial. Next, by
[
3
, Prop 1.6.6, Lemma 1.6.7]
, we have
H_{\abs}(\alpha)^{2}=\Theta(H_{\abs}([a:b:c]))
where
\alpha
is a root of
ax^{2}+bx+c
. We also have that
|\Delta_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]}|^{1/2}=\Theta(\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|})
.

Thus, we have the asymptotic bounds

\displaystyle|\{x\in{\mathfrak{X}}({\mathbb{Q}}):x\text{ non-split and nondiagonal},H(x)\leq B\}|=\Theta(

\displaystyle|\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,

\displaystyle\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac|)|}\leq B,

\displaystyle\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}|)

Thus, it suffices to upper and lower bound the latter set. To do this, we have two technical lemmas, which we prove right after this theorem.

Lemma 3.6
.

We have that

|\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B,\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}|

=O(|\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})\notin\{0,1\},\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq B\}|).

Lemma 3.7
.

We have that

\displaystyle|\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B,\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}|

\displaystyle=\Omega(|\{(a,b,c):\gcd(a,b)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{x}\leq B,a^{2}+b^{2}\equiv 5\mod 8,

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\sqf(a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2})\equiv b\equiv 0\mod 2\}|).

Now let

S:=\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})\notin\{0,1\},\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq B\}

be the upper-bounding set appearing in Lemma
3.6
. For this set, we split it up into different pieces, as follows. Define

S_{x}=\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime})\in S:\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{2})=x\}\text{ and }S_{y}=\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime})\in S:\sq(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{2})=y\}.

We have the following bounds on
S_{x}
’s and
S_{y}
’s, all of which are conditioned on GRH. The proof of each of the following three proposition will be its own section. Together, these bounds will be more than enough to give our upper bound on
S
. In particular, we do not need the full power of these propositions.

Proposition 3.8
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2\leq x\leq B^{2/3-\epsilon}\\
x\text{ squarefree}\end{subarray}}|S_{x}|=O_{\epsilon}(B\log B).

This is done in Section
4
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-1\geq x\geq-B^{2/5-\epsilon}\\

This is done in Section
5
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\sum_{1\leq y\leq B^{5/19-\epsilon}}|S_{y}|=O_{\epsilon}(B\log B).

This is done in Section
6
.

With these propositions, we may prove the upper bound. These three propositions give us bounds on
|S_{x}|
for
|x|\leq B^{2/5-\epsilon}
and
|S_{y}|
for
|y|\leq B^{5/19-\epsilon}
. Thus, it suffices to show

S\subset\bigcup_{|x|\leq B^{2/5-\epsilon}}S_{x}\cup\bigcup_{y\leq B^{5/19-\epsilon}}S_{y}

for some small
\epsilon
. Indeed, let
(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime})
be an element of
S
, let
x=\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})

The combination of these three theorems show that the diagonal

is an “accumulating substack.” Indeed, on this subvariety, the asymptotic of the point counts are on the order of

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We wish to show

|\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B,\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}|

is asymptotically upper bounded by

|\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})\notin\{0,1\},\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq B\}|.

For the upper bound, we may forget the conditions
a>0
and
\gcd(a,b,c)=1
. Also, we may make a linear change of variables
a=\frac{c^{\prime}+a^{\prime}}{2}
,
c=\frac{c^{\prime}-a^{\prime}}{2}
, with an inverse change of variables of
a^{\prime}=a-c
,
c^{\prime}=a+c
. Then,
b^{2}-4ac=a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2}
. So there is a bijection between

\{(a,b,c):\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B\}

and

\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{Z}}:\max(|\frac{c^{\prime}+a^{\prime}}{2}|,|b|,|\frac{c^{\prime}-a^{\prime}}{2}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq B,a^{\prime}\equiv c^{\prime}\mod 2\}.

For an upper bound, we may drop the conditions
a^{\prime}\equiv c^{\prime}\mod 2
. Furthermore,

|\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})\notin\{0,1\},\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq B\}|.

\displaystyle\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle=\max(|a-c|,|b|,|a+c|)

\displaystyle\leq 2\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)

\displaystyle=2\max(|\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}|,|b|,|\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2}|)

Thus, we have that

|\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B,\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}|

is bounded by

|\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})\notin\{0,1\},\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})|}\leq 2B\}|.

Since we’re only looking for an asymptotic upper bound, we may remove the
2
in front of the
B
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

We will show that

\{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}):\gcd(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1,\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})}\leq B,a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}\equiv 5\mod 8,

\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})\equiv b^{\prime}\equiv 0\mod 2,\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})\geq 2\}

injects into

\{(a,b,c)\in{\mathbb{Z}}:a>0,\gcd(a,b,c)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}\leq B,\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)\notin\{0,1\}\}

via the map

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})\to(\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2},\frac{b^{\prime}}{2},\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}).

Assuming the map is well defined, the injection follows as this is a linear map which is even injective over the reals. So, we just need to show well-definedness.

We first check the coprime condition. Since
a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}\equiv 5\mod 8
, and
b^{\prime}
is even, we must have that
a^{\prime}
is odd. Thus,
a^{\prime 2}\equiv 1\mod 8
, and so
\frac{b^{\prime}}{2}
must be odd, otherwise
b^{\prime 2}\equiv 0\mod 8
so that
a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}\not\equiv 5\mod 8
. We also have that by similar reasoning,
c^{\prime}
must be odd. So, we have that
a^{\prime},c^{\prime}
are odd, and
b^{\prime}
is even but
\frac{b^{\prime}}{2}
is odd. Thus,
\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2},\frac{b^{\prime}}{2},\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}
are all integers. Suppose now that these integers are not coprime. Then, there is some prime
p
dividing all of them. Now since
p|\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2}
and
\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}
, we have
p|a^{\prime}
. Also, since
p|\frac{b^{\prime}}{2}
,
p|b^{\prime}
. But this contradicts the assumption that
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
are coprime.

Now we only need to check the height bound. We have

\max\left(\left\lvert\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert,\left\lvert\frac{b^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert,\left\lvert\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert\right)\leq\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime}|)

and

\frac{1}{4}(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})=(\frac{b^{\prime}}{2})^{2}-4(\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2})(\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}),

so that

|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})|=|\sqf((\frac{b^{\prime}}{2})^{2}-4(\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2})(\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}))|.

Thus,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\geq\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{\prime 2}-c^{\prime 2})|}

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(\left\lvert\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert,\left\lvert\frac{b^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert,\left\lvert\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}\right\lvert\right)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf((\frac{b^{\prime}}{2})^{2}-4(\frac{a^{\prime}-c^{\prime}}{2})(\frac{a^{\prime}+c^{\prime}}{2}))|}

\displaystyle=\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{|\sqf(b^{2}-4ac)|}

∎

4
Estimate of
|S_{x}|
for
1\leq x\leq B^{2/3-\epsilon}

In this section, we prove Proposition
3.8
. Let
F(n)
be the number of ways
n
can be written as a sum of two positive squares, and let

\displaystyle G_{x}(n)

\displaystyle=\#\{\text{ideals }I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]}:N(I)=n,I\text{ is principal}\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\omega_{x}}\#\{(c,y)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}:c^{2}+xy^{2}=n\}&x\equiv 2,3\mod 4\\
\frac{1}{\omega_{x}}\#\{(c,y)\in(\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}:c^{2}+xy^{2}=n\}&x\equiv 1\mod 4\end{cases}

We may identify
S_{x}
with

\{(a,b,c,y):\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{x}\leq B,a^{2}+b^{2}=c^{2}+xy^{2}\},

by just letting
y=\sq(a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2})
. Since
x
is positive, we have
\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\leq B/\sqrt{x}
, and so
a^{2}+b^{2}
is at most
2B/\sqrt{x}
. Then, for a fixed positive
x
, we have
|S_{x}|\leq 4\omega_{x}\sum_{n=1}^{2B/\sqrt{x}}F(n)G_{x}(n)

F(n)
is a multiplicative function, and
G_{x}(n)
is a sum of multiplicative function indexed by the elements of the class group, and so we may use the theory of
L
-functions and Tauberian theorems to get good estimates on
|S_{x}|
.

Let

L_{x}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{F(n)G_{x}(n)}{n^{s}}.

With
L_{x}(s)
, we will proceed with the following steps.

1.

We show that
L_{x}(s)
can be meromorphically continued to
\Re(s)>1/2
, with a simple pole at
s=1
.

2.

We find the residue of
L_{x}(s)
at
s=1
.

3.

We use the Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian theorem as in Theorem
8.1
to obtain an estimate on the sum of the coefficients on
L_{x}(s)
, with an acceptable error term.

We may express
L_{x}(s)
as a sum of
L
-functions with Euler product. Let
\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])}
be the characters on the class group of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]
. Let
\chi\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])}
. Consider the following
L
-functions. Let

L_{0}(\chi,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{F(n)\sum_{\text{ideals }I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]},N(I)=n}\chi(I)}{n^{s}}.

Thus,

L_{x}(s)=\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])}}L_{0}(\chi,s).

We first make the following definitions.

\displaystyle P_{1,1}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ splits in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

\displaystyle P_{1,0}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ splits in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

\displaystyle P_{0,1}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and splits in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

\displaystyle P_{0,0}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

\displaystyle P_{1,ram}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ splits in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and ramifies in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

\displaystyle P_{0,ram}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[i]\text{ and ramifies in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\}

Also, we define

Z_{2,x,\chi}(s)=\begin{cases}1+\frac{\chi(\pi_{2})}{2^{s}}+\frac{\chi(\bar{\pi}_{2})}{2^{s}}+\frac{\chi(\pi_{2}^{2})}{2^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(\pi_{2}\bar{\pi}_{2})}{2^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(\bar{\pi}_{2}^{2})}{2^{2s}}+\dots&2\text{ splits as }\pi_{2}\bar{\pi}_{2}\text{ in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\\
1+\frac{\chi(\pi_{2})}{2^{s}}+\frac{\chi(\pi_{2}^{2})}{2^{2s}}+\dots&2\text{ ramifies in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\\
1+\frac{\chi(2)}{2^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(4)}{2^{4s}}+\dots&2\text{ is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}]\end{cases}

Then, we have the Euler product

\displaystyle L_{0}(\chi,s)

\displaystyle=Z_{2,x,\chi}(s)\prod_{p\in P_{1,1}}\left(1+\frac{2\chi(\pi)}{p^{s}}+\frac{2\chi(\bar{\pi})}{p^{s}}+\frac{3\chi(\pi^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{3\chi(\pi\bar{\pi})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{3\chi(\bar{\pi}^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{p\in P_{1,0}}\left(1+\frac{3\chi(p)}{p^{2s}}+\frac{5\chi(p^{2})}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right)\prod_{p\in P_{0,1}}\left(1+\frac{\chi(\pi^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(\pi\bar{\pi})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(\bar{\pi})^{2}}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{p\in P_{0,0}}\left(1+\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(p^{2})}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right)\prod_{p\in P_{1,ram}}\left(1+\frac{2\chi(\pi)}{p^{s}}+\frac{3\chi(\pi^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{p\in P_{0,ram}}\left(1+\frac{\chi(p)}{p^{2s}}+\frac{\chi(p^{2})}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right).

(4.1)

Let
\chi_{x}
be the character associated to the Jacobi Symbol
\chi_{x}(n)=\left(\frac{x}{n}\right)
. Having obtained that
L_{x}(s)
is a sum of Euler products, we get

Lemma 4.1
.

L_{x}(s)
may be meromorphically continued to
\Re(s)>1/2
, with a simple pole at
s=1
of residue
\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])|}\Res_{s=1}L_{0}(\chi_{\triv},s)

Proof.

From the definition of
L_{x}(s)
, it suffices to meromorphically continue each
L_{0}(\chi,s)
. From the product definition of
L_{0}(\chi,s)
as in Equation
4

\prod_{p\in P_{1,1}}\left(1+\frac{2\chi(\pi)}{p^{s}}+\frac{2\chi(\bar{\pi})}{p^{s}}+\frac{3\chi(\pi^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{3\chi(\pi\bar{\pi})}{p^{2s}}+\frac{3\chi(\bar{\pi}^{2})}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right),

everything else converges absolutely for
\Re(s)>1/2
. So, it suffices to continue this product. We may rewrite this product as

\prod_{\pi,\chi_{-1}(N(\pi))=1}(1+\frac{\chi(\pi)}{N(\pi)^{s}})^{2}

up to products of the form

\prod_{p}(1+\frac{a_{p,2}}{p^{2s}}+\frac{a_{p,3}}{p^{3s}}+\dots),

such that
f(i)=\max_{p}|a_{p,i}|

x
. That is,
f(i)\leq P(i)
for some polynomial
P

x

L(\chi,s)L(\chi\chi_{-1},s)

\prod_{p}(1+\frac{a_{p,2}}{p^{2s}}+\frac{a_{p,3}}{p^{3s}}+\dots)

with coefficients of bounded growth rate. Now

for
\chi=\chi_{\triv}

\prod_{p}(1+\frac{a_{p,2}}{p^{2s}}+\frac{a_{p,3}}{p^{3s}}+\dots)

are absolutely convergent for
\Re(s)>1/2
, we have that
L_{0}(\chi,s)
is holomorphic on
\Re(s)>1/2
for
\chi\neq\chi_{\triv}
and meromorphic with a simple pole at
s=1
for
\chi=\chi_{\triv}
. Thus, as
L_{x}(s)=\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])|}\sum_{\chi}L_{0}(\chi,s)
, we have that
L_{x}(s)
is meromorphic with a simple pole only at
s=1
of residue
\Res_{s=1}L_{0}(\chi_{\triv},s)
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

\Res_{s=1}L_{x}(s)=O(\frac{L(\chi_{x},1)\prod_{p|x}(1+p^{-1})^{2}}{\sqrt{x}})

\displaystyle L_{0}(\chi_{\triv},s)

\displaystyle=Z_{2,x,\chi_{0}}(s)\prod_{p\in P_{1,1}}\left(1+\frac{4}{p^{s}}+\frac{9}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)\prod_{p\in P_{1,0}}\left(1+\frac{3}{p^{2s}}+\frac{5}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{p\in P_{0,1}}\left(1+\frac{3}{p^{2s}}+\frac{5}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right)\prod_{p\in P_{0,0}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}+\frac{1}{p^{4s}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle\prod_{p\in P_{1,ram}}\left(1+\frac{2}{p^{s}}+\frac{3}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)\prod_{p\in P_{0,ram}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right).

First, we may bound

independently of

\prod_{p\in P_{1,1}}(1-p^{-s})^{-4}(1-p^{-2s})\prod_{p\in P_{1,0}\cup P_{0,1}}(1-p^{-2s})^{-2}(1+p^{-2s})\prod_{p\in P_{0,0}}(1-p^{-2s})^{-1}

(4.2)

Then, one may check local factor by local factor that

\zeta(s)L(\chi_{-1},s)L(\chi_{x},s)L(\chi_{-1}\chi_{x},s)\prod_{p\in P_{1,1}\cup P_{0,0}}(1-p^{-2s})\prod_{p\in P_{1,0}\cup P_{0,1}}(1+p^{-2s})

Proof of Proposition

.

By Corollary

\sum_{x\geq 2,\text{ squarefree}}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}O_{\delta}\left(B\frac{L(\chi_{x},1)\prod_{p|x}(1+p^{-1})^{2}}{x}+B^{1/2+\delta}x^{-1/4+\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\geq 2,\text{ squarefree}}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}\frac{L(\chi_{x},1)\sum_{d|x}\tau(d)/d}{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}\frac{\tau(d)}{d}\sum_{x^{\prime}\geq 2,dx^{\prime}\text{ squarefree}}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}d^{-1}}\frac{L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{dx^{\prime}}

We will record the bound of the inner sum in a lemma, to be used later as well.

Lemma 4.5

\sum_{x^{\prime}\geq 2}^{X}\frac{|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{x^{\prime}}=O(\log X+d^{1/2}\log(d))

Proof.

We may use summation by parts to get

\sum_{x^{\prime}\geq 2}^{X}\frac{|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{x^{\prime}}=O\left(\sum_{k=1}^{X}\frac{\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{k^{2}}\right).

\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)=\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{dx^{\prime}}{n}\right)}{n}.

Now, we will split the infinite sum over

into two parts. The first part will be

\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{dx^{\prime}}{n}\right)}{n}=\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|O(\frac{\sqrt{dx^{\prime}}\log(dx^{\prime})}{k})=O(d^{1/2}k^{1/2}\log(dk))

(4.4)

where the first equality is obtained through summation by parts and Polya-Vinogradov. So, we are left with the other part,

\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{(\frac{dx^{\prime}}{n})}{n}.

Here, we exchange summation and consider

\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{dx^{\prime}}{n}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right).

(4.5)

We separate this sum into two cases, depending on whether
n
is a square. So, we separate Equation
4.5
into

\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is a square}}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)+\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is not a square}}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)

First we deal with the case where
n
is a square, where we are happy to use the trivial estimate
|\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)|=|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)|=1
, and we obtain

\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is a square}}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)\leq\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is a square}}^{k}\frac{k}{n}=O(\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{\sqrt{k}}\frac{k}{n^{\prime 2}})=O(k).

Now we turn to the case where
n
is not a square and estimate

\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is not a square}}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right).

Using the fact that
|\mu(x^{\prime})|=\sum_{l^{2}|x^{\prime}}\mu(l)
, we obtain

\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)=\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}\sum_{l^{2}|x^{\prime}}\mu(l)\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{\sqrt{k}}\mu(l)\sum_{x^{\prime\prime}=1}^{k/l^{2}}\left(\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{n}\right),

where in the last equality, we made the substitution
x^{\prime\prime}=l^{2}x^{\prime}
. By Polya-Vinogradov, if
n
is not a square,
\left\lvert\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k/l^{2}}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right\lvert=O(\sqrt{n}\log n)
, and a trivial estimate gives us that it is
O(k/l^{2})
. Hence,

\left\lvert\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k/l^{2}}\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)\right\lvert=O(\min(\sqrt{n}\log n,k/l^{2})).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{\sqrt{k}}\mu(l)\sum_{x^{\prime\prime}=1}^{k/l^{2}}\left(\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=O(\sum_{l=1}^{\sqrt{k}}\min(\sqrt{n}\log n,k/l^{2}))

\displaystyle=O\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\sqrt{k}/(\sqrt{n}\log n))^{1/2}}\sqrt{n}\log n+\sum_{l=\sqrt{k}/(\sqrt{n}\log n))^{1/2}}^{\sqrt{k}}k/l^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=O(k^{1/2}n^{1/4}\log^{1/2}(n))

when
n
is not a square. Using this estimate, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is not a square}}^{k}\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{d}{n}\right)\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\sum_{n=1,n\text{ is not a square}}^{k}\frac{k^{1/2}n^{1/4}\log^{1/2}(n)}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=O(k^{3/4}\log^{1/2}(k))

\displaystyle=O(k).

Adding to Equation
4.4
, we obtain

\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)=O(k+(dk)^{1/2}\log(dk)).

Finally, we sum over
k
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{X}\frac{\sum_{x^{\prime}=1}^{k}|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{X}\frac{O(k+d^{1/2}k^{1/2}\log(dk))}{k^{2}}

\displaystyle=O(\log X+d^{1/2}\log(d)).

∎

With the lemma, we have

\sum_{x^{\prime}\geq 2}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}d^{-1}}\frac{|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{x^{\prime}}=O(\log(B^{2/3-\epsilon}d^{-1}))+O(d^{1/2}\log(d)).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}\frac{\tau(d)|\mu(d)|}{d^{2}}\sum_{x^{\prime}=1,(x^{\prime},d)=1}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}d^{-1}}\frac{|\mu(x^{\prime})|L(\chi_{dx^{\prime}},1)}{x^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}\frac{\tau(d)|\mu(d)|}{d^{2}}(O(\log(B^{2/3-\epsilon}d^{-1}))+O(d^{1/2}\log(d)))

\displaystyle=O(\log(B^{2/3-\epsilon})+O(1)

\displaystyle=O(\log(B)).

∎

5
Estimate of
|S_{x}|
for
-1\geq x\geq B^{2/5-\epsilon}

In this section, we prove Proposition
3.9
. Recall the definition

S_{x}=\{(a^{\prime},b,c^{\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2})=x,\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|x|}\leq B\}.

We will introduce a new set
S_{x}^{\prime}
which is easier to count and satisfies
|S_{x}|\leq|S_{x}^{\prime}|
. We first write
a^{\prime 2}+b^{2}-c^{\prime 2}=xy^{2}
, for some
y
. Rearranging the terms, we get
a^{\prime 2}-xy^{2}=c^{\prime 2}-b^{2}=(c^{\prime}-b)(c^{\prime}+b)
. Making the linear change of variables
b^{\prime}=c^{\prime}-b
and
c^{\prime\prime}=c^{\prime}+b
, we have that
S_{x}
is in bijection with
\{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}-b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime})=x,\max(|a^{\prime}|,|\frac{c^{\prime\prime}-b^{\prime}}{2}|,|\frac{c^{\prime\prime}+b^{\prime}}{2}|)^{2}\sqrt{|x|}\leq B,c^{\prime\prime}\equiv b^{\prime}\mod 2\}
. For the sake of an upper bound, we may forget the condition
c^{\prime\prime}\equiv b^{\prime}\mod 2
, and also we have that

\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime\prime}|)=\max(|a^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime}-b|,|c^{\prime}+b|)\leq 2\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b|,|c^{\prime}|)=2\max(|a^{\prime}|,|\frac{c^{\prime\prime}-b^{\prime}}{2}|,|\frac{c^{\prime\prime}+b^{\prime}}{2}|).

Hence, defining

S_{x}^{\prime}:=\{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}):\sqf(a^{\prime 2}-b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime})=x,\max(|a^{\prime}|,|b^{\prime}|,|c^{\prime\prime}|)^{2}\sqrt{|x|}\leq B\},

we have that
|S_{x}|\leq|S_{x}^{\prime}|
, and so now it suffices to bound
S_{x}^{\prime}
.

Note that by definition of
S_{x}^{\prime}
, any
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})\in S_{x}^{\prime}
must satisfy that
b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}\leq B/\sqrt{|x|}
. Now, writing
a^{\prime 2}-b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}=xy^{2}
, and rearranging, we get
b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}=a^{\prime 2}-xy^{2}
. Now define
\tau^{\prime}(n)
to be the number of divisors
d
of
n
so that both
d
and
n/d
are bounded by
(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}
. Then,
\tau^{\prime}(n)G_{-x}(n)
is the number of solutions
a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime},y
to the equation
b^{\prime}c^{\prime\prime}=a^{\prime 2}-xy^{2}=n
. So, for a fixed negative
x
, we have
|S_{x}^{\prime}|=\sum_{n=1}^{B/\sqrt{|x|}}\tau^{\prime}(n)G_{-x}(n)
. However,
\tau^{\prime}
is not a multiplicative function like
F(n)
, so we will approach this summation slightly differently. Denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{[1,(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}]}
the characteristic function of the interval
[1,(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}]
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{B/\sqrt{|x|}}\tau^{\prime}(n)G_{-x}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{B/\sqrt{|x|}}G_{-x}(n)\sum_{d|n}\mathbbm{1}_{[1,(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}]}(d)\mathbbm{1}_{[1,(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}]}(n/d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{(B/\sqrt{|x|})/d}G_{-x}(dn^{\prime})\mathbbm{1}_{[1,(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}]}(n^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=1}^{(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{(B/\sqrt{|x|})^{1/2}}G_{-x}(dn^{\prime}).

Now we will use the theory of
L
-functions and Tauberian theorems to estimate the inner sum, and subsequently the outer sum.

Let
x^{\prime}=-x
. We need to estimate

\sum_{d=1}^{(B/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})^{1/2}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{(B/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})^{1/2}}G_{x^{\prime}}(dn^{\prime})

as
x^{\prime}
ranges between the squarefree numbers in the interval
[2,B^{2/5-\epsilon}]
. Fixing
d
, we need to find an estimate on

\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{(B/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})^{1/2}}G_{x^{\prime}}(dn^{\prime}).

We do so by considering the
L
-function
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{x^{\prime},d,n}}{n^{s}}
, where

a_{x^{\prime},d,n}=\begin{cases}|\{\text{ideals }I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]}:N(I)=n,I\text{ is principal}\}|&d|n\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

so that

\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{(B/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})^{1/2}}G_{x^{\prime}}(dn^{\prime})=\sum_{n=1}^{d(B/\sqrt{x^{\prime}})^{1/2}}a_{x^{\prime},d,n}.

We will apply the Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian theorem here. Thus, as we did in the previous section, we will do the following.

1.

We show that
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
can be meromorphically continued to
\Re(s)>1/2
, with a simple pole at
s=1
.

2.

We find the residue of
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
at
s=1
.

3.

We use the Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian theorem as in Theorem
8.1
to obtain an estimate on the sum of the coefficients on
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
, with an acceptable error term.

We will follow similar steps as the last section. Let
\chi\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])}
. Then, define
L_{d}(\chi,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{\chi,d,n}}{n^{s}}
, where

a_{\chi,d,n}=\begin{cases}\sum_{\text{ideals }I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]}:N(I)=n}\chi(I)&d|n\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

so that
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)=\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}}L_{d}(\chi,s).

Let

\displaystyle P_{1}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ splits in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]\}

\displaystyle P_{0}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ is inert in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]\}

\displaystyle P_{ram}=\{\text{primes }p|p\text{ ramifies in }{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]\}.

Also let
v_{p}(d)
denote the exponent of
p
in
d
.

Define

L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)=\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])}}L_{d}(\chi,s)

where

L_{d}(\chi,s)=\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\chi(\pi^{j}\bar{\pi}^{i-j})}{p^{is}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\sum_{i=\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(p^{2i})}{p^{2is}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(\pi^{i})}{p^{is}},

where
\pi,\bar{\pi}
are primes lying above
p
.

There are two remarks on this function. One remark is that
L_{1}(\chi,s)
is just the Hecke L-function
L(\chi,s)
. The second remark on this function is that there is a relationship between
L_{1}(\chi,s)
and
L_{d}(\chi,s)
given by

\displaystyle L_{d}(\chi,s)

\displaystyle=L_{1}(\chi,s)\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\frac{\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\chi(\pi^{j}\bar{\pi}^{i-j})}{p^{is}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{i}\chi(\pi^{j}\bar{\pi}^{i-j})}{p^{is}}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{\sum_{i=\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(p^{2i})}{p^{2is}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(p^{2i})}{p^{2is}}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\frac{\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(\pi^{i})}{p^{is}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(\pi^{i})}{p^{is}}}

\displaystyle=L_{1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,0}(\chi,s)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,s),

where
Q_{d,1}
,
Q_{d,0}
, and
Q_{d,ram}
are holomorphic over
Re(s)>0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
is meromorphic over
Re(s)>0
, and only has a pole at
s=1
of residue
\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}\Res_{s=1}L_{d}(\chi_{\triv},s)
.

Proof.

L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
is an average of
L_{d}(\chi,s)
as
\chi
varies over
\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])}
. Now
L_{d}(\chi,s)=L_{1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,0}(\chi,s)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,s)
, with each factor holomorphic over
Re(s)>0
, except possibly
L_{1}(\chi,s)
when
\chi=\chi_{\triv}
, in which case there is a pole at
s=1
. Thus, the residue of
L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)
at
s=1
is
\frac{1}{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}\Res_{s=1}L_{d}(\chi_{\triv},s)
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

\Res_{s=1}L_{x^{\prime},d}(s)=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}\right).

Proof.

Since we’re looking at the residue at
1
, and by Lemma
5.1
, we just need to look at
L_{d}(\chi_{\triv},s)
. We have

L_{d}(\chi_{\triv},s)=L_{1}(\chi_{\triv},s)Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},s)Q_{d,0}(\chi_{\triv},s)Q_{d,ram}(\chi_{\triv},s).

Since all factors besides
L_{1}(\chi_{\triv},s)
are entire, we focus on the residue of
L_{1}(\chi_{\triv},s)
at
1
. However, this is just the Dedekind zeta function of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]
. Thus, by the class number formula,
L_{1}(\chi_{\triv},s)
has residue at
1
bounded by
C_{0}\frac{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}
, where
C_{0}
is a constant independent of
x^{\prime}
. Thus,
L_{d}(\chi_{\triv},1)
has residue bounded by

C_{0}\frac{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,0}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,ram}(\chi_{\triv},1).

Thus,
L_{d,x^{\prime}}(s)
has residue bounded by

C_{0}\frac{Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,0}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,ram}(\chi_{\triv},1)}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}.

Now, we investigate
Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},1)
. A local factor of
Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},1)
is of the form
\frac{\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}
, and we have the bound

\frac{\sum_{i=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}=p^{-v_{p}(d)}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{v_{p}(d)+i+1}{p^{i}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}=(v_{p}(d)+1)p^{-v_{p}(d)}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1+i/(v_{p}(d)+1)}{p^{i}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{i+1}{p^{i}}}\leq(v_{p}(d)+1)p^{-v_{p}(d)}.

Similar analyses of
Q_{d,0}(\chi_{\triv},1)
and
Q_{d,ram}(\chi_{\triv},1)
yields that

Q_{d,1}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,0}(\chi_{\triv},1)Q_{d,ram}(\chi_{\triv},1)\leq\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon_{1}>0
,

L_{x^{\prime},d}(\chi,1/2+it)=O_{\epsilon_{1}}(|x^{\prime}t|^{\epsilon_{1}}d^{-1/2}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}).

Proof.

Since
L_{x^{\prime},d}
is an average of
L_{d}(\chi,s)
, we just need to show the same bound for
L_{d}(\chi,s)
.

L_{d}(\chi,s)=L_{1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,1}(\chi,s)Q_{d,0}(\chi,s)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,s).

We will bound
L_{1}(\chi,1/2+it)
and
Q_{d,1}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,0}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,1/2+it)
separately.

The analytic conductor of
L_{1}(\chi,1/2+it)
is
x^{\prime}
. Since we are assuming GRH, we may use
[
7
, Cor. 5.20]
to obtain
L_{1}(\chi,1/2+it)=O_{\epsilon_{1}}(|x^{\prime}t|^{\epsilon_{1}})
.

For
Q_{d,1}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,0}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,1/2+it)
, we will bound as follows. Consider one local factor from
Q_{d,1}
. For example,

\frac{\sum_{j=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{l=0}^{j}\chi(\pi^{l}\bar{\pi}^{j-l})}{p^{(1/2+it)j}}}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sum_{l=0}^{j}\chi(\pi^{l}\bar{\pi}^{j-l})}{p^{(1/2+it)j}}}.

We may upper bound the numerator by

\sum_{j=v_{p}(d)}^{\infty}\frac{j+1}{p^{j/2}}=\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{j+1+v_{p}(d)}{p^{j/2}}=\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}}(\frac{1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{2}}+\frac{v_{p}(d)}{1-p^{-1/2}}).

As for the denominator, we may write it as

(1-\chi(\pi)p^{-1/2+it})^{-1}(1-\overline{\chi(\pi)}p^{-1/2+it})^{-1},

with the individual products lower bounded by
(1+p^{-1/2})^{-1}
, and so we obtain a upper bound of

\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}}(1+p^{-1/2})^{2}((1-p^{-1/2})^{-2}+v_{p}(d)(1-p^{-1/2})^{-1})\leq\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}

for the local factor at
p
. So,
Q_{d,1}(\chi,1/2+it)
may be bounded by

\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{1}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}.

We may apply a similar argument to
Q_{d,0}
and upper bound
Q_{d,0}(\chi,1/2)
by

\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}\frac{(1-p^{-1})^{-1}}{(1+p^{-1})^{-1}}\leq\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}(1-p^{-1})^{-2}\leq\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{0}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}.

As for
Q_{d,ram}
, we obtain an upper bound of

\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{ram}}\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}}(1-p^{-1/2})^{-2}\leq\prod_{p|d,p\in P_{ram}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)/2}(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}.

Thus, we obtain that

|Q_{d,1}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,0}(\chi,1/2+it)Q_{d,ram}(\chi,1/2+it)|\leq d^{-1/2}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}

∎

We now have all the ingredients for the Wiener-Ikehara Tauberian Theorem.

Proposition 5.4
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon_{1}>0
,

\sum_{d=1}^{X}\sum_{n=1}^{X}G_{x^{\prime}}(dn)=O_{\epsilon_{1}}\left(\frac{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}{x^{\prime}\prod_{p|x^{\prime}}(1-1/p)}X^{2}+x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}X^{3/2+\epsilon_{1}}\right).

Proof.

Using the conclusions of Lemmas
5.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
, we may apply Theorem
8.1
in the appendix, with
b=1
,
a=1
,
\kappa=1
, and
r=d^{-1/2}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}
. We then obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{X}G_{x^{\prime}}(dn)=O_{\epsilon_{1}}(\frac{Xd}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}+x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}(Xd)^{1/2+\epsilon_{1}}d^{-1/2}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}).

First we deal with the first term.

\sum_{d=1}^{X}\frac{Xd}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}\prod_{p\in P_{1}}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil}}\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\frac{1}{p^{v_{p}(d)}}=\frac{X}{\sqrt{x^{\prime}}}\sum_{d=1}^{X}\prod_{p\in P_{1}}(v_{p}(d)+1)\prod_{p\in P_{0}}\frac{1}{p^{2\lceil v_{p}(d)/2\rceil-v_{p}(d)}}.

To estimate the sum, we may again use the Tauberian theorem (Theorem
8.1
) associated to the
L
-function given by the Euler product

\prod_{p\in P_{1}}(1+\frac{2}{p^{s}}+\frac{3}{p^{2s}}+\dots)\prod_{p\in P_{0}}(1+\frac{2}{p^{2s}}+\frac{2}{p^{4s}}+\dots).

But this is almost exactly the Dedekind Zeta Function of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]
. Indeed, the Dedekind Zeta Function of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}]
is

\prod_{p\in P_{1}}(1+\frac{2}{p^{s}}+\frac{3}{p^{2s}}+\dots)\prod_{p\in P_{0}}(1+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}+\frac{1}{p^{4s}}+\dots)\prod_{p\in P_{ram}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{s}}+\frac{1}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right).

So, by the class number formula and the Tauberian theorem,

Thus, our final estimate for the first term is

O_{\epsilon_{1}}\left(\frac{|\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x^{\prime}}])|}{x^{\prime}\prod_{p|x^{\prime}}(1-1/p)}X^{2}+X^{3/2+\epsilon_{1}}x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}\right)

Now we deal with the second term,

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{X}x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}(Xd)^{1/2+\epsilon_{1}}d^{-1/2}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}

\displaystyle=x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}X^{1/2+\epsilon_{1}}\sum_{d=1}^{X}d^{\epsilon_{1}}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq x^{\prime\epsilon_{1}}X^{1/2+2\epsilon_{1}}\sum_{d=1}^{X}\prod_{p|d}\frac{v_{p}(d)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}.

For the summation, we may again use the Tauberian theorem on the
L
-function associated to the Euler product

\prod_{p}\left(1+\frac{2(1-p^{-1/2})^{-4}}{p^{s}}+\frac{3(1-p^{-1/2})^{-4}}{p^{2s}}+\dots\right)

Given that

-function associated to the product

The Tauberian theorem (Theorem

) with

then gives us something on the order of

. So, the final estimate of the second term is

.

Consequently,

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1,r\equiv(c/l)^{2}\mod(y/l)^{2}}^{X/l^{2}}F(rl^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\phi(y^{2}/l^{2})}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{({\mathbb{Z}}/(y^{2}/l^{2}){\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}}}\chi((c/l)^{2})\sum_{r=1}^{X/l^{2}}F(rl^{2})\overline{\chi}(r).

Proposition 6.1

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
l|y
. Let
\chi\in\widehat{({\mathbb{Z}}/(y/l)^{2}{\mathbb{Z}})}
be any character. Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon>0

Proof.

First we have

L^{l}(\chi,s)=L_{{\mathbb{Q}}[i]}(\chi_{\adelic},s)l^{-2s}\prod_{p|l,p\equiv 1\mod 4}Q_{\chi,l,p}(s).

For
L_{{\mathbb{Q}}[i]}(\chi_{\adelic},s)
, we may use the GRH to obtain a bound of
(\frac{y|s|}{l})^{\epsilon}
on the line
\Re(s)=1/2
. For
\prod_{p|l}Q_{\chi,l,p}(s)
, we have that for
s
on the line
\Re(s)=1/2

\displaystyle|Q_{\chi,l,p}(s)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(2v_{p}(l)+1+i)\chi(p^{i})/p^{is}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\chi(p^{i})/p^{is}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})(1-\overline{\chi(p)}p^{-s})\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(2v_{p}(l)+1+i)\chi(p^{i})/p^{is}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2v_{p}(l)+1}{(1+p^{-1/2})^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)p^{-i/2}

\displaystyle=(2v_{p}(l)+1)\frac{(1+p^{-1/2})^{2}}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{2}}\leq\frac{2v_{p}(l)+1}{(1-p^{-1/2})^{4}}

Lemma 6.3
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon>0

Proof.

Recall that
\sum_{r=1}^{X/l^{2}}F(rl^{2})\overline{\chi}(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{X}a^{l}_{\chi,n}
. So, we shall estimate the latter sum. For the rest of this proof, let
a_{n}:=a^{l}_{\chi,n}
. Let
X^{\prime}
be the closest half-integer to
X
. Perron’s formula
[
10

gives us that

\sum_{n=1}^{X^{\prime}}a_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{2-iT}^{2+iT}L^{l}(\chi,s)\frac{X^{\prime s}}{s}ds+R,

R=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{X^{\prime}/2<n<X^{\prime}}a_{n}\min(1,\frac{1}{T\log\frac{X^{\prime}}{n}})-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{X^{\prime}<n<2X^{\prime}}a_{n}\min(1,\frac{1}{T\log\frac{n}{X^{\prime}}})+O(\frac{X^{\prime 2}}{T}\sum_{n}\frac{|a_{n}|}{n^{2}}).

Since we took

\log\frac{X^{\prime}}{n}
and

\log(\frac{X^{\prime}}{X^{\prime}-1/2})\gg\frac{1}{X^{\prime}}.

After plugging in

Lemma 6.4

Let

be any function. Then,

\sum_{m=1,(m,q)=1}^{q}\left\lvert\sum_{\chi}f(\chi)\chi(m)\right\lvert^{2}=\phi(q)\sum_{\chi}|f(\chi)|^{2}.

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1,(m,q)=1}^{q}\left\lvert\sum_{\chi}f(\chi)\chi(m)\right\lvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\sum_{\chi^{\prime}}f(\chi)\overline{f(\chi^{\prime})}\sum_{m=1,(m,q)=1}^{q}\chi(m)\overline{\chi^{\prime}(m)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\chi}\sum_{\chi^{\prime}}f(\chi)\overline{f(\chi^{\prime})}\begin{cases}\phi(q)&\chi=\chi^{\prime}\\
0&\chi\neq\chi^{\prime}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\phi(q)\sum_{\chi}|f(\chi)|^{2}

Proof of Proposition

(m,(y/l)^{2})=1\end{subarray}}^{(y/l)^{2}}\left\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\equiv m^{2}\mod(y/l)^{2}\end{subarray}}^{X/l^{2}}F(rl^{2})-\frac{X}{\phi((y/l)^{2})}\Res_{s=1}L^{l}(\chi_{\triv},s)\right\lvert^{2}}

Now

({\mathbb{Z}}/(y/l)^{2}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}\cong\prod_{p|(y/l)^{2}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{v_{p}((y/l)^{2})}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}.

Thus, by the structure of unit groups of the form
({\mathbb{Z}}/p^{i}{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}
, we have that as
m

1
and
(y/l)^{2}

(m,(y/l)^{2})=1

\leq(N-M)^{1/2}\bigg\lceil\frac{N-M}{(y/l)^{2}}\bigg\rceil^{1/2}\sqrt{2^{\omega(y/l)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

(m,(y/l)^{2})=1\end{subarray}}^{(y/l)^{2}}\left\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\

(6.1)

using the fact that

.

Next, apply Lemma
6.4

f(\chi)=\frac{1}{\phi((y/l)^{2})}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{X/l^{2}}F(rl^{2})\overline{\chi}(r)-E_{0}(\chi)X\right).

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

where the second to last inequality is by Lemma

For the next steps, we need a bound on the main term, specifically
\Res_{s=1}L^{l}(\chi_{\triv},s)

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\chi_{\triv}\in\widehat{({\mathbb{Z}}/(y/l)^{2}{\mathbb{Z}})}
be the trivial character. Then,

\Res_{s=1}L^{l}(\chi_{\triv},s)=O(\frac{\prod_{p|l,p\equiv 1\mod 4}(2v_{p}(l)+1)}{l^{2}})

Proof.

Since

L^{l}(\chi_{\triv},s)=\zeta_{{\mathbb{Q}}[i]}(s)l^{-2s}\prod_{p|l,p\equiv 1\mod 4}Q_{\chi_{\triv},l,p}(s),

the residue at
s=1
is

(\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{Q[i]}(s))\prod_{p|l,p\equiv 1\mod 4}Q_{\chi_{\triv},l,p}(1)

which by the class number formula is

\frac{\pi}{l^{2}}\prod_{p|l,p\equiv 1\mod 4}Q_{\chi_{\triv},l,p}(1).

Finally, we have

\displaystyle|Q_{\chi_{triv},l,p}(1)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(2v_{p}(l)+1+i)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(2v_{p}(l)+1+i)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}\right|}{\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}\right|}

\displaystyle\leq(2v_{p}(l)+1)\frac{\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}\right|}{\left|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\chi_{triv}(p^{i})/p^{i}\right|}

\displaystyle=2v_{p}(l)+1

Taking the product over
p|l
and
p\equiv 1\mod 4
, we obtain our bound.

∎

We will also need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 6.6

(Erdos-Turan inequality)
.

Fix
y
and
l|y
. Let
i,j\in[0,y)
be any real number. Then,

\#\{c\in[i,j]:l|c,(c/l,y/l)=1\}=\frac{\phi(y/l)}{y}(j-i)+O(\sqrt{\phi(y/l)}).

Proof.

This is an application of the Erdos-Turan inequality to the probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1)
given by
\mu(S)=\frac{1}{\phi(y/l)}\#\{x\in S:yx\in{\mathbb{Z}},l|yx,(yx/l,y/l)=1\}
.
∎

6.2

Lemma 6.8
.

Fix any
c
. Suppose
r>c^{2}
. Then,
(r+c^{2})\sqrt{|r-c^{2}|}\leq By
implies that
r\geq c^{2}-\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}
and
r\leq c^{2}+\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}
.

Proof.

For the lower bound, we have

c^{2}\sqrt{c^{2}-r}\leq(r+c^{2})\sqrt{|r-c^{2}|}\leq By.

Thus,
c^{4}(c^{2}-r)\leq(By)^{2}
. Thus,
r\geq c^{2}-\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}

For the upper bound, we assume
r>c^{2}
. Thus, we have
2c^{2}\sqrt{r-c^{2}}\leq(r+c^{2})\sqrt{|r-c^{2}|}\leq By
. Hence
4c^{4}(r-c^{2})\leq(By)^{2}
. Thus,
r\leq c^{2}+\frac{(By)^{2}}{4c^{4}}
. In particular,
r\leq c^{2}+\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}
.
∎

From this lemma, we see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r,c)\in R_{y}\\

\displaystyle+\sum_{l|y,l>y^{3/5}}\sum_{i=0}^{B^{1/2-\delta}/y^{11/10}}O_{\epsilon}(y^{-1/5+\epsilon}((By)^{1/3}+iy^{4/5})(By)^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle+\sum_{l|y,l>y^{3/5}}O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon}y^{-1/5+2\epsilon}((By)^{1/3}+\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}})(\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{11/10}}))

Now on the regime we are considering,
y\leq B^{5/19-\epsilon}
. This is exactly equivalent to
(By)^{1/3}\leq\frac{B^{1/2-\epsilon}}{y^{3/10}}
. Now, since we are choosing
\delta\leq\epsilon
, we see that
(By)^{1/3}\leq\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle O_{\epsilon}(l^{-1}B^{\epsilon}y^{2/5+2\epsilon}((By)^{1/3}+\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}})(\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{11/10}}))

\displaystyle=O_{\epsilon}(l^{-1}B^{\epsilon}y^{2/5+2\epsilon}(\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}})(\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{11/10}}))

\displaystyle=O_{\epsilon}(l^{-1}B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon}y^{-1+2\epsilon}).

Similarly,

O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon}y^{-1/5+2\epsilon}((By)^{1/3}+\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}})(\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{11/10}}))=O_{\epsilon}(B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon}y^{-8/5+2\epsilon})

With this, we continue our bound and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{l|y}\sum_{i=0}^{B^{1/2-\delta}/y^{11/10}}E_{i,l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l|y,l\leq y^{3/5}}O_{\epsilon}(l^{-1}B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon}y^{-1+2\epsilon})+\sum_{l|y,l>y^{3/5}}O_{\epsilon}(B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon}y^{-8/5+2\epsilon})

\displaystyle=O_{\epsilon}(B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon}y^{-1+3\epsilon})

∎

6.3
\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}\leq c\leq B^{1/2}

In this range, we may use a trivial estimate.

Lemma 6.10
.

Assuming GRH, for all
\epsilon,\delta>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r,c)\in R_{y}\\
\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}\leq c\leq B^{1/2}\end{subarray}}F(r)=O_{\epsilon}(B^{1/2+\epsilon+3\delta}y^{9/10})

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(r,c)\in R_{y}\\
\frac{B^{1-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}\leq c\leq B^{1/2}\end{subarray}}F(r)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}\leq c\leq B^{1/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=c^{2}-\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}\\
r\equiv c^{2}\mod y^{2}\end{subarray}}^{c^{2}+\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}}F(r)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}\leq c\leq B^{1/2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=c^{2}-\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}\\
r\equiv c^{2}\mod y^{2}\end{subarray}}^{c^{2}+\frac{(By)^{2}}{c^{4}}}O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\frac{B^{1/2-\delta}}{y^{3/10}}<c\leq B^{1/2}}O_{\epsilon}(B^{\epsilon})\lceil\frac{B^{2}}{c^{4}}\rceil

\displaystyle=O_{\epsilon}(B^{1/2+\epsilon+3\delta}y^{9/10})

∎

6.4
Proof of Proposition
3.10

Now we may prove our proposition.

Proof of Proposition
3.10
.

By lemmas
6.7
,
6.9
, and
6.10
, we have that

|S_{y}^{\prime}|=O_{\epsilon_{0},\delta}(\frac{B}{y}\sum_{l|y}\frac{1}{l}\prod_{p|l}(2v_{p}(l)+1)+B^{1-2\delta+\epsilon_{0}}y^{-1+3\epsilon_{0}}+(By)^{1/2+\epsilon_{0}}+B^{2/3+\epsilon_{0}}y^{-1/3+\epsilon_{0}}+B^{1/2+\epsilon_{0}+3\delta}y^{9/10}),

for any
\epsilon_{0}\geq\delta>0
. We now make our choice of
\delta
and
\epsilon_{0}
to be
\epsilon/4
. Then, we may sum these terms over
y\leq B^{5/19-\epsilon}
and obtain that all terms are
O(B)
except for the first term, which we will now examine.

We want to bound

\sum_{y=1}^{B^{5/19-\epsilon}}\frac{B}{y}\sum_{l|y}\frac{1}{l}\prod_{p|l}(2v_{p}(l)+1).

It suffices to show

\sum_{y=1}^{B}\frac{1}{y}\sum_{l|y}\frac{1}{l}\prod_{p|l}(2v_{p}(l)+1)=O(\log B).

Now we have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{y=1}^{B}\frac{1}{y}\sum_{l|y}\frac{1}{l}\prod_{p|l}(2v_{p}(l)+1)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y=1,\exists p\leq B,p|y}^{\infty}\frac{1}{y}\sum_{l|y}\frac{1}{l}\prod_{p|l}(2v_{p}(l)+1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\leq B}\left(1+\frac{1+\frac{3}{p}}{p}+\frac{1+\frac{3}{p}+\frac{5}{p^{2}}}{p^{2}}+\dots\right).

Let

M=1+\frac{3}{2}+\frac{5}{2^{2}}+\frac{7}{2^{3}}+\dots.

Then, the Euler product is bounded by

\displaystyle\prod_{p\leq B}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{3}{p^{2}}+\frac{M}{p^{2}}+\frac{M}{p^{3}}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle=O(\prod_{p\leq B}(1+\frac{1}{p}))

\displaystyle=e^{\sum_{p\leq B}\log(1+1/p)+O(1)}

\displaystyle=e^{\sum_{p\leq B}\frac{1}{p}+O(1)}

\displaystyle=O(e^{\log\log B})

\displaystyle=O(\log B).

∎

The goal of this section is to prove Proposition
7.5
. Recall

T:=\{(a,b,c):\gcd(a,b)=1,\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)^{2}\sqrt{x}\leq B,a^{2}+b^{2}\equiv 5\mod 8,

\sqf(a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2})\equiv b\equiv 0\mod 2\}.

We will lower bound
|T|
by splitting it up into pieces, as follows. Define
T_{x}=\{(a,b,c)\in T:\sqf(a^{2}+b^{2}-c^{2})=x\}
, so that
\bigsqcup_{x=2}^{B^{2/3-\epsilon}}T_{x}\subset T
for any
\epsilon>0
. Also define

to be the number of ways
n

|T_{x}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{2B/\sqrt{x}}F^{\prime}(n)G_{x}(n)

where
1/2
comes from the fact that in every representation of
n
as a sum of two coprime squares, one of them is even and one of them is odd but we require
b
to be the even one.

To analyze
|T_{x}|
, we will consider the following
L
-function,

L_{x,lower}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n^{s}},

where
a_{n}=\begin{cases}F^{\prime}(n)G_{x}(n)&n\equiv 5\mod 8\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}
. Note that this
L
-function is extremely similar to
L_{x}(s)
, and so we will apply the same process to
L_{x,lower}

\widehat{({\mathbb{Z}}/8{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}}
denote the characters on the multiplicative group of

. Then,

L_{x,lower}(s)=\frac{1}{|({\mathbb{Z}}/8{\mathbb{Z}})^{\times}||\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi_{1}\in\widehat{\Cl({\mathbb{Q}}[\sqrt{-x}])}\\

\displaystyle L_{0,lower}(\chi,s)=

Here we make the abuse of notation for

is defined by

Appendix A

The following appendix almost follows word for word
[
4
, Appendix A]
. The only difference is that we kept track of the error dependence on the L-function.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(\lambda_{n})
be an increasing sequence of positive real numbers. Let
(c_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative real numbers. Define

f(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{s}}.

We make the following hypotheses

1.

f
converges on the half-plane
\Re(s)>a>0
;

2.

f
admits a meromorphic continuation to
\Re(s)>a-\delta_{0}>0
;

3.

and on this domain, there is a unique pole at
s=a
, of multiplicity
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We note that
\Theta=\lim_{s\to a}f(s)(s-a)^{b}>0
.

4.

Finally, we assume there exists positive constants
r,\kappa
so that

|f(s)\frac{(s-a)^{b}}{s^{b}}|\leq r(1+\Im(s))^{\kappa}.

Then, there exists a monic polynomial
P
of degree
b-1
so that for
\delta\leq\delta_{0}
, as
X
tends to
\infty
, we have

N(X):=\sum_{\lambda_{n}\leq X}c_{n}=\frac{\Theta}{a(b-1)!}X^{a}P(\log(X))+O(rX^{a-\delta}).

We define, for positive integers
k
, the function

\phi_{k}(X)=\sum_{\lambda_{n}\leq X}a_{n}(\log(X/\lambda_{n}))^{k},

and define
\phi_{0}(X)=N
.

Lemma 8.2
.

With the hypothesis of the previous theorem, there exists for integers
k>\kappa
a polynomial
Q
of degree
b-1
with leading coefficient
\frac{k!\Theta}{a^{k+1}(b-1)!}
so that for
\delta<\delta_{0}
, we have

\phi_{k}(X)=X^{a}Q(\log X)+O(rX^{a-\delta}),

where the implied constant is independent of
f
.

Proof.

For any
a^{\prime}>a
, we have

\int_{a^{\prime}+i{\mathbb{R}}}\lambda^{s}\frac{ds}{s^{k+1}}=\frac{2\pi i}{k!}(\log^{+}(\lambda))^{k},\lambda>0.

Consequently,

\phi_{k}(X)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int_{a^{\prime}+i{\mathbb{R}}}f(s)X^{s}\frac{ds}{s^{k+1}},

where the integral is absolutely convergent as
k>\kappa
.

We now shift the contour of integration to
\Re(s)=a-\delta
. We pick up a pole at
s=a
with residue
\frac{\Theta}{a^{k+1}(b-1)!}X^{a}Q(\log X)
, where
Q
is a monic polynomial of degree
b-1
. The resulting integral

\int_{\Re(s)=a-\delta}f(s)X^{s}\frac{ds}{s^{k+1}}

is an error term, and here we use the bound
|f(s)\frac{(s-a)^{b}}{s^{b}}|\leq r(1+\Im(s))^{\kappa}
and obtain that the integral is
O(rX^{a-\delta})
.
∎

Now we may prove Theorem
8.1
.

Proof.

We prove by inductive descent on
k
that the previous lemma is in fact true for all integers
k
. For
k=0
, the theorem is trivially proven. Now assuming the previous lemma holds for some fixed
k\geq 1
, we show that it is in fact true for
k-1
.

For any
\eta\in(0,1)
, we have the inequality

\frac{\phi_{k}(X(1-\eta))-\phi_{k}(X)}{\log(1-\eta)}\leq k\phi_{k-1}(X)\leq\frac{\phi_{k}(X(1+\eta))-\phi_{k}(X)}{\log(1+\eta)}.

Now fix
\delta^{\prime}
with
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}<\delta_{0}
. By the previous lemma,

|\phi_{k}(X)-X^{a}Q(\log X)|=O(rX^{a-\delta^{\prime}}).

Consequently, for
-1<u<1
,

\frac{\phi_{k}(X(1+u))-\phi_{k}(X)}{\log(1+u)}=\frac{k!\Theta}{a^{k+1}(b-1)!}X^{a}\frac{Q(\log X+\log(1+u))(1+u)^{a}-Q(\log X)}{\log(1+u)}+R(X),

where

|R(X)|=O(\frac{2rX^{a-\delta^{\prime}}}{|\log(1+u)|})=O(r\frac{X^{a-\delta^{\prime}}}{u}).

Now we have a series of equalities.

\displaystyle\frac{Q(\log X+\log(1+u))(1+u)^{a}-Q(\log X)}{\log(1+u)}

\displaystyle=Q(\log X)\frac{(1+u)^{a}-1}{\log(1+u)}+\sum_{n=1}^{b-1}\frac{1}{n!}Q^{(n)}(\log(X))(\log(1+u)^{n-1}(1+u)^{a})

\displaystyle=Q(\log X)(a+O(u))+Q^{\prime}(\log(X))(1+O(u))+O((\log X)^{b-1}u)

\displaystyle=(aQ+Q^{\prime})(\log X)+O((\log X)^{b-1}u).

Take
u=\pm 1/X^{\epsilon}
, where
\delta^{\prime}-\epsilon>\delta
. Then, we have
|R(X)|=O(rX^{a-\delta})
and

\frac{Q(\log X+\log(1+u))(1+u)^{a}-Q(\log X)}{\log(1+u)}=(aQ+Q^{\prime})(\log X)+O(rX^{-\delta}).

Thus, we have that
\phi_{k-1}(X)=\frac{1}{k}X^{a}(aQ+Q^{\prime})(\log X)+O(rX^{a-\delta})
and the leading coefficient of
\frac{1}{k}(aQ+Q^{\prime})
is precisely
\frac{(k-1)!\Theta}{a^{k}(b-1)!}
.
∎
