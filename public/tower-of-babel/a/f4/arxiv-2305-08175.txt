An Optimal and Scalable Matrix Mechanism for Noisy Marginals under Convex Loss Functions
Yingtai Xiao, Guanlin He, Danfeng Zhang, Daniel Kifer

Abstract
Noisy marginals are a common form of confidentiality-protecting data release and are useful for many downstream tasks such as contingency table analysis, construction of Bayesian networks, and even synthetic data generation. Privacy mechanisms that provide unbiased noisy answers to linear queries (such as marginals) are known as matrix mechanisms. We propose ResidualPlanner, a matrix mechanism for marginals with Gaussian noise that is both optimal and scalable. ResidualPlanner can optimize for many loss functions that can be written as a convex function of marginal variances (prior work was restricted to just one predefined objective function). ResidualPlanner can optimize the accuracy of marginals in large scale settings in seconds, even when the previous state of the art (HDMM) runs out of memory. It even runs on datasets with 100 attributes in a couple of minutes. Furthermore ResidualPlanner can efficiently compute variance/covariance values for each marginal (prior methods quickly run out of memory, even for relatively small datasets).

Contributions.

Our paper addresses these problems with a novel matrix mechanisms called ResidualPlanner. It can optimize for a wide variety of convex objective functions and return solutions that are guaranteed to be optimal under Gaussian noise. It is highly scalable – running in seconds even when other scalable algorithms run out of memory. It also efficiently returns the variance and covariances of each cell of the desired marginals.
It leverages the following insights. Since a dataset can be represented as a vector
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
of counts, and since a marginal query on a set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
of attributes can be represented as a matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

2.1
Differential Privacy

A mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
is an algorithm whose input is a dataset and whose output provides privacy protections. Differential privacy is a family of privacy definitions that guide the behavior of mechanisms so that they can inject enough noise to mask the effects of any individual. There are many versions of differential privacy that support Gaussian noise, including approximate DP, zCDP, and Gaussian DP.

The Matrix Mechanism
[

,

,

,

is a framework for providing unbiased privacy-preserving answers to a workload of linear queries, represented by a matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}
(so that the true non-private answer to the workload queries is
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}

select
,
measure
, and

. The purpose of the
select

what
we add noise to and
how much
noise to use. More formally, when a user’s preferred noise distribution is Gaussian, the select phase chooses a Gaussian linear mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})\equiv{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{0}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}})
whose noisy output can be used to estimate the true query answer
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
. Ideally,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
uses the least amount of noise subject to privacy constraints (specified by a privacy definition and settings of its privacy parameters).
The
measure
phase runs the mechanism on the data to produce (noisy) privacy-preserving outputs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}})
. The
reconstruct
step uses
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
to compute an unbiased estimate of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
. The unbiased estimate is typically
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}})^{\dagger}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}
, where
\dagger
represents the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse. This is the best linear unbiased estimate of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
that can be obtained from
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\omega}}

[
32
]
. This means that the goal of the select step is to optimize the choice of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}
so that the reconstructed answer is as accurate as possible, subject to privacy constraints. Ideally, a user would specify their accuracy requirements using a loss function, but existing matrix mechanisms do not allow this flexibility – they hard-code the loss function. In fact, adding support for new loss function used to require significant research and new optimization algorithms
[
53
,
46
,
18
]
because each new algorithm was customized to specific properties of a chosen loss function. On top of this, existing optimal matrix mechanism algorithms do not scale, while scalable matrix mechanisms are not guaranteed to produce optimal solutions
[
37
]
. Additionally, the reconstruction phase should also compute the variance of
each
workload answer. The variances are the diagonals of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}}^{T}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}^{-1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{B}}})^{\dagger}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{W}}}^{T}
and making this computation scale is also challenging.

4
ResidualPlanner

ResidualPlanner is our proposed matrix mechanism for optimizing the accuracy of marginal queries with Gaussian noise. It is optimal and more scalable than existing approaches. It supports optimizing the accuracy of marginals under a wide variety of loss functions and provides exact variances/covariances of the noisy marginals in closed-form. In this section, we first explain the loss functions it supports. We then describe the base mechanisms it uses to answer marginal queries. We next show how to reconstruct the marginal queries from the outputs of the base mechanisms and how to compute their variances in closed form. We then explain how to optimize these base mechanisms for different loss functions. The reason this selection step is presented last is because it depends on the closed form variance calculations. Then we analyze computational complexity. To aid in understanding, we added a complete numerical run-through of the steps in Section
B
of the supplementary material. All of our proofs also appear in the supplementary material.

4.1
Loss Functions Supported by ResidualPlanner

The loss functions we consider are generalizations of the sum of variances and max of variances used in prior work. Our more general class of loss functions is based on the following principle: different marginals can have different relative importance but within a marginal, its cells are equally important. That is, a loss function can express that the two-way marginal on the attribute set
\{
Race, Marital Status
\}
is more important (i.e., requires more accuracy) than the 1-way marginal on
\{
EducationLevel
\}
, but all cells within the
\{
Race, MaritalStatus
\}
marginal are equally important. This is a commonly accepted principle for answering differentially private marginal queries (e.g.,
[
32
,
52
,
30
,
53
,
37
,
51
,
46
,
18
,
42
,
39
,
4
,
34
]
) and is certainly true for the 2020 Census redistricting data
[
2
]
.

Let
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}=\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}_{k}\}
be a workload of marginals, where each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}_{i}
is a subset of attributes and represents a marginal. E.g.,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}=\{\{

\{

consists of 2 marginals, a two-way marginal on Race/MaritalStatus, and a one-way marginal on Education. Let

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}
.
For each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}_{i}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}
, let

be the function that returns the variances of the reconstructed answers to the marginal on

with one component for each cell of the marginal on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}_{i}
. A loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
aggregates all of these vectors together:
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}}_{k})
. We have the following regularity conditions on the loss function.

Definition 4.1

(Regular Loss Function)
.

We say the loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
is

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
is convex and continuous; (2)

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}}_{1},\dots,{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}v}}_{k})

. This latter condition just says that cells within the

2.1

\gamma

\gamma

We note that regular loss functions cover other interesting cases. For instance, suppose Alicia, Bob, and Carol wish to minimize the sum of variances on their own separate workloads. Taking the max over these three sum-of-variances as the loss function allows the data curator to minimize the largest unhappiness among the three data stakeholders.

4.2
Base Mechanisms used by ResidualPlanner

In the most common application setting, the user picks a privacy budget, a workload
of marginals and a loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
. Based on these choices, a matrix mechanism must
decide what linear queries to add noise to and how much noise to add to them. Then it uses those noisy measurements to reconstruct answers to the workload queries. In the case of ResidualPlanner, the linear queries that need noise are represented by
base mechanisms
that are described in this section. Each base mechanism has a scalar noise parameter that determines how much noise it uses (so optimizing the loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
is equivalent to finding a good value for the noise parameter of each base mechanism).
As long as the loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
is regular, we prove that an optimal mechanism can be constructed from the set of base mechanisms that we describe here.

4.3
Reconstruction

Next we explain how to reconstruct unbiased answers to marginal queries from the outputs of the base mechanisms and how to compute (co)variances of the reconstructed marginals efficiently, without any heavy matrix operations (inversion, pseudo-inverses, etc.). Then, given the closed form expressions for marginals and privacy cost (Theorem
4.5
), we will be able to explain in Section
4.4
how to optimize the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
parameters of the base mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
to optimize regular loss functions
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
.

Since the base mechanisms were built using a linearly independent basis, reconstruction is unique – just efficiently invert the basis. Hence, unlike PGM and its extensions
[
41
,
40
]
, our reconstruction algorithm does not need to solve an optimization problem and can reconstruct each marginal independently, thus allowing marginals to be reconstructed in parallel, or as needed by users. The reconstructed marginals are consistent with each other (any two reconstructed marginals agree on their sub-marginals).

4.4
Optimizing the Base Mechanism Selection

We now consider how to find the optimal Gaussian linear mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
that solves the optimization problems in Equations
1
or
2
. Given a workload on marginals
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}
, the optimization involves
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Var}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}};{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*})
for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}
(the variance of the marginal answers reconstructed from the output of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
) and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}pcost}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*})
, from which the privacy parameters of different flavors of differential privacy can be computed.

Theorem
4.4
says that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
works by releasing
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})
for each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
for appropriately chosen values of
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
. The privacy cost
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}pcost}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*})
is the sum of the privacy costs of the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
. Theorem
4.5
therefore shows that
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}pcost}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*})
is a positive linear combination of the values
1/\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
and is therefore convex in the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
values. Meanwhile, Theorem
4.7
shows how to represent, for each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
, the quantity
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Var}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}};{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*})
as a positive linear combination of
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}}
for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}})\subseteq{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
. Therefore, the loss function
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}}}
is also convex in the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
values.

Thus the optimization problems in Equations
1
and
2
can be written as minimizing a convex function of the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
subject to convex constraints. In fact, in Equation

\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

1/\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

6
Limitations, Conclusion, and Future Work.

In this paper, we introduced ResidualPlanner, a matrix mechanism that is scalable and optimal for marginals under Gaussian noise, for a large class of convex objective functions. While these are important improvements to the state of the art, there are limitations.

First, for some attributes, a user might not want marginals. For example, they might want range queries or queries with hierarchies (e.g., how many people drive sedans vs. vans; out of the sedans, how many are red vs. green, etc)
[
2
,
28
,
36
]
. In some cases, an attribute might have an infinite domain (e.g., a URL) and need to be handled differently
[
27
,
45
]
. In other cases, the user may want other noise distributions, like the Laplace.
These types of queries do not have the same type of symmetry as marginals that was crucial to proving the optimality of ResidualPlanner. For these situations, one of the key ideas of ResidualPlanner can be used – find a linear basis that compactly represents both the queries and “residual” (information provided by a query that is not contained in the other queries). Such a feature would result in scalability.
It is future work to determine how to extend both scalability and optimality to such situations. Another limitation is that this work considers the setting where an individual can contribute exactly one (rather than arbitrarily many) records to the dataset.

B.3

Recall that our workload, the marginals we want privacy-preserving answers to, is
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}=\{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}\}
. Its closure, denoted by
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
is all of its subsets. So,

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})=\{~\emptyset,~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\},~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}~\}

For each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
we need to form a base mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
. Each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
has a free parameter
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
that we are free to choose. Each mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
has the form
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})={{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}+N({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{0}}},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})
. That is, on input
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
, the mechanism multiplies it by a special “residual” matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
and then adds correlated Gaussian noise, with zero mean and with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
. The residual and covariance matrices for each base mechanism are shown below.

Note that for any
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
, the residual matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
has a similar structure to
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
except that where
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
has an identity matrix in its kron product,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
has a subtraction matrix (e.g.
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
or
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1&\phantom{-}0\\
1&\phantom{-}0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]
). Meanwhile the covariance matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
looks like
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}^{T}
except that the vectors full of 1s have been first removed.

How do we interpret the residual matrices? Well,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\emptyset}
is the sum query. In fact the matrix vector multiplication
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\emptyset}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
gives us the total number of people in the data.

Next,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\}}
tells us the information contained in the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\}
that is not contained in the sum query. If we know the total number of people in the data, then the only new information the marginal gives us is the difference between the number of people with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}=a
and the number of people with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}=b
. In other words,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
is this difference. Given this difference, and the total, once can recover the marginal on attribute
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}
.

Similarly,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}
contains the information in the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}
that is not provided by the sum query. Finally
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}
contains the information in the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}
not provided in the sum query, which is the number of people with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}=1
minus the number with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}=2
, and also the number of people with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}=1
minus the number with
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}=3
. The product
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
returns those two differences as a vector with two components.

Now,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}
and
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}
are more complicated, but have the same idea. For example,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}
represents new information that the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}
provides that is not captures by the sub-marginals (the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\}
and the marginal on
\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}
.

In general, the matrix
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
represents the new information on that the marginal on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
provides, which is not captured by the marginals on
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}
, for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}\subset{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
(strict subsets).

Now, Theorem
4.2
tells us that if we take all of the rows of all of the residual matrices, they will be linearly independent. Furthermore, given an attribute set
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
, the total number of rows of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}}
for all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}\subseteq{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
is the number of rows in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
. Furthermore, the space spanned by those rows is the same as the space spanned by the rows of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
.

This also means that if we know
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
for all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}^{\prime}\subseteq{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
then we can figure out
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{Q}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}}
(and vice versa).

Now, we want to get privacy-preserving (noisy) answers to the marginal queries in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}=\{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}\}
that are as accurate as possible subject to privacy constraints. We quantify accuracy using a regular (Definition
4.1
) loss function (e.g., sum of the variances of the answers to the marginals) and we quantify privacy by setting privacy parameters for either
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy,
\rho
-zCDP, or
\mu
-Gaussian differential privacy.

Theorem
4.4
says that to maximize the accuracy subject to privacy constraints, we need to take the closure of the workload,
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})=\{~\emptyset,~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\},~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},~\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\},\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}~\}
and carefully choose positive numbers
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
for each
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
– so that is 6 numbers total. These numbers are chosen
without
looking at the data (we explain how in Section
B.6
). Once we have these numbers, we run the mechanisms
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})
and return their outputs. In other words, we must release the outputs of:

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\emptyset}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\emptyset})
– produces 1 number (a vector with just one component)

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}})
– produces 1 number (a vector with just one component)

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}})
– produces 1 number (a vector with just one component)

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}})
– produces 2 numbers (a vector with 2 components)

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{1},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}})
– produces 1 number (a vector with 1 component)

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2},{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}})
– produces 2 numbers (a vector with 2 components)

Which gives us 8 total (noisy) numbers. In fact, any matrix mechanism for this workload must return at least 8 noisy numbers, by Theorem
4.4
.

From these outputs, one can reconstruct noisy answers to the marginals in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}}
(actually one can reconstruct noisy answers to any marginal in
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})
). We show how to do this in Section
B.4
. Then we show how to compute the privacy cost and variances of the algorithm in Section
B.5
.

B.5

, we need to run a mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}pcost}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}))=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{2}\}}}*\frac{1}{2}

•

{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}pcost}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{x}}};\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}))=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{3}\}}}*\frac{2}{3}

•

B.6
The Sum-of-Variances Loss Function

Now we can express the overall privacy cost symbolically in terms of the noise scale parameters. We can also express the variance of each marginal cell symbolically. We can use these symbolic expressions to set up any regular loss function and then run it through a convex optimizer to solve it.

In this section, we give an example for the weighted sum of variances, which is one of the most popular loss functions for the matrix mechanism in research settings (mostly because this loss function is easiest to work with).

Appendix D
Optimality Proof of ResidualPlanner

In this section, we prove the optimality of ResidualPlanner. It takes advantage of the symmetry inherent in marginals and regular loss functions.

The proof sketch is the following. Given one optimal mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
, we can create a variation
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
of that does the following. (1)
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
modifies each input record by applying some invertible function
f_{i}
to each attribute
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{i}
(for example, if
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{i}
is a tertiary attribute, we can modify the value of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Att}}_{i}
for each record using a function
f_{i}
where
f_{i}(1)=3
,
f_{i}(2)=1
,
f_{i}(3)=2
). This step can be viewed as simply renaming the attribute values within an attribute. (2) Then
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
runs
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
on the resulting dataset. Note that marginals can be reconstructed from the output of
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
by first running the reconstruction one would do for
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
and then inverting the
f_{i}
functions on the resulting marginals (i.e., rearranging the cells in each marginal to undo the within-attribute renaming caused by the
f_{i}
). This variation
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
has the same privacy properties as
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
and the same loss (due to the regularity condition on the loss). Hence
\widetilde{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}}
is also optimal.
Then we create yet another optimal privacy mechanism
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
that splits the privacy budget across all variations of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}
and returns their outputs. It turns out that the privacy cost matrix of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
has eigenvectors that are equal to the rows of the residual matrices
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{R}}}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
used by ResidualPlanner. Rewriting the privacy cost matrix of
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{M}}}^{*}
using this eigendecomposition, we create another mechanism (the mechanism that runs the base mechanisms of ResidualPlanner) that has the same privacy cost matrix and the same value
for the loss and hence is optimal.

The rest of this section explains these steps in details with formal proofs and running commentary that helps to better understand the notation and constructs in the proof.

Appendix H
Closed Form Solution to the Weighted Sum of Variances Loss

By Theorem
4.5
, the privacy cost is a linear combination of the
1/\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
values. By Theorem
4.7
, each reconstructed marginal’s
cell variances are a linear combination of the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
values. Thus, minimizing the weighted sum of reconstructed marginal variances subject to the privacy cost being
\leq c
can be formulated as a problem of the following type:

\displaystyle\begin{subarray}{c}\argmin\\
{\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}:~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})}\end{subarray}

\displaystyle\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})}v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}

(8)

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}\in{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\text{closure}}}({{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Wkload}})}\frac{p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}{\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}\leq c

where the
v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
are the linear coefficients of the
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
and the
p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
are the linear coefficients of the
1/\sigma_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}^{2}
in the privacy cost. The closed form solution is given by hte following lemma.

Lemma H.1
.

Given the optimization problem in Equation
8

The optimal objective function value is
T=\left(\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sqrt{v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}\right)^{2}/c
, the optimal value of each noise scale parameter is
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}=\sqrt{Tp_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}/(cv_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})}
.

Proof.

Clearly, for the optimal solution, the inequality constraint must be tight (i.e.,
=c
) because if it is not tight, we can lower variance while increasing privacy cost by dividing each
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}
by a number
>1
. Thus we just need to solve the problem subject to
\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}/\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}=c
.

From Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}=\left(\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\right)\left(\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\frac{p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}{\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}\right)/c\geq\left(\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sqrt{v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}\right)^{2}/c=T

Equality holds when

\frac{v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}{p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}\sigma^{4}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}=t
for all
{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}
(for some constant
t
). Since
c=\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\frac{p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}{\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}}=\sum_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}\sqrt{v_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}p_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}/{t}}
, then we must have
t=T/c
. Plugging this into the definition of
t
, we get
\sigma^{2}_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}=\sqrt{Tp_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}}/(cv_{{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{A}}}})}
.∎

Thus, if the loss function is the weighted sum of variances, ResidualPlanner does not need any optimization steps. The selection of the noise scales and the reconstruction phase are direct algorithms.

I.2.2
Max Variance

The next comparison is on optimization for Max Variance. We repeat that HDMM only optimizes for RMSE and this shows that optimizing for RMSE is highly suboptimal when one cares about max variance.

In contrast to RMSE, where the optimization problem generated by ResidualPlanner’s selection step can be solved in closed form, for Max Variance, the optimization needs a convex solver. Hence we include comparisons between the open source ECOS
[
14
]
optimizer to the commercial Gurobi optimizer
[
21
]
.
Thus, our results have columns labeled ResidualPlanner+ECOS and ResidualPlanner+Gurobi.

Tables
19
,
20
and
21
show the results for the CPS, Adult, and Loans datasets, respectively.
There is one item to note about numerical stability. Although Gurobi is generally faster and more numerically stable, the differences do not matter much. Situations where EOCS was worse are highlighted in red. For example, in Table
19
for the CPS dataset,
the dataset has only 5 attributes, so a 5-way marginal is basically the entire dataset. The optimal mechanism for 5-way marginals simply adds
N(0,1)
noise to each cell and optimizing for RMSE is equal to optimizing Max Variance for this special case. As we see, the Max Variance for ResidualPlanner+ECOS is
1.008
which is
0.8\%
worse than optimal. The reason for this is the numerical precision with which ECOS can solve the optimization problem that ResidualPlanner gives it. In general, however, it looks like open source optimizers should work fairly reliably for them to be used in real applications of ResidualPlanner.
