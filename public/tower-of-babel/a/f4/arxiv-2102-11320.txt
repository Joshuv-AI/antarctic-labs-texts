A category 𝒪 {\mathcal{O}} for oriented matroids
Ethan Kowalenko, Carl Mautner

Abstract
. We associate to a sufficiently generic oriented matroid program and choice of linear system of parameters a finite dimensional algebra, whose representation theory is analogous to blocks of Bernstein–Gelfand–Gelfand category 𝒪 {\mathcal{O}} . When the data above comes from a generic linear program for a hyperplane arrangement, we recover the algebra defined by Braden–Licata–Proudfoot–Webster. Applying our construction to nonlinear oriented matroid programs provides a large new class of algebras. For Euclidean oriented matroid programs, the resulting algebras are quasi-hereditary and Koszul, as in the linear setting. In the non-Euclidean case, we obtain algebras that are not quasi-hereditary and not known to be Koszul, but still have a natural class of standard modules and satisfy numerical analogues of quasi-heredity and Koszulity on the level of graded Grothendieck groups.

1.3.
Main results

As above, let the pair
({\mathbf{P}},U)
consist of a sufficiently generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
together with a parameter space
U\subset k^{E}
for the (unoriented) matroid
M=\underline{(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f)/g}
. Modifying the definition of Braden–Licata–Proudfoot–Webster to this setting, we introduce a finite-dimensional algebra
A({\mathbf{P}},U)
over
k
. In particular, in the realizable case of Example
1.4
and
1.5
, one recovers the original algebra
A({\mathbf{P}},V)=A({\mathcal{V}})
.

In the more general setting, we show that part (1) of Theorem
1.1
extends without modification:

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
({\mathbf{P}},U)
be a pair as above. The algebra
A({\mathbf{P}},U)
is quadratic with quadratic dual
A({\mathbf{P}}^{\vee},U^{\perp})
corresponding to the dual pair.

Similarly to the realizable case, the simple modules for

are labelled by the set

of bounded, feasible topes. For example, in Example

the bounded, feasible topes correspond to shaded regions in Figure

and standard modules

for each

.

However, unlike in the realizable case, the transitive closure of the cone relation need not define a poset. An oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is said to be

if the transitive closure of the cone relation on bounded, feasible topes of

is a poset.

Using this condition, we obtain the following analogue of Theorems

1.1
(3) and
1.3
.

Theorem 1.11

For a pair
({\mathbf{P}},U)
as above with the additional assumption that the program

is

, the algebra

is quasi-hereditary and Koszul.

Moreover, for any

the indecomposable projective cover

of

has a filtration with successive subquotients isomorphic to

Remark 1.12
.

While oriented matroid programs are not always Euclidean, every oriented matroid program of rank at most 3 (equivalently

We do not know whether or not every non-realizable oriented matroid

We observe in Example

For any generic oriented matroid program

In light of Theorem

Let

If the answer to this question is yes, it would appear to give a rather interesting algebraic invariant of the matroid

Question 1.15

Let

1.5.
Matroidal Schur algebras

Motivated in part by
[
BLPW10
]
, Braden and the second author defined a
hypertoric Schur algebra

[
BM19
]
- an analogue of the Schur algebra associated to affine hypertoric varieties. Recall that one can construct an affine hypertoric variety
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
with the input of a rational subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this setting the resulting hypertoric Schur algebra
R(V)
can be interpreted as a convolution algebra for a union of resolutions of stratum slices of
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
. In particular, for a rational polarized arrangement
(V,\eta,\xi)
with the same underlying subspace
V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the convolution algebra for the resolution
{\mathfrak{M}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
is a subalgebra of the associated hypertoric Schur algebra. Braden–Proudfoot–Webster showed in
[
BPW16
, Proposition 6.16, Example 6.18]
that the convolution algebra of the resolution
{\mathfrak{M}}\rightarrow{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
is categorified by Harish-Chandra bimodules for hypertoric category
{\mathcal{O}}
. One expects the entire hypertoric Schur algebra to be similarly categorified by Harish-Chandra bimodules with more general support and similarly to obtain a natural
q
-deformation of the hypertoric Schur algebra, or
q
-hypertoric Schur algebra.

In
[
BM17
]
, Braden and the second author observed that the hypertoric Schur algebras studied in
[
BM19
]
can be defined in terms of the underlying matroid. Following this observation, they defined a
matroidal Schur algebra

R(M)
associated to any matroid
M
.

One motivation for defining the category
{\mathcal{O}}
for oriented matroid programs in the present paper was to provide the foundation to categorify and find natural
q
-deformations of matroidal Schur algebras for orientable, but non-realizable matroids using an appropriate category of Harish-Chandra bimodules.

\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}=M
is its underlying unoriented matroid.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}
denote the set of bases for
M
. We let
C=C({\mathcal{M}})
denote the set of signed circuits and
C^{*}=C^{*}({\mathcal{M}})
the set of signed cocircuits, both regarded as subsets of the set of functions
E\rightarrow\{0,+,-\}
. Note the unoriented matroid
M=\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
has circuits
\{\underline{X}\mid X\in C\}
and cocircuits
\{\underline{Y}\mid Y\in C^{*}\}
. The dual oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}
is given by switching the roles of the circuits and cocircuits (i.e.
C({\mathcal{M}}^{\vee})=C^{*}({\mathcal{M}})
and
C^{*}({\mathcal{M}}^{\vee})=C({\mathcal{M}})
), while the bases
{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}
of the underlying matroid
M^{\vee}=\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\vee}
are the complements in
E
of the elements of
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Let
S\subset E
.
Then the set

\{X\in C({\mathcal{M}})\mid\underline{X}\subset E\backslash S\}

is the set of circuits of an oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}\backslash S
on
E\backslash S
, called the
deletion
of
S
from
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The set

\{X|_{S}:S\rightarrow\{0,+,-\}\mid X\in C({\mathcal{M}})\text{ and }\underline{X}\cap S\text{ is inclusion minimal and nonempty}\}

gives the set of circuits of an oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}/(E\backslash S)
on
S
, called the
contraction
of
{\mathcal{M}}
to
S
. Duality exchanges contraction and deletion:

({\mathcal{M}}/S)^{\vee}={\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}\backslash S\quad\text{and}\quad({\mathcal{M}}\backslash S)^{\vee}={\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}/S.

An element
i\in E
is a
loop
of
{\mathcal{M}}
if
\{i\}
is the support of a circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
. Dually,
i\in E
is a
coloop
of
{\mathcal{M}}
if
i
is not contained in the support of any circuit of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

An
oriented matroid program

{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is an oriented matroid
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
on the set
E\sqcup\{f,g\}
such that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f)/g={\mathcal{M}}
,
g
is not a loop and
f
is not a coloop. In particular, the rank of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is
d+1
, and this is defined to be the rank of
{\mathbf{P}}
.

The deletion
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
of
f
from
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is called the corresponding
affine oriented matroid
.

Remark 2.1
.

Our constructions will only depend on the contraction
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
, which is a single element extension of
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E\sqcup\{f\}
, and the deletion
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
, which is a single element lift of
{\mathcal{M}}
on
E\sqcup\{g\}
. Thus for our purposes it would be more natural to define an oriented matroid program as a pair, which we have taken to affectionately calling an
eft
, of a single element extension and single element lift of
{\mathcal{M}}
. We will refrain from doing so in this paper as the original notion appears to be standard in the field.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that the oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is
generic
for
{\mathcal{M}}
if

(1)

for any cocircuit
Y
of
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
, if
|z(Y)|>d
, then
Y(g)=0
, and

(2)

for any circuit
X
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
, if
|z(X)|>n-d
, then
X(f)=0
.

Remark 2.3
.

As the rank of the oriented matroid
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
on
E\sqcup\{f\}
is
d
, for any circuit
X
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
,
|\underline{X}|\leq d+1
and so
X
has
at least

n-d
zero entries. In the case of equality,
\underline{X}
contains a basis of
\underline{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g}
and so
z(X)
is independent in
(\underline{\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g})^{\vee}
.

Dually, for any cocircuit
Y
of
{\mathcal{N}}
,
|z(Y)|\geq d
and if equality holds
z(Y)
is independent in
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}
.

Example 2.4
.

As explained in Example
1.5
, every polarized arrangement
{\mathcal{V}}=(V,\eta,\xi)
naturally gives rise to an oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
. If
\eta
and
\xi
are generic in the sense of Theorem
1.1
, then
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic as well.

We now deduce some simple consequences of genericity.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is generic. Then
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
has no loops and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
has no coloops.

Proof.

We prove the first statement and the second follows by duality. By our assumption in the definition of an oriented matroid program,
g
is not a loop of
{\mathcal{N}}
, so
g\in\underline{Y}
for some cocircuit
Y
of
{\mathcal{N}}
. By Definition
2.2
(1),
|z(Y)|=d
and so Remark
2.3
implies
z(Y)
is independent. If there were a loop
i
of
{\mathcal{N}}
, then
i\in z(Y)
contradicting the fact that
z(Y)
is independent.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic. Then there is a natural bijection between the set
{\mathbb{B}}
of bases for
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
and the set of feasible cocircuits for
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
.

Proof.

Consider the map that takes a feasible cocircuit
Y
for
{\mathcal{N}}
to its zero set
b:=z(Y)
.
As
Y
is feasible,
Y(g)=+
and so by condition (1),
Y
must have
d
zero entries. Then
Y
has
n+1-d
non-zero entries and is a circuit of
{\mathcal{N}}^{\vee}
(which has rank
n-d
), so any subset of
Y
of size
n-d
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\vee}
. In particular,
\underline{Y}\backslash\{g\}
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\vee}
, so its complement
b\sqcup\{g\}
is basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}
. Thus
b
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}=\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}/g
.

To show that this is a bijection, suppose
b
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
. Then
b\sqcup\{g\}
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}
, its complement
E\backslash b
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\vee}
and so
(E\backslash b)\cup g
must contain a cocircuit
Y
for
{\mathcal{N}}
. By condition (1), either
\underline{Y}=(E\backslash b)\cup g
or
\underline{Y}\subset E\backslash b
. But the latter is not possible as
E\backslash b
is a basis for
\underline{{\mathcal{N}}}^{\vee}
. We conclude that there is a unique choice of feasible cocircuit
Y
with support
\underline{Y}=(E\backslash b)\cup g
.
∎

For
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
, we let
Y_{b}
be denote the corresponding feasible cocircuit.

We will often use three constructions to obtain new generic oriented matroid programs from a generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
: duality, deletion, and contraction.
Recall that duality for oriented matroid programs takes the program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
to the program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},f,g)
with underlying oriented matroid

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}\backslash g)/f=((\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g)\backslash f)^{\vee}={\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}.

For any
S\subset E
, we denote the contraction and deletion of
S
by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}/S:=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/S,g,f)\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{P}}\backslash S:=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash S,g,f),

respectively.

Note that
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic if and only if
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
is generic. If
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic and
S\subset b
for some
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
, then
{\mathbf{P}}/S
is generic and has rank
d+1-|S|
. If
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic and
S\cap b=\varnothing
for some
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
, then
{\mathbf{P}}\backslash S
is also generic of rank
d+1
.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
there exists a generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
such that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g)\backslash f={\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

For example, for any order on
E
, consider the lexicographic extension
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{M}}[E]
by a point
f
with respect to this order (Note that this is the same as taking the extension
{\mathcal{M}}[b_{\min}]
where
b_{\min}
is a lexicographically minimal basis of
{\mathcal{M}}
). By a Lemma of Todd
[
BLVS+99
, Lemma 7.2.6]
, any circuit
X
of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
with more than
n-d
zero entries satisfies
X(f)=0
. It then remains to define
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
as a single element of lifting of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
by a point
g
, such that
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
satisfies property (1) above. This can be done via the dual construction: consider the
colocalization

\tau:C({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})\rightarrow\{+,-,0\}
defined for any
X\in C({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})
by
\tau(X)=X_{i}
, where
i
is the minimal element of
E
such that
X_{i}\neq 0
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
be the lexicographic lifting of
{\mathcal{M}}^{\prime}
defined by
\tau
(in other words the dual of the lexicographic extension of
({\mathcal{M}}^{\prime})^{\vee}
associated to
\tau
).
∎

For the rest of the paper we assume that
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic.

2.2.
Bounded feasible topes and sign vectors

In this section we recall the notions of bounded and feasible topes and show in Corollary
2.15
that when
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic there is a natural bijection between bases
{\mathbb{B}}
of
M
and bounded feasible topes for
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Let
I
be any index set. For any functions
Z,Z^{\prime}\colon I\rightarrow\{0,+,-\}
, their
composition

Z\circ Z^{\prime}\colon I\rightarrow\{0,+,-\}
is defined by

Z\circ Z^{\prime}(i)=\begin{cases}Z(i)&\text{if}\;Z(i)\neq 0\\
Z^{\prime}(i)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We say that
Z
is a
face
of
Z^{\prime}
if
Z\circ Z^{\prime}=Z^{\prime}
.

The nonzero
covectors
of an oriented matroid on the set
E
are the functions
E\rightarrow\{0,+,-\}
which can be written as the composition of cocircuits. The set of covectors of
{\mathcal{M}}
is denoted by
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
, and includes the zero function
0
. It has a natural poset structure defined by
Y\leq X
if
Y
is a face of
X
.
The poset
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
is pure with minimal element
0
, the zero function.
The maximal elements of
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
are called
topes
, while the minimal elements of
{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})\backslash\{0\}
are the cocircuits of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

The rank of
Y\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
is given as
\rho(Y)=d-r(z(Y))
, where
r
is the rank function of the underlying matroid
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
. For
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
, the
join

Y_{1}\vee Y_{2}
is the minimal covector that has both
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
as faces, which only exists if there is a tope
T
with both
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
as faces. The
meet

Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2}
is the maximal covector face of both
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
. Note that the meet of
Y_{1},Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{M}})
always exists, but is the zero function when
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
do not have a common cocircuit face.

Definition 2.8
.

(Feasible covectors and affine space)
Let
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
be a generic program and let
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{N}})
denote the set of covectors for
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
. The
affine space
of
{\mathbf{P}}
is:

{\mathcal{A}}=\{Y\in{\mathcal{L}}~|~Y(g)=+\}.

We call elements of
{\mathcal{A}}

feasible
covectors.

We say that the
boundary of affine space
is:

{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}=\{Y\in{\mathcal{L}}~|~Y(g)=0\}

Notice that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
defines an oriented matroid on
E\cup\{g\}
which is equal to
{\mathcal{M}}
with
g
adjoined as a loop. Also, notice that the join of covectors in
{\mathcal{A}}
is also in
{\mathcal{A}}
if it exists, while their meet is in
{\mathcal{A}}
if and only if they share a common cocircuit face in
{\mathcal{A}}
; Otherwise, their meet is in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
.

Definition 2.9
.

(Feasible topes)
Let
{\mathcal{T}}({\mathcal{L}})
denote the set of topes of
{\mathcal{N}}
. We let

{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{A}}\cap{\mathcal{T}}({\mathcal{L}})

denote the set of feasible topes.

Notice that the definition of feasible topes does not depend on
f
.

Remark 2.10
.

By Lemma
2.5
the topes of
{\mathcal{N}}
are the covectors
T
such that
z(T)=\varnothing
.

A
sign vector
is a function
\alpha\colon E\rightarrow\{+,-\}
, usually written as
\alpha\in\{+,-\}^{E}
. There is an obvious injective map from
{\mathcal{F}}
to
\{+,-\}^{E}
given by forgetting the value at
g
(which is always
+
). We may refer to the sign vectors in the image as
feasible
sign vectors, and in a slight abuse of notation we identify feasible topes with the corresponding sign vectors. When there is a risk of confusion, we will write
T_{\alpha}
to denote the feasible tope of
{\mathcal{N}}
corresponding to a sign vector
\alpha
.

Definition 2.11
.

(Directions and optimality)

(1)

We refer to covectors in the boundary of affine space
Z\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
as
directions
in
{\mathcal{A}}
. We say that a direction is
increasing
(resp. decreasing or constant) with respect to
f
if
Z(f)=+
(resp.
Z(f)=-
or
Z(f)=0
).

(2)

For a feasible tope
T\in{\mathcal{F}}
and a feasible covector face
Y
of
T
, we say that the direction
Z\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
is
feasible
for
Y
in
T
if
Y\circ Z
is a face of
T
.

(3)

A feasible covector
Y
that is a face of
T\in{\mathcal{F}}
is an
optimal solution
for
T
if there is no feasible increasing direction for
Y
in
T
.

Definition 2.12
.

(Bounded sign vectors)
A sign vector
\alpha\in\{+,-\}^{E}
is
unbounded
if there exists a increasing direction
Z\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
such that
Z|_{E}\circ\alpha=\alpha
.
If no such
Z
exists, we say
\alpha
is
bounded
. Similarly, a tope
T
is bounded if the sign vector
T|_{E}
is bounded.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
denote the set of bounded sign vectors and
{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}
denote the set of bounded and feasible sign vectors.

Remark 2.13
.

Let
Y
be a bounded, feasible tope. Note that if
Y_{1},Y_{2}
are optimal solutions for
Y
, then so is
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}
. If
Y_{1}
is an optimal solution and
Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}
is a face of
Y_{1}
, then
Y_{2}
is also optimal. It follows that if
Y
has an optimal solution, then it has an optimal cocircuit.

Theorem 2.14
.

Assume
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic. Then every feasible, bounded tope
Y
has a unique optimal solution (cocircuit) and the resulting map from
{\mathcal{P}}
to the set of feasible cocircuits for
{\mathcal{N}}
is a bijection.

Proof.

Let
Y\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Recall that an optimal solution exists, without any condition on
{\mathbf{P}}
, by Bland and Lawrence’s Main Theorem of Oriented Matroid Programming (see
[
BLVS+99
, 10.1.13]
for a survey).

We first show that if
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic, then such a solution is unique.

Suppose that
Y
has two distinct optimal cocircuits
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
. Then
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}\in{\mathcal{A}}
must also be an optimal solution. Replacing
Y_{2}
by another cocircuit face of
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}
if necessary, without loss of generality, we may assume that
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are joined by an edge (i.e., the rank of
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}
is 2). There then exist two cocircuits at the boundary
\pm Z\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
on the pseudoline
\overline{Y_{1}Y_{2}}
such that
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}=Y_{1}\circ Z
and
Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}=Y_{2}\circ-Z
. (The cocircuit
Z
can by obtained via elimination of
g
from the pair
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
and this elimination is unique up to sign as
Y_{1},Y_{2}
form a modular pair). By optimality of
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
, we conclude that
\pm Z
must be constant directions. Note that
z(Y_{1}\circ Y_{2})
is an independent set in
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
of cardinality
d-1
. The contraction
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/(\{g\}\cup z(Y_{1}\circ Y_{2}))
is a rank 1 oriented matroid where
f
is a loop since the cocircuits are both zero on
f
. Thus
z(Y_{1}\circ Y_{2})\cup\{f\}
contains a circuit
X
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
such that
X(f)\neq 0
. This contradicts condition (2) since
X
is zero on at least
n-d+1
entries.

It remains to show that the map from
{\mathcal{P}}
to feasible cocircuits is a bijection. Given a cocircuit
Y\in{\mathcal{A}}
, we would like to show that
Y
is the optimal solution for a unique tope
T\in{\mathcal{P}}
. We construct such a
T
as follows. For any
i\in z(Y)
, we know that
{\mathbf{P}}_{i}:={\mathbf{P}}/(z(Y)\backslash\{i\})
is a generic program whose affine space is one-dimensional. There is a unique cocircuit
\overline{Z}_{i}
in
{\mathbf{P}}_{i}
such that
\overline{Z}_{i}(f)=-
, and this cocircuit is the restriction of a cocircuit
Z_{i}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
. Then
Z_{i}(j)=0
for
j\in z(Y)\backslash\{i\}
, while
Z_{i}(i)\neq 0
and
Z_{i}(f)=-
.
Then
T
is defined to be the composition
Y\circ Z
, where
Z
is the composition of all
Z_{i}
,
i\in z(Y)
, taken in any order.

This
T
is feasible since
T(g)=Y(g)=+
, and unique since it agrees on
z(Y)
with the unique bounded feasible tope of
{\mathbf{P}}\backslash(E\backslash z(Y))
. To show that
T
is bounded, recall that an equivalent definition for a feasible tope to be bounded is that it must be in the
bounded cone
of some
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
(see
[
BLVS+99
, Definition 10.1.8.ii, Corollary 10.1.10.ii]
), meaning that it agrees on
b
with a bounded tope of
{\mathbf{P}}\backslash(E\backslash b)
(see also Definition
2.18
). Since
T
is in the bounded cone of
z(Y)\in{\mathbb{B}}
, we have that
T
is bounded.
∎

Combining the bijections of Lemma
2.6
and Theorem
2.14
we obtain our desired correspondence.

Corollary 2.15
.

There is a natural bijection between the set
{\mathbb{B}}
of bases for
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
and the set
{\mathcal{P}}
of bounded feasible topes:

\mu:{\mathbb{B}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}},

which takes a basis
b
to the tope whose optimal cocircuit is
Y_{b}
. (Recall that
Y_{b}
is the feasible cocircuit with
z(Y_{b})=b
.)

We conclude this section with a discussion of the effect of duality on the bijection
\mu
.

Recall the following result about duality for oriented matroid programs.

Proposition 2.16
.

[
BLVS+99
, Corollary 10.1.11]

Let
{\mathcal{F}}^{\vee}
,
{\mathcal{B}}^{\vee}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}
respectively denote the sets of feasible, bounded, and bounded feasible sign vectors for the dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},f,g)
. Then

{\mathcal{F}}^{\vee}={\mathcal{B}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathcal{B}}^{\vee}={\mathcal{F}},\quad\text{and~so}\quad{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}={\mathcal{P}}.

Let
{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}
denote the set of bases of
\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\vee}
. Then
b\mapsto b^{c}:=E\backslash b
defines a bijection
{\mathbb{B}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}
. Let
\mu^{\vee}:{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}
be the bijection for the dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
defined as above. Recall that
{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}={\mathcal{P}}
.

Proposition 2.17

(Complementary Slackness)
.

For any
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
,
\mu(b)=\mu^{\vee}(b^{c})
.

Proof.

Recall that
{\mathbf{P}}
is generic if and only if
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
is generic. This is then the “Complementary Slackness” theorem of Bland applied to generic programs (cf.
[
BLVS+99
, Theorem 10.1.12]
).
∎

2.3.
Cone relation and Euclidean oriented matroid programs

In this section we consider a binary relation on the set
{\mathcal{P}}
of bounded feasible topes (or via
\mu
equivalently on the set of bases
{\mathbb{B}}
).

Definition 2.18
.

For any basis
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
we define the negative cone as

{\mathcal{B}}_{b}=\{\beta\in\{+,-\}^{E}\;|\;\mu(b)(i)=\beta(i)~\mathrm{for~all}~i\in b\}.

Notice that this set of sign vectors depends on
{\mathcal{M}}
and
f
but does not depend on
g
, in the sense that the signs
\mu(b)(i)
for
i\in b
only depend on the cocircuits of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g
.

Proposition 2.19

(Complementary Slackness)
.

For any
b\in{\mathbb{B}}
, let
X_{b^{c}}
be the feasible cocircuit of
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
with
z(X_{b^{c}})=b^{c}=E\backslash b
. Then for any
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}
,
\alpha\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
if and only if
X_{b^{c}}
is a face of
\alpha
.

Proof.

By definition, the tope
\alpha
is in the cone
{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
if and only if

\alpha(i)=\mu(b)(i)\quad\mathrm{for~all}~i\in b.

By Proposition
2.17
, we can rewrite the above condition as

\alpha(i)=\mu^{\vee}(b^{c})(i)\quad\mathrm{for~all}~i\in b.

Under the bijection
\mu^{\vee}
from Corollary
2.15
for the dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
, we have that
\mu^{\vee}(b^{c})(i)=X_{b^{c}}(i)
for all
i\in b
. Thus the previous condition becomes

\alpha(i)=X_{b^{c}}(i)\quad\mathrm{for~all}~i\in b,

which in turn is equivalent to
X_{b^{c}}
is a face of
\alpha
in the affine space associated to
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
.
∎

Definition 2.20

(Cone relation)
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we write
\beta\preceq\alpha
whenever
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu^{-1}(\alpha)}
.

The binary relation
\preceq
on
{\mathcal{P}}
is reflexive and anti-symmetric, but not necessarily transitive. Let
\leq
denote the transitive closure of
\preceq
. In general, the binary relation
\leq
on
{\mathcal{P}}
does not define a poset, as the closure may no longer be anti-symmetric.

In the following sections we will define algebras associated to the program
{\mathbf{P}}
. To ensure that these algebras are quasi-hereditary, we will need the relation
\leq
to define a poset. It turns out that this is equivalent to a well-known condition on the oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
, namely we ask that
{\mathbf{P}}
be

. To recall the definition, we first define the following graph associated to
{\mathbf{P}}
.

Definition 2.21
.

Let

be the graph whose vertices are vertices in
{\mathcal{A}}
(i.e., the feasible cocircuits for
{\mathcal{N}}
) and whose edges are the edges in
{\mathcal{A}}
(i.e., the feasible covectors of
{\mathcal{N}}
of rank 2). By our genericity condition, the graph is naturally directed by orienting each edge in increasing direction with respect to
f
.

Definition 2.22
.

For
b,b^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{B}}
, we write
b\leq b^{\prime}
if there is a directed path from
Y_{b}
to
Y_{b^{\prime}}
in the graph
G_{\mathbf{P}}
.

Definition 2.23
.

The program
{\mathbf{P}}
is

if the directed graph

contains no directed cycles. Equivalently,
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean if the binary relation on
{\mathbb{B}}
from Definition

is anti-symmetric.

By a result of Edmonds and Fukuda, the Euclidean property is well-behaved under duality:

Proposition 2.24
.

[
BLVS+99
, Corollary 10.5.9]

An oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is Euclidean if and only if its dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},f,g)
is Euclidean.

Importantly for us, for Euclidean programs, the transitive closure of the cone relations is a poset. In fact these two conditions are equivalent:

Lemma 2.25
.

The oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is Euclidean if and only if the transitive closure of the cone relation is anti-symmetric.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.24
, it suffices to show that the dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
is Euclidean if and only if the transitive closure on the cone relation is anti-symmetric. By definition,
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
is Euclidean if and only if the binary relation
({\mathbb{B}}^{\vee},\leq)
is anti-symmetric.
4
4

4

Here by
({\mathbb{B}}^{\vee},\leq)
we are referring to the binary relation on
{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}
coming from the directed graph
G_{{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}}
.

Thus it suffices to show that the bijection
\mu^{\vee}:{\mathbb{B}}^{\vee}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}={\mathcal{P}}
is order reversing. In other words, we wish to show that for

,

\mu(b_{1})\geq\mu(b_{2})\quad\textrm{if~and~only~if}\quad b_{1}^{c}\leq b_{2}^{c}.

As
({\mathcal{P}},\leq)
is the transitive closure of
\preceq
, without loss of generality we may suppose
\mu(b_{1})\succeq\mu(b_{2})
. Then
\mu(b_{2})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b_{1}}
and by Proposition
2.19
,
X_{b_{1}^{c}}
is a face of
\mu(b_{2})=\mu^{\vee}(b_{2}^{c})
. As
X_{b_{2}^{c}}
is the (unique) optimal cocircuit of the tope
\mu^{\vee}(b_{2}^{c})
, there is a directed path from
X_{b_{2}^{c}}
to
X_{b_{1}^{c}}
. Thus
b_{1}^{c}\leq b_{2}^{c}
as desired.

For the other direction, it suffices to consider the case where there is a directed edge from
X_{b_{2}^{c}}
to
X_{b_{1}^{c}}
. Then
X_{b_{1}^{c}}
is a cocircuit face of
\mu^{\vee}(b_{2}^{c})=\mu(b_{2})
. By Proposition
2.19
this implies
\mu(b_{2})\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b_{1}}
and so
\mu(b_{2})\leq\mu(b_{1})
, which completes the proof.
∎

2.4.
On the existence of Euclidean generic programs

Unlike genericity, it is not clear (at least to the authors) that any oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
can be extended to a Euclidean generic oriented matroid program
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
.

On the other hand, we have already seen in Example
1.7
a non-realizable Euclidean program
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
for which
{\mathcal{M}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g\backslash f
is realizable. There also appear to be many examples for which the oriented matroid

is non-realizable, and so our setting significantly generalizes that of
[
BLPW10
]
.

In this section we first give such an example and then give a criterion on
{\mathcal{M}}
for the existence of a Euclidean generic oriented matroid program lifting and extending
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Example 2.26
.

The Vámos matroid
{\mathcal{V}}
(rank 4 on 8 elements) is not representable over any field, but is orientable by
[
BV78
]
. We now define a Euclidean generic program
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}
by adjoining elements to
{\mathcal{V}}
as labelled and orientated in
[
BV78
]
. Let
(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}},g,f)
be the generic oriented matroid program obtained from
{\mathcal{V}}
by adjoining
g
using the lexicographic one-element lift defined by the cobasis
\{3,6,7,8\}
, and adjoining

by a lexicographic one-element extension defined by the basis
\{1,2,4,5\}
. One can verify that the graph

of this program has no directed cycles, so this program is Euclidean and generic.

We are grateful to Jim Lawrence for suggesting that we consider the following statement.

Proposition 2.27
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}
be an oriented matroid. If one can adjoin elements
g
and
f
to
{\mathcal{M}}
to obtain a Euclidean generic oriented matroid program
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
, then
{\mathcal{M}}
has a simplicial tope.

Before beginning the proof, we note that it is an open conjecture of Las Vergnas
[
LV80
]
(see also
[
BLVS+99
, 7.3.10]
) that any oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
should have a simplicial tope. Thus, the proposition implies that if for any oriented matroid
{\mathcal{M}}
there is a Euclidean generic oriented matroid program
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
for which
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g\backslash f={\mathcal{M}}
, then Las Vergnas’ conjecture holds.

Proof.

Suppose
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},g,f)
is a Euclidean generic oriented matroid program such that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}/g)\backslash f={\mathcal{M}}
.

As
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean, there exists a minimal vertex of

. Let

be the feasible cocircuit of
{\mathcal{N}}
corresponding to such a minimal vertex.
Let

be the tope whose optimal cocircuit is

, which exists by Theorem
2.14
. Then

is the only feasible cocircuit face of

, so for all other cocircuit faces

. There are
d
such subtopes that have

as a face, all of which are feasible. Any other subtope

covered by

cannot be feasible and as all non-feasible cocircuit faces

of

, then

. There can be at most one such subtope covered by

, Exercise 4.4]
, any tope of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
covers at least

and

covers exactly

∎

2.6.

Let

Lemma 2.34

is a parameter space for the face ring of the dual matroid

For any

satisfies condition (i) of Definition
7.4
by Corollary
7.3
. To show that
A
is quasi-hereditary it remains to show condition (ii).

We will use the following simple lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
i\in\mu^{-1}(\beta)
. Then the feasible sign vector
\beta^{i}
is either unbounded or
\beta^{i}\succeq\beta
.

Proof.

Suppose
\beta^{i}
is bounded. As
\beta^{i}(j)=\beta(j)
for all
j\neq i
, if
i\not\in\mu^{-1}(\beta^{i})
, then for all
j\in\mu^{-1}(\beta^{i})
,
\beta^{i}(j)=\beta(j)
. Thus
\beta\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu^{-1}(\beta^{i})}
or equivalently
\beta\preceq\beta^{i}
.

If
\beta^{i}
is bounded and
i\in\mu^{-1}(\beta^{i})
, then the optimal solutions of
\beta
and
{\beta^{i}}
are also optimal solutions of their common subtope
Y=\beta\wedge\beta^{i}
. But
Y
is a tope of
{\mathbf{P}}/\{i\}
and so
Y
has a unique optimal solution. Thus
\mu^{-1}(\beta)=\mu^{-1}(\beta^{i})
, which contradicts the fact that
\mu
is bijection.
∎

Theorem 7.6
.

Assume
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean. Then the kernel of the quotient homomorphism
P_{\alpha}\twoheadrightarrow V_{\alpha}
has a filtration with each successive subquotient isomorphic to
V_{\beta}
for
\beta\succ\alpha
. Each of these standard modules appears exactly once.

In particular,
A
is quasihereditary.

Proof.

For any
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}
, we define
P_{\alpha}^{\gamma}\subset P_{\alpha}
to be the submodule generated by paths which pass through
\gamma
. For any
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
, let

K_{\alpha}^{\beta}:=\sum_{\gamma>\beta}P_{\alpha}^{\gamma}.

After choosing a total order on
\{\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}\mid\alpha\leq\beta\}
refining
\leq
, the set of submodules
P_{\alpha}^{\beta}+K_{\alpha}^{\beta}
with
\beta\geq\alpha
forms a filtration of
K_{\alpha}
with successive subquotients

M_{\alpha}^{\beta}:=\left(P_{\alpha}^{\beta}+K_{\alpha}^{\beta}\right)/K_{\alpha}^{\beta}.

We pause to note that to make the definitions above, it is essential that
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean, for if
{\mathbf{P}}
were not Euclidean there would be no partial (or total) order refining the cone relation on
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Our goal now is to prove that
M_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is zero if
\beta\not\succeq\alpha
, and is isomorphic to
V_{\beta}
if
\beta\succeq\alpha
. Notice that
M_{\alpha}^{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}
.

If
\beta\not\succeq\alpha
, then there is an index
i\in\mu^{-1}(\beta)
such that
\alpha(i)\neq\beta(i)
. By Proposition
3.6
, any path starting at
\alpha
and passing through
\beta
can be written as
p_{\alpha,\beta}\cdot r
in
P_{\alpha}^{\geq\beta}
, where
p_{\alpha,\beta}
represents a taut path from
\alpha
to
\beta
and
r
represents a path starting at
\beta
. We may then apply Corollary
3.7
to the taut path
p_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\gamma=\beta^{i}
, to show that
p_{\alpha,\beta}
can be chosen to pass through
\beta^{i}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. By Lemma
7.5
,
P_{\alpha}^{\beta}\subset P_{\alpha}^{\beta^{i}}\subset K_{\alpha}^{\beta}
, so
M_{\alpha}^{\beta}=0
.

On the other hand, assume that
\beta\succ\alpha
. There is a natural map
P_{\beta}\rightarrow P_{\alpha}^{\beta}
given by composing any element of
P_{\beta}
with a fixed taut path
p_{\alpha,\beta}
from
\alpha
to
\beta
. This induces a homomorphism
V_{\beta}\rightarrow M_{\alpha}^{\beta}
that we wish to show is an isomorphism.

By Proposition
3.6
, any path starting at
\alpha
and passing through
\beta
can be expressed as a product of an element in
P_{\beta}
with some taut path from
\alpha
to
\beta
and by Proposition
3.5
the taut path can be chosen to be the one we have fixed. It follows that the map
P_{\beta}\rightarrow P_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is surjective and thus the induced map
V_{\beta}\rightarrow M_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is surjective as well.

Finally, we need to show that
V_{\beta}\rightarrow M_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is injective. We proceed by showing that they have the same dimension. The surjectivity of the map implies

\dim_{k}M_{\alpha}^{\beta}\leq\dim_{k}V_{\beta}=|\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}\mid\gamma\preceq\beta\}|,

so that

\dim_{k}P_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\succeq\alpha}\dim_{k}M_{\alpha}^{\beta}\leq|\{(\gamma,\beta)\in{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}\mid\alpha,\gamma\preceq\beta\}|.

We’re done if we can show this is an equality. As
A=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}}P_{\alpha}
, it suffices to prove that

\dim_{k}A=\sum_{\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}}\dim_{k}P_{\alpha}=\{(\alpha,\gamma,\beta)\in{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}\mid\alpha,\gamma\preceq\beta\}.

But recall that

A=A({\mathbf{P}},U)\simeq B({\mathbf{P}}^{\vee},U^{\perp})=\bigoplus_{(\alpha,\gamma)\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}\times{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}}R_{\alpha\gamma}^{\vee}

and so by Lemma
5.7

\dim_{k}B({\mathbf{P}}^{\vee},U^{\perp})=\sum_{(\alpha,\gamma)\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}\times{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}}|\{\text{common feasible cocircuit faces of $\alpha$ and $\gamma$}\}|.

We are then reduced to showing that the number of common feasible cocircuit faces in
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
of
\alpha
and
\gamma
is equal to the number of bounded feasible topes
\beta
of
{\mathbf{P}}
such that
\alpha\preceq\beta
and
\gamma\preceq\beta
.
This follows from Complementary Slackness (Proposition
2.19
).
∎

7.2.
The structure of projectives when
{\mathbf{P}}
is not Euclidean

Note that the definition of the standard modules makes sense for any
{\mathbf{P}}
and Lemma
7.2
holds even in the non-Euclidean case. However, when
{\mathbf{P}}
is not Euclidean, the transitive closure of the cone relation is not a poset and so the standard modules are not part of a highest weight structure.

Nonetheless, one might still hope for a version of Theorem
7.6
: that the kernel of
P_{\alpha}\twoheadrightarrow V_{\alpha}
has a filtration with successive subquotients isomorphic to
V_{\beta}
for
\beta\succ\alpha
.

In this section we observe that this is too optimistic a hope, but that it does hold on the level of graded Grothendieck groups.

Recall from Lemma
5.7
, that for any
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}
, the dimension
\dim_{k}R_{\alpha}
is equal to the number of feasible cocircuit faces of
\alpha
. We begin with a graded refinement of this statement.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
(h_{0},h_{1}\ldots,h_{d-1})
denote the
h
-vector of
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
or equivalently
h_{i}
is equal to the dimension of the graded piece of
R_{\alpha}
of degree
2i
. Then
h_{i}
is equal to the number of feasible vertices of
\alpha
with
i
outgoing edges.

Proof.

We proceed by showing that
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
is partitionable. Recall that a pure simplicial complex
\Delta
is
partitionable
, if it can be expressed as a disjoint union of closed intervals of the form

\Delta=[G_{1},F_{1}]\sqcup\ldots\sqcup[G_{s},F_{s}],

where each
F_{i}
is a facet of
\Delta
. By
[
Sta04
, Proposition III.2.3]
the
h
-polynomial of such a simplicial complex is given by

h_{i}=\#\{j:|G_{j}|=i\}.

Recall that
z(\alpha)=\varnothing
and
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
is isomorphic as a poset to
{\mathfrak{F}}_{lv}^{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha)\backslash\{0\}
. Thus we may identify a face of
\alpha
with the faces of the abstract simplicial complex
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
.

The facets
F_{1},\ldots,F_{s}
of
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
are the zero sets of the feasible vertices (i.e., feasible cocircuit faces) of
\alpha
.
If
F_{i}=z(X_{i})
for a feasible vertex
X_{i}
, let
G_{i}
be (the zero set of) the meet in
\Delta_{\alpha}
of the incoming edges of
X_{i}
.

Recall that each face of
\alpha
has a unique optimal solution (this follows from Theorem
2.14
). For each feasible face
Y
of
\alpha
, the face
z(Y)\in\Delta_{\alpha}
is in the interval
[G_{j},F_{j}]
if and only if
X_{j}
is the optimal solution of
Y
. Thus
[G_{1},F_{1}]\sqcup\ldots\sqcup[G_{s},F_{s}]
is a partition of
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
. Note that

|G_{j}|=d-\#\{\textrm{incoming~edges~to~}X_{j}\}=\#\{\textrm{outgoing~edges~from~}X_{j}\}.

We conclude that

h_{i}=\#\{j:|G_{j}|=i\}=\#\{\textrm{feasible~vertices~of~}Y\textrm{~with~}i\textrm{~outgoing~edges}\}.

∎

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
(h_{0},h_{1}\ldots,h_{d-1})
denote the
h
-vector of
z(\Delta_{\alpha\beta})
or equivalently
h_{i}
is equal to the dimension of the graded piece of
R_{\alpha\beta}
of degree
2i
. Then
h_{i}
is equal to the number of feasible vertices of
\alpha\wedge\beta
with
i
outgoing edges of
\alpha\wedge\beta
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be the tope in
{\mathbf{P}}/z(\alpha\wedge\beta)
given by the restriction of
\alpha\wedge\beta
. Then the simplicial complex
z(\Delta_{\alpha\beta})
is equal to the simplicial join
z(\Delta_{\gamma})*\Gamma
of
z(\Delta_{\gamma})
with the
(d_{\alpha\beta}-1)
-simplex
\Gamma
on the set
z(\alpha\wedge\beta)
. By standard properties of the
h
-polynomial, we have:

h(z(\Delta_{\alpha\beta}),x)=h(z(\Delta_{\gamma})*\Gamma,x)=h(z(\Delta_{\gamma}),x)h(\Gamma,x)=h(z(\Delta_{\gamma}),x).

We conclude that the
h
-vector of
z(\Delta_{\alpha\beta})
is equal to that of
z(\Delta_{\gamma})
. The result then follows from Lemma
7.7
.
∎

For an
A
-module
M
, let
[M]
denote the class of
M
in the Grothendieck group of
A
-modules. We will consider the Grothendieck group of the category of graded
A
-modules as a
{\mathbb{Z}}[q,q^{-1}]
-module, where

[M\langle-k\rangle]=q^{k}[M].

For a graded vector space
V=\oplus_{i}V_{i}
, we denote the graded dimension of
V
by

{\mathrm{grdim}}V=\sum_{i}(\dim V_{i})~q^{i}.

Theorem 7.9
.

For any generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
and any
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}
, the class of the indecomposable projective
P_{\alpha}
in the Grothendieck group can be expressed as the sum:

[P_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\gamma\succeq\alpha}q^{d_{\alpha\gamma}}[V_{\gamma}].

Proof.

For any
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
the graded composition series multiplicity of the simple
L_{\beta}
in the projective
P_{\alpha}
is equal to the graded dimension of the space of paths in
A
that start at
\alpha
and end at
\beta
. In other words we have:

[P_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}}({\mathrm{grdim}}~P_{\alpha}e_{\beta})\cdot[L_{\beta}]=\sum_{\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}}({\mathrm{grdim}}~e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta})\cdot[L_{\beta}].

By Theorem
5.12
,

{\mathrm{grdim}}~e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta}={\mathrm{grdim}}~R^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}\langle-d_{\alpha\beta}\rangle=q^{d_{\alpha\beta}}\cdot{\mathrm{grdim}}~R^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}.

By Corollary
7.8
, we can express the graded dimension of
R^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}
as

{\mathrm{grdim}}~R^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\#\{\mathrm{feasible~vertices~of~}\alpha\wedge\beta\mathrm{~in~}{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}\mathrm{~with~}i\mathrm{~outgoing~edges}\}\cdot q^{2i}.

Observe that by Proposition
2.19
the feasible vertices of
\alpha\wedge\beta
in
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
(i.e., common feasible vertices of both
\alpha
and
\beta
) are in bijection with the bounded feasible topes
\delta
of
{\mathbf{P}}
such that
\alpha\preceq\delta
and
\beta\preceq\delta
. We claim that the number of outgoing edges of
\alpha\wedge\beta
of the feasible vertex corresponding to
\delta
is equal to
|S^{\delta}_{\alpha\beta}|
. As in the proof of Corollary
7.8
, let
\gamma
be the bounded feasible tope
(\alpha\wedge\beta)|_{\underline{\alpha\wedge\beta}}
in the contraction
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}/z(\alpha\wedge\beta)
. Then the number of outgoing edges of the vertex of
\gamma
corresponding to
\delta
is equal to the distance between
\gamma
and the restriction
\overline{\delta}
of
\delta
to
\underline{\alpha\wedge\beta}
. But
\underline{\alpha\wedge\beta}=\{i\in E\mid\alpha(i)=\beta(i)\}
and so the distance between
\gamma
and
\overline{\delta}
is equal to the cardinality of the difference set

S(\gamma,\overline{\delta})=\{i\in E\mid\alpha(i)=\beta(i)\neq\delta(i)\}=S_{\alpha\beta}^{\delta}.

Rewriting the sum over topes
\delta
of
{\mathbf{P}}
such that
\alpha\preceq\delta
and
\beta\preceq\delta
and using the formula
d_{\alpha\delta}+d_{\delta\beta}=d_{\alpha\beta}+2|S_{\alpha\beta}^{\delta}|
, we find:

(7.1)

{\mathrm{grdim}}~e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta}=q^{d_{\alpha\beta}}{\mathrm{grdim}}~R^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\delta\succeq\alpha,\beta}q^{d_{\alpha\beta}+2|S_{\alpha\beta}^{\delta}|}=\sum_{\delta\succeq\alpha,\beta}q^{d_{\alpha\delta}+d_{\delta\beta}}.

Putting it all together,

[P_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\beta}\sum_{\delta\succeq\alpha,\beta}q^{d_{\alpha\delta}+d_{\delta\beta}}[L_{\beta}]

=\sum_{\delta\succeq\alpha}q^{d_{\alpha\delta}}\sum_{\beta\preceq\delta}q^{d_{\beta\delta}}[L_{\beta}]

=\sum_{\gamma\succeq\alpha}q^{d_{\alpha\delta}}[V_{\gamma}],

as we wished to show.
∎

We conclude this section with an example of a generic non-Euclidean program and sign vector

for which the kernel of

does not admit a filtration with successive standard subquotients.

Example 7.10
.

Let

.
Then

for the basis

7.3.
A
is a Koszul algebra when
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean

Recall the notion of a Koszul algebra:

Definition 7.11
.

Let
M=\bigoplus_{\ell\geq 0}M_{\ell}
be a graded
k
-algebra. A complex

\dots\rightarrow P_{3}\rightarrow P_{2}\rightarrow P_{1}\rightarrow P_{0}

of graded projective right
M
-modules is
linear
if each
P_{\ell}
is generated in degree
\ell
. We say that
M
is
Koszul
if every simple right
M
-module has a linear projective resolution.

Theorem 7.12
.

Assume
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean. Then for all
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}
, the standard module
V_{\alpha}
has a linear projective resolution.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
[
BLPW10
, Theorem 5.24]
.

Let
a
be the basis corresponding to the optimal cocircuit for
\alpha
. We will define the promised resolution as the total complex of the following multicomplex.

For any
S\subset a
, let
\alpha^{S}\in\{+,-\}^{E}
be the sign vector which disagrees with
\alpha
on exactly the entries in
S
. For example,
\alpha^{\varnothing}=\alpha
, and
\alpha^{\{i\}}=\alpha^{i}
for any
i\in a
. Notice that if
i\in S\subset a
and
\alpha^{S},\alpha^{S\backslash i}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, then there is a degree one map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\phi_{S,i}\colon&P_{\alpha^{S}}&\longrightarrow&P_{\alpha^{S\backslash i}},\\
&q&\mapsto&p(\alpha^{S\backslash i},\alpha^{S})\cdot q.\end{array}

We extend this to all
S\subset a
and
i\in a
by declaring that
P_{\alpha^{S}}=0
if
\alpha^{S}\not\in{\mathcal{P}}
and
\phi_{S,i}=0
if
i\not\in S
. Consider the module

\Pi_{\alpha}:=\bigoplus_{S\subset a}P_{\alpha^{S}},

which we view as being graded by the free abelian group
{\mathbb{Z}}\{\varepsilon_{i}\mid i\in a\}
where the summand
P_{\alpha^{S}}
is given degree
\varepsilon_{S}:=\sum_{i\in S}\varepsilon_{i}
. For each
i\in a
, consider the differential
\partial_{i}\colon\Pi_{\alpha}\rightarrow\Pi_{\alpha}
of degree
-\varepsilon_{i}
defined as the sum

\partial_{i}:=\sum_{S\subset a}\phi_{S,i}.

Observe that
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}
for any
i,j\in a
by relation (
A2
) and so we can view
\Pi_{\alpha}
as a multi-complex with differentials
\partial_{i}
for each
i\in a
.

Let
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}
denote the total complex of
\Pi_{\alpha}
. Then
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}
is a linear complex of projective modules and
H_{0}(\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha}^{\bullet})=V_{\alpha}
. It remains to show that the complex
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\alpha}^{\bullet}
is exact in positive degrees.

To do so, we will filter the multicomplex
\Pi_{\alpha}
. For each
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
, let
(\Pi_{\alpha})^{\beta}\subset\Pi_{\alpha}
be the submodule whose
\varepsilon_{S}
-graded part is defined as

\sum_{\gamma\geq\beta,\alpha^{S}}P^{\gamma}_{\alpha^{S}}\subset P_{\alpha^{S}},

that is, the submodule consisting of all paths from
\alpha^{S}
passing through some
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}
where
\gamma\geq\alpha^{S}
and
\gamma\geq\beta
.
Observe that the differentials
\partial_{i}
for
i\in a
are compatible with the submodules
(\Pi_{\alpha})^{\beta}
and so we have defined a filtration of
\Pi_{\alpha}
by the poset
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Computing the associated graded of this filtration yields a multi-complex

\widetilde{\Pi}_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}}(\Pi_{\alpha})^{\beta}/(\Pi_{\alpha})^{>\beta}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\bigoplus_{S}M^{\beta}_{\alpha^{S}}\right),

where
M^{\beta}_{\alpha^{S}}
is the subquotient of
P_{\alpha^{S}}
defined as in the proof of Theorem
7.6
.

Consider the resulting quotient multi-complexes for each
\beta\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Let
b=\mu^{-1}(\beta)
. Recall from the proof of Theorem
7.6
that
M^{\beta}_{\alpha^{S}}
is non-zero if and only if
\alpha^{S}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
.

If
\beta=\alpha
, then
M^{\beta}_{\alpha^{S}}=M^{\alpha}_{\alpha^{S}}=0
for any non-empty
S\subset a=b
. Thus the only non-zero summand of the
\alpha
-subquotient is
M^{\alpha}_{\alpha}=V_{\alpha}
in total degree zero.

If
\beta\neq\alpha
, choose an element
i\in a
such that
i\not\in b
. Consider those subsets
S\subset a
such that
i\not\in S
. Then we have
\alpha^{S}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
if and only if
\alpha^{S\cup\{i\}}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
. If
\alpha^{S}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
, then
M_{\alpha^{S\cup i}}^{\beta}\simeq V_{\beta}\simeq M_{\alpha^{S}}^{\beta}
and the differential induced by
\partial_{i}
is the isomorphism given by left multiplication with
p(\alpha^{S},\alpha^{S\cup\{i\}})
. On the other hand, if
\alpha^{S}\not\in{\mathcal{B}}_{b}
, then
M_{\alpha^{S\cup i}}^{\beta}=0=M_{\alpha^{S}}^{\beta}
. In particular, the differential induced by
\partial_{i}
on the
\beta
-component of the associated graded multi-complex is exact.

Recall that if any differential of a multi-complex is exact then the total complex of the multi-complex is also exact. We conclude that the total complex of the associated graded multi-complex is exact in positive degree. It then follows that the total complex of the original multi-complex must also be exact in positive degrees.
∎

Theorem 7.13
.

Assume
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean. Then
A
and
B
are Koszul algebras and
A
is Koszul dual to
B
.

Proof.

By
[
ÁDL03
, Theorem 1]
a quasi-hereditary algebra is Koszul if the standard modules have linear projective resolutions. Such resolutions exist for

7.4.
Numerical identity for Hilbert polynomials

We do not know whether or not the Euclidean condition on
{\mathbf{P}}
is necessary for
A
to be Koszul. In this section we prove that for any generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
the Hilbert polynomial of the algebra
A=A({\mathbf{P}},U)
satisfies the following numerical identity.

Let
H(A,q)
denote the Hilbert polynomial of
A
, which is the
{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}
-matrix with entries

H(A,q)_{\alpha,\beta}={\mathrm{grdim}}~e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta}.

Recall
[
BGS96
, Lemma 2.11.1]
that if
A
is Koszul, then there is an equality of matrices

H(A,q)H(A^{!},-q)^{T}=I.

Theorem 7.14
.

For any generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
, the algebra
A=A({\mathbf{P}},U)
satisfies the numerical identity above, that is, the Hilbert polynomials of
A
and its quadratic dual
A^{!}
satisfy the matrix equation

H(A,q)H(A^{!},-q)^{T}=I.

Remark 7.15
.

This identity does not necessarily imply that
A
is Koszul. See
[
Pos95
]
for an example of a non-Koszul quadratic algebra whose Hilbert series satisfies the numerical identity.

Proof.

Using equation (
7.1
) in the proof of Theorem
7.9
, the
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry of
H(A,q)
is given by

H(A,q)_{\alpha,\beta}={\mathrm{grdim}}~e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\succeq\alpha,\beta}q^{d_{\alpha\gamma}+d_{\gamma\beta}}.

In particular,
H(A,q)
factors as the product
H(A,q)=XX^{T}
, where
X
is the
{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}
-matrix with
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry given by

X_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{cases}q^{d_{\alpha\beta}}&\mathrm{if~}\beta\succeq\alpha\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Dually, using Proposition
2.19
we find that the
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry of
H(A^{!},-q)
is given by

H(A^{!},-q)_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{i}(-q)^{i}\dim e_{\alpha}A^{!}_{i}e_{\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\supset Y_{\mu^{-1}(\alpha)},Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}}(-q)^{d_{\alpha\gamma}+d_{\gamma\beta}},

in other words the sum runs over all
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}
for which the optimal solution (cocircuit) of both
\alpha
and
\beta
are faces of
\gamma
. Again this factors as a product
H(A^{!},-q)=YY^{T}
, where
Y
is the
{\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathcal{P}}
-matrix with
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry given by

Y_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{cases}(-q)^{d_{\alpha\beta}}&\mathrm{if~}Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}\mathrm{~is~a~face~of~}\alpha\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We wish to show that

H(A,q)H(A^{!},-q)^{T}=XX^{T}YY^{T}=I.

Note that it suffices to show
X^{T}Y=I
.

Computing the product
X^{T}Y
, we find that its
(\alpha,\beta)
-entry is given by

(X^{T}Y)_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{\gamma\in Q}q^{d_{\alpha\gamma}}(-q)^{d_{\gamma\beta}},

where
Q
is the set of all
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}
such that
\alpha\succeq\gamma
and
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}
is a face of
\gamma
. In other words,
Q
consists of all
\gamma\in{\mathcal{P}}
such that

(7.2)

\gamma(i)=\alpha(i)\mathrm{~if~}i\in\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\quad\mathrm{~and}\quad\gamma(i)=\beta(i)\mathrm{~if~}i\not\in\mu^{-1}(\beta).

We wish to show that

(X^{T}Y)_{\alpha,\beta}=\begin{cases}1&\mathrm{if}~\alpha=\beta\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
\alpha=\beta
, then
Q=\{\alpha\}
and the sum is equal to
q^{d_{\alpha\alpha}}(-q)^{d_{\alpha\alpha}}=1
.

Now assume that
\alpha\neq\beta
and let

J:=\mu^{-1}(\beta)\backslash\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\quad\mathrm{and}\quad J^{\prime}:=\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\backslash\mu^{-1}(\beta)

so that

J\sqcup J^{\prime}=(\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\cup\mu^{-1}(\beta))\backslash(\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\cap\mu^{-1}(\beta)).

As we have assumed that
\alpha\neq\beta
,
J
and
J^{\prime}
are nonempty.

Note that if
\alpha(i)\neq\beta(i)
for some
i\in J^{\prime}
, then by the conditions
(\ref{eq-Q})

Q
is empty and
(X^{T}Y)_{\alpha,\beta}=0
as desired. Thus we will assume that
\alpha(i)=\beta(i)
for all
i\in J^{\prime}
.

Let

K:=\{i\in\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\cap\mu^{-1}(\beta)\mid\alpha(i)\neq\beta(i)\}.

For
\delta\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}
is a face of
\delta
if and only if
\delta=\beta^{W}
for some subset
W\subset\mu^{-1}(\beta)
. On the other hand,
\delta=\beta^{W}\preceq\alpha
if and only if
K\cup J\supset W\supset K
. Thus
Q=\{(\beta^{K})^{S}\mid S\subset J\}
and

(X^{T}Y)_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{S\subset J}q^{d_{\alpha,(\beta^{K})^{S}}}(-q)^{d_{(\beta^{K})^{S},\beta}}=(-1)^{|K|}\sum_{S\subset J}(-1)^{|S|}q^{d_{\alpha,(\beta^{K})^{S}}+d_{(\beta^{K})^{S},\beta}}

=(-1)^{|K|}\sum_{S\subset J}(-1)^{|S|}q^{d_{\alpha,\beta}+2|S^{(\beta^{K})^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|}=(-1)^{|K|}q^{d_{\alpha,\beta}}\sum_{S\subset J}(-1)^{|S|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|},

where in the last line we have used
S^{(\beta^{K})^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}=S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}=\{i\in S\mid\alpha(i)=\beta(i)\}
. We will need the following lemma to finish this proof.

Lemma 7.16
.

Assume as above that
\alpha\neq\beta
and
\alpha(i)=\beta(i)
for all
i\in J^{\prime}
. Then there exists an element
t\in J
such that
\alpha(t)\neq\beta(t)
.

Proof.

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\alpha(i)=\beta(i)
for all
i\in J
, then
\alpha(i)=\beta(i)
for all
i\in J\sqcup J^{\prime}
. In the deletion-contraction program

\left({\mathbf{P}}/(\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\cap\mu^{-1}(\beta))\right)\setminus(\mu^{-1}(\alpha)\cup\mu^{-1}(\beta))^{c}

defined on the set
J\sqcup J^{\prime}
,
the restrictions of the sign vectors of
\alpha
and
\beta
are then equal and so describe the same tope
T
. Now
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\alpha)}
is the optimal solution for
\alpha
and
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}
is the optimal solution for
\beta
, so the restrictions
Y_{\alpha}
and
Y_{\beta}
of
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\alpha)}
and
Y_{\mu^{-1}(\beta)}
to
J\sqcup J^{\prime}
should both be the unique optimal solution of the tope
T
. But
z(Y_{\alpha})=J^{\prime}\neq J=z(Y_{\beta})
, which is a contradiction. Thus there exists a
t\in J
such that
\alpha(t)\neq\beta(t)
as desired.
∎

In particular if
S\subset J\backslash\{t\}
, we have
S^{\beta^{S\sqcup\{t\}}}_{\alpha,\beta}=S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}
.

Using this fact we rewrite the sum above:

(X^{T}Y)_{\alpha,\beta}=(-1)^{|K|}q^{d_{\alpha,\beta}}\sum_{S\subset J}(-1)^{|S|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|},

=(-1)^{|K|}q^{d_{\alpha,\beta}}\sum_{S\subset J\backslash\{t\}}\left((-1)^{|S|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|}+(-1)^{|S\sqcup\{t\}|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S\cup\{t\}}}_{\alpha,\beta}|}\right)

=(-1)^{|K|}q^{d_{\alpha,\beta}}\sum_{S\subset J\backslash\{t\}}\left((-1)^{|S|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|}-(-1)^{|S|}q^{2|S^{\beta^{S}}_{\alpha,\beta}|}\right)=0.

∎

7.5.
Self-dual projectives

Consider the duality functor

\textrm{d}:A\text{-mod}\rightarrow A\text{-mod}

defined by composing the equivalence
A^{\text{op}}\text{-mod}\simeq A\text{-mod}
induced by the isomorphism
A\cong A^{\text{op}}
given by reversing the arrows of the quiver
D_{E}
in Section
3.3
with the induced functor
A^{\text{op}}
-mod
\rightarrow A
-mod coming from vector space duality.

In the following result, for a fixed sign vector
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}
we will need to refer to both the corresponding bounded feasible tope in the affine space of
{\mathbf{P}}
and the corresponding bounded feasible tope in the affine space of
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
. To distinguish these two topes, we write
T_{\alpha}
to denote the tope in
{\mathbf{P}}
and
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
for the tope in
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
.

Theorem 7.17
.

For any generic oriented matroid program
{\mathbf{P}}
and
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

The projective
P_{\alpha}
is injective.

(2)

The projective
P_{\alpha}
is self-dual.

(3)

The simple
L_{\alpha}
is contained in the socle of some standard module
V_{\beta}
.

(4)

The bounded feasible tope
T_{\alpha}
covers an infeasible subtope
X
, meaning
X(g)=0
.

(5)

The bounded feasible tope
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
in the dual program
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
is in the core of the affine space for
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
. In other words
(T_{\alpha}^{\vee})^{\infty}=0
or equivalently the cocircuit faces of the tope
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
are all feasible.

When
{\mathbf{P}}
is Euclidean, and so
A
is quasi-hereditary by Theorem
7.6
, then the statements above are also equivalent to the following:

(6)

The projective
P_{\alpha}
is tilting.

Proof.

The implications
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)~(~\Leftrightarrow(6)
, if
A
is quasi-hereditary) are standard facts.

(2)\implies(3):
If
P_{\alpha}
is self-dual, then the socle of
P_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to the cosocle of
P_{\alpha}
, which is
L_{\alpha}
. Therefore when expressing
[P_{\alpha}]
as a sum of simple classes times powers of
q
in the Grothendieck group,
L_{\alpha}
is the only simple to appear in the top degree. On the other hand, by Theorem
7.9
,

[P_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\beta\in C_{\alpha}}q^{d_{\alpha\beta}}[V_{\beta}],

so the unique simple class appearing in the highest degree must also appear as the highest degree term of some
[V_{\beta}]
. We conclude that
L_{\alpha}
is the socle of
V_{\beta}
.

(3)\implies(4):
Let
b=\mu^{-1}(\beta)
. Lemma
7.2
says that
V_{\beta}
is spanned as a vector space by taut paths
p_{\gamma}
from
\beta
to
\gamma\preceq\beta
. A taut path
p_{\alpha}
is in the socle of
V_{\beta}
if there does not exist a longer taut path
p_{\gamma}
that factors through
p_{\alpha}
. Note that this is equivalent to the condition: if
i\not\in b
and
\alpha
has a feasible face
Y
such that
Y(i)=0
, then
\alpha(i)\neq\beta(i)
.

Recall that
Y_{b}\in{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the feasible cocircuit of
{\mathcal{N}}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
that is the optimal solution of the tope
T_{\beta}
. By the covector axioms of an oriented matroid, the composition
T:=(-Y_{b})\circ T_{\alpha}
is also a covector of
{\mathcal{N}}
and in particular an infeasible tope such that
T(i)=\alpha(i)
for all
i\in b
and for all
i
which are zero on a feasible face of
\alpha
.
A taut path
p
from
T_{\alpha}
to
T
exists in the tope graph of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
, and this path cannot change the sign corresponding to any feasible facet of
T_{\alpha}
. Thus the first sign change of the path
p
must be infeasible, which means that
T_{\alpha}
covers a subtope
X
with
X(g)=0
.

(4)\implies(5)
:
If the bounded tope
T_{\alpha}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\backslash f
covers a subtope
X
with
X(g)=0
then
\alpha
is a bounded feasible sign vector for both the original program
{\mathbf{P}}
as well as the reoriented program
{}_{-g}{\mathbf{P}}=({}_{-g}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},-g,f)
. Dually, this means that the tope
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee}\backslash g=({}_{-g}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee})\backslash(-g)
is bounded and feasible in both dual programs
{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}
and
{}_{-g}{\mathbf{P}}^{\vee}=({}_{-g}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}^{\vee},f,-g)
. In particular, the tope
\alpha\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}
does not have any cocircuit face
Y
with
Y(f)=0
, since this would imply
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
was unbounded in one of these generic programs.

(5)\implies(2)
: If
(T_{\alpha}^{\vee})^{\infty}=0
, then
e_{\alpha}Ae_{\alpha}\simeq R_{\alpha}^{\vee}=k[z(\Delta_{\alpha}^{\vee})]/(U^{\perp})
, where
\Delta_{\alpha}^{\vee}={\mathfrak{F}}_{lv}(T_{\alpha}^{\vee})\backslash\{0\}
. By Lemma
5.4
,
\|\Delta_{\alpha}^{\vee}\|=\|{\mathfrak{F}}_{lv}(T_{\alpha}^{\vee})\backslash\{0\}\|
is homeomorphic to a
(n-d-1)
-sphere and so a result of Munkres (see
[
Sta04
, Theorem II.4.3]
) implies that
R_{\alpha}^{\vee}
is Gorenstein, meaning that there is an isomorphism

\int:(e_{\alpha}Ae_{\alpha})_{n-d-1}\rightarrow k

such that
\langle x,y\rangle=\int xy
defines a perfect pairing on
e_{\alpha}Ae_{\alpha}
.

We wish to produce an isomorphism of
A
-modules
\textrm{d}(P_{\alpha})\cong P_{\alpha}
. To do so, we will show that the map

\langle-,-\rangle:e_{\alpha}A\times Ae_{\alpha}\rightarrow k

\qquad\qquad\qquad(p,q)\mapsto\int pq

defines a perfect pairing and so it will follow that
\textrm{d}(P_{\alpha})=(e_{\alpha}A)^{*}\cong Ae_{\alpha}=P_{\alpha}
as right
A
-modules.

To prove that
\langle-,-\rangle
is perfect, we first observe that it suffices to show that the map
\cdot p_{\beta,\alpha}:e_{\alpha}Ae_{\beta}\rightarrow e_{\alpha}Ae_{\alpha}
is injective for some taut path
p_{\beta\alpha}
from
\beta
to
\alpha
. This is because for any nonzero
x\in e_{\beta}Ae_{\alpha}
, if
x\cdot p_{\beta\alpha}\neq 0
, then there exists
y\in e_{\alpha}Ae_{\alpha}
such that
\int(xp)y=\int x(py)=\langle x,py\rangle\neq 0
. On the
B
side, this means showing that

\displaystyle\cdot u_{S_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}}:R_{\alpha\beta}^{\vee}\rightarrow R_{\alpha}^{\vee}

is injective. We proceed by showing

\displaystyle\cdot u_{S_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}}:\widetilde{R}_{\alpha\beta}^{\vee}\rightarrow\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}^{\vee}

is the injective map in a split short exact sequence of
\Sym U^{\perp}
-modules, which proves the claim by applying the functor
-\otimes_{\Sym U^{\perp}}k
.

The claim is obvious if
\alpha=\beta
or
T^{\vee}_{\alpha}\wedge T^{\vee}_{\beta}
is not feasible, so we assume
\alpha\neq\beta
and
T^{\vee}_{\alpha}\wedge T^{\vee}_{\beta}
is a proper non-empty face of
T_{\alpha}^{\vee}
.
To see the monomial map
\cdot u_{S_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}}:\widetilde{R}_{\alpha\beta}^{\vee}\rightarrow\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}^{\vee}
is injective and to determine its cokernel, consider the image of any non-zero monomial
m=\prod_{i\in S}u_{i}^{s_{i}}
in
\widetilde{R}^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}
, where
s_{i}>0
for any
i\in S
. As
m
is non-zero in
\widetilde{R}^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}
, there exists
Y\in\Delta_{\alpha\beta}^{\vee}
such that
S\subset z(Y)
. Note that
S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}=z(T^{\vee}_{\alpha}\wedge T^{\vee}_{\beta})\subset z(X)
for any
X\in\Delta^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}
. Thus
S\cup S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}\subset z(Y)
and the product
m\cdot u_{S_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}}=\prod_{i\in S\cup S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}}u_{i}^{t_{i}}
, where
t_{i}>0
for
i\in S\cup S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}
, is non-zero in
\widetilde{R}^{\vee}_{\alpha}
.

The computation above also shows that the cokernel of the map
\cdot u_{S_{\alpha\alpha}^{\beta}}:\widetilde{R}^{\vee}_{\alpha\beta}\rightarrow\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}^{\vee}
is the face ring
k[\Delta]
of

\Delta=\{S\subset E\mid S\subset z(Y)\;\text{for some}\>Y\in\Delta_{\alpha}\;\text{and}\>S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}\not\subset S\}.

=z(\Delta_{\alpha})\backslash\{S\in z(\Delta_{\alpha})\mid S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}\subset S\}.

Recall from Lemma
5.4
that the geometric realization of the simplicial complex
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
is a PL
(d-1)
-sphere. The subset of simplices
\{S\in z(\Delta_{\alpha})\mid S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}\subset S\}
is the open star of the simplex on the set
S^{\beta}_{\alpha\alpha}
and thus its complement
\Delta
in
z(\Delta_{\alpha})
is a PL
(d-1)
-ball. It follows that
k[\Delta]
is Cohen-Macaulay, again with parameter space
U^{\perp}
. Thus,
k[\Delta]
is a free
\Sym U^{\perp}
-module, and therefore the exact sequence

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}_{\alpha\beta}^{\vee}\hookrightarrow\widetilde{R}_{\alpha}^{\vee}\twoheadrightarrow k[\Delta]

splits.
∎

8.
Derived Morita equivalence

We conclude with a proof of Theorem
1.16
. Recall that
{\mathcal{M}}
is an oriented matroid,
U
a parameter space for
M=\underline{{\mathcal{M}}}
, and
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1},g_{1},f_{1}),{\mathbf{P}}_{2}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2},g_{2},f_{2})
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}},g_{2},f_{1})
are Euclidean generic oriented matroid programs such that

{\mathcal{M}}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}/g_{1})\backslash f_{1}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}/g_{2})\backslash f_{2}

and

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}/g_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}/g_{1},\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}\backslash f_{1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\backslash f_{2}.

We wish to show there is an equivalence of categories

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{1},U))\cong D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2},U)),

where
D(A)
denotes the bounded derived category of graded finitely generated
A
-modules.

Remark 8.1
.

Note that if
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{2}
are Euclidean, it is not automatic that
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}}
will be Euclidean as well. For example, one could take
\text{EFM}(8)
(see Example
7.10
) and then change the choice of
g
and
f
separately to obtain two realizable (and hence Euclidean) generic oriented matroid programs
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{2}
such that
{\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}}
is the non-Euclidean program
\text{EFM}(8)
.

We will prove Theorem
1.16
by reducing it to the following claim.

Proposition 8.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{P}}_{1}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1},g_{1},f)
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{2}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2},g_{2},f)
are generic Euclidean programs extending
{\mathcal{M}}
such that
{\mathcal{M}}_{1}/g_{1}={\mathcal{M}}_{2}/g_{2}
. Then there is an equivalence of categories

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{1},U))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2},U)).

Before giving a proof of this Proposition, we will use it to deduce Theorem
1.16
.

Proof that Proposition
8.2
implies Theorem
1.16
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.16
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}/g_{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{1}/g_{1}
. Then by Proposition
8.2
it follows that

(8.1)

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{1},U))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}},U).

On the other side, duality together with the assumptions of Theorem
1.16
give:

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}^{\vee}/f_{1}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}\backslash f_{1})^{\vee}=(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}\backslash f_{2})^{\vee}=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{\vee}/f_{2}.

Viewing
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
as playing the role of
g
in the Euclidean programs
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{mid}}^{\vee}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{\vee}
respectively, we can again apply Proposition
8.2
to find:

(8.2)

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2}^{\vee},U^{\perp}))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}}^{\vee},U^{\perp})).

Putting these equivalences (
8.1
) and (
8.2
) together with the equivalences from Koszul duality:

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}},U))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{\text{mid}}^{\vee},U^{\perp}))\quad\text{and}\quad D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2},U))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2}^{\vee},U^{\perp})),

gives the desired result:

D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{1},U))\simeq D(A({\mathbf{P}}_{2},U)).

∎

8.1.
The definition and properties of the functor

It remains to prove Proposition
8.2
. For the remainder of the paper we will let

To check that these definitions of
