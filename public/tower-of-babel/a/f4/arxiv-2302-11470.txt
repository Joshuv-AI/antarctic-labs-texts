Surjective morphisms from affine space to its Zariski open subsets
Viktor Balch Barth

Abstract
We prove constructively the existence of surjective morphisms from affine space onto certain open subvarieties of affine space of the same dimension. For any algebraic set Z ⊂ 𝔸 n − 2 ⊂ 𝔸 n Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n} , we construct an endomorphism of 𝔸 n \mathbb{A}^{n} with 𝔸 n ∖ Z \mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z as its image. By Noether’s normalization lemma, these results extend to give surjective maps from any n n -dimensional affine variety X X to 𝔸 n ∖ Z \mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z .



1
Introduction

In the context of Oka theory, Forstnerič
[
7
]
showed that every connected Oka manifold
Y
admits a surjective holomorphic map from an affine space
\mathbb{A}^{N}
.
Moreover, this affine space can be taken to be
\mathbb{A}^{\dim Y}
.
Motivated by this, Arzhantsev and Kusakabe
[
1
,
14
]
recently proved analogous results in the algebraic setting.
For this paper, we consider algebraic varieties over an algebraically closed field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic zero.

Definition 1.1
.

An algebraic variety
Y
is called an
A
-image
if for some positive integer
N
there is a surjective morphism
f:\mathbb{A}^{N}\to Y
.

Arzhantsev proved that every very flexible variety is an
A
-image
[
1
]
.
As shown in
[
2
]
, every very flexible variety is smooth and subelliptic.
Kusakabe extended Arzhantsev’s result by showing that any smooth subelliptic variety
Y
admits a surjective morphism
f:\mathbb{A}^{\dim Y+1}\to Y

[
14
]
.
For a definition of algebraic subellipticity, see
[
6
, Definition 5.6.13 (e)]
, and note that it was recently shown to be equivalent to algebraic ellipticity for smooth varieties
[
13
]
.
Whether any
A
-image
Y
also admits surjective morphisms
f:\mathbb{A}^{\dim Y}\to Y
is an open question

[
1
,
15
,
7
,
8
]
.
When
Y
is a smooth
proper
subelliptic variety, Forstnerič proved over the complex numbers that such a surjective map from
\mathbb{A}^{\dim Y}
does indeed exist
[
7
, Theorem 1.6]
. It is worth pointing out that Forstnerič and Kusakabe’s results give maps which are surjective even when restricted to the smooth locus
\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus\left(\mathrm{Sing}(f)\right)
.

In this paper, we ask whether there are surjective morphisms
f:\mathbb{A}^{\dim Y}\to Y
for open subvarieties
Y\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
. For certain families of cases we give a positive answer. First of all, note that any
A
-image
Y
must have complement
\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Y
of codimension at least two. If not, then
Y
admits a nonconstant invertible regular function
g
which pulls back to a nonconstant invertible regular function
f^{*}(g)
on
\mathbb{A}^{N}
, giving a contradiction.
However, as long as
\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Y
has codimension at least two,
Y
is an
A
-image
[
1
,
14
]
.

In the two-dimensional case, Jelonek gave the example of a surjective map
f:\and\to\and\setminus\{(0,-1)\}
defined by
f(z_{1},z_{2})=(z_{1}(z_{1}z_{2}+1)-z_{2},z_{1}z_{2})

[
11
]
. He also proved that no such map of lower degree exists. Modifying and generalizing this example yields the constructions used in
1.2
and
1.5
. Note that Jelonek’s map answered the question of the existence of a surjective morphism
f:\and\to\and\setminus\{(0,0)\}
even before it was asked in
[
15
]
(and then answered again in
[
1
]
).

In what follows we state our main results and their corollaries, postponing the proofs until
Section

2
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be an algebraic set of the form
Z=F\times W\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}\times\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
, where
F\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
is a finite set of
l
points, and
W\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
is the zero set of
m
polynomials
q_{i}
of degree at most
d
.
Then there exists a surjective map

f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
of degree
\deg(f)\leq\max(1,l-1)\cdot\max(l+2,m+d+1)
.

Given a closed algebraic set
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{k}
satisfying some mild conditions, there are bounds on the number
m
of polynomials required to specify
Z
as a set, and on the maximal degree
d
of these polynomials. For
Z
an irreducible closed subset of
\mathbb{A}^{k}
,
[
9
, Proposition 3]
states that it is described by
k+1
polynomials of degree bounded by
\deg Z
.
If
Z
is a smooth equidimensional variety, then by
[
3
, Theorem I]
it is described by
m=(k-\dim Z)(1+\dim Z)
polynomials, of degree bounded by
\deg Z
. As a consequence, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1.3
.

If
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
is an irreducible closed
algebraic set,
then there exists a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
with
\deg(f)\leq n+\deg(Z)
. If
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
is a smooth equidimensional
variety,
then there exists a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
with
\deg(f)\leq(n-2-\dim(Z))(1+\dim(Z))+\deg(Z)+1
.

Srinivas and Kaliman
[
16
,
12
]
showed that if
n\geq\max(2\dim Z_{1}+1,\dim TZ_{1})+1
, and
\varphi:Z_{1}\to Z_{2}
is an isomorphism of closed varieties in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
, then
\varphi
extends to an
\mathbb{A}^{n}
-isomorphism. We obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
Z_{1}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be a closed variety such that
n\geq\max(2\dim Z_{1}+1,\dim TZ_{1})+1
. Then for any
Z_{2}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
isomorphic to
Z_{1}
, there exists a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z_{2}
.

Over the real numbers, Fernando and Gamboa
[
5
]
showed the existence of a surjective morphism
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus F
for any finite set
F
of
l
points, and
n\geq 2
. For
F
a subset of a line, their map
f
has degree
2(l+2)
. Morphisms of lower degree, namely
\deg(f)=l+2
, were obtained by El Hilany
[
10
]
in the setting where
F
is a subset of a line,
l
is even, in dimension
2
over
\mathbb{K}
an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.
In the following proposition, by use of a different construction, we extend El Hilany’s degree bound to also hold for
l
odd, and for any
n\geq 2
.

Proposition 1.5
.

For
n\geq 2
, and any set of
l
points
F\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
, there exists a morphism
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}
such that
f(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus F
.
In general, if
l>2
, then there exists an
f
with
\deg(f)\leq(l-1)\cdot(l+2)
. If
F
is a subset of a line, then there exists
f
with
\deg(f)=l+2
.

In particular, if
l<3
, then there is a map
f
with
\deg(f)=l+2
. It is worth noting that for
Y
open in
\and
,
Y
is an
A
-image precisely if
\and\setminus Y
has codimension two. Hence any two-dimensional
A
-image
Y\subset\and
admits a surjective morphism from
\and
.

Finally, recall that the Noether normalization lemma
[
4
, Theorem 13.3]
states that for any
n
-dimensional affine variety
X
, there exists a finite, hence surjective, morphism
X\to\mathbb{A}^{n}
. As a composition of surjections is surjective, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 1.6
.

For any
n
-dimensional affine variety
X
, and any
Y
admitting a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to Y
, there exists a surjective map

\varphi:X\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\to Y.

In particular, this shows that from any
n
-dimensional affine variety
X
, there are surjective maps onto varieties
Y
as in
1.2
and
1.5
. By combining Noether normalization with Kusakabe’s result
[
14
]
, we conclude that any smooth subelliptic variety
Y
of dimension
n
admits a surjective map from any
(n+1)
-dimensional affine variety
X
.

2
Proofs and explicit constructions

We first construct a map
g
in
2.1
, which we then use to prove
1.2
. We then prove
1.5
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
g:\mathbb{A}^{n}_{z}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}_{w}
be given by

g=\big(z_{1}+z_{2}(r(z_{1})z_{2}+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{2}^{i+1}q_{i},\,r(z_{1})z_{2}+1,\,z_{3},\,\ldots,\,z_{n}\big),

(1)

where
r(z_{1})
has
l
simple roots
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j}
, and the
m
polynomials
q_{i}(z_{3},z_{4},\ldots,z_{n})
have degree at most
d
. Then
g

has image
\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
, where
Z=\{\beta_{j}\}_{j}\times\{0\}\times W
, where

W\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-2}
is the set of common zeros of the
q_{i}
.

Proof.

Fix
w\in\mathbb{A}^{n}_{w}
, and notice that any point
z
in the preimage
g^{-1}(w)
must satisfy
z_{3}=w_{3},\ldots,z_{n}=w_{n}
.
Fixing the values of
z_{3},\ldots,z_{n}
will in turn determine the values
q_{i}(z_{3},\ldots,z_{n})=\alpha_{i}
.
Thus whether
g^{-1}(w)
is empty only depends on the values of
w_{1},w_{2},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
, and it reduces to solving
the following system of equations for
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
.

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=z_{1}+z_{2}(r(z_{1})z_{2}+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}z_{2}^{i+1}\alpha_{i}

(2)

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=r(z_{1})z_{2}+1.

(3)

Solve
Eq.

2
for
z_{1}
, then substitute for
z_{1}
in
Eq.

3
and simplify to obtain

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=w_{1}-z_{2}w_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}z_{2}^{i+1}

(4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=1-w_{2}+z_{2}\cdot r\left(w_{1}-w_{2}z_{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}z_{2}^{i+1}\right).

(5)

If
w\in\left(\{\beta_{j}\}_{j}\times\{0\}\times W\right)
, then
(w_{1},w_{2},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})=(\beta_{j},0,\ldots,0)
for some
j
, and
Eq.

5
reduces to
0=1
, which is clearly unsolvable.

If
w\notin\left(\{\beta_{j}\}_{j}\times\{0\}\times W\right)
, then
(w_{1},w_{2},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\neq(\beta_{j},0,\ldots,0)
for any
j
, and one may solve
Eq.

5
for
z_{2}
, and then solve
Eq.

4
for
z_{1}
. Hence,
g(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
.
∎

We may now prove the main theorem.

Proof of
1.2
.

Given
Z=F\times W
as in the theorem statement, we modify the function
g
in
Eq.

1
by composing with suitable automorphisms.
For any
F\subset\and
, first pick a convenient coordinate system by applying an affine
\and
-automorphism
t
such that
t(F)=\{p_{j}\}_{j}
, where the points
p_{j}
have pairwise distinct first coordinates
\beta_{j}=t(p_{j})^{(1)}
. To simplify the exposition, we assume
F=\{p_{j}\}_{j}
.
Define

r(z_{1})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(z_{1}-\beta_{j}\right),

(6)

and use
2.1
to construct a surjective map
g:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus\left(\{\beta_{j}\}_{j}\times\{0\}\times W\right)
.
Let
L(z_{1})
be a Lagrange polynomial such that
L(\beta_{j})=p_{j}^{(2)}
for all
j
, and
h=(z_{1},z_{2}+L(z_{1}),z_{3},\ldots,z_{n})
, and set
f=h\circ g
. Then
f\left(\mathbb{A}^{n}\right)=\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus Z
.
The degree of
h
is at most
\max(1,l-1)
, and
g
has degree at most
\max(l+2,m+d+1)
. Hence the degree of
f
is bounded by
\max(1,l-1)\cdot\max(l+2,m+d+1)
.
∎

Example 2.2
.

Let
Z\subset\mathbb{A}^{4}
be two parallel copies (contained in
\{(1,0)\}\times\and
and
\{(-1,0)\}\times\and
respectively) of a nodal cubic curve
W=V(w_{3}^{2}-w_{4}^{3}-w_{4}^{2})\subset\and
. Then

f=\big(z_{1}+z_{2}((z_{1}^{2}-1)z_{2}+1)+z_{2}^{2}(z_{3}^{2}-z_{4}^{3}-z_{4}^{2}),\,(z_{1}^{2}-1)z_{2}+1,\,z_{3},\,z_{4}\big)

(7)

is a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{4}\to\mathbb{A}^{4}\setminus Z
.

Example 2.3
.

Let
d\geq n>2
be natural numbers, and for
i=1,\ldots,n-2
, let
q_{i}(z_{i+2})=\prod_{j=1}^{j=d-i-1}(z_{i+2}-j)
. Similarly, let
r(z_{1})=\prod_{j=1}^{j=d-2}(z_{1}-j)
. Then
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}
given by

f=\big(z_{1}+z_{2}(r(z_{1})z_{2}+1)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}z_{2}^{i+1}q_{i},\,r(z_{1})z_{2}+1,\,z_{3},\,\ldots,\,z_{n}\big),

(8)

has degree
d
, and the complement of the image of
f
is the set
\{1,2,\ldots,d-2\}\times\{0\}\times\{1,\ldots,d-2\}\times\{1,\ldots,d-3\}\times\ldots\times\{1,2,\ldots,d-n+1\}
, consisting of
(d-2)\cdot(d-2)!/(d-n)!
points.

The above example gives a function with superexponentially many points in the complement of the image as
n
and
d
grow. We now construct
f
in
1.5
, which only avoids
\deg(f)-2
points. However, for any
n
, the proposition gives a surjective map
f:\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
of degree only
\deg(f)=3
.

Proof of
1.5
.

Given
l
points
F=\{p_{j}\}_{j}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
, pick a coordinate system in which
p_{j}^{(1)}
are all pairwise distinct, as in the proof of
1.2
. Define the polynomial

r(z_{1})=\prod_{j=1}^{l}\left(z_{1}-p_{j}^{(1)}\right)
.
Then define
\sigma_{2,r}:\and_{z}\to\and_{w}
to be

\sigma_{2,r}=\big(z_{1}+z_{2}(r(z_{1})z_{2}+1),\,r(z_{1})z_{2}+1\big).

(9)

Note that
\sigma_{2,r}
is equal to
g
defined in
Eq.

1
, and hence
\deg(\sigma_{2,r})=l+2
.
For
n>2
, define
\sigma_{n,r}:\mathbb{A}^{n}_{z}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}_{w}
to be

\sigma_{n,r}=\big(z_{1}+z_{2}(r(z_{1})z_{2}+1),\,r(z_{1})z_{2}+1+z_{3},\,z_{3}^{2}+z_{4},\,\ldots,\,z_{n-1}^{2}+z_{n},\,z_{n}^{2}\big).

(10)

Let
n>2
and fix
w\in\mathbb{A}^{n}_{w}
. The same calculation as in the proof of
2.1
leads to an equation similar to
Eq.

5
, namely

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=1-(w_{2}-z_{3})+z_{2}\cdot r\left(w_{1}-(w_{2}-z_{3})z_{2}\right).

(11)

This is unsolvable for
z_{2}
if and only if
w_{1}\in\{p_{j}^{(1)}\}_{j}
and
w_{2}=z_{3}
. However,
z_{3}=\pm\sqrt{w_{3}\mp\sqrt{w_{4}\pm\sqrt{\ldots}}}
, which has multiple solutions unless
w_{3}=0,\ldots,w_{n}=0
.
Hence
\sigma_{n,r}(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus\left(\{p_{1}^{(1)},p_{2}^{(1)},\ldots,p_{l}^{(1)}\}\times\{0\}\times\ldots\times\{0\}\right)
.
Similarly to the proof of
1.2
, we may construct an
\mathbb{A}^{n}
-automorphism
h
by use of interpolation polynomials
L_{i}(z_{1})
such that
L_{i}\left(p_{j}^{(1)}\right)=p_{j}^{(i)}
for all
j
. By composing, we obtain
f=h\circ\sigma_{n,r}
, which satisfies
f(\mathbb{A}^{n})=\mathbb{A}^{n}\setminus F
, and has degree bounded by
\deg(f)\leq(l-1)(l+2).
If
F
is a subset of a line, then
h
can be an affine automorphism, and we obtain
\deg(f)=l+2
.
∎
