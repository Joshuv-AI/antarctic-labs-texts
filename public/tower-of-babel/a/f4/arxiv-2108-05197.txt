Mirror symmetry and rigid structures of generalized K3 surfaces
Atsushi Kanazawa

Abstract
. The present article is concerned with mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces, with particular emphasis on complex and Kähler rigid structures. Inspired by the works of Dolgachev, Aspinwall–Morrison and Huybrechts, we introduce a formulation of mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces by using Mukai lattice polarizations. This approach solves issues in the conventional formulations of mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces. In particular, we provide a solution to the problem of mirror symmetry for singular K3 surfaces. Along the way, we investigate complex and Kähler rigid structures of generalized K3 surfaces.

1.
Introduction

The present article is concerned with mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces, with particular emphasis on complex and Kähler rigid structures.
A few important aspects of mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces are unified in the framework of generalized K3 surfaces.

Mirror symmetry for a K3 surface
S
is a very subtle problem, as the complex and Kähler structures are somewhat mixed in
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{C})
.
In the foundational article
[
7
]
, Dolgachev formulated mirror symmetry for lattice polarized K3 surfaces as a duality between the algebraic and transcendental cycles.
Although his formulation works well in many cases, it cannot be definitive due to an assumption that does not hold in general.
A notable exception is a singular K3 surface, which has the maximal Picard number
20
.
This kind of K3 surfaces, also known as a rigid K3 surface, admits no deformation of complex structure while retaining the maximal Picard number.
A satisfactory formulation of mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces, particularly one that solves the puzzle of mirror symmetry for singular K3 surfaces, has been anticipated.

The celebrated work
[
3
]
of Aspinwall–Morrison is a key article explaining mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces from a physics perspective.
They discussed SCFTs on a K3 surface
S
and demonstrated that the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{(2,2)}
of
N=(2,2)
SCFTs fibers over the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{(4,4)}
of
N=(4,4)
SCFTs.
Mathematically such moduli spaces are related with space-like 4-spaces in
H^{*}(S,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{4,20}
equipped with the Mukai pairing, and

\mathfrak{M}_{(2,2)}
and
\mathfrak{M}_{(4,4)}
are identified with certain Grassmannians
\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))\cong\mathrm{O}(4,20)/(\mathrm{SO}(2)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)\times\mathrm{O}(20))

and
\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{4}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))\cong\mathrm{O}(4,20)/(\mathrm{SO}(4)\times\mathrm{O}(20))
respectively.
Then mirror symmetry is realized as an involution of these moduli spaces.
On the other hand, the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{HK}}
of
B
-field shifts of hyperKähler metrics
can be identified with an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{M}_{(4,4)}
by the period map
\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{HK}}
.
Inspired by this fact, Huybrechts showed in
[
13
]
that the counterpart of
\mathfrak{M}_{(2,2)}
is given by the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}
of generalized K3 surfaces,
which are the K3 version of the generalized Calabi–Yau structures introduced by Hitchin
[
12
]
.
The moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{K3}}\times H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
of
B
-field shifts of K3 surfaces endowed with a Ricci-flat metric is naturally contained in
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}
of real codimension
2
.
To summarize, there is the following diagram.

\textstyle{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{K3}}\times H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{gK3}}}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))=\mathfrak{M}_{(2,2)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{HK}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{HK}}}
\textstyle{\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{4}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))=\mathfrak{M}_{(4,4)}}

It is therefore natural to expect that points in
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{K3}}
might be mirror symmetric to points that are no longer in
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{K3}}
.
We will demonstrate that this is precisely the source of the problems in the conventional formulation of mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces (cf. Remark
4.6
).

There is a good chemistry between Hitchin’s generalized Calabi–Yau structures and mirror symmetry as they both embrace the symplectic and complex on an equal footing.
Inspired by works of Dolgachev, Aspinwall–Morrison and Huybrechts, we introduce a formulation of mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces (Section
4
).
The key features of our formulation are twofold, and both are guided by Hitchin’s theory.
The first is to extend the scope of lattice polarizations to the Mukai lattice.
Mixture of degrees of cycles is indispensable for generalized K3 surfaces.
The second is to treat the two structures in mirror symmetry on completely equal footing.
The Néron–Severi lattice and transcendental lattice are defined in the same fashion, and moreover the lattice polarizations are imposed on both.

The primary advantage of our formulation is that the elements in
H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})
are no longer treated as special.
This allows us to eliminate the artificial assumption present in Dolgachev’s formulation of mirror symmetry.
Indeed, the assumption is a reflection of the existence of the hyperbolic lattice
U\cong H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})
under mirror symmetry.
Our formulation naturally settles the two fundamental problems in the conventional formulation (Section
4.2
).

Along the way, we investigate the complex and Kähler rigid structures of generalized K3 surfaces (Section
3
).
The notion of a complex rigid structure is enhanced to the level of generalized K3 surfaces.
On the other hand, the notion of a Kähler rigid structure naturally appears as a counterpart of complex rigid structure from the viewpoint of mirror symmetry.
We define this notion by using a generalized Calabi–Yau structure, which captures an interesting sublattice of
H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
derived from the Kähler moduli space (Section
3.4
).

2.
Generalized K3 surfaces

Hitchin’s invention of generalized Calabi–Yau structures is a key to unify the symplectic and complex structures
[
12
]
.
Such structures have been extensively studied in 2-dimensions by Huybrechts
[
13
]
.
In this section, for the sake of completeness, we provide a brief summary of Huybrechts’ work.

2.1.
Generalized Calabi–Yau structures

Let
M
be a differentiable manifold underlying a K3 surface and
A^{2*}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)=\oplus_{i=0}^{2}A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2i}(M)
the space of even differential forms with
\mathbb{C}
-coefficients.
Let
\varphi_{i}
denote the degree
i
part of
\varphi\in A^{2*}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
.
We define a pairing on
A_{\mathbb{C}}^{2*}(M)
by

\langle\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\rangle:=\varphi_{2}\wedge\varphi^{\prime}_{2}-\varphi_{0}\wedge\varphi^{\prime}_{4}-\varphi_{4}\wedge\varphi^{\prime}_{0}\in A_{\mathbb{C}}^{4}(M),

which is the Mukai pairing on the level of differential forms.

Definition 2.1

(generalized Calabi–Yau structure)
.

A generalized Calabi–Yau structure on
M
is a closed form
\varphi\in A^{2*}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
such that

\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle=0,\ \ \ \langle\varphi,\overline{\varphi}\rangle>0.

The special appeal of generalized Calabi–Yau structures resides in the fact that they embrace complex and symplectic structures on an equal footing.
There are two fundamental Calabi–Yau structures:

(1)

A symplectic structure
\omega
on
M
induces a generalized Calabi–Yau structure

\varphi=e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}:=1+\sqrt{-1}\omega-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}.

(2)

A complex structure
J
of
M
makes
M
a K3 surface
M_{J}
.
A holomorphic
2
-form
\sigma
of
M_{J}
, which is unique up to scaling, defines a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi=\sigma
.
We also write
M_{\sigma}=M_{J}
.

For
B\in A^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
,
e^{B}
acts on
A^{2*}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
by exterior product, i.e.

e^{B}\varphi:=(1+B+\frac{1}{2}B\wedge B)\wedge\varphi.

This action is orthogonal with respect to the pairing
\langle-,-\rangle
, namely

\langle e^{B}\varphi,e^{B}\varphi^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\rangle.

A real closed 2-form is called a
B
-field.
For a
B
-field
B
and a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi
,
the
B
-field shift
e^{B}\varphi
is a generalized Calabi–Yau structure.

We will later show that a
B
-field is indispensable when we view complex and symplectic structures as special instance of a more general notion (Remark
2.3
).
The following shows that a generalized Calabi–Yau structure is a
B
-field shift of either one of the fundamental Calabi–Yau structures.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\varphi
be a generalized Calabi–Yau structure.

(A)

If
\varphi_{0}\neq 0
, then

\varphi=e^{B}(\varphi_{0}e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega})=\varphi_{0}e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}

with a symplectic
\omega
and a
B
-field
B
.

(B)

If
\varphi_{0}=0
, then

\varphi=e^{B}\sigma=\sigma+B^{0,2}\wedge\sigma

with a holomorphic 2-form
\sigma
(for a complex structure) and a
B
-field
B
.

B^{0,2}
is the
(0,2)
-part of
B
with respect to
\sigma
.

Generalized Calabi–Yau structures of (A) and (B) in Proposition
2.2
are called type
A
and type
B
respectively.

We consider the group
\mathrm{Diff}_{*}(M)
of the diffeomorphisms
f
of
M
such that the induced action
f^{*}
on
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
is trivial.
Generalized Calabi–Yau structures
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are called isomorphic if there exist an exact
B
-field
B
and a diffeomorphism
f\in\mathrm{Diff}_{*}(M)

such that
\varphi=e^{B}f^{*}\varphi^{\prime}
.

The most fascinating aspect of generalized Calabi–Yau structures is the occurrence of the classical Calabi–Yau structure
\sigma
(type
B
)
as well as of symplectic generalized Calabi–Yau structure
e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}
(type
A
) in the same moduli space.
This allows us to pass from the symplectic to the complex world in a continuous fashion.

Remark 2.3

(
[
12
]
)
.

For a complex structure
\sigma
, the real and imaginary parts
\mathrm{Re}(\sigma),\mathrm{Im}(\sigma)
are symplectic forms.
A family of generalized Calabi–Yau structures of type
A

\varphi_{t}:=te^{\frac{1}{t}(\mathrm{Re}(\sigma)+\sqrt{-1}\mathrm{Im}(\sigma))}=t+\sigma

converges, as
t
goes to
0
, to the generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\sigma
of type
B
.
In this way, the
B
-fields interpolate between generalized Calabi–Yau structures of type
A
and
B
.

2.2.
(hyper)Kähler structures

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\varphi
be a generalized Calabi–Yau structure.
We define
P_{\varphi}\subset A^{*}(M)
and
P_{[\varphi]}\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})
by

\displaystyle P_{\varphi}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Re}\varphi\oplus\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Im}\varphi\subset A^{*}(M),

\displaystyle P_{[\varphi]}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Re}\varphi]\oplus\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{Im}\varphi]\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})

i.e. the
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces spanned by the real and imaginary parts of
\varphi
and
[\varphi]
respectively.

P_{\varphi}
is positive with respect to the pairing
\langle-,-\rangle
at every point.

P_{[\varphi]}\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{4,20}
is a positive 2-plane with respect to the Makai pairing.

We say that generalized Calabi–Yau structures
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
are orthogonal if
P_{\varphi}
and
P_{\varphi^{\prime}}
are pointwise orthogonal.
This orthogonality is a stronger condition than just
\langle\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\rangle=0
.

Definition 2.5

(Kähler)
.

A generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi
is called Kähler if there exists another generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi^{\prime}
orthogonal to
\varphi
.
In this case,
\varphi^{\prime}
is called a Kähler structure for
\varphi
.

Example 2.6
.

Let
\varphi=\sigma
be a holomorphic
2
-form for a complex structure on
M_{\sigma}
.
If
\varphi^{\prime}
is a Kähler structure for
\varphi
, then it is of the form
\varphi^{\prime}=\varphi^{\prime}_{0}e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}
(type
A
)
for some scalar
\varphi_{0}^{\prime}
,
B
-field
B
and symplectic form
\omega
.
The orthogonality is equivalent to
\sigma\wedge B=\sigma\wedge\omega=0
.
Therefore
B
is a closed real
(1,1)
-form and either
\pm\omega
is a Kähler form with respect to
\sigma
.

A hyperKähler structure is then defined as a special instance of a Kähler structure.
Recall first that a Kähler form
\omega
on a K3 surface is a hyperKähler form if
2\omega^{2}=C\sigma\wedge\overline{\sigma}
for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
,
which may be assumed to be
1
by rescaling
\sigma
.

Definition 2.7

(hyperKähler)
.

A generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi
is hyperKähler if there exists a Kähler structure
\varphi^{\prime}
for
\varphi

such that

\langle\varphi,\overline{\varphi}\rangle=\langle\varphi^{\prime},\overline{\varphi^{\prime}}\rangle.

Such
\varphi^{\prime}
is called a hyperKähler structure for
\varphi
.

Remark 2.8
.

(1)

The definition of a (hyper)Kähler structure is symmetric for
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
.

(2)

Let
\varphi
be a generalized Calabi–Yau structure and
\varphi^{\prime}
a (hyper)Kähler for
\varphi
.
Then
e^{B}\varphi^{\prime}
is a (hyper)Kähler structure for
e^{B}\varphi
.

For the later use, we give a classification of the hyperKähler structures for a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi
.
By Remark
2.8
, we may first assume that
\varphi
is either of the form
\varphi=\lambda e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}
or
\varphi=\sigma
.

(A)

If
\varphi=\lambda e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}
, then a hyperKähler structure is either

(A)

a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi^{\prime}=\lambda^{\prime}e^{B^{\prime}+\sqrt{-1}\omega^{\prime}}
such that

(i)

\omega\wedge\omega^{\prime}=\omega\wedge B^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\wedge B=0
,
B^{\prime 2}=\omega^{2}+\omega^{\prime 2}
,

(ii)

|\lambda|^{2}\omega^{2}=|\lambda^{\prime}|^{2}\omega^{\prime 2}
,

(B)

a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma
, where either
\pm\omega
is a hyperKähler form such that
2|\lambda|^{2}\omega^{2}=\sigma\wedge\overline{\sigma}
.

(B)

If
\varphi=\sigma
, then a hyperKähler structure is a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi^{\prime}=\lambda e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}
,
where
B
is a closed
(1,1)
-form and either
\pm\omega
is a hyperKähler form such that
2|\lambda|^{2}\omega^{2}=\sigma\wedge\overline{\sigma}
.

Any hyperKähler structure is a
B
-field shift of one of the above cases.
In summary, there are only 3 possible combinations:

(type
A
, type
B
), (type
B
, type
A
), (type
A
, type
A
).

2.3.
Generalized K3 surfaces

Definition 2.9

(generalized K3 surface)
.

A generalized K3 surface is a pair
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
of generalized Calabi–Yau structures
such that
\varphi
is a hyperKähler structure for
\varphi^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.10
.

A K3 surface
M_{\sigma}
with a chosen hyperKähler structure
\omega
(which means
2\omega^{2}=\sigma\wedge\overline{\sigma}
)
can be identified with a generalized K3 surface
(e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega},\sigma)
.

A
B
-field shift
e^{B}(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})=(e^{B}\varphi,e^{B}\varphi^{\prime})
of a generalized K3 surface
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
is a generalized K3 surface.
Generalized K3 surfaces
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
and
(\psi,\psi^{\prime})
are called isomorphic if there exist a diffeomorphism
f\in\mathrm{Diff}_{*}(M)
and an exact
B
-field
B\in A^{2}(M)

such that
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})=e^{B}f^{*}(\psi,\psi^{\prime})
.
As indicated in the introduction, the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}
of generalized K3 surfaces has a nice description in terms of the cohomology groups.

Theorem 2.11
.

The period map

\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{gK3}}:\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})),\ \ \ (\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\mapsto(P_{[\varphi]},P_{[\varphi^{\prime}]})

is an immersion with dense image.
Here
\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))
is the Grassmannian parametrizing the orthogonal pairs of positive oriented 2-planes in
H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{4,20}
.

A point in the complement of the image of
\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{gK3}}
is orthogonal to a
(-2)
-class and can be interpreted as a degenerate structure.

3.
Rigid structures

3.1.
Lattices and K3 surfaces

In order to fix notation, let us begin with some basics of lattices and K3 surfaces.
The hyperbolic lattice
U
is the rank
2
even unimodular lattice defined by the Gram matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}
.

E_{8}
is the rank
8
negative-definite even unimodular lattice defined by the corresponding Cartan matrix.
We denote by
\Lambda_{K3}:=U^{\oplus 3}\oplus E_{8}^{\oplus 2}
the K3 lattice.
For a lattice
L
, we write
L_{\mathbb{Q}}:=L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
, and
L_{\mathbb{R}}
and
L_{\mathbb{C}}
are defined in a similar manner.

Let
S
be a K3 surface.
Then
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
with its intersection form is isomorphic to the K3 lattice
\Lambda_{K3}
.
The Néron-Severi lattice and transcendental lattice are defined respectively by

NS(S):=H^{1,1}(S,\mathbb{R})\cap H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z}),\ \ \ T(S):=NS(S)^{\perp},

where the orthogonal complement is taken in
H^{2}(S,\mathbb{Z})
.
The Picard number is
\rho(S):=\mathrm{rank}(NS(S))
.
The extended Néron-Severi lattice
NS^{\prime}(S)
is the sublattice

\displaystyle NS^{\prime}(S):

\displaystyle=H^{0}(S,\mathbb{Z})\oplus NS(S)\oplus H^{4}(S,\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong NS(S)\oplus U

of the Mukai lattice
H^{*}(S,\mathbb{Z})\cong U^{\oplus 4}\oplus E_{8}^{\oplus 2}
.

3.2.
Néron–Severi lattice and transcendental lattice

We introduce the Néron–Severi lattice and the transcendental lattice of a generalized K3 surface
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.1
.

The Néron–Severi lattice and transcendental lattices of
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
are defined respectively by

\displaystyle\widetilde{NS}(X):=\{\delta\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})\ |\ \langle\delta,\varphi^{\prime}\rangle=0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(X):=\{\delta\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})\ |\ \langle\delta,\varphi\rangle=0\}.

In
[
13
]
the Picard group
\mathrm{Pic}(\varphi)
and the transcendental lattice
T(\varphi)
are defined for a generalized Calabi–Yau structure
\varphi
.
Our definition of the transcendental lattice is different from
[
13
]
, where it is defined as the orthogonal complement of the Picard group.

It is important that we define
\widetilde{NS}(X)
and
\widetilde{T}(X)
on a completely equal footing,
and hence the intersection
\widetilde{NS}(X)\cap\widetilde{T}(X)
may be non-trivial.
Moreover, it is easy to see that

\varphi\in\widetilde{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{C}},\ \ \ \varphi^{\prime}\in\widetilde{T}(X)_{\mathbb{C}},

and

2\leq\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{NS}(X))\leq 22,\ \ \ 2\leq\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{T}(X))\leq 22.

For example,
\varphi
is chosen generically in
\widetilde{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{C}}
, then
\widetilde{NS}(X)^{\perp}=\widetilde{T}(X)
.

For later use, given
\psi\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{C})
, we denote by
L_{\psi}
the smallest sublattice
L\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
such that
\psi\in L_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then we may write

\widetilde{NS}(X)=L_{\varphi^{\prime}}^{\perp},\ \ \ \widetilde{T}(X)=L_{\varphi}^{\perp}.

Remark 3.2
.

In general,
H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z}),H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})\not\subset\widetilde{NS}(X)
.
This feature has an important consequence in our formulation of mirror symmetry (Section
4
).

The following examples will play an important role in Section
4
. (cf. Example 4.4 of
[
13
]
)

Example 3.3
.

We denote by
\delta_{i}
the degree
i
part of
\delta\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
.

(1)

If
\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma+B^{0,2}\wedge\sigma
, a
B
-field shift of
\sigma
by
B\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{NS}(X)

\displaystyle=\{\delta_{0}+\delta_{2}\ |\ \int_{M}\delta_{2}\wedge\sigma=\delta_{0}\int_{M}B^{0,2}\wedge\sigma\}\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=e^{B}NS^{\prime}(M_{\sigma})_{\mathbb{R}}\cap H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z}).

There is a natural inclusion
NS(M_{\sigma})\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})\subset\widetilde{NS}(X)
.
The inclusion is strict if
B\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Q})
, and then
\widetilde{NS}(X)
contains the lattice

\big(\mathbb{Z}(1,B,\frac{1}{2}B^{2})\cap H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})\big)\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})\cong U(m)

for
m
minimal with
m(1,B,\frac{1}{2}B^{2})\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
.
In particular, for the classical case
\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma
, we recover

\displaystyle\widetilde{NS}(X)

\displaystyle=H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z})\oplus NS(M_{\sigma})\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=NS^{\prime}(M_{\sigma}).

(2)

If
\varphi=e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}
for
B\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
and a symplectic form
\omega
, the condition
\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{M}\delta_{2}\wedge B-\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}\int_{M}(B^{2}-\omega^{2})+\int_{M}\delta_{4}=0,

\displaystyle\int_{M}\delta_{2}\wedge\omega-\delta_{0}\int_{M}B\wedge\omega=0.

In particular, if
\sigma
is a holomorphic 2-form for a complex structure on
M
,
then for a generic choice of
B,\omega\in NS(M_{\sigma})

\widetilde{T}(X)=T(M_{\sigma}).

3.3.
Complex rigidity

The singular K3 surfaces, also known as complex rigid K3 surfaces, do not admit any complex deformation while maintaining the maximal Picard number
20

(or equivalently, the minimal rank
2
of the transcendental lattice).
We first provide a generalization of the singular K3 surfaces.

Definition 3.4
.

A generalized K3 surface
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
is called complex rigid if
\varphi^{\prime}
is of type
B
and
\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{NS}(X))=22
.

Note that the condition
\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{NS}(X))=22
is equivalent to
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi^{\prime}})=2
.

Theorem 3.5
.

A complex rigid generalized K3 surface is of the form
X=(\lambda e^{B+B^{\prime}+\sqrt{-1}\omega},\sigma+B^{\prime}\wedge\sigma)
, where

(1)

M_{\sigma}
is a singular K3 surface,

(2)

B\in H^{1,1}(M_{\sigma},\mathbb{R})
,
B^{\prime}\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Q})
,

(3)

either
\pm\omega
is a hyperKähler form for
\sigma
.

In other words,
X
is a rational
B
-field shift of a singular K3 surface equipped with a complexified Kähler structure.

Proof.

Let
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
be complex rigid.
By the classification of hyperKähler structures (comments below Remark
2.8
),
\varphi
must be of type
A
.
Hence we can write
X=e^{B^{\prime}}(\lambda e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega},\sigma)
, where
Y=(\lambda e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega},\sigma)
is a generalized K3 surface,
i.e.
B
is a closed real
(1,1)
-form and either
\pm\omega
is a hyperKähler form for
\sigma
.
On the other hand, for
\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma+B^{\prime}\wedge\sigma
,
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi^{\prime}})=2
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(T(M_{\sigma}))=2
and
B^{\prime}
is rational.
This can be seen as follows.
If
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi^{\prime}})=2
,
there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{1},u_{2}\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
such that

\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma+B^{\prime}\wedge\sigma=au_{1}+\sqrt{-1}bu_{2}.

For
i=1,2
, let
v_{i}=(u_{i})_{2}\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
be the degree 2 part of
u_{i}
.
Then we may write
\sigma=av_{1}+\sqrt{-1}bv_{2}
and hence
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\sigma})=2
, which means
\mathrm{rank}(T(M_{\sigma}))=2
.
Moreover, we have

(1)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}=e^{B}\sigma=a(v_{1}+B^{\prime}\wedge v_{1})+\sqrt{-1}b(v_{2}+B^{\prime}\wedge v_{2}),

and in order for
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi^{\prime}})=2
, the
B
-field
B^{\prime}
must be rational.
Conversely, assume
\mathrm{rank}(T(M_{\sigma}))=2
and
B^{\prime}
is rational,
then there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
v_{1},v_{2}\in T(M_{\sigma})
such that
\sigma=av_{1}+\sqrt{-1}bv_{2}
,
and
\varphi^{\prime}
is of the form of the equation (
1
).
Since
B^{\prime}
is rational,
v_{1}+B^{\prime}\wedge v_{1},v_{2}+B^{\prime}\wedge v_{2}\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Q})
and hence
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi^{\prime}})=2
.
∎

3.4.
Kähler rigidity

A Kähler rigidity comparable with a complex rigidity naturally appears in the framework of generalized Calabi–Yau structures.

Here is a key observation.
Let
S
be a K3 surface with Picard number
1
.
We write
NS(S)=\mathbb{Z}H
with
H^{2}=2n>0
, and consider

v_{1}:=(1,0,-n),\ \ v_{2}:=(0,H,0)\in NS^{\prime}(S),

where the notation follows the decomposition
NS^{\prime}(S)=H^{0}(S,\mathbb{Z})\oplus NS(S)\oplus H^{4}(S,\mathbb{Z})
.
Then

\displaystyle e^{\sqrt{-1}H}

\displaystyle=(1,\sqrt{-1}H,-n)

\displaystyle=v_{1}+\sqrt{-1}v_{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}v_{1}+\mathbb{Z}v_{2})_{\mathbb{C}}\subsetneq NS^{\prime}(S)_{\mathbb{C}}.

Here

\mathbb{Z}v_{1}+\mathbb{Z}v_{2}\cong\langle 2n\rangle^{\oplus 2},

where
\langle k\rangle
denotes the lattice of rank
1
generated by
v
with
v^{2}=k
.
On the other hand, for
\epsilon^{2}\notin\mathbb{Q}
,

\displaystyle e^{\sqrt{-1}\epsilon H}

\displaystyle=(1,\sqrt{-1}\epsilon H,-\epsilon^{2}n)

\displaystyle=(1,0,-\epsilon^{2}n)+\sqrt{-1}\epsilon(0,H,0)

\displaystyle=(1,0,0)-\epsilon^{2}(0,0,n)+\sqrt{-1}\epsilon(0,H,0)\in NS^{\prime}(S)_{\mathbb{C}}.

Hence there is no proper sublattice
L\subsetneq NS^{\prime}(S)
such that
e^{\sqrt{-1}\epsilon H}\in L_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Therefore the Kähler structure
H
is not continuously deformable in such a way that
\mathrm{rank}(L_{e^{\sqrt{-1}\epsilon H}})=2
.
This new sublattice
L_{e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}}
is comparable with the transcendental lattice, which is the minimal sublattice
L\subset H^{2}(M_{\sigma},\mathbb{Z})
such that
\sigma\in L_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Definition 3.6
.

A symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
is called symplectic rigid if
\omega^{2}\in H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Q})
.

As the previous calculation shows,
(M,\omega)
is symplectic rigid if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(L_{e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}})=2
.

Definition 3.7
.

A generalized K3 surface
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
is called Kähler rigid if
\varphi
is of type
A
and
\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{T}(X))=22
.

Again, the condition
\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{T}(X))=22
is equivalent to
\mathrm{rank}(L_{\varphi})=2
.
We will next provide a characterization of the Kähler rigidity.
Recall that, in contrast to the complex rigidity, the hyperKähler structure
\varphi^{\prime}
can be either of type
A
or
B
.

Theorem 3.8
.

A Kähler rigid generalized K3 surface is of the form
X=(\lambda e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega},\varphi^{\prime})
, where
B\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Q})
and
\omega^{2}\in H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Q})
.
In other words,
X
is a rational
B
-field
B
shift of a symplectic rigid manifold equipped with a hyperKähler structure.

Proof.

Let
X=(e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega},\varphi^{\prime})
be Kähler rigid.
We consider an existence condition of a rank
2
sublattice
L\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
such that

e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}=1+B+\frac{1}{2}(B^{2}-\omega^{2})+\sqrt{-1}(\omega+B\wedge\omega)\in L_{\mathbb{C}}.

First,
B
needs to be rational, and hence so is
\omega^{2}
.
Then we may write the symplectic class
\omega=\kappa H
for
\kappa^{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
H\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})
with
H^{2}>0
.
In fact, in this case, there exist
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

m\mathrm{Re}(e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\kappa H}),\ n\mathrm{Im}(e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\kappa H})\in H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z}).

Then the complexification
L_{\mathbb{C}}
of the lattice

L=\mathbb{Z}m\mathrm{Re}(e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\kappa H})+\mathbb{Z}n\mathrm{Im}(e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\kappa H})\subset H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})

contains
e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\kappa H}
.
∎

4.
Mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces

Our formulation of mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces builds upon the combination of two novel ideas:
(1) lattice polarizations of the cycles (Dolgachev
[
7
]
) and (2) generalized K3 structures (Hitchin
[
12
]
and Huybrechts
[
13
]
).

4.1.
Mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces

We begin with a brief summary of mirror symmetry for lattice polarized K3 surfaces originally introduced by Dolgachev in
[
7
]
.
Fix an integer
1\leq\rho\leq 19
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
K
be a lattice of signature
(1,\rho-1)
.
A
K
-polarized K3 surface is a pair
(S,i)
of a K3 surface
S
and a primitive lattice embedding
i:K\hookrightarrow NS(S)
such that
i(K)
contains an ample class.

Two
K
-polarized K3 surfaces
(S,i)
and
(S^{\prime},i^{\prime})
are called isomorphic if there is an isomorphism
f:S\rightarrow S^{\prime}
of K3 surfaces such that
f^{*}\circ i^{\prime}=i
.

Definition 4.2
.

For a lattice
K
of signature
(1,\rho-1)
, assume there exists a lattice
L
such that

K^{\perp}=L\oplus U.

Then the family
\mathcal{S}
of
K
-polarized K3 surfaces and the family
\mathcal{S}^{\vee}
of
L
-polarized K3 surfaces are mirror symmetric.

In the above setting,
L
is of signature
(1,19-\rho)
and uniquely determined with
L^{\perp}=K\oplus U
(independent of the chose of a decomposition and an embedding)
[
7
]
.
Then, for a generic
K
-polarized K3 surface
S
and a generic
L
-polarized K3 surface
S^{\vee}
, we have

NS^{\prime}(S)\cong K\oplus U\cong T(S^{\vee}),\ \ \ T(S)\cong L\oplus U\cong NS^{\prime}(S^{\vee}).

They are considered as a duality between the algebraic cycles of
S
and the transcendental cycles of
S^{\vee}
as lattices, and vice versa (duality of Yukawa couplings).
Mirror symmetry can also be explored at the level of moduli spaces.
See for example
[
7
,
8
,
11
]
.

The crucial assumption in Dolgachev’s formulation is the existence of a decomposition of the form
K^{\perp}=L\oplus U
,
which reflects the decomposition

NS^{\prime}(S)=NS(S)\oplus\big(H^{0}(S,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{4}(S,\mathbb{Z})\big)

under mirror symmetry.
Hence the assumption is indispensable as long as we consider geometry where the point class and fundamental class play special roles.

Remark 4.3
.

It is also worth noting that there are geometric explanations for such a decomposition; the hyperbolic lattice
U
corresponds to:

(1)

a cusp (large complex structure limit) of the Baily–Borel compactification of the period domains
[
17
]
,

(2)

the lattice spanned by the fiber and section classes of a special Lagrangian fibration in SYZ mirror symmetry.

Therefore, a choice of such
U
corresponds to a choice of a mirror K3 surface.

4.2.
Problems

Although Dolgachev’s formulation works well in many cases, it has a few issues and cannot be considered definitive.
First of all,
NS^{\prime}(S)
and
T(S)
are not truly symmetric;
the minimal rank of
NS^{\prime}(S)
is
3
(as long as
S
is algebraic) while the minimal rank of
T(S)
is
2
.
Additionally, the assumption
K^{\perp}=L\oplus U
does not hold in general.
These issues fall into two categories.

(1)

There are decompositions of the form
K^{\perp}=L\oplus U(m)
but for
m\geq 2
.
Examples of such K3 surfaces are constructed for examples in
[
10
]
.
The duality between the algebraic cycles of a
K
-polarized K3 surface and the transcendental cycles of an
L
-polarized K3 surface holds only over
\mathbb{Q}
.

(2)

The situation is worse if
K^{\perp}
is of signature
(2,0)
and hence cannot be of the form
U(m)
for any
m\geq 1
.
Such a lattice
K
is given by
K=NS(S)
for a singular K3 surface
S
.
Therefore, the natural question “How can we understand mirror symmetry for singular K3 surfaces?” has been a mysterious problem for a long time.
Indeed, for a singular K3 surface, the Kähler moduli space is of dimension 20 and complex moduli space is of dimension 0, and there seems no mirror partner as a K3 surface.

4.3.
Mirror symmetry for generalized K3 surfaces

We will first introduce the notion of lattice polarizations of the Mukai lattice.
For integers
\kappa,\lambda\geq 2
such that
\kappa+\lambda=24
,
let
K
and
L
be even lattices of signature
(2,\kappa-2)
and
(2,\lambda-2)
respectively.

Definition 4.4
.

A
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surface is a pair
(X,i)
of a generalized K3 surface
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})

and a lattice embedding
i:K\oplus L\hookrightarrow H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})

such that

(1)

the restrictions
i|_{K}
and
i|_{L}
are primitive embeddings,

(2)

i(K)\subset\widetilde{NS}(X)
and
i(L)\subset\widetilde{T}(X)
.

Two
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces
(X,i)
and
(Y,j)
are called isomorphic
if there is a diffeomorphism
f\in\mathrm{Diff}_{*}(M)
such that
X=f^{*}Y
and
f^{*}\circ j=i
.
For simplicity, we usually omit
i
from the notation
(X,i)
when there is no confusion.

Definition 4.5
.

A family
\mathcal{X}
of
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces and a family
\mathcal{Y}
of
(L,K)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces are mirror symmetric.

As mentioned in Remark
3.2
, in general, the elements in
H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z})
and
H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})
are no longer “algebraic”
and there is no natural summand
U\cong H^{0}(M,\mathbb{Z})\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z})
in
\widetilde{NS}(X)
.
Therefore, there is no assumption on the lattices
K
and
L
in our formulation.

There may be several families of
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces, and we typically select one for the purpose of mirror symmetry.
This remark is particularly important due to the symmetry of Definition
4.4
.
Specifically, if
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
is a
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surface, then
(\varphi^{\prime},\varphi)
is a
(L,K)
-polarized generalized K3 surface in a natural way.
Nevertheless, as elucidated in Section
4.5
and Section
4.6
, we may obtain a non-trivial result by selecting appropriate families.
This issue is also related to the so-called multiple mirrors phenomenon.

Remark 4.6
.

Our formulation is compatible with Aspinwall–Morrison’s description of mirror symmetry for K3 surfaces from the viewpoint of SCFTs
[
3
]
.
They identified the moduli space of
N=(2,2)
SCFTs on a K3 surface with
the Grassmannian

\mathrm{Gr}_{2,2}^{po}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))\cong\mathrm{O}(4,20)/(\mathrm{SO}(2)\times\mathrm{SO}(2)\times\mathrm{O}(20))

parametrizing the orthogonal pairs of positive oriented 2-planes in
H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{4,20}
.
To a K3 surface
M_{\sigma}
with a Kähler class
\omega
, we may associate

(P_{\sigma},P_{e^{\sqrt{-1}\omega}})\in\mathrm{Gr}_{2,2}^{po}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R})).

There are “non-geometric points” that cannot be obtained in this way.
However, we now understand that these points are given by generalized K3 surfaces, as stated in Theorem
2.11
.

Then mirror symmetry is an involution of
\mathrm{Gr}_{2,2}^{po}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))
given by swapping the two 2-planes, which does not preserve the geometric points.
Therefore, it is understood that a mirror partner of a K3 surface is not necessarily a K3 surface, but rather a generalized K3 surface.
It is also explained that choosing
U
in
H^{*}(M,\mathbb{Z})
amounts to selecting a specific
N=(2,2)
SCFT to represent an
N=(4,4)
SCFT.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
be a
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surface.
A deformation of
\mathbb{C}\varphi
keeping
X
as a
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surface is called an
A
-deformation.
The
A
-moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{A}
is defined as the space of
A
-deformations of
X
.
A
B
-deformation and the
B
-moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{B}
are defined similarly.

We will provide an explicit description of the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{A}\times\mathfrak{M}_{B}
of the
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces.
We begin by introducing

\mathfrak{D}_{K}:=\{\varphi\in\mathbb{P}(K_{\mathbb{C}})\ |\ \langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle=0,\langle\varphi,\overline{\varphi}\rangle>0\},

a complex manifold of dimension
\kappa-2
, isomorphic to the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2}(K_{\mathbb{R}})\cong\mathrm{O}(2,\kappa-2)/(\mathrm{SO}(2)\times\mathrm{O}(\kappa-2))

parametrizing the positive oriented 2-planes in
K_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2,\kappa-2}
.
It has two connected components, each of which is isomorphic to a symmetric domain of type IV.

\mathfrak{D}_{L}
is defined in a similar manner.

Theorem 4.8
.

The moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{A}\times\mathfrak{M}_{B}
of the
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces
is identified with an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{D}_{(K,L)}:=\mathfrak{D}_{K}\times\mathfrak{D}_{L}
.
Moreover,
\mathfrak{M}_{A}
is identified with an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{D}_{K}
, and similarly,
\mathfrak{M}_{B}
is identified with an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{D}_{K}
.

Proof.

Let
(X=(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}),i)
be a
(K,L)
-polarized generalized K3 surface.
Then the conditions
i(K)\subset\widetilde{NS}(X)
and
i(L)\subset\widetilde{T}(X)
are equivalent to
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}
and
\varphi\in\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}
, respectively.
We will demonstrate that a point in an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\times\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}
defines a unique generalized K3 surface.
Firstly, we associate to
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\times\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}
an orthogonal pair of positive oriented 2-planes
(P_{\alpha},P_{\beta})\in\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))
,
which are generically not orthogonal to any
(-2)
-class.
Therefore, by Theorem
2.11
, the period map
\mathfrak{per}_{\mathrm{gK3}}:\mathfrak{M}_{\mathrm{gK3}}\rightarrow\mathrm{Gr}^{po}_{2,2}(H^{*}(M,\mathbb{R}))
ensures that,
generically,
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\times\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}
defines a unique generalized K3 surface.
Consequently, the moduli space
\mathfrak{M}_{A}\times\mathfrak{M}_{B}
may be identified with an open dense subset of
\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\times\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}\cong\mathfrak{D}_{(K,L)}
.

Let
H:=(H^{2}(M,\mathbb{C})\oplus H^{4}(M,\mathbb{Z}))\cap i(K)
.
Then, by Theorem
2.12
and the above discussion,
\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\setminus\mathbb{P}(H)
parametrizes
the
A
-deformation of
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
for a generic choice of
\varphi^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{D}_{i(L)}
.
Therefore,
\mathfrak{M}_{A}
is identified with
\mathfrak{D}_{i(K)}\setminus\mathbb{P}(H)
.
A parallel discussion can be carried out for
\mathfrak{M}_{B}
.
∎

4.4.
Comparison: classical and new

Next, we will demonstrate that our formulation of mirror symmetry inherently encompasses Dolgachev’s formulation.

For an integer
1\leq\rho\leq 19
, let
K^{\prime}
be a lattice of signature
(1,\rho-1)
.

-polarized K3 surface equipped with a

\displaystyle\widetilde{NS}(X)=NS^{\prime}(M_{\sigma})\cong K^{\prime}\oplus U,\ \ \ \widetilde{T}(X)=T(M_{\sigma})\cong L^{\prime}\oplus U.

-polarized K3 surface equipped with a

4.6.

The above discussion is readily generalized to any singular K3 surfaces.
Let
S
be a singular K3 surface such that
NS(S)=K^{\prime}
and
T(S)=L
, and set
K=K^{\prime}\oplus U
.
Then the mirror is given by a family
\mathcal{Y}
of
(L,K)
-polarized generalized K3 surfaces.
This family may include a K3 surface with
L_{e^{B+\sqrt{-1}\omega}}\cong L
,
although it is normally very hard to construct such a K3 surface explicitly.

In summary, the mirror partner of a singular K3 surfaces is a Kähler rigid generalized K3 surface, which is generally not a classical K3 surface.
This solution addresses the problem of mirror symmetry for singular K3 surfaces.

Remark 4.10
.

The initial motivation for the present article stems from the attractor mechanisms of the complex and Kähler moduli spaces of Calabi–Yau 3-folds
[
9
]
.
The connection arises from a Calabi–Yau 3-fold formed by the product of an elliptic curve and a K3 surface.
Then the complex (resp. Kähler) attractor mechanism specifies a set of isolated points in the moduli space, called the constellation,
and they correspond to the complex (resp. symplectic) rigid K3 surfaces.
For further elaboration, refer to
[
9
]
.

4.7.
Comments on relevant works

A generalized Calabi–Yau variety is a Fano variety
X
whose derived category
\mathrm{D}^{b}(X)
admits a semi-orthogonal decomposition,
with one component being the Calabi–Yau category
\mathcal{A}_{X}
.
This category provides a deformation of the derived category of a Calabi–Yau manifold of lower dimension.
In this sense,
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is thought to be a non-commutative Calabi–Yau manifold.

Another notion of a non-commutative Calabi–Yau manifold arises from a certain algebra introduced in
[
15
]
;
Here, the quotient category
\mathrm{qgr}(A/(f)):=\mathrm{gr}(A/(f))/\mathrm{tor}(A/(f))
is considered,
where
A
is a specific choice of skew polynomial algebra and
f
is a Fermat type element in the center of
A
.
This construction yields a non-commutative projective scheme in the sense of Artin and Zhang
[
2
]
.

In dimension 2, there are thus at least three notions of non-commutative K3 surfaces:

\mathcal{A}_{X}
for a Fano 4-fold
X
, the quotient category
\mathrm{qgr}(A/(f))
of modules of a certain algebra,
and the derived category
\mathrm{D}^{b}(S,\alpha)
of twisted coherent sheaves on a K3 surface
S
(Remark
4.9
).
The relation between
\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
\mathrm{D}^{b}(S,\alpha)
has recently been elucidated, with a certain correspondence provided in
[
1
,
14
]
.
However, there exist instances of
\mathcal{A}_{X}
that do not correspond to
\mathrm{D}^{b}(S,\alpha)
, and similarly, generalized Calabi–Yau structures that do not correspond to
\mathcal{A}_{X}
.
The connection between the quotient category
\mathrm{qgr}(A/(f))
and the other two cases remains unclear at this point.

Remark 4.11
.

The author has been informed by Belmans that the methods in
[
5
]
should generalize to higher dimensions,
and show that the derived category
\mathrm{D}^{b}(\mathrm{qgr}(A/(f))
of modules of the non-commutative K3 surfaces arising in
[
15
]
is of the form
\mathrm{D}^{b}(S,\alpha)
.
This work is currently in progress by the authors of
[
5
]
.

In
[
18
]
Sheridan and Smith proved, among other results, homological mirror symmetry for certain pairs of a rigid Calabi–Yau manifold and a generalized Calabi–Yau variety.
They in particular discussed homological mirror symmetry for a rigid K3 surface and a cubic fourfold
X
.
The corresponding
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is considered “non-geometric” and does not fall within the aforementioned correspondence.
Nevertheless it should correspond to a generalized Calabi–Yau structure and align with our result.
It is worth noting that our formulation is intrinsic and applicable to all singular K3 surfaces,
whereas
[
18
]
focuses on (generalized) Calabi–Yau varieties obtained through the Batyrev-Borisov construction (in all dimensions).
