Black Hole Greybody Factors from Korteweg-de Vries Integrals: Theory
Michele Lenzi, Carlos F. Sopuerta

Abstract
The dynamics of perturbed non-rotating black holes (BHs) can be described in terms of master equations of the wave type with a potential. In the frequency domain, the master equations become time-independent Schrödinger equations with no discrete spectrum. It has been recently shown that these wave equations possess an infinite number of symmetries that correspond to the flow of the infinite hierarchy of Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equations. As a consequence, the infinite set of associated conserved quantities, the KdV integrals, are the same for all the different master equations that we can consider. In this paper we show that the BH scattering reflection and transmission coefficients characterizing the continuous spectrum can be fully determined via a moment problem, in such a way that the KdV integrals provide the momenta of a distribution function depending only on the reflection coefficient. We also discuss the existence and uniqueness of solutions, strategies to solve the moment problem, and finally show the case of the Pöschl-Teller potential where all the steps can be carried out analytically.

General Relativity predicts the existence of Black Holes (BHs), regions of spacetime from where nothing can escape, not even light

;
Wald 1984
;

An astonishing fact about BHs is that they can come in any size, the only impediment against their existence is to have a viable formation mechanism. BHs are central objects in several areas of Physics and Astronomy. They also play a role in the development of fundamental physics theories that include gravity and go beyond the standard model of particle physics. Of special relevance are theories that involve higher dimensions or implement the holographic principle. From the observational point of view, accumulating evidence of BHs (or of objects that look like BHs) come from various sources, in particular X-rays (see, e.g.

In the case that the scattered particles or waves can be considered as small perturbations of the BH geometry, the best tool to study scattering processes on a non-trivial curved background is spacetime perturbation theory. In this case, BH perturbation theory (BHPT). As it was found in pioneering works in Refs.

Greybody factors are key physical quantities in this context. They are nothing but the transmission (and reflection) probabilities associated with the potential barrier and are therefore highly informative about the nature of the object (a BH in this case, but other more exotic compact object can also be considered) and its response to the perturbations. Moreover, the structure of the space of master functions gets enriched by the introduction

;

;

;

The key idea behind the inverse scattering method is to reconstruct the time evolution of a potential (the unknown satisfying the non-linear evolution equation) from the evolution of its scattering data, which is obtained from an associated linear and time-independent problem. In the case of the KdV equation, the linear problem is the time-independent Schrödinger equation. Another remarkable result is the appearance of an infinite series of conservation laws for the KdV equation with the corresponding set of conserved quantities

;

;

Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021b
that the KdV integrals are invariant under Darboux transformations.

Executive Summary
. In this paper we show that the KdV integrals associated with the BH effective potential fully determine the transmission probability, or greybody factors, which contains all the relevant physical information about BH scattering processes. The way in which the KdV integrals determine the transmission probability is through a
moment problem
, that is, the KdV integrals turn out to be the moments (up to a trivial multiplicative factor) of a distribution function associated with the transmission probability. We also show that this moment problem is determinate, so that a solution exists and it is unique. This means that it is possible to invert the moment problem to find the greybody factors. We discuss possible avenues to carry out the inversion and illustrate it with an example that can be analytically solved: The particular case of a Pöschl-Teller potential.

II
Summary of BHPT in the non-rotating case

The study of perturbations of BHs started with the seminal paper of Regge-Wheeler
Regge and Wheeler 1957
, which already laid many of the elements of the theory that are used now, at least in the non-rotating case. Let us now summarize these ingredients and the last developments that are the basis of the work presented in this paper. For more details see
Nagar and Rezzolla 2005
;
Martel and Poisson 2005
;
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
.

II.1
Formulation of the first-order perturbative equations

In relativistic perturbation theory we deal with spacetimes that describe phenomena that, in a sense, can be considered as
small
deviations of an idealized physical situation/system. This idealized situation is encoded in what we call the
background
spacetime, which typically is a spacetime with a certain degree of symmetry. In our case, the background is the Schwarzschild spacetime
Schwarzschild 1916
, i.e. an isolated non-rotating BH, which is static and spherically symmetric. The metric of the background spacetime has then the following simple form
1
1

1

We use a hat to denote quantities associated with the background spacetime.
:

d\hat{s}{}^{2}=\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=-f(r)\,dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,

(1)

where

f(r)=1-\frac{r_{s}}{r}\,,

(2)

where
r_{s}
is the location of the event horizon, the Schwarzschild radius:
r_{s}=2GM/c^{2}=2M\,
.

The physical spacetime is a member of a one-parameter family of spacetimes,
(\mathcal{M}_{\lambda},g_{\lambda})
, which is the way in which perturbation theory is usually formulated (see, e.g.
Stewart and Walker 1974
;
Wald 1984
). In this way, the perturbations are defined as the derivative terms of the Taylor series expansion in
\lambda
, evaluated on the background (
\lambda=0
). The parameter
\lambda
is, in general, a dummy parameter that controls the strength of the perturbations and, for simplicity, we can ignore it. Here, we only consider first-order (order
\lambda^{1}
) perturbations,
h_{\mu\nu}
(
|h_{\mu\nu}|\ll|\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}|
), so that the spacetime metric of the physical spacetime can be written as:

g_{\mu\nu}=\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu}\,.

(3)

We denote the first-order perturbation of any quantity
{\cal Q}
as:
\delta Q=Q-\widehat{Q}
. In particular,
h_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}-\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}=\delta g_{\mu\nu}
.

We can introduce Eq. (
3
) into the full Einstein equations and, keeping only terms linear in the metric perturbations
h_{\mu\nu}
, we obtain the equations for the perturbations, which schematically can be represented as:

\delta G[h]_{\mu\nu}=0\,,

(4)

where
\delta G
is a linear operator that when applied to the perturbations yields the first-order perturbative equations (in vacuum). These equations are partial differential equations (PDEs) that are coupled. A natural strategy is to try to solve them by looking for combinations of the metric perturbations (and their derivatives) so that they satisfy equations that are decoupled from the rest of variables. These type of combinations and their corresponding equations are usually known as master variables and equations respectively. Of course, the success of this strategy depends on the characteristics of the background spacetime.

Spacetime perturbations are subject to a gauge freedom that comes from the infinite ways we have to establish a correspondence between the background and physical spacetimes
Stewart and Walker 1974
;
Bruni et al. 1997
. A gauge transformation of the perturbations relates two of these correspondences. In practice, by pulling back the physical metric and the geometric structure associated with it to the background spacetime, a gauge transformation connects two points of the background spacetime in the form of a coordinate transformation:

x^{\mu}\quad\longrightarrow\quad x^{\prime\mu}={x}^{\mu}+\xi^{\mu}\,,

(5)

in such a way that
{x}^{\mu}
and
x^{\prime\mu}
are the coordinates of two points of the background spacetime that correspond to a single point of the physical spacetime. Here, the vector field
\xi^{\mu}
(
|\xi^{\mu}|\ll|\widehat{g}_{\mu\nu}|
), is the generator of the gauge transformation. The change induced in the metric perturbations looks as:

h_{\mu\nu}\quad\longrightarrow\quad{h^{\prime}}_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}-2\,\xi_{(\mu;\nu)}\,.

(6)

II.2
Spherically Symmetric Background

The spherical symmetry of the Schwarzchild metric (
1

\displaystyle-f(r)\,dt^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)}\,,

\displaystyle d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\varphi^{2}\,.

II.3
Master Equations for each Harmonic of the Metric Perturbations

The metric perturbations can be written as a multipolar expansion using the basis of scalar (
Y^{\ell m}
), vector (
Y^{\ell m}_{A}
,
X^{\ell m}_{A}
), and tensor spherical harmonics (

In this way, and thanks to the spherical symmetry of the background, the different harmonics decouple, and the harmonics with different parity decouple as well. That is, from the Einstein perturbative equations we obtain separate equations for each

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{\ell,m}h^{\ell m,\rm odd}_{\mu\nu}+h^{\ell m,\rm even}_{\mu\nu}\,,

\begin{split}h^{\rm odd}_{ab}&=0\,,\\

h^{\rm odd}_{AB}&=h_{2}X_{AB}\,,\end{split}\quad\begin{split}h^{\rm even}_{ab}&=h_{ab}Y\,,\\
h^{\rm even}_{aA}&=\mbox{\rm\T1\j}_{a}Y_{A}\,,\\
h^{\rm even}_{AB}&=r^{2}\left(KT_{AB}+GY_{AB}\right)\,.\end{split}

(12)

Here

\displaystyle\Psi_{\text{RW}}

\displaystyle\Psi_{\rm CPM}

III
The Full Landscape of Master Functions and Equations

In
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
, the following question was asked
3
3

3

Actually, this question was considered in a more general context, where a cosmological constant of arbitrary sign was also included.
: What are
all
the possible master equations that one can obtain for the vacuum perturbations of a Schwarzschild BH? To answer this question, Ref.
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
adopted a general and systematic way of searching for master functions and associated potentials without having to resort to ad hoc combinations of the perturbative field equations that yield decoupled master equations.

The main outcome of the analysis of
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
is a complete picture of the space of master functions and equations, or in other words, of the space of pairs
(V_{\ell},\Psi)
that satisfy a wave equation of the form given in Eq. (
13
). To that end, only two assumptions on the master functions were imposed: (i) The master functions are linear combinations of the metric perturbations and its first-order derivatives (as in the known master functions). (ii) The coefficients of these linear combinations are time independent, i.e. they only depend on the radial area coordinate
r
(motivated by the static character of the background). The analysis was done in an arbitrary gauge and, as a by-product, the resulting master functions turned out to be gauge invariant.

The resulting landscape found in
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
reveals the existence of two branches of possible pairs of potentials and master functions (master equations),
(V_{\ell},\Psi)
. The first branch contains the known cases and hence it is called the
standard
branch. For each parity there is just one potential, the Regge-Wheeler (odd-parity) and Zerilli (even-parity) potentials, i.e.

{}_{\rm S}V^{\rm odd/even}_{\ell}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}V^{\rm RW}_{\ell}&~~\mbox{odd parity}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
V^{\rm Z}_{\ell}&~~\mbox{even parity}\,,\end{array}\right.

In the standard branch, the most general master function can be written as follows

{}_{\rm S}\Psi^{\rm odd/even}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\mathcal{C}_{1}\Psi^{\rm CPM}+\mathcal{C}_{2}\Psi^{\rm RW}&\mbox{odd parity}\,,\\[5.69054pt]
\mathcal{C}_{1}\Psi^{\rm ZM}+\mathcal{C}_{2}\Psi^{\rm NE}&\mbox{even parity}\,,\end{array}\right.

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are arbitrary constants. Then, the most general master function in the odd-parity case is a linear combination of the Regge-Wheeler (
14
) and the Cunningham-Price-Moncrief (
15
) master functions. In the even-parity sector, the most general master function is a linear combination of the Zerilli-Moncrief (
16
) one and a new master function,

, found in
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
, which reads (see appendix
A
):

\Psi^{\rm NE}(t,r)=\frac{1}{\lambda(r)}t^{a}\left(r\tilde{K}_{:a}-\tilde{h}_{ab}r^{b}\right)\,.

(26)

The master functions
(\Psi^{\rm CPM},\Psi^{\rm RW})
and
(\Psi^{\rm ZM},\Psi^{\rm NE})

\displaystyle t^{a}\Psi^{\rm CPM}_{\,,a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\Psi^{\rm RW}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle t^{a}\Psi^{\rm ZM}_{\,,a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\Psi^{\rm NE}\,,

(28)

where the time vector

The second branch is completely new, and is called the

IV
Darboux Covariance of BH Perturbations

The structure and properties of the landscape of master functions and equations was studied in
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021b
. The conclusion is that all the pairs
(V,\Psi)
are related by Darboux transformations (DTs). To understand this, let us consider two such pairs, say
(v,\Phi)
and
(V,\Psi)
, that satisfy a wave equation like Eq. (

, the DT generating function, that maps one pair onto the other as

\displaystyle(v,\Phi)\rightarrow(V,\Psi):\quad\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{,x}+g\,\Phi\,,

(35)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v+2\,g_{,x}\,.

(36)

The DT generating function
g
cannot be arbitrary but it has to satisfy the following Riccati equation

\displaystyle g_{,x}-g^{2}+v={\cal C}\,,

(37)

where
{\cal C}
is an arbitrary constant. From Eq. (
36

g_{,x}

g_{,x}=\frac{V-v}{2}\,.

(38)

Introducing this into the derivative of the Riccati equation (

(39)

Then, the consistency between these expressions for

and

\left(\frac{\delta V_{,x}}{\delta V}\right)_{,x}+2\left(\frac{v_{,x}}{\delta V}\right)_{,x}-\delta V=0\,,

(40)

where
\delta V=V-v\,
. This is precisely equation (

, where

. Hence, all master equations in the Darboux branch are connected via a DT to the standard branch, with DT generating functions given by:

\displaystyle g^{\rm Z\rightarrow even}_{\rm RW\rightarrow odd}=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\,\left(V^{\rm even}_{\rm odd}-V^{\rm Z}_{\rm RW}\right)\,,

(41)

while the two parities in the standard branch are connected by a DT with generating function:

g^{\rm RW\rightarrow Z}=\frac{1}{2}\int dx\,\left(V^{\rm Z}-V^{\rm RW}\right)=-g^{\rm Z\rightarrow RW}\,.

(42)

In conclusion, there is an infinite set of master functions and equations linked by DTs, as illustrated by Figure

Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021b

It is important to mention that the original approach to DTs relies on the existence of a particular solution

V
Korteweg-de Vries Symmetries

We have described the complete landscape of possible master equations and functions. We have also seen the role of the DT as a way of connecting all of them and establishing the isospectrality of the underlying physical description corresponding to this infinite set. In this sense, the DT plays the role of a covariance or symmetry of this set of possible physical descriptions of (vacuum) BH perturbations.

There is an independent set of transformations of the master equations that introduces a different kind of symmetry, actually an infinite set of them, and which also have the property of being isospectral. The starting point is the work of Gardner, Greene, Kruskal and Miura
Gardner et al. 1967
, who found a way of solving the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation
Korteweg and de Vries 1895

{\rm KdV}[V]\equiv V_{,\tau}-6VV_{,x}+V_{,xxx}=0\,,

(50)

by using the inverse scattering method
Deift and Trubowitz 1979
;
Ablowitz and Segur 1981
;
Eckhaus and Van Harten 1981
;
Novikov et al. 1984
. This discovery constituted a breakthrough in the field that provided a very original way of solving the KdV equation by expressing it in terms of the spectral and scattering theory of the Schrödinger operator in Eq. (
44
).

Lax
Lax 1968
provided a different point of view. He considered a continuous deformation of the Schrödinger operator,
L_{V}
, which essentially means deforming the potential:

V(x)\rightarrow V(\tau,x)\,,

(51)

and introducing the following operator

\displaystyle P_{V}\psi=-4\psi_{,xxx}+6V\psi_{,x}+3V_{,x}\psi\,,

(52)

so that

\frac{\partial L_{V}}{\partial\tau}-\left[P_{V},L_{V}\right]=-{\rm KdV}[V]\cdot{\rm Id}\,.

(53)

The operators
(P_{V},L_{V})
form a Lax pair, that is a pair of operators whose commutator is the action of an operator corresponding to an integrable non-linear PDE, as the KdV equation, times the unity operator. From Eq. (
53
) we deduce that, if
V
satisfies the KdV equation (
50
), the operator
L_{V}
is deformed remaining similar to itself (the different
L_{V}
for different
\tau
are unitary equivalent):

L_{V}(\tau)=U(\tau)\cdot L_{V}(0)\cdot U^{-1}(\tau)\,,

(54)

where the unitary operator
U(\tau)
evolves as:

\frac{dU(\tau)}{d\tau}=P_{V}\cdot U(\tau)\,.

(55)

Then, it follows that all the spectral characteristics of the operator
L_{V}(\tau)
are preserved by the KdV flow (i.e., the evolution along the
time

\tau
). This establishes an important connection between integrable systems and isospectral problems.

Actually, if we consider a deformation of our time-independent Schrödinger equation (
44
) consisting of Eq. (
51
) and

\psi(x)\rightarrow\psi(x,\tau)\,,\qquad k\rightarrow k(\tau)\,,

(56)

and such that
\psi(t,x)
evolves with
P_{V}

\displaystyle\psi_{,\tau}=P_{V}\psi\,,

(57)

we have
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021b

\left({\rm KdV}[V]-(k^{2})_{,\tau}\right)\psi=0\,.

(58)

This means that if the potential (the Schrödinger equation) is deformed according to the KdV equation we must have

\psi\,(k^{2})_{,\tau}=0\,,

(59)

and therefore
k^{2}
is invariant under the KdV flow. This, in particular, applies to the complex quasinormal mode frequencies of the Schwarzschild BH.

The Lax approach opened the door for a Hamiltonian formulation of the KdV equation and the associated conservation laws. Gardner
Gardner 1971
was the first to notice that the KdV equation can be written in terms of a Hamiltonian. Zakharov and Faddeev
Zakharov and Faddeev 1971
provided a different point of view of the results of Gardner and collaborators
Gardner et al. 1967
;
Gardner 1971
. They used the Hamiltonian formalism to express the KdV conserved quantities in terms of action-angle variables that appear naturally when looking at the scattering problem associated with the time-independent Schrödinger equation (see Section
VI
). Actually, we can introduce an infinite hierarchy of KdV evolution equations, associated with the infinite chain of KdV conservation laws, using the Hamiltonian formulation of the KdV equation (see also
Faddeev and Takhtajan 1987
):

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{k}}V={\cal K}_{k}[V]\,,\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots

(60)

where we have introduced different evolution parameters
\tau_{k}
to illustrate that these equations are independent. The functionals
{\cal K}_{k}[V]
are (see Appendix
B
):

{\cal K}_{k}[V]=\left\{V,{\cal H}_{k+1}[V]\right\}_{\rm GZF}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k+1}[V]}{\delta V(x)}\,,

(61)

where the
{\cal H}_{k}[V]
constitute an infinite sequence of conserved quantities, which we show below how to construct, that are functionals of
V
:

{\cal H}_{k}[V]=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,h_{k}(V,V_{,x},V_{,xx},\ldots)\,,

(62)

and the densities
h_{k}(V,V_{,x},V_{,xx},\ldots)
are differential polynomials in the potential
V
.

In Eq. (
61
), we are using the Gardner-Zakharov-Faddeev bracket
Gardner 1971
;
Zakharov and Faddeev 1971
. But we can also use the Magri bracket
Magri 1978
(see Appendix
B
) to generate the KdV hierarchy of equations:

{\cal K}_{k}[V]=\left\{V,{\cal H}_{k}[V]\right\}_{\rm M}={\cal E}_{V}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k}[V]}{\delta V(x)}\,,

(63)

where the operator
{\cal E}_{V}
is defined in Appendix
B
. Equations (
61
) and (
63
) show the bi-Hamiltonian character of the hierarchy of KdV evolution equations. Actually, by comparing them we can obtain the Lenard
Praught and Smirnov 2005
infinite sequence of recursion relations (see
Gardner 1971
;
Lax 1968
):

\displaystyle{\cal D}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k+1}[V]}{\delta V(x)}={\cal E}_{V}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k}[V]}{\delta V(x)}\quad\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k+1}}{\delta V}=\left(-\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x^{3}}+4V\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+2V_{,x}\right)\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{k}}{\delta V}\,.

(64)

All we need to specify is the first density

h_{0}=\frac{1}{2}V\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\cal H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,V(x)\,.

(65)

It is easy to show that
{\cal H}_{0}
(known as the
mean height
) is a conserved quantity by evolving it with respect to
\tau
, using the KdV equation, and using the fact that the integral of total gradients vanishes (by using the decay of
V
at
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
). Then, from the Lenard recurrence, we get the next conserved quantity:

h_{1}=\frac{1}{2}V^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{\cal H}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,V^{2}(x)\,,

(66)

which is interpreted as the momentum of the KdV wave. Using the Lenard recurrence again we get
Whitham 1965

h_{2}=V^{3}+\frac{1}{2}V^{2}_{,x}~\Rightarrow~{\cal H}_{2}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\!\!dx\left(V^{3}+\frac{1}{2}V^{2}_{,x}\right)\,,

(67)

which is interpreted as the energy of the wave. Actually, the KdV equation itself can be written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V={\cal D}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{2}}{\delta V}={\cal E}_{V}\frac{\delta{\cal H}_{1}}{\delta V}=6VV_{,x}-V_{,xxx}\,.

(68)

In summary, the KdV dynamics admits two Hamiltonian structures and, under both of them, we have an infinite number of conserved quantities, which we have called
{\cal H}_{k}[V]
(
k=0,1,2,\ldots
), and are functionals made out of differential polynomials in the potential
V
. From the different equations, and in particular using the Lenard recursion, we can see that these conserved quantities are in involution, that is, they satisfy:

\left\{{\cal H}_{j},{\cal H}_{k}\right\}_{\rm GZF}=\left\{{\cal H}_{j},{\cal H}_{k}\right\}_{\rm M}=0\,,\quad j,k=0,1,2,\ldots

(69)

therefore, we can say that the KdV equation is an integrable system in the classical Liouville sense
Arnold 1989
. As a consequence, Zakharov and Faddeev
Zakharov and Faddeev 1971
were able to carry out the first construction of action-angle variables for the KdV equation (see Sec.
VI
).

To end this section, let us comment on the relation between the KdV flows and the DT. We have seen that the KdV deformation, actually any deformation from the infinite hierarchy of KdV equations, of the time-independent Schrödinger equation (
44
) constitutes an isospectral symmetry of the dynamics of BH perturbations. In addition, there is a strong connection between this set of deformations and the DTs. Following the work of
Matveev and Salle 1991
it is possible to show that the system of equations

L_{V}\psi=-k^{2}\psi\,,\qquad\partial_{\tau}\psi=P_{V}\psi\,,

(70)

is invariant under DTs provided the DT generating function
g
is KdV-deformed as

\displaystyle g_{,\tau}=-g_{,xxx}+6(V+g_{,x})g_{,x}\,.

(71)

VI
Black Hole Scattering

We have seen the rich structure and multiple properties of the space of possible master functions and equations for the dynamics of BH perturbations. Two different types of isospectral transformation play a key role, namely Darboux covariance and KdV deformations. Under DTs, the potential is transformed via Eq. (

VII
Greybody Factors from KdV Integrals: Moment Problem Methods

We have just seen that the greybody factors associated with BH scattering processes are connected to the KdV integrals of the BH potential(s) via a moment problem. Let us now discuss this new point of view to see how one can use it in order to provide practical methods to compute the greybody factors. We illustrate this discussion with a case where all the equations involved can be solved analytically, namely the case of a Pöschl-Teller potential.

Let us start by considering a particular interval of the real line,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. The moments of a distribution
p(x)
with support on
\mathcal{I}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{n}=\int_{\mathcal{I}}dx\,x^{n}\,p(x)\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots\,,

(111)

where
x
here is just a real coordinate on
\mathcal{I}
and
p(x)dx=d\rho(x)
defines a positive measure. We also assume that
\{\mu_{n}\}
is a sequence of real numbers. In broad terms, the moment problem can be thought as the inverse problem of finding the probability distribution
p(x)
from the knowledge of its moments
\{\mu_{n}\}
. To that end, we need to consider the three following elements:

1.

Existence: Is there a function
p(x)
on
\mathcal{I}
whose moments are given by
\{\mu_{n}\}
?

2.

Uniqueness: Do the moments
\{\mu_{n}\}
determine uniquely a distribution
p(x)
on
\mathcal{I}
?

3.

Construction: How can we construct all such probability distributions?

When the solution is unique the problem is said to be
determinate
, otherwise it is called
indeterminate
. When
\mathcal{I}
is the positive real line,
\mathcal{I}=[0,\infty)
, the moment problem is known as the
Stieltjes
moment problem
Stieltjes 1894
;
Stieltjes 1994
, when
\mathcal{I}=\mathbb{R}
it is known as the
Hamburger
moment problem
Hamburger 1919
;
Hamburger 1920
;
Hamburger 1921a
;
Hamburger 1921b
and, finally, when
\mathcal{I}=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
it is known as a Hausdorff moment problem. By looking at the definition of our BH moments in Eq. (
107
), it is clear that the problem we are facing is a special case of the Hamburger one, called the
symmetric Hamburger
moment problem, which is characterized by a probability distribution that is symmetric with respect to the origin so that the only non-vanishing moments are the even ones [see Eq. (
107
)].

This problem has a long history
Kjeldsen 1993
. It originated with the work of Stieltjes
Stieltjes 1894
;
Stieltjes 1994
on the study of analytic properties of continued fractions, although moments were already investigated by Chebyshev
Chebyshev 1874
and later by Markov
Markov 1994
(see
Kreĭn 1951
for an historical account) while studying limiting values of integrals with continued fractions. Nevannlina
Nevanlinna 1922
made contact with the field of complex functions and Riesz
Riesz 1921
;
Riesz 1922
;
Riesz 1923
with functional analysis. Important contributions to the moment problem were also made by Carleman, Wall and others (see
Shohat and Tamarkin 1943
;
Akhiezer 1965
for other references). For a more recent account on the connections with different mathematical branches see
Schmüdgen 2017
and references therein. Although many results regarding existence and uniqueness of solutions have been found, no general solution method exists.

For convenience (see the expressions of the KdV integrals shown in Appendix
E
), we reformulate the moment problem (
107
) in terms of the dimensionless variable
w=r_{s}k
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{2n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dw\,w^{2n}p(w)\,,~\hat{\mu}_{2n}=r^{2n+1}_{s}\mu_{2n}\,.

(112)

VII.1
Hamburger Moment Problem: Existence and Determinacy Conditions

Here we study our BH moment problem (
107
) as a Hamburger moment. We start by looking at what can we say about existence and uniqueness of the solution. We start by introducing the so-called
Hankel determinants
,
\{D_{n}\}
, associated to a real sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
as

\displaystyle D_{n}=\begin{vmatrix}\mu_{0}&\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\cdots&\mu_{n}\\
\mu_{1}&\mu_{2}&\mu_{3}&\cdots&\mu_{n+1}\\
\mu_{2}&\mu_{3}&\mu_{4}&\cdots&\mu_{n+2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\mu_{n}&\mu_{n+1}&\mu_{n+2}&\cdots&\mu_{2n}\end{vmatrix}\,.

(113)

Existence of solutions to the Hamburger moment problem is guaranteed provided all the Hankel determinants
D_{n}
are positive.
For a symmetric Hamburger moment problem like ours, all odd entries vanish, which makes it easier to check whether the Hankel determinants are positive.
A possible strategy is to consider the Cholesky decomposition (see, e.g.
Press et al. 1992
) to simplify the calculations. In Fig.
3
, we show the first Hankel determinants for different values of
\ell
. For small
\ell
, the
D_{n}
first decrease but then start increasing with
n
, while for bigger
\ell
the determinants grows rapidly (this appears to be related to the behavior of the moments, see Fig.
4
).
It seems natural to claim that the Hankel determinants will never become negative. Therefore, we conclude that solutions to the problem (
107
) exist.

The second fundamental condition to check is whether the solution is unique. There are a number of statements defining determinacy
Schmüdgen 2017
of the moment problem. One of the most used ones is the
Carleman condition
, which states that a sufficient condition for the Hamburger moment problem to be determinate is

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu_{2n}^{-\frac{1}{2n}}=\infty\,.

(114)

Of course, Carleman’s condition has the disadvantage of only providing a sufficient condition, i.e. there can be moment sequences for which this criterion does not hold but they are still determinate. However, it also gives us the following intuitive interpretation: If the even moments do not tend to infinity
too quickly
, then the moment problem is determinate. A practical approach to study this growth condition, which is equivalent to Carleman’s condition, is to study whether there is a constant
C
for which

\mu_{2n}\leq C^{n}(2n)!\equiv\mathcal{C}(n)\,,\;\mbox{for all}\;n>0\,.

(115)

In Appendix
E
we show analytic expressions for the KdV integrals for the RW potential (and hence, for any other potential associated with BH perturbations). As we can see, the KdV integrals grow with the harmonic number
\ell
at most as:
I_{2n+1}\propto L^{n+1}
where
L=\ell(\ell+1)
(see also Fig.

). Furthermore, Eq. (
108
) shows that
\hat{\mu}_{2n}\propto I_{2n+1}/2^{2n+1}
so that it seems natural to think that taking
C=(L/2)^{2}
will meet the criteria (
115
). The factorial term in Eq. (
115
) already grows faster than any power of
L
so that it only helps improving the condition. Indeed, notice from Fig.
5
that the difference

\Delta(n)=\mathcal{C}(n)-\hat{\mu}_{2n}\,,

(116)

is actually increasing, indicating that condition (
115
) is always satisfied with
C=(L/2)^{2}
.
Independently on whether or not there is a more stringent bound, the important result is that the moments (
108
), and thus the KdV integrals, uniquely determine the distribution (
109
) and therefore the BH greybody factors.

VII.2
Analytic Methods to Solve the Moment Problem

In order to construct solutions to the moment problem, it is useful to consider the connection with the theory of analytic functions
Nevanlinna 1922
. Actually, given a solution
p(k)
of the moment problem, one can define its
Stieltjes transform
function
Shohat and Tamarkin 1943
;
Akhiezer 1965
as

\displaystyle F(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!dk\,\frac{p(k)}{k-z}\,,

(117)

which is analytic for every
z\in\mathbb{C\setminus R}
. Besides, for any range of angles
\epsilon\leq\arg(z)\leq\pi-\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<\pi/2
, this function is asymptotically represented by the following series

\displaystyle F(z)=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{n}}{z^{n+1}}\,,

(118)

which results from formally expanding the denominator of Eq. (
117
) for large
|z|
.
The inverse is also true, i.e. given the function
F(z)
with those properties, there exists only one distribution
p(k)
that solves the moment problem with moments
\{\mu_{n}\}
, and such that
F(z)
has the integral representation (
117
) with asymptotics given in Eq. (
118
). From the series expansion, the Stieltjes transform can also been seen as the generating function for the moments
\mu_{n}
. Another important consequence of condition (
115
) is that it guarantees that
F(z)
actually has the asymptotic representation (
118
) (see Ref.
Bender and Orszag 1978
), not just has a formal expansion.

There is a one-to-one relation between the moment problem and the problem of finding the analytic function
F(z)
in
\mathbb{C\setminus R}
and with the asymptotic expansion of Eq. (
118
). We then conclude that the solution
p(k)
to the moment problem is determined uniquely by
F(z)
. Indeed, the
Stieltjes-Perron inversion formula

Stieltjes 1894
(see also
Shohat and Tamarkin 1943
;
Akhiezer 1965
;
Schmüdgen 2017
) provides the inverse relation between
F(z)
and
p(k)
and it reads

\displaystyle p(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{F(k+i\epsilon)-F(k-i\epsilon)}{2\pi i}

(119)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\Im F(k+i\epsilon)}{\pi}\,,

where the last equality holds if
\overline{F(z)}=F(\overline{z})
. This feature is guaranteed by the Herglotz property of the Stieltjes transform
Bender and Orszag 1978
, by which the sign of
\Im{F(z)}
is the same as the sign of
\Im{z}
. Therefore, the moment problem is equivalent to the problem of inverting the Stieltjes transform (
117
) with the use of the Stieltjes-Perron inversion formula (
119
), which relates the probability distribution with the branch cut discontinuity of the Stieltjes transform across the real axis.
It is clear that Eq. (
83
) defines a Stieltjes transform after identifying
p(k)
with the distribution given by Eq. (
109
) and

F(k+i\epsilon)=i\ln{a(k+i\epsilon)}\,.

(120)

Therefore, Eq. (
119
) now reads

\displaystyle\ln T(k)=-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}2\,\Re\ln{a(k+i\epsilon)}\,.

(121)

In order to illustrate how the procedure described above works, we are going to show what happens in a particular case where we can carry out all the steps using analytic techniques, namely the case of a Pöschl-Teller potential:

\displaystyle V(x)=\frac{V_{0}}{\cosh^{2}{(\alpha x)}}=\frac{\alpha^{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)}{\cosh^{2}(\alpha x)}\,.

(122)

Although this potential does not belong to the family of potentials describing the perturbations of Schwarzschild BHs, it is one of the exactly solvable problems in scattering theory (see Appendix
D
for details), and as such it is very interesting to consider in order to understand the procedure in an analytical way.

From Eq. (
196
) we can directly obtain the exact expression for
a(k)

\displaystyle a(k)=\frac{\Gamma(1-ik)\Gamma(-ik)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k-l)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k+l)\right)}\ ,

(123)

Notice that in the left-hand side of Eq. (
121
),
T(k)=|a(k)|^{-2}
, is evaluated on the real line, while
a(k+i\epsilon)
on the right-hand side, is the analytical continuation into the complex plane. Therefore, we consider the analytical extension of Eq. (
123
) for
k\in\mathbb{C}
, whose existence is guaranteed by the analytic properties of the Gamma function
Lebedev and Silverman 1972
. The logarithm of
a(k)
can then be decomposed as

\displaystyle\ln{a(k)}=\ln\Gamma\left(1-ik\right)+\ln\Gamma\left(-ik\right)

(124)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\ln{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k-l)\right)}-\ln{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k+l)\right)}\,.

After taking the real part of this and the limit for the imaginary part of
k
going to zero, we end up with the following expression for the transmission probability

\displaystyle\ln T(k)=\ln{\left[\frac{\sinh^{2}(\pi k)}{\cosh^{2}(\pi k)+\sinh^{2}(\pi l)}\right]}\,,

(125)

where we have used some mathematical properties of the Gamma function
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972
.
Therefore, we have recovered Eq. (
197
) and have proved, in this particular case, that the Stieltjes-Perron inversion formula provides the correct solution to the moment problem. However, given the necessity of the explicit analytical expression of the Stieltjes transform, this approach is not going to be useful in general cases.

A different approach may be attempted. By looking at Eqs. (
109
) and (
120
), it is clear that the asymptotic expansion (
118
) corresponds to Eq. (
86
), up to an imaginary unit. At this point, one may be tempted to use such expansion in terms of the moments together with the inversion formula (
119
) to obtain the greybody factors in terms of the KdV integrals.
However, the Stieltjes-Perron inversion formula depends heavily on the analytic properties of the Stieltjes function as it essentially relates the distribution, on the real line, to the jump of the Stieltjes transform across the real axis (which by definition is discontinuous there). Unfortunately, this behavior seems to be lost when we consider the asymptotic expansion (
118
). What is more, Eq. (
118
) is only valid in the complex plane, so that no such expansion is expected to hold on the real line. We can use again the case of the Pöschl-Teller potential to show this. The greybody factor
T(k)
is given in Eq. (
197
). Imagine to start from the asymptotic expansion of Eq. (
86
). The Stieltjes-Perron inversion formula (
119
) will clearly preserve the asymptotic form. However, we immediately see from Eq. (
197
) that the distribution (
109
) on the real line does not allow any asymptotic expansion around
k\to\infty
. Moreover, if we just naively substitute the general expansion (
118
) into Eq. (
119
), everything vanishes in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
.

Finally, it is important to mention that Padé approximants to the Hamburger series (
118
) are convergent
Baker and Graves-Morris 1996
. However, as for the series itself, we cannot take the limit of these Padé approximants to the real line because otherwise Eq. (
119
) would give a vanishing result.

VIII
Conclusions and Discussion

In this work, we have shown that the BH greybody factors are uniquely determined by the set of KdV integrals associated with the BH potential barrier that describes the response of the BH to gravitational perturbations. This result is a consequence of the rich structure and properties of the space of master functions and equations that was presented in previous works
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021a
;
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2021b
. In these works, it was shown that the space of master functions and equations admits different types of isospectral transformations/symmetries. First of all, DTs connect all possible master functions and equations and preserve the spectrum of QNMs as well as the transmission and reflection coefficients, characterizing the continuous part of the spectrum of the associated time-independent Schrödinger operator [See Eq. (
44
)]. For these reasons, we called Darboux covariance the existence of this symmetry. On the other hand, the time-independent master equations admit KdV deformations that are isospectral and that leave invariant the Bogoliubov coefficient
a(k)
(the inverse of the BH transmission coefficient). This conservation law gives rise to the infinite sequence of KdV integrals which, in turn, we have shown to determine completely the BH greybody factors. Moreover, the KdV integrals do not depend on the specific choice of master function/equation, they are the same for all the elements of the space of master functions/equations.

This deep connection between greybody factors, DTs, and KdV integrals has been shown to take place via a moment problem, i.e. the problem of finding a probability distribution given its moments. In our case, the moments are essentially given by the KdV integrals [see Eq. (
108
)]. Among the different possible moment problems, we have shown that the BH moment problem is of the (symmetric) Hamburger type.
We have argued that, due to the particular characteristics of the moment sequence, the moment problem admits a solution and that such solution is actually uniquely determined. However, there is no universal recipe to invert the moment problem and find the distribution given the sequence of moments. Only in a few particular cases, this inversion has been carried out analytically. In particular, when the sequence of moments corresponds to the Catalan numbers
Bostan et al. 2020
, and also in the case of the Pöschl-Teller potential
VII
, as we have seen in Sec.
VII
.

This work brings a completely new perspective to the study of BH scattering via the moment problem and the mathematical structures that have made it possible to arrive to this result.
In a subsequent paper
Lenzi and Sopuerta 2022
, we present some new approximate semi-analytical techniques for the solution of the BH moment problem (i.e. obtaining the BH greybody factors from the KdV integrals) and compare the results with previous works in the literature, showing that our new methods appear to be quite competitive and have a wide range of applications.

The results of this paper are restricted to the case of Schwarzschild BH scattering processes. But it is important to stress that the conclusions we have obtained are actually quite general. The only requirement is that the problem studied is described by a time-independent Schrödinger equation with an integrable potential barrier without bound states. In this sense, the developments of this paper can be extended directly to other physical problems of interest, in particular to rotating Kerr BHs by using the results that come from the Teukolsky master equations and their separable structure
Teukolsky 1972
;
Teukolsky 1972
;
Taylor 1972
. We can also go further in the development of these techniques to try to come out with a new perspective for the computation of quasinormal modes, which is of great interest in the context of gravitational wave physics. Finally, we can try to transfer these developments to other theories of gravity, with different action principle, and/or different dimensions, and/or extra fields.

Hamiltonian Formalism in the Infinite–Dimensional Case

In this case we have a continuous of dynamical variables, here labeled by the coordinate

The changes with respect to the finite-dimensional case are that trajectories are replaced by smooth functions

and phase space functions are replaced by functionals. A functional of

F[V]=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,f(V,V_{,x},V_{,xx},\ldots)\,.

(137)

Functional (Frèchet) derivatives are defined as

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta V(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{n}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial V^{(n)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial V}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial f}{\partial V_{,x}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial V_{,xx}}+\ldots\,,

where
V^{(n)}=d^{n}V(x)/dx^{n}

\frac{\delta V(x)}{\delta V(x^{\prime})}=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,,

(139)

where
\delta(x)

\left\{F,G\right\}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\!\!\!\!dx\!\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\!\!\!\!dx^{\prime}\omega(x,x^{\prime},V)\frac{\delta F}{\delta V(x)}\frac{\delta G}{\delta V(x^{\prime})}\,,

(140)

where

, the symplectic form, has to be such that the Poisson bracket is antisymmetric. A common choice is
Gardner 1971

\omega(x,x^{\prime},V)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{x}-\partial_{x^{\prime}}\right)\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,.

(141)

Since the partial derivative operator
\partial_{x}
is anti-self-adjoin with respect to the inner scalar product

\left(V,W\right)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,V(x)W(x)\,,

(142)

the Poisson bracket can be written as:

\left\{F,G\right\}_{\rm GZF}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta V(x)}{\cal D}\frac{\delta G}{\delta V(x)}\,,

(143)

where

{\cal D}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,.

(144)

the Poisson bracket in Eq. (
143
) is sometimes referred to as the Gardner-Zakharov-Faddeev bracket
Gardner 1971
;
Zakharov and Faddeev 1971
.
In this way, Hamilton’s equations can be written as follows:

\frac{\partial V}{\partial\tau}=\left\{V,{\cal H}[V]\right\}_{\rm GZF}={\cal D}\frac{\delta{\cal H}[V]}{\delta V(x)}\,.

(145)

A different choice of symplectic form was introduced by Magri
Magri 1978

\displaystyle\omega(x,x^{\prime},V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial^{3}_{x}-\partial^{3}_{x^{\prime}}\right)\right.

(146)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.2\left(V(x)\partial_{x}-V(x^{\prime})\partial_{x^{\prime}}\right)\right]\delta(x-x^{\prime})\,.

This leads to the following Poisson bracket:

\left\{F,G\right\}_{\rm M}=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dx\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta V(x)}{\cal E}_{V}\frac{\delta G}{\delta V(x)}\,,

(147)

where
{\cal E}_{V}
is the following operator

{\cal E}_{V}=-\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial x^{3}}+4V(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+2V_{,x}(x)\,,

(148)

It is known that the Magri bracket is closely related to the Virasoro algebra
Gervais 1985
.

Appendix C
1D Scattering from a Potential Barrier

We provide some basic elements of one-dimensional scattering theory
Faddeev 1959
;
Faddeev 1976
;
Deift and Trubowitz 1979
;
Novikov et al. 1984
;
Ablowitz and Segur 1981
, assuming an integrable potential barrier without bound states, only with continuous spectrum. We start by considering a set of independent solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation (
44
) near
x\rightarrow\infty
(spatial infinity for Schwarzschild BHs)

\displaystyle f_{1}(x,k)=e^{ikx}+{\cal O}(1)\,,\;f_{2}(x,k)=e^{-ikx}+{\cal O}(1)\,,

(149)

and a second set satisfying analogous conditions at
x\rightarrow-\infty
(the BH horizon)

\displaystyle g_{1}(x,k)=e^{ikx}+{\cal O}(1)\,,\;g_{2}(x,k)=e^{-ikx}+{\cal O}(1)\,.

(150)

Since the potential is real we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(x,k)=\overline{f}_{2}(x,k)\,,\quad g_{1}(x,k)=\overline{g}_{2}(x,k)\,,

(151)

and, in addition,

\displaystyle f_{1}(x,k)=f_{2}(x,-k)\,,\quad g_{1}(x,k)=g_{2}(x,-k)\,.

(152)

Therefore, the pairs
\left\{f(x,k)\,,\,\overline{f}(x,k)\right\}
and
\left\{g(x,k)\,,\,\overline{g}(x,k)\right\}
, where
f_{1}\equiv f
and
g_{1}\equiv g
, form two fundamental sets of solutions (for
k\neq 0
), known as the
Jost solutions

Taylor 1972
;
Newton 1982
.

The time-independent Schrödinger equation, with boundary conditions given by Eqs. (
149
) and (
150
), is equivalent to the following Volterra-type integral equations (see
Faddeev 1976
;
Deift and Trubowitz 1979
)

\displaystyle f(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{ikx}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dy\,G_{1}(x-y,k)V(y)f(y,k)\,,

(153)

\displaystyle\overline{g}(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-ikx}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!dy\,G_{2}(x-y,k)V(y)\overline{g}(y,k)\,,

(154)

where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are Green’s functions given by

\displaystyle G_{1}(x-y,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\theta(y-x)\frac{\sin{\left[k(x-y)\right]}}{k}\,,

(155)

\displaystyle G_{2}(x-y,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(x-y)\frac{\sin{\left[k(x-y)\right]}}{k}\,,

(156)

and
\theta(x)
is the Heaviside theta function, defined as

\displaystyle\theta(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}1&\mbox{for~}x>0\,,\\[2.84526pt]
0&\mbox{for~}x<0\,.\end{array}\right.

Starting from the Volterra equations one can: prove that the Jost functions are solutions of (
44
) using the method of successive approximations
Faddeev 1976
;
Deift and Trubowitz 1979
; show the linear independence of the pairs of solutions
\left\{f(x,k),\overline{f}(x,k)\right\}
,
\left\{g(x,k),\overline{g}(x,k)\right\}
; and study their analytic properties in the complex
k
-plane.
Indeed, the Wronskians read

\displaystyle W\left[f\,,\overline{f}\right]=W\left[g\,,\overline{g}\right]=2ik\,,

(160)

where we just considered Eqs. (
153
) and (
154
) and the Wronskian (
46
).
They are different form zero whenever
k\neq 0
, thus we have two pairs of linearly independent solutions which we can express as linear combinations of each other, that is,

\displaystyle f(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(k)g(x,k)+b(k)\overline{g}(x,k)\,,

(161)

\displaystyle\overline{f}(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{b}(k)g(x,k)+\overline{a}(k)\overline{g}(x,k)\,.

(162)

Here, we identify the
transition matrix

\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\begin{pmatrix}a(k)&b(k)\\
\overline{b}(k)&\overline{a}(k)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(163)

which relates solutions with asymptotics at
x\rightarrow-\infty
to solutions with asymptotics at
x\rightarrow+\infty
. The coefficients
a(k),b(k),\overline{a}(k),\overline{b}(k)
can be expressed in terms of the Wronskians of the solutions

\displaystyle a(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W[f\,,\overline{g}]}{2ik}\,,\quad b(k)=\frac{W[g\,,f]}{2ik}\,,

(164)

\displaystyle\overline{a}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{W[g\,,\overline{f}]}{2ik}\,,\quad\overline{b}(k)=\frac{W[\overline{f}\,,\overline{g}]}{2ik}\,,

(165)

so that, using Eqs. (
151
) and (
152
), we have

\displaystyle a(-k)=\overline{a}(k)\,,\quad b(-k)=\overline{b}(k)\,.

(166)

After replacing Eqs. (
161
) and (
162
) in the first equality of Eq. (
160
) we obtain

\displaystyle|a(k)|^{2}-|b(k)|^{2}=1\,.

(167)

The physical properties of scattering processes are described by the
scattering matrix
,
\mathcal{S}
, which relates outgoing and ingoing states with respect to the barrier. It is completely determined by the components of
\mathcal{T}
. Let us consider the outgoing states
f
and
\overline{g}
as functions of the the ingoing
\overline{f}
and
g
states:

\displaystyle f(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\overline{a}(k)}g(x,k)+\frac{b(k)}{\overline{a}(k)}\overline{f}(x,k)

(168)

\displaystyle\overline{g}(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\overline{b}(k)}{\overline{a}(k)}g(x,k)+\frac{1}{\overline{a}(k)}\overline{f}(x,k)\,,

(169)

where we used Eqs. (
161
) and (
162
). The scattering matrix
\mathcal{S}
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\overline{a}(k)}&\frac{b(k)}{\overline{a}(k)}\\[5.69054pt]
-\frac{\overline{b}(k)}{\overline{a}(k)}&\frac{1}{\overline{a}(k)}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(170)

Consider now the solution

\displaystyle f(x,k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}a(k)e^{ikx}+b(k)e^{-ikx}&\mbox{for~}x\to-\infty\,,\\[8.53581pt]
e^{ikx}&\mbox{for~}x\to\infty\,,\end{array}\right.

which corresponds to the situation in Figure
2
, where
f(x,k)/a(k)
represents an incoming plane wave from
x\rightarrow-\infty
. The coefficients of the transition matrix define the transmission and reflection coefficients

\displaystyle t(k)=\frac{1}{a(k)}\,,\quad r(k)=\frac{b(k)}{a(k)}\,,

(174)

and then

\displaystyle\frac{f(x,k)}{a(k)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}e^{ikx}+r(k)e^{-ikx}&\mbox{for~}x\to-\infty\,,\\[8.53581pt]
t(k)e^{ikx}&\mbox{for~}x\to\infty\,,\end{array}\right.

The transmission and reflection probabilities, also known as greybody factors, are given by the modulus square of the corresponding coefficients for real
k

T(k)=|t(k)|^{2}\,\quad R(k)=|r(k)|^{2}\,.

(178)

Looking at Eq. (
167
) we notice that the scattering matrix is unitary, i.e.
\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}^{\dagger}=1
, and

\displaystyle T(k)+R(k)=1\,.

(179)

Let us now rewrite Eq. (
153
) in terms of the function
\chi=fe^{-ikx}

Novikov et al. 1984
as follows

\displaystyle\chi(x,k)=1+\int_{x}^{\infty}\!\!dy\,\frac{e^{-2ik(x-y)}-1}{2ik}\,V(y)\,\chi(y,k)\,.

(180)

where we have used Eq. (
155
). Since we assume the potential to be integrable,
\chi(x,k)
admits an analytical continuation into
\Im(k)>0
, and so does the Jost function
f=\chi e^{ikx}
. The same procedure leads to the conclusion that
\overline{g}
can also be analytically continued into
\Im(k)>0
while
\overline{f}
and
g
can be continued into the lower half-plane, i.e.
\Im(k)<0
.

Finally, Eqs. (
164
) and (
165
) show that: (i)
a(k)
can be completely expressed in terms of
f
and
\overline{g}
, and hence it can be analytically continued into the region
\Im(k)>0
, having the following asymptotic behavior

\displaystyle a(k)=1+{\cal O}(1/k)\;\mbox{for}\;|k|\rightarrow\infty\,,\;\Im(k)>0\,.

(181)

(ii)
\overline{a}(k)
can be expressed in terms of
\overline{f}
and
g
and therefore, it can be analytically continued into the region
\Im(k)<0
. (iii) The integrability of the potential is not enough to guarantee the analyticity of
b
and
\overline{b}
.

Appendix D
The Pöschl-Teller Potential

The time-independent Schrödinger equation (
44
) for the case of a Pöschl-Teller potential [see Eq. (
122
)] can be solved
Landau and Lifshitz 1981
;
Çevik et al. 2016
by using a new coordinate,
y=\tanh(\alpha x)
, and use a new unknown

\displaystyle\psi(y)=2^{ik/\alpha}\left(1-y^{2}\right)^{-ik/2\alpha}\nu(y)\,,

(182)

such that it satisfies the Jacobi equation

\displaystyle\left(1-y^{2}\right)\nu^{\prime\prime}(y)+2y\left(\frac{ik}{\alpha}-1\right)\nu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle-\left[\lambda(1-\lambda)-\frac{k^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}-\frac{ik}{\alpha}\right]\nu(y)=0\,.

(183)

Introducing another coordinate,
z=(1-y)/2
, the Jacobi equation becomes a hypergeometric equation
Lebedev and Silverman 1972
;
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle(1-z)\nu^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left[\left(1-\frac{ik}{\alpha}\right)-2\left(1-\frac{ik}{\alpha}\right)z\right]\nu^{\prime}(z)

(184)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left(\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha}\right)\left(1-\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha}\right)\nu(z)=0\,.

The general solution reads

\displaystyle\psi(x,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\,2^{ik/\alpha}\left[1-\tanh^{2}(\alpha x)\right]^{-ik/2\alpha}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha},1-\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha},1-\frac{ik}{\alpha};\frac{1-\tanh(\alpha x)}{2}\right)

(185)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B\,\left[1-\tanh(\alpha x)\right]^{ik/2\alpha}\left[1+\tanh(\alpha x)\right]^{-ik/2\alpha}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\lambda,1-\lambda,1+\frac{ik}{\alpha};\frac{1-\tanh(\alpha x)}{2}\right)\ .

The reflection and transmission coefficients can be found from the asymptotics of the hypergeometric function
Abramowitz and Stegun 1972
. First, the asymptotic behavior for
\psi(x,k)
is

\displaystyle\psi(x,k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}A\,e^{ikx}+B\,e^{-ikx}&~x\rightarrow\infty\,,\\[5.69054pt]
A^{\prime}\,e^{ikx}+B^{\prime}\,e^{-ikx}&~x\rightarrow-\infty\,,\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle A^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\,\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(-\frac{ik}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(1-\lambda-\frac{ik}{\alpha})}

(189)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(-\frac{ik}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\lambda)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\,\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(\frac{ik}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\lambda)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}

\displaystyle\l

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(\frac{ik}{\alpha})}{\Gamma(\lambda+\frac{ik}{\alpha})\Gamma(1-\lambda+\frac{ik}{\alpha})}\ .

(190)

The physical situation of Fig.
2
requires

\displaystyle A=1\,,\quad B=0\,,\quad A^{\prime}=a(k)\,,\quad B^{\prime}=b(k)\,,

(191)

so that

\displaystyle a(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1-ik)\Gamma(-ik)}{\Gamma(\lambda-ik)\Gamma(1-\lambda-ik)}

(192)

\displaystyle b(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1-ik)\Gamma(ik)}{\Gamma(\lambda)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}\ ,

(193)

where we set
\alpha=1
to simplify the notation. Then, the transmission and reflection coefficients (
174
) are

\displaystyle t(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\lambda-ik)\Gamma(1-\lambda-ik)}{\Gamma(1-ik)\Gamma(-ik)}

(194)

\displaystyle r(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(ik)\Gamma(\lambda-ik)\Gamma(1-\lambda-ik)}{\Gamma(-ik)\Gamma(1-\lambda)\Gamma(\lambda)}\ .

(195)

When the Pöschl-Teller potential is a high barrier, i.e. when
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}+il
with
l>0

Çevik et al. 2016
, the transmission coefficient reads

t(k)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k-l)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-i(k+l)\right)}{\Gamma(1-ik)\Gamma(-ik)}\,.

(196)

Then, the transmission probability, or greybody factor, can be evaluated by taking the square of the modulus of Eq. (
196
):

\displaystyle T(k)=\frac{\sinh^{2}(\pi k)}{\cosh^{2}(\pi k)+\sinh^{2}(\pi l)}\,,

(197)

where here
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
