Azadkia–Chatterjee’s dependence coefficient for infinite dimensional data
Siegfried Hörmann, and Daniel Strenger

Abstract
We extend the scope of Azadkia–Chatterjee’s dependence coefficient between a scalar response Y Y and a multivariate covariate X X to the case where X X takes values in a general metric space. Particular attention is paid to the case where X X is a curve. Although extending this framework at the population level is relatively straightforward, analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the estimator proves to be complex. This complexity is largely related to the nearest neighbor structure of the infinite-dimensional covariate sample, leading us to explore a topic that has not been previously addressed in the literature. The primary contribution of this paper is to provide insights into this issue and propose strategies to address it. Our findings also have significant implications for other graph-based methods facing similar challenges.



1
Introduction

Assume that
Y
is a real random variable with distribution function
F(t)
.
For some covariate
X
, let
G_{X}(t)=P(Y\geq\penalty\ t|X)
.

Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
have studied a dependence coefficient between
Y
and
X
, which, for continuous
F
, can be expressed as

T(X,Y):=6\times\int\mathrm{Var}(G_{X}(t))dF(t).

(1)

Note that if
X
and
Y
are independent, then
G_{X}(t)=G(t)=P(Y\geq\penalty\ t)
, which is non-random, and hence we have
T(X,Y)=\penalty\ 0
. On the other hand, if
Y=f(X)
for some measurable function
f
, then
{G_{X}(t)=1\{Y\geq\penalty\ t\}}
and thus it follows that
T(X,Y)=6\int F(t)(1-F(t))dF(t)=1
.
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
show that
0\leq\penalty\ T(X,Y)\leq\penalty\ 1
. Moreover,
T(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent and
T(X,Y)=\penalty\ 1
if and only if
Y=f(X)
.

When
X
is a continuous scalar variable
this measure and variants thereof have been already introduced earlier in
Trutschnig (2011)
and
Dette et al. (2013)
. These authors relate the considered dependence measures to the copula that links the distributions of
X
and
Y
.

Chatterjee (2020)
has extended their work to arbitrary marginals and proposed a tuning parameter-free estimator. Since then, the dependence coefficient
T=T(X,Y)
has attracted a lot of attention.
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
have treated a further generalization allowing for multivariate
X
and conditional dependence.
An alternative approach for multivariate and continuously distributed
X
was given by
Griessenberger et al. (2022)
.
Cao and Bickel (2020)
relate it to the maximal correlation coefficient and study similar measures that are able to detect functional relationships of prespecified shape.

Much research has been conducted on the asymptotic behavior of related estimators and independence tests:
Shi et al. (2022)
prove the asymptotic normality of the estimator proposed by
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
when
X
and
Y
are independent and reveal a weakness in the corresponding test’s capability to identify local alternatives, a matter that is also treated by
Bickel (2022)
and
Lin and Han (2023)
. The latter papers also advise on how to overcome these issues.
Auddy et al. (2024)
investigate for which kind of contiguous alternatives the test possesses non-trivial power. As
T
is non-symmetric,
Zhang (2023)
studies the asymptotic behavior of a symmetrized version proposed by
Chatterjee (2020)
under the null hypothesis of independence. For a detailed review of recent developments, we refer to
Chatterjee (2023)
.

Motivated by our research in functional data analysis (FDA), a main target of this article is to explore asymptotic inferential procedures for
T
(consistent estimation and testing
T=0
) when the covariates
X
take values in an infinite-dimensional space. We are particularly interested in the case where
{X=(X(u)\colon u\in\mathcal{U})}
is a curve. A very common setting in FDA is that
{X\in L^{2}(\mathcal{U})}
, the space of square integrable functions on some continuum
\mathcal{U}
.

Attempts to generalize the setting of
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
to a broad class of topological spaces have been made previously by
Deb et al. (2020)
(unconditional dependence) and
Huang et al. (2022)
(conditional dependence). Those authors consider measures of association based on so called characteristic kernels. The empirical versions of these measures are relying on geometric graphs related to the sample, like the
k
-nearest neighbor graph.
Deb et al. (2020)
show that the measure
T
and the corresponding estimator can be viewed as a special case (see their Proposition 8.2).

A bottleneck arising in current literature arises from required bounds on the maximum degree of the underlying graphs. While the assumptions made can be shown to hold for multivariate data, they are either difficult to verify or, as we are going to demonstrate, may even fail in infinite-dimensional spaces. Our Theorem
2
below gives some insight in this matter and shows that nearest neighbor graphs for functional data can have maximal degrees which do not just diverge with sample size, but their rate of divergence can be rather fast. This result easily extends to
k
-nearest neighbor graphs and minimal spanning trees (see Remark
3
) and illustrates that assumptions that have been used previously in the literature may fail in infinite-dimensional settings. Since nearest-neighbor methods are an important ingredient to many statistical and machine learning procedures, our findings may have far-reaching implications for the applicability of graph based methods in the context of functional data.

The subsequent sections are organized as follows: In Section
2
, we will briefly outline the estimation approach in
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
. We show that under fairly mild technical conditions the estimator remains consistent in general metric spaces. We also use this example to explain the difficulties that one incurs when using nearest-neighbor-based statistics in infinite-dimensional spaces. In Section
3
we construct some specific functional random samples for which we are able to give upper and lower bounds on the maximal degree of the resulting nearest neighbor graphs. Additionally, we obtain the limiting distribution of the estimator by
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
under independence. The resulting independence test is shown to be universally consistent. We apply this test to real world data in Section
4
. In Section
5
we give detailed proofs. contains a simulation study as an illustration of the empirical performance of our theory.

2
An estimator for
T(X,Y)

For the remainder of this paper, we impose some mild technical requirements that will streamline our presentation. To this end, we define
{H(t):=P(d(X,X^{\prime})\leq\penalty\ t)}
, where
X^{\prime}
an independent copy of
X
.

Assumption 1
.

We have

1.

H(t)
is continuous;

2.

F(t)
is continuous;

3.

for
P_{Y}:=P\circ Y^{-1}
almost all
t
, the mapping
x\mapsto G_{x}(t)
is continuous
P_{X}:=P\circ X^{-1}
almost everywhere.

Assumption
1
constitutes a set of continuity conditions on the distribution of
(X,Y)
. Continuity assumptions have also been used in
Dette et al. (2013)
, while
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
work under general distributional assumptions. We note that Assumption
1
could be relaxed, but in the context of functional data, our assumptions are reasonably general and common. For example, a violation of (a) would arise if
P_{X}
is a discrete measure, which is quite uncommon for a functional data model.

Assumption
1
(b) implies that
\mathrm{Var}(G_{X}(t))=\mathbb{E}\,G_{X}^{2}(t)-G^{2}(t)
and
\int G^{2}(t)dF(t)=\penalty\ 1/3
. Hence, estimation of
T(X,Y)
reduces to estimation of

Q(X,Y):=\int\mathbb{E}\,G_{X}^{2}(t)dF(t).

(2)

Consider a random sample
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})
,
1\leq\penalty\ i\leq\penalty\ n\}
with
(X_{i},Y_{i})\sim(X,Y)
.
For every
{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, let
N(i)=N_{n}(i)
be the index of the nearest neighbor of
X_{i}
in the sample of covariates
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, that is,
d(X_{i},X_{N(i)})\leq\penalty\ d(X_{i},X_{j})
for all
j\neq i
. Assumption
1
(a) implies that
N(i)
is unique with probability one. If
n
is large, we expect that
X_{N(i)}
is close to
X_{i}
and therefore, considering Assumption
1
(c), we get by some heuristics that for almost all
t

\mathbb{E}\,G_{X}^{2}(t)\approx\mathbb{E}\,G_{X_{i}}(t)G_{X_{N(i)}}(t).

(3)

Moreover, noting that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\,G_{X_{i}}(t)G_{X_{N(i)}}(t)&=\mathbb{E}\,\Big(\mathbb{E}\,\big[1\{Y_{i}\geq\penalty\ t\}1\{Y_{N(i)}\geq\penalty\ t\}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\big]\Big)\\
&=\mathbb{E}\,1\{Y_{i}\geq\penalty\ t\}1\{Y_{N(i)}\geq\penalty\ t\},\end{split}

(4)

we obtain the approximation

Q(X,Y)\approx E\int 1\{Y_{i}\geq\penalty\ t\}1\{Y_{N(i)}\geq\penalty\ t\}dF(t)=E\min\{F(Y_{i}),F(Y_{N(i)})\}.

(5)

This motivates the estimators

\widehat{Q}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{F_{n}(Y_{i}),F_{n}(Y_{N(i)})\}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{T}_{n}=6\,\widehat{Q}_{n}-2,

(6)

with
F_{n}
being the empirical distribution function of
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
.

Remark 1
.

We note that
\widehat{T}_{n}
is, in essence, the estimator given in
Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
, where we have been taking into account that
Y
is supposed to have a continuous distribution.

Remark 2
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\mathcal{G}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
be the nearest neighbor graph related to the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
Then
\widehat{T}_{n}
is a functional of the responses
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
and of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

A non-trivial question is whether the heuristics leading to
\widehat{T}_{n}
can be rigorously justified, i.e. whether
\widehat{T}_{n}
is a consistent estimator of
T(X,Y)
. Our first result establishes weak consistency of
\widehat{T}_{n}
for i.i.d. data under the continuity properties stated in Assumption
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
X
takes values in a separable metric space
(H,d)
. Let Assumption
1
hold. Then we have that

\widehat{T}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}T(X,Y)\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(7)

Azadkia and Chatterjee (2021)
have shown that in the finite-dimensional setup, almost sure convergence can be obtained in (
7
) and Assumption
1
can be dropped.
A closer inspection of their paper reveals that the proof is crucially based on the fact that within a set of arbitrary points
{\mathcal{S}_{n}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}}
, an element
x_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
can be the nearest neighbor to at most
k(p)
points in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
, where
k(p)
is some finite constant that is independent of
n
. Formally, define the maximum degree
L_{n}=L_{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n}):=\max_{1\leq\penalty\ i\leq\penalty\ n}L_{i,n}
, where
L_{i,n}
is the number of elements in
\mathcal{S}_{n}\setminus x_{i}
that have
x_{i}
as their nearest neighbor. Then

L_{n}\leq\penalty\ k(p)\quad\text{for all\penalty\ $n\geq\penalty\ 1$}.

(8)

For example, if we generate a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
then trivially
L_{n}\leq\penalty\ 2
. In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
it is not hard to see that
L_{n}\leq\penalty\ 6
.
Kabatjanski and Levenstein (1978)
have shown that
k(p)\leq\penalty\ \mathrm{const}\times\gamma^{p}
for some absolute constant
\gamma>1
and for all
p\geq\penalty\ 1
.

In this paper, however, we are interested in an infinite-dimensional covariate space. Here we generally cannot bound
L_{n}
by a constant. Consider, for example, an orthonormal sequence of elements
x_{k}
,
k\geq\penalty\ 2
, in
L^{2}(\mathcal{U})
, i.e.

\int_{\mathcal{U}}x_{k}(u)x_{\ell}(u)du=\delta_{k,\ell},

(9)

where
\delta_{k,\ell}
denotes the Kronecker delta. Then it holds that
{d(x_{k},x_{\ell})=\sqrt{2}(1-\delta_{k,\ell})}
. If we set
x_{1}=0
, then
x_{1}
is the nearest neighbor to all other elements, and thus
L_{n}=n-1
.
In this example, however big is
n
,
d(x_{i},x_{N(i)})
is not becoming small, and the heuristics that lead to (
3
) are no longer applicable. This demonstrates that we need to explore and manage the order of magnitude of
L_{n}
in a random sample in infinite-dimensional spaces—an aspect that appears to be entirely unexamined in the literature. For insightful discussions on the problematic usage of nearest neighbor methods in high dimension, we refer to
Beyer et al. (1997)
and
Durrant and Kabán (2009)
.

Let us illustrate this problem on the data example that will be presented in Section
4
. These data consist of the age distributions of
n=2117
Austrian municipalities. In Figure
1
we see one curve which turns out to be the nearest neighbor of 66 other curves, which corresponds to
\approx 3\%
of the sample size.
The example confirms that our theoretical issue is also relevant in practice and that we may get nearest neighbor graphs with some rather large degrees.

:
Age distribution curves for 2117 municipalities in Austria. On the
y
-axis we see the proportions. The curve related to Wolfsberg (solid red) is the nearest neighbor of the age distribution curves of 66 municipalities (blue).

Main results

The data points from the counterexample in Section
2
constitute an infinite series of orthogonal functions, leading to a set of curves that differs significantly from of a random sample. In our next result we demonstrate that
L_{n}
may diverge at a polynomial rate even in random samples. To this end, we consider random elements
X_{i}
taking values in
L^{2}([0,1])
. The space
L^{2}([0,1])
is equipped with the inner product

\langle x,y\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}x(u)y(u)du

(10)

and the corresponding norm
\|x\|^{2}=\langle x,x\rangle
, which gives rise to the distance
{d(x,y)=\|x-y\|}
.
Let
\Sigma=\mathrm{Var}(X_{1})
be the covariance operator of
X_{1}
, that is

\Sigma(v)=\mathbb{E}\,(X_{1}-EX_{1})\langle X_{1}-EX_{1},v\rangle

(11)

and denote by

its eigenvalues and by

corresponding eigenfunctions.
Moreover, recall that a function

is called slowly varying at

{\ell(yx)/\ell(x)\to 1}
for any

\ell(x)=o(x^{\delta})

We use the notion

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a symmetric, positive semidefinite operator on

having eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
. Suppose that
\Sigma
is trace-class, i.e.,
\sum_{k\geq\penalty\ 1}\lambda_{k}<\infty

with covariance operator
\mathrm{Var}(X)=\Sigma
, such that for a random sample
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\penalty\
with
X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}X
the following holds:

(i)

If
\lambda_{k}=o\left(\sum_{j\geq\penalty\ k}\lambda_{j}\right)
, then
L_{n}=L_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\to\infty
in probability.

(ii)

If
\lambda_{k}=\ell(k)k^{-a}
with
\ell\in R_{0}
, then
L_{n}=\omega_{P}\Big(h(n)n^{1/({2a-1})}\Big)

(iii)

Under the same condition as in (ii), it holds that such that
{L_{n}=o_{P}(h(n)n^{2/(2a-1)})}
for some

.

For example, if we choose

is a kernel operator with kernel

It is well known that the eigenvalues of

and

.

Remark 3
.

It is easy to see from the proof of Theorem

An important message learned from Theorem

Assumption 2

We have

Before we discuss Assumption

Let Assumptions

The variable

Remark 4
.

Both,

Proof of Theorem

Lemma 1

Let

be the nearest neighbor of

Proof.

Let

Proposition 2
.

Under the setting of Proposition

Proof.

on a common probability space

(57)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&D_{n}^{1}=\sqrt{n}(\widehat{Q}_{n}-\widetilde{Q}_{n})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{n}(Z_{i}),\\
&D_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{n}(Z_{i})-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})\cdot\frac{|\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}\cap I_{j}^{m_{n}}|}{n},\\
&D_{n}^{3}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})\cdot\frac{|\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}\}\cap I_{j}^{m_{n}}|}{n}-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})\int_{I_{j}^{m_{n}}}h(t)dt,\\
&D_{n}^{4}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})\int_{I_{j}^{m_{n}}}h(t)dt-\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{1}{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})h(j/m_{n}),\\
&D_{n}^{5}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\frac{1}{m_{n}}B_{n}(j/m_{n})h(j/m_{n})-\int_{0}^{1}(B_{n}\cdot h)(t)dt.\end{split}

(59)

Denote by

the sup-norm on

P\left(|D_{n}^{1}|>\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\\
\leq\penalty\ P\left(\|\sqrt{n}(F_{n}-F)-B_{n}\circ F\|_{\infty}>\frac{\log^{3}n}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\to 0.

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D_{n}^{2}|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|B_{n}(Z_{i})-B_{n}(j/m_{n})|1\{Z_{i}\in I_{j}^{m_{n}}\}\leq\penalty\ Cm_{n}^{-\alpha}.\end{split}

We note that the bounds derived for

Inserting into (

By similar arguments we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D_{n}^{5}|&\leq\penalty\ 2\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|B_{n}(t)|/m_{n}+2\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\int_{I_{j}^{m_{n}}}|B_{n}(j/m_{n})-B_{n}(t)|dt=O_{P}(m_{n}^{-1/2}).\end{split}

In the last step, we used the basic fact that

.

It remains to show that

is normally distributed with mean zero. To calculate the variance, recall that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Var\left(\int_{0}^{1}B_{n}(t)h(t)dt\right)&=\mathbb{E}\,\left(\left(\int_{0}^{1}B_{n}(t)h(t)dt\right)^{2}\right)\\

∎

P\left(\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{M(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2}-\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{N(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2}<\epsilon\big|X_{1}^{q}=x\right)\leq\penalty\ \mathrm{const}\times n^{2}\epsilon,

(97)

where the constant is independent of
n
,
q
,
\epsilon
and
x
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4
.

We observe that

\{L_{1,n}>L_{1,n}^{q}\}\subset\{\exists i\in\{2,\ldots,n\}\colon\|X_{i}-X_{N(i|q)}\|^{2}>\|X_{1}-X_{i}\|^{2}\}.

(98)

Thus, by reasons of symmetry, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}P(L_{1,n}>L_{1,n}^{q})&\leq\penalty\ nP(\|X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)}\|^{2}>\|X_{2}-X_{1}\|^{2}).\end{split}

(99)

Denote by
\Pi^{q}
the projection onto the space spanned by
\{e_{q+1},e_{q+2},\ldots\}
. Then by Pythagoras’ theorem we have

\|X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)}\|^{2}=\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{N(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2}+\|\Pi^{q}(X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)})\|^{2}.

Thus for any
\epsilon>0
it holds that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P(\|X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)}\|^{2}>\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|^{2})\\
&=P(\|\Pi^{q}(X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)})\|^{2}>\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|^{2}-\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{N(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2})\\
&\leq\penalty\ P(\|\Pi^{q}(X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)})\|^{2}>\epsilon)+P(\|X_{1}-X_{2}\|^{2}-\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{N(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2}\leq\penalty\ \epsilon)\\
&\leq\penalty\ \epsilon^{-1}\times E\|\Pi^{q}(X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)})\|^{2}+P(\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{2}^{q}\|^{2}-\|X_{1}^{q}-X_{N(1|q)}^{q}\|^{2}\leq\penalty\ \epsilon)\\
&\leq\penalty\ \epsilon^{-1}\times\sum_{k>q}E\langle X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)},e_{k}\rangle^{2}+\mathrm{const}\times n^{2}\epsilon.\end{split}

(100)

We have used the Markov inequality and Lemma
13
. We remark that the independence of the scores
\langle X_{1},e_{k}\rangle
for
k\geq\penalty\ 1
, and the fact that
N(1|q)
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{\langle X_{i},e_{k}\rangle\colon 1\leq\penalty\ i\leq\penalty\ n,\,1\leq\penalty\ k\leq\penalty\ q\}
imply that
\langle X_{1},e_{k}\rangle\penalty\
and
\langle X_{N(1|q)},e_{k}\rangle
are independent and have the same distribution if
k>q
. Thus,

\sum_{k>q}E\langle X_{1}-X_{N(1|q)},e_{k}\rangle^{2}=2\sum_{k>q}E\langle X_{1},e_{k}\rangle^{2}=2\sum_{k>q}\lambda_{k}.

(101)

We have shown that

P(L_{1,n}>L_{1,n}^{q})\leq\penalty\ \mathrm{const}\times n\left(\epsilon^{-1}\times\sum_{k>q}\lambda_{k}+n^{2}\epsilon\right).

The proof is concluded by setting
\epsilon=\penalty\ n^{-1}\times\penalty\ \left(\sum_{k>q}\lambda_{k}\right)^{1/2}
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let Assumption
3
hold and assume that we can choose
q=q_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j>q}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=o(n^{-6}),

(102)

\displaystyle P(L_{n}^{q}>n^{1/4})

\displaystyle=o(1).

(103)

Then Assumption
2
is fulfilled.

Proof.

The result is immediate from (
89
) and Proposition
4
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

As shown by
Kabatjanski and Levenstein (1978)
, if
n
is large enough, it holds that
{P(L_{n}^{q}>n^{1/4})=0}
for
{\gamma>2^{0.401}}
, and
q_{n}\ <\frac{1}{4}\log_{\gamma}n
, a condition that is met for our choice of
q_{n}
. Finally, condition (
102
) follows directly from our assumptions.
∎

Remark 5
.

We note that a much slower rate of decay for
\lambda_{j}
can be obtained if we could allow a bigger value for
q
in (
103
). While to the best of our knowledge very little is known about distributional properties of
L_{n}^{q}
(even for fixed
q
), it seems certain that the exponential growth obtained from
Kabatjanski and Levenstein (1978)
is way too conservative when applied to a random sample. This is confirmed in extensive simulations, where we found
L_{n}
to grow at a relatively slow rate, even in cases of very slowly decaying eigenvalues, as, for example, for the Brownian motion. Based on the fact that nearest neighbors constitute a “local” property, we conjecture that for fixed
q
and
k
, the probability
P(L_{n}^{q}>k)
does not depend on the distribution of the score vectors
\Pi^{q}X
as long as they have continuous densities.
