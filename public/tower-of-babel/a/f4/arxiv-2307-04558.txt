On an uncertainty result by Donoho and Stark
Oriol Baeza Guasch

Abstract




Abstract.
In the work of Donoho and Stark
[
2
,
3
]
, they study a manifestation of the uncertainty principle in signal recovery. They conjecture that, for a function with support of bounded size
T
, the maximum concentration of its Fourier transform in the low frequencies
[-W/2,W/2]
is achieved when the support of the function is an interval. In
[
4
]
, they are able to prove a positive result under the extra assumption that
WT\leq 0.8
, using an inequality with symmetric rearrangements. In our work, we present a more elementary proof of their result, while also relaxing the required bound to
WT\leq 1
.

Finally, we also study a discrete version of the problem, by considering complex polynomials and their concentration on subsets of the unit circle, and we prove an analogous problem. Lastly, this result is used to improve an inequality by Montgomery, appearing in
[
10
]
.

1
Introduction

To state the original conjecture by Donoho and Stark, we must introduce the following operators, defined for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. First, the time-limiting operator for a given measurable subset
\mathcal{T}

(P_{\mathcal{T}}f)(t)=\begin{cases}f(t)\quad t\in\mathcal{T},\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(1.1)

and second the frequency-limiting operator for a given measurable subset
\mathcal{W}

(P_{\mathcal{W}}f)(t)=\int_{\mathcal{W}}e^{2\pi iwt}\hat{f}(w)\,dw

(1.2)

where
\hat{f}
is the Fourier transform of
f
, with the convention

\hat{f}(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)e^{-2\pi iwt}\,dt

(1.3)

Then, their conjecture can be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1

(Conj. 1,
[
3
]
)
.

The supremum
\sup||P_{\mathcal{W}}P_{\mathcal{T}}||
, where
\mathcal{W}
is an interval and
\mathcal{T}
ranges over measurable subsets with fixed measure, is attained when
\mathcal{T}
is also an interval.

In
[
4
]
, they are able to prove a positive result under the extra assumption given by the bound
WT\leq 0.8
, where this quantities refer to the sizes of the subsets:
W=|\mathcal{W}|
and
T=|\mathcal{T}|
.

However, we will rather work with a symmetric formulation of the statement, where we consider
\sup||P_{\mathcal{T}}P_{\mathcal{W}}||
instead. This will result more convenient for our work, given the interpretation that will be presented later, while being equivalent to the original conjecture.

Claim 1
.

The norms
||P_{\mathcal{T}}P_{\mathcal{W}}||=||P_{\mathcal{W}}P_{\mathcal{T}}||
are equal. In particular, both formulations of the conjecture are equivalent.

Proof.

The proof is immediate after the observation that each of the operators
P_{\mathcal{T}}
and
P_{\mathcal{W}}
are self-adjoint, so the adjoint operator of
P_{\mathcal{T}}P_{\mathcal{W}}
is precisely
P_{\mathcal{W}}P_{\mathcal{T}}
. As the norm of an operator in a Hilbert spaces is the same as its adjoint, the conclusion follows.

Next, we will give an interpretation of this symmetric formulation that motivates the result that we will rather prove. For that, we introduce the concentration operator, for a measurable set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
we define

c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt}

(1.4)

Now, because
||P_{\mathcal{W}}||=1
it follows straightforwardly that

||P_{\mathcal{T}}P_{\mathcal{W}}||=\sup_{f\in L^{2}:f=P_{\mathcal{W}}f}\frac{||P_{\mathcal{T}}f||}{||f||}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}:f=P_{\mathcal{W}}f}\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}:f=P_{\mathcal{W}}f}c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)

(1.5)

which we might interpret as calculating the concentration for a function
f
in the measurable set
\mathcal{T}
, and restricting our attention to functions whose Fourier transform have support in
\mathcal{W}
.

Therefore, altogether the main result that we will prove in this work is

Theorem
.

Let
W
and
T
be real numbers such that
WT\leq 1
. Then, for all measurable subsets
\mathcal{T}
of the real numbers with size
|\mathcal{T}|=T
, and functions
f
whose Fourier transform has support
\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
, the following inequality is true

\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\left|g(t)\right|^{2}\,dt

where
g
is the function given by the inverse Fourier transform of
|\hat{f}|
. In particular, by denoting
\mathbb{I}=[-T/2,T/2]
, it holds
c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)
.

Which by the previous reasoning improves the result by Donoho and Stark, by relaxing the bound required.

On the other hand, the difference in interpretation for the original conjecture is that there the concentration is computed in the frequency domain
\mathcal{W}
(rather than the temporal domain
\mathcal{T}
). Nonetheless, there is a similarity in the approaches for the proof: to
modify
the function
f
to obtain a function with higher concentration, but with support on a single interval of the same size. In particular, in their proof the improvement in concentration is given by
|f|^{*}
the symmetric decreasing rearrangement, which is defined as,

\mu_{f}(\alpha)=\left|\{t:f(t)\geq\alpha\}\right|<\infty\quad\implies\quad f^{*}(1/2\mu_{f}(\alpha))=f^{*}(-1/2\mu_{f}(\alpha))=\alpha

(1.6)

That is, the symmetric function decreasing about the origin that has the same measure of its level sets, and which will be supported on the interval
[-|\supp f|/2,|\supp f|/2]
.

The use of symmetric rearrangements is motivated in their proof because it allows to use the following lemma, by Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya.

Lemma 1

(Thm. 380,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
f,g
and
h
be positive functions. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)g(y)h(x-y)\text{ dxdy}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{*}(x)g^{*}(y)h^{*}(x-y)\text{ dxdy}

Their additional restriction
WT\leq 0.8
arises here, as they require that
|\sinc t|^{*}=\sinc t
. On the other hand, our restriction will arise from imposing only
|\sin t|=\sin t
in the domain, resulting in a less restrictive bound of
WT\leq 1
.

Finally, also worth mentioning that when restricting to both
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{T}
intervals, the concentration operator has been widely studied. In particular, the functions maximizing the concentration are called the Prolate Spheroidal Wave Functions, with their characterization described with detail in the work of Slepian, Pollak and Landau
[
12
,
11
,
9
]
, among others.

2
Previous lemmas

We first prove a useful inequality which takes advantage of the concavity/convexity of the sine function, together with the known inequalities by Jensen and Karamata.

Lemma 2
.

Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
L
a fixed real number. Then, for all real numbers
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
such that
x_{1}+x_{2}+\dots+x_{n}=L
, the expression

\Big|\sin(x_{1})+\sin(x_{2})+\dots+\sin(x_{n})\Big|

(2.1)

achieves its maximum when
\#\{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{n}\}\leq 2
, where the
y_{i}
are real numbers in
[0,2\pi)
such that
y_{i}\equiv x_{i}\pmod{2\pi}
. In other words, all
x_{i}
leave the same remainder modulo
2\pi
except maybe one.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is taken from a similar result for concave-convex functions in (Cirtoaje, 2006)
[
1
]
.

Let
y_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
be such that
y_{i}\equiv x_{i}\pmod{2\pi}
. It is clear that

\sin(x_{1})+\dots+\sin(x_{n})=\sin(y_{1})+\dots+\sin(y_{n})

(2.2)

Now, suppose (
2.2
) achieves a maximum at point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
and write without loss of generality
y_{1}\leq y_{2}\leq\dots\leq y_{n}
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
y_{1}<y_{n-1}
, and we distinguish two cases.

Case 1.
If
y_{n-1}\leq\pi
, using that
\sin(y)
is a concave function in
[0,\pi]
, we have by Jensen’s inequality

\begin{split}\sin(x_{1})+\sin(x_{n-1})&=\sin(y_{1})+\sin(y_{n-1})\\
&<2\sin\left(\frac{y_{1}+y_{n-1}}{2}\right)\\
&=\sin\left(y_{1}+\frac{y_{n-1}-y_{1}}{2}\right)+\sin\left(y_{n-1}-\frac{y_{n-1}-y_{1}}{2}\right)\\
&=\sin\left(x_{1}+\frac{y_{n-1}-y_{1}}{2}\right)+\sin\left(x_{n-1}-\frac{y_{n-1}-y_{1}}{2}\right)\end{split}

(2.3)

with the inequality being strict since the variables are different, contradiction.

Case 2.
If
y_{n-1}>\pi
and
y_{n}+y_{n-1}-\pi<2\pi
, using that
\sin(y)
is a strictly convex function in
(\pi,2\pi)
, we have by Karamata’s majorization inequality that

\begin{split}\sin(x_{n-1})+\sin(x_{n})&=\sin(y_{n-1})+\sin(y_{n})\\
&<\sin(\pi)+\sin(y_{n}+y_{n-1}-\pi)\\
&=\sin\left(y_{n-1}-(y_{n-1}-\pi)\right)+\sin\left(y_{n}+(y_{n-1}-\pi)\right)\\
&=\sin\left(x_{n-1}-(y_{n-1}-\pi)\right)+\sin\left(x_{n}+(y_{n-1}-\pi)\right)\end{split}

(2.4)

with the inequality being strict since
y_{n-1}>\pi
, contradiction.

Case 3.
If
y_{n-1}>\pi
and
y_{n}+y_{n-1}-\pi\geq 2\pi
, using that
\sin(y)
is a convex function in
(\pi,2\pi]
, we have by Karamata’s majorization inequality that

\begin{split}\sin(x_{n-1})+\sin(x_{n})&=\sin(y_{n-1})+\sin(y_{n})\\
&<\sin(y_{n}+y_{n-1}-2\pi)+\sin(2\pi)\\
&=\sin\left(y_{n-1}+y_{n}\right)+\sin\left(y_{n}-y_{n}\right)\\
&=\sin\left(x_{n-1}+y_{n}\right)+\sin\left(x_{n}-y_{n}\right)\end{split}

(2.5)

with the inequality being strict since
y_{n}<2\pi
.

Altogether, we conclude that
y_{1}=y_{n-1}
.

Now, we should study the maximum of

-\left(\sin(x_{1})+\dots+\sin(x_{n})\right)

(2.6)

but using that
\sin
is an odd function, we might just apply the previous argument to variables
-x_{i}
, and a similar conclusion is reached.
∎

In particular, the previous result serves to prove the main lemma required for the proof of the theorem.

Lemma 3
.

Let
r\geq 1
be a positive integer and
L
a fixed real value. Then, for all real variables
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r}
with
\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=L
it holds that

\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\leq 4\,\sin^{2}\left(L/2\right)

Proof.

To begin, define for simplicity

h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})\coloneqq\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}

(2.7)

The key observation is that the value of the expression
h
in (
2.7
) is independent of a shift of the variables
1
1

1

This observation arises more naturally during the proof of
Theorem

1
, for the case where the subset
\mathcal{T}
is a finite disjoint union of intervals. There, the
A_{p},B_{p}
will be basically the endpoints of these intervals, so it is not surprising that the expression presented above depends only on the sizes of the intervals and their relative position and is therefore invariant by a shift.

h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})=h(A_{1}+s,A_{2}+s,\dots,B_{r}+s)\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}

(2.8)

This can be easily seen after the following manipulation

\begin{split}h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})&=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\\
&=\sum_{p,q}\begin{pmatrix}\sin A_{p}\sin A_{q}+\sin B_{p}\sin B_{q}\\
+\cos A_{p}\cos A_{q}+\cos B_{p}\cos B_{q}\end{pmatrix}-2\sum_{p,q}\begin{pmatrix}\sin B_{p}\sin A_{q}\\
+\cos B_{p}\cos A_{q}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\sum_{p,q}\cos(A_{p}-A_{q})+\cos(B_{p}-B_{q})-2\cos(A_{q}-B_{p})\end{split}

(2.9)

In particular, this shifting will be of importance since it will allow us to restrict our attention when looking for the maximum of
h
to a smaller subset of variables satisfying an additional constraint.

Now, suppose that
h
achieves its maximum value under the given constraint
\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=L
at a point
(A_{1}^{*},A_{2}^{*},\dots,B_{r}^{*})
. Also, it is clear that for all points we have

\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}=-\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos(B_{p}+\pi)-\cos(A_{p}+\pi)\right)

(2.10)

and since
\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos(B_{p}+s)-\cos(A_{p}+s)
is a continuous function on
s
, there exists
s^{*}\in[0,\pi)
such that

\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos(B_{p}^{*}+s^{*})-\cos(A_{p}^{*}+s^{*})=0

(2.11)

Therefore, for all points
(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})
where
\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=L
it holds

\begin{split}0\leq h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})&\leq h(A_{1}^{*},A_{2}^{*},\dots,B_{r}^{*})\\[8.0pt]
&=h(A_{1}^{*}+s^{*},A_{2}^{*}+s^{*},\dots,B_{r}^{*}+s^{*})\\
&=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin(B_{p}^{*}+s^{*})-\sin(A_{p}^{*}+s^{*})\right)^{2}+0\\
&\leq\max_{\mathcal{S}}\left\{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin(B_{p})+\sin(-A_{p})\right)^{2}\right\}\end{split}

(2.12)

where the set over which we are maximizing is

\mathcal{S}={\left\{-A_{1},-A_{2},\dots,B_{r}\,\Bigg|\,\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=L\right\}}

(2.13)

However, by
Lemma

2
it is immediate that the last expression in (
2.12
) is maximized when all variables
B_{p}
and
-A_{q}
are equal modulo
2\pi
except maybe one, say without loss of generality that it is
B_{r}
. Thus, we can write

\max_{\mathcal{S}}\Bigg\{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin(B_{p})+\sin(-A_{p})\right)^{2}\Bigg\}=\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin(B_{p})+\sin(-A_{p})\right)^{2}\Bigg\}

(2.14)

where the new set is

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}={\left\{-A_{1},-A_{2},\dots,B_{r}\,\Bigg|\,\begin{smallmatrix}\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=L\\
-A_{1}\equiv-A_{2}\equiv\dots\equiv B_{r-1}\pmod{2\pi}\end{smallmatrix}\right\}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}

(2.15)

But even more, combining (
2.12
) and (
2.14
) we have

\begin{split}\max_{\mathcal{S}}h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})&\leq\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin(B_{p})+\sin(-A_{p})\right)^{2}\Bigg\}\\
&\leq\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\Bigg\}\\
&=\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}}h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})\end{split}

(2.16)

where the inequality comes from adding a non-negative term to the expression.

Now, using again that
h
is invariant by a shift of the variables, we can assume that
A_{1}=0
. Then, taking into account
0=-A_{1}\equiv-A_{2}\equiv\dots\equiv B_{r-1}
we have
\sin A_{1}=\sin A_{2}=\dots=\sin B_{r-1}=0
, and also
\cos B_{p}=\cos(-A_{p})=\cos A_{p}
for all indices
p<r
. Therefore, continuing (
2.16
) we deduce

\begin{split}\max_{\mathcal{S}}h(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,B_{r})&\leq\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\{A_{1}=0\}}\Bigg\{\big(\sin B_{r}-\underbrace{\sin A_{r}}_{=0}\big)^{2}+\big(\cos B_{r}-\cos A_{r}\big)^{2}\Bigg\}\\
&=4\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\{A_{1}=0\}}\left\{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{B_{r}-A_{r}}{2}\right)\right\}\end{split}

(2.17)

Finally, using again the equivalences
0=-A_{1}\equiv-A_{2}\equiv\dots\equiv B_{r-1}\pmod{2\pi}
and the sum constraintwe have

L=\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=B_{r}-A_{r}-2\pi k\implies\frac{B_{r}-A_{r}}{2}=\pi k+L/2

(2.18)

for some integer
k
, and hence

\max_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\cap\{A_{1}=0\}}\left\{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{B_{r}-A_{r}}{2}\right)\right\}=\sin^{2}\left(\pi k+L/2\right)=\sin^{2}\left(L/2\right)

(2.19)

Therefore, combining (
2.17
) and (
2.19
) the conclusion is immediate

\max_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\leq 4\,\sin^{2}\left(L/2\right)

(2.20)

Notice that equality can be achieved for example at point
(A,A,\dots,A,A+L)
, where
h
evaluates to

\Big(\sin(A+L)-\sin A\Big)^{2}+\Big(\cos(A+L)-\cos A\Big)^{2}=4\sin^{2}(L/2)

(2.21)

In particular, these points of equality will correspond later, during the proof of the theorem, with the cases where
\mathcal{T}
is a single interval.

∎

3
Proof of the theorem

Now, we are ready to introduce the proof of the theorem, which will be divided in two parts. Firstly, showing that the statement for the case where the support of the function is
\mathcal{T}=\sqcup_{p=1}^{r}(a_{p},b_{p})
a disjoint union of intervals. Second, we will see that this implies the result in the case of a general measurable subset, by using the regularity of the Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 1
.

Let
W
and
T
be real numbers such that
WT\leq 1
. Then, for a disjoint union of intervals
\mathcal{T}=\sqcup_{p=1}^{r}(a_{p},b_{p})
with total size
|\mathcal{T}|=\sum_{p=1}^{r}b_{p}-a_{p}=T
, and functions
f
whose Fourier transform has support
\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
, the following inequality is true

\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\left|g(t)\right|^{2}\,dt

(3.1)

where
g
is the function given by the inverse Fourier transform of
|\hat{f}|
. In particular, by denoting
\mathbb{I}=[-T/2,T/2]
, it holds
c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)
.

Proof.

Consider
\mathcal{T}=\sqcup_{p=1}^{r}(a_{p},b_{p})
a finite disjoint union of intervals with total size
T
. Now, by a straight-forward computation, we have that for
f
with
\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
it holds

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{T}}&|f|^{2}dt=\sum_{p=1}^{r}\int_{a_{p}}^{b_{p}}\left|f(t)\right|^{2}dt\\
&=\sum_{p=1}^{r}\int_{a_{p}}^{b_{p}}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}e^{2\pi i(\eta-\omega)t}d\eta d\omega dt\\
&=\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}}{\eta-\omega}\sum_{p=1}^{r}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(e^{2\pi i(\eta-\omega)b_{p}}-e^{2\pi i(\eta-\omega)a_{p}}\right)d\eta d\omega\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}}{|\eta-\omega|}\sum_{p=1}^{r}\begin{pmatrix}\hskip-10.00002pt\big[\sin(2\pi|\eta-\omega|b_{p})-\sin(2\pi|\eta-\omega|a_{p})\big]\quad\\
\quad-i\big[\cos(2\pi|\eta-\omega|b_{p})-\cos(2\pi|\eta-\omega|a_{p})\big]\end{pmatrix}d\eta d\omega\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{|\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}|}{|\eta-\omega|}\left[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}d\eta d\omega\\
\end{split}

(3.2)

where we used the triangular inequality and trigonometric identities. Also, we denote
B_{p}=2\pi|\eta-\omega|b_{p}
and
A_{p}=2\pi|\eta-\omega|a_{p}
to ease the notation, and we have the understanding that whenever
\eta=\omega
, the terms evaluate to the fix value

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|\eta-\omega|}\left[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}=\\
=\left[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}2\pi b_{p}-2\pi a_{p}\right)^{2}+0\right]^{1/2}=2\pi T\end{split}

(3.3)

which only depends on the size of
\mathcal{T}
.

Now, by the previous observation when
\eta=\omega
, and using
Lemma

3
for the case when
\eta\neq\omega
, we have that under the constraint given by the total size of the intervals
\sum_{p=1}^{r}B_{p}-A_{p}=2\pi|\eta-\omega|\sum_{p=1}^{r}b_{p}-a_{p}=2\pi|\eta-\omega|T
, it holds that

\frac{1}{|\eta-\omega|}\left[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\frac{2}{|\eta-\omega|}\Big|\sin\left(\pi|\eta-\omega|T\right)\Big|

(3.4)

So substituting in (
3.2
) we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f|^{2}dw&\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{|\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}|}{|\eta-\omega|}\Big|\sin\left(\pi|\eta-\omega|T\right)\Big|d\eta d\omega\\
\end{split}

(3.5)

Now, by hypothesis
WT\leq 1
, so it holds that

\pi|\eta-\omega|T\leq\pi WT\leq\pi

(3.6)

Thus, we can ignore the absolute value around sin, and we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f|^{2}dt&\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{|\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}|}{|\eta-\omega|}\sin\left(\pi|\eta-\omega|T\right)d\eta d\omega\\
&=\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}|\hat{f}(\omega)\overline{\hat{f}(\eta)}|\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}e^{2\pi i(\eta-\omega)t}dtd\eta d\omega\\
&=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}|\hat{f}(\omega)|e^{-2\pi i\omega t}\overline{|\hat{f}(\eta)|e^{-2\pi i\eta t}}d\eta d\omega dt\\
&=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|g|^{2}dt\end{split}

(3.7)

where we define
g
to be the function given by the inverse Fourier transform of
|\hat{f}|
, and
\hat{f}
is itself the Fourier transform of
f
.

It is clear that
\supp\widehat{g}=\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
, and also that
||g||=||f||
since by Plancherel

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f|^{2}dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}|^{2}dw=\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}|\hat{f}|^{2}dw=\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}|\widehat{g}|^{2}dw=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{g}|^{2}dw=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g|^{2}dt

(3.8)

so we get the desired conclusion and
c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)
, where
\mathbb{I}=[-T/2,T/2]
.
∎

Now, we extend this result to any measurable subset.

Theorem 2
.

Let
W
and
T
be real numbers such that
WT\leq 1
. Then, for all measurable subsets
\mathcal{T}
of the real numbers with size
|\mathcal{T}|=T
, and functions
f
whose Fourier transform has support
\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
,the following inequality is true

\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\left|g(t)\right|^{2}dt

(3.9)

where
g
is the function given by the inverse Fourier transform of
|\hat{f}|
. In particular, by denoting
\mathbb{I}=[-T/2,T/2]
, it holds
c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a measurable subset of the real numbers with measure
|\mathcal{T}|=T
, and
f
a function whose Fourier transform has limited support
\supp\hat{f}=[-W/2,W/2]
and for which the statement does not hold. Multiplying
f
by a scalar will not affect the concentration, so assume without loss of generality that
\|f\|=1
.

Therefore, we suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt>\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt=c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)

(3.10)

where
g
is the function given by the inverse Fourier transform of
|\hat{f}|
.

Next, it is well-known (see, for example, Theorem 3.4 in (Stein and Shakarchi, 2009)
[
13
]
) that for a measurable set
\mathcal{T}
with finite measure, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exist finitely many disjoint finite intervals
\mathbb{J}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{J}_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
|\mathcal{T}\Delta\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}\mathbb{J}_{k}|<\varepsilon
. Here,
\Delta
refers to the symmetric difference of two sets,
A\Delta B\coloneqq(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.

Now, construct a sequence of measurable subsets which can be expressed as a finite disjoint union of finite intervals
\{\mathcal{T}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, and such that

|\mathcal{T}_{n}\Delta\mathcal{T}|\leq\frac{1}{n}

(3.11)

Then, if we define the interval
\mathbb{I}_{n}=(-|\mathcal{T}_{n}|/2,|\mathcal{T}_{n}|/2)
we know by
Theorem

1
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}|f(t))|^{2}\,dt\leq\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}_{n}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt

(3.12)

Also, it is clear that

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\overset{n}{\longrightarrow}\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\\[14.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}_{n}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt\overset{n}{\longrightarrow}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt\end{cases}

(3.13)

Therefore, by Fatou’s lemma and the inequality in (
3.12
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\liminf\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}|f(t)|^{2}\,dt\leq\liminf\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}_{n}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{I}}|g(t)|^{2}\,dt

(3.14)

This clearly contradicts our assumption (
3.10
), as we wanted to show.

Lastly, again since the norm of functions
f
and
g
is the same, we conclude
c_{\mathcal{T}}(f)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(g)
.

∎

4
Discrete version, improving Montgomery’s result

Finally, we introduce a discrete version of the problem, which is solved using the same inequalities, and which also requires a similar additional bound.

Let us consider polynomials of degree
n\geq 1
and complex coefficients, that is
P\in\mathbb{C}_{n}[z]
. Moreover, we will restrict our attention to measurable subsets of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
, which we will represent by their arguments as a complex number.

To follow the notation of the previous work, for a measurable
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
and
P\in\mathbb{C}_{n}[z]
, let us denote

c_{\Omega}(P)=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)}{\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)}

(4.1)

Where
\dm(z)
is the Lebesgue measure on the unit circle, normalized to
2\pi
. In particular, the measure of a measurable subset
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, which we may write as
\Omega=\{e^{i\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
, is given by

|\Omega|=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dm(z)=\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}d\theta

(4.2)

Also, we will consider the norm of the polynomial
P(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}
to be

\|P\|=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta=2\pi\left(|a_{0}|^{2}+|a_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|a_{n}|^{2}\right)

(4.3)

Then, the analogous to Conjecture 1 in this case is the following.

Conjecture 2
.

Fix
n\geq 1
an integer and
\delta>0
. Then, among all measurable subsets
\Omega
of the complex unit circle with measure
|\Omega|=2\delta
, the maximum of the concentration operator is attained on an interval
\mathbb{I}
of this same length. That is,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\in\mathbb{C}_{n}[z]\\
|\Omega|=2\delta\end{subarray}}c_{\Omega}(P)=\sup_{P\in\mathbb{C}_{n}[z]}c_{\mathbb{I}}(P)

(4.4)

And using an analogous approach, we will be able to prove a positive result once the additional hypothesis that
n\delta\leq\pi
is added, which will serve a similar purpose as
WT\leq 1
required in the continuous version.

In some sense, we can relate the size of the subset
|\Omega|=2\delta
with
T
the size of the support in the time domain of
f
, and the degree
n
of
P
with
W
the size of the frequency domain of
\hat{f}
.

Lastly, notice that the position of the interval in the unit circle is not relevant, since for a given
P(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+a_{2}z^{2}+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}
and
\mathbb{I}=(-\delta,\delta)
, we can take
Q(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}e^{-i\theta}z+a_{2}e^{-i2\theta}z^{2}+\dots+a_{n}e^{-in\theta}z^{n}
and
\mathbb{J}=(\theta-\delta,\theta+\delta)
and it holds that
c_{\mathbb{I}}(P)=c_{\mathbb{J}}(Q)
. Therefore, we will be considering
\mathbb{I}=(-\delta,\delta)
.

In particular, the positive result we prove is the following.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Omega
be a measurable subset of the complex unit circle, and let
P(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}
be any polynomial of degree
n\geq 1
.

Denote
|\Omega|=2\delta
and suppose it holds that
n\delta\leq\pi
. Then, taking the interval
\mathbb{I}=\left(-\delta,\delta\right)
and the polynomial
Q(z)=|a_{0}|+|a_{1}|z+\dots+|a_{n}|z^{n}
, the following inequality is true

\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\leq\int_{\mathbb{I}}|Q(z)|^{2}\dm(z)

(4.5)

In particular,
c_{\Omega}(P)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(Q)
.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the original continuous problem. Again, we must work out first the case where the subset is a finite disjoint union of intervals, and later extend it to any measurable subset of the unit circle using the regularity of the Lebesgue measure.

Therefore, we will only include here the relevant details of the first part. First, by a straight-forward computation we have

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)&=\int_{\bigsqcup\limits_{p=1}^{r}(\alpha_{p},\beta_{p})}|P(e^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta\\
&=2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}a_{l}\overline{a_{m}}\,\sum_{p=1}^{r}e^{i(l-m)\frac{\beta_{p}+\alpha_{p}}{2}}\,\frac{\sin\left((l-m)\frac{\beta_{p}-\alpha_{p}}{2}\right)}{l-m}\\
&=2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{a_{l}\overline{a_{m}}}{l-m}\,\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin\left((l-m)\frac{\beta_{p}-\alpha_{p}}{2}\right)\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt\cdot\left[\cos\left((l-m)\frac{\beta_{p}+\alpha_{p}}{2}\right)+i\sin\left((l-m)\frac{\beta_{p}+\alpha_{p}}{2}\right)\right]\\
&=\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{a_{l}\overline{a_{m}}}{|l-m|}\,\Bigg[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin\left(|l-m|\beta_{p}\right)-\sin\left(|l-m|\alpha_{p}\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt-i\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos\left(|l-m|\beta_{p}\right)-\cos\left(|l-m|\alpha_{p}\right)\right)\Bigg]\\
&\leq\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{|a_{l}||a_{m}|}{|l-m|}\,\Bigg[\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\sin B_{p}-\sin A_{p}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}\cos B_{p}-\cos A_{p}\right)^{2}\Bigg]^{1/2}\end{split}

(4.6)

Now, by
Lemma

3
and using the fact that
0\leq|l-m|\delta\leq n\delta\leq\pi
by hypothesis, it holds

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)&\leq 2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{|a_{l}||a_{m}|}{|l-m|}\left|\sin\left(|l-m|\delta\right)\right|\\
&=2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{|a_{l}||a_{m}|}{|l-m|}\,\sin\left(|l-m|\delta\right)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{I}}|Q(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\end{split}

(4.7)

where
\mathbb{I}=(-\delta,\delta)
is a single interval of size
2\delta=|\Omega|
, and
Q(z)=|a_{0}|+|a_{1}|z+\dots+|a_{n}|z^{n}
is the polynomial whose coefficients are the norms of the coefficients of
P
.

Notice that the norm of the new polynomial is the same as the norm of the original one,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|P(e^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta=2\pi\left(|a_{0}|^{2}+|a_{1}|^{2}+\dots+|a_{n}|^{2}\right)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}|Q(e^{i\theta})|^{2}d\theta

(4.8)

and therefore
c_{\Omega}(P)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(Q)
.

∎

Now, we will use this to also improve an inequality result by Montgomery, when adding the hypothesis
n\delta\leq\pi
. The result in question appears in
[
10
]
, where he presents a similar inequality to what we have obtained but with an extra factor, and which only applies (in our context) to symmetric polynomials of even degree. Our improvement is then reducing this factor from
20
to
1
, which is actually the best possible, and extending it to any polynomial when the condition
n\delta\leq\pi
is added.

We might directly state his result in our context, by rather taking
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
and the functions
\varphi_{k}=\cos kx
. Nonetheless, it should be mentioned that Montgomery’s result applies in a more general setup for sets of functions
\{\varphi_{k}\}
which are uniformly bounded and satisfy a Bessels’ type inequality.

Theorem

(Thm. 1,
[
10
]
)
.

Let
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\cos kx
, and define

f^{**}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{*}\cos kx

(4.9)

where the
a_{k}^{*}
are the numbers
|a_{k}|
, permuted so that
\{a_{k}^{*}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a decreasing sequence. Then for any measurable set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, with measure
|\Omega|=2\delta
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|f|^{2}\leq 20\displaystyle\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|f^{**}|^{2}

(4.10)

Where this
f^{**}
rearrangement can be understood as a discrete version of the symmetric rearrangement
f^{*}
presented in the introduction.

Notice, however, that for
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}\cos kx
it holds that

f(x)=a_{0}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=-n\\
k\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{a_{|k|}}{2}e^{ikx}=e^{-inx}\left(a_{0}e^{inx}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{2n}\frac{a_{|k-n|}}{2}e^{ikx}\right)

(4.11)

so it is natural to consider the polynomial

P(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{n}+a_{n-1}z^{1}+\dots+a_{1}z^{n-1}+2a_{0}z^{n}+a_{1}z^{n+1}+\dots+a_{n-1}z^{2n-1}+a_{n}z^{2n}\right)

(4.12)

and we have that

|f(x)|^{2}=\Big|a_{0}e^{inx}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\neq n\end{subarray}}^{2n}\frac{a_{|k-n|}}{2}e^{ikx}\Big|^{2}=|P(e^{ix})|^{2}

(4.13)

Therefore, Montgomery’s result states in our context the following.

Theorem
.

Let
P(z)=b_{n}+b_{n-1}z+\dots+2b_{0}z^{n}+\dots+b_{n-1}z^{2n-1}+b_{n}z^{2n}
be a symmetric polynomial of even degree
2n\geq 2
, and define

P^{*}(z)=b_{n}^{*}+b_{n-1}^{*}z+\dots+2b_{0}^{*}z^{n}+\dots+b_{n-1}^{*}z^{2n-1}+b_{n}^{*}z^{2n}

where the
b_{k}^{*}
are the numbers
|b_{k}|
, permuted so that
\{b_{k}^{*}\}_{k=0}^{n}
is a decreasing sequence. Then for any measurable set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
, with measure
|\Omega|=2\delta
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\leq 20\displaystyle\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P^{*}(z)|^{2}\dm(z)

(4.14)

To the best of our knowledge, it is not known whether the result still holds when reducing the factor
20
to
1
without having any additional hypothesis. For example, this has been proven to be true when the integral is over the whole unit circle, in (Gabriel, 1932)
[
5
]
.

Theorem

(Thm. 4,
[
5
]
)
.

Given an integer
k\geq 1
, and the functions

A(\theta)=\displaystyle\sum_{r=-R}^{R}a_{r}e^{ir\theta},\qquad A^{*}(\theta)=\displaystyle\sum_{r=-R}^{R}a_{r}^{+}e^{ir\theta}

(4.15)

where the
a_{r}^{+}
are the numbers
|a_{r}|
ordered such that
a_{0}^{+}\geq a_{-1}^{+}\geq a_{1}^{+}\geq a_{-2}^{+}\geq a_{2}^{+}\geq\dots
, then

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|A(\theta)|^{2k}\text{d}\theta\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}|A^{*}(\theta)|^{2k}\text{d}\theta

(4.16)

It should be mentioned that this is actually an improvement on the same result appearing in (Hardy and Littlewood, 1948)
[
6
]
, but which had some additional symmetry hypothesis on the coefficients.

Back to our work, the statement that we will prove is then

Theorem 4
.

Let
P
be any polynomial of degree
n\geq 1
. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be a measurable set, with measure
|\Omega|=2\delta
. Suppose it holds that
n\delta\leq\pi
, then

\int_{\Omega}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\leq\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P^{*}(z)|^{2}\dm(z)

(4.17)

In particular, since they have the same norm, we have
c_{\Omega}(P)\leq c_{\mathbb{I}}(P^{*})
where
\mathbb{I}=(-\delta,\delta)
.

Here, the rearrangement of the coefficients in
P^{*}
will be described in the conclusion of the next
Lemma

4
. For example, the rearrangement can be taken as

\begin{cases}a_{\lceil n/2\rceil}^{*}\geq a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}^{*}\geq a_{\lceil n/2\rceil+1}^{*}\geq a_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1}^{*}\geq\dots\quad\hfill n\textup{ odd}\\
a_{n/2}^{*}\geq a_{n/2+1}^{*}\geq a_{n/2-1}^{*}\geq\dots\quad\hfill n\text{ even}\end{cases}

(4.18)

Informally: the largest coefficient is the central one, then the one to the right of the central one, then the one to the left of the central one, then the second to the right of the central one… and so on.

As for the proof of the theorem, we have already done most of the work in
Theorem

2
, and we only have left proving that rearranging the (real positive) coefficients of a polynomial increases the integral of its norm squared over the symmetric interval. The main lemma, which will give the explicit rearrangement needed in our case, is the following
2
2

2

It should be noted that this lemma is the discrete version of
Lemma

1
, which is rather the natural evolution for continuous functions. Therefore, the final proof for our improvement on the result by Montgomery uses a similar step as the proof given by Donoho and Stark in their continuous result.
.

Lemma 4

(Thm. 1,
[
7
]
)
.

Consider the form

S(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}s_{l-m}x_{l}y_{m}

Suppose that the coefficients being given satisfy
s_{0}\geq s_{1}\geq\dots\geq 0
and also
s_{\nu}=s_{-\nu}
, and the variables satisfy
x_{l}\geq 0
,
y_{m}\geq 0
, being given in every respect except arrangement. Then among the arrangements for which
S
assumes its maximum value there is one in which

(i)

x_{\mu}\leq x_{\mu^{\prime}}\,
if
\,|\mu^{\prime}-n/2|<|\mu-n/2|

(ii)

no two of
x_{\mu}-x_{\mu^{\prime}}
, where
\mu<\mu^{\prime},|\mu^{\prime}-n/2|=|\mu-n/2|
, have different signs.

and analogous conditions for variables
y_{\nu}
.

We are now ready to give the proof of our result.

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

As mentioned, we only have left to prove that for
P(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\dots+a_{n}z^{n}
with positive real coefficients, it holds that

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\leq\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P^{*}(z)|^{2}\dm(z)

(4.19)

where
P^{*}(z)=a_{0}^{*}+a_{1}^{*}z+\dots+a_{n}^{*}z^{n}
is the polynomial with coefficients rearranged as stated for variables
x_{l},y_{m}
in
Lemma

4
.
Now, by a simple computation

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)=2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{a_{l}\overline{a_{m}}}{l-m}\sin\left((l-m)\delta\right)

(4.20)

We might now take
x_{l}=y_{l}=a_{l}
the (positive) coefficients of the polynomial, and the variables
s_{\nu}=2\dfrac{\sin(\nu\delta)}{\nu}
with the understanding that
s_{0}=2\delta
.

The symmetry of the variables
s_{\nu}
is clear, and the hypothesis
n\delta\leq\pi
guarantees that they are positive and decreasing, so that we might apply
Lemma

4
. Hence, we deduce that

\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P(z)|^{2}\dm(z)\leq 2\sum_{l,m=0}^{n}\frac{a_{l}^{*}\overline{a_{m}^{*}}}{l-m}\sin\left((l-m)\delta\right)=\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}|P^{*}(z)|^{2}\dm(z)

(4.21)

Combining this inequality with
Theorem

2
completes the proof.
∎
