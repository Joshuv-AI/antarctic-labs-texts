Proper losses regret at least 1 / 2 1/2 -order
Han Bao bao.han@ism.ac.jp, Asuka Takatsu asuka-takatsu@g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp

Abstract
A fundamental challenge in machine learning is the choice of a loss as it characterizes our learning task, is minimized in the training phase, and serves as an evaluation criterion for estimators. Proper losses are commonly chosen, ensuring minimizers of the full risk match the true probability vector. Estimators induced from a proper loss are widely used to construct forecasters for downstream tasks such as classification and ranking. In this procedure, how does the forecaster based on the obtained estimator perform well under a given downstream task? This question is substantially relevant to the behavior of the p p -norm between true probability and estimated vectors when the estimator is updated. In the proper loss framework, the suboptimality of the estimated probability vector from the true probability vector is measured by a surrogate regret. First, we analyze a surrogate regret and show that the strict properness of a loss is necessary and sufficient to establish a non-vacuous surrogate regret bound. Second, we tackle an important open question that the order of convergence in p p -norm cannot be faster than the 1 / 2 1/2 -order of surrogate regrets for a broad class of strictly proper losses. This implies that strongly proper losses asymptotically achieve the optimal convergence rate.

Introduction

Proper losses

Intuitively, we say a loss is

Proper losses are prevailing in modern machine learning: for example, the cross-entropy loss popular in deep learning essentially corresponds to the log loss (or logarithmic score), and the Brier score is used for assessing model uncertainties
[
61
]

[

As such, probabilistic estimators are obtained via proper loss minimization.
It is common to post-process a minimizer of a proper loss for downstream tasks,
such as classification (by choosing the most likely label), ranking (by giving ranking scores to each label

[

[

Here, we are interested in the predictive performance of post-processed estimators in downstream tasks.
Given the true and estimated probability vectors

Can we relate the suboptimality of a forecaster for a downstream task to the surrogate regret?

Surrogate regret bounds relate the surrogate regret to the performance for downstream tasks, and have been derived for binary classification

Recently, a unified surrogate regret bound across different downstream tasks has been established

This is based on the observation that the suboptimality of the aforementioned downstream tasks can be controlled by the

However, the derived bound has been limited to the binary classification case, and it remains unclear when the surrogate regret bound is non-vacuous.
A reasonable loss should entail a non-vacuous regret bound, which is crucial to tackling numerous downstream tasks simultaneously.
Moreover, an important conjecture that the convergence rate of surrogate regret bounds cannot be faster than the
1/2
-order has yet to be solved.
This conjecture has a significant role in the choice of losses because the lower bound of the order of convergence contributes to delineating the optimality of a given proper loss.

In this article, we aim to study when the surrogate regret bounds are non-vacuous and how fast the order of convergence in terms of the

2.2
Convex analysis

In this subsection, let

(2)

\bm{\xi}^{0}

Let us denote an arbitrary

Classification, proper losses, and Savage representation

After introducing the learning problem of multiclass classification, we discuss loss functions and their properties.
We review the notion of proper losses and its connection to Bregman divergences.
Although this connection is already known, we formalize it rigorously.
In particular, we verify the existence of minimizers of the conditional risk and its measurable selection (
Lemma

1

3.1
Multiclass classification

We regard a Radon space
\mathcal{X}
as an input space, that is, the set of possible observations, and
\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq[N]
as a set of labels.
The set
\triangle^{N}
is identified with the space of all probability measures on
\mathcal{Y}
.
We fix a probability measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and denote by
\nu_{\mathcal{X}}
the marginal of
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
,
that is,
\nu(\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{Y})=\nu_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathcal{B})
holds for any measurable set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.
Then, by the disintegration theorem
[
29
, Chapter III-70 and 72]
,
there exists a Borel map
\mathbf{q}:\mathcal{X}\to\triangle^{N}
, uniquely defined
\nu_{\mathcal{X}}
-a.e.,
such that

\nu(\mathcal{B}\times\left\{{y}\right\})=\int_{\mathcal{B}}q_{y}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\nu_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for a measurable set }\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\text{ and }y\in\mathcal{Y}.

We deem
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x})\in\triangle^{N}
a true probability vector at the input
\mathbf{x}
induced from
\nu
.

Multiclass classification
is a task to learn a forecaster
y:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
to predict the most likely label

y(\mathbf{x})\in\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{y}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{for each $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}$.}

3.2
Proper losses

We continue to use the notation in the previous subsection.
To elicit the true probability map
\mathbf{q}:\mathcal{X}\to\triangle^{N}

\bm{\ell}

\triangle^{N}
to
[0,\infty]^{N}

Define the associated

\mathbb{L}[\widehat{\mathbf{q}}]\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}\ell_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\nu(\mathbf{x},y)\quad\text{for a Borel map~}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}:\mathcal{X}\to\triangle^{N}.

A minimizer of

\mathbf{q}:\mathcal{X}\to\triangle^{N}

The choice of
\bm{\ell}
directly affects the quality of an estimator.
It is more intuitive to work on the conditional counterpart of the full risk instead.
Let
\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
with slight abuse of notation.
For a loss
\bm{\ell}
, the associated

of
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}

and

L(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\coloneqq\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})}\right\rangle=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{y}\ell_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})\coloneqq\inf_{\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}}L(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

respectively.
Here, we regard
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
as a

and
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
as an

The full risk is rewritten as

\mathbb{L}[\widehat{\mathbf{q}}]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}L(\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}),\widehat{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\nu_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{x}).

Since the infimum of a family of linear functions is concave,
\underline{L}
is concave on
\triangle^{N}

Thus, the minimization problem of the full risk is reduced to that of the conditional risk
if the map

Note that

has a Borel selector for continuous

.

For a different type of (margin-based) losses, the existence of a measurable full risk minimizer has been studied

\bm{\ell}
is proper.
Then,
\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
by

f(\bm{\xi})\coloneqq\begin{cases}-\underline{L}(\bm{\xi})&\text{if }\bm{\xi}\in\triangle^{N},\\
\infty&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(5)

then
f
is a proper convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\domain f\subseteq\triangle^{N}
.
For
\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
, we have

f(\mathbf{q})=-L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})\geq-L(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=-L(\widehat{\mathbf{q}},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})+\left\langle{-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})},{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle=f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})+\left\langle{-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})},{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle,

(6)

where the inequality is thanks to the properness of
\bm{\ell}
.
Thus,
-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\in\partial f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
and
Eq.

4
hold for any
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
.

Conversely, suppose that there is a proper convex function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\domain f=\triangle^{N}

and, for all
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
, there exists
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\in\partial{f}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
satisfying
Eq.

4
.
Then, for
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
, we have
L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})=-f(\mathbf{q})
and

L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})=-f(\mathbf{q})\leq-f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})-\left\langle{\widehat{\mathbf{v}}},{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle=L(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\quad\text{for }\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N},

in turn,
\bm{\ell}
is proper.

Next, we show the equivalence of the strict properness of
\bm{\ell}
and the strict convexity of
f
on
\triangle^{N}
.
Let
f
be a convex function on
\triangle^{N}
such that (
4
) holds.
On the one hand, if
\bm{\ell}
is strictly proper, then
we have

\displaystyle f((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=L((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime},(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(1-t)L(\mathbf{q},(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})+tL(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle>(1-t)L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})+tL(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(1-t)f(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})+tf(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}),

for
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\triangle^{N}
and
t\in(0,1)
, proving the strict convexity of
f
on
\triangle^{N}
.
On the other hand, if
\bm{\ell}
is not strictly proper, then there exist distinct
\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\in\triangle^{N}
such that

f(\mathbf{q})=-L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})=-L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})+\left\langle{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\right\rangle,

where
\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\in\partial f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
satisfies
Eq.

4
such that
L(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=-f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})-\left\langle{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.
For any
t\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle-L((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime},(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)f(\mathbf{q})+tf(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})+(1-t)\left\langle{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}},{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})+\left\langle{\mathbf{v}^{\prime}},{(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-L((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime},(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime}).

This yields
f((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=(1-t)f(\mathbf{q})+f(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})
, that is,
f
is not strictly convex on
\triangle^{N}
.

This completes the proof of the proposition.

∎

As a by-product of the proof of
Proposition

4
,
we obtain the following property thanks to the construction (
5
).

Corollary 5

(Subgradient of conditional Bayes risk)
.

For a regular proper loss
\bm{\ell}
,
define a proper convex function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by (
5
).
Then, we have
-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\in\partial{f}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
for
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
.
In addition,
-\bm{\ell}+\gamma\mathbf{1}
remains to be a selector of
\partial f
for any
\gamma:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\bm{\ell}
be regular and proper.
Then we observe from inequalities (
3
) and (
6
) that

R=B_{(f,\nabla f)}=B_{(f,-\bm{\ell}+\gamma\mathbf{1})}

(7)

for a function on
\gamma
on
\triangle^{N}
.
This gives a closed form of a subgradient of
-\underline{L}
and is of interest per se.
Nevertheless, when one generates a proper loss from a proper convex function
f
,
it is more standard via the Savage representation (
4
) by
\ell_{y}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=L(\mathbf{e}_{y},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
, where
\mathbf{e}_{y}=[\delta_{1y}\;\cdots\;\delta_{Ny}]^{\top}
is the standard basis encoding the label
y\in[N]
.

Initial examples.

We quickly see some examples of multiclass proper losses to let readers familiarize with the definitions so far, and discuss more examples in
Section

6
.

The first example is the log loss,
\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=-\ln q_{y}
for
y\in[N]
.
This possibly takes
\infty
for
q_{y}=0
, for which we need the regularity (see
Definition

3
).
The log loss corresponds to the Kullback–Leibler divergence and Shannon entropy

R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=D_{\text{KL}}(\mathbf{q}\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\coloneqq\sum_{y\in[N]}q_{y}\ln\left(\frac{q_{y}}{\widehat{q}_{y}}\right),\quad\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=-\sum_{y\in[N]}q_{y}\ln q_{y}

as the regret and the conditional Bayes risk, respectively.
Here, we have
\nabla\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{q})-\mathbf{1}
, which is equivalent to
\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{q})\in\partial\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})
(in
Corollary

5
; and
Section

2.2
for equivalent subgradients).
Since each
\ell_{y}
depends solely on
q_{y}
, this type of loss functions is called
local
.
Indeed, the log loss is the only local proper loss
[
63
]
.
The locality is considered to be a desirable property in terms of interpretability
[
31
]
.

The second example is the Brier score
[
17
]
:

\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y^{\prime}\in[N]}(\delta_{yy^{\prime}}-q_{y^{\prime}})^{2}=-q_{y}+\frac{1+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}.

This is no longer local as
\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})
depends on
q_{y^{\prime}}
for
y^{\prime}\neq y
.
Interestingly, the lack of the locality has been reportedly relevant to the emergent ability of language models
[
32
]
.
The Brier score is strictly proper associated with the following regret and conditional Bayes risk

R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1-\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}^{2}}{2},

which are the squared
2
-norm distance and negative squared
2
-norm, respectively.
Here, we have
\nabla\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=-\mathbf{q}
, which is equivalent to
\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{q})\in\partial\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})

because
\bm{\ell}(\mathbf{q})=-\mathbf{q}+\gamma(\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{1}
with the choice
\gamma(\mathbf{q})\coloneqq(1+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{2}^{2})/2
.
Note that
\nabla\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})=-\mathbf{q}
is not proper when regarded as a loss function
[
39
, §4.1]
; for this reason, we must always interpret the formula
\bm{\ell}\in\partial\underline{L}
in
Corollary

5
under the subgradient equivalence.

3.4
Strongly proper losses

For
\kappa>0
, a loss
\bm{\ell}
is called
\kappa
-strongly proper
if

R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=L(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})-\underline{L}(\mathbf{q})\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{for $\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}$.}

(8)

Strongly proper losses have been introduced for
N=2

[
2
]
and for general
N\geq 3

[
84
]
to derive a surrogate regret bound in the form of (
1
).
For example, the log loss is
1
-strongly proper
[
84
, Lemma 3]
.
For
N=2
,
\bm{\ell}
is regular and strongly proper if and only if its conditional Bayes risk
-\underline{L}
is strongly convex (with respect to the
2
-norm)
[
2
, Theorem 10]
.
As an immediate consequence of the inequality (
8
), we have the
1/2
-order surrogate regret bounds for strongly proper losses:

\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\kappa}R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})}\qquad\text{for any $\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}$.}

(9)

Several binary losses are shown to be strongly proper
[
2
, Table 1]
.
Beyond binary losses, a similar bound to (
9
) has been known for
Fenchel–Young losses

[
15
]
(which is relevant to proper losses but defined over “dual” points of
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
),
but requires a restrictive condition on
-\underline{L}
, Legendre-type.
See
[
14
, Lemma 3]
and
[
70
, footnote 8]
for details.
In the next section, we derive surrogate regret bounds for general multiclass proper losses.

4
Regret bounds: Necessity of strict properness

In this section, we first study the moduli of convexity in
Section

4.1
.
Therein, we show that the strict convexity of a function is equivalent to the strict monotonicity of its modulus (
Theorem

8
), which ensures that its surrogate regret bound is non-vacuous.
Then, we show in
Section

4.2
the surrogate regret bounds for general multiclass proper losses beyond strongly proper losses.
In
Section

4.3
, we relate surrogate regret bounds to several downstream tasks.
Readers who are interested in the benefits of surrogate regret bounds may refer to this section first.

4.1
Moduli of convexity

Before introducing the moduli of convexity, we study
the
midpoint Jensen gap
of a convex function
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is defined by

J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})\coloneqq\frac{f(\mathbf{q})+f(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})}{2}-f\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)\quad\text{for $\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}$.}

The midpoint Jensen gap is nonnegative by the convexity of
f
on
\triangle^{N}
.
The midpoint Jensen gap is invariant under adding an affine function, and so is the modulus of convexity (defined later).
That is, the midpoint Jensen gaps of two convex functions
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f_{\lambda,\mathbf{u}}:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{\lambda,\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{q})\coloneqq f(\mathbf{q})+\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle+\lambda\quad\text{for $\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}$}

are the same, for any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, we will show that for continuous convex functions
f,g:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,
their midpoint Jensen gaps are the same if and only if
f-g
is affine.
This property is reminiscent of the condition for the payoff equivalence
[
48
, Theorem 3]

and the universal equivalence of surrogate losses
[
59
, Theorem 3]

[
28
, Theorem 1]
.
The proof is deferred to
Appendix

E
.

Proposition 6

(Uniqueness up to affine functions)
.

Let
f,g:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous convex functions.
Then, their midpoint Jensen gaps are the same if and only if
f-g
is affine.

We extend the moduli of convexity defined on
(\triangle^{2},\|\cdot\|_{1})

[
8
, Definition 4]
to
(\triangle^{N},\|\cdot\|_{p})
.
Note that the diameter of
(\triangle^{N},\|\cdot\|_{p})
is
2^{1/p}
.

Definition 7

(Modulus of convexity
[
11
]
)
.

For a convex function
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,
its
modulus of convexity
of
f
with respect to the
p
-norm is the function
\omega:[0,2^{1/p}]\to[0,\infty)
defined by

\omega(r)\coloneqq\inf\left\{{J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}\text{ with }\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\geq r}\right\}\quad\text{for }r\in[0,2^{1/p}].

While the notion of moduli of convexity dates back to the classical literature on optimization
[
64
]
and Banach spaces
[
35
]
, we view this as the smallest possible Jensen–Bregman divergence
[
56
]
with the fixed
p
-norm distance.
This idea is similar to variational problems for deriving tight Pinsker’s inequalities
[
77
]

[
34
]
.

We will show that the convexity and strict convexity of a function are translated to the monotonicity and strict monotonicity of its modulus, respectively.
This is an important result throughout this article because the moduli of convexity characterize surrogate regret bounds, as we will see in
Section

4.2
soon.

Theorem 8

(Monotonicity of modulus)
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function.
Then, the following assertions hold.

1.

The modulus
\omega
is non-decreasing on
[0,2^{1/p}]
and
\omega(0)=0
.

2.

f
is strictly convex on
\triangle^{N}
if and only if
\omega
is strictly monotone on
[0,2^{1/p}]
.

3.

f
is strongly convex on
\triangle^{N}
with respect to the
p
-norm if and only if there exists
\kappa>0
such that
\omega(r)\geq\kappa r^{2}
on
r\in[0,2^{1/p}]
.

From
Theorem

8
, the strong convexity of
f
is equivalent to the quadratic bound
\omega(r)\gtrsim r^{2}
.
Thus, the modulus of convexity quantifies the convexity of a function.

Before proving
Theorem

8
, we show a lemma that is repeatedly used in the rest of the article.
This lemma guarantees that the infimum of the modulus of convexity
\omega
is attainable, and the minimizer lies at the boundary of the constraint
\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\geq r
in its definition.
The proof is deferred to
Appendix

E
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function.
For
r\in[0,2^{1/p}]
, there exist
\mathbf{q}^{r},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{r}\in\triangle^{N}
such that
\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q}^{r},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{r})
and
\|\mathbf{q}^{r}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{r}\|_{p}=r
.

Proof of
Theorem

8

Define

\mathcal{D}^{N}(r)\coloneqq\left\{{(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\in\triangle^{N}\times\triangle^{N}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\geq r}\right\}.

For
r^{\prime},r\in[0,2^{1/p}]
with
r^{\prime}\leq r
, we observe from the monotonicity
\mathcal{D}^{N}(r)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{N}(r^{\prime})
that
\omega(r^{\prime})\leq\omega(r)
.
It is easily seen that
J(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q})=0
holds for any
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}

hence
\omega(0)=0
.
Thus, the first assertion follows.

To show the second assertion, assume the strict convexity of
f
on
\triangle^{N}
.
Let
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
.
By
Lemma

9
, there exist
\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
such that
\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})
and
\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}=r
.
Define a curve
c:[0,1]\to\triangle^{N}
by

c(t)\coloneqq(1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\quad\text{for $t\in[0,1]$.}

Since
f\circ c
is strictly convex on
[0,1]
, we have the strict inequality

\frac{f(c(\tau))-f(c(0))}{\tau}<\frac{f(c(1))-f(c(1-\tau))}{\tau}\quad\text{for $\tau\in(0,1/2]$.}

Consequently, we conclude

\omega((1-2\tau)r)\leq J(c(\tau),c(1-\tau))<J(c(0),c(1))=\omega(r)\quad\text{for $\tau\in(0,1/2]$,}

that is, the strict monotonicity of
\omega
on
[0,2^{1/p}]
.
Conversely, if
f
is not strictly convex on
\triangle^{N}
, there exist distinct
\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
such that

f((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})=(1-t)f(\mathbf{q})+tf(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})\quad\text{for }t\in[0,1].

This leads to
J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})=0
.
Consequently,
\omega
is not strictly increasing on
[0,\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}]
.

To show the third assertion, assume that
f
is
\widetilde{\kappa}
-strongly convex on
\triangle^{N}
with respect to the
p
-norm for some
\widetilde{\kappa}>0
.
By
Lemma

9
, there exist
\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
with
\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})
and
\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}=r
.
Then, the strong convexity of
f
with the choice
t=1/2
implies

\omega(r)=\frac{f(\mathbf{q})+f(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})}{2}-f\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)\geq\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}}{8}\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2}=\frac{\widetilde{\kappa}}{8}r^{2}.

Conversely, suppose that
\omega(r)\geq\kappa r^{2}
on
r\in[0,2^{1/p}]
.
This implies

J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{f(\mathbf{q})+f(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})}{2}-f\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)\geq\omega(\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p})\geq\kappa\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2}\quad\text{for any~$\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}$.}

(10)

Taking
t\in[1/2,1)
,
we have

\displaystyle f((1-t)\mathbf{q}+t\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=f\left((1-2t)\mathbf{q}+2t\,\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-2t)f(\mathbf{q})+2tf\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-2t)f(\mathbf{q})+2t\left[\frac{f(\mathbf{q})+f(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})}{2}-\kappa\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-t)f(\mathbf{q})+tf(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})-2\kappa t\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)f(\mathbf{q})+tf(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})-2\kappa t(1-t)\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2},

where we used (
10
).
Switching the roles of
\mathbf{q}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
yields the
4\kappa
-strongly convexity of
f
with respect to the
p
-norm.

All in all, the proof of the theorem is achieved.

∎

Despite the simple proof, this will lead to the necessity and sufficiency for a surrogate regret bound being non-vacuous in
Section

4.2
, together with
Theorem

10
.

4.2
Surrogate regret bounds

Now, we give surrogate regret bounds with respect to the
p
-norm.
The asymptotic behavior of a surrogate regret bound for a proper loss
\bm{\ell}
is essentially governed by the modulus of convexity of (the negative of) its conditional Bayes risk
-\underline{L}
.
This is an extension of surrogate regret bounds for binary classification
[
8
, Theorem 6]
to multiclass classification.
Its proof (shown below) is an immediate generalization from
[
8
]
.

Theorem 10

(Surrogate regret bounds)
.

Let
\bm{\ell}:\triangle^{N}\to[0,\infty]^{N}
be a regular proper loss
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
a proper convex function defined by (
5
) with its modulus of convexity
\omega
.
For
\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
, it holds

\omega(\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p})\leq\frac{1}{2}R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),

(11)

with the equality if
\mathbf{q}=\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
.
If
\bm{\ell}
is strictly proper additionally, then the equality of (
11
) holds if and only if
\mathbf{q}=\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
.

Proof

By the definition of
\omega
together with (
7
), it is sufficient to show

J(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\leq\frac{1}{2}B_{(f,-\bm{\ell})}(\mathbf{q}\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\quad\text{for $\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}$.}

By
Corollary

5
, we have

f\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}{2}\right)\geq f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})+\left\langle{-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})},{\frac{\mathbf{q}+\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}{2}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle=f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})},{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle,

which implies

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\left[f(\mathbf{q})-f(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})-\left\langle{-\bm{\ell}(\widehat{\mathbf{q}})},{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}}\right\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{2}B_{(f,-\bm{\ell})}(\mathbf{q}\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}).

The equality can be seen immediately by choosing
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{q}
.
If
\bm{\ell}
is strictly proper, then
\omega
is strictly monotone by
Theorem

8
and
f
is strictly convex, which indicates that the equality of (
11
) yields
\mathbf{q}=\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
.

∎

Let us discuss when a proper loss entails a
non-vacuous
bound, which means that
\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}
approaches zero whenever the surrogate regret
R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
goes to zero.
If
\bm{\ell}
is strictly proper, then
\omega
is strictly increasing, which in turn has an inverse function
\omega^{-1}
and leads (
11
) to

\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\leq\begin{cases}{\omega}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\right)&\text{if~~$\frac{1}{2}R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\leq\omega\Big(2^{\frac{1}{p}}\Big)$,}\\
2^{\frac{1}{p}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(12)

The strict monotonicity of
\omega^{-1}
is essential for non-vacuous bounds because we then have
\rho\downarrow 0
if and only if
\omega^{-1}(\rho)\downarrow 0
.
Otherwise, we cannot always expect that the estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
approaches
\mathbf{q}
even if the suboptimality
R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
vanishes.
By
Propositions

4
and
8
, the
strict
properness of
\bm{\ell}
is necessary and sufficient for the surrogate regret bound (
11
) being non-vacuous.
This is why strict properness matters.

If
\bm{\ell}
is strongly proper, the inequality (
8
) implies that the negative Bayes risk
-\underline{L}
is strongly convex.
Combining the
p
-norm bound (
12
) with
Theorem

8
, we recover the
1/2
-regret bound (
9
) modulo a constant.

Comparison: Pinsker’s inequality.

For illustration, let us consider the log loss under the binary case
N=2
, where we identify
[q\;1-q]\in\triangle^{2}
with
q\in[0,1]
.
Then, the generator function
f
defined by (
5
) is the negative binary Shannon entropy
f(q)=q\ln q+(1-q)\ln(1-q)
.
Its modulus of convexity (with
1
-norm) admits the following form:

\omega(r)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)\ln\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)\right].

Note that this form coincides with the calibration function of the logistic loss
[
74
, Table 1]
.
The
p
-norm bound gives

2^{\frac{1}{p}}|q-\widehat{q}|\leq\omega^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}D_{\text{KL}}(q\|\widehat{q})\right)\quad\text{for $q,\widehat{q}\in[0,1]$}.

(13)

Note
2^{1/p}|q-\widehat{q}|=\|[q\;1-q]^{\top}-[\widehat{q}\;1-\widehat{q}]^{\top}\|_{p}
.
Moreover, we can verify
\omega(r)\geq r^{2}/2
, which gives
|q-\widehat{q}|^{2}\lesssim D_{\text{KL}}(q\|\widehat{q})
, namely, Pinsker’s inequality.
The
p
-norm bound (
12
) can be viewed as generalizing Pinsker’s inequality by allowing other Bregman divergences in the upper bound and the
p
-norm distance in the lower bound.

Comparison: surrogate regret bounds for margin-based losses.

Margin-based classification
[
81
]
, where a learner acts on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued margin instead of a probabilistic estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
, is commonly used.
Let us consider binary classification based on the binary margin
z\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given a true probability
q\in[0,1]
(identified with
[q\;1-q]^{\top}\in\triangle^{2}
) and margin
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
the classification performance is evaluated by the (conditional) 0-1 regret

\text{Reg}_{01}(q,z)\coloneqq[q\mathds{1}_{\{z\leq 0\}}+(1-q)\mathds{1}_{\{z>0\}}]-\min\left\{{q,1-q}\right\},

where
q
and
1-q
indicates the class probabilities of
y=1
and
y=2
, respectively.

In the binary case
N=2
, we often use a (symmetric) margin-based losses
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
as a surrogate loss, which operates on the binary margin
z\in\mathbb{R}
:
the logistic loss
\phi_{\text{log}}(z)=\ln(1+\exp(-z))
and the hinge loss
\phi_{\text{hinge}}(z)=\max\left\{{0,1-z}\right\}
are common examples.
The prediction performances of the binary margin
z
for
y=1
and
y=2
are evaluated by
\phi(z)
and
\phi(-z)
, respectively.
For binary margin-based losses, surrogate regret bounds with respect to
\text{Reg}_{01}
have been studied intensively
[
13
, Theorem 3]
.
Here, we compare the 0-1 regret bounds and the
p
-norm regret bounds (
Theorem

10
).
Let us denote the conditional risk
of the binary margin
z
and Bayes risk given the true probability
q
by

L^{\text{mgn}}(q,z)\coloneqq q\phi(z)+(1-q)\phi(-z),\quad\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}(q)\coloneqq\inf_{z\in\mathbb{R}}L^{\text{mgn}}(q,z),

respectively, and define
\psi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
by

\psi(r)\coloneqq\inf\left\{{L^{\text{mgn}}\left(\frac{1+r}{2},z\right)}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{z\in\mathbb{R},z\leq 0}\right\}-\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}\left(\frac{1+r}{2}\right).

(14)

Then, for
(q,z)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
, we have

\psi(\text{Reg}_{01}(q,z))\leq L^{\text{mgn}}(q,z)-\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}(q).

(15)

The function
\psi
is called
\psi
-transform
, and later generalized as a calibration function
[
74
]
.
The
\psi
-transform and the modulus of convexity
\omega
characterize the 0-1 regret bound (
15
) and the
p
-norm bound (
11
), respectively, and are closely related with each other.
The modulus of convexity is defined (in
Definition

7
) by the best possible Jensen gap
J(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
such that the true probability
\mathbf{q}
and estimated probability
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
are distant at least
r
in the sense of the
p
-norm.
By contrast, the
\psi
-transform in (
14
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\psi(r)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{{L^{\text{mgn}}\left(\frac{1+r}{2},z\right)-\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}\left(\frac{1+r}{2}\right)}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{z\in\mathbb{R},z\leq 0}\right\}

(16)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{{L^{\text{mgn}}(q,z)-\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}(q)}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\text{Reg}_{01}(q,z)\geq r}\right\},

which is the best possible surrogate regret
L^{\text{mgn}}(q,z)-\underline{L}^{\text{mgn}}(q)
such that the margin prediction
z
is suboptimal with respect to the true class probability
q
by the level
r
at least.
Therefore, both
\psi
-transform and the modulus of convexity measures the best possible surrogate regret given a true probability and suboptimal prediction by the level
r
least.
Interested readers can refer to
[
74
, §4.1]
to find more details of (
16
).

Comparison: surrogate regret bounds for proper composite losses.

Let us leave a remark on the existing surrogate regret bounds for proper

A proper composite loss
[

]
are the composition of a proper loss

Our

4.3
Relating surrogate regret to downstream tasks

The upper bound for the
p
-norm (
12
) is useful for many scenarios to assess the predictive performance of plug-in forecasters, i.e., post-processed forecasters based on the estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
.
Thus, we can regard the
p
-norm bound as a
versatile
surrogate regret bound across different downstream tasks.
Subsequently, we provide several examples of downstream tasks to support this idea.

Task 1: multiclass classification.

Let us consider multiclass classification based on the post-process approach.
Given true and estimated probability vectors
\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
, respectively, the plug-in forecaster based on the estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
is given by
\widehat{y}\in\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\widehat{q}_{y}
, where the tie is broken arbitrarily.
Define
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
the 0-1 loss matrix by
L_{ij}\coloneqq 1-\delta_{ij}
for each
(i,j)\in[N]^{2}
, and
\mathbf{L}_{y}
denotes the
y
-th column vector of
\mathbf{L}
.
Here, the forecaster’s suboptimality in multiclass classification is measured by the (conditional)
0-1 regret

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{01}(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{n}(1-\delta_{n\widehat{y}})-\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{n}(1-\delta_{ny})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{n}\left(L_{n\widehat{y}}-\min_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}L_{ny}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{Y}}q_{n}\left(L_{n\widehat{y}}-L_{ny}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widehat{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle,

for
\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
.
Let
p^{\ast}
denote the Hölder conjugate of
p
.
The 0-1 regret can be bounded as

\mathrm{Reg}_{01}(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle{\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widehat{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\mathbf{L}_{\widehat{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}\|_{p^{\ast}}\leq 2^{1-\frac{1}{p}}\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p},

where the first inequality holds because
\left\langle{\widehat{\mathbf{q}}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widehat{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle\leq 0
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
attributed to the construction of
\widehat{y}
,
and the second inequality owes to Hölder’s inequality.
Eventually, the 0-1 regret is controlled by the surrogate regret
R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})
via (
12
) if
\bm{\ell}
is strictly proper,
which relates the estimation quality of
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
to the predictive performance of the post-processed forecaster via the
p
-norm.

In case of binary classification, a closely related surrogate regret bound was presented
[
53
, Lemma 4]
(but for class-imbalanced plug-in forecasters),
which has been later used to control the
1
-norm between the estimated and true class probabilities for F-measure optimization
[
45
]
.
Our result allows to generalize them to the multiclass case.

Note, however, that more direct control of the 0-1 regret is possible by

\Psi(\mathrm{Reg}_{01}(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}))\leq R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}),\qquad\text{where~~}\Psi(r)=\underline{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-\underline{L}\left(\frac{1}{2}+r\right),

which can be obtained via the second-order Taylor expansion of proper losses
[
68
, Corollary 27]
.
In this case, we may obtain non-vacuous bounds even for non-strictly proper losses.
Our
Theorem

10
does not provide such a tailored bound for the 0-1 regret but “one-size-fits-all” bounds for multiple tasks so that we can control the performance of other downstream tasks, not only classification.

Task 2: learning with noisy labels.

Let us consider multiclass classification with class-conditional label noises:
a true label
y
is observed as
\widetilde{y}
with probability
C_{y\widetilde{y}}
with a row-stochastic noise matrix
\mathbf{C}\in[0,1]^{N\times N}
.
In this scenario, our access is limited to the noisy target probability vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{C}^{\top}\mathbf{q}
, through which a noisy estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
is obtained.
By following the noise-correction strategy
[
84
]
, the plug-in forecaster based on the noisy estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
is given by
\widecheck{y}\in\argmax_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\widecheck{q}_{y}
, where
\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\coloneqq(\mathbf{C}^{\top})^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
(provided that
\mathbf{C}
is invertible).
Under this setup, the 0-1 regret of
\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}
given
\mathbf{q}
is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Reg}_{01}(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})

\displaystyle=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widecheck{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle{\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widecheck{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\langle{\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}},{\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}_{\widecheck{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y})}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}_{\widecheck{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y})\|_{p^{\ast}},

where the first inequality holds because
\left\langle{\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}},{\mathbf{L}_{\widecheck{y}}-\mathbf{L}_{y}}\right\rangle\leq 0
for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
attributed to the construction of
\widecheck{y}
,
and the second inequality owes to Hölder’s inequality.
The
p
-norm
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{q}}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}
can be minimized even with access to the noisy observations only, and the
p
-norm bound (
12
) controls this by the surrogate regret of a strictly proper loss.
This is also an extension of the previous surrogate regret transfer bounds
[
84
, Theorem 4]
beyond strongly proper losses.

Task 3: bipartite ranking.

Consider
N=2
and identify
\mathbf{q}=[q\;1-q]^{\top}\in\triangle^{2}
with the instance
q\in[0,1]
.
Given two instances

\widehat{q},\widehat{q}^{\prime}\in[0,1]
that yield a consistent ranking with
(q,q^{\prime})

In bipartite ranking, we use the estimates

when the predicate

and the first and second terms penalize an inconsistent ranking and tie, respectively.
This can be immediately related to the

\mathrm{Reg}_{\text{rank}}(q,q^{\prime},\widehat{q},\widehat{q}^{\prime})\leq|q-\widehat{q}|+|q^{\prime}-\widehat{q}^{\prime}|,

where the bound (

Thus, the ranking regret is controlled by the surrogate regret.

5
Lower bounds of surrogate regret order

We move on to the next main result: the surrogate regret order via the modulus of convexity cannot go beyond the square root.
To this end, we first review the Simonenko order function and the strong convexity used to establish the main result, and then show the main result.
In this section, let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function unless otherwise stated.

5.1
Power evaluation of moduli

To analyze how fast the surrogate regret bound (
11
) can be, we analyze the behavior of the modulus
\omega
in terms of power functions.
To this end, we introduce the order of
\omega
below, which is well-defined since
\omega(r)>0
for
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
from
Theorem

8
.

Definition 11

(Simonenko order function
[
72
]
)
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex function
and
\omega:[0,2^{1/p}]\to[0,\infty)
be the modulus of convexity of
f
.
The
Simonenko order function

\sigma:(0,2^{1/p}]\to[0,\infty]
(associated with
\omega
) is defined by

\sigma(r)\coloneqq\frac{rD^{-}\omega(r)}{\omega(r)}\quad\text{for }r\in\big(0,2^{\frac{1}{p}}\big],\text{~~where~~}D^{-}\omega(r)\coloneqq\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\omega(r)-\omega(r-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}.

The quantity
D^{-}\omega
is called the
upper left Dini derivative
of
\omega
at
r
.
If
\omega
is differentiable at
r
, then
D^{-}\omega(r)=\omega^{\prime}(r)
holds.
The Simonenko order function
\sigma
evaluates the order of
\omega
.
Note that the following result holds for general continuous functions beyond moduli of convexity, but we restrict ourselves to moduli of convexity for brevity.

Proposition 12

(Power evaluations of moduli)
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex function.
For a fixed
r_{0}\in(0,2^{1/p}]
, define
s,S\in[0,\infty]
by

s\coloneqq\inf_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r),\quad S\coloneqq\sup_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r),

and assume
S<\infty
.
Then, the function
r\mapsto\omega(r)r^{-s}
is non-decreasing on
(0,r_{0})
and
the function
r\mapsto\omega(r)r^{-S}
is non-increasing on
(0,r_{0})
.
Moreover, the following inequalities hold for any
r\in[0,r_{0}]
:

\left[\frac{\omega(r_{0})}{r_{0}^{S}}\right]r^{S}\leq\omega(r)\leq\left[\frac{\omega(r_{0})}{r_{0}^{s}}\right]r^{s}.

Roughly speaking,
Proposition

12
provides us
r^{S}\lesssim\omega(r)\lesssim r^{s}
as
r\downarrow 0
.
This order evaluation is useful when analyzing the asymptotic convergence rate of the surrogate regret bound (
11
).

Proposition

12
is easily proved when
\omega
is differentiable.
Indeed, if
\omega
is differentiable,
then it holds for
t\in(0,r_{0})
that

\frac{s}{t}=\frac{1}{t}\inf_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r)\leq\frac{\omega^{\prime}(t)}{\omega(t)}\leq\frac{1}{t}\sup_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r)=\frac{S}{t}

by the definition
s
and
S
,
and integrating it on
[r,r^{\prime}]\subseteq[0,r_{0}]
gives

s\ln\frac{r^{\prime}}{r}\leq\int_{r}^{r^{\prime}}\frac{\omega^{\prime}(t)}{\omega(t)}\mathrm{d}{t}=\ln\frac{\omega(r^{\prime})}{\omega(r)}\leq S\ln\frac{r^{\prime}}{r},

which is equivalent to the desired monotonicity.
Thus, the
p
-norm upper bound (
12
) is controlled by the rate
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=O(\rho^{1/S})
as
\rho\downarrow 0
.
Since we are interested in the behavior of
\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}
when
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}
is close to the minimizer of
R(\mathbf{q},\cdot)
, we focus on the asymptotic behavior of the Simonenko order function as
r\downarrow 0
.

The complete proof of
Theorem

8
for non-differentiable
\omega
is deferred to
Appendix

C
.

Asymptotic lower bound

r\downarrow 0
is controlled by the strong convexity parameter
\kappa^{f}_{p}
.
We consider a “local” version of the strong convexity parameter.

Definition 14

(Local strong convexity modulus)
.

For a convex function
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,
define
K_{p}^{f}:(0,2^{1/p}]\to\mathbb{R}
by

K^{f}_{p}(r)\coloneqq\frac{8\omega(r)}{r^{2}}.

This quantity is defined based on the alternative expression of the strong convexity parameter
\kappa^{f}_{p}
in (
18
).
From the relationship (
18
),
K^{f}_{p}(r)\geq\kappa^{f}_{p}
always holds on
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
.
We show that
K^{f}_{p}
is lower semi-continuous and left-continuous (but not continuous in general without additional assumptions) in
Appendix

D
.

Now, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of the moduli of convexity
\sigma(r)
at
r\downarrow 0
when the Bregman generator
f
is continuous on
\triangle^{N}
, which is our second main result.
Therein, we assume the continuity of
f
to prevent
f
from being discontinuous on the
\triangle^{N}\setminus\triangle^{N}_{+}
.

Theorem 15

(Lower bound of order)
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous strictly convex function.
Assume one of the following two conditions:

(C1)

\kappa^{f}_{p}>0
.

(C2)

K^{f}_{p}
is continuous on
(0,r_{0}]
for some
r_{0}\in(0,2^{1/p}]
and
K^{f}_{p}
converges as
r\downarrow 0
.

Then,

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq 2.

(19)

Moreover, if we assume both conditions, then

\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq 2.

(20)

Let us discuss the applicability of
Theorem

15
.
First,
f
is assumed to be strictly convex, which means that we deal with a strictly proper loss through the strictly convex negative Bayes risk
f=-\underline{L}
in (
5
).
In
Theorem

15
, we additionally require either (
C1
) of (
C2
).
The condition (
C1
) assumes nothing else but the strong convexity of
f
.
In other words, (
C1
) assumes that the underlying proper loss is strongly proper.
Indeed, the strong convexity parameter
\kappa_{p}^{f}>0
is equivalent to the modulus
\kappa
defining strongly proper losses in (
8
).
It is more interesting when (
C1
) does not hold but (
C2
) holds, where the underlying loss is strictly proper but no longer strongly proper.
The continuity assumption of
K_{p}^{f}
is mild enough to cover many reasonable examples of the negative Bayes risk
f
, as we will see in
Section

6
.

It follows from
Proposition

12
and
Theorem

15
that the
p
-norm bound (
12
) is controlled by the rate of
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
cannot be faster than
O(\rho^{1/2})
for a strictly proper
\bm{\ell}
satisfying either (
C1
) or (
C2
).
To see this, we invoke
Proposition

12
to observe that for a fixed
r_{0}\in(0,2^{1/p}]
,

\omega^{-1}(\rho)\leq r_{0}\omega(r_{0})^{-\frac{1}{S}}\cdot\rho^{\frac{1}{S}}\quad\text{for any $r\in[0,r_{0}]$ such that $\rho=\omega(r)$.}

If the bound (
19
) holds, then we have

2\leq\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\leq\sup_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r)=S,

which implies
\rho^{1/S}\geq\rho^{1/2}
(for
\rho<1
).
Thus, we discern the optimal rate
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=O(\rho^{1/2})
for
\rho\in[0,1]
.
This assures that strongly proper losses asymptotically achieve the optimal rate
O(\rho^{1/2})
as seen in (
9
).
Moreover, the optimal rate
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=O(\rho^{1/2})
penetrates into a broad family of strictly proper losses.

To prove
Theorem

15
, we leverage the following lemma to locally control
\sigma(r)
, which is proven in
Appendix

E
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
f:\triangle^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous convex function.
Then, for any
\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
and
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}K^{f}_{p}(r)=\kappa^{f}_{p},\quad J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})\geq\frac{\kappa^{f}_{p}}{8}\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}^{2},\quad\text{and}\quad D^{-}\omega(r)\geq\frac{\kappa^{f}_{p}}{4}r.

Proof of
Theorem

15

We observe from the strict convexity of
f
and
Theorem

8
that
K^{f}_{p}>0
on
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
.
By assuming (
C1
) only, it follows from
Lemma

16
that

\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)=\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{rD^{-}\omega(r)}{\omega(r)}\geq\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{r\cdot\dfrac{\kappa^{f}_{p}}{4}r}{\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r)}{8}r^{2}}=\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{2\kappa^{f}_{p}}{K^{f}_{p}(r)}=2.

(21)

In addition, assume (
C2
) together.
Then, in the similar manner to
Eq.

21
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{2\kappa^{f}_{p}}{K^{f}_{p}(r)}=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{2\kappa^{f}_{p}}{K^{f}_{p}(r)}=2.

Next, assume (
C2
) only, and
\kappa^{f}_{p}>0
does not hold.
In this case,
Lemma

16
indicates that
K^{f}_{p}(r)\downarrow 0
as
r\downarrow 0
, from which with the intermediate value theorem,
we can inductively define
(r_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq(0,r_{0}]
by

r_{j}\coloneqq\inf\left\{{r\in(0,2^{1/p}]}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{K^{f}_{p}(r)\geq\frac{1}{2}K^{f}_{p}(r_{j-1})}\right\}.

Then,
(r_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
because
K^{f}_{p}>0
always holds on
r\in(0,2^{1/p}]
and hence
K^{f}_{p}(r)=0
if and only if
r=0
.
We see

K^{f}_{p}(r)<\frac{1}{2}K^{f}_{p}(r_{j-1})=K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})\quad\text{for $r\in(0,r_{j}]$.}

This yields

\displaystyle D^{-}\omega(r_{j})=\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\omega(r_{j})-\omega(r_{j}-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{8}r_{j}^{2}-\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j}-\varepsilon)}{8}(r_{j}-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{8}r_{j}^{2}-\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{8}(r_{j}-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{4}r_{j},

which implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\sigma(r_{j})=\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{r_{j}D^{-}\omega(r_{j})}{\omega(r_{j})}\geq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\frac{r_{j}\cdot\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{4}r_{j}}{\dfrac{K^{f}_{p}(r_{j})}{8}r_{j}^{2}}=2.

Thus the proof of
Theorem

15
is completed.

∎

Lower bound of
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
.

Proposition

12
also implies that for some
r_{0}\in(0,2^{1/p}]
,

\omega^{-1}(\rho)\geq[r_{0}\omega(r_{0})^{-\frac{1}{s}}]\cdot\rho^{\frac{1}{s}}\quad\text{for $r\in[0,r_{0}]$ such that $\rho=\omega(r)$.}

If the bound (
20
) holds with the
strict
inequality, then we can choose
r_{0}
to satisfy

\frac{2+\varsigma}{2}\leq\inf_{r\in(0,r_{0}]}\sigma(r)=s\quad\text{for~~$\varsigma\coloneqq\liminf_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)>2$,}

which implies
\rho^{1/s}\geq\rho^{2/(2+\varsigma)}>\rho^{1/2}
(for
\rho<1
).
Thus,
\omega^{-1}
admits the lower bound
\Omega(\rho^{1/2})
for
\rho\in[0,1]
.

Comparison with the known lower bound.

A relevant lower bound
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=\Omega(\rho^{1/2})
has been shown previously for margin-based losses
[
36
, Theorem 4]

[
52
, Theorem 4.2]
.
These lower bounds assume that a loss is strongly convex and has a locally Lipschitz gradient
[
36
, Assumption 1]
or a loss satisfies a relaxed version of the strict convexity
[
52
, Theorem 4.2]
.
These conditions are assumed under the loss differentiability, whereas both our (
C1
) and (
C2
) do not need the differentiability of
\bm{\ell}
.
Ergo, the differentiability assumption is lifted to show the optimality of
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=O(\rho^{1/2})
.
To show
\omega^{-1}(\rho)=\Omega(\rho^{1/2})
in our case, (
C1
) and (
C2
) coupled with the existence of the limit of
K^{f}_{p}(r)
as
r\downarrow 0
suffice.

As a side note, convolutional Fenchel–Young losses have been recently proposed to circumvent these commonly known squared-root lower bounds for convex smooth surrogate losses
[
23
]
.
Their surrogate regret bounds are derived for the multiclass 0-1 loss (and its generalization), which is clearly different from our the
p
-norm distance.
Thus, it does not contradict with the square-root lower bounds of
Theorem

10
.

Remark 17
.

Our analysis with the Simonenko order evaluates the order of
\omega
by a power function in the form of
r^{S}\lesssim\omega(r)\lesssim r^{s}
for
r\in[0,2^{1/p}]
.
This is an evaluation for a
finite
range, which is more than an
asymptotic
evaluation.
Despite its subtlety, it often matters, as seen in the following example:

\omega(r)=r\sin\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)-\mathrm{Ci}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)+r\quad\text{for $r>0$},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathrm{Ci}(z)\coloneqq-\int_{z}^{\infty}t^{-1}\cos(t)\mathrm{d}{t}.

This
\omega
is monotonically increasing and satisfies
\omega(r)=O(r)
as
r\downarrow 0
.
However, when it comes to the finite evaluation, we cannot go faster than
\omega(r)\gtrsim r^{2}

just because this
\omega
satisfies (
C2
) and
Theorem

15
implies
\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq 2
.
Thus, the finite evaluation gives a better characterization when we assess the convergence rate of finitely large surrogate regret.
See
Fig.

to better understand the above example.

Log loss.

If we choose

f(\mathbf{q})=\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\ln\mathbf{q}}\right\rangle=\sum_{n\in[N]}q_{n}\ln q_{n},

where
\ln
is applied in the element-wise manner,
we can generate the log loss
\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=-\ln q_{y}
.
In the binary case, we have

\ell_{1}(\mathbf{q})=-\ln q\quad\text{and}\quad\ell_{2}(\mathbf{q})=-\ln(1-q)\quad\text{for $\mathbf{q}=[q\;1-q]^{\top}\in\triangle^{2}$}.

As we saw in
Section

3.3
and
Section

4.2
, the log loss is associated with the Kullback–Leibler divergence, and its surrogate regret bound slightly improves Pinsker’s inequality yet is asymptotically equivalent.

To see the asymptotic speed of the
1
-norm bound (
12
) for the log loss, we investigate the power evaluation of
\omega^{-1}(\rho)\lesssim\rho^{1/S}
through
Theorem

15
,
where
S
is given in
Proposition

12
.
We can see that both (
C1
) and (
C2
) are satisfied in this case.
Indeed, the continuity of
K_{p}^{f}
is obvious, and

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}K_{p}^{f}(r)

\displaystyle=4\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{(1+\frac{r}{2})\ln(1+\frac{r}{2})+(1-\frac{r}{2})\ln(1-\frac{r}{2})}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\left(\frac{1}{2+r}+\frac{1}{2-r}\right)

\displaystyle=1<\infty,

where L’Hôpital’s rule is used twice.
See also
Fig.

2
for the illustration of
K_{p}^{f}
.
Thus,
Theorem

15
yields
\limsup_{r\downarrow 0}\sigma(r)\geq 2
and
S\geq 2
,
which indicates that the polynomial rate of
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
cannot be faster than
\rho^{1/2}
.
The asymptotic lower bound of
\sigma
provided here is indeed tight, as we see
\sigma(r)\to 2
for
r\downarrow 0

[
8
, Appendix B.1]
.
All in all, we asymptotically have the
1
-norm bound

|q-\widehat{q}|\lesssim\sqrt{D_{\text{KL}}(q\|\widehat{q})}\quad\text{for $q,\widehat{q}\in[0,1]$,}

recovering Pinsker’s inequality.

Squared norms.

Consider the squared
\alpha
-norms for
\alpha>1
:

f(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{2}-1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Biggl(\sum_{n\in[N]}q_{n}^{\alpha}\Biggr)^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{2}.

By the Savage representation (
4
), we can generate proper losses:

\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=-\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{2-\alpha}q_{y}^{\alpha-1}+\frac{1+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{2}}{2}.

By plugging in
\alpha=2
, the Brier score in
Section

3.3
is recovered.

To see the asymptotic speed of the
p
-norm bound (
12
) for
(N,p)=(2,1)
,
we take the limit of
K_{p}^{f}
similarly to the log-loss case.
By using the closed form of
\omega(r)
provided in
Table

1
,
when
\alpha\in(1,2)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}K_{p}^{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{[(1+\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha}+(1-\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha}]^{\frac{2}{\alpha}}-4^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\left\{(2-\alpha)[(1+r)^{\alpha}+(1-r)^{\alpha}]^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-2}[(1+r)^{\alpha-1}-(1-r)^{\alpha-1}]^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(\alpha-1)[(1+r)^{\alpha}+(1-r)^{\alpha}]^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-1}[(1+r)^{\alpha-2}+(1-r)^{\alpha-2}]\right\}

\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)2^{\frac{2}{\alpha}-2}<\infty,

where L’Hôpital’s rule is used twice.
When
\alpha\geq 2
, we can similarly show the existence of the limit of
K_{p}^{f}
as
r\downarrow 0
.
Thus, these losses satisfy both
(
C1
) and (
C2
) of
Theorem

15
, which indicates that
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
cannot be faster than
O(\rho^{1/2})
.
Across different
\alpha>1
, we have
\sigma(r)\to 2
as

[
8
, Figure 4]
, and thus the provided asymptotic lower bound of
\sigma
is tight.

Pseudo-spherical losses.

Consider the
\alpha
-norms for
\alpha>1
:

f(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{n\in[N]}q_{n}^{\alpha}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-1\Biggr],

which has been sometimes used as the
\alpha
-norm information measure
[
22
]
.
By the Savage representation (
4
), we can generate proper losses:

\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Biggl(1-\frac{q_{y}^{\alpha-1}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha-1}}\Biggr),

which is called the
pseudo-spherical losses

[
38
]
.
By plugging in
\alpha=2
, the spherical loss is recovered.
The associated Bregman divergence is

R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Biggl(\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}-\frac{\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\widehat{\mathbf{q}}^{\alpha-1}}\right\rangle}{\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha-1}}\Biggr)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Biggl(\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}-\frac{1}{\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha-1}}\sum_{n\in[N]}q_{n}\widehat{q}_{n}^{\alpha-1}\Biggr).

Note that
\ln[\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}-(\alpha-1)R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})]/(\alpha-1)
can be identified with the (cross-entropy of)
gamma-divergences
, which is commonly used in robust regression
[
33
]
and inference with unnormalized models
[
43
]
.
With the limit
\alpha\downarrow 1
, the pseudo-spherical loss approaches the log loss, and the associated Bregman divergence approaches to the Kullback–Leibler divergence, correspondingly.

We illustrate the case
N=2
and
\alpha\in(1,3/2]\cup[2,\infty)
since the modulus
\omega
can be written analytically herein (shown in
Table

1
).
When
1<\alpha\leq 3/2
, by invoking L’Hôpital’s rule twice,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}K_{p}^{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\alpha-1}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-2}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}-\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=2^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}

\displaystyle<\infty.

When
\alpha\geq 2
, by invoking L’Hôpital’s rule twice,

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}K_{p}^{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\alpha-1}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}-\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(2-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}+1}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\left\{-\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1-2\alpha}{\alpha}}\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}-\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]^{2}\right.

\displaystyle+\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}+\left(1-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(2-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1-2\alpha}{\alpha}}\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}-\left(2-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\left.\quad-\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}+\left(2-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha}\right]^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left[\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}+\left(2-\frac{r}{2}\right)^{\alpha-2}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}&\text{if $\alpha=2$}\\
0&\text{if $\alpha>2$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle<\infty.

Thus, these losses satisfy (
C2
) of
Theorem

15
, which indicates that
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
cannot be faster than
O(\rho^{1/2})
.

is no longer always positive.
Indeed, (

) of

is satisfied when
\alpha\in(1,3/2]\cup\left\{{2}\right\}
but not satisfied when
\alpha>2
.
The previous

lower bounds typically require the local strong convexity
[

]

[

]
, which is similar to (

).
Therefore, our
Theorem

15
slightly lifted the assumptions, requiring (

) solely.
See
Fig.

2
to confirm that

Tsallis losses.

Consider the negative Tsallis
\alpha
-entropy

f(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha-1}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\Biggl(\sum_{n\in[N]}q_{n}^{\alpha}-1\Biggr)

as a convex potential, for
\alpha>1
.
The Tsallis entropies generalize the Shannon entropy for non-extensive systems
[

]
, and recovers the Shannon entropy at the limit
\alpha\downarrow 1
.
By the Savage representation (

), we can generate proper losses:

\ell_{y}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{\alpha q_{y}^{\alpha-1}-1}{\alpha-1}+\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha},

which recovers the log loss at the limit
\alpha\downarrow 1
.
We call them the
\alpha
-log loss
for convenience.
Note that this loss is slightly different from the
\alpha
-loss

[

]
.
Indeed, the
\alpha
-log loss is proper by its construction, while the
\alpha
-loss is known to be improper
[
73
]
; despite that both of them approach the log loss at the same limit.
The associated Bregman divergence to the
\alpha
-log loss is

R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}-\alpha\left\langle{\mathbf{q}},{\widehat{\mathbf{q}}^{\alpha-1}}\right\rangle+(\alpha-1)\|\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\alpha-1},

which is the
Tsallis divergence

[
27
]
, and also corresponds to
density power divergence
(or the beta-divergence)
[
12
]
up to constant, used in robust statistics.
The Tsallis divergence interpolates the Kullback–Leibler divergence and the squared
2
-norm distance at the limits of
\alpha\to 1
and
\alpha\to 2
, respectively.
Some literature opts another definition of the Tsallis divergence, defined by replacing
\ln
in the Kullback–Leibler divergence with the
\alpha
-logarithmic function
[
6
]
—precisely, this another definition should be distinguished as the

-divergence
[

]
.

To see the asymptotic speed of the
p
-norm bound (
12
) for
(N,p)=(2,1)
, we take the limit of

When

in

\displaystyle\lim_{r\downarrow 0}K_{p}^{f}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{8}{2^{\alpha}(\alpha-1)}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{(1+\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha}+(1-\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha}-2}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha}{2^{\alpha}}\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{(1+\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha-2}+(1-\frac{r}{2})^{\alpha-2}}{2}

7
Conclusion

In this work, we examine surrogate regret bounds on the
p
-norm,
\|\mathbf{q}-\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\|_{p}\lesssim\omega^{-1}(R(\mathbf{q},\widehat{\mathbf{q}}))
, which measures predictive performances of plug-in forecasters under downstream tasks such as classification and ranking.
A surrogate regret bound is characterized by the modulus of convexity
\omega
associated with the Bregman generator
f=-\underline{L}
for a given proper loss.
First, we show that the existence of non-vacuous regret bounds is equivalent to the strict properness of losses.
Then, we prove that the
p
-norm upper bound
\omega^{-1}(\rho)
cannot be faster than the
1/2
-order of surrogate regrets
O(\sqrt{\rho})
for a wide range of strictly proper losses.
Herein, the assumptions on loss functions are greatly relaxed so that we do not require the differentiability or the local strong convexity of loss functions anymore.
We demonstrate that many loss functions such as the log loss, Brier loss, pseudo-spherical losses, and
\alpha
-log losses satisfy the assumptions of our optimal-order argument.

As a side note, there is a fundamental relationship between a proper loss and a convex body
[
79
]

[
78
]
.
Specifically, the Bayes risk
\underline{L}
of a proper loss is the support function of the superprediction set (which is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
) associated with the proper loss.
Hence, we can work on a convex body instead of directly working on a proper loss.
This perspective has been used to consider aggregating algorithms.
In this connection, we studied the modulus of convexity of Bregman generators
f=-\underline{L}
, while the modulus of convexity of Banach spaces has been more commonly studied to measure the set curvature
[
35
]
.
We conjecture that the modulus of convexity of Bregman generators has a tight connection to the modulus of convexity of superprediction sets, which remains an interesting open question from the viewpoint of convex analysis.

This work is concerned with only
expected
surrogate regrets, induced from the full risk
\mathbb{L}[\widehat{\mathbf{q}}]
in
Section

3.2
.
Yet, its empirical estimation and optimization together play an important role in realistic learning scenarios.
To take them into account, it is more standard to consider a learner acting on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued margin, instead of
\triangle^{N}
-valued prediction as supposed in
Section

3
.
Proper
composite
losses are common therein, where a link function connects a probabilistic report
\widehat{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
to a real-valued report on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Hence, we can consider estimation and optimization of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
-valued functions.
In spite of the scarcity, the estimation error rates of class probability models under the binary case
N=2
have been studied rigorously for linear hypotheses
[
75
]
,
while the optimization error rates of proper composite losses by gradient descent under the binary case have been characterized recently
[
20
]
.
We hope to thoroughly understand how a proper loss behaves by integrating surrogate regrets, estimation, and optimization all at once, and leave this for future work.

Subgradient inequality

In this paper, we adopted a slightly non-conventional definition of subdifferentials
\partial f
in
Section

2

to allow some elements of the subgradient to be
-\infty
.

Lemma 18
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper convex function such that
\triangle^{N}\subseteq\domain{f}
.
For
\mathbf{q}^{0}\in\triangle^{N}
, the set
\partial f(\mathbf{q}^{0})
is nonempty.

Proof

Fix
\mathbf{q}^{0}\in\triangle^{N}
.
If
f
is subdifferentiable at
\mathbf{q}^{0}
,
then its subgradient belongs to
\partial f(\mathbf{q}^{0})
and the claim holds true.
Thus we assume that
f
is not subdifferentiable at
\mathbf{q}^{0}
.
Since
f
is subdifferentiable at
\mathbf{q}^{0}\in\triangle^{N}_{+}

[
65
, Theorem 23.4]
,
I\coloneqq|\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})|
satisfies
1\leq I\leq N-1
.
For
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
, define
\bm{\xi}^{\bm{\eta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\xi^{\bm{\eta}}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\eta_{n}&\text{if }n\in\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0}),\\
0&\text{if }n\notin\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0}),\end{cases}

and define a function
f_{I}:\mathbb{R}^{I}\to(-\infty,\infty]^{N}
by

f_{I}(\bm{\eta})\coloneqq f(\bm{\xi}^{\bm{\eta}})\quad\text{for }\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}.

Then,
f_{I}
is a proper convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{I}
such that
\triangle^{I}\subseteq\domain{f}_{I}
,
consequently,
f_{I}
is subdifferentiable at
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}\in\triangle^{I}_{+}

[
65
, Theorem 23.4]
.
For
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
, define
\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
by
\eta^{\mathbf{q}}_{n}=q_{n}
for
n\in\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
.
Then, for
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
with
\supp(\mathbf{q})\subseteq\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
,
we have
\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{I}
and
f_{I}(\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}})=f(\mathbf{q})
.
Moreover, if
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
satisfies
\supp(\mathbf{q})=\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
,
then
\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle_{+}^{I}
and hence
\partial f_{I}(\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}})\neq\emptyset
.
Choose
\mathbf{w}\in\partial f_{I}(\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}^{0}})
and define
\mathbf{v}\in[-\infty,\infty)^{N}
by

{v}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}{w}_{n}&\text{if }n\in\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0}),\\
-\infty&\text{if }n\notin\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0}).\end{cases}

From now on, we show that
\mathbf{v}\in\partial f(\mathbf{q}^{0})
.
For
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
, if
q_{n}>0
holds for some
n\notin\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
, then
\left\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{0}}\right\rangle=-\infty
and (
2
) holds.
On the other hand, if
q_{n}=0
for all
n\notin\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
, then
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
with
\supp(\mathbf{q})\subseteq\supp(\mathbf{q}^{0})
and

f(\mathbf{q})=f_{I}(\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}})\geq f_{I}\Big(\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}^{0}}\Big)+\left\langle{\mathbf{w}},{\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}}-\bm{\eta}^{\mathbf{q}^{0}}}\right\rangle=f(\mathbf{q}^{0})+\left\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}^{0}}\right\rangle,

that is, (
2
) holds for
\mathbf{q}\in\triangle^{N}
.
Thus,
\mathbf{v}\in\partial f(\mathbf{q}^{0})
follows.
This completes the proof of the lemma.

Proof of power evaluations without differentiability

In

See

Proof

We first show that

is non-increasing on

in a similar way to the existing argument
[

.
For
r\in(0,r_{0}]
and
\delta>0

\varepsilon_{r,\delta}>0
such that

\frac{r}{\omega(r)}\cdot\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{r,\delta})}\frac{\omega(r)-\omega(r-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\leq\sigma(r)+\frac{1}{2}\delta

(22)

by the definition of
D^{-}\omega
.
Define

g(t)\coloneqq S+\frac{1}{2}\delta+\frac{1}{t}[(1-t)^{S+\delta}-1]\quad\text{for $t\in(0,1)$.}

Then,
g
is continuous on

and

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}g(t)=S+\frac{1}{2}\delta-(S+\delta)=-\frac{1}{2}\delta<0,

which implies the existence of

such that

for
t\in(0,\tau)

Then, for any
u\in(0,\varepsilon_{r,\delta})\cap(0,r\tau)

\displaystyle-\frac{r^{S+\delta}}{\omega(r)}+\frac{(r-u)^{S+\delta}}{\omega(r-u)}

\displaystyle=\frac{ur^{S+\delta-1}}{\omega(r-u)}\left[\frac{r}{\omega(r)}\frac{\omega(r)-\omega(r-u)}{u}\right]-\frac{r^{S+\delta}}{\omega(r-u)}+\frac{r^{S+\delta}(1-\frac{u}{r})^{S+\delta}}{\omega(r-u)}

\displaystyle<0,

where the inequality (
22
) is used at the second line
and the third line follows from the definition of
S
.

The local modulus of convexity defined in

Subsequently, we show
Lemmas

24
and
25
, which are needed to invoke the method of Lagrangian multipliers later.

Lemma 24
.

For
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{U}^{N-1}
, the rank of the Jacobian of
\psi
at
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})
is zero if and only if
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{w}
.

Proof
Since we have

\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{u_{i}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=u_{i}-w_{i}+\sum_{n\in[N-1]}(u_{n}-w_{n})=-\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{w_{i}}}\quad\text{for $i\in[N-1]$,}

the rank of the Jacobian of
\psi
at
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})
is zero if and only if

u_{i}-w_{i}+\sum_{n\in[N-1]}(u_{n}-w_{n})=0\quad\text{for $i\in[N-1]$.}

Summing the above equation up gives

N\sum_{n\in[N-1]}(u_{n}-w_{n})=0,

and hence
u_{i}-w_{i}=0
for all
i\in[N-1]
,
which shows
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{w}
. The converse implication is trivial.

∎

Lemma 25
.

For
r\in(0,2^{1/2})
with
r^{2}<N/(N-1)
, let
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{U}^{N-1}
satisfy
\psi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=r^{2}/2

and set

u_{N}\coloneqq 1-\sum_{n\in[N-1]}u_{n},\quad w_{N}\coloneqq 1-\sum_{n\in[N-1]}w_{n}.

If there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}{2}\right)+\lambda\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{and}

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}{2}\right)+\lambda\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=0

for
n\in[N-1]
,
then there exists
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[N]

with
I\coloneqq|\mathcal{I}|
such that
0<I(N-I)<r^{-2}N

and

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+r\sqrt{a_{N}(I)}}{2I},\quad w_{n}=\frac{1-r\sqrt{a_{N}(I)}}{2I}\quad\text{for $n\in\mathcal{I}$,}

\displaystyle u_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-r\sqrt{a_{N}(I)}}{2(N-I)},\quad w_{n}=\frac{1+r\sqrt{a_{N}(I)}}{2(N-I)}\quad\text{for $n\in[N]\setminus\mathcal{I}$},\quad\text{where }a_{N}(I)\coloneqq\frac{I(N-I)}{N}.

Proof
For simplicity, set

\mathbf{v}\coloneqq\frac{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad v_{N}\coloneqq 1-\sum_{n\in[N-1]}v_{n}.

Assuming
Eq.

25
, we calculate

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}(\mathbf{u})-\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}(\mathbf{v})+\lambda\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{u_{n}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{u_{n}}{u_{N}}-\ln\frac{v_{n}}{v_{N}}\right)+\lambda[u_{n}-w_{n}-(u_{N}-w_{N})],

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}(\mathbf{w})-\frac{\partial{\phi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}(\mathbf{v})+\lambda\frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial{w_{n}}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln\frac{w_{n}}{w_{N}}-\ln\frac{v_{n}}{v_{N}}\right)-\lambda[u_{n}-w_{n}-(u_{N}-w_{N})],

for
n\in[N-1]
, which yields

\displaystyle\ln\frac{u_{n}}{u_{N}}-\ln\frac{v_{n}}{v_{N}}=-2\lambda[u_{n}-w_{n}-(u_{N}-w_{N})]=-\ln\frac{w_{n}}{w_{N}}+\ln\frac{v_{n}}{v_{N}}

(26)

Thus, we have

\frac{(u_{n}+w_{n})^{2}}{4u_{n}w_{n}}=\frac{v_{n}^{2}}{u_{n}w_{n}}=\frac{v_{N}^{2}}{u_{N}w_{N}}\eqqcolon\Lambda>1\quad\text{for $n\in[N-1]$},

where
\Lambda>1
holds; otherwise
u_{n}=w_{n}
holds for all
n\in[N]
, which contradicts
\psi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=r^{2}/2
.
By rearranging in
w_{n}
, we have

w_{n}^{2}-2u_{n}(2\Lambda-1)w_{n}+u_{n}^{2}=0\quad\text{for $n\in[N]$.}

Setting

\mu\coloneqq(2\Lambda-1)-2\sqrt{\Lambda^{2}-\Lambda}\in(0,1),

we have either
w_{n}=\mu u_{n}
or
w_{n}=\mu^{-1}u_{n}

for
n\in[N]
, by solving the quadratic equation with respect to
w_{n}
.
Set

\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\{i\in[N]\mid w_{i}=\mu u_{i}\},\quad I\coloneqq|\mathcal{I}|,\qquad\mathcal{J}\coloneqq\{j\in[N]\mid w_{j}=\mu^{-1}u_{j}\},\quad J\coloneqq|\mathcal{J}|.

Then
I+J=N
holds.

Assume
N\in\mathcal{I}
.
If
\mathcal{J}=\emptyset
,
then

\displaystyle w_{N}=1-\sum_{i\in[N-1]}w_{i}

\displaystyle=1-\mu\sum_{i\in[N-1]}u_{i}

\displaystyle\neq\mu-\mu\sum_{i\in[N-1]}u_{i}=\mu\left(1-\sum_{i\in[N-1]}u_{i}\right)=\mu u_{N}=w_{N},

which is a contradiction.
Thus
\mathcal{J}\neq\emptyset
holds.
On one hand, for
j\in\mathcal{J}
,
we observe from
Eq.

26
that

\ln\frac{1+\mu}{1+\mu^{-1}}=\ln\frac{u_{j}}{u_{N}}-\ln\frac{v_{j}}{v_{N}}=-2\lambda[u_{j}-w_{j}-(u_{N}-w_{N})]\\
=2\lambda(1-\mu)(\mu^{-1}u_{j}+u_{N}).

Since the left-hand side is independent of
j
and not zero,
then so is the right-hand side hence
u_{j}
is determined independent of
j
and
\lambda\neq 0
.
On the other hand, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
, it turns out that

\ln\frac{u_{i}}{u_{N}}-\ln\frac{v_{i}}{v_{N}}=0

and consequently
u_{i}=u_{N}
holds
by
Eq.

26
together with the property
\lambda\neq 0
.
This yields
u_{i}=u_{N}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}
.
Thus there exist
s,t\in(0,1)
such that

u_{i}=s,\ w_{i}=\mu s\quad\text{for }i\in\mathcal{I},\quad u_{j}=t,\ w_{j}=\mu^{-1}t\quad\text{for }j\in\mathcal{J}.

(27)

We see that

1=\sum_{n\in[N]}u_{N}=Is+Jt,\quad 1=\sum_{n\in[N]}w_{N}=\mu Is+\mu^{-1}Jt,

that is,
Jt=1-Is=\mu(1-\mu Is)
.
This can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Is}-1=\frac{Jt}{Is}.

We also find that

\displaystyle r^{2}

\displaystyle=2\psi(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w})=(1-\mu)^{2}\left(Is^{2}+\mu^{-2}Jt^{2}\right)=(1-\mu)^{2}\left(Is^{2}+\frac{I^{2}}{J}s^{2}\right)=\frac{I}{J}N(1-\mu)^{2}s^{2},

which in turn shows

r\sqrt{\frac{J}{NI}}=(1-\mu)s=2s-\frac{1}{I}.

We conclude

s=\frac{1}{2I}\left(1+r\sqrt{\frac{IJ}{N}}\right),\quad t=\frac{1}{2J}\left(1-r\sqrt{\frac{IJ}{N}}\right)

as desired.

The case
N\in\mathcal{J}
is proved by switching the role of
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{w}
in the argument for the case
N\in\mathcal{I}
, and the proof is achieved.

∎

By combining these lemmas, we have the following claim, which is the minimizers
(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})
for the negative Shannon entropy we show in
Section

6
.

Proposition 26
.

For
r\in(0,2^{1/2})
with
r^{2}<N/(N-1)
,
\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\in\triangle^{N}
satisfy
\|\mathbf{q}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}\|_{2}=r
and
\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})

if and only if there exist distinct
i,j\in[N]
such that

q_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1+2^{-1/2}r}{2}\delta_{ni}+\frac{1-2^{-1/2}r}{2}\delta_{nj},\quad\widecheck{q}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1-2^{-1/2}r}{2}\delta_{ni}+\frac{1+2^{-1/2}r}{2}\delta_{nj}.

In this case,

\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(1+\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\left(1-\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\ln\left(1-\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right].

Proof

There exist
\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\in\triangle^{N}
such that

\|\mathbf{q}^{\prime}-\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}\|_{2}=r,\quad\omega(r)=J(\mathbf{q},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\supp(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\supp(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})

from
Lemma

9
and
Corollary

23
.
By relabeling the indices
n\in[N]
and switching
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}
and
\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime}

if necessary, we may assume that
and there exists
N^{\prime}\in[N]
such that
N^{\prime}\geq 2
with

\supp(\mathbf{q}^{\prime})=\supp(\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=[N^{\prime}],

and
I\in[N^{\prime}-1]
with
I\leq N^{\prime}/2
such that
0<I(N^{\prime}-I)<r^{-2}N^{\prime}

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}q^{\prime}_{n}&=\frac{1+r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(I)}}{2I},\quad\widecheck{q}^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{1-r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(I)}}{2I}\quad\text{for $n\in[I]$,}\\
q^{\prime}_{n}&=\frac{1-r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(I)}}{2(N^{\prime}-I)},\quad\widecheck{q}^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{1+r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(I)}}{2(N^{\prime}-I)}\quad\text{for $n\in[N^{\prime}]\setminus[I]$},\end{split}

(28)

by
Lemma

25
,
where

a_{N^{\prime}}(x)\coloneqq\frac{x(N^{\prime}-x)}{N^{\prime}}.

We need to identify
I
and
N^{\prime}
hereafter.
Note that for any
N^{\prime}\in[N]
, we have

1\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\right)\leq 1\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)\leq r^{-2}

hence

I^{\prime}\coloneqq\max\left\{{i\in[N^{\prime}-1]}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{i(N^{\prime}-i)<r^{-2}N^{\prime},i\leq N^{\prime}/2}\right\}

is well-defined.

Setting

\overline{J}_{N^{\prime}}(x)\coloneqq\left[1+r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}\right]\ln\left[1+r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}\right]+\left[1-r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}\right]\ln\left[1-r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}\right]

for
1\leq x\leq N^{\prime}-1
,
we see that

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\widecheck{\mathbf{q}}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\overline{J}_{N^{\prime}}(I).

From this with the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{x}}\overline{J}_{N^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}}\left(1-\frac{2x}{N^{\prime}}\right)\ln\frac{1+r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}}{1-r\sqrt{a_{N^{\prime}}(x)}}\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{for $x\in[I^{\prime}]$,}

we observe

\min_{I\in[I^{\prime}]}\overline{J}_{N^{\prime}}(I)=\overline{J}_{N^{\prime}}(1)=\overline{J}(N^{\prime})

where

\overline{J}(y)\coloneqq\left(1+r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}\right)\ln\left(1+r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}\right)+\left(1-r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}\right)\ln\left(1-r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}\right)

for
y\geq 2
.
We calculate

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}{y}}\overline{J}(y)=\frac{ry^{-2}}{2\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}}\ln\frac{1+r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}}{1-r\sqrt{1-y^{-1}}}>0\quad\text{for }y\geq 2,

which leads to

\min_{N^{\prime}\in[N],N^{\prime}\geq 2}\overline{J}(N^{\prime})=\overline{J}(2).

Thus, in
Eq.

28
,
the correct choice is
(N^{\prime},I)=(2,1)
and
this completes the proof of the proposition.

∎
