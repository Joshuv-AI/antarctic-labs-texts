Dynamical classification of analytic one-frequency quasi-periodic SO ⁡ ( 3 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}) -cocycles
Xuanji Hou, Yi Pan, Qi Zhou

Abstract
We establish a close connection between acceleration and dynamical degree for one-frequency quasi-periodic compact cocycles, by showing that two vectors derived separately from each coincide. Based on this, we provide a dynamical classification of one-frequency quasi-periodic SO ⁡ ( 3 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}) -cocycles.



1
Introduction

The classification of dynamics is an important topic in dynamical systems. One well-known example is the classification of circle diffeomorphisms. Let
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
and
f:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
be an orientation-preserving
C^{r}
homeomorphism, where
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty,\omega\}
. Poincaré laid the foundations of the theory of circle diffeomorphisms and introduced the concept of rotation number in the classification of these diffeomorphisms. The rotation number
\rho(f)\in\mathbb{R}
of
f
is defined as the uniform limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{f}^{n}(x)-x}{n}.

The rotation number is independent of the choice of
x\in\mathbb{T}
and is invariant under conjugations, making it a useful dynamical invariant.
Poincaré’s classification theorem states that if the rotation number
\rho(f)
is rational
\frac{p}{q}
, then
f
has a periodic orbit of period
q
. If the rotation number is irrational, then
f
is
semi-conjugate
to the irrational (rigid) rotation. Denjoy
[
17
]
further showed that if the rotation number is irrational and
\ln Df
has bounded variation, then the semi-conjugacy is a homeomorphism. This result was later extended by Arnold
[
15
]
, as part of the KAM theory, who showed that if
f
is
C^{\omega}
close to a rotation and the rotation number
\rho(f)
satisfies the Diophantine condition, then the conjugacy is analytic. Subsequent work by Herman, Yoccoz, Katznelson, Ornstein, Sinai, and others
[
28
,
29
,
51
,
52
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
49
]
extended this result to the global version of analytic, smooth, or finitely differentiable circle diffeomorphisms
f
. They showed that when
f
is analytic (resp.
C^{\infty}
,
C^{k}
with
k>2
) and
\rho(f)
is Diophantine, the conjugacy is analytic (resp.
C^{\infty}
,
C^{k-r}
for some
0<r<k
). Moreover, the Diophantine condition can be weakened to the Brjuno condition, which turns to be necessary
[
52
]
. Therefore, the dynamics of the circle diffeomorphism
f
can be completely determined by its rotation number
\rho(f)
.

A one-frequency cocycle is a natural generalization of a circle diffeomorphism in high dimensions. Let
{\rm G}
be a Lie-subgroup of
{\rm GL}(s,\mathbb{C})
.
A
C^{r}
one-frequency
{\rm G}
-cocycle

(\alpha,A)
is a skew-product defined on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{s}
such that

(\alpha,A):\,\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{s}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{C}^{s},\quad(x,v)\mapsto(x+\alpha,A(x)\cdot v),

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
,
A\in C^{r}(\mathbb{T},{\rm GL}(s,\mathbb{C}))
,
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. The iterates of the cocycle
(\alpha,A)^{n}=(n\alpha,A_{n})
are defined as
A_{0}(x)=I
,

A_{n}(\cdot)=A(\cdot+(n-1)\alpha)\cdots A(\cdot),\quad n\geq 1,

and
A_{n}(\cdot)=A_{-n}(\cdot-n\alpha)^{-1}
for
n\leq-1
.

When
G=\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})
, taking projective action, it naturally induces the cocycle

T_{\phi}:\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T},\quad(x,g)\mapsto(x+\alpha,\phi(x)\cdot g).

Moreover, if
\phi
is smooth, then dynamical properties of
T_{\phi}
depend on the topological degree
d(\phi)
of
\phi
. For example, if
\phi
is
C^{2}
with
d(\phi)\neq 0
, then
T_{\phi}
is ergodic and it has a countable Lebesgue spectrum on the orthocomplement of the space of functions depending only on the first variable
[
33
]
. If
\phi
is absolutely continuous with
d(\phi)=0
, then
T_{\phi}
has a singular spectrum
[
26
]
.

However, when
G
is non-compact, hyperbolicity appears. Therefore, another important dynamical invariant is introduced. For a cocycle
(\alpha,A)\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}\times C^{0}(\mathbb{T},{\rm GL}(s,\mathbb{C}))
, we define the
Lyapunov exponents

L_{1}(\alpha,A)\geq L_{2}(\alpha,A)\geq...\geq L_{s}(\alpha,A)
as

L_{k}(\alpha,A):=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln(\sigma_{k}(A_{n}(x)))dx,\quad k=1,\cdots,s,

(repeatedly according to multiplicities), where for a matrix
B
we denote by
\sigma_{1}(B)\geq...\geq\sigma_{s}(B)
its singular values (eigenvalues of
\sqrt{B^{*}B}
). As the k-th exterior product
\Lambda^{k}B
of
B
satisfies
\|\Lambda^{k}B\|=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}(B)
, one can define

L^{k}(\alpha,A):=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}L_{j}(\alpha,A)=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\ln(\|\Lambda^{k}A_{n}(x)\|)dx,\quad k=1,\cdots,s.

(1.0.1)

Unfortunately, these Lyapunov exponents are far from enough to classify the cocycles. In fact, even for cocycles with all Lyapunov exponents vanishing, the dynamics still have a variety of possibilities
[
19
,
40
]
.

1.1
Accelerations

A breakthrough came recently, with the establishment of the global theory of one-frequency analytic Schrödinger operators. Avila
[
4
]

classified one-frequency analytic
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
cocycles and proposed another dynamical invariant called "accelerations". Indeed, for any analytic cocycle
(\alpha,A)\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},{\rm GL}(s,\mathbb{C}))
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
A
can be holomorphically extended to
\{|\Im z|\leq\delta\}
. Let
A_{\epsilon}(x):=A(x+i\epsilon)
for
|\epsilon|<\delta
. We can then define the
accelerations
of
(\alpha,A)
as follows:

\omega^{k}:=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon}(L^{k}(\alpha,A_{\epsilon})-L^{k}(\alpha,A)),\qquad\omega_{k}:=\omega^{k}-\omega^{k-1}.

(1.1.1)

Furthermore, we define
(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{s})
as the
acceleration vector
of
(\alpha,A)
. The most important observation is that the accelerations are quantized
[
4
,
7
]
, namely that there exists
l\in\{1,\cdots,s\}
such that both
l\omega_{k}
and
l\omega^{k}
are integers.
Actually the accelerations are dynamical invariants as they remain invariant under conjugations. We recall that
(\alpha,A_{i})\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},G)
,
i=1,2
, are conjugate to each other, if there exists
B\in C^{\omega}(\chi_{G}\mathbb{T},G)
1
1

1

\chi_{G}\in\{1,2\}
. In particular, for
G=\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
,
\chi_{G}=1
.
such that

A_{1}(\cdot)=B(\cdot+\alpha)A_{2}(\cdot)B(\cdot)^{-1}.

With these notations, Avila’s classification of one-frequency analytic
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles is introduced
[
4
]
. If
A
is homotopic to the identity, or in other words, if the topological degree
d(A)=0
, then the dynamical behavior can be determined by the Lyapunov exponent and acceleration. To be precise, if
\omega_{1}=0
, then the cocycle is subcritical (resp. uniformly hyperbolic) when
L_{1}(\alpha,A)=0
(resp.
L_{1}(\alpha,A)>0
). On the contrary, if
\omega_{1}\neq 0
,
(\alpha,A)
is critical (resp. supercritical or non-uniformly hyperbolic) when
L_{1}(\alpha,A)=0
(resp.
L_{1}(\alpha,A)>0
). On the other hand, if the topological degree
d(A)\neq 0
, i.e.,
A\colon

\mathbb{T}\to{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is homotopic to
\theta\mapsto R_{d(A)\theta}
, where

R_{\theta}:=\begin{pmatrix}\cos 2\pi\theta&-\sin 2\pi\theta\\
\sin 2\pi\theta&\cos 2\pi\theta\end{pmatrix},

Avila-Krikorian
[
11
]
proved that if
(\alpha,A)
is
L^{2}
-conjugate to rotations, then it is
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to rotations. In conclusion, the classification of analytic
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles depends on three dynamical invariants: the topological degree, Lyapunov exponent, and acceleration.

The natural inquiry is whether this kind of classification can be generalized to high-dimensional cocycles. As an initial attempt, we consider this question for cocycles valued in
{\rm SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
,
s\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Note that if
A
takes values in
\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
, then
L_{k}(\alpha,A)=0
,
k=1,\cdots,s
. Hence the monotonicity on singular values therefore Lyapunov exponents provides that

\omega_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\omega_{s}.

In particular, for any
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
-cocycle, as determinant is identically
1
, we have

\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}=0.

As singular values
\lambda,\lambda^{-1}
come in pairs, we deduce that nonzero accelerations are in pairs
\omega,-\omega
. Hence the acceleration vector must be in the form

(\omega_{1},0,-\omega_{1}),

(1.1.2)

i.e., essentially has one variable. We find that, contrary to the
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
case, the acceleration determines the dynamics of analytic one-frequency
{\rm SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
cocycles.

Theorem A
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},{\rm SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
, then we have the following:

1.

\omega_{1}=0
if and only if
(\alpha,A)
is
C^{\omega}
-almost reducible, i.e., the closure of the analytic conjugate class of
(\alpha,A)
contains a constant cocycle.

2.

Assuming additionally that
\alpha\in\mathrm{RDC}
, if
\omega_{1}\neq 0
, then
(\alpha,A)
is
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to

(\alpha,\exp\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega_{1}x+c&0\\
-\omega_{1}x-c&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}),

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

We say
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
satisfies a
Diophantine condition
DC(
\kappa,\tau
) if
\kappa>0
and
\tau>0
, where

||q\alpha-p||_{\mathbb{Z}}>\frac{\kappa}{|q|^{\tau}},~(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},~q\neq 0,

where
||\cdot||_{\mathbb{Z}}
represents the distance to the nearest integer for a real number. Furthermore, we say that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
is
recurrent Diophantine
if there exist some
\gamma>0,\tau>1
such that
G^{n}(\alpha)\in\mathrm{DC}(\gamma,\tau)
for infinitely many
n
, where
G:(0,1)\rightarrow(0,1)
is the Gauss map defined by
G(x)=\{\frac{1}{x}\}
and
\{x\}
denotes the fractional part of
x
. Let
\mathrm{RDC}
be the set of recurrent Diophantine numbers, which has a full Lebesgue measure. We point out that the condition
\alpha\in\mathrm{RDC}
is used for technical reason. It would be interesting to generalize the result to all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
.

Theorem
A
provides a comprehensive understanding of analytic one-frequency
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles. This theorem is particularly interesting for several reasons. Firstly, Theorem
A
(1), generalizes Avila’s well-known Almost Reducibility Conjecture (ARC) to high-dimensional compact groups. The ARC states that if
(\alpha,A)\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}\times C^{\omega}

(\mathbb{T},{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
is subcritical, then it is analytically almost reducible. This conjecture has important implications for both dynamical and spectral aspects, as highlighted in various works
[
2
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
27
,
45
]
. Notably, our proof of the generalized ARC in Theorem
A
is completely different from the proof in the case of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})

[
2
,
3
]
. Our method is applicable to compact group, demonstrating that ARC holds for general compact groups. For the sake of conciseness, we present the result in the case of
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
.

Moreover, Theorem
A
provides classification for one-frequency analytic
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
-cocycle: all cocycles can be classified into almost reducible ones and non-almost-reducible ones,
since the normal form cocycle in Theorem
A
(2) is obviously not almost-reducible. Furthermore, Theorem
A
(1) states that almost reducibility holds for all irrational frequency (not merely
\mathrm{RDC}
as in
[
25
,
39
]
), which is a completely new result.

Finally, we would like to mention that for the case
\omega_{1}=0
, the result should hold for general compact groups. However, when
\omega_{1}\neq 0
, the rigidity result, as demonstrated in Theorem
A
(2), can only be expected in a weaken version (achieving by a sequence of conjugations) for general compact groups. This is due to extra difficulty solving cohomological equation, arising in the possibility of the matrix form of dynamical degree being degenerate and having a large centralizer. One can consult
[
47
]
for more discussions.

1.2
Dynamical Degree

As mentioned earlier, for
G=\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, where the fundamental group of
G
is nontrivial, the topological degree plays a significant role in dynamical classification. However, for
G=\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
,
s\geq 3
, the fundamental group is
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
, rendering the topological degree less applicable. In contrast, another degree called the "dynamical degree" can be defined and utilized. The concept of dynamical degree was initially proposed by Krikorian
[
39
]
and formally introduced by Frączek
[
25
]
for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-cocycles. It can be extended to any compact cocycles and general cocycles
L^{2}
-conjugate to rotations
[
12
,
34
,
47
]
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
A\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T},\text{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
,
s\geq 3
,
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
be the operator defined as following:

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R}))\longleftrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})),\quad Y\mapsto A^{-1}(\cdot)Y(\cdot+\alpha)A(\cdot),

and
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{j}
represent its
j
-th iteration. It is known that
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
is a unitary operator. According to von Neumann’s ergodic theorem, there exists an
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
-invariant
D\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T},\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R}))
, such that the following convergence holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}A_{n}(\cdot)^{-1}\partial A_{n}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}^{j}(A(\cdot)^{-1}\partial A(\cdot))\xrightarrow{L^{2}}D,

(1.2.1)

and
A(x)^{-1}D(x+\alpha)A(x)=D(x)
almost everywhere. We call any matrix
D
in the algebraic conjugate class the
dynamical degree
of
(\alpha,A)
. The dynamical degree is invariant under
C^{1}
-conjugation. Since
D\in\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})
, there exist
Q\in\text{SU}(s)
and
d_{1},\cdots,d_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
D
is conjugate to the diagonal form

D=Q\cdot 2\pi i\text{diag}\left(d_{1},\cdots,d_{s}\right)\cdot Q^{-1}.

Furthermore, notice that conjugation of
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(2,\mathbb{R})
on diagonal matrix acts like a transposition on diagonal elements, we can properly order the diagonal entries of
D
such that

d_{1}\geq\cdots\geq d_{s}.

We thus define the
vector of dynamical degree
to be

\vec{d}=(d_{1},\cdots,d_{s}).

We note that the dynamical degree is quantized (
d_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k=1,\cdots,s
), as proven in
[
12
,
47
]
.
Since acceleration is also quantized
[
4
,
7
]
, a natural question arises as to whether there is any relation between them. In fact, for analytic
\text{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles, they are the same.

Theorem B
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
. The acceleration vector of
(\alpha,A)
is the same as the vector of dynamical degree of
(\alpha,A)
.

As the vector of dynamical degree has integer entries, we deduce the following result.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
. The accelerations of
(\alpha,A)
are all integers.

We recall
[
7
, Theorem 1.4]
: when
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{s},\mathbb{C}^{s}))
, for
k=1,\cdots,s
, there exists an integer
1\leq l\leq s
, such that
lw_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Our improvement is essentially due to the compactness of
\text{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
: eigenvalues always have algebraic multiplicity
1
, which leads to
l=1
.

Consequently, the dynamical degree can be used to classify analytic one-frequency quasi-periodic
\text{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles. In particular, we can establish the relation between acceleration, dynamical degree, and almost reducibility.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
and
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

The dynamical degree is zero,

2.

The largest acceleration
\omega_{1}
is zero,

3.

(\alpha,A)
is
C^{\omega}
almost reducible.

1.3
More Histories and Comments

A
quasi-periodic linear system on a Lie group
G
(referred to as a linear system for short) is an ordinary differential equation on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times G
of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{x}=\tilde{A}(\theta)x,\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega,\end{cases}

(1.3.1)

where
\tilde{A}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
(
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
), and
\omega\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is rationally independent. Poincaré time-
1
map of (
1.3.1
) is in fact a quasi-periodic cocycle on
G
. Naturally, conjugation and in particular almost reducibility results of the corresponding cocycle is closely related to the ones of the original linear system
[
54
]
, cf. section
3.1
.

Let the frequency
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
be Diophantine and
A\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}))
. In 1975, Dinaburg-Sinai
[
18
]
used the KAM approach to prove that for a typical one-parameter family of cocycles close to a constant, reducible cocycles are of positive measure. Subsequently, Eliasson
[
19
]
made a significant breakthrough by proving, under the same assumption, that reducible cocycles are of full measure, through an essential improvement of the KAM approach. By adapting and improving Eliasson’s approach, Krikorian
[
37
]
extended the full-measure reducibility result in
[
19
]
to the cases of semi-simple compact Lie groups,
and was further generalized to
\mathrm{GL}(s,\mathbb{R})
cocycles quite recently by Wang-Xu-You-Zhou
[
50
]
.
All of these results hold for smooth or finitely differentiable cases, regardless of whether we consider linear systems or cocycles
[
1
,
16
,
22
,
38
]
.

However, the Diophantine condition on frequencies in local problems can be relaxed when considering one-frequency cocycles or two-frequency linear systems on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, as shown in
[
2
,
6
,
31
]
. Both of these articles developed non-standard KAM techniques to deal with conjugation problems. Although the method developed in
[
31
]
only applies to linear systems, the result still holds for cocycles due to the local embedding theorem
[
54
]
.

One may inquire whether any conclusions can be derived regarding conjugation for cocycles or linear systems that are not necessarily close to constants (referred to as the global case). In the case of one-frequency cocycles, global results can be anticipated.
In
[
40
]
, Krikorian proved a profound result stating that for a recurrent Diophantine frequency,
C^{\infty}
-reducible smooth cocycles valued in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
are
C^{\infty}
-dense. Moreover, Krikorian proved that for a recurrent Diophantine frequency, non-reducible smooth cocycles on
\mathrm{SU}(2)
can be divided into countable conjugation classes. In fact, the proof in
[
40
]
indicates the existence of a quantified conjugation-invariant quantity, which is precised later by Fraczek
[
25
]
. In the case of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, Avila-Krikorian
[
10
]
proved that given recurrent Diophantine frequency, for almost every energy, the corresponding Schrödinger cocycle is either
C^{\omega}
-reducible or non-uniformly hyperbolic, based on Kotani theory
[
44
,
48
]
and the renormalization scheme introduced in
[
40
]
.

Furthermore, several interesting results have been established in
[
34
,
35
,
36
,
12
]
, which include topics such as degree, reducibility, almost-reducibility, non-reducibility, and spectrum of one-frequency cocycles on semi-simple compact groups. Avila’s global theory and accelerations in
[
4
,
7
]
for analytic one-frequency cocycles provide new insights into understanding global conjugation problems.

It is important to note that most of the conclusions about cocycles on semi-simple compact groups, whether local or global, require Diophantine or recurrent Diophantine conditions. In this paper, we aim to examine the relations among accelerations, dynamical degree, and almost-reducibility for analytic cocycles over any irrational frequency. We firstly recall the convergence of renormalization proved in
[
12
,
47
]
. Then by proving the preservation of acceleration along renormalization and using the explicit form of limit of renormalization, we deduce Theorem
B
concerning coincidence of acceleration vector and vector of dynamical degree. As for Theorem
A
, if
\omega_{1}=0
, we conclude by showing a local almost-reducibility result over any irrational frequency. We prove such result using the KAM scheme developed by Hou-You
[
31
]
and the local embedding theorem
[
54
]
, which are applicable only to the analytic case. On the other hand, if
\omega_{1}\neq 0
, we prove conjugation result through an adapted KAM scheme developed by Krikorian
[
39
]
for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-cocycles. A technical point in the function of this KAM scheme is the estimates on length, which were developed in
[
39
,
34
]
for compact cocycles. Since accelerations can only be defined for analytic cocycles, Corollary
1.2
holds only for the analytic case.

Finally, let us mention that the dynamics of cocycles has received significant attention from researchers, with a focus on the conjugation problems and their classifications. These questions are not only important in the field of dynamical systems, but also in mathematical physics, where they have wider applications in the spectral theory of Schrödinger operators
[
2
,
4
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
27
,
45
,
53
]
.

2
Preliminary

2.1
Lie group and Lie algebra

Let

\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}):=\{X\in\mathrm{M}(s,\mathbb{R})~:~X^{T}X=XX^{T}=I\},

\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})
be the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
, namely

\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R}):=\{X\in\mathrm{M}(s,\mathbb{R})~:~X=-X^{T}\}.

For a matrix
D\in\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})
, its
centralizer

\mathfrak{z}(D)
is defined to be

\mathfrak{z}(D)=\{C\in\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})~|~DC=CD\}.

In this article, we mainly work on
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
. As for the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
, it has a basis

J_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},J_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&0\end{pmatrix},J_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&0\end{pmatrix},

satisfying

[J_{1},J_{2}]=J_{3},\quad[J_{2},J_{3}]=J_{1},\quad[J_{3},J_{1}]=J_{2}.

Then we have the following observation.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
D\in\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})\backslash\{0\}
, its centralizer is the
1
-dimensional linear subspace generated by
D
.

Besides, it is easy to see that for
A\in\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
, it has eigenvalue
\{||A||i,-||A||i,0\}
and there exists
B\in\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
such that

B^{T}AB=||A||J_{1}.

2.2
Continued fraction expansion

Define as usual for
0<\alpha<1
,

a_{0}=0,~~\alpha_{0}=\alpha,\quad q_{0}=1,\quad p_{0}=0,\quad q_{-1}=0,\quad p_{-1}=1,

and inductively for
n\geq 1
,

a_{n}=[\alpha_{n-1}^{-1}],~~\alpha_{n}=\alpha_{n-1}^{-1}-a_{n}=G(\alpha_{n-1})=\{\frac{1}{\alpha_{n-1}}\},

p_{n}=q_{n}p_{n-1}+p_{n-2},\quad q_{n}=a_{n}q_{n-1}+q_{n-2},

(2.2.1)

where
[\cdot]
denotes the integer part,
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part and
G
is the Gauss map
G(x)=\{x^{-1}\}
. Let

\beta_{n}=\Pi_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j},\quad U(x)=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&-[x^{-1}]\end{matrix}\right).

Define

Q_{0}=I,\quad Q_{n}=U(\alpha_{n-1})\cdots U(\alpha_{0}).

Then (
2.2.1
) shows that

Q_{n}=(-1)^{n}\left(\begin{matrix}q_{n}&p_{n}\\
q_{n-1}&p_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right).

And we have

\beta_{n}=(-1)^{n}(q_{n}\alpha-p_{n})=\frac{1}{q_{n+1}+\alpha_{n+1}q_{n}},

\frac{1}{q_{n+1}+q_{n}}<\beta_{n}<\frac{1}{q_{n+1}}.

2.3
Renormalization of actions and cocycles

\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-actions and renormalization of actions will be a fundamental tool in this paper. We recall here, following
[
10
]
,
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action and the scheme of renormalization introduced in
[
40
]
. In particular, we focus on analytic case for
\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})
,
s\geq 3
.

Denote by
\Omega^{\omega}=\mathbb{R}\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
the group composed of skew-product diffeomorphisms
(\alpha,A):\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}),

(\alpha,A)(x,v)=(x+\alpha,A(x)v),

(2.3.1)

with the composition being the group operation. An
analytic fibered
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action
is a homomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\Omega^{\omega}
. We use
\Lambda^{\omega}
to denote the space of analytic fibered
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-actions, endowed with the pointwise topology.

For two elements in
\Omega^{r}
,
(\alpha_{1},A_{1}),(\alpha_{2},A_{2})
, they form a
commuting pair
, denoted by
\begin{pmatrix}(\alpha_{1},A_{1})\\
(\alpha_{2},A_{2})\end{pmatrix}
, if they are commuting with each other, i.e.,

A_{1}(x+\alpha_{2})A_{2}(x)=A_{2}(x+\alpha_{1})A_{1}(x).

As
\Phi(1,0)
and
\Phi(0,1)
determine
\Phi
and commute with each other by definition, they form a commuting pair. In below, we will not distinguish an
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action
\Phi
and the corresponding commuting pair
(\begin{smallmatrix}\Phi(1,0)\\
\Phi(0,1)\end{smallmatrix})
.

For any
\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}
, we define
\gamma^{\Phi}_{n,m}:=\Pi_{1}\circ\Phi(n,m)\in\mathbb{R}
and
A^{\Phi}_{n,m}:=\Pi_{2}\circ\Phi(n,m)\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
for all
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, where

\Pi_{1}:\mathbb{R}\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Pi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))\to C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
are coordinate projections. Let
\Lambda_{0}^{\omega}
be the set of
\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}
with
\gamma_{1,0}^{\Phi}=1
.

Notice that
C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
acts on
\Omega^{\omega}
by

\mathrm{Conj}_{B}(\alpha,A(\cdot))=(\alpha,B(\cdot+\alpha)A(\cdot)B(\cdot)^{-1}).

This action extends to an action, still denoted
\mathrm{Conj}_{B}
, on
\Lambda^{\omega}
. For
\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}
, the
\mathrm{Conj}_{B}(\Phi)
is given by

\mathrm{Conj}_{B}(\Phi)(n,m)=(\gamma^{\Phi}_{n,m},B(\cdot+\gamma_{n,m}^{\Phi})A_{n,m}^{\Phi}(\cdot)B(\cdot)^{-1}),\quad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

We say that
\Phi,\Phi^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\omega}
are
C^{\omega}
-conjugate
via
B\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
if

\Phi^{\prime}=\mathrm{Conj}_{B}(\Phi)
holds.

We say that a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action
\Phi
is
normalized
if
\Phi(1,0)=(1,I)
. In this case,
\Phi(0,1)=(\alpha,A)
can be viewed as an analytic cocycle, since
A
is automatically
1
-periodic. Conversely, given an analytic cocycle
(\alpha,A)
, we associate a normalized action
\Phi_{\alpha,A}
by setting
\Phi_{\alpha,A}(1,0)=(1,I),~~\Phi_{\alpha,A}(0,1)=(\alpha,A).

We cite the normalizing lemma from
[
10
]
, whose proof remains true for
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-actions valued in compact groups. We will provide a quantitative version of it in section
5.2
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\Phi\in\Lambda_{0}^{\omega}
,
\Phi
is
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to a normalized action.

Let
\Phi\in\Lambda_{0}^{\omega}
,
B\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
such that
\mathrm{Conj}_{B}(\Phi)
is normalized. Then we call
B
a
normalizing map
of
\Phi
. The choice of
B
may not be unique.

To define renormalization, we start with
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-actions. We introduce the following maps.

1.

Let
\lambda\neq 0
. Define
rescaling

M_{\lambda}:\Lambda^{\omega}\to\Lambda^{\omega}
by

M_{\lambda}(\Phi)(n,m):=(\lambda^{-1}\gamma_{n,m}^{\Phi},A^{\Phi}_{n,m}(\lambda\cdot)),\quad\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}.

(2.3.2)

2.

Let
U\in\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Define
base change

U:\Lambda^{\omega}\to\Lambda^{\omega}
by

U(\Phi)(n,m)=\Phi(n^{\prime},m^{\prime}),~~\binom{n^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}}=U\cdot\binom{n}{m},\quad\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}.

(2.3.3)

We point out that these two operations are pairwise commutative. Besides,
U
commute with
\mathrm{Conj}_{B}
while
M_{\lambda}\circ\mathrm{Conj}_{B}=\mathrm{Conj}_{B(\lambda\cdot)}\circ M_{\lambda}.

For
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action
\Phi\in\Lambda^{\omega}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
n
-th renormalization of
\Phi
around
0
to be

\mathcal{R}^{n}(\Phi):=M_{\beta_{n-1}}\circ{Q_{n}}(\Phi).

(2.3.4)

Based on the renormalization of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action, we introduce renormalization of cocycles. For a
C^{r}
cocycle
(\alpha,A)
, we consider renormlization on the related
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-action
\Phi_{\alpha,A}
. We can also express renormalization explicitly by commuting pair:

\mathcal{R}^{n}(\Phi_{\alpha,A})=M_{\beta_{n-1}}\circ(-1)^{n}\begin{pmatrix}p_{n-1}&-p_{n}\\
-q_{n-1}&q_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(1,I)\\
(\alpha,A)\end{pmatrix},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

As
\mathcal{R}^{n}(\Phi_{\alpha,A})\in\Lambda_{0}^{\omega}
, by Lemma
2.2
, there exists
B\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
such that
\mathrm{Conj}_{B}\mathcal{R}^{n}(\Phi_{\alpha,A})
is normalized. We denote the commuting pair of this normalized
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
action by
\begin{pmatrix}(1,I)\\
(\alpha_{n},\tilde{A}^{(n)})\end{pmatrix}
. Since normalizing map is not unique,
\tilde{A}^{(n)}
is not unique either. We call
(\alpha_{n},\tilde{A}^{(n)})
a
representative of the
n
-th renormalization
of
(\alpha,A)
.

A fundamental observation is that the conjugation relation is invariant under renormalizations. More precisely, we have the following result. The proof is relatively direct by definition, we refer to
[
47
, Lemma 4.4.1, Lemma 4.4.2]
for example.

Lemma 2.3
.

1. Representative of renormalization is unique up to conjugation.

2. Two cocycles are conjugate to each other if and only if they have conjugate representatives of renormalization.

Now we talk about the convergence of renormalization. As compact cocycle is a special case of cocycles
L^{2}
-conjugation to rotation, by
[
12
,
47
]
, we have the following results.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
,
A\in C^{\omega}_{h}(\mathbb{T},\mathrm{SO}(s,\mathbb{R}))
. There exist
D\in\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\exp(D)=I
, a sequence of representatives of renormalization
(\alpha_{n},\tilde{A}^{(n)})
,
C_{n}\in\mathfrak{z}(D)
,
\varphi_{n}\in C_{h}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T},\mathfrak{so}(s,\mathbb{R}))
such that

\tilde{A}^{(n)}(x)=\exp(\varphi_{n}(x))\exp((-1)^{n}Dx+C_{n}),

(2.3.5)

satisfying the estimate

\lim_{n\to\infty}||\varphi_{n}||_{h}=0.

This means representatives of renormalization
(\alpha_{n},\tilde{A}^{(n)})
converges to a precise normal form. This kind of normal form result was firstly stated for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
-cocycles in the
C^{\infty}
-topology
[
39
,
25
]
, and then for
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
-cocycles in the
C^{\infty}
and
C^{\omega}
-topology
[
10
,
11
]
. It is also generalized to quasi-periodic coycles valued in semi-compact Lie groups in the
C^{\infty}
-topology
[
34
]
.

3
Local results I. close to constant

To prove the conjugation result in Theorem
A
, by the preservation of conjugation along renormalization, it can be achieved by conjugating renormalizations. Moreover, by the convergence of renormalization given in Theorem
2.1
, we will fall into a neighborhood of some normal form after deep enough renormalization. Therefore we only need to study conjugation in local case, namely close to some normal form
(\alpha,\exp(Dx+C))
, where
\exp(D)=I
,
c\in\mathfrak{z}(D)
. We will deal with
D=0
in this section and

in the next section.

In this section, we will prove that any one-frequency
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
-cocycle close to constant is
C^{\omega}
-almost-reducible.

We start with introducing some notations. Let
h>0
,
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. We define the strip

We denote by
C_{h}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}^{d},*)
the space of all maps

admitting an analytic extension on
\mathbb{T}_{h}^{d}
, equipped with the norm

\|F\|_{h}:=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{h}}\|F(\theta)\|,

where
*
could be
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
,
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
or
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
.
Then

becomes the set of all analytic
*
-valued function on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Our main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Q}
,
h>0
,
L\geq 1
,
C\in\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
. There exists
\delta=\delta(h,L)

is
C^{\omega}
-almost-reducible, in the sense that, there exist

,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|A_{n}-\exp(C_{n})\|_{h_{n}}\|B_{n}\|_{h_{n}}^{L}=0,

(3.0.1)

for

and some

,

.

Remark 3.1
.

The perturbation

does not depend on the frequency
\alpha
. Thus the result is of semi-local nature

]

3.1
Change to almost reducibility of linear systems

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

. Consider the linear system

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=A(\theta)x\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega.\end{cases}

(3.1.1)

We say that

conjugates (

) to another linear system

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=A_{+}(\theta)x\\

for some

, if

\partial_{\omega}B(\theta)=B(\theta)A(\theta)-A_{+}(\theta)B(\theta),

where

\partial_{\omega}B(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}):=\omega_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}B(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})+\omega_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{2}}B(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}),\quad\text{for }\omega=(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

Denote by

the basic solution (which is in fact unique) of (

Let
\Phi_{+}
be the basic solution of (
3.1.2
). Then
B
conjugating (
3.1.1
) to (
3.1.2
) is equivalent to

B(\theta+t\omega)\Phi(t,\theta)B(0,\theta)^{-1}=\Phi_{+}(t,\theta).

(3.1.3)

We claim the following almost reducibility result for linear system, whose proof will be given later.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)\backslash\mathbb{Q}
,
\omega=(\alpha,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

and

, there exists

such that for any

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=(C+F(\theta))x\\

(3.1.4)

is
C^{\omega}
-almost-reducible, in the sense that, there exist
h_{n}>0
,
B_{n}\in C_{h_{n}}^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, such that
B_{n}
conjugates
(
3.1.4
) to

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=(C_{n}+F_{n}(\theta))x\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega,\end{cases}

(3.1.5)

for some

,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and the following estimate holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|F_{n}\|_{h_{n}}\|B_{n}\|_{h_{n}}^{L}=0.

(3.1.6)

To show that we can deduce Theorem
3.1
from Theorem
3.2
directly, we need to use in addition the following local embedding theorem proved in

]
. It was proved for any Lie group

Theorem 3.3

(
[
54
]
, Local embedding theorem)
.

Let

,

,

rationally

, (

) is

-almost-reducible, i.e., there exist

,

) to

(3.1.11)

for some

3.2
Proof of Theorem
3.2

In this subsection, we will introduce an alternative analytic space and norm to work with. We will also introduce an iteration scheme, by applying which we will be able to prove Theorem
3.2
. The proof of the iteration scheme will be given in the next subsection.

3.2.1
Spaces and Norms

To make the future estimates simpler, we introduce an alternative analytic space and norm. For
h>0
, we define
\mathcal{B}_{h}(*)
as the space of functions
F\in C^{\omega}(\mathbb{T}^{2},*)
(
*
can be
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
,
\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
) satisfying

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}||\hat{F}(k)||e^{2\pi|k|h}<\infty
, where
|k|:=|k_{1}|+|k_{2}|
for
k=(k_{1},k_{2})
, equipped with the norm

||F||^{\#}_{h}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}||\hat{F}(k)||e^{2\pi|k|h}<\infty.

It follows immediately that for any
F,G\in\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
, we have
FG\in\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
, and

||FH||^{\#}_{h}\leq||F||^{\#}_{h}||H||^{\#}_{h}.

Sometimes, for an analytic matrix valued function, we need to consider the relations between its analytic norm and the analytic norms of its entry functions. As for
\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
case, we obviously have the conclusion

\|\sum_{s=1}^{3}f_{s}J_{s}\|^{\#}_{h}=\left(\sum_{s=1}^{3}(\|f_{s}\|^{\#}_{h})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\forall f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathbb{R}),\,h>0.

(3.2.1)

For any
N>0
, we define the operators
\mathcal{T}_{N}
,
\mathcal{R}_{N}
on
\mathcal{B}_{h}(*)
as

(\mathcal{T}_{N}F)(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|<N}\hat{F}(k)e^{2\pi i<k,\theta>},\quad(\mathcal{R}_{N}F)(\theta):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},|k|\geq N}\hat{F}(k)e^{2\pi i<k,\theta>}.

Then
\mathcal{T}_{N}+\mathcal{R}_{N}=I
. And for any
0<h^{\prime}<h
, since

The spaces

And for any

,

(3.2.4)

Therefore

3.3
Proof of iteration scheme

In this subsection, we construct the iteration scheme. We will firstly introduce a nice subspace of
\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R}))
in the sense of conjugation, then give the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

For simplicity of notation, we denote
\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R}))
briefly by
\mathcal{B}_{h}
. Without lose of generality, we assume that

C=2\pi\rho J_{1},\qquad\rho\in\mathbb{R}.

3.3.1
A nice subspace of
\mathcal{B}_{h}

For
F\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, to construct
B\in\mathcal{B}_{h}(\mathrm{SO}(3,\mathbb{R}))
so that for some
C_{+}\in\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
and a smaller
F_{+}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,
B
conjugating
\begin{cases}\dot{x}=(C+F(\theta))x\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega\end{cases}
to
\quad\begin{cases}\dot{x}=(C_{+}+F_{+}(\theta))x\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega\end{cases}
, it is sufficient to solve

\partial_{\omega}B=B(C+F)-(C_{+}+F_{+})B.

(3.3.1)

If
B
is close to the identity, namely
B=e^{Y}
with
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
small, then (
3.3.1
) provides a linearized equation

\partial_{\omega}Y-[C,Y]=F+D,

(3.3.2)

for some constant
D\in\mathfrak{so}(3,\mathbb{R})
. However, when solving (
3.3.2
), one may encounter the small divisor problem, i.e., the liner operator

\mathcal{B}_{h}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{h},\quad Y\mapsto\partial_{\omega}Y-[C,Y],

(3.3.3)

may be not invertible or invertible but with an inverse operator of too large norm.

As studied in
[
31
,
32
]
, a key observation is that there exists a subspace
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)}
of
\mathcal{B}_{h}
, satisfying the property that for any
Y\in\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)}
, one has

\partial_{\omega}Y,\,[C,Y]\in\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)},

(3.3.4)

and the estimate holds

||\partial_{\omega}Y-[C,Y]||_{h}^{\#}\geq\eta||Y||^{\#}_{h},

(3.3.5)

for a uniform constant
\eta>0
. In other word, the restricted linear operator (
3.3.3
) on
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)}
behaves well. Therefore, we are able to solve (
3.3.2
) in
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)}
. To formulate the result precisely, we decompose the space

\mathcal{B}_{h}
into a direct sum
\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(nre)}\oplus\mathcal{B}_{h}^{(re)}
.

(
[

,

]

,

,

satisfying the estimates

Remark 3.2
.

\cdot\in\mathfrak{z}(D)
is preserved by renormalization. Hence there exists
C\in\mathfrak{z}(D)
such that
(\alpha,\exp(Dx+C))
has a representative of
n
th renormalization as
(\alpha_{n},\exp((-1)^{n}Dx+C^{\prime}))
.

In other word,
(\alpha,A)
has a representative of
n
th renormalization
(\alpha_{n},\tilde{A}^{(n)})
conjugate to
(\alpha_{n},\exp((-1)^{n}Dx+C^{\prime}))
, a representative of
n
th renormalization of
(\alpha,\exp(Dx+C))
. Then Lemma
2.3
gives that
(\alpha,A)
is
C^{\omega}
-conjugate to
(\alpha,\exp(Dx+C))
. Hence we complete the proof of Theorem
A
.
∎

Lemma A.1
.

which

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=F(\theta)x\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega=(\alpha,1)\end{cases}

\begin{cases}\dot{x}=Cx\\
\dot{\theta}=\omega=(\alpha,1)\end{cases}

with estimates

||B||_{h}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{(|q|+|p|)h}{|\tau|}||F||^{\#}_{h}\right\},\qquad\tau:=q\alpha-p.

(A.0.1)

Proof.

Let
x=q\theta_{1}-p\theta_{2},\tilde{h}=(|q|+|p|)h
,

(A.0.2)

\Phi(t)

\frac{1}{|\tau|}
-periodic ODE

\frac{dx}{dt}=G(\tau t)x

t+is
