Quantum speed limits for an open system in contact with a thermal bath Preprint: APS/123-QED
N. Il’in 1 , A. Aristova 2 , O. Lychkovskiy 1,3

Abstract
We prove fundamental rigorous bounds on the speed of quantum evolution for a quantum system coupled to a thermal bath. The bounds are formulated in terms of expectation values of few-body observables derived from the system-bath Hamiltonian. They do not rely on the Markov approximation and, as a consequence, are applicable beyond the limit of weak system-bath coupling.



I
Introduction

A Quantum Speed Limit (QSL) is an upper bound on the speed of evolution of a quantum state. The first QSL was put forward by Mandelstam and Tamm (MT)
Mandelstam and Tamm 1945
(see also
Fleming 1973
), who enquired into a rigorous formulation of the time-energy uncertainty relation. A large variety of QSLs have been discovered since then, as reviewed e.g. in
Pfeifer and Fröhlich 1995
;
Dodonov and Dodonov 2015
;
Frey 2016
;
Deffner and Campbell 2017
. QSLs have conceptual importance as rigorous analogs of the quantum uncertainty principle in the time-energy domain. They emerge in various branches of quantum science, including optimal control theory
Caneva et al. 2009
, quantum resource theory
Campaioli et al. 2022
, and abstract quantum information theory
Deffner 2020
. QSLs are deeply interrelated with the orthogonality catastrophe, adiabatic conditions, and adiabatic quantum computations
Lychkovskiy et al. 2017
;
Il’in and Lychkovskiy 2020
;
Lychkovskiy 2018
;
Kieu 2019
;
Suzuki and Takahashi 2020
;
Fogarty et al. 2020
;
Zhu et al. 2022
. They bound ultimate performance of classical and quantum computers
Margolus and Levitin 1998
;
Lloyd 2000
;
Svozil et al. 2005
;
Santos and Sarandy 2015
, heat engines
Del Campo et al. 2014
;
Abah and Lutz 2017
, thermometers
Campbell et al. 2018
;
Sekatski and Perarnau-Llobet 2021
and even batteries
Campaioli et al. 2017
;
Allan et al. 2021
.

QSLs can be particularly useful when dealing with many-body dynamics: They promise simple estimates on the rate of change of a quantum state where addressing this change exactly is not feasible. Importantly, to fulfill this promise, a QSL should be at least finite in the thermodynamic limit. Unfortunately, this is often not the case for the MT QSL and several other popular QSLs, as observed e.g. in
Bukov et al. 2019
. However, several more recent QSLs do meet this requirement
Mondal et al. 2016
;
Il’in and Lychkovskiy 2020
;
Il’in and Lychkovskiy 2021
;
Shiraishi and Saito 2021
;
Albeverio and Motovilov 2022a
;
Albeverio and Motovilov 2022b
;
Hamazaki 2022
.

The goal of the present paper is to derive
practical
QSLs for an open quantum system coupled to a large thermal bath. We will detail what can be considered to be “practical” in Section
II
. At this point, we just stipulate that a practical QSL should, at least, (i) be finite in the limit of an infinitely large bath and (ii) avoid expectation values of nonlocal observables.

The above goal has been pursued previously in refs.
del Campo et al. 2013
;
Kobayashi and Yamamoto 2020
. In these articles, practical QSLs have been derived in the setting where the open system dynamics is described by the Lindblad equation. The latter has its applicability limits, most importantly – the assumption of Markovianity that is usually justified in the limit of a weak system-bath coupling
Rivas and Huelga 2012
;
Mozgunov and Lidar 2020
;
Trushechkin 2021
.

In the present paper we take a different approach – we derive practical QSLs based on the underlying composite Hamiltonian that describes the system, the bath and the system-bath interaction. As a consequence, the derived QSLs are universally valid for arbitrary system-bath couplings.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
After setting the stage in Section
II
, we present in Section
III
a QSL by Mondal, Datta, and Sazim (MDS)
Mondal et al. 2016
which is the starting point for our study. This QSL typically remains finite in the limit of a large bath; however, it involves, in general, nonlocal observables and thus is not practical as such. We propose three ways to reduce it to more practical bounds. The most straightforward one is presented in Section
IV
. In Section
V
we develop a more refined approach that explicitly exploits the special structure of the thermal state of the bath. In Section
VI
we derive practical QSLs for baths consisting of noninteracting bosons or spins. In Sections
VII
and
VIII
we consider two illustrative examples. Most proofs are relegated to Appendix
A
. In Appendix
B
we present improvements of the thermal adiabatic condition derived in ref.
Il‘in et al. 2021
and a QSL derived in ref.
Il’in and Lychkovskiy 2021
. These improvements are based on mathematical insights gained in the present work.

II
Setting the stage

We consider the dynamics of a quantum system with the Hamiltonian
H^{S}_{t}
that is coupled to the
external bath with the Hamiltonian
H^{B}
via the coupling term
H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t}
. The total Hamiltonian of the system and the bath is therefore

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle H_{t}=H^{S}_{t}+H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t}+H^{B}.\end{array}

(1)

Note that the system Hamiltonian and the system-bath coupling can be time-dependent, while the bath Hamiltonian does not change with time.

The quantum state of the system and the bath is described by the density matrix
\rho_{t}
that evolves according to the von Neumann equation,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=[H_{t},\rho_{t}].\end{array}

(2)

We assume that at
t=0
this density matrix is initialized in a tensor product state,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\rho^{S}_{\rm in}\,\rho^{B}_{\beta},\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\rho^{B}_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H^{B}}/Z_{B},\qquad Z_{B}={\rm tr}\,e^{-\beta H^{B}}.\end{array}

(3)

Here the initial state of the bath,
\rho^{B}_{\beta}
, is thermal, while the initial state of the system,
\rho^{S}_{\rm in}
, is arbitrary (mixed or pure).

Thermal baths are typically large. Therefore, we require that a practical QSL does not diverge in the thermodynamic limit of the bath.

The second practical requirement is to avoid expectation values of nonlocal

Of course, these expectation values should be calculated with respect to the initial state

. In particular, this is often the case in control theory, where a desired

is

and one wishes to design a Hamiltonian that ensures this

Such expectation values can emerge from the commutation of

At the same time, we do not consider the thermodynamic limit for the system. We have in mind mostly systems of moderate sizes, such as single qubits or qudits. For this reason, we regard arbitrary operations over
\rho^{S}_{\rm in}
feasible and thus practical. In particular, we will employ the square root
\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}

\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}

We remark that a number of QSLs exist that apply to an open system whose evolution is described by an explicitly given quantum channel
Taddei et al. 2013

del Campo et al. 2013

Deffner and Lutz 2013
. It should be emphasized that they are very different in scope from the bounds derived in the present paper. Obtaining a quantum channel for the system
S

1
) is, in general, a prohibitively complex problem, therefore such QSLs can not be immediately applied in the setting under consideration.

To quantify the speed of evolution of a quantum state, one needs to define a metric on the space of quantum states. A number of such metrics are known
Audenaert 2014
. We choose the Hellinger distance defined as
Luo and Zhang 2004

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle D(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=1-{\rm tr}(\sqrt{\rho_{1}}\sqrt{\rho_{2}}).\end{array}

(4)

It is well suited for quantum state discrimination and relates by two-sided inequalities to other popular measures of quantum state distinguishability
Holevo 1972
;
Luo and Zhang 2004
;
Audenaert 2014
.

We note that often one requires a QSL to be
saturable
, which means the existence of an initial state that saturates the corresponding bound. Typically, a state saturating a QSL is a superposition of a pair of eigenstates of the Hamiltonian, see e.g.
Frey 2016
. A distinctive feature of QSLs derived in the present paper is that they apply to a restricted set of very different initial states of the form (
3
). It is therefore of no surprise that they are not saturated except trivial cases of no dynamics (i.e.
D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})=0
).

III
Mondal-Datta-Sazim QSL

All QSLs derived in the present paper are based on the MDS QSL
Mondal et al. 2016
which reads

\displaystyle D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 1-\cos\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\,I_{t^{\prime}}}\,d{t^{\prime}}\right),

(5)

where

\displaystyle I_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm tr}\left(-[H^{S}_{t}+H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t},\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}\sqrt{\rho^{B}_{\beta}}]^{2}\right)

(6)

is the Wigner-Yanase skew information
Wigner and Yanase 1963
. The inequality (
5
) is valid for times
t\in[0,t_{\rm max}]
, where
t_{\rm max}
is the single root of the equation

\int_{0}^{t_{\rm max}}\sqrt{2\,I_{t^{\prime}}}\,d{t^{\prime}}=\frac{\pi}{2}.

(7)

For this time interval, an upper bound on the skew information entails an upper bound on the Hellinger distance, i.e.
I_{t}\leqslant x_{t}
implies
D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})\leqslant 1-\cos\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{2\,x_{t^{\prime}}}\,d{t^{\prime}}\right)
for
t\in[0,t_{\rm max}]
. Therefore we will report our results in the form of upper bounds on
I_{t}
. Note that whenever the Hamiltonian does not depend on time,
t_{\rm max}=\pi/\sqrt{8I}
, where
I
is the Wigner-Yanase skew information that also does not depend on time.

When expanding the square of the commutator in the right hand side (r.h.s.) of eq. (
5
), one obtains a term

{\rm tr}\Big(\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}\,H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t^{\prime}}\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}e^{-\beta H^{B}/2}H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t^{\prime}}e^{\beta H^{B}/2}\rho^{B}_{\beta}\Big)

that contains the operator

e^{-\beta H^{B}/2}\,H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t^{\prime}}\,e^{\beta H^{B}/2}.

(8)

In general, this is a highly nonlocal operator
Avdoshkin and Dymarsky 2020
whose thermal expectation value can be neither effectively calculated nor measured in a realistic experiment. For this reason, the MDS QSL (
5
) should be deemed impractical in the considered setting.

Our strategy is to derive bounds based on the MDS QSL (
5
) that lack the above drawback. In the following three sections, we pursue three different approaches to this task.

Note that the bath Hamiltonian
H^{B}
drops from the commutator in the MDS QSL (
5
) (for a simple reason – since
H^{B}
commutes with
\rho_{0}
, see Appendix
A
).
H^{B}
is extensive in the bath size, therefore its elimination is instrumental for keeping the MDS QSL (as well as bounds it will be reduced to) finite in the thermodynamic limit.

There are two special cases when the operator (
8
) is local. The first one is when the bath is noninteracting, which is considered in Section
VI
. The second one is when the bath temperature is infinite,
\beta\rightarrow 0
. This is an instructive limit that will be considered when comparing QSLs derived in what follows.

For systems with finite Hilbert spaces, it is useful to additionally check the obtained QSLs in a trivial limit

\rho^{S}_{\rm in}\sim\mathbb{1},\qquad\beta\rightarrow 0.

(9)

If the bath has a finite Hilbert space, the von Neumann equation (
2
) has a trivial solution
\rho_{t}=\rho_{0}\sim\mathbb{1}
in this limit, and thus
D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})=0
. Obviously, this result is reproduced by the MDS QSL (
5
).
Although for baths with infinite Hilbert spaces the limit can be more subtle mathematically, we still expect the same result on physical grounds.

Note that it is the full quantum state of the system and the bath whose evolution is bounded by the inequality (
5
). All other QSLs presented in what follows share this feature. Due to the contractivity of the Hellinger distance with respect to the partial trace,
D({\rm tr}_{B}\rho_{0},{\rm tr}_{B}\rho_{t})\leq D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})

Luo and Zhang 2004
, the same QSLs hold for the reduced state of the system alone.

IV
A simple relaxation of MDS QSL

Since nonlocal operators in the MDS QSL (
5
) appear due to the commutator, a straightforward way to avoid them is to get rid of the commutator altogether. This can be done by using a simple inequality

{\rm tr}\big(-[A,\sqrt{\rho}]^{2}\big)\leqslant 2\,{\rm tr}(A-c)^{2}\rho

(10)

valid for an arbitrary real number
c
, quantum state
\rho
and self-adjoint operator
A
. Applying this inequality to the MDS QSL (
5
) we get

I_{t}\leqslant{\rm tr}\Big((H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t}+H^{S}_{t}-c_{t})^{2}\,\rho^{S}_{\rm in}\,\rho^{B}_{\beta}\Big),

(11)

A real function
c_{t}
is, in general, arbitrary and can be chosen to optimize the bound. Alternatively, one can simply take
c_{t}={\rm tr}(H^{\mathrm{int}}_{t}+H^{S}_{t})\rho_{0}
– we expect that normally this will be a good choice. This is corroborated by considering a specific example in Section
VII
, where this choice of
c_{t}
is employed.

Let us discuss the properties of the QSL (
11
). First of all, this simple bound lacks any nonlocal operators and, instead, contains only expectation values of few-body observables. Second, as long as the interaction Hamiltonian does not diverge with the bath size (which we assume), the bound is manifestly finite in the limit of infinitely large bath. Thus the QSL (
11
) fulfills our practicability requirements.

An advantage of the QSL (
11
) is its simple form. However, this simplicity comes at a cost: the bound can be quite loose, particularly at high bath temperatures. For example, this is evident in the trivial limit (
9
): the correct result
D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})=0
is not, in general, captured by the QSL (
11
). Still, in other cases the QSL (
11
) performs quite well, as will be exemplified in Section
VII
.

We note that the QSL (
11
) is similar to a relaxed version of the QSL (6) from ref.
Shiraishi and Saito 2021
which reads
{\cal L}(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})\leq t\|H^{\mathrm{int}}+H^{S}\|

Shiraishi and Saito 2021
, where
{\cal L}(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})
is the Bures angle and
\|...\|
is the operator norm. The latter QSL, however, is not applicable when the norm is infinite, as is often the case for bosonic systems or reservoirs.

V
QSLs explicitly depending on temperature

Here we present two QSLs that exploit the special structure of the bath thermal state and explicitly depend on temperature. They read

\displaystyle I_{t}\leqslant

\displaystyle\,\frac{\beta^{2}}{8}{\rm tr}\Big(-\big\{[H^{\rm int}_{t},H^{B}],\rho^{S}_{\rm in}\big\}\,[H^{\rm int}_{t},H^{B}]\,\rho^{B}_{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle+{\rm tr}\big(-[H^{S}_{t}+H^{\rm int}_{t},\sqrt{\rho^{S}_{\rm in}}]^{2}\,\rho^{B}_{\beta}\big)

(12)

and

I_{t}\leqslant\frac{1}{8}{\rm tr}\big(-[H^{S}_{t}+H^{\rm int}_{t},\,\beta H^{B}-\log\rho^{S}_{\rm in}]^{2}\,\rho^{S}_{\rm in}\,\rho^{B}_{\beta}\big).

(13)

In eq. (
12
),
\{\dots,\dots\}
stands for the anticommutator.

We remind that the proofs are presented in Appendix
A
. A technique developed in refs.
Il‘in et al. 2021
;
Il’in and Lychkovskiy 2021
is employed there. This technique allows one to replace commutators with
\sqrt{\rho^{B}_{\beta}}
in the MDS QSL (
5
) by commutators with
H^{B}
. This way the above bounds get rid of nonlocal operators, as desired.

Note that while
H^{B}
has reappeared in the bounds, it enters the bounds only through the commutator
[H^{\rm int}_{t^{\prime}},H^{B}]
. Typically, such commutator is an intensive quantity, and thus the bounds remain finite in the thermodynamic limit of the bath. This intuition is supported by considering specific models, see Sections (
VII
) and (
VIII
).

Observe that in the trivial limit (
9
) both inequalities (
12
) and (
13
) reproduce the correct result
D(\rho_{0},\rho_{t})=0
, in contrast to the QSL (
11
).

VI
QSLs for noninteracting baths

A noninteracting bath is a collection of noninteracting bosons, fermions, or spins. E.g. a noninteracting bosonic bath has the Hamiltonian

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle H^{B}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k},\end{array}

e^{-\beta H^{B}/2}\,\,b_{k}^{\dagger}\,e^{\beta H^{B}/2}=e^{-\beta\omega_{k}/2}\,\,b_{k}^{\dagger}

(15)

VII
Example 1: spin-boson model

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{p}=\ln\sqrt{\frac{1+p}{1-p}}.\end{array}

(33)

Finally, the exact value of the Wigner-Yanase information (
17
) reads

I_{t}=\Delta^{2}B_{p}^{2}(1-n^{2}_{z})+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\beta^{2}}K_{\beta}-\\
-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\beta^{2}}L_{\beta}(1-4B_{p}^{2}(1-n^{2}_{x})).

(34)

For the specific spectral density (
23
) one can calculate
K_{\beta}
,
M_{\beta}
and
L_{\beta}
explicitly:

K_{\beta}=2\psi^{(1)}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}},\quad M_{\beta}=2\psi^{(3)}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)-6\frac{\beta^{4}}{\alpha^{4}},\\
L_{\beta}=\psi^{(1)}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(35)

where

\psi^{(m)}(z)
is the polygamma function of order
m
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\psi^{(m)}(z)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{m}e^{-zt}}{1-e^{-t}}dt=\frac{d^{m+1}}{dz^{m+1}}\ln\Gamma(z),\end{array}

(36)

and
\Gamma(z)
is the Euler gamma function.

Using the above formulae, we compare the four QSLs. Several illustrative plots are presented in Fig.
1
. Of course, the QSL (
5
) with the Wigner-Yanase information given by eq. (
17
) is never outperformed by QSLs (

), (

), (

). This is because the later are different relaxations of the former. We emphasize, however, that for interacting baths the analog of eq. (
17
) is not available, and one is left with the three remaining ones. Fig.

illustrates that each of them can outperform the other two for certain bath temperatures and system initial states.

One can see from Fig.

11

). Furthermore, it turns out to coincide with the QSL (

13

IX
Summary and concluding remarks

In summary, we have proved quantum speed limits (
11
), (
12
), (
13
) for open systems in contact with thermal baths. They constitute different relaxations of the MDS QSL (
5
)
Mondal et al. 2016
. Furthermore, we explicitly computed the MDS bound (
5
) in cases of noninteracting bosonic and spin baths, see eqs. (
17
) (
20
). In contrast to prior knowledge, these QSLs
do not rest on Markovian or other approximations and are applicable irrespective of the strength and time dependence of the system-bath couplings. Importantly, all reported bounds are practical when applied to many-body baths, i.e. they do not diverge in the thermodynamic limit and contain only local quantities that can be calculated or measured.

The MDS QSL (
5
), (
17
), (
20
) provides the tightest bounds for noninteracting baths but is not applicable to interacting baths. The QSLs (
11
), (
12
), (
13
) are applicable for interacting baths and complement each other in different regimes: the QSL (
11
) is typically the best for pure initial states of the system or low bath temperatures, while QSLs (
12
) and (
13
) perform better for mixed initial states of the system and sufficiently large bath temperatures.

The bounds reported in the present paper allow one to quickly estimate the evolution speed of open quantum systems coupled to thermal reservoirs, particularly in the non-Markovian regime where solving the dynamical equations exactly can pose considerable difficulties. This can be useful in various applications, including the dissipative error correction
Cohen and Mirrahimi 2014
;
Reiter et al. 2017
;
De Neeve et al. 2022
, dissipative entanglement generation
Krauter et al. 2011
;
Martin-Cano et al. 2011
;
Cole et al. 2022
, dissipation-enhanced quantum sensing
Checinska et al. 2015
;
Reiter et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2017
;
Raghunandan et al. 2018
, structured reservoir engineering
Lütkenhaus et al. 1998
;
Schirmer and Wang 2010
;
Roy-Choudhury and Hughes 2015
;
González-Tudela and Cirac 2017
;
Basilewitsch et al. 2019

etc
. Another potential application of the reported results is to provides rigorous benchmarks for various computational methods addressing non-Markovian quantum dynamics.
