On hyperbolicity and Gevrey well-posedness. Part two: Scalar or degenerate transitions
Baptiste Morisse

Abstract
For first-order quasi-linear systems of partial differential equations, we formulate an assumption of a transition from initial hyperbolicity to ellipticity. This assumption bears on the principal symbol of the first-order operator. Under such an assumption, we prove a strong Hadamard instability for the associated Cauchy problem, namely an instantaneous defect of Hölder continuity of the flow from G σ G^{\sigma} to L 2 L^{2} , with 0 < σ < σ 0 0<\sigma<\sigma_{0} , the limiting Gevrey index σ 0 \sigma_{0} depending on the nature of the transition. We restrict here to scalar transitions, and non-scalar transitions in which the boundary of the hyperbolic zone satisfies a flatness condition. As in our previous work for initially elliptic Cauchy problems [B. Morisse, On hyperbolicity and Gevrey well-posedness. Part one: the elliptic case , arXiv:1611.07225], the instability follows from a long-time Cauchy-Kovalevskaya construction for highly oscillating solutions. This extends recent work of N. Lerner, T. Nguyen, and B. Texier [ The onset of instability in first-order systems , to appear in J. Eur. Math. Soc.].

Introduction

We consider the following Cauchy problem, for first-order quasi-linear systems of partial differential equations:

\partial_{t}u=\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}(t,x,u)\partial_{x_{j}}u+f(t,x,u)\;,\qquad u(0,x)=h(x).

(1.1)

The system is of size
N
, that is
u(t,x)
and
f(t,x,u)
are in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and the
A_{j}(t,x,u)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
. The time
t
is nonnegative, and
x
is in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We assume throughout the paper that the
A_{j}
and
f
are analytic in a neighborhood of some point
(0,x_{0},u_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{t}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{x}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{u}
.

Under assumptions of weak defects of hyperbolicity for the first-order operator, we prove ill-posedness of (
1.1
) in Gevrey spaces. Weak defect of hyperbolicity is here understood as a transition from hyperbolicity of the principal symbol at initial time, to ellipticity of the principal symbol for later times. Our results extend Métivier’s ill-posedness theorem in Sobolev spaces for initially elliptic operators
[
Mét05
]
, our own ill-posedness result in Gevrey spaces for initially elliptic operators
[
Mor16
]
, Lerner, Nguyen and Texier’s theorem on systems transitioning from hyperbolicity to ellipticity
[
LNT17
]
, and echo Lu’s construction of WKB profiles
[
Lu16
]
which are destabilized by terms not present in the initial data.

Our proofs use Métivier’s method developed in
[
Mét05
]
based on majoring series, hence the assumption of analyticity for the
A_{j}
and
f
. Our assumptions of weak defects of hyperbolicity mean that the operator in (
1.1
) experiences a transition in time from hyperbolicity to non hyperbolicity. The transition is possibly not uniform in space. Our assumptions bearing on the principal symbol, and the associated normal forms, are presented in Section
2
. Our results are Theorems
2.11
and
2.12
, stated in Section
2.4
. The proofs comprise Sections
3
to
5
.

In the companion paper
[
Mor17b
]
, we consider the case of genuinely non-scalar transitions.

1.1
Background

1.1.1
A long-time Cauchy-Kovaleveskaya result for elliptic Cauchy problems

Our article
[
Mor16
]
contains a long-time Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem, based on the paper
[
Mét05
]
by Métivier, which proves an Hadamard instability result for initially elliptic quasi-linear systems in Gevrey spaces. Precisely, the result of
[
Mor16
]
asserts that the flow associated to the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) fails to be Hölder from a highly regular
\sigma
-Gevrey space to the very lowly regular
L^{2}
space, locally in the
x
variable and for
\sigma
less than a critical exponent
\sigma_{0}
depending on initial spectrum, under the assumption of initial ellipticity for the first-order differential operator.

Here initial ellipticity is understood as an initial defect of hyperbolicity. That is for some
(x_{0},\vec{u}_{0},\xi_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{x}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{u}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{\xi}^{d},
the principal symbol at
(0,x_{0},\vec{u}_{0},\xi_{0})
:

A_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}(0,x_{0},\vec{u}_{0})\xi_{0,j}

has at least one couple of non-real eigenvalues, with imaginary part
\pm i\gamma_{0}
associated to eigenvectors
\vec{e}_{\pm}
.

In
[
Mor16
]
we posit in
(3.2)
the ansatz
u_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\varepsilon{\mathbf{u}}(t/\varepsilon,x,(x-x_{0})\cdot\xi_{0}/\varepsilon)
, where
{\mathbf{u}}(s,x,\theta)
is periodic in the
\theta
variable. We transform then the Cauchy problem (
1.1
) into the equation

\partial_{s}{\mathbf{u}}-\underline{A}(\varepsilon s,x)\partial_{\theta}{\mathbf{u}}=G({\mathbf{u}})

(1.2)

for some non-linear remainder term
G({\mathbf{u}})
. The leading term
\underline{A}(t,x)
is here the principal symbol

\underline{A}(t,x)=\sum_{j}A_{j}(t,x,\vec{u}_{0})\xi_{0,j}.

(1.3)

The ellipticity condition is an open condition bearing on the principal symbol
\underline{A}
. In particular, ellipticity at
(0,x_{0})
implies ellipticity around
(0,x_{0})
. The proof of
[
Mor16
]
introduces the propagator
U
defined by

\partial_{s}U(s^{\prime},s,x,\theta)-\underline{A}(\varepsilon s,x)\partial_{\theta}U(s^{\prime},s,x,\theta)=0\quad,\quad U(s^{\prime},s^{\prime},x,\theta)={\rm Id}.

(1.4)

By ellipticity, the propagator
U
has an exponential growth. We introduce an appropriate Banach space of functions of
(s,x,\theta)
which are analytical in the
x
variable and whose Fourier coefficients in
\theta
have an exponential growth which reflects the growth of the propagator. A fixed point argument shows existence and uniqueness, and exponential growth in this space, which implies the Hadamard instability.

The main issue in
[
Mor16
]
, compared to the previous analysis of Metivier
[
Mét05
]
, is that in Gevrey spaces, the Hadamard instability is recorded at much longer times than in Sobolev spaces. The instability is observed thanks to highly oscillating, well-polarized initial data, which generate solutions growing exponentially both in time and frequency. Observing an instability means that at some time, the
L^{2}
norm of the solution is far greater (with respect to the frequency) than the Sobolev or Gevrey norm of the initial datum. Considering the fundamental oscillation
e^{ix\cdot\xi}
with frequency
\xi
, a simple computation leads to the Sobolev norm
||e^{ix\cdot\xi}||_{H^{m}}\simeq|\xi|^{m}
, whether the Gevrey norm is
||e^{ix\cdot\xi}||_{\sigma,c,K}\simeq e^{(\sigma c^{\sigma})^{-1}|\xi|^{\sigma}}
(see (
2.25
) below and Lemma 3.3 in
[
Mor16
]
). Hence the observation of the instability is recorded as a much longer time in Gevrey spaces than in Sobolev spaces.

1.1.2
Lerner, Morimoto and Xu’s result on transition to ellipticity for scalar equations

In
[
LMX10
]
, Lerner, Morimoto and Xu introduce the notion of transition to ellipticity for initially hyperbolic systems. A prototypical example is the Burgers equation with a complex forcing:
\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u=i
. In the case of real data, the principal symbol is initially hyperbolic. Due to the complex forcing, the principal symbol is elliptic for ulterior times. For general equations (
1.1
) (with
N=1
: scalar equations), under bracket conditions generalizing the situation for Burgers with complex forcing, and describing a transition from hyperbolicity to ellipticity, the authors in
[
LMX10
]
prove a strong form of instability, namely that if local
C^{2}
solutions exist, then the complement of the analytic wave-front set of the datum is not empty. In particular, if the bracket conditions are formulated at
(x_{0},u_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, it is shown in
[
LMX10
]
that for any analytical datum
\underline{h}
such that
\underline{h}(x_{0})=u_{0}
, there exists smooth initial data
h
close to
\underline{h}
which do not generate local
C^{2}
solutions, a result analogous to Lebeau’s
theorem for Kelvin-Helmholtz
[
Leb02
]
. The proof of
[
LMX10
]
relies strongly on a representation of solutions based on the method of characteristics, specific to scalar equations, which was developed earlier in
[
Mét85
]
.

1.1.3
Lerner, Nguyen and Texier’s result on transition to ellipticity for general systems

In
[
LNT17
]
, Lerner, Nguyen and Texier extend the analysis of
[
LMX10
]
to systems (
1.1
). The result of
[
LNT17
]
shows an instantaneous lack of Hölder well-posedness of the flow, with an arbitrarily large loss of derivatives, under appropriate assumptions of transition to ellipticity. The analysis of
[
LNT17
]
is based on the method of approximation of pseudo-differential flows introduced in
[
Tex16
]
. One key observation in
[
LNT17
]
is that for systems, many types of transitions may occur. The focus in
[
LNT17
]
is on genuinely non-scalar transitions (more about this specific point in Remark
1.1
). For these, the propagator generically grows in time like the Airy function.

1.1.4
Defect of hyperbolicity in Maxwell systems

There is a strong analogy between the progression from
[
Mét05
]
to our present results and recent results
[
LT15
]
,
[
Lu16
]
in geometric optics. In
[
LT15
]
, Lu and Texier study large-amplitude solutions to Maxwell-based systems in the small wavelength limit. They show that in appropriate coordinates, resonances in frequency correspond to points of weak hyperbolicity. Thus at the resonances, the subprincipal symbol plays a role in the stability analysis. Under a Levi condition, hyperbolicity is violated around the resonances, and WKB solutions do not approximate exact solutions issued from appropriate nearby initial data, no matter how precise the order of the WKB approximation. This result is somehow analogous to Métivier’s initial ellipticity result. Following
[
LT15
]
, Lu studied in
[
Lu16
]
a situation in which WKB solutions are destabilized by terms which are not present in the initial data. That is, the Levi condition of
[
LT15
]
is satisfied initially, but higher-order harmonics of the WKB solutions, which are generated by the nonlinearities in the course of the propagation, are associated with higher-order resonances. For these resonances, the Levi conditions may not be satisfied, leading to instability. This framework is somehow similar to ours, with an instability which develops in time, starting from an initially hyperbolic situation.

1.1.5
Ill-posedness results for hydrostatic Euler and related equations

In
[
HKN16
]
, Han-Kwan and Nguyen study the hydrostatic Euler and some singular Vlasov equations through the point of view of an abstract PDE
\partial_{t}U-\mathcal{L}U=\mathcal{Q}(u,u)

are (bi)linear non-local operators. For each of those equations, the corresponding operator

[
Mét05
]
of Métivier, Han-Kwan and Nguyen develop an analytical framework in order to prove an Hadamard instability result in all Sobolev spaces. One main ingredient is, as in
[
Mét05
]
and the present work, to prove the existence of a family of analytical solutions for the abstract PDE which carry the expected exponential growth in time (called
loss of analyticity for the semi-group
in
[
HKN16
]
).

1.2
Overview of the paper

Our assumptions are based on the framework set out in
[
LNT17
]
, the results of which we extend in two distinct ways: we prove existence of solutions up to the observation time at which the Hadamard instability is recorded, and we measure the deviation in Gevrey spaces.

We assume that for a specific frequency
\xi_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
the linear part of the principal symbol at
u=\vec{u}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
defined by (
1.3
) has a real spectrum at time
t=0
while non real eigenvalues appear for
t>0
. In this sense the operator experiences a transition from initial hyperbolicity (
t=0
, real eigenvalues) to eventual ellipticity (
t>0
, non-real eigenvalues).

A sharp difference with the initially elliptic case lies in the normal forms of the operators. Indeed, the elliptic case is reducible to the case where
\underline{A}
is a triangular matrix with non real and conjugated diagonal entries.

By contrast, transitions in time appear in many ways. There is not one single normal form. Section
2
will be devoted to the descriptions of such transitions in time and the associated normal forms for systems of size
N=2
. In particular, this paper focuses on two particular normal forms, described in the next paragraphs.

1.2.1
The smoothly diagonalizable case

Under Assumptions
2.2
,
2.4
and
2.5
(see Proposition
2.6
below), there holds

\underline{A}(t,x)\approx\underline{A}^{{\rm S}}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\
-\gamma_{0}^{2}t&0\end{pmatrix}

with
\gamma_{0}>0
. Here
\approx
means equality up to higher order terms in the Taylor expansion in time and space, and up to a change of basis. The matrix
\underline{A}^{{\rm S}}(t)
is smoothly diagonalisable in
{\mathbb{C}}
, with smooth eigenvalues
\pm i\gamma_{0}t
. This case is mostly scalar ; it is analogous to a degenerate Cauchy-Riemann problem.

Our analysis shows that our method in
[

]
is robust enough to allow for such a weak defect of hyperbolicity. We replace ansatz
(3.2)
therein by

. For such

\partial_{s}U^{{\rm S}}(s^{\prime},s,\theta)-\underline{A}^{{\rm S}}(s)\partial_{\theta}U^{{\rm S}}(s^{\prime},s,\theta)=0

|U_{n}^{{\rm S}}(s^{\prime},s)|\lesssim\exp\left(\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma_{{\rm S}}^{\sharp}(\tau)d\tau\right)\;,\quad\forall\,0\leq s^{\prime}\leq s\;,\;\forall\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

(1.5)

for the Fourier coefficients of

, with
\gamma_{{\rm S}}^{\sharp}(\tau)=\gamma_{0}\tau
.

1.2.3
Example: compressible Euler with Van der Waals pressure law

Transitions of the principal symbol from hyperbolicity to ellipticity, as described in the above paragraphs, are observed in physical equations describing
phase transitions
. One such system (mentioned in both
[
Mét05
]
and
[
LNT17
]
) is the compressible Euler equations in one spatial dimension, with a Van der Waals pressure law:

\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}u_{1}+\partial_{x}u_{2}=0\\
&\partial_{t}u_{2}+\partial_{x}(p(u_{1}))=0\end{cases}

(1.7)

where
p
follows a Van der Waals equation of state, for which there holds
p^{\prime}(u_{1})\leq 0
, for some
u_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The system is hyperbolic (resp. elliptic) for
p^{\prime}(u_{1})>0
(resp. for
p^{\prime}(u_{1})<0
). For solutions which leave the hyperbolic zone, a phase transition occurs. This corresponds for us to the catastrophic growth recorded in the elliptic zone. If for instance the elliptic zone is defined by
\{|u_{1}|\leq\delta\}
, for some
\delta>0
, then solutions may enter the elliptic zone only to leave it immediately, due to the exponential growth.

2
Main assumptions and results

2.1
Branching eigenvalues and defect of hyperbolicity

We look at the possible cases of a defect of hyperbolicity, that is transitions from initial hyperbolicity to ellipticity at time
t>0
, following the work of Lerner, Nguyen and Texier of
[
LNT17
]
.

We introduce first

A(t,x,u)=\sum_{j}A_{j}(t,x,u)\xi_{0,j}.

(2.1)

We assume there are
(x_{0},\vec{u}_{0},\xi_{0})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
r_{0}>0
such that the principal symbol defined by

\underline{A}(t,x)=A(t,x,\vec{u}_{0})

(2.2)

satisfies

{\rm Sp}\left(\underline{A}(0,x)\right)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\;,\quad\forall x\in B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})

(2.3)

which stands for initial and local hyperbolicity around
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Note that, as soon as there is some
x_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\underline{A}(0,x_{1})
has non real spectrum, we are in the case of initial ellipticity treated in
[
Mor16
]
.

We assume also that, for small times
t>0
, there are some
x
close to
x_{0}
such that

{\rm Sp}\left(\underline{A}(t,x)\right)\not\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\quad,\quad\forall\,t>0.

(2.4)

Condition (
2.3
) stands for initial and local hyperbolicity around
x_{0}
; condition (
2.4
) expresses the ellipticity of
\underline{A}
at time
t>0
. Up to translations in
x
and
u
, which do not affect our forthcoming assumptions, and by homogeneity in
\xi
, we may assume

x_{0}=0\quad,\quad\vec{u}_{0}=0\quad,\quad|\xi_{0}|=1.

(2.5)

Since the
A_{j}
have real coefficients, non-real eigenvalues of
\underline{A}(t,x)
appear in conjugate pairs. For such a pair
\lambda_{\pm}(t,x)
, by reality of the eigenvalues at
t=0
we have a double eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}(0,x)=\lambda_{+}(0,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
of
\underline{A}(0,x)
. To avoid higher order transitions (which would involve eigenvalues of multiplicity
3
or greater), we assume the eigenvalues of
\underline{A}(0,0)
to be distinct and simple, except for one double eigenvalue:

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume the eigenvalues of
\underline{A}(0,0)
to be distinct and simple, except for one double eigenvalue.

We block diagonalize the principal symbol into
\underline{A}(t,x)^{(0)}
and
\underline{A}(t,x)^{(1)}
. The block
\underline{A}(t,x)^{(0)}
is a
2\times 2
matrix corresponding to the double eigenvalue, and the
(N-2)\times(N-2)
block
\underline{A}^{(1)}
has simple real eigenvalues at
t=0
in a whole neighborhood of
x=0
. Thanks to Assumption
2.1
the block diagonalization is smooth. Therefore we focus our discussion on
\underline{A}^{(0)}
, and we may assume
N=2
, that is
\underline{A}\equiv\underline{A}^{(0)}
.

The question is now to describe the possible matrices
\underline{A}(t,x)
satisfying conditions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
). Following
[
LNT17
]
, we reformulate the conditions (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) in terms of the characteristic polynomial of
\underline{A}
defined as

P(\lambda,t,x)={\rm det}\left(\lambda-\underline{A}(t,x)\right)

(2.6)

which is simply in the case

P(\lambda,t,x)=\left(\lambda-\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\underline{A}(t,x)\right)^{2}+\Delta(t,x)

(2.7)

where we define

\Delta(t,x)={\rm det}\,\underline{A}(t,x)-\left(\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\underline{A}(t,x)\right)^{2}.

(2.8)

Thus the real or complex nature of the spectrum depends on the sign of
\Delta
. So condition (
2.3
) is equivalent in terms of
\Delta

\Delta(0,x)\leq 0\quad,\quad\forall\,x\in B_{r_{0}}(0).

(2.9)

As a double eigenvalue
\lambda_{-}(0,x)=\lambda_{+}(0,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
of
\underline{A}(0,x)
corresponds to a double root of

, we formulate the following Assumption:

Assumption 2.2

(Branching eigenvalues)
.

In addition to
(
2.9
)
, we assume that there exists some
\lambda_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

P(\lambda_{0},0,0)=0\quad,\quad\partial_{\lambda}P(\lambda_{0},0,0)=0.

(2.10)

Remark 2.3
.

Note that condition (
2.10
) is equivalent to

\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}\underline{A}(0,0)\quad,\quad\Delta(0,0)=0.

For condition (
2.4
) to be satisfied, that is for a conjugate pair of eigenvalues to appear as
t>0
,
\Delta(t,x)
has to be positive for
t>0
. The eigenvalues of
\underline{A}
, which are the zeroes of

, are then expressed by the square roots of
\Delta
. Even though the regularity of
\Delta
, being an algebraic combination of the coefficients of
\underline{A}
, is analytic, the regularity of the square roots of
\Delta
can be of course much weaker. How much rougher than
\Delta

be has been studied in particular by Glaeser
[
Gla63
]
. The question of the regularity of the eigenvalues and of the eigenvectors is here of importance as we work in the analytic framework: we may not use non-smooth (in time and space) changes of basis, since the methods we use, following
[
Mét05
]
, strongly rely on analyticity. In particular, we may not diagonalize the principal symbol if the eigenvectors are not smooth.

2.2
The case of a smooth transition

For the square roots of
\Delta
to be as smooth as
\Delta
, the discriminant
\Delta
has to be the square of a smooth function
\delta(t,x)
:

\Delta(t,x)=\delta(t,x)^{2}.

In this case, note that
\Delta(0,x)=\delta(0,x)^{2}\geq 0
. Since we assume also that
\Delta(0,x)\leq 0
by (
2.9
), we get

\delta(0,x)=0\quad,\quad\forall x\in B_{r_{0}}(0).

This is equivalent to the existence of some analytic function
\tilde{\delta}(t,x)
such that

\delta(t,x)=t\tilde{\delta}(t,x).

We sum up all this in the following

Assumption 2.4

(Smooth transition)
.

There is a function
\delta(t,x)
analytic in the
t
and
x
variables such that

\Delta(t,x)=\left(t\delta(t,x)\right)^{2}

(2.11)

with

\delta(0,0)=\gamma_{0}>0.

(2.12)

Under Assumption
2.4
, since

2.3
The case of a stiff transition

If
\Delta
is not the square of a function, its square roots are typically not as smooth as
\Delta
. In fact, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
it is possible to find
\Delta
such that it is analytic, but its square roots are
C^{k}
and not
C^{k+1}
. The first non degenerate case of this kind is when

\partial_{t}\Delta(0,0)>0

(2.15)

which implies that
\Delta(t,0)^{1/2}\sim t^{1/2}
which is
C^{0}
but not
C^{1}
at
t=0
. With
\Delta(0,0)=0
by Assumption
2.2
, condition (
2.15
) and the implicit function theorem give the existence of an analytic function
t_{\star}(x)
such that

\Delta(t,x)=0\Longleftrightarrow t=t_{\star}(x)\quad\text{locally around }(t,x)=(0,0).

(2.16)

Introducing

e(t,x)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\Delta((1-\tau)t_{\star}(x)+\tau t,x)d\tau

there holds

\Delta(t,x)=\left(t-t_{\star}(x)\right)e(t,x).

(2.17)

As
\Delta
is analytic,
e
is also analytic, and satisfies

e(0,0)=\partial_{t}\Delta(0,0)>0

so that
e
is positive around
(0,0)
. Then the sign of
\Delta(t,x)
, hence the real or complex
nature of the spectrum of
\underline{A}(t,x)
, is given by the sign of
t-t_{\star}(x)
, a situation comparable to the one described in Section 1.2.3 of
[
LNT17
]
:

•

For
(t,x)
under the transition curve
\{(t_{\star}(x),x)\}
the eigenvalues of
\underline{A}(t,x)
are real.

•

For
(t,x)
above the transition curve, the eigenvalues of
\underline{A}(t,x)
have a non-zero imaginary part like
\pm i(t-t_{\star}(x))^{1/2}
.

The question is then to describe
t_{\star}
. First, as
\Delta(0,0)=0
,

t_{\star}(0)=0.

(2.18)

As
\Delta(0,x)\leq 0
for

, we have

t_{\star}(x)\geq 0\quad,\quad\forall x\in B_{r_{0}}(0)

which implies

\partial_{x}t_{\star}(0)=0

so that the Taylor expansion of

around

is as

t_{\star}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k}\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}t_{\star}(0)\,x_{j}x_{k}+O(x^{3})

and the Hessian
\left(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}t_{\star}(0)\right)_{j,k}
is a nonnegative matrix. But as we will see in the course of the proof of Proposition
5.4
, the non degenerate case
\left(\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}t_{\star}(0)\right)_{j,k}\neq 0
cannot be dealt with our method. We then assume

\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}t_{\star}(0)=0\quad,\quad\forall\,j,k=1,\ldots,d.

(2.20)

Just as before, inequality (
2.9
) implies that third order derivatives of

are null at

, and there holds

t_{\star}(x)=O(x^{4}).

In order to sum up those assumptions in a more intrinsic way, we express derivatives of

by derivatives of
\Delta
. By definition (

) of

, there holds

hence, differentiating with respect to

\partial_{x}t_{\star}(0)\,\partial_{t}\Delta(0,0)+\partial_{x}\Delta(0,0)=0.

As

\partial_{x}\Delta(0,0)=0.

By Faà di Bruno formula on iterate derivatives applied to the equality

, we may prove by induction that

is equivalent to the following

Assumption 2.7

(Degenerate stiff transition)
.

We assume

\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\,\Delta(0,0)=0\quad,\quad\forall\,\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}\text{ with }|\alpha|\leq 3.

We prove now a normal form expression for

Proposition 2.8

(Normal form for the stiff transition)

\partial_{s}V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)-in\begin{pmatrix}0&\varepsilon^{(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)}s\\
-\gamma_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)}s&0\end{pmatrix}V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)=-\varepsilon^{1/(1+\eta)}Q_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}Q_{0}\,V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)

(3.21)

with initial condition
V_{n}(s^{\prime},s^{\prime},x)=Q_{0}^{-1}\left(\varepsilon^{1/(1+\eta)}s^{\prime},x\right)
. We focus then on the autonomous differential system

\partial_{s}\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)-in\begin{pmatrix}0&\varepsilon^{(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)}s\\
-\gamma_{0}^{2}\varepsilon^{(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)}s&0\end{pmatrix}\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)=0

(3.22)

which becomes, with the choice
\eta=1
, the
\varepsilon
-free matrix equation

\partial_{s}\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)-in\,s\,\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-\gamma_{0}^{2}&0\end{pmatrix}\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)=0.

The complex constant change of basis

\underline{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-i\gamma_{0}&i\gamma_{0}\end{pmatrix}

leads us to the exact solution

\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)=\underline{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\exp(n\gamma_{0}(s^{2}-s^{\prime 2})/2)&0\\
0&\exp(-n\gamma_{0}(s^{2}-s^{\prime 2})/2)\end{pmatrix}\underline{Q}^{-1}

which satisfies the upper bound

\left|\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)\right|\lesssim\exp\left(\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}(\tau)d\tau\,|n|\right)

(3.23)

with
\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}
defined in (
3.19
). Getting back to (
3.21
), we use Duhamel formula to write

V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)=\widetilde{V}_{n}(s^{\prime},s)Q_{0}^{-1}(\varepsilon^{1/2}s^{\prime})-\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\varepsilon^{1/2}\widetilde{V}_{n}(\tau,s)\,\left(Q_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}Q_{0}\right)(\varepsilon^{1/2}\tau)\,V_{n}(s^{\prime},\tau)d\tau.

(3.24)

Note that
V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)
depends also on
x
through
Q_{0}=Q_{0}(t,x)
. Factorizing by the exponential growth
\exp(\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}(\tau)d\tau\,|n|)
, we get

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}\,|n|\right)\left|V_{n}(s^{\prime},s)\right|

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\,\exp\left(-\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}\,|n|\right)\left|\widetilde{V}(s^{\prime},s)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\varepsilon^{1/2}\exp\left(-\int_{\tau}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}\,|n|\right)\left|\widetilde{V}_{n}(\tau,s)\right|\,\left|Q_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}Q_{0}\right|(\varepsilon^{1/2}\tau)\,\exp\left(-\int_{s^{\prime}}^{\tau}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}\,|n|\right)|V_{n}(s^{\prime},\tau)|d\tau

and the bound holds uniformly in
x
. We introduce then

y_{\varepsilon}(s^{\prime},s)=\max_{x\in K_{\varepsilon}}\exp\left(-\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\gamma^{\sharp}_{{\rm S}}\,|n|\right)\left|V_{n}(s^{\prime},s,x)\right|\quad\text{with }K_{\varepsilon}=B_{R^{-1}}(0).

Thanks to the upper bound (
3.23
) there holds

y_{\varepsilon}(s^{\prime},s)\lesssim 1+\int_{s^{\prime}}^{s}\varepsilon^{1/2}\,\left|Q_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}Q_{0}\right|(\varepsilon^{1/2}\tau)\,y_{\varepsilon}(s^{\prime},\tau)d\tau

and we make use of the Gronwall inequality to get

y_{\varepsilon}(s^{\prime},s)\lesssim\exp\left(\varepsilon^{1/2}c\,(s-s^{\prime})\right)\quad\text{with}\quad c=\max_{x\in K_{\varepsilon}}\left|Q_{0}^{-1}\partial_{t}Q_{0}\right|

As
\varepsilon^{1/2}s
is small in our setting, we get the announced upper bound (
3.18
).

∎

Lemma 3.5

(Growth of the propagator: the Airy case)

Under Assumptions

and
2.7

Proof.

Lemma 3.6

(Growth of the free solution: the smooth case)

[
Mor16
]

(Fixed time spaces

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle 1

We are then reduced to study two cases:

•
