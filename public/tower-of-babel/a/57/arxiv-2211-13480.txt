1
Introduction

Let
G
be a simple Lie group with trivial center, with Iwasawa decomposition
G=KAN
. The
real rank
of
G
, i.e. the dimension of
A
, can be viewed as a measure of the complexity of
G
, with the real rank of
G
being 0 if and only if
G
is compact. It is known that in higher real rank, i.e.
\dim A\geq 2
, the group
G
satisfies various forms of rigidity (e,g. property (T), arithmeticity of lattices, Margulis’ super-rigidity: see
[
Zi84
]
for all that). The situation in real rank one is more subtle, with some forms of rigidity being satisfied and some other not, the situation depending very much on the group under consideration. Recall that, from Cartan’s classification (see e.g.
[
Ti67
]
), simple real Lie groups with real rank 1 fall into 3 infinite series and one exceptional group:

•

Adjoint group of
SO_{0}(n,1),\;(n\geq 2)
.

•

Adjoint group of
SU(n,1),\;(n\geq 2)
.

•

Adjoint group of
Sp(n,1),\;(n\geq 2)
.

•

F_{4(-20)}
.

The present paper deals with the exceptional group
F_{4(-20)}
. Several papers in harmonic analysis on real rank 1 simple Lie groups deal exclusively with the classical cases, i.e. the three infinite series above: see
[
Li74
]
for a sample. A notable exception is Takahashi’s paper
[
Ta79
]
, which was inspiring for us. It is therefore interesting to have explicit constructions of
F_{4(-20)}
, both algebraic and geometric.

Algebraically,
F_{4(-20)}
was constructed by Freudenthal
[
Fr53
]
as the automorphism group of the real Albert algebra
J_{1,2}
, which is one out of the three 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebras over the reals (see also chapter 19 in
[
Mo73
]
, or more recently
[
Al98
]
). Geometrically, it was constructed by Tits
[
Ti53
]
as the automorphism group of the hyperbolic plane over the octonions
Cay
. This provides a unified construction of all simple Lie groups of real rank 1 and with trivial centre: let
\mathbf{K}
be one of the four finite-dimensional division algebras over the reals, namely
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{H}
(the Hamilton quaternions), or
Cay
(the Cayley numbers). For
n\geq 2
, let
H^{n}(\mathbf{K})
be
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space over
\mathbf{K}
(with
n=2
when
\mathbf{K}=Cay
). Then any real rank 1 simple Lie group with trivial center is isomorphic to the (connected component of identity of the) automorphism group
Aut(H^{n}(\mathbf{K}))
for some
n\geq 2
and some
\mathbf{K}\in\{\mathbf{R},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{H},Cay\}
.

The present, mostly survey, paper aims to advertise Tits’ synthetic construction of
H^{2}(\mathbf{K})
from
[
Ti53
]
. Tits first defines the projective plane
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
as the affine plane
\mathbf{K}^{2}
together with a line at infinity, which provides a very suitable coordinatization of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
. The hyperbolic plane then appears as the set of interior points of a specific hyperbolic polarity, and is realized as the unit ball
\{(x_{1},x_{2}):|x_{1}|^{2}+|x_{2}|^{2}<1\}
in
\mathbf{K}^{2}
.

The paper is structured as follows: in section 2, we recall Cayley numbers and the triality principle; in section 3, we survey Tits’ constructions and results from
[
Ti53
]
. Only the final section 4 has some novelty. Let
KAN
be the Iwasawa decomposition of
F_{4(-20)}
; the maximal compact subgroup
K
was determined by Tits in
[
Ti53
]
, the one-parameter subgroup
A
appears in
[
Ta79
]
. The nilpotent subgroup
N
was determined by Takahashi
[
Ta79
]
in Freudenthal’s algebraic model, it is a central extension of the additive group of
Cay
by the additive group
Im(Cay)
of imaginary Cayley numbers. Our main contribution consists in writing down explicitly the action of
N
on
H^{2}(Cay)
: doing so we pay the price for working over a non-associative division algebra. Indeed, here is for example the action of the element
u(y,0)
in the centre
Z(N)
on
(x_{1},x_{2})\in H^{2}(Cay)
(see Corollary
4.3
below): we have
u(y,0)(x_{1},x_{2})=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})
, where:

x^{\prime}_{1}=((1-y)x_{1}+y)(-yx_{1}+1+y)^{-1}

and

x^{\prime}_{2}=(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}}))[(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}(-yx_{1}+1+y)^{-1}]

While the formula for
x^{\prime}_{1}
is what is expected for an action by Möbius transformations, the formula for

reveals the problems that arise with the lack of associativity.

Finally, let
MAN
be the minimal parabolic subgroup of
F_{4(-20)}
: we characterize
M
as the stabilizer in
K
of the point
(1,0)
on the boundary of
H^{2}(Cay)
.

2
Octonions and triality

Recall that
Cay
, the algebra of
Cayley numbers
or
octonions
, denotes the 8-dimensional non-associative division algebra over
\mathbf{R}
with basis
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{7}

and relations

•

e_{0}.e_{i}=e_{i}.e_{0}=e_{i}
for
i=0,1,...,7
.

•

e_{i}^{2}=-e_{0}
for
i>0
.

•

e_{i}.e_{j}=-e_{j}.e_{i}
for
i,j>0,\;i\neq j
.

•

e_{2}.e_{6}=e_{3}.e_{4}=e_{5}.e_{7}=e_{1}
, plus all relations deduced from this one by cyclic permutations of the indices
1,2,...,7
.

We will replace
e_{0}
by 1 for simplicity and identify the real octonion
a.e_{0}
with the real number
a
. Any octonion
x=a+\sum_{i=1}^{7}b_{i}e_{i}
has a
conjugate

\overline{x}=a-\sum_{i=1}^{7}b_{i}e_{i}
and a
norm

N(x)=x\overline{x}=\overline{x}x=a^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{7}b_{i}^{2}
. The
modulus
of
x
is
|x|=:\sqrt{N(x)}
. The norm satisfies
N(xy)=N(x)N(y)
for every
x,y\in Cay
.

The
imaginary part
of an octonion
x
is
Im(x)=\frac{x-\overline{x}}{2}
: the octonion
x
is said to be it imaginary if
x=Im(x)
; we denote by
Im(Cay)
the set of imaginary octonions.

The algebra
Cay
is a division algebra as the inverse of a non-zero octonion
x
is
x^{-1}=\frac{\overline{x}}{N(x)}
. The algebra
Cay
is not associative but
alternative
, namely for every
x,y\in Cay
:

•

(xy)x=x(yx)
;

•

x(xy)=x^{2}y
;

•

(xy)y=xy^{2}
.

For all this, see either the nice paper
[
Ba02
]
or Chapter 1 of the book
[
SV00
]
.

The norm allows us to identify
Cay
linearly isometrically with the 8-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbf{E}^{8}
. So the rotation group
SO(8)
acts linearly on
Cay
and the have the
triality principle
(see Theorem 3.2.1 in
[
SV00
]
) that we recall:

Theorem 2.1
.

For every
R\in SO(8)
, there exists
R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime}\in SO(8)
such that, for every
x,y\in Cay
:

R(xy)=R^{\prime}(x)R^{\prime\prime}(y)

Moreover
R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime}
are determined up to sign by this relation.

\blacksquare

We will need some special features of the triple
(R,R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime})
as above.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix
R\in SO(8)
, let
R^{\prime},R^{\prime\prime}\in SO(8)
be associated with
R
as in Theorem
2.1
.

1.

We have
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
if and only if
R=R^{\prime}
.

2.

Assume
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
. Then:

a)

For every non-zero octonion
x
, we have
R^{\prime\prime}(x^{-1})=R^{\prime\prime}(x)^{-1}
.

b)

R^{\prime\prime}
is a Jordan homomorphism, i.e

R^{\prime\prime}(xy+yx)=R^{\prime\prime}(x)R^{\prime\prime}(y)+R^{\prime\prime}(y)R^{\prime\prime}(x)

for every
x,y\in Cay
.

c)

For every
x\in Cay
, the map
R^{\prime\prime}
is a homomorphism in restriction to the subalgebra generated by
1
and
x
.

Proof
:

1.

If
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
, then for every
x\in Cay
we get:

R(x)=R(x.1)=R^{\prime}(x).R^{\prime\prime}(1)=R^{\prime}(x).1=R^{\prime}(x)

hence
R=R^{\prime}
. Conversely, assuming
R=R^{\prime}
, we get for every
x\in Cay
:

R(x)=R(x.1)=R^{\prime}(x)R^{\prime\prime}(1)=R(x)R^{\prime\prime}(1),

which forces
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
.

2.

a)

As
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
, we have
R^{\prime\prime}(\overline{x})=\overline{R^{\prime\prime}(x)}
, and the result follows from the formula for the inverse of
x
.

b)

Denoting by
\langle.|.\rangle
the standard scalar product on
Cay
associated with the norm
N
, we have by formula (1.8) in
[
SV00
]
:

xy+yx=\langle x|1\rangle y+\langle y|1\rangle x-\langle x|y\rangle 1.

Applying
R^{\prime\prime}
and using that it is a linear isometry, we get:

R^{\prime\prime}(xy+yx)=\langle R^{\prime\prime}(x)|R^{\prime\prime}(1)\rangle R^{\prime\prime}(y)+\langle R^{\prime\prime}(y)|R^{\prime\prime}(1)\rangle R^{\prime\prime}(x)-\langle R^{\prime\prime}(x)|R^{\prime\prime}(y)\rangle R^{\prime\prime}(1)

=\langle R^{\prime\prime}(x)|1\rangle R^{\prime\prime}(y)+\langle R^{\prime\prime}(y)|1\rangle R^{\prime\prime}(x)-\langle R^{\prime\prime}(x)|R^{\prime\prime}(y)\rangle 1=R^{\prime\prime}(x)R^{\prime\prime}(y)+R^{\prime\prime}(y)R^{\prime\prime}(x),

where the second equality follows from the assumption
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
.

c)

By formula (1.7) in
[
SV00
]
, the subalgebra generated by
1
and
x
is at most 2-dimensional, so it is commutative and the result follows from the preceding point.

\blacksquare

In this section we follow rather closely Tits’paper

3.1
The projective plane and its collineation group

Let
\mathbf{K}
denote one of the following division algebras over the reals:
\mathbf{R},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{H}
(the Hamilton quaternions), or
Cay
. The
projective plane

P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is the disjoint union

P^{2}(\mathbf{K})=(\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K})\coprod\mathbf{K}\coprod{(\infty)}

where
\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K}
is the set of points at finite distance and
\mathbf{K}\coprod{(\infty)}=\{(u):u\in\mathbf{K}\coprod\{\infty\}\}
is the set of point at infinity. A
line
in
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is a subset of one of the following three types:

•

[u,v]=\{(x,y)\in\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K}:y=ux+v\}\cup\{(u)\}
;

•

[u]=\{(x,y)\in\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K}:x=u\}\cup\{(\infty)\}
;

•

[\infty]=\mathbf{K}\coprod{(\infty)}
.

Lines of the first type are oblique, lines of the second type are vertical, and
[\infty]
is the line at infinity. It is readily seen that the incidence axioms of projective geometry are satisfied: two lines intersect in a unique point, two distinct points determine a unique line through them.

A
collineation
of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is a bijection of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
onto itself, that preserves the set of lines.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Théorème 4.9 in
[
Ti53
]
) The group of collineations of
P^{2}(Cay)
is isomorphic to the real form
E_{6(-26)}
of the exceptional Lie group
E_{6}
: it is therefore a simple Lie group of dimension 78, which is simply connected with trivial centre.

\blacksquare

3.2
The hyperbolic plane and its automorphism group

A
polarity
of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is a bijection
\Pi
between the set of points and the set of lines of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
, satisfying two conditions:

•

if
a
runs through a line
L
, then
\Pi(a)
runs in the sheaf of lines through a given point;

•

for any two points
a,b\in P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
, we have
a\in\Pi(b)\Leftrightarrow b\in\Pi(a)
.

We will need just one example of polarity, given on points by:

\Pi(x,y)=[-\overline{xy^{-1}},\overline{y^{-1}}]\;\;\mbox{if}\,y\neq 0;

\Pi(x,0)=[\overline{x^{-1}}]\;\;\mbox{if}\;x\neq 0;

\Pi(0,0)=[\infty].

This polarity is
hyperbolic
, meaning that the set of points
P\in P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
such that
P\in\Pi(P)
is non-empty. Actually this set is the sphere of equation
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1
in
\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K}
. The
hyperbolic plane

H^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is then the ball
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}<1
in
\mathbf{K}\times\mathbf{K}
. Lines in
H^{2}(\mathbf{K})
are traces on
H^{2}(\mathbf{K})
of lines in
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
that intersect it (“Klein model”). An automorphism of
H^{2}(\mathbf{K})
is a collineation of
P^{2}(\mathbf{K})
that commutes with
\Pi
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be the automorphism group of
H^{2}(Cay)
.

1.

(6.5 in
[
Ti53
]
) The stabilizer
K
of
(0,0)
in
G
is isomorphic to
Spin(9)
(the universal cover of
SO(9)
); it acts transitively on the sphere
|x|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1
and it is a maximal compact subgroup of
G
.

2.

(Théorème 6.8 in
[
Ti53
]
)
G
is isomorphic to the real form
F_{4(-20)}
of the exceptional Lie group
F_{4}
: it is therefore a simple Lie group of dimension 52, which is simply connected with trivial centre.
\blacksquare

Let us single out the following for future use:

Lemma 3.3
.

(see (4.6) in
[
Ti53
]
) An element
k\in K
fixes the points at infinity
(0)
and
(\infty)
if and only if there exists
R\in SO(8)
such that (in the notation of the triality principle Theorem
2.1
):

k(x,y)=(R^{\prime\prime}(x),R(y))

for every
(x,y)\in Cay\times Cay
.
\blacksquare

Let

Factoring out
2(1-\overline{x_{1})}
on the right in the numerator, we get:

\cosh(t_{0})+E=\frac{[\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}(1-x_{1})+(\overline{z}(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}})))(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}+y(1-x_{1})+1](1-\overline{x_{1}})}{r|1-x_{1}|}

hence

\cosh(t_{0})+E=\frac{[-(\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)x_{1}+(\overline{z}(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}})))(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}+1+\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y](1-\overline{x_{1}})}{r|1-x_{1}|}.

Comparing with (6), we get

\cosh(t_{0})+E=\frac{D}{r|1-x_{1}|}.

(18)

In view of (14), formula (5) follows immediately by combining (16) and (18).

We now turn to verifying formula (4). Again from (10), we have:

\sinh(t_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{r}{|1-x_{1}|}-\frac{|1-x_{1}|}{r})=\frac{r^{2}-|1-x_{1}|^{2}}{2r|1-x_{1}|}=\frac{x_{1}+\overline{x_{1}}-2|x_{1}|^{2}-|x_{2}|^{2}}{2r|1-x_{1}|},

hence by (17):

\sinh(t_{0})+E=\frac{|1-x_{1}|^{2}|z|^{2}+2\overline{z}(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}}))+2y|1-x_{1}|^{2}+2x_{1}-2|x_{1}|^{2}}{2r|1-x_{1}|}.

Factoring out
2(1-\overline{x_{1})}
on the right in the numerator, we get:

\sinh(t_{0})+E=\frac{[\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}(1-x_{1})+(\overline{z}(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}})))(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}+y(1-x_{1})+x_{1}](1-\overline{x_{1}})}{r|1-x_{1}|}

hence

\sinh(t_{0})+E=\frac{[(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}-y)x_{1}+(\overline{z}(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}})))(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y](1-\overline{x_{1}})}{r|1-x_{1}|}

(19)

In view of (13), formula (4) follows immediately by combining (18) and (19).

\blacksquare

u(y,0)(x_{1},x_{2})=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})
where:

x^{\prime}_{1}=((1-y)x_{1}+y)(-yx_{1}+1+y)^{-1}

(20)

x^{\prime}_{2}=(x_{2}(1-\overline{x_{1}}))[(1-\overline{x_{1}})^{-1}(-yx_{1}+1+y)^{-1}]

(21)

Proof
Formula (
21

. To obtain formula (

Remark 4.4
.

Let us observe that, if we replace the Cayley numbers by the Hamilton quaternions
\mathbf{H}
, formulae (4) and (5) simplify drastically thanks to associativity:

x^{\prime}_{1}=((1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}-y)x_{1}+\overline{z}x_{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)(-(\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)x_{1}+\overline{z}x_{2}+1+\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)^{-1},

x^{\prime}_{2}=(-zx_{1}+x_{2}+z)(-(\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)x_{1}+\overline{z}x_{2}+1+\frac{|z|^{2}}{2}+y)^{-1}

for

.
This is nothing but the classical action of the nilpotent part of

on

).

Proposition 4.5
.

We have
M=Stab_{K}(1,0)
, the stabilizer of
(1,0)
in
K
.

Proof
: Observe that
(1,0)
is the unique attracting point of
A
, i.e.

(1,0)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}a_{t}(x,y)

for every
(x,y)\in H^{2}(Cay)
. So for
m\in M
we have

m(1,0)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}ma_{t}(x,y)=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}a_{t}m(x,y)=(1,0),

so that
M\subset Stab_{K}(1,0)
. To prove the converse inclusion, take
k\in Stab_{K}(1,0)
. As
k
stabilizes the line
[0,0]
through
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
, it fixes its point
(0)
at infinity. It also fixes
\Pi[0,0]=(\infty)
. So lemma
3.3
applies, and there exists
R\in SO(8)
such that
k(x,y)=(R^{\prime\prime}(x),R(y))
. Since
k(1,0)=(1,0)
, we have
R^{\prime\prime}(1)=1
. We now check that
k
commutes with
a_{t}
: indeed, for every
(x,y)\in H^{2}(Cay)
:

a_{t}k(x,y)=((\cosh(t)R^{\prime\prime}(x)+\sinh(t))(\sinh(t)R^{\prime\prime}(x)+\cosh(t))^{-1},R(y)(\sinh(t)R^{\prime\prime}(x)+\cosh(t))^{-1})

=(R^{\prime\prime}((\cosh(t)x+\sinh(t))(\sinh(t)x+\cosh(t))^{-1}),R^{\prime}(y)R^{\prime\prime}((\sinh(t)x+\cosh(t))^{-1})\;\mbox{by lemma \ref{monlemme};}

=(R^{\prime\prime}((\cosh(t)x+\sinh(t))(\sinh(t)x+\cosh(t))^{-1}),R(y(\sinh(t)x+\cosh(t))^{-1}))\;\mbox{by Theorem \ref{trial};}

=ka_{t}(x,y).

This concludes the proof.
\blacksquare
