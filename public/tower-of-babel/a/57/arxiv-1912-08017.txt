1.
Introduction

Given a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the number of integer points within
P
can be regarded as a discrete analog of the volume of the body. For a
rational polytope
, meaning that the vertices of
P
have rational coordinates, Ehrhart
[
13
]
showed that the number of integer points in the integer dilates
tP:=\{tx:x\in P\}
can be written as a
quasi-polynomial
function of
t
, that is, as an expression of the form

L_{P}(t):=|tP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}+e_{d-1}(t)t^{d-1}+\dots+e_{0}(t),

(1)

for
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
. Here, each
quasi-coefficient

e_{k}(t)
is a periodic function with period dividing the
denominator
of
P
, defined to be the smallest integer
m
such that
mP
in an integer polytope. The function
L_{P}(t)
is called the
Ehrhart quasi-polynomial
of
P
(see e.g., Beck and Robins
[
6
]
). Quasipolynomial behavior appears in many different contexts, and appears for example in the recent work of Bogart, Goodrick, and Woods
[
8
]
.

The Ehrhart quasi-polynomial of
P
is not, however, the only discrete volume that we may define. It has a sister polynomial, which is another measure of discrete volume for polytopes. Namely, each integer point located on the boundary of the polytope is assigned a fractional weight, according to the proportion of the space around that point which the polytope occupies. Indeed, Ehrhart and Macdonald already defined this other discrete volume of
P
, calling it the solid angle sum, and we will adopt their notation, as follows.

At each point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the
solid angle
with respect to
P
:

\omega_{P}(x):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{vol}(B(x,\epsilon)\cap P)}{\mathrm{vol}(B(x,\epsilon))},

(2)

where
B(x,\epsilon)
denotes the ball centered at
x
with radius
\epsilon
.
Similarly to Ehrhart, Macdonald
[
20
,
21
]
showed that if
P
is a rational polytope and
t
is a positive integer, the sum of these fractionally-weighted integer points inside
tP
is a quasi-polynomial of
t
. We define the
solid angle sum

A_{P}(t):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\omega_{tP}(x)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}+a_{d-1}(t)t^{d-1}+\dots+a_{0}(t),

(3)

and similarly to (
1
), we call
a_{k}(t)
the quasi-coefficients of
A_{P}(t)
.

One of the motivations for studying these coefficients is that they capture geometric information about the polytope. Denote by
\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)
the
relative volume
of a face
F
, which differs from the usual volume inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a scaling factor such that the fundamental domain of the lattice of integer points on the linear space parallel to the face has volume
1
. Assuming that
P
is full-dimensional, it is an easy fact
e_{d}
is the volume of
P
and, if we further assume that
P
is an integer polytope, then it is also fairly easy to show that
e_{d-1}
is half the sum of the relative volumes of the facets of
P
, and
e_{0}=1
(see
[
6
]
). Analogous “simple” geometric interpretations for the other coefficients
e_{k}
are not yet known. On the other hand, one strong advantage that the solid angle sum has over the Ehrhart polynomial is that it is a better approximation to the volume of
tP
, in the following sense. For a full-dimensional integer polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, restricting attention to integer dilates
t
gives:

A_{P}(t):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\omega_{tP}(x)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}+a_{d-2}t^{d-2}+a_{d-4}t^{d-4}+\dots,

(4)

a polynomial function of
t
, which is an even polynomial in even dimensions, and an odd polynomial in odd dimensions, and also
a_{0}=0
. This was already proved by Macdonald
[
21
]
, using the purely combinatorial technique of the Möbius
\mu
-function of the face poset of
P
.

In this paper, our main focus is on the coefficients of the solid angle quasi-polynomial, as in equation (
3
).
One strong advantage that these quasi-polynomials have over their Ehrhart quasi-polynomial siblings is that
the solid angle quasi-polynomials are a
simple valuation
on the polytope algebra. This means that for any given two rational polytopes
P,Q\subset R^{d}
whose interiors are disjoint, we have

A_{P\cup Q}(t)=A_{P}(t)+A_{Q}(t)
, hence we never have to compute these valuations over intersections of such polytopes. However, for the Ehrhart polynomials, we have

L_{P\cup Q}(t)=L_{P}(t)+L_{Q}(t)-L_{P\cap Q}(t)
, so that in principle one has to compute these latter valuations over lower-dimensional intersections.

To state the main results of the literature, as well as our results here, we need to use the following definitions and data, associated to any polytope
P
. Given any face
F\subseteq P
, we define the
affine hull

\mathrm{aff}(F)
as the smallest affine space containing
F
and
\mathrm{lin}(F)
as the linear subspace parallel to
\mathrm{aff}(F)
. We also define the
cone of feasible directions
of
P
at
F
as

\mathrm{fcone}(P,F):=\{\lambda(y-x):x\in F,y\in P,\lambda\geq 0\}

and, picking any point
x_{F}
in the relative interior of the face
F
, we define the
tangent cone
of
P
at
F

\mathrm{tcone}(P,F):=x_{F}+\mathrm{fcone}(P,F),

as the cone of feasible directions translated to its original position.

McMullen
[
22
]
(see also Barvinok
[
4
, Chapter 20]
) proved the existence of functions
\mu
such that for rational
P
,

|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu(P,F),

(5)

where the sum is taken over all faces of
P
and
\mu
depends only on “local” geometric data associated to the face
F
, namely the cone
\mathrm{fcone}(P,F)
and the translation class of
\mathrm{aff}(F)
modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Since the volume is homogeneous with degree
\dim(F)
, applying (
5
) to
tP
for integer
t
, we see that this expression implies a formula of the type

e_{k}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim(F)=k\end{subarray}}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu(tP,tF).

(6)

Such formula is called a
local formula
for the quasi-coefficients. Since
\mathrm{fcone}(P,F)
does not change under dilations and, taking
m
as the denominator of
P
,
\mathrm{aff}(mF)
has integer points, we see that indeed
e_{k}(t)=e_{k}(t+m)
.

These formulas (
6
) are not unique. Indeed, when
P
is an integer polytope, Pommersheim and Thomas
[
24
]
constructed infinite classes of such formulas based on an expression for the Todd class of a toric variety;
For the case that
P
is a rational polytope, Barvinok
[
3
,
4
]
studied the algorithmic complexity of computing these coefficients, showing that fixing the codimension
\dim(P)-\dim(F)
,

is indeed computable in polynomial time and Berline and Vergne
[
7
]
computed a local formula based on a valuation that associates an analytic function to the tangent cone at each face. Garoufalidis and Pommersheim
[
15
]
showed that there exists such valuation (and hence a local formula) uniquely for each given “rigid complement map” of the vector space, which is a systematic way to extend functions initialy defined on subspaces to the entire space. Recently, Ring and Schürmann
[
26
]
also produced a method to build local formulas based on the choice of fundamental domains on sublattices. For simplicity, in this paper we assume
a fixed inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we also use to identify the space with its dual, and in this way these complement maps are simply given by orthogonal projection.

A simple way to see that the solid angle sum is indeed a quasi-polynomial and enjoys a lot of the same properties of the Ehrhart function follows by using a simple relation
[
6
, Lemma 13.2]
followed by the Ehrhart reciprocity law
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
:

A_{P}(t)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\omega_{P}(F)L_{\mathrm{int}(F)}(t)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\omega_{P}(F)(-1)^{\dim(F)}L_{F}(-t),

(7)

where the sum is taken over all faces of
P
and
\omega_{P}(F)
is defined as the solid angle of any point in the relative interior of the face
F
.
But we proceed in the opposite direction: first we give formulations for the solid angle quasi-polynomial using Fourier analytic methods and then show how the Ehrhart coefficients can be recovered from them.

We make one more remark concerning the domain of the dilation parameter. Linke
[
19
]
has shown that the Ehrhart function still preserves its quasi-polynomial structure when considered with positive real dilations instead only integer dilations. One of her main observations was that for a rational polytope
P
and
p,q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, one may use
L_{P}(p/q)=L_{\frac{1}{q}P}(p)
and this relation indeed extends to the quasi-coefficients. Letting
e_{k}(P;t):=e_{k}(t)
, Linke showed that

e_{k}(P;p/q)=e_{k}\big((1/q)P;p\big)q^{k}.

(8)

Assuming further that
P
is full-dimensional, Linke showed that the quasi-coefficients
e_{k}(P;t)
are piecewise polynomials of degree
d-k
with discontinuities only at rational points, which makes the extension to real dilates straightforward.

Taking this observation into account together with the fact that our methods enable the consideration of real dilations quite naturally, we state our results for all positive real dilations. We do note, however, that as long as we retrict attention to the class of
all
rational polytopes, the main content of the theorems relies only on the integer dilations due to the reduction (
8
) above.

A subtle but important difference occurs when one fixes a single polytope
P
and compares its Ehrhart function
L_{P}(t)
for integer versus real dilations, the latter carrying much more information. In the case where integer translations
P+w
are considered, we note that the invariance
L_{P+w}(t)=L_{P}(t)
is only guaranteed for integer dilations. Recently, Royer has carried out a detailed and extended study of precisely such an analysis. (Royer
[
27
,
28
]
).

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section

[

. Section

2.
Main results

Our first main result is a local formula for the codimension two quasi-coefficient

\dim G=d-2\end{subarray}}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(G)\bigg[\frac{c_{G}}{12k}\left(\frac{\|v_{F_{1}}\|}{\|v_{F_{2}}\|}+\frac{\|v_{F_{2}}\|}{\|v_{F_{1}}\|}\right)-s(h,k)+\frac{1}{4}\bigg].

We note that it is possible to obtain the latter formulas for the Ehrhart quasi-coefficients using the methods of Berline and Vergne
[
7
]
(see
[
7
, Proposition 31]
pertaining to a formula corresponding to Theorem
2.3
, although the correspondence is a nontrivial notational task).

2.3.
Comments about algorithmic aspects

In this section we show how to compute the local parameters in the formula from Theorem
2.1
, provided we are given the hyperplane description of the polytope. This formula uses the volumes of the faces of
P
, and we recall that the theoretical complexity of computing volumes of polytopes, from their facet description, is known to be
\#P
-hard (see
[
12
]
).
In addition, the formula (Theorem
2.1
) also uses solid-angles, which may be irrational. We therefore don’t make statements about the theoretical complexity of computing with such formula. However we remark that in practice such computations can be approximated (for the solid-angles), especially if the dimension of the polytope is fixed (see
[
12
]
).

For an integer vector
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, denote by
\gcd(x)
the greatest common divisor of its entries. Let the defining inequalities of the two facets
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
incident to a
(d-2)
-dimensional face
G
be
\left\langle a_{1},x\right\rangle\leq b_{1}
and
\left\langle a_{2},x\right\rangle\leq b_{2}
, with
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
a_{j}
is an outward-pointing normal vector to
F_{j}
, we can compute
v_{F_{j}}=\frac{1}{\gcd(a_{j})}a_{j}
. Hence
c_{G}:=-\left\langle N_{P}(F_{1}),N_{P}(F_{2})\right\rangle=-\frac{\left\langle v_{F_{1}},v_{F_{2}}\right\rangle}{\|v_{F_{1}}\|\|v_{F_{2}}\|}
.

Next we show how a lattice basis for
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
can be computed. We observe that by Lemma
3.2
below,
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
corresponds to the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
onto
\mathrm{lin}(G)^{\perp}
.
Denoting the
d\times 2
matrix with columns
v_{F_{1}}
and
v_{F_{2}}
by
U
, we have that
P=U(U^{\sf T}U)^{-1}U^{\sf T}
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{lin}(G)^{\perp}
. Indeed, one can check directly that
PU=U
,
P^{2}=P
and
Pv=0
for any
v\in\mathrm{lin}(G)
. Therefore the columns of
P
generate
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
. From a set of generating vectors, one can compute a lattice basis by an application of the LLL-algorithm (as described by Buchmann and Pohst
[
10
]
).

Let
m=1
and
j=2
, or
m=2
and
j=1
. We now proceed to compute
v_{F_{m},G}
, the
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
-primitive vector in the direction of
N_{F_{m}}(G)
. Let

f_{m,j}:=\left\langle v_{F_{m}},v_{F_{m}}\right\rangle v_{F_{j}}-\left\langle v_{F_{m}},v_{F_{j}}\right\rangle v_{F_{m}}.

(13)

It is an integer vector in
\mathrm{lin}(G)^{\perp}
orthogonal to
v_{F_{m}}
and since
\left\langle f_{m,j},v_{F_{j}}\right\rangle>0
(by Cauchy-Schwarz), it is a vector in the same direction of
N_{F_{m}}(G)
. Since
f_{m,j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\mathrm{lin}(G)^{\perp}\subseteq\Lambda_{G}^{*}
, it has integral coordinates in the computed basis for
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
. Computing them and dividing by their
\gcd
, we get
v_{F_{m},G}
.

Having a lattice basis for
\Lambda_{G}^{*}
, its determinant
\det(\Lambda_{G}^{*})
can be computed directly. Also, using
v_{F_{1},G}
and
v_{F_{2},G}
, we can compute
v_{2}
such that
v_{1}:=v_{F_{1},G}
and
v_{2}
is a lattice basis. Hence we can also compute
h
and
k
. To compute
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, we can use
\bar{x}_{G}=Px_{G}
if we already know a point
x_{G}\in G
and then write
\bar{x}_{G}
in terms of
v_{F_{1},G}
and
v_{F_{2},G}
. More generally, we observe that for any point
x_{G}\in G
we must have
\left\langle a_{1},x_{G}\right\rangle=b_{1}
and
\left\langle a_{2},x_{G}\right\rangle=b_{2}
, so:

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle a_{2},\bar{x}_{G}\right\rangle=\|a_{2}\|\left\langle N_{P}(F_{2}),x_{1}v_{F_{1},G}+x_{2}v_{F_{2},G}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=x_{1}\|a_{2}\|\|v_{F_{1},G}\|\left\langle N_{P}(F_{2}),N_{F_{1}}(G)\right\rangle=x_{1}\|a_{2}\|\|v_{F_{1},G}\|\sqrt{1-c_{G}^{2}}

\displaystyle=x_{1}\|a_{2}\||\det(v_{F_{1},G},v_{F_{2},G})|/\|v_{F_{2},G}\|=x_{1}\|a_{2}\|k/\|v_{F_{2}}\|,

(see the proof of Lemma
5.1
) thus

x_{1}=\frac{b_{2}}{k\gcd(a_{2})},\quad\text{and analogously,}\quad x_{2}=\frac{b_{1}}{k\gcd(a_{1})}.

The Dedekind-Rademacher sums can be computed efficiently by proceeding as in the Euclidean algorithm, see Rademacher
[
25
]
.

3.
Preliminaries

The current section contains some definitions and background on known results, which will be useful in proving our main results.

3.1.
Lattices

A
k
-dimensional lattice

L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a discrete additive subgroup generated by any
k
linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Any set of
k
vectors that generates
L
is called a
lattice basis
.
The
determinant

\det(L)
of
L
is the
k
-dimensional volume of any fundamental domain for
L
. It is easy to compute the volume of
L
: If
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is a matrix whose columns are formed by a lattice basis of
L
, then it is a standard fact that

\det(L)=\det(B^{\sf T}B)^{1/2}.

(14)

Due to this relation, we also use the notation
|\det(B)|:=\det(B^{\sf T}B)^{1/2}
.

The
dual lattice

L^{*}
is defined as

L^{*}:=\{y\in\mathrm{span}(L):\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }x\in L\}.

We will always use
span(
L
)
to mean that we are taking the span over
\mathbb{R}
,
so that
\mathrm{span}(L)
is always a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
. Next
we assume that
L
is a subset of another lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define

L^{\perp}:=\{v\in\Lambda^{*}:\left\langle v,x\right\rangle=0\text{ for all }x\in L\},

note that
L^{\perp}\subseteq\Lambda^{*}
. The lattice
L
is called a
primitive lattice
, with respect to
\Lambda
, when

\mathrm{span}(L)\cap\Lambda=L.

Next we state two well known facts about lower-dimensional lattices (i.e. when they do not have full rank) and defer the proofs to an appendix. The first lemma is always used when translating absolute volumes to relative volumes.

3.1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional lattice and let
L\subseteq\Lambda
be a primitive lattice with respect to
\Lambda
. Then

\det(L^{\perp})=\frac{\det(L)}{\det(\Lambda)}.

We note that Lemma
3.1
is non-trivial even in the case that
\Lambda:=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
L
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional sublattice.

3.2
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional lattice and
L\subseteq\Lambda
be a primitive lattice with respect to
\Lambda
. Then

L^{*}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathrm{span}(L)}(\Lambda^{*}).

3.2.
Fourier analysis

Let
\hat{f}
and
\mathcal{F}(f)
denote the
Fourier transform
of a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
, which is defined as

\hat{f}(\xi):=\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(u)e^{-2\pi i\left\langle u,\xi\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
let the
translation by
x
be
T_{x}(\xi):=\xi-x
, and the
convolution
between two functions be
(f\ast g)(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)g(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
.
We recall the following standard identities for the Fourier transform (see e.g.,
[
30
, Chapter I, Theorem 1.4]
), where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is an invertible matrix:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(f\circ T_{x})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\hat{f}(\xi)e^{-2\pi i\left\langle x,\xi\right\rangle},

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(f\ast g)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\hat{f}(\xi)\hat{g}(\xi),

(16)

\displaystyle(\hat{f}\circ M^{\sf T})(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\det(M)|}\mathcal{F}(f\circ M^{-1})(\xi).

(17)

The following theorem, known as the Poisson summation formula, is one of the main tools in Fourier analysis (Stein and Weiss
[
30
, Chapter VII, Corollary 2.6]
).

3.3
Poisson summation.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be a function that enjoys the following two decay conditions and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional lattice. Suppose there exist positive constants
\delta
,
C
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(a)
|f(x)|<C(1+|x|)^{-n-\delta}

(b)
|\hat{f}(x)|<C(1+|x|)^{-n-\delta}
.

Then for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\det(\Lambda)\sum_{x\in\Lambda}f(x+y)=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{*}}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\left\langle y,\xi\right\rangle},

(18)

and both sides of (
18
) converge absolutely.

A function that will play a special role in this work is the
Gaussian function
, that for
\epsilon>0
is

\phi_{d,\epsilon}(x):=\epsilon^{-d/2}e^{-\pi\|x\|^{2}/\epsilon}.

Its Fourier transform is (see e.g.,
[
30
, Chapter I, Theorem 1.13]
)

\hat{\phi}_{d,\epsilon}(\xi)=e^{-\epsilon\pi\|\xi\|^{2}}.

Note that
\hat{\phi}_{d,\epsilon}
doesn’t explicitly depend on
d
(except for the
2
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) so we also denote it by
\hat{\phi}_{\epsilon}
.

3.3.
Fourier transforms of polytopes and solid angle sums

In this section we present a summary of the main results from Diaz, Le, and Robins
[
11
]
.

A solid angle at any point
x\in P
, which is also the volume of a local spherical polytope, has an analytical representation that is convenient for our purposes. To introduce it, let
\bm{1}_{P}
denote the indicator function of
P
, that is
\bm{1}_{P}(x)=1
if
x\in P
and
\bm{1}_{P}(x)=0
if
x\notin P
. The solid angle can be computed as the limit of the convolution between the Gaussian
\phi_{d,\epsilon}
and the indicator function of
P
(cf. Diaz, Le and Robins
[
11
, Lemma 1]
):

\omega_{P}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{P}\phi_{d,\epsilon}(t-x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(\bm{1}_{P}\ast\phi_{d,\epsilon})(x).

(19)

More generally, if we replace
\bm{1}_{P}
by a continuous function supported on
P
, we have the following (cf. Diaz, Le and Robins
[
11
, Lemma 3 and Theorem 5]
):

3.4
.

Let
P
be a full-dimensional polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f
be a continuous function on
P
and zero outside
P
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}(f\ast\phi_{d,\epsilon})(x)=f(x)\omega_{P}(x).

Moreover,

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(x)\omega_{P}(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(f\ast\phi_{d,\epsilon})(x).

Note that the left-hand side of the above identity is a finite sum since
P
is compact and
\omega_{P}(x)=0
for
x\notin P
, while the right-hand side is the limit of an infinite series.

The method from Diaz, Le, and Robins consists of two steps: First, the solid angles are written with convolutions and the solid angle sum is represented with the series from Lemma
3.4
, next the Poisson summation formula is applied to represent
A_{P}(t)
as a series with the Fourier transform of
P
, leading to (cf. Diaz, Le, and Robins
[
11
, Lemma 2]
):

3.5
.

Let
P
be a full-dimensional polytope
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t
any positive real number. Then the solid angle sum of
P
can be written as follows:

A_{P}(t)=t^{d}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{1}}_{P}(t\xi)e^{-\pi\epsilon\|\xi\|^{2}}.

Through successive applications of Stokes formula (in the frequency space of Poisson summation), the Fourier transform of
P
is then written as a sum over the faces of
P

[
11
, Theorem 1]
. By treating these terms carefully, keeping track of ’generic’ and ’nongeneric’ frequency vectors on the right-hand-side of Poissson summation, one can find local formulas for the coefficients
a_{d-k}(t)
.

If
F
is a face of
P
, let
\mathrm{Proj}_{F}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{lin}(F)
. If
G
is a facet of
F
, denote by
N_{F}(G)
the unit normal vector in
\mathrm{lin}(F)
pointing outward to
G
and define the weight on the pair
(F,G)
:

W_{(F,G)}(\xi):=\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\frac{\left\langle\mathrm{Proj}_{F}(\xi),N_{F}(G)\right\rangle}{\|\mathrm{Proj}_{F}(\xi)\|^{2}}.

The
face poset

G_{P}
of
P
consists of all faces of
P
ordered by inclusion and a
chain

T
of
length

l(T)=k
is a sequence of faces
T=(F_{0}\to F_{1}\to F_{2}\to\dots\to F_{k})
with
F_{0}=P
and
F_{j}
a facet of
F_{j-1}
for every
j
.

The
admissible set

S(T)
of a chain is the set of all vectors orthogonal to
\mathrm{lin}(F_{k})
but not to
\mathrm{lin}(F_{k-1})
. For a point
\xi\in S(T)
, the
rational weight

\mathcal{R}_{T}(\xi)
is the product

\mathcal{R}_{T}(\xi):=\mathrm{vol}(F_{k})\prod_{j=1}^{k}W_{(F_{j-1},F_{j})}(\xi),

(20)

where the volume of
F_{k}
is the
(d-k)
-dimensional volume and the
exponential weight
is

\mathcal{E}_{T}(\xi):=e^{-2\pi i\left\langle\xi,x_{F_{k}}\right\rangle},

(21)

where
x_{F_{k}}
is any point from
F_{k}
, the last face from chain
T
. Note that since
\xi\in S(T)
, the value of
\left\langle\xi,x_{F_{k}}\right\rangle
does not depend on the choice of
x_{F_{k}}
.

The next result from Diaz, Le, and Robins gives a formula for
a_{k}(t)
, for any positive real
t
:

3.6
.

[
11
, Theorem 2]

Let
P
be a full-dimensional rational polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
t
be a positive real number. Then we have
A_{P}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}a_{k}(t)t^{k}
, where, for
0\leq k\leq d
,

a_{k}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{T:\,l(T)=d-k}\,\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap S(T)}\mathcal{R}_{T}(\xi)\mathcal{E}_{T}(t\xi)\hat{\phi}_{\epsilon}(\xi).

Using this theorem one can get more explicit formulas for the coefficients, although their complexity increases with the length of the chains considered.
For the quasi-coefficient
a_{d-1}(t)
, we have the following known formula, given in terms of the facets of
P
and the periodized Bernoulli polynomial
\overline{B}_{1}
:

3.7
.

[
11
, Theorem 3]

Let
P
be a full-dimensional rational polytope. Then the codimension one quasi-coefficient of the solid angle sum
A_{P}(t)
has the following local formula for all positive real values of
t
:

a_{d-1}(t)=-\hskip-8.5359pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P,\\
\dim(F)=d-1\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.5359pt\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\overline{B}_{1}\big(\left\langle v_{F},x_{F}\right\rangle t\big),

where
x_{F}
is any point in
F
and
v_{F}
is the primitive integer vector in the direction of
N_{P}(F)
.

4.
Lattice sums

Let
\Lambda
be a
k
-dimensional lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
be linearly independent vectors from
\Lambda^{*}
and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
be a matrix with them as columns. For a
k
-uple
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{k})
of positive integers, let
|e|:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}e_{j}
. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, our goal in this section is to evaluate

L_{\Lambda}(W,e;x):=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{|e|}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Lambda:\\
\left\langle w_{j},\xi\right\rangle\neq 0,\forall j\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{-2\pi i\left\langle x,\xi\right\rangle}}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left\langle w_{j},\xi\right\rangle^{e_{j}}}e^{-\pi\epsilon\|\xi\|^{2}}.

(22)

These limits of lattice sums come up in the development of the expression in Theorem
3.6
, they also appear in the work of Witten, on
2
-dimensional gauge theory
[
31
, pp. 363]
, and a similar expression with a different limit process instead of the Gaussian factor is called a
Dedekind sum
by Gunnels and Sczech
[
17
]
. This name is justified since the expression obtained in Theorem
4.3
can be written as a Dedekind-Rademacher sum in the case that
e=(1,1)
(cf. Section
5.1.4
).

For any
k
-uple
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{k})
of positive integers we define a
k
-dimensional Bernoulli polynomial

\mathcal{B}_{e}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\mathcal{B}_{e}(x):=B_{e_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots B_{e_{k}}(x_{k}).

Note that
\mathcal{B}_{e}
is supported in
[0,1]^{k}
. The reason for defining these polynomials is that their Fourier transforms, evaluated at integer inputs, are the inverse of products of linear forms, as stated in the lemma below (see e.g. Apostol
[
2
, Theorem 12.19]
).

4.1
.

For all
r\geq 1
, the Fourier transform of the Bernoulli polynomial
B_{r}(x)
satisfies:

\hat{B_{r}}(n)=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{if }n=0,\\
-\frac{r!}{(2\pi i)^{r}n^{r}}\quad\text{if }n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.\end{cases}

Thus for any
k
-uple
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{k})
of positive integers,

\hat{\mathcal{B}_{e}}(m)=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{if }m_{j}=0\text{ for some }j,\\
\frac{(-1)^{d}e_{1}!\cdots e_{k}!}{(2\pi i)^{|e|}m_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots m_{k}^{e_{k}}}\quad\text{if }m\in(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})^{k}.\end{cases}

Returning to the evaluation of
L_{\Lambda}(W,e;x)
, we assume first that
\Lambda
is the full-dimensional integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
; in this case
W
is invertible. Let
P_{W,x}
be the parallelepiped

P_{W,x}:=\{n\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:W^{-1}(n-x)\in[0,1]^{d}\}=x+W[0,1]^{d}.

We prove the following theorem, which gives a finite form for (
22
), in terms of a sum over the integer points in
P_{W,x}
and the
d
-dimensional Bernoulli polynomial times a local solid angle.

4.2
.

If
W\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d}
is an invertible matrix with columns
w_{1},\dots,w_{d}
,
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{d})
is a
d
-uple of positive integers and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then:

L_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(W,e;x)=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!|\det(W)|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap P_{W,x}}\mathcal{B}_{e}\big(W^{-1}(n-x)\big)\omega_{P_{W,x}}(n).

Proof.

We recognize each term inside sum (
22
) as the Fourier transform of a function, apply Poisson summation and then use Lemma
3.4
to compute the limit.

Using Lemma
4.1
and identity (
17
) with
\mathcal{B}_{e}
and
W
, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\left\langle w_{j},\xi\right\rangle\neq 0
for all
j
, we have:

\frac{1}{(2\pi i)^{|e|}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left\langle w_{j},\xi\right\rangle^{e_{j}}}=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!}(\hat{\mathcal{B}_{e}}\circ W^{\sf T})(\xi)=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!|\det(W)|}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}_{e}\circ W^{-1})(\xi).

To obtain the same term that appears in (
22
), we make use of identity (
15
) and recall that
\hat{\phi}_{\epsilon}(\xi)=e^{-\pi\epsilon\|\xi\|^{2}}
. Further noticing that
(\hat{\mathcal{B}_{e}}\circ W^{\sf T})(\xi)=0
when
\left\langle w_{j},\xi\right\rangle=0
for some
j
, we have:

L_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(W,e;x)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!|\det(W)|}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{B}_{e}\circ W^{-1}\circ T_{x})(\xi)\hat{\phi_{\epsilon}}(\xi).

Using identity (
16
) and Poisson summation (Theorem
3.3
),

L_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(W,e;x)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!|\det(W)|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big((\mathcal{B}_{e}\circ W^{-1}\circ T_{x})\ast\phi_{d,\epsilon}\big)(n).

Note that the support of
\mathcal{B}_{e}\circ W^{-1}\circ T_{x}
is exactly
P_{W,x}
and
(\mathcal{B}_{e}\circ W^{-1}\circ T_{x})(n)=\mathcal{B}_{e}\big(W^{-1}(n-x)\big)
. This enables us to use Lemma
3.4
and obtain

L_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(W,e;x)=\frac{(-1)^{d}}{e_{1}!\cdots e_{d}!|\det(W)|}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap P_{W,x}}\mathcal{B}_{e}\big(W^{-1}(n-x)\big)\omega_{P_{W,x}}(n).\qed

The situation is almost the same for the general case where
\Lambda
is a
k
-dimensional lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, however in this case we must restrict attention to the subspace spanned by
\Lambda
. Note that
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is not invertible but when we see it as a linear transformation
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathrm{span}(\Lambda)
it is, such inverse is called the
pseudoinverse
and can be computed as
W^{+}=(W^{\sf T}W)^{-1}W^{\sf T}
. Furthermore, it follows that
WW^{+}
is the orthogonal projection
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathrm{span}(\Lambda)}
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathrm{span}(\Lambda)
. The parallelepiped
P_{W,x}
becomes a
k
-dimensional parallelepiped in
\mathrm{span}(\Lambda)
:

P_{W,x}:=\{n\in\mathrm{span}(\Lambda):W^{+}(n-x)\in[0,1]^{k}\}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathrm{span}(\Lambda)}(x)+W[0,1]^{k}.

Identity (
17
) also has to be adapted, since we are dealing with a
k
-dimensional subspace embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More specifically, for
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
and

8.
Concrete polytopes and further remarks

We introduce a family of polytopes, called concrete polytopes, which come up naturally in our context, and in the context of multi-tiling. Consider Example
7.2
, where we had a polytope
P
whose solid angle sum was
A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}
, for all positive integer values of
t
. More generally, as done by Brandolini, Colzani, Robins, and Travaglini
[
9
]
, we say that a polytope
P
is
concrete
if:

A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d},

(33)

for all positive integer values of
t
. Such polytopes are very special, because their discrete volume
A_{P}(t)
matches exactly their continuous (Lebesgue) volume.

As another example, consider any integer polygon
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is then always true that
A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{2}
, for all positive integer values of
t
, which is an equivalent formulation of
Pick’s Theorem
.

The motivation for using the word ‘concrete’ is borrowed from the title of the book “Concrete Mathematics”, where Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik mention that the word ‘concrete’, which uses the first
3
letters of ‘continuous’, and the last
5
letters of ‘discrete’, embodies objects that are both “continuous” and “discrete”.

Another special family of concrete polytopes is the collection of
integer zonotopes
(see Lemma
8.2
below). Integer zonotopes are projections of cubes or, equivalently, integer polytopes whose faces (of all dimensions) are centrally symmetric (see e.g. Ziegler
[
32
, Section 7.3]
). Alexandrov
[
1
]
, and independently Shephard
[
29
]
, proved the following fact.

8.1
Alexandrov, Shephard.

Let
P
be any real,
d
-dimensional polytope, with
d\geq 3
. If the facets of
P

are centrally symmetric, then
P
is centrally symmetric.

The following statement appeared in
[
5
, Corollary 7.7]
, but we offer a proof here that is in the spirit of the current work.

8.2
Barvinok.

Suppose
P
is a
d
-dimensional integer polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

all of whose facets are centrally symmetric. Then
P
is a concrete polytope.

Proof.

We recall the formula for the solid angle polynomial
A_{P}(t)
from Lemma
3.5
:

A_{P}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{1}}_{tP}(\xi)e^{-\pi\epsilon\|\xi\|^{2}}.

(34)

The Fourier transform of the indicator function of a polytope may be written as follows, after one application of the ‘combinatorial Stokes’ formula (see
[
11
]
, equation (26)):

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{1}}_{tP}(\xi)=t^{d}\mathrm{vol}(P)[\xi=0]+\left(\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\right)t^{d-1}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim F=d-1\end{subarray}}}\frac{\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\rangle}{\|\xi\|^{2}}\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)[\xi\not=0],

(35)

where we sum over all facets
F
of
P
. Plugging this into (
34
) we get

\displaystyle A_{P}(t)-t^{d}\mathrm{vol}(P)=\left(\frac{-1}{2\pi i}\right)t^{d-1}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{e^{-\pi\epsilon\|\xi\|^{2}}}{\|\xi\|^{2}}\sum_{{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim F=d-1\end{subarray}}}\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\rangle\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)

(36)

Thus, if we show that the latter sum over the facets vanishes, then we are done.

The assumption that all facets of
P
are centrally symmetric implies that
P
itself is also centrally symmetric, by Lemma
8.1
.
We may therefore combine the facets of
P
in pairs of opposite facets
F
and
F^{\prime}
. We know that
F^{\prime}=F+c
, where
c
is an integer vector, using the fact that the facets are centrally symmetric.

Therefore, since
N_{P}(F^{\prime})=-N_{P}(F)
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\rangle

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)+\langle\xi,-N_{P}(F)\rangle\hat{\bm{1}}_{F+c}(t\xi)

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\rangle\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)-\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\rangle\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)e^{-2\pi i\langle t\xi,c\rangle}

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,N_{P}(F)\hat{\bm{1}}_{F}(t\xi)\big(1-e^{-2\pi i\langle t\xi,c\rangle}\big)=0,

because
\langle t\xi,c\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
for
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. We conclude that the entire right-hand side of (
36
) vanishes, proving the lemma.
∎

Fourier analysis can also be used to give more general classes of polytopes that satisfy the formula
A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}
, for positive integer values of
t
. A polytope
P
is said to
k
-tile

\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or
multi-tile
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at level
k
) by integer translations, if

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\bm{1}_{P}(x-\lambda)=k

(37)

for every
x\notin\partial P+\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Gravin, Robins, and Shiryaev
[
16
, Theorem 6.1]
gave a characterization of these polytopes in terms of solid angles.

8.3
Gravin, Robins, Shiryaev.

A polytope
P

k
-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by integer translations if and only if

\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\omega_{P+v}(\lambda)=k,

for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Note that the sum on the left is equal to
A_{P+v}(1)
, so this condition can be rephrased as asking for the function
P\mapsto A_{P}(1)
to be invariant under all real translates of
P
.
To see how multi-tiling implies the concrete polytope property, note that since
f(x):=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\bm{1}_{P}(x-\lambda)
is periodic modulo
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, it has a Fourier series (see e.g.,
[
30
, Chapter VII, Theorem 2.4]
)

f(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{\bm{1}}_{P}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle},

and so
P
k-tiles by integer translations if and only if
\hat{\bm{1}}_{P}(\xi)=0
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\hat{\bm{1}}_{P}(0)=k=\mathrm{vol}(P)
. By Lemma
3.5
, we see that this implies
A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
.

Note that the order simplex in Example
7.2
doesn’t
k
-tile
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by integer translations; however, this simplex is still concrete. To produce more general concrete polytopes, we introduce two new concepts:

The
Hyperoctahedral group

{\mathsf{B}_{d}}
is the group of symmetries of the hypercube
[-1,1]^{d}
; all of its
2^{d}d!
elements are simultaneously unimodular and orthogonal transformations, hence when an element of this group is applied to a polytope it preserves its solid angle polynomial.

The
polytope group

\mathscr{P}^{d}
(cf.
[
18
, Section 3.2]
) is the abelian group formally generated by the elements
[A]
where
A
runs through all sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which can be represented as the union of a finite number of polytopes with disjoint interiors and subject to the relations
[A]+[B]=[A\cup B]
whenever
A
and
B
are two sets with disjoint interiors. Since any element
P\in\mathscr{P}^{d}
can be uniquely represented as a finite sum
Q=\sum_{j}m_{j}[A_{j}]
where
m_{j}
are distinct nonzero integers and
A_{j}
are sets with pairwise disjoint interiors, any additive function
\varphi
defined on the set of polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(such as the volume
A\mapsto\mathrm{vol}(A)
or the indicator function
A\mapsto\bm{1}_{A}
viewed as a function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) can be uniquely extended to a function in
\mathscr{P}^{d}
by linearity, that is,
\varphi(Q):=\sum_{j}m_{j}\varphi(A_{j})
for an element
Q
written as above. With this extension, the definition of multi-tiling can also be extended to
\mathscr{P}^{d}
.

With these definitions, we may adapt the proof of (the forward direction of)
[
18
, Theorem 4.1]
and prove the following more general sufficiency condition for the concrete polytope property.

8.4
.

If
P
is a rational polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
Q:=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}[\gamma P]
multi-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by integer translations, then
A_{P}(t)=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}
for all positive integers
t
.

Proof.

If
P
is a rational polytope such that
Q=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}[\gamma P]

k
-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by integer translations, then for a positive integer
t
we also have that
Q_{t}:=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}[\gamma(tP)]

(t^{d}k)
-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
D:=[0,1]^{d}
, then
Q_{t}-(t^{d}k)[D]
tiles at level zero by integer translations and by
[
18
, Proposition 3.4]
we can represent it as a finite sum
Q_{t}-(t^{d}k)[D]=\sum_{j}([B_{j}]-[B_{j}^{\prime}])
where for each
j
,
B_{j}
,
B_{j}^{\prime}
are polytopes such that
B_{j}^{\prime}
is obtained from
B_{j}
by a translation along an integer vector, thus
A_{B_{j}}(1)=A_{B_{j}^{\prime}}(1)
. Hence

A_{Q_{t}}(1)=t^{d}kA_{D}(1)=t^{d}k=\mathrm{vol}(Q)t^{d}=|{\mathsf{B}_{d}}|\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d},

where we have used that if
Q

k
-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by integer translations, then
k=\mathrm{vol}(Q)
and that the action of
{\mathsf{B}_{d}}
preserves volumes, thus
\mathrm{vol}(\gamma P)=\mathrm{vol}(P)
for all
\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}
. Also,

A_{Q_{t}}(1)=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}A_{\gamma(tP)}(1)=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}A_{tP}(1)=|{\mathsf{B}_{d}}|A_{P}(t).\qed

The simplex
\vartriangleleft
, which we used in Example
7.2
, is now seen to satisfy
the condition of Theorem
8.4

\mathsf{B}_{3}

(because the orbit of
\frac{1}{2}\!\vartriangleleft

that tiles the space by integer translations) and this is an example of a rational (and non-integer) polytope that has the concrete polytope property.

As a side-note, this fact can also be seen in the expression given for
A_{\vartriangleleft}(t)
in Example
7.2
, verifying the fact that
a_{2}(1/2)=a_{1}(1/2)=a_{0}(1/2)=0
, and using the fact that all coefficients of the quasi-polynomial have period

.

It seems natural to ask whether multi-tiling is a necessary and sufficient condition for a polytope to be concrete, as follows.

Question
.
Is a rational polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
concrete if and only if

Q:=\sum_{\gamma\in{\mathsf{B}_{d}}}[\gamma P]

multi-tiles
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by integer translations?

This question has a negative answer, however, as very recently shown by Garber and Pak
[
14
]
. They produced a counterexample in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, based on the Dehn invariant of the direct sum of some tetrahedra and then extended it to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It remains an open question to give necessary and sufficient conditions for which
P
is concrete. Perhaps a more general type of tiling is required.

Question.

Suppose we know the solid angle quasi-polynomial
A_{P}(t)
, for all positive
t
, but we also know that it is associated to a rational polytope
P
. Can we recover
P
completely, up to the action of the finite hyperoctahedral group
{\mathsf{B}_{d}}
?

Question.

Can the current theory be extended to all real polytopes?

Appendix A
Local formulas, SI-interpolators and alternative approaches

In this appendix we briefly summarize the method developed by Berline and Vergne
[
7
]
, which produce local formulas for the Ehrhart quasi-coefficients based on a connection between exponential sums and integrals. We refer the reader to the original paper
[
7
]
for the formal definitions and detailed proofs and also to the subsequent works of Barvinok
[
4
]
and Garoufalidis and Pommersheim
[
15
]
, from which we borrow some concepts.

Consider the set
V
of all convex polyhedra in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A
valuation
in
V
is any map
\phi
from
V
to some vector space
that enjoys the property
\phi(\emptyset)=0
and also

\phi(P\cup Q)=\phi(P)+\phi(Q)-\phi(P\cap Q),

for all
P,Q\in V
such that
P\cup Q\in V
. In other words, valuations respect the inclusion-exclusion property enjoyed by polyhedra.
Our goal here is to describe a valuation
\mu
that associates to every polyhedra an analytic function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
which can be used to define a local formula for the Ehrhart polynomial
[
7
, Corollary 30]
:

|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(0),

and therefore for its coefficients

e_{k}(P;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim(F)=k\end{subarray}}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(tP,tF)\big)(0).

Here we focus on two particular valuations, called
the
exponential integral

I(P)
and the
exponential sum

S(P)
,
which associates to each polyhedra
P\in V
a meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
that is zero on polyhedra that contains lines and such that for every
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
which makes the right hand side absolutely integrable or summable,

I(P)(\xi)=\int_{P}e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{P}(x),\qquad S(P)(\xi)=\sum_{x\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle},

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{P}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on the affine span of
P
normalized so that
\det(\mathrm{lin}(P)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d})=1
. Notice that
I(P)
is the Fourier transform of
P
, up to a change of variables and an extension of domain.

Recall that if
F
is a face of a polyhedron
P
, the tangent cone of
P
at
F
is

\mathrm{tcone}(P,F):=\{x+\lambda(y-x):x\in F,y\in P,\lambda\geq 0\}.

Berline and Vergne
[
7
, Theorem 20]
proved the existence of a valuation
\mu
which associates to every rational affine cone in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
an analytic function such that for every rational polyhedron
P
, we have

S(P)(\xi)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\xi)I(F)(\xi),

(38)

where the sum is taken over the set of all faces of
P
. The valuation
\mu
is called a
SI-interpolator
by Garoufalidis and Pommersheim
[
15
]
and is uniquely defined up to a
certain rule that extends functions initially defined on subspaces, to functions that exist on the entire space (e.g., orthogonal projection).

Now we assume that
P
is a polytope (hence compact).
Since
\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)
is an analytic function, we may use its
Taylor expansion at
0
to define a differential operator for each face
F
of
P
:

D(P,F):=\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\partial_{x}).

(39)

This operator satisfies:

D(P,F)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}=\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\xi)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}.

(40)

In other words, the valuations
\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\xi)
are eigenvalues of the differential
operator (
39
).

Now, for any polynomial
h(x)
we may define an associated differential operator

D_{h}:=h(\partial_{\xi}),

which clearly satisfies

D_{h}e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}=h(x)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}.

Next, we take (
38
) and apply the definition of the operator

D(P,F)
to get

\displaystyle S(P)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\xi)I(F)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\int_{F}\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(\xi)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\int_{F}D(P,F)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{F}(x).

Applying
D_{h}
to both sides,

\displaystyle D_{h}S(P)(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\int_{F}D(P,F)D_{h}e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{F}(x)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h(x)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\int_{F}D(P,F)h(x)e^{\left\langle\xi,x\right\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{F}(x).

Evaluating the latter identity at
\xi=0
, we get:

\sum_{x\in P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h(x)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\int_{F}D(P,F)h(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}_{F}(x).

(41)

Equation (
41
) is called an Euler-Maclaurin summation formula since the sum
on the right is expressed in terms of integrals taken over the faces of
P
, of functions that depend only on local information along each face.
Applying it to the constant function
h(x)=1
and noticing that the constant term of
D(P,F)
is equal to
\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(0)
, we get a local formula for the Ehrhart polynomial
[
7
, Corollary 30]
:

|P\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(P,F)\big)(0),

and thus for its coefficients

e_{k}(P;t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim(F)=k\end{subarray}}\mathrm{vol}^{*}(F)\mu\big(\mathrm{tcone}(tP,tF)\big)(0).

Finally, we note that equation (
38
) together with
\mu({0})(\xi)=1
defines
\mu(K)
recursively on the dimension of
K
and this relation can be used to compute the coefficients for low dimensional cones
[
7
, Proposition 31]
.

Once the formulas for the Ehrhart coefficients are obtained by the method outlined above, the solid angle sum of a polytope can also be obtained with the following formula

A_{P}(t)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\omega_{P}(F)L_{\mathrm{int}(F)}(t)=\sum_{F\subseteq P}\omega_{P}(F)(-1)^{\dim(F)}L_{F}(-t).

This approach reverse the order of things we have taken in this paper. We take it here to complement Section
6
and show how the Ehrhart and solid angle sum expressions interrelate. Next we take the formulas from Theorems
6.3
and
2.3
as given and use them to recover Theorems
3.7
and
2.1
.

Expanding the Ehrhart quasi-polynomials of each face and comparing quasi-coefficients, we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(P)

\displaystyle t^{d}+a_{d-1}(P;t)t^{d-1}+a_{d-2}(P;t)t^{d-2}+\dots

\displaystyle=\mathrm{vol}(P)t^{d}+\Big(-e_{d-1}(P;-t)+\hskip-11.38092pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim(F)=d-1\end{subarray}}\hskip-11.38092pt\frac{1}{2}e_{d-1}(F;-t)\Big)t^{d-1}

\displaystyle\ +\Big(e_{d-2}(P;-t)-\hskip-12.80365pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subset P\\
\dim(F)=d-1\end{subarray}}\hskip-11.38092pt\frac{1}{2}e_{d-2}(F;-t)+\hskip-11.38092pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\subset P\\
\dim(G)=d-2\end{subarray}}\hskip-11.38092pt\omega_{P}(G)e_{d-2}(G;-t)\Big)t^{d-2}+\dots

Great care has to be taken before using Theorems
6.3
and
2.3
, because these theorems assume the polytopes to be full-dimensional, while to use the expressions above we must consider all of the lower dimensional faces
F
.

The main difference is that when
0\notin\mathrm{aff}(F)
, then
L_{F}(t)=0
for all

Proof.

Through this proof, for any set

of vectors in

is the matrix with

Let

be a basis for

and

be a completion to a basis for
\Lambda
(that is possible since

. Note that

, so that

(42)
