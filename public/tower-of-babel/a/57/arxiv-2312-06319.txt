Superdiffusion of vortices in two-component quantum fluids of light Preprint: APS/123-QED
M. Roldão, J. L. Figueiredo, P. Monteiro, J. T. Mendonça, H. Terças

Abstract
The quantum diffusion of a vortex in a two-component quantum fluid of light is investigated. In these systems, the Kerr nonlinearity promotes interactions between the photons, displaying features that are analogue of a Bose-Einstein condensates. Quantum fluids of light have the advantage of simulating matter-wave phenomena at room temperatures. While the analogy is true at the mean field level, the full quantum dynamics of an impurity in quantum fluids of light of, and therefore the ability of featuring genuine quantum noise, has never been considered. We numerically solve the problem by simulating a vortex-like impurity in the presence of noise with the Bogoliubov spectral density, and show that the vortex undergoes superdiffusion. We support our results with a theory that has been previously developed for the brownian motion of point-like particles.



Introduction.—
The first observations of diffusive processes in nature are due to Brown in 1827, who reported on the erratic motion of wildflower pollen suspended in water
Brown 1828
;
Erdos 2010
. Theoretical understanding of the brownian motion remained poor until 1905, with Einstein’s and Smoluchowski’s kinetic explanations, proposing that the heavy pollen particules are propelled by the sucessive kicks by the light water molecules
Einstein 1905
;
Smoluchowski 1906
. The success of the kinetic theory of the brownian motion led to Perrin’s estimation of Avogadro’s number in 1908
Bian et al. 2016
. An intriguing variation of the phenomenon appears in the quantum regime
Meyer 1996
;
Lampo et al. 2019
, with profound consequences in condensed matter systems, such as Anderson localization, which has been observed for both matter and light waves. Quantum brownian motion may originate from random external potentials such as disorder or via the coupling to a random reservoir. In the latter case, the Caldeira-Legget model provides a semi-empirical treatment of the coupling between a quantum particle and the environment, overcoming the difficulties of obtaining a Langevin equation from the standard procedures of quantization
Caldeira and Leggett 1983a
;
Caldeira and Leggett 1983b
;
Caldeira et al. 1993
. Depending on the specific form of the particle-bath coupling, the system may display normal or anomalous diffusion
Bouchaud and Georges 1990
;
Hegde and Chandrashekar 2022
. Over the recent years, a great deal of attention has been given to the investigation of quantum diffusion in cold atoms, with the intention of implementing the polaron problem by immersing quantum impurities in a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC)
Zipkes et al. 2010
;
Schmid et al. 2010
;
Catani et al. 2012
;
Spethmann et al. 2012
;
Scelle et al. 2013
;
Rentrop et al. 2016
;
Schmidt et al. 2018
. Recently, the quantum diffusion of a Bose polaron in BECs has been suggested as a route towards high-sensitivity measurements of temperature in the sub-nanokelvin regime
Khan et al. 2021
;
Khan et al. 2022
.

Another interesting physical system in which quantum brownian motion could possibly occur, albeit lacking investigation, are quantum fluids of light (QFL), often referred as the optical analogues of Bose-Einstein condensates
Carusotto and Ciuti 2013
;
Carusotto 2014
. Quantum fluids of light are platforms where photon-photon interactions are possible thanks to the Kerr nonlinearity, encompassed in the nonlinear part of the susceptibility,
\displaystyle\chi^{(3)}
. The fluidic characteristic of a light beam has been first discussed by Brambilla
Brambilla et al. 1991
and Staliunas
Staliunas 1993
, but have received a great deal of attention in the more recent years
Chiao and Boyce 1999
;
Larré and Carusotto 2015
;
Vocke et al. 2015
;
Michel et al. 2018
;
Ferreira et al. 2018
. At the mean-field level, paraxial QFLs are governed by an equation of the Ginzburg-Landau type, which contains a biunivocal correspondence with the Gross-Pitaevskii equation
Carusotto 2014
. Such correspondence has allowed the emulation many physical effects taking place in BECs, such as solitons and vortex formation
Swartzlander and Law 1992
, quantum turbulence
Rodrigues et al. 2020
;
Silva et al. 2017
;
Silva et al. 2021a
;
Ferreira et al. 2022
;
Baker-Rasooli et al. 2023
and spectral condensation
Sun et al. 2012
;
Šantić et al. 2018
, Rayleigh-Taylor instabilities
Jia et al. 2012
, and have even been pointed as analogue (also known as hydrodynamic, or Unruh
Unruh 1981
) black holes linked to the existence of a local sound speed
Marino 2008
;
Solnyshkov et al. 2011
;
Nguyen et al. 2015
;
Carusotto
;
Jacquet et al. 2023
. Moreover, spin-orbit coupling in QFLs are possible in nonparaxial configurations, making possible the quantum simulation with multi-component BECs
Martone et al. 2021
.

Another crucial benchmark of the viability of QFLs as quantum simulators of BECs has been the recent observation of the Bogoliubov spectrum, an aspect that is intimately related to the bosonic nature of the quantum excitations
Fontaine et al. 2018
;
Piekarski et al. 2021
. Therefore, testing the ability of the latter to produce superdiffusion would constitute a critical test to the full quantum mechanical nature of QFLs, therefore opening the door towards the simulation of open quantum systems with fully optical setups.

In this Letter, we theoretically investigate the brownian motion of a quantum vortex in a two-component quantum fluid of light propagating in a rubidium vapor, which is an appealing platform for applications quantum technologies
Reim et al. 2011
;
Katz and Firstenberg 2018
. In our scheme, the two components correspond to the different polarizations of the electric field
Silva et al. 2021a
. We show that a quantum vortex undergoes
superdiffusion
, which is compatible with the superohmic nature of the Bogoliubov spectrum.

Two-component quantum fluids of light.—
Quantum fluids of light (QFL) refer to the collective behavior of photons in nonlinear media. Considering a centrosymmetric media, such as alkali metals, the polarization field
\displaystyle\bm{P}
can be expanded to the third-order in the electric field
\displaystyle\bm{E}
as

\bm{P}=\epsilon_{0}\left(\chi^{(1)}\bm{E}+\chi^{(3)}|\bm{E}|^{2}\bm{E}\right)+\mathcal{O}(E^{5}),

(1)

where
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}
is the dielectric permittivity and
\displaystyle\chi^{(1)}=\sqrt{n_{1}^{2}-1}
, with
\displaystyle n_{1}=c/(\partial_{k}\omega_{L})
is the (linear) refractive index. The term
\displaystyle\chi^{(3)}
is commonly designated as the nonlinear optical Kerr effect
Mansuripur 2009
and leads to a dependence of the index of refraction on the applied electric field, i.e. the intensity of the photon beam. For rubidium, the Kerr susceptibility
\displaystyle\chi^{(3)}={\rm Re}\chi^{(3)}(\delta,T)+i\rm{Im}\chi^{(3)}(\delta,T)
is a function of the detuning
\displaystyle\delta=\omega_{L}-\omega_{0}
, where
\displaystyle\omega_{L}
is the laser frequency and
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
is the D2-transition frequency and
\displaystyle T
is the temperature
Aladjidi et al. 2022
. In what follows, we decompose the electric field into two orthogonal polarizations
\displaystyle\bm{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}
, with
\displaystyle\alpha=\{1,2\}
, as
\displaystyle\bm{E}(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{\alpha}E_{\alpha}(\bm{r}_{\perp},t)\bm{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}e^{ik_{L}z-i\omega_{L}t}
, with
\displaystyle\bm{r}_{\perp}=(x,y)
denoting the perpendicular direction and
\displaystyle z
the propagation direction. Under the paraxial approximation, each polarization is governed by the following equation
Kivshar and Agrawal 2003
;
Silva et al. 2021b

i\dfrac{\partial E_{\alpha}}{\partial z}=-\frac{1}{2k_{L}}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}E_{\alpha}-\frac{3}{2}n_{2}k_{L}|E_{\alpha}|^{2}E_{\alpha}-n_{2}k_{L}|E_{\beta}|^{2}E_{\alpha},

(2)

where the nonlinear refractive index is given by
\displaystyle n_{2}\equiv\chi^{(3)}/n_{0}
, with
\displaystyle n_{0}
being the rubidium vapor density (which, in turn, is controlled through the temperature
\displaystyle T
). Recent quantitative measurements show that its absolute value
\displaystyle|n_{2}|
increase with the vapour density and decrease with both
\displaystyle|\delta|
and beam intensity
\displaystyle|\bm{E}|^{2}

Wang et al. 2020
. As
\displaystyle n_{2}
is also frequency dependent, the frequency detuning
\displaystyle\delta
may be such that
\displaystyle n_{2}<0
, which corresponds to a self-defocusing medium. This is the conditions we consider in the remainder of this manuscript.

For a more quantitative analysis, it is convenient to map the optical problem into a Bose-Einstein condensate. In the zero temperature limit, BECs can be described by a macroscopic order parameter
\displaystyle\Psi
that obeys the Gross-Pitaevskii equation. This phenomenon of condensation involves a dominant occupation of the lowest quantum state, which motivates writing the
\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}
operator as a sum of two terms,
\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}(\bm{r},t)=\Psi(\bm{r},t)+\delta\hat{\varphi}(\bm{r},t)
. The first term
\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{r},t)=\bra{0}\hat{\Phi}(\bm{r},t)\ket{0}
is the mean field order parameter and refers to the condensed fraction of the system, while
\displaystyle\delta\hat{\varphi}(\bm{r},t)
corresponds to the fluctuations (or excitations), the fraction that is not condensed in the GS. Since we are considering a weakly interacting gas (
\displaystyle a_{s}\ll\lambda_{dB}
, the de Broglie wavelength), excitations are expected to be small. Neglecting the excitations, we have the Gross-Pitaevskii equation for the condensate

i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\left[-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}}{2m}+V_{\text{trap}}(\bm{r})+g|\Psi|^{2}\right]\Psi.

(3)

This equation is valid for Bose-Einstein condensates if the temperature of the system is much lower than the critical temperature of condensation,
\displaystyle T_{c}
. For these systems, the Bogoliubov dispersion relation is written as

\omega^{2}=c^{2}k^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}k^{4}}{4m^{2}},

(4)

where
\displaystyle c_{s}=\sqrt{gn_{0}/m}=\xi/\tau
is the speed of sound
Bogoliubov 1947
. Equations (
2
) and (
3
) have strong similarities between their terms, this being what justifies the parallelism established between nonlinear optics and the BEC physics.

Diffusion and non-Markovianity.—
We intend to simulate the quantum dynamics of an impurity in a Bose-Einstein condensate. This system is ruled by a system of coupled Gross-Pitaevskii equations. For quantum fluids of light, interactions between bosons have a fixed relation of
\displaystyle\sigma\equiv g_{21}/g_{11}=2/3

Kivshar and Agrawal 2003
;
Silva et al. 2021b
; space and time are scaled such that
\displaystyle\tau=t/t^{\prime}=\hbar/\mu
,
\displaystyle\xi_{i}=r/r^{\prime}=\hbar/\sqrt{g_{ii}m_{i}n_{0}}
and
\displaystyle g_{ii}=\mu_{i}/n_{0}
with
\displaystyle i=\{1,2\}
. We are interested in the case
\displaystyle m_{1}
=
\displaystyle m_{2}
and
\displaystyle g_{11}=g_{22}
, such that Eqs. (
2
) read

\begin{cases}i\dfrac{\partial\Psi_{1}}{\partial t^{\prime}}=\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}+|\Psi_{1}|^{2}+\sigma|\Psi_{2}|^{2}\right]\Psi_{1}\\
\\
i\dfrac{\partial\Psi_{2}}{\partial t^{\prime}}=\left[-\dfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}+\sigma|\Psi_{1}|^{2}+|\Psi_{2}|^{2}\right]\Psi_{2}.\end{cases}

(5)

To solve the system in Eq. (
5
), we follow a finite-difference, Cranck-Nicholson scheme. A Runge-Kutta method of
\displaystyle 4^{\text{th}}
order in the time domain is used. The simulation parameters were chosen to guarantee convergence
Leveque 1998
(
\displaystyle dx^{2}/dt\geq 2
),
\displaystyle dx=0.1\xi
,
\displaystyle dt=0.005\tau
. The wavefunction
\displaystyle\Psi_{1}
is the background that holds the bosonic excitations, while
\displaystyle\Psi_{2}
is the impurity, a vortex-like defect in density, which we initialize with the help of a imaginary-time evolution method. Dirichlet boundary conditions were used with a squared box of side
\displaystyle 120\xi.
The thermal bath corresponding to the Bogoliubov excitations has the prescribed spectral density. The cutoff frequency
\displaystyle\Lambda_{c}
, or the
\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{min}}
, selected the dominant frequency in the noise spectrum. A snapshot of the simulation can be found in Fig.

As described in
Khan et al. 2021
, the dynamics of an impurity in a BEC implies the interactions between this particle and the thermal heat bath where it is immersed. The total Hamiltonian is

H=H_{I}+H_{B}+H_{BB}+H_{IB}.

(6)

The spectral density of the heat bath is calculated through the tensor
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{g_{\bm{k}}}}=\underline{g_{\bm{k}}}\cdot\underline{g_{\bm{k}}}^{T}
as

\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)=\sum\limits_{\bm{k}\neq 0}\hbar\underline{\underline{g_{\bm{k}}}}\delta(\omega-\omega_{\bm{k}}).

(7)

For the generic d-dimensional case the spectral density is a diagonal tensor with entries
\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{d}
(due to spatial symmetry of the noise). For a point-like particle in two dimensions (QFL analogy),

\mathcal{J}_{2}(\omega)=\frac{\sigma^{2}\omega_{0}^{4}m_{B}^{2}}{g_{B}n_{0}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}+1}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}+1}-1\right)^{2}.

(8)

Here
\displaystyle m_{B}
is the bosonic mass,
\displaystyle n_{0}
is the untrapped density, and
\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\tau^{-1}=\mu/\hbar
. Other important aspect of the impurity dynamics is due to the Markovianity of its motion. Formally, Markovianity is tackled through the Langevin equation for the motion of the impurity
where the damping kernel
\displaystyle\Gamma(t)
is obtained from the Spectral density
\displaystyle\underline{\underline{J}}
as

\underline{\underline{\Gamma}}(t)=\frac{1}{m_{I}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\frac{1}{\omega}\;\underline{\underline{J}}(\omega)\cos(\omega t).

(9)

When the damping kernel is not zero, the dynamics of the system has a memory term. Only when the damping kernel is zero the dynamics is Markovian: in terms of the Spectral density, when
\displaystyle J(\omega)=\eta\omega
.

The mean square displacement of the impurity (MSD), for the long time limit in the two dimensional case, is

\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^{2}\rangle=\left[\langle\dot{x}(0)^{2}\rangle+\frac{\hbar\tau^{2}\Lambda_{c}^{3}}{6m_{I}}\right]\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{2}}\right)^{2},

(10)

where
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=1+\frac{1}{4}(\Lambda_{c}\tau)^{2}
.
Considering the characteristic healing length
\displaystyle\xi=\hbar/\sqrt{\mu m}
and
\displaystyle\tau=\hbar/\mu
, being the chemical potential
\displaystyle\mu=gn_{0}
, we get

\frac{\text{MSD}(t)}{\xi^{2}}=\frac{m_{B}}{m_{I}}\frac{\tau\Lambda_{c}^{3}}{6}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_{2}}\right)^{2},

(11)

choosing
\displaystyle\langle\dot{x}(0)^{2}\rangle=0
. The ratio
\displaystyle m_{B}/m_{I}
influences the amplitude of the movement, such as the cutoff frequency
\displaystyle\Lambda_{c}
. A superdiffusion regime have therefore been predicted and the system should contain non-Markovian effects for a Bogoliubov heat bath acting on an impurity
Khan et al. 2021

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{2}=\hbar\tau^{2}\Lambda_{c}^{3}/(6m_{I}\alpha_{2}^{2})

The accuracy of the mean-squared displacement, being a statistical quantity, is increased with the number of runs. Its error will increase in time for diffusive processes and will require more runs to predict more accurately its behaviour for larger time scales.

Conclusions and future work.—
Quantum fluids of light were introduced as an analogy between nonlinear optics and Bose-Einstein condensation, a formal analogy that may be of extreme importance and relevance in both areas, as well as for real quantum computing applications, such as the development of quantum memories. Regarding the study of diffusive effects on Bose-Einstein condensates, a numerical simulation involving the interaction between a vortex solution, as an impurity and a thermal Bogoliubov bath was studied. All the parameters were discussed, as well as the important and relevant details from the numerical point of view, such as the conditions to achieve numerical stability, but also physically, for example, the imaginary time evolution procedure. The results were analyzed and motivated a deeper study of the assumptions that were made, leading to a detailed discussion of the impurity density profile and its role in the interactions. The regimes of superdiffusion or standard Brownian diffusion were evaluated and their relation with the loss of markovianity was presented as a direct consequence of the interactions of the impurity with the quantum noise bath, in particular, a direct consequence of the noise spectrum that is used. The spectrum of the noise was discussed to have immense importance to the behaviour of the system and its super or subdiffusive regime. We concluded that in the situation where a white noise spectrum is included, or we reach the high-temperature regime, superdiffusion is lost, as well as the memory effects intrinsic to non-markovianity. Aiming to reach more diverse results in this matter, the next steps were discussed as well as other interesting configurations that might be studied with the same algorithm.
