Theoretical modeling of capillary surfer interactions on a vibrating fluid bath Preprint: Preprint #
Anand U. Oza, Giuseppe Pucci, Ian Ho, Daniel M. Harris

Abstract
We present and analyze a theoretical model for the dynamics and interactions of “capillary surfers,” which are millimetric objects that self-propel while floating at the interface of a vibrating fluid bath. In our companion paper Ho et al. 2022 , we reported the results of an experimental investigation of the surfer system, which showed that surfer pairs may lock into one of seven bound states, and that larger collectives of surfers self-organize into coherent flocking states. Our theoretical model for the surfers’ positional and orientational dynamics approximates a surfer as a pair of vertically oscillating point sources of weakly viscous gravity-capillary waves. We derive an analytical solution for the associated interfacial deformation and thus the hydrodynamic force exerted by one surfer on another. Our model recovers the bound states found in experiments and exhibits good quantitative agreement with experimental data. Moreover, a linear stability analysis shows that the bound states are quantized on the capillary wavelength, with stable branches of equilibria separated by unstable ones. Generally, our work shows that self-propelling objects coupled by interfacial flows constitute a promising platform for studying active matter systems in which both inertial and viscous effects are relevant.



I
Introduction

Over the last several decades, there has been significant interest in understanding the physics of so-called “wet” active matter systems, in which constituents consume energy in order to move through a fluid medium
Marchetti et al. 2013
;
Ramaswamy 2010
;
Gompper et al. 2020
. Such systems are ubiquitous in biology and span the Reynolds-number spectrum. On one end, organisms at the microscale interact through low-Reynolds number (viscous or Stokesian) hydrodynamic interactions
Winkler and Gompper 2018
;
Dombrowski et al. 2004
;
Wensink et al. 2012
. On the other end, schools of fish and flocks of birds generate relatively high-Reynolds number flows in which inertial effects are dominant
Portugal et al. 2014
;
Ashraf et al. 2017
;
Wu 2011
. Interfacial active systems consist of objects or organisms that self-propel at a liquid-gas interface, and typically exist in an intermediate regime in which both inertial and viscous forces are relevant
Klotsa 2019
. Examples include water-walking insects
Hsieh and Lauder 2004
;
Bush and Hu 2006
;
Hu et al. 2003
;
Hu and Bush 2005
, bio-inspired self-propellers
Yuan and Cho 2012
and self-assembled magnetic swimmers
Snezhko et al. 2009
;
Kokot et al. 2017
;
Sukhov et al. 2019
. Prior work has shown that floating solid bodies can self-propel due to the net flow generated by AC electrowetting
Yuan and Cho 2015
, and that floating water droplets
Pucci et al. 2011
;
Ebata and Sano 2015
;
Pucci 2015
and bouncing oil droplets
Couder et al. 2005
;
Bush and Oza 2021
may self-propel across a vibrating fluid bath due to interfacial Faraday waves. Moreover, camphor boats self-propel due to gradients in surface tension
Nagayama et al. 2004
;
Hirose et al. 2020
and thus exhibit rich collective behavior
Kohira et al. 2001
;
Suematsu et al. 2010
;
Heisler et al. 2012
;
Ikura et al. 2013
.

In a companion paper
Ho et al. 2022
, we report the discovery of a new interfacial active system named “capillary surfers” [Fig.
1
(a)]. A surfer consists of a millimetric hydrophobic body [Fig.
1
(b)] that floats on the surface of a vertically vibrating fluid bath of water-glycerol mixture [Fig.
1
(c)]. All experiments are performed below the Faraday instability threshold, above which subharmonic standing waves spontaneously form at the free surface
Faraday 1831
. A surfer is front-back asymmetric and thus tilts slightly backwards in equilibrium, with the contact line remaining pinned to the surfer’s base perimeter. The vibration of the bath results in the vertical oscillation of the surfer, and the subsequent generation of a radiated, propagating wavefield. The surfer thus moves along its long axis in the direction of its thinner half [Fig.
1
(a,c)], the velocity being constant in the absence of external perturbations and other surfers. In the following we refer to the front and back of the surfer as the “bow” and “stern,” respectively.

For a given surfer geometry, the surfer speed increases with the forcing acceleration and decreases with the forcing frequency [Fig. S1 in
Ho et al. 2022
]. Moreover, surfers interact through the wavefields that they generate and thus exhibit novel collective behavior. Specifically, experiments have demonstrated that when pairs of surfers are set into motion towards each other, they may spontaneously arrange into a variety of different bound states [Fig. 2 in
Ho et al. 2022
]. The system also exhibits multistability: multiple bound states may coexist for the same experimental parameters, and these states are quantized on the capillary length [Fig. 3 in
Ho et al. 2022
]. Collections of more surfers may self-organize due to their mutual capillary wavefield and exhibit ordered flocking states [Fig. 4 in
Ho et al. 2022
]. The goal of this paper is to construct and analyze a theoretical model for capillary surfer interactions in order to rationalize the experimental observations.

In order to build such a model, we require a theory for the interfacial deformation induced by capillary-scale floating objects. Approximate expressions for the capillary forces between stationary spherical and cylindrical bodies have been derived
Chan et al. 1981
;
Kralchevsky et al. 1992
;
Vella and Mahadevan 2005
;
Oettel et al. 2005
;
Danov et al. 2005
, and review articles have detailed experimental and theoretical efforts to understand the capillary interactions between bodies trapped at fluid interfaces
Kralchevsky and Nagayama 2000
;
Oettel and Dietrich 2008
. The
dynamic
problem, wherein the bodies oscillate at the interface and thus generate a time-dependent wavefield, has received comparatively less attention. Prior work has focused on the deformations generated by relatively large bodies, for which gravitational forces dominate over surface tension
John 1950
. Asymptotic expressions in both the long-
Ursell 1949
and short-wave limits
Ursell and Taylor 1953
;
Leppington 1972
;
Leppington 1973
;
Rhodes-Robinson and Ursell 1982
;
Simon and Ursell 1985
;
Keller 2013
have been derived. De Corato & Garbin
De Corato and Garbin 2018
were the first to derive expressions for small-amplitude capillary waves generated by a periodically oscillating point force at the interface, and the resulting lateral capillary forces experienced by two oscillating point particles.

Our paper is organized as follows. In §
II
, we generalize De Corato & Garbin’s work
De Corato and Garbin 2018
to account for the effects of gravity and weak viscosity. We thus obtain in §
III
a formula for the combined static and dynamic forces between two bodies that oscillate at a fluid interface. This formula is used in §
IV
to produce a theoretical model for capillary surfers that interact through their collectively generated wave field. In §
V
, we examine the existence and stability of bound states of surfer pairs, and compare our results with experimental data reported in our companion paper
Ho et al. 2022
. Examples of collective modes exhibited by larger populations of surfers are given in §
VI
. Conclusions and avenues for future work are presented in §
VII
.

III
Interaction force between a pair of objects oscillating on a fluid interface

In Section
IV
, we propose equations of motion for a collection of interacting surfers. Assuming the interactions to be pairwise, such a framework requires a model for the force between two surfers. To our knowledge, there does not exist an analytical expression for the capillary force between two finite-sized objects oscillating on a fluid interface. For this reason, we make the following simplifying approximations: the “static” part of the force, induced by the object’s weight, is approximated by treating each object as a floating disc. This force is responsible for the so-called “Cheerios effect”
Vella and Mahadevan 2005
, which causes floating objects to clump together. The “dynamic” part of the force, induced by the object’s oscillation on the fluid interface, is approximated by treating each object as an oscillating point particle, which is the problem we solved in §
II
. This approximation is expected to be valid when the distance between surfers is much larger than the surfer’s length
L
.

III.1
Static force between floating discs

We proceed by calculating the interfacial deformation

denotes a time average over the oscillation period
2\pi/\omega
, and we use the facts that
\mathrm{Y}_{0}^{\prime}=-\mathrm{Y}_{1}
and
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}_{-1}
. A plot of the dynamic force (
17
) is shown in Fig.

r\ll\lambda_{c}
, it differs from the static force (
16
) in that it oscillates between attractive and repulsive as

increases. Note also that Eq. (
17
), which incorporates the effects of viscosity, decays much faster than its inviscid counterpart (

IV
Trajectory equations for capillary surfers

We proceed by constructing the equations of motion for a collection of interacting surfers, the relevant variables being listed in Table
1
. Consider a surfer with the “boat” geometry shown in Fig.
1
(b), with length
L
, width
w
, asymmetry
a
, stern (bow) heights
h_{+}
(
h_{-}
), mass density
\rho_{\text{s}}
, and mass
m
, floating on the free surface of a fluid bath oscillating with acceleration
\gamma
and frequency
\omega
. Since there does not exist an analytical expression for the force between two surfers oscillating on a fluid interface, we model each surfer as a pair of masses chosen to represent the surfer’s asymmetric mass distribution in experiments,
m_{+}=La\rho_{\text{s}}wh_{+}
and
m_{-}=L(1-a)\rho_{\text{s}}wh_{-}
[Fig.
1
(c)]. These masses are assumed to be non-rotating and connected by a rigid massless rod of length
l=L/2
. For the “static” part of the force, induced by the surfer’s weight, we treat each mass as a disc, and use Eq. (
16
) to approximate the interaction force between two such discs. For the “dynamic” part of the force, induced by the surfer’s oscillation on the fluid interface, we treat each mass as a point particle and use Eq. (
17
) to approximate the interaction force between two point particles.

We describe the trajectory of the
i
th surfer by its center of mass
\bm{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and orientation (unit) vector
\bm{n}_{i}(t)
, which points from
m_{+}
to
m_{-}
[Fig.
1
(d)]. The masses are located at
\bm{x}_{i,\pm}=\bm{x}_{i}\mp\mu_{\mp}l\bm{n}_{i}
, where
\mu_{\pm}=m_{\pm}/m
. Each mass moves in response to two forces: wave forces
\bm{F}_{\pm}
, time-averaged over the forcing period
2\pi/\omega
of the bath, and drag forces
-D_{\pm}\dot{\bm{x}}_{i,\pm}
due to the viscous shear stress underneath each mass. The equations of motion are thus

\displaystyle m_{\pm}\left(\ddot{\bm{x}}_{i}\mp\mu_{\mp}l\ddot{\bm{n}}_{i}\right)+D_{\pm}\left(\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}\mp\mu_{\mp}l\dot{\bm{n}}_{i}\right)=\bm{F}_{\pm}.

(18)

We now assume that
D_{\pm}=m_{\pm}/\tau_{\text{v}}
, where
\tau_{\text{v}}=mH/\eta wL
is the viscous timescale obtained by computing the shear stress due to a locally fully-developed Couette flow on the underside of the surfer. For the sake of simplicity, we neglect the influence of hydrodynamic interactions between the two masses on the values of the drag coefficients. Adding the two equations in Eq. (
18
), we obtain the trajectory equation for the center of mass

\displaystyle m\left(\ddot{\bm{x}}_{i}+\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{v}}}\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}\right)=\bm{F}_{+}+\bm{F}_{-}.

(19)

To model the rotational dynamics, we take the cross product of the first equation in Eq. (
18
) with
-\mu_{-}l\bm{n}_{i}
, the second equation with
\mu_{+}l\bm{n}_{i}
, and add the two resulting equations:

\displaystyle\left[m_{-}(\mu_{+}l)^{2}+m_{+}(\mu_{-}l)^{2}\right]\bm{n}_{i}\times\ddot{\bm{n}}_{i}+\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{v}}}\left[m_{-}(\mu_{+}l)^{2}+m_{+}(\mu_{-}l)^{2}\right]\bm{n}_{i}\times\dot{\bm{n}}_{i}=l\bm{n}_{i}\times\left[\mu_{+}\bm{F}_{-}-\mu_{-}\bm{F}_{+}\right].

(20)

Writing
\bm{n}_{i}=(\cos\theta_{i},\sin\theta_{i})
, Eq. (
20
) reduces to

\displaystyle I\ddot{\theta}_{i}+\frac{I}{\tau_{\text{v}}}\dot{\theta}_{i}=l\bm{n}_{i}\times\left(\mu_{+}\bm{F}_{-}-\mu_{-}\bm{F}_{+}\right),

(21)

where
I=m_{+}(\mu_{-}l)^{2}+m_{-}(\mu_{+}l)^{2}=\mu_{+}\mu_{-}ml^{2}
is the moment of inertia in the plane.

The wave forces
\bm{F}_{\pm}
may be decomposed into three terms: a propulsive force due to radiation pressure
(F_{\text{p}}/2)\bm{n}_{i}
, an attractive capillary force (
16
) due to the surfer’s weight, and a dynamic wave force (
17
) due to the interfacial waves generated by the surfers. The latter two are expressed as a linear superposition of the forces generated by all other surfers, as shown schematically in Fig.
1
(d). We thus obtain the trajectory equations

\displaystyle m\ddot{\bm{x}}_{i}+\frac{m}{\tau_{\text{v}}}\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=F_{\text{p}}\bm{n}_{i}+F_{c}\sum_{p,q=\pm 1}\mu_{p}\mu_{q}\sum_{j\neq i}\Phi\left(k_{c}|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|\right)\frac{\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}}{|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|},

\displaystyle I\ddot{\theta}_{i}+\frac{I}{\tau_{\text{v}}}\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=-lF_{c}\sum_{p,q=\pm 1}\mu_{p}\mu_{q}\sum_{j\neq i}p\mu_{-p}\Phi\left(k_{c}|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|\right)\bm{n}_{i}\times\frac{\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}}{|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|},

(22)

where
F_{c}=(mg)^{2}k_{c}/\sigma
is the capillary force coefficient. The interaction force
\Phi
is obtained by adding Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
), where we assume that the surfer oscillation amplitudes
\zeta_{i}
are equal to the forcing amplitude
\gamma/\omega^{2}
of the bath:

\displaystyle\Phi(r)

\displaystyle=\alpha f_{\text{s}}(r)+\frac{\xi^{2}}{24}f_{\text{d}}(r),\quad\text{where}\quad\xi=\frac{\gamma}{g},\quad\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{\pi\text{ Bo }\left(\mathrm{K}_{0}(\sqrt{\text{Bo}})+2\mathrm{K}_{1}(\sqrt{\text{Bo}})/\sqrt{\text{Bo}}\right)},

\displaystyle f_{\text{s}}(r)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{K}_{1}(\sqrt{\beta}r)\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\text{d}}(r)=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\text{Re}\left[k_{j}\frac{\mathrm{H}_{-1}(-k_{j}r)+\mathrm{Y}_{1}(-k_{j}r)}{1+\beta/3k_{j}^{2}+(4/3){\rm i}\epsilon/k_{j}+(4/3)\epsilon^{2}k_{j}}\right].

(23)

The equations (
22
) account for the lateral force and torque balances on each surfer, respectively. The trajectory equations contain a single unknown parameter
F_{\text{p}}
, whose value
F_{\text{p}}=mU/\tau_{\text{v}}
is directly inferred from the experimentally measured free speed
U
of a single surfer in isolation. We observe from Table
1
that

for

Ho et al. 2022

V

8
), (
9
), (
13
) and (

\displaystyle h(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{mg}{\sigma}\sum_{p=\pm 1}\mu_{p}\sum_{i}\mathcal{H}(k_{c}|\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|,t),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\mathcal{H}(r,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\beta}}\frac{\mathrm{K}_{0}(\sqrt{\beta}r)}{\text{Bo }\mathrm{K}_{0}(\sqrt{\text{Bo}})+2\sqrt{\text{Bo}}\,\mathrm{K}_{1}(\sqrt{\text{Bo}})}+\frac{\xi}{12}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\text{Re}\left(\frac{C_{0}(-k_{j}r)}{1+\beta/3k_{j}^{2}+(4/3){\rm i}\epsilon/k_{j}+(4/3)\epsilon^{2}k_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{{\rm i}\omega t}\right).

(24)

We proceed by non-dimensionalizing the trajectory equations (
22

\bm{x}\rightarrow k_{c}\bm{x}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{m}\ddot{\bm{x}}_{i}+\dot{\bm{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{n}_{i}+\tilde{F}_{c}\sum_{p,q=\pm 1}\mu_{p}\mu_{q}\sum_{j\neq i}\Phi(|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|)\frac{\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}}{|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|},

\displaystyle\tilde{m}\tilde{l}\ddot{\theta}_{i}+\tilde{l}\dot{\theta}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\tilde{F}_{c}\sum_{p,q=\pm 1}p\mu_{q}\sum_{j\neq i}\Phi(|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|)\bm{n}_{i}\times\frac{\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}}{|\bm{x}_{j,q}-\bm{x}_{i,p}|},

(25)

where
\tilde{l}=lk_{c}
,
\bm{x}_{i,p}=\bm{x}_{i}-p\mu_{-p}\tilde{l}\bm{n}_{i}

\displaystyle\tilde{m}=\frac{k_{c}F_{\text{p}}\tau_{\text{v}}^{2}}{m}=k_{c}U\tau_{\text{v}}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{F}_{c}=\frac{F_{c}}{F_{\text{p}}}=\frac{F_{c}\tau_{\text{v}}}{mU}

VI
Collective modes

Experiments and simulations of our model (
26
) show that collections of capillary surfers exhibit novel self-organization phenomena. For example, a many-body promenade mode has been observed in experiment [Fig. 4(a) in
Ho et al. 2022
] and simulations [Fig.
9
(a), Supplemental Video 6]. Similarly, simulations are able to reproduce the “super-orbiting mode” [Fig.
9
(b), Supplemental Video 7], wherein eight surfers execute orbital motion around a fixed center of mass [Fig. 4(b) in
Ho et al. 2022
]. Owing to its simplicity, the theoretical model is also able to produce more exotic collective modes that are currently difficult to realize in experiments. For example, Fig.
9
(c) (Supplemental Video 8) shows an exotic promenade mode of 13 surfers, in which the spacing between neighboring surfers is approximately either one or two capillary wavelengths. This mode may be thus interpreted as an aggregate of
n=1
and
n=2
promenade modes [Fig.
5
], and exhibits an example of how the multistable quantized states obtained in §
V
can be used as building blocks for many-body states. Figure
9
(d) (Supplemental Video 9) shows a similar phenomenon, wherein a square lattice of 16 surfers executes a coherent flocking state with constant velocity. This state may be interpreted as a combination of the
n=3
tailgating [Fig.
4
] and promenade modes.

VII
Conclusion

We have presented a theoretical model (
22
) for the dynamics of capillary surfers [Fig.
1
(a-b)], bodies that self-propel while oscillating at the interface of a fluid bath. The interfacial deformation generated by such a body is calculated by splitting it into static and dynamic contributions, the former resulting from the body’s weight and the latter from the prescribed oscillation of the body at the interface. The static contribution (
16
) to the force is obtained in §
III.1
by approximating the surfer as a pair of floating discs with unequal masses [Fig.
1
(c-d)]. The dynamic contribution to the force [Eq. (
17
), Fig.
2
(c)] is obtained in §
III.2
by approximating the surfer as a pair of point sources of weakly viscous gravity-capillary waves, the point-source approximation being required because there does not exist a formula for the dynamic interfacial deformation generated by a finite-sized oscillating body. The resulting formula for the dynamic force is obtained by making use of the results in §
II
, in which we solved the quasipotential wave model (
1
) and thus derived a formula [Eq. (
8
), Fig.
2
(a-b)] for the small-amplitude (linear) wavefield generated by an oscillating point source.

The resulting model contains as its only free parameter the speed of a single surfer
U
, which is obtained from experiment
Ho et al. 2022
. For the case of two surfers, the model recovers the seven bound states observed in experiments
Ho et al. 2022
[Fig.
3
]. We found exact solutions for the head-to-head, back-to-back and tailgating modes in §
V.1
and investigated their stability in Appendix
C.1
. These solutions are quantized on the capillary wavelength
\lambda_{c}
, with stable branches of solutions separated by unstable ones [Fig.
4
]. An exact solution for the promenade mode is found in §
V.2
, and the theoretical predictions correctly capture the trends observed in experiment. Moreover, the theoretically predicted dependence of the distance between surfers on the forcing frequency is in excellent quantitative agreement with experiment [Fig.
5
]. However, the predicted dependence of the distance on the forcing acceleration for a fixed value of the forcing frequency (
f=100
Hz) exhibits small but systematic discrepancies with experiment. This is presumably due to the fact that the quasipotential approximation for the wavefield is valid in the low frequency regime
\epsilon\ll 1
. The model also overpredicts the promenade speed, presumably due to the fact that we neglect modulations in the surfers’ vertical dynamics. We also found exact solutions for the orbiting [Fig.
7
], jackknife and t-bone [Fig.
8
] modes in §
V.3
and investigated their stability in Appendix
C.2
.

All of the bound states described in §
V
exhibit multistability of a discrete set of interaction states, wherein a number of states quantized on the capillary wavelength may stably coexist for the same experimental parameters. This feature is due to the wave-mediated interactions between surfers, which result in long-range spatially-oscillatory forces defined by alternating regions of attraction and repulsion [Fig.
2
(c)]. Such interactions give rise to the collective modes shown in Fig.
9
, which may be viewed as combinations of pairwise bound states.

While the point force approximation is expected to be valid when the distance between surfers is much larger than the surfer’s length, many of the bound states and collective modes reported in experiments consist of closely-separated surfers
Ho et al. 2022
. A promising future direction would thus be to develop a theory for the dynamic deformation generated by a finite-sized body oscillating on a fluid interface. A theory for dynamically floating bodies may also shed light on the propulsion mechanism of surfers, thus allowing us to eliminate the
ad hoc
propulsive force
F_{p}\bm{n}_{i}
in our model (
22
). Moreover, Fig.
9
only describes a small sample of the rich variety of collective modes expected to arise in the surfer system. The self-organization and emergent collective behavior exhibited by large populations of surfers will thus be detailed in future work.

Appendix A
Inviscid linear waves generated by an oscillating point source

We here derive the linear wave field generated by a point force oscillating harmonically on the free surface of an inviscid fluid bath in the absence of gravity, a problem first considered by De Corato & Garbin
De Corato and Garbin 2018
. The derivation proceeds as in §
II
, with the reciprocal Reynolds number and wave Bond numbers set to zero,
\epsilon=\beta=0
. Equation (
5
) then reads

\displaystyle h_{1}(\bm{x})=\frac{F_{0}}{2\pi\sigma}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}k\,\frac{k^{2}}{k^{3}-1}\mathrm{J}_{0}(kk_{c}r).

(42)

Following Appendix A in
De Corato and Garbin 2018
, we compute the integral by rewriting the rational function in the integrand above,

\displaystyle\frac{k^{2}}{k^{3}-1}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{k-1}+\frac{1}{k+\varsigma}+\frac{1}{k+\bar{\varsigma}}\right),

(43)

and using the fact that
(
Prudnikov et al. 1992
, 2.12.3.6, p. 175)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{0}(kk_{c}r)}{k+k_{0}}\,{\rm d}k=\frac{\pi}{2}C_{0}(k_{0}k_{c}r)\quad\text{for }k_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\text{ with }\text{Im}(k_{0})\neq 0.

(44)

However, the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{J}_{0}(kk_{c}r)/(k-1)\,{\rm d}k
is divergent. To make sense of the integral, we employ the limiting absorption principle and interpret it as the following limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{J}_{0}(kk_{c}r)}{k-1\pm{\rm i}\epsilon}\,{\rm d}k

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\pi}{2}C_{0}\left(\left(-1\mp{\rm i}\epsilon\right)k_{c}r\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\pi}{2}\left[-\mathrm{H}_{0}\left(\left(1\pm{\rm i}\epsilon\right)k_{c}r\right)-\mathrm{Y}_{0}\left(\left(1\pm{\rm i}\epsilon\right)k_{c}r\right)\mp 2{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{0}\left(\left(1\pm{\rm i}\epsilon\right)k_{c}r\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\mathrm{H}_{0}(k_{c}r)+\mathrm{Y}_{0}(k_{c}r)\right)\mp{\rm i}\pi\mathrm{J}_{0}(k_{c}r),

(45)

where we use the facts
(
DLMF
, Eq. 10.11.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}_{0}(-z)=-\mathrm{H}_{0}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Y}_{0}(-z)=\mathrm{Y}_{0}(z)-2{\rm i}\,\text{sgn}\left(\text{Im}[z]\right)\mathrm{J}_{0}(z)\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\text{ with }\text{Im}[z]\neq 0.

(46)

We thus obtain

\displaystyle h_{1}^{\pm}(\bm{x})=\frac{F_{0}}{12\sigma}\left\{2\,\text{Re}\left[C_{0}(\varsigma k_{c}r)\right]-\mathrm{H}_{0}(k_{c}r)-\mathrm{Y}_{0}(k_{c}r)\mp 2{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{0}(k_{c}r)\right\}.

(47)

We note that the imaginary term is missing from Eq. (3.16) in Ref.
De Corato and Garbin 2018
.

To choose the correct sign in Eq. (
47
), we use the Sommerfeld radiation condition, which ensures that the waves propagate outward from the source:

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{r}\left({{\partial}\over{\partial r}}+{\rm i}k_{c}\right)h_{1}^{\pm}=0.

(48)

Substituting Eq. (
47
) into (
48
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{r}\left\{-2\,\text{Re}\left[\varsigma C_{1}(\varsigma k_{c}r)\right]+\mathrm{H}_{1}(k_{c}r)+\mathrm{Y}_{1}(k_{c}r)\pm 2{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{1}(k_{c}r)+{\rm i}\left[2\,\text{Re}\left[C_{0}(\varsigma k_{c}r)\right]-\mathrm{H}_{0}(k_{c}r)-\mathrm{Y}_{0}(k_{c}r)\mp 2{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{0}(k_{c}r)\right]\right\}=0,

(49)

where we use the facts that
\mathrm{Y}_{0}^{\prime}=-\mathrm{Y}_{1}
and
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathrm{H}_{-1}=2/\pi-\mathrm{H}_{1}
. Using the far field asymptotic results
(
DLMF
, Eq. 11.6.1)

\displaystyle C_{0}(z)\sim\frac{2}{\pi z}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{1}(z)\sim\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1+\frac{1}{z^{2}}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad|z|\rightarrow\infty\quad\text{in}\quad|\arg z|<\pi,

(50)

Eq. (
49
) reduces to

\displaystyle\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{r}\left\{\mathrm{Y}_{1}(k_{c}r)\pm{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{1}(k_{c}r)-{\rm i}\left[\mathrm{Y}_{0}(k_{c}r)\pm{\rm i}\mathrm{J}_{0}(k_{c}r)\right]\right\}=0.

(51)

Using the asymptotic forms for the Bessel function,

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{n}(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Y}_{n}(x)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\sin\left(x-\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{n\pi}{2}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad x\rightarrow\infty,

(52)

we deduce that Eq. (
51
) is satisfied for the solution with the positive sign; that is,
h_{1}^{+}(\bm{x})
satisfies the radiation condition (
49
). Using Eq. (
9
), we conclude that the wavefield has the form

\displaystyle h(\bm{x},t)=\frac{F_{0}}{12\sigma}\left\{\left[2\,\text{Re}\left[C_{0}(\varsigma k_{c}r)\right]-\mathrm{H}_{0}(k_{c}r)-\mathrm{Y}_{0}(k_{c}r)\right]\cos\omega t+2\mathrm{J}_{0}(k_{c}r)\sin\omega t\right\}.

(53)

We note that our result differs from that of Ref.
De Corato and Garbin 2018
due to the sine-term (see Eq. (3.17) therein). That is, the waveform in Ref.
De Corato and Garbin 2018
is a standing wave due to the authors’ assumption of a reflecting boundary condition at infinity (see Eq. (2.7) therein); however, our radiation condition (
48
) enforces the requirement that waves propagate outward from the source, which is evident from Supplemental Video 1 (right panel). Moreover, by combining Eqs. (
11
) and (
46
), it is evident that the waveform (
8
) that we derived for weakly viscous gravity-capillary waves reduces to
h_{1}^{+}(\bm{x})
in Eq. (
47
) if the effects of gravity (
\beta=0
) and viscosity (
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
) are neglected.

We conclude by computing the time-averaged force exerted by one oscillating particle on another: specifically, suppose particles (labeled 0 and 1) separated by a distance
r
exert vertical forces
F_{0}\cos\omega t
and
F_{1}\cos(\omega t+\phi_{1})
on the fluid interface. The force on particle 1 due to the interfacial deformation generated by particle 0 is

\displaystyle\langle F_{1}\cos(\omega t+\phi_{1})\bm{\nabla}h(\bm{x},t)\rangle=\frac{F_{0}F_{1}k_{c}}{24\sigma}\left\{\left[-2\,\text{Re}\left[\varsigma C_{1}(\varsigma k_{c}r)\right]+\mathrm{H}_{-1}(k_{c}r)+\mathrm{Y}_{1}(k_{c}r)\right]\cos\phi_{1}+2\mathrm{J}_{1}(k_{c}r)\sin\phi_{1}\right\}\hat{\bm{r}},

(54)

where
\hat{\bm{r}}
is a unit vector that points from particle 0 to 1. If the particles oscillate in-phase (
\phi_{1}=0
) or out-of-phase (
\phi_{1}=\pi
), we recover the expression derived in Ref.
De Corato and Garbin 2018
(see Eq. (3.20) therein); however, other phase relationships will result in deviations from that expression owing to the
\mathrm{J}_{1}
–term in Eq. (
54
).

Using arguments analogous to those in §

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\Omega}^{\text{T}}\bm{\delta}^{\circ\perp}&-\left(\omega_{0}\mathbb{J}-\frac{1}{\tilde{m}}\mathbb{I}\right)^{-1}\bm{\Omega}^{\text{T}}\mathbb{N}\bm{1}&\bm{0}&\bm{1}&\bm{0}\end{pmatrix}^{\text{T}}
