1.
Introduction

Mathematical modeling and analysis for the study of fluids are topics of great interest, both for our understanding of the phenomena related to fluids and for applications. The investigation of heat and fluid movement in porous media is of significant importance to numerous scientific and engineering domains. Many models of porous media, especially those which describe the flow of fluids, are based on
Darcy’s law
. It is an empirical law which describes a linear relationship between flow rate and pressure gradient (
\nabla p
), that is,

\boldsymbol{y}_{d}=-\frac{k}{\nu}\nabla p,

where
\boldsymbol{y}_{d}
is the Darcy velocity,
k
is the permeability of the fluid,
\nu>0
is the dynamic viscosity and
p
is the pressure in the fluid flow (cf.
[
49
]
). Many investigations demonstrate that as flow rate increases, the relationship changes from linear to nonlinear (see, for instance, oil reservoirs and petroleum development, as well as certain phenomena like radial flow patterns). In order to describe the high flow rate in porous media, Forchheimer introduced a quadratic term involving velocity into the Darcy law. This correction yields the
Darcy-Forchheimer law
, which takes the form

\nabla p=-\frac{\nu}{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}-\gamma\rho_{f}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f},

where
\boldsymbol{y}_{f}
is the Forchheimer velocity,
\gamma>0
is the Forchheimer coefficient and
\rho_{f}
is the density (cf.
[
49
]
). Based upon the
Darcy-Forchheimer law
, the following classical configuration of the convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Darcy model was originally derived in the framework of thermal dispersion in a porous medium using the method of volume averaging of the velocity and temperature deviations in the pores:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{f}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+(\boldsymbol{y}_{f}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+a_{0}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+a_{1}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{g},\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}&=0.\end{aligned}}}\right.

However, the quadratic nonlinearity in the Forchheimer equation can be further generalized to incorporate some additional nonlinear terms from the mathematical point of view. In fact, at higher flow rates through porous media, the most practical case is described by a
linear and cubic Darcy-Forchheimer law
(see
[
49
]
),

\nabla p=-\frac{\nu}{k}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}-\gamma\rho_{f}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}|^{2}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}.

On taking into account these nonlinear corrections of Darcy’s law, the author in
[
71
]
discussed the following convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Dracy model:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{f}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+(\boldsymbol{y}_{f}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+a_{0}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+a_{1}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+a_{2}|\boldsymbol{y}_{f}|^{2}\boldsymbol{y}_{f}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{g},\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}&=0.\end{aligned}}}\right.

In this work, we consider a further generalization of the convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Dracy equations by taking into account a
pumping term
which also have a similar nonlinearity but with the opposite sign.

1.1.
The Model

Let us consider a
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{L}\mathbb{Z}}}}\right)^{d}
where
d\in\{2,3\}
and
L>0
. The convective Brinkman-Forchheimer extended Darcy (CBFeD) equations describe the motion of incompressible fluid flows in a saturated porous medium and are given by

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{y}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{y}+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}+(\boldsymbol{y}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{y}+\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}+\gamma|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{g},\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{T}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}&=0,\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{T}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{T}^{d},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where
\boldsymbol{y}(x,t):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the velocity field at time
t
and position
x
,
p(x,t):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the pressure field,
\boldsymbol{g}(x,t):\mathbb{T}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an external forcing. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
p(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\boldsymbol{g}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfy the following periodic conditions:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(x+\mathrm{L}e_{i},\cdot)=\boldsymbol{y}(x,\cdot),\ p(x+\mathrm{L}e_{i},\cdot)=p(x,\cdot)\ \text{ and }\ \boldsymbol{g}(x+\mathrm{L}e_{i},\cdot)=\boldsymbol{g}(x,\cdot),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,d,
where
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
The constant
\mu>0
denotes the
Brinkman coefficient
(effective viscosity) and the constants
\alpha
and
\beta
are due to Darcy-Forchheimer law which are termed as
Darcy
(permeability of porous medium) and
Forchheimer
(proportional to the porosity of the material) coefficients, respectively. The nonlinear term
\gamma|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}
appearing in (
1.1
) functions as
damping
for
\gamma>0
and
pumping
for
\gamma<0
. The absorption exponent
r\in[1,\infty)
and
r=3
is known as the
critical exponent
. For
\gamma=0
, one obtains the convective Brinkman-Forchheimer (CBF) equations (
[
33
]
). The critical homogeneous CBF equations ((
1.1
) with
r=3
and
\boldsymbol{g}=\mathbf{0}
) have the same scaling as Navier-Stokes equations (NSE) only when
\alpha=0
(
[
33
]
). We refer the case
r<3
as
subcritical
and
r>3
as
supercritical
(or fast growing nonlinearities). The model is accurate when the flow velocity is too large for Darcy’s law to be valid, and apart from that the porosity is not too small (
[
49
]
). If one considers (
1.1
) with
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0
, then we obtain the
classical NSE
, and if
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
, then it can be considered as
damped NSE
.

1.2.
Literature review

We present a summary of the literature-based research on the model (
1.1
), approximation controllability and irreducibility.

1.2.1.
The deterministic problem.

The problem (
1.1
) has important applications not only from the physical but also from mathematics perspective. Due to Leary and Hopf (cf.
[
34
,
47
]
), the existence of at least one weak solution satisfying the energy inequality of 3D NSE is known. But the uniqueness is still a challenging open problem for the mathematical community. Therefore, several mathematicians came up with various modifications of 3D NSE (cf.
[
11
,
39
,
75
]
, etc.). The authors in
[
2
,
3
]
introduced NSE modified by an absorption term
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
with
r\geq 1
and proved the existence of weak solution for any
d\geq 2
and uniqueness for
d=2
. In the literature, modifications of the classical NSE by the damping term
\alpha\boldsymbol{y}+\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
is known as the
convective Brinkman-Forchheimer equations
(cf.
[
33
]
, etc.). In
[
49
]
, the authors considered the classical 3D NSE with damping
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
as well as pumping
\gamma|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}
(without the linear damping
\alpha\boldsymbol{y}
) and established the existence of weak solutions for
r>q
and uniqueness for
r>3
. Moreover, they demonstrated the global existence of a unique strong solution for
r>3
with the initial data in
\mathbb{H}^{1}
. Similar to 3D NSE, the existence of a unique global (in time) weak solution of 3D CBFeD with
r\in[1,3)
(for any
\beta,\mu>0
) and
r=3
(for
2\beta\mu<1
) is also an open problem.

1.2.2.
The stochastic problem.

Let us now discuss the stochastic counterpart of the system (
1.1
) and related models. The existence of a pathwise unique strong solution for the stochastic tamed NSE (driven by Gaussian), in the whole space as well as in the periodic boundary case, was established in
[
67
]
. They also proved the existence of a unique invariant measure for the corresponding transition semigroup. Recently, the authors in
[
10
]
improved their results for a slightly simplified system. In
[
48
]
, it is demonstrated that strong solutions exist and are unique for a wide class of SPDE, where the coefficients satisfy Lyapunov and local monotonicity conditions. The author used the stochastic tamed 3D Navier-Stokes equations as an example. All the above works established the existence and uniqueness of strong solutions in the following regularity class (see Subsection
2.1
):

\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})),\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

by assuming
\mu=1
. It appears to us that the model covered in these papers ia a special case of our model with
r=3
,
\mu=\beta=1
and
\alpha=\gamma=0
, so that the condition
2\beta\mu>1
is automatically holds (cf. the estimates (
4.16
), (
4.18
) and
[
67
, Lemma 2.3]
).

Let us now look into the results available in the literature for stochastic CBF equations and related models in bounded domains and torus. By exploiting a monotonicity
property of the linear and nonlinear operators as well as a stochastic generalization of the
Minty-Browder technique, the author in
[
50
,
51
]
established the existence and uniqueness of
a global strong solution

\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{r+1}(0,T;\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}),\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

for
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, satisfying the energy equality (Itô’s formula) for stochastic CBF equations (in bounded and torus) driven by multiplicative Gaussian and pure jump noises, respectively, for
d=2,3
and
r\in[3,\infty)

(2\beta\mu\geq 1)
for
d=r=3
. Under suitable assumptions on the initial
data (
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}
) and noise coefficients, the author has also showed the following regularity result:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}))\cap\mathrm{L}^{r+1}(0,T;\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p(r+1)}),\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.},

where
p\in[2,\infty)
for
d=2
and
p=3
for

. The author in
[
52

established, using the contraction mapping principle, the existence and uniqueness of local and global pathwise mild solutions for stochastic CBF equations perturbed by an additive Lévy noise in

, for

. For 2D and 3D stochastic CBF equations perturbed by a multiplicative Lévy noise, the existence of a weak martingale solution is proved in
[
53

. This is achieved by using a version of the Skorokhod embedding theorem for nonmetric spaces (for
d=2,3
and
r\in[1,\infty)
), a compactness method, and the classical Faedo-Galerkin approximation. Recently the author in
[

established the asymptotic irreducibility, asymptotic strong Feller property and ergodicity by an application of the asymptotic log harmonic inequality. Furthermore, for the CBFeD equations (
1.1
), the author in
[

showed that the solutions 2D and 3D stochastic CBFeD equations driven by Brownian motion can be approximated by 2D and 3D stochastic CBFeD equations forced by pure jump noise/random kicks on the state space
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H}),
where
\mathrm{D}([0,T];\mathbb{H})

[0,T]

.

1.2.3.
The control problem.

Controllability theory for evolution partial differential equations first emerged in the 1960s. Egorov
[
19
]
, Russell
[
68
]
, and Fattorini
[
20
]
established the basis for this theory. Specifically, these papers developed the moment method, which reduces the exact controllability problem’s solution to problems in the theory of exponential series, as well as introduced the duality principle, which reduces the controllability problem for an evolution equation to the adjoint equation’s observability problem. In the late 1980’s, Lions presented the subject of fluid flow controllability (see
[
43
]
), which refers to how the NSE system can be brought to a desired plausible state, for instance, vanishing velocity, by controlling the flow on a portion of the boundary. He posed the question of whether, for any finite energy initial data, the solution of NSE system starts at the initial data and then reaches rest. The question was fairly impressive as the answer was not even known in the case of the heat equation. The authors in
[
28
,
42
]
demonstrated the first breakthroughs in the heat equation utilising the Carleman estimate associated with elliptic and parabolic operators.

For many years, researchers have been studying the approximate controllability of NSE with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions on a bounded domain using controls acting on an arbitrarily chosen subdomain or subboundary. The approximate controllability (global) for 2D-NSE with Navier-slip boundary conditions was discussed in
[
12
]
by using the so-called
return method
, while the small time exact controllability for 2D-NSE has been proved in
[
13
]
, where the domain is a manifold without boundary. Likewise, the authors in
[
27
]
, proved small-time global exact null controllability when the control is supported on the whole boundary (see also
[
36
,
37
]
). Furthermore, the authors in
[
45
]
demonstrated that in 2D and 3D-NSE exact controllability could be achieved in a bounded domain employing finite-dimesional Galerkin approximations.
By using low-mode forcing, the authors in
[
1
]
proved the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-approximate controllability for 2D-NSE, while for 3D-NSE it has been established in
[
69
]
. For 3D-NSE, the author in
[
26
]
proved the local approximate controllability on the torus. Further in
[
70
]
, the author proved the exact controllabilty in projections as well as uniform approximate controllability for 3D-NSE. The global approximate null controllability of 3D NSE by means of a boundary control was proved in
[
30
]
.
Recently, the authors in
[
32
]
established feedback stabilization for the CBFeD system preserving the invariance of a given convex set. Furthermore, in
[
41
]
, the authors examined the local exact controllability to trajectories for CBF equations.

1.2.4.
Irreducibility

Control theory is a useful tool to study the properties of stochastic dynamical systems. One of the important properties of stochastic dynamic systems is the concept of
irreducibility
of transition semigroup associated to the system. An additional convincing reason for the study of the irreducibility property is its relevance in the analysis of the uniqueness and ergodicity of invariant measures. It is well known that the strong Feller property together with irreducibility ensure the uniqueness of invariant measures for the transition semigroup (see
[
58
,
60
]
). See, for instance, the classical work
[
15
]
and the books
[
59
,
60
]
for stochastic infinite-dimensional systems. The analysis of irreducibility for semilinear SPDEs with additive or multiplicative noises has been widely explored in the literature. The author in
[
5
]
proved the irreducibility of the transition semigroup associated with the two-phase Stefan problem (perturbed by a colored noise). The proof relies on a general result of approximate controllability for
maximal monotone
systems (see
[
4
, Chapter 2]
for a general introduction on maximal monotone operators). Additionally,
[
6
]
demonstrated the irreducibility of a stochastic obstacle problem by employing a similar methodology as in
[
5
]
. Later, the authors in
[
7
]
extend this result to a more general class of stochastic differential equations by using the theory of
m
-accretive operators
. For non-linear stochastic partial differential equations (e.g., stochastic Burgers equations, stochastic porous media equations, and stochastic fast diffusion equations), the author in
[
76
]
provided sufficient conditions (non-degenracy conditions) such that the Markov semigroup is irreducible and strong Feller. In the context of NSE, the author in
[
22
]
showed that the 3D-NSE with random forcing is irreducible under the full noise assumption (that is, noise interacts with all modes), and initial data belongs to the Sobolev space
\mathbb{H}^{1}
. For more details on ergodicity for 2D and 3D NSE perturbed by degenerate and non-degenerate noises, interested readers are referred to see
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
23
,
29
,
35
,
57
,
60
,
62
,
65
,
66
]
, etc. and references therein.

1.3.
Difficulties, novelties and approaches

The main advantage for considering the CBFeD system (
1.1
) in a
d
-dimensional torus is as follows: In the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the Helmholtz-Hodge projection
\mathcal{P}
and
-\Delta
commutes (cf.
[
64
, Theorem 2.22]
). So, the equality

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(-\Delta\boldsymbol{y}(x))\cdot|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x

(1.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2}|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{r-1}\/\mathrm{d}\/x+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{r-1}{(r+1)^{2}}}}\right]\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\nabla|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{\frac{r+1}{2}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x,

is quite useful in obtaining regularity results. It is also noticed in the literature that the above equality may not be useful in domains other than the whole domain or a
d
-dimensional torus (see
[
39
]
, etc. for a detailed discussion). Even though the equations are defined on a torus, we are not assuming the zero mean condition for the velocity field unlike the case of NSE, since the damping term
\beta|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y}
and the pumping term
\gamma|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y}
(
1<q<r
) do not preserve this property (see
[
49
]
). This yields that
0
is an eigenvalue of
-\Delta
, and hence
-\Delta
is not invertible. As a result, we cannot use the well-known Poincaré inequality, and we have to work with the full
\mathbb{H}^{1}
-norm.

As the Navier-Stokes system is dissipative and non-reversible, it is widely known that one cannot expect exact controllability of NSE with an arbitrary target function
[
12
,
44
]
. This also holds true for the CBFeD system. On the other hand, we do not know the answer for the approximate controllability of the system (
1.1
) as long as the control is acting in an interior domain (
[
30
]
). We first show the approximate controllability of the system (
1.1
) in the energy space (divergence-free
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-space, denoted by
\mathbb{H}
, see Subsection
2.1
) by using a distributed control and adopting ideas from the work
[
5
]
(also
[
6
,
7
]
). The
m
-accretivity of the linear and nonlinear operators play a crucial role in establishing the approximate controllability results. We use a density argument, a smooth approximation of the multi-valued map
\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot)
and finite time extinction property (see Lemma
3.2
and
3.3
) to obtain the required results. To the best of our knowledge, in the context of convective Brinkman-Forchheimer equations, the approximate controllability results are not that much explored in the literature. The only known work is
[
56
]
, where the author established the approximate controllability result in
\mathbb{H}
with respect to the initial data (viewed as an initial controller) by using the backward uniqueness.

Our second aim of this work is to prove the irreducibility of the transition semigroup associated with the stochastic counterpart of the CBFeD system (
1.1
) in the energy space
\mathbb{H}
by an application of the approximate controllability results for the system (
1.1
). But it is not immediate as established in the works
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, etc., where the irreducibility results are obtained in
\mathrm{H}^{-1}
. Moreover, the authors in
[
40
]
established irreducibility results in
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}^{\alpha}),
where
\mathrm{A}
is the Stokes operator (see Subsection
2.1
), for
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
(
r=1,2
) and for
\alpha\in[\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{2})
(
r=3
) for 2D stochastic CBF equations perturbed by non-degenerate noise. But we prove the irreducibility results in
\mathbb{H}
and in order to establish this, we assume that our noise is non-degenerate (see (
4.2
) and Example
4.1
below) and for
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{V}
, we use the regularity result given in (
1.3
) for the system (
1.1
). This method of proving irreducibility appears to be novel in the literature. Note that our results are true for
d=2,3
with
r\in(3,\infty)
and
2\beta\mu>1
for
r=3
as well as for critical and supercritical CBF equations.

\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{p}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}):=\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{p}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the completion of
\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the
\mathbb{H}^{s}
-norm and the norm on the space
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{p}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is given by

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{{\mathbb{H}}^{s}_{\mathrm{p}}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{0\leq|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\leq s}\|\mathrm{D}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}.

It is known from
[
63
]
that the Sobolev space of periodic functions
\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{p}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is same as the space

\mathbb{H}_{\mathrm{f}}^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\boldsymbol{y}:\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi ik\cdot x/\mathrm{L}},\ \overline{\boldsymbol{y}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{y}_{-k},\ \|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{{\mathbb{H}}^{s}_{\mathrm{f}}}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|k|^{2s})|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}<\infty}}\right\}.

We infer from
[
63
, Proposition 5.38]
that the norms
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbb{H}}^{s}_{\mathrm{p}}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathbb{H}}^{s}_{\mathrm{f}}}
are equivalent. Let us define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{p}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}):\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{y}=0\}.

We define the spaces
\mathbb{H}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
as the closure of
\mathcal{V}
in the Lebesgue spaces
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in(2,\infty)
, respectively. We also define the space
\mathbb{V}
as the closure of
\mathcal{V}
in the Sobolev space
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then, we characterize the spaces
\mathbb{H}
,
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
and
\mathbb{V}
with the norms

\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x,\quad\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}^{p}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{p}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\ \text{ and }\ \|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(|\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{y}(x)|^{2})\/\mathrm{d}\/x,

respectively.
Let
(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the inner product in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represent the induced duality between the spaces
\mathbb{V}
and its dual
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
as well as
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
and its dual
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}
, where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Note that
\mathbb{H}
can be identified with its own dual
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
. From
[
21
, Subsection 2.1]
, we have that the sum space
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}
is well defined and is a Banach space with respect to the norm (see
[
51
]
also)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle:=\inf\{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}+\|\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}}:\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\ \text{and}\ \boldsymbol{y}_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}\}

\displaystyle=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{|\langle\boldsymbol{y}_{1}+\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\boldsymbol{f}\rangle|}{\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}}:\boldsymbol{0}\neq\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}}\right\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}:=\max\{\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{V}},\|\cdot\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}\}
is a norm on the Banach space
\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
. Also the norm
\max\{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}},\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}\}
is equivalent to the norms
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}
and
\sqrt{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}^{2}}
on the space
\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
. Furthermore, we have

(\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}})^{\prime}=\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}\ \text{and}\ (\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p})^{\prime}=\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}},

Moreover, we have the continuous embeddings

\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{H}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}},

where the embedding
\mathbb{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}
is compact.

2.2.
Linear operator

Let
\mathcal{P}_{p}:\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p},

p\in[1,\infty)
be the Helmholtz-Hodge (or Leray) projection operator (cf.
[
8
,
25
]
, etc.). Note that
\mathcal{P}_{p}
is a bounded linear operator and for
p=2
,
\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{2}
is an orthogonal projection (see
[
64
, Section 2.1]
). We define the Stokes operator

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}&:=-\mathcal{P}\Delta\boldsymbol{y}=-\Delta\boldsymbol{y},\;\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}),\\
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})&:=\mathbb{V}\cap{\mathbb{H}}^{2}_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).\end{aligned}}}\right.

For the Fourier expansion
\boldsymbol{y}=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}\boldsymbol{y}_{k},
we calculate by using Parseval’s identity

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|^{2}\ \text{and}\ \|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=(2\pi)^{4}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{4}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{p}}}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{4}|\boldsymbol{y}_{k}|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}.

Moreover, by the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{p}}}
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{p}}}^{2}\geq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
and hence it is immediate that both the norms are equivalent and
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\mathrm{p}}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

2.3.
Bilinear operator

Let us define the
trilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
by

b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\boldsymbol{y}(x)\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{z}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{w}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}(x)\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{z}_{j}(x)}{\partial x_{i}}\boldsymbol{w}_{j}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x.

If
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}
are such that the linear map
b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathbb{V}
, the corresponding element of
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
. We also denote
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathcal{P}[(\boldsymbol{y}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{y}]
.
An integration by parts yields

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})&=-b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z}),\ &&\text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V},\\
b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z})&=0,\ &&\text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

By an application of Hölder’s inequality, we calculate

\displaystyle|b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w})|=|b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z})|\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathbb{V}},

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
,
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{V}
, so that we get

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}.

Hence, the trilinear map
b:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
has a unique extension to a bounded trilinear map from
(\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1})\times(\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}})\times\mathbb{V}
to
\mathbb{R}
. It can also be seen that
\mathcal{B}
maps
\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
into
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
and using interpolation inequality, we get for
r>3

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\langle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{z}\rangle}}\right|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|b(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y})}}\right|\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{2(r+1)}{r-1}}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{r+1}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbb{V}},

for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
. Thus, from here we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{r+1}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}},

for all
r>3
. By using (
2.1
) and interpolation inequality, we calculate for
r>3
(see
[
51
, Subsection 2.2]
)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y})-\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{2}{r-1}}+\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{\frac{r-3}{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{\frac{2}{r-1}}}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}},

for all
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
. Hence, the map
\mathcal{B}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
is a locally Lipschitz operator.

2.4.
Nonlinear operator

Let us define an operator
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathcal{P}(|\boldsymbol{y}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{y})
for
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\mathbb{L}^{r+1}
. Since the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
is bounded from
\mathbb{H}^{1}
into itself (cf.
[
72
, Remark 1.6]
), the operator
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
is well-defined and we have
\langle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r+1}.

For all
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}\cap{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}}^{r+1}
, we have (see
[
50
, Subsection 2.4]
)

(2.3)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})-\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\||\boldsymbol{y}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\||\boldsymbol{z}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2^{r-1}}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r+1},

for all
r\in[1,\infty)
. Moreover, we define
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathcal{P}(|\boldsymbol{y}|^{q-1}\boldsymbol{y})
for
1\leq q<r
. The nonlinear operator
\mathcal{C}_{2}(\cdot)
satisfies similar properties as
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\cdot)
. By the application of Taylor’s formula, we find

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})-\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),\boldsymbol{w}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq r\sup\limits_{0<\theta<1}\|\theta\boldsymbol{y}+(1-\theta)\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r-1}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}

\displaystyle\leq r\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}+\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}}}\right)^{r-1}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}},

for all
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
and for
r\geq 1
. From here, we conclude that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})-\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}}\leq r2^{r-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r-1}+\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r-1}}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}},

so that the operator
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\cdot)
is locally Lipschitz.

The following properties of linear and nonlinear operators will be exploited in the rest of the paper.

For
d=2,3
with

\displaystyle((\Gamma+\kappa\mathrm{I})(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})-(\Gamma+\kappa\mathrm{I})(\boldsymbol{y}_{2}),\boldsymbol{y}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{2})\geq 0,

3.
Approximate controllability

The aim of this section is to prove the approximate controllability result in
\mathbb{H}
for CBFeD equations. The problem of approximate controllability is a relaxed version of the exact controllability. Instead of requiring that the state of the system reaches the target exactly, one may wonder if, at least, one can get arbitrarily close to the target (cf.
[
9
]
). Mathematically speaking, this can be written as follows: we are given
T>0
fixed and we are given an element
\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
of a (suitable) function space. Think of
\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
as being the target. Can we find a control
\boldsymbol{v}
such that at time
T
there is a state
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot,T,\boldsymbol{v})
which is as “close” as we wish of
\boldsymbol{y}^{T}
?

Let us consider the following CBFeD equations with control:

(3.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))&=\mathrm{B}\boldsymbol{u}(t),\ \text{for a.e.}\ t>0,\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where the initial data
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\mathrm{B}:\mathbb{U}\to\mathbb{H}
is a bounded linear operator from Hilbert space
\mathbb{U}
to
\mathbb{H}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{U})
is a distributed control. Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
be fixed. Our aim is to find an appropriate controller such that in a given time, we can steer the system (
3.1
), from the initial state
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
to a neighborhood of the target
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
, that is, to prove that the system is
approximately controllable
. Mathematically speaking, the system (
3.1
) is said to be
approximately controllable
, if for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
, there exists a control
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{U})
such that for a given
\varepsilon>0,\ T>0
, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}(T)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\varepsilon.

Therefore, our goal is to show that

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a control
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{U})
such that

\|\boldsymbol{y}(T)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\varepsilon.

That is, the system (
3.1
) is approximately controllable.

We divide the proof of Proposition
3.1
into the following parts:

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})
. Then there exists a control
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})
such that the solution
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)
to the problem

(3.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))&=\boldsymbol{v}(t),\ \text{for a.e.}\ t\in[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\end{aligned}}}\right.

satisfies
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}))\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})
and
\boldsymbol{y}(T)=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
.

Proof.

We first consider the following Cauchy problem:

(3.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\rho(\mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}))&\ni\boldsymbol{0},\ \\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\end{aligned}}}\right.

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, where
\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot)
is a multivalued mapping defined as

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{y})=\begin{cases}\frac{\boldsymbol{y}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}},&\text{ if }\ \boldsymbol{y}\neq\mathbf{0},\\
\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{H}:\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq 1\},&\text{ if }\ \boldsymbol{y}=\mathbf{0}.\end{cases}

From Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
, it is clear that the operator
\Gamma(\cdot)+\rho(\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}))
is
quasi
m
-accretive
in
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}
. Then, from
[
31
, Theorem 1.3]
, we infer that the Cauchy problem (
3.3
) has a
unique strong solution

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}))\cap\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V}).

Since, a strong solution exists,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
is absolutely continuous, and multiplying (
3.3
) scalarly by
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
, we get

(3.5)

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}(t)),\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})+\rho\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=0,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. One can justify the above calculation by using a smooth approximation of the multi-valued map
\mathrm{sgn}(\cdot)
(
[
4
, pp. 110, 213]
, see (
3.10
) below). Using the quasi
m
-accretivity of
\Gamma(\cdot)
(see (
2.5
)), one gets

\displaystyle(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}),\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})

\displaystyle=(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})+(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq-\kappa\|\boldsymbol{y}_{1}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),

where
\kappa>0
is a sufficiently large constant specified in Proposition
2.1
. This gives together with (
3.5
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\kappa\|\boldsymbol{y}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}},

which implies

\displaystyle\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}(e^{-\kappa t}\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}})+(\rho-\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}})e^{-\kappa t}\leq 0,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. Integration from
0
to
t
yields

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa t}\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+(\rho-\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1-e^{-\kappa t}}{\kappa}}}\right)\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}_{0}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}},

for all
t\in[0,T].
This shows that for

we have
\|\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}=0
for all

\displaystyle t\geq T_{0}:=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{\kappa\|\boldsymbol{y}_{0}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}{\rho-\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}}}}}\right).

For sufficiently large

, one can take
T_{0}
just equal to an a-priori fixed

. Thus
\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot):=-\rho(\mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}))
(precisely, the single valued section) is the desired controller for which the solution
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)
of

(3.6)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}(t))&=\boldsymbol{v}(t),\ \text{a.e.}\ t\in[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\end{aligned}}}\right.

satisfies
\boldsymbol{y}(T)=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
. Moreover, it is clear that this control belongs to the constraint set

\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H}):\ \|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})}\leq\rho\},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{y}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{H})
such that the solution
\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)
to the problem (
3.2

\boldsymbol{y}(T)=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
.

Proof.

We prove this result in the following steps:

Step 1:

\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}

and

\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})
.
Let us first assume that
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})
. Then there exists a sequence
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A})
such that
\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
in
\mathbb{H}
as
j\to\infty
. For each

, the solution
\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(\cdot)
of the equation

(3.7)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t))+\rho\ \mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})&\ni\boldsymbol{0},\ \text{a.e.}\ t\in[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where

\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(T)=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
. From Lemma

\displaystyle\quad+(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. From Proposition
2.1

\displaystyle\leq-\rho\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}},

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
. Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+(\rho-\|\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}_{1})\|_{\mathbb{H}})\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\kappa\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2},

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq e^{-\kappa(t-T)}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(T)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2},

for all
t\in[0,T]
. This yields that
\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}
for all
t\geq T
. Let us now set

\boldsymbol{y}(\cdot)

(3.8)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}(t))+\rho\ \mathrm{sgn}(\boldsymbol{y}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})&\ni\boldsymbol{0},\ \text{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{y}(0)&=\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

, the system (
3.8
) has unique weak solution (cf.
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
). On subtracting (
3.8

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+(\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t))-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}(t)),\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t))\leq 0,

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

2.1

\displaystyle\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\leq 2\kappa\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t)\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2},

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}_{0}
in

as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{j}\to\boldsymbol{y}\text{ in }\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{H}).

Since the sequence

(3.10)

It is clear that as

(3.11)

\displaystyle\quad-\rho\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\underbrace{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\vartheta_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1})-\vartheta_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}),\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y})}}\right>}_{J_{\varepsilon}^{j}}.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\rho\int_{0}^{T}I_{\varepsilon}^{j}(t)\/\mathrm{d}\/t-\rho\int_{0}^{T}J_{\varepsilon}^{j}(t)\/\mathrm{d}\/t

When

\displaystyle D^{j}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}+\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}}}\right)\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\frac{\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}},\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y})}}\right>.

\displaystyle D^{j}_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}+\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}}}\right)\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y},\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y})\rangle

\displaystyle D^{j}_{1}+D^{j}_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}+\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}\bigg)\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\langle\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y},\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y})\rangle\bigg]

(3.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\frac{\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}}{\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}},\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}(\boldsymbol{y}^{j}-\boldsymbol{y})+\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y}^{j})-\Gamma(\boldsymbol{y})}}\right>.

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}

t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j})-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}=0.

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{y}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0})-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{y}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0})-\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j})\|_{\mathbb{H}}+\|\boldsymbol{y}^{j}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0}^{j})-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\to 0,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}(T,\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{H}.

4.
Stochastic Convective Brinkmann-Forchheiemer Extended Darcy Equations

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space equipped with an increasing family of sub-sigma fields
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of
\mathscr{F}
satisfying the usual conditions (that is, a normal filtration). The noise term on the stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
is described by a cylindrical Wiener process
\{\mathrm{W}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{H}
, and a covariance operator
\mathrm{Q}
. Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
be the space of all bounded linear operators on
\mathbb{H}
. Let the covariance operator
\mathrm{Q}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H})
be such that
\mathrm{Q}
is positive, symmetric and trace class operator with ker
\mathrm{Q}=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
. We assume that there exists a complete orthonormal system
\{\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{H}
of the covariance operator
\mathrm{Q}
and a bounded sequence
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive real numbers such that
\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}=\mu_{k}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\ k\in\mathbb{N}
. Here
\mu_{k}
is an eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenfunction
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
such that following holds:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\mathrm{Q}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}<\infty\ \text{ and }\ \sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\boldsymbol{y}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mu_{k}}(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\ \text{ for }\ \boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{H}.

We know that the stochastic process
\{\mathrm{W}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is an
\mathbb{H}
-valued cylindrical Wiener process with respect to a filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
if and only if for any
t\geq 0,
the process
\{\mathrm{W}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
can be expressed as
\mathrm{W}(t)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta_{k}(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
(cf.
[
61
]
), where
\{\beta_{k}(\cdot),\ k\in\mathbb{N}\}
, is a family of real-valued independent Brownian motions on the probability space
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}}}\right)
. In this section, we consider the following stochastic CBFeD equations subjected to an external deterministic force
\boldsymbol{f}
, driven by an additive Gaussian noise in
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
:

(4.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \/\mathrm{d}\/\mathbf{X}(t)+\{\mu\mathrm{A}\mathbf{X}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{X}(t))+\alpha\mathbf{X}(t)+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\mathbf{X}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\mathbf{X}(t))\}\/\mathrm{d}\/t&=\boldsymbol{f}\/\mathrm{d}\/t+\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}(t),\\
\mathbf{X}(0)&=\boldsymbol{x},\end{aligned}}}\right.

for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
, where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
,
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}
is a colored noise defined on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
with values in
\mathbb{H}
such that
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mu_{k}}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\beta_{k}(t)
. We shall assume further that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}),

where
\mathbb{U}
is a Hilbert space,
\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{U}
and the injection of
\mathbb{H}
into
\mathbb{U}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, that is, there exits a one-to-one map
\mathrm{J}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{U}
with
\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\mathrm{J}\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{y}
such that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\|\mathrm{J}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{U})}<\infty
. Note that Hilbert-Schmidt operators are compact, so one cannot expect the Hilbert-Schmidt operators from the same Hilbert space to themselves in an infinite-dimensional setup.

Example 4.1
.

For
\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}+1
, one can take
\mathrm{Q}=(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\varepsilon},

\{\mu_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}=\{(1+\lambda_{k})^{-\varepsilon}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{V}_{-(\varepsilon-1)}=\mathrm{D}((\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon-1}{2}}}\right)})
. Note that the asymptotic growth of
\lambda_{k}
are given by
\lambda_{k}\sim(k)^{\frac{2}{d}}
, for
k=1,2,\ldots
(cf.
[
24
]
). Then,
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
. Indeed for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{U}
, we find

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{V}}^{2}=\|(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\|(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{\mathrm{D}((\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon-1}{2}}}\right)})}^{2}<\infty.

Note that, since
\mathbb{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}
, therefore
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{H})
also. Moreover,
\mathrm{Q}
is a trace class operator, since

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\mathrm{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}((\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}(1+\lambda_{k})^{-\varepsilon}\sim\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+k^{\frac{2}{d}})^{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{\frac{2\varepsilon}{d}}}<\infty,

provided
\frac{2\varepsilon}{d}>1
or
\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}
. Furthermore, for
\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}+1
, the embedding
\mathbb{H}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{V}_{-(\varepsilon-1)}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, that is, the map
\mathrm{J}:\mathbb{V}_{-(\varepsilon-1)}\to\mathbb{H}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator. Indeed

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{J}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{U})}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\|\mathrm{J}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\|_{\mathbb{U}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\|_{\mathbb{U}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}((\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\varepsilon+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{\frac{2}{d}(\varepsilon-1)}}<\infty,

provided
\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}+1
. Let us define
\widetilde{\mathrm{Q}}:=\mathrm{A}\mathrm{Q}
and we calculate

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\widetilde{\mathrm{Q}}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\mathrm{Q}}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mathrm{A}(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}((1+\lambda_{k})^{-\varepsilon}\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{e}_{k},\boldsymbol{e}_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(1+\lambda_{k})^{-\varepsilon}\leq\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{-\varepsilon+1}\sim\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-\frac{2}{d}(\varepsilon-1)}<\infty,

provided
\frac{2}{d}(\varepsilon-1)>1
or
\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}+1
. Thus the covariance operator
\mathrm{Q}=(\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{A})^{-\varepsilon}
has the following two properties:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\mathrm{Q}&<\infty,\ \text{ for }\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2},\\
\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}(\mathrm{A}\mathrm{Q})&<\infty,\ \text{ for }\varepsilon>\frac{d}{2}+1.\end{aligned}}}\right.

for a.e.

Since
\mathbf{X}=\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{Y}
, therefore one can finally deduce (

5.
Irreducibility

In this section, as an application of the approximate controllability results established in Section
3
, we prove that the transition semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
associated to the system (
4.1
) is irreducible. For that, we assume that
r\in[3,\infty)
for
d=2
and
r\in[3,\infty)
for
d=3
(
2\beta\mu>1
for
d=r=3
).

For any
t\geq 0
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
, let
\mathbf{X}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}),
be the unique strong solution (in the probability sense) to the problem (
4.1
). Let
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the transition semigroup (see
[
60
, Chapter 2]
) associated to the system (
4.1
) and is given by

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}_{t}(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varphi(\mathbf{X}(t,\cdot))}}\right],\ t\geq 0,\ \varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{H}),

where
\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{H})
is the space of all bounded continuous functions on
\mathbb{H}
. A probability measure
\nu
having support in
\mathbb{V}
is said to be
invariant
for (
4.1
), with respect to semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, if

(5.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{H}}\mathrm{P}_{t}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\nu(\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})\nu(\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{x}),\ \varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{b}(\mathbb{H}).

In other words, the measure
\nu
is invariant if
\mathrm{P}_{t}^{*}\nu=\nu
, where
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}^{*}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the dual semigroup of
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. We say that the transition semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{H}
is
irreducible
if the transition probabilities
\mathrm{P}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathscr{U}):=\mathrm{P}_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{U}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathbf{X}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathscr{U}}}\right\}
are strictly positive for all
t>0
,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
and all open sets
\mathscr{U}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
, where
\mathds{1}_{\mathscr{U}}(\cdot)
is the characteristic function on
\mathscr{U}
(cf.
[
58
]
). Particularly, for all
r>0
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}
and
\mathscr{U}:=\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},r)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}:\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}<r\}
, then the transition semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is
irreducible
if

\displaystyle P_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},r)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})>0,

for all
t>0
. This means that, starting from any point, the process reaches all points immediately. From (
5.1
), the definition of irreducibility is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\mathbf{X}(t;\boldsymbol{x}_{0})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\geq r}}\right\}<1,\ \text{ for all }\ t>0,\ r>0,\ \boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{H}.

Irreducibility is an important property for the transition semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
since it implies that the invariant measure
\nu
associated with
\mathrm{P}_{t}
is
full
, that is,
\nu(\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x},r))>0
for any ball
\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x},r)
of center
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
and
r>0
. In fact from (
5.2
), it follows that

\nu(\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x},r))=\int_{\mathbb{H}}\mathrm{P}_{t}\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{x},r)}(\boldsymbol{x})\nu(\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{x})>0.

The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
r\in[3,\infty)
for
d=2
and
r\in[3,\infty)
for
d=3
(
2\beta\mu>1
for
d=r=3
). Then, under the assumption

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V}),

the transition semigroup
\{\mathrm{P}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
associated to the system (
4.1
) is irreducible.

Proof.

The proof is divided into the following number of steps:

Step 1.

A density argument.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
. Since the embedding
\mathbb{V}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}
is dense, so there exists a sequence

(5.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{x}\ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{H}\ \text{ as }\ n\to\infty.

for all

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t)+\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t))+\beta\mathcal{C}_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t))+\gamma\mathcal{C}_{2}(\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(t))&=\boldsymbol{f}^{n}(t)+\mathrm{B}\boldsymbol{u}(t),\\

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \|\boldsymbol{y}^{n}(T,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})-\boldsymbol{y}(T,\boldsymbol{x})\|_{\mathbb{H}}&<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\ \text{ for all }n\geq n_{1}\ \text{ and }\\
\|\mathbf{X}(T,\boldsymbol{x})-\mathbf{X}^{n}(T,\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\|_{\mathbb{H}}&<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\ \text{ for all }n\geq n_{2}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Using (
5.34
) and (
5.35
) in (
5
), we deduce

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{X}(T,\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq C_{T}\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{r+1}{2r}}+\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}(T)-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}+(C_{T}+1)\varepsilon.

Thus, for any
k>0
,
0<\varepsilon<\frac{k}{C_{T}+1}
and
n>\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mathbf{X}(T,\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\geq k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{T}\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{r+1}{2r}}+\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}(T)-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}+(C_{T}+1)\varepsilon\geq k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{T}\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{r+1}{2r}}+\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}(T)-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}\geq k-(C_{T}+1)\varepsilon}}\right)

(5.36)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}\|_{\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})}^{\frac{r+1}{2r}}+\|\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}(T)-\mathrm{B}\mathrm{V}(T)\|_{\mathbb{H}}\geq\frac{k-(C_{T}+1)\varepsilon}{\max\{C_{T},1\}}}}\right).

From the representation
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\mu_{k}}\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\beta_{k}(t)
, we conclude that
(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(\cdot),\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T))
is a Gaussian random variable in
\Lambda:=\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathbb{V})\times\mathbb{H}
(see
[
46
, Propositions 2.1.6 and 2.1.10]
). Now, if
(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(\cdot),\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T))
is non-degenrate, then one can immediately conclude from (
5
) that
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mathbf{X}(T,\boldsymbol{x}_{0})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{H}}\geq k}}\right)<1
, and the assertion of the theorem follows if we show that
(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(\cdot),\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T))
is non-degenerate.

Step 5:

Non-degeneracy
.
Let us take an arbitrary element
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}
in
\Lambda
and let
\mathcal{Q}
be the covariance operator of the Gaussian random variable
\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t)\\
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T)\end{pmatrix}
. Since,
\mathbb{E}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t)\\
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T)\end{pmatrix}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, then for any
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda
and
t\in[0,T]
, we calculate

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathcal{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t)\\
\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}}}\right\rangle}}\right|^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)}}\right]^{2}}}\right\}

(5.37)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)}}\right].

Since,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)
,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)}}\right]=(t\land T)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right).

Therefore, it implies from (
5
) that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\mathcal{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t),\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)}}\right)+T\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)+2t\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t),\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)

(5.38)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}t\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{\psi}(t)+2t\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{x}\\
T\mathrm{Q}\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}}}\right\rangle,

for all
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\psi}\\
\boldsymbol{x}\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda
.
Let
\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\end{pmatrix}\in\Lambda
be such that
\mathcal{Q}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\end{pmatrix}=0.
Then, from (
5
), it implies for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle t\mathrm{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)+2t\mathrm{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=0\ \text{ and }\ T\mathrm{Q}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=0.

Since
\mathrm{Q}
is non-degenerate, the last equation in the above gives
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
. Consequently the first equation in above implies that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. This, completes the proof that the random variable
(\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(\cdot),\sqrt{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrm{W}(T))
is non-degenerate.
∎

5.1.
Accessibility

Let us define
\mathbb{X}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathbf{X}(t,\boldsymbol{x}):t\geq 0,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}}}\right\}
, where
\mathbf{X}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}),
is the unique strong solution (in the probability sense) to the problem (
4.1
) with the initial data
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
. Then
\mathbb{X}
is said to be
accessible
to
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
if the resolvent
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
,
\lambda>0
satisfies

\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{x},\mathcal{U}):=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathbf{X}(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{U}}}\right\}\/\mathrm{d}\/t>0,

for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{H}
and all neighborhoods
\mathcal{U}
of
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
, where
\lambda>0
is arbitrary (
[
74
]
). An immediate consequence of Theorem
5.1
is that

Corollary 5.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
,
\mathbb{X}
is accessible to
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
.
