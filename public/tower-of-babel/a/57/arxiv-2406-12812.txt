Abstract
. We obtain the Kato square root property for coupled second-order elliptic systems in divergence form subject to mixed boundary conditions on an open and possibly unbounded set in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} under two simple geometric conditions: The Dirichlet boundary parts for the respective components are Ahlfors–David regular and a quantitative connectivity property in the spirit of locally uniform domains holds near the remaining Neumann boundary parts. In contrast to earlier work, our proof is not based on the first-order approach due to Axelsson–Keith–McIntosh but uses a second-order approach in the spirit of the original solution to the Kato square root problem on Euclidean space. This way, the proof becomes substantially shorter and technically less demanding.

1.
Introduction

Let
L
be a coupled second-order elliptic system in divergence form on an open set
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, with bounded, measurable, elliptic coefficients in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m})
, formally given by

(1)

\displaystyle Lu=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(b_{i}u)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}\partial_{i}u+du.

The number
m
is the system size. Precise assumptions on the coefficients are presented in Assumption
(B)
. For each component
i=1,\dots,m
, let
D_{i}\subseteq\partial O
be a closed, possibly empty, subset of the boundary. Group them in the array
{\overrightarrow{D}}=(D_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
. As an abuse of notation, we also identify
{\overrightarrow{D}}
with a subset of
(\partial O)^{m}
. Then, we complement the system
L
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
{\overrightarrow{D}}
. On the remaining (Neumann) boundary
(\partial O)^{m}\setminus{\overrightarrow{D}}
, natural boundary conditions are imposed.

To make the definition precise, define
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D_{i}}(O)
as the closure in
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)
of test functions vanishing in a neighborhood of
D_{i}
(see Definition
2.9
), and put
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)=\otimes_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D_{i}}(O)
.
The system
L
can be properly defined using a sesquilinear form

\mathfrak{a}\colon\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\times\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\to\mathbb{C}

using Kato’s form method. The form
\mathfrak{a}
is obtained from the (formal) definition of
L
above by virtue of integration by parts, see (
15
).

The system
L
is invertible and
m
-accretive in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
. In particular, it possesses a (unique)
m
-accretive square root
\sqrt{L}
with the property
(\sqrt{L})^{2}=L
. The question whether or not the identity
\dom(\sqrt{L})=\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
holds has become famous as the
Kato square root problem
. Let us mention that, if the coefficients of the system are self-adjoint, then this property is essentially trivial. However, in the non-symmetric case, even the case
O=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
m=1
has resisted all attempts of a resolution for more than 40 years. Eventually, it was the breakthrough result due to Auscher, Hofmann, Lacey, McIntosh, and Tchamitchian
[
8
]
that answered Kato’s question in the affirmative in the Euclidean case.

Given that
L
is an elliptic operator of second order, it might not seem too surprising that the proof in
[
8
]
relies on harmonic analysis techniques for second-order elliptic operators. Now, however, comes the plot twist: few years after the original solution of the Kato square root problem, Axelsson, Keith, and McIntosh proposed in their seminal paper
[
10
]
a new framework, called the
first-order approach
, in which the Kato square root problem (and many other problems from the so-called Calderón program) could be casted by reformulating them in terms of certain first-order differential operators (the so-called
perturbed Dirac operators
). Moreover, using this approach, they were also capable of resolving first cases of the Kato square root problem when the operator
L
is subject to mixed boundary conditions
[
9
]
.
Eventually, using the first-order approach became the gold standard when treating the Kato square root problem on manifolds
[
32
]
or rough open sets subject to mixed boundary conditions
[
15
,
22
,
23
]
.

Applications on sets with rough geometry come from various fields including, with exemplary references, elliptic and parabolic regularity
[
28
,
17
]
, Lions’ non-autonomous maximal regularity problem
[
1
,
24
]
and boundary value problems
[
4
,
5
]
.

The reader might wonder why the second-order proof on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
does
not
generalize to the setting on an open set
O
. To give an explanation, we have to dive a bit into the structure of the proof. What is common in both the second and first-order approaches is that the original problem is reduced to the validity of some quadratic estimate. In our case, the quadratic estimate reads

(
\heartsuit
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\Theta_{t}\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\nabla u\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{W}^{1,2}(O)}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\,.

The operator
\Theta_{t}
transforms a
“vector-field”
in
\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)m}
into a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
;
see Section
6
for a precise definition of
\Theta_{t}
and more details. To obtain the quadratic estimate (

♡

‣
1
), the task is further reduced to a Carleson measure estimate. The latter is the heart of the matter and relies on involved local
T(b)
–techniques, see Section
8
. The bottleneck for the treatment of open sets is, however, the reduction to a Carleson measure estimate, presented in Section
7
.
Indeed, to obtain the desired estimates, suitable smoothing operators in the spirit of Littlewood–Paley theory have to be introduced. In the original proof, these smoothing operators are convolution operators with a nice kernel which are applied component-wise to the vector field
\nabla u
, see
[
8
, Lem. 4.4]
. In the same lemma, a crucial argument is performed: using the Fourier transform, these component-wise smoothing operators commute nevertheless with any partial derivatives. Also in modern treatments of the Kato square root problem on Euclidean space, the same argument is crucially invoked
[
21
, Rem. 13.8]
.
Such a reasoning is, of course, impossible in our setting, nourishing the need for the first-order approach.

The objective of the current manuscript is hence the following: we aim to reprove the state-of-the-art result
[
15
]
on the Kato square root problem subject to mixed boundary conditions by virtue of a second-order approach, that is, with a proof in the spirit of
[
8
,
21
]
. Moreover, in
[
15
]
only the case
D_{i}\equiv D
was treated. We are going to generalize
[
15
]
to allow a varying Dirichlet part among the different components, which was already possible in
[
22
]
in a more restrictive geometric setting.

Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Solution of the Kato square root problem)
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set and, for
i=1,\dots,m
, let
D_{i}\subseteq\partial O
be a closed subset of the boundary. Set
{\overrightarrow{D}}=(D_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
. Suppose for every
i
that
D_{i}
is Ahlfors–David regular (Definition
2.1
) and that
O
is locally uniform near
\partial O\setminus D_{i}
(Definition
2.5
). Then,
\dom(\sqrt{L})=\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
holds with equivalence of norms

(2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{2}\approx\|\sqrt{L}u\|_{2}\mathrlap{\qquad(u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)),}

and the implicit constants depend on the coefficients of
L
only through the coefficient bounds.

The geometric setting announced in the theorem is very general and includes settings way below the Lipschitz class, including fractals like the von Koch snowflake. We will give more information on the geometrical setting in Section
2
.

Before we explain the central idea of our proof, let us comment on the motivation for a second-order proof for Theorem
1.1
. The first-order approach introduces an extra layer of information and complexity, which is crucial for applications to related areas such as elliptic boundary value problems (see the recent monograph
[
6
]
for background), but can also impede extensions to other settings and questions. One example is the Kato square root problem for parabolic operators. First, it was solved by Auscher, Egert and Nyström
[
7
]
using the first-order approach. Their proof is incredibly technical. Later, Ataei, Egert and Nyström
[
3
]
gave another proof using the second-order approach, which is not only substantially shorter and easier, but also generalizes the previous result in several aspects. So far, the Kato square root problem for parabolic operators on cylindrical domains was only treated by Ouhabaz
[
34
]
using regularity in time of the coefficients. We expect that the methods developed in this article will prove useful in the treatment of the Kato square root problem for parabolic operators on cylindrical domains without any regularity of the coefficients, taking
[
3
]
as a blueprint. Some of the authors of this article will pursue this question in future work.

We would also like to mention the recent result of Haardt and Tolksdorf
[
26
]
on the Kato square root problem for generalized Stokes operators, which employs likewise the second-order approach instead of the first-order approach due to reduced complexity.

Now, we shed some light on the central insight underlying this article. As we have already mentioned, the standard proofs of the Kato square root problem using the second-order approach use smoothing operators that belong to the functional calculus of
-\Delta
(or, if
L
contains lower-order terms as in our case,
-\Delta+1
) and apply them component-wise to the vector-field
\nabla u
(or, with lower-order terms, to
(u,\nabla u)^{t}
). For simplicity, we stick to the inhomogeneous case with lower-order terms from now on. Write
Tu=(u,\nabla u)^{t}
. Then, at least formally,
-\Delta+1=T^{*}T
. Now, reversing the order of terms on the right-hand side of the last identity, we define the operator
M\coloneqq TT^{*}
. Similar to
-\Delta+1
, the operator
M
can be defined using a sesquilinear form, and shares many desirable properties such as self-adjointness,
m
-accretivity, the square root property (for self-adjoint operators) and so forth with it. We will introduce the operators
-\Delta+1
and
M
, both subject to mixed boundary conditions, in detail in Section
3.2
. The crucial change in our approach is that we define the smoothing operators within the functional calculus of
M
instead of
-\Delta+1
. Since
M
acts on vector-fields by definition, the same is true for the smoothing operators that we define. Hence, we do not need to employ component-wise constructions. More concretely, our smoothing operators are simply resolvents of
M
, see Definition
7.3
. For them, the following beautiful identity, which is a consequence of Proposition
3.10
, holds

(3)

\displaystyle(1+t^{2}M)^{-1}Tu=T(1+t^{2}(-\Delta+1))^{-1}u,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O),t>0.

With (
3
), we can still commute the
“inhomogeneous gradient”

T
with the smoothing operator, similar to the commutation property using the Fourier transform on Eucliean space, but this operation will transform the
“vector-valued”
operator
M
into the
“scalar-valued”
operator
-\Delta+1
and vice versa.

Also, we mention here that the operator
M
has its place in the
T(b)
-type argument that is key to the solution of the Kato square root problem. We are going to perturb the operator
M
by the elliptic coefficient matrix

B=\begin{bmatrix}d&c\\
b&A\end{bmatrix}\colon O\to\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)m\times(n+1)m}

to obtain the (still sectorial) operator
MB
. Its resolvents will be used to define the
T(b)
-type test functions, see (
91
).

We tried to keep the present article as self-contained as possible. However, to avoid too much repetition, results and arguments that can literally be taken from
[
15
]
or
[
21
]
will not be carried out again. Hence, the reader is advised to keep copies of these manuscripts handy.

2.
Geometric setup

We adopt the geometric setting used in
[
15
]
. For convenience of the reader, we summarize below the concepts that are necessary to understand our article.

By
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)
,
s\in(0,n]
, we denote the
s
-dimensional
Hausdorff measure
of
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined as follows. For
\varepsilon>0
, we put

\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\varepsilon}(E)\coloneqq\inf\Bigl\{\sum_{i}\mathrm{r}(B_{i})^{s}:\bigcup_{i}B_{i}\supseteq E,\mathrm{r}(B_{i})\leq\varepsilon\Bigr\},

and since this value is increasing as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we define
\mathcal{H}^{s}(E)\coloneqq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{s}_{\varepsilon}(E)
, see
[
2
, Sec. 5.1]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A closed set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
Ahlfors–David regular
if there is comparability

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B\cap D)\approx\mathrm{r}(B)^{n-1}

uniformly for all open balls
B
of radius
\mathrm{r}(B)<\diam(D)
centered in
D
.

Remark 2.2
.

A practical interpretation is that Ahlfors–David regular sets behave
(n-1)
-dimensional at all scales. When
D
is bounded, then, for any
C\in(0,\infty)
, the restriction on the radius may equivalently be changed to
\mathrm{r}(B)\leq C
, at the cost of changing the implicit constants, see
[
14
, Lem. A.4]
. Sets with the latter property for
C=1
are called
(n-1)
-sets in
[
31
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

An open set
{\bm{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
interior thick
if

(ITC)

\displaystyle|B\cap{\bm{O}}|\gtrsim|B|

uniformly for all open balls
B
of radius
\mathrm{r}(B)\leq 1
centered in
{\bm{O}}
.

In analogy with Remark
2.2
, the restriction
\mathrm{r}(B)\leq 1
is arbitrary and can be changed to
\mathrm{r}(B)\leq C
for any fixed constant
C\in(0,\infty)
, up to a change in the implicit constant.

Convention 2.4

(Boldface symbols)

If the underlying set

instead of
O
. All other objects related to the interior thick set

, such as an elliptic system

, the Dirichlet boundary parts

and so on, will likewise be denoted by the corresponding bold letters

,

and so on.

2.1.
Locally uniform domains

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
and
\delta\in(0,\infty]
. Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
N\subseteq\bd O
. Recall the notation
N_{\delta}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\dist(z,N)<\delta\}
. Then
O
is called
locally an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-domain near
N
if the following properties hold.

(1)

All points
x,y\in O\cap N_{\delta}
with
|x-y|<\delta
can be joined in
O
by an
\varepsilon
-cigar with respect to
\bd O\cap N_{\delta}
, that is to say, a rectifiable curve
\gamma\subseteq O
of length
\ell(\gamma)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}|x-y|
such that

(5)

\displaystyle\dist(z,\bd O\cap N_{\delta})\geq\frac{\varepsilon|z-x|\,|z-y|}{|x-y|}\qquad\mathrlap{(z\in\gamma).}

(2)

O
has
positive radius near
N
, that is, there exists
c>0
such that all connected components
O^{\prime}
of
O
with
\bd O^{\prime}\cap N\neq\emptyset
satisfy
\diam(O^{\prime})\geq c
.

If the values of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
need not be specified, then
O
is simply called
locally uniform near
N
.

Remark 2.6
.

Definition
2.5
describes a quantitative local connectivity property of
O
near
N
. For an illustration of
\varepsilon
-cigars with respect to
\bd O
, the reader can refer, for instance, to
[
36
, Fig. 3.1]
. Having positive radius is of course only a restriction if
O
has infinitely many connected components.

Locally uniform domains near
N
are compared to other popular geometric frameworks for mixed boundary conditions in
[
15
, Prop. 2.5]
and the subsequent remarks. We refer the reader to this discussion for more information.

Locally uniform domains satisfy the following corkscrew condition around the Neumann part
N
.

Proposition 2.7

(Corkscrew condition)
.

Suppose that
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and locally an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-domain near
N\subseteq\partial O
. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa\in(0,1]
such that:

(6)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\overline{N_{\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{2}}}\cap O},r\leq 1\quad\exists z\in\mathrm{B}(x,r)\colon\quad\mathrm{B}(z,\kappa r)\subseteq O\cap\mathrm{B}(x,r).

In Sections
6
–
8
, we are going to establish Theorem
1.1
first under the additional assumption that
O
is interior thick.
Afterwards, we are going to use the corkscrew condition to verify interior thickness of some auxiliary set in Section
9
, which will enable us to get rid of the interior thickness condition in Theorem
1.1
. Moreover, in combination with Ahlfors–David regularity of

, the corkscrew condition yields

(see the proposition for a definition) of the full boundary

, see
[
15
, Cor. 2.11]
for a proof.

Proposition 2.8

(Porosity of
\partial O
)
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
D\subseteq\partial O
be closed. Suppose that
D
is Ahlfors–David regular and that
O
is locally uniform near
\partial O\setminus D
. Then
\partial O
is
porous
, that is to say, there exists
\kappa\in(0,1]
with the property:

(7)

\displaystyle\forall x\in\partial O,r\leq 1\quad\exists z\in\mathrm{B}(x,r)\colon\quad\mathrm{B}(z,\kappa r)\subseteq\mathrm{B}(x,r)\setminus\partial O.

In Section

, we are going to introduce so-called

, which mimic the concept of dyadic cubes within an interior thick set

.
Porosity of

2.2.
Sobolev spaces

The Hilbert space
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)
on an open set
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the collection of all
u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)
such that
\nabla u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{n}
with norm

(8)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)}\coloneqq\Big(\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)}^{2}+\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{n}}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

We introduce the subspace of functions that vanish on a subset of
\partial O
as follows.

Definition 2.9

(Sobolev space subject to mixed BC)
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
D\subseteq\partial O
be closed. The Hilbert space
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O)
is the
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)
-closure of the set of test functions

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{D}(O)\coloneqq\Bigl\{u|_{O}:u\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\;\text{and}\;\dist(\supp(u),D)>0\Bigr\}.

If
{\overrightarrow{D}}=(D_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
is an array consisting of sets
D_{i}\subseteq\partial O
, then define the vector-valued space

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D_{i}}(O).

Due to the product structure, it is often possible to restrict one’s attention to the case
m=1
, at least when pure function space questions are concerned. We will do so whenever possible.
The given definition is standard in the modelling of mixed boundary conditions, see for instance
[
33
, Sec. 4.1]
.
In the geometric context of Theorem
1.1
, the space
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O)
can equivalently be defined by virtue of a more natural class of test functions, see
[
13
, Sec. 7]
.

For pure Dirichlet boundary conditions, we recover
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\bd O}(O)=\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{0}(O)
. To the contrary, in the case of pure Neumann boundary conditions it is possible that
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\emptyset}(O)
does
not
coincide with
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)
. Indeed, a disk with a slit serves as a counterexample
[
19
, Ex. 1.1.10]
. A sufficient condition for the validity of the identity
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}(O)=\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\emptyset}(O)
is the existence of a bounded Sobolev extension operator. This is, for instance, the case if
O
is locally uniform, by virtue of Jones’ result
[
30
]
.

The following result provides a bounded extension operator for the space
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O)
. It can be obtained by combining
[
15
, Prop. 2.5]
with the main result of
[
13
]
. In the case
D=\emptyset
, it is compatible with Jones’ result.

Theorem 2.10

(Extension operator)
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
D\subseteq\bd O
be closed. If
O
is locally uniform near
\bd O\setminus D
, then there exists a bounded linear extension operator
\mathcal{E}:\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O)\to\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover, the operator
\mathcal{E}
is local and homogeneous in the following sense: there are
c\geq 1
and
r_{0}>0
, depending on geometry, such that, for
x\in\overline{O}
and
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, there holds the estimate

(11)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{E}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{B}(x,r))}\lesssim\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(B_{O}(x,cr))},\qquad u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O).

Using the local and homogeneous estimates for the extension operator, a local Poincaré inequality can be deduced for
\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{D}(O)
. This has been carried out in
[
16
, Prop. 3.9 & Rem. 3.10]
.

Corollary 2.11

(Local Poincaré inequality)
.

In the setting of Theorem
2.10
, there are
c\geq 1
and
r_{0}>0
depending on geometry such that, for
x\in\overline{O}
and
r\in(0,r_{0}]
, there holds the estimate

(12)

\displaystyle\|u-(u)_{\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,r)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,r))}\lesssim r\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,cr))}.

Remark 2.12
.

Using a telescoping sum, the following extension of Corollary
2.11
is possible. Let
x\in\overline{O}
and
\ell
a positive integer such that
2^{\ell}r\leq r_{0}
. Then, there holds the estimate

(13)

\displaystyle\|u-(u)_{\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,r)}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,2^{\ell}r))}\lesssim\bigl(\ell 2^{\nicefrac{{n\ell}}{{2}}}\bigr)2^{\ell}r\|\nabla u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{B}_{O}(x,c2^{\ell}r))}.

3.
Elliptic operator

In this section, we properly define the elliptic system appearing in the formulation of Theorem
1.1
. Then, we collect some basic, but important, properties for it.
Moreover, we define and discuss a counterpart to the Laplacian acting on vector fields, which will play a crucial role in the later analysis.

3.1.
Elliptic systems in divergence form

The main player in the Kato square root problem is the
elliptic system
of second order in divergence form

Lu=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial_{i}(b_{i}u)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{i}\partial_{i}u+du

on
O
subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions on
{\overrightarrow{D}}\subseteq(\partial O)^{m}
and Neumann boundary conditions on
(\partial O)^{m}\setminus{\overrightarrow{D}}
, where
m\geq 1
denotes the size of the system.

Throughout this article, we work under the following implicit assumption on the coefficients
A
,
b
,
c
and
d
.

Assumption (B)
.

The coefficients of
L
are bounded functions on
O
, that is,
A\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(O;\mathbb{C}^{nm\times nm})
,
b\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(O;\mathbb{C}^{nm\times m})
,
c\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(O;\mathbb{C}^{n\times nm})
and
d\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(O;\mathbb{C}^{m\times m})
, and they satisfy the (inhomogeneous) Gårding inequality: there is some
\lambda>0
such that

(14)

\mathfrak{a}(u,u)\geq\lambda(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{2}^{2})\,,\quad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\,,

where the form
\mathfrak{a}
is given by

(15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}(u,v)=\int_{O}A\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}+bu\cdot\nabla\bar{v}+(c\nabla u)\bar{v}+(du)\bar{v}\,,\quad u,v\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\,.

Formally,
L
can be written as the composition

(16)

Lu=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\Div\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}d&c\\
b&A\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\nabla\end{bmatrix}u=T^{*}BTu\,,

where the inhomogeneous gradient is given by
T=[1\ \ \nabla]^{t}
, has (formal) adjoint
T^{*}=[1\ \ -\Div]
, and
B
denotes the matrix

(17)

B=\begin{bmatrix}d&c\\
b&A\end{bmatrix}:O\to\mathbb{C}^{(n+1)m\times(n+1)m}\,

considered as a bounded multiplication operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{(n+1)m}
.

To make this precise, we define the gradient with appropriate boundary conditions as a closed operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
, and set the divergence as its Hilbert space adjoint.

Definition 3.1

(Gradient and divergence)
.

Denote by
\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}\to~\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{nm}
the gradient with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
{\overrightarrow{D}}
.
This is a closed and densely
defined operator, and we denote its (unbounded) adjoint by
-\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}}=(\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{*}:\dom(\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}})\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{nm}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
.

Before we define the inhomogeneous gradient, we fix the convenient notation

(18)

\displaystyle N=(n+1)m\,.

Elements
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
will sometimes be referred to as vector fields.

Definition 3.2

(The operators
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
and
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
)
.

Define the inhomogeneous gradient
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}=(1,\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{t}:\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
{\overrightarrow{D}}
, and denote by
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\dom(T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}})\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
its adjoint.
By a slight abuse of notation, we occasionally let
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
also denote the bounded extension
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}\to(\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O))^{*}
.

Note that
\|T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\|_{2}=\|u\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)}
for
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
.
If no confusion can arise, we sometimes write
T
instead of
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
, and
T^{*}
instead of
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
to ease notation.

Definition 3.3
.

We define the elliptic operator
L
as the composition

(19)

L=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}BT_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\dom(L)\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}

with maximal domain in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
.

While the direct definition is useful for algebraic manipulation,
L
can equivalently be defined as the operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
associated to the sesquilinear form
\mathfrak{a}
from (
15
). To this end, observe the identity

(20)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}(u,v)=\int_{O}BT_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\cdot\overline{T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v}\,,\quad u,v\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\,.

Assumption
(B)
ensures that the form
\mathfrak{a}
is sectorial, so that the associated operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
is well-defined.
For a proof of the equivalence of these two definitions see
[
12
, Lemma 12.1]
.
Also associated to
\mathfrak{a}
is a bounded operator

\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\to(\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O))^{*}

extending
L
to
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
.
As for
L
, the bounded extension
\mathcal{L}
is given as the composition

\mathcal{L}=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}BT_{\overrightarrow{D}}\,,

where this time
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
is understood in the
“very weak sense”
, that is, as the bounded operator
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}\to(\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O))^{*}
.

One easily verifies that the adjoint
L^{*}
is the operator associated to the adjoint form
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, which shares the same properties as
L
for Assumption
(B)
is invariant under taking adjoints.
We collect some more important properties of the operator
L
, and refer to
[
12
, Chapter 6]
and
[
21
]
for background and proofs.

Proposition 3.4
.

The operator
L
is
m
-accretive and sectorial.
In particular, the resolvent family
\{(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\}_{t>0}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(O))^{m}
.

By the von Neumann inequality and McIntosh’s theorem, we obtain quadratic estimates for
L
.

Corollary 3.5
.

The operator
L
has a bounded
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-calculus and satisfies quadratic estimates.

Besides uniform boundedness of resolvents, we sometimes need to control terms of the form
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}
, which can be achieved via the following simple result.

Lemma 3.6
.

The family
\{tT_{\overrightarrow{D}}(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\}_{t>0}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m},\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N})
, and its adjoint family is given by
\{t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}\}_{t>0}
, where
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}
is the bounded operator
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}\to(\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O))^{*}
.

3.2.
The operators
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
and
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}

Setting
B\equiv\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}
, we obtain the inhomogeneous Laplacian
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
given by the composition
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
with
corresponding form

(21)

\mathfrak{a}(u,v)=\left\langle T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u,T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v\right\rangle\,,\quad u,v\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)=\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})\,.

Considering the sesquilinear form derived from
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
,

(22)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}(F,G)=\left\langle T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}F,T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}G\right\rangle\,,\quad F,G\in\dom(T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}})\,,

we obtain a natural operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
acting on vector fields
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
.

Definition 3.7

(The operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
)
.

Define the operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}:\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})\subseteq\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}\to~\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
as the composition
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
with maximal domain

\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})=\bigl\{F\in\dom(T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}):T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}F\in\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})\bigr\}.

Remark 3.8
.

We claim that
\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})=\ran(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})
, and this is a closed subspace of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
. First, closedness of
\ran(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})
is equivalent to the fact that the Sobolev space
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
is complete. Second,
\ran(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})\subseteq\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
follows from invertibility of
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
. Indeed, let
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
and write
u=(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)v=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v
for some
v\in\dom(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)
. Hence,
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v=M_{\overrightarrow{D}}F
, where
F\coloneqq T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
.

Formally,
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
is given by the operator matrix
\begin{bmatrix}1&-\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}}\\
\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}}&-\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}}\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}}\,\end{bmatrix}.

Note, however, that for
F=(F_{0},F^{\prime})^{t}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{nm}
,
F\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
does not imply
F^{\prime}\in\dom(\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}}\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}})
, but only
F_{0}-\Div_{\overrightarrow{D}}F^{\prime}\in\dom(\nabla_{\overrightarrow{D}})
.

From the sesquilinear form
\mathfrak{b}
, the operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
inherits the same desirable properties as the inhomogeneous Laplacian.

Proposition 3.9
.

The operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
is self-adjoint and non-negative.
In particular,
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
has a bounded
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-calculus on its (closed) range and satisfies quadratic estimates as well as the square root property

(23)

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{D}}}F\rVert_{2}=\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}F\rVert_{2}\,,\quad F\in\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*})=\dom(\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{D}}})\,.

The following intertwining property between
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
and
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
is essential for our arguments in Section
7
.

Proposition 3.10

(Intertwining between
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
and
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
)
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
for some
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
.
Then, one has the intertwining relation

T_{\overrightarrow{D}}f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u=f(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\qquad\mathrlap{(u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)).}

Proof.

We suppose familiarity with the construction and basic properties of the sectorial functional calculus. A reader who is not yet familiar with this concept can consult the monograph
[
27
]
or
[
21
, Sec. 5]
.

Fix
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
. Also, let
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
and
f\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
. We comment first why the identity from the statement is well-defined. First of all,
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)
is well-defined since the operator
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
is sectorial of angle zero and injective (even invertible). Next, write
u=(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}v
for a suitable
v\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m}
, which uses the Kato square root property for
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
. Then, the representation
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u=(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)v
shows that
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u
is indeed an element of
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)=\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{D}})
, where we used a basic commutation property of the functional calculus and boundedness of the
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-calculus for
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
. Therefore, the left-hand side of the claimed identity is well-defined. For the right-hand side, note that
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
is in general not injective. However, if we restrict
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
to
\overline{\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})}=\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
(see Remark
3.8
for this identity), then
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
becomes injective and
f(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
can be defined as an operator on
\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
.
We are not going to distinguish
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
from its injective part notation-wise.
Since
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\in\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
by Remark
3.8
,
f(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
is indeed defined on the
“gradient field”

T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u
appearing in the statement.

We split the proof into 3 steps.

Step 1
: Show
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))^{-1}u=(z-M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
.

The operators
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1
and
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
are sectorial of angle zero, hence their resolvents for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[0,\infty)
are well-defined.
Put
v\coloneqq(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))^{-1}u
for convenience. We can apply
(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))
to the definition of
v
to find
(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))v=u
. Since
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
, we can apply
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
to the last identity. In conjunction with
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)=M_{\overrightarrow{D}}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
, this gives

(24)

\displaystyle T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))v=(z-M_{\overrightarrow{D}})T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v.

Finally, we apply
(z-M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}
to the last identity and plug in the definition of
v
to conclude

(25)

\displaystyle(z-M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(z-(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1))^{-1}u.

Step 2
: Show the intertwining relation for
f\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
.

Since
f\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
, we can write
f(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})
and
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)
via Cauchy integrals. Then, the claim follows from Step 1 by standard arguments.

Step 3
: The general case
f\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
.

Recall
the standard regularizer
e(z)=z(1+z)^{-2}
for the
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of an injective sectorial operator. By definition,
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u=e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}(ef)(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u
. As discussed at the start of the proof,
f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u
is again in
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
. Therefore, we can apply
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
to the last identity to get
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}(ef)(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u
. By construction of the functional calculus,
v\coloneqq(ef)(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u
is in the range of
e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)
. Furthermore,
v\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
. Put
w\coloneqq e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}v
. We claim that also
w\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
. Indeed,
w=f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)
as observed above.

Next, we show
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}v=e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v
. To this end, write
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)w=e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})T_{\overrightarrow{D}}w
, where we used Step 2 for the last manipulation. Therefore,
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v\in\ran(e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}}))
and we conclude
e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}v=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}w=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}v
as desired.

In summary, we deduce with another application of Step 2 to
ef\in\mathrm{H}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathrm{S}_{\varphi})
that

(26)

\displaystyle T_{\overrightarrow{D}}f(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u

\displaystyle=T_{\overrightarrow{D}}e(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)^{-1}(ef)(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u

(27)

\displaystyle=e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}(ef)(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{D}}+1)u

(28)

\displaystyle=e(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})^{-1}(ef)(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u

(29)

\displaystyle=f(M_{\overrightarrow{D}})T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u.\qed

Remark 3.11
.

The last proposition is our substitute for the observation made in
[
10
, Rem. 4.5]
.

One important property of elliptic operators in divergence form is their off-diagonal decay.
A standard way to derive them is by using Davies’ trick. In our geometric setting, this was done in
[
20
,
11
]
for the families
\{\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}L}\}_{t>0}
and
\{t\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}L}\}_{t>0}
. The same proof also applies to the family
\{t\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}L}\}_{t>0}
. Indeed, it suffices to observe that the coercivity condition used in the treatment of the gradient family is in fact an inhomogeneous coercivity condition.
Using the Laplace transform, these bounds can be transfered to the respective resolvent families.

Proposition 3.12

(Off-diagonal estimates)
.

The families
\{t(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\}_{t>0}
and
\{t\nabla(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\}_{t>0}
satisfy
\mathrm{L}^{2}
off-diagonal estimates,
that is, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that, for all
t>0
, all measurable subsets
E,F\subseteq O
and all
u\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)
, there holds

\lVert\mathbf{1}_{E}t(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{F}u\rVert_{2}+\lVert\mathbf{1}_{E}t\nabla(1+t^{2}L)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{F}u\rVert_{2}\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{\dist(E,F)}{t}}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{F}u\right\rVert_{2}\,.

By duality, also the adjoint family
\{t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}\Div\}_{t>0}

satisfies
\mathrm{L}^{2}
off-diagonal estimates.

3.3.
Perturbations of
M_{{\overrightarrow{D}}}

We perturb the operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
by the elliptic coefficient function
B
. The resulting operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}B
is again sectorial, a fact that we are going to exploit in the construction of
T(b)
-type test functions in Section
8
.

For instance in
[
19
, Prop. 6.1.17]
, it is shown that the perturbation
DB
of a self-adjoint operator
D
is bisectorial.
We make a minor adjustment to their argument to prove that, if
D
is non-negative, then
DB
is even sectorial.
We formulate the subsequent result only for the choice
D=M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
.
Let us mention that, if
B
were even strongly elliptic and not merely Gårding elliptic, then the subsequent result could be proved in an easier manner
[
12
, Prop. 6.9]
.

Lemma 3.13
.

If the coefficients
B
satisfy the Gårding ellipticity condition on
\ran T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
as stated in Assumption
(B)
, then the operator
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}B
is sectorial.
Moreover, the family
\{tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}B(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{D}}B)^{-1}\}_{t>0}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m})
.

Proof.

To ease notation, write
M
,
T
, and
T^{*}
for the operators
M_{\overrightarrow{D}}
,
T_{\overrightarrow{D}}
, and
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{D}}
, and write
\|\cdot\|
instead of
\|\cdot\|_{2}
. Recall that
B
satisfies Gårding’s inequality on
\ran(T)=\overline{\ran(M)}
.
The same is true for
B^{*}
because Assumption
(B)
is invariant under taking the adjoint.
From
[
19
, Prop. 6.1.17]
, we have the (non-orthogonal) topological decompositions of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
given by

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}

\displaystyle=\ker(MB)\oplus\overline{\ran(MB)}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}

\displaystyle=\ker(B^{*}M)\oplus\overline{\ran(B^{*}M)}\,.

Now, set

(32)

\omega\coloneqq\sup_{0\neq F\in\overline{\ran M}}|\arg\left\langle BF,F\right\rangle|\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\,,

and take
\phi\in(\omega,\pi)
.
Using the decompositions above, we prove that, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathrm{S}}_{\phi}
, we have the estimates

(33)

\displaystyle\lVert(z-MB)U\rVert

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lvert z\right\rvert\left\lVert U\right\rVert\,,\quad U\in\dom(MB)\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\lVert(\bar{z}-B^{*}M)U\rVert

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\lvert z\right\rvert\left\lVert U\right\rVert\,,\quad U\in\dom(B^{*}M)\,.

From this, we deduce that
MB
is sectorial as follows.
Owing to (
34
), we find that
\overline{z}-B^{*}M
is injective and that its range is closed.
Since
B
is bounded, we have
(B^{*}M)^{*}=MB
.
Hence, (
33
) yields that
\overline{z}-B^{*}M
has dense range. Summarizing the two facts, we find
\bar{z}\in\rho(B^{*}M)
.
Taking the estimate (
34
) into account, this shows sectoriality of
B^{*}M
.
Next, using that
B^{*}M
is closed, it follows

(35)

(MB)^{*}=(B^{*}M)^{**}=\text{cl}(B^{*}M)=B^{*}M\,.

Thus, arguing similarly as above entails sectoriality of
MB
.

We turn our attention to proving (
33
). Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathrm{S}}_{\phi}
.
Let
U\in\dom(MB)
and, according to (
30
), write
U=V+W
with
V\in\ker(MB)
and
W\in\overline{\ran(MB)}\cap\dom(MB)
.
Note that
BW\in\dom(M)
.
Hence, by positivity of
M
, it holds
\left\langle BW,MBW\right\rangle\geq 0
.
Moreover, since
W\in\overline{\ran(MB)}\subseteq\overline{\ran(M)}
, we have
\left\langle BW,W\right\rangle\in\overline{\mathrm{S}}_{\omega}
. Therefore,
\bar{z}\left\langle BW,W\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathrm{S}}_{\phi-\omega}
. Cover
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathrm{S}}_{\phi-\omega}
by the set

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert\bar{z}\rvert\operatorname{Re}\left\langle BW,W\right\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\lvert\left\langle BW,zW\right\rangle\rvert

(40)

\displaystyle\approx\lvert\operatorname{Im}\left\langle BW,zW\right\rangle\rvert+\operatorname{Re}\left\langle BW,-zW\right\rangle

Otherwise, if

\left\lVert(z-MB)W\right\rVert\gtrsim\left\lvert z\right\rvert\left\lVert W\right\rVert
.
Furthermore,
\left\lVert(z-MB)V\right\rVert=\left\lvert z\right\rvert\left\lVert v\right\rVert
for

.
Since

\displaystyle\left\lVert(z-MB)U\right\rVert\approx\left\lVert(z-MB)V\right\rVert+\left\lVert(z-MB)W\right\rVert\gtrsim\left\lvert z\right\rvert(\left\lVert V\right\rVert+\left\lVert W\right\rVert)\approx\left\lvert z\right\rvert\left\lVert U\right\rVert\,,

To prove (

4.
Review on dyadic structures

Dyadic cubes are the foundation of dyadic harmonic analysis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In this section, we recall a substitute on interior thick open sets for them, which will be fundamental for the analysis presented in Sections
7
and
8
.

To put things into a broader context, we note that an open and interior thick set
{\bm{O}}
equipped with the restricted Euclidean metric and restricted Lebesgue measure is a locally doubling space, that is, there exists
C\geq 2
such that for all
x\in{\bm{O}}
and
r\in(0,1]
it holds

(51)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathrm{B}_{\bm{O}}(x,2r)\right\rvert\leq C\left\lvert\mathrm{B}_{\bm{O}}(x,r)\right\rvert.

In
[
18
, Thm 11]
, Christ has constructed a full dyadic structure in spaces that fulfill the doubling property (
51
), even for all
r\in(0,\infty)
.
With the local doubling property from above, the same construction gives a dyadic structure on small scales, that is, we can decompose the space
{\bm{O}}
into small dyadic
“cubes”
but not into large dyadic
“cubes”
.
For our formulation here, we combine
[
23
, Thm. 7.4]
with the boundary estimate
[
15
, Lem. 5.2]
and the rescaling argument from
[
29
, Prop. 2.12]
. The boundary estimate, that is, property (5) in the subsequent proposition, is a consequence of porosity of
\partial{\bm{O}}
established in Proposition
2.8
. The remaining results hold for
{\bm{O}}
merely interior thick.

Proposition 4.1

(Existence of a dyadic structure)
.

Suppose that
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick and that
\partial{\bm{O}}
is porous. Then, there exists a collection
\{Q^{k}_{\alpha}\ :\ k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \alpha\in I_{k}\}
of open
sets, where
I_{k}
are countable index sets, and constants
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
,
\eta
,
C>0
such that the following hold:

(1)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it holds
\left\lvert{\bm{O}}\setminus\cup_{\alpha\in I_{k}}Q^{k}_{\alpha}\right\rvert=0
.

(2)

If
\ell\geq k
, then, for each
\alpha\in I_{k}
and each
\beta\in I_{\ell}
, either
Q^{\ell}_{\beta}\subseteq Q^{k}_{\alpha}
or
Q^{\ell}_{\beta}\cap Q^{k}_{\alpha}=\emptyset
.

(3)

If
\ell\leq k
, then, for each
\alpha\in I_{k}
, there is a unique
\beta\in I_{\ell}
such that
Q^{k}_{\alpha}\subseteq Q^{\ell}_{\beta}
.

(4)

For each
Q^{k}_{\alpha}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\in I_{k}
, there exists
z^{k}_{\alpha}\in{\bm{O}}
such that

\mathrm{Q}(z^{k}_{\alpha},a_{0}2^{-k})\cap{\bm{O}}\subseteq Q^{k}_{\alpha}\subseteq\mathrm{Q}(z^{k}_{\alpha},a_{1}2^{-k})\cap{\bm{O}}.

(5)

If
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\in I_{k}
, and
s>0
, then

(52)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\{x\in Q^{k}_{\alpha}\ :\ \mathrm{d}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Q^{k}_{\alpha})\leq s2^{-k}\}\right\rvert\leq Cs^{\eta}\left\lvert Q^{k}_{\alpha}\right\rvert.

Definition 4.2

(Dyadic
“cubes”
)
.

In the setting of Proposition
4.1
, put
\Box_{2^{-k}}\coloneqq\{Q^{k}_{\alpha}\ :\ \alpha\in I_{k}\}
. Members of this collection are called
dyadic
“cubes”
of
generation
2^{-k}
. For
t\in(2^{-(k+1)},2^{-k}]
, define also
\Box_{t}\coloneqq\Box_{2^{-k}}
. In this case, the
sidelength
of

is
\ell(Q)\coloneqq 2^{-k}
. Finally, the collection
\Box
of all (local)

“cubes”

Definition 4.3

(Children and parents)
.

Let
\ell\geq k
,
R\in\Box_{2^{-\ell}}
and
Q\in\Box_{2^{-k}}
. If
R\subseteq Q
, then
R
is called a
child of
R
, and

is called the
(unique) parent cube
of

.

With
R
and

as in the preceding definition, Proposition
4.1
(2) asserts that

is either a child of

or disjoint to

. Moreover, Proposition
4.1
(3) asserts the existence of a parent cube to
R
. In conjunction with (2), this parent cube has to be unique, which justifies our terminology of unique parent cubes in Definition
4.3
.

Definition 4.4

(Double
“cube”
)
.

If
Q\in\Box
, then
define the
doubled
“cube”

2Q
via
2Q\coloneqq\{x\in{\bm{O}}\colon\dist(x,Q)<\ell(Q)\}
.

Using property (4) of dyadic
“cubes”
, interior thickness of
{\bm{O}}
, and elementary geometric considerations, we find

.

Remark 4.5
.

(1) and (2) in Proposition
4.1
imply that, outside of a null set

, each

is uniquely contained in a
“cube”

for each

.

In the following definition,

denotes the collection of measurable functions which are integrable over bounded subsets of

.

Definition 4.6
.

For

,
x\in{\bm{O}}\setminus\mathfrak{N}
, and

define the

by

(53)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{t}u)(x):=\fint_{Q_{x,t}}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

where

is the unique

containing

. For

by component-wise action, that is

(54)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{t}F)(x):=\bigl((\mathcal{A}_{t}F_{i})(x)\bigr)_{i=1}^{k},

where

.

We investigate some first properties.

Proposition 4.7
.

\mathcal{A}_{t}^{2}=\mathcal{A}_{t}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is a contraction on

for all

.

Proof.

Fix

.
For the projection property, note that

where we used

for

in the second step.

For the second claim, decomposing

into the dyadic cubes

and applying Jensen’s inequality, we see for

that

(55)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{t}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}\int_{Q}\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{t}u\right\rvert^{2}=\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\left\lvert\fint_{Q}u\right\rvert^{2}\leq\sum_{Q\in\Box_{t}}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

The following is an important tool for later that can be found in
[
32
, Thm 4.3]
.
We will repeat the notion of a
Carleson measure
at the beginning of Section

.

Proposition 4.8

(Carleson’s inequality)
.

If

is a Carleson measure on

, then

5.
A coercivity property

Throughout this section, we suppose that
{\bm{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is interior thick and that
{\bm{D}}\subseteq\partial{\bm{O}}
is closed and Ahlfors–David regular. In addition, we suppose that
{\bm{O}}
is locally uniform near
\partial{\bm{O}}\setminus{\bm{D}}
. The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 5.1

(Coercivity of
-\Delta_{{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}+1
)
.

There exists
\alpha\in(0,1]
and a constant
c>0
such that for every function
v\in\dom(-\Delta_{{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}+1)
there holds the estimate

(56)

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}v\|_{2}\geq c\|T_{{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}v\|_{[\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m},\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})]_{\alpha}}.

In the Euclidean setting, that is to say, when
{\bm{O}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can take
\alpha=1
. Indeed, this follows by using the Fourier transform. In
[
9
]
, the authors introduce an abstract condition similar to Proposition
5.1
which they call (H8). For the Kato square root problem on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it amounts to showing that the domain of
-\Delta
coincides with
\mathrm{W}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as well as that the Hessian of a function
u\in\dom(-\Delta)
is controlled by
-\Delta u
in norm. On domains, the analogue to Proposition
5.1
is
[
23
, (H7)]
.

The proof of Proposition
5.1
will ocupy the rest of this section.

5.1.
Fractional Sobolev spaces

We will use fractional Sobolev spaces of functions that vanish on a subset
{\bm{D}}\subseteq\partial{\bm{O}}
. The starting point for their construction are the fractional Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which can be defined either as Bessel potential spaces or as Sobolev–Slobodeckij spaces if

is not an integer, see
[
35
]
.

Since
{\bm{D}}
is a
(n-1)
-set, see Remark
2.2
for this terminology, a version of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem allows us to define traces on
{\bm{D}}
. The following is a weakened version of
[
31
, Thm. VI.1]
that suffices for our purpose.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
s\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})
and
u\in\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
x\in{\bm{D}}
the limit

\displaystyle(\mathcal{R}_{{\bm{D}}}u)(x)\coloneqq\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{1}{|\mathrm{B}(x,r)|}\int_{\mathrm{B}(x,r)}u(y)\,\mathrm{d}y

exists. The restriction operator
\mathcal{R}_{{\bm{D}}}
maps
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
boundedly into
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{D}},\mathcal{H}^{n-1})
.

With the trace operator at hand, we introduce the closed subspace
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}_{{\bm{D}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

(57)

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}^{s,2}_{{\bm{D}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\bigl\{u\in\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\colon\mathcal{R}_{{\bm{D}}}u=0\bigr\}.

In the case
s=1
, this notion is consistent with Definition
2.9
, see for instance
[
14
, Lem. 3.3]
. Finally, we denote the distributional restriction to
{\bm{O}}
by

and define fractional Sobolev spaces on
{\bm{O}}
by restriction.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
s\in[0,\frac{3}{2})
and

. Put
\mathrm{W}^{s,2}({\bm{O}})\coloneqq\{u|_{{\bm{O}}}:u\in\mathrm{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

\mathrm{W}^{t,2}_{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})\coloneqq\{u|_{{\bm{O}}}:u\in\mathrm{W}^{t,2}_{{\bm{D}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}
and equip them with quotient norms.

.

These spaces are again Hilbert spaces by construction as quotients of Hilbert spaces. Since
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick, we have that
\mathrm{W}^{t,2}_{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})
is a closed subspace of

with an equivalent norm
[
14
, Lem. 3.4]
. As a cautionary tale, let us stress that in the context of this section

is embedded into but possibly
not
equal to the collection of all

with

and norm (

The (fractional) Sobolev spaces respect the following interpolation rule. The result was originally shown in

(Interpolation identity)

For

\displaystyle[\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}}),\mathrm{W}^{1,2}_{{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})]_{s}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{W}^{s,2}({\bm{O}})&(\text{if $s<1/2$})\\

We recall an identification result from

In analogy with the case

Proposition 6.1

As in

More precisely, writing

\Theta_{t}=t(1+t^{2}\mathcal{L})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}^{*}B

T_{D}^{*}
in the
“very weak formulation”
and keeping
\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{W}^{1,2}(O)}=\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\rVert_{2}^{2}
in mind, (
QE
) takes the form

(62)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\Theta_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{D}}u\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{D}}(O)\,.

Lemma 6.2
.

The family
\{\Theta_{t}\}_{t>0}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{m})
and satisfies
\mathrm{L}^{2}
off-diagonal estimates.

Proof.

Since the coefficient matrix
B
acts as a bounded multiplication operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(O)^{N}
and preserves the support, the claim is a direct consequence of Proposition
3.12
.
∎

7.
Principal part approximation

Throughout this section, we suppose that the pair
({\bm{O}},{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
satisfies the geometric assumptions from Theorem
1.1
as well as that
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick. Recall Convention
2.4
on the usage of bold symbols. The goal of this section is to reduce the validity of (
QE
) to a Carleson measure estimate. The latter will be verified in Section
8
.

7.1.
Presentation of the relevant operators

It is well-known that, owing to the off-diagonal estimates from Lemma
6.2
,
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
extends to a bounded operator
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\bm{O}})^{N}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\loc}({\bm{O}})^{m}
for all
t
, see for instance
[
21
, Prop. 11.12 & Def. 11.14]
. We exploit this fact in the subsequent definition of the principal part.

Definition 7.1

(Principal part)
.

Identifying the standard unit vectors
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{N}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
with their respective constant functions on
{\bm{O}}
, we define for
t\in(0,1]
the
principal part
of
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
by

(63)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{t}:=(\bm{\Theta}_{t}(e_{1}),\ldots,\bm{\Theta}_{t}(e_{N}))\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\bm{O}},\mathbb{C}^{m\times N})

Remark 7.2
.

For almost every
x\in{\bm{O}}
, the principal part
\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)
is a linear map from
\mathbb{C}^{N}
to
\mathbb{C}^{m}
acting as

(64)

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)w=\bm{\Theta}_{t}(w\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}})(x)\,,\quad w\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\,.

In particular, for
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
,
t\in(0,1]
and a dyadic cube
Q\in\Box_{t}
we have

(65)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})F=\mathbf{1}_{Q}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(F-(F)_{Q})\,.

Definition 7.3

(Smoothing operators)
.

For
t>0
, we define the smoothing operators

\bm{P}_{t}=(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N})\ ,\quad\bm{Q}_{t}=tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})\,.

A direct consequence of Proposition
3.10
with the function
f:z\to(1+t^{2}z)^{-1}
is the commuting relation

(66)

\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}=T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}\,.

Lemma 7.4
.

The families
\{\bm{P}_{t}\}
and
\{\bm{Q}_{t}\}
are uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N})
and
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})
, respectively.
Thus, also the adjoint family
\{\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}\}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N})
, and
\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}
is given by the bounded extension of
t\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
to
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m}
.

Proof.

Uniform boundedness of
\{\bm{P}_{t}\}
is by
m
-accretivity of
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
,
and for
\{\bm{Q}_{t}\}
one can use the sesquilinear form
\bm{\mathfrak{b}}
, see the proof of Lemma
3.13
or
[
21
, Lem. 11.7]
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

For
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, one has

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{Q}_{t}F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

If in addition
F\in\ran(T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
, then we have the reproducing formula

2\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}\bm{Q}_{t}F\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}=F\,.

Proof.

Owing to Proposition
3.9
, the square root property for the self-adjoint operator
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
gives

\left\lVert\bm{Q}_{t}F\right\rVert_{2}=\lVert tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}F\rVert_{2}=\lVert\sqrt{t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}F\rVert_{2}\,.

Hence, the first assertion follows by quadratic estimates for
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
.

For the reproducing formula, note that
\bm{Q}_{t}
maps into
\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
, so we can compute

\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}\bm{Q}_{t}=t^{2}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}=t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-2}=f(t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})\,,

where
f(z)=z(1+z)^{-2}
.
By Remark
3.8
we have
\ran(T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})=\ran(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
,
so the Calderón reproducing formula
[
21
, Thm. 6.16]
yields the assertion.
∎

With these operators, we first split the left-hand side of (
QE
) as

(67)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,.

The second term on the right-hand side can be controlled via a simple algebraic manipulation.

Proposition 7.6
.

For
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, there holds the quadratic estimate

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

Proof.

Note that
1-\bm{P}_{t}=t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}
and
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\subseteq\bm{\mathcal{L}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
.
Thus, for
F\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, we can compute

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})F

\displaystyle=t(1+t^{2}\bm{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F

\displaystyle=t^{2}(1+t^{2}\bm{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{L}}tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F

\displaystyle=t^{2}\bm{L}(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1}\bm{Q}_{t}F=(1-(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1})\bm{Q}_{t}F\,.

By
m
-accretivity, the resolvents
(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1}
are uniformly bounded, so we arrive at

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{Q}_{t}F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert F\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

where the last step follows from the quadratic estimate for
\bm{Q}_{t}
from Lemma
7.5
.
∎

7.2.
Reduction to finite time

We want to approximate the first term in (
67
) by the principal part. However, since the dyadic structure on
{\bm{O}}
and hence the approximation
\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}
only exist on small scales,
the integral for
t\geq 1
has to be treated separately.

Proposition 7.7
.

It holds

\int_{1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})\,.

Proof.

Denote by
H_{\alpha}
the complex interpolation space
[\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m},\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})]_{\alpha}
.
Since
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})
and
\mathrm{H}_{\alpha}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m}
, we have

\left\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{H_{\alpha}}.

Recall from (
66
) the commuting relation
\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u=T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}u
.
Since the resolvent
(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}
maps into
\dom(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1)
we can use Proposition
5.1
in conjunction with the square root property of
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1
and the intertwining property from Proposition
3.10
with the
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-function
f(z)=\frac{z^{\alpha}}{1+t^{2}z}
to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{H_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle={}\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}u\rVert_{H_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim{}\lVert(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}u\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle={}\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1+t^{2}(-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1))^{-1}u\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle={}\lVert(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert_{2}.

We can then estimate
1\leq t^{2\alpha}
to obtain a square function estimate for
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
with
f(z)=z^{\alpha/2}(1+z)^{-1}
, which reads

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{2\alpha}\lVert(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\lVert f(t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

where we used Proposition
3.9
in the final step.
∎

7.3.
Smoothed principal part approximation

As the next step, we want to approximate
\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
by the smoothed principle part
\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
. This will be the content of Proposition
7.10
. As a preparation, we establish the following lemma first.
To this end, recall from Remark
7.2
the (pointwise) matrix action of
\bm{\gamma}_{t}
.

Lemma 7.8
.

The family
\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N})
for
t\in(0,1]
. Moreover, for
F=(F_{0},\ldots,F_{n})\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})^{n+1}
, there holds

\left\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})F\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim t\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\right\rVert_{2},\quad t\in(0,1].

Proof.

Fix
t\in(0,1]
and the natural number
k
for which
t\in(2^{-(k+1)},2^{-k}]
. First, we work on a fixed
Q\in\Box_{t}
. By utilizing Proposition
4.1

(4)
, there exist constants
a_{0},a_{1}>0
and
z\in{\bm{O}}
such that

(68)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Q}(z,a_{0}2^{-k})\cap{\bm{O}}\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathrm{Q}(z,a_{1}2^{-k})\cap{\bm{O}}.

Let
Q_{*}\coloneqq\mathrm{Q}(z,a_{0}2^{-k})
and
Q^{*}\coloneqq\mathrm{Q}(z,a_{1}2^{-k})
. Then, the left-hand side of (
68
) becomes
Q_{*}\cap{\bm{O}}
and the right-hand side
Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}
.
Owing to interior thickness of
{\bm{O}}
, there holds

(69)

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\gtrsim\left\lvert Q_{*}\right\rvert.

For a bounded set
K\subseteq{\bm{O}}
and
b\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, the limit
\bm{\Theta}_{t}b:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{2^{k}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}}b)
exists in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(K)^{N}
and is independent of
Q^{*}
. Indeed, this is a consequence of the off-diagonal estimates of
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
, see for instance
[
21
, Prop. 11.12]
.
We have already employed the same construction when defining the principal part in Definition
7.1
.
Now, we define
annuli
of a
“cube”

Q
by

C_{1}(Q)\coloneqq 4Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}},\quad C_{\ell}(Q)\coloneqq\Bigl(2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\setminus 2^{\ell}Q^{*}\Bigr)\cap{\bm{O}},

where
\ell\geq 2
.
Using this notation in the definition of
\bm{\Theta}_{t}b
yields

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}F

\displaystyle=\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{2^{k}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}}(F)_{Q})=\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F)_{Q})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F)_{Q}).

Now, note for
\ell\geq 2
that

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}(C_{\ell}(Q),Q)}{t}\geq\dfrac{\mathrm{d}(C_{\ell}(Q),Q^{*})}{t}\gtrsim 2^{\ell}.

Both of the above, in conjunction with Lemma
6.2
, allow us to see that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}F)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F)_{Q})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{\ell}}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F)_{Q}\right\rVert_{2}.

By Hölder’s inequality and (
69
), we have

(70)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F)_{Q}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left\lvert C_{\ell}(Q)\right\rvert^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}\left\lvert\int_{Q}F(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rvert\leq\left(\dfrac{\left\lvert C_{\ell}(Q)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}F\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim 2^{\frac{\ell n}{2}}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}F\right\rVert_{2},

which gives us

(71)

\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}F)\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}2^{\frac{\ell n}{2}}e^{-c2^{\ell}}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}F\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}F\right\rVert_{2}.

Since the implicit constant is independent of
Q
and
t
, squaring the last bound and summing in
Q
yields the first assertion. Now, we can proceed with the second assertion. Since
\bm{\Theta}_{t}
is bounded on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, we have

\bm{\Theta}_{t}F=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}F).

Taking the difference with
\gamma_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}F
and again applying off-diagonal estimates, we have

(72)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})F\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{\ell}(Q)}(F-(F)_{Q}))\right\rVert_{2}

(73)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{\ell}}\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}}(F-(F)_{Q})\right\rVert_{2}.

Now, we distinguish whether
2^{\ell}t
is small or large. More precisely, there is a natural number
\ell_{0}
such that the local Poincaré inequality from Remark
2.12
is applicable on
2^{\ell}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}
if
\ell\leq\ell_{0}
(note that we tacitly use comparability between balls and cubes here). To the contrary, if
\ell>\ell_{0}
, we deduce that
2^{\ell}t\geq\kappa
, where
\kappa
is a constant depending on the geometry.
In the first case, fix
\ell\leq\ell_{0}
and deduce by virtue of the local Poincaré inequality (Remark
2.12
) the estimate

(74)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}}(F-(F)_{Q})\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\ell 2^{\nicefrac{{n\ell}}{{2}}}2^{\ell}t\|\nabla F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(c2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}})}\leq\ell 2^{\nicefrac{{n\ell}}{{2}}}2^{\ell}t\|T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(c2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}})}.

In the second case, for fixed
\ell>\ell_{0}
, estimate using Jensen’s inequality

(75)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}}}(F-(F)_{Q})\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim 2^{\nicefrac{{n\ell}}{{2}}}\|F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}})}\lesssim 2^{\nicefrac{{n\ell}}{{2}}}2^{\ell}t\|T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}})}.

Plugging the last two bounds back into (
72
) and using the double exponential decay, we obtain

(76)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})F\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim t\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}e^{-c2^{\ell}}\|T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(c2^{\ell+1}Q^{*}\cap{\bm{O}})}.

To conclude the proof, we have to sum the last bound over all dyadic
“cubes”
on scale
t
. Due to the rapid decay in
\ell
, this follows by standard arguments, see for instance
[
21
, Lem. 13.6]
for details.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

Instead of using a local Poincaré inequality on small scales, there is another approach to obtain the aforementioned result in the literature based on a weighted (global) Poincaré inequality, see
[
23
, Prop. 8.2]
. For that approach, the existence of an (inhomogeneous) Sobolev extension operator is necessary. Even though our local Poincaré inequality was deduced by using a local and homogeneous Sobolev extension operator, there might be other settings in which local Poincaré inequalities are easier to obtain than a Sobolev extension operator.

Proposition 7.10
.

There holds the quadratic estimate

\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})\,.

Proof.

On the one hand,
\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}
is uniformly bounded in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})
, see Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
7.8
. On the other hand, the
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})^{n+1},\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})
-norm of
\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}
is controlled by
t
by virtue of Lemma
7.8
. By interpolation, we obtain boundedness

\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}:(H_{\alpha})^{n+1}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m},\qquad\|\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}\|_{H_{\alpha}\to\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})}\lesssim t^{\alpha}\,,

where
H_{\alpha}
denotes again the interpolation space
[\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m},\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})]_{\alpha}
.
Therefore, in conjunction with the intertwining property and Proposition
5.1
(compare with the calculation in the proof of Proposition
7.7
), we obtain that

\left\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim t^{\alpha}\left\lVert\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{H^{\alpha}}\lesssim t^{\alpha}\lVert(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert_{2}.

With the
\mathrm{H}^{\infty}
-function
f:z\mapsto z^{\alpha/2}(1+z)^{-1}
, quadratic estimates for
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
give

\int_{0}^{1}\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\lVert f(t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert_{2}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.\qed

7.4.
Reduction to a Carleson measure estimate

So far, we have reduced (
QE
) to the quadratic estimate

\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})\,.

To eliminate the smoothing via the resolvents
\bm{P}_{t}
and to reduce this to a Carleson measure estimate, we split again

(77)

\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}(\bm{P}_{t}-1)T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}+\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

The second term can be dominated by

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)(\mathcal{A}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\lvert(\mathcal{A}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,,

where
\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\right\rVert
denotes the operator norm of
\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{N},\mathbb{C}^{m})
.
By Proposition
4.8
, this integral is controlled by
\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}
, provided
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\tfrac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure, which we are going to prove in Section
8
.
To treat the first term on the right-hand side of (
77
), we need the following interpolation inequality, which will be useful again in Section
8
.

Recall the notation
Tu=(u,\nabla u)^{t}
for
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}({\bm{O}})
. Later, we apply the subsequent results with
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})
, so that
Tu=T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u
as usual.

Lemma 7.11

(Interpolation inequality)
.

Let
\eta
be as in Proposition
4.1
. We have the bound

\displaystyle\left\lvert\fint_{Q}\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\lesssim\dfrac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}

for all
t\in(0,1]
,
Q\in\Box_{t}
and
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}({\bm{O}})
.

Proof.

Fix
t\in(0,1]
,
Q\in\Box_{t}
and
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}({\bm{O}})
. Write the estimate above as

X\lesssim t^{-\eta}Y^{\eta/2}Z^{1-\eta/2}.

First, observe that
X\leq Z
by Jensen’s inequality and that
Y=0
implies
Z=0
. Thus, we can safely assume
Y,Z>0
. Put
\tau:=Y^{1/2}Z^{-1/2}>0
and note that, for
\tau\geq t
, we have

X\leq Z\leq\tau^{\eta}t^{-\eta}Z=t^{-\eta}Y^{\eta/2}Z^{1-\eta/2}.

Hence, we can suppose
\tau<t
, which circumvents awkward cases. Let
Q_{r}=\{x\in Q:\ \,\mathrm{d}(x,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Q)\leq r\}
for
r>0
, and note that, by Proposition
4.1
(5), we have the estimate

(78)

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q_{r}\right\rvert\leq Cr^{\eta}\ell(Q)^{-\eta}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\leq Cr^{\eta}t^{-\eta}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert.

Convolve
\mathbf{1}_{Q\setminus Q_{\tau/2}}
by a suitable kernel to obtain
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
with range in
[0,1]
, equal to 1 on
Q\setminus Q_{\tau}
and satisfying
\left\lVert\nabla\varphi\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{c}{\tau}
for
c>0
depending only on
n
.
Now, write

\nabla u=\varphi\nabla u+(1-\varphi)\nabla u,

and apply integration by parts (here, we use that
\varphi
is compactly supported in
Q
) to see that

\int_{Q}\nabla u=-\int_{Q}u\nabla\varphi+\int_{Q}(1-\varphi)\nabla u.

From here, apply the inequality
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2(x^{2}+y^{2})
as well as Hölder’s inequality, noting that both
\nabla\varphi
and
1-\varphi
vanish on
Q\setminus Q_{\tau}
and using (
78
), to achieve

\displaystyle\left\lvert\int_{Q}\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\int_{Q}\mathbf{1}_{Q_{\tau}}\left\lvert\nabla\varphi\right\rvert^{2}\right)\left(\int_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)+2\left(\int_{Q}\mathbf{1}_{Q_{\tau}}\left\lvert 1-\varphi\right\rvert^{2}\right)\left(\int_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lvert Q_{\tau}\right\rvert\left(\tau^{-2}\int_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{\eta}t^{-\eta}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\left(\tau^{-2}\int_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}+\int_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right).

Translating back in terms of
X
,
Y
and
Z
, the above says

\displaystyle\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}X

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{\eta-2}t^{-\eta}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}Y+\tau^{\eta}t^{-\eta}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}Z

\displaystyle=2\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}t^{-\eta}Y^{\eta/2}Z^{1-\eta/2}.

Now, the claim follows when dividing the last inequality by
\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{2}
. ∎

The following inhomogeneous version of Lemma
7.11
is an easy consequence.

Corollary 7.12
.

With
\eta
as in Proposition
4.1
, we have

(79)

\left\lvert\fint_{Q}Tu\right\rvert^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert Tu\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\eta/2}

for all
t\in(0,1]
,
Q\in\Box_{t}
and
u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}({\bm{O}})
.

Proof.

We use Jensen’s inequality, Lemma
7.11
,
t\leq 1
and
a^{1-\eta/2}+b^{1-\eta/2}\leq 2(a+b)^{1-\eta/2}
for
a,b>0
, to estimate

(80)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\fint_{Q}Tu\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\fint_{Q}u\,\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert\fint_{Q}\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}

(81)

\displaystyle\lesssim\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}+\dfrac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}

(82)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}+\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}\right)

(83)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\eta}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\frac{\eta}{2}}\left(\fint_{Q}\left\lvert Tu\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\frac{\eta}{2}}\,.\qed

We can now control the first term on the right-hand side of (

).

Proposition 7.13
.

There holds the square function estimate

\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,\qquad u\in\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})\,.

Proof.

Recall from Proposition
4.7
that the averaging operator
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is a projection, that is,
\mathcal{A}_{t}^{2}=\mathcal{A}_{t}
.
By the uniform boundedness of
\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}
from Lemma
7.8
, we have

\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})\mathcal{A}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\,.

So, we are left to prove

(84)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,.

To establish this estimate, let us assume for the moment that we have the bound

(85)

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\zeta(s/t)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}\,,\quad v=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\text{ for some }F\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}),

for all
s\in(0,1]
,
t>0
, and with some non-negative function
\zeta\in\mathrm{L}^{1}((0,\infty),\,\mathrm{d}\tau/\tau)
.
Since
\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})
is a bounded operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{N}
, the Calderón reproducing formula in Lemma
7.5
with
F=T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u
gives

\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}\bm{Q}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,.

Hence, as
v\coloneqq\bm{Q}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u=tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u
is admissible for (
85
), Young’s convolution inequality with indices
1+\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{2}
on the multiplicative group
(0,\infty)
with measure
\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}\bm{Q}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\right)^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta(s/t)\left\lVert\bm{Q}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\right)^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}s}{s}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\zeta(\tau)\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}{\tau}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\lVert\bm{Q}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\,,

where the last step above follows from the quadratic estimate in Lemma
7.4
.

We turn our attention to proving (
85
).
By Definition
7.3
, one readily checks the relations

(86)

\bm{P}_{s}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}=\frac{t}{s}\bm{P}_{t}\bm{Q}_{s}^{*}\ ,\quad(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}=\frac{s}{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})\bm{Q}_{s}^{*}\,.

Indeed, this is proved by commuting resolvents of
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
. For instance, to see the second identity, calculate on the dense subset
\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})
of
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m}
that

\displaystyle(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}

\displaystyle=s^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+s^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}t(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{t}\,t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}s(1+s^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})\bm{Q}_{s}^{*}\,.

This identity extends to
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m}
by continuity.
Next, let
F\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
and
v=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F
.
For
s\leq t
, uniform
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-bounds (see Proposition
4.7
and Lemma
7.4
) give

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{s}{t}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{t})\bm{Q}_{s}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\frac{s}{t}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}\,.

For
t\leq s
, we have

\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}(1-\bm{P}_{s})\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\bm{P}_{s}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert_{2}+\frac{t}{s}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}\,.

So, we are left with estimating the first term on the right-hand side.
Since
F\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}),
we have
v=T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\in\dom(T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
, so
\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v=t(1+t^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}v
.
Writing out the averaging operator at scale
s
, we have

(87)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{s}\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\sum_{R\in\Box_{s}}\left\lvert R\right\rvert\left|\fint_{R}t\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\right|^{2}\,.

Combining
T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
and
T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
to
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
, we can
“commute”

\bm{P}_{t}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\subseteq M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}
, which yields

(88)

\displaystyle\left|\fint_{R}t\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\right|^{2}=\left|\fint_{R}tM_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right|^{2}=\left|\fint_{R}tT_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right|^{2}.

Using Corollary
7.12
and Hölder’s inequality (in the sequence space
\ell^{2}
), we can thus estimate the right-hand side of (
87
) by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s^{\eta}}\sum_{R\in\Box_{s}}\left\lvert R\right\rvert\left(\fint_{R}\left\lvert tT^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\eta/2}\left(\fint_{R}\left\lvert tT_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\eta/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\eta}}{s^{\eta}}\left(\sum_{R\in\Box_{s}}\int_{R}\left\lvert T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{\eta/2}\left(\sum_{R\in\Box_{s}}\int_{R}\left\lvert tT_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{1-\eta/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{\eta}}{s^{\eta}}\left\lVert T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rVert_{2}^{\eta}\left\lVert tM_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rVert_{2}^{2-\eta}\,.

Now, since
tM_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F=t\bm{P}_{t}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F=\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F=\bm{Q}_{t}^{*}v
,
Lemma
7.4
gives

\|tM_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\|_{2}\lesssim\|v\|_{2}
.
Similarly, the square root property for
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
applied twice, the fact that
F\in\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})\subseteq\dom(\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}})
and uniform boundedness of
\{\bm{P}_{t}\}_{t>0}
give

\left\lVert T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\right\rVert_{2}=\lVert\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}\bm{P}_{t}F\rVert_{2}=\lVert\bm{P}_{t}\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}F\rVert_{2}\lesssim\lVert\sqrt{M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}}F\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}F\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}\,.

Putting things together, we arrive at (
85
) with
\zeta(\tau)=\min\{\tau,\tau^{-1}+\tau^{-\eta}\}
, which belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{1}((0,\infty),\,\mathrm{d}\tau/\tau)
.
∎

,

and

8.
Carleson measure estimate and a first conclusion

In Section
7
, we have reduced the proof of the quadratic estimate (
QE
) (and hence of the Kato square root conjecture on the interior thick set
{\bm{O}}
) to proving that the measure
\left\lVert\gamma_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure on
(0,1]\times{\bm{O}}

(89)

\iint_{R(Q)}\left\lVert\gamma_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\,,\qquad Q\in\Box\,.

Here,

denotes the Carleson box over

The estimate (

by finitely many sectors.

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose that there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that, for each
“sector”

\iint_{R(Q)}\left\lVert\gamma_{t}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}}(\gamma_{t}(x))\right\rVert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\,,\qquad Q\in\Box\,.

\left\lVert\gamma_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}

It remains to establish the bound (

).

Construction of Tb–type test functions

\nu

.

There is a constant

where the set

was defined in Section

.

Proof of (a)

Proof of (b)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\fint_{Q}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}v\right\rvert^{2}

) and (a), we have

“cubes”
, we can control

\|v\|_{2}=\|T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}(1+\tau^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\|_{2}\lesssim\tau^{-1}\|\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\|_{2}\approx\tau^{-1}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Collecting the powers, we arrive at

\left|\fint_{Q}\tfrac{1}{2}b-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\right|^{2}=\tau^{4}\left|\fint_{Q}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}v\right|^{2}\lesssim\frac{\tau^{4-\eta-4+2\eta}}{\ell^{\eta}}=\varepsilon^{\eta}\,.

Write
C^{2}
for the implicit constant in the preceding bound.
Now, assertion (b) follows from

\operatorname{Re}\left(\xi\cdot\fint_{Q}b\right)=2+2\operatorname{Re}\left(\xi\cdot\fint_{Q}\tfrac{1}{2}b-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\right)\geq 2-2C\varepsilon^{\frac{\eta}{2}},

if we choose
\varepsilon
small enough so that
2C\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{\eta}}{{2}}}\leq 1
.

Proof of (c)

Our choice of
T(b)
–type test function
b
is not in the range of
T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
, so we cannot directly apply the principal part approximation estimate from Proposition
7.14
to it.
However, recalling (
93
), we split the integral as follows

\displaystyle\iint_{R(Q)}

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)(\mathcal{A}_{t}b)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\iint_{R(Q)}\left\lvert(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})b\right\rvert^{2}\,+\left\lvert\bm{\Theta}_{t}b\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint_{R(Q)}\left\lvert(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})(\tfrac{1}{2}b-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi})\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\right\rvert^{2}+\left\lvert\bm{\Theta}_{t}b\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon I+II+III\,.

Since
\tfrac{1}{2}b-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}
is in the range of
T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}
, Proposition
7.14
and property (a) above control the first term as

I\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\lVert(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})(\tfrac{1}{2}b-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi})\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lVert b\right\rVert^{2}_{2}+\lVert\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\rVert^{2}_{2}\lesssim\left\lvert Q\right\rvert.

Since we have
|Q|\lesssim|Q|/\varepsilon^{2}
, this completes the treatment of the first term.
The second term can be estimated via off-diagonal estimates and the definition of the principal part approximation, similarly to the proof of Lemma
7.8
.
Recall that we seek to control

\int_{0}^{\ell}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,.

On the
“cube”

Q
,
\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\overline{\xi}
is constant, so we have
\mathcal{A}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}(x)=\bar{\xi}
for almost every
x\in Q
. This yields
\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}=\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bar{\xi}
on
Q
.
With the notation from the proof of Lemma
7.8
, this leads to

(96)

\displaystyle\ \mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}=\mathbf{1}_{Q}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\bar{\xi}-\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi})=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathbf{1}_{Q}\bm{\Theta}_{t}(\mathbf{1}_{C_{j}(Q)\setminus 2Q}\bar{\xi})\,,

where we split
{\bm{O}}
into truncated annuli
C_{j}(Q)
.
Observe that, for
j\geq 1
and
t\leq 1
, we have

\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{2^{j}}{t}}=\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{2^{j-1}}{t}}\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{2^{j-1}}{t}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c2^{j-1}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{c}{t}}.

Then,
\mathrm{L}^{2}
off-diagonal estimates (see Proposition
3.12
) control the
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})
-norm of (
96
) by

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{\dist(C_{j}(Q)\setminus 2Q,Q)}{t}}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{C_{j}(Q)\setminus 2Q}\bar{\xi}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-c\frac{2^{j}}{t}}\,2^{\frac{jn}{2}}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{-c2^{j-1}}\,2^{\frac{jn}{2}}\Big)\mathrm{e}^{-c/t}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim t\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{1/2}\,,

where the last step follows from convergence of the series and estimating
\mathrm{e}^{-s}\lesssim\frac{1}{s}
for
s\geq 0
. It follows that

\int_{0}^{\ell}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{Q}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}-\bm{\Theta}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}t^{2}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\left\lvert Q\right\rvert\,.

For the last term
III
, observe that resolvents of
M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}
map into
\dom(M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B})\subseteq\dom(T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B})
, so we have

(97)

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{t}b

\displaystyle=2t(1+t^{2}\bm{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}(1+\tau^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}

(98)

\displaystyle=2t(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1}T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}(1+\tau^{2}M_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B})^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\,

(99)

\displaystyle=t(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}^{*}\bm{B}b.

By Lemma
3.13
, we have
\left\lVert T^{*}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\bm{B}b\right\rVert_{2}\lesssim\tau^{-1}\lVert\mathbf{1}_{2Q}\bar{\xi}\rVert_{2}\lesssim\tau^{-1}\left\lvert Q\right\rvert^{1/2}
.
Hence, uniform boundedness of the resolvents
(1+t^{2}\bm{L})^{-1}
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{2}({\bm{O}})^{m})
gives

III\lesssim\varepsilon^{-2}\ell^{-2}\int_{0}^{\ell}t^{2}|Q|\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}=\frac{\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,.\qed

8.2.
Stopping time argument

We can now exploit the dyadic structure on
{\bm{O}}
and the properties of the
T(b)
–test functions constructed in Proposition
8.2
to derive the key estimate (
90
).
For this, fix a matrix
\nu\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{N},\mathbb{C}^{m})
with
\left\lVert\nu\right\rVert=1
. As the adjoint matrix
\nu^{*}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{N})
also has norm
1
, we find unit vectors
\xi=\xi_{\nu}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
\eta=\eta_{\nu}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
with
\overline{\xi}=\nu^{*}\eta
.
To prove (
90
), the main point is to deduce from property (b) in Proposition
8.2
that
\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon}(x)
points roughly in direction
\overline{\xi}
, so that
\mu\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon}(x)
is not too small whenever
\mu\in\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}
, at least for all
x
in a
“substantial subset”
of
Q
.

Proposition 8.3
.

There is some
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and some
\kappa>0
such that, for all matrices
\nu\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{N})
of unit norm and all
Q\in\Box
, there exist pairwise disjoint dyadic children
Q_{j}\subseteq Q
such that for the sets

(100)

E(Q)=Q\setminus\bigcup_{j}Q_{j}\subseteq Q\ ,\qquad E^{*}(Q)=R(Q)\setminus\bigcup_{j}R(Q_{j})\,,

there hold the following properties:

(1)

\left\lvert E(Q)\right\rvert\geq\kappa\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
,

(2)

\left\lvert\mu(\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon})(x)\right\rvert\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert
for all
(x,t)\in E^{*}(Q)
and
\mu\in\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}
.

Proof.

Having Proposition
8.2
at hand, we can follow the Euclidean proof (for instance in
[
21
, Lem. 14.8]
) almost verbatim. Hence, we are only going to present the necessary changes.

The selection of the dyadic children
Q_{j}\subseteq Q
and the proof of part (a) is completely analogous to the Euclidean case.
Hence, we concentrate on (b). Let
(x,t)\in E^{*}(Q)
and
\mu\in\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}
.
For brevity, set
v\coloneqq(\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon})(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
As in the Euclidean case, the construction of
E^{*}(Q)
implies the bounds
|v|\leq\tfrac{1}{4\varepsilon}
and
\operatorname{Re}(\xi\cdot v)\geq\tfrac{3}{4}
.
Using the triangle inequality, the definition of
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}
and the upper bound for
|v|
, deduce

(101)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert}v\right\rvert\geq|\nu v|-\left\lVert\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert}-\nu\right\rVert|v|\geq|\nu v|-\varepsilon|v|\geq|\nu v|-\frac{1}{4}.

Next, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
and the relation
\overline{\xi}=\nu^{*}\eta
, we obtain the lower bound

(102)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nu v\right\rvert\geq\operatorname{Re}\langle\eta,\nu v\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle\nu^{*}\eta,v\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle\overline{\xi},v\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(\xi\cdot v)\,,

where the brackets denote the inner products in either
\mathbb{C}^{m}
or
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Plugging this bound back into (
101
) and using the lower bound for
\operatorname{Re}(\xi\cdot v)
, deduce

(103)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{\mu}{\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert}v\right\rvert\geq\operatorname{Re}(\xi\cdot v)-\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Multiplying this bound by
\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert
yields the claim.
∎

To conclude the proof of the key estimate (
90
), we cite the John–Nirenberg lemma for Carleson measures. The result is well-known and a detailed proof using the dyadic
“cubes”
from Definition
4.2
is presented in
[
19
, p. 191]
.

Lemma 8.4

(John–Nirenberg lemma for Carleson measures)
.

Let
\sigma
be a Borel measure on
{\bm{O}}\times(0,1]
such that there are
K,\kappa>0
with the following property:
Each dyadic
“cube”

Q\in\Box
has pairwise disjoint dyadic children
Q_{j}\in\Box
such that the sets
E(Q)\subseteq Q
and
E^{*}(Q)
defined in (
100
) satisfy

(1)

\left\lvert E(Q)\right\rvert\geq\kappa\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
.

(2)

\sigma(E^{*}(Q))\leq K\left\lvert Q\right\rvert
.

Then
\sigma
is a Carleson measure with norm at most
K/\kappa
.

Proof of the key estimate (
90
).

Fix a unit matrix
\nu\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{N})
and set
\xi=\xi_{\nu}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
By Proposition
8.3
(b) and Proposition
8.2
(c), we compute

\displaystyle\iint_{E^{*}(Q)}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x))\right\rVert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\leq 4\iint_{E^{*}(Q)}\left\lvert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)(\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon})(x)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x))\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle\leq 4\iint_{R(Q)}\left\lvert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)(\mathcal{A}_{t}b^{\xi,Q,\varepsilon})(x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim\frac{\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

By Lemma
8.4
, this and Proposition
8.3
(a) ensure that
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}}(\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x))\rVert^{2}\tfrac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure, which yields the asserted estimate (
90
).
∎

We are now done with the proof of the quadratic estimate (
QE
), and hence of Theorem
1.1
, under the additional assumption that
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick.
To convince the reader, we give below a short proof summarizing the different reduction steps of our proof.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
when
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick.

In Proposition
6.1
, we have seen that the Kato square root property is equivalent to (
QE
).
The integral on the left-hand side can be split as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}(1-\bm{P}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}+\int_{1}^{\infty}\lVert\bm{\Theta}_{t}\bm{P}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lVert(\bm{\Theta}_{t}-\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t})T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}+\int_{0}^{1}\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}\mathcal{A}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u\rVert^{2}_{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,.

The first and second terms are controlled by Proposition
7.6
and Proposition
7.7
, respectively, and the third one is estimated in Proposition
7.14
.
The last integral is bounded by

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\bm{O}}\left\lvert(\mathcal{A}_{t}T_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}u)(x)\right\rvert^{2}\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,.

By Proposition
4.8
, it then suffices to prove that
\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\left\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{t}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\tfrac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t}{t}
is a Carleson measure on
{\bm{O}}\times(0,1]
, that is, the validity of (
89
).
Splitting over angular sectors
\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\nu}
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{N})
reduces this to finding an
\varepsilon>0
such that (
90
) holds true for all (matrix) directions
\nu\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{m},\mathbb{C}^{N})
. The latter was accomplished right above via the usage of
T(b)
–type test functions and a stopping time argument.
∎

9.
Removing the thickness condition

In this final section, we remove the interior thickness condition imposed in the approach performed throughout Sections
6
-
8
, thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the general case. To do so, we employ the transference strategy pioneered in
[
15
, Sec. 6]
. If the Dirichlet parts were the same for all components, their argument would apply out of the box. For the reader’s convenience, and since we want to extend their result to varying Dirichlet parts for different components of the system, we recap their argument and comment on the necessary changes.

Let
{\bm{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and
{\bm{D}}_{j}\subseteq\bd{\bm{O}}
be closed, and assume that
{\bm{O}}
is locally uniform near
\partial{\bm{O}}\setminus{\bm{D}}_{j}
for every
j=1,\dots,m
. Put
{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}\coloneqq({\bm{D}}_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
.
Now, fix a decomposition of
{\bm{O}}
into pairwise disjoint open sets
O_{i}
. Define the array
{\overrightarrow{D}}_{i}
via
({\overrightarrow{D}}_{i})_{j}\coloneqq{\bm{D}}_{j}\cap\partial O_{i}

and consider elliptic operators
L_{i}
on
O_{i}
subject to a vanishing trace condition on
{\overrightarrow{D}}_{i}\subseteq(\partial O_{i})^{m}
and whose coefficients are the restriction of the coefficients of
\bm{L}
to
O_{i}
. Then, the following correspondence holds
[
15
, Prop. 6.8]
.

Proposition 9.1

(Transference principle)
.

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\dom(\sqrt{\bm{L}})=\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}({\bm{O}})^{m}
with
\|\sqrt{\bm{L}}u\|_{2}\approx\|u\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,2}({\bm{O}})}
,

(ii)

\dom(\sqrt{L_{i}})=\mathbb{W}^{1,2}_{{\overrightarrow{D}}_{i}}(O_{i})
with
\|\sqrt{L_{i}}u\|_{2}\approx\|u\|_{\mathbb{W}^{1,2}(O_{i})}
for all
i
, where the implicit constants are independent of
i
.

Now, the central idea in
[
15
]
is as follows: starting with a triple
(O,{\overrightarrow{D}},L)
as in Theorem
1.1
, they construct a new triple
({\bm{O}},{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}},\bm{L})
that satisfies all assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, contains
(O,{\overrightarrow{D}},L)
as a
“component”
in the sense explained above, and such that
{\bm{O}}
is in addition interior thick. Then, since we have proved Theorem
1.1
under the additional assumption that
{\bm{O}}
is interior thick, item (i) of Proposition
9.1
is verified. Therefore, Proposition
9.1
provides the square root property for all triples quantified in (ii), including the triple
(O,{\overrightarrow{D}},L)
, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the general case.

Hence, it only remains to clarify the construction of the triple
({\bm{O}},{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}},\bm{L})
. By assumption,
O
is locally an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-domain near
\partial O\setminus D_{j}\eqqcolon N_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,m
. Let
\Sigma
denote a grid of open axis-parallel cubes of diameter
\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{8}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\Sigma^{\prime}
contain those cubes
Q
from
\Sigma
for which
\overline{Q}
intersects
D
but which have empty intersection with the tube of size
\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{4}}
around
N^{*}\coloneqq\cup_{j=1}^{m}N_{j}
. Now, put

(104)

\displaystyle{\bm{O}}\coloneqq O\cup\bigcup_{Q\in\Sigma^{\prime}}(Q\setminus\partial O)\quad\text{and}\quad({\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})_{j}\coloneqq\partial{\bm{O}}\setminus N_{j}.

In
[
15
]
, all components were subject to the same Dirichlet boundary conditions, so that
N^{*}=N
in that paper. In our more general setting, all desired properties for
({\bm{O}},{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}})
except the interior thickness condition immediately follow by the same proof as in
[
15
, Sec. 6.3]
.

To show the interior thickness condition for
{\bm{O}}
, the authors of
[
15
]
argue first that only balls centered in
x\in O
have to be considered. Now, we need to distinguish cases: first, if
x
is in the
\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{2}}
-tube around
N^{*}
, then
x
is in the
\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{2}}
-tube around
N_{j}
for
some
index
j=1,\dots,m
, and thickness in
x
is provided by the corkscrew condition in Proposition
2.7
. Otherwise, as in
[
15
]
, it follows that
x\in\overline{Q}
for some
Q\in\Sigma^{\prime}
, so that thickness is guaranteed by the respective property of the cube
Q
. Thus,
{\bm{O}}
is indeed interior thick. Finally, the operator
L
can be extended to
{\bm{O}}
by setting it equal to
-\Delta_{\overrightarrow{\bm{D}}}+1
on
{\bm{O}}\setminus O
. This completes the proof.
\square
