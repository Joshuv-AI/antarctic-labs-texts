Higher-order, quantum magnetic inductions in chiral topological materials
Yizhou Liu, Hengxin Tan, Binghai Yan

Abstract
The classic magnetic induction effect is usually considered in electric circuits or conductor coils. In this work, we propose quantum induction effects induced by the Berry curvature in homogenous solids. Two different types of quantum inductions are identified in the higher order of a magnetic field. They are closely related to the magnetic-field-induced anomalous velocity proportional to the Berry curvature so that both quantum inductions become significantly enhanced near Weyl points. Further, we propose an ac-magnetic-field measurement to detect quantum induction effects and distinguish them from classical induction.



Introduction
.—Faraday’s law of induction, which describes the current generated by a varying magnetic field, is usually considered in the coil geometry. But it also exists in uniform gyrotropic materials as the low frequency limit of optical gyrotropy
Landau et al. 2013
and gains renewed interest in recent studies of topological materials.

In Weyl semimetals
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Yan and Felser 2017
, the chiral magnetic effect (CME) known previously in particle physics
Fukushima et al. 2008
can lead to a nonequilibrium current in the presence of a nonorthogonal electric field and magnetic field (
\boldsymbol{B}
)
Son and Yamamoto 2012
. The CME roots in the chiral anomaly and the Berry curvature of Weyl fermions
Nielsen and Ninomiya 1983
;
Son and Spivak 2013
.
A similar effect
was also proposed to generate current by a varying magnetic field (
\boldsymbol{\dot{B}}
) rather than the static
\boldsymbol{B}

Zyuzin et al. 2012
;
Vazifeh and Franz 2013
;
Jian-Hui et al. 2013
;
Başar et al. 2014
;
Goswami et al. 2015
. This dynamical
effect
was later recognized as a gyrotropic magnetic effect (GME)
Zhong et al. 2016
. It is equivalently a Faraday induction effect and not governed by the chiral anomaly or Berry curvature.

In this work, we find that the Faraday induction includes quantum contributions in the higher order in addition to GME. The quantum induction (QI) effects are intimately induced by the Berry curvature in general and related to the chiral anomaly for a Weyl semimetal. They can be significantly enhanced near Berry curvature monopoles such as Weyl points or multiply-degenerate points. We have calculated QIs in chiral topological semimetals CoSi and MnSi and proposed experiments to distinguish QIs from the coexisting classical induction (CI).

Semiclassical theory of induction
.—According to the semiclassical equation of motion of electrons
Chang and Niu 1996
;
Sundaram and Niu 1999
;
Xiao et al. 2010
the electronic velocity
\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}
under external magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}(t)
can be derived as,

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{k}}\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\frac{e}{\hbar}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}})\boldsymbol{B},

(1)

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\boldsymbol{v_{k}}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{m_{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}),

(2)

with
D_{\boldsymbol{k}}=1+\frac{e}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}
being the modified density of states
Xiao et al. 2005
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
refers to the ordinary band velocity
\boldsymbol{v_{k}}=\frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{m_{k}}
are the Berry curvature and orbital magnetic moment, respectively. The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
) is the magnetic-field-induced anomalous velocity which gives rise to the chiral anomaly in the presence of an extra electric field
Son and Spivak 2013
.

In this work, we focus on the effect of magnetic-field-induced current. The electric current is expressed as

\boldsymbol{j}=-e\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}D_{\boldsymbol{k}}\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}f,

(3)

with
f
being the nonequilibrium distribution function of electrons which the Boltzmann transport equation can determine in the relaxation time approximation:

-\frac{f-f_{0}}{\tau}=\dot{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}f+\dot{\boldsymbol{r}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{r}}f+\partial_{t}f,

(4)

where
f_{0}=\frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{F})/k_{B}T}+1}
is the equilibrium Fermi-Dirac distribution function with
\varepsilon_{F}
being the Fermi energy. For uniform but time-dependent magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}(t)
, the nonequilibrium distribution function
f
up to the first order of relaxation time
\tau
can be derived as

f=f_{0}+\tau(\boldsymbol{m_{k}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{B}})\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}+O(\tau^{2}),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{m_{k}}\equiv-\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial\boldsymbol{B}}
. By substituting Eqs. (
1
), (
2
), and (
5
) into (
3
), we get the final expression of the induction current as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}^{c}+\boldsymbol{j}^{q},

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}^{c}=

\displaystyle-e\tau\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\boldsymbol{m_{k}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{B}})\boldsymbol{v_{k}}\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=x,y,z}C_{\alpha\beta}\dot{B}_{\beta}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha},

(7)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}^{q}=

\displaystyle-\frac{e^{2}\tau}{\hbar}\boldsymbol{B}\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}})(\boldsymbol{m_{k}}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{B}})\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(1)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\dot{B}}\right)\boldsymbol{B}+\left(\sum_{\alpha,\beta=x,y,z}Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}\dot{B}_{\alpha}B_{\beta}\right)\boldsymbol{B}.

(9)

Equations (
6
)–(
9
) are the central results of this work. The total induction current
\boldsymbol{j}
can be divided into two parts,
\boldsymbol{j}^{c}
and
\boldsymbol{j}^{q}
, to represent the classical induction and quantum induction, respectively. Here,
\boldsymbol{j}^{q}
explicitly contains the Planck constant
\hbar
and also the Berry curvature
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
. The classical term can be rewritten into a compact form of
j^{c}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta}C_{\alpha\beta}\dot{B}_{\beta}
(
\alpha,\beta=x,y,z
) describing conventional Faraday electromagnetic induction in materials. The coefficients are

C_{\alpha\beta}=-e\tau\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}v_{\alpha}m_{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}},

(10)

which was consistent with previous formulations as the low-frequency limit of natural optical activity, or natural gyrotropy
Goswami et al. 2015
;
Zhong et al. 2016
. Corresponding inverse effect is the current-induced magnetization is described by
\boldsymbol{M}=C^{T}\boldsymbol{E}
(
T
stands for matrix transpose)
Zhong et al. 2016
;
Yoda et al. 2018
with
\boldsymbol{E}
and
\boldsymbol{M}
being the electric field and induced magnetization, respectively. According to Eq. (
10
), the diagonal coefficients (
C_{\alpha\alpha}
) vanish in the presence of inversion symmetry or mirror symmetry. Therefore, the ordinary induction can only occur in chiral systems
Yoda et al. 2015
or solenoids
Yoda et al. 2018
.

The quantum induction current
\boldsymbol{j}^{q}
is higher order in
\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{\dot{B}}
. Up to our knowledge, it is not discussed in literature so far. It can be further divided into two parts as:
\boldsymbol{j}^{q}=\left(\boldsymbol{Q}^{(1)}\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol{B}}\right)\boldsymbol{B}+\left(\sum_{\alpha\beta}Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}\dot{B}_{\alpha}B_{\beta}\right)\boldsymbol{B}
with
\boldsymbol{Q}^{(1)}=(Q^{(1)}_{x},Q^{(1)}_{y},Q^{(1)}_{z})
and
Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}
being the response coefficients of QIs whose expressions are given by:

\begin{split}Q^{(1)}_{\alpha}=&-\frac{e^{2}\tau}{\hbar}\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\boldsymbol{v_{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}})m_{\alpha}\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\\
Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}=&-\frac{e^{2}\tau}{\hbar^{2}}\int\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}m_{\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)m_{\beta}\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}.\end{split}

(11)

Recall
\boldsymbol{v_{k}}
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{k}}
are odd under inversion symmetry (
\mathcal{P}
) or time-reversal symmetry (
\mathcal{T}
) while
\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}}
and
\boldsymbol{m_{k}}
are even under
\mathcal{P}
but odd under
\mathcal{T}
.
Q^{(1)}_{\alpha}
exists when both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
are broken.
Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}
only requires
\mathcal{P}
-breaking, similar to
C_{\alpha\beta}
. Because
\boldsymbol{j}^{q}
is proportional to the Berry curvature
\boldsymbol{\Omega_{k}}
, it is naturally expected that the quantum induction is enhanced in
\mathcal{P}
-breaking topological semimetals such as the Weyl semimetal or triple-point semimetal. The Berry curvature contribution in Eq.(
10
) has the same origin as the chiral anomaly effect.

Weyl semimetal
.—Take the Weyl semimetal as an example. We can estimate the magnitudes of the coefficients
C
,
Q^{(1)}
, and
Q^{(2)}
. Near the Weyl point, the group velocity
v
is a constant while the Berry curvature and the orbital magnetic moment scale as
\Omega\propto\frac{1}{k^{2}_{F}}
and
m\propto\frac{1}{k_{F}}
, respectively, where
k_{F}=\frac{\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{W}}{\hbar v_{F}}
is the Fermi wave vector with
\varepsilon_{W}
and
v_{F}
being the energy of Weyl point and Fermi velocity, respectively. Therefore, we obtain
C\propto k_{F}
for a single Weyl point and
C\propto(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{W1})-(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{W2})=\varepsilon_{W2}-\varepsilon_{W1}
for a pair of Weyl points. Here
C
depends on the energy separation of Weyl points but less sensitive to
\varepsilon_{F}
. In contrast,
Q^{(1)}\propto\frac{1}{k_{F}}
and
Q^{(2)}\propto\frac{1}{k^{3}_{F}}
become divergent when the Fermi energy approaches Weyl points.

We consider a minimal model of a Weyl semimetal without
\mathcal{P}
or
\mathcal{T}
. A minimal model of Weyl Hamiltonian without
\mathcal{T}
has
one
pair of Weyl points. The inversion (and any mirror) symmetry is naturally broken if the energies of two Weyl points are different, which motivates us to consider the following two-band model:

\begin{split}&H_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\varepsilon_{0}+d_{x}\sigma_{x}+d_{y}\sigma_{y}+d_{z}\sigma_{z},\\
&\varepsilon_{0}=A_{0}\sin k_{z},~~~d_{x}=A\sin k_{x},~~~d_{y}=A\sin k_{y},\\
&d_{z}=M-B_{1}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})-B_{2}\cos k_{z},\end{split}

(12)

with
A_{0}
,
A
,
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
, and
M
being constants.
\sigma_{x,y,z}
refer to Pauli matrix. The band structure of such model is given by
\varepsilon_{s\boldsymbol{k}}=\varepsilon_{0}+s\sqrt{d^{2}_{x}+d^{2}_{y}+d^{2}_{z}}
(
s=\pm 1
refers to the upper or lower band) with the band crossing happens when
d_{x}=d_{y}=d_{z}=0
. Within the parameters range
|M-2B_{1}|<|B_{2}|<|M|
,
|B_{2}|<|M+2B_{1}|
, the band structure has a pair of Weyl points with opposite chiralities on the
k_{z}
-axis which are determined by
M-B_{2}\cos k_{z}=0
. The energies of the two Weyl points become different when
A_{0}\neq 0
, and the low energy effective Hamiltonian near the Weyl points become:

H_{\chi}=\hbar(v_{1}k_{x}\sigma_{x}+v_{1}k_{y}\sigma_{y}+\chi v_{2}k_{z}\sigma_{z})+\varepsilon_{\chi}+\chi\hbar v^{\prime}k_{z}.

(13)

with
v_{1}
being the group velocity along
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
axis and
v_{2}
the group velocity along
k_{z}
;
v^{\prime}
represents a tilting parameter of the Weyl cone along
k_{z}
axis;
\chi=\pm
refers the Weyl cone with positive (negative) chirality;
\varepsilon_{+}
and
\varepsilon_{-}
are the energies of Weyl points. Based on the approximation
\left.\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\approx-\delta(\varepsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\varepsilon_{F})
, the classical coefficients are calculated (for small tilted parameter
v^{\prime}
) as
SM
:

C_{\alpha\alpha}\approx\frac{e^{2}\tau\Delta\varepsilon}{12\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}},~~~\alpha=x,y,z

(14)

which depends only on the relaxation time
\tau
and the energy difference between two Weyl points
\Delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{+}-\varepsilon_{-}
. Since
\Delta\varepsilon
changes sign under inversion or mirror operations, the CI can only exist in chiral systems, consistent with our previous discussion.

Unlike CI
C_{\alpha\beta}
which depends only on the energy difference between two Weyl points but independent on the Fermi energy
\varepsilon_{F}
, the QIs
Q^{(1)}_{\alpha}
and
Q^{(2)}_{\alpha\beta}
significantly depends on
\varepsilon_{F}
according to our previous discussion. The QIs of the effective model is calculated as
SM
:

\displaystyle Q^{(1)}_{z}=-\frac{e^{3}\tau v^{2}_{1}v^{\prime}}{24\pi^{2}\hbar v_{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{+}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{-}}\right),~~~Q^{(1)}_{x,y}=0,

(15)

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{xx}=Q^{(2)}_{yy}\approx\frac{e^{4}v^{2}_{1}v^{2}_{2}\tau}{48\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{+})^{3}}-\frac{1}{(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{-})^{3}}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle Q^{(2)}_{zz}\approx\frac{e^{4}v^{4}_{1}\tau}{48\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{+})^{3}}-\frac{1}{(\varepsilon_{F}-\varepsilon_{-})^{3}}\right].

(17)

The quantum coefficients
Q^{(1)}
and
Q^{(2)}
become zero when
\varepsilon_{+}=\varepsilon_{-}
,
i.e.
when the inversion symmetry
\mathcal{P}
is restored. Remarkably, according to Eqs. (
15
)–(
17
), the quantum coefficients get maximized when
\varepsilon_{F}
approaches one of the Weyl points. Numerical results of the full lattice model in Eqs. (
12
) is shown in Ref.
SM
which is consistent with the analytical results.

In summary, we proposed two kinds of quantum induction effects in noncentrosymmetric topological materials, proportional to the Berry curvature on the Fermi surface. We illustrate the principle of our theory by studying two chiral topological materials CoSi and MnSi. Furthermore, we propose to observe the quantum induction effect in an ac magnetic field to differentiate it from the classical induction effect. Our findings provide another topological quantum effect induced by the Berry phase in solid-state materials.
