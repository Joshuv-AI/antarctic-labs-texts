Consistent data fusion with Parker
Antoon Bronselaer, Maribel Acosta

Abstract
When combining data from multiple sources, inconsistent data complicates the production of a coherent result. In this paper, we introduce a new type of constraints called edit rules under a partial key (EPKs). These constraints can model inconsistencies both within and between sources, but in a loosely-coupled matter. We show that we can adapt the well-known set cover methodology to the setting of EPKs and this yields an efficient algorithm to find minimal cost repairs of sources. This algorithm is implemented in a repair engine called Parker. Empirical results show that Parker is several orders of magnitude faster than state-of-the-art repair tools. At the same time, the quality of the repairs in terms of F 1 F_{1} -score ranges from comparable to better compared to these tools.

Introduction

We study the data fusion problem where data from different sources must be under certain consistency constraints.
This problem is modelled by assuming that each data source has the same schema and provides information on some entity in terms of single tuples.
Consistency is validated by means of two types of integrity constraints.
On one hand, tuples are subject to tuple-level constraints that are expressed as
edit rules
.
These edit rules model the internal consistency for each individual source.
On the other hand, tuples coming from different sources must agree on certain attributes and this is modelled as a (partial) key constraint.
This partial key constraint models the level agreement that sources must reach in order to be mutual consistent.

In Figure

Figure

The first four rules together enforce that a study has exactly one masking strategy.
The last two rules enforce that if a trial uses a placebo or an active comparator, then that trial is controlled.
Violations of these rules are shown in bold red font in panel (a).

Motivation

An immediate question might be why constraints are modelled as a mixture of FDs and ERs.
All constraints in Figure
1
can be represented in the
9
framework of conditional functional dependencies (CFDs)
[
1
,
2
]
or denial constraints (DCs)
[
3
]
, so why not use them?
The main problem with these formalisms, is that finding (minimal) repairs is a computationally intensive task
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
When using edit rules, however, the problem of finding (minimal) repairs boils down to finding (minimal) set covers of failing rules if the given rules satisfy a closure property
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
In a similar manner, finding (minimal) repairs for violations of a partial key constraint is easy if no backward repairing (i.e., modification of the key values) is allowed.
The main argument of this paper is that when combining both partial key constraints and edit rules, we can exploit the properties of both constraint types to efficiently prune the search space and still ensure minimal repairs.
Moreover, the combination of partial keys and edit rules alleviates the expressiveness of edit rules in such a way we can cover many constraints we observe in real life scenarios.
As such, an appealing balance between computational efficiency and expressiveness is obtained.

Contributions

We propose an efficient algorithm to find all minimal repairs when constraints are a mixture of a partial key and a set of edit rules.
The algorithm is implemented as the Parker (Partial Key and Edit Rule) repair engine and is available as a part of an open source framework

Empirical results show that Parker is superior on all tested datasets in terms of performance when compared to two state-of-the-art cleaning tools (HoloClean and Raha-Baran).
In terms of precision and recall, Parker is competitive with these tools and in some cases better.

Preliminaries

2.1
The relational model

We consider
\mathcal{A}
to be a countable set of attributes where for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
A
denotes the
domain
of
a
and is assumed to be countable.
A
schema

\mathcal{R}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
is a non-empty and finite subset of
\mathcal{A}
.
A
relation

R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
is defined by a finite set
R\subseteq A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{k}
.
Elements of
R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
are called
tuples
with schema
\mathcal{R}
.
For a relation
R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
, a set of attributes
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and a predicate
P
, we denote the
projection
of
R
over
X
by
R[X]
and
selection
over
P
by
R_{P}
.
For two relations
R^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime\prime}
with respective schemas
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
,
R^{\prime}\bowtie R^{\prime\prime}
denotes the
natural join
of
R^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime\prime}
.

2.2
Functional dependencies and partial keys

A
functional dependency
(FD)
[
11
]

\phi
defined over a schema
\mathcal{R}
is an expression of the form
X\rightarrow Y
such that
X\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
Y\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
A relation
R
with schema
\mathcal{R}

satisfies

\phi
(denoted by
R\models\phi
) if
\forall r_{1},r_{2}\in R:r_{1}[X]=r_{2}[X]\Rightarrow r_{1}[Y]=r_{2}[Y]
.
An FD
X\rightarrow Y
is
minimal
is there is no
X^{\prime}\subset X
such that
X^{\prime}\rightarrow Y
holds.
A set of attributes
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is called a
candidate key
if the FD
\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
holds and is minimal.
For a candidate key
\mathcal{K}
, a partial key is a subset of that candidate key that determines at least one attribute other than the key.

2.3
Edit rules

An
edit rule

[
8
]
is a tuple-level constraint that specifies which combinations of attribute values are not permitted to occur together.
There are different types of edit rules such as
linear
edit rules
[
8
,
10
]
,
ratio
edits
[
10
]
and
constant
edits
[
9
]
.
For simplicity, we will restrict ourselves to constant edit rules, even though the methodology is applicable to a broader category of constraints
2
2

2

The implementation in
ledc-sigma
allows rules equivalent to relational selection
\sigma
with variables.
.

A (constant)
edit rule
(ER)
E
on
\mathcal{R}=\{a_{1},...,a_{k}\}
is an expression of the form
E_{1}\times...\times E_{k}
where
E_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
.
A tuple
r

satisfies

E
(denoted by
r\models E
) if
r\notin E
.
If some
E_{i}
is empty for
E
, then
E
never fails and we call such rules
tautologies
.
Conversely, if all
E_{i}
equal
A_{i}
for rule
E
, then
E
is never satisfied and we call such rules
contradictions
.
In what follows, we assume that rules are neither a tautology nor a contradiction.
An attribute
a_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
is said to
enter
an edit rule
E
if and only if
E_{i}\subset A_{i}
.
Alternatively, we say that
E

involves

a_{i}
.
The set of attributes involved in
E
is denoted by
\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)
.

3
Edit Rules under Partial Key Constraints

3.1
Basic definition and problem statement

Suppose we have a set of data sources that all provide information on some entity.
They do so by each emitting one tuple that obeys a common schema
\mathcal{R}
.
This means that we can model the whole of these different sources as a single relation
R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
.
In our earlier example (Figure
1
, (a)), the sources correspond to member states of the EU and they each present information about one clinical trial identified by its study number.
Suppose now we want to fuse information coming from these sources in a consistent manner by enforcing two requirements.

1.

Each source is internally consistent according to a set of tuple-level constraints (Figure
1
, (b)).

2.

Sources agree on certain parts of the data and we formalize this by stating that some identifier of the entity we are describing, functionally determines some (not necessarily all) attributes (Figure
1
, (c)).

The combination of these two requirements can be formalized via the notion of an edit rule under a partial key (EPK) constraint and is defined as follows.

Definition 1

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a schema that can be partitioned into
\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
An edit rule under a partial key constraint (EPK constraint) is an edit rule
E
on
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
such that the FD
\phi:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{1}
needs to hold.

Definition
1
implies that any EPK is determined by a pair
(\phi,E)
.
It thus follows naturally that a relation
R
fails an EPK whenever either
R\not\models\phi
or there is some
r\in R
for which
r\not\models E
.
For a single schema
\mathcal{R}
, multiple edit rules can be defined, but we restrict ourselves to a single partial key.
A set of EPKs can then always be denoted by a couple
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}\right)
, where
\phi
provides the partial key constraint and
\mathcal{E}
is a set of edit rules.

The fusion model we introduced above can now be represented by a set of EPKs where
consolidation is done by repairing inconsistencies in an optimal way.
In more formal terms, if
R
fails some EPK, then we want to
modify

R
into a new relation
R^{*}
such that (i)
R^{*}
fails no EPKs and (ii) the differences between
R
and
R^{*}
are minimized.
A relation
R^{*}
is called a
repair
if is satisfies (i) and is called a
minimal repair
if it satisfies (i) and (ii).

In the scope of this paper, we focus on repairs achieved by updates only.
Moreover, we make the explicit assumption that
\mathcal{K}
does not contain errors, modelling the fact that sources make judgements on the right entities.
The minimization criterion of repairs is defined here in terms of a
cost function
for attribute changes.

Definition 2

A
cost function
for
a\in\mathcal{R}
is a positive-definite function
\Delta_{a}:A^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

If
\mathcal{R}
is entirely equipped with cost functions, then the cost for modifying some tuple
r
into a tuple
r^{*}
is simple computed by the sum of costs:

\Delta(r,r^{*})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}}\Delta_{a}\left(r[a],r^{*}[a]\right).

(1)

A repair
R^{*}
is now called
\Delta
-minimal if rows
r\in R
are modified into rows
r^{*}
such that the sum of all
\Delta\left(r,r^{*}\right)
is minimal.
One obvious cost function is the one that simply verifies whether
r[a]=r^{*}[a]
(cost
0
), or not (cost
1
).
Using this cost function,
\Delta(r,r^{*})
equals the number of attributes that were modified.
Figure
1
(d) shows a repair of Figure
1
(a) that is
\Delta
-minimal under this counting function.

In general, cost functions need not to be symmetric and we can assign a different cost function to each individual tuple.
It is usually a desirable feature that cost functions are at least constant with respect to changing a
null
value, meaning that
\Delta_{a}(\bot,v)
is independent of
v
.
We now formulate the main problem studied in this paper as follows.

:
High-level architecture of the Parker repair engine.

Problem statement

Given a relation
R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
and a set of EPKs, find all
\Delta
-minimal repairs
R^{*}
for some predefined cost function
\Delta
.
Solving this problem in a naive way, requires testing all possible repairs, which has a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(|R|\cdot\prod_{a_{i}\in\mathcal{R}}\left|A_{i}\right|\right)
.
By exploiting the properties of EPKs, we are able to construct a repair engine (Parker) that explores the search space in a very efficient manner.
The main architecture of Parker is shown in Figure
2
.
Besides the constraints, Parker is equipped with a cost model
\Delta
and a selection strategy to choose, from all
\Delta
-minimal repairs, a single repair.
We provide more details on both cost models and selection strategies in Section
5
.

3.2
Properties of EPKs

For constant ERs, an efficient method exists to find minimal repairs
[
8
]
.
If some tuple
r\in R
fails some
E\in\mathcal{E}
, a
solution
is a set of attributes
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
such that there exists a valid tuple
r^{*}
that
only
differs from
r
in
\mathcal{S}
.
In other words, a solution
\mathcal{S}
must satisfy:

\exists r^{*}:r^{*}\models\mathcal{E}\wedge r\left[\overline{\mathcal{S}}\right]=r^{*}\left[\overline{\mathcal{S}}\right].

(2)

We say that a repair
r^{*}
for
r
has solution
\mathcal{S}
if this above condition is satisfied.
For constant edit rules, solutions satisfy a particular property in the sense that constant edit rules are
interaction-free
in the sense that if we consider a single constant rule
E
and
r
is changed in two different attributes
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
to satisfy
E
, then one of both changes can be undone and
E
is still satisfied.

To find solutions efficiently,
\mathcal{E}
can be transformed into a
sufficient set
, denoted by
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.
This sufficient set has the property that any solution
\mathcal{S}
for a tuple
r
is always a set-cover of the
failing
rules from
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.
Set covers are hereby defined as follows:

Definition 3

A set cover for edit rules
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathcal{R}
is a set of attributes
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
such that
\forall E\in\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)\cap\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
.

In Figure
1
(c), the set of rules is sufficient, meaning that solutions to errors can be found by composing set covers to failing rules.
For example, the second tuple in Figure
1
(a) fails two rules and as the attribute ‘control’ covers both of these rules, we are certain there exists repair for the second tuple where we only change attribute ‘control’.

The construction of
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
is key in our approach for several reasons.
First,
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
can be constructed efficiently for small to medium-sized
\mathcal{E}
by using the FCF algorithm
[
9
]
.
Moreover, continuous research is devoted to making this construction step more efficient.
Second, construction of
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
allows to check if there a priori
exists
a tuple that satisfies
\mathcal{E}
or not.
If there exists one,
\mathcal{E}
is called
satisfiable
and it can be shown that
\mathcal{E}
is
satisfiable
if and only if
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
does not contain a contradiction.
This translates immediately to EPKs.

Proposition 1

A set of EPKs
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}\right)
is
satisfiable
if
\mathcal{E}
is satisfiable.

Third,
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
allows to find minimal repairs for EPKs, although not in a straightforward manner.
The main problem is that solutions and set covers of failing rules deal with
sets of attributes
.
However, the cost functions
\Delta_{a}
we use here, calculate cost in terms of
values
that attributes take.
We will show later that in the setting of EPKs, we
must
use such cost functions.
The consequence is that we can not simply look for minimal solutions in terms of attributes anymore, because the cost repairing those attributes depends on the values that these attributes take.
We therefore need to rely on a weaker connection between repairs and covers in terms of
\subset
-minimal covers.

Definition 4

For edit rules
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathcal{R}
, a set-cover
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
is
\subset
-minimal if no real subset of
\mathcal{C}
is a set-cover of
\mathcal{E}
.

Intuitively,
\subset
-minimal covers inform us about the different ways in how we can change tuples to satisfy failing rules.
In the example on Figure
1
(a), the second tuple fails two rules and we can either solve this by changing ‘control’ or by changing ‘placebo’ and ‘active compare’.
This intuition leads to the following proposition.

Proposition 2

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a set of edit rules on
\mathcal{R}
.
If
r^{*}
is a repair for
r
with solution
\mathcal{S}
then
\mathcal{S}
contains at least one
\subset
-minimal cover of the rules failed by
r
.

The main question is now whether or not a
\Delta
-minimal repair always has a solution
\mathcal{S}
that is
equal
to a
\subset
-minimal cover of failing rules of
\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.
Unfortunately, the answer to this question is negative because, in general, changing more attributes can lead to a lower overall cost.
This means we can not restrict ourselves to
\subset
-minimal covers when searching for
\Delta
-minimal repairs.
Fortunately, some properties exist to keep searching efficiently.
For a tuple
r
, a repair
r^{*}
is said to be
\mathcal{S}
-minimal
if it has changes only in
\mathcal{S}
and does so in a cost-minimal way.
This latter means there is no repair
r^{\prime}
with solution
\mathcal{S}
such that
\Delta(r,r^{\prime})<\Delta(r,r^{*})
.
Two important properties now relate such
\mathcal{S}
-minimal repairs to
\Delta
-minimal repairs and help in finding the latter efficiently.

Theorem 1

If
r^{*}
is a
\Delta
-minimal repair for
r
with solution
\mathcal{S}
and
there exists a
\subset
-minimal cover
\mathcal{C}
such that
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}
then for the tuple
r^{\prime}
satisfying
r^{\prime}[\mathcal{C}]=r^{*}[\mathcal{C}]\wedge r^{\prime}[\overline{\mathcal{C}}]=r[\overline{\mathcal{C}}]
we have that
(1)
r^{\prime}
is not a repair for
r
and (2)
\Delta\left(r,r^{\prime}\right)
is strictly smaller than the cost for any
\mathcal{C}
-minimal repair for
r
.

The crux of Theorem
1
is that it gives us a strategy for finding
\Delta
-minimal repairs.
More precisely, we can use all
\subset
-minimal cover of failing rules as a starting point.
For any such
\mathcal{C}
, we can iterate over possible values in order of
lowest cost first
.
We continue this iteration until we find a repair and due to the nature of iteration, this repair is then
\mathcal{C}
-minimal.
Theorem
1
now says that any potential
\Delta
-minimal repair
r^{*}
that changes
more
attributes than
\mathcal{C}
,
must
take values for
\mathcal{C}
that have lower cost than the repair we just found.
As such, if we find repairs that are
\mathcal{C}
-minimal, we only need to inspect those values for
\mathcal{C}
that have a strictly lower cost than the
\mathcal{C}
-minimal solutions and those are values we already iterated over.
A stronger result is that not each combination of values for
\mathcal{C}
with cost strictly lower than the
\mathcal{C}
-minimal solutions needs to be inspected.

Proposition 3

If
r
is a tuple that has a
\Delta
-minimal repair
r^{*}
using solution
\mathcal{S}
then we must have:

\forall a_{i}\in\mathcal{S}:r[a_{i}]\neq r^{*}[a_{i}]\wedge\exists E\in\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right):r^{*}[a_{i}]\notin E_{i}

Proposition
3
basically states we should only make changes to
r
that are necessary to satisfy some rule.
Because constant edit rules are interaction-free, Proposition
3
can be verified by observing
only
the values for
\mathcal{C}
.
This is particularly useful because Theorem
1
tells us that if some solution
\mathcal{S}
strictly contains a
\subset
-minimal cover
\mathcal{C}
, then the values taken for
\mathcal{C}
by some
\Delta
-minimal repair will themselves not resolve all failing rules.
For constant edit rules, we can now also verify whether the values for
\mathcal{C}
satisfy Proposition
3
.
We emphasize here that this latter observation holds
only
for constant edit rules.
It one wishes to use edit rules involving variables (e.g. a rule of the type
a_{1}\leq a_{2}
), then because such rules are not interaction-free, we can not apply this property if we want to find
\Delta
-minimal repairs.

4
Finding Minimal Repairs for EPKs

In this section, we exploit the properties of edit rules and EPKs introduced in the previous to compose an efficient algorithm for finding all
\Delta
-minimal repairs for a relation
R
constrained under a set of EPKs
(\phi,\mathcal{E})
.
To gradually introduce our approach, we will first study the case where
\phi
is a
full
key constraint.
In that scenario, we have
\phi:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
and
\mathcal{E}
contains edit rules defined over
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
We will introduce our algorithm in that case step by step.
Finally, we will generalize that algorithm to the case of partial keys.

4.1
Finding
\equiv_{\mathcal{K}}
tuples

To begin with,
\mathcal{K}
imposes an equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{K}}
on
R
where
r\equiv_{\mathcal{K}}r^{\prime}\Leftrightarrow r[\mathcal{K}]=r^{\prime}[\mathcal{K}]
.
It is easy to see that (i) finding
\Delta
-minimal repairs requires treating all equivalent tuples at the same time and (ii) tuples that are not equivalent can be treated independently from each other.
For that reason, we focus here on the treatment of a single equivalence class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
with
\mathbf{k}
some key value.
Semantically speaking, each class forms a group of information about a single entity provided by different sources.

4.2
Minimal cost iteration

If we have some class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
then our main interest lies in the attributes
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
which we can write as a set
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
.
Any
\Delta
-minimal repair must take values for
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
in the set
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{m}
.
Iterating over all these values this requires testing
\prod_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|
combinations which rapidly becomes intractable.
To make this more feasible, we can compose a ranking over the set
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{m}
such that lower ranked tuples imply a lower cost when using them as a repair.
In other words, this ranking allows us to iterate over possible repairs in order of
lowest cost first
(LCF).

In order to construct such an LCF ranking, we first consider weak orders
\leq_{i}
over the different attribute domains
A_{i}
.
For each attribute
a\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
and for any value
v\in A
, we can see that if
r^{*}
would be a repair for the class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
with
r^{*}[a]=v
, then the cost contributed by
a
is equal to:

c\left(v\right)=\sum_{r^{\prime}\in R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}}\Delta_{a}(r^{\prime}[a],v)

(3)

We hereby simply sum the costs required to change the value of
a
, for each member of the class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
into
v
.
The total cost for repairing class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
with tuple
r^{*}
is then equal to the sum of all
c(r^{*}[a])
over
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
In other words, we have that:

\beta(\mathbf{k},r^{*})=\sum_{r\in R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}}\Delta(r,r^{*})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}}c\left(r^{*}[a]\right)

(4)

We use the notation
\beta\left(\mathbf{k},.\right)
here to indicate the local objective we want to minimize for each class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
.
In the example of Figure
1
(a), suppose we consider a constant cost model
\Delta_{a}
for each attribute
a
where
\Delta_{a}(v,v^{\prime})
is
0
if
v=v^{\prime}
and
1
if
v\neq v^{\prime}
.
In that case, for attribute ‘open’ we find
c(\text{`No'})=c(\text{`Yes'})=2
.
Similarly, for attribute ‘control’, we find
c(\text{`No'})=1
and
c(\text{`Yes'})=3
.
Note here that, although the cost model
\Delta_{a}
is constant for individual rows, the
induced
cost for changing some value for
an entire class
, is not.

Consider now a weak order
\leq_{a}
over
A
such that
v\leq_{a}v^{\prime}
if and only if
c\left(v\right)\leq c\left(v^{\prime}\right)
.
Next, we choose a
total
order that is

.

As soon as we observe solutions with higher cost, we can stop the algorithm and return all tuples that satisfy
\mathcal{E}

Clearly, this search still has a worst case complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|\right)

Still, in this naive strategy, we will in general observe tuples for which we know they will not be consistent and skipping those tuples can further improve the efficiency of the search.

4.3
Exploiting independence of rules

One way of improving the complexity of LCF iteration is to treat groups of attributes independently.
Suppose we can partition
\mathcal{E}
in two sets
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
such that attributes involved in rules from
\mathcal{E}_{1}
are not involved in rules from
\mathcal{E}_{2}
and vice versa.
Formally:

\left(\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{1}}\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)\right)\cap\left(\bigcup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{2}}\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)\right)=\emptyset.

(5)

When this condition is satisfied, we say that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are
independent
.
For a partition into independent sets, there are two essential properties that hold.
First, Barcaroli and Venturi
[
12
]
showed that
if
\mathcal{E}
partitions into independent sets
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, then

\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}\right)\cup\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}_{2}\right)
.
Second, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 4

Let
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}\right)
be a set of EPKs on
\mathcal{R}
and
R
a relation with schema
\mathcal{R}
.
If
\mathcal{E}
partitions into two independent sets
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
such that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
are edit rules on
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
are edit rules on
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
with
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}=\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{K}
then
R^{*}
is a
\Delta
-minimal repair if and only if
R^{*}[\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime}]
is a
\Delta
-minimal repair for
R[\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime}]
against
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)
and
R^{*}[\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}]
is a
\Delta
-minimal repair for
R[\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}]
against
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}\right)
.

These results show for any
(\phi,\mathcal{E})
that if
\mathcal{E}
is composed of independent sets, then repairing inconsistencies can be done for
(\phi,\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
(\phi,\mathcal{E}_{2})
separately.
Note that recursive application of both propositions allows to generalize the results to a partition into
K
independent sets.
In Figure

4.4
Finding minimal repairs under full key constraints

We now combine all the results from the previous into an algorithm.
The main idea is that, when we perform an LCF iteration over some set
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{m}
, we try to keep
m
small by focusing on
\subset
-minimal covers.

Consider some class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
and let us now index tuples consistent with the LCF order, just as we did for individual attributes.
That is, the first tuple under LCF order is
r_{(1)}
, the second tuple
r_{(2)}
and so on.
There are now two cases.

\boxed{Case\ 1}
. Suppose
r_{(1)}\models\mathcal{E}
, then this tuple is one of the minimal repairs for
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
.
That means, if we replace the attributes
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
of each tuple in
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
with
r_{(1)}
, then the class is repaired with minimal cost.
Of course,
r_{(1)}
is not guaranteed to be the only minimal cost repair.
Therefore, consider the set of tuples:

M_{\mathbf{k}}=\{r\mid r\in A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{k}\wedge\beta(\mathbf{k},r)=\beta(\mathbf{k},r_{(1)})\}

(6)

This set contains the first
|M_{\mathbf{k}}|
tuples under LCF order that all have equal cost.
Clearly, if
\exists r^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbf{k}}:r^{\prime}\models\mathcal{E}
, then the set of minimal cost repairs for
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
is a subset of
M_{\mathbf{k}}
and it suffices to inspect
M_{\mathbf{k}}
.

\boxed{Case\ 2}
. If the first case does not apply, we have
\forall r^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbf{k}}:r^{\prime}\not\models\mathcal{E}
.
We then want to change
r^{\prime}
into some
r^{\prime\prime}\notin M_{\mathbf{k}}
.
If we do that by modifying its value for
a
, then:

\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime\prime}\right)=\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime}\right)+c\left(r^{\prime\prime}[a]\right)-c\left(r^{\prime}[a]\right)

(7)

for which we know that
c\left(r^{\prime\prime}[a]\right)-c\left(r^{\prime}[a]\right)>0
because
r^{\prime\prime}\notin M_{\mathbf{k}}
.
This can be generalized to:

\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime\prime}\right)=\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime}\right)+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}}c\left(r^{\prime\prime}[a]\right)-c\left(r^{\prime}[a]\right).

(8)

where we require that
c\left(r^{\prime\prime}[a]\right)>c\left(r^{\prime}[a]\right)
to ensure
r^{\prime\prime}\notin M_{\mathbf{k}}
.
The above expression shows that the cost for repairing
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
by
r^{\prime\prime}
can be written as the cost for
r^{\prime}
and the functions
c\left(.\right)
.
But since
r^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbf{k}}
, we know that
\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime}\right)
is already minimal.
Hence, in order to minimize
\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime\prime}\right)
we must minimize the second term of the right hand side, conditioned on
r^{\prime\prime}\models\mathcal{E}
.
To do so, we consider for each attribute
a\in\mathcal{R}
an
induced
cost function
\Delta_{a}^{\bullet}
on the set
A^{\bullet}=\{v\mid v\in A\wedge c(v)>r^{\prime}[a]\}
that satisfies
\forall v\in A^{\bullet}:\Delta_{a}^{\bullet}\left(r^{\prime}[a],v\right)=c\left(v\right)-c\left(r^{\prime}[a]\right)
.
This induced cost function gives the cost to change attribute
a
of tuple
r^{\prime}
accounting for all tuples in
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}
.
By construction,
\Delta_{a}^{\bullet}
is positive definite on
A^{\bullet}
and we can now write:

\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime\prime}\right)=\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime}\right)+\Delta^{\bullet}(r,r^{\prime\prime}).

(9)

It follows that if we find a
\Delta^{\bullet}
-minimal repair
r^{*}
for
r^{\prime}
, then clearly, we minimize
\beta\left(\mathbf{k},r^{\prime\prime}\right)
under the condition
r^{\prime\prime}\models\mathcal{E}
.
This now leads us the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 2

For any class
R_{\mathcal{K}=\mathbf{k}}\subseteq R
with schema
\mathcal{R}
, some cost model
\Delta
and a set of EPKs
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}\right)
, any
\Delta
-minimal repair
r^{*}
is the
\Delta^{\bullet}
-minimal repair of some tuple
r^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbf{k}}
.

The importance of Theorem
2
is that it reduces finding minimal repairs for EPKs
\left(\phi,\mathcal{E}\right)
to finding minimal repairs for
\mathcal{E}
, but with a modified cost model
\Delta^{\bullet}
.
In the previous section, we already pointed out that this can be done by inspection of
\subset
-minimal covers.
Now, as a final step, we connect this inspection to the notion of LCF iteration.
More precisely, suppose we have some
r^{\prime}\in M_{\mathbf{k}}
for which we have the induced cost model
\Delta^{\bullet}
.
If
r^{\prime}\not\models\mathcal{E}
, we compose the failing rules as
\{E\mid E\in\underline{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\wedge r^{\prime}\not\models E\}
.
Next, we find all
\subset
-minimal covers
\mathcal{C}
of this set of failing rules.
For each such cover
\mathcal{C}
, we can find a minimal
\mathcal{C}
-repair by using Algorithm

where

.
In other words, we do an LCF-tuple iteration, but consider only attributes in
\mathcal{C}
.
In general, we can expect
|\mathcal{C}|
to be significantly smaller than
|\mathcal{R}|
.
In a final step, we need to account for the fact that the solution attributes of a
\Delta
-minimal repair are not necessarily equal to a
\subset
-minimal cover, but can also be a superset of such a cover.
To account for this, we can use Theorem
1
and inspect only those value combinations for
\mathcal{C}
that occur in LCF order before any
\mathcal{C}
-minimal repair.
In addition, if we consider constant edit rules, we can use Proposition
3
to discard any value combinations for
\mathcal{C}
where some value changes to attributes in
\mathcal{C}
are unnecessary.

The case of partial key constraints

Consider now the general case of a partial key constraint
\phi:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{1}
and where
\mathcal{E}
contains edit rules defined over
\mathcal{R}_{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{2}
.
The main problem is now that a
\Delta
-minimal repair for
R[\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{R}_{1}]
and constraints
(\phi,\mathcal{E}_{1})
will not necessarily lead us to a
\Delta
-minimal repair for
R

In Figure

In order to solve the more generic problem of partial keys, we can again assume that each set of tuples

\mathcal{E}
(if there are, we can use a divide-and-conquer strategy).
In the example of Figure

In the first independent set, rules involve only attributes determined by ‘#study’, so that problem can be treated entirely by Algorithm

In the second independent set, rules involve both attributes determined by ‘#study’ (i.e., ‘control’) and attributes not determined by ‘#study’ (i.e., ‘placebo’ and ‘active compare’).

5
Cost Models and Repair Selection

After introducing the main methodology for finding repairs, it should be clear that cost functions are of crucial importance in our methodology.
Moreover, as minimal repairs are not unique, we require repair selection methods to select a single repair from the minimal alternatives.
In this section, we discuss some practical methods to build cost functions and repair selection methods.

5.1
Cost functions

In the most simple case, a cost function is
constant
and we have:

\Delta_{a}(v,v^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&v=v^{\prime}\\
\alpha&v\neq v^{\prime}\end{array}\right.

Here,
\alpha
is some constant integer cost that can be used to reflect the reliability of attribute
a
.
That is, a more reliable attribute
a
can be assigned a higher cost
\alpha
.
Using constant cost functions for edit rules has the advantage that all minimal repairs must have a solution equal to a
\subset
-minimal cover, which makes it much more easy to find repairs.
When using constant cost functions in the setting of EPKs, then
\alpha=1
leads to an induced cost function
\Delta_{a}^{\bullet}
that uses
majority voting
for values.

It has been established in the past that majority voting is not always the best choice as a fusion model
[
13
,
14
]
.
A better approach is to account for reliability of sources
[
14
]
, which in our case correspond to tuples in
R
.
Usually, reliability is estimated based on some training data.
We propose to deal with reliability in a different, non-supervised manner by assigning each row in
R
it’s own cost function.
The constant
\alpha
we use in a constant cost model is then multiplied with a factor that is inverse proportionate to the ‘badness’ of the row.
This multiplier is computed, for each row
r\in R
as follows:

\left(|\mathcal{R}|-\left|\{a\mid r[a]=\bot\vee\left(a\in\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)\wedge r\not\models E\right)\}\right|\right)^{\omega}

(10)

Hereby, we make a very course and pessimistic estimate of the number of attributes that are ‘in error’ by looking at those attributes that either occur in a failing rule or have a null value.
We then subtract that number from the total number of attributes (i.e.
|\mathcal{R}|
) and raise it to the power
\omega
, which when greater then
1
acts an amplifier of the effect.
We can then compute the cost for changing the value of attribute
a
for row
r
from
v
to
v^{\prime}
as:

\Delta_{a}(v,v^{\prime})\cdot\left(|\mathcal{R}|-\left|\{a\mid r[a]=\bot\vee\left(a\in\mathcal{I}\left(E\right)\wedge r\not\models E\right)\}\right|\right)^{\omega}

(11)

By doing so, we basically model that making a change to a row that contains many attributes that might be in error has a relatively low cost.
We will show in Section
7
that this heuristic can provide a significant boost in the quality of repairs.

Besides these two basic cost functions, a plenitude of other cost functions can be considered.
For example, when data are additive, the cost for changing
v
into
v^{\prime}
can account for the
magnitude

|v-v^{\prime}|
of the change.
Another way of dealing with cost, is to account for specific error mechanisms.
For example, when
v
and
v^{\prime}
are string data, one could assign a lower cost to a change where the Levenshtein distance between
v
and
v^{\prime}
is very small, indicating that the change from
v
to
v^{\prime}
can be explained by a typographical error.
In the same spirit, edits of dates could be equipped with error-aware cost functions.
As a final example, a cost function could also account for a priori preferences among values.
Consider for example a case with allergen information.
Assume there are different sources that provide conflicting information on the allergens of a single product.
One source could say that product A contains gluten and another source could say product A does not contain gluten.
In such a scenario, one is inclined to prefer the value of the source that induces the least
risk
and therefore model that the cost of changing ‘gluten’ into ‘no gluten’ is much greater than the cost of the opposite change.
We will demonstrate the impact of such a cost model in Section
7
.

5.2
Repair selection

When multiple repairs are found to have a minimal cost, it can be desirable to provide a further selection strategy to choose a ‘best’ repair from the minimal ones.
One obvious method to do so, is simply to pick a minimal repair at random.
In this line of thought, we consider each minimal repair to be equally good and make no further distinction between minimal repairs.

A second method would be to aim at respecting the (marginal and joint) distributions of the observed data as much as possible.
To that end, one often uses the clean data (i.e., the data in
R
that contains no violations of rules) to estimate the distributions of the data.
When such distributions are available, one can select a repair among all minimal repairs with a probability that is proportionate to the observed frequency of that repair.
There are several ways to implement such a strategy.
We will resort in our experiments to a simple method where make an estimate of the probability of observing a repaired object, meaning we measure the frequency of the entire object after repairing.
Alternatives could be to observe the frequency of only the part of the attributes that are changed or make a selection of the attributes for which frequencies are measured.

As a final note, we emphasize the distinction between cost functions and repair selection.
One could argue that repair selection becomes largely disposable if we would include for example information about frequency in the computation of cost.
We do however reject this idea.
In our methodology,
cost
is something that can be used to make
local
distinctions between repairs for a given entity.
It accounts for information about that entity and not other entities.
Whenever there are multiple repairs that are minimal in terms of cost,
global
information about the data, such as distributions, can be used to make a further distinction.
In general, we think this difference is important to avoid an all too large influence of the distributions in for example extremely skewed datasets.

6
Related Work

Data fusion covers a broad category of problems where information from different sources needs to be consolidated.
In this paper, we study the specific scenario where (i) each source is modelled by a single tuple of a relation and (ii) consolidation means merging into a single tuple for those attributes under the partial key constraint.
This seemingly simple problem, and slight variants of it, has been studied surprisingly often in the vast body of literature dealing with data fusion.
The very first approaches kept a close connection to the relational setting.
They used relational operators like union and (match) join
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
.
Continuing on this idea, the SQL language has been extended with a
fuse by
operator, yielding a declarative approach towards data fusion
[
19
,
20
]
.
An interesting observation is that these relational approaches usually use operators like subsumption and complement to ‘clean’ a relation and minimize the amount of
null
values
[
21
]
.
In that extent, it can be said that the even earliest approaches used some notion of consistency to optimize the result.

One particular problem with fusion by means of relational operators, is that it does not deal well with errors in data.
To that extent, attribute-level fusion functions can be used to deal with linear conversions
[
22
,
23
]
, specificity of information
[
24
]
and multi-valued data
[
25
,
26
]
.
A good overview of possible attribute-level fusion functions can be found in
[
27
]
.
Another approach to resolve inconsistencies is to add sufficiently many sources.
A particular problem hereby is that sources can copy from one another and knowing these dependencies turns out to be important in source selection
[
13
]
.
Moreover, it has been established that estimating the reliability of the different sources significantly influences the quality of the fusion operator
[
14
,
28
]
.
In the current paper, we have adopted this idea, but rather than using a training set to estimate reliability, we use an unsupervised estimate of the reliability based on failing rules and missing values.

The attribute-level fusion functions that are mentioned here aim to resolve inter-source inconsistencies.
The approach we develop here is more general in the sense it allows to deal with both intra-source and inter-source inconsistencies.
To that extent, some approaches from the field of constraint-based consistency verification follow the same strategy.
For example, Llunatic allows to model extended equality-generating dependencies (EGDs) and uses a generalization of the Chase algorithm to construct a Chase tree that can be used to search repairs of dirty data
[
4
,
5
]
.
In practical applications, the branching factor of a Chase tree is so high, that pruning strategies are required to keep the computational effort feasible.
A consequence thereof is that solutions are not guaranteed to be minimal, although there are good heuristics to approximate minimal solutions.
Another way to find repairs, is by learning a probabilistic model of the data
[
6
]
.
Such a methodology is used in HoloClean, that allows to model a set of denial constraints (DCs) that need to be satisfied on the data
[
6
]
.
HoloClean uses the available constraints in combination with other information like correlation analysis, outlier detection techniques, reference data… to learn a model for the data.
From that model, it then generates repairs for the given data.
Finally, Raha
[
29
]
and Baran
[
7
]
are two semi-supervised systems for “configuration-free” error detection (Raha) and repair (Baran).
The main idea here is that no constraints need to be given.
Instead, error patterns are learned from a small dirty dataset for which either users provide correct labels or a clean version is available.
Parker differs from these approaches by using a specific type of constraints (EPKs) that are less expressive than extended EGDs and DCs, but can use the set-cover approach to search for minimal repairs efficiently.
Hereby, a wide variety of cost functions can be used to model a broad range of error mechanisms.

Approaches

We compare our proposed solution Parker with recent state-of-the-art approaches for error correction using the following configurations.

1.

Parker.
We evaluate different configurations of Parker (denoted
\cdot/\cdot/\cdot
) with variations in the key constraint
\phi
, the cost model
\Delta
and the repair selection strategy, respectively.
For the
key constraint
, we either consider a full key constraint (F) or no key constraint (N).
We did not test a partial key scenario due to lack of golden standards for that configuration.
The case with no key constraint allows to study an edit-rules-only case versus EPKs.
For the
cost model
, our baseline scenarios are constant cost functions (C) and cost functions that adopt an estimate of reliability based on observed errors (E) as introduced in Section
5.1
.
In the latter case,
\omega=4
provided the best results.
For the ‘Allergen’ dataset, we additionally consider a preference cost function (P).
This cost function models combines a reliability estimate (
\omega=4
) with a risk-aversion strategy, where we are reluctant to changes that remove an indication of an allergen (high cost) and are more inclined to accept repairs that add indications of allergens (low cost).
For the
repair selection
, we evaluate the frequency-based (F) and random (R) strategies.
Frequency-based selection means here we sample repairs with a probability equal to their observed frequency in the clean data.
In both cases, a frequent pattern tree was constructed from the clean data.
As a final note, we also tested scenarios where we removed the edit rules and used only the key constraint.
Those scenarios are marked with a
*
and in that case, we do not consider cost functions with a reliability estimate as there are no edit rules to detect tuple-level errors.

2.

HoloClean
[
6
]
.
We evaluate the performance of HoloClean
11
11

11

https://github.com/HoloClean/holoclean

v1.0.0
using different error detector strategies: null detector (HoloClean-N), violation detector (HoloClean-V), and both detectors (HoloClean). The other parameters were used as provided by default.

[
29
,
7
]
.

12

12

Therefore, Raha and Baran were provided with the subset of each dataset for which the gold standard is available.
To detect errors, Raha relies on a labelling budget

Evaluation Metrics

We report on precision, recall, and the
F_{1}
metrics.
A
true positive
corresponds to a cell that is correctly repaired with respect to the gold standard.
Precision (
P
) is the number of correct repairs divided by the number of total repairs performed by the approach.
Recall (
R
) is the number of correct repairs divided by the number of errors measured by comparing the input data with the gold standard.

F_{1}
is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
We also measure the effectiveness of the approaches per attribute; we compute precision, recall, and
F_{1}
scores of the repairs in each attribute and average them by the number of attributes in the dataset to obtain the macro-precision (
P_{M}
), macro-recall (
R_{M}
) and macro-
F_{1}
(
F_{1M}
).
The efficiency of the approaches is measured as the runtime in seconds (
s
).

We compare the effectiveness of the repairs obtained with Parker and with the state-of-the-art approaches.
Table
2
presents a summary of the precision, recall, and
F_{1}

Overall, we observe that none of the studied approaches completely overcomes the other baselines.
This is due to the different repair strategies implemented by the approaches and the distribution of errors and coverage of edit rules in the datasets.

The effectiveness of Parker depends on the cost model, the repair selection strategy, and the leverage of the constraints.
In the Allergen dataset, the preferential cost model of Parker that implements a risk-aversion strategy clearly outperforms the other Parker configurations, since this type of model captures the intrinsic properties of this dataset.
Yet, Parker can only handle repairs when the input sources disagree on the attribute values of an entity.
For example, Parker could not compute correct repairs for the attribute
cashew
, as both web sources indicated the absence of an allergen for the same product.
This results in Parker achieving higher precision than recall.
A similar behaviour is observed in Parker over the EudracCT dataset.
In EudraCT, the error-based cost model (E) and frequency-based repair strategies (F) slightly outperform their corresponding counterparts with constant-based cost model (C) and random repairs (R), respectively.
In addition, when edit rules are disabled in Parker (N), we observe a major drop in both precision and recall.
This is due to the relatively high number of rows (
\sim 10\%
) that are incorrect due to violations of edit rules; this aspect is further discussed in Section
7.3
.

In the Flight dataset, we tested only the configuration of Parker that selects repairs randomly (R).
Here, the error-based cost model (E) outperforms the other configurations of Parker.
The reason for this is that E encodes the fact that sources with null values or violations of edit rules are considered less trustworthy, thus, increasing the cost of the repairs.
To verify that this behaviour is not due to the effects of the edit rules, we also tested the additional configuration Parker F/E/R* (not reported in the table), which achieves close performance to F/E/R, i.e., 0.79 in F1-score, precision, and recall.
This confirms that the gain in performance is mostly due to the error-based cost model (E) implemented by Parker, while the consideration of edit rules allow to correctly detect additional repairs.

Comparing the best configurations of Parker across the different datasets, we observe that Parker performs better on EudraCT and Flight than on the Allergen dataset.
The reason for this is the number of different sources describing the entities in each dataset.
The Allergen dataset only includes two sources and, as explained earlier, Parker cannot detect repairs where the input sources agree on incorrect values.
In contrast, the EudraCT and Flight datasets include several sources
14
14

14

Up to 44 sources in EudraCT and up to 51 in Flight.
.
For these cases, the chances that all the sources carry the same errors are very small; this is leveraged by Parker to identify the repairs.
Also, the configurations Parker F/C/F* and F/C/R* that ignore the edit rules do not outperform the other Parker configurations.
This shows the advantage of exploiting the edit rules when computing dataset repairs.
The impact of edit rules is further analyzed in Section
7.3
.

In comparison to the other approaches, Parker exhibits high precision values, and competitive
F_{1}
scores.
The main reason for the lower recall values can be attributed to the cases where entities are described by a few sources with the same errors.
In these cases, HoloClean and Raha+Baran can effectively learn repairs by taking into account the data distributions across the entire dataset, while Parker focuses on computing repairs at the entity level.
Table
2
also reports major differences between the performance of the state-of-the-art approaches.
The results indicate that the learning techniques of HoloClean are able to learn repairs from larger datasets, as observed in the high precision values of HoloClean in the EudraCT and Flight datasets.
Yet, HoloClean is not able to cover all possible errors in the data, which is reflected in the relatively low recall values in all datasets.
In contrast, Raha+Baran can effectively learn repairs from smaller datasets but it still does not outperform Parker in terms of precision.
From this, we can conclude that the learning techniques of Raha+Baran are recall-oriented allowing for identifying more types of repairs, while the cost model and the repair strategies of Parker are rather precision-oriented.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we presented a novel type of constraints called edit rules under a partial key (EPKs) to repair inconsistencies in data fusion.
Based on EPKs, we then proposed an approach to compute minimal cost repairs which allow to efficiently and effectively explore the space of repairs.
We demonstrated the theoretical properties regarding minimality of our approach.
Our proposed techniques were implemented in our Parker engine.
Our experimental study over three real-world datasets show that, on average, Parker computes more accurate repairs than the state-of-the-art approaches.
In particular, Parker outperforms the state of the art in the cases where every entity is described by several sources and the correct repairs can be devised using the edit rules.
In terms of efficiency, Parker showed to be orders of magnitude faster than the learning-based approaches.
Our results indicate that Parker is able to scale up to very large datasets.

Future work may focus on extending the computation of repairs by taking into consideration data distributions of the attributes.
This will allow Parker to capture other repairs that are not covered by EPKs; yet, this would require further investigations on the tradeoff between accuracy and scalability of the computation of the repairs.
Another line of work can investigate the application of our techniques to semantic data fusion, where additional ontological knowledge can be exploited to enhance the consistency constraints.
