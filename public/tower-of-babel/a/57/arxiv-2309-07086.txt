Complexity analysis of regularization methods for implicitly constrained least squares
Akwum Onwunta, Clément W. Royer

Abstract
Optimization problems constrained by partial differential equations (PDEs) naturally arise in scientific computing, as those constraints often model physical systems or the simulation thereof. In an implicitly constrained approach, the constraints are incorporated into the objective through a reduced formulation. To this end, a numerical procedure is typically applied to solve the constraint system, and efficient numerical routines with quantifiable cost have long been developed for that purpose. Meanwhile, the field of complexity in optimization, that estimates the cost of an optimization algorithm, has received significant attention in the literature, with most of the focus being on unconstrained or explicitly constrained problems. In this paper, we analyze an algorithmic framework based on quadratic regularization for implicitly constrained nonlinear least squares. By leveraging adjoint formulations, we can quantify the worst-case cost of our method to reach an approximate stationary point of the optimization problem. Our definition of such points exploits the least-squares structure of the objective, and provides new complexity insights even in the unconstrained setting. Numerical experiments conducted on PDE-constrained optimization problems demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed framework.

Introduction

PDE-constrained optimization problems arise in various scientific and
engineering fields
when searching for the optimal distribution of
a given quantity that satisfies
physical or mathematical laws described by
PDEs, such as heat conduction or electromagnetic
waves
[
4
,
26
,
20
,
28
]
. Similar constrained
formulations have also received recent interest from the machine learning
community, as they opened new possibilities for building neural network
architectures
[
25
]
. A popular approach to handle
PDE constraints is the so-called reduced formulation, in which the constraints
are incorporated into the objective and become
implicit
. By properly
accounting for the presence of these constraints while computing derivatives,
it becomes possible to generalize unconstrained optimization techniques to
the implicitly constrained setting
[
26
]
.

This paradigm resembles that of manifold
optimization
[
10
]
, a field of study that has gained significant
attention over the past decade and that produced the so-called
implicit

methods by accounting for constraints through Riemannian geometry.

Popular algorithms for both PDE-constrained and manifold optimization are
based on variants of the trust-region
method
[
19
,
5
]
.
Although these algorithms can be equipped with theoretical guarantees and
efficiently implemented, they are typically designed with general,
nonlinear objective functions and constraints in mind. Moreover, the cases
in which these methods are used often involve least-squares objectives, e.g.
when those problems amount to fitting a model to some observations or data.
Thus, one naturally wonders whether the least-squares structure can be
exploited in order to design algorithms tailored to these problems. In
the context of PDE-constrained optimization problems with explicit constraints,
methods tailored to least-squares formulations such as Gauss-Newton
techniques
[
22
]
, have shown promising performance
when compared to generic trust-region
approaches
[
8
]
. Still, it is
unclear whether exploiting this structure can be beneficial in an
implicitly-constrained setting.

Meanwhile,
worst-case complexity
has emerged as a way to
analyze algorithmic performance over the past decade, especially in the
nonconvex optimization community
[
12
]
.
A
complexity bound characterizes the worst-case performance of a given
optimization scheme according to a performance metric (e.g., number of
iterations, derivative evaluations, etc) and a stopping criterion (e.g.,
approximate optimality, predefined budget, etc). Recent progress in the area
has switched from designing optimization techniques with complexity guarantees
in mind to studying popular algorithmic frameworks through the prism of
complexity, with several results focusing on the least-squares
setting
[
7
,
9
,
11
,
16
]
,
as well as manifold optimization
algorithms
[
2
,
10
]
. Despite
these recent advances, complexity results remain unexplored in PDE-constrained
optimization, particularly in implicitly-constrained optimization problems.

In this paper, we study an algorithmic framework for least-squares problems
with implicit constraints. Our approach leverages the particular structure
of the objective in order to compute derivatives
through a carefully
designed adjoint equation
. Under standard assumptions for this
class of methods, we establish complexity guarantees for our framework. In
a departure from standard literature, our analysis is based on a recently
proposed stationarity criterion for least-squares
problems
[
11
]
. To the best of our knowledge,
these results are the first of their kind for implicitly constrained problems.
In addition, our complexity results improve over bounds recently obtained
in the unconstrained setting
[
9
]
,
thereby advancing our understanding of complexity guarantees for
least-squares problems. Numerical experiments on PDE-constrained problems
illustrate the practical relevance of the proposed stationarity criterion,
and show that our framework handles both small and large residual problems,
as well as nonlinearity in the implicit constraints.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
,
we present our formulation of interest, and discuss how its least-squares
structure is used to design our algorithmic framework. We establish complexity
guarantees for several instances of our proposed method in
Section
3
. In Section
4
, we investigate the performance
of our algorithm on classical benchmark problems from PDE-constrained
optimization. We finally summarize our work in Section
5
.

Least-squares optimization with implicit constraints

In this paper, we discuss algorithms for least-squares problems of the form

Problem (

2.1
Adjoint formula
for a least-squares problem

In this section, we derive an adjoint formula associated with the reduced
formulation (
2
). Even though the analysis relies on standard
arguments, to the best of our knowledge the formulas for the least-squares
setting are rather unusual in the literature. We believe that they may be of
independent interest, and therefore we provide the full derivation below.

To this end, we make the following assumption on our problem, which is a
simplified version of a standard requirement in
implicitly constrained problems
[
18
]
.

Similar assumptions are found in the PDE-constrained optimization
literature, see e.g.,
[
4
,
20
]
.

Assumption 2.1

For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n}}
, the following properties hold.

(i)

There exists a unique vector
y(u){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}
such that
c(y,u)=0
.

(ii)

The functions
J
and
c
are twice continuously
differentiable.

(iii)

The Jacobian of
c
with respect to its first argument,
denoted by
c_{y}(\cdot,\cdot)
, is invertible at any
(y,u)
such that

c(y,u)=0
.

We now introduce derivative notations for the rest of the paper. We let

\hat{J}(u):=J(y(u),u)
and
\hat{R}(u):=R(y(u),u)
denote the reduced
objective function of (
2
) and its associated residual
function, respectively. For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

\nabla\hat{J}(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the gradient of
\hat{J}
with respect
to
u
, while for any pair
(y,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let

\nabla_{y}J(y,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and
\nabla_{u}J(y,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote
the partial gradients of
J
at
(y,u)
with respect to its first and
second arguments, respectively.

It then follows
from the chain rule that

\nabla\hat{J}(u)=\nabla_{u}J(y(u),u)+c_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\lambda(u),

(3)

where
\lambda(u)
is a solution of the so-called adjoint equation

c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\lambda=-\nabla_{y}J(y(u),u).

This observation is at the heart of the adjoint method. In our case,
we can leverage the least-squares
nature
of our problem to
decompose (
3
) further. Indeed, the derivatives of

J
with respect to its first and second arguments are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\nabla_{y}J(y(u),u)&=&G_{y}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)\\
\nabla_{u}J(y(u),u)&=&G_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u),\\
\end{array}\right.

(4)

where
G_{y}(y,u)
and
G_{u}(y,u)
are the Jacobian matrices of
R
with respect to
y

and
u
, respectively
. Plugging these expressions in (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\hat{J}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}J(y(u),u)+c_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\lambda(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)+c_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\lambda(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)-c_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\left[c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{\dagger}\nabla_{y}J(y(u),u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)-c_{u}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\left[c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{\dagger}G_{y}(y(u),u)^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G_{u}(y(u),u)-G_{y}(y(u),u)c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\dagger}c_{u}(y(u),u)\right]^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[G_{u}(y(u),u)-G_{y}(y(u),u)c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\dagger}c_{u}(y(u),u)\right]^{\mathrm{T}}R(y(u),u),

where
A^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse
.

According to this expression, we can identify the Jacobian of
\hat{R}
at
u
,
which we denote by
\hat{G}(u)
, as

\hat{G}(u):={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}G_{u}(y(u),u)-G_{y}(y(u),u)}c_{y}(y(u),u)^{\dagger}c_{u}(y(u),u).

(5)

Algorithm
1
summarizes the analysis below, and describes the
adjoint method to compute derivative information when the objective has a
least-squares structure.

Algorithm 1

Adjoint equations for implicitly constrained least squares

0:

Point of interest
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and vector
y(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}

such that
c(y(u),u)=0
.

1:

Solve the equation

c_{y}(y(u),u)\zeta(u)=-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}c_{u}(y(u),u)}

(6)

for
\zeta(u)
.

2:

Compute
\hat{G}(u)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}G_{u}(y(u),u)+G_{y}(y(u),u)}\zeta(u)
.

In our main algorithm as well as its implementation, we will rely on
Algorithm
1
to compute derivative information.

2.2
Algorithmic framework

Our optimization procedure is described in Algorithm
2
. This method
builds on the Levenberg-Marquardt paradigm
[
22
]
and
more generally on quadratic regularization techniques.

At every iteration, the method computes a tentative step by approximately
minimizing a quadratic model of the function. This step is then accepted or
rejected depending on whether it produces sufficient function reduction
compared to that predicted by the model. The
k
th iteration of
Algorithm
2
will be called
successful
if
u_{k+1}\neq u_{k}
,
and
unsuccessful
otherwise. The model is defined using a
quadratic regularization parameter, that is decreased on successful
iterations and increased on unsuccessful ones.

Algorithm 2

Regularization method for constrained least squares

0:

Initial iterate
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
; initial parameter
\gamma_{0}>0
;
minimum regularization parameter
0<\gamma_{\min}\leq\gamma_{0}
; step
acceptance threshold
\eta\in(0,1)
.

1:

Solve the constraint
c(y,u_{0})=0
for
y
to obtain
y(u_{0})
.

2:

Evaluate
R_{0}=\hat{R}(u_{0})
and call Algorithm
1

to compute
G_{0}=\hat{G}(u_{0})
.

3:

for

k=0,1,2,\dotsc

do

4:

Compute a step
s_{k}
as an approximate solution to the following
problem

\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}m_{k}(u_{k}+s):=\frac{1}{2}\|R_{k}\|^{2}+g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}s+\frac{1}{2}s^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s,

(7)

where
g_{k}=G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}
is the gradient of
\hat{J}
at
u_{k}
and

H_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric matrix.

5:

Solve the constraint
c(y,u_{k}+s_{k})=0
for
y
to obtain
y(u_{k}+s_{k})
.

6:

Compute the ratio of actual to predicted decrease in
f
defined as

\rho_{k}\leftarrow\frac{\hat{J}(u_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})}{m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})}.

7:

if

\rho_{k}\geq\eta

8:

Set
u_{k+1}\leftarrow u_{k}+s_{k}
and
\gamma_{k+1}\leftarrow\max\{0.5\gamma_{k},\gamma_{\min}\}

Solve the constraint

10:

Evaluate

.

11:

else

Set

13:

end

end

Note that our method can be instantiated in several ways, depending on the
way
H_{k}
is computed at every iteration, and on how the
subproblem (
7
) is solved. In this paper, we assume that
H_{k}

is built using only first-order derivative information, and consider two
specific cases. When
H_{k}
is the zero matrix, then the method can be viewed as
a gradient method. When
H_{k}=G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}
, the method is a regularized
Gauss-Newton iteration, similar to the Levenberg-Marquardt method. Although
we focus on the two aforementioned cases in this paper, we point out that
other formulae such as quasi-Newton updates
[
22
]

could also be used without the need for second-order information. As for the
subproblem solve, we provide guarantees for exact and inexact variants of our
framework in the next section.

3
Complexity analysis

In this section, we investigate the theoretical properties of
Algorithm
2
through the lens of complexity. More precisely, we are
interested in bounding the effort needed to reach a vector
u_{k}
such that

\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R(u_{k})}\|\leq\epsilon_{R}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\frac{\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{G}(u_{k})^{\mathrm{T}}R(u_{k})}\|}{\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R(u_{k})}\|}\leq\epsilon_{g}.

(8)

Condition (
8
) implicitly distinguishes between two kinds of
approximate stationary points. When possible, one would ideally compute a
point for which the residuals are small, or a stationary point for the norm of
the residual. This
scaled gradient
condition was previously used for
establishing complexity guarantees for algorithms applied to nonlinear
least-squares problems
[
9
,
11
,
17
]
.

Section
3.1
provides an iteration
complexity bound for all instances of the algorithm based on the
condition (
8
), assuming that all linear algebra operations are
performed in an exact fashion. We then consider inexact variants of our
algorithms, and derive the associated complexity results in
Section
3.2
.

3.1
Iteration complexity

We begin by a series of assumptions regarding the reduced
formulation (
2
).
These assumptions are classical in
complexity analysis on nonlinear optimization
algorithms
[
12
]
.

Assumption 3.1

The function
\hat{J}:u\mapsto J(y(u),u)
is continuously differentiable
in
u
. Moreover, the gradient of
\hat{J}
with respect to
u
is

L
-Lipschitz continuous for
L>0
.

Note that the first part of Assumption
3.1
is implied by
Assumption
2.1
.

Assumption 3.2

There exists a positive constant
M_{H}>0
such that

\|H_{k}\|\leq M_{H}
for all
k
.

Assumption
3.2
is trivially satisfied when
H_{k}
is the zero matrix,
or whenever the iterates are contained in a compact set. In addition to
boundedness, we make an additional requirement on that matrix.

Assumption 3.3

For any iteration
k
, the matrix
H_{k}
is chosen as a positive
semidefinite matrix.

Note that both the zero matrix and the Gauss-Newton matrix
G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}

are positive semidefinite, and thus satisfy Assumption
3.3
.

Lemma 3.1

Let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
hold.
Suppose that the subproblem (
7
) is solved exactly at
iteration
k
. Then,

s_{k}=-(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}

(9)

where
I
is the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Moreover,

m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}.

(10)

Proof.
Under Assumption
3.3
, the subproblem (
7
) is a
strongly convex quadratic subproblem. It thus possesses a unique global
minimum given by
-(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}
, which is precisely
(
9
). Using this formula for
s_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=}

\displaystyle-R_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}-\frac{1}{2}R_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}R_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I\|}.

By Assumption
3.2
, we have

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I\|}\leq\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}\leq M_{H}+\gamma_{k}.

Hence, we have

m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}},

as required.

\Box

Our second ingredient for a complexity proof consists in bounding the
value of the regularization parameter.

Lemma 3.2

Let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3

hold
, and consider the
k
th iteration of Algorithm
2
.
Suppose that
\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|>0
.
Then,

(i)

If
k
is the index of an unsuccessful iteration, then

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{k}<\underline{\gamma}:=\tfrac{L+\sqrt{L^{2}+4(1-\eta)L\,M_{H}}}{2(1-\eta)}.}

(ii)

For any iteration
k
,

\gamma_{k}\leq\gamma_{\max}:=\max\left\{1,\gamma_{0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2\underline{\gamma}}\right\}.

(11)

Proof.
Suppose that the
k
th iteration is unsuccessful, i.e. that
\rho_{k}<\eta
.
Then, one has

\eta(m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}))<\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k}).

(12)

Using Assumption
3.1
, a Taylor expansion of
\hat{J}
around
u_{k}

yields

\displaystyle\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nabla\hat{J}(u_{k})^{\mathrm{T}}s_{k}+\frac{L}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}s_{k}+\frac{L}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k})-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}H_{k}s_{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}}+\frac{L}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\frac{L-\gamma_{k}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}},

where the last inequality holds because of Assumption
3.3
.
Combining this inequality with (
12
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\eta(m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\eta(m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k})+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L-\gamma_{k}}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(1-\eta)(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L-\gamma_{k}}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}.

From Lemma
3.1
, we obtain both an expression for
s_{k}

and a bound on the left-hand side. Noting that

\|s_{k}\|\leq\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}\|\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|\leq\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|}{\gamma_{k}},

we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\eta)(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{L}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\frac{(1-\eta)}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L-\gamma_{k}}}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{k}^{2}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Leftrightarrow\frac{1-\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{L-\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{k}^{2}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Leftrightarrow(2-\eta)\gamma_{k}^{2}-(L-M_{H})\gamma_{k}-L\,M_{H}<0.}

Overall, we have shown that if the
k
th iteration is unsuccessful, then
necessarily
(2-\eta)\gamma_{k}^{2}-(L-M_{H})\gamma_{k}-L\,M_{H}<0
, which can
only occur as long as

\gamma_{k}<\underline{\gamma}:=\frac{L-M_{H}+\sqrt{(L-M_{H})^{2}+4(2-\eta)L\,M_{H}}}{2(2-\eta)}.

By a contraposition
argument, we then obtain that

\gamma_{k}\geq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\underline{\gamma}}

implies that the iteration is successful and that

\gamma_{k+1}\leq\gamma_{k}
. Combining this observation with the initial
value of
\gamma_{0}
and the update mechanism for
\gamma_{k}
, we find that

\gamma_{k}
can never exceed

\max\{\gamma_{0},2\underline{\gamma}\}\leq\gamma_{\max}
, proving the desired
result.

\Box

Note that we choose
\gamma_{\max}
to be greater than or equal to
1

in order to simplify our bounds later on, but that the analysis below extends
to the choice
\gamma_{\max}=\max\{\gamma_{0},2\underline{\gamma}\}
.

8
). Then,

\left|S_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}\right|\quad\leq\quad\left\lceil\mathcal{C}_{{\cal S}}\log(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\epsilon_{R}^{-2})\epsilon_{R}^{-2}\right\rceil+1,

(13)

where
\mathcal{C}_{{\cal S}}=\tfrac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}{\eta}
.

Proof.
Let
k\in{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}
. By definition, the corresponding iterate

u_{k}
satisfies

\|R_{k}\|\geq\epsilon_{R}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|}{\|R_{k}\|}\geq\epsilon_{g}.

(14)

Moreover, since
k
corresponds to a successful iteration, we have

\rho_{k}\geq\eta
, i.e.

\hat{J}(u_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k+1})\geq\eta\left(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})\right)\geq\eta\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{k})}\geq\eta\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{\max})},

where we used the results of Lemmas
3.1

and
3.2
to bound the model decrease and
\gamma_{k}
,
respectively. Combining the last inequality with (
14
) leads
to

\displaystyle\hat{J}(u_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{\max})}\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{\max})}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{\|R_{k}\|^{2}}\|R_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{\max})}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\|R_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\hat{J}(u_{k}),

where the last line follows by definition of
\hat{J}(u_{k})
. Since

\tfrac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\in(0,1)
by definition of all
quantities involved, we obtain that

\left(1-\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)\hat{J}(u_{k})\geq\hat{J}(u_{k+1}).

(15)

Let now
{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{k}:=\{\ell<k|\ell\in{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}\}
.
Recalling that the iterate only changes on successful iterations and that
the function
\hat{J}
is bounded below by
0
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)^{\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{k}\right|}\hat{J}(u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\hat{J}(u_{k})

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)^{\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{k}\right|}\hat{J}(u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{R}^{2},

where the last line uses
k\in{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}
. Taking logarithms and re-arranging,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{k}\right|\ln\left(1-\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ln\left(\epsilon_{R}^{2}/(2\hat{J}(u_{0}))\right)

\displaystyle\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\left(\epsilon_{R}^{2}/(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\right)}{\ln\left(1-\frac{\eta}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ln\left(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\epsilon_{R}^{-2}\right)\frac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}{\eta}\epsilon_{g}^{-2},

where the last inequality comes from
-\ln(1-t)\geq t
for any
t\in(0,1)
.
As a result, we obtain that

\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}\right|\leq 1+\ln\left(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\epsilon_{R}^{-2}\right)\frac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}{\eta}\epsilon_{g}^{-2},

where the additional
1
accounts for the largest iteration in
{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}
.

\Box

Lemma 3.4

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
, let
{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}

be the set of unsuccessful iterations for which (
8
) does not hold. Then,

\left|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}\right|\leq\left\lceil 1+\log_{2}\left(\gamma_{\max}\right)\right\rceil\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}\right|.

(16)

Proof.
The proof tracks that
of
[
13
, Lemma 2.5]
for
the trust-region case. Between two successful iterations, the value
of
\gamma_{k}
only increases by factors of
2
. Combining this observation
with the fact that
\gamma_{k}\leq\gamma_{\max}
per Lemma
3.2

and accounting for the first successful iteration leads to the final
result.

\Box

Combining Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
finally
yields our main complexity result.

Theorem 3.1

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
, the number
of successful iterations (and Jacobian evaluations)
before reaching an iterate satisfying (
8
) satisfies

|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|={\cal O}\left(\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right)

(17)

and the total number of iterations (and residual evaluations)
before reaching such an iterate satisfies

|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|+|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|={\cal O}\left(\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right).

(18)

The result of Theorem
3.1
improves over that obtained by
Bergou et al
[
9
]
in a more
general setting, and is consistent with that in Gould et
al
[
17
]
, where a series of results with
vanishing dependencies in
\epsilon_{R}
were established. Compared to those
latter results, our bounds (
17
)
and (
18
) have logarithmic dependency on
\epsilon_{R}
but
do not involve increasingly larger constants.

To end this section, we provide a result tailored to our implicit
constrained setup, that accounts for the operations that are performed
throughout the course of the algorithm.

Corollary 3.1

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, suppose further
than Assumption
2.1
holds and that Jacobian evaluations are
performed using Algorithm

. Then, the number of
solves of the implicit constraint for
y
is

1+|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|+|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|={\cal O}\left(\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right),

(19)

while the number of adjoint solves (using (
6
))
is

1+|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}|={\cal O}\left(\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right).

(20)

3.2
Inexact variants

We now consider solving the subproblem (
9
) in an inexact
fashion. Such a procedure is classical in large-scale optimization, and is
primarily relevant whenever
H_{k}
is not chosen as a constant matrix.

Assumption 3.4

For any iteration
k
, the step
s_{k}
is chosen so as to satisfy

(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s_{k}=-g_{k}+t_{k},\quad\|t_{k}\|\leq\theta\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}}\|g_{k}\|

(21)

for
\theta\in[0,1)
.

Assuming that the linear system is solved to the accuracy expressed in
condition (
21
), one can establish the following result.

Lemma 3.5

Let Assumptions
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
hold.
For any iteration
k
, the step
s_{k}
satisfies

\|s_{k}\|\leq\frac{(1+\theta)\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|}{\gamma_{k}}

(22)

and

m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})\geq\frac{1-\theta^{2}}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}.

(23)

Proof.
Using Assumption
3.4
gives

\|t_{k}\|\leq\theta\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}}\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|\leq\theta\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|.

Since
s_{k}=(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}(-g_{k}+t_{k})
by construction, we obtain

\displaystyle\|s_{k}\|=\left\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}(-g_{k}+t_{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|g_{k}\|+\|t_{k}\|}{\|H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I\|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\theta)\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\theta)\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|}{\gamma_{k}},

proving (
22
).

We now use this inequality together with the definition of
s_{k}
to bound the
model decrease:

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}(-g_{k}+t_{k})-\frac{1}{2}(-g_{k}+t_{k})^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}(-g_{k}+t_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}g_{k}-\frac{1}{2}t_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}t_{k}.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain on one hand

g_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}g_{k}\geq\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I\|}\geq\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}},

while on the other hand

t_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}t_{k}\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)^{-1}\|}\|t_{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|t_{k}\|^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\leq\frac{\theta^{2}\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}},

where the last inequality comes from Assumption
3.4
.
As a result, we arrive at

\displaystyle m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\theta^{2}}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1-\theta^{2}}{2}\frac{\|g_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}},

using Assumption
3.2
to bound
\|H_{k}\|
. This
proves (
23
).

\Box

Similarly to the exact case, we now prove that the regularization parameter
is bounded from above.

Lemma 3.6

Let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
and

3.4
hold.
Consider the
k
th iteration of
Algorithm
2
, and suppose that
\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|>0
.
Then,

(i)

If
k
is the index of an unsuccessful iteration, then

\gamma_{k}<\tfrac{L(1+\theta)^{2}}{(1-\eta)(1-\theta^{2})}
.

(ii)

For any iteration
k
,

\gamma_{k}\leq\gamma_{\max}^{in}:=\max\left\{1,\gamma_{0},\frac{L(1+\theta)^{2}}{(1-\eta)(1-\theta^{2})}\right\}.

(24)

Proof.
By the same reasoning as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, we
know that for any unsuccessful iteration, we have

(1-\eta)(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k}))<\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L-\gamma_{k}}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}.

(25)

Using now the properties (
22
) and (
23
)
in (
25
), we obtain:

\displaystyle(1-\eta)(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k}))

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L-\gamma_{k}}}{2}\|s_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftarrow\frac{(1-\eta)(1-\theta^{2})}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(L-\gamma_{k})}}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{k}^{2}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Leftrightarrow\frac{(1-\eta)(1-\theta^{2})}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}<}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}(L-\gamma_{k})}{\gamma_{k}^{2}}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Leftrightarrow(2(1+\theta)-\eta)\gamma_{k}^{2}-\left[(1+\theta)^{2}L-M_{H}\right]\gamma_{k}-(1+\theta)^{2}L}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}<}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0}.

Overall, we have shown that if the
k
th iteration is unsuccessful, then
necessarily

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\gamma_{k}<\underline{\gamma}^{in}:=\frac{(1+\theta)^{2}L-M_{H}+\sqrt{((1+\theta)^{2}L-M_{H})^{2}+4(1+\theta)^{2}(2(1+\theta)-\eta)L}}{2(2(1+\theta)-\eta)}.}

Using
the updating rules on
\gamma_{k}
and accounting for
\gamma_{0}

we obtain that
\gamma_{k}\leq\max\left\{\gamma_{0},\underline{\gamma}^{in}\right\}\leq\gamma_{\max}^{in}

for all
k
, proving the desired result.

\Box

We can now state an iteration complexity result for the inexact variant.

Lemma 3.7

Let Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
and

3.4
hold. Let

\epsilon_{g}\in(0,1)
, and let
{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}
denote the set of successful
iterations for which
u_{k}
does not satisfy (
8
). Then,

\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}\right|\quad\leq\quad\left\lceil\mathcal{C}_{{\cal S}}^{in}\log(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\,\epsilon_{R}^{-2})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right\rceil+1,

(26)

where
\mathcal{C}_{{\cal S}}^{in}=\tfrac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}
.

Proof.
Let
k\in{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}
. By definition, the
k
th iteration
is successful, and we have per Lemma
3.6

\hat{J}(u_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})\geq\eta\left(m_{k}(u_{k})-m_{k}(u_{k}+s_{k})\right)\geq\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{k}}\geq\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}},

where the last inequality is a consequence of Lemma
3.6
.
In addition, the corresponding iterate
u_{k}
satisfies (
14
),
leading to

\displaystyle\hat{J}(u_{k})-\hat{J}(u_{k}+s_{k})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{2}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{2(M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in})}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{\|R_{k}\|^{2}}\|R_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}\frac{\|G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}R_{k}\|^{2}}{\|R_{k}\|^{2}}J(u_{k})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\hat{J}(u_{k}).

Using that
\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}<1
then
leads to

\left(1-\frac{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}\epsilon_{g}^{2}\right)\hat{J}(u_{k})\geq\hat{J}(u_{k+1}).

(27)

By proceeding as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
and
using (
27
) in lieu of (
27
),
one establishes that

\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}\right|\leq 1+\ln\left(2\hat{J}(u_{0})\epsilon_{R}^{-2}\right)\frac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}}{\eta(1-\theta^{2})}\epsilon_{g}^{-2},

proving the desired result.

\Box

To connect the number of unsuccessful iterations with that of successful
iterations, we use the same argument as in the exact case by replacing
the bound (
11
) with (
24
).

Lemma 3.8

Under the assumptions of Lemma
3.7
, let

{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}
be the set of unsuccessful iterations for which
(
8
) does not hold. Then,

\left|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}\right|\leq\left\lceil 1+\log_{2}\left(\gamma_{\max}^{in}\right)\right\rceil\left|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}\right|.

(28)

Our next theorem gives the total iteration complexity result by
combining Lemmas
3.7
and
3.8
.

Theorem 3.2

Under Assumptions
3.1
,
3.2
,
3.3
and

3.4
, the number
of successful iterations (and inexact step calculations)
before reaching an iterate satisfying (
8
) satisfies

|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right)

(29)

and the total number of iterations (and residual evaluations)
before reaching such an iterate satisfies

|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|+|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right).

(30)

The results of Theorem
3.2
match that of
Theorem
3.1
in terms of dependencies on

\epsilon_{g}
and
\epsilon_{R}
. To emphasize the use of inexact steps, we
highlighted the dependency with respect to the inexact tolerance

\theta
. As expected, one notes that this dependency vanishes when

\theta=0
(i.e. when we consider exact steps as in
Section
3.1
), and that the complexity bounds worsen
as
\theta
gets closer to
1
. A similar observation holds for
the results in the next corollary, that is a counterpart to
Corollary
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
as well as
Assumption
2.1
, the number of
solves for
y
is

1+|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|+|{\cal U}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|={\cal O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\theta^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}\,\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right),

(31)

while the number of adjoint solves (using Algorithm
1
)
is

1+|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|={\cal O}\left(\frac{1}{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}\,\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right).

(32)

In addition to the previous results, we can also exploit the inexact nature of
the steps to provide more precise guarantees on the computation cost of an iteration. More
precisely, suppose that we apply an iterative solver to the system

(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s=-g_{k}
in order to find an approximate solution
satisfying Assumption
3.4
. In particular, one can resort to
iterative linear algebra techniques such as Conjugate Gradient (CG), and obtain
guarantees on the number of matrix-vector products
necessary to reach the desired accuracy
[
22
]
.
A result tailored to our setting is presented below.

Proposition 3.1

Let Assumption
3.3
hold. Suppose that we
apply conjugate gradient (CG) to the linear system
(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s=-g_{k}
,
where
g_{k},H_{k},\gamma_{k}
are obtained from the
k
th iteration of
Algorithm
2
. Then, the conjugate gradient method computes an
iterate satisfying (
21
) after at most

\min\left\{n,\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}\log\left(\frac{2\kappa_{k}}{\theta}\right)\right\}

(33)

iterations or, equivalently, matrix-vector products, where

\kappa_{k}=\tfrac{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}
.

Proof.
Let
s^{(q)}
be the iterate obtained after applying
q
iterations
of conjugate gradient to
(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s=-g_{k}
. If
q=n
, then
necessarily the linear system has been solved exactly and (
21
)
is trivially satisfied. Thus we assume in what follows that
q<n
.

Standard CG theory gives
[
24
, Proof of Lemma 11]
:

\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s^{(q)}+g_{k}\|\leq 2\sqrt{c_{k}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{c_{k}}-1}{\sqrt{c_{k}}+1}\right)^{q}\|g_{k}\|,

(34)

where
c_{k}
is the condition number of
H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I
. Noticing that

c_{k}\leq\kappa_{k}
, we see that (
34
) implies

\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s^{(q)}+g_{k}\|\leq 2\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}+1}\right)^{q}\|g_{k}\|.

(35)

Suppose now that
s^{(q)}
does not satisfy (
21
). Then,

\|(H_{k}+\gamma_{k}I)s^{(q)}+g_{k}\|\geq\theta\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\|H_{k}\|+\gamma_{k}}}\|g_{k}\|=\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}}\|g_{k}\|.

(36)

Combining (
35
) and (
36
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}}\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}+1}\right)^{q}\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\frac{\theta}{2{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\kappa_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}+1}\right)^{q}.

Taking logarithms and rearranging, we arrive at

q\leq\frac{\ln(\theta/(2\kappa_{k}))}{\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}+1}\right)}\leq\frac{\ln(2\kappa_{k}/\theta)}{\ln\left(1+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}-1}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa_{k}}\ln\left(\frac{2\kappa_{k}}{\theta}\right),

(37)

where the last inequality used
\ln(1+\tfrac{1}{t})\geq\tfrac{1}{t+1/2}
.
Combining (
37
) with the fact that
q\leq n
yields our
desired bound.

\Box

Using the bounds on
\gamma_{k}
and
\|H_{k}\|
from our complexity analysis, we
see that the value (
33
) can be bounded from above by

\min\left\{n,\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\log\left(\frac{2\kappa}{\theta}\right)\right\},

(38)

with
\kappa=\tfrac{M_{H}+\gamma_{\max}^{in}}{\gamma_{\min}}
.
Using (
38
) in conjunction with the complexity bound of
Theorem
3.2
, we derive the following bound on the
number of matrix-vector products.

Corollary 3.3

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
, suppose that
we apply conjugate gradient to compute inexact steps in
Algorithm
2
. Then, the algorithm reaches a point
satisfying (
8
) in at most

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\min\left\{n,\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\kappa}\log\left(\frac{2\kappa}{\theta}\right)\right\}\times\left(1+|{\cal S}_{\epsilon_{g},\epsilon_{R}}^{in}|\right)\\
=&{\cal O}\left(\min\left\{n,\sqrt{\kappa}\log\left(\frac{\kappa}{\theta}\right)\right\}\,\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\theta^{2}}\right)\frac{1}{(1-\theta^{2})^{2}}\,\log(\epsilon_{R}^{-1})\epsilon_{g}^{-2}\right)\end{array}

(39)

matrix-vector products.

As a final note, we point out that there exist variants of the conjugate
gradient method that take advantage of a Gauss-Newton approximation

H_{k}=G_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}G_{k}

[
23
,
22
]
,
and require two Jacobian-vector products per iteration instead of a full
matrix-vector product with
H_{k}
. The corresponding complexity
bound is worse than that of Corollary
3.3
by a factor of
2
,
but the cost of Jacobian-vector products can be much lower than that of
matrix-vector products. Our implementation from the next section relies on this
approach.

4
Numerical illustration

In this section, we illustrate the performance of several instances of our
framework on classical PDE-constrained optimization problems. Our goal is
primarily to investigate the practical relevance of using
condition (
8
) as a stopping criterion.

For this reason, we are mainly interested in the evaluation and
iteration cost of our algorithm, and therefore report those statistics in the
rest of the section.

We implemented an inexact Gauss-Newton method based on
Algorithm
2
in MATLAB R2023a. The method uses
\eta=0.1
,

\gamma_{\min}=10^{-10}
and

\gamma_{0}=\max\{1,\|g_{0}\|,\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}+1\}
. Steps are computed inexactly
using the conjugate gradient method so as to satisfy
condition (
21
) from Assumption
3.4
. We compare
five variants of our algorithm corresponding to

\theta\in\{0,\ 10^{-6},\ 10^{-4},\ 10^{-2},\ 10^{-1},5.10^{-1}\}.

The case
\theta=0

4.1
Elliptic PDE-constrained problem

We first consider a standard elliptic optimal control problem, where the
control is chosen so that the temperature distribution (the state) matches
a desired distribution as closely as possible
[
15
,
26
]
. The
resulting problem can be written as

\min_{y,u}J(y,u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left(y(u(x))-z(x)\right)^{2}+\lambda u(x)^{2}\right]\,dx,

(40)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{subject\ to\ }\,\,\,\quad-\nabla\cdot(a(x)\nabla y(x))

\displaystyle=u(x),

\displaystyle\;\;\;\mbox{in}\;\;\mathcal{D},

(41)

\displaystyle y(x)

\displaystyle={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f},

\displaystyle\;\;\;\mbox{on}\;\;\partial\mathcal{D},

where the desired state
z
, the regularization parameter
\lambda>0
,
and the function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D})
are all given.
We set
\mathcal{D}:=[0,1]^{2}
,
\lambda=0.001
and

a(x)\equiv 1,\;\forall x\in\mathcal{D}
.

By discretizing (
40
) and (
41
) using piecewise linear finite
elements on a triangular grid, we arrive at the discretized formulation

\begin{array}[]{ll}\min_{{\bf y},{\bf u}}&\frac{1}{2}({\bf y}-{\bf z})^{T}M({\bf y}-{\bf z})+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\bf u}^{T}M{\bf u},\\
&\\
\mbox{subject\ to}&K{\bf y}=M{\bf u}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf f}},\end{array}

where the vectors
{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u}
denote the discrete forms of
the state, the desired state, respectively, and the control variables.

The matrices
K
and
M
correspond to the stiffness matrix and mass
matrix, respectively, while the vector
{\bf{f}}
corresponds to the Dirichlet
boundary conditions, respectively
[
15
, Chapter 5]
. Note that, in this
example, we have set
f\equiv 0
.

The
cost function (
4.1
)
can be written as
\frac{1}{2}\|R({\bf y},{\bf u})\|^{2}

R({\bf y},{\bf u})=\begin{bmatrix}M^{1/2}({\bf y}-{\bf z})\\
\sqrt{\beta}M^{1/2}{\bf u}\end{bmatrix},

(42)

fitting our formulation of interest (
1
).

this approach and apply Algorithm
2
to the

[

4.2
Burgers’ equation

We now describe our second test problem based on Burgers’ equation, a
simplified model for turbulence
[
6
,
14
,
27
,
18
]
. Control problems of this form are
often considered as the most fundamental nonlinear problem to handle. In our
case, they illustrate the performance of our algorithms in a
nonlinear, implicitly constrained setting.

Our formulation is as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\min_{y,u}J(y,u):=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\left[\left(y(t,x)-z(t,x)\right)^{2}+\omega u(t,x)^{2}\right]\,dt\,dx&\\
\operatorname*{subject\ to\ }&y_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\left(y^{2}+\nu y_{x}\right)_{x}=f+u&(x,t)\in(0,L)\times(0,T)\\
&y(t,0)=y(t,L)=0&t\in(0,T)\\
&y(0,x)=y_{0}(x)&x\in(0,L).\end{array}\right.

(43)

Here
L
and
T
are space and time horizons, respectively;

u:[0,T]\times[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the control of our optimization
problem;
y:[0,T]\times[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the state;

z:[0,T]\times[0,L]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the desired state;
\omega>0

is a regularization parameter;
f
is a source term, and
\nu
is
the viscosity parameter.

Given
u
,
y
can be computed by solving the PDE

\begin{array}[]{lll}y_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\left(y^{2}+\nu y_{x}\right)_{x}&=f+u&(x,t)\in(0,L)\times(0,T)\\
y(t,0)=y(t,L)&=0&t\in(0,T)\\
y(0,x)&=y_{0}(x)&x\in(0,L).\end{array}

(44)

We discretize (
44
) in time by applying the backward Euler scheme to
Burgers’ equation and a rectangle rule for the discretization of the objective
function, while the spatial variable is approximated by piecewise linear
finite elements. As a result, we obtain the following discretized version of
problem (
43
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\minimize_{u_{0},\dots,u_{N_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}}}&J(y_{0},\dots,y_{N_{t}},u_{0},\dots,u_{N_{t}})\\
\operatorname*{subject\ to\ }&c_{i+1}(y_{i},y_{i+1},u_{i+1};\nu)=0,\quad i=0,\dots,N_{t}-1,\\
\end{array}\right.

(45)

where

J(y_{0},\dots,y_{N_{t}},u_{0},\dots,u_{N_{t}}):=\delta_{t}\sum_{i=0}^{N_{t}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}(y_{i}-z)^{\mathrm{T}}M(y_{i}-z)+\tfrac{\omega}{2}u_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}Mu_{i}\right)

(46)

and

c_{i+1}(y_{i},y_{i+1},u_{i+1};\nu)=\frac{1}{\delta_{t}}My_{i+1}-\frac{1}{\delta_{t}}My_{i}+\frac{1}{2}By_{i+1}\odot y_{i+1}+\nu Cy_{i+1}-f-Mu_{i+1},

(47)

and
\odot
denotes the entrywise product.
In those equations,
\delta_{t}=\frac{T}{N_{t}}
represents the time step of
the discretization, while
M,B,C,\{f_{i}\}
are discretized versions of the
operators and the source term arising from the continuous formulation. More
precisely, we have

M=\frac{h}{6}\begin{bmatrix}4&1&&&\\
1&4&1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&1&4&1\\
&&&1&4\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0&1/2&&&\\
-1/2&0&1/2&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&-1/2&0&1/2\\
&&&-1/2&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}}

and

C=\frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}2&-1&&&\\
-1&2&-1&&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&-1&2&-1\\
&&&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}\times N_{x}},\quad f=\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}\\
\vdots\\
f_{N_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{x}},

with
h=\tfrac{L}{N_{x}}
being the space discretization step.
Following previous work
[
6
,
18
]
, we
assume that the desired state
z
does not depend on time.

To reduce the effects of boundary layers, we discretize Burgers’ equation
using continuous piecewise linear finite elements built on a piecewise uniform
mesh. We then solve the resulting discretized nonlinear PDE at each time step
using Newton’s method
[
6
]
.

Letting
{\bf u}
(resp.
{\bf y}
) as the concatenation of
u_{0},\dots,u_{N_{t}}

(resp.
y_{0},\dots,y_{N_{t}}
), one observes that the objective function can be
written as
\frac{1}{2}\|R({\bf y},{\bf u})\|^{2}
with

R({\bf y},{\bf u})=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\delta_{t}}M^{1/2}(y_{0}-z)\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{\delta_{t}}M^{1/2}(y_{N_{t}}-z)\\
\sqrt{\omega\delta_{t}}M^{1/2}u_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{\omega\delta_{t}}M^{1/2}u_{N_{t}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2(N_{t}+1)N_{x}}.

(48)

Volkwein
[

,

, and

. We set

with the first
N_{x}/2
coefficients equal to

1
and the others equal to
0
, while the initial control
u_{0}
is set to
the zero vector.
The resulting problem has large residuals.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a regularization method for least-squares problems
subject to implicit constraints, for which we derived complexity guarantees
that improve over recent bounds derived in the absence of constraints. To this
end, we leveraged a recently proposed convergence criterion that is
particularly useful when the optimal solution corresponds to a nonzero objective
value. Numerical testing conducted on PDE-constrained optimization problems
showed that the criterion used to derive our complexity bounds bears a
practical significance.

Our results can be extended in a number of
research directions. Although
we focused on deriving complexity bounds with objective and derivative
evaluations as cost units, we believe that those bounds can be combined with
complexity results on algebraic operations, in order to fully reflect the
numerical cost of our framework. In addition, our study assumes access to
deterministic evaluations
. We plan on extending our framework to account for
uncertainty in either the objective or the constraints, so as to handle a
broader range of problems.
