On a question of Luca and Schinzel over Segal-Piatetski-Shapiro sequences
Jean-Marc Deshouillers * , Mehdi Hassani and Mohammad Nasiri-Zare

Abstract
. We extend to Segal-Piatetski-Shapiro sequences previous results on the Luca-Schinzel question. Namely, we prove that for any real c c larger than 1 1 the sequence ( ∑ m ≤ n φ ⁡ ( ⌊ m c ⌋ ) / ⌊ m c ⌋ ) n (\sum_{m\leq n}\varphi(\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor)/\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor)_{n} is dense modulo 1 1 , where φ \varphi denotes Euler’s totient function. The main part of the proof consists in showing that when R R is a large integer, the sequence of the residues of ⌊ m c ⌋ \lfloor m^{c}\rfloor modulo R R contains blocks of consecutive values which are in an arithmetic progression.

1.
Introduction

At the Czech-Slovak Number Theory Conference in Smolenice in August 2007, F. Luca asked whether the sequences of arithmetic and geometric means of the first values of the Euler totient function are uniformly distributed modulo 1; A. Schinzel asked whether the weaker statement that those sequences are dense modulo 1 was known; this question was positively answered in
[
6
]
. This opened the way to extensions considering different multiplicative functions with constant mean value, or mean values over different sequences of integers as well as the original question of Luca in special cases (cf. the bibliography). One of the latest results is that of the first and third named authors of this paper

Theorem 1

(Theorem 1 of
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\varphi
denote the Euler function and
G
be a non constant polynomial with integral coefficients and taking positive values at positive arguments. The sequence

\left(\sum_{m\leq n}\frac{\varphi(G(m))}{G(m)}\right)_{n\geq 1}

is dense modulo 1.

Here, we extend this result to Segal-Piatetski-Shapiro sequences.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varphi
denote the Euler function and
c
be a real number number larger than
1
. The sequence

\left(\sum_{m\leq n}\frac{\varphi(\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor)}{\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor}\right)_{n\geq 1}

is dense modulo 1.

The case when
c
is an integer is but a special case of Theorem
1
. In this paper, we only consider the case when
c>1
is not an integer, i.e. the case of Segal - Piatetski-Shapiro sequences.

A way to tackle the Luca-Schinzel question on a sequence
(a(n))_{n}
is to obtain the arithmetical independence of some consecutive values of the sequence
(a(m))_{m}
so that for each given
\varepsilon
, one can find
H
consecutive values of the sequence
((\varphi(a(m+h))/a(m+h))_{m}
which are all less than
\varepsilon
but the sum of which is larger than
1
. A natural idea would be to expect a complete arithmetical independence of the sequence
(\lfloor(m+1)^{c}\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor(m+H)^{c}\rfloor))_{m}
. This is not the case: it is proved in
[
1
]
that the sequence of the residues of
\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor
modulo
k
is normal for no
k\geq 2
. It is even shown that in the sequence of the residues of
\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor
modulo
k
, there are finite blocks of consecutive values which do not occur.

In Section
3
, we are however going to prove that
\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor
can be locally approximated by a linear polynomial, more precisely, denoting by
\|c\|
the so-called ”distance to the nearest integer” of the real number
c
, we have

Theorem 3
.

Let
\beta=2^{(c+2)}(c+2)^{2}/\|c\|
and let
H
be a positive integer. If
R
is sufficiently large an integer, for any residue
r
modulo
R
, there exists
m\leq R^{\beta}
such that

(1)

\forall h\in[1,H]\colon\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\equiv r+h\;(\operatorname{mod}R).

In connection with
[
1
]
, we notice that this result implies that for any positive integer
H
and any non integral
c
larger than
1
, if
R
is large enough, the sequence of the residues of
\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor
modulo
R
contains the block
(1,2,\ldots,H)
.

We show how to use this result for proving Theorem
2
in Section
4
.

2.2.
The Erdős-Turán-Koksma-Szüsz inequality

This inequality permits to give an upper bound for the discrepancy of a sequence of elements in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
in terms of finitely many trigonometrical sums. The case when
s=1
has been given by Erdős and Turán in
[
10
]
and generalized to higher dimensions independently by Koksma
[
12
]
and Szüsz
[
14
]
. We quote here the formulation given by Drmota and Tichy
[
8
]
. We first recall the definition of the discrepancy.

An
interval

I
in
[0,1)^{s}
is a cartesian product
\prod_{1\leq i\leq s}[a_{i},b_{i})
, with
0\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}\leq 1
for
1\leq i\leq s
; its Lebesgue measure
\prod_{1\leq i\leq s}(b_{i}-a_{i})
is denoted by
\lambda(I)
. For an interval
I\subset[0,1)^{s}
, we denote by
\chi_{I}
its indicator (also called characteristic) function.

The
discrepancy
of a finite set
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\}
of elements of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined by

(3)

D_{N}(X)=D_{N}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})=\sup_{I\subset[0,1)^{s}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\chi_{I}(\{x_{n}\})-\lambda(I)\right|.

Lemma 1

(Multidimensional Erdős-Turán-Koksma-Szüsz inequality)
.

Let
s\geq 1
,
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\}
be a finite set of elements of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
K
an arbitrary positive integer. We have

(4)

D_{N}(X)\leq\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{s}\left(\frac{2}{K+1}+\sum_{0<\|k\|_{\infty}\leq K}\frac{1}{r(k)}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\e(k\cdot x_{n})\right|\right),

where, for
k=(k_{1},k_{2},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, we let
r(k)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\max\{1,|k_{i}|\}
and
u\cdot v
denote the usual scalar product of two elements
u
and
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
.

2.3.
Upper bound for some trigonometrical sums

The following lemma provides us with an upper bound for the trigonometric sums useful in the application of the ETKSz inequality. The exponent in (
5
) is not very strong, but the uniformity in the statement makes it very convenient for our purpose. It is a rather straightforward consequence of the van der Corput inequalities.

Lemma 2
.

Let
c
be a real number in
(1,\infty)\backslash\mathbb{N}
.
There exist two constants
A
and
B
such that for any
N
larger than
A
and any non-zero
(\lfloor c\rfloor+1)
-tuple
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{\lfloor c\rfloor})
satisfying

(5)

\forall\ell\in[0,\lfloor c\rfloor]\colon\text{ either }a_{\ell}=0\text{ or }N^{-\|c\|/2}\leq|a_{\ell}|\leq N^{\|c\|/2},

one has

S:=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}\operatorname{\e}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor c\rfloor}a_{\ell}n^{c-\ell}\right)\right|\leq BN^{1-\theta},

where
\theta=2^{-(c+2)}\|c\|
.

Proof.

We start with the case when all the coefficients are
0
except
a_{\lfloor c\rfloor}
. In this case, we use Kusmin-Landau inequality (cf. Theorem 2.1 of
[
11
]
). If
N
is large enough, we have, on
(N,2N]
,
\|\frac{d}{dt}(a_{\lfloor c\rfloor}t^{\{c\}})\|\gg N^{-\|c\|/2+\{c\}-1}
and thus
S\ll N^{1+\|c\|/2-\{c\}}\ll N^{1-\|c\|/2}\leq N^{1-\theta}
.

For the other cases, we use the following lemma, due to van der Corput, which one can find as a combination of Theorem 2.2 (for
q=0
) and Theorem 2.8 (for
q\geq 1
) of
[
11
]
.

Lemma 3
.

There exists an absolute constant
C
satisfying the following. Let
q
be a non-negative integer,
f
a real valued function defined on
[N,2N]
, with
q+2
continuous derivatives satisfying

\lambda\leq|f^{(q+2)}(t)|\leq\alpha\lambda,

for some
\lambda>0
and some
\alpha\geq 1
. Then, one has

(6)

\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}\e(f(n))\leq C\left(N(\alpha^{2}\lambda)^{\frac{1}{4Q-2}}+N^{1-\frac{1}{2Q}}\alpha^{\frac{1}{2Q}}+N^{(\frac{Q-1}{Q})^{2}}\lambda^{\frac{-1}{2Q}}\right),

where
Q=2^{q}
.

Let
\ell_{0}
be the smallest integer
\ell
such that
a_{\ell}
is non zero. The function of interest is thus
f(n)=\sum_{\ell=\ell_{0}}^{\lfloor c\rfloor}a_{\ell}n^{c-\ell}
. Since the non zero coefficients
a_{\ell}
, including
a_{\ell_{0}}
are small powers of
N
, the term
a_{\ell_{0}}n^{c-\ell_{0}}
dominates
f
and its derivatives dominate the derivatives of
f
. In the application of Lemma
3
, we take
q=\lfloor c\rfloor-\ell_{0}-1
; we first notice that
q
is non-negative (we already treated the case
\ell_{0}=\lfloor c\rfloor
); we also notice that
q\leq\lfloor c\rfloor-1\leq c-1
so that
1\leq Q\leq 2^{c-1}
. Furthermore,
\alpha
depends only of
c
, and (since our constants may depend on
c
), it plays no role in our application of (
6
); moreover
\lambda
is of the order
|a_{\ell_{0}}|N^{\{c\}-1}
, so that

\lambda\ll_{c}N^{\|c\|/2+\{c\}-1}\ll_{c}N^{-\|c\|/2}\text{ and }\lambda^{-1}\ll_{c}N^{\|c\|/2+1-\{c\}}\ll_{c}N^{3\|c\|/2}.

By (
6
), we have

S\ll_{c}N^{1-\frac{\|c\|}{8Q-4}}+N^{1-\frac{2}{Q}}+N^{(\frac{Q-1}{Q})^{2}+\frac{3\|c\|}{4Q}}.

We easily notice that each of the three terms in RHS of the last relation is less than
N^{1-\theta}
.
∎

2.4.
Contribution of large primes to
\varphi(n)/n

Lemma 4
.

Let
\alpha>0
. For any
C
in
(0,\min(\alpha,1))
there exists
N
such that for

, one has

(7)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\\
p\geq(\log n)^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\geq C.

Proof.

This is a simple consequence of classical results on the distribution of prime numbers. Explicit value for
N
in terms of
\alpha
and
C
, which is not relevant here, can be obtained from the evaluations given in
[
9
]
.

Since the LHS of (

) is an increasing function of
\alpha
, it enough to consider the case when

Let

be sufficiently large an integer. We denote by

the consecutive prime numbers such that

(8)

p_{0}<\log^{\alpha}n\leq p_{1}<\cdots<p_{r+1}\text{ and }p_{1}\cdots p_{r}\leq n<p_{1}\cdots p_{r+1}.

By a direct lower bound and by Mertens Theorem, for

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|n\\

From the first part of (

p_{0}=(1+o(1))\log^{\alpha}n

\log n=(1+o(1))(\theta(p_{r})-\theta(p_{0}))

and so we have

\log p_{0}/\log p_{r}=(1+o(1))\alpha\log\log n/\log\log n=\alpha+o(1).

Thus, for any

∎

2.5.
Elementary relations concerning integral and fractional parts

For convenience, we recall here some elementary relations.

Let
\ell
denote a non-negative integer and
x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}
real numbers. We have

(9)

\lfloor\ell x\rfloor\geq\ell\lfloor x\rfloor\;\text{ and }\;\{\ell x\}\leq\ell\{x\},

(10)

\text{if }\{x_{1}\}+\cdots+\{x_{\ell}\}<1,\;\text{ then }\;\lfloor x_{1}+\cdots+x_{\ell}\rfloor=\lfloor x_{1}\rfloor\cdots+\lfloor x_{\ell}\rfloor,

(11)

\text{if }\;\ell\{x\}<1,\;\text{ then }\;\lfloor\ell x\rfloor=\ell\lfloor x\rfloor.

Proof of those relations.

We multiply
x=\lfloor x\rfloor+\{x\}
by
\ell
, which leads to

\displaystyle\ell x=\,

\displaystyle\ell\lfloor x\rfloor+\ell\{x\}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\ell\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor\ell\{x\}\rfloor+\{\ell\{x\}\}

which leads in turn to
\lfloor\ell x\rfloor=\ell\lfloor x\rfloor+\lfloor\ell\{x\}\rfloor
and
\{\ell x\}=\{\ell\{x\}\}=\ell\{x\}-\lfloor\ell\{x\}\rfloor
, whence (
9
).

We have

x_{1}+\cdots+x_{\ell}=(\lfloor x_{1}\rfloor+\cdots+\lfloor x_{\ell}\rfloor)+(\{x_{1}\}+\cdots+\{x_{\ell}\});

The first term in the RHS is an integer and the second term is in
[0,1)
, which implies (
10
). Relation (
11
) is just the special case of (
10
) in which all the summands are equal.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
R
and
r
be integers with
0\leq r<R
,
u
be a real number in
[0,1]
, and
x
be a real number such that

\left\{\frac{x}{R}\right\}\in\left[\frac{r}{R},\frac{r+u}{R}\right).

We have

\lfloor x\rfloor\equiv r\,(\operatorname{mod}R)\;\text{ and }\;\{x\}<u.

Proof.

The hypothesis implies that there exists a rational integer
t
such that

\frac{x}{R}-t\in\left[\frac{r}{R},\frac{r+u}{R}\right).

This implies

tR+r\leq x<tR+r+u,\;\text{ whence }\;\lfloor x\rfloor=tR+r\;\text{ and }\;0\leq\{x\}<u.

∎

\frac{2}{H}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\\
p>L\end{subarray}}\frac{\varphi(p)}{p}\leq\varphi(\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor)/\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor)\leq\frac{3}{H}.

In order to prove (
13
), and thus Theorem
2
, it is enough to prove that for
L
large enough, we have

(14)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\\
p>L\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\geq 1/2.

By the definition of
R
and the prime number theorem, we have, when
L
is large enough

\displaystyle\log\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq(\beta c+1)\left(\sum_{p\leq L}\log p+\log H!\right)

\displaystyle\leq(\beta c+2)L.

When
L
is large enough, and thus
m
too, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\\
p>L\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\\
p>((\beta c+2)^{-1}\log\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

by the previous inequality

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor\\
p>(\log\lfloor(m+h)^{c}\rfloor)^{3/4}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

since
m
is large enough

\displaystyle\geq 1/2

\displaystyle\text{by Lemma \ref{largep}}.

Thus, (
14
) is proved, as well as Theorem
2
.

5.
Remarks and complements

We see this paper as an archetype of the more general study of the distribution modulo 1 of mean vaues of sequences
(f(a(n)))_{n}
, where
f
is a regular multiplicative function with constant mean value and
a
is a function in a Hardy field with polynomial growth.

In particular, it is challenging to determine the values of
c
for which the sequence
\left(\sum_{m\leq n}\varphi(\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor)/\lfloor m^{c}\rfloor\right)_{n\geq 1}
is uniformly distributed modulo
1
.
