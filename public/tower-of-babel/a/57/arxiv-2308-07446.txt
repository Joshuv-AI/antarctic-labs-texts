Effective recovery of Fourier spectra and spectral approximation by finite groups
Mircea Petrache, Rodolfo Viera

Abstract
We prove a result on approximate recovery, with high probability, of subgroups of a finite nonabelian group Γ \Gamma from their random perturbations. We use this for ad-hoc sequences of Γ n \Gamma_{n} while passing to the continuum limit, in order to obtain asymptotic almost sure recovery for rational lcsc nilpotent Lie groups. By comparison to limit theorems for groups of polynomial growth, it turns out that this setting is the natural general setting for recovery results, under polynomial growth assumptions on the Γ n \Gamma_{n} . This approach makes effective the convergence rate in previous Fourier recovery theorems in Euclidean space, and extends them to the nonabelian setting. A series of interesting further directions are highlighted by this approach.

Introduction

Motivated by problems in signal denoising
[
14
]
, hyperuniform point configurations
[
13
]
and diffraction theory
[
11
,
2
]
, to mention a few, in the last years there has been a lot of activity in random perturbations on discrete structures. Roughly speaking, the general framework is that, starting with a discrete set, one is concerned with how a structure of interest defined on it is affected by random perturbations of the set; for instance, in
[
13
]
the authors relate by a very precise formula the structure factors of a (randomized) lattice and a perturbed version of it. Very recently,
[
21
,
17
]
addressed the problem of studying the effect of random perturbations on the Fourier transform of a discrete set
X
in the Euclidean space, proving robustness-type results for the Fourier transform in lattices
[
21
]
and more general quasi-periodic subsets of Euclidean spaces, such as quasicrystals
[
17
]
. The above works leave open the following natural research questions, which we tackle here:

•

In defining Fourier transform on countable uniformly discrete subsets of Euclidean space, one makes an implicit idealization, since in (numerical and physics) applications we never directly work with infinite sets, but are bound to do only
approximations
to Fourier transforms on infinite spaces. It is thus natural to develop effective approximation error bounds for recovery results of structures such as the Fourier spectrum.

•

While lattices and quasicrystals in Euclidean spaces are structures relevant to crystallographic applications, it is natural to ask general structure recovery results of the same kind, in general geometries, as done in
[
14
]
in a different framework. In particular, it is natural to generalize such questions to the framework of nonabelian groups of polynomial growth.

In this work, we tackle both questions for the first time, and at the same time, we set a basis for applying geometric group theory tools to recovery questions. In this way, through robustness-type results, we intend to contribute to understanding how probabilistic aspects in a group are determined by its geometry; we refer the reader to
[
16
]
for an introduction to these topics. While there is a large and deep theory for the possible limits of discrete groups of polynomial growth
[
4
,
15
]
, the question of how to perform approximations that allows to ensure also convergence of further structures (such as Fourier transforms) is to our knowledge not developed. We hope that this work will motivate further advances towards a Fourier theory of approximate groups, by providing a concrete use case for such theory in the setting of recovery problems.

Setting and overview

We first define the property that a ”fingerprint” map
\mathcal{F}
applied to a random perturbation of a subgroup
H

•

Let
(G,\cdot)
be a topological group and
H\subset G
be a subgroup.

•

We let
\mathcal{D}
be a fixed set of probability measures over
G
.

•

We consider a fixed injective linear

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{M}_{b}(G)\to\mathcal{B}
where
\mathcal{B}
is a

-algebra and

on

For fixed

\begin{split}H_{\mu}(\omega)&:=\{h\cdot\xi_{h}(\omega):h\in H\}\subset G\qquad\omega\in\Omega.\end{split}

(1.1)

We then use the abbreviated notation

\delta_{H_{\xi}(\omega)}:=\sum_{h\in H_{\xi}(\omega)}\delta_{h},

where

is the Dirac delta at

Definition 1.1
.

With notations and definitions as above, we introduce the following notions:

(A)

For real numbers
\mathsf{err}\geq 0

-action on

\mathcal{D}-

(1.2)

such that, for all

for

as in (
1.1
),

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\mu}})-\Phi_{H}(\mu)\right\|_{\mathcal{B}}>\mathsf{err}\right\}\leq\delta.

(1.3)

(B)

We say that the
\mathcal{F}
-action on

\mathcal{D}-

Motivation for our hypotheses on
\mathcal{F}
.
While working with noise-robustness of fingerprints

in high generality may be interesting, some extra structure on

Explicit formulas for
\Phi_{H}
.
In case

are finite groups, it seems to be more often the case that only

\Phi_{H}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\mu}})\right]=\mathcal{F}\left(\mathbb{E}[\delta_{H_{\mu}}]\right)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H}*\mu)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})\mathcal{F}(\mu).

Above, we interpret

is a
*

For the case of infinite

we fix a distance compatible with the metric on

(1.6)

where

denotes the asymptotic ball growth in

is a discrete infinite group or a lattice in a nilpotent

, in which

. Then we consider the case of tame
\mu

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[|\xi|^{k+\epsilon}]<\infty

In the above case, we can build an explicit description of the function

(B). Indeed, assuming (

and
\pi\in\widehat{G}

\displaystyle\Phi_{H}(\mu)(\pi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\mu}})(\pi)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{C_{H}R^{k}}\sum_{x\in H_{\mu}(\omega)\cap B_{R}}\pi(x)\right]

(1.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{C_{H}R^{k}}\sum_{h\in H\cap B_{R}}\pi(h\cdot\xi_{h})\right]=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{C_{H}R^{k}}\sum_{h\in H\cap B_{R}}\pi(h)\mathbb{E}\left[\pi(\xi_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\pi)\int_{G}\pi(x)d\mu(x),

with the integral taken in the sense of Bochner. The first equality in the above second line is based again on the moment hypothesis on
\mu
, and will be justified in
Lemma

4.6

.

Main Results.
We now specialize to the setting of our main results. In the above paragraphs we have seen that

(A)

If

Then our main results are summarized in the next two theorems.

Theorem A
.

Let

be a finite group and

be a normal subgroup with
|H|\geq 24
. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the (normalized) Discrete Fourier Transform of

(1.8)

Note that

Possible application cases of Theorem A.
In numerical applications, such as for numerical simulations of materials, or for signal processing via finite codes, amongst others, one most commonly one has to control error bounds for finite discretizations, with special focus on asymptotics and scalability of such bounds. For the question of robustness to random perturbations,

Our second main result is concerned with extending the known recovery results for lattices in Euclidean spaces to more general groups. We consider the natural case of infinite discrete subgroups of nilpotent Lie groups:

Motivation for the hypothesis that

Theorem B describes recovery of discrete subgroups in a class of Lie groups

1.

For the good definition of

2.

Our almost-sure recovery proof is based on a version of the central limit theorem to

Possible application cases of Theorem B.
The special case of

Some natural open directions.
Note that a direct extension of

•

Quasicrystals

Approximate recovery in finite groups

2.1
Background on Representations of Finite Groups

This section establishes some basic terminology and the necessary machinery of Fourier Analysis in Finite (non-Abelian) Groups. Given a finite-dimensional vector space
V
over
\mathbb{C}
, define by
GL(V)
be the vector space of all the linear invertible linear transformations
T:V\to V
; this space is naturally identified with the space of invertible matrices
GL(n,\mathbb{C})
, where
n=\mathsf{dim}(V)
. Throughout this section,
\Gamma
will denote a finite group. A homomorphism
\pi:\Gamma\to GL(V)
is called a (finite dimensional)
representation
of
\Gamma
. Thus, for every
g\in\Gamma
,
\pi(g)
is a matrix whose complex entries are denoted by
\pi_{ij}(g)
. The dimension of
V
is denoted by
d_{\pi}
and is called the
degree
(or
dimension
) of the representation
\pi
. If
\pi
takes values into the set of unitary operators
\mathcal{U}(V)\cong\mathcal{U}(n,\mathbb{C}):=\{A\in GL(n,\mathbb{C}):\ AA^{*}=I\}
, then
\pi
is called a
unitary
representation of
\Gamma
; in particular the Hermitian inner product
\langle u,v\rangle:=uv^{t},\ u,v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is preserved under unitary transformations.

The building blocks of Fourier Analysis in (nonabelian) groups are the irreducible representations. Given a representation
\pi:\Gamma\to GL(V)
and a subspace
W\leq V
which is
\pi
-invariant, i.e,
\pi(g)W\subset W
for every
g\in\Gamma
, the map
\pi^{\prime}:\Gamma\to GL(W)
given by
\pi^{\prime}(g):=\pi(g)|_{W}
is called a
subrepresentation
of
\pi
. A representation
\pi
is called
irreducible
if its only subrepresentations are the trivial one and
\pi
itself.

We say that two representations
\pi_{1}:\Gamma\to GL(V_{\pi_{1}})
and
\pi_{2}:\Gamma\to GL(V_{\pi_{2}})
are
equivalent
if there is a isomorphism
T:V_{\pi_{1}}\to V_{\pi_{2}}
such that
T\pi_{1}(g)=\pi_{2}(g)T
for every
g\in\Gamma
; we denote this equivalence relation by
\pi_{1}\cong\pi_{2}
. The following Proposition says that every unitary representation can be decomposed into irreducible components (see Proposition
[
19
, Chapter 15]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

1.

Every representation is equivalent to a unitary representation.

2.

Every unitary representation
\pi:\Gamma\to\mathcal{U}(V_{\pi})
is equivalent to

\pi_{1}\oplus\pi_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus\pi_{r}:=\begin{pmatrix}\pi_{1}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&\pi_{r}\end{pmatrix}

where each block
\pi_{j}
is an irreducible subrepresentation of
\pi
.

Given two representations
\pi:\Gamma\to GL(V_{\pi})
and
\rho:\Gamma\to GL(V_{\rho})
, consider the space
I(\pi,\rho)
of
intertwining operators
defined by

I(\pi,\rho):=\{T:V_{\pi}\to V_{\rho}:\text{T is linear and }T\circ\pi(g)=\rho(g)\circ T,\text{ for every }g\in\Gamma\};

In particular,
I(\pi):=I(\pi,\pi)
is the set of linear operators
T:V_{\pi}\to V_{\pi}
which commute (under composition) with
\pi(g)
for every
g\in\Gamma
. The condition that two representations
\pi,\rho
are equivalent can now be reformulated by requiring that
I(\pi,\rho)
contains an isomorphism.

The following Lemma is one of the cornerstones in Fourier Analysis in non-abelian groups; we refer to
[
19
, Chapter 15]
for further details.

Lemma 2.2

(Schur’s Lemma)
.

1.

A unitary representation
\pi
of
\Gamma
is irreducible if and only if
I(\pi)
contains only scalars multiples of the identity.

2.

If
\pi
and
\rho
are unitary irreducible representations of
\Gamma
, then

\mathsf{dim}(I(\pi,\rho))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}1&\text{if }\pi\text{ and }\rho\text{ are equivalent,}\\
\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

By identifying representations under equivalence, we define the
dual space

\widehat{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
as the set of equivalence classes of irreducible unitary representations of
\Gamma
, i.e

\widehat{\Gamma}:=\{[\pi]:\ \pi:\Gamma\to U(V_{\pi})\text{ is an irreducible unitary representation of }\Gamma\},

where
[\pi]
denotes the equivalence class of
\pi
; we simply write
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
when there is no confusion about the notation. It is well-known that

\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}^{2}=|\Gamma|.

(2.1)

For a given
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
and a unitary representation
\rho
of
\Gamma
, we denote by
m(\pi,\rho)
be the number of times that
\pi
occurs in the decomposition in irreducible components of
\rho
given in
2.1
.

The entries
\pi_{ij}
of a representation
\pi:\Gamma\to GL(V)
can be regarded as elements of
L^{2}(\Gamma)
with the usual inner product

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}:=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\phi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}\qquad\text{ for every }\phi,\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma).

The following theorem establishes orthogonality relations for the entries of irreducible unitary representations (see Theorem 1 in
[
19
, Chapter 15]
).

Theorem 2.3

(The Schur Orthogonality relations)
.

If
\pi,\rho
are two inequivalent irreducible finite dimensional unitary representations of
\Gamma
, then

\langle\pi_{ij},\rho_{rs}\rangle=0\qquad\text{ for all }i,j,r,s.

Moreover, if
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta function, there holds

\langle\pi_{ij},\pi_{rs}\rangle=\frac{|\Gamma|}{d_{\pi}}\delta_{ij}\delta_{rs}.

Theorem 2.4

(Clifford’s Theorem)
.

Suppose that
H
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
and let
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
. Denote by
\pi|_{H}
be the restriction of
\pi
to
H
and consider its decomposition in irreducible components

\pi|_{H}\cong\pi_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\pi_{k}\qquad\text{where }\pi_{i}\in\widehat{H}\text{ for every }1\leq i\leq k.

(2.2)

Then for each
1\leq j\leq k
there exists
g_{j}\in G
such that for all
h\in H
we have
\pi_{j}(h)=\pi_{1}(g_{j}^{-1}hg_{j})
. Moreover, the following hold:

1.

All the irreducible components in (
2.2
) have the same degree, i.e, for every
1\leq i\leq k
there holds that
d_{\pi_{1}}=d_{\pi_{i}}
.

2.

All the irreducible components in (
2.2
) have the same multiplicity, i.e, for every
1\leq i\leq k
there holds that
m(\pi_{1},\pi|_{H})=m(\pi_{i},\pi|_{H})
.

2.2
Fourier Transform on Finite groups

Define the Fourier Transform of a function
f\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
by

\mathcal{F}(f)(\pi):=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}f(g)\pi(g),\qquad\text{ for every }\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}.

(2.3)

For
\phi,\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
define the convolution
\phi*\psi
as follows

\phi*\psi(g):=\sum_{h\in\Gamma}\phi(h)\psi(h^{-1}g).

Then with the above definitions, we get that

\mathcal{F}(\phi*\psi)(\pi)=|\Gamma|\mathcal{F}(\phi)(\pi)\mathcal{F}(\psi)(\pi).

(2.4)

Denote by
L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
be the vector space

L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma}):=\left\{f:\widehat{\Gamma}\to\bigsqcup_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathbb{C}^{d_{\pi}\times d_{\pi}}:\ f(\pi)\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\pi}\times d_{\pi}}\text{ for every }\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}\right\}.

We endow
L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}
given by

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}:=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\mathsf{Tr}(f(\pi)g(\pi)^{*}).

(2.5)

Here
\mathsf{Tr}(T)
is the
trace
of a linear operator
T:V\to V
, and is defined by
\mathsf{Tr}(T):=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle Te_{j},e_{j}\rangle
, where
(e_{i})
ranges over any orthonormal basis of
V
. In particular, for all
f\in L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
there holds that

\|f\|_{L^{2}({\widehat{\Gamma}})}^{2}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}|f_{ij}(\pi)|^{2},

(2.6)

where
f_{ij}(\pi)
,
i,j=1,\ldots,d_{\pi}
are the entries of the matrix
f(\pi)
in a basis
(e_{i}^{(\pi)})_{i=1}^{d_{\pi}}
of
V_{\pi}
.

By the
Plancherel Theorem
, we have that the Fourier Transform is an isometric isomorphism between
L^{2}(\Gamma)
and
L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
, i.e., that for any
f,g\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
there holds

|\Gamma|^{2}\langle\mathcal{F}(f),\mathcal{F}(g)\rangle_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}=\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

(2.7)

By the
Peter-Weyl Theorem
, the matrix entries
\pi_{ij}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
of
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
form a complete orthogonal set in
L^{2}(\Gamma)
. In particular, (
2.7
) can be written as

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle f,\pi_{ij}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\overline{\langle g,\pi_{ij}\rangle}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}.

(2.8)

Finally, the
Fourier inversion formula
says that every
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
can be reconstructed from its Fourier transform as follows

f(h)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\mathsf{Tr}(\pi(h^{-1})\mathcal{F}(\pi))=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle f,\pi_{ij}\rangle\pi_{ij}(h).

(2.9)

2.3
Estimating the Fourier Transform under random perturbations

Let
\Gamma
be a non-simple finite group and
H
a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
. Recall that the Fourier Transform of a function
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
by

\mathcal{F}(f)(\pi)=\widehat{f}(\pi):=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}f(g)\pi(g),\qquad\text{for }\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}.

(2.10)

Consider the Dirac delta of
H
defined by

\delta_{H}(g):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}1&\text{if }g\in H\\
\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

As in the Euclidean case, our first goal is to relate the Fourier Transform of
\delta_{H}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
with the annihilator set
H^{\perp}
defined by

H^{\perp}:=\{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}:\ \pi(h)=\mathsf{ld}_{V_{\pi}}\text{ for every }h\in H\}

(2.11)

We also have the following characterization, which must be a classical result, but for which we were not able to find an explicit reference:

Lemma 2.5
.

H^{\perp}\cong\widehat{\Gamma/H}
.

Sketch of the proof. .

Let
i:\Gamma\to\Gamma/H
be the canonical map associating to every
g\in\Gamma
its equivalence class
i(g)\in\Gamma/H
. The natural map
\phi:H^{\perp}\to\widehat{\Gamma/H}
given by

\phi(\pi)(i(g)):=\pi(g)

is well-defined and determines a group isomorphism between
H^{\perp}
and
\widehat{\Gamma/H}
. We leave the details to the reader.
∎

For the next discussion, we will study the unitary representations
\pi\not\in H^{\perp}
. Given a representation
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}\setminus H^{\perp}
, by
2.1
we can decompose
\pi|_{H}
(modulo equivalence) in irreducible components

\pi|_{H}=\pi_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\pi_{k},\qquad\text{ where }\pi_{i}\in\widehat{H}\text{ for every }i=1,\ldots,k.

In this setting, consider the matrix
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)_{i}
as the the
d_{i}\times d_{i}
-block of
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)
defined by

\left(\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\right)_{i}:=P_{i}^{T}\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)P_{i}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi_{i}(h),

(2.12)

where
P_{i}
is the projection onto the subspace of
V_{i}
where
\pi_{i}
acts, and
d_{i}
is the degree of
\pi_{i}
. Note that in the above definition, we use an abuse of notation: we have
\widehat{\delta_{H}}\in L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
and in general
2.1
cannot be applied to
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)
directly.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have that

1.

If
\pi\in H^{\perp}
, then
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{|H|}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi}}
.

2.

If
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}\setminus H^{\perp}
, then when we decompose
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)
as in (
2.12
), for all
i=1,\ldots,k
for which
\pi_{i}\neq\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi_{i}}}
, the block
\left(\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\right)_{i}
vanishes.

In particular, if
H
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, then

\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{|H|}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{Id}_{d_{\pi}}\delta_{H^{\perp}}(\pi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{|H|}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{Id}_{d_{\pi}}&\text{if }\pi\in H^{\perp}\\
\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Firstly, by the definition of the Fourier Transform, we get that

\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi(h).

(2.13)

If
\pi\in H^{\perp}
then it is direct that
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{|H|}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{ld}_{V_{\pi}}
.
Thus we concentrate on the case
\pi\not\in H^{\perp}
. In this case, there must exists
h^{\prime}\in H
such that
\pi(h^{\prime})\neq\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi}}
. For this choice of
h^{\prime}
, there holds

\begin{split}\pi(h^{\prime})\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi(h^{\prime}h)=\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\pi(h^{\prime}).\end{split}

(2.14)

In particular for each block in the decomposition of
\pi|_{H}
from
2.1
, we have that the operator
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)_{i}
belongs to
I(\pi_{i})
(if
\pi(h^{\prime})=\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi}}
) then
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)_{i}
obviously commutes with
\pi_{i}(h^{\prime})
). By Schur’s Lemma, there is a scalar
c_{i}
such that
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)_{i}=c_{i}\cdot\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi_{i}}}
. If
1\leq i\leq k
satisfies that
\pi_{i}(h^{\prime})\neq\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi_{i}}}
, then from (
2.14
) we get that
c_{i}=0
, otherwise
\pi_{i}(h^{\prime})=\mathsf{Id}_{V_{\pi_{i}}}
contradicting the choice of
\pi
and
h^{\prime}\in H
. Therefore the matrix
\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)_{i}
vanishes as claimed.

Now, assume that
H
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
. In this case, consider the matrix

P_{\pi}:=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\pi(g)\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\pi(g^{-1}).

There holds
P_{\pi}\in I(\pi)
, since for every
g^{\prime}\in\Gamma
we can write

\begin{split}\pi(g^{\prime})P_{\pi}&=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\pi(g^{\prime}g)\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\pi(g^{-1})=\sum_{p\in\Gamma}\pi(p)\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\pi(p^{-1}g^{\prime})=P_{\pi}\pi(g^{\prime}).\end{split}

Hence, by Schur’s Lemma, there is a constant
\lambda
such that

P=P_{\pi}=\lambda\cdot\mathsf{Id}_{d_{\pi}}.

(2.15)

On the other hand, since
H
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
, we have

P_{\pi}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\pi(g)\left(\sum_{h\in H}\pi(h)\right)\pi(g^{-1})=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\sum_{h\in H}\pi(ghg^{-1})=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\widehat{\delta_{gHg^{-1}}}(\pi)=|\Gamma|\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi).

(2.16)

Thus, from (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) we get that

\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)=\frac{\lambda}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{Id}_{d_{\pi}}.

(2.17)

For
\pi\in H^{\perp}
, by applying the above to the the identity of
\Gamma
, we obtain that
\lambda=|H|
. For
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}\setminus H^{\perp}
, we have that the values
c_{i}
from the paragraph following (
2.17
) are all equal to
c_{i}=\frac{\lambda}{|\Gamma|}
, and therefore
\lambda=0
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The last formula of
Lemma

2.6
is a consequence of a more general result, which says that
f\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
is a class function (i.e., if
f(ghg^{-1})=f(h)
for every
h,g\in\Gamma
) if and only if
\widehat{f}(\pi)
is a scalar multiple of
\mathsf{Id}_{d_{\pi}}
. See for instance Tao’s Lecture notes of Representation Theory
[
18
]
.

Now, to describe random perturbations of a subgroup
H
of a group
\Gamma
as above, let
(\xi_{h})_{h\in H}
be independent identically distributed random vectors defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{T},\mathbb{P})
, where for each
h\in H
the random variable
\xi_{h}
is
\Gamma
-valued. Like in the euclidean case, consider the realization set
H_{\xi}
as the subset of
\Gamma
defined by

H_{\xi}:=\{h\xi_{h}:\ h\in H\}.

(2.18)

Equivalently, if
\mu_{e}
denotes the law of
\xi_{\mathsf{id}}
, then the law of
h\xi_{h}
is the image measure of
\mu_{e}
under the
H
-left translations
L_{h}(h^{\prime}):=hh^{\prime}
.

The main result of this section is an approximate recovery of
\widehat{\delta_{H}}
from
\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}
as follows.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
L>3
and let
H
be a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
with cardinality
|H|
at least
6L/(L-3)
. Consider
\xi_{h},h\in H
be i.i.d.
\Gamma
-valued random variables with
\xi_{h}\sim\xi
and
C_{\xi,\Gamma}:=\sup_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathsf{Var}[\pi(\xi)]<\infty
, and assume that
\varepsilon>\left(\frac{12C^{2}L}{|H|}\right)^{1/2}
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{12C^{2}L}{\varepsilon^{2}|H|}
we have

\left\|\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}-\widehat{\delta_{H}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi(\cdot)]\right\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}^{2}\leq\frac{|H|^{4}}{|\Gamma|^{2}}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{|H|}\right)\max_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi},

(2.19)

where
\xi(\cdot):\widehat{\Gamma}\to\bigsqcup_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathbb{C}^{d_{\pi}\times d_{\pi}}
stands for the map
\xi(\pi):=\pi(\xi)
.

By the definition of
|\cdot|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}
fixed in (
2.5
), the left-hand side of (
2.19
) can be written as

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}-\widehat{\delta_{H}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi(\cdot)]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left\|\left(\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}(\pi)-\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\pi)\mathbb{E}(\xi(\pi))\right)e_{j}^{(\pi)}\right\|_{V_{\pi}}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|^{3}}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\sum_{h\in H}\pi_{il}(h)\left(\pi_{lj}(\xi_{h})-\mathbb{E}[\pi_{lj}(\xi)]\right)\right|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|^{3}}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle\pi_{il},\overline{\pi_{lj}(\xi_{h})-\mathbb{E}[\pi_{lj}(\xi)]}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}\right|^{2},\end{split}

(2.20)

and where
\{e_{1}^{(\pi)},\dots,e_{d_{\pi}}^{(\pi)}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
V_{\pi}
. Now for fixed
\pi\in L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
we define
f_{il},g_{lj}\in L^{2}(H)
and their extensions by zero
F_{il},G_{lj}\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
by

f_{il}^{(\pi)}(h)=f_{il}(h):=\pi_{il}(h)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{lj}^{(\pi)}(h)=g_{lj}(h):=\overline{\pi_{lj}(\xi_{h})-\mathbb{E}[\pi_{lj}(\xi)]},

F_{il}^{(\pi)}=F_{il}=f_{il}\delta_{H}\qquad\text{and}\qquad G_{lj}^{(\pi)}=G_{lj}=g_{lj}\delta_{H}.

With this notation, our main goal for estimating (
2.20
) is to find a suitable bound for
\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle f_{il},g_{lj}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}
, possibly depending on
i,j
, since (
2.20
) can be rewritten as:

\left\|\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}-\widehat{\delta_{H}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\xi(\cdot)]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|^{3}}\sum_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\pi}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle f_{il},g_{lj}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}\right|^{2}.

(2.21)

Observe that
f_{il}
is deterministic and
g_{lj}
is random.

By triangle inequality, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the Plancherel formula (
2.7
), we rewrite the summands in (
2.21
) via the Fourier transforms of
F_{il},G_{lj}
(compare with (
5.8
)):

\begin{split}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\langle F_{il},G_{lj}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\right|&=|\Gamma|^{2}\left|\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left\langle\widehat{F_{il}},\widehat{G_{lj}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}\right|\leq|\Gamma|^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\pi}}\left\|\widehat{F_{il}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}\left\|\widehat{G_{lj}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}.\end{split}

(2.22)

We concentrate first on determining
\|\widehat{F_{il}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
. We now fix our orthonormal basis of
V_{\pi}
so that it is adapted to the decomposition

\pi|_{H}=\pi_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\pi_{l(\pi)},\qquad\text{where }\pi_{k}\in\widehat{H}\text{ for every }k=1,\ldots,l(\pi).

Therefore
\pi_{il}\delta_{H}\in L^{2}({H})
appears as a matrix-entry of some irreducible component, whose index we denote
k(i,l)=k,1\leq k\leq l(\pi)
; we denote by
\pi_{pq}^{(k)}
the matrix entry of
\pi_{k}
corresponding to
\pi_{il}\delta_{H}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

As before, let
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
, let
\pi_{il}
be a matrix entry of
\pi
such that
\pi_{il}
is not constantly zero over
H
, and let
\pi_{k(i,l)}=\pi_{k}\in\widehat{H}
be the irreducible component of
\pi|_{H}
in which
\pi_{il}
appears. Then the following holds:

1.

The support of
\widehat{F_{il}}
is the set of all irreducible unitary representations
\rho\in\widehat{\Gamma}
for which
\pi_{k}
occurs in the decomposition in irreducible components of
\rho|_{H}
.

2.

If
m(\pi_{k},\rho|_{H})
is the number of times that
\pi_{k}
appears in the decomposition of
\rho|_{H}
in irreducible components, then

\|\widehat{F_{il}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}=\frac{|H|^{2}}{d_{\pi_{k}}^{2}|\Gamma|^{3}}\sum_{\rho\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\rho}\cdot m(\pi_{k},\rho|_{H})

(2.23)

where
\chi_{\rho}(h):=\mathsf{Tr}(\rho(h))
is the character of the representation
\rho:H\to GL(V_{\rho})
.

Proof point 1. of
Lemma

2.9
.

Let
\rho\in\widehat{\Gamma}
. By
2.1

\rho|_{H}
decomposes as

\rho|_{H}=\rho_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\rho_{l(\rho)},\qquad\text{where }\rho_{k}\in\widehat{H}\text{ for every }1\leq k\leq l(\rho).

With these notations, we will prove that

\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)\neq 0\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\exists\ 1\leq m\leq l(\rho)\text{ such that }\rho_{m}=\pi_{k}.

With respect to bases of
V_{\pi},V_{\rho}
adapted to the decompositions of
\pi|_{H},\rho|_{H}
, we have

\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi_{il}(h)\rho(h)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi_{pq}^{(k)}(h)\rho(h)=\left(\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\langle\pi_{pq}^{(k)},\overline{\rho_{rs}}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}\right)_{r,s=1}^{d_{\rho}}.

If a component
\rho_{m}
of
\rho|_{H}
is not equivalent to
\pi_{k}
, then Schur’s orthogonality relations (
2.3
) give

\langle\pi_{pq}^{(k)},\overline{\rho_{rs}^{(m)}}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}=0\qquad\text{for every entry $\rho_{rs}^{(m)}$ of $\rho_{m}$}.

(2.24)

In particular, if this is true for all
m
, we get

\left(\langle\pi_{pq}^{(k)},\overline{\rho_{rs}}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}\right)_{r,s=1}^{d_{\rho}}=0\qquad\text{for every }1\leq r,s\leq d_{\rho}.

This shows one direction of the claimed implication. Assume now that there is
1\leq m\leq l(\rho)
such that
\rho_{m}=\pi_{k}
. Then again, by
2.3
up to a change of basis of
V_{\rho}
we get

\langle\pi_{pq}^{(k)},\overline{\rho_{rs}^{(m)}}\rangle_{L^{2}(H)}=\frac{|H|}{d_{\pi_{k}}}\delta_{pr}\delta_{qs},

which in particular implies that
\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)\neq 0
. Summarizing, under our assumption that
\pi_{il}
is not constantly zero over
H
, we have

\mathsf{supp}(\widehat{F_{il}})=\{\rho\in\widehat{\Gamma}:\ \pi_{k}\text{ appears in the decomposition in irred. components of }\rho|_{H}\}.

∎

Remark 2.10
.

Consider the abelian case
\Gamma=\mathbb{T}_{M,N}^{d}=\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\right)^{d}
,
X=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}
. By a dimensionality argument, every irreducible representation
e^{-2\pi i\langle\cdot,\lambda\rangle}
of
\widehat{\mathbb{T}}_{M,N}^{d}\cong\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}
restricted to
X
is in fact an irreducible representation of
\widehat{X}=X
. Thus, for
F_{\lambda}:=e^{-2\pi i\langle\cdot,\lambda\rangle}\delta_{X}
, from
Lemma

2.9
part 1 we get that

\mathsf{supp}(\widehat{F_{\lambda}})=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}:\ N\alpha+N\lambda\equiv 0(mod\ M)\}=\mathbb{T}_{M}^{d}-\lambda=X^{*}-\lambda,

which coincides with the support of
\widehat{F_{\lambda}}
given in (
5.9
) below.

Proof of point 2. of
Lemma

2.9
.

We now use point 1. of
Lemma

2.9
to compute
|\widehat{F_{il}}|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
. If
\rho\in\mathsf{supp}(\widehat{F_{il}})
, then by
2.3
we obtain a coordinate expression

\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus A_{l(\rho)},\qquad\text{where }A_{m}\in M(d_{\rho_{m}}\times d_{\rho_{m}},\mathbb{C})\text{ for every }1\leq m\leq l(\rho),

and where from (
2.24
) there holds that
A_{m}=0
if
\rho_{m}\not\cong\pi_{k}
, whereas for
\rho_{m}\cong\pi_{k}
we have

A_{m}=(A_{rs}^{(m)})_{r,s=1}^{d_{\pi_{k}}}\qquad\text{where }A_{rs}^{(m)}=\frac{|H|}{d_{\pi_{k}}|\Gamma|}\delta_{pr}\delta_{qs}.

Then, by definition of
|\cdot|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
and the previous characterization of
\widehat{F_{il}}
, we get

\begin{split}\|\widehat{F_{il}}\|_{L^{(}\widehat{\Gamma})}^{2}&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\rho\in\mathsf{supp}(\widehat{F_{il}})}d_{\rho}\mathsf{Tr}(\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)\widehat{F_{il}}(\rho)^{*})\\
&=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{\rho\in\mathsf{supp}(\widehat{F_{il}})}d_{\rho}\frac{|H|^{2}}{d_{\pi_{k}}^{2}|\Gamma|^{2}}\cdot m(\pi_{k},\rho|_{H})\\
&=\frac{|H|^{2}}{d_{\pi_{k}}^{2}|\Gamma|^{3}}\sum_{\rho\in\widehat{\Gamma}}d_{\rho}\cdot m(\pi_{k(i,l)},\rho|_{H}).\end{split}

This implies the claimed equality (
2.23
), concluding the proof.
∎

To estimate the value of
|\widehat{G_{lj}}|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
, we set
\widetilde{G_{lj}}(g):=\overline{G_{lj}(g^{-1})}
, and from the formula for the Fourier Transform of a convolution (
2.4
), the definition of
\widetilde{G_{lj}}
and the property
\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{*}
for every unitary representation
\rho
, we get

\mathcal{F}(G_{lj}*\widetilde{G_{lj}})=|\Gamma|\widehat{G_{lj}}\widehat{G_{lj}}^{*},

(2.25)

and as we have
|A|^{2}=\mathsf{Tr}(AA^{*})
for the Frobenius norm of a matrix, we find

\left|\widehat{G_{lj}}(\rho)\right|^{2}=\frac{|H|}{|\Gamma|}\mathsf{Tr}\left(\widehat{\gamma_{lj}}(\rho)\right),

(2.26)

where
\gamma_{lj}
denotes the
autocorrelation of
G_{lj}
which is defined by

\gamma_{lj}(g):=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}g_{lj}(h)\overline{g_{lj}(g^{-1}h)}=\frac{1}{|H|}G_{lj}*\widetilde{G_{lj}}=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}G_{lj}(h)\overline{G_{lj}(g^{-1}h)},

(2.27)

where
\mathsf{supp}(\gamma_{lj})=H
. Hence, to estimate (
2.22
), in view of (
2.26
), it is sufficient to find with high probability the approximate value of
\gamma_{lj}
.

Since the
G_{lj}
are random, it is natural to use Chebyshev inequality to estimate (
2.27
). Observe that the random variables
G_{lj}(h)\overline{G_{lj}(g^{-1}h)}
, with
h\in H
, are not necessarily independent. Hence, for each
g\in H
the goal is decompose
H
into three subsets
H_{g,i}
,
i=1,2,3
, of cardinalities close to
|H|/3
, for which the random variables
G_{lj}(h)\overline{G_{lj}(g^{-1}h)}
, with
h
running over each of the
H_{g,i}
’s, are independent.

Lemma 2.11
.

For each
g\in H\setminus\{\mathsf{id}\}
we can partition the subgroup
H
into three subsets
H_{g,0},H_{g,1},H_{g,2}
of cardinalities satisfying
||H_{g,i}|-|H|/3|\leq 2
, so that for any
i=0,1,2
and every
h,h^{\prime}\in H_{g,i}
, with
h\neq h^{\prime}
, there holds that
\{h,g^{-1}h\}\cap\{h^{\prime},g^{-1}h^{\prime}\}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let us to start by listing the elements of
H
by
\{\mathsf{id}=h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{|H|}\}
and define
h_{i_{1}}:=h_{1}
. For every
g\in H\setminus\{\mathsf{id}\}
, let
\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{1}})
be the orbit of
h_{i_{1}}
under left-translation of
g
, i.e:

\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{1}}):=\{g^{k}h_{i_{1}}:\ 0\leq k<\mathsf{ord}(g)\}.

Then, let
h_{i_{2}}
be the next element of the list such that
h_{i_{2}}\not\in\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{1}})
, and define the orbit

\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{2}}):=\{g^{k}h_{i_{2}}:\ 0\leq k<\mathsf{ord}(g)\}.

Inductively and as long as
H\setminus\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{m-1}\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{\alpha}})\neq\emptyset
, we consider
h_{i_{m}}\in H
being the next element of the list which does not belong to some of the previous orbits and define:

\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{m}}):=\{g^{k}h_{i_{m}}:\ 0\leq k<\mathsf{ord}(g)\}.

Let
m_{H}
be the largest positive integer for which
H=\bigcup_{\alpha=1}^{m_{H}}\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{\alpha}})
. Since all the orbits
\mathcal{T}_{g}(h_{i_{\alpha}})
,
1\leq\alpha\leq m_{H}
have the same number of points, the conclusions of
Lemma

2.11
hold for the following subsets of
H
:

H_{g,i}:=\left\{g^{3k+i}h_{i_{\alpha}}:\ 0\leq k\leq\frac{\mathsf{ord}(g)}{3},\ 1\leq\alpha\leq m_{H}\right\},\qquad\text{where }i\in\{0,1,2\}.

∎

.

Let

and assume that

Proof.

For

3
Some elements on Fourier Analysis in nilpotent Lie groups

3.1
Uniform subgroups in nilpotent Lie groups

We start this section with some basic background on nilpotent Lie groups; for further details, see for instance
[
7
]
. Let
\Gamma
be a
d
-dimensional locally compact simply connected (lcsc) Lie group, and denote by
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra, with associated Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. It is well-known that the exponential map
\mathsf{exp}:\mathfrak{g}\to\Gamma
produces a global diffeomorphism between
\Gamma
and its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and thus we can think
\Gamma
topologically as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, the exponential map is a Lie isomorphism when
\mathfrak{g}
is endowed with the group law given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula.

For the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
we define its
lower central series

as the sequence of Lie subalgebras
\mathfrak{g}\trianglerighteq\mathfrak{g}_{1}\trianglerighteq\mathfrak{g}_{2}\trianglerighteq\ldots
, where

\mathfrak{g}_{0}:=\mathfrak{g}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{g}_{i}:=[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{i-1}].

We say that
\Gamma
is
nilpotent
if there exists a positive integer
s
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{s+1}=\{0\}
; when
\mathfrak{g}_{s}\neq\{0\}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{s+1}=\{0\}
, then
G
is
nilpotent of step
s
.

Now, assume that
\Gamma
is nilpotent of step
s
. A subset
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\}\subset\Gamma
is called a
strong Mal’cev basis
subordinated to
\{0\}=\mathfrak{g}_{s+1}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{s}\subset\ldots\subset\mathfrak{g}_{2}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{1}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{g}
if for each
0\leq i\leq s
there holds that

\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\mathsf{span}\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d_{i}}\},\qquad\text{where}\qquad d_{i}:=\mathsf{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{i}).

(3.1)

For
X_{j},X_{k}
in the Mal’cev basis,
j\leq k
, there exists a natural number
i
such that
X_{k}\in\mathfrak{g}_{i}
, and in particular we get that
[X_{j},X_{k}]\in\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}
. Since
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\}
is a strong Mal’cev basis, there are constants
c_{jk}^{l}
,
1\leq l\leq d_{i+1}
(called
structural constants
), such that

[X_{j},X_{k}]=\sum_{l=1}^{d_{i+1}}c_{jk}^{l}X_{l}.

For a Mal’cev basis as above, we can establish a diffeomorphism
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Gamma
given by the formula

\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\mathsf{exp}(x_{1}X_{1})\cdot\mathsf{exp}(x_{2}X_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathsf{exp}(x_{d}X_{d}),

(3.2)

which by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (see
[
7
, Proposition 1.2.7]
), is a polynomial diffeomorphism of the form

\phi(x)=\mathsf{exp}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}P_{j}(x)X_{j}\right),

(3.3)

where the
P_{j}
’s are polynomials without constant coefficients, and

P_{j}(x)=x_{j}+\text{ a polynomial on }x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{d},\qquad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,d.

Observe that if
\Gamma
admits a Mal’cev basis with rational structural constants, we can choose the polynomials
P_{j}
with integer coefficients.

If
\Gamma
is a lcsc nilpotent Lie group, by a
uniform subgroup
in
\Gamma
, we mean a discrete subgroup
H\subset\Gamma
such that
\Gamma/H
is compact. The nature of the structural constants is intrinsically related to the existence of a uniform subgroup in
\Gamma
. Indeed, the way to connect both concepts is given by the next (see
[
7
, Theorem 5.1.8]
):

\begin{split}\Gamma\text{ admits a uniform subgroup }H\Longleftrightarrow\mathfrak{g}\text{ admits a Mal'cev basis with rational structural constants.}\end{split}

Let
H\subset\Gamma
be uniform and let
K\subset\Gamma
be a subgroup. In this case, it is known that
K
is rational (i.e., its Lie algebra admits a Mal’cev basis with rational structural constants) if and only if
H\cap K
is uniform in
K
(see
[
7
, Theorem 5.1.11]
); this fact will be exploited in
Section

4.1
.

3.2
Induced representations

In this section, we give the basic and necessary elements of induced representations that will be used for the problem of recovering the discrete Fourier spectrum in nilpotent groups; for more details, see
[
10
, Chapter 6]
. Induced representations are representations of a group
\Gamma
constructed from a smaller subgroup
K
. Let
\Gamma
be a locally compact group,
K
be a closed subgroup, and
i:\Gamma\to\Gamma/K
be the canonical quotient map that sends an element
x\in\Gamma
to its equivalence class
[x]
, and where
\Gamma/K
denotes the space of right-cosets. Consider a unitary representation
\sigma:K\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{\sigma})
, where the inner product and the norm in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}
are denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}}
and
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}}
, respectively.

For our purposes, assume that
\Gamma/K
admits a left-invariant measure
\eta
(this is the case, for instance, when
K
is a closed subgroup of a nilpotent Lie group
\Gamma
). Let
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
be the space of all the Borel measurable functions
f:\Gamma\to\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}
such that

\int_{\Gamma/K}\|f(x)\|^{2}d\eta(xK)<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(\xi x)=\sigma(\xi)f(x)\text{ for }x\in\Gamma,\xi\in K.

(3.4)

Observe that for
f,g\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
and
x\in\Gamma
, the inner product
\langle f(x),g(x)\rangle_{\sigma}
only depends on the coset
i(x)
. Then, we can define an inner product in
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
by means of the formula:

\langle f,g\rangle=\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}}:=\int_{\Gamma/K}\langle f(x),g(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}}d\eta(xK).

(3.5)

Thus, the inner product (
3.5
) becomes
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
in a complete Hilbert space. Note that the left-translation
x\mapsto L_{x}:\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\to\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
, where
L_{x}(f)(y):=f(xy)
, is a unitary representation of
\Gamma
. Hence, we define the
induced representation by
\sigma
as the unitary representation
\mathsf{ind}_{K}^{\Gamma}(\sigma):\Gamma\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{F}_{\sigma})
given by left-translations; more precisely

[\mathsf{ind}_{H}^{G}(\sigma)(x)f](y):=f(xy).

(3.6)

For explicit computations of the Fourier transform, it is more useful to describe
\mathsf{ind}_{K}^{\Gamma}(\sigma)
by acting over a suitable space of square-integrable functions. To achieve this, let
\alpha:\Gamma/K\to\Gamma
be a Borel cross-section of
i:\Gamma\to\Gamma/K
, i.e., for which there holds
i\circ\alpha=\mathsf{id}_{\Gamma/K}
. Due to (
3.4
) we have that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is completely determined by its values on
\alpha(\Gamma/K)
; indeed, we have that for every
x\in\Gamma
there exist
h=h(x)\in K
and
k=k(x)\in\alpha(\Gamma/K)
such that
x=h(x)k(x)
, and then

f(x)=f(hk)=\sigma(h)f(k).

(3.7)

In addition, we have that the map
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}\ni f\mapsto f\circ\alpha\in L^{2}(\Gamma/K)
becomes in an isometry.

3.3
Elements of Kirillov’s orbit method

In this section, we introduce the necessary ingredients of the well-understood theory of irreducible representations in nilpotent groups developed in the remarkable work of Kirillov
[
12
]
. Henceforth let
\Gamma
be an lcsc nilpotent Lie group. For
g\in\Gamma
denote by
\Psi_{x}:\Gamma\to\Gamma
the conjugation given by
\Psi_{g}(x)=gxg^{-1}
and consider the map
\mathsf{Ad}_{g}:=(\Psi_{g})_{*}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
, where

((\Psi_{g})_{*}X)_{p}:=(d\Psi_{g})_{\Psi_{g}^{-1}(p)}X_{\Psi_{g}^{-1}(p)},\qquad\text{for }X\in\mathfrak{g}.

The
adjoint representation
corresponds to the map
\mathsf{Ad}:\Gamma\to GL(\mathfrak{g})
given by
\mathsf{Ad}(g):=\mathsf{Ad}_{g}
. The
coadjoint map

\mathsf{Ad}^{*}:\Gamma\to GL(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
is defined by the formula

[(\mathsf{Ad}^{*}x)l](Y):=l(\mathsf{Ad}x^{-1}Y),\qquad\text{where }x\in\Gamma,l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*},\text{and }Y\in\mathfrak{g}.

(3.8)

Formula (
3.8
) defines an action of
\Gamma
over
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
given by
x\cdot l:=(\mathsf{Ad}^{*}x)l
. Each
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
determines a symplectic form
B_{l}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
defined as follows:

B_{l}(X,Y):=l([X,Y]),\quad\text{where }X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}.

(3.9)

The
radical
of
B_{l}
is the set
r_{l}:=\{Y\in\mathfrak{g}:\ B_{l}(X,Y)=0\text{ for every }X\in\mathfrak{g}\}
. For lcsc nilpotent Lie groups, there is a relation between the radical of
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
and the stabilizer
\mathsf{Stab}_{l}:=\{g\in\Gamma:g\cdot l=l\}
under the coadjoint action, namely, that

\mathsf{Stab}_{l}=\mathsf{exp}(r_{l}).

An
isotropic
subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
for
B_{l}
is a subspace
W\subset\mathfrak{g}
for which
B_{l}(X,X^{\prime})=0
for every
X,X^{\prime}\in W
. There are maximal subalgebras
\mathfrak{m}\subset\mathfrak{g}
that are isotropic for
B_{l}
and have (maximal) isotropic dimension
d-k
, where

k=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{dim}(\mathfrak{g}/r_{l}).

Such subalgebras are called
polarizing subalgebras
for
l
, which always exist (see
[
7
, Theorem 1.3.3]
).

Let
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_{l}
be a polarizing subalgebra for a fixed
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, and denote
K_{l}:=\mathsf{exp}(\mathfrak{m})
. By the isotropy, the map
\chi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}:K_{l}\to S^{1}
given by

\chi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}(\mathsf{exp}(Y)):=e^{2\pi il(Y)},\quad\text{where }Y\in\mathfrak{m},

(3.10)

is a
1
-dimensional irreducible representation of
K_{l}
. Define
\pi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}:=\mathsf{ind}_{K}^{\Gamma}(\chi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}):\Gamma\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{l})
, where
\mathcal{H}_{l}
is the Hilbert space where
\pi_{l}
acts, defined like in
Section

3.2
. The next theorem is the cornerstone of Kirillov’s theory and claims that the above-inducing mechanism produces
all
the irreducible representations of the nilpotent group
\Gamma
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
. There exists a polarizing subalgebra
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_{l}
for
l
such that
\pi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}
is irreducible. Moreover, every irreducible representation of
\Gamma
arises in this way. Also, the following hold:

1.

if
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
are two polarizing subalgebras for
l
, then
\pi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}\cong\pi_{l,\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}}
. Thus, we can write
\pi_{l}=\pi_{l,\mathfrak{m}}
.

2.

\pi_{l}\cong\pi_{l^{\prime}}
if and only if
l
and
l^{\prime}
belong to the same
\mathsf{Ad}^{*}(\Gamma)
-orbit in
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.

Following the discussion at the end of
Section

3.2
, we point out that in the case of lcsc nilpotent Lie groups, for

s:=\mathsf{dim}(\Gamma/K_{l})=d-\mathsf{dim}(\mathfrak{m}_{l})=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{dim}(\mathfrak{g}/r_{l}),

(3.11)

we have that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{l}
is isometric to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
under the mapping
\mathcal{H}_{l}\ni f\mapsto f\circ\alpha\in L^{2}(\Gamma/K_{l})
, and where
\Gamma/K_{l}\cong\mathbb{R}^{s}
. In addition, we get that
s
cannot exceed
d/2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the previous notations, we have that
s<d/2
.

Proof.

Otherwise, from (
3.11
) we get that

\frac{d}{2}\leq s=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{dim}(r_{l})\Longrightarrow\mathsf{dim}(r_{l})=0.

This implies that
\mathsf{Stab}_{l}=\mathsf{exp}(r_{l})=\{\mathsf{id}\}
. In particular,
\mathfrak{g}^{*}/\mathsf{Ad}^{*}\Gamma
consists of only one orbit, and by
3.1
we have that
|\widehat{\Gamma}|=1
. Then the trivial representation
\Gamma\ni g\mapsto 1\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
is the only irreducible representation (under equivalence) of
\Gamma
, which is one dimensional and hence
\Gamma
is Abelian, and by Pontryagin duality, we have that
\Gamma=\{\mathsf{id}\}
, which is impossible since
\Gamma\cong(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot)
, where
d\geq 1
.
∎

3.4
Basics on the Fourier transform in nilpotent Lie groups

In this section, we provide the basic elements of Fourier Analysis which we shall use to deal with the recovery problem. Although this theory can be presented with all its abstraction in second countably, unimodular Type I groups (see
[
8
,
10
]
), as was explained above (see
1.1
) we are concerned on lcsc nilpotent Lie groups, and thus we present the theory in this last context; we refer to
[
9
]
for further details. Like in the Euclidean case, the basic space to define a Fourier transform in
\Gamma
is the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions. We say that a function
f:\Gamma\to\mathbb{C}
is in the
Schwartz space

\mathcal{S}(\Gamma)
if
f\circ\mathsf{exp}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathfrak{g})
. For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\Gamma)
we define its
group Fourier transform
by the linear mapping

(\forall\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}),\qquad\mathcal{F}(f)(\pi)=\widehat{f}(\pi):=\int_{\Gamma}f(x)\pi(x)dx:\mathcal{H}_{\pi}\to\mathcal{H}_{\pi};

(3.12)

more precisely, for
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
we have

\langle\widehat{f}(\pi)\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}}=\int_{\Gamma}f(x)\langle\pi(x)\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}}dx.

We say that a positive operator
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is
trace-class
if
T
has an orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}>0
such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}<\infty
; in this case we define
\mathsf{Tr}(T):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\lambda_{n}
. In particular, if
T
is a positive operator and trace-class, then for every orthonormal basis
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
for
\mathcal{H}
we get
\mathsf{Tr}(T)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\langle Tx_{n},x_{n}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. Finally, we say that an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is
trace-class
if the positive operator
|T|:=\sqrt{TT^{*}}
is trace-class, where the root of a positive operator
\sqrt{\cdot}
is in the sense of spectral calculus.

In analogy to the classical Fourier transform in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as an isometric isomorphism
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(and then extended to an operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to itself), the nature of the space
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{S}(\Gamma))
is well-understood as a function space after
\widehat{\Gamma}
is equipped with an appropriate measure, called the Plancherel measure below.

Theorem 3.3

(Thm. 1.8.2 in
[
9
]
)
.

There exists a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
in
\widehat{\Gamma}
called the Plancherel measure such that for every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\Gamma)
, the operator
\mathcal{F}(\phi)=\widehat{\phi}(\pi)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
is trace-class for any strongly continuous unitary representation
\pi
, and
\mathsf{Tr}(\widehat{\phi}(\pi))
depends only in the class of
\pi
. Moreover, the function
\pi\mapsto\mathsf{Tr}(\widehat{\phi}(\pi))
is integrable against
\mu
and there holds:

\phi(0)=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathsf{Tr}(\widehat{\phi}(\pi))d\mu(\pi).

Henceforth we fix
\mu
the Plancherel measure of
\widehat{\Gamma}
. Note that by applying
3.3
to
\phi_{x}(y):=f(yx)
and since
\widehat{\phi}(\pi)=\pi(x)\widehat{f}(\pi)
, we obtain the Fourier inversion formula

f(x)=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathsf{Tr}(\pi(x)\widehat{f}(\pi))d\mu(\pi)=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\mathsf{Tr}(\widehat{f}(\pi)\pi(x))d\mu(\pi).

An operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called
Hilbert-Schmidt
if
TT^{*}
is trace-class; we write

\|T\|_{\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H})}^{2}:=\mathsf{Tr}(TT^{*}).

The Fourier inversion formula applied to
\phi*\phi^{*}
, where
\phi^{*}(x)=\overline{\phi(x^{-1})}
yields the Plancherel formula below.

Theorem 3.4

(Thm. 1.8.5 in
[
9
]
)
.

Under the notation of
3.3
, for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\Gamma)
, for every strongly continuous
\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}
, the operator
\widehat{\phi}(\pi)
is Hilbert-Schmidt, and
\|\widehat{\phi}(\pi)\|_{\mathsf{HS}}
is constant in the equivalence class of
\pi
. Moreover
\pi\mapsto\|\widehat{\phi}(\pi)\|_{\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})}^{2}
is
\mu
-integrable and

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}|\phi(x)|^{2}dx=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\|\widehat{\phi}(\pi)\|_{\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})}^{2}d\mu(\pi).

Observe that
\widehat{\phi}(\pi)
is a bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}_{\pi}
for
\phi\in C_{c}(\Gamma)
. The Plancherel formula provides a first approach to the nature of the group Fourier transform as an operator, namely,
\mathcal{F}:C_{c}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
is an isometry, where the
direct integral

L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma}):=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}^{\oplus}\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})d\mu(\pi)

is the Hilbert space of the
\mu
-measurable fields of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
A:\widehat{\Gamma}\ni\pi\mapsto A_{\pi}\in\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
for which

\|A\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})}^{2}:=\int_{\widehat{\Gamma}}\|A_{\pi}\|_{\mathsf{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})}^{2}d\mu(\pi)<\infty.

(3.13)

Here measurability means that

\widehat{\Gamma}\ni\pi\mapsto\langle A_{\pi}\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi}}\qquad\text{is measurable }\forall\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi};

for additional details on direct integrals and operators fields, see

[
9
, Appendix B]
or
[
10
, Section 7.4]
. Like in the Euclidean case, the group Fourier transform can be extended in an isometry
\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma})
which is surjective.

Now, in order to compute the Fourier transform of the Dirac delta
\delta_{p},p\in\Gamma
, we need to define the group Fourier transform of a measure. First, let
L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})
be the space of
\mu
-measurable fields
A:\widehat{\Gamma}\ni\pi\mapsto A_{\pi}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})
such that

\|A\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})}:=\sup_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}}\|A_{\pi}\|_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi})}<\infty,

where the supremum is the essential supremum with respect to the Plancherel measure
\mu
. The space
L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})
together the pointwise composition

AB:\widehat{\Gamma}\ni\pi\mapsto A_{\pi}B_{\pi}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\pi}),

and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})}
is a von Neumann algebra.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
be the subspace of operators
T\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
which are left-invariant, i.e., such that
T\circ L_{x}=L_{x}\circ T
, for every
x\in\Gamma
, and where
L_{x}(f)(y):=f(x^{-1}y)
is the left-translation operator.

For each
\mu
-measurable field of uniformly bounded operators
A\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})
, define the operator
T_{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
by

T_{A}\phi:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(A_{(\cdot)}\widehat{\phi}(\cdot)).

It can be shown that the map
T:L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})\ni A\mapsto T_{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
is a von Neumann algebra isomorphism and an isometry. Moreover, every operator
T\in\mathcal{B}_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
arises in this way, namely, there exists a unique (up to zero measure sets with respect
\mu
)
\mu
-measurable field
A^{(T)}\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})
such that for every
\phi\in L^{2}(\Gamma)
:

\widehat{T\phi}(\pi)=A^{(T)}_{\pi}\widehat{\phi}(\pi)\qquad\mu\text{-almost everywhere}.

(3.14)

Thus, we consider
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
be the space of all the distributions
\kappa\in(C_{c}^{\infty})^{\prime}(\Gamma)
for which the convolution operator
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma)\ni f\mapsto f*\kappa
extends to a bounded operator
T_{\kappa}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma)
. Thus, we define the Fourier transform of the distribution
\kappa
by

\widehat{\kappa}(\pi):=A^{(T_{\kappa})}_{\pi},\qquad\text{where }A^{(T_{\kappa})}\text{ is like in }\eqref{convdist}.

Note that the convolution product between a function
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and a distribution
\kappa\in(C_{c}^{\infty}(\Gamma))^{\prime}
is given by

f*\kappa(x):=\langle\kappa,L_{x^{-1}}f^{*}\rangle.

In other words,
\widehat{\kappa}
is defined in such a way that

\mathcal{F}(T_{\kappa}f)(\pi)=\widehat{\kappa}(\pi)\widehat{f}(\pi),\qquad f\in L^{2}(\Gamma).

Over
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
we can define an involution
\kappa^{*}(x):=\overline{\kappa(x^{-1})}
. For
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
, consider the operators
T_{\kappa_{1}},T_{\kappa_{2}}\in B_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
as above and denote by
\kappa_{1}*\kappa_{2}
its kernel. Also we can consider a norm in
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
via the formula:

\|\kappa\|_{\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)}:=\|f\mapsto f*\kappa\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2})(\Gamma)}.

Hence the space
\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)
together with the involution, convolution product, and norm defined above is a von Neumann algebra isomorphic to
\mathcal{B}_{L}(L^{2}(\Gamma))
. In addition, the group Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{K}(\Gamma)\to L^{\infty}(\widehat{\Gamma})
is an isometric isomorphism.

Denote by
\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
be the space of finite complex Borel measures in
\Gamma
. Then we have the following inclusions:

L^{1}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)\subset\mathcal{K}(\Gamma);

this allows us to define the
group Fourier transform
of a finite complex Borel measure
\eta\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
by

(\forall\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}),\qquad\mathcal{F}(\eta)(\pi)=\widehat{\eta}(\pi):=\int_{\Gamma}\pi(x)d\eta(x),

(3.15)

where the above integral is in the sense of Bochner.

4
Recovery of the discrete spectrum in nilpotent Lie groups

Let
\Gamma
be an
d
-dimensional lcsc nilpotent Lie group,
H\subset\Gamma
be an
r
-dimensional lattice
,
r\leq d
, which means that
H
is an
r
-dimensional uniform subgroup of
\Gamma
, satisfying that
\mathsf{exp}^{-1}(H)
is an additive subgroup of
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Also, assume that
L\subset\Gamma
is a lattice containing
H
. From
[
7
, Theorem 5.1.6]
, there exists a Mal’cev basis
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\}
of
\mathfrak{g}
with rational structure constants such that

L=\mathsf{exp}(\mathbb{Z}X_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathsf{exp}(\mathbb{Z}X_{d})\subset\Gamma.

For this basis, we have the natural identifications
\Gamma\cong\phi^{-1}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
L\cong\phi^{-1}(L)=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
under the diffeomorphism
\phi^{-1}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined in (
3.2
). Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\Gamma=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot)
and
L=(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\cdot)
, where the law group is given by the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula. In addition, since
\mathsf{exp}^{-1}(H)
is an additive subgroup of
\mathfrak{g}
, after a rearrangement of the basis elements if it is necessary, we have that there must exist
q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\mathsf{exp}^{-1}(H)=q_{1}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}X_{1}+\ldots+q_{r}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}X_{r}\subset\mathfrak{g}.

Then, after applying the polynomial form of
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\Gamma
given by (
3.3
), we have that there are positive integers
N_{1},\ldots,N_{r}
such that

H=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}N_{i}\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r},

(4.1)

in which the identification is an group homomorphism when restricted to each direct summand.

If
\mathsf{vol}_{r}
denotes the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, and since
\mathsf{vol}(B_{R}^{r})\sim R^{r}
where
B_{R}^{r}:=[-R/2,R/2)^{r}
, we have that there exists a positive constant
\lambda_{r}=\lambda_{r}(H)
such that

\left||H\cap B_{R}^{r}|-\lambda_{r}\mathsf{vol}_{r}\left(B_{R}^{r}\right)\right|=o\left(\mathsf{vol}_{r}(B_{R}^{r})\right)=o(R^{r}),

(4.2)

Write
\delta_{H}:=\sum_{p\in H}\delta_{p}
and keep the notations of the representation theory of lcsc nilpotent Lie groups from
Section

3.3
. Following (
3.15
) and in view of (
4.2
), we define the
(averaged) Fourier transform of
H
by

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{l}):=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H\cap B_{R}^{r}})(\pi_{l})}{R^{r}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\sum_{p\in H\cap B_{R}^{r}}\pi_{l}(p);

(4.3)

more precisely, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{l}\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
and
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
, the following formula holds

\langle\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{l})\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\sum_{p\in H\cap B_{R}^{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\varphi(px)\psi(x)dx.

(4.4)

Let
\xi_{p},p\in H
be i.i.d random vectors defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{T},\mathbb{P})
with
\xi_{p}\sim\xi
and let
H_{\xi}
be the realization set
\{p\cdot\xi_{p}:\ p\in H\}
. In this section, our goal is to obtain the recovery of the Fourier Transform
\widehat{\delta_{H}}
from its random perturbations under suitable moment conditions over the perturbations
\xi_{p}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Under the previous notations, assume that there is a positive number
\varepsilon
such that
\mathbb{E}(|\mathsf{proj}_{r}(\xi)|^{r+\varepsilon})<\infty
, where
\mathsf{proj}_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto the first
r
coordinates. Then almost surely, for every
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})(\pi_{l})=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{l})\mathbb{E}[\pi_{l}(\xi)],

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})
stands for the limit
\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}\cap\left[-R/2,R/2\right)^{d}}\right)
.

In what follows, we are dedicated to the proof of
4.1
. Let
M,N
be positive integers,
N_{i}|N
for every
i=1,\ldots,r
, and consider the inclusions:

N\mathbb{Z}\subset N_{i}\mathbb{Z}\subset\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z},\qquad\text{for }1\leq i\leq r.

(4.6)

By taking the quotient by
N\mathbb{Z}
, we find an embedding of the discrete group

H_{N}=\Gamma^{r}_{N}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}N_{i}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{Z}/\frac{N}{N_{i}}\mathbb{Z}

into

\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\right),

It should be noted that, by the rationality of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{r}
, the rescalings
N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are still lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and thus
H_{N}\subset\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
are indeed finite groups.

To prove
4.1
, we will ”discretize” the random perturbations
p\cdot\xi_{p},p\in H
, restricted to
[-N/2,N/2)_{i=1}^{d}
by approximating them by group elements taking values in a finer and finer subgrid

\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right).

In the limit
M,N\to\infty
, after showing that the DFT converges (in a very precise sense to be stated below) to the usual Fourier transform, we will get the desired result as a consequence of an adequate quantitative recovery of the DFT (see
4.7
).

4.0.1
Discretizing
\xi

With the inclusions
N\mathbb{Z}\subset N_{i}\mathbb{Z}\subset\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathfrak{g}
in mind, for

, we restrict and discretize the maps
\xi_{(\cdot)}:H\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined immediately before

, to obtain
\xi^{(M,N)}_{(\cdot)}:H\times\Omega\to\Gamma^{d}_{M,N}

For

.

\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Gamma^{d}_{M,N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}/N\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Therefore for

we set
\xi_{p^{\prime}}^{(M,N)}(\omega):=v\in\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
defined via the property that the
N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-quotient of

, denoted by

v\cdot\left[-\frac{1}{2M},\frac{1}{2M}\right)^{d}.

.

.

Claim 4.3
.

For each

,

.

4.1
Discrete-to-continuum limits for irreducible representations in nilpotent Lie groups

We start by considering a dual element
l\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, with rational coefficients, being the representative of an orbit
\mathcal{O}_{l}
. Without loss of generality, we write:

l=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{c_{j}}{m_{j}}X_{j}^{*}\qquad\text{with}\qquad c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }\mathsf{gcd}(c_{j},m_{j})=1\text{ for every }j=1,\ldots,d,

(4.7)

where some of the coefficients
c_{j}
could be zero, and denote by
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}_{l}
its polarizing subalgebra; also write
K_{l}:=\mathsf{exp}(\mathfrak{m})
.

By
3.1
, the map
\pi_{l}:\Gamma\to\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H}_{l})
given by

\pi_{l}:=\mathsf{ind}_{K_{l}}^{\Gamma}(\chi_{l}),\quad\text{where}\qquad\chi_{l}(\mathsf{exp}(Y))=e^{2\pi il(Y)},

(4.8)

is a unitary irreducible representation of
\Gamma
and all the elements of
\widehat{\Gamma}
arise in this way; also, we have that
\mathcal{H}_{l}
is isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
, where
s=\mathsf{dim}(\Gamma/K_{l})
. More precisely, the action of
\pi_{l}
over
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
is described as follows: since
K_{l}\subset\Gamma
are Lie groups and
K_{l}
is closed and normal in
\Gamma
, we can consider
\alpha:\Gamma/K_{l}\cong\mathbb{R}^{s}\to\Gamma
be a continuous cross-section for the canonical continuous quotient map
i:\Gamma\to\Gamma/K_{l}
, like at the end of
Section

3.2
. Let
k:\Gamma\to K_{l}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d-s}
and
t:\Gamma\to\alpha(\Gamma/K_{l})\cong\mathbb{R}^{s}
be the continuous functions given by the factorization

x=k(x)t(x).

Then, for
x,y\in\Gamma=(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot)
and
f\in\mathcal{H}_{l}
we have that

(\pi_{l}(x)f)(y)=f(xy)=f(k(xy)t(xy))=\chi_{l}(k)f(t)=e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d-s}\frac{c_{j}k_{j}}{m_{j}}\right)f(t),

(4.9)

with
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
,
t=t(xy)\in\alpha(\Gamma/K_{l})
and where we identify
k=k(xy)\in K_{l}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the vector
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d-s})
. Thus, after identifying
\mathcal{H}_{l}\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
through the isometry
\mathcal{H}_{l}\ni f\mapsto f\circ\alpha\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
, we have that
\pi_{l}
is an irreducible unitary representation acting over
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
, and given by (
4.9
).

Since
K_{l}
is rational, it is not difficult to see that the rescalings
\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
and
N\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
, for
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
, are still lattices in
K_{l}
. Moreover, since
K_{l}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d-s}
is a normal subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the law group given by the polynomial coordinates in (
3.3
) is assumed to have integer coefficients, we obtain that
N\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
is normal in
\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
; therefore, it makes sense to consider the groups
\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
,
\Gamma_{M,N}^{d-s}
and
\Gamma_{M,N}^{s}
as good approximants, in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense, for
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\cdot)
,
K_{l}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d-s}
and
\alpha(\mathbb{R}^{d}/K_{l})\cong\mathbb{R}^{s}
, respectively, for
M,N\gg 1
.

Hence, in the process of approximating
\pi_{l}
by a suitable sequence of irreducible unitary representations, we proceed as follows. Fix positive integers
m|M
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
and consider

c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{d-s})\in(\mathbb{Z}/mM\mathbb{Z})^{d-s}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\mathsf{gcd}(c_{j},mM)=1\text{ for every }j=1,\ldots,d-s.

Then we define the unitary representation
\pi_{M,N,m,c}
of
\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
by:

(\pi_{M,N,m,c}(p)f)(q):=e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d-s}\frac{c_{j}k_{j}(pq)}{m}\right)f(t(pq)),\qquad\text{where }f\in\ell^{2}\left(\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)^{s}\right),

(4.10)

where the vectors

k(p)=(k_{1}(p),\ldots,k_{d-s}(p))\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}\qquad\text{and}\qquad t(p)\in\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}^{s}

in the formula (
4.10
), with
p\in\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
, are taken modulo
m\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
and
m\mathbb{Z}^{s}
, respectively.

From (
4.10
) we get that
\pi_{M,N,m,c}:\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}\to\mathcal{U}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma_{M,m}^{s}))
is an irreducible unitary representation of
\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
. Also observe that, although the map

\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}\times\mathbb{N}\ni(c,m)\mapsto\pi_{M,N,m,c}\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{M,N}^{d},

where
m|M
and the parameter
c
in the index is taken modulo
mM\mathbb{Z}^{d-s}
, probably is not exhaustive and there are many other irreducible representations of
\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}
which are not covered by the formula (
4.10
), the irreducible representations of the form
\pi_{M,N,m,c}
are sufficient to ”approximate”
\pi_{l}
; we develop this fact with more details in
Lemma

4.5
below.

Following
Section

2.2
, consider the Discrete Fourier Transform
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}:\ell^{2}(\Gamma_{M,N}^{d})\to L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{M,N}^{d})
. In particular, for
f\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma_{M,N}^{d})
, the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)
acts over an irreducible representation of the form
\pi_{M,N,m,c}
like in (
4.10
) as follows:

\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)(\pi_{M,N,m,c})=\frac{1}{M^{d}N^{d}}\sum_{p\in\Gamma_{M,N}^{d}}f(p)\pi_{M,N,m,c}(p):\ell^{2}\left(\Gamma_{M,m}^{s}\right)\to\ell^{2}\left(\Gamma_{M,m}^{s}\right).

To be able to pass information from
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})
to
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{X})
we need to use an appropriate discrete-to-continuum approximation, which we define next.

Remark 4.4

(periodization convention)
.

In order to compare functions on a discrete group
\Gamma_{M,N}^{s}
with functions on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
, we use the following convention: in the following, given
M\in\mathbb{N}
and a compactly supported function
\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
with
\mathsf{supp}(\psi)\subset[-M/2,M/2)^{s}
, we still denote its
M\mathbb{Z}^{s}
-periodization by
\psi
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a sequence of linear operators
(T_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
T_{n}\in\mathcal{B}\left(\ell^{2}(\Gamma_{M_{n},m_{n}}^{s})\right)
for
n\geq 1
, with
M_{n},m_{n}\to\infty
, converges to a linear operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s}))
if for every
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
the following holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle T_{n}\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\ell^{2}\left(\Gamma_{M_{n},m_{n}}^{s}\right)}=\langle T\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})},

(4.11)

where for a sufficiently large
n\geq 1
, we identify
\varphi,\psi
with its
m_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{s}
-periodizations like in
Remark

4.4
.

Now, for
H_{N}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}N_{i}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, with
N_{i}|N
for all
i=1,\ldots,r
, consider the realization set
(H_{N})_{\xi^{(M,N)}}=H_{\xi^{(M,N)}}:=\{p\cdot\xi_{p}^{(M,N)}:p\in H_{N}\}
. The key claim to prove
4.1
is that the Fourier Transform of
X
and
X_{\xi}
are weak limits (in the sense of
4.1
) of the Discrete Fourier Transforms
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H})
and
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(M,N)}}})
, respectively.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
l=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}X_{j}^{*}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, fix

, and consider the irreducible unitary representation
\pi_{l}
acting over
\mathcal{H}_{l}\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
given by the formula (
4.9
). For any pair of positive integers

For each

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{d-s}N^{d-r}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\pi_{km,N,m,c_{m}})=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{l})

(4.12)

where the limit for

is taken in the sense of
4.1

Proof.

First we will show, in the sense of
4.1
, that

\begin{split}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{d-s}N^{d-r}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{km,N,m,c_{m}})=\frac{1}{N^{r}}\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{r}}\right)(\pi_{l});\end{split}

(4.13)

then, after taking

we will get the conclusion of

4.5
. For this, fix

and consider two non-zero functions

. Then by definition of the inner product

\begin{split}&(km)^{d-s}N^{d-r}\langle\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\pi_{km,N,m,c_{m}})\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\Gamma_{km,m}^{s})}\\
&=\frac{1}{(km)^{s}N^{r}}\sum_{p\in\Gamma_{km,m}^{s}}(\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H})(\pi_{km,m,c_{m}})\varphi)(p)\overline{\psi(p)}\\

where the limit taken for
m\to\infty
in the sense of
4.1
. Hence the the conclusion of

4.5
follows by the definition of

and
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})
after considering

being the

-periodizations of

.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Under the assumptions of
Lemma

4.5
, suppose there exists a positive number
\varepsilon
such that
\mathbb{E}[|\mathsf{proj}_{r}(\xi)|^{r+\varepsilon}]<\infty
. Then the following limit holds almost surely :

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{d-s}N^{d-r}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(km,N)}}}\right)(\pi_{km,N,m,c_{m}})&=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{X_{\xi}})(\pi_{l}).\end{split}

(4.14)

Proof.

Firstly consider the sets
A_{N},A_{N}^{\prime}\subset X
given by

\begin{split}A_{N}&=\left\{p\in X\cap[-N/2,N/2)_{i=1}^{r}:\ p\cdot\xi_{p}\not\in[-N/2,N/2)_{i=1}^{d}\right\}\\
A_{N}^{\prime}&=\left\{p\in X\setminus[-N/2,N/2)_{i=1}^{r}:\ p\cdot\xi_{p}\in[-N/2,N/2)_{i=1}^{d}\right\}.\end{split}

Since
\mathbb{E}[|\mathsf{proj}_{r}(\xi)|^{r+\varepsilon}]<\infty
, by the same reasoning as Lemma 3.1 in
[
21
]
the following limits hold almost surely:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A_{N}|}{N^{r}}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A_{N}^{\prime}|}{N^{r}}=0.

(4.15)

Like in the limit in
Lemma

4.5
, given a positive integer
N
and
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
we need to estimate the value of:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{N^{r}}\left|\left\langle\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{d}}\right)(\pi_{l})\varphi,\psi\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{s})}-\frac{1}{(km)^{s}}\langle\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(km,N)}}})\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\ell^{2}\left(\Gamma_{km,m}^{s}\right)}\right|\\

(4.16)

where in the second line of (
4.16
), for the first term we have that
k=k(px)=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d-s}),t=t(px),p\in H_{\xi}\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{d}

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{s}}\sum_{p\in H_{\xi}\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{d}}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d-s}\lambda_{j}k_{j}(px)\right)\varphi(t(px))\overline{\psi(x)}dx\\

(4.17)

where

and

and

Since the function

when

goes to
\infty
.
Then from
4.3
, (

4.15

\begin{split}\eqref{2ndlimitDFT}&\leq\frac{1}{N^{r}}\sum_{p\in H\cap\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{r}}\left|\int_{\left[-\frac{N}{2},\frac{N}{2}\right)^{s}}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d-s}\lambda_{j}k(p\cdot\xi_{p}x)\right)\varphi(p\cdot\xi_{p}\cdot x)\overline{\psi(x)}dx\right.\\
&-\left.\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\frac{1}{km}\mathbb{Z}^{s}\\
q\in\left[-\frac{m}{2},\frac{m}{2}\right)^{s}\end{subarray}}e\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d-s}\frac{c_{m}^{(j)}k_{j}(p\cdot\xi_{p}q)}{m}\right)\varphi(p\cdot\xi_{p}^{(km,N)}\cdot q)\frac{\overline{\psi(q)}}{(km)^{s}}\right|+\frac{|S_{N}|}{N^{r}}+\frac{|S_{N}^{\prime}|}{N^{r}}\\

This concludes the proof of

.
∎

5
Examples

In this section, we illustrate with some examples the quantitative and almost sure recovery given by
2.8
and
4.1
. We mainly focus on the two canonical instances of abelian and non-abelian groups, namely, the Euclidean space and the Heisenberg group, where some explicit error bounds are provided. In addition, in order to clarify the computations in the setting of general finite groups, we decide to incorporate the corresponding ones for the Euclidean case.

5.1
Finite abelian groups

We consider a discrete torus
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}:=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}
where
N
is not a prime number, and its subset
H=(M_{1}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ldots\oplus(M_{r}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
for
r\leq d
and for some divisor
M_{i}|N
with
1\leq i\leq r
. The general case for the study in an ambient group
(\mathbb{Z}/N_{1}\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ldots\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/N_{d}\mathbb{Z})
instead
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
follows analogously; we restrict ourself to
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
only to simplify notations and computations. We denote by
\mathcal{F}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})
the Discrete Fourier Transform (see
[
19
, Ch. 2]
for a treatment of the
d=1
case, and the case of general
d
is an easy extension), given by

\mathcal{F}(f)(k)=\widehat{f}(k):=\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}e(p,k)f(p),\quad\text{where}\quad e(p,k)=e_{N}(p,k):=e^{\frac{-2\pi}{N}i\langle p,k\rangle}.

(5.1)

We apply (
5.1
) to the characteristic function

\delta_{H}(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{ if }p\in H,\\[5.69054pt]
0&\text{ else,}\end{array}\right.

and then we see the characteristic function of the dual set
H^{*}
of
H
appears. Precisely, we have

\begin{split}\widehat{\delta}_{H}=\frac{1}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\delta_{H^{*}},\quad\text{with }\quad H^{*}&:=\{k\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}:\ \forall p\in H,\ k\cdot p\equiv 0\ (\text{mod }N)\}\\
&=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\left(\frac{N}{M_{i}}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\right)\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d-r}.\end{split}

Note that
|H|=\frac{N^{r}}{(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}
and
|H^{*}|=(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})N^{d-r}
.

We then consider a random perturbation field of a configuration
H
as above, namely we fix a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{T},\mathbb{P})
and define a collection of
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
-valued random variables
\xi=(\xi_{p})_{p\in H}
, and the
\xi
-perturbed version of
H
will be denoted by
H_{\xi}=H_{\xi}(\omega):=\{p+\xi_{p}(\omega):\ p\in H\}
, which is a random subset of
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
, with
\omega\in\Omega
. We here consider that
\xi_{p},p\in H
are i.i.d. random variables.

The main quantitative recovery result in the framework of finite groups given by
2.8
reads as follows.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\xi_{p},p\in H
be i.i.d. random variables with
\xi_{p}\sim\xi
and
C:=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi,\lambda)]<\infty
, and assume that
\varepsilon>\sqrt{\frac{72(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}{N^{r}}}
and
\frac{N^{r}}{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}>12
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{72(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}{\varepsilon^{2}N^{r}}
we have

\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}\left|\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi}}}(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[e(\xi,\lambda)\right]\ \widehat{\delta_{H}}(\lambda)\right|\leq\frac{1}{|H^{*}|}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{|H|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(5.2)

Observe that
C\leq 4
due to the boundedness of
e(\cdot,\cdot)
. The regime
(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})^{1/r}\ll N
and the choice
\left(\frac{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}\right)^{1/2}\ll\varepsilon\lesssim 1
allow to obtain ”good recovery with small error”, and is thus the most interesting for recovery problems, in the following sense:

Corollary 5.2
.

Consider a sequence of
N^{(n)},M^{(n)}_{1},\ldots M_{r}^{(n)}
with corresponding torii
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}=\mathbb{T}_{N^{(n)}}^{d}
and subsets
H=H^{(n)}
, and
\xi_{p}^{(n)},p\in H^{(n)}
satisfying the hypotheses of
5.1
, with
\xi_{p}^{(n)}
independent from each other for different values of
n
.

If we have

(M_{1}^{(n)}\ldots M_{1}^{(n)})^{1/r}\ll N^{(n)},

then almost surely

\left\|\widehat{\delta_{H_{\xi^{(n)}}}}-\mathbb{E}[e(\xi^{(n)},\cdot)]\widehat{\delta_{H^{(n)}}}\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})}\to 0.

(5.3)

Given definition (
5.1
), it is natural in the pursuit of a bound (
5.2
) to consider the functions
f,g:H\times\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by as

f(p,\lambda):=e(p,\lambda),\quad g(p,\lambda):=\overline{e(\xi_{p},\lambda)-\mathbb{E}[e(\xi_{p},\lambda)]]}

(5.4)

Note that
g
is a random function, as it depends on the events
\omega
through the
\xi_{p}
’s, whereas
f
is deterministic. With definition (
5.4
) we restate the desired bound (
5.2
) as

\mathbb{P}\left\{\frac{\left\|\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}\right\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})}}{N^{d}}>\frac{1}{|H^{*}|}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{|H|}\right)^{1/2}\right\}\leq\frac{72(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}{\varepsilon^{2}N^{r}},

(5.5)

where we also use the notation
f_{\lambda}:=f(\cdot,\lambda),g_{\lambda}:=g(\cdot,\lambda)
. We note that
\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}=f_{\lambda}*g_{\lambda}(0)
, in which we use the convolution of functions
\phi,\psi
over
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
, defined by

\phi*\psi(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}\phi(y)\psi(x-y).

The function
f_{\lambda}*g_{\lambda}
is supported on
H+H=H
(the sum taking place in the group
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}
) and therefore

\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}=\sum_{y\in H}f_{\lambda}*g_{\lambda}(y)\delta_{0}(y),

(5.6)

where
\delta_{0}:=\delta_{H}
for the special choice
H=\{0\}
.

We extend the functions
f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}
to
F_{\lambda},G_{\lambda}:\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by zero outside
H
. Then we have

\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}=\langle F_{\lambda},G_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})},\quad F_{\lambda}*G_{\lambda}(y)=\delta_{H}(y)f_{\lambda}*g_{\lambda}(y).

With our normalizations, we check that

\displaystyle\widehat{F*G}(k)=\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}(F*G)(q)e(q,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{p,q\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}F(p)G(q-p)e(p,k)e(q-p,k)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{d}\widehat{F}(k)\widehat{G}(k),

and that

\displaystyle\langle F,G\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}F(p)\overline{G(p)}

(5.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\\
k\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\end{subarray}}F(p)e(p,k)\overline{G(p)e(p,k)}=\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\\
k\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\end{subarray}}F(p)e(p,k)\overline{G(p^{\prime})e(p^{\prime},k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N^{d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}\widehat{F}(k)\overline{\widehat{G}(k)}=N^{d}\langle\widehat{F},\widehat{G}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N})}.

Combining (
5.6
), (
5.7
) and (
5.8
), we get (observing that
\widehat{\delta}_{0}\equiv N^{-d}
by our definition)

\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}HX)}=\langle F_{\lambda}*G_{\lambda},\delta_{0}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d})}=N^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}\widehat{F_{\lambda}}(x)\overline{\widehat{G_{\lambda}}(x)}.

Note that, for
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
:

\widehat{F_{\lambda}}(x)=\frac{1}{N^{d}}\sum_{p\in H}e(p,\lambda)e(p,x)=\widehat{\delta_{H}}(\lambda+x)=\frac{1}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\delta_{H^{*}-\lambda}(x).

(5.9)

Therefore we get

\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}=\frac{N^{r}}{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}\sum_{y\in H^{*}-\lambda}\overline{\widehat{G}_{\lambda}(y)}.

(5.10)

We note that the
r=d
case of (
5.10
) simplifies continuum-case results such as
[
17
, Theorem A.4]
, or of
[
21
, Appendix]
.

We now study the terms
\widehat{G_{\lambda}}(y)
from (
5.10
). Denoting
\widetilde{\varphi}(x):=\overline{\varphi(-x)}
, we rewrite the autocorrelation
\gamma_{\lambda}
of the random functions
g(\lambda,q)=g_{\lambda}(q)
as follows:

\gamma_{\lambda}(q):=\frac{1}{|H|}G_{\lambda}*\widetilde{G_{\lambda}}(q)=\frac{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}G_{\lambda}*\widetilde{G_{\lambda}}(q)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}\sum_{p\in H}|g_{\lambda}(p)|^{2}&\text{ if }q=0,\\[8.53581pt]
\frac{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}\sum_{p\in H}g_{\lambda}(p)\overline{g_{\lambda}(p+q)}&\text{ else.}\end{array}\right.

(5.11)

From (
5.7
) we also obtain, for
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
,

|\widehat{G_{\lambda}}|^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{N^{d}}\widehat{G_{\lambda}*\widetilde{G_{\lambda}}}(x)=\frac{1}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\widehat{\gamma_{\lambda}}(x).

(5.12)

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>\left(\frac{72M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}\right)^{1/2}

and assume that
\frac{N^{r}}{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}>12
.
Under our hypotheses on
\xi
, with probability at least
1-\frac{72}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}
there holds

\left|\gamma_{\lambda}(q)-\mathsf{Var}\left[e(\xi,\lambda)\right]\delta_{0}(q)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

(5.13)

End of proof of
5.1
:

Consider the small probability event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
for which (
5.13
) holds. Conditioned on
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}
, we use the pointwise bound of Proposition (
5.3
), triangular inequality over
H
and (
5.12
), obtaining:

\left|N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})|\widehat{G_{\lambda}}(x)|^{2}-\mathsf{Var}\left[e(\xi,\lambda)\right]\widehat{\delta_{0}}(x)\right|\leq\varepsilon\frac{|H|}{N^{d}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}.

(5.14)

By substituting (
5.14
) in (
5.10
) we obtain the following estimation for
\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{l^{2}(H)}
valid in
\Omega\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
:

\displaystyle\frac{|\langle f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{\ell^{2}(H)}|}{N^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{1}\ldots M_{r}}\frac{1}{N^{d-r}}\sum_{y\in H^{*}-\lambda}|\widehat{G_{\lambda}(y)}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{\frac{3}{2}(d-r)}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\sum_{y\in H^{*}-\lambda}\left(\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi,\lambda)]\widehat{\delta_{0}}(y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|H^{*}|}{N^{\frac{3}{2}(d-r)}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi,\lambda)]}{N^{d}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{\frac{d-r}{2}}(M_{1}\dots M_{r})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi,\lambda)]}{N^{d}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\ldots M_{r})}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{d-r}(M_{1}\dots M_{r})}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{CM_{1}\ldots M_{r}}{N^{r}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|H^{*}|}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{C}{|H|}\right)^{1/2}.

The claimed bound (
5.5
) follows directly, completing the proof of
5.1
.
∎

5.2
Almost sure recovery of discrete subgroups in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a limit case

In this section, we see how the quantitative recovery given by
5.1
implies almost sure recovery of the Fourier spectrum for discrete subgroups of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By a (
r
-dimensional) discrete subgroup, we mean a subgroup
X\leq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form
H=v_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\ldots\oplus v_{r}\mathbb{Z}
, where
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r\leq d
.

By a (complex) measure, we mean a linear functional on the set of compactly supported
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that for every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is a positive constant
M_{K}
such that

|\mu(f)|\leq M_{k}\|f\|_{\infty},

for every
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported on
K
, and where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the supremum norm in
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. By the Riesz Representation Theorem, there is an equivalence between this definition of measure and the classical measure-theoretic concept of regular Radon measure.

Observe that in this case, the co-adjoint map
\mathsf{Ad}^{*}
reduces to the identity, and the space of coadjoint orbits
\mathfrak{g}/\mathsf{Ad}^{*}G
is equals to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, the elements of
\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
correspond to the
1
-dimensional irreducible representations

\chi_{\lambda}:=e^{-2\pi i\langle\cdot,\lambda\rangle},\qquad\text{where }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

which act naturally over
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; thus, in our case we have that
s:=\mathsf{dim}(G/K_{\lambda})=1
.

For a finite measure
\mu
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, its Fourier transform is defined by

\widehat{\mu}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle x,\lambda\rangle}d\mu(x),\qquad\text{for }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
H
being an
r
-dimensional discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
r\leq d
, we define its
(averaged) Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})
as follows

\begin{split}(\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\lambda)&:=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H\cap[-R/2,R/2)^{d}})(\lambda)}{R^{d}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\sum_{p\in H\cap[-R/2,R/2)^{d}}e^{-2\pi i\langle p,\lambda\rangle}\end{split}

(5.15)

For an
r
-dimensional discrete subgroup
H
, consider i.i.d random vectors
(\xi_{p})_{p\in H}
defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{T},\mathbb{P})
, and the corresponding realization set
X_{\xi}=\{p+\xi_{p}:\ p\in H\}
.
We define the Fourier Transform of
H_{\xi}
in duality with
C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
like in (
5.15
) by

\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}}\right):=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{r}}\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}\cap\left[-R/2,R/2\right)^{d}}\right).

(5.16)

Thus, in this section our main result is the almost sure recovery of
H
from its random perturbations under suitable moment conditions of the perturbations
\xi_{p}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Assume that
1\leq r\leq d
, that
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an
r
-dimensional discrete subgroup, and
\varepsilon>0
is such that
\mathbb{E}[|\pi_{r}(\xi)|^{r+\varepsilon}]<\infty
, where
\pi_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto the first
r
coordinates.
Then almost surely, there holds:

\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,\cdot\rangle}\right)\mathcal{F}\left(\delta_{H}\right).

We will restrict ourselves to the case
H=\mathbb{Z}^{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
r\leq d
; the case of more general discrete subgroups
H=v_{1}\mathbb{Z}\oplus\ldots\oplus v_{r}\mathbb{Z}
then follows by a change of basis via a suitable
A\in GL(\mathbb{R}^{d})
that sends
\{e_{1}\ldots,e_{r}\}
to
\{v_{1},\ldots v_{r}\}
, and is left to the reader.

For
M,N
being positive integers, consider the torus

H_{N}=\mathbb{T}^{r}_{N}:=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{r}\subset\mathbb{T}_{M,N}^{d}:=\left(\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}\right)/N\mathbb{Z}\right)^{d}.

5.2.1
Rescaled DFT

Now we check how the above rescaling is inherited to the discrete Fourier transforms of interest to us.
Consider the torii
\mathbb{T}_{MN}^{d}:=(\mathbb{Z}/MN\mathbb{Z})^{d}
and
\widetilde{H}:=(M\mathbb{Z}/NM\mathbb{Z})^{r}\simeq(M\mathbb{Z}/NM\mathbb{Z})^{r}\times\{0\}^{d-r}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{MN}^{d}
first. Then
NM,M
plays the role of
N,M
of the previous section.
In this case
\widetilde{H}^{*}=(N\mathbb{Z}/NM\mathbb{Z})^{r}\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/MN\mathbb{Z})^{d-r}
, and we have
|\widetilde{H}|=N^{r},|\widetilde{H}^{*}|=M^{d}N^{d-r}
.
For the case
r=d
, the continuum non-compact version of the problem is that in which
\widetilde{H}
is replaced by a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\widetilde{H^{*}}
is replaced by its dual lattice
\Lambda^{*}
, whose density satisfies
\mathrm{dens}(\Lambda)\mathrm{dens}(\Lambda^{*})=1
.

In the setting of
Section

5.1
(with the roles of
N,M_{1},\dots,M_{r}
replaced here by
MN,M,\dots,M
), the DFT of
f:\mathbb{T}_{MN}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by (
5.1
) as

\mathcal{F}_{MN}(f)(k):=\frac{1}{M^{d}N^{d}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{T}_{MN}^{d}}e^{\frac{-2\pi i}{MN}p\cdot k}f(p)\qquad\text{for }k\in\mathbb{T}_{MN}^{d},

(5.17)

For algebraic considerations (i.e., without including metric aspects), definition (
5.17
) would suffice.
However, our study is based on the fact that, as
M\to\infty
, the metric spaces
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N}
approximate better and better
\mathbb{R}^{d}/N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(since
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N}
is a
1/M
-net of
\mathbb{R}^{d}/N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, these spaces converge to each other in Gromov-Hausdorff sense, for example).
Thus we are led to define

e_{M,N}(p,k):=e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{N}\langle p,k\rangle},\quad p\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N}.

(5.18)

At the level of Fourier-dual spaces, we observe that the compact group
\mathbb{R}^{d}/N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is naturally in duality with
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
:
functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}/N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
correspond to
N\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-periodic functions over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the smallest frequency that can be sampled via these functions scales like
1/N
.
In other words, the Pontryagin dual of
\mathbb{T}_{M,N}^{d}
can be identified with
\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}
, where the family of characters is given by
e_{M,N}(\cdot,Nk^{\prime}),\ k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}
.
We then use the definition
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)=\mathcal{F}_{MN}\left(f\circ(\mathsf{dil}_{1/M})^{-1}\right)\circ\mathsf{dil}_{1/N}
, where for
a>0
the map
\mathsf{dil}_{a}:\mathbb{T}^{d}_{MN}\to a\mathbb{T}^{d}_{MN}
is defined by
\mathsf{dil}_{a}(p)=ap
.
Explicitly, we have then

\mathcal{F}_{M,N}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N,M}),\quad\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)(k^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{M^{d}N^{d}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N}}e_{M,N}(p,Nk^{\prime})f(p).

(5.19)

If
r=d
, one can check that
(\mathcal{F}_{M,N})^{-1}(g)=\mathcal{F}_{N,M}(\widetilde{g})
where
\widetilde{g}(k):=\overline{g(-k)}
.

As we showed before,
5.4
is a consequence of the fact that the Fourier transforms of the Dirac measures supported on
H=\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
H_{\xi}\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\xi}
correspond to the (normalized) limit of the DFT of
H_{N}
and
H_{\xi^{(M,N)}}
.
Lemma

5.5
below is the adapted version of
Lemma

4.5
in the Euclidean setting, where
s=\mathsf{dim}(G/K_{l})=1
, with the definitions of
Section

3.1
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(\xi_{p})_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}}
be i.i.d random vectors and let
\varepsilon>0
be such that
\mathbb{E}[|\pi_{r}(\xi)|^{r+\varepsilon}]<\infty
, where
\pi_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{r}
is the orthogonal projection onto the first
r
coordinates.
Let
H_{N}:=(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{r}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}_{M,N}
and let
\xi^{(M,N)}
be as in Subsection
4.0.1
.
Then almost surely, there holds:

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}M^{d-1}N^{d-r}\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{r}})\ \text{ and }\ \lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}M^{d-1}N^{d-r}\mathcal{F}_{M,N}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi}}\right)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{r}_{\xi}}),\end{split}

(5.20)

where the limit for
M\to\infty
is taken in the sense of
4.1
, and the limit with respect to

N\to\infty
is in the classical sense.

To prove the recovery result of
5.4
, we re-express
5.1
and
Corollary

5.2
for the torii
\mathbb{T}_{M,N}^{d}
and
X=\mathbb{T}_{N}^{r}
. To do this we replace
(N,M_{1},\dots,M_{r})\mapsto(NM,M,\dots,M)
obtaining the following result:

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\xi_{p},p\in H_{N}=\mathbb{T}_{N}^{r}
be i.i.d. random variables with
\xi_{p}\sim\xi
satisfying
C:=\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N,M}}\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi,\lambda)]<\infty
, and assume that
N^{r}>12
and
\varepsilon>\sqrt{\frac{72}{N^{r}}}
.
Then with probability at least
1-\frac{72}{\varepsilon^{2}N^{r}}
we have

\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{(H_{N})_{\xi}})(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[e(\xi,\lambda)\right]\ \mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\lambda)\right|\leq\frac{1}{M^{d}N^{d-r}}\sqrt{\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{N^{r}}}.

(5.21)

Proof of
5.4
:

Following
Lemma

5.5
, we need an estimation for the error in
5.6
, after multiplying the left-hand side of (
5.5
) by
M^{d-1}N^{d-r}
. Let
\xi^{(M,N)}
be as in the Subsection
4.0.1
and
\varepsilon>\sqrt{\frac{72}{N^{r}}}
with
N^{r}>12
. From (
5.5
), with probability at least
1-\frac{72}{\varepsilon^{2}N^{r}}
we get:

\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}}\left|M^{d-1}N^{d-r}\left(\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(M,N)}}})(\lambda)-\mathbb{E}\left[e(\xi^{(M,N)},\lambda)\right]\ \mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\lambda)\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{M}\sqrt{\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{N^{r}}},

(5.22)

where we use the fact that
\max_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d}}\mathsf{Var}[e(\xi^{(M,N)},\lambda)]\leq 4
.
We now bound (
5.22
) in the regime where
\varepsilon\to 0
and
M,N\to\infty
. We require
N^{-r/2}\ll\varepsilon
, so that the event (
5.22
) has probability tending to
1
. Hence, by letting
M\to\infty
and
\varepsilon\to 0
such that
\varepsilon N^{r}\gg 1
we get that almost surely

\left\|M^{d-1}N^{d-r}\left(\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(M,N)}}})-\mathbb{E}[e(\xi^{(M,N)},\cdot)]\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})\right)\right\|_{\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{N,M}^{d})}\to 0.

(5.23)

5.4
follows from it and
Lemma

5.5
via triangle inequality and from
4.3
.
∎

5.3
The Heisenberg group case

In this section, for our purposes we consider the (polarized) Heisenberg group
\Gamma
being the set
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the group law

(x,y,z)\cdot(u,v,w):=\left(x+u,y+v,z+w+xv\right).

(5.24)

Similarly, for a number field
F
, we define
\Gamma(F):=\{(x,y,z)\in\Gamma:\ x,y,z\in F\}
, with group operation as in (
5.24
). We consider especially
H_{N}=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
as the finite Heisenberg group with coefficients in the field
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, with sum and product operations as usual; observe that
H_{N}\cong\Gamma(\mathbb{Z})/\Gamma(N\mathbb{Z})
. Note that the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the Haar measure in
\Gamma
. Throughout this section, for
R>0
we denote

B_{R}:=[-R/2,R/2)^{3}.

Consider the usual Heisenberg group
\Gamma^{\prime}=(\mathbb{R}^{3},\odot)
with the group operation given by

(x,y,z)\odot(u,v,w):=\left(x+u,y+v,z+w+\frac{1}{2}(xv-yu)\right).

(5.25)

The Fourier Analysis in
\Gamma^{\prime}
is well-understood. Indeed, denote by
\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
be the group of unitary operators acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. By the Stone-von Neumann Theorem (see, for instance
[
10
, Theorem 6.49]
), all the irreducible unitary representations of
\Gamma^{\prime}
(under equivalence) are determined by one of the following:

1.

The representations
\pi^{\prime}_{\lambda}:\Gamma^{\prime}\to\mathcal{U}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
,
\lambda\neq 0
, where for
(x,y,z)\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, the operator
\pi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x,y,z)
acts on a function
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
via norm-preserving operations, explicitly given by the formula

\pi^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x,y,z)\varphi(u):=e^{2\pi i\lambda z+\pi i\lambda xy}e^{2\pi i\lambda yu}\varphi(u+x),\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}.

2.

The 1-dimensional irreducible unitary representations
\pi^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}:\Gamma^{\prime}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, given by

\pi^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,z)=e^{2\pi i(\alpha x+\beta y)}.

The connection between
\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\Gamma
is given by the isomorphism
\phi:\Gamma^{\prime}\to\Gamma
:

\phi(x,y,z):=\left(x,y,z+\frac{1}{2}xy\right).

This isomorphism, together with the classification of elements in
\widehat{\Gamma^{\prime}}
, induces a bijection at the level of irreducible unitary representations which maps
\pi^{\prime}_{\lambda},\pi^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}\in\widehat{\Gamma^{\prime}}
to the representations
\pi_{\lambda},\pi_{\alpha,\beta}\in\widehat{\Gamma}
given by

\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z):=\pi^{\prime}_{\lambda}\left(x,y,z-\frac{1}{2}xy\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,z):=\pi^{\prime}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(x,y,z-\frac{1}{2}xy\right),

respectively. More precisely, the irreducible representations
\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)
and
\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,z)
act over a function
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by:

\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)\varphi(u)=e^{2\pi i\lambda z}e^{2\pi i\lambda yu}\varphi(u+x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,z)=e^{2\pi i(\alpha x+\beta y)}.

(5.26)

Following (
4.3
), for the lattice
H=(\mathbb{Z}^{3},\cdot)\subset\Gamma
we define its (averaged) Fourier Transform
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})
by the following limit in the weak-sense:

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\lambda):=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H\cap B_{R}})(\pi_{\lambda})}{R^{3}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in H\cap B_{R}}\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z)

(5.27)

and

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\alpha,\beta):=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H\cap B_{R}})(\pi_{\alpha,\beta})}{R^{3}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in H\cap B_{R}}\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y,z).

(5.28)

More precisely, the limit (
5.27
) is defined in duality with functions with compact support, namely, for every
\varphi,\psi\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R})
there holds

\langle\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\lambda)\varphi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in H\cap B_{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2\pi i\lambda(yu+z)}\varphi(x+u)\overline{\psi(u)}du;

whereas for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in H\cap B_{R}}e^{2\pi i(\alpha x+\beta y)}.

For the lattice
H=(\mathbb{Z}^{3},\cdot)
,
consider the i.i.d random vectors
(\xi_{p})_{p\in H}
defined in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{T},\mathbb{P})
with common distribution
\xi
, and the randomly perturbed set

H_{\xi}=\{p\cdot\xi_{p}:\ p\in H\}\subset\Gamma.

With these notations, our main recovery result in the Heisenberg group reads as follows.

Theorem 5.7
.

Under the previous notations, assume that there is a positive number
\varepsilon
such that
\mathbb{E}(|\xi|^{3+\varepsilon})<\infty
. Then almost surely, for every non-zero
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, there holds:

\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})(\lambda)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\lambda)\mathbb{E}[\pi_{\lambda}(\xi)]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})(\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})(\alpha,\beta)\mathbb{E}[\pi_{\alpha,\beta}(\xi)].

Like in the Euclidean case,
5.7
will be a consequence of the approximate recovery given by
2.8
for the finite Heisenberg groups

\Gamma_{M,N}:=\Gamma\left(\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}\right)/N\mathbb{Z}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{N}:=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}).

Observe that
|\Gamma_{M,N}|=M^{3}N^{3}
and
|H_{N}|=N^{3}
.

Irreducible representations in
H_{n}
and scaling reasoning:
In this case we take
m|n
and consider

x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z},\quad a,b\in\mathbb{Z}/\frac{n}{m}\mathbb{Z},\quad c\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\text{ with }\mathsf{gcd}(c,m)=1,\quad f:\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C},

and denoting
e(x):=e^{2\pi ix}
we have representation formula

\pi_{a,b,c}(x,y,z)f(j)=e\left(\frac{ax+by}{n}\right)e\left(\frac{c(yj+z)}{m}\right)f(j+x).

Observe that by the definition of
\mathsf{gcd}
, the only case when
c=0
corresponds to
m=1
; in this case,
\pi_{a,b,0}
yields the
1
-dimensional representation:

\pi_{a,b}(x,y,z):=\pi_{a,b,0}(x,y,z)=e\left(\frac{ax+by}{n}\right).

As we pass to the continuum we rescale
H_{n}\mapsto\Gamma_{M,N}
with
n=MN
, and we rescale the representations
\pi_{a,b,c}
in order to recover continuum representations
\pi_{\lambda},\pi_{\alpha,\beta}
as in (
5.27
), (
5.28
).

Irreducible unitary representations in
\Gamma_{M,N}
:
To obtain the quantitative recovery for the finite Heisenberg group
\Gamma_{M,N}
, we need a description of its unitary irreducible representations.

Fix
m|M
,
c\in\mathbb{Z}/mM\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathsf{gcd}(c,Mm)=1
, and
a,b\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/\frac{M}{m}\mathbb{Z}
. Define the unitary irreducible representation
\pi_{a,b,c,m}:\Gamma_{M,N}\to\mathcal{U}\left(\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)\right)
by

\pi_{a,b,c,m}(x,y,z)f(j):=e\left(ax+by\right)e\left(\frac{c(yj+z)}{m}\right)f(j+x),\qquad\text{where }f\in\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right).

(5.29)

Following
Section

2.3
, the (normalized) Discrete Fourier Transform of a function
f\in\ell^{2}\left(\Gamma_{M,N}\right)
associates to every
a,b\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/\frac{M}{m}\mathbb{Z}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}/mM\mathbb{Z}
the operator over
\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)
given by:

\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)(a,b,c):=\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(f)(\pi_{a,b,c,m})=\frac{1}{M^{3}N^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in\Gamma_{M,N}}f(x,y,z)\pi_{a,b,c,m}(x,y,z);

(5.30)

We point out that formula (
5.30
) only works in duality with continuous functions supported on
\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{M}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)
. Since the Fourier Transform
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})
defined as in (
5.27
) and (
5.28
) acts in duality with compactly supported functions, to establish a limit from
\mathcal{F}_{M,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})
to
\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})
we require the discrete-to-continuum weak-limit in
4.1
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\lambda
be a non-zero real number and
k\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. For every positive integer
m
, let
\Gamma_{km,N}=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{km}\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\right)
, and let
H=(\mathbb{Z}^{3},\cdot)
and
H_{N}:=\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\leq\Gamma_{km,N}
. Let
a,b\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
and
c_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}/km^{2}\mathbb{Z}
be a sequence such that
\mathsf{gcd}(c_{m},km^{2})=1
and
c_{m}/m\to\lambda
when
m\to\infty
, then the following limit holds

\begin{split}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\pi_{a,b,c_{m},m})=\frac{1}{N^{3}}\sum_{(x,y,z)\in H\cap B_{N}}\pi_{a,b}(x,y,z)\pi_{\lambda}(x,y,z).\end{split}

(5.31)

In particular, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha,\beta\in[-k/2,k/2]
, and let
a_{N},b_{N}\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
such that
a_{N}\to\alpha
and
b_{N}\to\beta
when
N\to\infty
. Then the following limits hold

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\pi_{0,0,c_{m},m})&=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})\left(\lambda\right)\\
\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})(\pi_{a_{N},b_{N},0,m})&=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H})\left(\alpha,\beta\right),\end{split}

(5.32)

where all the limits for
m\to\infty
are in the sense of
4.1
and the limit with respect to
N
is weak in the classical sense.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
large enough such that
\lambda\in[-m_{0}/2,m_{0}/2]
.
Consider
a_{N},b_{N}\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
and
c_{m}
be as in
Lemma

5.8
. Let
\xi^{(km,N)}
be as in
Section

4.0.1
and suppose there exists a positive number
\varepsilon
such that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{3+\varepsilon}]<\infty
. Then the following weak limits hold almost surely :

\begin{split}\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(km,N)}}}\right)(\pi_{0,0,c_{m},m})&=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})(\lambda)\\
\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}(km)^{2}\mathcal{F}_{km,N}\left(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(km,N)}}}\right)(\pi_{a_{N},b_{N},0,m})&=\mathcal{F}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})(\alpha,\beta).\end{split}

(5.33)

The last ingredient to prove the main recovery result on the Heisenberg group is that the error given by
2.8
for finite Heisenberg groups converges to zero when considering a sequence of discrete Heisenberg-torii
\Gamma_{M,N}
, for
M,N\to\infty
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
N,m,k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider finite Heisenberg groups
\Gamma_{km,N}
and
H_{N}
as before. Let
\xi_{p}^{(N)}=\xi_{p},p\in H_{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
satisfying the hypotheses of
2.8
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}|H|}
for some suitable positive constant
C
there holds:

\left\|\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})-\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H})\mathbb{E}(\xi(\cdot))\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{km,N})}\leq\frac{(\varepsilon+4C^{2})N^{7}}{(km)^{5}}.

(5.34)

Proof.

From
2.8
and since for the finite Heisenberg groups
\Gamma_{km,N}
there holds that
\max\{d_{\pi}:\ \pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{km,N}\}=kmN=|\Gamma_{km,N}|^{1/3}
, then for
\frac{C^{\prime}}{|H|}<\varepsilon^{2}
we get:

\begin{split}\left\|\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi}})-\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H})\mathbb{E}(\xi(\cdot))\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\widehat{\Gamma}_{km,N})}&\leq\frac{|H|^{4}}{|\Gamma_{km,N}|^{2}}\left(\varepsilon+\frac{4C^{2}}{|H|}\right)\max_{\pi\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{km,N}}d_{\pi}\\
&=\frac{\varepsilon N^{7}}{(km)^{5}}+\frac{4C^{2}N^{4}}{(km)^{5}}.\end{split}

(5.35)

To conclude, it suffices to use the fact that
\frac{N^{4}}{(km)^{5}}\leq\frac{N^{7}}{(km)^{5}}
.
∎

Hence, from (
4.20
), (
4.21
) and
5.10
we get the next result which in fact implies (
4.19
).

Corollary 5.11
.

Under the hypotheses and notations of
5.10
, let
\xi_{p}^{(km,N)},p\in H_{N}
as in (
4.0.1
).
Then, with probability at least
1-\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}|H|}
, for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
a,b\in\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}
and every sequence
c_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}/km^{2}\mathbb{Z}
,
\mathsf{gcd}(c_{m},km^{2})=1
for which
c_{m}/m\to\lambda
when
m\to\infty
, there holds that:

(km)^{2}\left\|\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{\xi^{(km,N)}}})(\pi)\varphi-\mathcal{F}_{km,N}(\delta_{H_{N}})\mathbb{E}(\pi(\xi^{(km,N)}))\varphi\right\|_{\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{km}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)}\longrightarrow 0,

where
\pi
is of the form
\pi_{0,0,c_{m},m}
or
\pi_{a,b,0,m}
and
\phi\in\ell^{2}\left(\frac{1}{km}\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\right)
.

Proof of
5.7
.

It is a direct application of
Lemma

5.8
,
Lemma

5.9

Corollary

5.11
.
