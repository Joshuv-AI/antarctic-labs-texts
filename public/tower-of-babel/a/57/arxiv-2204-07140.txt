De-projecting the EFThedron
Li-Yuan Chiang, Yu-tin Huang, Laurentiu Rodina, He-Chen Weng

Abstract
The space of Wilson coefficients of EFT that can be UV completed into consistent theories was recently shown to be described analytically by a positive geometry, termed the EFThedron. However, this geometry, as well as complementary numerical methods of semi-definite programming, have so far focused on the positivity of the partial wave expansion, which allows bounding only ratios of couplings. In this paper we describe how the unitarity upper bound of the partial waves can be incorporated. This new problem can be formulated in terms of the well known L L -moment problem, which we generalize and solve from a geometrical perspective. We find the non-projective generalization of the EFThedron has an infinite number of non-linear facets, which in some cases have remarkably simple descriptions. We use these results to derive bounds on single couplings, finding that the leading derivative operators are bounded by unity, when normalized by the cut-off scale and loop factors. For general operators of mass dimension 2 ​ k 2k we find the upper bound is heavily suppressed at large k k , with an 1 / k 1/k fall-off.

M(s,t)=s^{\frac{D{-}4}{2}}\sum_{\ell=even}n^{D}_{\ell}f_{\ell}(s)P^{D}_{\ell}\left(1+\frac{2t}{s}\right)\,,

\displaystyle g_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi(q!)}\partial^{q}_{t}\left(\int_{M^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{s^{\prime{k{-}q{+}1}}}{+}\int_{-M^{2}{-}t}^{\infty}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{s^{\prime{k{-}q{+}1}}}\right){\rm Im}[M(s^{\prime},t)]

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{\ell}\;n^{D}_{\ell}\lambda^{D}_{\ell,k,q}\,\int_{M^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{ds^{\prime}}{s^{\prime{k{+}1}}}\;\rho_{\ell}(s^{\prime})\;,

Ignoring the upper bound on
\rho(z)
first, the space of couplings can be viewed as a projective positive geometry, defined by the “moment problem”

;

can be expressed as

a_{k}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(z)z^{k-1}dz,\quad\rho(z)\geq 0\,.

(9)

The solution is that the Hankel matrices

H=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}&\cdots\\
a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}&\\

(10)

a_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell}\lambda_{\ell,k,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k-1}dz,\quad\rho(z)\geq 0\,.

(11)

In

Chiang:2021ziz

\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
One can alternatively view eq.(
11

Since the boundaries of single moments are usually much easier to establish, the problem is reduced to computing the Minkowski sum of individually simple geometrical objects. This is the approach we will take in this paper.

2
De-projection of convex hulls

As reviewed in the previous section, the dispersive representation of EFT coefficients generally takes the form

a_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}v_{\ell,k,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k-1}dz,\quad 0\leq\rho_{\ell}(z)\leq 2\,.

(14)

When we neglect the upper bound on
\rho_{\ell}
, the space for allowed
a_{k,q}
is projective and one can only bound the ratios of
a_{k,q}
. The corresponding projective space is characterized by the convex hull of product moments as described in
Arkani-Hamed:2020blm
;
Chiang:2021ziz
.

To incorporate the effects of the upper bound, we will view the geometry as the Minkowski sum of convex hulls. To illustrate this point, we will first consider a simplified version of eq.(
14
) where the coefficients
v_{\ell,k,q}
are independent of
k
, and the vectors

{\bf v}_{\ell}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\ell,q_{1}}\\
v_{\ell,q_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
v_{\ell,q_{D}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(15)

are cyclically ordered by
\ell
in the sense that,

\textrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf v}_{i_{1}}&{\bf v}_{i_{2}}&{\bf v}_{i_{3}}&\ldots\end{pmatrix}\equiv\langle i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\cdots i_{D}\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}\cdots<i_{D}\,.

(16)

We begin with the space of couplings with equal
k
(“same mass-dimension”),

(a_{k,q_{1}},a_{k,q_{2}},\cdots)\,,

(17)

whose projective geometry is simply that of a cyclic polytope. We will show that the “de-projected” space can be constructed by taking the Minkowski sum of line segments, resulting in a convex polytope whose vertices are given by linear sums of successive
{\bf v}_{\ell}
s.

This result will allow us to derive the boundaries for a closely related geometry: the
L
-moment problem. This concerns the space of
a_{k}
given by

a_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k-1}dz,\quad 0\leq\rho_{\ell}(z)\leq L\,.

(18)

The limit
L\rightarrow\infty
corresponds to the Hausdorff moment problem, whose solution is given by the positivity of Hankel matrices constructed from
a_{k}
. We will derive the solutions for finite
L
.

2.1
Minkowski sums

In this section we review some basic notions related to Minkowski sums.
Let us consider two regions
A,B
with each point inside the regions defined by a vector. The Minkowski sum of the two regions is then given by all possible pairs of vectors, one from
A
and one from
B
, and taking their sum:

A{+}B=\{a{+}b,\forall a\in A,\forall b\in B\}\,.

(19)

It can be understood as translating the region
B
by all vectors
a\in A
, or vice versa. As an example consider
A
and
B
to be separate disks shown in Figure
6
. Their Minkowski sum is then displayed as the light blue region, which contains any vector in
B
shifted by all vectors in
A
.

Figure 6
:
The Minkowski sum of two disks
A
and
B
. Note that the sum of two points on the boundary of individual region is not necessarily on the boundary of the Minkowski sum.

We will be interested in characterizing this sum in terms of its boundaries
\partial(A{+}B)
, which are determined by the individual boundaries
\partial A
and
\partial B
. More precisely, any vector on
\partial(A{+}B)
must be comprised by the sum of a vector on
\partial A
and a vector on
\partial B
. However the reverse is not necessarily true, as illustrated in the above figure, where a sum of boundary vectors sits inside the Minkowski sum. In fact, the boundary of the Minkowski sum
A{+}B
is a
subset
of boundaries for the Minkowski sum
\partial A{+}\partial B
. Indeed for our example in Figure
7
, the Minkowski sum of
\partial A
(denoted by the red contour) and

. Thus when one is interested in the boundary of the Minkowski sum, only the boundaries of the individual sets are relevant.

2.2
De-projecting the cyclic polytope

Let us begin with

a_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)z^{k-1}dz=\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell,q}a_{k}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\ell}a_{k,q}^{(\ell)}\,,

(20)

where
\rho(z)
is bounded as
0\leq\rho(z)\leq L
. Note that each
a_{k}^{(\ell)}
is a 1D segment,
0\leq a_{k}^{(\ell)}\leq\frac{L}{k}
. For later convenience we will parameterize the segments as

a_{k}^{(\ell)}=m_{\ell}\frac{L}{k},\quad m_{\ell}\in[0,1]\,.

(21)

The combination

{\bf a}^{(\ell)}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k,q_{1}}^{(\ell)}\\
a_{k,q_{2}}^{(\ell)}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k,q_{D}}^{(\ell)}\end{pmatrix}=m_{\ell}\frac{L}{k}\begin{pmatrix}v_{\ell,q_{1}}\\
v_{\ell,q_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
v_{\ell,q_{D}}\end{pmatrix}=m_{\ell}{\bf v}_{\ell}\,,

(22)

then represents a segment in
D
dimensions, also parameterized by
m_{\ell}
. Eq.(
20
) is therefore

{\bf a}\equiv\sum_{\ell}m_{\ell}{\bf v}_{\ell}

(23)

that is,
{\bf a}
corresponds to a Minkowski sum of segments, with endpoints
(0,0,\cdots,0)
and
{\bf v}_{\ell}=\frac{L}{k}(v_{\ell,q_{1}},v_{\ell,q_{2}},\cdots,v_{\ell,q_{D}})
in
D
dimensions. As discussed previously, the boundary of the Minkowski sum
{\bf a}
is found from the Minkowski sum of the boundaries of
{\bf a}^{(\ell)}
. Therefore the endpoints of the segments, represented by vectors
{\bf v}_{\ell}
will be what is relevant to the problem.

Two dimensions

Let us start in two dimensions, where the

\langle i_{1},i_{2}\rangle>0,\quad\forall i_{1}<i_{2}\,.

(24)

{\bf a}&{\bf V}_{i}&{\bf V}_{i+1}\end{array}\right),\quad{\rm det}\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
{\bf a}&\bar{{\bf V}}_{i}&\bar{{\bf V}}_{i+1}\end{array}\right)\geq 0,\quad\,1\leq i\leq N\,.

where we have
{\bf V}_{1}=\bar{{\bf V}}_{N{+}1}={\bf 0}

\langle{\bf V}_{i,N},i\rangle\leq\langle{\bf a},i\rangle\leq\langle{\bf V}_{1,i},i\rangle\,.

\displaystyle P_{i}^{\textrm{lower}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\langle{\bf a}{-}{\bf V}_{1,i},i\rangle=\textrm{det}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\

There is however a more invariant definition: given a set of ordered segments, the vertices of the lower boundary are obtained from the successive sum starting from the beginning of the ordering, while the upper boundaries are from the successive sum starting from the end of the ordering. This is apparent from eq.(

2.3
De-projecting the moment problem: The
L
-moment

Let us now consider a different problem, where our previous result will guide us to the answer. We want to find the allowed space of moments
(a_{k_{1}},a_{k_{2}},\ldots)
that satisfy

a_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)z^{k-1}dz,\quad 0\leq\rho(z)\leq L\,.

(45)

This is known as the “
L
-moment problem”
akhiezer1934fouriersche
;
akhiezer1962some
. We will solve it in a straightforward fashion by taking the continuous limit of the polytope discussed in the previous section. To relate the two we express the above integral as a Riemann sum

a_{k}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)z^{k-1}dz\;\rightarrow\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho_{i}\frac{i^{k-1}}{N^{k}}\,,

(46)

where
\rho_{i}\equiv\rho(i/N)\leq L
, which again we can consider to be a segments of length
L
, parameterized by
m_{i}\in[0,1]
. We can then write the above as

a_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}v_{i,k}

(47)

where
v_{i,k}=Li^{k-1}/N^{k}
. Expressed as a discrete sum the
L
moment problem is equivalent to a Minkowski sum of segments, with endpoints at 0 and
L(v_{i,k_{1}},v_{i,k_{2}},\ldots)
. Furthermore, the
v_{i,k}
are ordered, as taking a collection of
N
such vectors the resulting
N\times N
determinant will be proportional to the Vandermonde determinant, and thus eq.(
16
) holds. The results in the previous section therefore apply to this case, so the vertices of the Minkowski sum in eq.(
47
) are given by eq.(
2.2
). As we take
N\rightarrow\infty
, the infinite number of polytope boundaries merge into a smooth curve and each vertex is a point on the curve.

Two dimensions

Let us take the two dimensional space
(a_{k_{1}},a_{k_{2}})
as an example. From the previous subsection, the vertices are

{V}_{1,i|k}=L\sum_{j=1}^{i}v_{j,k}=L\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{j^{k-1}}{N^{k}}\,.

(48)

Now we define
m\equiv\frac{i}{N}\in[0,1]
and
z\equiv\frac{j}{N}\in[0,m]
, which in the
N\rightarrow\infty

48

{V}_{1,i|k}=L\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{j^{k-1}}{N^{k}}\rightarrow L\int_{0}^{m}z^{k-1}dz=L\frac{m^{k}}{k}\,,

(49)

and the boundary vertex becomes a point on the parametric curve

(a_{k_{1}},a_{k_{2}})=({U}_{k_{1}}(m),{U}_{k_{2}}(m)),\quad{U}_{k}(m)\equiv L\frac{m^{k}}{k}\,.

(50)

Similarly for the vertices of type
{\bf V}_{i,N}
in the continuous limit we obtain:

(a_{k_{1}},a_{k_{2}})=(\overline{U}_{k_{1}}(m),\overline{U}_{k_{2}}(m)),\quad\overline{U}_{k}(m)\equiv L\frac{1-m^{k}}{k}\,.

(51)

Note that we can rephrase the boundary as a particular solution for
\rho(z)
. It is not hard to work out that the solution for lower and upper boundary are given by

\displaystyle{\rm lower}:\quad\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{I}(z),\;\;I=[0,m]

\displaystyle{\rm upper}:\quad\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{I}(z),\;\;I=[m,1]

z

To find the constraint equations in

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k_{2}}\left(\frac{k_{1}}{L}a_{k_{1}}\right)^{\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}}\leq a_{k_{2}}\leq\frac{L}{k_{2}}\bigg(1-\left(1-\frac{k_{1}}{L}a_{k_{1}}\right)^{\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}}\bigg)\,.

3
Minkowski sums of single
L
-moments

We now return to the our general problem

a_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell,k,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k-1},\quad 0\leq\rho_{\ell}\leq L\,.

(56)

We will solve this problem by treating the
a_{k,q}
as yet another Minkowski sum. We can write the above as

\begin{pmatrix}a_{k_{1},q_{1}}\\
a_{k_{1},q_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k_{D},q_{D}}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\ell}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k_{1},q_{1}}^{(\ell)}\\
a_{k_{2},q_{2}}^{(\ell)}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k_{D},q_{D}}^{(\ell)}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\ell}\begin{pmatrix}a_{k_{1}}^{(\ell)}v_{\ell,k_{1},q_{1}}\\
a_{k_{2}}^{(\ell)}v_{\ell,k_{2},q_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
a_{k_{D}}^{(\ell)}v_{\ell,k_{D},q_{D}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(57)

where for each spin, the
a_{k,q}^{(\ell)}
is identified as the solution to the
L
-moment problem
a_{k}=(a_{k_{1}},a_{k_{2}},\ldots,a_{k_{D}})
, with each component
a_{k_{i}}
rescaled by some number
v_{\ell,k_{i},q_{i}}
. The full geometry is then given by the Minkowski sum over all spins of such geometries.

As discussed in the beginning of the previous section, to compute the boundary of the Minkowski sum, one first computes the Minkowski sum of the boundaries. The final boundary can be found by extremizing this result, most simply using Lagrange multipliers, as we will discuss shortly. Alternatively we can revert the
L
-moment problem to its discrete version, and simply consider the whole problem as a double Minkowski sum of segments. We will explore this view point in detail in appendix
B
. Here we will present the result from the Lagrange method.

We first solve the problem for any 2D space
(a_{k_{1},q_{1}},a_{k_{2},q_{2}})
, for any
v_{\ell,k,q}
. This will be sufficient to derive optimal bounds on any 2D space of couplings without imposing null constraints, or bounds on any single coupling with one null constraint.

To implement more null constraints, or to obtain obtain the space of more couplings, would require us to solve the geometry in higher dimensions. In this case we only obtain partial results. First, we can easily derive boundaries when only a a finite number of spins are relevant. When the sum over infinite spins cannot be avoided, we are only able to find closed form expressions for spaces
(a_{k_{1},q_{1}},a_{k_{2},q_{2}},\ldots)
satisfying
k_{i+1}{-}k_{i}{=}\Delta_{k}
,
q_{i+1}{-}q_{i}{=}\Delta_{q}
, and when
v_{\ell,k,q}{=}v_{\ell,q}{=}g(\ell)f(\ell)^{q}
, where
g(\ell)
and
f(\ell)
can be general polynomials of
\ell
.

For the entire space of couplings, the problem can be formulated in terms of envelopes of various moment constraints. This is more amendable to numerical approaches which we explore in appendix
C
.

3.1
Extremizing the Minkowski sum

In this section we briefly explain how the boundary of a Minkowski sum can be obtained by extremizing. Let us use the example of the disks in Figure
7
to introduce this method. We can describe the boundaries of disks A and B as

\displaystyle\partial A(\theta_{A})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{x},a_{y})+r_{A}(\textrm{sin}\theta_{A},\textrm{cos}\theta_{A})\,,

\displaystyle\partial B(\theta_{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b_{x},b_{y})+r_{B}(\textrm{sin}\theta_{B},\textrm{cos}\theta_{B})\,,

(58)

The Minkowski sum of boundaries
\partial A(\theta_{A})+\partial B(\theta_{B})
is then a 2D region, with the two components given by

\displaystyle f_{x}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{x}{+}b_{x}){+}r_{A}\textrm{sin}\,\theta_{A}{+}r_{B}\textrm{sin}\,\theta_{B}

\displaystyle f_{y}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{y}{+}b_{y}){+}r_{A}\textrm{cos}\,\theta_{A}{+}r_{B}\textrm{cos}\,\theta_{B}\,.

(59)

To find the boundary of this region we can consider extremizing in one coordinate while the other is held fixed. This gives a constraint equation that will allow us to solve one parameter in terms of the remaining, giving the co-dimension one boundaries of the Minkowski sum
\partial A(\theta_{A})+\partial B(\theta_{B})
. More precisely, fixing the
x
-coordinate while extremizing in
y
, one solves

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{\theta_{A}}f_{x}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\\
\partial_{\theta_{B}}f_{x}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\end{array}\right)=\lambda\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{\theta_{A}}f_{y}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\\
\partial_{\theta_{B}}f_{y}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\end{array}\right)\,,

(60)

where
\lambda
is the Lagrangian multiplier. Note that this is simply saying that the two component vectors are proportional and hence

{\rm det}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{\theta_{A}}f_{x}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})&\partial_{\theta_{A}}f_{y}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\\
\partial_{\theta_{B}}f_{x}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})&\partial_{\theta_{B}}f_{y}(\theta_{A},\theta_{B})\end{array}\right)=0\;\;\rightarrow\;\;\cos\theta_{A}\sin\theta_{B}=\cos\theta_{B}\sin\theta_{A}\,.

(61)

We find two solutions:
\theta_{A}=\theta_{B}
and
\theta_{A}=\theta_{B}+\pi
. These precisely correspond to the outer and inner boundaries in Figure
7
, given by

\partial(\partial A+\partial B)=(a_{x}+b_{x},a_{y}+b_{y})+(r_{A}\pm r_{B})(\textrm{sin}\phi_{A},\textrm{cos}\phi_{A})\,.

(62)

This generalizes to higher dimensions. Consider the combination of two
D
-dimensional regions. We find the boundary of their Minkowski sum by taking the Minkowski sum of the boundary
(f_{x_{1}},f_{x_{2}},\cdots,f_{x_{D}})
, where
f_{x_{i}}
is a function of
2(D{-}1)
boundary variables,
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\cdots,\theta_{2D{-}2})
. Holding
(f_{x_{2}},\cdots,f_{x_{D}})
fixed and extremizing
f_{x_{1}}
we solve

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{\theta_{1}}f_{x_{1}}\\
\partial_{\theta_{2}}f_{x_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{\theta_{2(D{-}1)}}f_{x_{1}}\end{array}\right)=\sum_{i=2}^{D}\lambda_{i}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{\theta_{1}}f_{x_{i}}\\
\partial_{\theta_{2}}f_{x_{i}}\\
\vdots\\
\partial_{\theta_{2(D{-}1)}}f_{x_{i}}\end{array}\right)\,.

(63)

This gives us
2D{-}2
constraints on
D{-}1
Lagrangian multipliers
\lambda_{i}
and
2(D{-}1)
boundary variables
\theta_{i}
, giving us
D{-}1
-dimensional boundaries.

Finally we comment that since the Lagrange method relies on the variation of the individual boundaries, it will miss boundaries coming from the end points (or boundary of boundaries). These end point contributions must be analyzed by hand, which we will demonstrate through explicit examples.

Convergence for infinite spin limit

In general a physical theory will contain an infinite number of spins, so we must take the Minkowski sum to infinity,
N\rightarrow\infty
. One then needs to worry if the sum in
F_{k,q}
given by eq.(
78
) converges. If the sum diverges, then we only get a trivial projective boundary, such as
a_{k_{2}}\geq 0
. Convergence will therefore be necessary to obtain non projective bounds.

Assuming a simple case with
v_{\ell,k,q}=\ell^{q}
, the convergence is easy to determine. We have

r_{i,1}=\left(\frac{1}{\ell}\right)^{\frac{q_{2}-q_{1}}{k_{2}-k_{1}}},\quad F_{k,q}^{(1)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{\frac{q_{1}{-}q_{2}}{k_{2}{-}k_{1}}k{+}q}=\zeta\left(\frac{q_{2}{-}q_{1}}{k_{2}{-}k_{1}}k{-}q\right)\,,

(83)

which converges for

\frac{q_{2}{-}q_{1}}{k_{2}{-}k_{1}}k{-}q>1\,.

(84)

For example, for the space
(a_{1,0},a_{2,0})
we find
r_{i,1}=1
and the sum diverges

F_{1,0}^{(1)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}1=\infty\,.

(85)

This means there are no non-projective bounds for this 2D space for
v_{\ell,k,q}=\ell^{q}
. However, for the space
(a_{2,0},a_{3,1})
we instead get
r_{i,1}=\ell^{-1}
and the sum is finite

F_{2,0}^{(1)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\,.

(86)

Plugging this in eq.(
77
), we obtain the first section of the infinite spin configuration as:

(1):\quad a_{2,0}=L\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}m^{2},\quad a_{3,1}=L\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}m^{3},\quad m\in[0,1]\,.

(87)

The second section can similarly be obtained, using
F_{2,0}^{(2)}{=}4(F_{2,0}^{(1)}{-}1)

(2):\quad a_{2,0}=\frac{L}{2}\left(1+4\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-1\right)m^{2}\right),\quad a_{3,1}=\frac{L}{3}\left(1+8\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-1\right)m^{3}\right),\quad m\in[\frac{1}{2},1]\,.

(88)

We plot this in Figure

As mentioned previously when taking the Minkowski sum, if the

(+,-)

(+,+)

When

:

, with

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle a_{k,q}^{(+-)}(m)=Lv_{\ell_{-},k,q}\frac{(1-m^{k})}{k},&m\in[0,1]\,,\\

Section 2:

\displaystyle a_{k,q}(0,m,0),\quad m\in[0,1]\,,

Section 3:

\displaystyle a_{k,q}(0,1,m),\quad m\in[0,1]\,.

Higher dimensional spaces

Let us now attempt to solve the same problem in higher dimensions. As in 2D, for

\displaystyle a_{k,q}(m_{1},n_{1},m_{2},n_{2})

4
The non-projective EFThedron

We now return to the physical setting. Let us begin with the dispersive representation of the EFT couplings eq.(
8
)

g_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell,k,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k{-}1}dz,\quad 0\leq\rho_{\ell}(z)\leq 2\,,

(101)

where
v_{\ell,k,q}=16(2\ell{+}1)\lambda_{\ell,k,q}
.

Couplings are subjected to “null” constraints, which originate from permutation invariance of the amplitude. These constraints can be easily found by requiring the amplitude to be expandable in terms of
\sigma_{2}=s^{2}{+}t^{2}{+}u^{2}
and
\sigma_{3}=stu
. Since the dispersion relation is valid for
k,k{-}q\geq 2
, this means the first null constraint is at order
k=4
, so we must have that
\sigma_{2}^{2}{=}4(s^{4}{+}2s^{3}t{+}3s^{2}t^{2})\propto s^{4}g_{4,0}{+}s^{3}tg_{4,1}{+}s^{2}t^{2}g_{4,2}{+}\ldots
, leading to

g_{4,0}=\frac{g_{4,1}}{2}=\frac{g_{4,2}}{3}\,.

(102)

Since
\lambda_{\ell,4,0}=\frac{\lambda_{\ell,4,1}}{2}
from eq.(
7
), the first equality trivially holds. However,
\lambda_{\ell,4,2}=6-4\mathcal{J}^{2}+\mathcal{J}^{4}/2
, where
\mathcal{J}^{2}=\ell(\ell+1)
, so the second equality leads to the null constraint

n_{4}:2g_{4,2}-3g_{4,1}=0\,.

(103)

Similarly for
k=5
and
6
, we have:

n_{5}:g_{5,3}{-}2g_{5,1}=0,\quad n_{6}:g_{6,4}{-}g_{6,2}=0\,.

(104)

Geometrically these null constraints define hyperplanes in the space of couplings. Thus imposing a set of null constraints is equivalent to intersecting the space of couplings with a collection of hyperplanes.

We will first consider the equal
k
space, i.e.
(g_{k,q_{1}},g_{k,q_{2}},\cdots)
which we showed in Section
2
is a polytope. Its intersection with the null plane at the same order
k
will then give bounds on coefficients. More generally, to impose null conditions of different
k
order, it will be useful to consider the linear combinations of couplings that correspond to null constraints as one of the coordinates in our space. For example, we define

\displaystyle n_{4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}(2v_{\ell,4,2}{-}3v_{\ell,4,1})\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{3}dz

(105)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}16(2\ell+1)(\mathcal{J}^{4}-8\mathcal{J}^{2})\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{3}dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}u_{\ell,4}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{3}dz\,.

Then to impose the null constraint on a coupling
g_{k,q}
we simply consider the geometry in
(g_{k,q},n_{4})
space, and intersect with the line
n_{4}{=}0
. Importantly, since we have derived the boundary for any 2D space in Section
3
, this approach indeed allows us to find optimal bounds on any single coupling assuming one null plane. We also discuss partial results for a space of two couplings subject to one null constraint.

4.1

g_{k,q}

-null constraint

, the modification is relatively small. We compare the two in Figure

\frac{1}{k}
, and we expect this behavior to continue for even higher

It is interesting to compare this behavior with an example of a physical theory. We can consider a theory with a massive scalar
m_{x}

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{4}\ell}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{\left[\ell^{2}-m_{x}^{2}\right]\left[\left(\ell-p_{1}\right)^{2}-m_{x}^{2}\right]\left[\left(\ell-p_{1}-p_{2}\right)^{2}-m_{x}^{2}\right]\left[\left(\ell+p_{4}\right)^{2}-m_{x}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle+perm(2,3,4)\,.

(137)

5
Conclusions

In this paper we developed a non-projective generalization of the EFThedron, allowing us to incorporate the non-linear unitarity bound in full. This allows us to derive non-projective upper bounds for the EFT couplings, which shows that while the leading derivative couplings are of
\mathcal{O}(1)
, they are heavily suppressed at higher
k
. As a different application of this framework, we can also incorporate the low spin dominance condition, a property observed to hold for gravitational EFT
Bern:2021ppb
(see also
Figueroa:2022onw
). It would also be interesting to explore how to implement the upper bound on
\rho
for impact parameter space dispersion relations in gravitational EFT
Caron-Huot:2022ugt
;
Caron-Huot:2021rmr
.

In Section
2
we solved the single
L
-moment problem by treating it as a Minkowski sum of segments. To our knowledge this a new proof to this problem. It would be interesting if the same approach could be used to solve the double
L
-moment problem
PUTINAR1990288
, and perhaps understand new features of this much richer topic. At the same time, it would be interesting if the known solution to the double
L
-moment can be used to obtain bounds on our physical problem. This would enable the approach used in the projective case, which is easier to set up and extend to higher dimensional spaces and more null constraints.

Appendix A
Aspects of the
L
-moment problem

In this section we discuss some other interesting aspects of the
L
-moment problem, in particular the functions
\rho
that correspond to our boundary solutions, also known as extremal solutions, and the formulation of constraints in terms of “exponential Hankel” matrices.

A.1
Boundary distributions

The boundary solutions for
a_{k}
correspond to particular
\rho
, which we can easily work out. For instance, the boundary of a 2D space we found was given by

a_{k,q}=\int_{0}^{1}\rho z^{k-1}dz=L\frac{m^{k}}{k}\,,

(139)

and we find the corresponding boundary
\rho
is a step function

\chi_{I}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}L&\textrm{for }z\in I\,,\\
0&\textrm{for }z\notin I\,.\end{array}\right.

(140)

In general, a solution may contain a sum over several such functions,

\rho(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{I_{i}}(z)\,,

(141)

where
I_{i}=[m_{2i-1},m_{2i}]
,
m_{i}\leq m_{i+1}
. Just like the boundary itself, we can deduce the corresponding distributions directly from eqs.(
2.2
) and (
2.2
), depending on dimension, by simply taking
{\bf V}_{i_{1},i_{2}}\rightarrow\chi_{[m_{i_{1}},m_{i_{2}}]}
. For
D=\textrm{even}
we find

\displaystyle\textrm{lower: }\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{[0,m_{1}]}+\chi_{[m_{2},m_{3}]}+\ldots+\chi_{[{m_{D-2}},m_{D-1}]}\,,

\displaystyle\textrm{upper: }\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{[m_{1},m_{2}]}+\chi_{[m_{3},m_{4}]}+\ldots+\chi_{[{m_{D-1}},1]}\,,

(142)

and for
D=\textrm{odd}

\displaystyle\textrm{lower: }\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{[m_{1},m_{2}]}+\chi_{[m_{3},m_{4}]}+\ldots+\chi_{[{m_{D-2}},m_{D-1}]}\,,

\displaystyle\textrm{upper: }\rho(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{[0,m_{1}]}+\chi_{[m_{2},m_{3}]}+\ldots+\chi_{[{m_{D-1}},1]}\,.

(143)

A.2
Exponential Hankel conditions

Similar to the projective moment problem, necessary and sufficient conditions for the
L
-moments can be expressed in terms of positive semi-definite matrices. Using the notation of
PUTINAR1990288
, the conditions for the infinite dimensional problem are given in terms of two new sequences,
b
and
c
, defined by

\displaystyle\textrm{exp}\left[{-\frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{X}{+}\frac{a_{1}}{X^{2}}{+}\cdots\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{b_{0}}{X}{+}\frac{b_{1}}{X^{2}}{+}\cdots\right)\,,

\displaystyle\textrm{exp}\left[{\frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{X}{+}\frac{a_{1}}{X^{2}}{+}\cdots\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\left(\frac{c_{0}}{X}{+}\frac{c_{1}}{X^{2}}{+}\cdots\right)\,,

(144)

which must satisfy the usual Hankel positivity constraints, for all
n
.

1.

x\in(-\infty,\infty)
:

H_{n}(b)\geq 0\,.

(145)

2.

x\in[0,\infty)
:

H_{n}(b),\ H_{n}^{\textrm{shift}}(b)\geq 0\,.

(146)

3.

x\in[0,1]
:

H_{n}(b),\ H_{n}^{\textrm{shift}}(b),\ H_{n}^{\textrm{twist}}(c)\geq 0\,.

(147)

One can verify that for the boundary solutions we found particular subsets of the exponential Hankels indeed vanish. This implies the Hankel constraints are necessary conditions. Next we review the proof they are also sufficient conditions, in the
x\in(-\infty,\infty)
case.

Let us begin with the exponential map

e^{\frac{1}{L}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{x}{+}\frac{a_{1}}{x^{2}}{+}\cdots\frac{a_{2m}}{x^{2m{+}1}}\right)}=1{+}\frac{b_{0}}{x}{+}\frac{b_{1}}{x^{2}}{+}\cdots

(148)

Note that while there are
2m

a_{i}
s, the exponential map generates an infinite series for
b_{i}
s. We would like to show that if the
b_{i}
satisfy Hankel positivity condition, we can use them to build a solution for the
L
-moment problem for the
\{a_{i}\}
s. This applies to the Hamburger, Stieltjes and Hausdorff intervals
\mathcal{I}
. For a general discussion see
akhiezer1962some
.

So let us begin by
b_{i}
being a solution to the Hamburger moment problem. Note that since the LHS of eq.(
148
) has
2m{+}1

\{a_{i}\}
s,
this means that the first
2m{+}1

\{b_{i}\}
s on the RHS should fully determine the
\{a_{i}\}
s. This further suggests that
b_{2k{+}1},b_{k{+}2},\cdots
are also fully determined by
\{b_{0},b_{1},\cdots,b_{2m{+}1}\}
, and thus the
\{b_{i}\}
s correspond to a solution with
m{+}1
elements in the hull:

b_{i}=\sum_{a=1}^{m{+}1}\tilde{\rho}_{a}y_{a}^{i},\quad\tilde{\rho}_{a}>0\,.

(149)

Substituting this into the exponential map the RHS can be rewritten as

1{+}\frac{b_{0}}{x}{+}\frac{b_{1}}{x^{2}}{+}\cdots=1{+}\sum_{a=1}^{m{+}1}\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{a}}{x{-}y_{a}}\equiv\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(x)}\,,

(150)

where

\phi(x)=\prod_{a=1}^{m{+}1}(x{-}y_{a}),\quad\psi(x)=\prod_{a=1}^{m{+}1}(x{-}y^{\prime}_{a})\,,

(151)

and
y^{\prime}_{1}<y_{1}<y_{2}^{\prime}<y_{2}<\cdots<y^{\prime}_{m{+}1}<y_{m{+}1}
, i.e. the position of zeros for
\psi(x)
is distributed in between the zeros of
\phi(x)
. Now consider the following density:

\rho(u)\equiv\frac{L}{2}\left(1{-}\textrm{sign}\frac{\psi(u)}{\phi(u)}\right)\,.

(152)

We see that
\rho(u)
is either
L
or
0
. Since the zeros of
\psi(x)
and
\phi(x)
are interspaced, the integration directly gives

\int du\frac{\rho(u)}{x{-}u}=L\left(\int_{y^{\prime}_{1}}^{y_{1}}\frac{du}{x-u}{+}\int_{y^{\prime}_{2}}^{y_{2}}\frac{du}{x-u}{+}\cdots\right)=L\log\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(x)}\,.

(153)

Now combine eq.(
148
) with eq.(
150
), we have

\left(\frac{a_{0}}{x}{+}\frac{a_{1}}{x^{2}}{+}\cdots\frac{a_{2m}}{x^{2m{+}1}}\right)=L\log\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(x)}=\int du\frac{\rho(u)}{x{-}u}\quad\rightarrow\quad a_{k}=\int du\;\rho(u)u^{k}\,,

(154)

where
\rho(u)
is defined in eq.(
152
). Thus we have constructed a solution to the
L
-moment problem. Note that the solution corresponds to an
m
-state solution.

Appendix B
Discrete solution to the double moment problem

Let us understand the results in Section
3.2
also from the discrete perspective, instead of the Lagrange method. Our task is to find the boundary for the sum of two lower boundaries

a_{k_{1},q_{1}}=\frac{L}{k}v_{\ell_{1},k,q}m_{1}^{k}+\frac{L}{k}v_{\ell_{2},k,q}m_{2}^{k}

(155)

If we imagine discretizing both curves into small segments, we know the resulting boundary will be given by placing the segments in order according to their slope. The slopes of segments from curve 1 range from
[0,\theta_{1}]
, while those of curve 2 from
[0,\theta_{2}]
, where
\theta_{\ell}=v_{\ell,k_{2},q_{2}}/v_{\ell,k_{1},q_{1}}
. We show this in Figure
32
. If we assume
\theta_{2}>\theta_{1}
, then it is clear the sum will have one section from
[0,\theta_{1}]
formed with segments originating from both, placed in some particular order, and a second section from
[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
with segments just from curve 2, placed in their original order. In the continuous limit this leads to the result found via Lagrange multipliers. We work this out explicitly next.

Figure 32
:
Boundary of the Minkowski sum via discretization. Treating the two curves as polytopes with infinitesimal edges, the resulting polytope contains segments from both curves, in order of their slope.

Let us now consider a sum of an arbitrary number of moments. We assume the
v_{\ell}
are ordered and non-negative, such that
0<\theta_{\ell_{1}}\leq\theta_{\ell_{2}}\leq\ldots\leq\theta_{\ell_{n}}<\pi/2
. This implies all spins
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
have segments with slopes up to
\theta_{1}
, but only spins
\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n}
have segments with slopes from
\theta_{1}
to
\theta_{2}
, and so on.

The discretized version of the moments, is given by

a_{k,q}^{(\ell)}=v_{\ell,k,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho(z)z^{k-1}dz\rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\rho_{\ell,i}v_{\ell,k,q}\frac{i^{k-1}}{N_{\ell}^{k}},\quad 0\leq\rho_{i,\ell}\leq L\,.

(156)

We can view each moment as a Minkowski sum of segments
s_{i}^{(\ell)}
, defined in the terms of their endpoint obtained when
\rho_{i,\ell}=L
:

s_{i,k,q}^{(\ell)}=L\,v_{\ell,k,q}\frac{i^{k-1}}{N_{\ell}^{k}}\,,

(157)

with slopes

\theta_{\ell,i}=\theta_{\ell}\frac{i^{\Delta_{k}}}{N^{\Delta_{k}}}\,.

(158)

Note that as
i
varies from 0 to
N_{\ell}
the slopes of the segments vary from 0 to
\theta_{\ell}
.
Let us now consider the range
[0,\theta_{1}]
, where segments from all spins can appear. We wish to discretize all curves into segments
s_{i}^{(\ell)}
with equal slope for all
\ell
. We can achieve this by tuning
N_{\ell}
for each spin. To obtain segments of equal slope at equal
i
but different
\ell
, we require

\theta_{\ell_{1},i}=\theta_{\ell_{2},i}\Rightarrow\frac{N_{1}}{N_{2}}=\left(\frac{\theta_{1}}{\theta_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\Delta_{k}}}=r_{2,1}\,.

(159)

This means each spin must be discretized in a total of
N_{\ell_{i}}=N_{1}/r_{i,1}\geq N_{1}
segments. The factor
r_{i,1}
is finite for
\ell\neq 0
, so the continuous limit remains consistent. For a given spin
\ell_{j}
we obtain a set of
N_{1}
segments

s_{i,k,q}^{(\ell_{j})}=L\frac{i^{k}}{N_{1}^{k}}\ell_{j}^{q}r_{j,1}^{k},\quad i\in[0,N_{1}]\,,

(160)

with slopes

\theta_{\ell_{j},i}=\theta_{\ell_{1},i}=\theta_{1}\frac{i^{\Delta_{k}}}{N_{1}^{\Delta_{k}}}\,,

(161)

which unlike eq.(
158
) are now independent of spin, but still depend on
i
as they vary from 0 to
\theta_{1}
. We can now compute the Minkowski sum at fixed
i
over all spins, since the sum of segments of equal slope simply amounts to another segment of the same slope, with endpoint

S_{i,k,q}^{(1)}=\sum_{\ell}s_{i,k,q}^{(\ell)}=\frac{i^{k}}{N_{1}^{k}}F_{k,q}\,,

(162)

where

F_{k,q}=\sum_{i=1}v_{\ell,k,q}r_{i,j}^{k}\,.

(163)

Crucially, the new segments
S_{i}
are now ordered according to
i\in[0,N_{1}]
, as their slope increases monotonically with
i
, from 0 to
\theta_{1}
. Next, we can repeat the argument for the range
[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]
, and we obtain a new set of vectors
S_{i,k,q}^{(2)}
, also ordered by
i
. Taking the union of
{S}^{(1)}
and
{S}^{(2)}
we therefore obtain a set of segments ordered by
i\in[N_{1},N_{2}]
, and so the boundaries of their Minkowksi sum can be obtained from using the results in Section
2
. For
j\in[0,N_{1}]
, corresponding to the range,
[0,\theta_{1}]
we obtain a vertex on the boundary given by

V_{j|k,q}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{i^{k}}{N_{1}^{k}}F_{k,q}\,.

(164)

In the continuous limit this becomes

\displaystyle a_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k}m^{k}F_{k,q}\,,

(165)

matching what we found using the Lagrange method in eq.(
77
).

Appendix C
Towards a solution for the complete space

In this section we present some partial results on determining the complete space for the simpler “power” moment problem

a_{k,q}=\sum_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,q}\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\ell}(z)z^{k-1}dz\,,

(166)

where
\Lambda_{\ell,q}=g(\ell)f(\ell)^{q}
, with
g
and
f
any positive polynomials in
\ell
.

C.1
Diagonal spaces

Let us first consider 3D spaces of the form
a_{k_{1},q_{1}},a_{k_{2},q_{2}},a_{k_{3},q_{3}},
, where the set of
\{k_{i},q_{i}\}
s satisfy

k_{i+1}-k_{i}=\Delta_{k},\quad q_{i+1}-q_{i}=\Delta_{q}\,,

(167)

For two spins, the Minkowski sum of lower and upper boundaries of this space is given by

\displaystyle a_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k}\sum_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,q}\left(m_{1}^{(\ell)}-m_{2}^{(\ell)}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k}\sum_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,q}\left(1-m_{1}^{(\ell)}+m_{2}^{(\ell)}\right)\,,

(168)

for
1\geq m_{1}^{(\ell)}\geq m_{2}^{(\ell)}\geq 0
. We must compute the boundary of the Minkowski sum of these boundaries, which is again easily accomplished by the Lagrange multiplier method, also keeping in mind to check the boundaries of parametric space.

For such case, applying the Lagrange method we find:

\frac{m_{1}^{(\ell_{i})}}{m_{1}^{(\ell_{j})}}=\frac{m_{2}^{(\ell_{i})}}{m_{2}^{(\ell_{j})}}=r_{j,i}=\left(\frac{f(\ell_{i})}{f(\ell_{j})}\right)^{\frac{\Delta_{q}}{\Delta_{k}}}\,.

(169)

This reduces the Minkowski sum to just two independent parameters, as required to span the
2
dimensional boundary. Expressing for instance all parameters as
m_{i}^{(\ell_{j})}=m_{i}^{(\ell_{1})}r_{j,1}=m_{i}r_{j,1}
, the lower boundary is given by

a_{k,q}=\frac{L}{k}\left((m_{1})^{k}-(m_{2})^{k}\right)\sum_{i}\Lambda_{\ell,q}r_{i,1}^{k}\,.

(170)

which is valid for
1\geq m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq 0
, and similarly for the upper boundary.
The sum over infinite spins is now separate from the parameters, and so can be evaluated numerically. In higher dimensions, the sum of boundaries is given by

\displaystyle a_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k}\sum_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,q}\sum_{j=1}^{D-1}(-1)^{j+1}m_{j}^{(\ell)}\,,

\displaystyle\overline{a}_{k,q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L}{k}\sum_{\ell}\Lambda_{\ell,q}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{D-1}(-1)^{j+1}m_{j}^{(\ell)}\right)\,.

(171)

Remarkably, the result eq.(
169
) holds in fact in any number of dimensions, as long as the conditions (
167
) are satisfied, so it can be plugged in eq.(
C.1
) to obtain the
D{-}1
dimensional boundaries.

Finally we need to consider bounds of the parameter space. These will give new boundaries, which as before, can be simply deduced by imposing the upper integration limit as

m_{i}^{\ell_{j}}\rightarrow\textrm{min}[m_{i}^{(\ell_{j})},1]\,.

(172)

Explicitly, the boundary of a
D
-dimensional space will be labeled by
D-1
indices
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D-1})
, with
N\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq\ldots\geq i_{D-1}\geq 1
. A value
i_{j}=n
indicates
m_{j}^{\ell_{i<n}}=1
and
m_{j}^{\ell_{i\geq n}}=r_{i,n}m_{j}
, with
m_{j}\in[r_{n,n-1},1]
.

C.2
Approach to more general spaces

Here we introduce a tentative approach to obtain constraints on the complete space of couplings
a_{k,q}
. This is motivated by the previous solution to the projective moment problem discussed in
Chiang:2021ziz
. Recall that a sequence
a_{k}
can be expressed as a single moment

a_{k}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho(x)x^{k-1},\quad\rho\geq 0\,,

(173)

if and only if the Hankel matrix is totally positive,

H=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\ldots\\
a_{2}&a_{3}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\,.

(174)

For double moments,

a_{k,q}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(x,y)x^{k-1}y^{q-1},\quad\rho\geq 0\,,

(175)

the conditions are given by total positivity of the moment matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{2,1}&a_{1,2}&a_{3,1}&a_{2,2}&\ldots\\
a_{2,1}&a_{3,1}&a_{2,2}\\
a_{1,2}&a_{2,2}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\,.

(176)

There is in fact a simple way to derive the moment matrix from the Hankel matrix. This can be done by considering linear combinations of the double moments that can be written as single moments. For instance we can define

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle a_{1,1}=\int\rho\left(x+\gamma xy\right)^{0}\,,

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle a_{2,1}+\gamma a_{2,2}=\int\rho\left(x+\gamma xy\right)^{1}\,,

\displaystyle b_{3}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle a_{3,1}+2\gamma a_{3,2}+\gamma^{2}a_{3,3}=\int\rho\left(x+\gamma xy\right)^{2}\,,

(177)

where
\gamma
is some parameter. Importantly, the new couplings
b_{k}
are again a sum of single moments
b_{k}=\int\rho z^{k}
, with
z=x+\gamma xy
. Thus
b_{k}
satisfy Hankel conditions, leading to constraints back on the
a_{k,q}
, such as

H=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}&b_{2}\\
b_{2}&b_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{2,1}+\gamma a_{2,2}\\
a_{2,1}+\gamma a_{2,2}&a_{3,1}+2\gamma a_{3,2}+\gamma^{2}a_{3,3}\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\,.

(178)

Importantly, the above condition now must hold for all values of
\gamma
. Imposing this through the discriminant equation on the determinant, we recover exactly the same condition as from the
3\times 3
moment matrix in eq.(
176
). We have therefore derived the double moment conditions by taking an envelope, parameterized by
\gamma
, of the single moment conditions.

Let us now attempt to do the same for the double
L
-moment problem

a_{k,q}=\sum\ell^{q}\int\rho(z)z^{k-1}dx\,.

(179)

We mirror the argument above, by taking linear combinations

\displaystyle b_{1,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1,1}=\sum\int\rho(z)(z+\gamma z\ell)^{0}\,,

\displaystyle b_{2,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{2,1}+\gamma a_{2,2}=\sum\int\rho(z)(z+\gamma z\ell)^{1}\,,

\displaystyle b_{3,3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{3,1}+2\gamma a_{3,2}+\gamma^{2}a_{3,3}=\sum\int\rho(z)(z+\gamma z\ell)^{2}\,,

(180)

and so on, for
\gamma>0
. We have shown that diagonal spaces with
v_{\ell,k,q}=g(\ell)f(\ell)^{q}
satisfy simple boundary equations, and in the case above we simply have
v_{\ell,k,q}=(\ell+\gamma)^{q}
. The boundary (for the first section) is therefore

b_{k,k}=\frac{L}{k}(n^{k}-m^{k})\sum_{\ell}v_{\ell_{i},k,q}r_{i,1}^{k}\,,

(181)

where

r_{i,1}=\left(\frac{\ell_{1}+\gamma}{\ell+\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\Delta q}{\Delta k}}\,.

(182)

The boundary is now a function of the parameter
\gamma
, and if the analogy with the projective problem is valid, then we expect that taking the intersection of all constraints for all values of
\gamma
should give necessary and sufficient conditions on the
a_{k,q}
moments. Unfortunately, it seems unlikely a closed form can be found, since
\gamma
enters the constraints through the infinite sum in eq.(
181
), which is no longer a purely numerical factor. One can instead simply numerically compute the envelope (for
\gamma>0
). It would also be interesting to consider taking the continuous limit of the discrete sum. In that case the sum becomes a trivial integral, and it may be possible to obtain constraints relevant to the double continuous
L
-moment problem.

Appendix D
Low spin dominance

The solution for the non-projective polytope derived in Section
2
can also be utilized to explore the consequence of low spin dominance (LSD), first discussed in

. We begin with the dispersive representation for the couplings
