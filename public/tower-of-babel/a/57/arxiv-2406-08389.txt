Abstract
. The slope problem in holomorphic dynamics in the unit disk goes back to Wolff in 1929. However, there have been several contributions to this problem in the last decade. In this article the problem is revisited, comparing the discrete and continuous cases. Some advances are derived in the discrete parabolic case of zero hyperbolic step, showing that the set of slopes has to be a closed interval which is independent of the initial point. The continuous setting is used to show that any such interval is a possible example. In addition, the set of slopes of a family of parabolic function is discussed, leading to examples of functions with some regularity whose set of slopes is non-trivial.

1.
Introduction

One of the goals in the field of Discrete Complex Dynamics is to study the asymptotic behaviour of the iterated self-composition
g^{n+1}=g^{n}\circ g
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for a given holomorphic function
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
. Among other properties, this behaviour strongly depends on the fixed points of
g
on
\mathbb{D}
. For example, if
g
has a (necessarily unique) fixed point
\tau\in\mathbb{D}
(i.e.,
g(\tau)=\tau
) and it is not an automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
, then the Schwarz-Pick lemma assures that
g^{n}\to\tau
as
n\to+\infty
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
. In this case,
g
is said to be an elliptic function, and
\tau
is known as its Denjoy-Wolff point. Concerning non-elliptic functions, Denjoy and Wolff proved the following well-known result:

Theorem 1.1

(Denjoy-Wolff)
.

[
2
, Theorem 3.2.1]

Let
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
be a non-elliptic holomorphic function. Then there exists a point
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
g^{n}\to\tau
, uniformly on compact sets of
\mathbb{D}
. Furthermore,
\tau
is a boundary fixed point, that is,
\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}g(z)=\tau
.

Similarly as before,
\tau
is called the Denjoy-Wolff point of
g
.

This work is devoted to non-elliptic functions, which can be further divided in two subgroups whose dynamical properties are usually different. To present them, one can show (see
[
2
, Corollary 2.5.5]
) that the following limit exists:

\lambda=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\dfrac{g(z)-\tau}{z-\tau}\in(0,1].

If
\lambda<1
,
g
is called hyperbolic. In the case that
\lambda=1
,
g
is said parabolic.

From an initial point
w_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
, an orbit
\{w_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{D}
is defined as

w_{n+1}=g(w_{n})=g^{n+1}(w_{0}),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

For non-elliptic functions
g
, the Slope Problem consists on determining the directions through which the orbits approach the boundary Denjoy-Wolff point of
g
. That is, given
w_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
, calculating the numbers
s\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
such that there exists a subsequence of the orbit satisfying
\arg(1-\overline{\tau}w_{n_{k}})\to s
.

In this context, it might be useful to substitute
\mathbb{D}
as a domain in order to ease the calculations. To do so, if
g
is a non-elliptic function, then let
S\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
be a Möbius transformation with
S(\tau)=\infty
, where

\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid\text{Im}(z)>0\}

is the upper halfplane. Using
S
,
g
can be conjugated to a holomorphic function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
, given by
f=S^{-1}\circ g\circ S
. This function
f
is non-elliptic, and its Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
. That is, for every
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
,
f^{n}(z_{0})
converges to
\infty
as
n
goes to
\infty
.

Following the same conjugation, pick
w_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
and let
z_{0}=S(w_{0})\in\mathbb{H}
.
Define the orbit

z_{n+1}=f(z_{n})=f^{n+1}(z_{0}),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

It is possible to check that
\arg(1-\overline{\tau}w_{n_{k}})\to s
if and only if
\pi/2-\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to s
. Using this notation, the following idea can be introduced:

Definition 1.2
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a holomorphic function whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
. Then, given
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, we define the set of slopes of
f
as

\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=\{s\in[0,\pi]:\exists\{n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}\text{ with }\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to s\},

where
\arg
denotes the principal branch of the argument function.

The properties of the set
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]
depends on the properties of
f
. For example, if the function
f
is hyperbolic, the slope problem goes back to Wolff
[
14
]
and Valiron
[
13
]
. Namely, it turns out that the orbits converge non-tangentially to the Denjoy-Wolff point with a definite slope. That is,
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
is a singleton in
(0,\pi)
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
(see
[
2
, Theorem 4.3.4]
). Recently, Bracci and Poggi-Corradini proved that the function
\mathrm{Slope}[f,\cdot]\colon\mathbb{H}\to(0,\pi)
is surjective and harmonic
[
5
, Property 2 (a) and Property 2 (b)]
.

When
f
is parabolic, the behavior of the slope strongly depends on the hyperbolic step of the function (see Definition
2.1
). If
f
is of positive hyperbolic step, Pommerenke proved that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]\subset\{0,\pi\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
; see
[
12
, Remark 1]
. This can be slightly improved, as noted in Remark
2.3
: the set of slopes is always a singleton and does not depend on the starting point.

There are not many references in the literature about this problem when
f
is of zero hyperbolic step. Indeed, the study of such scenario is the purpose of this paper. One of the first references on this is the remarkable paper of Wolff
[
14
, Section 6]
, where he found a parabolic function
f
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]
contains at least two points. In the last two decades there are some new results on the set of slopes of parabolic functions that can be embedded into a continuous semigroup (see
[
3
,
7
,
11
]
) but, as far as we know, there are no advances in the discrete setting. In Section
2
, we obtain that the set of slopes of a parabolic function of zero hyperbolic step is always an interval which does not depend on the initial point of the orbit (see Theorem
2.5
). We also show that for any close interval
[a,b]\subset[0,\pi]
there exists a parabolic function
f
of zero hyperbolic step such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=[a,b]
(see Theorem
2.9
).

The Slope Problem can also be introduced in the continuous setting of Complex Dynamics. Indeed, the results that we have just introduced have an exact analogue in this other setting; see
[
4
, Sections 17.4-6]
for reference. There, the orbits are continuous curves defined through non-elliptic semigroups (see Definition
2.6
), and the directions through which orbits converge to the boundary are also studied. Even if the results are conceptually similar, the fact that the orbits can either be connected or discrete sets completely changes the problem from a mathematical point of view.

To develop some new ideas, we also introduce a family of parabolic functions that has been previously considered in the literature
[
1
,
6
]
. This family is defined in terms of a real parameter and a positive finite measure. In Section
3
we characterize the set of slopes of these functions depending on some integrability requirements. Using these ideas we are able to provide explicit examples of parabolic functions
f
of zero hyperbolic step with some regularity at the Denjoy-Wolff point for which
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=[0,\pi]
or
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=[0,\pi/2]
. This highly contrasts with some known results on semigroups, where regularity is used to assure that the orbits converge to the boundary with a definite slope; see
[
10
, Theorem 21]
or
[
4
, Proposition 17.5.5]
.

2.
Parabolic functions

From now on, we will work with functions
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
that are parabolic and whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
. These functions can be split into two different classes. To introduce them, let
\rho
be the pseudo-hyperbolic distance in
\mathbb{H}
given by

\rho(z,w)=\left|\dfrac{z-w}{z-\overline{w}}\right|,\quad z,w\in\mathbb{H}.

With the aid of Schwarz-Pick Lemma, it is clear that the sequence
\rho(z_{n+1},z_{n})=\rho(f(z_{n}),f(z_{n-1}))
is non-increasing for any initial point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
, and therefore it must converge. In fact, if its limit is zero for some initial point, then it is also zero for every initial point, as stated in
[
2
, Corollary 4.6.9.(i)]
. With this idea, the following property can be defined:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a parabolic function. We say that
f
is of zero hyperbolic step if
\rho(z_{n+1},z_{n})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
for some (equivalently, for every)
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
. Otherwise, we say
f
is of positive hyperbolic step.

Determining general conditions to distinguish the hyperbolic step of parabolic functions is a subtle task. For instance, the following is a well-known result about that:

Theorem 2.2
.

[
12
, Theorem 1]

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a parabolic function with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
. The following limit converges:

b=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}{y_{n}}\in\mathbb{R},

where
z_{n}=x_{n}+iy_{n}=f^{n}(z_{0})
,
n\in\mathbb{N},
is the orbit of some initial point
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
. Furthermore,
z_{n+1}/z_{n}\to 1
and
y_{n+1}/y_{n}\to 1
as
n\to+\infty
. Additionally,
b=0
if and only if
f
is of zero hyperbolic step.

Remark 2.3
.

Using the ideas in the last result, Pommerenke
[
12
, Remark 1]
proved that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]\subset\{0,\pi\}
if
f
is a parabolic function of positive hyperbolic step. However, note that if
z,w\in\mathbb{H}
are such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{Slope}[f,w]=\{\pi\}
, then
\rho(f^{n}(z),f^{n}(w))\to 1
, which contradicts the Schwarz-Pick Lemma. Therefore, one of the following must apply:
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{0\}
for every
z\in\mathbb{H}
or
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{\pi\}
for every
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Indeed, for any given
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, Theorem
2.2
implies that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}{y_{n+1}-y_{n}}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dfrac{x_{n+1}-x_{n}}{y_{n}}\dfrac{y_{n}}{y_{n+1}-y_{n}}=\alpha\cdot(+\infty),

where we have used that
y_{n+1}/y_{n}\to 1
, as shown in Theorem
2.2
. In that case, it is obvious that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=\{0\}
if and only if
\alpha>0
. Therefore, the sign of the limit
\alpha=\alpha(z_{0})
is independent of the initial point.

In contrast to the case of hyperbolic or parabolic functions with positive hyperbolic step, there are not many references concerning the zero hyperbolic step situation. To develop some advances on this topic, we are lead by the analogue results in continuous iteration. In seek of completeness, let us start by stating a lemma on sequences:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\{x_{n}\}
be a bounded sequence with
x_{n+1}-x_{n}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. The set of limit points of the sequence, that is,
L=\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\{n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}\text{ with }x_{n_{k}}\to s\}
is a closed and connected set.

Proof.

The set of limit points of a sequence is always closed. Therefore, it only remains to show that
L
is connected. To do so, let
a,b\in L
with
a\leq b
and choose any
a\leq x\leq b
. We can show that
x\in L
as follows:

Fix
\epsilon_{1}=1
. Let
N_{1}
be such that
\left|x_{n+1}-x_{n}\right|\leq 1
for all
n\geq N_{1}
. By definition, there exists
n_{1},m_{1}\geq N_{1}
such that
\left|x_{n_{1}}-a\right|\leq 1
and
\left|x_{m_{1}}-b\right|\leq 1
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
n_{1}\leq m_{1}
. Then, there exists
n_{1}\leq l_{1}\leq m_{1}
such that
\left|x_{l_{1}}-x\right|\leq 1
.

Similarly, fix
\epsilon_{k}=1/k
for
k\geq 2
. Let
N_{k}\geq l_{k-1}
be such that
\left|x_{n+1}-x_{n}\right|\leq\epsilon_{k}
for all
n\geq N_{k}
. By definition, there exists
n_{k},m_{k}\geq N_{k}
such that
\left|x_{n_{k}}-a\right|\leq\epsilon_{k}
and
\left|x_{m_{k}}-b\right|\leq\epsilon_{k}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
n_{k}\leq m_{k}
. Then, there exists
n_{k}\leq l_{k}\leq m_{k}
such that
\left|x_{l_{k}}-x\right|\leq\epsilon_{k}
. That is,
x_{l_{k}}\to x
, and so
x\in L
.
∎

As in the continuous setting (see
[
4
, p. 501]
), we can prove that the set of slopes is a (non necessarily trivial) closed and connected set, regardless of the orbit being discrete:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be a parabolic function of zero hyperbolic step with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
. Then,
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\mathrm{Slope}[f,w]
for all
z,w\in\mathbb{H}
. Furthermore, there exists
0\leq a\leq b\leq\pi
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=[a,b]
for any
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Step 1.

\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
does not depend on
z
.

The idea for this proof comes from
[
4
, p. 51, Lemma 1.8.6]
. Given
z_{0},w_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
, we will show that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]\subset\mathrm{Slope}[f,w_{0}]
. By symmetry, that is enough to conclude that the set of slopes does not depend on the starting point of the orbit.

To do so, let
s\in\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]
. In that case, there exists
\{n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to s
. Then, one can write
z_{n_{k}}=r_{n_{k}}e^{i\alpha_{n_{k}}}
, where
r_{n_{k}}\to+\infty
and
\alpha_{n_{k}}\to s
as
n_{k}\to+\infty
.

Now, pick a limit point
s^{\prime}
of the set
\{\arg(w_{n_{k}})\}
and a subsequence (which we will denote as
n_{k}
as well, for brevity) such that
\arg(w_{n_{k}})\to s^{\prime}
. We will show that
s=s^{\prime}
. Again, write
w_{n_{k}}=t_{n_{k}}e^{i\beta_{n_{k}}}
, where
t_{n_{k}}\to+\infty
and
\beta_{n_{k}}\to s^{\prime}
as
n_{k}\to+\infty
. As
f
is of zero hyperbolic step, by
[
2
, Corollary 4.6.9]
, we have

\rho(z_{n_{k}},w_{n_{k}})=\rho(f^{n_{k}}(z_{0}),f^{n_{k}}(w_{0}))\to 0,\quad\text{as }k\to+\infty.

But

\displaystyle\rho(z_{n_{k}},w_{n_{k}})

\displaystyle=\left|\dfrac{r_{n_{k}}e^{i\alpha_{n_{k}}}-t_{n_{k}}e^{i\beta_{n_{k}}}}{r_{n_{k}}e^{i\alpha_{n_{k}}}-t_{n_{k}}e^{-i\beta_{n_{k}}}}\right|=\left|\dfrac{\lambda_{n_{k}}e^{i\alpha_{n_{k}}}-(1-\lambda_{n_{k}})e^{i\beta_{n_{k}}}}{\lambda_{n_{k}}e^{i\alpha_{n_{k}}}-(1-\lambda_{n_{k}})e^{-i\beta_{n_{k}}}}\right|,

where

\lambda_{n_{k}}=\dfrac{r_{n_{k}}}{r_{n_{k}}+t_{n_{k}}}.

Suppose that
s\neq s^{\prime}
. Note that
\lambda_{n_{k}}\in(0,1)
. In particular, choose a subsequence (again, also denoted as
n_{k}
) such that
\lambda_{n_{k}}\to\lambda\in[0,1]
. In that case, taking limits, one has

\dfrac{\lambda e^{is}-(1-\lambda)e^{is^{\prime}}}{\lambda e^{is}-(1-\lambda)e^{-is^{\prime}}}=0,

where
\lambda e^{is}-(1-\lambda)e^{-is^{\prime}}\neq 0
because
s\neq s^{\prime}
.
Thus
\lambda e^{is}-(1-\lambda)e^{is^{\prime}}=0
, from which it follows that
\lambda=1/2
and
e^{i(s-s^{\prime})}=1
. Since
s-s^{\prime}\in[-\pi,\pi]
, we get
s=s^{\prime}
, which contradicts the assumption.

Step 2.

\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
is a closed interval.

Remember that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
is the set of limit points of
\{\arg(z_{n})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. But, due to Theorem
2.2
,
\arg(z_{n+1})-\arg(z_{n})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
.
Then, Lemma
2.4
assures that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
is a closed interval.
∎

As said before, the slope problem has also been of interest in the theory of continuous iteration, that is, the theory of semigroups. We recall:

Definition 2.6
.

A (continuous) semigroup (of holomorphic self-maps of the upper halfplane) is a family of holomorphic functions
\{\phi_{t}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\mid t\geq 0\}
such that

(a)

\phi_{0}
is the identity map in
\mathbb{H}
,

(b)

\phi_{s+t}=\phi_{s}\circ\phi_{t},
for
s,t\geq 0
,

(c)

the map
t\in[0,+\infty)\mapsto\phi_{t}
is continuous, with respect to the Euclidean topology and the topology given by the uniform convergence on compact sets of
\mathbb{H}
.

A semigroup
\{\phi_{t}\}
is parabolic (resp. of zero or positive hyperbolic step) if there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
\phi_{t_{0}}
is parabolic (resp. of zero hyperbolic step). It can be checked that in such a case all the functions
\phi_{t}
, with
t>0
, are parabolic (resp, of zero or positive hyperbolic step); see
[
4
, Remark 8.3.4, Definition 9.3.4]
.

One can also consider the set of slopes in the continuous setting: given a non-elliptic semigroup
\{\phi_{t}\}
, the set of slopes of the semigroup at
z\in\mathbb{H}
is defined as

\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]=\{s\in[0,\pi]:\exists\{t_{k}\}\subset[0,+\infty),\text{ with }t_{k}\to+\infty,\,\arg(\phi_{t_{k}}(z))\to s\}.

In this context, the following is a well-known and celebrated result:

Theorem 2.7
.

[
11
, Theorem 1]

For any
0\leq a\leq b\leq\pi
there exists a parabolic semigroup
\{\phi_{t}\}
of zero hyperbolic step whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]=[a,b]
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The actual statement in
[
11
, Theorem 1]
is different, as they work with the set
\pi/2-\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]
.

The theory of semigroups and the theory of discrete iteration of holomorphic self-maps are strongly linked. Indeed, one can use the ideas for semigroups in order to give the following result:

Theorem 2.9
.

Given
0\leq a\leq b\leq\pi
, there exists a parabolic function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
of zero hyperbolic step with Denjoy-Wolff point at
\infty
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=[a,b]
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Using Theorem
2.7
, fix a parabolic semigroup
\{\phi_{t}\}
of zero hyperbolic step whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]=[a,b]
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
. Set
f=\phi_{1}
, and notice that
z_{n}=f^{n}(z_{0})=\phi_{n}(z_{0})
. We will prove that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=[a,b]
. Clearly,
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]\subset\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]=[a,b]
. Then, it is enough to show that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]\supset\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]=[a,b]
. To do so, let
s\in\mathrm{Slope}[\{\phi_{t}\},z]
. Thus, let
t_{k}\geq 0
be an increasing sequence such that
t_{k}\to+\infty
and
\arg(\phi_{t_{k}}(z))\to s
. We may assume that
t_{1}\geq 1
. For each
t_{k}
, consider
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
given by
n_{k}\leq t_{k}<n_{k}+1
. We claim that
\rho(\phi_{t_{k}}(z),\phi_{n_{k}}(z))\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
, and then one can argue as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
to see that there exists a subsequence (again, denoted in the same way) such that
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to s
, from which we have that
s\in\mathrm{Slope}[f,z].

To prove the claim, let
K\subset\mathbb{H}
be the compact set given by
K=\{\phi_{s}(z):s\in[0,1]\}
, and notice that
\phi_{n_{k}}(z),\phi_{t_{k}}(z)\in\phi_{n_{k}}(K)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, it is enough to prove that

\sup_{w\in K}\rho(\phi_{n}(z),\phi_{n}(w))\to 0,\quad n\to+\infty.

To do this, up to a standard argument of compactness, it is enough to prove that

\rho(\phi_{n}(z),\phi_{n}(w))\to 0,\quad n\to+\infty,

for all
w\in K
. But this is a consequence of an idea due to Pommerenke
[
12
, Theorem 1]
(which can be found in
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
; see also
[
2
, Corollary 4.6.9.(iv)]
for a proof using models).
∎

3.
Examples with definite slope

In this section, the set
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]
will be characterized for a family of parabolic functions that has been previously considered in the literature. To introduce it, let us start by linking
[
1
, Theorem 6.2.1]
together with
[
9
, Chapter 5, Lemma 2]
to obtain the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Every parabolic function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
can be uniquely written as

f(z)=z+\beta+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{1+tz}{t-z}d\mu(t),\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu
is a positive finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

In
[
1
, Theorem 6.4.1]
, Aaronson studied some properties of
f
under the assumption that
\mu
is of compact support. For example, if
\beta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, it is shown that
x_{n}
is bounded, and so
\text{Slope}[f,z]=\{\pi/2\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
. If
\beta\neq\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, then
y_{n}
is bounded. Indeed, if
\beta>\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, then
x_{n}\to+\infty
and
\text{Slope}[f,z]=\{0\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
. Similarly, if
\beta<\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, then
x_{n}\to-\infty
and
\text{Slope}[f,z]=\{\pi\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

In this section, we generalize the former ideas of Aaronson for a larger family of functions. To introduce them, suppose that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<+\infty.

In that case,
f
can be rewritten as

f(z)=z+\beta-\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)+p(z),\quad p(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{1+t^{2}}{t-z}d\mu(t),\quad z\in\mathbb{H}.

This family of functions has also been considered in
[
6
]
, where its hyperbolic step is characterized in terms of
\mu
and
\beta
.

Let us begin by stating the following consequence of Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem:

Lemma 3.2
.

[
6
, Lemma 3.3]

Assume that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<\infty
. Then,
\displaystyle\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}p(z)=0
.

Using the former description, we can now show a result on the set of slopes of convergence:

Theorem 3.3
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}d\mu(t)<\infty
and
\beta=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
. Then
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{\pi/2\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

First of all, notice that using Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem, one can see that

\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}zp(z)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+t^{2})d\mu(t)<0,

which implies that
\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}\text{arg}(zp(z))=\pi
. Then, we rewrite

f(z)=z-\dfrac{q(z)}{z},\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

and

(1)

\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}q(z)>0.

With this idea in mind, fix
0<\alpha<\pi/2
, and define
S=\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\left|\arg(z)-\pi/2\right|<\alpha\}
. We will show that there exists
L=L(\alpha)
such that
f(S^{*})\subset S^{*}
, where
S^{*}=S\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Im}(z)>L\}.
To do so, we split
S
in three parts as follows: define
S_{0}=\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\left|\arg(z)-\pi/2\right|\leq\alpha/2\}\subset S
, and also
S_{-}=(S\setminus S_{0})\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Re}(z)<0\}
,
S_{+}=S\setminus(S_{0}\cup S_{-})
.

Note that, due to Lemma
3.2
, we can choose
L_{1}>0
such that

\left|\dfrac{q(z)}{z}\right|<1,\quad z\in S_{0},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{1}.

In that case, it is possible to find
L_{1}^{*}\geq L_{1}
such that, for all
L>L_{1}^{*}
,
f
maps
S_{0}^{*}=S_{0}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\mathrm{Im}(z)>L\}
into
S^{*}
.

Similarly, by (
1
), it is possible to find
L_{2}>0
such that

\left|\arg(q(z))\right|\leq\dfrac{\alpha}{4},\quad z\in S_{-},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}.

In that case,

\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\dfrac{5\alpha}{4}\leq\arg\left(-\dfrac{q(z)}{z}\right)\leq\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\dfrac{\alpha}{4},\quad z\in S_{-},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}.

In particular, this means that
\arg(f(z))>\arg(z)
if
z\in S_{-}
with
\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}
. We may also suppose that

\left|-\dfrac{q(z)}{z}\right|<1,\quad z\in S_{-},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}.

In that case, it is possible to find
L_{2}^{*}\geq L_{2}
such that, or all
L>L_{2}^{*}
,
f
maps
S_{-}^{*}=S_{-}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\mathrm{Im}(z)>L\}
into
S^{*}
. Similarly, it is also possible to find
L_{3}^{*}>0
such that, for all
L>L_{3}^{*}
,
f
maps
S_{+}^{*}=S_{+}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Im}(z)>L\}
into
S^{*}
.

Summing up, if
L>\max\{L_{1}^{*},L_{2}^{*},L_{3}^{*}\}
, then
S^{*}=S_{-}^{*}\cup S_{0}^{*}\cup S_{+}^{*}
, with
f(S_{-}^{*})\subset S^{*}
,
f(S_{0}^{*})\subset S^{*}
, and
f(S_{+}^{*})\subset S^{*}
. Then
f(S^{*})\subset S^{*}
, as we wanted to prove.

Using the former ideas, one can see that, for any
0<\alpha<\pi/2
, it is possible to find
y_{0}=y_{0}(\alpha)>0
such that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,iy_{0}]\subset[\pi/2-\alpha,\pi/2+\alpha]
. Using Theorem
2.5
, we conclude that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]\subset\{\pi/2\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.
∎

The case where
\beta\neq\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
can be characterized under more general hypotheses:

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<\infty
. If
\beta>\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, then
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{0\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
. If
\beta<\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t)
, then
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{\pi\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Proof.

Let us define

\tilde{\beta}=\beta-\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t).

Assume
\tilde{\beta}>0
(the case
\tilde{\beta}<0
follows using similar ideas). We claim that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\infty\\
\text{Im}(z)>L\end{subarray}}\dfrac{p(z)}{z}=0,

for any
L>0
. To prove the claim, we use that if
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{H}
, then

\left|\dfrac{t}{t-z}\right|\leq\dfrac{\left|z\right|}{y}.

Notice that this is equivalent to

((t-x)^{2}+y^{2})(x^{2}+y^{2})-y^{2}t^{2}=(xt-x^{2}-y^{2})^{2}\geq 0.

Thus, if
\left|t\right|\geq 1
,

\dfrac{1}{\left|z\right|}\dfrac{1+t^{2}}{\left|t-z\right|}\leq\dfrac{1}{\left|z\right|}\dfrac{2t^{2}}{\left|t-z\right|}\leq\dfrac{2}{y}\left|t\right|\leq\dfrac{2}{L}\left|t\right|,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\,y>L.

Therefore, the claim follows from Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem.

With this in mind, fix
0<\alpha<\pi/4
and define
S=\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\arg(z)<2\alpha\}
. Split
S
in two domains as follows: define
S_{u}=\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\alpha\leq\arg(z)<2\alpha\}
and
S_{l}=S\setminus S_{u}
. Note that
S_{u}
is a non-tangential region at
\infty
. Then, by Lemma
3.2
, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\infty\\
z\in S_{u}\end{subarray}}\arg(\tilde{\beta}+p(z))=0.

In that case, it is possible to find
L_{1}>0
such that
\arg(f(z)-z)=\arg(\tilde{\beta}+p(z))<\alpha
, if
z\in S_{u}
with
\text{Im}(z)>L_{1}
. Thus,
\arg(f(z))<2\alpha
, because
S
is a cone.

Similarly, one has

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\to\infty\\
z\in S_{l},\,\text{Im}(z)>L\end{subarray}}\left(\dfrac{\tilde{\beta}}{z}+\dfrac{p(z)}{z}\right)=0,

for any
L>0
. Then, it is possible to find
L_{2}>0
such that

\arg\left(1+\dfrac{\tilde{\beta}}{z}+\dfrac{p(z)}{z}\right)<\alpha,\quad z\in S_{l},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}.

Thus,

\arg(f(z))=\arg(z)+\arg\left(1+\dfrac{\tilde{\beta}}{z}+\dfrac{p(z)}{z}\right)<2\alpha,\quad z\in S_{l},\,\text{Im}(z)>L_{2}.

We may suppose that
L_{1}=L_{2}
. Then, define
S^{*}=S\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Im}(z)>L_{1}\}
,
S_{u}^{*}=S_{u}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Im}(z)>L_{1}\}
and
S_{l}^{*}=S_{l}\cap\{z\in\mathbb{H}:\text{Im}(z)>L_{1}\}
. It is clear that
S^{*}=S_{u}^{*}\cup S_{l}^{*}
, and we just justified that
f(S_{u}^{*})\subset S^{*}
and
f(S_{l}^{*})\subset S^{*}
. So,
f(S^{*})\subset S^{*}
.

In that case, for any
0<\alpha<\pi/4
, one could choose
z_{0}\in S^{*}=S^{*}(\alpha)
and then it is clear that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]\subset[0,2\alpha]
. Then, by Theorem
2.5
, we conclude that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]=\{0\}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.
∎

In contrast with Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
3.3
does not hold under the more general hypothesis given by

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<+\infty.

Indeed, for each
s\in(0,\pi)
, we now present a function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
that satisfies this hypothesis but
\text{Slope}[f,z]=\{s\}
. To do this, let
m
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
, and let us proceed with the following examples:

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mu
be the positive finite measure on
\mathbb{R}
given by

\dfrac{d\mu}{dm}(t)=\dfrac{1}{(1+t^{2})t}\chi_{(1,+\infty)}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<+\infty,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}d\mu(t)=+\infty.

With the former notation, let
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
be given by

f(z)=z+p(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

where

p(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{1+t^{2}}{t-z}d\mu(t)=\int_{(1,+\infty)}\dfrac{dt}{(t-z)t}=-\dfrac{\log(1-z)}{z}.

One can check that
\lim_{z\to\infty}\arg(zp(z))=\pi
. Then, following the same ideas as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, it is possible to see that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]={\pi/2}
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

Remark 3.6
.

Following the notation on Theorem
3.1
, note that
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z]={\pi/2}
if
\mu
is a symmetric measure and
\beta=0
; see the discussion preceding the Proposition 4.1 in

]
. However, Example

shows that this is not necessary, even in the case where

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<+\infty,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}d\mu(t)=+\infty.

Example 3.7
.

For

, let

be the positive finite measure on

given by

\dfrac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dm}(t)=\dfrac{1}{(1+t^{2})t^{\alpha}}\chi_{(0,+\infty)}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

With the former notation, let

where

This integral can be explicitly calculated by using Cauchy’s Residue Theorem. In order to do so, we use some curves given in the following figure:

4.
Examples with undefinite slope

The goal of this section is to explicitly construct parabolic functions
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
of zero hyperbolic step whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
such that
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi]
or
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi/2]
. To achieve this, consider
\{a_{k}\}\subset(0,+\infty)
,
\{\gamma_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
two sequences satisfying

(2)

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{a_{k}}{\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}<+\infty,

and define the holomorphic function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
given by

f(z)=z+\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}p_{k}(z),\quad p_{k}(z)=\dfrac{a_{k}}{\gamma_{k}-z},\quad z\in\mathbb{H}.

Following the notation on Theorem
3.1
, it is possible to check that

f(z)=z+\beta+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{1+tz}{t-z}d\mu(t),\quad z\in\mathbb{H},

where

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{a_{k}}{1+\gamma_{k}^{2}}\delta_{\gamma_{k}},\quad\beta=\int_{\mathbb{R}}td\mu(t),\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|t\right|d\mu(t)<+\infty.

Our goal is to show that, under suitable conditions on
\{a_{k}\}
and
\{\gamma_{k}\}
, one has
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi]
or
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi/2]
.

Let us also recall that the orbit of every
z_{0}\in\mathbb{H}
is denoted as

z_{n+1}=f(z_{n}),\quad z_{n}=x_{n}+iy_{n},

where
\{y_{n}\}
is a non-decreasing sequence since
\mathrm{Im}(f(z)-z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
.

4.1.
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi]

Suppose that

(3)

\displaystyle z_{0}=i,

(4)

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=(-1)^{k}\left|\gamma_{k}\right|,\quad\left|\gamma_{1}\right|\geq 1,\quad 4\left|\gamma_{k}\right|\leq\left|\gamma_{k+1}\right|,

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}a_{l}\leq\dfrac{\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}{4k},\quad\sum_{l=k+1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|}\leq\dfrac{\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}{8k},

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}a_{l}\leq\dfrac{a_{k}}{80k},\quad\sum_{l=k+1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|}\leq\dfrac{a_{k}}{160k\left|\gamma_{k}\right|},

For example, these conditions are achieved if
a_{k}=C_{1}(k!)^{2}
and
\left|\gamma_{k}\right|=C_{2}(k!)^{4}
for big enough
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

In order to clarify the exposition, note that (
4
) implies that

(7)

\left|\gamma_{k}\right|\uparrow+\infty,\quad\dfrac{\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}{k}\uparrow+\infty,\quad k\to+\infty,

and (
2
) and (
7
) imply that

(8)

\gamma_{k}^{2}/a_{k}\to+\infty,\quad k\to+\infty.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume (
2
)-(
6
). For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{n}\leq\left|\gamma_{k}\right|/k
, one has
\left|x_{n}\right|\leq 2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exist
k,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
\left|x_{N+1}\right|>2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
,
\left|x_{n}\right|\leq 2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
for
n\leq N
and
y_{N+1}\leq\left|\gamma_{k}\right|/k
. Define
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:\left|x\right|\leq 2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|,\,y\geq 1\}
, and notice that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for all
n\leq N
(see (
3
)). Moreover, if
z=x+iy\in\Omega
and
l\leq k
, then
\left|\text{Re}(p_{l}(z))\right|\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq a_{l}
, as
y\geq 1
.

Similarly, if
l>k
, then

\left|\text{Re}(p_{l}(z))\right|\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}-x\right|}\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|-2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|},

by (
4
). Using these inequalities and (
5
), one has

\left|\text{Re}(f(z_{N})-z_{N})\right|\leq\left|\gamma_{k}\right|/2
, and then
\left|x_{N}\right|\geq 3\left|\gamma_{k}\right|/2>\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
.

Indeed, if
x_{N+1}<-2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
(and similarly if
x_{N+1}>2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|
), then
-2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|\leq x_{N}\leq-3\left|\gamma_{k}\right|/2
. Thus,
\text{Re}(p_{l}(z_{N}))>0
for all
l\leq k
. Moreover,

\text{Re}(p_{k}(z_{N}))=\dfrac{a_{k}(\gamma_{k}-x_{N})}{(\gamma_{k}-x_{N})^{2}+y_{N}^{2}}\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{20\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}.

Therefore, by (
6
),
\text{Re}(f(z_{N})-z_{N})>0
, and then
x_{N+1}>x_{N}
. Note this is a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume (
2
)-(
6
). Then,
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=[0,\pi]
.

Proof.

It suffices to find two subsequences
\{z_{n_{k}}\}
and
\{z_{m_{k}}\}
with
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to 0
and
\arg(z_{m_{k}})\to\pi
. As the construction is similar for both of them, we will only show how to find the sequence
\{z_{n_{k}}\}
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
\gamma_{k}>0
(see (
4
)) there exists
N_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{N_{k}}\leq\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1}
. Note that the map

may not be injective.

Consider the set
A=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:x_{N_{k}}\geq\gamma_{k}/2>0\}
. For every
k\in A
one has
\arg(z_{N_{k}})\leq\arctan(2/k)
. Then, if
A
is infinite, we can find a sequence with
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
.

On the other hand, if
A
is finite, it is possible to kind
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
and
\gamma_{k}>0
one has

y_{N_{k}}\leq\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1},\quad-2\gamma_{k}\leq x_{N_{k}}\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2},

where Lemma
4.1
has been used. We claim that there exist
C>1
and
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
, such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{1}
and
\gamma_{k}>0
there exists
M_{k}\geq N_{k}
with
y_{M_{k}}\leq C\gamma_{k}/k
and
x_{M_{k}}\geq\gamma_{k}/2
. In particular,
\arg(z_{M_{k}})\leq\arctan(2C/k)
. Therefore, the existence of a sequence with
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
follows from the claim.

To prove the claim, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
and
\gamma_{k}>0
, define
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:-2\gamma_{k}\leq x\leq\gamma_{k}/2,\,1\leq y\leq\gamma_{k}/k\}
, and notice that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for
n\leq N_{k}
.

If
z=x+iy\in\Omega
and
l\leq k
, then
\text{Im}(p_{l}(z))\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq a_{l}
. Similarly, if
l>k
, then

\text{Im}(p_{l}(z))\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|-\left|x\right|}\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|-2\gamma_{k}}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|},

by (
4

\left|\text{Im}(f(z_{N_{k}})-z_{N_{k}})\right|\leq\gamma_{k}/k

\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1}\leq 2\gamma_{k}/k
.

Let us now define
\Omega^{*}=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:-2\gamma_{k}\leq x\leq\gamma_{k}/2,\,\gamma_{k}/k\leq y\leq C\gamma_{k}/k\}
for some

to fix. Note that if

and

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}k}{\gamma_{k}}.

Similarly, if

, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}-x\right|}\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|-2\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\left|\gamma_{l}\right|}

by (

\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))\geq\dfrac{a_{k}\gamma_{k}/2}{9\gamma_{k}^{2}+C^{2}\gamma_{k}^{2}/k^{2}}\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{20\gamma_{k}},

if

, and

\text{Im}(p_{k}(z))\leq\dfrac{a_{k}y}{(\gamma_{k}-x)^{2}}\leq\dfrac{4a_{k}y}{\gamma_{k}^{2}}.

Therefore, by (

(9)

\text{Re}(p(z))\geq\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))-\sum_{l\neq k}\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{40\gamma_{k}},\quad z\in\Omega^{*}.

Similarly, by (

(10)

\text{Im}(p(z))\leq\text{Im}(p_{k}(z))+\sum_{l\neq k}\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{5a_{k}y}{\gamma_{k}^{2}},\quad z\in\Omega^{*}.

To finish, choose
S_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with

(11)

\dfrac{100\gamma_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\leq S_{k}\leq\dfrac{\log(C/2)}{\log(1+5a_{k}/\gamma_{k}^{2})}.

In order to clarify this, by (
8
), define
\alpha_{k}=\gamma_{k}^{2}/a_{k}

for all

but, using (

x_{L_{k}}\geq x_{N_{k}+1}+S_{k}\dfrac{a_{k}}{40\gamma_{k}}\geq-2\gamma_{k}+\dfrac{5}{2}\gamma_{k}=\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}.

From these calculations, one can deduce that there must exist

This is the claim we wanted to prove, with any

, and any

.
∎

4.2.
\text{Slope}[f,z]=[0,\pi/2]

Suppose that

(12)

\displaystyle z_{0}=i,

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\geq 1,\quad 4\gamma_{k}\leq\gamma_{k+1},

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{k}a_{l}\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{24k},\quad\sum_{l=k+1}^{+\infty}\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}}\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{24k},

For example, these conditions are achieved if
a_{k}=C_{1}(k!)^{3}
and
\gamma_{k}=C_{2}(k!)^{6}
for big enough
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

In order to clarify the exposition, note that (
13
) implies that

(16)

\left|\gamma_{k}\right|\uparrow+\infty,\quad\dfrac{\left|\gamma_{k}\right|}{k}\uparrow+\infty,\quad k\to+\infty,

and (
2
) and (

) imply that

(17)

\gamma_{k}^{2}/a_{k}\to+\infty,\quad k\to+\infty.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume (
2
), (
12
)-(
15
). For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{n}\leq\gamma_{k}/k
, one has
0\leq x_{n}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists
k,N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{N+1}\not\in[0,3\gamma_{k}/2]
,
0\leq x_{n}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2
for
n\leq N
and
y_{N+1}\leq\gamma_{k}/k
.

Assume that
x_{N+1}>3\gamma_{k}/2
, and define
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq x\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2,\,y\geq 1\}
, and notice that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for all
n\leq N
. Moreover, if
z=x+iy\in\Omega
and
l\leq k
, then
\left|\text{Re}(p_{l}(z))\right|\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq a_{l}
. Similarly, if
l>k
, then

\left|\text{Re}(p_{l}(z))\right|\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}-x}\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}-2\gamma_{k}}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}},

by (
13
). Using these inequalities and (
14
), one has

\left|\text{Re}(f(z_{N})-z_{N})\right|\leq\gamma_{k}/4
, and then
x_{N}\in[5\gamma_{k}/4,3\gamma_{k}/2]
. Thus,
\text{Re}(p_{l}(z_{N}))<0
for all
l\leq k
. Moreover,

\left|\text{Re}(p_{k}(z_{N}))\right|=\dfrac{a_{k}(x_{N}-\gamma_{k})}{(\gamma_{k}-x_{N})^{2}+y_{N}^{2}}\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{5\gamma_{k}}.

Therefore, by (
15
),
\text{Re}(f(z_{N})-z_{N})<0
, and then
x_{N+1}<x_{N}
. Note this is a contradiction.

Similarly, if
x_{N+1}<0
, notice once more that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for all
n\leq N
. Moreover, if
x_{N}\in[0,\gamma_{1})
, then
\text{Re}(p_{l}(z_{N}))>0
for all
l\in\mathbb{N}
, and so
x_{N+1}>x_{N}
, which is a contradiction. In other case, find
L\in\mathbb{N}
such that
x_{N}\in[\gamma_{L},\gamma_{L+1})
. If
z=x+iy\in\Omega
and
l\leq L
, then
\left|\text{Re}(p_{l}(z))\right|\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq a_{l}
. Therefore, by (
14
), one has that

\text{Re}(p(z_{N}))\geq-\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left|\text{Re}(p(z_{N}))\right|\geq-\dfrac{\gamma_{L}}{2}.

Therefore,
x_{N+1}\geq\gamma_{L}/2
, which is also a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume (
2
), (
12
)-(
15
). Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:\gamma_{k}/2\leq x\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2,\,1\leq y\leq k\gamma_{k}\}
. Let
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for
N\leq n\leq M
and
z_{M+1}\not\in\Omega
. Then,
\gamma_{k}/2\leq x_{M+1}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2
and
y_{M+1}>k\gamma_{k}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that if
z_{N}\in\Omega
, then
\gamma_{k}/2\leq x_{N+1}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2
. To do this, split
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, where

\Omega_{1}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:3\gamma_{k}/4\leq x\leq 5\gamma_{k}/4,\,1\leq y\leq k\gamma_{k}\},

\Omega_{2}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:\gamma_{k}/4<\left|x-\gamma_{k}\right|\leq\gamma_{k}/2,\,1\leq y\leq k\gamma_{k}\}.

Assume
z\in\Omega_{1}
. If
l<k
, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{k}},

by (
13
). If
l>k
, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}},

also by (
13
). Since
y\geq 1
,
\left|p_{k}(z)\right|\leq a_{k}
. Therefore, by (
14
), one has

\left|p(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{4},

and so

\left|x_{N}-x_{N+1}\right|\leq\left|p(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{4},

meaning that

\left|x_{N+1}-\gamma_{k}\right|\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}.

Following a similar idea, assume
z\in\Omega_{2}
. If
l<k
, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{4a_{l}}{\gamma_{k}},

by (
13
). If
l>k
, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}},

also by (
13
). Similarly,

\left|\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))\right|\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{4k^{2}\gamma_{k}}.

Therefore, by (
15
), the sign of
\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))
is the same as the sign of
\text{Re}(p(z))
, that is, the sign of
\gamma_{k}-x
. This means that

\left|x_{N+1}-\gamma_{k}\right|\leq\left|x_{N}-\gamma_{k}\right|\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}.

∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume (
12
)-(
17
). Then,
\mathrm{Slope}[f,z_{0}]=[0,\pi/2]
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
, notice that
x_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, to show the result, it is enough to find two subsequences
\{z_{n_{k}}\}
and
\{z_{m_{k}}\}
with
\arg(z_{n_{k}})\to 0
and
\arg(z_{m_{k}})\to\pi/2
.

To do this, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
define
N_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{N_{k}}\leq\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1}
. Note that the map
k\mapsto N_{k}
may not be injective.

Consider the set
A=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\gamma_{k}/2\leq x_{N_{k}}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2\}
. For every
k\in A
one has
\arg(z_{N_{k}})\leq\arctan(2/k)
, and by Lemma
4.4
it is also possible to find
M_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with
M_{k}>N_{k}
such that
\arg(z_{M_{k}})\geq\arctan(2k/3)
. Then, if
A
is infinite, the result follows.

On the other hand, if
A
is finite, Lemma
4.3
assures that it is possible to find
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
one has

y_{N_{k}}\leq\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1},\quad 0\leq x_{N_{k}}\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}.

We claim that there exist
C>1
and
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
, such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{1}
there exist
N^{\prime}_{k},M^{\prime}_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with
M^{\prime}_{k}>N^{\prime}_{k}\geq N_{k}
such that
\arg(z_{N^{\prime}_{k}})\leq\arctan(2C/k)
and
\arg(z_{M^{\prime}_{k}})\leq\arctan(2k/3)
. Thus, the results follows from the claim.

To prove the claim, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq k_{0}
, define
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:-2\gamma_{k}\leq x\leq\gamma_{k}/2,\,1\leq y\leq\gamma_{k}/k\}
, and notice that
z_{n}\in\Omega
for
n\leq N_{k}
.

If
z=x+iy\in\Omega
and
l\leq k
, then
\text{Im}(p_{l}(z))\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq a_{l}
. Similarly, if
l>k
, then

\text{Im}(p_{l}(z))\leq\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}-x}\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}-2\gamma_{k}}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}},

by (
13
). Using these inequalities and (
14
), one has

\left|\text{Im}(f(z_{N_{k}})-z_{N_{k}})\right|\leq\gamma_{k}/k
, and then
\gamma_{k}/k<y_{N_{k}+1}\leq 2\gamma_{k}/k
.

Let us now define
\Omega^{*}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}:0\leq x\leq\gamma_{k}/2,\,\gamma_{k}/k\leq y\leq C\gamma_{k}/k\}
for some
C>1
to fix. If
z=x+iy\in\Omega^{*}
and
l<k
, then

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}k}{\gamma_{k}}.

In the case that
l>k
, one has

\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\leq\dfrac{a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}-\gamma_{k}/2}\leq\dfrac{2a_{l}}{\gamma_{l}},

by (
13
). Similarly,

\text{Im}(p_{k}(z))\leq\dfrac{4a_{k}y}{\gamma_{k}^{2}},\quad\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{4\gamma_{k}}.

Therefore,

(18)

\text{Im}(p(z))\leq\dfrac{5a_{k}y}{\gamma_{k}^{2}},\quad\text{Re}(p(z))\geq\text{Re}(p_{k}(z))-\sum_{l=1}^{k-1}\left|p_{l}(z)\right|\geq\dfrac{a_{k}}{8\gamma_{k}},

for all
z\in\Omega^{*}
, by (
15
).

To finish, choose
S_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
with

(19)

\dfrac{4\gamma_{k}^{2}}{a_{k}}\leq S_{k}\leq\dfrac{\log(C/2)}{\log(1+5a_{k}/\gamma_{k}^{2})}.

In order to clarify this, by (
17
), define
\alpha_{k}=\gamma_{k}^{2}/a_{k}
and note that, if
\log(C/2)>20
, then

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\dfrac{\log(C/2)}{\log(1+5/\alpha_{k})}-4\alpha_{k}=+\infty.

Define
L_{k}=N_{k}+1+S_{k}
, and assume that
z_{n}\in\Omega^{*}
for all
N_{k}+1\leq n\leq L_{k}
. In that case, using (
18
) and (
19
), one has

y_{L_{k}}\leq\left(1+\dfrac{5a_{k}}{\gamma_{k}^{2}}\right)^{S_{k}}y_{N_{k}+1}\leq C\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{k},

but, using (
18
) and (
19
),

x_{L_{k}}\geq x_{N_{k}+1}+S_{k}\dfrac{a_{k}}{8\gamma_{k}}\geq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2}.

From this calculations, one can deduce that there must exist
N_{k}+1<N^{\prime}_{k}<L_{k}
with
z_{n}\in\Omega^{*}
for all
N_{k}+1\leq n\leq N^{\prime}_{k}-1
and

x_{N^{\prime}_{k}}\geq\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{2},\quad y_{N^{\prime}_{k}}\leq C\dfrac{\gamma_{k}}{k}.

In particular,
\arg(z_{N^{\prime}_{k}})\leq\arctan(2C/k)
.

Moreover, if
k^{2}\geq C
, then
y_{N^{\prime}_{k}}\leq k\gamma_{k}
. Therefore, from Lemma
4.4
it is possible to find
M^{\prime}_{k}>N^{\prime}_{k}
such that

\gamma_{k}/2\leq x_{M^{\prime}_{k}+1}\leq 3\gamma_{k}/2,\quad y_{M^{\prime}_{k}+1}>k\gamma_{k},

from which it follows that
\arg(z_{M^{\prime}_{k}})\geq\arctan(2k/3)
.

This is the claim we wanted to prove, with any
C>1
such that
\log(C/2)>20
,
and any
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
with
k_{1}^{2}\geq C
.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

In the literature (see
[
8
]
), a parabolic function
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy-Wolff point
\tau
is said to be of angular-class of order
2
at
\tau
, denoted as
g\in C^{2}_{A}(\tau)
, if

g(z)=\tau+(z-\tau)+\dfrac{a_{2}}{2}(z-\tau)^{2}+\gamma(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{D},

where
a_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\gamma
is a holomorphic function on
\mathbb{D}
with
\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\dfrac{\gamma(z)}{(z-\tau)^{2}}=0
.

In
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
, the authors prove that a parabolic function
g\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is in
C^{2}_{A}(\tau)
if and only if its corresponding conjugated map
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
whose Denjoy-Wolff point is
\infty
satisfies that

\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}(f(z)-z)\in\mathbb{C}.

This can be used to show the key difference between the explicit examples given in this section and the one that Wolff proposed on
[
14
]
: the former limit is not convergent for Wolff’s function, while using Lemma
3.2
, any parabolic function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
such that
t\in L^{1}(\mu)
satisfies that
\angle\lim_{z\to\infty}(f(z)-z)=\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
where the notation of Theorem
3.1
has been used. Therefore, the examples of this section are endowed with the regularity of the class
C^{2}_{A}(\tau)
.

In the continuous setting, the examples developed by Kelgiannis in
[
11
, Theorem 1]
do not control how orbits behave. Instead, he used the concept of Koenigs map to propose a geometric description of the slope problem, using several estimates through different harmonic measures. In this sense, the proofs that are proposed in this paper seem rather more straight.
