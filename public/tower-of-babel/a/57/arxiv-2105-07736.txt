A deterministic Kaczmarz algorithm for solving linear systems
Changpeng Shao

Abstract

Introduction

Solving systems of linear equations is a fundamental problem in science and engineering. In practice, the size of linear equations is usually so large that iterative methods are more favourable. Among all the iterative methods, the
Kaczmarz method
(also known as
alternating projection
or
the alternating method of von Neumann
) is popular due to its simplicity and efficiency.

Assume that
A
is an
m\times n
real-valued matrix,
{\bf b}
is an
m\times 1
real-valued vector.
The Kaczmarz method solves the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
in the following way: Let the
i
-th row of
A
be
A_{i}^{{\tt T}}
, the
i
-th entry of
{\bf b}
be
b_{i}
. Arbitrarily choose an initial approximation
{\bf x}_{0}
of the solution. For
k=0,1,2,\cdots
, compute

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},

(1)

where
i_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
is chosen according to some predefined principles and
\alpha_{k}\in(0,2)
is the
relaxation parameter
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the 2-norm. In each iteration, the Kaczmarz method only uses one row of the matrix
A
, which makes it easy to implement. When the relaxation parameter
\alpha_{k}=1
, the Kaczmarz method has a clear geometric meaning. It returns the solution of
A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}={\bf b}_{i_{k}}
that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{k}
. Namely, the approximate solution
{\bf x}_{k+1}
discovered in the
k
-th step is the orthogonal projection of
{\bf x}_{k}
on the hyperplane defined by
A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}={\bf b}_{i_{k}}
.

This method was first discovered in 1937 by Kaczmarz
[
25
]
and was rediscovered in 1970 by Gordon, Bender, and Herman
[
19
]
in the field of image reconstruction. In the most original form,
\alpha_{k}=1
and
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
. Regarding this method, the conditions for convergence have been established, while useful theoretical estimates of the rate of convergence are difficult to obtain. Previous results
[
11
,
12
,
17
,
26
]
relate to some quantities (e.g.,
\det(A^{{\tt T}}A)
) depending on
A
which are usually hard to use as a criterion to compare the performance of the Kaczmarz method with other iterative methods.

A popular way to resolve this is to introduce randomness into the Kaczmarz method. In 2009, Strohmer and Vershynin
[
41
]
first introduced a randomized version of the Kaczmarz method, in which
\alpha_{k}=1
and
i_{k}
is chosen according to the probability distribution that
{\rm Prob}(j)=\|A_{j}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{F}^{2}
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
. They gave a tight estimate of the convergence rate (i.e., the number of iterations). The expected number of iterations quadratically depends on the scaled condition number of
A
.
They also provided evidence that, in some cases, their randomized Kaczmarz method is more efficient than the conjugate gradient method, the most popular algorithm for solving large linear systems. Later in 2015, Gower and Richtárik
[
21
]
developed a versatile randomized iterative method for solving linear equations. It includes Strohmer and Vershynin’s algorithm as a special case.

To further improve the efficiency of the randomized Kaczmarz method, the idea of parallelism was used, such as in
[
37
,
30
,
28
]
. The basic idea is to use multiple rows in each step of the iteration. This will increase the cost of each step of the iteration. But it can reduce the number of iterations as expected. One simple version (known as randomized block Kaczmarz method
[
28
]
) is

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\sum_{i\in Q_{k}}w_{i}\frac{b_{i}-A_{i}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i},

(2)

where the weights
w_{i}\in[0,1]
satisfy
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, and
\alpha_{k}\in(0,2)
. The multiset
Q_{k}
is a collection of row indices such that
i
is put into it with probability
\|A_{i}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{F}^{2}
.
It was shown (e.g., in
[
28
]
) that the number of iterations of this randomized block Kaczmarz method is quadratic in the condition number of
A
when
\#(Q_{k})=\lceil\|A\|_{F}^{2}/\|A\|^{2}\rceil
. Although the overall complexity does not change theoretically, it usually works very well in practice. This method was recently used in
[
38
]
to give the currently best quantum-inspired classical algorithm for solving linear equations. In addition to the above work, there are many studies aimed at accelerating or generalizing the (randomized) Kaczmarz method, for example, see
[
15
,
20
,
24
,
31
,
32
,
47
,
33
,
46
,
23
,
45
,
44
,
39
]
.

1.1
Our results

In this paper, we investigate the Kaczmarz method (
1
) from a new aspect. Firstly, instead of using
\alpha_{k}\in(0,2)
, we choose
\alpha_{k}=2
. This will generate a series of vectors
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
distributed on a sphere centered on a solution. Apparently,
{\bf x}_{k}
does not converge to any solution of the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
even if it is consistent. To obtain a solution, we study the problem of determining
N
and
{k_{1}},\ldots,{k_{N}}
such that the average
(1/N)\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\bf x}_{k_{j}}
is close to a solution. In this process, we find a new geometric fact of the solutions of linear systems.
Secondly, we will not introduce randomness. To be more exact, we still set
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
in the
k
-th step of the iteration. As a direct result, this Kaczmarz method is deterministic.

1.1.2
General consistent linear systems

We now turn to the general case. For consistent linear systems, there can be one or infinitely many solutions. In the latter case, the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm demonstrates some interesting properties. The following result states that if we run the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm from
{\bf x}_{0}
, we end up with the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
, see Figure
1(b)
for an illustration in dimension 3. In Figure
1(b)
, each circle is generated by the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm from some
{\bf x}_{0}
. The center of each circle is a solution of the linear system.

Theorem
12
(restated)
.

Assume that
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be an arbitrarily chosen initial vector, then
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
generated by the process (
5
) lies on a sphere
centered on the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
.

Regarding Theorems
7
,
9
, we prove that they are also correct if
A
is reflection consistent (see Definition
18
). Reflection consistency is almost equivalent to the claim that
m-r
is even, where
r
is the rank of
A
, see Corollary
19
. Although the condition that
m-r
is even is easy to satisfy, it is interesting to see that the algorithm requires this special condition.
To satisfy this condition, a simple approach is to randomly introduce some new linear constraints that do not change the solution set of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. A difficulty here is that not all linear constraints are allowed. We will show that the set of the new linear constraints such that the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm fails is a set with zero Lebesgue measure. So we can choose almost any linear constraints we want.

Finally, when the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is inconsistent, the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm fails to find the least-squares solution. We show that the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm indeed solves the linear system
A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A{\bf x}=A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}{\bf b}
, where
W
is the lower-triangular matrix such that
W+W^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
. Since solving least-squares problem
\min_{{\bf x}}\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
is equivalent to solving
A^{{\tt T}}A{\bf x}=A^{{\tt T}}{\bf b}
, the above finding explains why the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm fails to return the least-squares solution.
Note that the new linear system has a similar structure to the generalized least-square problem in statistics
[
18
]
, where
W
becomes a covariance matrix.
For consistent linear systems, the new linear system
A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A{\bf x}=A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}{\bf b}
is equivalent to the original one when
A
is reflection consistent.
We will show that under reasonable assumptions, which can be satisfied easily by the idea discussed in the above paragraph for consistent linear systems, the matrix
A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A
is invertible if
A
has full column-rank. In this case, the new linear system
A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A{\bf x}=A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}{\bf b}
has a unique solution. Moreover, similar theoretical guarantees to Theorems
7
,
9
also hold.
Nevertheless, it still remains a problem to modify the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm to solve the least-squares problems.

In this part, we theoretically compare the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm with two well-studied randomized Kaczmarz algorithms

From Equations (
8

9

{\bf y}_{i}={\bf x}_{2im}

{\bf z}_{i}={\bf x}_{(2i+1)m}

{\bf y}_{i}
(respectively
{\bf z}_{i}

{\bf y}_{i-1}
(respectively
{\bf z}_{i-1}

O(mn)

9

O(mn\eta(A)\log(1/\varepsilon))

\varepsilon

Compared to previous Kaczmarz methods
[
41
,
28

There is actually a simple approach to resolving this problem.
We can decompose the set

k\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}_{kj}:=\{{\bf x}_{*}+{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\cdots{\mathcal{R}}_{1}{\mathcal{R}}_{A}^{2i+j}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}):i=0,1,2,\ldots\}=\{{\bf x}_{k+(2i+j)m}:i=0,1,2,\ldots\},

1.3
Related work

There are many research papers on the Kaczmarz method, we list below the most related ones.
A similar idea was recently used by Steinerberger. In
[
40
]
, Steinerberger focused on the solving of nonsingular square linear systems using the randomized Kaczmarz method by setting
\alpha_{k}=2
as well. The randomness is similar to that of Strohmer-Vershynin’s algorithm. Steinerberger proved that

{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{j}-A^{-1}{\bf b}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}_{0}-A^{-1}{\bf b}\|

(14)

when
N=O(\kappa_{s}^{2}(A)/\varepsilon^{2})
. So by Markov’s inequality, with high probability the average of the first
N
vectors can be viewed as an approximation of the solution. Note that in our second algorithm, we have a similar estimate

\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{j}-A^{-1}{\bf b}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}_{0}-A^{-1}{\bf b}\|

(15)

when
N=O(n\eta(A)/\varepsilon)
. The
{\bf x}_{j}
’s in (
14
), (
15
) have different meanings. Different from Steinerberger’s result, our result is deterministic.
In
[
40
]
, Steinerberger posed an open question of finding a better and deterministic way to approximate the solution from the samples. Our algorithm can be viewed as an answer to this open question – we sample points in a deterministic way and sub-sample smartly so that the samples have predictable structures on the sphere and their average is unbiased with respect to the solution regardless of the linear system used for creating the samples.

Choosing
\alpha_{k}=2
in (
1
) is not new. It was highlighted in another famous row projection method proposed by Cimmino in 1938
[
9
]
. In Cimmino’s method, from
{\bf x}_{k}
, we perform
m
reflections (
5
) for
i_{k}\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
and use their average to define
{\bf x}_{k+1}
. It was shown by Cimmino that the sequence
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots\}
converges to a solution under the mild assumption that
{\rm Rank}(A)\geq 2
. In our algorithm,
{\bf x}_{k+1}
is obtained by one reflection and the sequence
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots\}
locates on a sphere centered on a solution. The Cimmino method is known to be more amenable to parallelism than the Kaczmarz method. However, the required number of iterations for Cimmino’s method could be large. Compared to Equation (
2
), the randomized block Kaczmarz method seems to be a generalization of the Cimmino method, except that we are now allowed to set
\alpha_{k}=2
. For more on the connection between the Kaczmarz method and the Cimmino method, we refer to
[
3
,
4
]
.

The idea of using cyclic subsequence
\{{\bf x}_{jm+k}:j=0,1,\ldots,k=0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
in the Kaczmarz method to solve inconsistent linear systems has been studied decades ago, for example see
[
42
,
7
,
14
]
. In those papers, the authors investigated the problem of using what kind of relaxation parameters the cyclic subsequences will converge to the least-square solution. In
[
14
]
, Eggermont, Herman, and Lent proved that if the relaxation parameters are periodic, then the cyclic subsequences converge to the least-square solution. It was shown in
[
7
]
that when the relaxation parameters tend to zero, the limits of the cyclic subsequences approach the least-square solution.

The original Kaczmarz algorithm
[
25
]
is deterministic. There are also some other versions of deterministic Kaczmarz algorithms. We name a few here.
For more, we refer to
[
36
,
34
,
6
,
16
]
and the references therein.
In 1954, Agmon
[
1
]
, Motzkin and Schoenberg
[
29
]
extended the Kaczmarz algorithm to solve linear inequalities by orthogonally projecting the current solution onto the chosen halfspace. In 1957, Hildreth
[
22
]
also proposed a similar deterministic algorithm to solve linear inequalities to find the closest point in the solution set to a given point. It is worth mentioning that Hildreth’s algorithm can be reduced to the original Kaczmarz algorithm. In
[
8
]
, Chen and Huang proposed a deterministic block Kaczmarz method for solving the least-squares problem which is competitive with randomized block Kaczmarz methods.
In
[
35
]
, Nutini et al introduced two greedy selection rules that make the Kaczmarz method deterministic. The greedy selection rules give faster convergence rates, and the costs are similar to the randomized ones in some applications.

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we use
\{{\bf e}_{1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}\}
to denote the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., for
{\bf e}_{j}
, the
j
-th entry is 1 and all other entries are 0. The
n\times n
identity matrix will be denoted as
I_{n}
. For the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, we always assume that
A
has no zero rows. All vectors that appear in this paper are assumed to be given in column forms. So when we say the
i
-th row of
A
, we shall use
A_{i}^{{\tt T}}
because
A_{i}
refers to a column vector. The operator norm of
A
is denoted as
\|A\|
. It is the maximal singular value of
A
. With
\|A\|_{F}
, we mean the Frobenius norm, which is the square root of the sum of the absolute squares of the elements of
A
. The condition number refers to the ratio of the largest singular value to the smallest nonzero singular value. For any two vectors
{\bf a},{\bf b}
, their inner product is denoted as
\langle{\bf a}|{\bf b}\rangle
. The transpose of
A
is denoted as
A^{\tt T}
. We remark again that the notation (
3
), (
4
) will be used frequently in this paper.

7
and

.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p}\in(0,2\pi)
be
p
distinct parameters and
\{{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{p}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set of orthogonal vectors, then the dimension of the vector space spanned by
\{\sum_{k=1}^{p}(1-e^{ij\theta_{k}}){\bf v}_{k}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
is
p
. Moreover, they lie on a sphere centered at
\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\bf v}_{k}
.

Proof.

We consider the following matrix with
p
columns:

M:=\begin{pmatrix}1-e^{i\theta_{1}}&1-e^{2i\theta_{1}}&1-e^{3i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1-e^{i\theta_{p}}&1-e^{2i\theta_{p}}&1-e^{3i\theta_{p}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}.

We aim to prove that
M
is nonsingular.

Since
1-e^{ij\theta_{k}}=(1-e^{i\theta_{k}})(1+e^{i\theta_{k}}+\cdots+e^{i(j-1)\theta_{k}})
and
e^{i\theta_{k}}\neq 1
, it follows that the rank of
M
is equal to the rank of the following matrix, which is obtained by dividing the
k
-th row of
M
with
(1-e^{i\theta_{k}})
:

\begin{pmatrix}1&1+e^{i\theta_{1}}&1+e^{i\theta_{1}}+e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&1+e^{i\theta_{p}}&1+e^{i\theta_{p}}+e^{2i\theta_{p}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}.

Via some column operations, which do not change the rank, the above matrix can be transformed to

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots\\
1&e^{i\theta_{2}}-e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{2}}-e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&e^{i\theta_{p}}-e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{p}}-e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}.

Now the singularity of
M
is equivalent to the singularity of the following matrix with
(p-1)
columns

\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta_{2}}-e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{2}}-e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
e^{i\theta_{p}}-e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{p}}-e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}.

Dividing the
j
-th row with
(e^{i\theta_{j}}-e^{i\theta_{1}})
, which is not zero by assumption, we obtain the following matrix

\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i\theta_{2}}+e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{2}}+e^{i\theta_{2}}e^{i\theta_{1}}+e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&e^{i\theta_{p}}+e^{i\theta_{1}}&e^{2i\theta_{p}}+e^{i\theta_{p}}e^{i\theta_{1}}+e^{2i\theta_{1}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}\xrightarrow{\text{Column operations}}\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i\theta_{2}}&e^{2i\theta_{2}}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
1&e^{i\theta_{p}}&e^{2i\theta_{p}}&\cdots\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, the claimed result about the dimension follows directly by induction on
p
. The second claim is straightforward.
∎

The following result is a generalization of Brady-Watt’s formula
[
5
]
. Although they only considered the full row-rank case, their result is true generally. Below, we give a simple proof by induction.

Lemma 3

(Brady-Watt’s formula)
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with no zero rows,
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
be given in (
4
), then

{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=I_{n}-2A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A,

where
W
is the lower-triangular matrix such that
W+W^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
m
.
When
m=1
, the result is clearly true. Now assume that

{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=I_{n}-2A^{{\tt T}}W_{1}^{-1}A,\quad{\mathcal{R}}_{B}=I_{n}-2B^{{\tt T}}W_{2}^{-1}B,

where
W_{1},W_{2}
are lower-triangular matrices such that
W_{1}+W_{1}^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
and
W_{2}+W_{2}^{{\tt T}}=2BB^{{\tt T}}
. Let
W
be the lower-triangular matrix such that

W+W^{{\tt T}}=2\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{{\tt T}}&B^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2AA^{{\tt T}}&2AB^{{\tt T}}\\
2BA^{{\tt T}}&2BB^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}+W_{1}^{{\tt T}}&2AB^{{\tt T}}\\
2BA^{{\tt T}}&W_{2}+W_{2}^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}.

Namely,

W=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}&0\\
2BA^{{\tt T}}&W_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

It is easy to show that

W^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}W_{1}^{-1}&0\\
-2W_{2}^{-1}BA^{{\tt T}}W_{1}^{-1}&W_{2}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Denote
C=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix}
.
We then can check that
{\mathcal{R}}_{C}={\mathcal{R}}_{B}{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=I_{n}-2C^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}C
, where
W
is the lower-triangular matrix such that
W+W^{{\tt T}}=2CC^{{\tt T}}
.
Finally, the claimed result follows by induction.
∎

As a corollary of Brady-Watt’s formula, we have the following result. We below present a new proof, which will be helpful in the estimate of the convergence rate in the next section.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
A
is an
n\times n
matrix without zero rows, and
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
is given in (
4
), then

\det({\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{n})=(-2)^{n}\det(A)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\|A_{i}\|^{-2}.

Consequently, 1 is not an eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
if
A
is nonsingular.

Proof.

From the definition of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
, it suffices to consider the case that each row of
A
has a unit norm. We prove the result by induction on
n
. Assume that
A^{{\tt T}}=QL
is the QL decomposition of
A^{{\tt T}}
, where
Q
is orthogonal and
L
is lower triangular. Then the
i
-th row of
A
, when written in a column vector, equals
A_{i}=QL_{i}
, where
L_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
L
. As a result, we have

{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=Q\prod_{i=1}^{n}(I_{n}-2L_{i}L_{i}^{{\tt T}})Q^{{\tt T}}.

The eigenvalues and the determinant of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
are the same as those of
\prod_{i=1}^{n}(I-2L_{i}L_{i}^{{\tt T}})
. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume that
A
is a lower triangular matrix satisfying that each row has a unit norm.

When
n=1
, we have
A=1
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=-1
. Thus,
\det({\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{1})=-2
. When
n=2
,

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
a&b\\
\end{pmatrix}
where
a^{2}+b^{2}=1
. Thus

{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}2a^{2}-1&-2ab\\
2ab&1-2b^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}
. We now can directly check that
\det({\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{2})=4b^{2}
. When
n\geq 3
, we denote the last row of
A
as
({\bf v}^{{\tt T}},w)
, where
{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is a column vector and
w\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
. For convenience, we denote the submatrix with the last row and column of
A
removed as
B
. Then it is easy to show that

{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}(I_{n-1}-2{\bf v}{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}){\mathcal{R}}_{B}&-2w{\bf v}\\
-2w{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}{\mathcal{R}}_{B}&1-2w^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(I_{n-1}-2{\bf v}{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}){\mathcal{R}}_{B}-I_{n-1}&-2w{\bf v}\\
-2w{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}{\mathcal{R}}_{B}&-2w^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
0&w\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(I_{n-1}-2{\bf v}{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}){\mathcal{R}}_{B}-I_{n-1}&{\bf v}\\
-2{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}{\mathcal{R}}_{B}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{n-1}&0\\
0&-2w\\
\end{pmatrix}.

It implies

\det({\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{n})=-2w^{2}\det\begin{pmatrix}(I_{n-1}-2{\bf v}{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}){\mathcal{R}}_{B}-I_{n-1}&{\bf v}\\
-2{\bf v}^{{\tt T}}{\mathcal{R}}_{B}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}=-2w^{2}\det({\mathcal{R}}_{B}-I_{n-1}).

By induction, we have
\det({\mathcal{R}}_{A}-I_{n})=(-2)^{n}\det(A)^{2}
.
∎

The next lemma will be used to estimate an upper bound of
\eta(A)
in terms of the condition number of
A
. The triangular truncation operator
T
is defined as an
m\times m
matrix such that
T_{ij}=1
if
i\geq j
and 0 otherwise. Denote

K_{m}:=\max\left\{\frac{\|A\circ T\|}{\|A\|}:A\text{ is }m\times m\text{ and nonzero},\circ\text{ is the Hadamard product}\right\}.

Lemma 5

(Theorem 1 of
[
2
]
)
.

For
m\geq 2
,

\left|\frac{K_{m}}{\log m}-\frac{1}{\pi}\right|\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{\pi}\right)\frac{1}{\log m}.

The above result suggests that
\|A\circ T\|=O(\|A\|\log m)
for any
m\times m
matrix
A
.

3
Consistent linear systems

We now turn our attention to the main results of solving linear systems
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
by the Kaczmarz method. First, we explicitly describe our main algorithm for solving consistent linear systems in Algorithm
1
below. Then, we analyze its correctness and efficiency. To better understand the geometric structures of the algorithm, we first focus on the case that
A
is invertible. We then extend the results to general consistent linear systems. In this section, the notation (
3
), (
4
), (
8
), (
9
) will be widely used.

Algorithm 1

Deterministic iterative reflection algorithm for solving consistent linear systems

1:

A consistent linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, an arbitrarily chosen initial vector
{\bf x}_{0}
and an accuracy
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

2:

Output
\tilde{{\bf x}}
such that
\|A\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf b}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

3:

For
k=0,1,2,\cdots
, compute

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},

(16)

where
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
.

4:

Stop the iteration at step
m(M-1)
if

\|A\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf b}\|\leq\varepsilon
, where
\tilde{{\bf x}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}{\bf x}_{jm}
.

5:

Output
\tilde{{\bf x}}
.

Remark 6
.

Before presenting all the results, we want to emphasize in a less rigorous way that Algorithm
1
only works for consistent linear systems under the mild assumption that
m-r
is even, where
r
is the rank of
A
.

3.1
Case 1:
A
is invertible

When
A
is invertible, the solution is unique. This greatly simplifies the analysis of Algorithm
1
. The geometric feature of the algorithm is also very clear in this case. Moreover, this case is a theoretical building block for the general case.

Note that
m=n
in this case. As discussed in the introduction (see (

in (

), (

{\mathbb{S}}_{0},{\mathbb{S}}_{1}

\mathbb{X}=\{{\bf x}_{l}:l=0,1,2,\ldots\}

2n

{\mathbb{S}}_{0},{\mathbb{S}}_{1}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}_{kj}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{{\bf x}_{*}+{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\cdots{\mathcal{R}}_{1}{\mathcal{R}}^{2i+j}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}):i=0,1,2,\ldots\}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\bf x}_{*}+({\mathcal{R}}_{k}\cdots{\mathcal{R}}_{1}{\mathcal{R}}_{n}\cdots{\mathcal{R}}_{k+1})^{2i+j}({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}):i=0,1,2,\ldots\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\bf x}_{k+(2i+j)n}:i=0,1,2,\ldots\},

{\mathbb{S}}_{0j}={\mathbb{S}}_{j}

Theorem 7
.

For
j=0,1
, let
{\bf c}_{j}
be the center of the minimal sphere supporting
{\mathbb{S}}_{j}
, then
{\bf c}_{0}+{\bf c}_{1}=2{\bf x}_{*}
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=U^{\dagger}DU
be the eigenvalue decomposition of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
, where
U
is unitary,
D
is diagonal, and “
{\dagger}
” refers to the conjugate transpose operator.
For convenience, we assume that
{\bf x}_{0}=0
. If
{\bf x}_{0}\neq 0
, we can consider
{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf y}_{j},{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf z}_{j}
instead. This is just a translation, which will not affect the final result.
With the above notation, we have
{\bf y}_{j}=U^{\dagger}(I-D^{2j})U{\bf x}_{*}
and
{\bf z}_{j}=U^{\dagger}(I-D^{2j+1})U{\bf x}_{*}
.

Denote
\tilde{{\bf y}}_{j}=U{\bf y}_{j},\tilde{{\bf z}}_{j}=U{\bf z}_{j},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}=U{\bf x}_{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}
, and
D={\rm diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{n}})
, where
|\theta_{i}|\leq\pi
for all
i
. Then

\tilde{{\bf y}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1-e^{2ij\theta_{k}})x_{k}{\bf e}_{k},\quad\tilde{{\bf z}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1-e^{i(2j+1)\theta_{k}})x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}.

For convenience, we denote all the distinct eigen-phases
\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}\}
of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
as
\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\pm\phi_{3},\ldots,\pm\phi_{L}\}
, where
\phi_{1}=\pi,\phi_{2}=0
and
0<\phi_{l}<\pi
for
3\leq l\leq L
. Certainly, it may happen that
\pi,0\not\in\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}\}
. However, this will not influence the following analysis.

We first determine the center of the minimal sphere supporting
{\mathbb{S}}_{0}
. Note that

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf y}}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{L}(1-e^{2ij\phi_{k}})\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}+\sum_{k=1}^{L}(1-e^{-2ij\phi_{k}})\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=-\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=3}^{L}\left(1-e^{ij(2\phi_{k})}\right)\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}+\sum_{k=3}^{L}\left(1-e^{ij(2(\pi-\phi_{k}))}\right)\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=-\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}\neq 0,\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=-\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}\neq 0
for all
k\geq 3

\sum_{k=3}^{L}~\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}+\sum_{k=3}^{L}~\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=-\phi_{k}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}=\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}-\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\pi}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}-\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=0}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}.

(20)

Regarding the center of the minimal sphere supporting
{\mathbb{S}}_{1}
, we notice that

\tilde{{\bf z}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1-e^{i\theta_{k}})x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1-e^{2ij\theta_{k}})e^{i\theta_{k}}x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}.

The first term is a translation and the second term can be analyzed in a similar way to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(1-e^{i\theta_{k}})x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{i\theta_{k}}x_{k}{\bf e}_{k}-\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\pi}e^{i\theta_{l}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}-\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=0}e^{i\theta_{l}}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}+\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=\pi}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}-\sum_{l:\theta_{l}=0}x_{l}{\bf e}_{l}.

By Lemma

\theta_{l}\neq 0
for all

. In this case
{\bf c}_{0}={\bf c}_{1}={\bf x}_{*}
. When this happens,
{\mathbb{S}}_{0},{\mathbb{S}}_{1}
are on the same minimal sphere.

Theorem
7
implies the correctness of Algorithm
1
. Next, we consider its efficiency.

Theorem 9
.

Let
A
be an
n\times n
invertible matrix,
{\bf x}_{0},{\bf b}
be two
n\times 1
vectors, and
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{-1}{\bf b}
. Let
{\bf y}_{j},{\bf z}_{j}
be given in (
8
), (
9
), then

\|\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}({\bf y}_{j}+{\bf z}_{j})-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|,

(22)

where

N=\left\lceil\frac{\pi\eta(A)}{2\varepsilon}\right\rceil

(23)

and
\eta(A)
is given in Definition
1
.

Proof.

Denote

\tilde{{\bf x}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}({\bf y}_{j}+{\bf z}_{j}).

From the construction of
{\bf y}_{j},{\bf z}_{j}
, we have

\tilde{{\bf x}}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}\Big({\bf x}_{*}+{\mathcal{R}}_{A}^{j}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*})\Big)={\bf x}_{*}+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}{\mathcal{R}}_{A}^{j}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}).

Assume that the unit eigenvectors of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
are
{\bf u}_{l},l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We decompose
{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{l}{\bf u}_{l}
, then by the orthogonality of
\{{\bf u}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{n}
, it is easy to show that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}{\mathcal{R}}_{A}^{j}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\left\|\sum_{l=1}^{n}\beta_{l}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}e^{ij\theta_{l}}\right){\bf u}_{l}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}|\beta_{l}|^{2}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}e^{ij\theta_{l}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}|\beta_{l}|^{2}\left|\frac{1-e^{2iN\theta_{l}}}{1-e^{i\theta_{l}}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4N^{2}}\sum_{l=1}^{n}|\beta_{l}|^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}(N\theta_{l})}{\sin^{2}(\theta_{l}/2)}.

Notice that if
0<|\theta_{l}|\leq\pi
, we have
|\sin(\theta_{l}/2)|\geq|\theta_{l}|/\pi
. This means that
{\sin^{2}(N\theta_{l})}/{\sin^{2}(\theta_{l}/2)}\leq\pi^{2}/\theta_{l}^{2}
. By Lemma
4
,
\theta_{l}\neq 0
for all
l
. Hence,

\|\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}}{4N^{2}\min_{l}|\theta_{l}|^{2}}.

Set the above estimate as
\varepsilon^{2}\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|^{2}
, we then have

N=\left\lceil\frac{\pi}{2\varepsilon\min_{l}|\theta_{l}|}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{\pi\eta(A)}{2\varepsilon}\right\rceil.

This completes the proof.
∎

As already mentioned in the introduction, based on the above result, to obtain an approximation of the solution up to relative error
\varepsilon
, we can modify Algorithm
1
by only using
O(\eta(A)\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations. The basic idea is as follows: In Equation (
22
), we choose
\varepsilon=1/2
, then
N=\lceil\pi\eta(A)\rceil
and
\|\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq 0.5\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|,
where

\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}({\bf y}_{j}+{\bf z}_{j})
. We now view
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}
as the new initial vector and repeat the above procedure. After
\log(1/\varepsilon)
iterations, we obtain an approximation of the solution up to relative error
\varepsilon
. The total number of iterations is
O(\eta(A)\log(1/\varepsilon))
.

In Algorithm
1
, we only focused on
{\bf x}_{jm}
for
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}
. So it costs
O(mn)
to compute
{\bf x}_{(j+1)m}
from
{\bf x}_{jm}
. Previous Kaczmarz algorithms usually use
O(n)
operations at each step of the iteration. We can solve this problem by proposing the following algorithm, the correctness of which is guaranteed by Corollary
8
and Theorem
9
.

Algorithm 2

The second deterministic iterative reflection algorithm

1:

A consistent linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, an arbitrarily chosen initial vector
{\bf x}_{0}
, and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

2:

Output
\tilde{{\bf x}}
such that
\|A\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf b}\|\leq\varepsilon
.

3:

For
k=0,1,2,\cdots
, compute

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},

(24)

where
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
.

4:

Stop the iteration at step
N
if

\|A\tilde{{\bf x}}-{\bf b}\|\leq\varepsilon
, where
\tilde{{\bf x}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{j}
.

5:

Output
\tilde{{\bf x}}
.

Compared with Algorithm
1
, this algorithm is easier to implement because it only uses
O(n)
operations at each step of the iteration. But it requires more steps to converge. The overall complexity of these two algorithms is the same in theory.
To run Algorithm
2
in practice, we can use a similar idea introduced above to reduce the dependence on
\varepsilon
to
\log(1/\varepsilon)
. That is we compute some
\tilde{{\bf x}}
and restart the iteration from
\tilde{{\bf x}}
if it is not a good approximation of the solution. It turns out that this is very efficient in practice, see the numerical results in Section
5
.

The convergence rates of the algorithms proposed in this paper highly depend on
\eta(A)
. To obtain a better understanding, at the end of this section, we build its connection to the condition number of
A
. Theoretically, we have the following general upper bound.

Proposition 10
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix, then
\eta(A)=O((\log m)\kappa^{2}(A))
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3
, we have
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=I_{n}-2A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A
, where
W
is the lower-triangular matrix such that
W+W^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
. Now assume that
e^{i\theta}
is the eigenvalue of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
such that
\theta
is minimal and nonzero. Set the corresponding unit eigenvector as
{\bf u}
, then we have

|1-e^{i\theta}|=2\|A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A{\bf u}\|
.
We consider the case that
\theta>0
is close to 0 so that the left-hand side is as small as
\theta
. The right-hand side is lower bounded by
2\sigma_{\min}(A)^{2}\sigma_{\min}(W^{-1})
, where
\sigma_{\min}
refers to the minimal nonzero singular value. Thus
1/\theta=O(\|W\|/\sigma_{\min}(A)^{2})
. Since
W+W^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
, we know that
W=(2AA^{{\tt T}})\circ T-{\rm diag}(\|A_{1}^{{\tt T}}\|,\cdots,\|A_{m}^{{\tt T}}\|)
, where
T
is the triangular truncation operator defined above Lemma
5
.
By Lemma
5
, we have

\displaystyle\|W\|\leq\frac{\log m}{\pi}\|2AA^{{\tt T}}\|+\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|A_{i}^{{\tt T}}\|\leq\frac{2\log m}{\pi}\|A\|^{2}+\|A\|.

Therefore, we have

1/\theta=O((\log m)\kappa^{2}(A))
.
∎

The upper bound in the above proposition can be reached in some cases. For example, consider the case
m=n=3
.
For simplicity, we assume that

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
\cos(x)&\sin(x)&0\\
\cos^{2}(x)&\cos(x)\sin(x)&\sin(x)\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\sin(x)\neq 0
. Then direct calculation shows that the singular values of
A
are

|\sin(x)|,\quad\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(2+\cos^{2}(x)\pm|\cos(x)|\sqrt{8+\cos^{2}(x)}\right)}.

Let
x
tend to 0, then the singular values are approximately equal to

|x|,3,|x|/3.

Thus the condition number is
\kappa(A)=\Theta(1/|x|)
. We can also compute that the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
are

-1,\,\,\,1-2\sin^{4}(x)\pm 2i\sin^{2}(x)\sqrt{1-\sin^{4}(x)}.

When
x
is close to 0, the eigenvalues are close to
-1,1\pm 2ix^{2}
, which means
\min|\theta_{l}|\approx x^{2}\approx 1/\kappa^{2}(A)
. Note that when
x\approx 0
, the rows of
A
are close to each other and
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
is close to
{\rm diag}\{-1,1,1\}
. In the high-dimensional case, as suggested by the numerical tests, for matrices with the same structure as

(i.e., the
i
-th row is the polar coordinate of a unit vector, all rows use the same parameter
x
), we always have
\min|\theta_{l}|\approx 1/\kappa^{2}
when

.

Below we show that for random matrices,
\eta(A)\approx\kappa(A)
. Let
U
be a random orthogonal matrix taken from the orthogonal group of dimension
n
uniformly according to the Haar measure. Denote the eigenvalues of
U
by
\{e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{n}}\}
. For any
\theta\geq 0
, denote
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}=\#\{j:0\leq\theta_{j}\leq\theta\}
. Then according to random matrix theory
[
27
, Proposition 4.7]
, we have

\left|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{N}_{\theta}]-\frac{n\theta}{\pi}\right|\leq\frac{1+\pi}{2\pi}.

(25)

If we choose
\theta=1/10n
, then
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{N}_{\theta}]\leq(1+\pi)/2\pi+1/10\pi<0.7
. By Markov’s inequality

{\rm Prob}[\mathcal{N}_{\theta}>1]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{N}_{\theta}]<0.7.

Thus
\mathcal{N}_{\theta}=0
with probability at least 0.3. Equivalently, with probability at least 0.3, we have
\min|\theta|\geq 1/10n
.

If we pick a point
{\bf v}
uniformly random on the unit sphere of dimension
n
, then
I_{n}-2{\bf v}{\bf v}^{T}
defines a reflection in the hyperplane orthogonal to
{\bf v}
. We obtain a random orthogonal matrix by forming a product of independent copies of these reflections. The question is how many random reflections are required to get close to the Haar measure. In
[
10

random reflections are required, where

is a universal constant independent of

is a random orthogonal matrix under the Haar measure when

.
In this case, from the above analysis,
\eta(A)<10n
with probability at least 0.3, and
\eta(A)\approx n/\pi\approx 0.32n
on average from (

). In summary, we have the following result.

Let
A
be an

Assume that

{\rm Prob}[\kappa(A)<xn]=e^{-2/x-2/x^{2}}.

For example, if we choose

3.2
Case 2:

We now turn to the general case. Assume that

is
m\times n

has full row-rank

For consistent linear systems with

{\mathcal{R}}_{i},{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
are the same as those in (

{\mathbb{S}}_{0},{\mathbb{S}}_{1}

Below,

still refers to the series of vectors generated by the procedure (

The following result holds for all consistent linear systems.

Theorem 12
.

Assume that
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be an arbitrarily chosen initial vector, then
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
generated by the process (
24

centered on the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
.

Proof.

We use
{\bf x}_{*}
to denote the solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
.
For any solution
{\bf x}
of the linear system, we have
b_{i}=\langle A_{i}|{\bf x}\rangle
. Thus

{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}={\mathcal{R}}_{i}({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x})
, where

. This means
\|{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}\|
. Moreover,
\langle{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle=\langle{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\mathcal{R}}_{i}|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle=\langle{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle
. This implies that if
\langle{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle=0
, then
\langle{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{*}\rangle=0
for all
k
. Equivalently, if
{\bf x}_{*}
is the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
, then
{\bf x}_{*}
is the solution that has the minimal distance to all
{\bf x}_{k}
.

in the introduction is an illustration of Theorem

, we can approximate any solution of

that has the minimal distance to a given vector we are interested in using Algorithm

Theorems

,

are not influenced too much.
We first prove an analogy of Theorem

.

Theorem 13
.

Assume that
A
is
m\times n
and
{\rm Rank}(A)=m
, then

\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{im}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|,

(26)

where

,

{\bf x}_{*}

.

Proof.

The proof here is similar to that of Theorem

. It suffices to show that

has no overlap in the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
corresponding to the eigenvalue 1. With this result, the estimation of

then follows directly by a similar argument to the proof of Theorem

.

By Brady-Watt’s formula (see Lemma

)
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=I_{n}-2A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A
and the fact that

has full row-rank, we know that

is an eigenvalue of

with multiplicity

.
Moreover, assume that
{\bf u}
is an eigenvalue of

, then

. Indeed, from Brady-Watt’s formula, we have

. Since

has full row-rank,

which further implies

Since
{\bf x}_{*}
is the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}

and

. This is a contradiction in that

Theorem 14
.

Assume that
{\rm Rank}(A)=m

For

\displaystyle{\bf c}_{0}

3.3
Case 3:
A
does not have full row-rank

In this section, we consider general consistent linear systems. We will show that Theorems
13
,
14
are almost correct.
From the proof of Theorem
13
, we know that a key point to the success of Algorithm
1
is that
{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}
has no overlap in the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
corresponding to the eigenvalue

. This result is usually no longer correct when

does not have full row rank. Below we give a simple method to fix this. We will show that if we randomly introduce some new linear constraints that do not change the solution set, then the new linear system will satisfy the above-expected condition with high probability. This idea may fail, while we will show that the set of the new linear constraints that makes the algorithm fails is a set with Lebesgue measure zero.

Lemma 15
.

Let
A
be a
r\times r
invertible matrix,
C
be a
l\times r
matrix with no zero rows. Let
B
be the
l\times l
lower triangular matrix such that
B+B^{{\tt T}}=C(A+A^{{\tt T}})C^{{\tt T}}
. If
B
is invertible, then

\det(I_{r}-A^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1}C)=\det(I_{l}-CA^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1}).

Proof.

For convenience, we denote
M=I_{r}-A^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1}C
and
N=I_{l}-CA^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1}
. Denote the
i
-th row of
C
as
{\bf c}_{i}^{{\tt T}}
.

We first suppose that
{\rm Rank}(C)=l
. Let the singular value decomposition of
C
be
C=UDV^{{\tt T}}
, where
D
is a matrix of the form
(\Lambda_{l\times l},\,\,0_{l\times(r-l)})
and
\Lambda_{l\times l}
is an invertible diagonal matrix. It is easy to check that
CM=NC
. So we have
DV^{{\tt T}}MV=U^{{\tt T}}NUD
. We decompose
V^{{\tt T}}MV=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&M_{2}\\
M_{3}&M_{4}\end{pmatrix}
, where
M_{1}
is
l\times l
, then

\Big(\Lambda_{l\times l},\,\,0_{l\times(r-l)}\Big)\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&M_{2}\\
M_{3}&M_{4}\end{pmatrix}=U^{{\tt T}}NU\Big(\Lambda_{l\times l},\,\,0_{l\times(r-l)}\Big).

So
\Lambda_{l\times l}M_{1}=U^{{\tt T}}NU\Lambda_{l\times l}
and
M_{2}=0
.
Note that
M
is invariant in the orthogonal complementary space generated of the rows of
C
. Namely, for any column vector
{\bf q}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
{\bf c}_{1}^{{\tt T}}{\bf q}=\cdots={\bf c}_{l}^{{\tt T}}{\bf q}=0
, we have
M{\bf q}={\bf q}
. This means
V^{{\tt T}}MV(V^{{\tt T}}{\bf q})=V^{{\tt T}}{\bf q}
. So
M_{4}=I_{r-l}
. Thus
\det(M)=\det(M_{1})=\det(N)
.

Now assume that
{\rm Rank}(C)<l
. We still use the above notation but with slight changes in the meaning. The whole analysis is similar.
Now we decompose

D=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t\times t}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad V^{{\tt T}}MV=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}&M_{2}\\
M_{3}&M_{4}\end{pmatrix},\quad U^{{\tt T}}NU=\begin{pmatrix}N_{1}&N_{2}\\
N_{3}&N_{4}\end{pmatrix},

where
M_{1},N_{1}
are

, we have

\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t\times t}M_{1}=N_{1}\widetilde{\Lambda}_{t\times t},M_{2}=0,N_{3}=0.

Note that for any left singular vector
{\bf u}
corresponding to the singular value 0 of
C
, we have
{\bf u}^{{\tt T}}N={\bf u}^{{\tt T}}
. For any right singular vector
{\bf v}
corresponding to the singular value 0 of
C
, we have
M{\bf v}={\bf v}
. Thus
M_{4}=I_{r-t},N_{4}=I_{l-t}
. Consequently,
\det(M)=\det(M_{1})=\det(N_{1})=\det(N)
.
∎

In the above lemma, note that
NB=B-CA^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}
is skew-symmetric, so
\det(I_{r}-A^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1}C)=\det(I_{l}-CA^{{\tt T}}C^{{\tt T}}B^{-1})=0
if

is odd. The above proof shows that without counting the multiplicity,

have the same set of eigenvalues. Moreover, when counting the multiplicity, the only difference is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue 1. The difference is
|r-l|
.

For any
r

\{{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

to denote the

.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\{{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{r}\}

\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{l}\in{\rm Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{r}\}

Let

Proof.

(a).
By Brady-Watt’s formula (see Lemma

{\mathcal{R}}_{v}=I_{n}-2V^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}V

(b). From the proof of (a), we know that

For convenience, denote

C=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf c}_{1}^{{\tt T}}\\

Let
P
be the skew-symmetric matrix defined in Proposition

Let

Making the same assumption as the above corollary, the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue 1 of the operator

Definition 18

(Reflection consistent)

Let

By Proposition

Corollary 19
.

Let

Certainly,

The condition

Theorem 20
.

Assume that the linear system

is consistent and

is reflection consistent. Then the average of

converges to
{\bf x}_{*}

Moreover,

(28)

where

Proof.

When

does not have an overlap in the subspace generated by the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalue 1. Consequently, the claimed results follow directly from a similar argument to the proof of Theorem

As a result, to improve the efficiency of Algorithm

, we can reduce the number of linear equations so that

is the rank. However, we usually do not know

in advance, especially when

Hence, even if

As a simple application of the above idea, we can use the number of linear equations to determine the parity of the rank. For instance, let us consider the following two linear systems:

A{\bf x}={\bf b}\quad{\rm and}\quad\begin{pmatrix}A\\
\tilde{{\bf a}}^{{\tt T}}\\
\end{pmatrix}{\bf x}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf b}\\
\tilde{b}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where

for some arbitrarily chosen

so that

is not the zero vector. When

does not have full row rank, Algorithm

only works for one of them.
Consequently, we can use the quality of the solution to check the parity of the rank of

4
Inconsistent linear systems

In this section, we focus on inconsistent linear systems
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, i.e., the least-squares problem
\arg\min_{{\bf x}}\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
.
We will show that Algorithm

usually fails to solve the least-squares problem. To this end, we first provide a more clear description of Algorithm

.

Assume that
A
is
m\times n
with no zero rows. Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be an arbitrarily chosen initial vector used in Algorithm

where

(31)

Proposition 21
.

FA=I_{n}-{\mathcal{R}}_{A}
.

Proof.

From equation (

Theorem 22

Assume that the linear system

For convenience, we denote the set

Next, we explore the conditions such that the two linear systems

Proposition 23
.

If
{\rm Rank}(A)=m

if and only if
(I_{n}-{\mathcal{R}}_{A}){\bf x}={\bf u}

Proof.

By Brady-Watt’s formula,
FA=I_{n}-{\mathcal{R}}_{A}=2A^{{\tt T}}W^{-1}A
for some invertible matrix

has full row-rank, we have

. Hence,

has full column-rank.
∎

More generally, combining Theorem

we have the following result.

Proposition 24
.

Suppose that
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent and
A
is reflection consistent, then for any

, we have
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
if and only if
(I_{n}-{\mathcal{R}}_{A}){\bf x}={\bf u}
.

Proof.

This is a direct corollary of Theorems

and

, we know that Algorithm
1
will return to a solution of

The above two results provide more evidence for the success of Algorithm
1
proposed in the above section.
The following result implies that when

has full column-rank, the linear system

Proposition 25
.

If
{\rm Rank}(A)=n

This follows directly from Theorem

5.1
Comparison of
\eta(A)
and
\kappa(A)

The complexity of the deterministic iterative reflection algorithm depends on the parameter
\eta(A)
, while the complexities of previous Kaczmarz algorithms depend on the (scaled) condition number of
A
. As discussed after Proposition
11
, for random Gaussian matrices,
\eta(A)
has order
\kappa(A)
instead of
\kappa^{2}(A)
. To see this more clearly, we numerically computed
\eta(A)
and
\kappa(A)
for around 10000 random matrices of size at most
1000\times 1000
whose entries are independently taking values from the standard normal distribution. The numerical results suggest that there might be two constants
c_{0},c_{1}
with
c_{0}\leq 0.55,c_{1}\geq 38.27
such that
c_{0}\leq\kappa(A)/\eta(A)\leq c_{1}
.

Although it is unclear about the dependence of the ratio
\kappa(A)/\eta(A)
on the dimension theoretically, the numerical tests here suggest that these two quantities are often comparable.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a new deterministic Kaczmarz algorithm by replacing orthogonal projections with reflections in the original Kaczmarz method. We established rigorous theorems about its correctness and efficiency. In this process, we discovered an interesting geometric fact about the solutions of consistent linear systems. Namely, any solution of a consistent linear system can be represented as the midpoint of the centers of two spheres. We feel it would be interesting to find more results from this fact. According to our numerical tests, the new Kaczmarz algorithm has a performance competitive with randomized Kaczmarz algorithms for consistent linear systems. However, it cannot be used to solve least-squares problems, which is partially caused by the structure of the algorithm. It would be interesting to find a way to fix this. Perhaps some new interesting geometric facts can be found in this way. Another interesting research topic is to find certain matrices such that
\eta(A)
is small. In this paper, we found some partial results about its connection to the condition number. But this might be worth a systematic study. Finally, from our numerical experiments, Steinerberger’s algorithm works very well in practice. However, Steinerberger only studied the convergence rate in the nonsingular case. So it would be interesting to better understand this algorithm theoretically in the general case.
