Calibration of Local Volatility Models with Stochastic Interest Rates using Optimal Transport Journal: Finance and Stochastics
Benjamin Joseph, Grégoire Loeper, Jan Obłój

Abstract
We develop a non-parametric, semimartingale optimal transport, calibration methodology for local volatility models with stochastic interest rate. The method finds a fully calibrated model which is the closest, in a way that can be defined by a general cost function, to a given reference model. We establish a general duality result which allows to solve the problem by optimising over solutions to a second order fully non-linear Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. Our methodology is analogous to Guo et al. 2022 and Guo et al. 2022a but features a novel element of solving for discounted densities, or sub-probability measures. As an example, we apply the method to a sequential calibration problem, where a Vasicek model is already given for the interest rates and we seek to calibrate a stock price’s local volatility model with volatility coefficient depending on time, the underlying and the short rate process, and the two processes driven by possibly correlated Brownian motions. The equity model is calibrated to any number of European options prices.

Introduction

Modelling involves inevitable trade-offs: “All models are wrong, some models are useful” as
Box & Draper 1987
put it. Models need to capture the important aspects of the system they represent but they also need to be tractable, and analytically and/or numerically solvable. In particular, calibration – picking model parameters which recover known outputs –
is an essential part of any modelling process. It is a key challenge faced by financial industry practitioners on a daily basis, as their pricing models need to match market prices of liquid instruments before they can be used to price any bespoke or illiquid products.

In practice, models for a key underlying, such as the S&P500 index, will need to be calibrated to a large number of options with different maturities and strikes. This may be nigh impossible for a simple parametric model.
Dupire 1994
derived a formula to calibrate a local volatility model to an arbitrary number of options, establishing it as a benchmark for equities modelling (see also
Atlan 2006
for its stochastic interest rate extension). However, it came with its own shortcomings. Its calibration poses serious numerical challenges, see
Bain et al. 2021
, and requires interpolation of the data as Dupire’s formula assumes a continuum of prices across strikes and maturities.
Moreover, it has been criticised for wrong dynamic behaviour compared to suitable stochastic volatility models, see
Hagan et al. 2002
. This issue can lead to potentially costly mispricing of exotic products. Naturally, this motivated further research and led, in particular, to the introduction of local stochastic volatility models (LSV) or forward variance stochasic volatility models, see
Bergomi 2015
for details and historical references. While successful in many ways, these models also came with shortcomings, often related to an inability to calibrate to a new class of products. Prominent among these was the challenge to calibrate jointly to SPX and VIX options, see
Guyon 2020
for the literature review and discussion.

Monte Carlo methods to calibrate with Dupire’s formula have been used in
Deelstra & Rayée 2013
;
Hok & Tan 2019
;
Ögetbil et al. 2022
. Another approach is to discretise the non-linear Fokker-Planck equation and directly solve it by finite differences as in
Ren et al. 2007
or finite volume methods as in
Wyns & Du 2017
;
Engelmann et al. 2021
.
Dupire 1996
derives a non-linear McKean SDE for the local volatility, which in fact recovered the mimicking result of
Gyöngy 1986
using a financial argument. This has been solved via Monte Carlo methods developed in
Henry-Labordère 2009
for an approximate calibration, and an exact calibration by the particle method of
Guyon & Henry-Labordère 2011
;
Guyon & Henry-Labordère 2012
.
Cozma et al. 2019
used variance reduction techniques to implement the particle method in a four factor model. All of these methods require a continuum of strikes and maturities to construct a surface of implied volatilities, which in practice results in interpolation and extrapolation of market data to build these surfaces. We adopt a different approach, without this requirement, by formulating the exact calibration of European options as discrete constraints within a convex optimisation problem.

Recently, a non-parametric exact calibration method based on optimal transport has been proposed, which aims to address the above challenges. This method uses semimartingale optimal transport as introduced by
Tan & Touzi 2013
, which is the semimartingale version of the celebrated work of
Benamou & Brenier 2000
(see also
Huesmann & Trevisan 2019
).
This approach has already been used in several contexts: for local volatility calibration in
Guo et al. 2019
; for local stochastic volatility calibration in
Guo et al. 2022
, for joint VIX SPX calibration in
Guo et al. 2022a
and for optimal investment in
Guo et al. 2022c
. The most general formulation of the method, pointing to the breadth of its applications, is found in
Guo & Loeper 2021
.

An overview of these results is given in
Guo et al. 2022b
. It is worth noting that whilst the link with optimal transport was not recognised, a variational approach to calibrating local volatility models was first constructed already in
Avellaneda et al. 1997
. The core contribution of the method is to build a fully calibrated model while trying to preserve desirable features of a given model. In effect, we project a given reference model onto the set of calibrated models. As the calibration constraints depend on one-dimensional marginals, classical mimicking results, see
Gyöngy 1986
and
Brunick & Shreve 2013
, allow us to restrict to Markovian models. This in turn allows us to use PDE methods to solve the dual problem.

The need for a calibrated interest rate model is common to all financial products involving future payments. While in a very low interest rate environment, which roughly held in the financial markets between 2009 and 2021, one may be tempted to ignore this need for short-dated products, it is no longer feasible for the current market conditions.
It is therefore natural to extend the semimartingale optimal transport approach to a setup that includes stochastic rates.
Our contribution here is to fill this gap and understand how to develop and calibrate joint models for rates and equities. This extension is not trivial, since the computation of the discount factor involves the whole path of the short rate, which renders the problem path dependent. We will show how to overcome this difficulty. We develop suitable duality results and seek to calibrate a stock price local volatility model with volatility coefficient depending on time, the underlying and the short rate process, and driven by a Brownian motion which can be correlated with the randomness driving the rates process. In a sequel paper
Joseph et al. 2023
, we consider a simultaneous joint calibration problem. A particular difficulty here is in dealing with the path dependent discount terms while keeping the number of state variables, and thus the dimension of the problem, at
d=2
. This is important for computational reasons as the numerical methods rely on solving a non-linear HJB equation and pricing PDEs, which becomes computationally more difficult by standard techniques when the dimension
d\geqslant 3
. However, our duality result covers any interest rate model, at the expense of harder numerical complexity.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}F(\alpha^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}_{t},\beta^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\bigg].

This gives the desired inequality.

3.2
A ‘discounted density’ transformation and superposition principle

While we could easily reduce to Markovian models in the state process

When a

Definition 4

Definition 5

4.1
Sequential Calibration Setup

We now specify the setting in which we seek to apply Theorem
4.1
. We first specify the state variables of our model: we consider
d=2
,
X_{t}=(r_{t},Z_{t})
, were
r_{t}
is the short rate and
Z_{t}
is the log-stock price. We start with a setting in which a model for the short rate is fixed and has already been calibrated. We refer to this as a “sequential calibration” problem. Our aim is then to calibrate a local volatility model for the stock price, where the local volatility function can depend on the short rate. For this we will employ the OT methodology developed above. The joint calibration problem in which both the short rate and the stock price are calibrated simultaneously using the OT methodology, and in particular the short rate model parameters can depend on the stock price, is considered in our sequel paper
Joseph et al. 2023
.

Specifically, we take now a given pre-calibrated Hull-White model for the interest rate, see
Hull & White 1990
;
Hull & White 1994
, and local volatility dynamics for the log-price

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z_{t}

\displaystyle=r_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(t,Z_{t},r_{t})+\sigma(t,Z_{t},r_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r_{t}

\displaystyle=\big(b(t)-ar_{t}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{t},

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\langle W^{1},W^{2}\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=\xi(t,Z_{t},r_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

where
a,\sigma_{r}>0
are constants and

is a function of time, calibrated so that the dynamics of
(r_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
match the market data (e.g., suitable interest rates caps and floors). Both
a
and
\sigma_{r}
being positive constants is not a particularly restrictive constraint as remarked in
Brigo & Mercurio 2007
;
Hull & White 1995
. Note that

will therefore need to be calibrated to fit the term structure of interest rates seen in the market. Our aim now is to calibrate
\sigma(t,Z_{t},r_{t})
and
\xi(t,Z_{t},r_{t})
using our OT-methodology. In order to calibrate the local volatility and correlation, we will want to find a cost function that forces
\alpha_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}
and
\beta_{t}^{\mathbb{P}}
to take the form above. As discussed before, we achieve this by using a functional form for
F
as long as
(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma
for some convex set
\Gamma
, with
F=+\infty
otherwise. The set
\Gamma
will enforce in particular that
\beta
is positive semidefinite and symmetric, and
\beta_{22}=\sigma_{r}^{2}
, which defines a set that is convex in
\beta
. With no further constraints on
\beta_{12}
, we obtain a non-explicit form of the Legendre transform of the cost function,
F^{*}
, which in general will require a numerical solver at each
(t,x)
. To reduce the computational difficulty of the numerical solution, as in
Guo et al. 2022
, we first restrict the correlation to the following form, which still keeps the set
\Gamma
convex

\xi(t,Z_{t},r_{t})=\frac{\sigma_{r}}{\sigma(t,Z_{t},r_{t})}\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t,Z_{t},r_{t}),\quad\text{for}\>t\in[0,T],

(26)

where
\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t,Z_{t},r_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
here is a fixed reference function. We then set

\displaystyle\Gamma(t,Z_{t},r_{t})=\bigg\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\>:\>

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=r_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{11},\>\alpha_{2}=b(t)-ar_{t},

\displaystyle\beta_{12}=\beta_{21}=\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}\sigma_{r}^{2},\>\beta_{22}=\sigma_{r}^{2}\bigg\},

(27)

where one would change the set

suitably if a different short rate model was fixed initially.
We also require the inequality
\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}(t,Z_{t},r_{t})^{2}\sigma_{r}^{2}\leqslant\sigma^{2}(t,Z_{t},r_{t})
to keep
\xi(t,Z_{t},r_{t})\in[-1,1]
as a correlation also. Since
r_{t}
is on a much lower scale to

, we have that
\sigma_{r}^{2}\ll\sigma^{2}
, so this condition is not financially restrictive. To enforce this condition, we will define a convex function

with a parameter

+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The coefficients of each term ensure that

is minimised over
x
at

with
\min_{x}H(x,\bar{x},s)=0
. We fix a reference local volatility function
\bar{\sigma}^{2}=\bar{\sigma}^{2}(t,Z_{t},r_{t})
that represents the desired model. Our aim is to find a calibrated model which does not deviate too much from the reference one. To achieve this we set

F(t,Z,r,\alpha,\beta)=\begin{cases}H(\beta_{11},\bar{\sigma}^{2},\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}^{2}\sigma_{r}^{2}),&\text{if}\>(\alpha,\beta)\in\Gamma(t,Z_{t},r_{t}),\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(28)

Remark 5

It is easy to check that the function

This cost function will ensure that we retain the Hull-White model in the interest rate, while also matching the market prices for the call options by calibrating the volatility of the stock. Additionally, we wish to enforce that the matrix

from our model characteristics is positive definite and that
\xi_{\mathrm{ref}}
remains a correlation function, and we achieve this by setting

as an argument of

In this section, we outline the numerical method used to solve the dual formulation in Theorem

Lemma 4

Suppose Problem
1

Then the gradients of the dual objective function are given by

The gradients are obtained via the same method in
Guo et al. 2022

-r\varphi
term in the HJB equation from the Feynman-Kac formula. The interpretation is the same here, that the gradients represent the difference between the model and market prices. We briefly outline the numerical method from
Guo et al. 2022
and
Guo et al. 2022a
which can be directly applied here.
We are first given an initial guess
\lambda
, which will usually be taken to be a zero vector, and then solve (
29
) to obtain
\varphi(0,Z_{0},r_{0})
. Since the HJB equation (
29
) has dirac deltas at the times
(\tau_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
, the solution also has jump discontinuities in time. However, in-between these jump discontinuities, the solution is continuous so can be solved using standard techniques backwards in time, and then the jump discontinuities can be incorporated into a terminal condition at the expiration time. That is, we take the final jump discontinuity as a terminal condition, solve the HJB equation up to the next calibrating option expiry, and then add the jump discontinuity to the solution as a new terminal condition at the next jump discontinuity in the same way as given in Definition
7
since we understand the solution in the viscosity sense. Then, given
\varphi(0,Z_{0},r_{0})
, we calculate the objective value (
32
), and apply an optimisation algorithm to update the
\lambda
to a new guess. As in
Guo et al. 2022
and
Guo et al. 2022a
, the L-BFGS algorithm of
Liu & Nocedal 1989
displayed good convergence, and with the gradients calculated in Lemma
4
the convergence can be made faster. We remark that to compute (
33

option. This expectation can either be computed via Monte Carlo methods, or using standard pricing PDE techniques by solving (
31

\beta_{11}^{*}
obtained from the solution of the HJB equation. This process is then repeated until
||\nabla_{\lambda}L(\lambda)||_{\infty}<\varepsilon
for some specified tolerance

We perform the constant rescaling in the short rate variable
r_{t}\mapsto Rr_{t}
where we choose
R=100
for stability reasons in the finite difference approximations to make it of the same order as the other coordinate. In addition, we rescale the calibrating option prices and their payoffs by their vegas computed from their Black-Scholes implied volatility. This not only helps the stability of the numerical method by reducing the magnitude of the jump discontinuities, but it also converts pricing errors into implied volatility errors since the vega represents how much the option price will change as the volatility changes by
1\%

We present numerical results showcasing the performance of our proposed calibration method. We use simulated data to investigate the advantages and drawbacks of our method, and in particular its dependence on the reference model
\bar{\sigma}
in (

Cox 1996
, with different sets of parameters for the model used to generate the option prices and for our reference model. The underlying

,
0\leqslant t\leqslant T

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{t}=r_{t}S_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma(t,S_{t})S_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t},

with
\sigma(t,S_{t})=\sigma S_{t}^{\gamma-1}
, where
\sigma\geqslant 0
and
\gamma\geqslant 0
are both constants. We remark that this is a special case of the SABR (“stochastic
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\rho
”) model derived in
Hagan et al. 2002
as a stochastic volatility extension of the CEV model.

We solve a pricing PDE to compute the generating model prices and consider the following instruments:

1.

calls on the underlying with an expiration of 60 days;

2.

calls on the underlying with an expiration of 120 days.

We note that the payoffs of these options are not smooth and cause instabilities when computing the derivatives in the terminal conditions of the HJB equation. Thus, we use the smoothed version of the call option payoffs, which for
\varepsilon\ll K
is given by:

(S_{T}-K)^{+}\approx\frac{S_{T}-K}{2}\Big[\mathrm{tanh}\Big(\frac{S_{T}-K}{\varepsilon}\Big)+1\Big].

We keep the interest rate model parameters the same throughout since that is assumed to be given.

We present two numerical examples, the “good” reference model where the initial reference model is parametrically close to the generating model, and the “bad” reference model where it is not parametrically close to the generating model and in particular has a correlation with a different sign. Note that in the extreme case when the generating model and the reference model are the same, the calibration procedure will stop instantly and recover the generating model. A feature of our method is that it is designed to find a calibrated model closest to the reference model, in the sense of minimising (
15
). This results in a spiky volatility surface – the method tries to stick to the reference model whilst making sharp deviations needed to calibrate to the given options’ prices. This is not necessarily a desired feature and we propose to smooth the surface to obtain a reasonable volatility surface while matching the market data. We used an iterative procedure: once we obtained convergence of the model prices to the generating model prices within the tolerance of
\varepsilon_{1}
, we then stored those characteristics as the new reference model, applied an interpolation method using cubic splines to smooth the surface and then restarted the process with the smoothed surface as our reference model. We remark that the final epoch of the calibration algorithm involved no interpolation, as this spoils calibration, so the overall algorithm provided the OT calibrated surface after iterating through smoothed reference models. In addition to providing more reasonable model dynamics, the smoothed reference model iteration also improved the numerical stability of the algorithm, and thus allowed us to achieve a better calibration. This came at a significant computational cost due to the iterations of the calibration routine, however both ran on a standard notebook laptop in a matter of hours. Both reference models were smoothed around 10 times before changes in the volatility surface plots were no longer noticeable.

We remark that the aforementioned reference model iteration could, in principle, be circumvented via a regularisation technique by encoding a smoothing penalty into the cost function, such as via a second order Tikhonov regularisation method. Clearly this would change the optimal characteristics given in (
30

\beta_{11}

\beta

6
Conclusions

This paper developed a non-parametric optimal-transport driven method to calibrate stock price model in a stochastic interest rate environment. By switching to sub-probability measures representing discounted densities the method manages not to increase the dimension of the state variables. The method is flexible and can accommodate different calibrating instruments. We developed a generic duality result and applied it in the context of Hull-White short rate model and a local volatility stock price model with short rate dependence. We illustrated the numerical performance of the method considering a sequential calibration problem: the short rate model is calibrated first in a parametric setting and it then feeds into our non-parametric local volatility calibration. We highlighted how some model features are pinned down well via the market prices of options. However others, notably the correlation between the Brownian motions driving the rates and the stock price, are much more sensitive to the modeller’s choice of their reference model. In a follow up paper
Joseph et al. 2023
, we apply the duality result developed here to consider simultaneous OT-driven calibration for a joint model for the stochastic interest rates and the stock price process, and demonstrate its performance on real market data. We note that here and in
Joseph et al. 2023
, we restrict ourselves to short-rate models to keep the dimension of the HJB equation to two state variables. It would be natural and interesting to extend this setup to include more stochastic factors, e.g., stochastic volatility for the stock prices. Each extra factor would raise the dimension of the HJB and imply significant numerical challenge.
It would also be of interest to consider more realistic fixed income models, such as market models of
Brace et al. 1997
;
Miltersen et al. 1997
;
Jamshidian 1997
or more recent multi-curve framework models, see
Henrard 2007
;
Henrard 2010
, where the post 2008 financial crisis effects of the LIBOR-OIS spreads are taken into account. This however would drastically increase the dimension of our state process. It would likely necessitate novel tools to solve the resulting PDEs, such as recent machine learning methods, see
Han et al. 2018
;
Weinan et al. 2021
. We believe these are very interesting avenues for future research.

. The tightness and convergence of
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n}
is then shown, and then we use the arguments to generalise the coefficients
a,b,c
.

A.1
Smooth Approximation of the Augmented Martingale Problem

Lemma 5

Let
(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a smooth solution of
\mathrm{dFP}(a,b,c)
with coefficients
a\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
b\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+})
,
c\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists a solution
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in{\mathcal{P}}(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1}))
to
\mathrm{aMP}(a,b,c)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{t}(\cdot,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)=\nu_{t}(\cdot)
.

Proof

Let
\mathbb{P}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and define the process

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{X}_{t}=a_{t}(\tilde{X}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+[b_{t}(\tilde{X}_{t})]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W^{\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}_{t},&0\leqslant t\leqslant T,\\
\tilde{X}_{0}=x_{0}.&\end{cases}

(36)

Since
a
and
b
are taken to be smooth, we automatically have existence and uniqueness of (
36
) (see
Stroock & Varadhan 1979
). Define
\tilde{Y}_{t}=\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}c_{s}(\tilde{X}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s)
. Then, by the same reasoning as Theorem
2.1
, we have that the curve of marginal laws of the augmented process
(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y})
at
t
, given by
\eta_{t}=(e_{t})_{\#}\boldsymbol{\eta}
solves the Fokker-Planck equation for
(t,x,y)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}

\partial_{t}\eta_{t}(x,y)+\nabla_{x}\cdot[\eta_{t}(x,y)a_{t}(x)]-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}_{x}:[\eta_{t}(x,y)b_{t}(x)]-\partial_{y}[yc_{t}(x)\eta_{t}(x,y)]=0.

We now define the discounted version of
\eta_{t}
by
\eta_{t}^{D}(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{t}(x,y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
, then by a similar calculation to that in the proof of Lemma
2
, we have that
(\eta_{t}^{D})_{t\in[0,T]}
solves the discounted Fokker-Planck equation (
11
). By a similar argument to
Figalli 2008
, we have uniqueness of (
11
), so
\eta^{D}_{t}=\nu_{t}
. By Itô’s formula, it is immediate that
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in{\mathcal{P}}(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1}))
solves
\mathrm{aMP}(a,b,c)
, so the result follows.

We let
(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a narrowly continuous solution of
\mathrm{dFP}(a,b,c)
for
a\in L^{1}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
b\in L^{1}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t;\mathbb{S}^{d})
, and
c\in L^{1}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t;\mathbb{R})
. We first build a smooth sequence
\nu_{t}^{n}
approximating
\nu_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, associated with diffusion operators
\mathcal{L}^{D,n}
. Then by Lemma
5
, there exists a sequence of solutions to the augmented martingale problem,
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n}
, associated with
\mathcal{L}^{A,n}
, such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{t}^{n}(\cdot,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)=\nu_{t}^{n}
. As in
Trevisan 2016
, we construct the approximations in two different ways for the proof of the general case.

Pushforward via Smooth Maps:

Let
\pi=(\pi^{1},\dots,\pi^{d})\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with uniformly bounded first and second derivatives. Define the standard diffusion operator,
\mathcal{L}^{S}
by
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})\ni f\mapsto\mathcal{L}^{S}=a\cdot\nabla_{x}f+\frac{1}{2}b:\nabla^{2}_{x}f
, then we can define the approximations of
(a,b,c)
by

\pi(a)^{i}_{t}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}[\mathcal{L}^{S}(\pi^{i})\nu_{t}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}\nu_{t}},\quad\pi(b)_{t}^{i,j}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}[b_{t}:(\nabla\pi^{i}\otimes\nabla\pi^{j})\nu_{t}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}\nu_{t}},\quad\pi(c)_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}[c_{t}\nu_{t}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{\#}\nu_{t}}.

Note that
\pi
preserves uniform bounds on
a,b,c
. Additionally, by an application of the chain rule to compute
\mathcal{L}^{D}(f\circ\pi)
, it is easy to see that
(\pi_{\#}\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
solves
\mathrm{dFP}(\pi(a),\pi(b),\pi(c))
, and therefore by Lemma
5
there exists
\boldsymbol{\eta}
a solution to
\mathrm{aMP}(\pi(a),\pi(b),\pi(c))
such that
\pi_{\#}\nu_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{t}(\cdot,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
.

Mollification by Convolutions:

Let
\kappa\geqslant 0
be a smooth probability density on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define

a^{\kappa}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(a_{t}\nu_{t})*\kappa]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu_{t}*\kappa]},\qquad b^{\kappa}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(b_{t}\nu_{t})*\kappa]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu_{t}*\kappa]},\qquad c_{t}^{\kappa}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(c_{t}\nu_{t})*\kappa]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu_{t}*\kappa]}

By
Trevisan 2016
we have that
a^{\kappa}
,
b^{\kappa}
, and
c^{\kappa}
preserve integrability, and have uniformly bounded first and second spatial derivatives on compact sets. Therefore,
(\nu_{t}*\kappa)_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
solves
\mathrm{dFP}(a^{\kappa},b^{\kappa},c^{\kappa})
. Additionally, by Lemma
5
there exists
\boldsymbol{\eta}
a solution to
\mathrm{aMP}(a^{\kappa},b^{\kappa},c^{\kappa})
such that
\nu_{t}*\kappa=\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{t}(\cdot,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
.

A.2
Compactness and Convergence of the Augmented Martingale Problem

We now assume that
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n}
is a sequence of superposition solutions corresponding to an approximation
\nu^{n}_{t}=\pi^{n}_{\#}\nu_{t}
in the pushforward case or
\nu_{t}^{n}=\nu_{t}*\kappa^{n}
in the mollification case. In the mollification case, we assume that
\kappa^{n}\to\delta_{0}
narrowly as
n\to\infty
. In the pushforward case we assume that
\pi_{n}\to\mathrm{Id}
locally uniformly, and
\nabla\pi^{n}\to\mathrm{Id}
, and
\nabla^{2}\pi^{n}\to 0
pointwise. Assume also that the sequences of derivatives are uniformly bounded so there exists
C\geqslant 0
such that
|\nabla\pi^{n}|,|\nabla^{2}\pi^{n}|\leqslant C
. Compactness for solutions of the augmented martingale problem then follows directly from
Trevisan 2016
since we could recast the superposition principle in the setting of
Trevisan 2016
by considering superposition solutions to
\mathrm{aFP}(a,b,c)
and then the tightness arguments are directly applicable.

We are looking for solutions of
\mathrm{aMP}(a,b,c)
with a discounted density coinciding with a given solution of
\mathrm{dFP}(a,b,c)
. We therefore consider the class of test functions
C^{1,2}_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1};\mathbb{R})\ni\tilde{f}(x,y)=yf(x)
where
f\in C^{1,2}_{b}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R})
. We therefore note that we always have

\int\int_{s}^{t}(\mathcal{L}_{r}^{A}\tilde{f})\circ e_{r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}^{D}_{r}f_{r})\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{r}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

From now, we use the shorthand
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
for notational ease. Now, let
\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}
be a discounted diffusion operator with coefficients
\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{c}
continuous and compactly supported. In view of the arguments of
Trevisan 2016
, due to narrow convergence of
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{n}
, we only need to provide bounds on

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int|\mathcal{L}^{D,n}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}+\int|\mathcal{L}^{D}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.

(37)

Pushforward via Smooth Maps:

Write
\pi(\mathcal{L}^{D})
for the discounted diffusion operator associated with coefficients
\big(\pi(a),\pi(b),\pi(c)\big)
. Then for a fixed
n
, we have

\displaystyle\int|\pi^{n}(\mathcal{L}^{D,n})f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi^{n}_{\#}\nu

\displaystyle\leqslant\int|\mathcal{L}^{D,n}(f\circ\pi^{n})-(\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f)\circ\pi^{n}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu,

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|a:(\nabla\pi_{i}\otimes\nabla\pi_{j})-\overline{a}^{i,j}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu

\displaystyle\quad+\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\mathcal{L}^{S}(\pi_{i})-\overline{a}^{i}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu+\int|c-\overline{c}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.

Since
\pi^{n}
converges to the identity, we have that (
37
) is bounded above by

\int\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|a:(\nabla\pi_{i}\otimes\nabla\pi_{j})-\overline{a}^{i,j}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu+2\int\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\mathcal{L}^{S}(\pi_{i})-\overline{a}^{i}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu+2\int|c-\overline{c}\circ\pi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.

Since continuous and compactly supported functions are dense in
L^{1}(\nu)
, the above can be made arbitrarily small by optimising over
\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{c}
.

Mollification by Convolution:

Let
\overline{\omega}
be a common bounded and continuous modulus of continuity for
\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{c}
. Let
\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D,n}}
be the discounted diffusion operator associated with coefficients

\overline{a}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(\overline{a}\nu)*\kappa^{n}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu*\kappa^{n}]},\qquad\overline{b}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(\overline{b}\nu)*\kappa^{n}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu*\kappa^{n}]},\qquad\overline{c}^{n}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[(\overline{c}\nu)*\kappa^{n}]}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}[\nu*\kappa^{n}]}.

We first show
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int|\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D,n}}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}=0
. Since
||f||_{C^{1,2}}\leqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\int|\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D,n}}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int|\overline{a}^{n}-\overline{a}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}+\int|\overline{b}^{n}-\overline{b}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}+\int|\overline{c}^{n}-\overline{c}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n},

\displaystyle\leqslant 3\int\overline{\omega}(z)\kappa^{n}(z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\to 0.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int|\mathcal{L}^{D,n}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int|\mathcal{L}^{D,n}f-\overline{\mathcal{L}^{D,n}}f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int|a-\overline{a}|+|b-\overline{b}|+|c-\overline{c}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.

By the same argument as before, we can make the right hand side arbitrarily small which concludes the proof.

A.3
Generalisation of Drift and Diffusion Coefficients

We now use the approximation and convergence described in Section
A.1
and Section
A.2
to generalise the coefficients
a,b
and prove Theorem
3.1
.

A.3.1
Smooth and Bounded Coefficients:

Let
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},

b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}
, and
c:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel maps such that

\int_{0}^{T}||a_{t}||_{C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||b_{t}||_{C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+||c_{t}||_{C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(38)

Lemma 6

Let
a,b,c
be Borel maps satisfying (
38
). Then for every
\overline{\nu}\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in{\mathcal{P}}\big(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\big)
solving
\mathrm{aMP}(a,b,c)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y\eta_{0}(\cdot,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)=\overline{\nu}(\cdot)
.

The proof is the same as
Trevisan 2016
, but by considering the augmented process
(X,Y)
where the regular martingale problem (see
Trevisan 2016
) is given by
\mathrm{aMP}(a,b,c)
.

Lemma 7

Let
a:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},

b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}^{d}_{+}
, and
c:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be Borel maps such that

\int_{0}^{T}||a_{t}||_{C^{2}(B)}+||b_{t}||_{C^{2}(B)}+||c_{t}||_{C^{2}(B)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\quad\text{for every bounded open }B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

Let
(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a narrowly continuous solution of
\mathrm{dFP}(a,b,c)
. If
\nu_{0}\leqslant 0
, then
\nu_{t}\leqslant 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore for
\overline{\nu}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
there exists a unique narrowly continuous solution
\nu
such that
\nu_{0}=\overline{\nu}
.

The proof is similar to that of
Trevisan 2016
, so we provide a sketch.

Proof

Let
g\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be non-negative. Let
\chi_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
be a cutoff function and let
a^{\varepsilon}_{R}
,
b^{\varepsilon}_{R}
,
c^{\varepsilon}_{R}
be a mollification with respect to time and space of
a\chi_{R}
,
b\chi_{R}
, and
c\chi_{R}
, so that
a^{\varepsilon}_{R}
,
b^{\varepsilon}_{R}
,
c^{\varepsilon}_{R}
satisfy (
38
). Let
\mathcal{L}^{D,\varepsilon}_{R}
be the diffusion operator associated with
\mathrm{dFP}(a^{\varepsilon}_{R},b^{\varepsilon}_{R},c^{\varepsilon}_{R})
. Let
f^{\varepsilon}
solve

\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}=-\mathcal{L}^{D,\varepsilon}_{R}f^{\varepsilon}+g,\qquad f^{\varepsilon}_{T}=0.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{S}
in Section
A.1
in the construction of the pushforward via smooth maps approximation. Since
\nu
solves
\mathrm{dFP}(a,b,c)
,
f^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1,2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f^{\varepsilon},\nu_{0}\leqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant-\int f^{\varepsilon}\chi_{R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{0},

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{R}\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{L}^{D}(f^{\varepsilon}\chi_{R})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu,

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{R}(g-\mathcal{L}^{D,\varepsilon}_{R}f^{\varepsilon}+\mathcal{L}^{S}f^{\varepsilon})+f^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}^{S}\chi_{R}+cf^{\varepsilon}\chi_{R}+b:\nabla f^{\varepsilon}\otimes\nabla\chi_{R}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \geqslant\int g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu-\sup_{t\in[0,T]}||f^{\varepsilon}_{t}||_{C^{2}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\int&\big[\chi_{R}(|a^{\varepsilon}_{R}-a|+|b^{\varepsilon}_{R}-b|+|c^{\varepsilon}_{R}-c|)\\
&+|\mathcal{L}^{S}\chi_{R}|+|b||\nabla\chi_{R}|)\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu.\end{aligned}

The sup is uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon>0
, and as
\varepsilon\to 0

(a_{R}^{\varepsilon},b_{R}^{\varepsilon},c_{R}^{\varepsilon})\to(a,b,c)
for
|x|\leqslant R
. Then letting
R\to\infty
, since
\nabla\chi_{R}\to 0
and
\mathcal{L}^{S}\chi_{R}\to 0
the right hand side converges to
\int g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu
.

A.3.2
Bounded Coefficients:

This case follows in the same manner as the bounded case in
Trevisan 2016
. We now assume that
a,b,c
satisfy

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a_{t}(x)|+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b_{t}(x)|+\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|c_{t}(x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty.

(39)

Using the regularisation kernel
\kappa=\exp(-\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}})
and
\kappa^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-n}\kappa(x/\varepsilon)
, we have with
\nu_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\nu_{t}*\kappa^{\varepsilon}
that
(\nu_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\in[0,T]}
solves
\mathrm{dFP}(a^{\varepsilon},b^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon})
with coefficients satisfying (
38
). Thus, existence of
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{P}}\big(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\big)
solving
\mathrm{aMP}(a^{\varepsilon},b^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon})
as a discounted superposition solution to
\nu^{\varepsilon}
follows. Since
\nu^{\varepsilon}_{0}
is a narrowly convergent sequence, there exists
\theta:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
increasing with
\lim_{x\to\infty}\theta(x)=\infty
such that
\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\int\theta(|x|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{0}\leqslant 1
. The proof of tightness of
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
is then an easy modification of that in
Trevisan 2016
, where the de la Vallée Poussin criterion modifies (
39
) to

\int_{0}^{T}\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a_{t}(x)|\big)+\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b_{t}(x)|\big)+\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|c_{t}(x)|\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

for
\Theta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
convex non-decreasing with
\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{-1}\Theta(x)=\infty
. Thus, in analogy to
Trevisan 2016
, with
f^{i}=(x_{i}\chi_{R})\circ e_{t}
for
i=1,\dots,d
and
f^{d+1}=[\log(y\chi_{R})]\circ e_{t}
. Note that the function
f^{d+1}
is always well-defined and real valued since
Y
is assumed to be supported on
(0,e^{T})
. Then, for the functional
\Psi:C([0,T];\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty]
, we have for
i=1,\dots,d
, with
x^{i}_{R}=x_{i}\chi_{R}
:

\displaystyle\int\Psi(x^{i}_{R}\circ\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\theta(|x^{i}_{R}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\int\big[\Theta(|\mathcal{L}^{S,\varepsilon}_{t}x^{i}_{R}|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Theta(b^{\varepsilon}_{t}:\nabla x^{i}_{R}\otimes\nabla x^{i}_{R})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta^{\varepsilon}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\int\Psi(f^{d+1}\circ\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\theta(|f^{d+1}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\int\Theta(|c^{\varepsilon}_{t}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta_{t}^{\varepsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

We now note that
\eta^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\nu_{t}^{A,\varepsilon}
where
(\nu_{t}^{A,\varepsilon})_{t\in[0,T]}
solves
\mathrm{aFP}(a^{\varepsilon},b^{\varepsilon},c^{\varepsilon})
, and further that by construction
\nu_{0}^{A,\varepsilon}=\nu_{0}^{\varepsilon}
. Thus, letting
R\to\infty
, noting that the
y
-marginal of
\eta^{\varepsilon}_{0}
is
\delta_{1}
, by the regular superposition principle (
Trevisan 2016
), we have the uniform bounds:

\displaystyle\int\Psi(x^{i}_{R}\circ\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\theta(|x^{i}_{R}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\int\big[\Theta(|\mathcal{L}^{S,\varepsilon}_{t}x^{i}_{R}|)

\displaystyle\qquad+\Theta(b^{\varepsilon}_{t}:\nabla x^{i}_{R}\otimes\nabla x^{i}_{R})\big]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu^{A,\varepsilon}_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\int\Psi(f^{d+1}\circ\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\theta(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\int\Theta(|c^{\varepsilon}_{t}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}^{A,\varepsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

Thus, by
Trevisan 2016
, we have the uniform bounds

\displaystyle\int\Psi(\gamma^{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\int_{0}^{T}\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|a_{t}(x)|\big)+\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b_{t}(x)|\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle\int\Psi\big(\log(\gamma^{d+1})\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\theta(0)+\int_{0}^{T}\Theta\big(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|c_{t}(x)|\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\gamma\mapsto\Psi\big(\log(\gamma^{d+1})\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Psi(\gamma^{i})
is coercive in
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
, we have tightness of
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
. The limit of the approximations obtaining a discounted superposition solution follows directly by the results of Section
A.2
.

A.3.3
Locally Bounded Coefficients:

We now assume that
a,b,c
satisfy

\int_{0}^{T}\sup_{x\in B}\{|a_{t}(x)|+|b_{t}(x)|+|c_{t}(x)|\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\qquad\text{for every bounded borel }B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(40)

Let
M\geqslant 1
and
\chi_{M}
be a cutoff function. Define
\pi_{M}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
\pi_{M}(x)=x\chi_{M}(x)
. Thus, by (
40
), we have
|\mathcal{L}^{D}(\pi^{i}_{M})|\leqslant||\pi^{i}_{M}||_{C^{2}}\sup_{|x|\leqslant 2M}\{|a(x)|+|b(x)|+|x_{r}c(x)|\}
and
|b:\nabla\pi^{i}_{M}\otimes\nabla\pi^{j}_{M}|\leqslant||\pi^{i}_{M}||_{C^{1}}\sup_{|x|\leqslant MR}|b(x)|
. Thus with
\nu^{M}_{t}\coloneqq\pi^{M}_{\#}\nu_{t}
, we have
(\nu_{t}^{M})_{t\in[0,T]}
solves
\mathrm{dFP}(\pi^{M}(a),\pi^{M}(b),\pi^{M}(c))
with coefficients satisfying (
39
) so we have
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{M}\in{\mathcal{P}}\big(C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\big)
solving
\mathrm{aMP}\big(\pi^{M}(a),\pi^{M}(b),\pi^{M}(c)\big)
as a discounted superposition solution to
\nu^{M}
. The argument of tightness is similar to that of the previous section with the modified bound to be

\int_{0}^{T}\int\Theta_{1}(|a_{t}|)+\Theta_{2}(|b_{t}|)+\Theta_{3}(|c_{t}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty

As before, we obtain the following inequalities

\displaystyle\int\Psi(x^{i}_{R}\circ\gamma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{M}(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int\theta(|x^{i}_{R}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta^{M}_{0}+\int_{0}^{T}\int\big[\Theta(|\mathcal{L}^{S,M}_{t}x^{i}_{R}|)
