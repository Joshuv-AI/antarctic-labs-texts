Rad-Phi2: Instruction Tuning Phi-2 for Radiology
Mercy Ranjit, Gopinath Ganapathy, Shaury Srivastav, Tanuja Ganu, Srujana Oruganti

Abstract
Small Language Models (SLMs) have shown remarkable performance in general domain language understanding, reasoning and coding tasks, but their capabilities in the medical domain, particularly concerning radiology text, is less explored. In this study, we investigate the application of SLMs for general radiology knowledge specifically question answering related to understanding of symptoms, radiological appearances of findings, differential diagnosis, assessing prognosis, and suggesting treatments w.r.t diseases pertaining to different organ systems. Additionally, we explore the utility of SLMs in handling text-related tasks with respect to radiology reports within AI-driven radiology workflows.We fine-tune Phi-2 , a SLM with 2.7 billion parameters using high-quality educational content from Radiopaedia ( Radiopaedia.org ) , a collaborative online radiology resource. The resulting language model, RadPhi-2-Base , exhibits the ability to address general radiology queries across various systems (e.g., chest, cardiac). Furthermore, we investigate Phi-2 for instruction tuning, enabling it to perform specific tasks. By fine-tuning Phi-2 on both general domain tasks and radiology-specific tasks related to chest X-ray reports, we create Rad-Phi2 . Our empirical results reveal that Rad-Phi2 Base and Rad-Phi2 perform comparably or even outperform larger models such as Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 and GPT-4 providing concise and precise answers. In summary, our work demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of utilizing SLMs in radiology workflows both for knowledge related queries as well as for performing specific tasks related to radiology reports, thereby opening up new avenues for enhancing the quality and efficiency of radiology practice.

1
Introduction

Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) have enabled the development of powerful language models that can generate natural language texts for various purposes and domains. However, most of the existing language models are trained on general domain texts, such as news articles, books, web pages, etc., and may not be able to handle the specific vocabulary, concepts, terminology, and style of radiology texts. Hence, it is required to have domain specific models that embody knowledge for that domain and trained for specific tasks in domain specific workflows to deal with the nuances of the field. But, the size and complexity of these language models are often prohibitive for practical applications, as they require a large amount of computational resources and memory to train and run.

In this paper, we explore the use of small language models (SLMs) for radiology related tasks, specifically text-related tasks relating to radiology knowledge and tasks that are useful in everyday radiology workflows pertaining to radiology reports.

SLMs are language models that have a relatively small number of parameters, compared to the state-of-the-art large language models, such as
GPT-4

(
OpenAI et al. 2023
)
. SLMs, specifically
Phi-2

(
Gunasekar et al. 2023
)
is a 2.7 billion parameter model that has shown remarkable performance in general domain language understanding, reasoning, and coding tasks, but its capabilities in the medical domain, especially on radiology content and tasks specific to a radiology workflows is not explored. Moreover,
Phi-2
is not an instruction tuned model and tends to produce verbose answers as it was trained from synthetic text books and currently not suitable for direct deployments in application workflows to perform specific tasks. We hypothesize that SLMs can be effectively adapted to the radiology domain by finetuning them on high-quality radiology content to bring-in the nuanced radiology knowledge and also be adapted to perform specific tasks that are useful in everyday radiology workflows. SLMs provide the advantage of having a domain specific model for easy consumption at lower computational cost and faster inference.

The
Phi
models
(
Gunasekar et al. 2023
)
success and its on-par performance to large language models was attributed to the high quality aka
“textbook-quality"
training data that was used for its training. The training corpus was augmented with carefully selected data, filtered based on educational value and content quality. It demonstrated that this choice of training data enabled
Phi-2
to achieve outstanding performance on complex benchmarks, matching or outperforming models up to 25x larger in size. We extend this approach of
Phi-2
by using high-quality and reliable educational content from Radiopaedia
(
Radiopaedia.org
)
to finetune
Phi-2
for radiology knowledge. Radiopaedia is a rapidly growing collaborative online peer-reviewed open-edit radiology resource, compiled by radiologists and other health professionals from across the globe. Training from this highly valuable and reliable source can embody the model with this knowledge making it useful to answer questions on radiology accurately. To test our hypothesis, we use
Phi-2
, a 2.7 billion parameter SLM, as our base model, and finetune it on question-answer pairs derived from the educational articles of Radiopaedia pertaining to different systems like chest, cardiac, gastrointestinal etc. The resulting model
Rad-Phi2 Base
, can answer queries on radiology pertaining to these systems accurately. We evaluate the performance of
Rad-Phi2-Base
and compare it with larger instruction tuned models, such as
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2

(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
which was finetuned on the same radiology question answer pairs and also
GPT-4
.
Rad-Phi2 Base
performs better than or comparable to the much larger counterparts while producing more crisp and to-the-point answers. It even surpassed the Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) approach
(
Lewis et al. 2021
)
that directly used a knowledge base of Radiopaedia articles to answer the queries.

Apart from answering queries on radiology, there are specific text-related tasks that can be useful in AI deployment scenarios of radiology workflows. Some useful tasks relating to radiology reporting workflows are writing diagnostic impression summary from radiology findings, tagging of radiology reports with appropriate abnormality labels to flag priority cases, noise free extraction of findings and impression from existing radiology reports, generating a comparison summary of findings between the current radiology report and its prior report etc. Some of these tasks were also cited in the earlier work
(
Bhayana 2024
)
in the context of application of language models in radiology workflows. To address these useful tasks that arise in the AI deployment scenarios of radiology workflows, we create an instruction tuning dataset for tasks pertaining to radiology reports of Chest X-rays and instruction-tune
Phi-2
and obtain the
Rad-Phi2 model
. But, as
Phi-2
was not an instruction tuned model in the first place, we first instruction tune
Phi-2
for general domain tasks using the Super Natural instruction tuning dataset
(
Yizhong and Swaroop 2022
)
. The resulting model
Phi2-Instruct(SupNatInst)
is then further instruction tuned for tasks specific to radiology reports. The resulting model,
Rad-Phi2
can perform specific tasks that are useful in AI driven radiology reporting workflows of Chest X-rays. Our results show that
Rad-Phi2
performs better or on-par with a larger model like
Mistral-7B-Instruct(v0.2)
finetuned on the same instruction tuning dataset.

We would also like to note that while
GPT-4
has reasonable radiology knowledge as noted in this work
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
, it still falls short on nuanced tasks like impression generation in comparison to domain specific models unless given with a large number of carefully curated examples in conjunction with sophisticated prompt engineering approaches
(
Ma et al. 2024
)
. SLMs enables to develop domain-specific models for these nuanced task types easily and quickly due to the low resource requirements associated with its size.

Our work demonstrates the feasibility and effectiveness of using SLMs for radiology specific tasks, and opens up new possibilities for improving the quality and efficiency of radiology practice.

Our key Contributions include:

•

We demonstrate the effectiveness of SLMs in the radiology domain by training on high quality radiology content from Radiopaedia
(
Radiopaedia.org
)
. The resulting model
Rad-Phi2-Base
can answer general queries on radiology like symptoms, radiological appearances of findings, differential diagnosis, prognosis, and treatments w.r.t diseases pertaining to 15 different systems like Chest, Cardiac etc. It performs better than larger models like
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
finetuned on the same dataset. It also performed better than
GPT-4
and the Retrieval Augmented Generation approach that directly used Radiopaedia content as the knowledge source while answering queries.

•

We construct an instruction tuning dataset of text related tasks that are useful in radiology report writing workflows of Chest X-rays using
GPT-4

•

We also demonstrate Instruction Tuning on SLMs to make it usable for practical tasks involved in radiology workflows. As
Phi-2
is not an instruction tuned model and tends to produce verbose answers, we first perform general domain instruction tuning using the Super Natural Instructions dataset followed by radiology specific instruction tuning on tasks related to radiology reports of Chest X-rays. Rad-Phi2
which is useful in performing everyday tasks in radiology report writing workflows of Chest X-rays.

•

Our ablation studies gives insights on the input formats that worked the best when instruction tuning the
Phi-2
model. We note that radiology specific instruction tuning performs better when preceded by general domain instruction tuning and semantic tasks like Natural Language Inference(NLI) can help with the performance of other tasks.

2
Related Work

The work, ‘Exploring the Boundaries of
GPT-4
in Radiology’
(
Liu et al. 2023a
)
evaluated
GPT-4
on a diverse range of common radiology text-based tasks. It was noted that that zero-shot prompting technique was sufficient for the
‘learn-by-instruction’
tasks like sentence similarity, radiology NLI, and disease progression. However, a large number of carefully curated examples with advanced prompt engineering were required for
‘learn-by-example’
tasks like impression summary writing to perform on par with the supervised models trained for the task. While it is beneficial to have
GPT-4
like instruction-tuned models that can work well on radiology content for
‘learn-by-instruction’
tasks, it is still beneficial to have specialized models for
‘learn-by-example’
kind of tasks where the required knowledge for the model needs to be fed in as carefully curated in-context examples. It is even better if a model can do both these kinds of tasks with high accuracy at lower computational and resource costs, which is what we accomplish in our work.

The work ‘Chatbots and Large Language Models in Radiology: A Practical Primer for Clinical and Research Applications’
(
Bhayana 2024
)
highlights how LLMs are useful in typical radiology workflows, providing key relevant information from clinical notes and prior reports, thus improving radiology interpretation and clinical decision making. The paper also noted LLMs when connected to sources like the American College of Radiology performed better than radiologists in applying this information to clinical scenarios. In regular practice, radiologists and clinicians refer to external knowledge sources like Radiopaedia
(
Radiopaedia.org
)
for information like clinical symptoms, radiological appearances of findings, differential diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment plan of a disease that aids them with clinical decision making. These knowledge sources are very reliable as they are maintained by practitioners of medical practice and are actively updated. However, this also poses a need for the AI models to be updated regularly with this knowledge. While Retrieval Augmented Generation
(
Lewis et al. 2021
)
can help LLMs augment their responses with the knowledge from the external sources, it may prove costly for high usage scenarios. This calls for a need for having Small Language Models that are easy to train, deploy, retrain, and iterate.

While instruction tuning to perform multiple tasks has been well explored on general domain tasks, it is less explored on radiology specific tasks.
Radiology-Llama2
(
Liu et al. 2023b
)
and
Radiology-GPT
(
Liu et al. 2023c
)
performed instruction tuning for one specific task of generating clinically useful impression summary from radiological findings. However, there are a variety of tasks that can be useful in radiology report writing workflows, like extracting a clean set of findings or impressions from a noisy radiology report, tagging the reports with abnormality labels or tubes, lines and devices labels, or comparing two reports to see the differential findings between them, querying the reports for specific questions or querying for general radiology knowledge with respect to various diseases like their radiographic appearances, symptoms, differential diagnosis or treatment plans to make a clinical decision. We consider all these useful tasks for instruction tuning.

We explore the usage of Small Language Models for Radiology-specific tasks, as these models are very easy to train and deploy due to their smaller size, while being very competitive in performance to large language models.
Phi-2
paper
(
Gunasekar et al. 2023
)
introduced a 2.7 billion-parameter language model that demonstrated outstanding reasoning and language understanding capabilities, showcasing state-of-the-art performance among base language models with less than 13 billion parameters. On complex benchmarks,
Phi-2
matched or outperformed models up to 25x larger in size. The authors attributed this success to the high-quality textbook-like data they trained the model on. This is very applicable in the medical field, where the information rendered by the model needs to be accurate, which mandates the training dataset to be noise-free and of high quality. Our work considers this need of very high-quality data for training the models, which is practically deployable in day-to-day radiology workflows.

We also specifically note that
Phi-2
is a base model that has not undergone alignment through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
)
, nor has it been instruction finetuned. Due to this, it is noted that it often produces irrelevant or extra text following its first answer to user prompts within a single turn. This is due to its training dataset being primarily textbooks, which results in textbook-like responses. Our work addresses these issues by instruction tuning
Phi-2
first for general domain tasks to make it usable for downstream applications in Radiology.

In summary, our paper addresses the need for having special instruction tuned models for radiology which has accurate radiology knowledge and also can perform useful tasks on radiology workflows which is not fully addressed in the above stated works. In addition, we also perform these tasks using SLMs that are easy to train, deploy and iterate on without compromising on quality.

General Domain Instruction Tuning

We performed minimal general domain instruction tuning before we performed radiology specific finetuning using the Super Natural Instructions dataset
(
Yizhong and Swaroop 2022
)
. This dataset is a benchmark of 1,616 diverse NLP tasks including non-english tasks and their expert-written instructions. The collection covers 76 distinct task types. We used only the english specific tasks with a total of 757 tasks and 2,410,002 training records corresponding to these tasks.

Radiology Specific Instruction Tuning

We create two datasets for radiology specific finetuning, one for general radiology question answering and another for instruction tuning for tasks specific to radiology reports of Chest X-rays.

For radiology question answering task, we create question answer pairs using
GPT-4
from the articles of Radiopaedia pertaining to 15 different systems. We reserve the summary articles pertaining to these systems for testing. The count of question answer pairs w.r.t different systems is detailed in Table
1
. This dataset was used to train the
Rad-Phi2-Base
model.

The instruction tuning dataset for tasks specific to radiology reports was constructed from the radiology reports of Mimic-CXR dataset
(
Johnson et al. 2019
)
that contains radiology reports pertaining to Chest X-rays. We use
GPT-4
processing and also annotations from the existing datasets derived from Mimic-CXR reports to construct this dataset. The prompts associated with the different tasks in the instruction tuning dataset is defined in Table
2
. This dataset was used to train the
Rad-Phi2
model. The number of training records in the instruction tuning dataset for various tasks is detailed in Table
3
.

We construct the below tasks:

EXTRACT FINDINGS: Extract the findings from a given radiology report.

EXTRACT IMPRESSION: Extract the impression section from a given radiology report.

CLEANUP RADIOLOGY REPORT TEXT:
Given a radiology report text of findings or impression which includes noisy details like mention of Dr. names, followup actions etc., remove the noisy details to produce a clean radiology text of findings. We used
GPT-4
processing to generate a clean set of findings from a radiology report.

QA COMPREHENSION:
Given a radiology report, answer the question based on the radiology report. The question could be about the abnormalities in the report, associated location or severity of findings etc. We use the Medical-Diff VQA dataset
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
to construct the QA comprehension task.

QA TEMPORAL FINDINGS:
Given a radiology report and its prior report, extract the differential set of findings between them. We use the Medical-Diff VQA dataset
(
Hu et al. 2023
)
to construct the QA temporal comprehension task from the corresponding radiology reports.

QA TEMPORAL PROGRESSION:
Given a radiology report, classify the progression of an abnormality as improved, worsened or unchanged. We use the ChestImagenome dataset
(
Wu et al. 2021
)
for the progression labels.

ABNORMALITY LABELS:
Given a radiology report, label the report as positive for a list of abnormalities. We tag the reports to findings/disease labels defined in the ChestImagenome dataset
(
Wu et al. 2021
)
.

TUBES, LINES AND DEVICES LABELS:
Given a radiology report, label the report for the presence of tubes, line and devices. We tag the reports to tubes, lines and device labels defined in the ChestImagenome dataset
(
Wu et al. 2021
)
.

IMPRESSION PREDICTION:
Given the findings from a radiology report, summarize the findings as a diagnostic impression.

Results

4.1
Evaluation Metrics

Lexical metrics

We use the lexical NLP metrics that measures the overlap between the predicted and reference texts. ROUGE-L
(
Lin 2004
)
measures the length of the longest common word sub sequence relative to the lengths of predicted and reference reports. BLEU-1
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
is based on uni-gram precision with a brevity penalty to discourage too short predictions. F1-score is a harmonic mean of unigram precision and recall. We use the implementation of
(
Li et al. 2023
)
for F1-score evaluations.

GPT-4 based Evaluation

As lexical metrics does not always necessarily provide an accurate evaluation for all cases, for example in a QA comprehension task where the model has to give an answer based on the context given, the predicted answer may be accurate in terms of semantics but the wording may be slightly different which results in a less accurate evaluation. To counter this, we use
GPT-4
based evaluation for QA comprehension tasks following the work of
(
Liu et al. 2023d
)
, where
GPT-4
is used to come up with relevancy and accuracy scores to measure hallucinations in the predictions in a instruction tuning setting. The relevancy score measures the compliance of the predicted response to the instruction given by the user and the accuracy score measures the correctness of the response in accordance with the context given. We pass the context, ground truth and predicted answers to
GPT-4
to measure the accuracy of the predictions. Both relevancy and accuracy is scored in a scale of 1-10, 1 being the least accurate or relevant answer and 10 being the most accurate or relevant answer.

Clinical Metrics

In addition to the above metrics, as we deal with radiology text generation for tasks like impression prediction, we also use a clinical metric named RadGraph F1 that measures the accuracy and completeness of the clinical facts generated by the model. The RadGraph F1 is based on the RadGraph model
(
Jain et al. 2021
)
that parses the radiology reports into graphs containing clinical entities like anatomy and observations and relations between them. Entities are considered to match if the text spans and assigned types are the same, and relations are matched if their endpoint
entities and relation type are the same. We specifically use the Radgraph python package which is an implementation of the Radgraph F1 metric based on rewards
(
Jain et al. 2021
)
. The reward based scores are given based on the completeness of the extraction of entities and relationships using the RadGraph model.

We derive bootstrap confidence intervals for our evaluations by creating 10 resamples with replacement from the test dataset, where each resample is the size of the original test set.

5
Discussion

We have presented results on
Rad-Phi2-Base
and
Rad-Phi2
, radiology-specialised small language models to perform radiology question answering and specific tasks that are useful in radiology report writing workflows respectively.

Rad-Phi2-Base
was finetuned from
Phi-2

(
Gunasekar et al. 2023
)
using high quality and reliable article content from Radiopaedia
(
Radiopaedia.org
)
, a trusted resource used by radiologists throughout the world. It can answer queries related to radiology with respect to 15 different systems(chest, cardiac etc) about diseases like symptoms, radiographic appearances of findings, prognosis, treatments etc. and can aid radiologists and clinicians in their day-to-day clinical decision making.
Rad-Phi2-Base
performed better than all larger models like
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
which was also finetuned on the same dataset and also
GPT-4
.
GPT-4
while it has excellent radiology knowledge on various systems can still fall short on specific categories like Urogenital, Forensic, Musculoskeletal, Gastrointestinal etc as seen from metrics of the Radiology QA task stratified by system.

Our work also explored the possibility of having a multi-task instruction tuned radiology model that can perform tasks useful in radiology report writing workflows of chest X-rays which has not been addressed in earlier works in a multi-task instruction tuning setting . This includes tasks like impression writing, temporal comparisons of reports for differential findings, extraction of a clean set of findings from radiology reports and tagging of reports with appropriate abnormality labels, tubes and lines labels or disease progression labels. Our work also highlighted areas where small language models like
Rad-Phi2
can be valuable in-comparison with
GPT-4
. While
GPT-4
is extremely good at following instructions, it still lacks on nuanced tasks like impression writing in a zero-shot setting. It requires advanced approaches like
Impression GPT

(
Ma et al. 2024
)
which uses an advanced prompt engineering algorithm with an iterative dynamic prompt optimisation approach to be comparable in performance with models finetuned for specific tasks like
Rad-Phi2
. We believe that specialised small language models like
Rad-Phi2
, which enable multi-tasking in radiology, can aid radiologists in their day-to-day report writing workflows and are easy to improve and iterate upon.

Rad-Phi2-Base
and
Rad-Phi2
, being smaller models still performs on-par with larger instruction-tuned LLMs like
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
. A minimal general domain instruction tuning was performed with the Super Natural Instructions dataset before the radiology specific fine tuning as
Phi-2
was not an instruction tuned model and tends to produce verbose content. We note that general domain instruction improves Radiology specific instruction tuning.
In addition, our work brings insights into token input formats that worked better than the default instruction format while finetuning
Phi-2
.
Future work can explore the possibilities of further instruction tuning with diverse instruction sets and other prompt strategies like few-shot and chain-of-thoughts.

6
Conclusion

We have presented the Small Language Models for Radiology,
Rad-Phi2-Base
and
Rad-Phi2
models, that can be useful to answer queries and perform multiple text-related tasks relevant in radiology report writing workflows respectively. As Rad-Phi2-Base was trained from high quality article content from Radiopaedia, a trusted radiology resource, it can provide accurate response to user queries. Despite being smaller and minimally instruction-tuned,
Rad-Phi2-Base
and
Rad-Phi2
shows competitive performance when compared with the larger instruction-tuned counterparts thus making language models more accessible for clinical utility in real-life radiology workflows.
