The Behavior of a Three-Term Hofstadter-Like Recurrence with Linear Initial Conditions
Nathan Fox

Abstract
In this paper, we study the three-term nested recurrence relation B ⁡ ( n ) = B ⁡ ( n − B ⁡ ( n − 1 ) ) + B ⁡ ( n − B ⁡ ( n − 2 ) ) + B ⁡ ( n − B ⁡ ( n − 3 ) ) B(n)=B(n-B(n-1))+B(n-B(n-2))+B(n-B(n-3)) subject to initial conditions where the first N N terms are the integers 1 1 through N N . This recurrence is the three-term analog of Hofstadter’s famous Q Q -recurrence Q ⁡ ( n ) = Q ⁡ ( n − Q ⁡ ( n − 1 ) ) + Q ⁡ ( n − Q ⁡ ( n − 2 ) ) Q(n)=Q(n-Q(n-1))+Q(n-Q(n-2)) . Nested recurrences are highly sensitive to their initial conditions. Some initial conditions lead to finite sequences, others lead to predictable sequences, and yet others lead to sequences that appear to be chaotic and infinite. A corresponding study to this one was previously carried out on the Q Q -recurrence. As with that work, we consider two families of sequences, one where terms with nonpositive indices are undefined and a second where terms with nonpositive indices are defined to be zero. We find similar results here as with the Q Q -recurrence, as we can completely characterize the sequences for sufficiently large N N . The results here are, in a sense, simpler, as our sequences are all finite for sufficiently large N N .

Introduction

The Hofstadter
Q
-recurrence
[
10
]

Q(n)=Q(n-Q(n-1))+Q(n-Q(n-2))

has been the subject of numerous studies. Hofstadter’s original
Q
-sequence starts with initial conditions
Q(1)=Q(2)=1
. This sequence has tantalizing properties that have thus far evaded proof, though they have been the subject of statistical studies
[
11
,
2
]
. As such, most studies of the
Q
-recurrence analyze sequences generated by other initial conditions
[
9
,
12
,
7
,
3
,
4
]
. One recent approach involves studying a family of initial conditions described by a parameter
[
8
]
.

In this paper, we apply the parametrized initial condition approach to a different recurrence, the three-term Hofstadter-like recurrence

B(n)=B(n-B(n-1))+B(n-B(n-2))+B(n-B(n-3)).

This recurrence is known to generate a well-behaved sequence
[
5
]
when given initial conditions
B(1)=1
,
B(2)=2
,
B(3)=3
,
B(4)=4
, and
B(5)=5
. For convenience, we refer to this well-behaved sequence as the
B
-sequence
. Aside from that one article, this recurrence has not been widely reported on. This is presumably because most natural initial conditions lead to finite sequences. If a sequence
B^{*}(n)
generated by the
B
-recurrence ever has
B^{*}(n-1)\geq n
or
B^{*}(n-1)\leq 0
, then
B^{*}(n)
would be undefined. When this sort of behavior occurs, we say that the sequence
dies
after
n-1
terms, or that it dies at index
n
. This assumes, as is standard, that the first term defined by the initial conditions is
B^{*}(1)
. In this paper, we consider such initial conditions, but we also consider infinite initial conditions that define values for
B^{*}(n)
when
n\leq 0
. In this realm,
B^{*}(n-1)\geq n
does not lead to sequence death, but
B^{*}(n-1)\leq 0
still does. So, to avoid some confusion later in the paper, when a sequence dies because
B^{*}(n-1)\leq 0
, we say that the sequence
ends
.

1.1
Notation

Going forward, the only recurrence relation we discuss is the
B
-recurrence, but we study it with many different initial conditions. We introduce analogous notation to
[
8
]
. The notation
B(n)
refers to the
n
th term of the
B
-sequence itself. The notation
B^{*}(n)
refers to a generic sequence that satisfies the
B
-recurrence. For any other specific sequence satisfying the
B
-recurrence, we use
B
with a subscript that we define for that particular sequence.

We use angle brackets to denote our initial conditions. For example,
\left<1,2,3,4,5\right>
is shorthand for the initial conditions for the
B
-sequence. Sometimes, we wish to define
B^{*}(n)=0
for all
n\leq 0
in order to prevent our sequences from dying too soon. This convention is noted with a symbol
\bar{0}
followed by a semicolon at the start of the initial conditions. For example,
\left<\bar{0};1,1,1\right>
is shorthand for
B^{*}(n)=0
for
n\leq 0
,
B^{*}(1)=1
,
B^{*}(2)=1
, and
B^{*}(3)=1
.

Structure of this Paper

This paper’s structure mirrors that of the analogous study on the
Q
-recurrence
[

]
. In Section
2

3
Linear Initial Conditions with Extra Zeroes

The sequences in Section
2
almost all die. As in
[
8
]
, we now consider what happens if we prevent them from dying quickly by defining their values to be zero at nonpositive integers. For an integer
N\geq 3
, let
B_{\bar{N}}
denote the sequence obtained from the
B
-recurrence with initial conditions
\left<\bar{0};1,2,3,\ldots,N\right>
.

In
[
8
]
, it was seen that the corresponding behavior for the Hofstadter
Q
-recurrences depends on the congruence class of
N
modulo
5
. Three of those cases lead to the end of the sequence, one leads to a semi-predictable pattern that seems to go on forever, and the fifth case leads to a dependence on the congruence class of
N
modulo
25
and, thereafter, potentially higher powers of
5
. For the sequences
B_{\bar{N}}
, the dependence is instead on the congruence class of
N
modulo
7
. Here, all seven cases lead to the end of the sequence without needing to consider any cases involving higher powers of
7
. But, all cases require fairly large values of
N
to be valid.
We have the following main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
N\geq 72
be
a
natural number. Then the following period-
7
pattern begins at index
N+67
in
B_{\bar{N}}
, where
k
denotes a positive integer:

\begin{cases}B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k)=7k+2\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+1)=N+7k+2\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+2)=N+7k+4\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+3)=7\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+4)=2N+2k+45\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+5)=2N+k-7\\
B_{\bar{N}}(N+7k+6)=N-2.\end{cases}

This pattern lasts through index
2N+\nu
, where

\nu=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }N\equiv 0\pmod{7}\\
-2&\text{if }N\equiv 1\pmod{7}\\
-2&\text{if }N\equiv 2\pmod{7}\\
-2&\text{if }N\equiv 3\pmod{7}\\
2&\text{if }N\equiv 4\pmod{7}\\
1&\text{if }N\equiv 5\pmod{7}\\
0&\text{if }N\equiv 6\pmod{7}.\end{cases}

After this,

•

If
N\equiv 0\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 196
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+27
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 1\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 2087
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+254
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 2\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 3201
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+524
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 3\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 4315
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+560
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 4\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 200
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+20
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 5\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 32478
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+4547
terms.

•

If
N\equiv 6\pmod{7}
and
N\geq 118
, then
B_{\bar{N}}
ends after
2N+9
terms.

The proof of Theorem
2
uses the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
K\geq 7
,
c\geq 1
and
0\leq\gamma\leq 6
. Then, let
\lambda
and
\mu
be integers such that

\lambda\geq\begin{cases}-2c+2&\text{if }\gamma=0\\
-2c+1&\text{if }\gamma=1\\
-2c+4&\text{if }\gamma=2\\
-2c+3&\text{if }\gamma=3\\
-2c+2&\text{if }\gamma=4\\
-2c+1&\text{if }\gamma=5\\
-2c&\text{if }\gamma=6\end{cases}\hskip 14.45377pt\text{and}\hskip 14.45377pt\mu\geq\begin{cases}-c&\text{if }\gamma=0\\
-c&\text{if }\gamma=1\\
-c+3&\text{if }\gamma=2\\
-c+2&\text{if }\gamma=3\\
-c+1&\text{if }\gamma=4\\
-c&\text{if }\gamma=5\\
-c-1&\text{if }\gamma=6.\end{cases}

Define
L=K-7c-\gamma
and
M=K+L+5
.
Then, for arbitrary integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L}
, denote the sequence resulting from the
B
-recurrence and the
initial conditions

\left<\bar{0};1,2,\ldots,K,6,a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{L},2K+\lambda-2,2K+\mu-1,K-2\right>

by
B_{C}
. The sequence
B_{C}
follows the following pattern from
B_{C}(M-3)
through
B_{C}(2K+\nu)

\begin{cases}B_{C}(M+7k)=L+7k+7\\
B_{C}(M+7k+1)=M+7k+2\\
B_{C}(M+7k+2)=M+7k+4\\
B_{C}(M+7k+3)=7\\
B_{C}(M+7k+4)=2K+2k+\lambda\\
B_{C}(M+7k+5)=2K+k+\mu\\
B_{C}(M+7k+6)=K-2\end{cases}

where

\nu=\begin{cases}-2&\text{if }\gamma=0\\
-2&\text{if }\gamma=1\\
2&\text{if }\gamma=2\\
1&\text{if }\gamma=3\\
0&\text{if }\gamma=4\\
-1&\text{if }\gamma=5\\
-2&\text{if }\gamma=6.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the index. The base cases are
B_{C}(M-3)
through
B_{C}(M-1)
, which are part of the initial conditions. Now, suppose
M\leq n\leq 2K+\nu
, and suppose that
B_{C}(n^{\prime})
is what we want it to be for all
M-3\leq n^{\prime}<n
.

There are seven cases to consider:

n-M\equiv 0\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k-B_{C}(M+7k-1))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k-B_{C}(M+7k-2))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k-B_{C}(M+7k-3))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k-\left(K-2\right))+B_{C}(M+7k-\left(2K+k-1+\mu\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k-\left(2K+2k-2+\lambda\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(L+7k+7)+B_{C}(-K+L+6k+6-\mu)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(-K+L+5k+7-\lambda).

We know that
n\leq 2K+\nu
. Since
n=M+7k
, we have
n-M=7k\leq 2K+\nu-M=K-L-5+\nu=7c+\gamma-5+\nu
. Observe that

\gamma-5+\nu=\begin{cases}-7&\gamma=0\\
-6&\gamma=1\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
7k\equiv 0\pmod{7}
, we actually have
7k\leq 7c-7
, since
\left\lfloor\frac{\gamma-5+\nu}{7}\right\rfloor
always equals
-1
.
In particular, this means that
L+7k+7\leq L+7c=K-\gamma\leq K
.
As a result,
B_{C}(L+7k+7)=L+7k+7
.

We also have

-K+L+6k+6-\mu=-7c-\gamma+6k+6-\mu\leq-7c-\gamma+\frac{6}{7}(7c-7)+6-\mu=-c-\gamma-\mu.

Observe that

-c-\gamma-\mu=\begin{cases}0&\gamma=0\\
-1&\gamma=1\\
-5&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

which implies that
-c-\gamma-\mu\leq 0
. In turn, this means that
-K+L+6k+6-\mu\leq 0
, implying that
B_{C}(-K+L+6k+6-\mu)=0
.

Similarly, we have that

-K+L+5k+7-\lambda=-7c-\gamma+5k+7-\lambda\leq-7c-\gamma+\frac{5}{7}(7c-7)+7-\lambda=-2c-\gamma+2-\lambda.

Observe that

-2c-\gamma+2-\lambda=\begin{cases}0&\gamma=0\text{ or }\gamma=1\\
-4&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

which implies that
-2c-\gamma+2-\lambda\leq 0
. In turn, this means that
-K+L+5k+7-\lambda\leq 0
, implying that
B_{C}(-K+L+5k+7-\lambda)=0
.
So,
B_{C}(M+7k)=L+7k+7
, as required.

n-M\equiv 1\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k+1
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k+1)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+1-B_{C}(M+7k))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+1-B_{C}(M+7k-1))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+1-B_{C}(M+7k-2))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+1-\left(L+7k+7\right))+B_{C}(M+7k+1-\left(K-2\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+1-\left(2K+k-1+\mu\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(K-1)+B_{C}(L+7k+8)+B_{C}(-K+L+6k+7-\mu).

We know that
B_{C}(K-1)=K-1
.
We also know that
n\leq 2K+\nu
. Since
n=M+7k+1
, we have
n-M-1=7k\leq 2K+\nu-M-1=K-L-6+\nu=7c+\gamma-6+\nu
. Observe that

\gamma-6+\nu=\begin{cases}-8&\gamma=0\\
-7&\gamma=1\\
-2&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
7k\equiv 0\pmod{7}
, we actually have

7k\leq\begin{cases}7c-14&\gamma=0\\
7c-7&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular, this means that if
\gamma=0
then
L+7k+8\leq L+7c-6=K-6<K
, and if
\gamma\neq 0
then
L+7k+8\leq L+7c+1=K-\gamma+1\leq K
.
As a result,
B_{C}(L+7k+8)=L+7k+8
.

We also have
-K+L+6k+7-\mu=-7c-\gamma+6k+7-\mu
.
When
\gamma=0
,
-7c-\gamma+6k+7-\mu\leq-7c-\gamma+\frac{6}{7}(7c-14)+7-\mu=-c-\gamma-5-\mu=-5<0.
When
\gamma\neq 0
,

-7c-\gamma+6k+7-\mu\leq-7c-\gamma+\frac{6}{7}(7c-7)+7-\mu=-c-\gamma+1-\mu.

Observe that

-c-\gamma+1-\mu=\begin{cases}1&\gamma=0\\
0&\gamma=1\\
-4&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Putting all cases together imply that
-c-\gamma+1-\mu\leq 0
when
\gamma\neq 0
. In turn, this all means that
-K+L+6k+7-\mu\leq 0
, implying that
B_{C}(-K+L+6k+7-\mu)=0
.
So,
B_{C}(M+7k+1)=\left(K-1\right)+\left(L+7k+8\right)=K+L+7k+7=M+7k+2
, as required.

n-M\equiv 2\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k+2
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k+2)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+2-B_{C}(M+7k+1))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+2-B_{C}(M+7k))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+2-B_{C}(M+7k-1))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+2-\left(M+7k+2\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+2-\left(L+7k+7\right))+B_{C}(M+7k+2-\left(K-2\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(0)+B_{C}(K)+B_{C}(L+7k+9).

We know that
B_{C}(0)=0
and that
B_{C}(K)=K
.
We also know that
n\leq 2K+\nu
.
Since
n=M+7k+2
, we have
n-M-2=7k\leq 2K+\nu-M-2=K-L-7+\nu=7c+\gamma-7+\nu
. Observe that

\gamma-7+\nu=\begin{cases}-9&\gamma=0\\
-8&\gamma=1\\
-3&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
7k\equiv 0\pmod{7}
, we actually have

7k\leq\begin{cases}7c-14&\gamma=0\text{ or }\gamma=1\\
7c-7&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular, this means that if
\gamma\leq 1
then
L+7k+9\leq L+7c-4=K-\gamma-4<K
, and if
\gamma\geq 2
then
L+7k+9\leq L+7c+2=K-\gamma+2\leq K
.
As a result,
B_{C}(L+7k+9)=L+7k+9
.
So,
B_{C}(M+7k+2)=K+L+7k+9=M+7k+4
, as required.

n-M\equiv 3\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k+3
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k+3)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+3-B_{C}(M+7k+2))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+3-B_{C}(M+7k+1))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+3-B_{C}(M+7k))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+3-\left(M+7k+4\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+3-\left(M+7k+2\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+3-\left(L+7k+7\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(-1)+B_{C}(1)+B_{C}(K+1)=0+1+6=7,

as required.

n-M\equiv 4\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k+4
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k+4)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+4-B_{C}(M+7k+3))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+4-B_{C}(M+7k+2))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+4-B_{C}(M+7k+1))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+4-7)+B_{C}(M+7k+4-\left(M+7k+4\right))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+4-\left(M+7k+2\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k-3)+B_{C}(0)+B_{C}(2).

We know that
B_{C}(0)=0
and that
B_{C}(2)=2
. By induction, we have
B_{C}(M+7k-3)=2K+2k+\lambda-2
. So,
B_{C}(M+7k+4)=2K+2k+\lambda
, as required.

n-M\equiv 5\pmod{7}
:

In this case,
n=M+7k+5
for some
k
. Applying the
B
-recurrence, we have

\displaystyle B_{C}(M+7k+5)

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+5-B_{C}(M+7k+4))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+5-B_{C}(M+7k+3))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+5-B_{C}(M+7k+2))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(M+7k+5-\left(2K+2k+\lambda\right))+B_{C}(M+7k+5-7)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+B_{C}(M+7k+5-\left(M+7k+4\right))

\displaystyle=B_{C}(-K+L+5k+10-\lambda)+B_{C}(M+7k-2)+B_{C}(1).

We know that
B_{C}(1)=1
. By induction, we have
B_{C}(M+7k-2)=2K+k+\mu-1
.
We also know that
n\leq 2K+\nu
.
Since
n=M+7k+5
, we have
n-M-5=7k\leq 2K+\nu-M-5=K-L-10+\nu=7c+\gamma-10+\nu
. Observe that

\gamma-10+\nu=\begin{cases}-12&\gamma=0\\
-11&\gamma=1\\
-6&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since
7k\equiv 0\pmod{7}
, we actually have

7k\leq\begin{cases}7c-14&\gamma=0\text{ or }\gamma=1\\
7c-7&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

From here, we have

n-M\equiv 6\pmod{7}

Future Work

Studying nested recurrence relations with symbolic initial conditions of this type was initiated in
[
8
]
with Hofstadter’s
Q
-recurrence, and it is continued here with the three-term analog to that recurrence. The obvious idea is to continue adding more terms to the recurrence. But, that study was undertaken in
[
6
]
, and it suggests that chaos reigns supreme for the recurrences with four or more terms, even for large
N
. One question, then, would be whether any other symbolic initial conditions lead to predictable solutions to these recurrences. Another direction would be to study these same sorts of initial conditions with other recurrences, such as the Conolly recurrence
[
1
]
or the Tanny recurrence
[
14
]
.

The other big research direction suggested by this work is further exploration and discovery of results like Lemmas
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
, and
5
. These five lemmas all describe temporary or permanent solutions to the
B
-recurrence that consist of interleavings of simpler sequences. These particular results appear in this paper because they are needed to analyze the sequences
B_{\bar{N}}
for various values of
N
. But, they are by no means exhaustive among results of this type. The first known solution of this type to a nested recurrence is Golomb’s solution to the
Q
-recurrence
[
9
]
, which uses initial conditions
\left<3,2,1\right>
. Later, Ruskey gave a solution to the
Q
-recurrence with exponentially growing subsequences
[
12
]
via initial conditions
\left<\bar{0};3,6,5,3,6,8\right>
. A general algorithmic framework for finding and proving these sorts of solutions was laid out in
[
7
]
. All of these solutions last forever; the first known occurrence of temporary interleaved solutions is in
[
8
]
, in analogous work to this paper on the
Q
-recurrence. Perhaps such solutions could also be worked into the framework from
[
7
]
.
