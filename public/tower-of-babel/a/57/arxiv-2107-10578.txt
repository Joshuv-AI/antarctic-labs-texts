One-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix: One random variable
Worachet Bukaew, Sikarin Yoo-Kong

Abstract
We propose a generalised Fisher information or a one-parameter extended class of the Fisher information for the case of one random variable. This new form of the Fisher information is obtained from the intriguing connection between the standard Fisher information and the variational principle together with the non-uniqueness property of the Lagrangian. A generalised Cramér-Rao inequality is also derived and a Fisher information hierarchy is also obtained from the two-parameter Kullback-Leibler divergence. An interesting point is that the whole Fisher information hierarchy, except for the standard Fisher information, does not follow the additive rule. Furthermore, the idea can be directly extended to obtain the one-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix for the case of one random variable with multi-estimated parameters. The hierarchy of the Fisher information matrices is obtained. The geometrical meaning of the first two matrices in the hierarchy is studied through the normal distribution. What we find is that these first two Fisher matrices give different nature of curvature on the same statistical manifold for the normal distribution. Keywords : Fisher information, Fisher information matrix, q q -deformation, Tsallis index.

2
Fisher information and Fisher information matrix

We shall start in this section to give a brief review of the basic Fisher information and the connection with Kullback-Leibler divergence. Given a set of random variables
X=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
, which belongs in the sample space
\Omega

(X\in\Omega)
, and associated probability density functions
p(x_{i}\mid\theta)
, which is identical and independent, we define the likelihood as

L(\theta\mid X)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_{i}\mid\theta)
,
where
\theta
is an unknown parameter in the probability model. In principle, the likelihood measures how good the statistical model is comparing to the sample of data
X
for given the values of the unknown parameter
\theta
. Then what we need is to find maximum value of the likelihood
\partial L(\theta\mid X)/\partial\theta=0
.
However, in practice, the likelihood might not be well behave in calculation. Since logarithm is monotonic increasing function, we shall work with the log-likelihood
\partial\log L(\theta\mid X)/\partial\theta=0
(also called the score function), which gives the same maximum points.
What we are interested is the nature of the maximum peak as an indicator for how good it is for the estimated value. If the peak is thin, the estimated value is fairly determined. On the other hand, if the peak is broad, the estimated value is uncertainly determined. With this reason, we therefore consider the second derivative of the log-likelihood averaged over all possible random variables as the measure called the Fisher information

\displaystyle I(\theta)\equiv-\left<\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right>\;.

(2.1)

Employing the fact that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta\mid X)=\frac{1}{L(\theta\mid X)}\frac{\partial L(\theta\mid X)}{\partial\theta}\;,

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle\left<\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right>=0\;.

(2.3)

The Fisher information can be re-expressed as

\displaystyle I(\theta)=-\left<\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right>=\left<\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right)^{2}\right>=\text{Var}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right]\;.

(2.4)

Here, we can conclude that Fisher information tells us how much we know about the internal structure from data with a given space of outcomes (sample space)
\Omega
.

The Fisher information also provides a information lower bound on the variance of an unbiased estimator for a parameter. This relation is known as the Cramér-Rao inequality. To obtain such relation, one can start to consider the unbiased estimator given by

\displaystyle B(\hat{\Theta})\equiv\left<\hat{\Theta}-\theta\right>=\int_{\Omega}...\int_{\Omega}(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)L(\theta\mid X)dX=0\;,

(2.5)

where
\hat{\Theta}=h(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})
is a point estimator. From now on, we might neglect subscription
\Omega
for the sake of simplicity. Next, we consider the derivative of (
2.5
) with respect to the parameter
\theta
resulting in

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left<\hat{\Theta}-\theta\right>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int...\int L(\theta\mid X)dX+\int...\int(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}L(\theta\mid X)dX\;.

(2.6)

Using the fact that
\int...\int L(X\mid\theta)dX=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int...\int\left[(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)\cdot L^{1/2}(\theta\mid X)\right]\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right)\cdot L^{1/2}(\theta\mid X)\right]dX

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\;.

(2.7)

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\int...\int{(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)^{2}L(\theta\mid X)}dX}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int...\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)\right)^{2}L(\theta\mid X)dX

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Var}(\hat{\Theta})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle I(\theta)\;.

(2.8)

What we have in (
2.8
) is that the variance of any such estimator is at least as much as the inverse of the Fisher information.

In general, the estimated parameters could come in a set i.e.,
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{n})
. What we have now is the element
(i,j)
of Fisher information matrix:
[I(\theta)]_{ij}
given by

\displaystyle[I(\theta)]_{ij}=-\left<\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}\log L(\theta|X)\right>=\left<\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\log L(\theta\mid X)\right)\right>\;.

(2.9)

The interesting point is that the Fisher matrix can also be obtained by considering the relative entropy or Kullback-Leibler divergence between two distribution
p(X)
and
q(X)
on the probability manifold

KL(p||q)=\int...\int p(X)\log\left(\frac{p(X)}{q(X)}\right)dX\;.

(2.10)

Then the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability distributions
L(\theta|X)
and
L(\theta^{\prime}|X)
, parametised by
\theta
, is given by

D(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\equiv KL(L(\theta|X)||L(\theta^{\prime}|X))=\int...\int L(\theta|X)\log\left(\frac{L(\theta|X)}{L(\theta^{\prime}|X)}\right)dX\;.

(2.11)

For
\theta
being fixed, the Kullback-Leibler divergence can be expanded around
\theta
as

D(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right)^{\text{T}}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{\prime}_{i}\partial\theta^{\prime}_{j}}D(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\right)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^{\prime}}\left(\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right)^{2}\right)\;,

(2.12)

where the second order derivative is

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{\prime}_{i}\partial\theta^{\prime}_{j}}D(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\right)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^{\prime}}=-\int...\int\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{\prime}_{i}\partial\theta^{\prime}_{j}}L(\theta^{\prime}|X)\right)\Big|_{\theta^{\prime}=\theta}L(\theta|X)dX=[I(\theta)]_{ij}\;.

(2.13)

With this connection, one may intuitively interpret the Fisher information as the metric between two point on the probability manifold. However, the Kullback-Leibler divergenece is not symmetric and does not follow the triangle inequality
[
29
]
. Then the Fisher information cannot be treated as a true metric.

3
One-parameter generalised Fisher information: One random variable

In this section, we shall derive a new type of the Fisher information called a one parameter generalised Fisher information. One of the key features which will play an important role to obtain such new Fisher information is the variational principle. The intriguing connection between the variational principle and Fisher information was proposed by Frieden
[
25
]
. Another feature is the non-uniqueness property of the Lagrangian which leads to the non-standard form of the Lagrangian called the multiplicative form
[
28
]
.

Let us now define
\theta
as actual value of a measurement quantity,
X=(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{N})
with
N
outcomes and
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{N})
as random errors associated with each measurement. Then we have

x_{i}=\theta+y_{i}\;.

(3.14)

Next, let
p(x_{i}|\theta)
be a probability distribution and
L(\theta\mid X)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(x_{i}\mid\theta)
be likelihood of probability distribution over the
x
’s with respect to
\theta
. Recalling the Fisher information
1
1

1

Here, we prefer the natural logarithm function.
(
2.1
), we can write the Fisher information in additive (see more details in
[
25
]
) form as

\displaystyle I(\theta)=\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln L(\theta\mid X)\right)^{2}L(\theta\mid X)\prod_{i=1}^{N}dx_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x_{i}\mid\theta)\right)^{2}p(x_{i}\mid\theta)dx_{i}\;.

(3.15)

In the case
N=1
, we do have
p(x\mid\theta)=p(x-\theta)=p(y)
. The Fisher information is simply reduced to

I[p(y)]=\int\frac{\left(p^{\prime}(y)\right)^{2}}{p(y)}dy\;\;\;\text{where}\;\;\;p^{\prime}(y)=\frac{dp(y)}{dy}.

(3.16)

Next, we do a transformation such that
q(y)=\sqrt{p(y)}
resulting in

I[q(y)]=4\int q^{\prime 2}(y)dy,\;\;\text{where}\;\;q^{\prime}(y)=\frac{dq(y)}{dy}.

(3.17)

We find that the Fisher information is now a functional with the input function
q(y)
. Here comes to an interesting point. If we define
I[q]\equiv S[q]
as an action functional and
\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)\equiv 4q^{\prime 2}(y)
as the Lagrangian, the variational principle would give the Euler-Lagrange equation

\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)}{\partial q(y)}-\frac{d}{dy}\left(\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)}{\partial q^{\prime}(y)}\right)=0,

(3.18)

resulting in
-8q^{\prime\prime}(y)=0
. This second order differential equation describes the motion for a free particle (how a position
q
change with time
y
). This would mean that the Fisher information (
3.17
) could be remarkably treated as the action functional for the free particle and, of course, in the absence of the interaction, the equation of motion in physics is a direct result of extramising the Fisher information:
\delta I[q]=0
.

Next, what we would like to focus is the property of the Lagrangian known as the non-uniqueness property. Commonly, two Lagrangians, differing by the total derivative with respect to time of some function
F(q,y)
, would give an identical equation of motion on extreamising the action. However, one could ask an inverse question as follows. Imposing the equation of motion, could we solve all possible Lagrangians directly from the Euler-Lagrange equation? The answer is definitely yes and this problem is known as the inverse problem of the calculus of variations
[
26
]
. Recently, Sarawuttinack et al
[
28
]
proposed a new type of Lagrangian called the multiplicative form for the case of one degree of freedom. Here we shall employ the same technique and propose an alternative Lagrangian for
\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)=4q^{\prime 2}(y)
as
\mathscr{L}_{\lambda}(q^{\prime},q;y)=4[e^{\lambda q^{\prime 2}(y)}-1]/\lambda
, where the parameter
\lambda
has to be an inverse square of the velocity. Of course, the Lagrangian
\mathscr{L}_{\lambda}(q^{\prime},q;y)
can be treated as one-parameter extended class of the standard Lagrangian
\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)
. It is not difficult to see that these two Lagrangians give exactly the same equation of motion. By considering the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
, one finds that
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to 0}\mathscr{L}_{\lambda}(q^{\prime},q;y)=\mathscr{L}(q^{\prime},q;y)=4q^{\prime 2}(y)
. Then what we have is the action functional in the form

I_{\lambda}[q(y)]=\frac{4}{\lambda}\int\bigg[e^{\lambda q^{\prime 2}(y)}-1\bigg]dy\;.

(3.19)

We shall call (
3.19
) as a one parameter generalised Fisher information. The reason can be seen as follows. If we expand the functional (
3.19
) with respect to the parameter
\lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}[q(y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int q^{\prime 2}(y)dy+4\frac{\lambda}{2!}\int q^{\prime 4}(y)dy+4\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}\int q^{\prime 6}(y)dy+...

(3.20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}[q(y)]+\frac{\lambda}{2!}I_{2}[q(y)]+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3!}I_{3}[q(y)]+...\;.

What we have in (
3.20
) is a hierarchy
\{I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},...\}
, where the first three functionals are

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(y)}{2q(y)}\right)^{2}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{2}}}\int\frac{p^{\prime 2}(y)}{p(y)}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{2}}}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x|\theta)\right)^{2}p(x|\theta)dx\;,

(3.21a)

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(y)}{2q(y)}\right)^{4}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{4}}}\int\frac{p^{\prime 4}(y)}{p^{2}(y)}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{4}}}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x|\theta)\right)^{4}p^{2}(x|\theta)dx\;,

(3.21b)

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(y)}{2q(y)}\right)^{6}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{6}}}\int\frac{p^{\prime 6}(y)}{p^{3}(y)}dy=\frac{4}{{2^{6}}}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x|\theta)\right)^{6}p^{3}(x|\theta)dx\;.

(3.21c)

The first term is nothing but the standard Fisher information
I_{1}[q]=I[q]
coinciding with the limit
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to 0}I_{\lambda}[q(y)]=I[q(y)]
. With the structure in (
3.21
), one could easily write
I_{n}[p(y)]
as

\displaystyle I_{n}(\theta)=\frac{4}{{2^{2n}}}\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x|\theta)\right)^{2n}p^{n}(x|\theta)dx\;,\;\;n=1,2,3,...\;\;\;.

(3.22)

Next point is that the standard Fisher information is in the average of the score function but the rest in the hierarchy is not. Then we shall seek a transformation to express the higher order Fisher information in the statistical average. We shall first consider the second function in (
3.21b
) and introduce a new variable
\phi_{1}\equiv p^{2}
such that
p^{\prime}(y)=\phi^{\prime}_{1}(y)/2p(y)
,
resulting in

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)=\frac{4}{4^{4}}\int\frac{\phi^{\prime 4}_{1}(y)}{\phi^{4}_{1}(y)}\phi_{1}(y)dy,\;\;\;\text{or}\;\;\;I_{2}(\theta)=\frac{4}{4^{4}}\int\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln\phi_{1}(x|\theta)\right]^{4}\phi_{1}(x|\theta)dx\;.

(3.23)

We shall call (
3.23
) as the 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
order Fisher information.
We can proceed the same technique of transformation, then the
n^{\text{th}}
order Fisher information (
3.22
) becomes

\displaystyle I_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{(2n)^{2n}}\int\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln\phi_{n-1}(x|\theta)\right]^{2n}\phi_{n-1}(x|\theta)dx,

(3.24)

where
\phi_{n-1}(y)=p^{n}(y)
and the generalised Fisher information (
3.20
) can be expressed in terms of infinite series as

I_{\lambda}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{n-1}}{n!}I_{n}(\theta)\;.

(3.25)

At this point, we may treat (
3.20
) as the generating function for the entire hierarchy of the Fisher information by expanding with respect to the parameter
\lambda
.

Working example
: The remaining thing is that what is the meaning of the higher order
n^{\text{th}}
Fisher information? For simplicity, we shall try to interpret the 1
{}^{\text{st}}
order and 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
order of the Fisher information in the hierarchy. To make thing more transparent, we shall work with the well known example: the normal distribution with two parameter

\displaystyle p(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\;,

(3.26)

where
\mu
is mean value and
\sigma^{2}
is variance, respectively.
Here, the parameter
\sigma
is given and the parameter
\mu
will be estimated. The standard Fisher information, the 1
{}^{\text{st}}
term in the hierarchy, is
1/\sigma^{2}
. This means that for normal distribution, Fisher information is proportional to the variance of the density function. Therefore, when the normal distribution is widely range (our data is in sort of spread), that is high value of
\sigma
, then we have a low Fisher information. On the other hand, if normal distribution comes in a narrow range, that is low value of
\sigma
, then we have a high Fisher information. These features imply that Fisher information can be used to measure the degree of disorder of a system as same as the entropy can do
[
23
]
.

For the 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
terms in the Fisher information heirarchy, we first make the transformation on the probability such that
\phi_{1}=p^{2}
, resulting in

\displaystyle\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}},

(3.27)

and

\displaystyle\ln\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln{\frac{1}{2 \pi\sigma^2}}-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ln{\frac{1}{2 \pi\sigma^2}}-\frac{x^{2}-2\mu x+\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\;.

Then we can compute the log-likelihood by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ln\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})}{\partial\mu}=\frac{2(x-\mu)}{\sigma^{2}}\;.

(3.29)

We then have

\displaystyle I_{2}[\theta]=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{(4^{4})}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})\right]^{4}\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{4}4}{(4^{4})\sigma^{8}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[x^{4}-4\mu x^{3}+6x^{2}\mu^{2}-4\mu^{3}x+\mu^{4}]\phi_{1}(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{4}4}{(4^{4})\sigma^{8}}\left[m_{4}-4\mu m_{3}+6\mu^{2}m_{2}-3\mu^{4}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(\sigma^{2})^{4}}m_{4}^{\prime}\;,

where

is called the

statistical raw moment and

is the 4
{}^{\text{th}}
central moment.
We can see that the 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
order Fisher information depends on the 4
{}^{\text{th}}
central moment for probability

and the reciprocal of the fourth power of the variance. The result seems to suggest that the 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
order Fisher information tells us about the outliers and tailedness
[

]
. Here let us elaborate a bit more. Suppose there are two normal distributions with different variances. For the large variance, the distribution will be broad and of course this means that it has higher chance to get the relatively rare events or long tail. On the other hand, the distribution will be quite sharp for the small variance and the tail will be short. Then with this particular example, we can see that the 2

\displaystyle\int\left[(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)\right]\left[\bigg(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x\mid\theta)\bigg)\;p^{q-1}(x\mid\theta)\right]p(x\mid\theta)dx\;.

(4.35)

Applying the Hölder’s inequality
[
33
]
to (

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[\int(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)^{\beta}p(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\beta}\left[\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x\mid\theta)\right)^{\alpha}(p^{q-1}(x\mid\theta))^{\alpha}p(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\alpha}

(4.36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\int(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)^{\beta}p(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\beta}\left[\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x\mid\theta)\right)^{\alpha}p^{\alpha(q-1)+1}(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\alpha},

where Hölder conjugates

\beta

Finally, employing (

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{q}}{q}=\frac{\int p^{q}(x\mid\theta)dx}{q}\leq\left[\int(\hat{\Theta}-\theta)^{\beta}p(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\beta}\left[\int\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(x\mid\theta)\right)^{\alpha}p^{\alpha(q-1)+1}(x\mid\theta)dx\right]^{1/\alpha}\;,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle I_{2}\;,

The Kullback–Leibler divergence revisited

Here in this section, we shall investigate on the connection between our Fisher information hierarchy and the Kullback–Leibler divergence. We shall begin with the Kullback–Leibler divergence

\displaystyle\mathit{D}(p(y)\parallel q(y))=\int p(y)\ln\left(\frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right)dy\;,

(5.41)

where
p
and
q
are two different points on the probability manifold. If
q(y)=p(y+\Delta)=p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y)
, where
p^{\prime}(y)=dp(y)/dy
, we have

\displaystyle\mathit{D}(p(y)\parallel p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y))=\int p(y)\ln\left(\frac{p(y)}{p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y)}\right)dy\;.

(5.42)

We then shall expand (
5.42
) with respect to
p^{\prime}
. Keeping only the first dominate term, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathit{D}(p(y)\parallel p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y))\approx\int\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(p^{\prime}(y)\right)^{2}}{p(y)}dy=\frac{1}{2}I(p(y))\;.

(5.43)

We could see that the right-hand side of (
5.43
) is nothing but the standard Fisher information.

Now we use two-parameter Kullback-Leibler divergence
[
16
]

D_{q,q^{\prime}}\left(p(y)\parallel p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y)\right)=\int p^{q}(y)\left(\ln\frac{p(y)}{p(y)+\Delta p^{\prime}(y)}\right)^{q^{\prime}}dy\;.

(5.44)

Here we do again the expansion with respect to
p^{\prime}
and we obtain the two-parameter generalisation of the Fisher information from the first dominant term

I_{a,b}(p)=\int p^{a}(y)\left(\frac{dp(y)}{dy}\right)^{b}dy\;,

(5.45)

where
a=q-q^{\prime}-1
and
b=q^{\prime}+1
with the requirements
q>0
and
q^{\prime}>0
. We find that, with a suitable choice of parameters, our whole hierarchy of Fisher information can be identified as shown in the table

6
One-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix: one random variable

With the result of the extension of the Fisher information in the case of the one estimated parameter, in this section, we will consider the case of the multi-estimated parameters:
\theta=\{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{N}\}
. Recalling the Fisher information matrix (
2.9
), we propose a one-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix

\displaystyle[I_{\lambda}(\theta)]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(e^{\lambda p(x\mid\theta)[I(\theta)]}-\mathbb{I}_{N\times N}\bigg)\;,

(6.46)

where
\mathbb{I}_{N\times N}
is the identity matrix with dimension
N\times N
. Expanding (
6.46
) with respect to the parameter
\lambda
, one obtains

\displaystyle[I_{\lambda}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(\lambda p(x\mid\theta)[I(\theta)]+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2!}p^{2}(x\mid\theta)[I(\theta)]^{2}+...\bigg)

(6.47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[I_{1}(\theta)]+\frac{\lambda}{2!}[I_{2}(\theta)]+...\;.

What we obtain now is a hierarchy of the Fisher information matrix:
\{[I_{1}(\theta)],[I_{2}(\theta)],...\}
. Obviously,
[I_{1}(\theta)]=[I(\theta)]
is nothing but the standard Fisher information matrix.

It is not difficult to see that if the number of estimated parameters is one, (
6.47
) simply reduces to

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(e^{[\partial_{\theta}\ln p]^{2}}-1\bigg)

(6.48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(\lambda p[(\partial_{\theta}\ln p)^{2}]+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2!}p^{2}[(\partial_{\theta}\ln p)^{2}]^{2}+...\bigg)\;,

where
p(x|\theta)=p
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}=\partial_{\theta}
. Of course, (
6.48
) is what we obtained in the previous section and various properties are studied.

Next, we would like to look further on the geometrical meaning of what we have in (
6.46
). For simplicity, we shall focus on the case of two estimated parameters. Then we have

\displaystyle[I_{\lambda}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(e^{\lambda p\begin{bmatrix}(\partial_{1}\ln p)^{2}&(\partial_{1}\ln p)(\partial_{2}\ln p)\\
(\partial_{2}\ln p)(\partial_{1}\ln p)&(\partial_{2}\ln p)^{2}\end{bmatrix}}-\mathbb{I}_{2\times 2}\bigg)

(6.49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\int dx\bigg(\lambda p\begin{bmatrix}(\partial_{1}\ln p)^{2}&(\partial_{1}\ln p)(\partial_{2}\ln p)\\
(\partial_{2}\ln p)(\partial_{1}\ln p)&(\partial_{2}\ln p)^{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2!}p^{2}{\begin{bmatrix}(\partial_{1}\ln p)^{2}&(\partial_{1}\ln p)(\partial_{2}\ln p)\\
(\partial_{2}\ln p)(\partial_{1}\ln p)&(\partial_{2}\ln p)^{2}\end{bmatrix}}^{2}+...\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[I_{1}(\theta)]+\frac{\lambda}{2!}[I_{2}(\theta)]+...\;,

where
\{\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial\theta_{i};i=1,2\}
.
The first one is the standard Fisher information matrix with dimension
2\times 2
given by

\displaystyle[I_{1}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{1}(\theta)_{11}&I_{1}(\theta)_{12}\\
I_{1}(\theta)_{21}&I_{1}(\theta)_{22}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\int dx\;p(\partial_{1}\ln p)^{2}&\int dx\;p(\partial_{1}\ln p)(\partial_{2}\ln p)\\
\int dx\;p(\partial_{2}\ln p)(\partial_{1}\ln p)&\int dx\;p(\partial_{2}\ln p)^{2}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(6.50)

The 2
{}^{\text{nd}}
order Fisher information matrix with the dimension
2\times 2
is given by

\displaystyle[I_{2}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}I_{2}(\theta)_{11}&I_{2}(\theta)_{12}\\
I_{2}(\theta)_{21}&I_{2}(\theta)_{22}\end{bmatrix}\;,

(6.51)

where

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)_{11}

\displaystyle=\int dx\;p^{2}\bigg((\partial_{i}\ln p)^{4}+(\partial_{i}\ln p)^{2}(\partial_{j}\ln p)^{2}\bigg)\;,

(6.52a)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)_{12}

\displaystyle=\int dx\;p^{2}\bigg((\partial_{i}\ln p)^{3}(\partial_{j}\ln p)+(\partial_{i}\ln p)(\partial_{j}\ln p)^{3}\bigg)\;,

(6.52b)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)_{21}

\displaystyle=\int dx\;p^{2}\bigg((\partial_{j}\ln p)^{3}(\partial_{i}\ln p)+(\partial_{j}\ln p)(\partial_{i}\ln p)^{3}\bigg)\;,

(6.52c)

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)_{21}

\displaystyle=\int dx\;p^{2}\bigg((\partial_{i}\ln p)^{2}(\partial_{j}\ln p)^{2}+(\partial_{j}\ln p)^{4}\bigg)\;.

(6.52d)

Working example
: To illustrate the geometrical meaning of the first two Fisher information matrices in (
6.49
), we shall again use the normal distribution

\displaystyle p(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}}\;,

(6.53)

as a prime example. We therefore define a statistical manifold
M=\{N(\mu,\sigma^{2})\}=\{p(x|\mu,\sigma)|(\mu,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
. It is not difficult to find that the log-likelihood is given by

\displaystyle\ln p(x|\theta=\{\mu,\sigma\})=\ln\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}\;.

(6.54)

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{1}}\ln p(x|\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln p(x|\theta)=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{2}}\ln p(x|\theta)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\ln p(x|\theta)=\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\;.

(6.55)

The first Fisher matrix
[I_{1}(\theta)]
: We compute first the component
[I(\theta)]_{11}

\displaystyle I_{1}(\theta)_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;p(\partial_{\mu}\ln p)^{2}

(6.56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{5}}\int dz\;z^{2}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\;,

where
z=x-\mu
is used. Since (
6.56
) is the Gaussian integral, therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{1}(\theta)_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{5}}\biggl\{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{3}\biggr\}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\;.

(6.57)

We note here that this component of the Fisher information matrix is nothing but the standard one obtained in the previous section. Next, the component
I_{1}(\theta)_{22}
can be computed as follows

\displaystyle I_{1}(\theta)_{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\;p(\partial_{\sigma}\ln p)^{2}

(6.58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int dz\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z^{4}}{\sigma^{6}}-\frac{2z^{2}}{\sigma^{4}}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{6}}\int dz\;z^{4}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{2}{\sigma^{4}}\int dz\;z^{2}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int dz\;\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\bigg\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{6}}\bigg(3\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{5}\bigg)-\frac{2}{\sigma^{4}}\bigg(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma^{3}\bigg)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\bigg)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\;.

Lastly, we find that the off-diagonal components
I_{1}(\theta)_{12}
and
I_{1}(\theta)_{21}
are the same. These two quantities vanish as follows

\displaystyle I_{1}(\theta)_{12}=I_{1}(\theta)_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\;p(\partial_{\mu}\ln p)(\partial_{\sigma}\ln p)

(6.59)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\;\bigg(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int dz\;\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\biggl\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{5}}\int dz\;z^{3}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{3}}\int dz\;z\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\biggr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;.

Finally, the standard Fisher information matrix can be expressed as

\displaystyle[I(\theta)]=[I_{1}(\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(6.60)

Of course with (
6.60
), one can write the line element

\displaystyle ds^{2}=g_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}\;,

(6.61)

where
i,j=1,2
. We now introduce a new set of variables such that

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}dx^{1}\\
dx^{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}d\mu\\
d\sigma\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}dx\\
dy\end{bmatrix}\;.

(6.62)

Then the matrix and its inverse become

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}1/y^{2}&0\\
0&1/y^{2}\end{bmatrix}\;,\;\;\;g^{ij}=\begin{bmatrix}y^{2}&0\\
0&y^{2}\end{bmatrix}\;.

(6.63)

We note that the factor
2
is dropped out at the moment for simplicity. With (
6.62
) and (
6.63
), (
6.61
) can be simply written as

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}\;.

(6.64)

Now, the distance between two point
P
and
Q
in space
M
is

\displaystyle s=\int_{P}^{Q}\mathscr{L}(y^{\prime},y;x)dx\;,

(6.65)

where
y^{\prime}=dy/dx
and
\mathscr{L}(y^{\prime},y;x)=\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}/y^{2}}
is treated as the Lagrangian. The Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}(y^{\prime},y;x)}{\partial y^{\prime}}-\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}(y^{\prime},y;x)}{\partial y}=0

(6.66)

yeilds

\displaystyle yy^{\prime\prime}+(1+y^{\prime 2})=0\;.

(6.67)

Equation (
6.67
) is the geodesic equation and the solution is given by

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{-2C_{2}x-C_{2}^{2}+C_{1}-x^{2}}

(6.68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{C_{1}-(x+C_{2})^{2}}\;,

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are constants. Therefore, (
6.68
) tells us that the shortest distance between
P
and
Q
will be on the semicircle-arc:
(x+C_{2})^{2}+y^{2}=C_{1}
, where
(C_{2},0)
is the centre and
\sqrt{C_{1}}
is the radius of the semicircle on the
M
. This manifold for the normal distribution is known as the hyperbolic half plane model or the Poincaré half plane model. In the case that we take back the factor
2
into consideration, the solution will take the from

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{-2C_{2}x-C_{2}^{2}+C_{1}-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}

(6.69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{C_{1}-(x+C_{2})^{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\;,

which means that geodesics in
M
is still half upper plane model, but the factor
\sqrt{2}
curls it to be half-ellipses
[

]
.

Next, the curvature of the manifold
M
will be computed. We then compute the first and second kind Christoffel symbols

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\gamma\alpha\beta}=[\alpha\beta,\gamma]=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial x^{\beta}}+\frac{\partial g_{\beta\gamma}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}-\frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^{\gamma}}\bigg)\;,\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\Gamma^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}=g^{\gamma\delta}\Gamma_{\gamma\alpha\beta}\;,

(6.70)

resulting in

\displaystyle\Gamma^{1}_{12}=\Gamma^{1}_{21}=\Gamma^{2}_{22}=-1/\sigma\;,\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\Gamma^{2}_{11}=1/2\sigma\;,

(6.71)

while other components are zero. Many components of the Riemann curvature tensor

\displaystyle R^{\eta}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\frac{\partial\Gamma^{\eta}_{\alpha\gamma}}{\partial x^{\beta}}-\frac{\partial\Gamma^{\eta}_{\beta\gamma}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}+\Gamma^{\tau}_{\alpha\gamma}\Gamma^{\eta}_{\tau\beta}-\Gamma^{\tau}_{\alpha\beta}\Gamma^{\eta}_{\tau\gamma}\;

(6.72)

are zero and the remaining components are

\displaystyle R^{1}_{212}=-R^{1}_{221}=-1/\sigma^{2}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;R^{2}_{121}=-R^{2}_{112}=-1/2\sigma^{2}\;.

(6.73)

The Ricci tensor can be computed by

R^{\eta}_{\alpha\eta\gamma}=R_{\alpha\gamma}\;.

Again, all remaining components are

R_{11}=-1/2\sigma^{2}
and
R_{22}=-1/\sigma^{2}\;.

Finally, we can find the Ricci scalar as

\displaystyle R=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}=\sigma^{2}(-1/2\sigma^{2})+(\sigma^{2}/2)(-1/\sigma^{2})=-1\;.

(6.74)

Then the curvature of the two-dimensional manifold
M
is

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{1}{2}R=-\frac{1}{2}\;,

(6.75)

which implies that the manifold
M
is the hyperbolic space, since its curvature is an uniform negative value
-1/2
.

The second Fisher matrix
[I_{2}(\theta)]
: Next, we investigate the geometrical nature of the space

with the matrix (

\displaystyle I_{2}(\theta)_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dx\;p^{2}\bigg((\partial_{\mu}\ln p)^{4}+(\partial_{\mu}\ln p)^{2}(\partial_{\sigma}\ln p)^{2}\bigg)

(6.76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int dx\;\bigg(\bigg(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(x-\mu)^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\bigg\{\int dz\;\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{4}+\int dz\;\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{z}{\sigma^{2}}\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{z^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigg)^{2}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\biggl\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{10}}\int dz\;z^{6}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{8}}\int dz\;z^{4}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{6}}\int dz\;z^{2}\bigg(e^{-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}z^{2}}\bigg)\biggr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\biggl\{\frac{1}{\sigma^{10}}\bigg(\frac{15\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^{7}}{2^{3}}\bigg)-\frac{1}{\sigma^{8}}\bigg(\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^{5}}{2^{2}}\bigg)+\frac{1}{\sigma^{6}}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma^{3}}{2}\bigg)\biggr\}

\displaystyle\frac{13\sqrt{\pi}}{2^{4}\pi\sigma^{5}}\;,

where we again use

. Similarly, the component

7
Conclusion

We succeed to construct the one-parameter generalised Fisher information in the case of the one random variable and one estimated parameter. The main method used to derive the one-parameter generalised Fisher information is the variational principle. We consider here the Fisher information as the action functional of the free particle Lagrangian. With the new insight of the one-parameter generalised Lagrangian
[
28
]
, one can naturally obtain the one-parameter generalised Fisher information. Furthermore, we can treat our one-parameter generalised Fisher information as the generator to obtain what we call the Fisher information hierarchy. The generalised Cramér-Rao inequality is also obtained with the help of the Hölder’s inequality. We find that our Fisher information hierarchy does not follow the addition property, expect for the standard Fisher information. The interesting point is that this non-additive property pops up when the Tsallis index is not equal to 1 as shown in table
1
. Of course, this point is quite interesting since this non-additive property has been widely discussed in the Tsallis statistic
[
12
]
.
Let us point out possibly indirect connection with the Tsallis entropy by recalling our one-parameter generalised Fisher information

I_{\lambda}[p]=\frac{4}{\lambda}\int\left[e^{\frac{\lambda}{4}\frac{p^{\prime 2}(y)}{p(y)}}-1\right]dy\;,

and Tsallis entropy

S_{q}[p]=\frac{1}{1-q}\left[\int p^{q}(y)-1\right]dy\;.

It might be seem a bit strange but these two quantities more or less similar in the sense that they both contain a parameter and under the suitable limit the standard quantities can be recovered. However, more direction connection with the entropy might be the relative entropy or the two-parameter Kullback–Leibler divergence and our whole hierarchy Fisher information can be identified with the two-parameter Fisher information with the appropriate choice of parameters.

In the case of the one random variable with multi-estimated parameters, the one-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix is obtained. The hierarchy of Fisher information matrices is computed. Geometrical interpretation for the first two Fisher information matrices in the hierarchy is investigated with well known example: the normal distribution. With the normal distribution, the statistical manifold is two-dimensional upper plane. The standard Fisher matrix gives a constant negative curvature and therefore, the space is hyperbolic. The geodesic between two points on the space will be on the ellipse. The
2^{\text{nd}}
order Fisher information matrix gives different geometrical nature on this very same space. The curvature is not constant and the geodesic equation is in the form of the hypergeometric function.

We finally note that the application of the one-parameter generalised Fisher information and one-parameter generalised Fisher information matrix is not so obvious and we shall leave this as an open problem for further investigation.
