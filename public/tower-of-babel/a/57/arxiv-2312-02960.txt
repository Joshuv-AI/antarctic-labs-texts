Bosonization of primary fields for the critical Ising model on multiply connected planar domains
Baran Bayraktaroglu, Konstantin Izyurov, Tuomas Virtanen And Christian Webb

Abstract
. We prove bosonization identities for the scaling limits of the critical Ising correlations in finitely-connected planar domains, expressing those in terms of correlations of the compactified Gaussian free field. This, in particular, yields explicit expressions for the Ising correlations in terms of domain’s period matrix, Green’s function, harmonic measures of boundary components and arcs, or alternatively, Abelian differentials on the Schottky double. Our proof is based on a limiting version of a classical identity due to D. Hejhal and J. Fay relating Szegő kernels and Abelian differentials on Riemann surfaces, and a systematic use of operator product expansions both for the Ising and the bosonic correlations.



1.
Introduction

The classical two-dimensional Ising model is one of most studied models of mathematical physics. Its importance is due to it being one of the simplest models where complicated phenomena such as phase transitions can be studied rigorously. The two-dimensional Ising model is even more special in that since the work of Onsager and Kaufman
[
40
,
35
]
it has been regarded as
exactly solvable
.

What one means by that is that relevant quantities in the model can be calculated explicitly. Of course, the precise meaning of “exact solvability” then depends on what these quantities are and what qualifies as “explicit”. The early work of Onsager and Kaufmann succeeded in calculating the free energy (per lattice site) of the model. Later on, similar or other combinatorial methods were used to calculate other thermodynamically relevant quantities, and eventually calculate the
scaling exponents
, such as the famous
\frac{1}{8}
magnetization exponent of Onsager and Yang
[
45
]
.

Beyond the scaling exponents,
correlation functions

carry even more refined information on the model; from a physicist’s point of view, their computation yields a complete understanding of the model. In the case of the Ising model, a lot of progress was made on computing these correlation functions in various regimes
[
39
,
41
]
, but the combinatorial methods were mostly limited simple geometries such as the full plane or a torus, and the critical case remained notoriously difficult. Underlying Onsager’s solution of the Ising model is the fact that it combinatorially corresponds to a
free fermionic theory
. However, the most natural observables in the theory are not fermions but spins. Expressing those in terms of fermions and analysing the resulting expressions proved to be challenging.

In a complete change of perspective, Belavin, Polyakov and Zamolodchikov
[
7
]
postulated that at criticality, the Ising model must have a scaling limit described by a conformal field theory (CFT). What’s more, they identified the relevant CFT as one of the
minimal models
. These are the simplest of CFTs, they are exactly solvable, in the sense that their correlation functions can be explicitly calculated. In a concrete application of their theory (a tiny one compared to the scope of the theory itself), Belavin, Polyakov and Zamolodchikov computed the 4-point spin correlation function in the full plane.

In fact, the CFT approach gives several different prescriptions on how to calculate the scaling limit of the Ising correlations, yielding
different
results of varying degree of “explicitness”; for a curious example of this phenomenon, see the discussion around
[
25
, Eq. 3.42–3.43]
. Part of the prediction is that the correlations must be conformally covariant, see (
1.2
) below. Moreover, the relevant CFT being a minimal model implies that they satisfy second-order partial differential equations, known as the BPZ equations. In the four-point case, these equations can be reduced by conformal covariance to a (hypergeometric) ODE. In general, such reduction is not possible, and clever methods were developed for solving BPZ equations
[
17
,
25
]
yielding solutions in terms of contour integrals. Whether BPZ equations can be always solved by those methods is still an active topic of research
[
26
,
36
,
27
,
44
]
.

Another way of computing the correlation functions, that can be applied to all minimal models, is the conformal bootstrap approach. In the case of the Ising model, the method that has eventually lead to the nicest explicit formulae for the correlations in the full plane and on a torus
[
15
]
and in the half-plane
[
9
]
is
bosonization
; see also
[
14
, Chapter 12]
. The meaning of bosonization is that the correlation functions of the critical 2d Ising model can be related to correlation functions of a free bosonic field theory. In the case of the full plane and half-plane (with homogeneous boundary conditions), the relevant theory is just a
Gaussian free field
, whose correlations are explicit in the strongest possible sense – thus so are the Ising correlations.

From the mathematical standpoint, most of the CFT arguments are still lacking rigorous grounds. In particular, how the Ising model (as a critical lattice model) leads to a minimal model of CFT is still not completely understood; see
[
31
,
3
,
4
]
for steps in this direction. The situation is somewhat better on the level of correlation functions, as techniques of discrete complex analysis allowed to prove the existence, the conformal covariance, and universality of their scaling limits. In
[
10
,
11
]
, it was proven that scaling limits of the correlations (of primary fields) in a critical Ising model on finitely connected planar domains exist and are conformally covariant. Part of the proof is a description of the limits, which, as the reader might already have anticipated, is different from the descriptions obtained by other methods. Namely, in
[
11
]
, the scaling limits of correlations are expressed in terms of solutions to Riemann boundary value problems.

In nice geometries, such as a half-plane or an annulus, these boundary value problems can be reduced to solving linear systems of equations, whose coefficients are explicit algebraic (quadratic irrational) or elliptic functions, and whose size grows linearly with the number of points considered. The spin or spin-disorder correlation functions (which is the most difficult case) are then given by exponentials of integrals of the solutions to those systems. Although in some sense, this may count as an “explicit” answer, it is not quite as explicit as the formulae in
[
15
,
9
]
. In some cases, these expressions can be then simplified – this has been done in the simply-connected case and for spin one-point function in the doubly connected case, see
[
10
, Theorem 1.2 and Appendix A2]
and
[
11
, Section 7]
; of course, in the simply connected case, the result is in agreement with the bosonization predictions, see
[
11
, Remark 7.3]
.

As explained in
[
11
, Section 7]
, once the spin-disorder correlations are computed, other correlations of primary fields can, in principle, be derived from those. However, in practice the procedure outlined in
[
11
, Section 7]
quickly gets quite messy; what’s more, there’s more than one way to write down any given correlation, which are not obviously equivalent. As an extreme example of that, the prescription for the fermionic correlation
\left\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\dots\psi_{z_{2n}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
is to consider the leading term of the asymptotics of a
4n
-point spin-disorder correlation
\left\langle\sigma_{z_{1}}\dots\sigma_{z_{2n}}\mu_{u_{1}}\dots\mu_{u_{2n}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
as
u_{i}\to z_{i}
; it is far from straightforward that the result is given simply by
\left\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\dots\psi_{z_{2n}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{Pf}\left[\frac{2}{z_{i}-z_{j}}\right]
.

The goal of this article is to provide a systematic way of writing down explicit formulae for the scaling limits of the critical Ising correlations in finitely-connected domains, as computed in
[
11
]
, by proving the bosonization prescriptions. Indeed, in Theorem
1.1
below, we show that the
squares
of correlation functions of primary fields of the Ising model on multiply connected planar domains are equal to certain correlations for the
compactified free field
. Those, in their turn, are given by explicit expressions involving the domain’s period matrix, Green’s function, harmonic measures of boundary components and boundary arcs, and derivatives thereof. To be completely precise, the expressions involve exponentials of Green’s function, polynomials in its derivatives, and multivariate theta functions of the harmonic measures (a.k.a. the Abel-Jacobi map).

We expect that the bosonization procedure can be carried out even in greater generality, such as general Riemann surfaces and even for the off-critical Ising model, but we do not discuss these generalizations further here.

Concerning mathematically rigorous analysis of bosonization in the setting of the Ising model, we mention the results on the exact bosonization on the lattice
[
20
,
21
]
, see the discussion after Theorem
1.1
, and
[
33
]
, where bosonization of the spin field of the Ising model in a simply connected domain is studied from the point of view of random generalized functions. For bosonizing free massless fermions (which are also relevant for the critical Ising model), there is of course also the classical article
[
38
]
.

1.1.
The setup and the main result

For the Ising correlations, we will follow the setup and notation of
[
11
]
. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote a bounded finitely connected planar domain
equipped with
boundary conditions
, defined by a subdivision of
\partial\Omega
into two subsets
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
and
\{\mathtt{free}\}
, each consisting of finitely many open arcs (the points
b_{1},\dots,b_{2k}\in\partial\Omega
separating them belong to neither of them). We will assume that the notation
\Omega
incorporates both the domain and the boundary conditions. See Figure
1.1

In
[
11
, Section 5.2]
, the following critical Ising correlation functions on
\Omega
were defined:

(1.1)

\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\cdot\dots\cdot\mathcal{O}_{z_{N}}\right\rangle_{\Omega},

where
z_{i}
are distinct points in
\Omega
, and
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{z_{i}},\mu_{z_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{z_{i}},\psi_{z_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{z_{i}}\}
are (labels for) the
primary fields
in the Ising model, called spin, disorder, energy, fermion, and conjugate fermion, respectively. (We will quite often drop the domain
\Omega
from the notation.) Although the term “primary field” comes from Conformal Field Theory literature, we do not claim that the correlations (
1.1
) are actually correlations in a CFT constructed in any rigorous sense; for the purpose of this paper, this is simply a collection of functions (more precisely, two-valued functions defined up to sign) of several variables in
\Omega
. The justification for calling them “correlation functions” stems from
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
, which shows that they are limits of suitably renormalized observables in the critical Ising model on fine mesh discretizations
\Omega^{\delta}
of
\Omega
, with free boundary conditions on
\{\mathtt{free}\}
and locally monochromatic ones on
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
, i.e., the spins are conditioned to be the same on all wired boundary arcs on the same connected component of
\partial\Omega
.

As indicated earlier, the main goal of this article is to relate the correlation functions (
1.1
) to correlations in a free bosonic theory, namely, that of a
compactified free field
. The latter is defined as a sum of two
independent
components,

\Phi=\varphi+\xi.

Here
\varphi
is the
Gaussian free field
in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e., the centered Gaussian field in
\Omega
with covariance given by the Green’s function
G_{\Omega}(x,y)=\left(\frac{-\Delta}{2\pi}\right)^{-1}
, see e.g.
[
43
]
and Section

. The
instanton component

\xi
is a harmonic function in
\Omega
picked at random from the countable set of all harmonic functions with the following boundary conditions:

•

\xi(x)\in\sqrt{2}\pi\mathbb{Z}
on
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
,
\xi(x)\in\sqrt{2}\pi(\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2})
on
\{\mathtt{free}\}
;

•

\xi
is constant on each wired and each free arc, moreover, it assumes the same values along
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
arcs in the same boundary component.

•

the value of
\xi
on adjacent fixed and free arcs differ by
\pm\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\pi

•

one of the boundary arcs is marked, and the value of
\xi
is fixed there (e.g., to
0
if the arc is wired or to
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\pi
if the arc is free).

The probability to choose a particular
\xi
is proportional to

(1.3)

\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}\right),

where
\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}
is the
regularized Dirichlet energy
of
\xi
, see Section
2.2
below, cf.
[
18
]
. The need for regularization comes from divergence of the Dirichlet energy at the jump points
b_{1},\dots,b_{2k}
; in particular, if there are none, then it is the usual Dirichlet energy
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}.
We note that the measure (
1.3
) and all the observables we will consider will be invariant under a shift of
\xi
by
\sqrt{2}\pi n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which is why the choice of a marked arc above is unimportant.

Let us motivate the term “compactified free field”, cf.
[
19
, Section 2]
; the arguments in this paragraph are heuristic. Assume for simplicity that there are no free arcs, and recall that the Gaussian free field is a standard Gaussian over the Sobolev space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e., it can be thought of as being sampled from all functions with probability proportional to
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\right)
. Note that integration by parts shows that the harmonic functions
\xi
are orthogonal to
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, i.e., we can write

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\varphi+\xi)|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}.

Therefore, one can think of
\Phi
as being sampled from all possible functions with the boundary conditions as above, with probability proportional to
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Phi|^{2}\right)
. Since we don’t care about shifts by integer multiples of
\sqrt{2}\pi
, we can consider instead
\Psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\exp(\sqrt{2}\mathfrak{i}\Phi)
, which is a “field” with values on the circle
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathbb{T}
, sampled with probability proportional to
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\Psi|^{2}\right)
, since
|\nabla\Psi|\equiv|\nabla\Phi|
. The boundary conditions simply become
\Psi\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
on
\partial\Omega
.

If any of the parity conditions is not satisfied, then the correlation on the left-hand side of (

Several remarks are in order. First, as is the case with the Ising correlations, we
do not
view the correlations in the right-hand side of (
1.4
) as expectations of actual random variables, or correlations in a quantum or statistical field theory constructed in any rigorous sense, although results in this direction covering some of our observables exist
[
34
,
33
]
. The issue, perhaps familiar to the reader, is that
\Phi
is too rough for the objects like
\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(z)\right)
or
|\nabla\Phi(z)|^{2}
to make sense as random variables (in the latter case, not even when averaged over a test function). Their correlations, however, can be defined by a standard procedure of first making
\Phi
into a smooth field (by convolution with a mollifier), and then passing to a limit. In some of the cases, for the limit to exist, we need to renormalize the field (by a multiplicative factor for
\sin
and
\cos
, and by an additive term for
|\nabla\Phi(z)|^{2}
); the colons
:\cdot:
indicate that this procedure has been applied. This corresponds to normal (or Wick) ordering in quantum field theory, although we will not use this connection in earnest. At the end of the day, the right-hand side of (
1.4
) is given by a concrete formula in terms of the Green’s function and harmonic measures in
\Omega
, see Definition
2.3
, and this formula is the only thing we need to know about it.

Second, there is a combinatorial explanation underlying Theorem
1.1
. A squared correlation function as in (
1.4
) can be interpreted as a correlation
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{N}}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{N}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}
, where
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}
are the corresponding observables in
two independent copies
of the Ising model. It has been shown
[
20
,
21
]
how, already at the discrete level, two such independent copies correspond to a dimer model whose height function is known to converge to the Gaussian free field; moreover, spin and disorder correlations have been shown to correspond, again on the discrete level, to so-called
electric correlators
in the height function picture. In
[
19
]
, Dubédat proved convergence of these electric correlators to the corresponding free field observables, which, combined with
[
20
]
, lead to an alternative proof of convergence of the spin and disorder Ising correlations in the case
\Omega=\mathbb{C}
, which manifestly gives (
1.4
); see also
[
21
]
. Carrying out this program more generally could also lead to a proof of Theorem
1.1
. The convergence of height functions to compactified GFF was recently proven in an independent work of Basok
[
6
]
, although it does not seem that his result fully covers the boundary conditions considered in this paper. In addition, going from convergence of height function to the convergence of all of the required observables involves a lot of work. To sum up, even if the program just described is feasible, a purely analytic proof of Theorem
1.1
, building on the already existing convergence results of
[
11
]
, is also of interest.

Third, the simplest case of (
1.4
), corresponding to
\Omega
being the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}=\psi_{z_{i}}
for all
i
, is already interesting; indeed, it states that

\left\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\dots\psi_{z_{2M}}\right\rangle^{2}_{\Omega}=(-8)^{M}\left\langle\partial\varphi(z_{1})\dots\partial\varphi(z_{2M})\right\rangle_{\Omega}.

The fermionic correlators satisfy the fermionic Wick’s rule, i.e., they are given by a Pfaffian of two-point correlations, whereas
\partial\varphi
are Gaussians and hence satisfy the bosonic Wick’s rule, i.e., they are given by what is sometimes called a Hafnian. All in all, this leads to the well known identity
[
14
, Eq. 12.53]
:

\left(\sum_{p}(-1)^{i(p)}\prod_{{a,b}\in p}\frac{1}{z_{a}-z_{b}}\right)^{2}=\sum_{p}\prod_{{a,b}\in p}\frac{1}{(z_{a}-z_{b})^{2}},

where the sums are over all pairings (i.e, partitions of
\{1,\dots,2M\}
into two-element sets) and the sign
i(p)
is the parity of the pairing (equal to the number of intersections if
p
is drawn as a planar link pattern). This identity can be derived from the Cauchy determinant; curiously, we do not use this identity, or its analogs for domains of higher connectivity, yielding an independent proof.

1.2.
Outline of the proof

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the following particular case, which we state as a separate theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Theorem
1.1
holds true in the particular case when for every
i
, either
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}=\sigma_{z_{i}}
, or
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}=\mu_{z_{i}}
. (We are still assuming that
|\{i:\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}=\sigma_{z_{i}}\}|
and
|\{i:\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}=\mu_{z_{i}}\}|
are both even.)

Given Theorem
1.3
, the derivation of Theorem
1.1
is based on the observation that other Ising fields featured therein can be obtained from
\sigma
and
\mu
by fusion, i.e., by considering the asymptotic expansions as some of the points merge. Thus, the proof boils down to checking the fusion rules, or operator product expansions, on the bosonic side, and comparing them with known results on the Ising side
[
11
, Section 6]
. We carry this out in Section
2
.

The remainder of the article is concerned with the proof of Theorem
1.3
. The main step in the proof is a derivation of the identity

(1.5)

\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{z}\psi_{w}\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}}\right)^{2}=-8\frac{\left\langle\partial\Phi(z)\partial\Phi(w)\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}}{\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}},

where each
\mathcal{O}_{v_{i}}
is either
\sigma_{v_{i}}
, or
\mu_{v_{i}}
, and we assume that the denominators are non-vanishing. The identity (
1.5
) turns out to be a limiting form of a classical identity in the theory of Riemann surfaces, due to Hejhal
[
30
]
and independently Fay
[
24
]
, that expresses squares of Szegő kernels of a compact Riemann surface in terms of Abelian differentials. The Riemann surface in question is the Schottky double of the domain
\Omega
, to which
n+k
small handles are attached, where
n
is the number of spins and disorders in
(
1.5
)
and
k
is the number of free arcs, see Section
4.1
. In Section
3
, we review the theory of Riemann surfaces needed to state the Hejhal–Fay identity. In Section
4
, we check that as we pinch the handles, the two sides of the Hejhal–Fay identity converge to the respective sides of (
1.5
), thus proving the latter. It is likely that one could adapt one of the existing proofs of Hejhal–Fay identity to give a direct proof of (
1.5
), however we found it easier to give a limiting argument.

Using the operator product expansions again, (
1.5
) implies

(1.6)

\partial_{v_{1}}\log\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{v_{1}}\log\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}.

This is the same as (
1.4
) up to a global normalization factor, which can be fixed using the operator product expansions on both sides once more. Some gimmicks are needed to work around the possible vanishing of the denominators. The route from (
1.5
) to (
1.6
) to (
1.4
) is closely parallel to the proof of convergence of the Ising correlations in
[
10
,
11
]
. We carry out this part of the proof in Section
5
.

2.
Bosonic correlation functions and operator product expansions: Proof of Theorem

In this section, we define the correlation functions appearing on the right-hand side of (

Operator Product Expansions (OPE)

[

Similar OPE were studied in the literature – see in particular
[
34
, Lecture 3]
. However, some of the fields we encounter (such as
:|\nabla\Phi|^{2}:
) differ from those studied in
[
34
]
, and the focus of
[
34
]
is on simply connected domains and the purely Gaussian case. For these reasons, we give a self-contained presentation.

2.1.
The instanton component

As we discussed in the introduction, the
instanton component

\xi
is a random harmonic function in
\Omega
, which we now describe somewhat more concretely. We assume for simplicity that the
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
part of
\partial\Omega
is non-empty, and
\xi
is normalized to be zero on one of the wired boundary arcs, and let
h_{1},\dots,h_{g}
denote the harmonic measures of other boundary components of
\Omega
, and
\hat{h}_{1},\dots,\hat{h}_{k}
denote the harmonic measures of the free boundary arcs. (If all boundary conditions are free, the only change is to normalize
\xi
to be
\frac{\sqrt{2}\pi}{2}
rather than
0
on one of the components) Any realization of
\xi
can be uniquely written as

(2.1)

\xi(z)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{g}s_{i}h_{i}(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{s}_{i}\hat{h}_{i}(z)\right)

where
\alpha=\sqrt{2}\pi
,
s_{i}=2m_{i}+N_{i}
with
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{g})\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}
and
\hat{s}=(\hat{s}_{1}\dots\hat{s}_{k})\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
, and
N_{i}=1
if the
i
-th boundary component is entirely free, and
N_{i}=0
otherwise. Note that
s
and
\hat{s}
can also be viewed as random variables.

The
Dirichlet energy

\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|^{2}
is in general infinite, because of the divergence near the points where boundary conditions have a jump. For a harmonic function
g
which is piecewise constant at the boundary except for jumps at
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
of size
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
respectively, we can define the
regularized Dirichlet energy
of
g
by subtracting the leading divergent term at each
b_{1},\dots,b_{r}
, i.e., we put

\langle\nabla g,\nabla g\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\langle\nabla g,\nabla g\rangle_{\Omega\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{r}B_{\varepsilon}(b_{i})}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}2{\alpha_{i}}^{2}\log\varepsilon\right).

Checking the existence of the limit is left to the reader. It is important to note for
g=\xi
, the last term equals
2\pi^{2}k\log\varepsilon
, i.e., it is non-random, since all the jumps are always
\pm\frac{\alpha}{2}
. We define the corresponding bilinear form by polarization:

\langle\nabla g,\nabla f\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}=\frac{1}{4}\langle\nabla(g+f),\nabla(g+f)\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}-\frac{1}{4}\langle\nabla(g-f),\nabla(g-f)\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}};

this will only involve regularizing terms for those boundary points where
both

f
and
g
have jumps. We then have

(2.2)

\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{g}s_{i}s_{j}\langle\nabla h_{i},\nabla h_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}\\
+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{g}\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_{i}\hat{s}_{j}\langle\nabla h_{i},\nabla\hat{h}_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{s}_{i}\hat{s}_{j}\langle\nabla\hat{h}_{i},\nabla\hat{h}_{j}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}\\
=:-4\pi(Q_{\Omega}(s)+B_{\Omega}(s,\hat{s})+\hat{Q}_{\Omega}(\hat{s})),

where
Q_{\Omega},\hat{Q}_{\Omega}
are quadratic forms, and
B_{\Omega}
is a bilinear form, depending on the conformal class of
\Omega
, we are able to drop the subscript “
\mathrm{reg}
” in the first two sums due to the above observation; in fact, we also have
\langle\nabla\hat{h}_{i},\nabla\hat{h}_{j}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}}=\langle\nabla\hat{h}_{i},\nabla\hat{h}_{j}\rangle_{\Omega}
for
i\neq j
.

Crucially,
Q_{\Omega}
is strictly negative definite, which in particular ensures that

Z=\sum_{\xi}\exp(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}})<\infty,

so that
\mathsf{\mathbb{P}}(\xi)=Z^{-1}\exp(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\langle\nabla\xi,\nabla\xi\rangle_{\Omega,\mathrm{reg}})
is a well defined probability measure. Moreover, we have the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

The expectation
\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})\right)

is finite for all
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, and defines a real-analytic function of its variables
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
,
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
. Moreover, the expectation can be exchanged with derivatives of all orders acting on these variables.

Proof.

Observe that by positivity of
-Q_{\Omega}
, we have
\mathsf{\mathbb{P}}(\max\{|s_{i}|\}>M)\leq\exp(-cM^{2})
for some
c>0
. Furthermore, note that by the maximum principle,
\max_{\Omega}{|\xi|}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}(\max\{|s_{i}|\}+\frac{1}{2})
. It follows that
\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})\right)
is a locally uniformly convergent series of real-analytic functions with radii of convergence uniformly bounded from below, and hence it is real-analytic.

Moreover, since
\xi
is harmonic, for each disk
D\subset\Omega
, we have
\max_{z\in D}|\partial^{n}\xi(z)|\leq C_{D,n}(\max\{|m_{i}|\}+\frac{1}{2}),
by expressing
\xi(z)
by Poisson formula in a larger disc
D^{\prime}\subset\Omega
and differentiating. Therefore, dominated convergence theorem yields the second claim.
∎

In fact, the expectations
\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})\right)
, which feature prominently in what follows, can be written quite explicitly in terms of
theta functions
, see Section
3.3
. Indeed,

(2.3)

\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})\right)=\\
=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{s,\hat{s}}\exp\left(Q_{\Omega}(s)+B_{\Omega}(s,\hat{s})+\hat{Q}_{\Omega}(\hat{s})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{g}s_{j}h_{j}(z_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{s}_{j}\hat{h}_{j}(z_{i})\right)\right),

where the partition function
Z
is given by the same sum with all
\gamma_{i}=0.
Summing first over
\hat{s}
and putting
\nu=(\frac{N_{1}}{2},\dots,\frac{N_{g}}{2})
, so that
s=2(m+\nu)
, we obtain

(2.4)

\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})\right)=\\
=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\hat{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\hat{H}(\hat{s},\{\gamma_{i}\},\{z_{i}\})\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}}\exp\left(4Q_{\Omega}(m+\nu)+2(m+\nu)\cdot v\right)=\\
=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\hat{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}}\hat{H}(\hat{s},\{\gamma_{i}\},\{z_{i}\})\cdot\theta_{Q_{\Omega}}(v|H),

where
\hat{H}(\hat{s},\{\gamma_{i}\},\{z_{i}\})=\exp\left(\hat{Q}_{\Omega}(\hat{s})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat{s}_{j}\hat{h}_{j}(z_{i})\right)
, the characteristic (see Section
3.3
for this terminology and notation) is given by
H=\binom{0}{\nu}
, and the vector
v=v(\hat{s},{\gamma_{i}},{z_{i}})
is given by its coordinates as

v_{j}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}h_{j}(z_{i})-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{16\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\hat{s}_{i}\langle\nabla h_{j},\nabla\hat{h}_{i}\rangle_{\Omega}.

We remark here that the matrix of
Q_{\Omega}
used in the definition of the theta function is in fact the period matrix
\tau
of the Schottky double
\widehat{\Omega}
of
\Omega
, and that
h_{i},\hat{h}_{j}
, and the coefficients of
B_{\Omega}
,
\hat{Q}_{\Omega}
can be in fact written in terms of Abelian integrals on
\widehat{\Omega}
, see Lemma
4.12
.

2.2.
The correlations of
\Phi
.

We start by setting up some notation and normalization conventions.

Definition 2.2
.

For
w\in\Omega
, the Green’s function
G_{\Omega}(\cdot,w)
is defined to be the unique harmonic function in
\Omega\setminus\{w\}
, vanishing at
\partial\Omega
, and satisfying the expansion

G_{\Omega}(z,w)=-\log|z-w|+O(1),\quad z\to w.

We denote
g_{\Omega}(z,w)=G_{\Omega}(z,w)+\log|z-w|
.

We recall that
G_{\Omega}(z,w)\equiv G_{\Omega}(w,z)
, and hence
g_{\Omega}(z,w)\equiv g_{\Omega}(w,z)
. The Gaussian free field
\varphi
is a random distribution in
\Omega
which is a centered Gaussian field with covariance
G_{\Omega}
, see
[
43
]
. We will actually not use any probabilistic aspects of the theory of GFF, only the structure of the related correlation functions, which we now describe.

Definition 2.3
.

Given arbitrary complex numbers
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
and distinct
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega
, the correlations of the normal-ordered exponentials of
\varphi
are defined as

(2.5)

\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\varphi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\exp\left(\sum_{i<j}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}G_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{i}}{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{i})\right)

The similar correlations of
\Phi=\varphi+\xi
are defined as

(2.6)

\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\varphi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(e^{\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})}\dots e^{\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})})

Finally, for each choice of
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}\in\{:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})}:,\partial\Phi(z_{i}),\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{i}),:|\nabla\Phi(z_{i})|^{2}:\}
, we define

(2.7)

\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}\right\rangle=\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{D}_{z_{n}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle,

where the linear operators
\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}
act by combination of differentiation and substitution of
\gamma_{i}=0
, as follows:

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}=\mathrm{Id}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})}:,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}=\partial_{\gamma_{i}}\partial_{z_{i}}|_{\gamma_{i}=0}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=\partial\Phi(z_{i}),

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}=\partial_{\gamma_{i}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{i}}|_{\gamma_{i}=0}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{i}),

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}=2\partial^{2}_{\gamma_{i}}\partial_{z_{i}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{i}}|_{\gamma_{i}=0}

\displaystyle\text{ if }\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:|\nabla\Phi(z_{i})|^{2}:.

Finally, the correlations involving
:\sin(\gamma\Phi):
or
:\cos(\gamma\Phi):
are defined using correlations of exponentials and linearity, e.g.

\left\langle:\sin(\gamma\Phi(z_{1})):\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{i}}\left(\left\langle:e^{\mathfrak{i}\gamma\Phi(z_{1})}:\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle-\left\langle:e^{-\mathfrak{i}\gamma\Phi(z_{1})}:\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle\right)

for any string
\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{2}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}
as above.

We record the following simple, but important observation:

Lemma 2.4
.

The function
\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}
is a real-analytic function of its
2n
variables as long as
z_{i}
are distinct, i.e., it is real-analytic in the region

(2.12)

\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C},\quad z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega:z_{i}\neq z_{j}\text{ if }i\neq j\}.

Moreover, the function

(2.13)

F(\gamma_{1},\dots\gamma_{n},z_{1},\dots,z_{n}):=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle

extends real-analytically to
z_{1}=z_{2}
, i.e., to the set

\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{C},\quad z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega:z_{i}\neq z_{j}\text{ if }i\neq j\text{ and }\{i,j\}\neq\{1,2\}\}.

Proof.

The real analyticity of
\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})))
is already noted above. The real analyticity of
\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\varphi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}
follows from the harmonicity, and hence real analyticity, of
G_{\Omega}
and
g_{\Omega}
in both variables, the latter one including on the diagonal. The only singular term at
z_{1}=z_{2}
comes from
\exp(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2}))
, however, we have
|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2}))=\exp(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2}))
, which is real analytic.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

In view of the above lemma, we can view the operators
\mathcal{D}_{z_{i}}
as simply picking out some of the coefficients in the Taylor expansion of
\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}
. In particular, the general correlations
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}\right\rangle
are also real analytic over (
2.12
).

Although we will not need it, Definition
2.3
can be motivated by the following regularization procedure. This is the only part of the article where we view
\Phi
as truly being a random generalized function, and in particular, that its mollification produces a random smooth function. Let
\rho:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a smooth, rotationally symmetric, compactly
supported function with
\int_{\mathbb{C}}\rho=1.
For
\varepsilon>0,
we put

\rho_{\varepsilon}(z)=\varepsilon^{-2}\rho(\frac{z}{\varepsilon})
and define the regularized bosonic field to be
\Phi_{\varepsilon}=\Phi\star\rho_{\varepsilon}=\varphi_{\varepsilon}+\xi_{\varepsilon},

where
\xi_{\varepsilon}=(\xi\star\rho_{\varepsilon})
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}=\varphi\star\rho_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, we define the regularized counterparts of the fields
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}
by

\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\varepsilon}_{z}:=\begin{cases}\partial\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z),&\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z}=\partial\Phi(z)\\
\overline{\partial}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z),&\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z}=\overline{\partial}\Phi(z)\\
\left(c_{\rho}\varepsilon\right)^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}e^{\gamma\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)},&\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z}=:e^{\gamma\Phi(z)}:\\
|\nabla\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)|^{2}-\varepsilon^{-2}d_{\rho}&\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z}=:|\nabla\Phi(z)|^{2}:\\
\end{cases}

where

c_{\rho}=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho(w_{1})\rho(w_{2})\log(|w_{1}-w_{2}|)dw_{1}dw_{2}\right);\quad d_{\rho}=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho^{2}.

Note that on one hand,
\xi_{\varepsilon}(z)=(\xi\star\rho_{\varepsilon})(z)=\xi(z)
for
\varepsilon
small enough (depending on
z
), since
\xi
is almost surely harmonic, on the other hand,
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a smooth random Gaussian field. Hence
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z}^{\varepsilon}
are actually
random variables
defined on the same probability space (they are measurable functions of
\Phi
).

Lemma 2.6
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0,
we have for any distinct
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega
,

\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\varepsilon}_{z_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\varepsilon}_{z_{n}})\to\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}\right\rangle.

Proof.

Observe that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is a centered
Gaussian field with covariance

(2.14)

G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{1}-z_{1})\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{2}-z_{2})G_{\Omega}(w_{1},w_{2})dw_{1}dw_{2}

satisfying the following two properties, as easily seen using harmonicity of
G_{\Omega}
:

•

For any
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
, one has
G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{2})=G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})

for
\varepsilon
small enough;

•

For a given
z\in\Omega
, one has
G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z,z)=g_{\Omega}(z,z)-\log(c_{\rho}\varepsilon)

for
\varepsilon
small enough.

This immediately implies the statement
of the lemma in the case
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\exp(\gamma_{i}z_{i}):

for all
i
, since for
\varepsilon
small enough (depending on
z_{i}
), writing
:\exp\gamma\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z):=(c_{\rho}\varepsilon)^{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}}e^{\gamma\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z)}
,
we have

\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\\
=(c_{\rho}\varepsilon)^{\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}^{2}}{2}}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1})+...+\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}))\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1})+...+\gamma_{n}\xi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}))\\
=(c_{\rho}\varepsilon)^{\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}+\dots+\gamma_{n}^{2}}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{i},z_{j})\right)\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+...+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n}))\\
=\exp\left(\sum_{i<j}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}G_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})+\sum_{i}\frac{\gamma_{i}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{i})\right)\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+...+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})).

Therefore, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that (in the notation of Definition
2.3
)

(2.15)

\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}^{\varepsilon}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle=\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{D}_{z_{n}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle

This is immediate if for each
i,
either
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=\partial\Phi(z_{i})
,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{i})
or
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\exp(\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})):
,
taking into account that the correlations
\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\exp(\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1})+...+\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}))

can be readily differentiated under the expectation sign, e.g., by a standard application of Borell–TIS inequality,
see
[
1
, Chapter 2]
.

Thus, it remains to handle
:|\nabla\Phi(z_{i})|^{2}:
. We start
with the computation for the case
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}=:|\nabla\Phi(z_{1})|^{2}:

and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\exp(\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})):
for
i\geq 2
and ignore
the instanton component for the moment. Recalling that
\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}=2\partial_{\gamma_{1}}^{2}\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}|_{\gamma_{1}=0}
,
a straightforward computation yields (again for small enough
\varepsilon
)

\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle=\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\varphi(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n}):\right\rangle\\
=\left(4\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\gamma_{i}\partial_{z_{1}}G(z_{1},z_{i})\right)\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\gamma_{i}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}G(z_{1},z_{i})\right)+2\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{1})\right)\\
\times\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{2}\varphi(z_{2}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n}):\right\rangle

For
z_{0},z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}
distinct,
we can calculate

\left\langle\partial_{z_{0}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{0})\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\exp\gamma_{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{2}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\\
=\left.\partial_{\gamma_{0}}\partial_{\gamma_{1}}\partial_{z_{0}}\partial_{z_{1}}\right|_{\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{1}=0}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{0}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{0}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\\
=\left(\left(\sum_{i\neq 0,1}\gamma_{i}\partial_{z_{0}}G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{0},z_{i})\right)\left(\sum_{i\neq 0,1}\gamma_{i}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{1},z_{i})\right)+\partial_{z_{0}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{0},z_{1})\right)\\
\times\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{2}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle.

If we now put
z_{0}=z_{1},
the left-hand side becomes

\frac{1}{4}\left\langle|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1})|^{2}:\exp\gamma_{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{2}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle.

For the right-hand side, compute, using (
2.14
) and
integrating by parts:

\partial_{z_{0}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}G_{\Omega,\varepsilon}(z_{0},z_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\partial_{z_{0}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{0}-z_{0})\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{1}-z_{1})G_{\Omega}(w_{0},w_{1})dw_{0}dw_{1}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{0}-z_{0})\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{1}-z_{1})\partial_{w_{0}}\partial_{\overline{w}_{1}}G_{\Omega}(w_{0},w_{1})dw_{0}dw_{1}\\
=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{0}-z_{0})\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{1}-z_{1})\left(\partial_{\overline{w}_{1}}\frac{1}{2(w_{1}-w_{0})}+\partial_{w_{0}}\partial_{\overline{w}_{1}}g_{\Omega}(w_{0},w_{1})\right)dw_{0}dw_{1}\\
=\partial_{z_{0}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{0},z_{1})+\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{0}-z_{0})\rho_{\varepsilon}(w_{0}-z_{1})dw_{0},

where in the last equality we have used
\partial_{\overline{w}_{1}}\frac{1}{(w_{1}-w_{0})}=\pi\delta(w_{1}-w_{0})

and the fact that all the derivatives of
g
are harmonic. Plugging
in
z_{0}=z_{1}
and noticing that
\partial_{z_{0}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{0},z_{1})|_{z_{0}=z_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{z_{1}}\partial_{\overline{z}_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{1}),

we conclude that

\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\\
=\left\langle\left(|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1})|^{2}-\varepsilon^{-2}d_{\rho}\right):\exp\gamma_{2}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{2}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle.

To extend this to an arbitrary string
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{2}},\dots,\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}

not including
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{i})|^{2}:
,
simply apply
\mathcal{D}_{z_{2}}\dots\mathcal{D}_{z_{n}}
to the
above identity. To allow for
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{i})|^{2}:,

notice that, renaming
z_{0}
into
z_{1}^{\prime}
, the above computation
can be re-interpreted as stating that

\mathcal{\tilde{D}}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma^{\prime}_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}^{\prime})::\exp\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\equiv 0,

where
\mathcal{\tilde{D}}_{z_{1}}
is a linear map taking as
input a real-analytic function of
z_{1}^{\prime},z_{1}\dots,z_{n},

\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{1}\dots,\gamma_{n}

and outputting a real-analytic function of
z_{1},\dots,z_{n},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{n}
;

\mathcal{\tilde{D}}_{z_{1}}
is constructed out of derivatives with
respect to
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},z_{1}^{\prime},z_{1}
, multiplication by
functions of
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
, and specializing
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}

or
\gamma_{1}
to zero and
z_{0}
to
z_{1}
. Crucially, if we
now similarly “double” each of the points
z_{i}
such that
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:|\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{i})|^{2}:
,
then each of the operators comprising
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{z_{i}}

commutes with ones comprising
\mathcal{\tilde{D}}_{z_{j}}
for
i\neq j
.
After unwinding the algebra, this observation implies (
2.15
)
whenever, for each
i
,
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:|\nabla\varphi(z_{i})|^{2}:

and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\exp(\gamma_{i}\varphi(z_{i})):.
The fully
general case readily follows
as above.

To include the instanton component, note that
\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\Phi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle

will include the term
\left(\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}\left\langle:\exp\gamma_{1}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{1}):\dots:\exp\gamma_{n}\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z_{n}):\right\rangle\right)\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}e^{\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})},

which is handled above, and the terms containing at most first-order
derivatives acting on the GFF correlations, which are readily seen
to be equal to their counterparts in the left-hand side of (
2.15
),
with no “renormalization” needed. We leave further details to the
reader.
∎

2.3.
Operator product expansions for the bosonic correlations

In this subsection, we derive the asymptotics of the correlations
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}\right\rangle
as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
. We start with the case of exponentials, on which all the rest will be based.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any fixed distinct
z_{2},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega
, any
\mathcal{O}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{3}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}
, we have the following expansion as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
, as long as
\gamma_{1}\neq-\gamma_{2}
:

(2.16)

\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\bigg(\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle\\
+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}(z_{1}-z_{2})\partial_{z_{2}}\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle\\
+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}\bigg).

If
\gamma_{1}=-\gamma_{2}
, the expansion reads

(2.17)

\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\\
\times\left(\left\langle\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+\gamma_{1}(z_{1}-z_{2})\left\langle\partial\Phi(z_{2})\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+\gamma_{1}(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})\left\langle\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}\right).

Moreover, all further Taylor coefficients of
|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle
at
z_{1}=z_{2}
vanish at
\gamma_{1}=0
, while the mixed second derivative

\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}\left(|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle\right)|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}

vanishes to the second order at
\gamma_{1}=0
.

Proof.

We first check (
2.16
) in the particular case
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})}:
for
i=3,\dots,n
, when it is a statement about the beginning of a Taylor expansion of the real-analytic function
F(z_{1})
given by (
2.13
) at
z_{1}=z_{2}
; we view
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}
as fixed parameters. We can write by definition

(2.18)

F(z_{1})=F_{1}(z_{1})\cdot F_{2}(z_{1})

where

F_{1}(z_{1})=C\exp\left(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})+\sum_{i=3}^{n}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{i}G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{i})+\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}}{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{1})\right),

F_{2}(z_{1})=\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\gamma_{2}\xi(z_{2})+\dots\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n}))),

and
C=\exp\left(\sum_{2\leq i<j\leq n}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}G_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{j})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\gamma^{2}_{i}}{2}g_{\Omega}(z_{i},z_{i})\right)
does not depend on
z_{1}
. Denote also

\displaystyle H_{1}(z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\varphi(z_{2})}::e^{\gamma_{3}\varphi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\varphi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle,

\displaystyle H_{2}(z_{2},\dots,z_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\exp((\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\xi(z_{2})+\gamma_{3}\xi(z_{3})+\dots\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n}))).

By putting
z_{1}=z_{2}
and comparing with (
2.6
), one readily checks that
F_{1}(z_{2})=H_{1}
and
F_{2}(z_{2})=H_{2}
, that is, that

F(z_{2})=H_{1}H_{2}=\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}::e^{\gamma_{3}\Phi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle.

Moreover, we have

(2.19)

\partial_{z_{1}}F(z_{1})=\left(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\partial_{z_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})+\frac{\gamma^{2}_{1}}{2}\partial_{z_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{1})+\sum_{i=3}^{n}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{i}\partial_{z_{1}}G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{i})\right)F(z_{1})\\
+F_{1}(z_{1})\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}(z_{1}),

while by definition

(2.20)

\partial_{z_{2}}(H_{1}H_{2})=\left(\frac{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})^{2}}{2}\partial_{z_{2}}g_{\Omega}(z_{2},z_{2})+\sum_{i=3}^{n}(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\gamma_{i}\partial_{z_{2}}G_{\Omega}(z_{2},z_{i})\right)H_{1}H_{2}\\
+H_{1}\partial_{z_{2}}H_{2}.

Note that
g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})
is a real-analytic function which is symmetric in
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
, therefore
\partial_{z_{2}}g_{\Omega}(z_{2},z_{2})=2\partial_{z_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}
. Also, we have

(2.21)

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\partial\xi(z_{2})e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\xi(z_{2})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})}),

(2.22)

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{2}}H_{2}

\displaystyle=(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\partial\xi(z_{2})e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\xi(z_{2})+\dots+\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})}).

Putting these observations together and comparing (
2.19
) with (
2.20
), we conclude

\partial_{z_{1}}F(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}=\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}::e^{\gamma_{3}\Phi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle.

Using that
\overline{\partial_{z_{1}}F(z_{1})}=\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\overline{F(z_{1})}
, we see that the same identity holds with
\overline{\partial}_{{z}_{1}}
and
\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}
instead of
\partial_{z_{1}}
and
\partial_{z_{2}}
respectively. This proves (
2.16
) in the case
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(z_{i})}:
for
i=3,\dots,n
. The general case follows immediately from the observation that
\mathcal{D}_{z_{3}},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{z_{n}}
commute both with multiplication by
|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}
and taking derivatives with respect to
z_{1}
. For
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\cos(\gamma\Phi(z_{i})):
or
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{i}}=:\sin(\gamma\Phi(z_{i})):
,
i=3,\dots,n
, we simply use linearity.

The expansion (
2.17
) follows from (
2.16
) by taking the limit
\gamma_{1}\to-\gamma_{2}
and recalling the definition of correlations involving
\partial\Phi
and
\overline{\partial}\Phi
; again this is fully justified since
F
(and
\mathcal{D}_{z_{3}}\dots\mathcal{D}_{z_{n}}F
) is a real-analytic function in all its parameters, particularly its Taylor coefficients with respect to
z_{1}
are also real-analytic functions of
\gamma_{1}
.

For the final claim, since we are talking about convergent Taylor series, we can first plug
\gamma_{1}=0
and then evaluate the derivatives with respect to
z_{1}
; they vanish identically since the function does not depend on
z_{1}
when
\gamma_{1}=0
. The stronger assertion about the mixed second derivative requires more work. We go back to (
2.19
) and apply
\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}
to both sides, taking into account that both
G_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})
and
g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{2})
are harmonic and therefore annihilated by
\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}
. This gives

\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F(z_{1})=\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}}{2}\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}g_{\Omega}(z_{1},z_{1})F_{1}(z_{1})F_{2}(z_{1})+\frac{\partial_{z_{1}}F_{1}(z_{1})}{F_{1}(z_{1})}(\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}F_{1}(z_{1}))F_{2}(z_{1})\\
+\partial_{z_{1}}F_{1}(z_{1})\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}F_{2}(z_{1})+\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}F_{1}(z_{1})\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}(z_{1})+F_{1}(z_{1})\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}(z_{1}).

As we have seen above, each of the first derivatives in the above expression (at
z_{1}=z_{2}
) vanish to the first order at
\gamma_{1}=0
, and the same is true of the
\frac{\partial_{z_{1}}F_{1}}{F_{1}}
(which is of course just the prefactor in (
2.19
)). It remains to treat
\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}
. We have

\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F_{2}=\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})))\\
=\gamma_{1}^{2}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\partial_{z_{1}}\xi(z_{1})\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\xi(z_{1})\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n})))\\
+\gamma_{1}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}(\partial_{z_{1}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\xi(z_{1})\exp(\gamma_{1}\xi(z_{1})+\dots\gamma_{n}\xi(z_{n}))),

but the last term vanishes because
\xi
is almost surely harmonic. This concludes the proof that
\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F=\gamma_{1}^{2}R(z_{1}),
where
R(z_{1})
is real-analytic at
z_{1}=z_{2}
and
\gamma_{1}=0
, i.e., we have proven the required assertion in the case
\mathcal{O}=:e^{\gamma_{3}\Phi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:
. It is then extended to the general case exactly as above.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

For a reader familiar with Conformal Field Theory, let us mention that in general, the
n
-th order terms in the expansion (
2.16
) can be expressed in terms of Virasoro descendants of
:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:
. In particular, the lemma above handles the case of the level 2 descendant
\overline{L}_{-1}L_{-1}:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:
; the reader can check that explicitly,

\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}F(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}=\frac{\gamma_{1}^{2}}{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})^{2}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}\left\langle:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}::e^{\gamma_{3}\Phi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:\right\rangle.

The identities (
2.16
)–(
2.17
) are examples of what is known as
Operator Product Expansions
(OPE). It will be convenient to abbreviate the expansions of this type by suppressing
\left\langle\,\cdot\,\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle
, e.g. with this convention (
2.16
) can be written as

(2.23)

:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\bigg(:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:\\
+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}(z_{1}-z_{2})\partial_{z_{2}}:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:\\
+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}\bigg).

The meaning of such identity is that they become valid expansions if we take a correlation of both sides with any string
\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{3}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{n}}
, with
z_{2},\dots,z_{n}
distinct, and use linearity to make sense of the resulting expression (in particular, taking derivatives outside the correlations).

Lemma 2.9
.

We have the following expansions as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
:

(2.24)

\partial\Phi(z_{1}):e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:=-\frac{\gamma_{2}}{2(z_{1}-z_{2})}:e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:+\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}:e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:+O(z_{1}-z_{2})

(2.25)

\partial\Phi(z_{1})\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})=\frac{1}{4}:|\nabla\Phi(z_{2})|^{2}:+O(z_{1}-z_{2}).

Proof.

The expansion (
2.24
) is obtained by applying
\mathcal{D}_{z_{1}}=\partial_{\gamma_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}|_{\gamma_{1}=0}
to (
2.16
); since the right-hand side (after the prefactor) is a convergent power series, it is clear that we can just differentiate term by term. We have, taking into account the “moreover” claim of Lemma
2.7
,

(2.26)

\partial_{z_{1}}:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:=\\
-\frac{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}{2(z_{1}-z_{2})}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left(:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:+\gamma_{1}\cdot(\dots)\right)\\
+|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}:e^{(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\Phi(z_{2})}:+O(z_{1}-z_{2})\right).

Taking the derivative with respect to
\gamma_{1}
at
\gamma_{1}=0
(which will kill the term abbreviated as
(\dots)
in the second line above) yields (
2.24
). In fact, we have a bit more information: inside a correlation with
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
, the
O(z_{1}-z_{2})
error term in (
2.26
) is given by

2(z_{1}-z_{2})\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}\hat{F}(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}+2(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z_{2}})\partial_{z_{1}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{1}}\hat{F}(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2}}+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2},

where
\hat{F}(z_{1})=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\Phi(z_{1})}::e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle
. Lemma
2.7
guarantees that when taking the derivative with respect to
\gamma_{1}
at
\gamma_{1}=0
this becomes

2(z_{1}-z_{2})\partial_{\gamma_{1}}\partial_{z_{1}}^{2}\hat{F}(z_{1})|_{z_{1}=z_{2},\gamma_{1}=0}+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2},

which is a refined form of the (
2.24
) error term. Crucially, there is no
(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})
part.

To prove (
2.25
) we apply
\mathcal{D}_{z_{2}}=\partial_{\gamma_{2}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}|_{\gamma_{2}=0}
to (
2.24
), with this refined error term. The first term vanishes and we get, as required,

(2.27)

\partial\Phi(z_{1})\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})=\partial_{\gamma_{2}}\left.\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}:e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\right)\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}+O(z_{1}-z_{2})\\
=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{\gamma_{2}}\left.\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}:e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}+O(z_{1}-z_{2}),

where in the last equality we have used the observation that
\overline{\partial}_{z_{2}}\partial_{z_{2}}\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{2}\Phi(z_{2})}:\mathcal{O}\right\rangle
vanishes to the second order at
\gamma_{2}=0
, which can be readily checked from the definition when
\mathcal{O}=:e^{\gamma_{3}\Phi(z_{3})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{n}\Phi(z_{n})}:
and then extended to the general case in the familiar way.
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

For all

2.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
given Theorem
1.3
.

We are ready to derive Theorem
1.1
from Theorem
1.3
.

Proof.

We start by extending the identity (
1.4
) to the case when some of the
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}
are
\psi_{z_{i}}
or
\psi_{z_{i}}^{\star}
, by induction on the number of such
z_{i}
. Assume on the one hand that we already know the identity

\left\langle\mu_{z_{1}}\sigma_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=\left\langle\sqrt{2}:\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(z_{1})\right):\sqrt{2}:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(z_{2})\right):\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle.

We compute the leading term of the asymptotics of both sides as
z_{1}\to z_{2}
. The asymptotics of the right-hand side is given by (
2.30
), specifically

(2.34)

\left\langle\sqrt{2}:\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(z_{1})\right):\sqrt{2}:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(z_{2})\right):\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle\\
=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle:\sin(\sqrt{2}\Phi(z_{2})):\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\partial\Phi(z_{2})\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle\\
+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\frac{\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}.

On the other hand
[
11
, Eq. (6.11) together with (6.10) and (5.8)]
gives

(2.35)

\left\langle\mu_{z_{1}}\sigma_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathfrak{i}\pi}{4}}}{2}\frac{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\psi_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+\frac{e^{\frac{\mathfrak{i}\pi}{4}}}{2}\frac{(\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\psi^{\star}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle+O(z_{1}-z_{2})\right)^{2}\\
=\frac{1}{2}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle\psi_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi^{\star}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle-\frac{\mathfrak{i}}{4}\frac{z_{1}-z_{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\psi_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{i}}{4}\frac{\overline{z}_{1}-\overline{z}_{2}}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left\langle\psi^{\star}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}+O(z_{1}-z_{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Comparing the coefficients yields

\left\langle\psi_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=\mathfrak{i}2\sqrt{2}\left\langle\partial\Phi(z_{2})\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle\psi^{\star}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=-\mathfrak{i}2\sqrt{2}\left\langle\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle,

that is, we get the bosonization identity with one more fermion than the string
\mathcal{O}
already contained, and with the parity conditions preserved. This enables the inductive argument.

In a similar manner we can extend the result to energies, by passing to the limit
z_{1}\to z_{2}
in the identity
\left\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\psi^{\star}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=8\left\langle\partial\Phi(z_{1})\overline{\partial}\Phi(z_{2})\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle
, using (
2.25
) and comparing with
[
11
, Eq. 6.3]
yields

\left\langle\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{z_{2}}\mathcal{O}\right\rangle^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle:|\nabla\Phi(z_{2})|^{2}:\hat{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle.

∎

2.5.
Extensions

In this section, we describe an extension of Theorem
1.1
to the boundary conditions
\mathcal{B}
defined by dividing the boundary into finitely many arcs, with boundary conditions on each arc set to
\{\mathtt{plus}\}
,
\left\{\mathtt{minus}\right\}
, or
\{\mathtt{free}\}
. This is the setup of
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
, where the scaling limits
\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}
of such correlations are computed, where
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{z_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{z_{i}}\}
for all
i
. Following
[
11
]
, we focus on correlation functions involving spin and energy only, although as discussed after
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
, it is in principle possible to include fermions and disorders, as well as boundary fields. We state the result somewhat loosely:

Proposition 2.12
.

The (continuous) correlation functions
\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}
, where
\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{z_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{z_{i}}\}
, featuring in
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]

Proof.

The proof is based on the fact that
\langle\mathcal{\mathcal{O}}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}
can be expressed in terms of the correlations
\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}
with locally monochromatic boundary condtions,
\mathcal{O}_{w_{i}}\in\{\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{w_{i}},\psi_{w_{i}},\sigma_{w_{i}}\}
by a series of passages to a limit, see
[
11
, Section 5]
. We now recall the procedure.

First, one defines the correlation
\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}
, still with the locally monochromatic boundary conditions, where some spins are allowed to be on the
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
part of the the boundary, see
[
11
, Proposition 5.4, Remark 5.5, Definition 5.6, Definition 5.7, Definition 5.9, Remark 5.11, and Definition 5.17]
. The bottom line of these definitions is that if, say,
w_{1},\dots,w_{l}\in\Omega
and
w_{l+1},\dots,w_{n}\in\{\mathtt{wired}\}
, then

(2.36)

\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{l}}\sigma_{w_{l+1}}\dots\sigma_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}\\
=\lim_{\hat{w}_{i}\to w_{i}}\prod_{i}2^{-\frac{1}{4}}\mathrm{crad}_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i})^{\frac{1}{8}}\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\sigma_{\hat{w}_{l+1}}\dots\sigma_{\hat{w}_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}.

(The factor of
2^{-\frac{1}{4}}
is missing in
[
11
]
by mistake.) The conformal radius in a multiply connected domain
\Omega
is defined by replacing it with a simply-connected domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, removing all boundary components except the one containing
w_{i}
; it is easy to see that then as
\hat{w}_{i}\to w_{i}
,
\log\text{crad}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\hat{w}_{i})=g_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\hat{w}_{i},\hat{w}_{i})=g_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i},\hat{w}_{i})+o(1)
. We note that these correlations are a scaling limit of the discrete correlations with locally monochromatic boundary conditions, and they in fact do not depend on the positions of
w_{l+1},\dots,w_{N}
within the
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
part of their boundary components. This is valid for arbitrary fields
\mathcal{O}_{w_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{w_{i}},\mu_{w_{i}},\psi_{w_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{w_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{w_{i}}\}
, but we do require
|\{i\leq l:\mathcal{O}_{w_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{w_{i}},\psi_{w_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{w_{i}}\}\}|+N-l
to be even, else the correlation is defined to be zero.

The next step is to define the correlations with
auxiliary boundary conditions

\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
, see
[
11
, Section 5]
. These are specified, in addition to the partition of
\partial\Omega
into
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
and
\{\mathtt{free}\}
, by a choice of distinct points
b_{1},...,b_{q}
on
\partial\Omega
, such that there’s an even number of them on each connected component of
\partial\Omega
, and the points either belong to
\{\mathtt{wired}\}
, or are endpoints of free arcs. One then defines (see
[
11
, Definition 5.24]
and the discussion thereafter)

(2.37)

\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}:=\lim_{z_{1}\to b_{1},...,z_{q}\to b_{q}}\frac{\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\cdots\psi_{z_{q}}\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}}{\langle\psi_{z_{1}}\cdots\psi_{z_{q}}\rangle_{\Omega}}.

In the discrete picture, this corresponds to the situation where the boundary spins are still random and “locally monochromatic” away from
b_{i}
, but forced to change sign at each
b_{i}\in\{\mathtt{wired}\}
and across each free arc that has exactly one of its endpoints in
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{q}\}
.

Finally, given a boundary condition
\mathcal{B}
specified by a partition of
\partial\Omega
into
\{\mathtt{plus}\}
,
\left\{\mathtt{minus}\right\}
and
\{\mathtt{free}\}
arcs, we define the auxilliary boundary condition
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
by including into
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{q}\}
all points separating a
\{\mathtt{plus}\}
arc from a
\left\{\mathtt{minus}\right\}
arc, and one endpoint of each free arc separating
\{\mathtt{plus}\}
from
\left\{\mathtt{minus}\right\}
. Further, for each boundary component that has at least one
\{\mathtt{plus}\}
or
\left\{\mathtt{minus}\right\}
arc, we pick a point on one of those arcs; denote by
w_{1},\dots,w_{d}
the resulting collection of points. Note that to recover
\mathcal{B}
from
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
, we need to specify the spin at each
w_{i}
. Define
\tau\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
as follows:
\tau_{j}=1
if the arc
j
is a subset of
\{
plus
\}
and
\tau_{j}=-1
if the arc
j
is a subset of
\{
minus
\}
. We then define

(2.38)

\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}=\frac{\sum_{S\subset\{1,...,d\}}\alpha_{S}(\tau)\langle\prod_{j\in S}\sigma_{w_{j}}\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}}{\sum_{S\subset\{1,...,d\}}\alpha_{S}(\tau)\langle\prod_{j\in S}\sigma_{w_{j}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}},

where the coefficients
\alpha_{S}(\tau)
are defined as follows. Consider the function
I_{\tau}:\{\pm 1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

I_{\tau}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}1,&\sigma=\tau,\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

and let
\alpha_{S}(\tau)
be its Fourier–Walsh coefficients (see also
[
11
, (2.18)]
), i.e., the unique coefficients
(\alpha_{S}(\tau))_{S\subset\{1,...,d\}}
such that for each
\sigma\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
,

I_{\tau}(\sigma)=\sum_{S\subset\{1,...,d\}}\alpha_{S}(\tau)\prod_{j\in S}\sigma_{j}.

By
[
11
, Lemma 5.25]
, the correlation (
2.38
) is well defined, i.e., the denominator does not vanish. The fact that
\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}
thus defined do correspond to a scaling limit of correlation functions of the discrete Ising model with the
+/-/
free
boundary conditions is the content of
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
. Note that in the left-hand side of (
2.38
), we allow arbitrary parity of the spin insertions – some terms simply vanish if they contain an odd amount of bulk and boundary spins in total.

Starting with the bosonization identity

\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}^{2}=\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega}

and applying all the above step, we can express
\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}
in terms of the (limits of) bosonic correlations.
∎

Let us remark that in the simply-connected case, the third step of the above procedure is trivial, and boundary conditions
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
are essentially equivalent to
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, let
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\Omega
. If
|\{i:\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{z_{i}},\psi_{z_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{z_{i}}\}\}|
is even, then
\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}=\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}},

by spin flip symmetry. If
|\{i:\mathcal{O}_{z_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{z_{i}},\psi_{z_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{z_{i}}\}\}|
is odd, we need to insert one boundary spin, say at
w_{1}
, and so, we have
d=1
,
I_{\tau}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}(1+\tau_{1}\sigma_{1})=\alpha_{\emptyset}(\tau_{1})+\alpha_{\{1\}}(\tau_{1})\sigma_{1}
, where
\tau_{1}=\pm 1
is the boundary condition at
w_{1}
, and

\langle\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\mathcal{B}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot 0+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\tau_{1}\langle\sigma_{w_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 0}=\tau_{1}\langle\sigma_{w_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{z_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{z_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}}.

Coming back the the general case, let us give a more concrete description of the first two steps in the above procedure; we do not expect the third step to get any more concrete in general.

For the first step, we have the following interesting extension of the main theorem:

Proposition 2.13
.

We have the following bosonization prescription for
w_{1},\dots w_{l}\in\Omega
and
w_{l+1},\dots,w_{N}\in\{\mathtt{wired}\}
:

\langle\mathcal{O}_{w_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{w_{l}}\sigma_{w_{l+1}}\dots\sigma_{w_{N}}\rangle^{2}_{\Omega}=\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{N}}\rangle_{\Omega},

where for
i=1,\dots,l
, each pair
\mathcal{O}_{w_{i}},\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}
is as in Theorem
1.1
while for
i=l+1,\dots,N
, we have
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}=(-1)^{\frac{\Phi(w_{i})}{\sqrt{2}\pi}}=(-1)^{\frac{\xi(w_{i})}{\sqrt{2}\pi}}
.

Proof.

We will square (
2.36
) and apply the bosonization formula of Theorem
1.1
inside the limit on the right-hand side. To compute the limit, we first study the limiting behavior of correlations of exponentials. Let
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\varphi(w_{i})}:
for
i=1,\dots,l,
and
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})}:
for
i=l+1,\dots,N
, then

(2.39)

\lim_{\hat{w_{i}}\to w_{i}}\prod^{N}_{i=l+1}\text{crad}_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i})^{-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{i}}{2}}\left\langle\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{1}}\dots\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{l}}\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{l+1}}\dots\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{N}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}=\left\langle\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{1}}\dots\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{l}}\right\rangle_{\Omega},

using the definition (
2.5
) and observing that
g_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i},\hat{w}_{i})=\log\text{crad}_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i})+o(1)
while
G_{\Omega}(w_{i},\hat{w}_{j})\to 0
(respectively,
G_{\Omega}(\hat{w}_{i},\hat{w}_{j})\to 0
) for each
i=1,\dots,l
and
j=l+1,\dots,N
(respectively,
l+l\leq i\neq j\leq N
). We specialize this to
\gamma_{l+1}=\dots=\gamma_{N}=\mathfrak{i}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},
and observe that by linearity, (
2.39
) also holds when
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{i}}=:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):
for all
i=l+1,\dots,N
, while if some of them are
:\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):
and others
:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):
, the limit vanishes.

Now, let’s include the instanton component: let
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(w_{i})}:
for
i=1,\dots,l,
and
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}=:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):
for
i=l+1,\dots,N
; we then just expand each cosine into exponentials, pass to the limit term by term, and then regroup terms. To help with the bookkeeping, we note that inside any correlation,

(2.40)

:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Phi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):=:\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\xi(\hat{w}_{i})\right)\\
-:\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\varphi(\hat{w}_{i})\right):\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\xi(\hat{w}_{i})\right),

since by definition, the usual Euler identities are valid for normal ordered sines and cosines. Plugging (
2.40
) into (
2.39
) and expanding, by the above observation, only the term with all cosines survives in the limit, i.e., we get

\lim_{\hat{w_{i}}\to w_{i}}\prod^{N}_{i=l+1}\text{crad}_{\Omega}(w_{i})^{-\frac{\gamma^{2}_{i}}{2}}\left\langle\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{1}}\dots\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{l}}\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{l+1}}\dots\check{\mathcal{O}}_{\hat{w}_{N}}\right\rangle_{\Omega}\\
=\left\langle:e^{\gamma_{1}\varphi(w_{1})}:\dots:e^{\gamma_{1}\varphi(w_{l})}:\right\rangle_{\Omega}\mathsf{\mathbb{E}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{l}e^{\gamma_{i}\xi(w_{i})}\prod_{i=l+1}^{N}\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\xi(w_{i})\right)\right).

It remains to note that
\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\xi(w_{i})\right)=(-1)^{\frac{\xi(w_{i})}{\sqrt{2}\pi}}
since
\xi\in\sqrt{2}\pi\mathbb{Z}
on the fixed part of the boundary. The same result holds if we replace
\check{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}=:e^{\gamma_{i}\Phi(w_{i})}:
with the bosonic counterparts of
\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{w_{i}}
of any fields
\mathcal{O}_{w_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{w_{i}},\mu_{w_{i}},\psi_{w_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{w_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{w_{i}}\}

\sigma_{w_{i}},\mu_{w_{i}}
) or by checking that the above limiting procedure commutes with corresponding
\mathcal{D}_{w_{i}}
(for
\psi_{w_{i}},\psi^{\star}_{w_{i}},\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{w_{i}}

3.
Hejhal–Fay identity on compact Riemann surfaces

Our proof of Theorem
1.3
is based on an identity, discovered by Hejhal
[
30
]
and independently by Fay
[
24
]
, which expresses the square of a Szegő kernel on a compact Riemann surface in terms of Abelian differentials. In this section we review concepts needed to state this identity (Theorem
3.13
): Riemann surfaces, Abelian differentials, spinors, Szegő kernels, and theta functions. No material here is original; the only bit for which we have not found an explicit reference in the literature is Lemma
3.14
.

3.1.
Riemann surfaces and Abelian differentials.

We start with the basics of the theory of Riemann surfaces that we will need in what follows. The material is standard and can be found in many sources, see e.g.
[
2
,
23
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A Riemann surface
\mathbb{\mathcal{M}}
is a connected two (real) dimensional smooth manifold equipped with an atlas
(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha},z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
, where

•

the
charts

(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
form an open cover of
\mathbb{\mathcal{M}}
,

•

the
local coordinate map

z_{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism between
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}
and some open subset of
\mathbb{C}
,

•

the
transition functions

\varphi_{\alpha,\beta}=z_{\beta}\circ z_{\alpha}^{-1}:z_{\alpha}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\cap\mathcal{U}_{\beta})\to z_{\beta}(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\cap\mathcal{U}_{\beta})
are holomorphic functions when
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha}\cap\mathcal{U}_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
.

Two atlases are equivalent if their union is also an atlas, and Riemann surfaces with equivalent atlases are not distinguished.

A Riemann surface is automatically oriented. The
genus
of a compact Riemann surface is defined to be 1/2 the rank of its first homology group
H_{1}(\mathbb{\mathcal{M}},\mathbb{Z})
, see e.g.
[
23
, Chapter I.2.5]
). Topologically, a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
is a sphere with
g
handles attached. A
Torelli marking
of a Riemann surface is a choice of generators
\{A_{j},B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{g}
of
H_{1}(\mathbb{\mathcal{M}},\mathbb{Z})
so that their intersection numbers (see
[
23

Spinors and Szegő kernels on a Riemann surface

To give an elementary introduction to spinors, or half-order differentials, we will introduce a class of finite atlases suited for our purpose. Given a triangulation

Proof.

5.1.
Matching the logarithmic derivatives

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0
and
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0

Proof.

We will use fusion rules on (
1.5
). We start with the Ising side.

Let’s assume we have a spin at
v_{1}
, i.e.
\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}=\sigma_{v_{1}}
. By properly normalizing the fusion rules from
[
11
, Thm. 6.2]

(5.1)

\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{z}\psi_{w}\sigma_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}\right)^{2}=\frac{\overline{\lambda}^{2}}{z-v_{1}}\left(\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{w}\mu_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}\right)^{2}+O(z-v_{1})\right)

where
\lambda=\exp(-\mathfrak{i}\pi/4)
. If we again use fusion rules, we get

(5.2)

\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{w}\mu_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}\right)^{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{w-v_{1}}\big(1+8\partial_{v_{1}}\log\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle(w-v_{1})+\\
+O(w-v_{1})^{2}\big)

The equations are similar if we instead put a disorder at
v_{1}
. We will just have
\overline{\lambda}
instead of
\lambda
, and
\mu_{v_{1}}
instead of
\sigma_{v_{1}}
on the right side. Thus, if we collect all of the fusion rules, we obtain

(5.3)

\partial_{v_{1}}\log\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle=\lim_{w\rightarrow v_{1}}\left(\lim_{z\rightarrow v_{1}}\left(\frac{\left\langle\psi_{z}\psi_{w}\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}\right)^{2}\frac{z-v_{1}}{8}-\frac{1}{8(w-v_{1})}\right)

And thus, we can plug in (
1.5
) to obtain

(5.4)

\partial_{v_{1}}\log\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle=-\lim_{w\rightarrow v_{1}}\left(\lim_{z\rightarrow v_{1}}\frac{\left\langle\partial\Phi(z)\partial\Phi(w)\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}\left(z-v_{1}\right)+\frac{1}{8(w-v_{1})}\right)

Now, for the GFF side we can use (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) to obtain

(5.5)

\lim_{z\rightarrow v_{1}}\frac{\left\langle\partial\Phi(z)\partial\Phi(w)\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}(z-v_{1})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\frac{\left\langle\partial\Phi(w)\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}_{v_{1}}\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{2}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle}

-:\cos(\Phi(v_{1})/\sqrt{2}):&\text{ if }\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}=:\sin(\Phi(v_{1})/\sqrt{2}):.\end{cases}

2.28

5.2.
Concluding the proof

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

As a first step, we handle the case
\mathcal{O}_{v_{i}}=\sigma_{v_{i}}
all
i
. Indeed, we then always have
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle>0
, as the cosines expand into exponentials with positive coefficients. Moreover, we also have
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle>0
, as defined in
[
11
, Section 5.1]
; note that
n
is even. Therefore, (
1.6
) is valid for
all
configurations of distinct points
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. Integrating and taking exponentials implies

(5.8)

\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle^{2}=C_{\Omega,n}\cdot\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle

for a constant

independent of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. The value
C_{\Omega,n}=1
can now be fixed by induction, letting
v_{1}\to v_{2}
and applying
the OPEs (
2.31
) and
[
11
, Theorem 6.3]
.

Notice that in particular, this implies that
\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\dots\sigma_{v_{n}}\right\rangle
is a real analytic function of (distinct) points
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, since, as noted in Section
2.2
,
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle
is always manifestly real analytic. Furthermore, we have (
1.5
); thus using
[
11
, Theorem 6.2]
, we can write

\left\langle\mu_{v_{1}}\mu_{v_{2}}\sigma_{v_{3}}\dots\sigma_{v_{n}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\sigma_{v_{1}}\dots\sigma_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\oint\oint\left(\frac{R(z,w,v_{1},\dots,v_{n})}{(z-v_{1})(w-v_{2})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,dzdw,

where

is a real-analytic function of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
, and the small contours of integration around
v_{1,2}
can be chosen to avoid its zeros. Therefore,
\left\langle\mu_{v_{1}}\mu_{v_{2}}\sigma_{v_{3}}\dots\sigma_{v_{n}}\right\rangle
is a real-analytic function of
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. Using the Pfaffian idenity
[
11
, Eq 5.19]
, we conclude that
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle
,
\mathcal{O}_{v_{i}}\in\{\sigma_{v_{i}},\mu_{v_{i}}\}
is always real analytic.

We are ready to conclude the proof in the general case by induction on
n
. Indeed, assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}=\mu_{v_{1}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{v_{2}}=\mu_{v_{2}}
. Fix
v_{3},\dots,v_{n}
such that
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{3}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0
, then by induction hypothesis also
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{3}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0
. Applying once again the OPE (
2.32
) and
[
11
, Theorem 6.3]
, we see that
\left\langle\mathcal{O}_{v_{1}}\dots\mathcal{O}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0
and
\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{1}}\dots\hat{\mathcal{O}}_{v_{n}}\right\rangle\neq 0

. In particular, (

∎
