Structure preserving numerical methods for the ideal compressible MHD system
Tuan Anh Dao, Murtazo Nazarov, Ignacio Tomas

Abstract
We introduce a novel structure-preserving method in order to approximate the compressible ideal Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) equations. This technique addresses the MHD equations using a non-divergence formulation, where the contributions of the magnetic field to the momentum and total mechanical energy are treated as source terms. Our approach uses the Marchuk-Strang splitting technique and involves three distinct components: a compressible Euler solver, a source-system solver, and an update procedure for the total mechanical energy. The scheme allows for significant freedom on the choice of Euler’s equation solver, while the magnetic field is discretized using a curl-conforming finite element space, yielding exact preservation of the involution constraints. We prove that the method preserves invariant domain properties, including positivity of density, positivity of internal energy, and the minimum principle of the specific entropy. If the scheme used to solve Euler’s equation conserves total energy, then the resulting MHD scheme can be proven to preserve total energy. Similarly, if the scheme used to solve Euler’s equation is entropy-stable, then the resulting MHD scheme is entropy stable as well. In our approach, the CFL condition does not depend on magnetosonic wave-speeds, but only on the usual maximum wavespeed from Euler’s system. To validate the effectiveness of our method, we solve a variety of ideal MHD problems, showing that the method is capable of delivering high-order accuracy in space for smooth problems, while also offering unconditional robustness in the shock hydrodynamics regime as well.

Introduction

Magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) equations model the dynamics of plasma, which is an
ionizied gas at high temperatures. The ideal MHD equations combines: the fluid
dynamics equations description of Euler equations, with zero-permittivity limit
of Maxwell’s equations and Ohm’s law closure. The model considers both the
movement of the conductive fluid and its interaction with magnetic fields. The
MHD equations are widely used in astrophysics applications as well as in
nuclear fusion research, where it is used to study and control instabilities in
the plasma confinement.

Solutions to the MHD system contain contact, shock, and rarefaction
waves. In addition, the interaction of fluid and magnetic fields at very high
temperatures pose additional challenges for MHD simulations. Despite these
difficulties, numerical solutions of the MHD system are vital to predict
phenomena in various scientific fields such as plasma physics and
astrophysics. Furthermore, when performing numerical simulations of the MHD
system, it is crucial to ensure the preservation of essential
structure of the solution, such as positivity properties, conservation of total
energy, and involution constraints.

Various schemes that retain several of these properties, in particular for the
compressible Euler equations, are published in existing literature. For
instance, the works of
[
32
,
42
,
14
]
, along with
references provided therein, represent just a subset of the comprehensive
research dedicated to achieving positivity-preserving approximations for the
compressible Euler equations, using finite volume, discontinuous Galerkin, and
finite element methods. Unfortunately, direct extension of these methods for
the MHD system is not straighforward due to the additional induction equation
for the magnetic field and corresponding to the magnetic stress/force. In
particular, the standard MHD model in divergence form is only valid if

\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{B}}\equiv 0
at all times. A slight violation of the
divergence-free condition can lead to negative internal energy, which will
cause the numerical simulation to fail catastrophically, see e.g.,
[
40
,
41
]
. It should be emphasized that the divergence formulation of the MHD
system is valid only for sufficiently smooth solutions. However, in the
case of weakly differentiable and discontinuous solutions,
\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{B}}

cannot be pointwise zero. To the best of our knowledge, none of the divergence
cleaning techniques, such as
[
10
]
, can completely eliminate the
discrepancy error of the divergence of
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
.

In this paper, instead of using the MHD equations in divergence form, see
(
2
), which is widely used in scientific works, we proposed to
use the induction equation and preserve the magnetic forces acting on the
momentum and total mechanical energy as source terms. More precisely, we
propose solving

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{m}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{m}+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}(\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}+\mathbb{I}p)

\displaystyle=-\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\,,

(1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\big(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}(E+p)\big)

\displaystyle=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\,,

(1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}-\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})

\displaystyle=0\,,

(1d)

where
\rho
is the density,
\bm{m}
is the momentum,
E=\frac{1}{2\rho}|\bm{m}|^{2}+\rho e
is the total mechanical energy,
e
is the
specific internal energy,
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
is the magnetic field,
p=p(\rho,e)
is
the pressure,
\mathbb{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the identity
matrix with
d
being the space dimension, and
\mu>0
is the
magnetic permeability constant. Taking the divergence to both sides of
(
1d
) we obtain the condition

\partial_{t}\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}=0,

implying that the evolution of the magnetic field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
is such
that
\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}(t)\equiv\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
for all
t\geq 0
, where

\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
is the initial data.

Note, that for the case of smooth (e.g.,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-continuous or better)
divergence-free solutions the formulation (
1
) is equivalent to
the MHD system in the divergence form (
2
). However, for the
case of weakly-differentiable and discontinuous solutions, we should regard
(
1
) and (
2
) as entirely different models. In
particular, there is no reason to believe that (
1
) and
(
2
) should produce the same families of discontinuous
solutions, see for instance
[
34
, p. 253]
on a related discussion.
We emphasize that formulation (
2
) is not valid without the
assumption
\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{B}}=\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{B}}_{0}=0
since it is an intrinsic
part of its derivation. On other hand, formulation (
1
) does not
need or use the condition
\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\equiv 0
: there is no
mathematical reason to incorporate such assumption.

From a practical point of view, regardless of whether we prefer
source-formulation (
1
) or divergence formulation
(
2
), any numerical method satisfying the following:

(i)

Preservation of pointwise stability properties: such as
pointwise positivity of the density and minimum principle of the specific
entropy;

(ii)

Preservation of involution constraints, in this case,
preservation of the weak-divergence;

(iii)

Preservation of total energy;

(iv)

Preservation of second order accuracy (or higher) for
smooth solutions;

(v)

Preservation of discrete entropy-dissipation properties.

is a desirable method for engineering and scientific applications. List
(i)-(v) is quite ambitious and we are not aware of any
numerical scheme capable of preserving properties (i)-(v)
simultaneously. We highlight that designing a scheme that
preserves just one of these properties (e.g., formal high-order accuracy,
see for instance
[
8
,
9
]
) does not pose a major
challenge. The mathematical challenge of structure preservation
lies in the satisfaction of two or more of these properties simultaneously. In
this manuscript, we advocate for the use of formulation (
1
),
instead of the usual divergence form (
2
), as better fit in
order to preserve properties (i)-(v) outlined above.

In
[
8
]
it was proved that by adding a viscous term to each equation
of the ideal MHD system (i.e., conservation of mass, conservation of momentum,
conservation of total energy, and the induction equation) one can achieve
positivity of density and internal energy, minimum principle of the specific
entropy, and satisfaction of all generalized entropies. In this article we
improve the result of
[
8
]
. We prove that the viscous regularization
of mass, momentum, and total mechanical energy is sufficient to achieve the
above mentioned properties (i.e., positivity of density and internal
energy, minimum principle of the specific entropy, and compatibility with all
generalized entropies). This shows that there is no need to regularize the
induction equation. This is a rather puzzling result, hinting at the
idea that the inclusion of the
\bm{\mathcal{B}}
field in the MHD Riemann problem is an
artificial construct. We propose to separate the evolution equation of
\bm{\mathcal{B}}

from the other components of the system (density, momentum, and total mechanical
energy), as originally described by the non-divergence formulation
(
1
). This is by no means a new idea: treating
\bm{\mathcal{B}}

independently using its own spatial discretization has been proposed, for
example, in
[
29
,
22
,
12
]
and references
therein. However, our approach still represents a major departure from
previously existing ideas and methods for the MHD system:

\bm{-}

The induction equation is not treated as an isolated object,
but rather as a constituent of a Hamiltonian system consisting in: the
balance of momentum subject to the Lorentz force
\mu(\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})
, coupled to the induction equation (
1d
), see for
instance expressions (
19
) and (
24
). By
treating a Hamiltonian system as such, this lends itself to natural
definitions of stability that we can preserve in the fully-discrete setting.

\bm{-}

We use no advective stabilization in any form or fashion for
the induction equation. This is tacitly suggested by the viscous regularization
argument indicating that no artificial viscosity is required for the magnetic
field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. This is also consistent with Hamiltonian systems, such as
(
24
), where the natural notion of stability is preservation
of quadratic invariants. We avoid construing the induction equation as an
advective system
[
19
,
21
]
, Friedrich’s system

[
5
]
, or vanishing-viscosity limit (e.g., conservation law).

\bm{-}

We use a primal (no vector potential) curl-conforming
framework in order to discretize the magnetic field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. This is
consistent with the preservation of weak divergence. We do not pursue to
preserve a zero strong-divergence, or use a div-conforming framework as
suggested for instance in
[
4
,
22
]
. However, we
show that the method can preserve zero weak-divergence to machine accuracy
for smooth as well as non-smooth regimes. We use no divergence cleaning.

\bm{-}

Energy of the non-divergence system (
1
) is
defined by a functional that consists in the sum of a linear
+

quadratic functional, see Section
2.2
. This is quite different
from the case of the divergence system (
2
) where energy
stability consists in preserving the property

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{E}(t)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{E}_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
, which is the
preservation of a linear functional.

\bm{-}

The resulting scheme preserves properties (i)-(iv) outlined
above. This scheme can be used for smooth as well as extreme
shock-hydrodynamics regimes. Property (v), entropy stability, can be preserved
as well, provided the hyperbolic solver used to discretize Euler’s subsystem is
entropy stable. We make no emphasis on property (v) since there the is a very
large literature on the matter. The scheme runs at the CFL of Euler’s
system, with no time-step size restriction due to magnetosonic waves. There
is, in principle, no limit on the formal spatial accuracy of the scheme.

The outline of the paper is as follows: in Section
2
we
provide all the necessary background in relationship to the mathematical
properties of the MHD and Euler’s system. In
Sections
3.1
-
3.3
we summarize the main
properties of the spatial and temporal discretizations that will be used. In
Section
3.4
we present the scheme and make precise its
mathematical properties. Finally, in Section
4
we present
numerical results illustrating the efficiency of the solver is the context of
smooth as well as non-smooth test problems. We highlight that the main ideas
advanced in this paper can be implemented using quite general hyperbolic solvers
for Euler’s equation. In Section
3.4
we outline the structure
and mathematical properties expected from such hyperbolic solver. For the sake
of completeness we also describe the hyperbolic solvers used for all
computations in
A
.

]

Definition 2.1

(Specific entropy, Gibbs identity and

Let

2.2
Energy balance of the non-divergence formulation

Proposition 2.1

The MHD system (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}E+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(E+p)\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\cdot\bm{n}-\mu(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{n})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{s}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}E\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(E+p)\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{s}+\mu\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(13)

Using the property
\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\cdot(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})=\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\cdot(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\,,
and inserting this identity into (
13
) yields

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\mu\int_{\Omega}(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\mu\int_{\partial\Omega}[(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})\times\bm{\mathcal{H}}]\cdot\bm{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{s}=0.

(14)

Finally, adding (
14
) to (
12
), and using
properties of the triple product yields the desired result.
∎

Remark 2.1

(Energy conservation and boundary conditions)
.

As noted in the introduction, non-divergence formulation (
1
) and
divergence formulation (
2
) should be treated as
different models. As such, each formulation accommodates
complementary set of boundary conditions. We start by noting that the
total energy considered in (
11
) is
\int_{\Omega}E+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
is not a linear functional but rather the sum
of a linear and a quadratic functional. As usual, conservation of total
energy
\int_{\Omega}E+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
holds for
the trivial case of periodic boundary conditions. Inspecting identity
(
11
), we note that another simple scenario where total energy is
conserved is when
\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}\equiv 0
and
\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{n}\equiv\bm{0}
on the entirety of the boundary. Tangent boundary conditions,
such as
\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{n}\equiv\bm{0}
, can be enforced in the
curl-conforming framework such as the Sobolev space
H(\text{curl}\,\mathbf{})
, see for
instance
[
30
,
2
]
, which will be used to discretize

\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. Note that the normal boundary conditions
(\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\bm{n}=0
, traditionally used for the divergence formulation
(
2
), are not meaningful in the context of non-divergence
model (
1
).

Remark 2.2

(Simplifying assumptions)
.

In the remainder of the paper, in order
to simplify arguments in relationship to boundary conditions, we assume that
periodic boundary conditions are used, or that the initial data is compactly
supported and that the final time is small enough to prevent waves from
reaching
the boundary. Alternatively, we could assume that boundary conditions

\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}\equiv 0
and
\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{n}\equiv\bm{0}
are
used
on the entirety of the boundary.

2.3
Euler’s equation with forces

Consider Euler’s system subject to the effect of a force, that is

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u})=\mathbf{s}(\bm{f}),

(15)

with

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\bm{m}\\
E\end{bmatrix}\ ,\ \ \bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{m}^{\top}\\
\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}+\mathbb{I}p\\
\rho^{-1}\bm{m}^{\top}(E+p)\end{bmatrix}\ ,\ \ \mathbf{s}(\bm{f})=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\bm{f}\\
\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{f}\end{bmatrix}.

(16)

In particular, system (
1a
)-(
1c
) can be rewritten as in
(
15
)-(
16
) using the particular choice of
force
\bm{f}=-\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. For
any force
\bm{f}
we have the property described in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4

(Invariance of entropy-like functionals

[
26
]
)
.

Let

\bm{u}=[\rho,\bm{m},E]\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}
be the state of Euler’s
system. Let

\Psi(\bm{u}):\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
any
arbitrary functional of the state satisfying
the
functional dependence
\Psi(\bm{u}):=\psi(\rho,\varepsilon(\bm{u}))
, where

\varepsilon(\bm{u}):=E-\frac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{2\rho}
is the internal
energy per unit volume. Then we have that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})\cdot\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})\equiv 0,

(17)

where
\nabla_{\bm{u}}
is the gradient with respect to the state, i.e.,
\nabla_{\bm{u}}=\big[\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho},\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{m}_{1}},...,\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{m}_{d}},\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\big]^{\top}
.

Proof.

Using the chain rule we observe that
\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\rho}\nabla_{\bm{u}}\rho+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varepsilon}\nabla_{\bm{u}}\varepsilon
, where

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{u}}\rho

\displaystyle=[1,0,...,0]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{u}}\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\big[\tfrac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{\rho^{2}},-\tfrac{\bm{m}_{1}}{\rho},...,-\tfrac{\bm{m}_{d}}{\rho},1\big]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}.

Taking the product with
\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})
we get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})\cdot\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\rho}\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{u}}\rho\cdot\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})}_{=\,0}+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varepsilon}\nabla_{\bm{u}}\varepsilon\cdot\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varepsilon}(-\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{f}+\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{f})=0.

∎

Remark 2.3

(Colloquial interpretation)
.

Lemma
2.4
is simply saying that the evolution in time
of an arbitrary functional
\Psi(\bm{u})
satisfying the functional dependence

\Psi(\bm{u}):=\psi(\rho,\varepsilon(\bm{u}))
is independent of the force

\bm{f}
. This follows directly by taking the dot-product of
(
15
) with
\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})
to get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})\cdot\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}\;=\;\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\bm{u})\;=\;-\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})\cdot\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u})+\underbrace{\nabla_{\bm{u}}\Psi(\bm{u})\cdot\mathbf{s}(\bm{f})}_{\equiv\,0}.

In particular, this holds true when
\Psi(\bm{u}):=\varepsilon(\bm{u})
.
Similarly, we can apply Lemma
2.4
to the specific internal
energy
e(\bm{u})=\rho^{-1}\varepsilon(\bm{u})
since

e(\bm{u})
satisfies the functional dependence
e(\bm{u})=\psi(\rho,\varepsilon(\bm{u}))
as well. We also note that condition
(
17
) is related to the so-called “complementary
degeneracy requirements” usually invoked in GENERIC systems, see

[
31
]
.

2.4
Splitting of the differential operator

Remark 2.4

(Choice of splitting)
.

We consider the splitting for the system
(
1
) in two evolutionary operators:

\displaystyle\text{Operator \#1 }\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\bm{m}&=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}\bm{m}+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}(\rho^{-1}\bm{m}\bm{m}^{\top}+\mathbb{I}p)&=\bm{0}\,,\\
\partial_{t}E+\text{div}\,\mathbf{}\big(\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}(E+p)\big)&=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}&=\bm{0}\,,\end{split}\right.

(18)

and

\displaystyle\text{Operator \#2 }\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho&=0\,,\\
\partial_{t}\bm{m}&=-\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\,,\\
\partial_{t}E&=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}\,,\\
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}&=\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}(\bm{v}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})\,.\end{split}\right.

(19)

Given some initial data
\bm{u}^{n}=[\rho^{n},\bm{m}^{n},E^{n},\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}]
for
each one of these operators, we would like to know what properties are
preserved by their evolution.

Proposition 2.2

(Evolution of Operator #1: preservation of linear
invariants and pointwise stability properties)
.

Assume periodic boundary
conditions. Assume that the initial data at time
t_{n}
is admissible, meaning

\bm{u}^{n}(\bm{x})=[\rho^{n},\bm{m}^{n},E^{n}](\bm{x})\in\mathcal{A}
for
all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
with
\mathcal{A}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\big\{[\rho,\bm{m},E]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\,\big|\,\rho>0,\,E-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{|\bm{m}|^{2}}{\rho}>0\big\}\,.

(20)

Then, the evolution of Operator #1 from time
t_{n}
to
t_{n+1}
,
preserves the following linear invariants

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\rho^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\rho^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\ \ \int_{\Omega}\bm{m}^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\\
\int_{\Omega}E^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}E^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\ \ \int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\ \ \end{split}

(21)

as well as the pointwise stability properties

\displaystyle\rho^{n+1}(\bm{x})\geq 0\ ,\ \ s(\bm{u}^{n+1}(\bm{x}))\geq\min_{\bm{x}\in\Omega}s(\bm{u}^{n}(\bm{x}))\ ,\ \varepsilon(\bm{u}^{n+1}(\bm{x}))\geq 0.

(22)

for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
.

Note that
\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
follows trivially from the fact that
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\equiv\bm{0}
for the case of Operator #1.
Properties (
21
) are a consequence of the divergence
theorem. On the other hand, establishing properties (
22
) is
rather technical, the reader is referred to
[
36
,
15
]
.
We note in passing that positivity of the internal energy is not
a direct property, but rather a consequence of the positivity of density and
minimum principle of the specific entropy.

Corollary 2.1

(Energy-stability of Operator #1: Linear + Quadratic
functional)
.

Assume periodic boundary conditions,
then the evolution described
by Operator #1 satisfies the following energy-estimate

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E^{n+1}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}E^{n}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

Proof.

This follows from the conservation property
\int_{\Omega}E^{n+1}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}E^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
, then we add
\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
to both sides of the equality, and use the
fact that
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}\equiv\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
since
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\equiv\bm{0}
.
∎

Regarding Operator #2, we start by noting that since
\partial_{t}\rho\equiv 0
, we can rewrite (
19
) as

\displaystyle\text{Operator \#2 }\left\{\begin{split}\partial_{t}\rho&=0\\
\rho\partial_{t}\bm{v}&=-\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}}\,,\\
\partial_{t}E&=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\bm{v}\,,\\
\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}}&=\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}(\bm{v}\times\bm{\mathcal{H}})\,,\end{split}\right.

(23)

and note that only the evolution of
\bm{v}
and
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
are actually
coupled. Assume periodic boundary conditions, multiply the evolution
equation for
\bm{v}
with a smooth test function
\bm{z}
and the evolution
equation for
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
with a vector-valued smooth test function
\bm{\mathcal{X}}

integrate by parts, then we get:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\rho\partial_{t}\bm{v},\bm{z})&=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}},\bm{z})\,,\\
(\partial_{t}\bm{\mathcal{H}},\bm{\mathcal{X}})&=(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{X}},\bm{v})\,,\end{split}

(24)

We will discretize (
24
) in space and time, see Section

3.3
. It is clear that, in order to make sense of the
integration by parts used to derive (
24
) the weak or
distributional
curl
of
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
should be well defined. Therefore, it is
natural to consider a curl-conforming space discretization for
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. Note
that tangent boundary conditions
\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\bm{n}\equiv\bm{0}
, which
can be directly enforced in the curl-conforming framework, are useful to achieve
energy-isolation of the MHD system, see Remark
2.1
.

Proposition 2.3

(Evolution of Operator #2: global energy stability and
pointwise invariants)
.

Let
\bm{u}^{n}=[\rho^{n},\bm{m}^{n},E^{n},\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}]^{\top}
be the initial data. Assume periodic
boundary conditions, then the evolution of
described by Operator #2 as defined in (
23
), preserves the
following global quadratic-invariant:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n+1}|\bm{v}^{n+1}|^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n}|\bm{v}^{n}|^{2}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,,

(25)

a well as pointwise invariance of the internal energy

\displaystyle(E^{n+1}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n+1}|\bm{v}^{n+1}|^{2})(\bm{x})=(E^{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n}|\bm{v}^{n}|^{2})(\bm{x})

(26)

for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
, with
\rho^{n+1}(\bm{x})=\rho^{n}(\bm{x})

since density does not evolve for the case of Operator #2.

Proof.

Energy stability (
25
) follows by taking

\bm{z}=\bm{v}
and
\bm{\mathcal{X}}=\mu\bm{\mathcal{H}}
in (
24
) and
adding both lines. On the other hand, the invariance of the internal energy
(
26
) is a direct consequence (
17
) and Remark

2.3
.
∎

Corollary 2.2

(Energy-stability of Operator #2: Linear + Quadratic
functional)
.

Under the assumptions of
Proposition
2.3
, the evolution of Operator #2 satisfies the
following energy-balance

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E^{n+1}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}E^{n}+\tfrac{\mu}{2}|\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(27)

Proof.

Integrating (
26
) with respect to space we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}E^{n+1}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n+1}|\bm{v}^{n+1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}E^{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n}|\bm{v}^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(28)

Identity (
27
) follows by adding (
25
) and
(
28
) leading to the cancellation of kinetic energy terms.
∎

Remark 2.5

(Invariant domain preservation for the evolution described by
Operator #2)
.

We note that the evolution described Operator #2 is such that
neither density nor internal energy evolve, then: specific entropy and
mathematical entropy remain invariant. Or equivalently in term of formulas

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\equiv 0\ \ \text{and}\ \ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(E-\tfrac{1}{2\rho}|\bm{m}|^{2}\big)\equiv 0\ \ \text{imply that}\ \ \tfrac{\partial s}{\partial t}\equiv 0\ \ \text{and}\ \ \tfrac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}\equiv 0.

(29)

here
\eta(\bm{u})=-\rho s(\bm{u})
is the mathematical entropy. In
conclusion: the evolution of Operator #2 cannot meaningfully affect the
evolution of density, internal energy, or specific entropy. Therefore,
Operator #2 cannot affect the preservation of invariant set properties.
Since the mathematical entropy remains constant during the evolution
described by Operator #2 we also have the global estimate:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\eta(\bm{u}(\bm{x},t_{n+1}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\eta(\bm{u}(\bm{x},t_{n}))\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,.

Remark 2.6

(Discrete-time evolution of total mechanical energy for
Operator #2)
.

From (
23
) we note that the evolution of velocity

\bm{v}
and magnetic field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
are independent from the evolution of the
total mechanical energy
E
. Therefore, in the time-discrete setting, given
an initial data
[\rho^{n},\bm{v}^{n},E^{n},\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n}](\bm{x})
at time

t_{n}
, we can compute
\bm{v}^{n+1}
and
\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}
by integrating
(
24
) in time neglecting the evolution law for the total
mechanical energy
\partial_{t}E=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}
. Once
\bm{v}^{n+1}
and

\bm{\mathcal{H}}^{n+1}
are available, the constraint (
26
) identifies a
unique function
E^{n+1}(\bm{x})
. More precisely, we may rewrite
(
26
) as

\displaystyle E^{n+1}(\bm{x}):=\big(E^{n}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n}|\bm{v}^{n}|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rho^{n+1}|\bm{v}^{n+1}|^{2}\big)(\bm{x}),

(30)

and use it in order to compute
E^{n+1}(\bm{x})
using the data

\rho^{n}
,
\rho^{n+1}=\rho^{n}
,
E^{n}
,
\bm{v}^{n}
and

\bm{v}^{n+1}
. This means that there is no particular use for
\partial_{t}E=-\mu(\bm{\mathcal{H}}\times\text{curl}\,\mathbf{}\bm{\mathcal{H}})\cdot\tfrac{\bm{m}}{\rho}
. Therefore,
we use (
30
) in order to update total mechanical energy in the
time-discrete setting. Note that, by construction, (
30
)
guarantees exact preservation of the internal energy, specific internal energy,
specific entropy and mathematical entropy as well (provided that
\rho^{n+1}\equiv\rho^{n}
).

Remark 2.7

(Induction equation as an independent object of
study)
.

We note that the induction equation, either
(
1d
) or (
2d
), is a very interesting object in
its own right. Broadly speaking, the devise of schemes for advective PDEs
endowed with involution-like constraints is a challenging task that has
received significant attention in the last years
[
11
,
39
,
5
,
28
,
23
,
24
,
20
,
19
,
21
,
33
]
. However, unless we make very strong assumptions
about
the velocity field, the induction equation does not satisfy
any global stability property (e.g.,
L^{2}
-stability). Similarly, to the best of
the
authors’ knowledge the induction does not satisfy any pointwise stability
property, such as max/min principles or invariant set properties. Since the
induction equation does not have natural notions of global or pointwise
stability, numerical stability not a well-defined concept. On the other
hand,
system (
23
) satisfies the global stability property
(
25
), and the pointwise stability property
(
26
),
outlining quite clearly the properties numerical methods should preserve.

3
Space and time discretization of the MHD system

3.1
Space discretization

In this subsection we outline the space discretization used for Euler’s
components
\{\rho,\bm{m},E\}
and the magnetic field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. The ideas
advanced in this manuscript work in two space dimensions (
d=2
) as well as
three space dimensions (
d=3
). Similarly, the scheme has no limitation on the
choice of polynomial degree and formal accuracy in space. However, for the sake
of concreteness, we focus on the case of
d=2
and spatial discretizations
capable of delivering second-order accuracy. In Remark
3.1

we also provide the proper generalization for the case of
quadrilateral/tetrahedral meshes.

We consider a simplicial mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
and a corresponding
scalar-valued continuous finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{h}
for each
component of Euler’s system:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}

\displaystyle=\big\{v_{h}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega)\;\big|\;v_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\widehat{K})\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}.

(31)

Here,
\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}):\widehat{K}\to K

denotes a diffeomorphism mapping from the unit simplex
\widehat{K}
to
the physical element
K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
,
and
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\widehat{K})
is polynomial space of at most first degree on
the reference element. We
define
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}=\big\{1:\text{dim}(\mathbb{V}_{h})\big\}
as the
index-set of global, scalar-valued degrees of freedom corresponding to

\mathbb{V}_{h}
. Similarly, we introduce the set of global shape functions

\{\phi_{i}(\bm{x})\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}
and the set of collocation
points
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}
satisfying the property

\phi_{i}(\bm{x}_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}
. We
assume that the partition of unity property
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})=1
for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
holds true. We introduce a
number of matrices that will be used for the algebraic discretization. We
define the consistent mass matrix entries
m_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
, lumped mass
matrix
m_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and the discrete divergence-matrix entries

\mathbf{c}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle m_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\ \ m_{i}=\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\ ,\ \ \mathbf{c}_{ij}=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{j}\phi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,.

(32)

Note that the definition of
m_{ij}
and the partition of unity property

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})=1
imply that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}=m_{i}
. Given two scalar-valued finite element
functions
u_{h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}u_{i}\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
and
v_{h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}v_{i}\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
we define the lumped inner product as

\displaystyle\langle u_{h},v_{h}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}u_{i}v_{i}\,.

(33)

For the case of vector valued functions
\bm{u}_{h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\bm{u}_{i}\phi_{i}\in[\mathbb{V}_{h}]^{2}
and
\bm{v}_{h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\bm{v}_{i}\phi_{i}\in[\mathbb{V}_{h}]^{2}
the lumped
inner-product is defined as
\langle\bm{u}_{h},\bm{v}_{h}\rangle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\bm{u}_{i}\cdot\bm{v}_{i}
.

We define the finite dimensional space

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}=\big\{\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{h}\in H(\text{curl},\Omega)\,\big|\,[\nabla_{\widehat{\bm{x}}}\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}})]^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in[\mathbb{P}^{1}(\widehat{K})]^{2}\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}

(34)

which will be used to discretize the magnetic field
\bm{\mathcal{H}}
. The finite
element space
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}
is known as the “rotated” or curl-conforming

\text{BDM}_{1}
space. The primary motivation to use this space is that it the
simplest curl-conforming finite element that spans all the vector-valued
polynomial space
[\mathbb{P}_{1}]^{2}
, therefore full second-order accuracy
should be expected in the
L^{p}
-norms when using this element.

Finally, we define the space

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{h}=\big\{\omega_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\omega_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\widehat{K})\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}\,.

(35)

It is easy to prove that the space
\mathbb{W}_{h}
satisfies the inclusion

\nabla\mathbb{W}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}
, more precisely, these two
spaces are part of a discrete exact sequence, see
[
1
]
. The space

\mathbb{W}_{h}
is used to define the weak divergence-free property, see
Proposition
3.1
.

Remark 3.1

(Quadrilateral and tetrahedral meshes)
.

In the context of tensor product elements, we have to use different definitions
for the set of spaces
\mathbb{V}_{h}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}
and
\mathbb{W}_{h}
defined
in (
31
), (
34
) and (
35
) respectively.
The simplest space we can use in order to discretize the components of
Euler’s system is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{h}

\displaystyle=\big\{v_{h}(\bm{x})\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega)\;\big|\;v_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}(\widehat{K})\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}.

(36)

for
k\geq 1
. On the other hand, the natural candiates for the spaces

\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}
and
\mathbb{W}_{h}
are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}

\displaystyle=\big\{\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{h}\in H(\text{curl},\Omega)\,\big|\,[\nabla_{\widehat{\bm{x}}}\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}})]^{\top}\bm{\mathcal{X}}_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(\widehat{K})\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}_{h}

\displaystyle=\big\{\omega_{h}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(\Omega)\,\big|\,\omega_{h}(\bm{T}_{K}(\widehat{\bm{x}}))\in\mathbb{Q}^{k+1}(\widehat{K})\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\big\}\,.

(38)

with
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\widehat{K})=[\mathbb{P}_{k,k+1,k+1}(\widehat{K}),\mathbb{P}_{k+1,k,k+1}(\widehat{K}),\mathbb{P}_{k+1,k+1,k}(\widehat{K})]
, where

\mathbb{P}_{p,q,r}
denotes the space of scalar-valued polynomials of at most

p
-th degree in the
x
-variable,
q
-th degree in the
y
-variable and

r
-th degree in the
z
-variable. The vector-valued polynomial family

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\widehat{K})
is the celebrated Nedelec space of the
first kind, see
[
6
]
. The choice of spaces described in
(
36
)-(
38
) can be generalized straightforwardly to
arbitrary polynomial degree for both the two and three space dimensions. An
alternative to the choices described in
(
37
)-(
38
), for the specific case of
two-space dimensions and a target of second-order accuracy, is using the

\text{BDM}_{1}
space on quadrilaterals for
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}}_{h}
, also denoted as

\mathcal{S}_{1}\Lambda^{1}
in the context of finite element exterior calculus,
and the serendipity element
\mathcal{S}_{1}\Lambda^{0}
for
\mathbb{W}_{h}
,
see
[
1
]
. However, the implementation of elements from the

\mathcal{S}_{k}\Lambda^{r}
family, and their generalization to higher-order
polynomial degrees is slightly more technical.

3.2
Discretization of the Operator #1: minimal
assumptions

The central ideas advanced in this paper are compatible with most of the
existing numerical methods used to solve Euler’s equation of gas dynamics.
In this subsection we limit ourselves to outline the minimal assumptions
made about the numerical scheme used to approximate the solution of Operator
#1. We assume that, given some initial data
\bm{u}_{h}=[\rho_{h}^{n},\bm{m}_{h}^{n},E_{h}^{n}]^{\top}
, a numerical approximation to the
solutions of
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=[\rho(\bm{x},t),\bm{m}(\bm{x},t),E(\bm{x},t)]^{\top}
at time
t_{n}
, we have at hand a numerical
procedure to compute the updated state as

\displaystyle\{\rho_{h}^{n+1},\bm{m}_{h}^{n+1},E_{h}^{n+1},\tau_{n}\}

\displaystyle:=\texttt{euler\_system\_update}(\{\rho_{h}^{n},\bm{m}_{h}^{n},E_{h}^{n}\}),

(39)

where
\{\rho_{h}^{n+1},\bm{m}_{h}^{n+1},E_{h}^{n+1}\}
is the approximate
solution at time
t_{n}+\tau_{n}
. Note that as described
in (
39
),
\tau_{n}
is a return argument of the procedure

euler_system_update
. In other words,

euler_system_update
determines the time-step size on its own. We
may at times, need to prescribe the time-step size used by

euler_system_update
, in such case the interface of the method
might look as:

\displaystyle\{\rho_{h}^{n+1},\bm{m}_{h}^{n+1},E_{h}^{n+1}\}

\displaystyle:=\texttt{euler\_system\_update}(\{\rho_{h}^{n},\bm{m}_{h}^{n},E_{h}^{n},\tau_{n}\}),

where
\tau_{n}
is supplied to
euler_system_update
. The
internals of
euler_system_update
are not of much relevance.
However, we may assume that
euler_system_update
is formally
second-order accurate, and most importantly, the following structural
properties:

\bm{\diamond}

Collocated discretization.
We assume that all the
components of Euler’s system (
18
) are discretized in a collocated
fashion meaning

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\rho_{i}\phi_{(}\bm{x})\,,\ \bm{m}_{h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\bm{m}_{i}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})\,,\ E_{h}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}E_{i}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})\,,\

where
\rho_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\bm{m}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
E_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\{\phi_{i}(\bm{x})\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}
is the basis
of the scalar-valued finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{h}
defined in
(
31
).

\bm{\diamond}

Conservation of linear invariants.
In the context
of periodic boundary conditions the hyperbolic solver preserves the linear
invariants:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\rho_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\rho_{i}^{n}\ ,\ \ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\bm{m}_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\bm{m}_{i}^{n}\ ,\ \ \sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}E_{i}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}E_{i}^{n},

(40)

where
m_{i}
was defined in (
32
).

\bm{\diamond}

Admissibility.
Assume that the initial data

\bm{u}_{i}^{n}=[\rho_{i}^{n},\bm{m}_{i}^{n},E_{i}^{n}]^{\top}
is admissible, meaning

\bm{u}_{i}^{n}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}
, where the set

\mathcal{A}
was defined in (
20
). Then the updated
state
\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}=[\rho_{i}^{n+1},\bm{m}_{i}^{n+1},E_{i}^{n+1}]^{\top}

is admissible for all
i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}
as well. We highlight that this is
rather low requirements for preservation of pointwise properties. In general,
positivity properties are not enough, and we might be interested in stronger
properties, such as the preservation of the local minimum principle of the
specific entropy, see
[
14
,
18
]
.

\bm{\diamond}

Entropy dissipation inequality.
We
may

assume that the scheme preserves a global entropy inequality, meaning

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1})\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{i}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n}),

(41)

in the context of periodic boundary conditions.

For the sake of completeness, in
A
we make precise
the implementation details of the hyperbolic solver used in all our
computations. For any practical purpose, we may simply regard

euler_system_update
as the user’s favorite choice of Euler scheme
(a black box) that is: consistent, conservative, it is mathematically
guaranteed not to crash, and may have some entropy-dissipation properties.

Remark 3.2

(Partition of unity and consistent mass-matrix)
.

We note that
hyperbolic solvers using a consistent mass matrix do not satisfy
conservation property (
40
) directly, but rather the identities

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\varrho_{j}^{n+1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\varrho_{j}^{n},

(42)

where
\{\varrho_{i}^{n+1}\}_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}
represents a quantity of
interest
such as density, momentum or total mechanical energy, with
m_{ij}
as
defined in (
32
). In this context, we consider the summation

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}
to both sides of identity
(
42
),
using the partition of unity property
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}=m_{j}
(see Section
3.1
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\varrho_{j}^{n+1}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\varrho_{j}^{n}\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\underbrace{\big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\big)}_{=\,m_{j}}\varrho_{j}^{n+1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\underbrace{\big(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}m_{ij}\big)}_{=\,m_{j}}\varrho_{j}^{n},

recovering to the usual conservation identity.

Remark 3.3

(Lumping and higher order elements)

In all our computations we use simplices. However, if we were to use tensor

(Properties preserved by Algorithm

Remark 4.1

(Solver performance: Why is the scheme competitive?)
.

Implicit-time integration requires the execution of Newton iterations, with
each iteration involving the inversion a Jacobian. In addition,
the inverse of the Jacobian is applied using a Krylov method, with
each iteration involving two matrix-vector products. The fundamental question
of whether implicit time-integration is competitive (or not) boils down to
having a low count of (linear and nonlinear) iterations. For the
method advanced in this paper we have rather exceptional linear and nonlinear
solver performance. For starters, the nonlinear system (
44
)
is solved with at most 4 Newton iterations: we hardcoded the logic to
stop the whole computation if more than 4 iterations are needed. This is in big
part because we are using the solution from the previous time-step as an initial
guess. On the other hand, even though the method does not have to respect the
CFL of the MHD system, and magnetosonic waves can be ignored, we still
have to respect the CFL of Euler system. Therefore the time-step sizes
are still moderate and the resulting Jacobian is just a
perturbation of the mass matrix. This means that an inexpensive Krylov method,
such as BiCGStab without any form of preconditioning, can be used in practice.
Usually less than a dozen matrix-vector product are used in order to apply the
inverse of the Jacobian. We believe that the scheme is quite
competitive and the incorporation of matrix-free linear algebra for system
(
44
) would make the current implementation suitable to
execute three-dimensional computations.

Appendix A
Hyperbolic solver used in this paper

This section provides a brief outline of the numerical methods used to solve
Euler’s system for all the computational experiments advanced in Section

4
. The Euler’s system solver presented in this section fills in
the role of
euler_system_update
invoked in Algorithm

3
, which is supposed to comply with the properties described in
Section
3.2
. This section does not introduce any novel concept,
idea, or numerical scheme, and it is only provided for the sake of completeness.
The main ideas advanced in this section were originally developed in the
sequence of papers
[
17
,
16
,
14
]
and references therein.

A.1
Low-order scheme

Low-order scheme is obtained using first-order Graph Viscosity method suggested
first in
[
17
]
. Let
t_{n}
be the current time,
\tau_{n}
is
the current time-step and we advance in time by setting
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\tau_{n}
. Let
\bm{u}_{h}^{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\bm{u}_{i}^{n}\phi_{i}(\bm{x})
be the finite element approximation at time
t_{n}
. The first
order approximation at time
t_{n+1}
is computed as

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{L},n+1}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}}{\tau_{n}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{\text{L}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n})=\bm{0},

(52)

where
m_{i}
and
\mathbf{c}_{ij}
were defined in (
32
), while the
set
\mathcal{I}(i)
is the so-called stencil, which is defined as

\mathcal{I}(i)=\big\{j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}\,|\,\mathbf{c}_{ij}\not=\bm{0}\big\}
,
while the low-order graph-viscosity
d_{ij}^{\text{L}}
is computed as

\displaystyle d_{ij}^{\text{L}}

\displaystyle:=\max(\lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{i}^{n},\bm{u}_{j}^{n},\bm{n}_{ij})\,|\mathbf{c}_{ij}|_{\ell_{2}},\ \lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n},\bm{u}_{i}^{n},\bm{n}_{ji})\,|\mathbf{c}_{ji}|_{\ell_{2}}),\quad\forall i\not=j,

(53)

\displaystyle d_{ii}^{\text{L}}

\displaystyle:=-\sum_{i\not=j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}d_{ji}^{\text{L}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \bm{n}_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf{c}_{ij}}{|\mathbf{c}_{ij}|_{\ell_{2}}}.

Here
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\bm{n})
is the maximum wave speed of
the one dimensional Riemann problem:
\partial_{t}\bm{u}+\partial_{x}(\mathbf{f}(\bm{u})\bm{n})=0
, where
x=\bm{x}\cdot\bm{n}
, with initial
condition:
\bm{u}(x,0)=\bm{u}_{L}=[\rho_{L},\bm{m}_{L},E_{L}]^{\top}
if

x<0
, and
\bm{u}(x,0)=\bm{u}_{R}=[\rho_{R},\bm{m}_{R},E_{R}]^{\top}
if
x\geq 0
. The maximum
wavespeed of this Riemann problem can be
computed exactly
[
38
, Chap. 4]
, however this comes at the
expense of solving a nonlinear problem. In theory and practice, any upper
bound of the maximum wavespeed of the Riemann problem could be used in
formula (
53
) while still preserving rigorous mathematical
properties of the scheme
[
17
,
18
]
. For the specific
case of the covolume equation of state;
p(1-b\rho)=(\gamma-1)e\rho
with
b\geq 0
; we can use
\lambda^{\#}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\mathbf{n})
which is defined by

\displaystyle\lambda^{\#}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\mathbf{n})=\max((\lambda_{1}^{-}(p^{\#}))_{-},\ (\lambda_{3}^{+}(p^{\#}))_{+}),

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{-}(p^{\#})=v_{L}-c_{L}\left(1+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{p^{\#}-p_{L}}{p_{L}}\right)_{+}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}^{+}(p^{\#})=v_{R}+c_{R}\left(1+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\left(\frac{p^{\#}-p_{R}}{p_{R}}\right)_{+}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle p^{\#}:=\left(\frac{c_{L}(1-b\rho_{L})+c_{R}(1-b\rho_{R})-\frac{\gamma-1}{2}(v_{R}-v_{L})}{c_{L}(1-b\rho_{L})\ p_{L}^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}+c_{R}(1-b\rho_{R})\ p_{R}^{-\frac{\gamma-1}{2\gamma}}}\right)^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma-1}},

(54)

where
z_{-}:=\max(0,-z)
,
z_{+}:=\max(0,z)
,
v_{L}=\bm{v}_{L}\cdot\bm{n}
,
v_{R}=\bm{v}_{R}\cdot\bm{n}
,
p_{L}
and
p_{R}
are the left and right pressures,
and
c_{L}
and
c_{R}
are left and right sound speeds. Here the formula
(
54
) is often referred to as the two-rarefaction estimate

[
38
]
. It is possible to show that
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\mathbf{n})\leq\lambda^{\#}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\mathbf{n})

[
16
]
for
1<\gamma\leq\frac{5}{3}
. For
all computations presented in this paper,
\lambda^{\#}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\mathbf{n})
is used instead of
\lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{L},\bm{u}_{R},\bm{n})
in order to compute the algebraic viscosities described in
(
53
). We finally mention that scheme
(
52
) equipped with the viscosity (
53
), is compatible with
the assumption (
41
).

Remark A.1

(Convex reformulation and CFL condition)
.

The scheme
(
52
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}=\Big(1-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)\backslash\{i\}}\tfrac{2\tau_{n}d_{ij}^{\textrm{L},n}}{m_{i}}\Big)\,\bm{u}_{i}^{n}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)\backslash\{i\}}\Big(\tfrac{2\tau_{n}d_{ij}^{\textrm{L},n}}{m_{i}}\Big)\,\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n},

(55)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n}+\bm{u}_{i}^{n})-\tfrac{|\mathbf{c}_{ij}|}{2d_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}}(\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})-\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{i}^{n}))\bm{n}_{ij}

(56)

are the so-called
bar-states. We note that the states
\{\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}
are admissible provided that
\bm{u}_{i}^{n}
and
\bm{u}_{j}^{n}

are admissible and that
d_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}\geq\max(\lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{i}^{n},\bm{u}_{j}^{n},\bm{n}_{ij})\,\|\mathbf{c}_{ij}\|_{\ell_{2}},\ \lambda_{\max}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n},\bm{u}_{i}^{n},\bm{n}_{ji})\,\|\mathbf{c}_{ji}\|_{\ell_{2}})
, see
[
17
,
18
]
. We note
that
\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}
is a convex combination of the bar-states

\{\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}
provided the condition

\Big(1-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)\backslash\{i\}}\tfrac{2\tau_{n}d_{ii}^{\textrm{L},n}}{m_{i}}\Big)\geq 0
holds. Therefore, we define the largest
admissible time-step size as

\displaystyle\tau_{n}=\text{CFL}\cdot\min_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}\big(-\tfrac{m_{i}}{2d_{ii}^{\textrm{L},n}}\big)

where
\text{CFL}\in(0,1)
is a user defined parameter.

A.2
High-order scheme

We note that the scheme (
52
) can only be first-order accurate.
Therefore we consider the high-order scheme:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}m_{ij}\frac{\bm{u}_{j}^{\text{{H}},n+1}-\bm{u}_{j}^{n}}{\tau_{n}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij}-d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n})=\bm{0},

(57)

Here
\{d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}\}_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}
are the high-order viscosities
which are meant to be such that
d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}\approx 0
in smooth regions of
the domain, while
d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}\approx d_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}
near shocks and
discontinuities. In addition,
d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}
must be symmetric and conservative,
i.e.,
d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}=d_{ji}^{\textrm{H}}
and

d_{ii}^{\textrm{H}}:=-\sum_{i\not=j\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}d_{ji}^{\textrm{H}}
.

In this paper, we use a high-order viscosity that is proportional to the
entropy residual (i.e. entropy-production) of the unstabilized scheme. Let us
start by considering the Galerkin solution
\bm{u}_{h}^{\text{G}}
defined as

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{G}}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}}{\tau_{n}}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij}=\bm{0}\ \text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}.

(58)

Let
\{\eta(\bm{u}),\bm{\mathbb{q}}(\bm{u})\}
be an entropy pair of the Euler system.
We define the entropy residual function
R^{n}_{h}(\bm{u}_{h})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}}R_{i}^{n}\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}
with nodal values

R_{i}^{n}
defined by

\displaystyle R_{i}^{n}:=m_{i}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{G}}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}}{\tau_{n}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{u}}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\bm{\mathbb{q}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij}\ \text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}.

(59)

Here
R_{i}^{n}
is proportional to the entropy production of the unstabilized
scheme (
58
). However, formula (
59
) is not practical, since
it requires computing
\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{G}}
. We derive a
formula for
R_{i}^{n}
that does not invoke
\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{G}}
: multiplying
(
58
) by
\nabla_{\bm{u}}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})
we get that

\displaystyle m_{i}\frac{\bm{u}_{i}^{\text{G}}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}}{\tau_{n}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{u}}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})=-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}(\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{u}}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})

which we use to replace the first term in (
59
):

\displaystyle R_{i}^{n}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}-(\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij})\cdot\nabla_{\bm{u}}\eta(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})+\bm{\mathbb{q}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})\mathbf{c}_{ij}\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}.

(60)

In practice, we use (
60
) in order to compute the entropy-viscosity
indicators. We are now ready to define the high-order nonlinear viscosity as

\displaystyle d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}:=\min\Big(d_{ij}^{\textrm{L}},c_{\text{EV}}\max(\widetilde{R}^{n}_{i},\widetilde{R}^{n}_{j})\Big),

where
c_{\text{EV}}
is a tunable constant, which is taken to be equal to 1 in
numerical examples in this manuscript, and
\widetilde{R}^{n}_{i}
is the normalized
entropy residual:

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}^{n}_{i}:=\frac{R^{n}_{i}}{\max\Big(\rho^{\max,\,n}_{i}s^{\max,\,n}_{i}-\rho^{\min,\,n}_{i}s^{\min,\,n}_{i},\epsilon\|\eta^{n}_{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big)},

where
w^{\max,\,n}_{i}:=\max_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}w^{n}_{j}
, and

w^{\min,\,n}_{i}:=\min_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}w^{n}_{j},

for
w
being
\rho
or
s
. Recall that the mathematical entropy is computed as

\eta(\bm{u})=-\rho s(\bm{u})
, where
s(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{\gamma-1}\log(e)-\log(\rho)
is the specific entropy. A small safety factor

\epsilon=10^{-8}
is used to avoid division by zero.

A.3
Convex limiting

The low-order and high-order methods can be convenient rewritten as

\displaystyle m_{i}\big(\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}\big)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}=\bm{0}\ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ m_{i}\big(\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}\big)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}=\bm{0}

where the algebraic fluxes
\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}
are defined as

\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\big(\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})+\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})\big)\mathbf{c}_{ij}-\tau_{n}d_{ij}^{\textrm{L},n}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\big(\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n})+\bm{\mathbb{f}}(\bm{u}_{i}^{n})\big)\mathbf{c}_{ij}-\tau_{n}d_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}(\bm{u}_{j}^{n}-\bm{u}_{i}^{n})

\displaystyle\qquad+(m_{ij}-\delta_{ij}m_{i})(\bm{u}_{j}^{\textrm{H},n+1}-\bm{u}_{j}^{n}-\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}+\bm{u}_{i}^{n}).

We define the algebraic flux-corrections
\mathbf{A}_{ij}=\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{L}}-\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\textrm{H}}
and set the final flux-limited solution to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{i}\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}=m_{i}\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\ell_{ij}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\end{split}

(61)

where
\ell_{ij}\in[0,1]
are the limiters. If
\ell_{ij}\equiv 0

for all
i
and
j
, then (
61
) recovers
\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}=\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}
. Similarly, if
\ell_{ij}\equiv 1

for all
i
and
j
then (
61
) leads to
\bm{u}_{i}^{n+1}=\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}
. The goal is to select limiters as large as it could be
possible while also preserving important bounds.

We want to enforce local bounds on the density and local minimum principle of the
specific entropy. However, logarithmic entropies, such as
s(\bm{u})=\ln\frac{p(\bm{u})}{\rho^{\gamma}}
, are not friendly in the context
Newton-like line search iterative methods. Therefore, we use
\tilde{s}(\bm{u})=\rho^{-\gamma}\varepsilon(\bm{u})
which leads to an entirely equivalent minimum
principle since

\displaystyle s(\bm{u})\leq s(\bm{v})\ \Leftrightarrow\ \tilde{s}(\bm{u})\leq\tilde{s}(\bm{v})\ \text{for all}\ \bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathcal{A}\,,

due to the monotonicity of
\ln x
. Therefore, at each node
i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}
we compute the bounds:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\text{min}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{1}_{h}^{-}\min_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\min\{\rho_{j}^{n},\overline{\rho}_{ij}^{n}\}

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\text{max}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{1}_{h}^{+}\max_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\max\{\rho_{j}^{n},\overline{\rho}_{ij}^{n}\}

\displaystyle\tilde{s}_{i}^{\text{min}}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{1}_{h}^{-}\min_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\min\{\tilde{s}_{j}^{n},\overline{\tilde{s}}_{ij}^{n}\}

where
\overline{\rho}_{ij}^{n}
denotes the density of the bar-state

\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n}
(see expression (
56
)), while

\overline{\tilde{s}}_{ij}^{n}:=\tilde{s}(\overline{\bm{u}}_{ij}^{n})
. Here

\mathbb{1}_{h}^{-}
and
\mathbb{1}_{h}^{+}
are just ad-hoc relaxations of the
unity with a prescribed decay rate with respect to the local meshsize
h
. More
precisely we consider

\displaystyle\mathbb{1}_{h}^{-}=1-\kappa(\tfrac{m_{i}}{|\Omega|})^{\frac{p}{d}}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathbb{1}_{h}^{+}=1+\kappa(\tfrac{m_{i}}{|\Omega|})^{\frac{p}{d}}\ \ \text{with}\ p=1.50,\ d=2.0,\ \text{and}\ \kappa=4.0\,.

We mention in passing that, asymptotically for
h\rightarrow 0
, the value of

\kappa
is has no importance and we may use any other
\kappa=\mathcal{O}(1)
. At each node
i\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbb{V}}
we define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}=\big\{\bm{u}=[\rho,\bm{m},E]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+2}\,\big|\,\rho_{i}^{\text{min}}\leq\rho\leq\rho_{i}^{\text{max}},\tilde{s}(\bm{u})\geq\tilde{s}_{i}^{\text{min}}\big\}

We note that (
61
) can be conveniently rewritten as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}(i)}\lambda_{i}(\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}+\ell_{ij}\mathbf{P}_{ij})\ \ \text{where}\ \ \lambda_{i}=\tfrac{1}{\text{card}\mathcal{I}(i)-1}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \mathbf{P}_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{i}m_{i}}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\end{split}

(62)

Convex-limiting is built on the observation that condition
\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{H},n+1}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
will hold if
\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1}+\ell_{ij}\mathbf{P}_{ij}\in\mathcal{B}_{i}
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}(i)
. Therefore, at
each node
i
we compute the preliminary limiters
l_{ij}
as

\displaystyle l_{ij}:=\texttt{compute\_line\_search}(\bm{u}_{i}^{\textrm{L},n+1},\mathbf{P}_{ij},\rho_{i}^{\text{min}},\rho_{i}^{\text{max}},\tilde{s}_{i}^{\text{min}})

with
compute_line_search
as defined in Algorithm

4
, while the final limiters are computed as

\ell_{ij}=\min\{l_{ij},l_{ji}\}
in order to guarantee conservation
properties of the scheme, see
[
14
,
13
,
25
]
for
both theory and implementation detail.

Algorithm 4

compute_line_search
(
\bm{u},\mathbf{P},\varrho^{\textit{min}},\varrho^{\textit{max}},\tilde{s}^{\textit{min}}
)

\displaystyle\ell^{\rho,\textit{min}}:=\max\{\ell\in[0,1]\,|\,\rho(\bm{u}+\ell\mathbf{P})\geq\varrho^{\textit{min}}\}

\displaystyle\ell^{\rho,\textit{max}}:=\max\{\ell\in[0,\ell^{\rho,\textit{min}}]\,|\,\rho(\bm{u}+\ell\mathbf{P})\leq\varrho^{\textit{max}}\}

\displaystyle\ell^{s}:=\max\{\ell\in[0,\ell^{\rho,\textit{max}}]\,|\,\tilde{s}(\bm{u}+\ell\mathbf{P})\geq\tilde{s}^{\textit{min}}\}

\displaystyle\texttt{Return:}\ell^{s}

Comments: input arguments are
\bm{u},\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\varrho^{\textit{min}},\varrho^{\textit{max}},\tilde{s}^{\textit{min}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
