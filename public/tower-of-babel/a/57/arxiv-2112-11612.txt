High-Field Magnetometry with Hyperpolarized Nuclear Spins
Ozgur Sahin, Erica de Leon Sanchez, Sophie Conti, Amala Akkiraju, Paul Reshetikhin, Emanuel Druga, Aakriti Aggarwal, Benjamin Gilbert, Sunil Bhave, Ashok Ajoy

Abstract
Quantum sensors have attracted broad interest in the quest towards sub-micronscale NMR spectroscopy. Such sensors predominantly operate at low magnetic fields. Instead, however, for high resolution spectroscopy, the high-field regime is naturally advantageous because it allows high absolute chemical shift discrimination. Here we propose and demonstrate a high-field spin magnetometer constructed from an ensemble of hyperpolarized C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} nuclear spins in diamond. The C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} nuclei are initialized via Nitrogen Vacancy (NV) centers and protected along a transverse Bloch sphere axis for minute-long periods. When exposed to a time-varying (AC) magnetic field, they undergo secondary precessions that carry an imprint of its frequency and amplitude. The method harnesses long rotating frame C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} sensor lifetimes T 2 ′ > T_{2}^{\prime}{>} 20s, and their ability to be continuously interrogated. For quantum sensing at 7T and a single crystal sample, we demonstrate spectral resolution better than 100 mHz (corresponding to a frequency precision < {<} 1ppm) and single-shot sensitivity better than 70pT. We discuss advantages of nuclear spin magnetometers over conventional NV center sensors, including deployability in randomly-oriented diamond particles and in optically scattering media. Since our technique employs densely-packed C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} nuclei as sensors, it demonstrates a new approach for magnetometry in the “coupled-sensor” limit. This work points to interesting opportunities for microscale NMR chemical sensors constructed from hyperpolarized nanodiamonds and suggests applications of dynamic nuclear polarization (DNP) in quantum sensing.

I
Introduction

The discrimination of chemical analytes with sub-micron scale spatial resolution is an important frontier in Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectroscopy
Sidles 2009
;
Meriles 2005
;
Degen et al. 2009
. Quantum sensing methods have attracted attention as a pathway to accomplish these goals
Degen et al. 2017
. These are typified by sensors constructed from the Nitrogen Vacancy (NV) defect center in diamond
Jelezko and Wrachtrup 2006
;
Manson et al. 2006
– electrons that can be optically initialized and interrogated
Gali et al. 2008
;
Doherty et al. 2012
, and made to report on nuclear spins in their environment. However, NV sensors are still primarily restricted to bulk crystals and operation at
low
magnetic fields (
B_{0}{<}
0.3 T)
Maze et al. 2008
;
Boss et al. 2017
;
Schmitt et al. 2017
;
Glenn et al. 2018
. Instead, for applications in NMR, high fields are naturally advantageous because the chemical shift dispersion is larger, and analyte nuclei carry higher polarization
Aslam et al. 2017
. The challenge of accessing this regime arises from the rapidly scaling electronic magnetogyric ratio
\gamma_{e}
, that makes electronic control difficult at high fields
Smits et al. 2019
;
Fortman et al. 2020
. Simultaneously, precise field alignment
Rondin et al. 2014
is required to obtain viable NV spin-readout contrast
Tetienne et al. 2012
. The latter has also made nanodiamond (particulate) magnetometers challenging at high fields. If viable, such sensors could yield avenues for “
targetable
” NMR detectors that are sensitive to analyte chemical shifts
Mochalin et al. 2012
;
Fu et al. 2007
;
Nie et al. 2021
, and provide a sub-micron scale spatial resolution determined by particle size
Glenn et al. 2018
;
Meriles 2005
;
Schaffry et al. 2011
. In this paper we propose alternate approach towards overcoming these challenges. We construct a magnetometer out of hyperpolarized
nuclear
spins (See Fig.
1
):
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei in the diamond serve as the primary magnetic field sensors, while NV centers instead play a supporting role in optically initializing them
Fischer et al. 2013
;
Ajoy et al. 2018a
.

The advantages of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei as sensors stem from their attractive properties. Their low
\gamma_{n}{\approx}{\gamma_{e}}/3000
, enables control and interrogation at high fields (
B_{0}{>}
1 T). In contrast to NV electronic spins, transitions of spin-1/2
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei are determined solely by magnetic field and not influenced by crystal lattice orientation
Ajoy et al. 2018a
;
Ajoy et al. 2020a
. They have long rotating frame lifetimes
T_{2}^{\prime}

Beatrez et al. 2021
, orders of magnitude greater than their NV center counterparts. Similarly, their longitudinal lifetimes
T_{1}{>}
10 min
Rej et al. 2015
;
Ajoy et al. 2019
are long even at modest fields. This can allow a physical separation between field regions corresponding to
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
initialization and sensing, and for the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensors to be transported between them
Ajoy et al. 2019
.
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei can be non-destructively readout via RF techniques
Ajoy et al. 2020b
, allowing continuous sensor interrogation without reinitialization. This allows real-time tracking of a changing magnetic field for extended periods
Shao et al. 2016
. RF
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
readout is also background-free and immune to optical scattering
Lv et al. 2021
;
Pillai et al.
, permitting deployment in real-world media.

While these properties appear attractive at first glance, nuclear spins are often considered ineffective as quantum sensors. The low
\gamma_{n}
, while ideal for high field operation, would be expected to yield low sensitivity
Degen et al. 2017
, and poor state purity (thermal polarization is
{\approx}10^{-5}
even at 7 T). Strong (
\sim
1 kHz) dipolar coupling between
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei makes Ramsey-like sensing protocols untenable. This results in a rapid free induction decay (FID)
T_{2}^{\ast}{<}
2 ms
Ajoy et al. 2020b
, and limits sensor integration time.

Here, we demonstrate that these shortcomings can be mitigated. We exploit optical hyperpolarization of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei (Fig.
1
A)
Fischer et al. 2013
;
London et al. 2013
;
Ajoy et al. 2018a
;
Schwartz et al. 2018
and spin-lock readout scheme that suppresses evolution under dipolar interactions
Beatrez et al. 2021
;
Rhim et al. 1976
. The resulting
{>}
10,000-fold extension in
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
lifetimes, from
T_{2}^{*}{\rightarrow}T_{2}^{\prime}
, provides the basis for expanding sensor readout to minute long periods
Beatrez et al. 2021
. These long
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
rotating-frame lifetimes can at least partially offset sensitivity losses arising from the low
\gamma_{n}
. The sensing strategy is described in Fig.
1
B. Hyperpolarized
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei are placed along the transverse axis
\hat{\textbf{x}}
(red arrow) on the Bloch sphere at high field, where they are preserved for multiple-second long
T_{2}â\texteuro\texttrademark
periods
Beatrez et al. 2021
. Any subsequent deviation of the spin state from
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane can be continuously monitored and constitutes the magnetometer signal. In the presence of the target magnetic field,
\textbf{B}_{\textrm{ac}}(t){=}B_{\textrm{AC}}\cos(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t+\phi_{0})\hat{\textbf{z}}
at a frequency
f_{\textrm{AC}}
, the nuclei undergo a secondary precession in the
\hat{\textbf{y}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{z}}
plane that carries an imprint of
f_{\textrm{AC}}
. Long
T_{2}^{\prime}
yields high spectral resolution.

II
Results and Discussion

As a proof of concept, experiments here are conducted on a single-crystal diamond, but can be extended to powders. The sample has
{\sim}
1 ppm NV center concentration and natural abundance
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
. Hyperpolarization occurs through a method previously described at 38 mT
Ajoy et al. 2018a
;
Ajoy et al. 2018b

2

III
Theory

We now turn to a theoretical description of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensor operation. We outline two complementary viewpoints to explain the observations in Fig.
4
and Fig.
5
: a first picture equivalent to DD quantum sensing, and second using an “rotating-frame” NMR experiment analogue. Consider first that the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
Hamiltonian in the lab-frame is,
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Z}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Z}}{=}w_{\textrm{L}}I_{z}
is the Zeeman Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}{=}\sum_{k<\ell}b_{k\ell}(3I_{kz}I_{\ell z}-\vec{I_{k}}\cdot\vec{I_{\ell}})
is the interspin dipolar interaction and
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}{=}\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}\cos(2\pi f_{\textrm{{AC}}}t+\phi_{0})I_{z}
is the applied AC field. Here
I
refer to spin-1/2 Pauli matrices,
w_{\textrm{L}}
is the nuclear Larmor frequency,
\phi_{0}
is the initial (arbitrary) phase of the AC field, and we estimate the median dipolar coupling
J{=}\left\langle b_{k\ell}\right\rangle{\approx}
663Hz. The spins are prepared initially along
\hat{\textbf{x}}
in a state
\rho(0){\sim}\varepsilon I_{x}
, where
\varepsilon{\approx}0.2\%
is the hyperpolarization level.

The sequence in Fig.
2
A can be conveniently treated in the rotating frame by average Hamiltonian theory (AHT)
Haeberlen 1976
. After
N
pulses, its action can be described by the unitary,
U(N\tau){=}\left[\exp(i\vartheta I_{x})\exp(i\mathcal{H}\tau)\right]^{N}
, where we assume
\delta
-pulses for simplicity. The signal obtained following the procedure in Fig.
2
then simply corresponds to the measurement of
[\left\langle I_{x}\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle I_{y}\right\rangle^{2}]^{1/2}
, or equivalently the spin survival probability in the
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane. This evolution can be expressed as,
U(N\tau){=}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\exp(i\mathcal{H}^{(j)}\tau)
, where
\mathcal{H}^{(j)}
are toggling frame Hamiltonians after every pulse
Haeberlen 1976
,
\mathcal{H}^{(j)}{=}\exp(ij\vartheta I_{x})\mathcal{H}\exp(-ij\vartheta I_{x})
. For time
t{=}N\tau
, this can be recast as,
U(t){=}\exp(i\mathcal{H}_{F}N\tau)
, where
\mathcal{H}_{F}
captures the effective system dynamics under the pulses. To leading order in parameter
\zeta{=}2\pi J\tau
in a Magnus expansion
Magnus 1954
;
Wilcox 1967
;
Blanes et al. 2009
, and assuming
\zeta{\ll}1
, the dynamics can be captured by an average Hamiltonian,
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{(j)}
.

Since non-commuting effects do not affect the leading order term, we can consider the effect of each of the dipolar part and the AC field separately,
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{F}{=}{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{dd}}+{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{AC}}
. For the former, we have
Ajoy et al. 2020b
,

{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{dd}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}^{(j)}_{\textrm{dd}}\approx\sum_{j<k}d_{jk}^{\textrm{CC}}\left(\frac{3}{2}\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{ff}}-\vec{I_{j}}\cdot\vec{I_{k}}\right),

(2)

with the flip-flop Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{ff}}=I_{jz}I_{kz}+I_{jy}I_{ky}
. The initial state
\rho(0)
is conserved under
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{dd}}
, since
[\rho(0){,}{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{dd}}]{=}0
. This leads to a quasi-equilibriation of spins along
\hat{\textbf{x}}
, with a lifetime that scales with a power law of the pulsing frequency
\tau^{-1}

Beatrez et al. 2021
. This is the red signal in Fig.
3
A (here
\zeta{=}
0.19). We note that for sufficiently small
\zeta
, Eq. (
2
) is valid for arbitrary flip-angle
\vartheta
, except for certain special values (
\vartheta{\approx}\pi,2\pi
).

A similar AHT analysis can be carried out for the
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}
term. Consider first a DC field (
f_{\textrm{AC}}{=}0
) and
\vartheta{=}\pi/2
. Fig.
2
C shows the toggling frame Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}^{(j)}_{\textrm{AC}}
, which consists only of single body terms and hence can be plotted in a phasor representation. In a cycle consisting of four pulses (required to complete a 2
\pi
rotation), the average Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{AC}}{=}0
, evident from the symmetrically distributed phasors in Fig.
4
(i)
. Hence the DC field is decoupled. Alternately, consider the resonant AC case (
f_{\textrm{AC}}{=}f_{\textrm{res}}
). The analysis here is simplest to carry out assuming a square-wave (as opposed to sinusoidal) field. In this situation, the phasor diagram is asymmetrical and the average Hamiltonian after four pulses,
{\mathcal{H}}^{(0)}_{\textrm{AC}}{\propto}-I_{y}
. When stroboscopically observed, the spins rotate away from
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane; this yields the dip in the integrated signal in Fig.
4
.

An illuminating alternate viewpoint is obtained by noting that under spin-locking the spins are requantized in the rotating frame with an effective field,
\Omega_{\textrm{eff}}=\Omega\left(\frac{t_{p}}{\tau}\right)
, where the factor in brackets is the pulsing duty cycle, and
\Omega
is the Rabi frequency. The resonance frequency identified above is then exactly,
f_{\textrm{res}}{=}\Omega_{\textrm{eff}}
. Therefore the experiment can cast as an rotating-frame
analogue
of a conventional (lab-frame) NMR experiment: the spins are quantized along
\hat{\textbf{x}}
,
\Omega_{\textrm{eff}}
serves analogous to the Larmor frequency, and at the resonance condition (
f_{\textrm{AC}}{=}f_{\textrm{res}}
),
B_{\textrm{AC}}
is the effective Rabi frequency. Spins initially prepared along
\hat{\textbf{x}}
, are then constantly tipped away from this axis by
f_{\textrm{AC}}
. The dip in Fig.
4
reflects this tilt away from the
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane. For the resonant case, the trajectory of the spins in the rotating frame can be simply written as,

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{x}\cos(\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}t)+I_{y}\sin(\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}t)\sin(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t)

(3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle I_{z}\sin(\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}t)\cos(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t)\>.

In effect, the spins are undergoing a “secondary” precession in the rotating frame around
\hat{\textbf{x}}
at frequency
\Omega_{\textrm{eff}}
. For each point on this motion, they are also precessing in the lab frame at
w_{\textrm{L}}
. The latter yields the inductive signal measured in the raw data in Fig.
2
B. Upon taking a Fourier transform (Fig.

\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane, which has the form,

S(t){=}\left[\cos^{2}(\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}t)+\sin^{2}(\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}t)\sin^{2}(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t)\right]^{1/2}
. This is the signal measured in Fig.
3

f_{\textrm{AC}}
and
2f_{\textrm{AC}}
, corresponding to the observed first and second harmonics respectively. While this analysis was for the resonant case, it is simple to extend it to off-resonant AC fields (see SI  ​
XIII
). Once again the oscillations can be demonstrated to be at exact harmonics of
f_{\textrm{AC}}
. Alternatively, if the FT phase (instead of magnitude) was taken in Fig.

\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane in the rotating frame, Eq. (
3
) indicates there will once again be oscillations. Here however, all the intensity will be in the primary harmonic, and for sufficiently small fields
\gamma_{n}|B_{\textrm{AC}}|{\ll}f_{\textrm{AC}}
, the oscillatory signal scales
{\propto}|B_{\textrm{AC}}|
, and can provide higher sensitivity. Extracting these oscillations comes with complications related to unwrapping the phase every

Overall Eq. (

IV
Discussion of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensor performance and special features

We now elucidate parameters of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensor performance and discuss some of its special features (see SI
SOM
for a summary). Our emphasis in this paper was not to optimize sensitivity, but through experiments similar to Fig.
4
(detailed in SI  ​
XV
) for a bias field
B_{0}{=}7
T, we obtain a single-shot sensitivity of
760\pm 127
pT
/\sqrt{\textrm{Hz}}
via measurement of signal amplitude, and
410\pm 90
pT
/\sqrt{\textrm{Hz}}
via measurement of signal phase respectively. This neglects hyperpolarization time because a sensor initialization event can allow interrogation for several minutes
Beatrez et al. 2021
. A single-shot of the measurement (for
t{=}34
s) can detect a minimum field of
{\approx}
70 pT. While the sensitivity here is lower than NV quantum sensors at low field, we emphasize that this corresponds to an exquisite AC field precision of
{\sim}10^{-11}
over the 7 T bias field. As such, the sensitivity here is competitive with respect to other high-field sensors (see SI  ​
XVII
for a detailed comparison), while also allowing advantages in the multiplexed sensing of AC fields that is not feasible via other sensor technologies at high field (SI  ​
XVII
).

Sensitivity itself can be significantly enhanced. Sample filling factor (presently
\eta{\approx}
10%) and the hyperpolarization level (
{\approx}
0.3%) can both be boosted by close to an order of magnitude. Employing a 10%
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
enriched sample would provide a further ten-fold gain in inductive signal strength
Ajoy et al. 2020b
. From this, we estimate a sensitivity approaching 2 nT
/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}
is feasible in a (10
\mu
m)
3
volume, sufficient to measure fields from precessing
1
H nuclei in the same volume at high field
Glenn et al. 2018
.

The frequency resolution of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensor is
\delta f{\approx}{1}/{N\tau}
. Currently, Fig.
5
demonstrates a resolution better than 100mHz. However, finite memory limitations restricted capturing the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
Larmor precession here to
t{<}35
s (Fig.
5
A). Overcoming these memory limits can allow acquisition of the entire spin-lock decay, lasting over 573 s
Beatrez et al. 2021
. Under these conditions, we estimate a frequency resolution of 2.2 mHz is feasible. This would correspond to a frequency precision of 3 ppt at a 7 T bias field, more than sufficient precision to discriminate chemical shifts. On the other hand, sensor bandwidth
\mathcal{B}{=}1/2\tau
is determined by the minimum interpulse delay (see Fig.
4
C). Current Rabi frequencies limit bandwidth to
{\sim}
20 kHz. We estimate that improving filling-factor
\eta
and RF coil Q factor (presently 50), could increase
\mathcal{B}
further to
{\approx}500
kHz. The sensor strategy lends itself to a wide operating field range. While our experiments were carried out at 7 T, the slow scaling
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
magnetogyric ratio makes sensing viable even for fields
{\gtrsim}
24 T. This greatly expands the field range for spin sensors, where the operating field is predominantly
{<}
0.3 T (notable exceptions are Refs.
Aslam et al. 2017
;
Fortman et al. 2020
, but require complex instrumentation).

We emphasize the robustness of our sensing method to pulse error. It can be operated with any flip angle
\vartheta{\neq}\pi

Ajoy et al. 2020b
. Moreover,
f_{\textrm{res}}
has a relatively wide profile
{\approx}
230 Hz (see Fig.
4
A), meaning that flip-angle (RF) inhomogeneity has little impact. These features are responsible for the
{>}275
k pulses applied to the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spins in experiments in this work.

Finally, we note some special features of our magnetometry protocol. Compared to previous work
Granwehr et al. 2005
using hyperpolarized gaseous
129
Xe nuclei as sensors, our experiments employed hyperpolarized nuclear spins in solids. This provides natural advantages due to an ability for in-situ replenishment of hyperpolarization at the sensing site. Moreover, multiple AC fields can be discerned in a Fourier reconstruction in a single-shot, as opposed to point-by-point
Granwehr et al. 2005
.

Fundamentally, experiments here illustrate the feasibility of quantum sensing in the
coupled sensor
limit
Zhou et al. 2020
;
Dooley et al. 2018
, making the spins sensitive to external fields while negating the effect of intersensor interactions. Sensor operation exploits “Floquet prethermalization”
Else and Nayak 2016
;
Khemani et al. 2016
;
Howell et al. 2019
;
Ueda 2020
– quasi-equilibrium nuclear states under periodic driving
Beatrez et al. 2021
. As such, this provides a compelling demonstration of exploiting stable non-equilibrium phases
Else and Nayak 2016
;
Khemani et al. 2016
;
Luitz et al. 2020
for sensing applications.

From a technological perspective, RF interrogated sensing, as described here, presents advantages in scattering environments
Lv et al. 2021
(see SI  ​
XVI
). All data in this paper are carried out with the diamond immersed in
{\sim}
4mL water, over 2000-fold the volume of the sample. Traditional NV sensors are ineffective in this regime due to scattering losses and concomitant fluorescence fluctuations. Similarly, optically hyperpolarized sensors present advantages because majority of the sensor volume can be illuminated by the impinging lasers, because there are no geometrical constraints from requirements of collection optics. In our experiments, we employed an array of low-cost laser diode sources
Sarkar et al. 2021
for hyperpolarization that illuminates the sample almost isotropically. This allows recruiting a larger volume of spins for sensing with a low cost overhead
Sarkar et al. 2021
. Extension to powder samples could be advantageous for optimally packing a sensor volume.

V
Outlook

The work presented here can be extended in several promising directions. First, the high resolution-to-bandwidth ratio (
\delta f/\mathcal{B}{\approx}10^{6}
) possible via
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensing at high fields (see Fig.
5
), suggests possibilities for detecting chemical shifts from precessing analyte nuclei external to the diamond. Seminal work by Warren et al.
Warren et al. 1993
;
Richter et al. 1995
and Bowtell
Bowtell 1992
showed that nuclear spins of one species (“sensor”) could be used to indirectly probe NMR information of other physically separated (“analyte”) nuclei. This indirect NMR detection strategy is appealing as the absolute dimensions diminish to sub-micron length scales
Meriles 2005
. Building on these ideas, we envision the possibility of micro-scale NMR detectors using hyperpolarized
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei in nanodiamond (ND) particles. These experiments require control applied to the target nuclei in order to heterodyne chemical shifts to within the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensor bandwidth
Mamin et al. 2013
;
Meinel et al. 2021
, and only one-half of the particle surfaces to be exposed to the analyte
Warren et al. 1993
. We will explore these experiments in a future manuscript.

Second, while the current experiments exploit a low-field DNP mechanism for the initialization of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensors, interesting opportunities arise from employing complementary
all-optical
DNP techniques that operates directly at high field
King et al. 2010
;
Scott et al. 2016
. This will allow in-situ sensors at high field without the need for sample shuttling.

More broadly, this work suggests an interesting applications of DNP for quantum sensing. It is intriguing to consider sensor platforms constructed in optically-active molecular systems
Bayliss et al. 2020
;
Yu et al. 2021
where abundant, long-lived nuclear spins, can be initialized via interactions with electronic spin centers. Finally, we envision technological applications of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
sensors described here for bulk magnetometry
Le Sage et al. 2012
, sensors underwater and in scattering media, and for spin gyroscopes
Ajoy and Cappellaro 2012
;
Ledbetter et al. 2012
;
Jaskula et al. 2019
;
Jarmola et al.
.

VI
Conclusions

We have proposed and demonstrated a high-field magnetometry approach with hyperpolarized
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclear spins in diamond. Sensing leveraged long transverse spin
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
lifetimes and their ability to be continuously interrogated, while mitigating effects due to interspin interaction. We demonstrated magnetometry with high frequency resolution (
{<}
100mHz), high field precision (
{\sim}10^{-11}
), and high-field (7T) operation, yielding advantages over counterpart NV sensors in this regime. This work opens avenues for NMR sensors at high fields, and opens new and interesting possibilities for employing dynamic nuclear polarization for quantum sensing.

XII
Average Hamiltonian Analysis

Here we present a more detailed average Hamiltonian analysis for the operation of the magnetometry sequence. For the majority of this section, unless otherwise explicitly stated, we treat the system in the lab frame. The total Hamiltonian of the system is
\mathcal{H}{=}\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Z}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}+\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}
where
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{Z}}{=}w_{\textrm{L}}I_{z}
is the Zeeman Hamiltonian,
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}{=}\sum_{k<\ell}b_{k\ell}(3I_{kz}I_{\ell z}-\overrightarrow{I_{k}}\cdot\overrightarrow{I_{\ell}})
corresponds to the dipolar interaction,
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{AC}}{=}\gamma_{n}B_{\textrm{AC}}\cos(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t+\phi_{0})I_{z}
is applied field to be sensed,
w_{\textrm{L}}=\gamma_{n}B_{0}
is the Larmor frequency, and
B_{\textrm{AC}}
,
w_{\textrm{AC}}
, and
\phi_{0}
are the applied AC field amplitude, frequency, and phase respectively.

We will simplify the dipolar Hamiltonian, separate from the rest of the terms, using average Hamiltonian theory (AHT) by only keeping the leading-order term. These assumptions are reasonable under the condition
\zeta{=}2\pi J\tau{\ll}1
. For each pulse period, we will treat the system in the toggling frame defined by the pulses up to that point. For the period after the
j^{\textrm{th}}
pulse, the toggling frame transformation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}^{(j)}=\exp\big(-ij\vartheta I_{x}\big)\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}\exp\big(ij\vartheta I_{x}\big),

(4)

Consider the case when
\vartheta{=}\frac{\pi}{2}
. In this case, the toggling frame wraps to the original frame after a period of
4\tau
. For a multiple period of
4\tau
then, the system dynamics is captured by the average Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}^{(0)}=\sum\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}^{(j)}=\sum_{j<k}b_{kl}\bigg(\frac{3}{2}(I_{jz}I_{kz}+I_{jy}I_{ky})-\vec{I_{j}}\cdot\vec{I_{k}}\bigg).

(5)

Higher order AHT terms are evaluated in detail in Ref.
Ajoy et al. 2020b
. The initial state
\rho(0){=}I_{x}
is protected against dipolar coupling because
[\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{dd}}^{(0)},\rho(0)]{=}0
. Since the state is protected over an average, we will neglect the dipolar term in all of the following sections.

We now treat the external field Hamiltonian,
H_{\textrm{AC}}
using the same method, once again assuming
\vartheta{=}\pi/2
. A DC field, whose lab frame Hamiltonian is given by
H_{\textrm{AC}}{=}\gamma_{n}BI_{z}
, will be rotated by
\pi/2
by each toggling frame transformation such that over a four pulse period, the Hamiltonian will average to 0. It is possible to map the toggling frame Hamiltonians for each pulse period on a phasor in order to see this more clearly (Fig.
4
C). For this case, the toggling frame Hamiltonians will cover the phasor symmetrically such that the vectoral sum on the phasor will be 0. This also works for the more general case
\vartheta{=}\frac{2\pi k}{n}
for integers
k
and
n
, as the points will trace a regular polygon on the phasor, which will still vanish when summed.

However, for a resonant AC field (assumed to be a square wave for simplicity) with frequency
f_{\textrm{AC}}{=}1/4\tau
, the average Hamiltonian is a linear combination of
I_{y}
and
I_{z}
(for the special cases where the AC field phase is
\phi{=}-\frac{\pi}{6}
or
\frac{5\pi}{6}
, the
I_{z}
terms cancel out but there is a net
I_{y}
term). For any AC field with
f_{\textrm{AC}}{\neq}f_{\textrm{res}}
, the external field Hamiltonian will average to zero, albeit over a longer timescale.

\mathcal{H}

\Delta\tau_{j}{=}(\tau_{j+1}-\tau_{j})

(i)

(ii)
