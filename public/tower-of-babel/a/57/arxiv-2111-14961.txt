Parsimony and the rank of a flattening matrix
Jandre Snyman, Colin Fox, David Bryant

Abstract
The standard models of sequence evolution on a tree determine probabilities for every character or site pattern. A flattening is an arrangement of these probabilities into a matrix, with rows corresponding to all possible site patterns for one set A A of taxa and columns corresponding to all site patterns for another set B B of taxa. Flattenings have been used to prove difficult results relating to phylogenetic invariants and consistency and also form the basis of several methods of phylogenetic inference. We prove that the rank of the flattening equals r ℓ T ​ ( A | B ) r^{\ell_{T}(A|B)} , when T T is binary, r r is the number of states and ℓ T ​ ( A | B ) \ell_{T}(A|B) is the parsimony length of the binary character separating A A and B B . A similar formula holds for non-binary trees. These results correct an earlier published formula. Since completing this work, we have learnt that an equivalent result has been proved much earlier by Casanellas and Fernández-Sánchez [ 11 ] , using a completely different proof strategy.

An important property of flattenings from phylogenies is their rank. Suppose that
e
is an edge in a phylogeny. Removing
e
partitions the tree, and hence the set of leaves, into two parts, inducing a partition
A|B
of the set of taxa. We say that
A|B
is a split of the tree corresponding to edge
e
. Allman and Rhodes
[
6
]
(Proposition 11) proved that, under minor assumptions, if
A|B
is a split in the tree then the rank of
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
is at most
r
, while if
A|B
is
not
a split of the tree then the rank of
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
is at least
r^{2}
.

Because of this property, flattenings and their ranks have played a prominent role in the mathematics of phylogenetics, particularly with respect to the development and construction of
phylogenetic invariants

[
7
,
3
,
17
]
. Roughly speaking, a phylogenetic invariant for a tree is a function on vectors of pattern probabilities such as (
1
) which is zero when the probability distribution comes from that tree and non-zero otherwise.

Flattenings have also led to methods for inferring phylogenies directly. In an original and influential chapter, Eriksson
[
14
]
outlines an efficient method for inferring phylogenies with few assumptions about the evolutionary process. The SVD quartets method
[
13
]
uses flattenings to infer trees for subsets of four taxa, subsequently assembling these four-taxa trees into one for the complete set of taxa. The method is statistically consistent even in the presence of incomplete lineage sorting. Quartet-based approaches based on flattening have also been developed by
[
16
,
12
]
. Allman, Kubako and Rhodes propose the ranks of flattenings as a measure of support for different edges in a phylogeny
[
2
]
.

In this paper we extend and complete the theorem of Allman and Rhodes and derive an exact formula for the rank of arbitrary flattenings of a tree. Our result corrects a formula appearing in
[
14
]
. We show that the rank of
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
is given by
r^{\ell_{T}(A|B)}
, where
\ell_{T}(A|B)
is the parsimony length of the binary character which assigns one state to all leaves in
A
and another state to all leaves in
B
. A slightly modified result holds for non-binary (multifurcating) trees.

In the next section we present the notation and basic results needed to establish this result. We note that we generalise from characters to maps on arbitrary subsets of vertices, and define our concepts in this more general context. The main result is proved in a series of lemmas and propositions in the final section.

Since completing this work, we have learnt that an equivalent result has been proved much earlier by Casanellas and Fernández-Sánchez
[
11
]
, using a completely different proof strategy.

Background

2.1
Trees and characters

An
unrooted phylogeny
is an acyclic, connected graph
T=(V,E)
with leaf set (taxon set)
L(T)
. We say that
T
is binary (full resolved) if every non-leaf vertex has degree three. In a
rooted phylogeny

T=(V,E_{\rho})
one vertex is selected as the root
\rho
and edges are directed away from
\rho
. The rooted phylogeny
T
is binary if every non-leaf vertex has out-degree
2
.

A
character
is a function
f
from
L(T)
to a set of states
[r]=\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
. Hence
r=4
for nucleotides and
r=20
for amino acids. We will consider a more general situation where
f
is a map from any subset of
V
to
[r]
, not just the set
L(T)
.

The
length
of a function
F:V\rightarrow[r]
is defined as

\ell_{T}(F)=|\left\{\{u,v\}\in E:F(u)\neq F(v)\right\}|

and the (parsimony) length a function
f
with domain
A\subseteq V
is the length of a minimum extension

\ell_{T}(f)=\min\{\ell_{T}(F):F|_{A}=f\}.

The length of a function or character can be expressed equivalently using vertex and edge cuts. For
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
or
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
we let
T\setminus E^{\prime}
and
T\setminus V^{\prime}
denote the graphs resulting from deleting
E^{\prime}
or
V^{\prime}
respectively.
The length
\ell_{T}(f)
equals the minimum cardinality of an edge cut
E^{\prime}\subseteq E
such that
f(u)=f(v)
whenever
u
and
v
are in the same component of
T\setminus E^{\prime}
. In a similar way, we let
\nu_{T}(f)
denote the minimum cardinality of a vertex cut
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
such that

f(u)=f(v)
whenever
u
and
v
are in the same component of
T\setminus V^{\prime}
. For all
f
we have
\nu_{T}(f)\leq\ell_{T}(f)
.

If
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
V
then we let
\ell_{T}(A|B)
and
\nu_{T}(A|B)
denote
\ell_{T}(f)
and
\nu_{T}(f)
, where
f
is the function given by

f(x)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{ if $x\in A$}\\
1&\mbox{ if $x\in B$.}\end{cases}

Steel
[
21
,
19
]
observed that the parsimony length of a character with two states can be expressed in terms of the size of disjoint path sets . This applies for both
\ell_{T}(A|B)
and
\nu_{T}(A|B)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be an unrooted phylogeny and let
A
and
B
be disjoint subsets of
V
. Then
\ell_{T}(A|B)
equals the maximum cardinality of a set of edge-disjoint paths connecting vertices in
A
with vertices in
B
, while
\nu_{T}(A|B)
equals the maximum cardinality of a set of vertex-disjoint paths connecting vertices in
A
with vertices in
B
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a binary phylogeny and suppose
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
L(T)
. Then
\ell_{T}(A|B)=\nu_{T}(A|B)
.

Proof.

If
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are any two paths in a binary phylogeny which begin and end at leaves then
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are vertex disjoint if and only if they are edge disjoint.

\blacksquare

2.2
Stochastic models on trees

The standard stochastic models on phylogenies determine the joint probabilities for the states at every vertex of the tree.
Let
T=(V,E_{\rho})
be a rooted tree with root
\rho
. Let
\pi_{\rho}
denote the root distribution and for each directed edge
(u,v)
we associate an
r\times r
transition probability matrix
P_{uv}
. Let
X_{v}
denote the random state associated with vertex
v
and, for
A\subseteq V
, let
X_{A}
denote the joint random variable
X_{a}:a\in A
.

The joint probability that
X_{v}=F(v)
for all
v\in V
is

\pi(X_{V}=F)=\pi_{\rho}(F(\rho))\prod_{(u,v)\in E_{\rho}}P_{uv}(F(u),F(v)).

The marginal probabilities for maps
f:A\rightarrow[r]
on some subset
A\subseteq V
are then given by

\pi(X_{A}=f)=\sum_{F:F|_{A}=f}\pi(F).

With this notation, the standard probability for a character
f:L(T)\rightarrow[r]
is
\pi(X_{L(T)}=f)
.

We will make three assumptions about the parameters of the stochastic model.

(C1)

The transition matrices
P_{uv}
are non-singular.

(C2)

\pi_{\rho}(i)>0
for all
i\in[r]
.

(C3)

P_{uv}(i,j)>0
for all edges
(u,v)
and
i,j\in[r]
.

We note that all three are satisfied in standard evolutionary models, where mutations along a branch are modelled using continuous time Markov chains.

These conditions have three immediate consequences.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that conditions (C1) and (C2) are satisfied. Then

1.

\pi(X_{v}=i)>0
for all
v\in V
and
i\in[r]
.

2.

For all
u,v\in V
(not necessarily adjacent) the
r\times r
matrix with entries
P_{uv}=\pi(X_{v}=j|X_{u}=i)
is non-singular.

3.

For all
u,v\in V
the
r\times r
matrix with entries
M_{ij}=\pi(X_{u}=i,X_{v}=j)
is non-singular.

Proof.

1.

See
[
20
]
, Theorem 2.

2.

Let
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
be the path from
v_{1}=u
to
v_{k}=v
. Then

P_{uv}=P_{v_{1}v_{2}}P_{v_{2}v_{3}}\cdots P_{v_{k-1}v_{k}},

which is non-singular by (C1).

3.

Let
\Pi_{u}
be the diagonal matrix with entries
\pi(X_{u}=i)
on the diagonal. Then
M=\Pi P_{uv}
so is non-singular by parts 1 and 2.

\blacksquare

For any edge
(u,v)
in the rooted tree
T=(V,E_{\rho})
we can define a transition matrix
P_{vu}
for the reverse edge
(v,u)
using Bayes’ rule:

\displaystyle P_{vu}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{u}=j|X_{v}=i)

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{v}=i|X_{u}=j)\frac{\pi(X_{u}=j)}{\pi(X_{v}=i)}

(3)

\displaystyle=P_{uv}(j,i)\frac{\pi(X_{u}=j)}{\pi(X_{v}=i)}.

This formula allows us to move the root to any vertex in the tree with no change to the distribution of joint random variables
X_{V}

[
4
,
20
]
. To move from the root from
\rho
to
\hat{\rho}
we make the new root distribution
\pi_{\hat{\rho}}=\pi(X_{\hat{\rho}})
and use (
3
) to determine transition matrices for all edges which are flipped when directing away from
\hat{\rho}
instead of from
\rho
(under the assumption that (C1) and (C2) both hold).

Conditional independence for the variables
X_{v}
are, as with any graphical model, determined by cuts in the graph. More specifically, if
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}
are the components of
T\setminus\{v\}
then
X_{A_{1}},X_{A_{2}},\ldots,X_{A_{k}}
are conditionally independent, given
X_{v}
, see
[
22
, Lemma 7.1]
. The following Proposition is a direct consequence of this result

Proposition 3
.

If
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
and
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}
are the components of
T\setminus\{V^{\prime}\}
then
X_{A_{1}},X_{A_{2}},\ldots,X_{A_{k}}
are conditionally independent, given
X_{V^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Apply Lemma 7.1 of
[
22
]
with respect to some
v\in V^{\prime}
and then apply the result recursively on the components.

\blacksquare

2.3
The main theorem

Let
T=(V,E)
be a phylogeny and suppose that
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
V
. We let
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
denote the matrix with rows indexed by maps
f_{A}:A\rightarrow[r]
, columns indexed by maps
f_{B}:B\rightarrow[r]
and
(f_{A},f_{B})
entry given by

\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}(f_{A},f_{B})=\pi(X_{A}=f_{A},X_{B}=f_{B}).

Hence
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
is an
r^{|A|}\times r^{|B|}
non-negative matrix with entries which sum to
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a phylogeny and let
A
and
B
be disjoint subsets of
V
. Suppose that the root distribution and transition matrices satisfy (C1)—(C3). Then

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})=r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}.

If
T
is binary and
A
and
B
subsets of
L(T)
then

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})=r^{\ell_{T}(A|B)}.

3
Proof of Theorem
1

We prove the main theorem in two steps. First, we show that
r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}
provides an
upper bound
for the rank of
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
; second we show that this upper bound is actually achieved.

Lemma 1
.

Let
T=(V,E)
be a phylogeny and suppose that
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
V
.
Then

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})\leq r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}.

Proof.

Let
C
be a minimum cardinality vertex cut of
T
such that each component of
T\setminus C
contains vertices from at most one of
A
or
B
. By Proposition
3

X_{A\setminus C}
and
X_{B\setminus C}
are conditionally independent given
X_{C}
. Hence for all maps
f_{A}:A\rightarrow[r]
and
f_{B}:B\rightarrow[r]
we can factor
\pi(X_{A}=f_{A},X_{B}=f_{B})
as

\displaystyle\pi(X_{A}=f_{A},X_{B}=f_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{C}}\pi(X_{A}=f_{A},X_{B}=f_{B}|X_{C}=f_{C})\pi(X_{C}=f_{C})

\displaystyle=\sum_{f_{C}}\pi(X_{A}=f_{A}|X_{C}=f_{C})\pi(X_{B}=f_{B},X_{C}=f_{C})

Define the
r^{|A|}\times r^{|C|}
matrix
R
by

R(f_{A},f_{C})=\pi(X_{A}=f_{A}|X_{C}=f_{C}),

and the
r^{|C|}\times r^{|B|}
matrix
S
by

S(f_{C},f_{B})=\pi(X_{B}=f_{B},X_{C}=f_{C}).

Then

\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}=RS

and

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})\leq\mathrm{rank}(R)\leq r^{|C|}=r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}.

\blacksquare

B^{\prime}\subseteq B
. Then

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}).

Proof.

For any
f_{A^{\prime}}:A^{\prime}\rightarrow[r]
and
f_{B^{\prime}}:B^{\prime}\rightarrow[r]
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}}(f_{A^{\prime}},f_{B^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{A^{\prime}}=f_{A^{\prime}},X_{B^{\prime}}=f_{B^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{A}:A\rightarrow[r]\\
g_{A}|_{A^{\prime}}=f_{A^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{B}:B\rightarrow[r]\\
g_{B}|_{B^{\prime}}=f_{B^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\pi(X_{A}=g_{A},X_{B}=g_{B})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{A}:A\rightarrow[r]\\
g_{A}|_{A^{\prime}}=f_{A^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{B}:B\rightarrow[r]\\
g_{B}|_{B^{\prime}}=f_{B^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}(g_{A},g_{B}).

Hence there is an
r^{|A^{\prime}|}\times r^{|A|}

0\!-\!1
(binary) matrix
U_{A}
and a
r^{|B^{\prime}|}\times r^{|B|}

0\!-\!1
matrix
U_{B}
such that

\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}}=U_{A}\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}U_{B}^{T}

and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})
.

\blacksquare

The next Lemma establishes Theorem
1
in the extremal case that
\nu_{T}(A|B)=|A|=|B|
. This is where the bulk of the work proving the main theorem is carried out.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
T=(V,E)
,
A
and
B
satisfy the conditions of Theorem
1
.
If
|A|=|B|=m
and there are
m
disjoint paths connecting elements of
A
to elements of
B
then
\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})=r^{m}
.

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
m
.

For the base case suppose that
m=1
,
A=\{a\}
and
B=\{b\}
. Then

\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}(i,j)=\pi(X_{a}=i,X_{B}=j)

which is full rank
r=r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}
by Proposition
2
.

Next, assume that the result holds when
|A|=|B|=m
. Suppose that there is a collection of
m+1
vertex disjoint paths connecting elements of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
, where
|A^{\prime}|=|B^{\prime}|=m+1
. Fix a vertex
\rho\in V
and let
p
be the path furthest away from
\rho
. Suppose that this path goes from
a\in A^{\prime}
to
b\in B^{\prime}
. Let
v\in p
be the vertex on
p
closest to
\rho
and let
e=\{v,w\}
be the first edge on the path from
v
to
\rho
. If there is a path
p^{\prime}
in the same component of
T\setminus\{e\}
then
v
is on the path from
p^{\prime}
to
\rho
, contradicting the choice of
p
.

Let
A=A^{\prime}\setminus\{a\}
and
B=B^{\prime}\setminus\{b\}
. Define the matrices
F,G
by

\displaystyle F(i,k)

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{a}=i|X_{v}=k)

\displaystyle G(k,j)

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{v}=k,X_{b}=j)

and for each
k\in[n]
define the
r^{m}\times r^{m}
matrix

\displaystyle H^{(k)}(f,g)

\displaystyle=\pi(X_{A}=f,X_{B}=g|X_{v}=k).

Note that
F
and
G
are both non-singular, by Lemma
2
. Furthermore, for each
k
the matrix
H^{(k)}
equals the flattening matrix
\mathrm{flat}_{A|B}
but with respect to root
\rho=w
and root distribution

\pi_{\rho}(i)=P_{vw}(k,i).

Hence by the induction hypothesis,
H^{(k)}
also has full rank.

Suppose that
\hat{f}:\hat{A}\rightarrow[r]
and
\hat{g}:\hat{B}\rightarrow[r]
. Let
i=\hat{f}(a)
,
j=\hat{g}(b)
and let
f,g
be the restrictions of
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
to
A
and
B
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}_{\hat{A}|\hat{B}}(\hat{f},\hat{g})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}F(i,k)H^{(k)}(f,g)G(k,j).

We will suppose that the null space of
\mathrm{flat}_{\hat{A}|\hat{B}}(\hat{f},\hat{g})
contains no non-zero vectors. To this end,
let
x
be a vector indexed by pairs
(g,j)
with
g:B\rightarrow[r]
and
j\in[r]
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{k=1}^{r}F(i,k)H^{(k)}(f,g)G(k,j)x(g,j)

\displaystyle=0

for all
f:A\rightarrow[r]
and
i\in[r]
. Rearranging, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}F(i,k)\left[\sum_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{r}H^{(k)}(f,g)G(k,j)x(g,j)\right]

\displaystyle=0

and since
F
is non-singular,

\displaystyle\sum_{g}\sum_{j=1}^{r}H^{(k)}(f,g)G(k,j)x(g,j)

\displaystyle=0

for all
f
and
k
. Define the vector
y
by

y(g,k)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}G(k,j)x(g,j)

(4)

so that for all
k
,

\displaystyle\sum_{g}H^{(k)}(f,g)y(g,k)

\displaystyle=0.

As
H^{(k)}
is non-singular,
y(g,k)=0
for all
g,k
, from which (
4
) and the fact that
G
is non-singular gives
x=0
. We have shown that the null space of
\mathrm{flat}_{\hat{A}|\hat{B}}
is trivial, proving the lemma.

\blacksquare

We can now prove the main theorem.

Proof.

(Theorem
1
)
Suppose that
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
V
. From Lemma
1
we have

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})\leq r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}.

By Proposition
1
there are
\nu_{T}(A|B)
vertex disjoint paths connecting vertices in
A
to vertices in
B
. Let
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
be the endpoints of these paths, so
|A^{\prime}|=|B^{\prime}|=\nu_{T}(A^{\prime}|B^{\prime})=\nu_{T}(A|B)
. By Lemma
3
we have
\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}})=r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}
and by Lemma
2
we have

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A|B})\geq\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{A^{\prime}|B^{\prime}})=r^{\nu_{T}(A|B)}.

The second part of the theorem now follows from Corollary
1
.

\blacksquare

We note that none of the conditions (C1)—(C3) on the root distribution and transition matrices can be eliminated. For example, consider the four taxa tree
in Figure
1
, a case studied in detail by
[
5
]
. The joint probability distribution for

is

\pi(X_{1}=a,X_{2}=b,X_{3}=c,X_{4}=d)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}P_{u1}(i,a)P_{u2}(i,b)\pi_{\rho}(i)P_{uv}(i,j)P_{v3}(j,c)P_{v4}(j,d).

Gather terms, we obtain a decomposition

\mathrm{flat}_{\{1,3\}|\{2,4\}}=UDV

where
U
and
V
are
r^{2}\times r^{2}

is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries

D_{ij;ij}=\pi_{\rho}(i)P_{uv}(i,j).

From here we see that if there is
i
such that
\pi_{\rho}(i)=0
, or
ij
such that
P_{uv}(i,j)=0
, then

\mathrm{rank}(\mathrm{flat}_{\{1,3\}|\{2,4\}})<r^{2}.
