Abstract
. We investigate composition operators C Φ C_{\Phi} on the Hardy-Smirnov space H 2 ​ ( Ω ) H^{2}(\Omega) induced by analytic self-maps Φ \Phi of an open simply connected proper subset Ω \Omega of the complex plane. When the Riemann map τ : 𝕌 → Ω \tau:\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\Omega used to define the norm of H 2 ​ ( Ω ) H^{2}(\Omega) is a linear fractional transformation, we characterize the composition operators whose adjoints are composition operators. As applications of this fact, we provide a new proof for the adjoint formula discovered by Gallardo-Gutiérrez and Montes-Rodríguez and we give a new approach to describe all Hermitian and unitary composition operators on H 2 ​ ( Ω ) . H^{2}(\Omega). Additionally, if the coefficients of τ \tau are real, we exhibit concrete examples of conjugations and describe the Hermitian and unitary composition operators which are complex symmetric with respect to specific conjugations on H 2 ​ ( Ω ) . H^{2}(\Omega). We finish this paper showing that if Ω \Omega is unbounded and Φ \Phi is a non-automorphic self-map of Ω \Omega with a fixed point, then C Φ C_{\Phi} is never complex symmetric on H 2 ​ ( Ω ) . H^{2}(\Omega).

1.
Introduction

The notion of composition operators appears in several branches of Mathematics
and its applications, as Dynamical Systems, Category Theory, Dirichlet Series
and Complex Analysis. For recent papers related to the subject in different
fields of Mathematics we refer to
[
3
,
8
,
22
,
24
]
and the
references therein. In this paper we will be interested in composition
operators in Complex Analysis.

Throughout this paper we will use the following notation:
\mathbb{C}
denotes
the complex plane,
\mathbb{U}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
is the open
unit disc,
\mathbb{C}_{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Re}(z)>0\}
is the open
right half-plane,
\Pi^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}(z)>0\}
is the
open upper half-plane and
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2\ldots\}.

Hereafter
\Omega
represents an open simply connected proper subset of the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and
\tau
is a Riemann map, i.e., a biholomorphic
map that takes
\mathbb{U}
onto
\Omega.
For
0<r<1,
let
\Gamma_{r}
be
the curve in
\Omega
defined by
\Gamma_{r}=\tau\left(\left\{|z|=r\right\}\right).
The Hardy-Smirnov space
H^{2}(\Omega)
is the set
of all analytic functions
f:\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
for which

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|:=\left(\sup_{0<r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{r}}|f(w)|^{2}|dw|\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

It is well-known that the definition of
H^{2}(\Omega)
does not
depend on the choice of
\tau
and that
\left\|\;\cdot\;\right\|
describes a
norm on
H^{2}(\Omega)
; moreover, different choices of
\tau
provide
equivalent norms (see
[
31
, p. 63]
). Each Hardy-Smirnov space

H^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space, and
H^{2}(\Omega)
is simply referred as
a Hardy space on
\Omega.
If
\Omega=\mathbb{U}
and
\tau
is the identity
map, then
H^{2}(\Omega)
reduces to the classical Hardy space of the open
unit disc.

If
\Phi
is an analytic self-map of
\Omega,
then
\Phi
induces a linear
composition operator
C_{\Phi}
on the space
H^{2}(\Omega)
defined
by

C_{\Phi}(f)=f\circ\Phi,\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega).

From now on we shall write
C_{\Phi}f
instead of
C_{\Phi}(f)
for short.
Operators of this type have been studied on a variety of spaces, with special
interest on the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
(see

[
4
,
7
,
25
,
30
,
31
]
). If
\Phi
is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{U}
,
then
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
(see
[
30
, Littlewood’s
Theorem, p. 16]
) but, for
\Omega\neq\mathbb{U}
,
C_{\Phi}
is
not bounded in general. The main direction of research in this framework
relies on the comparison between the properties of
C_{\Phi}
with those of

\Phi.
For instance, if
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{+},
Elliot and Jury
(
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
) established a boundedness criterion for

C_{\Phi}
in terms of angular derivatives. First, let us recall some
terminology. A sequence of points
z_{n}=\mathrm{Re}(z_{n})+i\mathrm{Im}(z_{n})
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
is said to approach
\infty
non-tangentially if

\mathrm{Re}(z_{n})\longrightarrow\infty
and the ratios
|\mathrm{Im}(z_{n})|/\mathrm{Re}(z_{n})
are uniformly bounded. We say that a self-map

\Phi
of
\mathbb{C}_{+}
fixes the infinity non-tangentially if
\Phi(z_{n})\longrightarrow\infty
whenever
z_{n}\longrightarrow\infty

non-tangentially, and we denote
\Phi(\infty)=\infty.
If
\Phi(\infty)=\infty,
we say that
\Phi
has a finite angular derivative at
\infty
if
the non-tangential limit
\Phi^{\prime}(\infty):=\displaystyle\lim_{z\longrightarrow\infty}z/\Phi(z)
exists. The result of Elliot and Jury
states that for an analytic self-map
\Phi
of
\mathbb{C}_{+},
the
composition operator
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
if, and
only if,
\Phi
has a finite angular derivative at
\infty
. This example
illustrates that the boundedness of
C_{\Phi}
on
H^{2}(\Omega)
is strongly
influenced by the choice of
\Omega
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denote the space of all bounded linear
operators on a separable complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}.
Recall that
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is called
normal
if
TT^{\ast}=T^{\ast}T,

Hermitian
if
T=T^{\ast},

unitary
if
TT^{*}=T^{*}T=Id,

hyponormal
if
\left\|T^{*}x\right\|\leq\left\|Tx\right\|
for all
x\in\mathcal{H},
and

cohyponormal
if its adjoint
T^{*}
is hyponormal.
It is simple to check that the Hermitian
and unitary composition operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
are precisely those
induced by
\Phi(z)=\lambda z
with
\lambda\in[-1,1]
and

|\lambda|=1,
respectively. For the case of normal operators (see

[
25
, Theorem 5.1.15]
), composition operators on

H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
are induced by
\Phi(z)=\lambda z
with
|\lambda|\leq 1.

The characterization of unitary composition operators on Hardy-Smirnov spaces
was given by Gunatillake, Jovovic and Smith in
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
:

C_{\Phi}
is a unitary bounded composition operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
if,
and only if, there are complex numbers
\lambda
and
r
with
|\lambda|=1

such that
\Phi(z)=\lambda z+r
is an automorphism of
\Omega.
The case of
Hermitian composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega)
was studied by Gunatillake
in
[
17
, Theorem 3.3]
, where it was shown that if
C_{\Phi}
is a
Hermitian bounded composition operator, then
\Phi(z)=\lambda z+r
for some
real number
\lambda
. However, this is not always sufficient to assure that

C_{\Phi}
is Hermitian.

A
conjugation
on
\mathcal{H}
is a conjugate-linear operator

C:\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

C
is
isometric
:
\left\langle Cf,Cg\right\rangle=\left\langle g,f\right\rangle,\forall f,g\in\mathcal{H}.

(2)

C
is
involutive
:
C^{2}=Id.

According to
[
12
]
,
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is
C
-
symmetric
if

CT=T^{\ast}C,
and
T
is
complex symmetric
if there exists a conjugation
C

with respect to which
T
is
C
-symmetric. As can be seen in

[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
, the class of complex symmetric operators
contains a large number of concrete examples including all normal operators,
binormal operators, Hankel operators, finite Toeplitz matrices, compressed
shift operators and the Volterra operators.

The investigation of complex symmetric composition operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
was initiated by Garcia and Hammond in
[
11
]
. They
described all (weighted) composition operators that are
J
-symmetric, where

J:H^{2}(\mathbb{U})\longrightarrow H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
is the conjugation
given by

(Jf)(z)=\overline{f(\overline{z})}

They have also shown that constant self-maps of
\mathbb{U}
and involutive
disc automorphisms induce non-normal complex symmetric composition operators
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).
Since then, several authors have been interested in
the subject, describing complex symmetric composition operators and exhibiting
concrete conjugations for which
C_{\Phi}
is complex symmetric (see

[
2
,
19
,
21
,
27
]
). Results on how the complex symmetry of

C_{\Phi}
affects the analytic self-map
\Phi
were obtained in

[
2
,
11
]
. More precisely, in
[
2
, Proposition p. 106]
the
authors prove that if
C_{\Phi}
is complex symmetric on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}),

then
\Phi
has a fixed point in
\mathbb{U}
and, additionally, if
\Phi
is
non-constant, then it is univalent (
[
11
, Proposition 2.5]
). In

[
28
, Theorem 2.5]
, Narayan, Sievewright and Tjani have shown that if

\Phi
is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{U}
which is not an elliptic
automorphism and
C_{\Phi}
is complex symmetric on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
, then

\Phi(0)=0
or
\Phi
is linear. They also characterized the non-automorphic
linear self-maps of
\mathbb{U}
that induce complex symmetric composition
operators (see
[
28
, Theorem 3.1]
). In
[
29
]
, Noor and Severiano
characterized all linear fractional self-maps
\Phi
of
\mathbb{C}_{+}
that
induce complex symmetric composition operators
C_{\Phi}
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+}).
In contrast with complex symmetric composition operators on

H^{2}(\mathbb{U}),
Hai and Severiano (
[
20
, Theorem 8.8]
) showed that
if
\Phi
is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{C}_{+}
with a fixed point in

\mathbb{C}_{+}
then
C_{\Phi}
is never complex symmetric on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
. Since unitary and Hermitian bounded operators are complex
symmetric,
[
17
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
provide
natural ways to obtain examples of complex symmetric composition operators on

H^{2}(\Omega).
However, it is not immediate with respect to which
conjugations these operators are complex symmetric.

In this paper we are mainly interested in describing the unitary, Hermitian,
(partially) normal and (partially) complex symmetric composition operators on

H^{2}(\Omega),
whenever
\Omega
is the range of a linear fractional Riemann
map
\tau
from
\mathbb{U}.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section

2
, we collect some general results that will be used in the subsequent
sections. In Section
3
, considering that
\Omega
is the range of a
linear fractional Riemann map
\tau
from
\mathbb{U}
, we obtain an
expression for the reproducing kernels on
H^{2}(\Omega)
. Using these
expressions, in Section
4
, we describe all composition operators whose
adjoints are also composition operators (Theorem
4.2
) and we
characterize the Hermitian and unitary composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega)
(Theorems
4.5
and
4.7
). In particular, we show how the
numbers
\lambda
and
r
given in
[
17
, Theorem 3.3]
and

[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
depend on the coefficients of
\tau.
We also
provide concrete examples of normal composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Section
5
is devoted to the study of complex symmetric composition
operators. We exhibit concrete examples of conjugations on each
H^{2}(\Omega)
(Propositions
5.2
and
5.4
) and, assuming that
\Omega

is the range of a linear fractional Riemann map with real coefficients, we
provide concrete examples of complex symmetric operators (see Propositions

5.3
and
5.5
). More precisely, in Propositions
5.3
and

5.5
we explicit the conjugations with respect to which Hermitian and
unitary composition operators are complex symmetric. We finish the paper by
showing that if
\Omega
is unbounded and
\Phi
is an analytic
non-automorphic self-map of
\Omega
with a fixed point of
\Omega
, then

C_{\Phi}
is not complex symmetric on
H^{2}(\Omega).

2.
Background

2.1.
Hardy-Smirnov space

For
1\leq p<\infty,
the classical Hardy space
H^{p}(\mathbb{U})
is the
space of all analytic functions
f:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
for
which

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{p}:=\left(\sup_{0<r<1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\right)^{1/p}<\infty.

The norm
\left\|\;\cdot\;\right\|_{p}
makes
H^{p}(\mathbb{U})
into a Banach space. For
p=2,
the inner product on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
is defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{2}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(n)\overline{\widehat{g}(n)},\quad f,g\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U})

where
(\widehat{f}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\widehat{g}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are the sequences of Maclaurin coefficients for
f
and
g,

respectively. Since

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\widehat{f}(n)|^{2},\quad f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U})

(see
[
25
, Theorem 1.1.12]
), it follows that the norm
\left\|\;\cdot\;\right\|_{2}
makes
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
into a Hilbert space.

The functions
\{K_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{U}\}
defined by

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\alpha}z},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}

are called
reproducing kernels
for
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).
These
functions have the fundamental property
f(\alpha)=\left\langle f,K_{\alpha}\right\rangle
for each
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{U}.

Henceforth we will use the symbol
\tau
to denote the Riemann map

\tau:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\Omega
which induces the norm of
H^{2}(\Omega).
The following result shows that the abstract aspect of

H^{2}(\Omega)
can be eased by an isometric isomorphism between
H^{2}(\Omega)
and
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f
be an analytic function on
\Omega.
Then
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
if and only if
(f\circ\tau)(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).
Moreover, the map
V
given by
Vf=(f\circ\tau)(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}
is an isometric isomorphism from
H^{2}(\Omega)
onto

H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

Proof.

See
[
6
, Corollary, p. 169]
.
∎

If
V
is the isometric isomorphism of Proposition
2.1
, then it is easy to check that
V^{-1}f=(\tau^{\prime}\circ\tau^{-1})^{-1/2}(f\circ\tau^{-1})
for each
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).
Moreover, if
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
is the inner product on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
defined
in (
2.1
), then

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle:=\left\langle Vf,Vg\right\rangle_{2},\quad f,g\in H^{2}(\Omega)

is an inner product on
H^{2}(\Omega)
and
\left\langle f,f\right\rangle=\left\|Vf\right\|^{2}_{2}=\left\|f\right\|^{2}.
Thus,
H^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space endowed with the inner product

\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle.

2.2.
Weighted composition operator
W_{\varphi}

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{U}
and
\psi:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be analytic functions. The weighted
composition operator
W_{\psi,\varphi}
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
is defined
by

\displaystyle W_{\psi,\varphi}f=\psi\cdot f\circ\varphi,\quad f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

In general,
W_{\psi,\varphi}
is not bounded. If
\psi
is bounded on

\mathbb{U},
then
W_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
.
However
W_{\psi,\varphi}
may still be bounded even if
\psi
is not bounded
on
\mathbb{U}.
The following lemma is well-known and shows how

W_{\psi,\varphi}^{\ast}
acts on reproducing kernels.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
W_{\psi,\varphi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
and

\alpha\in\mathbb{U}
then

(2.2)

\displaystyle W_{\psi,\varphi}^{*}K_{\alpha}=\overline{{\psi}(\alpha)}K_{\varphi(\alpha)}.

Proof.

See
[
31
, Lemma 3.2]
.
∎

It is immediate from the lemma above that if
\psi\equiv 1
, then
C_{\varphi}^{*}K_{\alpha}=K_{\varphi(\alpha)}
for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{U}.

Recall that two operators
T_{1}:\mathcal{H}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
and
T_{2}:\mathcal{H}_{2}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{1}
are

similar
if there is an isomorphism
U:\mathcal{H}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}_{2}
such that
UT_{1}U^{-1}=T_{2}.
If
U
is an isometry, we say
that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are
isometrically similar
. Using Proposition

2.1
we see that each composition operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
is
isometrically similar to a weighted composition operator on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

In fact, a straight computation reveals that for each analytic self-map
\Phi

of
\Omega
we have

VC_{\Phi}V^{-1}=W_{\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{\tau^{\prime}\circ\varphi}\right)^{1/2},\varphi},

where
\varphi=\tau^{-1}\circ\Phi\circ\tau.
For simplicity, in this case, we
write
W_{\varphi}
instead of
W_{\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{\tau^{\prime}\circ\varphi}\right)^{1/2},\varphi}.

We refer to
[
4
,
6
,
25
,
30
]
and

[
1
,
31
]
for more details on the Hardy space and on weighted composition operators, respectively.

2.3.
Complex symmetric operators

Each complex symmetric operator satisfies the following
spectral
symmetry property
: if
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
is
C
-symmetric and

\lambda\in\mathbb{C},
then

f\in\mathrm{Ker}(T-\lambda I)\iff Cf\in\mathrm{Ker}(T^{\ast}-\overline{\lambda}I).

The next simple lemma, which will be stated for further reference, shows how
to obtain a new conjugation from a given conjugation.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}.
If
U:\mathcal{H}_{1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
is an isometric isomorphism then
U^{-1}CU
is
a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}_{1}.

In Section
5
, we will make extensive use of the following well-known result.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be
C
-symmetric. If
T
is
isometrically similar to
T_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
via the
isometric isomorphism
U
, then
T_{1}
is
U^{-1}CU
-symmetric.

Proof.

See
[
12
, p. 1291]
.

∎

3.
Linear fractional Riemann maps

A function
k\in H^{2}(\Omega)
is a reproducing kernel for
H^{2}(\Omega)
at
the point
u\in\Omega
if
f(u)=\left\langle f,k\right\rangle
for all
f\in H^{2}(\Omega),
and in this case, such reproducing kernel is denoted by

k_{u}.
The reproducing kernels on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
are commonly used to
obtain properties of various bounded operators, such as: Hankel operators, Toeplitz operators
and weighted composition operators (see
[
5
,
10
,
11
]
). Generally,
this is done by testing the action of those bounded operators on the
reproducing kernels on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

This section is motivated by the observation done in the previous paragraph.
In this sense, we first characterize the functions that are the kernel
functions on
H^{2}(\Omega)
(see Lemma
3.1
), and then in Section

4
we use this characterization to study the composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).

3.1.
Reproducing kernels

A linear fractional Riemann map
\tau:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\Omega
is a
Riemann map of the following form

\tau(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}.

where
a,b,c
and
d
are complex numbers. Since
\tau
is a Riemann map, it
is subject to the further condition
ad-bc\neq 0.
For instance,
\tau_{1}:

\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\tau_{2}:

\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\Pi^{+}
given by

\tau_{1}(z)=\frac{1+z}{1-z}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau_{2}(z)=i\frac{1+z}{1-z}

are linear fractional Riemann maps.

As we see below, one of the advantages of studying
H^{2}(\Omega)
where

\Omega
is a range of a linear fractional Riemann map
\tau
is that the
reproducing kernels have a simple expression in terms of the coefficients of

\tau.

Lemma 3.1
.

The reproducing kernels on
H^{2}(\Omega)
are given by

k_{u}=\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}V^{-1}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)},\quad u\in\Omega,

where
V
is the isometric isomorphism of Proposition
2.1
. In particular,
if

(3.1)

\displaystyle\tau(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}\text{,}

then

k_{u}(w)=\frac{|ad-bc|}{|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-\overline{c}a)\overline{u}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]w},\quad u,w\in\Omega.

Proof.

Since
\tau
is a Riemann map,
\tau^{\prime}
never vanishes in
\mathbb{U}.

Hence, by Proposition
2.1
, for each
u\in\Omega
and
f\in H^{2}(\Omega),
we obtain

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))^{-1/2}(f\circ\tau)(\tau^{-1}(u))(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))^{1/2}

\displaystyle=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))^{-1/2}(Vf)(\tau^{-1}(u))

\displaystyle=\langle Vf,\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle f,\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}V^{-1}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\rangle,

which shows that
\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}V^{-1}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}
is the reproducing kernel for
H^{2}(\Omega)
at the point

u.
Now assume that
\tau
is as in (
3.1
). Then

\tau^{-1}(w)=\frac{b-dw}{cw-a}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{\prime}(z)=\frac{ad-bc}{(cz+d)^{2}},\quad w\in\Omega,z\in\mathbb{U}.

Hence

\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w))=\frac{ad-bc}{(c\tau^{-1}(w)+d)^{2}}=\frac{ad-bc}{\left[c\left(\frac{b-dw}{cw-a}\right)+d\right]^{2}}=\frac{(cw-a)^{2}}{ad-bc}

and

K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}(\tau^{-1}(w))=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\tau^{-1}(u)}\tau^{-1}(w)}=\frac{\overline{(cu-a)}(cw-a)}{\overline{(cu-a)}(cw-a)-\overline{(b-du)}(b-dw)}.

Combining these equalities, it turns out that
k_{u}(w)
is given by

\displaystyle k_{u}(w)

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}V^{-1}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\overline{(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u)))}^{-1/2}(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}(\tau^{-1}(w))

\displaystyle=\overline{\frac{(ad-bc)}{\overline{cu-a}}}^{1/2}\frac{(ad-bc)}{cw-a}^{1/2}\frac{\overline{(cu-a)}(cw-a)}{\overline{(cu-a)}(cw-a)-\overline{(b-du)}(b-dw)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|ad-bc|}{|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]w}

as desired.
∎

It follows easily from this lemma that if
\tau
is the identity or
\tau_{1},
then

\displaystyle K_{u}(z)=\frac{1}{1-\overline{u}z}\quad\text{and}\quad k_{u}(w)=\frac{1}{\overline{u}+w},\quad z\in\mathbb{U},w\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

are the reproducing kernels on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
and
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+}),
respectively.

Corollary 3.2
.

For every
u\in\Omega,
let
\delta_{u}
be the linear functional
\delta_{u}:H^{2}(\Omega)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by
\delta_{u}(f)=f(u).
Then
\delta_{u}
is bounded with
\left\|\delta_{u}\right\|=1/(|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(u)|^{2}})
and for each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle|f(u)|\leq\frac{\left\|f\right\|}{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(u)|^{2}}},\quad u\in\Omega.

Proof.

For
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
u\in\Omega,
we have
|f(u)|=|\left\langle f,k_{u}\right\rangle|
. Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
3.1
we get

\displaystyle|\delta_{u}(f)|=|\left\langle f,k_{u}\right\rangle|\leq\left\|f\right\|\left\|k_{u}\right\|=\left\|f\right\|\frac{\parallel K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\parallel_{2}}{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|}

Thus,
\delta_{u}
is a bounded linear functional of norm at most
\parallel K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\parallel_{2}/|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|.
Applying again Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta_{u}\left(\frac{1}{\left\|k_{u}\right\|}k_{u}\right)=\left\|k_{u}\right\|=\frac{\parallel K_{\tau^{-1}(u)}\parallel_{2}}{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|}.

This completes the proof.
∎

The proof of next result is analogous of the version for the Hardy space
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).

Corollary 3.3
.

If
C_{\Phi}
is a bounded operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
then
C_{\Phi}^{*}k_{u}=k_{\Phi(u)},
for
each
u\in\Omega.

Corollary 3.4
.

If
C_{\Phi}
is a bounded operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\frac{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(u)|^{2}}}{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(\Phi(\tau(u))))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(\Phi(\tau(u)))|^{2}}}:u\in\Omega\right\}<\infty.

Proof.

Since
C_{\Phi}
is bounded, it follows from Corollary
3.4
that

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|k_{\Phi(u)}\right\|}{\left\|k_{u}\right\|}=\frac{\left\|C_{\Phi}^{*}k_{u}\right\|}{\left\|k_{u}\right\|}\leq\left\|C_{\Phi}\right\|,\quad u\in\Omega.

Lemma
3.1
gives

\displaystyle\frac{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(u))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(u)|^{2}}}{|\sqrt{\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(\Phi(\tau(u))))}|\sqrt{1-|\tau^{-1}(\Phi(\tau(u)))|^{2}}}\leq\left\|C_{\Phi}\right\|\quad u\in\Omega.

∎

For analytic self-maps of
\mathbb{C}_{+},
we have that a composition operator is bounded on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
if and only if its inducing symbol satisfies the condition (
3.2
) (see
[
7
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Remark 3.5
.

We frequently use the following fact: if
\tau(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)

is a Riemann map with
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then
b\overline{d}\neq a\overline{c}.
Indeed, since
\tau
is a Riemann map, the equality
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}

implies that
c\neq 0
and
d\neq 0.
Hence

\displaystyle b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}=\frac{b}{d}|d|^{2}-\frac{a}{c}|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}\left(\frac{b}{d}-\frac{a}{c}\right)=|d|^{2}\left(\frac{bc-ad}{dc}\right)\neq 0.

Let
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega
with a fixed point in
\Omega.

Assuming
C_{\Phi}\neq I
is cohyponormal on
H^{2}(\Omega),
we use Lemma

3.1
to determine the fixed point of
\Phi.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
and let
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega.
If

\Phi(u)=u
for some
u\in\Omega
and
C_{\Phi}\neq I
is cohyponormal on

H^{2}(\Omega)
then
|c|<|d|
and

u=(a\overline{c}-b\overline{d})/(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}).

Proof.

First we note that, by Corollary
3.3
,
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}k_{u}=k_{u}.

Since
C_{\Phi}
is cohyponormal, we have
\mathrm{Ker}(C_{\Phi}^{*}-\overline{\lambda}I)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(C_{\Phi}-\lambda I),
for each

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}.
Hence

C_{\Phi}k_{u}=k_{u},
and by Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]\Phi(w)

\displaystyle=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]w

or equivalently

[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c](\Phi(w)-w)=0.

Since
C_{\Phi}\neq I,
it follows that
\Phi
is not the identity. Hence,

(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c=0.
By Remark

3.5
, if
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then
\overline{b}d\neq\overline{a}c.
This
implies that we must have
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},
and hence

u=(a\overline{c}-b\overline{d})/(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}).

Since
u\in\tau(\mathbb{U})
there is
z_{u}\in\mathbb{U}
such that

u=(az_{u}+b)/(cz_{u}+d).
A simple computation gives
z_{u}\overline{d}(ad-bc)=\overline{c}(bc-ad)
and hence
z_{u}=-\overline{c}/\overline{d}.

∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\Omega=\mathbb{C}_{+}
or
\Omega=\Pi^{+}
then
H^{2}(\Omega)
does not
contain normal bounded composition operators
C_{\Phi}
with
\Phi
fixing a
point of
\Omega.

In
[
17
]
, Gunatillake studied the bounded Hermitian composition
operators on the Hardy-Smirnov spaces
H^{2}(\Omega).
He showed that if

C_{\Phi}
is a bounded Hermitian operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
for some
constant map
\Phi
, then
\Omega
is a disc (see
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
).
Now we will show that
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
holds for cohyponormal
composition operators induced by constant maps.
First we show that if
H^{2}(\Omega)
supports a cohyponormal composition
operator induced by a constant map then
\tau
is a linear fractional Riemann
map, and then we apply the remark below to conclude that
\tau(\mathbb{U})
is
a disc.

Remark 3.8
.

For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{U},
the set
K_{\alpha}(\mathbb{U})

is a disc. Indeed, let us denote
\varphi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{U}
the automorphism of
\mathbb{U}
given by
\varphi_{\alpha}(z)=(\alpha-z)/(1-\overline{\alpha}z).
Then, it is enough to show that

K_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{U}))
is a disc. For each
z\in\mathbb{U},
we have

\displaystyle K_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha}(z))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\overline{\alpha}\varphi_{\alpha}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\overline{\alpha}z}{1-\overline{\alpha}z-|\alpha|^{2}+\overline{\alpha}z}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{|\alpha|^{2}-1}\right)z+\frac{1}{1-|\alpha|^{2}}.

and hence
K_{\alpha}(\mathbb{U})
is a disc.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\Phi
be a constant self-map of
\Omega.
If
C_{\Phi}
is a
cohyponormal operator on
H^{2}(\Omega),
then
\Omega
is a disc.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Phi\equiv w_{0}
for some
w_{0}\in\Omega
and that
C_{\Phi}

is a cohyponormal operator on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Since
W_{\varphi}
and
C_{\Phi}
are isometrically similar, it follows that

W_{\varphi}
also is cohyponormal. Now, we observe that a simple
computation gives
\varphi\equiv\alpha,
where
\alpha=\tau^{-1}(w_{0}).

Hence

(3.3)

\displaystyle(W_{\varphi}f)(z)=\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{\prime}(z)}{\tau^{\prime}(\alpha)}}f(\alpha),\quad f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}),z\in\mathbb{U}.

Since
\alpha
is a fixed point of
\varphi
in
\mathbb{U},
it follows from
Lemma
2.2
that
K_{\alpha}
is an eigenvector of
W_{\varphi}^{*}
associated
to the eigenvalue
1.
By the cohyponormality of
W_{\varphi},

K_{\alpha}

is also an eigenvector of
W_{\varphi}
associated to the eigenvalue
1,

which combined with (
3.3
) gives

\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{\prime}(z)}{\tau^{\prime}(\alpha)}}=\frac{1-|\alpha|^{2}}{1-\overline{\alpha}z},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}.

Hence

(3.4)

\displaystyle\tau^{\prime}(z)=\frac{c_{1}}{(1-\overline{\alpha}z)^{2}},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}

where
c_{1}=(1-|\alpha|^{2})^{2}\tau^{\prime}(\alpha).
Now, we study the
cases,
\alpha=0
and
\alpha\neq 0,
separately.

Case 1:

\alpha=0.

In this case,
\tau^{\prime}
is the
constant function identically to
c_{1}.
Since
\tau
is a Riemann map,

c_{1}\neq 0.
Therefore,
\tau(z)=c_{1}z+b
for some
b\in\mathbb{C},
which
implies that
\Omega
is a disc.

Case 2:

\alpha\neq 0.

In this case, (
3.4
) implies that
the Riemann map
\tau
must have the following form

\tau(z)=\frac{c_{1}}{\overline{\alpha}}K_{\alpha}(z)+c_{2},\quad z\in\mathbb{U}.

As we saw in Remark
3.8
, the
K_{\alpha}(\mathbb{U})
is a disc. Hence
the range of
\tau
must also be a disc.
∎

4.
Hermitian and unitary composition operators

Assume that
C_{\Phi}
and
C_{\Psi}
are Hermitian and unitary,
respectively. It is standard in composition operator theory to study the equalities

C_{\Phi}k_{u}=C_{\Phi}^{\ast}k_{u}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\Psi}C_{\Psi}^{\ast}k_{u}=k_{u},\quad u\in\Omega

to determine which symbols
\Phi
and
\Psi
induce Hermitian and unitary
composition operators, respectively (see, for instance,
[
1
,
5
,
17
,
18
]
). Here we shall introduce a different approach. We first
observe that if
C_{\Phi}
is Hermitian and
C_{\Psi}
is unitary, then

C_{\Phi}^{\ast}
and
C_{\Psi}^{\ast}
are composition operators and thus,
when
\tau
is a fractional linear Riemann
map, we use the expression of the reproducing kernels given by Lemma
3.1

to describe all the composition operators
C_{\Phi}
whose adjoints are
multiple of other composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).
So we use this
description to determine the symbols
\Phi
that induce Hermitian or unitary
composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega)
(see Theorems
4.5
and
4.7
).

Remark 4.1
.

By Lemma
3.9
, it follows that
C_{\Phi}^{*}
is never the
zero operator. Hence, if
\Phi
and
\Phi_{\star}
are analytic self-maps of

\Omega
with
C_{\Phi}^{*}=\lambda C_{\Phi_{\star}}
for some complex number

\lambda,
we must have
\lambda\neq 0.

Theorem below provides a formula for calculating the adjoints of certain
classes of composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d),\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\},

\Phi
and
\Phi_{\star}
be analytic self-maps of
\Omega

such that
C_{\Phi}
and
C_{\Phi_{\star}}
are bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega).
Then

C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=\lambda C_{\Phi_{\star}}
if and only if one of the following cases occurs

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then there is a complex number
r
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=\overline{\lambda}^{-1}w+r\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda w+\frac{(\lambda-1)(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
and
(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})=|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}
then there is a complex number
r
such
that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=\lambda w+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda\overline{r}w+\frac{(\lambda\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

(3)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
and
(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\neq|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}
then
\lambda=1
and there is

r\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.3
, we have that
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=\lambda C_{\Phi_{\star}}
for some complex number
\lambda\neq 0
if and only if

\displaystyle k_{\Phi(u)}(w)=\lambda k_{u}(\Phi_{\star}(w)),\quad u,w\in\Omega

or equivalently

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lambda\left\{|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{\Phi(u)}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{\Phi(u)}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]w\right\}

\displaystyle=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]\Phi_{\star}(w),\quad u,w\in\Omega.

Now we study the cases,
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
and
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},
separately.

Case 1:

|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}.

In this case, it turns out that
(
4.4
) is

(4.5)

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}[|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)\Phi(u)+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{w}]

\displaystyle=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)u+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{\Phi_{\star}(w)},\quad u,w\in\Omega.

Differentiating (
4.5
) with respect to
u,
we obtain

(4.6)

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}\Phi^{\prime}(u)(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)=(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c),\quad u\in\Omega.

By Remark
3.5
, we have
\overline{b}d\neq\overline{a}c
and hence
(
4.6
) gives
\Phi(u)=\overline{\lambda}^{-1}u+r
for some complex number

r.
Taking complex conjugate in (
4.5
) and differentiating with respect
to
w,
we obtain
\Phi_{\star}^{\prime}(w)=\lambda.
Hence,
\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda w+s
for some complex number
s.
Replacing the expressions of

\Phi(u)
and
\Phi_{\star}(w)
in (
4.5
), we have

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)u+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)\overline{\lambda}r+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{\lambda w}

\displaystyle=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)u+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{\lambda w}+(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{s},\quad u,w\in\Omega,

or equivalently,

(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)s=(\lambda-1)(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}).

Thus, we obtain (
4.1
).

Case 2:

|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}.

Differentiating (
4.4
)
with respect to
w,
we obtain

\lambda[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{\Phi(u)}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]=[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{u}+\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c]\Phi_{\star}^{\prime}(w),\quad u,w\in\Omega.

This implies that

\Phi(u)=ru+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},\quad u\in\Omega,

where
\overline{r}=\Phi_{\star}^{\prime}(w)/\lambda.
Taking the complex
conjugate in (
4.4
) and differentiating with respect to
u,
we get that

\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda\overline{r}w+\frac{(\lambda\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},\quad w\in\Omega.

Conversely, if
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2},

\Phi
and
\Phi_{\star}
are as in (
4.1
), then a straightforward computation shows that (
4.4
) holds. If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},

\Phi
and
\Phi_{\star}
are as in (
4.2
), then the left side of (
4.4
) is

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lambda\left\{|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\overline{ru}w+\overline{r}[(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)w]+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right\}.

while the right
side of (
4.4
) is

(4.8)

\displaystyle|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\lambda\overline{ru}w+\lambda\overline{r}[(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\overline{u}+(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)w]+\frac{(\lambda\overline{r}-1)|\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c|^{2}}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Putting these together, it follows that (
4.7
) is equal to (
4.8
) if and only if

\lambda\left[|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right]=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}+\frac{(\lambda\overline{r}-1)|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},

or equivalently,

(4.9)

\displaystyle(\lambda-1)\left(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}-\frac{|b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}|^{2}}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right)=0.

Therefore, (
4.2
) or (
4.3
) occurs if and only if (
4.9
) holds.
∎

Theorem
4.2
recovers classical results of Hermitian and unitary
composition operators on
H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
(see
[
26
, Theorems 2.4 and
3.1]
).

Remark 4.3
.

The linear fractional self-maps
\Phi
of
\Pi^{+}
that induce bounded
composition operators on
H^{2}(\Pi^{+})
are of the following
form

(4.10)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=\lambda w+r,

where
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
and
\mathrm{Im}(r)\geq 0.
In
[
9
]
,
Gallardo-Gutiérrez and Montes-Rodríguez provide a formula for the
adjoints of composition operators induced by such maps. The next result shows that this formula can be obtained from Theorem
4.2
.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
\Phi
is as in (
4.10
), then
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=\lambda^{-1}C_{\Phi_{\star}},
where
\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda^{-1}w-\lambda^{-1}\overline{r}.

Proof.

Since
\tau_{2}(z)=i(1+z)/(1-z)
is a linear fractional Riemann map from

\mathbb{U}
onto
\Pi^{+}
and
\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda^{-1}w-\lambda^{-1}\overline{r}
is a self-map of
\Pi^{+}
which induces a
bounded composition operator on
H^{2}(\Pi^{+}),
Theorem
4.2

gives
C_{\Phi}^{*}=\lambda^{-1}C_{\Phi_{\star}}.

∎

4.1.
Hermitian composition operators

Here, we assume that
\tau
is a linear fractional Riemann map and we describe all symbols
\Phi
whose
composition operators
C_{\Phi}
are Hermitian on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
and
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega
such that

C_{\Phi}
is a Hermitian composition operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
.

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
with

\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(4.11)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{R}
such
that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Conversely, let
\Phi
be as in (
4.11
) or (
4.12
) with conditions
(
1
) and (
2
), respectively. If
\Phi
is a self-map
of
\Omega
such that
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega)
then
C_{\Phi}

is Hermitian.

Proof.

Since
C_{\Phi}
is Hermitian, we have
C_{\Phi}^{*}=C_{\Phi}.

Applying Theorem
4.2
with
\lambda=1,
we obtain
\Phi=\Phi_{\star}.
To
find the expression of
\Phi,
we must consider two cases:

Case 1:

|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}.

By Theorem
4.2
, there is

r\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}.

Since
\Phi=\Phi_{\star},
it follows that
r(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)=\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}).

Case 2:

|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}.

By Theorem
4.2
, there is

r\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Since
\Phi=\Phi_{\star},
we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},\quad w\in\Omega.

Differentiating (
4.13
), we obtain
r=\overline{r}.

Conversely,
first we assume that
\Phi
is as in (
4.11
) with condition (
1
).
Then
\Phi_{\star}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}
with
\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})\in\mathbb{R}.
Hence, we obtain

\Phi_{\star}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}=w+\frac{r(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}=\Phi(w),\quad w\in\Omega.

Since
C_{\Phi}
is a bounded operator on

H^{2}(\Omega),
it follows from Theorem
4.2
that
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi_{\star}},
and hence
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi}.
Now, we assume that
\Phi
is as in
(
4.12
) with condition (
2
). Then

\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

with
r\in\mathbb{R}
(note that we can choose
\lambda=1
). So, it is
immediate that
\Phi=\Phi_{\star}.
By applying again Theorem
4.2
, we
obtain
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi_{\star}},
and hence
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi}.

∎

Example 4.6
.

It follows from Theorem
4.5
that
C_{\Phi}
is a bounded Hermitian composition operator on

H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})
(
resp.
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
) if and only if
\Phi(w)=w+ir
for some
r\geq 0
(
resp.
\Phi(w)=\lambda w
for some

\lambda\in[-1,1]
).

4.2.
Unitary composition operators

Similarly to the previous section, we assume that
\tau
is a
linear fractional Riemann map and
we determine which symbols
\Phi
induce unitary composition operators on

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
and
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega
such that

C_{\Phi}
is a unitary bounded composition operator on
H^{2}(\Omega)
.

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
with

\mathrm{Re}(\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))=0
such that

(4.14)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
with

|r|=1
such that

(4.15)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

Conversely, let
\Phi
be as in (
4.14
) or (
4.15
) with conditions
(
1
) and (
2
), respectively. If
\Phi
is a self-map
of
\Omega
such that
C_{\Phi}
invertible and bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega)
then

C_{\Phi}
is unitary.

Proof.

By hypothesis
C_{\Phi}
is unitary. Then
C_{\Phi}^{*}=C_{\Phi}^{-1}
and
since
C_{\Phi}^{-1}=C_{\Phi^{-1}},
we have
C_{\Phi}^{*}=C_{\Phi^{-1}}.

Applying Theorem
4.2
with
\lambda=1,
we obtain
\Phi^{-1}=\Phi_{\star}.
To find the expression of
\Phi,
we consider two cases.

Case 1:

|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}.

By Theorem
4.2
, there is

r\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{-1}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}.

Since
\Phi^{-1}
also is given by
\Phi^{-1}(w)=w-r,
we obtain

\displaystyle w-r=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c},\quad w\in\Omega.

which implies
\mathrm{Re}(\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))=0.

Case 2:

|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}.

By Theorem
4.2
, there is

r\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi^{-1}=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Since
\Phi^{-1}
also is given by

\Phi^{-1}(w)=\frac{w}{r}-\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})},

we get

(4.16)

\displaystyle\frac{w}{r}-\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},\quad w\in\Omega.

Differentiating (
4.16
), it follows that
|r|=1.

Conversely,
first we assume that
\Phi
is as in (
4.14
) with condition (
1
).
Then
\Phi_{\star}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}
with
\mathrm{Re}(\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))=0.
Hence

\Phi_{\star}(w)=w+\frac{\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}=w+\frac{-r(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}=\Phi^{-1}(w).

By Theorem
4.2
, we obtain
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi_{\star}},
and hence

C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi^{-1}}.
Now, we assume that
\Phi
is as in
(
4.15
) with condition (
2
). Then

\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

with
|r|=1.
Hence

\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}=\Phi_{\star}(w)=\frac{|r|^{2}}{\overline{r}}w+\frac{(|r|^{2}-r)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}=\Phi^{-1}(w).

Applying again Theorem
4.2
, we have
C_{\Phi_{\star}}=C_{\Phi}^{-1}

and hence
C_{\Phi}^{\ast}=C_{\Phi^{-1}}.

∎

that

(

4.3.
Normal composition operators

We finish this section characterizing the symbols in Theorem
4.2
that induce normal composition operators.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
and
\lambda,r\in\mathbb{C}
with
\lambda\neq 0.

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2},

\Phi(w)=\overline{\lambda}^{-1}w+r
and
\Phi_{\star}(w)=\lambda w+\frac{(\lambda-1)(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c}

are self-maps of
\Omega
and
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega).
Then
C_{\Phi}
is normal if and only if

(\lambda-1)(1-\overline{\lambda})(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})=2\mathrm{Re}(\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})(\overline{\lambda}-1)).

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},

\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

are self-maps of
\Omega
and
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega).
Then
C_{\Phi}
is normal.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
, in both cases
C_{\Phi}
is normal if and only if
C_{\Phi}C_{\Phi_{\star}}=C_{\Phi_{\star}}C_{\Phi}.
By Lemma
3.1
, this is
equivalent to the
\Phi\circ\Phi_{\star}=\Phi_{\star}\circ\Phi.
Now we study the cases,
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
and
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},
separately.

Case 1:

|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}.
A straightforward
computation gives

(\Phi\circ\Phi_{\star})(w)=\frac{\lambda(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)w+(\lambda-1)(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})+\overline{\lambda}r(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)}{\overline{\lambda}(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)}

and

(\Phi_{\star}\circ\Phi)(w)=\frac{\lambda(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)w+|\lambda|^{2}r(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)+(|\lambda|^{2}-\overline{\lambda})(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+|\lambda|^{2}\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})}{\overline{\lambda}(\overline{b}d-\overline{a}c)}.

Therefore,
\Phi\circ\Phi_{\star}=\Phi_{\star}\circ\Phi
if and only if

(\lambda-1)(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+2\mathrm{Re}(\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))=(|\lambda|^{2}-\overline{\lambda})(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})+2\mathrm{Re}(|\lambda|^{2}\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))

or equivalently

(\lambda-1)(1-\overline{\lambda})(|a|^{2}-|b|^{2})=2\mathrm{Re}(\lambda\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})(\overline{\lambda}-1)).

Case 2:

|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}.
Then

\displaystyle(\Phi\circ\Phi_{\star})(w)=\lambda|r|^{2}(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})w+\frac{(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c})(|r|^{2}-1)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}=(\Phi_{\star}\circ\Phi)(w),\quad w\in\Omega

which imples that
C_{\Phi}
is normal.
∎

5.
Complex symmetric composition operators

In Section
4
, we describe all composition operators that are Hermitian
and unitary and, as mentioned in Introduction, such operators are always
complex symmetric. So for the purpose of classifying composition operators
that are complex symmetric, Theorems
4.5
and
4.7
provide partial
answers. However, as noted in
[
23
]
, it is advantageous to determine
with respect to which conjugation an operator is complex symmetric. In some
situations, this allows us to obtain additional informations about the
operator, such as its spectrum and reduced spaces.

In
[
11
]
, Garcia and Hammond described all weighted composition
operators
W_{\psi,\varphi}
that are
J
-symmetric on
H^{2}(\mathbb{U}),

where
J:H^{2}(\mathbb{U})\longrightarrow H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
is the
conjugation defined by

(5.1)

\displaystyle(Jf)(z)=\overline{f(\overline{z})},\quad f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}),z\in\mathbb{U}.

Using the same rule as
J,
but in other spaces,
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
23
]

also described weighted composition operators and composition operators that
are
J
-symmetric.

In this section, we use the conjugation
J
defined in (
5.1
) and the
isometry between
H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
and
H^{2}(\Omega)
to construct concrete
examples of conjugations on
H^{2}(\Omega).
From these examples and assuming
that
\tau
is a linear fractional Riemann map
with real coefficients, we explicit conjugations with respect to which
Hermitian and unitary composition operators are complex symmetric (see
Propositions
5.3
and
5.5
).

We begin this section by studying the simplest case of composition operators,
those induced by constant self-maps.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\Phi
be a constant self-map of
\Omega.
If
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on

H^{2}(\Omega)
then
C_{\Phi}
is complex symmetric.

Proof.

As shown in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, if
\Phi
is constant then

C_{\Phi}
is similar to the operator
W_{\varphi}
of rank one (the range of

W_{\varphi}
is generated by
(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}
). By
[
16
, Theorem
2]
, operators of rank one are complex symmetric. Hence
C_{\Phi}
is
complex symmetric.
∎

5.1.
J_{\Omega}
-symmetry

By Lemma
2.3
the conjugation
J
allows us to induce a conjugation on
the Hardy space
H^{2}(\Omega)
defining

(5.2)

\displaystyle J_{\Omega}=V^{-1}JV,

where
V
is the isometric isomorphism of Proposition
2.1
. In the next
result we determine the expression for
J_{\Omega},
and we will see that if

\tau
is a linear fractional Riemann map wtih real coefficients then

J_{\Omega}
has the same rule that
J.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
J_{\Omega}
is the conjugation defined in (
5.2
), then

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}f)(w)=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}(\overline{\tau^{\prime}(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}}))^{1/2}\overline{f(\tau(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}))},\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega),w\in\Omega.

In particular, if
\tau
is the linear fractional Riemann map
\tau(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)
then

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}f)(w)=\overline{f\left(\frac{a\overline{b}-b\overline{a}-a\overline{dw}+b\overline{cw}}{c\overline{b}-d\overline{a}-c\overline{dw}+d\overline{cw}}\right)},\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega),w\in\Omega

and, if
a,b,c
and
d
are real numbers, then

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}f)(w)=\overline{f(\overline{w})},\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega),w\in\Omega.

Proof.

For each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in\Omega,
we have

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}f)(w)

\displaystyle=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}(JVf)(\tau^{-1}(w))

\displaystyle=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}\overline{(Vf)(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)})}

(5.3)

\displaystyle=(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}(\overline{\tau^{\prime}(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}}))^{1/2}\overline{f(\tau(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}))}.

Assuming that
\tau
is the linear fractional Riemann map
\tau(z)=(az+b)(cz+d),
then a simple computation gives
\overline{\tau^{\prime}(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)})}=\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w))
for each
w\in\Omega.
In
view of (
5.1
), we need to compute
\tau(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}).
Note
that

\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}=\frac{\overline{b-dw}}{\overline{cw-a}}

and hence

\tau(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)})=\frac{a\left(\frac{\overline{b-dw}}{\overline{cw-a}}\right)+b}{c\left(\frac{\overline{b-dw}}{\overline{cw-a}}\right)+d}=\frac{a\overline{b}-b\overline{a}-a\overline{dw}+b\overline{cw}}{c\overline{b}-c\overline{dw}+d\overline{cw}-d\overline{a}}

which implies

(5.4)

\displaystyle(\tau^{\prime}(\tau^{-1}(w)))^{-1/2}(\overline{\tau^{\prime}(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}}))^{1/2}\overline{f(\tau(\overline{\tau^{-1}(w)}))}=\overline{f\left(\frac{a\overline{b}-b\overline{a}-a\overline{dw}+b\overline{cw}}{c\overline{b}-d\overline{a}-c\overline{dw}+d\overline{cw}}\right)}.

If
a,b,c,
and
d
are real numbers, then

\frac{a\overline{b}-b\overline{a}-a\overline{dw}+b\overline{cw}}{c\overline{b}-d\overline{a}-c\overline{dw}+d\overline{cw}}=\frac{-ad\overline{w}+bc\overline{w}}{-da+cb}=\frac{(-ad+bc)\overline{w}}{-ad+bc}=\overline{w}

which gives
(J_{\Omega}f)(w)=\overline{f(\overline{w})}.

∎

Assuming that
\tau
is a linear fractional Riemann map with real
coefficients, we describe all composition operators
C_{\Phi}
that
are
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
with real
coefficients and let
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega
such
that
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric on
H^{2}(\Omega)
.

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
such
that

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
such
that

(5.6)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Conversely, let
\Phi
be as in (
5.5
) or (
5.6
) with conditions
(
1
) and (
2
), respectively. If
\Phi
is a self-map
of
\Omega
such that
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega)
then
C_{\Phi}

is
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric.

Proof.

Suppose that
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric. Then
J_{\Omega}C_{\Phi}^{*}k_{u}=C_{\Phi}J_{\Omega}k_{u}
for all
u\in\Omega.
By Lemma
3.1
,
we get

(5.7)

\displaystyle(bd-ac)\Phi(u)+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\Phi(u)+bd-ac]w

\displaystyle=(bd-ac)u+[(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})u+bd-ac]\Phi(w),\quad u,w\in\Omega.

Differentiating (
5.7
) with respect to
u,
we obtain

(5.8)

\displaystyle(bd-ac)\Phi^{\prime}(u)+(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\Phi^{\prime}(u)w

\displaystyle=(bd-ac)+(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})\Phi(w),\quad u,w\in\Omega.

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2},
then (
5.8
) gives
\Phi^{\prime}(u)=1
for each
u\in\Omega.
Hence,
\Phi(u)=u+r
for some
r\in\mathbb{C}.

In the case,
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2},
we have from (
5.8
) that

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=\Phi^{\prime}(u)w+\frac{(\Phi^{\prime}(u)-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Conversely, in both cases we have
\overline{\Phi(\overline{w})}\in\Omega,

for each
w\in\Omega.
For
\Phi
as in (
5.5
) of condition (
1
),
we have
\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{\Phi(\overline{w})}=w+\overline{r}.
By
Theorem
4.2
, it follows that
C_{\Phi}^{*}=C_{\Phi_{\star}}.
Hence, for
each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in\Omega,
we obtain

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}C_{\Phi}^{*}f)(w)=\overline{(C_{\Phi}^{*}f)(\overline{w})}=\overline{f(\Phi_{\star}(\overline{w}))}=\overline{f(\overline{w+r})}=(C_{\Phi}J_{\Omega}f)(w)

which shows that
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric. Now, assume that
\Phi

is as in (
5.6
) of condition (
2
). Thus

\displaystyle\Phi_{\star}(w)=\overline{\Phi(\overline{w})}=\overline{r}w+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d^{2}}.

By Theorem
4.2
, it follows that
C_{\Phi}^{*}=C_{\Phi_{\star}}.
Hence,
for each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in\Omega,
we obtain

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega}C_{\Phi}^{*}f)(w)=\overline{C_{\Phi_{\star}}f(\overline{w})}=\overline{f\left(\overline{rw}+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right)}=(J_{\Omega}C_{\Phi}f)(w)

and we conclude that
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega}
-symmetric.
∎

From the proposition above, we see that if
\tau
is a linear fractional map with real coefficients then the class of

J_{\Omega}
-symmetric operators contains the bounded Hermitian and unitary
composition operators on
H^{2}(\Omega).

Now, we observe that if
\Psi
induces a unitary composition operator on

H^{2}(\Omega),
then Lemma
2.3
ensures that
J_{\Omega,\Psi}:=C_{\Psi}J_{\Omega}C_{\Psi}^{-1}
is a conjugation on

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map
z\in\mathbb{U}\mapsto(az+b)/(cz+d)
with real coefficients and suppose that
\Psi
induces an unitary composition

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then
\Psi(w)=w+si
for some

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega,\Psi}f)(w)=\overline{f(\overline{w}-2si)},\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega),w\in\Omega.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
then
\Psi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}
with
|r|=1
and

\displaystyle(J_{\Omega,\Psi}f)(w)=\overline{f\left(\frac{\overline{r}}{r}\overline{w}-\frac{2\mathrm{Im}(r)(bd-ac)}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}i\right)},\quad f\in H^{2}(\Omega),w\in\Omega.

Proof.

For the case (
1
), it follows from Theorem
4.2
that
\Psi(w)=w+r
for
some
r\in\mathbb{C}
where
\mathrm{Re}(\overline{r}(b\overline{d}-a\overline{c}))=0.
Since
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2},
we have
b\overline{d}\neq a\overline{c}
and hence
\mathrm{Re}(r)=0.
Considering
s=\mathrm{Im}(r),

then for each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in\Omega,
we obtain

(C_{\Psi}J_{\Omega}C_{\Psi}^{-1}f)(w)=\overline{(C_{\Psi^{-1}}f)(\overline{w}-is)}=\overline{f(\overline{w}-is-is)}=\overline{f(\overline{w}-2is)}.

For the case (
2
), Theorem
4.2
provides

\Psi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

with
|r|=1.
Then for each
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in\Omega,
we have

\displaystyle(C_{\Psi}J_{\Omega}C_{\Psi}^{-1}f)(w)

\displaystyle=\overline{(C_{\Psi}^{-1}f)\left(\overline{rw}+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\overline{f\left(\frac{\overline{r}}{r}\overline{w}+\frac{(\overline{r}-1)(bd-ac)}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}-\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}\right)}

\displaystyle=\overline{f\left(\frac{\overline{r}}{r}\overline{w}-\frac{2i\mathrm{Im}(r)(bd-ac)}{r(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}\right)}

and this completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\tau
be the linear fractional Riemann map

with real coefficients and let
\Psi
induce a unitary
composition operator and let
\Phi
be an analytic self-map of
\Omega
such that

C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega,\Psi}
-symmetric on
H^{2}(\Omega).

(1)

If
|c|^{2}=|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
such
that

(5.9)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=w+r.

(2)

If
|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}
then there is
r\in\mathbb{C}
such
that

(5.10)

\displaystyle\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Conversely, let
\Phi
be as in (
5.9
) or (
5.10
) with conditions
(
1
) and (
2
), respectively. If
\Phi
is an analytic self-map
of
\Omega
such that
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega),
then
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega,\Psi}
-symmetric.

Proof.

Observe that in both cases
C_{\Phi}
is
J_{\Omega,\Psi}
-symmetric if and
only if
J_{\Omega}C_{\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1}}J_{\Omega}=C_{\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1}}^{*}.
By Proposition

, this is equivalent to

\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1}
is as in (

) and (

), respectively.

Case 1:

We have
\Psi(w)=w+si
for some

s\in\mathbb{R}.
If
\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1}
is as in (
5.5
), then

(\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1})(w)=w+r
for some
r\in\mathbb{C}.
Hence,

\Phi(w-is)=(w-is)+r.
Since
w\in\Omega\mapsto w+is
is invertible, it
follows that
\Phi(w)=w+r.
Conversely, if
\Phi(w)=w+r
then a simple
computation shows that
(\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1})(w)=w+r.

Case 2:

|c|^{2}\neq|d|^{2}.

We have
\Psi(w)=sw+\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}
with
|s|=1.
If
\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1}
is as in (
5.6
), then

(\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1})(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

for some
r\in\mathbb{C}.
Hence

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\frac{w}{s}-\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{rw}{s}+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}-\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}

\displaystyle=r\left[\frac{w}{s}-\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}\right]+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Since

w\in\Omega\mapsto\frac{w}{s}-\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}

is invertible, it follows that

\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}.

Conversely, if
\Phi(w)=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}},
then for
each
w\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle(\Psi\circ\Phi\circ\Psi^{-1})(w)

\displaystyle=s\Phi(\Psi^{-1}(w))+\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

\displaystyle=s\left[r\Psi^{-1}(w)+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}\right]+\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

\displaystyle=s\left[\frac{rw}{s}+\frac{(r-s)(bd-ac)}{s(|c|^{2}-|d|^{2})}\right]+\frac{(s-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

\displaystyle=rw+\frac{(r-1)(bd-ac)}{|c|^{2}-|d|^{2}}

and the proof is complete.
∎

5.2.
Unbounded domains

We finish this section showing that the existence of fixed point of
\Phi
together with geometric properties of

\Omega
gives information about complex symmetry of
C_{\Phi}
on
H^{2}(\Omega).

First we recall some concepts, which deals with the dynamics of analytic self-maps of
\mathbb{U}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
let
\varphi^{[n]}
denote the
n
-th iterate of the analytic self-map
\varphi:\mathbb{U}\longrightarrow\mathbb{U}.
If
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{U}}
is a point such that the sequence
\varphi^{[n]}
converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{U}
to
\omega,
then
\omega
is said to be an
attractive point
for
\varphi.

Denjoy-Wolff Theorem is concerned with the existence of attractive points for analytic self-maps of
\mathbb{U}.
It states that (see
[
4
, Theorem 2.51]
), if
\varphi
is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{U}
which is not the identity or an elliptic automorphism of
\mathbb{U}
(that is, an automorphism with a fixed point in
\mathbb{U}
), then there is a unique point
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{U}}
such that
\varphi^{[n]}(z)\rightarrow\omega
when
n\rightarrow\infty,
for each
z\in\mathbb{U}
.

Theorem 5.6
.

If
\Omega
is unbounded and
\Phi
is a non-automorphic analytic
self-map of
\Omega
with a fixed point in
\Omega
and
such that
C_{\Phi}
is bounded on
H^{2}(\Omega)
, then
C_{\Phi}
is not
complex symmetric.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\Omega
be a fixed point of
\Phi.
Suppose that
C_{\Phi}
is complex symmetric. Then

W_{\varphi}
is also complex symmetric. Let
C
be a conjugation on

H^{2}(\mathbb{U})
such
W_{\varphi}C=CW_{\varphi}^{*}
and let
f=CK_{\beta},

where
\beta=\tau^{-1}(\alpha).
Since
\Phi(\alpha)=\alpha,
we have

W_{\varphi}^{*}K_{\beta}=K_{\beta}
(see Lemma
2.2
). Hence
W_{\varphi}CK_{\beta}=CK_{\beta},
that is,
f
is an eigenvector of
W_{\varphi}

corresponding to the eigenvalue
1.
Thus,
g=f/(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}
is an
analytic function on
\mathbb{U}
with

\displaystyle g\circ\varphi=\frac{f\circ\varphi}{(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}}\left(\frac{\tau^{\prime}}{\tau^{\prime}\circ\varphi}\right)^{1/2}f\circ\varphi=\frac{f}{(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}}=g.

This
implies that
g\circ\varphi^{[n]}=g
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}.
Since

\varphi
is not an automorphism of
\mathbb{U}
and
\varphi(\beta)=\beta,

it follows from Denjoy-Wolff Theorem that
\varphi^{[n]}
converges to
\beta

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{U}
when
n\rightarrow\infty.
Thus,
g=g(\beta).
Since
f
is not identically
zero,
g=f/(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}
is a non-zero constant. Hence
(\tau^{\prime})^{1/2}=g^{-1}f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{U}).
Thus,
\tau^{\prime}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{U}),
which implies that
\tau
is continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{U}}
(see
[
6
, Theorem 3]
) and therefore
\tau(\mathbb{U})=\Omega
is bounded. This contradiction implies that
C_{\Phi}
is not
complex symmetric.
∎
