Large induced subgraphs of random graphs with given degree sequences
Angus Southwell, Nicholas Wormald

Abstract
We study a random graph G G with given degree sequence 𝒅 {\bm{d}} , with the aim of characterising the degree sequence of the subgraph induced on a given set S S of vertices. For suitable 𝒅 {\bm{d}} and S S , we show that the degree sequence of the subgraph induced on S S is essentially concentrated around a sequence that we can deterministically describe in terms of 𝒅 {\bm{d}} and S S . We then give an application of this result, determining a threshold for when this induced subgraph contains a giant component. We also apply a similar analysis to the case where S S is chosen by randomly sampling vertices with some probability p p , i.e. site percolation, and determine a threshold for the existence of a giant component in this model. We consider the case where the density of the subgraph is either constant or slowly going to 0 0 as n n goes to infinity, and the degree sequence 𝒅 {\bm{d}} of the whole graph satisfies a certain maximum degree condition. Analogously, in the percolation model we consider the cases where either p p is a constant or where p → 0 p\to 0 slowly. This is similar to work of Fountoulakis in 2007 and Janson in 2009, but we work directly in the random graph model to avoid the limitations of the configuration model that they used.



1
Introduction

Random graphs with given degree sequences are a well-studied random graph model. To define the model, let
{\bm{d}}=(d(1),\dots,d(n))
be the degree sequence of a graph. Then
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
is a uniform random graph with degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
. This graph model has been the focus of much study recently, both due to improvements in the tools to study the model and also as it has found applications as a null model for studying networks (see
[
7
]
). Compared to binomial random graphs, this model is much better suited to studying properties of graphs where the degrees of the vertices are not concentrated around a particular value. However, this comes at the cost of ease of analysis: events that are trivial to study in the binomial random graph model (such as the adjacency of two vertices) are quite non-trivial in
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
and not fully understood in general.

In this paper we study induced subgraphs of random graphs with given degree sequences, i.e. the degree sequence of the subgraph
G[S]
of
G\in\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
induced by
S\subseteq V(G)
. Our main results are that the degree sequence of the induced subgraph is close to a model degree sequence
{\bm{d}}_{H}
defined in
Definition

2.2
. In particular, the distribution of the degree of a vertex in
G[S]
is approximately binomial, in terms of its degree in
G
and the density of
S
in
G
(see
Section

2
for a precise statement). We use this approximation to show that with probability tending to 1 as
n\to\infty
(a.a.s.), the large entries in the degree sequence of
G[S]
are asymptotically equal to the corresponding entries in
{\bm{d}}_{H}
, and the frequencies of small entries in each sequence are also close. We formally state this in
Theorem

2.3
.

The result mentioned above applies to a given subset
S=S(n)
. We also make use of it to prove a similar result about the model where
G
is again a uniformly random graph with degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
, but where
S
is chosen randomly by taking each vertex independently with probability
p=p(n)
. This model is commonly known as
(site-)percolated random graph
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
with survival probability
p
. This is in contrast to
bond percolation
, where edges are deleted instead of vertices. Percolation problems have been studied on a wide range of graphs, both deterministic and random, since the 1950s. See, for example, the work of
Broadbent and Hammersley 1957
,
Fountoulakis 2007
,
Janson 2009b
, or
McDiarmid et al. 2021
. In this paper, we use the phrase “percolated random graph” and the notation
G_{\bm{d}}(p)
to refer to a uniformly random graph with degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
after site percolation with survival probability
p
. In this model,
S
is a random variable, where each subset
S\subset[n]
occurs with probability
p^{|S|}(1-p)^{n-|S|}
. We define a model sequence
{\bm{d}}_{A}
(formally given in
Definition

2.5
), which is a function of
{\bm{d}}
and
p
, and show that, for suitable
{\bm{d}}
and
p
, the degree sequence of the percolated random graph is close to the model sequence
{\bm{d}}_{A}
.

The relationship between the large entries of
{\bm{d}}_{A}
and
{\bm{d}}_{S}
in the percolated random graph model is less precise than the corresponding relationship between
{\bm{d}}_{H}
and
{\bm{d}}_{S}
in the model where
S
is given. Instead of estimating each entry, we estimate the degree of a vertex conditional on it being a member of
S
. As well as this, we give a result about the sum of the large entries in each sequence.
This is because of the potential lack of concentration of large degrees in the induced subgraph. For instance, if only one vertex
v
exists of very large degree
i
, and the rest have small degree
j
, then the maximum degree in the induced subgraph will be close to
ip
with probability
p
(in the case that
v\in S
), and at most
j
otherwise.

A common problem studied in random graphs and percolation models is the existence of a giant component.
Molloy and Reed 1995
used the configuration model (often denoted
\mathcal{C}({\bm{d}})
) to investigate the existence of a giant component in
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
.
The configuration model is a model proposed by
Bollobás 1980
to study
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
which constructs a random (pseudo)graph with the correct degree sequence from a random pairing of sets of points in bins corresponding to the vertices. It is much easier to analyse than
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
, but the need to transfer results to
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
resulted in strict conditions on the degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
in
[
11
]
.
Recently,
Joos et al. 2018
generalised this to fully describe the threshold for the existence of a giant component in
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
in terms of
{\bm{d}}
, for all sequences
{\bm{d}}
.

The results of
Molloy and Reed 1995
were used by
Fountoulakis 2007
to study the threshold for the existence of a giant component in a percolated random graph.
Again this was done by studying the configuration model. A key element of his proof is the following fact: the percolated random graph is distributed uniformly at random conditioned on its degree sequence. He then studied the distribution of the resulting degree sequence in both site and bond percolation models. This result has strict requirements on
{\bm{d}}
, such as a maximum degree of at most
n^{1/9}
, bounded average degree, and a sufficiently nice limiting distribution.
Fountoulakis then applied the aforementioned results of
[
11
]
to prove a threshold for the existence of a giant component in (site or bond) percolated
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
. Janson
[
5
]
used similar ideas and tools from the theory of branching processes to prove a similar result for a wider range of degree sequences.
Recently,
Fountoulakis et al. 2022
used results in
[
6
]
to prove results about the threshold for the existence of a giant component in bond percolated
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
. These results apply for a wider range of degree sequences than considered in
[
3
]
and
[
5
]
, but also assume that
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
has bounded average degree and that the survival probability
p\in(0,1]
is a constant.

In this paper we apply the recent result of
Joos et al. 2018
and results about our model degree sequences (
{\bm{d}}_{H}
for when
S
is fixed and
{\bm{d}}_{A}
for when
S
is random) to determine a threshold for the existence of a giant component in
G[S]
. This serves as an example of how our main result can be used to study induced subgraphs of
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
: by combining this result with known thresholds for properties of
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
, one can determine thresholds for these properties in
G[S]
.
Notably, our results allow for cases where
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
has maximum degree slightly less than
\sqrt{|E(G)|}
(see
2.1
for the precise condition),
as long as the density of the subgraph (relative to the whole graph
G
) is either bounded away from 0 and 1, or goes to
0
sufficiently slowly (roughly up to
n^{-\varepsilon}
for some small constant
\varepsilon>0
). We can achieve this extension of the results in
[
3
]
on site percolation by carrying out our analysis in
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
directly, as opposed to using the configuration model, and we utilise the switching method heavily. In contrast to the results in
[
4
]
on bond percolation, our results also apply in cases where
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
has average degree only slightly less than
\sqrt{|E(G)|}
and the survival probability is at least
|E(G)|^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}
, for a small constant
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. In particular, for nearly regular degree sequences
{\bm{d}}
(e.g. typical degree sequences arising from
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
or
\mathcal{G}(n,m)
), our results apply when the maximum degree is
O(n^{1-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
. In upcoming work, we also use our degree sequence characterisation to prove thresholds for the connectivity of
G[S]
, as well as results on its chromatic number and its automorphism group.

Main results

Here we give much of the notation we use, as well as describing our main results. Let
{\bm{d}}
be a graphical sequence of length

{\bm{d}}=(d(1),\dots,d(n))

are at least

. We also define
\Delta=\Delta({\bm{d}})
to be the value of the largest entry in
{\bm{d}}
. For a set
A\subseteq[n]
, let

be the

of

. We also use
M({\bm{d}})
to denote
d([n])
, and call it the
total degree
of a sequence
{\bm{d}}
. For brevity, we use
M
to denote
M({\bm{d}})
where
{\bm{d}}
is the degree sequence of the underlying random graph
G\in\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
. We always use
S=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{s}\}\subset[n]
to denote the vertices of the induced subgraph of

.

2.1
Subgraphs induced on a vertex set

Suppose
S=S(n)\subseteq[n]
, and suppose
({\bm{d}},S)
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}(\gamma^{-1}\log M)^{12}\leq\delta d(S)

(2.1)

for some
\delta\to 0
sufficiently slowly as
n\to\infty
(equivalently,
M\to\infty
). Throughout the proofs, we use
\delta
and various powers of it to bound the rate at which certain functions grow or shrink. We assume that
\delta=\Omega((\log\log M)^{-1})
, or equivalently that
\delta^{-1}=O(\log\log M)
. Define
{J}=\delta^{-1/16}\gamma^{-1}\log M
.
We suppose that
\gamma<1-c
for some constant
c>0
, but we allow
\gamma=\gamma(n)\to 0
. That is,
d({\overline{S}})\geq cM
for some constant
c>0
, but
d(S)=o(M)
is possible. The condition on
\gamma
given in
2.1
implies that

\displaystyle\gamma\geq\delta^{-1/13}\frac{(\Delta^{2}\log^{12}M)^{1/13}}{M^{1/13}}.

This immediately implies that
\gamma=\omega(M^{-1/13})
, a fact used throughout the proofs.
The powers of
\log M
in our definitions and results are not necessarily optimised, either for studying the distribution of the induced degree sequence in general or for studying the threshold for the existence of giant components.

We also note that the conditions given in
2.1
imply that
\mathcal{G}({\bm{d}})
is non-empty.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
{\bm{d}}
is a sequence of length
n
with even sum such that there exists a set
S\subset[n]
satisfying (
2.1
), then
{\bm{d}}
is graphical, that is, there exists a graph with degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
.

Proof.

The inequality (
2.1
) implies that
\Delta^{2}=o(d(S))
, which implies that
\Delta^{2}=o(M)
(since
\gamma\leq 1
).
Koren 1973
(Section 1) states that if a sequence
{\bm{d}}
is not graphical, then there exist disjoint, non-empty sets
A,B\subset[n]
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in A}d(i)-\sum_{j\in B}d(j)>a(n-1-b),

where
a=|A|
and
b=|B|
. Suppose that such sets
A
and
B
existed. The left hand side of this inequality is at most
a\Delta
. Thus, this inequality could only be true if
b>n-1-\Delta
. This implies that
a<\Delta+1
, and also that
\sum_{j\in B}d(j)=\sum_{j\in[n]}d(j)-\sum_{j\notin B}d(j)\geq M({\bm{d}})-\Delta^{2}
. Since
\sum_{i\in A}d(i)\leq\Delta^{2}=o(M)
, it follows that the left hand side tends to
-\infty
as
n\to\infty
, which is a contradiction. Therefore the inequality cannot hold, and the sequence is graphical.
∎

In view of this lemma, by supposing that
{\bm{d}}
is a sequence of length
n
with all entries at least 1 and even sum that satisfies
2.1
, we may assume that
{\bm{d}}
is graphical, which is useful when talking about probabilities in associated random graph models.
We next define a deterministic sequence
{\bm{d}}_{H}
that in some sense represents a typical degree sequence of
G[S]
. Let
{\bm{d}}_{S}
be the degree sequence of the graph

. For an arbitrary sequence
{\bm{d}}
, let

be the number of entries of

.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
d
, a sequence of length

,
and a set

be given. To define

\displaystyle N(k)=\left\lfloor\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}\leq k\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

Below this index, it is difficult to obtain asymptotic values for each entry of

Let

p_{i}=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.d_{S}(v_{1})=i\right|d_{S}(v_{2})=i_{2},\dots,d_{S}(v_{k})=i_{k}\right)
for
i\in\left\{0,\dots,d(v_{1})\right\}
. Recall that
\gamma=d(S)/M
. Then

3.3
implies that, for all
i\in\left\{0,\dots,d(v_{1})\right\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{p_{i+1}}{p_{i}}=\frac{d(v_{1})-i}{i+1}\frac{d(S)}{d({\overline{S}})}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{d(v_{1})-i}{i+1}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

Thus, we can express
p_{i}
in terms of
p_{0}
:

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle={d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right)^{i}p_{0}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)^{i}

\displaystyle={d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right)^{i}p_{0}\exp\left(O\left(i\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle={d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right)^{i}p_{0}\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right),

(3.4)

since
i\leq d(v_{1})
and the error term goes to 0 when
d(v_{1})\leq{\bar{{J}}}
. The sum of all
p_{i}
must be equal to 1, and thus

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d(v_{1})}\left[{d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)^{i}\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)p_{0}\right].

Since the error is uniformly bounded for all terms in the sum, and all terms are positive, the relative error of the whole sum is at most
O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d(v_{1})}\left[{d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)^{i}p_{0}\right]=1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

It follows from the previous equation that

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d(v_{1})}{d(v_{1})\choose i}\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right)^{i}\right)^{-1}\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)^{d(v_{1})}\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right).

Applying
Section

3.2
for all
i\leq d(v_{1})
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left.d_{S}(v_{1})=i_{1}\right|d_{S}(v_{2})=i_{2},\dots,d_{S}(v_{k})=i_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(v_{1})}=i_{1}\right)\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right)

(3.5)

for each choice of
i_{j}\leq d(v_{j})
for all
j\leq k
. By the law of total probability, summing over all ordered tuples
(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})
such that
i_{j}\leq d(v_{j})
for all
j\geq 2
gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(v_{1})=i_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})}\mathbb{P}\left({d_{S}(v_{1})=i_{1}}\left|{\bigcup_{j=2}^{k}\{d_{S}(v_{j})=i_{j}\}}\right.\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{j=2}^{k}\{d_{S}(v_{j})=i_{j}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(v_{1})}=i_{1}\right)\left(1+O\left(d(v_{1})\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right).

(3.6)

Since
d(v_{1})\leq{\bar{{J}}}
, the two required bounds follow.
∎

With this result, we can prove
Theorem

2.3
(b). We also use the following Chernoff bound: if
X\sim\textup{Bin}\left((\right)n,p)
and
\varepsilon>0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|X-np|\geq\varepsilon np\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\varepsilon^{2}np/3\right).

(3.7)

Lemma 3.7
.

For all
k
such that
d(i_{k})\leq\delta^{1/32}{J}
, the following holds. We have
d_{H}(k)\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}
, and with probability
1-o(M^{-2})
, we have
d_{S}(k)\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
k
is such that
d(i_{k})\leq\delta^{1/32}{J}
. The first claim is equivalent to the claim that
N(2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J})\geq k
, where
N(x)
is defined in
Definition

2.2
as the sum over all
i\in S
of the probability that
Z_{d(i)}\leq x
, rounded to the nearest integer. Since
{\bm{d}}
is ordered in non-decreasing order, we know that
d(i_{j})\leq d(i_{k})\leq\delta^{1/32}{J}
for all
j\leq k
. Thus, the Chernoff bound given in
3.7
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{k})}\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\delta^{1/32}{J}}\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)=1-o(M^{-3})

(3.8)

for all
j\leq k
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}\leq 2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)\geq k-o(M^{-2}),

and thus
N(2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J})\geq k
. This proves the first claim. For the second claim, note that if
d(k)\leq\delta^{1/32}{J}
, then
k\in S_{\rm{small}}
. Therefore,
Lemma

3.6
implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=j\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{k})}=j\right)(1+o(1))
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)>2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{k})}>2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)(1+o(1))\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\delta^{1/32}{J}}>2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)(1+o(1)).

Combining this with
Equation

3.8
then implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)>2\gamma\delta^{1/32}{J}\right)=o(M^{-3})
. The second claim then follows from the union bound over all such
k
.
∎

3.3
Concentration of the counts of small vertex degrees

Recall the definitions of
Y_{i}
and
\tilde{y}_{i}
:

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{d_{S}(j)=i\right\}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{y}_{i}=\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(j)}=i\right).

We can show concentration of
Y_{i}
around
\tilde{y}_{i}
for all
i\leq{J}
. Firstly,
Lemma

3.6
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]=\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)=\tilde{y}_{i}\left(1+O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\right).

We also know that if
{\bm{d}}_{S}
satisfies the concentration bound given in
Theorem

2.3
(a) then
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})=Y_{i}
for all
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
. Since this is a.a.s. true, studying
Y_{i}
and
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})
are effectively equivalent for small
i
. It also follows that

\displaystyle n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})=N(i)-N(i-1)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}\leq i\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}\leq i-1\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}=i\right)\pm 1=\sum_{i\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}=i\right)\pm(1+o(M^{-5})).

Thus,
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})=\tilde{y}_{i}\pm 2
. We study
Y_{i}
rather than
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})
as the analysis is slightly neater.
We get the following bound on the variance of
Y_{i}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

For all
i\leq{J}
,
\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\left(1+O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\right)
.

Proof.

Let
V_{k}
be the indicator variable for the event that vertex
k\in S_{\rm{small}}
has induced degree
i
. Then
Y_{i}=\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}V_{k}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\textup{Var}\left(\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}V_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}\textup{Var}\left(V_{k}\right)+\sum_{j\neq k}{\mbox{Cov}\left(V_{j},V_{k}\right)},

(3.9)

where the last sum is over all ordered pairs
(j,k)\in S_{\rm{small}}^{2}
where
j\neq k
.
Each
V_{k}
is a Bernoulli random variable, and thus

\textup{Var}\left(V_{k}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)\right)
.
So the first summation is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}\textup{Var}\left(V_{k}\right)=\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)(1-\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right))\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right].

(3.10)

Now consider the covariance summation in
Equation

3.9
.
An application of
Lemma

3.6
provides a bound on the covariance terms in the second summation when
j,k\in S_{\rm{small}}
:

\displaystyle{\mbox{Cov}\left(V_{j},V_{k}\right)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i,\ d_{S}(k)=i\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

Thus, the summation in
Equation

3.9
of the covariances over all ordered pairs
(j,k)\in S_{\rm{small}}^{2}
where
j\neq k
is equal to

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)-\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)^{2}\right)O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\sum_{k\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(k)=i\right)\right)^{2}-\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)^{2}\right)O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

Noting that
\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)^{2}\in[0,\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq k\in S_{\rm{small}}}{\mbox{Cov}\left(V_{j},V_{k}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}\cdot O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

(3.11)

Combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) gives an upper bound on the variance of
Y_{i}
:

\displaystyle\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]+O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}.

The claim of the lemma immediately follows.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Recall that we assume
\Delta^{2}(\gamma^{-1}\log M)^{12}\leq\delta\gamma M
for some
\delta\to 0
slowly. Since we suppose that
\delta=\omega(\log^{-1}M)
, this implies a bound on
\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{J}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\frac{1}{\gamma^{-12}\log^{12}M}\leq\delta^{1/2}\gamma{J}^{-8}\log^{-1}M,

\displaystyle\frac{{J}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{J}^{2}M}{\gamma^{2}M^{2}}\leq\gamma{J}^{-8}o(\log^{-2}M)\leq\delta^{1/2}\gamma{J}^{-8}\log^{-1}M,

and in particular,
\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{J}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}=o(\gamma{J}^{-8}\log^{-1}M)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Suppose
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
. If
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\geq\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]|\geq\frac{\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]}{2{J}^{3}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\gamma^{-1}{J}^{-1}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right),

and if
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\leq\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]|\geq{J}^{4}\sqrt{\log M}\right)=O\left(\frac{\gamma^{-1}{J}^{-1}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right).

Proof.

First suppose that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\geq\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}
. Applying Chebyshev’s inequality with
t=\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]|\geq\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)}{\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right).

Now let
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}^{-3}
. Then since
{\bar{{J}}}\leq{J}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)}{\alpha^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4{J}^{6}}{\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]}+4\gamma^{-2}{J}^{7}O\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{J}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\leq 8(\gamma{J}\sqrt{\log M})^{-1},

since
Remark

3.9
states that
\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{J}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}=o(\gamma{J}^{-8}\log^{-1}M)
. This proves the first part of the lemma. Now suppose that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]\leq\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}
. Then it follows that

\displaystyle\textup{Var}\left(Y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]^{2}O\left({\bar{{J}}}\left(\frac{\Delta^{2}}{d(S)}+\frac{{\bar{{J}}}^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}\right)\right)\leq\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}(1+o(1)).

Then applying Chebyshev’s inequality with
t:={J}^{4}\sqrt{\log M}
gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]|\geq t\right)\leq\frac{\gamma^{-1}{J}^{7}\sqrt{\log M}(1+o(1))}{{J}^{8}\log M}\leq 2(\gamma{J}\sqrt{\log M})^{-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We note here that the above proof is the only point in the paper that we use the assumption that
\delta=\Omega((\log\log M)^{-1})
. If the bound on
\frac{J^{2}M}{d(S)^{2}}
in
Remark

3.9
is replaced with
\delta^{1/4}\gamma{J}^{-8}\log^{-1}M
, then the bound on
\delta
can be removed entirely. This could be useful if slightly different error bounds on
{\bm{d}}_{S}
were required, trading precision in the frequencies of the smaller entries for greater precision in the values of the larger entries.

Now we prove
Theorem

2.3
(c).

Corollary 3.11
.

With probability
1-o(1)
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})\right\rvert\leq\frac{\gamma n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})}{{J}^{3}}+\gamma{J}^{5}

for all
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
.

Proof.

First we bound
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]
in terms of
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})
using
Lemma

3.6
and
Remark

3.9
bounding the error term. This implies that for each
i\leq{J}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]=\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(d_{S}(j)=i\right)=\sum_{j\in S_{\rm{small}}}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(j)}=i\right)(1+o(\gamma{J}^{-8}))=(n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})\pm 2)(1+o(\gamma{J}^{-8})),

since
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})=\tilde{y}_{i}\pm 2
for all such
i
. We also know that, with probability
1-o(1)
,
Y_{i}=n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})
for all
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
. Then we apply
Lemma

3.10
and the triangle inequality to say that a.a.s.  for all
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
,

\displaystyle|n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})|\leq|Y_{i}-\tilde{y}_{i}|+2

\displaystyle\leq|Y_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]|+|\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]-\tilde{y}_{i}|+2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]}{2{J}^{3}}+{J}^{4}\sqrt{\log M}+o(\tilde{y}_{i}\gamma{J}^{-8})+2

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\tilde{y}_{i}(1+o(1))}{2{J}^{3}}+{J}^{4}\sqrt{\log M}+1+o(\tilde{y}_{i}\gamma{J}^{-8})+2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})}{{J}^{3}}+\gamma{J}^{5},

since
{J}=\delta^{-1/16}\gamma^{-1}\log M
. This completes the proof.
∎

The factor of
\gamma
in the “multiplicative” error term (the
\gamma n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})/{J}^{3}
term) is particularly useful because it implies that, for
k\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}
, a.a.s.

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=0}^{k}n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})\right|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\frac{\gamma n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})}{{J}^{3}}+\gamma{J}^{5}\right)\leq\gamma^{2}{J}^{6}+\frac{\gamma|S|}{{J}^{3}}.

Since
|S|\leq d(S)=\gamma M
and
M_{H}\sim\gamma^{2}M
, this implies that this sum is
O(M_{H}/{J}^{3})
. This is particularly useful in certain proofs. The bounds given in
Lemma

3.10
are not optimal in either the multiplicative or the additive error. An similar proof of
Lemma

3.10
with a different threshold for
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{i}\right]
can give better error margins in one direction or the other, but this choice strikes a balance that is sufficient for our purposes. With this result, we can now prove
Theorem

2.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

Corollary

3.11
proves part (c), and part (b) follows from
Lemma

3.7
.
Now it remains to prove part (a).
We show in
Lemma

3.4
that a.a.s.
d_{S}(k)=\gamma d(i_{k})\left(1\pm 8\delta^{1/64}\right)
uniformly for all
k
such that
d(i_{k})\geq\delta^{1/32}{J}
. Now we prove claim (a) by showing that deterministically
d_{H}(k)=\gamma d(i_{k})\left(1\pm 3\delta^{1/64}\right)
. For each
i\in S
such that
d(i)\geq\delta^{1/32}{J}
, define

\displaystyle d(i)^{-}

\displaystyle=\gamma d(i)\left(1-3\delta^{1/64}\right)

and

\displaystyle d(i)^{+}

\displaystyle=\gamma d(i)\left(1+3\delta^{1/64}\right).

The Chernoff bound given in (
3.7
) implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{d(j)}-\gamma d(j)|\geq\delta^{1/64}\gamma d(j)\right)\leq 2\exp{-3\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}}=2M^{-3}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}\leq d(i)^{+}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}<d(i)^{-}\right)=1-o(M^{-2}).

(3.12)

We use
Equation

3.12
to show that

\displaystyle N\left(d(i_{k})^{-}-1\right)\leq k-1\quad\mbox{and}\quad N\left(d(i_{k})^{+}\right)\geq k

(3.13)

for all
k\geq\ell
. Together these statements imply that for all
i_{k}
such that
d(i_{k})\geq\delta^{1/32}{J}
, the
k
th
entry of
{\bm{d}}_{H}
is between
d(i_{k})^{-}
and
d(i_{k})^{+}
. To prove these claims, recall that the degree sequence
{\bm{d}}
is ordered in non-decreasing order, so
d(i_{j})\leq d(i_{k})
for all
j\leq k
and
d(i_{j})\geq d(i_{k})
for all
j\geq k
.
This means that, by
Equation

3.12
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq k}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}<d(i_{k})^{-}\right)=o(M^{-1}).

This in turn implies that

\displaystyle N\left(d(i_{k})^{-}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}<d(i_{k})^{-}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\sum_{j<k}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}<d(i_{k})^{-}\right)+\sum_{j\geq k}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}<d(i_{k})^{-}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\left\lfloor k-1+o(M^{-1})+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=k-1.

This proves the first statement in
Equation

3.13
. An analogous idea proves the second half:

\displaystyle N\left(d(i_{k})^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\sum_{i\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i)}\leq d(i_{k})^{+}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left\lfloor\sum_{j\leq k}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}\leq d(i_{k})^{+}\right)+\sum_{j>k}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(i_{j})}\leq d(i_{k})^{+}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\geq\left\lfloor k(1-o(M^{-1}))+\frac{1}{2}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=k.

The combination of these mean that the
k
th
entry of
{\bm{d}}_{H}
has a value between
d(i_{k})^{-}

4.1
Total degree

For brevity, we define
M_{H}:=M({\bm{d}}_{H})
and
M_{S}:=M({\bm{d}}_{S})
. For an arbitrary sequence of non-negative integers
{\bm{d}}
, define
M_{2}({\bm{d}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n({\bm{d}})}d(i)^{2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

M({\bm{d}}_{H})\sim\gamma^{2}M
always. Furthermore, a.a.s.
M({\bm{d}}_{S})\sim M({\bm{d}}_{H})
and
M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{S})-M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})=o(M_{H})
.

Proof.

Recall
M({\bm{d}}_{H})=\sum_{i\leq\Delta}in_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})
and that
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})=N(i)-N(i-1)
. Then

\displaystyle M({\bm{d}}_{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\leq\Delta}i(N(i)-N(i-1))=\sum_{i\leq\Delta}i\left(\sum_{j\in S}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(j)}=i\right)\pm 1\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{d(j)}\right]\pm\Delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}M\pm\Delta^{2}.

Since
\Delta^{2}\gamma^{-12}\log^{12}M\leq\delta\gamma M
by assumption, it follows that
\Delta^{2}=o(\gamma^{2}M)
. This proves the first claim of the lemma. Now we focus on the second claim. We give the proof that a.a.s.
M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{S})\sim M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})
and it follows by an identical proof that a.a.s.
M_{S}\sim M_{H}
, since
d_{H}(i),d_{S}(i)\geq 0
. Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest index such that
d_{H}(i)>2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}
for all
i\geq k
. Similarly, let
k^{\prime}
be the analogous quantity for
{\bm{d}}_{S}
. Then

\displaystyle M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{1}{2}\gamma{J}}i^{2}n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{H}(i)^{2}.

Now we apply
Theorem

2.3
. Note that
2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}=o(\gamma{J})
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
k\leq k^{\prime}
. Then a.a.s.

\displaystyle M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}}i^{2}n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{H}(i)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}}i^{2}n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})+O\left(\frac{\gamma M_{H}}{{J}}\right)+o(\gamma{J}^{6})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{S}(i)^{2}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}}i^{2}n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{S}(i)^{2}(1+o(1))+o(M_{H})

\displaystyle=M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{S})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{k^{\prime}}d_{S}(i)^{2}(1+o(1))+o(M_{H}).

Theorem

2.3
(c) implies that a.a.s.
|k-k^{\prime}|\leq\frac{\gamma|S|}{{J}^{3}}+\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}^{6}
, and
d_{S}(i)\leq 2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}
for all
i\leq k^{\prime}
by definition. Therefore, a.a.s.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=k+1}^{k^{\prime}}d_{S}(i)^{2}\leq(2\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J})^{2}\left(\frac{\gamma|S|}{{J}^{3}}+\delta^{1/32}\gamma{J}^{6}\right)=o(M_{H}),

since
\gamma^{3}{J}^{8}=o(\gamma^{2}M/{J})
. Therefore, a.a.s.
M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})=M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{S})(1+o(1))
, which completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Concentration of number of non-isolated vertices in
G[S]

For a sequence
{\bm{d}}
of non-negative integers, let

be the maximal subsequence of

with all positive entries (that is, all entries equal to zero removed).

Lemma 4.2
.

n({\bm{d}}_{H}^{*})=\Omega(\gamma|S|)
always, and a.a.s.
n({\bm{d}}_{S}^{*})\sim n({\bm{d}}_{H}^{*})
.

Proof.

For the first result, recall that

for all

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

With probability
1-o(1)
, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(d_{S}(i)-d_{H}(i)\right)=o(M_{H})
for every

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(d_{S}(i)^{2}-d_{H}(i)^{2}\right)=o(M_{H})

Proof.

We provide the proof of the second claim, as the first claim follows immediately from this and the fact that

We show that deterministically the result holds when
{\bm{d}}_{S}
satisfies the bounds given in
Theorem

2.3
.
Since these concentration results hold with probability
1-o(1)
, this is sufficient to prove the claim. First consider the case where
k
is such that

. In this case, we know that a.a.s.
d_{S}(j)\sim d_{H}(j)
for all

, and thus by
Lemma

4.1
it follows that a.a.s.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{S}(i)^{2}

\displaystyle=M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{S})-\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{S}(i)^{2}=M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})+o(M_{H})-\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{H}(i)^{2}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=M_{2}({\bm{d}}_{H})-\sum_{i=k+1}^{s}d_{H}(i)^{2}+o(M_{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{H}(i)^{2}+o(M_{H}).

Thus, it only remains to consider the case where
k
is such that
d(i_{k})\leq\delta^{1/32}{J}
. Since we assume
{\bm{d}}_{S}
satisfies the bounds given in
Theorem

2.3
, it follows that

for all

. Consider that if

, then the contribution of this term is

. Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(d_{S}(i)^{2}-d_{H}(i)^{2}\right)=\sum_{i\leq k:d_{S}(i)\neq d_{H}(i)}\left(d_{S}(i)^{2}-d_{H}(i)^{2}\right).

in

and the first entry equal to

is at most

is an arbitrary good subset of
[n]
which also satisfies the concentration bounds given in
Equation

6.1
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,{J}\}
. Since
S
is good, it follows that
\gamma(S)=p(1\pm p^{2}M^{-1/4})
. The set
S
being good also implies that the conditions of
Lemma

3.6
are met, and also that
\frac{1}{3}p{J}(p)<\frac{1}{2}\gamma(S){J}(\gamma(S))
. Recall from
Definition

2.2
the definition of
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))
, the number of entries in
{\bm{d}}_{H}
(for a given set
S
) with value
i
. Since
i\leq\frac{1}{2}\gamma(S){J}(\gamma(S))
,

\displaystyle n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq{J}(\gamma(S))}|S_{j}|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}(S)=i\right)\pm\left(1+o(M^{-5})\right),

where
Z_{j}(S)\sim\textup{Bin}\left(j,\gamma(S)\right)
.
Suppose
i\leq\frac{1}{3}p{J}(p)=\frac{1}{3}\gamma(S){J}(\gamma(S))
. Since
S
is good, the Chernoff bound (
3.7
) implies that
\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}(S)=i\right)=o(M^{-5})
for all
j\geq\frac{1}{2}{J}(p)
. Thus, for
i\leq\frac{1}{3}p{J}(p)
,

\displaystyle n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}|S_{j}|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}(S)=i\right)\pm 2.

(6.2)

Now we compare the summation to the value of
\tilde{w}_{i}
. If
d(v)\leq{J}(p)
, then for all
i\leq d(v)
it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X_{d(v)}=i\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{d(v)}=i\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p}{\gamma(S)}\right)^{i}\left(\frac{1-p}{1-\gamma(S)}\right)^{d(v)-i}

\displaystyle=\left(1+O\left(\frac{p^{2}i}{M^{1/4}}\right)\right)\left(1+O\left(\frac{p^{3}(d(v)-i)}{M^{1/4}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=1+O\left(\frac{p^{2}{J}(p)}{M^{1/4}}\right).

Recall that the conditions on
p
(specifically
Equation

2.2
) imply that
p=\omega(M^{-1/13})
. This implies that
\frac{p^{2}{J}(p)}{M^{1/4}}\leq\frac{p}{M^{1/100}{J}(p)^{3}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}|S_{j}|\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j}(S)=i\right)=\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}|S_{j}|\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)\left(1+o\left(\frac{p}{{J}(p)^{3}}\right)\right).

(6.3)

Since
i\leq\frac{1}{3}p{J}(p)
, the Chernoff bound given in (
3.7
) implies that

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{i}=p\sum_{v\in V}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{d(v)}=i\right)=p\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}n_{j}({\bm{d}})\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)+o(1).

Since we assume that
S
satisfies the concentration inequalities given in
Equation

6.1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}|S_{j}|\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)\left(pn_{j}({\bm{d}})+o\left(\frac{p^{2}n_{j}({\bm{d}})}{{J}(p)^{3}}+{J}(p)^{4}\sqrt{\log M}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)pn_{j}({\bm{d}})\left(1+o\left(\frac{p}{{J}(p)^{3}}\right)\right)+\sum_{j\leq{J}(p)}\mathbb{P}\left(X_{j}=i\right)o\left(\frac{p{J}(p)^{5}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{w}_{i}+o\left(\frac{p\tilde{w}_{i}}{{J}(p)^{3}}+\frac{p{J}(p)^{6}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right).

(6.4)

Combining
Equations

6.2
,
6.3
, and
6
it follows that, conditional on the aforementioned good set

,

\displaystyle n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))=\tilde{w}_{i}+o\left(\frac{p\tilde{w}_{i}}{{J}(p)^{3}}+\frac{p{J}(p)^{6}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right).

(6.5)

Since
S
is good, the pair
({\bm{d}},S)
also satisfy the conditions of
Theorem

(c). This implies that, for this fixed set

, a.a.s.

\displaystyle|n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))|\leq\frac{\gamma(S)n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))}{{J}(\gamma(S))^{3}}+\gamma(S){J}(\gamma(S))^{5}.

(6.6)

Recall that
n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{A})=\tilde{w}_{i}\pm 1
for all

. Thus, the bounds given in

and
6.6
and the triangle inequality imply that, conditional on the event that

is good and also satisfies the concentration bounds given in

\displaystyle|n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{A})|\leq|n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{S})-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))|+|n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{H}(S))-n_{i}({\bm{d}}_{A})|\leq\frac{pn_{i}({\bm{d}}_{A})}{{J}(p)^{3}}(1+o(1))+o\left(\frac{p{J}(p)^{6}}{\sqrt{\log M}}\right)

for all

. Since

satisfies these conditions a.a.s., this proves part (d).
∎
