Consistency and asymptotic normality in a class of nearly unstable processes
Marie Badreau, Frédéric Proïa

Abstract
. This paper deals with inference in a class of stable but nearly-unstable processes. Autoregressive processes are considered, in which the bridge between stability and instability is expressed by a time-varying companion matrix A n A_{n} with spectral radius ρ ⁡ ( A n ) < 1 \rho(A_{n})<1 satisfying ρ ⁡ ( A n ) → 1 \rho(A_{n})\rightarrow 1 . This framework is particularly suitable to understand unit root issues by focusing on the inner boundary of the unit circle. Consistency is established for the empirical covariance and the OLS estimation together with asymptotic normality under appropriate hypotheses when A A , the limit of A n A_{n} , has a real spectrum, and a particular case is deduced when A A also contains complex eigenvalues. The asymptotic process is integrated with either one unit root (located at 1 or − 1 -1 ), or even two unit roots located at 1 and − 1 -1 . Finally, a set of simulations illustrate the asymptotic behavior of the OLS. The results are essentially proved by L 2 L^{2} computations and the limit theory of triangular arrays of martingales.



1.
Introduction and motivation

This paper is dedicated to the boundary between stationarity and integration in time series, which has long since proven to be crucial for practitioners,
e.g.
in econometrics or finance. First of all, we refer the reader to
Brockwell and Davis 1991
for a large overview of linear time series. In the context of autoregressive processes (AR), it is well-known that the least squares (OLS) estimator is strongly consistent wherever its characteristic roots lie, see
Lai and Wei 1983
, but with very different convergence rates and limit distributions whether the process is stationary or integrated, or even explosive. According to the terminology of
Duflo 1997
, a stationary process will be described as
stable
and an integrated process as
unstable
in what follows. In the first case, the OLS estimator is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent with a Gaussian asymptotic behavior whereas in the other case, it is (at least)
n
-consistent with an asymmetrical asymptotic behavior that can be written as functionals of a standard Wiener process
(W(t),\,t\in[0,1])
. Precisely, for example in the univariate case, in the stable (left-hand side) and unstable (right-hand side,
\theta=\pm 1
) cases, either

\sqrt{n}\,\big(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\big)~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\mathcal{N}(0,1-\theta^{2})\hskip 14.22636pt\text{or}\hskip 14.22636ptn\,\big(\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\theta\big)~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\frac{\pm\int_{0}^{1}W(t)\,\mathrm{d}W(t)}{\int_{0}^{1}W^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t}

and these results are extended to the general case (see
e.g.
the substantial work of
Chan and Wei 1988
). This discontinuity has motivated numerous studies devoted to intermediate AR models aimed at bridging these two situations. Most of these studies involve the AR(1) process with time-varying coefficients, which is an easy-to-use relevant case to understand the underlying dynamics. Random coefficients have been suggested to bypass the difficulty: as it is explained in
Horváth and Trapani 2016
, thanks to a specific self-normalized WLS estimator of
\theta
, “there is no
unit root problem
in case of the RCA model”. Indeed, the authors get the asymptotic normality irrespective of the average value of the coefficient, only the rate is affected. In a different context, the so-called
volatility induced stationarity
detailed
e.g.
in
Ling 2004
and
Nielsen and Rahbek 2014
, enable to accommodate integration and stationarity in a double autoregression through stochastic volatility despite a unit root in the conditional mean of the process, see also
Trapani 2021
. We refer the reader to all the references contained in those papers. An AR(1) with non-random but time-varying coefficients may also be written in the triangular form

\theta_{n}=-1+c/v_{n}
. In the special case where

), the rate is

, whereas we will only focus here on the stable alternative to the unstable process. In the same vein,

develop a limit theory for nonstationary vector autoregressions with mixed roots in the vicinity of unity involving persistent and explosive components.

for all

. Along the same lines, we will thus consider that

\rho(A_{n})\rightarrow 1
, which corresponds to a stable but nearly unstable AR
(p)

has established some moderate deviation principles for the empirical covariance and the OLS estimator that hold for any

satisfies a large deviation principle with a speed
(b_{n}^{\,2})
and a rate function depending on the renormalized limit covariance of the process. Let us also mention the weak unit roots of
Park 2003
containing more applications than ours, including faster rates of convergence for the nearly unit root and non-linear models, but in a more restricted setting (we will come back to this in due time), or the recent work of
Buchmann and Chan 2013
who introduce a perturbation in the Jordan canonical form of the AR
(p)

in fine
different kind of results are obtained
via
different technical tools. Now, to complete these deviations and to generalize the results of
Phillips and Magdalinos 2007
, we aim at proving the consistency and asymptotic normality of the OLS estimator (
1.4
). The strategy remains the same, but the calculation steps will prove to be much trickier. In the second section, the assumptions and main results are provided together with comments and examples. The third section is dedicated to the technical proofs whereas the fourth section is the empirical part of the paper, containing simulations. A quick conclusion with considerations about further improvements ends the paper.

2.
Main results

Remark 2.1
.

For the empirical covariance of the process to be consistent, it must be renormalized by
1-\rho_{n}
. In the stable case (stationary and ergodic), it is well-known that
S_{n}
converges at rate
n
whereas in the unstable case (integrated with one unit root, either positive or negative),
S_{n}
converges at rate
n^{2}
. Let
p=1
to simplify. Then, under suitable hypotheses, in the stable (left-hand side) and unstable (right-hand side) cases, either

\frac{S_{n}}{n}~\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}~\gamma_{0}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{or}\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{S_{n}}{n^{2}}~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\sigma^{2}\!\int_{0}^{1}W^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

where
\gamma_{0}>0
is the variance of the stationary process and
(W(t),\,t\in[0,1])
is a standard Wiener process. Proposition
2.2
establishes the convergence of
S_{n,\,n}
at rate
n/(1-\rho_{n})\propto n^{1+\alpha}
for
v_{n}=n^{\alpha}
and
0<\alpha<1
. One can see that our model bridges the stable and unstable cases in terms of empirical covariance even if the limit behaviors as
\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}
and
\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}
do not make a connection with
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
.

Even if it is instructive to control the behavior of the covariance matrix in a stationary process, that result shall
not
help to prove the following theorems, dedicated to the OLS estimator, since the limit matrix is not invertible as soon as
p>1
. Indeed a direct calculation shows that
(A^{*}\otimes A^{*})\,e_{p^{2}}
is a column vector of size
p^{2}
with entries
\pm\pi^{\,2}_{11}
and different rates of convergence must be used to achieve our objectives.

Remark 2.2
.

On the basis of Theorem
2.3
, the triangle inequality directly implies that, under the same hypotheses,

(2.7)

\widehat{\theta}_{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~\theta.

But it is important to note that Theorem
2.4
cannot lead to the corresponding asymptotic normality. To understand this, consider for example the univariate setting of Corollary
2.5
where
\theta_{n}-\theta\propto 1/v_{n}
. Then,

\sqrt{n\,v_{n}}\,\big|\theta_{n}-\theta\big|\propto\sqrt{\frac{n}{v_{n}}}~\longrightarrow~+\infty.

However, this is not surprising. Indeed, the limit AR process generated by
\theta
is unstable. In that case, the OLS estimate is still consistent but not asymptotically normal.

In terms of estimation, the mixing induced by

is somewhat troublesome because it relies on the true and unknown eigenvalues of
A_{n}
. Since the eigenvalues of a square matrix depend continuously on its entries, see
e.g.
Thm. 2.4.9.2 of
Horn and Johnson 2012
, the consistency of
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
would justify replacing
A_{n}
by
\widehat{A}_{n}
before calculating its spectrum, so as to get consistent estimates of all quantities appearing in Theorem
2.4
and build hypotheses tests. For simulations (Section
4
), the following corollary will be used to illustrate the latter result.

Corollary 2.6
.

Assume that the assumptions of Theorem
2.4
hold. Then, for
\lambda_{1}=\pm 1
, we have the asymptotic distribution

\frac{\pi_{11}^{\,2}\,n\,v_{n}}{2\,c}\,\left[\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{(\lambda_{1}\rho_{n})^{\,i-1}}\,\big(\widehat{\theta}_{n,\,i}-\theta_{n,\,i}\big)\right]^{\,2}~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\chi^{2}_{1}.

Proof.

This is in fact the first marginal convergence of the asymptotic normality combined with the continuous mapping theorem, (

is
(1,1/\lambda_{n,\,1},\ldots,1/\lambda_{n,\,1}^{p-1})
.
∎

Lemma 3.1

Proof.

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
(H1)
holds. Then in the context of Lemma
3.1
, for all
n>n_{0}
, the first row of
P_{n}^{-1}
is real.

Proof.

This is obvious for
p=1
, so let
p>1
. Suppose that, for
j\geqslant 2
,
\lambda_{n,\,j}\in\textnormal{sp}(A_{n})
is a complex eigenvalue so that, since
A_{n}
is real, we must also have
\bar{\lambda}_{n,\,j}\in\textnormal{sp}(A_{n})
. Taking
i=1
in formula (3.4) of
Proïa 2020
, the first row of
P_{n}^{-1}
is written

\left(\frac{T_{n,\,1,\,k}\,\lambda_{n,\,1}^{p-1}\,\prod_{j=2}^{p}\lambda_{n,\,j}}{\prod_{j=2}^{p}(\lambda_{n,\,j}-\lambda_{n,\,1})}\right)_{1\,\leqslant\,k\,\leqslant\,p}

where

can be retrieved from the relation

\prod_{j=2}^{p}\left(X-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n,\,j}}\right)=T_{n,\,1,\,1}+T_{n,\,1,\,2}\,X+\ldots+T_{n,\,1,\,p}\,X^{p-1}

for real values of

. Combining such terms together with the fact that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
and for any

,

(x-z)(x-\bar{z})=x^{2}+|z|^{2}-2\,\textnormal{Re}(z)x\,\in\mathbb{R}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\lambda_{n,\,1}=\pm\rho_{n}\in\mathbb{R},

each entry of the first row of
P_{n}^{-1}
is real.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
(H1)
holds. Then, each element of the first column of

We remind that the entries of the first column of

are denoted by
\pi_{k1}

in the same formula as in the previous proof,

(3.2)

\pi_{k1}=\frac{(-\lambda_{k})^{p-1}}{\prod_{\ell\,\neq\,k}(\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda_{k})}.

Under
(H1)
, all eigenvalues are distinct and non-zero, which concludes the proof.
∎

.

(H3)

Proof.

Indeed, since

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
(H1)
,
(H2) (H21)
and
(H3)
hold. Then,

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}(1-\rho_{n})\,B_{n}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\,(A^{*}\otimes A^{*})

where
A^{*}
is defined in Proposition
2.2
.

Proof.

Once again the result is obvious for
p=1
, so let
p>1
. Because
\rho(A_{n}\otimes A_{n})<1
(see the proof of Lemma
3.4
),

B_{n}^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}A_{n}^{k}\otimes A_{n}^{k}.

For
n>n_{0}
, it follows from simple linear algebra that

(3.3)

B_{n}^{-1}=(P_{n}\otimes P_{n})\,\left[\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}D_{n}^{k}\otimes D_{n}^{k}\right](P_{n}^{-1}\otimes P_{n}^{-1}).

From Lem. 3.1 of
Proïa 2020
, we know that
P_{n}\rightarrow P
where the limit matrix
P
is invertible under
(H1)
, as a Vandermonde matrix with distinct entries. Thus we also have
P_{n}^{-1}\rightarrow P^{-1}
. It remains to observe that
D_{n}^{k}\otimes D_{n}^{k}
is a
p^{2}\times p^{2}
diagonal matrix with top-left element
(\pm\rho_{n})^{\,2k}
and all other elements given either by
(\pm\rho_{n})^{\,k}\,\lambda^{k}_{n,\,i}
, depending on whether
\lambda_{1}=1
or
\lambda_{1}=-1
, or by
\lambda^{k}_{n,\,i}\,\lambda^{k}_{n,\,j}
for
2\leqslant i,j\leqslant p
. Under
(H3)
,

(3.4)

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}(1-\rho_{n})\,\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(\pm\rho_{n})^{\,2k}=\frac{1}{2}

and, for
2\leqslant i,j\leqslant p
,

(3.5)

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(\pm\rho_{n})^{\,k}\,\lambda^{k}_{n,\,i}=\frac{1}{1\mp\lambda_{i}}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lambda^{k}_{n,\,i}\,\lambda^{k}_{n,\,j}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}.

Under
(H2) (H21)
, we can conclude that

(3.6)

\lim_{n\,\rightarrow\,+\infty}(1-\rho_{n})\,B_{n}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\,(P\otimes P)\,K_{p^{2}}\,(P^{-1}\otimes P^{-1})

where
K_{p^{2}}
is given in (
2.3
). In a more ‘elegant’ way,

\frac{1}{2}\,(P\otimes P)\,K_{p^{2}}\,(P^{-1}\otimes P^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}\,(P\,K_{p}\,P^{-1})\otimes(P\,K_{p}\,P^{-1})

using the fact that
K_{p^{2}}=K_{p}\otimes K_{p}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume that
(H1)
and
(H2) (H21)
hold, and that
p>1
. Then, the
(p-1)\times(p-1)
bottom-right block of

, which is the symmetric matrix given by

\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\pi_{21}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{2}^{2}}&\ldots&\frac{\pi_{21}\pi_{p1}}{1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{p}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\pi_{p1}\pi_{21}}{1-\lambda_{p}\lambda_{2}}&\ldots&\frac{\pi_{p1}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{p}^{2}}\end{pmatrix},

Proof.

First under
(H1)
, Lemma
3.3
implies
\pi_{k1}\neq 0
for all

, so the result is obvious for

. Now let

and denote by

the

(so that

) and note that, although it is not a Cauchy matrix, it is closely related to it (see Sec. 0.9.12 of

(1-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})/(\pi_{j1}\pi_{k1})

, and subtracting the last column from each other column, it follows that

\displaystyle d_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=2}^{k}\frac{\pi_{j1}\pi_{k1}}{(1-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{k})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\hskip 14.22636pt\times\begin{vmatrix}\frac{b_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}{(1-\lambda_{2}^{2})(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{k})}&\frac{b_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3})(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{k})}&\ldots&\frac{b_{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{k-1})(1-\lambda_{2}\lambda_{k})}&*\\
\frac{b_{3}(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{3}\lambda_{2})(1-\lambda_{3}\lambda_{k})}&\frac{b_{3}(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}{(1-\lambda_{3}^{2})(1-\lambda_{3}\lambda_{k})}&\ldots&\frac{b_{3}(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{3}\lambda_{k-1})(1-\lambda_{3}\lambda_{k})}&*\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{b_{k-1}(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{2})(1-\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{k})}&\frac{b_{k-1}(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})(\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{3})(1-\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{k})}&\ldots&\frac{b_{k-1}(\lambda_{k-1}-\lambda_{k})^{2}}{(1-\lambda_{k-1}^{2})(1-\lambda_{k-1}\lambda_{k})}&*\\
0&0&\ldots&0&1\end{vmatrix}

where

,

(

The solution of the recurrence is given by

(3.8)

\forall\,2\leqslant k\leqslant p,\hskip 14.22636ptd_{k}=\left(\prod_{j=2}^{k}\frac{\pi_{j1}^{2}}{1-\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)\left(\prod_{j=2}^{k}\prod_{i\,<\,j}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})^{2}}{(1-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})^{2}}\right).

If

holds, then all eigenvalues are distinct and since

is non-zero for all

is non-zero for all

Lemma 3.8

(Isolated terms)
.

Assume that
(H1)
and
(H3)
hold. Then,

\frac{T_{n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0

where
T_{n}
is given in (
3.12
).

Proof.

First, since
\Phi_{n,\,0}
has a finite moment of order 2, Markov’s inequality directly gives

(3.13)

\frac{\|\Phi_{n,\,0}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}^{\,T}\|}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

Then, by the triangle inequality and exploiting Lemma
3.1
, for all
n>n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{n,\,n}\,\Phi_{n,\,n}^{\,T}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\,\|A_{n}^{n}\|^{2}\,\|\Phi_{n,\,0}\|^{2}+2\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{n}^{k}\,E_{n-k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{st}\,\rho_{n}^{\,2n}\,\|\Phi_{n,\,0}\|^{2}+2\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{n}^{k}\,E_{n-k}\right\|^{2}~\,\overset{\Delta}{=}\,~T_{1,\,n}+T_{2,\,n}.

Note that, according to
(H3)
,

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{\,2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{c}{v_{n}}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{-2c\,n}{v_{n}}+o\left(\frac{n}{v_{n}}\right)\right)~\longrightarrow~0.

\mathbb{E}[|T_{1,\,n}|]\rightarrow 0
, and thus

(3.14)

\frac{T_{1,\,n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{n}^{k}\,E_{n-k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\llangle P_{n}\,D_{n}^{k}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,E_{n-k},\,P_{n}\,D_{n}^{\ell}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,E_{n-\ell}\rrangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\textnormal{tr}\big(P_{n}\,D_{n}^{\ell}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,E_{n-\ell}\,E_{n-k}^{\,T}\,(P_{n}^{-1})^{\,T}\,D_{n}^{k}\,P_{n}^{\,T}\big).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|T_{2,\,n}|]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\sigma^{2}\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\textnormal{tr}\big(P_{n}\,D_{n}^{k}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,K_{p}\,(P_{n}^{-1})^{\,T}\,D_{n}^{k}\,P_{n}^{\,T}\big)

\displaystyle C_{st}\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\|P_{n}\,D_{n}^{k}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,K_{p}\|^{2}

where
K_{p}
is given in (
2.3
), and obviously satisfies

. Hence, using the same arguments as before,

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|T_{2,\,n}|]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{st}\,\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\rho_{n}^{\,2k}

\displaystyle C_{st}\,v_{n}\,\frac{1-\exp\left(\frac{-2c\,n}{v_{n}}+o\left(\frac{n}{v_{n}}\right)\right)}{2c-\frac{c^{2}}{v_{n}}}~=~O(v_{n}).

\frac{T_{2,\,n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

), (

Lemma 3.9

Assume that

\frac{Z_{n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0

where
Z_{n}
is given in (
3.12
).

Proof.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|Z_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{n,\,0}\,E_{1}^{\,T}\|+\left\|\sum_{k=2}^{n}A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\,E_{k}^{\,T}\right\|+\left\|\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}A_{n}^{\ell}\,E_{k-1-\ell}\,E_{k}^{\,T}\right\|

\displaystyle\,\overset{\Delta}{=}\,

\displaystyle T_{0,\,n}+T_{1,\,n}+T_{2,\,n}.

Clearly, by independence and existence of moments of order 2,

(3.17)

\frac{T_{0,\,n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

Then, a direct calculation shows that

\displaystyle T_{1,\,n}^{\,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\llangle A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\,E_{k}^{\,T},\,A_{n}^{\ell-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\,E_{\ell}^{\,T}\rrangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\textnormal{tr}\big(\Phi_{n,\,0}^{\,T}\,(A_{n}^{\,T})^{\ell-1}\,A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\,E_{k}^{\,T}E_{\ell}\big)

exploiting the cyclic property of the trace. Hence, for any
n>n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{1,\,n}^{\,2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\textnormal{tr}\big(\mathbb{E}\big[\Phi_{n,\,0}^{\,T}\,(A_{n}^{\,T})^{\ell-1}\,A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\big]\,\mathbb{E}[E_{k}^{\,T}E_{\ell}]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\textnormal{tr}\big(\Phi_{n,\,0}^{\,T}\,(A_{n}^{k-1})^{\,T}\,A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\big)\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[\|A_{n}^{k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,0}\|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{st}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\rho_{n}^{\,2(k-1)}\,\mathbb{E}[\|\Phi_{n,\,0}\|^{2}]~=~O(v_{n})

using (
3.15
), Lemma
3.1
and the second-order moments of
\Phi_{n,\,0}
. Since
v_{n}=o(n)
,

(3.18)

\frac{T_{1,\,n}^{\,2}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{so that}\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{T_{1,\,n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

To handle the last term of the decomposition, let, for any
k\geqslant 1
,

U_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{n}^{k-j}\,E_{j}

and note that

(3.19)

\forall\,k\neq\ell,\hskip 14.22636pt\mathbb{E}\big[U_{\ell-1}^{\,T}\,U_{k-1}\,E_{k}^{\,T}E_{\ell}\big]=0.

Now,

\displaystyle T_{2,\,n}^{\,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=2}^{n}U_{k-1}\,E_{k}^{\,T}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\llangle U_{k-1}\,E_{k}^{\,T},\,U_{\ell-1}\,E_{\ell}^{\,T}\rrangle~=~\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n}\textnormal{tr}\big(U_{\ell-1}^{\,T}\,U_{k-1}\,E_{k}^{\,T}E_{\ell}\big)

exploiting again the cyclic property of the trace. Thanks to relations (
3.19
), it follows that, for
n>n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{2,\,n}^{\,2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\textnormal{tr}\big(\mathbb{E}\big[U_{k-1}^{\,T}\,U_{k-1}\big]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\textnormal{tr}\big((A_{n}^{\,T})^{k-1-i}\,A_{n}^{k-1-j}\,\mathbb{E}[E_{j}\,E_{i}^{\,T}]\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{4}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\textnormal{tr}\big((A_{n}^{\,T})^{k-1-j}\,A_{n}^{k-1-j}\,K_{p}\big)=\sigma^{4}\,\sum_{k=2}^{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-2}\|A_{n}^{\ell}\,K_{p}\|^{2}~=~O(nv_{n})

where
K_{p}
is given in (
2.3
), and where the rate
v_{n}
is obtained
via
the same lines as (
3.15
). Consequently,

(3.20)

\frac{T_{2,\,n}^{\,2}}{n^{2}}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{so that}\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{T_{2,\,n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

The combination of (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (
3.20
) concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

Suppose first that the eigenvalues of

are real, and consider the filtration

\forall\,n\geqslant 1,\,\forall\,1\leqslant k\leqslant n,\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k}=\sigma(\Phi_{n,\,0},\,\varepsilon_{1},\,\ldots,\,\varepsilon_{k}).

For all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\backslash\{0\}
, let also

(3.26)

m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}=a^{\,T}\,V_{n}^{\,1/2}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,k-1}\,\varepsilon_{k}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptM_{n}^{(a)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}

the way it appears in the right-hand side of (
3.25
). The sequence
(m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})
is clearly a scalar martingale difference array w.r.t.
\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k}
at fixed
n
and for
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. The predictable quadratic variation of
M_{n}^{(a)}
is

\displaystyle\langle M^{(a)}\rangle_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\,a^{\,T}\,V_{n}^{\,1/2}\,P_{n}^{-1}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Phi_{n,\,k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,k-1}^{\,T}\right](P_{n}^{-1})^{\,T}\,V_{n}^{\,1/2}\,a

since
(\varepsilon_{k})
is a white noise and
\Phi_{n,\,k-1}
is obviously
\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}
-measurable. Together with (
3.24
) and the definition of
S_{n,\,n-1}
in (
1.4
), that leads to the convergence

(3.27)

\frac{\langle M^{(a)}\rangle_{n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~\sigma^{2}\,a^{\,T}Ha\,>\,0

where

is the covariance matrix given in (
2.2
), positive definite as shown in the proof of Lemma
3.6
when

has only real eigenvalues. To apply the central limit theorem for arrays of martingales, see
e.g.
Thm. 1 of Sec. 8 in
Pollard 1984
, it remains to show that the Lindeberg’s condition is satisfied, in other words that

(3.28)

\forall\,\epsilon>0,\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[(m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})^{2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{|m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}|\,>\,\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}\big]~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0.

To prove (
3.28
), we can first see that, for any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})^{2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{|m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}|\,>\,\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{(a)}_{n,\,k-1}\,\xi_{n,\,k}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle T^{(a)}_{n,\,k-1}\,\sup_{1\,\leqslant\,k\,\leqslant\,n}\xi_{n,\,k}

where
T^{(a)}_{n,\,k-1}\,\overset{\Delta}{=}\,a^{\,T}\,V_{n}^{\,1/2}\,P_{n}^{-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,k-1}\,\Phi_{n,\,k-1}^{\,T}(P_{n}^{-1})^{\,T}\,V_{n}^{\,1/2}\,a\,>\,0
and with, by Hölder’s and Markov’s inequalities,

\displaystyle\xi_{n,\,k}

\displaystyle\,\overset{\Delta}{=}\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon_{k}^{\,2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{|m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}|\,>\,\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\eta_{\nu}^{\,2/(2+\nu)}\,\mathbb{P}\big((m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})^{2}\,>\,\epsilon^{\,2}n\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}\big)^{\nu/(2+\nu)}\leqslant~C_{st}\left(\frac{T^{(a)}_{n,\,k-1}}{n}\right)^{\!\nu/(2+\nu)}

in which
\eta_{\nu}
is the moment of order
2+\nu
of
(\varepsilon_{k})
, as defined in the statement of the theorem. But choosing
k=n
, (
3.27
) directly entails

(3.29)

\xi_{n,\,n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{so that}\hskip 14.22636pt\xi^{\,\sharp}_{n,\,n}\,\overset{\Delta}{=}\,\sup_{1\,\leqslant\,k\,\leqslant\,n}\xi_{n,\,k}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~0

according to Lem. 1.3.20 of

adapted to convergence in probability. Finally,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\big[(m_{n,\,k}^{(a)})^{2}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\{|m_{n,\,k}^{(a)}|\,>\,\epsilon\sqrt{n}\}}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k-1}\big]\,\leqslant\,\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}T^{(a)}_{n,\,k-1}\right)\xi^{\,\sharp}_{n,\,n}

and,
via
(
3.24
) and (
3.29
), Lindeberg’s condition (
3.28
) is satisfied. Together with (
3.27
), this is sufficient to establish that

\forall\,a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\backslash\{0\},\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{M_{n}^{(a)}}{\sqrt{n}}~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\mathcal{N}(0,\,\sigma^{2}\,a^{\,T}Ha).

Because
a
is arbitrary, we can now make use of the Cramér-Wold device to get the
p
-vectorial convergence

(3.30)

\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\,\sigma^{2}\,H)

with

Coming back to (
3.24
) and (
3.25
), and applying Slutsky’s lemma, the proof is complete for the real case. If the spectrum of

contains complex eigenvalues, we shall exploit the fact that the first row of
P_{n}^{-1}
(say,

3.2

\mathcal{F}_{n,\,k}

\frac{\langle M\rangle_{n}}{n}~\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}~\sigma^{4}\,\frac{\pi_{11}^{2}}{2}\,>\,0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{so that}\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{M_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}~\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}~\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,\sigma^{4}\,\frac{\pi_{11}^{2}}{2}\right)
