Discretisation of regularity structures
Dirk Erhard 1 and Martin Hairer 2

Abstract
We introduce a general framework allowing to apply the theory of regularity structures to discretisations of stochastic PDEs. The approach pursued in this article is that we do not focus on any one specific discretisation procedure. Instead, we assume that we are given a scale ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 and a “black box” describing the behaviour of our discretised objects at scales below ε \varepsilon .



1
Introduction

The theory of regularity structures is a framework developed by the second author in
[
Regularity
]
which
allows to renormalise stochastic PDEs of the form

{\mathcal{L}}u=F(u,\nabla u,\xi),

(1)

that are ill-posed in the classical sense. Here,
{\mathcal{L}}
is typically a parabolic differential operator,
\xi
is a very irregular random input and
F
is some local non-linearity.
The naïve approach to study well-posedness of (
1
) would be to consider a sequence of smooth approximations

{\mathcal{L}}u_{\varepsilon}=F(u_{\varepsilon},\nabla u_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon}),

(2)

and simply declare the limit of this sequence to be the solution to (
1
).
However, in many cases it turns out that the sequence
u_{\varepsilon}
either does not possess a limit or the limit is trivial.
A way to circumvent this is to renormalise, which can be interpreted as a kind of “recentering” of the equation,
see
[
BHZalg
]
.

This amounts to allowing some of the constants appearing in the definition of
F
appearing in the right hand side of (
2
) to be
\varepsilon
-dependent and more specifically to diverge as
\varepsilon
tends to zero.
The theory of regularity structures is very successful when applied to approximations
u_{\varepsilon}
that are
functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
it however presently does not in general apply to discrete approximations of stochastic PDEs or to equations where
the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
itself is perturbed by a higher-order term, in such a way that its
scaling properties are different at small scales (think for instance of
{\mathcal{L}}
given by
\partial_{t}-\Delta
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{t}-\Delta+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}
).
In the present article, we develop a general framework that is able to deal with these cases.
The guiding principle is a separation of scales, i.e., above a certain scale
\varepsilon
we show that the theory of regularity structures still applies, whereas scales below
\varepsilon
are treated as a black box.
As a result, our framework is flexible and does not rely on any specific discretisation procedure. More precisely
the results in the article show that, given a stochastic PDE such that the theory of regularity structures
can be applied to renormalise it, our present framework can be used to treat a large class of natural discretisations
for it.

1.1
Discussion

Let us first comment on our motivation for this work as well as its relation to some previous work.
One of the guiding questions in the study of particle systems is the question of universality, i.e., what
are the characterising features for a class of particle systems that converge to the same limit
under appropriate rescaling and how can one prove this convergence?
The bulk of the literature is concerned with scaling limits of a
fixed
system which necessarily
implies that the resulting limit is itself a scale-invariant object.
The present article however is motivated by the situation where one considers a
family
of systems
indexed by some parameter and one simultaneously tunes this parameter as one rescales the system.
In this way, one typically obtains scaling limits that are
not
scale invariant themselves.
One of the insights of the theory presented in
[
Regularity
]
is that they are however
locally

described by linear combinations of objects that are scale-invariant, but with different scaling exponents.

So far, the state of the art for answering such questions (in the second case where the limiting object
can, at least formally, be described by a singular stochastic PDE) is to
heavily rely on special features of the model(s) under consideration. In the type of situation of interest to
us, some standard techniques consist of

When applied in the correct context these techniques can be very powerful. The drawback however is that they are often quite sensitive to small perturbations of the model. Another problem of analytical techniques like regularity structures
or paraproducts is that while they provide a rather clean and general-purpose toolbox in the continuum, their
extensions to the discrete setting require purpose-built modifications that are much less reusable.
It is therefore desirable to have a robust theory at hand which is insensitive to the details of the
underlying discrete setting, and it is the goal of the present article to make a first attempt at
developing such a theory.

Another motivation to introduce the framework developed here stems from the fact that a common way to derive properties of a stochastic PDE is to approximate it by discrete systems for which the desired property holds and then show
that it remains stable under passage to the limit. This methodology was for instance successfully applied by Hairer and Matetski

to prove that the

[

is invariant for the
\Phi_{3}^{4}
-equation.
In the specific case where the solution to the stochastic PDE at hand is a function of time,
[
MatetskiDiscrete
]
developed a framework adapting the theory of regularity structures to allow for certain spatial discretisations.

1.2
Strategy

This article aims to justify the philosophy that small scales actually do not matter much. The way we formulate this here is that we
assume some reasonable (albeit technical) assumptions on scales smaller than
\varepsilon
, that are almost independent of the specific discretisation one deals with, and we show that this implies desirable estimates on all scales up to order one. The precise strategy to do so then results in a series of statements that are described further below.

The main idea to accommodate a large class of discretisations of stochastic PDEs is to consider the behaviour below scale
\varepsilon

as encoded in a “black box”. In order to describe this, our main ingredient
is a sequence of linear spaces
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
that can be viewed as subspaces of
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of distributions, and
that possess a natural family of (extended) seminorms. We then work with stochastic PDEs of the type (
2
)
such that
u_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
.
Examples for
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
include
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(thus recovering the original setting in
[
Regularity
]
), but also the space of functions defined on a discrete grid of mesh size
\varepsilon
, or simply some space of smooth functions.
To cast this into the framework of regularity structures we first work with an abstract version of
u_{\varepsilon}
, i.e., we write
u_{\varepsilon}
as a generalised Taylor expansion. Here, the monomials may represent the classical Taylor monomials or they may represent abstract expressions that are functions of
\xi
. The control of these monomials typically amount to the control of explicit stochastic objects and heavily depend on the choice of the equation at hand.

The “Taylor coefficients” can be thought of as the “derivatives” of an abstract version of a
{\mathcal{C}}^{\gamma}
-function and they are given by
the solution of an abstract fixed point problem. We denote the class of abstract functions just described by
{\mathcal{D}}_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
. The subindex
\varepsilon
indicates that the way regularity is measured above and below scale
\varepsilon
may differ. Indeed, above scale
\varepsilon
regularity is measured as in the continuous setting
[
Regularity
]
, whereas below scale
\varepsilon
, besides some natural constraints, the way to measure regularity is not further determined. The idea of introducing
{\mathcal{D}}^{\gamma}
-spaces that depend on a parameter
\varepsilon
is not
new and already appeared in the works
[
KPZJeremy
,
Phi4Weijun
]
. In a sense the current article generalises some of the ideas developed there.

Note that the solution to (
2
) is a random space-time function / distribution, whereas the approach outlined above gives rise to an abstract “modelled distribution”
f\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
. To link the abstract object with a concrete object we construct a “reconstruction map”
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}
. Unlike in
[
Regularity
]
,
we do not think of
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}f
as a distribution, but rather as an element of
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
, which could for
example represent a space of functions defined on a discrete grid at scale
\varepsilon
. The way to construct
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}
is to
postulate
the existence of a map
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}
satisfying certain estimates on scales below
\varepsilon
and to then
show that analogous estimates automatically hold on all larger scales. Note that there may be several candidates for
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}
all satisfying the same quantitative estimates on small scales, so that in our context the reconstruction
map is in general not uniquely defined. To proceed, we further need to define operations on
{\mathcal{D}}_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}
in order to actually construct abstract solutions to (
2
).
In particular we define an abstract notion of convolution
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
against the Green function
K^{\varepsilon}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
2
) (which is an operator mapping into
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
!) and we show that
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
satisfies a certain Schauder estimate. It turns out that unlike in the continuous setting
[
Regularity
]
it is not necessarily true that the convolution operator
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
can be defined in a natural way so that
it intertwines with
{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}
in the sense that

{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}=K^{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{R}}^{\varepsilon}.

(3)

We however argue that in many cases one can enforce (
3
) by tweaking the definition of
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}
.
So far the strategy outlined above appears to only allow us to describe solutions to (
2
),
but, as shown in
[
Regularity
]
and
[
BHZalg
]
, the encoding of renormalisation procedures in the theory
of regularity structures is of purely algebraic nature and does not depend on any specific discretisation procedure, see also Remark
1.1
below.

We finish with some concluding remarks that comment on how one does apply the theory developed in this article.

Remark 1.1

To apply the theory developed in this article to a concrete problem, an important ingredient is the construction of a
suitable regularity structure and a suitable renormalised model. In the usual (continuous) case, a framework was
built in
[
Regularity
, Sec. 8]
, and further refined in
[
BHZalg
,
Ajay
]
that automatises this construction.
To perform an analogous construction in the present context, one then needs an algebra structure on
{\mathcal{X}}_{\varepsilon}
,
as well as a representation of the Taylor polynomials, which is often the case.
The general analytical results of
[
Ajay
]
however fall out the scope of this article and would have to be adapted.

It was shown in
