1.
Introduction

The existence of periodic orbits and limit cycles for differential systems is a very active topic of research, mainly due to its relation to the Hilbert 16th Problem, and the existence of such objects in systems of piecewise smooth differential equations has been studied recently by several authors. For cases in
\mathds{R}^{2}
, there are many works that determine the maximum number of limit cycles for a given class of vector fields and different separation manifolds (see
[
5
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
6
]
). There are also studies of piecewise smooth differential systems in
\mathds{R}^{3}
(see
[
17
]
,
[
12
]
), for which the discontinuity manifold is a plane.

Moreover, there are also many open cases. For instance, for piecewise linear systems with a straight line of discontinuity,
[
9
]
and
[
15
]
prove the existence of a case with three nested limit cycles, and
[
13
]
provides a very detailed description of different configurations for such systems and their respective lower bounds for the maximum number of limit cycles. However, as far as we know, the maximum number of limit cycles in piecewise linear systems with a straight line of discontinuity is still an open question. Nevertheless, droping the assumption that the discontinuity is a straight line,
[
3
]
and
[
21
]
show us that there exists piecewise linear systems having exactly
n
limit cycles, for any
n\in\mathds{N}
.

The generalized Liénard differential equation

(1)

\ddot{x}+f(x)\dot{x}+g(x)=0,

introduced in
[
14
]
, where
f(x)
and
g(x)
are polynomial functions on the variable
x
, is a subclass of polynomial vector fields with applications on modelling oscilattion circuits that have been extensively studied in the past years.

This differential system has been extensively studied in the literature
[
2
,
22
,
24
,
23
]
. In 1976, Lins, de Melo and Pugh
[
1
]
conjectured that for
g(x)=x
and
f(x)
of degree
n\geq 1
, the system
1
has at most
\left[\frac{n-1}{2}\right]
limit cycles — where the brackets denote the integer part function. This was proved to be true for
n=4

[
13
]
. For
n\geq 6
, Maesschalck and Dumortier proved in 2011
[
7
]
that such equations can have
\left[\frac{n-1}{2}\right]+2
limit cycles, contradicting the conjecture from
[
1
]
. The conjecture for
n=5
remains open.

One of the works that started the study of limit cycles on discontinuous Liénard polynomial differential systems was done by Llibre and Teixeira and is summarized by the paper
[
16
]
. In their work, they proposed the study of the system

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}[l]\dot{x}=y+\varepsilon\cdot\text{sgn}(g_{m}(x,y))\cdot f(x),\\
\dot{y}=-x,\end{matrix}

where
f(x)
is a polynomial of degree
n
and the zero set of the function
\text{sgn}(g_{m}(x,y))
,
m\in\{0,2,4,6...\}
, is the union of
m/2
distinct straight lines passing through the origin, dividing the plane in sectors of angles
2\pi/m
. They managed to prove, using the periodic averaging method for regularized discontinuous systems, that for
m=0,2
and 4 the lower bounds for the maximum number of limit cycles of system (
2
) are, respectively,
\left[\frac{n-1}{2}\right]
,
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]
and
\left[\frac{n-1}{2}\right]
. They also left unproved the conjecture that, for
m\geq 6
, a lower bound for the maximum number of limit cycles of this system should be
\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(n-\frac{m-2}{2}\right)\right]
. This was then proven to be true in
[
8
]
.

In
[
20
]
, the authors studied the number of limit cycles in the system

(3)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}[l]\dot{x}=y,\\
\dot{y}=-x-\varepsilon(f(x)\cdot y+\text{sgn}(y)(k_{1}x+k_{2})),\end{matrix}

where
f
is a polynomial of degree
n\in\mathds{N}
and
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathds{R}
. The approach chosen by them was also based on the ideas of the regularization method — specifically by introducing a piecewise linear function of the type

\varphi_{w}(y)=\begin{cases}-1&\text{ if }y<-w,\\
\frac{y}{w}&\text{ if }-w<y<w,\\
1&\text{ if }y>w.\end{cases}.

The original system (
3
) is then replaced by

(4)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}[l]\dot{x}=y,\\
\dot{y}=-x-\varepsilon(f(x)\cdot y+\varphi_{w}(y)(k_{1}x+k_{2})),\end{matrix}

and it is easy to see that, taking
w\rightarrow 0
,
\varphi_{w}(y)\rightarrow\text{sgn}(y)
. The main result of
[
20
]
is the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

(Martins-Mereu)

For every
n\geq 1
and
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small, the maximum number of limit cycles of the system (
4
) bifurcating from the periodic orbits of the linear center
\dot{x}=y
,
\dot{y}=-x
is
[n/2]+1
. Moreover, there are systems (
4
) having exactly
[n/2]+1
limit cycles.

By proving this case, the similar problem for the differential system (
3
) comes as a corollary, taking the limit
w\rightarrow 0
. In this paper, we generalize this theorem replacing
k_{1}x+k_{2}
by an arbitrary real polynomial
g(x)
of degree
m\geq 1
. Our proof will not pass through the regularization process — in one hand, this should ease the process of calculating the averaged function but, in the other hand, we need to take a more careful look at the conditions of the functions we are averaging. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 2
.

(Main Result)
Let
f(x)
and
g(x)
be real polynomials of degrees
n\geq 1
and
m\geq 1
, respectively, and consider the system

(5)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}[l]\dot{x}=y,\\
\dot{y}=-x-\varepsilon\cdot(f(x)\cdot y+\text{sgn}(y)\cdot g(x)).\end{matrix}

Then, for
|\varepsilon|
sufficiently small, the number
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
is a lower bound to the maximum number of limit cycles of the system (
5
) bifurcating from the periodic orbits of the linear center
\dot{x}=y
,
\dot{y}=-x
. Moreover, we can choose
f
and
g
such that this number of cycles is indeed achieved.

Remark 1
.

Even though we aim to find a
maximum number
of limit cycles of a system, we can only guarantee a
lower bound of the maximum number
of limit cycles, in the sense of it being the maximum number of cycles
that can be found through first-order averaging
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
U
be an open neighbourhood of
0
where the vector field given by a piecewise smooth differential system is defined, and let
\Sigma
be the hypersurface along which the discontinuities occur. Since any embedded hypersurface is locally the inverse image of a regular value, let
\Sigma=f^{-1}(0)\cap U
, where
f
is the
germ
of a
C^{r}
function with
r>1
and which has 0 as a regular value. Here the
germ of a function
refers to the equivalence class of all functions which are locally equal to one another, see
[
19
]
for more details.

Note that the hypersurface
\Sigma
splits
U
into the following open sets:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{+}=\{(x,y)\in U:f(x,y)>0\}\text{ and }\Sigma^{-}=\{(x,y)\in U:f(x,y)<0\}.

We may then define the germs of a discontinuous vector field as

(6)

\displaystyle Z(x,y)=\begin{cases}X(x,y),\text{ if }(x,y)\in\Sigma^{+}\\
Y(x,y),\text{ if }(x,y)\in\Sigma^{-}\end{cases},

and we denote the vector field by
Z=(X,Y)
.

The trajectories on
\Sigma^{+}
and
\Sigma^{-}
that do not reach
\Sigma
can be defined by the vector fields
X
and
Y
in the same way as for smooth systems. Troubles may arise from what happens along
\Sigma
, hence we must take a careful look into these possibilities.

Let
Xf(p)=X(p)\cdot\nabla f(p)
denote the Lie derivative of
f
with respect to
X
at the point
p
, and define:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{c}=\{p\in\Sigma:Xf(p)\cdot Yf(p)>0\},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{s}=\{p\in\Sigma:Xf(p)<0,Yf(p)>0\},

\displaystyle\Sigma^{e}=\{p\in\Sigma:Xf(p)>0,Yf(p)<0\},

which we name
crossing region, sliding region
and
escaping region
, respectively.

If
Xf(p)=0
or
Yf(p)=0
, then
p
is a
tangency point
; we assume that these tangency points are isolated in
\Sigma
.

Note that if
p\in\Sigma^{c}
, i.e. p is in a crossing region, then we can define the trajectory through
p
by simply matching the trajectories defined by
X
and
Y
. However, if
p\in\Sigma^{s}
or
\Sigma^{e}
, the trajectory can’t be defined so directly; in this case, one may use the Filippov convention, but this case will not be studied in this paper.

Now, the differential system that we’re interested on averaging shall have the following form

(7)

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=\varepsilon F(t,x)+\varepsilon^{2}R(t,x,\varepsilon),

with

\displaystyle F(t,x)=\begin{cases}F^{1}(t,x),\text{ if }x\in S_{1},\\
F^{2}(t,x),\text{ if }x\in S_{2},\end{cases}

\displaystyle R(t,x,\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}R^{1}(t,x,\varepsilon),\text{ if }x\in S_{1},\\
R^{2}(t,x,\varepsilon),\text{ if }x\in S_{2},\end{cases}

where
F^{1,2}:I\times S_{1,2}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}
,
R^{1,2}:I\times S_{1,2}\times(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{n}
and
S_{1,2}=D\cap\Sigma^{\pm}
. Or, alternatively, using the function
\mathcal{X}(A)=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }x\in A\\
0,\text{ if }x\notin A\end{cases}
,
we can rewrite those as:

\displaystyle F(t,x)=\mathcal{X}_{S_{1}}F^{1}(t,x)+\mathcal{X}_{S_{2}}F^{2}(t,x),

\displaystyle R(t,x,\varepsilon)=\mathcal{X}_{S_{1}}R^{1}(t,x,\varepsilon)+\mathcal{X}_{S_{2}}R^{2}(t,x,\varepsilon).

Then, if
F^{1,2}
and
R^{1,2}
are
T
-periodic functions, we can define the averaged function
F_{0}(z)
as

(8)

\displaystyle F_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}F(t,z)dt,

In
[
11
]
, the authors proved the following result:

Theorem 3
.

(First order averaging for discontinuous systems,
[
11
]
)
Assuming the following hypothesis:

H1

There exists an open bounded set
C\subset D
such that, for each
z\in\overline{C}
, the curve
\{(t,z):t\in I=\mathds{S}^{1}=\mathds{R}/T\}
reaches transversely the set
\Sigma
and only at generic points of discontinuity;

H2

For
j=1,2
, the continuous functions
F^{j}
and
R^{j}
are locally Lipschitz with respect to
x
, and T-periodic with respect to the time variable
t
;

H3

For
a\in C
with
F_{0}(a)=0
, there exists a neighborhood
U\subset C
of
a
such that
F_{0}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\overline{U}\setminus\{a\}
and
d_{B}(F_{0},U,0)\neq 0
.

Then for
|\varepsilon|\neq 0
sufficiently small, there exists a T-periodic solution
x(t,\varepsilon)
of system (
7
) such that
x(0,\varepsilon)\rightarrow a
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Theorem
3
stabilishes the correspondence between the zeros of the averaged function and the closed orbits of the system
7
, so that roughly we can replace the problem of finding limit cycles by the problem of finding zeros of a polynomial. In order to deal with these polynomials, we introduce the following theorem, as stated in
[
16
]
:

Theorem 4
.

(Descartes’ Theorem)

Consider the real polynomial
p(x)=a_{i_{1}}x^{i_{1}}+...+a_{i_{r}}x^{i_{r}}
, with
r>1
,
0\leq i_{1}<...<i_{r}
and the numbers
a_{i_{j}}
are not simultaneously zeros for
j\in\{1,2,...,r\}
. If
a_{i_{j}}\cdot a_{i_{j+1}}<0
, we say that they have a variation of sign. If the number of variations of signs is
m
, then
p(x)
has at most
m
positive real roots. Moreover, it’s always possible to choose the coefficients of
p(x)
in such a way that
p(x)
has exactly
r-1
positive real roots.

With these tools, we may now prove the main theorem of this work.

3.
Proof of the Main Result

Let
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}
and
g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j}
. We start rewriting the system in polar coordinates

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\dot{x}=\dot{r}\cos{\theta}-r\sin{\theta}\cdot\dot{\theta}\\
\dot{y}=\dot{r}\sin{\theta}+r\cos{\theta}\cdot\dot{\theta}\end{matrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\theta}&-r\sin{\theta}\\
\sin{\theta}&r\cos{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\dot{r}\\
\dot{\theta}\end{pmatrix},

then inverting the coordinate change matrix:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{r}\\
\dot{\theta}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\begin{pmatrix}r\cos{\theta}&r\sin{\theta}\\
-\sin{\theta}&\cos{\theta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}r\sin{\theta}\\
-r\cos{\theta}-\varepsilon\cdot(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\varepsilon\cdot\sin{\theta}\cdot(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))\\
-1-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\cdot(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))\end{pmatrix}.

To ease our calculations, put
a=(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))
. Then, admitting
\theta
as the new independent variable, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\frac{-\varepsilon\cdot\sin{\theta}\cdot a}{-1-\frac{\varepsilon}{r}\cdot\cos{\theta}\cdot a}=\phi(\varepsilon).

Expanding the series of
\phi
around
\varepsilon=0
we get

\displaystyle\phi(\varepsilon)=\phi(0)+\phi^{\prime}(0)\cdot\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Note that
\phi(0)=0
, and

\phi^{\prime}(0)=\left.\frac{-\sin{\theta}\cdot a(-1-\frac{\xi}{r}\cdot\cos{\theta}\cdot a)-(-\xi\sin{\theta}\cdot a\cdot\frac{\cos{\theta}\cdot a}{r})}{(-1-\frac{\xi}{r}\cdot\cos{\theta}\cdot a)^{2}}\right|_{\xi=0}=\sin{\theta}\cdot a,

hence

\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\sin{\theta}\cdot(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))\cdot\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

Writing
F(r,\theta)=\sin{\theta}\cdot(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))
and
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})=R(r,\theta)\cdot\varepsilon^{2}
, define the averaged function
F_{0}(r)
as the following integral:

\displaystyle F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin^{2}{\theta}+\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})\cdot\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta.

Note that
\text{sgn}(r\sin{\theta})=\text{sgn}(\sin{\theta})
, since
r>0
, which yields the value
+1
when
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and
-1
when
\theta\in(\pi,2\pi)
, then:

\displaystyle F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta+\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta-\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta\right],

and since

\int_{\pi}^{2\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta=\int_{\pi}^{0}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta=-\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta,

the averaged function can be written as:

\displaystyle F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int_{0}^{2\pi}(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta+2\cdot\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta\right].

Let
I
and
J
denote the following integrals:

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{2\pi}(f(r\cos{\theta})\cdot r\sin^{2}{\theta},

\displaystyle J=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin{\theta}\cdot g(r\cos{\theta}))d\theta.

For the first integral we have:

\displaystyle I=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}r^{i}\cos^{i}{\theta}\cdot r\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}r^{i+1}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{i}{\theta}\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta.

As in
[
20
]
, we use the following formulas:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2k+1}\theta\cdot\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta=0,k=0,1,2...

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2k}\theta\cdot\sin^{2}{\theta}d\theta=\pi\alpha_{k}\neq 0,k=0,1,2...

thus

\displaystyle I=\sum_{i=0}^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}\pi a_{2i}\alpha_{2i}r^{2i+1}.

Hence
I
is a polynomial formed exclusively by odd exponents of
r
, and with
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+1
terms.

For the second integral, notice that

\displaystyle J=\int_{0}^{\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}r^{j}\cos^{j}{\theta}\cdot\sin{\theta}d\theta=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}r^{j}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos^{j}{\theta}\cdot\sin{\theta}d\theta.

In order to evaluate this integral, consider the change of variables
u=\cos{\theta}
,
du=\sin{\theta}d\theta
then:

\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos^{j}{\theta}\cdot\sin{\theta}d\theta=\int_{1}^{-1}u^{j}du=\frac{(-1)^{j+1}-1}{j+1}.

If
j
is odd, then
j+1
is even and
\frac{(-1)^{j+1}-1}{j+1}=0
; but, when
j
is even, i.e. when
j+1
is odd, then
\frac{(-1)^{j+1}-1}{j+1}=-\frac{2}{j+1}\neq 0
. Hence

\displaystyle J=\sum_{j=0}^{\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]}\frac{-2b_{2j}r^{2j}}{2j+1},

which means that
J
is a polynomial with
\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
monomials, formed by even exponents of
r
.

Since
F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(I+2J)
and
I
and
J
don’t have any powers of
r
in common, it follows that
F_{0}(r)
is a polynomial with
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+2
terms. Therefore, by the Descartes’ Theorem,
F_{0}(r)
can have at most
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
roots; then, if we could apply Theorem
3
, it would follow that the system (
5
) can have
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
limit cycles bifurcating from the linear center. Moreover, since we can choose the coefficients from
F_{0}(r)
such that it has exactly
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
roots, this maximum number of limit cycles can indeed be achieved. Thus, to complete this proof, all we need to do is verify that the hypotheses from Theorem
3
are fulfilled.

First of all, for a given bounded set
D
, it is easy to see that
F^{j}(r,\theta)
and
R^{j}(r,\theta,\varepsilon)
,
j=1,2
, are Lipschitz with respect to the variable
r
since they are polynomials in
r
; moreover, they are also periodic on
\theta
with period
2\pi
, hence the hypothesis
H2
is satisfied. We are left to show that so are the hypotheses
H1
and
H3
— this will be done by proving the following propositions:

Proposition 1
.

For
|\varepsilon|\neq 0
sufficiently small, there exists an open bounded set
C
such that every solution of system (
5
) reaches
\Sigma
on
\Sigma^{c}
.

Proof of Proposition
1
:
Since the original system is autonomous, it’s sufficient to analyze under which conditions the set
\Sigma^{c}
exists.

First, notice that we can write
\Sigma=h^{-1}(0)
, where
h(x,y)=y
. Let
X
and
Y
denote the smooth pieces of the system, i.e.:

X(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
-x-\varepsilon(f(x)\cdot y+g(x))\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }Y(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
-x-\varepsilon(f(x)\cdot y-g(x))\end{pmatrix}.

Let
p\in\Sigma
be the point where the solution crosses the discontinuity, then
p=(x,0)
; computing the Lie derivatives on
p
:

\displaystyle Xh(p)=X(p)\cdot\nabla h(p)=(0,-x+\varepsilon\cdot g(x))\cdot(0,1)=-x+\varepsilon\cdot g(x),

\displaystyle Yh(p)=Y(p)\cdot\nabla h(p)=(0,-x-\varepsilon\cdot g(x))\cdot(0,1)=-x-\varepsilon\cdot g(x),

thus
Xh(p)\cdot Yh(p)=x^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}(g(x))^{2}
. Therefore, for a sufficiently small
|\varepsilon|
and
x\neq 0
,
p\in\Sigma^{c}
, hence we can find a bounded set such that every solution passing through it reaches
\Sigma
at a crossing point. ∎

Proposition 2
.

The coefficients of the polynomials
f
and
g
can be chosen in a way that, for every
a\in\mathds{R}
with
F_{0}(a)=0
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
a
such that
F_{0}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\overline{U}\setminus\{a\}
and
d_{B}(F_{0},U,0)\neq 0

Proof of Proposition
2
:
Let
Z=\{r\in\mathds{R}^{+}:F_{0}(r)=0\}
be the set of positive zeros of the averaged function. The Brouwer degree of a
C^{1}
-function
f
with respect to a neighborhood
V
of zero is given by (see
[
4
]
):

d_{B}(f,V,0)=\sum_{a\in f^{-1}(0)\cap V}\text{sign(det}Df(a))

and, since
F_{0}
is a polynomial on
r\in\mathds{R}
, then

d_{B}(F_{0},V,0)=\sum_{a\in Z\cap V}\text{sign}(F_{0}^{\prime}(a)).

Let
a\in Z
, then there exists a neighborhood
U
such that
F_{0}(z)\neq 0

\forall z\in\overline{U}\setminus\{a\}
(e.g. consider an open interval with radius half the distance to the next zero of
F_{0}
); then

d_{B}(F_{0},U,0)=\text{sign}(F_{0}^{\prime}(a)).

Thus what is left to prove is that we can choose the coefficients of
F_{0}
such that
F_{0}^{\prime}(a)\neq 0
for every
a\in Z
.

Recall that, if
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}
and
g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{j}x^{j}
, then

\displaystyle F_{0}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}\pi a_{2i}\alpha_{2i}r^{2i+1}+2\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]}\frac{-2b_{2j}r^{2j}}{2j+1}\right],

or simply

\displaystyle F_{0}(r)=\sum_{i=0}^{\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]}\tilde{a}_{2i}r^{2i+1}+\sum_{j=0}^{\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]}\tilde{b}_{2j}r^{2j}.

Since
m\geq 1
, we can choose
b_{0}\neq 0
, hence
\tilde{b}_{0}\neq 0
. We will show that this is sufficient to prove that, in order to choose the coefficients of a polynomial with a maximum number of positive roots, we can’t have the derivatives vanishing at the roots.

Consider
p(x)=c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}x^{l_{j}}
a polynomial with
n+1
terms for which we have chosen the coefficients such that it has
n
positive roots. Notice that Theorem
4
guarantees such choice and this number is maximal. Since
p(x)
is a
C^{\infty}
-function, then so is
p^{\prime}(x)
; hence, the Mean Value Theorem implies that between each zero of
p(x)
there should exist a zero of
p^{\prime}(x)
. However,
p^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}l_{j}\cdot c_{j}x^{l_{j}-1}
is a polynomial with
n
terms; thus, by Theorem
4
, it can have at most
n-1
positive roots, which means that all the positive real roots of
p^{\prime}(x)
lie between the positive roots of
p(x)
, therefore
p
can’t have it’s derivative vanishing at its positive roots.

Since
F_{0}
fulfills the above conditions, we can choose
\tilde{a}_{2i}
and
\tilde{b}_{2j}
so that
F_{0}(r)
has
\left[\frac{n}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{m}{2}\right]+1
roots and for every
a
with
F_{0}(a)=0
there’s a neighborhood
U\ni a
in which
F_{0}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in U
and

d_{B}(F_{0},U,0)=\text{sign}(F_{0}^{\prime}(a))\neq 0.

Furthermore, since
\tilde{a}_{2i}=\pi a_{2i}\alpha_{2i}
with
\alpha_{2i}\neq 0
and
\tilde{b}_{2j}=\frac{-4b_{2j}}{2j+1}
, we can choose
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
,
i=1,...,n
and
j=1,...,m
, such that we obtain the desired coefficients for
F_{0}
. ∎
