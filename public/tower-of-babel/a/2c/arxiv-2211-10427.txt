Sharp lower bounds for the number of maximum matchings in bipartite multigraphs
Alexandr V. Kostochka, Douglas B. West, Zimu Xiang

1
Introduction

We study lower bounds for the number of maximum matchings in bipartite
multigraphs. A
matching
is a set of pairwise disjoint edges.
An
X,Y
-bigraph
is a bipartite (multi)graph with parts
X
and
Y
.
For a set
S
of vertices in a graph, let
N(S)
denote the union of the
neighborhoods of the vertices in
S
.
Hall’s Condition
for
X
in an

X,Y
-bigraph is the condition that
\left|N(S)\right|\geq\left|S\right|
for every subset
S
of

X
. The celebrated theorem of Philip Hall
[
4
]
for an
X,Y
-bigraph
G

states that if Hall’s Condition holds for
X
, then
G
contains a matching
covering the vertices of
X
. We call such a matching an
X
-matching.

Let
\Phi(G)
denote the number of maximum matchings (matchings with the most
edges) in
G
. When Hall’s Condition holds, the maximum matchings are

X
-matchings.

We use the term
simple
X,Y
-bigraph
when multiedges are forbidden.
For a set
S
of vertices in a graph
G
, let
\delta_{S}(G)=\min_{v\in S}d_{G}(v)
,
where
d_{G}(v)
denotes the degree of vertex
v
in
G
.
Marshall Hall
[
3
]
proved
\Phi(G)\geq\prod_{i=1}^{\min\{k,n\}}(k-i+1)
when

G
is a simple
X,Y
-bigraph with
\left|X\right|=n
and
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
that
satisfies Hall’s Condition for
X
. In particular, when
k\leq n
this lower
bound is
k!
, and its sharpness for simple graphs is a special case of our
general sharpness construction.

There are also lower bounds for bipartite multigraphs. A multigraph is

k
-regular
if every vertex has degree
k
. The famous theorem of
Egorychev
[
1
]
and Falikman
[
2
]
, proving what was known as
van der Waerden’s Conjecture, implies that every
k
-regular bipartite
multigraph with
n
vertices in each part has at least
n!(k/n)^{n}
perfect
matchings (that is, matchings covering all the vertices). Since

n!\approx(n/{\rm e})^{n}\sqrt{2\pi n}
by Stirling’s Approximation, the leading
order of growth in this lower bound for
k
-regular multigraphs is
(k/{\rm e})^{n}
,
while Hall’s lower bound for simple graphs, which is fixed for large
n
, is
only about
(k/{\rm e})^{k}
.

In order to combine these situations, we enlarge the graph context by allowing
multiedges and the multigraph context by weakening regularity to a minimum
degree requirement applied only to
X
. To further obtain a spectrum of
problems that in some sense bridges the gap between bipartite graphs and
bipartite multigraphs, we introduce a neighborhood condition and prove the
following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
k,r,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
k\geq r
. Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with
\left|X\right|=n
.
If
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
and
\left|N_{G}(x)\right|\geq r
for all
x\in X
, then

\Phi(G)\geq\begin{cases}r!(k-r+1)&\text{if $n\geq r$,}\\
[r+n(k-r)]\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(r-i)&\text{if $n\leq r$.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, the bounds are sharp in all cases.

When
r=k
, the bound simplifies to
\prod_{i=0}^{\min\{n,k\}-1}(k-i)
, the bound of
M. Hall. This is achieved in our Construction
2.1
. The proof will
show also that
\Phi(G)
attains this minimum only when
G
satisfies Hall’s
Condition and the maximum matchings are
X
-matchings. In Theorem
2.5

we further explore the characterization of constructions achieving equality in
the lower bound, in which Construction
2.1
plays a crucial role.

Setting
r=1
in Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to eliminating the
restriction on
r
. Theorem
1.1
then only guarantees
\Phi(G)\geq k
in
an
X,Y
-bigraph
G
with
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
satisfying Hall’s Condition, which
is sharp by the construction in Construction
2.1
, independent of
n
.
However, in this construction
\left|Y\right|=\left|X\right|
and
\delta_{Y}(G)=1
. Forbidding this
situation yields a better lower bound, further indicating the sharpness of the
previous result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
and
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 1

that satisfies Hall’s Condition. If
\left|Y\right|>\left|X\right|
or
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2
, then
sharp lower bounds on
\Phi(G)
are as follows:

\Phi(G)\geq\begin{cases}2k-2,&\text{if $\left|X\right|=2$ or $k=2$;}\\
2k-1,&\text{if $\left|X\right|>2$ and $k=3$;}\\
2k,&\text{if $\left|X\right|\geq 3$ and $k\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

We will see that the extremal configurations in Theorem
1.2
can also be
excluded by a further restriction, partially combining the restrictions of
Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
k,n\geq 2
, let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
and

\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2
that satisfies Hall’s Condition. If
|N_{G}(x)|\geq 2
for
each
x\in X
, then

\Phi(G)\geq\min\{n(k-2)+2,4k-4\}.

In these two results, the guaranteed lower bound on
\Phi(G)
does not grow as

\left|Y\right|
grows, but the sharpness examples generally require
\left|Y\right|=\left|X\right|
. In
general, as
\left|Y\right|-\left|X\right|
grows, the guaranteed number of
X
-matchings also
grows. Some of the results in the next theorem reduce to parts of
Theorem
1.2
when
t=0
and need Theorem
1.2
as a basis.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with
\left|X\right|\geq 2
,
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
, and

t=\left|Y\right|-\left|X\right|\geq 0
. If Hall’s Condition holds for
X
, then

\Phi(G)\geq\begin{cases}k(t+1),&\text{if $\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 1$;}\\
(2k-t-2)(t+1),&\text{if $\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2$ and $\left|X\right|=2$;}\\
2k(t+1)-1,&\text{if $\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2$ and $k=3$;}\\
2k(t+1),&\text{if $\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2$ and $k\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

Finally, when every vertex in
G
must have at least two neighbors and subsets
of
X
must have neighborhoods larger than their size, the lower bound on

\Phi(G)
in terms of the various parameters is surprisingly large, and it
grows with
\left|X\right|
, with
\left|Y\right|-\left|X\right|
, and with
\left|E(G)\right|
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph in which every vertex has at least two neighbors.
Let
n=\left|X\right|\geq 2
, and
t=\left|Y\right|-\left|X\right|\geq 0
, and
b=\left|E(G)\right|-2\left|Y\right|
.
If
\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
for every nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
, then

\Phi(G)\geq[(n-1)t+2+b](t+1),

and the lower bound is sharp in all cases.

In Section 2, we prove Theorem
1.1
and discuss the requirements for
equality. Section 3 develops tools applicable to special cases of
Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.5
. Section 4 contains the proof of
Theorem
1.5
, our most difficult result, which also has applications
in the later sections. In Section 5, we consider lower bounds when the
neighborhood requirement for vertices in
X
is replaced by a condition on
Y
,
as in Theorem
1.2
. Section 6 considers the presence of excess vertices
in
Y
, as in Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
. Sharpness
constructions are given for each lower bound.

Many of our proofs follow the technique of P. Hall
[
4
]
, Halmos and
Vaughan
[
5
]
, and Mann and Ryser
[
10
]
, which separates the problem
into two cases depending on whether some proper subset
S
of
X
satisfies

\left|N(S)\right|=\left|S\right|
. This distinction is related to a classical notion studied by
Lovász and Plummer
[
9
]
. In a graph with a perfect matching, an edge is

allowed
if it belongs to a perfect matching, otherwise
forbidden
.
Hetyei
[
6
]
defined a graph to be
elementary
if its allowed edges
form a connected subgraph. It is easy to see that an
X,Y
-bigraph with a
perfect matching is elementary if and only if
\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
for every
nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
. In such graphs, all edges are “allowed”.

In some sense, the case
\left|N(S)\right|=\left|S\right|
reduces the problem to a smaller
graph, and more interesting behavior arises when this is forbidden.
In order to study lower bounds on
\Phi(G)
in a simple
X,Y
-bigraph
G
,
Liu and Liu
[
7
]
introduced the concept of
positive surplus
for
an
X,Y
-bigraph, meaning
\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
for all nonempty
S
in
X
,
including
S=X
. In particular,
\left|Y\right|>\left|X\right|
for such a graph.
We say that an
X,Y
-bigraph is
X
-surplus
if
\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
for every
nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
; this condition is a common extension of
“positive surplus” and “elementary bipartite”. In particular,
Theorem
1.5
applies to all
X
-surplus
X,Y
-bigraphs
in which every vertex of
Y
has at least two neighbors.

Liu and Liu proved several lower bounds for the number of maximum matchings
in a simple
X,Y
-bigraph with positive surplus, which we combine into one
statement. Note that maximum matchings in such graphs are
X
-matchings.

Theorem 1.6

(Liu and Liu
[
7
]
)
.

If
G
is a simple
X,Y
-bigraph with positive surplus, and
\left|Y\right|-\left|X\right|=t\geq 1
,
then

\Phi(G)\geq\begin{cases}\left|X\right|+1,&\text{if $G$ is connected;}\\
\left|E(G)\right|+(\left|X\right|-1)(t-2),&\text{if $G$ is connected;}\\
2\left|E(G)\right|-2\left|Y\right|,&\text{if $t=1$ and $\delta(G)\geq 2$;}\end{cases}

The lower bound
2\left|E(G)\right|-2\left|Y\right|
when
t=1
and
\delta(G)\geq 2
is the
special case

of our general Theorem
1.5
(restricted to
simple graphs), since
[(n-1)1+2+b]2=2\left|E(G)\right|-2\left|Y\right|
. Our proof of the
general result includes a proof of their result for
t=1
. The case

t=0
we prove by different methods in Section
3
, and it serves as
a base case for Theorem
1.5
. It can be stated as follows, allowing
multiedges.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
G
is an elementary
X,Y
-bigraph, then
\Phi(G)\geq\left|E(G)\right|-\left|V(G)\right|+2
,
and this is sharp.

We prove Theorem
1.7
as a consequence of the existence of an ear
decomposition of an elementary
X,Y
-bigraph in which every added ear beyond
the initial even cycle has odd length (obtained in
[
6
,
8
,
9
]
).
A further consequence of this material in Section
3
will be
Theorem
1.3
for the case
\left|X\right|=\left|Y\right|
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
G\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n,k,r}
with

, then there exists
G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{n,k,r}
with

\Phi(G^{\prime})\leq\Phi(G)
and
V(G^{\prime})=V(G)
such that in
G^{\prime}
every vertex of
X

has exactly
r
distinct neighbors, one with multiplicity
k-r+1
and the
others with multiplicity
1
. When

, in

we can only guarantee one
such vertex.

Proof.

When
r=1
and the smallest neighborhood size among vertices in
X
is
1
,
we have
x
with only one neighbor, and we may delete excess copies of the
incident edge to bring

down to

to obtain the desired
G^{\prime}
.
Hence we may assume

.

We use induction on

. When
n=1
, we have
\Phi(G)=d_{G}(x)
, where

X=\{x\}
. If
d_{G}(x)>k
, then we can delete a copy of an edge having highest
multiplicity to reduce the number of
X
-matchings without losing the
hypotheses. Hence we may assume
d_{G}(x)=k
, and we can redistribute the edges
so that one pair has multiplicity
k-r+1
and the other
r-1
edges have
multiplicity
1
. Note that
\Phi(G)=k
for all
G
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{1,k,r}
having

G^{\prime}
minimizing

is not unique. Now

.

Case 1:

\left|N(S)\right|=\left|S\right|
for some nonempty proper subset

of
X
.

Let

. Let
G_{1}=G[S\cup N(S)]
and
G_{2}=G-(S\cup N(S)
. Since

, there is a perfect matching in

; it must consist of
a perfect matching in

and an

-matching in
G_{2}
.

In fact,
\Phi(G)=\Phi(G_{1})\Phi(G_{2})
. We first reduce the number of

X
-matchings by applying the induction hypothesis to
G_{1}
. Since
G_{1}

retains all edges of
G
incident to

, we have
G_{1}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{s,k,r}
, and
the induction hypothesis provides
G_{1}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{s,k,r}
with

\Phi(G_{1}^{\prime})\leq\Phi(G_{1})
such that the desired properties holding in
G_{1}^{\prime}
for
all vertices of
S
. Let
G^{o}
be the graph obtained from

by replacing

with

while maintaining all edges incident to
X-S
.

Let
M
be an
X
-matching in

, and consider
x\in X-S
. Let
y
be the
mate of

in

; note that
y\in N(S)-X
. We will keep all
X
-matchings
in

that use a specific edge
xy
and destroy all

-matchings that do
not use (that copy of) that edge. In particular, when we do not change the

, an

-matching cannot use any edge joining

to

. We replace the edges incident to

by one copy of

and edges to

and the others with

. We can do this because

.
Applying this transformation successively for each vertex of

.

Case 2:

G
is
X
-surplus.

Consider

and

, which exist since

. Let

, and let

and
\sigma^{\prime}=\Phi(G-\{x,y^{\prime}\})
.
We may assume
\sigma\leq\sigma^{\prime}
. Let

be the multigraph obtained from

by (1) shifting all copies of

to be copies of

if
\left|N_{G}(x)\right|>r
,

\left|N_{G}(x)\right|=r
. Since

-surplus,

. Moving

Theorem 2.3

Fix

, we have

, and the claimed lower bound is

.
We are guaranteed a vertex

having one neighbor with multiplicity

.
Every

Hence we may assume

.

We use induction on

.
When

, the formulas reduce to

\prod_{i=0}^{\min\{k,n\}-1}(k-i)
, our restricted graph

is simple, and the lower

n=1\leq r
, the empty product

\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(r-i)
is

, and the number of
X
-matchings guaranteed is

.
Hence we may assume

and

.

Case 1:

\left|N(S)\right|=\left|S\right|
for some nonempty proper subset

of

.

Every

-matching in

matches

into

. Each matching of

into

can be completed to an

-matching by some matching of

into

. Hence it suffices
to show that the subgraph

induced by

has at least the desired

, this graph lies in

.

Since every vertex of

has at least

. Now the

\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq r!(k-r+1)

and hence
\Phi(G)\geq r!(k-r+1)
, as desired.
Note that Case 1
cannot occur
if

.

Case 2:

is

-surplus.

As noted earlier, we may assume

. In order to prove the lower bound on

\Phi(G)
, we may assume that

. Let

-bigraph obtained from

.
Each vertex of

loses one incident edge, but neighborhoods are unchanged,

. By the induction hypothesis,

if

if

This lower bound does not count the

that use at least one
of the deleted edges, which are copies of edges that remain. We need to find

, and we need to find

.

Since

is

for any subset

of

that remains, and every remaining

keeps at least

neighbors. The result of M. Hall thus
implies that every edge of

appears in at least
q

-matchings, where

. Since each edge deleted from

is a copy
of an edge in

, we obtain
q

-matchings in

that use only that one
missing edge plus edges in

. Thus we have at least

-matchings in

. In fact,

is exactly the

, and it exceeds the desired value when

.

\Box

To complete the proof of Theorem

, we consider the case where

does not have an

-matching. In this case we obtain a larger lower bound.
Let
\alpha^{\prime}(G)
be the maximum size (number of edges) of a matching in a
(multi)graph

. For a subset

of

in

, the

is

\max\{0,\left|S\right|-\left|N(S)\right|\}
. The Defect Formula of Ore
[

]
, proved by
the same technique as the corollary below, states for an

-bigraph

that

\alpha^{\prime}(G)=\left|X\right|-p
, where

is the maximum defect among subsets of

.

Corollary 2.4
.

Fix

with
k\geq r
. Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with

\left|X\right|=n
,
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
, and
\left|N_{G}(x)\right|\geq r
for all

.
Let

. If

and

\Phi(G)\geq(k-r+1)(r+p)!/p!.

Furthermore, the bound is sharp in all cases.

Proof.

Form
G^{\prime}
from

by adding
p
“universal” vertices to

, adjacent via
edges of multiplicity
1
to all vertices of
X
. Since this adds
p
vertices
to the neighborhood of each subset of

, we have
\alpha^{\prime}(G^{\prime})=n
.

For each
X
-matching in

, deleting the edges covering the
p
added
vertices yields a maximum matching in

. Furthermore, each maximum matching
in

corresponds to
p!

X
-matchings in

in this way, since the
uncovered vertices of

in a maximum matching in

can be matched to the

.

Since the added vertices increase the neighborhood of each subset of

and
the degree of each vertex of

by

, we have

. Now Theorem
2.3
completes the proof.

\Box

When

We use induction on

3
Elementary Graphs

In this section we consider imposing both a neighborhood restriction on
vertices of
X
and a degree restriction on vertices of
Y
, proving the
special case of Theorem
1.3
where
\left|Y\right|=\left|X\right|
. Our proof uses properties
of the “elementary” graphs mentioned in the introduction.

There are many equivalent characterizations of the connected bipartite graphs
in which all edges are
allowed
, meaning that they appear in a perfect
matching. These equivalences can be found in Lovász and Plummer
[
9
]
,
though most of the results appeared already in Hetyei
[
6
]
and/or
Lovász
[
8
]
.

The
2
-connected graphs are characterized by the existence of ear
decompositions. An
ear
in a graph is a path whose internal vertices have
degree
2
. An
ear decomposition
iteratively deletes an ear (except
that the endpoints of the ear stay) until what remains is only a cycle. An
ear may be a single edge, so it suffices to begin with a cycle and add ears to
obtain a spanning subgraph (and multiedges are irrelevant). An
odd ear

is an ear of odd length, and an
odd ear decomposition
uses only odd ears.
In a bipartite graph, the endpoints of an odd ear lie in opposite parts. Also,
since all cycles are even, we may view an odd ear decomposition of a bipartite
graph as starting from an edge and adding paths of odd length. This allows

K_{1,1}
to have an odd ear decomposition.

Theorem 3.1
.

For an
X,Y
-bigraph
G
with a perfect matching, the following are equivalent.

(a) The subgraph consisting of allowed edges is connected (i.e.,
G
is
“elementary”).

(b)
\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
whenever
\varnothing\neq S\subsetneq X
(i.e.,
G
is
near-surplus).

(c)
G-x-y
has a perfect matching, whenever
x\in X
and
y\in Y
(i.e.,
G

is “bicritical”.

(d)
G
is connected and every edge is allowed.

(e)
G
has an odd ear decomposition.

The implications (a)
\Rightarrow
(b)
\Rightarrow
(c)
\Rightarrow
(d)
\Rightarrow
(a) follow immediately.
Lovász and Plummer
[
9
]
presented an accessible argument for building an
odd ear decomposition of
G
from any given edge, using property (d).
It is easy to prove that (e) implies the other properties.

Theorem 3.2
.

Every elementary
X,Y
-bigraph
G
has at least
\left|E(G)\right|-\left|V(G)\right|+2
perfect
matchings, and the bound is sharp.

Proof.

Let
m=\left|E(G)\right|
and
n=\left|V(G)\right|
; we use induction on
m-n
. When
m-n=0
,

G
is an even cycle, and there are two perfect matchings. When
m-n>0
,
we consider the odd ear decomposition provided by Theorem
3.1
(e).
Let
G^{\prime}
be the graph obtained by removing one ear
P
in the decomposition,
having endpoints
x
and
y
. This removes one more edge than vertex. By the
induction hypothesis,
G^{\prime}
has at least
m-n+1
perfect matchings. Each
extends by a matching of the internal vertices of
P
to a perfect matching
of
G
.

To produce one more perfect matching in
G
, start with a perfect matching
M

in the ear, covering
x
and
y
and any vertices between them. Since
G
is
elementary,
G^{\prime}-\{x,y\}
has a perfect matching
M^{\prime}
. Now

is a
perfect matching of

not counted among the

matchings above.

For sharpness, let

be a union of paths with odd length, all having
endpoints

and

has odd length. In all other paths,

paths with endpoints

and

Recall that an

Lemma 4.1

If

Proof.

Note that

Let

-matching in

be the subset of

This generates

-matchings.

Sharpness is achieved by the

-bigraph with

\Box

Corollary 4.2

In a leafless

-surplus

-bigraph

-matchings, where

Since

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with an
X
-matching, such that
|X|\geq 3
,

\delta_{X}(G)\geq k\geq 1
, and
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 1
. If
|Y|-|X|=t\geq 1
, then

\Phi(G)\geq k(t+1)
, which is sharp by
G_{n,k,t}
in Construction
6.3
.

Proof.

We have the usual two cases depending on whether
G
is
X
-surplus.

Case 1:

|N(S)|=|S|
for some nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
.

By Theorem
1.1
, there are at least
k

S
-matchings in
G
.
Since
\Phi(G)\geq 1
, for some
T\subseteq Y-N(S)
there is a perfect matching in
the subgraph
G^{\prime}
induced by
(X-S)\cup T
. Note that
N(T)=X-S
.
For each such matching
M
and each vertex
y\in Y-N(S)-T
,
we can use an edge
xy
with
x\in N(T)
instead of the edge covering
x
in

M
to obtain a matching
M^{\prime}
that combines with any
S
-matching. Doing this
with each vertex of
Y-N(S)-T
yields a total of
k(t+1)

X
-matchings
when
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 1
.

Case 2:

\left|N(S)\right|>\left|S\right|
for every proper subset
S
of
X
.

We will use induction on
n+t
, where
n=\left|X\right|
. We first prove the claim when

t=1
. Since
\left|X\right|\geq 3
, we have
\left|Y\right|\geq 4
; consider
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}\in Y
.
Obtain
G_{1}
from
G
by merging
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
into a new vertex
y^{\prime}_{1}
; that
is, deleting
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
and let
y^{\prime}_{1}
inherit the incident edges from
both. Obtain
G_{2}
from
G
by similarly merging
y_{3}
and
y_{4}
into a new
vertex
y^{\prime}_{2}
. Since neighborhoods of subsets in
X
decrease by at most
1

when moving from
G
to
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
, both satisfy Hall’s Condition, and
Theorem
1.1
yields at least
k

X
-matchings in each. Such matchings
also cover the modified
Y
. Hence the
X
-matchings in
G_{1}
come from

X
-matchings in
G
that cover one of
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
and both of
\{y_{3},y_{4}\}
,
while those in
G_{2}
come from
X
-matchings in
G
that cover one of

\{y_{3},y_{4}\}
and both of
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
. These sets of matchings are disjoint,
so
\Phi(G)\geq 2k
, as desired.

Thus we may assume
t>1
. Let
Y^{\prime}
be the set of vertices in
Y
with
exactly one neighbor.

Case 2a:

There exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y^{\prime}
having distinct neighbors.

Let
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
be the neighbors of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, respectively.
Obtain
G^{\prime}
from
G
by merging
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
into a new vertex
y^{\prime}
.
Since
G
is near-surplus,
\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq 1
. The induction hypothesis applies
to yield
kt

X
-matchings in
G^{\prime}
, each of which comes from an
X
-matching
in
G
covering at most one of
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
. We need
k
additional

X
-matchings using both
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Let
G^{\prime\prime}=G-\{x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\}-Y^{\prime\prime}
,
where
Y^{\prime\prime}
consists of any other vertices in
Y^{\prime}
whose only neighbor lies
in
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. Since vertices of
X-\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
have no neighbors in
Y

that were deleted,
G^{\prime\prime}
satisfies Hall’s Condition. Although we no longer
can guarantee excess vertices in
G^{\prime\prime}
, still Theorem
1.1
guarantees

k
matchings in
G^{\prime\prime}
the cover
X-\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
, and adding
x_{1}y_{1}
and

x_{2}y_{2}
to these yields the desired extra
k

X
-matchings in
G
.

Case 2b:

Y^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, and all vertices of
Y^{\prime}
have the same
neighbor
x^{\prime}
in
X
.

Let
G^{\prime}=G-x^{\prime}-Y^{\prime}
, and let
s=\left|Y^{\prime}\right|
and
X^{\prime}=X-\{x^{\prime}\}
. Since

N_{G^{\prime}}(x)=N_{G}(x)
for
x\in X^{\prime}
, we can apply the induction hypothesis if

\left|X\right|\geq 4
to find
k(t-(s-1)+1)
matchings in
G^{\prime}
that cover
X^{\prime}
. We can
extend each to an
X
-matching in
G
in
s
ways, and all these matchings are
distinct. Since
s(t+2-s)
is minimized on the allowed interval for
s
when

s=1
, we again obtain
\Phi(G)\geq k(t+1)
.

If
\left|X\right|=3
, then note that each vertex of
Y-Y^{\prime}
is adjacent to each vertex
of
X^{\prime}
, since by the definition of
Y^{\prime}
the vertices of
Y-Y^{\prime}
have at least
two neighbors. Hence when we choose an edge incident to some
x\in X^{\prime}
, we can
complete an
X
-matching containing it in at least
s(t+2-s)
ways. This gives
at least
k(t+1)

X
-matchings starting with either vertex of
X^{\prime}
, and we
may count each matching twice. Again
\Phi(G)\geq k(t+1)
.

Case 2c:

Y^{\prime}=\varnothing
, so
\left|N(y)\right|\geq 2
for all
y\in Y
.

Fix a vertex
x\in X
. Among all
y\in N(x)
, let
y^{\prime}
be the neighbor
y

that minimizes
\Phi(G-x-y)
(this is nonzero for all
y
). Obtain
G^{\prime}

from
G
by replacing each edge incident to
x
with a copy of
xy^{\prime}
.
Note that
\Phi(G^{\prime})\leq\Phi(G)
. Furthermore, each vertex of
Y
has lost
at most one neighbor (
x
), so
\delta_{Y}(G^{\prime})\geq 1
, but vertices of
X-\{x\}

have lost no neighbors. Let
G^{\prime\prime}=G-x-y^{\prime}
. We can apply the induction
hypothesis to
G^{\prime\prime}
to obtain
k(t+1)
matchings covering
X-\{x\}
in
G^{\prime\prime}
,
and each extends by copies of
xy^{\prime}
to
k

X
-matchings in
G^{\prime}
.
Hence
\Phi(G)\geq k^{2}(t+1)
.

\Box

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
G
be an
X,Y
-bigraph with an
X
-matching, such that
|X|\geq 3
,

\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
, and
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2
. If
|Y|-|X|=t\geq 1
, then

\Phi(G)\geq 2k(t+1)
(sharp by
G^{\prime}_{n,k,t}
in Construction
6.3
),
except that
\Phi(G)\geq 2k(t+1)-1
when
(n,k,t)=(3,3,1)
(sharp by
G_{7}

in Construction
6.3
).

Proof.

We have the usual two cases depending on whether
G
is
X
-surplus.

Case 1:

|N(S)|=|S|
for some nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
.

By Theorem
1.1
, there are at least
k

S
-matchings in
G
.
Let
X^{\prime}=X-S
. Since
G
has an
X
-matching, there is a perfect matching in
the subgraph
G^{\prime}
induced by
X^{\prime}\cup T
, for some
T\subseteq Y-N(S)
. Note that

N(T)=X^{\prime}
. Since
\delta_{Y}(G)\geq 2
, applying Theorem
1.1
to
G^{\prime}
as a

T,X^{\prime}
-bigraph yields at least two perfect matchings in
G^{\prime}
. For each such
matching
M
and each vertex
y\in Y-(N(S)\cup T)
, we can introduce an edge

xy
to replace the edge incident to
x
in
M
. Thus
\Phi(G)\geq k(2+2t)
.
(We cannot guarantee more here because
y
may have only one neighbor in
X^{\prime}
.)

Case 2:

|N(S)|>|S|
for every nonempty proper subset
S
of
X
.

This case cannot occur unless
k\geq 2
holds, and it requires
\left|N(x)\right|\geq 2
for
all
x\in X
. Note that after merging two vertices in
Y
, the resulting graph
still satisfies Hall’s Condition for an
X
-matching; this allows us to use
induction on
t
. For the base case, we show that
\Phi(G)\geq 4k
when
t=1
.
We will use Theorem
1.2
, which provides different lower bounds based on

k
, so we consider three cases, for
k=2
,
k=3
, and
k\geq 4
.

If
G
is leafless, then
\left|E(G)\right|\geq 2\left|Y\right|=2(n+t)
, and hence
b\geq 0
in the
statement of Theorem
1.5
, which yields

\Phi(G)\geq[(n-1)\cdot 1+2](1+1)\geq 8
, since
n\geq 3
.
Hence we may assume there exists
y\in Y
with unique neighbor
x^{\prime}
, with

x^{\prime}y
having multiplicity at least
2
.
Let
G^{*}=G-y
and
G^{\prime}=G-y-x^{\prime}
. Let
X^{\prime}=X-\{x^{\prime}\}
and
Y^{\prime}=Y-y
.
Each
X
-matching in
G^{*}
is also an
X
-matching in
G
. Each
X^{\prime}
-matching
in
G^{\prime}
extends to an
X
-matching in
G
that is not in
G^{*}
in at least two
ways by adding a copy of
x^{\prime}y
. Hence

\Phi(G)\geq\Phi(G^{*})+2\Phi(G^{\prime}).

Since
G^{*}
contains all edges incident to each vertex of
Y^{\prime}
, we have

\delta_{Y^{\prime}}(G^{*})\geq 2
. Hence Theorem
1.1
applies to
G^{*}
as a

Y^{\prime},X
-bigraph to yield
\Phi(G^{*})\geq 2
. Also, by the characterization
of sharpness for
k=2
in Theorem
5.2
,
\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq 3
.
Thus,
\Phi(G)\geq 2+2\cdot 3=8
.

For
k\geq 3
, since
|X|\geq 3
and
t\geq 1
, we have distinct

y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4}\in Y
. Merging two vertices in
Y
yields a graph
G^{\prime}

satisfying Hall’s Condition, so Theorem
5.2
yields

\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq 2k
when
k\geq 4
and
\Phi(G^{\prime})\geq 2k-1
when
k=3
.
These are perfect matchings in
G^{\prime}
, since
t=1
.

These matchings when
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are merged yield
X
-matchings in
G
that
omit
y_{1}
or
y_{2}
and cover the rest of
Y
. When
y_{3}
and
y_{4}
are
merged, the resulting
X
-matchings in
G
omit
y_{3}
or
y_{4}
and cover the
rest of
Y
. Hence in total we have
4k
distinct
X
-matchings if
k\geq 4
.

When
k=3
, Theorem
5.2
gives us at least six
X
-matchings after each
merge unless
\left|X\right|=3=\left|Y\right|-1
and the graph resulting from the merge is
G_{6}
.
Note that the merge does not lose any edges. In
G_{6}
, one vertex of the
reduced part
Y^{\prime}
has degree
5
, while the other two vertices have degree
2
.
Hence
G
cannot reduce to
G_{6}
in both merges on a pairing of four vertices,
so
\Phi(G)\geq 11
. In order to have
\Phi(G)\geq 11
, each pairing must produce
one copy of
G_{6}
. Hence
G
has one vertex in
Y
with degree
3
and three
vertices with degree
2
. Using also that
G
is
X
-surplus, we obtain

\Phi(G)\geq 12
unless
G
is
G_{7}
as in Construction
6.3
.

Having completed the proof for
t=1
, we proceed by induction.
Suppose
t\geq 2
. Note that a merge of two vertices in
Y
when
t=2
cannot
produce
G_{7}
, because
G_{7}
has maximum degree
3
, and merging any two
vertices with degree at least
2
will produce a vertex with degree at least

4
. Hence when we perform a merge in
Y
, the resulting graph will satisfy
Hall’s Condition and have at least
2kt

X
-matchings.
We consider three subcases.

Case 2a:

There exist
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
having distinct unique
neighbors.

Merging
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
yields
2kt

X
-matchings in
G
that all use exactly
one of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. Since
\left|X\right|\geq 3
, when we delete
y_{1}
and
y_{2}

and their neighbors, we obtain an
X^{\prime\prime},Y^{\prime\prime}
-bigraph
G^{\prime\prime}
satisfying Hall’s
Condition with
\delta_{X^{\prime\prime}}(G^{\prime\prime})\geq k
. Hence Theorem
1.1
applies
to yield at least
k

X^{\prime\prime}
-matchings in
G^{\prime\prime}
, each of which extends in at
least four ways to cover
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
and their neighbors. Since these

X
-matchings were not counted previously,
\Phi(G)\geq 2k(t+2)>2k(t+1)
.

Case 2b:

Some vertex of
Y
has only one neighbor, and all
y\in Y

having only one neighbor have the same neighbor
x_{1}\in X
.
Let

Y_{1}=\{y\in Y\colon\,\left|N(y)\right|=1\}
; note
Y_{1}\subseteq N(x_{1})
.

If
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
, then pick
y_{1}\in Y_{1}
and
y_{2}\in Y-Y_{1}
. Form

G^{\prime}
from
G
by merging
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
. By the induction hypothesis,

G
has at least
2kt

X
-matchings that cover at most one of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.
If we can choose
y_{2}
having exactly one neighbor in
\{x_{2},x_{3}\}
, which
we may assume by symmetry is
x_{2}
, then we can match
x_{1}
with
y_{1}
in
two ways and match
x_{2}
with
y_{2}
in one way, without using any neighbor of

x_{3}
. Hence we can complete the
X
-matching by matching
x_{3}
in at least

k
ways, yielding at least
2k

X
-matchings that cover both
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
.

If no such vertex
y_{2}
exists, then
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
are adjacent to all
of
Y-Y_{1}
. Since
\left|Y-Y_{1}\right|=\left|N(\{x_{2},x_{3}\})\right|>2
in Case 2, we can
choose
y_{3}\in Y-Y_{1}-\{y_{2}\}
. Merging
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
yields at least
2kt

X
-matchings in
G
that cover at most one of
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
. Since both

x_{2}
and
x_{3}
are adjacent to both of
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
, we can match

\{x_{2},x_{3}\}
into
\{y_{2},y_{3}\}
in at least two ways and match
x_{1}
into

Y_{1}
in at least
k
ways, again establishing the lower bound.

If
\left|X\right|\geq 4
, then
|X-\{x_{1}\}|\geq 3
. Let
s=\left|Y_{1}\right|
. Since

\left|N(X-\{x_{1}\})\right|>n-1
, we have
1\leq s\leq t
. There are at least
2s
ways to
match
x_{1}
into
Y_{1}
. The graph
G-x_{1}
satisfies the hypotheses of
Theorem
6.4
, since vertices of
Y-Y_{1}
have a neighbor in
\{x_{2},x_{3}\}
,
by the definition of
Y_{1}
. Hence
\Phi(G-x_{1})\geq k(t-s+2)

and
\Phi(G)\geq 2k(t+2-s)s\geq 2k(t+1)
.

Case 2c:

Every vertex of
Y
has at least two neighbors.

Since we are in the
X
-surplus case, every vertex of
X
has at least two
neighbors, so

is leafless. Also

. Hence Theorem
1.5

applies, and
\Phi(G)\geq[(n-1)t+2+b](t+1)
. Since
\delta_{X}(G)\geq k
,
there are at least

edges in

, so

.
It suffices to show
(n-1)t+2+nk-2(n+t)\geq 2k
, or

(n-3)t+2+n(k-2)\geq 2k
. Since

by hypothesis, we need only show

(n-2)k\geq 2(n-1)
, or
k/2\geq 1+1/(n-2)
. Already

suffices, and
we earlier completed the proof for

. When

, we have the desired
inequality unless

.

In the case

, consider the needed inequality
(n-1)t+2+b\geq 2k
.
With

being the number of edges, this reduces to

. This holds if

, so we may assume that
all three vertices of

have degree exactly
3
. With every vertex of
Y

having at least two neighbors and only nine edges in total, this requires

\left|Y\right|\leq 4
. Hence

, but we have already completed the case

.
