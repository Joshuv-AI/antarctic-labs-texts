Abstract
. This paper explores continuous-time and state-space optimal stopping problems from a reinforcement learning perspective. We begin by formulating the stopping problem using randomized stopping times, where the decision maker’s control is represented by the probability of stopping within a given time—specifically, a bounded, non-decreasing, càdlàg control process. To encourage exploration and facilitate learning, we introduce a regularized version of the problem by penalizing the performance criterion with the cumulative residual entropy of the randomized stopping time. The regularized problem takes the form of an ( n + 1 ) (n+1) -dimensional degenerate singular stochastic control with finite-fuel, where the regularized free boundary becomes the graph of a function mapping the state variable of the original stopping problem into the probability of stopping. We address this singular control problem through the dynamic programming principle, which enables us to identify the unique optimal exploratory strategy. Finally, we propose both model-based and model-free reinforcement learning algorithms tailored for exploratory optimal stopping problems. We establish policy improvement guarantees for the proposed algorithms. Moreover, the model-free method is of actor–critic type and it is scalable in high-dimensions under neural network parameterization.

Optimal stopping, singular stochastic control, free boundary problem, entropy regularization, reinforcement learning

1.
Introduction

In optimal stopping (OS) problems, a decision maker chooses a time to
take a given action based on an adaptively observed stochastic process in order to optimize an expected performance criterion. Problems of this type are found in the area of Statistics, where the action taken may be to test a hypothesis or to estimate a parameter; in the area of Operations Research, where the action may be to replace a machine, hire a secretary, or reorder stock; and in Mathematical Finance,
in the context of pricing American contingent claims or in irreversible investment problems
(we refer to the textbook
[
51
]
and to the references therein).

Despite the wide-ranging applications of optimal stopping across various fields, most of the existing literature relies on the
full knowledge
of the system, including both the underlying process and the reward function. Little is known about addressing these problems in a model-free context, where decision-makers interact with an unknown system and learn to improve their decisions over time. In this regard, machine learning techniques, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), offer a promising framework. However, the existing literature has largely focused on “frequently controlling the system”, leaving the RL framework for optimal stopping largely unexplored.

Generally speaking, RL aims to learn optimal strategies to control an unknown dynamical system (or random environment) by
interacting with the system through exploration and exploitation
[
60
]
.
In recent years, the availability of vast amounts of data and advances in computational resources have spurred extensive research in both theoretical developments and practical applications of RL.
While much of the existing literature on RL has focused on discrete-time problems,
continuous-time RL is rapidly growing and presents unique challenges and technical difficulties in algorithmic design, stability analysis, and efficiency guarantees.
A key question is how to balance the trade-off between exploring unknown information in the continuous-time system and exploiting current information to take near-optimal actions.
In their seminal work,
[
70
]
propose adding Shannon’s entropy to the objective function of a continuous-time relaxed linear-quadratic control problem to encourage exploration, leading to an optimal distribution policy of Gaussian-type with the mean capturing the exploited policy and the variance quantifying the degree of exploration.
Since then, there have been studies on continuous-time RL through the lens of partial differential equations and control theory under entropy regularization, primarily focusing on
regular controls
, such as controlling the drift and volatility coefficients of the underlying stochastic processes
[
3
,
34
,
37
,
38
,
46
,
66
,
69
,
70
]
.

One key challenge that distinguishes OS from regular control is that OS involves a non-smooth decision—whether to stop or continue—while regular controls gradually change the dynamics through drift and/or volatility coefficients.
As a result, gradient-based RL algorithms, although popular for regular controls, cannot be directly applied to the “stop-or-continue” decisions in our setting
[
55
,
59
]
.
To overcome this difficulty, we replace these sharp rules based on the hitting times by stopping probabilities, leading to a
randomized stopping time
that follows the stopping probability.
Unlike the heuristic of a fuzzy boundary considered in
[
55
]
, where the agent stops with a probability proportional to the distance to the boundary, our approach to randomized stopping time is based on principled guidance—regularized objective function with cumulative residual entropy.
Another advantage of randomized stopping time is its exploratory nature; it stops at different scenarios according to certain probability, thereby collecting more information from the unknown environment in the RL regime.
Compared to classic RL settings, exploration is particularly essential for optimal stopping problems because the terminal reward can only be collected upon making the stopping decision, contributing to the
reward sparsity
challenge in RL
[
20
,
33
]
.
Therefore, randomized stopping time helps the decision-maker learn more about the unknown terminal reward.

Following the above idea, this paper proposes a framework to address continuous time and state-space OS problems via continuous-time RL.
We first embed the perspective of exploration-exploitation into OS, and then use this construction to design a new type of RL algorithm for which we provide a convergence analysis.

1.1.
Our work and contributions

In this section, we provide an informal discussion on the key ideas and contributions of this work.
The precise assumptions and statements of the results can be found in Sections
2
-
4
.

The classical OS problem (without regularization) is given, for any initial state
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho s}\pi(X^{x}_{s}){\rm d}s+e^{-\rho\tau}G(X^{x}_{\tau})\right],

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad{\rm d}X^{x}_{t}=b(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sigma(X^{x}_{t})dW_{t},\quad X^{x}_{0}=x,

where the maximization is performed over stopping times
\tau
of the (augmented) filtration generated by the Brownian motion
W
,
\rho>0
, and the functions
b,\sigma,\pi,G
are the data of the problem (satisfying the assumptions in Section
2
).

To model exploration, we follow the ideas in
[
68
]
and let the agent randomize the choice of the stopping time.
The resulting strategies are chacterized by nondecreasing processes
\xi=(\xi_{t})_{t}
with
0\leq\xi_{t}\leq 1
,
where
\xi_{t}
is the probability that the agent stops before time
t
according to

\xi_{t}=\mathbb{P}(\tau\leq t|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W})
.
We refer to such processes
\xi
as
singular controls
.
However, allowing for randomized stopping times
does not change
the optimal value function and, more importantly, it does not imply that optimal actions are necessarily randomized/exploratory (see Proposition
2.4
).
From the RL point of view, this non-exploratory behavior of the optimal control suggests that, without proper modification of the objective function,
optimizing
and
collecting information
do not naturally come together.

To overcome the above-mentioned non-exploratory phenomenon and to incentivize exploration, we reward intermediate values of
\xi_{t}
by perturbing the original OS problem via the
cumulative residual entropy (CRE)
[
53
]
of the probability measures
\xi

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\rm CRE}(\xi)&:=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(1-{\xi_{t}}\right)\log\left(1-{\xi_{t}}\right){\rm d}t.\end{aligned}

The adoption of CRE in RL is
novel
in the literature, as Shannon’s entropy and Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence are more often used. It is worth noting that this CRE criterion is more suitable for encouraging randomized optimal stopping time by postponing the exercise time via a large cumulative probability.

The resulting
entropy regularized OS problem
takes the form of the singular control problem:

V^{\lambda}(x):=\sup_{\xi}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\underbrace{\big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})(1-\xi_{t}){\rm d}t+G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\big)}_{\text{exploitation}}-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\underbrace{\left(1-\xi_{t}\right)\log\left(1-\xi_{t}\right)}_{\text{exploration}}{\rm d}t\bigg],

where
\lambda>0
is a temperature parameter that balances the exploitation and exploration.
For any
\lambda>0
, such a problem admits a unique optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}
.
The proposed regularization naturally approximates the original OS problem.
Indeed, we show that (see formal statements in Propositions
2.9
and
2.10
):

•

\sup_{x}|V^{\lambda}(x)-V(x)|\leq\lambda(\rho e)^{-1}\to 0
as
\lambda\to 0
.

•

For any sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k}\to 0
, the sequence
(\xi^{\lambda_{k}})_{k}
converges (up to subsequences) to an optimal singular control
\xi^{*}
of the original OS with randomized stopping times.

These convergence results have also been discovered in
[
70
]
for the linear-quadratic control with Shannon’s entropy, using explicit formulas for the value functions and optimal controls.

A detailed study of the entropy regularized OS problem is then performed by applying the dynamic programming principle (DPP).
To this end, we introduce the additional controlled state variable
Y^{y,\xi}_{t}=y-\xi_{t}
,
y\in[0,1]
, and define, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
, the extended problem

V^{\lambda}(x,y):=\sup_{\xi_{t}\leq y}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y^{y,\xi}_{t}-\lambda Y^{y,\xi}_{t}\log(Y^{y,\xi}_{t})\big){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\bigg].

This is an
(n+1)
-dimensional
degenerate
singular stochastic control problem with finite-fuel.
Notice that
V^{\lambda}(x,1)=V^{\lambda}(x)
.
In particular, we show that (see Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
for the formal statements):

•

The value function
{{V^{\lambda}}}
is
W_{loc}^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1))
and it is the unique solution to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) variational inequality

\max\big\{\left(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho\right)V^{\lambda}(x,y)+\pi(x){y-\lambda y\log y},-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)\big\}=0,

with
V^{\lambda}(x,0)=0
, where

\mathcal{L}_{x}f(x,y):=b(x)D_{x}f(x,y)+\frac{1}{2}\tr\big(\sigma\sigma^{*}(x)D_{x}^{2}f(x,y)\big).

•

The optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}
is of reflecting type and is given by

{\xi^{\lambda}_{t}:=\sup_{s\leq t}\big(y-g_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{s})\big)^{+}},

with the reflecting free boundary defined as
{{g_{\lambda}}}(x):=\sup\big\{y\in[0,1]\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\big\}
.

These results illustrate that the optimal control of the entropy regularized OS problem is not related to any strict stopping time; that is, by introducing the entropy regularization, we obtain optimal strategies of exploratory type.
From the RL point of view, the exploratory behavior of the optimal control is desirable, as it suggests that
optimizing
and
collecting information
from the environment can actually be achieved together.
Moreover, we obtain a characterization of the minimal optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
in terms of the optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}
, given by

\tau^{*}=\inf\{t\,|\,\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\geq 1-e^{-1}\},\quad\text{for any $\lambda\geq 0$.}

Thus, learning the optimal reflecting strategy
\xi^{\lambda}
provides the optimal stopping time of the original problem.
Finally, it is important to notice that, while the free boundary of the original stopping problem can be (only) locally expressed as a graph of function, the regularized free boundary becomes the graph of a global function mapping the state variable of the original stopping problem into the probability of stopping.

From the technical point of view, showing the regularity of
V^{\lambda}
(required for the characterization of
\xi^{\lambda}
) involves several challenges.
In particular, while the regularity in
x
is studied via semi-convexity estimates and PDE arguments, the regularity in
y
is obtained via a probabilistic connection with a different but related OS problem.

Our theoretical analysis suggests a new approach to design RL algorithms for OS problems, which aims at learning the boundary of the
\lambda
-entropy regularized problem rather than the boundary of the original OS problem.
Notice that the value mismatch introduced by the temperature parameter
\lambda
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
.
The proposed RL framework consists of two versions: a model-based numerical version when all model parameters are known, and a model-free learning version when model parameters are not known. For the first version, where all model parameters are known, we design a Policy Iteration algorithm to numerically search for
g_{\lambda}
. For the second version, where the model parameters are unknown, we provide a sample-based Policy Iteration algorithm. Notably, we do not directly estimate model parameters in the design, which enhances robustness against model misspecification and environmental shifts
[
1
,
25
,
32
]
. Our new approach also overcomes the instability issue often encountered when directly learning the boundary of the original OS problem
[
55
]
.

We establish the theoretical foundation of the algorithm for the real option example. Specifically, in the
k
th iteration of the first step, assuming sufficient regularities, we use the second order derivative of
V^{\lambda}_{g_{k}}
(i.e., the value function associated with the policy using reflecting boundary
g_{k}
) with respect to
y
to update
g_{k+1}
, which lies in a region where
V^{\lambda}_{g_{k}}
has a
better regularity
. Mathematically, define the new boundary
{g}_{k+1}(x)
as:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}&\max\Big\{0\leq y<g_{k}(x)\,\Big|\,\partial_{yy}V^{\lambda}_{g_{k}}(x,y)=0\Big\}\,\,\text{if}\,\,\partial_{yy}^{-}V^{\lambda}_{g_{k}}(x,g_{k}(x))>0,\\
&{g}_{k+1}(x)=g_{k}(x)\qquad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where we define the notation
\partial_{yy}^{-}f(x,y):=\lim_{h\rightarrow 0-}\frac{\partial_{y}f(x,y+h)-\partial_{y}f(x,y)}{h}
as the left
y
-derivative of
\partial_{y}f
(if exists). With such an updating scheme, we have the following results (see Theorem
4.2
for the formal statement):

•

Policy improvement:

V^{\lambda}_{g_{k+1}}(x,y)\geq V^{\lambda}_{g_{k}}(x,y)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Policy improvement follows from the weak form of Itô’s formula. Related results appear in
[
70
]
for linear–quadratic control and in
[
21
]
for a one-dimensional real options problem. However, the updating schemes in (
1.1
) are different.

We then develop an actor–critic algorithm in an unknown environment: the agent observes only discretely sampled reward increments and changes of dynamics along trajectories (Algorithm
2
). Specifically, the critic (value network) is trained by minimizing a TD(0)-style squared TD error, and the actor (policy network) is updated according to (
1.1
), with the critic replacing the unknown value function (see Algorithm
3
). The algorithm is tested in numerical experiments and demonstrates promising performance (see Section
5
).

1.2.
Related literature

Our work is related to different streams of literature.

Randomized optimal stopping and singular control.

First of all, we relate to the literature on randomized stopping times.
These typically appear when proving the existence of equilibria in zero-sum or nonzero-sum optimal stopping games (see
[
18
,
43
,
57
,
58
,
68
]
, among others) and can be thought of as the “mixed strategies” in stopping times. Loosely speaking, according to a randomized stopping time, a decision maker chooses
a probability of stopping and at any time decides or not to stop according to that distribution. As discussed in
[
58
]
and
[
68
]
, different but equivalent definitions of randomized stopping times can be developed. In this paper, we follow the approach in
[
68
]
, which identifies randomized stopping times with nondecreasing càdlàg processes starting from zero at the initial time and bounded above by one.
This in turn leads to the fact that the exploratory formulation of the optimal stopping problem is mathematically equivalent to an
(n+1)
-dimensional degenerate singular stochastic control with finite-fuel (see, e.g.,
[
16
,
40
,
42
]
).

Continuous-time RL for regular control.

Initial studies on continuous-time RL primarily focused on algorithm design and the potential for time discretization
[
24
,
50
,
65
]
.

Following the seminal work of
[
70
]
, which provided a mathematical perspective within the framework of linear-quadratic control, there has been a recent surge in employing stochastic control theory and partial differential equations (PDEs) to establish the theoretical foundation of continuous-time RL
[
3
,
34
,
38
,
37
,
46
,
66
,
69
]
.
In particular,
[
66
]
generalized the framework in
[
70
]
and studied the exploratory Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation arising from a general entropy-regularized stochastic control problem;
[
38
]
developed q-Learning algorithm in continuous time;
[
37
]
established policy gradient and actor-critic learning frameworks in continuous time and space. Despite these developments, the overall convergence results of control-inspired or PDE-inspired algorithms, such as the Policy Improvement Algorithm (PIA), remain largely unexplored.
Very recently,
[
34
]
proved a qualitative convergence result for the PIA with bounded coefficients when the diffusion term is not controlled.
[
69
]
generalized the result to unbounded coefficients or controlled diffusion terms by a uniform estimate for the value sequence generated by PIA.
[
46
]

provides a much simpler proof of the convergence of the PIA using Feynman-Kac-type probabilistic representation instead of sophisticated PDE estimates.
Moreover,
[
3
]
showed the convergence of the PIA for an optimal dividend problem, taking advantage that the state process is one dimensional and taking non-negative values. In addition to the PIA method, another popular continuous-time RL algorithm is the policy gradient method.
[
54
]
demonstrated a linear convergence of this method for certain finite-horizon non-linear control problems, while
[
28
]
showed its convergence for finite-horizon exploratory linear-quadratic control problems.

Another important aspect of quantifying the effectiveness of continuous-time RL algorithms is regret analysis. For theoretical developments in this area, see
[
6
,
30
,
63
,
49
,
64
]
.

We note that all the works mentioned above focus on regular controls.

Machine learning for optimal stopping.

RL for optimal stopping is closely related to the challenging RL scenario of sparse rewards
[
20
,
33
]
. More specifically, the terminal reward

G(X_{\tau})
can only be collected upon making the stopping decision, contributing to the sparsity of the rewards. This sparsity significantly complicates the learning process compared to the more straightforward regular control problems in continuous-time settings or classical Markov decision processes in discrete time.

When model parameters are fully known to the decision maker,
[
55
]
and
[
59
]
developed deep-learning-based learning algorithms to learn the stopping boundaries.
[
8
]
considered randomized optimal stopping problems and
established convergence rates for the corresponding forward and backward Monte Carlo optimization algorithms. See also
[
2
]
for solving high-dimensional singular control problems using deep learning methods.

It is worth noting that
[
19
]
proposed a comprehensive framework for policy gradient methods tailored to continuous-time RL. This framework leverages the connection between stochastic control problems and randomized problems, allowing for applications beyond diffusion models, such as regular, impulse, and optimal stopping/switching problems. However, no theoretical convergence results have been established yet. The most relevant paper to our setting is
[
23
]
, which considers a regularized American Put Option problem under Shannon’s entropy in one dimension, which involves a single risky asset that follows a geometric Brownian motion. The author employs an intensity control formulation to encourage exploration and demonstrates the convergence of PIA for the regularized problem under a given temperature parameter.
In comparison to the latter work, we highlight some novelties of our approach.
Notably, our analysis of the regularized problem is based on regularity analysis that combines PDE arguments and a probabilistic connection with another OS problem. In contrast, the analysis in
[
23
]
relies primarily on explicit calculations, which are challenging to extend to higher dimensions. Moreover, within our framework, we can show the convergence of the optimal policies of the regularized problems to the original OS-optimal policy as the temperature parameter
\lambda
tends to zero.
While our convergence holds regardless of the dimension of the problem, such a convergence is not clear in
[
23
]
even for the specific American Put Option problem. Recently,
[
14
]
proposed an alternative approach by approximating the optimal stopping problem as a penalized regular control problem with binary decisions,
0
and
1
. They then applied entropy regularization to the binary decision process, transforming it into a classical entropy-regularized stochastic control problem, to which existing RL algorithms can be applied.

Another relevant strand of literature leverages non-parametric statistics for stochastic processes to design learning algorithms for control
[
10
,
12
,
11
,
13
]
. Specifically,
[
12
]
and
[
11
]
addressed one-dimensional singular and impulse control problems with a non-parametric statistical diffusion structure, where strategies are characterized by one or two values that need to be learned.
[
13
]
extended this approach to multivariate reflection problems, while
[
10
]
emphasized statistical properties such as regret and non-asymptotic PAC bounds.

2.
Exploratory formulation and entropy regularization of the OS problem

Consider a discount factor
\rho>0
and, for
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, continuous functions

b:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\ \pi,G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}.

Let
W=(W_{t})_{t}
be an
m
-dimensional Brownian motion on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{P})
and let
\mathbb{F}^{W}=(\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t})_{t}
be the right-continuous extension of the filtration generated by
W
.
Introduce the second-order differential operator

\mathcal{L}_{x}\phi(x):=b(x)D_{x}\phi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\tr(\sigma\sigma^{*}(x)D_{x}^{2}\phi(x)),\quad\phi\in W_{loc}^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right),

and denote by
\mathcal{T}
the set of
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-stopping times.

For an initial condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a stopping time
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, define the profit functional

\displaystyle J(x;\tau)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho s}\pi(X^{x}_{s}){\rm d}s+e^{-\rho\tau}G(X^{x}_{\tau})\right],

\displaystyle\text{subject to }{\rm d}X^{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sigma(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}W_{t},\quad X^{x}_{0}=x,

and consider the optimal stopping problem

(2.1)

V(x):=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}J(x;\tau).

A stopping time
\tau^{*}
is said to be optimal if
J(x;\tau^{*})=V(x)
and the function
V
is referred to as the value function.

In order to have a well-defined problem, we enforce some requirements on the data of the problem.

Assumption 2.1
.

The following conditions hold true:

(1)

There exists a constant
L>0
such that, for
\phi=b,\sigma
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+|x|),\quad|\phi(\bar{x})-\phi(x)|\leq L|\bar{x}-x|,\quad\text{for any $x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

(2)

There exist
p\geq 2
and
K>0
such that, for
\phi=G,\pi
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq K(1+|x|^{p}),\quad\text{for any $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

(3)

\rho
is large enough:

\rho>p(\frac{3}{2}L+(2p-1)L^{2}).

The following result is classical.

Proposition 2.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exists an optimal stopping time.

Proof.

We limit ourselves to directing the reader to a reference. More specifically, Assumption
2.1
implies (see estimate (
3.10
) below and the related (
3.5
)) that, for a suitable constant
C<\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}|\pi(X^{x}_{t})|{\rm d}t+\sup_{t\geq 0}e^{-\rho t}|G(X^{x}_{t})|\right]<C(1+|x|^{p}).

Hence, the result follows by Corollary 2.9 at p. 46 in
[
51
]
(notice that, differently from
[
51
]
, we do not require stopping times to be finite).
∎

Some of the later results (see in particular Proposition
2.4
and Proposition
2.10
below) enjoy a sharper statement under uniqueness of the optimal stopping time, which is typically satisfied in standard Markovian settings.
For the sake of illustration, we discuss the following sufficient conditions in the one-dimensional case.

Lemma 2.3
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, if

(2.2)

\displaystyle\text{$n=1$, $G\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, $\sigma^{2}(x)\geq c>0$ and $D_{x}G(x)\leq C(1+|x|)$ for some $c,C\in(0,\infty)$,}

\displaystyle\text{the function $\hat{\pi}:=\pi+(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)G$ is strictly increasing and there exists $\bar{x}$ s.t. $\hat{\pi}(\bar{x})=0$,}

then the optimal stopping time is unique.

The proof of this lemma relies on classical arguments in OS and it is provided in Appendix
A
.

2.1.
Exploratory formulation via singular controls

Inspired by
[
68
]
, we introduce a notion of randomized/exploratory stopping times.
Define the set of
singular controls
as the set of processes

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(1):=\Big\{\xi:

\displaystyle\,\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to[0,1],\text{ $\mathbb{F}^{W}$-adapted, nondecreasing, càdlàg, with $\xi_{0-}=0$}\Big\},

and assume the probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{P})
to be large enough to accommodate a
W
-independent uniformly distributed random variable
U:\Omega\to[0,1]
.

Given any
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
, we consider the random variable
\tau^{\xi}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
by setting
\tau^{\xi}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,|\,\xi_{t}>U\right\}
, with the convention
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
, and we will refer to it as
randomized/exploratory stopping time
.
Notice that
\tau^{\xi}
is not necessarily an
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-stopping time.
However, if
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, the process
\xi^{\tau}\in\mathcal{A}(1)
defined by
\xi^{\tau}:=(\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq\tau\}})_{t}
is such that
\tau=\tau^{\xi^{\tau}}
, which gives a natural inclusion of
\mathcal{T}
into
\mathcal{A}(1)
.
Moreover, we have

\mathbb{P}\big(\tau^{\xi}\leq t\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W}\big)=\mathbb{P}\left(U\leq{\xi}_{t}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W}\right)=\int_{0}^{\xi_{t}}{\rm d}u=\xi_{t},

so that
\xi_{t}
can be interpreted as the probability of stopping before time
t
.
Notice also that
\xi_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\xi_{t}
is not necessarily equal to 1: indeed, the related randomized stopping time
\tau^{\xi}
is not necessarily finite.

For
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
, by evaluating the performance of the randomized stopping time
\tau^{\xi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J(x;\tau^{\xi})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{\xi}}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho\tau^{\xi}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau^{\xi}}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t})\mathds{1}_{\{t\leq\tau^{\xi}\}}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t})\mathbb{P}\big(\tau^{\xi}\in{\rm d}t\mid{\mathcal{F}}^{W}\big)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t})\mathds{1}_{\left\{\xi_{t}<U\right\}}{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t})\left(1-\xi_{t}\right){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right].

Here and in the sequel, with slight abuse of notation we set
\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi_{t}{\rm d}\xi_{t}:=\int_{0-}^{\infty}\phi_{t}{\rm d}\xi_{t}
for any continuous function
\phi
for which the integral is well defined.
The previous expression suggests to define a profit functional in terms of the singular controls
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
by

J^{0}(x;\xi):=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t})\left(1-\xi_{t}\right){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right],\quad\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1),

as a natural extension of
J
to randomized stopping times.

However, allowing for randomized stopping times does not change the optimal value and (crucial for our story) it does not imply that optimal actions are necessarily randomized, as discussed in the next proposition and remark.

Proposition 2.4
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

V(x)=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)}J^{0}(x;\xi)=J^{0}(x;\xi^{*}),

with
\xi^{*}:=(\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq\tau^{*}\}})_{t}
and
\tau^{*}
optimal for
J(x;\cdot)
.
Moreover, if the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
is unique (e.g., if (
2.2
) holds), then
\xi^{*}:=(\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq\tau^{*}\}})_{t}
is the unique optimal control for
J^{0}(x;\cdot).

Proof.

For generic
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
and
z\in[0,1]
, we can define the stopping time
\tau^{\xi}(z)
as

\tau^{\xi}(z):=\inf\{t\geq 0\,|\,\xi_{t}\geq z\},

with the convention
\inf\emptyset:=+\infty
.
Indeed, notice that
\tau^{\xi}(z)=+\infty
if
z>\xi_{\infty}
.
By using Fubini’s theorem and then the change of variable formula (see Chapter 0 in
[
56
]
), we have

\displaystyle J^{0}(x

\displaystyle;\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t})\left(1-\xi_{\infty}+\int_{t}^{\infty}{\rm d}\xi_{s}\right){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1-\xi_{\infty})\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho s}\pi(X^{x}_{s}){\rm d}s+e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t})\right){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(1-\xi_{\infty})\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\pi(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\xi_{\infty}}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\tau^{\xi}(z)}e^{-\rho s}\pi(X^{x}_{s}){\rm d}s+e^{-\rho{\tau^{\xi}(z)}}G(X^{x}_{\tau^{\xi}(z)})\bigg){\rm d}z\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau^{\xi}(z)}e^{-\rho s}\pi(X^{x}_{s}){\rm d}s+e^{-\rho{\tau^{\xi}(z)}}G(X^{x}_{\tau^{\xi}(z)})\bigg]{\rm d}z=\int_{0}^{1}J(x;\tau^{\xi}(z)){\rm d}z.

Thus, since
\tau^{\xi}(z)
is an
\mathbb{F}^{W}
-stopping time, by the optimality of
\tau^{*}
we have

J^{0}(x;\xi)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}J(x;\tau){\rm d}z=J(x;\tau^{*})=V(x)=J^{0}(x;\xi^{*}),

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\xi^{*}
is such that
\tau^{\xi^{*}}(z)=\tau^{*}
for any
z\in(0,1]
. This proves the first part of the proposition.

Assume now that the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
is unique.
By repeating the previous argument with
\xi
being optimal, we obtain that
J(x;\tau^{\xi}(z))=J(x;\tau^{*})

{\rm d}z
-a.e. in
(0,1)
.
By uniqueness of the optimal stopping time, we deduce that
\tau^{\xi}(z)=\tau^{*}

{\rm d}z
-a.e. in
(0,1)
, which in turn implies
\xi=\xi^{*}
.
∎

Remark 2.5

(Non-exploratory behavior of the optimal controls)
.

We elaborate on Proposition
2.4
from the RL and exploratory points of view.
Under the uniqueness of the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
, the optimal singular control
\xi^{*}
in fact corresponds to the strict stopping time
\tau^{*}
.
Thus, no randomization is needed for the optimal strategy.
In particular, once an action becomes necessary, the agent stops the process, which prevents the gradual collection of information on the performance of other actions.
From the RL point of view, this non-exploratory behavior of the optimal control
\xi^{*}
suggests that optimizing and collecting information do not naturally come together, and a modification is necessary in order to overcome this phenomenon (see the related Remark
3.6
below for a discussion on the consequences and benefits of our entropy regularization).

2.2.
Entropy regularization

In light of Proposition
2.4
and of the related Remark
2.5
, we introduce a regularization term to incentivize exploration/randomization. This is achieved by incorporating an entropy term to regularize the problem, drawing motivation from the RL literature.

Since the sample paths of the processes
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
are not necessarily absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we choose (a discounted version of) the
cumulative residual entropy
(see
[
53
]
) weighted by a parameter
\lambda>0
;
namely, we consider, for
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
the entropy

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\lambda}(\tau^{\xi}):=

\displaystyle-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{\xi}\geq t\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{t}^{W}\right)\log\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\tau^{\xi}\geq t\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}^{W}\right)\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(1-\xi_{t}\right)\log\left(1-\xi_{t}\right){\rm d}t=:\Lambda^{\lambda}(\xi).

The entropy
\Lambda^{\lambda}
is nonnegative and achieves its highest values when the probability
\xi_{t}
is near the level
e^{-1}
, thus incentivising the use of randomized stopping times.

Building on this intuition, we define an entropy regularized OS problem

(2.4)

V^{\lambda}(x):=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)}J^{\lambda}(x;\xi),

where the exploration–exploitation trade off is captured by the profit functional

J^{\lambda}(x;\xi):=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\underbrace{\big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})(1-\xi_{t}){\rm d}t+G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\big)}_{\text{exploitation}}-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\underbrace{\left(1-\xi_{t}\right)\log\left(1-\xi_{t}\right)}_{\text{exploration}}{\rm d}t\bigg].

A control
\xi^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{A}(1)
is said to be optimal if
J^{\lambda}(x;\xi^{\lambda})=V^{\lambda}(x)
.

Before studying the entropy regularized problem and its connections with the original stopping problem, we list here few remarks.

Remark 2.6
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
, the existence of a unique optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}
can be established by exploiting the strict concavity of the functional
J^{\lambda}(x;\cdot)
in the control variable
\xi
(see in particular Theorem 8 in
[
48
]
).
Moreover, Theorem
3.4
below will characterize such an optimal control and illustrate that the randomized strategy maximizing
J^{\lambda}
does not correspond to a (classical) stopping time (see also Remark
3.6
).

Remark 2.7

(Connection between optimal stopping and singular control)
.

It is well-known in the stochastic control literature that optimal stopping problems and singular control problems enjoy intimate connections.
On the one hand, the derivative (in the controlled state variable) of the value function of a singular control problem can be written as the value function of an optimal stopping problem
[
7
,
9
,
17
,
31
,
41
]
.
On the other hand, randomized stopping strategies can be naturally seen as finite-fuel singular controls (see
[
68
]
or the discussion above), which has the advantage of compactifying the space of stopping strategies (thus explaining its heavy use in the Dynkin game literature).

In this framework, our entropy regularization adds two crucial properties on the latter connection.
First, while solely considering randomized strategies still leads to a degenerate singular control problem (where the optimal actions are pure jumps, as in Proposition
2.4
), our cumulative residual entropy regularizes the problem by making it concave and allows for a more explicit characterization of the optimal control in terms of a reflecting strategy (see Theorem
3.4
below). Secondly, this entropy regularization very well approximates the original OS problem, both in terms of value and optimal strategy (see Propositions
2.9
and
2.10
in the next subsection). For these reasons, our entropy regularization seems to be a useful tool in order to approximate/characterize the equilibria also in the context of Dynkin games, which we leave for future research.

Remark 2.8
.

Our choice to work with a Markovian formulation is motivated by RL.
Since the typical optimization problem that RL aims to solve (in the discrete time case) is a Markov decision problem, starting our study with a Markovian optimal stopping problem seems to be a natural choice.
However, it should be pointed out that our entropy regularization formulation (and its subsequent analysis) also allows us to treat optimal stopping problems in a non-Markovian framework.
In this regard, we limit ourselves to mentioning that (while we will solve the Markovian entropy-regularized problem using the DDP approach in Section
3
below), in order to tackle the non-Markovian entropy-regularized OS problem, one can rely on representation theorems via backward stochastic differential equations (see
[
4
,
5
]
among others).

2.3.
Vanishing entropy limit

With elementary arguments, one can show that the entropy regularized problem approximates the original OS problem as the temperature parameter
\lambda
tends to zero.

Proposition 2.9
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, for any
\lambda,\bar{\lambda}\in[0,1]
we have

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|V^{\lambda}(x)-V^{\bar{\lambda}}(x)|\leq|\lambda-\bar{\lambda}|(\rho e)^{-1}.

In particular,
V^{\lambda}\to V
uniformly on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as
\lambda\to 0
.

Proof.

Take
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda,\bar{\lambda}\in[0,1]
.
If
\lambda\geq\bar{\lambda}
, for every
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
we have
J^{\lambda}(x;\xi)\geq J^{\bar{\lambda}}(x;\xi)
, so that

0\leq V^{\lambda}(x)-V^{\bar{\lambda}}(x).

Moreover, upper bounding the right-hand side above, we find

\displaystyle 0\leq V^{\lambda}(x)-V^{\bar{\lambda}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)}J^{\lambda}(x;\xi)-\sup_{\bar{\xi}\in\mathcal{A}(1)}J^{\bar{\lambda}}(x;\bar{\xi})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)}\Big(J^{\lambda}(x;\xi)-J^{\bar{\lambda}}(x;\xi)\Big)

\displaystyle=(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(-(1-\xi_{t})\log(1-\xi_{t})\big){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})\frac{1}{\rho}\sup_{z\in[0,1]}(-z\log z)=(\lambda-\bar{\lambda})(\rho e)^{-1},

which is the claimed bound.
We conclude the proof by choosing
\bar{\lambda}=0
and taking limits as
\lambda\to 0
.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda>0
, let
\xi^{\lambda}
be the unique optimal control as in Remark
2.6
.
Under Assumption
2.1
, the following statements hold true:

(1)

There exist a subsequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\to 0
and an optimal control
\xi^{*}
for
J^{0}(x;\cdot)
such that

\xi^{\lambda_{k}}\to\xi^{*}
weakly in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega\times[0,\infty))
as
k\to\infty
.

(2)

If the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
is unique (e.g., if (
2.2
) holds), then
\xi^{\lambda}\to(\mathds{1}_{\{t\geq\tau^{*}\}})_{t}
weakly in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega\times[0,\infty))
as
\lambda\to 0
.

Proof.

Since the family
(\xi^{\lambda})_{\lambda}
is bounded, we can find a subsequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\to 0
and a limit point
\xi^{*}
such that

\xi^{\lambda_{k}}\to\xi^{*}
weakly in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega\times[0,\infty))
as
k\to\infty
.
We need to show that
\xi^{*}
is optimal for
J^{0}(x;\cdot)
.

First of all, using Proposition
2.9
, we find

(2.5)

\displaystyle|V(x)-J^{0}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})|

\displaystyle\leq|V(x)-J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})|+|J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})-J^{0}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})|

\displaystyle\leq|V(x)-V^{\lambda_{k}}(x)|+{\mathbb{E}}[\Lambda^{\lambda_{k}}(\xi^{\lambda_{k}})]

\displaystyle\leq C{\lambda_{k}}\to 0,\quad\text{as $k\to\infty$.}

Moreover, since
\sup_{t\geq 0}\xi^{\lambda_{k}}_{t}\leq 1
, we can employ (after some minimal adjustment to take care of our infinite time horizon
[0,\infty)
) Lemma 3.5 in
[
39
]
in order to find a subsequence (not relabelled)
(\lambda_{k})_{k}
with
\lambda_{k}\to 0
and a limit point
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)
such that, setting

\zeta^{m}_{t}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\xi^{\lambda_{k}}_{t},

we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}f_{t}{\rm d}\zeta^{m}_{t}\to\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}f_{t}{\rm d}\xi_{t},\ \mathbb{P}\text{-a.s., as $m\to\infty$, for any $f\in C_{b}([0,\infty))$.}

It is easy to show that
\xi=\xi^{*}
.
Furthermore, since
\zeta^{m}\in\mathcal{A}(1)
, by Proposition
2.4
and the concavity of
J^{0}(x;\cdot)
we find

\displaystyle V(x)\geq J^{0}(x;\zeta^{m})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}J^{0}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\big(J^{0}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})-J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}[\Lambda^{\lambda_{k}}(\xi^{\lambda_{k}})]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}J^{\lambda_{k}}(x;\xi^{\lambda_{k}})-(e\rho)^{-1}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\lambda_{k}}\to V(x),\quad\text{as $m\to\infty$,}

where the limits in the right-hand side follows from (
2.5
).
Thus, we obtain

\lim_{m}J^{0}(x;\zeta^{m})=V(x).

By using the convergence of
\zeta^{m}
to
\xi^{*}
and the dominated convergence theorem, the latter limits implies that

J^{0}(x;\xi^{*})=\lim_{m}J^{0}(x;\zeta^{m})=V(x),

proving the optimality of
\xi^{*}
(
cf.
Proposition
2.4
).

If the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
is unique, then, by Proposition
2.4
, the optimal randomized stopping time
\xi^{*}
is unique as well.
Therefore,
\xi^{*}
is the unique limit point of any subsequence of
(\xi^{\lambda})_{\lambda}
, so that the whole sequence converges to
\xi^{*}
.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

From the RL algorithmic design point of view, it is important to notice that Proposition
2.9
quantifies the error which is made when replacing the original OS problem (
2.1
) with the entropy regularized OS problem (
2.4
).

Remark 2.12
.

Regarding Propositions
2.9
and
2.10
, one could try to investigate the convergence of the optimal strategies in terms of their characterization.
More precisely, we will prove in Theorem
3.4
below that the optimal singular control of the entropy-regularized OS problem is of reflection type,
while an optimal stopping time for the OS problem is (under suitable assumptions) an hitting time
\tau^{*}=\inf\{t\geq 0\,|\,V(X^{x}_{t})=G(X^{x}_{t})\}
.
Thus, it is natural to question whether, and in what sense, the reflecting boundary of the entropy-regularized OS problem (
2.4
) converges to the optimal stopping boundary of the original OS problem (
2.1
).
Although establishing this result at a general level may be challenging, a positive answer is provided in our companion paper
[
21
]
, in which we study a classical example of an OS problem arising from real option evaluation.
Moreover, this question is further addressed in Section
3.2
.

3.
Analysis of the entropy regularized OS problem

In this section we study the entropy regularized OS problem, obtaining a characterization of the value function in terms of a related HJB equation (see Theorem
3.2
) and a characterization of the optimal control as a reflecting strategy (see Theorem
3.4
).
We then show further relations with the original OS problem, deriving a formula for the minimal optimal stopping time in terms of the reflecting strategy (see Theorem
3.9
) and a further vanishing entropy limit (see Theorem
3.11
).

3.1.
Solving the entropy regularized OS problem via dynamic programming

To approach problem (
2.4
) via DPP we introduce, for
y\in[0,1]
, the additional controlled state process

Y_{t}^{y,\xi}=y-\xi_{t}

and the set of admissible controls

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(y):=\{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(1)\text{ with }\xi_{t}\in[0,y]\text{ a.s.\ for any }t\geq 0\}.

Thus, for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]
and
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)
, define the profit functional

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big((\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y_{t}^{y,\xi}-\lambda Y_{t}^{y,\xi}\log(Y_{t}^{y,\xi})\big){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\right],

subject to

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\rm d}X^{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sigma(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}W_{t},\quad X^{x}_{0}=x,

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y_{t}^{y,\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\rm d}\xi_{t},\quad Y^{y,\xi}_{0-}=y.

We then consider the optimization problem

(3.4)

V^{\lambda}(x,y):=\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)}J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi),

and we notice that
V^{\lambda}(x)=V^{\lambda}(x,1)
.

In order to study the entropy regularized problem, we introduce the following requirements, which imply Assumption
2.1
.

Assumption 3.1
.

The following conditions hold true:

(1)

There exists a constant
L>0
such that, for
\phi=b,\sigma
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+|x|),

\displaystyle|\phi(\bar{x})-\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L|\bar{x}-x|,

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x)-\delta\phi(\bar{x})-(1-\delta)\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq L\delta(1-\delta)|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

for any
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\delta\in[0,1]
.

(2)

There exist
p\geq 2
and
K>0
such that, for
\phi=G,\pi
, we have

\displaystyle|\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq K(1+|x|^{p}),

\displaystyle|\phi(\bar{x})-\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq K(1+|\bar{x}|^{p-1}+|x|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|,

\displaystyle|\phi(\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x)-\delta\phi(\bar{x})-(1-\delta)\phi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq K\delta(1-\delta)(1+|\bar{x}|^{p-2}+|x|^{p-2})|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

for any
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\delta\in[0,1]
.

(3)

The matrix
a(x):=\sigma^{*}\sigma(x)
is uniformly elliptic; that is, there exists a constant
\kappa_{\sigma}>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}(x)z_{i}z_{j}\geq\kappa_{\sigma}|z|^{2},\quad\text{for any $z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

(4)

\rho
is large enough: namely,

\displaystyle\rho>\max\Big\{

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(4(p-1))}{2},\hat{c}_{1}(2),{\hat{c}_{1}(4)},\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-1))+\hat{c}_{1}(2)}{2},\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2p)}{2},\hat{c}_{0}(p),

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-1))+\hat{c}_{2}(2)}{2},\hat{c}_{2}(2),\frac{\hat{c}_{2}(4)}{2},\frac{\hat{c}_{1}(8)}{2},\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(4(p-2))}{2}\Big\},

where, for generic
q\geq 0
we define the constants

(3.5)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{0}(q)

\displaystyle:=q\Big(\frac{3}{2}L+(q-1)L^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{1}(q)

\displaystyle:=q\Big(L+\frac{q-1}{2}L^{2}\Big),

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{2}(q)

\displaystyle:={2q\Big(L+\frac{2q-1}{2}L^{2}\Big)}+{(2q-1)L+2(q-1)^{2}L^{2}},

which are related to the growth behavior of the underlying diffusion (see the estimates (
3.10
)-(
3.15
) below).

The following theorem characterizes the value function of the entropy regularized OS problem.
The proof of the existence of a solution to the HJB equation is given in Subsection
3.4
, while the proof of its uniqueness is given in Subsection
3.5
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The value function
V^{\lambda}
is
C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])\cap W_{loc}^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1)\right)
, is concave in
y
, is such that
V^{\lambda}(x,y)\leq C(1+|x|^{p})
and it solves in the a.e. sense the HJB equation

\max\big\{\left(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho\right)V^{\lambda}(x,y)+\pi(x)y-\lambda y\log y,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)\big\}=0,

with boundary condition
V^{\lambda}(x,0)=0
.

Moreover, the HJB equation admits a unique solution in the class of
C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])\cap W_{loc}^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1)\right)
functions which are concave in
y
and such that
V(x,y)\leq C(1+|x|^{p})
.

We next move to the characterization of the optimal control.
Define the free boundary function
g_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
by

(3.6)

g_{\lambda}(x):=\sup\big\{y\in[0,1]\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\big\},

where we set
g_{\lambda}(x):=1
if
\{y\in[0,1]\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\}=\emptyset
.
Notice that the function
g_{\lambda}
is well defined by the concavity of
V^{\lambda}
, and that it is upper semi-continuous because of the continuity of
-V_{y}^{\lambda}+G
.
Intuitively, the function
g_{\lambda}
separates the “exploration region”

(3.7)

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:=\{(x,y)\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\},

in which no action is required, from the “stopping region”

(3.8)

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}:=\{(x,y)\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)=0\},

in which the agent has to act.

Remark 3.3
.

We underline that, while the free boundary of the original stopping problem can be (only) locally expressed as a graph of function, the regularized free boundary becomes the graph of the global function
g_{\lambda}
mapping the state variable
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the original stopping problem into the probability of stopping
y\in[0,1]
.

The following theorem characterizes the optimal control in terms of the function
g_{\lambda}
.
Its proof can be essentially found in the Step 2 in Subsection
3.5
and it is briefly summarized in Subsection
3.6
.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]
, there exists a unique optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{A}(y)
, which is given by the reflection policy at the boundary
g_{\lambda}
; that is,

\xi^{\lambda}_{t}:=\sup_{s\leq t}\big(y-g_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{s})\big)^{+},\quad t\geq 0,\quad\xi^{\lambda}_{0-}=0.

A few comments are in order.

Remark 3.5

(Optimal control and the related Skorokhod problem)
.

Notice that the optimal control
\xi^{\lambda}
is the control with minimal total variation such that

(X^{x}_{t},Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\lambda}})\in\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}},\quad\text{for any $t\geq 0,\ \mathbb{P}$-a.s.,}

where
\overline{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}
is the closure of
{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}
.
Such a process is also known as the solution to the Skorokhod reflection problem for the underlying state process in the waiting region
{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}}
.
Generally, characterizing the optimal singular control in terms of the related Skorokhod problem represents a challenging open problem in singular control theory (we refer to the discussion in
[
22
]
for further details).
In our setting, the explicit characterization of
\xi^{\lambda}
(as in Theorem
3.4
) is possible due to the fact that
X^{x}
is uncontrolled and the controls are one-dimensional.

Remark 3.6

(Exploratory behavior of optimal controls)
.

Unlike the OS problem with exploratory strategies but without modification on the objective function (see Proposition
2.4
and the related Remark
2.5
), Theorem
3.4
clarifies that the optimal control of the entropy regularized OS problem is no longer a strict stopping time.
In other words, introducing entropy regularization leads to optimal strategies of an exploratory nature—more precisely, strategies that are exclusively exploratory.

From an RL perspective, the exploratory behavior of the optimal control is crucial, as it allows for simultaneous optimization and information gathering from the environment. Specifically, once the state process reaches the boundary of the exploration region, not only is action required, but, given the regularity of the free boundary
g_{\lambda}
, an infinite number of infinitesimal actions are necessary to prevent the state process from entering the stopping region. This enables the agent to continuously gather information on the optimality of selecting a particular reflecting boundary in a non-episodic manner.

3.2.
More on the vanishing entropy limit

In this subsection we discuss some further results on the connection between our original OS problem and its entropy regularized version.

We begin by recalling from (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) the definitions of exploration region
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\}
and stopping region
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)=0\}
.
For any
y\in[0,1]
, define the sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(y)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)=0\},

and notice that, since
-V_{y}^{\lambda}+G\leq 0
(cf. Theorem
3.2
), one has
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(y)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
.
Following the arguments in
[
41
]
(see in particular Theorem 3.4) we can show that, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1]
, one has the representation

(3.9)

V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\pi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)-\lambda(1+\log y)\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau}\right)\right].

From this representation, we derive the following monotonicity property of the exploration region.

Lemma 3.7
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for
0\leq\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}
, we have that:

(1)

If
y>e^{-1}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}(y)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
;

(2)

If
y=e^{-1}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}(y)=\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
;

(3)

If
y<e^{-1}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}(y)
.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first claim.
When
y>e^{-1}
, we have
1+\log y>0
, hence
-\bar{\lambda}(1+\log y)<-\lambda(1+\log y)
.
Thanks to the representation (
3.9
), we obtain that

V_{y}^{\bar{\lambda}}(x,y)\leq V_{y}^{\lambda}(x,y),\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and $y>e^{-1}$.}

Hence, from the definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
we find
\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}(y)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
.

For
y=e^{-1}
, we have
1+\log y=0
, hence
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(y)
does not depends on
\lambda
, proving the second claim.
The third claim can be proved analogously.
∎

For
\lambda\geq 0
, denote by
\tau^{\lambda}(x,y)
the minimal optimal stopping time of the problem (
3.9
).
Under Assumption
3.1
, Theorem 2.3.5 in
[
45
]
characterizes the minimal stopping time as the first hitting time to the region
{\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}}
; i.e.,
\tau^{\lambda}(x,y)=\inf\{t\,|\,X_{t}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(y)\}
.
Since
\tau^{0}(x,y)
does not depend on
y
, it will be simply denoted by
\tau^{0}(x)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)\to\tau^{0}(x)
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., as
\lambda\to 0
.

Proof.

Thanks to Lemma
3.7
,
for
0\leq\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}
, we have that

\mathcal{E}_{\bar{\lambda}}(1)\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(1)
.
This implies that
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(1)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\bar{\lambda}}(1)
, from which we deduce that

\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)=\inf\{t\,|\,X_{t}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(1)\}\geq\inf\{t\,|\,X_{t}^{x}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bar{\lambda}}(1)\}=\tau^{\bar{\lambda}}(x,1).

Therefore, the family
\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)
is nonincreasing in
\lambda
.
Using this monotonicity, we can define a limiting stopping time

\tau^{*}(x):=\sup_{\lambda>0}\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\tau^{\lambda}(x,1),

and we notice that
\tau^{0}(x)\geq\tau^{*}(x)
.

Next, by optimality of
\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)}e^{-\rho t}\left(\pi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)-\lambda(1+\log y)\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\pi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right)-\lambda(1+\log y)\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau}\right)\right],\quad\text{for any $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$.}

Taking limits as
\lambda\to 0
in the latter inequality (using the growth conditions in Assumption
3.1
and the dominated convergence theorem), we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}(x)}e^{-\rho t}\pi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau^{*}(x)}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau^{*}(x)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\pi\left(X^{x}_{t}\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau}\right)\right],\quad\text{for any $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$.}

Hence,
\tau^{*}(x)
is also optimal. Thus, since
\tau^{0}(x)
is the minimal optimal stopping time and
\tau^{0}(x)\geq\tau^{*}(x)
, we conclude that
\tau^{*}(x)=\tau^{0}(x)
.
∎

For the rest of this subsection, we fix the initial condition of the problem to
(x,1)
, for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Recall the definition of optimally reflecting strategy
\xi^{\lambda}
from Theorem
3.4
, and define an associated stopping time

\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1):=\inf\{t\,|\,\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\geq 1-e^{-1}\}.

The results in the previous subsection now allows to obtain the following characterization, revealing that
\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1)
is actually independent of
\lambda
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for any
\lambda\geq 0
, we have
\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1)=\tau^{0}(x).

Proof.

Given the characterization of
\xi^{\lambda}
of Theorem
3.4
and the definition of
\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1)
, we have

\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1)=\inf\{t\,|\,X^{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(e^{-1})\}.

By Lemma
3.7
, we have
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(e^{-1})=\mathcal{S}_{0}(e^{-1})
, so that

\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1)=\inf\{t\,|\,X^{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(e^{-1})\}=\inf\{t\,|\,X^{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(e^{-1})\}=\tau^{0}(x),

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

From an algorithmic point of view, such a representation has a clear advantage:
If we were able to learn
\xi^{\lambda}
correctly, we would be able to find the original optimal stopping time
\tau^{0}(x)
simply by the expression
\tau^{0}(x)=\inf\{t\,|\,\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\geq 1-e^{-1}\}
.
We also notice that, in such an expression, the dependence on the threshold
e^{-1}
is a direct consequence of our choice of the entropy function
y\mapsto-y\log y
.

We next introduce the following family of reflecting strategies

\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}_{t}:=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\land(1-e^{-1})+(1-e^{-1})\mathds{1}_{[\hat{\tau}^{0}(x),\infty)}(t).

In light of the Theorem
3.9
, we have

\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}_{t}=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\land(1-e^{-1})+(1-e^{-1})\mathds{1}_{[\hat{\tau}^{\lambda}(x,1),\infty)}(t)
. This expression characterizes
\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}
as a reflecting process at the boundary
g_{\lambda}
until the threshold
e^{-1}
, with a jump to
0
at the first hitting time of the threshold.

For the family
\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}
, we can prove a general convergence result, which improves our previous Proposition
2.10
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}_{t}\to\xi^{0}_{t}:=\mathds{1}_{[\tau^{0}(x),\infty)}(t)
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s., as
\lambda\to 0
.

Proof.

By the reflecting property of the strategy
\xi^{\lambda}
, the first time at which
\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}
increases is
\tau^{\lambda}(x,1)
, so that we have

\xi^{0}_{t}:=\mathds{1}_{[\tau^{0}(x),\infty)}(t)\leq\hat{\xi}^{\lambda}_{t}\leq\ \mathds{1}_{[\tau^{\lambda}(x,1),\infty)}(t).

Therefore, we conclude the proof by using Lemma
3.8
in order to take limits as
\lambda\to 0
in the latter inequalities.
∎

3.3.
Preliminary estimates

Before diving into the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we discuss some estimates that will be used several times in the sequel.

First, for a generic
q\geq 1
and
\hat{c}_{0}(q),\hat{c}_{1}(q),\hat{c}_{2}(q)
as in (
3.5
), we have the estimates

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{x}_{t}|^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{q})e^{\hat{c}_{0}(q)t},\quad q\geq 1,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}|^{q}]

\displaystyle\leq|\bar{x}-x|^{q}e^{\hat{c}_{1}(q)t},\quad q\geq 2,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\delta X^{\bar{x}}_{t}+(1-\delta)X^{x}_{t}-X^{\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x}_{t}\big|^{q}\big]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)|\bar{x}-x|^{2q}e^{\hat{c}_{2}(q)t},\quad q\geq 2,

for any
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \delta\in[0,1]
.
Even if these estimates are obtained through standard stochastic calculus techniques, it was difficult for us to find a reference in which the exact orders of growth
\hat{c}_{0},\hat{c}_{1},\hat{c}_{2}
appear (importantly, these constants are those appearing in our Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
).
For this reason, we provide a proof in Appendix
B
.

When
\rho
is large enough (as in Condition
4
of Assumption
3.1
),
the estimates above together with the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality imply that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq\tau}e^{-\rho t}|X^{x}_{t}|^{q}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{q}),\quad q=2(p-1),p,2(p-2),

(3.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq\tau}e^{-\rho t}|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}|^{q}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C|\bar{x}-x|^{q},\quad q=2,4

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq\tau}e^{-\rho t}\big|\delta X^{\bar{x}}_{t}+(1-\delta)X^{x}_{t}-X^{\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x}_{t}\big|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)|\bar{x}-x|^{4},

for any
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \delta\in[0,1]
and
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
.
Again, we provide a proof in Appendix
B
.

Finally, using the growth of
\pi
and
G
in Condition
2
of Assumption
3.1
, as well as the estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.13
), we find

(3.16)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}|\Pi(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})|{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}|G(X^{x}_{t})|{\rm d}\xi_{t}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}(1+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p}){\rm d}t+\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t\leq T}e^{-\rho t}(1+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p})\xi_{T}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|x|^{p}),

where we have used the fact that
\xi_{t}\leq y\leq 1
for any
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)
.

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
3.2
: solution to the HJB

The concavity of
V^{\lambda}
follows by straightforward arguments (see Remark
2.6
for a reference), while its growth rate was shown in (
3.16
).
Thus, we will only show in the sequel that
V^{\lambda}
has the claimed regularity and it solves the HJB equation
in the almost everywhere sense.
The argument is based on a penalization scheme (see e.g.
[
71
]
among others) and on a connection with optimal stopping (see e.g.
[
41
]
).

The proof is divided into five steps, where we will often use the notation
\Pi(x,y):=y\pi(x)-\lambda y\log y
.

Step 1 (Penalization).

Let
\beta:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a
C^{\infty}
nondecreasing convex function such that

\beta(z)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{ if }z\leq 0,\\
{2z-1},&\text{ if }z\geqslant 1.\end{cases}

For any
\varepsilon>0
, consider the control set
U_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{(a^{1},a^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,|\,sa^{1}-a^{2}\leq\frac{\beta(s)}{\varepsilon}\ \forall s\in\mathbb{R}
and
0\leq a^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big\}

and the functional

\displaystyle J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;(u,\eta))

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left(\Pi\left(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,u}_{t}\right)-G(X^{x}_{t})u_{t}-\eta_{t}\right){\rm d}t\right],\quad(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad{\rm d}X^{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=b(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sigma(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}W_{t},\quad X^{x}_{0}=x,

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y^{y,u}_{t}

\displaystyle=-u_{t}{\rm d}t,\quad Y^{y,u}_{0}=y,

where

\text{$\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y):=\{(u,\eta):\Omega\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow U_{\varepsilon}\,|\,Y^{y,u}_{t}\geqslant 0\ \forall t\geqslant 0,\mathbb{P}$-a.s. $\}$}.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, define the optimization problem

(3.17)

V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y):=\sup_{(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)}J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;(u,\eta)).

Similarly to (
3.16
), we can find
C
such that, for
y
small enough, one has

(3.18)

\displaystyle|V

\displaystyle{}^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\left|\Pi\left(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,u}_{t}\right)-G(X^{x}_{t})u_{t}-\eta_{t}\right|{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}y(|\pi(X^{x}_{t})|-\lambda\log y){\rm d}t+\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}|G(X_{t}^{x})|\big)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq Cy\bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}(1+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p}-\lambda\log y){\rm d}t+\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t\leq T}e^{-\rho t}(1+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p})\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq Cy(1+|x|^{p}-\lambda\log y)\to 0,\quad\text{as $y\to 0$},

which implies the continuity of
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
for
y=0
.

Standard arguments (see e.g. Chapter IV.10 in
[
26
]
) give that
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
is a solution to the HJB equation in the a.e. sense; that is,
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1])\cap W_{loc}^{2,2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1)\right)
and

(3.19)

\rho V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathcal{L}_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\Pi(x,y)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta\left(G(x)-V_{y}^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right)=0,\quad\text{a.e.\ in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1),$}

with boundary condition
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,0)=0
.

Moreover, following the rationale of the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
71
]
, one can show that

V^{\lambda}(x,y)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)
.

Step 2 (Uniform Lipschitz estimates for
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
in
x
).

Take
x,\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
y\in(0,1]
,
\zeta>0
and
(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)
such that
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)-\zeta\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y;u,\eta)
.
Since
(u,\eta)
is suboptimal for
(x,y)
, we have

(3.20)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)

\displaystyle-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y;u,\eta)-J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;u,\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(Y^{y,u}_{t}(\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-\pi(X_{t}^{x}))+u_{t}(G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x}))\big){\rm d}t\bigg].

In order to continue this estimate, we notice that by monotone convergence theorem, we have

(3.21)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u_{t}(G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})){\rm d}t\bigg]\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}u_{t}|G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})|{\rm d}t\bigg].

Furthermore, since
(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)
, we have
\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}{\rm d}s\leq y\leq 1
.
Thus, using Assumption
3.1
and Hölder inequality, we can estimate the right-hand side of (
3.21
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}u_{t}|G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})|{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}|G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})|\Big)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{p-1}+|X_{t}^{x}|^{p-1}\big)\big|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}+|X^{x}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}\big)\Big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}|^{2}\big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Finally, using the estimates (
3.13
) and (
3.14
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}u_{t}|G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})|{\rm d}t\bigg]\leq C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|,

which, thanks to (
3.21
), allows to conclude that

(3.22)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}u_{t}(G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{t}^{x})){\rm d}t\bigg]\leq C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|.

We next estimate the term in (
3.20
) which involves
\pi
.
Since
Y^{y,u}_{t}\leq y\leq 1
, using Assumption
3.1
and Hölder inequality we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}Y^{y,u}_{t}(\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-\pi(X_{t}^{x})){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{p-1}+|X_{t}^{x}|^{p-1}\big)\big|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(1+\big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X^{x}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}|^{2}\big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}t.

Finally, using the estimates in (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}Y^{y,u}_{t}(\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-\pi(X_{t}^{x})){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|\int_{0}^{T}e^{\big(-\rho+\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-1))}{2}+\frac{\hat{c}_{1}(2)}{2}\big)t}{\rm d}t.

Hence, thanks to Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
, we conclude that

(3.23)

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}Y^{y,u}_{t}(\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-\pi(X_{t}^{x})){\rm d}t\bigg]\leq C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|.

Plugging (
3.22
) and (
3.23
) into (
3.20
), we obtain

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta\leq C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|,

which, by the arbitrariness of
\zeta
, gives the local Lipschitz property of
V^{\varepsilon}
in
x
.

Step 3 (Uniform Lipschitz estimates for
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
in
y
).

Take
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
y,\bar{y}\in(0,1]
,
\zeta>0
and
(\bar{u},\bar{\eta})\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{y})
such that
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y})-\zeta\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y};\bar{u},\bar{\eta})
.
We will distinguish the two cases
\bar{y}\leq y
and
\bar{y}>y
.

If
\bar{y}\leq y
, then we have
Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\geq 0
, so that
(\bar{u},\bar{\eta})\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)
.
By suboptimality of
(\bar{u},\bar{\eta})
for the initial condition
(x,y)
, we have

(3.24)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y})

\displaystyle-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y};\bar{u},\bar{\eta})-J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;\bar{u},\bar{\eta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(\pi(X_{t}^{x})(\bar{y}-y)-\lambda(Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t})\big){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle=C(1+|x|^{p})|\bar{y}-y|+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(-\lambda(Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t})\big){\rm d}t\bigg],

where we have used Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
together with (
3.10
).
In order to continue the latter estimate, let
\hat{z}
be the maximum point of the function
z\mapsto-\lambda z\log z
(i.e.,
\hat{z}=e^{-1}
), and notice that
z\mapsto-\lambda z\log z
is increasing in
[0,\hat{z}]
and Lipschitz in
[\frac{\hat{z}}{2},1]
, with Lipschitz constant smaller than
C_{\lambda}:=1+|\log(\hat{z}/2)|
.
Suppose now that
y-\bar{y}\leq\frac{\hat{z}}{2}
.
If
Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\leq\frac{\hat{z}}{2}
, then
Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\leq\hat{z}
, and since
Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\leq Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}
, we obtain
-\lambda(Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t})\leq 0
by monotonicity.
On the other hand, if
Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\geq\frac{\hat{z}}{2}
, then
Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\geq\frac{\hat{z}}{2}
, and by Lipschitzianity we find

-\lambda(Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}\log Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t})\leq C_{\lambda}(Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t})=C_{\lambda}(y-\bar{y})
.
Going back to (
3.24
), this argument implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq y-\bar{y}\leq\frac{\hat{z}}{2}\implies V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y})

\displaystyle-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta\leq C(1+|x|^{p}+|\log(\hat{z}/2)|)|\bar{y}-y|,

which give the local Lipschitz property of
V^{\varepsilon}
in
y
in the case
\bar{y}\leq y
.

On the other hand, if
\bar{y}\geq y
, then the control
(\bar{u},\bar{\eta})
is not necessarily admissible for
y
, as
Y_{t}^{y,\bar{u}}
could become smaller than 0.
Define then
u_{t}:=\bar{u}_{t}\frac{y}{\bar{y}}
and
\eta_{t}:=\bar{\eta}_{t}\frac{y}{\bar{y}}
.
Notice that, if
s\bar{u}_{t}-\bar{\eta}_{t}\leq 0
, then
su_{t}-\eta_{t}=\frac{y}{\bar{y}}(s\bar{u}_{t}-\bar{\eta}_{t})\leq 0\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta(s)
, while
su_{t}-\eta_{t}=\frac{y}{\bar{y}}(s\bar{u}_{t}-\bar{\eta}_{t})\leq s\bar{u}_{t}-\bar{\eta}_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta(s)
if
s\bar{u}_{t}-\bar{\eta}_{t}>0
.
Thus, the process
(u,\eta)
takes values in the set
U_{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, since

(3.25)

Y^{y,u}_{t}=y-\int_{0}^{t}u_{s}{\rm d}s=\frac{y}{\bar{y}}\Big(\bar{y}-\int_{0}^{t}\bar{u}_{s}{\rm d}s\Big)=\frac{y}{\bar{y}}Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}\geq 0,

it follows that
(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)
.
Hence, by the suboptimality of
(u,\eta)
for the initial condition
(x,y)
, using (
3.25
), we find

(3.26)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(x,\bar{y})-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y};\bar{u},\bar{\eta})-J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;u,\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(-\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1+\log\big(rY^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}+(1-r)Y^{y,u}_{t}\big)\Big)(Y^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}_{t}-Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}){\rm d}r

\displaystyle+\pi(X_{t}^{x})(\bar{y}-y)\frac{Y_{t}^{\bar{y},\bar{u}}}{\bar{y}}+G(X_{t}^{x})\frac{\bar{y}-y}{\bar{y}}\bar{u}_{t}-\frac{\bar{y}-y}{\bar{y}}\bar{\eta}_{t}\Big){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(-\lambda\int_{0}^{1}\Big(1+\log(r\bar{y}+(1-r)y)+\log\big(\frac{Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}}{\bar{y}}\big)\Big)\frac{Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}}{\bar{y}}{(\bar{y}-y)}\Big){\rm d}r

\displaystyle+|\pi(X_{t}^{x})|(\bar{y}-y)+G(X_{t}^{x})\frac{\bar{y}-y}{\bar{y}}\bar{u}_{t}\Big){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq(\bar{y}-y)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\lambda\Big(1+|\log y|-\log\big(\frac{Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}}{\bar{y}}\big)\frac{Y^{y,\bar{u}}_{t}}{\bar{y}}\Big)+|\pi(X_{t}^{x})|+\frac{G(X_{t}^{x})}{\bar{y}}\bar{u}_{t}\Big){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C(\bar{y}-y)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\lambda(1+|\log y|)+|\pi(X_{t}^{x})|+\frac{G(X_{t}^{x})}{\bar{y}}\bar{u}_{t}\Big){\rm d}t\bigg].

Similarly to the arguments that lead to (
3.22
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\bar{u}_{t}G(X_{t}^{x}){\rm d}t\bigg]\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\bar{u}_{t}|G(X_{t}^{x})|{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}|G(X_{t}^{x})|\big)\int_{0}^{T}\bar{u}_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}(1+|X_{t}^{x}|^{p})\big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+|x|^{p}),

where we have used the estimate in (
3.13
).
Plugging the last inequality into (
3.26
), we obtain

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,\bar{y})-V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq C|\bar{y}-y|\bigg(\frac{1+|x|^{p}}{\bar{y}}+|\log y|+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X_{t}^{x}|^{p}\big){\rm d}t\bigg]\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C|\bar{y}-y|\Big(\frac{1+|x|^{p}}{\bar{y}}+|\log y|\Big)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\big(-\rho+\hat{c}_{0}(p)\big)t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C|\bar{y}-y|\Big(\frac{1+|x|^{p}}{\bar{y}}+|\log y|\Big),

where we have used the condition
\rho>\hat{c}_{0}(p)
given in Assumption
3.1
.
This shows the local Lipschitz property of
V^{\varepsilon}
in
y
for
\bar{y}\geq y
.

Step 4 (Uniform estimates for
D^{2}_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
).

We first show the following semiconvexity estimate: there exists a constant
C
such that

(3.27)

V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x^{\delta},y)-\delta V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)-(1-\delta)V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

for each
\delta\in[0,1]
,
\bar{x},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
y\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon>0
, and where

x^{\delta}:=\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x
.

Take an arbitrary
\zeta>0
and
(u,\eta)\in\mathcal{U}_{\varepsilon}(y)
such that
V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x^{\delta},y)-\zeta\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x^{\delta},y;u,\eta)
.
Notice that such a control is admissible also for the initial conditions
(\bar{x},y)
and
(x,y)
.
Since
(u,\eta)
is not necessarily optimal for
(x,y)
or
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(x^{\delta},y)-\delta V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)-(1-\delta)V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x^{\delta},y;u,\eta)-\delta J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y;u,\eta)-(1-\delta)J^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y;u,\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\Big(Y_{t}^{y,u}\big(\pi(X_{t}^{\delta})-\delta\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-(1-\delta)\pi(X_{t}^{x})\big)

\displaystyle+u_{t}\big(G(X_{t}^{\delta})-\delta G(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-(1-\delta)G(X_{t}^{x})\Big){\rm d}t\bigg].

Next, set
\hat{X}_{t}:=\delta X^{\bar{x}}_{t}+(1-\delta)X^{x}_{t}
,
X^{\delta}_{t}:=X^{x^{\delta}}_{t}
and define, for a generic function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the transformations

\displaystyle\Delta[\varphi]_{t}

\displaystyle:=|\varphi(X_{t}^{\delta})-\varphi(\hat{X}_{t})|,

\displaystyle\Gamma[\varphi]_{t}

\displaystyle:=|\varphi(\hat{X}_{t})-\delta\varphi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-(1-\delta)\varphi(X_{t}^{x})|.

Then, applying the monotone convergence theorem, we rewrite (
3.28
) as

(3.29)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle(x^{\delta},y)-\delta V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(\bar{x},y)-(1-\delta)V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(Y_{t}^{y,u}\Delta[\pi]_{t}+u_{t}\Delta[G]_{t}+Y_{t}^{y,u}\Gamma[\pi]_{t}+u_{t}\Gamma[G]_{t})\Big){\rm d}t\bigg].

We next estimate each term on the right-hand side of (
3.29
) separately.

First, using Condition
2
in Assumption
3.1
, Hölder inequality, and the SDE estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.12
), we find

(3.30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}Y_{t}^{y,u}\Delta[\pi]_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|\hat{X}_{t}|^{p-1}+|X_{t}^{\delta}|^{p-1}\big)\big|\hat{X}_{t}-X^{\delta}_{t}\big|{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(1+\big(\mathbb{E}\big[|\hat{X}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\big(\mathbb{E}\big[|X^{\delta}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}\big]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\Big(\mathbb{E}\big[|\hat{X}_{t}-X^{\delta}_{t}|^{2}\big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\big(-\rho+\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-1))}{2}+\frac{\hat{c}_{1}(2)}{2}\big)t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

where the last inequality follows by Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
.

Secondly, using Condition
2
in Assumption
3.1
, Hölder inequality, and the SDE estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.12
), we find

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}u_{t}\Delta[G]_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}\Delta[G]_{t}\big)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|\hat{X}_{t}|^{p-1}+|X_{t}^{\delta}|^{p-1}\big)\big|\hat{X}_{t}-X_{t}^{\delta}\big|\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|\hat{X}_{t}|^{2(p-1)}+|X_{t}^{\delta}|^{2(p-1)}\big)\Big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}|\hat{X}_{t}-X_{t}^{\delta}|^{2}\big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|.

where the last inequality follows from the estimates (
3.13
) and (
3.15
).

Thirdly, using Condition
2
in Assumption
3.1
, Hölder inequality, and the SDE estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we find

(3.32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}Y_{t}^{y,u}\Gamma[\pi]_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{p-2}+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p-2}\Big)|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|^{2}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(1+\big(\mathbb{E}[|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{2(p-2)}]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\big(\mathbb{E}[|X^{x}_{t}|^{2(p-2)}]\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\mathbb{E}[|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|^{4}]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}dt

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-2}+|\bar{x}|^{p-2})|\bar{x}-x|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\big(-\rho+\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-2))}{2}+\frac{\hat{c}_{1}(4)}{2}\big)t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-2}+|\bar{x}|^{p-2})|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

where the finiteness of the integrals follows again from Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
.

Fourthly, using Condition
2
in Assumption
3.1
, Hölder inequality, and the SDE estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we find

(3.33)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}u_{t}\Gamma[G]_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\big(e^{-\rho t}\Gamma[G]_{t}\big)\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}{\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{p-2}+|X^{x}_{t}|^{p-2}\big)|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|^{2}\Big)\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}\big(1+|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}|^{2(p-2)}+|X^{x}_{t}|^{2(p-2)}\big)\Big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[\sup_{t\leq T}\Big(e^{-\rho t}|X^{\bar{x}}_{t}-X^{x}_{t}\big|^{4}]\Big)\Big]\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-2}+|\bar{x}|^{p-2})|\bar{x}-x|^{2}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\big(-\rho+\frac{\hat{c}_{0}(2(p-2)}{2}+\frac{\hat{c}_{1}(4)}{2}\big)t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\delta(1-\delta)(1+|{x}|^{p-2}+|\bar{x}|^{p-2})|\bar{x}-x|^{2},

where we have used Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
.

Plugging (
3.30
), (
3.31
), (
3.32
) and (
3.33
) back into (
3.29
), we obtain (
3.27
).

We will now use (
3.27
) in the PDE (
3.19
) in order to obtain an estimate for the derivative
D_{x}^{2}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}
.
Notice that (
3.27
) implies that, for any
r>0
and
B_{r}(0):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,|\,|x|<r\}
, one can find a constant
C_{r}>0
such that

(3.34)

\partial^{2}_{zz}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\geq-C_{r},\quad\text{for any $x\in B_{r}(0)$, $z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with $|z|=1$, and $y\in[0,1]$.}

Choose an orthonormal basis
z^{1},...,z^{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\tr(\sigma\sigma^{*}(x)D_{x}^{2}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{\sigma}^{i}(x)\partial^{2}_{z^{i}z^{i}}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y),

where
c_{\sigma}^{1},...,c_{\sigma}^{n}
are the eigenvalues of the symmetric, nonnegative definite
matrix
\sigma\sigma^{*}(x)
.
Since
V^{\varepsilon}
solves the PDE in (
3.19
), using the latter identity together with (
3.34
) and the estimates in the previous steps, unless to take a larger constant
C_{r}
(independently from
\varepsilon
) we obtain

\displaystyle-C_{r}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{\sigma}^{i}(x)\partial^{2}_{z^{i}z^{i}}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-b(x)D_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\Pi(x,y)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta\left(G(x)-V_{y}^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-b(x)D_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)-\Pi(x,y)\leq C_{r},\quad\text{for any $(x,y)\in B_{r}(0)\times(0,1)$}.

This in turn implies that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta\left(G(x)-V_{y}^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right)\leq C_{r},\quad\text{for any $(x,y)\in B_{r}(0)\times(0,1)$},

so that, by using the uniform ellipticity of
\sigma
(
cf.
Condition
3
in Assumption
3.1
), by Theorem 9.11 p. 235 in
[
29
]
we conclude that, for any
q>0
, unless to enlarge again the constant
C_{r}
(independently from
\varepsilon
), for any
y\in(0,1)
we have

(3.35)

\int_{B_{r/2}(0)}|D_{x}^{2}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)|^{q}{\rm d}x\leq C_{r}\bigg(1+\int_{B_{r/2}(0)}\Big|\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta\left(G(x)-V_{y}^{\lambda,\varepsilon}(x,y)\right)\Big|^{q}{\rm d}x\bigg)\leq C_{r},

thus giving the desired estimate.

Step 5 (Lipschitzianity of
V^{\lambda}_{y}
via optimal stopping).

Following the arguments in
[
41
]
(see in particular Theorem 3.4) we can show that, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,1]
, one has the representation

V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)=\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\Pi_{y}\left(X^{x}_{t},y\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho{\tau}}G\left(X^{x}_{\tau}\right)\right],

that we will now employ to prove the local Lipschitzianity of
V^{\lambda}_{y}
.

We first show the Lipschitzianity in
y
.
Take
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\bar{y},y\in(0,1]
with
\bar{y}\leq y
.
By the monotonicity of
\Pi_{y}
in
y
we have
0\leq V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)
.
Moreover,
for
\zeta>0
and
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
such that
V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-\zeta\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\Pi_{y}(X_{t}^{x},\bar{y}){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho\tau}G(X^{x}_{\tau})\right]
, we find

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\left(\Pi_{y}\left(X_{t}^{x},\bar{y}\right)-\Pi_{y}\left(X_{t}^{x},y\right)\right){\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}(\log y-\log\bar{y}){\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}{\rm d}t\right]\frac{1}{\bar{y}}(y-\bar{y})\leqslant\lambda C\frac{1}{\bar{y}}(y-\bar{y}).

From the arbitrariness of
\zeta
, we conclude that

0\leqslant V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)\leqslant C\frac{1}{\bar{y}}(y-\bar{y})
, showing the Lipschitzianity in
y
.

We next show the Lipschitzianity in
x
.
For
\bar{x},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y\in(0,1]
, take
\zeta>0
and
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
such that
V^{\lambda}_{y}(\bar{x},y)-\zeta\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho t}\Pi_{y}(X_{t}^{\bar{x}},{y}){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho\tau}G(X^{\bar{x}}_{\tau})\right]
.
We observe that

(3.36)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{y}(\bar{x},y)

\displaystyle-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)-\zeta

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho{t}}\left(\Pi_{y}(X_{t}^{\bar{x}},y)-\Pi_{y}(X_{t}^{x},y)\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho\tau}\left(G(X_{\tau}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{\tau}^{x})\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{-\rho{t}}\left(\pi(X_{t}^{\bar{x}})-\pi(X_{t}^{x})\right){\rm d}t+e^{-\rho\tau}\left(G(X_{\tau}^{\bar{x}})-G(X_{\tau}^{x})\right)\bigg].

Thus, by repeating the arguments that lead to (
3.22
) and (
3.23
), we obtain

V^{\lambda}_{y}(\bar{x},y)-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)-\zeta\leq C(1+|x|^{p-1}+|\bar{x}|^{p-1})|\bar{x}-x|.

Hence, the local Lipschitzianity in
x
follows by the arbitrariness of
\zeta
and exchanging the role of
x
and
\bar{x}
.

Step 6 (Regularity of
V^{\lambda}
and variational inequality).

We begin by noticing that the continuity of
V^{\lambda}
in
y=0
can be shown as in (
3.18
).

By the estimates in Steps 2 and 3, for any
r,\delta>0
and any ball
B_{r}(0):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\,\,|x|<r\}
, we can find a subsequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
such that

(3.37)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda,\varepsilon_{n}}\to V^{\lambda}\text{ as $n\to\infty$, uniformly in $B_{r}(0)\times(\delta,1)$,}

\displaystyle(D_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon_{n}},V^{\lambda,\varepsilon_{n}}_{y})\to(D_{x}V^{\lambda},V^{\lambda}_{y})\text{ as $n\to\infty$, weakly in $\mathbb{L}^{2}(B_{r}(0)\times(\delta,1))$.}

Moreover, thanks to the estimate in Step 4, for any fixed
y\in(0,1]
we can select a further subsequence
(\varepsilon^{y}_{n})_{n}
such that

(3.38)

\displaystyle D_{x}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{y}_{n}}(\cdot,y)\to D_{x}V^{\lambda}(\cdot,y)\ \text{ as $n\to\infty$, uniformly in $B_{r}(0)$,}

\displaystyle D_{x}^{2}V^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{y}_{n}}(\cdot,y)\to D^{2}_{x}V^{\lambda}(\cdot,y)\ \text{ as $n\to\infty$, weakly in $\mathbb{L}^{2}(B_{r}(0))$.}

Therefore, taking limits in (
3.19
) we obtain that

\text{for any $y\in(0,1)$}\quad(\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V^{\lambda}}(\cdot,y)-\Pi(\cdot,y)\geq 0\quad\text{and}\quad V^{\lambda}_{y}(\cdot,y)-G\geqslant 0,\quad\text{${\rm d}x$-a.e.}

which yields that

(3.39)

\max\big\{\left(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho\right)V^{\lambda}(x,y)+\pi(x)y-\lambda y\log y,-V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)\big\}\leq 0.

To complete the proof, if
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is such that
V^{\lambda}_{y}(\bar{x},\bar{y})-G(\bar{x})>0
, by continuity of
V^{\lambda}_{y}
(
cf.
Step 5) we can find
r>0
such that
V^{\lambda}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-G(x)>0
for any
x\in B_{r}(0)
.
Thus, for a.a.
x\in B_{r}(0)
there exists
\bar{n}_{x}
such that
{V}^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{\bar{y}}_{n}}_{y}(x,\bar{y})-G(x)>0
for any
n\geq\bar{n}_{x}
, which gives
(\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V}^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{\bar{y}}_{n}}(x,\bar{y})-\Pi(x,\bar{y})=0
for any
n\geq\bar{n}_{x}
so that

\lim_{n}(\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V}^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{\bar{y}}_{n}}(\cdot,\bar{y})-\Pi(\cdot,\bar{y})=0,\quad\text{a.e.\ in $x\in B_{r}(0)$.}

Since
(\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V^{\lambda}}(\cdot,\bar{y})-\Pi(\cdot,\bar{y})
is the weak limit in
\mathbb{L}^{2}(B_{r}(\bar{x}))
of
\big((\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V}^{\lambda,\varepsilon^{\bar{y}}_{n}}(\cdot,\bar{y})-\Pi(\cdot,\bar{y})\big)_{n}
, the latter limits in turn implies that
(\rho-\mathcal{L}_{x}){V^{\lambda}}(\cdot,\bar{y})-\Pi(\cdot,\bar{y})=0
a.e. in
B_{r}(\bar{x})
, thus showing that (
3.39
) actually holds with equality and completing the proof.

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
3.2
: uniqueness for the HJB

Uniqueness of the solution follows from a verification argument, that we prove in two steps.

Step 1.

Let
\tilde{V}
be a solution of the HJB equation as in the statement of the theorem.
Fix
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]
and consider a generic
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)
.
For
R>0
, set
\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,|\,|X^{x}_{t}|\geq R\}
.
Thanks to the regularity of
\tilde{V}
, we can employ (a generalized) Itô’s formula to the process
\big(e^{-\rho t}\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t})\big)_{t}
(see
[
27
, Chapter 8, Section VIII.4, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
), up to the
stopping time
\tau_{R}\wedge T
, for some
T>0
, to obtain

(3.40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\rho\tau_{R}\wedge T}\tilde{V}(X_{\tau_{R}\wedge T},Y^{y,\xi}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T})\Big]=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(x,y)+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-}){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\,\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-}){\rm d}Y_{t}^{y,\xi}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{R}\wedge T}\Big(\Delta\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t})-\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})\Delta Y^{y,\xi}_{t}\Big)\bigg],

with the notations
\Delta\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t})=\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t})-\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})
and
\Delta Y^{y,\xi}_{t}=Y^{y,\xi}_{t}-Y^{y,\xi}_{t-}
.
The concavity of
\tilde{V}
in
y
now implies that

\sum_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{R}\wedge T}\Big(\Delta\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t})-\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})\Delta Y^{y,\xi}_{t}\Big)\leq 0.

Moreover, since
\tilde{V}
solves the HJB equation, we have
\mathbb{P}\otimes{\rm d}t
-a.e.

(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})\leq-\Pi(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})\quad\text{and}\quad-\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-})\leq-G(X^{x}_{t}).

Plugging the latter two inequalities into (
3.40
) and then rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Pi(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi}_{t-}){\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{V}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\rho\tau_{R}\wedge T}\tilde{V}(X_{\tau_{R}\wedge T},Y^{y,\xi}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T})\Big].

Finally, thanks to the growth conditions of
\pi
and
G
, to the growth of
\tilde{V}
, to the estimates (
3.10
) and (
3.13
),
we can use the dominated convergence theorem to take limits, first as
T\to\infty
and then as
R\to\infty
, in order to obtain

J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi)\leq\tilde{V}(x,y).

By arbitrariness of
\xi
, we deduce that
V^{\lambda}(x,y)\leq\tilde{V}(x,y)
.

Step 2.

We adapt the arguments in
[
15
]
to construct a control
\xi^{\lambda}
which satisfies

J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi^{\lambda})=\tilde{V}(x,y).

This identity, together with the previous step, implies that
V^{\lambda}=\tilde{V}
and that
\xi^{\lambda}
is optimal.

Define the function
\tilde{g}_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
by

\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(x):=\sup\big\{y\in[0,1]\,|\,-\tilde{V}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\big\},

where we set
\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(x):=1
if
\{y\in[0,1]\,|\,-\tilde{V}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\}=\emptyset
.
The function
\tilde{g}_{\lambda}
is well defined by concavity of
\tilde{V}
.
Noticing that
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\,|\,y<\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(x)\}=\{(x,y)\,|\,-\tilde{V}_{y}(x,y)+G(x)<0\}
, by continuity of
\tilde{V}_{y}
we deduce that the set
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]\,|\,y<\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(x)\}
is open, so that
\tilde{g}_{\lambda}
is lower semi-continuous.

Next, for fixed
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,1]
, define the process
\xi^{\lambda}
by setting

\xi^{\lambda}_{t}:=\sup_{s\leq t}\big(y-\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{s})\big)^{+},\quad t\geq 0.

Such a process is clearly monotone nondecreasing, so that it admits left limits.
To show its right-continuity, use the upper semi-continuity of
x\mapsto(y-\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(x))^{+}
to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{s\downarrow t}\xi^{\lambda}_{s}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\lor\lim_{s\downarrow t}\sup_{t\leq r\leq s}\big(y-\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{r})\big)^{+}

\displaystyle=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\lor\limsup_{s\downarrow t}\big(y-\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{s})\big)^{+}\leq\xi^{\lambda}_{t}\lor\big(y-\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{t})\big)^{+}=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}.

By monotonicity of
\xi^{\lambda}
, this implies that
\lim_{s\downarrow t}\xi^{\lambda}_{s}=\xi^{\lambda}_{t}
.
Thus, since this process is clearly adapted, it is progressively measurable, showing the admissibility of
\xi^{\lambda}
; i.e.,
\xi^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{A}(y)
.

We will now prove the optimality of
\xi^{\lambda}
by repeating, with some modifications, the arguments of Step 1.
For
Y^{y,\lambda}:=Y^{y,\xi^{\lambda}}
,
\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0\,|\,|X^{x}_{t}|\geq R\}
and for
T>0
we find

(3.41)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\rho\tau_{R}\wedge T}\tilde{V}(X_{\tau_{R}\wedge T},Y^{y,\lambda}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T})\Big]=

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(x,y)+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-}){\rm d}t\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\,\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-}){\rm d}Y_{t}^{y,\lambda}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{R}\wedge T}\Big(\Delta\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t})-\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})\Delta Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}\Big)\bigg],

with the notations
\Delta\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t})=\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t})-\tilde{V}(X_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})
and
\Delta Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}=Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}-Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-}
.

Notice that, by construction of
\xi^{\lambda}
, if the process
Y^{y,\lambda}
jumps at time
t
, then we have
\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{t})<\lim_{s\uparrow t}\tilde{g}_{\lambda}(X^{x}_{s})
and

\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},\zeta)=G(X^{x}_{t}),\quad\text{for any $\zeta\in[Y^{y,\lambda}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-}]$}.

Therefore, we obtain

\Delta\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t})-\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})\Delta Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}=G(X^{x}_{t})(Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}-Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})-G(X^{x}_{t})(Y^{y,\lambda}_{t}-Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})=0.

Using the latter equality in (
3.41
), together with the fact that the process
\xi^{\lambda}
increases only at times
t
in which
\tilde{V}_{y}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})=G(X^{x}_{t})
and the fact that
(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)\tilde{V}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})=\Pi(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-})\ \mathbb{P}\otimes{\rm d}t
-a.e., we find

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(x,y)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Pi(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\lambda}_{t-}){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}^{\lambda}\bigg]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\rho\tau_{R}\wedge T}\tilde{V}(X_{\tau_{R}\wedge T},Y^{y,\lambda}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T})\Big].

Taking limits (first as
T\to\infty
and then as
R\to\infty
) we conclude that

\tilde{V}(x,y)=J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi^{\lambda}),

thus proving the optimality of
\xi^{\lambda}
.
This completes the proof.

.

4.
General algorithm

In this section, we propose an RL framework to learn the reflection boundary
g_{\lambda}
(namely the policy) and the corresponding value function
V^{\lambda}
, without prior knowledge on the form of
g_{\lambda}
. Our proposed RL framework has two versions: a model-based numerical version (see Section
4.1
) and a model-free learning version (see Section
4.2
). In the first version, where all model parameters are known, we design a Policy Iteration algorithm to numerically find
g_{\lambda}
. In the second version, where the environment and model parameters are unknown, we provide a sample-based Actor-critic algorithm to learn
g_{\lambda}
, especially in high dimensions.

In line with the RL literature, from now on the value function
V^{\lambda}
will be referred to as
optimal value function
, while the profit
J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi)
of a given policy
\xi\in\mathcal{A}(y)
will be referred to as
value function
associated to the policy
\xi
.

4.1.
Model-based numerical schemes

In this subsection, we present a numerical scheme to solve for the optimal boundary, utilizing full knowledge of the underlying system. As such, this method is termed a model-based analysis.

Motivated by the results in the previous section, here we focus on a subclass of control policies that can be fully characterized by some reflection boundary
g\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For a function
g\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define the associated policy
\xi^{g}
of the following form:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\xi^{g}_{t}=\sup_{s\leq t}\big(y-g(X_{s}^{x})\big)^{+},t\geq 0.

Define the associated value function as:

(4.2)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle J^{\lambda}(x,y;\xi^{g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}\big((\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}}-\lambda Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}}\log(Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}})\big){\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}G(X^{x}_{t}){\rm d}\xi_{t}^{g}\right]

subject to
Y^{y,\xi^{g}}_{t}=y-\xi_{t}^{g}
, with
\xi_{t}^{g}
defined in (
4.1
).

The policy in (
4.1
) defines two areas: the exploration area and the stopping area, both associated with
g
:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(g)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Big\{(x,y)\,\,\Big|\,\,y\leq g(x)\Big\},

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(g)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Big\{(x,y)\,\,\Big|\,\,y>g(x)\Big\}.

In order to show that the updated policy performs better that the original one, we need the following technical condition, which can be easily verified in examples of dimension one (see our companion paper
[
21
]
).

Assumption 4.1
.

Assume the following boundary value problem has a unique
{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])\cap{C}^{2}\big(\overline{\mathcal{E}(g)}\big)
solution:

(4.5)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{x}-\rho)u+\pi(x)\,y-\lambda y\log y=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{E}(g),

(4.6)

\displaystyle-u_{y}+G(x)=0,\quad\text{on}\quad\mathcal{S}(g).

In addition, the solution satisfies
\mathcal{L}_{x}u\in\mathbb{L}_{loc}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1])
.

Under Assumption
4.1
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)\equiv u(x,y).

With the results in Theorem
4.1
, we update the new boundary as it follows:

(4.8)

\widetilde{g}(x):=\begin{cases}&\max\Big\{0\leq y<g(x)\,\Big|\partial^{-}_{yy}V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)=0\Big\}\,\,\text{if}\,\,\partial^{-}_{yy}V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,g(x))>0,\text{ and }\\
&\widetilde{g}(x)=g(x)\qquad\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where, for a generic function
h
with left and right second order derivatives, we denote by
\partial_{yy}^{-}h(y):=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0-}\frac{h(y+\varepsilon)-2h(y)+h(y-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}
the left second-order derivative of
h
. The idea behind the updating rule is to preserve the concavity of the value function
V^{\lambda}_{g}
in
y
, while eliminating regions where the second derivative becomes positive. This differs from
[
21
]
, where the updating rule is designed based on the cross derivative
V_{xy}
which is natural when
x
is one-dimensional. In the high-dimensional regime, however, it is more natural to consider the second-order derivative with respect to
y
. Both updating rules lead to policy improvement results.

We can show that the updating rule (
4.8
) always improves in terms of the value function.

Theorem 4.2

(Policy improvement)
.

Assume Assumption
4.1
hold and
\widetilde{g}
is updated according to (
4.8
).
Then, it holds that

(4.9)

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{\widetilde{g}}(x,y)\geq V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y),

where
V^{\lambda}_{g},V^{\lambda}_{\widetilde{g}}
are value functions associated with policies
\xi^{g},\xi^{\widetilde{g}}
; see (
4.1
).

Proof.

First of all,
V^{\lambda}_{g}\in{C}^{2}\left(\overline{\mathcal{E}(g)}\right)
according to Assumption
4.1
. Denote
Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}
as the process under control
\xi^{\widetilde{g}}
, with
\widetilde{g}
defined in (
4.8
). Fix the initial condition
(x,y)
and take
R>0
. Set
\tau_{R}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\|X^{x}_{t}\|\geq R\}
. Apply Ito’s formula in the weak version to
V^{\lambda}_{g}
(see
[
27
, Chapter 8, Section VIII.4, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
16
, Theorem 4.2]
) up to the
stopping time
\tau_{R}\wedge T
for some
T>0
. We then obtain

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{-\rho(\tau_{R}\wedge T)}V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{\tau_{R}\wedge T})\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)+\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\mathcal{L}_{x}V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-})-\rho V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-})\Big){\rm d}t\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\,\partial_{y}V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-}){\rm d}Y_{t}^{\widetilde{g}}\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\Delta V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t})-\partial_{y}V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-})\Delta Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}-\lambda Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}\log\Big(Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}\Big)\Big){\rm d}t\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\,\partial_{y}V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-}){\rm d}(Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}})_{t}^{\rm cont}\Big]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{0\leq t\leq{\tau_{R}\wedge T}}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\Delta V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-})\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)-\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}-\lambda Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}\log\Big(Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}\Big)\Big){\rm d}t

\displaystyle+{\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}G(X_{t}^{x}){\rm d}\xi^{\tilde{g}}_{t}}\Big],

with the notations
\Delta V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t})=V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t})-V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}_{t-})
and
\Delta Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}=Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}-Y_{t-}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}}
.
The second equality holds because

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\mathcal{L}_{x}\,u(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{g}}_{t-})-\rho\,V^{\lambda}_{g}(X^{x}_{t},Y^{y,\xi^{g}}_{t-})\Big){\rm d}t\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{\tau_{R}\wedge T}e^{-\rho t}\Big(\pi(X^{x}_{t})Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}}-\lambda Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}}\log\Big(Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{g}}\Big)\Big){\rm d}t\Big],

as pure jumps could only possibly happen at time
0
,
\xi^{\widetilde{g}}
keeps
(X^{x}_{t},Y_{t}^{y,\xi^{\widetilde{g}}})
within
\overline{\mathcal{E}(g)}
for
t>0
and (
4.5
).
The last inequality holds by the design of (
4.8
).
In particular, for
(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{g})\cap\mathcal{E}(g)\subseteq\mathcal{E}(g)
, by mean value theorem,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,y)=\partial_{y}V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,g(x))-\partial_{yy}V^{\lambda}_{g}(x,\tilde{y})\leq G(x),

with
\tilde{y}\in[y,g(x)]
, where the last inequality holds due to the design of (
4.8
).

In addition, on
\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{g})\cap\mathcal{E}(g)
,

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+|\Delta_{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{*}\left(b\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)-b\left(X_{t}^{x}\right)\right)+\frac{q-1}{2}\left|\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{x}\right)\right|^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon+|\bar{x}-x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Delta_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Delta_{t}^{*}\left(\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{x}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\left(L+\frac{q-1}{2}L^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+|\Delta_{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}{\rm d}t.

Taking expectations and using Grönwall inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varepsilon+|\Delta_{t}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\leq\left(\varepsilon+|\bar{x}-x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}e^{q\left(L+\frac{q-1}{2}L^{2}\right)t},

which completes the proof sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Proof of (
3.12
).

Set

\displaystyle\hat{X}_{t}:=\delta X_{t}^{\bar{x}}+(1-\delta)X_{t}^{x},\quad X_{t}^{\delta}:=X_{t}^{\delta\bar{x}+(1-\delta)x},\quad\Gamma_{t}:=\hat{X}_{t}-X_{t}^{\delta},

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{t}:=\delta b\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)+(1-\delta)b\left(X_{t}^{x}\right),\quad\hat{\sigma}_{t}:=\delta\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)+(1-\delta)\sigma\left(X_{t}^{x}\right).

By Itô’s formula, with elementary estimates we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}=\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left[\left(\hat{b}_{t}-b\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}t+\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}\right]

\displaystyle+q\frac{q-2}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-4}{2}}\left|\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right)\right|^{2}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{q}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{q-2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left|\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right|^{2}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left[\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{b}_{t}-b\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right)+\frac{q-1}{2}\left|\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right|^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}.

Using the properties of
b
and
\sigma
in Condition
1
in Assumption
3.1
, we continue with

\displaystyle\Big(\varepsilon+

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left[\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(b\left(\hat{X}_{t}\right)-b\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right)+(q-1)\left|\sigma\left(\hat{X}_{t}\right)-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right|^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left[\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{b}_{t}-b\left(\hat{X}_{t}\right)\right)+(q-1)\left|\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(\hat{X}_{t}\right)\right|^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left(L+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Big(\left|\Gamma_{t}^{*}\right|\delta(1-\delta)L\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+(q-1)\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}L^{2}\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{4}\Big){\rm d}t.

Next, since
y\bar{y}\leq\frac{1}{2}(y^{2}+\bar{y}^{2})
, after rearranging some of the terms we arrive at the expression

\displaystyle\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\left(L+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\bigg(\frac{L}{2}\left(\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}+\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{4}\right)

\displaystyle+(q-1)L^{2}\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{4}\bigg){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\bigg[\left(\frac{3}{2}L+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\left(\frac{L}{2}+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{4}\bigg]{\rm d}t.

Next, using Young inequality with exponent
\frac{q}{q-2}
and its conjugate
\frac{q}{2}
, we obtain

(B.2)

\displaystyle\Big(\varepsilon+

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+q\left(\frac{3}{2}L+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}q\left(\frac{L}{2}+(q-1)L^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{q-2}{q}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}+\frac{2}{q}\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2q}\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Gamma_{t}^{*}\left(\hat{\sigma}_{t}-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\delta}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+\left((2q-1)L+2(q-1)^{2}L^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\left(L+2(q-1)L^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left|X_{t}^{\bar{x}}-X_{t}\right|^{2q}{\rm d}t.

Taking expectations and using Grönwall, (
3.11
) implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Gamma_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\leq\left(\varepsilon^{\frac{q}{2}}+C\delta^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}|\bar{x}-x|^{2q}e^{2q\left(L+\frac{2q-1}{2}L^{2}\right)t}\right)e^{\left((2q-1)L+2(q-1)^{2}L^{2}\right)t},

which in turn gives (
3.12
) by sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
.

Proof of (
3.13
).

First, by Itô’s formula we have

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}

\displaystyle\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho s}\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}(X_{t}^{x})^{*}\sigma(X_{t}^{x}){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\bigg[-\rho+q\bigg(\frac{\left(X_{t}^{x}\right)^{*}}{1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}}b\left(X_{s}^{x}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{q}{2}-1\right)\frac{|(X_{t}^{x})^{*}\sigma(X_{t}^{x})|^{2}}{\left(1+|X_{t}^{x}|^{2}\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\sigma(X_{t}^{x})|^{2}}{1+|X_{t}^{x}|^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+|x|^{2})^{\frac{q}{2}}+q\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\left|X^{x}_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}(X^{x}_{t})^{*}\sigma\left(X^{x}_{t}\right){\rm d}W_{t}

\displaystyle+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\left|X^{x}_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}{\rm d}t.

Next, for
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
and
T>0
, Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\leq\tau\land T}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\left(1+|x|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}+C\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+C\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{-2\rho t}\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{q-2}\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}|\sigma(X_{t}^{x})|^{2}{\rm d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

Thus, using (
3.10
) and Jensen inequality we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\leq\tau\land T}e^{-\rho t}\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C\left(1+|x|^{q}\right)+C\left(1+|x|^{q}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\left(-\rho+\hat{c}_{0}(q)\right)t}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+C\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2\rho t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1+\left|X_{t}^{x}\right|^{2}\right)^{q}\right]{\rm d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|x|^{q}\right)+C\left(1+|x|^{q}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(-2\rho+\hat{c}_{0}(2q))t}{\rm d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|x|^{q}\right),

where the finiteness of the integrals follows from the conditions
\rho>\hat{c}_{0}(q)
and
2\rho>\hat{c}_{0}(2q)
and the particular choice of
q
in (
3.13
) (which are part of Condition
4
in Assumption
3.1
).
The estimate (
3.13
) follows taking limits as
T\to\infty
and using the dominated convergence theorem.

\displaystyle+q\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho t}\left(\varepsilon+\left|\Delta_{t}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\Delta_{t}^{*}\left(\sigma\left(X_{t}^{\bar{x}}\right)-\sigma\left(X_{t}^{x}\right)\right){\rm d}W_{t}.
