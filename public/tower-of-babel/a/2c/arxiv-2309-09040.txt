Moving Frames: Difference and Differential-Difference Lagrangians This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Symmetry, Invariants, and their Applications in honor of Peter J. Olver. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Olver.html
Lewis C. WHITE and Peter E. HYDON

Abstract
This paper develops moving frame theory for partial difference equations and for differential-difference equations with one continuous independent variable. In each case, the theory is applied to the invariant calculus of variations and the equivariant formulation of the conservation laws arising from Noether’s theorem. The differential-difference theory is not merely an amalgam of the differential and difference theories, but has additional features that reflect the need for the group action to preserve the prolongation structure. Projectable moving frames are introduced; these cause the invariant derivative operator to commute with shifts in the discrete variables. Examples include a Toda-type equation and a method of lines semi-discretization of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation.

Introduction

The modern formulation of moving frames introduced by Fels and Olver
[
2
,
3
]
is a powerful tool in the analysis of partial differential equations (PDEs). It enables one to reduce a given system of PDEs to an invariant system by factoring out Lie symmetry group orbits (locally, at least).
If the symmetries are extraneous to the problem of interest (for instance, projective symmetries in computer vision
[
20
]
), the moving frame provides a major simplification. For a clear, straightforward introduction to moving frames, see Mansfield’s text
[
12
]
.

Many PDE systems of interest are Euler–Lagrange equations with a Lie group of variational symmetries. For such systems, moving frames are most effectively applied by invariantizing the Lagrangian functional directly
[
10
]
. With this approach, Noether’s theorem has an elegant formulation in terms of the adjoint action of the Lie group on a set of invariants
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

Difference equations have discrete independent variables, so any Lie symmetries act only on the dependent variables. Commonly, the action varies with the discrete variables. Moving frames can be adapted to an equation on a finite set of points by using a finite-dimensional product space. Finite difference approximation of a given differential equation requires consistency as the points coalesce. This constraint led to the introduction of multi-space
[
19
]
, which has been used to construct (highly accurate) invariant approximations of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
[
9
]
. More generally, a discrete moving frame attaches a finite-dimensional product space to each base point, without imposing coalescence or any other structure in advance
[
14
]
. The discrete moving frame construction works for any number of independent variables and has been used to generalize multi-space to higher dimensions
[
17
]
.

Difference equations have an intrinsic structure that arises from their mesh point labels. We restrict attention to the most common case, an
m
-dimensional logically rectangular mesh. The labels
n^{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\ i=1,\dots,m
, can be regarded as the independent variables. Each label belongs to an ordered set, so it is helpful to incorporate this ordering into the moving frame definition. This has been achieved for ordinary difference equations (O
\Delta
Es), resulting in the invariant variational calculus and equivariant Noether’s theorem
[
15
,
16
]
. The current paper extends this approach to partial difference equations (P
\Delta
Es) and to differential-difference equations (D
\Delta
Es) with one continuous independent variable.

Section
2
summarizes the building-blocks of the P
\Delta
E theory, from which we develop difference moving frames (see Section
3
), the invariant calculus of variations (see Section
4
), and the equivariant formulation of Noether’s conservation laws (see Section
5
). Even for scalar P
\Delta
Es, it is necessary to use several generating invariants and to take the relations between these into account. The more dependent and independent variables there are, the more complex these relations can become. For clarity, we illustrate the general theory with fairly straightforward examples. In particular, we use a Toda-type equation as a running example to show the various aspects of the theory.

There is one aspect of the P
\Delta
E theory that is simpler than its counterpart for PDEs: the Lie group action on the independent variables is trivial. This is not necessarily the case for D
\Delta
Es, although there are constraints on the group action, as discussed in Section
6
. These constraints suggest the idea of a projectable moving frame, which is a major simplification. Section
7
outlines the invariant variational calculus and Noether conservation laws for D
\Delta
Es, emphasizing those aspects of the theory that do not follow immediately from the P
\Delta
E theory. Section
8
presents some examples, including a method of lines semi-discretization of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. Concluding remarks are given in Section
9
.

2.2
The difference variational calculus

This section summarizes the difference variational calculus from the formal viewpoint introduced by Kupershmidt
[
11
]
. It closely resembles the formal differential variational calculus described in
[
18
]
. Summation by parts replaces integration by parts, and a difference version of the divergence is used, which we now describe.

Each shift operator
\mathrm{S}_{{\bf J}}
, where
\mathbf{J}=\bigl(j^{1},\dots,j^{m}\bigr)
, may be written as a product of unit shift operators,
\mathrm{S}_{i}:=\mathrm{S}_{{\bf 1}_{i}}
, and their inverses. Here
{\bf 1}_{i}
is the multi-index whose only non-zero entry is the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
one, which is
1
. Thus
\mathrm{S}_{i}
is the forward shift in the
n^{i}
-direction. By the composition rule for translations,
\mathrm{S}_{i}\mathrm{S}_{j}=\mathrm{S}_{j}\mathrm{S}_{i}
and
\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}=\mathrm{S}_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\mathrm{S}_{m}^{j_{m}}
; consequently,
(\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}})^{-1}=\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{J}}
. The identity operator,
\mathrm{id}:=\mathrm{S}_{\bf 0}
, maps every function to itself, and the forward difference operator in the direction
n^{k}
is

\mathrm{D}_{n^{k}}=\mathrm{S}_{k}-\mathrm{id}
.
A
difference divergence
is an expression of the form
\operatorname{Div}(F)=\mathrm{D}_{n^{k}}F^{k}
for some
F:=\bigl(F^{1},\dots,F^{m}\bigr)
. It is straightforward to write a given expression of the form
(\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}}-\mathrm{id})f
, where
f
is a function, as a difference divergence; however, the resulting functions
F^{k}
are not unique if
m>1
and may be messy.

Definition 2.2
.

A
conservation law
of a given system of P
\Delta
Es is a difference divergence expression,
\mathcal{C}=\operatorname{Div}(F)
, such that
\mathcal{C}=0
on all solutions of the system.

A linear difference operator on
P_{{\bf{n}}}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
is an operator of the form
\mathcal{H}=h^{\mathbf{J}}\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}}
, where each
h^{\mathbf{J}}
is a function. The formal adjoint of
\mathcal{H}
is the operator
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
defined by

f\mathcal{H}g-\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}f\bigr)g\in\mathrm{im(Div)}

for all functions
f
,
g
. Explicitly,
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}f=\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{J}}\bigl(h^{\mathbf{J}}f\bigr)
, because

fh^{\mathbf{J}}\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}}g-\bigl(\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{J}}\bigl(h^{\mathbf{J}}f\bigr)\bigr)g=(\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}}-\mathrm{id})\bigl\{\bigl(\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{J}}\bigl(h^{\mathbf{J}}f\bigr)\bigr)g\bigr\}.

A special case is the very useful
summation by parts
formula

f(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}g)=(\mathrm{S}_{-\bf J}f)g+(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}-\mathrm{id})\{(\mathrm{S}_{-\bf J}f)g\}.

The basic variational problem is to find the extrema of a given functional

\mathcal{L}[{\bf u}]=\sum_{\bf n}\mathrm{L}({\bf n},[{\bf u}]),

where
[{\bf u}]
represents finitely many shifts of the dependent variables.
Extrema are found by requiring that

\bigg\{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\mathcal{L}[{\bf u}+\epsilon{\bf w}]\bigg\}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}=0,

for all
{\bf w}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q}
that vanish sufficiently rapidly as any independent variable approaches infinity.
Using summation by parts,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon=0}\mathrm{L}(\mathbf{n},[{\bf u}+\epsilon{\bf w}])=\left({\mathrm{S}}_{{\bf J}}w^{\alpha}\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u_{{\bf J}}^{\alpha}}\right)=w^{\alpha}\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})+\operatorname{Div}(A_{\bf u}({\bf n},[\mathbf{u}],[{\bf w}])),

where

\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}=\mathrm{S}_{-\bf J}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\bf J}^{\alpha}}

is the difference Euler–Lagrange operator with respect to
u^{\alpha}
, and

\operatorname{Div}(A_{\bf u}({\bf n},[\mathbf{u}],[{\bf w}]))=\sum_{\bf J}(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}-\mathrm{id})\left(w^{\alpha}{\mathrm{S}}_{-{\bf J}}\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u_{{\bf J}}^{\alpha}}\right).

As
\mathbf{w}
is arbitrary and the sum of
\operatorname{Div}(A_{\bf u}({\bf n},[\mathbf{u}],[{\bf w}]))
over
\mathbf{n}
is zero (by Stokes’ theorem), the extrema satisfy the following system of Euler–Lagrange (difference) equations,

\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})={\mathrm{S}}_{{-\bf J}}\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u_{{\bf J}}^{\alpha}}\right)=0.

Remark 2.3
.

If the dependent variables are regarded as depending smoothly on a continuous parameter
t
as well as
\mathbf{n}
, the same result is achieved by using

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_{(u^{\alpha})^{\prime}=w^{\alpha}}\mathrm{L}[{\bf u}]=0,

where
(u^{\alpha})^{\prime}=\mathrm{d}u^{\alpha}/\mathrm{d}t
.
This approach is used later to derive the Lie group invariant version of the Euler–Lagrange equations.

2.3
Variational point symmetries and Noether’s theorem

We now outline some relevant facts about Lie point symmetries, with application to variational calculus (see
[
7
,
18
]
for further details). Let
G
be an
R
-dimensional Lie group parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\bigl(\varepsilon^{1},\dots,\varepsilon^{R}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{R}
in some neighbourhood of the identity,
e

G

\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})

\Gamma
depends smoothly on
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, with
\Gamma({\bf 0})=e
.
Locally, the left action of
G

{\bf u}=\bigl(u^{1},\dots,u^{q}\bigr)
is denoted by
\widehat{\bf u}=\Gamma(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\cdot{\bf u}
. The
R

\mathcal{X}
of infinitesimal generators has a basis

{\bf v}_{r}=Q_{r}^{\alpha}({\bf n},{\bf u})\partial_{u^{\alpha}},\qquad r=1,\dots,R,\qquad\text{where}\quad Q_{r}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial\widehat{u}^{\alpha}}{\partial\varepsilon^{r}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}={\bf 0}},

so every infinitesimal generator of a one-parameter (local) Lie subgroup of point transformations is of the form
{\bf v}=Q^{\alpha}({\bf n},{\bf u})\partial_{u^{\alpha}}
,
where
Q^{\alpha}=c^{r}Q_{r}^{\alpha}
for some real constants
c^{r}
.
The
q
-tuple
\mathbf{Q}=\bigl(Q^{1},\dots,Q^{R}\bigr)
is the
characteristic
of the Lie subgroup whose infinitesimal generator is
\mathbf{v}
.

Each infinitesimal generator is a tangent vector field on the total space. It is represented on the prolongation space
P_{\bf n}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
by
the prolonged vector field

\operatorname{pr}{\bf v}=(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}Q^{\alpha})\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{\bf J}^{\alpha}}.

From here on, we refer to
\operatorname{pr}{\bf v}
simply as
{\bf v}
, because it will always be clear whether the generator is acting on the total space or the prolongation space over
\mathbf{n}
. Note that
\mathbf{n}
is invariant under the Lie group action, as are the shift operators
\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{J}}
.

Denote the left action of a general group element
g\in G
(not necessarily in the neighbourhood of the identity) on the total space by
\widetilde{\bf u}=g\cdot{\bf u}
and define

\widetilde{\bf v}_{r}=Q_{r}^{\alpha}({\bf n},\widetilde{\bf u})\partial_{\widetilde{u}^{\alpha}},\qquad r=1,\dots,R.

The adjoint representation of
g
on
\mathcal{X}
can be expressed as a matrix,
\mathcal{A}{\rm d}(g)=(a_{r}^{s}(g))
, whose components are determined from the following relations (see
[
15
]
):

{\bf v}_{r}=a_{r}^{s}(g)\widetilde{\bf v}_{s},\qquad r=1,\dots,R.

(2.1)

By regarding the infinitesimal generators as differential operators and applying the left-hand side of the identity (
2.1
) to each
\widetilde{u}^{\alpha}
in turn, one obtains

\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}^{\alpha}}{\partial u^{\beta}}\right)Q_{r}^{\beta}({\bf n},{\bf u})={\bf v}_{r}(\widetilde{u}^{\alpha})=Q_{s}^{\alpha}({\bf n},\widetilde{\bf u})a_{r}^{s}(g),

(2.2)

where the matrix
\bigl(\partial\widetilde{u}^{\alpha}/\partial u^{\beta}\bigr)
is the Jacobian matrix of the transformation
g\colon\mathbf{u}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}
.
Prolonging this result to each
u_{\bf J}^{\alpha}
gives the identities

\left(\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}_{\bf J}^{\alpha}}{\partial u_{\bf J}^{\beta}}\right)Q_{r}^{\beta}({\bf n+J},{\bf u_{J}})=Q_{s}^{\alpha}({\bf n}+{\bf J},\widetilde{\bf u}_{\mathbf{J}})a_{r}^{s}(g).

Definition 2.4
.

The point transformations generated by
\mathbf{v}
are
variational symmetries
of the Lagrangian
\mathrm{L}(\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])
if there exist functions
B^{i}({\bf n},[{\bf u}])
such that

\mathbf{v}(\mathrm{L}):=(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}Q^{\alpha})\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u_{\bf J}^{\alpha}}=\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}B^{i}.

(2.3)

The Lagrangian is invariant under the symmetries generated by
\mathbf{v}
if
B^{i}=0
for all
i
.
Summing (
2.3
) by parts leads to Noether’s theorem for P
\Delta
Es (see
[
7
]
).

Theorem 2.5

(difference Noether’s theorem)
.

Suppose that a Lagrangian
\mathrm{L}
has a variational symmetry with characteristic
\bf{Q}\neq{\bf 0}
. Then the system of Euler–Lagrange equations has the following conservation law:

-\mathrm{D}_{n^{i}}B^{i}+\sum_{\bf J}(\mathrm{S}_{\bf J}-\mathrm{id})\left(Q^{\alpha}{\mathrm{S}}_{-{\bf J}}\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u_{{\bf J}}^{\alpha}}\right)=0.

(2.4)

From here on, we restrict attention to Lie groups of variational point symmetries that leave the Lagrangian invariant, so
B^{i}=0,\ i=1,\dots,m
.

Definition 2.6

(moving frame)
.

Given a smooth Lie group action
G\times M\rightarrow M
, a moving frame is a smooth equivariant map
\rho\colon\mathcal{U}\subset M\rightarrow G
. Here
\mathcal{U}
is called the domain of the frame.

Given a left action,
g\cdot z
, a left equivariant map satisfies
\rho(g\cdot z)=g\rho(z)
and a right equivariant map satisfies
\rho(g\cdot z)=\rho(z)g^{-1}
.
The frame is called left or right accordingly.
The inverse of a right frame is a left frame.

To find a right frame for an
R
-dimensional Lie group,
G
, write the cross-section
\mathcal{K}
as a system of equations
\psi_{r}(z)=0
,
r=1,\dots,R
.
Then solve the
normalization equations
,

\psi_{r}(g\cdot z)=0,\qquad r=1,\dots,R\text{,}

(2.5)

to obtain the unique group element
g=\rho(z)
that maps
z
to the intersection of its group orbit with
\mathcal{K}
(see Figure

Both
\rho(g\cdot z)
and
\rho(z)g^{-1}
satisfy the equation
\psi_{r}(\rho(g\cdot z)\cdot(g\cdot z))=0
,
so by uniqueness, the solution is a right frame.

An important part of the method of moving frames is to choose a cross-section
\mathcal{K}
that makes computations as simple as possible. It is usually easiest to choose a cross-section on which
R
of the coordinates on
\mathcal{U}
are constant. The normalization equations are then expressed as

g\cdot z_{1}=c_{1},\qquad g\cdot z_{2}=c_{2},\qquad\dots,\qquad g\cdot z_{R}=c_{R},

where
z_{r}
are coordinates and

are fixed constants. We will use this approach in our examples.

Given a left action
G\times M\rightarrow M
and a right frame
\rho

\iota(z)=\rho(z)\cdot z=g\cdot z|_{g=\rho(z)}.

The components of
\iota(z)
are invariant under the Lie group action, because

\iota(g\cdot z)=\rho(g\cdot z)\cdot(g\cdot z)=\rho(z)\cdot g^{-1}g\cdot z=\rho(z)\cdot z=\iota(z).

The non-constant components of
\iota(z)
are called the
normalized invariants
. Note that
\rho(\rho(z)\cdot z)
is the identity element of
G

\iota(\iota(z))=\iota(z)
. In other words, the operator
\iota
projects
z

\iota(f(z))=f(\iota(z))
.
Note that
\iota(\iota(f(z)))=\iota(f(z))
, so
\iota
projects out the invariant component of

; hence,
\iota
is called the
invariantization operator
. Indeed, if

is any invariant, the following
replacement rule
applies:

F(z)=F(\iota(z)).

(2.6)

Consequently, the set of all invariants is generated by the normalized invariants
\iota(z)
.

2.5
Discrete moving frames

The discrete moving frame developed by Mansfield, Marí Beffa and Wang
[
14
]
and Marí Beffa and Mansfield
[
17
]
can be thought of as a moving frame adapted to discrete base points.
The Lie group action on
M
is extended to the diagonal (left) action on the Cartesian product manifold
\mathcal{M}=M^{N}
:

g\cdot(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{N})\mapsto(g\cdot z_{1},g\cdot z_{2},\dots,g\cdot z_{N}).

No assumptions are made about any relationship between the elements
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}
.

Definition 2.7

(discrete moving frames)
.

Let
G^{N}
denote the Cartesian product of
N
copies of the group
G
.
A map

\rho\colon\ M^{N}\rightarrow G^{N},\qquad\rho(z)=(\rho_{1}(z),\dots,\rho_{N}(z))

is a right discrete moving frame if

\rho_{k}(g\cdot z)=\rho_{k}(z)g^{-1},\qquad k=1,\dots,N,

and a left discrete moving frame if

\rho_{k}(g\cdot z)=g\rho_{k}(z),\qquad k=1,\dots,N.

The frame is right (resp. left) equivariant under the action of the Lie group.

A set of normalization equations yields a corresponding right discrete moving frame. The component
\rho_{k}
is the unique element of
G
that takes
z
to the cross-section
\mathcal{K}_{k}
.
The sequence of moving frames with a nontrivial intersection of domains

3
Difference moving frames

In view of Remark

3.1

Let

A set of invariants is a

As

\frac{\mathrm{d}u^{\delta}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\theta^{\delta}_{\alpha}([\mathbf{u}])\sigma^{\alpha}.

Proposition 4.1

(invariant Euler–Lagrange equations)
.

Suppose that the Lagrangian

be an
R
-parameter Lie group of variational point symmetries for a given Lagrangian functional,

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\int\mathrm{L}(x,\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])\mathrm{d}x,

that leave the one-form
\mathrm{L}(x,\mathbf{n},[\mathbf{u}])\mathrm{d}x
invariant under the group action. Then

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x=\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{x}=\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}J_{x}\mathrm{d}x.

(7.1)

In particular, invariance under the transformations generated by
\mathbf{v}_{r}
amounts to the condition

\mathbf{v}_{r}(\mathrm{L})+\mathrm{L}D\xi_{r}=0

(see
[
18
]
for details). A useful equivalent form of this condition is

X_{r}(\mathrm{L})+D(\mathrm{L}\xi_{r})=0,

(7.2)

where
X_{r}
is the generator in characteristic form (whose action on
x
and
\mathrm{d}x
is trivial).

The Euler–Lagrange equations are

\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L}):=\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{K}}(-D)_{(j)}\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{j;\mathbf{K}}}\right)=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,m.

Just as for P
\Delta
Es, these equations can be obtained by allowing
[\mathbf{u}]
(but
not

x
) to depend smoothly on a continuous invariant parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, using
\phantom{.}{}^{\prime}
to denote the derivative with respect to
t
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathrm{L}}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{j;k}}(u^{\alpha}_{j;k})^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x+\operatorname{Div}(A_{\mathbf{u}})\mathrm{d}x,

(7.3)

where
\operatorname{Div}(A_{\mathbf{u}})
consists of the divergence terms arising from the summation and integration by parts. Note that
X_{r}(\mathrm{L})
is obtained from
\mathrm{d}\mathrm{L}/\mathrm{d}t
by setting
[(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}=Q^{\alpha}_{r}]
. So, from (
7.2
) and (
7.3
), the Noether conservation law corresponding to invariance under
\mathbf{v}_{r}
is

\displaystyle 0=-Q^{\alpha}_{r}\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Div}(A_{\mathbf{u}})\big|_{[(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}=Q^{\alpha}_{r}]}-X_{r}(\mathrm{L})=\operatorname{Div}(A_{\mathbf{u}})\big|_{[(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}=Q^{\alpha}_{r}]}+D(\mathrm{L}\xi_{r}).

(7.4)

Evaluating (
7.1
) on the frame gives

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x=\iota(\mathrm{L})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=:L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}(\iota(x),\mathbf{n},[\boldsymbol{\kappa}])\iota(\mathrm{d}x),

where
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
denotes the generating invariants that depend on
[\mathbf{u}]
(and possibly also on
x
). Explicitly,
\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathcal{J}\mathrm{d}x
, so
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\mathrm{L}\mathcal{J}^{-1}
. As the frame is projectable,
\mathcal{J}
depends only on
x
; in particular, if
x
is invariant,
\mathcal{J}=1
.

To obtain the invariantized Euler–Lagrange equations, differentiate the one-form
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)
with respect to
t
. Each
\kappa^{\beta}
depends on
t
through its dependence on
[\mathbf{u}]
, giving rise to the syzygies

\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\beta}/\mathrm{d}t=\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\beta}\sigma^{\alpha}
, where
\sigma^{\alpha}=\iota((u_{0;0}^{\alpha})^{\prime})
and each
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is a an invariant differential-difference operator. Specifically,

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\beta}=\iota\left(\frac{\partial\kappa^{\beta}}{\partial u^{\alpha}_{j;\mathbf{K}}}\right)\mathcal{D}_{(j)}\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}},

where
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}D
is the invariantized total derivative and
\mathcal{D}_{(j)}:=\mathcal{D}^{j}
. Note that
\mathcal{D}
commutes with each
\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}}
; thus the invariant derivatives and shifts of
\kappa^{\beta}
can be written as
\kappa^{\beta}_{j;\mathbf{K}}:=\mathcal{D}_{(j)}\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\kappa^{\beta}
.
Integration by parts is straightforward: given two functions,
f
and
g
,

f(\mathcal{D}g)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=f(Dg)\mathrm{d}x=\{D(fg)-(Df)g\}\mathrm{d}x=\{\mathcal{D}(fg)-(\mathcal{D}f)g\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

As
\mathcal{D}(fg)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)
is a divergence, define the adjoint of
\mathcal{D}
relative to
\iota(\mathrm{d}x)
to be
\mathcal{D}^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}=-\mathcal{D}
. (The bold dagger
\boldsymbol{\dagger}
distinguishes this adjoint from the standard adjoint,
\dagger
.) As the frame is projectable,
\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}}^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}=\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}}^{\dagger}=\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{K}}
.
Therefore, using the notation
(-\mathcal{D})_{(j)}:=(-1)^{j}\mathcal{D}_{(j)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\partial\kappa^{\beta}_{j;\mathbf{K}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\beta}_{j;\mathbf{K}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\left((-\mathcal{D})_{(j)}\mathrm{S}_{-\mathbf{K}}\frac{\partial L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\partial\kappa^{\beta}_{j;\mathbf{K}}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\kappa^{\beta}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}\bigr)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{\alpha}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}(\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}))\bigr\}\sigma^{\alpha}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\mathcal{H}}+A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

(7.5)

The one-forms
\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)
and
\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\mathcal{H}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)
are defined by the above;
\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}
has the same form as
\operatorname{Div}
, but
with
D
replaced by
\mathcal{D}
. The following identity is useful:

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}\bigl(A^{0};A^{1},\dots,A^{m}\bigr)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\operatorname{Div}\bigl(A^{0};\mathcal{J}A^{1},\dots,\mathcal{J}A^{m}\bigr)\mathrm{d}x.

(7.6)

Similarly to P
\Delta
Es, the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
component of
A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
(resp.
A_{\mathcal{H}}
) is of the form
F^{i}_{\beta}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)^{\prime}

(
resp.
H^{i}_{\alpha}\sigma^{\alpha}
)
; here
F^{i}_{\beta}
and
H^{i}_{\alpha}
are invariant differential-difference operators.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose that the Lagrangian one-form
\mathrm{L}(\mathbf{n},x,[\mathbf{u}])\mathrm{d}x
is invariant under an
R
-parameter Lie group of point transformations.
In the above notation,

\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x=\bigl(\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\beta}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigr){\sigma^{\alpha}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x),

(7.7)

so the invariantized Euler–Lagrange D
\Delta
Es are

\iota(\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L}))=\bigl(\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}^{\beta}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=0,\qquad\alpha=1,\dots,q.

(7.8)

Furthermore,

\operatorname{Div}(A_{\mathbf{u}})\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\mathcal{H}}+A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

(7.9)

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as for P
\Delta
Es. In view of (
7.6
),

0=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x-L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x))=\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\int\mathrm{E}_{u^{\alpha}}(\mathrm{L})(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x-\bigl\{\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}_{\alpha}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}(\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}))\bigr\}\sigma^{\alpha}\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

This holds for arbitrary functions
(u^{\alpha}_{0;\mathbf{0}})^{\prime}
, which are independent at each base point
\mathbf{n}
. Therefore, (
7.7
) follows, from which (
7.8
) is obtained by invariantizing and using the independence of the functions
\sigma^{\alpha}
. Equation (
7.9
) is derived by equating the right-hand sides of (
7.3
) and (
7.5
), taking (
7.7
) into account.
∎

In the original variables, there are two types of contribution to each Noether conservation law (
7.4
) for which
\xi_{r}\neq 0
. The first type arises from integration and summation by parts, so can be treated in much the same way as for P
\Delta
Es. However, unlike the P
\Delta
E case,
A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
cannot be neglected.
Each fundamental invariant
\kappa^{\beta}
satisfies
\mathbf{v}_{r}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)=0
. However, only the vertical variables
[\mathbf{u}]
depend on
t
, so the characteristic form of the generator is used. If
t=\varepsilon^{r}
, then
\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)^{\prime}
, evaluated at
\varepsilon^{r}=0
, reduces to

X_{r}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)=\mathbf{v}_{r}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)-\xi_{r}D\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)=-\xi_{r}D\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)=-\mathcal{D}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)\iota(\xi_{s})a_{r}^{s}(\rho).

(7.10)

The last equality arises from (
6.3
), evaluated on the moving frame
\rho
.

The counterpart of the remaining term in (
7.4
), namely
D(\mathrm{L}\xi_{r})
, is derived as follows. From (
6.5
) and (
7.1
),

\mathrm{L}\xi_{r}=\mathrm{L}J_{x}^{-1}\widetilde{\xi}_{s}a_{r}^{s}(g)=\widetilde{\mathrm{L}}\widetilde{\xi}_{s}a_{r}^{s}(g),

for all
g\in G
; on the moving frame, this amounts to

\mathrm{L}\xi_{r}=L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\xi_{s})a_{r}^{s}(\rho).

Consequently,

D(\mathrm{L}\xi_{r})\mathrm{d}x=\mathcal{D}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\xi_{s})a_{r}^{s}(\rho))\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

(7.11)

Combining all of the above, we obtain the following formulation of the Noether conservation laws.

Proposition 7.2
.

Suppose that the conditions of Proposition
7.1
hold. Then Noether’s theorem gives the
R
conservation laws

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{r})\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=0,\qquad r=1,\dots,R,

(7.12)

whose components are

\displaystyle A_{r}^{0}=H_{\alpha}^{0}\{\iota(Q^{\alpha}_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho)\}-F_{\beta}^{0}\big\{\mathcal{D}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)\iota(\xi_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho)\big\}+L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\xi_{s})a_{r}^{s}(\rho),

(7.13)

\displaystyle A_{r}^{i}=H_{\alpha}^{i}\{\iota(Q^{\alpha}_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho)\}-F_{\beta}^{i}\big\{\mathcal{D}\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)\iota(\xi_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho)\big\},\qquad i=1,\dots,m.

(7.14)

Proof.

For each
r
in turn, replace
t
by
\varepsilon^{r}
and
u^{\alpha}
by

\widehat{u}^{\alpha}=u^{\alpha}+\varepsilon^{r}Q^{\alpha}_{r}(\mathbf{n},x,[\mathbf{u}])+\mathcal{O}\bigl((\varepsilon^{r})^{2}\bigr)

(prolonged as necessary), and evaluate the results at
\varepsilon^{r}=0
. Using the same reasoning as for P
\Delta
Es,

\sigma^{\alpha}_{r}\big|_{\varepsilon^{r}=0}=\left\{\frac{\partial(g\cdot u^{\alpha})}{\partial u^{\beta}}Q^{\beta}_{r}\right\}\bigg|_{g=\rho}=\bigl\{\widetilde{Q}^{\alpha}_{s}a^{s}_{r}(g)\bigr\}\bigg|_{g=\rho}=\iota(Q^{\alpha}_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho),

which is substituted for
\sigma^{\alpha}
in
A_{\mathcal{H}}
. The proof is completed by replacing
\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)^{\prime}
in
A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
by the right-hand side of (
7.10
), and adding the remaining term (
7.11
).
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

Each component of the conservation laws (
7.12
) is equivariant with respect to the moving frame
\rho
, because there exist functions
f^{i}_{s}
of the invariants such that

A^{i}_{r}=f^{i}_{s}(\mathbf{n},\iota(x),[\boldsymbol{\kappa}])a_{r}^{s}(\rho),\qquad i=0,\dots,m,\quad r=1,\dots,R.

Proof.

The invariant differential-difference operators in (
7.13
) and (
7.14
) act linearly on products of invariants and adjoint components
a_{r}^{s}(\rho)
. Consequently, every term in
A_{r}^{i}
is of the form
\phi^{i}_{l}a_{r}^{l}(\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}})
, where
\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}}=\mathcal{D}_{(j)}\mathrm{S}_{\mathbf{K}}\rho
and
\phi^{i}_{s}
is invariant. The invariantization of
\rho_{1;0}
(in a matrix representation) is the curvature matrix,
\rho_{1;0}\rho^{-1}
(see
[
6
,
12
]
). By applying powers of
\mathcal{D}
to the curvature matrix and eliminating derivatives of order
1,\dots,j-1
, one finds that the term in braces in the identity below is invariant:

\rho_{j;0}=\bigl\{\rho_{j;0}\rho^{-1}\bigr\}\rho.

Shifting this, and using our earlier result that
\rho_{0;\mathbf{K}}\rho^{-1}
is invariant for a difference moving frame, shows that

\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}}=\bigl\{\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}}\rho_{0;\mathbf{K}}^{-1}\bigr\}\bigl\{\rho_{0;\mathbf{K}}\rho^{-1}\bigr\}\rho=\bigl\{\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}}\rho^{-1}\bigr\}\rho

is a product of invariants (in braces) and
\rho
. The adjoint matrices
\bigl(a_{r}^{l}(g)\bigr)
constitute a Lie group representation, so

\phi^{i}_{l}a_{r}^{l}(\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}})=\bigl\{\phi^{i}_{l}a^{l}_{s}\bigl(\rho_{j;\mathbf{K}}\rho^{-1}\bigr)\bigr\}a_{r}^{s}(\rho),

which is in the required form.
∎

8
Examples

Example 8.1
.

To illustrate the calculations in a simple context (without any particular application), consider the Lagrangian one-form

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(u_{1;0})^{2}}{u_{0;1}-u_{0;0}}\mathrm{d}x,

(8.1)

whose Euler–Lagrange equation is

\mathrm{E}_{u}(\mathrm{L})=\left(\frac{-2u_{2;0}}{u_{0;1}-u_{0;0}}+\frac{2u_{1;0}u_{1;1}-(u_{1;0})^{2}}{(u_{0;1}-u_{0;0})^{2}}-\frac{(u_{1;-1})^{2}}{(u_{0;0}-u_{0;-1})^{2}}\right)=0.

(8.2)

The one-form (
8.1
) is invariant under the two-parameter Lie group of point transformations

g\colon\ (x,n,u)\longmapsto(\widetilde{x},n,\widetilde{u})=(bx,n,bu+a).

The infinitesimal generators are linear combinations of
\mathbf{v}_{1}=\partial_{u}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}=x\partial_{x}+u\partial_{u}
, which yield
(\xi_{1},Q_{1})=(0,1)
and
(\xi_{2},Q_{2})=(x,u_{0;0}-xu_{1;0})
. Then (
7.4
) gives the following conservation laws (expressed as one-forms for comparison):

\displaystyle-Q_{1}\mathrm{E}_{u}(\mathrm{L})\mathrm{d}x=D\left(\frac{2u_{1;0}}{u_{0;1}-u_{0;0}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+D_{n}\left(\frac{-(u_{1;-1})^{2}}{(u_{0;0}-u_{0;-1})^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=0,

\displaystyle-Q_{2}\mathrm{E}_{u}(\mathrm{L})\mathrm{d}x=D\left(\frac{u_{1;0}(2u_{0;0}-xu_{1;0})}{u_{0;1}-u_{0;0}}\right)\mathrm{d}x+D_{n}\left(\frac{(u_{1;-1})^{2}(xu_{1;0}-u_{0;0})}{(u_{0;0}-u_{0;-1})^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x=0.

(8.3)

Note that (
8.3
) includes the term
D(\mathrm{L}\xi_{2})\mathrm{d}x
.

Reflecting the identities
\mathbf{v}_{1}=b\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}=-a\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
, the adjoint representation is given by

a_{1}^{1}(g)=b,\qquad a_{1}^{2}(g)=0,\qquad a_{2}^{1}(g)=-a,\qquad a_{2}^{2}(g)=1.

The normalization
\iota(x)=1,\ \iota(u_{0;0})=0
gives a projectable moving frame
\rho
, on which

a=\frac{-u_{0;0}}{x},\qquad b=\frac{1}{x}.

Therefore,
\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=x^{-1}\mathrm{d}x
and the invariantized total derivative operator is
\mathcal{D}=xD
. On the moving frame, the adjoint representation has components

a_{1}^{1}(\rho)=\frac{1}{x},\qquad a_{1}^{2}(\rho)=0,\qquad a_{2}^{1}(\rho)=\frac{u_{0;0}}{x},\qquad a_{2}^{2}(\rho)=1.

A generating set of invariants is

\kappa^{1}=\iota(u_{1;0})=u_{1;0},\qquad\kappa^{2}=\iota(u_{0;1})=\frac{u_{0;1}-u_{0;0}}{x},

which satisfy the syzygy

\kappa_{0;1}^{1}=\iota(u_{1;1})=\kappa^{1}+\kappa^{2}_{1;0}+\kappa^{2}.

(8.4)

The Lagrangian one-form amounts to

L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\iota(\mathrm{d}x),

and so

\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{1}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}},\qquad\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{2}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=-\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{2}.

In terms of
\sigma=\iota(u_{0;0}^{\prime})=x^{-1}u_{0;0}^{\prime}
, the
t
-derivatives of the generating invariants are
\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}^{\beta}\sigma
, where

\mathcal{H}^{1}=\mathcal{D}+\mathrm{id},\qquad\mathcal{H}^{2}=\mathrm{S}-\mathrm{id}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{2}\bigl(\kappa^{2}\bigr)^{\prime}\right)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigl(\kappa^{\beta}\bigr)^{\prime}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}\sigma\bigr)\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\bigl(\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigr)\sigma\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\mathcal{H}})\iota(\mathrm{d}x),

where

\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\mathcal{H}})=\mathcal{D}\bigl(\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{1}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\sigma\bigr)+D_{n}\bigl(\bigl\{\mathrm{S}^{-1}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{2}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigr\}\sigma\bigr).

In this example,
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
does not involve derivatives or shifts of the generating invariants, and hence
\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}(A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=0
.
The invariantized Euler–Lagrange equation is

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{H}^{\beta}\bigr)^{\boldsymbol{\dagger}}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{\beta}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=(-\mathcal{D}+\mathrm{id})\left(\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\right)+\bigl(\mathrm{S}^{-1}-\mathrm{id}\bigr)\left(-\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\bigl(\kappa^{1}-\kappa^{1}_{1;0}\bigr)}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\kappa^{1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}+2\kappa^{2}_{1;0}\bigr)}{\bigl(\kappa^{2}\bigr)^{2}}-\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}_{0;-1}}{\kappa^{2}_{0;-1}}\right)^{2}.

For comparison, one can invariantize (
8.2
) directly, using (
8.4
) and

\iota(u_{2;0})=\kappa^{1}_{1;0},\qquad\iota(u_{1;-1})=\kappa^{1}_{0;-1},\qquad\iota(u_{0;-1})=-\kappa^{2}_{0;-1}.

From Proposition
7.2
, the conservation laws given by Noether’s theorem amount to

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\bigl\{(\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{1}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\iota(Q_{s})+L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\iota(\xi_{s}))a_{r}^{s}(\rho)\bigr\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad{}+D_{n}\bigl\{\bigl\{\mathrm{S}^{-1}\mathrm{E}_{\kappa^{2}}(L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\bigr\}\iota(Q_{s})a^{s}_{r}(\rho)\bigr\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=0.

(8.5)

Substituting

\iota(Q_{s})a_{1}^{s}(\rho)=\frac{1}{x},\qquad\iota(Q_{s})a_{2}^{s}(\rho)=\frac{u_{0;0}}{x}-\kappa^{1},\qquad\iota(\xi_{s})a_{1}^{s}(\rho)=0,\qquad\iota(\xi_{s})a_{2}^{s}(\rho)=1,

into (
8.5
) gives the conservation laws

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\left\{\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{x\kappa^{2}}\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+D_{n}\left\{-\frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}_{0;-1}}{\kappa^{2}_{0;-1}}\right)^{2}\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\left\{\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{u_{0;0}}{x}-\kappa^{1}\right)+\frac{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+D_{n}\left\{\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}_{0;-1}}{\kappa^{2}_{0;-1}}\right)^{2}\left(\kappa^{1}-\frac{u_{0;0}}{x}\right)\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}\left\{\frac{\kappa^{1}}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\frac{2u_{0;0}}{x}-\kappa^{1}\right)\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x)+D_{n}\left\{\left(\frac{\kappa^{1}_{0;-1}}{\kappa^{2}_{0;-1}}\right)^{2}\left(\kappa^{1}-\frac{u_{0;0}}{x}\right)\right\}\iota(\mathrm{d}x).

Example 8.2
.

Method of lines semi-discretizations are a common source of D
\Delta
Es with just one continuous independent variable. The nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation for a field with real and imaginary parts
u
and
v
respectively has the following (non-integrable) semi-discretization, with uniform step length
h
:

\displaystyle-v_{1;0}+u_{0;0}\bigl(u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}\bigr)+h^{-2}(u_{0;-1}-2u_{0;0}+u_{0;1})=0,

\displaystyle u_{1;0}+v_{0;0}\bigl(u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}\bigr)+h^{-2}(v_{0;-1}-2v_{0;0}+v_{0;1})=0.

These are the Euler–Lagrange equations corresponding to the Lagrangian one-form

\displaystyle\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x=\left\{\frac{1}{2}(v_{0;0}u_{1;0}-u_{0;0}v_{1;0})+\frac{1}{4}\bigl(u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}\bigr)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\phantom{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{d}x=}{}-\frac{1}{2}h^{-2}\bigl((u_{0;1}-u_{0;0})^{2}+(v_{0;1}-v_{0;0})^{2}\bigr)\right\}\mathrm{d}x,

which is invariant under the two-parameter abelian Lie group of point transformations

g\colon\ (x,n,u,v)\longmapsto(\widetilde{x},\widetilde{n},\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v})=(x+a,n,u\cos b+v\sin b,-u\sin b+v\cos b).

The infinitesimal generators are
\mathbf{v}_{1}=\partial_{x}
and
\mathbf{v}_{2}=v\partial_{u}-u\partial_{v}
, so (using variable names rather than indices, for clarity)

(\xi_{1},Q_{1}^{u},Q_{1}^{v})=(1,-u_{1;0},-v_{1;0}),\qquad(\xi_{2},Q_{2}^{u},Q_{2}^{v})=(0,v_{0;0},-u_{0;0}).

As the Lie group is abelian, the adjoint representation is the identity, so
a_{r}^{s}(g)=\delta_{r}^{s}
for all
g
.

We now choose the normalization
\iota(x)=0
,
\iota(v_{0;0})=0
, temporarily restricting attention to
u_{0;0}>0
. (Other normalizations can be used for the remaining coordinate patches.) This gives the frame
\rho
defined by

a\big|_{\rho}=-x,\qquad b\big|_{\rho}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{0;0}}{u_{0;0}}\right).

In the calculations that follow,
\cos b
and
\sin b
(but not
b
) are evaluated on the frame, so we use

a\big|_{\rho}=-x,\qquad\cos b\big|_{\rho}=\frac{u_{0;0}}{\sqrt{u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}}},\qquad\sin b\big|_{\rho}=\frac{v_{0;0}}{\sqrt{u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}}},

which extends to other coordinate patches. Note that
\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathrm{d}x
, and so the invariantized total derivative is
\mathcal{D}=D
.
The invariants are generated by

\displaystyle\kappa^{1}=\iota(u_{0;0})=\sqrt{u_{0;0}^{2}+v_{0;0}^{2}},\qquad\kappa^{2}=u_{0;0}v_{1;0}-v_{0;0}u_{1;0},\qquad\kappa^{3}=u_{0;0}u_{0;1}+v_{0;0}v_{0;1}.

To see this, note that all derivatives can be obtained from

u_{1;0}=\frac{u_{0;0}\kappa^{1}\kappa^{1}_{1;0}-v_{0;0}\kappa^{2}}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}},\qquad v_{1;0}=\frac{u_{0;0}\kappa^{2}+v_{0;0}\kappa^{1}\kappa^{1}_{1;0}}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}},

and that under the constraint
\iota(v_{0;1})\geq 0
, all shifts can be obtained from

u_{0;1}=\frac{u_{0;0}\kappa^{3}-v_{0;0}\phi}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}},\qquad v_{0;1}=\frac{u_{0;0}\phi+v_{0;0}\kappa^{3}}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}},

where

\phi=\bigl\{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\kappa^{1}_{0;1}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(\kappa^{3}\bigr)^{2}\bigr\}^{1/2}.

We adopt this constraint for definiteness; if it is not satisfied, replace
\phi
by
-\phi
throughout. By calculating
u_{1;1}
(or
v_{1;1}
), one obtains the syzygy

\frac{\kappa^{3}\kappa^{1}_{1;1}}{\kappa^{1}_{0;1}}-\kappa^{3}_{1;0}+\frac{\kappa^{1}_{1;0}}{\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\phi\kappa^{2}}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}}-\frac{\phi\kappa^{2}_{0;1}}{\bigl(\kappa^{1}_{0;1}\bigr)^{2}}=0.

In terms of the generating parametric derivatives,

\sigma^{u}=\iota(u_{0;0}^{\prime})=\frac{u_{0;0}u_{0;0}^{\prime}+v_{0;0}v_{0;0}^{\prime}}{\kappa^{1}},\qquad\sigma^{v}=\iota(v_{0;0}^{\prime})=\frac{u_{0;0}v_{0;0}^{\prime}-v_{0;0}u_{0;0}^{\prime}}{\kappa^{1}},

the derivatives of the generating invariants are

\displaystyle\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}=\sigma^{u},\qquad\bigl(\kappa^{2}\bigr)^{\prime}=\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{1}}\sigma^{u}+\bigl(\kappa^{1}D-\kappa^{1}_{1;0}\bigr)\sigma^{v},

\displaystyle\bigl(\kappa^{3}\bigr)^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\kappa^{3}}{\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}}{\kappa^{1}_{0;1}}\mathrm{S}\right)\sigma^{u}+\left(\frac{\phi}{\kappa^{1}}-\frac{\phi}{\kappa^{1}_{0;1}}\mathrm{S}\right)\sigma^{v}.

As
\iota(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathcal{D}=D
, the invariant Euler–Lagrange equations and conservation laws can be calculated directly from

L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=-\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{4}-\frac{1}{2}h^{-2}\bigl(\bigl(\kappa^{1}_{0;1}\bigr)^{2}-2\kappa^{3}+\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{2}\bigr).

Differentiating this, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\kappa^{2}\bigr)^{\prime}+\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{3}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}-h^{-2}\bigl(\kappa^{1}_{0;1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}_{0;1}\bigr)^{\prime}-\bigl(\kappa^{3}\bigr)^{\prime}+\kappa^{1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\bigl\{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{3}-2h^{-2}\kappa^{1}\bigr\}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\kappa^{2}\bigr)^{\prime}+h^{-2}\bigl(\kappa^{3}\bigr)^{\prime}+\underbrace{D_{n}\bigl(-h^{-2}\kappa^{1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}\bigr)}_{\mathcal{D}\mathrm{iv}A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}

(8.6)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left\{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{3}-\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{h^{2}}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}_{0;1}}\mathrm{S}\right\}\sigma^{u}

\displaystyle+\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{1}D+\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{1}_{1;0}+\frac{\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}-\frac{\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}_{0;1}}\mathrm{S}\right\}\sigma^{v}+D_{n}\bigl(-h^{-2}\kappa^{1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}\bigr)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\left\{\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{3}-\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{h^{2}}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}_{0,-1}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}\right\}\sigma^{u}+\left\{\kappa^{1}_{1;0}+\frac{\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}-\frac{\mathrm{S}^{-1}\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}\right\}\sigma^{v}

\displaystyle+D\left(-\frac{1}{2}\kappa^{1}\sigma^{v}\right)+D_{n}\left(\frac{\kappa^{3}_{0,-1}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}\sigma^{u}-\frac{\mathrm{S}^{-1}\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}\sigma^{v}\right)+D_{n}\bigl(-h^{-2}\kappa^{1}\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{\prime}\bigr).

Consequently, the invariantized Euler–Lagrange equations are

\displaystyle\iota(\mathrm{E}_{u}(\mathrm{L}))

\displaystyle=\bigl(\kappa^{1}\bigr)^{3}-\frac{2\kappa^{1}}{h^{2}}-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}+\frac{\kappa^{3}_{0,-1}}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}=0,

\displaystyle\iota(\mathrm{E}_{v}(\mathrm{L}))

\displaystyle=\kappa^{1}_{1;0}+\frac{\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}-\frac{\mathrm{S}^{-1}\phi}{h^{2}\kappa^{1}}=0.

In this example, the conservation laws have contributions from both
A_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
(see (

A_{\mathcal{H}}

a_{r}^{s}(\rho)=\delta_{r}^{s}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\iota(Q_{1}^{u})=-\kappa^{1}_{1;0},\qquad\iota(Q_{1}^{v})=-\kappa^{2}/\kappa^{1},\qquad\iota(Q_{2}^{u})=0,\\
&\iota(Q_{2}^{v})=-\kappa^{1},\qquad\iota(\xi_{1})=1,\qquad\iota(\xi_{2})=0,\end{split}

we obtain the following conservation laws from Proposition
7.2

\displaystyle 0={}

9
Concluding remarks

For P
\Delta
Es and D
\Delta
Es, the prolongation space over a fixed base point
\mathbf{n}
provides a continuous setting in which moving frames can be used. Difference moving frames respect the ordering of each discrete independent variable and the arbitrariness of the base point. For variational problems, we have shown how to calculate the invariantized Euler–Lagrange equations and equivariant Noether conservation laws directly from an invariant Lagrangian,
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.

We have treated the coordinates
n^{i}
on the lattice of independent variables,
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, as being fixed by the Lie group of transformations. For a formulation that allows discrete symmetries of the lattice, it would be necessary to replace the Lagrangian
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
by the Lagrangian
m
-form,
L^{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\mathrm{vol}
, where
\mathrm{vol}
denotes the difference volume form (see
[
8
,
13
]
). This adds complexity, but little extra insight. However, when each
n^{i}
is fixed, only the coefficients of difference forms are transformed by the group action, so one can use moving frames as we have done, without the additional machinery of difference forms.

Our treatment of D
\Delta
Es has been restricted to a single continuous independent variable and a projectable moving frame, enabling the shift and invariant differential operators to commute. More generally, let
p
be the number of continuous independent variables. If
p>1
, the complexity increases, because the invariant differential operators do not necessarily commute with one another. However, if the moving frame is projectable, all shift operators commute with the invariant derivatives, enabling existing results from PDE theory to be used for the differential part of the calculations. The requirement for the group action to be projectable is not sufficient to guarantee the existence of a projectable moving frame. Nevertheless, projectable moving frames are relevant to many D
\Delta
Es of interest, including some well-known integrable systems and method of lines semi-discretizations of PDEs.
