Information Lower Bounds for Robust Mean Estimation
Rémy Degenne, Timothée Mathieu

Abstract
We prove lower bounds on the error of any estimator for the mean of a real probability distribution under the knowledge that the distribution belongs to a given set. We apply these lower bounds both to parametric and nonparametric estimation. In the nonparametric case, we apply our results to the question of sub-Gaussian estimation for distributions with finite variance to obtain new lower bounds in the small error probability regime, and present an optimal estimator in that regime. In the (semi-)parametric case, we use the Fisher information to provide distribution-dependent lower bounds that are constant-tight asymptotically, of order 2 ​ log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) / ( n ​ I ) \sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)/(nI)} where I I is the Fisher information of the distribution. We use known minimizers of the Fisher information on some nonparametric set of distributions to give lower bounds in cases such as corrupted distributions, or bounded/semi-bounded distributions.



keywords
Nonparametric statistics, mean estimation, sub-Gaussian estimator, Kullback-Leibler divergence

1
Introduction

Mean estimation is the task of finding an estimate of the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]
of a probability distribution
F
, given only access to
n\in\mathbb{N}
independent samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
In this work,
F
will be a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}
(
F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
), with finite mean
\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]
.
We are interested in estimators, measurable functions
\tilde{\mu}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for a number of samples
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that when
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are i.i.d. with law
F
, the estimation error
|\tilde{\mu}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|
is as small as possible with probability close to 1.
We will also write simply
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
for the random variable
\tilde{\mu}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
.

The empirical mean

Perhaps the simplest mean estimator is the empirical mean,
\hat{\mu}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
. For any distribution
F
with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, by the central limit theorem,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|\geq\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta/2)\right)\to\delta\>.

We are however interested in non-asymptotic guarantees, for a given sample size
n
.
For Gaussian random variables with variance
\sigma^{2}
, the empirical mean is also Gaussian and
\delta
is a non-asymptotic upper bound of the deviation probability written above.
In particular, on Gaussian distributions the empirical mean has deviation bounded by
\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}
(a slightly weaker bound than
\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}}\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta/2)
since
\Phi^{-1}(1-\delta/2)\leq\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)}
), without using any knowledge of the variance
\sigma^{2}
.
By Chernoff’s method, we can extend the properties of the empirical mean to sub-Gaussian distributions: for any
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
-sub-Gaussian probability distribution, the empirical mean has error less than
\sqrt{\frac{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}
with probability
1-\delta
, for any
\delta\in(0,1]
.

In contrast, lower bounds in
(
Catoni, 2012
)
show that for the class of distributions with finite second moment, Chebyshev’s inequality is essentially tight for the empirical mean
(
Catoni, 2012
, Proposition 6.2)
, and that on the other hand there exists other estimators with error width much smaller.
Then, the empirical mean is not a good estimator for that class of distributions and one needs to design more sophisticated estimators to achieve optimal rates of convergence for distribution with only finite moments bounds (and not an infinity of finite moments).

Definition 1
.

An estimator
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is a
(x(n,\delta,F),\delta)
-estimator on
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
if for all
F\in\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle F^{\otimes n}(|\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|\geq x(n,\delta,F))\leq\delta\>.

For a given set of distributions
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we will be interested in two main questions:

•

Can we get lower bounds on
x(n,\delta,F)
for a given set of distributions
\mathcal{D}
?

•

Can we design estimators with error upper bounds that match those lower bounds?

Sub-Gaussian estimators

Several recent works on mean estimation follow
Devroye et al. (2016)
and consider the question of finding
sub-Gaussian
estimators.
An estimator is said to be sub-Gaussian on a class of distributions
\mathcal{D}
with constant
C
if it is a
(C\sqrt{\frac{2\Var_{F}[X]\log(2/\delta)}{n}},\delta)
-estimator on
\mathcal{D}
, where
\Var_{F}[X]
is the variance of
F\in\mathcal{D}
.
The empirical mean is a sub-Gaussian estimator with constant 1 on the set of sub-Gaussian distributions, for any
\delta
.

Devroye et al. (2016)
showed that there is no sub-Gaussian estimator on the class of distributions with moment
1+\alpha
if
\alpha<1
. The main class of interest in that line of work is then distributions with finite variance.

We have now several algorithms which are sub-Gaussian for a constant
C>1
, for
\delta\in(e^{-cn},1)
for some
c>0

(
Lugosi and Mendelson, 2019
;
Cherapanamjeri et al., 2019
;
Minsker and Ndaoud, 2021
;
Cherapanamjeri et al., 2022
)
. See also
(
Gobet et al., 2023
)
which provides an overview of these results.
On the other hand, using techniques based on median-of-means,
Lee and Valiant (2022)
and
Minsker (2023)
designed algorithms that are sub-Gaussian for
C=1+o(1)
(close to optimal since
C=1
is the best attainable constant asymptotically), under hypotheses
\log(1/\delta)/n\to 0
and
\delta\to 0
.

Devroye et al. (2016)
showed that the
\delta\geq e^{-cn}
barrier is not improvable: for any
C
, there is no sub-Gaussian estimator with constant
C
for
\delta\leq e^{-cn}
, for
c
small enough.
Our lower bounds shed light on the optimal deviation of an estimator in the
\delta\leq e^{-cn}
regime. We derive the optimal function
y(n,\delta)
such that
(\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]y(n,\delta)},\delta)
-estimators exist. While
y(n,\delta)\approx\log(1/\delta)/n
if
\log(1/\delta)/n
is small enough, we also describe its shape for
\delta\ll e^{-n}
, where the notion of sub-Gaussian estimator with a given constant is not an adequate concept.

1.1
Related work

In addition to the literature on sub-Gaussian estimators, our article is related to the following works.

Robust estimation.

Originating in the works of
Huber (1964)
;
Huber (1967)
and
Hampel (1971)
, robustness theory was at first interested in providing efficient estimators of location parameters in the presence of outlier data, also called corruption. Most results were asymptotic and ignored the finite sample rates of convergence
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009
)
. Mean estimation with sub-Gaussian rates under heavy-tail assumptions is closely linked to robust estimation under corruption, although estimators that perform well for Heavy-tail distributions do not necessarily perform well on corrupted data.

Information lower bounds.
The lower bounds we derive for estimation come from a reduction to testing, which follows
(
Tsybakov, 2009
)
. We then bound the error probability of tests using information theoretic quantities like the Kullback-Leibler divergence (
\mathrm{KL}
).
This general idea was already present in the work of
Wald (1945)
.
Lower bound techniques for tests using
\mathrm{KL}
were then the subject of further development in the sequential testing literature
(
Hoeffding, 1953
;
Hoeffding, 1960
;
Lorden, 1983
)
, and more recently in the multi-armed bandit literature
(
Lai and Robbins, 1985
;
Burnetas and Katehakis, 1996
;
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
)
.

Garivier et al. (2019)
demonstrated that the data-processing inequality of
\mathrm{KL}
is an easy-to-use and important tool to obtain lower bounds.

Lower bounds based on the Fisher information.

The classical bound by Cramer and Rao
(
Cramér, 1946
;
Radhakrishna Rao, 1945
)
says that in a sufficiently smooth model, the minimal variance an estimator can achieve is controlled by the inverse of the Fisher information. The Fisher information appear in our case through the asymptotics of the likelihood ratio (see Section 7.2 of
(
Le Cam and Yang, 2000
)
), which we use in Section
4
to get the expression for the rates of convergence in a semi-parametric setting. A more recent line of work using the Fisher information can be found in
(
Gupta et al., 2022
;
Gupta et al., 2023
)
in which the authors obtain lower bound and upper bound on the estimation error with a dependency in the inverse of the Fisher information of a smoothed version of the target distribution. Compared to our work in Section
4
, the lower bound provided in
(
Gupta et al., 2022
, Section 6)
is asymptotic and valid only for target distributions that are smoothed versions of a distribution, for
\delta\ll 1
.

Summary of our contributions

•

We prove a generic lower bound on
x(n,\delta,F)
for a
(x(n,\delta,F),\delta)
-estimator on any set of distributions with finite means (Theorem
7
).

•

We apply that lower bound for
\mathcal{D}
the set of distributions with finite variance (Theorem
10
) and we introduce an estimator with a matching upper bound for
\delta\ll 1/n
(Theorem
11
).

•

We prove an asymptotic high probability lower bound for the estimation error in a semi-parametric setting using the Fisher information of the sampling distribution
\mathrm{I}(F)=\int(f^{\prime})^{2}/f
with
f
the density of
F
(Theorem
17
), using quadratic mean differentiability assumptions. We apply this lower bound to the case of corrupted, bounded and semi-bounded distributions.

2
Testing and estimation lower bounds

In order to obtain a lower bound on the performance of an estimator, we reduce that problem to testing, as is often done in the estimation literature (see for example
(
Tsybakov, 2009
)
). Suppose that all distributions in
\mathcal{D}
have finite mean. Then for any
x:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{F\in\mathcal{D}}F\left(|\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{F}|\geq x(F)\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\max\left\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{D},\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq c-x(F)}F(\tilde{\mu}_{n}\geq c),\sup_{G\in\mathcal{D},\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq c+x(G)}G(\tilde{\mu}_{n}\leq c)\right\}\>.

For
E_{c}=\{\tilde{\mu}_{n}\geq c\}
and
\mathcal{F}_{c}=\{F\in\mathcal{D},\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq c-x(F)\}
,
\mathcal{G}_{c}=\{G\in\mathcal{D},\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq c+x(G)\}
, we obtain a lower bound of the form

\displaystyle\sup_{F\in\mathcal{D}}F\left(|\tilde{\mu}_{n}-\mu_{F}|\geq x(F)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\max\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{c}}F(E_{c}),\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{c}}G(E_{c}^{c})\}\>.

The only reason this is not an equality is that
E_{c}^{c}=\{\tilde{\mu}_{n}<c\}
might differ from
\{\tilde{\mu}_{n}\leq c\}
, and since we don’t assume anything on
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
the probability of the two events might be different.
In order to get estimation lower bounds, it then suffices to get lower bounds on
\max\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F(E),\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}G(E^{c})\}
for an event
E
and two families of distributions
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
.
This can be seen as a testing bound: for a test trying to tell whether a distribution belongs to
\mathcal{F}
or
\mathcal{G}
and for
E
the event that the test returns
\mathcal{G}
, this quantity is the error probability of the test.

2.1
Divergences between probability distributions

The lower bounds we prove for testing problems use tools from information theory, notably several divergences between probability distributions which we recall here.

Definition 2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be two probability distributions on a measurable space
\Omega
, let
\mu
be a dominating measure of both (for example
P+Q
) and let
p=\frac{dP}{d\mu}
and
q=\frac{dQ}{d\mu}
. We recall the definition of the following divergences, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\text{Kullback-Leibler: }\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\left[\log\frac{dP}{dQ}(X)\right]\text{ if }P\ll Q\>,\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)=+\infty\text{ otherwise,}

\displaystyle\text{Rényi: }D_{\alpha}(P,Q)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\int p^{\alpha}q^{1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\mu\>.

The Rényi divergence is finite if and only if
P
and
Q
are not mutually singular.
We refer the reader to
(
Van Erven and Harremos, 2014
)
for a thorough review of the properties of the Rényi divergences and their relations to
\mathrm{KL}
.
Particularly relevant here are the relations
\frac{1}{2}D_{1/2}(P,Q)=-\log(1-\mathrm{H}^{2}(P,Q))
, where
\mathrm{H}(P,Q)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\int(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{q})^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu}
is the Hellinger distance and
(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P,Q)=\inf_{R\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}(\alpha\mathrm{KL}(R,P)+(1-\alpha)\mathrm{KL}(R,Q))
.
The Kullback-Leibler and Rényi divergences satisfy data processing inequalities, from which we can obtain the following lemma (proved in Appendix
A
), which is central to the proofs in this paper.

Lemma 3
.

For all probability measures
P,Q
on a measurable space
\Omega
, event
E
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)

\displaystyle\geq P(E)\log\frac{1}{Q(E)}-\log 2\>,

(1)

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(P,Q)

\displaystyle\geq\min\{\alpha,1-\alpha\}\log\frac{1}{\max\{P(E),Q(E^{c})\}}-\log 2\>.

(2)

For KL, we will also use the following change of measure lemma, proved in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4
.

For all probability measures
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, all events
E
of
\Omega
and all
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle Q(E)e^{\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)+\beta}

\displaystyle\geq P(E)-P\left(\log\frac{dP}{dQ}-\mathrm{KL}(P,Q)>\beta\right)\>.

(3)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, probability measures
P,F,G\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, all events
E
of
\Omega^{n}
and all
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle 2\max\{F(E),G(E^{c})\}e^{n\max\{\mathrm{KL}(P,F),\mathrm{KL}(P,G))+n\beta}

(4)

\displaystyle\geq 1-P^{\otimes n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{dP}{dF}(X_{i})-\mathrm{KL}(P,F)>\beta\right)-P^{\otimes n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{dP}{dG}(X_{i})-\mathrm{KL}(P,G)>\beta\right)\>.

Definition 5
.

The Chernoff divergence between two finite measures
F
and
G
on a measurable space
\Omega
, denoted by
D_{\mathcal{C}}(F,G)
, is the quantity

D_{\mathcal{C}}(F,G)=\inf_{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}\max\{\mathrm{KL}(P,F),\mathrm{KL}(P,G)\}
.

This is not a distance because it does not satisfy the triangle inequality, but it is symmetric, non-negative and equal to zero if
F=G
. Remark that it satisfies the inequality
D_{\mathcal{C}}(F,G)\geq\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}(1-\alpha)D_{\alpha}(F,G)
. We will use in particular
D_{1/2}(F,G)\leq 2D_{\mathcal{C}}(F,G)
. For any divergence
D
between probability distributions and any sets
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, we write
D(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})=\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F},G\in\mathcal{G}}D(F,G)
.

2.2
Lower bounds

From the lower bounds on the divergences discussed in the previous section, we can deduce lower bounds on the probability of error of any test tasked with deciding whether a distribution belongs to a set
\mathcal{F}
or another set
\mathcal{G}
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
be two sets of probability distributions and let
E
be an event. Then

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{4\max\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F(E),\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}G(E^{c})\}}\leq D_{1/2}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\leq 2D_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\>.

Proof.

Let
\delta=\max\{\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F(E),\sup_{G\in\mathcal{G}}G(E^{c})\}
. By inequality (
2
), for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
G\in\mathcal{G}
,
D_{1/2}(F,G)\geq\log\frac{1}{4\delta}
. We conclude the proof by taking an infimum over
F
and
G
.
∎

The two inequalities of the theorem are tight. Indeed, with
B_{a}
the Bernoulli distribution with parameter
a
, let
\mathcal{F}=\{B_{a}\}
and
\mathcal{G}=\{B_{1-a}\}
. Let
E=\{X=1\}
. Then

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{4\max\{B_{a}(E),B_{1-a}(E^{c})\}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{1}{4a}\>,

\displaystyle D_{1/2}(B_{a},B_{1-a})=2D_{\mathcal{C}}(B_{a},B_{1-a})=2\mathrm{KL}(B_{\frac{1}{2}},B_{a})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{1}{4a}+\log\frac{1}{1-a}\>.

As
a\to 0
, these two quantities become arbitrarily close.
Theorem
6
is thus tight on Bernoulli distributions, hence on tests from one sample. However, if we consider two sets of product distributions on product spaces like
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then as
n\to+\infty
we get an inequality involving
D_{\mathcal{C}}
instead of
2D_{\mathcal{C}}
(see Theorem
25
in Appendix
A
).
The main tools used to remove the factor 2 are Lemma
4
, Equation (
4
), and the law of large number to argue that the probabilities in Equation (
4
) go to zero.

We now combine the reduction from estimation to testing and the testing bound of Theorem
6
, to obtain an estimation lower bound.

Theorem 7
.

For any
\left(x(n,\delta,F),\delta\right)
-estimator on a set
\mathcal{D}
of distributions with finite means,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}\leq\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}D_{1/2}\left(\{F\in\mathcal{D}\mid\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq c-x(n,\delta,F)\},\{G\in\mathcal{D}\mid\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq c+x(n,\delta,G)\}\right)\>.

(5)

We can use Theorem
7
to obtain lower bounds on
x(n,\delta,F)
for various classes of distributions by obtaining upper bounds of the Rényi divergence between these two sets, or on the Chernoff divergence (since
D_{1/2}\leq 2D_{\mathcal{C}}
).
We now discuss two simple examples.

Lemma 8
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is the set of all Gaussian distributions and if there exists a
(\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]y(n,\delta)},\delta)
-estimator. Then Equation (
5
) simplifies to
\frac{1}{n}\log(1/4\delta)\leq 2y(n,\delta)
, and it follows that
y(n,\delta)\geq\frac{1}{2n}\log(1/4\delta)
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is the set of all Laplace distributions and if there exists a
(\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]\log(1/4\delta)},\delta)
-estimator. Then Equation (
5
) simplifies to
\frac{1}{n}\log(1/4\delta)\leq e^{-2\sqrt{y(n,\delta)}}+2\sqrt{y(n,\delta)}-1
and it follows that
\delta\geq\frac{1}{4}e^{-4n}
.

The proof of this lemma is given in Appendix
C
. Note that we get the same lower bounds for any set
\mathcal{D}
that contains all Gaussian (resp. Laplace) distributions (but may be larger).

In Lemma
8
, in the Gaussian case, there is a factor
2
in the denominator which seems to contrast with what we expect from the central limit theorem. This factor
2
should indeed disappear asymptotically, this can be done with the same techniques as in the proof of Theorem
10
.
However, as already discussed below Theorem
6
, when
n=1
this factor
2
cannot be removed in general if
\mathcal{D}
contains Bernoulli distributions. In the case of Laplace distributions, we get that an estimator cannot be sub-Gaussian with optimal constant unless
\delta\geq\frac{1}{4}e^{-4n}
, this result was already proved with a slightly worst constant in
(
Devroye et al., 2016
, Theorem 4.3)
(their lower bound is
\delta\geq e^{1-10n}
).

3
Lower bounds for nonparametric estimation

3.1
The set of distributions with finite variance

We consider in this section the set of distributions
\mathcal{D}_{2}=\{F\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\mid\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}[X^{2}]<+\infty\}
. We are interested in lower bounds on
\left(\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]y(n,\delta)},\delta\right)
-estimators, which include the sub-Gaussian estimators for
y(n,\delta)=\log(2/\delta)/n
.
In order to apply Theorem
7
, it suffices to be able to compute a
1/2
-Rényi or Chernoff divergence between two particular sets of distributions.
This is what we do in the next lemma, which uses heavily the work done in
(
Nishiyama, 2020
)
on the computation of the minimal Hellinger distance between two sets of distributions with given mean and variance.

Lemma 9
.

For all
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle D_{1/2}\left(\left\{F\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\mid\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq c-\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]x}\right\},\left\{G\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\mid\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq c+\sqrt{2\Var_{G}[X]x}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\log(1+2x)\>.

Furthermore, for these pairs of sets,
D_{1/2}=2D_{\mathcal{C}}
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.
Now that we have computed the Chernoff divergence between the two sets of measures used in the testing bound, we get an estimation lower bound.

Theorem 10
.

For any
\left(\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]y(n,\delta)},\delta\right)
-estimator on
\mathcal{D}_{2}
,
y(n,\delta)
satisfies the inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\log(1+2y(n,\delta))\>,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log(1+2y(n,\delta))+\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\log(\sqrt{1+2y(n,\delta)}+\sqrt{2y(n,\delta)})\>.

If the error probability depends on
n
with
\delta_{n}\to 0
, then
\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}}\log(\sqrt{1+2y(n,\delta_{n})}+\sqrt{2y(n,\delta_{n})})=o\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+2y(n,\delta_{n}))\right)
.

The first inequality is an immediate consequence of Theorem
7
and Lemma
9
. To obtain the second inequality, we use Lemma
4
, Equation (
4
), with explicit minimizers
F_{n}
and
G_{n}
for the Rényi divergence (coming from
(
Nishiyama, 2020
)
).
Given these minimizers, we are able to control the variance of the likelihood ratio appearing in Equation (
4
). The two expressions on the right of the second inequality then correspond to the
\mathrm{KL}
and variance terms.
While Theorem
7
is more easily applicable to many problems, the finer knowledge we have of the minimizers of the Rényi divergence for the finite variance case allows us to get a better asymptotic bound with Equation (
4
).

A new estimator

We introduce a new mean estimator, based on our lower bounds, and prove that its error is close to the lower bound in the regime
\delta\ll 1/n
.
The estimator is however hard to compute, and its main purpose is to be a witness of the tightness of the lower bounds.

For
c\in\mathbb{R}
and a positive function
y(n,\delta)
, let
\mathcal{F}_{c}=\{F\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\mid\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq c-\sqrt{2\Var_{F}[X]y(n,\delta)}\}
and
\mathcal{G}_{c}=\{G\in\mathcal{D}_{2}\mid\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq c+\sqrt{2\Var_{G}[X]y(n,\delta)}\}
. Let
\hat{P}_{n}
be the empirical distribution of
n
samples and let
\hat{d}_{L,n}:c\mapsto\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{c}}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{P}_{n},F)
and
\hat{d}_{R,n}:c\mapsto\inf_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{c}}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{P}_{n},G)
.
The function
\hat{d}_{L,n}
is non-negative, non-increasing and equal to 0 for
c
large enough.
The function
\hat{d}_{R,n}
is non-negative, non-decreasing and equal to 0 for
c
small enough.
We define the estimator

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\sup\{c\in\mathbb{R}\mid\hat{d}_{L,n}(c)>\hat{d}_{R,n}(c)\}\>.

Theorem 11
.

For
y(n,\delta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{2}{n}\log\frac{2e(n+1)^{2}}{\delta}\right)-1\right)
,
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is a
(\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}y(n,\delta)},\delta)
-estimator.

The idea of the proof is that on one hand,
\max\{\hat{d}_{L,n}(c),\hat{d}_{R,n}(c)\}
is larger than a Chernoff divergence. On the other hand, if the estimator
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
makes a large mistake, then with probability
1-\delta
the same quantity has to be small by a concentration argument about the infimum of Kullback-Leibler divergence, and we obtain a contradiction if
y(n,\delta)
is well-chosen.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\hat{P}_{n}
be the empirical distribution of
n
samples from a probability distribution

. If
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq m
and
\Var_{P}[X]\leq\sigma^{2}
then with probability

\displaystyle\inf_{F,\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq m,\Var_{F}[X]\leq\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{KL}(\hat{P}_{n},F)<\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{e(n+1)^{2}}{\delta}\>.

The same result holds with all inequalities
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\leq m
replaced by
\mathbb{E}_{P}[X]\geq m
.

See Appendix
B
for a proof, which follows previous work
(
Agrawal et al., 2021
)
and uses duality of the Kullback-Leibler divergence and a bound on the regret of an online learning algorithm.

From Theorem
10
, we get that for any
(\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}y(n,\delta)})
-estimator,

has to be larger than
\frac{1}{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)-1\right)
. And if
\delta\to 0
, the lower bounds become closer to
\frac{1}{2}\left(\exp\left(\frac{2}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)-1\right)
.
The difference between this second lower bound and the error width of
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is the term
2e(n+1)^{2}
in the logarithm.
The estimator
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
is thus close to optimal in the regime
\delta\ll 1/n
, but for larger

its error bound depends on
\log(n)/n
instead of
\log(1/\delta)/n
.
The

factor is the usual price for obtaining a bound valid for all times. We would like to have a concentration result for a given

without that

term, and we conjecture that such a concentration inequality should hold (in which case the estimator is optimal for all

Together, Theorem

and Theorem

characterize the optimal error for an estimator with error probability
\delta

. We see that the notion of sub-Gaussian estimator is inadequate when

3.2
Lower bound for finite

moments

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let

be the distributions with finite moment of order

Theorem 13
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. For any
\left(\varepsilon_{n}M_{1+\alpha}(F),\delta\right)
-estimator on
\mathcal{D}_{1+\alpha}
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

satisfies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}\geq\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\>.

If
\log(1/\delta)/n\to 0
, then the bound becomes
\varepsilon_{n}\geq\frac{1}{1+o(1)}\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{2\delta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}
.

(
Devroye et al., 2016
, Theorem 3.1)
is a very similar lower bound on that problem, and we use the distributions they build for their lower bounds in our proof. The lower bound technique is however different. There is a small mistake in the constants in their proof, and their corrected result is that for
\delta\in(e^{-4n},1/2)
,
\varepsilon_{n}\geq 2^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}
.
The differences between their result and ours are first that we have a worse power of 2 in front and second, that they have a
\log(2/\delta)
where we have
\log(1/2\delta)
, so their bound is slightly better. Note however that we don’t need
\delta>e^{-4n}
.

4
Lower bound on estimation in (semi-)parametric families

In this section, we apply our lower bound technique to the case of the location model
\mathcal{D}=\{F(x-\mu)\mid\mu\in\mathbb{R}\}
for some smooth distribution
F
and to smooth bounded or semi-bounded distributions.

4.1
Quadratic Mean Differentiability and the Fisher information for parametric lower bounds

We assume that we are dealing with a parametric family of distributions that are smooth in the sense that the distributions form a quadratic mean differentiable family (abbreviated q.m.d.) defined in Definition
15
.
See Section 7.2 in
(
Le Cam and Yang, 2000
)
for background on this concept.

Definition 15

(Q.m.d family of distributions)
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
, and let
\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
be a family of distributions absolutely continuous with respect to a measure
\mu
.
\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
is said to be quadratic mean differentiable at
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
if there exists
\eta:\mathbb{R}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
such that for
|h|\to 0

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sqrt{p_{\theta_{0}+h}(x)}-\sqrt{p_{\theta_{0}}}-h\eta(x,\theta_{0})\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu x=o(|h|^{2})\>.

In order to prove that a family of distributions is q.m.d., one may use
(
Lehmann et al., 2005
, Theorem 12.2.1)
that we recall in Appendix
E
. It can be shown that most exponential families of distributions are q.m.d. Another example is Laplace distributions: although it is not smooth, the Laplace family of distributions parametrized by its mean is q.m.d. (see Section 7.2 of
(
Vaart, 2000
)
). The family of uniform distributions is an example of distributions which are not q.m.d.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}\}
be a q.m.d. family of distributions on
\mathbb{R}
absolutely continuous with respect to a common measure
\mu
. We denote
\mathrm{I}(F_{\theta})
the Fisher information defined by
\mathrm{I}(F_{\theta}):=4\int\eta(x,\theta)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
.
If moreover the distributions are continuously differentiable with respect to the parameter, we have
\mathrm{I}(F_{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{F_{\theta}}\left[(\frac{\partial f_{\theta}}{\partial\theta}(X))^{2}/f_{\theta}(X)^{2}\right].

In particular, we will be interested in the parametric location family defined for some distribution
F
by
\mathcal{F}=\{F(x-\mu)\mid\mu\in\mathbb{R}\}
, for which we will assume that
f
is derivable and the Fisher information becomes

\mathrm{I}(F):=\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[\frac{(f^{\prime}(X))^{2}}{f(X)^{2}}\right].

The following theorem found in
(
Lehmann et al., 2005
, Theorem 12.2.3)
uses the Fisher information to specify the asymptotic mean and variance of the likelihood ratios in a q.m.d. family.

Theorem 16

(Convergence of likelihood ratio for a q.m.d. family)
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
, suppose that
\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
is q.m.d. and absolutely continuous with respect to a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that
0<\mathrm{I}(P_{\theta})<\infty
and fix
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
. We have under
P_{\theta_{0}}^{\otimes n}
, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}
,
h\neq 0
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{p_{\theta_{0}}}{p_{\theta_{0}+h/\sqrt{n}}}\right)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{h^{2}}{2}\mathrm{I}(P_{\theta_{0}}),h^{2}\mathrm{I}(P_{\theta_{0}})\right)

It is straightforward to see that the mean of the left-hand-side of the limit in Theorem
16
is
n\mathrm{KL}(P_{\theta_{0}},P_{\theta_{0}+h/\sqrt{n}})
. The expression for the mean in the asymptotics follows because the Fisher information is linked to the second derivative of KL (
(
Calin and Udrişte, 2014
, Theorem 4.4.5)
and
(
Abbasnezhad and Arghami, 2006
, Theorem 2.1)
). We now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
and let
\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
be a q.m.d. family which is absolutely continuous with respect to a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\underline{\mathrm{I}}=\inf\{\mathrm{I}(P_{\theta})\mid\theta\in\Theta,\>0<\mathrm{I}(P_{\theta})<\infty\}
.
If there exists a sequence of
(\eta/\sqrt{n},\delta)
-estimators on
\{P_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\Theta\}
for samples sizes
n\to+\infty
, then
\eta
satisfies

\displaystyle\eta\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\underline{\mathrm{I}}}}\left(\sqrt{1+2\log\frac{1}{4\delta}}-1\right)\>.

If there exists a family of estimators
(\mathcal{E}_{n,\delta})_{n\in\mathbb{N},\delta\in(0,1)}
and reals
(\eta_{\delta})_{\delta\in(0,1)}
such that each
\mathcal{E}_{n,\delta}
is a
(\eta_{\delta}/\sqrt{n},\delta)
-estimator, then

\liminf_{\delta\to 0}\eta_{\delta}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\underline{\mathrm{I}}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{-1}\geq 1
.

Theorem
17
is more general than
(
Gupta et al., 2022
, Theorem 1.3)
because they compare smoothed Fisher informations while our result is applicable directly to the target distribution.
The proof is an application of Lemma
4
, equation (
4
), in which we control the two probabilities of deviation with Theorem
16
(see Appendix
D
).

4.2
Lower bounds on location estimation based on the Fisher information

4.2.1
Distribution that are invariant by translation

Corollary 18
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{D}
, with
\mathcal{D}
invariant by translation (i.e.
F(\cdot+h)\in\mathcal{D}
for any

f

Corollary
18

. In particular, in the case of robust mean estimation in a corrupted setting, the lower bound will ignore the asymptotic bias that is inevitable when dealing with corrupted distributions, see
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009

.

Until now, in this section, everything was in a fully parametric setting, using a smooth parametric family of distribution. However, we can use Corollary
18
in a semi-parametric way. Let us define
\mathcal{D}_{F}=\{F(\cdot-h)\mid h\in\mathbb{R}\}
and
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{F}\mathcal{D}_{F}
.
We can obtain a lower bound for each
\mathcal{D}_{F}
with Corollary
18
and then combine these lower bounds to obtain that if there exists a sequence of
(\eta/\sqrt{n},\delta)
-estimators on
\mathcal{D}
for samples sizes

, then
\eta
satisfies

\eta\geq\frac{1}{\inf_{F\in\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{I}(F)}\left(\sqrt{1+2\log\frac{1}{4\delta}}-1\right).

From this, we see that it may be interesting to look at minimizer of the Fisher information over nonparametric set of distributions and our lower bound would still apply to those.

Lemma 19

Let
\mathcal{D}

4.2.2
Distribution with bounded or semi-bounded support

In the case of distributions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
or supported on an interval, the minimal Fisher information is known (see
Bercher and Vignat (2009)
) but we cannot apply the results from Section
4.2.1
because
\mathrm{KL}\left(F,F(\cdot-h)\right)
will be infinite as soon as

in the case of support on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and it will always be infinite for support on an interval. Instead, we consider the random variable
Y_{h}=X+2hU
where
U\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1])
. This can be seen as a smoothing.
Remark that the c.d.f. or
Y_{h}
is

F_{Y_{h}}(x)=F\left(X+2hU\leq x\right)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}F(x-2u)\mathrm{d}u

whose p.d.f is

f_{Y_{h}}(x)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}f(x-2u)\mathrm{d}u.
Remark that because
f
is absolutely continuous,
f_{Y_{h}}
is derivable with respect to

. In order to have a well-defined Fisher information, we take the convention that for any

f(x)=0

f^{\prime}(x)/f(x)=0
.

Theorem 20
.

If there exists a sequence of

-estimators for samples sizes

Then, using the minimum Fisher information for bounded and semi-bounded distributions from

5
Conclusion and open problems

We have provided lower bounds for mean and location estimation and have shown several applications of the bounds, to nonparametric settings like the distributions with bounded variance as well as to parametric cases with lower bounds depending on the Fisher information.

About the generic lower bounds, we observed in Theorem
6
that the factor 2 in the
2D_{\mathcal{C}}
upper bound is necessary for Bernoulli distributions and
n=1
sample. However, that factor 2 disappears when
n\to+\infty
, a fact that we prove with a different proof and that requires more information about the set of distributions we consider.
An interesting goal would be to have a single bound with a factor transitioning from 2 to 1 as
n
grows (possibly under assumptions on the sets of distributions).

For the finite variance case, the main remaining questions are first whether the estimator we introduced is optimal also for
\delta>1/n
, which could require a better concentration on the infimum of Kullback-Leibler divergence, and second whether we can compute efficiently that estimator (or modify it to make it efficiently computable).

In the case of the distributions with finite moment of order
1+\alpha
, our lower bound could presumably be improved by computing exactly the Chernoff divergence between the sets of distributions with high and low mean and the optimal distributions as was done in the variance case. The current bound is based on a guess for good distributions taken from
(
Devroye et al., 2016
)
.

There have been a lot of interest in robust multivariate mean estimation, in particular lower bounds can be found in
(
Chen et al., 2018
, Theorem 2.2)
, the authors show that the optimal rate is of order
\Omega(\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)/n}+\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|\log(1/\delta)/n})
where
\Sigma
is the covariance matrix. Several estimators achieve this optimal rate
(
Depersin and Lecué, 2019
;
Dalalyan and Minasyan, 2022
)
but the optimal constants in factor of the complexities term are still unknown.

Moment

Let

By Lemma

B.3
Log-Lipschitz condition

of Theorem

.

Let
h\in\mathbb{R}
. By inequality between KL and

distance (see for instance
(
Gibbs and Su, 2002

(6)

Then, using that

is Lipschitz, we have:

\bullet
If

, then by Lipschitz property
\log(f(x+h))-\log(f(x))\leq Lh

\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{f(x)}\leq\frac{e^{Lh}f(x)-f(x)}{f(x)}=e^{Lh}-1\,.

\bullet
If
f(x+h)\leq f(x)
, then by Lipschitz property
\log(f(x))-\log(f(x+h))\leq Lh
hence
f(x)\leq f(x+h)e^{Lh}
and

\frac{f(x)-f(x+h)}{f(x)}\leq\frac{e^{Lh}f(x+h)-f(x+h)}{f(x)}=(e^{Lh}-1)\frac{f(x+h)}{f(x)}\leq e^{Lh}-1\,.

In both cases,
\frac{|f(x+h)-f(x)|}{f(x)}\leq e^{Lh}-1
. Injecting this in Equation (
6
) and using the formula for the total variation distance as the
L^{1}
distance between the densities, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(F(x),F(x+h))\leq(e^{Lh}-1)\int|f(x+h)-f(x)|\mathrm{d}x=(e^{Lh}-1)\mathrm{TV}(F(x),F(x+h))\,.

Then, by Pinsker’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(F(x),F(x+h))\leq(e^{Lh}-1)\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}(F(x),F(x+h))}\,,

which implies

\mathrm{KL}(F(x),F(x+h))\leq\frac{1}{2}(e^{Lh}-1)^{2}.

Now, it remains to apply Theorem
7
(with the Chernoff divergence) with
G_{+}=F(x+\varepsilon_{n})
and
G_{-}=F(x-\varepsilon_{n})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{4\delta}\leq(e^{L\varepsilon_{n}}-1)^{2}\>.

Isolate
\varepsilon_{n}

Theorem 27

(Sufficient condition for q.m.d.)
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
. Assume the following conditions:

•

\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)}
is an absolutely continuous function of
\theta
in some neighborhood of
\theta_{0}
for
\mu
-almost all
x
.

•

For
\mu
-almost all
x
, the derivative
p_{\theta}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}p_{\theta}(x)
exists at
\theta=\theta_{0}
.

•

The Fisher information
\mathrm{I}(F_{\theta})
is finite and continuous at
\theta=\theta_{0}
.

Then
\{P_{\theta},\quad\theta\in\Theta\}
is q.m.d. at
\theta_{0}
with quadratic mean derivative
\eta(x,\theta)
given by

\eta(x,\theta):=\begin{cases}p^{\prime}_{\theta}(x)/(2\sqrt{p_{\theta}(x)})&\text{ if }p_{\theta}(x)>0\text{ and }p_{\theta}^{\prime}(x)\text{ exists}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For completeness, we remind that
g(\theta)
is an absolutely continuous function on an interval
[a,b]
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
if for any
\theta\in[a,b]
,
g(\theta)=g(a)+\int_{a}^{\theta}h(x)\mathrm{d}x
for some integrable function
h
. In particular, if
g
is pointwise continuously derivable the
g
is absolutely continuous.
