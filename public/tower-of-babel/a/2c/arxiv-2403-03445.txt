Evaluations and Relations for Finite Trigonometric Sums
Bruce C. Berndt, Sun Kim, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. Several methods are used to evaluate finite trigonometric sums. In each case, either the sum had not previously been evaluated, or it had been evaluated, but only by analytic means, e.g., by complex analysis or modular transformation formulas. We establish both reciprocity and three sum relations for trigonometric sums. Motivated by certain sums that we have evaluated, we add coprime conditions to the summands and thereby define analogues of Ramanujan sums, which we in turn evaluate. One of these analogues leads to a criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis, analogous to the Franel–Landau criterion.

1.
Introduction

Evaluations of finite trigonometric sums arise in many contexts in mathematics and physics. The paper
[
3
]
by the first author and B. P. Yeap contains a total of 89 references in which evaluations arise in many diverse areas within mathematics and physics.

In our recent paper
[
4
]
, we devised two methods to evaluate trigonometric sums. First, we developed new approaches in contour integration, which extended ideas that two of us made in
[
7
]
. Second, we devised a new method for evaluating trigonometric sums.

In the present paper, we continue our examinations of finite trigonometric sums that we made in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
. In particular, we further develop the aforementioned approach in
[
4
]
. However, we also introduce new ideas, which we relate below.

First, in Section
2

Second, recall that Ramanujan sums (defined in (
3.1
)) have a co-prime requirement on the indices of summation. Our evaluations of several sine sums in Section
2
motivated us to define certain analogues of Ramanujan sums in Section
3
. More precisely, we take certain trigonometric sums from Section
2
and add a co-prime condition to the summands. These sums have apparently not been studied in the literature. Remarkably, we are able to find elegant evaluations of these ‘new’ sums.

The Frenel–Landau criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis can be readily established using Ramanujan sums. We use one of the aforementioned analogues of Ramanujan sums to establish a new criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis. Our new criterion has two interesting and different features that are not found in the Frenel–Landau criterion.

Third, the coefficients
a_{n}
of finite trigonometric sums evaluated in the literature are generally of three types:
a_{n}=1,a_{n}=(-1)^{n}
, and
a_{n}=\chi(n)
, where
\chi
is a Dirichlet character. In Theorem
5.6
in Section
5

a_{n}
are
\pm 1

As remarked above, in
[
4
]
we introduced a new method for the evaluation of finite trigonometric sums. Our method yielded evaluations and identities for which only analytic proofs were previously available. This study continues in the present paper, but now with a concentration on reciprocity theorems and identities involving three trigonometric sums.
In the transformation formulae of the Dedekind eta-function, and in many generalizations and/or analogues of the Dedekind eta function, sums frequently arise that have representations as finite cotangent sums. Generally, they satisfy reciprocity theorems and, in some cases, three term relations, in the terminology of the classical literature. (It seems more appropriate to instead call these identities ‘three sum’ relations, and so we shall do so in the sequel.) These reciprocity theorems and three sum relations are normally derived from the transformation formulas themselves or by using the residue theorem. In Section
7
, we provide different, and perhaps shorter, proofs of reciprocity theorems and three sum relations for several cotangent sums, In particular, we establish a reciprocity theorem for a modified Dedekind sum associated with Eisenstein series, which was introduced by the first author in
[

2

2.
Trigonometric Sums Initially Arising from Ramanujan’s Theory of Theta Functions

In the section, we give elementary proofs for the evaluations (1.5)–(1.10) in

Theorem 2.1

(Equations (1.5) and (1.6) in
[
13
]
)
.

If
k
is an odd positive integer
\geq 3
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}(-1)^{j-1}\sin\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=\frac{(-1)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}}{2},

(2.1)

and

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}(-1)^{j-1}\csc\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=\frac{k+(-1)^{\frac{k+1}{2}}}{2}.

(2.2)

Proof.

Note that

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}(-1)^{j-1}\sin\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\sin\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}.

With

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\sin\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e^{\frac{(2j-1)\pi i}{2k}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\left\{\frac{e^{\frac{\pi i}{2k}}\big(1-(-e^{\frac{\pi i}{k}})^{k}\big)}{1+e^{\frac{\pi i}{k}}}\right\}=0,

we obtain (
2.1
).

Similarly, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\frac{k-1}{2}}(-1)^{j-1}\csc\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\csc\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)-\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}.

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\csc\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=2i\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\frac{e^{\frac{(2j-1)\pi i}{2k}}}{e^{\frac{(2j-1)\pi i}{k}}-1}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e^{\frac{(2j-1)\pi i}{2k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}e^{\frac{(2j-1)\pi i}{k}t}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}e^{-\frac{(2t+1)\pi i}{2k}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e^{\frac{(2t+1)j\pi i}{k}}.

(2.3)

Using the identity

\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}e^{\frac{(2t+1)j\pi i}{k}}=\begin{cases}-k,&\text{if}~t=\frac{k-1}{2},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

in (
2.3
) we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{j-1}\csc\Big(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2k}\Big)=-i(-i)(-k)=k.

This completes the proof of (
2.2
).
∎

(2.5)

Proof of (

Using the product formula for sine, we find that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{(j-1)k\pi}{n}\right)\sin\left(\frac{(j+1)k\pi}{n}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{jk\pi}{n}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{\cos\left(\frac{2k\pi}{n}\right)-\cos\left(\frac{2jk\pi}{n}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{jk\pi}{n}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{jk\pi}{n}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)\sin^{2}\left(\frac{jk\pi}{n}\right)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)}-\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{jk\pi}{n}\right)}.

Now, recall

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{2}-1}\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k\pi}{n}\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{n^{2}-1}{3}-1\right)=\frac{n^{2}-4}{6}.

(2.10)

If we set

Putting (

3.
Analogues of Ramanujan Sums

In his famous paper
[
18
]
,
[
19
, p. 179]
, Ramanujan began by defining the sums

c_{q}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}e^{2\pi i\,kn/q},

(3.1)

which are now known as
Ramanujan Sums
, and he derived several properties of them. Excellent surveys on Ramanujan sums have been written by M. Ram Murty
[
15
]
and M. T. Rassias and László Tóth
[
20
]
.

The evaluations in the proceeding sections, in particular, (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), motivate us to define sums with the same summands, but now with a co-prime requirement on the indices of the summands, i.e., analogues of Ramanujan sums (
3.1
). Appearing in our proofs is the Möbius function
\mu(n)
, and, in particular, we need the property
[
16
, p. 111]

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\begin{cases}1,\quad&\text{if }n=1,\\
0,&\text{if }n>1.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Theorem 3.1
.

If
k
is an odd positive integer with
k\geq 3
, then

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(k):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
(\ell,k)=1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=\dfrac{(-1)^{(k-1)/2}}{2}\mu(k).

(3.3)

Proof.

Observe that (
2.1
) can be written in the form

S(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=\dfrac{(-1)^{(k+1)/2}}{2}.

(3.4)

Using (
3.2
), we can write
\tilde{S}(k)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(k)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|\ell\\
d|k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
d|\ell\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right).

(3.5)

Now set

\displaystyle k=dq,\quad\ell=db.

(3.6)

Thus, the inner sum on the right-hand side of (
3.5
) takes the shape

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
d|\ell\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(db-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{(d-1)/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(b-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right),

(3.7)

since
b
and
d
are both odd. Putting (
3.7
) in (
3.5
), recalling that
q=k/d
, and employing (
3.4
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(k)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(d-1)/2}\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(b-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(d-1)/2}\mu(d)\cdot\dfrac{(-1)^{(k/d+1)/2}}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(d+k/d)/2}\mu(d).

(3.8)

Now if
A
and
B
are odd integers, it is easily checked that
A+B\equiv AB+1\,(\textup{mod}\,4)
. Thus,

d+\dfrac{k}{d}\equiv k+1\,(\textup{mod}\,4)\,\,\Rightarrow\dfrac{1}{2}\left(d+\dfrac{k}{d}\right)\equiv\dfrac{k+1}{2}\,(\textup{mod}\,2)\,\,\Rightarrow(-1)^{(d+k/d)/2}=(-1)^{(k+1)/2}.

Thus, by (
3.8
) and (
3.2
),

\tilde{S}(k)=\dfrac{(-1)^{(k+1)/2}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=\dfrac{(-1)^{(k+1)/2}}{2}\left\{\sum_{d|k}\mu(d)-\mu(k)\right\}=\dfrac{(-1)^{(k-1)/2}}{2}\mu(k),

which completes the proof of (
3.3
).
∎

When
k
is a prime number, it is easily seen that (
2.1
) and (
3.4
) are in agreement.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k
be an odd positive integer with
k\geq 3
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(k):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
(\ell,k)=1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=\dfrac{k}{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|k\\
p\equiv 1\,(\textup{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|k\\
p\equiv 3\,(\textup{mod}\,4)\end{subarray}}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{p}\right),

(3.9)

where the products are taken over all primes
p\equiv 1\,(\textup{mod}\,4)
and
p\equiv 3\,(\textup{mod}\,4)
, respectively, with
p
dividing
k
in each product.

If
k
is prime, then (
3.9
) yields

\tilde{T}(k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{k}{2}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{k}\right),\quad&\text{if }k\equiv 1\,(\textup{mod}\,4),\\
\dfrac{k}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{1}{k}\right),\quad&\text{if }k\equiv 3\,(\textup{mod}\,4),\end{cases}

which is in agreement with (
2.2
).

Proof.

We begin by writing (
2.2
) in the form

T(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=\dfrac{k+(-1)^{(k+1)/2}}{2}.

(3.10)

As above, employing (
3.2
), we can write
\tilde{T}(k)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(k)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|\ell\\
d|k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
d|\ell\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right).

(3.11)

Making the changes of variables (
3.6
), we find that the inner sum above can be represented by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq\ell<k\\
\ell\textup{ odd}\\
d|\ell\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi\ell}{2k}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(db-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{(d-1)/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(b-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(d)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq b<q\\
b\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(b-1)/2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi b}{2q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(d)\dfrac{q+(-1)^{(q+1)/2}}{2},

(3.12)

where
\chi
denotes the non-principal character modulo 4, and we applied (
3.10
) for the last equality.
Remembering that
q=k/d
, we insert (
3.12
) into (
3.11
) to arrive at

\tilde{T}(k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)\chi(d)\dfrac{k/d+(-1)^{(k/d+1)/2}}{2}.

(3.13)

Observe that, since

(3.14)

Hence, from (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\mu(d)\chi(d)}{d}-\dfrac{\chi(k)}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|k\\
d<k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k}{2}\sum_{d|k}\dfrac{\mu(d)\chi(d)}{d}-\dfrac{\mu(k)\chi(k)}{2}-\dfrac{\chi(k)}{2}\sum_{d|k}\mu(d)+\dfrac{\mu(k)\chi(k)}{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k}{2}\prod_{p|k}\left(1+\dfrac{\mu(p)\chi(p)}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k}{2}\prod_{p|k}\left(1-\dfrac{\chi(p)}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{k}{2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|k\\

which is identical to (

4.
An Analogue of the Franel–Landau Criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis

First define the Farey sequence and Farey fractions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let

\mathfrak{F}_{Q}=\left\{\frac{a}{q}:\,\,a,q\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,1\leq q\leq Q,\,\,1\leq a\leq q,\,\,(a,q)=1\right\}.

These fractions are the Farey fractions of order
Q
.
Let
N(Q)
denote the number of Farey fractions in
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
. The Farey sequence of order
Q
is obtained by arranging the fractions
\gamma_{j}
of
\mathfrak{F}_{Q}
in increasing order, i.e.,
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{N(Q)}
.

For brevity, set
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}
. Recall the definition of the Ramanujan sum in (
3.1
). From
[
22
, p. 10, Equation (1.5.3)]

c_{q}(1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
(k,q)=1\end{subarray}}^{q}e(k/q)=\mu(q).

(4.1)

Summing over
q,1\leq q\leq Q
, we find that

\sum_{\gamma_{j}\in\mathfrak{F}_{Q}}e(\gamma_{j})=\sum_{1\leq q\leq Q}\mu(q).

(4.2)

It is well-known
[
11
, p. 261]
that the Riemann Hypothesis is equivalent to the statement

\sum_{1\leq q\leq Q}\mu(q)=O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}),

(4.3)

as
Q\to\infty
, for each
\epsilon>0
. For brevity, abbreviate the Riemann Hypothesis by RH. Hence, by (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), as
Q\to\infty
,

\text{RH holds}\iff\sum_{\gamma_{j}\in\mathfrak{F}_{Q}}e(\gamma_{j})=O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}),

(4.4)

for every
\epsilon>0
.

Recall that

\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\mu(q)}{q^{s}}=\dfrac{1}{\zeta(s)},\quad\text{Re}(s)>1.

From the product representation of
\zeta(s)
it follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\geq 1\\
q\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\mu(q)}{q^{s}}=\dfrac{1}{\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{s}}\right)\zeta(s)},\quad\text{Re}(s)>1.

Using an argument analogous to that yielding (
4.4
), we can deduce that, as
Q\to\infty
,

\text{RH holds}\iff\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq q\leq Q\\
q\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mu(q)=O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}),

(4.5)

for every
\epsilon>0
.

Recall from Theorem
3.1
that

\tilde{S}(q):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq a<q\\
a\textup{ odd}\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{(a-1)/2}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi a}{2q}\right)=\dfrac{(-1)^{(q-1)/2}}{2}\mu(q).

(4.6)

Let
\chi(n)
denote the non-principal character modulo 4, i.e.,

\chi(n)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{(n-1)/2},\quad&n\textup{ odd}\\
0,&n\textup{ even}.\end{cases}

Multiplying both sides of (
4.6
) by
\chi(q)
and
summing over odd
q
,
1\leq q\leq Q
, we find that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,q\textup{ odd}\\
\frac{a}{q}\in\mathfrak{F}_{Q}\end{subarray}}\chi(aq)\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi a}{2q}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq q\leq Q\\
q\textup{ odd}\end{subarray}}\mu(q).

(4.7)

Combining (
4.7
) with (
4.5
), we conclude the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

As
Q\to\infty
,

\textup{RH holds}\iff\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,q\textup{ odd}\\
\frac{a}{q}\in\mathfrak{F}_{Q}\end{subarray}}\chi(aq)\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi a}{2q}\right)=O_{\epsilon}(Q^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}),

(4.8)

for each
\epsilon>0
.

The truth of (
4.4
) involves cancellations of
\sin\left(2\pi a/q\right)
and
\cos\left(2\pi a/q\right)
. However, in (
4.8
),
\sin\left(\pi a/(2q)\right)>0
. Thus, the required cancellations in (
4.8
) arise from the changes of sign of
\chi(aq)
.

Theorem
4.2
gives a criterion for the Riemann Hypothesis to hold, but, as a point of contrast, the coefficients
\chi(aq)
of the Dirichlet
L
-function,
L(s)
:=
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)n^{-s}
,
\text{Re}(s)>0
, with its own Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, appear.

Theorem
3.1
has some further
applications, which are not pursued in the present paper. Such
applications provide new connections between the Riemann Hypothesis and the
distribution of Farey fractions with parity constraints in the numerators and denominators
[
6
]
.

H. Edwards’s book
[
11
, pp. 263–267]
gives a detailed discussion of the Franel–Landau criterion. With a plethora of references, the comprehensive survey
[
10
]
by C. Cobeli and the third author provides accounts of many related problems.

Proof.

Definition 7.5

If

and

are relatively prime positive integers, then the Dedekind sum

is defined by

s(h,k):=\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\Big(\Big(\frac{n}{k}\Big)\Big)\Big(\Big(\frac{hn}{k}\Big)\Big).

The Dedekind sums satisfy a famous reciprocity theorem
[

. If

s(h,k)+s(k,h)=-\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{12}\left(\dfrac{h}{k}+\dfrac{1}{hk}+\dfrac{k}{h}\right).

Moreover,
s(h,k)
has the representation
[

, p. 26]

s(h,k)=\dfrac{1}{4k}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}\cot\Big(\dfrac{\pi n}{k}\Big)\cot\Big(\dfrac{\pi hn}{k}\Big).

From Theorems
7.3
and

and

\displaystyle\qquad+pq\sum_{n=1}^{\mu-1}\cot\Big(\frac{\pi np}{\mu}\Big)\cot\Big(\frac{\pi nq}{\mu}\Big)=\frac{p^{2}+q^{2}+\mu^{2}}{3}-pq\mu.

(Theorem 2.14 in

)

\displaystyle\quad=\mu\left(\frac{2}{{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k\mu}+1}+\frac{2}{\xi_{\beta}^{h\mu}+1}-1\right)-4\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1)(\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1)}.

Proof.

Analogously, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\tan\left(k\Big(\frac{j}{\mu}+\frac{\alpha}{h}\Big)\pi\right)\tan\left(h\Big(\frac{j}{k}+\frac{\beta}{k}\Big)\pi\right)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\frac{(\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}-1)}{(\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1)}\frac{(\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}-1)}{(\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1)}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1}\right)\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\left\{1-\frac{2}{\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1}-\frac{2}{\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1}+\frac{4}{(\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1)(\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1)}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\mu\left(1-\frac{2}{{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k\mu}+1}-\frac{2}{\xi_{\beta}^{h\mu}+1}\right)-4\sum_{j=1}^{\mu}\frac{1}{(\nu_{j}^{k}{\omega}_{\alpha}^{k}+1)(\nu_{j}^{h}\xi_{\beta}^{h}+1)},

where we employed (
7.26
) for the last equality.
∎
