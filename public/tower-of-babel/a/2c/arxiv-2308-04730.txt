State-dependent Delay Differential Equations on H 1 H^{1}
Johanna Frohberg and Marcus Waurick

Abstract
Abstract Classically, solution theories for state-dependent delay equations are developed in spaces of continuous or continuously differentiable functions. The former can be technically challenging to apply in as much as suitably Lipschitz continuous extensions of mappings onto the space of continuous functions are required; whereas the latter approach leads to restrictions on the class of initial pre-histories. Here, we establish a solution theory for state-dependent delay equations for arbitrary Lipschitz continuous pre-histories and suitably Lipschitz continuous right-hand sides on the Sobolev space H 1 H^{1} . The provided solution theory is independent of previous ones and is based on the contraction mapping principle on exponentially weighted spaces. In particular, initial pre-histories are not required to belong to solution manifolds and the generality of the approach permits the consideration of a large class of functional differential equations even for which the continuity of the right-hand side has constraints on the derivative.



Keywords
State-dependent delay equations, functional differential equation, weak solutions

Introduction

State-dependent delay equations are delay equations where the retarded argument of the right-hand side is dependent on the solution itself. This fact necessitates a different solution theory compared to other (delay) differential equations.

There are several approaches and notions to address unique existence of state-dependent delay equations in the literature. In order to sketch their main properties, we consider the following particular state-dependent delay equation. Note that in Section
4
, we will provide a solution theory for Lipschitz continuous solutions for equations of the form (
1
).

Given
h,T>0
and a Lipschitz continuous pre-history
\phi\colon(-h,0)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}(t)=g(t,x(t),x(t+r(x_{t}))),&t\in(0,T);\\
x_{0}=\phi,\end{cases}

(1)

where we used the usual notation
x_{t}(s)\coloneqq x(t+s)
;
r
, taking values in
[-h,0]
, is a suitable delay functional defined on a set of functions defined on
[-h,0]
and
g\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous and Lipschitz continuous uniformly in
t
; that is, there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
u,v,w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\|g(t,u,w)-g(t,v,z)\|\leq L(\|u-v\|+\|w-z\|).

If
r
was constant, the equation at hand is a (state-independent) delay differential equation, which can be dealt with in a well-known straightforward manner. Indeed, integrating the equation, a fixed point of the integral equation is a solution of the differential equation. The contraction mapping principle can be employed either using a weighted norm or by a suitable time-stepping method analysing the Picard iterates, see, e.g.,
[
16
,
24
]
,
[
25
, Chapter 4]
.

The theory is somewhat less elementary if one considers non-constant
r
. In this situation, one needs to involve also the derivative of
x
: a counter-example for uniqueness in spaces of continuous functions is already given in
[
29
]
. We shall sketch a technical reason for the involvement of the derivative of the unknown in any fixed point statement. If one wants to use a similar approach as in the
r
-is-constant case, formal integration of (
1
) (assuming everything is well-defined) leads to

x(t)=\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s,x(s),x(s+r(x_{s})))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then, a possible map
\Phi
for any fixed point argument reads

\Phi\colon x\mapsto\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(s,x(s),x(s+r(x_{s})))\,\mathrm{d}s.

In order for
\Phi
to be contractive on some function space and at the same time using Lipschitz continuity of
g
, one is required to estimate a term of the following form

\int_{0}^{t}\|x(s+r(x_{s})-y(s+r(y_{s})\|\,\mathrm{d}s\\
\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|x(s+r(x_{s})-y(s+r(x_{s})\|\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\|y(s+r(x_{s})-y(s+r(y_{s})\|\,\mathrm{d}s.

The first term on the right-hand side can easily be dealt with in several function spaces and associated norms, the second term requires knowledge of the size of the derivative of
y

a priori
. Besides, note that in certain cases, the knowledge of the size of the derivative is also necessary for suitable Lipschitz estimates for
r
, see
[
26
, Section 3]
or Section
6.2
below.

There are several ways in the literature of addressing well-posedness of state-dependent delay equations. For a general introduction to delay and so-called functional differential equations, we refer to
[
17
,
7
]
. We note in passing that further generalisations of functional differential equations to infinite-dimensional state spaces are structurally more complicated, see, e.g.,
[

]
. In any case, concerning state-dependent delay equations, we refer to the fundamental contributions
[
13
,
26
]
for many examples and a guide to the literature. In order to position our approach to the ones in the literature, we briefly mention four main sources:

(i)

the solution manifold approach introduced by
[
26
]
,

(ii)

the retraction approach introduced at least in
[
18
]
and used quite recently in
[
4
]
,

(iii)

the mixed norm approach in
[

]
, and

(iv)

the prolongation approach in
[

,

]
.

In (i) and (ii), the equation (
1
) is considered as functional differential equation. In
[
26
]
solutions in the space of continuously differentiable functions are looked out for. As a consequence, the equation itself dictates a necessary condition for the initial value namely that
\phi^{\prime}(0)=g(0,\phi(0),\phi(r(\phi)))
(see also
[
28
]
). This condition defines a submanifold
X_{g}
of
C^{1}
—the so-called solution manifold. It can be shown that given suitable differentiability assumptions on the right-hand side, well-posedness for the equation in question follows. Moreover, in this situation, solutions starting in
X_{g}
can be differentiated with respect to initial data (
[
27
]
) allowing for a principle of linearised stability. Ultimately, the well-posedness theorem is based on the contraction mapping principle in
C^{1}
with a sensible analysis of the constants involved. In applications, even though it is often only based on identifying the particular form of the derivative, to check the individual conditions can be quite technical, see, e.g.,
[
9
]
.

The approach in
[
18
]
is based on an existence result in
[
12
, Theorem 2.2.1]
(which in turn uses Schauder’s fixed point theorem) and an a posteriori uniqueness analysis for ‘almost locally Lipschitz continuous functionals’, i.e., functionals that are locally Lipschitz continuous with respect to the uniform norm if additionally the set of functions is constrained to be Lipschitz continuous. Hence, the underlying space for unique existence is the space of continuous functions subject to a Lipschitz condition. Identifying certain continuity conditions for the right-hand sides only on a specific set of continuous functions, the general theory is applied using certain (Lipschitz) retractions; that is, (Lipschitz) continuous mappings defined on the whole of
C
projecting onto the said specific set of functions. Given that the underlying space is a space of continuous functions such retractions need to be considered specifically for each set in turn. We particularly refer to the convex and compact subset of
C
constructed in
[
11
, Section 5]
, that is not a Lipschitz retract; see also Section
7
for more technicalities arising in the retraction approach for continuous functions. In any case, using Lipschitz continuous functions as potential solutions only, one realises that the condition to lie on the solution manifold need not be met here (let alone that under the general assumptions considered in
[
18
]
the mere existence of the manifold is in question). The argument in
[
4
]
for existence of solutions for all times requires arguments involving positivity.

The approach in (iii) was developed in order to study differentiability in certain Sobolev type norms. Similar to the rationale developed here—in
[
14
]
, a contraction mapping principle is applied namely a variation of the so-called Uniform Contraction Principle. The norms studied are mixed norm type spaces looking at the Lipschitz norm of the initial pre-history combined with a
W^{1,p}
-norm for the solution for positive times. Note that the contraction principle can be applied, if the final time horizon,
T
, is chosen small enough, see
[
14
, Lemma 4.2]
.

In (iv), the author addresses well-posedness on a more fundamental level introducing the concept of prolongations. This leads to sufficient and necessary conditions for well-posedness, see
[
21
]
. Also the role of a potential jump in the continuity of the derivative is considered. In any case, however, the pre-history space is a space of continuous or continuously differentiable functions and the contraction mapping theorem is involved using a time-stepping approach for the integrated differential equation and metrics related to the uniform norm (possibly combined with its derivative), see
[
21
, p 3515]
or
[
22
, Proof of Lemma 5.11]
.

In this note we develop a solution theory of equations of the type in (
1
) using spaces of weakly differentiable functions; more precisely
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
; that is, the space of square integrable functions with square integrable derivative. More so, we will provide a generalisation of (
1
) towards (non-autonomous)
functional differential equations
on
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(i.e., where the right-hand side of (
1
) is not only given as
delay differential equation
with some
g
and some delay functional
r
, but where the right-hand side is assumed to be explicitly time-dependent and generally dependent on
x_{t}
as
G
below in Theorem
1.1
). Similar to
[
4
,
18
]
, we have to suitably restrict to Lipschitz continuous functions for the initial data.

There are no other restrictions however and we obtain global in time solutions for (
1
) right away. The theorem containing the solution theory spelled out for functional differential equations reads as follows (in Theorem
5.1
below we also state and prove a blow-up result). By
\|z\|_{\infty}
, we denote the essential supremum of a function
z
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
h,T>0
,
G\colon[0,T]\times H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
continuous. Assume that for all
\beta>0
there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty},\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\beta
and
t\in[0,T]

\|G(t,\phi)-G(t,\psi)\|\leq L\|\phi-\psi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Then for all Lipschitz continuous
\phi\colon(-h,0)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists
0<T_{1}\leq T
and a unique solution
x\in H^{1}(-h,T_{1};\mathbb{R}^{n})
of

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}(t)=G(t,x_{t}),&(0<t<T_{1}),\\
x_{(0)}=\phi.\end{cases}

It follows that
x
is Lipschitz continuous.

Already for (
1
), by means of a counterexample we show that the space
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the initial data to lie in is too big warranting well-posedness. Our approach here is based on exponentially weighted spaces and is inspired by Morgenstern’s proof for the classical Picard–Lindelöf theorem using an exponentially weighted norm for the space of continuous functions. Exponentially weighted
L_{2}
-type spaces are natural in the context of partial differential equations (see
[
23
,
25
]
) and can successfully be applied to ordinary (delay) differential equations (see
[
16
,
24
,
25
]
). Note that in these references the time-derivative is established as a continuously invertible linear operator; in fact this is also some part of the reason why the present approach works. However, we shall suppress this viewpoint in the following in the interest of readability. In contrast to the approach (iii) (
[
14
]
) from above, the Lipschitz continuity of the solution is not built in in the underlying space the contraction mapping principle is applied to, but rather a consequence of the regularity properties of the equation. Moreover, the exponential weights permit the consideration of the whole final time horizon right away and no time-stepping is needed. We note in passing that the mixed norm space in
[
14
]
is no Hilbert space.

Using the Hilbert space
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
instead, we are in the position to make use of the projection theorem. This particularly proves useful when we employ retraction techniques to extend (Lipschitz) continuous functions on
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as every closed convex subset
C\subseteq H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a retract of
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
; the retraction is given by the metric projection onto
C
,
P_{C}
, see in particular Proposition
3.3
below. This helps to show that Theorem
1.1
also contains a solution theory for right-hand sides initially given on certain subsets only. Indeed, some particulars of this are presented in Proposition
6.1
. In this proposition, we show that the functional differential equations guaranteeing unique local existence as considered in
[
18
,
4
]
provide a particular case of the solution theory spelled out here: Using
P_{C}
, we can always extend a suitably Lipschitz continuous functional to the whole of
H^{1}
and then apply Theorem
1.1
. We emphasise that Kirszbraun’s theorem, see
[
15
]
, even asserts that given a Lipschitz continuous
f\colon\operatorname{dom}(f)\to H_{2}
,
H_{1},H_{2}
Hilbert spaces, defined on
any
subset
\operatorname{dom}(f)\subseteq H_{1}
can be Lipschitz continuously extended to the whole of
H_{1}
; hinting at the flexibility allowed for in the present Hilbert space approach.

Concerning our proof strategy of Theorem
1.1
, Proposition
3.3
is in fact applied to
V_{\beta}\subseteq H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
the set of weakly differentiable functions with derivative bounded by
\beta
. This will be used to identify the evaluation functional defined on
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[-h,0]
suitably composed with the delay functional
r
as a Lipschitz continuous mapping on
V_{\beta}
(see Theorem
3.2
). The existence of solutions up to the final time
T
for the more particular equations of the type (
1
) is then guaranteed with a contradiction argument and a blow-up technique.

Another technical advantage of
H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(or rather
L_{2}
) is that the pre-history mapping assigning to each function
x
the pre-histories
s\mapsto x_{s}
can be realised as a Lipschitz continuous mapping with arbitrarily small norm given the considered norm is suitably (exponentially) weighted, see Theorem
2.2
or
[
16
]
. This observation already proved useful in the context of neutral (delay) differential equations, see
[

, Section 5.3.3]
. Note that an analogous property for the space of continuous functions is not true, see
[
24
, Remark 4.15]
. Since
H^{1}
embeds continuously into the space of continuous functions, the presented approach lies somewhat inbetween the retraction approach in
[
4
]
and the
C^{1}
-technique in
[
26
]
. Note that, however, similar to the results presented in
[
4
]
, the developed assumptions are easily applicable and more often than not boil down to routine arguments.

We remark that the solution theory developed here is independent of previous solution results in the literature and is based on the contraction mapping principle on
H^{1}
combined with a retraction technique valid for Hilbert spaces.
We emphasise that the present approach addresses the non-autonomous case right away and the assumptions are formulated in such a way that the well-known (non-autonomous) Picard–Lindelöf theorem for ODEs is a simple corollary of the theory developed here. Indeed, classical Picard–Lindelöf needs to be given an initial value only rather than an initial pre-history. Hence, the restriction to Lipschitz continuous pre-histories imposed in the well-posedness theorem here offers no constriction for the classical case.

Functional analytic prerequisites and the pre-history map

We use standard terminology for the function spaces involved and briefly recall the most important ones here, see, also, e.g.
[
25
]
. For this, let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a non-empty interval. For
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
L_{2,\rho}(I)
is the space of equivalence classes of (real-valued) square-integrable functions on
I
with respect to the measure with Lebesgue density
t\mapsto\exp(-2\rho t)
; and
H_{\rho}^{1}(I)
is the subspace of
L_{2,\rho}(I)
precisely containing those elements in
L_{2,\rho}(I)
that are once weakly differentiable with derivative in
L_{2,\rho}(I)
. For this, let
L_{1,\textnormal{loc}}(I)
be the space of equivalence classes of locally integrable functions. We call
f\in L_{1,\textnormal{loc}}(I)

weakly differentiable
if there exists
g\in L_{1,\textnormal{loc}}(I)
such that for all
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(I)
, the space of infinitely often continuously differentiable functions with compact support in
I
, we have

-\int_{I}f(t)\phi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{I}g(t)\phi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

in this case one can show that
g\in L_{1,\textnormal{loc}}(I)
is uniquely determined; we write
f^{\prime}\coloneqq g
and we set

\displaystyle H_{\textnormal{loc}}^{1}(I)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{f\in L_{2,\textnormal{loc}}(I);f\text{ weakly differentiable with }f^{\prime}\in L_{2,\textnormal{loc}}(I)\}\text{ and }

\displaystyle H_{\rho}^{1}(I)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{f\in H_{\textnormal{loc}}^{1}(I);f,f^{\prime}\in L_{2,\rho}(I)\}.

In case of
\rho=0
, we also use
H^{1}(I)
and
L_{2}(I)
, respectively. For
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

\langle x,y\rangle_{0,\rho}\coloneqq\int_{I}x(t)\cdot y(t)\exp(-2\rho t)dt\text{ and }\langle x,y\rangle_{1,\rho}\coloneqq\langle x,y\rangle_{0,\rho}+\langle x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\rangle_{0,\rho}

and, analogously, we define the norms via
\|x\|_{L_{2,\rho}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{0,\rho}}
and
\|x\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}}\coloneqq\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{1,\rho}}
, respectively. We mention that endowed with this scalar product
H_{\rho}^{1}(I)
is a Hilbert space. The corresponding Hilbert space of
X
-valued functions spaces will be denoted by
L_{2,\rho}(I;X)
, etc.
Note that by the following version of the Sobolev embedding theorem, every
f\in H_{\rho}^{1}(I)
has a continuous representative.

Throughout the manuscript, we shall almost always choose a continuous representative for such
f
(which then is easily seen to be unique) allowing for pointwise arguments.

Theorem 2.1

(Sobolev-embedding theorem)
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a non-empty interval. Then every
f\in H^{1}_{\textnormal{loc}}(I)
admits a continuous representative
\widetilde{f}
. If
f\in H^{1}(a,b)
for some
-\infty<a<b<\infty
, then

\|\widetilde{f}\|_{C[a,b]}\leq((b-a)^{1/2}+(b-a)^{-1/2})\|f\|_{H^{1}(a,b)}.

Proof.

The proof can be found in
[
2
, Theorem 4.9]
; see also the standard reference
[
1
, Theorem 4.12, Case A]
.
∎

Throughout the remainder of this section, we let
h>0
be our fixed pre-history horizon and
T\in(0,\infty]
be the final time considered in this section. In the next couple of lines, we want to define the pre-history map in the present
L_{2}
-type setting. For this recall that the mapping

C[-h,T]\ni f\mapsto(t\mapsto f_{t}\in C[-h,0])\in C([0,T];C[-h,0])

is well-defined. This mapping can be extended to the
L_{2}
-setting in the following sense. For a function
f
defined on an interval
I
, we denote by
[f]_{{\textnormal{a.e.}}}
the equivalence class of Lebesgue measurable functions almost everywhere equal to
f
on
I
. Recall that continuous functions with compact support in
I
,
C_{c}(I)
, are densely embedded into
L_{2}(I)
in the sense that
C_{c}(I)\to L_{2}(I),f\mapsto[f]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}
is one-to-one and has dense range. As a matter of jargon using a slight abuse of notation, for this dense embedding result, one usually just writes ‘
C_{c}(I)\subseteq L_{2}(I)
densely’.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\rho>0
. Then the mapping

\widetilde{\Theta}\colon C_{c}(-h,T)\subseteq L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)\to L_{2,\rho}(0,T,L_{2}(-h,0))

given by
\widetilde{\Theta}f\coloneqq[(0,T)\ni t\mapsto[f_{t}]_{{\textnormal{a.e.}}}]_{{\textnormal{a.e.}}}
is continuous and, thus, has a unique continuous extension denoted by
\Theta
satisfying the norm estimate

\|\Theta\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}.

Proof.

Let
f\in C_{c}(-h,T)
. Then we compute, using that the value of the Lebesgue integral does not depend on the individual Lebesgue measurable representative,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\Theta}f\|^{2}_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T;L_{2}(-h,0))}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{-h}^{0}|f(t+s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\exp(-2\rho t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-h}^{0}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t+s)|^{2}\exp(-2\rho t)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{0}\int_{0}^{T}|f(t+s)|^{2}\exp(-2\rho(t+s))\,\mathrm{d}t\exp(2\rho s)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{-h}^{0}\exp(2\rho s)\,\mathrm{d}s\|[f]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\rho}\|[f]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}^{2}.

This estimate confirms continuity of
\widetilde{\Theta}
. That densely defined continuous linear operators defined on a Banach space taking values in a possibly different Banach space can be uniquely extended to the whole of the first Banach space preserving its operator norm is a standard result, see, e.g.,
[
25
, Corollary 2.1.5]
.
∎

We call the thus defined mapping
\Theta
the
pre-history map
.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
\Theta
is linear and, thus, commutes with the weak derivative. Indeed, let
f\in H_{\rho}^{1}(-h,T)
and choose its continuous representative. Then, for
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(-h,0)
, which is extended by zero on the whole of
\mathbb{R}
, we compute for
t\in(0,T)
using that
(s\mapsto\phi(s-t))
is supported in
(-h,T)
and that
f
is weakly differentiable

\displaystyle-\int_{-h}^{0}((\Theta f)(t))(s)\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{-h}^{0}f(t+s)\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{-h+t}^{t}f(s)\phi^{\prime}(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s=-\int_{-h}^{T}f(s)\phi^{\prime}(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{T}f^{\prime}(s)\phi(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-h+t}^{t}f^{\prime}(s)\phi(s-t)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-h}^{0}((\Theta f^{\prime})(t))(s)\phi(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

confirming
(\Theta f)(t)^{\prime}=(\Theta f^{\prime})(t)
, where all derivatives are weak derivatives.
As a consequence, we have
for
f\in H_{\rho}^{1}(-h,T)

\displaystyle\|\Theta f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T,H^{1}(-h,0))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\|\Theta f(t)\|_{H^{1}(-h,0)}^{2}\exp(-2\rho t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\big(\|\Theta f(t)\|_{L_{2}(-h,0)}^{2}+\|(\Theta f(t))^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}(-h,0)}^{2}\big)\exp(-2\rho t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\|\Theta f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T,L_{2}(-h,0))}^{2}+\|t\mapsto((\Theta f)(t)^{\prime})\|_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T,L_{2}(-h,0))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Theta f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T,L_{2}(-h,0))}^{2}+\|\Theta(f^{\prime})\|_{L_{2,\rho}(0,T,L_{2}(-h,0))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\rho}\big(\|f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}^{2}+\|f^{\prime}\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}^{2}\big).

Hence,
for

\hat{\Theta}\colon H_{\rho}^{1}(-h,T)\to L_{2,\rho}(0,T,H^{1}(-h,0))

given by
f\mapsto\Theta f
we have

\|\hat{\Theta}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\rho}}

For
f\in C[-h,T]
and
t\in[0,T]
, we get
(s\mapsto f(t+s))\in C[-h,0]
. Thus, similar to the reasoning for the pre-history map, for
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,
\widetilde{\tau}_{t}\colon C_{c}[-h,T]\subseteq L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)\to L_{2}(-h,0)
given by
\widetilde{\tau}_{t}f\coloneqq[(s\mapsto f(t+s))]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}
is linear and continuous. Thus, it admits a unique continuous extension, which we denote by
\tau_{t}
. Then a different representation of
\Theta
is

\Theta f=(t\mapsto\tau_{t}f).

Indeed, the equality is readily confirmed for
f\in C_{c}(-h,T)
. Uniqueness of the continuous extension of densely defined bounded linear operators thus implies equality on the whole of
L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)
.
This perspective helps us to prove the next observation concerning the pre-history map. We introduce the space of
L_{2}(-h,0)
-valued continuous functions
C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))
endowed with the norm given by
\|F\|_{\rho}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}\|F(t)\|_{L_{2}(-h,0)}
for all
F\in C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for all
f\in L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)
,
\Theta f
has a unique representative in
C([0,T);L_{2}(-h,0)).

If, in addition,
T<\infty
, then
\Theta f
has a unique representative in
C([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))
and
\Theta
induces a map

L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)\to C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0)),

which is continuous with operator norm bounded by
1
.

Proof.

The proof rests on a standard approximation technique and similar results can be found in (almost) any basic text on
C_{0}
-semi-group theory. We provide the short arguments here. First of all, since for all
t\in(0,\infty)
, we have
L_{2,\rho}(-h,\infty)\hookrightarrow L_{2,\rho}(-h,t),f\mapsto f|_{(-h,t)}
continuously the case
T=\infty
readily follows from the case
T<\infty
. Hence, we may assume
T<\infty
in the following.

Let
f\in C[-h,T]
. Then
\tau_{t}f\to\tau_{s}f
in
L_{2,\rho}(-h,0)
as
t\to s
in
[0,T]
by continuity of
f
and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem.

Next, it is elementary to see that for each
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\|\tau_{t}f\|_{L_{2}(-h,0)}\leq\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}\|f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}.

Thus, dividing by
\mathrm{e}^{\rho t}
and computing the supremum on both sides with respect to
t\in[0,T]
, we get

\|(t\mapsto\tau_{t}f)\|_{\rho}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathrm{e}^{-\rho t}\|\tau_{t}f\|_{L_{2}(-h,0)}\leq\|f\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}.

Hence,

C[-h,T]\subseteq L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)\to C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0)),f\mapsto\Theta f

is continuous as an operator from (a subset of)
L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)
to
C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))
with operator norm bounded by
1
. Continuous extension by density of
C[-h,T]
in
L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)
shows the remaining assertions.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Similar to Theorem

, we can also spell out the corresponding continuity statement for

. More precisely, assuming

. Indeed, using

densely (in the sense of the embedding

as before). With Proposition

we estimate for

\|t\mapsto[\Theta f(t)]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}\|_{C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))}\leq\|[f]_{{\textnormal{a.e.}}}\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}

and

\|t\mapsto[\Theta f^{\prime}(t)]_{\textnormal{a.e.}}\|_{C_{\rho}([0,T];L_{2}(-h,0))}\leq\|[f^{\prime}]_{{\textnormal{a.e.}}}\|_{L_{2,\rho}(-h,T)}.

By Remark

,
\Theta f^{\prime}(t)=\Theta f(t)^{\prime}
for all

which implies that

.
In particular, for

3
A key estimate for the solution theory

In order to provide a solution theory for state-dependent delay equations, we need to derive a key estimate. This estimate will render the anticipated functional differential equation as ordinary differential equation with Lipschitz continuous right-hand side. For this, we introduce the
Lipschitz semi-norm
,
\|g\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}
for a mapping
g\colon X\to Y
between metric spaces
(X,d)
and
(Y,e)
given by

\|g\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\coloneqq\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}\in X,x_{1}\neq x_{2}}e(g(x_{1}),g(x_{2}))/d(x_{1},x_{2})\in[0,\infty].

Evidently,
g
is Lipschitz continuous if and only if
\|g\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}<\infty
.

For
\beta>0
, we introduce

V_{\beta}\coloneqq\{\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n});\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\beta\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the norm in
L_{\infty}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
where
h>0
.

Remark 3.1
.

V_{\beta}\subseteq H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is closed and convex. Indeed, convexity being clear; we briefly address closedness. Let
(\psi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
V_{\beta}
converging to some
\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In particular, by the definition of the norm in
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
(\psi_{k}^{\prime})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\psi^{\prime}
in
L_{2}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, by the Fischer–Riesz theorem, we may assume without loss of generality that
(\psi_{k}^{\prime})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges almost everywhere to
\psi^{\prime}
; hence the
L_{\infty}
-norm estimate is preserved.

In order to present the key ingredient warranting well-posedness of state-dependent delay equations, we reformulate said equation as a functional differential equation. For this, similarly to
[
26
]
, we introduce the evaluation mapping
\operatorname{ev}\colon H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\times[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\operatorname{ev}(\phi,t)\coloneqq\phi(t),

which is well-defined by Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
h,\beta>0
. Let both
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r\colon H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[-h,0]
be Lipschitz continuous.

Then
f\colon V_{\beta}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

f=g\circ\operatorname{ev}\circ(\operatorname{id}\times r)

satisfies

\|f\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\leq\|g\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}(2h^{1/2}+\beta\|r\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}+h^{-1/2}).

Proof.

We compute for
\phi,\psi\in V_{\beta}

\displaystyle\|f(\phi)-f(\psi)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\|g\circ\operatorname{ev}\circ(\operatorname{id}\times r)(\phi)-g\circ\operatorname{ev}\circ(\operatorname{id}\times r)(\psi)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\|\phi(r(\phi))-\psi(r(\psi))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

Focussing on the right-hand factor of the right-hand side, we further estimate

\displaystyle\|\phi(r(\phi))-\psi(r(\psi))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(0)-\phi(r(\phi))+\psi(0)-\psi(r(\psi))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}+\|\psi(0)-\phi(0)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\int_{r(\phi)}^{0}\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{r(\psi)}^{0}\psi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}+(h^{1/2}+h^{-1/2})\|\psi-\phi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})},

using Theorem
2.1
.
Assuming without restriction that
r(\phi)\leq r(\psi)
and using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we treat the integral terms next:

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{r(\phi)}^{0}\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{r(\psi)}^{0}\psi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|\int_{r(\psi)}^{0}\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{r(\psi)}^{0}\psi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}+\Big\|\int_{r(\phi)}^{r(\psi)}\phi^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\|1\|_{L_{2}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\phi^{\prime}-\psi^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+|r(\psi)-r(\phi)|\beta

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{h}\|\phi^{\prime}-\psi^{\prime}\|_{L_{2}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\beta\|r\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}\|\psi-\phi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Putting the above inequalities together, we conclude the proof.
∎

In applications, the delay functional
r
assigning the time at which the unknown is to be evaluated on the right-hand side might not be Lipschitz continuous on the whole of
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover,
f
as given in Theorem
3.2
is only Lipschitz continuous on
V_{\beta}
for all
\beta>0
. However, in all the cases we consider in Section
6
,

admits a Lipschitz continuous
extension
to
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and so does
f
from Theorem
3.2
. The reason for this is the Hilbert space structure of
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which provides the possibility of defining projections onto closed convex subsets. Further to the comments in the introduction, we mention that in general also the metric projection

4
Solution theory for state-dependent delay equations

Throughout this section, we let
h,T>0
. We emphasise that
T<\infty
. We aim to provide a solution theory for any Lipschitz continuous given pre-history
\phi:[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, of the following initial value problem on
(0,T)
,

\begin{cases}x^{\prime}(t)=g(t,x(t),x(t+r(x_{(t)}))),&t\in(0,T);\\
x_{0}=\phi.\end{cases}

(3)

The main assumptions for the equation just introduced are the following.

(H1)

g\colon[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous and uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to the spatial variables, i.e., there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
and
x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\|g(t,x,u)-g(t,y,v)\|\leq L(\|x-y\|+\|u-v\|).

(H2)

r\colon H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[-h,0]
is Lipschitz continuous.

We call
x\colon[-h,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a
solution of (
3
)
, if
x
is continuous and weakly differentiable on
(0,T)
and so that the first equation in (
3
) holds for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and
x|_{[-h,0]}=\phi
.

We aim to show the following theorem. In the following, for an interval
(a,b)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we say that
\psi\in L_{2}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
bounded
, if it is essentially bounded, that is,
\psi\in L_{\infty}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In consequence,
\phi\in H^{1}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
has a bounded derivative if and only if
\phi^{\prime}\in L_{\infty}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Theorem 4.1

(Picard–Lindelöf for SDDEs)
.

Let
g
and
r
be as in (H1) and (H2). Then for all
\phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded derivative, there exists a unique solution
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
3
).

Remark 4.2
.

(a) By the Sobolev embedding theorem, any
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous. This, however, does not require the derivative of
x
to be continuous—even if
x
is solution of (
3
). This has the following effect: the continuity of
t\mapsto\tau_{t}x
as an
L_{2}
(or
H^{1}
)-valued function (see Proposition
2.4
) forces the right-hand side of (
3
) to be continuous in
t
. Thus, as
t\to 0+
, the right-hand side converges to
g(0,\phi(0),\phi(r(\phi))
; the left-hand side does also converge; the limit, however, is
x^{\prime}(0+)
and is
a priori unrelated
to
\phi^{\prime}
. Therefore,
\phi
does not ‘see’ the solution manifold. As a consequence, in Theorem
4.1
there is no restriction on the initial value other than its (bounded) weak differentiability. In particular, the solution manifold is not needed for the formulation of the theorem.

(b) It follows from the equation, that any solution
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
3
) satisfies
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq C([-h,T],\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
x|_{[0,T]}\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence, if
T>h
and
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
solves (
3
) for some
\phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded derivative; then
x(h+\cdot)
belongs to the solution manifold as introduced in
[
26
]
.

(c) A special case of Theorem
4.1
is the classical form of the theorem of Picard–Lindelöf, see e.g.
[
25
, Theorem 4.2.6 (and proof)]
.

(d) Let
\phi\colon(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
-\infty<a<b<\infty
. Then
\phi\in H^{1}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded derivative if and only if
\phi
is Lipschitz continuous. Indeed, the ‘only if’-part is a straightforward consequence of integration by parts and a standard estimate for the integral. For the ‘if’-part note that Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
implies boundedness of
\phi
on
(a,b)
. Hence,
\phi\in L_{2}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By Rademacher’s theorem (see, e.g.,
[
8
, Section 3.1.2]
), the differential quotient of
\phi
exists at almost every point; by Lipschitz continuity, the differential quotient is bounded by
\|\phi\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}
. Hence, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem implies that
\phi
is weakly differentiable with derivative given by the almost everywhere limit of the difference quotients. As these are uniformly bounded, the weak derivative
\phi^{\prime}
is bounded and hence in
L_{2}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, eventually yielding
\phi\in H^{1}(a,b;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded derivative.

(e) A combination of the arguments in (a) and (d) shows that any solution of (
3
) is Lipschitz continuous. Indeed, for this it suffices to observe that
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto g(t,x(t),x(t+r(x_{t})))
is continuous and that the pre-history
\phi
is Lipschitz continuous by assumption.

The next (non-)example of Theorem
4.1
is a variant of the one provided in
[

]
. This example shows that the assumptions in Theorem

are sharp. We emphasise, however, that the considered equation does satisfy (H1) and (H2) and, thus, Lipschitz continuous pre-histories imply unique existence of solutions by Theorem

.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the state-dependent delay equation given by

\displaystyle-\int_{-h}^{T}\hat{\phi}(t)\psi^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

Remark 4.5

Next, we prove exponential bounds for any solution of (

Lemma 4.6

Since

\displaystyle\|x(t)\|

.

We apply our findings to a state-dependent delay differential equation with multiple delays.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with bounded derivative,
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}:H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[-h,0]
Lipschitz continuous. Let
g\colon[0,T]\times(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be continuous. Assume there exists
L\geq 0
such that for all
t\in[0,T]
,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{m},v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\|g(t,u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})-g(t,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

Then there exists a unique
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
x_{0}=\phi
and

x^{\prime}(t)=g(t,x(t+r_{1}(x_{t})),\ldots,x(t+r_{m}(x_{t})))\quad(t\in(0,T)).

Proof.

We apply Theorem
5.1
with

G\colon[0,T]\times H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}^{n},(t,\phi)\mapsto g(t,\phi(r_{1}(\phi)),\ldots,\phi(r_{m}(\phi))).

Then
G
is continuous as composition of continuous mappings. Moreover, let
\beta>0
. Then, if
\phi,\psi\in V_{\beta}
, by Theorem
3.2
applied to
g=\operatorname{id}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r=r_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we get

\|\phi(r_{j}(\phi))-\psi(r_{j}(\psi))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\leq(2h^{1/2}+\beta\|r_{j}\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}+h^{-1/2})\|\phi-\psi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Hence, for all
\phi,\psi\in V_{\beta}
we get for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle\|G(t,\phi)-G(t,\psi)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|\phi(r_{j}(\phi))-\psi(r_{j}(\psi))\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(L\sum_{j=1}^{m}(2h^{1/2}+\beta\|r_{j}\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}+h^{-1/2})\big)\|\phi-\psi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Thus,
G
is almost uniformly Lipschitz continuous. Note that for
x\in H^{1}(-h,T)
and
t\in[0,T]
, we have

G(t,x_{t})=g(t,x_{t}(r_{1}(x_{t})),\ldots,x_{t}(r_{m}(x_{t})))=g(t,x(t+r_{1}(x_{t})),\ldots,x(t+r_{m}(x_{t}))).

By Theorem
5.1
we find
T_{0}\leq T
chosen maximally and a unique
x
satisfying (FDE) for the particular
G
considered here. In order to prove the final claim,
x\in H^{1}(-h,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we use the alternatives for
x
defined on
(-h,T_{0})
with
T_{0}
maximal in Theorem
5.1
. Thus, it suffices to show that
\|x\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}<\infty
. For this, we assume by contradiction,
\|x\|_{\textnormal{Lip}}=\infty
. Using the (FDE), we get

\lim_{t\to T_{0}-}\|g(t,x_{t}(r_{1}(x_{t})),\ldots,x_{t}(r_{m}(x_{t})))\|=\lim_{t\to T_{0}-}\|G(t,x_{t})\|=\infty.

Since
g
is continuous and thus bounded on bounded sets we deduce that

\kappa(s)\coloneqq\max_{\tau\in[-h,s]}\|x(\tau)\|\to\infty

(11)

as
s\to T_{0}-
. Integrating the (FDE) on
[0,t]
for some
t\in(0,T_{0})
, we compute

\displaystyle\|x(t)\|

\displaystyle=\|\phi(0)+\int_{0}^{t}G(s,x_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}\|G(s,x_{s})\|\,\mathrm{d}s=\|\phi(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}\|g(s,x_{s}(r_{1}(x_{s})),\ldots,x_{s}(r_{m}(x_{s})))\|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\|\phi(0)\|+\int_{0}^{t}\|g(s,0,\ldots,0)\|\,\mathrm{d}s+L\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{t}\|x_{s}(r_{j}(x_{s}))\|\,\mathrm{d}s

where

. It follows that

and Gronwall’s inequality implies

Hence,

contradicting (

∎

Applications

In this section, we consider two different state-dependent delay equations. One example is an elementary problem from Newtonian mechanics and the other one is an application from mathematical biology. In either cases, we shall not go through the model assumptions in full detail as this section is merely meant to illustrate the versatility of the solution theory developed in the previous section. More so, we rather focus on the delay functional

in each application. We briefly sketch how the model assumptions can be relaxed to obtain a more general solution theory for the individual equation at hand.

.

Next, following

Theorem 6.2
.

The mapping

r\colon H^{1}(-h,0)\to[-h,0]

is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
\phi,\psi\in H^{1}(-h,0)
. We denote the corresponding solutions of the above ODEs determining
r(\phi)
and
r(\psi)
by
y_{\phi}
and
y_{\psi}
, respectively. Then by integrating the ODEs in question, we obtain

x_{1}=x_{2}-\int_{0}^{-r(\psi)}g(y_{\psi}(t),\psi(-t))\,\mathrm{d}t=x_{2}-\int_{0}^{-r(\phi)}g(y_{\phi}(t),\phi(-t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, from
r(\psi)<r(\phi)
(without restriction) we deduce from the assumption on

,

\displaystyle\varepsilon|r(\phi)-r(\psi)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-r(\phi)}^{-r(\psi)}g(y_{\phi}(t),\phi(-t))\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{-r(\psi)}(g(y_{\psi}(t),\psi(-t))-g(y_{\phi}(t),\phi(-t)))\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{h}|(g(y_{\psi}(t),\psi(-t))-g(y_{\phi}(t),\phi(-t)))|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{h}L|y_{\psi}(t)-y_{\phi}(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{h}L|\psi(-t)-\phi(-t)|\,\mathrm{d}t,

where

is the Lipschitz constant of

and

|y_{\psi}(t)-y_{\phi}(t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}L|\psi(-s)-\phi(-s)|\,\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{e}^{Lt}\leq L\sqrt{t}\mathrm{e}^{Lt}\|\phi-\psi\|_{L_{2}}

Thus, combining the estimates, we get, for some number

\varepsilon|r(\phi)-r(\psi)|\leq C\|\phi-\psi\|_{L_{2}}\leq C\|\phi-\psi\|_{H^{1}}

Remark 6.3
.

Note that the estimate derived in the proof of Theorem
6.2
is better than anticipated in the claim of the theorem. In fact,
r
is merely Lipschitz continuous under the
L_{2}
-norm rather than the
H^{1}
-norm. However, in order to guarantee existence of
r(\phi)
continuity of
\phi
is required. Thus, the domain of
r
is
H^{1}
, which is embedded into the space of continuous functions by Theorem
2.1
.

6.2
A positioning problem

The second illustration of our results is taken from
[
26
]
and describes a device positioned at the point
x(t)
on the real axis determining its position by emitting signals to a given reference point
-w<0
, where the signal with constant speed
c
is reflected. After some time,
s(t)
, depending on the position of the device, the signal is received again by the device. The time
s(t)
yields a position
\hat{x}
according to

\hat{x}=\tfrac{c}{2}s(t)-w,

this derived position being the actual position if
x(t)=0
. For the right-hand side this computed position is used for the acceleration
a(\hat{x})
. As in
[
26
, Section 3]
, we consider the given model only if
x
is in between
-w
and some a priori given
w_{+}>0
. For some Lipschitz continuous
a\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the set of equations we consider here is

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
v\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}v\\
-\mu v+a(s\tfrac{c}{2}-w)\end{pmatrix}\text{ with }cs(t)=x(t-s(t))+x(t)+2w.

for some fixed real numbers
c,\mu
.

As in
[
26
]
, we let
h\coloneqq(2w+2w_{+})/c
and obtain
0<s\leq h
assuming
x(t)<w_{+}
. Thus,
h
is our final time horizon (in negative direction). Next, we want to apply our main theorem (Theorem
4.1
) and the permanence principle (Theorem
4.12
) for obtaining a solution theory for all initial values
\phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})
with bounded derivative. In order to provide the proper setting, we define for
0<\alpha<\min\{c,w,w_{+}\}

W_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{\phi=(\xi,\eta)\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2});-w+\alpha\leq\xi\leq w_{+}-\alpha,|\xi^{\prime}(s)|\leq c-\alpha\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }s\in(-h,0)\}.

It is not difficult to see that
W_{\alpha}
is a closed and convex subset of
H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\phi=(\xi,\eta)\in W_{\alpha}
. Then there exists a unique
s_{\phi}\in[0,h]
such that

cs_{\phi}=\xi(-s_{\phi})+\xi(0)+2w.

Moreover,

W_{\alpha}\ni\phi\mapsto s_{\phi}\in[0,h]

is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
s,t\in[-h,0]
,
s<t
. Then by the fundamental theorem of calculus for
H^{1}
-functions, we obtain for
\phi=(\xi,\eta)\in W_{\alpha}

|\xi(s)-\xi(t)|\leq\int_{s}^{t}|\xi^{\prime}(\tau)|\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq(c-\alpha)|t-s|.

Thus,
[0,h]\ni s\mapsto\tfrac{1}{c}(\xi(-s)+\xi(0)+2w)
is a self-mapping strict contraction and thus admits a unique fixed point.
Similarly to the proof of the same property in
[
26
]
, we estimate for
\phi=(\xi,\eta),\psi=(\zeta,\chi)\in W_{\alpha}

\displaystyle c|s_{\phi}-s_{\psi}|

\displaystyle=|\xi(-s_{\phi})-\zeta(-s_{\psi})+\xi(0)-\zeta(0)|

\displaystyle\leq(c-\alpha)|s_{\phi}-s_{\psi}|+2\sup_{s\in[-h,0]}|\phi(s)-\psi(s)|.

Hence, by Theorem
2.1
,

|s_{\phi}-s_{\psi}|\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}(h^{1/2}+h^{-1/2})\|\phi-\psi\|_{H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})}.\qed

Next, with the projection
\pi_{\alpha}\colon H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})\to H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})
on
W_{\alpha}
and

r_{\alpha}\colon H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})\to[-h,0]

given by
r_{\alpha}(\phi)=s_{\pi_{\alpha}(\phi)}
, the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
are satisfied, for the right-hand side

G(t,(x,v)_{t})\coloneqq g(t,(x(t),v(t)),(x(t+r_{\alpha}((x,v)_{t})),v(t+r_{\alpha}((x,v)_{t}))))\\
=\begin{pmatrix}v(t)\\
-\mu v(t)+a(\frac{x(t+r_{\alpha}((x,v)_{t}))+x(t)}{2})\end{pmatrix}.

Indeed, (H1) is evident by the Lipschitz continuity of
a
; note that in
[
26
]
following up on a solution theory in
C^{1}
,
a
needed to be continuously differentiable. For the satisfaction of (H2), we need to find a Lipschitz estimate for the delay functional in terms of
x
on
V_{\beta}
. This is provided in Theorem
6.4
, which is a Sobolev regular variant of the similar result
[
26
, Proposition 8]
. We emphasise that we do not need a differentiability statement there.
By Theorem
6.4
for a Lipschitz continuous
\phi\in H^{1}(-h,0;\mathbb{R}^{2})
we obtain unique existence of
(x,v)
such that

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
v\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}=G(\cdot,(x,v)_{(\cdot)})\text{ subject to }(x,v)_{0}=\phi

If, in addition,
\phi\in W_{\alpha^{\prime}}
for some
0<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
, then by the permanence principle Theorem
4.12
, we deduce the existence of some
T>0
such that
(x,v)_{t}\in W_{\alpha}
for all
0\leq t\leq T
and, thus,
(x,v)
satisfies on
(0,T)

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
v\end{pmatrix}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}v(\cdot)\\
-\mu v(\cdot)+a\big(\frac{x(\cdot+s_{(x,v)_{(\cdot)}})+x(\cdot)}{2}\big)\end{pmatrix}.

Further comments on other approaches concerning functional differential equations

In this section, we fix
h,T>0
and consider a functional differential equation in a simplified form. For a scalar state
x
consider

x^{\prime}(t)=f(x_{t}),t\in[0,T],

(12)

subject to some initial pre-history
\phi\colon[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}
, where
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
with
f
defined on a subset
U
of a function space of functions from
[-h,0]
into
\mathbb{R}
.

(a) Let us point out the subtle difference of the functional differential equation in
[
4
,
18
]
and the one in Theorem
1.1
. In
[
18
]
, the existence of solutions was shown for continuous
G\colon C[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}
; uniqueness requires almost local Lipschitz continuity for
G
. The delay functional,
r
, considered in Theorem
6.4
is defined on Lipschitz continuous functions satisfying a certain bound for the functions itself and for the Lipschitz norm. Recall
V_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{x\in H^{1}(-h,0);|x^{\prime}(t)|\leq\alpha\text{ for a.e.\penalty\ }t\in(-h,0)\}
. In order to apply the retraction approach in the setting (
12
), where the continuity properties of the delay functional
r
are inherited by a suitable
f
, one is required to analyse, whether there exists a continuous extension of
f\colon V_{\alpha}\subseteq C[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}
to the whole of
C[-h,0]
given
f
is continuous. Since
V_{\alpha}
is a closed and convex subset of
C[-h,0]
it is possible to apply
[
5
, Proposition 1.19(i)]
to obtain the
retraction

p\colon C[-h,0]\to V_{\alpha}
(i.e., a continuous map such that
\rho|_{V_{\alpha}}=\operatorname{id}_{V_{\alpha}}
). Thus,
F\coloneqq f\circ p
extends
f
continuously to the whole of
C[-h,0]
. Whether this extension retains to be almost locally Lipschitz if
f
has the said property is a priori unclear. Note that for this, by
[
4
, Lemma 2.7]
, it suffices to show that
p
can be chosen to be (locally) Lipschitz. The quest is to show whether or not
p
can be chosen to be Lipschitz continuous.

In the following we describe possible arguments that are relevant to this problem.

(b) An argument in
[
18
]
shows that
C([-h,0];[A,B])
for some
-\infty<A<B<\infty
is a Lipschitz
retract
, that is, there exists a Lipschitz continuous retraction from
C[-h,0]
onto
C([-h,0];[A,B])
. Next, McShane’s Lemma shows
W_{1,0}\coloneqq\{f\in C[-h,0];\|f\|_{\text{Lip}}\leq 1,f(0)=0\}
is a Lipschitz retraction of
C[-h,0]
, see
[
19
]
. Indeed, for this, consider the mapping

E\colon C[-h,0]\to C[-h,0]\cap V_{1},f\mapsto\big(s\mapsto\min_{t\in[-h,0]}(f(t)-|s-t|)\big),

which is a Lipschitz retraction on the space of Lipschitz continuous functions with Lipschitz semi-norm bounded by
1
. The mapping
H\colon C[-h,0]\cap V_{1}\to W_{1,0},f\mapsto f-f(0)
is, in turn, Lipschitz continuous. Then
E\circ H
is the desired retraction.

(c) Whether or not
W_{\alpha}
from above is a Lipschitz retract of
C([-h,0];\mathbb{R}^{n})
does not follow from (b) and needs to be addressed separately. More generally, let
W\subseteq C[-h,0]
be closed and convex. Note that by the Sobolev embedding theorem, Theorem
2.1
, any closed and convex subset
W\subseteq C[-h,0]
yields a closed and convex subset
W\cap H^{1}(-h,0)\subseteq H^{1}(-h,0)
. Hence,
W\cap H^{1}(-h,0)
is a Lipschitz retract by Proposition
3.3
and any Lipschitz continuous mapping on
G\colon W\cap H^{1}(-h,0)\subseteq C[-h,0]\to\mathbb{R}
can be extended to a Lipschitz continuous mapping on the whole of
H^{1}(-h,0).

(d) By
[
5
, Theorem 1.6]
,
C[-h,0]
is an absolute Lipschitz retract, that is, for every metric space
M
containing
C[-h,0]
, there exists a Lipschitz continuous retraction from
M
onto
C[-h,0]
.
W
from (c) is a Lipschitz retract of
C[-h,0]
if and only if
W
is a so-called absolute Lipschitz retract, see
[
5
, Proposition 1.2 or subsequent remark (ii)]
. In fact, this puts one into the situation of
[
11
]
, where it was shown that if
W
is additionally a compact subset of
C[-h,0]
then such sets are so-called absolute uniform retracts, still a weaker property than being an absolute Lipschitz retract. Whether or not (a closed, compact)
W
is an absolute Lipschitz retract apparently needs to be addressed for each set separately as there does not seem to be a general answer, see in particular the counterexample in
[
11
, Section 5]
.

8
Conclusion

We have developed a solution theory for state-dependent delay equations (and in passing) for a certain class of functional differential equations that require Lipschitz continuity assumptions only. The assumptions are easily verified in practice and do not involve potentially complicated computations and analysis of certain derivatives. The underlying Hilbert space structure permits localisation techniques using the projection theorem so that many equations can be dealt with on the whole domain. The concept of a solution manifold is not needed for existence and uniqueness of solutions to differential equations with state-dependent delay. Being an element of the solution manifold for the initial value is a mere requirement for continuously differentiable solutions. Solutions starting off of the solution manifold will eventually end up on the solution manifold if the solution exists for a long enough time. Other approaches not using the solution manifold are based on compactness results, Schauder’s fixed point theorem and a retraction technique. Compactness results or Schauder’s fixed point theorem are not required in the present approach. Moreover, by Kirszbraun’s Theorem, see
[
15
]
, a retraction approach seems to be more widely applicable in the Hilbert space framework developed in this manuscript.
