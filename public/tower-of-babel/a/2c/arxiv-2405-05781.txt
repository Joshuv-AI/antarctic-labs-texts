Nonparametric estimation of a future entry time distribution given the knowledge of a past state occupation in a progressive multistate model with current status data
Samuel Anyaso-Samuel, Somnath Datta

Abstract
Case-I interval-censored (current status) data from multistate systems are often encountered in cancer and other epidemiological studies. In this article, we focus on the problem of estimating state entry distribution and occupation probabilities, contingent on a preceding state occupation. This endeavor is particularly complex owing to the inherent challenge of the unavailability of directly observed counts of individuals at risk of transitioning from a state, due to the cross-sectional nature of the data. We propose two nonparametric approaches, one using the fractional at-risk set approach recently adopted in the right-censoring framework and the other a new estimator based on the ratio of marginal state occupation probabilities. Both estimation approaches utilize innovative applications of concepts from the competing risks paradigm. The finite-sample behavior of the proposed estimators is studied via extensive simulation studies where we show that the estimators based on severely censored current status data have good performance when compared with those based on complete data. We demonstrate the application of the two methods to analyze data from patients diagnosed with breast cancer.



1
Introduction

Multistate models are a popular type of survival model that allows for studying the progression of the disease using multivariate time-to-event data. In biomedical applications, situations arise in which patients progress reversibly or irreversibly through a succession of disease stages that describe their current condition. The patients usually begin from an initial disease state and move through one or more intermediate states before entering a final absorbing state. The disease stages correspond to the states of the multistate system. Moreover, in certain applications, the transition of patients through their multistate system may be subject to a severe form of (case-I) interval censoring often referred to as current status censoring
(
Jewell and van der
Laan 2003
;
Sun 2006
)
. Under such an observation scheme, the researcher is only restricted to information on the state occupied by the patient at a (single) random inspection time during the entire study period.

Current status data from multistate models often arise in epidemiological and biomedical research, as well as in observational studies where continuous monitoring of study participants is not feasible. With event history data from a multistate system, practitioners are often interested in estimating the probability of occupying a given state, at a specific time.
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
proposed marginal estimators for state occupation probabilities following the product integration approach of
Aalen and
Johansen 1978
. Their nonparametric approach is valid without imposing the Markov assumption, and it has been further studied under different settings
(
Lan and Datta 2010
;
Lan
et al. 2017
;
Anyaso-Samuel et al. 2023
)
.

In progressive multistate systems, where individuals navigate through successive states within a disease process, a pertinent question arises: “What is the probability of occupying a future state
k
, given a prior visit to state
j
?” We denote this conditional probability as
\Psi_{k|j}
. Furthermore, interest may extend to studying the probability of reaching a future state
k
by time
t
, given a previous visit to state
j
, represented as
\Psi_{k|j}(t)
. Historically, these conditional probabilities have garnered less attention, as multistate systems are often assumed to adhere to Markovity. However, practitioners increasingly recognize the limitations of imposing a Markov assumption, which may be unverifiable and violated. Given a progressive multistate system, understanding the proportion of individuals on a unique path capable of occupying a future state becomes crucial. Our paper addresses this gap by focusing on the conditional estimation of future state occupation probabilities given prior occupation of a specific state, within the context of current status censoring.

In the context of right-censored data,
Yang
et al. 2023
recently proposed an estimator for the future state occupational probability conditional on a past state visit. Constructing such estimators is difficult since information on the transitions out of the non-absorbing states within the multistate system is only partially observed.
With current status data, the problem becomes even more challenging since the path that individuals would have taken after they were observed is unknown. To tackle this problem of estimating
\Psi_{k|j}
and
\Psi_{k|j}(t)
, we propose two estimation methods. The first is based on fractional at-risk sets. Here, we formulate the at-risk processes with weights defined by the fractional observation that a person contributes to the at-risk set of transitioning out of state
j
. We estimate this fractional weight as the probability that a patient will eventually reach state
j
given that they were occupying a known state at a random inspection time. The idea for utilizing fractional weighted at-risk sets follows from the seminal work of
Datta and
Satten 2000
and the work of
Yang
et al. 2023
. We begin the construction of the second estimator by providing insight that the conditional quantity
\Psi_{k|j}
can be expressed as the ratio of two marginal state occupation probabilities for multistate models with a tree structure. This leads to the formulation of an estimator based on product limit estimators of state occupation probabilities
(
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
)
. For both estimation methods, we do not impose the assumption that the multistate systems are Markov, but the methods require that the systems have a directed tree structure. Following our construction of estimators for the conditional future state occupation probabilities, we derive estimators for the distribution function of the state entry conditional on a prior occupation of a given state.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation along with marginal nonparametric estimators for current status multistate data. In Section
3
, we discuss the estimation of
\Psi_{k|j}
based on the fractional weight approach. The alternate estimator based on the product-limit method is discussed in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we describe the estimation of pointwise confidence intervals for the estimators based on both approaches. Detailed simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed estimators are presented in Section
6
. In Section
7
, we illustrate the application of both estimation methods in analyzing data from a breast cancer study. The paper concludes with a discussion in Section
8
.

2
Nonparametric estimators for a multistate system

2.1
Notation and Convention

Consider a multistate system with a directed tracking structure, where
\mathcal{S}=\{0,1,\ldots,Q\}
represents the finite state space. In this system, each individual
i=1,\ldots,n
occupies a state denoted by
S_{i}(t)\in\mathcal{S}
at time
t\geq 0
. Additionally,
C_{i}
stands for the random inspection time for individual
i
. For an individual, the observed current-status data is represented as the pair
\{C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i})\}
, where
C_{i}
is assumed to be independent of the multistate process
\{S_{i}(t),t\geq 0\}
, based on the random censoring assumption. To model the multistate current-status data, we adopt a framework where each state
j\in\mathcal{S}
can be reached from the initial state
0
through a unique path:
0=s_{1}\rightarrow s_{2}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow s_{(j)+1}=j
, where
(j)
denotes the number of steps it takes to reach
j
from the root node 0.

Let
U_{jj^{\prime}}
denote the (unobserved) transition time at which an individual transitions from
j
to
j^{\prime}
. To estimate temporal functions of the multistate system, two essential functionals are the counting process denoted by
N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
and the at-risk process denoted by
Y_{j}(t)
. The counting process
N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
counts the number of transitions from state
j
to
j^{\prime}
in the time interval
[0,t)
. On the other hand, the at-risk process
Y_{j}(t)
represents the number of individuals who are at risk of transitioning out of state
j
at time
t
. These processes are expressed as follows

\displaystyle N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathcal{I}(U_{i,jj^{\prime}}\leq t),\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle Y_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\mathcal{I}(S_{i}(t-)=j),

where
\mathcal{I}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function and
X(t-)
is the state occupied just before time
t
. By the law of large numbers,

\displaystyle n^{-1}N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)\xrightarrow{P}\text{Pr}(U_{jj^{\prime}}\leq t),

for any
t\geq 0
. Since the inspection time
C
is independent of the multistate process, we can write

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(U_{jj^{\prime}}\leq t)=\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{I}(U_{jj^{\prime}}\leq C)\ |\ C=t),

where
\mathcal{I}(U_{jj^{\prime}}\leq C)
is the indicator of the event that the transition from state
j
to
j^{\prime}
has taken place by time
C
.

Using the theory of nonparametric regression,
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
proposed nonparametric estimators of
N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
and
Y_{j}(t)
under this context of current status data. For
N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
, they proposed a two-step estimation approach where first, isotonic regression via the pooled adjacent violators (PAV) algorithm
(
Barlow et al. 1972
)
is performed based on the pairs
(C_{i},\ \mathcal{I}(U_{i,jj^{\prime}}\leq C_{i}))
. Let
\widehat{N}^{P}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
denote the fitted responses from the isotonic regression. Then secondly, kernel smoothing
(
Mukerjee 1988
;
Nadaraya 1964
;
Watson 1964
)
with a log-concave density,
K>0
is performed to remove long flat parts of
\widehat{N}^{P}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
. The resulting estimator of
N_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\widehat{N}^{P}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)\times\frac{K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}{\sum^{n}_{i=1}K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}\right\},

(1)

where
K_{h}(\cdot)=h^{-1}K(\cdot/h)
with a bandwidth sequence
0<h=h(n)\downarrow 0
chosen by the criteria specified by
Wand and
Jones 1995
.
Moreover, to estimate
\text{Pr}(X(t-)=j)
, which represents the probability limit of
n^{-1}Y_{j}(t)
,
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
employed the kernel smoothing approach. Accordingly, the estimation of
Y_{j}(t)
is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{j}(t)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\mathcal{I}(S_{i}(C_{i})=j)\times\frac{K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}{n^{-1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}\right\},

(2)

where it is assumed that
S_{i}(C_{i}-)=S_{i}(C_{i})
with probability 1. The estimators outlined in (
1
) and (
2
) pave the way for feasible inference in multistate models with current status data. For example,
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
elaborates on the estimation of key marginal functionals, such as the transition probabilities and state occupation probabilities, employing the product limit approach described by
Andersen
et al. 1993
.

This paper primarily proposes estimators for future state occupation probabilities and state entry distributions conditioned on a prior state visit. We employ the methodology described above to estimate the counting process and at-risk set, which serves as the foundational framework for constructing our proposed conditional estimators.

i

X_{ij}
will likely be unknown, making estimating the future entry states difficult. For instance, if the

S_{i}(C_{i})=2
, then
\widehat{\phi}_{i1}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))=0
. For
S_{i}(C_{i})\in\{1,3,4\}

3.2
Extension to general multistate model with a tree structure

Next, we expand the explanation of the estimator for
\Psi_{k|j}=P(X_{k}=1~|~X_{j}=1)
to encompass a broader multistate model context. The estimator (based on fractional weights) of the probability of occupying state
k
given a prior visit to state
j
is denoted as
\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|j}
. The estimation of
{\Psi}_{k|j}
relies on the assumption that state
j
lies on the unique path connecting states
0
and
k
; otherwise,
{\Psi}_{k|j}=0
. Let
m
represent the number of transitions needed to move from state
0
to state
k
. Constructing the estimator for
\Psi_{k|j}
follows a recursive approach based on
m
.

When
m=1
, the computation of
\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|j}
involves using the Aalen-Johansen estimator for transition probabilities within a competing risks framework. On the other hand, when
m=2
, then, one can obtain
\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|j}
by following the procedure outlined in Section
3.1
. More generally, for any state
k
that is situated at a distance of
m
from state 0, the estimation of
{\Psi}_{k|j}
relies on the application of the chain rule of conditional probability, given that the states of interest are aligned along a distinct path originating from the root node.

Following the application of the chain rule, for every state
j
on the path from state 0 to
k
, where
k
is a distance
m
from state 0; define

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|j}=\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{\tilde{k}|j}\cdot\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|\tilde{k}}

(6)

where
\tilde{k}
is the state just before state

0

\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{\tilde{k}|j}

\tilde{k}
is less than

. To construct the estimator
\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|\tilde{k}}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{[1]}_{k|\tilde{k}}=\int^{\infty}_{0}\widehat{S}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(s-)\frac{d\widehat{N}_{k|\tilde{k}}(s)}{\widehat{Y}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(s)}.

where

\displaystyle\widehat{S}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{(0,t)}\left[1-\frac{d\widehat{N}_{.|\tilde{k}}(v)}{\widehat{Y}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(v)}\right].

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{k|\tilde{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\frac{\widehat{N}^{P}_{\tilde{k}k}(t)K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}{\sum^{n}_{i=1}K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}\right\}\text{ and }\widehat{N}_{.|\tilde{k}}(t)=\sum_{k}\widehat{N}_{k|\tilde{k}}(t),

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\left\{\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}\times\frac{\mathcal{I}(S_{i}(C_{i})\in 0^{*})K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}{n^{-1}\sum^{n}_{i=1}K_{h}(C_{i}-t)}\right\},

where
\widehat{N}^{P}_{\tilde{k}k}(\cdot)
is obtained by the isotonic regression of the pairs
(C_{i},\ \mathcal{I}(U_{i,\tilde{k}k}\leq C_{i}))
. The estimator of the number of persons at risk in state
0^{*}

\widehat{Y}_{0^{*}|\tilde{k}}(t)

\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))

\tilde{k}
based on the information observed at their inspection time

. We interpret
\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))
as the contribution of the
i
th individual to the “at-risk” set of transitioning out of state
\tilde{k}
. If individual

\tilde{k}
at their inspection time, then,
\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))=1

\tilde{k}

\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))=0
. The estimation of
\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}(X_{i\tilde{k}}=1\ |\ S_{i}(C_{i})=\ell)

\widehat{\phi}_{i\tilde{k}}(C_{i},S_{i}(C_{i}))

4
Product limit estimators

We propose a novel estimator for estimating the conditional state occupation probability
\Psi_{k|j}
. This estimator is defined in terms of marginal state occupation probabilities. Due to the tree structure of the multistate system, any state will be reached from the initial state 0 by a unique path. Define
\mathcal{S}^{k}
as the set of states
\ell
such that state
k
is on the path from 0 to
\ell
. Then, the conditional quantity,
\Psi_{k|j}
, can be expressed as the ratio of two marginal functions given by

\displaystyle\Psi_{k|j}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(S(t)\in\mathcal{S}^{k})}{\mathbb{P}(S(\infty)\in\mathcal{S}^{j})}

(9)

where the numerator is the marginal occupation probability of occupying state
k
or any of the other states that come after
k
in the progressive system at time
t
, while the denominator is the marginal occupation probability of occupying state
j
or any of the other states that come after
j
in the system during an individual’s lifetime. We denote the resulting estimator defined by the ratio of marginal estimates of state occupation probabilities by
\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{k|j}
.
Since Equation (
9
) is a function of marginal state occupation probabilities, we begin with estimating these marginal probabilities by calculating the counting process and at-risk set using the method of
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
, described in Section
2.1
. Afterward, we obtain the Aalen-Johansen estimates of the marginal state occupation probabilities (see details below). Finally,
\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{k|j}
is obtained by the plug-in method.

The product-limit estimator of the marginal state occupation probability relies on the Nelson-Aalen estimate of the cumulative transition intensity, denoted as
\boldsymbol{A}(t)
. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}(t)=\{\widehat{A}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)\}
denote the corresponding estimator given by the expression:

\displaystyle\widehat{A}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)=\begin{cases}\int^{t}_{0}\mathcal{I}\Big(\widehat{Y}_{j}(u)>0\Big)\widehat{Y}_{j}(u)^{-1}\text{d}\widehat{N}_{jj^{\prime}}(u)&j\neq j^{\prime},\\
-\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}\widehat{A}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)&j=j^{\prime},\end{cases}

(10)

where
\widehat{N}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)
and
\widehat{Y}_{j}(t)
respectively denote the estimators of the counting process and the at-risk set (
ref:
Eqns (
1
)-(
2
)). The product limit of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}(t)
leads to the (marginal) Aalen-Johansen estimator of the transition probability matrix
{\boldsymbol{P}}(s,t)
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}(s,t)=\prod_{(s,t]}\{\boldsymbol{I}+\text{d}\widehat{\boldsymbol{A}}(u)\},

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{I}
denotes the
Q\times Q
identity matrix. Consequently, the marginal estimator of the state occupation probability is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\pi}_{j}(t)=\sum^{Q}_{j^{\prime}=1}\widehat{\pi}_{j^{\prime}}(0)\widehat{P}_{j^{\prime}j}(0,t),

(12)

where
\widehat{\pi}_{j^{\prime}}(0)=\{\widehat{Y}_{j^{\prime}}(0+)\}/\{\sum^{Q}_{j=1}\widehat{Y}_{j}(0+)\}
, and
\widehat{P}_{j^{\prime}j}(0,t)
is the
j^{\prime}j
th element of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}(0,t)
.
Datta and
Satten 2001
demonstrated that Equation (
12
) provides a consistent estimator for the state occupation probability, even in non-Markov models. This finding allows us to estimate the conditional state occupation probability without the Markov assumption using a product-limit approach similar to the Aalen-Johansen calculation.

Once we construct
\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{k|j}
using the product-limit approach, directly estimating the conditional entry distribution
F_{k|j}(t)
becomes straightforward by substituting
\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{k|j}
into Equation (
8
).

4.1
A toy example

We demonstrate the formulation of the new estimator using an illustrative multistate system (refer to Figure
4
) that can describe the progression of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). This system comprises seven distinct states representing various health conditions individuals may experience over time. Commencing from the healthy state (alive and event-free), which mirrors normal lung function, individuals might either die without any COPD events or transit to the mild COPD state upon the onset of mild airflow limitation typical in early-stage COPD. Subsequently, from the mild COPD state, individuals could either decease or progress to moderate COPD as their symptoms exacerbate, and airflow limitation intensifies. Advancing from the moderate COPD state, individuals could die or further deteriorate to severe COPD, typified by notable limitations in physical activity, frequent exacerbations, and a heightened risk of respiratory failure. Our focus lies in estimating the proportion of individuals that will reach the severe COPD state given a past occupation of the mild COPD state. This conditional probability is denoted by
\Psi_{5|1}
. Additionally, we will describe the estimation of
\Psi_{3|1}
and
\Psi_{5|3}
.

The expression in (
9
) leads to the respective estimators for these temporal functions:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{3|1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\pi}_{3^{*}}(t)}{\widehat{\pi}_{1^{*}}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(t)\in\{3,5,6\}\Big)}{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(\infty)\in\{1,3,4,5,6\}\Big)}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{5|3}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\pi}_{5^{*}}(t)}{\widehat{\pi}_{3^{*}}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(t)\in\{5\}\Big)}{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(\infty)\in\{3,5,6\}\Big)}

\displaystyle\widehat{\Psi}^{[2]}_{5|1}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\pi}_{5^{*}}(t)}{\widehat{\pi}_{1^{*}}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(t)\in\{5\}\Big)}{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}\Big(S(\infty)\in\{1,3,4,5,6\}\Big)}.

Let’s consider the estimators for
{\Psi}_{3|1}(t)
and
{\Psi}_{5|1}(t)
. For
\widehat{\Psi}_{3|1}(t)
, the numerator estimates the probability of occupying the artificial state
3^{*}
that comprises state 3 and other subsequent states along its path, at time
t
. Whereas, for
\widehat{\Psi}_{5|1}(t)
, the numerator estimates the occupation probability of state 5 at time
t
. The denominator of the respective estimators has the same form. Under the competing risk framework, the denominator simply estimates the transition probability of moving from state 0 to state 1 at some time during the observation period. In terms of state occupation probabilities, the denominator is the probability of occupying the artificial state
1^{*}
that comprises state 1 and other subsequent states along its path at some time during the observation period.

For a simple comparison of the new approach based on the ratio of product limit estimators (PLE, hereinafter) with the approach based on the fractional risk sets (FRE, hereinafter), Figure
3
shows the curves from the estimation of the probability of occupying state 5 conditional on a previous visit of state 1,
{\Psi}_{5|1}(t)
, using simulated data. The 95% pointwise confidence interval (see Section
5
) corresponding to each probability curve is also shown on the plots. Based on this simulated setting, the plots reveal the validity of both methods in estimating the true probability function. Moreso, the estimators are comparably similar. In Section
6
, we carry out a thorough investigation comparing the performance of both estimators.

\Psi_{5|1}
based on simulated data from the seven-state illness death model using the two proposed methods. The dark solid line represents the true probability curve, the dark dotted line represents the estimated probability and the grey dashed lines denote the confidence intervals.

5
Bootstrap-based Confidence Intervals

The utilization of nonparametric regression in conjunction with the PAV step for formulating the estimators proposed in Section
3
and
4
imposes challenges for the asymptotic analysis for these estimators. To quantify uncertainty, a bootstrap-based procedure may provide a practical alternative. However,
Faraway and
Jhun 1990
pointed out that a direct application of the bootstrap will generally lead to invalid results for the “smoothed” nonparametric estimators since it cannot capture the bias component of the smoothed estimator.

Following guidelines from existing work on the bootstrap for nonparametric regression estimators
(
Li and Datta 2001
;
Shao and Tu 1995
)
, we describe a smoothed bootstrap procedure for estimating the pointwise confidence intervals for our estimators. First, using random sampling with replacement, we generate bootstrap censored times
\{C^{*}_{i};\ 1,...,n\}
from the original data
\{C_{i};\ 1,...,n\}
. For the
i
th observation, we then generate the corresponding state
S^{*}_{i}(C^{*}_{i})
from the distribution given by

\displaystyle\text{Prob}(S=j)=\frac{\sum^{n}_{l=1}\mathcal{I}(S_{l}(C_{l})=j)K_{\tilde{h}}(C_{l}-C^{*}_{i})}{\sum^{n}_{l=1}K_{\tilde{h}}(C_{l}-C^{*}_{i})}.

In other words, we generate
S^{*}_{i}(C^{*}_{i})
from a smoothed distribution of the states whose inspection times were close to
C^{*}_{i}
. Following the theory of the smoothed bootstrap, the bandwidth
\tilde{h}=h^{\omega}
needs to be larger than the bandwidth
h
selected in the estimation based on the original data. We recommend setting
\omega=0.8
if
0<h<1
, and
\omega=1.2
if
h>1
.

For
0<\alpha<1
, let
\widehat{\Delta}_{\alpha}(t)
be the
(1-\alpha)
bootstrap percentile of the distribution of

\displaystyle\big|\sin^{-1}\big(\sqrt{\widehat{\theta}^{*}_{k|j}(t;h)}\big)-\sin^{-1}\big(\sqrt{\widehat{\theta}_{k|j}(t;\tilde{h})}\big)\big|

where
\widehat{\theta}^{*}_{k|j}(t;h)
is calculated using the bandwidth
h
from the original estimation but is based on the bootstrap sample, while
\widehat{\theta}_{k|j}(t;\tilde{h})
uses the new bandwidth
\tilde{h}
but is based on the original sample. The
(1-\alpha)\times 100\%
pointwise CI for the temporal function
\theta_{k|j}(t)
is given by

\displaystyle\Big[\sin^{2}\Big\{

\displaystyle\max\Big(0,\sin^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{\widehat{\theta}_{k|j}(t;h)}\Big)-\widehat{\Delta}_{1-\alpha/2}\Big)\Big\},

\displaystyle\quad\sin^{2}\Big\{\min\Big(\frac{\pi}{2},\sin^{-1}\Big(\sqrt{\widehat{\theta}_{k|j}(t;h)}\Big)+\widehat{\Delta}_{1-\alpha/2}\Big)\Big\}\Big].

The construction of the confidence interval described above is similar to
Datta and
Sundaram 2006
. In the subsequent section, we conduct a Monte Carlo study to examine the behavior of the smoothed bootstrap confidence intervals.

6
Simulation study

We performed extensive simulation studies to evaluate the performance of the proposed methods based on: (i) fractional risk sets (FRE), and (ii) the ratio of product limit estimators (PLE) in calculating the conditional estimators. We considered two separate multistate systems - the five-state illness-death model shown in Figure
1
, and the seven-state multistate model shown in Figure
4
. For each multistate system, the exact transition times were generated from a common lognormal hazard. We considered sample sizes of 100, 200, 500, and 1000.

6.1
Five-State Illness-Death Model

Earlier, in Section
3.1
, we introduced the five-state illness-death model. For our simulation experiment, our primary interest lies in estimating the probability of occupying state 3 having visited state 1, denoted by
\Psi_{3|1}
. We also study the performance of the estimators for the subdistribution function
\Psi_{3|1}(t)
, and the future entry distribution for state 3 given the prior occupation of state 1, denoted by
F_{3|1}(t)
. Our data simulation process initiates with each individual starting in state 0 at time 0, with potential transitions to either state 1 or state 2. Specifically, we simulate the scenario where 60% of individuals follow the
0\rightarrow 1
transition path, while the remaining 40% follow the
0\rightarrow 2
transition path. The transition out of state 0 is governed by an independent Bernoulli distribution. For those individuals transitioning to state 1, we further simulate their progression, with 60% transitioning to state 3 and 40% to state 4. This transition from state 1 is also controlled by a separate independent Bernoulli distribution. Following this simulation scheme, the true value of
\Psi_{3|1}
is 0.6.

Let
V_{i,0}
and
V_{i,1}
denote the respective waiting time in states 0 and 1, for individual
i
. We simulate:
V_{i,0}\sim\text{LogNorm}(0,0.5)
and
V_{i,1}\sim\text{LogNorm}(0,0.5)
. We study the performance of our proposed estimators under the scenarios where the random inspection time for each individual is generated from (i) the uniform distribution with a lower bound of 0 and an upper bound equal to the maximum waiting time observed across all individuals in the sample, and (ii) the Weibull distribution with (shape)
\eta=3
and (scale)
\tau=2.5
.

6.2
Seven-State Multistate Model

Here, we consider the more complex multistate system shown in Figure
4
. Primarily, we are interested in estimating the probability of occupying State 5 having visited State 1, denoted by
\Psi_{5|1}
. From Eqn
6
, we have
\Psi_{5|1}=\Psi_{3|1}\times\Psi_{5|3}
. In addition to studying the performance of the estimators for
\Psi_{5|1}
, we will also study the performance of the estimators for
\Psi_{5|3}
, along with the subdistribution functions;
\Psi_{5|1}(t)
and
\Psi_{5|3}(t)
, and the future entry distributions;
F_{5|1}(t)
, and
F_{5|3}(t)
. For the data simulation, all individuals start in state 0 at time 0, and they have two possible paths for transition: either to state 1 or state 2. We generate the data as follows: 80% of the individuals follow the
0\rightarrow 1
path, while the remaining 20% follow the
0\rightarrow 2
path. Among those who transitioned to state 1, there are two possible paths: 70% transition to state 3, and the remaining 30% transition to state 4. Moreover, among those that visited state 3, 60% proceeded to state 5, while the remaining 40% transitioned to state 6. Note that, the respective transitions out of state 0, state 1, and state 3 are controlled by three separate Bernoulli distributions. Following this simulation approach, the true value of
\Psi_{5|1}
is 0.6.

To explore the performance of our proposed estimators, we simulate individual waiting times in states 0 and 1 denoted by
V_{i,0}
and
V_{i,1}
respectively. We simulate:
V_{i,0}\sim\text{LogNorm}(0,0.5)
,
V_{i,1}\sim\text{LogNorm}(0,0.5)
, and
V_{i,3}\sim\text{LogNorm}(0,0.7)
. We consider two scenarios where we generate the random inspection time for each individual from (i) the uniform distribution with limits 0 and the maximum waiting time across the entire sample and, (ii) the Weibull distribution with
\eta=2.5
and
\tau=4.5
.

\Psi_{k|j}

\Psi_{k|j}(t)

\displaystyle\text{MAD}=\mathbb{E}\int|\widehat{\theta}^{\text{Emp}}(x)-\widehat{\theta}(x)|\ d\theta_{n}(x),

\widehat{\theta}

\widehat{\theta}^{\text{Emp}}

Discussion

This paper carefully employs techniques from the framework of competing risks to construct nonparametric estimators of future state entry probabilities and future state entry distributions, predicated on the prior occupation of a state, utilizing current status multistate data. The cross-sectional nature of the censored event history data brings about additional complexities when attempting to construct nonparametric estimates of temporal functions within a multistate model. We tackle this problem by harnessing ideas from nonparametric regression modeling to formulate estimators of the counting process and at-risk sets that serve as foundational elements in constructing estimators of the probability functions. To estimate the conditional quantities in the context of current status data, we employed a method (FRE) that was only available for right-censored data, and we also introduced a novel method (based on PLE). Using data from a real-world application and simulation studies for illustrative purposes, we show that both estimation methods yield valid and comparable results.

In practice, each estimation method has its benefits and challenges. Constructing the estimator based on the FRE approach could appear challenging since it requires the calculation of the fractional weights. Although the PLE method may be easier to implement once the artificial states have been specified correctly, the FRE method may be preferred for calculations involving distant states since it produces more reliable estimates for such states.

The intuitive approaches we propose provide a more direct route for analyzing the severely limited event data since they utilize the averaging principle rather than maximizing a likelihood function. Suppose we consider a less complicated setting (survival model with two states); the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (NPMLE) of the survival function has undesirable properties since it is undefined over a set of regions. For the general multistate model with current status data, an NPMLE approach would be more problematic.

We operate under the assumption of a progressive multistate system, structured as a directed tree, where each state is reachable via a unique path originating from the root node. This implies knowledge of path information from past states at a random inspection time, although the exact transition times remain unknown. While this assumption often holds due to the inherent structure of the multistate system, there are instances where distinctions among paths can be made by incorporating auxiliary states into an expanded multistate system. Additionally, a considerable number of inspections in a specific state are necessary to calculate the occupation probability of that state. In general, a relatively large sample size is required for valid nonparametric estimation of occupation probabilities, especially when dealing with current status data, where information is significantly limited compared to complete or right-censored data.

We could extend the proposed methods to multistate models with case-II interval-censored data by utilizing similar formulas for estimating the counting process and the at-risk sets. Since the estimators of these functionals are based on averaging, we could sum over the multiple inspection times for each individual. Ignoring the correlation among the inspection times will not inhibit the unbiasedness and consistency properties of the resulting estimators. However, the efficiency of the estimators could be improved by including a weighted variance-covariance matrix in the PAV step of the isotonic regression to account for the events recorded multiple times for each individual.

Further, researchers analyzing current status multistate data may also be interested in assessing the impact of measured risk factors on the probability of occupying a future state given a prior visit to a particular state. Regression modeling based on jackknife pseudo-values
(
Andersen
et al. 2003
)
presents a flexible approach for estimating covariate effects in event history analysis. Recently,
Anyaso-Samuel et al. 2023
extended the pseudo-value approach to model the effect of covariates on state occupation probabilities with multistate current status data. By jackknifing any of the estimators proposed here and utilizing the guidelines provided in
Anyaso-Samuel et al. 2023
, researchers can easily conduct covariate inference for the future state entry probabilities.
