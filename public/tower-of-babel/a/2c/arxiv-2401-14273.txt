Uniformly rotating vortices for the lake equation
Taoufik Hmidi, Haroune Houamed, Emeric Roulley, Mohamed Zerguine

Abstract
. We investigate the existence of time-periodic vortex patch solutions, in both simply and doubly-connected cases, for the two-dimensional lake equation where the depth function of the lake is assumed to be non-degenerate and radial. The proofs employ bifurcation techniques, where the most challenging steps are related to the regularity study of some nonlinear functionals and the spectral analysis of their linearized operators around Rankine type vortices. The main difficulties stem from the roughness and the implicit form of the Green function connecting the fluid vorticity and its stream function. We handle in part these issues by exploring the asymptotic structure of the solutions to the associated elliptic problem. As to the distribution of the spectrum, it is tackled by a fixed-point argument through a perturbative approach.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Aspects of interest

In this paper, we are interested in the study of time-periodic solutions of the lake equations, given by

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p=0,\\
\operatorname{div}(bv)=0,\end{cases}

supplemented with an initial datum.
In this set of equations, the space variable, denoted by
x
hereafter, takes values in the whole plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The time-independent function
b:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
describes the bottom topography (or depth function) of the lake, whereas the vector field
v
refers to the velocity field of the fluid. Finally, the scalar function
p
designates the pressure. Further assumptions on the coefficient
b
will be specified later on. The equations (
1.1
), describing balanced flow without gravity waves, are basically recovered from the shallow-water equations in an appropriate time-space scale limit
[
24
]
, see also
[
3
]
.

One of the most important features of the equations (
1.1
) is their equivalent vorticity formulation, which we recall next

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\omega}{b}\right)+v\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\omega}{b}\right)=0,\\
\operatorname{div}(bv)=0,\\
\omega=\operatorname{curl}v.\end{cases}

Note that the divergence free condition in (
1.2
) allows us to express
bv
as

(1.3)

bv=-\nabla^{\perp}\psi_{b},\qquad\nabla^{\perp}\overset{\rm def}{=}\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{x_{2}}\\
\partial_{x_{1}}\end{pmatrix},

where the potential
\psi_{b}
solves the elliptic problem

(1.4)

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla\psi_{b}}{b}\right)=\omega.

The lake equation, formulated as (
1.2
), has garnered considerable attention over the past few decades, resulting in numerous significant contributions from both analytical and numerical perspectives. Interested readers can find more comprehensive series of results on the well-posedness of (
1.1
) in
[
1
,
4
,
35
,
42
,
43
]
and the references therein. Most of these results are established in bounded domains, with or without island, under various assumptions on the depth function
b
. For additional insights and progress on specific types of solutions, such as point vortices and steady states, we refer to
[
6
,
15
,
16
,
34
,
44
,
50
]
.

It is worth noting that the vorticity reformulation (
1.2
) shares structural similarities with the classical Euler equations, governing the motion of an ideal incompressible fluid. This similarity is observed by setting
b\equiv 1
in the equations above. Additionally, under reasonable conditions on the depth function
b
, a Yudovich theory for the lake equation (
1.2
) can be established, akin to its known statement for the Euler equations
[
51
]
. As a consequence of such a theory, the structure of an initial patch

\omega|_{t=0}=b\mathds{1}_{D_{0}},

for any bounded domain
D_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, remains unchanged by the flow. More precisely, the solution associated with the preceding initial datum is given by

(1.5)

\omega(t,\cdot)=b\mathds{1}_{D_{t}},\qquad D_{t}=\Psi(t,D_{0}),

for all positive times
t\geq 0
, where
\Psi(t,\cdot)
is the flow of the log-Lipschitz velocity field
v
, defined as the unique solution of the integro-differential equation

\Psi(t,x)=x+\int_{0}^{t}v\big(\tau,\Psi(\tau,x)\big)d\tau,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In this paper, our focus extends to a specific subclass of patches where the transported domain
D_{t}
is exactly a rotation, by some angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
, of the initial domain, referred to as V-states. In other words, we are seeking solutions of the form (
1.5
), where

(1.6)

D_{t}=D_{0}e^{{\rm i}t\Omega},

for all times
t\geq 0
. It is important to note that, in general and conversely to the Eulerian case, the area of the transported patch may not be conserved. However, as a by-product of the specific ansatz (
1.6
), the area of the transported domain in that case remains unchanged by the flow.

Notably, in the case where the depth function is radially symmetric, one can prescribe a huge class of stationary solutions with radial shapes. Among these steady states, it is readily seen that the functions

\omega(t,\cdot)=b(|\cdot|)\mathds{1}_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;|x|<a\}},\quad a>0,

and

\omega(t,\cdot)=b(|\cdot|)\mathds{1}_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;a_{2}<|x|<a_{1}\}},\quad 0<a_{2}<a_{1},

are solutions of (
1.2
) for all
t\geq 0
.

Therefore, it is natural to seek time-periodic solutions in the vicinity of these steady states. The justification for the existence of such solutions is the central theme of this work, and further details on this will be provided later on.

Note that time-periodic (and time-quasi-periodic) solutions have previously been extensively studied and established for various fluid models. The initial discovery in this direction for the Euler equations, specifically for ellipses, dates back to Kirchhoff
[
41
]
. Subsequently, Deem and Zabusky
[
14
]
identified cases with higher symmetries through numerical simulations. Burbea
[
5
]
then proposed an analytical approach to rigorously justify their existence, based on local bifurcation theory. Specifically, for any
m
-fold symmetry,
m\geq 1
, there exists only one branch of V-states bifurcating from the Ranking vortices at the angular velocity
\Omega_{m}=\frac{m-1}{2m}
.

Similar structures for the doubly-connected case have been explored in
[
13
]
. In addition to that, several other properties of the V-states have been investigated for Euler equations, including questions about the regularity of the boundary
[
8
,
37
]
, the global bifurcation
[
31
]
and the existence of multipole vortex patches
[
19
,
20
,
27
,
33
,
36
]
. Further other aspects have also been addressed, such as the implementation of Nash–Moser scheme and KAM techniques to construct time-quasi-periodic solutions; see
[
2
,
29
,
32
]
.

Similar inquiries have been discussed about other active-scalar equations such as the generalized surface quasi-geostrophic equation (SQG)
[
12
,
25
,
26
,
28
,
39
,
46
]
, the quasi-geostrophic shallow-water equations
[
18
,
38
,
49
]
or the Euler-
\alpha
model
[
48
]
. A general approach generalizing the previous results in the simply-connected case has been obtained in
[
40
]
. More recently, bifurcation theory has been implemented for more complex coupled Hamiltonian systems, see
[
30
]
for an interconnected layers model arising in geophysical flows and
[
47
]
for the Vlasov–Poisson equation. At last, we also refer the interested reader to another set of results in a three-dimensional setting
[
21
,
23
,
22
]
.

1.2.
Main results

Before we get into the details of the main contribution of the paper, allow us to setup the basis of our assumptions on the bottom topography
b
as well as some notations to be constantly used thereafter.

Overall sufficient conditions on
b

The depth function
b
will be assumed to enjoy certain conditions which are consistent with the vortex-patch solutions of our interest throughout this paper, though they might not be optimal in some sense. The sufficient conditions on
b
are now listed below for the sake of clarity and convenience, even though they will not be required as all of them in every single result of the paper. More details of that will be discussed subsequently.

•

(Regularity) We assume that the function
b
enjoys the regularity

(Hb1)

b\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{R}).

•

(Positivity) We assume that
b
is positive, i.e.,

(Hb2)

b(x)>0,\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

•

(Constancy at infinity) There exists
R_{\infty}>0
and
b_{\infty}>0
such that

(Hb3)

b(x)=b_{\infty},\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B(0,R_{\infty}).

•

(Radial symmetry) There exists
\mathtt{b}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

(Hb4)

b(x)=\mathtt{b}(|x|),\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For a mere of simplicity, with an abuse of notation, we will keep using
b
instead of
\mathtt{b}
.

At last, we introduce the sequence

(1.7)

\Lambda_{n}(\alpha,\beta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(\alpha,\beta e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\cos(n\eta)d\eta,

for any given
\alpha,\beta>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

where
K_{b}
refers to the kernel associated with the elliptic equation (
1.4
), which will be defined and discussed with details in Section
2.2
.

The first main result of the paper concerns the existence of time-periodic solutions of (
1.2
) bifurcating from a given disc, which we state next.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a>0
and
b
be a function satisfying (
Hb1
), (
Hb2
), (
Hb3
) and (
Hb4
). Then, there is a positive constant
M(a,b)>0
such that, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with

m>M(a,b),

there exists a curve of
m
-fold symmetric simply-connected V-states for (
1.2
) bifurcating from the stationary solution

\omega=b\mathds{1}_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;|x|<a\}},

at the angular velocity

\Omega_{m}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\int_{0}^{a}\tau b(\tau)d\tau-\Lambda_{m}(a,a).

Remark 1.1
.

A precise expression of the constant
M(a,b)
is given in Proposition
3.1
, below. In particular, this constant is identically zero if
b
is a constant function. Moreover, it holds that

\lim_{\left\|b^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0}M(a,b)=0

and when
b\equiv 1
, that

M(a,b)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Omega_{m}=\frac{m-1}{2m},

for any integer
m\geq 1
. These values of the angular velocities correspond to the same values of Burbea’s result, previously established for Euler equations
[
5
]
.
Accordingly, Theorem
1.1
above establishes “sharp” existence of
m
-fold symmetric
V
-states for any
m\geq 1
(resp. large symmetries
m\gg 1
) if
\left\|b^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
is sufficiently small (resp. if
\left\|b^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
is arbitrary large).

The second main result of the paper establishes the existence of doubly-connected time-periodic solutions of (
1.2
) bifurcating from an annulus, which is the content of the next theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
0<a_{2}<a_{1}
and
b
be a function satisfying (
Hb1
), (
Hb2
), (
Hb3
) and (
Hb4
). Then, there is a positive constant
N(a_{1},a_{2},b)>0
such that, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
with

m>N(a_{1},a_{2},b),

there exist two curves of
m
-fold symmetric doubly-connected V-states bifurcating from the stationary solution of (
1.2
)

\omega=b\mathds{1}_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;a_{2}<|x|<a_{1}\}},

at the angular velocities

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}^{\pm}

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2a_{1}^{2}}\int_{a_{2}}^{a_{1}}\tau b(\tau)d\tau+\frac{\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})-\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle\quad\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}^{2}}\int_{a_{2}}^{a_{1}}\tau b(\tau)d\tau-\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)^{2}-4\Lambda_{m}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})}.

Remark 1.2
.

By setting
b\equiv 1
in Theorem
1.2
we recover the same values of the angular velocities for the Euler equations established in
[
13
]
. Moreover, in that case, the condition on
m
can be shown to align with the constraints on the same symmetry parameter derived from the results on the Euler equations
[
13
]
.

Comments about the assumptions on the depth function
b

It is apparent through the statements of our theorems above that the assumptions (
Hb1
)–(
Hb4
) are sufficient for the construction of time-periodic solutions of (
1.2
). However, we do not make any claim about their necessity and optimality. Though, let us briefly shed light on the relevance of these conditions to the mechanism underlying our methods and proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Further discussion, specifically focused on the elliptic problem (
1.4
), will be provided in Section
2
.

•

Condition (
Hb1
) is closely tied to defining the kernel associated with the elliptic equation (
1.4
). While it has been shown to be useful and has been employed in other papers on the lake equation
[
1
,
17
,
44
]
, it is not strictly required in many parts in the study of the elliptic problem (
1.4
). More precisely, it is particularly related to the decomposition proposed in
[
17
]
of the kernel
K_{b}
, see (
2.8
). Additionally, it is notably employed in the regularity analysis in Section
3.3
.

•

Condition (
Hb2
) was commonly imposed in various other papers
[
1
,
17
,
44
]
. Our proofs heavily rely on it and our approach possibly breaks-down otherwise. Allowing the depth function to degenerate, as in
[
4
,
35
]
, for example, would necessitate non-trivial changes in all the arguments presented in this paper. Therefore, it is currently unclear whether time-periodic solutions could be constructed if
b
vanishes on a non-empty set.

•

Condition (
Hb3
) was conjectured in
[
17
]
as sufficient to extend all the results therein from bounded domains to the whole plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
[
17
, open problem 2, page 1493]
). Additionally, our motivation for imposing this condition is justified by the objective of studying rotating solutions, whose construction is primarily local-in-space. In other words, roughly speaking, solutions of the form (
1.5
) are barely impacted by the behavior of the depth function at infinity. Nevertheless, condition (
Hb3
) can possibly be relaxed. Indeed, we believe that most of the results in Section
2
about the elliptic problem (
1.4
) can potentially be established under appropriate assumptions on the decay at infinity of derivatives of the depth function
b
, as is for instance discussed in
[
44
]
. Even though, we emphasize that, with condition (
Hb3
) in hand, several properties of solutions of (
1.4
) can be proven through direct computations via constructive proofs, which will be detailed later on in Section
2
.

•

It will be noticed that the condition (
Hb4
) appears to be necessary in the construction of the V-states. Indeed, this is used to generate Rankine vortices from which we can bifurcate under suitable assumptions. Thus, for the sake of simplicity, we have decided to discuss the elliptic problem (
1.9
) under the radial symmetry assumption on the depth function
b
, that is (
Hb4
). Nevertheless, most of the results in Section
2
could be extended to cover more general cases of the depth function
b
.

1.3.
Challenges and roadmap of the proofs

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
will be built upon the scheme laid out by the generalized version of implicit-functions theorem, that is, Crandall-Rabinowitz’s Theorem
A.1
. To that end, in the simply connected case, the starting point consists in observing that time-periodic solutions are patches, by definition, which obey the following contour dynamics equation

(1.8)

\partial_{t}z(t,\theta)\cdot\mathbf{n}\big(t,z(t,\theta)\big)+\frac{1}{b\big(z(t,\theta)\big)}\partial_{\theta}\Big(\psi_{b}\big(t,z(t,\theta)\big)\Big)=0,

for all
t\geq 0
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, where the unknown function
z(t,\cdot)\subset\partial D_{t}
refers to a parametrization of the boundary of the patch at time
t\geq 0
, whereas
\mathbf{n}\big(t,z(t,\theta)\big)
denotes the outward normal vector to the boundary at the point
z(t,\theta)
and
\psi_{b}
is the stream function that solves the elliptic problem (
1.4
). In addition to that, time-periodic solutions will be characterized by a particular form

z(t,\theta)=R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}(\theta+t\Omega)},

for some function
R>0
and some angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
. At last, as is aforementioned, it is natural to look for solutions close to the stationary state

\omega=b\mathds{1}_{\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\;|x|<a\}},

for a given radius
a>0
. Therefore, this leads us to the ansatz

z(t,\theta)=\sqrt{a^{2}+2r(\theta)}e^{{\rm i}(\theta+t\Omega)},

where
r:\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Afterwards, by incorporating the preceding ansatz in the contour dynamics equation, one sees that the time-variable can be discarded from the equations, whereby we arrive at the stationary problem

\mathcal{F}(\Omega,r)=0,

for some functional
\mathcal{F}
. The details of that are postponed and will be discussed in Section
3.1
. Likewise in the case of doubly-connected V-states near a given annulus, we emphasize that a similar analysis leads to a two-dimensional functional system

\mathcal{G}(\Omega,r_{1},r_{2})\overset{\rm def}{=}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{1}(\Omega,r_{1},r_{2}),\mathcal{G}_{2}(\Omega,r_{1},r_{2})\Big)=0,

for some functionals
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
which will be precised and discussed in Section
4
, as well.

The strategy is then to show that the functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
satisfy all the prerequisites in the statement of Crandall-Rabinowitz’s Theorem
A.1
. This is mainly related to the regularity study of these nonlinear functionals and the spectral analysis of their linearized operators around the stationary solutions. In contrast to
[
5
,
7
,
26
,
37
]
, the kernels involved in these functionals are not explicit and with a quite rough regularity, making their analysis much more delicate. In particular, our analysis cannot be included in the general work
[
40
]
. To give an insight onto the encountered difficulties throughout our proofs, let us now focus on the main aspects on to the reconstruction of the stream functions related to the elliptic problem

(1.9)

-\operatorname{div}(\tfrac{1}{b}\nabla\psi_{b})=f,

with data
b
,
f
and unknown
\psi_{b}.
We prove in Proposition
2.2
a Dekeyzer–Schaftingen-type decomposition
[
17
]
in the form

(1.10)

\psi_{b}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{b}(x,y)f(y)dy,\qquad K_{b}(x,y)=-\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}+S_{b}(x,y),

with

S_{b}
being symmetric and of regularity
W^{2,p}
, for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Note that a similar decomposition was obtained recently in
[
44
]
, using different arguments. Such a decomposition reveals to be useful to describe the discrete spectrum of the linearized operator at the equilibrium state, laid out in Lemma
3.4
and Proposition
4.1
. More specifically, although no explicit form of the eigenvalues associated with this linearized operator is available, we are able to develop a suitable asymptotic expansion of the spectrum through a contraction argument. This is very crucial and is discussed in Proposition
3.1
.

As for the regularity of the functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
, we rely on a new decomposition obtained in Lemma
2.1
taking the form, in the particular case
k=1
from that lemma,

\psi_{b}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|b(y)f(y)dy+\phi_{f}(x),

with
\nabla\phi_{f}\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for any
p>2
, provided that
f
is compactly supported and belongs to the Lebesgue space
L^{p}
. We believe that the generic statement of Lemma
2.1
could be used to explore more important questions on related topics.

In the sequel, we are going to use the standard notations for Lebesgue, Sobolev and Hölder spaces:
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
C^{\alpha}(\Omega)
,
\dot{C}^{\alpha}(\Omega)
…, where
p\in[1,\infty]
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. The preceding spaces are then equipped with their standard (semi-)norms. Note that a particular attention will be paid to homogeneous Sobolev spaces
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in Section
B.2.1
.

For a mere of simplicity, we employ “
\lesssim
” instead of “
\leq C
” where the dependance on the constant
C>0
is not crucial. Moreover, we occasionally use the symbol “
\lesssim_{\delta}
” to emphasize the dependance of the preceding inequality on some parameter or function
\delta
.

2.
On an elliptic problem with variable coefficients

This section is dedicated to the analysis of the elliptic problem

(2.1)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\psi_{b}\right)=f,

where
f=f(x)
and
b=b(x)
are two given real functions defined on the entire domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Further precise assumptions on both
f
and
b
will be explicitly stated, later on. Notice that (
2.1
) is relevant to the lake equation (
1.1
) as the stream function
\psi_{b}
is recovered from the vorticity
\omega
by solving (
2.1
) with the source term
f=\omega
.

Our primary focus here is to discuss some qualitative and quantitative properties of
\psi_{b}
as the solution of (
2.1
) with a general source term
f
. The main novelty in this section concerns the new decomposition of solutions given by Lemma
2.1
. Additionally, many other results are collected here, though they may not be brand-new. For the sake of completeness, we supply this section with a self-contained appendix (Appendix
B
), which provides a collection of some complementary results and proofs that we present in a different (simple and self-contained) fashion compared to some other references.

2.1.
A priori bounds and decomposition of solutions

To begin with, let us assume that, under reasonable conditions on the source term
f
to be specified subsequently, the elliptic problem (
2.1
) admits at least a solution
\psi_{b}
. This solution can in fact be shown to be unique in appropriate functional spaces. We defer the discussion about the existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
2.1
) to Appendix
B.2
. Moreover, the solution of (
2.1
) enjoys the classical elliptic regularities as will be stated in the next proposition. For the sake of completeness, a full justification of this proposition will be detailed in Appendix
B.1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
b
be a
C^{1}
function satisfying assumptions (
Hb2
), (
Hb3
) and (
Hb4
). Further consider a source term
f
with the properties that

f\in L^{1}\big((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2}\big)\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for some
p\in[2,\infty)
. Then, any solution of (
2.1
) enjoys the bound

(2.2)

\left\|\nabla^{2}\psi_{b}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
.

Remark 2.1
.

By Sobolev embedding, it is readily seen that the preceding proposition entails the control

\nabla\psi_{b}\in L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for all
n\in(2,\infty)
. The endpoint case
n=2
is not attained in general. However, the case
2=\infty
is reachable in the case
p>2
. This is crucial and, once again, for a mere sake of completeness, it will be further discussed in Appendix
B.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the results of the preceding proposition can be extended to cover similar bounds on derivatives of the solution as soon as the source term enjoys extra Sobolev regularities. More precisely, it is straightforward to show that, if the source term
f
is compactly supported
1
1

1

Here, the assumption on the compactness of the support of the source term is made for the sake of simplicity. This can be relaxed under adequate assumption on the growth of the function
b
.
and belongs to
W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, then it holds that

\left\|\nabla^{2}\psi_{b}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{j,m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{W^{j,m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
and
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. Indeed, to see that for

, we just need to expand (

) after applying a directional derivative

, for

, to write that

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b(x)}\nabla(\partial_{i}\psi_{b})(x)\right)=\partial_{i}f(x)-\Delta\psi_{b}\frac{\partial_{i}b}{b^{2}}-\nabla\left(\frac{\partial_{i}b}{b^{2}}\right)\cdot\nabla\psi_{b}\overset{\rm def}{=}F.

This means that
\partial_{i}\psi_{b}
satisfies the elliptic equation (
2.1
) with the new source term

The justification of our claim above for

amount then to showing that the new source term

In the next simple, but essential, lemma, we establish a new decomposition of the solution of (
2.1
). In particular, this new decomposition allows us to regard (
2.1
) as a regular perturbation of the classical Poisson’s problem with a suitable source term where the size of its

Lemma 2.1

Let

) and (

Remark 2.3
.

We emphasize that the assumption on the support of the source term can be traded with weaker assumptions on its growth in Lebesgue spaces along with suitable assumptions on the growth of the function

in the absence of the stronger condition (

). For the sake of simplicity, we chose to restrict our selves to the case where the condition (

).

Remark 2.4
.

Later on, we will only need to apply the preceding lemma with

. This in particular allows us to write that

\psi_{b}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|b(y)f(y)dy+\phi_{f}(x),

, where the remainder term solves the equation

(2.3)

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\phi_{f}\right)=\frac{\nabla b}{b^{2}}\cdot\nabla(\Delta)^{-1}(bf)

and enjoys the bound

(2.4)

\nabla\phi_{f}\in W^{2,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Notably, this remainder enjoys a better regularity compared to the decomposition of the solution proposed in
[
17
]
.

Proof.

Observe first that the elliptic equation (
2.1
) can be recast as

-\Delta\psi_{b}=bf-\frac{\nabla b}{b}\cdot\nabla\psi_{b}.

This inspires introducing the following expansion of
\psi_{b}

\psi_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{\phi}_{j}+\phi_{k},

where
\widetilde{\phi}_{j}
is defined by the induction scheme

-\Delta\widetilde{\phi}_{1}\overset{\rm def}{=}bf\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\Delta\widetilde{\phi}_{j}\overset{\rm def}{=}-\frac{\nabla b}{b}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{j-1},\quad\text{for all}\quad j\in\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket,

whereas the remainder term
\phi_{k}
in the expansion above is governed by the equation

(2.5)

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\phi_{k}\right)=-\frac{\nabla b}{b^{2}}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{k}.

Notice that the assumptions on the supports of
f
and
b
imply that the right-hand sides in the previous elliptic equations are compactly supported. Therefore, classical elliptic regularity for Poisson’s problem (or simply consider Proposition
2.1
with
b\equiv 1
) ensure the following bounds

(2.6)

\left\|\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\phi}_{j}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

and

(2.7)

\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{j}\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
,
n\in(2,p]
and
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
. Likewise for the remainder term, Proposition
2.1
yields the same bounds

\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{k}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

and

\left\|\nabla\phi_{k}\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

We are now ready to justify the claims of the lemma. To that end, we introduce the functions

\varrho_{1}\overset{\rm def}{=}bf\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varrho_{j}\overset{\rm def}{=}-\frac{\nabla b}{b}\cdot\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{j-1},\quad\text{for all}\quad j\in\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket.

Accordingly, we define
\varphi_{k}
as

\varphi_{k}\overset{\rm def}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varrho_{j},

whereby obtaining that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{\phi}_{j}=(-\Delta)^{-1}\varphi_{k}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|\cdot-y|\varphi_{k}(y)dy.

Moreover, due to the bounds established in the first part of the proof together with the support assumption on
\nabla b
deduced from (

\left\|\varphi_{k}\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
q\in[1,p]
. Let us now be more precise about the regularity of the summands

for any

and

\left\|\nabla^{j+1}\widetilde{\phi}_{j}\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Indeed, the previous claim is already proven for

only relies on the observation that any solution of Poisson’s equation with a source term belonging to

and

At the end, we particularly have shown that

with

\left\|\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{k}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Therefore, it follows that the source term in (

(by virtue of the preceding bound altogether with (

for all

\left\|\phi_{k}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{i+2,m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
and
i\in\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
, thereby completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.1

Lemma 3.2

It is readily seen that, if

is odd. Likewise for the
m-
fold symmetry, i.e., if
r
is a
m-
fold symmetric function, then so is
r^{\prime}
. Thus, regarding the expression of
\mathcal{F}
in (
3.3
), we can restrict our focus on showing that the function

\theta\mapsto\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta

is even and
m
-fold symmetric, as soon as
r
is an even
m
-fold symmetric function. The parity part follows by applying the second symmetry property of the kernel
K_{b}
given by (
2.12
), followed by the change of variables
\eta\to-\eta
. As for the
m
-fold symmetry property, it is shown by employing the first symmetry property of the kernel
K_{b}
given by (
2.12
), followed by the change of variables
\eta\to\eta+\frac{2\pi}{m}
. The proof of Lemma
3.2
is now completed.
∎

3.2.
Spectral analysis

In this section, we study the linearized operator associated with the functional
\mathcal{F}
at the equilibrium
r=0
and we show that it acts as a Fourier multiplier. We emphasize, however, that all the computations in this section will be completed with a detailed regularity analysis in Section
3.3
, below.

To begin, let us now compute the differential of the functional
\mathcal{F}
at any point
r
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be given by (
3.3
). Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
be a given small parameter in such a way that the function
\mathcal{F}
is differentiable according to Proposition
3.2
. Then, the differential of
\mathcal{F}
at
r\in\mathcal{B}_{m,\varepsilon}^{\alpha}
in the direction
h\in X_{m}^{\alpha}
is given by

d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega,r)[h]=\Omega h^{\prime}+\frac{1}{b\circ R}\partial_{\theta}\Big(Q(r)h+P(r)[h]\Big)+L(r)h,

where we set

\displaystyle P(r)[h](\theta)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}h(\eta)K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b\big(R(\eta)\big)d\eta,

\displaystyle Q(r)(\theta)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{R(\theta)}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}\nabla_{x}K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta,

\displaystyle L(r)(\theta)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{-b^{\prime}\big(R(\theta)\big)}{R(\theta)b^{2}\big(R(\theta)\big)}\partial_{\theta}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta\right),

for all
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proof.

Recalling that
\mathcal{F}
is given by (
3.3
), we only focus on differentiating

r\mapsto\frac{1}{b\big(R(\theta)\big)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad r\mapsto\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho.

For the first function above, it is readily seen that

d_{r}\left(\frac{1}{b\circ R}\right)[h]=-\frac{b^{\prime}\circ R}{R\times(b^{2}\circ R)}h.

As for the second one, by a direct computation, we find that

\displaystyle d_{r}\bigg(\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}

\displaystyle K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho\bigg)[h]

\displaystyle=K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b\big(R(\eta)\big)h(\eta)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{R(\theta)}\cdot\int_{0}^{R(\eta)}\nabla_{x}K_{b}\big(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho\right)h(\theta).

At last, gathering the foregoing two identities completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Now, we show that the linearized operator at the equilibrium
r=0
acts as a Fourier multiplier whose coefficients are precise in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
a>0
,
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in(0,1).
Then, for any
h\in X_{m}^{\alpha}
taking the form

h(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\cos(nm\theta),

for all
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and some
h_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that

d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega,0)[h](\theta)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nm\big(\Omega-Q(a,0)+\Lambda_{nm}(a,a)\big)h_{n}\sin(nm\theta),

where
Q(a,0)
and
\big(\Lambda_{n}(a,a)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are defined by (
3.1
) and (
1.7
), respectively.

Proof.

First, observing that

P(0)[h](\theta)=b(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(ae^{{\rm i}\theta},ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\cos(nm\eta)d\eta,

yields, in view of (
2.12
), that

P(0)[h](\theta)=b(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}(\eta-\theta)}\big)\cos(nm\eta)d\eta.

Therefore, by a change of variables, we infer that

\displaystyle P(0)[h](\theta)

\displaystyle=b(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\cos\big(nm(\eta+\theta)\big)d\eta

\displaystyle=b(a)\cos(nm\theta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\cos(nm\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle\quad-b(a)\sin(nm\theta)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\sin(nm\eta)d\eta.

Now, the second identity in (
2.12
) implies that the following function is even

\eta\mapsto K_{b}(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}).

As a consequence, we obtain the oddness of the function

\eta\mapsto K_{b}(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta})\sin(nm\eta)

and by
2\pi
-periodicity, we deduce that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\sin(nm\eta)d\eta=0,

thereby establishing that

P(0)[h](\theta)=b(a)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\cos(nm\theta)\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{b}\big(a,ae^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\cos(nm\eta)d\eta.

Consequently, it follows that

\frac{1}{b\circ R(r)}\partial_{\theta}\Big(P(r)[h]\Big)\big|_{r=0}=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\sin(nm\theta)\Lambda_{nm}(a,a).

Next, we take care of
Q(0)
. To that end, observe first that, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
,

\displaystyle Q(0)(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{a}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}\nabla_{x}K_{b}\big(ae^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}\partial_{a}\Big(K_{b}\big(ae^{{\rm i}\theta},\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)\Big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}\partial_{a}\Big(K_{b}\big(a,\rho e^{{\rm i}(\eta-\theta)}\big)\Big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta.

Thus, by performing a change of variables, again, we find that

Q(0)(\theta)=\frac{1}{a}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{a}\partial_{x_{1}}K_{b}\big(a,\rho e^{{\rm i}\eta}\big)b(\rho)\rho d\rho d\eta.

Thus, by the explicit formula for the right-hand side given in Lemma
2.2
, we deduce that

Q(0)(\theta)=-b(a)Q(a,0),

which in turn yields

\frac{1}{b\circ R(r)}\partial_{\theta}\Big(Q(0)[h]\Big)\big|_{r=0}=-Q(a,0)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\sin(nm\theta).

In addition, we deduce from (
3.9
) that

(3.11)

L(0)\equiv 0.

All in all, gathering the preceding identities concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

3.3.
Regularity analysis

The aim of this section is to provide a detailed analysis of the regularity properties of the functional
\mathcal{F}
. This analysis allows us in particular to justify the computations laid out in Section
3.2
, above, which will also serve in the doubly connected case, later on.

In the sequel of the paper, for a fixed integer
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and real parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the spaces
X_{m}^{\alpha}
and
Y_{m}^{\alpha}
are defined by

X_{m}^{\alpha}\overset{\rm def}{=}\left\{f\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T}):f(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}\cos(nm\theta),\ f_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in\mathbb{T}\right\}

and

Y_{m}^{\alpha}\overset{\rm def}{=}\left\{g\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}):g(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}\sin(nm\theta),\ g_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in\mathbb{T}\right\}

and equipped with the usual
C^{1+\alpha}
and
C^{\alpha}
norms, respectively. Moreover, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, we set

\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{m,\varepsilon}\overset{\rm def}{=}\big\{f\in X_{m}^{\alpha}:\left\|f\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}<\varepsilon\big\}.

In addition to that, allow us to clarify some vectorial notations that will be exclusively used in this section. For any given two vectors
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the bilinear application

u\otimes v:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is defined, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, by

(u\otimes v)(x,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}(u\cdot x)(v\cdot y).

Applying it to the canonical basis
(e_{1},e_{2})
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we obtain, for any
(i,j)\in\{1,2\}^{2}
, that

(u\otimes v)(e_{i},e_{j})=(u\cdot e_{i})(v\cdot e_{j})=u_{i}v_{j},

which gives the following representation of the tensor-product

u\otimes v\overset{\rm def}{=}\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}v_{1}&u_{1}v_{2}\\
u_{2}v_{1}&u_{2}v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Now, for given two matrices
A=(A_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}
and
B=(B_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}
in
M_{2}(\mathbb{R}),
we define the double contraction of
A
and
B
by

A:B\overset{\rm def}{=}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}A_{ij}B_{ij}.

In particular, one also sees that, for given vectors
u=(u_{1},u_{2})
,
u^{\prime}=(u_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}^{\prime})
,
v=(v_{1},v_{2})
and
v^{\prime}=(v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\big(u\otimes v\big):\big(u^{\prime}\otimes v^{\prime}\big)=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}u_{i}v_{j}u_{i}^{\prime}v_{j}^{\prime}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}u_{i}u_{i}^{\prime}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2}v_{j}v_{j}^{\prime}\right)=(u\cdot u^{\prime})(v\cdot v^{\prime}).

3.3.1.
On the continuity of some singular operators

For a later use, we now discuss some fundamental results which will allow us to show the continuity of operators with singular kernels. We first recall an important lemma whose proof can be found in
[
39
, Lemma 2.6]
and
[
13
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
and consider a measurable complex-valued function
K
, defined on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{T}\setminus\{(\theta,\theta),\ \theta\in\mathbb{T}\}
. Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|K(\theta,\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{\alpha}},\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta\neq\eta\in\mathbb{T},

and that
\theta\mapsto K(\theta,\eta)
is differentiable with

|\partial_{\theta}K(\theta,\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{1+\alpha}},\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta\neq\eta\in\mathbb{T}.

Then, the complex-valued function defined on
\mathbb{T}
, for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, by

\theta\mapsto\mathcal{T}f(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K(\theta,\eta)f(\eta)d\eta,

belongs to
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and satisfies

\left\|\mathcal{T}f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{\alpha}C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Now, we establish three essential consequences of Lemma
3.5
which will come in handy in the regularity analysis of contour dynamics, later on.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
a>0
and
(\alpha,s)\in(0,1)\times[0,1]
. Consider a real-valued functions
r\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
with

(3.22)

\left\|r\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\leq\varepsilon_{0},

for some small parameter
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Accordingly, we define the function

(3.23)

\theta\mapsto R(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\sqrt{a^{2}+2r(\theta)}.

Then, the function

\theta\in\mathbb{T}\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{s}f(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|f(\eta)d\eta,

defined for any
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, belongs to
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and enjoys the bound

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{s}f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\leq C_{\alpha}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

for some constant
C_{\alpha}>0
which is independent of
s
.

Proof.

The proof directly follows by applying Lemma
3.5
provided that we show that the kernel defined by

(\theta,\eta)\mapsto K_{s}(\theta,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\log\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|

satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
3.5
for a constant
C
which is independent of
s
. To that end, observe first, by a continuity argument together with the smallness assumption (
3.22
), that

(3.24)

\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}R(\theta)\geq\frac{a}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}R(\theta)\leq 2a.

Besides, writing, for any
s\in[0,1]
and any
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

(3.25)

\displaystyle\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=R^{2}(\theta)+s^{2}R^{2}(\eta)-2sR(\theta)R(\eta)\cos(\eta-\theta)

\displaystyle=\big(R(\theta)-sR(\eta)\big)^{2}+4sR(\theta)R(\eta)\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right),

it then follows, by continuity and using (
3.25
) and (
3.24
), that there exists a small parameter
s_{0}\in(0,1)
in such a way that

\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\geq\frac{R(\theta)}{2}\geq\frac{a}{4}\left|\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|,\quad\text{for all}\quad s\in[0,s_{0}].

On the other hand, by utilizing (
3.25
) and (
3.24
) again, we obtain that

\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\geq 2\sqrt{s_{0}R(\theta)R(\eta)}\left|\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|\geq a\sqrt{s_{0}}\left|\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|,\quad\text{for all}\quad s\in[s_{0},1].

Putting together the last two estimates leads to the lower bound

(3.26)

\inf_{s\in[0,1]}\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\geq\frac{a\sqrt{s_{0}}}{4}\left|\sin\left(\tfrac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|,\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}.

Moreover, using the fact that

(3.27)

x\mapsto x^{\gamma}\big|\log x\big|\in L^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),

for all
\gamma>0
, we then deduce that

|K_{s}(\theta,\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{\gamma}},

for all
\eta\neq\theta\in\mathbb{T}
and some constant
C>0
which is independent of
s
.

Likewise, computing that

\partial_{\theta}K_{s}(\theta,\eta)=\partial_{\theta}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\cdot\frac{R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}}{\left|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|^{2}},

and utilizing the bounds (
3.22
), (
3.24
) and (
3.26
), we infer that

|\partial_{\theta}K_{s}(\theta,\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|}\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{1+\alpha}},

for all
\eta\neq\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, up to suitably change the constant
C
, above. At last, Lemma
3.5
applies to conclude the proof of Corollary
3.1
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\alpha,s
,
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
be respectively defined in terms of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
as in Corollary
3.1
by also assuming that
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
satisfy the smallness assumption (
3.22
). Further define the following operator acting on
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
by

f\mapsto\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{s}f\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widetilde{K}_{s}(\cdot,\eta)f(\eta)d\eta,

where we set, for any
\theta\neq\eta\in\mathbb{T}
,

\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\log\left|R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|-\log\left|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|.

Then,
\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{s}f\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and it holds, for any
\beta\in[0,\alpha)
, that

\left\|\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{s}f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\beta}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}.

Proof.

First remark that the mean value theorem implies, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, that

\big|\log x-\log y\big|\leq\frac{|x-y|}{\min\{x,y\}}\cdot

Thus, combining this estimate with (
3.26
) and employing the triangular inequality, yields

\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\Big|\left|R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|-\left|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\Big|}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|}\cdot

Now, observing that

(3.28)

R_{1}-R_{2}=2\frac{r_{1}-r_{2}}{R_{1}+R_{2}},

and combining this identity with (
3.24
), it is readily seen then that

\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

which implies in turn that

\big|\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)\big|\lesssim\frac{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|}\cdot

On the other hand, we deduce from (
3.27
) that

|\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{\gamma}},

for any
\gamma>0
. Hence, in view of the preceding two bounds, we write, for any
\theta\neq\eta\in\mathbb{T}
and
\beta\in(0,1)
to be chosen in a moment, that

\displaystyle\big|\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|\widetilde{K}(\theta,\eta)\big|^{\beta}\big|\widetilde{K}(\theta,\eta)\big|^{1-\beta}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\beta}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{\beta+\gamma(1-\beta)}}\cdot

Therefore, choosing
\beta>0
small enough by setting, for instance, that

\gamma=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{1-\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta\in(0,\alpha),

we end up with the desired control

(3.29)

|\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)|\lesssim\frac{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\beta}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{\alpha}}\cdot

Likewise, we first observe that

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)-\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\right|}{\left|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|}

\displaystyle+\left|\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\right|\left|\frac{R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}}{\left|R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|^{2}}-\frac{R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}}{\left|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|^{2}}\right|.

Then writing, in view of the elementary bounds

\left|\frac{X_{1}}{|X_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{X_{2}}{|X_{2}|^{2}}\right|\leq 3\frac{|X_{1}-X_{2}|}{\min\{|X_{1}|,|X_{2}|\}^{2}},

and

\left|\frac{X_{1}}{|X_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{X_{2}}{|X_{2}|^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\min\{|X_{1}|,|X_{2}|\}},

with the values

X_{k}=X_{k}(\theta,\eta)=R_{k}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{k}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta},\qquad k\in\{1,2\},

that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{X_{1}}{|X_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{X_{2}}{|X_{2}|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{X_{1}}{|X_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{X_{2}}{|X_{2}|^{2}}\right|^{\alpha}\left|\frac{X_{1}}{|X_{1}|^{2}}-\frac{X_{2}}{|X_{2}|^{2}}\right|^{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha}\frac{|X_{1}-X_{2}|^{\alpha}}{\min\{|X_{1}|,|X_{2}|\}^{1+\alpha}},

it follows, by further utilizing the bound (
3.26
), that

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha}\frac{\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|}+\frac{\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\alpha}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{1+\alpha}}\cdot

Now, notice that deriving (
3.28
) and estimating the resulting identity in
L^{\infty}
, yields

\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}.

Hence, since
\beta<\alpha
and
r_{1},r_{2}
satisfy the smallness condition (
3.22
), we arrive at the control

\displaystyle|\partial_{\theta}\widetilde{K}_{s}(\theta,\eta)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\alpha}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{1+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\beta}}{\left|\sin\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)\right|^{1+\alpha}}\cdot

At last, combining the preceding control with (
3.29
) allows us to apply Lemma
3.5
, thereby completing the proof of Corollary
3.2
.
∎

3.1
and
3.2
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Under the assumptions of Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
, further consider a complex valued function
h\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and define the operators
\mathcal{T}_{h,R}
and
\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{h}
by

f\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{h,R}f\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}K_{h,R}(\cdot,\eta)f(\eta)d\eta,

and

f\mapsto\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{h}f\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\widetilde{K}_{h}(\cdot,\eta)f(\eta)d\eta,

where, we set, for any
\theta\neq\eta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

K_{h,R}(\theta,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}}{|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{h(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{R(\theta)}-\frac{h(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}}{R(\eta)}\right),

and

\widetilde{K}_{h}(\theta,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}K_{h,R_{1}}(\theta,\eta)-K_{h,R_{2}}(\theta,\eta).

Then,
\mathcal{T}_{h,R}f
and
\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{h}f
belong to
C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
with

\left\|\mathcal{T}_{h,R}f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\left\|h\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

and

\left\|\mathcal{\widetilde{T}}_{h}f\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim_{\alpha}\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\beta}\left\|h\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}},

for all
\beta\in(0,\alpha)
.

The next two lemmas can be seen as variations of Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
. Even though their statements are similar, the proofs of these lemmas require a careful attention and they do not straitforwardly follow from the statements of the previous results of this section.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f
be an element of
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
and
R
be as in Corollary
3.1
. Then, the function defined, for any
(\rho,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}
, by

A(\rho,\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|\rho e^{i\theta}-R(\eta)e^{i\eta}\right|f(\eta)d\eta

enjoys the bound

\left\|\partial_{\rho}A\right\|_{L^{p}\big((0,\delta)\times\mathbb{T}\big)}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},

for any finite
\delta>0
and any
p\in[1,\infty)
.

Remark 3.1
.

A direct consequence of the preceding lemma is that the function

x\mapsto\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|x-R(\eta)e^{i\eta}\right|f(\eta)d\eta

belongs to
W^{1,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and, up to a multiplicative constant, obeys the same bound from the lemma in terms of the given function
f
.

Proof.

To get started, noticing

\partial_{\rho}A(\rho,\theta)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\rho-R(\eta)+2R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}f(\eta)d\eta,

and recalling the bounds from (
3.24
)

(3.30)

\frac{a}{2}\leq R(\eta)\leq 2a,\quad\text{for all}\quad\eta\in\mathbb{T},

yields that

\left(\frac{\rho-R(\eta)+2R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{0<\rho<\frac{a}{4}\}\cup\{4a<\rho<\delta\}}=O(1),

for all
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}
. Therefore, we deduce that

(3.31)

\left\|\partial_{\rho}A\right\|_{L^{p}\big((0,\frac{a}{4})\cup(4a,\delta)\times\mathbb{T}\big)}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

It remains now to establish the same control on the compact domain
(\frac{a}{4},4a)
, where the variable
\rho
is away from the origin but within the singular set of the function

D(\rho,\theta,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{\rho-R(\eta)+2R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{a}{4}<\rho<4a\}}.

To that end, letting
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
to be chosen in a little while, we first notice the bound

(3.32)

\displaystyle|D(\rho,\theta,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{\big(\rho R(\eta)\big)^{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{\big|\rho-R(\eta)\big|^{1-2\varepsilon}\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}+\frac{1}{\rho}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{a}{4}<\rho<4a\}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{1}{\big|\rho-R(\eta)\big|^{1-2\varepsilon}\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}+1\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{a}{4}<\rho<4a\}},

which is obtained by decomposing the nominator in the definition of the function
D
along with the elementary interpolation inequality

(3.33)

c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}\gtrsim c_{1}^{2\varepsilon}c_{2}^{2(1-\varepsilon)},

which is valid for any positive numbers
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
and any parameter
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. Thus, for any given
p\in(1,\infty)
, we obtain that

\left\|D(\cdot,\theta,\eta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}\lesssim 1+\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}\left(\int_{\frac{a}{4}}^{4a}\frac{d\rho}{\big|\rho-R(\eta)\big|^{p(1-2\varepsilon)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

whence, by making an adequate change of variables and choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently close to
\frac{1}{2}
in such a way that

(3.34)

p(1-2\varepsilon)<1\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2},

we infer that

\displaystyle\left\|D(\cdot,\theta,\eta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}\left(\int_{0}^{4a}\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma^{p(1-2\varepsilon)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)},

for any
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}
. Hence, by virtue of the choice
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, it follows that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\|D(\cdot,\theta,\eta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}|f(\eta)|d\eta\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},

where we have utilized the fact that

\eta\mapsto\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}),

uniformly in
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
as soon as
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2}
. Consequently, we deduce that

\left\|\partial_{\rho}A\right\|_{L^{p}\big((\frac{a}{4},4a)\times\mathbb{T}\big)}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

All in all, combining this bound with (
3.31
) concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next lemma in this section establishes a continuity aspect with respect to the functional
R
in the preceding lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
be respectively defined in terms of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
as in Corollary
3.1
. Furtehr define, for
i\in\{1,2\}
and any given function
f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
,

A_{i}(\rho,\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|\rho e^{i\theta}-R_{i}(\eta)e^{i\eta}\right|f(\eta)d\eta,

for any
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}
. Then, it holds that

\left\|\partial_{\rho}A_{1}-\partial_{\rho}A_{2}\right\|_{L^{p}\big((0,\delta)\times\mathbb{T}\big)}\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}^{\beta}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},

for any
\beta\in[0,\frac{1}{p})
and
\delta>0
.

Proof.

We begin with noticing that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\rho-R_{1}(\eta)+2R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}-\frac{\rho-R_{2}(\eta)+2R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}\right|\mathds{1}_{\{0<\rho<\frac{a}{4}\}\cup\{4a<\rho<\delta\}}

\displaystyle=O\left(|R_{1}(\eta)-R_{2}(\eta)|\right),

for all
\theta,\eta\in\mathbb{T}
, whose justification solely relies on the bound (
3.30
) and the fact that the variable
\rho
is localized away from the potential values of
R(\eta)
. Thus, it follows that

(3.35)

\left\|\partial_{\rho}A_{1}-\partial_{\rho}A_{2}\right\|_{L^{p}\big((0,\frac{a}{4})\cup(4a,\delta)\times\mathbb{T}\big)}\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}.

Now, we focus our attention on obtaining a similar bound within the domain
(\frac{a}{4},4a)\times\mathbb{T}
where the function

\displaystyle D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)-D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\left(\frac{\rho-R_{1}(\eta)+2R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}-\frac{\rho-R_{2}(\eta)+2R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\frac{a}{4}<\rho<4a\}}

is singular. Here, we need to perform additional splittings. We proceed by introducing the notation

\alpha=\alpha(\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}|R_{1}(\eta)-R_{2}(\eta)|\leq 1

and, accordingly, writing

\displaystyle|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)-D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq\Big(|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)|+|D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)|\Big)\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}}

\displaystyle+\big|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)-D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)\big|\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|>2\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|\geq\alpha\}}

\displaystyle+\big|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)-D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)\big|\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|>2\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|\geq\alpha\}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)|+|D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)|\Big)\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}}

\displaystyle+2\big|D_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)-D_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta)\big|\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|>\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|\geq\alpha\}}

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\mathcal{D}_{1}(\rho,\theta,\eta)+\mathcal{D}_{2}(\rho,\theta,\eta).

Notice that the validity of this decomposition is due to the partition

\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}=\left(\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}\cap\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}\right)\cup\{|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|>2\alpha\}\cup\{|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|>2\alpha\},

and the triangular inequality

|\rho-R_{1}(\eta)|\geq|\rho-R_{2}(\eta)|-\alpha\geq\alpha,

in the case

and

. Next, we take care of

\mathcal{D}_{2}
separately. To that end, by means of the same arguments laid out in the proof of Lemma

to estimate the function

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}_{1}(\cdot,\theta,\eta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(\int_{\frac{a}{4}}^{4a}\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{|\rho-R_{i}(\eta)|\leq 2\alpha\}}}{\big|\rho-R_{i}(\eta)\big|^{p(1-2\varepsilon)}}d\rho\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}}+\frac{1}{\sin^{2\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\theta-\eta}{2}\right)}\alpha^{\frac{1}{p}-(1-2\varepsilon)},

for all

, where the smallness condition (

was employed in the last line. Hence, it follows that

(3.36)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\|\mathcal{D}_{1}(\cdot,\theta,\eta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}|f(\eta)|d\eta\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}^{\beta}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},

uniformly in
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, where we set

\beta\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{p}-(1-2\varepsilon)>0.

\displaystyle(D_{1}

\displaystyle-D_{2})(\rho,\theta,\eta)=\frac{\big(R_{2}(\eta)-R_{1}(\eta)\big)\big(1-2\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\eta-\theta}{2})\big)}{\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}

\displaystyle+\Big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)+2R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\tfrac{\eta-\theta}{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{\big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}-\frac{1}{\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(R_{2}(\eta)-R_{1}(\eta)\big)\big(1-2\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})\big)}{\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}

\displaystyle+\Big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)+2R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}\left(\tfrac{\eta-\theta}{2}\right)\Big)\big(R_{2}(\eta)-R_{1}(\eta)\big)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{(\rho-R_{1}(\eta))+(\rho-R_{2}(\eta))+2\rho\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})}{\left(\big(\rho-R_{1}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{1}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})\right)\left(\big(\rho-R_{2}(\eta)\big)^{2}+4\rho R_{2}(\eta)\sin^{2}(\frac{\eta-\theta}{2})\right)}\right).

\displaystyle-D_{2})(\rho,\theta,\eta)\big|

We conclude this section with the following simple lemma that establishes a continuity property of composed Hölder-functions. This will serve to simply the conclusion of the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
be two Lipschitz-continuous functions on
\mathbb{T}
. Further consider a function
\Phi\in C^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for some
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Then, it holds that

\left\|\Phi(m_{1})-\Phi(m_{2})\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C_{\Phi}\left\|m_{1}-m_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}^{\kappa-\alpha},

for any
\alpha\in[0,\kappa]
and for some constant
C_{\Phi}>0
depending on the
C^{\kappa}
-norm of
\Phi
.

Proof.

We only focus our attention on the estimate of the difference in
\dot{C}^{\kappa}
as its control in
L^{\infty}
easily follows from the fact that the supremum of continuous functions is attained on compact sets. Now, we introduce the parameter

\delta\overset{\rm def}{=}\left\|m_{1}-m_{2}\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}

and we write that

\displaystyle\sup_{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq 1}

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\Big(\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{1})\big)\Big)-\Big(\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{2})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{2})\big)\Big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sup_{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq\delta}\left(\frac{\left|\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{2})\big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}+\frac{\left|\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{2})\big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sup_{\delta\leq|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq 1}\left(\frac{\left|\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{1})\big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}+\frac{\left|\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{2})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{2})\big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}\right).

Therefore, employing the assumption that
\Phi
belongs to
C^{\kappa}(B_{\bar{m}})
, with

\bar{m}\overset{\rm def}{=}\max\{\left\|m_{1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})},\left\|m_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\},

it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq 1}

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{1}(\theta_{2})\big)-\Phi\big(m_{2}(\theta_{2})\big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|\Phi\right\|_{C^{\kappa}(B_{\bar{m}})}\sup_{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq\delta}\left(\frac{\left|m_{1}(\theta_{1})-m_{1}(\theta_{2})\right|^{\kappa}}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}+\frac{\left|m_{2}(\theta_{1})-m_{2}(\theta_{2})\right|^{\kappa}}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left\|\Phi\right\|_{C^{\kappa}(B_{\bar{m}})}\sup_{\delta\leq|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq 1}\left(\frac{\left\|m_{1}-m_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}^{\kappa}}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}\right).

Hence, by employing the Lipschitz-regularity assumption on the functions
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
, we arrive at the bound

\displaystyle\sup_{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\leq 1}

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\Big(\Phi\big(r_{1}(\theta_{1})\big)-\Phi\big(r_{2}(\theta_{1})\big)\Big)-\Big(\Phi\big(r_{1}(\theta_{2})\big)-\Phi\big(r_{2}(\theta_{2})\big)\Big)\right|}{|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|^{\alpha}}\leq 2\left\|\Phi\right\|_{C^{\kappa}(B_{\bar{m}})}\delta^{\kappa-\alpha},

thereby completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

and (

), we deduce that

\left\|w\left(R(\cdot)e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{\dot{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\left\|R\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\left\|\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\right\|_{L^{p_{\alpha}}(D_{0})}<\infty.

All in all, combining these bounds with the assumption that
r\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
yields
\Upsilon_{2}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. In addition to that, we emphasize that the same preceding arguments apply to estimate
\Upsilon_{1}
, entailing the same conclusion that
\Upsilon_{1}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
, too.

Now, we are left with justifying the continuity of functional

r\mapsto d_{r}\mathcal{H}(r)

from
\mathcal{B}_{m,\varepsilon}^{\alpha}
into
\mathcal{L}(X_{m}^{\alpha},Y_{m}^{\alpha})
, i.e., we claim that

\sup_{\left\|h\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}<1}\left\|d_{r}\mathcal{H}(r_{1})[h]-d_{r}\mathcal{H}(r_{2})[h]\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\to 0,

as

\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\to 0.

For the sake of clarity, we keep the same notations for all the functionals introduced above and they will be further indexed, hereafter, by
r_{1}
or
r_{2}
to precise the dependence on the functions
r_{1}
or
r_{2}
, respectively.

Below, we will only focus on the analysis of non-classical terms
\Upsilon_{1}
and
\Upsilon_{2}
, given by the expansion of
d_{r}\mathcal{H}_{2}
, above, for the treatment of the remaining terms follows by a direct application of Corollaries
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
. Note that we can further restrict our proof below to
\Upsilon_{2}
, for the same analysis applies to
\Upsilon_{1}
, as well. To that end, we first write that

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2,r_{1}}(\theta)-\Upsilon_{2,r_{2}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\big(\Pi_{r_{1}}(\theta)-\Pi_{r_{2}}(\theta)\big):J_{r_{1}}(\theta)+\Pi_{r_{2}}(\theta):\big(J_{r_{1}}(\theta)-J_{r_{2}}(\theta)\big),

where, we denote, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

\Pi_{r}(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\left(\partial_{\theta}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\otimes\frac{h(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}}{R(\theta)}\right)

and

J_{r}(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{D_{0,r}}\log|R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-y|\nabla_{y}^{2}\left(b^{2}(|y|)\right)dy.

Therefore, it is readily seen that
J_{r_{1}}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
and that

\left\|\Pi_{r_{1}}-\Pi_{r_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\left\|h\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}.

Now, we write, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

\displaystyle\big(J_{r_{1}}-J_{r_{2}}\big)(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{0,r_{1}}}\left(\log|R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-y|-\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-y|\right)\nabla_{y}^{2}\left(b^{2}(|y|)\right)dy

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\int_{D_{0,r_{1}}}-\int_{D_{0,r_{2}}}\right)\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-y|\nabla_{y}^{2}\left(b^{2}(|y|)\right)dy

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\Delta_{1}(\theta)+\Delta_{2}(\theta).

Thus, by denoting

x_{s}(\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}sR_{1}(\theta)+(1-s)R_{2}(\theta),

and employing Taylor’s expansion, we obtain, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\big(R_{1}(\theta)-R_{2}(\theta)\big)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\int_{D_{0,r_{1}}}\nabla_{x}\log|x_{s}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-y|\nabla_{y}^{2}\left(b^{2}(|y|)\right)dyds

\displaystyle=-2\pi\big(R_{1}(\theta)-R_{2}(\theta)\big)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\Big(\nabla\Delta^{-1}\big(\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\big)\Big)\big(x_{s}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\big)ds.

In the sequel of the proof, we will be using the bound

\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{T}}R_{k}(\theta)\leq 2a,\qquad k\in\{1,2\},

which has been shown in (
3.24
).
Thus, it follows, by the composition law

\displaystyle\left\|\Big(\nabla\Delta^{-1}\big(\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\big)\Big)\big(x_{s}(\cdot)e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}+\left\|R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\right)\left\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2}))\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})},

that

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\left(1+\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}+\left\|R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\right)\left\|\nabla\Delta^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2}))\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}.

Now, as the function

v\overset{\rm def}{=}\Delta^{-1}(\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2}))

is a solution of Poisson’s equation

\Delta v=\mathds{1}_{D_{r_{1}}}\nabla^{2}(b^{2}),

the bounds

\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\right\|_{L^{p_{\alpha}}(D_{r_{1}})}

and

\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{\dot{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|v\right\|_{\dot{W}^{2,p_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\right\|_{L^{p_{\alpha}}(D_{r_{1}})}

follow by a direct application of the Lemma
B.1
and Proposition
2.1
, thanks to the assumptions (
Hb1
) and (
Hb3
) on the depth function
b
, where
p_{\alpha}
is defined in (
3.40
).
Hence, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})}\left\|\nabla^{2}(b^{2})\right\|_{L^{p_{\alpha}}(D_{r_{1}})},

which takes care of the estimate on
\Delta_{1}
.
As for
\Delta_{2}
, we first perform a change of variables in polar coordinates to rewrite it, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, as

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|u_{1}(s,\eta)dsd\eta

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|u_{2}(s,\eta)dsd\eta,

where we denote, for
k\in\{1,2\}
, that

u_{r_{k}}(s,\eta)\overset{\rm def}{=}sR^{2}_{k}(\eta)\left(\nabla_{y}^{2}\Big(b^{2}\big(|y|\big)\Big)\right)\Big|_{y=sR_{k}(\eta)}.

Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|-\log|R_{2}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{2}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|\right)u_{r_{2}}(s,\eta)dsd\eta

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\log|R_{1}(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}-sR_{1}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|\left(u_{r_{1}}-u_{r_{2}}\right)(s,\eta)dsd\eta.

Hence, by virtue of Corollaries
3.1
and
3.2
, we infer, for all
\beta\in(0,\alpha)
, that

\left\|\Delta_{2}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\beta}\left\|u_{r_{2}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|u_{r_{1}}-u_{r_{2}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}([0,1]\times\bar{B}_{2a})}.

Thus, as the function
u_{r_{1}}-u_{r_{2}}
is continuous, whence its maximum on the closed set
[0,1]\times\bar{B}_{2a}
is attained, we deduce that

\left\|\Delta_{2}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\beta}\left\|u_{r_{2}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+|(u_{r_{1}}-u_{r_{2}})(s_{0},\eta_{0})|,

for some
s_{0},\eta_{0}\in[0,1]\times\bar{B}_{2a}
. Hence, due to the preceding point-wise bound and assumption (
Hb1
), it is readily seen that

\lim_{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\to 0}\left\|\Delta_{2}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}=0.

All in all, combining all the preceding bounds, we finally deduce that

\lim_{\left\|r_{1}-r_{2}\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}\to 0}\left(\sup_{\left\|h\right\|_{C^{1+\alpha}}<1}\left\|\Upsilon_{2,r_{1}}-\Upsilon_{2,r_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\right)=0,

which concludes the proof of the desired regularity properties for
\mathcal{H}
.

Regularity of
\mathcal{K}

The desired regularity of that term stems from the high-regularity of the perturbation
\phi
, which is the crux of the new decomposition in Lemma
2.1
. This is a generic property and can be observed in other settings. We proceed first by writing, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

\mathcal{K}(r)(\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right).

Therefore, by virtue of the fact that
\phi_{R}\in W^{3,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for all
p\in(1,\infty)
, it is then readily seen that

\theta\mapsto\mathcal{K}(r)(\theta)\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}),

as soon as
r\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. Moreover, we compute, for any
h\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
, that

\displaystyle d_{r}\big(\mathcal{K}(r)\big)[h](\theta)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h(\theta)}{R(\theta)}e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\cdot\nabla\phi_{R}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\partial_{\theta}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\otimes\frac{h(\theta)}{R(\theta)}e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right):\nabla^{2}\phi_{R}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h(\theta)}{R(\theta)}e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right)\cdot\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R}\left(R(\theta)e^{{\rm i}\theta}\right),

where the last term above comes from differentiating the expression (
3.38
) with respect to
R
(inside the integral) and is given by

(3.41)

\mathcal{E}_{R}(x)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{b}(x,\rho e^{{\rm i}\theta})\frac{b^{\prime}}{b^{2}}(\rho)\partial_{\rho}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|\rho e^{{\rm i}\theta}-R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|b\big(R(\eta)\big)d\eta\right)\rho d\rho d\theta,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In particular, by virtue of Lemma
3.6
and the assumption (
Hb3
) on the function
b
, one sees that

(r,\theta)\mapsto\frac{b^{\prime}}{b^{2}}(\rho)\partial_{\rho}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|\rho e^{{\rm i}\theta}-R(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|b\big(R(\eta)\big)d\eta\right)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}),

for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. Thus, by means of Proposition
2.1
(see also Remark
2.5
), we deduce that

\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R}\in W^{1,p_{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

with
p_{\alpha}
being defined in (
3.40
).

Hence, by a similar argument, relying on the
W^{2,p_{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{loc}}
-regularity of
\nabla\phi_{R}
, which is again a consequence of Remark
2.4
, together with the symmetry properties established in Lemma
3.2
, we deduce that

d_{r}\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{L}(X_{m}^{\alpha},Y_{m}^{\alpha}).

We are now left with proof of continuity of
d_{r}\mathcal{K}
with respect to the
r
-variable. To that end, we first write by means of a direct computation that

\displaystyle\left\|d_{r}\mathcal{K}(r_{1})[h]-d_{r}\mathcal{K}(r_{2})[h]\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}}\left\|(\nabla\phi_{R_{1}},\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}})\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}\left\|\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h}{R_{1}}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h}{R_{2}}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla\phi_{R_{1}}\big(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)-\nabla\phi_{R_{2}}\big(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\left\|\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h}{R_{2}}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\otimes\frac{h}{R_{1}}-\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\otimes\frac{h}{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}}\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}\big(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\big(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\left\|\partial_{\theta}\left(\frac{h}{R_{2}}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle+\left\|\partial_{\theta}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\otimes\frac{h}{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}.

The most challenging terms in this collection of bounds are

(3.42)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}\big(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\big(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\big(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\big(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\big)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

and

(3.43)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|R_{1}\right\|_{C^{1}}\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}

\displaystyle+\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}},

whereas the remaining ones can be estimated in a similar, or even easier, way. Notably, we already have prepared the ground to estimate them due to the analysis laid out in the previous lemmas. In particular, noticing that

\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}},\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\in W^{1,p_{\kappa}}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\hookrightarrow C^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for
\kappa\in(0,1)
being defined as

\kappa\overset{\rm def}{=}1-\frac{2}{q_{\kappa}}>1-\frac{2}{p_{\alpha}}=\alpha,

it then follows by a direct application of Lemma
3.8
that

\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\kappa-\alpha}

and

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\left(R_{1}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{2}}\left(R_{2}e^{{\rm i}\cdot}\right)\right\|_{C^{\alpha}}\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{C^{1}}^{\kappa-\alpha}.

This takes care of the last terms in the right-hand sides of (
3.42
) and (
3.43
). On the other hand, the first terms in the right-hand sides of these inequalities will be controlled by using a different argument. More precisely, owing to the definition (
3.41
), employing Sobolev embedding and the elliptic-gain of regularity (see Proposition
2.1
and Remark
2.5
), we infer that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}-\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{2,p_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\frac{b^{\prime}}{b^{2}}(\rho)\partial_{\rho}(\mathcal{A}_{1}-\mathcal{A}_{2})(\rho,\theta)\right\|_{L^{p}(\rho d\rho d\theta;(0,R_{\infty})\times\mathbb{T})},

where
R_{\infty}
is the constant introduced in assumption (
Hb3
) and with

\mathcal{A}_{i}(\rho,\theta)\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log|\rho e^{{\rm i}\theta}-R_{i}(\eta)e^{{\rm i}\eta}|b\big(R(\eta)\big)d\eta.

Thus, by a direct application of Lemma
3.7
, it is readily seen that

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}-\nabla\mathcal{E}_{R_{1}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}\lesssim\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{\beta},

for any
\beta\in[0,\frac{1}{p_{\alpha}})
.

At last, it only remains to estimate the first term in (
3.43
). To that end, we notice that the representation (
3.38
) entails that

\phi_{R_{1}}-\phi_{R_{2}}=\phi_{\widetilde{R}}(x),

where
\phi_{\widetilde{R}}
is given, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, by

\phi_{\widetilde{R}}(x)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{b}(x,z)\frac{\nabla b}{b^{2}}(z)\cdot\nabla_{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|z-y|b(y)\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y\in D_{1}\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y\in D_{2}\}}\right)dydz,

which represents the remainder term in the decomposition given in Lemma
2.1
of the solution of (
2.1
) with the new source term

f=\mathds{1}_{D_{1}}-\mathds{1}_{D_{2}}.

Therefore, by virtue of Sobolev embedding and the elliptic-gain of regularity (see in particular Remark
2.4
) we deduce the bound

\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}\lesssim\left\|\phi_{\widetilde{R}}\right\|_{\dot{W}^{3,p_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|\mathds{1}_{D_{1}}-\mathds{1}_{D_{2}}\right\|_{L^{p_{\alpha}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

which in turn implies that

\lim_{\left\|R_{1}-R_{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0}\left\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{1}}-\nabla^{2}\phi_{R_{2}}\right\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{2a})}=0.

All in all, combining the previous estimates concludes the justification of the desired regularity properties for
\mathcal{K}
and completes the proof of the proposition.

3.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We are now in position to employ all the preceding results above and from the previous sections to finally establish all the prerequisites to applying Crandall-Rabinowitz’s Theorem
A.1
and, subsequently, deduce Theorem
1.1
. This is achieved by the following proposition.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
a>0
,
M(a,b)
be defined through (
3.15
) and
\varepsilon>0
be determined by Proposition
3.2
. Then the following assertions hold for all integers
m>M(a,b)
:

(1)

The linearized operator

d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega,0):X_{m}^{\alpha}\to Y_{m}^{\alpha}

has a
non trivial kernel if and only if

\Omega\in\left\{\Omega_{nm}\overset{\rm def}{=}Q(a,0)-\Lambda_{nm}(a,a),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\right\},

where
Q(a,0)
and
\big(\Lambda_{n}(a,a)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are defined by (
3.1
) and (
1.7
), respectively.

(2)

Moreover,
\ker\big(d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)\big)
is a one-dimensional vector space generated by

\theta\mapsto\cos(m\theta).

(3)

Furthermore, the range of
d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)
is closed and of co-dimension one.

(4)

As for the transversality condition, we have that

\partial_{\Omega}d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)\big[\cos(m\cdot)\big]\notin\textnormal{Range}\big(d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)\big).

Proof.

(1)
Let
h
be a function in
X_{m}^{\alpha}
given, for all
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, by the expansion

h(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}\cos(nm\theta),

for some scalars
h_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We also recall, in view of Lemma
3.4
, that

d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega,0)[h](\theta)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nmq_{nm}(\Omega,a)h_{n}\sin(nm\theta),

where, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we define

q_{nm}(\Omega,a)\overset{\rm def}{=}\Omega-\Omega_{nm}.

Observe that it is readily seen that

q_{nm}(\Omega,a)=0\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\Omega=\Omega_{nm}.

Hence, it follows that the kernel of
d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{nm},0)
is non trivial.

(2)
From the previous point, we know that the kernel of
d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)
is non trivial. To show that it is one-dimensional, it is sufficient to prove, for any integer
n\geq 2
, that

\Omega_{m}\neq\Omega_{nm}.

This fact is a consequence of the strict monotonicity of the sequence
\big(\Lambda_{n}(a,a)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
, previously proved in Proposition
3.1
-
(2)
. Consequently, we deduce that

\dim\Big(\ker\big(d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)\big)\Big)=1

and that an obvious generator of the kernel is the function

\theta\mapsto\cos(m\theta).

(3)
Now, we show that the range of
d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m}(a),0)
coincides with the space

Z_{m}^{\alpha}\overset{\rm def}{=}\left\{g\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}):g(\theta)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}g_{n}\sin(nm\theta),\ g_{n}\in\mathbb{R},\ \theta\in\mathbb{T}\right\},

which is clearly a closed sub-space of
Y_{m}^{\alpha}
with co-dimension one. This will take care of the third claim in the statement of the Proposition
3.3
, above. To see that, we emphasize first that

\textnormal{Range}\big(d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m},0)\big)\subset Z_{m}^{\alpha}.

Hence, it only remains to justify the converse of the preceding inclusion. To that end, we consider an element
g\in Z_{m}^{\alpha}
and taking the following form

g(\theta)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}g_{n}\sin(nm\theta),\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta\in\mathbb{T}.

Then, observe that the strict monotonicity of
\big(\Lambda_{nm}(a,a)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
in Proposition
3.1
-
(4)
implies that

(3.44)

q_{nm}(\Omega_{m},a)=\Lambda_{nm}(a,a)-\Lambda_{m}(a,a)<\Lambda_{2m}(a,a)-\Lambda_{m}(a,a)<0,

for all
n\geq 2.
Next, we introduce the function
h
by setting, for all
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

h(\theta)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}h_{n}\cos(nm\theta),\qquad h_{n}\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{-g_{n}}{nmq_{nm}(\Omega_{m},a)},\quad\textnormal{for all}\quad n\geq 2.

Clearly, we formally have
d_{r}\mathcal{F}(\Omega_{m}(a),0)[h]=g
and to make this construction rigorous it only remains to show that
h\in C^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{T})
. To that end, notice first that using (
3.44
) and applying Cauchy-Schwarz and Bessel inequalities, we get

\displaystyle\left\|h\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{|g_{n}|}{n}\lesssim\|g\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|g\|_{C^{\alpha}}.

Hence, we are left with proving that
h^{\prime}\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}).
To prove that, we first notice that

h^{\prime}(\theta)=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{g_{n}}{q_{nm}(\Omega_{m}(a),a)}\sin(nm\theta).

Then, let us define the sequence
\big(\widetilde{q}_{n}(a)\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0,1\}}
by

\widetilde{q}_{n}(a)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{q_{nm}(\Omega_{m}(a),a)}-\frac{1}{\Lambda_{m}(a,a)}=\frac{\Lambda_{nm}(a,a)}{\Lambda_{m}(a,a)[\Lambda_{nm}(a,a)-\Lambda_{m}(a,a)]}.

Observe that the preceding sequence is well-defined thanks to (
3.44

(3)
which implies, since

The next step consists in the study of singularities of the matrix

, previously introduced in Proposition

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
0<a_{2}<a_{1}
, there exists
N(a_{1},a_{2},b)\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that, for any
n\geqslant N(a_{1},a_{2},b),
there are two distinct angular velocities

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}^{\pm}\overset{\rm def}{=}\

\displaystyle\frac{Q(a_{1},a_{2})}{2}+\frac{\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})}{2}

\displaystyle\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(Q(a_{1},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)^{2}-4\Lambda_{n}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})}.

for which the matrix
M_{n}\big(\Omega_{n}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)
, introduced in (

Moreover, the sequences
\big(\Omega_{n}^{\pm}\big)_{n\geq N(a_{1},a_{2},b)}
are strictly monotone satisfying

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}^{+}=Q(a_{1},a_{2}),\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}^{-}=0

and

\Omega_{p}^{-}\neq\Omega_{q}^{+},

for all
p,q\geqslant N(a_{1},a_{2},b)
.

Proof.

Owing to (
4.5
), we first compute that

P_{n}(\Omega)\overset{\rm def}{=}\det\big(M_{n}(\Omega,a_{1},a_{2})\big)=\Omega^{2}+\beta_{n}(a_{1},a_{2})\Omega+\gamma_{n}(a_{1},a_{2}),

where

\beta_{n}(a_{1},a_{2})\overset{\rm def}{=}-Q(a_{1},a_{2})+\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2}),

and

\gamma_{n}(a_{1},a_{2})\overset{\rm def}{=}-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})\Big(\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-Q(a_{1},a_{2})\Big)+\Lambda_{n}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2}).

The discriminant of the preceding second order polynomial is given by

(4.7)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\beta^{2}_{n}(a_{1},a_{2})-4\gamma_{n}(a_{1},a_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(Q(a_{1},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)^{2}-4\Lambda_{n}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2}).

Hence, by virtue of the results of Proposition
3.1
, it is readily seen that

\Delta_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\big(Q(a_{1},a_{2})\big)^{2}>0.

Thus, there exists
N=N(a_{1},a_{2},b)\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that

(4.8)

\Delta_{n}>0,\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geqslant N.

Therefore, the polynomial
P_{n}
admits two distinct roots
\Omega_{n}^{\pm}
being as given as in the statement of the proposition. Note that, up to increasing the value of
N
if needed, we can assume that

(4.9)

Q(a_{1},a_{2})>\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})+\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2}),

for all
n\geq N
, which allows us to write that

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=Q(a_{1},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})+\mathtt{r}_{n}

and

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})-\mathtt{r}_{n},

where we set

\displaystyle\mathtt{r}_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2}\big(Q(a_{1},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{4\Lambda_{n}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})}{\left(Q(a_{1},a_{2})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)^{2}}}-1\right).

Moreover, observe, due to (
3.12
), (
3.14
), that

\mathtt{r}_{n}=O\left(\Lambda_{n}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})\right)=O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right),

whence,

\Omega_{n+1}^{+}-\Omega_{n}^{+}=\frac{b(a_{1})}{2n(n+1)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right)

and

\Omega_{n+1}^{-}-\Omega_{n}^{-}=-\frac{b(a_{2})}{2n(n+1)}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{3}}\right).

All in all, we deduce that the sequence
(\Omega_{n}^{+})_{n\geq N}
is strictly increasing whereas the sequence
(\Omega_{n}^{-})_{n\geq N}
is strictly decreasing. Hence, combining this with

\Omega_{n}^{-}<\Omega_{n}^{+},

for all
n\geq N
, yields that

\Omega_{p}^{-}<\Omega_{q}^{-}<\Omega_{q}^{+}<\Omega_{p}^{+},

for all
p>q\geq N,
thereby showing that there is no spectral collisions. This completes the proof of the proposition.

∎

Proposition 4.3

(3)

The transversality condition is satisfied, that is

\partial_{\Omega}d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)[r_{0}]\not\in\textnormal{Range}\Big(d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)\Big).

Proof.

(1)
According to the analysis laid out in Proposition

, we deduce from the simplicity of the spectrum that

\det\Big(M_{m}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)\Big)=0,

and, for all integer

\det\Big(M_{mn}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)\Big)\neq 0.

Consequently, in view of the representation (

at the value
\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)
, one deduces that the kernel of this operator is one-dimensional generated by the vector
r_{0}
as defined in the statement of the proposition.

(2)
From the strict monotonicity and limit properties of the sequences
\big(\Omega_{n}^{\pm}\big)_{n>N(a_{!},a_{2},b)}
, as

, obtained in Proposition

, we find that

\Omega_{m}^{\pm}\not\in\left\{Q(a_{1},a_{2}),0\right\}.

Therefore, by virtue of Proposition

Hence, combining this with the one-dimensional property of its kernel, established before, we deduce that the range of the this operator is closed and of co-dimension one.

Now, let us be more precise about the range of the linearized operator. To that end, we endow the vector space
Y_{m}^{\alpha}\times Y_{m}^{\alpha}
with the pre-Hilbertian structure given by the following

\displaystyle\Bigg(\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\sin(nm\theta),\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\sin(nm\theta)\right)

\displaystyle\left|\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}\sin(nm\theta),\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n}\sin(nm\theta)\right)\right.\Bigg)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}b_{n}+c_{n}d_{n}.

Then, observe that

u_{0,m}^{\pm}\overset{\rm def}{=}\begin{pmatrix}\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\vskip 5.69054pt\\
\frac{b(a_{2})}{b(a_{1})}\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{2})\end{pmatrix}\in\ker\Big(M_{m}^{\top}\big(\Omega^{\pm}_{m},a_{1},a_{2}\big)\Big).

The notation

.
Hence, defining

y_{0}:\theta\mapsto u_{0,m}^{\pm}\sin(m\theta),

we then write in view of
(

), for any
(h_{1},h_{2})\in X_{m}^{\alpha}\times X_{m}^{\alpha}
given by

h_{1}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}^{(1)}\cos(nm\theta),\qquad h_{2}(\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}^{(2)}\cos(nm\theta),

for some

, that

\displaystyle\left(d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)[h_{1},h_{2}]\big|y_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=-m\left\langle M_{m}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}^{(1)}\\
h_{1}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},u_{0,m}^{\pm}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}

\displaystyle=-m\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}^{(1)}\\
h_{1}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix},M_{m}^{\top}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)u_{0,m}^{\pm}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

where, we utilize the notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}
for the Euclidean scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
This proves that

\textnormal{Range}\Big(d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)\Big)\subset\mathtt{span}_{\alpha}^{\perp_{(\cdot|\cdot)}}(y_{0}),

where

\mathtt{span}_{\alpha}^{\perp_{(\cdot|\cdot)}}(y_{0})\overset{\rm def}{=}\Big\{y\in Y_{m}^{\alpha}\times Y_{m}^{\alpha}:\;(y|y_{0})=0\Big\}.

Now, since
\mathtt{span}(y_{0})
has a finite dimension which is equal to one, then the orthogonal supplementary theorem yields that

Y_{m}^{\alpha}\times Y_{m}^{\alpha}=\mathtt{span}(y_{0})\oplus\mathtt{span}_{\alpha}^{\perp_{(\cdot|\cdot)}}(y_{0}).

This proves that
\mathtt{span}_{\alpha}^{\perp_{(\cdot|\cdot)}}(y_{0})
is of codimension one in
Y_{m}^{\alpha}\times Y_{m}^{\alpha}.
The range being also of codimension one in this space, then applying

\textnormal{Range}\Big(d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)\Big)=\mathtt{span}_{\alpha}^{\perp_{(\cdot|\cdot)}}(y_{0}).

(3)
According to (
4.10
), notice that the transversality property holds once we show that the quantity

\displaystyle\Big(\partial_{\Omega}d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)[r_{0}]|y_{0}\Big)=\left(\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)^{2}-\Lambda_{m}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})

does not vanish.
To that end, exploiting the fact that

\det\Big(M_{m}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},a_{1},a_{2}\big)\Big)=0,

which allows us to write

\displaystyle-\Lambda_{m}^{2}(a_{1},a_{2})

\displaystyle=\left(\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)\left(\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-Q(a_{1},a_{2})+\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})\right),

we then obtain that

\displaystyle\Big(\partial_{\Omega}

\displaystyle d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)[r_{0}]|y_{0}\Big)

\displaystyle=\left(\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\right)\left(2\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-Q(a_{1},a_{2})+\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})\right).

Therefore, we deduce that the identity

\Big(\partial_{\Omega}d_{(r_{1},r_{2})}\mathcal{G}\big(\Omega_{m}^{\pm},0,0\big)[r_{0}]|y_{0}\Big)=0

holds if and only if

2\Omega_{m}^{\pm}-Q(a_{1},a_{2})+\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})-\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})=0

or

\Omega_{m}^{\pm}=\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2}).

Now, by virtue of the expressions of
\Omega_{m}^{\pm}
given in (

\Delta_{m}(a_{1},a_{2})=0

\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{1})+\Lambda_{m}(a_{2},a_{2})-Q(a_{1},a_{2})=\pm\sqrt{\Delta_{m}(a_{1},a_{2})},

which, upon raising to the power-two, yields that

\Lambda_{m}(a_{1},a_{2})=0.

Notably, this identity is violated by the third result in Proposition
3.1

where

Here, we recall the celebrated Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem which was utilized to prove our main theorems.

Theorem A.1

(Crandall-Rabinowitz
[
11
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two Banach spaces. Consider
V\subset X
to be a neighborhood of

such that

Assume that

enjoys the following properties.

(1)

Existence of trivial branch:

(2)

\partial_{\Omega}F
,

(3)

in

such that

and

\Big\{(\Omega,x)\in U:F(\Omega,x)=0\Big\}=\Big\{\big(\psi(s),sx_{0}+s\phi(s)\big):\;|s|<a\Big\}\cup\Big\{(\Omega,0)\in U\Big\}.

B.1.
Elliptic regularity

We begin with providing a full justification of Proposition
2.1
which establishes the primary elliptic regularities enjoyed by the solutions of (

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We begin with splitting the solution of (
2.1
) as
\psi_{b}=\psi_{b,\text{rad}}+\bar{\psi}_{b}
, where each part of this decomposition is governed by its own elliptic equation

(B.1)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\psi_{b,\text{rad}}\right)=f_{\text{rad}}

and

(B.2)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right)=\bar{f},

where,
f_{\text{rad}}
refers to the radially symmetric part of
f
which is defined by

(B.3)

\displaystyle f_{\text{rad}}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta,

where,
r\overset{\rm def}{=}|x|
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whereas
\bar{f}\overset{\rm def}{=}f-f_{\text{rad}}
denotes the remaining part of the source term which satisfies

(B.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}(x)dx=0.

Now, observe that, from their definitions,
f_{\text{rad}}
and
\bar{f}
enjoy the bounds

\left\|f_{\text{rad}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

and

\left\|\bar{f}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq 2\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Noticing, moreover, that
f_{\text{rad}}
and
b
are radial functions, by construction and due to (
Hb3
) respectively, one can easily solve (
B.1
) using radial coordinates to show that the function

(B.5)

\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}-\int_{0}^{r}\frac{b(s)}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau ds,\quad\text{for all}\quad r\overset{\rm def}{=}|x|\geq 0

is an exact solution of (
B.1
). In order to show that, we only need to recast (
B.1
) in the radial coordinates, which writes as

-\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{r\frac{d}{dr}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)}{b(r)}\right)=rf_{\text{rad}}(r),

for all
r>0
. It follows then that the unique solution of this second order ODE with boundary conditions

\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(0)=\frac{d}{dr}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(0)=0

is then given by (
B.5
). We are not going to show that these boundary conditions are satisfied as this will not serve the purpose of our results; but one can show that by employing the ideas that we develop below.

Let us now take care of the bounds on
\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
at first as they will be obtained by direct estimates on its explicit expression. To that end, computing, for all
r>0,
that

\frac{d}{dr}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)=-\frac{b(r)}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau,

and, whence

-\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)=\frac{b^{\prime}(r)}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau ds-\frac{b(r)}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau ds+b(r)f_{\text{rad}}(r).

Therefore, it is readily seen that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|b^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left(\int_{0}^{R_{\infty}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tau|f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)|d\tau\right)^{m}rdr\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}

\displaystyle+\left\|b\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\tau|f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)|d\tau\right)^{m}rdr\right)^{\frac{1}{m}}

\displaystyle+\left\|b\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
, where we have used (
Hb3
) to localize the first integral in the right-hand side on a bounded set. Thus, writing, by rescaling, that

\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau=\int_{0}^{1}\sigma rf_{\text{rad}}(\sigma r)d\sigma,

yields

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla^{2}\psi_{b,\text{rad}}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|b\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left\|f(\sigma\cdot)\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\sigma d\sigma+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|b\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\sigma^{1-\frac{2}{m}}d\sigma+1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|b\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

We turn our attention now to the control of
\bar{\psi}_{b}
. To that end, we first notice that, the assumptions on
f
combined with the property (
B.4
) allows us to employ Lemma
B.3
below and deduce that
\bar{f}\in\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

\left\|\bar{f}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Thus, performing an
L^{2}
energy estimate for (
B.2
) leads to the bound

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b(x)}|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}(x)\bar{\psi}_{b}(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\bar{f}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

whence

\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

We now establish the remaining bounds on
\bar{\psi}_{b}
by a bootstrap argument. To see that, we expand the derivatives in the left-hand side of (
B.2
) to obtain that

(B.6)

-\Delta\bar{\psi}_{b}=b\bar{f}-\frac{\nabla b}{b}\cdot\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}.

Then, observing that the right-hand side above belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, it then follows, by virtue of classical elliptic regularity, that

\left\|\nabla^{2}\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

This allows us to deduce, by interpolation argument, that

\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\nabla^{2}\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
n\in[2,\infty)
. Accordingly, this new bound combined with the assumptions on
b
imply that the right-hand side of (
B.6
) belongs, after all, to
L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for all
m\in(1,p]
, with

\left\|b\bar{f}-\frac{\nabla b}{b}\cdot\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

where we have employed Hölders’ inequalities.
At last, by means of classical elliptic regularity of Poisson’s equation (
B.6
), we conclude that

(B.7)

\left\|\nabla^{2}\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
, thereby completing the proof of the proposition.
∎

\left\|\nabla\psi_{b,\text{rad}}\right\|_{L^{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\qquad\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{\kappa}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

(B.8)

Our next concern is to show that any solution of (

Lemma B.1
.

Let

), (

) and (

\psi_{b}
of the elliptic equation (

\left\|\nabla\psi_{b}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Proof.

We begin with decomposing the solution as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
by writing

\psi_{b}=\psi_{b,\text{rad}}+\bar{\psi}_{b},

where
\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
is defined in (

, introduced in (

\displaystyle|\nabla\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)|

for all

follows. Next, we take care of
\bar{\psi}_{b}
. To that end, we recall from (

2.1
) enjoys the bound

\left\|\psi_{b}-\psi_{b,rad}-c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
is given by (
B.5
) and satisfies the bound

\left\|\psi_{b,\text{rad}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{A})}\lesssim_{b}A\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for any ball
B_{A}
of finite radius
A>0
.

Proof.

We begin with the control of
\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
which directly follows from its explicit expression. More precisely, we obtain from (
B.5
) that

\displaystyle|\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)|

\displaystyle\leq r\sup_{0<\sigma\leq r}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}|\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)|d\tau\right),

for all
r>0
.
Therefore, the boundedness of
\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
follows by applying Hölder’s inequality, as is shown in the proof of the preceding lemma, to find that

\displaystyle|\psi_{b,\text{rad}}(r)|

\displaystyle\lesssim r\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
r>0
. Now, we turn our attention to the control of
\bar{\psi}_{b}\overset{\rm def}{=}\psi_{b}-\psi_{b,\text{rad}}
by first recalling that it is governed by the equation

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right)=\bar{f},

where
\bar{f}
is mean-free. More precisely, this source term satisfies that

(B.10)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{f}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta=0,

for all
r>0
. In light of this, it follows that the function

r\mapsto\bar{\phi}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{\psi}_{b}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta

is a radial solution of the same elliptic equation, i.e.

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{\phi}\right)=0,

whence is constant. The rigorous justification of that is a consequence of the analysis laid out in a subsequent section (see in particular Theorem
B.1
, below). One deduces then that

(B.11)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\partial_{r}\bar{\psi}_{b}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta=\frac{d}{dr}\bar{\phi}\equiv 0.

Therefore, one sees, by expanding the equation governing
\bar{\psi}_{b}
that this latter obeys the Poisson equation

-\Delta\bar{\psi}_{b}=b\bar{f}-\frac{b^{\prime}}{b}\partial_{r}\bar{\psi}_{b}\overset{\rm def}{=}\bar{F},

where the new source term
\bar{F}
is mean-free. More precisely, one can easily check, by virtue of (
B.10
) and (
B.11
), that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{F}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta=0,

for all
r>0
. Thus, we emphasize that Poisson’s equation

-\Delta u=\bar{F}

admits a unique solution
u
in
\dot{H}^{1}
. In other words, all solutions with finite square-integrable gradient coincide up to constants. This will be justified in a subsequent section (see Theorem
B.1
, again, for
b\equiv 1
). As a consequence, owing to the fundamental representation of solutions of Poisson’s equation, we deduce that there is
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{b}(x)-c

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\bar{F}(y)dy,

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We now focus our attention on the boundedness of the function in right-hand side. To that end, we first notice that
\bar{F}
is compactly supported, thanks to the assumptions on the source term
f
and the coefficient
b
. Thus, it holds that

\bar{\psi}_{b}(x)-c=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|y|\leq A}\log|x-y|\bar{F}(y)dy,

for any
A>0
with
\operatorname{supp}f\subset B_{A}
and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence, by further employing the fact that
\bar{F}
is mean-free, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Big|\bar{\psi}_{b}(x)-c\Big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq A+1}\int_{|y|\leq A}\log|x-y|\bar{F}(y)dy+\mathds{1}_{|x|>A+1}\int_{|y|\leq A}\log\left(\frac{|x-y|}{|x|}\right)\bar{F}(y)dy\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{A}\left\|\bar{F}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where we utilized the elementary facts that

(x,y)\mapsto\log|x-y|\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq A+1}\mathds{1}_{|y|\leq A}\in L^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{x};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{y}^{2})\big)

and

(x,y)\mapsto\log|x-y|\mathds{1}_{|x|>A+1}\mathds{1}_{|y|\leq A}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}).

It then remains to control
\bar{F}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
which can be established by employing Hölder’s inequality to infer that

\left\|\bar{F}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\nabla\bar{\psi}_{b}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Consequently, by a further use of (
B.8
), we deduce that

\left\|\bar{\psi}_{b}-c\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

which is the expected control of
\bar{\psi}_{b}
, thereby completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

B.2.
Existence and uniqueness of solutions

In this section, we show the existence and uniqueness, in a sense that will be precised later, of solutions to the elliptic problem (
2.1
) in the whole domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here, we only discuss the case of a variable coefficient
b
obeying certain conditions that are directly connected to our interest in the present paper. We refer to
[
45
]
for a nice and self-contained notes where the content of the first paragraph of this section is further presented in more details and full generality, along with several examples and discussions on relevant topics.

B.2.1.
Functional framework and introduction to the equations

To get started, allow us to clarify a few things about the functional space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that we were using in the previous few sections as well as the appropriate far–boundary conditions that we should supplement (
2.1
) with as
|x|\to\infty
.
Henceforth, we follow the notations from
[
45
]
by introducing, for any measurable function
u
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, its equivalence class defined by

[u]\overset{\rm def}{=}\{u+c:c\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Denoting by
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
the space of smooth and compactly supported functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we further introduce its equivalence class

\dot{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\overset{\rm def}{=}\big\{[u]:u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\big\}.

Therefore, the Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined as

\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\overset{\rm def}{=}\big\{[u]:u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\big\},

which can be shown to coincide with the space corresponding to the completion of
\dot{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by the norm
\left\|[\cdot]\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
that we now define as

\left\|[u]\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\overset{\rm def}{=}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for any
u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The justification of the fact that the preceding mapping is a norm on
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is straightforward and omitted here. Note in passing that
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a Hilbert space when endowed with the inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}\overset{\rm def}{=}\langle\nabla\cdot,\nabla\cdot\rangle_{L^{2}}.

At last, the topological dual of
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
in the usual
L^{2}
-duality sense is denoted by
\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
which can be characterized as

\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\big\{\operatorname{div}F:F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\big\},

where
\operatorname{div}F
is to be understood in the distributional sense. In a similar fashion, one can define the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{W}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and its corresponding dual space
\dot{W}^{-1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, for any
p\in(1,\infty)
.
One fundamental example that is relevant to the scope of this section is given in the following lemma.

Lemma B.3
.

Let
f
be a mean-free function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Assume further that
f
has an integrable first moment, i.e., that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|xf(x)|dx<\infty.

Then,
f
belongs to
\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. More precisely, there exists
C>0
such that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)u(x)dx\right|\leq C\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The proof uses Fourier analysis and is detailed in
[
45
]
. Note in passing that, as a direct consequence of the previous lemma, if
f
is compactly supported, mean-free function and belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then it lies in
\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In particular, it holds that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}fu(x)dx\right|\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for any
u\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Now, we want to discuss the existence of weak solutions (we will precise the sense of that in a moment) of the elliptic problem

(B.12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla u\right)&=f,\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
u&\sim 0,\quad\text{as}\quad|x|\to\infty,\end{aligned}\right.

where the far–boundary conditions are understood in the sense of the equivalence class defined at the beginning of this section,
b
is a fixed non-negative function and the source term
f
is a given function whose properties will be made precise, later on.

B.2.2.
Compatibility of the far–boundary conditions and the source term

Before we step over the key elements to showing the existence of weak solutions of (
B.12
), allow us first to highlight a very important observation that connects the far–boundary conditions in (
B.12
) with admissible properties (or let us say necessary assumptions) of the source term
f
so that the first and the second lines in that system are, in some sense, compatible.

To see that, we now assume that the source term is a radial function and we look for a radial solution of (
B.12
). As this is already done in the foregoing sections, we just recall that, in this case, the solution of the first line in (
B.12
) is, up to constants, given by its explicit expression

u_{\text{rad}}(r)=-\int_{0}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau f(\tau)d\tau d\sigma,

for all
r>0
. We are now going to show that, if
f
has a non-vanishing mean (which is also assumed to be a positive function here for simplicity, though the hypothesis on its sign can completely be removed), then the far–boundary condition in (
B.12
) as
r\to\infty
are strongly violated by the solution
u_{\text{rad}}
. To that end, under the preceding assumptions on
f
, it is readily seen that there is
A>0
such that, for any
\sigma\geq A
, it holds that

\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau f(\tau)d\tau>\frac{m}{4\pi}>0,

where we denoted

m\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)dx=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau f(\tau)d\tau.

it then follows that

\displaystyle\left|u_{\text{rad}}(r)\right|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau f(\tau)d\tau d\sigma\geq\left(\frac{m}{4\pi}\inf_{\sigma>0}b(\sigma)\right)\int_{A}^{r}\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}\geq\left(\frac{m}{4\pi}\inf_{\sigma>0}b(\sigma)\right)\log r,

for all
r>A
. This clearly leads to the unboundedness of
u_{\text{rad}}
at infinity, thereby violating the far–boundary conditions in (
B.12
). Note that the preceding
log
-growth is optimal as, it is by a direct estimate, that one can show, moreover, that

|u_{\text{rad}}(r)|\leq O(1)+\left(\frac{m}{2\pi}\sup_{\sigma>0}b(\sigma)\right)\log r,

for all
r>0
.
This particular example of solutions shows that the finite far–boundary conditions are not compatible with source terms with non-vanishing mean.
This is not the end of the story for (
B.12
) as we will show in the next section that this problem admits a unique weak solution in an affine space which is made of shifting
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by the typical “bad” solution
u_{\text{rad}}
.

At the end, allow us to conclude this section by mentioning that the consideration of the space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+u_{\text{rad}}
is strongly motivated by the seminal work of Chemin
[
9
]
(see in particular Lemma 1.3.1 in
[
9
]
) where he sheds light on a similar observation that permits to uniquely solve (
B.12
) in the whole domain
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and in the case
b\equiv 1
. More precisely, it is shown in
[
9
]
that a divergence free velocity field is uniquely derived from a given two-dimensional vorticity
\omega
and a “stationary” vector field
\sigma
in the affine space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})+\sigma
. In the next section, we provide a generalization of that in the case of variable coefficient
b
.

B.2.3.
Existence and uniqueness of weak solutions

Here, we shall establish a well-posedness result for (
B.12
). To that end, allow us first to recall the notion of weak solutions that we are concerned with. We say that
u
is a weak solution to (
B.12
), for a fixed coefficient
b>0
and a given source term
f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, if it belongs to
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and satisfies the following weak reformulation

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b(x)}\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x)v(x)dx,

for every test function
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

The central result of this section is now stated in the next theorem which builds on
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]
and whose elements of proof rely on the ingredients from the previous sections.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
b
be a non-negative, radially symmetric function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
f
be a function in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Assume further that the first moment of
f
is integrable, as well. Then, there is a unique weak solution
u
of the elliptic problem (
B.12
) with source term
f
in the affine space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})+u_{\text{rad}}
, where
u_{\text{rad}}
is given by

u_{\text{rad}}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}-\int_{0}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau d\sigma,

for all
r\geq 0
, and
f_{\text{rad}}
stands for the radially symmetric part of
f
, which is defined by

f_{\text{rad}}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta,

for all
r\geq 0
. Moreover, it holds that

\left\|\nabla(u-u_{\text{rad}})\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Proof.

Observe that
u_{\text{rad}}
belongs to
C^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\dot{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, which can be justified along the same lines as is shown in the proofs of Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
B.2
above, and solves (
B.12
) with source term
f_{\text{rad}}
. Thus, it is readily seen that
u_{\text{rad}}
is also a solution of the variational problem

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b(x)}\nabla u_{\text{rad}}(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{\text{rad}}(x)v(x)dx,

for every test function
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Therefore, we now need to show the existence of a unique solution
\bar{u}\overset{\rm def}{=}u-u_{\text{rad}}
in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of the equation

(B.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b(x)}\nabla\bar{u}(x)\cdot\nabla v(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}(x)v(x)dx,

for all test function
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, where we set
\bar{f}\overset{\rm def}{=}f-f_{\text{rad}}
. It is straightforward to check that
\bar{f}
is a mean-free function that satisfies all the assumptions in Lemma
B.3
; whence, it follows that

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}(x)v(x)dx\right|\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}((1+|x|)dx;\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|\nabla v\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for any test function
v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. That is to say that

v\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}(x)v(x)dx

belongs to
\dot{H}^{-1}
. On the other hand, it is readily seen that the bilinear operator

(v_{1},v_{2})\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b(x)}\nabla v_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla v_{2}(x)dx

defines a scalar product on the Hilbert space
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
which is equivalent to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\dot{H}^{1}}
, due to the assumptions on the function
b
. At last, the existence of a unique solution
w
of (
B.13
) in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is achieved by a direct application of Reisz’s representation theorem, thereby concluding the proof of the theorem.
∎

B.3.
A notion of self-adjointness

Here, we establish a property of solutions of (
2.1
) that is, in some sense, a soft version of the self-adjointness of the elliptic operator

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\cdot\right).

The details of that are given in the next lemma.

Lemma B.4
.

For any given test functions
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

(B.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{1}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{2}(x)dx=1,

there is a couple of solutions of (
B.12
) with source terms
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
, denoted by
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, respectively, such that

(B.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{1}\omega_{2}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{1}g_{2}(x)dx.

In addition to that, any couple of solutions
(h_{1},h_{2})
of (
2.1
) with respective source terms
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
is also a solution of (
B.15
) if and only if

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(h_{1}-h_{2})(x)=0.

Remark B.1
.

We emphasize that the preceding lemma can be generalized to hold for any test functions
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
with non-vanishing means that are not necessarily identical. This will hold up to a suitable rescaling argument. As this is not going to serve in our paper, we only restrict our selves to the case of test functions with unitary means.

Remark B.2
.

Note that, under the non-vanishing assumptions on the means of the test functions (
B.14
), Lemma
B.4
above provides us with a characterization of all admissible solutions
(g_{1},g_{2})
of (
2.1
) and (
B.15
). Notice, furthermore, that such characterization is clearly not valid if one of the test functions (or both of them) is mean-free. Indeed, in such situations, (
B.15
) is invariant by translating solutions by any constants.

Proof.

We begin with introducing the unique couple of solutions that satisfies the vanishing far-boundary solutions by setting

(B.16)

g_{i}\overset{\rm def}{=}g_{i,rad}+\bar{g}_{i},

where, for a fixed real number
A>0
such that

\operatorname{supp}\omega_{1},\operatorname{supp}\omega_{2}\subset B_{A},

we explicitly define
g_{i,rad}
, for all
r\geq 0
, by

g_{i,rad}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}-\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau\omega_{i,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau,

with

\omega_{i,\text{rad}}(r)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\omega_{i}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta,

and
\bar{g}_{i}
is defined as the unique solution of (
2.1
) with vanishing far-boundary condition

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\bar{g}_{i}(x)=0,

and source term

f=\bar{\omega}_{i}\overset{\rm def}{=}\omega_{i}-\omega_{i,\text{rad}}.

Note that the far-boundary conditions are given in classical sense since
\bar{g}_{i}
is continuous, thanks to Lemma
B.1
. In this sense, the solution
g_{i}
of (
2.1
) is uniquely defined, for any
i\in\{1,2\}
.

We now claim that
(g_{1},g_{2})
is a solution of (
B.15
). To show that, we write, by definition of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{1}\omega_{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{g}_{1}\bar{\omega}_{2}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{1,rad}\omega_{2,rad}(x)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}g_{1,rad}\bar{\omega}_{2}(x)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{g}_{1}\omega_{2,rad}(x)dx

\displaystyle\overset{\rm def}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathcal{K}_{j},

where each term in the preceding sum will be dealt with separately.

About
\mathcal{K}_{1}

The first term
\mathcal{K}_{1}
is the easiest one and can be dealt with by simple integrations by parts

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{g}_{1}\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{g}_{2}\right)(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{g}_{1}\cdot\nabla\bar{g}_{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{g}_{1}\right)\bar{g}_{2}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{\omega}_{1}\bar{g}_{2}(x)dx,

where all the foregoing computations are justified because both
\bar{g}_{1}
and
\bar{g}_{2}
belong to
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

About
\mathcal{K}_{2}

We turn our attention now to deal with
\mathcal{K}_{2}
where we are going to employ the support properties of the test functions
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
as well as the explicit expression of the solutions
g_{1,\text{rad}}
and
g_{2,\text{rad}}
in the case where the source terms are radially symmetric. To see that, we write by direct computations that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2}=-2\pi\int_{0}^{A}\left(\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau\omega_{1,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau d\sigma\right)r\omega_{2,rad}(r)dr.

Thus, by means of two integration by parts, which are fully justified due to the fact that

\frac{d}{dr}g_{i,\text{rad}}\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, we infer that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{2}

\displaystyle=-2\pi\int_{0}^{A}\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau\omega_{1,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau\frac{d}{dr}\left(\int_{0}^{r}\sigma\omega_{2,rad}(\sigma)d\sigma\right)dr

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{A}\frac{b(r)}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\tau\omega_{1,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau\int_{0}^{r}\sigma\omega_{2,rad}(\sigma)d\sigma dr

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{A}\int_{0}^{r}\tau\omega_{1,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau\frac{d}{dr}\left(\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau\omega_{2,rad}(\tau)d\tau d\sigma\right)dr

\displaystyle=-2\pi\int_{0}^{A}r\omega_{1,\text{rad}}(r)\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau\omega_{2,rad}(\tau)d\tau d\sigma dr.

Thus, we deduce that

\mathcal{K}_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{1,rad}\cdot g_{2,\text{rad}}(x)dx.

About
\mathcal{K}_{3}
and
\mathcal{K}_{4}

We finally turn our attention to compute the terms
\mathcal{K}_{3}
and
\mathcal{K}_{4}
. to that end, observing from the definition of
\bar{\omega}_{1}
and
\bar{\omega}_{2}
that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{\omega}_{i}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta=0,

for all
r\geq 0
, yields that

\mathcal{K}_{3}=\int_{0}^{A}g_{1,\text{rad}}(r)\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{\omega}_{2}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta\right)rdr=0.

Moreover, noticing that

-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\right)=-\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{b}\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{1}{b}\frac{\partial_{r}}{r}+\frac{1}{b}\frac{\partial_{\theta\theta}}{r^{2}},

it is readily seen, since
\bar{g}_{i}
solves the equations

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla\bar{g}_{i}\right)&=\bar{\omega}_{i},\\
\bar{g}_{i}(x)&=0,\quad\text{as}\quad|x|\to\infty,\end{aligned}\right.

for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{g}_{i}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta=0,

for all
r\geq 0
. Therefore, one sees that

\mathcal{K}_{3}=\int_{0}^{A}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\bar{g}_{1}\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\big)d\theta\right)\omega_{2,rad}(r)rdr=0.

All in all, gathering the previous identities yields the validity of (
B.15
) with the particular choice of solutions
(g_{1},g_{2})
introduced in (
B.16
).

Now, let
(h_{1},h_{2})
be another couple of solutions of (
B.15
). Thus, as
h_{i}
and
g_{i}
are also solutions to the elliptic equation (
2.1
) with the same source term
\omega_{i}
, for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, then, by uniqueness from Theorem
B.1
, there exist two real numbers
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

(h_{1},h_{2})=\left(g_{1}+c_{1},g_{2}+c_{2}\right).

Therefore, by plugging this identity in (
B.15
), we infer that
c_{1}=c_{2}
. Hence, we deduce that

h_{1}-h_{2}=g_{1}-g_{2},

which yields that

(B.17)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(h_{1}-h_{2})(x)=\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(g_{1}-g_{2})(x).

Let us now show that
g_{1}-g_{2}
satisfies the vanishing far-boundary conditions. Note that, by definition of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
(see (
B.16
)), this is reduced to showing that

(B.18)

\lim_{r\to\infty}\big(g_{1,rad}-g_{2,rad}\big)(r)=0.

To see that, we further notice that for large values of
r
, in particular for any
r>A
, it holds that

g_{i,rad}(r)=\left(\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}d\sigma\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\tau\omega_{i,\text{rad}}(\tau)d\tau\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{A}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}d\sigma,

for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, where we utilized the assumption that
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are supported in the ball
B_{A}
and with unitary means. Consequently, we deduce the validity of (
B.18
) which yields that (
B.17
) holds, as well.

Conversely, we now assume that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are two solutions of the elliptic equation (
2.1
) with respective source terms
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
such that

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}(h_{1}-h_{2})(x)=0.

Therefore, again, by uniqueness of solutions to (
2.1
), there exist two real numbers
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

(h_{1},h_{2})=\left(g_{1}+c_{1},g_{2}+c_{2}\right).

Thus, it follows that

c_{1}-c_{2}=\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\Big((h_{1}-h_{2})-(g_{1}-g_{2})\Big)(x)=0.

Hence, one deduces that

(h_{1},h_{2})=\left(g_{1}+c,g_{2}+c\right),

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. At last, by virtue of the assumption (
B.14
),
one readily sees that

(h_{1},h_{2})
solves (
B.15
) as soon as
(g_{1},g_{2})
does so. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

B.4.
Singular kernel and proof of Proposition
2.2

Here, we shall provide a full justification of Proposition
2.2
which played a central role in the proof of our main theorems.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

The three bounds in the statement of the proposition are consequences of Proposition
2.1
and Lemma
B.2
. Indeed, by a direct application of the estimates therein, we obtain the controls, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\left\|\nabla^{2}S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\sqrt{b(y)}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\right)\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
, and

\left\|\nabla S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\sqrt{b(y)}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\right)\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for all
n\in(2,p]
, as well as

\left\|S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}\lesssim_{b}\sqrt{b(y)}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

for any ball
B_{\rho}
or radius
\rho>0
.
Therefore, by further employing assumption (
Hb3
), it follows that

\left\|\nabla^{2}S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|b\right\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\right\|_{L^{m}(B_{R_{\infty}})},

for all
m\in(1,p]
, and

\left\|\nabla S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|b\right\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\right\|_{L^{n}(B_{R_{\infty}})},

for all
n\in(2,p]
, as well as

\left\|S_{b}(\cdot,y)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\rho})}\lesssim_{b}\left\|b\right\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\left\|\log|\cdot-y|\right\|_{L^{2}(B_{R_{\infty}})}.

Consequently, the claimed bounds on
S_{b}
are obtained by taking the supremum on
y
over any compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Once
S_{b}
is constructed, it is then by a direct computation that one can show that the function

x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{b}(x,y)f(y)dy,

solves the elliptic equation (
2.1
), where
K_{b}
is as introduced in the statement of the proposition.
We are now left with the proof of the properties of
K_{b}
. Before doing so, let us notice that
K_{b}
is determined up to constants. Thus, we now select a suitable representative and prove that this one satisfies all symmetries in the statement of the proposition. At the end, since all the symmetry properties are stable by shifting the solution by constants, the same properties will eventually remain satisfied by any kernel
K_{b}
.

The choice of the representative kernel draws insight from Lemma
B.4
and is done by first selecting the unique solution
S_{b}
of (
2.8
) by setting

(B.19)

S_{b}(x,y)=S_{b,rad}(|x|,y)+\bar{S}_{b}(x,y),

where
S_{b,rad}
is explicitly defined by

S_{b,rad}(r,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}-\int_{R_{\infty}}^{r}\frac{b(\sigma)}{\sigma}\int_{0}^{\sigma}\tau f_{\text{rad}}(\tau,y)d\tau+\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})b(y)}\log R_{\infty},

where
R_{\infty}>0
is the radius of the ball introduced in (
Hb3
), and
f_{\text{rad}}
denotes the radially symmetric part of
f
with respect to the
x
variable, i.e.,

f_{\text{rad}}(r,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f\big(r(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),y\big)d\theta,

where

f(x,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(y)}\Delta_{x}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(x)}}\right),

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
y\neq x
, and where
\bar{S}_{b}(\cdot,y)
is the unique solution of the elliptic equation (
2.1
) with source term
f-f_{\text{rad}}
and vanishing far-boundary conditions, i.e.

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\bar{S}_{b}(x,y)=0,

for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Note in passing that

(B.20)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)dx=1-\frac{\sqrt{b(y)}}{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}},

for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Indeed, to see that, we first observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)dx=\frac{\sqrt{b(y)}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\Delta_{x}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(x)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}}\right)dx,

for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, as the function

x\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(x)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}}

is continuous and compactly supported, thanks to the assumption (
Hb3
), it then follows that it belongs to
L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. One then sees, by a direct integration by parts, that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)dx

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b(y)}\left((-\Delta_{y})\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(x)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}}\right)dx\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b(y)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(y)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}}\right),

where we utilized the fact that

(x,y)\mapsto-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|

is the fundamental solution of the Laplace operator
(-\Delta)
in dimension two of space. This shows the validity of (
B.20
) which will come in handy later on. It is to be emphasized in a moment that the correction by the term

\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})b(y)}\log R_{\infty}

in the definition of
S_{b,\text{rad}}
is carefully chosen to compensate the behavior, as
|x|\to\infty
, of the first term in the right-hand side of (
2.10
). Indeed, in view of the very definition of
S_{b,\text{rad}}
above, we emphasize that

(B.21)

S_{b,\text{rad}}(r,y)=-\frac{b(R_{\infty})}{2\pi}\log\left(\frac{r}{R_{\infty}}\right)+\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})b(y)}\log r,

for all
r\geq R_{\infty}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. To see that, it is enough to notice that

S_{b,rad}(r,y)=-\frac{b(R_{\infty})}{2\pi}\left(\int_{R_{\infty}}^{r}\frac{d\sigma}{\sigma}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(x,y)dx+\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})b(y)}\log R_{\infty},

for large values of
r\geq R_{\infty}
and all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where we employed the assumption (
Hb3
), again. Therefore, the expansion (
B.21
) follows by incorporating (
B.20
) in the preceding identity.

Let us now move on to the proof of the remaining claims in the proposition. To that end, in term of the definition of
S_{b}
above, we now select the kernel
K_{b}
by setting

K_{b}(x,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}+S_{b}(x,y),

and we emphasize, again, that it can be shown by a direct computations—which do not rely on the very fine definition of
S_{b}
given in (
B.19
), but only the fact that it solves (
2.8
)—that the function

x\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{b}(x,y)\omega(y)dy

is a distributional solution of (
2.1
) with source term
\omega
, for any
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Moreover, this distributional solution can be shown to satisfy the elliptic regularity estimates of Proposition
2.1
. We again skip the proof of that here and refer to
[
44
]
.

Now, we turn our attention to prove the symmetry properties of
K_{b}
. To that end, due to the assumption (
Hb4
), observing that the function

(x,y)\mapsto-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}

satisfies all the symmetry properties claimed in the statement of the proposition, then, we only need to show that
S_{b}(x,y)
does not perturb these symmetries. The principle step towards showing that consists in justifying that the two solutions of (
2.1
) given by

\psi_{b}[\omega_{i}]\overset{\rm def}{=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K_{b}(\cdot,y)\omega_{i}(y)dy,\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\{1,2\}

are also solutions to equation (
B.15
). That is to say, we claim that

(B.22)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{2}\psi_{b}[\omega_{1}](x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{1}\psi_{b}[\omega_{2}](x)dx,

for any test functions
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

(B.23)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{1}(x)dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{2}(x)dx=1.

Let us assume the validity of (
B.22
) for a moment and first continue the proof of (
2.11
). In view of (
B.22
) and the fact that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}\omega_{1}(x)\omega_{2}(y)dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}\omega_{1}(y)\omega_{2}(x)dxdy,

it is readily seen that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}S_{b}(x,y)\omega_{1}(x)\omega_{2}(y)dxdy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}S_{b}(x,y)\omega_{1}(y)\omega_{2}(x)dxdy,

whereby we deduce that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Big(S_{b}(x,y)-S_{b}(y,x)\Big)\omega_{1}(x)\omega_{2}(y)dxdy=0,

for all test functions
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
in
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with unitary means. Thus, we arrive at the conclusion that

S_{b}(x,y)=S_{b}(y,x),

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, whereby (
2.11
) follows.

We are now left with the proof of (
B.22
). To that end, we notice, in view of the second part of Lemma
B.4
, that the couple
(\psi_{b}[\omega_{1}],\psi_{b}[\omega_{2}])
of solutions satisfies (
B.22
) if and only if

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\Big(\psi_{b}[\omega_{1}]-\psi_{b}[\omega_{2}]\Big)(x)=0.

Thanks to the definitions of
\psi_{b}[\omega_{1}]
and
\psi_{b}[\omega_{2}]
as well as (
B.23
), it is readily seen that the preceding limit is a direct consequence of the following two properties:

(B.24)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{1}(x)\overset{\rm def}{=}\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{S}_{b}(x,y)h(y)dy=0

and

(B.25)

\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{2}(x)\overset{\rm def}{=}\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(x)b(y)}+S_{b,\text{rad}}(x,y)\right)h(y)dy=0,

for any test function
h\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(y)dy=0.

The proof of (
B.24
) directly follows by applying the dominated convergence theorem of Lebesgue, for
\bar{S}_{b}(x,y)
vanishes as

by construction. As for the justification of (

B.21
) altogether with the assumption that

is mean-free to obtain that

\mathcal{A}_{2}(x)=-\frac{\sqrt{b(R_{\infty})}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\log\left(\frac{|x-y|}{|x|}\right)\sqrt{b(y)}h(y)dy,

for all

. Therefore, noticing that the integral in the right-hand side above can be restricted to a compact set, as

B.25

B.22
) and, eventually, a justification of (
2.11

We now show the stability by rotations and involution of
S_{b}(x,y)
, which, as previously pointed out, will imply (
2.12

Hb4

\operatorname{div}_{x}\left(\frac{1}{b}\nabla_{x}\cdot\right)=\frac{1}{b}\left(\partial_{rr}+\frac{\partial_{r}}{r}+\frac{\partial_{\eta\eta}}{r^{2}}\right)+\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{1}{b}\right)\partial_{r},

for any

, one sees that the preceding operator is invariant by the transformation
x\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\cdot x
, for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. In addition to that, it is readily seen that the function

(x,y)\mapsto\log|x-y|\sqrt{b(y)}\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(x)}}\right)

is invariant by the transformation

(x,y)\rightarrow(\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\cdot x,\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\cdot y),

for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. Thus,
by uniqueness of the solution
\bar{S}_{b}
of (
2.8
), we deduce that

\bar{S}_{b}(\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\cdot x,\mathcal{R}_{\theta}\cdot y)=\bar{S}_{b}(x,y),

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi].
Likewise, the stability of the solution by involution can be proven along the same lines and by employing the same arguments as before. The details of that are left the reader. This leaves us with a justification of the (
2.12

is radially symmetric with respect to the

f_{\text{rad}}(|x|,y)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\Big||x|e^{{\rm i}\theta}-|y|e^{{\rm i}\eta}\Big|\sqrt{b(|y|)}\Delta_{x}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b(|x|)}}\right)d\theta,

where we identity
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with

which implies that

is radial with respect to the

is radial in
x
and

∎

Appendix C
A key formula

For the sake of completeness, we conclude with a justification of a crucial classical identity that has been used several times in this paper.

Lemma C.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
x,y\in(0,\infty)
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, it holds that

(C.1)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|ye^{{\rm i}\theta}-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=-\cos(n\theta)\frac{\mathtt{m}^{n}(x,y)}{2n},

where

\mathtt{m}(x,y)\overset{\rm def}{=}\min\left\{\frac{x}{y},\frac{y}{x}\right\}.

Proof.

First, we recall the identity (see
[
29
, identity (4.16)]
, for instance)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|1-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=-\frac{x^{n}}{2n},

which holds for any
x\in(0,1]
.
Therefore, combining the preceding identity with the parity and periodicity of the trigonometric function
\eta\mapsto\cos(n\eta)
, it follows, for any
x\in[1,\infty)
, that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|1-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=-\frac{x^{-n}}{2n}\cdot

Hence we deduce, for any
x\in[1,\infty)
, that

(C.2)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|1-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=-\frac{\min^{n}\{x,x^{-1}\}}{2n}\cdot

Thus, observing that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|ye^{{\rm i}\theta}-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=\log|y|\underbrace{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(n\eta)d\eta}_{=0}+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|1-\frac{x}{y}e^{{\rm i}(\eta-\theta)}\right|\cos(n\eta)d\eta,

and employing the change of variables
\eta\to\eta+\theta
along with (
C.2
) infers, for all
x\in(0,\infty)
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, that

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log|ye^{{\rm i}\theta}-xe^{{\rm i}\eta}|\cos(n\eta)d\eta=-\cos(n\theta)\frac{\mathtt{m}^{n}(\frac{x}{y},1)}{2n}+\frac{\sin(n\theta)}{2\pi}\underbrace{\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|1-\tfrac{x}{y}e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\sin(n\eta)d\eta}_{=0},

where we have used the fact that the function

\eta\mapsto\log\left|1-\tfrac{x}{y}e^{{\rm i}\eta}\right|\sin(n\eta)

is odd and
2\pi
-periodic. In conclusion, (
C.1
) follows and the proof of the lemma is now completed.
∎
