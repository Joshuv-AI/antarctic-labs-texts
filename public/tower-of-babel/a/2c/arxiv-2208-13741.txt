Recursive Session Logical Relations
Farzaneh Derakhshan, Stephanie Balzer

Abstract
. Program equivalence is the fulcrum for reasoning about and proving properties of programs. For noninterference, for example, program equivalence up to the secrecy level of an observer is shown. A powerful enabler for such proofs are logical relations . Logical relations only recently were adopted for session types—but exclusively for terminating languages. This paper scales logical relations to recursive session types. It develops a logical relation for progress-sensitive noninterference for linear session types, tackling the challenges non-termination and concurrency pose. The contributions include secrecy-polymorphic processes and the logical relation with metatheory. A distinguishing feature is the choice of “step index” of the logical relation, allowing for a natural proof of transitivity and soundness.



1.
Introduction

Logical relations
are a powerful and well-established proof method to show logical
equivalence
of programs.
Asserting equivalence of two programs is at the heart of many program verification problems.
One such verification problem is
noninterference
, guaranteeing that an adversary cannot infer any secrets from made observations.
Noninterference amounts to a program equivalence up to the secrecy level of the adversary.

While logical relations have been applied to a wide application domain—from functional languages to imperative languages with higher-order store—they have only recently been explored in a session-typed setting.
In this setting computation is carried out by
concurrently
interacting
processes
that exchange messages along channels.
Computation is inherently
effectful
because message exchange enact state transitions in the involved processes.
Message exchange can also lead to a change in channel topology when channels are sent along channels.
Given their ability to prescribe change enacted by message exchange, session types
(
Honda 1993
;
Honda
et al. 1998
;
Caires and
Pfenning 2010
;
Wadler 2012
)
are prominent in message-passing concurrency.
Session types moreover enjoy a strong theoretical foundation by a Curry-Howard correspondence between
linear logic
and the session-typed
\pi
-calculus
(
Caires and
Pfenning 2010
;
Wadler 2012
;
Kokke
et al. 2019
)
.

Surprisingly, logical relations for session types have primarily focused on unary logical relations for proving termination
(
Pérez et al. 2012
;
Pérez et al. 2014
;
DeYoung
et al. 2020
)
, except for a binary relation for
parametricity
(
Caires et al. 2013
)
and noninterference
(
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
)
, and have been
exclusively
formulated for the
terminating
linear session type fragment.

This paper develops a logical relation for program equivalence for
recursive
session types.
Support of possibly non-terminating programs is necessary for many realistic applications, especially for emerging new platforms and computing models, such as cloud computing.
In these domains, noninterference is an important correctness concern.
To demonstrate practicality of our logical relation, we instantiate it for
progress-sensitive noninterference

(
Heidin and
Sabelfeld 2011
)
.
A progress-sensitive formulation treats divergences as an observable outcome and thus provides a stronger guarantee in a concurrent setting where the
termination channel

(
Sabelfeld and
Myers 2003
)
can be scaled to many parallel computations, each leaking “just” one bit
(
Stefan et al. 2012
;
Alpernas et al. 2018
)
.

To assert progress-sensitive noninterference by typing checking, we develop a flow-sensitive information flow control (IFC) type system for intuitionistic linear session types, yielding the language
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
.
A distinguishing feature of
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
is its support of
secrecy polymorphism
, allowing processes to be instantiated at varying secrecy levels.

Our meta theoretic results include, besides fundamental theorem and noninterference, the proof that our logical relation is an equivalence relation as well as soundness.
The former requires proof of transitivity, a notoriously challenging property to prove, particularly in the presence of recursion.
Recursive types mandate the use of a measure like step indexing or later modalities
(
Appel and
McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2006
;
Dreyer
et al. 2009
)
to keep the logical relation well-founded.
A naive adoption of step indexing is too crude for program equivalence in a message-passing setting.
Instead, we devise an index that uses the number of messages observed as an upper bound.
This choice paves the way for proving transitivity.
To ensure soundness of the logical relation, we prove that any two logically equivalent programs are indistinguishable by forming a bisimulation bounded by the number of observations between them.

Additional challenges that we had to address when developing the logical relation where posed by the fact that
(i)
concurrency rules out lock-step execution of programs, complicating synchronization of the two programs to ensure relatedness, and that
(ii)
high-secrecy code can include non-termination, complicating a progress-sensitive statement, which demands that both runs will either be observably productive or not.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

•

flow-sensitive IFC type system for an intuitionistic linear session type language with
recursive types
and
secrecy-polymorphic
process definitions, yielding the language
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
with proof of type safety;

•

recursive session logical relation
for noninterference bounded by the number of observable messages, amounting to the first logical relation for session types with recursive types;

•

statement and proof of logical equivalence, proving
transitivity
, symmetry, and
progress-sensitive noninterference
for well-typed
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
programs;

•

statement and proof of adequacy theorem, asserting a
weak stratified bisimulation
for logically equivalent programs closed with the same context.

2.1.
Motivating example

We illustrate intuitionistic session-typed programming and IFC on a banking example.
Let’s assume that our bank has two customers, Alice and Bob, with accounts each.
In a secure system, we expect that Alice’s account can only be queried by Alice or the bank, but neither by Bob or any walk-in customer.
The same must hold for Bob’s account.
We can express this policy by defining corresponding secrecy levels and a lattice on them:

\mathbf{guest}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{alice}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{bank}\hskip 18.49988pt\mathbf{guest}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{bob}\sqsubseteq\mathbf{bank}

The above session types dictate the following protocol:
When a customer opens an account with the bank, the bank sends them a PIN and authorization process (
\mathsf{customer}
).
A customer can now repeatedly query their account’s balance (
\mathit{high}
,
\mathit{med}
,
\mathit{low}
), preceding authentication.
To authenticate themselves, a customer sends their PIN to the authorization process.
The authorization process then goes ahead and sends the PIN to the verifier.
If the authentication by the verifier is successful, the verifier sends the message
\mathit{succ}
to the authorization process, otherwise the message
\mathit{fail}
.
In either case, the verifier returns the PIN to the authorization process.
The latter relays the outcome of the authentication to the customer, yielding the bank account in case of
\mathit{succ}
and the PIN in case of
\mathit{fail}
.
Once done, a customer returns the account to the authorization process and receives their PIN, allowing them to start over.

2.2.
Possible worlds for IFC

IFC type systems for binary session types have only recently been contributed by
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
.

Derakhshan
et al. 2021
extend the
terminating
subset of
\mathsf{SILL}
with a flow-sensitive IFC type system, yielding the language
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
.
Our work is based on
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
, so we briefly summarize its main features.

\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
exploits the rooted tree structure resulting from intuitionism.
Observing that a process configuration naturally aligns with the security lattice (see
Figure

1
),
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
enforces this alignment by corresponding invariants preserved by typing.
To this end, the typing judgment is enriched with possible worlds from hybrid logic to denote a process’ running secrecy
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}@c}
, maximal secrecy
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d]}
, and the security lattice
\Psi_{0}
of the application:

\Psi_{0};\Delta\vdash P{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}@c}::(x{:}A{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d]})

The idea is that a process’
maximal secrecy
indicates the maximal level of secret information the process may ever obtain whereas its
running secrecy
denotes the highest level of secret information the process has obtained so far.
We read the typing judgment as:
“Process
P
, with maximal secrecy
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d]}
and running secrecy
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}@c}
, provides a session of type
A
along channel variable
x
, given the typing of sessions offered along channel variables in
\Delta
and given the secrecy levels in the security lattice
\langle\Psi_{0},\sqsubseteq,\sqcup\rangle
”
.

\Delta
is a
linear

context that consists of a finite set of assumptions of the form
y_{i}{:}B_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d_{i}^{\prime}]}
,
indicating for each channel variable
y_{i}
its maximal secrecy
{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d_{i}^{\prime}]}
and the
offered session type
B_{i}
.
The typing judgment uses channel variables, admitting
\alpha
-variance as usual.
Channel variables contrast with channels that are created at run-time upon process spawning.
When the distinction is clear from the context we refer to either as “channel” for brevity.

To enforce that a process configuration is in alignment with the security lattice, the following invariants are maintained by typing, referred to as the
tree invariant
:

(i)

\forall y{:}B{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}[d^{\prime}]}\in\Delta\,(\Psi_{0}\Vdash{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}d^{\prime}}\sqsubseteq{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}d})
and

(ii)

\Psi_{0}\Vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0.6,0}c}\sqsubseteq{\color[rgb]{0.72,0,0}d}

ensuring that the maximal secrecy of a child node is capped by the maximal secrecy of its parent and that the running secrecy of a process is less than or equal to its maximal secrecy, resp.

The tree invariant is crucial for preventing both direct and indirect flows but not sufficient to exclude them completely.
To ensure secure information flow, the type system needs to additionally ensure (1) that no channel of high secrecy is sent along one with lower secrecy and (2) that a process does not send any information along a low-secrecy channel after obtaining some high secrecy information.
Condition (1) prevents direct flows and is enforced by requiring that the maximal secrecy level of a carrier channel and the channels sent over it match.
Condition (2) is met by ensuring that a process’ running secrecy is a sound approximation of the level of secret information the process has obtained so far.
To this end, the type system increases the running secrecy upon each receive to
at least
the maximal secrecy of the sending process and correspondingly guards sends by making sure that the running secrecy of the sending process is
at most
the maximal secrecy of the receiving process.

Our work extends
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
with secrecy-polymorphic processes and recursive types, yielding the language
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
.

\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
carries over
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
’s tree invariant and conditions (1) and (2).
An impatient reader may want to glance at
Figure

2
, ignoring the rules for process definitions, to see how the tree invariant and conditions (1) and (2) are maintained.

3.1.
Polymorphic IFC type system

The support of recursive session types necessitates process definitions.
Process definitions in
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
are
polymorphic
in their secrecy levels, allowing a process to be instantiated at varying secrecy levels.
To ensure that instantiations respect the tree invariant and constraints imposed on the typing (see
Section

2.2

The body of a process is then verified assuming that these constraints are met.
It is the obligation of the caller—including the process itself in case of a recursive call—to assert that the constraints are met for the provided instantiations.

4.
Noninterference recursive logical relation

This section develops the logical relation used to prove progress-sensitive noninterference for
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}^{+}}
.
Transitivity and adequacy of the relation are shown in
Section

5
.

4.1.
Challenges

Noninterference
essentially amounts to a
program equivalence up to
the secrecy level
\xi
of the observer, requiring that two runs of a program can only differ in outcomes of secrecy level higher than or incomparable to
\xi
.
In a functional setting, the fundamental theorem of the
logical relation
for equivalence is stated for two open expressions, showing that they evaluate to related values, if given related substitutions.
In a session type setting, on the other hand, the fundamental theorem is stated by
plugging
an open configuration of processes (“open programs”) with closing configurations, showing that the programs send related messages, if receiving related messages from the configurations as well
(
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
)
.

The
session logical relation
for noninterference developed by
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
, while serving as a baseline for our development, does not scale to our setting.
It is phrased for the terminating language
\mathsf{SILL}_{\mathsf{sec}}
and its statement intimately relies on this assertion.
To support
recursive types
, and thus
non-terminating
programs, we had to address the following challenges:

•

Well-foundedness:
Recursive types mandate use of a measure like step-indexing or later modalities
(
Appel and
McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2006
;
Dreyer
et al. 2009
)
to keep the logical relation well-founded.
A naive adoption, typically tied to the number of unfoldings or computation steps, however, is too crude for a progress-sensitive statement of noninterference for message passing and challenge transitivity.

•

Nondeterminism:
While linear session-typed programs enjoy confluence, processes run concurrently.
As a result, two runs of a program cannot be aligned in lockstep, and the logical relation has to accommodate messages that are not being sent simultaneously while ensuring their relatedness.

•

Non-termination:
Noninterference allows two program runs to execute different code, as long as the same observable outcomes are produced.
In the presence of recursive types, any such high-secrecy code includes divergence for either of the two runs.
This discrepancy complicates a progress-sensitive statement, which demands that both runs will either diverge or not.

4.2.2.

Because of recursive types, the logical relation can no longer be defined inductively by the multiset of the size of the types in its interface

4.2.4.
Non-termination

Non-termination complicates the proof of noninterference, if a
progress-sensitive
statement is wished for.
The crux is to show that either both runs are observably productive or not.
This requirement is expressed by the term interpretation of the logical relation (see
Figure

7

\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}}_{2}
to produce the same set of outgoing channels as
\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
.
The challenge is aggravated by the fact that two runs of a program can differ after branching on a high-secrecy channel, including divergence for just one of them.
Given this discrepancy between executed high-secrecy code, it seems almost impossible to show that the same observable outcomes are produced nonetheless.

Let’s remind ourselves that noninterference amounts to a program equivalence, equating the program to itself up to the secrecy level of the observer.
This means that the two program runs will initially be the same and only diverge upon branching on high-secrecy messages.
If we can identify, in the proof of the fundamental theorem, which parts of the two program runs align and which ones differ, we should be able to use this information to our advantage.
In addition, we can exploit the guarantee made by the type system that “low-secrecy code” cannot sequentially depend on “high-secrecy code” because flows from high to low are ruled out by typing.

To make this reasoning more tangible, we introduce the notion of a
relevant node
.
A relevant node is a process or message in
\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}}
that can directly or indirectly influence what messages are made ready for transmission across the interface
{\color[rgb]{0.0898,0.4258,0.0859}\Delta}\Vdash{\color[rgb]{0.0898,0.4258,0.0859}K}
.

\forall\,x\in(\Theta_{1}\cap\Theta_{2}).\,{\bf\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0.5}\forall m.}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1}}};{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{2}}})\in\mathcal{V}^{\xi}_{\Psi_{0}}\llbracket\Delta\Vdash K\rrbracket_{x;\cdot}^{m+1}\end{array}

Proof.

The proof relies on two results: (1) For each
\Upsilon_{1}
, if
\mathcal{D}_{2}\mapsto^{*_{\Upsilon_{1}}}_{\Delta\Vdash K}\mathcal{D}
for some
\mathcal{D}
, we can build a unique
minimal
configuration
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
with
\mathcal{D}_{2}\mapsto^{*_{\Upsilon_{1}}}_{\Delta\Vdash K}\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
. It is minimal in the sense that for every
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}
, if
\mathcal{D}_{2}\mapsto^{*_{\Upsilon_{1}}}_{\Delta\Vdash K}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}_{2}\mapsto^{*_{\Upsilon_{1}}}_{\Delta\Vdash K}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}
. (2) A standard backward closure property for the value interpretation of the logical relation. An interested reader may consult Lem. C.8, C.10, C.14 in the appendix.
∎

Our choice of step index is an enabler for the proof of
Lemma

5.7
. Choosing the number of observations as the index ensures that the set
\Upsilon_{1}
built in
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{1}}}\;\mapsto^{{*}_{\Theta;\Upsilon_{1}}}_{\Delta\Vdash K}\;{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{1}}}
is independent from the index
m
. This allows us to build the unique minimal
\mathcal{D}^{\prime\prime}_{2}
for each
\Upsilon_{1}
without the need to instantiate
\forall\,m.
in the assumption. If we were to use the number of computation or unfolding steps as a step index, the set
\Upsilon_{1}
would depend on the number of steps of the first program, and proving
Lemma

5.7
would have been more challenging, if not impossible.

5.2.
Adequacy

We show that two logically equivalent programs are indistinguishable by any well-typed context.

The relation
\sim^{m}
is a weak stratified bisimulation based on a labeled transition system (LTS).
It is bounded by the number of observations that can be made and thus “stratified” to make the definition inductive.
For
{({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{1}}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{2}}})\in\mathsf{Tree}_{\Psi_{0}}(\Delta\Vdash K)
, we read
{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{1}}}}\sim^{m+1}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{2}}}
as: if
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{1}}}
, after none or many internal steps, produces an outgoing message
q
or an incoming message
\overline{q}
along an observable channel
x\in\Delta
or
x\in K
, then
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{2}}}
will produce the same outgoing message
q
or incoming message
\overline{q}
along
x
with none or many internal steps. Moreover, the post-step configurations are related by
\sim^{m}
. The same holds when
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}_{2}}}
produces a message.

Theorem 5.8 (Adequacy).

If
(\Delta\Vdash\mathcal{D}_{1}::K)\equiv^{\Psi_{0}}_{\xi}(\Delta\Vdash\mathcal{D}_{2}::K)
and
{({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}}}},{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}}})\in\mathsf{Tree}_{\Psi_{0}}(\Delta\Vdash K)
,
then

\forall m.{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{1}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}}}}\sim^{m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}{\mathcal{C}}},{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}{\mathcal{D}_{2}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\mathcal{F}}}.

Proof.

The techniques we used in the proof are similar to those used in the proof of transitivity and depend on the rely-guarantee property that we explained in
Section

5.1.2
. See Thm. C.24 in the appendix.
∎

6.
Related work

The seminal works on IFC type systems for sequential imperative languages
(
Volpano
et al. 1996
)
and multi-threaded imperative languages
(
Smith and Volpano 1998
)
have spurred a multitude of work on IFC type systems for imperative programming (c.f.,
(
Sabelfeld and
Myers 2003
)
). We focus here on the more closely related systems that we categorize as follows:

IFC type systems for binary session types:
The work most closely related to ours is the one by
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
, which develops an IFC type system for intuitionistic linear logic session types.
The authors prove noninterference for their language, using a logical relation, the first logical relation for noninterference for session types.
In contrast to our work, their work does not support recursive types and exploits that programs are terminating.
Moreover, we phrase a stronger adequacy statement and prove our logical relation to be an equivalence relation.

IFC type systems for multiparty session types:

IFC type systems have also been explored for multiparty session types
(
Capecchi et al. 2010
;
Capecchi et al. 2014
;
Castellani et al. 2016
;
Capecchi et al. 2016
)
.
These works explore declassification
(
Capecchi et al. 2010
;
Capecchi et al. 2014
)
and flexible run-time monitoring techniques
(
Castellani et al. 2016
;
Capecchi et al. 2016
)
.
Our work not only differs in use of session type paradigm (i.e., binary vs. multiparty) but also in use of a logical relation for showing noninterference.

IFC type systems for process calculi:

Our work is more distantly related with IFC type systems developed for process calculi
(
Honda
et al. 2000
;
Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Crafa
et al. 2002
;
Crafa and Rossi 2005
;
Crafa and Rossi 2006
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
;
Crafa and Rossi 2007
;
Hennessy and
Riely 2002
;
Hennessy 2005
;
Kobayashi 2005
;
Zdancewic and
Myers 2003
;
Pottier 2002
)
by virtue of its underlying concurrent, message-passing computation model.
These works prevent information leakage by associating a security label with channels/types/actions
(
Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
)
, read/write policies with channels
(
Hennessy and
Riely 2002
;
Hennessy 2005
)
, or capabilities with expressions
(
Crafa
et al. 2002
)
.

Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
moreover also use a substructural type system and prove a sound embedding of Dependency Core Calculus (DCC)
(
Abadi
et al. 1999
)
into their calculus.
Our work sets itself apart from these works in its use of session types and meta theoretic developments based on a novel recursive logical relation.

Logical relations for session types and noninterference in general:

The application of logical relations to session types has focused predominantly on unary logical relations for proving termination
(
Pérez et al. 2012
;
Pérez et al. 2014
;
DeYoung
et al. 2020
)
with the exception of a binary logical relation for parametricity
(
Caires et al. 2013
)
and noninterference
(
Derakhshan
et al. 2021
)
discussed above.
Our noninterference definition is moreover
inspired by
Bowman and Ahmed 2015
, which also define noninterference in terms of a logical
relation, albeit in a sequential purely functional setting.

Gregersen et al. 2021
recently contributed a logical relation for a higher-order functional language with higher-order store, recursive types, existential types, impredicative type polymorphism, and label polymorphism.
They use a semantic approach to proving noninterference, allowing integration of syntactically well-typed and ill-typed components, if the latter are shown to be semantically sound.
Besides the difference in language, the authors consider termination-insensitive noninterference only.

Relationship to logical relations for stateful languages:

Logical relations have been scaled to accommodate state using Kripke logical relations
(
Pitts and Stark 1998
)
.
Kripke logical relations are indexed by a
possible world W
, providing a semantic model of the heap.
Invariants can then be imposed that must be preserved by any future worlds W’.
Kripke logical relations have been successfully combined with step indexing
(
Appel and
McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2006
)
to address circularity arising in higher-order stores
(
Ahmed
et al. 2009
;
Dreyer
et al. 2010
;
Dreyer
et al. 2012
)
and to express state transition systems.
Our
session logical relation
does
not
use a Kripke logical relation because it relies on session typing of the configurations that it relates.
Session typing in turn governs state transitions and avoids circular configuration topologies by virtue of linearity.
