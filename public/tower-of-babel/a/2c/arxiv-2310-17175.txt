Interaction of liquid crystals with a rigid body
Tim Binz, Felix Brandt, Matthias Hieber, Arnab Roy

Abstract
. This article investigates the interaction of nematic liquid crystals modeled by a simplified Ericksen-Leslie model with a rigid body. It is shown that this problem is locally strongly well-posed, and that it also admits a unique, global strong solution for initial data close to constant equilibria. The proof of the global strong solution relies on a new splitting method for the director in a mean value zero and average part.

1.
Introduction

Ericksen
[
11
]
and Leslie
[
30
]
pioneered the development of a continuum theory for the flow of nematic liquid crystals. Their theory models the flow of nematic crystals
from a fluid dynamical point of view and describes the evolution of the underlying system by a certain fluid equation coupled to the configuration
d
of rod-like crystals. The original
derivation is based on the
conservation laws for mass and linear momentum as well as on the constitutive relations given by Ericksen
[
11
]
and Leslie
[
30
]
. The system subject to general Leslie and
general Ericksen stress is quite involved, and many analytical questions related to the resulting model remain open until today.

A simplified system was introduced and analyzed by Lin
[
32
]
as well as by Lin and Liu
[
33
]
.
They studied first the situation where the nonlinearity in the equation for the director
d
is replaced by a
Ginzburg-Landau term and proved the existence of global weak solutions under suitable assumptions. Considering the original situation where the nonlinear terms describing the evolution of the
director
d
are kept in their original form and are not replaced by Ginzburg-Landau terms, a rather complete understanding of the
well-posedness and the dynamics of the simplified system in the strong sense was obtained in
[
23
]
. In particular, it was shown that the condition
|d|=1
is preserved by the system for all

t>0
.

Only recently, a thorough analysis of the Ericksen-Leslie system subject to the general Ericksen and Leslie stresses was obtained by Hieber and Prüss
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
. For related results
concerning various assumptions on the Leslie as well as on the Ericksen coefficients, we refer to Lin
[
31
]
, Lin and Liu
[
34
]
, and Wu, Xu and Liu
[
46
]
as well as to the
survey articles
[
27
]
and
[
45
]
.

The present article investigates for the first time the interaction problem of a rigid body with nematic liquid crystals described by a simplified Ericksen-Leslie model as considered in
[
23
]
. It
establishes the existence and uniqueness of a strong solution, both, locally-in-time and globally-in-time for initial data close to equilibria. One essential feature of our approach is that we do not
use the Ginzburg-Landau energy functional, but we show that the director condition is preserved in the interaction problem.
Moreover, the equilibria are deduced from an investigation of the energy functional.

The main difficulties in our stability analysis of the equilibria arise from the lack of autonomy of the transformed system on a fixed domain as well as the lack of invertibility of the linearization, so classical tools
such as principles of linearized stability fall short in this case. Instead, inspired by the work of Haak, Maity, Takahashi and Tucsnak
[
22
]
in the context of compressible fluids, we split
the equations satisfied by the director
into one part with mean value zero and the associated average part. This enables us to handle the linearized problem on the whole time interval
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and paves the way to the
global well-posedness result with certain decay properties.

The interaction of a rigid body immersed in an incompressible Newtonian fluid without a liquid crystal component is, of course, a very classical topic.
We refer here only to the works of Desjardins and Esteban

in the weak setting and to Galdi

, Galdi and Silvestre
[

]
, Takahashi

]
, Cumsille and Takahashi
[
7
]
,
Geissert, Götze and Hieber
[

]
, Kaltenbacher, Kukavica, Lasiecka, Triggiani, Tuffaha and Webster
[

, Maity and Tucsnak
[

For related results in the context of elastic structures, we refer for example to the articles of Coutand and Shkoller

; for results in the non-viscous case let us refer for example to the work of Glass, Lacave and Sueur

Q

Let us emphasize that the interaction problem under consideration is different from the ones associated to incompressible Newtonian fluids due to the elasticity component in the stress tensor and its
interaction with the rigid body.

The physical motivation to study liquid crystal-rigid body interaction results from so-called liquid crystal colloids formed by dispersion of colloidal particles in the liquid crystal host medium.
The rigid body in our analysis can be viewed as a colloidal particle. For further details on the physical background, we refer e.g. to
[
42
,
38
]
or
[
37
]
.

The first step for solving the interaction problem is the transformation of the moving domain problem to a fixed domain problem by means of a local change of coordinates due to Inoue and Wakimoto
[
28
]
.
In return, additional time-dependent terms arise, and the transformed system is of a more complicated shape.
The proof of the local strong well-posedness relies on the maximal regularity of the linearized interaction problem.

With regard to the global result, we require maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,\infty))
-regularity of the linearization around constant equilibria.
In fact, we obtain a maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,\infty))
-regularity type estimate with exponential decay of the complete system of equations by invoking the fluid-structure semigroup from
[
36
]
and performing the splitting of the equations satisfied by the director into a part with mean value zero in the spatial domain and the remaining average part.

The proofs are completed by Lipschitz estimates of the nonlinear terms for which we rely on appropriate estimates of the terms associated to the coordinate transform in the local and global setting.
In particular, we verify that no collision of the rigid body and the outer boundary of the viscous incompressible fluid with active liquid crystals can occur provided the rigid body starts with an initial distance from the boundary, and the initial values are taken sufficiently close to the equilibria.

This paper is organized as follows.
In
Section 2
, the model for the interaction problem is introduced, and the main results on the local strong well-posedness as well as the global strong well-posedness close to constant equilibria are stated.
The aim of
Section 3
is twofold:
On the one hand, it is shown that the director condition is preserved in the interaction problem.
On the other hand, this section discusses the energy of the interaction problem and deduces the equilibrium solutions from there.

Section 4
is dedicated to the transformation of the interaction problem to a fixed domain, and the main results are reformulated in this reference configuration.
In
Section 5
, we establish the maximal regularity of the linearized problem.
Moreover, we show the maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}([0,\infty))
-regularity of the linearization around constant equilibria up to a shift.

Section 6
presents the proof of the local strong well-posedness by a fixed point argument.
For this, estimates of the nonlinear terms based on a good control of the terms resulting from the coordinate transform are proved.
In
Section 7
, we first introduce the maximal regularity type result in order to get exponential decay and then conclude the proof of the global strong well-posedness close to constant equilibria from the contraction mapping principle by invoking adjusted estimates of the transform.

2.
Description of the model and main results

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote a bounded domain with boundary of class
\mathrm{C}^{3}
, and let
0<T\leq\infty
represent a positive time.
We consider a rigid body moving inside
\mathcal{O}
which is assumed to be closed, bounded and simply connected.
Furthermore, the domain occupied by the rigid body is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(t)
for
t\in(0,T)
, and the remaining part of the domain
\mathcal{F}(t)\coloneqq\mathcal{O}\setminus\mathcal{S}(t)
is assumed to be filled by a viscous incompressible fluid with active liquid crystals.
The initial domain of the rigid body is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{0}
.
Besides, we suppose that its boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}_{0}
is of class
\mathrm{C}^{3}
as well.
Then
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\mathcal{O}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{0}
is the initial fluid domain.
The solid domain
\mathcal{S}(t)
at time
t
is given by

(2.1)

\mathcal{S}(t)=\left\{h(t)+Q(t)y:y\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\right\},

where
h(t)
represents the center of mass of the body, and
Q(t)\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
is associated to the rotation of the rigid body.
Moreover, since
Q^{\prime}(t)Q^{\top}(t)
is skew symmetric, for each
t>0
, we can represent this matrix by a unique vector
\Omega(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

Q^{\prime}(t)Q^{\top}(t)y=\Omega(t)\times y,\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0pty\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

By
\mathcal{F}_{T}
,
\mathcal{S}_{T}
and
\partial\mathcal{S}_{T}
, we denote the spacetime related to
\mathcal{F}(t)
,
\mathcal{S}(t)
and
\partial\mathcal{S}(t)
, respectively, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{T}\coloneqq\{(t,x):t\in(0,T),\,x\in\mathcal{F}(t)\},\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{S}_{T}\coloneqq\{(t,x):t\in(0,T),\,x\in\mathcal{S}(t)\},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\partial\mathcal{S}_{T}\coloneqq\{(t,x):t\in(0,T),\,x\in\partial\mathcal{S}(t)\}.

The velocity of the rigid body is described by

u^{\mathcal{S}}(t,x)=h^{\prime}(t)+\Omega(t)\times(x-h(t)),\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0pt(t,x)\in\mathcal{S}_{T},

where
h^{\prime}\colon(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the translational velocity of the center of mass, and
\Omega\colon(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the angular velocity of the body.

In the context of the Ericksen-Leslie model for nematic liquid crystals, the model variables are the fluid velocity
u^{\mathcal{F}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the fluid pressure
\pi^{\mathcal{F}}\colon\mathcal{F}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the director
\overline{d}\colon\mathcal{F}_{T}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{2}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:|x|=1\right\}
.
We set
\mathbb{D}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(\nabla u^{\mathcal{F}}+(\nabla u^{\mathcal{F}})^{\top})
. The stress tensor in the situation of the simplified isothermal Ericksen-Leslie model under consideration is given by
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{T}_{N}+\mathbb{T}_{E}
, where

\mathbb{T}_{N}=2\mu\mathbb{D},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{T}_{E}=-\lambda\nabla\overline{d}\left(\nabla\overline{d}\right)^{\top},\kern 5.0pt\text{so}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{T}=2\mu\mathbb{D}-\lambda\nabla d(\nabla d)^{\top},

and
\mu>0
and
\lambda>0
are constants.
Upon assuming that the constant fluid density
\rho^{\mathcal{F}}
equals one, and for another constant
\gamma>0
, we obtain the following
simplified isothermal Ericksen-Leslie model

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\partial_{t}u^{\mathcal{F}}}+\left(u^{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla\right)u^{\mathcal{F}}+\nabla\pi^{\mathcal{F}}&=\mu\Delta u^{\mathcal{F}}-\lambda\mdiv\left(\nabla\overline{d}\left(\nabla\overline{d}\right)^{\top}\right),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{F}_{T},\\
\mdiv u^{\mathcal{F}}&=0,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{F}_{T},\\
\gamma\left[\partial_{t}\overline{d}+\left(u^{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\nabla\right)\overline{d}\right]-\lambda\Delta\overline{d}&=\lambda|\nabla\overline{d}|^{2}\overline{d},&&\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{F}_{T}.\end{aligned}\right.

With regard to the rigid body, let
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}
denote its density.
For simplicity, we assume that
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}\equiv 1
in the sequel, yielding that
m_{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{\mathcal{S}(t)}\,\mathrm{d}x
is its total mass.
In addition, without loss of generality, we suppose that the center of mass of the rigid body starts in the origin, i.e.,
h(0)=0
. Let
J(t)
be the inertia matrix and the initial value of the inertial matrix is
J_{0}
. Here
J_{0}
and
J(t)
are given by

J_{0}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}\left(|x|^{2}\Id-x\otimes x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptJ(t)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}(t)}\left(|x-h(t)|^{2}\Id-(x-h(t))\otimes(x-h(t))\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

respectively.
Consequently,
J(t)
is symmetric positive definite and satisfies

(2.3)

J(t)=Q(t)J_{0}Q^{\top}(t)\kern 5.0pt\text{as well as}\kern 5.0ptJ(t)a\cdot b=\int\limits_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}(a\times Q(t)y)\cdot(b\times Q(t)y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0pt\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

For the underlying total stress tensor

(2.4)

\Sigma(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}},\overline{d})=\left(\mathbb{T}-\pi^{\mathcal{F}}\Id\right)=2\mu\mathbb{D}-\lambda\nabla\overline{d}\left(\nabla\overline{d}\right)^{\top}-\pi^{\mathcal{F}}\Id,

the motion of the rigid body can be described by Newton’s law, i.e.,

(2.5)

m_{\mathcal{S}}h^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{S}(t)}\Sigma(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}},\overline{d})\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt(J\Omega)^{\prime}(t)=-\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{S}(t)}(x-h(t))\times\Sigma(u^{\mathcal{F}},\pi^{\mathcal{F}},\overline{d})\nu(t)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

where
\nu(t)
denotes the unit outward normal to the boundary of
\mathcal{F}(t)
, i.e., it is directed towards
\mathcal{S}(t)
.

We assume that the fluid velocity satisfies homogeneous boundary conditions on the outer boundary and coincides with the velocity of the rigid body on their common interface, while the director is supposed to fulfill homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions, i.e.,

(2.6)

u^{\mathcal{F}}=0,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}\overline{d}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\partial\mathcal{O},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptu^{\mathcal{F}}=u^{\mathcal{S}},\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}\overline{d}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial\mathcal{S}_{T}.

The system is completed by the initial conditions

(2.7)

u^{\mathcal{F}}(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\kern 5.0pt\overline{d}(0,x)=d_{0}(x)\kern 5.0pt\text{in}\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{F}_{0},\kern 5.0pth(0)=0,\kern 5.0pth^{\prime}(0)=\ell_{0},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt\Omega(0)=\omega_{0}.

In fact, let
X\colon\mathcal{F}_{T}\to\mathcal{F}_{0}
be a map such that

\varphi\colon\mathcal{F}_{T}\to(0,T)\times\mathcal{F}_{0},\kern 5.0pt(t,x)\mapsto(t,X(t,x))

\mathrm{C}^{2}

and

By

Next, we settle some notation with regard to the initial data.
For this purpose, let us recall the space of solenoidal vector fields on

Denoting the principle variable by

\displaystyle\mathrm{X}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\bigl\{

\displaystyle z_{0}\in\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})^{3}\cap\mathrm{L}_{\sigma}^{q}(\mathcal{F}_{0})\times\mathrm{B}_{qp}^{2-\nicefrac{{2}}{{p}}}(\mathcal{F}_{0})^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}:v_{0}=0,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}d_{0}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial\mathcal{O},\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle v_{0}=\ell_{0}+\omega_{0}\times y,\kern 5.0pt\partial_{\nu}d_{0}=0,\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt\partial\mathcal{S}_{0}\bigr\},

where

denotes the Besov space with parameters
p,q\in(1,\infty)
and

.
The fact that the trace and the normal derivative are defined is guaranteed by the following conditions.
Let

3.
The director condition, energy and equilibria

We verify that the director part
\overline{d}
of a strong solution to the interaction problem (
2.2
)–(
2.7
) always satisfies the condition
|\overline{d}(t,x)|=1
in
Subsection 3.1
.

Subsection 3.2
is dedicated to showing that the energy of the interaction system is non-increasing, and to determining the set of equilibrium solutions.

3.1.
Preservation of the director condition

Due to the physical interpretation of the director
\overline{d}
, it is natural to include the condition

(3.1)

|\overline{d}(t,x)|=1,\kern 5.0pt\text{for all}\kern 5.0pt(t,x)\in\mathcal{F}_{T}.

Assuming

4.
Change of variables

The transformation to a fixed domain presented in the sequel is classical, see for instance the work of Inoue and Wakimoto
[
28
]
.
However, we provide it here for convenience.
To this end, let us denote by

the skew-symmetric matrix satisfying

We then consider the initial value problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}X_{0}(t,y)&=m(t)(X_{0}(t,y)-h(t))+h^{\prime}(t),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\

The solution of (

for

, where

is valid provided

,
\Omega\in\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(0,T)^{3}

The inverse

of

Y_{0}(0,x)&=x,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pty\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\end{aligned}\right.

where
M(t)\coloneqq Q^{\top}(t)m(t)Q(t)
and
\ell(t)\coloneqq Q^{\top}(t)h^{\prime}(t)
.
Next, we adjust the diffeomorphisms
X_{0}
and
Y_{0}
of
\mathcal{F}(t)
as well as
\mathcal{S}(t)
such that the transform only acts in a suitable open neighborhood of the moving rigid body.
To this end, we define a new diffeomorphism

implicitly by demanding that it solves the initial value problem

(4.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}X(t,y)&=b(t,X(t,y)),&&\kern 5.0pt\text{on}\kern 5.0pt(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
X(0,y)&=y,&&\kern 5.0pt\text{for}\kern 5.0pty\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.\end{aligned}\right.

The right-hand side

will be constructed such that the transform fulfills the above requirements.
We assume that the rigid body starts from a position with strictly positive distance from the outer boundary, i.e.,
\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{S}_{0},\partial\mathcal{O})>r
for some

.
As the body velocity is continuous, we only consider the solution up to a time for which a small distance such as
\nicefrac{{r}}{{2}}
of the rigid body and the outer boundary is preserved.
Accordingly, we define a cut-off function

Equipped with this cut-off function, we define the right-hand side

of (
4.2
) by

where

\mathcal{F}_{0}
.
This operator is bounded and yields
\mathrm{div}B_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}g=g

\int_{\mathcal{F}_{0}}g\,\mathrm{d}y=0

We also refer to
[
3

14

18

for further details on the Bogovskiĭ operator.
In our situation, it readily follows that

Consequently,

We also observe that the solution has continuous mixed partial derivatives

In order to estimate the other addend, we rewrite
