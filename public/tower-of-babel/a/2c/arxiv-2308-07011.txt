Unique special solution for discrete Painlevé II
Walter Van Assche

Abstract
We show that the discrete Painlevé II equation with starting value a − 1 = − 1 a_{-1}=-1 has a unique solution for which − 1 < a n < 1 -1<a_{n}<1 for every n ≥ 0 n\geq 0 . This solution corresponds to the Verblunsky coefficients of a family of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. This result was already proved for certain values of the parameter in the equation and recently a full proof was given by Duits and Holcomb [ 3 ] . In the present paper we give a different proof that is based on an idea put forward by Tomas Lasic Latimer [ 6 ] which uses orthogonal polynomials. We also give an upper bound for this special solution.



1
Introduction

The unicity of a positive solution of discrete Painlevé equations has been investigated before.

•

The discrete Painlevé I equation

x_{n}(x_{n+1}+x_{n}+x_{n-1}+K)=\alpha n,\qquad\alpha>0

with
x_{0}=0
, has a unique positive solution (Lew and Quarles
[
7
]
, Nevai
[
8
]
). This was extended
to a more general recurrence relation by Alsulami et al.
[
1
]
.

•

There is a unique solution for the alternative discrete Painlevé I equation

\begin{cases}a_{n}+a_{n+1}=b_{n}^{2}-t,\\
a_{n}(b_{n}+b_{n-1})=n,\end{cases}

with
t>0
and
a_{0}=0
, for which
a_{n+1}>0
and
b_{n}>0
for all
n\geq 0
(Clarkson et al.
[
2
]
).

•

The
q
-discrete Painlevé equation

a_{n}(a_{n+1}+q^{1-n}a_{n}+q^{2}a_{n-1}+q^{-2n+3}a_{n+1}a_{n}a_{n-1})=(1-q^{n})q^{n-1}

with
0<q<1
and
a_{0}=0
, has a unique positive solution (Latimer
[
6
]
, where a somewhat more general
equation was investigated).

The discrete Painlevé II equation that we will consider in this paper is

x_{n+1}+x_{n-1}=\frac{\alpha nx_{n}}{1-x_{n}^{2}},

(1.1)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. This is a one parameter version of d-P
(A_{3}^{(1)},D_{5}^{(1)})
with symmetry

A_{3}^{(1)}
and surface type
D_{5}^{(1)}
in the geometric classification
[
5
]
.
We will show that there is a unique solution with
x_{0}=1
for which
-1<x_{n}<1
for

n\geq 1
. In a similar way there is also a unique solution with
x_{0}=-1
for which
-1<x_{n}<1
for all
n\geq 1
,
which follows by taking
-x_{n}
in the previous problem. This result was proved for
\alpha>1
and
\alpha<-1

in
[
15
, Thm. 3.10 and Cor. 3.12]
. The proof is based on a fixed point argument but this does not work for
|\alpha|\leq 1
. Recently Duits and Holcomb
[
3
, Thm. 2.1]
proved the unicity for all real
\alpha\neq 0
. Their proof used
a convexity argument showing that a related quantity has a unique minimum.
We will prove the unicity using a different approach, which was used by Latimer in
[
6
]
for
proving the uniqueness of a
q
-discrete Painlevé equation. His appoach uses orthogonal polynomials on the real line,
but we will use orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. We will use a different version of (
1.1
), with a shift

x_{n+1}=a_{n}
and
\alpha=-2/t
.
This corresponds better to the framework of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle.
Our main result is:

Theorem 1
.

The discrete Painlevé II equation

a_{n+1}+a_{n-1}=\frac{-2(n+1)a_{n}}{t(1-a_{n}^{2})}

with initial value
a_{-1}=-1
has a unique solution for which
-1<a_{n}<1
for all
n\geq 0
. This
solution is obtained by taking
a_{0}=I_{1}(t)/I_{0}(t)
, where
I_{n}(t)
is the modified Bessel function of order
n
.

This unique solution corresponds to a special function solution in terms of modified Bessel functions,
see e.g.,
[
12
]
. The sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains the Verblunsky coefficients of
a family of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle which was first investigated by Periwal and Shevitz
[
11
]
and
which Ismail calls the modified Bessel polynomials
[
4
, pp. 236–239]
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Section 3 and depends on two results for the orthogonal polynomials
on the unit circle with Verblunsky coefficients given by the required solution
(a_{n})_{n}
. These results (Lemmas
1
and
2
) are proved in Section 2 which also contains the necessary background about orthogonal polynomials
on the unit circle.
In Section 4 we give an upper bound for this unique solution, which shows that it tends to zero very fast.

2
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle

We will use some basic notions from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC). We recommend
the books of Szegő
[
14
, Chapter XI]
and Simon
[
13
]
for the general theory.
Suppose
\nu
is a positive measure on
[0,2\pi]
and consider the orthonormal polynomials
\{\varphi_{n}(z),n=0,1,2,\ldots\}

on the unit circle for this measure
\nu
, for which

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\varphi_{n}(z)\overline{\varphi_{m}(z)}\,d\nu(\theta)=\delta_{m,n},\qquad z=e^{i\theta},

with
\varphi_{n}(z)=\kappa_{n}z^{n}+\cdots
and
\kappa_{n}>0
. We will denote the monic orthogonal polynomials
by
\Phi_{n}(z)=\varphi_{n}(z)/\kappa_{n}
. These monic polynomials can be computed using the Szegő-Levinson
recurrence

z\Phi_{n}(z)=\Phi_{n+1}(z)+\overline{a_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z),

(2.1)

where
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\Phi}_{n}(1/z)
is the reversed polynomial, with
\overline{\Phi}_{n}
the polynomial
with complex conjugated coefficients. The parameters
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are called Verblunsky coefficients.
They are given by
a_{n}=-\overline{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}
and they satisfy
|a_{n}|<1
for
n\geq 0
, and
a_{-1}=-1
.
Verblunsky’s theorem (or the spectral theorem for OPUC
[
13
, §1.7 and §3.1]
) says that for any sequence of complex numbers

a_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
for which
|a_{n}|<1
for
n\geq 0
, there is a unique probability measure
\nu
on the unit circle such that the polynomials
\Phi_{n}
generated by the recurrence (
2.1
) are the monic orthogonal polynomials for this measure

\nu
:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi_{n}(z)\overline{\Phi_{m}(z)}\,d\nu(\theta)=\frac{\delta_{m,n}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}},\qquad z=e^{i\theta}.

An important relation between
\kappa_{n}
and
a_{n}
was found by Szegő (see
[
14
, Eq. (11.3.6)]
):

\kappa_{n}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\varphi_{k}(0)|^{2},

from which we find that
\kappa_{n+1}^{2}-\kappa_{n}^{2}=|\varphi_{n+1}(0)|^{2}=\kappa_{n+1}^{2}|a_{n}|^{2}
, so that

\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{\kappa_{n+1}^{2}}=1-|a_{n}|^{2}.

(2.2)

We will be using real coefficients
a_{n}
for which
-1<a_{n}<1
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq-1}
with
a_{-1}=-1
be a solution of

-\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n}^{2})(a_{n+1}+a_{n-1})=(n+1)a_{n},

(2.3)

for which
-1<a_{n}<1
for all
n\geq 0
, and let
\{\Phi_{n}(z),n=0,1,2,\ldots\}
be the monic orthogonal
polynomals on the unit circle for which

z\Phi_{n}(z)=\Phi_{n+1}(z)+a_{n}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z),

(2.4)

holds. Then

\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(z)=n\Phi_{n-1}(z)+B_{n}\Phi_{n-2}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

(2.5)

with

B_{n}=\frac{t}{2}\frac{\kappa_{n-2}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}=\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n-2}^{2})(1-a_{n-1}^{2}).

Proof.

The recurrence (
2.5
) is clearly true for
n=1
. For
n=2
we have
\Phi_{2}(z)=z\Phi_{1}(z)-a_{1}\Phi_{1}^{*}(z)

and
\Phi_{1}(z)=z-a_{0}
, so that

\Phi_{2}(z)=z^{2}-a_{0}(1-a_{1})z-a_{1}.

Then on one hand we find
\Phi_{2}^{\prime}(z)=2z-a_{0}(1-a_{1})
and on the other hand

2\Phi_{1}(z)+B_{2}\Phi_{0}(z)=2(z-a_{0})+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})(1-a_{1}^{2}).

So (
2.5
) holds for
n=2
if we can show that

a_{0}(1-a_{1})-2a_{0}+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})(1-a_{1}^{2})=0.

(2.6)

Use (
2.3
) with
n=0
and
a_{-1}=-1
to find that
2a_{0}=t(1-a_{0}^{2})(1-a_{1})
, then (
2.6
) easily follows.

We will prove (
2.5
) by induction. We can find
\Phi_{n+1}(z)
using the recurrence (
2.1
)

\Phi_{n+1}(z)=z\Phi_{n}(z)-a_{n}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z).

In order to expand
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)
in the basis
\{\Phi_{k}(z),0\leq k\leq n\}
we use the relation
[
14
, Eq. (11.3.5)]

\kappa_{n}\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\varphi_{k}(z)\overline{\varphi_{k}(0)},

which for the monic polynomials becomes

\kappa_{n}^{2}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\kappa_{k}^{2}\Phi_{k}(z)\overline{\Phi_{k}(0)}.

Recall that
a_{k}=-\overline{\Phi_{k+1}(0)}
, so that we find

\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}a_{k-1}\Phi_{k}(z).

(2.7)

This gives

\Phi_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)=z\Phi_{n}^{\prime}(z)+\Phi_{n}(z)+a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}a_{k-1}\Phi_{k}^{\prime}(z).

The induction hypothesis allows us to write
\Phi_{k}^{\prime}(z)
for
0\leq k\leq n
in terms of
\Phi_{k-1}
and
\Phi_{k-2}

using (
2.5
). This gives

\Phi_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)=z[n\Phi_{n-1}(z)+B_{n}\Phi_{n-2}(z)]+\Phi_{n}(z)+a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}a_{k-1}[k\Phi_{k-1}(z)+B_{k}\Phi_{k-2}(z)].

We can use (
2.1
) to replace
z\Phi_{n-1}(z)
and
z\Phi_{n-2}(z)
, to find

\Phi_{n+1}^{\prime}(z)=(n+1)\Phi_{n}(z)-na_{n-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n-1}^{2}}a_{k-1}\Phi_{k}(z)+B_{n}\Phi_{n-1}(z)\\
-B_{n}a_{n-2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n-2}^{2}}a_{k-1}\Phi_{k}(z)+a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}ka_{k-1}\Phi_{k-1}(z)+a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}a_{k-1}B_{k}\Phi_{k-2}(z).

(2.8)

We will now check the coefficients of
\Phi_{k}(z)
on the right hand side of (
2.8
) to show that (
2.5
)
indeed holds for
n+1
. The coefficient of
\Phi_{n}(z)
is
n+1
, which agrees with (
2.5
). The coefficient of
\Phi_{n-1}(z)
is

-na_{n-1}a_{n-2}+B_{n}+na_{n}a_{n-1}=na_{n-1}(a_{n}-a_{n-2})+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n-2}^{2})(1-a_{n-1}^{2}).

If we use (
2.3
) for
n-1
then this is

-\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n-1}^{2})(a_{n}+a_{n-2})(a_{n}-a_{n-2})+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n-2}^{2})(1-a_{n-1}^{2})\\
=\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{n-1}^{2})(1-a_{n}^{2})=B_{n+1},

which agrees with (
2.5
).
The coefficient of
\Phi_{k}(z)
in (
2.8
) for
k\leq n-2
is given by

\frac{\kappa_{k}^{2}}{\kappa_{n}^{2}}\Bigl[-na_{n-1}a_{k-1}\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{\kappa_{n-1}^{2}}-B_{n}a_{n-2}a_{k-1}\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{\kappa_{n-2}^{2}}+(k+1)a_{n}a_{k}\frac{\kappa_{k+1}^{2}}{\kappa_{k}^{2}}+a_{n}a_{k+1}B_{k+2}\frac{\kappa_{k+2}^{2}}{\kappa_{k}^{2}}\Bigr].

The expression between the square brackets simplifies to

-a_{k-1}\left(\frac{na_{n-1}}{1-a_{n-1}^{2}}+\frac{t}{2}a_{n-2}\right)+a_{n}\left(\frac{(k+1)a_{k}}{1-a_{k}^{2}}+\frac{t}{2}a_{k+1}\right).

Using (
2.3
) with
n-1
in the first term and with
n=k
in the second term then gives

-a_{k-1}\left(-\frac{t}{2}(a_{n}+a_{n-2})+\frac{t}{2}a_{n-2}\right)+a_{n}\left(-\frac{t}{2}(a_{k+1}+a_{k-1})+\frac{t}{2}a_{k+1}\right)=0,

which agrees with (
2.5
).
∎

Let us denote the moments of the orthogonality measure
\nu
for these orthogonal polynomials by

\mu_{n}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}z^{n}\,d\nu(\theta),\qquad z=e^{i\theta}.

We will normalize the orthogonality measure to be a probability measure, so that
\mu_{0}=1
.
These moments satisfy a three term recurrence relation:

Lemma 2
.

Let
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the moments of the measure
\nu
for the orthogonal polynomials
(\Phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.
Then

(n-1)\mu_{n-1}+\frac{t}{2}(\mu_{n}-\mu_{n-2})=0.

(2.9)

Proof.

Write
z^{n}
in the basis
\{\Phi_{k}(z),0\leq k\leq n\}

z^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k,n}\Phi_{k}(z),

with
c_{n,n}=1
, then

c_{k,n}=\kappa_{k}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{k}(z)}\,d\nu(\theta).

(2.10)

We then see that the orthogonality implies

\mu_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k,n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\Phi_{k}(z)\,d\nu(\theta)=c_{0,n},

where we used
\mu_{0}=1
. Taking the derivative gives

nz^{n-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k,n}\Phi_{k}^{\prime}(z),

and using (
2.5
) then leads to

nz^{n-1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k,n}[k\Phi_{k-1}(z)+B_{k}\Phi_{k-2}(z)].

Integrating then gives

n\mu_{n-1}=c_{1,n}+c_{2,n}B_{2}.

The relation (
2.10
) then given

c_{1,n}=\kappa_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\overline{z-a_{0}}z^{n}\,d\nu(\theta)=\kappa_{1}^{2}(\mu_{n-1}-a_{0}\mu_{n}),

and

c_{2,n}=\kappa_{2}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\overline{z^{2}-a_{0}(1-a_{1})z-a_{1}}z^{n}\,d\nu(\theta)=\kappa_{2}^{2}[\mu_{n-2}-a_{0}(1-a_{1})\mu_{n-1}-a_{1}\mu_{n}].

If we use
\kappa_{2}^{2}=\kappa_{1}^{2}/(1-a_{1}^{2})
and
\kappa_{1}^{2}=1/(1-a_{0}^{2})
, then we have

n\mu_{n-1}=\frac{1}{1-a_{0}^{2}}\left(\mu_{n-1}-a_{0}\mu_{n}+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})[\mu_{n-2}-a_{0}(1-a_{1})\mu_{n-1}-a_{1}\mu_{n}]\right).

The relation (
2.3
) with
n=0
and
a_{-1}=-1
gives

a_{0}=\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})(1-a_{1}),\quad\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})a_{1}=\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})-a_{0},

and if we use this to remove
a_{1}
from the equation, then

\displaystyle n\mu_{n-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-a_{0}^{2}}\left(\mu_{n-1}-a_{0}\mu_{n}+\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})\mu_{n-2}-a_{0}^{2}\mu_{n-1}-\frac{t}{2}(1-a_{0}^{2})\mu_{n}+a_{0}\mu_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{n-1}-\frac{t}{2}\mu_{n}+\frac{t}{2}\mu_{n-2},

which is the recurrence relation in (
2.9
).
∎

3
Proof of Theorem
1

By means of Lemmas
1
and
2
we have now established that every solution of the discrete
Painlevé II equation (
2.3
) with
a_{-1}=-1
and
-1<a_{n}<1
for every
n\geq 0
, corresponds
to the Verblunsky coefficients of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle, for which the moments satisfy the linear
recurrence relation

(n-1)\mu_{n-1}=\frac{t}{2}(\mu_{n-2}-\mu_{n}).

The general solution of this recurrence relation is a linear combination of two linearly independent solutions.
A well known result for modified Bessel functions is that they satisfy the recurrence

Z_{\nu-1}(z)-Z_{\nu+1}(z)=\frac{2\nu}{z}Z_{\nu}(z),

i.e., both
Z_{\nu}(z)=I_{\nu}(z)
and
Z_{\nu}(z)=e^{\nu\pi i}K_{\nu}(z)
are a solution of this recurrence,
see e.g.,
[
10
]
or
[
9
, Eq. 10.29.1]
. This means that

\mu_{n}=C_{1}I_{n}(t)+C_{2}(-1)^{n}K_{n}(t),

for constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. For
t
fixed one has as
n\to\infty

I_{n}(t)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{et}{2n}\right)^{n},\quad K_{n}(t)\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2n}}\left(\frac{et}{2n}\right)^{-n},

(see
[
9
, 10.41.1 and 10.41.2]
)
so that
I_{n}(t)
remains bounded as
n\to\infty
and
K_{n}
is unbounded. Since

|\mu_{n}|=\left|\int_{0}^{2\pi}z^{n}\,d\nu(\theta)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi}|z^{n}|\,d\nu(\theta)=\mu_{0},

it follows that we have to take
C_{2}=0
, so that

\mu_{n}=C_{1}I_{n}(t).

For
n=0
we have
\mu_{0}=1
giving
C_{1}=1/I_{0}(t)
, and hence

\mu_{n}=\frac{I_{n}(t)}{I_{0}(t)}.

There is only one measure on the unit circle with these moments. The integral representation

I_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{t\cos\theta}\cos n\theta\,d\theta,

(see
[
9
, Eq. 10.32.3]
) shows that the measure
\nu
is absolutely continuous with density
v(\theta)=e^{t\cos\theta}/[2\pi I_{0}(t)]
. This is the measure that appears in the random unitary matrix model that was investigated by Periwal and Shevitz

[
11
]
, for which they showed that the Verblunsky coefficients satisfy (
2.3
). We have now shown that this is the only measure with this property, and hence there is a unique solution of (
2.3
) with
a_{-1}=-1
and
-1<a_{n}<1
for every
n\geq 0
.

4
Bounds for the unique solution

We return to the discrete Painlevé equation as given by (
1.1
). An upper bound for the
unique solution described in Theorem
1
is given by:

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\neq 0
, then the solution of (
1.1
) which satisfies
x_{0}=1

and
-1<x_{n}<1
for
n\geq 1
satisfies

|x_{n}|\leq\frac{2^{n}}{|\alpha|^{n}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)!!}=\frac{4^{n}}{|\alpha|^{n}}\frac{n!}{(2n)!},\qquad n\geq 0.

(4.1)

Proof.

We will make some successive approximations and start with

|x_{n}|\leq 1:=b_{n,0},\qquad n\geq 0.

(4.2)

From Theorem
1
we know that
-1<x_{n}<1
for
n\geq 1
, hence from (
1.1
)
we find

|\alpha|n|x_{n}|=(1-x_{n}^{2})|x_{n+1}+x_{n-1}|\leq|x_{n+1}|+|x_{n-1}|

so that

|x_{n}|\leq\frac{|x_{n+1}|+|x_{n-1}|}{|\alpha|n},\qquad n\geq 1.

(4.3)

If we insert our initial bound (
4.2
) in this inequality, then

|x_{n}|\leq\frac{2}{|\alpha|n}:=b_{n,1},\qquad n\geq 1.

(4.4)

In general, our
k
-th approximation is
|x_{n}|\leq b_{n,k}
for
n\geq k
, and (
4.3
)
gives the recurrence relation

b_{n,k}=\frac{b_{n+1,k-1}+b_{n-1,k-1}}{|\alpha|n},\qquad n\geq k.

(4.5)

We claim that

b_{n,k}=\frac{2^{k}}{|\alpha|^{k}}\frac{(n-k-1)!!}{(n+k-1)!!},\qquad n\geq k

(4.6)

where

n!!=\begin{cases}n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots 2,&\textrm{if $n$ is even},\\
n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots 1,&\textrm{if $n$ is odd}.\end{cases}

This can easily be proved by induction on
k
. Indeed, for
k=0
the formula (
4.6
) gives

b_{n,0}=1
, which corresponds to (
4.2
). For
k=1
formula (
4.6
) gives

b_{n,1}=2/(|\alpha|n)
for
n\geq 1
, which corresponds to (
4.4
). Suppose that
(
4.6
) is true for
k-1
, then (
4.5
) gives for
n\geq k

\displaystyle b_{n,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\alpha|n}\left(\frac{2^{k-1}}{|\alpha|^{k-1}}\frac{(n-k+1)!!}{(n+k-1)!!}+\frac{2^{k-1}}{|\alpha|^{k-1}}\frac{(n-k-1)!!}{(n+k-3)!!}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\alpha|n}\frac{2^{k-1}}{|\alpha|^{k-1}}\frac{(n-k-1)!!}{(n+k-1)!!}\left(n-k+1+n+k-1\right)

and the right hand side is indeed equal to (
4.6
). We now have the bound

|x_{n}|\leq b_{n,k}=\frac{2^{k}}{|\alpha|^{k}}\frac{(n-k-1)!!}{(n+k-1)!!},\qquad n\geq k.

The bound in (
4.1
) corresponds to the choice
k=n
.
∎

Stirling’s formula gives the asymptotic behavior

\frac{4^{n}}{|\alpha|^{n}}\frac{n!}{(2n)!}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{e}{|\alpha|n}\right)^{n}

which shows that
x_{n}
tends to zero faster than exponentially.
