Horofunctions and metric compactification of noncompact Hermitian symmetric spaces The authors gratefully acknowledge the support by the EPSRC (UK) (grant EP/R044228/1)
Cho-Ho Chu, María Cueto-Avellaneda, Bas Lemmens

Abstract
Given a Hermitian symmetric space M M of noncompact type, we give a complete description of the horofunctions in the metric compactification of M M with respect to the Carathéodory distance, via the realisation of M M as the open unit ball D D of a Banach space ( V , ∥ ⋅ ∥ ) (V,\|\cdot\|) equipped with a Jordan structure, called a JB ∗ \mathrm{JB}^{*} -triple. The Carathéodory distance ρ \rho on D D has a Finsler structure. It is the integrated distance of the Carathéodory differential metric, and the norm ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| in the realisation is the Carathéodory norm with respect to the origin 0 ∈ D 0\in D . We also identify the horofunctions of the metric compactification of ( V , ∥ ⋅ ∥ ) (V,\|\cdot\|) and relate its geometry and global topology to the closed dual unit ball (i.e., the polar of D D ). Moreover, we show that the exponential map exp 0 : V ⟶ D \exp_{0}\colon V\longrightarrow D at 0 ∈ D 0\in D extends to a homeomorphism between the metric compactifications of ( V , ∥ ⋅ ∥ ) (V,\|\cdot\|) and ( D , ρ ) (D,\rho) , preserving the geometric structure. Consequently, the metric compactification of M M admits a concrete realisation as the closed dual unit ball of ( V , ∥ ⋅ ∥ ) (V,\|\cdot\|) .

Hermitian symmetric space, bounded symmetric domain, horofunction, metric compactification,
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple, Carathéodory distance.

1
Introduction

Compactifications of symmetric spaces is a particularly rich subject, which has been studied extensively
[
4
,
16
]
.
A variety of compactifications of symmetric spaces have been introduced with different applications in mind. For instance, Satake
[
33
]
introduced his compactifications in his study of automorphic forms, and the Martin and Furstenberg compactifications
[
13
,
29
]
were introduced to analyse harmonic functions.

Recently, metric (or horofunction) compactifications with respect to invariant Finsler metrics have been used to investigate different types of compactifications of symmetric spaces. In particular, it was shown in
[
17
,
34
]
that generalised Satake compactifications and Martin compactifications of symmetric spaces can be realised as metric compactifications under suitable invariant Finsler metrics. In
[
22
]
an explicit invariant Finsler metric was constructed on symmetric spaces whose metric compactification gives the maximal Satake compactification, and in
[
14
]
the minimal Satake compactification of
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}_{n}
was realised as a metric compactification.

However, for relatively few noncompact type symmetric spaces, with invariant Finsler metrics, is the metric compactification well understood. In this paper we completely determine the metric compactifcation of noncompact type Hermitian symmetric spaces under the Carathéodory distance and provide a detailed analysis of its geometry and global topology. The Carathéodory distance plays an important role in the geometry and analysis of Hermitian symmetric spaces, hence it is natural to consider its metric compactification.

Given a Hermitian symmetric space
M
of noncompact type, the Harish-Chandra embedding,

M\approx\mathcal{D}\subset\mathfrak{p}^{+}\hookrightarrow M_{c},

identifies
M
bihomorphically with a bounded symmetric domain
\mathcal{D}
in a complex Euclidean space
\mathfrak{p}^{+}
,
which is biholomorphic to an open dense subset of the compact dual
M_{c}
of
M
. By the seminal works of Loos
[
28
]
and Kaup
[
23
]
, one can equip
\mathfrak{p}^{+}
with a Jordan algebraic structure and a norm
\|\cdot\|
so that

\mathcal{D}
is biholomorphic to the open unit ball
D
of
(\mathfrak{p}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
, which is called a
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple.

Since the Carathéodory distance is invariant under biholomorphisms, one can transfer from
M
to the open unit ball
D
in the
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple
V=(\mathfrak{p}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
to study the metric compactification and exploit Jordan theory and functional analysis. This is our task in the paper.

For symmetric spaces of noncompact type, the metric compactification with respect to the Riemannian distance can be identified with its geodesic compactification (
[
2
]
and
[
19
, Proposition 12.6]
), and is known to be homeomorphic to a Euclidean ball, see
[
5
, Chapter II.8]
. It has also been observed for various classes of finite dimensional normed spaces that the geometry and global topology of the metric compactification is closely related to the closed dual unit ball of the norm. In
[
20
,
21
]
this connection was established for normed spaces with polyhedral unit balls, see also
[
10
]
. At present, however, it is unknown
[
22
, Question 6.18]
if this duality phenomenon holds for general finite dimensional normed spaces.

In
[
24
,
26
]
it was shown that the duality phenomenon does not only appear in finite dimensional normed spaces, but also occurs in metric compactifications of certain symmetric spaces with invariant Finsler norms. More precisely, it was shown in
[
24
,
26
]
for symmetric cones equipped with the Thompson and Hilbert distances that the geometry and global topology of the metric compactification coincides with the geometry of the closed dual unit ball of the Finsler norm in the tangent space at the base point of the metric compactification.

In these symmetric spaces
N
the connection between the geometry of the metric compactification and the dual unit ball manifests itself in the following way. The horofunction boundary
N(\infty)
in the metric compactification of the symmetric space
N
carries an equivalence relation where two horofunctions
g
and
h
are equivalent if
\sup_{x\in N}|g(x)-h(x)|<\infty
. This relation yields a natural partition of the horofunction boundary. On the other hand, the boundary of the closed dual unit ball
B^{*}
of the Finsler norm on the tangent space
T_{b}N
at the base point
b\in N
, is partitioned by the relative interiors of its boundary faces. For the symmetric spaces
N
considered in
[
24
,
26
]
it was shown that there exists a homeomorphism
\varphi
from the metric compactification
N\cup N(\infty)
onto
B^{*}
, which maps each equivalence class of
N(\infty)
onto the relative interior of a boundary face of
B^{*}
. In this sense the dual ball captures the geometry of the metric compactification.

Moreover, in
[
24
]
it was observed that the metric compactification of
N
is closely related to the metric compactification of the normed space
(T_{b}N,\|\cdot\|_{b})
, where
\|\cdot\|_{b}
is the Finsler norm on the tangent space
T_{b}N
at the base point
b\in N
. More explicitly, it was shown that the exponential map
\exp_{b}\colon T_{b}N\longrightarrow N
extends to a homeomorphism between the metric compactifications of the normed space
(T_{b}N,\|\cdot\|_{b})
and
N
under the invariant Finsler distance. Furthermore, the extension preserves the equivalence classes in the horofunction boundaries. A case in point is the Hilbert distance on
\mathrm{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})/\mathrm{SU}_{n}
, which can be realised as the manifold of positive-definite Hermitian

matrices with determinant
1
. The tangent space at the identity

is the real vector space of the
n\times n
Hermitian matrices with trace
0
and has Finsler norm,
|A|_{I}=\max\sigma(A)-\min\sigma(A)
, where
\sigma(A)
is the set of eigenvalues of
A
.

For a noncompact type Hermitian symmetric space
M
identified as the open unit ball

of a JB*-triple
V=(\mathfrak{p}^{+},\|\cdot\|)
, with Carathéodory distance
\rho
, the
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple norm

is the corresponding Finsler norm

[
23
, (4.5)]
. In this paper we show that the analogues of the results in
[
24
,
26
]
hold for the metric compactification of
(D,\rho)
.

More specifically, the following results are established. We provide a complete description of the horofunctions of
(D,\rho)
in Theorem
4.4
. We also determine the horofunctions of the
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple

in Theorem

an explicit homeomorphism

between the horofunction compactification of
(V,\|\cdot\|)
and its closed dual unit ball. Further, we show in Section

maps each equivalence class in the horofunction boundary of

onto the relative interior of a boundary face of the closed dual unit ball. Finally we prove in Section

and

.

\mathrm{JB}^{*}

2
Horofunctions

The origins of the idea of the metric (or horofunction) compactification go back to Gromov
[
2
]
. It has proven to be a valuable tool in numerous fields such as, geometric group theory, complex and real dynamics, Riemannian geometry, and geometric analysis. It captures asymptotic geometric properties of metric spaces and provides ways to analyse mappings or groups acting on them. Here we mostly follow the set up as in
[
31
]
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\mathbb{R}^{X}
be the space of all real functions on
X
equipped with the topology of pointwise convergence. Fix a point
b\in X
, called the
base point
, and write
\mathrm{Lip}^{1}_{b}(X)
to denote the set of all functions
h\in\mathbb{R}^{X}
such that
h(b)=0
and
h
is
1
-Lipschitz, i.e.,
|h(x)-h(y)|\leq d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

The set
\mathrm{Lip}^{1}_{b}(X)
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
. Indeed, it is easy to verify that the complement of
\mathrm{Lip}^{1}_{b}(X)
is open. Moreover, as
|h(x)|=|h(x)-h(b)|\leq d(x,b)
for all
x\in X
and
h\in\mathrm{Lip}^{1}_{b}(X)
, we have that
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)\subseteq[-d(x,b),d(x,b)]^{X}
. The set
[-d(x,b),d(x,b)]^{X}
is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem, and hence
\mathrm{Lip}^{1}_{b}(X)
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
.

For
y\in X
define the real valued function,

h_{y}(z)=d(z,y)-d(b,y)\hskip 20.00003pt(z\in X).

(2.1)

Note that
h_{y}(b)=0
and
|h_{y}(z)-h_{y}(w)|=|d(z,y)-d(w,y)|\leq d(z,w)
for all
z,w\in X
, and hence
h_{y}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Denote the closure of
\{h_{y}\colon y\in X\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{X}
by
\overline{X}
, which is compact. The set
X(\infty)=\overline{X}\setminus\{h_{y}\colon y\in X\}
is called the
horofunction boundary of
(X,d)
. The elements of
X(\infty)
are called
horofunctions
, and the set
X\cup X(\infty)
is called the
metric (or horofunction) compactification of
(X,d)
. The elements of
\overline{X}
are called
metric functionals
, and the metric functionals
h_{y}
in (
2.1
) are called
internal points
.

The topology of pointwise convergence on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
coincides with the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets, see
[
30
, p. 291]
. In general the topology of pointwise convergence on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
is not metrizable, and hence horofunctions are limits of nets rather than sequences. However, if the metric space is separable, then the topology is metrizable and each horofunction is the limit of a sequence. In fact, one can verify that given a countable dense subset
\{y_{k}\colon k\in\mathbb{N}\}
of
(X,d)
, the function
\sigma
on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)\times\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
given by,

\sigma(f,g)=\sum_{k}2^{-k}\min\{1,|f(y_{k})-g(y_{k})|\}\mbox{\hskip 10.00002ptfor $f,g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)$,}

is a metric whose topology coincides with the pointwise convergence topology on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
,
[
30
, p. 289, Ex. 10*]
.

The metric compactification may not be a compactification in the usual topological sense, as the embedding
\iota\colon y\in X\mapsto h_{y}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
may fail to have the necessary properties. However, the embedding
\iota\colon X\longrightarrow\iota(X)
is always a continuous bijection. Indeed, if
x\in X
and we consider a neighbourhood
U=\{h\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)\colon|h(y)-h_{x}(y)|<\varepsilon,\,y\in X\}
of
h_{x}
for
\varepsilon>0
, then for
z\in X
with
d(x,z)<\varepsilon/2
we have that

|h_{z}(y)-h_{x}(y)|\leq|d(y,z)-d(y,x)|+|d(b,z)-d(b,x)|\leq 2d(z,x)<\varepsilon,

and hence
\iota(z)\in U
, which shows that
\iota
is continuous. Moreover, if
x,z\in X
and
h_{x}=h_{z}
, then
0=(h_{z}(x)-h_{x}(x))+(h_{x}(z)-h_{z}(z))=2d(x,z)
, which gives injectivity.

It can happen that
\iota\colon X\longrightarrow\iota(X)
does not have a continuous inverse. If, however,
(X,d)
is proper and geodesic, then the metric compactification will be a compactification in the usual topological sense. We provide some details of this fact below. Recall that a metric space
(X,d)
is
proper
if all its closed balls are compact. Note that a proper metric space is separable, as each closed ball is compact and hence separable.

A map
\gamma
from a, possibly unbounded, interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
into a metric space
(X,d)
is called a
geodesic path
if

d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=|s-t|\mbox{\hskip 10.00002ptfor all }s,t\in I.

The image,
\gamma(I)
, is called a
geodesic
. A metric space
(X,d)
is said to be
geodesic
if for each
x,y\in X
there exists a geodesic path
\gamma\colon[a,b]\longrightarrow X
connecting
x
and
y
, i.e,
\gamma(a)=x
and
\gamma(b)=y
.

Hermitian symmetric spaces with Carathéodory distance are proper geodesic metric spaces (cf.
[
15
]
). In the discussion below we will focus on the metric compactification of such metric spaces.

The horofunctions of a proper geodesic metric space
(X,d)
are precisely the limits of converging sequences
(h_{x_{k}})
such that
d(b,x_{k})\longrightarrow\infty
. A slightly stronger assertion was shown in
[
31
, Theorem 4.7]
, but for our purposes the following statement will suffice, see also
[
25
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
(X,d)
is a proper geodesic metric space, then
h\in X(\infty)
if and only if there exists a sequence
(x_{k})
in
X
with
d(b,x_{k})\longrightarrow\infty
such that
(h_{x_{k}})
converges to
h\in\overline{X}
as
k\longrightarrow\infty
.

We use this lemma to show that, in this case, the embedding
\iota\colon X\longrightarrow\iota(X)
has a continuous inverse.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
(X,d)
is a proper geodesic metric space, then
\iota\colon X\longrightarrow\iota(X)
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

From the previous observations it remains to show that
\iota\colon X\longrightarrow\iota(X)
has a continuous inverse.
Let
h_{z_{0}}=\iota(z_{0})
where
z_{0}\in X
. Note that as
(X,d)
is proper it is also separable. So, to prove continuity of
\iota^{-1}
at
h_{z_{0}}
, we can use sequences, as the topology of pointwise convergence on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}^{1}(X)
is metrizable. Let
(z_{k})
be a sequence in
X
with
h_{z_{k}}\longrightarrow h_{z_{0}}
. By Lemma
2.2
we know that
(z_{k})
is bounded, and hence after taking a subsequence we may assume that
z_{k}\longrightarrow z
. It follows that
h_{z}=h_{z_{0}}
, and hence the injectivity of
\iota
implies that
z=z_{0}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Thus, the metric compactification is a compactification in the usual topological sense if
(X,d)
is a proper geodesic space.

Special horofunctions come from so-called almost geodesics sequences. They were introduced by Rieffel
[
31
]
and further developed by Walsh and co-workers in
[
1
,
27
,
35
,
36
]
. A sequence
(x_{k})
in
(X,d)
is called an
almost geodesic
if for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
N\geq 0
such that

d(x_{n},x_{m})+d(x_{m},x_{0})-d(x_{n},x_{0})<\varepsilon\mbox{\hskip 10.00002ptfor all }n\geq m\geq N.

In particular, every unbounded almost geodesic sequence yields a horofunction for a proper geodesic metric space, see
[
31
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a proper geodesic metric space. If
(x_{k})
is an unbounded almost geodesic in
(X,d)
, then

h(z)=\lim_{k}d(z,x_{k})-d(b,x_{k})

exists for all
z\in X
and
h\in X(\infty)
.

Given a proper geodesic metric space
(X,d)
, a horofunction
h
is called a
Busemann point
if there exists an almost geodesic
(x_{k})
in
X
such that
h(z)=\lim_{k}d(z,x_{k})-d(b,x_{k})
for all
z\in X
. The collection of all Busemann points is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{X}
.

The set of Busemann points can be equipped with a metric known as the detour distance, which was introduced in
[
1
]
, and is defined as follows. Suppose
(X,d)
is a proper geodesic metric space and
h,h^{\prime}\in X(\infty)
are horofunctions. Let
W_{h}
be the collection of neighbourhoods of
h
in
\overline{X}
. Then the
detour cost
is given by

H(h,h^{\prime})=\sup_{W\in W_{h}}\left(\inf_{x\colon\iota(x)\in W}d(b,x)+h^{\prime}(x)\right),

and the
detour distance
is defined by

\delta(h,h^{\prime})=H(h,h^{\prime})+H(h^{\prime},h).

It is known
[
27
,
36
]
that if
(x_{k})
is an almost geodesic converging to a horofunction
h
, then

H(h,h^{\prime})=\lim_{k}d(b,x_{k})+h^{\prime}(x_{k})

(2.2)

for all horofunctions
h^{\prime}
. Moreover, on the set of Busemann points
\mathcal{B}_{X}
the detour distance is a metric where points can be at infinite distance from each other, see
[
27
,
36
]
.

The detour distance induces a partition of
\mathcal{B}_{X}
into equivalence classes, called
parts
, where
h
and
h^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{B}_{X}
are equivalent if
\delta(h,h^{\prime})<\infty
. In particular, if all horofunctions are Busemann points, so
\mathcal{B}_{M}=X(\infty)
, then
X(\infty)
is the disjoint union of parts, each of which is a metric space under the detour distance.

The horofunction boundary
X(\infty)
has a natural partition induced by the equivalence relation:

h\sim h^{\prime}\mbox{\hskip 10.00002ptif \hskip 10.00002pt}\sup_{x\in X}|h(x)-h^{\prime}(x)|<\infty.

It was shown in
[
36
, Proposition 4.5]
that two Busemann points
h
and
h^{\prime}
in
X(\infty)
have finite detour distance if, and only if,
h\sim h^{\prime}
. Thus, if all horofunctions are Busemann points, then the partition of
X(\infty)
into parts coincides with the partition into equivalence classes
X(\infty)/\sim
.

We also like to note that each (surjective) isometry
\psi\colon X\longrightarrow X
extends as a homeomorphism to
X(\infty)
by

\psi(h)(x)=h(\psi^{-1}(x))-h(\psi^{-1}(b))\hskip 20.00003pt(x\in X,h\in X(\infty)).

It is known that on the Busemann points in
X(\infty)
the extension
\psi
is an isometry under the detour distance, see e.g.,
[
27
]
. Also if
h\sim g
in
X(\infty)
, then
\psi(h)\sim\psi(g)
.

3
Jordan algebraic structures

In this section, we recall some necessary definitions and results concerning Jordan algebraic structures associated with Hermitian symmetric spaces.
Throughout,
M
will denote an arbitrary Hermitian symmetric space of noncompact type. To determine the horofunctions of
M
and analyse the geometry and global topology of its metric compactification with respect to
the Carathéodory distance (and base point
b\in M
), we make use of the fact that
M
can be realised as the open unit ball in a
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple. Indeed, there exists a biholomorphism,

\psi\colon M\penalty\ \penalty\ ^{\Large\underrightarrow{\psi_{1}}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{D}\penalty\ \penalty\ ^{\Large\underrightarrow{\psi_{2}}}\penalty\ \penalty\ D\subset V,

(3.1)

onto the open unit ball
D
of a finite dimensional
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple
V
, with
\psi(b)=0
, where
\psi_{1}
is the Harish-Chandra embedding and
\psi_{2}
is the Kaup Riemann mapping, which is unique up to a linear isometry
[
23
, Theorem 4.9]
.

Since the biholomorphism
\psi
preserves the Carathéodory distance, it induces a homeomorphism between the metric compactifications of
M
and
D
. Hence we can, and will, work in the setting

D\subset V,\mbox{ where $D$ is the open unit ball of a finite dimensional $\mathrm{JB}^{*}$-triple $V$.}

The results can be transferred to the corresponding ones for
M
via
\psi
.

Let us now present some of the essential background on Jordan triple systems, where we focus on the finite dimensional case. A
\mathrm{JB}^{*}
-triple
is a complex Banach space
V
equipped with
a continuous triple product

\{\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\}\colon V\times V\times V\longrightarrow V,

called a
Jordan triple product
, which is linear and symmetric in the outer variables, and conjugate linear in the
middle variable, and satisfies the following axioms:

(i)

\{a,b,\{x,y,z\}\}=\{\{a,b,x\},y,z\}-\{x,\{b,a,y\},z\}+\{x,y,\{a,b,z\}\}
,  (Jordan triple identity)

(ii)

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a
is Hermitian, that is,

\|\exp it(a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)\|=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iii)

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a
has nonnegative spectrum
\sigma(a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)
;

(iv)

\|a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a\|=\|a\|^{2},\hskip 20.00003pt

for
a,b,x,y,z\in V
,
where
a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b:V\longrightarrow V
is a bounded linear map, called a
box operator
,
defined by

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b(x)=\{a,b,x\}\hskip 20.00003pt(x\in V)

(3.2)

and condition (iv) can be replaced by

\|\{a,a,a\}\|=\|a\|^{3}\hskip 20.00003pt(a\in V).

(3.3)

We note that the box operator in (
3.2
) satisfies
\|a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b\|\leq\|a\|\|b\|
, as the triple product is a contractive mapping, that is,

\|\{a,b,c\}\|\leq\|a\|\|b\|\|c\|\hskip 10.00002pt{\rm for\penalty\ all}\penalty\ a,b,c\in V.

Remark 3.1
.

By definition, a Hermitian operator
T\colon V\longrightarrow V
has
real numerical range, which is the closed convex hull of its spectrum

\sigma(T)
and
\|T\|=\sup\{|\lambda|\colon\lambda\in\sigma(T)\}

[
3
, pp. 46–54]
.
In particular, given
a,b

A prime example of a JB*-triple is the space of

Given
\mathrm{JB}^{*}

Given

is family of mutually orthogonal minimal tripotents.

Remark 4.3
.

The minimal tripotents
e_{1k},\ldots,e_{rk}
in the spectral decomposition of

induce a joint Peirce
decomposition of

, with joint Peirce projections

and

By (

is of the form

B(y_{k},y_{k})^{-1/2}=\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}(1-\alpha_{ik}^{2})^{-1/2}(1-\alpha_{jk}^{2})^{-1/2}P_{ij}^{k}\hskip 10.00002pt(\alpha_{0k}=0).

As

converges to a minimal tripotent

for

as above, and the

of Peirce projections (cf.

).

Furthermore, if

are mutually orthogonal tripotents and

for all

, see
[

, Lemma 2.1(i)]
.

\displaystyle h(z)

.

Conversely, each function

of the form in (

) is a horofunction of

.

Proof.

Let
h=\lim_{k}h_{a_{k}}
be a horofunction, where

and

. For each

be a spectral decomposition, with

After taking a subsequence, we may assume
\alpha_{ik}=r_{k}-\lambda_{ik}\longrightarrow\alpha_{i}\in[0,\infty]
and

for all

, where

and
2^{-1}(\|r_{k}^{-1}(x-a_{k})\|+1)\longrightarrow 1
. Hence (

) follows readily from Lemma

and the preceding remark.

Conversely, let

be of the form (

), where

is a nonempty subset of

For

\alpha_{ik}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\alpha_{i}&(i\in I)\\
k&(\rm{otherwise})\end{matrix}\right.

\lim_{k\longrightarrow\infty}(2r_{k})^{-1}(\|(x-a_{k}){\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,(x-a_{k})\|-r_{k}^{2})=\sup_{u\in V_{2}(e_{I})\colon\langle u,u\rangle=1}\langle(-\frac{1}{2}(e_{I}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,x+x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{I})-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}(e_{i}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{i}))u,u\rangle

Remark 5.3
.

In the course of proving the preceding theorem, we observe that each horofunction
h\in V(\infty)
can actually be constructed from a sequence
(a_{k})
going to infinity along a straight line, which is a geodesic in the normed space
V
. In fact, the sequence
a_{k}=ke_{I}-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}e_{i}
used in the proof lies on the straight line,
t\mapsto te_{I}-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}e_{i}
in the flat
\oplus_{i\in I}\mathbb{R}e_{i}
. Also note that if
k\geq m
with
k\geq\max_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}
, then
\|a_{k}\|=k
and
h_{a_{k}}(a_{m})=\|a_{k}-a_{m}\|-\|a_{k}\|=(k-m)-k=-m
,
so that
h(a_{m})=-m
for all
m
.

By the remark we have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.4
.

Each horofunction in
{V}(\infty)
is a Busemann point.

For general finite dimensional normed vector spaces it need not be true that all horofunctions are Busemann points, see
[
35
]
.

6
Homeomorphism onto the dual unit ball

In this section we give a homeomorphism of the metric compactification of
V
onto the closed dual unit ball
B^{*}
of
(V,\|\cdot\|)
. We subsequently show in the next section that this homeomorphism maps each equivalence class in
V(\infty)/\sim
onto the relative interior of a boundary face of
B^{*}
. So, the dual ball
B^{*}
captures the geometry of the metric compactification of
V
.

To prove these results we need a lemma concerning the partial ordering on the set of tripotents of a JB
∗
-triple. Recall that, given two tripotents
c
and
e
in
V
, we shall write
c\leq e
if
e-c
is a tripotent in
V
orthogonal to
c
, or equivalently, if and only if
P_{2}(c)e=c
(
[
12
, Corollary 1.7]
). We also have
c\leq e
if and only if
c
is a projection in the JB
∗
-algebra
V_{2}(e)
. Particularly,

c=\{e,c,e\}

(6.1)

(cf.
[
6
, pp.34-36]
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Given two tripotents
c,e\in V
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

c\leq e
,

(ii)

\{c,P_{2}(c)e,c\}+\{c,c,P_{2}(c)e\}=2c
,

(iii)

\{c,e,c\}+\{c,c,e\}=2c
,

(iv)

B(e,c)w=0
for all
w\in V_{2}(c)
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). If
c\leq e
, then
P_{2}(c)e=c
and (ii) follows directly.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). We note that
\{c,P_{2}(c)e,c\}\in V_{2}(c)
by (
3.9
), and
\{c,c,P_{2}(c)e\}=P_{2}(c)e\in V_{2}(c)
.

Observe that
c
is a maximal tripotent in the JB*-triple
V_{2}(c)
, and hence
c
is an extreme point of the closed unit ball of
V_{2}(c)
(
[
6
, Theorem 3.2.3]
). As
\|\{c,P_{2}(c)e,c\}\|,\|P_{2}(c)e\|\leq 1
, we conclude that
P_{2}(c)e=c
, that is,
c\leq e
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Suppose
c\leq e
. The orthogonality between
e
and
e-c
gives clearly (iii).

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(i). The arguments used in the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) apply,
by observing that
\{c,e,c\}=\{c,P_{2}(c)e,c\}\in V_{2}(c)
from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), which gives
\{c,c,e\}=2c-\{c,e,c\}\in V_{2}(c)
.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iv). Let
w\in V_{2}(c)
. Using orthogonality, (
6.1
) and the Jordan triple identity, we deduce

\displaystyle B(e,c)w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w-2\{e,c,w\}+\{e,\{c,w,c\},e\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w-2\{e,c,w\}+2\{e,c,\{e,c,w\}\}-\{w,c,\{e,c,e\}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w-2\{e,c,w\}+2\{e,c,\{e,c,w\}\}-\{w,c,c\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w-2\{e,c,w\}+2\{e,c,\{e,c,w\}\}-w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w-2w+2w-w=0.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i).
Let
a=P_{2}(c)e\in V_{2}(c)
. We show
a=c
. Using the identities (JP1), (JP3) and (JP2) in
[
28
, Appendix]
,
one deduces that

Q(c)B(a,c)w=Q(c)B(e,c)w=0,\hskip 20.00003pt(w\in V_{2}(c)).

Now take
w=c-\{c,a,c\}\in V_{2}(c)
. Then we have

\displaystyle B(a,c)w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B(a,c)c-B(a,c)\{c,a,c\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c-2a+\{a,c,a\}-(\{c,a,c\}-2\{a,c,\{c,a,c\}\}+\{a,\{c,\{c,a,c\},c\},a\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c-2a+\{a,c,a\}-\{c,a,c\}+2\{c,a,a\}-\{a,a,a\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{c-a,c-a,c-a\},

where

\{c,a,a\}=\{c,a,\{c,c,a\}\}=\{\{c,a,c\},c,a\}-\{c,\{a,c,c\},a\}+\{c,c,\{c,a,a\}\}=\{\{c,a,c\},c,a\}
.
As
\{c-a,c-a,c-a\}\in V_{2}(c)
, it follows from (
3.12
) that

\|\{c-a,c-a,c-a\}\|=\|Q(c)\{c-a,c-a,c-a\}\|=\|Q(c)B(a,c)w\|=0,

so that
c-a=0
by (
3.3
).
∎

By the Riesz representation theorem, the map

We now analyse the geometry of the metric compactification of

For the basic terminology from convex analysis we follow

[

To analyse the equivalence classes it is useful to recall that for Busemann points

Lemma 7.3

Suppose that

(8.8)

Proposition 8.5

Let

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}e_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\mu_{i}c_{i}
.

Proof.

Let

. Then

.
Since

and
h^{\prime}
are Busemann points, we have by (

) that

H(h,h^{\prime})=\lim_{t}t+h^{\prime}(\gamma(t))=\lim_{t}\frac{1}{2}\log\|\exp(2t)\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq p}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}B(\gamma(t),\gamma(t))^{-1/2}B(\gamma(t),e)P^{\prime}_{ij}\|,

in (

),

.

Let

.

\lim_{t}\exp(2t)B(\gamma(t),\gamma(t))^{-1/2}B(\gamma(t),e)w_{kl}=\exp{(\alpha_{k})}\exp{(\alpha_{l})}w_{kl}=\lambda_{k}^{-1}\lambda_{l}^{-1}w_{kl},

For

[
28
, Corollary 3.15]
. Recall that by (
