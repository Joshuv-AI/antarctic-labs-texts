TELET: A Monotonic Algorithm to Design Large Dimensional Equiangular Tight Frames for Applications in Compressed Sensing
R. Jyothi, P. Babu

Abstract
An Equiangular tight frame (ETF) - also known as the Welch-bound-equality sequences - consists of a sequence of unit norm vectors whose absolute inner product is identical and minimal. Due to this unique property, these frames are preferred in different applications such as in constructing sensing matrices for compressed sensing systems, robust transmission and quantum computing. Construction of ETFs involves solving a challenging non-convex minimax optimization problem, and only a few methods were successful in constructing them, albeit only for smaller dimensions. In this paper, we propose an iterative algorithm named TE chnique to devise L arge dimensional E quiangular T ight-frames ( TELET -frames) based on the majorization minimization (MM) procedure - in which we design and minimize a tight upper bound for the ETF cost function at every iteration. Since TELET is designed using the MM approach, it inherits useful properties of MM such as monotonicity and guaranteed convergence to a stationary point. Subsequently, we use the derived frames to construct optimized sensing matrix for compressed sensing systems. In the numerical simulations, we show that the proposed algorithm can generate complex and real frames (in the order of hundreds) with very low mutual coherence value when compared to the state-of-the-art algorithm, with a slight increase in computational cost. Experiments using synthetic data and real images reveal that the optimized sensing matrix obtained through the frames constructed by TELET performs better, in terms of image reconstruction accuracy, than the sensing matrix constructed using state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Orthonormal basis is a powerful tool and has applications in diverse fields such as in computer vision, signal and image processing
[
1
]
. Sometimes, instead of orthonormal basis, one may prefer the use of an overcomplete spanning set of vectors. An example where such a situation occurs is in Gabor analysis, wherein orthonormal Gabor basis with good time-frequency localization do not exist, while it is not difficult to find overcomplete Gabor system with excellent time-frequency localization
[
2
]
. The overcomplete spanning set of vectors in finite dimensions is called a
Frame
. Formally, a frame is defined as a sequence of vectors
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
drawn from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
(
d\leq N
) that satisfies the generalized Parseval condition:

\begin{array}[]{ll}a\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}|{\boldsymbol{v}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}|^{2}\leq b\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{2}^{2}\>\>{\textrm{for all}}\>\>{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\end{array}

(1)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the Euclidian norm,
(\cdot)^{H}
is the Hermitian operator,
a
and
b
are the lower and upper frame bounds, respectively. If
a=b
in (
1
), then the frame is called
a
-tight frame, and if
a=b=1
, it is a Parseval frame. When the frame is
a
-tight, the condition in (
1

{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{H}=a\,{\mathbf{I}}_{d}
, where
{\boldsymbol{X}}=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}]\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
is the frame synthesis operator. This implies that the
d
non-zero singular values of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
are equal and is equal to
\sqrt{a}
. Hence, being a tight frame is a spectral constraint on the matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}
, which as discussed later in this section is used by the state-of-the-art algorithms to construct
a
-tight frames. When each
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
of an
a
-tight frame has unit norm i.e. if
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=1
, then such an
a
-tight frame is called as a Unit Norm Tight Frame (UNTF). These frames are commonly used in the construction of signature sequences of CDMA systems
[
3
]
. In applications such as sparse approximation
[
4
]
, robust transmission
[
5
]
, and quantum
computing
[
6
]
, it is required that the vectors comprising the UNTFs be maximally uncorrelated. The maximum correlation among the pair of vectors in a frame can be estimated using the mutual coherence metric
\mu({\boldsymbol{X}})
which is defined as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu({\boldsymbol{X}})=\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\rm maximize}\>\dfrac{\left|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\right|}{\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\|_{2}}\end{array}

(2)

Frames with small mutual coherence are known as incoherent frames. A lower bound (known as the Welch bound) on the minimum achievable correlation for any arbitrary frame is given as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu({\boldsymbol{X}})\geq\sqrt{\dfrac{N-d}{d(N-1)}}\end{array}

(3)

Unit norm tight frames which achieve equality in the Welch bound are known as Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF). Geometrically, it corresponds to a frame in which each pair of distinct unit norm vectors meet at the same angle
\theta={\rm{cos}}^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{N-d}{d(N-1)}}\right)
.
Note that, all orthonormal bases are ETFs with
\mu({\boldsymbol{X}})
equal to zero. Hence, ETFs can be perceived as a generalization of orthonormal bases. However, ETFs do not exist for all values of
d
and
N
. A necessary condition for the existence of a real ETF (
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
’s taking only real values) is given by

while a complex ETF (
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
’s taking complex values) can only exist when
N\leq d^{2}

[
7
]
.

\eta e^{i{\rm{arg}}G^{t}(i,j)}&i\neq j,|G^{t}(i,j)|>\eta\\

In Step. II, the nearest

I-A

Application to compressed sensing

Based on the theoretical findings of
[
17
]
, ETFs can be employed in the CS framework. In the following we briefly discuss the CS model and the need for optimal sensing matrix in CS systems. The CS framework involves reconstructing the high-dimensional
K
-sparse signal vector
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
from its corresponding low-dimensional measurement vector
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times 1}
(
d<<N
) via a carefully constructed sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times N}
using the relation
{\mathbf{y}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
The vector
{\boldsymbol{u}}
is
K
-sparse, since it can be represented using
K
non-zero coefficients over a fixed dictionary
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
i.e.
{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{s}}
(where
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
has
K
non-zero entries i.e.
\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\|_{0}=|\{i:s_{i}\neq 0\}|=K
). Hence, the low-dimensional measurement vector
{\mathbf{y}}
can be expressed as
{\mathbf{y}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{s}}
.
Now, given the measurement vector
{\mathbf{y}}
, the dictionary
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
and the sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
, the sparse signal vector
{\boldsymbol{s}}
can be reconstructed by solving the following
\ell_{0}
minimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\rm minimize}\>\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\|_{0}\>\>\>\>{\rm{s.t.}}\>\>{\mathbf{y}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{s}}\end{array}

(5)

The problem in (
5
) is NP-hard and can be solved using greedy algorithms such as orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)
[
18
,
19
]
or Basis Pursuit (BP) algorithm
[
20
]
.
As shown in
[
17
]
, the
K
-sparse signal
{\boldsymbol{s}}
can be uniquely recovered from the measurements
{\mathbf{y}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{s}}
as a solution to (
5
) as long as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|{\boldsymbol{s}}\|_{0}<\dfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\dfrac{1}{\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}})}\right]\end{array}

(6)

where
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
is called the equivalent dictionary. Note that the above upper bound is dictated by mutual coherence of
\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}})
, smaller the value of
\mu({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}})
, larger the sparsity of signal
{\boldsymbol{s}}
which can be successfully recovered. Also, as reported in
[
18
]
, the incoherence property of
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}
plays an important role in the performance of the OMP and BP algorithm. Hence, given the dictionary
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
, we need to find the optimal sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
such that
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}
is as close as possible to an ETF.

Elad in
[
21
]
was the first to come up with the idea of optimizing the sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
such that the equivalent dictionary is incoherent. However, instead of decreasing the mutual coherence of the equivalent dictionary, they worked on decreasing its average mutual coherence. Since then many different approaches have appeared. Majority of the approaches
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
designed the sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
such that the Gram matrix of the equivalent dictionary
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}
is close to a target Gram matrix
{\boldsymbol{G}}_{{\rm{Target}}}
.
The authors in
[
10
]
, obtained the optimal sensing matrix by first constructing the incoherent dictionary
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}
as an ETF using the alternating projection method (as discussed earlier) and then found the optimal sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
over the fixed dictionary by solving the following least squares problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}{\rm minimize}\|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED}}-{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\|_{F}^{2}\end{array}

(7)

Sometimes, even though the optimal sensing matrix is constructed such that the equivalent dictionary is as close as possible to an ETF, it may not perform well. This could happen when the sparse representation error (SRE) defined as
{\boldsymbol{E}}={\boldsymbol{U}}-{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{S}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{U}}=[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},\cdots{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R}]
is the original signal set and
{\boldsymbol{S}}
is its corresponding sparse coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{S}}=[{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2},\cdots{\boldsymbol{s}}_{R}]
is not taken into account. Projecting SRE to the measurement domain through the sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
that is not properly designed can affect the performance of the CS systems. Hence, the optimal sensing matrix must be constructed by also taking the SRE into consideration. Huang et.al. in
[
9
]
found the optimal sensing matrix such that equivalent dictionary is close to a target frame and also by taking the SRE into account:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}}{\rm minimize}\>\>\omega\|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{{\rm{ED-Target}}}-{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\|_{F}^{2}+(1-\omega)\|{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}({\boldsymbol{U}}-{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}{\boldsymbol{S}})\|_{F}^{2}\end{array}

(8)

where
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}
is a certain target equivalent dictionary and
\omega
is the trade-off factor which takes the value from
[0,1]
.

Contributions and outline

In this paper, we propose an algorithm named

II
ETF Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

A unit norm ETF is constructed by minimizing the maximum absolute inner product between pairs of distinct vectors in a frame:

\displaystyle\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}}{\rm minimize}

\displaystyle\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\rm maximize}\>\left|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\right|

(9)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\>\>\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=1\quad i=1,2,\cdots N

Suppose
{\boldsymbol{x}}=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}^{T},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}^{T},\cdots{\boldsymbol{x}}_{N}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{Nd\times 1}
is formed by stacking
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{l}
be a
d\times dN
selection matrix defined as
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{l}=[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times(l-1)d},{\mathbf{I}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times(N-l)d}]
, then we can obtain
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}
using the following relation:

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{l}={\boldsymbol{S}}_{l}{\boldsymbol{x}},\>l=1,2,\cdots,N\end{array}

(10)

Using the above relation, we rewrite the absolute inner product
|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}|
in (
9
) as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}|=|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{H}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{j}^{H}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}|=|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{H}{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}|\end{array}

(11)

Then using (
11
), the problem in (
9
) becomes:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}})

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=1\quad i=1,2,\cdots N

where
\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}})=2\left(|{{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}^{H}{{{{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}}}{{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}}}|^{2}\right)
. Please note that we have squared the objective function (which will not change the optimum) and scaled by two for our convenience. The problem in (
12
) can be further rewritten as:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}}},\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle\text{vec}^{H}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{Y}}}){\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}_{ij}}\text{vec}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{Y}}})

(13)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=1\quad i=1,2,\cdots N

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{Y}}}=\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}^{H},

where
{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}_{ij}}=\text{vec}\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)\text{vec}^{H}\left(\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)^{H}\right)+\text{vec}\left(\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)^{H}\right)\text{vec}^{H}\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)
. We solve the above non-convex minimax problem using the MM procedure which will be explained in the next subsection.

II-A

MM for the minimax problem

Consider the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\chi}{\rm minimize}\>f({\boldsymbol{x}})\end{array}

(14)

where the objective function
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
is equal to the maximum value of the
i^{th}
sub-function
\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
i.e.
f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
. The MM based algorithms solves the above problem by first constructing a surrogate function
g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
which majorizes the objective function
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
at the current iterate
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
and in the next step, the surrogate function is minimized to get the next iterate.

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\in\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\chi}{\rm arg\>min}\>g\left({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)

(15)

Majorization combines the tangency and the upper bound condition, i.e., a function qualifies to be a surrogate function if it satisfies the following conditions:

g\left({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)=f\left({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)

(16)

g\left({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)\geq f\left({\boldsymbol{x}}\right)

(17)

Constructing
g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
for the minimax problem in (
14
) does not look obvious, but can be worked out as follows, let:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(18)

where each
\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is a tight upper bound on
\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})
satisfying the following conditions:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(19)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\geq\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\end{array}

(20)

We now show that the surrogate function
{g}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
defined in (
18
) satisfies condition (
16
) and (
17
):

Proof of
g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
satisfying condition (
16
)
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(21)

where the first and second equality is by (
18
) and (
19
) and the last equality is by the definition of
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
.

Proof of
g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
satisfying condition (
17
)
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{g}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\geq\underset{i=1,2,\cdots,I}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{f}_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(22)

where the first equality is given by (
18
), second inequality is by (
20
) and the last equality is given by the definition of
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
Using (
15
), (
16
) and (
17
) it can be shown that the sequence of points

Majorization Function construction

In this subsection, we construct the surrogate function for the objective function in (

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{{{\boldsymbol{Y}}}}},\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

Lemma III.1

Let
f({\boldsymbol{x}})\in\mathbb{C}^{md\times 1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous twice differentiable function in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and if
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
has a bounded curvature, then there exists a matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}})
and
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
can be upper bounded as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})+{\rm{Re}}\left(\nabla f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})^{H}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})^{H}{\boldsymbol{M}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(24)

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
is the value taken by
{\boldsymbol{x}}
at the
t^{th}
iteration. The upper bound for
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
is quadratic and differentiable in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Proof:

Suppose there exists a matrix
{\boldsymbol{M}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{M}}\succeq\nabla^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}})
, then we have the following inequality by second order
Taylor expansion:

\begin{array}[]{ll}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})+{\rm{Re}}\left(\nabla f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})^{H}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})^{H}{\boldsymbol{M}}({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(25)

and equality is achieved at
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
.
∎

Lemma III.2

Given any
{{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}}
, a concave function
f\left({\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{md\times 1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
can be upper bounded as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}{f}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}\right)\leq{f}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)+{\rm{Re}}\left(\left(\nabla f\left({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)\right)^{H}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)\right)\end{array}

(26)

The upper bound for
f\left({\boldsymbol{x}}\right)
is linear in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Proof:

(28)

where

{\boldsymbol{x}}

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=-2|(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t})^{H}{{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t}|^{2}+2\left(-(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t})^{H}\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{t}_{ij}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t}+2\text{Re}\left((\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t})^{H}{\hat{{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{t}_{ij}}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right)\right)+2\lambda_{\text{max}}({{\boldsymbol{B}}}^{t}_{ij})N-\\
2d\left(-N^{2}+2\text{Re}\left(N\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{H}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{t}\right)\right)+2N^{2}d.\end{array}

The above surrogate function
\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
is linear in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and hence can now be minimized. Note that although we do double majorization i.e. find surrogate of the surrogate function, since
\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
is a tighter surrogate for
\tilde{g}_{ij}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)
, it can be viewed as a direct surrogate to the objective function
\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}})
in (
12
). The surrogate function in (
30
) can be rewritten compactly as:

\displaystyle\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=4\text{Re}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}{}^{H}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij}}})+s^{t}_{ij}

(31)

where
s^{t}_{ij}=-6|({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j}|^{2}+4N|({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j}|+4N^{2}d
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{ij}^{t}=\left({\mathbf{A}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)(({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})+\left({\mathbf{A}}_{ij}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\right)(({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})-\left(|({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})|{\mathbf{I}}_{Nd}\right){\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-Nd{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
. The superscript

in
s^{t}_{ij}
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{ij}^{t}
denotes that the computation of these quantities depends on the previous iterate value
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
. The only computational complexity involved in computing
\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
is the matrix vector product
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
. Exploiting the sparse structure of
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}
, the matrix vector product can be carried out efficiently as:
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}=[{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(j-1)\times 1},({{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{t}})^{T},{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(N-j)\times 1}]^{T}
and
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}=[{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(i-1)\times 1},({{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{t}})^{T},{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(N-i)\times 1}]^{T}

Then the majorizing function for
\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}\bar{g}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(32)

Hence at any iteration, given
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
, the surrogate minimization problem becomes:

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle 4\text{Re}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}{}^{H}{{{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij}}})+s^{t}_{ij}

(33)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}=1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N

III-B

Solution to the Minimization Problem

The surrogate minimization problem in (
33
) is non-convex in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
because of the equality constraint. However,
the equality constraint can be relaxed with the inequality constraint as the optimal solution of the relaxed problem would lie only on the boundary of the relaxed constraint set
[
29
]
. The relaxed problem is:

\displaystyle\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots,N(i\neq j)}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle 4\text{Re}(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}{}^{H}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij})+s^{t}_{ij}

(34)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N\

Next, we rewrite the inner maximization problem over the indices as maximization over a simplex variable:

\displaystyle\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{{\boldsymbol{q}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}=1}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle 4\text{Re}\left(\left({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}

(35)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N\

where
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}=[s^{t}_{12},\,s^{t}_{13}\cdots s^{t}_{N(N-1)}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N(N-1)\times 1}
and
{{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}=[{{\boldsymbol{d}}}^{t}_{12},{{\boldsymbol{d}}}^{t}_{13},\cdots,{{\boldsymbol{d}}}^{t}_{N(N-1)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2Nd\times N(N-1)}
.
The objective function in the above problem is bilinear in
{\boldsymbol{q}}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and the constraints are compact convex sets, then by minimax theorem
[
30
]
, we can swap minimax to maximin without altering the solution:

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{{\boldsymbol{q}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}=1}{\text{\text{maximum}}}$}

\displaystyle\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{\text{\text{minimize}}}

\displaystyle 4\text{Re}\left(\left({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}

(36)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N\

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\text{$\underset{{\boldsymbol{q}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}=1}{\text{\text{maximize}}}$}

\displaystyle h({\boldsymbol{q}})

(37)

where

\begin{array}[]{ll}h({\boldsymbol{q}})=\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{{\textrm{minimize}}}\>4\text{Re}\left(\left({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}\right)^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{s}}^{t}\\
\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>\>{\textrm{subject to}}\>\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N\end{array}

(38)

The above problem has a closed-form solution and is given as

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{i}=-\dfrac{{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{t}_{i}}}{\|{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{t}_{i}}\|_{2}},\>i=1,2,\cdots,N

(39)

where
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{t}
is made of blocks
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{t}_{1},{\boldsymbol{a}}^{t}_{2},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{a}}^{t}_{N}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{t}={{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}
where
{\boldsymbol{q}}
is obtained by solving the problem in (
37
). However, the problem in (
37
) does not have a closed-form solution and hence we solve it iteratively using Mirror Descent Algorithm (MDA) whose update step is given as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k+1}={\underset{{\boldsymbol{q}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}=1}{\text{\text{arg\>max}}}}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{h}}^{k})}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}+\dfrac{1}{\gamma_{k}}{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\psi}\left({\boldsymbol{q}},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)

(40)

where
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}
represents the value taken by
{\boldsymbol{q}}
at the
k^{th}
iteration of the MDA algorithm (this should not be confused with superscript
t
which is used as the iteration index in the outer loop),
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{k}\in\partial h({\boldsymbol{q}}^{k})
is the subgradient of
h({\boldsymbol{q}})
,
\gamma_{k}>0
is the step size given by
\gamma_{k}=\dfrac{\eta}{\sqrt{k}}
where
\eta
is some constant and
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\psi}\left({\boldsymbol{q}},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)
is Bregman-like distance generated by
\psi
and is given by
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{\psi}\left({\boldsymbol{q}},{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)=\psi({\boldsymbol{q}})-\psi({\boldsymbol{q}}^{k})-\nabla^{T}\psi\left({\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)\left({\boldsymbol{q}}-{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)
. Since the constraint in the maximization problem is a unit simplex, similar to
[
31
]
, we choose
\psi({\boldsymbol{q}})
as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\psi({\boldsymbol{q}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N(N-1)}q_{i}\textrm{log}\>q_{i}&{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}\\
+\infty&\rm{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right..\end{array}

(41)

where
\mathcal{Q}=\{{\boldsymbol{q}}\in\mathbf{R}^{N(N-1)}|{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{q}}=1,{\boldsymbol{q}}\geq 0\}
. Hence, the update step in (
40
) is simplified to:

\begin{array}[]{ll}q_{j}^{k+1}=\dfrac{q_{j}^{k}\textrm{exp}\left(-\gamma_{k}h_{j}^{k}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N(N-1)}q_{j}^{k}\textrm{exp}\left(-\gamma_{k}h_{j}^{k}\right)}\>j=1,2,\cdots N(N-1)\end{array}

(42)

The subgradient
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{k}
is given as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\boldsymbol{h}}^{k}=4\text{Re}\left(({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t})^{H}{\mathbf{y}}^{k}\right)+{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{t}\end{array}

(43)

where

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{y}}^{k}=\underset{{\mathbf{y}}}{\rm{min}}\>4\text{Re}\left(\left(\left({{\boldsymbol{D}}}\right)^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}\right)^{H}{\mathbf{y}}\right)\\
\hskip 14.22636pt\>{\textrm{subject to}}\>\|{\mathbf{y}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1,\,i=1,2,\cdots,N\end{array}

(44)

The above problem has closed-form solution and is given by
{\mathbf{y}}^{k}_{i}=-\dfrac{{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{k,t}_{i}}}{\|{{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{k,t}_{i}}\|_{2}}
for
i=1,2\cdots N
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{k,t}={{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}
(we have used the superscript
k,t
for the vector
{\boldsymbol{a}}
here since it depends on the
k^{th}
iteration value of
{\boldsymbol{q}}
of the MDA algorithm and also on the
t^{th}
iteration value of
{\boldsymbol{D}}
). The update step in (
42
) is repeated until convergence and once the optimal
{\boldsymbol{q}}
is found,
{\boldsymbol{x}}
can be calculated using (
39
).
The pseudocode of the proposed algorithm is shown in Algorithm 1.

Before we end this subsection, we will here discuss the computational complexity of TELET. As shown in the algorithmic development, TELET consists of an inner loop which implements MDA to solve the surrogate minimization problem and an outer loop which updates each element of
{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij}
and
s^{t}_{ij}
. We first discuss the complexity of MDA - which is mostly dictated by the matrix vector product
({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t})^{H}{\mathbf{y}}^{k}
and
({{\boldsymbol{D}}}^{t}){\boldsymbol{q}}^{k}
. The columns of the matrix
{{\boldsymbol{D}}^{t}}
denoted as
{{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij}}
has a special structure which we utilize to carry out this matrix vector operation efficiently. Note that each
{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}_{ij}
is made by adding a sparse vector:
[{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(j-1)\times 1},({{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{t}})^{T},{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(N-j)\times 1}]^{T}(({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})+[{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(i-1)\times 1},({{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}^{t}})^{T},{\boldsymbol{0}}^{T}_{d(N-i)\times 1}]^{T}(({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})
- which consists of
2d
non-zero elements and a non-sparse vector:
(|({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j})|+Nd){\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
- which is nothing but a scalar vector product. Exploiting this structure, the matrix vector products can be carried out efficiently with complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}d\right)
per iteration. In the case of outer loop, the major computations are that of computing the inner products
({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{i})^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}_{j}
which requires only
\mathcal{O}\left(Nd\right)
computation per iteration. Hence, after neglecting the lower terms, the computational complexity of TELET is equal to
\mathcal{O}\left(N^{2}d\right)
.

III-C

Proof of convergence

We denote the objective function of the problem in (
9
) as
f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
. Then, from the derivation of the proposed algorithm, we know that
f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is majorized by
g({\boldsymbol{x}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
in (
32
) at
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
over the constraint
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq 1
. Using (
15
), (
21
) and (
22
) it can be shown that the sequence of points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\}
generated by the MM procedure monotonically decrease the objective function:

\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1})\leq g({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\leq g({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}|{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})=f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\end{array}

(45)

where the first inequality and the last equality are obtained by using (
21
) and (
22
). The second inequality is by (
15
). Since
f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
in (
9
) is bounded below by zero and the points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\}
monotonically decrease the objective function, it is guaranteed that the sequence
\{f_{{}_{ETF}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})\}
will converge to a finite value.

In the following, we show that the sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\}
converges to the stationary point of the problem in (
9
). Assume that there is a subsequence
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j}}
converging to a limit point
{{\mathbf{p}}}
. Then from (
21
), (
22
) and (
45
) we get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j+1}}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j+1}})=f_{{}_{\textrm{ETF}}}({\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j+1}}})\leq f_{{}_{\textrm{ETF}}}({\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j}+1}})\\
\leq g(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j}+1}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j}})\leq g(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{r_{j}})\end{array}

(46)

where
g(.)
is the surrogate function as defined in (
32
). Then, letting
j\rightarrow\infty
, we get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}g({{\mathbf{p}}}|{{\mathbf{p}}})\leq g(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|{{\mathbf{p}}})\end{array}

(47)

which implies
g^{\prime}({{\mathbf{p}}}|{{\mathbf{p}}})\geq 0
. Since the first order behavior of surrogate function is same as function
f_{{}_{\textrm{ETF}}}({\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}})
(
[
28
]
),
g^{\prime}({{\mathbf{p}}}|{{\mathbf{p}}})\geq 0
implies
f^{\prime}_{{}_{\textrm{ETF}}}({{{\mathbf{p}}}})\geq 0
. Hence,
{{\mathbf{p}}}
is the stationary point of
f_{{}_{\textrm{ETF}}}({\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}})
and therefore the proposed algorithm converges to the stationary point of the ETF problem.

III-D

SQUAREM acceleration scheme

As discussed in Sec.
II
, the choice of surrogate function will dictate the convergence speed of the MM based algorithm. In our case, since the surrogate function is constructed through double majorization of the objective function, it may lead to slower convergence of the proposed algorithm. Squared Iterative Method (SQUAREM)
[
32
]
is based on Cauchy-Brazilai-Brownein method, to accelerate the convergence rate.
Let
F_{\rm{{MM}}}(\cdot)
denote the nonlinear fixed-point iteration map of the proposed algorithm. Then the MM updating scheme can be expressed as:

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}=F_{\rm{{MM}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})

(48)

The pseudocode of the proposed algorithm accelerated using SQUAREM acceleration scheme is shown in Algorithm. 2.

Algorithm 2: Proposed algorithm acceleration scheme via SQUAREM

Input
: Number of vectors in frame
N
and the dimension of each vector
d
.

Initialize
: Set
t=0
. Initialize
{{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{0}}

Repeat
:

1)
\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{1}=F_{MM}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})
,
\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{2}=F_{MM}(\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{1})

2)
{\mathbf{r}}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{1}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{2}-\bar{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{1}-{\mathbf{r}}

3)  Compute step length
\alpha=-\dfrac{\|{\mathbf{r}}\|_{2}}{\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{2}}

4)  Compute
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-2\alpha{\mathbf{r}}+\alpha^{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}
and normalize the frame.

5)  Modify the step length
\alpha
if
\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})>\underset{i,j=1,2,\cdots N\>(i\neq j)}{\rm maximize}\>\bar{f}_{ij}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1})
as shown in section 6 in
[
32
]
. Update
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}={\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-2\alpha{\mathbf{r}}+\alpha^{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}
and normalize the frame.

t\leftarrow t+1
,
until convergence

are reached. All the algorithms were made to run until the following criteria was met:

|\mu(\textbf{X}^{k})-\mu_{\textrm{CB}}|<10^{-5}

(50)

where
\mu_{\textrm{CB}}
is the composite bound (which is a tighter bound when compared to the Welch bound in the region
N>d^{2}
), or until certain maximum number of iterations was met. The composite bound in the case of complex frames is defined as:

\mu_{\textrm{CB}}=\begin{cases}\text{if }N\leq d^{2}:&\\
\sqrt{\dfrac{N-d}{d(N-1)}},&\\
\text{if }d^{2}<N\leq 2(d^{2}-1):&\\
{\rm{max}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{d}},\sqrt{\dfrac{2N-d^{2}-d}{(d+1)(N-d)}},1-2N^{-\dfrac{1}{d-1}}\right)&\\
\text{if }N>2(d^{2}-1):&\\
{\rm{max}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{2N-d^{2}-d}{(d+1)(N-d)}},1-2N^{-\dfrac{1}{d-1}}\right)&\end{cases}

In the case of real frames, composite bound is defined as:

\mu_{\textrm{CB}}={\rm{max}}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{N-d}{d(N-1)}},\sqrt{\dfrac{3N-d^{2}-2d}{(d+2)(N-d)}}\right)

and
{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}
algorithm. The mutual coherence value attained by the two best algorithms are comparable for small and medium dimension frames. {\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}
algorithm. We also calculated the percentage decrease in the mutual coherence value achieved by the proposed algorithm with respect to coherence value achieved by the
{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}
using the formula:

{\textrm{Mutual coherence decrease in}}\>\%=\left(\dfrac{\mu_{\textrm{ICBP}}-\mu_{\textrm{TELET}}}{\mu_{\textrm{ICBP}}}\right)\times 100

{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}

{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}
algorithm was found to be about
5
times computationally faster than the proposed algorithm. Similarly, in the case of real frames, for small and medium dimensions, the mutual coherence value attained by
{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{TELET}}

{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}

{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{TELET}}

{\textrm{Frame}}_{\textrm{ICBP}}

Compressed sensing application

As discussed in the introduction section, an optimal sensing matrix must be constructed such that its equivalent dictionary
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{{\rm{ED}}}={\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
has small mutual coherence and also by taking the sparse representation error into account.
Therefore, using the model proposed in
[
9
]
, we solve the following problem to construct an optimal sensing matrix:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}},{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}}{\rm minimize}\>\>\omega\|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}-{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\|_{F}^{2}+(1-\omega)\|{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\boldsymbol{E}}\|_{F}^{2}\end{array}

(52)

where
\omega
is the trade-off factor which takes value in
[0,1]
,
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}
is a target equivalent dictionary with properties of the ETF. The above problem is solved iteratively using the alternating minimization scheme wherein minimization is performed with respect to one matrix while keeping the other matrix fixed and vice-versa. At the
t^{th}
iteration, the sensing matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}^{t}
is fixed and the equivalent dictionary
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rm{ED-Target}}^{t+1}
is updated using the TELET algorithm shown in Algorithm 2. Next, using the updated equivalent dictionary the problem in (
52
) can be solved - which has a closed-form solution and can be found leveraging Theorem
2
in
[
9
]
.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed an iterative algorithm TELET based on the Majorization Minimization principle to construct equiangular tight frames. Since the proposed algorithm is developed using Majorization Minimization procedure, it enjoys nice properties such as monotonicity and guaranteed convergence to a stationary point of the ETF problem. We also apply TELET algorithm to construct optimized sensing matrix for compressed sensing systems. We show through numerical simulations that when compared to the state-of-the-art algorithms TELET can construct complex and real frames with very low mutual coherence value, especially for large frame dimensions. However, with respect to run time, when compared to state-of-the-art algorithm, it is found that TELET is computationally slower. We also compared the performance of the optimized sensing matrix obtained using TELET algorithm with the state-of-the-algorithms used in compressed sensing systems using both synthetic and real images and found that the proposed algorithm performs better in terms of reconstruction accuracy.

1. Proof of
\lambda_{\text{max}}({\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}_{ij})=d

Proof:

We first recall that:

{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}}_{ij}}=\text{vec}\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)\text{vec}^{H}\left(\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)^{H}\right)+\text{vec}\left(\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)^{H}\right)\text{vec}^{H}\left({{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}\right)

(55)

Notice that by construction of
{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}={\boldsymbol{S}}_{j}^{H}{\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}
, (where
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{l}=[{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times(l-1)},{\mathbf{I}}_{d},{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times(N-l)d}]
is the selection matrix), it will have
d
off-diagonal elements equal to one and zeros everywhere else. Let
{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}=\text{vec}({\mathbf{A}}_{ij})\text{vec}^{H}({{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}^{H}})={\boldsymbol{q}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{s}}_{ij}^{H}
. Then it is easy to see that:

\text{Tr}({\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}^{H})=\|{\boldsymbol{s}}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{q}}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}=d^{2}.

(56)

\text{Tr}({\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij})=0

(57)

Since
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{ij}
is the sum of two rank one matrices, its rank can be at most two. Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
be the two non-zero eigen values of
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{ij}
. Then using (
56
) and (
57
) we have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\rm{Tr}({\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}+{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}^{H})=0\end{array}

(58)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}=\rm{Tr}({\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}+{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}^{H})^{2}=2\rm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{ij}^{H}})=2d^{2}\end{array}

(59)

Using (
58
) and (
59
) we get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\left(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}\right)\right)=-d^{2}\end{array}

(60)

Then, we can find the maximum eigen value by solving the following equation:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\lambda^{2}-d^{2}=0\end{array}

(61)

whose solution is equal to
\lambda=\pm d
, choosing its maximum gives
\lambda_{max}({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{ij})=d

∎
2. Proof of
\lambda_{\text{max}}({{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij})=|c_{ij}|=|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{H}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}|

Proof:

To prove the above, we first define
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
matrices as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}=\end{array}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{ij}\\
c_{ij}^{*}&0\end{array}\right),\>\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times d}&c_{ij}{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}\\
c_{ij}^{*}{\mathbf{I}}_{d\times d}&{\boldsymbol{0}}_{d\times d}\end{array}\right)

One can easily see that the eigenvalues of
\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}
is equal to
\lambda_{\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}}=\pm|c_{ij}|
.
Next, the eigenvalues of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
can be obtained by solving the following equation (which we obtained by cofactor expansion):

{\lambda_{\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}}}=(-{\lambda}^{2}+|c_{ij}|^{2})^{d-1}\rm{det}(\bar{{\mathbf{A}}}_{ij}-\lambda{\mathbf{I}})=0

(62)

Hence, the maximum eigenvalue of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
will be equal to
|c_{ij}|
. Also, note that by inserting zero vector columns and rows between the blocks of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
wont change its maximum eigenvalue. We use these observations to prove that
\lambda_{\text{max}}({{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij})=|c_{ij}|
where
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{ij}={\mathbf{A}}_{ij}(c_{ij}^{*})+{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}^{H}(c_{ij})
. By construction, the matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{ij}
will be a block diagonal matrix with a main block equal to
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
(with or without the zero vectors inserted between its blocks) and the remaining main blocks equal to zero square matrices. Sometimes, depending on the value of
(i,j)
, the entire matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{ij}
will be equal to
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
with zero vectors inserted between the blocks. For the later case, as discussed earlier, the addition of zero vectors do not change the maximum eigenvalue of
\bar{{\boldsymbol{B}}}_{ij}
, hence the maximum eigenvalue of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{ij}=|c_{ij}|
. For the former case, we make use of the property that the eigenvalues of a block diagonal matrix will be the eigenvalues of its blocks which implies the maximum eigenvalue of
{\boldsymbol{B}}_{ij}=|c_{ij}|
.
∎
