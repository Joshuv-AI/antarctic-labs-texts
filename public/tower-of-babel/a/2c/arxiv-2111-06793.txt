On the half-space matching method for real wavenumber Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Anne-Sophie Bonnet-Ben Dhia, Simon N. Chandler-Wilde, Sonia Fliss

Abstract
The Half-Space Matching (HSM) method has recently been developed as a new method for the solution of 2D scattering problems with complex backgrounds, providing an alternative to Perfectly Matched Layers (PML) or other artificial boundary conditions. Based on half-plane representations for the solution, the scattering problem is rewritten as a system coupling (1) a standard finite element discretisation localised around the scatterer and (2) integral equations whose unknowns are traces of the solution on the boundaries of a finite number of overlapping half-planes contained in the domain. While satisfactory numerical results have been obtained for real wavenumbers, well-posedness and equivalence of this HSM formulation to the original scattering problem have been established only for complex wavenumbers. In the present paper we show, in the case of a homogeneous background, that the HSM formulation is equivalent to the original scattering problem also for real wavenumbers, and so is well-posed, provided the traces satisfy radiation conditions at infinity analogous to the standard Sommerfeld radiation condition. As a key component of our argument we show that, if the trace on the boundary of a half-plane satisfies our new radiation condition, then the corresponding solution to the half-plane Dirichlet problem satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition in a slightly smaller half-plane. We expect that this last result will be of independent interest, in particular in studies of rough surface scattering.

Introduction and the scattering problem

1.1
The HSM method

Recently a new method, called the Half-Space Matching (HSM) method, has been developed as an (exact) artificial boundary condition for two-dimensional time-harmonic scattering problems. This method is based on explicit or semi-explicit expressions for the outgoing solutions of radiation problems in half-planes, these expressions established by using Fourier, generalized Fourier, or Floquet transforms when the background is, respectively, homogeneous
[
6
,
5
]
(and possibly anisotropic
[
32
,
3
,
31
]
), stratified
[
27
]
, or periodic
[
17
,
18
]
. The domain exterior to a bounded region enclosing the scatterers is covered by a finite number
\mathcal{N}
of half-planes (at least three). The unknowns of the formulation are the traces
\varphi^{1},\ldots,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}
of the solution on the boundaries of these half-planes and the restriction of the solution to the bounded region. The system of equations which couples these unknowns is derived by writing compatibility conditions between the different representations of the solution. This coupled system includes second-kind integral equations on the infinite boundaries of the half-planes.

This new formulation is attractive and versatile as a method to truncate computational domains in problems of scattering by localized inhomogeneities in complex backgrounds, including backgrounds that may be different at infinity in different directions. It has been employed successfully in numerical implementations for various applications, like
optical waveguides (including cases with different stratifications in different parts of the background domain)
[
27
]
, or ultrasonic non-destructive testing (with an anisotropic elastic background
)

[
32
,
31
]
.

Up to now the theoretical and numerical analysis of the method has remained an open question in the challenging, and practically relevant, non-dissipative case when waves radiate out to infinity. But a rather complete analysis has been carried out in the simpler dissipative case, when the solution (and its traces) decay exponentially at infinity. In that case the analysis can be done using an
L^{2}
framework for the traces: the associated formulation has been shown to be of Fredholm type and well-posed in a number of cases where the background is homogeneous (but not necessarily isotropic)
[
6
,
5
]
, with the sesquilinear form of the weak formulation coercive plus compact, enabling the numerical analysis of the method
[
5
]
.
This analysis fails in the non-dissipative case, not least because of the slow decay at infinity of the solution which results in non-
L^{2}
traces.

1.2
Our main results

In this paper we address well-posedness of the HSM formulation in the non-dissipative case for the scalar Helmholtz equation when the background is homogeneous and isotropic, so that

(1)

outside some ball. (Here, assuming

1.3
The significance of our results

The main significance of Theorems
3.5
and
4.1
is that these are the first well-posedness results in the important non-dissipative case for the HSM method, a method which, as discussed above, has already proved effective for computing scattering by localised inhomogeneities in a range of complex backgrounds. Our theorems, challenging as they are to prove, are for the simplest case when the background is homogeneous and isotropic. However, we expect that these results and formulations will be an important stepping stone to more complex cases, and we discuss this further in concluding remarks to the paper.

The scattering problem and the structure of the paper

1

1

. The source term

is also assumed to be compactly supported, so that (

) holds outside some ball. We suppose that the wavenumber is such that

j\in\mathbb{Z}/\mathcal{N}\mathbb{Z}
,

U^{j}(\varphi^{j})=U^{j+1}(\varphi^{j+1})\quad\text{in}\;\Omega^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1},

(22)

and the function defined by

u=U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
in
\Omega^{j}
,
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
,
is the unique solution
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
of (
6
).

Proof.

To prove (
22
), let us set
v=U^{j}(\varphi^{j})-U^{j+1}(\varphi^{j+1})
. By definition of the half-plane representations
U^{j}
and
U^{j+1}
, it is clear that
v\in H^{1}(\Omega^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1})
and that
v
satisfies
\Delta v+k^{2}v=0
in
\Omega^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1}
. Moreover, the compatibility conditions (
20
) imply that
v
vanishes on the boundary of
\Omega^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1}
. Using that
\Im(k)\neq 0
, one deduces that
v=0
by uniqueness of the solution of the Dirichlet problem in
\Omega^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1}
. The rest of the proof is straightforward.

It has been shown in previous papers
[
6
,
5
]
that, for both mathematical analysis and computation, it is more convenient to consider the Half-Space Matching formulation in an
L^{2}
-framework, which means that
\varphi^{j}
is sought in
L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})
instead of
H^{1/2}(\Sigma^{j})
so that the formulation is

\begin{array}[]{c}\text{Find}\;(\varphi^{1},...,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}})\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{1})\times\ldots\times L^{2}(\Sigma^{{\mathcal{N}}})\;\text{such that for }j\in\mathbb{Z}/\mathcal{N}\mathbb{Z}\\[5.0pt]
\begin{array}[]{|ll}\varphi^{j}=D_{j-1,j}\,\varphi^{j-1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j-1},\\
\varphi^{j}=g|_{\Gamma^{j}},&\text{on}\;\Gamma^{j},\\
\varphi^{j}=D_{j+1,j}\,\varphi^{j+1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1}.\end{array}\end{array}

(23)

Let us emphasize that this makes sense since
D_{j,j\pm 1}\psi
is well-defined by (
19
) and (
14
) for all
\psi\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})
and the operators
D_{j,j\pm 1}
are in fact continuous from
L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})
to
L^{2}(\Omega^{j}\cap\Sigma^{j\pm 1})

[
6
]
.
It has been shown in
[
6
]
that problem (
23
) is of Fredholm type. More precisely, by rewriting it in an operator form, it is proven that the associated operator is Fredholm of index 0. This means that existence of a solution is equivalent to its uniqueness. We refer the reader to
[
6
]
for more detail. Here, we focus on the question of uniqueness. We need to prove that, for a solution of (
23
),
g=0
implies
\varphi^{j}=0
for all
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
. The sketch of the proof of this uniqueness result, which is much less straightforward than in the
H^{1/2}
framework, has been given in
[
6
]
. We give a detailed presentation of this proof in the following paragraph, in a form that will be directly used for our main result, which is uniqueness for the case of a real wavenumber
k
.

2.2
The uniqueness result in an
L^{2}
-framework

The difficulty in proving a uniqueness result for problem (
23
) comes from the fact that the half-plane solutions
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
(with
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
defined now by (
11
), (
13
), or (
14
)) are in general not in
H^{1}(\Omega^{j})
, assuming only that
\varphi^{j}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})
.
But we will take advantage of the following lemma which summarizes key properties of the half-plane solutions with
L^{2}
and/or
L^{\infty}
Dirichlet boundary data.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
\Im(k)\geq 0,\Re(k)>0
. Let
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})+L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

and define
u(\boldsymbol{x})
, for
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, by

u(\boldsymbol{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2}-t)\phi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

(24)

where
\mathcal{H}
is defined by (
15
).
Then the integral is well-defined as a Lebesgue integral for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
u\in{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
, and
\Delta u+k^{2}\,u=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Moreover, if
\phi
is continuous on an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, then
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\cup\gamma)
where
\gamma:=\{(0,t);\;t\in I\}
, and
u(0,t)=\phi(t),\;t\in I
. Finally, if
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Im(k)>0
, then

u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and there exists a constant
C_{\infty}>0
independent of
\phi
such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})}\leq C_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

(25)

whereas if
\Im(k)=0
then, for any
L>0
,
u\in L^{2}(\omega_{L})
where
\omega_{L}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+};\;x_{1}<L\}
, and there exists a constant
C_{L}>0
independent of
\phi
such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\omega_{L})}\leq C_{L}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(26)

Proof.

From asymptotics of the Hankel function
H^{(1)}_{1}
for large and small argument, it follows that, for every
k
with
\Im(k)\geq 0
,
\Re(k)>0
, there exists a
C>0
such that

|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2})|\leq C\frac{x_{1}}{|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2}}(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|^{1/2})e^{-\Im(k)|\boldsymbol{x}|}.

(27)

It follows that, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, the integral in (
24
) is well-defined as a Lebesgue integral for any
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})+L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
To see that
u\in{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
and satisfies the Helmholtz equation, it suffices to argue as in
[
9
, Theorem 3.2]
. If
\phi
is continuous on an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, to see that
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\cup\gamma)
and that
u(0,t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in I
, it is enough to show that
u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\cup\tilde{\gamma})
and that
u(0,t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in\tilde{I}
for every compact
\tilde{\gamma}:=\{(0,t);\;t\in\tilde{I}\}\subset\gamma
. So suppose
\tilde{I}\subset I
is compact and write
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
where the support of
\phi_{2}
does not intersect
\tilde{I}
and
\phi_{1}
is bounded and continuous. Correspondingly, we can split
u
as
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
, where
u_{j}
is defined by (
24
) with
\phi
replaced by
\phi_{j}
. It follows from the definition that
u_{2}\in{C}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\cup\tilde{\gamma})
with
u_{2}(0,t)=0
for
t\in\tilde{I}
, while
u_{1}\in{C}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}})
and
u_{1}(0,t)=\phi_{1}(t)
for
t\in\tilde{I}
by
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
, so that
u(0,t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in\tilde{I}
.
Concerning the
L^{2}
-estimates (
25
) and (
26
) let us recall that for
\psi_{1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\psi_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
the convolution product
\psi_{1}*\psi_{2}
belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with the estimate (Young’s convolution inequality)

\|\psi_{1}*\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\psi_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\|\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

For a given
x_{1}>0
, this implies that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}dx_{2}\leq\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2})|dx_{2}\right]^{2}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

From (
27
), we deduce that, for some
C^{\prime}>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2})|dx_{2}\leq C^{\prime}(1+x_{1})^{1/2}e^{-\Im(k)x_{1}},\quad x_{1}>0.

One easily obtains the estimates (
25
) for
\Im(k)>0
and (
26
) for
\Im(k)=0
.

Now to prove the uniqueness result, we need some results concerning
L^{2}
solutions of the homogeneous Dirichlet problem in a domain with corners. We gather in the next lemma the results that we need which are proved in
[
19
, Chapter 2]
(see Theorem 2.3.3 and the proof of Theorem 2.3.7).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
Q
be a bounded polygonal domain with
N
vertices denoted by
S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}
. Let us suppose that
Q
has
M<N
reentrant vertices that can be supposed to be
S_{1},\ldots,S_{M}
without loss of generality. Let
P:=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}\}
and suppose that
w\in{C}(\bar{Q}\setminus P)\cap{C}^{2}(Q)
satisfies
\Delta w=0
in
Q
and
w=0
on
\partial Q\setminus P
. Then, if
w\in L^{2}(Q)
, there exists
\tilde{w}\in H^{1}(Q)
and
M
complex constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{M}
such that

w(\boldsymbol{x})=\tilde{w}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\,r_{m}^{-\pi/\alpha_{m}}\sin(\pi\theta_{m}/\alpha_{m}),\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in Q,

where, for each
m
,
\alpha_{m}
is the interior angle of
Q
at
S_{m}
and
(r_{m},\theta_{m})
are the polar coordinates of
\boldsymbol{x}
whose origin is
S_{m}
such that
\theta_{m}
is the angle from one of the sides of
\partial Q
containing
S_{m}
.
In particular if
M=0
(
Q
is convex) then
w=0
.

The previous lemma will be used to prove that particular
L^{2}
solutions of the Helmholtz equations in unbounded domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions are in fact
H^{1}
in every bounded subset of the domain. To prove that they are
H^{1}
in the whole domain, we will resort to the following result, whose proof follows the arguments of
[
30
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an unbounded open set such that
\Pi_{R}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Pi;|\boldsymbol{x}|<R+1\}
is a Lipschitz domain for all sufficiently large
R>0
, and
v\in L^{2}(\Pi)
a function such that
v_{|B}\in H^{1}(B)
for all bounded open subsets
B
of
\Pi
. If
v
satisfies the Helmholtz equation
\Delta v+k^{2}v=0
in
\Pi
and its trace is zero on
\partial\Pi
, then
v\in H^{1}(\Pi)
.

Proof.

Let us define the truncation function
\chi_{R}(\boldsymbol{x}):=F_{R}(|\boldsymbol{x}|)
where
F_{R}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
is chosen such that
0\leq F_{R}\leq 1
,
F_{R}=1
on
[0,R]
,
F_{R}=0
on
[R+1,+\infty)
and there exists a constant
c>0
independent of
R
such that
|F_{R}^{\prime}|\leq c\sqrt{F_{R}}
(this can be achieved by defining
F_{R}
so that
F_{R}>0
on
(R,R+1)
and vanishes quadratically at
R+1
). One has for all
R>0

\int_{\Pi}(\Delta v+k^{2}v)\overline{v}\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}=0.

Since
v\in H^{1}(\Pi_{R};\Delta):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\Pi_{R});\Delta u\in L^{2}(\Pi_{R})\right\}

we can apply Green’s first identity in
\Pi_{R}
. Using that
v
vanishes on
\partial\Pi
, this yields

\int_{\Pi}|\nabla v|^{2}\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\leq k^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Pi)}+\left|\int_{\Pi}\overline{v}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\right|.

Since
|\nabla\chi_{R}|\leq c\sqrt{\chi_{R}}
, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\int_{\Pi}|\nabla v|^{2}\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\leq k^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Pi)}+c\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Pi)}\sqrt{\int_{\Pi}|\nabla v|^{2}\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}},

from which we deduce the existence of a constant
C>0
independent of
R
such that

\int_{\Pi}|\nabla v|^{2}\chi_{R}\,d\boldsymbol{x}\leq C\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Pi)}.

This being true for all
R
, we can conclude that
v\in H^{1}(\Pi)
.

We are now able to prove the following well-posedness result.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\Re(k)>0
and
\Im(k)>0

Suppose

1.

Step 1. As in the proof of Proposition

To conclude that

The HSM method for real wavenumber

3.1
The Half-Space Matching formulation

In this section we consider the Dirichlet problem of the previous section, but now with a real wavenumber

. We seek
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
such that

\begin{cases}-\Delta u-k^{2}\,u=0\;\text{in}\;\Omega,\\[4.0pt]

(28)

for a given

\lim_{R\to+\infty}R^{1/2}\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\partial u(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial r}-{\mathrm{i}}ku(\boldsymbol{x})\right|;|\boldsymbol{x}|=R\right\}=0.

(29)

It is well known that this problem has a unique solution.

Let us now give a Half-Space Matching formulation of problem (
28
-
29
).
If we set as before
\varphi^{j}:=u\big|_{\Sigma^{j}}
for

, one can easily check by a Green’s identity argument that the half-plane representations (

), with

given by (

) or (

)), still hold.
A main difference with the case of a complex wavenumber is that now the solution of (

) decays only slowly, like

as

, so that we cannot expect that
\varphi^{j}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})
. However
\varphi^{j}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})+L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{j})
so that, for
x^{j}_{1}>a^{j}
, the integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1}^{j}-a^{j},x_{2}^{j}-y_{2}^{j})\,\varphi^{j}(a^{j},y_{2}^{j})\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}^{j}

is still well-defined by Lemma

. Then one can check, exactly as in the case of a complex wavenumber, that the
\varphi^{j}
’s satisfy the following Half-Space Matching equations:

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{|ll}\varphi^{j}=D_{j-1,j}\,\varphi^{j-1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j-1},\\
\varphi^{j}=g|_{\Gamma^{j}},&\text{on}\;\Gamma^{j},\\
\varphi^{j}=D_{j+1,j}\,\varphi^{j+1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1},\end{array}\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}/\mathcal{N}\mathbb{Z}\end{array}

where

(31)

with
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
given by (
11
).
The idea is again, for numerical purposes, to replace problem (
28
-
29
) by the system of equations (
30
). But in contrast to the case with
\Im(k)>0
, it is not clear in which space the functions
\varphi^{j}
’s must be sought to make (

) uniquely solvable. In what follows, we will provide a framework which ensures existence and uniqueness of the solution of (

). More precisely, in this framework, the unique solution of (

) is given by
\varphi^{j}:=u\big|_{\Sigma^{j}}
, where
u
is the unique solution of (
28
-
29
).

Let us first recall, as just remarked above, that
U^{j}(\varphi)
is well-defined when
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})+L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{j})
by Lemma
2.3
, and, indeed, we will require that the solution of (

) satisfies
\varphi^{j}\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})+L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,{\mathcal{N}}
.
But, as discussed in §
1.2
, our uniqueness result requires an additional property:
analogously to requiring that

satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition when

is real, we will require, for

, that
\varphi^{j}
has the following asymptotics playing the role of a radiation condition:

\varphi^{j}(a^{j},t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{+}^{j}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|t|}|t|^{-1/2}\left(1+O(|t|^{-1})\right),&\mbox{as}\quad t\to+\infty,\\
c_{-}^{j}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|t|}|t|^{-1/2}\left(1+O(|t|^{-1})\right),&\mbox{as}\quad t\to-\infty,\end{array}\right.

(32)

for some constants

. This obviously holds when
\varphi^{j}:=u\big|_{\Sigma^{j}}
, where
u
is the unique solution of (
28
-
29
), since, as discussed in §

,
u
satisfies (
3
).

The main result of the present paper is Theorem

stating that the radiation condition (

) is strong enough to ensure uniqueness for (

) for

real. We will prove it by following the same steps as in the case of a complex wavenumber, proving first the compatibility of half-plane representations in the overlaps between half-planes, and then uniqueness for the reconstructed solution in the exterior domain
\Omega
.
To establish these results, we will use classical uniqueness results for the Helmholtz equation on unbounded domains when the solution satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity. A preliminary question is: can we prove, using (
32
), that the half-plane representations (
14
) satisfy a Sommerfeld condition at infinity? We will establish, in the next subsection, a slightly weaker result which will be sufficient for the proof of Theorem
3.5
.

3.2
Properties of the half-plane solution

We consider in this subsection the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}:=(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.
Let us prove a preliminary lemma for the solution of the Dirichlet problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
given by (
24
), in the case that the Dirichlet data decays sufficiently rapidly at infinity.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies, for some
p>1
,

|\phi(t)|\leq(1+|t|)^{-p},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(33)

and define
u(\boldsymbol{x})
, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, by

u(\boldsymbol{x}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2}-t)\phi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(34)

Then there exists a constant
C(p)>0
such that

|u(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(p)(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

(35)

Proof.

We will use the bound (
27
),
where
C>0
, there and throughout the remainder of the proof, denotes a constant independent of
\boldsymbol{x}
(and also independent of
p
), not necessarily the same at each occurrence.
It will be convenient for the proof to denote by
u[\phi]
the function
u
defined by (
34
).
Then defining
\chi_{x_{2}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, for
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, to be the characteristic function of the interval

I_{x_{2}}:=\left\{t\in\mathbb{R};|x_{2}-t|\leq(1+|x_{2}|)/2\right\},

we have
u[\phi]=u[\chi_{x_{2}}\phi]+u[(1-\chi_{x_{2}})\phi]
. We will establish the bound (
35
) for each term of the sum (cf. the argument between (4.16) and (4.17) in
[
1
]
).

For the first term we have

|u[\chi_{x_{2}}\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2}-t)|\chi_{x_{2}}(t)|\phi(t)|\mathrm{d}t,

which gives

|u[\chi_{x_{2}}\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq 2\|\chi_{x_{2}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{0}^{\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},s)|\mathrm{d}s.

(36)

Now note that if
|t|<|x_{2}|/3
and
|x_{2}|\geq 3
, then
|t-x_{2}|>2|x_{2}|/3\geq(1+|x_{2}|)/2
, so that
\chi_{x_{2}}(t)=0
. Thus, for
|x_{2}|\geq 3
, (
33
) implies that

\|\chi_{x_{2}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\sup_{|t|\geq|x_{2}|/3}|\phi(t)|\leq(1+|x_{2}|/3)^{-p},

while also
\|\chi_{x_{2}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 1
, for every
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, so that

\|\chi_{x_{2}}\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C(1+|x_{2}|)^{-p},\quad x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(37)

Moreover, it follows from (
27
) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},s)|\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cx_{1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2}}\left[(x_{1}^{2}+s^{2})^{-1}+(x_{1}^{2}+s^{2})^{-3/4}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\int_{0}^{\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2x_{1}}}\left[(1+t^{2})^{-1}+x_{1}^{1/2}(1+t^{2})^{-3/4}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle C(1+x_{1})^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2x_{1}}}(1+t^{2})^{-3/4}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle C(1+x_{1})^{1/2}\min\left(1,\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2x_{1}}\right).

Combining this with (
36
) and (
37
), we get the following estimate:

|u[\chi_{x_{2}}\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(1+|x_{2}|)^{-p}(1+x_{1})^{1/2}\min\left(1,\frac{1+|x_{2}|}{2x_{1}}\right),

which gives the expected bound (recalling that
p>1
)

|u[\chi_{x_{2}}\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}.

To see that the same bound holds for
u[(1-\chi_{x_{2}})\phi]
, we note that

|u[(1-\chi_{x_{2}})\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1},x_{2}-t)(1-\chi_{x_{2}})(t)|\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t.

Observing that, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{x_{2}}
,

(x_{1}^{2}+(x_{2}-t)^{2})^{1/2}\geq(x_{1}^{2}+(1+|x_{2}|)^{2}/4)^{1/2}\geq C(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|),

and using (
27
) and (
33
), it follows that, for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,

|u[(1-\chi_{x_{2}})\phi](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C\frac{x_{1}}{(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{3/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi(t)|\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{C}{p-1}(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2}.

(38)

It follows from standard interior elliptic regularity results that if
u\in{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})
satisfies

\Delta u+k^{2}u=0\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},

then for every
\varepsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
with
x_{1}>\varepsilon
, so that
\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{2};|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|\leq\varepsilon\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
:

|\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C\>\frac{1+k^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon}\max_{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|\leq\varepsilon}|u(\boldsymbol{y})|

(39)

where
C
is an absolute constant (see, e.g.
[
13
, Lemma 2.7]
).
Combining this estimate with the previous lemma, we have immediately the following corollary:

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that
\phi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies (
33
) for some
p>1
. Then there exists a constant
C(p)>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,
the function
u
defined by (
34
) satisfies

|\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(p)\>\frac{1+k^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{\varepsilon}(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad x_{1}>\varepsilon,\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(40)

Now, using the previous results, we will establish a radiation condition for
u
defined by (
34
) when its trace
\varphi
satisfies itself a radiation condition.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
\phi\in{C}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\phi(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{+}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|t|}|t|^{-1/2}+O(|t|^{-p}),&\mbox{as}\quad t\to+\infty,\\
c_{-}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|t|}|t|^{-1/2}+O(|t|^{-p}),&\mbox{as}\quad t\to-\infty,\end{array}\right.

(41)

for some constants
c_{+},c_{-}\in\mathbb{C}
and
p>1
. Define
u
by (
34
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. Then the following properties hold:

(i)

there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|u(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+};

(42)

(ii)

for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{R\to+\infty}R^{1/2}\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\partial u(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial r}-{\mathrm{i}}ku(\boldsymbol{x})\right|;x_{1}>\varepsilon\mbox{ and }|\boldsymbol{x}|=R\right\}=0.

(43)

Proof.

We use the same notation
u[\phi]
for (
34
) as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. We prove this proposition by writing

as
\phi=\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)}
, and correspondingly writing the integral (
34

\phi^{(1)}
is the trace on the boundary of a solution of the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
that clearly satisfies (
42
) and (
43
), and
\phi^{(2)}(t)
decays sufficiently rapidly as
|t|\to\infty
so that, thanks to Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
3.2
,
u[\phi^{(2)}]
also satisfies (
42
) and (
43
).

In more detail, from the asymptotics of the Hankel function
H_{0}^{(1)}
it follows that, for some non-zero
c^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
,

\Phi(\boldsymbol{x},(0,t))=c^{*}\,{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|t-x_{2}|}\,|t|^{-1/2}+O(|t|^{-3/2})\quad\mbox{as}\quad|t|\to\infty,

(44)

for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Pick
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},z_{2}),\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}=(z^{\prime}_{1},z^{\prime}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
k(z_{2}-z^{\prime}_{2})/\pi\not\in\mathbb{Z}
, and with
z_{1},z^{\prime}_{1}<0
. Define
\phi^{(1)}\in C(\mathbb{R})
by

\phi^{(1)}(t):=(c/c^{*})\Phi(\boldsymbol{z},(0,t))+(c^{\prime}/c^{*})\Phi(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime},(0,t))\;\text{ for}\;t\in\mathbb{R}.

(45)

Then
\phi^{(1)}(t)
has the same leading asymptotic behaviour as
\phi(t)
as

provided

c{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}kz_{2}}+c^{\prime}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}kz^{\prime}_{2}}=c_{+}\quad\mbox{and}\quad c{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}kz_{2}}+c^{\prime}{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}kz^{\prime}_{2}}=c_{-}.

(46)

Let us choose
c
and
c^{\prime}
so that these two linear constraints hold; this is possible since
k(z_{2}-z^{\prime}_{2})/\pi\not\in\mathbb{Z}
implies that the determinant
\exp({\mathrm{i}}k(z^{\prime}_{2}-z_{2}))-\exp({\mathrm{i}}k(z_{2}-z^{\prime}_{2})=2{\mathrm{i}}\sin(k(z_{2}-z^{\prime}_{2}))\neq 0
. Then it is clear from an application of Green’s second identity that,
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
u[\phi^{(1)}](\boldsymbol{x})=(c/c^{*})\Phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{x})+(c^{\prime}/c^{*})\Phi(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x})
(see, e.g., the proof that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(v)
\Rightarrow
(i) in
[

).
Hence

satisfies items (i) and (ii).

It remains to show that the same is true for

where

. It follows from (

, where

|\phi^{(2)}(t)|\leq c_{0}(1+|t|)^{-p^{\prime}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(47)

Item (i) is therefore a direct consequence of Lemma
3.1
. To see that

also satisfies the radiation condition (ii), choose

and,
for each

, let

be the characteristic function of the interval

.
Now, by (

|(1-\chi_{A}(t))\phi^{(2)}(t)|\leq c_{0}(1+|t|)^{-q}(1+A)^{q-p^{\prime}}

so that, by (
35
) and (

depending only on

such that

|u[(1-\chi_{A})\phi^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(q)c_{0}(1+A)^{q-p^{\prime}}(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+},

and from Corollary
3.2
, for all
\varepsilon>0

C(q,\varepsilon)>0
depending only on

and
\varepsilon
such that

|\nabla u[(1-\chi_{A})\phi^{(2)}](\boldsymbol{x})|\leq C(q,\varepsilon)c_{0}(1+A)^{q-p^{\prime}}(1+|\boldsymbol{x}|)^{-1/2},\quad x_{1}>\varepsilon,\,x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us set for any function

defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

M_{R,\varepsilon}(v):=R^{1/2}\sup\left\{\left|\frac{\partial v(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial r}-{\mathrm{i}}kv(\boldsymbol{x})\right|;x_{1}>\varepsilon\mbox{ and }|\boldsymbol{x}|=R\right\}.

It follows from the estimates above that, for all
R>0
,

M_{R,\varepsilon}(u[(1-\chi_{A})\phi^{(2)}])\leq\max(C(q),C(q,\varepsilon))c_{0}(1+A)^{q-p^{\prime}}.

Thus, for all
\varepsilon>0
, given

such that

for all

. But also, for each

Suppose that

Proof.

Again, we write

The second step of the proof is simpler. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition

4
General configurations

Let us now recall how to extend the HSM formulation to the general problem presented in the introduction and extend the previous uniqueness result to this new formulation. More precisely, for a real wavenumber
k>0
we consider, as discussed in §
1.4
, the isotropic Helmholtz equation (
5
) in a Lipschitz domain
\Omega
,
where
\rho-1\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
are compactly supported and
\Omega
is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
minus a set of Lipschitz obstacles which are supposed to lie in a bounded domain. To ensure uniqueness we impose some constraint on
\rho
, e.g. that
\rho
is real-valued or that
\Im(\rho)\geq 0
. As announced in §
1.4
the problem we consider is to seek
u\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
that satisfies (
5
), the Sommerfeld radiation condition (
2
), and a boundary condition on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
. To be specific we will impose the homogeneous Neumann condition

\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=0\quad\mbox{on}\quad\Gamma,

(51)

but the modifications to use other boundary conditions and/or include inhomogeneous terms are straightforward.

It is well known that this problem is uniquely solvable; in particular, with one of the above constraints on
\rho
, uniqueness follows by a Green’s identity, the Rellich lemma, and unique continuation arguments (e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 8.7]
).

Let
\mathcal{O}
be the interior of
a convex polygon containing the supports of all these perturbations. The half-space matching method has been mainly presented for the case where
\mathcal{O}
is a square (see
[
6
]
). But, whereas in the problem in §
3
the polygon
{\mathcal{O}}
is given and the number of trace unknowns is imposed by the number of sides of
{\mathcal{O}}
, here we have freedom to choose the polygon
\mathcal{O}
. In particular, choosing
\mathcal{O}
to be a triangle has the advantage of minimising the number of trace unknowns. In the sequel, we suppose that
\mathcal{O}
is a polygon with
\mathcal{N}
edges and we adopt the same notations as introduced in §
2
for the edges
\Gamma^{1},\ldots,\Gamma^{\mathcal{N}}
, the angles between the edges, the
\mathcal{N}
overlapping half-planes
\Omega^{1},\ldots\Omega^{\mathcal{N}}
, their boundaries
\Sigma^{1},\ldots,\Sigma^{\mathcal{N}}
, and their associated local coordinate systems.

Let us now introduce a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega_{b}\subset\Omega
containing
\overline{\mathcal{O}}\cap\Omega
such that
\partial\Omega_{b}\setminus\Gamma
is connected (for examples see Figure
4
), and a partition
\Gamma_{b}^{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{b}^{\mathcal{N}}
of
\partial\Omega_{b}\setminus\Gamma
such that, for all
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
,
\Gamma_{b}^{j}
is connected and
\Gamma_{b}^{j}\subset\Omega^{j}
with
\overline{\Gamma_{b}^{j}}\cap\Sigma^{j}=\emptyset
. There is, of course, no unique choice for such a partition, but the uniqueness result given in Theorem
4.1
below implies that the solution of the HSM formulation that we will write down is independent of the choice of the partition.

The unknowns of the HSM formulation are the traces
\varphi^{1},...,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}
of the solution
u
on the infinite lines
\Sigma^{1},...,\Sigma^{\mathcal{N}}
, and the restriction
u_{b}:=u|_{\Omega^{b}}
to the bounded domain
\Omega_{b}
. Let us derive the equations linking
\varphi^{1},...,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}}
and
u_{b}
. On the one hand, one can show as in the case of scattering by a polygon (see §
3.1
) that the
\varphi^{j}
’s satisfy the equations

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{|ll}\varphi^{j}=D_{j-1,j}\,\varphi^{j-1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j-1},\\
\varphi^{j}=D_{j+1,j}\,\varphi^{j+1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1},\end{array}\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}/\mathcal{N}\mathbb{Z},\end{array}

(52)

where the operators
D_{j,j\pm 1}
are defined in (
31
). Compared to the problem of scattering by a polygon, the conditions for the traces on
\Gamma^{j}
(linked to the given data
g
in the case of scattering by a polygon) have to be replaced by an equality between
\varphi^{j}
and
u_{b}
on
\Gamma^{j}
:

\varphi^{j}=u_{b}\;\text{on}\;\Gamma^{j},\;j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}.

As in the case of scattering by a polygon, since the wavenumber is real, the traces
\varphi^{j}
are not in
L^{2}
but they decay like
|y_{2}^{j}|^{-1/2}
at infinity and we will require, as in §
3.1
, that, for
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
,
\varphi^{j}
satisfies the asymptotic condition (
32
) at
\pm\infty
.

On the other hand, we can derive a variational formulation for
u_{b}
in
\Omega_{b}
. Since
-\Delta u_{b}-k^{2}\rho u_{b}=f
in
\Omega_{b}
,
f
is supported in
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{O}\cap\Omega
, and
u
satisfies (
51
), the following Green’s identity holds for all
v_{b}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{b})
, where
n
is the normal pointing out of
\Omega_{b}
:

\int_{\Omega_{b}}\left(\nabla u_{b}\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{b}}-k^{2}\rho u_{b}\overline{v_{b}}\right)-\int_{\partial\Omega_{b}\setminus\Gamma}\frac{\partial u_{b}}{\partial n}\overline{v_{b}}\,=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}f\overline{v_{b}}.

(53)

The last idea is to replace the normal derivative on each part
\Gamma_{b}^{j}
of
\partial\Omega_{b}\setminus\Gamma
by an integral representation as a function of
\varphi^{j}
. Indeed, since, for all
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
,
\Gamma_{b}^{j}\subset\Omega^{j}
, we must have

\frac{\partial u_{b}}{\partial n}-{\mathrm{i}}ku_{b}=\frac{\partial U^{j}(\varphi^{j})}{\partial n}-{\mathrm{i}}kU^{j}(\varphi^{j})\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{j}_{b},\;j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}},

(54)

where
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
is the restriction of the solution
u
to the half-plane
\Omega^{j}
and is expressed in terms of
\varphi^{j}
in (
11
). Let us emphasize that our choice of imposing equality of Robin traces instead of normal derivatives is so that later we have uniqueness for the formulation for all
k>0
. Note also that, since
\overline{\Gamma_{b}^{j}}\cap{\Sigma^{j}}=\emptyset
, the Robin trace of
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
on
\Gamma^{j}_{b}
is
{C}^{\infty}
(see Lemma
2.3
). To express (
54
) succinctly we introduce the Dirichlet-to-Robin (DtR) operators
\Lambda^{j}
, given by

\Lambda^{j}\varphi:=\left(\frac{\partial U^{j}(\varphi)}{\partial n}-{\mathrm{i}}kU^{j}(\varphi)\right)\Big|_{\Gamma^{j}_{b}}.

(55)

We note, by Lemma
2.3
, that
\Lambda^{j}\varphi
is well-defined (at least) for all functions
\varphi\in L^{2}(\Sigma^{j})+L^{\infty}(\Sigma^{j})
.
One can use (
11
) to write
\Lambda^{j}\varphi
explicitly, for
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
, as

\Lambda^{j}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}^{j})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\nabla\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1}^{j}-a^{j},x_{2}^{j}-y_{2}^{j})\cdot n(x_{1}^{j},x_{2}^{j})\right.\\
\left.-{\mathrm{i}}k\mathcal{H}(k;x_{1}^{j}-a^{j},x_{2}^{j}-y_{2}^{j})\right)\,\varphi(a^{j},y_{2}^{j})\,\mathrm{d}y_{2}^{j},\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}^{j}=(x_{1}^{j},x_{2}^{j})\in\Gamma^{j}_{b}.

With this notation, the equality (
54
) can be written as

\frac{\partial u_{b}}{\partial n}-{\mathrm{i}}ku_{b}=\Lambda^{j}\varphi^{j}\mbox{ on }\Gamma^{j}_{b},\;j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}.

(56)

Our complete formulation reads as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{|lcl}\varphi^{j}=D_{j-1,j}\,\varphi^{j-1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j-1},\\
\varphi^{j}=u_{b}\;\text{on}\;\Gamma^{j},\\
\varphi^{j}=D_{j+1,j}\,\varphi^{j+1},&\text{on}\;\Sigma^{j}\cap\Omega^{j+1},\\
\end{array}\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}/\mathcal{N}\mathbb{Z},\\[20.0pt]
\forall v_{b}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{b}),\hfill\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{b}}\left(\nabla u_{b}\cdot\overline{\nabla v_{b}}-k^{2}\rho u_{b}\overline{v_{b}}\right)-{\mathrm{i}}k\sum_{j=0}^{3}\int_{\Gamma^{j}_{b}}u_{b}\overline{v_{b}}-\sum_{j=0}^{3}\int_{\Gamma^{j}_{b}}\Lambda^{j}\varphi^{j}\overline{v_{b}}=\int_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}f\overline{v_{b}},\end{array}

(57)

where the operators
D_{j,j\pm 1}
are defined in (
31
), and the operators
\Lambda^{j}
in (
55
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
k>0
. There exists, for each
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{O}}^{\prime})
, a unique solution
(\varphi^{1},...,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}},u_{b})\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Sigma^{1})\times\ldots\times L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Sigma^{\mathcal{N}})\times H^{1}(\Omega_{b})
of (

) such that, for all
j=1,...,{\mathcal{N}}
,
\varphi^{j}
satisfies the radiation condition (
32
).

Existence holds by the construction above of a solution to (

,
2

,

Proposition 4.2

Suppose

and let
(\varphi^{1},...,\varphi^{\mathcal{N}},u_{b})\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Sigma^{1})\times\ldots\times L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Sigma^{\mathcal{N}})\times H^{1}(\Omega_{b})

5
Conclusion

The objective of this paper was to prove the well-posedness of the HSM formulation applied to the Helmholtz equation (
5
) in the case of a real wavenumber
k>0
. This objective has been achieved: we have proved
well-posedness provided we impose the radiation condition (
32
) at infinity on the trace-unknowns
\varphi^{j}
of the HSM equations, this radiation condition analogous to the standard Sommerfeld radiation condition for the original boundary value problem. Let us recall that the results of the present paper also complete a proof of well-posedness of the complex-scaled HSM formulation presented in
[
4
, §5]
.

An open question is whether the radiation condition (
32
) on the trace-unknowns, while natural, is necessary for uniqueness. The answer is not at all clear to us. But one piece of evidence that suggests that this radiation condition may not in fact be needed is that one achieves accurate results in numerical experiments by simply truncating the trace unknowns, setting, for each
j
,
\varphi^{j}=0
outside some sufficiently large finite section of
\Sigma^{j}
, not making any use of the radiation condition (
32
) (see
[
6
, Fig. 7]
).

Let us finish by mentioning that our main results should be extendable to more complex configurations with obstacles extending to infinity. For instance, one might consider the general configuration of §
4
but add a screen that is a semi-infinite line
\gamma^{1}
, choosing the half-planes
\Omega^{j}
so that
\gamma^{1}
is orthogonal to
\Sigma^{1}
and
\gamma^{1}\cap\Omega^{j}=\emptyset
, for all
j\neq 1
(see the left hand part of Figure
5
). In this case
\Omega^{1}\setminus\gamma^{1}
has two connected components that are quarter-planes. If
\gamma^{1}
is sound soft or sound hard, i.e. the solution satisfies a Dirichlet or Neumann homogeneous boundary condition on
\gamma^{1}
, one can derive an expression for
U^{1}(\varphi^{1})
, the solution in
\Omega^{1}
given Dirichlet data
\varphi^{1}
on
\Sigma^{1}
, by solving separately in each quarter-plane, combining formula (
13
) with reflection arguments.

More interesting is the case where two parallel semi-infinite lines
\gamma^{j}_{\pm}
are introduced, so that now, for some
j
,
\Omega^{j}
has three connected components, two of them quarter-planes and one of them a semi-infinite strip which is a waveguide (this is the case for
\Omega^{1}
and
\Omega^{3}
in the right hand part of Figure
5
). One can derive an expression for
U^{j}(\varphi^{j})
in each quarter-plane as above, and an expression in the waveguide by solving as a modal series expansion.

For each of these configurations, and for many other variations on these geometries, we can write down HSM formulations, and we expect that one should be able to prove well-posedness of the HSM formulation and equivalence with other formulations by adapting the results proved in the present paper.
