The test of exponentiality based on the mean residual life function revisited
Bruno Ebner

Abstract
We revisit the family of goodness-of-fit tests for exponentiality based on the mean residual life time proposed by Baringhaus & Henze, 2008 . We motivate the test statistic by a characterisation of Shanbhag, 1970 and provide an alternative representation, which leads to simple and short proofs for the known theory and an easy to access covariance structure of the limiting Gaussian process under the null hypothesis. Explicit formulas for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the operator associated with the limit covariance are given using results on weighted Brownian bridges. In addition we derive further asymptotic theory under fixed alternatives as well as approximate Bahadur efficiencies, which provide an insight into the choice of the tuning parameter with regard to the power performance of the tests.

Introduction

We revisit the family of tests for exponentiality based on the residual life time as proposed by
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
, and we provide further theoretical insight into the asymptotic behaviour of the test under alternatives. The problem of interest is testing the assumption that data are distributed according to the exponential distribution with unknown scale parameter. To be precise, let
X
be a positive random variable and we write shorthand
X\sim\mbox{Exp}(\lambda)
,
\lambda>0
, if
X
follows an exponential distribution with scale parameter
\lambda
, hence the density is given by

f(x,\lambda)=\lambda\exp(-\lambda x),\quad x>0.

(1)

Note that
X\sim

\mbox{Exp}(\lambda)
if, and only if,
X/\lambda\sim

\mbox{Exp}(1)
, which shows that the exponential distribution belongs to the scale family of distributions, for a detailed discussion see
Johnson et al., 1994
, Chapter 19. In the following we denote the family of exponential distributions by
\mathcal{E}:=\{\mbox{Exp}(\lambda):\,\lambda>0\}
. Let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\dotso
be positive independent and identically distributed (iid.) random variables with distribution
\mathbb{P}^{X}
defined on an underlying probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. We test the composite hypothesis

H_{0}:\;\mathbb{P}^{X}\in\mathcal{E}

(2)

against general alternatives, based on the sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

This testing problem has been studied extensively in the literature, and it is of ongoing interest, see
Cuparić et al., 2021
;
Cuparić et al., 2020
;
Jiménez-Gamero et al., 2020
;
Milošević & Obradović, 2016b
;
Xiong et al., 2022
;
Villaseñor & González-Estrada, 2020
for some recent related publications,
Ascher, 1990
;
Henze & Meintanis, 2005
;
Spurrier, 1984
for surveys on the topic, and
Allison et al., 2017
;
Ossai et al., 2022
for reviews of testing procedures as well as of extensive competitive Monte Carlo power studies. The focus of this article is on the procedure proposed in
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
. That paper studies a family of tests of exponentiality based on the mean residual life function with test statistic

G_{n,a}=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\min(Y_{j},z)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}\leq z\}\right]^{2}\exp(-az)\,\mbox{d}z,\quad a>0.

Here,
Y_{j}=X_{j}/\overline{X}_{n}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, is the scaled data, where
\overline{X}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}
. This family of tests is an extension of the test in
Baringhaus & Henze, 2000
insofar as the exponential weight function in the original test statistic is replaced with a more flexible weight function
w(z)=e^{-az}
,
z>0
, which depends on some so-called tuning parameter
a>-1
. In
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
the authors provide the limiting null distribution of the test statistics
G_{n,a}
and a simulation study including simulated critical values, as well as a power study for different values of the tuning parameter
a
. Further insight on the behaviour of the tests under alternatives is hitherto missing. We fill this gap in the literature.

We start our investigation by providing an alternative representation of
G_{n,a}
motivated by a characterisation of the exponential law due to
Shanbhag, 1970
, which is obviously related to the mean residual life function.
Shanbhag, 1970

states that for a random variable
X
with
\mathbb{P}(X>y)>0
for
y>0
, we have
X\sim\mbox{Exp}(\lambda)
if and only if

\mathbb{E}[X|X>y]=y+\frac{1}{\lambda},\quad\mbox{for all }y>0,\lambda>0.

Direct calculations show that under the same condition this characterisation can be restated in the following way:
X\sim\mbox{Exp}(\lambda)
if and only if

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X-y-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\mathbf{1}\{X>y\}\right]=0,\quad\mbox{for all }y>0,\lambda>0.

(3)

In view of the scale invariance of the family
\mathcal{E}
, we consider the scaled data
Y_{j}=X_{j}/\overline{X}_{n}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, and propose the weighted
L^{2}
-type test statistic (fixing w.l.o.g.
\lambda=1
in the characterisation)

T_{n,a}=T_{n,a}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}>y\}\right|^{2}\exp(-ay)\,\mbox{d}y,

(4)

which depends on the tuning parameter
a\geq 0
. Note that
T_{n,a}
is scale invariant, i.e. invariant w.r.t. transformations of the form
x\mapsto bx
,
b>0
, since it only depends on the scaled data
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
. Scale invariance is indeed a desirable property, since the family
\mathcal{E}
is closed under such transformations.
Putting
x\wedge y=\min(x,y)
for real numbers
x,y
and using
n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}=1
, some algebra shows

T_{n,a}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{a}(|Y_{j}-Y_{k}|-1)e^{-aY_{j}\wedge Y_{k}}+\frac{1}{a^{2}}(Y_{j}+Y_{k}-2(Y_{j}\wedge Y_{k}+1))e^{-aY_{j}\wedge Y_{k}}-\frac{2}{a^{3}}\left(e^{-aY_{j}\wedge Y_{k}}-1\right),

(5)

for each
a>0
, as well as

T_{n,0}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{3}(Y_{j}\wedge Y_{k})^{3}-\frac{Y_{j}+Y_{k}-2}{2}(Y_{j}\wedge Y_{k})^{2}+(Y_{j}-1)(Y_{k}-1)(Y_{j}\wedge Y_{k}).

An efficient implementation of (
5
) in the statistical computing language
R
, see
R Core Team, 2021
, can be found in Appendix
B
.

Remark 1.1
.

1.

Some algebra shows that
T_{n,a}\equiv G_{n,a}
holds for all
a>-1
, hence both test statistics are identical.

2.

Representation (
4
) leads to a new compact and formula of the covariance kernel of the limiting Gaussian process, which admits direct conclusions for the limiting distribution of the test statistic.

3.

Obviously the restriction
a\geq 0
can be generalised to negative values. In this article we mostly focus on the positive half axis, since the power results in the simulation study of
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
suggest that negative tuning parameters lead to low power of the tests. The authors suggest to use the tuning parameter
1\leq a\leq 2
as a generally good choice.

4.

Note that the test statistic
T_{n,0}
is related to the test of exponentiality based on the integrated distribution function proposed in
Klar, 2001
, Section 2. The main difference in the definitions of the tests lies in the fact, that
\mathbb{P}(X>y)
in (
3
) is estimated by and not replaced with the theoretical known value under
H_{0}
. This difference has an impact on the limiting distribution and the power of the tests as is shown in the sequel.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we provide a simple and direct proof of the asymptotic limiting distribution of
T_{n,a}
under the null hypothesis and provide the first four cumulants of the limiting null distribution as well as explicit formulas of the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions associated with the integral operator induced by the covariance structure of the limiting Gaussian process. These findings corroborate the results of
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
in a very short and direct way.
In Section
3
we provide new limit results under fixed alternatives as well as a proof of the consistency of the testing procedures. Local approximate Bahadur efficiencies are deduced and interpreted in connection to empirical power results in Section
4
. We conclude the article by pointing out open problems for further research in Section
5
and by an Appendix containing explicit formulas and some source code.

2
Limiting distribution under the null hypothesis

In this section we assume that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
is an iid. sequence of random variables with
X_{1}\sim\mbox{Exp}(1)
. Due to the
L^{2}
-structure of the test statistics, a convenient setting –is in dependence of the constant
a\geq 0
– the separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}_{a}=L^{2}([0,\infty),\mathcal{B},\exp(-at){\rm d}t)
of (equivalence classes of) measurable functions
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

satisfying
\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(t)|^{2}\exp(-at)\,{\rm d}t<\infty
. Here,
\mathcal{B}
stands for the Borel sigma-field on
[0,\infty)
. The scalar product and the norm in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
will be denoted by

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)g(t)\,\exp(-at)\,{\rm d}t,\quad\|f\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=\langle f,f\rangle_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{1/2},\quad f,g\in\mathbb{H}_{a},

respectively.

A change of variable shows that

T_{n,a}=\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{2}(y)\exp(-ay/\overline{X}_{n})\,\mbox{d}y,

where

Z_{n}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-y-\overline{X}_{n})\mathbf{1}\{X_{j}>y\},\quad y>0.

Define the auxiliary process

Z_{n}^{\ast}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{X_{j}>y\}-(X_{j}-1)\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>y),\quad y>0.

Notice that
Z_{n}^{*}
is a sum of centred iid. random variables, and we have
\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}^{*}(s)Z_{1}^{*}(t)]=K_{0}(s,t)
,
s,t>0
, where
K_{0}
defined below. By the central limit theorem in Hilbert spaces there exists a centred Gaussian process
Z
in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
having covariance kernel

K_{0}(s,t)=\exp(-s\vee t)-\exp(-(s+t)),\quad s,t>0,

(6)

such that
Z_{n}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z
in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
as
n\to\infty
. Here and in what follows, we write
s\vee t=\max(s,t)
, and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution. Likewise
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
will denote convergence in probability. Note that
K_{0}
is an alternative representation of the limiting covariance kernel
\rho
in (7) of
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Under the standing assumptions, there exists a centred Gaussian process
Z
in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
with covariance kernel
K_{0}
defined in (
6
), such that

T_{n,a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\|Z\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2},\quad\mbox{as}\,n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

We first consider the case
a=0
. Note that

\displaystyle\|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-1)\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{1}\{X_{k}>y\}-\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>y)\right)\right|^{2}\mbox{d}y.

By the strong law of large numbers
n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}-1)
converges to 0 a.s., and the central limit theorem in
\mathbb{H}_{0}
implies that the second term is a tight sequence. Hence,
\|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}^{2}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, and
T_{n,0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\|Z\|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}^{2}
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
follows from
Z_{n}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z
in
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, Slutski’s lemma and the continuous mapping theorem.

For the case
a>0
define

\widetilde{T}_{n,a}=\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{2}(y)\exp(-ay)\,\mbox{d}y.

A first-order Taylor expansion yields

\exp(-ay/\overline{X}_{n})=\exp(-ay)+\frac{ay}{\Delta_{n}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{ay}{\Delta_{n}}\right)\left(\overline{X}_{n}-1\right),

(7)

where
\Delta_{n}\in(\min(\overline{X}_{n},1),\max(\overline{X}_{n},1))
.
From the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
7
) we obtain

\displaystyle|T_{n,a}-\widetilde{T}_{n,a}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{2}(y)|\exp(-ay/\overline{X}_{n})-\exp(-ay)|\mbox{d}y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}\int_{0}^{\infty}Z_{n}^{2}(y)\mbox{d}y\int_{0}^{\infty}|\exp(-ay/\overline{X}_{n})-\exp(-ay)|\mbox{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}T_{n,0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ay}{\Delta_{n}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{ay}{\Delta_{n}}\right)\left|\overline{X}_{n}-1\right|\mbox{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\overline{X}_{n}^{-3}T_{n,0}\left|\overline{X}_{n}-1\right|.

The strong law of large numbers implies
\overline{X}_{n}\to 1
a.s., and since
T_{n,0}
is a tight sequence (see case
a=0
),
|T_{n,a}-\widetilde{T}_{n,a}|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
follows. By the central limit theorem in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
there exists a centred Gaussian process
\widetilde{Z}
with covariance kernel
K_{0}
such that
Z_{n}^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\widetilde{Z}
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
and the same reasoning as for the case
a=0
yields
\|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Invoking Slutzki’s lemma and the continuous mapping theorem, we obtain
\widetilde{T}_{n,a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\|\widetilde{Z}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2}
from which the claim follows.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

By direct evaluation of integrals the first two cumulants of the distribution of
\|Z\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2}
are

\kappa_{1,a}=\mathbb{E}\|Z\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(t,t)\exp(-at)\,\mbox{d}t=\frac{1}{(a+1)(a+2)}

and

\kappa_{2,a}=\mathbb{V}(\|Z\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2})=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}^{2}(s,t)\exp(-a(s+t))\,\mbox{d}s\mbox{d}t=\frac{2}{(a^{2}+3a+2)(2a+3)(a+2)},

where
\mathbb{V}(\cdot)
denotes the variance. Following the methodology in
Henze, 1990
;
Stephens, 1976
, we calculate the third and fourth cumulants according to

\kappa_{j,a}=2^{j-1}(j-1)!\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{j}(t,t)\exp(-at)\,\mbox{d}t,

where
K_{j}(s,t)
, the
j^{\text{th}}
iterate of
K_{0}(s,t)
, is given by

\displaystyle K_{j}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{j-1}(s,u)K_{0}(u,t)\exp(-au)\,\mbox{d}u,\quad j\geq 2,

\displaystyle K_{1}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K_{0}(s,t).

Direct calculation shows that

\kappa_{3,a}=\frac{16}{(1+a)(2a+3)(a+2)^{3}(3a+4)}

and

\kappa_{4,a}=\frac{48(11a+16)}{(3a+4)(2a+3)^{2}(a+2)^{4}(1+a)(4a+5)}.

These formulas are very useful in order to fit a Pearson system of distributions to approximate the critical values of the test statistic
T_{n,a}
. The approximated quantiles of the limiting distribution are found in Table
1
and source code is provided in Appendix
B
. A comparison with Table 1 in
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
shows that the approximation of the critical values is a reasonable fit to the empirical critical values for
a\geq 0
.

From the theory of Gaussian processes it is well known (see
Shorack & Wellner, 1986
, p. 206) that for each
a>0
an orthogonal decomposition of the process
Z
yields
\|Z\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(a)N_{j}^{2},
where
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
are iid. standard normal, and
\lambda_{1}(a),\lambda_{2}(a),\ldots
is the decreasing sequence of positive eigenvalues of the integral operator
\mathcal{K}_{a}:\mathbb{H}_{a}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}_{a},\,f\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{a}f(\cdot)=\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(\cdot,t)f(t)\exp(-at)\mbox{d}t.

To calculate the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(a)
,
j=1,2,\ldots
, of
\mathcal{K}_{a}
, one has to solve the homogeneous Fredholm integral equation of the second kind

\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(x,t)f(t)\exp(-at)\mbox{d}t=\lambda f(x),\quad x>0,

see, for example,
Kac & Siegert, 1947
. Usually these problems are very hard to solve explicitly and numerical or Monte Carlo simulation techniques are used to obtain approximations of
\lambda_{j}(a)
, see Section 5 in
Ebner & Henze, 2021
for a stochastic approximation method or the method presented in
Božin et al., 2020
.

In the following we give explicit formulas for general tuning parameters
a
. Firstly, note that
K_{0}
admits the representation

\displaystyle K_{0}(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min(\exp(-s),\exp(-t))-\exp(-s)\exp(-t),\quad s,t>0.

(8)

Let
a=1
. Using the alternative representation in (
8
) together with
x=\exp(-s)
and a substitution (
y=\exp(-t)
), we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(x,t)f(t)\exp(-t)\mbox{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}(\min(x,y)-xy)f(-\log(y))\mbox{d}y.

Since
\mathbb{K}(x,y)=\min(x,y)-xy
,
x,y\in[0,1]
, is the covariance kernel of the Brownian bridge
B(t)
, say, the eigenvalue problem is solved in this case, see e.g.
Anderson & Darling, 1952
. We conclude that the eigenvalues and corresponding eigenfunctions are

\lambda_{k}(1)=\frac{1}{(k\pi)^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f_{k,1}(t)=\sin\left(k\pi\exp(-t)\right),\quad t>0.

By analogy the general case
a>0
leads to

\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{0}(x,t)f(t)\exp(-at)\mbox{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}(\min(x,y)-xy)f(-\log(y))y^{a-1}\mbox{d}y,

which is connected to the eigenvalue problem of the weighted Brownian bridge
t^{a-1}B(t)
. This problem is solved in Theorem 1.4 in
Deheuvels & Martynov, 2003
, which states the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions explicitly. Let
\nu=(a+1)^{-1}
(note that there is a typographical error in
Deheuvels & Martynov, 2003
) and denote by
J_{\nu}
the Bessel functions of the first kind and by
0<z_{\nu,1}<z_{\nu,2}<\ldots
the ascending sequence of zeros of
J_{\nu}
, for details on the zeros of Bessel functions see
Watson, 1995
, Chapter XV. We have for
k=1,2,\ldots

\lambda_{k}(a)=\left(\frac{2\nu}{z_{\nu,k}}\right)^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad f_{k,a}(t)=\frac{J_{\nu}\left(z_{\nu,k}\exp(-\frac{t}{2\nu})\right)}{\sqrt{\nu}J_{\nu-1}(z_{\nu,k})}\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{\nu}-1\right)\frac{t}{2}\right),\quad t>0.

(9)

By using the identities in Remark
2.2
(compare to Corollary 1.3 in
Deheuvels & Martynov, 2003
) we see that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{j}_{k}(a)=\kappa_{j,a}/(2^{j-1}(j-1)!)
,
j=1,2,3,4
. The largest twenty eigenvalues are given in Table
2
, and for the sake of completeness, we approximated the scaled cumulants by the sum of the respective powers of the first 100 eigenvalues. We see that for the mean, there is still some difference in the first few digits, which is explained by the low speed of convergence to 0 of the eigenvalues. Interestingly, the eigenvalues in Table
2
in each row are strictly decreasing, which is explained by the fact that for fixed
k
the function
z_{\nu,k}/\nu
is increasing for
\nu\downarrow 0
, see
Olver et al., 2010
, p. 236.

.

for the case

a>-1
by solving a related differential equation in

. From these results we see that (
9
) also holds in these cases.

3
Limiting distribution under fixed alternatives and consistency

In this section we assume that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of iid. random variables with cumulative distribution function
F
,
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}|^{3}]<\infty
. The moment condition is motivated by the scale invariance of
T_{n,a}
and hence there is no loss of generality compared to fixed alternatives with existing third moment.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\frac{T_{n,a}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{2}(y)\exp(-ay)\,{\rm d}y=\Delta_{a},\quad a\geq 0,\quad\mbox{as}\;n\to\infty,

where
z(y)=\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{1}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{X_{1}>y\}\right]
,
y>0
.

Proof.

Note that by the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
we have
n^{-1}\|Z_{n}-Z_{n}^{*}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}^{2}\to 0
a.s. Since by the strong law of large numbers in Hilbert spaces
n^{-1/2}Z_{n}^{*}\to z
a.s. in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
the claim follows by the continuous mapping theorem and Slutski’s lemma.
∎

Under the assumption
X_{1}\sim\mbox{Exp}(1)
, the characterisation in (
3
) gives
z\equiv 0
and hence
\Delta_{a}=0
. Since
\Delta_{a}
equals 0 if and only if
X_{1}
follows an exponential distribution, we conclude that the tests
T_{n,a}
are consistent against each alternative distribution with existing first moment.

Example 3.2
.

In this example we give explicit formulas for
\Delta_{a}
for different
\Gamma(\beta,\beta)
distributions. Note that for an suitable alternative we need
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
to be satisfied. Direct evaluations show that

1.

if
X\sim\Gamma(2,2)
, then
\Delta_{a}=2/(a+4)^{3}
,

2.

if
X\sim\Gamma(3,3)
, then
\Delta_{a}=2(a^{2}+30a+252)/(a+6)^{5}
, and

3.

if
X\sim\Gamma(4,4)
, then
\Delta_{a}=2\left({a}^{4}+56\,{a}^{3}+1344\,{a}^{2}+16640\,a+94720\right)/\left(a+8\right)^{7}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Note that higher values of
\Delta_{a}
in dependence of the tuning parameter
a
do not imply greater power of the test against this alternative. To visualise this behaviour, we performed a Monte Carlo (MC) simulation study with different
\Gamma(\beta,\beta)
distributions and plot the empirical rejection rate of the tests in Figure
1
. Note that under
\Gamma(1,1)=\mbox{Exp}(1)
, hence under the null hypothesis, the type I error is well calibrated below the significance level of 0.05 (here visualised by a solid line).

Figure 1:

T_{n,a}
for different tuning parameters
a
. Each dot represents the empirical rejection rate of the test at a significance level of 0.05 under the stated
\Gamma(\beta,\beta)
distribution (MC simulation with 10000 replications) .

In the following we derive the limiting distribution of the test statistic under fixed alternatives under the stated assumptions in the beginning of the section.
Define

W_{n}(y)=Z_{n}(y)-\sqrt{n}z(y),\quad W_{n}^{*}(y)=Z_{n}^{*}(y)-\sqrt{n}z(y),\quad y>0.

It is easy to see that
\|W_{n}-W_{n}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Under the standing assumptions there exists a centred Gaussian process
W
in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
with covariance kernel

\displaystyle K(s,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(s\lor t)+st\mathbb{P}(X>s\lor t)-(s+t)\Psi_{1}(s\lor t)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}(X>s)\left(\Psi_{2}(t)-t\Psi_{1}(t)\right)-\mathbb{P}(X>t)\left(\Psi_{2}(s)-s\Psi_{1}(s)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{V}(X)\mathbb{P}(X>s)\mathbb{P}(X>t)-z(s)z(t),\quad s,t>0,

where
s\lor t=\max(s,t)
, and
\Psi_{\ell}(s)=\mathbb{E}[(X-1)^{\ell}\mathbf{1}\{X>s\}]
,
s>0
, for
\ell=1,2
. In
\mathbb{H}_{a}
we have

W_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}W\quad\mbox{as}\,n\rightarrow\infty.

Proof.

Since
\|W_{n}-W_{n}^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
the limit process is determined by the limit behaviour of
W_{n}^{*}
. We have

W_{n}^{*}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big[(X_{j}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{X_{j}>y\}-(X_{j}-1)\mathbb{P}(X_{j}>y)-z(y)\big],\quad y\geq 0,

and
\mathbb{E}[W_{n}^{*}(y)]=0
,
y>0
, since
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=1
. Hence
W_{n}^{*}
is a sum of iid. elements in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
and thus converges as
n\to\infty
to a centred Gaussian process
W
with covariance kernel
K(s,t)=\mathbb{E}[W_{1}^{*}(s)W_{1}^{*}(t)]
,
s,t\geq 0
, in
\mathbb{H}_{a}
by the central limit theorem in Hilbert spaces. The formula for
K
is obtained by tedious but straightforward calculations.
∎

By the results of Lemma
3.4
and Theorem 1 in
Baringhaus et al., 2017
, we thus have

\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{T_{n,a}}{n}-\Delta_{a}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{N}(0,\sigma_{a}^{2}),

(10)

where

\sigma_{a}^{2}=4\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K(x,y)z(x)z(y)\exp(-a(x+y))\,\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y.

For some families of distributions,
\Delta_{a}
and
\sigma_{a}^{2}
can be calculated explicitly for fixed tuning parameters see Example
3.2
.

In general, however, we have to find a consistent estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}^{2}
of
\sigma_{a}^{2}
. In this spirit, we replace the probabilities by relative frequencies, the variance by the empirical variance
s_{y}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-1)^{2}
, and the expectation by empirical counterparts

\Psi_{n,\ell}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-1)^{\ell}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}>s\},\quad\ell=1,2,\quad\mbox{and}\quad z_{n}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-s-1)\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}>s\},\quad s>0,

based on the scaled random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
. Denoting by
K_{n}
the resulting estimator of
K
obtained from plugging-in the empirical counterparts into the formula of
K
, the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}^{2}
of
\sigma_{a}^{2}
is

\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}^{2}=4\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}K_{n}(x,y)z_{n}(x)z_{n}(y)\exp(-a(x+y))\,\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y.

Writing
\widehat{\tau}_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-1)^{2}
, and

\displaystyle S_{1n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Psi_{n,2}(s\lor t)z_{n}(s)z_{n}(t)\exp(-a(s+t))\,\mbox{d}t\mbox{d}s,

\displaystyle S_{2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}st\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}>s\lor t\}z_{n}(s)z_{n}(t)\exp(-a(s+t))\,\mbox{d}t\mbox{d}s

\displaystyle S_{3n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}(s+t)\Psi_{n,1}(s\lor t)z_{n}(s)z_{n}(t)\exp(-a(s+t))\,\mbox{d}t\mbox{d}s,

\displaystyle S_{4n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\Psi_{n,2}(t)-t\Psi_{n,1}(t)\right)z_{n}(t)\exp(-at)\mbox{d}t

\displaystyle S_{5n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}\{Y_{j}>t\}z_{n}(t)\exp(-at)\,\mbox{d}t,

\displaystyle S_{6n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{n}^{2}(t)\exp(-at)\,\mbox{d}t,

we have

\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}^{2}=4\left[S_{1n}+S_{2n}-S_{3n}-2S_{4n}S_{5n}+\widehat{\tau}_{n}^{2}S_{5n}^{2}-S_{6n}^{2}\right].

Using the functions
\upsilon_{\ell,a}(x,y)
,
x,y>0
,
\ell=1,2,3
, from Appendix
A
, we have

\displaystyle S_{1n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-1)^{2}\upsilon_{1,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k})\upsilon_{1,a}(Y_{j},Y_{l}),

\displaystyle S_{2n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{3}}\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{n}\upsilon_{2,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k})\upsilon_{2,a}(Y_{j},Y_{l}),

\displaystyle S_{3n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n^{3}}\sum_{j,k,l=1}^{n}(Y_{j}-1)\upsilon_{2,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k})\upsilon_{1,a}(Y_{j},Y_{l}),

\displaystyle S_{4n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\left[(Y_{j}-1)^{2}\upsilon_{1,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k})-(Y_{j}-1)\upsilon_{2,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k})\right],

\displaystyle S_{5n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\upsilon_{1,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{6n}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\upsilon_{3,a}(Y_{j},Y_{k}).

Since
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{n,a}
is a consistent sequence of estimators of
\sigma^{2}_{a}
for each fixed tuning parameter
a>0
, we have (in the spirit of Corollary 1 in
Baringhaus et al., 2017
) under the stated assumptions at the beginning of this section

\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}}\left(\frac{T_{n,a}}{n}-\Delta_{a}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{N}(0,1)\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(11)

This result has immediate consequences, see Section 3 of
Baringhaus et al., 2017
, which are detailed in the following subsections.

3.2
Neighbourhood-of-model validation

A clear drawback in the field of goodness-of-fit testing is that if a level-
\alpha
-test does not lead to the rejection of the hypothesis
H_{0}
, the conclusion that
H_{0}
is ’confirmed’ is generally wrong. To overcome this problem, the results of (
10
) lead to a so-called ’neighbourhood-of-model validation’, see Subsection 3.3 in
Baringhaus et al., 2017
. In this spirit one could see
\Delta_{a}
as some sort of distance to the null hypothesis. If we argue to ’tolerate’ a given value
\widetilde{\Delta}
, we can consider the testing problem

H_{\widetilde{\Delta}}:\Delta_{a}(F)\geq\widetilde{\Delta}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad K_{\widetilde{\Delta}}:\Delta_{a}(F)<\widetilde{\Delta}.

From (
10
) we obtain an asymptotic level-
\alpha
-test by rejecting
H_{\widetilde{\Delta}}
whenever

T_{n,a}\leq n\widetilde{\Delta}-\sqrt{n}\widehat{\sigma}_{n,a}\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha).

To prove this statement follow the reasoning in
Baringhaus et al., 2017
, subsection 3.3.

4
Asymptotic Bahadur efficiencies

In this section we only consider the case that
a>0
. We start the investigation by giving an alternative representation of
\Delta_{a}
in Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{3}]<\infty
. If
a>0
, we have

\frac{T_{n,a}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Delta_{a}=\frac{2}{a^{3}}-\frac{1}{a^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left((1-|Y_{1}-Y_{2}|)(a^{2}+a)+a+2\right)\exp(-aY_{1}\wedge Y_{2})\right],

and if
a=0
, we have

\frac{T_{n,0}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Delta_{0}=\mathbb{E}\left[(Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2})(Y_{1}-1)(Y_{2}-1)+(Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2})^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}(Y_{1}+Y_{2})\right)+\frac{1}{3}(Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2})^{3}\right],

where
Y_{j}=X_{j}/\mu
,
j=1,2
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, applied to the iid. random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]=1
, we have

\frac{T_{n}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{\mathbb{P}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Delta_{a}=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{2}(y)\exp(-ay)\,\mbox{d}y,

where
z(y)=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{1}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}>y\}]
,
y>0
. Since
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are independent, it follows that

z^{2}(y)=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{1}-y-1)(Y_{2}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2}>y\}].

Fubini’s Theorem yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[(Y_{1}-y-1)(Y_{2}-y-1)\mathbf{1}\{Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2}>y\}]\exp(-ay)\,\mbox{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2}}(Y_{1}-y-1)(Y_{2}-y-1)\exp(-ay)\,\mbox{d}y\right].

Straightforward integration and some algebra concludes the proof in both cases.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

An alternative way of proving Lemma
4.1
is to start by the representation (
5
) and to use symmetry arguments, the law of large numbers and Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, i.e. to adapt the lines of proof of Theorem 3.1 in
Ebner & Henze, 2021
.

A useful tool for a theoretical comparison of the performance of two tests is the asymptotic relative Bahadur efficiency. This concept has been used throughout the literature on exponentiality tests, see
Cuparić et al., 2019
;
Jovanović et al., 2015
;
Milošević, 2016
;
Milošević & Obradović, 2016a
;
Volkova & Nikitin, 2015
, and for more details on the theory we refer to
Bahadur, 1971
;
Nikitin, 1995
. For a brief introduction to the concept, see Section 5 of
Jiménez-Gamero et al., 2020
. In this spirit and using the same notations, we calculate the local approximate Bahadur slope of
T_{n,a}
. Assume that
0\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
, where
\Theta
is an open parameter space, and
\mathcal{G}=\{G(x;\vartheta):\vartheta\in\Theta\}
is a family of distribution functions with density
g(x;\vartheta)
, such that
\vartheta=0
corresponds to the standard exponential density
g(x;0)=\exp(-x)
,
x>0
, and for each
\vartheta>0
the density
g(x;\vartheta)
is not a density corresponding to an exponential distribution in
\mathcal{E}
. Moreover, we assume that the regularity assumptions WD in
Nikitin & Peaucelle, 2004
are satisfied.
In the following assume that
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent identical copies of
X
following the distribution with density
g(\cdot;\vartheta)
with existing expectation
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu(\vartheta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}xg(x;\vartheta)\mbox{d}x
. Then Lemma
4.1
yields

\frac{T_{n,a}}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta}}}{{\longrightarrow}}b_{T_{a}}(\vartheta),

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}_{\vartheta}}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in probability under the true parameter
\vartheta
, and

b_{T_{a}}(\vartheta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{a}(x,y;\vartheta)g(x;\vartheta)g(y;\vartheta)\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y

with

a^{3}h_{a}(x,y;\vartheta)=2-\left(\left(1-\frac{|x-y|}{\mu(\vartheta)}\right)(a^{2}+a)+a+2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{a}{\mu(\vartheta)}x\wedge y\right),\quad x,y>0,\;a>0,

and

h_{0}(x,y;\vartheta)=\frac{x\wedge y}{\mu(\vartheta)}\left(\frac{x}{\mu(\vartheta)}-1\right)\left(\frac{y}{\mu(\vartheta)}-1\right)+\left(\frac{x\wedge y}{\mu(\vartheta)}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{x+y}{2\mu(\vartheta)}\right)+\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{x\wedge y}{\mu(\vartheta)}\right)^{3},\quad x,y>0.

Note that
\mu(0)=1
,
b_{T_{a}}(0)=0
, and after some algebra we have
b_{T_{a}}^{\prime}(0)=0
. Here and in the following, all derivatives are calculated w.r.t.
\vartheta
. Writing
\mu_{1}=\mu^{\prime}(0)
, the same reasoning as in Appendix B of
Cuparić et al., 2021
gives

\displaystyle b_{T_{a}}^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{a}(x,y;0)g^{\prime}(x;0)g^{\prime}(y;0)\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y+4\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{a}^{\prime}(x,y;0)g(x;0)g^{\prime}(y;0)\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y

\displaystyle+\mu_{1}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{a}^{\prime\prime}(x,y;0)g(x;0)g(y;0)\mbox{d}x\mbox{d}y.

Expanding
b_{T_{a}}(\vartheta)
into a Taylor series around
\vartheta_{0}=0
, we obtain

b_{T_{a}}(\vartheta)=\frac{b_{T_{a}}^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2}\vartheta^{2}+O(\vartheta^{3}),\quad\mbox{as}\;\vartheta\rightarrow 0.

From Section
2
we know that the limiting distribution of
T_{n}
is
\|Z\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}_{a}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(a)N_{j}^{2},
where
N_{1},N_{2},\ldots
are iid. standard normal, and
(\lambda_{j}(a))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is the decreasing sequence of positive eigenvalues of the integral operator
\mathcal{K}_{a}
. Using the result in
Zolotarev, 1961
, the logarithmic tail behaviour of the limiting distribution of
\widetilde{T}_{n,a}=\sqrt{T_{n,a}}
is

\log\left(1-F_{\widetilde{T}_{a}}(x)\right)=-\frac{x^{2}}{2\lambda_{1}(a)}+O(x^{2}),\quad x\rightarrow\infty.

Since the limit in probability of
\widetilde{T}_{n,a}/\sqrt{n}
is
\sqrt{b_{T_{a}}(\vartheta)}
, the approximate local Bahadur slope is given by

c^{*}_{T_{a}}(\vartheta)=(\lambda_{1}(a))^{-1}b_{T_{a}}^{\prime\prime}(0)\vartheta^{2}+o(\vartheta^{2}),\quad\mbox{as}\,\vartheta\rightarrow 0.

We compare the approximate Bahadur slope to the double Kullback-Leibler distance, also called Kullback-Leibler information numbers see
Nikitin & Tchirina, 1996
,

KL(g)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(g^{\prime}(x;0))^{2}}{g(x;0)}\mbox{d}x-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\prime}(x;0)\,\mbox{d}x\right)^{2},

where
G(x;\vartheta)=\int_{0}^{x}g(t,\vartheta)\mbox{d}t
is the cumulative distribution function of
g(\cdot;\vartheta)
. It is well known that the Kullback-Leibler information numbers are an upper bound for Bahadur efficiencies, see
Bahadur, 1971
;
Raghavachari, 1970
. Hence we compute the approximate Bahadur efficiencies, given by

\mbox{eff}(g)=\frac{b_{T_{a}}^{\prime\prime}(0)}{2\lambda_{1}(a)KL(g)}.

These are equivalent to the comparison of the local approximate Bahadur slopes of
T_{n,a}
and the likelihood ratio test as in
Cuparić et al., 2021

5
Conclusions and Outlook

We revisited the family of tests of exponentiality of
Baringhaus & Henze, 2008
and provided new insight into the asymptotic behaviour of the tests under fixed alternatives as well as local Bahadur efficiencies. These results facilitate the comparison of the performances to other well known tests of exponentiality. As a result we visualised the dependence of the power of the tests on the choice of the tuning parameter
a
. This effect might be controlled by implementing a data dependent choice of the tuning parameter due to
Tenreiro, 2019
. We leave this investigation open for further research.

We finish the article by pointing out other related open questions.
As stated in Remark
1.1
, the family of tests based on the integrated distribution function presented in
Klar, 2001
is very close to the test statistic
T_{n,a}
. For this family of tests corresponding theoretical results as in Section
3
and
4
are missing. There is little hope to solve the eigenvalue problem in this case, but results regarding Bahadur efficiency may be obtained by approximating the largest eigenvalue numerically. Another open question is due to the conservative behaviour of the confidence interval
I_{n,a}
in Subsection
3.1
, which suggests that an improvement in view of the length of the confidence interval might be found. We leave this investigation open for further research.

In
Smuts et al., 2019
the authors propose a test of exponentiality based on a characterisation of the exponential law by a conditional second moment equation involving the hazard rate. Theoretical insight into this family of tests are hitherto missing, so it would be interesting to see corresponding results, since the tests based on the mean residual life function are tests based on a characterisation by a first conditional moment equation.

The following code can be used for approximation of the critical values of the test statistic by a Pearson system of distributions using the

package
PearsonDS
, see

.

#Cumulants from Remark 2.2
kappa_1<-function(a) {return(1/((a+1)*(a+2)))}
kappa_2<-function(a) {return(2/((a^2+3*a+2)*(2*a+3)*(a+2)))}
kappa_3<-function(a) {return(16/((a+1)*(a+2)^3*(2*a+3)*(3*a+4)))}
kappa_4<-function(a) {return(48*(11*a+16)/((a+1)*(a+2)^4*(2*a+3)^2*(3*a+4)*(4*a+5)))}

#The function provides the approximation of the 1-alpha quantile of the limiting
#distribution for a tuning parameter a
cv.T<-function(alpha,a)
{
require(PearsonDS)
kum=c(kappa_1(a),kappa_2(a),kappa_3(a),kappa_4(a))
mom.a=c(kum[1:2],kum[3]*kum[2]^(-3/2),3+kum[4]*kum[2]^(-2))
return(qpearson(1-alpha,moments=mom.a))
}
