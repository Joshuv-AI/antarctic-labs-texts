A Stochastic Gradient Descent Theorem and the Back-Propagation Algorithm
Hao Wu

Abstract
. We establish a convergence theorem for a certain type of stochastic gradient descent, which leads to a convergent variant of the back-propagation algorithm.

1.
A Convergence Theorem for Stochastic Gradient Descent

1.1.
Statement of the theorem

We present elements of
R^{l}
by column vectors and denote by
\|\mathbf{z}\|=\sqrt{z^{T}z}
the standard norm on
\mathbb{R}^{l}
. For any
0<r\leq\infty
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
, we denote by
B^{l}_{r}(\mathbf{z})
the open ball of radius
r
centered on
\mathbf{z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{l}
. In particular,
B^{l}_{\infty}(\mathbf{z})=\mathbb{R}^{l}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix a
0<\rho\leq\infty
. Assume that:

(1)

\mu
is a probability measure on
B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
.

(2)

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function satisfying:

(a)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the function
f(\mathbf{x},\ast)
is
\mu
-measurable on
B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
.

(b)

For any
\mathbf{y}\in B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
, the function
f(\ast,\mathbf{y})
has continuous firs-order partial derivatives over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(c)

For any
r>0
,

•

the function
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is bounded on
B^{n}_{r}(\mathbf{0})\times B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
,

•

\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|
is bounded on
B^{n}_{r}(\mathbf{0})\times B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
, where
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is the gradient vector of
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
with respect to
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

•

\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|
is Lipschitz with respect to
\mathbf{x}
on
B^{n}_{r}(\mathbf{0})\times B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
. That is, there is an
M>0
such that
\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y})\|\leq M\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in B^{n}_{r}(\mathbf{0})
and
\mathbf{y}\in B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
.

(d)

There is an
R_{0}>0
such that
\mathbf{x}^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{x}\geq R_{0}
and
\mathbf{y}\in B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
.

(3)

\{a_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of positive numbers satisfying
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}<\infty
. Define
A=\sup\{a_{k}~|~k\geq 0\}
.

(4)

•

\mathbf{x}_{0}
is any fixed element of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

•

R_{1}=\max\{\sqrt{\|\mathbf{x}_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}},\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}+2AR_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}}\}
,

•

\Phi=\sup\{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|~|~(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in B^{n}_{R_{1}}(\mathbf{0})\times B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})\}
.

(5)

\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of independent random variables with identical probability distribution taking values in
B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})
, where the probability distribution of each
\mathbf{y}_{k}
is
\mu
.

(6)

For any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
F(\mathbf{x})=\int_{B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})}f(\mathbf{x},\ast)d\mu
.

Fix a positive number
\varphi
satisfying
\varphi\geq\Phi
. Define a sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
of random variables by

(1.1)

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\text{ for }k\geq 0.

Then

•

\{F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\}
converges almost surely to a finite number,

•

\{\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\}
converges almost surely to
\mathbf{0}
,

•

any limit point of
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
is almost surely a stationary point of the function
F
.

Before moving onto the proof of Theorem
1.1
, it is worth pointing out that the most restrictive assumption about the function
f
in this theorem is Assumption (
2d
). All the other assumptions about
f
are fairly non-restrictive. Assumption (
2d
) was essentially used by Bottou in
[
2
, Section 5]
. However, to deduce the convergence of the stochastic gradient descent, he imposed additional restrictive assumptions in
[
2
, Subsection 5.1]
. In the definition of the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
above, we divide the learning rate
a_{k}
by the (potentially large) constant
\varphi
, which eliminates the need for Bottou’s additional assumptions at the cost of having smaller steps in the gradient descent. So, comparing to Bottou’s results, Theorem
1.1
applies to a more general class of functions but leads to a potentially slower convergence.

1.2.
Proof of the theorem

The key step in the proof Theorem
1.1
is to prove that
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}
is bounded, which turns out to be fairly simple. The rest of the proof is somewhat more technical, but mainly consists of arguments similar to those already appeared in literature on stochastic approximation such as
[
1
,
3
,
4
]
.

Lemma 1.2
.

\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|<R_{1}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

We claim that
\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\leq R_{1}^{2}
, which implies the lemma. We prove this claim by an induction on
k
. The claim is true for
k=0
by the definition of
R_{1}
. Assume that the claim is true for
k
. Consider two cases:

Case 1:

\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\leq R_{0}
. In this case, we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{k}-\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{\varphi^{2}}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}+2a_{k}\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|\frac{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|}{\varphi}+a_{k}^{2}\frac{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|^{2}}{\varphi^{2}}\leq R_{0}^{2}+2AR_{0}+a_{k}^{2}.

So
\|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\leq R_{0}^{2}+2AR_{0}+\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\leq R_{1}^{2}
.

Case 2:

R_{0}<\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|<R_{1}
. In this case, using Assumption (
2d
), we have that

\|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}-2\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{\varphi^{2}}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}+a_{k}^{2}.

So
\|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\leq R_{1}^{2}
.

Combining these two cases, one can see that the claim is true for
k+1
. This completes the induction and proves the claim. And the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 1.3
.

\nabla F
is Lipschitz on
B^{n}_{R_{1}}(\mathbf{0})
. For any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in B^{n}_{R_{1}}(\mathbf{0})
, we have
\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\|\leq M\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|
, where
M
comes from Condition (
2c
) in Theorem
1.1
. Moreover, for such
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, we have
F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq F(\mathbf{x})+(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\frac{M}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

By the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have that the gradient of
F
is
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})=\int_{B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\ast)d\mu
. So, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in B^{n}_{R_{1}}(\mathbf{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\int_{B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\ast)-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\ast))d\mu\|\leq\int_{B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\ast)-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\ast)\|d\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B^{m}_{\rho}(\mathbf{0})}M\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|d\mu=M\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|.

Next, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in B^{n}_{R_{1}}(\mathbf{0})
, define
g(t):=F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
for
0\leq t\leq 1
. Then
g^{\prime}(t)=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. And

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-F(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(1)-g(0)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{\prime}(t)dt=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}(\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\nabla F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}))dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})dt+\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}(\nabla F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}))dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\int_{0}^{1}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|\cdot\|\nabla F((1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x})\|dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\int_{0}^{1}M\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|\cdot\|(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+M\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}tdt=(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x})^{T}\nabla F(\mathbf{x})+\frac{M}{2}\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 1.4
.

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2})
converges and, consequently,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
converges almost surely.

Proof.

By Lemma
1.2
, we know that
\mathbf{x}_{k}<R_{1}
and therefore
\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|\leq\varphi
. Let
M
be as in Lemma
1.3
. We have that

(1.2)

F(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\leq F(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}M.

Taking the expectation on both sides of Inequality (
1.2
), we get

E(F(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}))\leq E(F(\mathbf{x}_{k}))-\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}E(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k}))+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}M.

But

E(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k}))=E(E(\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})~|~\mathbf{x}_{k}))=E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}).

So

(1.3)

E(F(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}))\leq E(F(\mathbf{x}_{k}))-\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2})+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}M.

Summing Inequality (
1.3
) from
0
to
k
, we get that

(1.4)

E(F(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}))\leq F(\mathbf{x}_{0})-\frac{1}{\varphi}\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{j})\|^{2})+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}^{2}.

By Lemma
1.2
and Assumption (
2c
), there is a
B>0
such that
|F(\mathbf{x}_{k})|\leq B
for all
k\geq 0
. Then Inequality (
1.4
) implies that

\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{j})\|^{2})\leq\varphi\left(B+F(\mathbf{x}_{0})+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}^{2}\right).

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}
converges, this inequality shows that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}E(\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2})
converges. It then follows that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
is finite with probability
1
. In other words,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
converges almost surely.
∎

Lemma 1.5
.

Both
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
converge almost surely.

Proof.

Set

(1.5)

u_{k}=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})).

Consider the sequence
\{z_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}u_{j}\}
of random variables. By Lemma
1.2
and Assumption (
2c
), there is a constant
U
such that
|u_{k}|\leq U
for all
k
. Note that each
\mathbf{x}_{k}
is determined by
\mathbf{y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}
and is independent of
\mathbf{y}_{k}
and that
E(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})~|~\mathbf{x}_{k})=\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. So
E(u_{k}~|~\mathbf{y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k-1})=0
and
E(z_{k}~|~\mathbf{y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k-1})=z_{k-1}
. This shows that
\{z_{k}\}
is a Martingale relative to
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}
. For any
k\geq 0
, we have that

E(z_{k})=E(z_{k-1})+a_{k}E(u_{k})=E(z_{k-1})+a_{k}E(E(u_{k}~|~\mathbf{y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}))=E(z_{k-1})=\cdots=E(z_{0})=0.

So the variance of
z_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle Var(z_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(z_{k}^{2})=E((\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}u_{j})^{2})=E(a_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2}+(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}u_{j})^{2}+2a_{k}u_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}u_{j}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(a_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2})+Var(z_{k-1})+2a_{k}E(u_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}u_{j}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(a_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2})+Var(z_{k-1})+2a_{k}E(E(u_{k}(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}u_{j})~|~\mathbf{y}_{0},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(a_{k}^{2}u_{k}^{2})+Var(z_{k-1})\leq a_{k}^{2}U^{2}+Var(z_{k-1})

Summing from
0
to
k
, we get
Var(z_{k})\leq U^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}^{2}
for all
k
. But
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}
converges. So
\{Var(z_{k})\}
is a bounded sequence. Thus, by the Martingale Convergence Theorem,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}u_{k}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}z_{k}
converges almost surely. By Lemma
1.4
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
also converges almost surely. Therefore,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}u_{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}

converges almost surely.

Next we consider
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. Assume that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
converges. By Inequality (
1.2
), one can see that

\{v_{k}:=F(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{1}{\varphi}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j})+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\}

is a decreasing sequence. By Lemma
1.2
and Assumption (
2c
),
\{F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\}
is a bounded sequence. Since
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}
both converge,
\{\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j})\}
and
\{\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}\}
are also bounded sequences. This shows that
\{v_{k}\}
is a bounded decreasing sequence. Therefore,
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{k}
converges. But
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{j})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{j})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}a_{j}^{2}=0
. So
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}v_{k}
converges. This proves that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
converges if
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
converges. But the latter converges almost surely. So does the former.
∎

Lemma 1.6
.

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})=0
almost surely.

Proof.

By Lemmas
1.4
and
1.5
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
all converge almost surely. So, to prove the current lemma, we only need to show that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})=0
if
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{T}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
all converge. In the rest of this proof, we assume that the latter three all converge.

First, we claim that
\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
. Otherwise, there would be a
t>0
and
\kappa>0
such that
\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\geq t
if
k\geq\kappa
. Then
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}\geq\sum_{k=\kappa}^{\infty}a_{k}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}\geq t^{2}\sum_{k=\kappa}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty
since
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty
. This is a contradiction. Thus,
\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
.

Next, we claim that
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
, too. We prove this claim by contradiction. Let us assume that
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=s>0
. Let
M
be as in Lemma
1.3
. Recall that
\Phi=\sup\{\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\|~|~\|\mathbf{x}\|<R_{1},\|\mathbf{y}\|<\rho\}
and
\varphi\geq\Phi
. Since
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}a_{k}=0
, there is a
K>0
such that
a_{k}M\leq\frac{s}{8}
for
k>K
. Since
\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
and
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=s>0
, there exist two infinite sequences
\{p_{i}\}
and
\{q_{i}\}
of positive integers such that

•

K<p_{1}<q_{1}<p_{2}<q_{2}\cdots<q_{i-1}<p_{i}<q_{i}<p_{i+1}<\cdots
,

•

for
i=1,2\dots
,
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|<\frac{s}{4}
,
\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}})\|>\frac{s}{2}
and
\frac{s}{4}\leq\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\leq\frac{s}{2}
for
p_{i}<k<q_{i}
.

By Lemmas
1.2
and
1.3
, we have that, for all
i\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{s}{4}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}})\|-\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|\leq\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|\leq M\|\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}}-\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}}\|=M\|\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})\|\leq\frac{M\Phi}{\varphi}\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}\leq M\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}.

This shows that

(1.6)

\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}\geq\frac{s}{4M}\text{ for all }i.

Using Lemmas
1.2
and
1.3
again, we get that, for each
p_{i}
,

\displaystyle\frac{s}{4}-\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}+1})\|-\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|\leq\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}+1})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|\leq M\|\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}+1}-\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{p_{i}}M}{\varphi}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}},\mathbf{y}_{p_{i}})\|\leq\frac{a_{p_{i}}M\Phi}{\varphi}\leq\frac{s}{8}

since
p_{i}>K
. Thus,
\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})\|\geq\frac{s}{8}
. By Inequality (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})-\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}u_{k}-\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{2}+\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}M,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})-\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}u_{k}-\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}}{\varphi}\frac{s^{2}}{64}+\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}M

where
u_{k}
is defined in Equation (
1.5
). Thus,

0<\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}\leq\frac{64\varphi}{s^{2}}\left(F(\mathbf{x}_{p_{i}})-F(\mathbf{x}_{q_{i}})-\frac{1}{\varphi}\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}u_{k}+\frac{M}{2}\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}^{2}\right)

But
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}F(\mathbf{x}_{k})
,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}u_{k}
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}
all converge. So the above inequality implies that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=p_{i}}^{q_{i}-1}a_{k}=0
, which contradicts Inequality (
1.6
). Hence,
\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
.

Altogether, we have proved that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|=0
and therefore
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nabla F(\mathbf{x}_{k})=0
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It is clear that Theorem
1.1
follows from Lemmas
1.5
and
1.6
.
∎

2.2.
Adequate augmentation.

The classical back-propagation algorithm has many interesting and important applications. But this algorithm is not convergent in general. We will use Theorem
1.1
to modify it and get a convergent algorithm. For this purpose, we introduce the concept of
adequate augmentation
. Before stating the definition, let us introduce some notations first. We view
\Lambda
as a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and write

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|\Lambda\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{i-1}}(\lambda^{i}_{j,j^{\prime}})^{2}},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\Lambda}P(\mathbf{x},\Lambda)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{i-1}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{i}_{j,j^{\prime}}}P(\mathbf{x},\Lambda)\right)^{2}},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{T}\nabla_{\Lambda}P(\mathbf{x},\Lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{i-1}}\lambda^{i}_{j,j^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{i}_{j,j^{\prime}}}P(\mathbf{x},\Lambda)

for any function
P:\mathbb{R}^{n_{l+1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.1
.

For a given choice of the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
, a function

is called an
adequate augmentation
with respect to

if

•

\alpha
is differentiable,

•

\nabla\alpha
is locally Lipschitz, that is, for any
r>0
there is a
c_{r}>0
such that
\|\nabla\alpha(\Lambda)-\nabla\alpha(\Lambda^{\prime})\|\leq c_{r}\|\Lambda-\Lambda^{\prime}\|
whenever
\|\Lambda\|,\|\Lambda^{\prime}\|\leq r
,

•

for every
\rho>0
, there is an
R_{\rho}>0
such that
\Lambda^{T}\nabla_{\Lambda}\left(E(F(\mathbf{x},\Lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))+\alpha(\Lambda)\right)\geq 0
whenever
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
and
\|\Lambda\|\geq R_{\rho}
.

With some fairly nonrestrictive assumptions, it is not hard to find adequate augmentations for a given error function. We give some examples of adequate augmentations for the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
below. The key to our constructions is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{i-1}}E(F(\mathbf{x},\Lambda),\mathbf{y})\|\cdot M\cdot M\leq M^{2}\theta^{i-1}_{\rho}(\|\Lambda\|^{i}+1).

Consequently,

\|\nabla_{\Lambda^{i}}E(F(\mathbf{x},\Lambda),\mathbf{y})\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{i-1}}|\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda^{i}_{j,j^{\prime}}}E(\mathbf{z}^{0},\mathbf{y})|\leq n_{i}n_{i-1}M^{2}\theta^{i-1}_{\rho}(\|\Lambda\|^{i}+1).

Thus, Inequality (
2.9
) is true for
i
and
\theta_{\rho}^{i}=\max\{2n_{i}n_{i-1}M\theta^{i-1}_{\rho},n_{i}n_{i-1}M^{2}\theta^{i-1}_{\rho}\}
. This proves Inequality (
2.9
) for
i=1,\dots,l+1
.

Finally, we have that
\|g(\mathbf{x})\|\leq\Omega_{\rho}
for any
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
and, consequently,

\|\nabla_{\Lambda}E(F(\mathbf{x},\Lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\|\nabla_{\Lambda^{i}}E(F(\mathbf{x},\Lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\theta_{\rho}^{i}(\|\Lambda\|^{i}+1)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\theta_{\rho}^{i}(\|\Lambda\|^{l+1}+2).

So the lemma is true for
\Theta_{\rho}=2\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}\theta_{\rho}^{i}
.
∎

Based on Lemma
2.2
, we have the following examples of adequate augmentations for the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Define

(1)

\alpha_{1}(\Lambda)=\delta\|\Lambda\|^{t}
, where
\delta,t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\delta>0
and
t>l+2
,

(2)

\alpha_{2}(\Lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\|\Lambda\|<r,\\
\delta(\|\Lambda\|-r)^{t}&\text{if }\|\Lambda\|\geq r,\end{cases}

\delta,r>0
and
t>l+2
,

(3)

\alpha_{3}(\Lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\|\Lambda\|<r,\\
e^{\|\Lambda\|-r}-\sum_{p=0}^{q}\frac{(\|\Lambda\|-r)^{p}}{p!}&\text{if }\|\Lambda\|\geq r,\end{cases}

.

Proof.

One can see that

3.
Augmented Back-Propagation Algorithm for Acyclic Neural Networks

The augmented back-propagation algorithm is not limited to feed-forward networks. We give in this section the augmented back-propagation algorithm for neural networks whose underlying directed graphs are acyclic. Such acyclic neural networks are straightforward generalizations of feed-forward networks and include, for example, feed-forward networks with biases.

3.1.
Acyclic Neural Networks

Let
G
be a finite directed graph without loops and parallel edges. That is,

•

the set
\mathfrak{V}
of vertices of
G
is finite,

•

the set
\mathfrak{E}
of edges of
G
is a subset of
(\mathfrak{V}\times\mathfrak{V})\setminus\{(v,v)~|~v\in\mathfrak{V}\}
.

Every edge
e\in\mathfrak{E}
has a starting vertex
s(e)
and a terminal vertex
t(e)
. For any
v\in\mathfrak{V}
, the set of edges pointing out of (resp. into)
v
is denoted by
Out(v)=\{e\in\mathfrak{E}~|~s(e)=v\}
(resp.
In(v)=\{e\in\mathfrak{E}~|~t(e)=v\}
.) We also set
\mathfrak{V}_{in}=\{v\in\mathfrak{V}~|~In(v)=\emptyset\}
and
\mathfrak{V}_{out}=\{v\in\mathfrak{V}~|~Out(v)=\emptyset\}
.
\mathfrak{V}_{in}
and
\mathfrak{V}_{out}
are called the sets of input and output vertices of
G
, respectively. A directed path in
G
is a sequence
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l}
of distinct edges satisfying

•

t(e_{i})=s(e_{i+1})
for
i=1,\dots,l-1
,

•

t(e_{1}),\dots,t(e_{l})
are distinct.

\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l}
is called a directed cycle if we further have that
t(e_{l})=s(e_{1})
.

In the rest of this section, we assume that
G
is
acyclic
, that is,
G
does not contain any directed cycles. For each vertex
v
, we define the
depth
of
v
to be

(3.1)

d(v):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }v\in\mathfrak{V}_{in},\\
\max\{l~|~\text{there is a directed path }\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l}\text{ in }G\text{ satisfying }t(e_{l})=v\}&\text{if }v\notin\mathfrak{V}_{in}.\end{cases}

We also define the
height
of
v
to be

(3.2)

h(v):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }v\in\mathfrak{V}_{out},\\
\max\{l~|~\text{there is a directed path }\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{l}\text{ in }G\text{ satisfying }s(e_{1})=v\}&\text{if }v\notin\mathfrak{V}_{out}.\end{cases}

d(v)
and
h(v)
are well defined since
G
is acyclic. We have the following simple observations.

Lemma 3.1
.

(1)

\max\{h(v)~|~v\in\mathfrak{V}\}=\max\{d(v)~|~v\in\mathfrak{V}\}
. We call this common value the
height

H(G)
of
G
from now on.

(2)

\{d(v)~|~v\in\mathfrak{V}\}=\{0,1,\dots,H(G)\}
and
\{h(v)~|~v\in v\}=\{0,1,\dots,H(G)\}
.

(3)

d(s(e))\leq d(t(e))-1
and
h(t(e))\leq h(s(e))-1
for any edge
e\in\mathfrak{E}
.

A neural network underlay by
G
consists of

•

a weight function
\lambda:\mathfrak{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

•

an assignment of a function
\sigma_{v}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to each vertex
v\in\mathfrak{V}\setminus(\mathfrak{V}_{in}\cup\mathfrak{V}_{out})
.

Order the input and output vertices as
\mathfrak{V}_{in}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and
\mathfrak{V}_{out}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m}\}
. And denote by
N
the number of edges in
G
. Note that, with an ordering of the edges of
G
,
\lambda
can be viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then the above neural network defines a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
F(\mathbf{x},\lambda)=\mathbf{z}
where
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda:\mathfrak{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{z}=[z_{1},\dots,z_{m}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are related by the following forward propagation

(3.3)

\begin{cases}z(v_{i})=x_{i}&\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,n\\
z(v)=\sigma_{v}(\sum_{t(e)=v}\lambda(e)z(s(e)))&\text{for }v\in\mathfrak{V}\setminus(\mathfrak{V}_{in}\cup\mathfrak{V}_{out}),\\
z_{j}=z(u_{j})=\sum_{t(e)=u_{j}}\lambda(e)z(s(e))&\text{for }j=1,2,\dots,m.\end{cases}

It follows from Lemma
3.1
that forward propagation (
3.3
) is a well defined inductive definition. From now on, we assume that
\sigma_{v}
is differentiable for each
v\in\mathfrak{V}
. It is straightforward to check that
F
is differentiable in both
\mathbf{x}
and
\lambda
.

Fix a differentiable error function
E:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The function
E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})=E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
is differentiable. Its partial derivatives with respect to
z(v)
and
\lambda(e)
can be computed by the following backward propagation.

(3.4)

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial z(v)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})=\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)\cdot\lambda(e)\cdot(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e}))),\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)\cdot z(s(e))\cdot(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e}))).\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.1
again, backward propagation (
3.4
) is a well defined inductive computation.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function. Assume that:

•

The sequence
\{(\mathbf{x}(k),\mathbf{y}(k))\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies
g(\mathbf{x}(k))=\mathbf{y}(k)
.

•

Fix a
\lambda_{0}:\mathfrak{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

•

\{\eta_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

For the above setup, the classical back-propagation algorithm is given inductively by the following steps:

•

Forward propagation:

(3.5)

\begin{cases}z(v_{i},k)=x_{i}(k)&\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,n\\
z(v,k)=\sigma_{v}(\sum_{t(e)=v}\lambda_{k}(e)z(s(e),k))&\text{for }v\in\mathfrak{V}\setminus(\mathfrak{V}_{in}\cup\mathfrak{V}_{out}),\\
z_{j}(k)=z(u_{j},k)=\sum_{t(e)=u_{j}}\lambda(e)z(s(e),k)&\text{for }j=1,2,\dots,m,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{x}(k)=[x_{1}(k),\dots,x_{n}(k)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

•

Backward propagation:

(3.6)

\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z(v)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)|_{z(v)=z(v,k)~\forall v\in\mathfrak{V},~\lambda(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)~\forall e\in\mathfrak{E}}\\
=\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\lambda_{k}(e)\cdot(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda_{k}(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e}),k))\cdot\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)|_{z(v)=z(v,k)~\forall v\in\mathfrak{V},~\lambda(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)~\forall e\in\mathfrak{E}},\\
\\
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)|_{z(v)=z(v,k)~\forall v\in\mathfrak{V},~\lambda(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)~\forall e\in\mathfrak{E}}\\
=z(s(e),k)\cdot(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda_{k}(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e}),k))\cdot\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)|_{z(v)=z(v,k)~\forall v\in\mathfrak{V},~\lambda(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)~\forall e\in\mathfrak{E}}.\end{cases}

•

Weight update:

(3.7)

\lambda_{k+1}(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)-\eta_{k}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right)|_{z(v)=z(v,k)~\forall v\in\mathfrak{V},~\lambda(e)=\lambda_{k}(e)~\forall e\in\mathfrak{E}}.

3.2.
Adequate augmentations

The definition and properties of adequate augmentations generalize easily to acyclic neural networks. Let us introduce the following notations first. We view a function
\lambda:\mathfrak{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and write

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|\lambda\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}(\lambda(e))^{2}},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\lambda}P(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}P(\mathbf{x},\lambda)\right)^{2}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\lambda^{T}\nabla_{\lambda}P(\mathbf{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\lambda(e)\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}P(\mathbf{x},\lambda)

for any function
P:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3.2
.

For a given choice of the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
, a function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called an
adequate augmentation
with respect to
E,F,g
if

•

\alpha
is differentiable,

•

\nabla\alpha
is locally Lipschitz, that is, for any
r>0
there is a
c_{r}>0
such that
\|\nabla\alpha(\lambda)-\nabla\alpha(\lambda^{\prime})\|\leq c_{r}\|\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\|
whenever
\|\lambda\|,\|\lambda^{\prime}\|\leq r
,

•

for every
\rho>0
, there is an
R_{\rho}>0
such that
\lambda^{T}\nabla_{\lambda}\left(E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))+\alpha(\lambda)\right)\geq 0
whenever
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
and
\|\lambda\|\geq R_{\rho}
.

As in the case of feed-forward networks, it is not hard to find adequate augmentations for a given error functions satisfying some non-restrictive properties. Again, we give some examples of adequate augmentations for the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
below. The key to our constructions is Lemma
3.3
, which is a straightforward generalizations of Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
. Assume that

•

the functions
\{\sigma_{v}~|~v\in\mathfrak{V}\}
are uniformly
C^{1}
-bounded, that is, there is an
M>0
such that
\sigma_{v}(t)\leq M
and
(\sigma_{v})^{\prime}(t)\leq M
for all
v\in\mathfrak{V}
,

•

the function
g(\mathbf{x})
is locally bounded, that is, for every
\rho>0
, there is an
\Omega_{\rho}>0
such that
\|g(\mathbf{x})\|\leq\Omega_{\rho}
whenever
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
.

Then, for every
\rho>0
, there is a
\Theta_{\rho}>0
such that
\|\nabla_{\lambda}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\|\leq\Theta_{\rho}(\|\lambda\|^{H(G)}+1)
if
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
.

Proof.

The proof of Lemma
3.3
follows more or less that of Lemma
2.2
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
M\geq\max\{1,\rho\}
. Fix a
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1},\dots,y_{m}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{y}\|\leq\Omega_{\rho}
. We first prove that there is a function
\theta:\mathfrak{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfying

(3.11)

\begin{cases}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z(v)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|\leq\theta(v)(\|\lambda\|^{h(v)+1}+1)&\text{for every }v\in\mathfrak{V},\\
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|\leq M^{2}\theta(t(e))(\|\lambda\|^{h(t(e))+1}+1)&\text{for every }e\in\mathfrak{E}.\end{cases}

By Lemma
3.1
, the function
\theta
can be constructed by an induction on
h(v)
.

Assume that
h(v)=0
, then
v=u_{j}\in\mathfrak{V}_{out}
for some
j
and

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z(v)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|=2|z(u_{j})-y_{j}|\leq 2|z(u_{j})|+2|y_{j}|=2\left|\sum_{e\in In(u_{j})}\lambda(e)z(s(e))\right|+2|y_{j}|\leq 2\|\lambda\|M\sqrt{\#In(u_{j})}+2\Omega_{\rho},

where
\#In(u_{j})
is the cardinality of
In(u_{j})
. So we can define that
\theta(v)=\theta(u_{j})=\max\{2M\sqrt{\#In(u_{j})},2\Omega_{\rho}\}
.

Now assume that
\theta
is defined for all
u\in\mathfrak{V}
satisfying
0\leq h(u)\leq h-1
and that
v\in\mathfrak{V}
satisfies
h(v)=h
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have that
h(t(e))\leq h-1
for all
e\in Out(v)
. So, by Equation (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z(v)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|\cdot|\lambda(e)|\cdot\left|(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e})))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\theta(t(e))(\|\lambda\|^{h(t(e))+1}+1)\cdot\|\lambda\|\cdot M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\theta(t(e))(\|\lambda\|^{h(t(e))+2}+\|\lambda\|)\leq M\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\theta(t(e))(2\|\lambda\|^{h+1}+2).

Here, we use the convention that
\sigma_{u}(z)=z
for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
if
u\in\mathfrak{V}_{out}
. So we can define that
\theta(v)=2M\sum_{e\in Out(v)}\theta(t(e))
. This defines
\theta(v)
for any
v
with
h(v)=h
, which completes the inductive construction of
\theta
and proves that the first inequality in (
3.11
) is satisfied.

Using Equation (
3.4
) again, we get that, for any
e\in\mathfrak{E}
,

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|=\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z(t(e))}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),\mathbf{y})\right|\cdot|z(s(e))|\cdot\left|(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e})))\right|

Note that
|z(s(e))|\leq\max\{M,\rho\}=M
and
\left|(\sigma_{t(e)})^{\prime}(\sum_{\hat{e}\in In(t(e))}\lambda(\hat{e})z(s(\hat{e})))\right|\leq\max\{M,1\}=M
. So, the second inequality in (
3.11
) follows from the first one.

Finally, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq\rho
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, by the second inequality in (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\lambda}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\|\leq\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda(e)}E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M^{2}\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\theta(t(e))(\|\lambda\|^{h(t(e))+1}+1)\leq M^{2}\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\theta(t(e))(\|\lambda\|^{h(G)}+2).

So
\Theta_{\rho}=2M^{2}\sum_{e\in\mathfrak{E}}\theta(t(e))
satisfies the requirements in the lemma.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Define

(1)

\alpha_{1}(\lambda)=\delta\|\lambda\|^{t}
, where
\delta,t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\delta>0
and
t>h(G)+1
,

(2)

\alpha_{2}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\|\lambda\|<r,\\
\delta(\|\lambda\|-r)^{t}&\text{if }\|\lambda\|\geq r,\end{cases}
where
\delta,r,t\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy
\delta,r>0
and
t>h(G)+1
,

(3)

\alpha_{3}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\|\lambda\|<r,\\
e^{\|\lambda\|-r}-\sum_{p=0}^{q}\frac{(\|\lambda\|-r)^{p}}{p!}&\text{if }\|\lambda\|\geq r,\end{cases}
where
r>0
and
q\geq 1
.

Then,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are all adequate augmentations for the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
under the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
.

Proof.

One can see that
\alpha_{i}
is differentiable and that
\nabla\alpha_{i}
is locally Lipschitz for
i=1,2,3
. A straightforward computation shows that, for
i=1,2,3
,
\lambda^{T}\nabla\alpha_{i}(\lambda)>\Theta_{\rho}(\|\lambda\|^{h(G)+1}+1)
if
\|\lambda\|\gg 1
. So, by Lemma
3.3
, we have that, for
i=1,2,3
,

\lambda^{T}\nabla_{\lambda}\left(E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))+\alpha_{i}(\lambda)\right)\geq\lambda^{T}\nabla\alpha_{i}(\lambda)-\|\lambda\|\cdot\|\nabla_{\lambda}\left(E(F(\mathbf{x},\lambda),g(\mathbf{x}))\right)\|>0\text{ for }\|\lambda\|\gg 1.

Thus,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
are all adequate augmentations for the error function
E(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
.
∎
