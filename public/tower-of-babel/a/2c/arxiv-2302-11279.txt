FEM-BEM coupling in Fractional Diffusion
Markus Faustmann and Alexander Rieder

Abstract
We derive and analyze a fully computable discrete scheme for fractional partial differential equations posed on the full space ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} . Based on a reformulation using the well-known Caffarelli-Silvestre extension, we study a modified variational formulation to obtain well-posedness of the discrete problem. Our scheme is obtained by combining a diagonalization procedure with a reformulation using boundary integral equations and a coupling of finite elements and boundary elements. For our discrete method we present a-priori estimates as well as numerical examples.



1
Introduction

In this work, we study stationary fractional partial differential equations posed on the full space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=2,3
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\beta}u+su

\displaystyle=f\;\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$},\qquad\quad\mathcal{L}u:=-\operatorname{div}\big(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u\big)

(1.1)

with
s\geq 0
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Fractional PDEs of this type are oftentimes used to model non-local effects in physics, finance or image processing,
[
BV16
,
SZB+18
]
.

Regarding the formal definition of non-integer powers
\mathcal{L}^{\beta}
of differential operators, there are various different descriptions in literature such as Fourier transformation, semigroup approaches, singular integrals or spectral calculus, see
[
LPG+20
]
.
A distinct advantage of full-space formulations as in (
1.1
) is that all of these definitions are equivalent,
[
Kwa17
]
, while there are significant differences in the definitions, if one restricts the problem to a bounded domain.

Nonetheless, there are usually no closed form solutions to these problems available and therefore numerical approximations are used. In order to derive a computable approximation, most numerical methods employ formulations on bounded domains, for which there is a fairly well developed literature.
We mention the surveys
[
BBN+18
,
LPG+20
]
as well as finite element methods for the integral definition of the fractional Laplacian
[
AB17
,
ABH19
,
FKM22
]
, for the spectral definition
[
NOS15
,
NOS16
]
, and semigroup approaches

[
BP15
,
BLP19
]
. We especially mention the very influential reformulation using the extension approach by Caffarelli and Silvestre
[
CS07
]
(see also
[
ST10
]
for a more general setting), which allows to use PDE techniques in the analysis. This approach paired with an
hp
-FEM approach in the extended direction has proven to be an
effective strategy both for elliptic
[
MPSV18
,
BMN+19
,
BMS23
,
FMMS22b
,
FMMS22a
]
as well as
parabolic problems
[
NOS16
,
MR21
]
.

Many numerical approaches for the full-space formulation, like
[
AKMR21
]
for the fractional Allen-Cahn equation, rely on truncation of the full-space problem to a bounded domain, which induces an additional truncation error that needs to be investigated.
A different approach that avoids any truncation errors is the use of a coupling of finite elements on a truncated domain and boundary elements appearing from a reformulation of the unbounded exterior part as a boundary integral equation.
We refer to the classical works
[
JN80
,
Cos88
,
Han90
]
for the one-equation/Johnson-Nédélec coupling and the symmetric coupling for elliptic transmission problems. For the standard Laplacian these methods are well-posed and thoroughly analyzed,
[
Say09
,
Ste11
,
AFF+13
]
.

In this work, we introduce a method for elliptic full-space fractional operators that combines the mentioned Caffarelli-Silvestre extension approach with FEM-BEM coupling techniques.
More precisely, inspired by
[
LS09
,
Say09
]
, we reformulate the extension problem as a variational problem, where the solution on a bounded domain and an exterior solution
in an exotic Hilbert space are sought. Using suitable Poincaré inequalities, we show well-posedness of the continuous formulation.
In order to obtain a computable approximation, we then use the diagonalization procedure of
[
BMN+19
]
, which leads to a sequence of Helmholtz-type transmission problems. For those, we employ a standard coupling of FEM and BEM of symmetric type,
as proposed by
[
Cos88
,
Han90
]
.
Finally, we present an a priori analysis for a discretization with
hp
-FEM in the extended variable.

This work builds on the recent a priori analysis of
[
FR23
]
, where
the regularity and the decay properties of the analytic full-space solution are established.

1.1
Layout

The present paper is structured as follows: In the remainder of Section 1, we introduce our model problem as well as necessary notation and most notably, the Caffarelli-Silvestre extension problem. In Section 2, we formulate our main results: well-posedness of our formulation, the fully-discrete scheme using the diagonalization procedure together with the symmetric FEM-BEM coupling, and, finally, a best-approximation result. Section 3 provides the proofs for the well-posedness and the diagonalization procedure and, most notably, a Poincaré type estimate. Section 4 contains the proofs for the a-priori analysis of the fully discrete formulation using
hp
-finite elements in the extended variable, which builds upon the regularity and decay properties of
[
FR23
]
. Finally, Section 5 presents some numerical examples that validate the proposed method.

1.2
Notations

Throughout the text we use the symbol
a\lesssim b
meaning that
a\leq Cb
with a generic constant
C>0
that is independent of any crucial quantities in the analysis. Moreover, we write
\simeq
to indicate that both estimates
\lesssim
and
\gtrsim
hold.

We employ classical integer order Sobolev spaces
H^{k}(\Omega)
on (bounded) Lipschitz domains
\Omega
and the fractional Sobolev spaces
H^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
defined, e.g., via Fourier transformation. We also need Sobolev spaces on the boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
of a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted by
H^{t}(\Gamma)
with
t\in[-1,1]
. One way to properly define them is by using local charts, see
[
SS11
]
for details.

1.3
Assumptions on the model problem

Let
d=2,3
. We consider (
1.1
) and, for functions
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define the self adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}^{\beta}

using spectral calculus

\mathcal{L}^{\beta}u:=\int_{\sigma(\mathcal{L})}z^{\beta}dE\;u,

where
E
is the spectral measure of
\mathcal{L}
and
\sigma(\mathcal{L})
is the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
. Using standard techniques this definition can be extended to tempered distributions.

For the data, we assume
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
\mathfrak{A}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
is pointwise symmetric and positive definite in the sense that there exists
\mathfrak{A}_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{A}(x)y,y)_{2}\geq\mathfrak{A}_{0}\left\|y\right\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

In order to avoid several additional difficulties due to decay conditions at infinity, we assume
s\geq\sigma_{0}>0
for the case
d=2
.

Additionally, in order to be able to apply FEM-BEM coupling techniques, we make the following assumptions on the coefficients in the model problem: There exists a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

1.

\operatorname{supp}f\subseteq\Omega
,

2.

\mathfrak{A}\equiv I
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
.

This is not the most general setting where our techniques can be applied. For example,
also a lowest order term could be included in the definition of
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.1
); see also Remark
2.10
.

1.4
Degenerate elliptic extension

In the same way as in our previous work
[
FR23
]
, we use a reformulation of the fractional PDE as the Dirichlet-to-Neumann mapping for a degenerate elliptic PDE in a half space in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, the so called Caffarelli-Silvestre extension,
[
CS07
,
ST10
]
.

We recall the definition of weighted Sobolev spaces used for the extension. For any bounded open subset

D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, we define
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)
as the space of square integrable functions with respect to the weight
y^{\alpha}
and the Sobolev space
H^{1}(y^{\alpha},D)\subset L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)
of functions with finite norm

\left\|\mathcal{U}\right\|_{H^{1}(y^{\alpha},D)}^{2}:=\int\int_{D}y^{\alpha}\Big(\big|\nabla\mathcal{U}(x,y)\big|^{2}+\big|\mathcal{U}(x,y)\big|^{2}\Big)\,dx\,dy.

We also employ the spaces
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y}))
and
H^{1}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y}))
for
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
defined in an analogous way by omitting the
x
-integration.

For
unbounded
sets
D
, we additionally use the weight

\displaystyle\rho(x,y):=(1+\left|x\right|^{2}+\left|y\right|^{2})^{1/2}\qquad(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

to take care of the behaviour at infinity. In this case, we define the
space
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},D)
as the space of all square integrable functions
\mathcal{U}
(with respect to the weight function
y^{\alpha}\rho^{-2}
) such that the norm

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{U}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},D)}^{2}:=\int\int_{D}{y^{\alpha}\Big(\big|\nabla\mathcal{U}(x,y)\big|^{2}+\rho(x,y)^{-2}\big|\mathcal{U}(x,y)\big|^{2}\Big)\,dx\,dy}

(1.2)

is finite.
Commonly used cases are
D=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
(full space),
D=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y})
for
\mathcal{Y}>0
(corresponding to truncation in
y
-direction), or
D=\omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y})

for
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathcal{Y}>0
.

Moreover, we also employ spaces acting only in
x
. Using the weight

\displaystyle\rho_{x}(x):=\rho(x,0),

we introduce
L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H_{\rho_{x}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as in (
1.2
) by omitting the
y
-integration.

For functions
\mathcal{U}
in
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
, one can give meaning to their trace at
y=0
, which we denote by
\operatorname*{tr}_{0}\mathcal{U}
. In fact, by
[
KM19
, Lemma 3.8]
and
[
FR23
, Lem. 3.1]
we have the trace estimates

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\right|_{H^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}&\lesssim\left\|\nabla\mathcal{U}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}\\
\|(1+|x|^{2})^{-\beta/2}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}&\lesssim\left\|\nabla\mathcal{U}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}\qquad\text{ if }d=3.\end{split}

(1.3)

Then, the extension problem reads as: find
\mathcal{U}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\big(y^{\alpha}\mathfrak{A}_{x}\nabla\mathcal{U}\big)

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}$},

(1.4a)

\displaystyle d^{-1}_{\beta}\partial_{\nu^{\alpha}}\mathcal{U}+s\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=f\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

(1.4b)

where
d_{\beta}:=2^{1-2\beta}\Gamma(1-\beta)/\Gamma(\beta)
,
\alpha:=1-2\beta\in(-1,1)
,
\partial_{\nu^{\alpha}}\mathcal{U}(x):=-\lim_{y\rightarrow 0}y^{\alpha}\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(x,y)
, and
\mathfrak{A}_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{A}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
.
By
[
ST10
]
, the solution to (
1.1
) is then given by
u=\mathrm{tr_{0}}\,\mathcal{U}
.

For the domain
\Omega
with boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, we also introduce the usual trace operators
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}
(denoting the trace coming from the interior of
\Omega
) and
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{+}
(denoting the trace coming from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
) and correspondingly the normal derivative operators
\partial_{\nu,\Gamma}^{\pm}
(see
[
SS11
]
for details).
The normal vector
\nu
is always assumed to face out of
\Omega
.
With theses operators, the jumps across
\Gamma
are defined as

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket\gamma u\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u-\gamma^{+}_{\Gamma}u,\qquad{\left\llbracket\partial_{\nu}u\right\rrbracket}=\partial^{-}_{\nu,\Gamma}u-\partial^{+}_{\nu,\Gamma}u.

(1.5)

We will apply these operators for functions in
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
, where they
are to be understood pointwise with respect to
y
.
This is equivalent to taking the trace and normal derivative along the lateral boundary
\Gamma\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

2
Main results

2.1
Variational formulation

The weak formulation of (
1.4
) in
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
reads as finding
\mathcal{U}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
such that

\displaystyle A(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathfrak{A}_{x}(x)\nabla\mathcal{U}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{V}\;dxdy}+sd_{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}\,dx}=d_{\beta}(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

(2.1)

for all
\mathcal{V}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
. Well-posedness of the continuous formulation follows from
[
FR23
, Prop 2.3]
.

In order to also include our discretization scheme, we work in a slightly expanded
variational form, inspired by
[
LS09
,
Say09
]
.
In short, one can formulate an equivalent problem for the solution inside
\Omega
and
a function
\mathcal{U}_{\star}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined in a modified Hilbert space.

Definition 2.1
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
. We consider the space

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}:=\Big\{(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in H^{1}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))\times H_{\rho}^{1}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y})):\;

\displaystyle\qquad{\left\llbracket\gamma\mathcal{U}_{\star}\right\rrbracket}=\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\;\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}\mathcal{U}_{\star}=0,\;s\,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\Big\}

equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\|(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\|\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}\|_{H^{1}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}+\|\mathcal{U}_{\star}\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\star}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

We note that, by definition, the additional condition of
\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{\star}
being in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is only needed for
s\neq 0
as in this case the norm in
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
contains said
L^{2}
-term, which has to be finite.

With
\mathcal{U}=(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
and
\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
, we define the bilinear form
B:\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\times\mathbb{H}_{\infty}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle B(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V}):=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}{y^{\alpha}\mathfrak{A}_{x}(x)\nabla\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}\;dxdy}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{y^{\alpha}\nabla\mathcal{U}_{\star}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\star}\;dxdy}

\displaystyle+sd_{\beta}\int_{\Omega}{\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}\,dx}+sd_{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}_{\star}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}_{\star}\,dx}.

(2.2)

For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the weak formulation is given as the problem of finding
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle B(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle=d_{\beta}\int_{\Omega}{f\,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}}\;dx\qquad\forall\;\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}.

(2.3)

Problems (
1.4
) and (
2.3
) are connected as follows: If
\mathcal{U}_{\infty}=(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}
solves
(
2.3
), then the function
\mathcal{U}:=\begin{cases}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\quad\text{ in }\Omega\\
\mathcal{U}_{\star},\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\overline{\Omega}\end{cases}
solves (
2.1
).

In order to obtain a computable formulation, we start by cutting the problem from the infinite cylinder
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
to a finite
cylinder in the
y
-direction. To do so, we fix a parameter
\mathcal{Y}>0
to be chosen later and
introduce the truncated bilinear forms

\displaystyle{A}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{A}_{x}(x)\nabla\mathcal{U}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{V}\;dxdy}+sd_{\beta}\int_{\Omega}{\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}\,dx},

\displaystyle{A}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{\nabla\mathcal{U}\cdot\nabla\mathcal{V}}\;dxdy}+sd_{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{V}\,dx}.

The “big” bilinear form is then given by

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}\big((\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star}),(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}_{\star})\big):={A}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}_{\Omega})+{A}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}_{\star},\mathcal{V}_{\star}),

and the cutoff problem reads as: Find

\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
such that

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}\big(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}\big)=d_{\beta}\big(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}}\big)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}.

(2.4)

By the following theorem, we obtain well-posedness of the weak formulation of both variational formulations.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume either
d=3
or
s>0
.
Then, problem (
2.3
) has a unique solution
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{H}_{\infty}

satisfying

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{U}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C\min(1,s^{-1})\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)
. Then, the truncated problem (
2.4
) has a unique solution
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, for which the estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{Y}}\right)\min(1,s^{-1})\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

holds.
Additionally, the bilinear forms in (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are coercive.

The proof of the theorem is given in Section
3
and, in fact, reduces to the application of suitable Poincaré inequalities.

2.2
The discrete scheme

In this section, we describe our discrete scheme to approximate solutions to the truncated variational formulation (
2.4
). The main idea is to employ a tensor product structure for the approximation by using the diagonalization procedure described in
[
BMN+19
]
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{y}
be an arbitrary finite dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y}))
of dimension
N_{y}+1
.
Following the ideas of
[
BMN+19
]
,
we chose an orthonormal basis
(\varphi_{j})_{j=0}^{N_{y}}
of
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{y}
in
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y}))

and generalized eigenvalues
\mu_{j}\geq 0
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\varphi_{i}^{\prime}\varphi_{j}^{\prime}\,dy}+s\varphi_{i}(0)\varphi_{j}(0)=\mu_{j}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\varphi_{i}\varphi_{j}\,dy}=\mu_{j}\delta_{ij}\qquad\forall\;0\leq i,j\leq N_{y}.

(2.5)

It is easy to see that for
s=0
, the constant function is an eigenfunction corresponding
to the
eigenvalue
\mu=0
. Moreover, the assumption
s>0
for
d=2
guarantees that there is not a zero eigenvalue. If zero is an eigenvalue (for
d=3
), we assume that the eigenvalues are ordered such that
\mu_{0}=0
.

We now give a formal definition of our (semi-)discrete subspace of
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, which has tensor product structure with respect to the variables
x,y
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}\subset H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
be finite dimensional spaces and
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)
. Additionally, assume that
1\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}
. We introduce the closed subspace
\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}:=\operatorname*{cls}\Big\{\mathcal{U}_{h}=(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j,\Omega}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)}\text{ with }u_{j,\Omega}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\star}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j,\star}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)}\text{ with }u_{j,\star}\in H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma),

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket\gamma u_{j,\star}\right\rrbracket}=\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j},\quad\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j,\star}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}\Big\}.

(2.6)

Then, the semi-discrete problem reads as:
Find

\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
such that

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}\big(\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\big)=d_{\beta}\big(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}}\big)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\quad\text{for all }\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}.

(2.7)

Using the orthogonal basis for the
y
-direction, we can actually diagonalize some of the bilinear forms and obtain
an equivalent sequence of scalar problems.
In fact, functions
(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
solve (
2.7
), if and only if
they can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j,\Omega}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{\star}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j,\star}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)},

(2.8)

with
u_{j,\Omega}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}
,
u_{j,\star}\in H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma)
,
where the functions
u_{j,\Omega}
,
u_{j,\star}
solve

\big(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u_{j,\Omega},\nabla v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big(\mu_{j}u_{j,\Omega},v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\big<\partial_{\nu,\Gamma}^{-}u_{j,\star},\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}v\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
=d_{\beta}\varphi_{j}(0)(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x},

(2.9a)

and

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{j,\star}+\mu_{j}u_{j,\star}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma$}

(2.9b)

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket\gamma u_{j,\star}\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j},\quad\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j,\star}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}.

(2.9c)

We refer to Lemma
3.3
for a proof of this statement.

The equation for
u_{j,\star}
is still posed on an unbounded domain. We
will replace this with boundary integral equations. Therefore,
given
\mu\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(\mu)\geq 0
, we introduce

\displaystyle G(z;\mu):=\begin{cases}\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}\left(i\mu\left|z\right|\right),&\text{ for $d=2$,}\\
\frac{e^{-\mu\left|z\right|}}{4\pi\left|z\right|},&\text{ for $d=3$,}\end{cases}\quad\text{for $\mu\neq 0$ and}\quad G(z;0):=\begin{cases}\frac{-1}{2\pi}\ln(\left|z\right|),&\text{ for $d=2$,}\\
\frac{1}{4\pi\left|z\right|},&\text{ for $d=3$,}\end{cases}

where
H_{0}^{(1)}
denotes the first kind Hankel function of order
0
. The single-layer and double-layer potential
are then defined as

\displaystyle\big(\widetilde{V}(\mu)\varphi\big)\left(x\right)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Gamma}{G(x-z;\mu)\varphi(z)\;dz},\qquad\big(\widetilde{K}(\mu)\psi\big)\left(x\right):=\int_{\Gamma}{\partial^{-}_{\nu,\Gamma}G(x-z;\mu)\psi(z)\;dz},

and the corresponding boundary integral operators are given by

\displaystyle V(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\gamma^{\pm}_{\Gamma}\widetilde{V}(\mu),

\displaystyle\quad K(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(\gamma^{+}_{\Gamma}\widetilde{V}(\mu)+\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}\widetilde{V}(\mu)),

(2.10)

\displaystyle K^{t}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\nu.\Gamma}^{+}\widetilde{K}(\mu)+\partial_{\nu,\Gamma}^{-}\widetilde{K}(\mu)),

\displaystyle\quad W(\mu)

\displaystyle:=-\partial_{\nu,\Gamma}^{-}\widetilde{K}(\mu).

(2.11)

We then have the following result, giving a computable approximation of (
1.1
) that
only relies on well-known operators.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\varphi_{j},\mu_{j}
be the generalized eigenfunctions and eigenvalues from (
2.5
).
For all
j=0,\dots,N_{y}
,
let
(u_{j},\lambda_{j})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}
solve

\displaystyle\big(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u_{j},\nabla v_{h}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big(\mu_{j}u_{j},v_{h}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big<W(\mu_{j})\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j}

\displaystyle+(-1/2+K^{\prime}(\mu_{j}))\lambda_{j},\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}v_{h}\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=d_{\beta}\varphi_{j}(0)\big(f,v_{h}\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(2.12a)

\displaystyle\big<(1/2-K(\mu_{j}))\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j},\xi_{h}\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\big<V(\mu_{j})\lambda_{j},\xi_{h}\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=0

(2.12b)

for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}
and
\xi_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}

Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(x,y):=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)},\qquad\mathcal{U}_{\star}(x,y):=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{\Big(\widetilde{V}(\mu_{j})\lambda_{j}(x)-\widetilde{K}(\mu_{j})\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j}(x)\Big)\varphi_{j}(y)}

The problems (
2.12
) are standard FEM-BEM coupling problems
for what is often called the modified Helmholtz or Yukawa equation. As such, existence and uniqueness of solutions
(u_{j},\lambda_{j})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\times\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}
is well-known, see
[
LS09

. Consequently, we also obtain well-posedness of the semi-discrete formulation (
2.7
) as we have constructed a solution in
\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
. Uniqueness follows from coercivity of the bilinear form.

Corollary 2.5
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)

Due to the construction in Theorem

2.3

In the extended variable
y

Let
\mathcal{Y}>0
and
\mathcal{T}_{y}
be a geometric grid on

with mesh grading factor

levels of growth towards

\displaystyle x_{0}:=0,\qquad x_{\ell}:=\sigma^{L-\ell}\;\text{ for $\ell=0,\dots,L+M$,}\qquad x_{L+M+1}:=\mathcal{Y}.

(2.13)

By

\mathcal{S}^{p,1}(\mathcal{T}_{y}):=\{u\in C(0,\mathcal{Y})\;:\;u|_{(x_{\ell},x_{\ell+1})}\in P_{p}\;\forall\ell=0,\dots,L+M\}

3
Well-posedness and FEM-BEM formulation

In this section, we provide the proofs of Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
.

3.1
Poincaré inequalities

We now show the well-posedness of our variational formulations. The main ingredient is a Poincaré type estimate, which uses the following compactness result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain.
Assume
u_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
H^{1}(y^{\alpha},D)
and

\left\|\nabla u_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}\to 0
.
Then,
u_{n}\to 0
in
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)
.

Proof.

We can cover the Lipschitz domain
D
by a finite number of Lipschitz domains

D_{1},\dots,D_{m}
which are starshaped with respect to a ball,
see for example
[
Maz11
, Sect. 1.1.9, Lemma 1]
.
Thus, without loss of generality we may assume that
D
is
starshaped with respect to a ball.
With
c_{n}:=\int_{D}{u_{n}}
we compute

\displaystyle\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|u_{n}-c_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}^{2}+2(u_{n},c_{n})_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}-\left\|c_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\nabla u_{n}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}+2\left|(u_{n},c_{n})_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla u_{n}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}+2\left|c_{n}\right|\left|(u_{n},1)_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},D)}\right|\to 0,

where we used the Poincaré estimate of
[
NOS15
, Corollary 4.4]
and the assumed weak convergence.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
. Let
\mathcal{U}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
with
\int_{\Gamma}{{\left\llbracket\gamma\mathcal{U}\right\rrbracket}}ds_{x}=0
for almost every
y\in(0,\mathcal{Y})
.

1.

Let
0\leq\mu\leq 2
and
\mathcal{Y}=\infty
. There holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}|\mathcal{U}|^{2}\,dx}dy

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu}|\nabla\mathcal{U}|^{2}\,dx}dy

(3.1)

provided the right-hand side is finite.

2.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)
. There exists
\mu_{0}>0
such that for all
\mu\in[0,\mu_{0})
there holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}|\mathcal{U}|^{2}\,dx}dy

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu}|\nabla\mathcal{U}|^{2}\,dx}dy+|3-d|\|\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\right)

(3.2)

provided the right-hand side is finite.

Proof.

The estimates follow from techniques employed in
[
AGG94
, Theorem 3.3]
using a proof by contradiction.
In the first step, we show (
3.1
) (which essentially is covered by
[
AGG94
, Theorem 3.3]
, we only account for the additional weight
y^{\alpha}
) and (
3.2
) for functions vanishing inside a ball containing the origin. Finally, using a compactness argument this assumption is removed in the second step.

Step 1:

First, assume that
\mathcal{U}\equiv 0
on a sufficiently large (half) ball
B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and has compact support.

We employ spherical coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, chosen such that
y=r\cos(\varphi)
and
collect the remaining
d-1
angles into
\hat{\varphi}
.
Using
\rho^{\mu-2}=(1+\left|x\right|^{2}+y^{2})^{-(\mu-2)/2}<r^{\mu-2}
for
\mu\leq 2
, we calculate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rho^{\mu-2}|\mathcal{U}(x,y)|^{2}\,dxdy}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\hat{\varphi}}\int_{\varphi=-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}{r^{d+\alpha+\mu-2}\cos(\varphi)^{\alpha}|\mathcal{U}(x,y)|^{2}|J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})|\,dr\,d\varphi d\hat{\varphi}},

where we denoted by
J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})
the angular components of the Jacobian in the transformation theorem. Integration by parts in
r
and using the assumed support properties of
\mathcal{U}
gives

\int_{\hat{\varphi}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}{r^{d+\alpha+\mu-2}\cos(\varphi)^{\alpha}|\mathcal{U}(x,y)|^{2}|J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})|\,dr\,d\varphi d\hat{\varphi}}\\
\begin{aligned} &\lesssim\int_{\hat{\varphi}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}{r^{d+\alpha+\mu-1}\cos(\varphi)^{\alpha}|\mathcal{U}(x,y)||\nabla\mathcal{U}(x,y)||J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})|\,dr\,d\varphi d\hat{\varphi}}\\
&\lesssim\Big(\int_{\hat{\varphi}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}{r^{d+\alpha+\mu-2}\cos(\varphi)^{\alpha}\mathcal{U}(x,y)^{2}|J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})|\,dr\,d\varphi d\hat{\varphi}}\Big)^{1/2}\\
&\quad\times\Big(\int_{\hat{\varphi}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_{R}^{\infty}{r^{d+\alpha+\mu}\cos(\varphi)^{\alpha}\left|\nabla\mathcal{U}(x,y)\right|^{2}|J(\varphi,\hat{\varphi})|\,dr\,d\varphi d\hat{\varphi}}\Big)^{1/2}.\end{aligned}

Transforming back to
(x,y)
-variables and using
r^{\mu}\leq\rho^{\mu}
for
\mu\geq 0
this gives the desired bound.
By density, we can remove the requirement of compact support of
\mathcal{U}
.

Step 2:

In order to get rid of the requirement that
\mathcal{U}
vanishes on the ball
B_{R}(0)
, we
use a compactness argument. To keep the notation succinct we set
\mu=0
in the following, the general case
\mu>0
can be done with the exact same arguments.
Assume that (
3.1
) does not hold, i.e., there exists
a sequence
\mathcal{U}_{n}\in H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{U}_{n}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}=1,\qquad\int_{0}^{\infty}{y^{\alpha}\|\nabla\mathcal{U}_{n}(y)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)}\,dy}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Since
\mathcal{U}_{n}
is a bounded sequence in the Hilbert space
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))
, there exists a
weakly convergent subsequence (also denoted by
\mathcal{U}_{n}
) and we denote the weak limit by
\mathcal{U}
.

Since the seminorm is lower semicontinuous, we get
\left|\mathcal{U}\right|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}=0
.
A simple calculation (using polar coordinates, similar to the estimate above) shows that – as we are in half-space in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
d+1>2
and
\int_{\Gamma}{{\left\llbracket\gamma\mathcal{U}\right\rrbracket}ds_{x}}=0

– the space
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
does not contain piecewise constant functions except for
0
, which means that
\mathcal{U}=0
.

We now show strong convergence of the sequence to
\mathcal{U}=0
. To that end, fix a ball
B_{R}:=B_{R}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with sufficiently large
R
such that
\Omega\times\{0\}\subset B_{R}
and
consider a smooth cutoff function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi\equiv 1
on

B_{R}
and
\psi\equiv 0
on
B_{2R}
.
We thus decompose
\mathcal{U}_{n}
as

\mathcal{U}_{n}=\psi\mathcal{U}_{n}+(1-\psi)\mathcal{U}_{n}=:\mathcal{U}_{n}^{1}+\mathcal{U}_{n}^{2}.

From the compactness result of Lemma
3.1

applied to
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\cap B_{\widetilde{R}}
and
B_{\widetilde{R}}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
separately,
we deduce that

\mathcal{U}_{n}\to\mathcal{U}
in
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},B_{\widetilde{R}})
and thus
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{1}\to\psi\mathcal{U}=0
in
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},B_{\widetilde{R}})
on all bounded half balls
B_{\widetilde{R}}
with sufficiently large
\widetilde{R}
.

Since
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{2}
vanishes on
B_{R}
, we can apply step 1 of the proof
to determine:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{U}_{n}^{2}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\mathcal{U}_{n}^{2}\right|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|(1-\psi)\mathcal{U}_{n}\right|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},B_{2R}(0)\backslash\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}+\left|\mathcal{U}_{n}\right|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},B_{2R}(0)^{c}\backslash\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|\mathcal{U}_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},B_{2R}(0))}+\left|\mathcal{U}_{n}\right|_{H_{\rho}^{1}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}\to 0.

Overall, we get that
\mathcal{U}_{n}\to 0
in
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))
, which is a contradiction to the assumption
\left\|\mathcal{U}_{n}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\infty))}=1
for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Step 3:
Estimate (
3.2
) for the case
d=3
follows directly from multiplying a full-space Poincaré-inequality (see for example
[
AGG94
, Theorem 3.3]
for
\mu=0
and a similar calculation to step 1 for
0<\mu\leq 2
with polar coordinates only in
x
) applied only in
x
with
y^{\alpha}
and integrating over
(0,\mathcal{Y})
.

Step 4:
It remains to show (
3.2
) for
d=2
, which was also shown in
[
FR23
, Lem. 3.2]
. We write
\mathcal{U}(x,y)=\mathcal{U}(x,0)+\int_{0}^{y}\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(x,\tau)\;d\tau
, which gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}|\mathcal{U}|^{2}\;dxdy\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}|\mathcal{U}(x,0)|^{2}+y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}\Big(\int_{0}^{y}\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(x,\tau)\;d\tau\Big)^{2}\;dxdy.

Since
\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}dy\lesssim 1
for sufficiently small
\mu<\mu_{0}
with
\mu_{0}
depending only on
\alpha
, the first term on the left-hand side can be bounded by
C\left\|\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}
. For the second term, we employ a weighted Hardy-inequality, see e.g.
[
Muc72
]
, to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu-2}\Big(\int_{0}^{y}\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(x,\tau)\;d\tau\Big)^{2}\;dxdy\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\rho^{\mu}|\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}|^{2}\;dydx,

which shows the claimed inequality.
∎

We can now look at the well-posedness of our discrete problem.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
and
(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
On the interior domain
\Omega
, we integrate a standard Poincaré-like estimate
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}{\rho^{-2}|\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}|^{2}dx}dy}\leq\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}{|\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}|^{2}dx}dy}\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\int_{\Omega}{|\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}|^{2}dx}dy}.

(3.3)

By the conditions on
\gamma^{-}\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}
and
{\left\llbracket\gamma\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\rrbracket}
,
we observe that the function

\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}:=\begin{cases}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\quad\text{ in }\Omega\\
\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}},\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\overline{\Omega}\end{cases}
has a jump across
\partial\Omega
with
vanishing integral mean.
Applying Lemma
3.2
to
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, we get with (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\Omega}y^{\alpha}\left|\nabla\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right|^{2}dxdy+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}y^{\alpha}\left|\nabla\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right|^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\quad+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}y^{\alpha}\left|\nabla\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right|^{2}dxdy+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\Omega}y^{\alpha}\left|\nabla\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right|^{2}dxdy

\displaystyle\quad+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\left\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}+\left\|\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+s\|{\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2},

which shows coercivity.

In order to bound the right-hand side in (
2.4
), we can directly use the definition of the
\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
-norm together with
\operatorname*{supp}f\subset\Omega
for
s>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}}\;dx

\displaystyle\leq s^{-1}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}s\left\|\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq s^{-1}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}.

For
\mathcal{Y}=\infty
and
s=0
, which implies
d=3
by assumption, the trace estimate (
1.3
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}}\;dx

\displaystyle\leq\|{\rho(x,0)^{\beta}f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|{\rho(x,0)^{-\beta}\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\infty}}.

For the case
\mathcal{Y}<\infty
and
s=0
, we use a cut-off function
\chi
satisfying
\chi\equiv 1
on
(0,\mathcal{Y}/2)
,
\operatorname*{supp}\chi\subset(0,\mathcal{Y})
and
\left\|\nabla\chi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})}\lesssim\mathcal{Y}^{-1}
. As
\Omega
is bounded, this gives with the trace estimate
[
KM19
, Lem. 3.7]

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}}\;dx

\displaystyle\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\mathrm{tr_{0}}{(\chi\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\left\|\chi\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}+\left\|\nabla(\chi\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega})\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\Big(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{Y}}\Big)\left\|\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}+\left\|\nabla\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\frac{1}{\mathcal{Y}}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}\right\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Diagonalization

We now apply the diagonalization procedure of
[

]
to show that solutions of (

We recall that

is the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
from (

Lemma 3.3
.

Functions
(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
solve (

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\bullet}^{\mathcal{Y}}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{u_{j,\bullet}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)},

where

and

\displaystyle u_{j,\Omega}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x},\qquad u_{j,\star}\in H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma)\quad\forall j\geq 0

such that for all

\displaystyle\big(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u_{j,\Omega},\nabla v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big(\mu_{j}u_{j,\Omega},v\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\big<{\left\llbracket\partial_{\nu}u_{j,\star}\right\rrbracket},\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}v\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}=d_{\beta}\varphi_{j}(0)(f,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

(3.4a)

\displaystyle-\Delta u_{j,\star}+\mu_{j}u_{j,\star}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma$},

(3.4b)

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket\gamma u_{j,\star}\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j},\quad\gamma^{-}_{\Gamma}u_{j,\star}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}.

(3.4c)

Proof.

At first, we show unique solvability of (
3.4
). For that, we consider the weak formulation of (
3.4
) given by

(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u_{j,\Omega},\nabla v_{j,\Omega})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\mu_{j}u_{j,\Omega},v_{j,\Omega})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\nabla u_{j,\star},\nabla v_{j,\star})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)}+\mu_{j}(u_{j,\star},v_{j,\star})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
=d_{\beta}\varphi_{j}(0)(f,v_{j,\Omega})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(3.5)

The equivalence between the weak form and the strong form follows from standard arguments, and we refer to
[
LS09
, Sect.7]
.
Coercivity of the weak formulation in
H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)
is clear for
\mu_{j}>0
as
\mathfrak{A}
is positive definite.
For
\mu_{j}=0
, one can employ Poincaré estimates on
\Omega
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with weights) to obtain coercivity in
H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)
. Therefore, for each
j

(u_{j,\Omega},u_{j,\star})\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\times H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)\subset H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)
exists.

We now show that if the

solve (
3.4
)
then
\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}:=(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})
with

solves (
2.7
). By

.
We next look at the weak formulation of (

). First we focus on the
\star
-contribution. Taking
\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}=v_{j,\star}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)
with arbitrary

as test function, we compute

\displaystyle A^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma}(\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{N_{y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{u_{\ell,\star}(x)v_{j,\star}(x)dx}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\varphi^{\prime}_{\ell}(y)\varphi^{\prime}_{j}(y)\,dy}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{\nabla u_{\ell,\star}(x)\nabla v_{j,\star}(x)dx}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\varphi_{\ell}(y)\varphi_{j}(y)\,dy}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{u_{\ell,\star}(x)v_{j,\star}(x)\;dx}\cdot s\varphi_{\ell}(0)\varphi_{j}(0)

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{u_{j,\star}(x)v_{j,\star}(x)dx}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma}{\nabla u_{j,\star}(x)\nabla v_{j,\star}(x)dx}.

For the interior contribution, the same diagonalization procedure gives
for
\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}:=v_{j,\Omega}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)

\displaystyle A^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega}(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{A}\nabla u_{j,\Omega},\nabla v_{j,\Omega})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\mu_{j}u_{j,\Omega},v_{j,\Omega})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Summing up, and using the weak form (
3.5
) we get that

B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}})=d_{\beta}(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\qquad\text{for all }\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{v_{j,\Omega}\varphi_{j}},\sum_{j=0}^{N_{y}}{v_{j,\star}\varphi_{j}}\Big).

By density we can extend this equality to all test functions
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}
in the space
\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
and get (
2.7
). Since the bilinear form

B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive we get that the function

\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
thus constructed is the only solution to (
2.7
),
which establishes the stated equivalence.
∎

Proof of Theorem

The statement follows from Lemma

4
Error analysis

The key to the error analysis are the decay and regularity properties shown in
[
FR23
]
. In order to make the present paper more accessible, we summarize the key results of
[
FR23
]
in the following.

4.1
Decay and regularity

The solution to the truncated problem is in fact a weak solution to a Neumann problem.
Thus, in this section, we consider solutions
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
to the following truncated problem:

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\big(y^{\alpha}\mathfrak{A}_{x}\nabla{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{Y}}\big)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y})$},

(4.1a)

\displaystyle d_{\beta}^{-1}\partial_{\nu^{\alpha}}{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{Y}}+s\mathrm{tr_{0}}{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}$},

(4.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}{\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on $\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\mathcal{Y}\}$}.

(4.1c)

Then, the truncation error can be controlled via the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1

(Decay in
y
,
[
FR23
, Prop. 2.5]
)
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}>0
. Let
\mathcal{U}
solve (
1.4
) and
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
solve (
4.1
).
Let
\mu
be given by
\mu:=\begin{cases}1+\left|\alpha\right|&s>0\\
1+\alpha&s=0\end{cases}.

Then, the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}+s\|\mathrm{tr_{0}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\lesssim\mathcal{Y}^{-\mu}\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

The goal in the following is to employ
hp
-FEM in the extended variable
y
. Therefore, weighted analytic regularity estimates are the key to the a-priori analysis.

Proposition 4.2

(Regularity in
y
,
[
FR23
, Prop. 2.6]
)
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
and let

\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\mathcal{U}
solve (
4.1
). Then,
there exist constants
C,K>0

and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\big\|{y^{\ell-\varepsilon}\nabla\partial^{\ell}_{y}\mathcal{U}}\big\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}\leq CK^{\ell}\ell!\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

All constants are independent of
\ell,\mathcal{Y},
and
\mathcal{U}
.

Denoting by
L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y});X)
the Bochner spaces of square integrable functions (with respect to the weight
y^{\alpha}
) and values in the Banach space
X
, the regularity results of the previous Proposition can be captured by the solution being in some countably normed space. For constants
C,K>0
, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},X):=\Big\{\mathcal{V}\in C^{\infty}((0,\mathcal{Y});X):

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{V}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y});X)}<C,

\displaystyle\left\|y^{\ell+1-\varepsilon}\mathcal{V}^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y});X)}<CK^{\ell+1}(\ell+1)!\;\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Big\}.

Corollary 4.3
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty]
and let
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
solve (
4.1
).
Then, there are constants
C,K>0
such that there holds

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

(4.2)

4.2
Fully discrete analysis

In order to derive error bounds, we employ the reformulation in (
2.1
) together with the already established decay bounds for the truncation in
\mathcal{Y}
.

We will need two quasi-interpolation operators – one for the
x
-variables and one for the
y
-direction.
Their construction and properties are the subject of the next two lemmas.

Lemma 4.4

(Interpolation in
x
)
.

Let
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}\subset H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)
be finite dimensional and
\pi_{\Omega}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}
be a linear operator. Then,
there exists a linear operator
\Pi_{x}:L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{V}_{h}^{x}\times L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that the following properties hold
for
(u^{h},u_{\star}^{h}):=\Pi_{x}(u,u_{\star})
with
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,
u_{\star}\in H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma)
satisfying
u_{\star}|_{\Omega}=0
and
{\left\llbracket\gamma u_{\star}\right\rrbracket}=\gamma^{-}u
:

(i)

\gamma^{-}u^{h}_{\star}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}
;

(ii)

{\left\llbracket\gamma u_{\star}^{h}\right\rrbracket}=\gamma^{-}u^{h}
;

(iii)

If
\pi_{\Omega}
is stable in the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-norm, then

\displaystyle\|u^{h}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u^{h}_{\star}\|_{H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)}^{2}\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|u_{\star}\right\|_{H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)}^{2}.

If
\pi_{\Omega}
is stable in the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm, and
u_{\star}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
then

\displaystyle\|{u^{h}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|{u^{h}_{\star}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|u_{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

(iv)

There hold the approximation properties:

\displaystyle\|u^{h}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{\star}^{h}-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u-\pi_{\Omega}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

\displaystyle\|u^{h}-u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{\star}^{h}-u_{\star}\|_{H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u-\pi_{\Omega}u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

We note that a very similar operator is introduced in
[
MR17
, Lemma 4.3]
.
We define:

\displaystyle u^{h}:=\pi_{\Omega}u,\qquad u^{h}_{\star}:=u_{\star}+\delta,

where
\delta=-u_{\star}
in
\Omega
and
\delta:=-\mathcal{E}(u-\pi_{\Omega}u)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Omega
, where

\mathcal{E}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the Stein extension operator

[
Ste70
, Chapter VI.3]
that is stable both in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

By construction, we have (
i
), since
u^{h}_{\star}=0
in the interior.
Since
\gamma^{+}\mathcal{E}v=\gamma^{-}v
due to the extension property, we
get (
ii
) by

\llbracket{\gamma u_{\star}^{h}}\rrbracket=\gamma^{+}u_{\star}-\gamma^{+}\mathcal{E}(u-\pi_{\Omega}u)=\gamma^{-}u-\gamma^{-}u+\gamma^{-}\pi_{\Omega}u=\gamma^{-}u^{h}.

The stability estimates follow from the stability of the extension operator and the assumed stabilities of
\pi_{\Omega}
as

\displaystyle\|{u^{h}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|{u^{h}_{\star}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|u_{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\left\|\mathcal{E}(u-\pi_{\Omega}u)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|u_{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}+\left\|u-\pi_{\Omega}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\lesssim\left\|u\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\left\|u_{\star}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

The approximation property can be seen in a similar fashion using
\rho_{x}^{-2}<1

\displaystyle\|u^{h}-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|u_{\star}^{h}-u_{\star}\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi_{\Omega}u-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathcal{E}(u-\pi_{\Omega}u)\|_{L^{2}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi_{\Omega}u-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathcal{E}(u-\pi_{\Omega}u)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\lesssim\|\pi_{\Omega}u-u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

The
H^{1}
-estimates follows analogously.

Lemma 4.5

(Interpolation in
y
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
solve (
4.1
).
Let
\mathcal{T}_{y}
be a geometric grid on
(0,\mathcal{Y})
with mesh grading factor
\sigma
,
and
L
-refinement layers towards
0
as given by (
2.13
). Let
\varepsilon>0
be given by Proposition
4.2
.
Then, choosing
L=p
,
there exists an operator

\Pi_{y}:H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))\rightarrow H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))

such that
\Pi_{y}{\mathcal{U}}(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{S}^{p,1}(\mathcal{T}_{y})
for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and such that the following estimate holds:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{y^{\alpha}|\nabla(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{y}{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}})|^{2}dxdy}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-2\kappa p}\mathcal{Y}^{2\varepsilon}.

The constants
C,\kappa>0
are independent of
p,\mathcal{Y}
.

Proof.

We use the
hp
-interpolation operator from
[
BMN+19
, Sec. 5.5.1]
for
\Pi_{y}
. This operator is constructed on a geometric mesh in an element-by-element way. On the first element a linear interpolant in
\sigma^{L}/2
and
\sigma^{L}
is used, while the remaining elements are mapped to the reference element, on which a polynomial approximation operator that has exponential convergence properties (in the polynomial degree) for analytic functions is used.

For the operator on the reference element, we take the Babŭska-Szabó polynomial approximation operator
\widehat{\Pi}_{p}
on
(-1,1)
defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Pi}_{p}v(y):=v(-1)+\int_{-1}^{y}\Pi^{L^{2}}_{p-1}v^{\prime}(t)dt,

where
\Pi^{L^{2}}_{p-1}:L^{2}(-1,1)\rightarrow P_{p-1}
denotes the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection, see e.g.
[
AM15

. By construction, this operator has the commutator property

\displaystyle(\widehat{\Pi}_{p}v)^{\prime}=\Pi^{L^{2}}_{p-1}v^{\prime}.

Regularity in countably normed spaces gives exponential error bounds for
\Pi_{y}
, see
[
BMN+19
, Lem. 11]
. In fact, for functions in
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

Consequently, we can employ Proposition
4.2
to obtain
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and together with
[
BMN+19
, Lem. 11(i)]
this gives the error estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\cdot,y)-\Pi_{y}{\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\cdot,y)}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}dy}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-2\kappa p}\mathcal{Y}^{2\varepsilon}

for a constant
\kappa>0
. Interchanging
\Pi_{y}
and
\nabla_{x}
gives the estimate for the
x
-derivatives.

For the
y
-derivatives the situation is a bit more involved, as the same argument can not be made as
\Pi_{y}
and
\partial_{y}
do not commute.

[
BMN+19
, Lem. 11(ii)]
gives an exponentially convergent error bound for the

-derivative provided
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{2}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
(essentially meaning
\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
).
However, in our setting, the requirement
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
does not hold.
Nonetheless, we have Corollary
4.3
giving
\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathcal{B}^{1}_{\varepsilon,0}(C,K;\mathcal{Y},L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, which is enough to regain the exponential estimate as seen in the following.

On the first element
(0,\sigma^{L})\in\mathcal{T}_{y}
, the definition of the piecewise linear interpolation gives

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\Pi_{y}v(y)=\frac{v(\sigma^{L})-v(\sigma^{L}/2)}{\sigma^{L}/2}=\frac{2}{\sigma^{L}}\int_{\sigma^{L}/2}^{\sigma^{L}}\partial_{y}v(y)dy,

which is nothing else than the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection of
\partial_{y}v
on
(\sigma^{L}/2,\sigma^{L})
.
By choice of the Babŭska-Szabó operator and denoting by
\widetilde{\Pi}^{L^{2}}_{p-1}
the mapped
L^{2}
-projection onto an element in
\mathcal{T}_{y}
, we have due to the commutator property and the preceding discussion

\displaystyle\partial_{y}(\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}})|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{i}}=\widetilde{\Pi}^{L^{2}}_{p-1}\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{i}}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{i})\qquad\forall K_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{y}

since
\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{i})
, which implies that
\partial_{y}\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
. The error estimate for the
y
-derivative follows from scaling arguments.
More precisely, we decompose

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{y}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}})\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}=\sum_{K_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{y}}\left\|\partial_{y}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}})\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{i})}^{2},

where
K_{i}=(x_{i},x_{i+1})
.
Using a Hardy inequality, one obtains a bound for the approximation error on the first element using second derivatives only; see
[
BMN+19
, Lem. 15]
. Together with a scaling argument this leads to

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{y}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}})\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\sigma^{L}))}^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{\varepsilon L}\left\|\partial_{yy}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha+2-2\varepsilon},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\sigma^{L}))}^{2}.

By Corollary
4.3
we can bound the right-hand side.
For the remaining elements, we employ a scaling argument from

[
AM15
, Thm. 3.13]
.
Denoting by
h_{K_{i}}
the diameter of
K_{i}
, we infer
y\sim h_{K_{i}}
on
K_{i}
for
i>0
.
For any univariate function
v
satisfying
\left\|y^{\ell-\varepsilon}v^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},(0,\mathcal{Y}))}<CK^{\ell}\ell!
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
there holds

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{v}^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{h_{K_{i}}}h_{K_{i}}^{2(\ell+1)}\left\|v^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{L^{2}(K_{i})}^{2}\lesssim h_{K_{i}}^{2\varepsilon-\alpha+1}\left\|y^{\ell-\varepsilon}v^{(\ell+1)}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},K_{i})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h_{K_{i}}^{2\varepsilon-\alpha+1}K^{\ell}\ell!,

(4.3)

where
\widehat{v}
is the pull-back of
v
to the reference element. The exponential approximation properties of the Babŭska-Szabó polynomial approximation operator then provides

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{v}-\widehat{\Pi}_{p}\widehat{v}\right\|_{H^{1}(-1,1)}^{2}\lesssim h_{K_{i}}^{2\varepsilon-\alpha+1}e^{-\kappa p}.

(4.4)

Together with

\displaystyle\left\|(v-\Pi_{y}v)^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(y^{\alpha},K_{i})}^{2}\lesssim h_{K_{i}}^{\alpha-1}\left\|(\widehat{v}-\widehat{\Pi}_{p}\widehat{v})^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2},

we can employ (
4.4
) for
v(y)=\mathcal{U}(y,\cdot)
and square integrate over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, noting that (
4.2
) holds due to Corollary
4.3
.
Summing over
i
and using
\sum_{i}h_{K_{i}}^{2\varepsilon}\lesssim\mathcal{Y}^{2\varepsilon}
shows the claimed estimate.

Finally, to show that the operator does indeed map to
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
, we note that
by the previous considerations we have
\partial_{y}\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}\in L^{2}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))

as well as
\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}(\cdot,y)=\mathcal{U}(\cdot,y)\in L_{\rho_{x}}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for certain values
y\in(0,\mathcal{Y})

where it is interpolatory. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, this is sufficient to show that
\Pi_{y}\mathcal{U}\in L^{2}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))
.
∎

We can now define an interpolation operator acting on both
x
and
y
in a tensor product fashion.
In order to keep notation compact, we write
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
for the
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)
-norm and

\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}}
for the
H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\Gamma)
-norm.

Lemma 4.6

(Tensor approximation)
.

Fix
\mathcal{Y}\in(0,\infty)
and let
\mathcal{U}=(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
. Define
\Pi(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star}):=\Pi_{x}\otimes\Pi_{y}(\mathcal{U}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
with the operators
\Pi_{x}
from Lemma
4.4
and
\Pi_{y}
from Lemma
4.5
. Assume that the operator
\pi_{\Omega}
in the definition of
\Pi_{x}
is both
L^{2}
- and
H^{1}
-stable.
Then, the following approximation estimate holds

\displaystyle\Big\|\mathcal{U}-\Pi\mathcal{U}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\Big(\big\|\nabla(1-\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}(y)\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\big\|\nabla(1-\pi_{\Omega})\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(y)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)\,dy.}

Proof.

By the Poincaré inequality (
3.2
) and the trace inequality (
1.3
), we only have to estimate the gradient norms. We start with the
x
-derivatives. Employing the
H^{1}
-stability
and approximation properties of
\Pi_{x}

from Lemma
4.4
(
iii
) and (
iv
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big\|\nabla_{x}(\mathcal{U}-\Pi\mathcal{U})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|\nabla_{x}\mathcal{U}-\nabla_{x}(\Pi_{x}\otimes I)\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|\nabla_{x}(\Pi_{x}\otimes I)\mathcal{U}-\nabla_{x}(\Pi_{x}\otimes\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big\|(I-\pi_{\Omega})\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(y)\big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}dy+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|(I-\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}(y)\big\|_{H^{1}_{\rho_{x}}}^{2}\,dy}.

Employing again Poincaré inequalities, we can reduce the right-hand side to norms of derivatives only.
For the
y
-derivative, we proceed similarly using the
L^{2}
- stability and approximation properties of
\Pi_{x}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big\|\partial_{y}(\mathcal{U}-\Pi\mathcal{U})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}-\partial_{y}(\Pi_{x}\otimes I)\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}dy

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|\partial_{y}(\Pi_{x}\otimes I)\mathcal{U}-\partial_{y}(\Pi_{x}\otimes\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|(1-\pi_{\Omega})\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}(y)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+y^{\alpha}\big\|\partial_{y}(I-\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}(y)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,dy},

which finishes the proof.
∎

In order to obtain a best-approximation estimate for the semi-discretization, we observe that the difference
\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{h}
satisfies some form of Galerkin orthogonality.

Lemma 4.7

(Galerkin orthogonality)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}>0
,
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}
be the solution of (
2.4
)
and
\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
solve (
2.7
).
Then, for all
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
and
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}
, there holds

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{h},\mathcal{V}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{h})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big<{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}{\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,dy}.

Proof.

Compared to “standard” Galerkin orthogonality, we observe
that
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}
is not an admissible
test function in (
2.4
) due to the weak condition of
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}

compared to
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in(H^{-1/2}(\Gamma))^{\circ}=\{0\}
. Also,
if we work in the
H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})
-setting (i.e. working with global functions instead of pairs),
the test function
\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}\chi_{\Omega}+\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\chi_{\Omega^{c}}
is not continuous along
\Gamma

due to a possible jump of size
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}
. However, if we use the pointwise equation
(
4.1
) and integrate back by parts, we get that

\displaystyle B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big<{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket},\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}{\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,dy}.

Since
\langle{\lambda_{h},\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
vanishes due to the requirement in
\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in(\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda})^{\circ}
,
we can subtract such a term from the right-hand side without changing the equality, which shows the
stated Galerkin orthogonality.
∎

Finally, we are in position to show our main result, Theorem
2.7
, by combining the decay estimate with the previous two lemmas.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

We start with the triangle inequality

\|\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\leq\|\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}+\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}.

For the first term, we use the decay properties of Proposition
4.1
to obtain

\|\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}\lesssim\|\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}-\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{H^{1}(y^{\alpha},\Omega\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}+\|\mathcal{U}_{\star}-\mathcal{U}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{H^{1}_{\rho}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Gamma\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}\lesssim\mathcal{Y}^{-\mu/2}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

For the second term, we employ the coercivity of Theorem
2.2
,
the Galerkin orthogonality of Lemma
4.7
,
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}|_{\Omega}=0
, and a trace inequality for
\Omega
, which gives for arbitrary
\lambda_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{h}^{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=(\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=B^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}})+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\big<{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h},\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}(\mathcal{U}_{h,\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\big>_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,dy}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}\|\gamma_{\Gamma}^{-}(\mathcal{U}_{h,\star}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{\star}^{\mathcal{Y}})\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}dy}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}dy}+\varepsilon\|\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2\varepsilon\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}+(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-1})\|{\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}dy}.

Taking
\varepsilon
sufficiently small and absorbing the first term in the left-hand side gives

\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}\lesssim\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}{y^{\alpha}\|{\llbracket\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}\rrbracket}-\lambda_{h}\|^{2}_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}dy}.

As
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
was arbitrary, we can take
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}=\Pi(\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\Omega},\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star})\in\mathbb{H}_{h,\mathcal{Y}}
with the operator
\Pi
of Lemma
4.6
. Then, Lemma
4.6
together with the approximation properties of the
hp
-interpolation in
\mathcal{Y}
gives

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}-\Pi\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\Big(\big\|\nabla(1-\Pi_{y})\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\big\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\big\|\nabla(1-\pi_{\Omega})\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)\,dy

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{Y}^{2\varepsilon}e^{-2\kappa p}+\int_{0}^{\mathcal{Y}}y^{\alpha}\big\|\nabla(1-\pi_{\Omega})\mathcal{U}_{\Omega}^{\mathcal{Y}}(y)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,dy.

Combining all estimates gives the stated result.
∎

Finally, we present the proof of Corollary
2.9
that gives first order convergence for a specific choice of discrete spaces.

Proof of Corollary
2.9
.

Employing
[
FR23
, Pro. 2.8]
– which with the same techniques also holds for
\mathcal{Y}<\infty
and a constant independent of
\mathcal{Y}
– together with the assumptions on
\Omega,\mathfrak{A},
and
f
, we obtain control of second order
x
-derivatives of
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}
.

As
\lambda:=\partial_{\nu}^{+}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}
, it is piecewise smooth, depending on the
regularity of
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}
.
For
m=0,1
, denoting by
\pi_{L^{2}}
the
L^{2}
-projection onto
\mathcal{S}^{0,0}(\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma})
and using a trace estimate, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\lambda(y)-\lambda_{h}(y)\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{sauter_schwab}{}{}, \tiny Thm\penalty\ 4.1.33]}}}}{{\lesssim}}\!\!h^{1/2}\|\lambda(y)-\pi_{L^{2}}\lambda(y)\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{sauter_schwab}{}{}, \tiny Prop\penalty\ 4.1.31]}}}}{{\lesssim}}\!\!h^{1/2}h^{m}\sum_{K\in\mathcal{T}_{\Gamma}}{\|\lambda(y)\|_{H^{m}(K)}^{2}}\lesssim h^{1/2}h^{m}\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}(y)\|_{H^{m+3/2}(B_{R}(0)\backslash\Gamma)}^{2}.

Interpolating between
m=0
and
m=1
gives

\displaystyle\|\lambda(y)-\lambda_{h}(y)\|_{H^{-1/2}(\Gamma)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim h\|\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{\star}(y)\|_{H^{2}(B_{R}(0)\backslash\Gamma)}^{2}.

Multiplying with
y^{\alpha}
and integrating with respect to
y
then controls the second term on the right-hand side of Theorem
2.7
.
For the first term, the approximation properties of the Scott-Zhang projection together with control of the second order
x
-derivatives gives first order convergence in
h
. Finally, the last two terms in Theorem
2.7
can also be bounded by
Ch
by choice of
\mathcal{Y}
and
p
.
∎

5
Numerics

In this section, we present two numerical examples to underline the a-priori estimates of Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
2.9
.
As previously already mentioned, a nice feature of our numerical scheme is that software packages developed for integer order differential operators can be employed directly.
As such, we implement our method based on a coupling of the libraries NGSolve (
[
Sch21
]
, for the FEM-part) and Bempp-cl (
[
BS21
]
, for the BEM-part) libraries.

In order to validate our numerical method, we consider the case
s=0

and the standard fractional Laplacian, i.e.,
\mathfrak{A}=I
.
In this case a representation formula is available
from
[
CS07
]
. In fact, the fundamental solution for the fractional Laplacian is given by

\displaystyle\Psi(x):=\frac{C_{d,\beta}}{\left|x\right|^{d-2\beta}}\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{0\},\;d\neq 2\beta

with
C_{d,\beta}:=\frac{\Gamma(d/2-\beta)}{2^{2\beta}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\beta)}
.
Thus, for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we can write

u(x)=C_{d,\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\frac{f(y)}{\left|x-y\right|^{d-2\beta}}\;dy}.

We then calculate
u(x)
at random sampling points
x_{j}
using spherical coordinates
and Gauss-Jacobi numerical integration to
deal with the singularity at
r=|x-x_{j}|=0
as well as standard Gauss-Quadrature for the other coordinate directions.

In order to compute the energy error,
we compute the energy differences.
For standard FEM with bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and right-hand side
f(\cdot)
, it is well known that one can compute the energy error by the identity
\|u-u_{h}\|^{2}_{E}=a(u,u)-a(u_{h},u_{h})=f(u)-f(u_{h}).

Due to the more complicated form of our method, most notably the presence of the cutoff error, such an identity does not hold exactly. Nevertheless, we expect the following identity to hold approximately

\|\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{U}_{h}^{\mathcal{Y}}\|_{H^{1}(y^{\alpha},\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\mathcal{Y}))}^{2}\approx(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We now further replace the unknown value
(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
by the
extrapolation from
(f,\mathrm{tr_{0}}\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for different refinements
using Aitken’s
\Delta^{2}
-method. This will be our approximation of the true energy error.
For the
L^{2}
-error, we use the approximation
\mathcal{U}^{\mathcal{Y}}_{h}
on the finest grid as our standin
for the exact solution compare it to the other approximations
by computing the
L^{2}
-difference of the traces at
y=0
using Gauss quadrature.

For the geometry, we used the unit cube
\Omega:=[-1,1]^{3}
.
In the bounded domain
\Omega
, we use piecewise linear Lagrangian finite elements on a quasi-uniform mesh of maximal mesh width
h
.
