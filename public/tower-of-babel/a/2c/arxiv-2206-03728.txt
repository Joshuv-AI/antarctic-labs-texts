Exactly solvable quadratic differential equation systems through generalized inversion
Ádám Bácsi, Albert Tihamér Kocsis

Abstract
We study the autonomous systems of quadratic differential equations of the form x ˙ i ​ ( t ) = 𝐱 ​ ( t ) T ​ 𝐀 i ​ 𝐱 ​ ( t ) + 𝐯 i T ​ 𝐱 ​ ( t ) \dot{x}_{i}(t)=\mathbf{x}(t)^{T}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{x}(t)+\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}(t) with 𝐱 ⁡ ( t ) = ( x 1 ​ ( t ) , x 2 ​ ( t ) , … , x i ​ ( t ) , … ) \mathbf{x}(t)=(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),\dots,x_{i}(t),\dots) which, in general, cannot be solved exactly. In the present paper, we present a subclass of analytically solvable quadratic systems, whose solution is realized through a multi-dimensional generalization of the inversion which transforms a quadratic system into a linear system. We provide a constructive algorithm which, on one hand, decides whether the system of differential equations is analytically solvable with the inversion transformation and, on the other hand, provides the solution. The presented results apply for arbitrary, finite number of variables.



1
Introduction

Linear, ordinary, autonomous systems of differential equations are celebrated for their analytical integrability which makes them excellent tools in almost all fields of science. This solvability breaks down when entering the realm of non-linear ordinary differential equation systems where no general solution method exists. In most cases, non-linear systems are handled with sophisticated numerical methods such as Runge-Kutta schemes
[
1
]
.

Despite of the great accuracy of numerical methods, it is also desirable to find analytical techniques to solve non-linear systems. Beside the inherent beauty of exact solutions, they can also play the role of benchmark for numerical methods. In this work, we focus on first-order, quadratic, autonomous differential equation systems (QDEs) which emerge in various research fields including models of population dynamics
[
2
]
, fluid dynamics
[
3
]
, control systems
[
4
]
and even quantum dynamics
[
5
]
. Classification of two-variable quadratic systems have been studied extensively
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

There exists no general method to find analytical solution of QDEs. Except for the special case, when the number of variables is just one. By denoting this variable with
x(t)
, the quadratic differential equation is written as

\displaystyle\dot{x}=ax^{2}+vx

(1)

with
a\neq 0
and
v
being constants. An additional constant term on the right-handside could be eliminated by shifting
x(t)
.
By introducing
y(t)=x(t)^{-1}
, the equation is transformed to the linear equation of

\displaystyle\dot{y}=-a-vy

(2)

and, hence, the quadratic differential equation (
1
) becomes exactly solvable.

In this paper, we investigate a class of QDEs with more than one variable which can be solved exactly by transforming the QDE to a linear differential equation system (LDE). The transformation is realized through a multi-variable extension of
y(t)=x(t)^{-1}
, similar to the generalized spherical inversions presented in Refs.
[
10
,
11
]
.

2
Generalized inversion transformation

Let us consider some differentiable functions
x_{1}(t)
,
x_{2}(t)
, …,
x_{n}(t)
whose dynamics is governed by the QDE

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}

(3)

where
\mathbf{A}_{i}
are
n\times n
matrices which are chosen to be symmetric and
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}
are row vectors of
n
components. The entries of
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
are independent from
t
and are assumed to be real without loss of generality
1
1

1

In case of complex entries, the differential equations can be split up to their real and imaginary parts.
. Our goal is to determine if the variables
x_{i}(t)
can be transformed with a multivariable variant of the inversion in such a way that the QDE (
3
) is transformed to an LDE. The multivariable inversion is defined as

\displaystyle y_{i}(t)=\frac{x_{i}(t)}{\mathbf{x}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}(t)}

(4)

with some symmetric matrix
\mathbf{B}
which does not depend on time. Note that we use the same
\mathbf{B}
matrix for all
i
. This ensures that the transformation is easily inverted by

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=\frac{y_{i}(t)}{\mathbf{y}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}(t)}\,.

(5)

The transformations (
4
) and (
5
) will henceforth be referred to as
B
-transformation and inverse
B
-transformation, respectively.
The variables
y_{i}(t)
are expected to obey the linear differential equations

\displaystyle\dot{y_{i}}=\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{y}+w_{i}\,.

(6)

with time-independent row vectors
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}
and the scalars
w_{i}
. Since Eq. (
6
) is exactly solvable for arbitrary initial conditions, the QDE can also be solved by applying the inverse
B
-transformation (
5
).
The goal of the present paper is to provide a procedure based on which one can decide whether a given QDE, defined through
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and
\mathbf{v}_{i}
, can be
B
-transformed to an LDE.

To start our study, let us calculate the time derivative of Eq. (
5
)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=\frac{\dot{y}_{i}}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}}-\frac{y_{i}}{\left(\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}\right)^{2}}\big(\dot{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{B}\dot{\mathbf{y}}\big)\,.

(7)

Substituting the LDE differential equations (
6
) and then transforming all
y_{i}
variables back to
x_{i}
by means of Eq. (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{x}+w_{i}\left(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\right)-\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}x_{i}-x_{i}\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}-x_{i}\frac{\mathbf{x}^{T}\left(\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}\right)\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}}

(8)

with the matrix
\mathbf{M}
built from the row vectors
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}
and the vector
\mathbf{w}
consisting of the constants
w_{i}
.

By comparing Eq. (
8
) with the original QDE (
3
), one can note that Eq. (
8
) is not necessarily a quadratic differential equation. The last term has an
\mathbf{x}
-dependent denominator and, hence, is neither quadratic nor linear in general. This term scales with
\mathbf{x}
as a linear term but becomes an actual linear term only if

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{M}=-\lambda\mathbf{B}

(9)

holds with some scalar
-\lambda
. The minus sign is chosen for later convenience. The condition (
9
) means that
\mathbf{B}
must be the eigenmatrix
[
12
]
of
\mathbf{M}
with the eigenvalue of
-\lambda
. In this case, the differential equations (
8
) become

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{i}=\left(\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}+\lambda\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\right)\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\left(w_{i}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{w}\circ\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\right)-\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}\circ\mathbf{w}^{T}\right)\mathbf{B}\right)\mathbf{x}

(10)

where
\circ
denotes dyadic product and
\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}
is the unit row vector with
1
in the
i
th entry and zero otherwise.
The equation is already a quadratic differential equation containing quadratic and linear terms in
\mathbf{x}
.

Comparing the linear terms of Eqs. (
10
) and (
3
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}

(11)

where
\mathbf{V}
is constructed from the row vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}

and
\mathbf{I}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.
Substituting (
11
) into the condition (
9
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}=\lambda\mathbf{B}

(12)

indicating that the matrix
\mathbf{B}
must be a symmetric eigenmatrix of
\mathbf{V}
as well.

Beside the linear terms, the quadratic terms of Eq. (
10
) and (
3
) must also equal. Since both
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and the kernel in the quadratic terms in Eq. (
10
) are symmetric, they must equal

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{i}=w_{i}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}\left(\mathbf{w}\circ\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\right)-\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}\circ\mathbf{w}^{T}\right)\mathbf{B}

(13)

for all
i
.
Note that the second and third terms are matrices full of zeros except for the
i
th row and
i
th column, respectively. Therefore, omitting the
i
th row and column in the equation leads to the proportionality condition

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}^{i}=w_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{i}

(14)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{i}^{i}
(
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{i}
) is the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix which is obtained from
\mathbf{A}_{i}
(
\mathbf{B}
) by skipping the
i
th row and column. For the
i
th column of Eq. (
13
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{i}^{i}\equiv\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}=w_{i}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}-\left(\mathbf{e}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{w}\right)\mathbf{e}_{i}\,.

(15)

To summarize, the QDE as given in Eq. (
3
) can be transformed with a
B
-transformation to an LDE if we find a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{B}
fulfilling Eq. (
12
) with some constant
\lambda
and, at the same time, obeying Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) with some constants
w_{i}
.
The linear terms of the LDE can be calculated by expressing
\mathbf{V}
from Eq. (
11
).

3
Algorithm

Let us provide a detailed description of the procedure based on the findings of the previous section.
The starting point of the algorithm is Eq. (
3
) determined by the matrices
\mathbf{A}_{i}
and vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.

1.

As a first step, one has to build up the matrix
\mathbf{V}
from the row vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{T}
and find all symmetric eigenmatrices based on
\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}=\lambda\mathbf{B}
.
Note that the eigenmatrices are definite only up to an overall multiplication factor similarly to the case of usual eigenvectors.

To obtain the eigenmatrices, one may calculate the vector-eigenvalues
s_{j}
of
\mathbf{V}^{T}
and the corresponding right-handside eigenvectors
\mathbf{r}_{j}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}^{T}\mathbf{r}_{j}=s_{j}\mathbf{r}_{j}
. The sum of the geometric multiplicity of all eigenvalues is denoted by
N
. The matrix-eigenvalues of
\mathbf{V}
are given by
s_{1}+s_{1}
,
s_{1}+s_{2}
, …,
s_{1}+s_{n}
,
s_{2}+s_{2}
,
s_{2}+s_{3}
, …,
s_{N}+s_{N}
and the eigenmatrix corresponding to
s_{j}+s_{m}
is
\mathbf{P}_{jm}=\left(\mathbf{r}_{j}\circ\mathbf{r}_{m}^{T}+\mathbf{r}_{m}\circ\mathbf{r}_{j}^{T}\right)/2
. Note that the number of symmetric eigenmatrices is
N(N+1)/2
which is utmost
n(n+1)/2
when
n
eigenvectors are found.

In case of degenerate matrix eigenvalues
\lambda
, all linear combinations within the subspace are potential
\mathbf{B}
matrices. For an example, see Sec.
5
.

2.

For each potential eigenmatrix
\mathbf{B}
, the proportionality condition (
14
) must be checked for all
i
with some constants
w_{i}
,
which may also be zero.

If Eq. (
14
) cannot be fulfilled with any potential
\mathbf{B}
matrix, then the QDE is not solvable by
B
-transforming to an LDE.
If Eq. (
14
) is satisfied with a
\mathbf{B}
matrix and some
w_{i}
constants, one may proceed to Step 3.

3.

For the potential pairs of
\mathbf{B}
and
\mathbf{w}
surviving Step 2, one has to check Eq. (
15
) for all
i
.
If it holds true for all
i
, then the QDE (
3
) can be transformed to an LDE of the form of Eq. (
6
) where the constants
w_{i}
are the coefficients of proportionality found in Eq. (
14
) in Step 2 and the vectors
\mathbf{m}_{i}^{T}
are the row vectors of
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{V}-\lambda\mathbf{I}

with
\lambda
the eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenmatrix
\mathbf{B}
.

4.

The solution of the QDE can be obtained by solving first the linear equation (
6
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}(t)=e^{\mathbf{M}t}\mathbf{y}_{0}+\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)

(16)

where
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}/(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0})
is the initial condition with
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}(t=0)
and
\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)=\left(e^{\mathbf{M}t}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{w}
if
\mathbf{M}
is invertible. If
\mathbf{M}
is not invertible, the function
\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)
has linear time-dependence in the nullspace of
\mathbf{M}
. Using the inverse
B
transformation, the solution of the QDE is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)=\frac{e^{\mathbf{M}t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\left(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)}{e^{-\lambda t}+2\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}e^{\mathbf{M}t}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\left(\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)\left(\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)\right)}\,.

(17)

Before presenting examples, let us make a remark on the region where the
B
-transformation is undefined, i.e., where
\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}=0
. This region can be any quadratic hypersurface depending on the specific structure of the
\mathbf{B}
matrix. The region also involves the nullspace of
\mathbf{B}
, i.e., the points where
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}=0
. In Sec.
4
, the hypersurface contains two straight lines while in Sec.
5
, it consists of two cone-shaped surfaces. This raises the question how the system behaves if the initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}
is an element of the region, i.e.
\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
.

First, one can prove that if the initial condition of the QDE is on the hypersurface, the dynamics is constrained to the hypersurface for the whole time-evolution. To justify this, we define
b(t)=\mathbf{x}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}(t)
whose dynamics is derived from the quadratic equations (
3
) as
\dot{b}=b\left(\lambda-2\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}\right)
. If the initial condition is on the hypersurface, i.e.,
b(0)=0
, then the differential equation solves to
b(t)=0
.

Second, it can also be shown that inside the hypersurface, the quadratic differential equation is analytically solvable and the solution is obtained simply by taking
\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
in Eq. (
17
) which reads as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t)=\frac{e^{\mathbf{M}t}\mathbf{x}_{0}}{e^{-\lambda t}+2\mathbf{y}_{p}(t)^{T}\mathbf{B}e^{\mathbf{M}t}\mathbf{x}_{0}}\,.

(18)

Note that (
18
) does not obviously solve the quadratic differential equation because it was derived from Eq. (
17
) which was computed through the
B
transformation. However, by substituting (
18
) directly into (
3
) and taking advantage of
\mathbf{x}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x}_{0}=0
and the properties of
\mathbf{B}
, one can prove that the QDE is solved indeed by (
18
).
Hence, if a QDE is exactly solvable by a
B
-transformation, the integrability is preserved also in the region where the generalized inversion is undefined.

6
Conclusion

We studied the system of quadratic differential equations of the form of Eq. (
3
). Although these differential equation systems cannot be solved in general, we have presented a procedure by means of which some quadratic systems can be solved analytically. The solution is performed through the
B
-transformation of
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}/(\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})
which is a multi-dimensional generalization of the inversion
y=x^{-1}
.

In Sec.
3
, we have described the algorithm which allows one to decide whether a quadratic system can be transformed with a
B
-transformation to a linear differential equation system. If so, the algorithm also yields the linear system which is exactly solvable. The algorithm works for arbitrary number of variables.
