The worldline formalism in strong-field QED
Christian Schubert

Abstract
The worldline formalism provides an alternative to Feynman diagrams that has been found particularly useful for external-field calculations in quantum electrodynamics. Here I summarize its present range of applications, which includes Schwinger pair creation, photon splitting in constant fields and plane-wave backgrounds, as well as non-linear Compton scattering in constant fields.



1
The trouble with strong-field QED

Since strong background fields must be treated non-perturbatively, QED calculations in the presence of such fields
are usually done using Feynman rules involving the exact electron propagator in the field, and on-shell polarization eigenstates in the field.
Of course, a closed-form expression for this propagator is available only for a limited number of field configurations.
And even in these fortuitous cases, in practice those generalized Feynman rules lead to exceedingly cumbersome calculations.
In the words of the authors of a well-known textbook
[
1
]
,
“it appears doubtful to us that the formulation of spinor electrodynamics employing the usual Dirac algebra is appropriate for higher-order external-field calculations.
Even computer-aided algebraic-manipulation programs seem to be of limited use”.

Presently, the main alternative to the Feynman diagram approach is the “worldline formalism”. In this talk, I give a short review of its present state of development
for QED calculations involving constant and plane-wave fields, as well as for Schwinger pair creation in general electric fields. However, let us start with some history.

As far as QED is concerned, the starting point is the following path-integral representation of the
x
-space propagator of a relativistic charged scalar particle in
an external field

\displaystyle D^{xx^{\prime}}[A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}|\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,{\rm exp}\Bigl[-T(-(\partial+ieA)^{2}+m^{2})\Bigr]|x\rangle

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,\,{\rm e}^{-m^{2}T}\int_{x(0)=x}^{x(T)=x^{\prime}}{\cal D}x(\tau)\,e^{-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\bigl({1\over 4}\dot{x}^{2}+ie\dot{x}\cdot A(x(\tau))\bigr)}

Here the path integral is over all trajectories running in the fixed proper-time
T
from
x
to
x^{\prime}
in (euclidean) spacetime.
Expanding the field in
N
plane waves,

\displaystyle A^{\mu}(x(\tau))=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\varepsilon_{i}^{\mu}\,{\rm e}^{ik_{i}\cdot x(\tau)}

(2)

and Fourier transforming the endpoints one gets a path-integral representation of the “
N
-photon-dressed propagator”
in momentum space, Fig.
1
, where summation over all permutations of the photons is understood.

Similarly, one has the following representation for the one-loop effective action in scalar QED:

\displaystyle\Gamma[A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\rm{\rm Tr}\,}{\rm ln}\Bigl[-(\partial+ieA)^{2}+m^{2}\Bigr]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dT}{T}\,{\rm Tr}\,{\rm exp}\Bigl[-T(-(\partial+ieA)^{2}+m^{2})\Bigr]

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dT}{T}\,\,{\rm e}^{-m^{2}T}\int_{x(0)=x(T)}{\cal D}x(\tau)\,e^{-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\bigl({1\over 4}\dot{x}^{2}+ie\dot{x}\cdot A(x(\tau))\bigr)}

The path integral is now over all closed loops with periodicity

The plane-wave ansatz (

leads to a path-integral representation of the one-loop

In
[
3
]
, Feynman found a generalization of (
3
) to the spinor QED case where the electron spin is
implemented simply by adding, under the path integral, the “spin factor”
{\rm Spin}[x,A]
,

\displaystyle{\rm Spin}[x,A]={\rm tr}_{\Gamma}{\cal P}\exp\Biggl[{{i\over 4}e\,[\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}]\int_{0}^{T}d\tau F_{\mu\nu}(x(\tau))}\Biggr]

(4)

Here
\cal P
denotes path ordering which will now have to be imposed for generic

. To a large extent, this spoils
the elegance as well as practical usefulness of the formalism (and may have been a reason for Feynman not to pursue
further this formalism). Following the advent of Grassmann variables in the sixties, Fradkin
[
13
]
showed that
it can be avoided replacing the Feynman spin factor by the following path integral,

\displaystyle{\rm Spin}[x,A]=\int{\cal D}\psi(\tau)\,\exp\Biggl[-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\Biggl({1\over 2}\psi\cdot\dot{\psi}-ie\psi^{\mu}F_{{\mu\nu}}\psi^{\nu}\Biggr)\Biggr]

(5)

where
\psi^{\mu}(\tau)
is a Lorentz-vector valued function of proper time with components that are anticommuting functions,

\psi^{\mu}(\tau_{1})\psi^{\nu}(\tau_{2})=-\psi^{\nu}(\tau_{2})\psi^{\mu}(\tau_{1})
, and the Grassmann path integral has to be evaluated with anti-periodic boundary conditions,

\psi^{\mu}(T)=-\psi^{\mu}(0)
.

While for the closed-loop case the choice of the spin-factor representation is essentially the only decision to be made, for the open fermion line
there are further choices to be made originating from the boundary conditions for the spin degrees of freedom
[
3
,
13
,
14
,
11
]
.
Quite recently, a worldline representation of the
x
-space Dirac propagator in a Maxwell field that seems promising for many applications has been constructed
in
[
15
,
16
]

\displaystyle S^{xx^{\prime}}[A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[m+iD\!\!\!\raise 0.6458pt\hbox{/}^{\prime}\bigr]K^{xx^{\prime}}[A]\,

\displaystyle K^{xx^{\prime}}[A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dT\,\,{\rm e}^{-m^{2}T}\,{\rm e}^{-{1\over 4}\frac{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}{T}}\int_{q(0)=0}^{q(T)=0}Dq\,\,{\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\bigl({1\over 4}\frac{\dot{q}^{2}}{4}+ie\,\dot{q}\cdot A+ie\frac{x^{\prime}-x}{T}\cdot A\bigr)}

(6)

\displaystyle\times\,2^{-\frac{D}{2}}{\rm symb}^{-1}\int_{\psi(0)+\psi(T)=0\hskip-30.0pt}D\psi\,\,{\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{T}d\tau\,\bigl[{1\over 2}\psi_{\mu}\dot{\psi}^{\mu}-ieF_{\mu\nu}(\psi+\eta)^{\mu}(\psi+\eta)^{\nu}\bigl]}

Here
\eta^{\mu}
is an external anticommuting Lorentz vector, and the “symbol map”

converts products of
\eta

into antisymmetrised products of Dirac matrices:

\displaystyle{\rm symb}\bigl(\gamma^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}}\bigr)\equiv(-i\sqrt{2})^{n}\eta^{\alpha_{1}}\eta^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots\eta^{\alpha_{n}}

(7)

where
\gamma^{\alpha\beta\cdots\rho}
denotes the totally antisymmetrised product of gamma matrices:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{n}}\equiv\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}{\rm sign}(\pi)\gamma^{\alpha_{\pi(1)}}\gamma^{\alpha_{\pi(2)}}\cdots\gamma^{\alpha_{\pi(n)}}

(8)

3
String-inspired treatment of the worldline path integral

The modern approach to the worldline formalism came up in the nineties when, following the development of string perturbation
theory, worldline path integrals started to be seen as one-dimensional analogs of Polyakov’s worldsheet path integral (see

[
17
,
18
]
for more on the history of the subject). Thus in the “string-inspired” version of the worldline formalism, all path integrals
are, be it by approximation, expansion or transformation, manipulated into gaussian ones, which reduces their evaluation to the
determination of appropriate worldline Green’s functions and the standard combinatorics of Wick contractions.
In the QED case, the appropriate worldline correlator for the worldline coordinate and Grassmann fields are

\displaystyle\langle x^{\mu}(\tau_{1})x^{\nu}(\tau_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\,\delta^{\mu\nu}\,,\quad G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=|\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}|-\frac{\Bigl(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\Bigr)^{2}}{T}

(9)

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\mu}(\tau_{1})\psi^{\nu}(\tau_{2})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}G_{F}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\,\delta^{\mu\nu}\,,\quad G_{F}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})={\rm sign}(\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})

(10)

This allows one to write down compact Koba-Nielsen type master formulas for the one-loop
N
-photon amplitudes
in scalar and spinor QED
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
and (with a different Green’s function for the coordinate path integral)
also for the corresponding photon-dressed propagators
[
19
,
15
,
16
]
.
The simplest one of these various master formulas is the one for the
N
-photon amplitude in scalar QED,

\displaystyle\Gamma[\{k_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{(-ie)}^{N}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}}{dT\over T}{(4\pi T)}^{-{D\over 2}}e^{-m^{2}T}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau_{i}

\displaystyle\hskip-8.0pt\times\exp\biggl\{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\bigl[{1\over 2}G_{ij}k_{i}\cdot k_{j}+i\dot{G}_{ij}k_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}+{1\over 2}\ddot{G}_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}\bigr]\biggr\}\mid_{{\rm lin}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})}

Here
\tau_{i}
parametrizes the position of photon
i
along the loop,
and we have abbreviated
G_{ij}\equiv G(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})
,
\dot{G}_{ij}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{i}}G_{ij}
, etc.
The notation
\mid_{{\rm lin}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})}
means that only terms linear in each of the photon
polarization vectors are to be kept.
This master formula holds the full information on the
N
-photon amplitudes in scalar QED (on-shell and off-shell).

A formally analogous master formula can be written down for the spinor loop using a one-dimensional superspace formalism,
but in practice it is usually preferable to use a pattern-matching procedure, discovered (in the QCD context) by Bern and Kosower
[
5
,
6
]
.
Namely, it is possible to remove all the second derivatives of the Green’s function
G
through integration-by-parts (this can be done
maintaining the full permutation symmetry between the
N
photons
[
20
]
), after which, up to the global normalization, all the effects of the electron spin
can be taken into account by applying, simultaneously to all “
\tau
-cycles”

\dot{G}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\dot{G}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\dot{G}_{i_{n}i_{1}}
in the resulting integrand, the replacement rule

\displaystyle\dot{G}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\dot{G}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\dot{G}_{i_{n}i_{1}}\rightarrow\dot{G}_{i_{1}i_{2}}\dot{G}_{i_{2}i_{3}}\cdots\dot{G}_{i_{n}i_{1}}-G_{Fi_{1}i_{2}}G_{Fi_{2}i_{3}}\cdots G_{Fi_{n}i_{1}}

(12)

In the original string-based formalism of
[
5
,
6
]
this rule comes from worldsheet supersymmetry.
In the worldline formalism it relates to a formal supersymmetry between the electron spin and orbital degrees of freedom,
familiar from the non-relativistic Pauli equation (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).

4
QED in a constant field

Let us now come to the main topic of this talk, QED in external fields, starting with the constant-field case.
Quite analogously to the Feynman-diagrammatic approach, where the effect of a constant background field
can be absorbed through a change of the electron propagator, in the worldline formalism it can be taken
care of by the following changes of the worldline Green’s functions and the global path-integral normalization factors

[
8
,
9
,
11
,
22
]
:

1.

Change the worldline Green’s functions
G,G_{F}
to field-dependent ones
{\cal G}_{B},{\cal G}_{F}

\displaystyle G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{B}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\frac{T}{2{\cal Z}^{2}}\biggl({{\cal Z}\over{{\rm sin}{\cal Z}}}\,{\rm e}^{-i{\cal Z}\dot{G}_{12}}+i{\cal Z}\dot{G}_{12}-1\biggr)

\displaystyle G_{F}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{F}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=G_{F12}{{\rm e}^{-i{\cal Z}\dot{G}_{12}}\over{\rm cos}{\cal Z}}

where the right-hand sides are to be understood as power series in the Lorentz matrix
{\cal Z}_{\mu\nu}\equiv eF_{\mu\nu}T
(see
[
22
,
18
]

for more practically useful representations).

2.

Add factors
{\rm det}^{{1\over 2}}\bigl[\frac{\cal Z}{{\rm sin}{\cal Z}}\bigr]
for the coordinate path integral
and
{\rm det}^{{1\over 2}}[{\rm cos}{\cal Z}]
for the Grassmann path integral.

For example, applying these rules to the master formula (
) transforms it into the following master formula for the
scalar QED
N
-photon amplitudes in a constant field
[

,
11
]

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm scal}[k_{1},\varepsilon_{1};\ldots;k_{N},\varepsilon_{N};F]={(-ie)}^{N}{\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}}{dT\over T}{(4\pi T)}^{-{D\over 2}}e^{-m^{2}T}{\rm det}^{{1\over 2}}\biggl[\frac{\cal Z}{{\rm sin}{\cal Z}}\biggr]\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau_{i}

\displaystyle\times\exp\biggl\{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\Bigl[{1\over 2}k_{i}\cdot{\cal G}_{Bij}\cdot k_{j}-i\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\dot{\cal G}_{Bij}\cdot k_{j}+{1\over 2}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\ddot{\cal G}_{Bij}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}\Bigr]\biggr\}\mid_{{\rm lin}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})}

(14)

For the transition to the spinor-loop case amplitudes one still has the same choice as in the vacuum case, i.e. between the introduction of
a worldline super formalism or an appropriate generalization of the replacement rule (
12

[
18
]

The determinant factors
{\rm det}^{{1\over 2}}\bigl[\frac{\cal Z}{{\rm sin}{\cal Z}}\bigr]
and
{\rm det}^{{1\over 2}}\bigl[\frac{\cal Z}{{\rm tan}{\cal Z}}\bigr]

by themselves just produce the vacuum amplitudes in the constant field, i.e. the Weisskopf
[

and Euler-Heisenberg

The constant-field master formula (

and of the photon-splitting amplitudes in a magnetic field

10

, for both scalar and spinor QED. More recently, it was also used for the calculation
of the one-photon (tadpole) amplitudes
[
26

27
]
, which are irrelevant at the one-loop level but have attracted attention following the recent demonstration by
Gies and Karbstein that they cannot be neglected when appearing as subdiagrams at the multiloop level
[

1.

The loop momentum has already been integrated out from the beginning at the level of the construction of the coordinate path integral.

2.

The loop is parametrized globally from the beginning, rather than segmented into three propagators with their individual proper-time

3.

This also has made it possible to avoid fixing an ordering for the three photons along the loop.

4.

Specifically for scalar QED: the same integration-by-parts that has led to the replacement rule (

5.

Specifically for spinor QED: the considerable algebraic work caused by the nontrivial Dirac matrix structure of the generalized electron

All these advantages will gain significance with increasing

The final three-parameter integrals are of the same type as
the ones found in the standard approach, but far from identical. They have to be done numerically.

In

For a generalization to Einstein-Maxwell theory see

5
Photon-dressed scalar propagator in a constant field

Generalization to the open scalar line, i.e. the derivation of a master formula for the
N
-photon dressed scalar propagator in a constant
field, has been achieved in
[
35
]
. In momentum space, this formula reads

\displaystyle D^{pp^{\prime}}(k_{1},\varepsilon_{1};\cdots;k_{N},\varepsilon_{N};F)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-ie)^{N}(2\pi)^{D}\delta\Bigl(p+p^{\prime}+\sum^{N}_{i=1}k_{i}\Bigr)\int^{\infty}_{0}dT\,{\rm e}^{-m^{2}T}\frac{1}{{\det}^{{1\over 2}}\left[{\rm cos{\cal Z}}\right]}

\displaystyle\hskip-90.0pt\times\int^{T}_{0}d\tau_{1}\cdots\int^{T}_{0}d\tau_{N}\,\,{\rm e}^{\sum^{N}_{i,j=1}\bigl(k_{i}\cdot{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}}{}_{ij}\cdot k_{j}-2i\varepsilon_{i}\cdot{}^{\bullet}\!{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}}{}_{ij}\cdot k_{j}-\varepsilon_{i}\cdot{}^{\bullet}\!{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}^{\hskip-1.42262pt\bullet}}{}_{ij}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}\bigr)}\,{\rm e}^{-Tb\cdot(\frac{{\rm tan{\cal Z}}}{{\cal Z}})\cdot b}\Big|_{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}\cdots\varepsilon_{N}}

The essential new ingredient here is the open-line worldline Green’s function
{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
,
which is related to the closed-loop one
{\cal{G}}_{B}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
by

\displaystyle{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})={1\over 2}\Bigl({\cal{G}}_{B}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})-{\cal{G}}_{B}(\tau,0)-{\cal{G}}_{B}(0,\tau^{\prime})+{\cal{G}}_{B}(0,0)\Bigr)

(16)

Contrary to the latter, it is not translation invariant, so that one has to distinguish between a derivative with respect to the
first (left ‘bullet’) and second variable (right ‘bullet’). Similarly, a left (right) ‘open circle’ on

denotes an integral
\int_{0}^{T}d\tau
(
\int_{0}^{T}d\tau^{\prime}

The Lorentz vector
b^{\mu}
is defined by

\displaystyle b\equiv p+\frac{1}{T}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\Bigl[\Bigl(\tau_{i}-2ieF\cdot{}^{\circ}\!{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}}{}_{i}\cdot\Bigr)k_{i}-i\left(1-2ieF\cdot{}^{\circ}\!{\underset{\smile}{\Delta}^{\hskip-1.42262pt\bullet}}{}_{i}\cdot\Bigr)\varepsilon_{i}\right]

i.e. the final propagators on each end of the scalar line in Fig.

The
N=1

[

6
QED in a plane-wave background

In strong-field QED, equally important as the constant-field background is the plane-wave one, defined by a vector potential
A(x)
of the form

\displaystyle eA_{\mu}(x)=a_{\mu}(n\cdot x)

(18)

where
n^{\mu}
is a null vector,
n^{2}=0
, and as is usual we will further impose the
light-front gauge condition

n\cdot a=0
.
Like the constant-field case it leads to Dirac and Klein-Gordon equations that can be solved exactly.
Nevertheless, it was treated in the worldline formalism only relatively recently, mainly because it was
not understood how to manipulate the corresponding worldline path integrals into gaussian form.
Building on work on the vacuum polarization case by Ilderton and Torgrimsson
[
38
]
,
the following master formula for the scalar QED
N
-photon amplitude in a plane-wave background
was obtained in 2019 by J.P. Edwards and the author
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\rm scal}(\{{k_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\}};a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-ie)^{N}(2\pi)^{3}\delta\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{1}\bigr)\delta\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{2}\bigr)\delta\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{+}\bigr)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx_{0}^{+}\,{\rm e}^{-ix_{0}^{+}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}^{-}}

\displaystyle\hskip-80.0pt\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dT}{T}\,{(4\pi T)}^{-{D\over 2}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau_{i}\,{\rm e}^{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\bigl[{1\over 2}G_{ij}k_{i}\cdot k_{j}-i\dot{G}_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot k_{j}+{1\over 2}\ddot{G}_{ij}\varepsilon_{i}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}\bigr]}

\displaystyle\hskip-80.0pt\times\,{\rm e}^{-M^{2}T+2\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}\cdot\bigl(I(\tau_{i})-\langle\langle I\rangle\rangle\bigr)-2i\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(a(\tau_{i})-\langle\langle a\rangle\rangle\bigr)\cdot\varepsilon_{i}}\mid_{{\rm lin}(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{N})}

Here we have introduced lightcone coordinates with

n^{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(0,0,1,i)
,
x^{+}\equiv n\cdot x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x^{3}+ix^{4})
(“light-front time”),
and
x^{-}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-x^{3}+ix^{4})
.
M
denotes the “effective mass”, defined by

\displaystyle M^{2}\equiv m^{2}+\langle\langle a^{2}\rangle\rangle-\langle\langle a\rangle\rangle^{2}

(20)

and

\displaystyle I_{\mu}(\tau)\equiv\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\Bigl(a_{\mu}(\tau^{\prime})-\langle\langle a_{\mu}\rangle\rangle\Bigr)

(21)

where
\langle\langle\rangle\rangle
stands for the worldloop average,

\langle\langle f\rangle\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}d\tau f(\tau)\,.

Differently from the constant-field case, for the plane-wave background it seems not to be possible to reduce the transition from
scalar to spinor QED to an algebraic rule such as (
12

generalization of the Wick-contraction rule (
10
), namely
[

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\mu}(\tau)\psi^{\nu}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle={1\over 2}\mathfrak{G}_{F}^{\mu\nu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

(22)

where

\hskip-15.00002pt\mathfrak{G}_{F}^{\mu\nu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\equiv\biggl\{\delta^{\mu\nu}+2in^{\mu}{\cal J}^{\nu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})+2i{\cal J}^{\mu}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)n^{\nu}+2\Bigl[{\cal J}^{2}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})-\frac{T^{2}}{4}\langle\langle a^{\prime}\rangle\rangle^{2}\Bigr]n^{\mu}n^{\nu}\biggr\}G_{F}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

(23)

and we have further defined

\displaystyle J_{\mu}(\tau)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\Bigl(a^{\prime}_{\mu}(\tau^{\prime})-\langle\langle a^{\prime}_{\mu}\rangle\rangle\Bigr)

(24)

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{\mu}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle J_{\mu}(\tau)-J_{\mu}(\tau^{\prime})-\frac{T}{2}\dot{G}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\langle\langle a^{\prime}_{\mu}\rangle\rangle

(25)

QED in a combined plane-wave and constant-field background

Combining a plane-wave field with a constant one generically does not lead to a Dirac or Klein-Gordon equation that would permit
exact solutions. However, there is one well-known special case that is still solvable, defined by the existence of a Lorentz frame where
the electric and the magnetic field are parallel and the direction of propagation of the wave is in the same direction
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
.
This suggests that it should also be possible to extend the above master formulas to this case. In
[
43
]
R. Shaisultanov and the author
derived this now most general known master formula for the
N
-photon amplitudes, and also elucidated why it is only in that special case that the
corresponding worldline path integrals can still be manipulated into gaussian form.

8
Schwinger pair creation

Going beyond the constant and plane-wave background fields usually means abandoning all hope for an exact evaluation
of the
N
-photon amplitudes or dressed propagators. Approximation schemes must be employed, of which the most universal
one in QED is the large-mass expansion of the effective action or the field-dependent propagator, corresponding to the low-energy
approximation for the corresponding photon amplitudes.
Although the worldline formalism has been shown to be a very powerful tool for the calculation of the large-mass, or heat-kernel, expansion of the effective
action
[
44
,
8
,
45
,
46
]
, presently of more interest for the strong-field QED community is the application of the large-mass approximation
to the imaginary part of the effective action, since this gives the Sauter-Schwinger pair-creation rate in the field.
In the leading approximation, the path integral is just replaced by a single trajectory, the “worldline instanton”
[
47
]
. While
for a constant or plane-wave field this would, due to the gaussian character of the worldline path integrals, still be an exact procedure,
for more general fields it yields a semiclassical approximation that is expected to work well only in the large-mass limit.

Outlook

The worldline formalism at this stage is ready for applications to

•

Schwinger pair creation in arbitrary electric fields.

•

Closed-loop and open-line processes in arbitrary constant fields.

•

Closed-loop processes in plane-wave backgrounds.

In particular, it applies to (linear and non-linear) Compton scattering and to light-by-light scattering in either a constant or plane-wave field.
