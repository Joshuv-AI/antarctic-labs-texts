Rigorous derivation of the Oberbeck-Boussinesq approximation revealing unexpected term
Peter Bella, Eduard Feireisl, Florian Oschmann

Abstract
We consider a general compressible viscous and heat conducting fluid confined between two parallel plates and heated from the bottom. The time evolution of the fluid is described by the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system considered in the regime of low Mach and Froude numbers suitably interrelated. Surprisingly and differently to the case of Neumann boundary conditions for the temperature, the asymptotic limit is identified as the Oberbeck–Boussinesq system supplemented with non–local boundary conditions for the temperature deviation.

Introduction

The Rayleigh–Bénard problem concerns the motion of a compressible fluid confined between two parallel plates
heated from the bottom. For the sake of simplicity, we suppose the motion is space–periodic with respect to the horizontal variable. Accordingly, the underlying spatial domain may be identified with the flat torus
in the horizontal plane:

\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d-1}\times(0,1),\ \mathbb{T}^{d-1}=\left([0,1]\Big|_{\{0,1\}}\right)^{d-1},\ d=2,3.

The state of the fluid at a given time
t
and a spatial position
x\in\Omega
is characterized by the
mass density
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
, the absolute temperature
\vartheta=\vartheta(t,x)
, and the velocity
{\bf u}={\bf u}(t,x)
.
We consider the motion in the asymptotically incompressible and stratified regime. Accordingly,
the time evolution of the flow is governed by the scaled
Navier–Stokes–Fourier (NSF) system
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho{\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\nabla_{x}G,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta))+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}\left(\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vartheta}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}:\nabla_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)\cdot\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right),

(1.3)

supplemented with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\bf u}|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\vartheta|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\vartheta_{B}.

(1.5)

The viscous stress is given by Newton’s rheological law

\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u})=\mu(\vartheta)\left(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf u}-\frac{2}{d}{\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I}\right)+\eta(\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}\mathbb{I},

(1.6)

while the internal energy flux satisfies Fourier’s law

{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)=-\kappa(\vartheta)\nabla_{x}\vartheta.

(1.7)

Here, the more conventional internal energy (heat) equation

\partial_{t}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta))+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u})+{\rm div}_{x}{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)=\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}{\bf u}-p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}

(1.8)

is replaced by the entropy balance equation (
1.3
).
We point out that the equations
(
1.3
), (
1.8
) are
equivalent

in the framework of classical solutions as long as the thermodynamic functions
p(\varrho,\vartheta)
,
e(\varrho,\vartheta)
,

s(\varrho,\vartheta)
are interrelated through Gibbs’ equation

\vartheta Ds=De+pD\left(\frac{1}{\varrho}\right).

(1.9)

The entropy balance equation, however, is more convenient for the weak formulation introduced in Section
2
. In particular,
the weak solutions based on the entropy formulation enjoy several useful properties, notably the so–called weak strong uniqueness principle.

Besides Gibbs’ equation, we impose the hypothesis of
thermodynamic stability
written in the form

\frac{\partial p(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\varrho}>0,\ \frac{\partial e(\varrho,\vartheta)}{\partial\vartheta}>0\ \mbox{for all}\ \varrho,\vartheta>0.

(1.10)

The scaling in (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) represented by a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
corresponds to the
Mach number
{\rm Ma}=\varepsilon
(the ratio between a “characteristic” fluid’s velocity and the local speed of sound) and the Froude number
{\rm Fr}=\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
(the ratio between inertial and gravitational forces of the fluid), see e.g. the survey paper by Klein et al.
[
16
]
.
Our goal is to identify the asymptotic limit of solutions for
\varepsilon\to 0
.

1.1
Asymptotic limit

The zero–th order terms in the asymptotic limit are determined by the stationary problem

\nabla_{x}p(\varrho,\vartheta)=\varepsilon\varrho\nabla_{x}G.

(1.11)

Accordingly, a solution of the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system can be written in the form

\varrho_{\varepsilon}=\overline{\varrho}+\varepsilon\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon},\ \vartheta_{\varepsilon}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon},

where
\overline{\varrho}
,
\overline{\vartheta}
are positive constants and
\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{R}
,

\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{T}
satisfy in the asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the so called
Boussinesq relation

\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{R}+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{T}=\overline{\varrho}\nabla_{x}G.

(1.12)

Without loss of generality, we suppose

\int_{\Omega}G\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0,\ \overline{\varrho}=\fint_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\,{\rm d}{x}\equiv\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\ \Rightarrow\ \int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0,

meaning, in particular, the total mass of the fluid is constant independent of
\varepsilon
.

Anticipating convergence of the temperature deviations

\mathfrak{T}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{T}

we impose the boundary condition

\vartheta_{\varepsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},\ \Theta_{B}=\Theta_{B}(x),

(1.13)

where the perturbation
\Theta_{B}
is not necessarily positive.

1.2
Limit system

Formally, the limit problem is expected to be the
Oberbeck–Boussinesq (OB) system
:

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}\Big(\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf U}\Big)+\nabla_{x}\Pi

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\overline{\vartheta},\nabla_{x}{\bf U})+\mathfrak{R}\nabla_{x}G,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\left(\partial_{t}\mathfrak{T}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{T}\right)-\overline{\varrho}\ \overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}){\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}G

\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{\vartheta})\Delta_{x}\mathfrak{T},

(1.14)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{\overline{\varrho}}\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\left(\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\partial e(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}+\overline{\varrho}^{-1}\overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}

(1.15)

stand for the coefficient of thermal expansion,
and the specific heat at constant pressure, respectively. The quantities
\mathfrak{R}
,
\mathfrak{T}

satisfy the Boussinesq relation (
1.12
).

We refer to the survey by Zeytounian
[
20
]
and the list of references therein for a formal derivation of the Oberbeck–Boussinesq system. A rigorous proof of convergence of a family of (weak) solutions
(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
to
the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system, meaning

{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\to{\bf U},\ \frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{T}\ \mbox{in some sense,}

was obtained in
[
10
]
(see also
[
11
, Chapter 5]
) for the conservative boundary conditions

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,\ (\mathbb{S}(\vartheta_{\varepsilon},\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\cdot{\bf n})\times{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}\cdot{\bf n}|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0.

(1.16)

Note that the conservative boundary conditions (
1.16
) imply that the average

\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{T}(t,\cdot)\,{\rm d}{x}

is a constant of motion. In particular, the Boussinesq relation (
1.12
) can be written as

\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\mathfrak{R}+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\mathfrak{T}=\overline{\varrho}G

(1.17)

recalling the convention
\int_{\Omega}G\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0
. The form (
1.17
) of the Boussinesq relation is rather misleadingly interpreted in the sense that the Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation can be
derived from the Navier–Stokes–Fourier system on condition that the density is supposed to be an affine function of the temperature, cf. the discussion in Maruyama
[
17
]
.

The present paper can be seen as the first attempt to address the same singular limit problem under the inhomogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions
imposed on the temperature, physically relevant for the well–known Rayleigh–Bénard convection problem. The result is stated in the framework of the
general theory of weak solutions to the primitive Navier–Stokes–Fourier system with inhomogeneous boundary conditions developed recently in
[
7
]
and the
monograph
[
12
]
.

Rather surprisingly, we show that the limit system is in fact
different
if the Dirichlet boundary conditions (
1.4
), (
1.5
) are imposed. In accordance with (
1.4
), (
1.13
), we consider the
scaled Navier–Stokes–Fourier system with the
boundary conditions

{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\ \vartheta_{\varepsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}=\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B}.

(1.18)

Imposing (
1.18
) in place of (
1.16
) drastically changes the behaviour of the fluid
as the resulting system is no longer energetically closed and the dynamics is driven by the temperature gradient. Indeed the total mass conservation discussed by several authors
(Barletta et al.
[
2
,
3
]
, Maruyama
[
17
]
) must be enforced through the
condition

\int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{R}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0\ \Rightarrow\ \int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{R}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0

in (
1.12
). Accordingly, the conventional Boussinesq relation (
1.17
) must be replaced by

\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\mathfrak{R}+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\mathfrak{T}=\overline{\varrho}G+\chi(t),

(1.19)

where, in accordance with our convention
\int_{\Omega}G\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0
,

\chi=\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{T}\,{\rm d}{x}.

(1.20)

Relation (
1.20
) indicates that the limit problem could involve “non–local” terms.
Indeed we show that a family of (weak) solutions to the NSF system
(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
admits a limit

{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\to{\bf U},\ \frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{T},\ \frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varrho}}{\varepsilon}\to\mathfrak{R}

solving a
modified Oberbeck–Bousinesq system

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}\Big(\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf U}\Big)+\nabla_{x}\Pi

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\overline{\vartheta},\nabla_{x}{\bf U})+\mathfrak{R}\nabla_{x}G,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\left(\partial_{t}\mathfrak{T}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{T}\right)-\overline{\varrho}\ \overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}){\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}G

\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{\vartheta})\Delta_{x}\mathfrak{T}+\overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\partial_{t}\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{T}\,{\rm d}{x},

(1.21)

together with the Boussinesq relation

\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{R}+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{T}=\overline{\varrho}\nabla_{x}G,\ \int_{\Omega}\mathfrak{R}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=0,

(1.22)

and the boundary conditions

{\bf U}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\ \mathfrak{T}|_{\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{B}.

(1.23)

To simplify even more, using relation (
1.19
) to replace
\mathfrak{R}
with
\mathfrak{T}
in the momentum equation while observing that two gradient terms hide into
\Pi
, the system (
1.21
)–(
1.23
) turns into

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.24)

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}\Big(\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf U}\Big)+\nabla_{x}\Pi

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\overline{\vartheta},\nabla_{x}{\bf U})-\overline{\varrho}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\mathfrak{T}\nabla_{x}G,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\left(\partial_{t}\mathfrak{T}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathfrak{T}\right)-\overline{\varrho}\ \overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}){\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}G

\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{\vartheta})\Delta_{x}\mathfrak{T}+\overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\partial_{t}\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{T}\,{\rm d}{x},

\displaystyle{\bf U}|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,\ \mathfrak{T}|_{\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{B}.

Finally, performing a simple change of variables

r=\mathfrak{R},\ \Theta=\mathfrak{T}-\lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\fint_{\Omega}\mathfrak{T}\,{\rm d}{x},\ \mbox{where}\ \lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})=\frac{\overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\overline{\varrho}c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\in(0,1),

we may rewrite system (
1.21
)–(
1.23
) in the form of
conventional Oberbeck–Boussinesq system

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}{\bf U}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}\Big(\partial_{t}{\bf U}+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf U}\Big)+\nabla_{x}\Pi

\displaystyle={\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\overline{\vartheta},\nabla_{x}{\bf U})+r\nabla_{x}G,

\displaystyle\overline{\varrho}c_{p}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})\Big(\partial_{t}\Theta+{\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta\Big)-\overline{\varrho}\ \overline{\vartheta}\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}){\bf U}\cdot\nabla_{x}G

\displaystyle=\kappa(\overline{\vartheta})\Delta_{x}\Theta,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\varrho}\nabla_{x}r+\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\nabla_{x}\Theta

\displaystyle=\overline{\varrho}\nabla_{x}G

(1.25)

with the no–slip boundary condition for the velocity

{\bf U}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

(1.26)

and a
non–local
boundary condition for the temperature deviation

\Theta|_{\partial\Omega}=\Theta_{B}-\frac{\lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{1-\lambda(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}\fint_{\Omega}\Theta\,{\rm d}{x}.

(1.27)

Here and hereafter, we always suppose that the coefficient of thermal expansion
\alpha(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
at the reference state
(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
is strictly positive. In particular,
it follows directly from (
1.2
) and (
1.10
) that
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Parabolic equations with the non–local boundary terms similar to (
1.27
) have been recognized by Day
[
9
]
in the context of some models in thermoelasticity, and subsequently studied
by a number of authors, see Chen and Liu
[
8
]
, Friedman
[
13
]
, Gladkov and Nikitin
[
15
]
, Pao
[
19
]
, among others.

We point out that our results justify the Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation as a rigorous description of the limit behaviour of solutions to the complete Navier–Stokes–Fourier system only in the regime where the temperature deviation from the constant state
\overline{\vartheta}
is small of order
\varepsilon
. Indeed the relevance of the Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation for the Rayleigh–Bénard convection with large deviation of the boundary temperature
has been questioned by several authors, see Bormann
[
6
]
, Fröhlich, Laure, and Peyret
[
14
]
, Nadolin
[
18
]
, among others.

1.3
The strategy of the convergence proof

Our approach is based on the concept of
weak solutions
to the NSF system with energetically
open boundary conditions developed recently in
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
. In particular, the relative energy inequality
based on the ballistic energy balance is used to measure the distance between the solutions of the primitive and target systems. In contrast with
[
10
]
,
[
11
, Chapter 5]
, strong convergence is obtained
with certain explicit estimates of the error depending on how close are the initial data to their limit values.

The paper is organized as follows.
We start with the concept of weak solutions for the NSF system with Dirichlet boundary conditions
introduced in
[
7
]
. In particular, we recall the ballistic energy and the associated relative energy
inequality in Section
2
. Then, in Section
3
, we record the available results on solvability of the OB system in the framework of strong solutions.
The main results on convergence to the target OB system are stated in Section
4
. The rest of the paper is then devoted to the proof of convergence.
In Section
5
, we derive the basic energy estimates that control the amplitude of the fluid velocity as well as
the distance of the density and the temperature profiles from their limit values independent of the scaling
parameter
\varepsilon
. The proof of convergence to the OB system is completed in Section
6
by means of the
relative energy inequality.

2
Weak solutions to the primitive system

Following
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
, we introduce the concept of weak solutions to the NSF system.

Definition 2.1

(
Weak solution to the NSF system).

We say that a trio
(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u})
is a weak solution of the NSF system (
1.1
)–(
1.7
),
with the initial data

\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},\ (\varrho{\bf u})(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0}{\bf u}_{0},\ (\varrho s)(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0}),

if the following holds:

•

The solution belongs to the
regularity class
:

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for some}\ \gamma>1,\ \varrho\geq 0\ \mbox{a.a.~in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle{\bf u}

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})),

\displaystyle\vartheta^{\beta/2},\ \log(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}(\Omega))\ \mbox{for some}\ \beta\geq 2,\ \vartheta>0\ \mbox{a.a.~in}\ (0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle(\vartheta-\vartheta_{B})

\displaystyle\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega)).

(2.1)

•

The
equation of continuity
(
1.1
) is satisfied in the sense of distributions,
more specifically,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Big[\varrho\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\varrho(0)\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.2)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\overline{\Omega})
.

•

The
momentum equation
(
1.2
) is satisfied in the sense of distributions,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u}:\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}p(\varrho,\vartheta){\rm div}_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left[\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}\boldsymbol{\varphi}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\varrho\nabla_{x}G\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t-\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}{\bf u}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.3)

for any
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

•

The
entropy balance
(
1.3
) is replaced by the inequality

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\varphi+\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi+\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varphi}{\vartheta}\left[\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)\cdot\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\varphi(0,\cdot)\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.4)

for any
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}([0,T)\times\Omega)
,
\varphi\geq 0
.

•

The
ballistic energy balance

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon^{2}\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)-\tilde{\vartheta}\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}}{\vartheta}\left[\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}{\bf u}):\nabla_{x}{\bf u}-\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)\cdot\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\psi\int_{\Omega}\left[\varepsilon\varrho{\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}G-\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta)\partial_{t}\tilde{\vartheta}-\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{\vartheta}-\frac{{\bf q}(\vartheta,\nabla_{x}\vartheta)}{\vartheta}\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{\vartheta}\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle+\psi(0)\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\varrho_{0}|{\bf u}_{0}|^{2}+\varrho_{0}e(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})-\tilde{\vartheta}(0,\cdot)\varrho_{0}s(\varrho_{0},\vartheta_{0})\right]\ \,{\rm d}{x}

(2.5)

holds for any
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}[0;T)
,
\psi\geq 0
, and any
\tilde{\vartheta}\in C^{1}([0;T)\times\overline{\Omega})
satisfying

\tilde{\vartheta}>0,\ \tilde{\vartheta}|_{\partial\Omega}=\vartheta_{B}.

Although quite general, the weak solutions in the sense of Definition
2.1
comply with the weak–strong uniqueness principle, meaning they coincide with the strong solution as long as the latter exists, see
[
12
, Chapter 4]
.

2.1
Relative energy inequality

Following
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
, we introduce the scaled
relative energy

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}-{\tilde{\bf u}}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left[\varrho e-\tilde{\vartheta}\Big(\varrho s-\tilde{\varrho}s(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})\Big)-\Big(e(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})-\tilde{\vartheta}s(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})+\frac{p(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})}{\tilde{\varrho}}\Big)(\varrho-\tilde{\varrho})-\tilde{\varrho}e(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})\right].

The hypothesis of thermodynamic stability (
1.10
) can be equivalently rephrased as (strict) convexity
of the total energy expressed with respect to the conservative entropy variables

E_{\varepsilon}\Big(\varrho,S=\varrho s(\varrho,\vartheta),{\bf m}=\varrho{\bf u}\Big)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\frac{|{\bf m}|^{2}}{\varrho}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varrho e(\varrho,S),

whereas the relative energy can be written as
Bregmann distance

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left(\varrho,S,{\bf m}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\widetilde{S},\widetilde{{\bf m}}\right)=E_{\varepsilon}(\varrho,S,{\bf m})-\left<\partial_{\varrho,S,{\bf m}}E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\varrho},\widetilde{S},\widetilde{{\bf m}});(\varrho-\tilde{\varrho},S-\widetilde{S},{\bf m}-\widetilde{{\bf m}})\right>-E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{\varrho},\widetilde{S},\widetilde{{\bf m}})

associated to the convex functional
(\varrho,S,{\bf m})\mapsto E_{\varepsilon}(\varrho,S,{\bf m})
, see
[
12
, Chapter 3, Section 3.1]
.
Finally, as stated in
[
7
]
, any weak solution in the sense of Definition
2.1
satisfies the relative
energy
inequality

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\tilde{\vartheta}}{\vartheta}\left(\mathbb{S}(\vartheta,\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\kappa(\vartheta)|\nabla_{x}\vartheta|^{2}}{\vartheta}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\left(\varrho(s-s(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta}))\partial_{t}\tilde{\vartheta}+\varrho(s-s(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})){\bf u}\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{\vartheta}-\left(\frac{\kappa(\vartheta)\nabla_{x}\vartheta}{\vartheta}\right)\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{\vartheta}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

for a.a.
\tau>0

Here, we have denoted

2.2
Constitutive relations

The existence theory developed in
[
7
]
,
[
12
]
is conditioned by certain restrictions imposed on the
constitutive relations (state equations) similar to those introduced in the monograph

[
11
, Chapters 1,2]
. Specifically, the
equation of state
takes the form

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+p_{\rm rad}(\vartheta),

(2.8)

where
p_{\rm m}
is the pressure of a general
monoatomic
gas,

p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{2}{3}\varrho e_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta),

(2.9)

enhanced by the radiation pressure

p_{\rm rad}(\vartheta)=\frac{a}{3}\vartheta^{4},\ a>0.

Accordingly, the internal energy reads

e(\varrho,\vartheta)=e_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+e_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta),\ e_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{a}{\varrho}\vartheta^{4}.

Moreover, using several physical principles it was shown in
[
11
, Chapter 1]
:

•

Gibbs’ relation
together with (
2.9
) yield

p_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)

for a certain
P\in C^{1}[0,\infty)
.
Consequently,

p(\varrho,\vartheta)=\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)+\frac{a}{3}\vartheta^{4},\ e(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\vartheta^{\frac{5}{2}}}{\varrho}P\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right)+\frac{a}{\varrho}\vartheta^{4},\ a>0.

(2.10)

•

Hypothesis of thermodynamic stability
(
1.10
) expressed in terms of

P
gives rise to

P(0)=0,\ P^{\prime}(Z)>0\ \mbox{for}\ Z\geq 0,\ 0<\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z}\leq C\ \mbox{for}\ Z>0.

(2.11)

In particular, the function
Z\mapsto P(Z)/Z^{\frac{5}{3}}
is decreasing, and we suppose

\lim_{Z\to\infty}\frac{P(Z)}{Z^{\frac{5}{3}}}=p_{\infty}>0.

(2.12)

•

Accordingly, the associated entropy
takes the form

s(\varrho,\vartheta)=s_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)+s_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta),\ s_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{\varrho}{\vartheta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),\ s_{\rm rad}(\varrho,\vartheta)=\frac{4a}{3}\frac{\vartheta^{3}}{\varrho},

(2.13)

where

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(Z)=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\frac{5}{3}P(Z)-P^{\prime}(Z)Z}{Z^{2}}<0.

(2.14)

•

In addition, we impose the
Third law of thermodynamics
, cf. Belgiorno
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
, requiring the entropy to vanish
when the absolute temperature approaches zero,

\lim_{Z\to\infty}\mathcal{S}(Z)=0.

(2.15)

Finally, we
suppose the transport coefficients are continuously differentiable functions satisfying

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\mu}(1+\vartheta)

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\vartheta),\ |\mu^{\prime}(\vartheta)|\leq\overline{\mu},

\displaystyle 0\leq\eta(\vartheta)

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\eta}(1+\vartheta),

\displaystyle 0<\underline{\kappa}(1+\vartheta^{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq\kappa(\vartheta)\leq\overline{\kappa}(1+\vartheta^{\beta}),\ \mbox{where}\ \beta>6.

(2.16)

As a consequence of the above hypotheses, we get the following estimates:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\frac{5}{3}}+\vartheta^{4}\lesssim\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\varrho^{\frac{5}{3}}+\vartheta^{4},

(2.17)

\displaystyle s_{\rm m}(\varrho,\vartheta)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(1+|\log(\varrho)|+[\log(\vartheta)]^{+}\right),

(2.18)

see
[
11
, Chapter 3, Section 3.2]
. Here and hereafter, the symbol

5
Basic energy estimates

In order to perform the limit claimed in Theorem
4.1
, we need bounds on the sequence

(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
independent of
\varepsilon
. We start by introducing the notation borrowed from

[
11
]
distinguishing the “essential” and “residual” range of the
thermostatic variables
(\varrho,\vartheta)
. Specifically, given a compact set

K\subset\left\{(\varrho,\vartheta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ \Big|\ \varrho>0,\vartheta>0\right\}

and
\varepsilon>0
, we introduce

g_{\rm ess}=g\mathds{1}_{(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\in K},\ g_{\rm res}=g-g_{\rm ess}=g\mathds{1}_{(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus K}

for any measurable
g=g(t,x)
. Note carefully that this decomposition depends on
\varepsilon
. As a matter of fact, the characteristic function
\mathds{1}_{(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\in K}
can be replaced by its smooth regularization by a suitable convolution kernel.

In the subsequent analysis, we consider

K=\overline{\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}\subset(0,\infty)^{2},\ {\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}\ \mbox{- an open neighborhood of}\ (\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}).

As shown in
[
11
, Chapter 5, Lemma 5.1]
, the relative energy enjoys the following coercivity properties:

E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)\geq C\left(\frac{|\varrho-\tilde{\varrho}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{|\vartheta-\tilde{\vartheta}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+|{\bf u}-{\tilde{\bf u}}|^{2}\right)

if
(\varrho,\vartheta)\in K=\overline{\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}
,
(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
,

E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho,\vartheta,{\bf u}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)\geq C\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varrho e(\varrho,\vartheta)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varrho|s(\varrho,\vartheta)|+\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\right)

whenever
(\varrho,\vartheta)\in R^{2}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}
,
(\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{U}(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})
. The constant
C
depends on
K
and the distance

\sup_{t,x}{\rm dist}\left[(\tilde{\varrho}(t,x),\tilde{\vartheta}(t,x));\partial K\right].

In other words,

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)_{\rm ess}

\displaystyle\geq C\left(\frac{|\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\varrho}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{|\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\tilde{\vartheta}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-{\tilde{\bf u}}|^{2}\right)_{\rm ess},

(5.1)

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\tilde{\varrho},\tilde{\vartheta},{\tilde{\bf u}}\right)_{\rm res}

\displaystyle\geq C\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varrho_{\varepsilon}e(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\varrho_{\varepsilon}|s(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})|+\varrho_{\varepsilon}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)_{\rm res}.

(5.2)

5.1

In agreement with hypothesis (
4.2
), we have

\int_{\Omega}E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),\vartheta_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot),{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\lesssim 1\ \mbox{independently of}\ \varepsilon\to 0,

(5.3)

where
\Theta_{B}=\Theta_{B}(x)
is a suitable extension of the temperature boundary condition inside
\Omega
.
Plugging this ansatz in the relative energy inequality (

) we obtain

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B}}{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}\left(\mathbb{S}(\vartheta_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}):\mathbb{D}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\kappa(\vartheta_{\varepsilon})|\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon}(s(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon})-s(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon{\Theta_{B}})){\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta_{B}-\frac{\kappa(\vartheta_{\varepsilon})\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta_{B}\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}G\cdot{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B})}{\partial\vartheta}-\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\right)\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}{\overline{\varrho}}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta_{B}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}{\overline{\varrho}}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta_{B}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t.

; hence the integral is controlled by dissipation.

Next,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\beta-1}{\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}}\ \,{\rm d}{x}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}\vartheta^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\leq\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+C(\delta)\int_{\Omega}|\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x},

(5.11)

where the first term is controlled by dissipation and the second one by Poincaré’s inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{x}\vartheta_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\beta}{2}}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B})^{\beta}\ {\rm d}\sigma_{x}.

(5.12)

Step 3:
We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\nabla_{x}G\cdot{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\Omega}G{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\int_{0}^{\tau}\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varrho})G\ \,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varrho}}{\varepsilon}G\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}.

Seeing that

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)-c_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\varrho}}{\varepsilon}G\ \,{\rm d}{x}

\displaystyle\lesssim E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)+1

(5.13)

we can add this term to the relative energy on the left–hand side of (
2.6
).

Step 4:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B})}{\partial\vartheta}-\frac{\partial p(\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta})}{\partial\vartheta}\right)\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}}{\overline{\varrho}}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Theta_{B}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|\ \,{\rm d}{x}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}{x}+\int_{\Omega}\varrho_{\varepsilon}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\ \,{\rm d}{x}\lesssim\int_{\Omega}E_{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\vartheta_{\varepsilon},{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\Big|\overline{\varrho},\overline{\vartheta}+\varepsilon\Theta_{B},0\right)\ \,{\rm d}{x}+1.

(5.14)

The last integral on the right–hand side of (

Conclusion, uniform bounds

In view of the estimates obtained in the previous section, we may apply Grönwall’s lemma to the relative energy inequality (

). As the initial data satisfy (

(5.15)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{W^{1,2}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

(5.16)

(5.17)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle{\rm ess}\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\left\|\left[\frac{\vartheta_{\varepsilon}-\overline{\vartheta}}{\varepsilon}\right]_{\rm ess}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
