Abstract
. We prove the existence of at least two positive weak solutions for mixed local-nonlocal singular and critical semilinear elliptic problems in the spirit of [ 38 ] , extending the recent results in [ 32 ] concerning singular problems and, at the same time, the results in [ 8 ] regarding critical problems.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set with smooth enough boundary
\partial\Omega
. We consider the following mixed local-nonlocal singular and critical semilinear elliptic problem:

(
\mathrm{P_{\varepsilon}}
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u=\dfrac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{2^{\ast}-1}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u>0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\textrm{in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\lambda>0
is a positive real parameter,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u=-\Delta u+\varepsilon(-\Delta)^{s}u,\qquad s\in(0,1),\quad\varepsilon\in(0,1].

The choice of
1
as upper bound for
\varepsilon
is purely esthetic.
Along the paper it will sometimes be useful to denote the above problem as (

P
ε

)
λ

to make it clear the choice of the parameter.

The study of singular problems when
\varepsilon=0
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{0}=-\Delta
, is pretty old and it dates back to
[
31
]
and

[
44
]
; nevertheless, it is probably the famous paper by Crandall, Rabinowitz and Tartar
[
24
]
which
has raised the attention to such a kind of singular PDEs. This attention
increased
even more after the celebrated paper by Lazer and Mckenna
[
41
]
, where it was proved that for

very
singular problems (i.e.,
\gamma\geq 3
), classical solutions may not belong to the natural Sobolev
space. This is one of the reasons why variational methods are not always fruitful in dealing with singular
problems, leading to approximation techniques extensively developed after
[
12
]
and used also in presence of
measure sources
[
43
]
. Usually, most of the difficulties appear in cases when the right hand side takes the
form

\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}},

where
f
may be only a
L^{1}
function. The situation is obviously easier for
f
constant plus a possible perturbation (that may or may not be of power type). In this case, the moving plane has been settled in
[
20
]
and variational methods may be used, see e.g.
[
25
,
39
,
38
]
. Since we will deal with mixed local-nonlocal problems, we also want to mention a few purely nonlocal results dealing with singular problems, see
[
5
,
21
,
22
]
, dealing with existence and qualitative properties of solutions.

As already mentioned, the main character involved in this paper is the linear mixed local-nonlocal operator,

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}:=-\Delta+\varepsilon(-\Delta)^{s},\quad s\in(0,1),\quad\varepsilon\in(0,1],

see Section
2
.
The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
written above is just a special instance of a wide more general class of operators whose study began in the ’60s, see
[
13
]
and
[
19
]
for generalizations, in connection with the validity of a maximum principle. On the other hand, the operator
\mathcal{L}
can be seen as the infinitesimal generator of a stochastic process having both a Brownian motion and a Lévy flight, and hence there is a vast literature which establishes several regularity properties
adopting probabilistic techniques, see e.g.
[
23
]
and the references therein.

More recently, the study of regularity properties related to this operator (and its quasilinear generalizations) has seen an increasing interest, mainly adopting more analytical and PDEs approaches, see, e.g.,

[
1
,
6
,
10
,
18
,
26
,
29
,
34
,
35
,
45
]
.
It is worth mentioning that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
seems to be
of interest in biological applications, see, e.g.,

[
30
]
and the references therein.

The second actor, appearing in the right hand side of (

P
ε

), is given by the critical perturbation of a mild singular term, namely

\dfrac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{2^{\ast}-1},

where
\lambda>0
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
(hence the term
mild
) and
2^{\ast}:=\tfrac{2n}{n-2}
(for
n\geq 3
) is the critical Sobolev exponent. The problem we are interested in is pretty classical and can be formulated as follows

Find values of the parameter
\lambda
for which (

P
ε

) admits one or more positive weak solutions.

We stress that, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, one is dealing with a problem
which depends on
\varepsilon
; hence,
any possible solution
u_{\lambda}
will
depend on
\varepsilon
in a not explicit way.

Before entering in the details of our result, we first make a
brief review of the existing literature concerning mixed local-nonlocal singular problems (with
\varepsilon=1
).

•

In
[
2
]
, the authors prove existence and regularity results of positive solutions
of the following
linear and purely singular
problem

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u=\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

where
\gamma>0
is a fixed parameter.

•

In
[
36
]
, the authors prove existence of positive solutions of
the
quasilinear counterpart
of the singular problem (
1.1
), that is,

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}u=\dfrac{f(x)}{u^{\gamma}}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

in connection with mixed local-nonlocal Sobolev inequalities.
Regularity of solutions under different summability assumptions on
f
and symmetry results are proved as well.

•

In
[
32
]
, Garain proved the existence of at least two positive solutions for a

subcritical
perturbation of problem (
1.1
), namely

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{p}u+(-\Delta_{p})^{s}u=\dfrac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+u^{p}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

where
\lambda>0,\,\gamma\in(0,1)
and
p\in(1,2^{\ast}-1)
.
The case of nonlinearities of the form
g(x,u)=\lambda h(u)u^{-\gamma}
is also considered.

•

In
[
11
]
, Biroud extended some of the results in
[
36
]
to the case of variable exponent
\gamma(x)
.

•

In
[
33
]
, the authors proved some existence results
for a mixed anisotropic operator with variable singular exponent.

Keeping the previously mentioned results in mind, we are now ready to state the first main result of this paper. In what follows,
we refer to Definition
2.2
for the precise definition of
weak solution

of (

P
ε

)
λ
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(
with
n\geq 3
)
be a bounded open set
with smooth enough boundary, and let
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, there exists
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}>0

such that

a)

problem (

P
ε

)
λ
admits at least one weak solution for every
0<\lambda\leq\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
;

b)

problem (

P
ε

)
λ
does not admit weak solutions
for every
\lambda>\Lambda_{\varepsilon}
.

Moreover, for every
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda_{\varepsilon})
, the weak solution
u_{\lambda}
found above is a local minimizer of the functional

(1.2)

I_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(u):=\dfrac{1}{2}\rho_{\varepsilon}(u)^{2}-\dfrac{\lambda}{1-\gamma}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{1-\gamma}\,dx-\dfrac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}}\,dx

in the
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
-topology.

The above theorem falls in the framework of the results of Haitao
[
38
]
and Hirano, Saccon and Shoji
[
39
]
, where the authors considered critical perturbations of mild singular terms using sub and supersolution methods (
[
38
]
) or a Nehari manifold approach (
[
39
]
). We also note that Theorem
1.1
extends the results in
[
32
]
.

Let us now spend a few words on the proof of Theorem
1.1
. First of all, we decided to follow the approach of Haitao
[
38
]
. The argument runs as follows. The existence of a first positive solution is obtained by means of a sub/supersolution variational scheme, which extends to the critical case a result in
[
32
]
. The role of the subsolution is naturally played by the solution of the purely singular problem (
1.1
),
while a supersolution can be found exploiting the monotonicity of
\eqref{eq:Problem}_{\lambda}
with respect to the parameter, see Lemma
4.5
.
As in
[
38
]
, in Lemma
4.6
, the first solution found is proved to be a local minimizer in the
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
-topology for the functional
I_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
defined in (
1.2
). In the purely local case
[
38
]
,
this is proved using a quantitative strong maximum principle established
in
[
17
, Theorem 3]
. Since we do not have such a result at our disposal, we exploit a weak Harnack-type inequality for weak supersolutions of mixed operators with a zero order term, see Section
3
.
In doing this, we heavily rely on the recent regularity results obtained by Garain and Kinnunen
[
34
]
.
The fact that the first solution is a local minimizer is the key point to start when looking for a second positive solution. Classically, this is done by Ekeland variational principle combined with a perturbation of the first solution with a suitable family of functions built upon the Aubin-Talenti functions. This choice is pretty natural in the purely local case and it is replaced by the nonlocal Talenti functions in the purely nonlocal case
[
37
]
. Thanks to the recent results in
[
8
]
, in the mixed local-nonlocal case the situation is closer to the purely local case: indeed, in
[
8
, Propositions 1.1 and 1.2]
it has been proved that the best constant in the natural mixed Sobolev quotient is never achieved and that it coincides with the purely local one, therefore suggesting that the classical Aubin-Talenti functions are the good candidates to play with in critical problems. Despite this, the presence of the fractional Laplacian creates a lack of scaling invariance of the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
(and this occurs for every
\varepsilon
) which prevents from a clear repetition of the classical arguments, see the proof of Lemma
5.1
. See also
[
40
]
where a similar situation occurs with the
(p,q)
-Laplacian.

To deal with this phenomenon we assume that
\varepsilon
is
sufficiently small

(so that the role of the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
in the lacking of homogeneity
is somehow negligible), and we have to carefully keep track of the dependence on
\varepsilon
of several crucial estimates.
Once done this, we can prove the existence of a second solution.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(
with
n\geq 3
)
be a bounded open set
with smooth enough boundary, and let
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
Then, there exist

\lambda_{\star}>0
(independent of
\varepsilon
) and
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,1]

such that problem (

P
ε

)
λ

admits
two weak solutions
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
and all
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\star})
.

We notice that a careful inspection of Lemma
5.1
shows that a better result can be obtained at the price of quite big restrictions, namely

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
n=3
and
s\in\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
. Then, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
problem (

P
ε

)
λ
admits at least two positive weak solutions for every
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda)
.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we collect all the relevant notation, definitions and preliminary results; in Section

we prove a weak Harnak-type inequality for weak supersolutions of

which we believe being of independent interest. In Section

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect some preliminary definitions and results which
will be used in the rest of the paper. First of all, we review some basic properties
of the fractional Laplace operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
;
we then properly
introduce the adequate functional setting for the study
of problem (

P
ε

)
λ
, and we give the precise definition
of
weak sub/supersolution
of this problem.

i) The fractional Laplacian.
Let
s\in(0,1)
be fixed, and let

u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function. The
fractional Laplacian
(of order
s
) of
u

at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined (up to a

normalization constant

C_{n,s}>0
that we neglect) as

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=2\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy=2\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x-y|\geq\varepsilon\}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dy,

provided that the above limit
exists and is finite
.

Since we are interested in
weak solutions

of problem (

P
ε

)
λ
,
we recall some basic
facts concerning the
Weak Theory
of
(-\Delta)^{s}
.
To begin with we recall that, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an arbitrary open set,
the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}
is related
(essentially via the Euler-Lagrange equation)
to the
fractional Sobolev space

H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
, which is defined as follows:

H^{s}(\mathcal{O}):=\Big\{u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O}):\,[u]^{2}_{s,\mathcal{O}}=\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy<\infty\Big\}.

While we refer to
[
42
]
for a thorough introduction
on fractional Sobolev spaces, here we list the few basic properties of
H^{s}(\mathcal{O})

we will exploit in this paper.

a)

H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
is a real Hilbert space, with the scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle_{s,\,\mathcal{O}}:=\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\qquad(u,v\in H^{s}(\mathcal{O})).

b)

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
is a
linear subspace of
H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
; in addition,
in the particular case when
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have that

C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
dense
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

c)

If
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
or if
\mathcal{O}
has
bounded boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}\in C^{0,1}
,
we have the
continuous embedding

H^{1}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
,
that is, there exists
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}(n,s)>0
s.t.

(2.1)

\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq\mathbf{c}\,\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\quad\text{for every $u\in H^{1}(\mathcal{O})$}.

In particular, if
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a

bounded open set
(with no regularity assumptions on
\partial\mathcal{O}
) and if

u\in H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
, setting
\hat{u}=u\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we have

(2.2)

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|\hat{u}(x)-\hat{u}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq\beta\,\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx,

where
\beta>0
is a suitable constant depending on
n,s
and on
|\Omega|
. Here
and throughout,
|\cdot|
denotes the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

The relation between the space
H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
and the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}

is rooted in the following
fractional integration-by-parts formula
: let

\mathcal{O},\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open sets (bounded or not) such that
\mathcal{O}\Subset\mathcal{V}
,
and let
u\in\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{V})\subseteq H^{s}(\mathcal{O})

(see the above (
2.1
)); given any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{O}}(-\Delta)^{s}u\,\varphi\,dx&=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&=\iint_{\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad\quad-2\,\iint_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{O})\times\mathcal{O}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\end{split}

(notice that, since
u\in\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap C^{2}(\mathcal{V})
, we
have
(-\Delta)^{s}u\in C(\mathcal{V})
).

As a consequence of the above formula, it is then natural to define the
fractional Laplacian

(-\Delta)^{s}u

of a function
u\in\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
as the

linear functional
acting on the space
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
as follows

(2.3)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(\varphi)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.

Since we are assuming that
u\in\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap H^{s}(\mathcal{O})
, and since the kernel

|z|^{-n-2s}
is integrable at infinity, it is easy to recognize
that the functional
(-\Delta)^{s}u
is actually a
distribution on
\mathcal{O}
; more precisely,
for every compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{O}
there exists a constant

C>0
such that

(2.4)

\begin{gathered}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)||\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq C\big(1+d(K,\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{O})^{-2s}\big)\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{O})}\\[2.84544pt]
\text{for every $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})$ with $\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq K$}\end{gathered}

(here, the constant
C
depends on
n,s
and on
u
), and this is enough to guarantee
that
(-\Delta)^{s}u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
(as
\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}\leq c\,\|\varphi\|_{C^{1}(K)}
for some absolute constant
c>0
).
In the particular case when
u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
the above (
2.4
) shows that the distribution

(-\Delta)^{s}u
can be continuously extended to

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

(recall that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is
dense
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
), and thus

(-\Delta)^{s}u\in(H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{\prime}.

In this case, we also have

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}[u+tv]^{2}_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}=(-\Delta)^{s}u(v)\quad\text{for all $v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$}.

ii) The space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

Now we have briefly recalled some basic facts
from the Weak Theory for
(-\Delta)^{s}
, we are in a position
to introduce the adequate functional setting for the study of mixed local-nonlocal operators.

Let then
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega
.
In view of the usual definition of the (local) Sobolev space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
and taking into account (
2.3
), we
define the space

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as the completion
of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
w.r.t. the
global norm

\rho(u):=\left(\||\nabla u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u]^{2}_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right)^{1/2},\qquad u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Due to its relevance in the sequel, we also introduce a distinguished notation
for the
cone of the non-negative functions
in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
: we set

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega):=\{u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega):\,\text{$u\geq 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}\}.

Since this norm
\rho
is induced by the scalar product

\mathcal{B}(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\langle u,v\rangle_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}

(where
\cdot
denotes the usual scalar product in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
), the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

is a real
Hilbert space
; most importantly, since
\Omega
is bounded
and
\partial\Omega
is Lipschitz, by combining the above
(
2.1
) with the classical Poincaré
inequality we infer that

\vartheta^{-1}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\rho(u)\leq\vartheta\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad\text{for every $u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$},

where
\vartheta>1
is a suitable constant depending on
n,s
and on
|\Omega|
.
Thus,
\rho(\cdot)
and the full
H^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

are
actually equivalent
on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, so that

(2.5)

\begin{split}\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)&=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,\,\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}\\
&=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$ and
$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\}.\end{split}

We explicitly observe that, on account of (
2.5
),
the functions in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
naturally
satisfy the nonlocal Dirichlet condition
prescribed in problem (

P
ε

)
λ
, that is,

\text{$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$ for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

Remark 2.1

(Properties of the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
)
.

For a future reference, we list in this remark some properties
of the function space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
which will be repeatedly
exploited in the rest of the paper.

1)

Since both
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
are
closed
under the
maximum/minimum operation, it is readily seen that

u_{\pm}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\quad\text{for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$},

where
u_{+}=\max\{u,0\}
and
u_{-}=\max\{-u,0\}
.

2)

On account of (
2.2
), for every

u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we have

(2.6)

[u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq\beta\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx.

As a consequence,
the norm
\rho_{\varepsilon}
is
globally equivalent
on
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
to the

H_{0}^{1}
-norm: in fact, by (
2.6
) there exists a constant
\Theta=\Theta_{n,s}>0
such that

(2.7)

\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\rho(u)\leq\Theta\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

3)

By the (local) Sobolev inequality, for every
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

we have

\displaystyle S_{n}\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\leq\rho(u)^{2}.

This, together with Hölder’s inequality
(recall that
\Omega
is
bounded
), proves the
continuous embedding

\text{$\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)$ for every $1\leq p\leq 2^{*}$}.

4)

By combining (
2.7
) with the
compact embedding
of

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)
(holding true for every
1\leq p<2^{*}
),
we derive that also the embedding

\text{$\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\Omega)$ is compact
for every $1\leq p<2^{*}$}.

As a consequence, if
\{u_{k}\}_{k}
is a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, it is possible
to find a (unique) function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that (up to a sub-sequence)

a)

u_{n}\to u
weakly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

b)

u_{n}\to u

strongly
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for every
1\leq p<2^{*}
;

c)

u_{n}\to u
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
.

Clearly, since both
u_{n}
(for all
n\geq 1
) and
u

identically vanish

out of
\Omega
, see
(
2.5
), we can replace

\Omega
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the above assertions b)-c).

We now observe that, since we aim
to consider (a weak realization of) the
\varepsilon
-dependent operator

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=-\Delta+\varepsilon(-\Delta)^{s}

(the latter being the leading operator
of problem (

P
ε

)
λ
), it follows once again
from (
2.3
)
that the bilinear form naturally associated with
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}
is the following

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\varepsilon\,\langle u,v\rangle_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}};

in its turn, this form
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}
induces the
\varepsilon
-dependent quadratic form

\rho_{\varepsilon}(u)=\||\nabla u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\varepsilon\,[u]^{2}_{s}\qquad(\text{for $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}).

While in this perspective it should seem more
natural
to use
the
norm
\rho_{\varepsilon}
in place of
\rho
on the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
it is readily seen that these two norms
are indeed
equivalent on
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

(and equivalent to the
H_{0}^{1}
-norm),
uniformly with respect to
\varepsilon
:
in fact, taking into account (
2.7
) (and since
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
), we have

(2.8)

\||\nabla u|\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\rho_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq\rho(u)\leq\Theta\||\nabla u|\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

for some
\Theta>0
only depending on
n,s
. On account of (
2.8
),
we can indifferently use
\rho_{\varepsilon}(\cdot),\,\rho(\cdot)
and the
H_{0}^{1}
-norm
to define the topology of the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, and this choice
does not produce any dependence on
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Notation.
We conclude this second part of the section
with a short list of notation, which will be used in the sequel;
here, as usual,
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
is a fixed parameter.

1)  Given any open set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(not necessarily bounded), we set

\begin{split}\mathrm{a)}&\,\,\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}(u,v)=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\varepsilon\,\langle u,v\rangle_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,\,(\text{for $u,v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$});\\
\mathrm{b)}&\,\,\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}(u)=\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{O}}(u,u)\,\,(\text{for $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}).\end{split}

Since
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\subseteq H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(see (
2.5
)),
the above forms
\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}
are
well-defined; moreover,
again by taking into account (
2.5
) we have

•

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\Omega}(u,v)=\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u,v)\equiv\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(u,v)

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

•

\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\Omega}(u)=\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon,\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u)\equiv\rho_{\varepsilon}(u)
for all
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

2)  Given any
bounded
open set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set

\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})}:=\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}=\Big(\int_{\mathcal{O}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx\Big)^{1/2}.

iii) Weak sub/supersolutions of problem (

P
ε

)
λ
.
Thanks to
all the preliminaries recalled so far, we are finally ready to give
the precise definition of
weak sub/supersolutions
of problem (

P
ε

)
λ
.
Actually, for a reason which will be clear in Section
3
, we consider
the more general problem

(2.9)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u=\frac{\lambda}{u^{\gamma}}+f(x,u)&\text{in $\Omega$}\\
u>0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u=0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\end{cases}

where
f:\Omega\times(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary Carathéodory function satisfying
the following growth condition:
there exists a constant
K_{f}>0
such that

(2.10)

|f(x,t)|\leq K_{f}(1+t^{2^{*}-1})\quad\text{\emph{for a.e.\,$x\in\Omega$ and every $t>0$}}.

Clearly, problem (

P
ε

)
λ
is of the form (
2.9
), with the choice

f(x,t)=t^{2^{*}-1}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f:\Omega\times(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}

be a Carathéodory function satisfying the above condition
(
2.10
).
We say that a function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a
weak subsolution

(resp.
supersolution
) of problem (
2.9
)
if it satisfies the following properties:

i)

u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
and
u^{-\gamma}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
;

ii)

for every test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\text{$\varphi\geq 0$ in $\Omega$}
,
we have

(2.11)

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(u,\varphi)\leq\,[\text{resp.}\,\geq]\,\,\lambda\int_{\Omega}u^{-\gamma}\varphi\,dx+\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)\varphi\,dx.

We say that
u
is a
weak solution
of problem
(
2.9
) if it is both a weak subsolution and a weak
supersolution of the same problem.

Remark 2.3
.

We now list here below some comments concerning the above Definition
2.2
. In what follows,
we tacitly understand that
f:\Omega\to(0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function satisfying
the growth
assumption (
2.10
).

1)  If
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a weak sub/supersolution of problem
(
2.9
), all the integrals appearing in (
2.11
)

exist and are finite
.
Indeed, by combining
(
2.6
) with the Hölder inequality, for every

\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\text{$\varphi\geq 0$ in $\Omega$}
,
we obtain (recall that
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
)

(2.12)

\begin{split}|\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(u,\varphi)|&\leq\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}+[u]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\cdot[\varphi]_{s,\mathbb{R}^{n}}\\
&\leq(1+\beta^{2})\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}<+\infty.\end{split}

Moreover, using (
2.10
) and the Sobolev inequality, we also get

(2.13)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u)|\cdot|\varphi|&\leq K_{f}\Big(\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}-1}|\varphi|\,dx\Big)\\
&(\text{using H\"{o}lder's inequality})\\
&\leq K_{f}\big(\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}^{2^{*}-1}\cdot\|\varphi\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\big)\\
&\leq K_{f}\big(\|\varphi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+S_{n}^{-2^{*}/2}\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2^{*}-1}\cdot\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\big)\\
&(\text{again by H\"{o}lder's inequality and Poincaré inequality})\\
&\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\big(1+\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2^{*}-1}\big)<+\infty,\end{split}

where
S_{n}>0
is the best Sobolev constant in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
C>0
depends on
n,\,f,\,|\Omega|
.
Finally, since
we are assuming that
u^{-\gamma}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, we obviously
have

0\leq\int_{\Omega}u^{-\gamma}\varphi\,dx\leq\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\int_{\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)}u^{-\gamma}\,dx<+\infty.

We explicitly stress that this last estimate
(which is related with the
singular term

) is the unique estimate involving
the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the test function
\varphi
; on the contrary,
estimates (
2.12
)-(
2.13
) only involve
the
H_{0}^{1}
-norm
of
\varphi
.

2)  If
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a weak
solution
of
problem
(
2.9
), and if
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a non-negative
test function (that is,
\varphi\geq 0
in

), by (

)-(

)
we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\Omega}u^{-\gamma}\varphi\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(u,\varphi)+\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)\varphi\,dx\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\big(1+\|u\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2^{*}-1}\big);

from this, by using a standard
density argument
,
and by taking into account Remark
2.1
-1), we can easily prove the following facts:

a)

u^{-\gamma}\varphi\in L^{1}(\Omega)

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;

b)

identity (
2.11
)
actually holds
for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, see
[
36
]
.

3)  Let
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
be a weak
solution
of problem (
2.9
).
Since, in particular, we know that

u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
(and
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
), it is quite easy
to recognize that
u
is a
weak supersolution of the equation

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u=0\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

in the sense of
[

, Definition 2.5]
(see also

[
34
, Remark 2.6]

and note that, by
(
2.1
) and the definition
of
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we have

).
As a consequence, we are entitled to apply
[

, Lemma 8.1]
, ensuring that

\begin{gathered}\text{for every $\mathcal{O}\Subset\Omega$ there exists $c(\mathcal{O},u)>0$
s.t.\,$u\geq c(\mathcal{O},u)>0$ a.e.\,in $\mathcal{O}$}.\end{gathered}

We explicitly notice that, since the function

intrinsically depends on

(as it is a weak supersolution of an equation depending on

.

3.
Some auxiliary results

Before embarking on the proof of Theorem

i)  Weak Harnack-type inequality for
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}+a(x)
.
The first auxiliary result we aim to prove is a

(3.1)

\text{$\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}u+a(x)u=0$ in $\Omega$},

where
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\text{$a\geq 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}
. As already
discussed in the introduction, such a result turns out to be a proper substitute
of
[

, and it will be used as a
key tool
in the proof of Theorem
1.1
-b).

To begin with, since equation (
3.1
)
is not
a particular
case of the one appearing in problem
(
2.9
), we give the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. We say that a function

u\in\mathcal{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
is a

weak subsolution
(resp.
supersolution
) of equation (
3.1
) if

(3.2)

\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}(u,\varphi)+\int_{\mathcal{O}}a(x)u\varphi\,dx\leq\,[\text{resp}.\,\geq]\,\,0

for every
\mathcal{O}\Subset\Omega
and every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\mathcal{O})
.

Remark 3.2
.

By combining (
2.1
)-(
2.2
) with
(
2.4
), it is easy to recognize that
the above Definition

is well-posed
. In fact,
let
\mathcal{V}
be a bounded open set
with smooth boundary

\mathcal{O}\Subset\mathcal{V}\Subset\Omega
.
Since
u\in H^{1}(\mathcal{\mathcal{V}})
(recall that

and

), from (
2.1
) we deduce that

u\in H^{s}(\mathcal{V})
; as a consequence, we are then entitled to
apply (
2.4
) with the choice
K=\overline{\mathcal{O}}\Subset\mathcal{V}
: this gives

\begin{gathered}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)||\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq C\big(1+d(\overline{\mathcal{O}},\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})^{-2s}\big)\|\varphi\|_{H^{s}(\mathcal{V})}\end{gathered}

and this estimate holds for every

(here,

is a constant

and on

Now, by combining this last estimate with

),
we get

\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)||\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dx\,dy\leq C^{\prime}\big(1+d(\overline{\mathcal{O}},\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{V})^{-2s}\big)\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})},

for a suitable constant

;
from this, by taking into account that
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
is dense in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\mathcal{O})\subseteq H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we
easily
derive that

|\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon,\mathcal{O}}(u,\varphi)|<+\infty\quad\text{for every $\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\mathcal{O})$}.

Finally, since

, we also have

\int_{\mathcal{O}}|a(x)u\varphi|\,dx\leq\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}<+\infty

and this proves that Definition

is well-posed.

With Definition

at hand, we are ready to state the

3.1
).
Throughout what follows, if

(with respect to

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
a\in L^{\infty}(\Omega),\,\text{$a\geq 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}
. Moreover,
let

be a weak supersolution of
equation (

) such that

Then, there exist

and

,

, such that

(3.3)

\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u^{Q}\,dx\right)^{1/Q}\leq c\essinf_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u+c\left(\dfrac{r}{R}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Tail}(u_{-};x_{0},R),

whenever

.

In order to prove Proposition

first establish the following

Lemma 3.4
.

Let

, and
let

[

]

3.1

We arbitrarily fix

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\psi^{2}|\nabla w|^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\,\iint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times B_{r}(x_{0})}\dfrac{|w(x)\psi^{2}(x)-w(y)\psi^{2}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}w^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+\iint_{B_{r}(x_{0})\times B_{r}(x_{0})}\max\{w(x),w(y)\}\dfrac{|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

(4.15)

\text{$u_{\lambda}\geq\underline{u}\geq C>0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega_{\delta}$},

\text{$v=\overline{u}-u_{\lambda}\geq C_{1}>0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega_{\delta}$}.

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\underline{S}_{j}}\Big\{\frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma}(|\underline{u}-\underline{w}_{j}|^{1-\gamma}-|\underline{u}|^{1-\gamma})+\frac{1}{2^{*}}(|\underline{u}-\underline{w}_{j}|^{2^{*}}-|\underline{u}|^{2^{*}})\Big\}dx.

\displaystyle\geq I_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(v_{j})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big([\overline{w}_{j}]^{2}_{s}+[\underline{w}_{j}]^{2}_{s}\big)+\varepsilon\,\langle\overline{u},\overline{w}_{j}\rangle_{s,\mathbb{R}^{2n}}-\varepsilon,\langle\underline{u},\underline{w}_{j}\rangle_{s,\mathbb{R}^{2n}}+\mathcal{R}^{(1)}_{j}+\mathcal{R}^{(2)}_{j}

\displaystyle 0

Proof.

We closely follow the approach in
[
38

. First of all, we

ε

)

\displaystyle I_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\leq I_{\lambda,\varepsilon}(u_{\lambda})+\frac{t^{2}R^{2}}{2}K_{1}-\frac{t^{2^{*}}R^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}K_{2}

&\qquad-t^{2^{*}-1}R^{2^{*}-1}D_{0}\varepsilon^{\alpha(n-2)/2}\\
&\qquad\qquad+C(tR+t^{2}R^{2}+t^{2^{*}}R^{2^{*}}+R^{\beta})o(\varepsilon^{\alpha(n-2)/2}).\end{split}

Notice that the above choice of

such that

(5.31)

\begin{cases}I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda}+RU_{\varepsilon})<I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})&\text{for all $R\geq R_{0}$},\\
I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda}+tR_{0}U_{\varepsilon})<I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{n}S_{n}^{n/2}&\text{for all $t\in[0,1]$}.\end{cases}

In particular, from (
5.31
) we easily see that

\eta_{0}(t)=u_{\lambda}+tR_{0}U_{\varepsilon}\in\Gamma

(by enlarging
R_{0}
if needed),
and thus
\Gamma\neq\varnothing
, as claimed.

Now we have proved that
\Gamma\neq\varnothing
, we can proceed towards the end of the proof.
To this end we first observe that, since it is non-empty, this set
\Gamma
is a

complete metric space
, when endowed with the distance

d_{\Gamma}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}):=\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}\big(\eta_{1}(t)-\eta_{2}(t)\big);

moreover, since
I_{\lambda}
is
real-valued and continuous
on
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
it is easy to recognize that the functional
\Phi:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\Phi(\eta):=\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}I_{\lambda}(\eta(t)),

is (well-defined and) continuous on
\Gamma
. In view of these facts,
we are then entitled to apply the Ekeland Variational Principle
to this functional
\Phi
on
\Gamma
: setting

\gamma_{0}:=\inf{\Gamma}\Phi(\eta),

there exists a sequence
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k}\subseteq\Gamma
such that

(5.32)

\begin{split}\mathrm{i)}&\,\,\Phi(\eta_{k})\leq\gamma_{0}+1/k,\\
\mathrm{ii)}&\,\,\Phi(\eta_{k})\leq\Phi(\eta)+1/k\,d_{\Gamma}(\eta_{k},\eta)\quad\text{for every $\eta\in\Gamma$}.\end{split}

Now, starting from
(
5.32
) and proceeding exactly as in the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 3.5]
(see also

[
37
, Lemma 4.3]
), we can find another sequence

v_{k}=\eta_{k}(t_{k})\in T

(for some
t_{k}\in[0,1]
) such that

a)
I_{\lambda}(v_{k})\to\gamma_{0}
as
k\to+\infty
;

b)  there exists some
C>0
such that, for every
w\in T
, one has

(5.33)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{B}_{\varepsilon}(v_{k},w-v_{k})-\lambda\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{-\gamma}(w-v_{k})\,dx\\
&\qquad\qquad-\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2^{*}-1}(w-v_{k})\,dx\geq-\frac{C}{k}(1+{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(w)).\end{split}

In particular, choosing
w=2v_{k}
in (
5.33
), we get

(5.34)

{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{2}-\lambda\int_{\Omega}v^{1-\gamma}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2^{*}}\,dx\geq-\frac{C}{k}(1+2{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})).

By combining (
5.34
)
with assertion a), and exploiting Hölder’s and Sobolev’s inequalities,
we then obtain the following estimate

(5.35)

\begin{split}\gamma_{0}+o(1)&=\frac{1}{2}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{1-\gamma}\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{1-\gamma}\,dx-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2^{*}}\,dx\\
&\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\Big){\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{2}-\lambda\Big(\frac{1}{1-\gamma}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\Big)\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{1-\gamma}\,dx\\
&\qquad-\frac{C}{2^{*}\,k}(1+2{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k}))\\
&\geq\Big(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\Big){\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{2}-C\big({\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{1-\gamma}-2{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})-1\big),\end{split}

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
n
and on
|\Omega|
. Since, obviously,

c_{0}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}>0,

it is readily seen from (
5.35
) that the sequence
\{v_{k}\}_{k}

is
bounded in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
(otherwise, by possibly choosing a sub-sequence
we would have
{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})\to+\infty
, and hence
the right-hand side of (
5.35
) would diverges as
k\to+\infty
, which
is not possible).

In view of this fact, we can thus proceed as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
to
show that
\{v_{k}\}_{k}
weakly converges (up to a sub-sequence)
to a
weak solution

v_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
of
problem (

P
ε

)
λ
,
further satisfying the identity

(5.36)

{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k}-v_{\lambda})^{2}-\|v_{k}-v_{\lambda}\|^{2^{*}}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}=o(1)\quad\text{as $k\to+\infty$}.

In view of these facts, to complete the proof we are left to show that

v_{\lambda}\not\equiv u_{\lambda}
. To this end we first observe that,
given any
\eta\in\Gamma
, we have

\text{${\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\eta(0)-u_{\lambda})=0$\quad and \quad${\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\eta(1)-u_{\lambda})>\varrho_{1}$},

and hence there exists a point
t_{\eta}\in[0,1]
such that
{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(\eta(t_{\eta})-u_{\lambda})=\varrho_{1}
;
as a consequence, since
we are assuming that

Case B)
holds, we obtain

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\eta\in\Gamma}\Phi(\eta)\geq\inf\big\{I_{\lambda}(\eta(t_{\eta})):\,\eta\in\Gamma\big\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf\{I_{\lambda}(u):\text{$u\in T$ and ${\rho_{\varepsilon}}(u-u_{\lambda})=\varrho_{1}$}\}>I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda}).

On the other hand, since we already know that
\eta_{0}(t)=u_{\lambda}+tR_{0}U_{\varepsilon}\in\Gamma
,
from (
5.31
) (and the very definition of
\gamma_{0}
) we derive the following
estimate

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(\eta_{0})=\max_{0\leq t\leq 1}I_{\lambda}(\eta_{0}(t))<I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{n}S_{n}^{n/2}.

Summing up, we have

(5.37)

I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})<\gamma_{0}<I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})+\frac{1}{n}S_{n}^{n/2}.

Now, since the sequence
\{v_{k}\}_{k}
weakly converges in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

to
v_{\lambda}
as
k\to+\infty
,

the same assertions
in (
5.25
)
hold also in this context; this, together with (
5.37
)
and the above property a) of the sequence
\{v_{k}\}_{k}
, gives

(5.38)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k}-v_{\lambda})^{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\|v_{k}-v_{\lambda}\|^{2^{*}}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\big({\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{k})^{2}-{\rho_{\varepsilon}}(v_{\lambda})^{2}\big)-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\big(\|v_{k}\|^{2^{*}}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}-\|v\|^{2^{*}}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\big)+o(1)\\[4.26773pt]
&\qquad=I_{\lambda}(v_{k})-I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})+o(1)=\gamma_{0}-I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})+o(1)\\
&\qquad<\frac{1}{n}S_{n}^{n/2}-\delta_{0},\end{split}

for some
\delta_{0}>0
such that
1/n\,S_{n}^{n/2}-\delta_{0}>0
(provided that
k
is large enough).

Gathering (
5.36
)-(
5.38
),
and arguing as in
[
47
, Proposition 3.1]
, it is then easy to recognize that

v_{k}\to v_{\lambda}

strongly in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
;
as a consequence, by the continuity of the functional

I_{\lambda}
and by (
5.33
)-(
5.37
), we get

I_{\lambda}(u_{\lambda})<\gamma_{0}=\lim_{k\to+\infty}I_{\lambda}(v_{k})=I_{\lambda}(v_{\lambda}),

and this finally proves that
v_{\lambda}\not\equiv u_{\lambda}
, as desired.
∎
