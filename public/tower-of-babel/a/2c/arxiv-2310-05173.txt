On quadratic polynomial mappings from ℂ 3 \mathbb{C}^{3} to ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} Thanks: The author was partially supported by the grant of Narodowe Centrum Nauki number 2019/33/B/ST1/00755.
M. Farnik

Abstract
. We classify quadratic polynomial mappings from ℂ 3 \mathbb{C}^{3} to ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} up to affine equivalence and topological equivalence. This is a part of a larger project, we have already classified mappings from ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} to ℂ n \mathbb{C}^{n} and inted to classify mappings from ℂ 3 \mathbb{C}^{3} to ℂ 3 \mathbb{C}^{3} .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
14D06, 14Q20, 14R99, 51M99

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}^{k}}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
denote the space of polynomial mappings
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):\mathbb{K}^{k}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
where
\deg f_{i}\leq d_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
F,G\in\Omega_{\mathbb{K}^{k}}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
. We say that
F
is
topologically
(respectively
affinely
or
linearly
)
equivalent
to
G
if there are homeomorphisms (respectively affine or linear automorphisms)
\Phi:\mathbb{K}^{k}\to\mathbb{K}^{k}
and
\Psi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
such that
F=\Psi\circ G\circ\Phi
. In the papers
[
3
,
4
]
it was shown that for
\mathbb{K}
equal
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{R}
and a fixed
n>0
the space
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}^{2}}(2,\ldots,2)
of quadratic
\mathbb{K}^{2}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
mappings splits into a finite number of equivalence classes with respect to affine equivalence (hence also with respect to topological equivalence). Moreover, the authors provided a full classification of mappings in
\Omega_{\mathbb{K}^{2}}(2,\ldots,2)
. Here we focus on classifying the mappings in
\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(2,2)
. We believe that obtaining the classification of
\Omega_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(2,2)
using similar methods is also possible but significantly more complicated than in the complex case. In the future we intend to classify mappings in
\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(2,2,2)
, classifying quadratic mappings from
\mathbb{C}^{3}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a natural first step towards achieving this goal.

Unlike in the
\mathbb{K}^{2}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
case, there are infinitely many equivalence classes of quadratic
\mathbb{C}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
mappings with respect to affine equivalence. This is quite obvious since
\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(2,2)
is an affine space of dimension
20
and the groups of of affine automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
\mathbb{C}^{2}
have dimensions
12
and
6
, respectively. However,
\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(2,2)
splits into a finite number of equivalence classes with respect to topological equivalence. In fact there is a Zariski open dense subset
U\subset\Omega_{\mathbb{C}^{3}}(2,2)
such that every mapping
F\in U
has the same, generic, topological type. If a mapping
f
has a generic topological type then we say that
f
is a
topologically generic
mapping. The affine equivalence classes of topologically generic mappings are parametrized by a two dimensional variety. There are also three topological types with affine equivalence classes parametrized by one dimensional varieties.

Now we present our main result – we give the complete classification of quadratic polynomial mappings from
\mathbb{C}^{3}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with respect to affine equivalence and with respect to topological equivalence. The notation and definitions are set up in Section
2
. We select only one representative for each family of topologically equivalent mappings having infinitely many affine equivalence classes, they are
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
F_{4}
and
F_{8}
. Items are enumerated according to the topological equivalence. We use the same number and distinct letters to enumerate affine equivalence classes of topologically equivalent mappings. There are
47
classes for topological equivalence. There are
4
infinite families and
60
classes for affine equivalence.

Notation and definitions

Throughout the paper we will consider mappings
F=(f,g):\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We take
f=a_{1}x^{2}+a_{2}xy+a_{3}xz+a_{4}y^{2}+a_{5}yz+a_{6}z^{2}+a_{7}x+a_{8}y+a_{9}z+a_{10}
and, similarly,
g=b_{1}x^{2}+\ldots+b_{10}
. Note that
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
denote coefficients at the appropriate monomials and often change. After composing
F
with an affine or linear automorphism the coefficients change but we do not introduce new symbols. By
h_{x}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}
we denote the partial differential of a function
h
with respect to a variable
x
. We denote
m_{xy}=f_{x}g_{y}-f_{y}g_{x}
and similarly for
m_{xz}
and
m_{yz}
. We denote the critical set of
F
by
C(F)=V(m_{xy},m_{xz},m_{yz})
, we call the set of critical of
F
the discriminant of
F
and denote it by by
\Delta(F)=F(C(F))
.

We denote by
\tilde{F}=(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
the mapping obtained by taking the homogeneous parts of maximal degree of the components of a mapping
F=(f,g)
. If both components of
F
are of degree two then we will often consider
\tilde{F}
as a birational mapping
(\tilde{f}:\tilde{g}):\mathbb{P}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
. We take
\tilde{m}_{xy}=\tilde{f}_{x}\tilde{g}_{y}-\tilde{f}_{y}\tilde{g}_{x}
, it is the homogeneous part of maximal degree of
m_{xy}
.

We use
x
,
y
and
z
for the coordinates in the source
\mathbb{C}^{3}
or
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and
p
,
q
for the coordinates in the target
\mathbb{C}^{2}
or
\mathbb{P}^{1}.
The letters
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
,
A
,
B
will denote parameters or coordinates in parameter space.

If there is no danger of confusion we will omit the arguments of a mapping, i.e., we will write
(f_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\ldots,f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))
instead of

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto
(f_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),\ldots,f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))
.

Let
F:\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a mapping. We say that
P\in C(F)
is a
cusp
of

if
F^{-1}(F(P))\cap\mathbb{C}(F)=\{P\}
and there are neighborhoods
U_{1}
of
P_{1}
and
U_{2}
of
F(P)
, open sets
V_{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
V_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
and biholomorphic mappings
h_{1}:(U_{1},P)\rightarrow(V_{1},0)
,
h_{2}(U_{2},F(P))\rightarrow(V_{2},0)
such that
F|_{U_{1}}=h_{2}^{-1}\circ F_{1}\circ h_{1}
for
F_{1}(x,y,z)=(x,y^{3}+xy+z^{2})
. Note that if

is a cusp of

then

is a smooth point of
C(F)
and a critical point of the restriction
F|_{C(F)}
. Moreover the irreducible component of
\Delta(F)
containing

is a curve with a cusp singularity at
F(P)
.

Similarly, we call
P\in C(F)
a
double cusp
if
F
restricted to some neighborhood of

is biholomorphically equivalent to
(x,y^{4}+xy+z^{2})
.

We say that
P_{1},P_{2}\in C(F)
are a

of

if
F^{-1}(F(P_{1}))\cap\mathbb{C}(F)=\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
, the points
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
belong to the same irreducible component of
C(F)
and there are neighborhoods
U_{1}
of
P_{1}
,
U_{2}
of
P_{2}
and
U_{3}
of
F(P_{1})
, open sets
V_{1},V_{2}\subset\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
V_{3}\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
and biholomorphic mappings
h_{1}:(U_{1},P_{1})\rightarrow(V_{1},0)
,
h_{2}(U_{2},P_{2})\rightarrow(V_{2},0)
and
h_{3}(U_{3},F(P_{1}))\rightarrow(V_{3},0)
such that

for

and

for
F_{1}(x,y,z)=(y^{2}+z^{2},x)
. Note that if
P_{1}
and

are a node of

are smooth points of

and regular points of the restriction

containing

4.

Proof.

Step 1.
Scheme of the proof.

By the argument above it is sufficient to show that the family of mappings
F_{A,B}=(x^{2}+Bz^{2}+2Ay,Ay^{2}+Bz^{2}+2x+2Bz)
, for

. Since

is connected it is enough to show local topological triviality. Let
\mathcal{F}=(F_{A,B},\id_{V}):\mathbb{C}^{3}\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}\times V

We will loosely follow the methods developed in Section 3 of
[
2
]
. We pass to the real structure to construct and integrate real vector fields. To keep the notation brief we will use the complex coordinates when possible. We identify a complex point
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with the real point
(\Ree x_{1},\Imm x_{1},\ldots,\Ree x_{k},\Imm x_{k})
and a complex mapping
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})
with a real mapping
(\Ree f_{1},\Imm f_{1},\ldots,\Ree f_{k},\Imm f_{k})

Fix some

be a small closed ball centered at

and contained in
V
.
Take

and let
\partial_{0}
be the constant vector field on
K
with value
(A-A_{0},B-B_{0})/||(A-A_{0},B-B_{0})||
. Let
\gamma:[0,\varepsilon]\rightarrow K
be the unique integral curve of
\partial_{0}
with
\gamma(0)=(A_{0},B_{0})
. Obviously
\gamma(t_{1})=(A,B)
for
t_{1}=||(A-A_{0},B-B_{0})||
. Let
\pi
be the projection from
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times K
to
K
. We will construct vector fields
w_{0}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times K
and
v_{0}
on
\mathbb{C}^{3}\times K
such that
d\pi(w_{0})=\partial_{0}
and
d\mathcal{F}(v_{0})=w_{0}
. Note that we treat here
\pi
as a restriction of projection
\mathbb{R}^{8}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
\mathcal{F}
as a
\mathbb{R}^{10}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{8}
mapping. If we construct the vector fields correctly, then for a point
(p_{0},q_{0})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
there will be
\gamma_{1}
– the unique integral curve of
w_{0}
such that
\gamma_{1}(0)=(p_{0},q_{0},A_{0},B_{0})
. Then,
\gamma_{1}(t_{1})=(p_{1},q_{1},A,B)
for some
(p_{1},q_{1})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. We define a homeomorphism
\Psi_{A,B}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
by setting
\Psi_{A,B}(p_{0},q_{0})=(p_{1},q_{1})
. Similarly, we define a homeomorphism
\Phi_{A,B}:\mathbb{C}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{3}
by setting
\Phi_{A,B}(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})=(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})
, where
(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1},A,B)=\gamma_{2}(t_{1})
and
\gamma_{2}
is the unique integral curve of

with
\gamma_{2}(0)=(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0},A_{0},B_{0})

.

There are two conditions that we will need

and

to satisfy. Firstly, the vector fields will be rugose, this implies that

and
\gamma_{2}
are unique and

for all

(see
[

Step 2.
Stratification and rugosity.

We define the stratification

\mathcal{S}=\{Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}\}=\{\mathbb{C}^{2}\times V\setminus\Delta(\mathcal{F}),\Delta(\mathcal{F})\setminus\Sing(\Delta(\mathcal{F})),\Sing(\Delta(\mathcal{F}))\}.

is a Whitney stratification. Moreover,

if and only if

and
(p,q,A,B)\in\Sing(\Delta(\mathcal{F}))
if and only if
(p,q)\in\Sing(\Delta(F_{A,B}))
. See
[

for the proof of an analogous fact for

We also define a Whitney stratification on
\mathbb{C}^{3}\times V

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5}\}=\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}(Y_{1}),\mathcal{F}^{-1}(Y_{2})\cap C(\mathcal{F}),\mathcal{F}^{-1}(Y_{3})\cap C(\mathcal{F})

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(Y_{2})\setminus C(\mathcal{F}),\mathcal{F}^{-1}(Y_{3})\setminus C(\mathcal{F})\}.

Note that
(x,y,z,A,B)\in C(\mathcal{F})
if and only if
(x,y,z)\in C(F_{A,B})
and
(x,y,z,A,B)\in X_{3}

and

Let us now recall the definition of a rugose vector field. Let
X\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a variety with stratification
\{X_{i}\}
, let
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a real function. We say that
\phi
is a
rugose function
if the following conditions are fulfilled:

•

The restriction
\phi|_{X_{i}}
to any stratum
X_{i}
is a smooth function.

•

For any stratum
X_{i}
and for any

there exists a neighborhood
U
of

in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and a constant
C>0
such that for any

and any

we have
|\phi(y)-\phi(x_{1})|\leq C||y-x_{1}||
.

A
rugose map
is a map whose components are rugose functions. A vector field
v
on
X
is called a rugose vector field if
v
is a rugose map and
v(x)
is tangent to the stratum containing
x
for any
x\in X
.

The stratification
\mathcal{S}
induces a Whitney stratification on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times K
consisting of the restricted strata. We will construct the vector field

so that it will be rugose with respect to the stratification induced by
\mathcal{S}
. For an open subset

by a rugose vector field we will mean a vector field rugose with respect to the stratification
\{Y_{i}\cap U\}
. For a biholomorphic mapping
\phi:U\mapsto U^{\prime}
we obtain the Whitney stratification
\{\phi(Y_{i}\cap U)\}
of

which we use to define rugose vector fields on
U^{\prime}
. In this setting, if we construct a rugose vector field on
U^{\prime}
then by lifting it to
U
via
\phi
we obtain a rugose vector field on
U
. Similarly, the vector field

will be rugose with respect to the stratification induced by
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
.

F_{52}=(xy+z,x),\quad F_{53}=(xy,x)

and for
\tilde{F}=\tilde{F}_{15}
we obtain

(6.44)

F_{54}=(x^{2}+y^{2}+z,0),\quad F_{55}=(x^{2}+y^{2},0).

For
F
such that
\tilde{F}=\tilde{F}_{16}
we have

(6.45)

F=(x^{2}+a_{7}x+a_{8}y+a_{9}z+a_{10},y+b_{10}).

By composing
F
with translations we can assume that
a_{7}=a_{10}=b_{10}=0
and obtain
F=(x^{2}+a_{8}y+a_{9}z,y)
. Composing with
(p-a_{8}q,q)
we obtain
a_{8}=0
and depending on whether
a_{9}=0
we obtain one of the following two:

(6.46)

F_{56}=(x^{2}+z,y),\quad F_{57}=(x^{2},y).

For
F
such that
\tilde{F}=\tilde{F}_{17}
we have

(6.47)

F=(x^{2}+a_{7}x+a_{8}y+a_{9}z+a_{10},x+b_{10}).

By composing
F
with translations we can assume that
a_{7}=a_{10}=b_{10}=0
and obtain
F=(x^{2}+a_{8}y+a_{9}z,x)
. Assume that
a_{8}\neq 0
or
a_{9}\neq 0
, by symmetry we can take
a_{8}\neq 0
. Composing with
(x,a_{8}^{-1}(y-a_{9}z),z)
we obtain

(6.48)

F_{58}=(x^{2}+y,x).

On the other hand, for
a_{8}=a_{9}=0
we have

(6.49)

F_{59}=(x^{2},x).

Similarly, for
\tilde{F}=\tilde{F}_{18}
we obtain

(6.50)

F_{60}=(x^{2}+y,0),\quad F_{61}=(x^{2},0).

Finally for
F
equal to
\tilde{F}_{19}
,
\tilde{F}_{20}
or
\tilde{F}_{21}
we must have
F=(\tilde{f}+a_{10},\tilde{g}+b_{10})
, so after composing with a translation we obtain

(6.51)

F_{62}=(x,y),\quad F_{63}=(x,0),\quad F_{64}=(0,0).
