Fast and robust cross-validation-based scoring rule inference for spatial statistics
Helga Kristín Ólafsdóttir, Holger Rootzén, David Bolin

Abstract
Scoring rules are aimed at evaluation of the quality of predictions, but can also be used for estimation of parameters in statistical models. We propose estimating parameters of multivariate spatial models by maximising the average leave-one-out cross-validation score. This method, LOOS \LOOS , thus optimises predictions instead of maximising the likelihood. The method allows for fast computations for Gaussian models with sparse precision matrices, such as spatial Markov models. It also makes it possible to tailor the estimator’s robustness to outliers and their sensitivity to spatial variations of uncertainty through the choice of the scoring rule which is used in the maximisation. The effects of the choice of scoring rule which is used in LOOS \LOOS are studied by simulation in terms of computation time, statistical efficiency, and robustness. Various popular scoring rules and a new scoring rule, the root score, are compared to maximum likelihood estimation. The results confirmed that for spatial Markov models the computation time for LOOS \LOOS was much smaller than for maximum likelihood estimation. Furthermore, the standard deviations of parameter estimates were smaller for maximum likelihood estimation, although the differences often were small. The simulations also confirmed that the usage of a robust scoring rule results in robust LOOS estimates and that the robustness provides better predictive quality for spatial data with outliers. Finally, the new inference method was applied to ERA5 temperature reanalysis data for the contiguous United States and the average July temperature for the years 1940 to 2023, and this showed that the LOOS estimator provided parameter estimates that were more than a hundred times faster to compute compared to maximum-likelihood estimation, and resulted in a model with better predictive performance.



1
Introduction

Proper scoring rules are widely used for forecast evaluation and statistical model comparisons in areas such as meteorology, climate science and finance
(
Lerch and Thorarinsdottir, 2013
,
Friederichs and Thorarinsdottir, 2012
,
Carvalho, 2016
,
Serafini et al., 2022
)
. The observations are compared to the predictive distribution through scores, with higher values implying a better prediction. To keep the forecaster earnest, the expected score should be maximised for the true model. When this property holds, the score is said to be proper
(
Gneiting and Raftery, 2007
)
.

Specifically, a scoring rule
S
is a function which takes a predictive distribution
\mathbb{P}
and an observation
x
of a random variable
X
and returns a real-valued score
S(\mathbb{P},x)
which gives a measure of the quality of the prediction, where in this paper higher values represent higher quality. Suppose
X
has distribution
\mathbb{Q}
, and let
S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}):=E_{\mathbb{Q}}[S(\mathbb{P},X)]
denote the expectation of
S(\mathbb{P},X)
.
The scoring rule
S
is said to be proper for a set of distributions
\mathcal{P}
if for all
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}

S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\leq S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}).

If strict inequality holds, the score is said to be strictly proper.

In practise, average scoring rules are used for model comparison. That is, if
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
is a vector of observations, and
\mathbb{P}
denotes a statistical model with corresponding predictive distributions,
\mathbb{P}_{1},\ldots,\mathbb{P}_{m}
, then the score of
\mathbb{P}
is typically computed as

\bar{S}(\mathbb{P},x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}S(\mathbb{P}_{i},y_{i}).

It is easy to see that if
S
is proper, then so is the average score.

Many proper scoring rules exist, such as the logarithmic score
{\slog(\mathbb{P},y)=\log f(y)}
, where
f(\cdot)
is the density of
\mathbb{P}

(
Good, 1952
)
. Another popular scoring rule is the Hyvärinen score
(
Hyvärinen, 2005
)

{S(\mathbb{P},y)=\partial^{2}\log f(y)-0.5|\partial\log f(y)|^{2}}
, where
\partial
is the derivative with respect to
y
. The arguably most popular scoring rule is the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)
(
Baringhaus and Franz, 2004
,
Gneiting and Raftery, 2007
,
Zamo and Naveau, 2018
)
, which is a kernel score
(
Dawid, 2007
)
and a part of a wider class of proper scoring rules called generalised kernel scores
(
Bolin and Wallin, 2023
)
that can be formulated as

S_{g}^{h}(\mathbb{P},y)=h(\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}}[g(X,X^{\prime})])+2h^{\prime}(\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}}[g(X,X^{\prime})])(\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[g(X,y)]-\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}}[g(X,X^{\prime})])

(1)

where
g
is a non-negative continuous negative definite kernel and
h
is a monotonically decreasing convex function
(
Bolin and Wallin, 2023
)
. For CRPS, the function
h
is chosen as
h(x)=-\frac{1}{2}x
and
g(x,y)=|x-y|
, while
h(x)=-\log(x)
and
g(x,y)=|x-y|
results in the scaled CRPS (SCRPS)
(
Bolin and Wallin, 2023
)
.
The choice
h(x)=-\sqrt{x}
and
g(x,y)=|x-y|
results in a score which in this paper will be called the root score and denoted
\sroot
. The properties of
\sroot

are in between those of
\CRPS
and the
\SCRPS
in terms of, e.g., robustness, see Appendix
A
.

While all these scores are proper, they emphasise different aspects of the predictions and have various advantages and disadvantages. The Hyvärinen score is homogeneous in the density function, meaning that the normalising constant is unnecessary for parameter estimation, which can be beneficial when the normalising constant is difficult to calculate
(
Hyvärinen, 2005
,
Hyvärinen, 2007
,
De Fondeville and Davison, 2018
)
. The logarithmic score and the SCRPS are scale invariant, meaning that the observations will contribute equally to the expected score, regardless of the model uncertainty at the observed locations
(
Bolin and Wallin, 2023
)
. For further details on scale invariance, see Appendix
A
. Moreover, for models with unknown or ill-obtainable density, inference can be found through scores such as the CRPS, since it consists of expected values that can be computed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.
Therefore, the choice of scoring rule is important when comparing statistical models.

Proper scoring rules have also been used for statistical inference. For a parametric statistical model, with parameters
\bm{\theta}
, optimising a proper score
S
with respect to the parameters yields an asymptotically unbiased estimator
(
Dawid et al., 2016
)
, and performing statistical inference based on scoring rules can be advantageous. For example,
De Fondeville and Davison (2018)
used scoring rules for inference of certain extreme value models which are difficult to fit using likelihood-based methods. As pointed out by
Gneiting and Raftery (2007)
, the advantage with optimum score estimation is that the choice of the scoring rule can be adapted to the problem at hand. One example is
Gneiting et al. (2005)
where they showed empirically that a predictive regression model estimated using a CRPS-based optimum score estimator performed better than one based on maximum likelihood plug-in estimates for a meteorological problem. Optimising strictly proper scores forms a special case of
M
-estimation
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2011
)
as for example optimising the logarithmic score in one dimension corresponds to log-likelihood estimation. See
Gneiting and Raftery (2007)
and
(
Dawid et al., 2016
)
, and the references therein, for further details about this.

In this work, we focus on the estimation of random field models in spatial statistics through optimum score estimation. As far as we know, there has not been any systematic comparison between different scoring rules in this scenario, which is a main motivation for this work.
We propose a scoring rule inference approach based on maximising the average score of leave-one-out conditional predictions. Using this leave-one-out cross-validation (
\LOOS
) score for estimation thus optimises the predictive ability of the model instead of maximising the likelihood. We show that this approach has two main advantages. First, for spatial Markov models, the computational cost is in general much lower than a standard likelihood approach. Second, the inference method can be made robust against outliers by choosing a robust scoring rule. For this estimation approach, we compare different choices of scoring rules in terms of computation time, statistical efficiency and robustness.

The rest of the article is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces the details of the a leave-one-out cross-validation approach to model inference. Section
3
contains results from inference comparison using the different approaches on the spatial models in terms of time, accuracy, robustness and predictive quality. A case study on rainfall anomalies is carried out in Section
4
. Finally, discussion and conclusions are found in Section
5
. Details on the scoring rules and the models used for comparison are described in two appendices.

2
Scoring rule inference

A straightforward approach to scoring rule parameter estimation for multidimensional random variables is to use multidimensional scoring rules, such as the log-likelihood score or energy scores
Gneiting et al. (2008)
. However, in spatial statistics the aim often is spatial prediction. Different models (or prediction methods) are then compared by cross-validation: a set of values in a spatial data set are in turn removed from the data, the remaining data are used to find the predictive distribution for the left out values, and finally the value of the scoring rule used on this prediction is computed. The sum of these scores over all values in the data set are then used to compare different prediction methods.
In this section, we introduce and use leave-one-out score (LOOS) optimisation for parameter estimation. We give the general background of maximum score estimation, the formal definition of LOOS, examine the computational requirements for LOOS in different scenarios and compare these with full likelihood estimation, and discuss robustness of the LOOS estimators.

2.1
Background

Consider observations of a random variable
X\sim\mathbb{Q}
.
For a proper scoring rule
S
, the divergence
D
(or discrepancy function) associated with score
S
is

D(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q})-S(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}),

where
S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})
is the expected score of forecast
\mathbb{P}
, and
H(\mathbb{Q})=S(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q})
is the generalised entropy
associated with
S
. Now assume that
\mathbb{Q}
belongs to a family of parametric models
P_{\bm{\theta}}
with true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. Then under conditions discussed in
Dawid et al. (2016)
we have that

\bm{\theta}_{0}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}D(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})=\arg\max_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{Q}),

where the second equality is because the generalised entropy does not depend on
\bm{\theta}
.

For
m
independent observations
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{m}
of
X
, the average score of the observations is used for inference, and the estimator becomes

\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\arg\max_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},y_{k}).

(2)

According to
Dawid et al. (2016)
, again under suitable conditions, the estimation equation

\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\hskip 5.69054pt\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},y_{k})=0

used to solve Eq. (
2
) is unbiased.
Moreover, under suitable regularity conditions (for references see
Dawid et al. (2016)
), the estimator in Eq. (
2
) is consistent and asymptotically normal, with mean
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and variance
V
, where
V^{-1}
is the Godambe information matrix
(
Godambe, 1960
)
, i.e.

V=K^{-1}J(K^{-1})^{\top},

(3)

with

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},X)|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},X)^{\top}|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}]

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},X)|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right].

When
S
is the logarithmic score,
V^{-1}
is the Fisher information matrix.

2.2
Leave-one-out scoring rule parameter estimation for spatial statistics

As set out above, we propose using the leave-one-out score for parameter estimation. Suppose that we have a set of data
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
which is an observation of a multivariate random variable
\bm{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
with distribution
\mathbb{P}(\bm{\theta})
depending on a parameter vector
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},p\geq 1
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}
denote the conditional distribution of
X_{i}|\bm{X}_{-i}
, where
\bm{X}_{-i}
denotes all elements of
\bm{X}
except for
X_{i}
. For observations
\bm{y}
of
\bm{X}
, we condition each
\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}
on
\bm{X}_{-i}=\bm{y}_{-i}
and define the leave-one-out score as the average of the one-dimensional scores over these conditional distributions,

LOOS(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i}).

An estimate of
{\bm{\theta}}
then is obtained as the maximiser of this scoring rule,

\hat{\bm{\theta}}=\arg\max_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i}).

(4)

The composite
\LOOS
score is clearly a proper scoring rule as long as
S
is. This is thus a special case of the general procedure in Section
2.1
where the scoring rule is chosen as the composite
\LOOS
score. If
S
is chosen as the logarithmic score, the
\LOOS
score is nothing but the pseudo-likelihood of
Besag (1975)
, which also was pointed out by

Dawid et al. (2016)
Example 1 in the context of Markov models.

The LOOS estimator is thus easy to maximise numerically for any model where
S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}({\bm{\theta}}),\bm{X}_{i})
can be evaluated analytically. In the following subsections, we illustrate this for Gaussian random fields, and the analytic expressions for several common scoring rules for Gaussian distributions are given in Appendix
B
.

The efficiency of the estimator can be assessed through the Godambe information matrix in Eq. (
3
) with

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}({\bm{\theta}}),X_{i})|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}(\bm{\theta}),X_{j})^{\top}|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}]

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}(\bm{\theta}),X_{i})|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right].

Thus, the asymptotic efficiency clearly depends on the choice of scoring rule
S
, and in the following we will investigate this numerically in a few common scenarios.

2.3
LOOS estimation for Gaussian random fields

A standard scenario in spatial statistics is that the data are modelled as observations of a Gaussian random field
X
. Let
X_{i}=X(s_{i})
be an observation of the field at location
s_{i}
. Then
\bm{X}=(X_{1},...,X_{n})\sim\MVN(\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta}),\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta}))
be a multivariate normal distribution with mean
\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta})
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\theta})
determined by the mean and covariance functions of
X
. In this case,

X_{i}|\bm{X}_{-i}=\bm{x}_{-i}\sim N(\mu_{i}+\bm{\Sigma}_{i,-i}\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,-i}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{-i}-\bm{\mu}_{-i}),\bm{\Sigma}_{i,i}-\bm{\Sigma}_{i,-i}\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,-i}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,i}),

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,\cdot}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\cdot,-i}
denote all but the
i
-th row and column of
\bm{\Sigma}
, respectively, and
\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,i}
denotes the
i
-th column of
\bm{\Sigma}
where the
i
-th element has been removed.
The
\LOOS
expression can thus be calculated analytically for any scoring rule that has a closed-form expression for Gaussian distributions, see Appendix
B
.

Note that the computation of the conditional distribution requires the inverse of an
n-1\times n-1
matrix, which for a general covariance matrix,
\bm{\Sigma}
, requires
O(n^{3})
floating point operations. As this has to be done
n
times, it thus seems as if the total cost is
O(n^{4})
, which is significantly higher than the
O(n^{3})
cost of evaluating the likelihood. However, the cost can be reduced significantly by first computing
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
at
O(n^{3})
cost, and then obtaining all inverses
\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,-i},i=1,\ldots n
as rank-two updates of
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
. Specifically, let
\bm{e}_{i}
denote the column vector of length
n
with all zero elements except a
1
at the
i
-th entry, and let
\bm{f}_{i}
denote
i
-th column of
\bm{\Sigma}
except that the
i
-th element of this vector is set to zero. Defining the matrices

\bm{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{e}_{i}&\bm{f}_{i}\end{bmatrix}
and

\bm{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{f}_{i}&\bm{e}_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

we then have via the Woodbury matrix identity that

(\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{U}\bm{V})^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U}(I-\bm{V}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{U})^{-1}\bm{V}\bm{\Sigma}^{-1},

which only requires the inverse of a
2\times 2
matrix. Omitting the
i
-th row and
i
-th column from
(\bm{\Sigma}-\bm{U}\bm{V})^{-1}
yields
\bm{\Sigma}_{-i,-i}^{-1}
. Thus, the computational cost of LOOS estimation is of the same order as the cost of likelihood-based estimation in this case. However, as seen below, in important special cases LOOS is faster.

2.4
LOOS estimation for Gaussian Markov random fields

In spatial statistics, Gaussian Markov random fields (GMRFs) are often used to reduce computational costs. Examples include conditional autoregressive (CAR) models
(
Besag, 1975
)
for areal data and the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) approximations of Gaussian random fields with Matérn covariances
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
.

A GMRF is a multivariate normal random variable
\bm{X}=(X_{1},...,X_{n})\sim\MVN(\bm{\mu}(\bm{\theta}),Q(\bm{\theta})^{-1})
, where the sparsity pattern of the precision matrix
\bm{Q}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}
determines conditional independence properties of the field
(
Rue and Held, 2005
)
. Typically,
\bm{Q}
is a highly sparse matrix, and this sparsity can be used to reduce the computational costs for likelihood-based inference down to
O(n)
in one spatial dimension,
O(n^{3/2})
in two spatial dimensions, and
O(n^{2})
in three spatial dimensions
(
Rue and Held, 2005
)
.

The fact that the model is specified through the precision matrix also simplifies LOOS estimation. The reason is that the conditional distribution in terms of the precision matrix is

X_{i}|\bm{X}_{-i}=\bm{y}_{-i}\sim N(\mu_{i}-\frac{1}{Q_{ii}}\sum_{j\neq i}Q_{ij}(y_{j}-\mu_{j}),Q_{ii}^{-1}).

Thus, no matrix inversion is required to compute the conditional variance, and the conditional mean can be computed as

\mu_{i}-\frac{1}{Q_{ii}}\sum_{j\neq i}Q_{ij}(y_{j}-\mu_{j})=\frac{1}{Q_{ii}}(\bm{Q}\bm{\mu})_{i}-\frac{1}{Q_{ii}}(\bm{Q}\bm{y})_{i}+y_{i},

which means that the matrix vector product
\bm{Q}\bm{\mu}
only has to be computed once, and which has cost
O(n)
if
\bm{Q}
is sparse and only has a few non-zero elements per row.
Thus, in total the computational cost for LOOS estimation for GMRFs is
O(n)
in any spatial dimension, compared to the
O(n^{3/2})
cost for the likelihood-based inference in two dimensions and
O(n^{2})
in three dimensions.

2.5
LOOS estimation for latent GMRFs

A common scenario is that the data
\bm{y}
is modelled as an observation of a GMRF
{\bm{X}\sim N(\bm{\mu},\bm{Q}^{-1})}
observed under Gaussian measurement noise,
\bm{Y}|(\bm{X}=\bm{x})\sim N(\bm{A}\bm{x},\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}I)
,
where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a projection matrix linking the GMRF to the observations. It is well-known
(
Rue and Held, 2005
, see, e.g.)
that

\bm{X}|(\bm{Y}=\bm{y})\sim N(\bm{\mu}_{x|y},\bm{Q}_{x|y}^{-1})
,
where
\bm{Q}_{x|y}=\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{\varepsilon}\bm{A}+\bm{Q}
and
\bm{\mu}_{x|y}=\bm{Q}_{x|y}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{y}/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
.
To perform the LOOS estimation, we need to compute the distribution of
Y_{i}|(\bm{Y}_{-i}=\bm{y}_{-i})
, which is a Gaussian distribution with mean

\mu_{i|-i}=\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{i|-i}^{-1}\bm{A}_{-i,\cdot}^{\top}\bm{y}_{-i}/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}

and variance
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+q_{i}
, where
q_{i}
is the
i
-th diagonal element of

\widehat{\bm{Q}}_{i|-i}=\bm{A}_{-i,\cdot}^{\top}\bm{A}_{-i,\cdot}/\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+\bm{Q}

Computing one of these conditional distributions has the same cost as computing the log-likelihood of the model. However, using the same type of low-rank updates as in the standard Gaussian field case, the computational cost of the LOOS expression can be reduced to be of the same order as the cost of evaluating the likelihood.

2.6
Robustness

It is well-known that maximum likelihood estimation can be sensitive to outliers in the data. One popular measure of sensitivity is through the influence function (IF)
(
Hampel, 1974
)
, which measures the standardised effect of a small contamination in the data at a point
y
. Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbb{P})
denote the
\LOOS
estimator of Eq. (
4
) when the data comes from the model
\mathbb{P}
. The IF is then defined as

IF(\bm{y})=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon})-\hat{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})}{\varepsilon},

where
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}+\varepsilon\delta_{\bm{y}}
. A standard definition of robustness is that the IF should be bounded. According to
(
Dawid et al., 2016
, Eq. (27))
, the IF for
\LOOS
can be rewritten as

IF(\bm{y})=K^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}LOOS(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\bm{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}S(\mathbb{P}_{i|-i}(\bm{\theta}),y_{i}).

Thus, if the scoring rule
S
is bounded in
y
for all values of
\bm{\theta}
, then the
\LOOS
estimator is robust. This boundedness of the scoring rule is in fact the definition of robustness of scoring rules used in
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
.
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
further introduced the notion of model sensitivity of a scoring rule as follows: If for a given
\mathbb{P}
there exists a number
\alpha_{\mathbb{P}}
such that
|S(\mathbb{P},y)|\asymp|y|^{\alpha_{\mathbb{P}}}
, then
S
is said to have model sensitivity
\alpha_{\mathbb{P}}
for
\mathbb{P}
. They further introduced the sensitivity index
\alpha=\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\alpha_{\mathbb{P}}
and noted that a scoring rule is robust if
\alpha=0
.

The model sensitivity of a scoring rule is useful as this provides a means to compare the sensitivity of different non-robust scoring rules, where a higher
\alpha_{\mathbb{P}}
indicates a more sensitive scoring rule. Examples of the sensitivity index for Gaussian models for different scoring rules are shown in Table
1
. The sensitivity indices of all scoring rules except for the root score
\sroot
are derived in
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
. In the table,
\rCRPS
refers to the robust CRPS, obtained by taking
g(x,y)=|x-y|1\{|x-y|<c\}
for some
c>0
and
h(x)=-\frac{1}{2}x

(
Bolin and Wallin, 2023
)
. This scoring rule is interesting from a robustness point of view as it has sensitivity index 0, and thus results in a bounded IF for the LOOS estimator.

As discussed in the introduction, the root score,
\sroot
, scoring rule is obtained by choosing
h(x)=-\sqrt{x}
and
g(x,y)=|x-y|
in Eq. (
1
). This results in the score

\sroot(\mathbb{P},y)=-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[|X-y|]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}}[|X-Y|]}},

which can also be formulated in terms of CRPS as

\sroot(\mathbb{P},y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{\CRPS(\mathbb{P},y)}{\sqrt{|\CRPS(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P})|}}-\sqrt{4|\CRPS(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P})|}\right)

since
\CRPS(\mathbb{P},y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}\mathbb{P}}[|X-X^{\prime}|]-\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[|X-y|]
. The root score is interesting since it has the same sensitivity index as the SCRPS and CRPS and it has scale invariance properties which are in between those of
\CRPS
and the
\SCRPS
, see Appendix
A
for details and for the explanation of the scale dependence on
\sigma
in Table
1
.

The values in the table suggest that only the rCRPS is robust, and that the CRPS is less sensitive to outliers than the log-score, as also noted by
Gneiting and Raftery (2007)
, but that neither the CRPS or the log-score are robust. Further, neither the SCRPS or the root score are robust, and will therefore not result in a bounded IF for the
\LOOS
estimator.

Simulation studies

In this section, we investigate the properties of
\LOOS
estimation through simulation in a few different scenarios. First, we compare the
\LOOS
estimator with maximum likelihood estimation using the different scoring rules shown in Table
1
on a spatial GMRF model. Next, we consider a latent GMRF model, and a latent non-stationary GMRF model. Finally, we investigate the effects of the different estimation methods on predictive performance.

3.1
Analysis of a GMRF model

The most common covariance model for Gaussian processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in spatial statistics is the Matérn covariance

C(h)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)}(\kappa h)^{\nu}K_{\nu}(\kappa h),

where
\nu>0
is a smoothness parameter,
\kappa>0
represents the correlation range, and
\sigma^{2}
is the variance of
\bm{X}
. A Gaussian process
\bm{X}
with this covariance function can be represented as the solution to the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE)

(\kappa^{2}-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}(\tau\bm{X})=\mathcal{W},

(5)

where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator,
\Gamma
is the gamma function,
K_{\nu}
is the Bessel function of the second kind,
\tau^{2}=\Gamma(\nu)/(\sigma^{2}\kappa^{2\nu}(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(\nu+d/2))
,
\alpha=\nu+d/2
, and
\mathcal{W}
is a Gaussian white noise
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
.

We fix
\alpha=2
and use the SPDE approach of
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
to obtain a GMRF approximation of this Gaussian process. A basis of piecewise linear basis functions
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is constructed based on a triangulation of the region of interest (Figure
1
), where
\varphi_{i}
is constructed to be 1 at the
i
-th node in the triangulation and zero at all other nodes. The mesh was obtained using the
inla.mesh.2d
function in R-INLA
(
Rue et al., 2009
)
and has
n=435
nodes.
The Gaussian process is approximated as
x(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\varphi_{i}(s)
, where the vector of weights
\bm{X}
is a GMRF,
\bm{X}\sim N(0,\bm{Q}^{-1})
, with precision matrix

\bm{Q}=\tau^{2}(\kappa^{2}\bm{C}+\bm{G})\bm{C}^{-1}(\kappa^{2}\bm{C}+\bm{G}).

(6)

Here
\bm{G}
and
\bm{C}
are two sparse matrices that only depend on the mesh, and not on the parameters of the model
(
Lindgren et al., 2011
)
.
The data for this model is
y_{i}=x_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
and the parameters to be estimated are
\bm{\theta}=(\tau,\kappa)
.

We simulate
10
independent samples of the field, each with
435
observations using the parameters
\bm{\theta}=(0.16,1.75)
, corresponding to a field with variance one and practical correlation range (
\sqrt{8}/\kappa
) approximately equal to 1.6. These parameters will be used in the following sections as well. For the rCRPS, we set
c=2
.
Based on the data, the parameters are estimated using maximum likelihood and the
\LOOS
estimator based on the log-score, CRPS, SCRPS, rCRPS and the
\sroot
scoring rule. The optimisation was done using
optim
in R
(
R Core Team, 2021
)
, and was repeated 300 times, each time with a new simulated data set. To test the sensitivity to outliers in the GMRF model, outliers were introduced by choosing an outlier’s location
i
at random from
1,..,m
and then replacing
y_{i}
by
|y_{i}|+5
. This is done independently for
k
of the 10 samples generated of the field, for
k\in\{0,5,10\}
and for each of the 300 independent repetitions.

5
Conclusion

In this article we have proposed a leave-one-out cross-validation score,
\LOOS
as loss function for model inference on random field models in spatial statistics. The approach was studied for different choices of scoring rules in terms of statistical efficiency, time complexity, robustness and predictive performance. The scores used for the
\LOOS
comparison were logarithmic score, CRPS, SCRPS and a new kernel score,
\sroot
, based on the generalised kernel score with properties between the scale invariant SCRPS and the non-scale invariant CRPS.

The
\LOOS
estimators are asymptotically unbiased, as mentioned in
Dawid et al. (2016)
. Without outliers in the data, their variance is slightly larger than for the log-likelihood estimator.
When the data contains outliers, the
\LOOS
estimators with generalised kernel scores are more robust against outliers than the log-likelihood and LOOS estimation based on the log-score. Among the generalised kernel scores tested, the rCRPS gave the most robust estimates whereas the scale invariant SCRPS gave the lowest variance. The new
\sroot
score provides a good balance between robustness and low asymptotic variance.

We showed how to compute the LOOS estimator efficiently for GMRFs. The main computational advantage of LOOS for such models is that it does not require the computation of the determinant of the precision matrix, which is the main computational cost in likelihood estimation. For latent GMRF models and for standard covariance-based Gaussian random fields, the computation time of the LOOS estimator is similar to that of maximum likelihood.

The LOOS estimator can be computed without Monte Carlo approximation for each model where the score of conditional distributions is available in closed form. One interesting class of models to test LOOS estimation on in future work are the Type G non-Gaussian random fields discussed in
Bolin and Wallin (2020)
and
Cabral et al. (2023)
,
Cabral et al. (2024)
. These often has Markov properties, which means that the LOOS estimator can be computed with low computational cost. LOOS estimation is particularly interesting for such non-Gaussian models as likelihood-based estimation of flexible non-Gaussian models can be sensitive to outliers.

Another interesting topic to investigate is to modify the score by using different types of cross-validation. One option, for example, would be to select a random subset of locations to predict in each iteration of the optimisation. That could greatly reduce the computational cost of inference for models without Markov properties.

Appendix A
Local scale invariance of
\sroot

The concept of local scale invariance of proper score was defined by
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
as a means of describing scores that are not affected by the magnitude of predictive uncertainty. If
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}
is a location-scale family with parameters
\bm{\theta}=(\mu,\sigma)
, such as a univariate Gaussian distribution, and one considers a small perturbation
d\bm{\theta}
of the parameters, then locally one can write

S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})-S(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}+d\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})=d\bm{\theta}^{\top}s(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})d\bm{\theta}

since
S
is proper.
The function
s(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})
is the scale function of
S
on
\mathbb{P}
. If the scale function exists and can be written as

s(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}s(\mathbb{P}_{0,1})

then
S
is said to be locally scale invariant. The important thing here is the scaling
1/\sigma^{2}
with the scale, which is what makes the magnitude of the score invariant to the magnitude of the prediction uncertainty. The dependence on
\sigma
for the scale function of different scoring rules is shown in Table
1
, and there one can see that the log-score and the SCRPS are scale invariant.
For further details, see
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
.

We now derive the scale function of
\sroot
, which has not been investigated before. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
be a prediction with parameters
\theta
. For shorter notation, we define
G_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}}=E_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}}[|X-Y|]
and
G_{\mathbb{P}}=G_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}}
. In addition, we have the following notation for their derivatives:
\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}Q}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{Q}}
,
\mathrm{E}_{Q\dot{P}}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}G_{\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}}
, and
\mathrm{E}_{\ddot{P}Q}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}^{2}G_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{Q}}
. The
\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}\dot{P}}
denotes the gradient applied to both arguments of
G_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}}
. Consider the score derivatives wrt
\theta
, i.e.
\nabla_{\theta}\sroot(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})
and
\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\sroot(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})|_{\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{P}}
. Firstly,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\sroot(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})=\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}Q}}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}-\frac{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q}}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\sroot(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})=\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\ddot{P}Q}}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}Q}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}Q}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}-\frac{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q}}(\mathrm{E}_{\ddot{P}P}+\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}\dot{P}})}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}+\frac{3G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q}}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{5/2}}.

Inserting
\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
gives

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\sroot(\mathbb{P}_{\theta},\mathbb{Q})|_{\mathbb{Q}=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\ddot{P}P}}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\ddot{P}P}+\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}\dot{P}}}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}+\frac{3\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}^{3/2}}-\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}\dot{P}}}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}P}^{\top}}{G_{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}}}-\mathrm{E}_{\dot{P}\dot{P}}\right).

This gives scale function

s(\mathbb{Q}_{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{\sigma}}\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}}[H_{\mathbb{Q}}(X,Y)]

where
H_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a
2\times 2
positive semi-definite matrix independent of
\theta
. The scoring rule
\sroot
is therefore not locally scale invariant as the logarithmic score and the SCRPS, but it is less dependent on scale than the CRPS and the Hyvärinen score. According to
Bolin and Wallin (2023)
, the scoring rule is then also homogeneous of order
1/2
, which should not be confused with homogeneity in the density.

Appendix B
Score equations for the Normal distribution

For
\mathbb{P}=N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, we have

\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[|X-y|]=2\sigma\phi\left(\frac{\mu-y}{\sigma}\right)+(\mu-y)\left(2\Phi\left(\frac{\mu-y}{\sigma}\right)-1\right)

and

\mathrm{E}_{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}}[|X-X^{\prime}|]=2\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}
