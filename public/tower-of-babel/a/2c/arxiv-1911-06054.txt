On finite CG-groups
Sekhar Jyoti Baishya

Abstract
. A finite group G G is a CG-group if ∣ Cent ⁡ ( G ) ∣ = ∣ G ′ ∣ + 2 \mid\Cent(G)\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2 , where G ′ G^{\prime} is the commutator subgroup and Cent ⁡ ( G ) \Cent(G) is the set of distinct element centralizers of G G . In this paper we give some results on CG-groups. We also give a negative answer to [ 29 , Conjecture 2.3] given by K. Khoramshahi and M. Zarrin.

Introduction

Given a group
G
, let
\Cent(G)

G

\Cent(G)=\{C(x)\mid x\in G\}
, where
C(x)
is the centralizer of the element
x
in
G
. The study of finite groups in terms of
|\Cent(G)|
, becomes an interesting research topic in last few years. Starting with Belcastro and Sherman
[
17
]
in 1994 many authors have been studied and characterised finite groups
G
in terms of
\mid\Cent(G)\mid
. More information on this and related concepts may be found in
[
1
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

While studying the number of distinct element centralizers of a finite group
G
, we observe that there are lots of examples of finite groups (including family of finite groups) for which
|\Cent(G)|=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
, where
G^{\prime}
is the commutator subgroup of
G
(the smallest example of such group being
S_{3}
). We have also observed that such groups have many interesting properties (for example, in most of the cases
G^{\prime}
is abelian, proper element centralizers are abelian etc). This motivates us to introduce the notion of CG-group with a hope that this might give some new solvability criterian for a finite group. We call a finite group
G
to be a CG-group if
\mid\Cent(G)\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
. In this paper, we give some examples of CG-groups and some necessary and sufficient conditions for a finite group to be a CG-group. Apart from these, we also give a negative answer to the conjecture
[
29
, Conjecture 2.3]
given by K. Khoramshahi and M. Zarrin.

In this paper, all groups are finite (however Lemma
3.1
and Remark
3.2
holds for any group) and all notations are usual. For example
G^{\prime}
,
Z(G)
denotes the commutator subgroup and the center of a group
G
respectively,
C_{n}
denotes the cyclic group of order
n
,
D_{2n}
denotes the dihedral group of order
2n
,
A_{n}
denotes the alternating group of degree
n
,
S_{n}
denotes the symmetric group of degree
n
and
C_{n}{\rtimes}_{\theta}C_{p}
denotes semidirect product of
C_{n}
and
C_{p}
, where
\theta:C_{p}\longrightarrow\Aut(C_{n})
is a homomorphism.

2.
Some Examples and basic results

We begin with some examples and counter examples of finite CG-groups. As we have already mentioned, the smallest CG-group is
S_{3}
(in fact in view of
[
13
, Corollary 2.5]
, any group with central quotient of order
pq
is a CG-group, where
p,q
are primes, not necessarily distinct). The next higher order CG-groups are
D_{8}
and
Q_{8}
. We will see that any non-abelian group order
pqr
or
p^{4}
, where
p,q,r
are primes not necessarily distinct is a CG-group.
On the other hand, in view of
[
17
, Theorem 7]
, no finite perfect group is a CG-group (recall that a group
G
is said to be perfect if
G^{\prime}=G
). Consequently, no finite simple, semisimple group (a group is said to be semisimple if it is a direct product of non-abelian simple groups) is a CG-group and
A_{4}
is the only CG-group among the alternating groups
A_{n},n\geq 3
. We now give some more examples of finite CG-groups:

Proposition 2.1
.

The generalized dihedral group
D(m,n)=\langle a,b\mid a^{m}=b^{n}=1,bab^{-1}=a^{-1}\rangle
;
m\geq 3,n\geq 2
and
n
even is a CG-group.

Proof.

By
[
4
, Fact 2]
, we have
Z(D(m,n))=\langle a^{\frac{m}{2}},b^{2}\rangle
or
\langle b^{2}\rangle
according as
m
is even or odd. Therefore
\langle\langle a\rangle,Z(D(m,n))\rangle
is an abelian normal subgroup of
D(m,n)
of prime index. Now, the result follows using
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
.
∎

As an immediate corollary we have the following result:

Corollary 2.2
.

The dihedral group
D_{2n},n\geq 3
is a CG-group.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\frac{G}{Z(G)}\cong C_{n}{\rtimes}_{\theta}C_{p}
be non-abelian,
n
be an integer,
p
be a prime and
\theta:C_{p}\longrightarrow\Aut(C_{n})
is a non-trivial homomorphism. Then
G
is a CG-group.

Proof.

See
[
13
, Proposition 2.9]
.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

The generalized quaternion group
Q_{4m}=\langle a,b\mid a^{2m}=1,b^{2}=a^{m},bab^{-1}=a^{-1}\rangle,m\geq 2
is a CG-group.

Proof.

It is well known that
\frac{Q_{4m}}{Z(Q_{4m})}\cong D_{2m}
, for any
m\geq 2
. Therefore by Proposition
2.3
,
G
is a CG-group.
∎

Let
I(G)
be the set of all solutions of the equation

in
G
. Define
\alpha(G)=\frac{\mid I(G)\mid}{\mid G\mid}
. Then we have the following result:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group of order

, where

is any integer and

is an odd integer. If
\alpha(G)>\frac{1}{2}
, then

is a CG-group.

Proof.

See
[
13
, Corollary 2.6]
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

The semidihedral or quasidihedral group
SD_{2^{n}}=\langle x,y\mid x^{2^{n-1}}=y^{2}=1,yxy^{-1}=x^{2^{n-2}-1}\rangle
is a CG-group for any

.

Proof.

It follows from
[

, noting that
SD_{2^{n}},n\geq 3
has an abelian normal subgroup of prime index.
∎

Proposition 2.7
.

The modular

-group
Mod_{n}(p)=\langle x,y\mid x^{p^{n-1}}=y^{p}=1,yxy^{-1}=x^{1+p^{n-2}}\rangle

Proof.

It follows from
[

, noting that
Mod_{n}(p),n\geq 3

∎

Proposition 2.8
.

The group
U_{6n}=\langle x,y\mid x^{2n}=y^{3}=1,x^{-1}yx=y^{-1}\rangle

Proof.

It follows from Proposition

∎

The linear groups plays a significant role in the theory of finite groups. We now study the CG-groups among the linear groups of degree two. In the following results, the groups
GL(2,q),SL(2,q),PGL(2,q)
and
PSL(2,q)
denote the general linear, special linear, projective general linear and projective special linear groups respectively of degree
2
over the field of size
q
, where
q
is a prime power.

Proposition 2.9
.

The group
G=GL(2,q)
is a CG-group if and only if
q=2
.

Proof.

If
q=2
, then
G=S_{3}
is a CG-group. Next, suppose
q>2
. In view of
[
2
, Proposition 3.26]
), the proper centralizers of
G
are precisely the members of the family
\{xDx^{-1},xIx^{-1},xPZ(G)x^{-1}\mid x\in G\}
, where

(a)

D
is the subgroup of all diagonal matrices in
G
, and the number of conjugates of
D
in
G
is
\frac{q(q+1)}{2}
,

(b)

I
is a cyclic subgroup of
G
, and the number of conjugates of
I
in
G
is
\frac{q(q-1)}{2}
,

(c)

P
is the Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
consisting of all upper triangular matrices with
1
in the diagonal, and the number of conjugates of
PZ(G)
in
G
is
q+1
.

Therefore
\mid Cent(G)\mid=\frac{q(q+1)}{2}+\frac{q(q-1)}{2}+(q+1)+1=q^{2}+q+2
. Also by
[
16
, Theorem 3.1.19]
, for
q>2
, we have
G^{\prime}=SL(2,q)
having order
q(q-1)(q+1)
. In the present scenario, one can easily verify that
G
is not a CG-group.
∎

Proposition 2.10
.

The group
G=SL(2,q)
is a CG-group if and only if
q=2
or
3
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
SL(2,2)
and
SL(2,3)
are CG-groups. On the otherhand, if
q>3
, then by
[
32
]
, we have
G=G^{\prime}
and consequently using
[
17
, Theorem 7]
,
G
is not a CG-group.
∎

We now compute the number of distinct element centralizers of

.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
G=PGL(2,q)
. Then

\mid\Cent(G)\mid=\begin{cases}2q^{2}+q+2&\;\text{if \, $q$ \, is odd, $q>3$}\\
q^{2}+q+2&\;\text{if \, $q$ \, is even, $q>4$}\\
5&\;\text{if \, $q=2$}\\
14&\;\text{if \, $q=3$}\\
22&\;\text{if \, $q=4$}\par\end{cases}

Proof.

If
q
is even, then
PGL(2,q)=PSL(2,q)
(see
[
35
]
). Now, the result follows from
[
39
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Now, suppose
q
is odd. We have
G=PGL(2,3)=S_{4}
and
\mid\Cent(G)\mid=14
. Next, suppose
q\geq 5
. In view of
[
24
, Page 5]
, it follows that the centralizer of any involution (element of order

) in

is either

or

. Hence by
[

, Proposition 2.4]
,
\mid\Cent(PGL(2,5))\mid=57
, noting that the size of element centralizers in

are
4,5,6,8
and

. On the other hand, if

, then using
[

, Table 4]
, it follows that the size of the proper element centralizers of

are
q,q-1,q+1,2(q-1)
and
2(q+1)
. Again, let

and

be any two distinct involutions in

. Since

, therefore

noting that

, which is a contradiction. Therefore

. In the present scenario, in view of
[

,

\mid Cent(G)\mid=(q+1)+q(q+1)+q(q-1)+1=2q^{2}+q+2
.

We conclude the section with the following lemmas:

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
. Then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.

Proof.

The proof follows using
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
, noting that if
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
, then
\mid(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid
.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that all sylow subgroups are abelian. Then

is a CG-group if and only if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.

Proof.

In the present scenario, we have
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
(see
[

, p. 118]

3.
The main results

In this section, we prove the main results of the paper. However, we begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be any group. If
\mid\frac{G}{Z(G)}\mid=pqr
where
p,q,r
are primes (not necessarily distinct), then
C(x)
is abelian for any
x\in G\setminus Z(G)
.

Proof.

Let
x\in G\setminus Z(G)
. If
\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}
is cyclic, then
C(x)
is abelian. Now, suppose
\mid\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}\mid=pq
. Then
o(xZ(G))=p,q
or
pq
. If
o(xZ(G))=pq
, then
C(x)
is abelian. Next suppose
o(xZ(G))\neq pq
. Then there exists some
y\in C(x)
such that

\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}=\langle xZ(G),yZ(G)\rangle
. Consequently,
C(x)=\langle x,y,Z(G)\rangle
and hence
C(x)
is abelian. If
\mid\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}\mid=pr
or
qr
, then using similar arguments we can show that
C(x)
is abelian.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Recall that a group
G
is said to be a CA-group if
C(x)
is abelian for any
x\in G\setminus Z(G)
. It is easy to see that for such groups
C(x)\cap C(y)=Z(G)
for any two distinct proper centralizers
C(x)
and
C(y)
. Also we have seen that if
\mid\frac{G}{Z(G)}\mid=pqr
where
p,q,r
are primes (not necessarily distinct), then
G
is a CA-group.

The authors in
[
29
, Proposition 3.6]
showed that if
G
is a finite group such that
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is isomorphic to a simple group, then then
G
and
G^{\prime}
are isoclinic groups.
In the following we generalize this result as follows:

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is perfect. Then
G
and
G^{\prime}
are isoclinic groups.

Proof.

Suppose
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is perfect. Then
\frac{G}{Z(G)}=(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}=\frac{G^{\prime}Z(G)}{Z(G)}
and consequently,
G^{\prime}Z(G)=G
. Therefore in view of
[
31
, Lemma 2.7]
,
G
is isoclinic to
G^{\prime}
.
∎

As an immediate consequence we have the following necessary condition for a finite CG-group.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is perfect. Then
G
is not a CG-group.

Proof.

Suppose
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is perfect. Then by Proposition
3.3
,
G
is isoclinic to
G^{\prime}
and consequently, using
[
42
, Lemma 2.3]
, we have
\mid\Cent(G)\mid=\mid\Cent(G^{\prime})\mid
. Now, if
G
is a CG-group, then
\mid\Cent(G)\mid=\mid\Cent(G^{\prime})\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
, which is impossible by
[
17
, Theorem 7]
.
∎

The following result follows using technique similar to
[
9
, Lemma 2.7]
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
p
be the smallest prime divisor of the order of a group
G
and
\mid G^{\prime}\mid=p
. Then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}\cong C_{p}\times C_{p}
.

As an application to this we have the following result. Given a group
G
,
\omega(G)
denotes the size of a maximal set of pairwise non-commuting elements of
G
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
G
be a finite non-abelian metacyclic
p
-group, where
p>2
is a prime. Then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}\cong C_{p}\times C_{p}
.

Proof.

In view of
[
23
]
, we have
\omega(G)=\frac{\mid G^{\prime}\mid}{p}(1+p)
. Now, suppose
G
is a CG-group. Then
\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2\geq\omega(G)+1=\frac{\mid G^{\prime}\mid}{p}(1+p)+1
. Consequently, we have
\mid G^{\prime}\mid=p
. Now, the result follows using Proposition
3.5
.
∎

The following proposition gives a sufficient condition for a finite group to be a CG-group.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
G
be a finite non-abelian group with an abelian normal subgroup of prime index. Then

(a)

G
is a CG-group.

(b)

If
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is abelian, then
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is elementary abelian.

(c)

If
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is non-abelian, then
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
a CG-group. In particular, if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is of order
p^{r}
for some prime
p
, then
\mid Cent(G)\mid=p^{r-1}+2
.

.

Proof.

a) See
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
. It may be mentioned here that
[
22
, Theorem 3.4]
) is a particular case of this result, where the author obtained the result for
p
-groups (
p
a prime )only, noting that
\mid Cent(G)\mid=\omega(G)+1
if and only if
G
is a CA-group (
[
1
, Lemma 2.6]
).

b) By
[
20
, Theorem A]
,
G
is a CA-group and consequently,
\{\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}/x\in G\setminus Z(G)\}
is a partition of
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
(see
[
1
, Remark 2.1]
). Therefore if
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is abelian, by
[
43
, p. 571]
, we have
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is elementary abelian.

c) Suppose
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is non-abelian. Let
N
be an abelian normal subgroup of
G
of prime index. Then
N=C(x)
for some
x\in G\setminus Z(G)
. In the present scenerio, we have
\frac{C(x)}{Z(G)}=C(xZ(G))
, and consequently,
C(xZ(G))
is an abelian normal subgroup of
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
of prime index. Therefore by
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
,
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.

Again, suppose
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is non-abelian of order
p^{r}
for some prime
p
. By
[
20
, Theorem A]
,
G
is a CA-group. Therefore
C(x)\cap C(y)=Z(G)
for any
x,y\in G\setminus Z(G),xy\neq yx
. Now,
N=C(x)
is a centralizer of
G
of index
p
. Clearly
C(x)
will contain exactly
p^{r-1}
distinct right cosets of
Z(G)
. Therefore the number of right cosets of
Z(G)
(other than
Z(G)
) left for the remaining proper centralizers is
p^{r}-p^{r-1}=p^{r-1}(p-1)
. In the present scenario, one can verify that any proper centralizer other than
C(x)
will contain exactly
p
distinct right cosets of
Z(G)
. Hence
\mid Cent(G)\mid=p^{r-1}+2
.
∎

As an application of the above proposition, we have the following result for minimal non-abelian group. Recall that a minimal non-abelian group is a non-abelian group all of whose proper subgroups are abelian. By
[
26
, Aufgaben III. 5.14]
, we have if
G
is a finite minimal non-abelian group, then
G
can have at the most two distinct prime divisors and if
G
is not a prime power group, then
G=PQ
, where
P
is a cyclic
p
-Sylow subgroup of
G
and
Q
is the elementary abelian minimal normal
q
-Sylow subgroup of
G
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a finite minimal non-abelian group. Then

(a)

G
is a CG-group. In particular, (see
[
8
, Proposition 2.2]
) if
G
is a
p
-group (
p
a prime), then we have
\mid Cent(G)\mid=p+2
. Otherwise,
G
is a primitive
\mid Q\mid+2
centralizer group, where
Q
is the normal Sylow subgroup of
G
.

(b)

If
G
is a
p
-group (
p
a prime), then
\frac{G}{Z(G)}\cong C_{p}\times C_{p}
. Otherwise,
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.

.

Proof.

a) Let
G
be a finite minimal non-abelian group. If
G
is a
p
-group, for some prime
p
, then
G
has an abelian normal subgroup of prime index and hence by Proposition
3.7
,
G
is a CG-group.

Next, suppose
G
is not a
p
-group. Let
P
be a cyclic Sylow subgroup and
Q
be the elementary abelian Sylow subgroup of
G
respectively. Then
P
has a normal subgroup
H
of prime index and consequently,
QH
is an abelian normal subgroup of
G
of prime index. Therefore by Proposition
3.7
,
G
is a CG-group.

Now, if
G
is a
p
-group, then
\mid Cent(G)\mid=p+2
, noting that we have
\mid G^{\prime}\mid=p
by
[
19
, Lemma 2.5]
. On the otherhand, in view of
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have
G
is a primitive
\mid Q\mid+2
centralizer group, where
Q
is the normal Sylow subgroup of
G
, niting that in the present scenario, we have
G^{\prime}=Q
and
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
.

b) If
G
is a
p
-group, then the result follows from Peoposition
3.5
, noting that we have
\mid G^{\prime}\mid=p
by
[
19
, Lemma 2.5]
. On the other hand, if
G
is not a prime power group, then
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is not a prime power group, noting that in the present scenario, we have
Z(G)\subsetneq P
, where
P
is a cyclic Sylow subgroup of
G
. Now, the result follows from Proposition
3.7
.
∎

Recall that a Frobenius group
G

is Frobenius. In this connection we have the following result:

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
G
be a Frobenius group with kernel
K
and complement
H
.

(a)

If
G
is minimal Frobenius, then
G
is a CG-group,

(b)

If
K
is cyclic, then
G
is a CG-group,

(c)

If
H
is abelian, then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
G^{\prime}
is abelian.

.

Proof.

a) If
G
is a minimal Frobenius group, then by
[
33
, Theorem 3.2.14]
,
K
is elementary abelian and
H
has prime order. Again, by
[

Remark 3.10
.

If

is a finite solvable group in which centralizers of non-identity elements are abelian, then

is a Frobenius group with abelian kernel and cyclic complement (see
[
38
, Proposition 3.1.1, Proposition 1.2.4]
). In this connection we have the following result:

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
C(x)
is abelian for every non-identity element

. Then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
G
is a Frobenius group with abelian kernel and cyclic complement.

Proof.

Suppose
G
is a CG-group. If
G
is non-solvable, then by
[
2
, Lemma 3.9]
,
G
is simple and consequently using
[
17
, Theorem 7]
,
G
is not a CG-group. Therefore
G
is solvable. Now, the result follows from Remars
3.10
.

Conversely, suppose
G
is a Frobenius group with abelian kernel
K
and cyclic complement. By
[
25
, Theorem 2.2]
, we have
G^{\prime}=K
. Therefore from the definition of Frobenius group,
G
is a CG-group.
∎

As an immediate consequence we have the following result:

Proposition 3.12
.

If

,

.

If

,

Proof.

It is well known that if

We need the following result to prove our next proposition:

Proposition 3.14
.

(Lemma 12.12

)

We now give another sufficient condition for a finite group to be a CG-group.

Proposition 3.15

Let

Using the third isomorphic theorem, we get

. Consequently, we have

\frac{G}{K}

3.14
,
\mid K\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid\mid K\cap Z(G)\mid
which forces
\mid\frac{K}{Z(G)}\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid
. Therefore by
[

, Proposition 3.1]
,

is a CG-group.
∎

Proposition 3.23
.

For finite groups with central quotient of order

Proposition 3.25
.

Let

\mid\frac{G}{Z(G)}\mid=p^{3}

is a CG-group if and only if

has an abelian normal subgroup of prime index.

Proof.

Let

be a CG-group. In view of Remark

for any

\mid C(x)\mid=\frac{\mid G\mid}{p^{2}}

It may be mentioned here that all the examples of CG-groups given in the earlier section are CA-groups. However, there exists CG-groups which are not CA-groups. For example, it can be verify that
S_{4}
is a CG-group but not a CA-group. In this connection, we give the following result on CG-groups whose central quotient is
S_{4}
.

Proposition 3.27
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\frac{G}{Z(G)}\cong S_{4}
. Then
G
is a CG-group if and only if
G^{\prime}\cong A_{4}
.

Proof.

If
G^{\prime}\cong A_{4}
, then we have
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
noting that
\mid(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}\mid=\mid\frac{G^{\prime}}{G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)}\mid=\mid A_{4}\mid
. Therefore by
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
,
\mid Cent(G)\mid=\mid Cent(\frac{G}{Z(G)})\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
.

Conversely, suppose
\mid Cent(G)\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
. In view of
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have
\mid Cent(G)\mid=\mid Cent(\frac{G}{Z(G)})\mid
and consequently,
\mid G^{\prime}\mid=\mid(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}\mid=\mid\frac{G^{\prime}}{G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)}\mid
, forcing
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
. Therefore we have
A_{4}\cong(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}=G^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 3.28
.

Recall that a minimal non-nilpotent group is a non-nilpotent group all of whose proper subgroups are nilpotent. If
G
is a finite minimal non-nilpotent group then
G
is a solvable group of order
p^{m}q^{n}
(
p,q
are distinct primes) with a unique Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
and a cyclic Sylow
q
-subgroup
Q
. Moreover, we have
G^{\prime}=P
(see
[
37
]
).

Proposition 3.29
.

Let
G
be a finite minimal non-nilpotent group.

(a)

If
G^{\prime}
is abelian, then
G
is a CG-group.

(b)

\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.

.

Proof.

a) By Remark
3.28
, we have
G^{\prime}=P
. Again, by
[
7
, Lemma 2.3]
, we have
\frac{G}{Z(G)}=\frac{PZ(G)}{Z(G)}\rtimes\frac{QZ(G)}{Z(G)}
is a Frobenius group with cyclic complement. Now, if
G^{\prime}
is abelian, then
PZ(G)
will be abelian and hence
G
is a CG-group by Proposition
3.15
.

b) Let
G
be a minimal non-nilpotent group. By
[
7
, Lemma 2.3]
,
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a Frobenius group with elementary abelian kernel
\frac{K}{Z(G)}
and complement of prime order. Again, by
[
25
, Theorem 2.2]
,
(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}=\frac{K}{Z(G)}
. Therefore from the definition of Frobenius group,
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is a CG-group.
∎

Proposition 3.30
.

Let
G
be a finite group such that
\frac{G}{Z(G)}
is minimal non-nilpotent. If
G^{\prime}
is abelian, then
G
is a CG-group.

Proof.

In view of Remark
3.28
, we have
\frac{G}{Z(G)}=\frac{G^{\prime}Z(G)}{Z(G)}\rtimes\frac{Q}{Z(G)}
, where

is a subgroup of

such that
\frac{Q}{Z(G)}
is cyclic. Now, if
G^{\prime}
is abelian, then

will be abelian and consequently, by third isomprphic theorem, we have
\frac{G}{G^{\prime}Z(G)}\cong\frac{Q}{Z(G)}
. Therefore in view of Proposition
3.14
, we have
\mid G^{\prime}Z(G)\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid\mid G^{\prime}Z(G)\cap Z(G)\mid=\mid G^{\prime}\mid\mid Z(G)\mid
and hence
\mid G^{\prime}\cap Z(G)\mid=1
. Therefore using
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
and Proposition
3.29
, we have

\mid Cent(G)\mid=\mid Cent(\frac{G}{Z(G)})\mid=\mid(\frac{G}{Z(G)})^{\prime}\mid+2=\mid G^{\prime}\mid+2
.
∎

.
