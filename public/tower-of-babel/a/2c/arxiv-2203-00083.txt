Sampling-Based Winner Prediction in District-Based Elections
Palash Dey, Debajyoti Kar, Swagato Sanyal

Abstract
In a district-based election, we apply a voting rule r r to decide the winners in each district, and a candidate who wins in a maximum number of districts is the winner of the election. We present efficient sampling-based algorithms to predict the winner of such district-based election systems in this paper. When r r is plurality and the margin of victory is known to be at least ε \varepsilon fraction of the total population, we present an algorithm to predict the winner. The sample complexity of our algorithm is 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ε 4 ​ log ⁡ 1 ε ​ log ​ 1 δ ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) . We complement this result by proving that any algorithm, from a natural class of algorithms, for predicting the winner in a district-based election when r r is plurality, must sample at least Ω ⁡ ( 1 ε 4 ​ log ⁡ 1 δ ) \Omega\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) votes. We then extend this result to any voting rule r r . Loosely speaking, we show that we can predict the winner of a district-based election with an extra overhead of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ε 2 ​ log ⁡ 1 δ ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) over the sample complexity of predicting the single-district winner under r r . We further extend our algorithm for the case when the margin of victory is unknown, but we have only two candidates. We then consider the median voting rule when the set of preferences in each district is single-peaked. We show that the winner of a district-based election can be predicted with 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ε 4 ​ log ⁡ 1 ε ​ log ​ 1 δ ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) samples even when the harmonious order in different districts can be different and even unknown. Finally, we also show some results for estimating the margin of victory of a district-based election within both additive and multiplicative error bounds.

Introduction

Voting and election serve as one of the most popular methodologies to aggregate different preferences, eventually choosing one of many candidate options. In political elections, one of the hottest questions for NEWS media and many other people is who will win in the upcoming election
[
23
]
? To predict the winner of an upcoming election, a pollster typically samples some votes with the hope that the sampled votes will help him/her correctly predict the winner. However, sampling votes, depending on the sampling requirement and procedure, typically involves substantial cost. Hence, a natural goal of the pollster is to minimize the cost, which often translates to minimizing the number of samples, without compromising the quality (or success rate) of prediction. Intuitively speaking, this is the winner prediction problem, which is the main focus of our paper.

The same winner prediction problem becomes relevant not only for predicting the winner of an upcoming election, but also in many other applications like social surveys, post election audit, etc. Organizations and companies, for example, often conduct various surveys to predict the success of their products which they are planning to manufacture. We carry out post election audits on paper ballots to check if there are any human or machine-related errors in the election process
[
21
,
26
,
14
,
24
,
27
]
.

Bhattacharyya and Dey resolved the sample-complexity of the winner prediction problem for many popular voting rules, for example,
k
-approval, Borda, approval, maximin, simplified Bucklin, and plurality with run off
[
3
]
. A voting rule is a function which selects one winner from a set of votes. We refer to the chapter by Zwicker for an introduction to voting and some common voting rules
[
28
]
. However, Bhattacharyya and Dey only considered single district elections whereas many real-world election systems, especially political elections in many countries, for example, US Presidential election, Indian general elections, etc. are district based. In a district-based election system, the voters are partitioned into districts. We use some voting rule
r
, the plurality voting rule for US Presidential election and Indian general elections, to select a winner in each district. The candidate (for US Presidential election) or the political party (Indian general elections) who wins in a maximum number of districts is declared as the winner of the election. In the plurality voting system, each voter votes for one of the candidates and the candidate who receives the maximum number of votes is declared as the winner. We study the winner prediction problem for district-based elections in this paper.

Our problem is also closely related to the general question: do we need to see all the votes to determine the winner? Conitzer and Sandholm developed preference elicitation policies as a sequence of questions posed to the voters

Preliminaries

We now define an election
E
. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a set of
N
voters and
C
be a set of
m
candidates. The vote of each voter
v\in\mathcal{V}
is a complete order over the set of candidates. Let
\mathcal{L}(C)
denote the set of all complete orders over
C
. Thus
\mathcal{L}(C)^{N}
denotes the set of all preference profiles of the
N
voters. A map
r\colon\mathcal{L}(C)^{N}\rightarrow C
is called a
voting rule
. Throughout we assume that there is an arbitrary but fixed rule for resolving ties. For any
a\in\mathcal{L}(C)
, let
s(a)
denote the most preferred candidate in
a
. A voting rule
r
is said to be
top-ranked
if
r(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})=r(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})
whenever
s(a_{i})=s(b_{i})
,
\forall i\in[N]
, i.e the winner of the election depends only the most preferred candidates of the
N
voters. For a top-ranked voting rule, we say that a candidate
x\in C

receives
vote
a\in\mathcal{L}(C)
if
x=s(a)
. We study two top-ranked voting rules - the plurality rule and the median rule.

Given an election
E
, for any two candidates
x,y\in C
, let
\pi_{E}(x,y)
denote the number of voters who prefer
x
to
y
. Define
\rho_{E}(x,y)=\pi_{E}(x,y)-\pi_{E}(y,x)
. Then a candidate
x
is called the
Condorcet winner
of the election if
\rho_{E}(x,y)>0
,
\forall y\in C\setminus\{x\}
. The Condorcet winner, if exists, is unique.

The
Margin Of Victory
(MOV) of an election
E
, denoted by
\text{MOV}(E)
, is defined as the minimum number of votes to be altered so as to change the winner of the election.

Bhattacharyya and Dey introduced the
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Determination
problem in
[
9
]
as follows:

Definition 7

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Determination
)
.

Given an election
E
whose margin of victory is at least
\varepsilon N
, determine the winner of the election with probability at least
1-\delta
.

They established upper and lower bounds for various homogeneous voting rules. Another related work is by Dey and Narahari
[
12
]
where they study the
(c,\varepsilon,\delta)-
Margin of Victory
problem.

Definition 8

(
(c,\varepsilon,\delta)-
Margin of Victory
)
.

Given an election
E
, determine
\text{MOV}(E)
with an additive error of at most
c\text{MOV}(E)+\varepsilon N
with probability at least

.

We repeatedly use the following concentration bounds.

Theorem 1

(
[
4
]
)
.

[Chernoff Bound]

Let
X_{1},\ldots X_{l}
be a sequence of
l
independent 0-1 random variables (not necessarily identical). Let
X=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}
and
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then for any
\theta\geqslant 0
,

1.

Additive form:
\text{Pr}(|X-\mu|\geqslant\theta l)\leqslant 2e^{-2\theta^{2}l}
.

2.

Multiplicative form:
\text{Pr}(|X-\mu|\geqslant\theta\mu)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\theta^{2}}{3}\mu}
.

Theorem 2

(
[
18
]
)
.

[Hoeffding’s Inequality]

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{l}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables such that
X_{i}\in[a,b]
,
\forall i\in[l]
, for some real numbers
a<b
. Let
\overline{X}=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}X_{i}}{l}
. Then for any
\theta\geqslant 0
,
\text{Pr}(|\overline{X}-\mathbb{E}[\overline{X}]|\geqslant\theta)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{2\theta^{2}}{(b-a)^{2}}l}
.

3
Winner Prediction for Plurality

For each candidate
x\in C
, let
g(x)
denote the number of votes where
x
is most preferred. Then the single-district plurality rule declares a candidate
x
with the highest value of
g(x)
, as the winner. Since plurality is a top-ranked voting rule, each vote can also be viewed as a single candidate.

We now introduce some notations. Given any list
L=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
of candidates, let
\text{MAJ}(L)
(resp.
\text{SEC-MAJ}(L)
) denote the candidate with the largest (resp. second largest) frequency in
L
(tie-breaking rule is arbitrary but fixed with respect to some arbitrary but fixed rule). We state some of the known results on upper and lower bounds on sample complexity. Let
E
be an election where the single-district plurality rule is used to decide the winner. The result below is a slight modification of Theorem 7 in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 3

(
[
9
]
)
.

If
\frac{3}{\vartheta^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}
votes are sampled uniformly at random with replacement, then with probability at least
1-\delta
, for every candidate, the fraction of sampled votes received differs from the true fraction of votes received by less than
\vartheta
, for any
\vartheta>0
.

Proof.

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 1

(
[
9
]
)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be defined by
f(x)=e^{-\frac{\lambda}{x}}
. Then

f(x)+f(y)\leqslant f(x-h)+f(y+h)

whenever
x,y,h>0
,
\frac{\lambda}{x+y}>2
and
h\leqslant x<y
.

For each candidate
x
, let
X^{x}_{i}
be the random variable indicating whether
x
receives the
i^{\text{th}}
sampled vote. Then
X^{x}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}X^{x}_{i}
denotes the number of sampled votes received by
x
. Let
\hat{g}(x)=\frac{N}{l}\cdot X^{x}
denote the predicted number of votes of candidate
x
. Thus
\text{Pr}(\left|\hat{g}(x)-g(x)\right|\geqslant\vartheta N)=\text{Pr}(\left|\frac{l}{N}\cdot\hat{g}(x)-\frac{l}{N}\cdot g(x)\right|\geqslant\frac{\vartheta N}{g(x)}\cdot\frac{lg(x)}{N})\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\vartheta^{2}lN}{3g(x)}}
. The final inequality follows by applying the multiplicative form of Chernoff bound (
Theorem

1
) with
\theta=\frac{\vartheta N}{g(x)}
.

By union bound,
\text{Pr}(\exists x\in C,\left|\hat{g}(x)-g(x)\right|\geqslant\vartheta N)\leqslant\sum\limits_{x\in C}2e^{-\frac{\vartheta^{2}lN}{3g(x)}}\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\vartheta^{2}l}{3}}=\delta
. The second inequality follows from Lemma
1
: since
g(x)\in[0,N]

\forall x\in C
, and
\sum\limits_{x\in C}g(x)=N
,
\sum\limits_{x\in C}2e^{-\frac{\vartheta^{2}lN}{3g(x)}}
is maximised when
g(x)=N
for some candidate
x
and
g(y)=0
,
\forall y\in C\setminus\{x\}
. Thus with probability at least
1-\delta
, for each candidate, the predicted number of votes differs from the true number of votes received by less than
\vartheta N
.
∎

3.1
Algorithm when MOV is known

In this section we assume that we know a lower bound
\varepsilon N
on
\text{MOV}(E)
. We present an algorithm that predicts the winner of the election correctly with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Algorithm 1

1:

Sample
l_{1}=\frac{1024}{3\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
districts from
D
uniformly at random with replacement.

2:

In each of the sampled districts, sample
l_{2}=\frac{192}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{64}{\varepsilon}
votes uniformly at random with replacement and predict their winners using the single-district plurality rule.

3:

return
a candidate that wins in maximum number of sampled districts.

Clearly
l_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
and
l_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
. Thus the above algorithm uses
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
samples.

Lemma 2
.

The sample complexity of
Algorithm

1
is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

To analyze the success probability of our
Algorithm

1
, we instead analyze a different algorithm,
Algorithm

2

Algorithm

1
.

Algorithm 2

1:

From each district, sample
l_{2}=\frac{192}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{64}{\varepsilon}
votes uniformly at random with replacement. Let

be the candidate that receives the largest number of sampled votes in district

,

2:

Sample

(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
. Let the list of sampled candidates be

We show that with probability at least

The crux of our analysis is showing that with high probability the following two statements hold:

2.

The margin of victory of

We now attempt to formalise the above notion. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
denote the true winners of the
k

w=\text{MAJ}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
denote the winner of the election and let
w^{\prime}=\text{SEC-MAJ}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
. For any candidate
x\in C
, let
f(x)
denote the number of districts in which
x
wins. For
x\in C
and

, let

denote the actual number of votes received by candidate

We first show that

wins in
\Omega(\varepsilon k)
districts more than

Lemma 3
.

f(w)-f(w^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{3}
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into two cases:

Case 1
-
f(w)\geqslant k/3
: Clearly if the winner is changed from
w
to
w^{\prime}
in
(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
districts in which
w
has won, then
w^{\prime}
would become the winner of the resulting election. The total population of the least populated
(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
districts in which
w
wins is at most
\frac{N}{f(w)}\cdot(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))\leqslant\frac{3N}{k}\cdot(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
. Clearly if
w^{\prime}
receives all votes in each of these districts, then
w^{\prime}
would become the new winner of the election. But since
\text{MOV}(E)\geqslant\varepsilon N
, we must have
\frac{3N}{k}\cdot(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))\geqslant\varepsilon N
, implying
f(w)-f(w^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{3}
, as desired.

Case 2
-
f(w)<k/3
: In this case
f(w^{\prime})\leqslant f(w)<k/3
. Thus there exist more than
k/3
districts where neither
w
nor
w^{\prime}
has won. If
w^{\prime}
is made the winner in
(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
districts out of these, then clearly
w^{\prime}
would become the new winner of the election. Again the total population of the least populated
(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
such districts is at most
\frac{N}{k/3}\cdot(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))
. Hence
\frac{3N}{k}\cdot(f(w)-f(w^{\prime}))\geqslant\varepsilon N
, implying that
f(w)-f(w^{\prime})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{3}
.
∎

Now we show that for each district
d_{j}
, with high probability, the numbers of votes secured by
y_{j}
and
c_{j}
are close.

Lemma 4
.

\text{Pr}\left(g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon n_{j}}{4}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
,
\forall j\in[k]
.

Proof.

Let
C_{j}=\{x\in C\mid g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(x)\leqslant\varepsilon n_{j}/4\}
. We need to show that
\text{Pr}(y_{j}\in C_{j})\geqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
. Let
X^{x}_{j}
be the random variable denoting the number of sampled votes received by candidate
x
in district
d_{j}
. Let
\hat{g}_{j}(x)=\frac{n_{j}}{l_{2}}\cdot X^{x}_{j}
. From
Theorem

3
, we have
\text{Pr}(\forall x\in C,|\hat{g}_{j}(x)-g_{j}(x)|\leqslant\varepsilon n_{j}/8)\geqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
. If this holds, then
\hat{g}_{j}(c_{j})
would be at least
g_{j}(c_{j})-\varepsilon n_{j}/8
while for any candidate
x\in C\setminus C_{j}
,
\hat{g}_{j}(x)
would be at most
g_{j}(x)+\varepsilon n_{j}/8<g_{j}(c_{j})-\varepsilon n_{j}/8
. Hence
\text{Pr}(y_{j}\in C_{j})\geqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{E}
denote the event that the difference
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})
exceeds
\varepsilon n_{j}/4
in at most
\varepsilon k/16
districts
d_{j}
. The next lemma shows that if
k
is sufficiently large,
\mathcal{E}
happens with high probability.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that
k\geqslant\frac{96}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
. Then
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{E})\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be the random variable denoting the number of districts
d_{j}
where
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})>\varepsilon n_{j}/4
. From Lemma
4
, it follows that
\mathbb{E}[Y]\leqslant\varepsilon k/32
. Using the multiplicative form of Chernoff bound (
Theorem

1
) with
\theta=1
,
\text{Pr}\left(\overline{\mathcal{E}}\right)=\text{Pr}(Y>\varepsilon k/16)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon k}{96}}\leqslant\delta/2
.
∎

If
\mathcal{E}
holds, the list
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
can be transformed into another list
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
where for each
j\in[k]
,
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(u_{j})\leqslant\varepsilon n_{j}/4
, by altering at most
\varepsilon k/16
entries. The next lemma lists some properties of the list
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
be as defined above. Then

1.

\text{MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=w
.

2.

Suppose in each district
d_{j}
,
u_{j}
is made the winner by transferring
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(u_{j})
votes received by
c_{j}
to
u_{j}
, keeping everything else the same. Let
E^{\prime}
denote the resulting election. Then
\text{MOV}(E^{\prime})\geqslant 3\varepsilon N/4
.

3.

f(w)-f(\text{SEC-MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}))\geqslant\varepsilon k/4
.

Proof.

1.

Since in district
d_{j}
, the winner can be changed from
c_{j}
to
u_{j}
by altering at most
\varepsilon n_{j}/4
votes, the total number of votes altered to go from
E
to
E^{\prime}
is at most
\sum\limits_{j\in[k]}\varepsilon n_{j}/4=\varepsilon N/4
. Since
\text{MOV}(E)=\varepsilon N
, the winner of the election cannot change by altering only
\varepsilon N/4
votes. Thus
w
must be the winner of the election
E^{\prime}
and hence
\text{MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=w
.

2.

The number of votes altered to go from
E
to
E^{\prime}
is at most
\varepsilon N/4
as noted in part 1. Hence in order to change the winner of the election, at least further
\varepsilon N-\frac{\varepsilon N}{4}=\frac{3\varepsilon N}{4}
votes must be altered and therefore
\text{MOV}(E^{\prime})\geqslant 3\varepsilon N/4
.

3.

Applying Lemma
3
to the election
E^{\prime}
, it directly follows that
f(w)-f(\text{SEC-MAJ}

(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}))\geqslant\frac{(3\varepsilon/4)k}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon k}{4}
.

∎

Finally we show that
Algorithm

2
(and hence
Algorithm

1
) returns the true winner with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Lemma 7
.

Algorithm

1
predicts the true winner with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

6
, we have
\text{MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=w
and
f(w)-f(\text{SEC-MAJ}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}))\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon k}{16}=\frac{3\varepsilon k}{16}
. It follows from Corollary
1
that sampling
\frac{3}{(3\varepsilon/32)^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta/2}=\frac{1024}{3\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
candidates uniformly at random with replacement from the list
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
would predict
w
as the winner with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
.

Finally let
\mathcal{F}
be the event that
Algorithm

1
does not predict the winner correctly. Then
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{F})\leqslant\text{Pr}(\mathcal{F}|\mathcal{E})+\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{E}})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}=\delta
.
∎

Combining
Lemma

2
and
Lemma

7
, we have the following the result.

Theorem 5
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner Prediction
for the plurality rule with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

3.2
Optimality

We now show that the sample complexity of our algorithm is essentially optimal (upto constant factors and logarithmic terms), if we restrict our attention to a special class of algorithms for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Prediction
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be any algorithm that works in the following way (here
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
depend only on
k,\varepsilon,\delta
and the
n_{j}
’s):

1.

Sample
l_{1}
districts uniformly at random with replacement from
D
.

2.

Sample
l_{2}
votes uniformly at random with replacement from each of the
l_{1}
sampled districts and predict their winners using the single-district plurality rule.

3.

Return a candidate that wins in maximum number of sampled districts.

Then for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, we have
l_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{64\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{8e\sqrt{\pi}\delta}
and
l_{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{1600\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{3}{4e\sqrt{\pi}}
even when there are 2 candidates A and B and each district has equal population
n=N/k
.

Proof.

Similar to the analysis of
Algorithm

1
, we may propose an alternate sampling algorithm
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, whose probability of predicting the winner is easily seen to be the same as that of
\mathcal{B}
.
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
first samples
l_{2}
votes uniformly at random with replacement from each district and predicts their winners using the single-district plurality rule. It then samples
l_{1}
candidates uniformly at random with replacement from the list of predicted winners (call it
L
) and returns a majority candidate.

Lower bound on
\mathbf{l_{1}}
:
We provide a reduction from the
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Determination
problem. Consider the following single-district election
E
: there are 2 candidates A and B, and
N^{\prime}
voters, out of which
\left(\frac{1}{2}+4\varepsilon\right)N^{\prime}
vote for A and the remaining vote for B. Clearly then
\text{MOV}(E)=4\varepsilon N^{\prime}
. We create another election
E^{\prime}
with the same 2 candidates A and B as follows: for each voter
v
, we create a district
d_{v}
consisting of
n
voters (for some sufficiently large
n
). Let
D^{A}=\{d_{v}\mid v\text{ votes for A}\}
and
D^{B}=\{d_{v}\mid v\text{ votes for B}\}
. Let
D=D^{A}\cup D^{B}
. Thus
\left|D^{A}\right|=\left(\frac{1}{2}+4\varepsilon\right)N^{\prime}
,
\left|D^{B}\right|=\left(\frac{1}{2}-4\varepsilon\right)N^{\prime}
and
|D|=k=N^{\prime}
. The total number of voters in the election
E^{\prime}
is
N=nk=nN^{\prime}
. In each
d_{v}\in D^{A}
, let A receive
3n/4
votes and B receive
n/4
votes while in each
d_{v}\in D^{B}
, let B receive all
n
votes. This completes the description of
E^{\prime}
. Clearly
\text{MOV}(E^{\prime})=\frac{n}{4}\cdot 4\varepsilon k=\varepsilon N
.

Now since B receives all votes in each district in
D^{B}
, the algorithm
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
would surely predict B as the winner in
|D^{B}|
districts. Thus B occurs at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}-4\varepsilon\right)k
times in the list
L
, and therefore A occurs at most
\left(\frac{1}{2}+4\varepsilon\right)k
times. Hence if
l_{1}<\frac{1}{64\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{8e\sqrt{\pi}\delta}
, we would have an algorithm with sample complexity less than
\frac{1}{4\cdot(4\varepsilon)^{2}}\log\frac{1}{8e\sqrt{\pi}\delta}
for predicting the winner of
E
with probability at least
1-\delta
, contradicting
Theorem

4
.

Lower bound on
\mathbf{l_{2}}
:
Consider the following election
E
: there are 2 candidates A and B and a set
D
of
k
districts. Each district has the same population
n
. A wins in
11k/20
districts (call this set
D^{A}
) by receiving
\left(\frac{1}{2}+20\varepsilon\right)n
votes in each, and B wins in the remaining districts (set
D^{B}
) by receiving all the
n
votes in each. Thus
\text{MOV}(E)=20\varepsilon n\cdot\left(\frac{11k}{20}-\frac{k}{2}\right)=\varepsilon N
.

Clearly in each district of
D^{B}
, B would be predicted as the winner (by
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
). Now consider any
d\in D^{A}
. If
l_{2}<\frac{1}{4\cdot(20\varepsilon)^{2}}\log\frac{1}{8e\sqrt{\pi}\cdot(1/6)}
, then from
Theorem

4
, the probability that A is predicted as the winner in district
d
would be at most
1-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6}
. Let
\mathcal{Y}
be the random variable denoting the number of districts where A is declared as the winner. Hence
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}]\leqslant\frac{11k}{20}\cdot\frac{5}{6}=\frac{11k}{24}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{1}
denote the event that
\mathcal{Y}\leqslant 7k/15
. Using Markov’s inequality,
\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{1})\leqslant 55/56
. Now let
\mathcal{W}
be the random variable denoting the number of sampled districts (in the samples drawn in the second step of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
) where A is the predicted winner. Let
\mathcal{D}_{2}
denote the event that
\left|\frac{\mathcal{W}}{l_{1}}-\frac{\mathcal{Y}}{k}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{32}
. Since
l_{1}\geqslant\frac{1}{64\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{8e\sqrt{\pi}\delta}\geqslant 3\cdot 32^{2}\log\frac{2}{\delta}
(for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
), using
Theorem

3
,
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{2})\geqslant 1-\delta
. Finally let
\mathcal{D}_{3}
denote the event that algorithm
\mathcal{B}
predicts A as the winner of the election. Note that
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{3}|\mathcal{D}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{2})=0
as conditioned on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
, the fraction of sampled districts where A can be the predicted winner is at most
\frac{7}{15}+\frac{1}{32}<\frac{1}{2}
. So
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{3}|\mathcal{D}_{1})=\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{3}|\mathcal{D}_{1}\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\leqslant\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{2})\leqslant\delta
. Thus
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{3})\leqslant\text{Pr}(\mathcal{D}_{3}|\mathcal{D}_{1})+\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}_{1})\leqslant\delta+\frac{55}{56}<1-\delta
, a contradiction (assuming
\delta<1/112
).
∎

3.3
Generalization

Now consider the following setting: suppose the winner of each district is decided using some voting rule
r
and the overall winner of the election
E
is a candidate that wins in maximum number of districts. Let
\text{MOV}(E)\geqslant\varepsilon N
. Suppose we wish to predict the winner of such an election with probability at least
1-\delta
. Observe that if, as in the proof of
Lemma

4
, we can ensure that in each district
d_{j}
, with high probability (at least
1-\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
)
y_{j}
can be made the winner by altering at most
\varepsilon/4
fraction of the population of
d_{j}
, then the rest of the proof would be exactly similar.

Let
\chi_{r}(m,\varepsilon,\delta)
be the number of samples required so that the predicted winner of a single-district election using using rule
r
with
n
voters and
m
candidates, can be made winner by changing at most
\varepsilon n
votes (note that we need
\chi_{r}
to be independent of the population
n
). Then we have the following result.

Theorem 7
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner Prediction
for arbitrary voting rule
r
with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{r}(m,\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

3.4
Algorithms when MOV is unknown

We now consider two restricted settings of the district-level plurality election. We assume that there are only 2 candidates
A
and
B
. Wlog assume that A is the true winner of the election. Let
n=N/k
denote the average population of a district. We assume that no bound on the margin of victory is known to us. We present two algorithms that work even in this setting, whose sample complexity can be bounded in terms of the (unknown) MOV.

3.4.1
When
n_{j}\leqslant\kappa n

Suppose there exists a parameter
\kappa
(
\geqslant 4
) such that the population of each district is at most
\kappa
times the average population of a district. Let
\text{MOV}(E)=\varepsilon N
which is unknown to the algorithm.

Algorithm 3

1:

\gamma\leftarrow\frac{1}{3}
.

2:

Sample
l_{1}=\frac{5\kappa^{2}}{18\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\gamma\delta}
districts from
D
uniformly at random with replacement.

3:

From each of the sampled districts, sample
l_{2}=\frac{5\kappa^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2l_{1}}{\gamma\delta}
votes uniformly at random with replacement and predict their winners using the single-district plurality rule.

4:

If there exists a candidate that wins in at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\gamma}{\kappa}\right)l_{1}
sampled districts by receiving at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2\gamma}{\kappa}\right)l_{2}
sampled votes in each, then declare that candidate as the winner and halt.

5:

\gamma\leftarrow\frac{\gamma}{3}
.

6:

goto
2.

For estimating the success probability as well as bounding the sample complexity of the above algorithm, we show the following.

1.

Whenever
Algorithm

3
terminates, it predicts A as the winner with high probability.

2.

As the value of
\gamma
goes below
(1-\Omega(1))\varepsilon
, the probability that
Algorithm

3
does not terminate decreases exponentially with the number of iterations.

The idea is to show that the proportions of votes received by A and B in each sampled district is represented faithfully in the samples drawn in the second step of
Algorithm

3
. Also it can be shown that
Algorithm

3
samples enough districts from the set of districts where A (and B) has won with a “large” margin of victory. Conditioning on these two events, (1.) follows by showing that whenever
Algorithm

3
terminates, the predicted winner must have won in more than
k/2
districts and therefore must be the true winner (i.e. A).

For (2.), using the fact that
\text{MOV}(E)=\varepsilon N
, it can be shown that A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(\varepsilon)
fraction of votes in at least
\frac{1}{2}+\Omega(\varepsilon)
fraction of districts. Thus, when the value of
\gamma
goes below
(1-\Omega(1))\varepsilon
,
Algorithm

3
terminates with high probability.

We now attempt to formalise the above notions. Let
\tau^{A}(\gamma)
(resp.
\tau^{B}(\gamma)
) denote the fraction of districts where A (resp. B) receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}
fraction of votes in the election
E
. When
\gamma=(\frac{1}{3})^{i}
, let
\mathcal{F}^{A}_{i}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{i}
) denote the event that the fraction of sampled districts where A (resp. B) wins with at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}
fraction of votes lies within an additive error of
3\gamma/\kappa
from
\tau^{A}(\gamma)
(resp.
\tau^{B}(\gamma)
). Let
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{A}_{i}\cap\mathcal{F}^{B}_{i}
.

Lemma 8
.

\text{Pr}\left(\mathcal{F}_{i}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{243^{i}}
.

Proof.

Let
W
be the random variable denoting the number of sampled districts where A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}
fraction of votes. Then
\mathbb{E}[W]=\frac{\tau^{A}(\gamma)}{k}\cdot l_{1}
. Using the additive form of Chernoff bound (
Theorem

1
) with
\theta=3\gamma/\kappa
, we have
\text{Pr}\left(\overline{\mathcal{F}}^{A}_{i}\right)=\text{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{W}{l_{1}}-\frac{\tau^{A}(\gamma)}{k}\right|\geqslant\frac{3\gamma}{\kappa}\right)=\text{Pr}\left(\left|W-\frac{\tau^{A}(\gamma)}{k}\cdot l_{1}\right|\geqslant\frac{3\gamma}{\kappa}\cdot l_{1}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{18\gamma^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\cdot l_{1}}
. Since
l_{1}=\frac{5\kappa^{2}}{18\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\gamma\delta}\geqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{18\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\gamma^{5}\delta}
, we get
\text{Pr}\left(\overline{\mathcal{F}}^{A}_{i}\right)\leqslant\frac{\gamma^{5}\delta}{2}=\frac{\delta}{2\cdot 243^{i}}
. In a similar way, it follows that
\text{Pr}\left(\overline{\mathcal{F}}^{B}_{i}\right)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2\cdot 243^{i}}
. Thus using union bound, we get
\text{Pr}\left(\mathcal{F}_{i}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{243^{i}}
.
∎

Again when
\gamma=(\frac{1}{3})^{i}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
denote the event that in each of the sampled districts, the fraction of sampled votes received by A lies within an additive error of
\gamma/\kappa
from the true fraction of votes received by A in that district. Note that if
\mathcal{E}_{i}
holds, then in each district, the sampled fraction of votes received by B also lies within an additive error of
\gamma/\kappa
from the true fraction of votes received by B in that district.

Lemma 9
.

\text{Pr}(\mathcal{E}_{i})\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{243^{i}}
.

Proof.

Consider any sampled district
d
and let
\beta_{d}
be the true fraction of votes received by A in
d
. Let
Z_{d}
be the random variable denoting the number of sampled votes received by A in
d
. Clearly then
\mathbb{E}[Z_{d}]=\beta_{d}l_{2}
. Using the additive form of Chernoff bound (
Theorem

1
) with
\theta=\gamma/\kappa
, we have
\text{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{Z_{d}}{l_{2}}-\beta_{d}\right|>\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)=\text{Pr}\left(\left|Z_{d}-\mathbb{E}[Z_{d}]\right|>\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\cdot l_{2}\right)\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\cdot l_{2}}
. Since
l_{2}=\frac{5\kappa^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2l_{1}}{\gamma\delta}\geqslant\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\log\frac{2l_{1}}{\gamma^{5}\delta}
, we get
\text{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{Z_{d}}{l_{2}}-\beta_{d}\right|>\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)\leqslant\frac{\gamma^{5}\delta}{l_{1}}=\frac{\delta}{243^{i}\cdot l_{1}}
. Now using union bound,
\text{Pr}\left(\overline{\mathcal{E}}_{i}\right)=\text{Pr}\left(\exists d,\left|\frac{Z_{d}}{l_{2}}-\beta_{d}\right|>\frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{243^{i}}
.
∎

Now using the fact that
\text{MOV}(E)=\varepsilon N
, we show the following result.

Lemma 10
.

Assuming
k
is even and
\varepsilon k/\kappa
is an integer, there exist at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\right)k
districts where A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes.

Proof.

Let
D^{A}
be the set of districts where A wins and let
|D^{A}|=\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)k
. For each
d\in D^{A}
, let
\text{MOV}(E_{d})
be the minimum number of votes to be changed in district
d
in order to make B the winner of that district. Wlog let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)k}
be the districts of
D^{A}
arranged in non-decreasing order of
\text{MOV}(E_{d})
. Clearly if B is made the winner in the districts
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\nu k+1}
, then B would become the winner of the election. Since
\text{MOV}(E)=\varepsilon N
, we must have
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\nu k+1}\text{MOV}(E_{d_{j}})\geqslant\varepsilon N
. Thus
\text{MOV}(E_{d_{\nu k+1}})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon N}{\nu k+1}\geqslant\varepsilon n
(since
\nu\leqslant 1/2
) and therefore
\text{MOV}(E_{d_{j}})\geqslant\varepsilon n
,
\forall\nu k+1\leqslant j\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)k
.

Now let
I=\{d\in D^{A}\mid\text{MOV}(E_{d})<\varepsilon n/2\}
. Thus we have
\varepsilon N\leqslant\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\nu k+1}\text{MOV}(E_{d_{j}})\leqslant|I|\cdot\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}+(\nu k+1-|I|)\cdot(\frac{\kappa n}{2}+1)
(since the population of each district is at most
\kappa n
,
\text{MOV}(E_{d})\leqslant\kappa n/2+1
,
\forall d\in D^{A}
). Algebraic simplification would yield
\nu k-|I|\geqslant\frac{\left(1-\frac{\nu}{2}\right)\varepsilon N-\left(\frac{\kappa n}{2}+1\right)}{\left(\frac{\kappa n}{2}+1\right)-\frac{\varepsilon n}{2}}>\frac{\frac{3\varepsilon N}{4}}{\frac{\kappa n}{2}+1}-1\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{\kappa}-1
. Since
\nu k-|I|
is an integer, we must have
\nu k-|I|\geqslant\varepsilon k/\kappa
. Thus in at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\right)k
districts,
\text{MOV}(E_{d})\geqslant\varepsilon n
. Finally since the population of any district is at most
\kappa n
,
\text{MOV}(E_{d})\geqslant\varepsilon n
implies that A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes in district
d
.
∎

Now let
S=\{\eta\mid
There exist at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{\kappa}\right)k
districts where A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\eta}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes
\}
. Let
\alpha=\max\limits_{\eta\in S}\eta
and let
D_{\alpha}
be the set of districts where A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes. Let
\rho
be the unique positive integer such that
\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{\rho}\leqslant\alpha<\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{\rho-1}
. We now argue that
Algorithm

3
terminates with high probability as the value of
\gamma
goes below
\alpha
.

Lemma 11
.

For
j\geqslant 2
, the probability that
Algorithm

3
does not terminate when
\gamma=\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{\rho+j}
is at most
\frac{3\delta}{243^{\rho+j}}
.

Proof.

By definition, in each district of
D_{\alpha}
, A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes. Now assuming the event
\mathcal{E}_{\rho+j}
holds, if any district from
D_{\alpha}
is sampled, the fraction of sampled votes received by A in that district would be at least

4
Winner Prediction for Median Rule

We now turn our attention to another popular voting rule - the median rule. Here there is a
harmonious order

\mathcal{R}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
over the candidates. For each candidate
x\in C
, let
g(x)
denote the number of votes where
x
is most preferred. Then the winner of the election is the candidate
c_{t}
such that (i)
\sum_{i=1}^{t}g(c_{i})\geqslant N/2
, and (ii)
\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}g(c_{i})<N/2
.

Like the plurality rule, the median rule is also an example of a top-ranked voting rule. We first restrict our attention only to the single-district case. We assume that a lower bound of
\varepsilon N
is known on the Margin of Victory of the election.

4.1

Let us first consider the setting where the harmonious order

is known to the algorithm. Our algorithm is as follows:

Algorithm 5

1:

, Let

.

Let
c_{s}
be the candidate such that

.

3:

return

c_{s}

be the true winner of the election. Using the fact that

\varepsilon N
between
\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}g(c_{i})
and

.

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}g(c_{i})\leqslant\frac{N}{2}-\varepsilon N

Proof.

Clearly

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}g(c_{i})\geqslant\frac{N}{2}+\varepsilon N

4:

return
the candidate
x
such that
h^{\prime}(x,y)>0
,
\forall y\neq x
.

Algorithm

6
outputs the Condorcet winner out of a sample of
l
votes. Since the preferences are single-peaked, it follows from
Lemma

26
that
Algorithm

6
in fact predicts the winner resulting by applying the median rule with respect to the order
\mathcal{R}
, on the
l
samples. It has already been proven in the previous subsection that that this would predict the winner correctly with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Theorem 11
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Determination
for the median rule with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
when the Harmonious Order is not known.

4.3
Optimality

It is clear that the median rule reduces to the plurality rule when there are 2 candidates.
Theorem

4
gives a lower bound of
\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
on the sample complexity for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner-Determination
for any voting rule that reduces to the single-district plurality rule for 2 candidates. Thus the sample complexities of
Algorithm

5
and
Algorithm

6
are optimal upto constant factors.

4.4
The multiple districts case

We now consider the case when the
N
voters are arranged into
k
districts. The winner of each district is decided by applying the median rule. The Harmonious orders in the districts may or may not be the same and may or may not be known to us. If the Harmonious order is unknown in a district, we make the assumption that the preference of each voter in that district is single-peaked with respect to some order
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
. The overall winner of the election is a candidate that wins in maximum number of districts.

It follows as a corollary of
Theorem

10
and
Theorem

11
that when
r
is the median rule,
\chi_{r}(m,\varepsilon,\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
. Thus assuming a lower bound of
\varepsilon N
on the Margin of Victory of the election
E
, we get the following result using
Theorem

7
.

Corollary 2
.

There exists an algorithm with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
Winner Prediction
for the median rule.

5
Winner Prediction with imperfect Samples

Till now, we assumed that we could get uniform random samples from the population. However this might not always be the case. We now present algorithms for
(\varepsilon,\delta,\gamma)-
Winner-Determination
and
(\varepsilon,\delta,\gamma)-
Winner-Prediction
.

5.1
Algorithm for single-district election

Suppose plurality rule is used to determine the winner. Our algorithm is as follows:

Algorithm 7

1:

Sample
\frac{3}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}
votes from the distribution
U
without replacement.

2:

return
a candidate that receives the largest number of sampled votes.

We continue to use the same notations as introduced in
Section

3
. For
x\in C
, let
\mathcal{S}(x)
be the set of candidates that vote for
x
. Let
w=\arg\max_{x\in C}|\mathcal{S}(x)|
be the winner of the election and
w^{\prime}=\arg\max_{x\in C\setminus\{w\}}|\mathcal{S}(x)|
be a candidate receiving the second largest number of votes. Since
\text{MOV}(E)\geqslant\varepsilon N
, we must have
|\mathcal{S}(w)|-|\mathcal{S}(w^{\prime})|\geqslant 2\varepsilon N-1
. Since
d_{\text{TV}}(U,V)\leqslant\gamma
, we immediately have the following result.

Lemma 27
.

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(w)}p_{i}\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{S}(w)|}{N}-\gamma
,
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(w^{\prime})}p_{i}\leqslant\frac{|\mathcal{S}(w^{\prime})|}{N}+\gamma
.

Hence we get
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(w)}p_{i}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}(w^{\prime})}p_{i}\geqslant\frac{|\mathcal{S}(w)|-|\mathcal{S}(w^{\prime})|}{N}-2\gamma\geqslant 2(\varepsilon-\gamma)-\frac{1}{N}
. Thus the margin of victory of the election
with respect to
the distribution
U
is at least
(\varepsilon-\gamma)N
. Hence using
Corollary

1
,
\frac{3}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta}
samples are enough to predict the winner correctly with probability at least
1-\delta
. Hence we have the following result.

Theorem 12
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta,\gamma)-
Winner-Determination
for the plurality rule with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

5.2
Algorithm for the multiple-districts case

Our algorithm and its analysis is very similar to that of
Algorithm

1
.

Algorithm 8

1:

Sample
l_{1}=\frac{3072}{(3\varepsilon-32\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
districts from
D
uniformly at random with replacement.

2:

In each of the sampled districts, sample
l_{2}=\frac{192}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{64}{\varepsilon}
votes uniformly at random with replacement and predict their winners using the single-district plurality rule.

3:

return
a candidate that wins in maximum number of sampled districts.

The sample complexity of the above algorithm is easily seen to be
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
. For analysing the success probability, we may again propose an alternate sampling algorithm whose probability of predicting the winner is same as that of
Algorithm

8
.

Algorithm 9

1:

From each district, sample
l_{2}=\frac{192}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{64}{\varepsilon}
votes uniformly at random with replacement. Let
y_{j}
be the candidate that receives the largest number of sampled votes in district
d_{j}
,
j\in[k]
.

2:

Sample
l_{1}=\frac{3072}{(3\varepsilon-32\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
candidates uniformly at random with replacement from the list
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
. Let the list of sampled candidates be
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{l_{1}})
.

3:

return

\text{MAJ}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{l_{1}})
.

Now it can be easily verified that
Lemma

4
continues to hold, i.e.
\text{Pr}\left(g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})\leqslant\varepsilon n_{j}/4\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{32}
. Thus for sufficiently large
k
, with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, the difference
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})
would exceed
\varepsilon n_{j}/4
in at most
\varepsilon k/16
districts
d_{j}
. Conditioning on this, the list
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
can be transformed into a list
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
such that
g_{j}(c_{j})-g_{j}(y_{j})\leqslant\varepsilon n_{j}/4
,
\forall j\in[k]
by altering at most
\varepsilon k/16
entries. From
Lemma

6
,
\text{MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})=w
and
f(w)-f(\text{SEC-MAJ}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}))\geqslant\varepsilon k/4
. Thus
f(w)-f(\text{SEC-MAJ}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}))\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k}{4}-\frac{\varepsilon k}{16}=\frac{3\varepsilon k}{16}
. Hence viewing
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
as a single-district plurality election, it follows from
Theorem

12
that sampling
\frac{3}{(\frac{3\varepsilon}{32}-\gamma)^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}
districts from the distribution
U
without replacement would predict the winner with probability at least
1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. Hence overall, the probability of correctly predicting the winner is at least
1-\delta
.

Theorem 13
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta,\gamma)-
Winner-Prediction
for the plurality rule with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(\varepsilon-\gamma)^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

6
Estimating Margin of Victory

For this section, we use
\gamma N
to denote the margin of victory of a district-based election;
\varepsilon
will be used for denoting error bounds. We first present an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Additive
for the district-level plurality election. This gives an estimate of the margin of victory within an additive
\varepsilon N
error. We then bootstrap our algorithm to get an estimate of the margin of victory within a multiplicative error of
1\pm\varepsilon
. Our algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Multiplicative
in fact works for any voting rule for which there exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Additive
.

6.1
Estimating MOV within additive error bounds

We consider the district-level plurality election with 2 candidates A and B. Wlog we assume that A is the true winner of the election. As before,
n=N/k
denotes the average population of a district and we assume that the population of each district is bounded by
\kappa n
, for some constant
\kappa\geqslant 2
.

Algorithm 10

1:

Sample
l_{1}=\frac{27\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{16}{\delta}
districts from
D
uniformly at random with replacement.

2:

In each of the sampled districts, sample
l_{2}=\frac{27\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{8l_{1}}{\delta}
votes uniformly at random with replacement and predict the number of votes received by A and B.

3:

return
the margin of victory of the sampled election.

It is easily seen that
l_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
and
l_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\right)
. Thus the sample complexity of the above algorithm is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{6}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
.

Lemma 28
.

Algorithm

10
uses
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{6}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
samples.

As in
Section

3.4.1
and
Section

3.4.2
, let
D^{A}
(resp.
D^{B}
) be the set of districts where A (resp. B) wins and let
\left|D^{A}\right|=(\frac{1}{2}+\nu)k
(assume
k
is even so that
\nu k
is an integer). For each
d\in D^{A}
, let
\text{MOV}(E_{d})
be the minimum number of votes to be changed in district
d
in order to make B the winner of that district. Wlog let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\nu\right)p}
be the districts of
D^{A}
arranged in non-decreasing order of
\text{MOV}(E_{d})
. Let
D^{A}_{1}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{\nu p+1}\}
and
D^{A}_{2}=D^{A}\setminus D^{A}_{1}
. Since
\text{MOV}(E)=\gamma N
, we have
\gamma N\leqslant\sum\limits_{d\in D_{1}^{A}}\text{MOV}(E_{d})\leqslant\gamma N+\kappa n
.

As in the analyses of
Algorithm

1
and
Theorem

6
, for estimating the probability that the returned estimate lies in the range
[(\gamma-\varepsilon)N,(\gamma+\varepsilon)N]
,
Algorithm

7
may be viewed as first sampling
l_{2}
votes in each district and predicting the number of votes received by A and B in each, and then sampling
l_{1}
districts uniformly at random with replacement.

Let
T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}
respectively be the set of districts sampled from
D^{A}_{1},D^{A}_{2},D^{B}
. Let
\left|T_{i}\right|=k_{i}
so that
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=l_{1}
. Observe that
T_{1}
(resp.
T_{2},T_{3}
) can be thought of as a uniform random sample of
k_{1}
(resp.
k_{2},k_{3}
) districts from
D^{A}_{1}
(resp.
D^{A}_{2},D^{B}
).

We now define the following events.

\mathcal{M}_{i}
:
\left|\frac{k_{i}}{l_{1}}-\frac{\left|D^{A}_{i}\right|}{k}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}
,
i\in[2]
.

\mathcal{M}_{3}
:
\left|\frac{k_{3}}{l_{1}}-\frac{\left|D^{B}\right|}{k}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}
.

\mathcal{M}_{4}
: In each district, the predicted fraction of votes received by A (and therefore B) lies within an additive error of less than
\varepsilon/3\kappa
from the true fraction of votes received by A (resp. B) in that district.

\mathcal{M}_{5}
:
\left|\frac{k_{1}}{\left|D^{A}_{1}\right|}-\frac{l_{1}}{k}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon l_{1}}{10k}
.

From
Theorem

3
, it directly follows that
\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{3}\mathcal{M}_{i}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{8}
and
\text{Pr}\left(\mathcal{M}_{4}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{4}
. Thus
\text{Pr}\left(\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{M}_{i}\right)\geqslant 1-\left(\frac{\delta}{8}-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)=1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
. We assume throughout that the event
\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{4}\mathcal{M}_{i}
holds true.

Let
e
denote the estimate returned by
Algorithm

7
. Let
e_{i}=\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\sum\limits_{d\in T_{i}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})
,
i\in[3]
. Thus
e=e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\{A,B\}
be the winner of the sampled election. In the following four lemmas, we show that
e\in[(\gamma-\varepsilon)N,(\gamma+\varepsilon)N]
with high probability.

Lemma 29
.

If
\mathcal{C}=A
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, then
e\leqslant(\gamma+\varepsilon)N
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{2}
holds and
\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})\leqslant\kappa n
,
\forall d\in D^{A}_{2}
, we have
e_{2}\leqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}l_{1}}{3\kappa}\cdot\kappa n=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}N}{3}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon N}{18}
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Similarly, since
\mathcal{M}_{4}
holds, if A is predicted as the winner in any district of
D^{B}
, the maximum possible Margin of Victory of A would be
\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}\cdot\kappa n=\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}
. Thus
e_{3}\leqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot l_{1}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}
.

We now upper bound
e_{1}
. If
\nu\leqslant\varepsilon/2\kappa
, we have
e_{1}\leqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\kappa}+\frac{1}{k}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}\right)l_{1}\cdot\kappa n\leqslant\frac{11\varepsilon N}{18}
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Hence the estimate
e=e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}\leqslant\frac{11\varepsilon N}{18}+\frac{\varepsilon N}{18}+\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}<(\gamma+\varepsilon)N
.

Now let
\nu>\varepsilon/2\kappa
. Clearly
\mathbb{E}[k_{1}]=\frac{l_{1}}{k}\cdot\left|D^{A}_{1}\right|>\nu l_{1}
. Applying the multiplicative form of Chernoff bound (
Theorem

1
) with
\theta=\varepsilon/10
, we get
\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{5})\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{300}\cdot\nu l_{1}}\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{600\kappa}l_{1}}\leqslant\delta/4
(since
l_{1}=\frac{27\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{16}{\delta}\geqslant\frac{600\kappa}{\varepsilon^{3}}\log\frac{8}{\delta}
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
). Notice that for
e_{1}
to be maximum, A must be declared the winner in each district of
T_{1}
. Let
U_{j}
be the predicted Margin of Victory of A in the
j^{\text{th}}
district of
T_{1}
,
j\in[k_{1}]
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{6}
denote the event that
\left|\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k_{1}}U_{j}}{k_{1}}-\frac{\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})}{\nu p+1}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon n}{8\nu}
. Note that each
U_{j}\in[0,\kappa n]
. Also, conditioned on
\mathcal{M}_{1}
,
k_{1}\geqslant\left(\nu-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}\right)l_{1}
. Since
\nu>\varepsilon/2\kappa
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, we have
k_{1}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4\kappa}l_{1}
. Applying Hoeffding’s inequality (
Theorem

2
) with
a=0
,
b=\kappa n/2
and
\theta=\varepsilon n/8\nu
, we have
\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{6}|\mathcal{M}_{1})\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{32\kappa^{2}\nu^{2}}\cdot k_{1}}\leqslant 2e^{-\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{32\kappa^{3}}\cdot l_{1}}\leqslant\delta/4
, since
l_{1}=\frac{27\kappa^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\log\frac{16}{\delta}\geqslant\frac{32\kappa^{3}}{\varepsilon^{3}}\log\frac{8}{\delta}
. Thus
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{6})\geqslant\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{6}|\mathcal{M}_{1})\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{1})\geqslant\left(1-\frac{\delta}{4}\right)\left(1-\frac{\delta}{8}\right)\geqslant 1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
.

Finally assume that
\mathcal{M}_{5}\cap\mathcal{M}_{6}
holds true. This happens with probability at least
1-\left(\frac{\delta}{4}+\frac{3\delta}{8}\right)=1-\frac{5\delta}{8}
. Now
\mathcal{M}_{5}
implies that
\frac{k}{l_{1}}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right)\frac{\nu k}{k_{1}}
. Also
\mathcal{M}_{4}
implies that
\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})\leqslant\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}(E_{d})+\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}\cdot\kappa n\cdot(\nu k+1)\leqslant\gamma N+\kappa n+\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}
. Hence
e_{1}=\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k_{1}}U_{j}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right)\frac{\nu k}{k_{1}}\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k_{1}}U_{j}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})+\frac{\varepsilon N}{8}+\frac{\varepsilon n}{8\nu}\right)\leqslant\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right)\left(\gamma N+\frac{\varepsilon N}{2}\right)\leqslant\left(\gamma+\frac{11\varepsilon}{18}\right)N
(as
\gamma\leqslant 1/2
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small). Thus with probability at least
1-\left(\frac{3\delta}{8}+\frac{5\delta}{8}\right)=1-\delta
, the estimate returned
e=e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}\leqslant(\gamma+\frac{11\varepsilon}{18})N+\frac{\varepsilon N}{40}+\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}=(\gamma+\varepsilon)N
.
∎

Lemma 30
.

If
\mathcal{C}=A
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, then
e\geqslant(\gamma-\varepsilon)N
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

If
\gamma<\varepsilon
, we are done since
e\geqslant 0\geqslant(\gamma-\varepsilon)N
. Hence assume that
\gamma\geqslant\varepsilon
. Since
\text{MOV}(E_{d})\leqslant\kappa n
,
\forall d\in D^{A}
and
\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}(E_{d})\geqslant\gamma N\geqslant\varepsilon N
, we have
\left|D^{A}_{1}\right|\geqslant\varepsilon k/\kappa
. As in the proof of Lemma
29
, Chernoff bound would give
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{5})\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{4}
. Observe that
e
will be minimised when in each district of
D^{A}
, the true fraction of votes received by A exceeds the predicted fraction by
\varepsilon/3\kappa
. Let
\overline{T}_{1}\subset T_{1}
be the set of districts of
T_{1}
where B is the predicted winner. Let
D^{\prime}
be the set of sampled districts that are considered in the computation of the margin of victory in step 3 of
Algorithm

7
and let
T^{\prime}_{1}=\overline{T}_{1}\cup D^{\prime}
and let
\left|T^{\prime}_{1}\right|=k^{\prime}_{1}
. Let
\mathcal{U}_{j}
be the predicted Margin of Victory of A in the
j^{\text{th}}
district of
T^{\prime}_{1}
, where
j\in[k^{\prime}_{1}]
(note that
\mathcal{U}_{j}
is negative in the districts of
\overline{T}_{1}
). Let
\mathcal{M}_{7}
denote the event that
\left|\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}\mathcal{U}_{j}}{k^{\prime}_{1}}-\frac{\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})}{\nu k+1}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon n}{8\nu}
(note that here
\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})
could be negative). Again similar to the proof of Lemma
29
, Hoeffding’s inequality gives
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{7}|\mathcal{M}_{1})\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{4}
and therefore
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{7})\geqslant 1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
. Therefore
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{M}_{5}\cap\mathcal{M}_{7})\geqslant 1-\frac{5\delta}{8}
.

Now
\mathcal{M}_{1}
implies that
k_{1}\geqslant\left(\nu-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}\right)l_{1}\geqslant\frac{15\varepsilon}{8\kappa}l_{1}
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{2}
implies that
k_{2}\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}\right)l_{1}
, it follows that
k^{\prime}_{1}\geqslant k_{1}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}l_{1}\geqslant k_{1}-\frac{4\varepsilon}{15}k_{1}=\left(1-\frac{4\varepsilon}{15}\right)k_{1}
. Thus assuming
\mathcal{M}_{5}\cap\mathcal{M}_{7}
holds,
e_{1}\geqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}\mathcal{U}_{j}\geqslant\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right)\left(1-\frac{4\varepsilon}{15}\right)\left(\sum\limits_{d\in D^{A}_{1}}\text{MOV}^{\prime}(E_{d})-\frac{\varepsilon N}{8}-\frac{\varepsilon n}{8\nu}\right)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{11\varepsilon}{30}\right)\left(\gamma N-\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}-\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}-\frac{\varepsilon N}{8}-\frac{\varepsilon n}{8\nu}\right)>(\gamma-\varepsilon)N
. Hence with probability at least
1-\delta
, the estimate
e\geqslant e_{1}\geqslant(\gamma-\varepsilon)N
.
∎

Lemma 31
.

If
\mathcal{C}=B
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, then
e\leqslant\varepsilon N
with probability at least
1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{4}
holds, if B is predicted as the winner in any district of
D^{A}
, then the maximum possible Margin of Victory of B would be
\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}\cdot\kappa n=\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}
. Thus
e_{1}+e_{2}\leqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot l_{1}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon n}{3}=\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}
. Again since
\mathcal{M}_{3}
holds,
k_{3}\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}\right)l_{1}
. Thus
e_{3}\leqslant\frac{k}{l_{1}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{3\kappa}l_{1}\cdot\kappa n=\frac{\kappa\varepsilon^{2}N}{3}\leqslant\frac{2\varepsilon N}{3}
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
. Hence with probability at least
1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
,
e=e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon N}{3}+\frac{2\varepsilon N}{3}=\varepsilon N
.
∎

Lemma 32
.

If
\mathcal{C}=B
, then
\gamma<\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
and thus
e\geqslant 0>(\gamma-\varepsilon)N
.

Proof.

Suppose
\gamma\geqslant\varepsilon
. Then from Lemma
10
, there exist at least
\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\right)k
districts where A receives at least
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}
fraction of votes. Thus
\nu\geqslant\varepsilon/3\kappa
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{4}
holds, the predicted fraction of votes received by A in each of these districts is more than
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}-\frac{\varepsilon}{3\kappa}=\frac{1}{2}
. Also since
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
hold,
k_{1}\geqslant\left(\nu-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}\right)l_{1}\geqslant\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa}\right)l_{1}
and

and and therefore
k_{1}+k_{2}>\frac{1}{2}l_{1}
. This contradicts the fact that B is the predicted winner in more than half of the sampled districts. Thus with probability at least
1-\frac{3\delta}{8}
,
\gamma<\varepsilon
.
∎

Combining Lemma
29
, Lemma
30
, Lemma
31
and Lemma

, it follows that the estimate returned by
Algorithm

7
lies in the range
[(\gamma-\varepsilon)N,(\gamma+\varepsilon)N]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Theorem 14
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Additive
with sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{6}}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\delta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
for the district-level plurality election.

6.2
Estimating MOV within multiplicative error bounds

We now present a black-box algorithm that, given any algorithm for the
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Additive
problem, returns an estimate of the margin of victory within a multiplicative error of
1\pm\varepsilon
with high probability.

Let
E
be any arbitrary election with
m
candidates and
N
voters. Suppose there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for the
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Additive
problem; thus for any
\varepsilon,\delta>0
,
\text{Pr}(|\mathcal{A}(\varepsilon,\delta)-\gamma N|\leqslant\varepsilon N)\geqslant 1-\delta
. Using
\mathcal{A}
as a black box, we design an algorithm for the
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Multiplicative
problem. Our algorithm is as follows.

Algorithm 11

1:

for

i=1,2,\ldots,\log_{1+\varepsilon}N

do

2:

e_{i}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}},\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}\right)
,
\lambda_{i}\leftarrow\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}/\log(1+\varepsilon)}+1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}

3:

If
e_{i}\geqslant\lambda_{i}N
,
return

e_{i}
.

4:

end

for

5:

return

1
.

We show that with high probability the estimate
e_{i}
would be less than
\lambda_{i}N
, when
i<\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\gamma}\right)
and would exceed
\lambda_{i}N
when
i\geqslant\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\gamma}\right)
. Thus
Algorithm

11
would return for some
i=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\gamma}\right)
. Using this, it can be shown that the estimate returned must lie in the range
[(1-\varepsilon)\gamma N,(1+\varepsilon)\gamma N]
with high probability.

We now formalise the above notion. Let
\psi_{1}=\frac{\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}{\log(1+\varepsilon)}
and
\psi_{2}=\frac{\log\frac{2}{\varepsilon}}{\log(1+\varepsilon)}
. Let
p
be the unique positive integer such that
\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p+1}}\leqslant\gamma<\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p}}
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{i}
denote the event that
\left|\mathcal{A}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}},\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}\right)-\gamma N\right|\leqslant\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}
. Thus
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{K}_{i})\geqslant 1-\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}
. Now we show the following two results.

Lemma 33
.

If
\mathcal{K}_{i}
holds,
e_{i}<\lambda_{i}N
, for
i\leqslant p+\psi_{1}
.

Proof.

For
i\leqslant p+\psi_{1}
, we have
e_{i}\leqslant\gamma N+\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}\leqslant\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p}}+\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}\leqslant\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i-\psi_{1}}}+\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\psi_{1}}+1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}N=\lambda_{i}N
.
∎

Lemma 34
.

If
\mathcal{K}_{i}
holds,
e_{i}\geqslant\lambda_{i}N
, for
i\geqslant p+\psi_{2}
.

Proof.

For
i\geqslant p+\psi_{2}
,
e_{i}\geqslant\gamma N-\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}\geqslant\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p+1}}-\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}\geqslant\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i-\psi_{2}+1}}-\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{\psi_{2}-1}-1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{i}}N\geqslant\lambda_{i}N
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.
∎

Now let
\mathcal{K}=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{p+\psi_{2}}\mathcal{K}_{i}
. Since
\text{Pr}(\overline{\mathcal{K}}_{i})\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}
, by union bound, the probability that at least one of the events
\mathcal{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{p+\psi_{2}}
does not hold is at most
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p+\psi_{2}}\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}\leqslant\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{2^{i}}=\delta
. Thus
\text{Pr}(\mathcal{K})\geqslant 1-\delta
. Now if
\mathcal{K}
holds,
Algorithm

11
returns an
e_{i}
, for
i
lying in the range
\{p+\psi_{1}+1,\ldots,p+\psi_{2}\}
.

Let
\sigma
denote the estimate returned by the algorithm. Then
\sigma\leqslant\gamma N+\frac{N}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p+\psi_{1}+1}}\leqslant\left(1+\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\psi_{1}}}\right)\gamma N=(1+\varepsilon)\gamma N
. Again
\sigma\geqslant\gamma N-\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{p+\psi_{2}}}N\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon)^{\psi_{2}}}\right)\gamma N=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\gamma N\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\gamma N
. We thus have the following result.

Lemma 35
.

Algorithm

11
returns an estimate in the range
[(1-\varepsilon)\gamma N,(1+\varepsilon)\gamma N]
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Combining
Theorem

14
and
Lemma

35
, we have the following result.

Theorem 15
.

There exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,\delta)-
MOV-Multiplicative
with expected sample complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{7}}\frac{1}{\gamma^{6}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\gamma}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)
for the district-level plurality election with 2 candidates when the population of each district is bounded by a constant times the average population of a district, where
\gamma N
is the (unknown) margin of victory of the election.

Proof.

From
Lemma

35
, the estimate returned by
Algorithm

11
lies in the range
[(1-\varepsilon)\gamma N,(1+\varepsilon)\gamma N]
with probability at least
1-\delta
. We therefore need to only bound the sample complexity.

For a particular value of
i
, it is clear that
Algorithm

11
uses at most
\mathcal{O}\left((1+\varepsilon)^{6i}\left(i+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)
samples. Let
p
be as defined as before. Let
\varphi(j)
denote the number of samples collected if
Algorithm

11
halts when
i=p+\psi_{2}+j
. Then
\varphi(j)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p+\psi_{2}+j}\mathcal{O}\left((1+\varepsilon)^{6i}\left(i+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)\leqslant\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+\varepsilon)^{6(p+\psi_{2}+j)}\left(p+\psi_{2}+j+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)
. For
j\geqslant 1
, let
M_{j}
denote the event that
Algorithm

11
halts when
i=p+\psi_{2}+j
and let
M_{0}
denote the event that
Algorithm

11
halts for some
i\leqslant p+\psi_{2}
. Then for
j\geqslant 1
,
\text{Pr}(M_{j})\leqslant\text{Pr(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{alg:alg5} does not halt when }i=p+\psi_{2}+j-1)\leqslant\frac{\delta}{2^{p+\psi_{2}+j-1}}
. Also trivially
\text{Pr}(M_{0})\leqslant 1
. Hence the expected sample complexity is at most
\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}\varphi(j)\text{Pr}(M_{j})\leqslant\varphi(0)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+\varepsilon)^{6(p+\psi_{2}+j)}\left(p+\psi_{2}+j+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{2^{p+\psi_{2}+j-1}}\right)
. Now
\varphi(0)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+\varepsilon)^{6(p+\psi_{2})}\left(p+\psi_{2}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)
, while the second term in the sum is at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+\varepsilon)^{6(p+\psi_{2})}\left(p+\psi_{2}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{2^{p+\psi_{2}}}\right)
. Hence the overall expected sample complexity is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(1+\varepsilon)^{6(p+\psi_{2})}\left(p+\psi_{2}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{7}}\frac{1}{\gamma^{6}}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon\gamma}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^{2}\right)
.
∎

7
Conclusion and Future Work

We have initiated the study of the sample complexity for predicting the winner in a district-based election. We have shown some preliminary results for the problem for some voting rules. We believe that the problem and our results are both practically and theoretically interesting. An important future direction of research is to find the sample-complexity lower bounds for various voting rules. Some of our lower bounds work only for some class of algorithms. Also, extending our algorithm for winner prediction when the margin of victory is not known, to arbitrary number of candidates is an important future direction of research; our algorithm works only for two candidates.
