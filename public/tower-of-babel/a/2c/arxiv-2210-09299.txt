Abstract
. Given a norm ν \nu on ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} , the set of ν \nu -Dirichlet improvable numbers 𝐃𝐈 ν \mathbf{DI}_{\nu} was defined and studied in the papers [ AD , KR1 ] . When ν \nu is the supremum norm, 𝐃𝐈 ν = 𝐁𝐀 ∪ ℚ \mathbf{DI}_{\nu}=\mathbf{BA}\cup{\mathbb{Q}} , where 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{BA} is the set of badly approximable numbers. Each of the sets 𝐃𝐈 ν \mathbf{DI}_{\nu} , like 𝐁𝐀 \mathbf{BA} , is of measure zero and satisfies the winning property of Schmidt. Hence for every norm ν \nu , 𝐁𝐀 ∩ 𝐃𝐈 ν \mathbf{BA}\cap\mathbf{DI}_{\nu} is winning and thus has full Hausdorff dimension. In the present article we prove the following dichotomy phenomenon: either 𝐁𝐀 ⊂ 𝐃𝐈 ν \mathbf{BA}\subset\mathbf{DI}_{\nu} or else 𝐁𝐀 ∖ 𝐃𝐈 ν \mathbf{BA}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu} has full Hausdorff dimension. We give several examples for each of the two cases. The dichotomy is based on whether the critical locus of ν \nu intersects a precompact g t g_{t} -orbit, where { g t } \{g_{t}\} is the one-parameter diagonal subgroup of SL 2 ⁡ ( ℝ ) \operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}) acting on the space X X of unimodular lattices in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Thus the aforementioned dichotomy follows from the following dynamical statement: for a lattice Λ ∈ X \Lambda\in X , either g ℝ ​ Λ g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda is unbounded (and then any precompact g ℝ > 0 g_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}} -orbit must eventually avoid a neighborhood of Λ \Lambda ), or not, in which case the set of lattices in X X whose g ℝ > 0 g_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}} -trajectories are precompact and contain Λ \Lambda in their closure has full Hausdorff dimension.



1.
Introduction

Dirichlet’s theorem (
[
S2
, Theorem 1A]
) states that for every real number
\alpha
and every
T>1
, the set of inequalities

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha q\rangle&\leq T^{-1},\\
\ \ |q|&<T\end{cases}

has a solution
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Here
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the distance of a real number to a nearest integer.
The question of improving Dirichlet’s theorem was initiated by Davenport–Schmidt in
[
DS
]
. A real number
\alpha
is said to be
Dirichlet improvable
, written
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
, if there is a
c<1
such that the system

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\langle\alpha q\rangle&<cT^{-1},\\
\ \ |q|&<T\end{cases}

can be solved in
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
for all sufficiently large
T
.
[
DS
, Theorem 1]
shows that
\mathbf{DI}_{\infty}
is exactly the set
\mathbf{BA}\cup{\mathbb{Q}}
. In fact, this was known as far back as the paper of Morimoto
[
Mo
]
.
We recall that badly approximable numbers are those real numbers whose continued fraction coefficients (partial quotients) are bounded from above.

A normed variant of Dirichlet’s theorem (also referred to as Minkowski’s approximation theorem) was studied in
[
AD
,
KR1
]
. The starting point is the observation that the defining condition in (
1.1
) can be restated as

\Lambda_{\alpha}\cap\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}cT^{-1}&0\\
0&T\end{array}}\right]B_{\infty}(1)\neq\{0\}

where
\Lambda_{\alpha}
denotes the unimodular lattice
\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha\\
0&1\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
and
B_{\infty}(1)
denotes the supremum norm ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
centered at the origin and with radius
1
.

Now, let
X
denote the set of unimodular lattices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, fix a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and define the
critical radius
of
\nu
:

(1.2)

r_{\nu}:=\sup\big\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}:\text{there exists }\Lambda\in X\text{ with }\Lambda\cap B_{\nu}(r)=\{0\}\big\}.

By Minkowski’s convex body theorem,
r_{\nu}
is finite. Let
B_{\nu}(r)
denote the
\nu
-norm ball with radius
r
centered at the origin. We say that a real number
\alpha
is
\nu
-Dirichlet improvable
, written
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
, if there is some
c<1
with

\Lambda_{\alpha}\cap\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}cT^{-1}&0\\
0&T\end{array}}\right]B_{\nu}\left(r_{\nu}\right)\neq\{0\}

for all sufficiently large
T
. When
\nu
is the supremum norm (denoted by the subscript
\infty)
, we have that
r_{\infty}=1
and so recover the definition of Davenport–Schmidt.
When
\nu
is the Euclidean norm (denoted by the subscript
2
), we see that
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{2}
if and only if there is some
c<1
such that

\left(\frac{T\langle\alpha q\rangle}{c}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{q}{T}\right)^{2}<\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}

is solvable in
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
for all sufficiently large
T
.
The normalizing constant
2/\sqrt{3}
is equal to the square of the critical radius of the Euclidean norm, see e.g.
[
KRS
]
for a discussion and references.

Consider the following preliminary properties:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
KR1
]
, Theorems 3.1 and 1.3)
.

For each norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the set
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
is of measure zero but winning in the sense of Schmidt.
In particular,
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
has full Hausdorff dimension.

See
[
S1
]
and a paragraph before Proposition
2.2
for a discussion of Schmidt games and winning.
The fact that
\mathbf{BA}
has full Hausdorff dimension goes back to Jarnik in 1928 (see
[
BRV
, Theorem 7.1]
for an elementary proof).
Thus, natural questions that come up in the study of Theorem
1.1
are

(a)

Do there exist norms for which
\mathbf{BA}\not\subset\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
?

(b)

If yes, can one characterize the norms for which
\mathbf{BA}\not\subset\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
?

The existence of norms which satisfy the above non-containment is a consequence of the equality

{\mathbb{Q}}=\bigcap_{\nu}\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}

(see
[
KR2
, Theorem 1.7]
).
In the present paper we address Question (b) by giving a convenient dynamical condition on
\nu
which completely determines whether or not
\mathbf{BA}
is a subset of
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
.
In fact, our main results are completely dynamical in nature and this paper is a twofold study of limit points of bounded diagonal orbits in
X
and criteria to detect whether certain submanifolds intersect precompact orbits.

We briefly explain how dynamics comes into play; the idea essentially comes from the work of Davenport and Schmidt
[
DS
]
.
Each norm
\nu
gives rise to the following compact subset of
X
:

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}:=\big\{\Lambda\in X:\Lambda\cap B_{\nu}\left(r_{\nu}\right)=\{0\}\big\}.

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
is referred to as the
critical locus
of the norm.
It follows from the definition of
r_{\nu}
and from Mahler’s compactness criterion that
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
is a non-empty compact subset of
X
. Equivalently, lattices in
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
give the densest lattice packings of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by the domain
B_{\nu}\left(\frac{r_{\nu}}{2}\right)
.
We have the following notation for present and future use:

(1.3)

{g_{t}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{t}&0\\
0&e^{-t}\end{array}}\right],\text{ where }t\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \text{ and }g_{E}:=\left\{g_{t}:t\in E\right\}\text{ for a subset
$E$ of ${\mathbb{R}}$}.}

Proposition 1.2

(
[
D
]
, Theorem 2.20 and
[
KR1
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

We have the following two equivalences:

(i)

A real number
\alpha
belongs to
\mathbf{BA}
if and only if

g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\Lambda_{\alpha}\text{ is precompact in }X.

(ii)

Given a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we have that
\alpha\in\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
if and only if
there exists
t_{0}>0
and a neighborhood
U\supset\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
such that

g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>t_{0}}}\Lambda_{\alpha}\cap U=\varnothing.

With this context, we state our main theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix a lattice
\Lambda\in X
. If
g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda
is precompact in
X
, then the set

\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}:=\left\{\alpha\in\mathbf{BA}:\Lambda\in\overline{g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\Lambda_{\alpha}}\right\}

has Hausdorff dimension
1
. Conversely, if
g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda
is not precompact in
X
, then for every
\alpha\in\mathbf{BA}
there exists
t_{0}>0
and a neighbourhood
U\supset\Lambda
such that

g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>t_{0}}}\Lambda_{\alpha}\cap U=\varnothing.

Remark 1.4
.

The converse is elementary and left to the reader as an exercise, see
[
KR2
, Proposition 4.1]
. Thus the main content of the theorem is the first part.
Its proof below can be easily modified to show that the intersection of
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
with any open interval has Hausdorff dimension
1
. However,
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
is not winning as follows from
[
AGK
, Theorem 2.8]
. (This last theorem was used to prove Theorem
1.1
.)

As a corollary of Proposition
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
, we have our dichotomy phenomenon:

Theorem 1.5
.

Fix a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. If
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
contains a lattice
\Lambda
such that

g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda
is precompact in
X
,
then
\mathbf{BA}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
has full Hausdorff dimension.
Conversely, if no such lattice exists, then
\mathbf{BA}\subset\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
.

Remark 1.6
.

The converse has already been proved in the more general multidimensional setting of weighted approximation of systems of linear forms, see
[
KR2
, Proposition 4.1]
.

It can be shown that the Euclidean critical locus
\mathcal{L}_{2}
intersects a precompact
{\mathbb{R}}
-orbit (in fact, quite a few of them). Moreover, there is a very large class of norms which also satisfy this condition. Let us say that a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is

irreducible
if whenever
\eta\neq\nu
is a norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with
\eta({\bf v})\geq\nu({\bf v})
for any
{\bf v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we have
r_{\eta}>r_{\nu}
.
Mahler introduced this distinguished set of norms (or rather, in his terminology, of convex bounded symmetric domains) in
[
Ma1
]
, and they turned out to be central to proving Theorem
1.1
among other results.
Examples of irreducible norms are those whose unit balls are ellipses, parallelograms and Reinhardt’s curvilinear octagon (see
[
KRS
, Examples 2.2, 2.3, 3.3]
).
The critical locus
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}

of an irreducible norm
\nu

which does not come from a parallelogram is necessarily a one-dimensional
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-submanifold of
X
(see
[
Ma2
, Theorem 3]
). Considerations in the tangent bundle of
X
then allow us to conclude that
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
must intersect some precompact orbit
g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda
(see Proposition
2.5
below). Thus, we obtain as a corollary:

Theorem 1.7
.

If
\nu
is an irreducible norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose unit ball is not a parallelogram, then
\mathbf{BA}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
has full Hausdorff dimension. In particular,
\mathbf{BA}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{2}
has full Hausdorff dimension.

We also mention that, since the critical locus of a hexagonal norm is a singleton (see
[
KRS
, Example 2.4]
), one can easily construct norms
\nu
which are not irreducible and for which
\mathbf{BA}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu}
has full dimension. For example, if
\nu
is a norm with
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}=\{\Lambda_{0}\}
, one can choose an element
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
which makes
g_{\mathbb{R}}g\Lambda_{0}
precompact, and then notice that
\mathcal{L}_{\nu\circ g^{-1}}=g\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
.

We organize the paper as follows.
In §
2
we explore the structure of
X
more carefully to establish, under the assumption that
\nu
is a non-parallelogram irreducible norm, the existence of lattices in
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
with precompact
{\mathbb{R}}
-orbits. With this out of the way, all that remains is to prove Theorem
1.3
in the case when the mentioned lattice
\Lambda
has a precompact
{\mathbb{R}}
-orbit. To prove it we use a symbolic representation of the diagonal flow. In §
3
we study a specific Poincare section for
X
which arises in the exposition of
[
C
]
.
From this it becomes apparent that the set
\mathcal{S}_{\Lambda}
from Theorem
1.3
consists of badly approximable numbers having certain predetermined blocks of coefficients appearing in their continued fraction expansion. We then apply a dimension estimate from
[
U
]
in §
4
to show this set has full Hausdorff dimension. Some remarks on possible generalizations of the set-up of this paper are made in the last section.

2.
Critical loci which intersect precompact orbits

We denote
G:=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\Gamma:=\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Identify
X
with
G/\Gamma
via the map
g\mapsto g{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
In addition to the notation of (
1.3
),
for
x,y,\in{\mathbb{R}}
denote

u_{x}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\\
0&1\end{array}}\right]\text{ and }v_{y}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
y&1\end{array}}\right].

We next have the stability of precompact orbits under the action of certain subgroups of
G
:

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
\Lambda\in X
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that the maps

(2.1)

(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{3}\to X;\ (x,t,y)\mapsto v_{y}g_{t}u_{x}\Lambda\ \text{ and }\ (x,t,y)\mapsto u_{x}g_{t}v_{y}\Lambda

are diffeomorphisms.
Moreover, for any fixed
(x,t,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, we have the equivalences:

(2.2)

g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\left(v_{y}g_{t}u_{x}\Lambda\right)\text{ is precompact in }X\iff g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\left(u_{x}\Lambda\right)\text{ is precompact in }X

and

(2.3)

g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{<0}}\left(u_{x}g_{t}v_{y}\Lambda\right)\text{ is precompact in }X\iff g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{<0}}\left(v_{y}\Lambda\right)\text{ is precompact in }X.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from
[
V
, Theorem 2.10.1]
.
For the remaining part of the proposition, note that for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}

(2.4)

{g_{s}v_{y}g_{t}u_{x}\Lambda=v_{ye^{-2s}}g_{s+t}u_{x}\Lambda\ \text{ and }\ g_{s}u_{x}g_{t}v_{y}\Lambda=u_{xe^{2s}}g_{s+t}v_{y}\Lambda.}

Alternatively, see
[
D
, Proposition 2.12]
.
∎

Our main tool in establishing the existence of precompact orbits passing through critical loci is the following proposition which constructs a winning set of precompact orbits along the
u_{x}
and
v_{y}
directions about any fixed lattice. It is a well known application of Schmidt’s results in
[
S1
]
; we give an elementary proof here for convenience (see also
[
KW
, Theorem 3.7]
and
[
Mc
, Theorem 1.3]
).

For the benefit of the reader we recall the rules of Schmidt’s game introduced in
[
S1
]
. It
involves two parameters
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
and is played by two players Alice and Bob on a complete metric space (which we shall take to be the set of real numbers) with a target set
S
. Bob starts the game by choosing a closed ball
B_{0}=B(x_{0},r_{0})
in
{\mathbb{R}}
with center
x_{0}
and radius
r_{0}
. After Bob chooses a closed ball
B_{i}=B(x_{i},{r}_{i})
, Alice chooses
A_{i}=B(x_{i}^{\prime},{r}_{i}^{\prime})\subset B_{i}
with
{r}^{\prime}_{i}=\alpha{r}_{i}
,
and then Bob chooses
B_{i+1}=B(x_{i+1},{r}_{i+1})\subset A_{i}
with
{r}_{i+1}=\beta{r}^{\prime}_{i}
,
etc. Alice wins the game if the unique point
\bigcap_{i=0}^{\infty}A_{i}=\bigcap_{i=0}^{\infty}B_{i}
belongs to
S
, and Bob wins otherwise. The set
S
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-winning
if Alice has a winning strategy,
and is
winning
if it is
(\alpha,\beta)
-winning for some
\alpha>0
and all
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
\Lambda\in X
,
the sets

\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}:g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}u_{x}\Lambda\text{ is precompact in }X\right\}\ \text{ and }\ \left\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}:g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{<0}}v_{y}\Lambda\text{ is precompact in }X\right\}

are winning.

Proof.

Recall that we are given
\Lambda\in X
. Let us first
consider the case when

(2.5)

\Lambda=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}a&0\\
c&a^{-1}\end{array}}\right]\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&b\\
0&1\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}

for some
a\in{\mathbb{R}}\smallsetminus\{0\}
and
b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Take
\alpha<1/2
. After making the first move Alice can ensure that all
x
in the ball of her choice satisfy
a+cx\neq 0
.
For
\phi(x):=a^{-1}x(a+cx)^{-1}
, we have

u_{x}\Lambda=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}a+cx&0\\
c&(a+cx)^{-1}\end{array}}\right]\ \left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\phi(x)+b\\
0&1\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}.

Using the equivalence of Proposition
2.1
and the characterization of Proposition
1.2
(i), we see that

\phi(x)+b\in\mathbf{BA}{\ \Longleftrightarrow\ }g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}u_{x}\Lambda\text{ is precompact in }X.

Since
\phi^{-1}
is Lipschitz, the winning property of
\mathbf{BA}
and
[
S1
, Theorem 1]
establishes the winning property for the first set in the proposition.

Next we consider the case when
\Lambda
cannot be written in the form (
2.5
). This happens if and only if
\Lambda=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&a\\
b&c\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
for some
a,b,c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. A consequence is that, for any
x\neq 0
,
u_{x}\Lambda
can be written in the form (
2.5
); thus one can replace
\Lambda
with
u_{x}\Lambda
and reduce the problem to the previously considered case.
Hence in both cases we have the winning property for the first set in the proposition; the second set, in view of an elementary observation that

(2.6)

{g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}u_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\text{ is precompact in }X\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \text{so is }g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{<0}}v_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{2},}

can be handled similarly.
∎

We have the notation for the following subgroups of
G
:

F:=\{g_{t}:t\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\ H^{+}:=\{u_{x}:x\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\ H^{-}:=\{v_{y}:y\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

Remark 2.3
.

We use the notation
T_{m}(M)
to denote the tangent space of a (
\mathcal{C}^{1}
or smoother) manifold
M
at a point
m
. We also use the prefix
d
to indicate the derivative of a smooth map.

T_{z}(Fz)
is understood to be the image, in
T_{z}(X)
, of the tangent space
T_{e}(F)
via the derivative of the map
f\mapsto fz
. The same interpretation is used for the other subgroups of
G
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
Z\subset X
be a one-dimensional compact
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-submanifold satisfying, for some
z\in Z
,

(2.7)

T_{z}(X)=T_{z}(Z)+T_{z}(Fz)+T_{z}(H^{+}z)

and

(2.8)

T_{z}(X)=T_{z}(Z)+T_{z}(Fz)+T_{z}(H^{-}z).

Then
Z
contains a lattice
\Lambda
for which

g_{\mathbb{R}}\Lambda
is precompact in
X
.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Let

\phi:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to X

be a local parameterization of
Z
with
\phi(0)=z
.
Using the first set of local coordinates at
z
in (
2.1
), and the tangent space decomposition of (
2.8
), we can write

\phi(s)=v_{y(s)}g_{t(s)}u_{x(s)}z

where
x(\cdot)
,
{t}(\cdot)
,
y(\cdot)
are now
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functions and
x^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
.
Thus the inverse function
x^{-1}
exists in a neigborhood of
0
and is Lipschitz.
Using Proposition
2.2
,
[
S1
, Theorem 1]
and the equivalence (
2.2
), we see that

\left\{s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon):g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}}\phi(s)\text{ is precompact in }X\right\}

is winning.
An entirely analogous argument using (
2.7
) shows that

\left\{s\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon):g_{{\mathbb{R}}_{<0}}\phi(s)\text{ is precompact in }X\right\}

is winning. Since the intersection of winning sets is also winning
[
S1
, Theorem 2]
, we arrive at the desired result.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

If
\nu
is an irreducible norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose unit ball is not a parallelogram, then
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
is a one-dimensional compact
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-submanifold of
X
satisfying conditions (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) at every point
z\in\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
must intersect some precompact
{\mathbb{R}}
-orbit.

Proof.

The first condition (
2.7
) is proved in
[
KR1
, Theorem 3.11]
.
For the second condition, consider the linear automorphism of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
given by

{\bf v}\mapsto p{\bf v},\text{ where }p:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}}\right].

Of course, it also induces a map on subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which gives a diffeomorphism of
X

\Lambda\mapsto p\Lambda.

On the level of critical loci and norms, we have that

p\mathcal{L}_{\nu}=\mathcal{L}_{\nu\circ p}.

Moreover, if
\nu
is irreducible with unit ball not a parallelogram, then the same holds true for
\nu\circ p
.
(Indeed, the irreducibility of
\nu\circ p
follows from the definition after noting that
r_{\nu\circ p}=r_{\nu}
; on the other hand, it is clear that the image of a parallelogram under a linear transformation remains a parallelogram.)
On the level of tangent spaces we have that, for
z\in X
,

(dp)T_{z}(Fz)=T_{pz}(Fpz),\ (dp)T_{z}(H^{+}z)=T_{pz}(H^{-}pz),\ (dp)T_{z}(H^{-}z)=T_{pz}(H^{+}pz).

The upshot now is that since
\mathcal{L}_{\nu\circ p}
satisfies (
2.7
) at
pz\in p\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\nu}
satisfies (
2.8
) at
z
.
This shows that Proposition
2.4
can be applied, giving the final assertion in the present proposition.
∎

3.
Minimal vectors and a Poincare section for
X

We study a well known correspondence between diagonal orbits in
X
and orbits in an invertible extension of the Gauss map.
We follow the exposition of
[
C
, §3.1]
and presume the reader is familiar with the theory of continued fractions at the level of
[
K
]
.

If
a,b
are real numbers, we define the rectangle
R(a,b)
to be

R(a,b):=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:|x_{1}|\leq a\text{ and }|x_{2}|\leq b\right\}.

Definition 3.1
.

(Minimal vectors)
Let
\Lambda\in X
. A vector
{\bf r}=(r_{1},r_{2})\in\Lambda
is called a

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\{[0,1]\}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{1}:=\left\{\overline{\{\alpha\in[0,1]:a_{1}(\alpha)=2\}}\right\}{=\left\{[1/3,1/2]\right\}}.

Now, assuming we have defined
\mathcal{E}_{m}
as a collection of closed intervals
\left\{\overline{I}\right\}
where each
I
is of the form (

) (with

), we define the family

as follows:
\mathcal{E}_{m+1}:=

(4.7)

\begin{cases}\bigcup\limits_{I\in\mathcal{E}_{m}}\bigcup\limits_{i\leq L}\left\{\overline{\{\alpha\in I:a_{m+1}(\alpha)=i\}}\right\}&\text{if for all }k,\ m+1\notin\{m_{k}-(k-1),\dots,m_{k}+k\},\\

The cylindrical intervals of (

) are either disjoint or satisfy a containment relation.
Thus each

which belong to some

can intersect in at most a point. The diameter of these sets converge to

as

. Moreover for each

Thus the conditions for Theorem
4.3
are satisfied.
We also have that

\mathcal{S}\big(L,(m_{k})\big)=\bigcap_{m}\cup\mathcal{E}_{m}.

We now turn to computing
\Theta_{m}
. Assume
\mathcal{E}_{m+1}
is defined according to the first case in equation (
4.7
). If
I\in\mathcal{E}_{m}
, then, using Proposition
4.1
, we can wirte

\begin{split}|\cup\mathcal{E}_{m+1}\cap I|&=\left|\left\{\alpha\in I:a_{m+1}(\alpha)\leq L\right\}\right|\\
&=\left|\left\{\alpha\in I:z_{m}(\alpha)\geq(L+1)^{-1}\right\}\right|\\

Here,

Now if

5.
Further questions on Dirichlet improvability and precompact orbits

Some amusing problems on
\nu
-Dirichlet numbers which we omitted are:

(c)

In the case of the Euclidean norm, does
\mathbf{DI}_{2}\smallsetminus\mathbf{BA}
have full Hausdorff dimension?

(d)

In general, for two norms
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, what can be said about
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu_{1}}\smallsetminus\mathbf{DI}_{\nu_{2}}
?

We expect (c) to have an affirmative answer, although constructing the required orbits in
X
will require a more delicate inductive procedure than the one produced here.

Dirichlet-improvability can be studied in a variety of different settings. And each setting relates, via the Dani correspondence, to diagonal orbits on a homogeneous space avoiding a critical locus.
The setting of diagonal flows on
\operatorname{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{R}})/\operatorname{SL}_{d}({\mathbb{Z}})
, described in
[
KR2
]
, is as follows. Fix positive integers
m,n
with
m+n=d
and a set of weights

{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}=({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}\text{ with }\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}=1.

Fix also a norm
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and define the critical radius
r_{\nu}
in analogy with (
1.2
). A matrix
A\in M_{m,n}({\mathbb{R}})
is said to be
(\nu,{\boldsymbol{\omega}})
-Dirichlet improvable (written
A\in\mathbf{DI}_{\nu,{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
) if there is a constant
c<1
such that

\Lambda_{A}\cap\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(ct^{-1}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}&0\\
0&(t)^{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\end{array}}\right]B_{\nu}(r_{\nu})\neq\{0\}

for all
t
sufficiently large.
Here, we define a positive real number
x
raised to a vector power
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
as the
k\times k
matrix

(x)^{\bf a}:=\operatorname{diag}(x^{a_{1}},\dots,x^{a_{k}}),

and use the notation

\Lambda_{A}:=\left[{\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{m}&A\\
0&I_{n}\end{array}}\right]{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

We also have the set of
weighted badly approximable
matrices:

\mathbf{BA}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=\left\{\text{$A\in M_{m,n}$}({\mathbb{R}}):\inf_{{\bf p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m},\,{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\smallsetminus\{{\bf 0}\}}\|A{\bf q}-{\bf p}\|_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|{\bf q}\|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}>0\right\}

where we have used the quasi-norms

\|{\bf x}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}:=\max_{i}|x_{i}|^{1/\alpha_{i}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\|{\bf y}\|_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}:=\max_{j}|y_{j}|^{1/\beta_{j}}.

Now one can naturally ask for a comparison study between
\mathbf{DI}_{\nu,{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
and
\mathbf{BA}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
We predict that the same dichotomy phenomenon as in Theorem
1.5
holds for this case.
(See
[
KR2
]
for some preliminary theorems about these sets.)

An approach to this problem via symbolic dynamics is currently unavailable. However, one can try and modify the construction (via equidistibution of expanding horocycles) in
[
KM
]
to force the resulting precompact orbits to have prescribed limit points. (Of course, it is necessary that these limit points themselves have precompact
{\mathbb{R}}
-orbits.)
We mention that, although this technique via equidistribution was available to us, we chose to use continued fractions in the case of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{R}})/\operatorname{SL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
for the simplicity of their heuristics and as an exercise in understanding Poincare sections.
