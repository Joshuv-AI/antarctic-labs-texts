Existence of global symmetries of divergence-free fields with first integrals
David Perrella, Nathan Duignan, David Pfefferlé

Abstract
The relationship between symmetry fields and first integrals of divergence-free vector fields is explored in three dimensions in light of its relevance to plasma physics and magnetic confinement fusion. A Noether-type Theorem is known: for each such symmetry, there corresponds a first integral. The extent to which the converse is true is investigated. In doing so, a reformulation of this Noether-type Theorem is found for which the converse holds on what is called the toroidal region. Some consequences of the methods presented are quick proofs of the existence of flux coordinates for magnetic fields in high generality; without needing to assume a symmetry such as in the cases of magneto-hydrostatics (MHS) or quasi-symmetry.



I
Introduction

There is a growing interest in divergence-free vector fields
B
that admit either a symmetry or a first integral. A symmetry
u
is a divergence-free vector field and a first integral
\psi
is a function which respectively satisfy

[u,B]=0,\qquad B\cdot\nabla\psi=0.

Here
[u,B]
is the Lie-bracket of vector fields
u
and
B
. In the context of plasma physics, one considers magneto-hydrostatics (MHS) under which
B
satisfies

J\times B=\nabla p,\qquad J=\nabla\times B

where
p
is the pressure and
J
is the current density. In this case,
J
is a symmetry of
B
and
p
is a first integral of both
B
and
J
. Another class of magnetic fields, called
quasi-symmetric
, arise when there exists a divergence-free vector field
u
which possesses
B
as a symmetry and
\|B\|^{2}
as a first integral
Burby et al. 2020
;
Rodríguez et al. 2020
. In magnetic confinement fusion devices, transport of heat and particles along magnetic field-lines is generally several orders of magnitude faster than across. Promoting first integrals is a way of guaranteeing temperature and density gradients because then the field-lines lie on lower-dimensional manifolds. Without substantial gradients, the core of a fusion device would not be hot enough to sustain fusion reactions and the outer region cold enough for the device’s walls to resist damage.

For any
B
admitting both a symmetry and an integral, the field is integrable in the broad sense
Burby et al. 2021
as defined in
Bogoyavlenskij 1998
. On the other hand, divergence-free fields generally admit no symmetries nor integrals and are chaotic
Arnold 1965
;
Cardona et al. 2021
.

Between the cases of chaos and integrability lies magnetic fields
B
which admit either an integral or a symmetry, but a priori not both. For example, if one has a vacuum magnetic field, which is a divergence-free vector field
B
satisfying
\nabla\times B=0
, and it is assumed to have nested flux surfaces, it is not immediately clear whether or not
B
also possesses a symmetry.

It has been established
Haller and Mezic 1998
;
Burby et al. 2021
that a divergence-free vector field
B
in 3D admits a Noether theorem: loosely, this states that for each symmetry field of
B
, there corresponds a first integral of
B
. However, the converse is false: there exists
B
which has a first integral but no corresponding symmetry (see for instance
Example

II.12
). Fortunately, a partial converse is available. If
B
is non-vanishing and has a first integral
\psi
, as indicated in
Borisov and Mamaev 2008
, an extension of an argument due to Kolmogorov
Sinai 1976
shows that in a neighbourhood
U
of a compact, regular level-surface of
\psi
, there exists coordinates
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\psi):U\to\mathbb{T}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
and a smooth positive function
f>0
such that

B/f=a(\psi)\partial_{\theta_{1}}+b(\psi)\partial_{\theta_{2}}.

Thus, the vector field
u=b(\psi)\partial_{\theta_{1}}-a(\psi)\partial_{\theta_{2}}
on
U
, for example, is a symmetry for the rescaled field
B/f
. In this case,
B
is said to be semi-rectified in this neighborhood
U

Perrella et al. 2022
. However, this converse is local to a given regular level surface of
\psi
.

The main result of this paper is a
converse conformal Noether theorem for divergence-free vector fields
; while
B
may not admit a symmetry, a rescaling of
B
does. We call such a symmetry a
conformal symmetry
of
B
. The proof is independent of Kolmogorov’s original ergodic methods and is more global than the above construction. The result takes place on a 3-manifold
M
with (possibly empty) boundary and is centred around flux systems.

Definition I.1
.

A
flux system
is a triple
(B,\nu,\mu)
where
B
is a vector field on
M
,
\nu
is a closed 1-form on
M
, and
\mu
is a volume form on
M
, such that

\nu(B)=0,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{B}\mu=0,

where
\mathcal{L}_{B}
denotes the Lie derivative with respect to
B
.
We call
(B,\nu,\mu)
a
tangential flux system
if
B
is tangent to
\partial M
and the kernel of
\nu
contains all vectors tangent to
\partial M
.

For most applications
\nu=d\psi
will be exact so that
\psi
will be a first integral of
B
. This occurs automatically if the first cohomology of
M
is trivial,
H^{1}(M)=0
. Our main result is split into two parts. The first part highlights the role played by
adapted 1-forms
.

Definition I.2
.

A 1-form
\eta\in\Omega^{1}(M)
is said to be
adapted to a flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
if

\eta(B)>0,\qquad d\eta\wedge\nu=0.

Clearly only non-vanishing
B
could admit such a 1-form. The first part of the main result is stated as follows.

Theorem I.3
.

Let
(B,\nu,\mu)
be a flux system on
M
. Assume that there exists a 1-form
\eta
adapted to the flux system. Then, the vector field
u
satisfying

\iota_{u}\mu=\frac{\nu\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}

is the unique vector field
u
such that

\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu,\qquad\eta(u)=0

and the vector fields
u
and rescaling
{\tilde{B}}=B/\eta(B)

1.

are linearly independent at
x\in M
whenever
\nu|_{x}\neq 0
,

2.

satisfy
\nu({\tilde{B}})=0=\nu(u)
,

3.

commute
[u,{\tilde{B}}]=0
,

4.

and preserve the volume form
{\tilde{\mu}}:=\eta(B)\mu
.

Theorem

I.3
is constructive and global. It shows that, given a flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
, one can use the 1-form
\eta
, if it exists, to uniquely generate a vector field
u
which is a symmetry for the rescaled field
{\tilde{B}}
. It will also be shown how adapted 1-forms relate to the theory in
Burby et al. 2021
, which in turn can be used to prove
Theorem

I.3
from a more abstract perspective.

The second part addresses an outstanding question left from the first: for a given flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
with
B
having no zeroes and
\nu
having nowhere dense critical points, does an adapted 1-form always exist? We provide a partial answer to this question: the semi-global existence of an adapted 1-form. By semi-global, we mean in the sense of
Llibre and Peralta-Salas 2012
, that it holds on an open subset of
M
determined by the flux system. We call this subset the toroidal region (defined in
Section

III
). In essence, the toroidal region is the largest subset of
M
consisting of invariant tori of
B
and for which
B
is non-vanishing, together with periodic orbits of
B
neighboured by the tori. In the context of magnetic fields, these periodic orbits are called

. The following example illustrates the toroidal region.

Example I.4
.

Consider the manifold
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with
x,y,t
the respective coordinate projections of each factor. Define the flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
where

B=y\partial_{x}+(2x-4x^{3})\partial_{y}+\partial_{t},\qquad\nu=d\psi,\qquad\mu=dx\wedge dy\wedge dt,

and
\psi=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}-x^{2}(1-x^{2})
. In this case, the toroidal region
\mathcal{T}=M\backslash\{\psi=0\}
has three connected components divided by
\{\psi=0\}
as depicted in
Fig.

1
.

Figure 1:
A constant-
t
Poincaré section of
B
. The toroidal region is everything except for
\{\psi=0\}
which intersects the Poincaré section in a figure eight (dashed).

The second part is stated as follows.

Theorem I.5
.

Let
(B,\nu,\mu)
be a tangential flux system on
M
. Then, the toroidal region
\mathcal{T}
is an open subset of
M
for which the flux system restricted to
\mathcal{T}
satisfies the assumptions, and therefore the conclusion, of
Theorem

I.3
.

In fact, motivated by the work of
Peralta-Salas et al. 2021
;
Kuperberg 1996
we exhibit an example (
Example

IV.3
) flux system
(B,d\psi,\mu)
on a solid torus for which an adapted 1-form exists on all level sets of
\psi
except the unique level set contained in the complement of the toroidal region. Interestingly, this flux system still possesses a symmetry and

is in fact a torus.

The techniques used to prove
Theorem

I.5
also bring about the following classification of the types of topologies which can occur for the toroidal region. Some possible toroidal domains are given in

.

Proposition I.6
.

Let
C
be a connected component of
\mathcal{T}
considered as an open submanifold of

with boundary and consider the manifold interior
\interior C=C\backslash\partial C
.

1.

If
\interior C
is compact, then
C=\interior C
does not have boundary nor contain axes.

2.

Otherwise,

\mathbb{T}^{2}\times I
where

(where

.

II
The Conformal Noether theorem given an adapted 1-form

The main purpose of this section is to prove
Theorem

I.3
. The remaining material puts this theorem into its context as a (partial) converse to a conformal Noether Theorem. In
Section

II.1
, the (forward) conformal Noether Theorem is presented and an explicit counterexample to the (non-conformal) converse Noether Theorem is provided. This is then related to
Theorem

I.3
as a conformal Noether Theorem. In
Section

II.2
, an elementary proof of
Theorem

I.3
is given. An alternative proof is then given in
Section

II.3
by contextualising the theorem in the theory of presymplectic geometry. From this more abstract perspective, the theorem can be proved using a coisotropic embedding of the manifold
M
into a symplectic manifold
\tilde{M}
. By revealing the connection to symplectic geometry, this version of the proof of
Theorem

I.3
gives insight into why one should expect the conformal Noether theorem to hold. Lastly, as
Theorem

I.3
relies on the existence of an adapted 1-form,
Section

II.5
provides contexts in which adapted 1-forms are guaranteed to exist globally.

Throughout this section, we will make extensive use of the formulae
Lee 2012

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{X}\alpha

\displaystyle=d\iota_{X}\alpha+\iota_{X}d\alpha,

\displaystyle\iota_{[X,Y]}\alpha

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{X}\iota_{Y}\alpha-\iota_{Y}\mathcal{L}_{X}\alpha,

for any vector fields
X,Y
and any
k
-form
\alpha
on
M
. In particular, if
B
is a divergence-free vector field with respect to a volume form
\mu
,
f
is a positive function and
u
is a vector field, writing
{\tilde{B}}=B/f
and
{\tilde{\mu}}=f\mu
, one has
\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}{\tilde{\mu}}=\mathcal{L}_{B}\mu=0
and therefore

\iota_{[u,{\tilde{B}}]}{\tilde{\mu}}=\mathcal{L}_{u}\iota_{\tilde{B}}{\tilde{\mu}}-\iota_{\tilde{B}}\mathcal{L}_{u}{\tilde{\mu}}=d\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu-\iota_{\tilde{B}}\mathcal{L}_{u}{\tilde{\mu}}=d\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu-(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u)\iota_{B}\mu

(1)

where
\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u
is the divergence of
u
with respect to
{\tilde{\mu}}
:
(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u){\tilde{\mu}}=\mathcal{L}_{u}{\tilde{\mu}}
.

II.1
The Conformal Noether theorem

The forward direction of the conformal Noether theorem for divergence-free vector fields is the same observation as that of
(
Haller and Mezic 1998
, Thm. 3.1)
but applied to Equation (
1
), which allows functions
f\neq 1
.

Theorem II.1
.

Let
B
be a vector field on a 3-manifold
M
with boundary and assume it is divergence-free with respect to
\mu
. Suppose there exists a vector field
u
on
M
and a positive function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
[u,B/f]=0
and
u
is divergence-free with respect to
{\tilde{\mu}}=f\mu
. Then the 1-form
\nu:=\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu
makes
(B,\nu,\mu)
a flux system.

Proof.

Equation (
1
) reads

\iota_{[u,{\tilde{B}}]}{\tilde{\mu}}=d\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu-(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u)\iota_{B}\mu

where
{\tilde{B}}=B/f
. In particular, from
[u,{\tilde{B}}]=0
and
\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u=0
, we get that
d\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=0
. Hence,
\nu=\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu
is closed. Moreover,
\nu(B)=0
(and also
\nu(u)=0
) making
(B,\nu,\mu)
a flux system.
∎

The
u
in
Theorem

II.1
is what we call a conformal symmetry of
B
. Ideally, a converse to
Theorem

II.1
would read as follows.

Converse Conformal Noether “Theorem”:
Given a flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
, there exists a function
f
and a vector field
u
which is divergence-free with respect to
f\mu
such that
\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
. Consequently,
u
is a conformal symmetry satisfying
[u,B/f]=0
and
\nu(u)=0
.

What
Theorem

I.3
says in relation to this Converse Conformal Noether“Theorem” is that this “Theorem” is true under the additional assumption that a 1-form adapted to
(B,\nu,\mu)
exists. However, the naive converse Noether “Theorem” is false: that is, the above “Theorem” is false if one requires that
f=1
. This is seen in
Example

II.12
in
Section

II.4
.

II.2
An elementary proof of Thm.
I.3

Here we give a constructive and elementary proof of
Theorem

I.3
. To do so, we will use the following lemma. Let
M
be a 3-manifold with (possibly empty) boundary.

Lemma II.2
.

Suppose
B
is divergence-free with respect to the volume form
\mu
,
\eta
a 1-form such that
\eta(B)>0
, and
\nu
a closed 1-form. Then
(B,\nu,\mu)
is a flux system if and only if there exists a unique vector field
u
such that
\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
and
\eta(u)=0
, in which case,
u
satisfies

\iota_{u}\mu=\frac{\nu\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma is similar to that of
(
Burby et al. 2021
, Lemma 3.4)
. One direction is clear; if
\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
then
0=\iota_{B}\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\iota_{B}\nu
regardless of whether
\eta(u)=0
. Hence,
(B,\nu,\mu)
is a flux system.

To prove the other direction, it suffices to show that the linear map
\hat{b}:\Gamma(TM)\to\Omega^{1}(M)
from vector fields to 1-forms defined by

\hat{b}(X)=\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta

is a bundle isomorphism with inverse
\hat{b}^{-1}:\Omega^{1}(M)\to\Gamma(TM)
defined by

\iota_{\hat{b}^{-1}(\alpha)}\mu=\frac{\alpha\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}+\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)^{2}}\iota_{B}\mu

where products (interior or exterior) and evaluations (of k-forms) are interpreted are occurring at the according fibres of
TM
and
T^{*}M
. Indeed, with this fact, if
u^{\prime}
is a vector field satisfying
\iota_{u^{\prime}}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
and
\eta(u^{\prime})=0
, then

\hat{b}(u^{\prime})=\iota_{u^{\prime}}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(u^{\prime})\eta=\nu.

On the other hand, the vector field
u=\hat{b}^{-1}(\nu)
satisfies
\nu=\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(u)\eta
and evaluating this equation on
B
shows that
\eta(u)=0
because
\eta(B)>0
.

Now, to establish the isomorphism, let
X\in\Gamma(TM)
and
\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(M)
. Then, because
\eta(B)>0
, the following are equivalent.

\displaystyle\hat{b}(X)=\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta=\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow(\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta)(B)=\alpha(B)\text{ and }(\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta)\wedge\eta=\alpha\wedge\eta.

On the other hand,

(\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta)(B)=\eta(X)\eta(B)

and

\displaystyle(\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu+\eta(X)\eta)\wedge\eta

\displaystyle=\iota_{X}\iota_{B}\mu\wedge\eta

\displaystyle=\iota_{X}(\iota_{B}\mu\wedge\eta)-\eta(X)\iota_{B}\mu

\displaystyle=\eta(B)\iota_{X}\mu-\eta(X)\iota_{B}\mu.

Hence, we have the additional equivalent statements.

\displaystyle\hat{b}(X)=\alpha

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\eta(X)\eta(B)=\alpha(B)\text{ and }\eta(B)\iota_{X}\mu-\eta(X)\iota_{B}\mu=\alpha\wedge\eta

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\eta(X)=\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)}\text{ and }\iota_{X}\mu=\frac{\alpha\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}+\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)^{2}}\iota_{B}\mu.

Now, observe that

\iota_{X}\mu\wedge\nu=\eta(X)\mu,\qquad\left(\frac{\alpha\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}+\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)^{2}}\iota_{B}\mu\right)\wedge\eta=\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)}\mu,

and therefore

\iota_{X}\mu=\frac{\alpha\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}+\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)^{2}}\iota_{B}\mu\implies\eta(X)=\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)}.

Hence, we obtain the equivalence

\displaystyle\hat{b}(X)=\alpha\Longleftrightarrow\iota_{X}\mu=\frac{\alpha\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}+\frac{\alpha(B)}{\eta(B)^{2}}\iota_{B}\mu

and therefore the proof of our claim.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

I.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

I.3
.

Let
(B,\nu,\mu)
be a flux system on
M
. Assume that
\eta
is an adapted 1-form for
(B,\nu,\mu)
. Then, by
Lemma

II.2
the vector field
u
given by

\iota_{u}\mu=\frac{\nu\wedge\eta}{\eta(B)}

is the unique vector field satisfying

\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu,\qquad\eta(u)=0.

It remains to show claims 1 to 4 of
Theorem

I.3
on the relationship between
u
and
{\tilde{B}}:=\eta(B)^{-1}B
. Claims 1 and 2 can immediately be seen from
\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
. Equation (
1
) with
{\tilde{B}}
and
{\tilde{\mu}}=\eta(B)\mu
shows that claims 3 and 4 are equivalent. Indeed,

\iota_{[u,{\tilde{B}}]}{\tilde{\mu}}=d\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu-(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u)\iota_{B}\mu=d\nu-(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u)\iota_{B}\mu=-(\Div_{{\tilde{\mu}}}u)\iota_{B}\mu.

In addition, claim 4 holds because

\mathcal{L}_{u}{\tilde{\mu}}=d\iota_{u}{\tilde{\mu}}=d(\nu\wedge\eta)=\nu\wedge d\eta=0

where we used that
d\eta\wedge\nu=0
. Thus, all claims hold, completing the proof.
∎

Remark II.3
.

From the proof of
Lemma

II.2
, the map
\hat{b}
can be understood as being a vector bundle isomorphism
b:TM\to T^{*}M
whereby

b(v)=\iota_{v}(\iota_{B}\mu)|_{x}+\eta|_{x}(v)\eta|_{x}

for
v\in TM
. This clarifies that
\hat{b}
is a purely linear-algebraic construction.

II.3
A proof of Thm.
I.3
through a coisotropic embedding

In this section, theoretical context is given to
Theorem

I.3
. In particular, we will take the perspective of
Burby et al. 2021
in viewing a non-vanishing, divergence-free vector field
B
on a 3-manifold
M
as giving
M
the structure of a presymplectic manifold. Through this perspective, the connection between
Theorem

I.3
and the usual Noether theorem for symplectic manifolds can be understood.

II.3.1
A brief introduction to presymplectic geometry

The vector field
B
and the volume form
\mu
which it preserves gives rise to a presymplectic manifold. This change in perspective is facilitated by the observation that the 2-form

\beta=\iota_{B}\mu

is necessarily closed (
d\beta=0
) due to the fact that
B
is divergence-free, and that
\beta
is of maximal rank by the non-vanishing assumption of
B
. In the plasma physics literature, the form
\beta
is often called the
flux form
.

Any closed, maximal rank 2-form
\beta
is called
presymplectic
and makes
(M,\beta)
a
presymplectic manifold
. The study of a presymplectic manifold is called presymplectic geometry. We follow
Burby et al. 2021
in briefly summarising some key observations in presymplectic geometry. In particular, we will point out some differences to symplectic geometry arising from the fact that
\beta
is only of maximal rank and not non-degenerate.

Definition II.4

(Presymplectic and Hamiltonian Vector Fields)
.

A vector field
u
on
M
is
presymplectic with respect to
\beta
or is
locally Hamiltonian
if there exists a closed 1-form

\nu
such that
\iota_{u}\beta=\nu
. The pair
(u,\nu)
is called a
presymplectic pair
. If in addition
\nu
is exact, that is
\nu=-dH
for some
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, then
u
is called a
Hamiltonian vector field
for the Hamiltonian
H
, and
(u,H)
is a
Hamiltonian pair
.

Remark II.5
.

Observe for any vector field
u
that
\mathcal{L}_{u}\beta=\iota_{u}d\beta+d\iota_{u}\beta=d\iota_{X}\beta
and hence
u
preserves
\beta
if and only if
u
is presymplectic.

Every Hamiltonian pair is presymplectic, and the converse is true if the first de Rham cohomology of
M
,
H^{1}(M)
, vanishes. It is true that, given a presymplectic pair,
B
preserves
\nu
as shown in the following.

Lemma II.6

(
Burby et al. 2021
)
.

Suppose that
(u,\nu)
is a presymplectic pair for
\beta
. Then

1.

\nu(B)=0
;

2.

\mathcal{L}_{B}\nu=0
;

3.

\beta\wedge\nu=0
.

Moreover, if
(u,H)
is a Hamiltonian pair,
B
is tangent to surfaces of constant
H
, that is
\mathcal{L}_{B}H=0
.

Lemma

II.6
has the following natural converse which follows immediately from
Lemma

II.2
.

Lemma II.7
.

If
\nu
is a closed 1-form with
\nu(B)=0
, then there exists
u
such that
(u,\nu)
is a presymplectic pair.

It is natural to compare symplectic vector fields to presymplectic vector fields. A symplectic 2-form
\omega
is non-degenerate and consequently can only be defined on some even dimensional manifolds. The non-degeneracy of a symplectic form implies that, given any function
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, there exists a unique Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
defined by
\iota_{X_{H}}\omega=-dH
.
In the presymplectic setting, given a function
H
on
M
, a Hamiltonian vector field need
not
exist. Moreover, even if it does exist, the Hamiltonian vector field is not unique. Indeed, if
u
is a Hamiltonian vector field for
\nu
then any vector field of the form
u+hB
is also Hamiltonian for
\nu
, for any function
h
on
M
.

Perhaps more surprisingly, unlike the symplectic case, not all presymplectic vector fields are divergence-free.

Lemma II.8

(
Burby et al. 2021
)
.

A presymplectic vector field
u
is divergence-free if and only if
[u,B]=0
.

The consequence of
Lemmas

II.7
and
II.8
is that one must be careful when trying to translate well-known results and theory from symplectic geometry to presymplectic geometry. In particular, as we will see in the proceeding sections, one needs additional assumptions in order to provide a Noether theorem for presymplectic vector fields, and consequently, divergence-free vector fields.

II.3.2
Converse Conformal Noether Theorem

In this section we establish a second proof of
Theorem

I.3
from the perspective of presymplectic geometry. Before doing so, we discuss an initial approach to the Converse Conformal Noether “Theorem” described in
Section

II.1
from this perspective.

Let
\nu
be a closed 1-form such that
\nu(B)=0
. Then
Lemma

II.7
enables us to fix a vector field
u_{0}
so that
(u_{0},\nu)
is a presymplectic pair:
\iota_{u_{0}}\beta=\nu
. Then, there exists a divergence-free vector field
u
satisfying
\iota_{u}\beta=\nu
if and only if there exists a function
h\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that the vector field
u=u_{0}+hB
satisfies
\Div_{\mu}u=0
or equivalently that
h
satisfies the cohomological equation

B(h)=-\Div_{\mu}u_{0}.

However, in general, only special vector fields are able to solve given cohomological equations
Kocsard 2009
;
Newcomb 1959
. In fact,
Example

II.12
in
Section

II.4
shows that such a
h
need not exist. On the other hand, we have that

\iota_{[u,B]}\beta=\mathcal{L}_{u}\iota_{B}\beta-\iota_{B}\mathcal{L}_{u}\beta=-\iota_{B}d\nu=0.

so
[u,B]=\kappa B
for some function
\kappa
and
u
being a conformal symmetry is equivalent to another cohomological equation. This line of approach requires a deeper understanding of the vector field
B
and the associated cohomological equation to make any conclusion.

An alternative approach comes delivered in a more abstract package. For the sake of the exposition, assume that
\nu=d\psi
is exact. The journey begins with
\eta
-embeddings of the presymplectic manifolds.

Theorem II.9

(
\eta
-embedding
Burby et al. 2021
)
.

Let
(M,\beta)
be a presymplectic manifold. Suppose there exists a 1-form
\eta
on
M
such that
\beta\wedge\eta
is a volume form. Let
\tilde{M}=M\times\mathbb{R}\ni(z,v)
, define the projection
\pi:\tilde{M}\to M
and take
\tilde{\beta}=\pi^{*}\beta,\,\tilde{\eta}=\pi^{*}\eta
. Then,
(\tilde{M},\omega)
is a symplectic manifold with

\omega=\tilde{\beta}+d(v\tilde{\eta}).

Moreover,
M
is coisotropically embedded as
v=0
in
\tilde{M}
.

Note that on a presymplectic manifold
(M,\beta)
, a metric
g
provides the required 1-form by setting
\eta:=B^{\flat}
. There is of course some choice in
\eta
, and we say any specific embedding is an
\eta
-embedding of
(M,\beta)
.

The
\eta
-embedding provides a mechanism to apply the tools of symplectic geometry to that of presymplectic geometry. In order to find a conformal symmetry, we will need to not only embed the presymplectic manifold associated to
B
into a symplectic manifold, but do so in such a way as to have the lift of
B
to
\tilde{M}
be part of an integrable system (in the sense of Liouville). With a little more structure imposed on
\eta
, this is indeed possible.

Theorem II.10
.

Assume
\eta
is a 1-form adapted to the flux system
(B,d\psi,\mu)
. Let
(M\times\mathbb{R},\omega)
be the symplectic manifold obtained from the
\eta
-embedding of
(M,\beta)
, take coordinates
(z,v)\in M\times\mathbb{R}
and let
\tilde{\psi}=\pi^{*}\psi
. Then
H=v
and
\tilde{\psi}
form an integrable system on
M\times\mathbb{R}
with associated Hamiltonian vector fields respectively satisfying

X_{H}|_{v=0}=\frac{1}{\eta(B)}B,\quad X_{\tilde{\psi}}|_{v=0}=u-\frac{\eta(u)}{\eta(B)}B,

for any vector field
u
such that
\iota_{u}\beta=-d\psi
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of
(
Burby et al. 2021
, Thm. 4.11)
which has stronger hypotheses to
Theorem

II.10
. It is enough to observe that the original proof follows with the weaker hypotheses. The proof is included here for completeness.

By
Lemma

II.7
, there always exists some
u
such that
\iota_{u}\beta=-d\psi
, although such a
u
is not guaranteed to be divergence-free.
We begin by computing the Hamiltonian vector fields associated with
H
and
\tilde{\psi}
on
v=0
. They are defined through Hamilton’s equations:

\iota_{X_{H}}\omega=-dv,\quad\iota_{X_{\tilde{\psi}}}\omega=-d\tilde{\psi}.

A calculation reveals that
X_{H}|_{v=0}=\frac{1}{\eta(B)}B
and
X_{\tilde{\psi}}|_{v=0}=u-\frac{\eta(u)}{\eta(B)}B
, as desired.

To show that
(H,\tilde{\psi})
form an integrable system on
M\times\mathbb{R}
we require the Poisson bracket
\{\tilde{\psi},H\}=0
. A quick way to see this is to pull back
\omega
to the common level set
(\tilde{\psi},H)=(\tilde{\psi}_{0},H_{0})
. The condition
H=H_{0}
implies
v=H_{0}
. The pullback of
\omega
to the level set is therefore

\pi_{\tilde{\psi}_{0},H_{0}}^{*}(\tilde{\beta}+d(v\tilde{\eta}))=0+\pi_{\tilde{\psi}_{0}}^{*}d(H_{0}\tilde{\eta})=H_{0}\pi_{\tilde{\psi}_{0}}^{*}d\tilde{\eta}=0,

since
\tilde{\beta}=\pi^{*}\beta
and
d\tilde{\eta}=\pi^{*}d\eta
both vanish when pulled back to a
\tilde{\psi}
-surface. It follows that
\omega
vanishes on the common level sets, and hence, that
\{\tilde{\psi},v\}:=\iota_{X_{H}}\iota_{X_{\tilde{\psi}}}\omega=0
.
∎

By
\eta
-embedding the presymplectic manifold generated from the field
B
of a flux system
(B,d\psi,\mu)
,
Theorem

II.10
provides an integrable system (in the sense of Liouville) given by functions
u,\tilde{\psi}
and associated Hamiltonian vector fields
X_{v},X_{\tilde{\psi}}
. As
u,\tilde{\psi}
Poisson commute, it follows that
X_{v},X_{\tilde{\psi}}
commute. In particular the vector fields

{\tilde{B}}:=X_{v}|_{v=0}=\frac{1}{\eta(B)}B,\qquad\tilde{u}:=X_{\rho}|_{u=0}=u-\frac{\eta(u)}{\eta(B)}B

will commute. In fact
\iota_{\tilde{u}}\beta=-d\psi
. We thus have what we want: a conformal symmetry
\tilde{u}
of
B
. Hence, we have provided an alternative proof of
Theorem

I.3
for the case
\nu
is exact.
Theorem

II.10
can be adjusted to prove the case where
\nu
is no longer exact.

II.4
A counter-example to the converse naive Noether theorem

To understand the counter-example, one must bear in mind the following elementary results in the study of Diophantine vector fields (for a definition of Diophantine vector fields see
Kocsard 2009
). The first statement follows directly from
(
Perrella et al. 2022
, Proposition 23 and 24)
while the second statement is in
(
Kocsard 2009
, Proposition 2.6)
.

Proposition II.11
.

On the 2-torus
S=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}
, the following holds.

1.

Let
0<f\in C^{\infty}(S)
and consider the vector field

X=f(a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y})

where
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
(a,b)
is not Diophantine, then
X
is not smoothly conjugate to a Diophantine vector field. That is, there does not exist a diffeomorphism
\varphi:S\to S
such that

\varphi_{*}X=c\partial_{x}+d\partial_{y}

for any Diophantine vector
(c,d)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

2.

A vector field
X
on
S
is smoothly conjugate to a Diophantine vector field, if and only if, for every
h\in C^{\infty}(S)
, there exists a
g\in C^{\infty}(S)
and a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
solving the cohomological equation

X(g)=h-c.

With this in mind, we now consider the example.

Example II.12
.

First consider the 2-torus
S=(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}
. With the natural coordinates
x,y
, consider the vector fields

X_{0}=a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y},\qquad Y_{0}=c\partial_{x}+d\partial_{y}

where
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be linearly independent from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
but not Diophantine and
(c,d)
is Diophantine and linearly independent from
(a,b)
. We will show that for some
0<f\in C^{\infty}(S)
, the only vector fields commuting with
X=fX_{0}
are constant multiples of
X
.

For the time being, let
f\in C^{\infty}(S)
be arbitrary and set
X=fX_{0}
. Any vector field
Z
may be written, for some
g,h\in C^{\infty}(S)
, as

Z=gX+hY_{0}.

In this way, we obtain

\displaystyle[X,Z]

\displaystyle=[X,gX+hY_{0}]

\displaystyle=[X,gX]+[fX_{0},hY_{0}]

\displaystyle=(fX(g)-hY_{0}(f))X_{0}+fX_{0}(h)Y_{0}.

Hence, if
[X,Z]=0
, then
X_{0}(h)=0
and since
X_{0}
has dense orbits in
S
,
h
must be constant. Moreover, we have the cohomological equation

X(g)=Y_{0}(h\ln f)

(2)

Applying the first and second statement of
Proposition

II.11
to
X
, there exists
H\in C^{\infty}(S)
such that, for any function
g\in C^{\infty}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}

X(g)\neq H-c.

Fix such a
H
. Suppose that
h\neq 0
. Applying the second statement of
Proposition

II.11
to
Y_{0}
, there exists function
f
and a constant
c^{\prime}
such that

Y_{0}(h\ln f)=H-c^{\prime}.

Fixing this choice of
f
, (
2
) is false. Therefore, we must have that
h=0
and so
X(g)=0
. This implies as before that

is constant and
Z=gX
, a constant multiple of

. Hence, our claim is true.

With this in mind, we now consider the manifold with (possibly empty) boundary
M=S\times I
where
I
is some interval. With the natural coordinates
x,y,z
, consider the flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
where

B=F(a\partial_{x}+b\partial_{y}),\qquad\nu=dz,\qquad\mu=F^{-1}dx\wedge dy\wedge dz

and
F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)
where

is as before. Then, the previous paragraph shows that

is not rectifiable, not even locally about a single constant-

linearly independent from

[u,B]=0,\qquad\nu(u)=0

let alone satisfying
\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu
.

II.5
Contexts in which an adapted 1-form is guaranteed globally

With some additional assumptions on a flux system, an adapted 1-form can be guaranteed to exist globally. Fix a 3-manifold
M
with (possibly empty) boundary.

Proposition II.13
.

Let
B
be a vector field on
M
. Let
\eta
be a 1-form such that

\iota_{B}d\eta=0,\qquad\eta(B)>0.

If
(B,\nu,\mu)
is a flux system, then
\eta
is a 1-form adapted to
(B,\nu,\mu)
.

Proof.

It suffices to note that
d\eta\wedge\nu
is a top-form on
M
and hence
d\eta\wedge\nu=0
is equivalent to
\iota_{B}(d\eta\wedge\nu)=0
. Moreover,

\iota_{B}(d\eta\wedge\nu)=(\iota_{B}d\eta)\wedge\nu+d\eta\wedge\iota_{B}\nu=0

∎

There are some particular cases of
Proposition

II.13
of interest. If
B
is vector field and
\eta
is a closed 1-form such that
\eta(B)>0
, then the hypothesis of
Proposition

II.13
are satisfied. If
M
is closed, any vector field
B
with a global Poincaré section possesses such an
\eta
by the work of
Schwartzman 1962
. In plasma physics, magnetic fields
B
in a vacuum on an oriented
M
satisfy
dB^{\flat}=0
with respect to the metric
g
placed on
M
. Hence, all non-vanishing vacuum fields have the closed 1-form
\eta=B^{\flat}
satisfying the assumptions of
Proposition

II.13
.

Alternatively, if
B
is a vector field and
\eta
is a 1-form such that
d\eta
is non-vanishing, then
B
and
\eta
satisfy the hypothesis of
Proposition

II.13
if and only if
\eta
is a contact form and
B
is a Reeb-like vector field of
\eta
. In this context,
B
is a Reeb-like field if there exists a non-vanishing function
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
B/f
is the Reeb vector field of
\eta
. For such fields,
B
is divergence-free with respect to
\mu=\frac{1}{\eta(B)}\eta\wedge d\eta
. The following is an example of a contact system giving rise to an adapted 1-form.

Example II.14
.

Set
M=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{3}
with periodic coordinates
x,y,z:(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{3}\to\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the contact form

\eta=\sin zdx+\cos zdy

whereby
\eta\wedge d\eta=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
, the standard volume form on
M
. The associated Reeb vector field is given by

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\sin z\partial_{x}+\cos z\partial_{y}.

With
\nu=dz
, we get a flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
and
\eta
an adapted 1-form.

It is worth highlighting that
Example

II.14
gives an example of a flux system such that
B
has no global Poincaré section, and
\eta
is not closed. In fact, the adapted 1-forms for the flux systems which arise from contact forms can never be closed in the case that
M
is closed. This is because of the following elementary fact.

Proposition II.15
.

Let
B
be a Reeb-like vector field for a contact form
\eta
on a manifold
M
. Then,
B
has no closed Poincaré section. In particular, if
M
is closed,
B
has no closed 1-form
\kappa
for which
\kappa(B)>0
.

Proof.

Let
S
be a closed hypersurface of dimension
2n
of
M
where
\dim M=2n+1
(
n\geq 1
). Considering the volume-form
\mu=\eta\wedge(d\eta)^{n}
and the Reeb vector field
R
, we have
\iota_{R}\mu=(d\eta)^{n}
. Then considering the inclusion
i:S\subset M
, because
i^{*}(d\eta)^{n}
is exact on
S
, Stokes’ Theorem gives

\int_{S}i^{*}\iota_{R}\mu=\int_{S}i^{*}(d\eta)^{n}=0.

Hence,
R
is not transverse to
S
. Thus,
B
is not transverse to
S
either. In particular,
B
has no Poincaré section. Moreover, it follows from the same techniques as in
Tischler 1970
that a vector field
X
possesses a closed transverse section provided there exists a closed 1-form
\kappa
such that
\kappa(X)>0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

As an example with relevance to applications, Beltrami fields can be thought of as generalisations of Reeb-like vector fields in 3 dimensions
Etnyre and Ghrist 2000
. If
M
has a metric and an orientation, a Beltrami field
B
satisfies

\nabla\cdot B=0,\qquad\nabla\times B=\lambda B

for some
\lambda\in C^{\infty}(M)
. In terms of
\beta=\iota_{B}\mu
where
\mu
is the induced volume form, this equation reads

dB^{\flat}=\lambda\beta

and therefore
\eta=B^{\flat}
is a 1-form where the assumptions of
Proposition

II.13
are satisfied provided that
B
is non-vanishing.

III
An adapted 1-form exists in the toroidal region

The main purpose of this section is to prove
Theorem

I.5
. The proof for a given tangential flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
consists of two steps. In step one, the local existence of closed 1-forms adapted to the flux system is established in a neighborhood of each of the torus and axis in the toroidal region. Step one is achieved using tubular neighborhoods and in the case of the tori, we use flowouts which are essentially the restriction of a transverse flow to the torus; these are also used in the second step. In step two, we patch together these closed 1-forms using a partition of unity. In step two, care is taken to ensure that the functions in the partition of unity have differentials that are linearly dependent with
\nu
. The construction of the partition relies on techniques which also prove
Proposition

I.6
. These techniques apply more generally to closed 1-forms in a region which we call the closed region. This generalisation is adopted to clarify the role that each element of the flux system plays in the proof and because it does not introduce any additional difficulties to the proof.

This section proceeds as follows. In
Section

III.1
, the toroidal region is formally defined and some auxiliary definitions used to prove
Theorem

I.5
are introduced. Then, in Section
III.2
,
Theorem

I.5
is proven under the assumption that the suitable partition of unity has been constructed. Lastly, in Section
Section

III.3
, the partition of unity is constructed and
Proposition

I.6
is proven.

III.1
The toroidal region and the closed region

We begin by introducing some convenient notation used throughout this section. If
M
is a manifold with (possibly empty) boundary,
S
is a submanifold, and

is the inclusion, then for any
k
-form
\nu
on

The toroidal region arises from a more general concept which we call the closed region. The closed region accounts for the simplest kind of critical sets which can occur for a closed 1-form
\nu
on
M
such that
\partial M^{*}\nu=0
and will now be defined.

Definition III.1
.

Let
\Omega=\{x\in M:\nu|_{x}\neq 0\}
be the set of non-critical points of

Appendix

be the associated foliation on

1.

A closed leaf

of the induced foliation is a

\nu
. The

2.

A

3.

Set

(on

)

The regular closed leaves of

We now define the toroidal region.

Assume that

(on

)

It is well-known from index theory that the only compact orientable surface admitting a nowhere zero vector field is the 2-torus. In particular, the regular tori are indeed tori.

Remark III.9

We now establish the local existence an adapted 1-form in a neighbourhood of each regular torus of a tangential flux system.

Lemma III.10
.

Let
(B,\nu,\mu)
be a tangential flux system on

. Let

be a regular torus. Then, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
S
in
\mathcal{T}
and a closed 1-form

\eta(B)>0
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

III.6
and
Lemma

III.7
we may take an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and an embedding
\Sigma:S\times I\to M
such that
\Sigma^{*}\nu=dt
where

is projection onto
I\subset\mathbb{R}
. Now, the map
\Sigma
induces a volume form
{\tilde{\mu}}=\Sigma^{*}\mu\in\Omega^{3}(S\times I)
and vector field
{\tilde{B}}
on

which is
\Sigma
-related to

. In particular, we have the relations

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}{\tilde{\mu}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle dt({\tilde{B}})

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand, with the vector field
\partial_{t}
on
S\times I
, observe that

\iota_{\partial_{t}}(dt\wedge\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}})=(\iota_{\partial_{t}}dt)\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}-dt\wedge\iota_{\partial_{t}}\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}=\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}.

Thus,
{\tilde{\mu}}=dt\wedge\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}
so that

0=\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}(dt\wedge\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}})=(\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}dt)\wedge\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}+dt\wedge\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}=dt\wedge\mathcal{L}_{{\tilde{B}}}\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}.

Thus with the induced vector field

on

{\tilde{B}}
and area form
\mu_{0}=(S_{0})^{*}\iota_{\partial_{t}}{\tilde{\mu}}

\mathcal{L}_{B_{0}}\mu_{0}=0
. On the other hand, it is well known in general (see for instance

) that any area-preserving vector field

\eta_{0}
such that
\eta_{0}(X)>0
. In particular, fixing such an
\eta_{0}
for

, we obtain a closed 1-form
\tilde{\eta}=\pi^{*}\eta_{0}
on

such that
\tilde{\eta}({\tilde{B}})|_{S}>0
. Moreover, the set
\tilde{U}=\{x\in S\times I:\tilde{\eta}({\tilde{B}})>0\}
is an open neighbourhood of
S_{0}
in

. The result now easily follows from pushing forward

into

with
\Sigma
.
∎

We now establish an adapted 1-form in a neighbourhood of each axis of a tangential flux system. This follows immediately from the following well-known result.

Proposition III.11
.

Let

Proposition III.14

Let

With this, we may prove

Proof of

.

Let

III.3

Here we will construct the partition of unity and classify the closed region

III.3.1
Without boundary or axes

Let
M
be a manifold without boundary of dimension
n
and
\nu
be a closed 1-form on
M
. Here, we will assume that the region
\mathcal{C}^{2}
coincides with
M
(so that
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{2}=M
). Denote by
\mathcal{F}
the rank
n-1
foliation by
\nu
(consisting of all the regular closed leaves). The following shows that non-injectivity of a flowout implies compactness.

Lemma III.15
.

Assume
M
is connected and there exists a non-injective flowout
\Sigma
of some
S\in\mathcal{F}
for some open interval
I
containing
0
. Then,
M
is compact.

Proof.

Because
\Sigma
is not injective but
\Sigma(\cdot,t):S\to M
is injective for each
t\in I
, there exists points
(x,s),(y,t)\in S\times I
with
\Sigma(x,s)=\Sigma(y,t)
and
s<t
. Now by
Lemma

III.7
, we have a flowout
\Sigma^{\prime}
of
S^{\prime}=\Sigma(S,s)\in\mathcal{F}
with interval
I^{\prime}=I-s
. With the point
x^{\prime}=\Sigma(x,s)\in S^{\prime}
and time
0<t^{\prime}=t-s\in I^{\prime}
we get

\Sigma^{\prime}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\Sigma^{\prime}(\Sigma(x,s),t^{\prime})=\Sigma(x,t).

On the other hand, since
x^{\prime}=\Sigma(y,t)
we have
\Sigma(y,t)\in S^{\prime}
. So that
\Sigma(x,t)\in S^{\prime}
and hence
\Sigma^{\prime}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in S^{\prime}
. Altogether, we conclude so far that
\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime},t^{\prime})=S^{\prime}
.

Now, consider the compact subset

K=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times[0,t^{\prime}])\subset M.

We show that
K
is open. Because
\Sigma^{\prime}
is an open map by
Lemma

III.7
, it suffices to find
\epsilon>0
so that
K=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times(-\epsilon,t^{\prime}+\epsilon))
. To this end, we consider the flowout
\Sigma^{\prime\prime}
of
S^{\prime}=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime},t^{\prime})
with interval
I^{\prime\prime}=I^{\prime}-t^{\prime}
and make the following periodicity argument. On the one hand, for
z\in S^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}\in I^{\prime\prime}
, by
Lemma

III.7
we have the identity

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(\Sigma^{\prime}(z,t^{\prime}),t^{\prime\prime})=\Sigma^{\prime}(z,t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime}).

Since
\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times\{t^{\prime}\})=S^{\prime}
, we have in particular that, for
t^{\prime\prime}\in I^{\prime\prime}
,

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(S^{\prime}\times\{t^{\prime\prime}\})=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times\{t^{\prime}+t^{\prime\prime}\}).

On the other hand,
I=I^{\prime}\cap I^{\prime\prime}
is an open interval containing
0
and by
Lemma

III.7
, for each
z\in S^{\prime}
and
r\in I
, we have

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(z,r)=\Sigma^{\prime}(z,r).

Thus for each
r\in I
,

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(S^{\prime}\times\{r\})=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times\{r\}).

Now, for
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small we have that
[0,\epsilon),(t^{\prime}-\epsilon,t^{\prime}]\subset[0,t^{\prime}]
and
\pm\epsilon\in I
so that the above relations imply

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times[t^{\prime},t^{\prime}+\epsilon))=\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(S^{\prime}\times[0,\epsilon))=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times[0,\epsilon))\subset K,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times(-\epsilon,0])=\Sigma^{\prime\prime}(S^{\prime}\times(-\epsilon,0])=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times(t^{\prime}-\epsilon,t^{\prime}])\subset K.

This shows that

K=\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times(-\epsilon,t^{\prime}+\epsilon)).

Thus,
K
is open, as claimed. To conclude,
M
being connected implies
M=K
, a compact subset of
M
. That is,
M
is compact.
∎

With
Lemma

III.15
the following useful lemma can be proved. The lemma will be used substantially in

due to the convenient dichotomy it gives rise to.

Lemma III.16
.

Assume that
M
is connected. Then, one of the following holds.

1.

For any

, there exists a flowout
\Sigma
of
S
along
N
with an interval

which is open and contains

such that

2.

is compact.

Proof.

Let

. Then, consider the subset

for which there exists an open interval

containing
0
and a flowout
\Sigma
of
S
with interval
I
such that

. This set

is open because each flowout
\Sigma
of
S
with any open interval
I
containing
0
is an open map by

III.7
and

a union of images
\Sigma(S\times I)
of such maps.

We now show that
U
is closed. Let
y\in M
and consider the unique

containing

. Then, let
\Sigma^{\prime}:S^{\prime}\times I^{\prime}\to M
be a flowout of
S^{\prime}
with some interval

containing

and consider
\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}\times I^{\prime})

\Sigma
of

\Sigma^{\prime\prime}

It is clear that

Lemma III.17

Let
\{U_{S}\}_{S\in\mathcal{F}}

,

, an open interval

containing
0
with a flowout
\Sigma_{S}^{\prime}:S\times I_{S}^{\prime}\to M

. Now, for each

,
{\Sigma^{\prime}_{S}}^{-1}(U_{S})
is a neighbourhood of

in

0

contained in

. Now, for each

\psi_{S}^{\prime}:I_{S}\to\mathbb{R}

\psi_{S}^{\prime}(0)=1
. Then we obtain the function
\psi_{S}:S\times I_{S}\to\mathbb{R}

which is compactly supported in

. Moreover, if

denotes projection into

, we have the relation

By use of
\Sigma_{S}
, this then gives a function

which
1
on

and is compactly supported in

and additionally satisfies

Now, the open subsets

and leaves

.

containing

, a leaf

, and a flowout

of

of

. Now, for

is a neighbourhood of

. Thus, for each

, (using compactness of

), we may choose an open interval

of

. Now, fix a partition of unity

. For each

where

on

IV
An obstruction to the global existence of adapted 1-forms

Reeb cylinders, and more generally Kuperberg plugs, were identified in
Peralta-Salas et al. 2021
as obstructions to the existence of MHS fields in arbitrary geometries. In this section, we will use a similar argument to show that the existence of Reeb cylinders are an obstruction to the global existence of an adapted 1-form.

Definition IV.1
.

A
Reeb cylinder
is a pair
(C,X)
where
C
is an oriented cylinder and
X
is a non-vanishing vector field on
C
tangent to
\partial C
such that, considering the connected components
C_{0},C_{1}
of
\partial C
whereby, as a 1-chain,
\partial C=C_{0}-C_{1}
, the following hold:

1.

X
is positively (respectively negatively) oriented along the 1-chain
C_{0}
and
C_{0}
is the alpha-limit of each orbit except
C_{1}
.

2.

X
is negatively (positively) oriented along the 1-chain
C_{1}
and is the omega-limit for every orbit except
C_{0}
.

The following elementary principle shows why Reeb cylinders are obstructions.

Proposition IV.2
.

Let
M
be a 3-manifold with (possibly empty) boundary and
(B,\nu,\mu)
a flux system on
M
. Suppose that
(B,\nu,\mu)
possesses a non-trivial Reeb cylinder: that there exists a cylinder
C
immersed in
M
which
B
is tangent to and such that
C
together with the induced vector field
X
on
C
forms a Reeb cylinder, and the critical points of
\nu|_{C}
are nowhere dense on
C
. Then, there cannot exist a 1-form adapted to
(B,\nu,\mu)
on
M
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(B,\nu,\mu)
possesses a Reeb cylinder
(C,X)
. Then, observe that
C^{*}\nu
is closed on
C
. Moreover, the periodic orbits of
B
assumed on
\partial C
generate the homology of
C
. Hence, from the fact that
\nu(B)=0
, we get that
C^{*}\nu=df
is exact on
C
. However, because of the orbit structure of
X
and
df(X)=0
, we have that
f
is constant. Thus,
C^{*}\nu=0
.

Now, suppose that
\eta
is a 1-form on
M
such that
d\eta\wedge\nu=0
. We will show that we cannot have
\eta(B)|_{\partial C}>0
and thus there are no adapted 1-forms. Indeed, at points
x\in C
where
\nu|_{x}\neq 0
, because
C^{*}\nu|_{x}=0
, we obtain that
C^{*}d\eta|_{x}=0
. Since such points are dense in
C
by assumption, we conclude that
C^{*}d\eta=0
so that
C^{*}\eta
is a closed 1-form on
C
. Now, regarding
C
as a 2-chain, Stokes’ Theorem says

0=\int_{C}d\eta=\int_{C_{0}}\eta-\int_{C_{1}}\eta.

However, if
\eta(B)|_{\partial C}>0
, then by the orientation of
X
along
\partial C
, accounting for the two cases of orientation of the periodic orbits, the quantity

\int_{C_{0}}\eta-\int_{C_{1}}\eta

is either strictly positive or strictly negative. Thus,
\eta(B)|_{\partial C}>0
cannot hold.
∎

V
Flux-coordinates and rectifiability

In the study of magnetically confined plasma, it is often convenient to use special coordinates adapted to the magnetic field, so called
magnetic
or
flux-coordinates

D’Haeseleer et al. 1991
;
Helander 2014
. The main advantage of flux-coordinates is that field-lines are straight. From a computational point of view, magnetic differential equations in flux-coordinates have a simple form and can be solved by means of Fourier series in the corresponding poloidal and toroidal angles. Most notably, in the study of magneto-hydrostatics (MHS), particular choices of flux-coordinates are extensively used, such as Hamada coordinates
Hamada 1962
, PEST coordinates
Ray C. Grimm and Johnson 1976
, Boozer coordinates
Boozer 1981
, etc. The methods presented in this paper provide the means of proving the existence of flux-coordinates without assuming that the magnetic field is MHS nor that the magnetic field is quasi-symmetric, nor that it has any symmetry by assumption.

In this section, quick and rigorous proofs of the existence of flux-coordinates are given for flux systems in general (
Corollaries

V.4
and
V.9
). It is highlighted that flux-coordinates all come about because of conformal symmetries; different coordinates correspond to different pairs of commuting vector fields.
It is important to note that the full machinery of
Theorem

I.5
is not necessary. We only need the technical
Lemma

III.10
, the elementary
Proposition

III.11
, and the algebraic
Theorem

I.3
. The work in
Burby et al. 2021
is a particular case of the results in this section. In
Section

V.1
, we will focus on flux-coordinates near regular tori. In section
Section

V.2
, we consider flux-coordinates near axes relying heavily on the work done in
Burby et al. 2021
. From this point on in this section, fix a 3-manifold
M
with boundary.

To establish flux-coordinates, in relation to symmetries, we remark the following.

Remark V.1
.

If
(B,\nu,\mu)
is a flux system on a manifold
M
with boundary and
\eta

implies that that

given by

Moreover, the vector field

\iota_{u}\iota_{{\tilde{B}}}{\tilde{\mu}}=\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=\nu,\qquad[u,{\tilde{B}}]=0.

An adapted 1-form is locally accessible for many flux systems

is a periodic orbit of

and thus, one may rescale the tangential flux system on

In plasma physics, there are choices of an adapted 1-form which may canonically be available. For instance, if
M
is an oriented Riemannian 3-manifold with boundary, then a non-vanishing divergence-free vector field

B\cdot\nabla\psi=0,\qquad J\cdot\nabla\psi=0,

where

as an adapted 1-form. Then,
{\tilde{B}}=B/B^{2}
and

are commuting vector fields. The resulting coordinates (when

and

\partial M

. This was observed in

and in

.

Another relevant and common adapted 1-form in the context of toroidal confinement is
\eta=d\varphi=(-ydx+xdy)/(x^{2}+y^{2})
, where, in an abuse of notation,
\varphi(x,y,z)=\arctan(y/x)
is the geometric toroidal angle of a solid torus

\mathbb{R}^{3}
away from the

is closed (but deceivingly not exact) and the assumption that

is valid on physical grounds (the toroidal component is usually dominant in fusion devices such as tokamaks and stellarators). The related conformal symmetry of

is

u=\frac{\nabla\psi\times\nabla\varphi}{B^{\varphi}}

and is purely poloidal: the integral curves lie on constant
\varphi

(and are hence closed). Consequently, the geometric toroidal angle

can serve as a coordinate, and a poloidal angle (another independent coordinate), can be found to make the field-lines of

We will now discuss how symmetries give rise to flux-coordinates and write down the consequences for general flux systems.

V.1

Assume that

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=B^{1}\partial_{\theta_{1}}+B^{2}\partial_{\theta_{2}},

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\rho(B^{2}dz\wedge d\theta_{1}-B^{1}dz\wedge d\theta_{2})

\displaystyle\vartheta

\displaystyle=\rho(u^{2}dz\wedge d\theta_{1}-u^{1}dz\wedge d\theta_{2})

VI
Discussion

This work considered flux systems on a manifolds with (possibly empty) boundary.
Theorem

I.3
identified an algebraic condition, namely the existence of an adapted 1-form, in order to conclude that the flux system had a conformal symmetry. This can be viewed as a partial converse to a conformal Noether theorem (
Theorem

II.1
). Assuming that the flux system was tangential, in other words, certain natural boundary conditions were obeyed,
Theorem

I.5
showed that the toroidal region is an apriori open set on which an adapted 1-form exists and in turn, a conformal symmetry. This provides a converse to a conformal Noether theorem on the topologically simplest region generated by the flux system. However, there remains some open questions and interesting further directions from the perspective of both plasma physics, and of a purely mathematical view.

In the context of plasma physics, this work demonstrates that structural features of magnetic fields, such as the existence of certain flux-coordinates, or symmetries, can be deduced from a flux function (first integral) alone, and the stronger assumption that the field satisfies MHS is not required. This has a direct implication for Grad’s Conjecture
Grad 1967
which loosely states, under some non-degeneracy assumptions appealing to plasma confinement, that an MHS field with nested flux surfaces must possess a continuous geometric symmetry. This paper reveals that these structural features are purely topological in nature and that, because they are present for a large family of divergence-free vector fields, they alone do not give any insight into Grad’s conjecture.

It is not known whether symmetry and first integrals are essential for good confinement; chaotic fields are in some scenarios desirable for the exhaust of heat and deleterious impurities
Pedersen and al. 2022
. In the face of Grad’s conjecture, if symmetry and first integrals are mathematically impossible beyond Euclidean symmetry, the flavour of the question changes to how ”good” or ”close” fields are to ”useful” or ”approximate” symmetry. These questions are primordial for the viability of stellarators and other non-axisymmetric magnetic confinement devices.

Turning to a more mathematical view of this paper, a first theoretical question is that of the global existence of an adapted 1-form
\eta
, for any flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
. In this work,
Theorem

I.5
was proved, guaranteeing for any flux-system the existence of an adapted 1-form in the toroidal region. This region is the most relevant for flux systems appearing in plasma physics. However, mathematically it is crucial to ask whether the toroidal region is the largest possible region on which
\eta
exists. The answer is no. Indeed, the flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
on
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
given in
Example

I.4
with

B=y\partial_{x}+(2x-4x^{3})\partial_{y}+\partial_{t},\qquad\nu=d\psi,\qquad\mu=dx\wedge dy\wedge dt

and
\psi=\tfrac{1}{2}y^{2}-x^{2}(1-x^{2})
, is such a counter-example. The toroidal region is
\mathcal{T}=M\backslash\{\psi=0\}
and yet
\eta=dt
is a 1-form adapted
(B,\nu,\mu)
which is defined globally. More generally, any flux-system satisfying the hypothesis of
Proposition

II.13
for which
M
is not the toroidal region serves as a counter-example. Besides including
Example

I.4
, this also includes contact systems and cosymplectic systems.

Having established the fact that
\eta
can exist on a larger region than the toroidal region, the natural question is then what is the largest region
\eta
can be guaranteed. Unfortunately, finding an adapted 1-form for a flux system in high generality appears difficult. One approach to start finding an adapted 1-form of a flux system
(B,\nu,\mu)
in a general setting can be roughly summarised as follows. On the non-critical set
U=\{\nu\neq 0\}
, assuming that
(B,\nu,\mu)
is tangential, the leaves
L
of the foliation generated by
\nu
in
U
have the property that
B
is divergence-free with respect to the area-form
\mu_{L}=L^{*}i_{N}\mu
in
L
where
N:U\to TM
is a vector field satisfying
\nu(N)=1
. To obtain an adapted 1-form in
U
, one needs a closed 1-form
\eta_{L}
on
L
such that
\eta_{L}(B)>0
. This problem is solved in this paper when
L
is a 2-torus by Kolmogorov’s Theorem
Perrella et al. 2022
. In general, finding
\eta_{L}
is purely about structural properties of non-vanishing, area preserving vector fields on
L
and thus may depend on the differential topology of
L
. Due to the non-vanishing and area preserving properties, the possible dynamics are reduced and hence the desired
\eta_{L}
for each
L
might be obtained. However, even if each
\eta_{L}
is obtained, one still needs to glue each of the
\eta_{L}
’s in
U
together. This involves the structure of the foliation and hence the structure of
\nu
. Restricting to the toroidal region made the gluing relatively easy because the
L
were compact and flowouts preserving the foliation could be used. Such tools are perhaps not usable for general
L
.

If
\eta
can be found on the region where
\nu
is non-critical, it still remains to show
\eta
exists on regions including the critical points. The most natural hypothesis for the critical region
K=\{\nu=0\}
(or a subsets thereof) is that it consists of integral curves of
B
which are open in the relative topology of
K
. If these integral curves are 1-dimensional and periodic and contained in
\interior M
, then as observed in
Section

V
there exists an adapted 1-form. This local construction has nothing to do with
\nu
or the toroidal region and can be done anywhere there is such an integral curve. An interesting consequence of these local adapted 1-forms, both a mathematically and physically, is that the work in
Burby et al. 2021
should directly imply that flux-coordinates exist for these more general periodic orbits under some Morse-Bott non-degeneracy assumptions. This remains to be carefully checked in the so-called hyperbolic case of their work.

However, this problem of gluing in general cannot be resolved. This was exhibited by the flux system in
Example

IV.3
in
Section

IV
. An interesting feature of this example is it in fact possesses a global symmetry despite there not existing any global adapted 1-form. In particular,
Theorem

I.3
is not the mechanism through which every symmetry of a flux system is generated. This shows that the problem of constructing a global adapted 1-form faces problems of orientation which are not faced by symmetries. Indeed, considering for instance a Reeb cylinder
(C,X)
where

C=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\times[-\pi/2,\pi/2],\qquad X=\sin y\partial_{x}+\cos y\partial_{y}

with standard coordinates
x,y
, we have that
\partial_{x}
is a symmetry of
X
and its integral curves are positively oriented with those of
X
along the boundary component
C_{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\times\{\pi/2\}
but negatively oriented with those along the boundary component
C_{0}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\times\{-\pi/2\}
. This is impossible for a closed 1-form to achieve: more precisely, as seen in
Proposition

IV.2
, there cannot be a closed 1-form
\eta_{C}
on
C
such that
\eta_{C}(X)>0
.

While adapted 1-forms are not the only mechanism producing symmetries,
Theorem

I.3
generates all symmetries of a certain type. More precisely, if
(B,\mu,\nu)
is a flux system with an adapted 1-form
\eta
, then
Theorem

I.3
there exists a vector field
u
, a positive function
f
and a 1-form
\eta^{\prime}
such that

i_{u}i_{B}\mu=\nu,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{u}f\mu=0,\qquad\eta^{\prime}(u)=0,\qquad\eta^{\prime}(B)>0

(6)

where of course, as discussed, the first two equations imply that
u
is a conformal symmetry of
B
. Under our assumptions,
\eta^{\prime}
may be taken as
\eta
and
f
as
\eta(B)
. Conversely, if one starts with a symmetry
u
for which there exists an
\eta^{\prime}
and an
f
as in Equation (
6
), then the 1-form
\eta=f\eta^{\prime}/\eta^{\prime}(B)
is an adapted 1-form. Indeed, we have
[u,B/f]=0
,
\eta(B)=f
, and
\eta(u)=0
so that

d\eta(u,B/f)=u(\eta(B)/f)-B(\eta(u))-\eta([u,B/f])=0-0-0=0

and consequently
d\eta\wedge\nu=0
, as required.

One necessary condition for a conformal symmetry
u
to posses such a 1-form
\eta^{\prime}
as in
Eq.

6
is that
u
vanishes at the critical points of
\nu
. However, this is not sufficient, as the following example illustrates.

Example VI.1
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, set
B=\partial_{x}
and
u=(y^{2}+z^{2})(\partial_{x}+z\partial_{y}-y\partial_{z})
. Then
u
and
B
are divergence-free with respect to the standard volume-form
\mu=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
and additionally satisfy

\iota_{u}\iota_{B}\mu=d\psi

where
\psi=-\frac{1}{4}(y^{2}+z^{2})^{4}
. The critical set of
\psi
is
\{y=0=z\}
which is nowhere dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
u
only vanishes on this critical set. However, there does not exist a 1-form
\eta^{\prime}
such that

\eta^{\prime}(u)=0,\qquad\eta^{\prime}(B)>0.

Indeed, letting
\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{1}dx+\eta^{2}dy+\eta^{3}dz
be a 1-form such that
\eta^{\prime}(u)=0
, then

0=\eta^{\prime}(u)=(y^{2}+z^{2})(\eta^{\prime}(B)+z\eta^{2}-y\eta^{3})

and consequently, because
\{y=0=z\}
is nowhere dense in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

\eta^{\prime}(B)+z\eta^{2}-y\eta^{3}=0

and hence
\eta^{\prime}(B)
must vanish on the critical set of
\psi
.

On the other hand,
(B,d\psi,\mu)
possesses the adapted 1-form
dx
and by
Theorem

I.3
, we must have a symmetry field
\tilde{u}
generated from
dx
. One finds that
\tilde{u}=(y^{2}+z^{2})(z\partial_{y}-y\partial_{z})=u-(y^{2}+z^{2})B
. Thus, in this instance, a modification of the symmetry
u
into another symmetry
\tilde{u}
was necessary for there to exist an
\eta^{\prime}
such that
\eta^{\prime}(\tilde{u})=0
and
\eta^{\prime}(B)>0
.

Although in
Example

VI.1
there was a way of modifying the given symmetry
u
into a symmetry
\tilde{u}
which posesses an
\eta^{\prime}
as in
Eq.

6
, this modification is not always possible: as
Example

IV.3
has no adapted 1-form, yet still possess a symmetry.

In conclusion, there is still much to understand about the global existence of
\eta
and more generally, on the global existence of a conformal symmetry
u
for a given flux-system. Future work would do well to investigate for which domains
\eta
or these conformal symmetries may exist. The global existence of a conformal symmetry, whether that is through
\eta
or not, is required to ensure the conformal Noether theorem demonstrated in this paper is as strong as the well-known Noether theorem in symplectic geometry.
