Multiplication by finite Blaschke factors on a general class of Hardy spaces
Apoorva Singh, Niteesh Sahni

Abstract
. A broader class of Hardy spaces and Lebesgue spaces have been introduced recently on the unit circle by considering continuous ∥ . ∥ 1 \|.\|_{1} -dominating normalized gauge norms instead of the classical norms on measurable functions and a Beurling type result has been proved for the operator of multiplication by the coordinate function. In this paper, we generalize the above Beurling type result to the context of multiplication by a finite Blaschke factor B ⁡ ( z ) B(z) and also derive the common invariant subspaces of B 2 ​ ( z ) B^{2}(z) and B 3 ​ ( z ) B^{3}(z) . These results lead to a factorization result for all functions in the Hardy space equipped with a continuous rotationally symmetric norm.

. Furthermore, in the recent past, the validity of the above results have been proved in the context of general Lebesgue

,

]
,

]
,
[

. For a finite Blaschke factor

, invariance in

In

-dominating normalized gauge norm

The statement of the general Beurling’s theorem in

Suppose

-dominating normalized gauge norm and

The key components of the proof for Theorem

In this paper, we study the invariance in a general class of Hardy spaces equipped with continuous

2.
Notation and Preliminary Results

Throughout the paper,
{\mathbb{D}}
stands for the open unit disk in the complex plane and it’s boundary, the unit circle, is denoted by
{\mathbb{T}}
. The classical Lebesgue space on the unit circle
L^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, is a Banach space under the norm

\|f\|_{p}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lvert f\rvert^{p}dm\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
dm
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{T}}
. The space
L^{\infty}
is a Banach space under the essential supremum norm

\|f\|_{\infty}=inf\{\;M\;:\;m\{e^{i\theta}:\lvert f(e^{i\theta})\rvert>M\}=0\}.

It is well known that any function
f\in L^{p}
can be represented as a Fourier series
f(z)=

\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
. The Fourier coefficients are given by
a_{n}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}fz^{-n}dm
. The Hardy space
H^{p}
is defined as the norm-closure (weak*-closure if
p=\infty
) of the analytic polynomials in
L^{p}
.
H^{p}
turns out to be a closed subspace of
L^{p}
which can be expressed as:

H^{p}=\{f\in L^{p}\;:\;a_{n}=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}fz^{-n}dm=0,\;\text{for all}\;n<0\}.

The space
H^{\infty}=H^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
is a Banach space under the essential supremum norm and it multiplies all
H^{p}
spaces back into
H^{p}
.

A norm
{\alpha}
on
L^{\infty}
is called a
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm if:

(1)
{\alpha}(1)=1
,

(2)
{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)={\alpha}(f)
for every
f\in L^{\infty}
,

(3)
{\alpha}(f)\geq\|f\|_{1}
for every
f\in L^{\infty}
.

Further, we say that a norm
{\alpha}
is continuous if

\lim\limits_{m(E)\rightarrow 0^{+}}{\alpha}(\chi_{E})=0,

where
\chi_{E}
stands for a characteristic function of
E\subset{\mathbb{T}}
.

Furthermore, we can also define
{\alpha}
norm over all the measurable functions
f
on
{\mathbb{T}}
as

{\alpha}(f)=\sup\{{\alpha}(s):s~\text{is a simple function},~0\leq s\leq\lvert f\rvert\}.

The space
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}=\{f:{\mathbb{T}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
measurable such that
{\alpha}(f)<\infty
} is a Banach space under the continuous
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm
{\alpha}
. The
{\alpha}
-closure of
L^{\infty}
is the general Lebesgue space
L^{\alpha}
. Some important facts about
L^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
spaces established in
[
3
]
are
L^{\infty}\subsetneq\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
, and
L^{\infty}\subsetneq L^{\alpha}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}\subset L^{1}
. The space
L^{\infty}
multiplies
L^{\alpha}
back into
L^{\alpha}
and the following inequality is satisfied:
{\alpha}(fg)\leq\|f\|_{\infty}{\alpha}(g)
for all
f\in L^{\infty}
and
g\in L^{\alpha}
. In fact this inequality holds for all
g\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}}
.

The
{\alpha}
-closure of
H^{\infty}
is denoted by
H^{\alpha}
and it is a closed subspace of
L^{\alpha}
. The general Hardy space
H^{\alpha}
is a Banach space under the norm
{\alpha}
. We also have a simpler description of
H^{\alpha}
proved in
[
3
]
, which is
H^{\alpha}=H^{1}\cap L^{\alpha}
. Further, we have an important relationship that
H^{\infty}\subsetneq H^{\alpha}\subset H^{1}
.

A special class of norms on
L^{\infty}
, called rotationally symmetric norms, are also
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norms. We say that a norm
{\alpha}
on
L^{\infty}
is rotationally symmetric if

(1)
{\alpha}(1)=1
,

(2)
{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)={\alpha}(f)
for every
f\in L^{\infty}
,

(3)
{\alpha}(f_{w})={\alpha}(f)
. Here
f_{w}(z)=f(\overline{w}z)
for each
w\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
f\in L^{\infty}
.

It has been proved in
[
5
]
that a rotationally symmetric norm
{\alpha}
must satisfy
\|.\|_{1}\leq{\alpha}(.)\leq\|.\|_{\infty}
. Therefore, these norms are also
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norms and are a special case of the same. Besides the
p
-norms
(1\leq p<\infty)
, other important examples of
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norms are presented in
[
4
]
.

A Blaschke factor with
n
zeros
{\alpha}_{1},{\alpha}_{2},\ldots,{\alpha}_{n}\in{\mathbb{D}}
is defined as

B(z)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{z-{\alpha}_{i}}{1-\overline{{\alpha}_{i}}z}.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
{\alpha}_{1}=0
(due to the conformal invariance of
H^{2}
). The operator of multiplication by
B(z)
denoted by
T_{B}
is an isometry on
H^{\alpha}
. This can be easily seen, since
\lvert B(z)\rvert=1
a.e on
{\mathbb{T}}
and
{\alpha}(Bf)
=
{\alpha}(\lvert Bf\rvert)
=
{\alpha}(\lvert B\rvert\lvert f\rvert)
=
{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)
=
{\alpha}(f)
. A closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
H^{\alpha}
is invariant under the operator
T_{B}
if
B\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{M}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under any subalgebra
A
of
H^{\infty}
, if
af\in\mathcal{M}
whenever
a\in A
and
f\in\mathcal{M}
. The set
H^{\infty}(B)
stands for the set
\{foB:f\in H^{\infty}\}
which turns out to be a subalgebra of
H^{\infty}
generated by
B
. We shall show in Lemma
2.5
that a closed subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
H^{\alpha}
is invariant under the operator
T_{B}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under the subalgebra
H^{\infty}(B)
. The weak*-closure of the linear span of
\{1,B^{2},B^{3},\ldots\}
is a subalgebra of
H^{\infty}
and is denoted by
H^{\infty}_{1}(B)
. It can be shown that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under
H^{\infty}_{1}(B)
if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under both
T_{B^{2}}
and
T_{B^{3}}
.

Put
B_{j}(z)
=
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{j}\dfrac{z-\alpha_{i}}{1-\overline{\alpha_{i}}z}
. In
[
24
]
, it has been proved that the collection
\left\{e_{jm}=\dfrac{\sqrt{1-{\lvert{\alpha}_{j+1}\rvert}^{2}}}{1-\overline{{\alpha}_{j+1}}z}B_{j}B^{m}:0\leq j\leq n-1,m=0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
is an orthonormal basis for
H^{2}
, and that
H^{2}
can be decomposed as an orthogonal direct sum

H^{2}=e_{00}H^{2}(B)\oplus e_{10}H^{2}(B)\oplus\cdots\oplus e_{n-1,0}H^{2}(B)

(2.1)

where
H^{2}(B)
is the
\|.\|_{2}
-closure of all the analytic polynomials in
B
. A similar decomposition has also been proved for
H^{p}
spaces
[
22
]
. Formally, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
H^{p}
is the algebraic direct sum
e_{00}H^{p}(B)\oplus e_{10}H^{p}(B)\oplus\cdots\oplus e_{n-1,0}H^{p}(B)
. Here
H^{p}(B)
is the
\|.\|_{p}
-closure of all the analytic polynomials in
B
.

The invariance under multiplication by
B(z)
is characterized in terms of
B
-inner functions which are more general than inner functions. Every inner function is a
B
-inner function for
B(z)=z
. An
H^{\infty}
function
\phi
is called
B
-inner if
\{B^{m}\varphi:m=0,1,2,\ldots\}
is an orthonormal set in
H^{2}
. In general, for an
r
tuple
(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{r})
of
H^{\infty}
functions, we can speak of a
B
-inner matrix
A
which is constructed as follows:

For each
1\leq j\leq r
, we can write
\varphi_{j}
as

\varphi_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}e_{i0}\;\varphi_{ij}
where
\varphi_{ij}\in H^{2}(B)
. The
n\times r
matrix given by
A=(\varphi_{ij})_{n\times r}
is a
B
-inner matrix if
A^{*}A=I
. In
[
24
]
, the condition
A^{*}A=I
has been shown to be equivalent to the fact that the set
\{B^{m}\varphi_{i}:1\leq i\leq r,m=0,1,2,\ldots\}
is orthonormal in
H^{2}
. This is in line with the characterization of classical inner functions and usually provides a convenient way of testing the
B
-inner condition. When
B(z)=z^{n}
, we shall call
B
-inner functions simply as
n
-inner functions.

Coming back to the basics of
L^{\alpha}
, the dual norm
{\alpha}^{\prime}
of
{\alpha}
on
L^{\infty}
is defined as:

{\alpha}^{\prime}(h)=\sup\Big\{\Big\lvert\int\limits_{{\mathbb{T}}}fhdm\Big\rvert
:
f\in L^{\infty}
and
{\alpha}(f)\leq 1\Big\}

=\sup\Big\{\int\limits_{{\mathbb{T}}}\lvert fh\rvert dm
:
f\in L^{\infty}
and
{\alpha}(f)\leq 1\Big\}
.

It has been shown that if
\alpha
is a
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm, or a rotationally symmetric norm, then
{{\alpha}}^{\prime}
is also a
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm
[
3
]
, or a rotationally symmetric norm
[
5
]
, respectively. We can now define the space
\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
along the same lines as
\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}}
. The dual space of
L^{\alpha}
can be identified with
\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
. The precise formulation of an isometric isomorphism is given in the proposition below:

Proposition 2.1

(Chen
[
3
]
)
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm (or, a continuous rotationally symmetric norm) and let
{\alpha}^{\prime}
be the dual norm of
{\alpha}
. Then
(L^{{\alpha}})^{\#}
=
\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
,i.e., for every
\phi\in(L^{\alpha})^{\#}
, there is an
h\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
such that
\|\phi\|={\alpha}^{\prime}(h)
and

\phi(f)=\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}fhdm

for all
f\in L^{\alpha}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Chen
[
3
]
)
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm on
\mathcal{L}^{\alpha}
and if
0\leq f_{1}\leq f_{2}\leq\ldots
and
f_{n}\rightarrow f\;a.e.
. Then,
{\alpha}(f_{n})\rightarrow{\alpha}(f)
.

Remark 2.3
.

In particular taking
{\alpha}=\|.\|_{1}
, we get the classical monotone convergence theorem.

The lemma below states a version of Holder’s inequality valid on
L^{\alpha}
. Its proof relies on the generalized monotone convergence theorem stated above (Proposition
2.2
).

Lemma 2.4
.

If
f\in L^{\alpha}
and
h\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
, then
fh\in L^{1}
. Further,
\|fh\|_{1}\leq{\alpha}(f){\alpha}^{\prime}(h)
.

Proof.

The proof is easy when
f
and
h
both belong to
L^{\infty}
. This can be seen through the following calculation:

\left\|\frac{f}{{\alpha}(f)}\;h\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{{\alpha}(f)}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\lvert fh\rvert dm\leq{\alpha}^{\prime}(h).

(2.2)

The inequality in (
2.2
) follows by the definition of
{\alpha}^{\prime}
.
It can also be re-phrased as
\|fh\|_{1}\leq{\alpha}(f)\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(h)
.

We now show the validity of inequality (
2.2
) for any
f\in L^{\alpha}
and
h\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
. Since
\lvert f\rvert
and
\lvert h\rvert
are measurable functions on
{\mathbb{T}}
, there exist increasing sequences of non-negative simple functions
{s_{n}}
and
{r_{n}}
respectively, such that
s_{n}\to\lvert f\rvert\;a.e.
and
r_{n}\to\lvert h\rvert\;a.e.
By Proposition
2.2
, we have
{\alpha}(s_{n})\to{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)
and
{\alpha}^{\prime}(r_{n})\to{\alpha}^{\prime}(\lvert h\rvert)
as
n\to\infty
. Thus,

{\alpha}(s_{n})\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(r_{n})\to{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(\lvert h\rvert)\;\;\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\to\infty.

Further, note that the sequence
s_{n}r_{n}\to\lvert fh\rvert\;a.e.
So, by the classical monotone convergence theorem, we have

\|s_{n}r_{n}\|_{1}\to\|fh\|_{1}\;\;\;as\;\;n\to\infty.

Applying the inequality (
2.2
), we get

\|s_{n}r_{n}\|_{1}\;\leq\;{\alpha}(s_{n})\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(r_{n}).

Finally, taking limit as
n\to\infty
, we conclude that for every
f\in L^{\alpha}
and
h\in\mathcal{L^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}}
:

\|fh\|_{1}\;\leq{\alpha}(\lvert f\rvert)\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(\lvert h\rvert)={\alpha}(f)\;{\alpha}^{\prime}(h).

∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating gauge norm. Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is a closed subspace of
H^{\alpha}
. Then,
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under
T_{B}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under the algebra
H^{\infty}(B)
.

Proof.

The condition
H^{\infty}(B)\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{M}
trivially implies the invariance of
\mathcal{M}
under the operator
T_{B}
. We now prove the converse.

Suppose
T_{B}(\mathcal{M})\subset\mathcal{M}
. Then,
(T_{B})^{n}\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{M}
for all
n=1,2,3,\ldots
This implies that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under multiplication by all polynomials in
B
. We shall show that
\mathcal{M}
is also invariant under multiplication by all functions in
H^{\infty}(B)
.
Let
k\in H^{\infty}(B)
, and
h\in\mathcal{M}
be arbitrary. We can find a sequence of Cesaro means
\{\sigma_{n}(B)\}
converging to
k
in the weak* topology. That is, for every
f\in L^{1}
:

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\sigma_{n}(B)f~dm\longrightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}kf~dm\;\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow\infty.

(2.3)

In view of Lemma
2.4
and equation (
2.3
) we conclude that for any non-zero element
u\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
:

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\sigma_{n}(B)hu~dm\longrightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}khu~dm\;\;\;\text{as}\;\;n\rightarrow\infty.

Note that
\sigma_{n}(B)
are polynomials in
B
, and hence
\sigma_{n}(B)h\in\mathcal{M}\subset L^{\alpha}
. Looking at the general form of any bounded linear functional on
L^{\alpha}
(Proposition
2.1
), it follows that
\sigma_{n}(B)h

\rightarrow

kh
weakly in
L^{\alpha}
. Because all closed subspaces are also weakly closed, we infer that
kh\in\mathcal{M}
. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Generalization of Invariant Subspace Theorem in
H^{\alpha}
space

In
[
22
]
, the closed subspaces of
H^{p}
for
0<p\leq\infty
, that are invariant under multiplication by all powers of
B
, except the first power has been characterized. We extend the result for a far more general Hardy spaces
H^{\alpha}
, where
{\alpha}
is
\|.\|_{1}
-dominating normalized gauge norm. Before stating our main result of this section, it is convenient to first present the proof of the invariance theorem explicitly for
H^{\infty}
space. The idea of the proof is new and fairly different as seen in
[
22
]
.

We first prove the following decomposition for

4.

For a finite Blaschke factor

Recall that

denotes the closed linear span of
\{B^{m}:m=0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
\oplus
is an algebraic direct sum. This direct sum becomes an orthogonal direct sum when
p=2

Consider
f\in H^{\alpha}
, and since
f\in H^{1}
, we can write
f(z)
as a Fourier series

f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(j)z^{j}

where
\hat{f}(j)
=
\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(z)z^{-j}dm
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots

Consider the
n
th Cesaro means

\sigma_{n}(f)=\frac{S_{0}(f)+S_{1}(f)+\cdots+S_{n}(f)}{n+1}

where
S_{k}(f)
=
\sum_{j=0}^{k}\hat{f}(j)z^{j}
stands for the
k
th partial sum.

The following lemma shows that any function in
H^{\alpha}
converge to it’s Cesaro means in
{\alpha}
-norm.

Lemma 4.1

(Chen
[
5
]
)
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous rotationally symmetric norm and

. Then
{\alpha}(\sigma_{n}(f)-f)\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
.

We now obtain a similar decomposition for
H^{\alpha}
in the context of the Blaschke factor

, when
{\alpha}
is a continuous rotationally symmetric norm.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous rotationally symmetric norm. We can decompose the Banach space

as

where

=
\left[H^{\infty}(z^{n})\right]_{\alpha}
and
\oplus
is the algebraic direct sum.

Proof.

Since
H^{\infty}(z^{n})\subset H^{\infty}\subset H^{\alpha}

. Observe that for any natural number
k
:
{\alpha}(z^{k}f)={\alpha}(\lvert z^{k}f\rvert)={\alpha}(f)
for every

M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus zM_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus z^{n-1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\subset H^{\alpha}

For the reverse inclusion, take an arbitrary

. We can write
f(z)
as a Fourier series

f(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hat{f}(j)z^{j}

where
\hat{f}(j)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(z)z^{-j}\;dm
for

Let

is a rotationally symmetric norm, so

For each

Next, a simple computation shows that the coefficient of

\hat{f}(m)\;\left[\frac{\omega^{n(j+m)}-1}{\omega^{j+m}-1}\right]\\
=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}0\;\;,&n\nmid{j+m}\\
\hat{f}(m)\;n\;,&n\mid j+m\;.\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

Equation (
4.1
) is valid for all

and all
j=1,2,\ldots,n
. Therefore

Thus

g_{n-j}=z^{n-j}h_{n-j}(z),

where

.
Because

, we can see that

.

We now claim that

.

Since
f(\omega^{k}z)\in H^{\alpha}
for all

and
\sigma_{l}(f(\omega^{k}z))
be the
l

. By Lemma
4.1
,

{\alpha}(\sigma_{l}(f(\omega^{k}z))-f(\omega^{k}z))\longrightarrow 0\;\;\;\text{as}\;\;l\to\infty.

A straightforward calculation shows that the sum

\sigma_{l}(f(z))+\omega^{j}\sigma_{l}(f(\omega z))+\omega^{2j}\sigma_{l}(f(\omega^{2}z))+\cdots+\omega^{(n-1)j}\sigma_{l}(f(\omega^{n-1}z)

.

Further, the sum in equation (

) converges in

to

Therefore,

.

That is,

\frac{1}{n}\kappa_{l}(z^{n})\longrightarrow h_{n-j}\;\text{as}\;l\to\infty\;\text{in }\;H^{\alpha}.

Hence the claim follows.
Therefore,

f=h_{0}+zh_{1}+\cdots+z^{n-1}h_{n-1}

\vdots\\

O
is outer. Hence,
\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}\in H^{\alpha}
. By Lemma
4.1
, the Cesaro means
\sigma_{l}\left(\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}\right)
converge to
\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}
in
H^{\alpha}
. Since
\sigma_{l}\left(\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}\right)
is a polynomial in
z^{n}
, so
\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
.

Hence,
f=J_{1}\dfrac{g_{1}}{O}+J_{2}\dfrac{g_{2}}{O}+\cdots+J_{r}\dfrac{g_{r}}{O}

\in J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
. This establishes that
W=J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
.

Hence equation (
4.3
) can be written as:

\mathcal{M}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\oplus\langle\varphi_{i}\rangle\right)\oplus z^{2n}\;(J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})).

∎

We now derive the
T_{z^{n}}
-invariant subspaces of
H^{\alpha}
, when
{\alpha}
is a continuous rotationally symmetric norm. The proof proceeds on similar lines as that of Theorem
4.5
, so we only present a sketch.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
{\alpha}
be a continuous rotationally symmetric norm and
\mathcal{M}
be a closed subspace of
H^{\alpha}
. Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is invariant under the operator
T_{z^{n}}
. Then there exist
n
-inner functions
J_{1},\ldots,J_{r}
(
r\leq n
) such that

\mathcal{M}=J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

Proof.

On the same lines as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, we can show that
\mathcal{M}\cap H^{\infty}
is a non-trivial weak*-closed subspace of
H^{\infty}
which is invariant under
T_{z^{n}}
.

By Theorem
3.4
, there exist
n
-inner functions
J_{1},\ldots,J_{r}
(with
r\leq n
) such that

\mathcal{M}\cap H^{\infty}=J_{1}H^{\infty}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}H^{\infty}(z^{n}).

Therefore,

[\mathcal{M}\cap H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}=[J_{1}H^{\infty}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}.

Further, in view of Lemma
3.11
it can be deduced that
[\mathcal{M}\cap H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}=\mathcal{M}
. Also, it has been established in Theorem
3.5
that
[J_{1}H^{\infty}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}=[J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}
.

So,
\mathcal{M}
takes the form:

\mathcal{M}=[J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}.

In order to show that
\mathcal{M}
takes the desired form it suffices to establish that

\mathcal{M}\subset J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

Note that for any
f\in M
, there exists an outer function
O
such that
Of
takes the form:

Of=J_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+J_{r}g_{r}~~~(\;g_{i}\in H^{\infty}(z^{n})\;).

Now, proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
4.5
, we find that

f=J_{1}\dfrac{g_{1}}{O}+J_{2}\dfrac{g_{2}}{O}+\cdots+J_{r}\dfrac{g_{r}}{O}
. This completes the proof because
\dfrac{g_{i}}{O}\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
.
∎

5.
General Inner-Outer Factorization in
H^{\alpha}

In this section, we derive the general inner-outer factorization of
H^{\alpha}
functions. The definition of inner and
n-
inner functions have been discussed beforehand.
The outer function in
H^{\alpha}
is studied by Chen in
[
5
]
. Let
f\in H^{\alpha}
is outer if
f
is a cyclic vector for
H^{\infty}
acting on
H^{\alpha}
which means
[f\cdot H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}=H^{\alpha}
.
We now define an
n
-outer function in
H^{\alpha}
.

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
{\alpha}
is continuous rotationally symmetric norm and
f\in H^{\alpha}
. We say
f
is
n
-outer function in
H^{\alpha}
if:

[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}=\;p(z)\;M_{\alpha}(z^{n})

(5.1)

where
p(z)
is a polynomial of degree less than
n
and
M_{\alpha}(z^{n})=[H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}
.

Remark 5.2
.

Observe that for any
f\in H^{\alpha}
,
[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}=\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}
where
\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}
is an
{\alpha}
-closed linear span of
\{f\cdot z^{nk}:k=0,1,\ldots\}
.

It is trivial to show that
\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}\subset[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}
.

For the reverse inclusion, let
g\in H^{\infty}(z^{n})
.
Then, the sequence of Cesaro means
\{\sigma_{m}(g)\}
converges to
g
in the weak* topology. Therefore,

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\sigma_{m}(g)hdm\longrightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}ghdm~~~~\forall~~h\in L^{1}.

Thus, for any non-zero element
u\in\mathcal{L}^{{\alpha}^{\prime}}
, we see that

\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\sigma_{m}(g)fudm\longrightarrow\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}gfudm.

Therefore, sequence
\{\sigma_{m}(g)f\}
converge weakly to
gf
in
L^{\alpha}
. But each
\sigma_{m}(g)
is a polynomial in
z^{n}
, so
\sigma_{m}(g)f\in\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}
. Since
\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}
, being a closed subspace of
H^{\alpha}
is also weakly closed, and hence
g\cdot f\in\bigvee\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\{f\cdot z^{nk}\}
.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that when
f
is
n
-outer function in
H^{\alpha}
such that
f\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
then

[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}=M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

This can be seen as follows:

Since
f
is
n
-outer in
H^{\alpha}
, so we can write
f(z)=p(z)g(z)
for some
g\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
. Since the
deg\;p(z)<n
, we can write
p(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\cdots+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}
. Therefore,

f(z)=a_{0}g(z)+a_{1}zg(z)+\cdots+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}g(z).

But
f\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
. This is possible only when
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n-1}
to be 0. Therefore
p(z)
is a constant polynomial and the result follows.

We now derive a factorization of
H^{\alpha}
functions into the sum of products of
n
-inner and
n
-outer functions. We use Theorem
4.5
to obtain a general inner-outer factorization of
H^{\alpha}
functions.

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose
{\alpha}
is continuous rotationally symmetric norm and
f\in H^{\alpha}
then

f=J_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+J_{r}f_{r}\;\;(r\leq n)

where, each
J_{i}
is
n
-inner and
f_{i}\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
is
n
-outer for all
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Proof.

Consider
\mathcal{M}=[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}
. Then
\mathcal{M}
is a closed subspace of
H^{\alpha}
which is invariant under
T_{z^{n}}
. By Theorem
4.5
, there exist
n
-inner functions
J_{1},\ldots,J_{r}
such that

\mathcal{M}=J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

Since
f\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
such that

f=J_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+J_{r}f_{r}.

We claim that each
f_{i}
is
n
-outer.

If possible assume that some
f_{i}
is not
n
-outer function, then

[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}\subsetneq M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

We can write

J_{i}[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}\subsetneq J_{i}M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

Note that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\subset[J_{1}f_{1}H^{\infty}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{i}f_{i}H^{\infty}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}f_{r}H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\subset[J_{1}[f_{1}\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{i}[f_{i}\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}[f_{r}\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\subsetneq[J_{1}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{i}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{r}M_{\alpha}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{[M]_{\alpha}}

i.e.,
\mathcal{M}\subsetneq\mathcal{M}
which is a contradiction. Therefore, each
f_{i}
is
n
-outer function and thus the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The factorization established in Theorem
5.4
is different to the one presented in
[
17
]
for classical
H^{p}
spaces. In
[
17
]
, the authors decomposed every
H^{p}
function as a product of
z^{n}
-
p
-inner and
p
-outer functions. Whereas, we are decomposing
H^{\alpha}
in terms of
n
-inner and
n
-outer functions.

A function
\varphi\in H^{\infty}
is called a
z^{n}
-
p
-inner function if
\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\varphi|^{p}z^{n}dm<\infty
. We have not attempted to define a concept on the similar lines for
H^{\alpha}
.

Corollary 5.6

(Singh and Thukral
[
24
]
)
.

Suppose
f
is in
H^{2}
then

f=\varphi_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+\varphi_{r}f_{r}\;\;(r\leq n)

where for each
i
,
f_{i}\in H^{2}(z^{2})
and is
n
-outer and
\varphi_{i}
is
n
-inner.

Theorem 5.7
.

Every
n
-outer function which belongs to
M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
is an outer function.

Proof.

Let
f\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
be an
n
-outer function. By Remark
5.2
, we have

[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}=M_{\alpha}(z^{n}).

(5.2)

In order to prove that
f
is an outer function, we need to show that
H^{\alpha}=[f\cdot H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}
.

It is trivial to note that
[f\cdot H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}\subset H^{\alpha}
. So only the reverse inequality needs to be established.

Let
h\in H^{\alpha}
and write
h
as

h=h_{1}+zh_{2}+\cdots+z^{n-1}h_{n}

for some
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in M_{\alpha}(z^{n})
.

By equation (
5.1
), we see that each
h_{i}\in[f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})]_{\alpha}
, hence there exists a sequence
\{fh_{k}^{(i)}\}\subset f\cdot H^{\infty}(z^{n})
such that

{\alpha}(fh_{k}^{(i)}-h_{i})\to 0\;\;\text{as}\;\;k\to\infty.

Therefore,

{\alpha}(z^{i}fh_{k}^{(i)}-z^{i}h_{i})\to 0\;\;\text{as}\;\;k\to\infty.

This means that

f\cdot\left(h_{k}^{(1)}+zh_{k}^{(2)}+\cdots+z^{n-1}h_{k}^{(n)}\right)\longrightarrow h\;\;\text{in}\;\;H^{\alpha}\;\;\text{as}\;\;k\to\infty.

Thus, we have
h\in[f\cdot H^{\infty}]_{\alpha}
. So the result follows.
∎
