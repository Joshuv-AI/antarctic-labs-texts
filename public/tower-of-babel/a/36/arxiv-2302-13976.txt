A Counterexample to the Lévy Flight Foraging Hypothesis in the Narrow Capture Framework Preprint: APS/123-QED
Justin C. Tzou, Leo Tzou

Abstract
The Lévy flight foraging hypothesis asserts that biological organisms have evolved to employ (truncated) Lévy flight searches due to such strategies being more efficient than those based on Brownian motion. However, we provide here a concrete two-dimensional counterexample in which Brownian search is more efficient. In fact, we show that the efficiency of Lévy searches worsens the farther the Lévy flight tail index deviates from the Brownian limit. Our counterexample is based on the framework of the classic narrow capture problem in which a random search is performed for a small target within a confined search domain. Our results are obtained via three avenues: Monte Carlo simulations of the discrete search processes, finite difference solutions and a matched asymptotic analysis of the elliptic (pseudo)-differential equations of the corresponding continuum limits. Asymptotic analysis of the Lévy search yields an expression for the average search time accurate to 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) , providing insights into how the latter is impacted by various features of the target and search domain.



I
Introduction

It is a widely held belief that random search algorithms using Lévy flights can find a target faster than using Brownian motion
Shlesinger and Klafter 1986
;
Viswanathan et al. 1999
;
Viswanathan et al. 2008
;
Sims et al. 2019

) generation of Lévy-like movement patterns in certain organisms. In

This so called “Lévy flight foraging hypothesis” forms the basis of many biological models (e.g.,
Viswanathan et al. 1996

;

;

) for maximizing the amount of sparsely spaced targets being captured relative to the distance traversed

;
Buldyrev et al. 2021
;
Levernier et al. 2021

In contrast to these existing works, we present an alternative means of quantifying efficiency via measuring the expected search time of a small,
stationary
target in a finite domain and provide an example in two-dimensions for which the Brownian search strategy is more efficient than strategies based a Lévy flight of any tail index
\alpha

\alpha
that worsens the farther
\alpha

The framework we employ for this comparison is consistent with that of first passage time problems for Brownian search (e.g.,
Redner 2001
;
Benichou et al. 2015
;
Bénichou and Voituriez 2014
;
Tejedor et al. 2009
) in a geometry motivated by the narrow capture problem used to model biological and ecological processes (e.g.,

;
Holcman and Schuss 2015
;
Holcman and Schuss 2014
;
Singer et al. 2006
;
Bressloff 2021a
;
Pillay et al. 2010
;
Kurella et al. 2015
;
Delgado et al. 2015

We emphasize here that, as is typical of narrow capture (and narrow escape) problems, we assume the search target is small and stationary. From a modeling perspective, the latter assumption of stationary targets means that, for example, in the modeling of animal foraging, we must assume that the search objective either does not move or moves very slowly in comparison to the searcher. The former scenario may arise when animals forage for patches of food (such as grass) or water sources. The latter scenario may be pertinent if the foraging predator diffuses on a much faster time scale than does the prey (see, e.g.,
Kurella et al. 2015
) so that the target may be approximated as stationary. Brownian search times of mobile targets are considered in, e.g.,
Tzou et al. 2014
;
Tzou and Kolokolnikov 2015
;
Lindsay et al. 2017
;
Gabel et al. 2012
;
Lawley and Miles 2019
.

We develop three different approaches to arrive at our result. First, we devise and implement a Monte Carlo simulation to calculate the expected search time of searches based on Lévy flight. Second, we implement a numerical method for solving (pseudo)-differential equations which yields detailed information about the expected search time as a function of initial position. We use this numerical solution to gain insight into the potential mechanism behind why Brownian searches appear to take less time than Lévy flights. Third, in §
V.2
, we develop a matched asymptotic analysis to derive leading order analytic predictions for the expected search times. We remark that the comprehensive set of results from these three different approaches provide more quantitative and qualitative insight than the analytic asymptotic estimate for the search time discussed recently in
Chaubet et al. 2022
.

The qualitative differences in the two paths are due to the probability distributions of their respective jump lengths. For the Brownian search, jump lengths are normally distributed with zero mean and variance of
\Delta t
sufficiently small, leading to the linear-in-time mean squared displacement
\langle|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^{2}\rangle\propto\Delta t
. In the Lévy search with tail index
0<\alpha<1
, jump lengths
|\mathbf{y}|
are given by
|\mathbf{y}|=(\Delta t)^{1/(2\alpha)}|\mathbf{k}|
, where
\mathbf{k}
is distributed according to a power-law distribution with tail
\sim|\mathbf{k}|^{-(2+2\alpha)}
(see, e.g.,
Metzler and Klafter 2000
;
Valdinoci 2009
;
Dipierro et al. 2022
;
Metzler and Klafter 2004
;
Benichou et al. 2015
and references therein). This leads to an unbounded mean squared displacement, and the super-linear scaling
\langle|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^{2\delta}\rangle^{1/\delta}\propto(\Delta t)^{1/\alpha}
for
\delta<\alpha
.

In the next section, we present our main findings and give possible reasons for the inferiority of Lévy search strategies within the narrow escape framework.

III
Monte Carlo Simulation of Lévy Flight Search on
\mathbb{T}^{2}

We describe here the Monte Carlo algorithm that we used to generate the simulated Lévy flight search times of the previous section.
The algorithm is motivated by the description of Lévy flights on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by Valdinoci
Valdinoci 2009
. We remark that the purpose of
Valdinoci 2009
is to describe the derivation of the (continuous) fractional heat equation from a discrete Lévy flight framework. It is this discrete framework that we invoke in §
V.1
to derive the pseudo-differential equation that we subject to analysis in §
V.2
, resulting in the main result (
1
). We base the simulations on this discrete framework. Other algorithms for simulating stable Lévy process are given in, e.g.,
Mantegna 1994
;
Weron and Weron 2005
;
Chambers et al. 1976
.

We describe the process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from which we can derive the process on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
simply by identifying
(n+r,m+s)\equiv(r,s)
for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
r,s\in(0,1)
. For the
(j+1)-th
step starting from
\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
with
j=0,1,2,\ldots
, the
j
-th displacement of the Lévy flight with tail index
0<\alpha<1
is given by
h\mathbf{k}
, where
h
is a sufficiently small parameter (in particular, sufficiently smaller than the size of the target) and
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is a random variable drawn from a power-law distribution with tail
\sim~|\mathbf{k}|^{-2-2\alpha}
(see §
III.1
). Since
\langle|\mathbf{k}|^{2\delta}\rangle
is bounded for
0<\delta<\alpha<1
, we have the scaling of the expected displacement
\langle|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^{2\delta}\rangle\propto h^{\delta}
, or
\langle|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^{2\delta}\rangle^{1/\delta}\propto h
. We then let
\Delta t=D_{\alpha}h^{2\alpha}
so that
\langle|\Delta\mathbf{x}|^{2\delta}\rangle^{2/\delta}\propto(\Delta t)^{1/\alpha}
as required. Here,
D_{\alpha}
is a constant chosen so that the process is consistent with Brownian motion in the limit
\alpha\to 1^{-}
(see (
26
) of §
V.1
). As mentioned above, from this jump process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
one can deduce the process on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
simply by modding out
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

In §
III.1
, we describe our process for sampling from a discrete power law distribution. We then describe, in §
III.2
, the Monte carlo simulation of the search process from which we generate the Monte Carlo estimates for search times in Fig.
2
.

III.1
Rejection sampling algorithm for
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}

We describe here the rejection sampling algorithm that we use to draw samples
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
from the discrete power law distribution

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{cases}0&\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}\\
C_{\alpha}|\mathbf{k}|^{-2-2\alpha}&\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}\end{cases}\,,

(2a)

where
C_{\alpha}
is the normalizing constant given by

C_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,,\,\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{0}}|\mathbf{k}|^{-2-2\alpha}}\,,

(2b)

where
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

First observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\tilde{C}_{\alpha}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}}(\mathbf{k})\,,

(3)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\tilde{C}_{\alpha}|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}^{-2-2\alpha}
for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
,
\tilde{C}_{\alpha}
is the normalization constant (given below), and
|\cdot|_{\infty}
is the
\ell^{\infty}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}}
serves as a good proposal distribution for rejection sampling because the process of sampling from it is relatively simple, which we describe now.

The distribution
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}(k)
depends purely on the
\ell^{\infty}
norm of the random variable
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. As such we observe for each fixed
\hat{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, a random variable
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{k}=\hat{\mathbf{k}})=\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}=|\hat{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty})\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{k}=\hat{\mathbf{k}}\mid|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}=|\hat{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty})\,,

where
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{k}=\hat{\mathbf{k}}\mid|\mathbf{k}|_{\infty}=|\hat{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty})
is uniformly distributed amongst the
8|\hat{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty}
points having
\ell^{\infty}
norm
|\hat{\mathbf{k}}|_{\infty}
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, using the explicit form of
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}_{\alpha}
and the fact there are
8n
points on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
having
\ell^{\infty}
norm
n
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|=n)=\tilde{C}_{\alpha}8n/n^{2+2\alpha}=\tilde{C}_{\alpha}8/n^{1+2\alpha}\,,

(4)

where
\tilde{C}_{\alpha}
is given by

\tilde{C}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}8n^{-1-2\alpha}}\,.

(5)

This distribution can be sampled from using inversion sampling for discrete distributions.

Remark 1

We observe that in the special case when
\alpha=1/2
we can derive explicit analytic expressions for
\mathbb{P}(|k|=n)
. Indeed, we can sum over
n
in (
4
) to yield the condition

1=8\tilde{C}_{1/2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3}\tilde{C}_{1/2}.

That is,
\tilde{C}_{1/2}=\frac{3}{4\pi^{2}}
. Inserting this back to (
4
) we get, for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\mathbf{k}|=n)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\,,

(6)

for
\alpha=1/2
.

The rejection sampling algorithm for the discrete power law distribution
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
is thus as follows:

1.

sample
n\in\{1,\dots,10000\}
from (
4
) using inversion sampling;

2.

for this
n\in\mathbb{N}
sample
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
uniformly from the
8n
points on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
have
\ell^{\infty}
norm
n
;

3.

for this
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, sample
r\in\left(0,\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\tilde{C}_{\alpha}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}}(\mathbf{k})\right)
uniformly. If
r\leq\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
, accept this
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. If not, reject and repeat.

Numerical experiments show that this rejection sampling algorithm accepts approximately
69\%
of the time.

III.2
Monte Carlo algorithm

We describe here the Monte Carlo algorithm used to simulate Lévy flight searches.Let
\mathbb{T}^{2}
be the flat torus
[0,1]\times[0,1]
with periodic boundary conditions, and
u_{\epsilon}^{disc}(\mathbf{x})
be the expected discrete Lévy flight search time (obtained via simulation of a discrete process) of a circular target
B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{0})
of radius
\epsilon>0
centered at
(1/2,1/2)
starting from
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Then for a Lévy flight tail index
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
h>0
sufficiently small, we perform the following Monte Carlo procedure to compute an approximation of
u_{\epsilon}^{disc}(\mathbf{x})
:

Set
T=0
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
.

Repeat the following until
\mathbf{x}\in B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{0})
:

1.

sample
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
from
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}
using the above rejection sampling algorithm;

2.

set
\mathbf{y}:=(y_{1},y_{2})=\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{k}
;

3.

set
\mathbf{x}=(y_{1}\bmod\lfloor y_{1}\rfloor,y_{2}\bmod\lfloor y_{2}\rfloor)
, which accounts for the periodic boundary conditions of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
;

4.

set
T=T+\Delta t
, where
\Delta t=D_{\alpha}h^{2\alpha}
, with
D_{\alpha}
is given in (
26
) of §
V.1
.

The
j
-th run of the above generates a stopping time
T_{j}
. After executing
M
runs and generating stopping times
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{M}
we calculate

\displaystyle u_{\epsilon}^{disc}(\mathbf{x})\approx\frac{T_{1}+T_{2}+\dots+T_{M}}{K}\,,

(7)

for large
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Repeating this process over a grid of points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and then averaging, we are able to obtain an approximation of the global mean first passage time (GMFPT); i.e., the spatial average of
u_{\epsilon}^{disc}(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

IV
The Elliptic (Pseudo)-Differential Equations

In this section, we briefly discuss the elliptic pseudo-differential equation satisfied by
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
, the MFPT of the Lévy search starting from point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
in the continuous limit. Adopting the electrostatics approach of
Redner 2001
for Brownian searches, we show in §
V.1
that
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
satisfies the
exterior problem

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}=-1\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})},\ \ u_{\varepsilon}=0\ {\rm on}\ \overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\,,

(8a)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
is the fractional Laplacian of order
\alpha
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
given by

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\times\\
\int\limits_{[0,1]\times[0,1]}\!\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,,

(8b)

where we have identified
\mathbb{T}^{2}
isomorphically with
[0,1)\times[0,1)
so that the expression
|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|
is well-defined.

We remark that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
converge to that of the usual (local) Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
. Furthermore, for a Lévy flight on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
would simply become the standard fractional Laplacian of order
\alpha
given in (
8b
) of §
V.1
. The lattice sum kernel of (
8a
) sums the probabilities of all the possible paths from
\mathbf{y}
to
\mathbf{x}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. By analogy with
Redner 2001
;
Kurella et al. 2015
, we have that the second moment of the MFPT (SMFPT),
w_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
, satisfies

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}w_{\varepsilon}=-2u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})},\ \ w_{\varepsilon}=0\ {\rm on}\ \overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\,.

(8c)

The boundary value problems for the Brownian search time (
v_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
) and SMFPT (
\tau_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
) are well-known (
Redner 2001
):

\Delta v_{\varepsilon}=-1\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})},\ \ v_{\varepsilon}=0\ {\rm on}\ \partial B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,,

(9a)

\Delta\tau_{\varepsilon}=-2v_{\varepsilon}\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})},\ \ \tau_{\varepsilon}=0\ {\rm on}\ \partial B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,.

(9b)

The
variance
of the MFPT (VMFPT) is then given by
\textrm{VMFPT}=\textrm{SMFPT}-\textrm{MFPT}^{2}
, plotted in Fig.
4(b)
. Finite difference solutions of (
8a
)-(
9b
) are straightforward, although discretizing the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
is computationally expensive. As such, asymptotic methods for equations of the forms (
8a
) and (
8c
), such as that provided in §
V.2
, may be helpful in reducing computation requirements.

V
Asymptotic solution of
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})

In §
V.1
, we provide a derivation of the pseudo-differential equation (
8a
) for
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
starting from a random walk formalism. In §
V.2
, we provide an two-term asymptotic solution for
u_{\varepsilon}
leading to the main result (
1
) using a matched asymptotic analysis. Through this analysis, we highlight how effects of target and search domain geometry enter into the expression for the GMFPT. In §
V.3
, we discuss how this analysis extends to the flat 3-D torus as well as closed 2-D manifolds.

V.1
Derivation of elliptic (pseudo)-differential equations for average Brownian and Lévy search times

We begin with the derivation of (
8
), the pseudo-differential equation for the average search time via Lévy flight of a circular target of radius
\varepsilon
starting from some point
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
. The derivation is based on a continuum limit of a discrete process, and is given in
Redner 2001
for a Brownian search, which we reproduce first for the purpose of completeness.

Consider a Brownian particle on an
N\times N
grid with spacing
h=1/(N-1)
. At regular intervals of
\Delta t
, the particle hops from its current location
(x_{1},x_{2})
to one of its four neighboring points,
(x_{1}+h,x_{2})
,
(x_{1}-h,x_{2})
,
(x_{1},x_{2}+h)
, and
(x_{1},x_{2}-h)
with equal probability. Here the addition by
\pm h
are understood to be carried out mod
1
. Let
v_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
be the expected search time starting from
(x_{1},x_{2})
. Then
v_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
must be the average of the expected search time starting from one of its four neighboring points, plus the
\Delta t
traversal time. That is,

v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{4}\left[v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}+h,x_{2})+v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-h,x_{2})+\right.\\
\left.v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2}+h)+v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2}-h)\right]+\Delta t\,.

(10)

Dividing (
10
) by
\Delta t
and rearranging, we have

\frac{1}{4\Delta t}\left[v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}+h,x_{2})+v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1}-h,x_{2})+v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2}+h)\right.\\
\left.+v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2}-h)-4v_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})\right]+1=0\,.

(11)

Taking the limit
h\to 0
and
\Delta t\to 0
in (
11
) while maintaining
h^{2}/(4\Delta t)=1
, we obtain

\Delta v_{\varepsilon}+1=0\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\,;\qquad v_{\varepsilon}=0\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\partial B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\,,

(12)

where the boundary condition of (
12
) is the statement that the search time starting from the boundary of the target is zero. For the unit torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
v_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
would satisfy periodic conditions on the boundary.

Following the idea of
Valdinoci 2009
, we now generalize this derivation to Lévy flights characterized by a jump length distribution whose tail decays according to the power law
\sim|\mathbf{k}|^{-2-2\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
. For the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1)\times[0,1)
, we have the same
N\times N
grid with spacing
h=1/(N-1)
. Instead of (
10
) which allows only for nearest-neighbor jumps, we have

u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{i}})=\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{j}})+\Delta t\,.

(13)

In (
13
),
{\mathbf{i}}=(i_{1},i_{2})\in[0,N-1]\times[0,N-1]
,
{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},j_{2})
, and
w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})
is a sum of the probabilities of all possible paths from
h{\mathbf{i}}
to
h{\mathbf{j}}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, taking into account that there are an infinite number of ways to travel from one point to another via a straight line owing to the periodic nature of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. From
Valdinoci 2009
, a jump of
h\mathbf{k}
has the probability given by
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
, where the discrete probability mass function
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is given by (
2
) of §
III.1
.

The probability of reaching
h{\mathbf{i}}
from
h{\mathbf{j}}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
must then be given by the lattice sum

w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}C_{\alpha}|{\mathbf{i}}-({\mathbf{j}}+\mathbf{m}(N-1))|^{-2-2\alpha}\,,

(14)

where
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},m_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and we have tiled
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the unit square, and
{\mathbf{j}}+\mathbf{m}(N-1)
is the point corresponding to
{\mathbf{j}}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
in the square with bottom left vertex at
\mathbf{m}(N-1)
. In (
14
),
C_{\alpha}
is the normalization constant of (
2b
). Since

\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}C_{\alpha}|{\mathbf{i}}-\mathbf{k}|^{-2-2\alpha}=1\,,

(15)

(
13
) can be rewritten

\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})(\mathbf{k})\left[u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{j}})-u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{i}})\right]+\Delta t=0\,.

(16)

Using the formal scaling law of
Valdinoci 2009
,

\Delta t=D_{\alpha}h^{2\alpha}\,,

(17)

for some constant
D_{\alpha}
to be determined, we divide both sides of (
16
) by
\Delta t
to obtain

\frac{1}{D_{\alpha}}h^{2}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}w(h{\mathbf{i}},h{\mathbf{j}})\left[\frac{u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{j}})-u_{\varepsilon}({h{\mathbf{i}}})}{h^{2\alpha+2}}\right]+1=0\,.

(18)

Using (
14
) in (
18
), we obtain

\frac{C_{\alpha}}{D_{\alpha}}h^{2}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}|{\mathbf{i}}-({\mathbf{j}}+\mathbf{m}(N-1))|^{-2-2\alpha}\left[\frac{u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{j}})-u_{\varepsilon}(h{\mathbf{i}})}{h^{2\alpha+2}}\right]+1=0\,.

(19)

Letting
\mathbf{x}\equiv h{\mathbf{i}}
and
\mathbf{y}\equiv h{\mathbf{j}}
, and recalling that
h=1/(N-1)
, (
19
) becomes

\frac{C_{\alpha}}{D_{\alpha}}h^{2}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left[\frac{u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\right]+1=0\,.

(20)

In the limit
h\to 0^{+}
, (
20
) is the Riemann sum approximation to the pseudo-differential equation

\frac{D_{\alpha}}{C_{\alpha}}\int\limits_{[0,1]\times[0,1]}\!\left[u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\right]\\
\times\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}+1=0\,,

(21)

which is the desired form given in (
8b
).

To motivate the selection of
D_{\alpha}
, we rewrite (
21
) in terms of the fractional Laplacian defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by considering the periodic extension of
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we denote
U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
U_{\varepsilon}(x_{1},x_{2})=u_{\varepsilon}((x_{1}\bmod 1,x_{2}\bmod 1))
. The equation (
21
) can now be written

\frac{D_{\alpha}}{C_{\alpha}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}+1=0\,,

(22)

It is shown in
Valdinoci 2009
that the integral term in (
22
) is well defined as a principal value integral when
\mathbf{x}
is near
\mathbf{y}
:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})}\!\frac{U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,,

(23)

where
B_{\delta}(\mathbf{y})
is the ball of radius
\delta
centered at
\mathbf{y}
. With the fractional Laplacian in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,,

(24)

(
22
) becomes

-\frac{D_{\alpha}}{C_{\alpha}}\frac{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}(-\Delta)^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+1=0\,.

(25)

Then setting

D_{\alpha}=C_{\alpha}\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\,,

(26)

so that the coefficient in front of
(-\Delta)^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
is equal to one, we finally arrive at

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+1=0\,.

(27)

Returning to
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, we recover (
8
).

In (
26
),
C_{\alpha}
is the normalization constant of (
2b
). Note that the fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
can be defined in terms of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)[\bm{\xi}]
by
\mathcal{F}((-\Delta)^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x}))[\bm{\xi}]=-|\xi|^{2\alpha}\mathcal{F}(f(\mathbf{x}))[\bm{\xi}]
. Thus, as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, the Fourier multiplier of
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
approach those of the usual Laplacian operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Note that the Fourier multiplier definition of the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
also allows us to deduce that the operator in (
21
) is also the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
as defined via a spectral decomposition. Indeed, it is well known that
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}
are eigenfunctions for the Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. To compute the action of the operator in (
21
) on
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}
, we extend it periodically as smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and apply the operator in (
22
) to the extension. Observing that it has a Fourier multiplier representation, we see that
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}
gets mapped to
-|\mathbf{k}|^{2\alpha}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot x}
via standard Fourier transform calculations. Therefore, the operator in (
21
) has the same eigenfunctions as the Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
and the corresponding eigenvalues are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian raised to the power of
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

V.2
Asymptotic derivation of leading order Lévy flight search time

In this section, we provide an asymptotic derivation of the leading order behavior of
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
in (
1
); i.e., the spatial average of
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
. We begin with the elliptic pseudo-differential equation (
8
) for
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
, where

B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
denotes the ball of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

To simplify notation, we choose a coordinate system which identifies
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with
[0,1)\times[0,1)
so that
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(1/2,1/2)
, without loss of generality. This will ensure that the distance
d_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\cdot,\cdot)
function on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
satisfies
d_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\cong[0,1)\times[0,1)
.

In the inner region, we let

\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})/\varepsilon\,,\qquad u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0}+\varepsilon\mathbf{z})\sim U(\mathbf{z})\,.

In the inner variable
\mathbf{z}
, we now show that
A_{\alpha}\sim-\varepsilon^{-2\alpha}(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, where
-\varepsilon^{-2\alpha}(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}
is the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to the
\mathbf{z}
variable. To see this, we apply
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
to
f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))
, which results in

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))=\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\\
\times\int\limits_{[0,1]\times[0,1]}\!\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))-f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))}{|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,.

(28)

To obtain an expression in the form of the fractional Laplacian (
24
) from (
28
), we manipulate the denominator in the sum to obtain

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2+2\alpha}}\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\int\limits_{[0,1]\times[0,1]}\!\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))-f(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))}{|\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})-(\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{0})+\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,.

(29)

Substituting
\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{0})\to\mathbf{Y}
and
\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0})\to\mathbf{X}
, (
29
) becomes

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\alpha}}\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\\
\times\int\limits_{R_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\!\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\frac{f({\mathbf{Y}})-f({\mathbf{X}})}{|{\mathbf{X}}-({\mathbf{Y}}+\varepsilon^{-1}\mathbf{m})|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,,

(30)

where the region of integration
R_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})
is the square of side length
\varepsilon^{-1}
centered at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Now in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, only the
\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{0}
term in the sum of (
30
) contributes at a nonzero term, while the region of integration approaches
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, yielding

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\mathbf{X})\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\alpha}}\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\pi|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{f({\mathbf{Y}})-f({\mathbf{X}})}{|{\mathbf{X}}-{\mathbf{Y}}|^{2+2\alpha}}\,d{\mathbf{Y}}\,.

(31)

Comparing to (
24
), we find that the right-hand side of (
31
) is simply the fractional Laplacian with respect to the rescaled
{\mathbf{X}}=\varepsilon^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}))
variable, scaled by a factor of
\varepsilon^{-2\alpha}
. That is,
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\sim-\varepsilon^{-2\alpha}(-\Delta_{\mathbf{X}})^{\alpha}
, as required.

We now expand
U\sim\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2}U_{0}+\varepsilon^{2\alpha}U_{1}
, an expansion which we motivate below, so that the leading order term of the inner solution
U_{0}
satisfies the radially symmetric exterior problem on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

-(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}U_{0}=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,,\quad U_{0}=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,;

(32a)

U_{0}\sim S_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{2-2\alpha}}+\chi_{\alpha}\right)\ {\rm as}\ |\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(32b)

where
S_{\alpha}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant to be found,
\chi_{\alpha}
is a constant that depends on
\alpha
and, in general, the geometry of the target. We show below that (
32
) may be reformulated as an integral equation on
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
(i.e., the domain obtained by rescaling the target by
\varepsilon^{-1}
to
\mathcal{O}(1)
size). For the special case here where
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
is the unit ball, we then refer to
Kahane 1981
for an explicit solution of this integral equation, which in turn yields an explicit expression for
\chi_{\alpha}
.

In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
where the target size shrinks to zero, the exterior problem for
u_{\varepsilon}
in (
8a
) becomes the pseudo-differential equation in the punctured domain
\mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus~\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}
,

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}=-1\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}\,,

(33a)

with a prescribed local behavior near
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, which is given by the far-field behavior of
U\sim\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2}U_{0}
in (
32b
),

u_{\varepsilon}\sim\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2}S_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-2\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}+\chi_{\alpha}\right)\kern 5.0pt\mbox{as}\kern 5.0pt\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_{0}\,

(33b)

where
d_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the distance on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with respect to the flat metric. We remark that, with
S_{\alpha}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
, (
33b
) indicates that
u_{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
in the outer region, which must be the case given that (
33a
) is independent of
\varepsilon
. This requirement is what motivated the expansion for
U
in the inner region.

Note also that (
33b
) prescribes the singular structure of
u_{\varepsilon}
near
\mathbf{x}_{0}
as well as its regular part at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. Along with (
33a
), (
33b
) suggests that
u_{\varepsilon}
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
may be expressed in terms of the source-neutral Green’s function
G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})
,

u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\sim G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})+\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}\,,

(34)

where
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
is the spatial average of
u_{\varepsilon}
, while
G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})
satisfies

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}G_{\alpha}=-1+\delta(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|)\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}\,;\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\!G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})\,d\mathbf{x}=0\,;

(35a)

G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})\sim-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}+R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|)\kern 5.0pt\mbox{as}\kern 5.0pt\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_{0}\,;\qquad c_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{4^{\alpha}\pi\Gamma(\alpha)}\,.

(35b)

We remark that the pseudo-differential equation of (
35a
) is consistent, since the right-hand side integrates to zero over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. The integral condition of (
35a
) is required to uniquely specify
G_{\alpha}
since the constant function lies in the nullspace of
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
. In (
35b
), the coefficient
c_{\alpha}
is obtained simply by replacing
\alpha
by
-\alpha
in (
24
). In particular, the leading order singular term of
G_{\alpha}
in (
35b
) is identical to the free space Green’s function of (
42
) below. The regular part of the Green’s function, denoted
R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})
in (
35b
), is given by

R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})=\lim_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_{0}}G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})+\frac{c_{\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}\,.

(36)

In fact, if we set
\rho,\tilde{\rho}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\rho(t)=1
for
t\in(-1/8,1/8)
and
{\rm supp}(\rho)\subset(-1/4,1/4)
and
\tilde{\rho}(t)=1
for
t\in(-1/4,1/4)
and
{\rm supp}(\tilde{\rho})\subset(-1/2,1/2)
, we can write

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\frac{c_{\alpha}\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})}{|\cdot-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\frac{c_{\alpha}\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})}{|\cdot-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(1-\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\frac{c_{\alpha}\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})}{|\cdot-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}\right)\,,

where
\rho(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})=\rho(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2})
and same for
\tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})
. Observe that since
\left(1-\tilde{\rho}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})\right)
and
\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})
has disjoint support, the second term is infinitely smooth by the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
is given by an integral kernel which is smooth away from the diagonal. For the first term, we can directly compute using normal coordinates centered at
\mathbf{x}_{0}
to obtain

-\left(\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\frac{c_{\alpha}\rho(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{0})}{|\cdot-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\delta(|\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}|)+C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

We may then write
G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})=-\frac{c_{\alpha}\rho(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{0})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}+R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})
where
R_{\alpha}(\cdot;\mathbf{x}_{0}):\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}R_{\alpha}(\cdot;\mathbf{x}_{0})=-1+C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
. It can then be shown (see Ch. 10 of
Taylor 2013
) that
R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})
is infinitely smooth on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

To determine
S
and
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
, we perform a leading order matching of the local behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
in (
34
) to the required singularity structure of (
33b
). That is, from (
34
) and (
35b
), we have that as
\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_{0}
,

-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}+R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})+\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=\\
\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2}S_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2-2\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2-2\alpha}}+\chi_{\alpha}\right)\,.

(37)

Matching
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{2\alpha-2})
and
\mathcal{O}(1)
, we arrive at
S_{\alpha}=c_{\alpha}
, and the GMFPT of
u_{\varepsilon}
,

\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2}\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{4^{\alpha}\pi\Gamma(\alpha)}\chi_{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})\,.

(38)

We note that the leading order term (
38
) depends on the geometry of target through the constant
\chi_{\alpha}
to be computed below. This is in contrast to the 2-D narrow capture problem with Brownian motion, where target geometry effects enter only at the
\mathcal{O}(1)
correction term. Global geometric properties of the search domain are contained in the
\mathcal{O}(1)
correction term of (
38
) through the regular part of the Green’s function (
36
).
We remark also that the dependence of search time on the starting location
\mathbf{x}
enters only at
\mathcal{O}(1)
through
G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})
in (
34
), which is subdominant to the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2})
constant term
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
. Thus, in the
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
limit considered here, the better search strategy will always be the one for which
\alpha
is closer to the Brownian limit of 1, regardless of from where the search begins.

To determine
\chi_{\alpha}
, we require the solution to (
32
). Letting
U_{0}(\mathbf{z})=S_{\alpha}u(\mathbf{z})
in (
32
), we have for
u(\mathbf{z})
,

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,,\quad u=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,.

(39a)

u\sim-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{2-2\alpha}}+\chi_{\alpha}\kern 5.0pt{\rm as}\kern 5.0pt|\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(39b)

where
-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
is with respect to the
\mathbf{z}
variable. Note that we have normalized the coefficient of
|\mathbf{z}|^{2\alpha-2}
in the far-field behavior (
39b
), which leaves
\chi_{\alpha}
as the parameter to be determined.

We next let

v(\mathbf{z})=u(\mathbf{z})-\chi_{\alpha}\,,

so that
v
satisfies

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}v=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,,\quad v=-\chi_{\alpha}\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,;

(40a)

v\sim-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{2-2\alpha}}\kern 5.0pt{\rm as}\kern 5.0pt|\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(40b)

The exterior problem (
40
) for
v(\mathbf{z})
may be reformulated as the following problem over all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
without boundary,

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}v=\frac{1}{c_{\alpha}}f(\mathbf{z})\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\,;\quad f(\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,;

(41a)

v\equiv-\chi_{\alpha}\kern 5.0pt\mbox{on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,;\quad v\sim-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{2-2\alpha}}\kern 5.0pt{\rm as}\kern 5.0pt|\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(41b)

where
c_{\alpha}
is the constant given in (
35b
), and
f(\mathbf{z})
is an unknown function to be found by imposing the first condition in (
41b
). We now derive this integral equation for
f(\mathbf{z})
.

First, the free space Green’s function
G_{f}(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{0})
with source centered at the origin satisfying

-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}G_{f}=\delta(\mathbf{z})\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{2}\,;

(42a)

G_{f}(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{0})\to 0\kern 5.0pt{\rm as}\kern 5.0pt|\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(42b)

is given by

G_{f}(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{0})=-c_{\alpha}\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{2-2\alpha}}\,,

(43)

where
c_{\alpha}
is given in (
35b
). Then the solution
v(\mathbf{z})
of (
41
) may be written as a convolution of the right-hand side of (
41
) with
G_{f}(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{0})
of (
43
), which yields

v(\mathbf{z})=-\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{f(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{2-2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,,

(44)

where the region of integration in (
44
) is only over
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
because
f(\mathbf{z})
is compactly supported in
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
(see (
41a
)). To impose the normalizing condition on
v
given in the far-field condition(
41b
), we expand the kernel as
|\mathbf{z}|\to\infty
in (
44
) as
|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{2\alpha-2}\sim|\mathbf{z}|^{2\alpha-2}+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{z}|^{2\alpha-3})
, noting that
|\mathbf{y}|\leq 1
by virtue of the region of integration. Substituting this leading order expansion into (
44
) and comparing to the required far-field behavior of
v(\mathbf{z})
in (
41b
), we obtain the normalizing condition for
f(\mathbf{z})
,

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!f(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}=1\,.

(45a)

Next, we require that
v(\mathbf{z})\equiv-\chi_{\alpha}
for
\mathbf{z}\in B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
, yielding

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{f(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{2-2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}=\chi_{\alpha}\,,\quad\mathbf{z}\in\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,.

(45b)

The integral equation (
45b
) together with the normalizing condition (
45a
) are to be solved simultaneously for
f(\mathbf{z})
and
\chi_{\alpha}
. To determine an explicit solution to (
45
), we appeal to the result of
Kahane 1981
(see Theorem 3.1, and, in particular, (3.37)), which states that for
0<\beta<1
and any
\mathbf{z}
such that
|\mathbf{z}|\leq 1
,

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{2\beta}}\frac{1}{(1-|\mathbf{y}|^{2})^{1-\beta}}d\mathbf{y}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sin(\beta\pi)}\,.

(46a)

Identifying
\beta
in (
46a
) with
1-\alpha
, and noting that

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{1}{(1-|\mathbf{y}|^{2})^{1-\beta}}\,d\mathbf{y}=\frac{\pi}{\beta}\,,

(46b)

we find from (
46
) that

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{2-2\alpha}}\frac{(1-\alpha)/\pi}{(1-|\mathbf{y}|^{2})^{\alpha}}d\mathbf{y}=\\
\frac{1-\alpha}{\pi}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\sin((1-\alpha)\pi)}\,.

(47)

Comparing (
46a
) with (
45
), we find that the solution to (
45
) is given by

f(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1-\alpha}{\pi(1-|\mathbf{z}|^{2})^{\alpha}}\,,\qquad\chi_{\alpha}=\frac{\pi(1-\alpha)}{\sin((1-\alpha)\pi)}\,.

(48)

Substituting
\chi_{\alpha}
from (
48
) into the expression for the GMFPT
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
in (
38
), with
0<\alpha<1
, we arrive at
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
as given in (
1
).

For the GMFPT
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}^{(L)}
on the flat 2-D torus of side length
L
with a circular target of radius
\varepsilon
, a simple rescaling shows that
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}^{(L)}=L^{2}\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
where
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
is the GMFPT on the flat torus of unit side length given in (
1
). This scaling was obtained empirically through particle simulations in
Levernier et al. 2018
. In contrast, our systematic approach via a asymptotic analysis resulting in (
1
) yields not only the
\alpha
-dependent prefactor in the leading order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2})
term, but also the geometry-dependent
\mathcal{O}(1)
correction term. Neither of these terms are given in
Levernier et al. 2018
, which focuses on much broader class of random walks,
including Lévy walks where particle velocity is fixed in contrast to the possibility of unbounded velocities allowed by Lévy flights (see also
Dybiec et al. 2017
)
. In the next section, we outline the matched asymptotic method for the flat 3-D torus as well as closed 2-D manifolds.

V.3
Matched asymptotic method for the (flat) 3-D torus and 2-D manifolds

We now give a brief description of how the analysis of §
V.2
can be extended to the flat 3-D torus as well as 2-D closed manifolds. For the unit 3-D torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}=[0,1)^{3}
with a spherical target of radius
\varepsilon
centered at
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(1/2,1/2,1/2)
, we have the elliptic problem for the MFPT
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
(in analogy with (
8a
)),

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}u_{\varepsilon}=-1\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{T}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})},\ \ u_{\varepsilon}=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}\,,

(49a)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
is the fractional Laplacian of order
\alpha
on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
given by

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(3/2+\alpha)}{\pi^{3/2}|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\\
\times\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\!\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\frac{f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{m})|^{3+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,.

(49b)

This fact can be seen by running the same argument as that at the end of §
V.1
for the fractional Laplacian on the flat 2-D torus.

To analyze
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
near
\mathbf{x}_{0}
, we change variables to
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}+\varepsilon\mathbf{z}
and consider the inner problem on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

-(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}U_{0}=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,,\quad U_{0}=0\kern 5.0pt{\rm on}\kern 5.0pt\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,;

(50a)

U_{0}\sim S_{\alpha}\left(-\frac{1}{|\mathbf{z}|^{3-2\alpha}}+\chi_{\alpha}\right)\ {\rm as}\ |\mathbf{z}|\to\infty\,,

(50b)

where
-(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}
in (
50a
) is the 3-D fractional Laplacian given by

-(-\Delta_{\mathbf{z}})^{\alpha}f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2\alpha}}\frac{4^{\alpha}\Gamma(3/2+\alpha)}{\pi^{3/2}|\Gamma(-\alpha)|}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\!\frac{f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{3+2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}\,.

(51)

Following the same analysis that resulted in (
38
), we obtain that the GMFPT of a search of a small circular target of radius
\varepsilon
in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
is given by

\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2\alpha-3}\frac{\Gamma(3/2-\alpha)}{4^{\alpha}\pi^{3/2}\Gamma(\alpha)}\chi_{\alpha}-R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})\,,

(52)

where
R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})
is the regular part of the Green’s function satisfying

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}G_{\alpha}=-1+\delta(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|)\,,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{x}_{0}\}\,;\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\!G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})\,d\mathbf{x}=0\,;

(53a)

G_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}_{0})\sim-\frac{c_{\alpha}}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|^{3-2\alpha}}+R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}|)\kern 5.0pt\mbox{as}\kern 5.0pt\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}_{0}\,;\qquad c_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\Gamma(3/2-\alpha)}{4^{\alpha}\pi^{3/2}\Gamma(\alpha)}\,.

(53b)

To compute
\chi_{\alpha}
, we follow the analysis leading to (
45
) to recast (
50
) as an integral equation inside the 3-D unit ball
B_{1}(\mathbf{0})
,

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!\frac{f(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}|^{3-2\alpha}}\,d\mathbf{y}=\chi_{\alpha}\,,\quad\mathbf{z}\in\overline{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\,.

(54a)

subject to the normalization condition

\int_{B_{1}(\mathbf{0})}\!f(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}=1\,.

(54b)

The solution to (
54a
), as given by
Kahane 1983
, is

f(\mathbf{y})=\frac{\sin((1-\alpha)\pi}{\pi^{5/2}}\frac{\Gamma(3/2-\alpha)}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\chi_{\alpha}I\frac{1}{(1-|\mathbf{y}|^{2})^{\alpha}}\,;

(55a)

I\equiv-\frac{\alpha\textrm{B}(1/2,-\alpha)}{2(1/2-\alpha)}\,,

(55b)

where the beta function in (
55b
) is defined by
\textrm{B}(z_{1},z_{2})\equiv\Gamma(z_{1})\Gamma(z_{2})/\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})
is the beta function. Imposing the integral condition (
54b
) on
f(\mathbf{y})
in (
55
) yields
\chi_{\alpha}
, given by

\chi_{\alpha}=\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}(3/2-\alpha)(1/2-\alpha)\Gamma(1/2-\alpha)}{\alpha|\Gamma(-\alpha)|\sin((1-\alpha)\pi)}\,.

(56)

We note that the quantity
(1/2-\alpha)\Gamma(1/2-\alpha)>0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, so
\chi_{\alpha}>0
. Together with (
52
), the global MFPT is given to
\mathcal{O}(1)
by

\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{2\alpha-3}\frac{2}{4^{\alpha}\pi\alpha}\frac{\left[(1/2-\alpha)\Gamma(1/2-\alpha)\right]^{2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)|\Gamma(-\alpha)|\sin((1-\alpha)\pi)}-R_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{0};\mathbf{x}_{0})\,.

(57)

Since
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the leading order scaling of
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon}
in (
57
) is asymptotically larger than the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
search time of a Brownian particle in a confined 3-D volume
Cheviakov et al. 2010
.

The analysis on a closed compact 2-D manifold
M
of unit area and which is independent of the target size
\epsilon
can proceed in a manner similar to the matched asymptotic method of §
V.2
, with the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
replaced by that given in the Introduction section of
Applebaum and Estrade 2000
(with the parameter
a=0
, signifying pure Lévy flight). This operator (denoted
\mathscr{A}
in
Applebaum and Estrade 2000
) assumes that a jump from a point
p\in M
occurs along any one of the geodesics emanating from
p
, and that the length of this jump is measured along that geodesic curve. A circular target
B_{\varepsilon}(p_{0})
of radius
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
centered about
p_{0}\in M
is then taken to be the set of all points on
M
whose distance from
p_{0}
is less than or equal to
\varepsilon
.

In the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
region centered about
p_{0}
and in the appropriate Riemannian normal coordinates (see
Tzou and Tzou 2020
for a similar matched asymptotic analysis involving the Laplace-Beltrami operator), it can be shown that
\mathscr{A}
to leading order in
\varepsilon
reduces to the (flat) fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In these coordinates, the analysis of
u_{\varepsilon}
in the inner region proceeds exactly as for the 2-D torus in §
V.2
. Analysis of
u_{\varepsilon}
in the outer region away from
p_{0}
also remains largely unchanged with the main exception being that the Green’s function would involve the operator
\mathscr{A}
of
Applebaum and Estrade 2000
instead of
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
in §
IV
.

As a result, we predict that the global MFPT on a closed 2-D manifold of unit area will possess the same leading order behavior (
1
). However, depending on
\alpha
and the curvature of
M
at the center of the target, weaker algebraic and/or logarithmic singularities in
\varepsilon
may follow the leading
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2})
term. Moreover, computation of the regular part of the Green’s function for the
\mathcal{O}(1)
correction term may be challenging for general manifolds due to the difficulty of computing the operator
\mathscr{A}
itself. Further complications (and interesting behavior of
u_{\varepsilon}
) may arise if
M
contains points connected to
p_{0}
by a family of geodesics that can be parameterized by a continuous parameter (such pairs of points are absent on the flat torus).

VI
Discussion

Through Monte Carlo simulations, direct numerical solutions and asymptotic analysis of the limiting nonlocal exterior problem (
8
), we have shown that the average search time of a Lévy flight with tail index
0<\alpha<1
on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with a small circular target of radius
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
scales as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2\alpha-2})
. In addition, our asymptotic analysis has yielded the
\mathcal{O}(1)
correction term to search time, along with insights into how target geometry and search domain geometry and size impact search times.

By comparing to average search times of the Brownian walk on the same domain, which obey the well-known
\mathcal{O}(|\log\varepsilon|)
scaling, we have provided a concrete counterexample to the Lévy flight foraging hypothesis. We emphasize that our comparison is limited only to the narrow escape framework, and is not a general statement on the superiority of Brownian over Lévy search strategies. One possible avenue may be to assess whether a search strategy based on a combination of Brownian motion and Lévy flight (e.g.,
Palyulin et al. 2016
) can be optimized to be faster than pure Brownian search in the narrow escape framework. For this, the result of
Applebaum and Estrade 2000
may be useful, where an infinitesimal generator is obtained that governs the continuous limit of a Lévy process in which Brownian motion is “interlaced” with long jumps.

While we presented results only for small target sizes
\varepsilon
, we note that Lévy search times exceeded Brownian search times for all
\varepsilon<0.5
. Within this first-passage time framework, it may be insightful to seek possible scenarios in which the Lévy search strategy is superior.

We now discuss some avenues for future work, several of which are projects currently in progress. While a single target
\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a very simple domain on which to perform this comparison, it would be interesting to consider more complex domains. For example, a finite domain with reflecting boundaries containing perhaps small reflecting obstacles may present challenges from both a modeling and analytic perspective. From a particle simulations perspective, reflective domains and obstacles would require computing trajectories of flights that undergo reflections. From the pseudo-differential equation perspective, one must formulate an analog to the
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
operator of (
8b
); this new operator must account for all possible paths between all pairs of points, including those that reflect off boundaries and obstacles.

A domain featuring non-constant curvature would also present computational challenges - geodesics would need to be computed for both the Monte Carlo algorithm as well as the finite difference method for discretizing the corresponding infinitesimal generator (see
Chaubet et al. 2022
). This would add to the already significant computational cost. The sphere, on the other hand, has simple geodesics and may be a good candidate for a follow-up study, especially considering the interesting predictions made in
Chaubet et al. 2022
regarding the possible optimality of starting the search from the point antipodal to the center of the target (see Theorem 1.1 part (iii)).

Another domain feature that we have not considered is the inclusion of more than one target, one or more of which may be of non-circular shape. The multiple-target problem has been considered at length for Brownian motion on flat 2- and 3-dimensional geometries using hybrid asymptotic-numerical methods see (e.g.,
Delgado et al. 2015
;
Lindsay et al. 2017
;
Pillay et al. 2010
;
Cheviakov et al. 2010
;
Coombs et al. 2009
;
Bressloff and Newby 2013
;
Bressloff 2021b
;
Holcman and Schuss 2015
and the references therein). Several numerical optimization studies have been done to find optimal target arrangements that minimize the spatial average of the stopping time (e.g.,
Cheviakov and Zawada 2013
;
Cheviakov et al. 2012
;
Ridgway and Cheviakov 2018
;
Cheviakov and Ward 2011
). The inclusion of more than one target also gives rise to the question of splitting probabilities (see e.g.,
Kurella et al. 2015
;
Schuss 2009
;
Chevalier et al. 2010
) and shielding effects
Kurella et al. 2015
, and how they compare to their Brownian counterparts.

One useful aspect of such a hybrid methods is their ability to capture the higher order correction terms of the GMFPT, which encode effects of target locations/configurations. This can be accomplished by performing a higher order matching in the asymptotic solution for
u_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
in Section B, and computing the regular part of the Green’s function
G_{\alpha}
in (
35
). The greater ease of solving this
\varepsilon
-independent problem without singular features has made possible the computational optimization studies referenced above. An analogous hybrid analytic-numerical theory for the fractional Laplacian on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
or the more general operator on Riemannian manifolds would open various avenues of research. A similar method was recently developed for the Laplacian on Riemannian 2-manifolds using techniques in microlocal analysis
Tzou and Tzou 2019
;
Tzou and Tzou 2020
, which allowed for predictions of localized spot dynamics in reaction-diffusion systems on manifolds.

Numerical results suggest that the variance of the stopping time may have a larger scaling with
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
than the mean. The large variance suggests that the mean of the stopping time may not be particularly informative of the probability distribution of stopping times. Asymptotic computation of the variance of the MFPT for the narrow escape problem has been done in, e.g.,
Kurella et al. 2015
;
Lindsay et al. 2016
for Brownian motion. To capture
all
moments of the probability distribution, however, would require analysis of the diffusion equation. In
Lindsay et al. 2016
;
Bressloff 2021a
, a Laplace transform in the time variable was employed to transform the problem to an elliptic boundary value problem, on which the hybrid asymptotic-numerical tools of
Ward et al. 1993
could be applied before transforming back. A analogous approach may be possible to characterize the full distribution of stopping times of a Lévy flight on Riemannian manifolds.

Finally, for Brownian motion, the problem of finding target configurations that optimize GMFPT is closely related to the problem of finding stable equilibrium configurations of localized spots in the Schnakenberg reaction-diffusion system (cf.
Tzou et al. 2017
and
Cheviakov and Ward 2011
). Furthermore, the question of whether a
mobile
target leads to lower GMFPT has been found to be closely related to a certain instability of the aforementioned localized spot equilibria
Tzou et al. 2014
;
Tzou and Kolokolnikov 2015
;
Xie and Kolokolnikov 2017
. It would be interesting to explore whether these relationships still hold when Brownian motion is replaced by Lévy flights.
