Abstract
. For a first-order theory T T , the Constraint Satisfaction Problem of T T is the computational problem of deciding whether a given conjunction of atomic formulas is satisfiable in some model of T T . In this article we develop sufficient conditions for polynomial-time tractability of the constraint satisfaction problem for the union of two theories with disjoint relational signatures. To this end, we introduce the concept of sampling for theories and show that samplings can be applied to examples which are not covered by the seminal result of Nelson and Oppen.



1.
Introduction

Reasoning tasks for intelligent agents often require to check whether certain configurations or situations are legitimate or possible. Such decision problems can often be modelled as
Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs)
. The CSP of a first-order theory
T
with finite relational signature
\tau
is the computational problem of deciding whether a set of atomic
\tau
-formulas is satisfiable in some model of
T
. In fact, any decision problem is polynomial-time Turing equivalent to a CSP
[
5
]
.
Often, reasoners have to decide instances of
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
where
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are first-order theories with disjoint relational signatures such that
\CSP(T_{1})
and
\CSP(T_{2})
are well understood. This problem has already be studied by Nelson and Oppen
[
17
]
and many others have continued this line of research (see for example Baader and Schulz
[
2
]
). CSPs of unions of theories are at the heart of SMT-Solvers (SAT Modulo Theories) and occur frequently in software verification
[
10
]
.

The results of Nelson and Oppen
[
17
,
18
]
provide sufficient conditions for the polynomial-time tractability of
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, covering a great variety of theories.
Schulz
[
19
]
as well as Bodirsky and Greiner
[
4
]
have shown that in many situations, the conditions of Nelson and Oppen are also necessary for polynomial-time tractability (unless P = NP).

In this article we will present new sufficient conditions for polynomial-time tractability of
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
, and present examples which show that our conditions are incomparable to the conditions provided by Nelson and Oppen.
To prove our results, we introduce the notion of
sampling
for theories and show how to apply samplings in order to solve
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
in polynomial time.
In particular, we construct a sampling for
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
from given samplings for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.

2.
Sampling for a Theory

Let
\tau
be a finite
relational signature
, i.e., a finite set of relation symbols, each of which endowed with a natural number denoting its arity. A
\tau
-structure

\mathfrak{A}
is a tuple starting with a set
A
, followed by an
n
-ary relation
R^{\mathfrak{A}}\subseteq A^{n}
for each symbol
R\in\tau
of arity
n
. We will always denote structures with uppercase fraktur letters and their domain with the corresponding uppercase Latin letter.
A
theory
is a set of first-order sentences. A
\tau
-theory
is a theory
where all non-logical symbols are in
\tau
. By
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
we denote the
theory of
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e., the set of all first-order sentences which are satisfied in
\mathfrak{A}
. A
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
is a
model
of a
\tau
-theory
T
, if all sentences of
T
are satisfied in
\mathfrak{A}
. An
atomic
\tau
-formula
is either of the form
R(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
with
R\in\tau
of arity
n
and variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, of the form
x_{1}=x_{2}
, or of the form
\bot
, indicating the logical
“false”
.

We now present the central definitions of this article.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\tau
be a finite relational signature and
T
a
\tau
-theory.

•

A
sampling
for
T
is a sequence
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite sets of finite
\tau
-structures such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all conjunctions of atomic
\tau
-formulas
\phi
with at most
n
variables, there exists a model for
T
in which
\phi
is satisfiable if and only if there exists
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
such that
\phi
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
.
An element of
L_{n}
is called
n
-sample
, or simply
sample
, for
T
.

•

Let
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sampling for a theory
T
.
We define
\lvert L_{n}\rvert\coloneqq\sum_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\lvert B\rvert
.
A sampling
(L_{n})
is
polynomial
if there exists a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
such that
\lvert L_{n}\rvert\leq p(n)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

A sampling
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
computable
if there exists an algorithm that, given
n
, computes
L_{n}
. If this algorithm runs in time polynomial in
n
, then
(L_{n})
is
computable in polynomial time
.

•

A
sampling
for a structure
\mathfrak{A}
is a sampling for
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
.

•

For
n\geq 1
, let
S_{n}(T)
be the class of all finite
\tau
-structures
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
such that a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas with at most
n
variables is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
if and only if it is satisfiable in some model of
T
.

While we are not aware that samplings have been defined for theories before, there is a definition for structures by Bodirsky, Macpherson and Thapper
[
6
]
and we will now compare the two notions.
We need the classical notion of a CSP which is defined for structures instead of theories. Let
\tau
be a finite relational signature and
\mathfrak{A}
a

\tau
-structure.
\CSP(\mathfrak{A})
is the computational problem of deciding
whether a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}
.
Notice that
\CSP(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))=\CSP(\mathfrak{A})
holds for all relational
structures
\mathfrak{A}
, because
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
explicitly states which instances
are satisfiable.
Therefore, the definition of
‘sampling for
\mathfrak{A}
’
in
[
6
]
can be attained from our definition by imposing the additional conditions on
(L_{n})
that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the set
L_{n}
may only contain one structure
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
and that
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
needs to have a homomorphism to
\mathfrak{A}
.
Hence, any structure which has
‘efficient sampling’
in the definition of Bodirsky, Macpherson and Thapper has a sample which is computable in polynomial time in our definition, and is thus polynomial.

We will now prove that samplings for
structures
only require one element in each
L_{n}
, just like in the definition of Bodirsky, Macpherson and Thapper. However, we will see in Example
3.11
that dropping the requirement that
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
is homomorphic to
\mathfrak{A}
allows smaller samples in some cases and can therefore reduce the runtime of algorithms that run on the samples.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
T
be a theory with finite relational signature.
T
has the
Joint Homomorphism Property (JHP)
if for any two models
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}
of
T
there exists a model
\mathfrak{C}
of
T
such that both
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{C}
.

Proposition 2.3

(Proposition 2.1 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
T
be a theory with finite relational signature. Then there exists a model
\mathfrak{A}
of
T
such that
\CSP(\mathfrak{A})=\CSP(T)
if and only if

T
has the
Joint Homomorphism Property (JHP)
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a set
L
of
\tau
-structures we define the
disjoint union

\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L}\mathfrak{B}
as the structure with domain
\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L}B
and a
k
-ary relation

holds on a tuple

if and only if there exists
\mathfrak{B}\in L
such that
t\in B^{k}
and

holds in
\mathfrak{B}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
T
be a theory with JHP and let
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sampling for
T
. Then
(L^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
L^{\prime}_{n}\coloneqq\{\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\mathfrak{B}\}
is also a sampling for
T
. Moreover,
\lvert L^{\prime}_{n}\rvert=\lvert L_{n}\rvert
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas with variables in
V\coloneqq\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
.
If
\phi
is satisfiable in
T
, then it is satisfiable in some
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
and therefore in
\mathfrak{C}\coloneqq\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\mathfrak{B}
.
Assume that
\phi
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{C}
. Let
s\colon V\rightarrow C
be a satisfying assignment. Then
s
induces a partition
V_{1},\dots,V_{k}
on
V
, where two elements of
V
are in the same part if they are mapped into the same element of
L_{n}
. However,
\phi
cannot contain a conjunct
R(\dots,x_{i},\dots,x_{j},\dots)
with
x_{i}\in V_{i}
and
x_{j}\in V_{j}
for
i\neq j
, as this relation would not hold in
\mathfrak{C}
. Hence,
\phi
is equivalent to a conjunction
\phi_{1}\AND\dots\AND\phi_{k}
, where each
\phi_{i}
is itself a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas,
s
maps all variables of
\phi_{i}
into
V_{i}
and
\phi_{i}
and
\phi_{j}
have no common variables for
i\neq j
.
Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a model of
T
such that
\CSP(\mathfrak{A})=\CSP(T)
, which exists by Proposition
2.3
.
As
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sampling for
T
, it is also a sampling for
\mathfrak{A}
and therefore, each
\phi_{i}
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}
. Hence,
\phi
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}
and therefore in
T
.
We have
\lvert L^{\prime}_{n}\rvert=\sum_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\lvert B\rvert=\lvert L_{n}\rvert
.
∎

Due to Proposition
2.5
, and the fact that
\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\mathfrak{B}
has the same size as
|L_{n}\rvert
, we will now often assume that samplings for structures contain only one element
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
in
L_{n}
(for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
). We will write
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
instead of
(\{\mathfrak{B}_{n}\})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. It will become clear in Section
3
why we will not assume that sampling of structures make this assumption in general.

To justify why our definition of
‘sampling’
allows
L_{n}
to contain multiple structures in general, we would like to give an example of a theory
T
which has a sampling but no sampling
(L_{n})
of
T
has only one element in
L_{n}
for some
n\geq 2
.

Example 2.6

(similar to Example 2.2 in
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\tau
be a signature consisting of the unary symbols
O,P,Q
and the binary symbol
I
. Let
T
be the set of the following sentences

\displaystyle\forall x,y.((O(x)\AND O(y))\Rightarrow x=y),

\displaystyle\forall x.\neg(P(x)\AND Q(x)),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\forall x,y.(I(x,y)\Leftrightarrow\neg(x=y)).

The following is a sampling for
T
which is computable in polynomial time: For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
L_{n}
consist of
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
defined on
\{1,\dots,2n\}
where
P^{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}=P^{\mathfrak{B}_{2}}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
,
Q^{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}=Q^{\mathfrak{B}_{2}}\coloneqq\{n+1,\dots,2n\}
,
O^{\mathfrak{B}_{1}}\coloneqq\{1\}
and
O^{\mathfrak{B}_{2}}\coloneqq\{n+1\}
. In both structures, the
I
-relation denotes the inequality relation.
While it is easy to check that
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sampling for
T
, there is no sampling with only one element in
L_{n}
for some
n\geq 2
. To prove this, consider the formulas
\phi_{1}\mathrel{\vcentcolon\equiv}O(x)\AND P(x)
, which is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{1}
, and
\phi_{2}\coloneqq O(y)\AND Q(y)
, which is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{2}
. Assume that both are satisfiable in some
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{B}
. Then there exists
a\in O^{\mathfrak{B}}\cap P^{\mathfrak{B}}
and
b\in O^{\mathfrak{B}}\cap Q^{\mathfrak{B}}
.
If
a=b
, then
\psi_{1}\coloneqq P(x)\AND Q(x)
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
.
If
a\neq b
, then
\psi_{2}\coloneqq O(x)\AND O(y)\AND I(x,y)
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
or
\psi_{3}\coloneqq I(x,y)\AND x=y
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
(in case
I^{\mathfrak{B}}
is not the complement of equality). However, none of
\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3}
is satisfiable in some model of
T
, contradiction.

To characterise theories with a computable sampling, we need the following definition.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\tau
be a relational signature and let
\phi
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas with variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. The
canonical database

D(\phi)
is defined as the structure with domain
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
such that a tuple
t
is in a relation
R^{D(\phi)}
for
R\in\tau
if and only if
R(t)
is a conjunct of
\phi
.

Clearly, there is a homomorphism from
D(\phi)
to
\mathfrak{A}
if and only if
\phi
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
T
be a theory with finite relational signature. Then,
T
has a computable sampling if and only if
\CSP(T)
is decidable.

Proof.

Let
\tau
be the signature of
T
.
Suppose there exists a computable sampling
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
T
. Let
\phi
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau
-formulas with at most
n
variables. Then there are only finitely many maps from
D(\phi)
to structures in
L_{n}
and for each of them we can determine whether it is a homomorphism or not. Therefore
\CSP(T)
is decidable.

Conversely, suppose that
\CSP(T)
is decidable. There are only finitely many atomic formulas on
n
variables because
\tau
is finite and the arity of each relation is finite. Hence, there are only finitely many conjunctions of atomic formulas with at most
n
variables such that no conjunct is repeated. Using the decision procedure for
\CSP(T)
, we may determine which of the conjunctions are satisfiable in some model of
T
and define
L_{n}
as the set of all canonical databases of the satisfiable conjunctions. Then, by construction,
(L_{n})
is a sampling for
T
.
∎

To determine whether a theory has a polynomial sampling the following general observation is helpful.

Observation 2.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a structure with finite relational signature
\tau
.
By Proposition
2.5
, determining whether a polynomial sampling for
\mathfrak{A}
exists is equivalent to determining whether for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
such that
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is a polynomial sampling. If
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
and there exists a homomorphism
h\colon\mathfrak{B}_{n}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, then
h(\mathfrak{B}_{n})\in S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
and
\lvert h(B_{n})\rvert\leq\lvert B_{n}\rvert
.
If we can prove that each element of
S_{n}
homomorphically maps to
\mathfrak{A}
we can therefore assume that
L_{n}
consists of a single substructure of
\mathfrak{A}
. An element of
S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
which is a substructure of
\mathfrak{A}
is called an
n
-universal substructure
of
\mathfrak{A}
. Minimal sizes of
n
-universal substructures are easier to determine and have been explored in the past
[
16
,
12
,
15
]
.

In general,
S_{n}(\Th(\mathfrak{A}))
may contain structures which are smaller than the smallest
n
-universal substructure of
\mathfrak{A}
and Example
3.11
will provide an example for this case.

To exemplify the use of Observation
2.9
we will now use it to prove that a certain theory does not have a polynomial sampling. The theory
T
in Lemma
2.10
is contained in the theory of every undirected, loopless graphs with a binary relation symbol
E
for the edge relation
and a binary relation symbol
N
for the set of all pairs of distinct elements that are not related by an edge.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\tau
consist of the binary relation symbols
E
and
N
and let
T
contain only the sentence

\displaystyle\forall x,y.\big(

\displaystyle\neg E(x,x)\AND\neg(E(x,y)\AND\neg E(y,x))

\displaystyle\neg N(x,x)\AND\neg(E(x,y)\AND N(x,y))\big).

Then there is a sampling for
T
but not a polynomial sampling.

Proof.

First notice that
\CSP(T)
is decidable (in polynomial-time) because it suffices to check, after eliminating equalities by substitution of variables, that the instance does not contain
\bot
,
E(x,x)
,
N(x,x)
,
E(x,y)\AND N(x,y)
or
E(y,x)\AND N(x,y)
for any two variables
x,y
.
Therefore, there exists a sampling
(L_{n})
for
T
by Proposition
2.8
.
Fix
n\geq 2
.
Notice that the disjoint union of two models of
T
is again a model of
T
. Therefore,
T
has the JHP and we may without loss of generality assume that
L_{n}
contains only one structure
\mathfrak{B}
due to Proposition
2.5
.
For the structure
(B;E^{\mathfrak{B}})
we know that it is without loops as otherwise
E(x,x)
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
, contradiction.
Furthermore, we may assume that
(B;E^{\mathfrak{B}})
is an undirected graph, because adding the reverse direction to an edge does not increase the size of
\mathfrak{B}
and does not change the satisfiability of instances in
\mathfrak{B}
. To prove the latter, notice that instances satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
cannot become unsatisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
, where
E(a,b)
has been added, because all homomorphisms to
\mathfrak{B}
are also homomorphisms to
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
. Suppose there exists an instance
\phi
which is not satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
but there exists a homomomorphism
h\colon D(\phi)\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
. Let
\phi^{\prime}
be the instance where all conjuncts
E(x,y)
in
\phi
with
h(x)=a
and
h(y)=b
are replaced by
E(y,x)
. Then
h
is a homomomorphism from
D(\phi^{\prime})
to
\mathfrak{B}
, and therefore
\phi^{\prime}
is satisfiable in some model of
T
. As
E
is symmetric in all models of
T
,
\phi
is satisfiable in some model of
T
and therefore in
\mathfrak{B}
, contradiction.

Lastly, we know that
N^{\mathfrak{B}}\subseteq B^{2}\setminus E^{\mathfrak{B}}
must hold, as
E(x,y)\AND N(x,y)
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
otherwise.
Therefore,
\mathfrak{B}
is homomorphic to a model
\mathfrak{R}
of
T
in which
\forall x,y.(N(x,y)\Leftrightarrow x\neq y\AND\neg E(x,y))
holds and which contains all undirected loopless finite graphs as induced subgraphs. Then
\lvert\mathfrak{B}\rvert
is at least the size of the smallest
n
-universal substructure of
\mathfrak{R}
by Observation
2.9
. However, due to results by Noga Alon
[
1
]
we know that any substructure of
\mathfrak{R}
which contains all graphs of size at most
n
as induced subgraphs has size exponential in
n
and that this does not depend on the choice of
\mathfrak{R}
.
∎

Theories with polynomial samplings include all theories of finite structures, the theory of successor on the natural numbers, and the theories of structures in Lemma

3.
Sampling for Unions of Theories

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be two theories with disjoint relational signatures and that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
have polynomial samplings. In this section we will present sufficient conditions on
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
such that
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
has polynomial sampling.
To this end, we will need to construct models for
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
. Let
\tau_{1},\tau
be relational signatures with
\tau_{1}\subseteq\tau
and
\mathfrak{A}
a
\tau
-structure. The
\tau_{1}
-reduct
of
\mathfrak{A}
, written

, is the structure we get when all relations with symbols not contained in
\tau_{1}
are removed from
\mathfrak{A}
.
The following definition goes back to Tinelly and Ringeisen
[
20
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
be
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
structures respectively. A
\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}
-structure

is a
fusion
of
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
if and only if
\mathfrak{A}^{\tau_{i}}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
for
i=1
and
i=2
.

Proposition 3.2

(Proposition 1 and Lemma 1 in
[
2
]
)
.

For
i=1,2
, let
T_{i}
be a
\tau_{i}
theory.
A
\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
is a model of

if and only if
\mathfrak{A}
is a fusion of a model for
T_{1}
and a model for
T_{2}
. Furthermore, two structures have a fusion if and only if their domains have the same cardinality.

The proof of Lemma 1 in
[
2
]
essentially argues that any bijection between the domains of two structures with disjoint signatures defines a fusion of the two structures, which is a fact we will use later on.

It is an easy observation that an instance
\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2}
of
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is satisfiable if and only if for
i=1
and
i=2
there exist models
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
of
T_{i}
with
\lvert A_{1}\rvert=\lvert A_{2}\rvert
such that
\phi_{i}
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
and the satisfying assignments of
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
identify exactly the same variables.
The following notion describes a property of a sampling that allows us to transfer identifications of variables between models and samples.

Definition 3.3
.

We call a sampling
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
T

equality-matching
if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all conjunctions of atomic
\tau
-formulas
\phi
with variables in
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}

and for any conjunction
\psi
of equalities and negated equalities on
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, there exists a model of
T
in which
\phi\AND\psi
is satisfiable if and only if there exists
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
such that
\phi\AND\psi
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
.
A sampling is
equality-matching
for a structure
\mathfrak{A}
if it is equality-matching for
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
.

To be equality-matching will be the property that samplings for
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
must satisfy in order to construct a sampling for
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
in Theorem
3.8
.
An example for an equality-matching sampling is the one constructed in Example
2.6
because
I
is the negation of equality in all models of
T
and the constructed samples and therefore, the instances of
\CSP(T)
themselves can specify how to identify variables. Also, the sampling constructed in the proof of Proposition
2.8
is equality-matching if there exists
I\in\tau
such that in all models of
T
we have
I(x,y)\Leftrightarrow\neg(x=y)
.

Another class of theories where an equality-matching sampling always exists are
\omega
-categorical theories. A theory is
\omega
-categorical
if it has only one countable model (up to isomorphism).
For a structure
\mathfrak{A}
, the
orbit
of
t\in A^{k}
is
\{\alpha(t)\mid\alpha\in\Aut(\mathfrak{A})\}
where
\Aut(\mathfrak{A})
is the set of automorphisms of
\mathfrak{A}
and
\alpha
is applied componentwise.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
T
be an
\omega
-categorical theory with finite relational signature. Then there exists an equality-matching sampling for
T
.

Proof.

If
T
has an infinite model
\mathfrak{A}
then there exists a countably infinite model
\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}
of
T
such that
\Th(\mathfrak{A})=\Th(\mathfrak{A}^{\prime})
by the Downward Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem (see
[
13
]
, page 90).
If the unique countable model
\mathfrak{A}
of
T
is finite, then
(L_{n})
with
L_{n}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{A}\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
is an equality-matching sampling for
T
and we are done.
We may therefore assume that an instance
\phi
in conjunction with equalities and disequalities is satisfiable in some model of
T
if and only if it is satisfiable in a fixed countably infinite structure
\mathfrak{A}
.
By the theorem of Engeler, Ryll-Nardzewski and Svenonius (see
[
13
]
page 341) there are only finitely many orbits of
n
-tuples in
\mathfrak{A}
.
To construct an
n
-sample for
\mathfrak{A}
we pick a tuple from each of the finitely many orbits of length
n
and define
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
to be the substructure of
\mathfrak{A}
whose domain is the union of all elements appearing in these tuples. We claim that
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is an equality-matching sampling for
T
.

Let
\phi
be a conjunction of atomic formulas and
\psi
a conjunction of equalities and negated equalities, both on variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Clearly, if there exists a homomorphism from
D(\phi\AND\psi)
to
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
then there exists a homomorphism from
D(\phi\AND\psi)
to
\mathfrak{A}
because
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
embedds into
\mathfrak{A}
. If
h\colon D(\phi\AND\psi)\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
is a homomorphism, then the image of
h
is a tuple
t
of length at most
n
. Then, there is a tuple
t^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
with the same orbit as
t
. Hence, we can choose
\alpha\in\Aut(\mathfrak{A})
such that
\alpha(t)=t^{\prime}
and
\alpha\circ h
is a homomorphism from
D(\phi\AND\psi)
to
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
.
∎

It follows from Proposition
2.8

that there are are
\omega
-categorical structures without computable sampling, because some of those structures have an undecidable CSP
[
7
]
.
There are also theories with a sampling computable in polynomial time, but without polynomial equality-matching sampling.
Indeed, let
\tau
contain the binary relations
E
and
N
and let
T
contain only

\displaystyle\forall x,y\,\big(

\displaystyle\neg(E(x,y)\AND\neg E(y,x))\AND

\displaystyle\neg(E(x,y)\AND N(x,y)\AND x\neq y)\big).

Then
L_{n}\coloneqq(\{0\},E,N)
with
E=N\coloneqq\{(0,0)\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
yields a sampling for
T
.
If there exists a polynomial equality-matching sampling for
T
, then there exists a polynomial sampling for
T^{\prime}\coloneqq T\cup\Th(\mathbb{Z};\neq)
by Proposition
3.10
. However, in
\CSP(T^{\prime})
we can define relations

which must satisfy the theory from Lemma
2.10
by
E^{\prime}(x,y)\coloneqq E(x,y)\AND x\neq y
and
N^{\prime}(x,y)\coloneqq N(x,y)\AND x\neq y
. Therefore, a polynomial equality-matching sampling for
T^{\prime}
cannot exist.

It is in general not true that if
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equality-matching then
(L^{\prime}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq(\mathop{\vphantom{\bigcup}\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\bigcup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\displaylimits_{\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}}\mathfrak{B})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is equality-matching as well. An example is
\mathfrak{A}=(\{0,1\};<)
with
L_{4}
consisting of two copies of
\mathfrak{A}
. Then
x_{1}<x_{2}\AND x_{3}<x_{4}\AND\bigwedge_{i<j}x_{i}\neq x_{j}
is not satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}
or
L_{4}
, but is satisfiable in
(L_{4}^{\prime})
.
Even homomorphic images of equality-matching samplings, such as in Observation

, need not be equality-matching.

For later use, we now present two classes of structures with equality-matching sampling computable in polynomial time.
If
\mathfrak{B}
is a reduct of
\mathfrak{A}
and all relations in
\mathfrak{A}
have a first-order definition in
\mathfrak{B}
, then
\mathfrak{A}
is called a
first-order expansion
of
\mathfrak{B}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

All reducts of first-order expansions of
{(\mathbb{Q};<)}
have an equality-matching sampling computable in polynomial time.

Proof.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
be the substructure of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
with domain
\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
. Then
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is an equality-matching sampling for
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. This is because the orbit of a tuple
t
is fully determined by the order and identification of the coordinates of
t
and all possible orderings and identifications of
n
variables homomorphically map to
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
. Therefore, the constructed sampling is of the same kind as the one constructed in the proof of Proposition 3.4 and therefore equality-matching.

Any relation which is first-order definable over a structure is a union of orbits of that structure. As the constructed sampling consist of substructures of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
and each
n
-sample includes all orbits of length
n
, the constructed sampling is also a sampling for reducts of first-order expansions of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
be a partition of
\mathbb{Q}
where all parts are infinite and co-infinite.
All reducts of first-order expansions of

(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
have an equality-matching sampling computable in polynomial time.

Proof.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
define
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
as the substructure of
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
with domain
\{a_{1,1},\dotsc,a_{n,m}\}
where
a_{i,j}\in P_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
. Then
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is an equality-matching sampling for
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
. This is because the orbit of an
n
-tuple
t
in
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
is fully determined by identifications of coordinates of
t
and which coordinate belongs to which
P_{i}
. It is easy to see that all possible identifications and assignments of
n
variables to
P_{1},\dots P_{m}
can be realized in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
. Therefore, the constructed sampling is of the same kind as the one constructed in the proof of Proposition 3.4 and therefore equality-matching.

The reason why
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is also an equality-matching sampling for reducts of first-order expansions of
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
is the same as in the proof of Lemma 3.5.
∎

Another condition we need in order to construct a sampling for the union of two theories is a primitive positive version of the classical concept of
no algebraicity
in model theory (see
[
13
]
).
A formula is called
primitive positive (pp)
if it is a conjunction of atomic formulas where some variables may be existentially quantified.

Definition 3.7
.

A
\tau
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
has
no pp-algebraicity
if for any primitive positive
\tau
-formula
\phi(x)
with parameters
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
the set
\{b\in A\mid\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(b)\}
is either contained in
\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
or infinite.

It is easy to check that first-order expansions of
(\mathbb{Q};<)
or
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dotsc,P_{m})
do not have pp-algebraicity because
a<x<b
has either no or infinitely many satisfying assignments in
(\mathbb{Q};<)
(for all
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}
), and in
(\mathbb{Q};P_{1},\dots,P_{m})
any two distinct elements in
P_{i}
are indistinguishable for first-order definable relations. When it is clear from context that we mean a set, we will use
[n]
as a shorthand for
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

We are now ready to present the first main result.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be theories with finite relational and disjoint signatures
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
respectively.
If all models of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
do not have pp-algebraicity and
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
have equality-matching samplings
(O_{n}),(P_{n})
respectively, then there exists an equality-matching sampling
(L_{n})
for
T\coloneqq T_{1}\cup T_{2}
with
\lvert L_{n}\rvert=\lvert O_{n}\rvert\cdot\lvert P_{n}\rvert
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
(O_{n})
and
(P_{n})
are computable in polyonmial time, then
(L_{n})
is also computable in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
(O_{n})
and
(P_{n})
be equality-matching samplings of
T_{1},T_{2}
, respectively.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{\mathfrak{B}_{i,1},\dots,\mathfrak{B}_{i,p_{i}}\}
be
O_{n}
for
i=1
and
P_{n}
for
i=2
.
For all
i\in[p_{1}],j\in[p_{2}]
we now define a
\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}
-structure
\mathfrak{M}_{i,j}
with domain
M_{i,j}\coloneqq B_{1,i}\times B_{2,j}
as follows.

For
R\in\tau_{1}
of arity
k
we define

\displaystyle((a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{k},b_{k}))\in R^{\mathfrak{M}_{i,j}}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\forall i,j.(a_{i}=a_{j}\Leftrightarrow b_{i}=b_{j}))\AND(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in R^{\mathfrak{B}_{1,i}}.

Analogously, we define for
R\in\tau_{2}
of arity
k

\displaystyle((a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{k},b_{k}))\in R^{\mathfrak{M}_{i,j}}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle(\forall i,j.(a_{i}=a_{j}\Leftrightarrow b_{i}=b_{j}))\AND(b_{1},\dots,b_{k})\in R^{\mathfrak{B}_{2,j}}.

Let
\phi_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau_{i}
-formulas for
i=1
and
i=2
. Notice that forcing
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
to have the same variables can be done without loss of generality by introduction of dummy constraints like
x=x
.
Suppose there exists a homomorphism
g\colon D(\phi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\AND\phi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
where
\mathfrak{A}
is a model of
T
. Then
g\colon D(\phi_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}^{\tau_{i}}
is also a homomorphism for
i=1
and
i=2
. As the chosen samplings are equality-matching, there exists
u_{i}\in[p_{i}]
and homomorphisms
h_{i}\colon D(\phi_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}_{i,u_{i}}
for
i=1
and
i=2
such that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
both identify variables in the same way that
g
does. Then, by construction,
x_{i}\mapsto(h_{1}(x_{i}),h_{2}(x_{i}))
defines a homomorphism from
D(\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2})
to
\mathfrak{M}_{u_{1},u_{2}}
identifying the same variables as
g
.

For the reverse direction suppose that
h
is a homomomorphism from
D(\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2})
to
\mathfrak{M}_{u_{1},u_{2}}
.
We want to show the existence of a homomorphism
g
from

D(\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2})
to
\mathfrak{A}
, where
\mathfrak{A}
is a model of
T
and
g
and
h
identify the same variables.
To simplify presentation, we would like to assume that
h
is injective.
Suppose there exists
x_{i},x_{j}
such that
h(x_{i})=h(x_{j})
. Then we replace all occurrences of
x_{j}
in
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
by
x_{i}
and iterate until
h
is injective on the remaining variables. Call the resulting formulas
\phi_{1}^{\prime}
and
\phi_{2}^{\prime}
. If there exists an injective homomorphism
g
from
D(\phi_{1}^{\prime}\AND\phi_{2}^{\prime})
to a model of
T
, we can extend
g
to the formerly substituted variables via
g(x_{j})\coloneqq g(x_{i})
to get a homomorphism from
D(\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2})
to some model of
T
which has the same identifications as
h
. Hence, it is sufficient to prove the existence of
g
under the assumption that
h
is injective.

Let
h_{i}\coloneqq\pi_{i}^{2}\circ h
for
i\in[2]
, where
\pi^{2}_{i}
is the projection to the
i
-th coordinate out of of two coordinates. Now suppose there exists a set
S\subseteq h(D(\phi))
of size at least two such that any two elements in
S
are equal in the first coordinate (the case with the second coordinate can be proven analogously). Without loss of generality we will assume
S=\{h(x_{1}),\dots,h(x_{k})\}
. Furthermore, we may assume that
S
is maximal, i.e., for all
i>k
we have
h_{1}(x_{i})\neq h_{1}(x_{1})
.
For
i\in[k]
let
\psi_{i}(x_{i},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})
be the conjunction of all conjuncts in
\phi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
which do not contain
x_{j}
for all
j\in[k]\setminus\{i\}
.
Then
\phi_{1}
is equivalent to
\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\psi_{i}(x_{i},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{n})
because no conjunct of
\phi_{1}
can contain more than one variable from
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, as this conjunct would not be satisfied in the image of
h_{1}
in
\mathfrak{M}_{u_{1},u_{2}}
.
As
T_{1}
has an equality-matching sampling, there exists a homomorphism
g_{1}\colon D(\phi_{1})\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}_{1}
for some model
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
of
T_{1}
, such that
g_{1}
has the same identifications as
h_{1}
.
By the definition of no pp-algebraicity, the set

\{a\in A\mid\mathfrak{A}_{1}\models\psi_{i}(a,g_{1}(x_{k+1}),\dots,g_{1}(x_{n}))\}

is infinite for all
i\in[k]
.
Hence, there exists a homomorphism
g_{1}^{\prime}\colon D(\phi_{1})\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}_{1}
such that
g_{1}^{\prime}(x_{i})\neq g_{1}^{\prime}(x_{j})\neq g_{1}^{\prime}(x_{l})=g_{1}(x_{l})
for all
i,j\in[k]
and
l\in[n]\setminus[k]
.
As the sampling for
T_{1}
is equality-matching, there exists
u_{1}^{\prime}\in[p_{1}]
and a homomorphism
h_{1}^{\prime}\colon D(\phi_{1})\rightarrow\mathfrak{B}_{1,u_{1}^{\prime}}
with the same identifications as
g_{1}^{\prime}
. This yields a homomorphism
x_{i}\mapsto(h_{1}^{\prime}(x_{i}),h_{2}(x_{i}))
to
\mathfrak{M}_{u_{1}^{\prime},u_{2}}
where strictly less variables are mapped to the same row or column than by
h
.

Iterated application of this argument to
h_{1}
and, in an analogous way, to
h_{2}
proves that there exist injective homomorphisms
h_{1}^{*}
and
h_{2}^{*}
such that
h^{*}
defined by
x_{i}\mapsto(h_{1}^{*}(x_{i}),h_{2}^{*}(x_{i}))
is a homomorphism from
D(\phi_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\AND\phi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2}))
to
\mathfrak{M}_{u_{1}^{*},u_{2}^{*}}
.
As
(O_{n})
and
(P_{n})
are equality-matching samplings there exist injective homomorphisms
g_{i}^{*}\colon D(\phi_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}))\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{*}
where
\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{*}
is a model of
T_{i}
for
i=1
and
i=2
. Now observe that
\mathfrak{A}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}^{*}
are both either empty (in which case the theorem becomes trivial) or infinite because no pp-algebraicity implies that
\{x\mid\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{*}\models x=x\}
is infinite or empty. If
A_{i}^{*}
is uncountable, then there exists a
\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{\prime}
with countable domain such that
\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{\prime})=\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{*})
due to the Downward Löwenheim-Skolem Theorem (see
[
13
]
, page 90) and we may substitute
\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{*}
by
\mathfrak{A}_{i}^{\prime}
. Hence, we may assume that
\lvert A_{1}^{*}\rvert=\lvert A_{2}^{*}\rvert
and therefore, there exists a bijection
f\colon A_{1}^{*}\rightarrow A_{2}^{*}
such that
f(g_{1}^{*}(x_{i}))=g_{2}^{*}(x_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
. However, any bijection from
A_{1}^{*}
to
A_{2}^{*}
induces a fusion
\mathfrak{A}
of
\mathfrak{A}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}^{*}
with domain
A_{1}^{*}
and therefore a model of
T
by Proposition
3.2
. Clearly,
g\colon D(\phi)\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}^{*}
defined by
g(x_{i})\coloneqq g_{1}^{*}(x_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
is an injective homomorphism. Hence,
(L_{n})
with
L_{n}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{M}_{1,1},\dots,\mathfrak{M}_{p_{1},p_{2}}\}
is an equality-matching sampling for
T
.

We have
|L_{n}\rvert=\sum_{i\in[p_{1}],j\in[p_{2}]}\lvert\mathfrak{B}_{1,i}\rvert\cdot\lvert\mathfrak{B}_{2,j}\rvert=\lvert O_{n}\rvert\cdot\lvert P_{n}\rvert
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and to determine whether a relation
R^{\mathfrak{M}_{i,j}}
holds on an tuple
t
requires at most
n^{2}
checks for equality and a check if
t\in R^{\mathfrak{B}_{1,i}}
or
t\in R^{\mathfrak{B}_{2,j}}
. Therefore, if both samplings are computable in polynomial time,
(L_{n})
is computable in polynomial time as well.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

Notice that with almost the same construction, an equality-matching sampling for any finite number of theories can be constructed as long as all theories have an equality-matching sampling and only models without pp-algebraicity. For that we use a higher-dimensional version of the matrix defined in the proof, where a relation
R\in\tau_{i}
holds on a tuple
t
if only if any two entries of
t
either differ in all their coordinates or are equal in all their coordinates and the projection to the
i
-th dimension of each element of
t
is in
R
as defined in the respective sample for
T_{i}
.

Note that if
\mathfrak{A}
has a finite signature and is without pp-algebraicity, then all models of
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
are without pp-algebraicity. This holds because for each primitive positive formula
\phi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the theory
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
contains a sentence expressing that for any choice of parameters
x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
any satisfying assignment for
x_{1}
is either equal to one of the parameters or there are at least
n
different satisfying assignments for
x_{1}
.

If one of the theories, say
T_{1}
, does not satisfy the conditions of Theorem
3.8
because it has models with pp-algebraicity, we can prove the following.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
T_{1}
be a theory with finite relational signature and let
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
be an infinite structure with signature
\tau_{2}
whose relations are first-order definable over the empty signature. If
T_{1}
has an equality-matching sampling
(L_{n})
and only infinite models, then the expansion of the samples in
(L_{n})
with relations in
\tau_{2}
by their first-order definitions is an equality-matching sampling for
T_{1}\cup\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{2})
.

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\tau_{1}
be the signature of
T_{1}
. Call the expanded sampling
(L_{n}^{\prime})
.
Let
\phi_{i}
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau_{i}
formulas.
Then
\phi_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is equivalent to
\bigvee_{i\in[k]}\psi_{i}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, where each
\psi_{i}
specifies by a conjunction of equalities and disequalities which variables must be equal and which must differ . However, for every
i\in[k]
the formula
\phi_{1}\AND\psi_{i}
is satisfiable in some element of
L_{n}
if and only if it is satisfiable in some model of
T_{1}
because
(L_{n})
is equality-matching. Therefore,
\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2}
is satisfiable in some model of
T_{1}
if and only if it is satisfiable in some structure in
L_{n}^{\prime}
.
∎

There are also theories with a
sampling
but without an equality-matching sampling:
for example,
\mathfrak{A}\coloneqq(\mathbb{Z};R_{a},R_{m})
where

R_{a}:=\{(x,y,z)\mid x+y=z\}
and
R_{m}:=\{(x,y,z)\mid x\cdot y=z\}
has a trivial CSP and therefore a sampling. If there exists an equality-matching sampling for
\Th(\mathfrak{A})
we can construct a sampling for
T\coloneqq\Th(\mathfrak{A})\cup\Th(\mathbb{Z};\neq)
by Proposition
3.10
. Then we can define
z=1
by the primitive positive formula
\exists x,y\,(x\cdot z=x\AND y\cdot z=y\AND x\neq y)
.
It is now easy to see that Hilbert’s tenth problem, which is undecidable, is many-to-one reducible to
\CSP(T)
, contradicting Proposition
2.8
.

The following example shows that we cannot drop the assumption of no pp-algebraicity in Theorem
3.8
.

Example 3.11
.

Let
\mathrm{succ}
be the successor relation on
\mathbb{N}
. Let
P_{0},P_{1}
be two disjoint, infinite sets (two colours). Then
T_{1}\coloneqq\Th(\mathbb{N};\mathrm{succ})
and
T_{2}\coloneqq\Th(P_{0}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}P_{1};P_{0},P_{1})
both have an equality-matching sampling which can be computed in polynomial time. However, any sampling
(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
has
\lvert L_{n}\rvert\geq 2^{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and therefore no polynomial sampling exists.

To prove this, first notice that we can construct a model
\mathfrak{A}
for
T\coloneqq T_{1}\cup T_{2}
such that
\CSP(\mathfrak{A})=\CSP(T)
. This can be done by giving each natural number the colour
P_{0}
or
P_{1}
in such a way that the resulting sequence over
\{0,1\}
includes any binary number as consecutive subsequence.
Therefore, when examining lower bounds for
\lvert L_{n}\rvert
, we may assume by Proposition
2.5
that for each
n
there is only one element
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(T)
in
L_{n}
. Now observe the following:

(1)

In any
n
-sample
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, no element
x
can satisfy
P_{0}(x)\AND P_{1}(x)
and there is no pp-formula which can force a node to have no colour. Hence, if some node in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
has no colour, we can colour this node arbitrarily and the result is again in
S_{n}(T)
. Therefore, without loss of generality, each node in the sampling has exactly one colour.

(2)

When we fix a node
c
in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
such that
\phi_{c}\coloneqq\mathrm{succ}(c,x_{2})\AND\dots\AND\mathrm{succ}(x_{n-1},x_{n})
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, then there exists exactly one map
s\colon[n]\rightarrow\{0,1\}
such that
\psi_{c,s}\coloneqq\phi_{c}\AND\bigwedge_{i\in[n]}P_{s(i)}(x_{i})
is satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
. If there were two distinct maps
s
and
s^{\prime}
, then
\psi_{x_{1},s}\AND\psi_{x_{1},s^{\prime}}
would be satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, but not in
\mathfrak{A}
.

Now observe that atomic
\tau_{1}\cup\tau_{2}
-formulas with
n
variables can encode a binary number
b
of length
n
via
\mathrm{succ}(x_{1},x_{2})\AND\dots\AND\mathrm{succ}(x_{n-1},x_{n})\AND\bigwedge_{i\in[n]}P_{b_{i}}(x_{i})
. By item (
2
) each element of
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
is the start of at most one binary number and therefore,
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
must have at least
2^{n}
elements.

We would like to remark that for all
n
there exists
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(T)
of size
2^{n}
such that
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is an equality-matching sampling of
T
. It can be constructed via a de-Brujin sequence. However, the smallest
n
-universal substructure of a model of
T
has size
2^{n}+n-1
, which is larger.

Even though we cannot drop no-pp-algebraicity in general, there are structures with pp-algebraicity where the sets definable by primitive positive formulas are somewhat tame and an equality-matching sampling of the union of their theories exists and is computable in polynomial time. We will demonstrate this with the following example, which will also occur in the next section.

Example 3.12
.

Let
E_{1}
be the relation

\{(2a,2a+1),(2a+1,2a)\mid a\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Let
E_{2}
be a copy of
E_{1}
. Then we consider
T\coloneqq\Th(\mathbb{N};E_{1})\cup\Th(\mathbb{N};E_{2})
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we will now construct
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in S_{n}(T)
such that
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is an equality-matching sampling for
T
computable in polynomial time.

Consider formulas
\gamma_{k}
and
\delta_{k}
describing an
alternating sequence
and an
alternating cycle
, both of length
2k
, respectively.
Formally, we define
\gamma_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k+1})
as the formula
\bigwedge_{i\in[k]}(E_{1}(x_{2i-1},x_{2i})\AND E_{2}(x_{2i},x_{2i+1}))
and
\delta_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k})
as the formula
\gamma_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{2k},x_{1})
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
be the structure containing
\lceil\frac{n}{2k}\rceil
many copies of
D(\delta_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{2k}))
, for each
k\in\{1,\dots,\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil\}
. Such
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
has size in
O(n^{2})
.

We now prove that
(\mathfrak{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sampling for
T
. To this end, note that
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
can be extended to a model of
T
by adding infinitely many alternating cycles of all positive even lengths.
We will now analyze which instances
\phi
of
\CSP(T)
are satisfiable and prove that these are satisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
.
Let
\phi_{i}
be a conjunction of atomic
\tau_{i}
-formulas for
i=1
and
i=2
and suppose that
\phi\coloneqq\phi_{1}\AND\phi_{2}
contains only variables from
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and is satisfiable in some model of
T
.
If
\phi
contains conjuncts of the form
E_{i}(x,y)\AND E_{i}(y,z)
then
x=z
must hold in any model of
T
. Hence, we may simplify our analysis by replacing
z
by
x
in
\phi
. The instance
E_{i}(x,x)
is never satisfiable. Therefore, if
\phi
contains the conjunct
E_{i}(x,y)
then
x
and
y
do not appear in any other
E_{i}
conjunct, i.e., each variable appears at most once in all
E_{1}
conjuncts and at most once in all
E_{2}
conjuncts. Hence,
\phi
can only describe alternating sequences and alternating cycles, both of which homomorphically map into alternating cycles of suitable length and therefore to
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
.

4.
Exemplary Application to CSPs

We finally show how to use the sampling
(L_{n})
constructed in Theorem
3.8
in order to prove the polynomial-time tractability of
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
.
To do this, we use algorithms for the CSPs of the structures
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in L_{n}
which run in
uniform
polynomial time, i.e., the runtime is polynomial in the size of the instance and in
\lvert B_{n}\rvert
.
The following generalises a result for CSPs of structures by Bodirsky, Macpherson, and Thapper

[
6
]

to CSPs of theories.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T
be a theory with finite relational signature and let
(L_{n})
be a sampling of
T
computable in polynomial time. If there is an algorithm that solves
\Csp(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
for every
\mathfrak{B}_{n}\in L_{n}
in uniform polynomial time, then
\Csp(T)
is polynomial-time tractable.

Proof.

To decide an instance
\phi
of
\CSP(T)
on
n
variables we run the algorithm on
(\mathfrak{B},\phi)
for all
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
. If the algorithm accepts on any of these tuples, we return
‘satisfiable’
, otherwise we return
‘unsatisfiable’
. This is correct and complete by the definition of sampling. Furthermore, the procedure runs in polynomial time, because
|L_{n}|
is polynomial in
n
and the algorithms worst-case runtime is polynomial in
n
and
|L_{n}|
and therefore polynomial in
n
.
∎

To describe classes of finite structures whose CSP can be be solved by a uniform polynomial-time algorithm, the following concepts from universal algebra are important.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a relational structure with signature
\tau
and let
f
be an operation of arity
k
on
\mathfrak{A}
.
We call
f
a
polymorphism
of
\mathfrak{A}
if it is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{A}^{k}
to
\mathfrak{A}
, i.e., whenever
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}\in R^{\mathfrak{A}}
, for some
R\in\tau
, then
f(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})\in R^{\mathfrak{A}}
, where
f
is applied componentwise.
An operation
f\colon A^{k}\rightarrow A
is
totally symmetric
if for all
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k},y_{1},\dotsc,y_{k}\in A
we have

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k})=f(y_{1},\dotsc,y_{k})
whenever
\{x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}\}=\{y_{1},\dotsc,y_{k}\}
.
If
k\geq 3
and for all
a,b\in A
we have
f(a,b,b,\dots,b)=f(b,a,b,b,\dots,b)=\dots=f(b,b,\dots,b,a)=b
, then
f
is called a
near-unanimity operation
.

Proposition 4.3

(Corollary 3.6 in
[
14
]
)
.

There is an algorithm that solves
\CSP(\mathfrak{B})

in uniform polynomial time for all finite relational structures
\mathfrak{B}
with a near-unanimity polymorphism.

Results about the applicability of fast uniform algorithms for CSPs of finite relational structures with a near-unanimity polymorphism can be found in the work of Kozik (2016).

Proposition
4.3
and Proposition
4.1
can be applied to Example
3.12
with the following near-unanimity operation
f
defined on
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

f(x,y,z)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}y&\text{if }y=z,\\
x&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Note that
f
is a polymorphism of
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
, for all
n
, because if
(t_{1,1},t_{1,2})
,
(t_{2,1},t_{2,2})
and
(t_{3,1},t_{3,2})
are in
E_{k}
and
t_{i,1}=t_{j,1}
, then
t_{i,2}=t_{j,2}
and therefore
f(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=(t_{i,1},t_{i,2})\in E_{k}
for all
k\in[2],i,j\in[3],i\neq j
.
In particular,
\CSP(T)
with
T
from Example
3.12
can be solved in polynomial time.

An example for a totally symmetric polymorphism is the minimum operation over
(\mathbb{Q};<)
. Also, we can define minimum as an operation of any arity.
The following result builds on results of Feder and Vardi
[
11
]
.

Theorem 4.4

(Section 3 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}
be a finite structure with finite relational signature. Then the arc-consistency procedure solves
\CSP(\mathfrak{B})
iff
\mathfrak{B}
has totally symmetric polymorphisms of all arities.

The Arc-consistency algorithm runs in worst-case time
O(n^{2}m^{2})
where
n
is the number of variables in the instance and
m
is the size of the domain
[
8
]
.
Theorem
4.4

and Proposition
4.1
immediately yield the following.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
(L_{n})
be a sampling for
T
which is computable in polynomial time. If all samples in
(L_{n})
have totally symmetric polymorphism of all arities, then
\CSP(T)
is polynomial-time tractable.

We can slightly relax the requirement that all the structures in
L_{n}
have totally symmetric polymorphisms.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
T
be a theory with finite relational signature and let
(L_{n})
be a sampling of
T
computable in polynomial time. If for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
there exists a model
\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{B}}
of
T
and a homomorphism
h\colon\mathfrak{B}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{B}}
such that
h(\mathfrak{B})
has totally symmetric polymorphisms of all arities, then
\CSP(T)
is polynomial-time tractable.

Proof.

To decide an instance with
n
variables, we run arc-consistency on each pair
(\mathfrak{B},\phi)
where
\phi
is the instance and
\mathfrak{B}\in L_{n}
. Whenever arc-consistency returns
‘unsatisfiable’
for an instance on a structure
\mathfrak{B}
, the instance is unsatisfiable in
\mathfrak{B}
(this holds for any finite structure). Hence, if all runs reject, the instance is unsatisfiable.

Suppose that the arc-consistency procedure returned
‘satisfiable’
on
(\mathfrak{B},\phi)
. Then, the algorithm produced for each variable a non-empty list of values in
\mathfrak{B}
such that no list can be shortened due to constraints in the instance. Thus, when we apply
h
to all these values, we gain non-empty lists of values over
\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{B}}
where all relations that did hold in
\mathfrak{B}
hold again. Hence, arc-consistency cannot return
‘unsatisfiable’
when run on the image of
h
.
By Theorem 4.4, this means that the instance is satisfiable on the image of
h
, therefore in
\mathfrak{A}_{\mathfrak{B}}
and therefore in some element of
(L_{n})
, by definition of sampling.
As
(L_{n})
is computable in polynomial time and arc-consistency runs in uniform polynomial time, we can decide
\CSP(T)
in polynomial time.
∎

We conclude this section with a concrete reasoning task that can be
solved in polynomial time with our methods,
and that is not covered by the combination results of Nelson and Oppen
[
17
,
18
]
.

Example 4.7
.

Given parts of a huge machine, each of which can be mounted by exactly one of two robots, and precedence constraints on the order in which parts can be mounted, calculate a possible order in which the machine can be assembled. Some parts must be mounted by one robot, some by the other robot, and some parts can be mounted by both robots. The two robots are not allowed to work simultaneously. This reasoning task can be modelled as
\CSP(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
where

•

T_{1}:=\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{1})
for a first-order expansion
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
of
(\mathbb{Q};<)

that allows to model the precedence constraints,
and

•

T_{2}:=\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{2})
where
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
has two disjoint countably infinite unary relations and their union as domain.

By Lemma
3.5
and
Lemma
3.6
,
the theories
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
have polynomial-time computable samplings, and both structures have no pp-algebraicity.
Hence, by Theorem
3.8

there exists a polynomial-time computable sampling
(L_{n})
for
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
.
Let
\tau_{i}
be the signature of
\mathfrak{A}_{i}
, for

i\in\{1,2\}
.
For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every
\mathfrak{C}_{n}\in L_{n}
there exists an injective homomorphism
h_{1}
from

\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{\tau_{1}}
to
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and an injective homomorphism
h_{2}
from

\mathfrak{C}_{n}^{\tau_{2}}
to
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
; this follows by inspection of the construction of
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
in the proof of Theorem
3.8

and the proofs of Lemma
3.5
and
Lemma
3.6
.
As
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
are both countably infinite and
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are injective, there exists a bijection
f
between
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that
f(h_{1}(a))=h_{2}(a)
for all
a\in C_{n}
, inducing a fusion
\mathfrak{A}
of
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
.
By construction of
\mathfrak{A}
, the map
h_{1}
is a homomorphism from
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
to
\mathfrak{A}
.

Also note that the minimum operation is a polymorphism of all unary relations. Hence,
if
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
has the minimum operation
as a polymorphism then the minimum operation is also a polymorphism of
\mathfrak{A}
.
Then Lemma
4.6

implies
that
\Csp(T_{1}\cup T_{2})
is polynomial-time tractable.

An example for such a structure
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
is
\mathfrak{A}_{1}\coloneqq(\mathbb{Q};<,R)
where
R
is the ternary relation defined by
R(x,y,z)\Leftrightarrow x=\min(y,z)
;
it is easy to check that the minimum operation is a polymorphism of
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
.
This example is not covered by the conditions of Nelson and Oppen, since

\Th(\mathfrak{A}_{1})
is
not convex
:
the formula
R(x,y,z)\wedge x\neq y
is satisfiable,
the formula
R(x,y,z)\wedge y\neq z
is satisfiable, but the formula
R(x,y,z)\wedge x\neq y\wedge y\neq z
is not satisfiable in
\mathfrak{A}_{1}
.

We mention that the polynomial-time tractability of

\Csp(\mathfrak{A}_{1})
for
\mathfrak{A}_{1}=({\mathbb{Q}};<,R)
in our example was already known in the SMT community
[
3
]
.
It is straightforward to generalise the example to any finite number
m
of robots, by replacing the structure
\mathfrak{A}_{2}
by
the structure
({\mathbb{Q}};P_{1},\dots,P_{m})
that we have already encountered in Lemma
3.6
.
