Abstract
. Let G G be a finite group, p p a prime and B B a Brauer p p -block of G G with defect group D D . We prove that if the number of irreducible ordinary characters in B B is 5 5 then D ≅ 𝖢 5 , 𝖢 7 , 𝖣 8 D\cong{\sf C}_{5},{\sf C}_{7},{\sf D}_{8} or 𝖰 8 {\sf Q}_{8} , assuming that the Alperin–McKay conjecture holds for B B .

1.
Introduction

Suppose that
G
is a finite group,
p
is a prime, and
B
is a Brauer
p
-block of
G
with defect group
D
. Let
k(B)
denote the number of irreducible ordinary characters that belong to
B
. In
[
BF59
]
, R. Brauer and W. Feit proved that

k(B)\leq\frac{1}{4}|D|^{2}+1.

Besides this bound, very little is known about the number of irreducible complex characters in the block
B
. However, much has been conjectured. For instance, Brauer’s
k(B)
-conjecture claims that
k(B)\leq|D|
, and the Héthelyi–Külshammer conjecture posits that if
k(B)>1
then
k(B)\geq 2\sqrt{p-1}
(see
[
Br63
]
and
[
HK00
]
). Remarkably, neither of these conjectures has even been reduced to simple groups.

Another fundamental problem on the number
k(B)
is Brauer’s problem 21, which asks whether for any positive integer
n
there are finitely many isomorphism classes of prime power order groups that occur as defect groups of blocks with exactly
n
irreducible characters. This problem was shown to have a positive answer for
p
-solvable groups in
[
Kul90
]
. Using this result and E. Zelmanov’s solution to the restricted Burnside problem, it was shown to have a positive answer as a consequence of the Alperin–McKay conjecture in
[
KR96
]
. However, no classification of the possible defect groups is given for any value of
k(B)
.

R. Brauer and C. Nesbitt proved in 1941 that if
k(B)=1
then
|D|=1
(see
[
BN41
]
). The next step came 41 years later, when J. Brandt proved in
[
Bra82
]
that
k(B)=2
if and only if
|D|=2
. If
B
is the principal block, the classifications of defect groups for
k(B)\leq 6
have been obtained in
[
KS21
]
,
[
RSV21
]
and
[
HSV23
]
but at the present time there is no classification for arbitrary blocks.

It is however a consequence of the Alperin–McKay conjecture that
k(B)=3
if and only if
|D|=3
, as shown in
[
KNST14
, Theorem 4.2]
. In
[
MRS23
]
we proved that if
k(B)=4
then
D\cong{\sf C}_{2}\times{\sf C}_{2},{\sf C}_{4},
or
{\sf C}_{5}
also assuming that the Alperin–McKay conjecture holds for
B
. The main result of this paper is to extend these results.

Theorem A
.

Suppose that
B
is a Brauer
p
-block of a finite group
G

with defect group
D
. Assume that
k(B)=5
. If the
Alperin–McKay conjecture holds for
B
, then
D
is isomorphic to
{\sf C}_{5},{\sf C}_{7},{\sf D}_{8}
or
{\sf Q}_{8}
.

The proof of Theorem
A
is achieved via a reduction to a problem on primitive permutation groups of low rank with a small number of projective characters, in the sense of Schur. To solve this problem we use known classifications of these groups, and use some very
ad hoc
arguments for each case in the classifications. Further, we care to remark that the defect groups that we find coincide with those found for principal blocks in
[
RSV21
]
.

There is a complementary conjecture to Brauer’s problem 21, known as Donovan’s conjecture. It asks whether, for a finite
p
-group
D
, there are finitely many Morita equivalence classes of blocks with defect group isomorphic to
D
. Although this conjecture remains open, there has been a recent breakthrough for abelian defect groups
[
EL19
]
when
p=2
.
Assuming the Alperin–McKay conjecture and using the progress in Donovan’s conjecture that is gathered in the Block library
[
Wiki
]
, it is possible to determine the Morita equivalence classes of blocks with
k(B)\in\{4,5\}
by using
[
MRS23
, Theorem A]
and Theorem
A
.

Section
2
is devoted to the proof of the reduction theorem. In Section
3
we show some preliminary general results on projective characters for the proof of the reduced statement and in Section
4
we provide the results and known classifications of primitive permutation groups that we need and complete the proof of the main result.

2.1.
Preliminary results

We begin by stating some necessary results for the reduction theorem. First, we recall the known results on small values of
k(B)
. We denote by
{\rm IBr}(B)
the set of irreducible Brauer characters in the
p
-block
B
and by
l(B)=|\mathrm{IBr}(B)|
. We also denote by
k_{0}(B)
the number of height zero irreducible characters in
B
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
B
a
p
-block of
G
with defect group
D
. Then

(i)

k(B)=1
if and only if
|D|=1
,

(ii)

k(B)=2
if and only if
|D|=2
,

(iii)

if the Alperin–McKay conjecture holds for
B
and
k(B)=3
then
|D|=3
,

(iv)

if the Alperin–McKay conjecture holds for
B
and
k(B)=4
then
|D|\in\{4,5\}
,

(v)

if
l(B)=1
and
k(B)=5
then
D\cong{\sf C}_{5},{\sf Q}_{8},{\sf D}_{8}
.

Proof.

Part (i) is
[
Nav98
, Theorem 3.18]
, (ii) is
[
Bra82
]
, (iii) is
[
KNST14
, Theorem 4.2]
, (iv) is
[
MRS23
]
and (v) appears in
[
CK92
]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
D
is a
2
-group such that
D^{\prime}={\bf Z}(D)
and assume
D/D^{\prime}\cong{\sf C}_{2}\times{\sf C}_{2}
. Then
D\cong{\sf Q}_{8}
or
{\sf D}_{8}
.

Proof.

Since
D/D^{\prime}\cong{\sf C}_{2}\times{\sf C}_{2}
then
D
is dihedral, semidihedral or generalized quaternion. Thus
|{\bf Z}(D)|=2
and
|D|=8
, as desired.
∎

We end the preliminaries by stating Brauer’s famous character counting formula. Throughout this work we write
n_{q}
for the largest power of a prime
q
that divides the natural number
n
.

Lemma 2.3

(Brauer’s formula)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and
B
a
p
-block of
G
. Let
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t}\}
be representatives of the non-central
G
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements. Then

k(B)=l(B)|{\bf Z}(G)|_{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{b\in\mathrm{Bl}({\bf C}_{G}(x_{i}))\atop b^{G}=B}l(b).

Proof.

See
[
Nav98
, Theorem 5.12]
.
∎

2.2.
The reduction theorem

We now show that our main result can be proved assuming the following theorem on affine primitive permutation groups, whose proof can be found in Section
4.6
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
p\geq 5
a prime,
D\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}(G)
, and assume
D
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
with
p
-complement
K
. Let
Z={\bf Z}(G)
and suppose
Z={\bf O}_{p^{\prime}}(G)
. Assume that
G/Z
is an affine primitive permutation group of rank
2,3
or
4
on
D
. Let
\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(Z)
. If
|\operatorname{Irr}(G|\lambda)|=5
we have
|D|=5,7
.

Next we prove that Theorem
2.4
implies Theorem
A
when
D\triangleleft\,G
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
B
a
p
-block of
G
with
k(B)=5
. Let
D
be a defect group of
B
and assume
D\triangleleft\,G
. Then
D
is isomorphic to
{\sf{C}}_{5},{\sf{C}}_{7},{\sf{D}}_{8}
or
{\sf{Q}}_{8}
.

Proof.

Write
|D|=p^{d}
.

Step 1. We may assume that
D\in{\rm Syl}_{p}(G)
. Further, we may assume that
D
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
with
p\neq 2,3
.

The first part is
[
Rey63
, Theorem 6]
. Suppose that
N\triangleleft\,G
with
1<N<D
. Let
\overline{B}
be a block of
G/N
dominated by
B
with defect group
D/N
(see
[
Nav98
, Theorem 9.9]
). Then
1<k(\overline{B})<5
by
[
Nav98
, Theorem 3.18]
and
[
Nav98
, Theorem 6.10]
.
By Theorem
2.1
,
D/N
is isomorphic to
{\sf C}_{2},{\sf C}_{3},{\sf C}_{2}\times{\sf C}_{2},{\sf C}_{4}
or
{\sf C}_{5}
, and hence
p\leq 5
.

Suppose that
D
is non-abelian. Then by setting
N={\bf Z}(D)
we have that necessarily
D/{\bf Z}(D)\cong{\sf C}_{2}\times{\sf C}_{2}
. Since
D^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi(D)\subseteq N
, arguing similarly, we have
N=D^{\prime}
, so we are under the hypotheses of Lemma
2.2
and thus
D
is isomorphic to either
{\sf D}_{8}
or
{\sf Q}_{8}
.

Thus we may assume that
D
is abelian. By
[
Rey63
, Theorem 9]
,
k_{0}(B)=k(B)=5
. By
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.3]
and
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.6]
we have that
p\not=2,3
. Then
D/N\cong{\sf C}_{5}
and
k(\overline{B})=4
by Theorem
2.1
. Since all the irreducible characters in
{\rm Irr}(D)\setminus{\rm Irr}(D/N)
lie under the same irreducible character of
B
, they are
G
-conjugate. Let
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(D)\setminus\operatorname{Irr}(D/N)
. Then
|G:G_{\theta}|=5^{d}-5
which is divisible by
5
, a contradiction with the fact that
D
is a Sylow subgroup. Hence we may assume that
D
is a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. In particular,
D
is abelian and again by
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.3]
and
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.6]
we may assume
p\geq 5
.

Step 2. We may assume that
|{\bf Z}(G)|_{p}=1
.

Let
Z\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}({\bf Z}(G))
and suppose that
1<Z
. Then
D=Z
by Step 1, and
G
has a normal
p
-complement
K
, that is,
G=K\times Z
. In this case, by
[
Nav98
, Theorem 10.20]
,
\operatorname{Irr}(B)=\operatorname{Irr}(K\times Z|\lambda)
for some
\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(K)
(which is
G
-invariant because
Z
is central). Therefore
|\operatorname{Irr}(B)|=|\operatorname{Irr}(K\times Z|\lambda)|=|\operatorname{Irr}(Z)|
which forces
Z=D\cong{\sf C}_{5}
.

Step 3. We may assume that
{\bf Z}(G)={\bf O}_{p^{\prime}}(G)
.

Let
N={\rm\textbf{O}}_{p^{\prime}}(G)
and let
\lambda\in{\rm Irr}(N)
be such that if
b=\{\lambda\}\in{\rm Bl}(N)
then
B
covers
b
. By the Fong–Reynolds correspondence
[
Nav98
, Theorem 9.14]
we may assume that
b
is
G
-invariant, and hence
\lambda
is
G
-invariant. Now
(G,N,\lambda)
is an ordinary-modular character triple and by
[
Nav98
, Problem 8.13]
there exists an isomorphic ordinary-modular character triple
(H,M,\varphi)
with
M
a
p^{\prime}
-group and
\varphi
linear and faithful, in particular
M
is central. Since
G/N\cong H/M
we have that
M={\bf O}_{p^{\prime}}(H)
and by
[
Nav98
, Theorem 10.20]
applied twice we obtain that

k(B)=|{\rm Irr}(G|\lambda)|=|{\rm Irr}(H|\varphi)|=k(B_{1}),

where
B_{1}
is some
p
-block of
H
. Moreover, if
Q/M\in{\rm Syl}_{p}(H/M)
, since
M\subseteq{\bf Z}(H)
, we have that
Q=Q_{1}\times M
where
Q_{1}={\bf O}_{p}(H)
, so
Q_{1}
is a defect group of
B_{1}
and

D\cong DN/N\cong Q/M\cong Q_{1}.

Hence may assume that
\lambda
is linear and faithful, in particular
N
is central and by Step 2 we have
N={\bf Z}(G)
.

Step 4. Let
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}\}
be a set of representatives of the
G
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements of
G
. Then
t\in\{2,3,4\}
.

We may assume that
x_{1}=1
, so
\{x_{2},\ldots,x_{t}\}
are a set of representatives of the non-trivial
G
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements of
G
. By Brauer’s formula from Lemma
2.3
and Step 2 we have

(2.1)

k(B)=l(B)+\sum_{i=2}^{t}\sum_{b\in\mathrm{Bl}({\bf C}_{G}(x_{i})),\\
b^{G}=B}l(b).

Notice that we may assume
l(B)>1
by Theorem
2.1
. Thus
l(B)\geq 2
and
b^{G}=B
for some
b\in{\rm Bl}({\bf C}_{G}(x_{i}))
by
[
Nav98
, Theorem 4.14]
, and we have that either
t\in\{2,3,4\}
, concluding the proof of the step.

Before the next step of our proof we fix some notation. Let
Z={\bf Z}(G)={\bf O}_{p^{\prime}}(G)
. By
[
Nav98
, Theorem 10.20]
we have that
\operatorname{Irr}(B)=\operatorname{Irr}(G|\lambda)
, where
\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(Z)
is the character from Step 3. Let
\hat{\lambda}=1_{D}\times\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(D\times Z)
and let
b\in{\rm Bl}(D\times Z)
be the
p
-block containing
\hat{\lambda}
. Notice that
b
is
G
-invariant since
\hat{\lambda}
is
G
-invariant.

Step 5. We have that
G/Z
is an affine primitive permutation group of rank 2, 3 or 4.

By the Schur–Zassenhaus theorem there is
K\leq G
such that
G=KD
and
K\cap D=1
, and write
\overline{G}=G/Z
,
\overline{D}=DZ/Z
and
\overline{K}=K/Z
. We have that
\overline{G}
acts on
\Omega=\{\overline{K}d\mid d\in D\}
transitively via the action of right multiplication. Notice that
\overline{K}
is the stabilizer of the trivial class in
\Omega
. Since
\overline{K}
is maximal in
\overline{G}
, we have that this action is primitive (see
[
Isa08
, Corollary 8.14]
, for instance) and
\overline{G}
is a primitive permutation group.

Since the action of
\overline{K}
on
\Omega
has the same number of orbits (and orbit sizes) as the action by conjugation of
\overline{G}
on
\overline{D}
, by Step 4 it has 2, 3 or 4 orbits. Hence the rank of
\overline{G}
is 2, 3 or 4, as wanted.

Now we apply Theorem
2.4
to get the desired result. ∎

In the final step of the previous proof we have considered an action on the right cosets of some subgroup. However, from now on, every action considered will be an action by conjugation on some normal subgroup, or on its set of irreducible characters.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
A
, which we restate. Recall that if
B
is a block of a finite group
G
with defect group
D
then the Alperin–McKay conjecture states that
k_{0}(B)=k_{0}(b)
where
b
is its Brauer correspondent block in
{\bf N}_{G}(D)
.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
B
a
p
-block of
G
with
k(B)=5
and
D
a defect group of
B
. Assume
k_{0}(B)=k_{0}(b)
where
b\in\mathrm{Bl}({\bf N}_{G}(D))
is the Brauer correspondent of
B
in
{\bf N}_{G}(D)
. Then
D
is isomorphic to
{\sf C}_{5},{\sf C}_{7},{\sf D}_{8}
or
{\sf Q}_{8}
.

Proof.

By
[
Nav98
, Theorem 9.9]
,
b
dominates some block
\overline{b}\in\mathrm{Bl}({\bf N}_{G}(D)/\Phi(D))
with defect group
D/\Phi(D)
. We have
k(\overline{b})=k_{0}(\overline{b})\leq k_{0}(b)
by
[
Rey63
, Theorem 9]
. We explore all the possibilities for
k(\overline{b})
. If
k(\overline{b})=1
then
D/\Phi(D)=1
which is impossible. If
k(\overline{b})=2
then by the main result of
[
Bra82
]
,
|D/\Phi(D)|=2
and then
D
is a cyclic 2-group. In particular,
k(B)=k_{0}(B)
by
[
Dad66
]
, which contradicts
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.3]
. If
k(\overline{b})=3
by
[
KNST14
, Theorem 4.1]
we have
|D/\Phi(D)|=3
so
D
is a cyclic 3-group. Again
k(B)=k_{0}(B)
which contradicts
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.6]
.

If
k(\overline{b})=4
by the main result of
[
MRS23
]
we have
D/\Phi(D)\in\{\mathsf{C}_{4},\mathsf{C}_{2}\times\mathsf{C}_{2},\mathsf{C}_{5}\}
. The first case is impossible. If
|D/\Phi(D)|=5
then
D
is cyclic, so applying
[
Dad66
]
,
k(b)=k_{0}(b)=k_{0}(B)=k(B)=5
and it follows from Theorem
2.4
applied to
b
that
|D|=5
. Thus we assume
D/\Phi(D)\cong\mathsf{C}_{2}\times\mathsf{C}_{2}
, and notice that by
[
Lan81
, Corollary 1.3]
,
D
is nonabelian, and
k_{0}(B)=4
. By arguing similarly, we get that
D/D^{\prime}\cong\mathsf{C}_{2}\times\mathsf{C}_{2}
so
D
is semidihedral, dihedral or generalized quaternion. Since these groups are metacyclic we may use
[
Sam14
, Theorem 8.1]
to conclude that the only possibilities are
D\cong\mathsf{Q}_{8}
and
D\cong\mathsf{D}_{8}
.

Finally if
k(\overline{b})=5
, we have that
5=k(\overline{b})\leq k_{0}(b)=k_{0}(B)\leq 5
, so
k(\overline{b})=k_{0}(b)=5
. By Theorem
2.5
applied to
{\bf N}_{G}(D)/\Phi(D)
we have that
D/\Phi(D)
is cyclic of order 5 or 7. Then
D
is abelian and
k(b)=k_{0}(b)=5
by
[
Rey63
, Theorem 6]
. By Theorem
2.5
applied to
{\bf N}_{G}(D)
, we obtain that
D
is cyclic of order 5 or 7, concluding the proof.
∎

3.
Some results on projective characters

We begin this section with the following observation.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group and suppose that
G
has a normal Sylow
p
-subgroup
D
with
p
-complement
K
. Let
B\in\mathrm{Bl}(G)
and suppose that there exists
\lambda\in{\rm Irr}({\bf O}_{p^{\prime}}(G))
,
G
-invariant lying under some irreducible character in
B
. Then we have
l(B)=|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|
.

Proof.

By
[
Nav98
, Lemma 2.32]
we have
\mathrm{IBr}(G)=\mathrm{IBr}(G/D)
which can be identified with
\mathrm{IBr}(K)=\operatorname{Irr}(K)
and the result follows.
∎

In proving Theorem
2.4
we will be dealing with groups with few projective characters. The next results will help us deal with these, by allowing us to bring Sylow subgroups into the picture.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
N\triangleleft\,G
,
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
and assume
\theta
is
G
-invariant and that
G/N
is cyclic. Then
\theta
extends to
G
.

Proof.

See
[
Nav18
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

We denote by
{\rm Lin}(G)
the set of linear characters of
G
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Suppose that
N\triangleleft\,G
and assume
\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
is
G
-invariant and linear. Let
o(\lambda)
be the order of
\lambda
as an element of
\operatorname{Lin}(N)
. If every Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G/N
has trivial Schur multiplier whenever
p
divides
o(\lambda)
then
\lambda
extends to
G
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
Isa06
, Theorem 6.26]
and
[
Isa06
, Theorem 11.7]
.
∎

If
N\triangleleft\,G
,
\lambda\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
is
G
-invariant and
|\operatorname{Irr}(G|\lambda)|=1
then we say
\lambda
is fully ramified in
G
. In this case, it is easy to see that
|G:N|
must be a square. One of the first applications of the classification of finite simple groups to character theory was proving that, in this case,
G/N
is solvable (this is known as the Howlett–Isaacs theorem, see
[
HI82
]
).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
Z\triangleleft\,G
and let
\lambda\in{\rm Irr}(Z)
be
G
-invariant. Then
\lambda
is fully ramified in
P
for every
P/Z\in\operatorname{Syl}_{p}(G/Z)
and every prime
p
dividing
|G|
if and only if
\lambda
is fully ramified in
G
. In particular, if
\lambda
is fully ramified in
G
then the nontrivial Sylow subgroups of
G/Z
are not cyclic.

Proof.

This is
[
Nav18
, Theorem 8.3]
and
[
Nav18
, Problem 8.1]
.
∎

The following results, which extend the Howlett–Isaacs theorem, appeared in
[
Hig88
]
in the language of projective representations.

Theorem 3.5

(Higgs)
.

Let
Z\triangleleft\,G
and let
\lambda\in{\rm Irr}(Z)
be
G
-invariant and suppose that
|{\rm Irr}(G|\lambda)|=2
. Then
G/Z
is solvable. Furthermore, if
q
is an odd prime and
Q/Z\in{\rm Syl}_{q}(G/Z)
, then
|{\rm Irr}(Q|\lambda)|=1
, and in particular,
|Q/Z|
is a square. If
q=2
,
|{\rm Irr}(Q|\lambda)|=2
, and in particular,
|Q/Z|
is an odd power of 2.

Proof.

This is
[
Hig88
, Theorem B and Proposition 2.2]
.
∎

It remains open whether if

4.1.
Preliminaries on primitive permutation groups

To prove Theorem
2.4
we need to deal with primitive permutation groups. The degree of a primitive permutation group is the size of the set on which it is acting, in our case,
|D|
. We begin by recalling some important definitions related to the structure of these groups.

Let

be a

can be identified with the Galois field

. The semilinear group

is defined as the set of maps

\Gamma(V)=\{x\mapsto ax^{\sigma}\mid x\in V,a\in V\setminus\{0\},\sigma\in{\rm Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})\}.

Notice that

is a metacyclic group. Indeed, it contains the normal subgroup

\Gamma_{0}(V)=\{x\mapsto ax\mid a\in V\setminus\{0\}\}

which is cyclic and isomorphic to the multiplicative group of

\Gamma(V)/\Gamma_{0}(V)\cong{\rm Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}/\mathbb{F}_{p})

is cyclic of order

We frequently use the notation
\Gamma(p^{k})=\Gamma(V)
. If a subgroup

is
2
-transitive on

then
\Gamma_{0}(V)\subseteq H
. Semilinear groups appear very naturally in the classifications of primitive permutation groups.

The following are the classifications of affine primitive permutation groups that we will need. We eliminate any case that contradicts any hypothesis in Theorem

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume hypotheses of Theorem

. If

[

We claim first that

[
Nav98

is

-invariant,

Then

Proposition 4.6

Assume that the rank of

4.5.
The imprimitive case

In this section we prove Theorem
2.4
for case (iv) in Theorem
4.3
and
4.4
.

In this case, we can write the Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
as
D=V_{1}\times V_{2}
or as
D=V_{1}\times V_{2}\times V_{3}
. In this context, the subgroups
V_{i}
are sometimes called imprimitivity spaces. Let
A/Z={\bf N}_{{K/Z}}(V_{1})/{\bf C}_{K/Z}(V_{1})
. Then
K/Z\cong(A/Z)\wr S
where
S\leq{\sf S}_{n}
, and
n
is the number of imprimitivity spaces (this can be found in
[
MW93
, Theorem 2.8]
). Recall that we write
|D|=p^{d}
. Let
|V_{1}|=p^{m}
so that
d=2m
or
d=3m
, and let
H/Z
be the base group of
(A/Z)\wr S
, which is a direct product of
n
copies of
A/Z
, so that
H\triangleleft\,K
and
K/H\cong S
. We assume this notation and structure for this whole section.

The following result deals with the imprimitive case in rank 3.

Proposition 4.12
.

Assume that
K/Z
is as in case (iv) of Theorem
4.3
. Then Theorem
2.4
holds.

Proof.

In this case,
K/Z=(A/Z)\wr{\sf S}_{2}
. Write
|V_{1}|=p^{m}
so that
d=2m
(and recall that
d>2
by Proposition
4.7
). The orbits of
K/Z
on
D
have sizes
1
,
2(p^{m}-1)
and
(p^{m}-1)^{2}
, by
[
Lie87
, Table 12]
. By
[
Hup98
, Theorem 18.10]
, the orbits of the action of
K/Z
on
D
have exactly the same sizes as the
K
-orbits on
{\rm Irr}(D)
. Notice that if
\alpha,\beta
are representatives of the nontrivial
K
-orbits on
D
then at least one of
\alpha
and
\beta
are fully ramified in their stabilizer in
G
.

Assume first that
\alpha
is fully ramified in
G_{\alpha}
so that
|G_{\alpha}:ZD|
is a square. We have
|G:G_{\alpha}|=2(p^{m}-1)
,
|G_{\alpha}:ZD|=s^{2}
so
|G:ZD|=2s^{2}(p^{m}-1)
. Now
|G:ZD|=|K:Z|=2|A/Z|^{2}
, which forces
p^{m}-1
to be a square. This is a contradiction with
[
Mor69
, p. 301]
if
m
has an odd prime divisor, so we assume that
m
is a
2
-power, so
p^{m}-1=(p^{\frac{m}{2}}+1)(p^{\frac{m}{2}}-1)
which can not be a square.

Thus we have that
\beta
is fully ramified in
G_{\beta}
. We have
|G:ZD|=2|A:Z|^{2}
is not a square. Since
|G:G_{\beta}|=(p^{d}-1)^{2}
and
|G_{\beta}:ZD|=|G:ZD|/|G:G_{\beta}|
can not be a square, a contradiction since
\beta
is fully ramified in
G_{\beta}
.
∎

In what follows we will be dealing with imprimitive groups of rank 4, that is, those in case (iv) of Theorem
4.4
. In this case, we may have
2
or
3
imprimitivity spaces, and in these cases respectively
S\cong{\sf S}_{2}
or
S\cong{\sf C}_{3},{\sf S}_{3}
. Recall that in this case, Brauer’s formula forces
|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|=l(B)\leq 2
and that we assumed
l(B)>1
via Theorem
2.1
. Thus by Theorem
3.5
,
G
is solvable. Then the possible structure of
A/Z
is determined by Huppert’s theorem, i.e.,
A/Z
is one of the solvable groups from Theorem
4.2
. We deal with each of these cases separately.

Proposition 4.13
.

Assume that
S\cong{\sf S}_{2}
. Then
|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|>2
. In particular, Theorem
2.4
holds.

Proof.

Assume first that
A/Z\leq\Gamma(p^{m})
. By
[
MRS23
, Lemma 4]
,
K
has an abelian Sylow
q
-subgroup
Q
for some odd prime
q
, and
|QZ/Z|>1
. Thus
\lambda
extends to
QZ
and
QZ/Z\in\operatorname{Syl}_{q}(K/Z)
, and by Theorem
3.5
we have
|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|>2
, as desired.

Hence we may assume
A/Z
is one of the solvable groups in case (ii) of Theorem
4.2
. It is easy to check in
[
GAP
]
that for all possibilities of
A/Z
, there is a normal subgroup
N/Z\triangleleft\,A/Z
such that
N/Z\cong{\mathrm{SL}}(2,3)
. We assume first that
S\cong{\sf S}_{2}
. Let
B/Z
denote the
K
-conjugate subgroup of
A/Z
so that
K/Z=(A/Z\times B/Z)\rtimes S
and let
M/Z
be the
K
-conjugate of
N/Z
in
B/Z
.

By Lemma
3.3
,
\lambda
extends to
\operatorname{Irr}(N)
and by Gallagher’s theorem there is a unique character
\alpha\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
of degree
3
in
\operatorname{Irr}(N|\lambda)
. Since
H
normalizes
N
and
\lambda
is
K
-invariant, it follows that
\alpha
is
MN
-invariant and again by Lemma
3.3

\alpha
extends to
MN\triangleleft\,K
. By Gallagher’s theorem, there are characters of three distinct degrees in
\operatorname{Irr}(MN|\alpha)\subseteq\operatorname{Irr}(MN|\lambda)
, which contradicts
|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|=2.

∎

Proposition 4.14
.

Assume that
S\cong{\sf S}_{3}
or
{\sf C}_{3}
. Then
|\operatorname{Irr}(K|\lambda)|>2
. In particular, Theorem
2.4
holds.

Proof.

By
[
Vau93
, p. 33]
and
[
Hup98
, Theorem 18.10]
there is a
G
-orbit on
\operatorname{Irr}(D)
of size
3(p^{m}-1)^{2}
(here, we are considering the natural action of
G
on
\operatorname{Irr}(D)
induced by the action by conjugation on
D
). Now by Theorem
3.5
we have that the Sylow
q
-subgroups have square order, and the Sylow
2
-subgroups have order an odd power of
2
. Thus we may write
|G:ZD|=|K:Z|=2b^{2}
for some positive integer
b
. If
\mu
is a representative of the orbit of size
3(p^{m}-1)^{2}
, then

|G_{\mu}:ZD|=\frac{|G:ZD|}{|G:G_{\mu}|}=\frac{2b^{2}}{3(p^{m}-1)^{2}}

so
|G_{\mu}:ZD|_{3}
can not be a square. This contradicts Lemma
4.9
.
∎
