Nonstandard diffeology and generalized functions
Kazuhisa Shimakawa

Abstract
. We introduce a nonstandard extension of the category of diffeological spaces, and demonstrate its application to the study of generalized functions. Just as diffeological spaces are defined as concrete sheaves on the site of Euclidean open sets, our nonstandard diffeological spaces are defined as concrete sheaves on the site of open subsets of nonstandard Euclidean spaces, i.e. finite dimensional vector spaces over (the quasi-asymptotic variant of) Robinson’s hyperreal numbers. It is shown that nonstandard diffeological spaces form a category which is enriched over the category of diffeological spaces, is closed under small limits and colimits, and is cartesian closed. Furthermore, it is shown that the space of nonstandard smooth functions on (the extension of) a Euclidean open set is a smooth differential algebra that admits an embedding of the differential vector space of Schwartz distributions. Since our algebra of generalized functions comes as a hom-object in a category, it enables not only the multiplication of distributions but also the composition of them. To illustrate the usefulness of this feature, we show that the homotopy extension property can be established for smooth relative cell complexes by exploiting extended maps.

1.
Introduction

Schwartz distributions make it possible to differentiate
functions whose derivatives do not exist in the classical
sense, e.g. locally integrable functions.
They are widely used in the theory of partial differential
equations, where it may be easier to establish the existence
of weak solutions than classical ones, or appropriate
classical solutions may not exist.
However, there is a serious drawback that distributions
cannot be multiplied nor composed except for very special
cases. Thus, the formulas such as

(e^{\delta(x)})^{\prime}=e^{\delta(x)}\delta^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{($\delta$: Dirac's delta)}

do not make sense within the framework of Schwartz
distributions.

A viable way to resolve the problem of multiplication will
be to construct a differential algebra which includes the
space of distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
as a linear subspace. At
first glance, this does not look feasible considering the
Schwartz impossibility result stating that no differential
algebra containing the space of distributions preserves the
product of continuous functions.
Nevertheless, if we only want to preserve the product of
smooth functions then the construction of such an algebra is
possible, and the first example of such is given by
J. F. Colombeau. But Colombeau’s algebra of generalized
functions does not solve the second problem, for it is not
closed under composition.

Unlike the case of multiplicativity, composability of
distributions has not attracted much attention. But
recently,
[
Giordano 2021
]
created a solution to the problem
of composability by introducing a category which has as its
morphisms smooth set-theoretic maps on (multidimensional)
points of a ring of scalars having infinitesimals and
infinities, and includes Schwartz distributions.
The aim of this article is to present an yet another
approach to constructing a category of generalized maps
containing Schwartz distributions.
Giordano’s approach is based on Colombeau’s algebra of
generalized functions and employs as the scalar a partially
ordered ring. On the other hand, we use Robinson’s field of
nonstandard numbers as the scalar field. While this brings
us simplicity in the construction of the theory, it
complicates the relation between Colombeau’s algebra and
ours. Still, we can construct a chain of homomorphisms
relating Colombeau’s algebra to our algebra of
nonstandard-valued smooth functions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we briefly
recall basic facts about the category of diffeological
spaces
\Diff
, and then introduce two variants of the
nonstandard number field: one is the field of hyperreals

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
(and its complex version
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{C}}
), and the other is the
field of quasi-asymptotic real numbers
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}
(and its
complex version
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
).
Like the field of asymptotic numbers,
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}

(
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\,\mathbb{C}
) can be written as a quotient

\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})/\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
of subalgebras

\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\subset\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
. But
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
is
larger than the field of asymptotic numbers in the sense
that the latter is a subquotient of the former. For both

\bullet=*
and
\rho
,
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}
is a non-Archimedean real
closed field and
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{C}}
is an algebraically closed field of
the form
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{C}}=\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}+\sqrt{-1}\,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}
. Moreover, via the
ultrapower construction we can show that
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{F}}
is a smooth
field with respect to the diffeology induced by the standard
diffeology of
\mathbb{F}
.

In Section 3 we construct nonstandard extensions
\bEucOp

of the site of open subsets of Euclidean spaces
\EucOp
.
We take
D
-open subsets of nonstandard Euclidean spaces

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
as objects of
\bEucOp
. The construction of the
set of morphisms
\bCinf(U,V)
in
\bEucOp
proceeds by
several steps. For
\bullet=*
,
U\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and

V=\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
, we take as
\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
the diffeological
space
\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
of smooth maps
U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
. There
exist partial derivatives

\partial_{i}\colon\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\to\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

with respect to which
\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
is a smooth
differential algebra over
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
.
On the other hand, if
\bullet=\rho
and
U\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}

then
\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
is not adequate as
\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
as it
is not closed under partial derivatives. Instead, we take
the subalgebra
\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
of
\Cinf(U,\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))

consisting of those
f\colon U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
such that

D^{\alpha}f(x)\in\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and
define
\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
as the image of
\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))

under the homomorphism
\Cinf(U,\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))\to\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

induced by the projection
\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
. With this
definition we can show that
\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
is a smooth
differential algebra over
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
.
For general
V\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
, we define
\bCinf(U,V)
to be
the subspace of
\bCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{l})=\bCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}})^{l}

consisting of those vector valued function

f\colon U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
satisfying
f(U)\subset V
. By the
chain rule for differentiation there exists a well defined
composition

\bCinf(V,W)\times\bCinf(U,V)\to\bCinf(U,W)

for every
U\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k},\,V\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
and

W\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Thus we obtain an extended site

\bEucOp
with the coverage consisting of
D
-open covers.

Based on the results of the preceding sections, we define a
nonstandard extension
\bDiff
of the category
\Diff
to be
the category of concrete sheaves on
\bEucOp
. We see in
Section 4 that
\bDiff
is closed under small limits and
colimits, is enriched over itself and cartesian closed. It
is also shown that there is an adjunction

\tr\dashv\dg\colon\Diff\rightleftarrows\bDiff
such
that
\tr
extends the embedding
\EucOp\to\bEucOp

induced by the extension
\mathbb{R}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}
and
\dg
extends the
forgetful (or degradation) functor
\bEucOp\to\Diff
.

In Section 5 we construct a continuous linear embedding

\I_{U}\colon\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})\quad(U\in\EucOp)

which restricts to the inclusion

\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
given by
\tr
. The
construction of
\I_{U}
is analogous to the construction of
the embedding
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)\to\Gs(U)
into Colombeau’s special
algebra described in
[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
: We first construct an
embedding of the space of compactly supported distributions
and expand it over
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
by employing sheaf-theoretic
argument.

The mutual relation between
\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
and

\Gs(U)
can be clarified through their relations to the
algebra
\vphantom{\mathbb{E}(U)}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{E}(U)}
of asymptotic functions
introduced by
[
Oberguggenberger and
Todorov 1998
]
.
We show in Section 6 that there is a homomorphism of
differential algebras
\Gs(U)\to\vphantom{\mathbb{E}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{E}}(U)
which
respects the embeddings of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
and (the smooth version
of)
\vphantom{\mathbb{E}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{E}}(U)
is a subquotient of

\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
. Note however that neither
\Gs

nor
\vphantom{\mathbb{E}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{E}}
is closed under composition. In
order to attain composability, we need quasi-asymptoticity
instead of asymptoticity.

Finally, in Section 7 we present several examples exhibiting
that the use of extended morphism provides a flexible
environment to study homotopy theory of diffeological
spaces.
More specifically, we consider those set maps
X\to Y
that
extends to a morphism
\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\rho}{\!X}\to\vphantom{Y}^{\rho}{Y}
in
\rDiff
.
Such maps are called quasi-asymptotic maps from
X
to

Y
. Smooth maps are evidently quasi-asymptotic and, more
generally, so are piecewise smooth maps. By extending
smooth maps to quasi-asymptotic ones we can establish e.g. strict concatenation of paths in a space and homotopy
extension property for smooth relative cell complexes.

2.
Smooth fields of nonstandard numbers

2.1.
The category of diffeological spaces

We briefly recall the basic definitions and properties of
diffeological spaces. For details, see the book

[
Iglesias-Zemmour 2013
]
.

Denote by
\EucOp
the site consisting of open sets in the
Euclidean spaces and smooth (i.e. infinitely
differentiable) maps between them endowed with the the
coverage consisting of open covers.

Definition 2.1
.

A diffeological space is a concrete sheaf on
\EucOp
.
A smooth map between diffeological spaces is a morphism
between the corresponding sheaves. The category
consisting of diffeological spaces and smooth maps is
denoted by
\Diff
.

Given a diffeological space
X
, let us write

\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert=X(\mathbb{R}^{0})
and call it the underlying set of

X
. Then each section
\sigma\in X(U)
determines and
is determined by a set map
U\to\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
which takes every

u\in U
to the image of
\sigma
under the map

X(U)\to X(\mathbb{R}^{0})=\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
induced by
\mathbb{R}^{0}\to U
,

0\mapsto u
.
Thus we arrive at an alternative (in fact, more familiar)
definition of a diffeological space as a pair

(X,\mathcal{D})
consisting of a set
X
and the set of its
plots

\mathcal{D}\subset\coprod_{U\in\EucOp}\hom_{\Set}(U,X)
subject to the conditions:

(1)

Every constant map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X
belongs to

\mathcal{D}
.

(2)

A map
\sigma\colon U\to X
is in
\mathcal{D}
if and
only if it is locally so.

(3)

If
\sigma\colon U\to X
belongs to
\mathcal{D}
then
so does
\sigma\circ\phi\colon V\to X
for any smooth
map
\phi\colon V\to U
.

In this context, a smooth map from
(X,\mathcal{D})
to

(Y,\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
can be defined as a set map

f\colon X\to Y
such that

f\circ\sigma\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
holds for every

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}
.
From now on, we identify each section
\sigma\in X(U)
with
the corresponding plot which we simply denote

\sigma\colon U\to X
instead of

\sigma\colon U\to\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
unless distinction is
necessary.
Observe that every plot
\sigma\colon U\to X
can be
written as a composition

(2.1)

U=U\times\{\sigma\}\xrightarrow{\subset}U\times X(U)\xrightarrow{\subset}\underset{U\in\EucOp}{\textstyle\coprod}U\times X(U)\xrightarrow{\pr}\underset{U\in\EucOp}{\textstyle\coprod}U\times X(U)/\!\sim\ \ \cong\ \left\lvert{X}\right\rvert

where
\pr
is the projection with respect to the
equivalence relation generated by

(u,\tau\circ\phi)\sim(\phi(u),\tau)
for
u\in U
,

\tau\in X(V)
and
\phi\in\Cinf(U,V)
, and the last
bijection identifies the class of

(u,\sigma)\in U\times X(U)
with

\sigma(u)\in\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
.

The following constructions play crucial role in later
discussions.

Subspace
A smooth injection
f\colon X\to Y

is called an induction if
X
is the pullback of
Y
by
f
,
i.e.

X(U)=f^{*}Y(U):=\{\sigma\in\hom_{\Set}(U,X)\mid f\circ\sigma\in Y(U)\}\quad(U\in\EucOp)\,.

A subspace
A
of
X
is a subset of
\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
equipped
with the diffeology such that the inclusion
A\to X
is an
induction.

Quotient space
A smooth surjection

f\colon X\to Y
is called a subduction if
Y
is the
pushforward of
X
by
f
, i.e.

\displaystyle Y(U)=f_{*}X(U):=\{\sigma\in

\displaystyle\hom_{\Set}(U,Y)\mid\forall u\in U,

\displaystyle\exists\tau\in X(V)\ (u\in V\subset U),\ \sigma|V=f\circ\tau\}\quad(U\in\EucOp)\,.

If
\mathcal{R}
is an equivalence relation on the
underlying set of
X
then the quotient space

X/\mathcal{R}
is the set of equivalence classes

\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert/\mathcal{R}
equipped with the diffeology such that
the projection
X\to X/\mathcal{R}
is a subduction.

Product
Given a family of diffeological spaces

\{X_{j}\}_{j\in J}
their product
\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}
is
the sheaf

U\mapsto\textstyle\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}(U)\quad(U\in\EucOp)\,.

For each
k\in J
the
k
-th projection

\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}\to X_{k}
is the sheaf morphism

\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}(U)\to X_{k}(U)
.

Coproduct
The coproduct
\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}
is
defined as the sheafification of the presheaf

U\mapsto\textstyle\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}(U)\quad(U\in\EucOp)\,.

More explicitly, a set map

\sigma\colon U\to\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}
is a plot of

\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}
if for every
x\in U
there exist a
neighborhood
V\subset U
and a plot
\tau\in X_{j}(V)
for
some
j
such that
\tau=\sigma|V
holds.

Hom-object
Given
X,\,Y\in\Diff
,
\Cinf(X,Y)

denotes the set of smooth maps
X\to Y
equipped with the
coarsest diffeology such that the evaluation map

\ev\colon\Cinf(X,Y)\times X\to Y
is smooth.
Explicitly, we have

\Cinf(X,Y)(U):=\{\sigma\colon U\to\hom_{\Diff}(X,Y)\mid\forall\tau\in X(U),\ (u\mapsto\sigma(u)(\tau(u))\in Y(U)\}\,.

D
-Topology
Given a diffeological space
X
, its

D
-topology is the finest topology on
\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
such that
every plot of
X
is continuous; or equivalently, the
quotient topology with respect to the projection

\coprod U\times X(U)\to\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
(see
(
2.1
)), where each

U\in\EucOp
is endowed with standard metric topology.
Clearly, any smooth map between diffeological spaces induces
a continuous map between underlying sets equipped with

D
-topology. Hence there is a functor

L\colon\Diff\to\Top
which assigns to any diffeological
space its underlying set equipped with
D
-topology. Its
right adjoint
R\colon\Top\to\Diff
is given by

RY(U)=\hom_{\Top}(U,Y)\quad(Y\in\Top,\ U\in\EucOp)

that is,
RY
has the same underlying set as
Y
and all the
continuous maps
U\to Y
as its plots.
The unit of the adjunction
\eta\colon X\to RLX
is given
by the natural inclusion
X(U)\subset RLX(U)
which
identifies each plot
U\to X
of
X
with the corresponding
continuous map
U\to LX
regarded as a plot of
RLY
. On
the other hand, the counit
\varepsilon\colon LRY\to Y
is
given by the identity of the underlying set which is
continuous because the topology of
LRY
is finer than the
original topology of
Y
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
X=\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}
be the product of
diffeological spaces. Then
LX
has the box topology
generated by the family

\{\prod_{j\in J}U_{j}\mid U_{j}\ \text{open in}\ LX_{j}\}
, hence
LX
is not homeomorphic to the topological
product
\prod_{j\in J}LX_{j}
unless
LX_{J}
is nontrivial
for only finitely many
j
.
Notice that the left adjoint functor

L\colon\Diff\to\Top
preserves colimits but not
necessarily limits.

Any subset
X
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
gives rise to a diffeological space

U\mapsto\{f\colon U\to X\mid\text{the composition
$U\xrightarrow{f}X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}$ is smooth}\}

which we denote by the same letter
X
and call its
diffeology the standard diffeology of
X
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
X
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
then the
D
-topology
associated with the standard diffeology of
X
is the
subspace topology inherited from the standard metric
topology on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. For general
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
the

D
-topology of
X
is finer than its subspace topology.

Example 2.4
.

The
D
-topology of any interval in
\mathbb{R}
is the standard
subspace topology, while the
D
-topology of the set of
rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
is not the subspace
topology but the discrete topology.

We summarize the basic features of
\Diff
in the following.

Theorem 2.5
.

The category
\Diff
enjoys the following properties:

(1)

\Diff
is closed under small limits and colimits.

(2)

\Diff
is enriched over itself with
\Cinf(X,Y)
as
hom-object.

(3)

\Diff
is cartesian closed with

\Cinf(X,Y)
as exponential object.

(4)

There is a left adjoint functor

L\colon\Diff\to\Top
extending the inclusion

\EucOp\to\Top
.

2.2.
Nonstandard number fields via ultrapower
construction

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a free ultrafilter on the set of nonnegative
integers
\mathbb{N}
that do not contain any finite subset.
Given a predicate
P(n)
defined on
\mathbb{N}
, let us write
“
P(n)
a.e.” to mean
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid P(n)\}\in\mathcal{U}
.
Then there is a total preorder “
\leq
” on the algebra

\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
defined by

(a_{n})\leq(b_{n})\ \ \text{if and only if}\ \ a_{n}\leq b_{n}\ \text{\rm a.e.}

and we define an ordered set
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
to be the quotient of

\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
by the equivalence relation:

(a_{n})\sim(b_{n})\ \ \text{if and only if}\ \ (a_{n})\leq(b_{n})\ \&\ (b_{n})\leq(a_{n})\,.

Equivalently, we can define
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}/\nN(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})

where

\nN(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid x_{n}=0\ \text{\rm a.e.}\}\,.

Similarly, we define
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}/\nN(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})
where

\nN(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid x_{n}=0\ \text{\rm a.e.}\}\,.

Members of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
are called nonstandard (real or
complex) numbers. Nonstandard reals are also called
hyperreals following A. Robinson.
It is well known that with respect to the addition and
multiplication induced by the level-wise operations

((a_{n}),(b_{n}))\mapsto(a_{n}+b_{n})
or
(a_{n}b_{n})
,
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
is
a non-Archimedean real closed extension of
\mathbb{R}
and
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{C}}
is
an algebraically closed field of the form:

\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{C}}=\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}+\sqrt{-1}\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
.

Now, let
\rho\in\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
be a positive infinitesimal
represented by the net
(1/n)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
and denote by

\vphantom{\mathbb{N}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{N}}
the set of hypernatural numbers, i.e. the
image of
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
under the projection
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
.
For
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\,\mathbb{C}
we define
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}=\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})/\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
,
where

\displaystyle\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle=\{x\in\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}\mid\exists c\in\vphantom{\mathbb{N}}^{*}{\mathbb{N}},\ \left\lvert{x}\right\rvert\leq\rho^{-c}\},

\displaystyle\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

\displaystyle=\{x\in\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}\mid\forall d\in\vphantom{\mathbb{N}}^{*}{\mathbb{N}},\ \left\lvert{x}\right\rvert\leq\rho^{d}\}.

Alternatively, we can put
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}=\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})/\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
,
where

\displaystyle\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\exists(c_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}},\ \left\lvert{x_{n}}\right\rvert\leq n^{c_{n}}\ \text{\rm a.e.}\},

\displaystyle\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\forall(d_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}},\ \left\lvert{x_{n}}\right\rvert\leq 1/n^{d_{n}}\ \text{\rm a.e.}\}.

Members of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
are called quasi-asymptotic
(real or complex) numbers.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\bullet
be
*
or
\rho
. Then we have the
following.

(1)

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}
is a real closed, Cantor
complete and non-Archimedean extension of

\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{C}}
is an algebraically closed
field of the form
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{C}}=\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}+\sqrt{-1}\,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

We only consider the case
\bullet=\rho
as the case

\bullet=*
is well known. It is clear that
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
is a
ring and that we have
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}=\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}+\sqrt{-1}\,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}
.
To see that
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
is a field, suppose

(a_{n})\in\rM(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})
represents a non-zero class in

\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
. Then there exist
(c_{n}),\,(d_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such
that

\Phi=\{n\mid 1/n^{d_{n}}\leq\left\lvert{a_{n}}\right\rvert\leq 1/n^{c_{n}}\}\in\mathcal{U}\,.

Let us define
(b_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}}
by
b_{n}=1/a_{n}
if

n\in\Phi
and
b_{n}=1
if otherwise. Then

(b_{n})\in\rM(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})
because
\left\lvert{b_{n}}\right\rvert\leq n^{d_{n}}

a.e., and we have
[b_{n}]=[a_{n}]^{-1}
in
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
, implying

\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
is a field.
To see that
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
is algebraically closed, let

P(x)=x^{p}+a_{1}x^{p-1}+\cdots+a_{p}\in\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}[x]

and choose representatives
(a_{k,n})\in\rM(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})

for
a_{k}\in\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let

P_{n}(x)=x^{p}+a_{1,n}x^{p-1}+\cdots+a_{p,n}

and take
x_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
P_{n}(x_{n})=0
. Then

(x_{n})\in\rM(\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}})
because we have

\left\lvert{x_{n}}\right\rvert\leq 1+\left\lvert{a_{1,n}}\right\rvert+\cdots+\left\lvert{a_{p,n}}\right\rvert
, and hence determines an element

[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}
that satisfies
P([x_{n}])=0
. Thus
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{C}}

is algebraically closed, and consequently, its real part

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}
is a real closed field.
Moreover,
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}
is non-Archimedean because it contains a
non-zero infinitesimal
\rho=[1/n]
.
∎

Now, let us regard
\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}
as the product of copies of the
field
\mathbb{F}
endowed with the standard diffeology.
Then we can equip
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
with the quotient diffeology by the
projection
q\colon\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
, and consequently its
subquotient
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}=\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})/\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
. With respect to
these diffeologies we can prove the following.

Proposition 2.7
.

Both
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
and
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
are smooth field, that is, their
addition, multiplication, and inversion
are smooth with respect to the prescribed diffeology.
There are smooth embeddings
\mathbb{F}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
and
\mathbb{F}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}

induced by the diagonal inclusion
\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

For both
\bullet=*
and
\rho
, the addition and the
multiplication of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{F}}
are smooth because they are
induced by the smooth homomorphisms

\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}
,

((a_{n}),(b_{n}))\mapsto(a_{n}+b_{n})
or
(a_{n}b_{n})
, and
their restrictions to

\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\times\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\to\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
.
To see that the inversion of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
is smooth, consider the
product
i^{\mathbb{N}}\colon(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}
of
the smooth inclusion
i\colon\mathbb{F}^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}
. Then
the composition

q\circ i^{\mathbb{N}}\colon(\mathbb{F}^{\times})^{\mathbb{N}}\to(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}
is a subduction, and hence the inversion

(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}\to(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}
is smooth as it is
induced by the
\mathbb{N}
-fold product of copies of the smooth
inversion
\mathbb{F}^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}^{\times}
.
Similarly, the smoothness of the inversion

(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}\to(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}
follows from the facts
that
\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\setminus\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
is closed under
inversion of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
and also that the projection

\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\setminus\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\to(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})^{\times}
is a
subduction.
∎

Remark
.

In many publications, the symbol
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
denotes the field
of asymptotic numbers defined as a quotient

\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})/\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
, where

\displaystyle\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\exists c\in\mathbb{N},\ \left\lvert{x_{n}}\right\rvert\leq n^{c}\ \text{\rm a.e.}\}\,,

\displaystyle\aN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

\displaystyle=\{(x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}}\mid\forall d\in\mathbb{N},\ \left\lvert{x_{n}}\right\rvert\leq 1/n^{d}\ \text{\rm a.e.}\}\,.

In this paper, we denote
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{F}}=\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})/\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

to avoid the conflict with our usage of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
. Again,

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{R}}
is a non-Archimedean real closed field and
\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{C}}
is
an algebraically closed field of the form

\vphantom{\mathbb{C}}{}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{C}}=\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{R}}+\sqrt{-1}\,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{R}}
. We may regard
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\mathit{a}}{\mathbb{F}}
as a
subquotient of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
because we have

\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\subset\aN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\subset\aM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\subset\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
.

3.
Differential calculus on nonstandard Euclidean
spaces

3.1.
Topology of nonstandard Euclidean spaces

Let
k\geq 0
. Suppose we are given a net
(A_{n})
of
subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then an internal set defined by

(A_{n})
is a subset of the form

[A_{n}]=\{\,[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\mid(x_{n})\in\textstyle\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n}\cap\bM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}\,\}

where we put
\nM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
in the case

\bullet=*
. Let
\mathcal{B}(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k})
be the set of
internal sets
[V_{n}]\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
such that
V_{n}
is open
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 0
and
\bullet=*
or
\rho
. Then

\mathcal{B}(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k})
is a basis for the
D
-topology of

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, that is, a subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
is
D
-open in

\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
if and only if it is the union of members of

\mathcal{B}(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k})
.

Proof.

As the left adjoint
L\colon\Diff\to\Top
preserves
colimits,
U
is
D
-open if so is its preimage
U^{\prime}
by
the projection
\bM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. But as the

D
-topology of the product
(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}=(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{N}}
is
the box topology,

U^{\prime}
is open if and only if every its point is contained
in a neighborhood of the form

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}V_{n}\cap\bM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}
with
V_{n}
open
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This implies the statement for
U
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

With respect to the
D
-topology,
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
is Hausdorff and

\mathbb{R}^{k}
is discrete in
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Definition 3.3
.

For any subset
M
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
we denote by
\vphantom{\!M}{}^{\bullet}{\!M}
the
internal subset of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
defined by the constant net

(M)\subset(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{N}}
. In particular, if
U
is an
open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
then
\vphantom{U}{}^{\bullet}{U}
is a
D
-open subset
of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

3.2.
Infinitely differentiable functions on
nonstandard Euclidean spaces

Let
K
be a smooth field. A diffeological space
A
is called a smooth algebra
over
K
if it has a
K
-algebra structure such that
addition, multiplication, and scalar multiplication are
smooth.
If, in addition, there are smooth
K
-linear operators

\partial_{i}\colon A\to A
(
1\leq i\leq k
) such that

(i)

\partial_{i}(fg)=(\partial_{i}f)g+f(\partial_{i}g)
,
and

(ii)

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}f

for
1\leq i<j\leq k
,

then
A
is called a smooth differential algebra (SDA for
short) over
K
.
For any multi-index
\alpha=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
let
D^{\alpha}
denote the operator

\partial_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\partial_{k}^{a_{k}}\colon A\to A
.
Thanks to (ii) above,

D^{\alpha}D^{\beta}=D^{\alpha+\beta}
holds for any

\alpha,\,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

We now assign to any
D
-open subset
U\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
a
smooth differential algebra
\bCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{F}})
over
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{F}}

consisting of “infinitely differentiable” functions

U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{F}}
.

3.2.1.
The case
\bullet=*

We put

\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})=\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
. By the definition of the
diffeology of
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
we have the following.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
U
be a
D
-open subset of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Then a
function
f\colon U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
belongs to
\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

if and only if for each
x\in U
there exist a
D
-open
neighborhood
V=[V_{n}]\subset U
with
V_{n}
open in

\mathbb{R}^{k}
and a net of smooth functions

(g_{n}\colon V_{n}\to\mathbb{F})
such that

f([y_{n}])=[g_{n}(y_{n})]
holds for every

(y_{n})\in\prod_{n}V_{n}
.

This lemma enables us to define operators
\partial_{i}
on

\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
as in the following manner:
Let
f\in\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
and
x\in U
. Choose a net of
smooth functions
(g_{n}\colon V_{n}\to\mathbb{F})
such that

V=[V_{n}]
is a neighborhood of
x
and
f(y)=[g_{n}(y_{n})]

holds for all
y=[y_{n}]\in[V_{n}]
. Then we put

\partial_{i}f(x):=[\partial_{i}g_{n}(x_{n})]\in\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}\quad(1\leq i\leq k,\ x=[x_{n}])

This does not depend on the choices of
(g_{n})
and
(x_{n})
,
and determines a smooth map
\partial_{i}f\colon V\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
.
To see this, suppose
(h_{n}\colon V_{n}\to\mathbb{F})
is an another
family of smooth functions satisfying
f(y)=[h_{n}(y_{n})]

for
y=[y_{n}]\in V
. Let

k_{n}=h_{n}-g_{n}\colon V_{n}\to\mathbb{F}\ (n\in\mathbb{N})
and define
a smooth map
k\colon V\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
by

k([y_{n}])=[k_{n}(y_{n})]
. Then
k
is a constant function
with value
0
and we obtain

[\partial_{i}g_{n}(x_{n})]=[\partial_{i}h_{n}(x_{n})]
by the
following.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
k\colon V\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
is constant then we have

[\partial_{i}k_{n}(y_{n})]=0
for all
[y_{n}]\in V
.

Proof.

Suppose that
[\partial_{i}k_{n}(y_{n})]>0
holds for some

[y_{n}]\in V
. Then we have
\partial_{i}k_{n}(y_{n})>0

a.e. and there exists a net
(z_{n})
with
z_{n}\in V_{n}

such that
k_{n}(z_{n})>k_{n}(y_{n})
holds a.e. But this means

[k_{n}(z_{n})]>[k_{n}(y_{n})]
, contradicting to the assumption
that
k
is constant.
Similar contradiction occurs if we suppose

[\partial_{i}k_{n}(y_{n})]<0
, hence

[\partial_{i}k_{n}(y_{n})]=0
holds everywhere.
∎

For the particular set
\vphantom{U}{}^{*}{U}=[U]
arising from an open
subset
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
there is an injection

i_{U}\colon\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\nCinf(\vphantom{U}^{*}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

induced by the diagonal inclusion

\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\Cinf(U^{\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
, that is,

i_{U}(f)(x)=[f(x_{n})]
for
x=[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{*}{U}
.
Clearly, we have the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
D
-open subset
U
of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

is a smooth differential algebra over
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
. If
U
is
an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
then

i_{U}\colon\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\nCinf(\vphantom{U}^{*}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
is an
inclusion of smooth differential algebras.

The next proposition implies that the intermediate value
theorem (IVT) and the mean value theorem (MVT) hold at least
locally for any member of
\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
U
be a
D
-open subset of the form

U=[U_{n}]\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and
f\in\nCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}})
.
Suppose
f
is represented by a net of smooth functions

(f_{n}\colon U_{n}\to\mathbb{R})
, that is,
f(z)=[f_{n}(z_{n})]

holds for any
z=[z_{n}]\in U
. Then the following
hold for any
x,\,y\in U
such that
(1-t)x+ty\in U

for all
t\in\vphantom{[0,1]}^{*}{[0,1]}
.

IVT:
If
f(x)\neq f(y)
then for any
r\in\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}

between
f(x)
and
f(y)
there exists
z\in U
such that

f(z)=r
holds.

MVT:
There exists
c\in\vphantom{(0,1)}^{*}{(0,1)}
such that

f(y)-f(x)=\nabla f((1-c)x+cy)\cdot(y-x)
holds.

Here we denote by
\nabla
the gradient

(\partial_{i},\cdots,\partial_{k})
and by
\cdot
the dot
product.

Proof.

To prove IVT assume we have
f(x)<f(y)
and choose
representatives
(x_{n}),\,(y_{n})
and
(r_{n})
for
x
,
y

and
r
, respectively. Then
f_{n}(x_{n})<r_{n}<f_{n}(y_{n})

holds for almost every
n
and there exists

(c_{n})\in(0,1)^{\mathbb{N}}
that satisfies

f_{n}((1-c_{n})x_{n}+c_{n}y_{n})=r_{n}
a.e. Thus, by letting

c=[c_{n}]
and
z=(1-c)x+cy\in U
we have

f(z)=[f_{n}(z_{n})]=[r_{n}]=r
as desired.
MVT is proved similarly: For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
choose

c_{n}\in(0,1)
that satisfies

f_{n}(y_{n})-f_{n}(x_{n})=\nabla f_{n}((1-c_{n})x_{n}+c_{n}y_{n})\cdot(y_{n}-x_{n})\,.

Then
f(y)-f(x)=\nabla f((1-c)x+cy)\cdot(y-x)
holds
for
c=[c_{n}]\in\vphantom{(0,1)}^{*}{(0,1)}
.
∎

3.2.2.
The case
\bullet=\rho

Let
U
be a

D
-open subset of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and consider the projection

q^{k}\colon\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. For

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{M},\,\mathbf{N}
define subalgebras

\rL(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
of
\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
by

\displaystyle\rL(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})):=\{\,f\in

\displaystyle\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\mid\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k},

\displaystyle\forall y\in(q^{k})^{-1}(U),\ D^{\alpha}(f\circ q^{k})(y)\in\rL(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\,\}

Then
\rN(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))\subset\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
and we
can define
\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
to be the image of

\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
under the homomorphism

\Cinf(U,\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))\to\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
induced by

q\colon\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

There is
an isomorphism of smooth algebras

\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))/\rN(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))\cong\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

induced by the surjection

q_{*}\colon\rM(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))\to\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that we have

\ker q_{*}=\rN(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
. The inclusion

\ker q_{*}\supset\rN(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
is evident. To
verify the converse inclusion, suppose

f\colon U\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}
belongs to
\ker q_{*}
.
Let
U^{\prime}=(q^{k})^{-1}(U)\subset(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}})^{k}
and

g=f\circ q^{k}\in\nCinf(U^{\prime},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
. Then we have

g(y)\in\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
for all
y\in U^{\prime}
. But this means by
the MVT that we have

\partial_{i}g(y)\in\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\quad(1\leq i\leq k,\ y\in U^{\prime})\,.

Consequently, we have

D^{\alpha}g(y)=D^{\alpha}(f\circ q^{k})(y)\in\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})

for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and hence

f\in\rN(\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}}))
.
∎

We now construct an inclusion of
\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
into

\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
. For given
f\in\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
let

\vphantom{\!f}{}^{*}{\!f}=i_{U}(f)\in\Cinf(\vphantom{U}^{*}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
, so that we have

\vphantom{\!f}{}^{*}{\!f}(x)=[f(x_{n})]
for
x=[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{*}{U}
.
Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
,
D^{\alpha}(\vphantom{\!f}^{*}{\!f})

takes values in
\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})
because for any

(x_{n})\in U^{\mathbb{N}}
there is a net
(c_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}

satisfying
\left\lvert{D^{\alpha}f(x_{n})}\right\rvert\leq n^{c_{n}}

(
n\geq 2
).
Moreover, if
y-x\in\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}})^{k}
then we have

D^{\alpha}(\vphantom{\!f}^{*}{\!f})(y)-D^{\alpha}(\vphantom{\!f}^{*}{\!f})(x)\in\rN(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\quad(\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k})

by the MVT applied to
D^{\alpha}(\vphantom{\!f}^{*}{\!f})
.
Hence there is a unique function

\vphantom{\!f}{}^{\rho}{\!f}\in\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
making the diagram

\textstyle{\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{*}{\mathbb{R}})^{k}\cap\vphantom{U}^{*}{U}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\vphantom{\!f}{}^{*}{\!f}}
\scriptstyle{q^{k}}
\textstyle{\rM(\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{*}{\mathbb{F}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{q}
\textstyle{\vphantom{U}{}^{\rho}{U}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\vphantom{\!f}{}^{\rho}{\!f}}
\textstyle{\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}}

commutative. Thus we obtain a natural homomorphism

i_{U}\colon\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}),\ \ f\mapsto\vphantom{\!f}^{\rho}{\!f}

which is injective as its composition with the restriction
homomorphism
\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})\to\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

coincides with the injection
\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\Cinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

induced by the extension
\mathbb{F}\subset\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
.

Proposition 3.9
.

For any
D
-open subset
U
of
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
\rCinf(U,\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

is a smooth differential algebra over
\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
. If
U

4.
The category of nonstandard diffeological spaces

Recall that diffeological spaces can be defined as the
concrete sheaves on the site
\EucOp
. This leads us to the
definition of extended diffeological spaces below.

Definition 4.1
.

For
\bullet=*,\,\rho
we denote by
\bDiff
the
category of concrete sheaves on
\bEucOp
.
Objects of
\bDiff
are called nonstandard
diffeological spaces. More specifically, they are
called hyperdiffeological spaces when
\bullet=*

and quasi-asymptotic spaces when
\bullet=\rho
.

As in the case of usual diffeological spaces, nonstandard
diffeological spaces can be interpreted in terms of plots
and their axioms though the sources of plots are
D
-open
subsets of nonstandard Euclidean spaces
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\bullet}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

For any
X,\,Y\in\bDiff
define a hom-object

\bCinf(X,Y)\in\bDiff
by the formula:

\bCinf(X,Y)(U)=\{\sigma\colon U\to\hom_{\bDiff}(X,Y)\mid\forall\tau\in X(U),\ (u\mapsto\sigma(u)(\tau(u))\in Y(U)\}\,.

Then we have the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

The category
\bDiff
is

(1)
closed under small limits and
colimits,

(2)
enriched over itself with

\bCinf(X,Y)
as hom-object, and

(3)
cartesian closed with

\bCinf(X,Y)
as exponential objects.

Moreover, there is an adjunction

\tr\dashv\dg\colon\Diff\rightleftarrows\bDiff
such
that

(4)

\tr
extends

I\colon\EucOp\to\bEucOp
and preserves small colimits
and finite limits,

(5)

\dg
extends the inclusion

\bEucOp\to\Diff
and preserves small limits.

Proof.

(1) A category is closed under small limits if it has
equalizers and small products, and is closed under small
colimits if it has coequalizers and small coproducts.
Thus (i) follows from the constructions below.

Product
The product
\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}
of

X_{j}\in\bDiff
(
j\in J
) is the sheaf

U\mapsto\textstyle\prod_{j\in J}X_{j}(U)\quad(U\in\bEucOp)\,.

Coproduct

The coproduct
\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}
of
X_{j}\in\bDiff

(
j\in J
) is the sheafification of the presheaf

U\mapsto\textstyle\coprod_{j\in J}X_{j}(U)\quad(U\in\bEucOp)\,.

Equalizer
The equalizer
\Eq(f,g)
of a pair

f,\,g\colon X\to Y
is a subsheaf

U\mapsto\Eq(f,g)(U)=\{\sigma\in X(U)\mid f\circ\sigma=g\circ\sigma\}\quad(U\in\bEucOp)\,.

Coequalizer
The coequalizer
\Coeq(f,g)
of a pair

f,\,g\colon X\to Y
is the sheafification of the
quotient presheaf

U\mapsto\Coeq(f,g)(U)=\{p\circ\sigma\mid\sigma\in Y(U)\}\quad(U\in\bEucOp)

where
p
is the projection of
\left\lvert{Y}\right\rvert
onto its quotient
by the minimal equivalence relation such that

f(x)\sim g(x)
for every
x\in\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert
.

(2) We need to show that the composition

\bCinf(Y,Z)\times\bCinf(X,Y)\xrightarrow{\circ}\bCinf(X,Z)
is a morphism in
\bDiff
. Let

(\tau,\sigma)\in\bCinf(Y,Z)(U)\times\bCinf(X,Y)(U)

and
\eta\in X(U)
. Then

u\mapsto\sigma(\eta(u))\in Y(U)
and consequently,

u\mapsto\tau(\sigma(\eta(u)))=(\tau\circ\sigma)(\eta(u))\in Z(U)

implying that
\tau\circ\sigma\in\bCinf(X,Z)(U)
as
desired.

(3) Define a set map

\gamma\colon\left\lvert{\bCinf(X\times Y,Z)}\right\rvert\to\left\lvert{\bCinf(X,\Cinf(Y,Z))}\right\rvert
by the formula:

\gamma(f)(x)(y)=f(x,y)\quad(f\in\left\lvert{\bCinf(X\times Y,Z)}\right\rvert,\ x\in\left\lvert{X}\right\rvert,\ y\in\left\lvert{Y}\right\rvert)\,.

We show that
\gamma
is an isomorphism in
\bDiff
, i.e. for every
U\in\bEucOp
the post-composition

\bCinf(X\times Y,Z)(U)\to\bCinf(X,\Cinf(Y,Z))(U),\quad\sigma\mapsto\gamma\circ\sigma

is a well defined isomorphism.
Suppose
\sigma\in\bCinf(X\times Y,Z)(U)
and

(\tau,\eta)\in X(U)\times Y(U)
. Then we have

(u\mapsto\sigma(u)(\tau(u),\eta(u))=\gamma(\sigma(u))(\tau(u))(\eta(u)))\in Z(U)\,.

This shows that

\gamma\circ\sigma\in\bCinf(X,\Cinf(Y,Z))(U)
for

U\in\bEucOp
. Hence
\gamma
is a well defined smooth
map.
To see that
\gamma^{-1}
is smooth, let

\sigma\in\bCinf(X,\bCinf(Y,Z))(U)
,
\tau\in X(U)
and

\eta\in Y(U)
. Then we have

(u\mapsto\sigma(u)(\tau(u))(\eta(u))=\gamma^{-1}(\sigma(u))(\tau(u),\eta(u))))\in Z(U)

implying that

(u\mapsto\gamma^{-1}(\sigma(u)))\in\bCinf(X\times Y,Z)(U)
. Hence
\gamma^{-1}
is smooth and gives an
inverse to
\gamma
.

We now construct an adjunction

\tr\dashv\dg\colon\Diff\rightleftarrows\bDiff
and
show that (4) and (5) hold.
Given
X\in\Diff
, denote by
\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\bullet}{\!X}
the object of

\bDiff
whose underlying set is the quotient set

\textstyle\left\lvert{\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\bullet}{\!X}}\right\rvert:=\underset{U\in\EucOp}{\coprod}\,\vphantom{U}^{\bullet}{U}\times X(U)/\!\sim

where

is identified
with

such that
\sigma=\tau\circ\phi

and
\vphantom{\phi}{}^{\bullet}{\phi}(u)=v
, and whose plots are those set
maps locally of the form

(4.1)

W\xrightarrow{\phi}\vphantom{U}^{\bullet}{U}\xrightarrow{\vphantom{\sigma}{}^{\bullet}{\sigma}}\left\lvert{\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\bullet}{\!X}}\right\rvert\quad(W\in\bEucOp,\ \phi\in\bCinf(W,\vphantom{U}^{\bullet}{U}),\ \sigma\in X(U))

where
\vphantom{\sigma}{}^{\bullet}{\sigma}
is the natural map

\vphantom{U}{}^{\bullet}{U}=\vphantom{U}^{\bullet}{U}\times\{\sigma\}\subset\vphantom{U}^{\bullet}{U}\times X(U)\to\left\lvert{\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\bullet}{\!X}}\right\rvert
into the colimit. Then

On the other hand,

is

5.
Schwartz distributions as quasi-asymptotic
functions

5.1.
The space of Schwartz distributions

Let
U
be an open subset of a Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The
space of Schwartz distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
is a continuous
dual of the vector space
\mathcal{D}(U)
of test functions

U\to\mathbb{F}
equipped with a locally convex topology such that
a sequence
\{\varphi_{n}\}
converges to
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(U)

if and only if

(1)

there exists a bounded subset
M
such that

\supp\varphi_{n}\subset M
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and

(2)

D^{\alpha}\varphi_{n}
converge uniformly to

D^{\alpha}\varphi
for any multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

Any locally integrable function
f\colon U\to\mathbb{F}
gives
rise to a distribution

T_{f}\colon\varphi\mapsto\langle{T_{f}}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{U}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\quad(\,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(U)\,)

Thus we have

\tCinf(U)\subset\mathcal{C}^{0}(U)\subset\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
as
topological vector spaces.

For any
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
we can define

D^{\alpha}T
by

\langle{D^{\alpha}T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=(-1)^{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert}\langle{T}\,|\,{D^{\alpha}\varphi}\rangle\quad(\,\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(U)\,)

This extends the usual partial derivative of smooth
functions because

D^{\alpha}(T_{f})=T_{D^{\alpha}f}\ \text{for all
$f\in\tCinf(U)$ and $\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}$.}

holds for all
f\in\tCinf(U)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

There is an injection of differential vector spaces

\I_{U}\colon\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

which is continuous with respect to the
D
-topology on

\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
and extends the inclusion

\tCinf(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
.

To prove the theorem we reinterpret the space of
distributions in terms of smooth functionals
by utilizing the notion of “convenient vector space”
introduced by
[
Fröhlicher and Kriegl 1988
, 2.6.3]
.
Every topological vector space
X
admits a canonical
diffeology characterized as
the coarsest diffeology with respect to which every
continuous linear functional
X\to\mathbb{F}
is smooth (cf.
[
Kock and Reyes 2004
, Section 2]
).
A convenient vector space is a topological vector space
X

such that a linear functional
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{F}
is
continuous if and only if it is smooth with respect to the
canonical diffeology of
X
.
With respect to the strong topology,
\mathcal{D}(U)
is a convenient
vector space (cf.
[
Fröhlicher and Kriegl 1988
, Remark 3.5]
),
hence its topological dual
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
has the same underlying
space as the smooth dual
D^{\prime}(U)
of the smooth vector space

D(U)
of test functions on
U
equipped with canonical
diffeology (cf.
[
Giordano and Wu 2015
, Definition 4.1]
).

The
D
-topology of
D(U)
is finer than the locally convex
topology of
\mathcal{D}(U)
by
[
Giordano and Wu 2015
, Corollary 4.12]
.
Hence we have the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

The identity map
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)\to D^{\prime}(U)
is continuous with
respect to the
D
-topology on
D^{\prime}(U)
.

Consequently, Theorem
5.1

follows from its smooth version below.

Theorem 5.3
.

There is an injection of smooth differential vector spaces

I_{U}\colon D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

which extends the inclusion

\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
.

To construct
I_{U}
, we first introduce as in

[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
a linear embedding

E^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
and extend it to
D^{\prime}(U)
by
employing sheaf-theoretic construction.
Here
E^{\prime}(U)
denotes the smooth dual of
\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
, that
is, the subspace of
\Cinf(\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F}),\mathbb{F})
consisting of

\mathbb{F}
-linear functionals on
\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
. We may regard

E^{\prime}(U)
as a smooth version of the space of compactly
supported distributions.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
U\in\EucOp
we have the following.

(1)
The linear map
E^{\prime}(U)\to D^{\prime}(U)

induced by the inclusion
D(U)\to\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
is a
smooth injection.

(2)
Any
\eta\in D(U)
induces a
smooth homomorphism
D^{\prime}(U)\to E^{\prime}(U)
which takes
T
to
the product
\eta\,T
.

Proof.

Following
[
Giordano and Wu 2015
]
denote by

D^{\mathrm{s}}(U)
the same set as
D(U)
but considered
as a subspace of
\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
. Let

D^{\mathrm{s}}_{K}(U)=\{f\in D^{\mathrm{s}}(U)\mid\supp f\subset K\}\subset D^{\mathrm{s}}(U)\,.

for any
K\Subset U
. Then we see by
[
Kock and Reyes 2004
, Theorem
2.3]
(see also
[
Giordano and Wu 2015
, Theorem 4.5 and Corollary
4.11]
) that the following hold in
\Diff
:

(5.1)

D(U)\subset D^{\mathrm{s}}(U),\ \ D_{K}(U)=D^{\mathrm{s}}_{K}(U)\quad(V\in\EucOp)

In fact,
[
Kock and Reyes 2004
, Theorem 2.3]
says that a set
map
\sigma\colon V\to D(U)\subset\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
is a
plot of
D(U)
if and only if its transpose

\sigma^{\vee}
belongs to

\Cinf(V\times U,\mathbb{F})=\Cinf(V,\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F}))
and is
locally of uniformly bounded support, implying
(
5.1
).
Thus the inclusion
D(U)\subset\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
is smooth,
and hence induces a smooth linear map
E^{\prime}(U)\to D^{\prime}(U)

which is injective because
D(U)
is dense in

\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
.

Similarly, the multiplication by
\eta
defines a smooth
homomorphism

\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to D^{\mathrm{s}}_{K}(U)=D_{K}(U)\to D(U)\ \ (K=\supp\eta)

which in turn induces

D^{\prime}(U)\to E^{\prime}(U),\ T\mapsto\eta\,T
.
∎

For any
T\in E^{\prime}(U)
and
\varphi\in\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})

denote by
T*\varphi
the function on
U
given by the
formula

(T*\varphi)(x)=\langle{\widetilde{T}}\,|\,{\varphi_{x}}\rangle\quad(x\in U)

where
\widetilde{T}\in E^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is the evident extension
of
T
and
\varphi_{x}
is the smooth function

t\mapsto\varphi(x-t)
.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have the following:

(1)
The correspondence

(T,\varphi)\mapsto T*\varphi
determines a smooth map

E^{\prime}(U)\times\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})\to\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\,.

(2)
We have

D^{\alpha}(T*\varphi)=D^{\alpha}T*\varphi
for any

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

Proof.

(1) It suffices to prove that the following
operations are smooth.

(i)

\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})\times U\to\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})
,
(\varphi,x)\mapsto\varphi_{x}
, and

(ii)

E^{\prime}(U)\times\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})\to\mathbb{F}
,
(T,\psi)\mapsto\langle{\widetilde{T}}\,|\,{\psi}\rangle
.

It is evident that (i) is smooth. The smoothness of (ii)
is a consequence of the facts that the inclusion

E^{\prime}(U)\to E^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is smooth and that
E^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a
smooth dual of
\Cinf(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathbb{F})
.

(2) This is clear by the definition.
∎

Now, let
\varrho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
be a smooth
function that has rapidly decreasing partial derivatives and
satisfies

(5.2)

\text{(i)}\ \int\varrho(x)\,dx=1\,,\qquad\text{(ii)}\ \displaystyle\int x^{\alpha}\varrho(x)\,dx=0\ \text{for}\ \alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\setminus\{0\}

and define
j_{U}\colon E^{\prime}(U)\to\Cinf(U^{\mathbb{N}},\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
by

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))=((T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n}))\quad(\,T\in E^{\prime}(U),\ (x_{n})\in U^{\mathbb{N}}\,)

where
\varrho_{n}(x)=n^{k}\varrho(nx)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

n\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that
\varrho_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})

enjoys the properties similar to
(
5.2
).
Also, let
\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})=U^{\mathbb{N}}\cap\rM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}
, so
that
\vphantom{U}{}^{\rho}{U}
is the image of
\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})
under the
projection
\rM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

The following hold for every
T\in E^{\prime}(U)
.

(1)

j_{U}(D^{\alpha}T)((x_{n}))=D^{\alpha}(j_{U}(T))((x_{n}))
for
any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and
(x_{n})\in U^{\mathbb{N}}
.

(2)

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))\in\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})

for any
(x_{n})\in\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})
.

(3)
If

(x_{n}),\,(y_{n})\in\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})
and

(x_{n})-(y_{n})\in\rN(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})^{k}
then

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))-j_{U}(T)((y_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\,.

(4)
If

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
for all

(x_{n})\in\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})
then
T=0
.

(5)
If
T=T_{f}
for some

f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(U,\mathbb{F})
then for any
(x_{n})\in U^{\mathbb{N}}

we have

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))-(f(x_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\,.

Proof.

(1)
j_{U}(D^{\alpha}T)((x_{n}))=((D^{\alpha}T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n}))=(D^{\alpha}(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n}))=D^{\alpha}(j_{U}(T))((x_{n}))
by
Lemma
5.5
(2).

(2) Every
T\in E^{\prime}(U)
is of the form

\sum_{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert\leq r}D^{\alpha}f_{\alpha}
, where

f_{\alpha}
is a continuous function (regarded as a
distribution) such that
\supp f_{\alpha}
is contained in
an arbitrary neighborhood of
\supp T
(see

[
Grosser et al. 2001
, 1.2.9]
). Thus we need only consider the case

T=D^{\alpha}f
with

f\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{c}(U)\subset\tCinf(\mathbb{R}^{k})
. But
then, we have

\begin{split}(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})&=\int f(x_{n}-t)D^{\alpha}\varrho_{n}(t)\,dt\\
&=\int f(x_{n}-t)n^{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert+k}D^{\alpha}\varrho(nt)\,dt\\
&=n^{\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert}\int f(x_{n}-s/n)D^{\alpha}\varrho(s)\,ds\,.\end{split}

Hence for each
n\geq 2
there exists
c_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such
that
\left\lvert{(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})}\right\rvert\leq n^{c_{n}}
, implying

((T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n}))\in\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
.

(3) By the local Lipshitz continuity of
T*\varrho_{n}

there exists
K_{n}>0
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
that satisfies

\left\lvert{(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})-(T*\varrho_{n})(y_{n})}\right\rvert\leq K_{n}\left\lvert{x_{n}-y_{n}}\right\rvert\,.

Here we may assume
(K_{n})\in\rM(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})
because

(T*\varrho_{n})
takes values in
\rM(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
. It
follows, in particular, that if

(\left\lvert{x_{n}-y_{n}}\right\rvert)\in\rN(\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}})
then

(\left\lvert{(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})-(T*\varrho_{n})(y_{n})}\right\rvert)\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
, and hence

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))-j_{U}(T)((y_{n}))=((T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})-(T*\varrho_{n})(y_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})\,.

(4) For any
\varphi\in\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
there holds

\langle{T*\varrho_{n}}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle\to\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle
since
T*\varrho_{n}\to T
in

E^{\prime}(U)
. On the other hand, we have

\langle{T*\varrho_{n}}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle\to 0
because the
condition
((T*\varrho_{n})(x))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
implies

T*\varrho_{n}\to 0
uniformly on
\supp T
. Hence we
have
\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=0
for any

\varphi\in\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
, implying
T=0
.

(5) Let
j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))-(f(x_{n}))=(\Delta_{n}(x_{n}))
,
so that we have

\Delta_{n}(x_{n})=(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})-f(x_{n})=\int(f(x_{n}-t)-f(x_{n}))\,\varrho_{n}(t)\,dt\,.

By the Taylor expansion formula applied to
f

we can write
f(x_{n}-t)-f(x_{n})=P(d,t)+R(d,t)
, where

\displaystyle P(d,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq\left\lvert{\alpha}\right\rvert<d}\frac{D^{\alpha}f(x_{n})}{\alpha\,!}(-t)^{\alpha},

\displaystyle R(d,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\left\lvert{\beta}\right\rvert=d}\frac{D^{\beta}f(x_{n}-\theta t)}{\beta\,!}\,(-t)^{\beta}\ \ \text{for some $\theta\in(0,1)$}\,.

But we have
\displaystyle\int P(d,t)\,\varrho_{n}(t)\,dt=0
because

\displaystyle\int t^{\alpha}\varrho_{n}(t)\,dt=0
holds for

\alpha\neq 0
. Hence

\begin{split}\Delta_{n}(x)&=\int(P(d,t)+R(d,t))\,\varrho_{n}(t)\,dt\\
&=\int R(d,t)\,n^{k}\varrho(nt)\,dt\\
&=\int R(d,s/n)\,\varrho(s)\,ds\\
&=\sum_{\left\lvert{\beta}\right\rvert=d}\int\dfrac{D^{\beta}f(x_{n}-\theta s/n)}{\beta\,!}\,(-s/n)^{\beta}\,\varrho(s)\,ds\\
&=n^{-d}\sum_{\left\lvert{\beta}\right\rvert=d}\int\dfrac{D^{\beta}f(x_{n}-\theta s/n)}{\beta\,!}\,(-s)^{\beta}\,\varrho(s)\,ds\,.\end{split}

Since
f
is compactly supported and

\varrho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, we can attain

\left\lvert{\Delta_{n}(x_{n})}\right\rvert\leq n^{-d_{n}}
for any
d_{n}\in\mathbb{N}

(
n\geq 2
) by taking
d
sufficiently large.
Consequently, we have

j_{U}(f)((x_{n}))-(f(x_{n}))=(\Delta_{n}(x_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Lemmas above we have the
following.

Proposition 5.7
.

The map
j_{U}
induces an injection of smooth differential
vector spaces

J_{U}\colon E^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

which restricts to the inclusion

D(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
given by
\tr|D(U)
.

In order to extend
J_{U}
to an injection

D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
we need the proposition
below. Denote by
\mathcal{A}
the sheaf of rings

U\mapsto\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
.

Proposition 5.8
.

The sheaf of
\mathcal{A}
-modules

U\mapsto\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
is fine, i.e. admits
a partition of unity.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{\lambda}\}
be an open cover of
U

and choose a smooth partition of unity

\{\chi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
subordinate to
\mathfrak{U}
. Assign
to each
j\in\mathbb{N}
a sheaf morphism

\chi_{j\,*}\colon\rCinf(\vphantom{(}^{\rho}{(}-),\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})\to\rCinf(\vphantom{(}^{\rho}{(}-),\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
defined by

\chi_{j\,*}(f)=(\tr(\chi_{j})|\vphantom{V}^{\rho}{V})f\quad(\,f\in\rCinf(\vphantom{V}^{\rho}{V},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})\,)

Then we can show that the family
\{\chi_{j\,*}\}

provides a partition of unity, that is, satisfies (i)
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\chi_{j\,*}=1
, and (ii)
\supp\chi_{j\,*}\subset U_{\lambda(j)}
for some

\lambda(j)
.
∎

Now, let
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{\lambda}\}
be an open covering of

U
such that the closure
\overline{U}_{\!\lambda}
is
compact for each
\lambda
and
\{\psi_{\lambda}\}
be a
family of elements of
D(U)
such that

\psi_{\lambda}\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of

\overline{U}_{\!\lambda}
.

Lemma 5.9
.

The following hold.

(1)
If
T\in E^{\prime}(U)
then

\supp J_{U}(T)=\vphantom{\,\supp T}^{\rho}{\,\supp T}\subset\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}
.

(2)
For any
T\in D^{\prime}(U)
and

\lambda,\,\mu\in\Lambda
, we have

J_{U}(\psi_{\lambda}T)|\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}\cap\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\mu}=J_{U}(\psi_{\mu}T)|\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}\cap\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\mu}
.

Proof.

(1) To prove the inclusion

\supp J_{U}(T)\subset\vphantom{\,\supp T}^{\rho}{\,\supp T}
, it suffices to
show that
x\in\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}\setminus\vphantom{\,\supp T}^{\rho}{\,\supp T}

implies
J_{U}(T)(x)=0
. Here, we may suppose
x
is
represented by a net
(x_{n})\in\rM(U^{\mathbb{N}})
such that

x_{n}\not\in\supp T
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. But then, by
arguing as in Proposition 1.2.12 of
[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
we can
show that
\left\lvert{(T*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})}\right\rvert\leq 1/n^{d_{n}}

holds for any
d_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus we have

j_{U}(T)((x_{n}))\in\rN(\mathbb{F}^{\mathbb{N}})
, implying

J_{U}(T)(x)=0
. The converse inclusion

\supp J_{U}(T)\supset\vphantom{\,\supp T}^{\rho}{\,\supp T}
is proved similarly
by using the argument of Proposition 1.2.12 of

[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
.
(2) This is clear from the fact that

\psi_{\lambda}\equiv\psi_{\lambda}\equiv 1
on

U_{\lambda}\cap U_{\mu}
.
∎

Proposition
5.8
and
Lemma
5.9
enable us to extend
J_{U}

to
I_{U}\colon D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
by putting

I_{U}(T)=\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\,\chi_{j\,*}(J_{U}(\psi_{\lambda(j)}\,T))\quad(\,T\in D^{\prime}(U)\,)

where
\{\chi_{j}\}
is a smooth partition of unity
subordinate to the open cover
\mathfrak{U}
of
U
.

Denote by
\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})_{0}
the differential
subalgebra of
\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
consisting of those

f\colon\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
represented by a net of smooth
functions
(f_{n})\in\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})^{\mathbb{N}}
, so that we have

f(x)=[f_{n}(x_{n})]
for every
x=[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}
. By
the construction,
I_{U}
factors as a composition

D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})_{0}\xrightarrow{\subset}\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

and there is a bilinear pairing

\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})_{0}\times D(U)\to\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}}
which takes

([f_{n}],\varphi)
to
[\langle{f_{n}}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle]
, where

\langle{f_{n}}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=\int_{U}f_{n}(x)\varphi(x)\,dx\quad(n\in\mathbb{N})\,.

We show that this pairing is compatible with the duality
pairing
D^{\prime}(U)\times\mathcal{D}(U)\to\mathbb{F}
under

I_{U}
.

Lemma 5.10
.

We have
\langle{I_{U}(T)}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle
for every
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(U)
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(U)
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in D(U)
and

M=\max\{j\in\mathbb{N}\mid\supp\varphi\cap\supp\chi_{j}\neq\emptyset\}
. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{M}\chi_{j}=1

on
\supp\varphi
, and we have

\langle{I_{U}(T)}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\,\langle{\chi_{j\,*}(J_{U}(\psi_{\lambda(j)}\,T))}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=[\langle{T}\,|\,{\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{M}\chi_{j}\psi_{\lambda(j)}\,\varphi}\rangle]=[\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle]=\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle

for every
T\in D^{\prime}(U)
.
∎

To complete the proof of
Theorem
5.3
it suffices to
verify the following.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
U
be a
D
-open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then we have the
following.

(1)

I_{U}\colon D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
does not depend
on the choice of
\{U_{\lambda}\}
,
\{\psi_{\lambda}\}

and
\{\chi_{j}\}
.

(2)

I_{U}\colon D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
is a linear
injection of smooth differential vector spaces.

(3)

I_{U}|E^{\prime}(U)=J_{U}\colon E^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
.

(4)

I_{U}|_{\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})}=\tr\colon\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
.

Proof.

(1) This follows from the fineness of the sheaf

U\mapsto\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

and Lemma
5.9
. (Cf. Proposition 1.2.18 of
[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
.)

(2) It is evident that
I_{U}
is a linear map of smooth
vector spaces. Its injectivity follows from
Lemma
5.10
because

I_{U}(T)=0
implies that
\langle{T}\,|\,{\varphi}\rangle=0
holds
for every
\varphi\in D(U)
, meaning
T=0
. That
I_{U}

commutes with differential operators can be proved as in
Proposition 1.2.17 of
[
Grosser et al. 2001
]
by using the fact that

\psi_{\lambda(j)}=1
on a neighborhood of the closure
of
U_{\lambda(j)}
.

(3) Let
T\in E^{\prime}(U)
. To verify
I_{U}(T)=J_{U}(T)
, it
suffices to show that
J_{U}(T)-I_{U}(T)=0
holds on each

\vphantom{U}{}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}
. Let

F=(1-\psi_{\lambda})T\in E^{\prime}(U)
. Then for any

x=[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}
we have

J_{U}(T)(x)-I_{U}(T)(x)=[(F*\varrho_{n})(x_{n})]=J_{U}(F)(x)=0

because

\vphantom{U}{}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}\subset(\vphantom{\,\supp F}^{\rho}{\,\supp F})^{\,c}=\supp J_{U}(F)^{\,c}
holds by Lemma
5.9

(1).

(4) Let
f\in\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})
. Then for any

\lambda\in\Lambda
and

x=[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}_{\lambda}
we have

I_{U}(f)(x)=I_{U}(\psi_{\lambda}f)(x)=J_{U}(\psi_{\lambda}f)(x)=\tr(\psi_{\lambda}f)(x)=\tr(f)(x)

by (3) above and
Proposition
5.7
.
Thus
I_{U}|_{\Cinf(U,\mathbb{F})}=\tr
holds.
∎

Remark
.

Denote by
\EucOp_{0}
the sub-site of
\EucOp
having
smooth injections as morphisms. Then both

U\mapsto D^{\prime}(U)
and
\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})
are sheaves on

\EucOp_{0}
and the injections

I_{U}\colon D^{\prime}(U)\to\rCinf(\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U},\vphantom{\mathbb{F}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{F}})

.)

6.
The relation with Colombeau’s algebra

We first recall briefly the definition of the special
Colombeau algebra of generalized functions and its smooth
version introduced by
[
Giordano and Wu 2015
]
.

6.1.
Special Colombeau algebra

Let
J=(0,1]
and consider the space
\tCinf(U)^{J}
of

J
-nets in the locally convex space of
\mathbb{F}
-valued smooth
functions on an open subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then the special
Colombeau algebra on
U
is defined to be the quotient

\Gs(U)=\sM(\tCinf(U)^{J})/\sN(\tCinf(U)^{J})

of the subalgebra of “moderate nets” over that of
“negligible nets.” More explicitly,

\displaystyle\sM(\tCinf(U)^{J})

7.
Applications of asymptotic functions to homotopy
theory of diffeological spaces

Quasi-asymptotic maps can be expected to provide “weak
solutions” to problems that are hard or impossible to solve
within the usual framework of smooth maps. In this section,
we present some modest applications related to homotopy
theory of diffeological spaces.

7.1.
Quasi-asymptotic maps between diffeological
spaces

Definition 7.1
.

Given
X,\,Y\in\Diff
denote by
\wCinf(X,Y)
the
diffeological subspace of
\rCinf(\vphantom{\!X}^{\rho}{\!X},\vphantom{Y}^{\rho}{Y})

consisting of such morphisms
\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\rho}{\!X}\to\vphantom{Y}^{\rho}{Y}
that
restrict to a set map
X\to Y
. Members of
\wCinf(X,Y)

are called quasi-asymptotic maps from
X
to
Y
.
There are a smooth inclusion
\Cinf(X,Y)\to\wCinf(X,Y)

given by the unit
1\to\dg\circ\tr
and a smooth
composition

\wCinf(Y,Z)\times\wCinf(X,Y)\to\wCinf(X,Z)

induced by the composition in
\rDiff
. Thus we obtain
a category
\wDiff
enriched over
\Diff
with
diffeological spaces as objects and
\wCinf(X,Y)
as the
set of morphism from
X
to
Y
.

Beware that
\wDiff
is no longer a concrete category
because
\wCinf(X,Y)
is not in general a subset of

\hom_{\Set}(X,Y)
.

Proposition 7.2
.

\wDiff
is closed under small limits and colimits.

Proof.

Since the forgetful functor
\rDiff\to\Set
reflects
small limits and colimits, so is its restriction

\wDiff\to\Set
.
∎

Typical examples of quasi-asymptotic maps are given by
piecewise smooth maps defined as follows.

Definition 7.3
.

A continuous map

defined on an open
subset
U
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is said to be piecewise smooth
if for each

there is an open subset

W\subset U
such that
x\in\overline{W}
and
f|W
is
smooth.

The functions
\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
below are apparently piecewise
smooth.

Euclidean norm:

x\mapsto\|{x}\|

Maximum value:

(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})\mapsto\max\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\}

Minimum value:

(x_{1},\cdots,x_{k})\mapsto\min\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{k}\}

Note also that if
f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{l}
is piecewise smooth
then so is its restriction to arbitrary open subset

.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let

be a continuous map from
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
to

Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{l}
. Suppose

extends to a piecewise

defined on an open subset

is quasi-asymptotic, that
is, there is

.

Proof.

Let

(

) be the

and denote

Also let
\vphantom{\!g}{}^{\rho}{\!g}\colon\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}\to\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
be the
continuous map which takes
[x_{n}]\in\vphantom{U}^{\rho}{U}
to

[g(x_{n})]\in\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
. To prove the proposition it
suffices to show that

(7.1)

holds for all

,
for this means

satisfies

.
If
W
is an open subset of

such that

is smooth on

W
then (

) holds for all

x\in\vphantom{W}^{\rho}{W}
by Proposition
5.11

(4). But, as both

and
\vphantom{\!g}{}^{\rho}{\!g}
are continuous
and
\vphantom{\mathbb{R}}{}^{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{l}
is Hausdorff under

-topology,
(

) extends to all

Consequently, (

there is an open

∎

The following is also useful for constructing
quasi-asymptotic maps.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let

and

be maps between diffeological spaces. Suppose

is a
smooth subduction. Then

is quasi-asymptotic if and

.

Proof.

The “only if” part is obvious. To prove the “if”
part, suppose that there exists

such that

holds.
Let
\vphantom{p}{}^{\rho}{p}=\tr(f)\in\rCinf(\vphantom{\!X}^{\rho}{\!X},\vphantom{\!Y}^{\rho}{\!Y})
.
Then
\vphantom{p}{}^{\rho}{p}
is a subduction in
\rDiff
because
\tr

preserves colimits. Hence

factors as a

\vphantom{\!X}{}^{\rho}{\!X}\xrightarrow{\vphantom{p}{}^{\rho}{p}}\vphantom{Y}^{\rho}{Y}\xrightarrow{F}\vphantom{\!Z}^{\rho}{\!Z}

with

, implying that

∎

7.2.
Concatenation of quasi-asymptotic paths

Let

be the unit interval in
\mathbb{R}
and

. Denote
by

the space of all quasi-asymptotic paths in

We show that any two quasi-asymptotic paths

such that

concatenated together to form a new quasi-asymptotic path.
Let

\Path(X)\times_{X}\Path(X)=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\Path(X)\times\Path(X)\mid\alpha(1)=\beta(0)\}

Theorem 7.6
.

There is a smooth map

\Path(X)\times_{X}\Path(X)\xrightarrow{*}\Path(X)

which takes

to a path

\alpha*\beta\in\Path(X)
such that

has value
\alpha(2t)
if

and
\beta(2t-1)
if

.

Proof.

Let
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
be non-decreasing piecewise
linear functions
\mathbb{R}\to I
such that
\ell_{1}(t)=2t
for

and
\ell_{2}(t)=2t-1
for

.
Then

and

are quasi-asymptotic by

and hence so is

given by

. ∎

7.3.
Smooth cell complexes and homotopy extension
property

As in the case of
\Top
or

\Diff
, there is a notion of homotopy in the category

\bDiff
: a homotopy between morphisms
f,\,g\in\bCinf(X,Y)
is a morphism
H\in\bCinf(X\times\vphantom{\!I}^{\bullet}{\!I},Y)
such that
H\circ i_{0}=f
and
H\circ i_{1}=g
, where
i_{\alpha}
is the inclusion
X\to X\times\{\alpha\}\subset X\times\vphantom{\!I}^{\bullet}{\!I}
for
\alpha=0,\,1
.
Likewise, a quasi-asymptotic homotopy between
quasi-asymptotic maps
f,\,g\in\wCinf(X,Y)
is defined to be a quasi-asymptotic map
H\in\wCinf(X\times I,Y)
satisfying
H\circ i_{0}=f
and
H\circ i_{1}=g
.

The following is a diffeological analog to the notion of
relative cell complex due to
[
Hovey 1999
]
which plays a
crucial role in the homotopy theory of topological spaces.

Definition 7.7
.

A pair of diffeological spaces
(X,A)
is called a smooth
relative cell complex if there is an ordinal
\delta
and a

\delta
-sequence
Z\colon\delta\to\Diff
such that the
composition
Z_{0}\to\mbox{colim}Z
coincides with the
inclusion
i\colon A\to X
and for each successor ordinal

\beta<\delta

\phi_{\beta}\colon\partial I^{k}\to Z_{\beta-1}
, called an
attaching map, such that
Z_{\beta}
is diffeomorphic to
the adjunction space
Z_{\beta-1}\cup_{\phi_{\beta}}I^{k}
,
i.e. we have a pushout square

\textstyle{\partial I^{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cap}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{\beta}}
\textstyle{Z_{\beta-1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{I^{k}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Phi_{\beta}}
\textstyle{Z_{\beta},}

In particular, if
A=\emptyset
then

is called a
smooth cell complex.

In
\Top
, relative cell complexes have a useful property
that they satisfy homotopy extension property.
Unfortunately, its smooth version no longer holds as can be
seen from the fact that
\partial I\times I\cup I\times\{0\}

is not a smooth deformation retract of

improve the situation by extending morphisms from smooth
maps to quasi-asymptotic ones. More precisely, we have the
following.

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
(X,A)
be a smooth relative cell complex and

be a quasi-asymptotic map. Suppose
there is a quasi-asymptotic homotopy

and

To prove this, we need several lemmas.
For

and

Lemma 7.9

is a deformation retract of

in
\wDiff
.

Proof.

Let

be the radial projection from

. Then

is piecewise linear
(hence piecewise smooth) with respect to the decomposition
of

determined by the vertices of

and

Hence

is a
quasi-asymptotic map given as a restriction of

Moreover, for every

\vphantom{i}{}^{\rho}{i}\circ\widehat{p}\simeq 1

Thus

\wDiff

∎

Corollary 7.10

Any quasi-asymptotic map

We also need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.11

Let

\textstyle i\times 1\bigcup\Phi\times 1\colon Y\times I\coprod I^{k}\times I\to Z\times I

Let

given

, and let

. Since

is a plot of

, there exists a plot

such that

or a plot

or

. This means

Let
