The Honeycomb Conjecture in normed planes and an alpha-convex variant of a theorem of Dowker Thanks: Partially supported by the ERC Advanced Grant “ERMiD”, the National Research, Development and Innovation Office, NKFI, K-147544 grant, and project TKP2021-NVA-09 provided by the Ministry of Innovation and Technology of Hungary.
Zsolt Lángi, Shanshan Wang

Abstract
. The Honeycomb Conjecture states that among tilings with unit area cells in the Euclidean plane, the average perimeter of a cell is minimal for a regular hexagonal tiling. This conjecture was proved by L. Fejes Tóth for convex tilings, and by Hales for not necessarily convex tilings. In this paper we investigate the same question for tilings of a given normed plane, and show that among normal, convex tilings in a normed plane, the average squared perimeter of a cell is minimal for a tiling whose cells are translates of a centrally symmetric hexagon. We also show that the question whether the same statement is true for the average perimeter of a cell is closely related to an α \alpha -convex variant of a theorem of Dowker on the area of polygons circumscribed about a convex disk. Exploring this connection we find families of norms in which the average perimeter of a cell of a tiling is minimal for a hexagonal tiling, and prove some additional related results. Finally, we apply our method to give a partial answer to a problem of Steinhaus about the isoperimetric ratios of cells of certain tilings in the Euclidean plane, appeared in an open problem book of Croft, Falconer and Guy.

1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to investigate certain properties of mosaics. A
mosaic
or
tiling
of the Euclidean
d
-space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a countable family of compact sets
\mathcal{T}
, called cells, with the property that
\bigcup\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the interiors of any two distinct cells are disjoint. Here, for convenience, it is usually assumed that every cell has nonempty interior. A tiling is
convex
, if every cell in it is convex; it is known that in this case every cell is a convex polytope
[
23
, Theorem 1]
. If, for a convex tiling
\mathcal{T}
, there are universal constants
0<\hat{r}<\hat{R}
such that every cell of
\mathcal{T}
contains a ball of radius
\hat{r}
and is contained in a ball of radius
\hat{R}
, the tiling is called
normal
. In this paper we deal only with normal, convex tilings.

One of the classic problems of geometry, regarding planar mosaics, is the Honeycomb Conjecture, which states, roughly speaking, that in a decomposition of the Euclidean plane into cells of equal area, the average perimeter of the cells is minimal for the regular hexagonal grid; i.e. a tiling in which every cell is a unit area regular hexagon (for an investigation of mathematically rigorous variants of this problem, see
[
18
]
). This conjecture first appeared in Roman time in a book of Varro about agriculture
[
26
]
. Despite its ancient origin, it took a surprisingly long time to find a satisfactory proof for this conjecture: it was solved by L. Fejes Tóth for normal, convex mosaics
[
10
]
in the 1940s , while the most general version is due to Hales
[
15
]
in 2001, who dropped the condition of convexity; the proofs of both statements require heavy computation based on the metric properties of the Euclidean plane. Despite its solution, this problem still seems to be interesting for mathematicians
[
3
,
4
]
, engineers
[
28
]
and even for philosophers
[
19
]
.

Clearly, it is a natural question to ask if a similar statement holds for tilings in any normed plane. The aim of this paper is to investigate this problem. To do it, we recall a few well-known facts about normed planes. In particular, we recall that every origin-symmetric convex disk
M
(i.e. every compact, convex set
M
with nonempty interior and satisfying
M=-M
) is the unit disk of a normed plane, and the unit disk of a normed plane is an origin-symmetric convex disk. In the paper, for a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
, we denote the unit disk of
\mathcal{M}
by
M
, and the norm of a point
p\in\mathcal{M}
by
||p||_{M}
. For convenience, we imagine that
\mathcal{M}
also has an underlying Euclidean structure, and denote the Euclidean norm of
p
by
||p||
.

We also note that any finite dimensional real normed space can be equipped with a Haar measure, and that this measure is unique up to multiplication of the standard Lebesgue measure by a scalar (cf. e.g.
[
25
]
). This scalar does not play a role in our investigation and in the paper
\area(\cdot)
denotes
2
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Finally, as usual, we define the perimeter of any convex disk
K
in
\mathcal{M}
, also called the
M
-perimeter
of
K
and denoted by
\perim_{M}(K)
, as the supremum of the sums of the
M
-lengths of the sides of any convex polygon inscribed in
K
(see e.g.
[
22
]
).

We ask the following question, where by a hexagonal tiling we mean a tiling whose cells are translates of a given centrally symmetric hexagon.

Question 1.1
.

Is it true that in any normal, convex tiling in any normed plane
\mathcal{M}
, with unit area cells, the average perimeter of a cell, if it exists, is minimal for a hexagonal tiling?

Before stating our first result, we note that there are optimization problems in discrete geometry whose solution for tilings in the Euclidean plane is a regular hexagonal tiling, while in any normed plane it is a (not necessarily regular) hexagonal tiling. As examples, we mention the
densest circle packing problem
(see
[
9
,
11
,
20
,
21
]
for its solution in the Euclidean plane and in any normed plane) as well as the
simultaneous packing and covering problem

[
29
]
, which can be regarded as isoperimetric problems involving the area and inradius of cells, and the circumradius and inradius of cells of a tiling, respectively.

In the paper we need the following definition, where by
\mathbf{B}^{2}
we denote the Euclidean closed unit disk centered at the origin
o
, and
\card(\cdot)
denotes the cardinality of a set.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a convex, normal tiling in the normed plane
\mathcal{M}
. For any
R>0
, let
\mathcal{T}(R)
denote the family of cells of
\mathcal{T}
contained in
R\mathbf{B}^{2}
. Let
\alpha>0
.
We define the
lower average
\alpha
th powered perimeter
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
as the quantity

(1.1)

\underline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}(R)}\left(\perim_{M}(C)\right)^{\alpha}}{\card(\mathcal{T}(R))}.

We define the
upper average
\alpha
th powered perimeter
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
, denoted by
\overline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
, in the same way, replacing
\liminf
by
\limsup
.
If
\underline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})=\overline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
, we call this quantity the

\alpha
th powered perimeter
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
, and denote it by
P_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})
.
We define the quantities
\underline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T}),\overline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T})
and
P_{\log}(\mathcal{T})
similarly, replacing
\left(\perim_{M}(C)\right)^{\alpha}
by
\log\left(\perim_{M}(C)\right)
in the above definitions.

We note that, as usual in convex geometry, we may denote the quantities
\underline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T})
,
\overline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T})
and
P_{\log}(\mathcal{T})
also by
\underline{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T}),\overline{P}_{0}(\mathcal{T})
and
P_{0}(\mathcal{T})
, respectively. We also remark that whereas the above procedure is the standard way to define the ‘average’ of a certain geometric quantity of infinitely many objects in the plane, the Euclidean disk
\mathbf{B}^{2}
in the definition of

is often replaced in the literature by other figures, for instance squares or hexagons (see e.g.
[
11
,
12
]
).

Remark 1.3
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in(0,\infty)
with
\alpha<\beta
and any normal, convex tiling
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathcal{M}
, we have
\exp(\underline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T}))\leq\left(\underline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})\right)^{1/\alpha}\leq\left(\underline{P}_{\beta}(\mathcal{T})\right)^{1/\beta}
and
\exp(\overline{P}_{\log}(\mathcal{T}))\leq\left(\overline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})\right)^{1/\alpha}\leq\left(\overline{P}_{\beta}(\mathcal{T})\right)^{1/\beta}
. Furthermore, if

is a hexagonal tiling, we have equality in all the previous inequalities.

Our first result is the following theorem, which, as it is shown by Remark
1.3
, can be regarded as a ‘weaker’ variant of the Honeycomb Conjecture.

Theorem 1.4
.

For any normed plane
\mathcal{M}
there is a hexagonal tiling
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
of
\mathcal{M}
with unit area tiles such that for any convex, normal tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{M}
with unit area tiles, we have

\underline{P}_{2}(\mathcal{T})\geq P_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{hex}).

We will see that the proof of Theorem
1.4
relies on the following classical result of Dowker
[
8
]
on the area of convex polygons circumscribed about a convex disk.

Theorem 1.5

(Dowker)
.

For any convex disk
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let

A_{K}(n)=\inf\{\area(P):P\hbox{ is a convex }n\hbox{-gon circumscribed about }K\}.

Then the sequence
\{A_{K}(n)\}
is convex. In other words, for any
n\geq 4
, we have

A_{K}(n-1)+A_{K}(n+1)\geq 2A_{K}(n).

Based on this result, we define two properties.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. We say that the normed plane
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
\alpha
-honeycomb property
, if there is a hexagonal tiling
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that for any convex, normal tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{M}
, we have

\underline{P}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T})\geq P_{\alpha}(\mathcal{T}_{hex}).

Similarly, we say that it satisfies the
log-honeycomb
(or
0
-honeycomb
)
property
if the same holds for the lower average log-perimeter of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
.

Observe that by Theorem
1.4
, any norm with unit disk
M
satisfies the
2
-honeycomb property.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. We say that a convex disk
K
satisfies the
\alpha
-Dowker property
if the sequence
\{A_{K}^{\alpha}(n)\}
is convex. Furthermore, we say that it satisfies the
log-Dowker
(or
0
-Dowker
)
property
if the sequence
\{\log A_{K}(n)\}
is convex.

In our proof we only need the following, weaker variant of the
\alpha
-Dowker property.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
. We say that a convex disk
K
satisfies the
weak
\alpha
-Dowker property
if

(1.2)

\frac{n-6}{n-m}A_{K}^{\alpha}(m)+\frac{6-m}{n-m}A_{K}^{\alpha}(n)\geq A_{K}^{\alpha}(6)

holds for any
3\leq m<6<n
. Similarly, we say that
K
satisfies the
weak log-Dowker
(or
weak
0
-Dowker
)
property
if

(1.3)

\frac{n-6}{n-m}\log A_{K}(m)+\frac{6-m}{n-m}\log A_{K}(n)\geq\log A_{K}(6)

holds for any
3\leq m<6<n
.

For brevity, if
\alpha=1
in any of the above definitions, we may omit it from the notation.
In our next result, we recall the notion of
isoperimetrix
of a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
with unit disk
M
, defined as the rotated copy of the Euclidean polar of
M
, about the origin

, by
\frac{\pi}{2}
. We denote the isoperimetrix of

by

.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
M
be an
o
-symmetric convex disk. For any
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
, if the isoperimetrix

of
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the weak

-Dowker property, then the normed plane
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
(2\alpha)
-honeycomb property.

It is an elementary exercise to check that
A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(n)=n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}
, implying that
\mathbf{B}^{2}
satisfies the log-Dowker property. Thus, Theorem
1.9
immediately yields Corollary

.

Corollary 1.10
.

The Euclidean plane satisfies the log-honeycomb property.

2.
Proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.9

First, we observe that Theorem
1.9
, combined with Theorem
1.5
readily implies Theorem
1.4
. Thus, we only need to prove Theorem
1.9
. Throughout this section, we deal with a given convex, normal tiling
\mathcal{T}
in a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
. We permit the angles of cells to be straight angles; due to this, we can regard
\mathcal{T}
as an
edge-to-edge
tiling, in which every edge of every cell belongs to exactly one more cell. During our investigation,
\mathcal{T}(R)
denotes the family of cells of
\mathcal{T}
contained in
R\mathbf{B}^{2}
, and for every
C\in\mathcal{T}
,

denotes the number of sides of

.
We start with collecting some elementary observations and known facts about tilings and normed planes.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
0<\hat{r}<\hat{R}
be given such that every cell of
\mathcal{T}
contains a Euclidean disk of radius
\hat{r}
and is contained in a Euclidean disk of radius
\hat{R}
. Then every cell
C\in\mathcal{T}
has at most
\frac{9\hat{R}^{2}}{\hat{r}^{2}}-1
neighbors.

Proof.

For every cell
C\in\mathcal{T}
, there is a Euclidean disk of radius
3\hat{R}
that contains
C
as well as all its neighbors. The area of every cell is at least
\hat{r}^{2}\pi
. Thus, the assertion follows from a simple area estimate.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R)
denote the family of cells of
\mathcal{F}
that intersect the circle
R\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then
\card(\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R))=\Theta(R)
.

Proof.

Since the diameter of every cell
C\in\mathcal{T}
is at most
2\hat{R}
, every cell is contained in the closure of the set
(R+2\hat{R})\mathbf{B}^{2}\setminus(R-2\hat{R})\mathbf{B}^{2}
. Thus,

\card(\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R))\leq\frac{(R+2\hat{R})^{2}\pi-(R-2\hat{R})^{2}\pi}{\hat{r}^{2}\pi}=\frac{8\hat{R}R}{\hat{r}^{2}}
, implying that
\card(\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R))=\mathcal{O}(R)
. On the other hand, the union of these cells covers
R\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and the diameter of each cell is at most
2\hat{R}
. Thus,
\card(\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R))\geq\frac{2\pi}{2\arcsin\frac{\hat{R}}{R}}
, implying that
\card(\mathcal{T}_{bd}(R))=\Omega(R)
.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

We define the
lower average number of sides
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
as

\underline{v}(\mathcal{T})=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}(R)}v(C)}{\card(\mathcal{T}(R))}.

We define the
upper average number of sides

\overline{v}(\mathcal{T})
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
in the same way, replacing
\liminf
by
\limsup
. If
\underline{v}(\mathcal{T})=\overline{v}(\mathcal{T})
, we call this quantity the
average number of sides
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
R>2\hat{R}
, set
v_{R}(\mathcal{T})=\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}(R)}v(C)}{\card(\mathcal{T}(R))}
. Then,
v_{R}(\mathcal{T})\leq 6+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{R}\right)
, and
\overline{v}(\mathcal{T})\leq 6
.

Proof.

Consider the graph
G
whose vertices are the vertices of the cells of
\mathcal{T}(R)
, and two vertices are connected by an edge if they are the endpoints of an edge of a cell in
\mathcal{T}(R)
. Then
G
is a planar graph. Let
V(G)
,
E(G)
and
F(G)
denote the numbers of vertices, edges and faces of
G
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{E}_{bd}(G)
denote the family of the edges of
G
in the boundary of
\bigcup\mathcal{T}(R)
. We note that every edge in
\mathcal{E}_{bd}(G)
belongs to a cell of
\mathcal{T}
intersecting
R\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and thus, it follows from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
that
E_{bd}(G):=\card(\mathcal{E}_{bd}(G))=\mathcal{O}(R)
. These lemmas, an area estimate and the observation that
\bigcup\mathcal{T}(R)
contains
(R-2\hat{R})\mathbf{B}^{2}
and is contained in
(R+2\hat{R})\mathbf{B}^{2}
shows that
V(G)
,
E(G)
and
F(G)
are all of order of magnitude
\Theta(R^{2})
.

We observe that there might be vertices of
G
whose degree is less than
3
. If
p
is such a vertex, then its degree is
2
, it lies in
\bd\left(\bigcup\mathcal{T}(R)\right)
, and it belongs to two edges in
\mathcal{E}_{bd}(G)
. Thus, the number of such edges is at most
E_{bd}(G)=\mathcal{O}(R)
. We merge these edges into one edge (i.e. we apply SP-reduction) and continue this process until we obtain another planar graph
G^{\prime}
in which the degree of every vertex is at least
3
. If
V(G^{\prime}),E(G^{\prime})
and
F(G^{\prime})
denote the numbers of vertices, edges and faces of
G^{\prime}
, then by the construction of
G^{\prime}
we have
V(G^{\prime})\leq V(G)
and
E(G^{\prime})\leq E(G)
with
V(G)-V(G^{\prime})=E(G)-E(G^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}(R)
, and
F(G^{\prime})=F(G)
.

Applying Euler’s formula for
G^{\prime}
and using the fact that the degree of every vertex of
G^{\prime}
is at least
3
yields that
E(G^{\prime})\leq 3F(G^{\prime})-6
, implying that
E(G)\leq 3F(G)+\mathcal{O}(R)
. On the other hand,
v_{R}(\mathcal{T})\leq\frac{2E(G)}{F(G)}
, and as
E(G)
and
F(G)
are of
\Theta(R^{2})
, it follows that
v_{R}(\mathcal{T})\leq 6+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{R}\right)
, which readily yields the assertion.
∎

The original isoperimetric inequality, observed by Zenodorus in ancient Greece, states that among regions of a given perimeter in the Euclidean plane, Euclidean disks have maximum area. This result was generalized for any normed plane by Busemann
[
5
]
in the following form.

Theorem 2.5

(Busemann)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a normed plane. The area enclosed by a simple, closed curve
\Gamma
of a given
M
-length is maximized if
\Gamma
is the boundary of a plane convex body
K
homothetic to the so-called
isoperimetrix

M_{iso}
of
\mathcal{M}
, obtained as the polar of the rotated copy of the unit disk
M
of
\mathcal{M}
by
\frac{\pi}{2}
(see Figure
1
).

Figure 1
.
The isoperimetrix
M_{iso}
of a norm with unit disk
M
. The dotted circle is the Euclidean unit disk
\mathbf{B}^{2}
centered at
o
. The left-hand side panel shows
M
and its polar
M^{\circ}
, the isoperimetrix in the right-hand side panel is a rotated copy of
M^{\circ}
by
\frac{\pi}{2}

.

We note that by Theorem
2.5
, the isoperimetrix of the Euclidean plane is a Euclidean circular disk.

A variant of this inequality, in Euclidean plane, states that among convex polygons with given perimeter and fixed directions of their sides, the ones circumscribed about a circular disk have maximal area. This statement is called L’Huilier’s inequality, which is generalized for arbitrary normed planes by Chakerian
[
6
]
in the following way.

Theorem 2.6

(Chakerian)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a normed plane with unit disk
M
. Let the isoperimetrix of the plane be
M_{iso}
. Let
K
be an arbitrary convex
n
-gon in
\mathcal{M}
, and let
K^{*}
be the convex
n
-gon circumscribed about
M_{iso}
whose sides have the same outer unit normals as the sides of
K
. Let the
M
-perimeter of
K
be
L
, the area of
K
be
F
, and the area of
K^{*}
be

. Then

L^{2}-4fF\geq 0,

with equality if and only if
K
is homothetic to
K^{*}
.

\underline{P}_{2\alpha}(\mathcal{T})=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}(R)}\left(\perim_{M}(C)\right)^{2\alpha}}{\card(\mathcal{T}(R))}.

For any

By Lemma

Based on this, it follows that

\underline{P}_{2\alpha}(\mathcal{T})\geq 4^{\alpha}\left(A_{M_{iso}}(6)\right)^{\alpha}.

Now the only thing we need to show is that for some hexagonal tiling

.

be a tiling whose cells are translates of a homothetic copy of

of unit area. Then the desired equality follows from the equality part of Theorem
2.6
.

Remark 2.8
.

If the isoperimetrix
M_{iso}
of the norm has at most six sides (which is equivalent to the property that
M
has at most six sides), then
M
satisfies the log-honeycomb property.

3.
Results about polygonal norms

The goal of this section is to investigate the
\alpha
-honeycomb properties of polygonal norms, and the
\alpha
-Dowker properties of polygons.

3.1.
An algorithm to check the
\alpha
-honeycomb property of a polygonal norm

There is no straightforward way to check whether in a specific normed plane the honeycomb conjecture is true or not. Nevertheless, the sufficient condition in Theorem
1.9
can be checked using a suitable algorithm.
In this subsection we give a simple algorithm that checks if the isoperimetrix of a polygonal norm satisfies the
\alpha
-Dowker, or weak
\alpha
-Dowker property for some given
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
. We assume that
\alpha>0
, and note that our argument can be modified in a natural way for the log-Dowker or weak log-Dowker property.

Remark 3.1
.

If the unit disk
M
of a norm is a
(2k)
-gon, then
M_{iso}
is also a
(2k)
-gon, and for any
n\geq 2k
, we clearly have
A_{M}(n)=\area(M_{iso})
. Thus, to check if
M^{*}
satisfies the
\alpha
-Dowker property, it is sufficient to check if the inequality

(3.1)

A_{M_{iso}}^{\alpha}(n-1)+A_{M_{iso}}^{\alpha}(n+1)\geq 2A_{M_{iso}}^{\alpha}(n)

holds for all values
4\leq n\leq 2k
.
Similarly, to check if
M_{iso}
satisfies the weak
\alpha
-Dowker property, it is enough to check if the inequality in (
1.2
) is satisfied for
m=3,4,5
and
6<n\leq 2k
.

We also recall that there is an algorithm, given by Aggarwal, Chang and Yap
[
1
]
that computes, for any given convex polygon
P
with
s
sides, the area of a minimum area circumscribed convex
t
-gon for any given
3\leq t<s
, in
\mathcal{O}(s^{2}\log s\log t)
steps.

Thus, given an
o
-symmetric
(2k)
-gon
M
, we can provide the following algorithm to check if, say, the weak
\alpha
-Dowker property is satisfied for
M_{iso}
.

Step 1

: We compute
M_{iso}
. Since
M_{iso}
is the rotated copy of the polar of
M
by
\frac{\pi}{2}
, and since the polar of an
o
-symmetric convex
(2k)
-gon can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(k)
steps, this step requires
\mathcal{O}(k)
steps.

Step 2

: Using the algorithm in
[
1
]
, we compute the quantities
A_{M_{iso}}(n)
for all
3\leq n\leq 2k
. This step requires
\mathcal{O}(k^{3}\log^{2}k)
steps.

Step 3

: We check the inequality in (
1.2
) for
m=3,4,5
and all
6<n\leq 2k
, in
\mathcal{O}(k)
steps.

To check the
\alpha
-Dowker property of
M_{iso}
, in Step 3 we check the inequality in (
3.1
) in
\mathcal{O}(k)
steps.

3.2.
Norms where the unit disk is a regular polygon

In this subsection we investigate which normed planes, with an
o
-symmetric regular polygon as their unit disk, satisfy the honeycomb property.
Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 3.2
.

If the unit disk of a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
is a regular
(2k)
-gon with
k\neq 4,5,7
, then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the honeycomb property.

Note that if the unit disk
M
of
\mathcal{M}
is a regular
(2k)
-gon, then
M_{iso}
is also a regular
(2k)
-gon. Thus,
Theorem
3.2
readily follows from combining Theorems
3.3
and
1.9
.

Theorem 3.3
.

A regular
(2k)
-gon

, with

, satisfies the weak
\frac{1}{2}
-Dowker property if and only if

.

Consider a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
whose unit disk is a regular
(2k)
-gon with
k=4,5
or
7
. A possible interpretation of Theorem
3.3
is that in this case to minimize the ‘average’ perimeter it is not worth using ‘optimal’ hexagons. Nevertheless, it seems unlikely that one can fit ‘optimal’
k
-gons with more than one different values of
k
to tile the plane. This observation might indicate that these planes may also satisfy the honeycomb property.

Remark 3.4
.

According to Lemma
3.7
, if

, then

is a convex combination of

and

does not satisfy the
\alpha
-Dowker property for any
\alpha<1

To prove Theorem

, we first find the values of

of the regular
(2k)
-gon

, with

and

, we have

In the following we assume that the regular polygon

is circumscribed about the unit disk

For our investigation, we need Lemma

Lemma 3.5

Let

Proof.

Clearly, every side of

Suppose that there is a side

does not belong to

be the point of

closest to the midpoint of

around

in a suitable direction yields a circumscribed polygon whose area is strictly smaller than

, a contradiction (see Figure

Now, let

be a minimum area convex
n
-gon circumscribed about

Remark 3.6

We note that if

Lemma 3.7

Let

(3.2)

(3.3)

A(k,3)=\begin{cases}3\tan\frac{\pi}{3},&\text{if}~2k=3q,\\
\frac{2}{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2k}\right)}\left(2\sin\left(\frac{q\pi}{k}\right)+\sin\left(\frac{(q+1)\pi}{k}\right)\right),&\text{if}~2k=3q+1,\\
\frac{2}{\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2k}\right)}\left(\sin\left(\frac{q\pi}{k}\right)+2\sin\left(\frac{(q+1)\pi}{k}\right)\right),&\text{if}~2k=3q+2.\end{cases}

Let

Let the sides of

in counterclockwise order in

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1

Set

Subcase 1.1
: every side of

Let the sides of

and

4.
Results about not necessarily polygonal norms

In this section we collect our results about the
\alpha
-honeycomb properties of general norms, and the
\alpha
-Dowker properties of general convex disks.
For our first result, recall that
A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(n)=n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}
. In the following theorem, we let

\varepsilon_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(5)}+\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(7)}-2\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(6)}}{\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(5)}+\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(7)}+2\sqrt{A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(6)}}=0.002623\ldots,

and denote the Hausdorff distance of the convex bodies
K,L
by
d_{H}(K,L)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a normed plane with unit disk
M
, and assume that
d_{H}(M,\mathbf{B}^{2})\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Then
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the honeycomb property.

Proof.

Observe that
d_{H}(M,\mathbf{B}^{2})\leq\varepsilon
implies that
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbf{B}^{2}\subseteq M\subseteq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbf{B}^{2}
. On the other hand, since the isoperimetrix
M_{iso}
of
\mathcal{M}
is a rotated copy of the polar of
M
, this yields that
\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\mathbf{B}^{2}\subseteq M_{iso}\subseteq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\mathbf{B}^{2}
.

By Theorem
1.9
, it is sufficient to prove that
M_{iso}
satisfies the weak
\frac{1}{2}
-Dowker property. Since for any
n\geq 3
and
K\subseteq L
, we have
A_{K}(n)\leq A_{L}(n)
, it follows that

\frac{n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}}{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}\leq A_{M_{iso}}(n)\leq\frac{n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}
. This yields that it is sufficient to prove that for any
3\leq m<6<n
and
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, we have

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{n-6}{n-m}\sqrt{m\tan\frac{\pi}{m}}+\frac{6-m}{n-m}\sqrt{n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{6\tan\frac{\pi}{6}}.

By the properties of
A_{\mathbf{B}^{2}}(n)=n\tan\frac{\pi}{n}
, the left-hand side is minimal if
m=5
and
n=7
. Thus, it is sufficient to show that

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\left(\sqrt{5\tan\frac{\pi}{5}}+\sqrt{7\tan\frac{\pi}{7}}\right)\geq\frac{2}{1-\varepsilon}\sqrt{6\tan\frac{\pi}{6}}

holds for any
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. But this readily follows from the definition of
\varepsilon_{0}
.
∎

Our next result shows that convex disks with sufficiently smooth boundaries ‘asymptotically’ satisfy the log-Dowker property.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
K
is a convex disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
C^{4}
-class boundary and strictly positive curvature everywhere, then there is some value
n(K)\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
n\geq n(K)
, we have

\log A_{K}(n-1)+\log A_{K}(n+1)\geq 2\log A_{K}(n).

In the proof we intend to use the following result of Ludwig
[
17
]
.

Lemma 4.3

(Ludwig)
.

Let
K
be a convex disk in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
C^{4}
-class boundary and strictly positive curvature everywhere. Then there are quantities
A,B>0
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
, depending only on
K
, such that

A_{K}(n)=A+\frac{B}{n^{2}}+\frac{C}{n^{4}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right).

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Let us define
f(n)=A_{K}(n-1)A_{K}(n+1)-A^{2}_{K}(n)
. Then

f(n)=\left(A+\frac{B}{(n-1)^{2}}+\frac{C}{(n-1)^{4}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right)\cdot\\
\cdot\left(A+\frac{B}{(n+1)^{2}}+\frac{C}{(n+1)^{4}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right)-\left(A+\frac{B}{n^{2}}+\frac{C}{n^{4}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)\right)^{2}.

Thus, an elementary computation yields that

f(n)=AB\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}-\frac{2}{n^{2}}\right)+AC\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{4}}+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{4}}-\frac{2}{n^{4}}\right)+\\
+B^{2}\left(\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}(n+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right).

On the other hand, it can be checked that the orders of magnitude of
\frac{1}{(n-1)^{4}}+\frac{1}{(n+1)^{4}}-\frac{2}{n^{4}}
and
\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}(n+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{n^{4}}
are both
o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right)
. Thus, after simplification, we obtain that

f(n)=\frac{AB(6n^{2}-2)}{(n^{2}-1)^{2}n^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{n^{4}}\right),

which is positive if
n
is sufficiently large.
∎

In Section
3
we have seen that there are polygons which do not satisfy the
\alpha
-Dowker property for any
\alpha<1
(see Remark
3.4
). Our next theorem can be regarded as a counterpoint of this observation. Before stating it, recall that a convex disk
K
is smooth if its every boundary point belongs to a unique supporting line of
K
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
K
be smooth and strictly convex. Then there is some value
\alpha<1
such that
K
satisfies the weak
\alpha
-Dowker property.

Proof.

First, observe that by our conditions, any supporting line of
K
intersects
K
in a unique point, and for any point
p
of
\bd(K)
there is a unique supporting line of
K
that contains
p
. This yields, in particular, that for any
n\geq 3
and any minimum area convex
n
-gon
Q_{n}
circumscribed about
K
,
Q_{n}
has exactly
n
sides, and the tangent points on those sides are distinct. For any point
p\in\bd(K)
, we denote the unique closed half plane supporting
K
at
p
by
H(p)
, and the boundary of
H(p)
by
L(p)
. We call boundary points of
K
with opposite unit normal vectors
opposite
, or
antipodal
points. Note that if
p,q\in\bd(K)
are not antipodal points of
K
, then there is a unique closed arc of
\bd(K)
connecting them that does not contain antipodal points. We denote this arc by
\widehat{pq}
, and denote by
A(\widehat{pq})
the area of the bounded, connected region
R(\widehat{pq})
whose boundary consists of
\widehat{pq}
and the segments of
L(p)
and
L(q)
connecting
p,q
to the intersection point of the two lines. We observe that the problem of finding
A_{K}(n)
coincides with the problem of finding a tiling of
\bd(K)
by
n
arcs
\widehat{x_{i}x_{i+1}}
,
i=1,2,n
,
x_{n+1}=x_{1}
such that
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}A(\widehat{x_{i}x_{i+1}})
is minimal.

First, we fix an arbitrary integer
n\geq 4
, and show that
A_{K}(n-1)+A_{K}(n+1)>2A_{K}(n)
. To do it, we follow the proof of the original theorem of Dowker
[
8
, Theorem 1]
.

Let
Q_{n-1}
and
Q_{n+1}
denote a minimum area convex
(n-1)
-gon and
(n+1)
-gon, respectively, circumscribed about
K
.
Let the tangent points of
K
on the sides of
Q_{n-1}
be
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n-1}
in counterclockwise order, and we denote the tangent points of
Q_{n+1}
in counterclockwise order on
\bd(K)
by
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n+1}
, where the indices are understood mod
(n-1)
and
(n+1)
, respectively. Note that then
Q_{n-1}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n-1}H(p_{i})
and
Q_{n+1}=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n+1}H(q_{j})
.
Since the areas of these regions are finite, for any two consecutive tangent points
p_{i}
,
p_{i+1}
, no arc
\widehat{p_{i}p_{i+1}}
contains antipodal points of
K
, and the same is true for the points
q_{j}
.

Due to the possible existence of coinciding points in the above two sequences, we unite these sequences as a single sequence
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{2n}
in such a way that the points are in this counterclockwise order in
\bd(K)
,
v_{1}=p_{1}
.
In the proof we regard this sequence as a cyclic sequence, where the indices are determined mod
2n
, and, with a little abuse of notation, we say that
\widehat{v_{i}v_{j}}

covers

\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}}
if
\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}}\subseteq\widehat{v_{i}v_{j}}
and
i<k<l<j<i+2n
.

Observe that the family of arcs
\widehat{p_{i}p_{i+1}}
,
\widehat{q_{j}q_{j+1}}
is a
2
-tiling
\mathcal{T}_{0}
of
\bd(K)
; that is, every point of
\bd(K)
belongs to at least two such arcs, and no point belongs to the interior of more than two such arcs.
Our main goal will be to modify the
2
-tiling
\mathcal{T}_{0}
in such a way that the value of
f
does not increase but the number of covering pairs strictly decreases.

Note that since
\mathcal{T}_{0}
is the union of two tilings consisting of
(n-1)
and
(n+1)
arcs, respectively,
\mathcal{T}_{0}
contains covering pairs.
Assume that
\widehat{v_{i}v_{j}}
covers
\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}}
. Then let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
denote the
2
-tiling of
\bd(K)
in which
\widehat{v_{i}v_{j}}
and
\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}}

are replaced by
\widehat{v_{i}v_{l}}
and
\widehat{v_{k}v_{j}}
.
Our main observation, as in the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 1]
is that in this case

(4.1)

A(\widehat{v_{i}v_{j}})+A(\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}})\geq A(\widehat{v_{i}v_{l}})+A(\widehat{v_{k}v_{j}}),

and if the four points are pairwise distinct, then here we have strict inequality.

According to our conditions,
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}_{0}}A(S)\geq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}A(S)
. Furthermore, as
\mathcal{T}_{0}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}
are
2
-tilings, and
\widehat{v_{k}v_{l}}
is already covered twice by the arcs considered in the modification, the number of covering pairs in
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is strictly less than in
\mathcal{T}_{0}
. Repeating this procedure we obtain a
2
-tiling
\mathcal{T}_{m}
of
\bd(K)
for which
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}_{0}}A(S)\geq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}_{,}}A(S)
and which does not contain covering pairs. Then,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
decomposes into two tilings
\{\widehat{v_{1}v_{3}},\widehat{v_{3}v_{5}},\ldots,\widehat{v_{2n-1}v_{1}}\}
and
\{\widehat{v_{2}v_{4}},\widehat{v_{4}v_{6}},\ldots,\widehat{v_{2n}v_{2}}\}
, each of which contains exactly
n
arcs.

Let
V_{1}
be defined as the circumscribed convex
n
-gon touching

at the vertices

with odd indices, and

be the circumscribed convex
n

with even indices. Then

(4.2)

\area(Q_{n-1})+\area(Q_{n+1})\geq\area(V_{1})+\area(V_{2}).

Since

and

are convex

-gons circumscribed about

or

, we are done. Thus, in the following we assume for contradiction that

.
Then, by the remark right after (

in its interior, and vice versa. In particular, there are at least two vertices of

.

Without loss of generality, let

corresponding to

and

. For

, and let

.

5.
A conjecture of Steinhaus

We recall the following conjecture, appearing as Problem C15 in
[
7
]
.

Conjecture 1

(Steinhaus)
.

For any tiling
\mathcal{T}
in the Euclidean plane with tiles whose diameters are at least
D
for some fixed
D>0
, the maximum isoperimetric ratio
\frac{\perim(C)^{2}}{\area(C)}
of the cells
C
of
\mathcal{T}
is minimal if
\mathcal{T}
is a regular hexagonal tiling.

To state our result regarding this conjecture, we need Definition
5.1
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a tiling of a normed plane
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathcal{T}(R)
denote the family of cells of
\mathcal{T}
in
R\mathbf{B}^{2}
. Then the
lower average isoperimetric ratio
of a cell of
\mathcal{T}
is defined as

\underline{I}(\mathcal{T})=\liminf_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}(R)}\frac{\perim_{M}(C)^{2}}{\area(C)}}{\card(\mathcal{T}(C))}.

If we replace the
\liminf
in the above definition by
\limsup
, we obtain the
upper average isoperimetric ratio

\overline{I}(\mathcal{T})
of a cell. If these quantities are equal, the common value is called the
average isoperimetric ratio
of a cell, denoted by
I(\mathcal{T})
.

Our result is the following.

Theorem 5.2
.

For any normed plane
\mathcal{M}
there is a hexagonal tiling
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that for any convex, normal tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{M}
, we have

\underline{I}(\mathcal{T})\geq I(\mathcal{T}_{hex}).

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{M}
is a Euclidean plane, then
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
is a regular hexagonal tiling.

Proof.

The first part of Theorem
5.2
follows from a straightforward modification of the proof of Theorem
1.9
. For the second statement we remark that, according to this proof, the cells of
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
are homothetic copies of a minimum area hexagon circumscribed about the isoperimetrix of the norm. Thus, if the norm is Euclidean, then these hexagons are regular.
∎

Theorem
5.2
can be stated also in the following, slightly more general form.

Theorem 5.3
.

For any normed plane
\mathcal{M}
there is a hexagonal tiling
\mathcal{T}_{hex}
of
\mathcal{M}
such that for any convex tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathcal{M}
satisfying

\underline{I}(\mathcal{T})\geq I(\mathcal{T}_{hex}).

Remark 5.4
.

We note that Theorem

remains valid if we replace

\underline{v}(\mathcal{T})

\underline{I}(\mathcal{T})

There is a construction in

14
]
showing that it is possible to tile the plane with convex heptagons. In this tiling, as we move away from the origin, the cells get longer and thinner, showing that it is not normal. On the other hand, an elementary geometric consideration proves Remark
5.5
, where for any convex disk

in
\mathbb{R}^{2}

Remark 5.5
.

For any
\lambda>0
there is some
\mu>0
such that if

, then
\frac{\cirr(K)}{\ir(K)}\leq\mu

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the family of convex disks, containing
o
, with unit perimeter and isoperimetric ratio at most

. Then for any

and

∎

E_{2}\subseteq E_{1}
. Thus, we can apply an arbitrarily small deformation to the cells of
\mathcal{T}
to obtain an edge-to-edge convex tiling
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the same combinatorial properties; this deformation can be done subsequently after each step. As a consequence, for simplicity, if a side of a polygon, appearing in one of the two steps above, is dissected into smaller segments by the vertices of the neighbors of the cell, we regard each smaller segment as a separate edge of the cell.
Using this interpretation, in the following, we call the edges of
\mathcal{T}
in
\bd\left(\bigcup\mathcal{T}_{k}\right)

boundary edges
of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, and the edges of
\mathcal{T}
in
\inter\left(\bigcup\mathcal{T}_{k}\right)

interior edges
of
\mathcal{T}_{k}
.

Fix any positive number
\nu>0
. First, consider the case that we apply Step A
k_{1}
times, and after that we apply Step B once. Then
N_{k_{1}}=3^{k_{1}}
, and thus,
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
has
4\cdot 3^{k_{1}}
boundary edges. In addition,
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
consists of
4k_{1}+1
cells. We count the number of interior edges: Note that
\mathcal{T}_{0}
has
4
edges. Then in each step before the
k_{1}
th one we add
12
edges, which are all interior edges of
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
. Finally, in the
k_{1}
th step we add four interior edges of
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
. Thus, in total,
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
has
12k_{1}-4
interior edges. Now, if we set
R_{1}=N_{k_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}
, then

v_{\square}(R_{1})=\frac{\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}_{\square}(R_{1})}v(C)}{\card(\mathcal{T}_{\square}(R_{1}))}=\frac{4\cdot 3^{k_{1}}+2(12k_{1}-4)}{4k_{1}+1}.

Observe that if
k_{1}
is sufficiently large, then
v_{\square}(R_{1})>\nu
. We choose
k_{1}
to satisfy this inequality, and fix this value in the following.

Now we apply Step A
k_{2}-k_{1}-1
times and after that we apply Step B once. Furthermore, we set
R_{2}=N_{k_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}
. Note that if
k_{2}
is sufficiently large, then the contribution of
\mathcal{T}_{k_{1}}
to both the numerator and the denominator of the fraction defining
v_{\square}(R_{2})
is negligible, and thus, by the argument in the previus paragraph, we can choose some sufficiently large integer
k_{2}
such that
v_{\square}(R_{2})>\nu
.

Continuing this process, we obtain a tiling
\mathcal{T}
, and a sequence
\{R_{n}\}
with
R_{n}\to\infty
, such that
v_{\square}(R_{n})>\nu
for all values of
n
. This yields that
\overline{v}_{\square}(\mathcal{T})\geq\mu
.

6.
An additional remark

Estimating the numbers of sides of cells of tilings has been a longstanding problem of geometry. The classical book of Grünbaum and Shephard
[
14
]
shows that it is possible to tile the Euclidean plane with convex heptagons, but that any normal tiling contains infinitely many cells with at most six sides. Here we recall that a (not necessarily convex) tiling
\mathcal{T}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called
normal
if

(1)

every cell is homeomorphic to
\mathbf{B}^{2}
;

(2)

the intersection of every pair of cells is connected;

(3)

the cells are
universally bounded
, i.e. there are universal constants
0<\hat{r}<\hat{R}
such that every cell contains a Euclidean disk of radius
\hat{r}
and is contained in a Euclidean disk of radius
\hat{R}
.

The statement of Grünbaum and Shephard was reproved in a simpler way by Kazanci and Vince in
[
16
]
.
For convex, normal mosaics, more is known. More specifically, the second part of Lemma
2.4
, stating that the upper average number of sides of a cell of such a tiling is at most six is proved in the book
[
12
]
of L. Fejes Tóth in a different way. The fact that if all cells have at least six sides, then the number of cells with more than six sides is finite is due to Stehling
[
24
]
. Akopyan
[
2
]
gave a quantitative estimate for the number of these sides, while Frettlöh et al.
[
13
]
showed that the order of magnitude given in
[
2
]
is sharp.

We remark that the proofs of the Lemmas
2.1
-
2.4
(as well as Definition
2.3
) can be generalized for all normal mosaics in a straightforward way. Thus, we gave a short proof of the following statement, generalizing the result of L. Fejes Tóth for any normal mosaic.

Theorem 6.1
.

For any normal mosaic
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\overline{v}(\mathcal{T})\leq 6
.
