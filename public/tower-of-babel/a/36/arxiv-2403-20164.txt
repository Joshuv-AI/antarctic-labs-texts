A single-bubble source for gravitational waves in a cosmological phase transition
Kfir Blum, Mehrdad Mirbabayi

Abstract
We show that quantum fluctuations of an expanding phase transition bubble give rise to gravitational wave (GW) emission, even when considering a single bubble, without bubble collisions or plasma effects. The ratio of GW energy to the total bubble energy reservoir increases with time as ∝ t \propto t . If the bubble expands for long enough before percolation destroys it, back-reaction due to the GW emission becomes important after t br ∼ ( 16 ​ π 5 ) ​ m pl 2 ​ R 0 3 t_{\rm br}\sim(16\pi^{5})m_{\rm pl}^{2}R_{0}^{3} , where R 0 R_{0} is the bubble nucleation radius and m pl m_{\rm pl} is the reduced Planck mass. As seen by experiments today, the GW energy spectrum would appear blue. However, simple estimates suggest that the signal falls short of detection by even ambitious future experiments.



I
Introduction

First-order cosmological phase transitions (PTs) are thought to proceed via the nucleation of bubbles of the true vacuum state
Coleman:1977py
;
Callan:1977pt
;
Coleman:1980aw
, and are considered a source of stochastic gravitational waves (GWs) with possible observational implications (see, e.g.
10.1093/mnras/218.4.629
;
Kamionkowski:1993fg
). Existing calculations focus on the collision of bubbles and plasma turbulence (for a review, see, e.g.
Weir:2017wfa
). This is because semiclassical estimates suggest that the dominant bubble configuration is spherical
Coleman:1977th
;
Blum:2016ipp
, and a spherical bubble cannot radiate GWs by itself.
Here we point out that even if bubbles are spherical on average, quantum fluctuations lead to non-vanishing deformations. Thus GWs are produced also by a single bubble.

To estimate the effect, we quantize the fluctuations on top of a thin-wall classical bubble, matching the fluctuation initial conditions to vacuum fluctuations of the state prior to nucleation. Solving for the time evolution of the fluctuation mode functions, we calculate the quantum root-mean-square (RMS) of the scalar field energy momentum tensor (EMT), and use this to compute the GW energy production. Our analysis applies under the assumption that the bubble size is much less than the Hubble distance.

We find that an expanding bubble converts a fraction
\alpha
of its latent heat into GW energy, where
\alpha
grows with time since nucleation as
\alpha\approx(t/t_{\rm br})
with
t_{\rm br}\approx(16\pi^{5})m_{\rm pl}^{2}R_{0}^{3}
.
Here,
m_{\rm pl}=1/\sqrt{8\pi G}
is the reduced Planck mass, and
R_{0}
is the radius of the bubble at nucleation (typically comparable to the inverse of the energy scale characterizing the spontaneous symmetry breaking).
The power spectral density (energy per logarithmic frequency bin) of the GWs as seen today peaks at the frequency
f_{\rm peak}\approx 1/(R_{0}(1+z_{*}))
, where
z_{*}
is the redshift during the PT.

Gravitational back-reaction may become significant around
t\sim t_{\rm br}
. Assuming the PT occurs in a radiation-dominated background with temperature
T_{*}
, the back-reaction time becomes shorter than a Hubble time for
(R_{0}m_{\rm pl})^{3}\lesssim 10^{-4}(m_{\rm pl}/T_{*})^{2}
. In this regime, the classical treatment of bubble expansion and dynamics may be called into question.

Although we did not perform a survey of models, simple estimates suggest that the signal falls below the sensitivity of even the most ambitious currently planned GW experiments, like the Big-Bang Observer (BBO)
Moore:2014lga
.

The theory of domain wall fluctuations was developed long ago in Ref.
Vilenkin
, though to our knowledge the resulting GW emission has not been worked out. There have been several works on the quantum state of the system after bubble nucleation
Sasaki:1993ha
;
Sasaki:1993gy
;
Tanaka:1994qa
, and on the spectrum of cosmological perturbations (scalars and tensors) as measured by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) observers residing inside one expanding bubble
Yamamoto:1994te
;
Yamamoto:1996qq
;
Garriga
. However, the map between those results and our current computation seems quite complex, since from the global perspective the unperturbed bubble itself breaks homogeneity and isotropy of the Minkowski slices, so scalar fluctuations of the bubble wall source GWs at linear order; in contrast, on the open FLRW slices inside the bubble, scalar and tensor perturbations are decoupled at linear order.

II
Bubble-wall fluctuations

Semi-classically, the decay of a false vacuum in 4d is dominated by an O(4)-symmetric Euclidean saddle
Coleman:1977py
. In real time, it describes the nucleation of a spherically symmetric bubble with a profile that is solely a function of
R
in the following parametrization of Minkowski spacetime,

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dR^{2}+R^{2}\left(-d\tau^{2}+\cosh^{2}\tau d\Omega^{2}\right).

(1)

The
R,\tau
coordinates cover the region of spacetime located outside of the lightcone centered on the bubble. In the thin-wall regime
Coleman:1977py
, this is where interesting dynamics happens, with an expanding region of true vacuum separated from the false vacuum by a wall at
R=R_{0}
. In terms of the wall tension
\sigma
and the difference
\epsilon
between the vacuum energy density in the true vs. false vacua,

\displaystyle R_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3\sigma}{\epsilon}.

(2)

Of course, any realization of this process exhibits quantum fluctuations around the symmetric bubble. Our goal in this paper is to calculate GW production by these fluctuations. In general, this is a challenging task; we will simplify the problem by focusing on the GW production in the regime
t\gg R_{0}
, after the bubble expands beyond its radius at nucleation. The idea is that the long-lived fluctuations of the bubble configuration in the thin wall regime are the transverse excitation modes, which can be described by a 3d effective field theory (EFT).

The dynamics of transverse excitations of a brane is governed by the Nambu-Goto (or Dirac-Born-Infeld in the case of codimension 1) effective action at leading order in the derivative expansion. In addition, the domain wall between the false and true vacua experiences pressure. The action in Euclidean time is therefore the 3d area minus the 4d volume:

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{3}\sigma-V_{4}\epsilon.

(3)

For the O(4)-symmetric bubble of radius
R
, this action is
S=2\pi^{2}\left(\sigma R^{3}-\frac{\epsilon}{4}R^{4}\right)
, whose stationary point is given by Eq. (
2
).

We write the action in static gauge, in which the wall world-volume is parameterized by
y^{\alpha}=(\tau,\theta,\varphi)
, the same coordinates that appear in Eq. (
1
), and parameterize the fluctuations by

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{0}e^{\zeta}.

(4)

The action Eq. (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma R_{0}^{3}\int d^{3}y\sqrt{g}\left(e^{3\zeta}\sqrt{1+g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\zeta\partial_{\beta}\zeta}-\frac{3}{4}e^{4\zeta}\right),

(5)

where
g_{\alpha\beta}
is the metric of a unit 3-sphere. It analytically continues to the metric of dS
3
, which is multiplying
R^{2}
in Eq. (
1
). We are interested in the regime
\sigma R_{0}^{3}\gg 1
, in which
\zeta
is weakly coupled. The quadratic action for
\zeta
reads

\displaystyle S^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sigma R_{0}^{3}\int d^{3}y\sqrt{g}\left(g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\zeta\partial_{\beta}\zeta-3\zeta^{2}\right).

(6)

In Lorentz signature,
\zeta
has tachyonic mass. This will lead to a growing mode solution, which we will analyze in detail.

The EMT of the bubble deviates from spherical symmetry, already at linear order in
\zeta
. Hence, the power spectrum of GWs can be determined from the power spectrum of
\zeta
. This follows from a standard inflationary computation. We expand
\zeta
in spherical harmonics on the 2-sphere,

\displaystyle\zeta(\tau,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,m}\zeta_{lm}(\tau)Y_{lm}(\theta,\varphi),

(7)

and quantize each
\zeta_{lm}

\displaystyle\zeta_{lm}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{l}(\tau)a_{lm}+f^{*}_{l}(\tau)a^{\dagger}_{lm},\;\;\;\;\;\;[a_{lm},a^{\dagger}_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}.

(8)

The mode function
f_{l}(\tau)
is a solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\left(\cosh^{2}\tau\partial_{\tau}f_{l}(\tau)\right)+\left(l(l+1)-3\cosh^{2}\tau\right)f_{l}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(9)

If we analytically continue
\tau\to-i\tau
, the bubble wall becomes a 3-sphere. We choose the state of
\zeta_{lm}
to be the Hartle-Hawking vacuum, given by
a_{lm}|0\rangle=0
if
f_{l}(\tau)
is chosen to be the solution that is regular on this sphere
Hartle:1983ai
.
This gives

\displaystyle f_{l}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{l}}{4}\frac{2Q_{l,2}\left(\tanh\tau\right)+i\pi P_{l,2}\left(\tanh\tau\right)}{\cosh\tau},

(10)

where
N_{l}
is a normalization constant and
P_{l,m}
and
Q_{l,m}
are the associated Legendre functions of first and second kind.
The normalization is fixed by the canonical commutation relation between
\zeta_{lm}
and its conjugate momentum
\pi_{lm}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{lm})}=\sigma R_{0}^{3}\cosh^{2}\tau\partial_{\tau}\zeta_{lm}
. Requiring
[\zeta_{lm},\pi_{l^{\prime}m^{\prime}}]=\sigma R_{0}^{3}\cosh^{2}\tau\left(f_{l}\partial_{\tau}f^{*}_{l^{\prime}}-\partial_{\tau}f_{l^{\prime}}f^{*}_{l}\right)\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}=i\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}
at
\tau\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle N_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma R_{0}^{3}}}\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma\left(l-1\right)}{\Gamma\left(l+3\right)}}.

(11)

Because of the tachyonic mass, the mode function grows exponentially at late times,

\displaystyle f_{l}(\tau)\to\frac{N_{l}}{2}e^{\tau}\left(1+\left(l(l+1)-1\right)e^{-2\tau}+...\right).

(12)

At large
l
this goes as

\displaystyle f_{l}(\tau)\approx\frac{e^{\tau}}{\sqrt{\pi\sigma R_{0}^{3}}l^{2}},\;\;\;\;{\rm for}\penalty\ \penalty\ \tau\gg\log l\gg 1,

(13)

which is dictated by dS
3
dilation isometry. Note however that the expansion in
\zeta
breaks down when

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle\gg

\displaystyle\tau_{\rm NL}=\ln\sqrt{\sigma R_{0}^{3}},

(14)

corresponding to Minkowski time

\displaystyle t_{\rm NL}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{R_{0}}{2}\sqrt{\sigma R_{0}^{3}}.

(15)

It is important to understand the physical meaning of the growth of
f_{l}

II.2
Energy momentum tensor

In the thin-wall approximation, and up to a cosmological constant, the EMT is the sum of the wall tension and a bulk contribution from the difference between the vacuum energies. For the purpose of calculating GW emission, we can express the EMT in Minkowski coordinates
x^{\mu}=(t,{\bf x})

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}x^{\mu}\partial_{\beta}x^{\nu}\delta\left(\xi\right)-\frac{3\sigma}{R_{0}}\eta^{\mu\nu}\theta\left(-\xi\right),

(22)

where
h^{\alpha\beta}
is the inverse of the induced metric on the wall and
\xi

\tanh\tau=t/r

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\cosh^{2}\tau}{r},\;\;\;\partial_{i}\tau=-\frac{\cosh^{2}\tau}{r^{3}}tx^{i}.

(23)

The wall is localized at
\sqrt{{\bf x}^{2}-t^{2}}=R_{0}e^{\zeta}

Using this and Eq. (
23

\displaystyle d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{t}{R_{0}}(1-\zeta)-\partial_{t}\zeta,\frac{x^{i}}{R_{0}}(1-\zeta)-\partial_{i}\zeta\right).

From Eq. (

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{0}^{2}e^{2\zeta}\left(g_{\alpha\beta}+\partial_{\alpha}\zeta\partial_{\beta}\zeta\right),

\displaystyle T^{0i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\left[\left(\sinh\tau\cosh\tau+\cosh 2\tau\partial_{\tau}\zeta\right)\hat{x}^{i}-\tanh\tau\left(\partial_{\theta}\zeta\hat{\theta}^{i}+\frac{\partial_{\varphi}\zeta}{\sin\theta}\hat{\varphi}^{i}\right)\right]\delta\left(\xi\right),

\displaystyle=

III
Gravitational waves

At linear order, the transverse-traceless gravitational waves satisfy

\displaystyle\ddot{h}_{\pm}(t,{\bf k})+k^{2}h_{\pm}(t,{\bf k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 16\pi G\varepsilon_{\mp}^{ij}\left(\hat{k}\right)T_{ij}\left(t,{\bf k}\right),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\pm}^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{x}\pm i\hat{y}\right)^{i}\left(\hat{x}\pm i\hat{y}\right)^{j}.

(31)

The solutions are

\displaystyle h_{\pm}\left(t,{\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 16\pi G\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\frac{\sin k(t-t^{\prime})}{k}T_{\pm}\left(t^{\prime},{\bf k}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle T_{\pm}\left(t,{\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}xe^{i{\bf kx}}\varepsilon_{\mp}^{ij}T_{ij}\left(t,{\bf x}\right).

In Eq. (
32

IV
Discussion

It is useful to consider the ratio between the GW energy and the latent heat of the bubble. Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm GW}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm GW}}{\frac{4\pi}{3}\epsilon t^{3}}\approx\frac{8\pi G}{16\pi^{5}R_{0}^{2}}\frac{t}{R_{0}},

(52)

where we used
R_{0}=3\sigma/\epsilon

This result suggests that the GW energy overcomes the total energy budget of the bubble within a back-reaction time defined via
\alpha_{\rm GW}(t_{\rm br})=1
, and given by

\displaystyle t_{\rm br}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 16\pi^{5}m_{\rm pl}^{2}R_{0}^{3}.

(53)

We will consider the duration of the PT,

, as a free parameter.
For our estimates below, it is useful to define

as a fraction of the Hubble time,

\displaystyle t_{\rm PT}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\rm PT}\,t_{H},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;f_{\rm PT}<1.

If the PT takes place in a radiation-dominated background with temperature

and
g_{*}
effective relativistic degrees of freedom (dof), the Hubble time

\displaystyle t_{H}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}g_{*}}{90}}\frac{T_{*}^{2}}{m_{\rm pl}}\approx 3\,\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{T_{*}^{2}}{m_{\rm pl}},

(55)

so the ratio of the back-reaction time to the PT duration is

\displaystyle\frac{t_{\rm br}}{t_{\rm PT}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1.6\times 10^{4}}{f_{\rm PT}}\,\left(\frac{g_{*}}{100}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(R_{0}T_{*}\right)^{2}\left(R_{0}m_{\rm pl}\right).

(56)

Our field theory analysis can only be trusted for

. However, we have no obvious a-priori expectation for the product

. As long as

, the back-reaction time is longer than the duration of the PT and back-reaction can probably be neglected for the purpose of estimating the GW energy, and in the usual analysis of bubble dynamics. Note that this would indeed be the case if

, which may be natural if thermal effects are important in triggering the phase transition.

The “strength of the PT” is often parameterized by the ratio of the latent heat energy density to the background total energy density,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm PT}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{3m_{\rm pl}^{2}H_{*}^{2}}.

(57)

With this, the fraction of GW energy density to the total energy density at the time of the PT is

\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm GW*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm GW}(t_{\rm PT})\,\alpha_{\rm PT}=\alpha_{\rm PT}\frac{t_{\rm PT}}{t_{\rm br}}.

The fraction of GW energy density observable today is

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+z_{*})^{4}}\frac{H_{*}^{2}}{H_{0}^{2}}\Omega_{\rm GW*}

where

km/s/Mpc,

Altogether, we find

\displaystyle 4\times 10^{-12}\,\alpha_{\rm PT}\,f_{\rm PT}\,\left(\frac{100}{g_{*}}\right)^{\frac{5}{6}}\left(\frac{1}{R_{0}T_{*}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{T_{*}}{10^{16}{\rm GeV}}\right)

V
Summary

We analyzed the gravitational waves (GWs) produced by an expanding phase transition bubble in the thin-wall regime. Quantum fluctuations around the classical bubble configuration lead to nontrivial angular moments for the bubble’s energy momentum tensor, sourcing GWs from a single bubble even before the onset of bubble collisions.

The total GW energy produced by the bubble grows with time as
E_{\rm GW}\propto t^{4}
. As a result, the ratio between the GW energy and the total energy reservoir of the bubble (corresponding to the latent heat in the true vacuum region inside the bubble) scales as
t
. Back-reaction effects (which we did not analyze) should become important if the bubble continues to live and expand for a time
t>t_{\rm br}
, with
t_{\rm br}\approx(16\pi^{5})m_{\rm pl}^{2}R_{0}^{3}
.

Assuming that percolation occurs before back-reaction becomes important, the produced GW energy spectrum peaks at frequency
f_{*\rm peak}\approx 1/R_{0}
. If the phase transition takes place in a radiation-dominated epoch with temperature
T_{*}
, with
g_{*}
relativistic degrees of freedom in the plasma, the peak frequency observable today is
f_{\rm peak}\approx 550\penalty\ {\rm GHz}/\left(g_{*}^{\frac{1}{3}}R_{0}T_{*}\right)
. This is much higher than current and planned GW detector sensitivities, meaning that the spectrum would appear blue: either
\propto f
or
\propto f^{3}
, depending if the experiment can observe above or below a break frequency corresponding to the onset of quadrupole domination. Unfortunately, however, simple estimates suggest that the signal falls below the sensitivity of even the most ambitious currently planned experiments, like the BBO experiment.

Our analysis leaves room to many additional questions, for instance: are there models in which the back-reaction time is shorter than the duration of the phase transition? what happens in this case? what is the prediction of the thermal fluctuation analogue of our quantum fluctuation process? what happens if plasma friction slows down the bubble wall, in comparison to our scenario in which the bubble wall traversed the usual Rindler orbit? Finally, we have only been able to calculate GW production from the late stage of the evolution of the bubble, after it expands significantly. It would be interesting to tackle the problem of bubble fluctuation at nucleation. This problem would require going beyond the semiclassical approximation.

helicity traceless-transverse projector for GWs with
{\bf k}||\hat{z}
is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{+}^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{x}+i\hat{y}\right)^{i}\left(\hat{x}+i\hat{y}\right)^{j}.

(92)

In polar coordinates, the relevant projections are

\displaystyle\epsilon^{ij}_{+}\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin^{2}\theta\,e^{2i\varphi}}{2},

(93)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{ij}_{+}\hat{n}_{i}\partial_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sin\theta\,e^{2i\varphi}}{2r}\left(\cos\theta\partial_{\theta}+\frac{i}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}+\sin\theta\,r\partial_{r}\right).

(94)

With this, the GW source term is

\displaystyle T_{+}\left(t,{\bf k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d^{3}xe^{i{\bf kx}}\varepsilon_{+}^{ij}\Pi_{ij}\left(t,{\bf x}\right)

(95)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}\int drr^{2}\,\frac{R_{0}\,r}{r^{2}-t^{2}}\delta_{w}\left(r-\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}+t^{2}}\right)\int d\theta\sin^{3}\theta\,\int d\varphi\,e^{ikr\cos\theta+2i\varphi}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sigma R_{0}\int drr\,\frac{R_{0}}{\sqrt{r^{2}-t^{2}}}\delta_{w}\left(r-\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}+t^{2}}\right)\int d\theta\sin^{2}\theta\,\int d\varphi\,e^{ikr\cos\theta+2i\varphi}\left(\cos\theta\partial_{\theta}+\frac{i}{\sin\theta}\partial_{\varphi}+\sin\theta\,r\partial_{r}\right)\zeta

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma R_{0}}{2}\int dr\,\frac{R_{0}}{r}\delta_{w}\left(r-\sqrt{R_{0}^{2}+t^{2}}\right)\int d\theta\sin^{3}\theta\,\int d\varphi\,\partial_{r}\left[\frac{r^{3}}{\sqrt{r^{2}-t^{2}}}\,e^{ikr\cos\theta+2i\varphi}\zeta\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma R_{0}r_{w}}{2}\int d\theta\sin^{3}\theta\,\int d\varphi\,e^{ikr_{w}\cos\theta+2i\varphi}\left(-\frac{r_{w}t}{R_{0}^{2}}\partial_{\tau}\zeta+\left(4+\frac{t^{2}}{R_{0}^{2}}+ikr_{w}\cos\theta\right)\zeta\right).

This reproduces Eq. (
34
).

Appendix C
Asymptotics

Below, we will first discuss the limits
kt\gg l^{2}
and
kt\ll l
, and then the limit
kt\sim l\gg 1
, which determines the large
t
behavior of
E_{\rm GW}
.

C.1
x=kt\gg l^{2}

In this limit, one of the terms in the expression (
III.1
) for
T_{+,l}
dominates,

\displaystyle I_{l}(x)\approx 2ix^{3}\int_{-1}^{1}dc\ c(1-c^{2})\,e^{icx}P_{l,2}(c).

When
x\gg l^{2}
, the integral is dominated by
x\sim\pm 1
, where we can use the asymptotic behavior of
P_{l,2}
to get

\displaystyle I_{l}(x)\approx-4i^{l}\frac{\Gamma(l+3)}{\Gamma(l-1)}\cos\left(x-\frac{l\pi}{2}\right).

(96)

Substituting in (
41
) gives

\displaystyle H_{l}(x)\approx-2i^{l}\frac{\Gamma(l+3)}{\Gamma(l-1)}x\sin\left(x-\frac{l\pi}{2}\right).

(97)

Therefore, the UV part of (
42
) is

\displaystyle\left\langle E_{{\rm GW},l}\right\rangle_{\rm UV}\approx\frac{G\sigma t^{3}}{R_{0}^{3}}\int^{\infty}d\ln x\frac{2l}{\pi^{3}x}.

(98)

C.2
x=kt\ll l

In this limit, we can expand
e^{ixc}
in the integrand of
I_{l}(x)
. There is a nonzero overlap between
(1-c^{2})P_{l,2}(c)
and
c^{n}
if
l-n=0
mod
2
, and
n\geq l-2
. Therefore, at small
x
,
I_{l}(x)\propto x^{l}
. We can use

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dc\ c^{n}P_{l}(c)=\frac{2^{l}\left((-1)^{l+n}+1\right)\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{l+n}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma(l+n+2)\Gamma\left(\frac{n-l}{2}+1\right)},

(99)

and that
P_{l,2}(c)=(1-c^{2})P_{l}^{\prime\prime}(c)
to find its coefficient:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dc\ c^{l-2}(1-c^{2})P_{l,2}(c)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{-l-1}\Gamma(l-1)}{\Gamma\left(l+\frac{3}{2}\right)}.

(100)

Substituting in (
41
) and Taylor expanding
\sin(x-w)
gives

\displaystyle H_{l}(x)\propto x^{l+2}.

(101)

It is the
|H^{\prime}_{l}(x)|^{2}\propto x^{2l+2}
term in (
42
) that dominates
E_{l}
in the IR:

\displaystyle\left\langle E_{{\rm GW},l}\right\rangle_{\rm IR}\approx\frac{G\sigma t^{3}}{R_{0}^{3}}\int_{0}d\ln x\ c_{\rm IR}x^{2l-1}.

(102)

Because of the steep growth, the IR contribution is subdominant in the large
l
limit. So it is not useful to calculate
c_{\rm IR}
.

C.3
x=kt\sim l\gg 1

For any
l
, there is an exact, but unwieldy, expression for
I_{l}(x)
in terms of Bessel functions. At large
l
, it simplifies to

\displaystyle I_{l}(x)\approx f(x)j_{l+4}(x)+g(x)j^{\prime}_{l+4}(x)

(103)

where

\displaystyle f(x)=-i^{l}\frac{2l^{6}\left(8l^{4}-8l^{2}x^{2}+x^{4}\right)}{x^{4}},\qquad g(x)=-i^{l}\frac{2l^{6}\left(8l^{3}x-4lx^{3}\right)}{x^{4}},

(104)

and
j_{l}(x)
is the spherical Bessel function. It has a classical turning point at
x_{t}=\sqrt{l(l+1)}
. At large
l
, it decays rapidly in the forbidden region, i.e. within
\Delta x\sim l^{1/3}
when
x<x_{t}
. When
x-x_{t}\gg l^{1/3}
, it rapidly oscillates and is well approximated via WKB:

\displaystyle j_{l}(x)\approx\frac{\sin(x+\Phi(x))}{x\sqrt{\omega(x)}}

(105)

where

\displaystyle\omega(x)\equiv\sqrt{1-\frac{l(l+1)}{x^{2}}},\qquad\Phi(x)\equiv-\int_{x}^{\infty}dy\ [\omega_{l}(y)-1]-\frac{l\pi}{2}.

(106)

Since
E_{l}
is dominated by the contribution within a region of size
\Delta x\sim l\gg l^{1/3}
, we can calculate its asymptotic behavior by using the above WKB expression. In this regime, derivatives of sine and cosine are much larger than the prefactors, hence

\displaystyle I_{l}(x)\approx\frac{1}{x}\left[\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{\omega(x)}}\sin(x+\Phi(x))+g(x)\sqrt{\omega(x)}\cos(x+\Phi(x))\right].

(107)

Since the prefactors are slowly varying, such that
\frac{d}{dx}\sim\frac{1}{x}
, we can approximate

\displaystyle\int^{x}dw\ \frac{f(w)}{w\sqrt{\omega(w)}}\sin(x-w)\sin(w+\Phi(w))\approx\frac{f(x)}{2x\sqrt{\omega(x)}}\sin(x+\Phi(x))\left(\frac{1}{2+\Phi^{\prime}(x)}-\frac{1}{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}\right),

(108)

with a similar expression for the cosine integral, resulting in

\displaystyle H_{l}(x)\approx\frac{x}{l^{2}}\left[\frac{f(x)}{\sqrt{\omega(x)}}\sin(x+\Phi(x))-g(x)\sqrt{\omega(x)}\cos(x+\Phi(x))\right].

(109)

Note that when
x\gg l
the above expression matches the one in (
97
), even though the latter was derived assuming
x\gg l^{2}
. Hence, the power spectrum in (
102
) holds for all
k\gg l/t
.

The UV spectrum
P_{l}(s)\propto 1/s
implies that
E_{{\rm GW},l}
is dominated by
x\sim l
and we have to keep all terms in (
109
). So to leading order in
l
,

\displaystyle\left\langle E_{{\rm GW},l}(t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{G\sigma t^{3}l}{\pi^{3}R_{0}^{3}}\int_{l}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{4}}\frac{(2x^{2}-l^{2})}{\sqrt{1-\frac{l^{2}}{x^{2}}}}=\frac{3G\sigma t^{3}}{4\pi^{2}R_{0}^{3}}.

(110)
