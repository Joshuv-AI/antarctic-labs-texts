Decompositions of the positive real numbers into disjoint sets closed under addition and multiplication
Gergely Kiss, Gábor Somlai, Tamás Terpai

Abstract
The main purpose of this paper is to prove that the positive real numbers can be decomposed into finitely many disjoint pieces which are also closed under addition and multiplication. As a byproduct of the argument we determine all the possible decompositions of the transcendental extension of the rational field of rank one into two pieces. Further, we prove that the positive elements of a real algebraic extensions of the rational numbers are indecomposable into two pieces.



1
Introduction

Róbert Freud asked whether the positive real numbers can be cut into two disjoint sets both closed under addition and multiplication.

The analogous question can be asked for any subfield of
{\mathbb{R}}
. We denote by
K^{+}
the positive elements of a subset
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. We call a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}

invariant
, if it is closed under addition and multiplication. By a
decomposition of

K^{+}
(or
K
) into
k
subsets we mean that

K^{+}
is the union of
k
pairwise disjoint non-empty invariant subsets. We denote this by

K^{+}=H_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup H_{k}.

We say that a decomposition of
K^{+}
into non-empty subsets is
non-trivial
if
k\geq 2
.
Freud asked whether there exists a non-trivial decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
into two pieces.
Finally, we say that
K^{+}
is
k
-decomposable
(
k\geq 2
) if there exists a non-trivial decomposition of
K^{+}
into
k
invariant pieces. During the preparation of this paper the authors noticed that the same problem was also discussed by Kane
[
3
]
for the positive real numbers. Note however, that our method is fundamentally different from that of Kane.

Elekes and Keleti
[
2
]
investigated the decomposition of the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
into Borel subsets which are closed under addition.
For a decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}=\cup_{\alpha<\kappa}A_{\alpha}
, where each
A_{\alpha}
is a Borel set closed under addition, they proved that every
A_{\alpha}
is of Lebesgue measure zero and is of first category, or is equal to
{\mathbb{R}}
,
(-\infty,0)
,
(-\infty,0]
,
(0,\infty)
, or
[0,\infty)
.
Furthermore, if
{\mathbb{R}}
is decomposed into the disjoint union of
\kappa
Borel sets, all closed under addition, then
\kappa\in\{1,2,3\}
or
\kappa
is uncountable.
They also proved that it is consistent with ZFC that
{\mathbb{R}}
has a decomposition into
\omega_{1}
pieces which are all
F_{\sigma}
and closed under addition.

We give simple constructions of non-trivial decompositions of the set of positive elements of transcendental extensions of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
It will be shown in Section
2
that if
\alpha
is a transcendental number, then any decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
is simply determined by a unique non-trivial derivation. Our method to answer the original question relies on the fact that such a derivation can be extended to a derivation on
{\mathbb{R}}
, see
[
5
]
.
Since our construction is based on the decomposition of a finitely generated subfield of
{\mathbb{R}}
, it is natural to investigate all the possible decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
. As a corollary presented in Section
2
we prove the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

The positive real numbers can be written as the union of
k
disjoint nonempty invariant sets for every natural number
k
.

One can simply extend this construction to a decomposition into countable infinitely many pieces.

Our main goal is to characterise the possible decompositions of
K^{+}
for a subfield
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}
. We introduce the notion of separating hyperplanes, which play an important role in the decomposition of transcendental extensions of
{\mathbb{Q}}
of degree
1
.
One of our main results is Theorem

, which describes the possible 2-decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
, where
\alpha
is a transcendental number. Since the statement is somewhat technical we will only formulate the precise description in Section

.
On the other hand, non-trivial finite decompositions for the algebraic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
do not exist.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
a
be an algebraic element over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)^{+}
is not 2-decomposable.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
we construct decompositions of finite degree transcendental extensions of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Section
3
contains a useful general lemma (Lemma
3.1
) that helps describing the decompositions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. Further it is proved in this section that the elements of a finite decomposition of

{\mathbb{Q}}
in Section
4
. Such a decomposition is lifted to its field of fractions
{\mathbb{Q}}(x)
in Section
5

Finally in Section

2
Fundamental construction

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
be a transcendental extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Now we present the basic construction that gives a decomposition of
K^{+}
.

We may identify the elements of
K
with rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
so
K
can be written as
\frac{p}{q}(\alpha)
, where
p
and
q
are in
\mathbb{Q}\left[x\right]
. Let
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
consist only of the elements
r\in\mathbb{Q}(x)
with
r(\alpha)>0
.

Let

\begin{split}&H_{+}=\left\{r\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}\mid r^{\prime}(\alpha)>0\right\},\\
&H_{0}=\left\{r\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}\mid r^{\prime}(\alpha)=0\right\},\\
&H_{-}=\left\{r\in{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}\mid r^{\prime}(\alpha)<0\right\},\end{split}

where
r^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of
r
. Note that
H_{0}={\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

H_{+}\sqcup H_{0}\sqcup H_{-}=K^{+}
gives a 3-decomposition of
K^{+}
.

Proof.

It is clear that these sets are closed under addition since every derivation is a linear operator. Moreover, the Leibniz rule

(r_{1}r_{2})^{\prime}(\alpha)=r_{1}^{\prime}(\alpha)r_{2}(\alpha)+r_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha)r_{1}(\alpha)

and the fact that
r_{1}(\alpha),r_{2}(\alpha)>0
shows that these sets are also closed under multiplication.
∎

The same argument shows that
(H_{+}\cup H_{0})\sqcup H_{-}
and
H_{+}\sqcup(H_{0}\cup H_{-})
are 2-decompositions of
K^{+}
.
In Section
5
we will show that these are the only ones.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\alpha
be a transcendental element over
\mathbb{Q}
.
All the possible non-trivial decompositions of
K^{+}={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
into two pieces are the following:

\begin{split}{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}&=(H_{+}\cup H_{0})\sqcup H_{-},\\
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}&=H_{+}\sqcup(H_{0}\cup H_{-}).\end{split}

Now we generalise the previous construction to finite transcendental extensions. Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})
, where

and

. The

will also be denoted by

. Now

can be identified with

denotes the elements

such that

. Let

dx_{i}(r)=\frac{\partial r(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})}{\partial x_{i}}~~\forall r\in K,

and let

Clearly,

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be algebraically independent over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, where
m\geq 2
.
For every
2\leq n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist infinitely many different ways to nontrivially decompose
K\cong{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
into
n
pieces.

Proof.

Every derivation
D
is a linear combination
l
of partial derivatives
dx_{i}
. The derivation
D
implies a 2-decomposition of
K^{+}
as
K^{+}=\left(H_{D,+,l}\cup H_{D,0,l}\right)\sqcup H_{D,-,l}
. Our strategy is to take finitely many derivations such that no two of them are multiples of each other and take one of the two pieces as above for each of them. Then the intersection of these pieces with careful choice of the sign of the derivation will be a nonempty invariant set.

Now we can construct infinitely many different decompositions into
n
pieces by taking infinitely many different sets of
n-1
pairwise linearly independent derivations
l_{1}
,
\dots
,
l_{n-1}
. As an initial step we consider the decomposition
K^{+}=\left(H_{D,+,l_{1}}\cup H_{D,0,l_{1}}\right)\sqcup H_{D,-,l_{1}}
. Then we iteratively cut one of the pieces of the previous step into two pieces. More precisely, in the
j^{th}
step for
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
we created the pieces
H_{1},\dots H_{j+1}
. Now we take
\left(H_{D,+,l_{j}}\cup H_{D,0,l_{j}}\right)
and
H_{D,-,l_{j}}
that are invariant sets. Clearly, there is a set
H_{k}
for some
1<k<j+1
such that
H_{k}\cap(H_{D,+,l_{j}}\cup H_{D,0,l_{j}})
and
H_{k}\cap H_{D,-,l_{j}}
are nonempty and invariant sets, as they are the intersection of invariant sets. The existence of such
H_{k}
is guaranteed by the fact that
l_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
are pairwise linearly independent and
\cup_{i=1}^{j+1}H_{i}=K^{+}
. Then instead of
H_{k}
we add
H_{k}\cap(H_{D,+,l_{j}}\cup H_{D,0,l_{j}})
and
H_{k}\cap H_{D,-,l_{j}}

to the system of invariant sets
H_{i}
(
i\neq k
) increasing the number of pieces by one. ∎

Our first goal is to find a solution so that we can extend the previously obtained decompositions of
\mathbb{Q}(\underline{\alpha})^{+}
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Now we deal with the problem of extension of a derivation obtained from Proposition
2.1
.
Indeed, let us assume that there exists a nontrivial decomposition of the positive elements of a finitely generated field

over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, where the pieces are determined by inequalities involving derivations in the manner of (
1
) and Proposition

.

The idea is that extending (not necessarily uniquely) these derivations to

{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and the number of pieces stays the same as for the decomposition of
K^{+}
.
Indeed, let
K
be an arbitrary field over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
a
be an algebraic element over
K
. Then by
[
5
, Lemma 14.2.2]
, a derivation on

extends uniquely to

. Let

be a set of algebraically independent elements over

.
[

, Lemma 14.2.3]
shows that if

is a derivation, and
u:S\to{\mathbb{R}}
is an arbitrary function, then there exists a unique derivation
\hat{d}:F(S)\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
\hat{d}|_{K}=d
and
\hat{d}|_{S}=u
.

Let
D_{i}
be derivations on
K=\mathbb{Q}(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})~(m\geq 2)
determined by
l_{i}
which give a decomposition into
n
parts. The previously discussed extensions imply that every derivation
D_{i}
from a subfield
K
of
{\mathbb{R}}
can be extended to
{\mathbb{R}}
. A standard transfinite recursion verifies the existence of the extensions, which proves Theorem
1.1
.

Let
H_{D_{i},+,l_{i}},H_{D_{i},0,l_{i}},H_{D_{i},-,l_{i}}
be defined by (
1
) where
K=\mathbb{R}
. The existence of these extended derivations immediately imply the existence of a nontrivial decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
into
n
pieces. Note that no new pieces were constructed. On the other hand, if there is a decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
into
n
pieces, defined by some of the conditions (
1
),
then there exists a field
K
which is finitely generated as a field over
{\mathbb{Q}}
and the intersection of any of the
n
pieces with

is non-empty. Indeed, take an element from each piece of the decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
, denote them by
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}
and take the field
K={\mathbb{Q}}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n})
, which is finitely generated.

Given that for any countable cardinal
k
we constructed
k
-decompositions of a finitely generated field
K
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of transcendence degree at least 2, it is natural to ask into what those decomposition pieces can look like.
We believe that the pieces of each finite decomposition can be determined using a sequence of derivations. First of all, we conjecture that in the case of finite decompositions the pieces as
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex sets are separated by separating hyperplanes, which are closed under multiplication (and addition) but we should say a bit more. The separating hyperplanes can also contain elements of the field so if we would like to properly describe the decompositions, then we have to specify what happens on these hyperplanes (whose existence we only conjecture). We believe that the partition on these hyperplanes is also determined by derivations defined on these one codimensional subspaces giving rise to
2
codimensional separating hyperspaces.
This process can be continued in order to obtain a proper description for finitely many pieces.

3
Elementary observations

In this section we prove a technical lemma, which will be used during the process when we determine all
2
-decompositions of some extensions of the field of rational numbers.
As a consequence of this lemma we immediately get that for every non-trivial decomposition into finitely many pieces, the pieces cannot all be measurable.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a field. Clearly,
K
contains
\mathbb{Q}
and let us assume that we have a decomposition
K^{+}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup H_{\lambda}
into disjoint invariant pieces. It is easy to see that each part of the decomposition is closed under multiplication by positive integers and therefore also under multiplication by positive rational numbers. The following lemma gives more information about the decomposition.

Lemma 3.1
.

1.

Let us assume that
a,a+q\in H_{1}
, where
q\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
. Then every element of the form
a+r
with
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{+}
is also in
H_{1}
.

2.

If
\lambda
is finite, then for every
a\in K^{+}
and
a+q>0
, where
q\in\mathbb{Q}
, we have that
a
and
a+q
are in the same part of the decomposition.

Proof.

1.

It follows from the previous observations that
H_{1}
is closed under convex combinations with rational weights, which gives the result for
0<r<q
.
It also shows that we only have to show that there exist a sequence of rational numbers
q_{i}
tending to infinity such that
a+q_{i}
are all in
H_{1}
.

Now
(a+2r)^{2}=a^{2}+4(a+r)r
, showing that
(a+2r)^{2}\in H_{1}
whenever
a
and
a+r\in H_{1}
as well. It follows that
a+nr
is also in
H_{1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.

Easy consequence of the previous case and the pigeonhole principle.

∎

As a corollary we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

For any finite decomposition of
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)^{+}
, the polynomials of degree
1
can only be cut into one or two pieces. If there are two pieces, then the membership is determined by the sign of the (leading) coefficient of the monomial of degree
1
.

As another consequence of the lemma we prove the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}=H_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup H_{k}
be a decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
with
k\geq 2
. Then at least one of the pieces is not Lebesgue measurable.

Proof.

Let us assume that every piece is Lebesgue measurable. Then one of them has to be of positive measure.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
that each piece is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
and for each piece
H_{i}
we have
H_{i}+H_{i}=\left\{h+h^{\prime}\mid h,h^{\prime}\in H_{i}\right\}\subseteq H_{i}
. Steinhaus’s theorem
[
8
]
implies that if a set
H\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is of positive measure, then
H+H
contains an interval. Therefore
H_{i}
contains an interval, which contradicts the fact that the other pieces are dense.
∎

Observation 3.4
.

1.

A similar proof would show that at least one of the pieces is not Borel using Piccard’s theorem but this simply follows from the fact that one of the pieces is not Lebesgue measurable.

2.

As a strengthening of Theorem
3.3
we obtain that if a set in the decomposition is measurable, then it is of measure zero.

3.

Similarly, every Borel set appearing in the decomposition is of first category by Piccard’s theorem, which says for a Borel set
A
of second category we have that
A+A
contains an interval, see
[
4
]
.

Note that the proof of
[
2
, Lemma 2.2]
applied for
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
would actually give a similar result.

4
One variable polynomial ring

In this section we investigate the decompositions of the ring extension
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]
, where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
is transcendental. Our arguments will work in slightly greater generality so as to be applicable in the case of an algebraic
\alpha
as well, and to make them clearer we introduce the following notation:

{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]\mid p(\alpha)>0\}.

Note that in the case of transcendental
\alpha
this is just a relabeling of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
, and we will consider them to be identified in the natural way.

We show that for any real
\alpha
there are only two different decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
into
2
nonempty pieces.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and let
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
be a decomposition into two invariant pieces. Then

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle\subset(H_{+}\cup H_{0})\text{ and }H_{2}\subset(H_{-}\cup H_{0}),

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\text{ or}

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle\subset(H_{-}\cup H_{0})\text{ and }H_{2}\subset(H_{+}\cup H_{0}),

where

\begin{split}H_{+}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}\mid p^{\prime}(\alpha)>0\},\\
H_{0}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}\mid p^{\prime}(\alpha)=0\},\\
H_{-}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}\mid p^{\prime}(\alpha)<0\}.\\
\end{split}

In the case of transcendental
\alpha
the pieces coincide with the ones listed in Theorem
2.2
, since
H_{0}={\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
and Lemma
3.1
ensures that it must belong to either
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
in its entirety.

We introduce some notation concerning the topology of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
that we use in our investigation.
We denote by
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
the set of polynomials over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of degree smaller than
N
and by
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{N}
the set of polynomials over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of degree exactly
N
. The vector space
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
is
N
dimensional over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and as a topological space can
naturally be identified with
{\mathbb{Q}}^{N}
, making it a metric space as well.
Using the natural topology induced by the metric, it is clear that the addition is a continuous function from
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\times{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
. Similarly, the multiplication is a continuous map from
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\times{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<2N-1}
.

Using a suitable fixed ordering of the coordinates the elements of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
can be identified with infinite sequences of rational numbers that contain only a finite number of nonzero coordinates. This space can be equipped with the distance
d\big((a_{i}),(b_{i})\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{|a_{i}-b_{i}|}{2^{i}}
, making it a metric space. This is not a complete metric space but if we restrict our attention to the set of polynomials of degree at most
N<\infty
, then the metric is equivalent to the product metric on
{\mathbb{Q}}^{N+1}
and hence the obtained topological space endowed with the subspace topology is homeomorphic to the natural topology defined above.

Our strategy in the remainder of this section is to find and describe a hyperplane separating the two parts
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
of the decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
. These are
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex sets so the existence of such a hyperplane would be guaranteed in the finite dimensional vector spaces
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
: we embed
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cong{\mathbb{Q}}^{N}
into
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and the closures of
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex sets are closed convex sets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
that might only intersect on their boundary. It is well known that in this case there is a nonzero vector
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
such that the scalar product of
(\lambda_{i})
with all the elements of
H_{1}
are nonnegative and the scalar products with all the elements of
H_{2}
are nonpositive. The following example shows that for infinite dimensional space this is not always the case in general, although in our case we will be able to conclude that. Thus before we start proving Proposition
4.1
we give an example of a pair of (
{\mathbb{Q}}
-)convex sets in an infinite dimensional vector space, which are not separated by a hyperplane (determined by a normal vector).

In what follows
LC(f)
denotes the leading coefficient of a one-variable polynomial
f
.

Example 4.2
.

Let

\begin{split}A&=\{f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]\colon LC(f)>0\},\\
B&=\{f\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]\colon LC(f)<0\}\end{split}

It is clear that sets
A
and
B
are closed under addition and both are
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex. Furthermore, the set
A
is also closed under multiplication.

Let us assume indirectly that there exist a nonzero vector
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
such that the scalar product of
(\lambda_{i})
with the elements of
A
is nonnegative and the scalar product of the elements of
B
with
(\lambda_{i})
is nonpositive. Since
x^{i}
is contained in
A
for every
i\geq 0
, we have that
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
for every
i\geq 0
. If for some
i
we had
\lambda_{i}>0
, then for all positive
\varepsilon
the polynomial
-\varepsilon x^{i+1}+x^{i}
would belong to
B
, but its scalar product with
(\lambda_{i})
would be
\lambda_{i}-\varepsilon\lambda_{i+1}
, which is positive for a sufficiently small positive
\varepsilon
– a contradiction. Hence all
\lambda_{i}=0
in contradiction with the assumption of
(\lambda_{i})
not being the zero vector.

Assume that for some
N
, none of the members of the following decomposition are empty:
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}=(H_{1}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N})\sqcup(H_{2}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N})
.
Then
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cap H_{1}
and
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cap H_{2}
are separated by a hyperplane as we discussed above; by this, we mean that both pieces are contained in one of the two (closed) halfspaces determined by the hyperplane.
Such a hyperplane is determined by one of its normal vectors
v_{N}
. If
M\geq N
, then we denote by
v_{M,N}
the vector consisting of the first
N
coordinates of
v_{M}
.
Further we say that two vectors
u
and
v
are
equivalent
if
u
is a nonzero multiple of
v
. In this case we write
u\sim v
.

It is clear that we may only expect
v_{N}
to be unique up to multiplication by nonzero real numbers. Also, it could happen that
(H_{j}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N})
is contained in a proper subspace of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
. We would like to exclude these pathologies and we intend to show that
v_{M,N}\sim v_{N}
if
M\geq N
(
M,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
). First we show that if there is a decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
into two pieces, then the linear polynomials are already separated.

Lemma 4.3
.

If all the linear polynomials in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
are contained in
H_{j}
, then
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}=H_{j}
.

Proof.

We show that if the linear polynomials are contained in

, then the positive rationals are also in

. Indeed, every element of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
can be written as the sum of linear polynomials taking positive values at

{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{1}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}\subset H_{j}
implies that
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<2}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}\subset H_{j}
. Without loss of generality we may assume

.

We prove by induction on

that
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<i}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}={\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<i}\cap H_{1}
for every

.
Let

be of degree
i-1
with
g(\alpha)>0
.
Assume first that
LC(g)>0
. Then there exists a polynomial

of degree
i-1
written as the product of a positive constant and linear terms of the form

, where
\beta<\alpha
, such that

and
LC(g)=LC(h)
. This can always be done by choosing these

’s close enough to
\alpha
. Then
(g-h)(\alpha)>0
and

is of degree at most

so

and

are in

by induction. Since

is closed under multiplication and addition we have

.

A similar argument works if

but in this case

is the product of a positive constant and
i-2
linear term of the form

and one of the form
\beta^{\prime}-x
, where

is chosen to be close enough that

and

. Then similarly as above

follows.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

At least one of
H_{1}
and

contains polynomials with positive and negative leading coefficient of degree

for every
2\leq i
and

.

Proof.

By Lemma

the linear polynomials are divided into two nonempty parts. Thus

and

both contain squares of polynomials of degree

, which have a positive leading coefficient. Since

is a partition

or

contains polynomials of negative leading coefficient of degree

as well.
Thus the statement is true for

.

Now since both parts contain linear polynomials, multiplying by a suitable power of such a linear polynomial shows that for every

one of

and

contains polynomials of degree

with positive and negative leading coefficients as well.

The second statement directly follows from Lemma
4.3
by taking the

∎

As a consequence of Lemma

we obtain that for every

the part of

that evaluates positively on
\alpha
contains elements from both parts of the decomposition. Thus we obtain a decomposition of a halfspace (the polynomials that evaluate to positive numbers) of an

{\mathbb{Q}}
into two convex subsets. Recall that

can be embedded in

such that the closures of

(

) in

are convex sets intersecting only in a hyperplane. These type of convex subsets in finite dimensional vector space over

are separated by hyperplanes and in this case the hyperplane is uniquely determined by the pieces since they cover a halfspace, the positive part of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
. Moreover,
v_{N}
, the normal vector of the hyperplane is not equal to

since at least one of the two parts of the decomposition contains both elements with positive and negative scalar product with
(0,\ldots,0,1)
by Lemma

.

As a consequence of Lemma

we have that that

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and

, and one of them,
H_{1}
, say, contains the elements of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}
. Since the complement of a hyperplane containing
{\mathbb{Q}}
does not have a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex intersection with
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
, the set

is not contained in any proper subspace of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
for every

. We can prove the same for

.

Lemma 4.5
.

H_{i}
is not contained in any proper subspace of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
for every

and

.

Proof.

By Lemma

, the statement holds for

that is not orthogonal to

.
Thus the first

that

contains polynomials of degree

∎

.

1.

.

Let us assume indirectly that there are

. Then there is a

naturally embeds into

.
Now

and

are nonzero nonequivalent vectors since

As a corollary of this argument we might assume that

is always an extension of

if

. Thus we obtain an infinite sequence

for every

.

Observation 4.7
.

As
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cong{\mathbb{Q}}^{N}
naturally embeds to
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
, the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
naturally embeds to
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, an infinite dimensional vector space over
{\mathbb{R}}
. Further denote

{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}=\{p(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]\mid p(\alpha)>0\}.

It follows from the previous argument that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are separated by the hyperplane of the infinite dimensional space of
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
orthogonal to the vector
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Therefore we can take the closure of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
that is also separated by this hyperplane. This observation is critically used in the sequel.

In our argument we only use hyperplanes that are defined by a vector
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
as follows:
\mathcal{H}=\{\sum a_{i}x^{i}\mid\sum a_{i}\lambda_{i}=0\}
. From now on, we implicitly assume this for all of our hyperplanes.

Above we showed that the invariant sets of a 2-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
can be separated by a hyperplane of this type.
It will be called a
separating hyperplane
of the decomposition and we denote it by
\mathcal{H}
. Next we describe the possible separating hyperplanes of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
.

Note that the separating hyperplane lives in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, and our condition of the pieces
H_{j}
being closed under addition and multiplication is in (a subset of)
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
. The points of the hyperplane in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
are typically (but not necessarily) not contained in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
. However, the usual addition and multiplication in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
extend to those of
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
in a continuous way, and we note that the two closed halfspaces defined by the separating hyperplane intersect
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}
in pieces that are also closed under addition and multiplication. Indeed, these two halfspaces contain
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, respectively, as a dense subset.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
be a decomposition into
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex subsets. Then for the separating hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
the set
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
consists of those elements of
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
that are limits of sequences both from
H_{1}
and from
H_{2}
. Additionally, these sequences can be chosen be consist of polynomials of at most the same degree as their limit point.

Proof.

Take any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. In
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
, the set
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
is the common boundary of its two halfspaces, so only elements of
\mathcal{H}
can be limits of sequences in both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. On the other hand, as
H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}={\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are dense in the intersections of corresponding halfspaces with
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
. Take an arbitrary positive element
h\in\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
. Since
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
, there exists a sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cap H_{1}
that converges to
h
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
. The same argument can be applied to a sequence of elements
H_{2}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
tending to
h
as well.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[x]
be a separating hyperplane for a
2
-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
. Then
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
is closed under addition and multiplication (i.e., invariant).

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is a linear subspace and
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
is closed under both addition and multiplication,
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
is closed under addition.

Now we prove that
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
is closed under multiplication as well. Namely, we show that for all
N
, if we multiply two elements of
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
, then the product lies in
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<2N-1}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
. By Lemma
4.4
we have that if
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
cut
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
into two pieces, then both pieces intersect
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}
non-trivially for all
N\geq 2
.

Now we take two elements
h,k\in\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
. By Lemma
4.8
there exist sequences
(h_{i,n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(k_{i,n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{<N}\cap H_{i}
(
i=1,2
) that are converging in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
to
h
and
k
, respectively. Since
h_{i,n}k_{i,n}
are in
H_{i}
, Lemma
4.8
implies that
hk\in\mathcal{H}
. Moreover it is clear that
hk\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
as
hk
is a limit of polynomials that evaluate positively on
\alpha
and
hk(\alpha)\neq 0
, finishing the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

{\mathbb{Q}}\subset\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

Every element of
{\mathbb{Q}}
can be approximated by
r_{1}\alpha-r_{2}
and
r^{\prime}_{1}-r^{\prime}_{2}\alpha
, which are in different parts by Corollary

. Hence by the previous argument
{\mathbb{Q}}\in\mathcal{H}
.
∎

Corollary 4.11
.

The hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
itself is closed under multiplication.

Proof.

Lemma
4.9
tells us that
\mathcal{H}\cap{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
is invariant, so its defining coefficients
(\lambda_{i})
satisfy the corresponding algebraic condition

\displaystyle\forall(a_{i}),(b_{i}):\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}a_{i}=0,\sum_{i}a_{i}\alpha^{i}>0,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}b_{i}=0,\sum_{i}b_{i}\alpha^{i}>0\Longrightarrow

(2)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}a_{j}b_{i-j}=0.

The equalities
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}x_{i}=0,\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}y_{i}=0
define a closed plane in each
{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}\times{\mathbb{R}}[x]^{<N}
, and on it the two additional inequalities cut out a relative open set. Since the conclusion holds on this open set, it must also hold on its Zariski closure, which is the whole plane. That is, the condition (
2
) holds true without requiring the inequalities and
\mathcal{H}
is indeed closed under multiplication.
∎

Our next step in describing the hyperplanes that can serve as a separating hyperplane is to determine which are closed under multiplication.

Recall that the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
is uniquely (up to scaling) determined by a sequence
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers. A polynomial
p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\deg(p)}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
is in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
\sum_{i=0}^{\deg(p)}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{i}=0
.

Lemma 4.12
.

\lambda_{1}\neq 0
.

Proof.

By the way of contradiction, let us suppose that
\lambda_{1}=0
. Then
x\in\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is closed under multiplication and addition by Lemma
4.9
we have that all polynomials whose constant coefficient is
0
are contained in
\mathcal{H}
. Also by Lemma
3.1
we have that each piece of the decomposition is closed under translation by positive rational numbers, hence so is
\mathcal{H}
. This shows that
\mathcal{H}
contains an open set in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{H}
is a separating hyperplane.
∎

As a special case we obtain again that the (positive) linear polynomials are cut into two nonempty pieces by
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.

It is clear that
\lambda_{i}x-\lambda_{1}x^{i}
and
\lambda_{j}x-\lambda_{1}x^{j}
belong to
\mathcal{H}
. By Lemma
4.9
, the product of two such polynomials also belongs to
\mathcal{H}
. Thus

\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}x^{2}-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{1}x^{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{1}x^{i+1}+\lambda_{1}^{2}x^{i+j}\in\mathcal{H},

which is equivalent to the fact that

\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{j}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{i+1}+\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{i+j}=0.

In particular, choosing
i=2
gives the following recursion for the coefficients:

\lambda_{2}^{2}\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{j+1}-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}\lambda_{j}+\lambda_{1}^{2}\lambda_{j+2}=0.

This recursion for the coefficients shows that
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
determine the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}
.
Since the sequences
(\lambda_{i})
and
(\delta\lambda_{i})
determine the same hyperplane for every
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
we may assume that
\lambda_{1}=1
.

Lemma 4.13
.

The following are the only hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{R}}[x]
with
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
that are closed under multiplication:

1.

\mathcal{H}_{\beta,\gamma}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p(\beta)=p(\gamma)\}
for some real numbers
\beta\neq\gamma
,

2.

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p^{\prime}(\delta)=0\}
for some
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

3.

\mathcal{H}_{z,\overline{z}}=\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p(z)=p(\overline{z})\}=\{p\in{\mathbb{R}}[x]:\operatorname{Im}p(z)=0\}
for some
z=u+iv\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

It is readily verified that these hyperplanes are closed under multiplication. The argument above implies that the pair
(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
(under the assumption
\lambda_{1}=1
) determines such a hyperplane. We will show that all values
(\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
occur in one of the listed cases, and this will finish the proof of Lemma
4.13
. To calculate
\lambda_{i}
, we will use the polynomial
x^{i}-\lambda_{i}x
that has to belong to
\mathcal{H}
.

In the first case (
\mathcal{H}_{\beta,\gamma}
) we get the condition
\gamma^{i}-\lambda_{i}\gamma=\beta^{i}-\lambda_{i}\beta
, which yields
\lambda_{i}=\frac{\beta^{i}-\gamma^{i}}{\beta-\gamma}
and in particular

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\beta+\gamma,

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}+\beta\gamma+\gamma^{2}.

It is straightforward to see that these value pairs cover the region
\lambda_{2}^{2}<\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{3}
of the parameter space.

In the second case (
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
) we get the condition

, in particular

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=2\delta,

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=3\delta^{2},

and these cover the parameter pairs that satisfy
\lambda_{2}^{2}=\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{3}
.

Finally, in the third case (
\mathcal{H}_{z,\overline{z}}
) we get

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Im}z^{2}}{\operatorname{Im}z}=2u,

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Im}z^{3}}{\operatorname{Im}z}=3u^{2}-v^{2},

covering the rest of the parameter space (
\lambda_{2}^{2}>\frac{4}{3}\lambda_{3}
).

∎

All of these hyperspaces naturally separate two classes of polynomials determining the possible decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
:

•

\mathcal{H}_{\beta,\gamma}
splits

into

\displaystyle S_{\beta,\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p(\beta)>p(\gamma)\right\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle S_{\gamma,\beta}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p(\gamma)>p(\beta)\right\};

•

\mathcal{H}_{z,\overline{z}}
splits
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
into

\displaystyle S_{z,\overline{z}}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:\operatorname{Im}p(z)=-\operatorname{Im}p(\overline{z})>0\right\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle S_{\overline{z},z}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:\operatorname{Im}p(z)=-\operatorname{Im}p(\overline{z})<0\right\};

•

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}
splits
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
into

\displaystyle S^{+}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p^{\prime}(\delta)>0\right\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle S^{-}_{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]:p^{\prime}(\delta)<0\right\}.

It remains to check which of these classes are closed under multiplication in
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
.
We claim that the separating hyperspace for a

-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
can only be

and it follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 4.14
.

For every

such that

the open subspaces

For every

the open subspaces

3.

For every
\delta\neq\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}

1.

If
\alpha
,
\beta
and

are 3 different real numbers, then there exists

such that

The same argument works for

It can easily be seen that if

with

and

we have

is not closed under multiplication.

If

, then there exist

∎

Proof of Proposition

5
Lifting the decomposition

In this section we show how a 2-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
for a transcendental
\alpha
extends to
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
. As we described the possible 2-decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
, this will imply the characterisation of 2-decompositions stated in Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
is a non-trivial decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
that is closed under addition and multiplication. This decomposition restricts to
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}=(H_{1}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+})\sqcup(H_{2}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+})
. One of these sets may be empty but we will first assume that this is not the case and conclude that then this splitting uniquely determines the decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
as described in (
1
).

By Proposition
4.1
we have two choices for

H_{1}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
and
H_{2}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
H_{+}\subset H_{1}
(i.e.,
H_{1}
contains all polynomials that have positive derivative at
\alpha
) and
H_{-}\subset H_{2}
(i.e.,
H_{2}
contains all polynomials that have negative derivative at
\alpha
). The constant rational polynomials can be included in
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
arbitrarily, but two nonconstant polynomials whose derivatives have different signs at
\alpha
must lie in different classes of the decomposition.

We would like to decide which set contains
\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}(\alpha)
for
p_{1},p_{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
coprime polynomials, with
p_{2}
nonconstant. If
p_{1}(\alpha)\in H_{1}
and
p_{2}(\alpha)\in H_{2}
, then
\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}(\alpha)\in H_{1}
, otherwise
p_{2}\cdot\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}=p_{2}
would imply that
p_{1}(\alpha)\in H_{2}
, a contradiction. The same argument works with the role of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
reversed, therefore, from now on we assume that the sign of
p_{1}^{\prime}(\alpha)
and
p_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha)
is the same, and
p_{1}
is not a (non-zero) rational multiple of
p_{2}
.
Since
\alpha
is transcendental we have
p_{1}(\alpha)\neq p_{2}(\alpha)
. We show that there exists a linear polynomial
l(x)=ax+b\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
such that
(p_{1}l)^{\prime}(\alpha)
and
(p_{2}l)^{\prime}(\alpha)
have different signs. Indeed,
(p_{i}(x)(ax+b))^{\prime}=p_{i}^{\prime}(x)(ax+b)+ap_{i}(x)
. Such an expression is positive at
\alpha
if and only if
\frac{p_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{p_{i}(\alpha)}>\frac{-a}{a\alpha+b}
. Note that in our case
a\alpha+b
has to be positive.

We claim that
\frac{p_{1}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{p_{1}(\alpha)}\neq\frac{p_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha)}{p_{2}(\alpha)}
. The equality would imply
(p_{1}^{\prime}p_{2}-p_{1}p_{2}^{\prime})(\alpha)=0
, which would in turn give
\left(\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}\right)^{\prime}(\alpha)=0
.

Now it remains to find
a
and
b
in
{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
\frac{-a}{a\alpha+b}
lies between

c:=\left(\frac{p_{1}^{\prime}}{p_{1}}\right)(\alpha)
and
d:=\left(\frac{p_{2}^{\prime}}{p_{2}}\right)(\alpha)
. This can be done by choosing
a
to be a rational number between
c
and
d
and then choosing
b\in{\mathbb{Q}}
in a way that
a\alpha+b
is suitably close to
1
.

To summarise, the algorithm that decides which of
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
contains
t=\frac{p_{1}}{p_{2}}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(x)^{+}_{x=\alpha}
(with
p_{1},p_{2}\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
) is as follows. If
p_{2}
is constant or
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are in different classes of the decomposition, then
t
is in the same class as
p_{1}
. Otherwise, it follows from the previous argument that
t(x)
can also be written as
\frac{p_{1}(x)l(x)}{p_{2}(x)l(x)}
, where
p_{1}(x)l(x)
and
p_{2}(x)l(x)
are not in the same class. This assign a membership to
t
in
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
. Note that the decomposition extends uniquely from
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
. Thus, if
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
is decomposed into two pieces non-trivially, then the only possible decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
is the one given in Theorem
2.2
.

To finish the proof of Theorem
2.2
it remains to prove that if there is a 2-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
, then
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
is also decomposed into two parts non-trivially. Indirectly, suppose that
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
is contained in one of the invariant sets, say
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}\subset H_{1}
. This also implies that
{\mathbb{Q}}\subset H_{1}
. We show that in this case
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}\subset H_{1}
. For this it is enough to show that for every polynomial
q\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
the rational function
\frac{1}{q}(\alpha)
also belongs to
H_{1}
. Indeed, if it is the case, then all
\frac{p}{q}(\alpha)\in H_{1}
as the product of
p(\alpha)\in H_{1}
and
\frac{1}{q}(\alpha)\in H_{1}
and
H_{1}
is closed under multiplication. Therefore, we indirectly assume that there is a
q\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
such that
\frac{1}{q}(\alpha)\in H_{2}
. Now we take
{\mathbb{Q}}\left(\frac{1}{q}\right)^{+}_{\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q(\alpha)}}
, which is the set of the positive elements of a field of transcendence degree 1. Since
{\mathbb{Q}}\subset H_{1}
and
\frac{1}{q}(\alpha)\in H_{2}
,
{\mathbb{Q}}(\frac{1}{q})^{+}_{\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q(\alpha)}}
is cut into two pieces non-trivially, hence its decomposition is determined by the sign of the derivative with respect to
\frac{1}{q}
evaluated at
\alpha
.

In particular, as
{\mathbb{Q}}[q]^{+}_{q=q(\alpha)}\subset{\mathbb{Q}}(\frac{1}{q})^{+}_{\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{q(\alpha)}}
,
{\mathbb{Q}}[q]^{+}_{q=q(\alpha)}
is also decomposed into two parts non-trivially. Indeed, take any rational number
r>q(\alpha)
. The numbers
q(\alpha)
and
r-q(\alpha)
are both positive and the derivatives (with respect to
\frac{1}{q}
) of
q(x)
and
r-q(x)
as rational functions of
\frac{1}{q}
have opposite signs at
\alpha
. Hence
q(\alpha)
and
r-q(\alpha)
belong to different
H_{i}
’s, in contradiction to our initial indirect assumption.

Thus we get that if there is a 2-decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
, then
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
is also decomposed into two parts non-trivially. This finishes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

As we have seen earlier each element of
{\mathbb{Q}}(x)^{+}_{x=\alpha}\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=\alpha}
can be written as
\frac{p}{q}
, where
p(\alpha)
and
q(\alpha)
are positive. Also, it is clear that such a quotient is not uniquely written so
p^{\prime}(\alpha)
and
q^{\prime}(\alpha)
have different sign.
Now having positive derivative means that the corresponding real valued function is locally increasing at
\alpha
and function with negative derivative at
\alpha
locally decrease at
\alpha
. Having assumed that all these functions are positive at
\alpha
we obtain that a decreasing decreasing function divided by one that is increasing is decreasing and vice-versa. This confirms that the extension preserves the property that the invariant sets are determined by a derivation.

6
Algebraic extension

In this section we prove that there is no nontrivial decomposition of an algebraic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
into 2 pieces. Now we recall Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.2.

Let
a
be an algebraic element over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)^{+}
is not
2
-decomposable.

Proof.

Let
F={\mathbb{Q}}(a)
, where
a
is an algebraic number and let
n
be the degree of the extension
[F:{\mathbb{Q}}]
.
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)
is an
n
dimensional vector space over
{\mathbb{Q}}
, and we consider it embedded into
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}
in the natural way. The multiplication on
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)
also induces a multiplication on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, making it a commutative ring. Any partition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)^{+}
into
{\mathbb{Q}}
-convex sets gives rise to a covering of a closed halfspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by the closures of the partition classes.
Since our space is finite-dimensional, these closures are separated by a hyperplane
H
.

Consider now the ring homomorphism
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}[x]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\Phi\left(\sum c_{i}x^{i}\right)=\sum{a^{i}\otimes c_{i}}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(a)\otimes{\mathbb{R}},

Its restriction to
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
is surjective and sends
p
to
p(a)
, so taking the preimages of the partition classes on
{\mathbb{Q}}(a)^{+}
gives a partition of
{\mathbb{Q}}[x]^{+}_{x=a}
into 2 nonempty invariant sets. These sets are separated by the preimage
\Phi^{-1}(H)
of the hyperplane
H
, so this preimage is a separating hyperplane and contains the kernel of
\Phi
.

Lemma 6.1
.

The kernel of
\Phi
is the ideal generated by the minimal polynomial of
a
.

Proof.

The multiples of the minimal polynomial of
a
are clearly in the kernel of
\Phi
, and form a set of codimension
n
.
∎

By Proposition
4.1
, the separating hyperplane can only be
\mathcal{H}_{a}
(notation of Lemma
4.13
), but the minimal polynomial of
a
is in the kernel of
\Phi
but not in
\mathcal{H}_{a}
- a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
K
be an algebraic field extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then there is no
2
-decomposition of
K
.

Proof.

Let
K
be an algebraic field over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Each non-trivial decomposition of
K^{+}
gives a non-trivial decomposition of some subfield of
K
that is a finite extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Since
{\mathbb{Q}}
is a perfect field every finite extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
is simple. Therefore it is enough to show that there is no
2
-decomposition of
F={\mathbb{Q}}(a)
and we are done by Theorem
1.2
.
∎

7
Further directions

There are several different questions arising. We have determined the possible 2-decompositions of a degree 1 transcendental extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
. There are several obstacles on going one step further in many directions.

•

One option would be to try to determine all
k
-decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
for every positive integer
k
. The main obstacle here is to prove that the parts of the decomposition are separated by hyperplanes (which are closed under multiplication and are defined by a nonvanishing sequence of coefficients). If it were true we could use Lemma
4.13
and a suitable adaptation of Lemma
4.14
to determine all decompositions into finitely many pieces. Thus we conjecture that
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
does not have proper
k
-decompositions if
k>3
and there is a unique
3
-decomposition, which is the following:

{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}=H_{+}\sqcup H_{0}\sqcup H_{-}.

•

It is rather an interesting open question whether there exists a decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
or at least
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
into (countably) infinitely many sets that are closed under addition and multiplication. Such a construction could be given by taking each
l_{r}(\alpha)\in{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
into different sets for different
r\in{\mathbb{Q}}
, where
l_{r}(x)
are linear functions of the form
x-r
or
r-x
(depending on the positivity of
l_{r}(\alpha)
). This would imply dissection of
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
by rings generated by linear functions. It can be seen that the elements of these rings are disjoint. On the other hand, it is easy to show that not all quadratic functions are covered in this way so we should extend this construction for polynomials of higher degree. We note that it is not trivial to decide whether this construction can be extended to
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
and further to
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
. The problem of finding disjoint invariant sets leads to seeking the solutions of equations of type
P\circ Q(x)=R\circ S(x)
, where
P,Q\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}[x]
are polynomials and
R,S\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}(x)
are rational functions. Even if
P,Q\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{+}[x]
are also polynomials, the answer to this problem is not clear.

Note that for general
P,Q,R,S\in{\mathbb{Q}}[x]
this problem was first investigated and solved by J. F. Ritt
[
7
]
. On the other hand, the general solution, if
P,Q,R,S\in{\mathbb{Q}}(x)
are rational functions is not known. The best of our knowledge is based on the work of F. Pakovich
[
6
]
, therefore the interested reader is referred to that and the reference therein.

Concerning our investigation we ask whether this construction can work at least for
{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha]^{+}
and if so we conjecture that there is a decomposition of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)^{+}
into countably infinitely many invariant sets.

•

It would be nice to extend our result to transcendental extensions of degree at least 2. The potential separating hyperplanes, which are closed under multiplication, can be described as in Section
4
and at least if the transcendence degree of the extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}
is 2 we obtain that the possible separating hyperplanes are determined by derivations. (This has been verified by the authors but since this has no immediate consequence, we do not include the proof here.) However, the existence of separating hyperplanes is not yet clear even in the case of
2
-decompositions. Moreover, generally it seems to be a challenging problem to prove or disprove whether any
2
-decomposition of
{\mathbb{R}}
is determined by derivations (or by the concept of separating hyperplanes).

•

It is also an interesting question how we can determine the finite decompositions of
{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})^{+}
into invariant sets for algebraically independent elements
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. To illustrate a difficulty that arises we take the field
K={\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
, where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are algebraically independent elements, and we try to find all 2-decomposition of
K^{+}
. One may try to prove that all such decompositions come from taking a derivation of the form
d=c_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{1}}+c_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{2}}
, where
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are arbitrary. Then we can find two different 2-decompositions of
K
similar to those in Theorem
2.2
such that

\begin{split}H_{1}&=\{r\in K\colon d(r)\geq 0\},H_{2}=\{r\in K\colon d(r)<0\}\\
H^{\prime}_{1}&=\{r\in K\colon d(r)>0\},H^{\prime}_{2}=\{r\in K\colon d(r)\leq 0\}.\end{split}

(3)

But in fact, there are other 2-decompositions of
K
. For example, we take the sets

\displaystyle H^{1}_{+}

\displaystyle=\left\{r\colon\frac{\partial r(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{1}}>0\right\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{-}

\displaystyle=\left\{r\colon\frac{\partial r(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{1}}<0\right\},

\displaystyle H^{1}_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{r\colon\frac{\partial r(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{1}}=0\right\}.

Then we take
H^{1}_{0}
and divide it into 2 invariant set as follows:

\displaystyle H^{1,2}_{+}

\displaystyle=H^{1}_{0}\cap\left\{r\colon\frac{\partial r(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{2}}>0\right\},

\displaystyle H^{1,2}_{0,-}

\displaystyle=H^{1}_{0}\cap\left\{r\colon\frac{\partial r(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{\partial\alpha_{2}}\leq 0\right\}.

Now set
H_{1}=H^{1}_{+}\cup H^{1,2}_{+}
and
H_{2}=H^{1}_{+}\cup H^{1,2}_{0,-}
. It is straightforward to verify that this gives a 2-decomposition of
K
. Moreover, it is easy to show that
H_{1}
cannot be a set of the form (
3
) defined by a derivation
d
that is a linear combination of the partial derivatives.
Note however that this example is still based on the idea of using separating hyperplanes.
