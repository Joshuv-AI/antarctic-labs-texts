Abstract
For linear systems A ​ x = b Ax=b we develop iterative algorithms based on a sketch-and-project approach. By using judicious choices for the sketch, such as the history of residuals, we develop weighting strategies that enable short recursive formulas. The proposed algorithms have a low memory footprint and iteration complexity compared to regular sketch-and-project methods. In a set of numerical experiments the new methods compare well to GMRES , SYMMLQ and state-of-the-art randomized solvers.

Introduction

For data science and scientific computing, consider the solution of large, possibly sparse, general linear systems

Ax=b,

(1)

where
A
is a real
m\times n
matrix, the unknowns are
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

is the right-hand side.
We develop methods for
general systems with a focus on square and overdetermined problems.
Direct algorithms use a factorization of
A
(Golub and Van Loan
[
10
]
). With appropriate pivoting
strategies, these methods are very accurate and reliable. Sparse factorizations exist for almost all direct algorithms (Davis
[
7
]
).
Nevertheless, sparse pivoting strategies are sometimes too costly.
When
A
is only available as a linear operator, or the system is too large or
otherwise not suitable for a direct method, then iterative methods are most effective
(Saad
[
17
]
, Barrett et al.
[
2
]
).
In particular, for modern data-driven applications, system
eq.

1
may constitute
a random subset of a larger dataset, or it may be contaminated by noise, so that highly accurate
solutions are not needed or even desired. In this context,
sketching methods
have become popular (Woodruff et al.
[
20
]
), especially in the artificial intelligence and machine learning
community (Liberty
[
13
]
).
For some sketching matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
with
r\ll m
,
a solution of

S^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Ax=S^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}b

(2)

approximates
x
in the original system (
1
).
If
n
is not too large, Blendenpik
[
1
]
and LSRN
[
14
]

use (
2
) to construct a preconditioner for LSQR to solve (
1
).
The value of
r>0
needs to be set in advance. Higher values of
r
usually result in higher computational cost, but also improved numerical performance.

The methods of Gower and Richtárik
[
11
]

and Richtárik and Takác
[
16
]

solve a sequence of sketched problems

\min\|p\|_{B}\text{\ \ subject to\ \ }S^{T}Ap=S^{T}(b-Ax),\qquad x\leftarrow x+p

with a different random
S
each time, a symmetric positive definite

B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and
\|p\|_{B}=(p^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Bp)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
This process is referred to as a
Sketch-and-Project
approach.

In Gower and Richtárik’s analysis

[
11
]
, the convergence rate
\rho
for solving (
1
) (
0<\rho<1
) depends on the smallest eigenvalue of a certain matrix. Because the convergence
depends on a rate that can be arbitrarily close to one, the observed numerical performance may be slow.

1.1
Notation

The integer
k\geq 1
represents the iteration index.
Vector
e_{j}
denotes the
j^{\text{th}}
column of the identity matrix
I
, with dimension
depending on the context. The
i^{\textnormal{th}}
row of
{A}
is
a_{i*}={e_{i}}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{A}
, while the

j^{\textnormal{th}}
column is
a_{j}=Ae_{j}
. For the
k^{\text{th}}
solution estimate
{x}_{k}
, the residual vector is
{r}_{k}:=b-A{x}_{k}
, with associated vector
{y}_{k}:=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k}
. We abbreviate
“symmetric positive definite” to spd and “symmetric indefinite” to sid.
Lower-case Greek letters represent scalars. For an spd matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the scaled 2-norm of an
n
-vector
p
is

\|p\|_{B}=(p^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Bp)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. The standard 2-norm is
\|p\|_{2}=\|p\|=(p^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}p)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

1.2
Sketching methods

Based on the idea that the next iterate

{x}_{k}
solves a sketched system, a class of methods can be derived in the form

\displaystyle{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A({x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k})

\displaystyle={S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}b

(3)

\displaystyle{x}_{k}

\displaystyle={x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k}

(4)

for
k=1,2,\dots,
where

S_{k}
is
m\times r
with
r\ll m
, and

{p}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the update. Note that

{S}_{k}
in
eq.

3
has index
k
and depends
on the iteration. To compute
p_{k}
, we can solve the sketched system
eq.

3

in the equivalent form

{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A{p}_{k}={S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}.

(5)

When the sketch is
{S}_{k}={e}_{i_{k}}
(one random
column of the identity), the minimum-norm solution of
eq.

3
and hence (
5
) is

{p}_{k}=\frac{{b}_{i_{k}}-{a}_{i_{k}*}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{x}_{k-1}}{\|{a}_{i_{k}*}\|^{2}_{2}}{a}_{i_{k}*}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

This update and the corresponding iterate
{x}_{k}={x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k}
is the popular
randomized Kaczmarz method (Strohmer and Vershynin
[
19
]
).
Note that for a symmetric full-rank square matrix
B
and
sketch
{S}_{k}={e}_{i_{k}}
, the update

{p}_{k}=\frac{{b}_{i_{k}}-{a}_{i_{k}*}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{x}_{k-1}}{\|{a}_{i_{k}*}\|^{2}_{B^{-1}}}B^{-1}{a}_{i_{k}*}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}

(6)

is also a valid solution of
eq.

5
. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, an update like
eq.

6
remains largely unexplored.
Importantly, there exist infinitely many updates like
eq.

6
because there exist arbitrarily
many
B
’s.

1.3
Contributions

In the context of sketch-and-project methods, this article develops judicious weights
B
, which we view as implicit preconditioning strategies. Instead of using
a uniform weight across all problems
Ax=b
(e.g.,
B=I
for all
problems) we consider weights for different types of linear system. For instance,
if
A
is symmetric we consider weighting by the matrix itself (i.e.,
B=A
).
Each specific choice of
B
results in a different method. When
A
is symmetric indefinite, we develop a new method that performs well compared to state-of-the-art random or deterministic methods. For general
A
, we develop
a
B
that results in a nested algorithm. Numerical experiments on relatively
large sparse systems demonstrate that the proposed method is effective compared to
GMRES
and alternative weightings.

2
PLSS

The Projected Linear Systems Solver (Brust and Saunders
[
6
]
) is a family of methods
that allows the use of deterministic or random sketches. The main assumptions about the sketch are its
size and rank at iteration
k
:

{S}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{s}_{1}&{s}_{2}&\cdots&{s}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k},\quad\textnormal{rank}({S}_{k})=k.

(7)

Arbitrary sketches (
7
) with updates computed from (
17
)
(see below) lead to an iteration
{x}_{k}={x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k}
that enjoys a finite termination property:
convergence to a solution of
Ax=b
in at most
\textnormal{min}(m,n)
iterations
in exact arithmetic.
This result differs from convergence for the expected difference

\mathbb{E}\|{x}_{k+1}-x^{*}\|_{B}\leq\rho\mathbb{E}\|{x}_{k}-x^{*}\|_{B}
with a rate
0\leq\rho<1
in
[
11
]
,
because it guarantees a solution in finitely many steps in exact arithmetic.
We emphasize that the finite termination property
also applies when the sketch is random. Note too that conditions (
7
) for the sketch
are general. In particular, the sketch can be generated each iteration from scratch as in conventional methods
[
11
,
16
]
,
where a random normal matrix is recomputed at each iteration. In these conventional implementations
the random normal sketches do not expand as
{S}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
but use a constant
subspace size
r>0,{S}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
. We believe this is the reason why the methods in
[
11
,
16
]

don’t have the finite termination property.

Instead of recomputing the sketch at every iteration, we have another practical possibility of expanding the sketch recursively:

{S}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{s}_{1}&{s}_{2}&\cdots&{s}_{k-1}&|&{s}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{S}_{k-1}&|&{s}_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

Of course, a direct implementation of expanding sketches results in growing memory and computational complexity.
However, for judicious sketch choices we can develop a very efficient recursion.

To use the sketch (
7
) in a practical method, note that the solution of
a consistent linear system
Ax=b
(square, overdetermined or underdetermined) can be computed via the iteration

x_{k}={x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,

(8)

where

\begin{bmatrix}B&A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{S}_{k}\\[3.0pt]
{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{p}_{k}\\[3.0pt]
{z}_{k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\[3.0pt]
{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(9)

Here,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is an arbitrary nonsingular symmetric matrix and
z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is an auxiliary vector that does not have to be explicitly computed.
System
eq.

9
has a unique solution when
A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}S_{k}
has full rank. It corresponds
to the first-order optimality conditions for the optimization problem (
17
) below.
(Linear equality constrained optimization is discussed in
[
5
,
4
]
.)
We emphasize that iteration
eq.

8
–
eq.

9
applies to general linear systems
Ax=b
. The iteration is parametrized by a symmetric
parameter matrix
B
and by the choice of sketching matrix
S_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
.

2.1
Update formulas

Initially assume that
B
has full rank, so that its inverse
W:=B^{-1}
exists.
Solving
eq.

9
gives the explicit formula

{p}_{k}=WA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{S}_{k}({S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}AWA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{S}_{k})^{-1}{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}.

(10)

This is the basis for straightforward
randomized solvers in which
{S}_{k}
is chosen as a random sketch.
For instance, the approaches of Gower and Richtárik
[
11
]
use random normal sketches

{S}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
with rank parameter
r>0
. Because these methods
need to solve with the
r\times r
matrix
{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}AWA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{S}_{k}
, typically
r
is
a relatively small integer. However, for small
r
the method can converge only slowly.

2.2
Finite termination

In contrast to using a fixed-size sketch, with sketches
eq.

7
we can prove finite termination for iteration
eq.

8
–
eq.

9
.
We emphasize that the sketch can be a random matrix, like a random normal Gaussian, or it could come
from a deterministic process. In the following we describe some of the intuition for proving finite termination. To keep matters simple we analyse the situation of a nonsingular square system. (Detailed results for square, rectangular and singular problems are in
[
6
]
.)
Suppose iteration
eq.

8
–
eq.

9

has not yet converged. This means that at iteration
k=n
, the sketch is a full-rank square
matrix
S_{k}=S_{n}
. Therefore, the update formula is

p_{n}=WA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}S_{n}(S_{n}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}AWA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}S_{n})^{-1}S_{n}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{n-1},\quad k=n.

By the assumption that both
S_{n}
and
A
are square, the update formula implies

p_{n}=A^{-1}r_{n-1}=A^{-1}(b-Ax_{n-1})

and hence

x_{n}=x_{n-1}+p_{n}=A^{-1}b.

Since
A^{-1}b
is the solution of (
1
) (for square
A
) we conclude that
the iteration converges in at most
n
iterations, independent of whether the sketch
is random or deterministic. The main assumption is that the sketch satisfies the rank
condition
eq.

7
. Finite termination is a useful and desirable property because it leads to efficient methods.

2.3
Sketch variations

To keep computational cost low, the original versions of
PLSS
consider mainly simple weights for
B
and its inverse
W
,
such as
B=I
or
B=\textnormal{diag}\big(\|a_{1}\|,\ldots,\|a_{n}\|\big)
.
On the other hand,
the sketch
S_{k}
in

is developed meticulously, typically by recursively expanding the previous sketch. Different choices for the columns in (
7

{s}_{k}=\begin{cases}\textnormal{randn}&(\textnormal{random normal})\\
{r}_{k-1}&(\textnormal{residuals})\\
e_{i_{k}}&(\textnormal{generalized Kaczmarz})\\
a_{i_{k}}&(\textnormal{generalized normal equations})\\
\end{cases}

(11)

Each of these variations results in a different method. However, the effects of the weighting matrix

remain largely unexplored.

2.4
Orthogonal updates

We note that when the product
{S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}r_{k-1}
is a multiple of

e_{k}
, say
{S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}r_{k-1}=\delta_{k}e_{k}
,
for some scalar
\delta_{k}
, then the updates
p_{k}
in (
10
)
are orthogonal with respect to the weighting matrix
W^{-1}=B
:

{p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{i}W^{-1}{p}_{j}={p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{i}B{p}_{j}=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq j\leq k

(12)

In order to see this orthogonality, suppose
E_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&\cdots&e_{k}\end{bmatrix}
is the matrix of identity columns, so that

S_{k-1}=S_{k}E_{k-1}
. From (
10
) this means that

p_{i}\in\text{Range}(WA^{T}S_{k}E_{i})
and therefore

(E^{T}_{i}{S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}AW)W^{-1}{p}_{k}={E}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{i}{S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}{r}_{k-1}=\delta_{k}{E}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{i}{e}_{k}=0,\quad 1\leq i<k.

In other words, the updates are orthogonal with respect to the inner product
defined by
W^{-1}
, so that (
12
) is valid.

2.5
PLSS residual

When the sketch consists of previous residuals, i.e.,
s_{i}={r}_{i-1}
for

i=1,2,\ldots,k
and
B
is spd, remarkably the update formula
(
10
) simplifies to a short one-step recurrence (we refer to this method as
PLSS
residual). Further, note that all residuals (with this sketch) are orthogonal.
In particular, the residuals satisfy

{r}_{k}=b-A{x}_{k}=b-A({x}_{k-1}+{p}_{k})={r}_{k-1}-A{p}_{k},

and therefore the second block row in (
9
) results in

0={S}_{k}^{T}(A{p}_{k}-{r}_{k-1})=-{S}_{k}^{T}{r}_{k}.

(13)

As
{S}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{r}_{0}\ldots{r}_{k-1}\end{bmatrix}
contains all previous
residuals, (
13
) implies orthogonality of all residuals. This is an
important property of PLSS with residual sketches. For instance, the product

{S}_{k}^{T}{r}_{k-1}
simplifies to
\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{2}_{2}{e}_{k-1}
.
Using this property and further simplifications, we can reduce the explicit formula in (
10
)
to a short recursion (
[
6
, Theorem 1]
):

{p_{k}}={\beta}_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}W{y}_{k},

(14)

where

\displaystyle{y}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}

\displaystyle{\theta}_{k-1}

\displaystyle={p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}W^{-1}{p}_{k-1}

\displaystyle{\omega}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle{\phi}_{k-1}

\displaystyle={y}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}W{y}_{k}

\displaystyle{\beta}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=1\big/\biggl(\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}-1\biggr)

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=1\big/\biggl(1-\frac{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}\biggr).

Method (
14
) is extremely efficient compared to
the full update formula (
10
), even though they are mathematically equivalent (modulo that
{S}_{k}
is based on past residuals).

2.6
PLSS Kaczmarz

A generalization of the randomized Kaczmarz method can be developed from

PLSS
when the sketching matrix is augmented with one random identity column
at each iteration:
{s}_{i_{j}}={e}_{i_{j}}
for

i_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. The sketch is then
{S}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{e}_{i_{1}}&\ldots&{e}_{i_{k}}\end{bmatrix}
and the second block row

{S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}{r}_{k-1}=({r}_{k-1})_{i_{k}}{e}_{k}

Using the definition

The generalized randomized Kaczmarz method is then given
(after simplification of (

where

\displaystyle{\eta}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{({r}_{k-1})_{i_{k}}}{a_{i_{k}*}{d}_{k-1}}

\displaystyle{d}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=W{y}_{k-1}-{P}_{k-1}G^{-1}_{k-1}P^{T}_{k-1}{y}_{k-1}

\displaystyle=A^{T}{r}_{k-1}.

3.1
Symmetric positive definite

When

\displaystyle\underset{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\textnormal{ minimize }}\quad\frac{1}{2}p^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Bp

\displaystyle\textnormal{ subject to }\>{S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Ap={S}_{k}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}.

For the solution

to be a global minimizer of (

3.1.1
Symmetric positive definite: Method 1

Since
p_{k}
in recurrence
eq.

14
is expressed in terms of

,
an immediate choice of weighting could be
B=A^{-1}
, so that

. The only difficulty
for implementing the recurrence in this case is computing the scalar

as it depends on
A^{-1}
(which is not available). Nevertheless, we can exploit the
short recurrence
eq.

14
to deduce a relation for
A^{-1}p_{k}
. Premultiplying

p_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{w}_{k-1}

and using the notation

With this relation a short recursive algorithm can be developed that does not require
any computations with
A^{-1}

it derives from the relations in

14

and ensure that all scalars in

Algorithm 1: PLSS (spd

)

Given:

{\beta}_{k-1}=1\big/\big(\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}-1\big)

A few notes about Algorithm 1.
First, it is designed for symmetric

and therefore
any occurrences of

can be replaced by

Second, it has low memory usage:
only 6 vectors of size

Third, it is computationally efficient, especially compared to randomized algorithms that compute the update via

16

Also, it possesses the finite termination property because the sketch consists of the history

3.1.2
Symmetric positive definite: Method 2

We can develop a second algorithm by choosing
B=A
so that
W=A^{-1}
. At first this
choice seems to make recursion
eq.

14
difficult to compute because
the vector

{w}_{k-1}=W{y}_{k-1}=A^{-1}{y}_{k-1}

is needed in the update. However, when
A
is symmetric we have

{w}_{k-1}=A^{-1}{y}_{k-1}=A^{-1}(A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1})={r}_{k-1}.

In other words, the computations with the inverse cancel out. The scalars can also
be computed directly:

{\theta}_{k-1}={p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}W^{-1}{p}_{k-1}={p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}{v}_{k-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad{\phi}_{k-1}={y}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}W{y}_{k-1}={r}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}{y}_{k-1},

where
v_{k}=Ap_{k}
. Because
p_{k}
satisfies

p_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{r}_{k-1},

v_{k}
can be found to follow the relation

v_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{v}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{y}_{k-1}.

The algorithm using this weighting follows.

Algorithm 2: PLSS (spd
B=A
)

Given:
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0};\quad y_{0}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{0};

\omega_{0}=\|r_{0}\|^{2};\quad\phi_{0}=y_{0}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{0};\quad p_{1}=(\omega_{0}/\phi_{0})r_{0};\quad v_{1}=Ap_{1}

x_{1}=x_{0}+p_{1};\quad r_{1}=r_{0}-v_{1};\quad y_{1}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{1};

for
k=2,3,\dots,n

{\theta}_{k-1}={p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}{v}_{k-1};\quad{\phi}_{k-1}={y}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}{r}_{k-1}

{\omega}_{k-1}=\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{2}

{\beta}_{k-1}=1\big/\big(\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}-1\big)

{\gamma}_{k-1}=\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}}{\beta}_{k-1}

v_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{v}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{y}_{k-1}

p_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{r}_{k-1}

x_{k}={x}_{k-1}+p_{k}

r_{k}={r}_{k-1}-v_{k}

y_{k}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{k}

end

As before,
A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
can be replaced by
A
. The memory footprint is low
(storing and updating only five vectors), and importantly, the algorithm is very efficient: each iteration needs only
one
matvec operation,
{y}_{k}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{k}
.
Again the method enjoys finite termination. Finally, the residuals generated by this algorithm are equivalent to those from CG
[
12
]
.

3.2
Least squares

When problem
eq.

1
is overdetermined with
m>n
, the system
is typically inconsistent. To find the least-squares solution we have to solve

A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Ax=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}b
.
A simple and often effective approach is to apply Algorithm 2, i.e.,
PLSS (
W=\widehat{A}
)

with
\widehat{A}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A
and
\widehat{b}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}b
. Recall that Algorithm 2 uses
only one matvec with
\widehat{A}
per iteration, which can be implemented as two products
with
A
. In particular, the product
\widehat{A}r
(for some
r\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) is
implemented as
\widehat{A}r=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}(Ar)
.

3.3
Symmetric indefinite

When
B
is symmetric but indefinite, the block system
eq.

9
still
characterizes the critical points of an optimization problem. However, the objective

\frac{1}{2}p^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}Bp
is not a norm anymore because it can assume negative values.
Therefore, the solution to
eq.

9
is typically not the minimizer.
(This is good because the minimizer is typically unbounded.) Broadly,
p_{k}
in
this context is related to a saddle point in a quadratic programming problem.
For an example, one can compare the updates generated with an indefinite weighting
B

in
fig.

1
. Independent of everything else, as long as the sketch remains
full-rank, finite termination is maintained (even with indefinite
B
).
Because
p_{k}
can’t be interpreted as a minimizer in the indefinite case, we develop
the update using an analogy to spd
B
.
We view
B
as an
implicit
preconditioner with the main purpose of
simplifying computations. Recall the short recurrence
eq.

14
for the update with residual sketches (independent of the definiteness of
B
).
Since

W{y}_{k-1}=WA^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}{r}_{k-1}=WA{r}_{k-1},

a significant simplification occurs when
W=A^{-1}
so that
W{y}_{k-1}={r}_{k-1}
.
This means we choose
B=A
, even if
A
is indefinite. The result is an algorithm
that is computationally equivalent to Algorithm 2. We summarize
the method as follows.

Algorithm 3: PLSS (sid
B=A
)

Given:
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0};\quad y_{0}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{0}

Apply Algorithm 2

Note that Algorithm 2 (and hence Algorithm 3) does not require any square roots,
and thus there is no concern about square roots of negative
quantities in the indefinite case causing breakdown. Since
\omega_{k-1}=0
appears in the denominator,
we potentially have to guard for this value becoming too small.
However,
\omega_{k-1}=0
implies
{r}_{k-1}=0
and therefore this quantity is
small only when the method has converged. Further, the denominator in
\beta_{k-1}
must be
guarded from becoming zero. With
W=A^{-1}
in
\beta_{k-1}
from Algorithm 2
(and the notation
eq.

16
) we see that

\beta_{k-1}=\frac{1}{\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}-1}=\frac{1}{\frac{({p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}A{p}_{k-1})({r}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}A{r}_{k-1})}{\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{4}}-1}.

The denominator is zero if

({p}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}A{p}_{k-1})({r}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k-1}A{r}_{k-1})=\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{4}
.

A possible remedy is to restart the method from
{x}_{k-1}
should this condition arise.
(In the numerical experiments we don’t observe breakdowns from this condition.)
Algorithm 3 is efficient because it uses only one matvec per iteration.
It is equivalent to CG but applicable to symmetric indefinite systems, whereas CG is
typically designed for spd matrices. In
section

4.1
(Experiment I: Indefinite Systems) we observe that
PLSS (
B=A
)
(Algorithm 3) is robust and fast (see
tables

2
and
3
).

3.4
General square systems

When
A
is square, we can’t directly apply the strategies for
B
from
sections

3.1
and
3.3
. Specifically, it is not possible to choose
B=A
or

B=A^{-1}
as before because
A
is not necessarily symmetric. It is fine to set
B=I
,
which results in the original
PLSS
algorithm. Another approach is to consider the
symmetric weighting
B=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A
. Substituting
W=B^{-1}
in the update
eq.

8

(with residual sketches), we obtain

{p}_{k}=A^{-1}{S}_{k}({S}^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{k}{S}_{k})^{-1}{S}_{k}{r}_{k-1}=A^{-1}{r}_{k-1}.

The main difficulty is evaluating
A^{-1}{r}_{k-1}
. In fact, if
A^{-1}r_{0}
could
be computed exactly the method based on this choice for
B
would converge in one
iteration:

p_{1}=A^{-1}r_{0}=A^{-1}b-x_{0},\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{1}=x_{0}+p_{1}=A^{-1}b.

However,
A^{-1}{r}_{k-1}
is typically not computed exactly. A possibility is to apply

PLSS
(
B=I
) as a subproblem solver inside an outer loop in order to evaluate

A^{-1}{r}_{k-1}
approximately. This approach has two nested loops and we don’t want the inner loop
to take many iterations. Therefore, we include a second stopping tolerance

\epsilon_{k}=\|r_{k}\|\big/k
to terminate the inner loop early. The resulting Algorithm 4
uses
PLSS
(
B=I
) represented as the function in
fig.

2

with its own stopping tolerance
\epsilon_{0}
.

function
plss(
x_{0},b,A,\epsilon
)

r_{0}=b-Ax_{0};\quad y_{0}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{0};

\omega_{0}=\|r_{0}\|^{2};\quad\phi_{0}=\|y_{0}\|^{2};\quad p_{1}=(\omega_{0}/\phi_{0})y_{0};\quad v_{1}=Ap_{1}

x_{1}=x_{0}+p_{1};\quad r_{1}=r_{0}-v_{1};\quad y_{1}=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}r_{1};k=2;

while
\|{r}_{k-1}\|>\epsilon

{\theta}_{k-1}=\|{p}_{k-1}\|^{2};\quad{\phi}_{k-1}=\|{y}_{k-1}\|^{2}

{\omega}_{k-1}=\|{r}_{k-1}\|^{2}

{\beta}_{k-1}=1\big/\big(\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}{\phi}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}{\omega}_{k-1}}-1\big)

{\gamma}_{k-1}=\frac{{\theta}_{k-1}}{{\omega}_{k-1}}{\beta}_{k-1}

p_{k}={\beta}_{k-1}{p}_{k-1}+{\gamma}_{k-1}{r}_{k-1}

v_{k}=Ap_{k}

end

A few remarks about Algorithm 4. First, inside the loop, plss is called with an initial guess
of
\frac{8}{10}{p}_{k-1}
. This is not essential, and the zero
starting point is a valid option. However, when the residuals don’t change rapidly for every iteration it can be advantageous to use information from the previous solution. The tolerances

\epsilon_{k}=\frac{\|{r}_{k-1}\|}{k-1}
are only one implementation that force increasingly
accurate solutions. Other sequences are possible. Further, if
PLSS
(
B=I
) could solve
the initial system
Ap_{1}=r_{0}
to full accuracy, Algorithm 4 would only need one iteration.
However, as this is unrealistic, the algorithm will typically use multiple iterations
and its convergence depends on how well the subproblem solver approximates

Ap_{k}={r}_{k-1}
. When matvec products with
A
can be computed cheaply, the overall
approach in Algorithm 4 can be useful. Numerical experiments for general square
A
are in

section

4.2
.

Experiment III

This experiment tests Algorithm 4 on a difficult unsymmetric matrix (
West0479
)
[
8
]

for iterative solvers, when no preconditioner is used. The matrix comes from a
chemical engineering process via A. Westerberg. Even though the system is small
(
n=479
), the condition number is large:
\kappa(A)\approx 10^{12}
.

For comparison,
we include three random normal solvers with
r=50,100,150
. We run Algorithm 4 with inner iterations limit
10n
. The convergence tolerance for
all methods is
\epsilon=10^{-6}
. As Algorithm 4 has cheap inner and costly outer
iterations, a comparison based on iterations only may not be informative.
Instead we compare computation times.
Figure

5
shows
the outcomes. We see that Rand
(r=150)
and Algorithm 4 (
PLSS
with
B=A^{\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}A
)
perform well. Importantly, Algorithm 4 scales to larger problems because the subproblem
solver has low inner iterations complexity. Every iteration of the randomized algorithm needs
O(r^{3})
operations.

5
Conclusion

Structured sketch-and-project methods for linear systems have been developed here.
The methods are characterized by a finite termination property for both
random and deterministic sketches. When the history of
past residuals forms the sketch, we exploit a short recurrence to develop
effective weighting schemes. The techniques enable us to incorporate
information from the linear system to obtain an implicit preconditioning.
In numerical experiments on large sparse problems, the
proposed methods compare well to state-of-the-art deterministic and random solvers.
