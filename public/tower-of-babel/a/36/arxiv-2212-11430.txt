Abstract
. We are concerned with the minimal entropy conditions for one-dimensional scalar conservation laws with general convex flux functions. For such scalar conservation laws, we prove that a single entropy-entropy flux pair ( η ⁡ ( u ) , q ⁡ ( u ) ) (\eta(u),q(u)) with η ⁡ ( u ) \eta(u) of strict convexity is sufficient to single out an entropy solution from a broad class of weak solutions in L loc ∞ L^{\infty}_{\rm loc} that satisfy the inequality: η ​ ( u ) t + q ​ ( u ) x ≤ μ \eta(u)_{t}+q(u)_{x}\leq\mu in the distributional sense for some non-negative Radon measure μ \mu . Furthermore, we extend this result to the class of weak solutions in L loc p L^{p}_{\rm loc} , based on the asymptotic behavior of the flux function f ⁡ ( u ) f(u) and the entropy function η ⁡ ( u ) \eta(u) at infinity. The proofs are based on the equivalence between the entropy solutions of one-dimensional scalar conservation laws and the viscosity solutions of the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi equations, as well as the bilinear form and commutator estimates as employed similarly in the theory of compensated compactness.

1.
Introduction

We are concerned with the minimal entropy conditions for one-dimensional scalar conservation laws with general convex flux functions:

\displaystyle u_{t}+f(u)_{x}=0\qquad\,\,\mbox{for $(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}:=(0,\infty)\times(-\infty,\infty)$},

(1.1)

\displaystyle u|_{t=0}=u_{0}(x),

(1.2)

for the initial data function
u_{0}\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}
for
p\geq 1
.

It is well known that, due to the nonlinearity of the flux function
f(u)
, no matter how smooth the initial data function
u_{0}(x)
is,
the solution may form shock waves generically in a finite time.
Thus, the solution should be understood in a weak sense, which means that the solution as a function with suitable integrability
solves equation (
1.1
) in the distributional sense.
In general, the weak solutions are not unique, so we need entropy conditions to characterize the unique entropy solution among the weak solutions.

As shown by Oleinik
[
18
]
, for equation (
1.1
) with uniformly convex flux function
f(u)\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
, the entropy condition, so called Condition (E),
is sufficient to single out the unique weak solution (physically relevant) among all possible weak solutions.
Condition (E) is the Oleinik’s one-sided inequality for the entropy solution
u(t,x)
:

\frac{u(t,x_{2})-u(t,x_{1})}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\leq\frac{1}{ct}\qquad\mbox{for\ any\ $x_{2}>x_{1}$\ and\ $t>0$},

(1.3)

where
c:=\inf\{f^{\prime\prime}(u):u\in{\mathbb{R}}\}>0
.
Condition
(
1.3
)
implies the regularizing effect that the initial data function in
L^{\infty}

are regularized to
BV_{\rm loc}
instantaneously for the solutions.
This condition also yields many fine properties such as the regularity, the decay rates, and the convergence of approximation schemes, among others, for the solutions; see Dafermos
[
9
]
and Lax
[
16
]
.

For one-dimensional scalar conservation laws with general flux functions, not necessarily convex (even for the multi-dimensional case), a general method for enforcing the uniqueness of solutions in
L^{\infty}
was established by Kruzkov
[
14
]
, by following the earlier results for solutions in
BV_{\rm loc}
by Conway-Smoller
[
6
]
and Vol
′
pert
[
21
]
.
Besides the existence of weak solutions in
L^{\infty}
, Kruzkov also proved the uniqueness by the so-called Kruzkov’s entropy condition:
An entropy solution is a weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}
satisfying that

\eta(u)_{t}+q(u)_{x}\leq 0\qquad\,\,\mbox{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}$}

(1.4)

for any
(\eta(u),q(u))\in\{(\eta_{k}(u),q_{k}(u))\}_{k\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, the family of which is defined by

\eta_{k}(u):=|u-k|,\quad\,\,q_{k}(u):={\rm sgn}(u-k)\big(f(u)-f(k)\big).

It is equivalent to saying that
(
1.4
)
holds for all entropy-entropy flux pair
(\eta(u),q(u))
with convex entropy function
\eta(u)
and

q^{\prime}(u)=\eta^{\prime}(u)f^{\prime}(u)\qquad\,\,{a.e.}\,\,u\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.5)

When the flux function is uniformly convex, the two entropy conditions
(
1.3
)
and
(
1.4
)
are equivalent,
which implies that the entropy solutions characterized by Oleinik’s condition (E) coincide with the Kruzkov entropy solutions.
In 1989, Kruzkov
[
2
]
posed an important open question on whether only one single convex entropy
\eta(u)
satisfying
(
1.4
)

can enforce the uniqueness of the solution, which is called the
Minimal Entropy Condition
in De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
.
In view of the lack of convex entropy functions for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, the question of
Minimal Entropy Conditions
becomes important for the mathematical theory of hyperbolic conservation laws.

Panov
[
19
]
first gave a positive answer to this question by proving that the weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}
satisfying
(
1.1
)
and
(
1.4
)
, with a flux function
f(u)
and a single entropy function
\eta(u)
that are both uniformly convex, is the unique entropy solution in Oleinik’s sense, or equivalently Kruzkov’s sense.
This result was also proved by De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
and Krupa-Vasseur
[
13
]
.
In De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
, they further proved that, for the Burgers flux:
f(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
and the special convex entropy
\eta(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
,
the weak solution
u\in L^{4}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
satisfying the
Minimal Entropy Condition
:

\big(\frac{1}{2}u^{2}\big)_{t}+\big(\frac{1}{3}u^{3}\big)_{x}\leq\mu\qquad\mbox{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)$},

(1.6)

for some non-negative Radon measure
\mu
with
\lim_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(t,x))}{r}=0
for each
(t,x)\in\Omega
, must be the entropy solution of the Burgers equation,
where
B_{r}(t,x)
denotes the disk with center
(t,x)
and radius
r>0
.
For the Kruzkov-type estimates via the entropy inequalities bounded by Radon measures; see Bouchut-Perthame
[
3
]
.

One of the motivations of this paper is that the uniform convexity is a strong restriction on both the flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
, since most of convex functions
g(u)
such as those with
g^{\prime\prime}(u)=0
possessing only isolated roots and/or with an asymptotic line, saying
g(u)=|u|^{\alpha}
with
\alpha>2
and
g(u)=e^{ku}
with
k\neq 0
, are not uniformly convex.
Another motivation is based on the conjecture by De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
that the result for the Burgers equation with the minimal entropy condition (
1.6
)
could be generalized to allow for different strictly convex flux functions and entropy functions.

The purpose of this paper is to give a positive answer to the question of
Minimal Entropy Conditions
by generalizing the previous results in
[
19
,
15
]
in three aspects:

(i)

The flux function
f(u)
is required to be only convex, which allows itself to be linear degeneracy in general.

(ii)

The minimal entropy condition similar to
(
1.6
)
is generalized to be as follows:
There exists a strictly convex entropy function
\eta(u)
such that

\eta(u)_{t}+q(u)_{x}\leq\mu\qquad\mbox{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)$}

(1.7)

for some non-negative Radon measure
\mu
satisfying that

\mathop{\underline{\lim}}\limits_{r\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{r}\mu(B_{r}(t,x))=0\qquad\mbox{for every $(t,x)\in\Omega$}.

(1.8)

(iii)

The weak solution
u
is required to be only in
L^{p}_{\rm loc}
for
p\geq p_{0}
, instead of
u\in L^{\infty}
or
u\in L^{4}_{\rm loc}
,
with
p_{0}\geq 1
determined by the asymptotic behavior of the flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
as
u\rightarrow\pm\infty
.

As well known in convex analysis (
cf.

[
11
]
), a function
g(u)
is called convex if, for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
,

g(\lambda u_{1}+(1-\lambda)u_{2})\leq\lambda g(u_{1})+(1-\lambda)g(u_{2})\qquad\mbox{for any $\lambda\in[0,1]$},

or equivalent to saying that
g(u)
is locally Lipschitz continuous and

g^{\prime}(u)\,\,\,\,~~{\rm is~nondecreasing~on}~{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.9)

Furthermore, a function
g(u)
is called strictly convex if, for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
with
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
,

g(\lambda u_{1}+(1-\lambda)u_{2})<\lambda g(u_{1})+(1-\lambda)g(u_{2})\qquad\mbox{for any $\lambda\in(0,1)$},

or equivalent to saying that
g(u)
is locally Lipschitz continuous and

g^{\prime}(u)\,\,\,\,~~{\rm is~strictly~increasing~on}~{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.10)

Notice that the strict convexity condition
(
1.10
)
for the entropy function
\eta(u)
is equivalent to saying that
\eta(u)
is convex and satisfies that there is no interval in
\mathbb{R}
in which
\eta(u)
is affine, which is a generalized notion of classical genuine nonlinearity.
In general, such an entropy function
\eta(u)
allows itself to be not locally uniformly convex, even to possess a measure-zero degenerate set
\{u\,:\,\eta^{\prime\prime}(u)=0\}
for the case that
\eta(u)\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Furthermore, condition
(
1.10
)
on the entropy function
\eta(u)
is optimal; otherwise, if
\eta(u)
is linearly degenerate on some interval
[a,b]
,
then, for
u_{0}(x)
ranged in
[a,b]
, inequality
(
1.7
)
does not provide further restrictions on the weak solutions, which can not enforce the uniqueness.
Moreover, the convexity condition
(
1.9
)
on the flux function
f(u)
is necessary in general since, for the case of the flux function without convexity,
a single strictly convex entropy function
\eta(u)
for
(
1.7
)
is not sufficient to enforce the uniqueness; see Remark
2.11
.
In addition, condition
(
1.8
)
on the non-negative Radon measure
\mu
is optimal; see Remark
2.10
below.

As in Panov
[
19
]
and De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
, our proofs are also based on the relation between the one-dimensional scalar conservation laws
(
1.1
)
and the corresponding Hamilton-Jacobi equations:

w_{t}+f(w_{x})=0\qquad\,\,\,\mbox{for $(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}$}.

(1.11)

Formally, (
1.1
) and (
1.11
) would be equivalent via the relation:
w_{x}=u
.
It follows from the existence and uniqueness theory for viscosity solutions of
(
1.11
)
, introduced first by Crandall-Lions in
[
7
]
(also see Lions
[
17
]
), that the continuous function
w
is a viscosity solution of
(
1.11
)
if and only if
u=w_{x}
is an entropy solution of
(
1.1
)
.
In particular, it can be shown that, if
w
is a viscosity solution of
(
1.11
)
, which can be obtained by the vanishing viscosity method,
then
u=w_{x}
is an entropy solution of
(
1.1
)
.
In this paper, we prove that the weak solution
u
satisfying the minimal entropy condition
(
1.7
)
for a single strictly convex entropy function
\eta(u)

implies that
w
, with
w_{x}=u
, is actually a viscosity solution of
(
1.11
)
,
which yields that
u
must be an entropy solution satisfying
the entropy inequality
(
1.4
)
.
Our analysis is highly motivated by the arguments in De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
(also see Ambrosio-Lecumberry-Rivière
[
1
]
);
in particular, the proofs are based on the bilinear form and commutator estimates, for which similar arguments have been employed
in the theory of compensated compactness (
cf.

[
5
,
20
]
).

This paper is organized as follows:
In §2, we first introduce some basic concepts and then present the main theorems of this paper.
In §3, we prove several lemmas on the averages of functions and properties of linear degeneracy of convex functions for the subsequent development.
In §4, we complete the proof of Theorem
2.3
for the case that
u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
.
In §5, we prove Theorem
2.4
for the case that
u\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}
, based on the asymptotic behavior of the flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
as
u\to\pm\infty
.

2.
Basic Notions and Main Theorems

In this section, we first present the notion of entropy solutions of scalar conservation laws (
1.1
) and the notion of viscosity solutions of Hamilton-Jacobi equations (
1.11
), respectively.
Then we state the main theorems of this paper and make several related remarks.

Definition 2.1

(Entropy Solutions)
.

Let
f(u)\in{\rm Lip}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
.
A function
u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
is called an entropy solution of the scalar conservation law
(
1.1
)
if, for any convex entropy-entropy flux pair
(\eta(u),q(u))\in{\rm Lip}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfying
q^{\prime}(u)=\eta^{\prime}(u)f^{\prime}(u)
almost everywhere,
u
satisfies
(
1.1
)
in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and

\eta(u)_{t}+q(u)_{x}\leq 0\qquad\mbox{ in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)$}.

(2.1)

Definition 2.2

(Viscosity Solutions)
.

A function
w\in C(\Omega)
with
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}
is called a viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
(
1.11
)
if
w
satisfies that

(i)

For any
C^{1}
–function
\varphi
such that
w-\varphi
has a local maximum at some point
(t,x)
,

\varphi_{t}(t,x)+f(\varphi_{x}(t,x))\leq 0{\rm;}

(2.2)

(ii)

For any
C^{1}
–function
\varphi
such that
w-\varphi
has a local minimum at some point
(t,x)
,

\varphi_{t}(t,x)+f(\varphi_{x}(t,x))\geq 0.

(2.3)

A function
w
is called a viscosity subsolution (
resp
., supersolution)
if (i) (
resp
., (ii)) holds.

Our first main theorem is for the case:
u\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
.

(2.4)

\eta(u)\in{\rm Lip}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})

as in
(
1.10
)

\mu

(
1.8
)
.
Then the locally Lipschitz function
w=w(t,x)
with

and

is a viscosity solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi
equation
(
1.11
)
,

(
1.1
)
in
\Omega
.

For the case that
u\in L^{p}_{\rm loc}
with
p\geq 1
, we need the asymptotic
behavior of the flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
as
u\to\pm\infty
.
More precisely, we need the flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
to satisfy:

(i)

The flux function
f(u)
and the entropy function
\eta(u)
both grow at least linearly as

u\to\pm\infty
,

i.e.
,

\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{f(u)}{|u|}\geq M_{1}>0,\quad\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{\eta(u)}{|u|^{\beta+1}}\geq M_{2}>0\qquad{\rm for\ some\ }\beta\geq 0;

(2.5)

(ii)

The quadratic form
Q(u):=uq(u)-f(u)\eta(u)
grows faster than the entropy flux function
q(u):=\int_{0}^{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi
,
i.e.
,

\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{Q(u)}{|q(u)|}=\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{uq(u)-f(u)\eta(u)}{|q(u)|}=\infty;

(2.6)

(iii)

The entropy function
\eta(u)
grows not
too slow with respect to
the flux function
f(u)
,
i.e.
, there exist some constants
\gamma\geq 1
and
C>0
such that

\frac{Q(u)}{\eta(u)}\leq C\Big(\frac{Q(u)}{f(u)}\Big)^{\gamma}\qquad\,\,\mbox{\rm as $u\rightarrow\pm\infty$}.

(2.7)

Remark 2.5
.

Notice that, although condition
(
2.5
)
admits the flux function
f(u)

with linear growth rate,
f(u)
can not be a linear function for large
|u|
,
in which case
condition
(
2.6
)
does not hold.
Condition
(
2.7
)
means that the entropy function
\eta(u)
, used to enforce the uniqueness,
should be able to control the growth of the flux function
f(u)
as
u\rightarrow\pm\infty
.
In particular, under conditions
(
2.5
)
–
(
2.6
)
, it is direct to check that
(
2.7
)
holds for
\gamma=\max\{\lambda,1\}
if

|f^{\prime}(u)|\leq C|\eta^{\prime}(u)|^{\lambda}\qquad\,\,\mbox{\rm as $u\rightarrow\pm\infty$}

(2.8)

for some
\lambda\geq 0
and sufficiently large
C>0
. In fact, without loss of generality, consider the case that
u\rightarrow\infty
. Then there exist two cases:

{\rm(i)}
If
\lambda\in[0,1]
,
(
2.5
)
implies that
f^{\prime}(u)\lesssim\eta^{\prime}(u)
so that
f(u)\lesssim\eta(u)
. Then
(
2.7
)
holds for
\gamma=1
;

{\rm(ii)}
If
\lambda>1
,
it follows from
(
2.8
)
that

f^{\prime}(u)=f^{\prime}(u)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}f^{\prime}(u)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}\lesssim\eta^{\prime}(u)\,f^{\prime}(u)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}=\eta^{\prime}(u)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\big(\eta^{\prime}(u)f^{\prime}(u)\big)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}},

(2.9)

which, by the Hölder inequality, implies that

\displaystyle f(u)=\int_{0}^{u}f^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\big(\eta^{\prime}(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)\big)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi\Big)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\Big(\int_{0}^{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi\Big)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=(\eta(u)-\eta(0))^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}q(u)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\simeq\eta(u)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}q(u)^{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}}.

(2.10)

This, by
(
2.6
)
, infers that

f(u)^{\lambda}\lesssim\eta(u)q(u)^{\lambda-1}\leq\eta(u)Q(u)^{\lambda-1},

(2.11)

which implies that
(
2.7
)
holds for
\gamma=\lambda
.

As shown above, the reason to require
\gamma\geq 1
is that, since
(
2.5
)
and
(
2.6
)

imply that
\lim_{u\rightarrow\pm\infty}\frac{Q(u)}{f(u)}=\infty
,

(
2.7
)
also holds for
\gamma=1
if it holds for some
\gamma\in[0,1)
.

.

To understand conditions (

)–(

)

(2.12)

(i)

Furthermore, if

Remark 2.11
.

Our proofs depend highly on the convexity of the flux functions

,

i.e.
,

is nondecreasing.
As shown in Dafermos
[

itself.
For the case of the flux function

may not be sufficient to enforce the uniqueness; instead of which the minimal number

As mentioned above,
our proofs are based on the equivalence between the entropy solutions

)

)

)

)

3.
Averages of Functions and Properties of Linear Degeneracy for Convex Functions

In order to prove the main theorems,
we need Proposition
3.2
below on the averages of functions,
which generalizes Proposition 3.2 in De Lellis-Otto-Westdickenberg
[
15
]
,
and Proposition
3.4
on the properties of linear degeneracy for general convex functions.

Definition 3.1

(Averages of Functions)
.

Assume that
\mu
is a probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
.
For every vector-valued map
h\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}},\mu)
, set

\langle h(u)\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}h(u)\,{\rm d}\mu(u).

(3.1)

Let
f(u),\eta(u)\in W^{1,\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})

and
q(u):=\int^{u}_{0}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi
.
When
\mu
is compactly supported, we define the bilinear form:

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)&:=\langle(-f(u),u)\cdot(\eta(u),q(u))\rangle-\langle(-f(u),u)\rangle\cdot\langle(\eta(u),q(u))\rangle\\
&\,=\langle uq(u)\rangle-\langle u\rangle\langle q(u)\rangle-\big(\langle\eta(u)f(u)\rangle-\langle\eta(u)\rangle\langle f(u)\rangle\big).\end{split}

(3.2)

When
\mu
is of noncompact support, we define
\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)
whenever the
functions in
(
3.2
)
are all
\mu-
summable.

Then we have the following properties of the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)
in
(
3.2
)
:

Proposition 3.2

(Bilinear Form)
.

Let
f(u),\eta(u)\in W^{1,\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}})
and
q(u):=\int^{u}_{0}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)f^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi
.

(i)

If
\bar{f}(u)=f(u)-(au+b)
and
\bar{\eta}(u)=\eta(u)-(cu+d)

for constants
a,b,c
, and
d
, then

\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)=\mathcal{B}(\bar{f},\eta)=\mathcal{B}(f,\bar{\eta})=\mathcal{B}(\bar{f},\bar{\eta}).

(3.3)

(ii)

If
f(u)
and
\eta(u)
are both convex,
then

\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)\geq\langle\eta(u)-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\rangle\langle f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\rangle\geq 0.

(3.4)

Proof.

We now give the proof for the two cases respectively.

(i) Since
\bar{f}(u)=f(u)-(au+b)
,

\bar{q}(u)=\int^{u}_{0}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\bar{f}^{\prime}(\xi)\,{\rm d}\xi=\int^{u}_{0}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-a\big)\,{\rm d}\xi=q(u)-a\eta(u)+a\eta(0).

(3.5)

Then, by the definition of
\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)
in
(
3.2
)
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(\bar{f}(u)+au+b,\eta(u))

\displaystyle=\langle u(\bar{q}(u)+a\eta(u)-a\eta(0))\rangle-\langle u\rangle\langle\bar{q}(u)+a\eta(u)-a\eta(0)\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\langle\eta(u)(\bar{f}(u)+au+b)\rangle+\langle\bar{f}(u)+au+b\rangle\langle\eta(u)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u\bar{q}(u)\rangle-\langle u\rangle\langle\bar{q}(u)\rangle-\langle\eta(u)\bar{f}(u)\rangle+\langle\bar{f}(u)\rangle\langle\eta(u)\rangle=\,\mathcal{B}(\bar{f},\eta).

Similarly, for
\bar{\eta}(u)=\eta(u)-(cu+d)
, we have

\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)=\mathcal{B}(f,\bar{\eta})=\mathcal{B}(\bar{f},\bar{\eta}).

(ii) By the definition of
\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)
in
(
3.2
)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)

\displaystyle=\big\langle(u-\langle u\rangle)q(u)\big\rangle-\big\langle\big(\eta(u)-\langle\eta(u)\rangle\big)\big(f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle(u-\langle u\rangle)q(u)\big\rangle-\big\langle\big(\eta(u)-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big(f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\big\langle\big(\langle\eta(u)\rangle-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big(f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle(u-\langle u\rangle)q(u)-\big(\eta(u)-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big(f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\big\langle\eta(u)-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\big\rangle\big\langle f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=:\langle P(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle+\langle Q(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle,

(3.6)

For
\langle P(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle
,
P(u,\langle u\rangle)=0
when
u=\langle u\rangle
.
When
u\neq\langle u\rangle
, by the nondecreasing of
f^{\prime}(u)
,
there exists at least
one
\zeta
such that

c(\zeta):=\frac{f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)}{u-\langle u\rangle}\in[f^{\prime}(\zeta{-0}),f^{\prime}(\zeta{+0})].

Since
f^{\prime}(u)
and
\eta^{\prime}(u)
are both nondecreasing,
we see that,
if
u>\langle u\rangle
, then

\displaystyle P(u,\langle u\rangle)

\displaystyle=(u-\langle u\rangle)\int^{u}_{\langle u\rangle}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-c(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=(u-\langle u\rangle)\Big(\int^{u}_{\zeta}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-c(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi-\int^{\zeta}_{\langle u\rangle}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\big(c(\zeta)-f^{\prime}(\xi)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi\Big)

\displaystyle\geq(u-\langle u\rangle)\Big(\int^{u}_{\zeta}d(\zeta)\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-c(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi-\int^{\zeta}_{\langle u\rangle}d(\zeta)\big(c(\zeta)-f^{\prime}(\xi)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi\Big)

\displaystyle=(u-\langle u\rangle)d(\zeta)\int^{u}_{\langle u\rangle}\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-c(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=0,

(3.7)

where
d(\zeta)\in[\eta^{\prime}(\zeta{-0}),\eta^{\prime}(\zeta{+0})]
. Similarly, if
u<\langle u\rangle
, then

P(u,\langle u\rangle)=\big(\langle u\rangle-u\big)\int^{\langle u\rangle}_{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\big(f^{\prime}(\xi)-c(\zeta)\big)\,{\rm d}\xi\geq 0.

(3.8)

Thus, we conclude that
P(u,\langle u\rangle)\geq 0
so that

\langle P(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle\geq 0.

(3.9)

For
\langle Q(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle
, since
f(u)
and
\eta(u)
are both convex, we use the Jensen inequality
to see that

\langle f(u)\rangle-f(\langle u\rangle)\geq 0,\qquad\langle\eta(u)\rangle-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\geq 0.

(3.10)

Combining
(
3
)
–
(
3.10
)
together, we conclude that

\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)=\langle P(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle+\langle Q(u,\langle u\rangle)\rangle\geq\langle\eta(u)-\eta(\langle u\rangle)\rangle\langle f(u)-f(\langle u\rangle)\rangle\geq 0,

as desired.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

If
f(u),\eta(u)\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
are both uniformly convex with
c_{1}:=\inf f^{\prime\prime}(u)>0
and
c_{2}:=\inf\eta^{\prime\prime}(u)>0
, in view of
(
3.3
)
, choose
\bar{f}(u)
and
\bar{\eta}(u)
such that

\bar{f}(\langle u\rangle)=\bar{f}^{\prime}(\langle u\rangle)=0
and
\bar{\eta}(\langle u\rangle)=\bar{\eta}^{\prime}(\langle u\rangle)=0
. By a simple calculation, we have

\mathcal{B}(f,\eta)=\mathcal{B}(\bar{f},\bar{\eta})\geq\langle\bar{\eta}(u)\rangle\langle\bar{f}(u)\rangle\geq\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{4}\langle|u-\langle u\rangle|^{2}\rangle^{2}\geq\frac{c_{1}c_{2}}{4}\langle|u-\langle u\rangle|\rangle^{4}.

(3.11)

Finally, we give some properties of a general convex function
f(u)\in{\rm Lip}_{{\rm loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
.

Proposition 3.4

(Linear Degeneracy)
.

Let
f(u)\in{\rm Lip}_{{\rm loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
be convex,
and let the point sets
I^{\pm}(u)
be defined by

I^{\pm}(u):=\big\{v\in\bar{{\mathbb{R}}}\,:\,f(v)-f(u)-f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})(v-u)=0\big\}\qquad\,\,\mbox{\rm for any $u\in{\mathbb{R}}$},

(3.12)

where
\bar{{\mathbb{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
. Then

(i)

I^{\pm}(u)
is a single point set or a closed interval in
\bar{{\mathbb{R}}}
given by

I^{\pm}(u)=[\inf I^{\pm}(u),\ \sup I^{\pm}(u)].

(3.13)

In particular, if
f^{\prime}(u)
is strictly increasing, then
I^{\pm}(u)=\{u\}
for any
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(ii)

The first derivative of function
f(u)
satisfies that

\begin{cases}f^{\prime}(v)<f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})\qquad{\rm for\ }v<\inf I^{\pm}(u),\\[2.84526pt]
f^{\prime}(v)=f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})\qquad{\rm for\ }v\in I^{\pm}(u),\\[2.84526pt]
f^{\prime}(v)>f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})\qquad{\rm for\ }v>\sup I^{\pm}(u).\end{cases}

(3.14)

Furthermore, if
I^{\pm}(u)
is a closed interval, then
f(u)

is linearly degenerate on
I^{\pm}(u)
.

Proof.

We prove the two properties in two steps, respectively.

(i) First, it follows from
(
3.12
)
that
u\in I^{\pm}(u)
,
i.e.
,
I^{\pm}(u)\neq\emptyset
.

If
I^{\pm}(u)
has more than one point, then, for any two points
u_{1},u_{2}\in I^{\pm}(u)
with
u_{1}<u_{2}
,

[u_{1},u_{2}]\subset I^{\pm}(u)
. In fact, by the definition of
I^{\pm}(u)
in
(
3.12
)
, we have

f(u_{i})-f(u)-f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})(u_{i}-u)=0\qquad\,\,\mbox{for $i=1,2$}.

(3.15)

Since
f(u)
is convex, for any
v\in(u_{1},u_{2})
,
i.e.
,

v=\theta u_{1}+(1-\theta)u_{2}
for
\theta\in(0,1)
,

f(v)\geq f(u)+f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})(v-u).

(3.16)

On the other hand, from
(
3.15
)
, we have

f(v)=f(\theta u_{1}+(1-\theta)u_{2})\leq\theta f(u_{1})+(1-\theta)f(u_{2})=f(u)+f^{\prime}(u{\pm 0})(v-u).

(3.17)

Combining
(
3.16
)
with
(
3.17
)
,
v\in I^{\pm}(u)
so that

Remark 3.6

4.1.
