Sendov’s conjecture for sufficiently high degree polynomials
Terence Tao

Abstract
. Sendov’s conjecture asserts that if a complex polynomial f f of degree n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 has all of its zeroes in closed unit disk { z : | z | ≤ 1 } \{z:|z|\leq 1\} , then for each such zero λ 0 \lambda_{0} there is a zero of the derivative f ′ f^{\prime} in the closed unit disk { z : | z − λ 0 | ≤ 1 } \{z:|z-\lambda_{0}|\leq 1\} . This conjecture is known for n < 9 n<9 , but only partial results are available for higher n n . We show that there exists a constant n 0 n_{0} such that Sendov’s conjecture holds for n ≥ n 0 n\geq n_{0} . For λ 0 \lambda_{0} away from the origin and the unit circle we can appeal to the prior work of Dégot and Chalebgwa; for λ 0 \lambda_{0} near the unit circle we refine a previous argument of Miller (and also invoke results of Chijiwa when λ 0 \lambda_{0} is extremely close to the unit circle); and for λ 0 \lambda_{0} near the origin we introduce a new argument using compactness methods, balayage, and the argument principle.

We now prove Theorem

\eta

U_{\eta}
(defined in (

\eta

\eta^{(n)}

\eta^{(\infty)}

U_{\eta^{(n)}}
converges locally in

to

\log\frac{1}{|z|}
by a continuous function plus an error small in

). Here of course we equip the complex plane

with Lebesgue measure. In particular we have

U_{\lambda}(z)\to U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)

and

locally in

as
n\to\infty

For
z
in the exterior region
\overline{D(0,1)}^{c}

z-\lambda\in D(z,1)

[|z|-1,|z|+1]

\frac{1}{z-\lambda}
takes values in a disk with a diameter that is a rotation of
[\frac{1}{|z|+1},\frac{1}{|z|-1}]

U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)=U_{\zeta^{(\infty)}}(z)

(2.2)

for almost all

At this point we could proceed either via potential theory or via Fourier analysis; we shall adopt the latter approach. If we write

Similarly for
\zeta^{(\infty)}
. From (

\begin{split}P^{R}_{w}(Re^{i\theta})&\coloneqq{\operatorname{Re}}\frac{1+\frac{r}{R}e^{i(\theta-\alpha)}}{1-\frac{r}{R}e^{i(\theta-\alpha)}}\\

Setting
R\coloneqq 1+n^{-1/3}
, we obtain the claim. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.10
(i).

2.2.
Proof of (ii)

Now we suppose
a^{(\infty)}=1
, so that
a=1-o(1)
. By passing to subsequences as necessary we may assume that
n
is sufficiently large whenever this is required.

We will combine the above theory with the analysis of Dégot
[
7
]
. A key quantity will be the mean

\mu\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\lambda=\mathbb{E}\zeta

from Lemma
1.6
(ii).

First, from applying
[
7
, Theorem 5]
(which relies ultimately on the powerful theorem of Grace
[
9
]
) we obtain the lower bound

|f(\delta)|\geq\frac{1-\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}-\delta a}}{n}|f^{\prime}(a)|

(2.11)

for any
0<\delta<a
, so in particular

|f(\delta)|\gtrsim\frac{1}{n}|f^{\prime}(a)|

(2.12)

whenever
1\lesssim\delta\leq 1/2
. Meanwhile, from (
1.12
) and the fact that
|a-\zeta|>1
we have

|f^{\prime}(a)|>n.

(2.13)

On the other hand, by
[
7
, Lemma 1]
, we have the elementary inequality

|f(\delta)|\leq\left(\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}-2\delta{\operatorname{Re}}\mu}\right)^{n}.

Combining these three estimates, we conclude that for all
1\lesssim\delta\leq 1/2
we have

\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}-2\delta{\operatorname{Re}}\mu}\geq 1-O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

which implies in particular that

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\leq\frac{\delta}{2}+o(1).

Sending
\delta
to zero arbitrarily slowly with
n
(cf. Example
1.13
), we conclude that

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\leq o(1)

(compare with
[
7
, Corollary 1, Remark 2]
). On the other hand, since
{\operatorname{Re}}\zeta
is bounded below by
-1
, we have

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\geq\varepsilon-\mathbb{P}({\operatorname{Re}}\zeta\leq\varepsilon)

and thus

\mathbb{P}({\operatorname{Re}}\zeta\leq\varepsilon)\gtrsim 1

(2.14)

for
n
large enough and any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.

Next, from (
1.12
) one has for any
0\leq\delta\leq 1/2
that

\log|f^{\prime}(a)|-\log|f^{\prime}(\delta)|=(n-1)\mathbb{E}\log\frac{|a-\zeta|}{|\delta-\zeta|}.

Since
\zeta
takes values in the lune
\overline{D(0,1)}\backslash\overline{D(a,1)}
, which lies to the left of the perpendicular bisector of
\delta
and
a
, we see that
\log\frac{|a-\zeta|}{|\delta-\zeta|}\geq 0
, with equality only when
\zeta
has real part
\frac{a+\delta}{2}=\frac{1+\delta}{2}-o(1)
. As a consequence, if
{\operatorname{Re}}\zeta\leq 0.1
(say), we see that
\log\frac{|a-\zeta|}{|\delta-\zeta|}\gtrsim 1
. From (
2.14
), we conclude that

\log|f^{\prime}(a)|-\log|f^{\prime}(\delta)|\gtrsim n

and thus

f^{\prime}(\delta)\lesssim e^{-cn}|f^{\prime}(a)|

for all
0\leq\delta\leq 1/2
and some absolute constant
c>0
. A similar argument also gives

f^{\prime}(z)\lesssim e^{-cn}|f^{\prime}(a)|

for
z\in D(0.5,0.01)
(say). By the fundamental theorem of calculus, this implies that

f(0)=f(\delta)+O\left(e^{-cn}|f^{\prime}(a)|\right)

for all
0\leq\delta\leq 1/2
, hence by (
2.12
) (for say
\delta=1/2
) we have

|f(0)|\gtrsim\frac{1}{n}|f^{\prime}(a)|.

From Lemma
1.6
(iii) we have
|f(0)|\leq 1
; comparing this with (
2.12
), (
2.13
) we see that

|f(0)|\sim|f(\delta)|\sim 1;\quad|f^{\prime}(a)|\sim n

(2.15)

for all
0\leq\delta\leq 1/2
(cf.,
[
7
, Theorem 6]
). In particular,
f(0)
is non-zero. A similar fundamental theorem of calculus argument also shows

|f(z)|\sim 1

(2.16)

uniformly for
z\in D(0.5,0.01)
. In particular,
f
has no zeroes in this disk (cf.
[
7
, Theorem 4]
).

From (
2.15
) and Lemma
1.6
(iii) we have

\mathbb{E}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda|}=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{|f(0)|}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}

(giving the first estimate of (
1.21
)), hence on taking limits (using Fatou’s lemma)

\mathbb{E}\log\frac{1}{|\lambda^{(\infty)}|}=0.

Thus
\lambda^{(\infty)}
almost surely lies in the unit circle
\partial D(0,1)
. In particular, the logarithmic potential
U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
is harmonic in
D(0,1)
. On the other hand, from (
2.16
) and Lemma
1.6
(iii) we have

U_{\lambda}(z)=O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

uniformly for
z\in D(0.5,0.01)
, thus on taking limits we see that
U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
vanishes on
D(0.5,0.01)
. By unique continuation for harmonic functions, we thus see that
U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
vanishes on all of
D(0,1)
. Using the expansion (
2.4
)
we conclude that all the non-zero Fourier coefficients of
\lambda^{(\infty)}
vanish, thus by Fourier uniqueness
\lambda^{(\infty)}
is uniformly distributed on the unit circle
\partial D(0,1)
. A standard computation (using Jensen’s formula, (
2.3
), or the two-dimensional version of the Newton shell theorem) then gives

U_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)=\min\left(\log\frac{1}{|z|},0\right)

(2.17)

for
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

Returning to (
2.15
), we use Lemma
1.6
(iii) again to conclude that

\mathbb{E}\log|\zeta-a|=\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{|f^{\prime}(a)|}{n}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}

(giving the second estimate of (
1.21
)) and hence on taking limits

\mathbb{E}\log|\zeta^{(\infty)}-1|=0.

As
\zeta^{(\infty)}
was already almost surely in
\overline{D(0,1)}\backslash\overline{D(1,1)}
, it is thus almost surely in the arc
\overline{D(0,1)}\cap\partial D(1,1)
(cf.,
[
7
, Remark 3]
). In particular, the logarithmic potential
U_{\zeta^{(\infty)}}
is harmonic outside of this arc. By (
2.2
), (
2.17
),
U_{\zeta^{(\infty)}}
agrees with
\log\frac{1}{|z|}
in
\overline{D(0,1)}^{c}
; by unique continuation of harmonic functions, it thus agrees with
\log\frac{1}{|z|}
almost everywhere in
\mathbb{C}
(here we use the connectedness of the complement of the arc
\overline{D(0,1)}\cap\partial D(1,1)
in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
). Taking distributional Laplacians using (
1.9
), we conclude that the law of
\zeta^{(\infty)}
is the Dirac probability measure at
0
. This proves Theorem
1.10
(ii).

Remark 2.1
.

A similar argument applies when
0<a^{(\infty)}<1
to conclude that
\zeta^{(\infty)}
is almost surely
0
and simultaneously
|\zeta^{(\infty)}-a^{(\infty)}|
is almost surely
1
, which is absurd. This gives a slightly different way to recover the main results of Dégot
[
7
]
(although many of the key ingredients in both arguments are the same).

3.
Getting a contradiction near the origin

In this section we treat the case where
a
is somewhat close to the origin:

Theorem 3.1

(Contradiction near the origin)
.

Theorem
1.3
holds when
a=o(\frac{1}{\log n})
.

We now prove this theorem. The strategy is to control the number of zeroes of
f
near the origin by applying the argument principle to the log derivative
\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}
, with the aim to show that this number is either zero or negative, contradicting the hypothesis of having a zero
a
near the origin.

By passing to a subsequence if necessary we may assume that
n
is sufficiently large.

From Theorem
1.10
(i) we know that in this case
\zeta^{(\infty)},\lambda^{(\infty)}
are almost surely in the semicircle
C=\{e^{i\theta}:\pi/2\leq\theta\leq 3\pi/2\}
with the same distribution, and that

\mathbb{P}(|\lambda|\leq 1/2)=o\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)

(3.1)

In other words,
\overline{D(0,1/2)}
contains at most
o(n/\log n)
of the zeroes of
f
(counting multiplicity).

A key function in our analysis will be the Stieltjes transform
s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
of
\lambda^{(\infty)}
. This function is fixed and holomorphic on the connected domain
\mathbb{C}\backslash C
, and is not identically zero since it is asymptotic to
1/z
as
|z|\to\infty
. Hence the zeroes of this transform on
\mathbb{C}\backslash C
are isolated. In particular, one can find a fixed annulus
\overline{D(0,r_{2})}\backslash D(0,r_{1})
for some fixed
0<r_{1}<r_{2}<1/2
on which
s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
is bounded away from zero, thus

|s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)|\sim 1

uniformly for
r_{1}\leq|z|\leq r_{2}
.

Let
m
denote the number of zeroes of
s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
in the disk
D(0,r_{1})
, then
m
is a fixed non-negative integer, and by the argument principle we see that for any radius
r_{1}<r<r_{2}
, the contour
\theta\mapsto s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(re^{i\theta}),0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
stays at a distance
\sim 1
from the origin and winds exactly
m
times anti-clockwise around the origin.

From Lemma
1.6
(iv) we have

s_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}.

Since
\lambda
converges in distribution to
\lambda^{(\infty)}
and ranges in the compact set
\overline{D(0,1)}
, we see from the local integrability of the Stieltjes kernel
\frac{1}{z}
we see that
\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}
converges locally in
L^{1}(\mathbb{C})
to

s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)
.

Suppose for the moment that this convergence was in fact locally uniform instead of merely being locally in
L^{1}(\mathbb{C})
, so that

\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}=s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)+o(1)

uniformly for
z\in\overline{D(0,r_{2})}\backslash D(0,r_{1})
. Then by Rouche’s theorem and the argument principle, for
n
sufficiently large and any
r_{1}<r<r_{2}
, the contour

\theta\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}(re^{i\theta}),0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi

(3.2)

would also wind
m
times anti-clockwise around the origin, and the number of zeroes of
f^{\prime}
in
D(0,r)
minus the number of zeroes in
f
in
D(0,r)
(counting multiplicity) would be equal to
m
. But
f^{\prime}
has no zeroes in
\overline{D(a,1)}\supset D(0,1/2)\supset D(0,r)
, and
f
contains at least one zero
a
in
D(0,r)
, hence we have

m\leq-1

(3.3)

which is absurd since
m
is a non-negative integer.

Now we need to get around the technical issue that the convergence is a little bit worse than locally uniform. Here we will take advantage of the relatively small number of zeroes in or near the annulus
\{r_{1}\leq|z|\leq r_{2}\}
, as provided by (
3.1
). We use Lemma
1.6
(iv) and split

\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}=\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|>1/2}}{z-\lambda}+\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{z-\lambda},

where we use
1_{E}
to denote the indicator of an event
E
. Since
\lambda
converges in distribution to
\lambda^{(\infty)}
, which is supported in
C
, we have from (
1.19
) (approximating
1_{|\lambda|>1/2}
by continuous functions in the usual fashion) to conclude that

\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|>1/2}}{z-\lambda}=\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{z-\lambda^{(\infty)}}+o(1)=s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(z)+o(1)

uniformly for
z\in D(0,r_{2})
. If
|z|=r
, we have from the triangle inequality that

\left|\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{z-\lambda}\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{\left|r-|\lambda|\right|}.

To get a good bound for the right-hand side we exploit the freedom to select
r
via the probabilistic method (cf. the standard proof of the Hadamard factorisation theorem, see e.g.,
[
28
, Corollary 5.4]
). Whenever
|\lambda|\leq 1/2
, direct calculation gives

\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\frac{dr}{\max\left(\left|r-|\lambda|\right|,n^{-10}\right)}\lesssim\log n

hence by (
3.1
) and the Fubini–Tonelli theorem we have

\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{\max\left(\left|r-|\lambda|\right|,n^{-10}\right)}\ dr=o(1).

Hence by Markov’s inequality we see that

\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{\max\left(\left|r-|\lambda|\right|,n^{-10}\right)}=o(1)

for all
r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
outside of a set of measure
o(1)
(we do
not
require this set to be fixed). Also, since there are at most
n
zeroes, we see that one surely has
\left|r-|\lambda|\right|\geq n^{-10}
for all
r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
outside of a set of measure
O(n^{-10+1})=o(1)
. We conclude that for
n
large enough, we can find
r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
(depending on
n
) such that

\mathbb{E}\frac{1_{|\lambda|\leq 1/2}}{\left|r-|\lambda|\right|}=o(1).

Putting all this together, we conclude that for this particular choice of
r
, one has

\frac{1}{n}\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}(re^{i\theta})=s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}(re^{i\theta})+o(1)

uniformly in
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
. By Rouche’s theorem, we conclude (again for
n
large enough) that for this choice of
r
, the contour (
3.2
) winds around the origin
m
times, so we again obtain the absurd conclusion (
3.3
). This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Remark 3.2
.

It is instructive to compare this argument with the near-counterexample in Example
1.4
. Here
\lambda^{(\infty)},\zeta^{(\infty)}
are uniformly distributed on unit circle
\partial D(0,1)
. Unlike the situation with Theorem
1.10
(i), the essential range of these random variables is now all of the circle
\partial D(0,1)
rather than just the semicircle
C
; in particular, this support now disconnects the origin from infinity, and we can no longer prevent the Stieltjes transform
s_{\lambda^{(\infty)}}
from vanishing in the disk
D(0,1)
; indeed this transform does vanish by the residue theorem. As a consequence, we no longer possess an annulus in which one can use the argument principle to prevent
f
from acquiring a zero (or
f^{\prime}/f
acquiring a pole) near the origin.

Remark 3.3
.

A variant of this argument can also cover the range
n^{-O(1)}\leq a\leq o(1)
as follows. From (
2.11
), (
2.13
) one has

|f(\delta)|>1-\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}-\delta a}=\frac{\delta(a-\delta)}{1+\sqrt{1-\delta(a-\delta)}}

for any
0<\delta<a
, so in particular

\log\frac{1}{|f(\delta)|}\lesssim\log\frac{1}{\delta}

for
0<\delta\leq a/2
. Applying Lemma
1.6
(iii), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\log\frac{1}{|\zeta-\delta|}\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

On the other hand,
\log\frac{1}{|\zeta-\delta|}
is bounded from below by
\log\frac{1}{1+\delta}=-O(\delta)
. Applying Markov’s inequality, we conclude for any fixed compact
K\subset D(0,1)
that

\mathbb{P}(\zeta\in K)\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\delta

if
n
is large enough. Setting
\delta\coloneqq n^{-C}
for a large fixed
C
, we conclude in particular that

\mathbb{P}(\zeta\in K)\lesssim\frac{\log n}{n}

and this estimate can be used as a replacement for (
1.20
) in the above arguments, in particular by establishing (
3.1
) in this regime.

4.
A motivational near-counterexample

In Example
1.4
a near-counterexample was provided to Sendov’s conjecture in which
a
was close to (and in fact equal to)
1
. In this section we analyze (somewhat informally) a more general family of near-counterexamples of this form. Unsurprisingly, we will eventually be able to show that no element of this family is actually a counterexample to the conjecture, but the task of definitively ruling out a counterexample turns out to be surprisingly delicate, and will serve to motivate the arguments in the next section. However, none of the discussion in this section is strictly necessary for the proof of Theorem
1.2
, and the impatient reader may skip ahead to the next section if desired.

Let
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
be fixed real constants, and let

P(z)=(z-\lambda_{1})\dots(z-\lambda_{m})

(4.1)

be a fixed monic polynomial with distinct zeroes
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}
, which we will choose later. For
n>m
, we consider monic degree
n
polynomials of the form

f(z)\coloneqq\left(z+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right)^{n-m}P(z)-\left(a+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right)^{n-m}P(a)

(4.2)

where

a\coloneqq 1-\frac{c_{1}}{n}=1-o(1).

Clearly
f
has a zero at
a
. The derivative can be computed as

f^{\prime}(z)=(n-m)\left(z+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right)^{n-m-1}\left(P(z)+\frac{z+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}{n-m}P^{\prime}(z)\right)

(4.3)

and thus has a zero of order
n-m-1
at
-\frac{c_{2}}{n}
, which lies just outside of
\overline{D(a,1)}
thanks to our hypothesis
c_{2}>c_{1}
, while also inheriting the
m
zeroes of
P(z)+\frac{z+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}{n-m}P^{\prime}(z)
, which will take the form
\lambda_{1}+o(1),\dots,\lambda_{m}+o(1)
by Rouche’s theorem (indeed one can easily upgrade the
o(1)
errors here to
O(1/n)
if desired). (In particular, a random zero
\zeta
of
f^{\prime}
will converge to zero in probability, but not necessarily converge uniformly, which is consistent with Theorem
1.10
(ii).) If we now make the hypothesis that the zeroes
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
lie in the open lune
D(0,1)\backslash\overline{D(1,1)}
(so in particular
P(a)\neq 0
), then all the zeroes of
f^{\prime}
lie outside of
\overline{D(a,1)}
when
n
is large enough.

We would therefore obtain a contradiction to Sendov’s conjecture if we could ensure that all zeroes
\lambda
of
f
stayed inside the disk
\overline{D(0,1)}
for large enough
n
.
If
\lambda
is a zero of
f
, then from (
4.2
) one has

\left|\lambda+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right|^{n-m}|P(\lambda)|=\left|a+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right|^{n-m}|P(a)|.

The right-hand side is
\sim 1
, which (because the zeroes of
P
are in the open disk
D(0,1)
) already forces

\left|\lambda+\frac{c_{2}}{n}\right|=1+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(4.4)

By taking logarithms and using (
4.1
), we can rewrite the previous equation as

\log\left|\frac{\lambda+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}{a+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}\right|=\frac{1}{n-m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|\frac{a-\lambda_{j}}{\lambda-\lambda_{j}}\right|.

(4.5)

If we take an ansatz

\lambda=\left(1+\frac{t}{n}\right)e^{i\theta}-\frac{c_{2}}{n}

for some real numbers
t,\theta
with
t=O(1)
(to be compatible with (
4.4
)), we have the Taylor expansions

\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{\lambda+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}{a+\frac{c_{2}}{n}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{n}-\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\log\left|\frac{a-\lambda_{j}}{z-\lambda_{j}}\right|

\displaystyle=\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n-m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right);

inserting these expansions into (
4.5
) and then multiplying by
n
, we conclude that

t=c_{2}-c_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

On the other hand, from a further Taylor expansion we have

|\lambda|=1+\frac{t-c_{2}\cos\theta}{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)

so for
n
large enough we would be able to guarantee that all zeroes
\lambda
were in the disk
\overline{D(0,1)}
if we could ensure the inequality

c_{2}-c_{1}-c_{2}\cos\theta+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|<0

(4.6)

for all
0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
.

One can rule out this inequality by taking an average in
\theta
(or equivalently, extracting the zeroth Fourier coefficient). Indeed, observe from Jensen’s formula (or (
2.3
)) that
\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|
has a mean value of
\log|1-\lambda_{j}|
for
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
; meanwhile,
c_{2}\cos\theta
has a mean of zero. Thus if (
4.6
) held for all
0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
, then we would have

c_{2}-c_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log|1-\lambda_{j}|<0.

But from hypothesis the quantities
c_{2}-c_{1}
and
\log|1-\lambda_{j}|
are non-negative, which (barely) gives a contradiction.

This rules out the most naive effort to generate counterexamples to Sendov’s conjecture using this ansatz. But one might hope that by some more careful analysis of the various
O(1/n^{2})
errors that were discarded that one might be able to still salvage a counterexample assuming only the non-strict inequalities

c_{2}-c_{1}-c_{2}\cos\theta+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|\leq 0

(4.7)

and similarly relaxing the conditions
c_{2}>c_{1}
,
\lambda_{j}\in D(0,1)\backslash\overline{D(1,1)}
to their non-strict counterparts
c_{2}\geq c_{1}
,
\lambda_{j}\in\overline{D(0,1)}\backslash D(1,1)
. Then the same averaging argument as before will now give

c_{2}-c_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log|1-\lambda_{j}|\leq 0

and this forces
c_{2}-c_{1}
and
\log|1-\lambda_{j}|
to vanish. Thus the only surviving endpoint is when
c_{1}=c_{2}
and when all the
\lambda_{j}
lie on the arc
\overline{D(0,1)}\cap\partial D(1,1)
. The left-hand side of (
4.7
) now is non-positive while also having zero mean, and must be identically zero, thus

-c_{2}\cos\theta+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|=0

(4.8)

for all
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
.

We have exhausted the information we can glean from the zeroth Fourier coefficient. The first Fourier coefficient ends up providing the constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}=c_{2}
, which is interesting (it reflects the tendency of the mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}\lambda=\mathbb{E}\zeta
to be surprisingly close to zero, which we will take advantage of in the next section) but does not end up being directly useful for the purpose of establishing a contradiction. To get such a contradiction we will instead take the
second
Fourier coefficient, which allows us to ignore the cosine term in (
4.8
). Indeed, from (
2.3
) and Fourier analysis we have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left|\frac{1-\lambda_{j}}{e^{i\theta}-\lambda_{j}}\right|e^{-2i\theta}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=\frac{\lambda_{j}^{2}}{4}

and

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos\theta e^{-2i\theta}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=0.

Thus, from extracting the second Fourier coefficient of (
4.8
) we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_{j}^{2}=0.

(4.9)

Now we recall the key geometric observation from Figure
2
: the zeroes
\lambda_{j}
lie on the arc
\overline{D(0,1)}\backslash D(1,1)
and thus either vanish, or have argument either in
[\pi/3,\pi/2]
or
[-\pi/2,-\pi/3]
. Thus the squares
\lambda_{j}^{2}
either vanish, or have argument in

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\geq\frac{\log|a|}{1.9}+O(|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2})+O\left((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}\right);

since
\log|a|=-(1-a)(1+o(1))
, we conclude that

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\geq-\frac{1-a}{1.9+o(1)}+O(|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2})+O((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}).

(5.19)

On the other hand, we have
|\zeta-a|\geq 1
surely, hence on squaring

|\zeta|^{2}-2a{\operatorname{Re}}\zeta+a^{2}\geq 1

hence on taking expectations and rearranging using (
5.3
) and the fact that
\frac{1-a^{2}}{2a}=(1-a)(1+o(1))
, we obtain

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu\leq-(1-a)(1+o(1))+O(|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2}).

Comparing this with (
5.19
) we conclude

{\operatorname{Re}}\mu,1-a\lesssim|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2}+(\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}

for
n
large enough, and hence for all
n
(since the
o(1)
term on the right-hand side can be arbitrarily large for bounded
n
).
Inserting these bounds back into (
5.18
), we now conclude that

(\sin\theta+o(1)){\operatorname{Im}}\mu\leq O(|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2})+O((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n})

whenever
e^{i\theta}
is a fixed element of
\partial D(0,1)
that avoids
S
. If one applies this bound for some
\theta
close to
\pm\pi/2
for either choice of sign
\pm
, we conclude that

\pm{\operatorname{Im}}\mu\leq O(|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2})+O((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}).

Putting all this together, we conclude that

\mu,1-a\lesssim|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2}+(\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}.

Since
|\mu|^{2}=o(|\mu|)
, we can delete the
|\mu|^{2}
term on the right-hand side. This concludes the proof of (i).

Now we prove (ii). If
e^{i\theta}\in I
, then by Theorem
1.10
(ii) there exists a zero
\lambda
of
f
that lies within
o(1)
of
e^{i\theta}
; it also lies inside the disk
\overline{D(0,1)}
, thus one may write
\lambda=e^{i\theta}(1-\eta)
where
\eta=o(1)
is such that
|1-\eta|\leq 1
. From (
5.12
) we have

U_{\zeta}(a)-U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta)=o(\sigma^{2})+O((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}).

(5.20)

By the fundamental theorem of calculus one has

\log\frac{1}{|e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta-\zeta|}-\log\frac{1}{|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|}=\log|1-e^{-i\theta}\zeta|-\log|1-\eta-e^{-i\theta}\zeta|={\operatorname{Re}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\eta dt}{1-\eta t-e^{-i\theta}\zeta}.

We can Taylor expand

\frac{1}{1-\eta t-e^{-i\theta}\zeta}=\frac{1}{1-\eta t}+\frac{e^{-i\theta}\zeta}{(1-\eta t)^{2}}+O(|\zeta|^{2})

hence on taking expectations and simplifying using (
5.3
), (
5.13
) and
\eta=o(1)

U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta)-U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})={\operatorname{Re}}\eta\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{1-\eta t}+o(\sigma^{2})+O\left((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}\right).

Replacing
\zeta
by
0
, we also have

U_{0}(e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta)-U_{0}(e^{i\theta})={\operatorname{Re}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\eta dt}{1-\eta t}.

Since
U_{0}(e^{i\theta})=0
and
U_{i}(e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta)=\log\frac{1}{|1-\eta|}\geq 0
, we thus have

U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta}-e^{i\theta}\eta)-U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\geq-o(\sigma^{2})-O\left((\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}\right).

Combining this with (
5.20
), we conclude that

U_{\zeta}(a)-U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\geq-o(\sigma^{2})-(\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}

and the claim (ii) follows from the underspill principle.

Now we prove (iii). Suppose first that
\varphi
is non-negative. The fixed set
S
only intersects
\partial D(0,1)
in at most finitely many points. Thus, for any fixed
0<\delta\leq 1
, one can find a fixed compact subset
I
of
\partial D(0,1)\backslash S
whose complement in
\partial D(0,1)
has measure at most
\delta
with respect to the uniform probability measure
dm
on
\partial D(0,1)
. Integrating (ii) we see that if
n
is sufficiently large depending on
\delta,\varepsilon
then

\left(\int_{I}\varphi\ dm\right)U_{\zeta}(a)-\int_{I}\varphi(e^{i\theta})U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\ dm(e^{i\theta})\geq-o(\sigma^{2})-o(1)^{n}.

(5.21)

Now consider the expression

\int_{\partial D(0,1)\backslash I}\varphi(e^{i\theta})\left(\log|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|+{\operatorname{Re}}(e^{-i\theta}\zeta)\right)\ dm(e^{i\theta}).

On the one hand, from Cauchy-Schwarz and the uniform square-integrability of the functions
e^{i\theta}\mapsto\log|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|+{\operatorname{Re}}(e^{-i\theta}\zeta)
we can bound this quantity by
O(\delta^{1/2})
(where the implied constant can depend on
\varphi
, but is independent of
\delta
). On the other hand, if
|\zeta|\leq 1/2
(say), we can perform a Taylor expansion

\log|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|+{\operatorname{Re}}(e^{-i\theta}\zeta)=O(|\zeta|^{2})

and we can bound this quantity instead by
O(\delta|\zeta|^{2})
. Thus for all
\zeta\in\overline{D(0,1)}
we can obtain a bound of
O(\delta^{1/2}|\zeta|^{2})
. Taking expectations and using (
5.13
) to dispose of the
{\operatorname{Re}}e^{i\theta}\zeta
contribution we conclude that

\int_{\partial D(0,1)\backslash I}\varphi(e^{i\theta})U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\ dm(e^{i\theta})=O(\delta^{1/2}\sigma^{2})+o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}.

Also we certainly have

\int_{\partial D(0,1)\backslash I}\varphi\ dm=O(\delta).

Combining these estimates with (
5.21
), (
5.16
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\partial D(0,1)}\varphi\ dm\right)U_{\zeta}(a)-\int_{\partial D(0,1)}\varphi(e^{i\theta})U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\ dm(e^{i\theta})

\displaystyle\quad\geq-O\left(\delta^{1/2}\sigma^{2}\right)-o\left(\sigma^{2}\right)-o(1)^{n}

where the implied constant in the
O\left(\delta^{1/2}\sigma^{2}\right)
term is independent of
\delta
. Sending
\delta
sufficiently slowly to zero, we conclude that

\int_{\partial D(0,1)}\varphi(e^{i\theta})(U_{\zeta}(a)-U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta}))\ dm(e^{i\theta})\geq-o(\sigma^{2})-o(1)^{n}.

(5.22)

If we apply (
5.22
) with
\varphi
identically equal to one, we see from Jensen’s formula (or (
2.3
)) that the contribution of the
U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})
term vanishes, giving

-U_{\zeta}(a)\leq o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}.

From Lemma
1.6
(i) the left-hand side is positive, and we obtain (
5.16
). (Compare this with the argument used to rule out (
4.6
).) From (
5.22
) we now have

\int_{\partial D(0,1)}\varphi(e^{i\theta})U_{\zeta}(e^{i\theta})\ dm(e^{i\theta})\leq o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}

whenever
\varphi\colon\partial D(0,1)\to\mathbb{C}
is fixed smooth and non-negative. Since one can add an arbitrary constant to
\varphi
without affecting the left-hand side by Jensen’s formula (or (
2.3
)), this inequality in fact holds for all fixed smooth real
\varphi
; replacing
\varphi
with
-\varphi
if necessary we now obtain (
5.15
) for real
\varphi
, and the complex case now follows by the triangle inequality.
∎

We can now finish the proof of Theorem
5.1
, following the “second Fourier coefficient” strategy used to rule out (
4.7
). From Taylor expansion and Fourier inversion one has

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-2i\theta}\log\frac{1}{|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|}\ \frac{d\theta}{2\pi}=\frac{1}{4}\zeta^{2}

for any
\zeta\in\overline{D(0,1)}
(one can work first with
\zeta\in D(0,1)
and then take limits if desired). Applying Proposition
5.6
(iii) and the Fubini–Tonelli theorem, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\zeta^{2}=o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}

and in particular

\mathbb{E}{\operatorname{Re}}(\zeta^{2})=o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}

(5.23)

To derive a contradiction from this, observe that as
1\leq|a-\zeta|\leq 2
, we surely have

|a-\zeta|-1\sim\log|a-\zeta|

and hence by the triangle inequality

|1-\zeta|=1+O((1-a)+\log|a-\zeta|).

Thus
\zeta
lies within
O((1-a)+\log|a-\zeta|)
of an element
\tilde{\zeta}
of the arc
\overline{D(0,1)}\cap D(1,1)
. In particular, using once again the crucial observation from Figure
2
, the argument of
\tilde{\zeta}
lies in either
[\pi/3,\pi/2]
or
[-\pi/2,-\pi/3]
, so that

{\operatorname{Re}}(\tilde{\zeta}^{2})\leq-\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{\zeta}|^{2}.

Writing
\tilde{\zeta}
in terms of
\zeta
and an error of size
O((1-a)+\log|a-\zeta|)
, we conclude that

{\operatorname{Re}}(\zeta^{2})\leq-\frac{1}{2}|\zeta|^{2}+O\left(|\zeta|\left((1-a)+\log|a-\zeta|\right)\right)+O\left(\left((1-a)+\log|a-\zeta|\right)^{2}\right).

Using the Young inequality
|zw|\leq\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|w|^{2}
for various choices of
z,w
to absorb some error terms into other terms, we conclude that

{\operatorname{Re}}(\zeta^{2})\leq-\frac{1}{4}|\zeta|^{2}+O((1-a)^{2}+\log^{2}|a-\zeta|)

(say). Thus on taking expectations

\mathbb{E}{\operatorname{Re}}(\zeta^{2})\leq-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}|\zeta|^{2}+O((1-a)^{2}+\mathbb{E}\log^{2}|a-\zeta|).

From (
5.14
) and the bound
1-a=o(1)
we have

(1-a)^{2}\leq o(\sigma^{2})+(\varepsilon+o(1))^{n}

for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, hence by the underspill principle

(1-a)^{2}\leq o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}.

(5.24)

Similarly, from (
5.16
) and the bound
0\leq\log|a-\zeta|\lesssim 1
we have

\mathbb{E}\log^{2}|a-\zeta|\leq o(\sigma^{2})+o(1)^{n}.

Using (
5.3
), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}{\operatorname{Re}}(\zeta^{2})\leq-\frac{1+o(1)}{4}\mathbb{E}|\zeta|^{2}+o(1)^{n}

which when combined with (
5.23
), (
5.3
) gives

|\mu|^{2}+\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}|\zeta|^{2}=o(1)^{n}.

(5.25)

Applying (
5.24
) we conclude that

a=1-o(1)^{n}.

But this contradicts (
5.1
), and thus concludes the proof of Theorem
5.1
.
