Introduction

These notes constitute the first part of a detailed exposition of the theory of nilspaces developed by Camarena and Szegedy in the paper
[
1
]
. We treat what can be called the algebraic part of the theory, in which nilspaces are studied without any topological assumption. We elaborate on some of the central concepts and arguments from the first two chapters of
[
1
]
, present more detailed proofs, and illustrate some of the key notions with concrete examples.

The main objects studied in
[
1
]
are
nilspaces
. These objects are defined in terms of some axioms, which rely on the basic notions of discrete cubes and cube morphisms. We shall begin in the next section by recalling these notions and their elementary properties.

As stated in
[
1
]
, important examples of nilspaces are provided by filtered groups and filtered nilmanifolds. In Chapter
Notes on nilspaces: algebraic aspects
, we elaborate on this by treating the theory of cubes on filtered groups, which was pioneered by Host and Kra
[
11
,
12
]
and developed in several other works including
[
6
,
7
,
14
]
. We treat this theory from the viewpoint of general nilspaces. This provides natural motivation for various important objects and results related to this theory, notably polynomial maps and Leibman’s theorem, and it also provides illustrations of several tools and ideas used by Camarena and Szegedy.

One of the central results in the algebraic part of
[
1
]
is the description of a general nilspace as an iterated
abelian bundle
. In Chapter
Notes on nilspaces: algebraic aspects
, after having gathered some basic tools in Section
7
, we present a detailed proof of this result in Section
8
, following the arguments of Camarena and Szegedy. The final section of the chapter concerns additional algebraic tools introduced in
[
1
]
. These tools are of inherent interest but are also important for the topological part of the theory of nilspaces.

We treat the topological part of the theory separately, in
[
2
]
. That part studies nilspaces equipped with a compact topology that is compatible with the cube structure, called
compact nilspaces
. This study is motivated principally by the analysis of
uniformity norms
in arithmetic combinatorics, and of the analogous
uniformity seminorms
in ergodic theory, leading in particular to the theory of
higher order Fourier analysis
; see
[
3
]
for an introduction to this theory from the viewpoint of arithmetic combinatorics. An alternative treatment of compact nilspaces is given by Gutman, Manners, and Varjú in
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.

Discrete cubes

Recall that an
affine homomorphism
from an abelian group
\ab_{1}
to an abelian group
\ab_{2}
is a map of the form
x\mapsto\phi(x)+t
, where
\phi:\ab_{1}\to\ab_{2}
is a homomorphism and
t
is some fixed element of
\ab_{2}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
n
be a non-negative integer. The
discrete
n
-cube
is the set
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We denote the elements
(0,\ldots,0),(1,\ldots,1)
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
by
0^{n},1^{n}
respectively. A
discrete-cube morphism
is a map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
that is the restriction of an affine homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. An
automorphism
of the discrete
n
-cube is a bijective morphism
\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
. These automorphisms form a group, denoted by
\aut(\{0,1\}^{n})
.

We recall that
\aut(\{0,1\}^{n})
is generated by the group
S_{n}
of permutations of
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
together with the reflections
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}\mapsto 1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}
,
i\in[n]
, more precisely we have
\aut(\{0,1\}^{n})\cong S_{n}\ltimes(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{n}
.

For
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we shall write
\supp(v)
for the
support
of
v
, i.e.

\supp(v)=\{i\in[n]:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}=1\},

and we shall denote by
|v|
the cardinality of
\supp(v)
.

Morphisms of discrete cubes can be described more explicitly in several ways. Below we give a description that will be quite useful later on. Let us first denote the four maps
\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}
as follows: we denote by
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
the maps with constant value 0 and 1 respectively, we denote the identity map by
\tau_{0}
, and we denote by
\tau_{1}
the reflection map (the bijection sending 0 to 1).

Lemma 1.2
.

A map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is a discrete-cube morphism if and only if it is of the form
\phi\big(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(m)$}\big)=\big(w\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,w\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}\big)
where, for each
j\in[n]
, either
w\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}
is a constant function of
v
or there is a unique
i=i(j)\in[m]
and
k=k(j)\in\{0,1\}
such that
w\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=\tau_{k}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$})
.

Example 1.3
.

To illustrate the lemma, we can consider a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
. We could have for instance
\phi(v)=(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
, where no coordinate of
\phi(v)
is a constant function of
v
. In this case, embedding
\{0,1\}^{3}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the set of vertices of the unit cube, the image of
\phi
consists of the vertices of a square going diagonally through the cube, namely
\{(0,1,0),(1,0,0),(0,1,1),(1,0,1)\}
. We could also have that some coordinate of
\phi(v)
is constant, for instance
\phi(v)=(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},0,1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
. In this case, in the embedding just mentioned, the image of
\phi
consists of the vertices of a face of the unit cube, namely
\{(0,0,1),(1,0,1),(0,0,0),(1,0,0)\}
.

Proof of Lemma
1.2
.

Note that, firstly, from the definition of a morphism we can deduce that
\phi(v)=Mv+\phi(0^{m})
, for some matrix
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
. Then, since
\phi
takes values in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, for each
j\in[n]
the
j
-th row of
M
has at most one non-zero entry, with index
i=i(j)
. This entry must then equal 1 or
-1
, which then determines the
i
-th entry of
\phi(0^{m})
to be 0 or 1 respectively, and the result follows.
∎

We shall now define an important family of subsets of a discrete cube, namely its
faces
. For this we use the following notation. Given a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
and
i\in[m]
, we denote by
J_{\phi}(i)
the set of indices of the coordinates of
\phi(v)
that are not constant functions of
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}
(in other words, the coordinates of
\phi(v)
which vary as
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}
varies), that is

J_{\phi}(i):=\{j\in[n]:\phi(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=\tau_{0}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$})\textrm{ or }\tau_{1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$})\}.

(1)

In the example
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
,
v\mapsto(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
seen above, we would have
J_{\phi}(1)=\{1,2\}
and
J_{\phi}(2)=\{3\}
.

Note that a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is injective if and only if
J_{\phi}(i)
is non-empty for every
i\in[m]
. We define

J_{\phi}:=\bigsqcup_{i\in[m]}J_{\phi}(i).

(2)

This is the set of coordinates of
\phi(v)
that are not constant functions of
v
. We denote the set of subsets of size
k
of a set
S
by
\binom{S}{k}
.

Definition 1.4

(Faces and face maps)
.

Let
m\leq n
be non-negative integers. An
m
-face
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
(short for
m
-dimensional face) is a set
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
specified by fixing
n-m
coordinates of
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, that is

F=\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}=x\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}\;\;\forall\,i\in I\}
, for some fixed set
I\in\binom{[n]}{n-m}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{I}
. We call a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
an
m
-
face map
if
\phi
is injective and the image
\tIm(\phi)
is an
m
-face. Equivalently,
\phi
is an
m
-face map if
|J_{\phi}(i)|=1
for every
i\in[m]
.

For instance, the map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
,
v\mapsto(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},0,1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
from Example
1.3
is a 2-face map and
\tIm(\phi)
is the 2-face
\{w\in\{0,1\}^{3}:w\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}=0\}
. The map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
,
v\mapsto(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
is an injective morphism, but is not a face map.

We shall often specify an
m
-face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
by the data
(I,x)
. We shall then refer to the cardinality of
I
as the
codimension
of
F
, denoted by
\codim(F)
. For later use, let us also reserve the special notation
\phi_{F}
for the
m
-face map that has image equal to
F
in the “simplest way”. More precisely,
\phi_{F}
will always denote the
m
-face map such that for all
j<j^{\prime}
in
J_{\phi_{F}}
we have
i(j)<i(j^{\prime})
in
[m]
, and
\phi_{F}(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i(j))$}
for all
j\in J_{\phi_{F}}
. For instance if
F=\{w\in\{0,1\}^{3}:w\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}=0\}
then
\phi_{F}:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{3}
,
v\mapsto(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},0,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}\big)
. Note that if
m=n
then
\phi_{F}
is just the identity in
\aut(\{0,1\}^{n})
.

Definition 1.5

(Face restriction)
.

Let
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
be an
m
-face and let
g
be a map defined on
\{0,1\}^{n}
. The
face restriction
of
g
to
F
is the map
g\co\phi_{F}
on
\{0,1\}^{m}
.

2
Definition of a nilspace

Definition 2.1
.

A
nilspace
is a set
\ns
together with a collection of sets
\cu^{n}(\ns)\subset\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
, for each non-negative integer
n
, satisfying the following axioms:

(i)

(Composition) For every morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
and every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, we have

\q\co\phi\in\cu^{m}(\ns)
.

(ii)

(Ergodicity)
\cu^{1}(\ns)=\ns^{\{0,1\}}
.

(iii)

(Corner completion) Let
\q^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{1^{n}\}\to\ns
be such that every restriction of
\q^{\prime}
to an
(n-1)
-face containing
0^{n}
is in
\cu^{n-1}(\ns)
. Then there exists
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
such that
\q(v)=\q^{\prime}(v)
for all
v\neq 1^{n}
.

The elements of
\cu^{n}(\ns)
are referred to as the
n
-cubes
(or
n
-dimensional cubes) on
\ns
. A map
\q^{\prime}
satisfying the premise of the third axiom is called an
n
-corner
. We shall call an
n
-cube
\q
satisfying the third axiom for
\q^{\prime}
a
completion
of
\q^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
k
-step nilspace
(or
k
-
nilspace
) is a nilspace such that every
(k+1)
-corner has a unique completion.

As a first manipulation of the axioms, one may check that the set underlying a 0-step nilspace, if non-empty, must be a singleton.

Occasionally we will have to consider spaces
\ns
for which one of the last two axioms above may fail. We shall have to deal with such spaces explicitly only from Chapter
Notes on nilspaces: algebraic aspects
onwards, so we postpone their discussion until then, recording only the following definition for now.

Definition 2.3
.

A
cubespace
is a set
\ns
together with a collection of sets
\cu^{n}(\ns)\subset\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
, for each
n\geq 0
, satisfying the composition axiom from Definition
2.1
and such that
\cu^{0}(\ns)=\ns
. A cubespace
\ns
is said to be
k
-fold ergodic
if
\cu^{k}(\ns)=\ns^{\{0,1\}^{k}}
.

The nilspace axioms can be seen as variants of the axioms defining the notion of
parallelepiped structures
, notion introduced by Host and Kra in
[
12
]
. For instance, the corner completion axiom appears in an equivalent form in
[
12
, Definition 4]
. There is in fact a sense in which the concept of parallelepiped structures is equivalent to that of a nilspace. We shall detail this in Subsection
7.2
.

The term ‘nilspace’ may seem deceptive at first, in that it is not clear from the axioms that a nilspace should be related to anything involving nilpotency. It is a nontrivial fact that there is indeed such a relation. A first explicit description of this phenomenon appeared in the setting of ergodic theory, in the work of Host and Kra
[
11
]
. The notion of cubes on a filtered group, treated in the next chapter, originates in that work. In the later paper
[
12
]
, Host and Kra initiated the program of understanding the above-mentioned relation in a more conceptual and general way, starting from the axioms that define parallelepiped structures. This greater generality consisted especially in that there was no measure-preserving system underlying the definition of these structures, unlike in
[
11
]
. The results included the determination of a nilpotent group naturally associated with such a structure (see
[
12
, Section 3.8]
). The work of Camarena and Szegedy in
[
1
]
can be seen as a continuation of this program (see the end of Subsection
7.2
).

In the next chapter we begin to study some natural examples of nilspaces, starting with the cube structures on filtered groups introduced by Host and Kra. In fact, the results in later chapters will indicate that these filtered groups, together with their quotients (including nilmanifolds), constitute the main examples of nilspaces.

Chapter
\thechapter
Main examples of nilspaces

In this chapter we begin our study of nilspaces by examining some natural examples.

We start in Section
3
with a basic case, namely that of cubes on abelian groups. We shall provide several characterizations of these cubes and show that an abelian group together with these cubes satisfies the nilspace axioms.

In Section
4
we shall then generalize this cube structure to any filtered group (not necessarily commutative) and show that this also yields a nilspace structure on such groups.

The nilspaces covered in Section
4
have the particular feature that the cubes of a fixed dimension form a group under pointwise multiplication. Taking quotients of these nilspaces in certain ways produces new nilspaces that do not have this group property; this is illustrated in Section
5
.

3
Standard cubes on abelian groups

Let
(G,+)
be an abelian group. An
n
-cube on
G
is a map
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
of the form
1
1

1

Strictly speaking, the term ‘
n
-dimensional parallelepiped’ may be more accurate here, but we shall use the shorter ‘
n
-cube’ for convenience.

\q(v)=x+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}\,h_{1}+\cdots+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}\,h_{n},

for some fixed elements
x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in G
. This definition of cubes on
G
is quite familiar in arithmetic combinatorics (it is involved in the definition of the Gowers uniformity norms, for instance). In Proposition
3.2
below we shall record other equivalent ways to view these cubes. To this end we use the following function.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G
be an abelian group. We define the function
\sigma_{2}:G^{\{0,1\}^{2}}\to G
as follows: for
g:\{0,1\}^{2}\to G
,
\sigma_{2}(g):=g\scalebox{0.8}{$(00)$}-g\scalebox{0.8}{$(10)$}+g\scalebox{0.8}{$(11)$}-g\scalebox{0.8}{$(01)$}
.

Note that
\q
is a
2
-cube on
G
if and only if
\sigma_{2}(\q)=0
. Note also that if
\q
is a
2
-cube on
G
then from the expression
\q(v)=x+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}h_{1}+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}h_{2}
it clearly follows that
\q
can be extended to an affine map
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to G
, namely the map
(n_{1},n_{2})\mapsto x+n_{1}h_{1}+n_{2}h_{2}
. The converse holds also.

These remarks generalize in a straightforward way to
n
-cubes for
n>2
, as follows.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
be a map. The following properties are equivalent.

(i)

There exist
x,h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{n}\in G
such that for every
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we have

\q(v)=x+v\cdot h:=x+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}h_{1}+\cdots+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}h_{n}.

(3)

(ii)

The map
\q
extends to an affine homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to G
.

(iii)

Every
2
-face map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfies

\sigma_{2}(\q\co\phi)=0.

(4)

We call the map
\q
a
(
standard
)

n
-cube
on
G
if it satisfies any of these properties.

Proof.

The equivalence of (
i
) and (
ii
) is clear. It is also clear that (
ii
) implies (
iii
), since every 2-face map
\phi
is by definition the restriction to
\{0,1\}^{2}
of an affine homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and so
\q\co\phi
is the restriction of an affine homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to G
, i.e. a 2-cube.
If
\q
satisfies (
iii
), then let
x:=\q(0^{n})
and for each
i\in[n]
let
h_{i}:=\q(e_{i})-x
, where
e_{i}
denotes the
i
-th vector in the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We claim that
\q
satisfies (
3
) with this choice of elements
x,h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
. This can be shown by induction on
|v|
. For
|v|=0,1
the property holds by definition of
x
and
h_{i}
, so let
|v|>1
and suppose that
\q
satisfies (
ii
) for all
v^{\prime}
with
|v^{\prime}|<|v|
. Now there clearly is a 2-face-map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{2}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
v=\phi(1^{2})
and
|\phi(w)|<|v|
for all
w\neq 1^{2}
, so we have by induction
\q(\phi(w))=x+\phi(w)\cdot h
for each
w\in\{0,1\}^{2}\setminus\{1^{2}\}
. By (
4
), we then have
\q(\phi(11))=\q(\phi(10))+\q(\phi(01))-\q(\phi(00))=x+(\phi(10)+\phi(01)-\phi(00))\cdot h=x+v\cdot h
as required.
∎

The cubes characterized by this proposition yield a basic example of a nilspace.

Proposition 3.3
.

An abelian group together with the collection of all standard
n
-cubes on
G

(
for each non-negative integer
n
)
is a 1-step nilspace.

Proof.

The ergodicity axiom holds (from (
3
) say). Composition is also clear (by property
(ii)
from Proposition
3.2
, for instance). The unique corner completion for
n\geq 2
follows from the fact that, by (
3
), a standard
n
-cube is determined by its values at elements
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with
|v|\leq 1
. Thus, given an
n
-corner
\q^{\prime}
on
G
, letting
x=\q^{\prime}(0^{n})
and
h_{i}=\q^{\prime}(e_{i})-x
for each
i\in[n]
, the unique
n
-cube
\q
completing
\q^{\prime}
is given by the formula
\q(v)=x+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}h_{1}+\cdots+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}h_{n}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

For an element
g\in G
and a set
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
g^{F}
denote the element of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
defined by
g^{F}(v)=g
if
v\in F
and
0_{G}
otherwise. Then it follows from (
3
) that the standard
n
-cubes form the subgroup of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
generated by the ‘constant elements’

and the elements
h_{i}^{F_{i}}
,
i\in[n]
, where
F_{i}
is the face
\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}=1\}
of codimension 1. (In fact each

Remark 3.5
.

In Proposition
3.2
one can replace ‘Every 2-face map’ with ‘Every morphism’ without affecting the equivalence. We omit the details here, as a more general remark of this kind will be made later (see Remark

).

4
Filtered groups of degree
k
as
k
-step nilspaces

In this section the main objectives are the following. First we shall extend the notion of standard cubes to any group and show that these cubes form a nilspace structure; see Proposition
4.8
. We shall then seek alternative characterizations of these cubes to obtain a generalization of Proposition
3.2
. This will be obtained eventually as Proposition
4.28
, and the task will involve in particular a treatment of polynomial maps between filtered groups, in Subsection
4.2
.

4.1
Cubes on a general filtered group

Let us begin by recalling the definition of a filtered group. Given a group
G
, the commutator
[g,h]
of two elements
g,h\in G
is defined by
[g,h]=g^{-1}h^{-1}gh
. Thus
gh=hg[g,h]
. Given two subgroups
H_{1},H_{2}
of
G
, we denote by
[H_{1},H_{2}]
the subgroup of
G
generated by all the commutators
[h_{1},h_{2}]
,
h_{i}\in H_{i}
.

Definition 4.1
.

A
filtration
on a group
G
is a sequence
G_{\bullet}=(G_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
of subgroups of
G
satisfying
G\geq G_{0}\geq G_{1}\geq\cdots
and such that
[G_{i},G_{j}]\subset G_{i+j}
for all
i,j\geq 0
. We refer to
(G,G_{\bullet})
as a
filtered group
. If some term in the sequence
G_{\bullet}
is the trivial subgroup
\{\mathrm{id}_{G}\}
, then the
degree
of the filtration, denoted by
\deg(G_{\bullet})
, is the smallest integer
k
such that
G_{k+1}=\{\mathrm{id}_{G}\}
. The filtered group
(G,G_{\bullet})
is then said to be
of degree

k
.

We shall usually assume that
G_{0}=G_{1}=G
, except in some clearly indicated places. Note that
G_{i}
is a normal subgroup of
G
for each
i\geq 0
. We shall denote by
\pi_{i}
the quotient homomorphism
G\to G/G_{i}
.

One can always take as a filtration the lower central series on
G
, and for many purposes this filtration is the natural one to use. However, other filtrations do arise naturally (see for instance Subsection
4.4
), and in some settings, typically quantitative ones, it is convenient to work with a general filtration
[
6
]
. We shall do so here, thus for the remainder of this section we suppose that an arbitrary filtration
G_{\bullet}
has been fixed for the given group
G
.

The general cube structure on a filtered group that we shall study originated in the work of Host and Kra
[
11
]
. The definition relies on the following notion, which builds up on Remark
3.4
.

Definition 4.2

(Face groups)
.

Given a face
F
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
and an element
g\in G
, we write
g^{F}
for the element of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
defined by
g^{F}(v)=g
if
v\in F
and
\mathrm{id}_{G}
otherwise. We define

G_{(F)}=\{g^{F}:g\in G_{\codim(F)}\}\leq G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}.

We refer to these subgroups
G_{(F)}
as the
face groups
in
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
.

Definition 4.3

(Cubes on a filtered group)
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group. The group of
n
-cubes on
(G,G_{\bullet})
, denoted by
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
, is the subgroup of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
generated by the face groups.

Let us make a remark on the notation
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
. On one hand this involves the same ‘
\cu^{n}
’ as in Definition
2.1
; this will be justified by the main result of this subsection, which shows that
G
together with
(\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet}))_{n\geq 0}
is a
k
-step nilspace with
k=\deg(G_{\bullet})
. On the other hand, as we shall see, these cubes do depend significantly on the filtration, which is why the notation specifies the filtration rather than just the group. For instance, if
G
is abelian and
G_{\bullet}
is the lower central series
G_{0}=G_{1}=G
,
G_{2}=\{\mathrm{id}_{G}\}
, then
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
consists of the standard
n
-cubes seen in the previous section, while if
G_{\bullet}
is the degree-
k
filtration
G_{0}=G_{1}=\cdots=G_{k}
,
G_{k+1}=\{\mathrm{id}_{G}\}
then we obtain a rather different but still very natural nilspace structure (see Subsection
4.4
).

Note that it is immediate from the definition that
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
is globally invariant under composition with an automorphism of
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

We now give a more explicit description of these cubes, in terms of certain ‘coefficients’, that generalizes the expression
\q(v)=x+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}h_{1}+\cdots+v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}h_{n}
for standard abelian cubes. To that end we use the following notion.

Definition 4.4

(Upper faces)
.

An
upper face
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
is a set of the form

F(v)=\{u\in\{0,1\}^{n}:\supp(u)\supseteq\supp(v)\}

for some
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Note that
\codim(F)=|\supp(v)|
.
We order the upper faces of
\{0,1\}^{n}
by writing
F(u)<F(v)
if
u<v
in the colex order, thus

F_{0}=\{0,1\}^{n}=F(0^{n})<F((1,0,\ldots,0))<\cdots<F(1^{n})=\{1^{n}\}=F_{2^{n}-1}.

Note that given two upper faces
F_{i}<F_{j}
, their intersection is an upper face
F_{k}\geq F_{j}
, and
\codim(F_{k})\leq\codim(F_{i})+\codim(F_{j})
.

As observed in Remark
3.4
, any standard abelian cube is a unique sum of upper-face group elements for faces of codimension at most 1. This can be generalized as follows.

Lemma 4.5

(Unique factorization of cubes)
.

An element
\q\in G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
lies in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if it has a unique factorization of the form

\q=g_{0}^{F_{0}}g_{1}^{F_{1}}\cdots g_{2^{n}-1}^{F_{2^{n}-1}},

(5)

with
g_{i}\in G_{\codim(F_{i})}

\forall\,i
.

Recall that for each face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
there is unique data
I\subset[n],\,x\in\{0,1\}^{I}
such that

F=\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}=x\scalebox{0.8}{$(i)$}\;\;\forall\,i\in I\}.

Upper faces are precisely those such that the corresponding element
x
has all entries equal to 1.

Proof.

First we claim that every element
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
can be written as a product of face-group elements
g^{F}
where
F
is an
upper
face, in other words that
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
is generated just by the upper-face groups
G_{(F_{i})}
. This follows from the fact that every face-group element
g^{F}
is a product of upper-face group elements. Indeed, the indicator function
1_{F}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
can always be written as an integer combination of indicator functions of upper faces of codimension at most
\codim(F)
(this can be proved by induction on
\codim(F)
).

Our task is thus reduced to showing that any product of finitely many upper-face group elements has the claimed unique factorization. This has been done in several places, for instance in
[
5
, Appendix E]
, but we recall the argument here for completeness.

Observe that if
F<F^{\prime}
are two upper faces, then
G_{(F^{\prime})}\cdot G_{(F)}\subset G_{(F)}\cdot G_{(F^{\prime})}\cdot G_{(F\cap F^{\prime})}
. Indeed, for every
g\in G_{\codim(F)},g^{\prime}\in G_{\codim(F^{\prime})}
, we have

{g^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}}\;g^{F}=g^{F}\;{g^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}}\;[{g^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}},g^{F}]
, where
[{g^{\prime}}^{F^{\prime}},g^{F}]=[g^{\prime},g]^{F^{\prime}\cap F}\in G_{(F^{\prime}\cap F)}
. Using this fact we can obtain the desired rearrangement by first moving all elements of
G_{(F_{0})}
to the left of the product, then using the closure of this group to combine them as a single element
g_{0}^{F_{0}}
; next we move to the left all
G_{(F_{1})}
elements in the remaining product, to put them next to
g_{0}^{F_{0}}
, and combine these to obtain
g_{1}^{F_{1}}
. Continuing this way yields the claimed factorization.

Uniqueness follows from (
5
). Indeed, note first that
\supp(v_{i})\supset\supp(v)
implies
v_{i}\geq v
. This implies that for any upper face
F(v_{i})
, the only other upper faces
F(v)
containing
v_{i}
are those such that
\supp(v)\subset\supp(v_{i})
and
v<v_{i}
. From (
5
) we can therefore determine the coefficients
g_{i}
by induction as follows:

g_{0}=\q(v_{0}),\text{ and for each }i>0,\;g_{i}=\Big(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<i:\;\\
\supp(v_{j})\subset\supp(v_{i})\end{subarray}}g_{j}^{F_{j}}\Big)^{-1}\cdot\q\,(v_{i}),

(6)

where the order from left to right in the product is increasing in the colex order of
v_{j}
.
∎

For
j\in[n]
, let
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}:=\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:|v|\leq j\}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

If
G_{\bullet}
has degree
d
, then for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
, every value
\q(v)
with
|v|>d
is a word in the values
\q(v)
with
|v|\leq d
. In particular, the map
\q
is entirely determined by its restriction to
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,d}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{i}
be an upper face with
\codim(F_{i})=|v_{i}|\leq d
. By (
6
),
g_{i}
is a word in the values
\q(v_{j})
with
v_{j}\leq v_{i}
with
\supp(v_{j})\subset\supp(v_{i})
. On the other hand any
g_{i}
with
\codim(F_{i})>d
is trivial by assumption. Hence we are done, by uniqueness of (
5
).
∎

This corollary will be used to show that
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
satisfies the corner-completion axiom. The following result shows that these cubes also satisfy the composition axiom.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
and let
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
be a morphism. Then
\q\co\phi\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
.

Given a map
g
defined on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, for each
i\in\{0,1\}
we define
g(\cdot,i)
on
\{0,1\}^{n-1}
by

g(v,i)=g((v,i))
, where
(v,i):=\big(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n-1)$},i\big)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let us first assume that
\phi
is injective, thus
\phi
is a bijection
\{0,1\}^{m}\to\tIm(\phi)
. For every
v\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, we have

\q(\phi(v))=\prod_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}g_{i}^{F_{i}}(\phi(v))=\prod_{i:F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)\neq\emptyset}g_{i}^{F_{i}}(\phi(v)).

For each face
F_{i}
such that
F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)\neq\emptyset
, with data
(I_{i},x_{i})
, fix some
y_{i}=\phi(v_{i})\in\tIm(\phi)
such that
{y_{i}}|_{I_{i}}=x_{i}
. Let
\beta
denote the function
J_{\phi}\to[m]
,
j\mapsto i
for all
j\in J_{\phi}(i)
(recall (
1
)). We can then see that
F^{\prime}_{i}:=\phi^{-1}(F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi))
is a face of
\{0,1\}^{m}
of codimension at most
\codim(F_{i})
. Indeed,
F_{i}^{\prime}
is the face with data
I^{\prime}=\beta(J_{\phi}\cap I_{i})
and
x^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}(y_{i})|_{I^{\prime}}={v_{i}}|_{I^{\prime}}
. (To see this, note that
v\in F_{i}^{\prime}
if and only if
\phi(v)\in\tIm(\phi)\cap F_{i}
, if and only if
\phi(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=x_{i}\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}
for each
j\in I_{i}
, and this in turn holds if and only if
\phi(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=y_{i}\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}
for each
j\in J_{\phi}\cap I_{i}
. Thus we have
v\in F_{i}^{\prime}
if and only if
\phi(v)|_{J_{\phi}\cap I_{i}}=\phi(v_{i})|_{J_{\phi}\cap I_{i}}
, and this holds if and only if
v|_{I^{\prime}}=v_{i}|_{I^{\prime}}
.)

Therefore we have

\q(\phi(v))=\prod_{i:F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)\neq\emptyset}g_{i}^{F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)}(\phi(v))=\prod_{i:F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)\neq\emptyset}g_{i}^{\phi^{-1}(F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi))}(v)=\prod_{i:F_{i}\cap\tIm(\phi)\neq\emptyset}g_{i}^{F_{i}^{\prime}}(v),

hence
\q\co\phi
is a product of face-group elements and is therefore in
\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
.

Now suppose that there is a set
S\subset[m]
of indices of coordinates of
v
on which
\phi(v)
does not depend. We show by induction on
|S|
that
\q\co\phi\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
. The case
|S|=0
corresponds to
\phi
being injective. If
|S|>0
, fix any
s\in S
, and note that it suffices to show that
\q\co\phi\co\theta\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
for the automorphism
\theta
permuting coordinate-indices
s
and
m
, so we may assume that
s=m
. Thus
g^{\prime}:=\q\co\phi
is a map
\{0,1\}^{m}\to G
such that
g^{\prime}(\cdot,0)=g^{\prime}(\cdot,1)
is an element of
\cu^{m-1}(G_{\bullet})
(by induction), with coefficients
g_{i}
. We claim that
g^{\prime}\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
. In fact,
g^{\prime}
can be checked to be the
m
-cube with the following coefficients: if
v_{i}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
has
v_{i}^{\prime}\scalebox{0.8}{$(m)$}=0
, then
g_{i}^{\prime}
equals the coefficient
g_{j}
corresponding to
v_{j}=(v_{i}^{\prime}\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},v_{i}^{\prime}\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$},\ldots,v_{i}^{\prime}\scalebox{0.8}{$(m-1)$})
, and otherwise
g_{i}^{\prime}=\mathrm{id}_{G}
.
∎

We can now give the main result of this subsection.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group. Then
G
together with
(\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet}))_{n\geq 0}
is a nilspace. It is a
k
-step nilspace if and only if
\deg(G_{\bullet})\leq k
.

Proof.

Ergodicity holds if and only if the first two terms of
G_{\bullet}
are
G_{0}=G_{1}=G
. The composition axiom follows from Lemma
4.7
. To prove the completion axiom, we argue by induction on
d=\deg(G_{\bullet})
. For
d=1
the group
G
is abelian and completion was proved for Proposition
3.3
. Let
d>1
and let
G^{\prime}=G/G_{d}
, with filtration
G^{\prime}_{\bullet}=(G_{i}/G_{d})
of degree at most
d-1
. By induction
\cu^{n}(G^{\prime}_{\bullet})
satisfies the completion axiom. Suppose that
\q^{\prime}
is an
n
-corner on
G
. Then
\pi_{d}\co\q^{\prime}
is clearly an
n
-corner on
G/G_{d}
, so it has a completion, which by surjectivity of
\q\mapsto\pi_{d}\co\q
equals
\pi_{d}\co\tilde{\q}
for some
\tilde{\q}\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
. We shall now produce a sequence
\q_{0},\q_{1},\ldots,\q_{d}
of elements of
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
such that
\q_{j}
agrees with
\q^{\prime}
on all of
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
.

First, we obtain
\q_{0}
such that
\q_{0}(0^{n})=\q^{\prime}(0^{n})
and
\pi_{d}\co\q_{0}=\pi_{d}\co\q^{\prime}
(just left-multiplying every entry of
\tilde{\q}
by
\q(0^{n})\,\tilde{\q}(0^{n})^{-1}\in G_{d}
). Now for
1\leq j\leq d
suppose that a cube
\q_{j-1}
has been constructed such that
\pi_{d}\co\q_{j-1}=\pi_{d}\co\q^{\prime}
and
\q_{j-1}(v)=\q^{\prime}(v)
for all
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}_{<\,j}
. We shall obtain
\q_{j}
by correcting the differences between
\q_{j-1}
and
\q^{\prime}
at elements
v
with
|v|=j
. Note that
\pi_{d}\co\q_{j-1}=\pi_{d}\co\q^{\prime}
implies that these discrepancies involve only elements of
G_{d}
, that is for any such
v
there is
g_{v}\in G_{d}\subset G_{j}
such that
\q_{j-1}(v)g_{v}=\q(v)
. Letting
F(v)
be the corresponding upper face (of codimension
j
), we therefore have
g_{v}^{F(v)}\in G_{(F(v))}
. We define
\q_{j}=\q_{j-1}\cdot\prod_{v:|v|=j}g_{v}^{F(v)}
. This lies in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
, and agrees with
\q
on
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
as required (note that each
F(v)
intersects
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
only at
v
).

We have thus obtained a cube
\q_{d}\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
that agrees with
\q^{\prime}
on all of
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,d}
. This agreement is now easily extended to every
(n-1)
-face containing
0^{n}
(and thus to all of
\{0,1\}^{n}\setminus\{1^{n}\}
) applying composition and Corollary
4.6
.
∎

The general nilspace structure described in Proposition
4.8
is the one that we shall consider by default on a filtered group. Thus, from now on, given such a group
(G,G_{\bullet})
, by “a cube on
G
” we shall always mean an element of
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
for some
n\geq 0
. There is, however, a slightly more general version of this cube structure, that can also be useful.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group, let
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}
be non-negative integers, and let
F=F(v)
be an upper face in
\{0,1\}^{n}
. We denote by
G_{(F)}^{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}
the subgroup of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
consisting of elements
g^{F}
where
g\in G_{\sum_{j\in\supp v}i_{j}}
. We denote by
\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
the subgroup of
G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
generated by the groups
G_{(F)}^{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}
.

This notion appeared already in
[
7
, Definition B.2]
. (Note that
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})=\cu^{n}_{(1,\dots,1)}(G_{\bullet})
.) Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 4.10
.

An element
\q\in G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
lies in
\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if it has a unique factorization of the form

\q=g_{0}^{F_{0}}g_{1}^{F_{1}}\cdots g_{2^{n}-1}^{F_{2^{n}-1}},

(7)

with
g_{i}\in G_{\sum_{j\in\supp v_{i}}i_{j}}

\forall\,i
.

We now wish to complete the picture concerning the cube structures
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
by showing that they satisfy an analogue of Proposition
3.2
. We have already done this for part
(i)
of that proposition (in Lemma
4.5
). For part
(ii)
, we need an appropriate analogue of an affine homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to G
. A suitable notion turns out to be that of a
polynomial map
from
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to
G
. This motivates the discussion of general polynomial maps between filtered groups, given in the next subsection. We shall see in particular that these maps are precisely the morphisms that preserve the nilspace structures formed by these cubes.

4.2
Nilspace morphisms between filtered groups: polynomial maps

Definition 4.11

(Nilspace morphism)
.

Let
\ns,\nss
be nilspaces. A map
g:\ns\to\nss
is a
nilspace morphism
if for every
n\geq 0
, for every cube
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
we have
g\co\q\in\cu^{n}(\nss)
. We denote the set of these morphisms by
\hom(\ns,\nss)
.

In the case of two filtered groups
(G,G_{\bullet})
,
(H,H_{\bullet})
we shall denote the set of morphisms between the corresponding nilspaces by
\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
.

Definition 4.12

(Polynomial maps)
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
and
(H,H_{\bullet})
be filtered groups. Given a map
g:H\to G
, and
h\in H
, we define the map
\partial_{h}g:H\to G
by
\partial_{h}g(x)=g(x)^{-1}g(xh)
.
We say that
g
is a
polynomial map
(adapted to
H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet}
) if for every sequence of non-negative integers
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}
and elements
h_{j}\in H_{i_{j}}
,
j\in[n]
, we have
\partial_{h_{1}}\partial_{h_{2}}\cdots\partial_{h_{n}}g(x)\in G_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}}
for all
x\in H
. The set of such maps is denoted by
\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
.

Example 4.13
.

For
H=\mathbb{Z}
with the lower central series, the corresponding set of polynomial maps is denoted by
\poly(\mathbb{Z},G_{\bullet})
and its elements are called
polynomial sequences
on
G
(one can add ‘adapted to
G_{\bullet}
’ when the filtration needs to be specified)
[
6
,
16
]
. If
H
is an abelian group with lower central series
H_{\bullet}
, and
G
is abelian with
G_{\bullet}
being the degree
d
filtration
G_{0}=\cdots=G_{d}=G
,
G_{d+1}=\{\mathrm{id}_{G}\}
, then
\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
is the set of maps
g
such that for every
x\in H,h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d+1})\in H^{d+1}
we have
\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}}(-1)^{|v|}g(x+v\cdot h)=0
. For
H=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
these are the ‘globally polynomial phase functions’, familiar in arithmetic combinatorics; see for instance
[
4
, §3]
.

The main result of this section is the following important fact.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
,
(H,H_{\bullet})
be filtered groups. Then

\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})=\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet}).

(8)

This result yields a functor from the category of filtered groups with polynomial maps to the category of nilspaces with nilspace morphisms, namely the functor that sends a filtered group
(G,G_{\bullet})
to the nilspace
\big(G,(\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet}))_{n\geq 0}\big)
and sends a map in
\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
to the same map viewed as a morphism in
\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
. (This functor is forgetful; see Remark
6.2
.)

Theorem
4.14
has the following remarkable consequence.

Corollary 4.15
.

For any filtered groups
(G,G_{\bullet})
,
(H,H_{\bullet})
, the set
\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
with pointwise multiplication is a group.

This corollary follows from (
8
) together with the fact that
\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
is a group under pointwise multiplication, which is a special case of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace and let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group. Then
\hom(\ns,G_{\bullet})
is a group under pointwise multiplication.

This lemma follows clearly from the fact that the cubes
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
form a group.

Corollary
4.15
was first established for polynomial sequences. This special case is known as the Lazard-Leibman theorem, it was proved by Leibman in
[
16
]
, and earlier, for Lie groups, by Lazard
[
15
]
. The more general case of polynomial maps between groups was then proved by Leibman in
[
17
]
. These original proofs did not go via the charaterization of polynomial maps as nilspace morphisms, as we do here. A special case of this characterization appeared in
[
6
, Proposition 6.5]
, and the general version appeared (with slightly different language) in
[
7
, Theorem B.3]
.

Another nice consequence of Theorem
4.14
is the following result, which gives the desired generalization of part (ii) of Proposition
3.2
.

Corollary 4.17
.

A map
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
is in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if it extends to a polynomial map
g\in\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
.

Note that throughout this section the implicit filtration on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is just the lower central series
G_{0}=G_{1}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
G_{2}=\{0^{n}\}
. Let us establish this corollary before turning to the proof of the theorem.

Proof of Corollary
4.17
.

The ‘if’ direction is the easiest. Note first that the map
\theta
that embeds
\{0,1\}^{n}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
in the natural way is trivially in
\cu^{n}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.
Hence if
\q=g\co\theta
for
g\in\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
, then, since by the theorem
g
is a morphism of cubes, we have
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
.

For the ‘only if’ direction, given an
n
-cube
\q
, we write the unique factorization

\q(v)=g_{0}^{F_{0}}(v)\;g_{1}^{F_{1}}(v)\;\cdots\;g_{2^{n}-1}^{F_{2^{n}-1}}(v),

and we then define the map
g:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to G
by

g(\mathbf{t})=g(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}g_{j}^{\binom{\mathbf{t}}{v_{j}}},

where
\binom{\mathbf{t}}{v_{j}}:=\binom{t_{1}}{v_{j}\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}}\binom{t_{2}}{v_{j}\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}}\cdots\binom{t_{n}}{v_{j}\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}}
, and where
v_{j}
is the element determining the upper face
F_{j}=F(v_{j})
(recall Definition
4.4
). Note that the restriction of
g
to
\{0,1\}^{n}
equals
\q
. Observe also that each map
\mathbf{t}\mapsto g_{j}^{\binom{\mathbf{t}}{v_{j}}}
is in
\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
(this is easily seen from the definition) and so, by the group property for
\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
(which follows from Theorem
4.14
) we have
g\in\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
as well.
∎

To prove Theorem
4.14
, we shall use the following construction, which will also play a crucial role in the next chapter.

Definition 4.18

(Arrows)
.

Let
\ns
be a set, let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let us denote elements of
\{0,1\}^{n+k}
by pairs
(v,w)
,
v\in\{0,1\}^{n},w\in\{0,1\}^{k}
. Given two maps
\q_{0},\q_{1}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\ns
, we define the
n
-dimensional
k
-arrow
, denoted by
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
, to be the following map:

\displaystyle\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}\quad:\quad\{0,1\}^{n+k}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\quad\ns

\displaystyle(v,w)\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\q_{0}(v),&w\neq 1^{k}\\
\q_{1}(v),&w=1^{k}\end{array}\right..

Usually, the dimension of the cubes
\q_{0},\q_{1}
(and hence of the arrow
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
) will be clear from the context, and we shall then call
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
just ‘the
k
-arrow of
\q_{0},\q_{1}
’.

We shall see in Chapter
Notes on nilspaces: algebraic aspects
that arrows yield a useful general construction of new nilspaces from old ones. For now we shall use arrows just for cubes on filtered groups.

The following result provides a convenient way to check whether an arrow on a filtered group is itself a cube. Given a filtration
G_{\bullet}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
G_{\bullet}^{+\ell}
the shifted filtration, with
i
-th term
G_{i+\ell}
.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group, let
\q_{0},\q_{1}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
, and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
lies in
\cu^{n+k}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n+k})}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if
\q_{0}\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
and
\q_{0}^{-1}\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet}^{+\ell})
, where
\ell=\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+k}i_{j}
.

Proof.

If
f=\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
is in
\cu^{n+k}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n+k})}(G_{\bullet})
then by (
7
) we have that
\q_{0}\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
. Moreover, from (
7
) and the definition of
f
we see that every coefficient
g_{(v,w)}
of
f
corresponding to an upper face
F((v,w))
of
\{0,1\}^{n+k}
with
w\neq 0^{k},1^{k}
is trivial. It follows that for
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we have

\q_{0}^{-1}(v)\q_{1}(v)=f(v,0^{k})^{-1}\,f(v,1^{k})=\prod_{u\in\{0,1\}^{n}}g_{(u,1^{k})}^{F((u,1^{k}))}(v,1^{k}),

where
g_{(u,1^{k})}\in G_{\sum_{j\in\supp u}i_{j}+\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+k}i_{j}}
, so
\q_{0}^{-1}\,\q_{1}
is indeed in
\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet}^{+\ell})
as claimed.

For the converse, note that the factorizations for
\q_{0}
and
\q_{0}^{-1}\,\q_{1}
given by (
7
) combine to give a factorization of the form (
7
) for
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
4.14
in two steps.

Lemma 4.20
.

We have
\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})\subset\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
.

Proof.

We have to show that for every
g\in\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
, for every integer
n\geq 0
, integers
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\geq 0
and elements
h_{j}\in H_{i_{j}}
,
j\in[n]
, we have
\partial_{h_{1}}\cdots\partial_{h_{n}}g(x)\in G_{i_{1}+\cdots+i_{n}}
for all
x\in H
. This is clear for
n=0
. For
n>0
, by induction it suffices to show that for every
i\geq 0
and
h\in H_{i}
we have
\partial_{h}g\in\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet}^{+i})
. Given any
\q\in\cu^{m}(H_{\bullet})
, we have by Lemma
4.19
that
\langle\q,\q\cdot h\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{m+i}(H_{\bullet})
. We then have
g\co\langle\q,\q\cdot h\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{m+i}(G_{\bullet})
. But
g\co\langle\q,\q\cdot h\rangle_{i}=\langle g\co\q,g\co(\q\cdot h)\rangle_{i}
, and then this being in
\cu^{m+i}(G_{\bullet})
and
g\co\q
being in
\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
implies by Lemma
4.19
that
(g\co\q)^{-1}\,\cdot\,g\co(\q\cdot h)\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet}^{+i})
. Since
(g\co\q)^{-1}\,\cdot\,g\co(\q\cdot h)=(\partial_{h}g)\co\q
, the proof is complete.
∎

We now prove a result which implies the opposite inclusion
\hom(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})\supset\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
.

Lemma 4.21
.

For every integer
n\geq 0
the following holds. Given any filtered groups
(G,G_{\bullet})
,
(H,H_{\bullet})
and any map
g\in\poly(H_{\bullet},G_{\bullet})
, for any
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\geq 0
and any
\q\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(H_{\bullet})
we have
g\co\q\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
.

Thus polynomial maps conserve in fact the more general cube structures from Definition
4.9
. Note that the last two lemmas combined tell us that nilspace morphisms also conserve these structures.

Proof.

(We follow an argument from
[
7
, Appendix B]
.) We argue again by induction on
n
. Given
n>0
let
\q\in\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(H_{\bullet})
and note that, letting
\q_{0}:=\q(\cdot,0),\q_{1}:=\q(\cdot,1)
, by Lemma
4.19
we have
\q_{0}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet})
and
\q_{0}^{-1}\q_{1}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
. We want to show that
g\co\q
lies in
\cu^{n}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})}(G_{\bullet})
. Since
(g\co\q)(\cdot,t)=g\co\q_{t}
for
t=0,1
, and by induction
g\co\q_{0}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(G_{\bullet})
, by Lemma
4.19
it suffices to show that
(g\co\q_{0})^{-1}g\co\q_{1}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
.

Now
\q_{0}^{-1}\q_{1}=h_{1}^{F_{1}}h_{2}^{F_{2}}\cdots h_{t}^{F_{t}}
for upper-face group elements
h_{j}^{F_{j}}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
. Moreover, note that
(g\co\q_{0})^{-1}g\co\q_{1}
has the telescoping expansion

\displaystyle(g\co\q_{0})^{-1}\;\;g\co\big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}\big)\;g\co\big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}\big)^{-1}\;\;g\co\Big(\big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}\big)h_{2}^{F_{1}}\Big)\;g\co\Big(\big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}\big)h_{2}^{F_{1}}\Big)^{-1}\cdots

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\cdots g\co\Big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}h_{2}^{F_{2}}\cdots h_{t-1}^{F_{t-1}}\Big)\;g\co\Big(\q_{0}h_{1}^{F_{1}}h_{2}^{F_{2}}\cdots h_{t-1}^{F_{t-1}}\Big)^{-1}g\co\q_{1}.

Therefore, it suffices to show that for every cube
\q\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet})
and every upper-face-group element
h^{F}\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
, we have
(g\co\q)^{-1}g\co(\q h^{F})\in\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
.

Let
f(v):=(g\co\q)^{-1}g\co(\q h^{F})(v)
, and note that this equals
\partial_{h}g(\q(v))
for
v\in F
and
\mathrm{id}_{G}
otherwise. Let
w\in\{0,1\}^{n-1}
be the element with support
I
of size
k
such that
F=F(w)
. By composing with an automorphism (permuting the indices
i_{j}
accordingly) we can assume that
I=[n-k,n-1]
and then
f
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional arrow
\langle\mathrm{id}_{G},(\partial_{h}g)\co\q\rangle_{k}
.
By Lemma
4.19
,
f
is therefore in
\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
if and only if
(\partial_{h}g)\co\q
restricted to
\{0,1\}^{n-1-k}
lies in
\cu^{n-1-k}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1-k})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}+\sum_{j\in I}i_{j}})
. Since
h\in G_{\sum_{j\in I}i_{j}}
, we have that
\partial_{h}g
is a
G_{\sum_{j\in I}i_{j}}
-valued polynomial map on
H
, so by the induction hypothesis
\partial_{h}g
maps
\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(H_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
into
\cu^{n-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}+\sum_{j\in I}i_{j}})
under composition. In particular, since
\q
restricted to
\{0,1\}^{n-1-k}
lies in
\cu^{n-k-1}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1})|_{[n-1]\setminus I}}(H_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}})
, we have
(\partial_{h}g)\co\q|_{\{0,1\}^{n-1-k}}\in\cu^{n-1-k}_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-1-k})}(G_{\bullet}^{+i_{n}+\sum_{j\in I}i_{j}})
as required.
∎

In the next subsection we complete the generalization of Proposition
3.2
by giving the analogue of part
(iii)
. This analogue will consist in a characterization of cubes on filtered groups in terms of certain equations that generalize (
4
). Recall that Corollary
4.6
tells us that if
G
has degree
d
then the value of every
(d+1)
-cube at every point
v\in\{0,1\}^{d+1}
is a word in the values at the other points. The equations just mentioned will tell us explicitly what this word is. This third characterization of cubes on filtered groups will be very convenient to describe an important type of nilspace structure on an abelian group, namely the so-called
degree-
k
structure
(see Subsection
4.4
).

4.3
Defining cubes on a filtered group in terms of equations

We shall use the following generalization of the function
\sigma_{2}
from Definition
3.1
.

Definition 4.22
.

Let
G
be a group. We define the function
\sigma_{n}:G^{\{0,1\}^{n}}\to G
recursively, for each integer
n\geq 0
, as follows. Let
\sigma_{0}(g):=g
for any
g\in G=G^{\{0,1\}^{0}}
, and for
n>0
, given
g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
, let

\sigma_{n}(g):=\sigma_{n-1}\big(g(\cdot,1)\big)^{-1}\;\sigma_{n-1}\big(g(\cdot,0)\big).

(10)

We have
\sigma_{1}(g)=g_{1}^{-1}\,g_{0}
,
\sigma_{2}(g)=g_{01}^{-1}\;g_{11}\;g_{10}^{-1}\;g_{00}
, and so on. Expanding the product
\sigma_{n}(g)
and reading right to left (say), we see that it is an alternating product taken along a Gray code (or reflective binary code) on the discrete
n
-cube.

Definition 4.23
.

The
Gray order

\gamma_{n}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to[0,2^{n}-1]
is defined by induction on
n
as follows. For
n=1
we set
\gamma_{1}(0)=0,\gamma_{1}(1)=1
. For
n>1
we set

\gamma_{n}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$})=\gamma_{n-1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n-1)$})
if
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}=0
, and if
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}=1
we set
\gamma_{n}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$})=2^{n}-\gamma_{n-1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},\ldots,v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n-1)$})
.

We note the expression
\sigma_{n}(g)=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}g(\gamma_{n}^{-1}(j))^{(-1)^{j}}
. We will have more use below for expression (
10
), which is helpful in some inductive arguments. In fact, that expression leads to a third viewpoint on
\sigma_{n}
described in the following lemma, which will be crucial below.

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
G
be a group and let
g:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to G
. Then

\sigma_{n}(g)=\partial_{e_{n}}\partial_{e_{n-1}}\cdots\partial_{e_{1}}g(0^{n}).

(11)

Here
\{e_{i}:i\in[n]\}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

For
n=1
we clearly have
\sigma_{1}(g)=\partial_{e_{1}}g(0)
. Suppose that
n>1
, suppose the claim holds for
m<n
and let
g:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to G
. Then by induction
g(\cdot,0)
and
g(\cdot,1)
satisfy

\sigma_{n-1}(g(\cdot,i))=\partial_{e_{n-1}}\cdots\partial_{e_{1}}g(0^{n-1},i),\quad i=0,1.

By (
10
) we then have

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{n-1}(g(\cdot,1))^{-1}\;\sigma_{n-1}(g(\cdot,0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{e_{n-1}}\cdots\partial_{e_{1}}g(0^{n-1},1)\;\big)^{-1}\;\partial_{e_{n-1}}\cdots\partial_{e_{1}}g(0^{n-1},0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{e_{n}}\partial_{e_{n-1}}\cdots\partial_{e_{1}}g(0^{n}).

∎

Note that the increasing sequence of elements of
\{0,1\}^{n}
taken in the Gray order gives a Hamiltonian path in the graph of 1-faces on this cube, starting from
0^{n}
.

The main result of this section is the following.

Proposition 4.25
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group. We have
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if for every
m
-face map
\phi
into
\{0,1\}^{n}
we have
\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)\in G_{m}
.

Thus, the
n
-cubes on
(G,G_{\bullet})
are the maps
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
that satisfy the equation

\pi_{m}\co\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)=\mathrm{id}

for every face map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.

We split the proof of Proposition
4.25
into two parts. Firstly, we establish the following stronger statement in one direction.

Lemma 4.26
.

If
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
then for every morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
we have
\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)\in G_{m}
.

Proof.

Lemma
4.7
implies
\q\co\phi\in\cu^{m}(G_{\bullet})
. Corollary
4.17
gives
g\in\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{m},G)
such that
\q\co\phi(v)=g(v)
for all
v\in\{0,1\}^{m}
. The result then follows from (
11
) and the definition of
\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{m},G_{\bullet})
.
∎

Secondly, we have the following result in the converse direction.

Lemma 4.27
.

Suppose that
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
satisfies
\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)\in G_{m}
for every
m
-face map
\phi
into
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on the degree
d
of
G_{\bullet}
. For
d=1
,
G
is abelian and the claim follows from (the proof of) Proposition
3.2
, so let
d>1
and suppose that the lemma holds for every filtered group of degree less than
d
. Let
G^{\prime}=G/G_{d}
, and let
G^{\prime}_{\bullet}=(G_{i}/G_{d})_{i\geq 0}
, a filtration of degree at most
d-1
. Suppose that
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
satisfies the premise of the lemma. Then
\pi_{d}\co\q
satisfies the premise in
(G^{\prime},G^{\prime}_{\bullet})
, so
\pi_{d}\co\q\in\cu^{n}(G^{\prime}_{\bullet})
.

Note that
\q\mapsto\pi_{d}\co\q
is a surjection
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})\to\cu^{n}(G^{\prime}_{\bullet})
(this follows from the surjectivity of
\pi_{d}:G\to G^{\prime}
combined with (
5
), or just with Definition
4.3
). Hence there exists
\q^{\prime}\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
such that
\pi_{d}\co\q=\pi_{d}\co\q^{\prime}
. We shall now produce a sequence
\q_{0},\q_{1},\ldots,\q_{d}
of elements of
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
such that
\q_{j}
agrees with
\q
on all of
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
.

First, we obtain
\q_{0}
such that
\q_{0}(0^{n})=\q(0^{n})
and
\pi_{d}\co\q_{0}=\pi_{d}\co\q
, by left-multiplying every entry of
\q^{\prime}
by
\q(0^{n})\,\q^{\prime}(0^{n})^{-1}\in G_{d}
. Now for
1\leq j\leq d
suppose that
\q_{j-1}
has been constructed such that
\pi_{d}\co\q_{j-1}=\pi_{d}\co\q
and
\q_{j-1}(v)=\q(v)
for all
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq j-1}
. We shall obtain
\q_{j}
by correcting the discrepancies between
\q_{j-1}
and
\q
at elements
v
with
|v|=j
. Note that
\pi_{d}\co\q_{j-1}=\pi_{d}\co\q
implies that these discrepancies involve only elements of
G_{d}
, that is for any such
v
there is
g_{v}\in G_{d}\subset G_{j}
such that
\q_{j-1}(v)g_{v}=\q(v)
. Letting
F(v)
be the corresponding upper face (of codimension
j
), we therefore have
g_{v}^{F(v)}\in G_{(F(v))}
. We define
\q_{j}=\q_{j-1}\cdot\prod_{v:|v|=j}g_{v}^{F(v)}
. This lies in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
, and agrees with
\q
on
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
as required (note that each
F(v)
intersects
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq\,j}
only at
v
).

We have thus obtained
\q_{d}
. We now claim that the agreement between
\q_{d}
and
\q
extends to all of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and so
\q=\q_{d}\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
. Note that the above process cannot be continued in order to prove this claim, because now the new elements
g_{v}^{F(v)}
that we would multiply by are not guaranteed to lie in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
anymore, since now
\codim(F(v))>d
. However, we have that
\q
and
\q_{d}
both satisfy the premise of the lemma (for
\q_{d}
this follows from Lemma
4.26
) and agree on
\{0,1\}^{n}_{\leq d}
. This can be used to complete the proof, as follows. First let us show that for any
v
with
|v|=d+1
we have
\q(v)=\q_{d}(v)
. Since
|v|=d+1
, we can find a
(d+1)
-face map
\phi
into
\{0,1\}^{n}
with
\phi(0^{d+1})=v
and
|\phi(w)|\leq d
otherwise. Using the premise we then have
\q(v)=\q\co\phi(0^{d+1})=\q\co\phi(0^{d+1})\;\sigma_{d+1}(\q\co\phi)^{-1}=\q_{d}\co\phi(0^{d+1})\;\sigma_{d+1}(\q_{d}\co\phi)^{-1}=\q_{d}(v)
. We can now use a similar argument to see that
\q(v)=\q_{d}(v)
for
|v|=d+2
, then for
|v|=d+3
, and so on, concluding finally that
\q=\q_{d}\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
.
∎

Let us record the generalization of Proposition
3.2
that we have finally obtained.

Proposition 4.28
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group, and let
\q:\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
. We have
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
if and only if one of the following equivalent statements holds:

(i)

There is a unique expression
\q=g_{0}^{F_{0}}g_{1}^{F_{1}}\cdots g_{2^{n}-1}^{F_{2^{n}-1}}
with
g_{i}\in G_{\codim(F_{i})}
for each
i
.

(ii)

The map
\q
extends to a polynomial map in
\poly(\mathbb{Z}^{n},G_{\bullet})
.

(iii)

For every
m
-face map
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
we have
\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)\in G_{m}
.

Remark 4.29
.

One may replace “every
m
-face map” in property
(iii)
above by “every morphism”. Indeed, if the property holds with every
m
-face map, then the proposition tells us that
\q
is a cube. But then, given any morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
, by composition
\q\co\phi
is also a cube, so applying property
(iii)
using as an
m
-face map the identity map on
\{0,1\}^{m}
, we deduce that
\sigma_{m}(\q\co\phi)\in G_{m}
as required.

4.4
Cubes of degree
k
on abelian groups

In this section we record a special case of the previous nilspace structures that will play an important role in the sequel. This is a
k
-step nilspace structure defined on an abelian group.

Definition 4.30
.

Let
\ab
be an abelian group. The
degree-
k
structure
on
\ab
, denoted by
\cD_{k}(\ab)
, is the nilspace formed by

Motivation for a general algebraic characterization

Looking back at the examples of nilspaces that we have treated so far, we see that while not all of them have a group structure, on all of them there is an

7.1

Definition 7.1

Definition 7.3

Let

7.2
Nilspaces and parallelepiped structures

Before we continue the development of the theory, in this subsection we pause to illustrate the use of some of the tools from the previous subsection. We shall apply these tools to treat a natural question concerning nilspaces, namely the question of the relation between the nilspace axioms from
[
1
]
and the axioms from
[
12
]
defining parallelepiped structures. In the introduction, we mentioned that the former axioms are a variant of the latter, and here we shall detail this remark.

In
[
12
]
the notion of an abstract parallelepiped structure was introduced. It was defined explicitly only for parallelepipeds of dimension 2 and 3 but, as noted at the end of
[
12
]
, the generalization to higher dimensions is clear. We formulate this generalization below using notation from previous sections. Recall in particular the notation
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}
for
k
-arrows from Definition
4.18
, the notation
\phi_{F}
for the face-restriction maps from Definition
1.5
, and the notation
\aut(\{0,1\}^{n})
for the group of automorphisms of the discrete cube, from Definition
1.1
.

Definition 7.10

(Parallelepiped structures)
.

Let
\ns
be a set and let
n
be a positive integer. We say that a finite sequence
(P_{m})_{m=1}^{n}
of sets
P_{m}\subset\ns^{\{0,1\}^{m}}
is an
n
-parallelepiped structure
on
\ns
if
P_{1}=\ns^{\{0,1\}}
and the following axioms are satisfied for every
m\in[2,n]
:

(i)

(Face restrictions) For every
p\in P_{m}
and every
(m-1)
-face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
, we have
p\co\phi_{F}\in P_{m-1}
.

(ii)

(Symmetries) For every
p\in P_{m}
and every
\theta\in\aut(\{0,1\}^{m})
, we have
p\co\theta\in P_{m}
.

(iii)

(Equivalence relation) The relation
\approx_{P_{m}}
on
P_{m-1}
, defined by
p\approx_{P_{m}}p^{\prime}
if and only if
\langle p,p^{\prime}\rangle_{1}\in P_{m}
, is an equivalence relation.

(iv)

(Closing property) Let
p^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus\{1^{m}\}\to\ns
be such that for every
(m-1)
-face
F
containing
0^{m}
we have
p^{\prime}\co\phi_{F}\in P_{m-1}(\ns)
. Then there exists
p\in P_{m}(\ns)
such that
p(v)=p^{\prime}(v)
for all
v\neq 1^{m}
.

These axioms are the natural generalizations to dimension
n
of the four axioms in
[
12
, Definition 4]
. Note that the fourth axiom above is just the corner-completion axiom from Definition
2.1
stated for the elements of
P_{m}
.

To relate parallelepiped structures to nilspaces, we want to show that the sets
P_{m}
above can be viewed as cube sets forming a nilspace structure on
\ns
. However, note that on a nilspace
\ns
there is a cube set
\cu^{n}(\ns)
defined for
every

n\in\mathbb{N}
, whereas an
n
-parallelepiped structure defines parallelepipeds only of dimension up to
n
. Nevertheless, we can consider what we shall call a
sequence of parallelepiped structures
on a set
\ns
, namely an infinite sequence
(P_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the initial segment
(P_{m})_{m\in[n]}
is an
n
-parallelepiped structure on
\ns
. We then have the following result.

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
\ns
be a set and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
P_{n}\subset\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
. Then
\big(\ns,(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big)
is a nilspace if and only if
(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of parallelepiped structures on
\ns
.

Proof.

To see the forward implication, note first that the composition axiom for the nilspace immediately implies axioms
(i)
and
(ii)
above for each
m
, and trivially implies also axiom
(iv)
since we are assuming the completion axiom. Moreover, axiom
(iii)
above is also satisfied, indeed the reflexivity and symmetry of the relation
\approx_{P_{m}}
are again direct consequences of the composition axiom, and the transitivity is a consequence of the concatenation property (Lemma
7.7
).

To see the backward implication, the only difficulty is in checking that the composition axiom holds, that is, if
p\in P_{n}
and
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is a morphism, then
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
. We check this in several steps as follows.

First let us suppose that
\phi
is an
m
-face map, with image an
m
-face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
. Note that we may then assume that
\phi
is just the restriction map
\phi_{F}
, by axiom
(ii)
above. We now prove that
p\co\phi_{F}\in P_{m}
by induction on
n-m
, as follows. If
n=m+1
then we have
p\co\phi_{F}\in P_{m}
immediately by axiom
(i)
. If
n>m+1
then let
F^{\prime}
be an
(n-1)
-face in
\{0,1\}^{n}
that contains
F
, and note that by axiom
(i)
we have
p\co\phi_{F^{\prime}}\in P_{n-1}
. Since
\phi_{F^{\prime}}
is injective we can take its inverse map
\phi_{F^{\prime}}^{-1}:F^{\prime}\to\{0,1\}^{n-1}
. Note that
\phi_{F^{\prime}}^{-1}\co\phi_{F}
is a face map
\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n-1}
. Hence, by induction, we have that
P_{m}\ni(p\co\phi_{F^{\prime}})\co(\phi_{F^{\prime}}^{-1}\co\phi_{F})=p\co\phi_{F}
, as required.

Our next step is to check that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
if
\phi
is an injective morphism. Thus we still have
m\leq n
, we assume that
|J_{\phi}(i)|\geq 1
for every
i\in[m]
, and we recall that we have equality here for every
i
if and only if
\phi
is an
m
-face map. We shall prove that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
by induction on
|J_{\phi}|=\sum_{i\in[m]}|J_{\phi}(i)|
. The basic case
|J_{\phi}|=m
is clear by the previous paragraph. Suppose then that
|J_{\phi}|>m
. By Lemma
7.9
there is
\theta\in\aut(\{0,1\}^{m})
and morphisms
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
,
k\leq 4
, such that
\phi\co\theta
is the concatenation of
\phi_{1}\prec\cdots\prec\phi_{k}
and
|J_{\phi_{i}}|<|J_{\phi}|
for each
i
. We have by axiom
(ii)
above that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
if and only if
p\co\phi\co\theta\in P_{m}
, so for our purposes we may assume that
\theta
is the identity. Thus
\phi
is the concatenation of
\phi_{1}\prec\cdots\prec\phi_{k}
, and therefore
p\co\phi
is the concatenation of
(p\co\phi_{1})\prec\cdots\prec(p\co\phi_{k})
. By induction, each map
p\co\phi_{i}
is in
P_{m}
. On the other hand, if two maps in
P_{m}
are adjacent, then by the transitivity of the relation
\approx_{P_{m}}
their concatenation is also in
P_{m}
. Applying this to the maps
p\co\phi_{i}
we deduce that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
.

Finally, we check that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
for
every
morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
. Let
\ell=\ell(\phi)
denote the number of indices
i\in[m]
such that
J_{\phi}(i)=\emptyset
. We shall prove that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
by induction on
\ell
. The basic case
\ell=0
is clear by the previous paragraph, since
\phi
is then injective. If
\ell>0
, then
J_{\phi}(i)=\emptyset
for some
i
, and note that we may assume that
i=m
(using an automorphism of
\{0,1\}^{m}
if necessary, as in the previous paragraph). But then letting
F
denote the
(m-1)
-face
\{v_{m}=0\}\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
, we have
\ell(\phi\co\phi_{F})<\ell(\phi)
, so by induction we have
p\co\phi\co\phi_{F}\in P_{m-1}
. Since
\phi(v)
does not depend on
v_{m}
, we also have that
p\co\phi=\langle p\co\phi\co\phi_{F},p\co\phi\co\phi_{F}\rangle_{1}
. Hence, by the reflexivity of
\approx_{P_{m}}
, it follows that
p\co\phi\in P_{m}
.
∎

Remark 7.12
.

In
[
12
]
Host and Kra use the term
strong parallelepiped structure
to refer to an
n
-parallelepiped structure for which we also have that for each
p^{\prime}
satisfying the premise of the closing property, there is a
unique

p
satisfying the conclusion. Parallelepiped structures without this uniqueness in the closing property are called
weak
parallelepiped structures. In light of Proposition
7.11
, we have that
\big(\ns,(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\big)
is a
k
-step nilspace if and only if
(P_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of parallelepiped structures on
\ns
and
(P_{n})_{n=1}^{k+1}
is strong.

As we mentioned in the introduction, in
[
12
]
a relation was established between parallelepiped structures and nilpotent groups. Indeed, Host and Kra showed that for every strong parallelepiped structure of dimension 3 on a set
\ns
there is a group acting on
\ns
by bijections that preserve the parallelepipeds in a certain natural sense, and this group is 2-step nilpotent (see
[
12
, Proposition 12]
). In light of Proposition
7.11
, this result of Host and Kra is applicable to any 2-step nilspace. One of the central ways in which the results in
[
1
]
continue the program started in
[
12
]
is by providing a generalization, for higher-step nilspaces, of this relation to nilpotent groups. This generalization is detailed in Subsection
8.4
below, where we treat the
translation group
of a nilspace.

7.3
Tricubes and the tricube composition

Here we shall describe another operation that gives a very useful construction of new cubes on a nilspace. To this end we shall use special examples of cubespaces, called tricubes. These objects play an important role in the theory. Their associated operation, called the tricube composition, can be viewed in a certain sense as an analogue, for cubes on nilspaces, of the convolution operation for functions.

Definition 7.13
.

The
tricube
of dimension
n
is the set
T_{n}=\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
together with the following cubespace structure. First we define, for each
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, the map

\trem_{v}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to T_{n},\quad\trem_{v}\big(w\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$},w\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$},\dots,w\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}\big)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=(2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}-1)(1-w\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}).

(15)

Thus
\trem_{v}
is an injective map embedding
\{0,1\}^{n}
into
T_{n}
as the subcube with base point
\trem_{v}(0^{n})
equal to the ‘outer point’
(2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}-1,2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}-1,\ldots,2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}-1)
of
T_{n}
, and with
\trem_{v}(1^{n})=0^{n}
. A map
\q:\{0,1\}^{m}\to T_{n}
is declared to be a cube if there exists a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
and some element
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\q=\trem_{v}\co\phi
.

Thus an
m
-cube on
T_{n}
is just a map
\{0,1\}^{m}\to T_{n}
that factors as a cube-morphism through one of the embeddings
\trem_{v}
. Note that this cubespace structure on
T_{n}
is the smallest one such that all the maps
\trem_{v}
are cubes. Note also the following basic fact.

Lemma 7.14
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the cubespace
T_{n}
is the product cubespace
(T_{1})^{n}
.

Given two maps
f_{1},f_{2}:S\to\ns
we write
f_{1}\times f_{2}
for the map
S\to\ns\times\ns
,
s\mapsto(f_{1}(s),f_{2}(s))
.

Proof.

Let
k_{0},k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, let
n=k_{0}+k_{1}
, and note that for every
v_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{k_{0}},v_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{k_{1}}
we have from (
15
) that
\trem_{v_{0}}\times\trem_{v_{1}}(w)=\trem_{(v_{0},v_{1})}(w)
,
w\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Every map
\q:\{0,1\}^{m}\to T_{n}
can be written
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
for
\q_{i}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to T_{k_{i}}
. Then we have
\q\in\cu^{m}(T_{n})
if and only if for every
v=(v_{0},v_{1})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
there is a morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\q=\trem_{v}\co\phi
. But
\phi
can also be written
\phi_{0}\times\phi_{1}
and is a morphism if and only if each
\phi_{i}
is, whence
\q
is an
m
-cube on
T_{n}
if and only if each map
\q_{i}\in\cu^{m}(T_{k_{i}})
.
∎

To form a new cube on a nilspace
\ns
using
T_{n}
, we shall typically take
2^{n}
cubes
\q_{v}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, one for each
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and take the morphism
\phi:T_{n}\to\ns
defined as follows: for each
u\in T_{n}
, we take any embedding
\trem_{v}
containing
u
in its image, and then we let
\phi(u)=\q_{v}(\trem_{v}^{-1}(u))
. Note that this is a well-defined map provided that the cubes
\q_{v}
can indeed be “glued together into
T_{n}
”, that is for every
v,v^{\prime}
the maps
\q_{v}\co\trem_{v}^{-1},\q_{v^{\prime}}\co\trem_{v^{\prime}}^{-1}
agree on the intersection of the images of
\trem_{v},\trem_{v^{\prime}}
.
Note that if
\phi
is thus well-defined then it is also a cubespace morphism. Given all this, the new cube on
\ns
is then obtained by taking the values of
\phi
on the ‘outer points’ of
T_{n}
, i.e. the elements of
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, via the following map.

Definition 7.15

(Outer-point map)
.

We denote by
\omega_{n}
the embedding
\{0,1\}^{n}\to T_{n}
defined by
\omega_{n}(v)=\trem_{v}(0^{n})=(2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}-1,\ldots,2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}-1)
.

The new cube mentioned above is thus
\phi\co\omega_{n}
. That this map is indeed a cube is a nontrivial fact, which we record as follows.

Lemma 7.16

(Tricube composition)
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace and let
f:T_{n}\to\ns
be a morphism. Then the composition
f\co\omega_{n}
is in
\cu^{n}(\ns)
.

The point of this result lies in that
\omega_{n}
is not a cube on
T_{n}
(if it were a cube then the result would be trivial, since
f
is a morphism). The idea of the proof is that, while the cube structure on
T_{n}
is not rich enough for
\omega_{n}
to be in
\cu^{n}(T_{n})
, there is an injective morphism
q:T_{n}\to\{0,1\}^{2n}
such that, firstly,
q\co\omega_{n}
is a morphism of discrete cubes, and secondly
q(T_{n})
has the extension property, so that
f\co q^{-1}
extends to a cube in
\cu^{2n}(\ns)
, and therefore
f\co\omega_{n}=f\co q^{-1}\co q\co\omega_{n}
is a cube on
\ns
. Let us detail this further.

First we describe the embedding of
T_{n}
, which will also be useful later on.

Lemma 7.17
.

Let
\lambda:\{-1,0,1\}\to\{0,1\}^{2}
be the function
\lambda(1)=(1,0)
,
\lambda(0)=(0,0)
,
\lambda(-1)=(0,1)
. Then
q=\lambda^{n}:v\mapsto\big(\lambda(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}),\ldots,\lambda(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$})\big)
is an injective morphism
T_{n}\to\{0,1\}^{2n}
. Moreover,
q(T_{n})
is simplicial in
\{0,1\}^{2n}
.

Proof.

The map
\lambda
is easily checked to be a morphism, and it is also easily checked that products of morphisms are morphisms relative to the product cubespace structure, whence
q
is indeed a morphism. To see that
q(T_{n})
is simplicial, suppose that
w=q(v)\in q(T_{n})
and
u\in\{0,1\}^{2n}
has
\supp u\subset\supp w
. Let
v^{\prime}
be obtained from
v
by switching to 0 every entry
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}
such that the indices corresponding to
\lambda(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$})
are not in the support of
u
, i.e.
\{2j-1,2j\}\not\subset\supp u
. Then
u=q(v^{\prime})
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
7.16
.

Since
q(T_{n})
is simplicial in
\{0,1\}^{2n}
and
f\co q^{-1}:q(T_{n})\to\ns
is a morphism (relative to
q(T_{n})
as a subcubespace of
\{0,1\}^{2n}
), by Lemma
7.5
the map
f\co q^{-1}
extends to a cube
\q:\{0,1\}^{2n}\to\ns
. Since
f\co\omega_{n}=\q\co(q\co\omega_{n})
, it now suffices to show that
q\co\omega_{n}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{2n}
is a morphism. To see this, note that
\lambda
restricted to
\{-1,1\}
equals the map
x\mapsto(\frac{1+x}{2},\frac{1-x}{2})
. For each
j\in[n]
, the
j
-th coordinate of
\omega_{n}(v)
is
2v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}-1
, whence
\lambda(\omega_{n}(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$})=(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$},1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$})=(\tau_{0}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}),\tau_{1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}))
, where
\tau_{0},\tau_{1}
are the identity and reflection introduced before Lemma
1.2
. It follows that
q\co\omega_{n}
is a morphism
\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}^{2n}
, indeed we have

q\co\omega_{n}(v)=\Big(\,\big(\tau_{0}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$}),\tau_{1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(1)$})\big)\,,\,\big(\tau_{0}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$}),\tau_{1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(2)$})\big)\,,\ldots,\,\big(\tau_{0}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$}),\tau_{1}(v\scalebox{0.8}{$(n)$})\big)\,\Big).\qed

Remark 7.18
.

Given Lemma
7.17
, we may give an alternative definition of tricubes. More precisely, if we identify
\{0,1\}^{2n}
with
(\{0,1\}^{2})^{n}
the natural way, then thanks to the embedding
q
we may redefine
T_{n}
as the subcubespace of
\{0,1\}^{2n}
of the form
\{(1,0),(0,0),(0,1)\}^{n}
. This alternative definition would be natural in the sense that the cubespace structure on the tricube is easily described as the restriction of the one on
\{0,1\}^{2n}
. In particular, the proof of Lemma
7.16
itself relies essentially on this identification of
T_{n}
with a simplicial subcubespace of
\{0,1\}^{2n}
. However, the cube structure on
T_{n}
as described in Definition
7.13
is also intuitively clear and, in addition, we shall make much more use in the sequel of the intuition and notation related to viewing
T_{n}
as
\{-1,0,1\}^{n}
. There may be one way in which this view could be misleading, namely, that one could think that morphisms
T_{n}\to\ns
extend automatically to morphisms from more general subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
into
\ns
; let us emphasize that this is not the case.

7.4
Arrow spaces

Recall from Definition
4.18
that for two maps
f_{0},f_{1}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\ns
, the corresponding
k
-arrow is the map

\langle f_{0},f_{1}\rangle_{k}:\{0,1\}^{n+k}\to\ns,\;\;(v,w)\;\mapsto\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}f_{0}(v),&w\neq 1^{k}\\
f_{1}(v),&w=1^{k}\end{array}\right..

Note that taking the
1
-arrow is an invertible operation
\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}\times\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}\to\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n+1}}
,
f_{0}\times f_{1}\mapsto\langle f_{0},f_{1}\rangle_{1}
, with inverse the map
f\mapsto f(\cdot,0)\times f(\cdot,1)
, which we denote by
\ia_{n}
.

Definition 7.19

(Arrow spaces)
.

Let
\ns
be a cubespace. For each positive integer
k
, the
k
-th arrow space
over
\ns
, denoted by
\ns\Join_{k}\ns
, is the cubespace defined on the cartesian product
\ns\times\ns
by letting
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{k}\ns)
if and only if
\q=\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
with
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{k}\in\cu^{n+k}(\ns)
.

Example 7.20
.

Let us illustrate this construction in the case of filtered groups. If
\ns
consists of a filtered group
(G,G_{\bullet})
with cube sets
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
,
n\geq 0
, then it follows from Lemma
4.19
that a product map
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
is in
\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{k}\ns)
if and only if
\q_{0},\q_{1}
are both in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
and the map
v\mapsto\q_{0}(v)^{-1}\q_{1}(v)
is in
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet}^{+k})
(here recall that given a filtration
G_{\bullet}
we denote by
G_{\bullet}^{+k}
the shifted filtration, which has
i
-th term
G_{i+k}
). In particular, if
(G,G_{\bullet})
is abelian with the lower central series, then
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
is in
\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{1}\ns)
if and only if
\q_{0}
is a standard
n
-cube on
G
and
\q_{1}
is a translate of
\q_{0}
, i.e. for some
h\in G
we have
\q_{1}(v)=\q_{0}(v)+h
for all
v
. Still in this abelian case, for
k\geq 2
we have
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{k}\ns)
if and only if
\q_{1}=\q_{0}
is a standard
n
-cube on
G
.

Arrow spaces with
k>1
will be used starting in Subsection
8.4
, for now we use mainly the case
k=1
.

Note that every cube
\q\in\cu^{n+1}(\ns)
is a
1
-arrow of
n
-cubes, indeed
\q=\langle\q(\cdot,0),\q(\cdot,1)\rangle_{1}
, where each map
\q(\cdot,i)
is in
\cu^{n}(\ns)
by the composition axiom. Therefore, we have

\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{1}\ns)=\ia_{n}(\cu^{n+1}(\ns))\subset\cu^{n}(\ns)\times\cu^{n}(\ns).

(16)

For
k>1
, not every cube in
\cu^{n+k}(\ns)
is a
k
-arrow of
n
-cubes. Denote by
\textrm{Ar}_{k}^{n}(\ns)
the subset of
\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n+k}}
that is the image of the set of maps
f_{0}\times f_{1}
,
f_{i}\in\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
under

Proof.

The forward implication is clear, by the composition axiom. For the converse, let

-fold ergodic nilspaces as degree-

Recall from Subsection

Proposition 8.14
.

Let

be a
k
-step,
k
-fold ergodic nilspace. Then there is an abelian group
\ab
such that

is isomorphic to
\cD_{k}(\ab)
.

In other words, we have that

is a principal homogeneous space of

Examples of

on a

-step coset nilspace

-fold ergodic

lies in that it will enable us to recover a similar picture concerning the equivalence classes of

for a
general

k
-step nilspace.

Note that by Lemma

, if we fix an element

and apply

to

repeatedly

Lemma 8.15
.

Let

and suppose that Proposition

\ns
be a
k
-fold ergodic

-nilspace, fix

if and only if
x_{0}-x_{1}=y_{0}-y_{1}
in

Proof.

Let
\nss=\ns\Join_{1}\ns
. Let
\q_{0},\q_{1}:\{0,1\}\to\ns
be the maps defined by
\q_{0}(i)=x_{i}
,
\q_{1}(i)=y_{i}
for

in

\cu^{2}(\cD_{1}(\ab))

Let

(using (

(k-1)

-nilspace

Proof of Proposition

.

We argue by induction on

. The case

. Fix

, and suppose that

. This holds trivially for

,

are both

, first we claim that if

then adding an element

to the values of

Now, given any cube

3
3

3

The edges of this graph are the 1-faces of

.

We now deduce that

∎

We close this section by using Proposition

Let

. Then

.

By Proposition

, the constant

. To see that completion holds, let

8.3
Abelian bundles, and the bundle decomposition

In this section we shall establish a general decomposition theorem for nilspaces, Theorem
8.19
. This important result relies on the following basic concept.

Definition 8.17

(Abelian bundle)
.

Let
\ab
be an abelian group. An
abelian bundle
over a set
S
with structure group
\ab
(or
\ab
-
bundle over

S
) is a set
\bnd
with an action
\alpha:\ab\times\bnd\to\bnd
,
(z,x)\mapsto z+x
, and a map
\pi:\bnd\to S
(called the
bundle map
or
projection
) satisfying the following properties:

1. The action
\alpha
is free:
\forall\,x\in\bnd
we have
\{z\in\ab:z+x=x\}=\{0_{\ab}\}
.

2. The map
s\mapsto\pi^{-1}(s)
is a bijection from
S
to the set of orbits of
\ab
in
\bnd
.

A set
\bnd
is a
k
-
fold abelian bundle
with structure groups
\ab_{1},\ab_{2},\ldots,\ab_{k}
if there is a sequence
\bnd_{0},\bnd_{1},\ldots,\bnd_{k}=\bnd
where
\bnd_{0}
is a singleton and
\bnd_{i}
is a
\ab_{i}
-bundle over
\bnd_{i-1}
. We denote by
\pi_{i+1,i}
the bundle map
\bnd_{i+1}\to\bnd_{i}
. More generally
\pi_{i,j}
denotes the map
\pi_{j+1,j}\co\pi_{j+2,j+1}\co\cdots\co\pi_{i,i-1}:\bnd_{i}\to\bnd_{j}
,
i\geq j
. We write
\pi_{i}
for
\pi_{k,i}
.

A
relative
k
-fold abelian bundle
is a generalization of a
k
-fold abelian bundle in which the ground set
\bnd_{0}
can be an arbitrary set.

We shall often call the set
S
the
base
of the bundle. Note that it follows from the second condition above that the action of
\ab
is transitive on each fibre of
\pi
, i.e. on each set
\pi^{-1}(s)
,
s\in S
. Thus, an abelian bundle has the algebraic properties of a principal bundle with abelian structure group
\ab
, without any topological assumptions.

Definition 8.18
.

A
degree-
k
bundle
is a cubespace
\ns
that is also a
k
-fold abelian bundle, with factors
\bnd_{0},\bnd_{1},\ldots,\bnd_{k}=\ns
and structure groups
\ab_{1},\ab_{2},\ldots,\ab_{k}
, and with the following property: for every integer
i\in[0,k-1]
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\cu^{n}(\bnd_{i})=\{\pi_{i}\co\q:\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)\}
, and for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\bnd_{i+1})
we have

\{\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\bnd_{i+1}):\pi_{i}\co\q=\pi_{i}\co\q_{2}\}=\{\q+\q_{3}:\q_{3}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{i+1}(\ab_{i+1}))\}.

(18)

Note that the condition
\q_{3}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{j}(\ab_{j}))
is satisfied for all maps
\q_{3}\in\ab_{j}^{\{0,1\}^{n}}
if
n\leq j
, since
\cD_{j}(\ab_{j})
is
j
-fold ergodic.

We can now state the main result of this section.

Theorem 8.19
.

Let
\ns
be a cubespace. Then
\ns
is a
k
-step nilspace if and only if it is a degree-
k
bundle. Moreover, we then have
\cF_{i}(\ns)=\bnd_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
.

We split the proof into several results.

Lemma 8.20
.

A degree-
k
bundle
\ns=\bnd_{k}
is a
k
-nilspace. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{F}_{i}(\ns)=\bnd_{i}
for every

.

Proof.

Ergodicity can be shown to hold by induction on

. Indeed, a degree-
1
bundle is an affine abelian group, so clearly ergodic. For

, given

, the projection
\pi_{k-1}\co f
is in
\cu^{1}(\bnd_{k-1})
by induction, then by surjectivity there is
\q\in\cu^{1}(\ns)
with
\pi_{k-1}\co f=\pi_{k-1}\co\q
, but then

where
\q_{3}\in\ab_{k}^{\{0,1\}}=\cu^{1}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
, so by (
18

.

The completion axiom is also checked by induction. Completion is trivial on
\bnd_{0}
so suppose that

and we have completion on any degree-
(k-1)
bundle. Let
\q^{\prime}
be an

-corner on
\bnd_{k}

has a completion
\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\bnd_{k-1})

\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
such that
\q_{1}=\pi\co\q_{2}
. The map
\q_{3}^{\prime}=\q^{\prime}-\q_{2}

-corner on

.

To check that

induces a bijection from the classes of

\q_{1}(1^{i+1})=y

\q_{0}(v)=\q_{1}(v)

\pi_{i}\co\q_{0}

-cubes on the

, and they complete the same

. Next, note that if
\pi_{i}(x)=\pi_{i}(y)

. The map
\q_{0}

-step nilspace

. By Corollary

. To complete the proof of Theorem

Definition 8.21
.

Let
\ns
be a

-nilspace. Let

.

Given
(x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1})\in M

By Lemma

,
(x_{0},x_{1})\sim_{k-1}(y_{0},y_{1})

Lemma 8.23

Let

, obtained by restricting to
\{0,1\}^{k+1}\setminus\{(0^{k},1)\}
the 1-arrow

from Lemma

, is a
(k+1)
-corner (modulo an automorphism), for then by completion on
\ns
there exists a unique such

. To see that

is a
(k+1)
-corner, note that any of its
k
-face restrictions is either a map
f_{1}
equal to
y_{0}
at
0^{k}
and
x_{0}
elsewhere in
\{0,1\}^{k}
, or is a map
f_{2}
equal to
x_{0}
on one
(k-1)
-face and
x_{1}
on the opposite face. We have

by Lemma

, since
x_{0}\sim_{k-1}y_{0}
.
We have

because
f_{2}=f_{2}^{\prime}\co\phi
, where
f_{2}^{\prime}
is the 1-cube
(x_{0},x_{1})
and
\phi:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}
is the morphism projecting to some fixed coordinate. Hence there is indeed a unique
y_{1}
completing

to a cube

of the form given in Lemma
8.22
.

To see that
y_{1}\in F_{1}
, note that since
\pi_{k-1}(x_{0})=\pi_{k-1}(y_{0})
, the
(k+1)
-corner
\pi_{k-1}\co f^{\prime}
is constant on the
k
-face involving only
x_{0},y_{0}
. We can complete this to the cube that is the constant

on the opposite face. Since this completion is unique, it must be the cube
\pi_{k-1}\co f
, so we must have
\pi_{k-1}(y_{1})=\pi_{k-1}(x_{1})
.
∎

Recall that by Lemma
8.15
, if

is a fibre of
\sim_{k-1}
and
x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}\in F
then
(x_{0},y_{0})\sim(x_{1},y_{1})
if and only if

in
\ab_{F}
. This means that inside any fibre

the elements of

are in bijection with the fibres of
\sim
, the bijection being well-defined by
(x_{0},y_{0})\mapsto a=y_{0}-x_{0}\in\ab_{F}
. We now use this fact to relate the groups of two distinct fibres of
\sim_{k-1}
.

Lemma 8.24
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-nilspace and let
F_{0},F_{1}
be distinct fibres of
\sim_{k-1}
. Define
\vartheta:\ab_{F_{0}}\to\ab_{F_{1}}
by

\vartheta(a)=b\;\textrm{ if and only if }\;(x_{0},x_{0}+a)\sim(x_{1},x_{1}+b)\;\textrm{ for some }\;x_{0}\in F_{0},\;x_{1}\in F_{1}.

(19)

Then
\vartheta
is a group isomorphism.

Proof.

The map
\vartheta
is well-defined, for if
(x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})\sim(x_{0},x_{0}+a)
with
x_{1}^{\prime}\in F_{1}
, then
y_{1}^{\prime}
is also in
F_{1}
, and then by transitivity
(x_{1},x_{1}+b)\sim(x_{1}^{\prime},y_{1}^{\prime})
, so
y_{1}^{\prime}=x_{1}^{\prime}+b
. Moreover,
\vartheta
is a bijection because given
b\in\ab_{F_{1}}
and any
x_{1}\in F_{1}
, for any
x_{0}\in F_{0}
there is a unique
y_{0}\in F_{0}
such that
(x_{0},y_{0})\sim(x_{1},x_{1}+b)
, but then
\vartheta(y_{0}-x_{0})=b
.

To see that
\vartheta
is a homomorphism, let
a,a^{\prime}\in\ab_{F_{0}}
, fix
x_{0}\in F_{0}
, let
y_{0}=x_{0}+a
,
z_{0}=y_{0}+a^{\prime}
. Fix any
x_{1}\in F_{1}
and let
y_{1},z_{1}\in F_{1}
be the unique elements given by Lemma
8.23
such that
(x_{0},y_{0})\sim(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(y_{0},z_{0})\sim(y_{1},z_{1})
. By transitivity of
\sim_{k-1}
we have
(x_{0},z_{0})\sim(x_{1},z_{1})
, and it follows from the definitions that
\vartheta(a+a^{\prime})=\vartheta(a)+\vartheta(a^{\prime})
.
∎

Thanks to these isomorphisms, we can now identify all the groups
\ab_{F}
and talk about a single abelian group
\ab
acting on every fibre
F
, sending
x\in F
to
x+z\in F
for each

. Thus
\ab
acts on all of
\ns
. The action is also free and transitive on each fibre.

We have thus shown that the given
k
-step nilspace
\ns
is a
\ab
-bundle over
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\ns)
, with bundle map the canonical projection
\pi_{k-1}
for
\sim_{k-1}
. It remains only to show that

is a degree-
k
bundle.

Lemma 8.25
.

The
\ab
-bundle
\ns
satisfies (
18
).

Proof.

First we claim that if
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
and we let any

act on the values of
\q
at the two points of an arbitrary 1-face in

, then the resulting map
\q^{\prime}
is also in
\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
. To see this, note that without loss we can suppose that the given 1-face is
0^{k+1},(1,0,\dots,0)
, and let us denote the values of
\q
at these points by
x
,
y
respectively. We have
(x,x+a)\sim(y,y+a)
, which means that the corresponding map

from Lemma
8.22
is in
\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
.
Let
\phi
be the map

used in the proof of Lemma

agrees with

on the outer points, and we also have that it has constant value

on the subcube

the map

on face

both to

, changing

, we see that the resulting map is still a morphism

.

Finally, this last claim enables us to show that (

) holds. For suppose that

have

. Then starting at any given

-face of

to the values of

at one of those vertices. Repeating this along a Hamiltonian path of 1-faces on

-cube

\{\q_{2}\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns):\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{2}=\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{1}\}\subset\{\q_{1}+\q_{3}:\q_{3}\in\cu^{k+1}(\cD_{k}(\ab))\}.

into

∎

The proof of Theorem

is now complete.

Definition 8.26
.

Let
\ns
be a

-step nilspace, let

be two fibres of

, and let

,

. We define the

to be the map
\phi_{x_{0},x_{1}}
sending each

such that

, that is the unique

defined by

.

8.4
Translations on a
k
-step nilspace form a filtered group of degree
k

Given any subset
F
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
and any map
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
, we define the map

\alpha^{F}:\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}}\to\ns^{\{0,1\}^{n}},\quad\alpha^{F}(f):v\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\alpha(f(v)),&v\in F\\
f(v),&v\not\in F\end{array}\right..

Definition 8.27

(Translations)
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace and let
i
be a positive integer. A map
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
is a
translation of height

i
on
\ns
(or
i
-translation
) if for every
n\geq i
and every face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
of codimension
i
, the map
\alpha^{F}
is cube-preserving, that is for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
we have
\alpha^{F}(\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
. We denote the set of translations of height
i
by
\tran_{i}(\ns)
.

Using discrete-cube automorphisms, we see that in order for
\alpha
to be an
i
-translation it suffices to have
\alpha^{F}
being cube-preserving for
some
face
F
of codimension
i
. Note also that
\tran_{i}(\ns)\supset\tran_{j}(\ns)
for any
i\leq j
. We shall often write simply
\tran(\ns)
for
\tran_{1}(\ns)
, and refer to translations of height 1 simply as translations.

These translations were introduced by Host and Kra in
[
11
,
12
]
, where they were studied in relation to parallelepiped structures and shown to form a nilpotent group. Analogously, in this subsection we treat the following main result.

Proposition 8.28
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace. Then
\tran(\ns)
is a group and
(\tran_{i}(\ns))_{i\geq 0}
is a filtration of degree at most
k
.
(
We set
\tran_{0}(\ns)=\tran_{1}(\ns)=\tran(\ns)
.
)

We refer to
\tran(\ns)
as the
translation group
of
\ns
. Before we turn to the proof of Proposition
8.28
, let us collect some basic observations about translations, which will also help to gain intuition about them. We begin with simple examples using the nilspaces from Chapter
Notes on nilspaces: algebraic aspects
.

Example 8.29
.

Let
(G,G_{\bullet})
be a filtered group of degree
k
, let
\Gamma
be a subgroup of
G
, and let
\ns
be the corresponding
k
-step coset nilspace
G/\Gamma
with cube sets
\cu^{n}(\ns)=\{\pi_{\Gamma}\co\q:\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})\}
. Let
i\in[k]
,
g\in G_{i}
, and let
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
be the map
x\Gamma\mapsto(gx)\Gamma
. Then
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\ns)
. To see this, note that for any
n
-cube
\pi_{\Gamma}\co\q
on
\ns
and any face
F
of codimension
i
in
\{0,1\}^{n}
, we have
\alpha^{F}(\pi_{\Gamma}\co\q)=\pi_{\Gamma}\co(g^{F}\q)
, where
g^{F}\q
is the pointwise-multiplication map
\{0,1\}^{n}\to G
,
v\mapsto g^{F}(v)\q(v)
. By the definition and group property of
\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
, we have
g^{F}\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
. Hence
\alpha^{F}(\pi_{\Gamma}\co\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, and so indeed
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\ns)
.

The following result describes some basic general properties of translations.

Lemma 8.30
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace. Then every translation on
\ns
is a nilspace morphism
\ns\to\ns
. Furthermore, for each
i\geq 1
we have

\forall\,\alpha\in\tran(\ns),\;\;\textrm{ if }\;x,y\in\ns\;\textrm{satisfy }\;x\sim_{i}y,\;\textrm{then }\alpha(x)\sim_{i}\alpha(y),

(20)

and we also have

\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\ns)\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;\forall\,x\in\ns,\;\alpha(x)\sim_{i-1}x.

(21)

Proof.

To see that every
\alpha\in\tran(\ns)
is a morphism, note that given any cube
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, the map
\alpha\co\q
can be written as the composition
\alpha^{F}\co\alpha^{F^{\prime}}(\q)
for two opposite faces
F,F^{\prime}
of codimension 1, so
\alpha\co\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
as required.

To see (
20
), recall that by Lemma
8.4
we have
x\sim_{i}y
if and only if there is
\q\in\cu^{i+1}(\ns)
such that
\q(1^{i+1})=y
and
\q(v)=x
otherwise. Now given
\alpha\in\tran(\ns)
, for this cube
\q
we have by the previous paragraph that
\alpha\co\q\in\cu^{i+1}(\ns)
, and therefore
\alpha(x)\sim_{i}\alpha(y)
.

To see (
21
), note that letting
\q
denote the constant
i
-dimensional cube with value
x
, and letting
\q^{\prime}
denote the map
\{0,1\}^{i}\to\ns
such that
\q^{\prime}(v)=\q(v)=x
for
v\neq 0^{i}
and
\q^{\prime}(0^{i})=\alpha(x)
, we have by the definition of
i
-translations that
\q^{\prime}
is also in
\cu^{i}(\ns)
, so by the fact recalled above we have
\alpha(x)\sim_{i-1}x
.
∎

Thus, translations of height
i
leave the equivalence classes of
\sim_{i-1}
in
\ns
globally invariant, and, more generally, translations conserve all the equivalence relations
\sim_{j}
. When
\ns
is a
k
-step nilspace, we can deduce the following fact, relating translations to the notion of
local
translation from Definition
8.26
.

Lemma 8.31
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace. Then for every
\alpha\in\tran(\ns)
and
x\in\ns
, the restriction of
\alpha
to the class of
\sim_{k-1}
containing
x
is the local translation
\phi_{x,\alpha(x)}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
y\sim_{k-1}x
, so that there exists
\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
such that
\q(1^{k})=y
and
\q(v)=x
otherwise. Consider the corner
\q^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{k+1}\setminus\{1^{k+1}\}\to\ns
defined by letting the restriction of
\q^{\prime}
to
\{v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k)$}=0\}
be equal to
\q
and the restriction of
\q^{\prime}
to
\{v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k)$}=1\}\setminus\{1^{k}\}
be equal to
\alpha(\q)
. Then since the arrow
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{1}
is a
(k+1)
-cube, the unique way to complete
\q^{\prime}
is to set the value at
1^{k+1}
equal to
\alpha(y)
. This means that, letting
\sim
denote the relation from Definigion
8.21
, we have
(x,y)\sim(\alpha(x),\alpha(y))
. Hence, setting
y=x+a
, we must have
\alpha(y)=\alpha(x)+a
, and so by Definition
8.26
the result follows.
∎

Let us turn to the proof of Proposition
8.28
. Our first step towards this result is the following lemma relating
\tran_{i}(\ns)
to the
i
-th arrow space
\ns\Join_{i}\ns
(recall Definition
7.19
). Note that if
\alpha\in\tran(\ns)
then for every cube
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
the map
\{0,1\}^{n+1}\to\ns
consisting of
\q
and
\alpha\co\q
on opposite faces is a cube. The converse holds as well. The resulting characterization generalizes to translations of any height, as follows.

Lemma 8.32
.

For
i\geq 1
, a map
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
is in
\tran_{i}(\ns)
if and only if the map
h_{\alpha}:\ns\to\ns\times\ns
defined by
h_{\alpha}(x)=(x,\alpha(x))
is a morphism into
\ns\Join_{i}\ns
. In other words, we have
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\ns)
if and only if for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
the
i
-arrow
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{i}
is in
\cu^{n+i}(\ns)
.

Proof.

If
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\ns)
, and
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, then
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{i}(v,w)=\alpha^{F}(\q^{\prime})(v,w)
, where
F
is the face of
\{0,1\}^{n+i}
defined by

(so
\codim(F)=i
) and
\q^{\prime}
is the
(n+i)
-cube
\q\co\phi
where
\phi:\{0,1\}^{n+i}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is the morphism
(v,w)\mapsto v
. Hence
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{i}
is an
(n+i)
-cube on
\ns
as required.

For the converse, suppose that
h_{\alpha}
is a morphism into
\ns\Join_{i}\ns
. Given
n\geq i
, let
F_{0}
denote the face
\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=1\;\;\forall\,j\in[n-i+1,n]\}
of codimension
i
, and let
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
. We want to show that
\alpha^{F_{0}}(\q)
is also in
\cu^{n}(\ns)
. Let
Q
be the product cubespace
\{0,1\}^{n-i}\times T_{i}
where
T_{i}
is the
i
-dimensional tricube. Let
f_{1}
be the identity on
\{0,1\}
and
f_{2}
be the function with
f_{2}(-1)=0,f_{2}(0)=1,f_{2}(1)=1
. Let
f=f_{1}^{n-i}\times f_{2}^{i}:Q\to\{0,1\}^{n}
. This is a surjective morphism with two useful properties: firstly, the
n
-dimensional subcube of
Q
containing
1^{n}
(i.e.
\{0,1\}^{n}
viewed as a subcube of
Q
) is mapped by
f
onto the face
F_{0}
(as
f_{2}
is constantly
1^{i}
on
\{0,1\}^{i}
); secondly, letting
\mathrm{id}
denote the identity map on
\{0,1\}^{n-i}
and
\omega_{i}
the outer-point map
\{0,1\}^{i}\to\{-1,1\}^{i}
(recall Definition
7.15
), we have
\q\co f\co\,(\mathrm{id}\times\omega_{i})=\q
. Now let
g=\q\co f
, and let
g^{\prime}:Q\to\ns
be the map obtained from
g
by applying
\alpha
to the values of
g
on
\{0,1\}^{n-i}\times\{1^{i}\}
. Then
g^{\prime}\co\,(\mathrm{id}\times\omega_{i})=\alpha^{F_{0}}(\q)
. Therefore, to show that
\alpha^{F_{0}}(\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
it suffices to show that
g^{\prime}
is a morphism from
Q
to
\ns
.

To show this, note first that
g:Q\to\ns
is already a morphism (using Lemma
7.16
and the product structure on
Q
). Given any cube (or morphism)
\tilde{\q}:\{0,1\}^{m}\to Q
, we want to show that
g^{\prime}\co\tilde{\q}\in\cu^{m}(\ns)
. Now
\tilde{\q}=\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
where
\q_{0}
is a cube
\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n-i}
and
\q_{1}
is a cube
\{0,1\}^{m}\to T_{i}
. If
\q_{1}
is one of the cubes on
T_{i}
not having
1^{i}
in its image, then
g^{\prime}\co\tilde{\q}=g\co\tilde{\q}
is indeed a cube on
\ns
(since
g
is a morphism).
If
\q_{1}
is the cube on
T_{i}
with
1^{i}
in its image, then by Definition
7.13
we have
\tilde{\q}=\q_{0}\times(\trem_{1^{i}}\co\phi)
for some morphism
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{i}
. The preimage under
\tilde{\q}
of
\{0,1\}^{n-i}\times\{1^{i}\}
is a face
F\subset\{0,1\}^{m}
of codimension
i
, which we can suppose without loss of generality to be the face with last
i
components equal to 1. Moreover, by definition of
f
the map
g\co\tilde{\q}
gives the same cube
\q^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{m-i}\to\ns
when restricted to each of the other faces
\tilde{\q}^{-1}(\{0,1\}^{n-i}\times\{w\})
, for each
w\in\{0,1\}^{i}\setminus\{1^{i}\}
. It follows that
g^{\prime}\co\tilde{\q}=\langle\q^{\prime},\alpha\co\q^{\prime}\rangle_{i}
, which is a cube by our assumption on

, so we are done.
∎

Next, we show that if
\ns
is a

-step nilspace then the cube-preserving property for

needs to be checked only for

-cubes.

Lemma 8.33
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace. A map
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
is in
\tran_{i}(\ns)
if and only if for every
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
we have
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{k+1+i}(\ns)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
8.32
it suffices to check that given any
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
we have
\q^{\prime}=\q\times(\alpha\co\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)
. By Lemma
8.13
it suffices to check that any restriction of
\q^{\prime}
to a
(k+1)
-dimensional face

containing some

with

is a
(k+1)
-cube. Now any such restriction is of the form
\q_{0}\times(\alpha\co\q_{0})
, where

is the restriction of

to

. This restriction is in
\cu^{k+1}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)
by definition if
\langle\q_{0},\alpha\co\q_{0}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{k+1+i}(\ns)
, which is the case by our assumption.
∎

We can now establish the first main claim of Proposition
8.28
.

Lemma 8.34
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace. Then
\tran_{i}(\ns)
is a group for every
i\geq 1
.

Since every translation in
\tran(\ns)
is a nilspace morphism from
\ns
to itself, with Lemma
8.34
we then have that
\tran(\ns)
is a subgroup of the group
\aut(\ns)
of nilspace automorphisms of
\ns
.

Proof.

It is clear that composition of two elements of
\tran_{i}(\ns)
is still in
\tran_{i}(\ns)
, so we just need to check invertibility.

First we show, by induction on
k
, that any translation is an invertible map. For
k=1
this is clear since in this case a translation is just addition of some fixed group-element. Now suppose that invertibility holds for
k-1
and let
\alpha
be a translation on
\ns
. By Lemma
8.31
,
\alpha
preserves the classes of
\sim_{k-1}
, so
\alpha
induces a well-defined map on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\ns)
, namely
\pi_{k-1}(x)\mapsto\pi_{k-1}\co\alpha(x)
. We denote this map by
h(\alpha)
. If
\q^{\prime}
is an
n
-cube on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\ns)
and
F
is a face of codimension 1, then letting
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
satisfy
\pi_{k-1}\co\q=\q^{\prime}
, we have
\alpha^{F}(\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, so
h(\alpha)^{F}(\q^{\prime})=\pi_{k-1}\co\alpha^{F}(\q)
is in
\cu^{n}(\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\ns))
, hence
h(\alpha)
is a translation on
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}(\ns)
. By induction, this has an inverse
h(\alpha)^{-1}
. Now fix any
y\in\ns
and let
F_{2}
denote the
\sim_{k-1}
class containing
y
. Let
F_{1}
denote the class mapped onto
F_{2}
by
\alpha
, namely
F_{1}=\pi_{k-1}^{-1}\big(h(\alpha)^{-1}\pi_{k-1}(y)\big)
. Since
h(\alpha)
is bijective, any
x
satisfying
\alpha(x)=y
must lie in
F_{1}
. Lemma
8.31
implies that
\alpha
is a bijection from
F_{1}
to
F_{2}
, so there must be a unique such
x
in
F_{1}
.

Finally, note that the inverse of an
i
-translation
\alpha
is an
i
-translation. This can be shown by induction on
i
. For
i>1
, let
F
be the face
\{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}:v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=1,\;\forall\,j\in[n-i+1,n]\}
. It suffices to show that
{\alpha^{-1}}^{F}
is cube-preserving. By inclusion-exclusion the indicator-function
1_{F}
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
can be written as a linear combination
1_{F}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}1_{F_{j}}
where
\lambda_{j}\in\{-1,1\}
and
F_{j}
are faces of codimension at most
i-1
. Each map
{\alpha^{-1}}^{F_{j}}
is cube-preserving, by induction, and so their composition
{\alpha^{-1}}^{F}
is also cube-preserving.
∎

Note that the map
h
in the above proof is a homomorphism from
\tran(\ns)
to
\tran(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. We can now establish the filtration property.

Lemma 8.35
.

For every
i,j\geq 1
we have
[\tran_{i}(\ns),\tran_{j}(\ns)]\subset\tran_{i+j}(\ns)
.

(Note that setting
\tran_{0}(\ns)=\tran_{1}(\ns)
the lemma then holds for all
i,j\geq 0
.)

Proof.

Let
\alpha_{1}
be in
\tran_{i}(\ns)
and
\alpha_{2}\in\tran_{j}(\ns)
. Let
F
be a face in
\{0,1\}^{n}
of codimension
i+j
, thus
F
is defined by a choice of
i+j
coordinates that are each fixed to be 0 or 1. We can write
F=F_{1}\cap F_{2}
where
F_{1}
is a face of codimension
i
and
F_{2}
is a face of codimension
j
. We then have
[\alpha_{1}^{F_{1}},\alpha_{2}^{F_{2}}]={\alpha_{1}^{F_{1}}}^{-1}{\alpha_{2}^{F_{2}}}^{-1}\alpha_{1}^{F_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{F_{2}}=[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{F}
. Therefore, for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
we have
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{F}(\q)\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
and so
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{F}\in\tran_{i+j}(\ns)
.
∎

Corollary 8.36
.

If
\ns
is a
k
-step nilspace then
(\tran_{i}(\ns))_{i\geq 0}
is a filtration of degree
k
.

Proof.

The group
\tran_{k+1}(\ns)
consists of maps
\alpha:\ns\to\ns
such that given
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
and the
(k+1)
-codimensional face
F=\{1^{k+1}\}
we have
\alpha^{F}(\q)\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
. For every
x\in\ns
, the cube
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
with constant value
x
is the unique completion of the restriction of
\alpha^{F}(\q)
to
\{0,1\}^{k+1}\setminus F
, so we must have
\alpha(x)=x
. Hence
\alpha
is the identity.
∎

This completes the proof of Proposition
8.28
.

We conclude this subsection with a few additional remarks on translation groups.

Firstly, one may wonder whether there is a simple relationship between the groups
\tran_{i}(\ns)
and the groups
\ab_{i}
describing the bundle structure of
\ns
. For general
k
-step nilspaces, it is not hard to establish such a relation in the case of the group
\tran_{k}(\ns)
.

Lemma 8.37
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace. Let
\tau
denote the map sending
z\in\ab_{k}
to the map
\tau_{z}:\ns\to\ns
,
x\mapsto x+z
. Then we have

\tran_{k}(\ns)=\tau(\ab_{k})
.

Proof.

To see that
\tran_{k}(\ns)\subset\tau(\ab_{k})
, note first that if
\alpha\in\tran_{k}(\ns)
then by (
21
) we have
\alpha(x)\sim_{k-1}x
for all
x\in\ns
. Combined with Lemma
8.31
, this implies that for each fibre
\pi_{k-1}^{-1}(y)
,
y\in\ns_{k-1}
, there exists
z_{y}\in\ab_{k}
such that on this fibre
\alpha
is the map
x\mapsto x+z_{y}
. Now if
y,y^{\prime}
are distinct points in
\ns_{k-1}
then for any
x,x^{\prime}
in the fibres
\pi_{k-1}^{-1}(y)
,
\pi_{k-1}^{-1}(y^{\prime})
respectively, note that the map
\q:\{0,1\}^{k+1}\to\ns
with
\q(\cdot,0)=x
and
\q(\cdot,1)=x^{\prime}
is in
\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
(by the ergodicity and composition axioms). The map
\q^{\prime}
obtained from
\q
by changing
\q(0^{k+1})
to
x+z_{y}
and
\q(0^{k},1)
to
x^{\prime}+z_{y^{\prime}}
is still a cube, since it is
\alpha^{F}(\q)
for the
k
-codimensional face
F=\{0^{k+1},(0^{k},1)\}
. Then, since
\pi_{k-1}\co\q=\pi_{k-1}\co\q^{\prime}
, we must have
z_{y}=z_{y^{\prime}}
(since
\ns
is a degree-
k
bundle and so
\sigma_{k+1}(\q-\q^{\prime})=0
). Hence
\alpha\in\tau(\ab_{k})
. To see that
\tau(\ab_{k})\subset\tran_{k}(\ns)
, note that if
\alpha=\tau(z)
, then for every
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
we have the
k
-arrow identity
\langle\q,\alpha\co\q\rangle_{k}=\langle\q,\q\rangle_{k}+\langle 0,z\rangle_{k}
. This is in
\cu^{2k+1}(\ns)
, by (
18
), because
\langle\q,\q\rangle_{k}\in\cu^{2k+1}(\ns)
and
\langle 0,z\rangle_{k}\in\cu^{2k+1}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
. Hence
\alpha\in\tran_{k}(\ns)
by Lemma
8.32
.
∎

For general nilspaces the relationship between other translation groups and structure groups is less easy to describe. In the topological part of the theory, we shall be able to give such a description under some natural topological assumptions (see
[
2
, Proposition 2.9.20]
).

A second remark is that one may distinguish, among all cubes on a nilspace, those that arise from the action of the translation groups, in the following sense.

Definition 8.38
.

Two cubes
\q_{1},\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
are
translation equivalent
if there are translations
\alpha_{1}\in\tran_{i_{1}}(\ns),\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}\in\tran_{i_{\ell}}(\ns)
, and a face
F_{j}
of codimension
i_{j}
for each
j\in[\ell]
, such that
\q_{2}=\alpha_{\ell}^{F_{\ell}}\cdots\alpha_{1}^{F_{1}}(\q_{1})
. A cube is called a
translation cube
if it is translation equivalent to a constant cube.

In other words, letting
G=\tran(\ns)
and
G_{\bullet}
be the filtration with
G_{i}=\tran_{i}(\ns)
, we have by Definition
4.3
that
\q_{1},\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
are translation equivalent if and only if there is
\q\in\cu^{n}(G_{\bullet})
such that
\q_{2}(v)=\q(v)(\q_{1}(v))
for all
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Remark 8.39
.

As indicated in Section
6
, all the examples of nilspaces
\ns
seen up to that section have the property that all cubes on
\ns
are translation cubes. This is not the case for a general nilspace. Indeed, it follows from the ergodicity axiom that if every cube on
\ns
is a translation cube then the action of
\tran(\ns)
on
\ns
is transitive. There are examples of nilspaces for which this transitivity does not hold, see
[
12
, Example 6]
.

In the remainder of this chapter we gather additional algebraic tools. Apart from the valuable information that these tools can provide on a nilspace, they are also very useful in the topological part of the theory.

9
Additional tools

9.1
Nilspace morphisms as bundle morphisms

Theorem
8.19
describes a
k
-step nilspace as a degree-
k
bundle. In light of this, it is natural to ask for a description of how a morphism between two
k
-nilspaces relates the bundle structures. The first result of this section, Proposition
9.2
, provides such a description. To formulate it we need the following concept.

Definition 9.1

(Bundle morphism)
.

Let
\bnd
and
\bnd^{\prime}
be two
k
-fold abelian bundles with factors
\bnd_{i},\bnd^{\prime}_{i}
(
i\in[0,k]
), structure groups
\ab_{i},\ab_{i}^{\prime}
and projections
\pi_{i},\pi_{i}^{\prime}
(
i\in[k]
). A
bundle morphism
from
\bnd
to
\bnd^{\prime}
is a map
\psi:\bnd\rightarrow\bnd^{\prime}
satisfying the following properties:

(i)

For every
i\in[k]
, if
\pi_{i}(x)=\pi_{i}(y)
then
\pi_{i}(\psi(x))=\pi_{i}(\psi(y))
. Thus
\psi
induces well defined maps
\psi_{i}:\bnd_{i}\rightarrow\bnd^{\prime}_{i}
.

(ii)

For every
i\in[k]
there is a map
\alpha_{i}:\ab_{i}\rightarrow\ab_{i}^{\prime}
such that for every
x\in\bnd_{i}
and
a\in\ab_{i}
we have
\psi_{i}(x+a)=\psi_{i}(x)+\alpha_{i}(a)
.

The maps
\alpha_{i}
are called the
structure morphisms
of
\psi
. We say that
\psi
is
totally surjective
if all its structure morphisms are surjective.

Note that the condition required of the maps
\alpha_{i}
implies that they are group homomorphisms.

Proposition 9.2
.

Let
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
be
k
-step nilspaces. Then a nilspace morphism
\ns\to\ns^{\prime}
is a bundle morphism between the corresponding
k
-degree bundles.

Proof.

Condition (i) from Definition
9.1
holds, for if
x\sim_{i}y
then there is an
(i+1)
-cube
\q
equal to
x
everywhere except at one vertex where it equals
y
, and then
\psi\co\q
is a cube equal to
\psi(x)
everywhere except at one vertex where it equals
\psi(y)
, so
\psi(x)\sim_{i}\psi(y)
.

For condition (ii), we first consider the case where
\ns
and
\ns^{\prime}
are
i
-fold ergodic and therefore degree-
i
abelian torsors (by Proposition
8.14
). By Lemma
7.24
and Proposition
6.1
, if we fix
x\in\ns
and apply
\partial_{x}^{i-1}
and
\partial_{\psi_{i}(x)}^{i-1}
to
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
respectively, the resulting nilspaces are isomorphic to
\cD_{1}(\ab_{i}),\cD_{1}(\ab_{i}^{\prime})
respectively, for some abelian groups
\ab_{i},\ab_{i}^{\prime}
. Since
\psi_{i}
preserves cubes, it follows that it is an affine homomorphism between the torsors
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
, which means that there is a homomorphism
\alpha:\ab_{i}\to\ab^{\prime}_{i}
such that for each
x\in\ns
we have
\psi_{i}(x+a)-\psi_{i}(x)=\alpha(a)
.

Now every equivalence class
F
of
\sim_{i-1}
in
\bnd_{i}
is isomorphic to such a degree-
i
abelian torsor
\cD_{i}(\ab_{i})
and so
\psi_{i}
restricted to
F
satisfies
\psi_{i}(x+a)=\psi_{i}(x)+\alpha_{F}(a)
, for some homomorphism
\alpha_{F}:\ab_{i}\to\ab_{i}^{\prime}
which could depend on
F
. We claim that
\alpha_{F}
is in fact independent of
F
, modulo the isomorphism from Lemma
8.24
. Indeed, if
F_{1},F_{2}
are two classes in
\ns
and we have
(x,x+a)\sim(y,y+a)
(where
\sim
is the relation from Definition
8.21
, so
(x,y)\sim_{i-1}(x+a,y+a)
in
\ns\Join_{1}\ns
), then since
\sim
is defined in terms of cubes this relation is preserved by
\psi_{i}
, so we have
(\psi_{i}(x),\psi_{i}(x)+\alpha_{F_{1}}(a))\sim(\psi_{i}(y),\psi_{i}(y)+\alpha_{F_{2}}(a))
, which implies that
\alpha_{F_{1}}(a)
and
\alpha_{F_{2}}(a)
are indeed identified by the isomorphism from Lemma
8.24
.
∎

Recall from Definition
8.17
the notation
\pi_{i,j}
for the projection
\bnd_{i}\to\bnd_{j}
,
i\geq j
.

Definition 9.3

(Sub-bundle)
.

Let
\bnd
be a
k
-fold abelian bundle with factors
\bnd_{0},\bnd_{1},\dots,\bnd_{k}=\bnd
, structure groups
\ab_{1},\ab_{2},\dots,\ab_{k}
, and projections
\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{k}
. A
sub-bundle
of
\bnd
is a
k
-fold abelian bundle
\bnd^{\prime}
with factors
\bnd^{\prime}_{0}=\bnd_{0},\bnd^{\prime}_{1}\subseteq\bnd_{1},\ldots,\bnd_{k}^{\prime}\subseteq\bnd_{k}
, structure groups
\ab_{i}^{\prime}\leq\ab_{i}
,
i\in[k]
, and with each projection
\pi_{i,i-1}^{\prime}
being the restriction of
\pi_{i,i-1}
to
\bnd_{i}^{\prime}
and satisfying the following condition: for every
x\in\bnd_{i}^{\prime}
we have
{\pi^{\prime}}^{\,-1}_{i,i-1}(\pi_{i,i-1}^{\prime}(x))=x+\ab_{i}^{\prime}
, equivalently
\{z\in\ab_{i}:x+z\in\bnd_{i}^{\prime}\}=\ab_{i}^{\prime}
.

In particular, if
k=1
then a sub-bundle is an orbit of
\ab_{1}^{\prime}
inside a principal homogeneous space of
\ab_{1}
.

Given a bundle morphism
\psi:\bnd\to\bnd^{\prime}
, we shall now define a certain relative abelian bundle on
\bnd^{\prime}
that generalizes in a natural way the concept of the kernel of a homomorphism between abelian groups. Let us first discuss briefly the intuition that leads to this definition.

Given abelian groups
\ab,\ab^{\prime}
and a surjective homomorphism
\psi:\ab\to\ab^{\prime}
, the kernel
\ker\psi
is the preimage
\psi^{-1}(\{0\})
, and
\ab
is the disjoint union of cosets of
\ker\psi
, these cosets being the preimages
\psi^{-1}(t)
,
t\in\ab^{\prime}
. We can thus view
\ab
as an abelian bundle over
\ab^{\prime}
with structure group
\ker\psi
. When we consider more generally a bundle morphism
\psi:\bnd\to\bnd^{\prime}
, defining the kernel of
\psi
as the preimage of a single point as in the abelian case is not clear since typically there is no distinguished point on
\bnd^{\prime}
playing the role of
0_{\ab^{\prime}}
. However, one can still view
\bnd
as a
relative
bundle with base
\bnd^{\prime}
(recall Definition
8.17
), and then restrict this bundle structure to each preimage
\psi^{-1}(t)
,
t\in\bnd^{\prime}
, to obtain coherent bundle structures on these preimages. This is what we shall establish formally in the next two lemmas. (Recall from Definition
9.1
the induced maps
\psi_{i}
and the structure morphisms
\alpha_{i}
.)

Definition 9.4

(Kernel of a bundle morphism)
.

Let
\bnd,\bnd^{\prime}
be
k
-fold abelian bundles, and let
\psi:\bnd\to\bnd^{\prime}
be a totally surjective bundle morphism. The
kernel
of
\psi
is the relative
k
-fold abelian bundle denoted by
K
, with structure groups
\{\ker(\alpha_{i})\}_{i=1}^{k}
, defined as follows. First, for each
i\in[k]
we define
K_{i}=\{(x,y)\in\bnd_{i}\times\bnd^{\prime}:\psi_{i}(x)=\pi_{i}(y)\}
.
We then define, for each
i\in[k]
, an action of
\ker(\alpha_{i})
on
K_{i}
by
(x,y)+a=(x+a,y)
. Finally, the projection
\pi_{i}:K_{j}\to K_{i}
is defined by
\pi_{i}(x,y)=(\pi_{i}(x),y)
for
j\geq i
.

Note that we can identify
K=K_{k}
with
\bnd_{k}
using the bijection
(x,y)\leftrightarrow x
, and
K_{0}
can be identified with
\bnd^{\prime}
using the bijection
(x,y)\leftrightarrow y
. These bijections enable us to identify
\pi_{0}:K\to K_{0}
with
\psi:\bnd\to\bnd^{\prime}
.

Let us confirm that this definition makes
K
a relative abelian bundle.

Lemma 9.5
.

For each
i\in[k]
,
K_{i}
is a
\ker(\alpha_{i})
-bundle over
K_{i-1}
with bundle map
\pi_{i-1}
.

Proof.

First we check that
\pi_{i-1}
is surjective. Let
(x,y)\in K_{i-1}
, so
\psi_{i-1}(x)=\pi_{i-1}(y)
. Let
z\in\bnd_{i}
be such that
\pi_{i-1}(z)=x
. We have
\pi_{i-1}(\psi_{i}(z))=\psi_{i-1}(\pi_{i-1}(z))=\psi_{i-1}(x)=\pi_{i-1}(y)
, so there exists
a^{\prime}\in\ab_{i}^{\prime}
such that
\psi_{i}(z)+a^{\prime}=\pi_{i}(y)
. Since
\alpha_{i}
is surjective by assumption, there is
a\in\ab_{i}
with
\alpha_{i}(a)=a^{\prime}
. Then the pair
(z+a,y)
is in
K_{i}
and maps to
(x,y)
under
\pi_{i-1}
.

Clearly
\ker(\alpha_{i})\subset\ab_{i}
acts freely on
K_{i}
, so it remains to check that it acts transitively on the fibres of
\pi_{i-1}
. Let
(x_{1},y)
and
(x_{2},y)
be any elements in the same fibre of
\pi_{i-1}:K_{i}\to K_{i-1}
. Since
\pi_{i-1}(x_{1})=\pi_{i-1}(x_{2})
there is
a\in\ab_{i}
with
x_{1}=x_{2}+a
. Then
\pi_{i}(y)=\psi_{i}(x_{1})=\psi_{i}(x_{2}+a)=\psi_{i}(x_{2})+\alpha_{i}(a)=\pi_{i}(y)+\alpha_{i}(a)
, whence
a\in\ker(\alpha_{i})
.
∎

With the bijections
K\leftrightarrow\bnd
and
K_{0}\leftrightarrow\bnd^{\prime}
described above, we can thus view
\bnd
as a bundle over
\bnd^{\prime}
. Note that no set
K_{i}
is contained in
\bnd_{i}
, we just have a map
(x,y)\mapsto x
from
K_{i}
to
\bnd_{i}
. Now a key fact is that, while this map is not a bijection, if we fix a single
t\in\bnd^{\prime}
then the map restricts to a bijection identifying the set
\{(x,y)\in K_{i}:y=t\}
with
\psi_{i}^{-1}(\pi_{i}(t))
. From this it follows promptly that
\psi^{-1}(t)
has a
k
-fold abelian-bundle structure, inherited from
K
via these bijections (thus with structure groups
\ker\alpha_{i}
), and this makes
\psi^{-1}(t)
a sub-bundle of
\bnd
. This gives a useful description of a preimage
\psi^{-1}(t)
, which we record as follows.

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
\bnd,\bnd^{\prime}
be
k
-fold abelian bundles and let
\psi:\bnd\to\bnd^{\prime}
be a totally surjective bundle morphism. Then for any
t\in\bnd^{\prime}
the preimage
\psi^{-1}(t)
is a sub-bundle of
\bnd
, with factors
\psi_{i}^{-1}(\pi_{i}(t))
and structure groups
\ker(\alpha_{i})
,
i\in[k]
.

Proof.

One way to prove this was described above, namely by restricting the bundle structure from
K
to
\psi^{-1}(t)
via the bijections
(x,y)\mapsto x
. This way has the advantage of showing how all these preimages live inside the bundle
K
.

Alternatively, one may check directly that the stated factors and structure groups do indeed make
\psi^{-1}(t)
a sub-bundle of
\bnd
, essentially by repeating the arguments in the previous proof.
∎

9.2
Fibre-surjective morphisms and restricted morphisms

This section describes specific kinds of morphisms between nilspaces. This material is useful mainly in the topological part of the theory, but since the material itself is purely algebraic we include it here.

To begin with, recall that on one hand, by Proposition
9.2
, a morphism of nilspaces is a bundle morphism of the corresponding abelian bundles, and on the other hand, for abelian bundles we have a notion of totally-surjective bundle morphisms (recall Definition
9.1
). The following definition gives a counterpart of this notion for nilspaces, as shown in Lemma
9.8
.

Definition 9.7

(Fibre-surjective morphism)
.

Let
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
be nilspaces. A morphism
\psi:\ns\to\ns^{\prime}
is said to be
fibre surjective
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the image of an equivalence class of
\sim_{n}
in
\ns
is a full equivalence class of
\sim_{n}
in
\ns^{\prime}
.

Note that if
\ns
is
k
-step then
\ns^{\prime}
must also be
k
-step. Indeed, if
\q^{\prime}
is a
(k+1)
-corner on
\ns^{\prime}
, then any two completions
\q_{1},\q_{2}
of
\q^{\prime}
satisfy
\q_{1}(1^{k+1})\sim_{k}\q_{2}(1^{k+1})
, so by surjectivity there must be a class of
\sim_{k}
in
\ns
in which some element is sent to
\q_{1}(1^{k+1})
and another is sent to
\q_{2}(1^{k+1})
by
\psi
. Since
\ns
is
k
-step this class is a singleton, so
\q_{1}(1^{k+1})=\q_{2}(1^{k+1})
.

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
be
k
-step nilspaces. A map
\psi:\ns\to\ns^{\prime}
is a fibre-surjective morphism if and only if it is a totally surjective morphism between the corresponding
k
-fold abelian bundles.

Proof.

A totally surjective bundle morphism between
k
-step nilspaces is fibre-surjective by definition. To see the converse, note first that by Proposition
9.2
we know that
\psi
is a bundle morphism. Then, since the orbits of the groups
\ab_{i},\ab_{i}^{\prime}
are equivalence classes of
\sim_{i}
, the condition for fibre-surjectivity implies that all the structure morphisms from Definition
9.1
are surjective.
∎

The main result concerning fibre-surjective morphisms here is the following lemma stating that they are actually factor maps. More precisely, letting
\sim
denote the equivalence relation on
\ns
defined by
x\sim y
if and only if
\psi(x)=\psi(y)
, then
\ns/\sim
with the quotient cubespace structure is isomorphic to
\ns^{\prime}
. Thus
\ns^{\prime}
can be viewed as a factor of
\ns
in the sense of Definition
8.2
.

Lemma 9.9
.

Let
\ns,\ns^{\prime}
be
k
-step nilspaces and let
\psi:\ns\to\ns^{\prime}
be a fibre-surjective morphism. Then every cube
\q^{\prime}\in\cu^{n}(\ns^{\prime})
can be lifted to a cube
\q\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
such that
\psi\co\q=\q^{\prime}
. In other words,
\ns^{\prime}
is a factor nilspace of
\ns
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
. For
k=0
the claim is clear (a
0
-step non-empty nilspace is a 1-point space). Let
k>0
and suppose that the claim holds for
k-1
. Viewing
\psi
as a bundle morphism, we see that it induces a fibre-surjective map
\psi^{\prime}
from
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
to
\cF_{k-1}(\ns^{\prime})
. By induction, there exists
\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
such that
\psi^{\prime}\co\q_{1}=\pi_{k-1}\co\q^{\prime}
. Lifting
\q_{1}
to a cube
\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
(using Lemma
8.11
), the fact that
\psi
preserves the fibres of
\sim_{k-1}
implies that
\pi_{k-1}\co\psi\co\q_{2}=\q^{\prime}
. Hence, the map
\q_{3}=\psi\co\q_{2}-\q^{\prime}
is in
\cu^{n}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}^{\prime}))
. Now, letting
\alpha_{k}:\ab_{k}\to\ab_{k}^{\prime}
be the surjective homomorphism, note that if there was a cube
\q_{4}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
such that of
\alpha_{k}\co\q_{4}=\q_{3}
, then we would have
\psi\co(\q_{2}-\q_{4})=\psi\co\q_{2}-\alpha_{k}\co\q_{4}=\q^{\prime}+\q_{3}-\alpha_{k}\co\q_{4}=\q
, and so we would have a lift
\q=\q_{2}-\q_{4}
as desired. To see that
\q_{4}
exists, note that this is trivial for
n\leq k
(by
k
-ergodicity of degree-
k
abelian groups), and for
n\geq k+1
note that we can take an
n
-corner of
\q_{3}
, lift this under
\alpha_{k}
to an
n
-corner on
\cD_{k}(\ab_{k})
(by induction on
n
), and then complete it (uniquely) to an
n
-cube
\q_{4}
, which then has to satisfy
\alpha_{k}\co\q_{4}=\q_{3}
.
∎

We now move on to another specific type of morphism.

Definition 9.10

(Restricted morphism)
.

Let
P
and
\ns
be cubespaces, let
S
be a subcubespace of
P
, and let

be an arbitrary function. We define the
restricted morphism set

\hom_{f}(P,\ns)
to be the set of morphisms
P\to\ns
whose restriction to
S
equals
f
.

For instance, given an abelian group
\ab
, the set
\hom_{S\to 0}(P,\cD_{k}(\ab))
(used below) is the set of morphisms
\phi
from
P
to
\cD_{k}(\ab)
such that
\phi(S)=0_{\ab}
. Note that this set is itself an abelian group under pointwise addition in
\ab
.

We shall now describe
\hom_{f}(P,\ns)
as a bundle.

Lemma 9.11
.

Let
P
be a subcubespace of
\{0,1\}^{n}
with the extension property, let
S
be a subcubespace of
P
with the extension property in
P
, let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace and

be a morphism. Then
\hom_{f}(P,\ns)
is a
k
-fold abelian bundle that is a sub-bundle of
\ns^{P}
with factors
\hom_{\pi_{i}\co f}(P,\ns_{i})
and structure groups
\hom_{S\to 0}(P,\cD_{i}(\ab_{i}))
, where
\ab_{i}
is the
i
-th structure group of
\ns
.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
k
to check the condition in Definition
9.3
. For
k=1
we know that
\ns
is the principal homogeneous space of an abelian group
\ab
, and then
\hom_{f}(P,\ns)
is a principal homogeneous space of
\hom_{S\to 0}(P,\cD_{1}(\ab))
in
\ns^{P}
.

For
k>1
suppose that
\ns
is a
k
-step nilspace and the result is already established for
\ns_{k-1}=\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
. Let
f_{2}:P\to\ns_{k-1}
be a morphism with restriction
f_{2}|_{S}=\pi_{k-1}\co f
. We claim that

can be lifted to an element

.

Indeed, by Lemma
8.11
, there is a lift

of

. Then the function

is a morphism from
S
to
\cD_{k}(\ab_{k})
. By the extension property there is a morphism

Note that any other lift

must satisfy

∎

We end this section with a result relating various fibre-surjective morphisms.

Lemma 9.12
.

Let
P\subset\{0,1\}^{n}

\{0,1\}^{n}
and

be a subcubespace with the extension property in

For statement

from Definition

9.3
Extensions and cocycles

This subsection treats an important method of building a new nilspace from an old one. The resulting new nilspace is called an extension, and consists in an abelian bundle over the old nilspace, equipped with a cube structure adequately related to the one on the old nilspace. The formal definition is the following.

Definition 9.13
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace. A
degree-
k
extension
of
\ns
is an abelian bundle
\nss
over
\ns
, with structure group
\ab
and bundle map
\pi:\nss\to\ns
, such that
\nss
is a cubespace with the following properties:

(i)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the map
\q\mapsto\pi\co\q
is a surjection
\cu^{n}(\nss)\to\cu^{n}(\ns)
.

(ii)

For every
\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\nss)
we have

\{\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(\nss):\pi\co\q_{2}=\pi\co\q_{1}\}=\{\q_{1}+\q_{3}:\q_{3}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k}(\ab))\}.

(22)

The extension
\nss
is called a
split extension
if there is a (cube-preserving) morphism
m:\ns\to\nss
such that
\pi\co m
is the identity map on
\ns
.

This notion is motivated by the fact that, by the bundle-decomposition result (Theorem
8.19
), any
k
-step nilspace can be built up from the one-point space by
k
consecutive extensions of increasing degree.

With each extension one can associate a certain function called a cocycle, which encodes algebraic information about the extension. These cocycles can then be used to parametrize the different extensions of a nilspace. This is very useful in particular in the topological part of the theory, to classify compact nilspaces.

To define cocycles we use the following notation. Recall that
\aut(\{0,1\}^{k})
is generated by permutations of
[k]
and coordinate-reflections. For
\theta\in\aut(\{0,1\}^{k})
we write
r(\theta)
for the number of reflections involved in
\theta
. Equivalently,
r(\theta)
is the number of coordinates equal to 1 in
\theta(0^{k})
.

Definition 9.14

(Cocycle)
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace, let
\ab
be an abelian group, and let
k\geq-1
be an integer. A
cocycle of degree

k
is a function
\rho:\cu^{k+1}(\ns)\to\ab
with the following two properties:

(i)

If
\q\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
and
\theta\in\aut(\{0,1\}^{k+1})
then
\rho(\q\co\theta)=(-1)^{r(\theta)}\rho(\q)
.

(ii)

If
\q_{3}
is the concatenation of adjacent cubes
\q_{1},\q_{2}\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
then
\rho(\q_{3})=\rho(\q_{1})+\rho(\q_{2})
.

The set of
\ab
-valued cocycles of degree
k
is an abelian group under pointwise addition, denoted by
Y_{k}(\ns,\ab)
. Note that
Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
is just
\ab^{\ns}
.

In going through the results below on extensions and cocycles, it can be useful to have a concrete case in mind as a source of intuition.

Example 9.15
.

Consider the Heisenberg nilmanifold
H/\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbb{R}&\mathbb{R}\\[1.00006pt]
&1&\mathbb{R}\\[1.00006pt]
&&1\end{pmatrix}/\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}\\[1.00006pt]
&1&\mathbb{Z}\\[1.00006pt]
&&1\end{pmatrix}
from Example
5.2
. As mentioned there, one can identify
H/\Gamma
with
[0,1)^{3}
. Let us write this as
H/\Gamma\cong\begin{pmatrix}1&[0,1)&[0,1)\\[1.00006pt]
&1&[0,1)\\[1.00006pt]
&&1\end{pmatrix}
. Now, identifying the circle group
\mathbb{T}
with
[0,1)
, we have that the map

\pi:H/\Gamma\to\mathbb{T}^{2},\;\;\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{3}\\[1.00006pt]
&1&x_{2}\\[1.00006pt]
&&1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(x_{1},x_{2})

is a bundle map showing that
H/\Gamma
is an abelian bundle with fibre
\mathbb{T}
over
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Moreover, since
H/\Gamma
is a 2-step nilspace, by the bundle characterization we have that it is a degree-2 extension of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by
\mathbb{T}
(recall the proof of Lemma
8.25
). We can then find a cocycle of degree 2 associated with this extension, with the following procedure (which will be treated more formally and generally below). Consider the map
\cs:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to H/\Gamma
,
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&0\\[1.00006pt]
&1&x_{2}\\[1.00006pt]
&&1\end{pmatrix}
. This is a cross section for this extension, i.e. it satisfies
\pi\co\cs(x)=x
on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
(see Definition
9.20
). We then have that for every
x\in H/\Gamma
, the points
x
and
\cs\co\pi(x)
are in the same fibre of the map
\pi
. This fibre is an affine version of
\mathbb{T}
, so we can take the difference
\cs\co\pi(x)-x\in\mathbb{T}
. We have thus defined a function
f:H/\Gamma\to\mathbb{T}
,
x\mapsto\cs\co\pi(x)-x
. Now consider the function
\rho
on
\cu^{3}(H/\Gamma)
sending a cube
\q
to
\sigma_{3}(f\co\q)=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{3}}(-1)^{|v|}f(\q(v))
. Using the defining property (
22
) of the extension, it can be checked that
\rho(\q)
is unchanged if we vary the values
\q(v)
within their
\mathbb{T}
-fibres in
H/\Gamma
. More precisely, for every other cube
\q^{\prime}
such that
\pi\co\q^{\prime}=\pi\co\q
, we have
\rho(\q^{\prime})=\rho(\q)
. (We shall prove this more generally in Lemma
9.21
.) Thus
\rho
can be viewed as a function on
\cu^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
, and one can check that it is a cocycle (see Lemma
9.21
). Note that, while
\rho
was defined using the structure of
H/\Gamma
, we end up with a function of cubes on
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. This observation is important because it suggests that we may be able to go in the opposite direction, that is, that given a cocycle on the cubes of a given nilspace
\ns
(here
\ns=\mathbb{T}^{2}
), it may be possible to construct an extension of
\ns
that has some cross section with associated cocycle equal to
\rho
. This will be confirmed in Proposition
9.26
and subsequent results.

Before we go into the details indicated by the above example, let us describe how, given a cocycle
\rho
of degree
k
, we can define a new cocycle of degree
k+1
by taking the difference of
\rho
on opposite faces of
(k+2)
-cubes. This will lead to the notion of a coboundary.

Definition 9.16
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\rho:\cu^{k}(\ns)\to\ab
be a cocycle. We define
\partial\rho:\cu^{k+1}(\ns)\to\ab
by
\partial\rho(\q)=\rho(\q(\cdot,0))-\rho(\q(\cdot,1))
.

Lemma 9.17
.

Let
k\geq 0
and let
\rho
be a cocycle of degree
k-1
. Then
\partial\rho
is a cocycle of degree
k
.

Proof.

We check that properties (i) and (ii) from Definition
9.14
hold.

Let
\theta
be an automorphism of
\{0,1\}^{k+1}
and note that there is a unique
j\in[k+1]
such that
\theta(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k+1)$}
or
1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k+1)$}
. Suppose first that
\theta(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k+1)$}
. Let
\theta^{\prime}
be the restriction of
\theta
to
\{0,1\}^{k}
, with the image of
\theta^{\prime}
being the cube obtained from
\{0,1\}^{k+1}
by omitting the
j
-th coordinate. For
i=0,1
let
\q_{j,i}
denote the restriction of
\q
to the face
v\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}=i
. We then have

\displaystyle\partial\rho(\q\co\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho(\q\co\theta(\cdot,0))-\rho(\q\co\theta(\cdot,1))\hskip 56.9055pt=\;\;\rho(\q_{j,0}\co\theta^{\prime})-\rho(\q_{j,1}\co\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{|\theta^{\prime}(0^{k})|}\rho(\q_{j,0})-(-1)^{|\theta^{\prime}(0^{k})|}\rho(\q_{j,1})\;\hskip 5.69046pt=\;\;(-1)^{|\theta(0^{k+1})|}(\rho(\q_{j,0})-\rho(\q_{j,1}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{|\theta(0^{k+1})|}\partial\rho(\q).

If
\theta(v)\scalebox{0.8}{$(j)$}
was
1-v\scalebox{0.8}{$(k+1)$}
, then instead of the expression after the second equality above, we would have
\rho(\q_{j,1}\co\theta^{\prime})-\rho(\q_{j,0}\co\theta^{\prime})=-(-1)^{|\theta^{\prime}(0^{k})|}(\rho(\q_{j,0})-\rho(\q_{j,1}))=(-1)^{|\theta(0^{k+1})|}\partial\rho(\q)
. This proves (i).

For property (ii), suppose that
\q^{\prime\prime}
is the concatenation of
\q,\q^{\prime}\in\cu^{k+1}(\ns)
. Then we have

\partial\rho(\q^{\prime\prime})=\rho(\q(\cdot,0))-\rho(\q^{\prime}(\cdot,1))=\rho(\q(\cdot,0))-\rho(\q(\cdot,1))+\rho(\q^{\prime}(\cdot,0))-\rho(\q^{\prime}(\cdot,1))=\partial\rho(\q)+\partial\rho(\q^{\prime}).\qed

Note also that if
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
are two cocycles of degree
k-1
, then
\partial(\rho_{1}+\rho_{2})=\partial\rho_{1}+\partial\rho_{2}
. Thus, for every
k\geq 0
the map
\partial
is a homomorphism from
Y_{k-1}(\ns,\ab)
to
Y_{k}(\ns,\ab)
.

Recall from Definition
4.22
that given an abelian group
\ab
and a function

, we write
\sigma_{n}(f)
for
\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{n}}(-1)^{|v|}f(v)
.

Definition 9.18
.

A
coboundary
of degree
k
is an element of the abelian group
\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
. Equivalently, a coboundary of degree
k
is an element of
Y_{k}(\ns,\ab)
of the form
\q\mapsto\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q)
for some function
f:\ns\to\ab
. We then define the abelian group

H_{k}(\ns,\ab)=Y_{k}(\ns,\ab)\;/\;\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab).

(23)

Example 9.19
.

In the case of degree-2 cocycles
\cu^{3}(\mathbb{T}^{2})\to\mathbb{T}
, such as those constructed using the Heisenberg nilmanifold in Example
9.15
, we have that two such cocycles
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
represent the same element of
H_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{2},\mathbb{T})
if and only if the difference map
\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}
is of the form
\q\to\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{3}}(-1)^{|v|}f\co\q(v)
for some function
f:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}
.

Our aim now is to show that every degree-
k
extension of
\ns
can be represented by an element of
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
. This will eventually be achieved in Corollary
9.29
. Our first step in this direction is the following.

9.3.1
Associating an element of
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
with a given degree-
k
extension

Definition 9.20
.

Let
\nss
be a degree-
k
extension of
\ns
with structure group
\ab
and projection
\pi:\nss\to\ns
. A
cross section
for this extension is a map
\cs:\ns\to\nss
such that
\pi\co\cs
is the identity on
\ns
.

Lemma 9.21
.

Let
\nss
be a degree-
k
extension of
\ns
with structure group
\ab
and projection
\pi:\nss\to\ns
, let
\cs
be a cross section, and define
f:\nss\to\ab
by
f(y)=\cs\co\pi(y)-y
. Define
\rho:\cu^{k+1}(\ns)\to\ab
by
\rho(\q)=\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q^{\prime})
for some
\q^{\prime}\in\cu^{k+1}(\nss)
such that
\pi\co\q^{\prime}=\q
. Then
\rho
is a cocycle of degree
k
. We shall refer to
\rho
as the
cocycle generated by the cross section

\cs
, and denote it
\rho_{\cs}
.

Proof.

We first check that
\rho
is well-defined. Suppose that
\q^{\prime\prime}
is another
(k+1)
-cube on
\nss
such that
\pi\co\q^{\prime\prime}=\pi\co\q^{\prime}=\q
. Then by definition of the extension we have
\q^{\prime\prime}-\q^{\prime}=\q_{2}\in\cu^{k+1}(\mathcal{D}_{k}(\ab))
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|v|}f\co(\q^{\prime}+\q_{2})(v)\;\;\;=\;\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|v|}\big(\;\cs\co\pi(\q^{\prime}(v))-(\q^{\prime}(v)+\q_{2}(v))\;\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q^{\prime})-\sigma_{k+1}(\q_{2})\;\;\;=\;\;\;\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q^{\prime}).

Let us now check that the cocycle properties hold.

Let
\theta\in\aut(\{0,1\}^{k+1})
. Then

\displaystyle\rho(\q\co\theta)\;=\;\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\,(\q\co\theta))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|v|-|\theta(v)|}(-1)^{|\theta(v)|}f(\q\co\theta(v))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r(\theta)}\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|\theta(v)|}f(\q\co\theta(v))\;=\;(-1)^{r(\theta)}\rho(\q),

where we have used that
|\,|\theta(v)|-|v|\,|
is exactly the number of coordinates that
\theta

.

The second property follows from the basic property of
\sigma_{k+1}
observed in (

∎

Let us now examine how the cocycle

varies when we change the cross section

.

Lemma 9.22
.

Let
\nss
be a degree-
k
extension of
\ns
with structure group
\ab
and projection
\pi:\nss\to\ns
, and let
\cs:\ns\to\nss
be a cross section. Then for every other cross section
\cs^{\prime}
, we have
\rho_{\cs^{\prime}}\in\rho_{\cs}+\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
. Conversely, every element of
\rho_{\cs}+\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
is a cocycle
\rho_{\cs^{\prime}}
for some cross section
\cs^{\prime}
.

Thus the coset
\rho_{\cs}+\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
is the set of
all cocycles generated by cross sections for the extension
\nss
. This coset is the element of
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
that we associate with the extension
\nss
.

Proof.

Letting
\q^{\prime}
be any lift of
\q
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\cs^{\prime}}(\q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(\cs^{\prime}\co\pi(\q^{\prime})-\q^{\prime})=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|v|}\big(\,\cs^{\prime}\co\pi(\q^{\prime}(v))-\q^{\prime}(v)\,\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k+1}}(-1)^{|v|}[\,\cs^{\prime}\co\pi(\q^{\prime}(v))-\cs\co\pi(\q^{\prime}(v))+\cs\co\pi(\q^{\prime}(v))-\q^{\prime}(v)\,]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{k+1}(\cs\co\pi(\q^{\prime})-\q^{\prime})+\sigma_{k+1}(\cs^{\prime}\co\q-\cs\co\q)=\rho_{\cs}(\q)+\sigma_{k+1}((\cs^{\prime}-\cs)\co\q).

Since
\cs^{\prime}-\cs
is in

, the function
\q\mapsto\sigma_{k+1}((\cs^{\prime}-\cs)\co\q)
is a coboundary of degree
k
as claimed.

Conversely, given any coboundary
\q\mapsto\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q)
for some

, we have
\rho_{\cs}(\q)+\sigma_{k+1}(f\co\q)=\sigma_{k+1}((\cs+f)\co\pi(\q^{\prime})-\q^{\prime})=\rho_{\cs+f}
, where
\cs+f:x\mapsto\cs(x)+f(x)
is indeed a cross-section (where

denotes the action of
\ab
on
\nss
).
∎

Thus, every degree-

extension
\nss
of
\ns

\ab
has an associated element
\rho_{\cs}+\partial^{k+1}Y_{-1}(\ns,\ab)
of

.

Definition 9.23
.

We say that two degree-

extensions of
\ns
with structure group
\ab
are

if they correspond to the same element of

.

Note that if
\nss
is a split extension, with cube-preserving cross section

, then
\rho(\q)=\sigma_{k+1}(\cs\co\q-\q^{\prime})=0
since
\cs\co\q
is a
(k+1)
-cube on
\nss
with image
\q
under
\pi
, so

must be in

. Thus the element of

corresponding to

We shall now go in the opposite direction, to show that given any element of

, there is a unique class of equivalent extensions of degree
k
over
\ns
with group

corresponding to this element. In

]
, Camarena and Szegedy leave this task for the topological part of the paper, to avoid repetition, and in that part they combine the purely algebraic construction with topological tools to obtain, given a measurable cocycle, an extension which is also a

9.3.2
Obtaining an extension from a cocycle

Let
\ns
be a nilspace and let
\ab
be an abelian group. Our aim here is to show that any
\ab
-valued cocycle
\rho
on
\cu^{k}(\ns)
yields a
\ab
-bundle over
\ns
which admits a compatible nilspace structure.

For each

we denote by
\cu^{k}_{x}(\ns)
the set of
k

\pi(\q):=\q(0^{k})=x
. We consider the restrictions of the cocycle
\rho
to each such set
\cu^{k}_{x}(\ns)
, denoting such a restriction by
\rho_{x}
. The extension that we shall construct consists essentially of the functions
\rho_{x}
, but we need to define an action of
\ab
on these functions such that there is a natural projection from this space to
\ns
that respects the orbits of the action. This leads to the following natural definition of the extension.

Definition 9.24
.

Let
\rho:\cu^{k}(\ns)\to\ab
be a cocycle of degree
k-1
. We then define the set
M=M(\rho)
as follows.

M=\bigcup_{x\in\ns}\{\rho_{x}+z\,:\,z\in\ab\}.

(24)

We define the map
\tilde{\pi}:M\to\ns
by
\rho_{x}+z\mapsto x
, for every

and
z\in\ab
. We also define an action of
\ab
on

by
(\rho_{x}+z,z^{\prime})\mapsto\rho_{x}+z+z^{\prime}

One checks easily that

is a
\ab

\ns

\tilde{\pi}
.

Now we shall define cubes on

. Given any function

the function
\{0,1\}^{k}\to\ab
,
v\mapsto\rho_{x}(v)-f(v)
, where

.

Definition 9.25
.

We define
\cu^{k}(M)
to be the set of functions

such that
\tilde{\pi}\co f\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
and

\rho(\tilde{\pi}\co f)=\sigma_{k}(a).

(25)

For
n\neq k
, a function

is declared to be in
\cu^{n}(M)
if
\tilde{\pi}\co f\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
and every
k
-dimensional face-restriction of

\cu^{k}(M)
.
4
4

4

If

We can now complete the main task of this subsection.

Proposition 9.26
.

The set
M
together with the cube sets
\cu^{n}(M)
,
n\geq 0
, is a nilspace.

Proof.

To see the composition axiom, suppose that
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to M
is a cube and that
\phi:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
is a morphism. If
m<k
then

just by Definition
9.25
and the composition axiom for
\ns
. Supposing then that

, it suffices to check that for every face map
\phi^{\prime}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{m}
we have that

. Now
\phi\co\phi^{\prime}
is a morphism
\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
. If
\phi\co\phi^{\prime}
is not injective then we are done because
\phi\co\phi^{\prime}
factors through a lower-dimensional morphism
\psi:\{0,1\}^{\ell}\to\{0,1\}^{n}

and we have that

is in
\cu^{\ell}(M)
as in the case

above. If

is injective, then using Lemma

repeatedly we see that it is a concatenation of face maps
\phi_{j}:\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
(modulo automorphisms of
\{0,1\}^{k}
). For each of these face maps, we have that

satisfies (
25
) by assumption. It follows that
f\co\phi\co\phi^{\prime}
also satisfies (
25
), since both functions involved in this equation are additive on adjacent cubes (for the cocycle this holds by definition, and for
\sigma_{k}
it follows from (
14

To see the completion axiom, note that, since the axiom holds for
\ns
, by the argument establishing completion in the proof of Lemma
8.20
, it suffices to check that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\cu^{n}(\ns)=\{\tilde{\pi}\co\q:\q\in\cu^{n}(M)\}
and for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(M)
we have

\{\q_{2}\in\cu^{n}(M):\tilde{\pi}\co\q=\tilde{\pi}\co\q_{2}\}=\{\q+\q_{3}:\q_{3}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-1}(\ab))\}.

(26)

For

this equation follows from the definition of
\cu^{n}(M)
and the fact that
\cD_{k-1}(\ab)
is
(k-1)
-fold ergodic. To see the case

, recall that
\q_{3}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-1}(\ab))
if and only if
\sigma_{k}(\q_{3}\co\phi)=0
for every
k
-face map
\phi
into
\{0,1\}^{n}
.
∎

Note that if
\ns
is of step
s
, then

is of step
\max(s+1,k-1)
, as can be seen by checking the uniqueness of completion in the proof of Lemma
8.20
. The typical examples of extensions involve extending a

-step nilspace by a cocycle of degree

to obtain a

), but it can be useful to consider a degree-

s

(see for instance Lemma

).

Recall from Lemma
9.21
that given a degree-
k
extension of a nilspace, every cross section
\cs
for this extension generates a cocycle
\rho_{\cs}
. As our last main goal in this subsection, we want to show that, up to isomorphisms of nilspaces, every extension of
\ns
by

is of the form

above for some cocycle

. The formal statement will be given as Corollary

. The main idea is that we can just take
\rho
to be the cocycle generated by some cross section for the extension.

First let us confirm a fact that ought to be true, namely that for

itself, the obvious cross-section does indeed generate

.

Lemma 9.27
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace, let
\ab
be an abelian group, and let
\rho:\cu^{k}(\ns)\to\ab
be a cocycle. Let
\cs:\ns\to M(\rho)
be the cross section
x\mapsto\rho_{x}
. Then
\rho_{\cs}=\rho
.

(In particular, every cocycle can be viewed as a cocycle generated by a cross section of some extension.)

Proof.

Let
\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
and let
\q^{\prime}
be a cube in
\cu^{k}(M)
with
\tilde{\pi}\co\q^{\prime}=\q
. We thus have
\q^{\prime}(v)=\rho_{\tilde{\pi}(\q^{\prime}(v))}-a(v)=\rho_{\q(v)}-a(v)
for a
\ab
-valued function
a
satisfying
\sigma_{k}(a)=\rho(\q)
. Then by definition of
\rho_{\cs}
, we have

\rho_{\cs}(\q)=\sigma_{k}\big(\cs\co\tilde{\pi}(\q^{\prime})-\q^{\prime}\big)=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\big(\cs\co\q(v)-(\rho_{\q(v)}-a(v))\big)=\sum_{v}(-1)^{|v|}a(v)=\rho(\q).\qed

Lemma 9.28
.

Let
\ns
be a nilspace. Let
\nss
be a degree-
k
extension of
\ns
by an abelian group
\ab
. Let
\cs:\ns\to\nss
be a cross-section and let
\rho=\rho_{\cs}
be the associated cocycle. Then
\nss
is isomorphic as a nilspace to the extension
M=M(\rho)
in (
24
).

Proof.

Let
\pi:\nss\to\ns
be the projection for the extension. The isomorphism is given by the following map:

\theta:\nss\to M,\;\;x\mapsto\rho_{\pi(x)}+(x-\cs\co\pi(x)).

(27)

It is easily checked that
\theta
is a bijection. We claim that for every
\q\in\cu^{n}(\nss)
, the map
\theta\co\q
satisfies the conditions in Definition
9.25
. We check this for

. The first condition is that
\tilde{\pi}\co\theta\co\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
. But
\tilde{\pi}\co\theta\co\q=\pi\co\q
, so the condition holds. Next, consider the function
a(v)=\rho_{\tilde{\pi}\co\theta\co\q}(v)-\theta\co\q(v)=\cs\co\pi(\q(v))-\q(v)
. The second condition is that
a
satisfies (
25
), that is
\rho(\tilde{\pi}\co\theta\co\q)=\sigma_{k}(a)
. But this holds indeed, since
\rho(\tilde{\pi}\co\theta\co\q)=\rho(\pi\co\q)=\sigma_{k}(a)
, by definition of
\rho_{\cs}
(recall Lemma
9.21
). The cases
n\neq k
follow similarly.
∎

Recall from Subsection
9.3
that
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
denotes the quotient of the abelian group of degree-
k
cocycles by the subgroup of coboundaries. Combining the results from this section, we now deduce the following correspondence between
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
and the degree-
k
extensions of
\ns
up to isomorphisms of nilspaces.

Corollary 9.29
.

Let
\Phi
denote the map which sends each class
C\in H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
to the isomorphism class of
M(\rho)
, for any choice of
\rho\in C
. Then
\Phi
is a surjection from
H_{k}(\ns,\ab)
to the set of isomorphism classes of degree-
k
extensions of
\ns
by
\ab
.

Proof.

We first check that
\Phi(C)
is well-defined. If
\rho^{\prime}
is another element of the class
C
of
\rho
then, as seen in Lemma
9.22
, there is another cross section
\cs^{\prime}
of
\nss
such that
\rho^{\prime}=\rho_{\cs^{\prime}}
. Then, by Lemma
9.28
we have
M(\rho)\cong\nss\cong M(\rho^{\prime})
. To see that
\Phi
is surjective, note that given a degree-
k
extension
\nss
of
\ns
, there is a cross section
\cs:\ns\to\nss
and corresponding cocycle
\rho=\rho_{\cs}
, and so the isomorphism class of
\nss
is
\Phi(C)
where
C
is the class of
\rho
.
∎

Remark 9.30
.

Note that
\Phi
need not be injective. This is a counterpart for nilspaces of known facts concerning group extensions, for instance that the abelian group of classes of extensions
1\to A\to E\to G\to 1
of a group
G
by an abelian group
A
can be larger than the set of extensions
E
themselves up to group isomorphism.

We end this subsection with an alternative formulation of condition (
25
) defining cubes on the extension
M(\rho)
. This formulation uses tricubes and is very useful in particular in the topological part of the theory.

Recall from Definition
7.13
and Lemma
7.16
the concept of a morphism from a tricube
T_{k}
into a nilspace
\ns
, and the tricube composition
t\co\omega_{k}
(where
\omega_{k}
is the outer-point map from Definition
7.15
). Let
\ab
be an abelian group, and let
\xi:\cu^{k}(\ns)\to\ab
be an arbitrary function. We then define the alternating sum

\beta(t,\xi)=\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\,\xi(t\co\trem_{v}).

(28)

For a cocycle
\rho
, the following result reduces
\beta(t,\rho)
to the value of
\rho
on the outer-point set of
T_{k}
.

Lemma 9.31
.

Let
t:T_{k}\to\ns
be a morphism into a nilspace
\ns
, and let
\rho
be a cocycle of degree
k-1
on
\ns
. Then

\beta(t,\rho)=\rho(t\co\omega_{k}).

Proof.

This follows from the fact that the outer-point map
\omega_{k}
can be expressed as a sequence of concatenations of cubes of the form
\trem_{v}
composed with automorphisms of
\{0,1\}^{k}
.
∎

Given a cube
\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
, let us denote by
\hom_{\q\co\omega_{k}^{-1}}(T_{k},\ns)
the set of morphisms
t:T_{k}\to\ns
that agree with
\q
on the subset
\{-1,1\}^{k}
. We can now give the alternative form of (
25
).

Lemma 9.32
.

Condition (
25
) is equivalent to the following equation holding for some
(
and therefore every
)

t\in\hom_{(\tilde{\pi}\co f)\co\omega_{k}^{-1}}(T_{k},\ns)\colon

\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\,f(v)(t\co\trem_{v})=0.

(29)

Proof.

Given such a morphism
t
, note that for each
v\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, the map
t\co\trem_{v}
is a cube in
\cu^{k}(\ns)
with base-point
t\co\psi_{v}(0^{k})=t\co\omega_{k}(v)=\tilde{\pi}\co f(v)
. Therefore, we have trivially that
\rho_{\tilde{\pi}\co f(v)}(t\co\trem_{v})=\rho(t\co\trem_{v})
. By Lemma
9.31
, we then have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\,f(v)(t\co\trem_{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\,\rho_{\tilde{\pi}\co f(v)}(t\co\trem_{v})\;-\;(-1)^{|v|}\,a(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta(t,\rho)-\sum_{v\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{|v|}\,a(v)\;=\;\rho(\tilde{\pi}\co f)-\sigma_{k}(a),

and the result follows.
∎

Given a nilspace

Lemma 9.35
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace, let

Recall that by Definition

if the

Next we claim that if

then

satisfies the ergodicity axiom. To see this let

be arbitrary, with

\alpha(\pi_{k-1}(x_{0}))=\pi_{k-1}(x_{1})
and

where
\q_{i}(0)=x_{i}
,
\q_{i}(1)=y_{i}
for

, and where
\q_{0},\q_{1}
are in
\cu^{1}(\ns)
by ergodicity in
\ns
. We are claiming that

, i.e. that
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{1+i}(\ns)

, we have that
\langle\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0},\alpha\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{1+i}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))

\langle\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0},\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{1}\rangle_{i}=\pi_{k-1}\co\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}
. Recalling Remark

then
\pi_{k-1}\co\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{i+1}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))

.

To check the completion axiom, let
\q^{\prime}
be an

-corner on
\cT
. Thus, as an
n
-corner on
\ns\Join_{i}\ns
, the map
\q^{\prime}
equals
\q_{0}^{\prime}\times\q_{1}^{\prime}

\q_{0}^{\prime},\q_{1}^{\prime}

-corners on
\ns
. We know from Lemma
7.21
that
\q^{\prime}
can be completed to an

-cube on
\ns\Join_{i}\ns
, but here we need to complete it on

. Then by Lemma

, that is
\langle\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0},\alpha\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{n+i}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. Let us lift the latter cube to
\tilde{\q}\in\cu^{n+i}(\ns)
, that is
\pi_{k-1}\co\tilde{\q}=\langle\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0},\alpha\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0}\rangle_{i}
. Now let

be the

\ns

. (This was shown to be a corner in the proof of Lemma

, we have that

, and so

(as in previous arguments, e.g. the proof of Lemma

which agrees with

at every

\overline{\q}
is still a lift of
\langle\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0},\alpha\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0}\rangle_{i}
. It follows that
\overline{\q}=\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}
for some completion
\q_{1}
of
\q_{1}^{\prime}
such that
\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{1}(1^{n})=\alpha\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q_{0}(1^{n})
, and so
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
completes
\q^{\prime}
on
\cT
.
∎

The nilspace that captures whether
\alpha
can be lifted is the factor

\cT^{*}:=\cF_{k-1}(\cT(\alpha,\ns,i)).

(31)

In order to establish the main result, we need to relate
\cT^{*}
to
\ns
. To do so, the key fact is that each element of
\cT^{*}
corresponds in a natural way to some local translation on
\ns
(recall Definition
8.26
). To state this fact formally, we shall be careful to distinguish the projection
\cT\to\cT^{*}
, which we denote by
\pi_{k-1,\cT}
, from the projection
\ns\to\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
, denoted by
\pi_{k-1,\ns}
.

Proposition 9.36
.

Let
\tau\in\cT^{*}
, and fix any
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\cT
such that
\tau=\pi_{k-1,\cT}((x_{0},x_{1}))
. Then
\tau
is the following equivalence class of points in
\cT
:

\tau=\{(y_{0},y_{1})\in\ns\times\ns:\pi_{k-1,\ns}(y_{0})=\pi_{k-1,\ns}(x_{0}),\;\pi_{k-1,\ns}(y_{1})=\pi_{k-1,\ns}(x_{1}),\;y_{1}=\phi_{x_{0},x_{1}}(y_{0})\},

(32)

for the local translation
\phi_{x_{0},x_{1}}
.

Proof.

Given an element
\tau=\pi_{k-1,\cT}((x_{0},x_{1}))\in\cT^{*}
, first we have that
\alpha\co\pi_{k-1,\ns}(x_{0})=\pi_{k-1}(x_{1})
(since
(x_{0},x_{1})\in\cT
), and so the fibres
F_{0}=\{y_{0}\in\ns:y_{0}\sim_{k-1}x_{0}\}
and
F_{1}=\{y_{1}\in\ns:y_{1}\sim_{k-1}x_{1}\}
satisfy
\alpha(F_{0})=F_{1}
. The equivalence class
\tau
is the set of couples
(y_{0},y_{1})\in\cT
such that
(x_{0},x_{1})\sim_{k-1}(y_{0},y_{1})
in
\cT
, i.e. such that there exists a cube
\q\in\cu^{k}(\cT)
satisfying
\q(v)=(x_{0},x_{1})
for
v\neq 1^{k}
and
\q(1^{k})=(y_{0},y_{1})
. But then, since by definition
\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)
, and
\cu^{k}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)\subset\cu^{k}(\ns\Join\ns)
, we deduce that
(x_{0},x_{1})\sim_{k-1}(y_{0},y_{1})
in
\ns\Join\ns
. This, by Lemma
8.24
, tells us that
y_{0}=x_{0}+a
and
y_{1}=x_{1}+b
where
b=a
(under the identification of
\ab_{F_{0}}
and
\ab_{F_{1}}
with
\ab_{k}
provided by Lemma
8.24
), so we have indeed
y_{0}\in F_{0},y_{1}\in F_{1}
and
y_{1}=\phi_{x_{0},x_{1}}(y_{0})
where
\phi_{x_{0},x_{1}}
is the local translation corresponding to
x_{0},x_{1}
.
∎

With this characterization of
\cT^{*}
, we can see that
\ab_{k}
has an action on
\cT^{*}
via the first coordinate, namely for
a\in\ab_{k}
we set

a+\tau=\pi_{k-1,\cT}((a+x_{0},x_{1})).

(33)

The fact that this action is well-defined follows from the fact that
\ab_{k}\times\ab_{k}
has an action on
\cT
given by
(a,b)+(x_{0},x_{1})=(x_{0}+a,x_{1}+b)
, which is clear. We now want to use this
\ab_{k}
-action to view
\cT^{*}
as an extension of
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
. To that end we shall apply the following result concerning
i
-arrows.

Lemma 9.37
.

Let
\ab
be an abelian group, let
\q_{0},\q_{1}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\ab
, and let
i,k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}
lies in
\cu^{n+i}(\cD_{k}(\ab))
if and only if
\q_{0}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k}(\ab))
and
\q_{1}-\q_{0}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-i}(\ab))
.

Proof.

This is obtained as a special case of Lemma
4.19
, recalling Definition
4.30
.
∎

Lemma 9.38
.

Let
\gamma:\cT^{*}\to\cF_{k-1}(\ns),\;\pi_{k-1,\cT}((x_{0},x_{1}))\mapsto\pi_{k-1,\ns}(x_{0})
. Then
\cT^{*}
is a degree-
(k-i)
extension of
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
with bundle map
\gamma
and structure group
\ab_{k}
.

Proof.

That
\gamma
is well-defined follows from the fact that if
(y_{0},y_{1})
is any other couple in
\tau=\pi_{k-1,\cT}((x_{0},x_{1}))
then
x_{0}\sim_{k-1}y_{0}
, by (
32
).

Next, we check that
\gamma
is a morphism with the surjectivity property (i) in Definition
9.13
. Every
n
-cube
\q
on
\cT^{*}
is
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q^{\prime}
for some
n
-cube
\q^{\prime}
on
\cT
, and the latter has the form
\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
for two
n
-cubes on
\ns
. It follows that
\gamma\co\q=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
is an
n
-cube on
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
, whence
\gamma
is a morphism. To see the surjectivity, let
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
be an
n
-cube on
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
, with
\q_{0}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
. Then
\q_{1}^{\prime}=\alpha\co\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
is also an
n
-cube on
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
, satisfying
(\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0})\times\q_{1}^{\prime}\in\cu^{n}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns)\Join_{i}\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
, since
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. Thus
\q_{1}^{\prime}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{1}
for some
\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\ns)
, and
\langle\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0},\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{1}\rangle_{i}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{n+i}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. There is therefore some
\tilde{\q}\in\cu^{n+i}(\ns)
such that
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q^{\prime}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}
. Modifying
\tilde{\q}
as we did in the proof of Lemma
9.35
, we obtain a new cube
\overline{\q}
still satisfying
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\overline{\q}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}
but now with
\overline{\q}=\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}^{\prime\prime}\rangle
for some
n
-cube
\q_{1}^{\prime\prime}
with
\q_{1}^{\prime\prime}(v)\sim_{k-1}\q_{1}(v)
for all
v
. Thus
\q:=\q_{0}\times\q_{1}^{\prime\prime}
is an
n
-cube on
\cT
, and
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q
is an
n
-cube on
\cT^{*}
with
\gamma
-image equal to
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
, as required.

Finally, we check condition (ii) from Definition
9.13
. Let
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q
be an arbitrary cubes in
\cu^{n}(\cT^{*})
, thus
\q=\q_{0}\times\q_{1}
is in
\cu^{n}(\cT)
.

We first show that the left side of condition (ii) is included in the right side. Assume that
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q^{\prime}
is another cube with
\q^{\prime}=\q_{0}^{\prime}\times\q_{1}^{\prime}\in\cu^{n}(\cT)
such that
\gamma\co\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q^{\prime}=\gamma\co\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q
. This means that
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}^{\prime}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
, and since
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{1}=\alpha\co\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q_{0}
and similarly for
\q_{1}^{\prime}
, we have in fact
\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q^{\prime}=\pi_{k-1,\ns}\co\q
. We therefore have
\ab_{k}
-valued functions
\q^{*}_{0}:=\q_{0}-\q_{0}^{\prime}
and
\q^{*}_{1}:=\q_{1}-\q_{1}^{\prime}
, and these are cubes in
\cu^{n}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
since
\ns
is a degree-
k
extension of
\cF_{k-1}(\ns)
with structure group
\ab_{k}
. Moreover, we have
\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i},\langle\q_{0}^{\prime},\q_{1}^{\prime}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{n+i}(\ns)
. and these cubes are also equal modulo
\pi_{k-1,\ns}
, so we can also take their
\ab_{k}
-valued difference, which is
\langle\q_{0}^{*},\q_{1}^{*}\rangle_{i}
. The latter cube must again be in
\cu^{n+i}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
, and by Lemma
9.37
this holds if and only if
\q_{1}^{*}-\q_{0}^{*}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-i}(\ab_{k}))
. Now, our assumption above implies that we can take the
\ab_{k}
-valued difference
\gamma\co\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q^{\prime}\,-\,\gamma\co\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q
, relative to the
\ab_{k}
-action defined in (
33
). This difference is a function
a:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\ab_{k}
, and we claim that
a(v)=\q_{1}^{*}(v)-\q_{0}^{*}(v)
. To see this, observe using (
33
) that for each
v\in\{0,1\}^{n}
the difference
a(v)
is the element of
\ab_{k}
which has to be added to
\q_{0}(v)
in order to have
(\q_{0}(v)+a(v))-\q_{0}^{\prime}(v)=\q_{1}(v)-\q_{1}^{\prime}(v)
. (Indeed, this is how to ensure that
a(v)+\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q(v)=\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q^{\prime}(v)
.) Our claim follows, and then as shown above we have
a=\q_{1}^{*}-\q_{0}^{*}\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-i}(\ab_{k}))
.

The opposite inclusion in condition (ii) is seen similarly. Indeed, if we add
a\in\cu^{n}(\cD_{k-i}(\ab_{k}))
to
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co\q
we obtain the map
\pi_{k-1,\cT}\co((\q_{0}+a)\times\q_{1}))
, which is a cube on
\cT^{*}
if
(\q_{0}+a)\times\q_{1}\in\cu^{n}(\cT)
, which holds if
\langle\q_{0}+a,\q_{1}\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{n+i}(\ns)
. But this does hold, because
\langle\q_{0}+a,\q_{1}\rangle_{i}=\langle\q_{0},\q_{1}\rangle_{i}+\langle a,0\rangle_{i}
and
\langle a,0\rangle_{i}\in\cu^{n+i}(\cD_{k}(\ab_{k}))
by Lemma
9.37
.
∎

We can now establish the main result.

Proposition 9.39
.

Let
\ns
be a
k
-step nilspace and let
\alpha\in\tran_{i}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. If
\cT^{*}=\cT^{*}(\alpha,\ns,i)
is a split extension then
\alpha
has a lift in
\tran_{i}(\ns)
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a morphism
m:\cF_{k-1}(\ns)\to\cT^{*}
such that
\gamma\co m
is the identity map. We define a map
\beta:\ns\to\ns
as follows. For each
x\in\ns
, the element
m(\pi_{k-1}(x))\in\cT^{*}
represents a local translation
\phi
, from the class
F_{0}
of
\sim_{k-1,\ns}
containing
x
, to the class
\alpha(F_{0})
. Let
\beta(x)=\phi(x)
. We claim that
\beta\in\tran_{i}(\ns)
. To prove this, by Lemma
8.32
it suffices to show that for every
\q\in\cu^{k}(\ns)
we have that
\q\times(\beta\co\q)\in\cu^{k}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)
. Now
\q\times(\beta\co\q)
is a lift to
\cT
of the
\cT^{*}
-valued map
m\co\pi_{k-1}\co\q
. The latter map is in
\cu^{k}(\cT^{*})
because
m
is a morphism and
\pi_{k-1}\co\q\in\cu^{k}(\cF_{k-1}(\ns))
. But then, by Lemma
8.11
, the lift
\q\times(\beta\co\q)
is in
\cu^{k}(\cT)\subset\cu^{k}(\ns\Join_{i}\ns)
, so we are done.
∎
