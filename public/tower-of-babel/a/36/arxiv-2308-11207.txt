Quartic Gradient Flow Preprint: RESCEU-16/23
Muzi Hong, Ryusuke Jinno

Abstract
Saddle-point configurations, such as the Euclidean bounce and sphalerons, are known to be difficult to find numerically. In this Letter we study a new method, Quartic Gradient Flow, to search for such configurations. The central idea is to introduce a gradient-flow-like equation in such a way that all the fluctuations around the saddle-point have eigenvalues that are square of the eigenvalues of the original quadratic operator. We illustrate how the method works for the Euclidean bounce and sphalerons.



Introduction.

Saddle-point configurations often appear in physics.
One of the best known examples is the bounce solution that appears in the calculation of the tunneling rate of false vacuum decay
Coleman 1977
;
Callan and Coleman 1977
.
Another example is the sphaleron solution
Manton 1983
;
Klinkhamer and Manton 1984
well-known for its important applications in baryogenesis (
e.g.
, Refs.
Arnold and McLerran 1987
;
Kuzmin et al. 1985
;
Fukugita and Yanagida 1986
).

A saddle point has negative mode(s), whereas a minimum has only positive modes.
This property of the former can cause problems when one tries to find it numerically.
For example, finding multi-field bounces numerically is a non-trivial problem, and a number of approaches have been proposed
Claudson et al. 1983
;
Kusenko 1995
;
Kusenko et al. 1996
;
Dasgupta 1997
;
Moreno et al. 1998
;
John 1999
;
Cline et al. 1999a
;
Cline et al. 1999b
;
Athron et al. 2019
;
Konstandin and Huber 2006
;
Park 2011
;
Wainwright 2012
;
Akula et al. 2016
;
Masoumi et al. 2017
;
Espinosa 2018
;
Espinosa and Konstandin 2019
;
Jinno 2018
;
Piscopo et al. 2019
;
Guada et al. 2019
;
Chigusa et al. 2020
;
Sato 2020
;
Sato 2021
;
Guada et al. 2020
.
In this context, the gradient flow method, which has been used in various fields of physics to solve differential equations, cannot be used at least naively due to these negative modes.
However, a recent proposal
Chigusa et al. 2020
showed that the effect of the negative mode(s) can be eliminated by adding an auxiliary term to the flow equation, and thus gradient flow can be used for the search of the bounce (see also Ref.
Sato 2020
;
Sato 2021
).
The same technique was applied to sphalerons in Ref.
Hamada and Kikuchi 2020
.

In this Letter, we explore yet another approach for the search of saddle points using gradient flow.
We construct a flow equation in such a way that, instead of the original eigenvalues of the fluctuations, their squares appear.
As a result, all modes become positive (up to zero modes) and the flow converges to the desired solution.
This possibility is also mentioned in the footnote 4 of Ref.
Chigusa et al. 2020
.

The structure of the Letter is as follows.
We introduce the idea in Section 2, and show the numerical results for the calculation of the bounce solutions and sphaleron solutions in Section 3.
We discuss future applications and conclude in Section 4.

Central idea.

The central idea of this Letter is to introduce a gradient-flow-like equation in such a way that all the fluctuations around the saddle-point solution have positive eigenvalues alone.
We illustrate with a single real scalar field, but extension to multi-field is straightforward.
We introduce Quartic Gradient Flow as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\hat{\cal M}[\phi]\left[\frac{\delta S}{\delta\phi}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(1)

Here
\hat{\cal M}[\phi]=\delta^{2}S/\delta\phi\delta\phi=-\Delta+\cdots
, and
\phi=\bar{\phi}+\delta\phi
denotes the saddle-point solution and a fluctuation around it.

To see how it works, let us expand the equation around the saddle point.
The action around the saddle point can be expanded to second order as

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int d^{d}x~\left({\cal L}[\bar{\phi}]+\frac{1}{2}\delta\phi\,\hat{\cal M}[\bar{\phi}]\,\delta\phi+{\cal O}(\delta\phi^{3})\right),

(2)

and thus the flow reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\hat{\cal M}^{2}[\bar{\phi}]\delta\phi

\displaystyle\simeq 0.

(3)

We may expand the fluctuation in terms of the eigenfunctions of
\hat{\cal M}[\bar{\phi}]
as

\displaystyle\delta\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}\delta\phi_{n}

(4)

where
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq c_{3}\leq\cdots
is assumed without losing generality, and the
n
-th eigenfunction and the
n
-th eigenvalue are denoted as
\delta\phi_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}
, respectively.
Often there exist negative mode(s)
\lambda_{1}<0
.
The difficulty of naive gradient flow approaches lies in the negativity of this eigenvalue.
However, because of the squared operator
\hat{\cal M}^{2}[\bar{\phi}]
, Quartic Gradient Flow behaves as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{c}+\Lambda^{2}\vec{c}

\displaystyle\simeq 0,\qquad\Lambda^{2}={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}^{2},\lambda_{2}^{2},\lambda_{3}^{2},\cdots),

(5)

for the coefficients
\vec{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\cdots)
.
Since the square of a negative value is positive, the flow is expected to converge to the saddle-point solution up to zero-mode directions

S^{\prime}=\int d^{d}x\left(\frac{\delta S}{\delta\phi}\right)^{2}.

(6)

This action has been proposed in Ref.
Moreno et al. 1998
;
John 1999

Example 1: single-field Euclidean bounce.

We first consider the Euclidean bounce of a single real scalar field in
d
-dimensions.
In this case the functional
S
is the Euclidean action
S_{E}
.
The bounce configuration is known to be
O(d)
symmetric, and thus we may write the action as

\displaystyle S_{E}

\displaystyle=\int d^{d}x~\left[\frac{1}{2}(\partial\phi)^{2}+V(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle=S_{d-1}\int r^{d-1}dr~\left[\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{r}\phi)^{2}+V(\phi)\right].

(7)

Here the metric is
{\rm diag}(+,+,\cdots)
in the first line, and
S_{d-1}
is the surface area of a
(d-1)

The quadratic operator and the Euler-Lagrange equation are

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal M}}[\phi]

\displaystyle=-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}+V^{\prime\prime}(\phi),

(8)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S_{E}}{\delta\phi}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{r}^{2}\phi-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}\phi+V^{\prime}(\phi)=0,

(9)

respectively, and thus Quartic Gradient Flow takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\left[\partial_{r}^{2}+\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}-V^{\prime\prime}\right]\left[\partial_{r}^{2}\phi+\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}\phi-V^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(10)

In this case, Quartic Gradient Flow becomes

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\left[\Delta-V^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\right]\left[\Delta\phi-V^{\prime}(\phi)\right]

\displaystyle=0.

Example 3: sphalerons.

We also consider SU(2) sphalerons.
Sphalerons are stationary unstable solution of the gauge and Higgs fields.
The starting action is

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g^{2}}\int d^{d}x~\left[-\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+|D_{\mu}\Phi|^{2}+V(\Phi)\right],

(23)

and after imposing spherical symmetry and fixing the gauge, the energy becomes (see Appendix
II
for derivation)

\displaystyle E=\frac{4\pi}{g^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr

\displaystyle\left[|\partial_{r}\chi|^{2}+r^{2}|\partial_{r}\phi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2r^{2}}(|\chi|^{2}-1)^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2}(|\chi|^{2}+1)|\phi|^{2}-\textrm{Re}(\chi^{*}\phi^{2})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\lambda}{g^{2}}r^{2}\left(|\phi|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}v^{2}\right)^{2}\right],

(24)

where
\chi
and
\phi
are complex fields.

As far as ordinary sphalerons are considered, only the real parts of
\chi
and
\phi
take nonzero values
Klinkhamer and Manton 1984
;
Yaffe 1989
.
Thus, for the purpose of finding the sphaleron solution, we may restrict them to be real and in that case the solution becomes a minimum of the energy (
24
), not a saddle point.
However, since our purpose of studying sphalerons here is to illustrate how Quartic Gradient Flow works in the presence of negative modes, we include the imaginary parts as well in the following analysis.
In this case the sphaleron becomes a saddle point of Eq. (
24
).
We also note that the full expression (
24
) is necessary in finding the most relevant saddle-point solutions for larger
\lambda/g^{2}
known as bisphalerons
Yaffe 1989
, though we restrict ourselves to ordinary sphalerons here.

We further redefine
\eta\equiv r\phi
so that

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{g^{2}}{8\pi}E

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\left[(\partial_{r}\chi_{r})^{2}+(\partial_{r}\chi_{i})^{2}+(\partial_{r}\eta_{r})^{2}+(\partial_{r}\eta_{i})^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+2U(\chi,\eta;r)\left.\right]+\textrm{const.},

(25)

with
\chi=\chi_{r}+i\chi_{i}
,
\eta=\eta_{r}+i\eta_{i}
, where the constant term comes from integration by parts, and

\displaystyle U(\chi,\eta;r)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4r^{2}}(|\chi|^{2}-1)^{2}+\frac{1}{4r^{2}}(|\chi|^{2}+1)|\eta|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2r^{2}}\textrm{Re}(\chi^{*}\eta^{2})+\frac{\lambda}{2g^{2}r^{2}}\left(|\eta|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}g^{2}v^{2}r^{2}\right)^{2}.

(26)

Then we may use Quartic Gradient Flow for
d=1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\vec{\phi}+\hat{\cal M}[\phi]\left[\frac{\delta E^{\prime}}{\delta\vec{\phi}}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(27)

with
\vec{\phi}=(\chi_{r},\chi_{i},\eta_{r},\eta_{i})^{T}
and

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal M}}[\phi]

\displaystyle=-\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\partial_{r}^{2}-\partial_{\chi_{r}}U&-\partial_{\chi_{r}}\partial_{\chi_{i}}U&-\partial_{\chi_{r}}\partial_{\eta_{r}}U&-\partial_{\chi_{r}}\partial_{\eta_{i}}U\\
-\partial_{\chi_{i}}\partial_{\chi_{r}}U&\partial_{r}^{2}-\partial_{\chi_{i}}U&-\partial_{\chi_{i}}\partial_{\eta_{r}}U&-\partial_{\chi_{i}}\partial_{\eta_{i}}U\\
-\partial_{\eta_{r}}\partial_{\chi_{r}}U&-\partial_{\eta_{r}}\partial_{\chi_{i}}U&\partial_{r}^{2}-\partial_{\eta_{r}}U&-\partial_{\eta_{r}}\partial_{\eta_{i}}U\\
-\partial_{\eta_{i}}\partial_{\chi_{r}}U&-\partial_{\eta_{i}}\partial_{\chi_{i}}U&-\partial_{\eta_{i}}\partial_{\eta_{r}}U&\partial_{r}^{2}-\partial_{\eta_{i}}U\end{array}\right),\qquad\frac{\delta E^{\prime}}{\delta\vec{\phi}}=-\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\partial_{r}^{2}\chi_{r}-\partial_{\chi_{r}}U\\
\partial_{r}^{2}\chi_{i}-\partial_{\chi_{i}}U\\
\partial_{r}^{2}\eta_{r}-\partial_{\eta_{r}}U\\
\partial_{r}^{2}\eta_{i}-\partial_{\eta_{i}}U\end{array}\right).

Discussion and Conclusions.

In this Letter we study a novel method to calculate saddle-point configurations.
The central idea is to introduce a gradient-flow-like equation in such a way that all the fluctuations around the saddle point have positive eigenvalues in the flow equation, Eq. (
1
).
This is made possible by the non-negativity of the eigenvalues of the operator
\hat{\cal M}^{2}[\bar{\phi}]
around the saddle point, with
\hat{\cal M}[\bar{\phi}]
being the quadratic operator in the action.
The algorithm of Quartic Gradient Flow (
1
) is simple but powerful: it is applicable to general saddle-point searches without setting up different algorithms for different problems nor finding tailor-made ansatz.
We illustrate it with the bounce and sphaleron configurations.

However, we also find several areas for improvement.
One of the major ones is the boundary condition.
As detailed in the Appendix
I
, for
O(d)
bounce solutions we fix the value at the origin by imposing a boundary condition consistent with the original bounce equation (see the text below Eqs. (
S1
)–(
S4
)).
Without it the configuration would pass through the bounce and reach the trivial solution.
Part of the reason for this behavior might be the forward Euler method we use in this Letter.
It may cause divergence near the origin in the spherical coordinate when the potential contains terms involving divergent factors such as
\sim 1/r
.
We confirmed that this behavior does not occur if one uses backward Euler method instead.
Also, as shown in Fig.
3
for the sphaleron case, the forward Euler method cannot fix the value at the origin.
Although one can always use boundary conditions proposed in Refs.
Manton 1983
;
Klinkhamer and Manton 1984
;
Yaffe 1989
to speculate the field value at the origin, we expect advanced numerical method can calculate the full configuration accurately without additional assumptions.

We present possible future directions from the viewpoint of both numerical methods and physical applications.
As mentioned above, explicit numerical methods such as the forward Euler method may bring divergence if the problem involves spherical coordinates.
In contrast, implicit methods such as the backward Euler and the Crank-Nicolson methods generally do not lead to such divergence.
Implementing the latter methods may allow for convergence of the configuration to the desired solution without some of the boundary conditions imposed in this study.
As for physical applications, one may think of various saddle point searches.
For example, one could try the method for thin-wall bounce, which we did not consider in this Letter.
Sphalerons also have many possible applications.
Sphaleron configurations with Chern-Simons number different from
1/2
, called bisphalerons, are known to appear when
m_{H}^{2}/m_{W}^{2}
is larger than the Standard Model
value
Yaffe 1989
, and their properties might be investigated with Quartic Gradient Flow.
It would also be interesting to study the properties of sphalerons with a finite Weinberg angle, see
e.g.
, Refs.
James 1992
;
Klinkhamer and Laterveer 1992
;
Kunz et al. 1992
.
Moreover, other types of sphalerons exist in the Standard Model such as
S^{*}
and
\hat{S}

Klinkhamer 1993
;
Klinkhamer and Rupp 2003
.
Exploring these configurations using Quartic Gradient Flow would be exciting possibilities, which we leave for future work.

The first example is the single-field bounce.

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{i+1}-\phi_{i-1}}{2\Delta r}+{\cal O}(\Delta r^{2}),

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi_{i+2}-2\phi_{i+1}+2\phi_{i-1}-\phi_{i-2}}{2\Delta r^{3}}+{\cal O}(\Delta r^{2}),

We follow the discussion in Ref.

(S9)

The SU(2) gauge field is
A_{\mu}\equiv A^{a}_{\mu}(\tau^{a}/2i)

F_{\mu\nu}\equiv[D_{\mu},D_{\nu}]

\Phi

D_{\mu}\equiv(\partial_{\mu}+A_{\mu})

We follow Yaffe’s notation and use

Here, we neglect U(1)
Y

Klinkhamer and Manton 1984

When an O(3) rotation of spatial directions can be canceled by a combination of SU(2) gauge and SU(2) global transformations, the field configurations are called “spherically symmetric.”
An ansatz for the most general spherically symmetric configuration is given by Refs.
Witten 1977
;
Ratra and Yaffe 1988

\displaystyle A_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=[a_{0}(r,t)\vec{\tau}\cdot\hat{\vec{x}}]/2i,

(S10)

\displaystyle A_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\{[\alpha(r,t)-1]e_{j}^{1}/r+\beta(r,t)e_{j}^{2}/r+a_{1}(r,t)e_{j}^{3}\}/2i,

(S11)

\displaystyle=[\mu(r,t)+i\nu(r,t)\vec{\tau}\cdot\hat{\vec{x}}]\xi,

(S12)
