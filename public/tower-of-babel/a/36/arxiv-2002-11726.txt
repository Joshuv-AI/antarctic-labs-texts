Quenched topological boundary modes can persist in a trivial system
Ching Hua Lee, Justin C.W. Song

Abstract
Topological boundary modes can occur at the spatial interface between a topological and gapped trivial phase and exhibit a wavefunction that exponentially decays in the gap. Here we argue that this intuition fails for a temporal boundary between a prequench topological phase that possess topological boundary eigenstates and a postquench gapped trivial phase that does not possess any eigenstates in its gap. In particular, we find that characteristics of states (e.g., probability density) prepared in a topologically non-trivial system can persist long after it is quenched into a gapped trivial phase with spatial profiles that appear frozen over long times postquench. After this near-stationary window, topological boundary mode profiles decay albeit, slowly in a power-law fashion. This behavior highlights the unusual features of nonequilibrium protocols enabling quenches to extend and control localized states of both topological and non-topological origins.

Probability density and quench protocol –

Frozen TBMs and Wick-rotated diffusion –
However, when
m\alpha/(\hbar v)\gg 1
, the Larmor precession of the postquench state for various
p_{x}
components oscillate in near lock-step, see below. Indeed, in this limit, the oscillation frequency
\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+\hbar^{2}v^{2}p_{x}^{2}}\approx\Delta+(\hbar vp_{x})^{2}/(2\Delta)
is dominated by a large
\Delta=(m^{2}+\hbar^{2}v^{2}p_{y}^{2})^{1/2}\approx m

To understand this long-time behavior more intuitively, we note that
G_{t}(x)
can be interpreted as a 1D heat kernel corresponding to a Wick-rotated “diffusion” process (i.e. evolution by Schrödinger’s equation involving the

While scattering processes in ordinary diffusion processes lead to a “Gaussian-blurred” distribution characterized by a (real) Gaussian decaying kernel, the imaginary Gaussian kernel

A large

8

The persistence of TBM characteristics can form the basis for a strategy to extend TBM features long after the topological system that supports it is gone: namely, by quenching to a large gap wherein postquench eigenstates are energetically inaccessible. Since the persistent TBM PD arises from the fast postquench Larmor precession enforced by large
m
, we expect that it is robust against inelastic scattering with energies far smaller than the gap scale as well as slowly varying disorder with typical lengths longer than the TBM width. The frozen and melting quenched PD regimes can be readily prepared and measured in ultra-cold atom optical lattices
Jotzu2014
;
Stuhl2015
;
flaschner2016experimental
;
alba2011seeing
;
hauke2014tomography
with its pseudospin components of the wavepacket independently extracted
alba2011seeing
;
hauke2014tomography
, and its real-space dynamics tracked
flaschner2016experimental
.

While we expect that the frozen and melting quench dynamics that we discuss here to be most easily realized in ultra-cold atomic optical lattices, or in photonic
Rechstman2013
or circuit platforms
lu2019probing
;
helbig2019observation
;
hofmann2019reciprocal
;
bao2019topoelectrical
wherein interactions are weak, it is interesting to consider how the quench dynamics will behave in electronic systems wherein interactions can be present. In an electronic platform, interactions can lead to dephasing that can disrupt the lock-step precession of the postquench states that underlie the Frozen and melting regime
Wilson2016
. As a result, we expect the unusual frozen and melting regimes to only manifest at times shorter than the dephasing time of the electronic system.

Perhaps most striking, is the contrast in behavior of TBMs across a sharp
temporal
boundary (as realized in the quench at
t=0
) against those found at spatial boundaries. In the latter, boundary states (topological or otherwise) that exist in an energy gap typically exponentially decay over space into a gapped insulating bulk. Whereas in the former, TBM PD persists, freezing for a long window then decaying in a power-law fashion even when there are no eigenstates in trivial gap. This underscores the stark difference between sharp spatial and temporal interfaces and displays the dichotomy between equilibrium and out-of-equilibrium responses.

III
Methods

IV
Postquench dynamical evolution

In this section, we review how the topological boundary mode (TBM) in Eq. (
3
) of the main text and its characteristics (namely probability density (PD) and pseudospom expectation) evolve postquench. For generality, we do not make any assumption on the form of the initial state (unless otherwise stated), other than it can be spatially inhomogeneous in the x-direction. We shall also assume the most general post-quench Hamiltonian that is translation-invariant in both directions. Hence our following results are applicable for a broader class of quench settings than those discussed in the main text.

IV.1
Wavefunction evolution

The postquench (
t>0
) system is described by the Hamiltonian
\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}(\mathbb{p})=\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p})\cdot\bm{\sigma}
with bulk eigenstates
|\varepsilon_{\mathbb{p}}^{\pm}\rangle
with eigenenergies
\pm\varepsilon_{p}=\pm|{\bf d}({\bf p})|=\pm\sqrt{\hbar^{2}v^{2}|\mathbb{p}|^{2}+m^{2}}
.
The wavefunction of the TBM initial state [see Eq. (
3
) of the main text] at
t=0
can be written as
\langle{\bf r}|\psi_{p_{y}}\rangle=\sum_{p_{x}}\mathcal{N}\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})e^{ip_{x}x}e^{ip_{y}y}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
, where
\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})
is the Fourier transform of
\Phi(x)
. The time-evolved wavefunction postquench can be directly evaluated as
\psi_{p_{y}}({\bf r},t)=\langle{\bf r}|e^{-i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}|\psi_{p_{y}}\rangle
.

Writing
{\rm exp}(-i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t)=\sum_{{\bf p},\pm}e^{\mp i\varepsilon_{p}t}|\varepsilon_{\mathbb{p}}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\varepsilon_{\mathbb{p}}^{\pm}|
, and noting that
|\varepsilon_{\mathbb{p}}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\varepsilon_{\mathbb{p}}^{\pm}|=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}\pm\hat{{\bf d}}(\mathbb{p})\cdot\bm{\sigma}\big)|\mathbb{p}\rangle\langle\mathbb{p}|
, where
\langle{\bf r}|{\bf p}\rangle=e^{i{\bf p}\cdot{\bf r}}
is the wavefunction of the momentum eigenstate, and
\hat{{\bf d}}({\bf p})={\bf d}({\bf p})/|{\bf d}({\bf p})|
, we obtain

\psi_{p_{y}}({\bf r},t)=\sum_{p_{x},\mathbb{r}^{\prime}}e^{i\mathbb{p}\cdot(\mathbb{r}-\mathbb{r}^{\prime})}(\cos\varepsilon_{p}t-i\hat{{\bf d}}(\mathbb{p})\cdot\bm{\sigma}\sin\varepsilon_{p}t)\langle{\bf r}^{\prime}|\psi_{p_{y}}\rangle

(13)

In obtaining Eq. (
13
) we summed across the
\pm
bands as well as inserted the resolution of the identity
\sum_{{\bf r}^{\prime}}|{\bf r}^{\prime}\rangle\langle{\bf r}^{\prime}|
. Summing across
{\bf r}^{\prime},p_{x}^{\prime}
by writing out
\langle{\bf r}|\psi_{p_{y}}\rangle=\sum_{p_{x}}\mathcal{N}\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})e^{ip_{x}x}e^{ip_{y}y}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
we obtain the compact form

\psi_{p_{y}}({\bf r},t)=e^{ip_{y}y}\Big[C(x,t)-i{\bf S}(x,t)\cdot\bm{\sigma}\Big]|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle

(14)

where
C
and
{\bf S}
are the same as the main text. We reproduce these here for the convenience of the reader

\displaystyle C(x,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\sum_{p_{x}}\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})e^{ip_{x}x}\cos\varepsilon_{p}t

(15)

\displaystyle{\bf S}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\sum_{p_{x}}\hat{{\bf d}}(\mathbb{p})\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})e^{ip_{x}x}\sin\varepsilon_{p}t

(16)

In Eq. (
14
), we see that
\psi(t,\mathbb{r})
comprises two pseudospinor contributions, one proportional to
\cos\varepsilon_{p}t
in the direction of the original spinor
|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
, and another proportional to
i\sin\varepsilon_{p}t
for which
|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
has undergone a
\hat{{\bf d}}(\mathbb{p})\cdot\bm{\sigma}
spinor rotation.

IV.2
Probability density dynamics

The evolution of the probability density postquench can be obtained from direct evaluation of Eq. (
4
) of the main text. This amounts to finding the square amplitude of the wavefunction above,
|\psi_{p_{y}}({\bf r},t)|^{2}
. This can be evaluated as

\langle\rho\rangle_{t,x}=\langle\Psi^{(0)}|\Big[C^{*}+i{\bf S}^{*}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\Big]\Big[C-i{\bf S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\Big]|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle,

(17)

where we have suppressed the explicit
x,t
dependence of
C,{\bf S}
for brevity. This expression can be readily simplified by recalling the vector identity:

({\bf a}\cdot\bm{\sigma})({\bf b}\cdot\bm{\sigma})={\bf a}\cdot{\bf b}\mathbb{I}+i({\bf a}\times{\bf b})\cdot\bm{\sigma}.

(18)

This yields the compact expression for PD as

\langle\rho\rangle_{x,t}=|C|^{2}+|{\bf S}|^{2}+i({\bf S}^{*}C-C^{*}{\bf S})\cdot\hat{{\bf s}}+i({\bf S}^{*}\times{\bf S})\cdot\hat{{\bf s}}

(19)

where
\hat{{\bf s}}=\langle\Psi^{(0)}|\bm{\sigma}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
. Writing
{\bf S}^{*}C-C^{*}{\bf S}=2i{\rm Im}\,({\bf S}^{*}C)
we obtain Eq. (
5
) in the main text.

We note, parenthetically, when
{\bf d}({\bf p})
describes a Dirac model, further simplifications can be made to Eq. (
19
). For example, for Dirac models
\varepsilon_{p}
is even in
p_{x}
. This forces
C
to be real, and
\text{Re}[\mathbb{S}]
(
\text{Im}[\mathbb{S}]
) to arise solely from the even(odd) components of
\hat{{\bf d}}(p_{x},p_{y})
. Hence
\text{Im}[\mathbb{S}]\parallel\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{1}
and
\text{Re}[\mathbb{S}]\perp\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{1}
, and the time-evolved probability density reads as

\langle\rho\rangle_{x,t}=C^{2}+(\text{Im}[\mathbb{S}]\cdot\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{1}-\text{Re}[\mathbb{S}]\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{3})^{2}+(\mathbb{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{2})^{2},

(20)

with the last term nonzero only for
p_{y}\neq 0
.

IV.3
Pseudospin dynamics

The evolution of the pseudospin expectation (density) postquench can be evaluated in the same fashion as above. Using the
C,{\bf S}
notation above, the pseudospin expectation density in Eq. (
11
) of the main text can be re-written compactly as

\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}=\langle\Psi^{(0)}|\Big[C^{*}+i{\bf S}^{*}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\Big]\bm{\sigma}\Big[C-i{\bf S}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\Big]|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle,

(21)

This expression can be readily simplified by repeated application of the identiy
\sigma_{a}\sigma_{b}=\delta_{ab}\mathbb{I}+i\epsilon_{abc}\sigma_{c}
and recalling the identity
\epsilon_{abc}\epsilon_{ade}=\delta_{bd}\delta_{ce}-\delta_{be}\delta_{cd}
. Here
\epsilon_{abc}
is the Levita-Cevita symbol, and
a,b,c,d,e
indices run over
x,y,z
. Focussing on the
a
-th component of the pseudospin expectation density, we obtain

\displaystyle[\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}]_{a}

\displaystyle=|C|^{2}\hat{{\bf s}}_{a}-2{\rm Im}\,({\bf S}^{*}_{a}C)+\epsilon_{abc}\big(C^{*}{\bf S}_{b}\hat{{\bf s}}_{c}+C{\bf S}^{*}_{b}\hat{{\bf s}}_{c}\big)

\displaystyle+{\bf S}^{*}_{a}{\bf S}_{b}\hat{{\bf s}}_{b}+{\bf S}^{*}_{b}\hat{{\bf s}}_{b}{\bf S}_{a}-{\bf S}_{b}^{*}{\bf S}_{b}\hat{{\bf s}}_{a}+i\epsilon_{abc}{\bf S}_{b}{\bf S}^{*}_{c}

(22)

Re-writing in terms of real and imaginary parts of
{\bf S}
and
C
, we obtain Eq. (
12
) in the main text. In obtaining Eq. (
12
) we have noted the identity
({\bf u}+i{\bf v})\times({\bf u}-i{\bf v})=-2i{\bf u}\times{\bf v}
, where
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}
are vectors with real components.

Similar to that discussed above, simplifications to Eq. (
22
) arise when
\varepsilon_{p}
is
even in
p_{x}
. This forces
C
to be real. In that case,
\text{Re}[\mathbb{S}]
(
\text{Im}[\mathbb{S}]
) arises from the integral of the even(odd) components of
\hat{{\bf d}}(p_{x},p_{y})
.
If
\hat{{\bf d}}(\mathbb{p})
is furthermore purely even/odd, Eq. (
22
) simplifies to

\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}^{\text{even}}=\hat{{\bf s}}(|C|^{2}-|\mathbb{S}|^{2})+2\text{Re}[\mathbb{S}^{*}C]\times\hat{{\bf s}}+2\text{Re}[(\mathbb{S}^{*}\cdot\hat{{\bf s}})\mathbb{S}]

(23)

and

\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}^{\text{odd}}=\hat{{\bf s}}(|C|^{2}-|\mathbb{S}|^{2})-2\text{Im}[\mathbb{S}^{*}C]+2\text{Re}[(\mathbb{S}^{*}\cdot\hat{{\bf s}})\mathbb{S}].

(24)

In either case, the last term of Eq. (
22
) always disappears, since it requires
\mathbb{S}
to have both real and imaginary parts.

Recalling that the TBM modes discussed in the main text have
\hat{{\bf s}}=\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{2}
, one also obtains the squared expected pseudospin magnitude (not to be confused with
\langle\rho\rangle_{t,\mathbb{r}}
)

|\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}|^{2}=(|C|^{2}+|\mathbb{S}|^{2}-2(\mathbb{S}\cdot e_{1})(\mathbb{S}\cdot e_{3}))^{2}

(25)

From the rotational invariance of Eq. (
22
), one can also easily deduce the expressions valid for general initial states with
\hat{{\bf s}}\neq\hat{\mathbb{e}}_{2}
.

V
Frozen and melting regimes

In this section, we provide a detailed description of how both frozen and melting regimes arise in the limit of large
m\alpha/\hbar v\gg 1
. Before we proceed, we note
C
and
{\bf S}
can be expressed as
C(x,t)=[(F_{0}(x,t)+F_{0}(x,-t)]/2
and
S_{x,y,z}=[F_{x,y,z}(x,t)-F_{x,y,z}(x,-t)]/2i
with

F_{j}(x,t)=\mathcal{N}\int\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})e^{ip_{x}x}e^{i\varepsilon_{p}t/\hbar}f_{j}(p_{x})\frac{dp_{x}}{2\pi},

(26)

where
j=0,x,y,z
, and the
j
-dependent weights as
f_{0}=1
and
(f_{x},f_{y},f_{z})={\bf d}({\bf p})/|{\bf d}({\bf p})|
. Since the dynamics of
C(x,t)
and
{\bf S}(x,t)
are controlled by
F_{j}(x,t)
, we will analyze the temporal dynamics of
F_{j}(x,t)
in the limit of
m\alpha/\hbar v\gg 1
.

In so doing, we first note that in the large postquench mass limit we can write

\varepsilon_{p}=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+\hbar^{2}v^{2}p_{x}^{2}}\approx\Delta+(\hbar vp_{x})^{2}/(2\Delta)

(27)

where
\Delta=(m^{2}+\hbar^{2}v^{2}p_{y}^{2})^{1/2}
. We note parenthetically, when the postquench mass is large and by focussing on small
p_{y}<\alpha^{-1}\ll m/\hbar v
, we have
\Delta\approx m
.

The tight localization of the TBM state arises from large
p_{x}\sim\alpha^{-1}
components. When
m\alpha/(\hbar v)\gg 1
, the differences in their precession frequency are very much suppressed and can be well approximated by Eq. (
27
). Applying Eq. (
27
) into the precession frequency of Eq. (
26
) we obtain

F_{j}(x,t)\approx\frac{\mathcal{N}e^{i\Delta t/\hbar}}{2\pi}\int\Phi(x^{\prime})e^{ip_{x}(x-x^{\prime})}e^{i\frac{\hbar(vp_{x})^{2}t}{2\Delta}}f_{j}dp_{x}dx^{\prime},

(28)

where
(f_{x},f_{y},f_{z})\approx(\hbar vp_{x}/\Delta,\hbar vp_{y}/\Delta,m/\Delta)
, and we have re-written
\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})=\int dx^{\prime}e^{-ip_{x}x^{\prime}}\Phi(x^{\prime})
.
Recalling the identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iax}dx=e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}},\quad a>0,

(29)

where we have taken the principal value, and integrating out
p_{x}
by completing the square in the argument of the exponential function, we obtain

F_{0,z}(x,t)\approx\mathcal{N}\sqrt{\frac{i\Delta}{hv^{2}t}}\Psi_{t}(x)e^{i\Delta t/\hbar}

(30)

where

\Psi_{t}(x)=\int dx^{\prime}\Phi(x+x^{\prime})G_{t}(x^{\prime}),\quad G_{t}(x)=e^{-i\Delta x^{2}/(2t\hbar v^{2})},

(31)

where we have changed dummy variables
x-x^{\prime}\to x^{\prime}
. We note that in obtaining the PD profile in both frozen and melting regimes,
F_{x,y}
contributions are small (suppressed in the large
m\alpha/\hbar v\gg 1
limit) and are negligible in
\langle\rho\rangle_{x,t}
. This is because while the integrand in
F_{z}
is proportional to
m/\Delta\rightarrow 1
, the integrand in
F_{x},F_{y}
are proportional to
p_{x},p_{y}
respectively, which are much smaller than the gap size
m
.

V.1
Frozen Regime

The imaginary Gaussian kernel in Eq. (
31
) plays a crucial role in the dynamical evolution of the probability density. At short times,
G_{t}(x)
exhibit spatial oscillations far faster than the spatial variations of
\Phi(x)
. Indeed,
\Phi(x)
varies significantly for
x
varying on order of the TBM width,
\alpha
. In contrast,
G_{t}(x)
changes rapidly on length scales of order
\sqrt{2t\hbar v^{2}/\Delta}
, and for short times, this length scale can be far shorter than
\alpha
. As a result, for short time windows (controllable by postquench
m
)

0<t\ll\frac{\Delta\alpha^{2}}{2\hbar v^{2}}\approx\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar v}\tau,\quad\tau=\frac{\alpha}{2v},

(32)

we have
\Delta\alpha^{2}/(2t\hbar v^{2})\gg 1
.

In this short time window Eq. (
32
),
G_{t}\rightarrow e^{-i\pi/4}v\sqrt{2\pi\hbar t}\delta(\Delta x)
, and
\Phi(x+x^{\prime})
is effectively replaced by a constant
\Phi(x)
times a factor containing
t^{1/2}
, and we can approximate

\Psi_{t}(x)\approx\Phi(x)\int G_{t}(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=e^{-i\pi/4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar v^{2}t}{\Delta}}

(33)

yielding
F_{0,z}(x,t)=\mathcal{N}e^{i\Delta t/\hbar}
. As a result, at these short times
F_{0,z}(x,t)\approx\mathcal{N}\Phi(x)e^{i\Delta t/\hbar}
, so that
C(x,t)\approx\mathcal{N}\Phi(x){\rm cos}(\Delta t/\hbar)
and
S_{z}\approx\mathcal{N}\Phi(x){\rm sin}(\Delta t/\hbar)
, where we have noted that
F_{x,y}
are suppressed for large
m\alpha/\hbar v
.
Substituting into Eq. (
5
) in the main text we obtain the Frozen profile

\langle\rho\rangle_{t,x}\approx\mathcal{N}^{2}|\Phi(x)|^{2},\quad 0<t<\frac{\tau m\alpha}{\hbar v}.

(34)

This frozen (i.e. near-stationary in time) profile closely conforms to that found from a direct numerical evaluation of the probability density found in Fig.2b of the main text.

V.2
Melting regime

In the opposite regime to Eq. (
32
) (maintaining
m\alpha/\hbar v\gg 1
),
t\gg\frac{\Delta\alpha^{2}}{2\hbar v^{2}}=\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar v}\tau
, the imaginary Gaussian kernel in Eq. (
31
) varies slowly in
x
. This will tend to spread
F_{0,z}(x,t)
and hence the density of states out in space. The amplitude of
F_{0,z}(x,t)
decays as
t^{-1/2}
as in Eq. (
30
). We similarly obtain
C(x,t)\approx[\Delta/(hv^{2}t)]^{1/2}\mathcal{N}{\rm cos}(\Delta t/\hbar+\pi/4)\Psi_{t}(x)
and
S_{z}\approx[\Delta/(hv^{2}t)]^{1/2}\mathcal{N}{\rm sin}(\Delta t/\hbar+\pi/4)\Psi_{t}(x)
. Note that
S_{x},S_{y}\ll S_{z}
because the values of
p_{x},p_{y}
that contribute to the integral giving
F_{x},F_{y}
are much smaller than
\Delta
in the large
m\alpha/\hbar v\gg 1
limit.
Plugging this into Eq. (
5
) in the main text we obtain, the power-law melting profile found in Eq. (
10
) of the main text.

Interestingly, in this regime the imaginary Gaussian kernel in Eq. (
31
) varies slowly in
x
far more slowly than
\Phi(x+x^{\prime})
varies in position. As a result, for
x\lesssim\alpha
close to the domain wall
\Psi_{t}\approx\int\Phi(x+x^{\prime})=\mathcal{N}^{-1}
. In this limit, we find that the probability density at different
x\lesssim\alpha
collapse onto each other and decay as

\langle\rho\rangle_{t,x}\to\frac{\Delta}{hv^{2}t},\quad t\gg\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar v}\tau,

(35)

displaying a universal
x
-independent power-law decay. Indeed, this collapse of PD at different
x
positions in the long time limit is seen in a direct numerical evaluation in Fig. 2b of the main text. Indeed, we expect that at sufficiently long times, when
\Psi_{t}(x)
has spread out, larger
x>\alpha
within the width of
\Psi_{t}(x)
will also similarly exhibit the universal
x
-independent power-law decay.

V.3
Illustration: exactly solvable example

While we have obtained approximate expressions for
\Psi_{t}(x)
[and hence,
F_{j}(x,t)
] above, it is instructive to illustrate the “frozen” and “melting” regime behavior through an exactly solvable model. To do so we consider the case where the initial TBM state
\Phi(x)
takes the form of a Gaussian:
\Phi(x)=e^{-x^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}
, where
\alpha
is the standard deviation. We can then obtain by direct integrating Eq. (
31
)

\Psi_{t}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{hv^{2}\alpha^{2}t}{\hbar v^{2}t+i\Delta\alpha^{2}}}\Big[{\rm exp}(-x^{2}/2\alpha^{2})\Big]^{1+\frac{\hbar v^{2}t}{\hbar v^{2}t+i\Delta\alpha^{2}}}

(36)

Evidently, when
t\ll\frac{\alpha^{2}\Delta}{\hbar v^{2}}
then we have

\Psi_{t}(x)\to\sqrt{\frac{hv^{2}t}{i\Delta}}\Big[{\rm exp}(-x^{2}/2\alpha^{2})\Big],\quad t\ll\frac{\alpha^{2}\Delta}{\hbar v^{2}}

(37)

Substituting in Eq. (
30
), we obtain
F_{j}(x,t)\approx g_{j}e^{i\Delta t}\mathcal{N}{\rm exp}(-x^{2}/2\alpha^{2})\propto\Phi(x)e^{i\Delta t}
that leads to the frozen (near-stationary in time) PD profile as discussed above.

In the opposite limit
t\gg\frac{\alpha^{2}\Delta}{\hbar v^{2}}
,

\Psi_{t}(x)\to\sqrt{2\pi\alpha^{2}}\Big[{\rm exp}(-x^{2}/\alpha^{2})\Big],\quad t\gg\frac{\alpha^{2}\Delta}{\hbar v^{2}}

(38)

Recalling that
\sqrt{2\pi\alpha^{2}}=\mathcal{N}^{-1}
is the inverse normalization constant for a Gaussian profile
\Phi(x)=e^{-x^{2}/2\alpha^{2}}
, and substituting into Eq. (
30
), we similarly obtain the power-law decay in the PD described in Eq. (
35
) (in the limit
x\ll\alpha
). Note that the Gaussian profile above is special in that it does not spread due to its covariance under convolution by a Gaussian Kernel. For more general profiles, we will expect spread effects like what shown in Fig. 1 of the main text, as well as that shown in
Figs.
S-1
and
S-2
of the Supplementary Information.

VI
Precession and dynamical equations of motion

Although we have already provided the full solution to the PD and pseudospin expectation, it is still instructive to understand how the dynamical equations governing these quantities are related to the well-known spin precession equation. Due to the lack of translation invariance in the initial state, the evolution is not diagonal in momentum space, and the equations of motion will involve projectors.
With
|\psi_{p_{y}}(t,\mathbb{r})\rangle=|\mathbb{r}\rangle\langle\mathbb{r}|e^{-i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle
, the rate of change of the PD can be directly evaluated from Eq. (
4
) of the main text and takes the form

\displaystyle\frac{d\langle\rho\rangle_{t,\mathbb{r}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\langle\Psi^{(0)}|e^{i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}\left[\hat{{\bf H}}_{f},\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}\right]e^{-i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\text{Im}\langle(\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}\,\tilde{\mathbb{d}})\cdot\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t},

where
\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}=|\mathbb{r}\rangle\langle\mathbb{r}|
is projection operator onto the real-space state
|\mathbb{r}\rangle
at position
\mathbb{r}
,
\tilde{\mathbb{d}}=\sum_{\mathbb{p}}\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p})|\mathbb{p}\rangle\langle\mathbb{p}|
is the projection operator onto the spinor eigenstate corresponding to
\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p})
and
\langle...\rangle_{t}
denotes an expectation evaluated with the evolved initial state at time
t
(after the quench).

The projection
\hat{{\bf P}}
enters because the expectation was evaluated at the particular position
\mathbb{r}
, not across the entire wavefunction
\psi_{p_{y}}(t,\mathbb{r})
. Importantly, the simultaneous presence of operators
\hat{{\bf P}}
and
\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}
, which are respectively diagonal in real-space and momentum-space, leads to a nontrivial imaginary part of the expectation
\text{Im}\langle P_{\mathbb{r}}\,\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}\rangle_{t}
, even though
\text{Im}\langle\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}\rangle_{t}=0
due to the Hermiticity of
\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}
. Indeed the rate of change
\frac{d\langle\rho\rangle_{t,\mathbb{r}}}{dt}
at the specific position
{\bf r}
can be construed as a measure of the “non-Hermiticity” of
P_{\mathbb{r}}\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}
, which is manifested through the imaginary part of its eigenvalues. This exhibits how the probability density at position
{\bf r}
changes over time evolving to other positions.

Analogously, the rate of change of the pseudospin expectation is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,\mathbb{r}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\langle\Psi^{(0)}|e^{i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}\left[\hat{{\bf H}}_{f},\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}\bm{\sigma}P_{\mathbb{r}}\right]e^{-i\hat{{\bf H}}_{f}t}|\Psi^{(0)}\rangle

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\text{Re}\,\langle(\hat{{\bf P}}_{{\bf r}}\,\tilde{\mathbb{d}})\times\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t}.

This is reminiscent of the usual precession equation with RHS given by
2\,\tilde{\mathbb{d}}\times\bm{\sigma}
, except that the
\hat{{\bf P}}_{{\bf r}}
projector now appear prominently. Comparing Eqs.
39
and
40
, we see that the PD and pseudospin evolutions are both governed by
\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}\tilde{\mathbb{d}}
. Explicitly, the effect of
\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}
is to render this operator product non-local in momentum space:

\hat{{\bf P}}_{\mathbb{r}}\,\tilde{\mathbb{d}}=\sum_{\mathbb{p}}\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p})|\mathbb{r}\rangle\langle\mathbb{r}|\mathbb{p}\rangle\langle\mathbb{p}|=\sum_{\mathbb{p},\mathbb{p}^{\prime}}\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p})|e^{i(\mathbb{p}^{\prime}-\mathbb{p})\cdot\mathbb{r}}|\mathbb{p}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\mathbb{p}|.

(41)

Due to the spatial inhomogeneity of the real-space profile of the initial state, both
\mathbb{p}
and
\mathbb{p}^{\prime}
are not connected by a delta function, resulting in a more complicated expression involving both
\mathbb{p}
and
\mathbb{p}^{\prime}
. This can be interpreted as an interference mechanism between different momentum components, which gives rise to time dependence in the PD as well as the pseudospin expectation. As we discuss in the main text, this interference leads to a “Wick-rotated” diffusion process, by which slow decay emerges in the melting regime of both
\langle\rho\rangle_{x,t}
and
\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{x,t}
arise.

VI.1
Relation to Bloch dynamics for single spins

One can relate the above results to the much simpler well-known precession equation

\frac{d\tilde{{\bf S}}(\mathbb{p},t)}{dt}=2\,\tilde{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbb{p},t)\times\mathbb{d}(\mathbb{p}),

(42)

by defining a magnetization i.e. spectral pseudospin denstiy function
\tilde{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbb{p},t)
via
\langle\bm{\sigma}(t)\rangle=\pi\mathcal{N}^{2}\sum_{p_{x}}|\bar{\Phi}(p_{x})|^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{S}}(\mathbb{p},t)
, where
\langle\bm{\sigma}(t)\rangle=\int\langle\bm{\sigma}\rangle_{t,x}\,dx
is the total pseudospin expectation for the entire system. With the spatial inhomogeneity integrated over, we indeed obtain an equation of motion that is local in momentum.
