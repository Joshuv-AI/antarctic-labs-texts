A Symmetry problem for some quasi-linear equations in Euclidean space
Ramya Dutta, Pierre-Damien Thizy

Abstract
. We prove sharp asymptotic estimates for the gradient of positive solutions to certain nonlinear p \displaystyle p -Laplace equations in Euclidean space by showing symmetry and uniqueness of positive solutions to associated limiting problems.

1.
Introduction

In this article we are concerned with symmetry and uniqueness results for two quasi-linear elliptic equations in Euclidean space under point-wise bounds for the solutions. The first is the positive entire solutions of the eigenvalue problem for the
\displaystyle p
-Laplace operator in Euclidean space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}v=-\lambda v^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(1.1)

By standard elliptic regularity theory
[
11
]
,
[
33
]
any weak solution
\displaystyle v\in W_{\text{loc}}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of (
1.1
) is in fact
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We remark that when
\displaystyle\lambda<0
, by a standard application of Picone’s identity (see for example
[
2
, Theorem-2.1]
) it follows that there are no positive
\displaystyle W^{1,p}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
solutions to (
1.1
). When
\displaystyle\lambda=0
, the solutions
\displaystyle v
are called the
\displaystyle p
-harmonic functions and by a well known Liouville theorem the only positive
\displaystyle p
-harmonic functions are constant. We refer to
[
21
, Corollary-6.11]
for the Liouville theorem and for more details on
\displaystyle p
-harmonic functions in general. Henceforth we will only consider the case
\displaystyle\lambda>0
.

The second is a two weight quasi-linear elliptic equation in the punctured space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
arising from the Euler-Lagrange equation of the Hardy’s inequality in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
with two radial weights. Namely we consider the positive weak solutions
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,p}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
of the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(|x|^{-ap}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)=\frac{\mu}{|x|^{(a+1)p}}u^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

(1.2)

where,
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
,
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\displaystyle-\infty<\mu\leq\overline{\mu}_{a}
and
\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{a}:=\left|\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p}\right|^{p}
. The number
\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{a}
is the best constant in Hardy’s inequality when
\displaystyle a\neq\frac{n-p}{p}
(see
[
22
, Proposition-1.1]
and also
[
1
]
,
[
3
]
). Again by standard elliptic theory all positive weak solutions
\displaystyle u
of (
1.2
) are in
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
.

1.1.
The eigenfunction equation in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}

We are concerned with solutions to the problem (
1.1
) together with the point-wise bounds

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\Delta_{p}v=-\lambda v^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
&C_{1}e^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}\leq v(x)\leq C_{2}e^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}\end{split}

(1.3)

where,
\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}>0
are positive constants,
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}:=\left(\frac{\lambda}{p-1}\right)^{1/p}
when
\displaystyle\lambda>0
. Note that
\displaystyle v(x)=Ce^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}
solves the equation (
1.3
). The main result here is the
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry and uniqueness (up to multiplication by a positive constant) of solutions to the problem (
1.3
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let,
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
and
\displaystyle\lambda>0
. Then any weak solution of the problem (
1.3
) has
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry and is given by
\displaystyle v(x)=Ce^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
.

Problem of
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry and monotonicity of positive solutions has been extensively studied in context of
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian. For symmetry results in half-space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, we refer to
[
10
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
16
]
and for results in this direction for full space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we refer to
[
14
]
,
[
19
]
and references therein.

A similar problem was considered in
[
12
]
for the positive eigenfunctions in the real Hyperbolic space
\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{n}
of sectional curvature
\displaystyle-1
, where the moving plane technique was adapted appropriately to prove horospherical symmetry which lead to the uniqueness up to multiplication by a positive constant. A suitable adaptation of moving plane technique does not seem to be immediate for the Euclidean problem (
1.3
). Instead we will use a variation of the strong maximum (or minimum) principle to show that the solutions of problem (
1.3
) have
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry from which the uniqueness result follows.

Problem (
1.3
) together with the point-wise bounds appear naturally as a limiting problem in various contexts. As a first application of Theorem-
1.1
we look at sharp asymptotic gradient estimates at infinity of positive solutions of
\displaystyle p
-Laplace equations with a non-linearity. In
[
20

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}u-\frac{\mu|u|^{p-2}u}{|x|^{p}}+\lambda|u|^{p-2}u=f(u)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}

(1.4)

for
\displaystyle u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
where,
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n

\displaystyle 0\leq\mu<\overline{\mu}:=\left(\frac{n-p}{p}\right)^{p}
,
\displaystyle\lambda>0

\displaystyle f\in C(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{|f(t)|}{|t|^{q-1}}\leq A<+\infty,\quad\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{|f(t)|}{|t|^{p^{\ast}-1}}\leq A<+\infty

\displaystyle 1<p<q<p^{\ast}=\frac{np}{n-p}

The sharp point-wise asymptotic estimates of the solutions and its gradient for the case
\displaystyle\lambda=0

\displaystyle\mu=0

\displaystyle\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
be the two roots of the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma^{p-1}(n-p-(p-1)\gamma)=\mu

(1.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma_{1}<\frac{n-p}{p}<\gamma_{2}\leq\frac{n-p}{p-1}

\displaystyle\mu\in\left[0,\overline{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}:=\left(\frac{\lambda}{p-1}\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle B_{\rho}(0)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(0)

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(x)\asymp|x|^{-\gamma_{1}},\text{ for }x\in B_{\rho}(0)\setminus\{0\}

(1.7)

\displaystyle\displaystyle u(x)\asymp|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{p(p-1)}}e^{-\alpha_{\lambda}|x|},\text{ for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}(0)

(1.8)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle n,p,\mu,q,A,f
of the equation (
1.4

\displaystyle f\asymp g
for positive functions
\displaystyle f,g

\displaystyle C_{1}g\leq f\leq C_{2}g

\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}>0

1.2.
The two weight quasi-linear equation with Hardy potential in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

We consider a class of quasi-linear elliptic Euler-Lagrange equations arising from the Hardy’s inequality (see
[

,
[
3

\displaystyle n\geq 2

\displaystyle p\in(1,n)

\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\frac{n-p}{p}\right\}

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{a}:=\left|\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p}\right|^{p}>0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\overline{\mu}_{a}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|v|^{p}}{|x|^{(a+1)p}}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\nabla v|^{p}}{|x|^{ap}}\,dx,\text{ for }v\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\right).

(1.16)

We are considering the problem

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}&-\Delta_{p,a}u-\frac{\mu}{|x|^{(a+1)p}}u^{p-1}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\\
&C_{1}|x|^{-\gamma}\leq u(x)\leq C_{2}|x|^{-\gamma}\end{split}

(1.17)

where,
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
,
\displaystyle-\Delta_{p,a}u:=-\operatorname{div}\left(|x|^{-ap}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\right)

\displaystyle-\infty<\mu\leq\overline{\mu}_{a}

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\gamma|^{p-2}\gamma(n-(a+1)p-(p-1)\gamma)=\mu.

\displaystyle a=\frac{n-p}{p}
. The radial function
\displaystyle u(x)=|x|^{-\gamma}

) if and only if
\displaystyle\gamma
satisfies (

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0\leq\gamma_{1,a}\leq\gamma_{a}^{\ast}\leq\gamma_{2,a}\leq\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1},\text{ when }\mu\in[0,\overline{\mu}_{a}]\text{ and }a<\frac{n-p}{p}

(1.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{1,a}<0<\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}<\gamma_{2,a},\text{ when }\mu<0\text{ and }a<\frac{n-p}{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{1,a}<\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}<0<\gamma_{2,a},\text{ when }\mu<0\text{ and }a>\frac{n-p}{p}.

Theorem 1.6

\displaystyle 1<p<n

2.
Main ideas and motivation for the proof of Theorem-
1.1

Before going to the proof of Theorem-
1.1
we briefly motivate the strategy of proof. We remark that a similar problem of classification of eigenfunctions was also considered in context of the real Hyperbolic space of constant sectional curvature
\displaystyle-1
in
[
12
, Theorem-3.8]
where the result was proved using a suitable modification of the moving plane method. A similar adaptation of moving plane technique does not seem to be immediate for the Euclidean problem (
1.3
). Instead we will use a variation of the strong maximum (or minimum) principle to show that the solutions of problem (
1.3
) have
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry which in turn implies the uniqueness result of Theorem-
1.1
.

It is interesting to note that the solutions of problem (
1.3
) correspond to the equality case in a gradient estimate with the best constant for positive eigenfunctions in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A gradient estimate with the best constant for positive eigenfunctions of
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian on complete non-compact manifolds
\displaystyle(M,g)
with negative lower bound on Ricci curvature
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}\geq-(n-1)g
was proved in
[
32
]
. A straightforward adaptation of
[
32
, Theorem-1.1]
gives the best constant for the gradient estimate of positive eigenfunctions in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We add a detailed proof of the lemma in Appendix for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle n\geq 2
,
\displaystyle 1<p<n
and
\displaystyle\lambda>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle v\in C^{1,\alpha}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be a positive eigenfunction of the
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian corresponding to the eigenvalue
\displaystyle-\lambda
i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}v=-\lambda v^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.1)

Then
\displaystyle v
satisfies the sharp gradient estimate

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\nabla\log v|\leq\alpha_{\lambda}

(2.2)

where,
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}=\left(\frac{\lambda}{p-1}\right)^{1/p}
.

We start by considering the simple situation where equality is attained in the gradient estimate (
2.2
) by a positive solution of (
2.1
) at an interior point
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We use the notation
\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{T}
that is
\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v})_{ij}=(\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j})
for
\displaystyle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\left<(\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v})\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right>=\left<\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\right>\left<\mathbf{u},\mathbf{y}\right>

for
\displaystyle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We denote the formally linearized
\displaystyle p
-Laplace operator by

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{v}(f):=\operatorname{div}\left(|\nabla v|^{p-2}A(\nabla f)\right)

(2.3)

where,

A(\nabla f):=\left[\text{Id}+(p-2)|\nabla v|^{-2}(\nabla v\otimes\nabla v)\right](\nabla f)=\nabla f+(p-2)|\nabla v|^{-2}\left<\nabla v,\nabla f\right>\nabla v.

Note that when
\displaystyle|\nabla v|>0
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left<A(\nabla f),\nabla f\right>=|\nabla f|^{2}+(p-2)|\nabla v|^{-2}\left<\nabla v,\nabla f\right>^{2}\geq\min\{1,p-1\}|\nabla f|^{2}

(2.4)

so that
\displaystyle L_{v}
is a strictly positive definite elliptic operator whenever
\displaystyle|\nabla v|>0
. Suppose
\displaystyle v
to be a
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
positive solution of (
2.1
), by standard elliptic regularity theory
\displaystyle v
is smooth away from the critical point set of
\displaystyle v
i.e.,
\displaystyle v\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v})
where the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{v}:=\{\nabla v=0\}
of critical points of
\displaystyle v
is closed. Therefore,
\displaystyle v_{x_{j}}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x_{j}}
(for
\displaystyle j=1,\cdots,n
) satisfies the linearized equation corresponding to (
2.1
) weakly,

\displaystyle\displaystyle-L_{v}(v_{x_{j}})=-(p-1)\lambda v^{p-2}v_{x_{j}}

(2.5)

for each
\displaystyle j=1,\cdots,n
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v}
. That is

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla v|^{p-2}\left<\nabla v_{x_{j}},\nabla\varphi\right>+(p-2)|\nabla v|^{p-4}\left<\nabla v,\nabla v_{x_{j}}\right>\left<\nabla v,\nabla\varphi\right>\,dx=-(p-1)\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v^{p-2}v_{x_{j}}\varphi\,dx

(2.6)

for all
\displaystyle\varphi\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v})
with compact support. We also make a note of the simple fact that

\displaystyle\displaystyle-L_{v}(v)=-(p-1)\lambda v^{p-2}v.

(2.7)

Therefore taking linear combination of (
2.5
) and (
2.7
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle-L_{v}(\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>+cv)=-(p-1)\lambda v^{p-2}(\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>+cv)

(2.8)

is satisfied weakly in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v}
for any
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle v
be a positive
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
solution of (
2.1
) in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle|\nabla\log v|(x_{0})=\alpha_{\lambda}
for some
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\displaystyle v(x)=Ce^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
and
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let us denote
\displaystyle\frac{\nabla\log v(x_{0})}{|\nabla\log v(x_{0})|}=\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Then from the gradient estimate of Lemma-
2.1
and (
2.8
), we know
\displaystyle(\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>)
is a non-negative weak solution of the linearized equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle-L_{v}(\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>)=-(p-1)\lambda v^{p-2}(\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>)

(2.9)

in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v}
where,
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{v}=\{\nabla v=0\}
. Furthermore,
\displaystyle(\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>)(x_{0})=0
implies
\displaystyle|\nabla v(x_{0})|>0
i.e.,
\displaystyle L_{v}
is strictly positive definite and elliptic in a neighborhood
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(x_{0})
for some
\displaystyle\delta>0
. Applying Harnack inequality (see
[
30
]
,
[
34
]
) for (
2.9
) in
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(x_{0})
we get
\displaystyle(\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>)\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle B_{\delta}(x_{0})
. This in particular also implies
\displaystyle|\nabla v|>0
in
\displaystyle\overline{B_{\delta}(x_{0})}
. Let
\displaystyle\Omega
denote the connected component of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{v}
such that
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\Omega
. Again applying Harnack inequality we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>\equiv 0\text{ in }\Omega.

(2.10)

Then
\displaystyle|\nabla v|>0
on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
which leads to a contradiction unless
\displaystyle\partial\Omega=\emptyset
i.e.,
\displaystyle\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
. But from the gradient estimate
\displaystyle|\nabla v|\leq\alpha_{\lambda}v
we get
\displaystyle\left<\nabla v,\xi^{\prime}\right>\equiv 0
for any
\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}\in\xi^{\perp}
i.e.,
\displaystyle\left<\xi^{\prime},\xi\right>=0
. This implies
\displaystyle v
is only a function of
\displaystyle\left<x,\xi\right>
. This combined with
\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}v-\left<\nabla v,\xi\right>\equiv 0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}
from (
2.10
) gives the desired conclusion
\displaystyle v(x)=Ce^{\alpha_{\lambda}\left<x,\xi\right>}
.
∎

In light of the inequality (
2.2
) and Lemma-
2.2
it is natural to expect rigidity for positive solutions
\displaystyle v
of (
2.1
) among the class of functions

\displaystyle\displaystyle\log v=\alpha_{\lambda}E+b

(2.11)

where,
\displaystyle E
satisfies
\displaystyle|\nabla E|\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle b
is a bounded function in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The only entire solutions of
\displaystyle|\nabla E|\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
are given up to additive constants by the affine functions
\displaystyle E(x)=E(x,\xi)+C
where,

\displaystyle\displaystyle E(x,\xi):=\left<x,\xi\right>

(2.12)

(see for instance
[
9
, Lemma-7.1]
and
[
4
]
). Note that is the Busemann function in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
corresponding to
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is precisely the affine function

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}(t-|x-t\xi|)=\left<x,\xi\right>.

(2.13)

Positive solutions
\displaystyle v
of (
2.1
) in class (
2.11
) are necessarily extremals of the inequality (
2.2
), i.e.,
\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\log v|=\alpha_{\lambda}
but the boundedness assumption on
\displaystyle b
or equivalently the point-wise bounds on
\displaystyle v
assumed in (

) does not immediately imply that equality in (
2.2
) happens at an interior point
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We show that the boundedness assumption on
\displaystyle b
is sufficient to establish
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional symmetry of the solutions which in turn implies that equality must hold in (

) at in interior point
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
i.e., we must have
\displaystyle|\nabla\log v|\equiv\alpha_{\lambda}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\displaystyle b
must be a constant function. Although the best constant in the inequality (
2.2
) of Lemma-
2.1
is not directly used in the proof of Theorem-

, it is implicitly being used through the point-wise bounds assumed in (

). We will also see this in proof of Theorem-
1.4
, where the best constant of the relevant gradient estimate (

) is not known a priori, but the point-wise bounds on the solutions are sufficient for the purpose.

Note that by making an orthogonal change of coordinates, without loss of generality we may assume

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}v

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\lambda v^{p-1}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}e^{\alpha_{\lambda}x_{n}}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle x_{n}
variable. The uniqueness (up to multiplication be a positive constant) will follow from this. By translation invariance of (

\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
-estimate (

[
11
]

\displaystyle c(n,p,\lambda)>0

\displaystyle|\nabla\log v|\leq c(n,p,\lambda)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle w:=\log v

3.1
) and (
3.2
) we get
\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\Delta_{p}w=-\lambda+(p-1)|\nabla w|^{p}\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}x_{n}+c_{1}\leq w(x)\leq\alpha_{\lambda}x_{n}+c_{2}

(3.4)

\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{+}:=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap\{x_{n}>0\}

\displaystyle w_{\nu}:=\left<\nabla w,\nu\right>

Step-I:
We claim that in (
3.5

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha(\nu),\beta(\nu)\in\{0,\alpha_{\lambda}\left<\nu,e_{n}\right>\}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\nu\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{+}

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)

\displaystyle\alpha(\nu)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)=0

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)\neq 0

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)=\alpha_{\lambda}\left<\nu,e_{n}\right>

Let
\displaystyle\left\{y^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle w_{\nu}(y^{(k)})\to\beta(\nu)
as
\displaystyle k\to\infty

\displaystyle w^{(k)}(x):=w(x+y^{(k)})-\alpha_{\lambda}y^{(k)}_{n}
. As before we note that
\displaystyle w^{(k)}
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}&-\Delta_{p}w^{(k)}=-\lambda+(p-1)|\nabla w^{(k)}|^{p}\\
&\alpha_{\lambda}x_{n}+c_{1}\leq w^{(k)}(x)\leq\alpha_{\lambda}x_{n}+c_{2}\\
&w^{(k)}_{\nu}(0)=w_{\nu}(y^{(k)})\to\beta(\nu).\end{cases}

(3.7)

Furthermore, from definition we have
\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w^{(k)}_{\nu}=\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w_{\nu}=\beta(\nu)
for all
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}
. Applying
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
-estimate (
[
33
, Theorem-1]
,
[
11
]
) we have
\displaystyle[w^{(k)}]_{C^{1,\alpha}(B_{R})}\leq C_{R}
for some constant
\displaystyle C_{R}>0
. Letting
\displaystyle R\to\infty
and passing through a subsequence (which we continue to index with
\displaystyle k
) we have
\displaystyle w^{(k)}\to w^{(\infty)}
in
\displaystyle C^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\displaystyle w^{(\infty)}
also satisfies (
3.3
) and (
3.4
). Owing to
\displaystyle C^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
convergence we have
\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w^{(\infty)}_{\nu}\geq\beta(\nu)
and

\inf_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}w^{(\infty)}_{\nu}=w^{(\infty)}_{\nu}(0)=\beta(\nu)

\displaystyle w^{(\infty)}_{\nu}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let us denote by
\displaystyle v^{(\infty)}=e^{w^{(\infty)}}
which also satisfies (

) and (

). Denoting
\displaystyle v^{(\infty)}_{\nu}:=\left<\nabla v^{(\infty)},\nu\right>

\displaystyle(v_{\nu}^{(\infty)}-\beta(\nu)v^{(\infty)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle-L_{v^{(\infty)}}(v^{(\infty)}_{\nu}-\beta(\nu)v^{(\infty)})=-(p-1)\lambda\left(v^{(\infty)}\right)^{p-2}(v^{(\infty)}_{\nu}-\beta(\nu)v^{(\infty)})

in

\displaystyle\beta(\nu)\neq 0

\displaystyle(v^{(\infty)}_{\nu}-\beta(\nu)v^{(\infty)})(0)=0

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\alpha=-\gamma\kappa_{1}
when
\displaystyle\gamma\neq 0
and leads to a contradiction when
\displaystyle\gamma=0
(as
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
), proving the claim.

Step-II:
From previous step (
3.38
) when
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0
we have
\displaystyle\alpha\leq 0
i.e.,

V(u)=\kappa_{1}V_{0}(u)+\kappa_{2}V_{i_{0},j_{0}}(u)\leq 0

and when
\displaystyle\gamma<0
we have
\displaystyle\beta\geq 0
i.e.,

V(u)=\kappa_{1}V_{0}(u)+\kappa_{2}V_{i_{0},j_{0}}(u)\geq 0

for all
\displaystyle\kappa_{1}>0
and
\displaystyle\kappa_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. In either case letting
\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\to 0^{+}
and setting
\displaystyle\kappa_{2}=\pm 1
we get
\displaystyle V_{i_{0},j_{0}}(u)\equiv 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq i_{0}<j_{0}\leq n
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\left<\nabla u,\xi\right>=0
for all
\displaystyle\xi\in V_{0}^{\perp}
and hence
\displaystyle u
must be a radial function. By abuse of notation we denote
\displaystyle u(x)=u(r)
where,
\displaystyle r=|x|
. Then if
\displaystyle\gamma\geq 0
we have
\displaystyle V_{0}(u)=ru^{\prime}(r)\leq 0
i.e.,
\displaystyle u
is non-increasing and if
\displaystyle\gamma<0
we have
\displaystyle V_{0}(u)=ru^{\prime}(r)\geq 0
i.e.,
\displaystyle u
is non-decreasing. Then the equation (
1.17
) can be written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\left(r^{n-1-ap}|u^{\prime}(r)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(r)\right)^{\prime}=\mu\,r^{n-1-(a+1)p}u^{p-1}(r)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty)

(3.48)

\displaystyle\displaystyle C_{1}r^{-\gamma}\leq u(r)\leq C_{2}r^{-\gamma}.

(3.49)

Also from the point-wise gradient estimate (
3.33
) we have
\displaystyle r|u^{\prime}(r)|\leq\tilde{\gamma}u(r)
.

First we consider the case
\displaystyle\mu=0
so that either
\displaystyle\gamma=0
or
\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}
(when
\displaystyle n-(a+1)p\neq 0
). We have
\displaystyle\left(r^{n-1-ap}|u^{\prime}(r)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(r)\right)^{\prime}=0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
and consequently either
\displaystyle u^{\prime}(r)\equiv 0
in which case
\displaystyle u
is a constant function or
\displaystyle|u^{\prime}(r)|=Cr^{-\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}-1}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
. If
\displaystyle n-(a+1)p=0
then
\displaystyle|u^{\prime}(r)|=\frac{C}{r}
can be ruled out by the point-wise bounds (
3.49
). So the remaining possibility is
\displaystyle u(r)=Cr^{-\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
.

Otherwise when
\displaystyle\mu\neq 0
we have
\displaystyle\gamma\neq 0,\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}
as well. We use the point-wise bounds (
3.49
) on RHS of (
3.48
), the point-wise gradient estimate (
3.33
)

r|u^{\prime}(r)|\leq\tilde{\gamma}u(r)\implies r^{n-1-ap}|u^{\prime}(r)|^{p-1}\leq C^{\prime}r^{(p-1)\left(\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}-\gamma\right)}

and integrate (

) from
\displaystyle 0
to
\displaystyle r
when
\displaystyle\gamma<\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}
or from
\displaystyle r
to
\displaystyle\infty
when
\displaystyle\gamma>\frac{n-(a+1)p}{p-1}
to get the point-wise estimate for the gradient
\displaystyle|u^{\prime}(r)|\asymp r^{-\gamma-1}
in
\displaystyle(0,\infty)
. This in particular implies
\displaystyle\alpha:=\sup_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{V_{0}(u)}{u}=\sup_{r>0}\frac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{u(r)}
and

in (

) are never equal to
\displaystyle 0

we must have
\displaystyle\alpha=\beta=-\gamma
. Hence
\displaystyle\frac{ru^{\prime}(r)}{u(r)}\equiv-\gamma
in
\displaystyle(0,\infty)

\displaystyle u(r)=Cr^{-\gamma}
for some
\displaystyle C>0
. ∎

4.1.
Proof of the sharp gradient estimate for eigenfunctions

In this section we present a proof of the sharp gradient Lemma-
2.1
.

Proof of Lemma-
2.1
.

By translation invariance of (
2.1
),
\displaystyle C^{1,\alpha}
-estimate
[
11
]
,
[
33
]
and Harnack inequality
[
34
]
we have

|\nabla v|(x_{0})\leq\sup_{B_{1/2}(x_{0})}|\nabla v|\leq C\sup_{B_{1}(x_{0})}v\leq c(n,p,\lambda)v(x_{0})

for all
\displaystyle x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some universal constant
\displaystyle c(n,p,\lambda)>0
i.e.,
\displaystyle|\nabla\log v|\leq c(n,p,\lambda)
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We set the notations
\displaystyle w:=-(p-1)\log v
and the constant
\displaystyle\kappa^{p/2}:=(p-1)^{p-1}\lambda
. Then
\displaystyle w
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{p}w=-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}+|\nabla w|^{p}

(4.1)

and further we use the notation
\displaystyle f=|\nabla w|^{2}
.

Claim
:
\displaystyle f\leq\kappa
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Suppose to the contrary that
\displaystyle\{f>\kappa\}
is non-empty. Following the notation of linearized
\displaystyle p
-Laplacian from (
2.3
) and
[
32
, Lemma-2.1]
we have the point-wise Bochner identity

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{w}(f)=2f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}w_{ij}^{2}+\left(\frac{p}{2}-1\right)|\nabla f|^{2}f^{\frac{p}{2}-2}+pf^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<\nabla w,\nabla f\right>

(4.2)

wherever
\displaystyle f=|\nabla w|^{2}>0
. In this case we note that
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}=0
in the identity. By standard Elliptic regularity theory we know
\displaystyle w,f\in C^{\infty}
wherever
\displaystyle f=|\nabla w|>0
. By Sard’s theorem almost all level sets
\displaystyle\{f=c\}
for
\displaystyle c>0
are regular and smooth. The outward unit normal to
\displaystyle\partial\{f>c\}=\{f=c\}
is given by
\displaystyle\nu:=-\frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}
for these levels.

Let
\displaystyle\delta>0
be such that
\displaystyle\{f=\kappa+\delta\}
is a regular level set. Denote
\displaystyle\omega:=\left(f-(\kappa+\delta)\right)_{+}
and the support of
\displaystyle\omega
as
\displaystyle\Omega:=\left\{f>\kappa+\delta\right\}
, so that
\displaystyle\omega\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. The first step is to show that there exists positive constants
\displaystyle a,b>0
depending only on the parameters
\displaystyle n,p,\delta
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L_{w}(\varphi)\omega\,dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(a\omega-b|\nabla\omega|)\,dx

(4.3)

for all non-negative test functions
\displaystyle\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Applying integration by parts twice on LHS of (
4.3
) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L_{w}(\varphi)\omega\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}L_{w}(\omega)\varphi\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<A(\nabla\varphi),\nu\right>\omega\,d\sigma-\int_{\partial\Omega}f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<A(\nabla\omega),\nu\right>\varphi\,d\sigma

(4.4)

where,
\displaystyle\nu=-\frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}
is the outward unit normal to
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. By a abuse of notation we will also denote
\displaystyle\nu=-\frac{\nabla\omega}{|\nabla\omega|}
on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. Note that the first boundary term on RHS of (
4.4
) vanishes as
\displaystyle\omega=0
on
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
and as for the second boundary term in (
4.4
) we have

-\int_{\partial\Omega}f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<A(\nabla\omega),\nu\right>\varphi\,d\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega}f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<A(\nabla\omega),\frac{\nabla\omega}{|\nabla\omega|}\right>\varphi\,d\sigma\geq 0.

Therefore

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L_{w}(\varphi)\omega\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}L_{w}(\omega)\varphi\,dx

(4.5)

for all non-negative test functions
\displaystyle\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

It suffices to show that
\displaystyle L_{w}(\omega)\geq(a\omega-b|\nabla\omega|)
in
\displaystyle\Omega
. Using an orthonormal frame of coordinates
\displaystyle\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\displaystyle e_{n}=\frac{\nabla w}{|\nabla w|}
we can rewrite equation (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta_{p}w=|\nabla w|^{p-2}\left(\Delta u+(p-2)|\nabla w|^{-2}\left<(\nabla^{2}w)\nabla w,\nabla w\right>\right)=-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}+|\nabla w|^{p}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left(w_{11}+\cdots+w_{nn}+(p-2)w_{nn}\right)=f^{\frac{p}{2}}-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle w_{11}+\cdots+w_{n-1,n-1}=f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}-(p-1)w_{nn}.

(4.6)

Then using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the first term on the RHS of equation (
4.2
) followed by the substitution of the identity (
4.6
) we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}w_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{(w_{11}+\cdots+w_{n-1,n-1})^{2}}{n-1}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{in}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{(f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}-(p-1)w_{nn})^{2}}{n-1}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{in}^{2}.

(4.7)

Note that
\displaystyle\nabla f=2|\nabla w|(w_{1n},\cdots,w_{nn})^{T}=2f^{1/2}(w_{1n},\cdots,w_{nn})^{T}
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\left<\nabla f,\nabla w\right>=2fw_{nn}
. Also,

\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{in}^{2}=\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{4f}.

Therefore, using these we rewrite the RHS of inequality (
4.7
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}w_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq\frac{(f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1})^{2}}{n-1}-2\frac{p-1}{n-1}w_{nn}\left(f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}\right)+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}w_{in}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{(f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1})^{2}}{n-1}-\frac{p-1}{n-1}\left(f-f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}\right)\frac{\left<\nabla f,\nabla w\right>}{f}+\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{4f}.

(4.8)

Using inequality (
4.8
) in the identity (
4.2
) we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{w}(\omega)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\geq 2f^{1-\frac{p}{2}}\frac{(f^{\frac{p}{2}}-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1})^{2}}{n-1}-2\frac{p-1}{n-1}f^{-1}\left(f^{\frac{p}{2}}-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1}\right)\left<\nabla f,\nabla w\right>

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{4f}+pf^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<\nabla w,\nabla f\right>.

(4.9)

Note that we have
\displaystyle 0<\kappa+\delta\leq f=|\nabla w|^{2}\leq C(n,p,\lambda)
,
\displaystyle f=\omega+\kappa+\delta
and
\displaystyle\nabla f=\nabla\omega
in
\displaystyle\Omega
. Using the mean value theorem we may write

\displaystyle\displaystyle(f^{\frac{p}{2}}-\lambda(p-1)^{p-1})^{2}=\frac{p^{2}}{4}\xi^{p-2}(f-\kappa)^{2}\geq c_{0}\omega

(4.10)

where,
\displaystyle\kappa<\xi<f
and
\displaystyle c_{0}
is a constant depending on
\displaystyle n,p,\kappa,\delta
. Finally using this in (
4.9
) together with the point-wise bounds on
\displaystyle f
,
\displaystyle|\nabla w|
in
\displaystyle\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{w}(\omega)\geq a\omega-b|\nabla\omega|\text{ in }\Omega

(4.11)

and consequently together with the inequality (
4.5
) we get (
4.3
).

Testing (
4.3
) with
\displaystyle\varphi^{2}\omega^{q}
for some non-negative test function
\displaystyle\varphi
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
we get the inequality

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}f^{\frac{p}{2}-1}\left<A(\nabla(\varphi^{2}\omega^{q})),\nabla\omega\right>\,dx\geq\int_{\Omega}\left(a\varphi^{2}\omega^{q+1}-b\varphi^{2}\omega^{q}|\nabla\omega|\right)\,dx.

(4.12)

Rearranging the terms in (
4.12
) and using the inequality
\displaystyle\left<A(\nabla\omega),\nabla\omega\right>\geq\min\{1,p-1\}|\nabla\omega|^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle a\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq b\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q}|\nabla\omega|\,dx+c_{1}\int_{\Omega}\varphi\omega^{q}|\nabla\varphi||\nabla\omega|\,dx-c_{2}q\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q-1}|\nabla\omega|^{2}\,dx.

(4.13)

Again using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle a\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx+\frac{b}{4\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q-1}|\nabla\omega|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx+\frac{c_{1}}{4\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q-1}|\nabla\omega|^{2}\omega^{q-1}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad-c_{2}q\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q-1}|\nabla\omega|^{2}\,dx.

(4.14)

Then choosing
\displaystyle\epsilon<a/2
to be sufficiently small and
\displaystyle q>1
to be large enough such that
\displaystyle c_{2}q>\frac{b+c_{1}}{4\epsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle a\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\omega^{q+1}\,dx.

(4.15)

Choosing
\displaystyle\varphi\equiv 1
in
\displaystyle B_{k}
such that
\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi|\leq 2
in
\displaystyle B_{k+1}\setminus B_{k}
for positive integers
\displaystyle k
we successively get

\displaystyle\displaystyle a\int_{B_{k}}\omega^{q+1}\,dx\leq 4\epsilon\int_{B_{k+1}\setminus B_{k}}\omega^{q+1}\,dx\implies\frac{c}{\epsilon}\int_{B_{k}}\omega^{q+1}\,dx\leq\int_{B_{k+1}}\omega^{q+1}\,dx.

(4.16)

Iterating this for
\displaystyle k\geq 1
we get

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\omega^{q+1}\,dx\geq Ce^{R\log\frac{c}{\epsilon}}

(4.17)

for all
\displaystyle R
sufficiently large. However,
\displaystyle\omega
is a bounded function and the volume of the ball
\displaystyle|B_{R}|=|B_{1}|R^{n}
only grows polynomialy. This leads to a contradiction unless
\displaystyle\omega\equiv 0
, i.e.,
\displaystyle f\leq\kappa
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This completes the proof of the sharp gradient estimate from above. ∎
