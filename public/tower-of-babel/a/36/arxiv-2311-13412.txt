Improving tensor regression by optimal model averaging
Qiushi Bu, Hua Liang, Xinyu Zhang, Jiahui Zou

Abstract
Tensors have broad applications in neuroimaging, data mining, digital marketing, etc. CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) tensor decomposition can effectively reduce the number of parameters to gain dimensionality-reduction and thus plays a key role in tensor regression. However, in CP decomposition, there is uncertainty which rank to use. In this article, we develop a model averaging method to handle this uncertainty by weighting the estimators from candidate tensor regression models with different ranks. When all candidate models are misspecified, we prove that the model averaging estimator is asymptotically optimal. When correct models are included in the candidate models, we prove the consistency of parameters and the convergence of the model averaging weight. Simulations and empirical studies illustrate that the proposed method has superiority over the competition methods and has promising applications. Keywords: Tensor regression; Model averaging; Model misspecification; Cross-validation; Asymptotic optimality



1
Introduction

In the information age, a huge amount of information can be collected, and the dimension of the data to be processed is increasing. Tensors, also called multidimensional arrays, refer to data with higher spatial dimensions, rather than high-dimensional arrays with many variables in one dimension. For example, neuroimaging data
(
Zhou et al., 2013
;
Li et al., 2018b
)
, longitudinal time series data
(
Wang et al., 2021
;
Si et al., 2022
)
, and digital marketing data
(
Bi et al., 2018
)
all have 3 or more dimensions. A simple way to process a tensor is to ignore the spatial structure and vectorizing it. However, simply vectorizing a tensor and ignoring its spatial structure may result in a much larger parameter size than the sample size, and the parameters solved by this method may be inefficient
(
Yuan and Zhang, 2016
)
. Taking the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data with dimension
\mathbb{R}^{64\times 64\times 64}
as an example, the number of free parameters will be
64\times 64\times 64=262144
if we use linear regression after vectorization.

Tensor decomposition solves this problem very well because it can effectively reduce the size of parameters. Various tensor decomposition methods have been developed, and two widely used methods are CP
(
Harshman, 1970
)
and Tucker tensor decomposition
(
Tucker, 1966
)
. The CP tensor decomposition can significantly compress a tensor by representing it as a linear combination of rank-1 basis tensors. An
N
-way tensor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{I_{1}\times I_{2}\times\dots\times I_{N}}
is rank-1 if it can be presented as the outer product of
N
vectors, and the rank of a tensor is the smallest number for which a tensor can be written as the sum of rank-1 tensors
(
Kolda and Bader, 2009
)
. If we apply CP tensor decomposition with a rank-5 approximation to the same MRI data, the number of free parameters will reduce to
(64+64+64)\times 5=960
, which is much smaller than the value used in the vectorization method.
Guo et al., 2011
first apply the CP tensor decomposition in the linear regression models, and
Zhou et al., 2013
extend it to the generalized linear models (GLMs). Since then, there has been rapid development in the area of CP tensor regression. For example,
Lock, 2018
studies the case where the response variable is also a tensor, and
Ke et al., 2023
apply the CP tensor decomposition to quantile regressions.

Algorithms for tensor decomposition all require a given rank, but the rank is unknown in applications.
Håstad, 1990
demonstrates that calculating the rank is an NP-complete problem. The existing literature mainly uses model selection methods to choose a rank. For example,
Wang et al., 2022
use the Akaike information criterion (AIC) to choose rank, while
Zhou et al., 2013
,
Zhang et al., 2019
and
Ke et al., 2023
apply the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) to select the model.
Zhang et al., 2019
and
Ke et al., 2023
also prove the rank selected by BIC recovers the true rank for the Gaussian linear model when the true model is included in the set of candidate models. However, model selection methods are sometimes unstable since a small perturbation in the data may cause the selected model to be completely different
(
Yuan and Yang, 2005
)
. Simulations in the current paper also show the poor performance of model selection methods under high noise levels. Differing from model selection which chooses the “best” model based on certain criteria, model averaging weights the estimators of different models and avoids putting all eggs in one unevenly woven basket
(
Longford, 2005
)
.

There are two main streams of model averaging: Bayesian model averaging (BMA) and frequentist model averaging (FMA). Although BMA is flexible and can work for a wide range of models
(
Hoeting et al., 1999
)
, it still has drawbacks. Different prior distributions can have a profound impact on regression results, but choosing an appropriate prior distribution is often challenging and experiential. FMA can be further divided into different categories, including smoothed information criteria
(
Buckland et al., 1997
;
Hjort and Claeskens, 2003
)
, adaptive weighting
(
Yuan and Yang, 2005
;
Yang, 2001
;
Zhang et al., 2013
)
, and optimal weighting
(
Wan et al., 2010
;
Liu, 2015
;
Lu and Su, 2015
)
. Among these methods, the optimal weighting method is well developed and widely used. Initially, it focuses on linear models such as Mallows model averaging
(
Hansen, 2007
)
, jackknife model averaging
(
Hansen and Racine, 2012
)
, and heteroskedasticity robust
C_{p}

(
Liu and Okui, 2013
)
. In addition to linear regression models, researchers have studied model averaging under other frameworks such as GLMs
(
Zhang et al., 2016
)
, nonlinear models
(
Feng et al., 2022
)
and semi-parametric models
(
Li et al., 2018a
)
. Model averaging for high-dimensional linear regression is also widely studied
(
Ando and Li, 2014
;
Yu and Feng, 2014
;
Feng and Liu, 2020
)
. However, few researchers have focused on model averaging on multidimensional arrays. This article develops the optimal model averaging method for the CP tensor regression for the first time.

Based on the block relaxation algorithm proposed by
Zhou et al., 2013
, we develop a model averaging method based on Kullback–Leibler (
\mathrm{KL}
) loss to weight the estimators from candidate models with different ranks under the GLM framework.
\mathrm{KL}
divergence is commonly used in evaluating the performance of the generalized linear model
(
Ando and Li, 2017
;
Zou et al., 2022
)
as a replacement for the square loss. To avoid overfitting, we use a
J-
fold cross-validation (CV) to calculate model averaging weights.
Hansen and Racine, 2012
and
Ando and Li, 2017
apply leave-one-out cross-validation on linear models and generalized linear models, respectively. Once the weights are obtained, the model averaging estimator can be represented as a weighted average of estimators from different models.

One theoretical contribution of this article is to show that our proposed estimator is asymptotically optimal when all candidate models are misspecified. Although
Zhang et al., 2019
and
Ke et al., 2023
prove that BIC model selection can choose the true rank, it only works when the candidate model set contains the true model. The asymptotic optimality guarantees that our method is at least better than BIC model selection in the case of misspecification. The simulation results also confirm this. Furthermore, the consistency of the model averaging estimator and the convergence of the weight are studied when at least one correct model is included in the set of candidate models. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time the CP tensor regression is involved in the FMA framework. This article provides a new solution to the rank selection problem in tensor regressions and tensor decompositions.

The remainder of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
begins with the notation and methodologies of the CP tensor regression, and then develops the model averaging procedure. Section
3
provides theoretical results of the model averaging estimator, including asymptotic optimality under the misspecified framework, root
-n
consistency and the weight convergence when the set of candidate models contains the correct model. Section
4
presents experimental results on simulated data and human brain MRI data to illustrate the advantage of the proposed method. Further discussions and conclusions are given in Section
5
. Finally, technical proofs are provided in the Appendix.

2
Model setup and estimation

2.1
Notation and preliminaries

We introduce the notations about tensors, i.e., we use uppercase calligraphic letters, such as
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, to represent tensors, uppercase bold letters, such as
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, to represent matrices, and lowercase bold letters, such as
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, to represent vectors.
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denotes the
\ell_{2}
norm of a vector. The
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
operator stacks the elements of a tensor
\mathcal{B}
into a column vector. Specifically, the
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{D})
entry of
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\dots\times p_{D}}
maps to the
j
th entry of
\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{B})
, where
j=1+\sum_{d=1}^{D}\left(i_{d}-1\right)\prod_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d-1}p_{d^{\prime}}
. The operator
\circ
represents the outer product, which means each element of the outer product is the product of the corresponding vector elements:
(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r)}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(r)}_{D})_{i_{1},\dots,i_{D}}=\beta_{1,i_{1}}^{(r)}\times\dots\times\beta_{D,i_{D}}^{(r)}
. The inner product of two tensor is written as
<\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}>=\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{B})
. Given a
D-
dimensional rank-
R
tensor
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\dots\times p_{D}}
, the CP tensor decomposition factorizes it into a sum of
R
rank-1 tensors:

\mathcal{B}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{(r)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{D}^{(r)},

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d}^{(r)}=(\beta^{(r)}_{d,1},\dots,\beta^{(r)}_{d,p_{d}})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{d}}
is a column vector for
d=1,\cdots,D
and
r=1,\cdots,R
. Let
\mathbf{B}_{d}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d}^{(R)}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{d}\times R}
for
d=1,\dots,D
, then the decomposition can be concisely expressed as
\mathcal{B}=\llbracket\mathbf{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{D}\rrbracket
for a shorthand
(
Kolda, 2006
)
. The operator
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket
establishes a connection between the
D
-dimensional tensor and these
D
matrices. Figure
1
gives an intuitive explanation of the CP decomposition in the 3-dimensional case.

2.2
The CP tensor regression model

Suppose there are
n
independent observations
\{(y_{i},\mathcal{X}_{i}),i=1,\dots,n\}
, where
\mathcal{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{D}}
is a
D
-dimensional tensor.
p_{1},\dots,p_{D}
are all finite numbers and independent of
n
. We assume that the response variable
y_{i}
belongs to an exponential family:

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(y_{i}|\theta_{i},\phi)=\mathrm{exp}\left\{\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b(\theta_{i})}{\phi}+c(y_{i},\phi)\right\},

(1)

where
b(\cdot)
and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
are known functions, and
\theta_{i}
and
\phi>0
denote the natural and dispersion parameters, respectively. Under the canonical link function,
\theta_{i}
relates the parameter
\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times\cdots\times p_{D}}
to the covariate
\mathcal{X}_{i}
in the form

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=<\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}_{i}>.

However, the number of free parameters,
\prod_{i=1}^{D}p_{i}
, is often very large. Therefore, directly using the maximum likelihood method to estimate the parameters in (
1
) will result in significant computational difficulties. If we know the rank of
\mathcal{B}
is
R
, we can utilize the CP tensor decomposition to reduce the parameters, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{(r)}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{\beta}_{D}^{(r)}=\llbracket\mathbf{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{D}\rrbracket,

where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{d}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{d}}
is a column vector for
d=1,\cdots,D
,
r=1,\cdots,R
, and
\mathbf{B}_{i}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}^{(R)}]
. Under the canonical link function, we can estimate the parameters
\mathcal{B}
via maximizing the loglikelihood function:

\begin{split}&\ell\left(\mathbf{B}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{B}_{D}\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}\theta_{i}-b\left(\theta_{i}\right)}{\phi}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c\left(y_{i},\phi\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{y_{i}<\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}_{i}>-b\left(<\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}_{i}>\right)}{\phi}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c\left(y_{i},\phi\right).\end{split}

(2)

Zhou et al., 2013
introduce a block relaxation algorithm to estimate the parameters, where
\mathbf{B}_{d}
is sequentially updated for
d=1,\dots,D
. To address the non-uniqueness issue in the solution, they incorporate some constraints to control the scale and permutation indeterminacy for ensuring the uniqueness of estimated parameters.

2.3
Model averaging estimation

As mentioned above, a non-negligible problem is that we do not know which
R
is the best since the algorithm assumes a given rank. Model averaging inspires us to average possible ranks rather than to just rely on one “good”
R
, which helps reduce the risk of choosing a “poor”
R
by mistake. Supposing we have
S
candidate models, the rank and the estimated parameter of the
s
th model are
r_{s}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)}=\sum_{r=1}^{r_{s}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}^{(r)}\circ\cdots\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{D}^{(r)}
, respectively.
S
is fixed and does not change with the sample size. Furthermore, we assume that the rank of the candidate models is sorted in ascending order, i.e.,
r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots<r_{S}
. For notational simplicity, we add
0
vectors to the low-rank decomposition so that all decompositions have the same matrix size. Then

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)}=\sum_{r=1}^{r_{s}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}^{(r)}\circ\cdots\circ\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{D}^{(r)}+\sum_{r=r_{s}+1}^{r_{S}}\boldsymbol{0}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{0}=\llbracket\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),D}\rrbracket,

where
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),d}=[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}^{(1)},\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{d}^{(r_{s})},\boldsymbol{0},\dots,\boldsymbol{0}]
for
d=1,\dots,D
. Let
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1}\cdots,w_{S})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{W}=\{\mathbf{w}\in[0,1]^{S}:\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}=1\}
be the weight vector, and
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{d}(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),d}
for
d=1,\dots,D
. From the perspective of model averaging, we define the model averaging estimator as the combination of several coefficient arrays with different ranks:

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)}=\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}\llbracket\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),1},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{(s),D}\rrbracket=\llbracket\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{1}(\mathbf{w}),\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{D}(\mathbf{w})\rrbracket.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}
be the true parameter for
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{y})
,
\theta_{0i}=<\mathcal{B}_{0},\mathcal{X}_{i}>
for
i=1,\dots,n
,
B_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b(\theta_{0i})
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=(\theta_{01},\cdots,\theta_{0n})^{\mathrm{T}}
. Furthermore, let
B\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b[\theta_{i}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}]
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}=(\theta_{1}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\},\cdots,\theta_{n}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\})^{\mathrm{T}}
. After obtaining the estimators
{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}}_{(1)},\dots,{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}}_{(S)}
, we get the
\mathrm{KL}
loss of
\boldsymbol{\theta}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}
:

\begin{split}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{w})&=2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}^{*}}\log\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\text{Pr}(y_{i}^{*}|\theta_{0i},\phi)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\text{Pr}[y_{i}^{*}|\theta_{i}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\},\phi]}\\
&=2\phi^{-1}B\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}B_{0}+2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\end{split}

(3)

where
y_{i}^{*}
is another realization from
\text{Pr}(\cdot|\theta_{i},\phi)
and independent of
y_{i}
. Note that the last two terms are not related to
\mathbf{w}
, so we just need to minimize
2\phi^{-1}B\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}
to obtain the optimal weights.
Considering
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is unknown, we tend to use
\mathbf{y}
to replace
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, but this may lead to overfitting. Hence, we utilize the
J-
fold cross-validation to relieve this trouble and set the criterion to calculate model-averaging weights. For simplicity of expression, we assume that
n/J
is an integer and
n_{0}=n/J
. We introduce additional notations for the weight selection criterion after introducing cross-validation. Let
\theta_{j,i}\{\mathcal{B}\}=\theta_{(j-1)n_{0}+i}\{\mathcal{B}\}
,
\mathcal{X}_{j,i}=\mathcal{X}_{(j-1)n_{0}+i}
,
y_{j,i}=y_{(j-1)n_{0}+i}
, and
\mathbf{y}^{(j)}=(y_{j,1},\dots,y_{j,n_{0}})^{\mathrm{T}}
be the elements in the
j
th fold, and
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(s)}
be the estimator of
\mathcal{B}
of the
s
th model without the
j
th fold. Then we have
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}w_{s}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(s)}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{[-j]}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\}=(\theta_{j,1}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\},\cdots,\theta_{j,n_{0}}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\})^{\mathrm{T}}
and
B^{[-j]}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}b[\theta_{j,i}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\}]
.

B\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}
in (
3
) change into

\widetilde{B}\{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}B^{[-j]}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}b[\theta_{j,i}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}(\mathbf{w})\}],

and

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{[-1]}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-1]}(\mathbf{w})\}^{\mathrm{T}},\dots,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{[-J]}\{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-J]}(\mathbf{w})\}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}.

Then the weight selection criterion
\mathrm{CV}_{J}(\mathbf{w})
is written as

\mathrm{CV}_{J}(\mathbf{w})=2\phi^{-1}\widetilde{B}\{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{T}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\{\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{w})\}.

In general, the selection of
J
is 5 or 10
(
Ando and Li, 2014
;
Zhang and Liu, 2023
;
Gao et al., 2023
)
.
Arlot and Lerasle, 2016
also argue that choosing
J
to be 5 or 10 is a good choice. In this article, we assume
J=O(1)
in the asymptotic theory part, and
J=5
in the simulations and real data experiments.
Finally, the resultant weight vector is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{w}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{CV}_{J}(\mathbf{w}).

(4)

Once the estimated weights are available, we can calculate the model averaging estimator, denoted by
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widehat{w}_{s}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)}
. We term the proposed method as
T
ensor
R
egression
M
odel
A
veraging (TRMA).

3
Asymptotic theory

3.1
Asymptotic optimality

It should be emphasized that the conclusions in this paper are based on the assumption that the number of parameters
\prod_{d=1}^{D}p_{d}
and the number of models
S
are fixed, and the sample size
n
tends to infinity. Before presenting the theorems, we first specify some regularity conditions.
Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathrm{vec}([{\mathbf{B}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{B}}_{D}])
,
\epsilon_{i}=y_{i}-\mu_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})^{\mathrm{T}}=\mathbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

Condition 1
.

For any
s=1,\dots,S
, there exists
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{(s)}=\llbracket\mathbf{B}^{*}_{(s),1},\dots,\mathbf{B}^{*}_{(s),D}\rrbracket
belonging to a compact space
\mathbb{B}
, such that

\left\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{B}^{*}_{(s)}}\right\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(5)

Besides,
\mathcal{B}^{*}_{(s)}
and the true value
\mathcal{B}_{0}
for the unknown parameter lie in the interior of the compact space
\mathbb{B}
.

Remark 1
.

\mathcal{B}^{*}_{(s)}
is the quasi-true parameter that minimizes
\mathrm{KL}
loss between the
s
th candidate model and the true model. The quasi-true parameter equals the true parameter if the working model is correctly specified.
Remark 5 in
Zhou et al., 2013
guarantees that the maximum likelihood estimator is the best rank-
R
approximation of
\mathcal{B}_{true}
in the sense of Kullback–Leibler distance. Given Condition
1
, it is easy to verify that

\left\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{(s)})-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{B}^{*}_{(s)})\right\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(6)

The difference between (
5
) and (
6
) is that (
5
) requires the consistency of the vectors after the CP decomposition, while (
6
) indicates the consistency of the whole tensor. Equation (
5
) is stronger than (
6
).

Condition 2
.

There is a constant
c_{0}
such that

\sup_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\|\frac{\partial\theta_{i}\{\mathcal{B}\}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{B}}}\right\|\leq c_{0}<\infty.

Remark 2
.

Condition
2
states that the gradients of the systematic part are well defined, which is similar to Condition (C1) in
Zhang et al., 2016
.

Condition 3
.

There exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\|^{2}}{n}\leq c_{1}<\infty

and

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left\|\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\right\|\leq c_{2}<\infty.

Condition 4
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
, there exist positive constants
c_{\min}
and
c_{\max}
independent of n such that

0<c_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\min}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\right\},

and

\lambda_{\max}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\right\}\leq c_{\max}<\infty,

where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
represent the minimum and maximum eigenvalue of a matrix, respectively.

Remark 3
.

Condition
3
places restrictions on the variability of covariates, which is similar to Condition 8 in
Ando and Li, 2014
and Conditions 2-3 in
Zou et al., 2022
. Condition
4
requires that the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of the matrix
1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}
are bounded, which is similar to Condition C.1 in
Zhao et al., 2020
. Condition
4
also implies that the matrix
1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}
is of full rank, which is a necessary condition to ensure the identifiability of the tensor regression.

Condition 5
.

(i) There exists a positive constant
c_{3}
such that for
k=1,2
,

\sup_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\left|b^{(k)}\left(\theta_{i}\{\mathcal{B}\}\right)\right|\leq c_{3}<\infty;

(ii) there is a positive constant
c_{4}
such that

\inf_{\mathcal{B}\in\mathbb{B}}\min_{1\leq i\leq n}b^{{}^{\prime\prime}}\left(\theta_{i}\{\mathcal{B}\}\right)\geq c_{4}>0;

Remark 4
.

Condition
5
regulates the behavior of function
b(\cdot)
. Common distributions such as the Poisson distribution, binary distribution and normal distribution all satisfy this condition. This condition is similar to Condition R2 in
Ando and Li, 2017
.

Let

\mathrm{KL}^{*}(\mathbf{w})=2\phi^{-1}B\{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}\{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(\mathbf{w})\}-2\phi^{-1}B_{0}+2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},

and
\xi_{n}=\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{KL}^{*}(\mathbf{w})
.

Condition 6
.

n\xi_{n}^{-2}=o(1)
.

Remark 5
.

Condition
6
requires
\xi_{n}
to grow faster than
n^{1/2}
, which means the candidate models are not too close to the true model. It rules out the case where the true model exists in the set of candidate models. In particular, if the
{s^{*}}
th model is the correct model and included in the set of candidate models, we have
\xi_{n}\leq\mathrm{KL}^{*}(\mathbf{w}_{s^{*}})=2\phi^{-1}(B\{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{(s^{*})}\}-B_{0})-2\phi^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\mathrm{T}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\{{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{(s^{*})}\}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})=0
, where
\mathbf{w}_{s^{*}}
is a weight vector in which the
s^{*}
element is 1 and other elements are 0. In this case,
\xi_{n}=0
and
n\xi_{n}^{-2}
will not converge to 0. This is also a common condition in model averaging articles, for example in
Ando and Li, 2014
,
Liu et al., 2020
and
Zhang et al., 2023
.

Theorem 1
.

If Conditions
1
-
6
hold, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}})}{\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbf{w})}\to 1

(7)

in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem
1
shows that the TRMA estimator given by (
4
) is asymptotically optimal in the sense of
\mathrm{KL}
divergence when all candidate models are misspecified. In other words, the weight
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
yields a KL loss that is asymptotically equivalent to that of the infeasible optimal weight. Model misspecification is the common case because real tensor data rarely have low-rank decompositions. Theorem
1
guarantees that our proposed method will not be worse than other methods as
n\to\infty
under model misspecification.

3.2
Consistency

Following
Zhang and Liu, 2019
, the true model refers to the just-fitted model. Models
1,\dots,s_{0}
are termed as the underfitted models and models
s_{0}+2,\dots,S
correspond to the overfitted models. Both just-fitted models and overfitted models are called correct models.

Zhou et al., 2013
prove that the estimated parameter of the true model
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\widehat{\mathcal{B}}}
converges to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
. In this article, we do not require the decomposed parameter to converge to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}
, but are more concerned with the consistency of the original tensor. That is, we focus on whether the model averaging estimator converges to
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. The following theorem gives the answer.

Theorem 2
.

If there exist correct models in the set of candidate models and Conditions
1
-
5
hold, then

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}))-\mathrm{vec}(\mathcal{B}_{0})\right\|=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

(8)

Theorem
2
states that the TRMA estimator converges to the true parameter at the rate of
\sqrt{n}
when the correct models are contained in the candidate model set. This theorem can be viewed as a supplement of Theorem
1
, which assumes all candidate models are misspecified.

3.3
Weights of misclassified models

Now we focus on the properties of weights when the correct models exist. Let

\Delta_{j}=\left\{\left(\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(1)})-\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(s_{0}+1)})\right),\ldots,\left(\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(s_{0})})-\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{[-j]}_{(s_{0}+1)})\right)\right\}^{\mathrm{T}},

and
\widehat{w}_{\Delta}=\sum_{s=1}^{s_{0}}\widehat{w}_{s}
be the sum of the optimal weights assigned to underfitted models.

Condition 7
.

For sufficiently large
n
, there exists a positive constant
c_{0}
such that

\min_{1\leq j\leq J}\lambda_{\min}\left({\Delta_{j}}{\Delta_{j}}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)\geq c_{0}

holds almost surely.

Remark 6
.

Condition
7
is similar to Assumption 5 in
Liu and Zhang, 2022
. This condition requires that there is a certain gap between the parameters of the underfitted models and those of the true model.

Theorem 3
.

If Conditions
1
-
5
and
7
hold and there exist correct models in the set of candidate models, we have

\widehat{w}_{\Delta}=o_{p}(1).

Theorem
3
means that the weights assigned to the underfitted models converge to zero as
n\to\infty
. In other words, the TRMA estimator ignores the “wrong” models and is determined only by the correct models. The three theorems take into account both misspecified and correctly specified frameworks, and in each case, our proposed method demonstrates a certain degree of superiority.

4.3
Real data examples

In this section, we apply the TRMA method and other methods to analyse two datasets. The first one is the skin cancer dataset (
https://www.isic-archive.com/
). Moles are types of skin growths or lesions, and most of them are benign and harmless. Environmental factors such as prolonged sun exposure, or changes in hormone levels such as puberty, may cause moles to darken or lesion. In this case, the benign mole can develop into melanoma, a serious skin cancer. The data contain 1800 photos of benign skin moles and 1497 photos of malignant skin moles
(
ISIC, 2022
)
. The purpose of this experiment is to distinguish whether the moles are benign or malignant. We downsize the origin photos to a size of
64\times 64\times 3
for the convenience of calculation, and use each image as the independent variable
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. The response variable
y_{i}
is binary, with 1 representing the malignant mole and 0 representing the benign mole. The set of candidate models consists of rank-1 to rank-5 CP binary tensor regression models. We then apply various model averaging and model selection methods to these five models. We conduct three sets of experiments, randomly choosing 50%, 75%, and 90% of the whole data as the training set to estimate the parameters, and the remaining as the testing set to compare the misclassification rates of different models. To avoid errors caused by the number of training samples and uneven data partitioning, we repeat the experiment 100 times. Figure

presents the boxplot of the misclassification rates on the testing dataset over the 100 replications.

The second dataset is the attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) data (
http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/adhd200/
). ADHD is one of the most common neurodevelopmental disorders of childhood. Common ADHD symptoms include inattention, poor impulse control, and emotional hyperactivity. The data are obtained from the ADHD-200 Global Competition datasets

. The response variable

5
Discussion

In this article, we propose a model averaging method to avoid selecting a single poor model for the CP tensor regression by weighting estimators of different models. The weights are determined by minimizing a CV-type
\mathrm{KL}
divergence. We prove that the proposed estimator is asymptotically optimal when all candidate models are misspecified. When at least one candidate model is correctly specified, we also prove the TRMA estimator is root
-n
consistent and the weights of the model averaging estimator are assigned to the correct models. The proposed method is demonstrated to be efficient and stable through both simulations and real data examples.

However, there are still some areas worthy of further research. First, while our method is based on the CP tensor regression, other tensor decomposition methods such as Tucker decomposition and tensor train decomposition also have their advantages. Combining model averaging with other tensor regression methods will be a promising avenue for future research. Second, the root
-n
consistency of the model averaging estimator requires the number of parameters to be fixed. In practice, especially in clinical data, the sample sizes are relatively small. It is a challenging question whether the statistic has the same property after relaxing this restriction. Finally, the article only considers the case where the data are independent and identically distributed. For longitudinal data or time series data, the conclusion may be different, and this is a field that requires a further investigation.
