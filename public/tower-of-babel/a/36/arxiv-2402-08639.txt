Abstract
. Let Y ⊆ ℝ n Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} be a closed definable subset and X ⊆ ℝ n X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} be a smooth manifold. We construct a version of Morse Theory for the restriction to X X of the Euclidean distance function from Y Y . This is done using the notion of critical points of Lipschitz functions and applying the theory of continuous selections. In this theory, nondegenerate critical points have two indices: a quadratic index (as in classical Morse Theory), and a piecewise linear index (that relates to the notion of bottlenecks). This framework is flexible enough to simultaneously treat and unify the study of two cases of interest for computational algebraic geometry: bottlenecks and nearest point problems. We provide a technical toolset guaranteeing the applicability of the theory to the case where X , Y X,Y are generic algebraic hypersurfaces and use it to bound the number of critical points of the distance from Y Y restricted to X X , among other applications.

A Morse Theory for distance functions

Distance functions have been the object of extensive study over the years, classically in differential geometry

Various interesting properties of geometric objects in

Studying critical points of functions has many applications ranging from data analysis tasks such as the characterization of the texture of porous materials in

1.3.
Nondegenerate critical points

In the definable framework one can even prove that the cohomology of
X_{Y}^{t}
changes in a controlled manner when passing a critical value (
Proposition

2.4.1
) but, as in the classical theory, in order to be able to say more, one needs to introduce the notion of
nondegenerate
critical points. This is done using the theory of
continuous selections
as follows.

Following
[
APS97
]
, we say that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous selection of the finite list
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions if
f
is continuous and if for every
x\in X
there exists
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
such that
f(x)=f_{i}(x)
. In this case we write
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
. For
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
, we denote by
S_{i}\subseteq X
the set

(6)

S_{i}\coloneqq\left\{x\in X\,\bigg|\,f(x)=f_{i}(x)\right\}.

Given
x\in X
, the
effective index set
of
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
at
x\in X
is defined as

(7)

I(x)\coloneqq\left\{i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\,\bigg|\,x\in\overline{\operatorname{int}(S_{i})}\right\}.

Note that continuous selections are locally Lipschitz, so we can talk about their critical points using the language introduced above. We can think of these functions as “piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions”.

Now comes the crucial definition.

Definition 1.3.1

(Nondegenerate critical points,
Definition

2.5.2
)
.

For
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
, a critical point
x\in C(f)
is said to be
nondegenerate
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For every
i\in I(x)
the set of differentials
\{D_{x}f_{j}\mid j\in I(x)\setminus\{i\}\}
is linearly independent.

(ii)

Denote by
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I(x)}
the unique list of numbers such that

(8)

\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}=1\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}D_{x}f_{i}=0

and by
\lambda f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
the function defined by
\lambda f(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(z).
Then the second differential of
\lambda f
is nondegenerate on

(9)

V(x)\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in I(x)}\ker(D_{x}f_{i}).

When
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0})
,
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
and the above definition reduces to the usual definition of nondegenerate critical point, in the sense of Morse Theory.

Compared to what happens with classical Morse functions, we see that a nondegenerate critical point of a continuous selection in general comes with two indices:

-

the number
k(x)\coloneqq\#I(x)-1
, which we call the
piecewise linear index
;

-

the number
\iota(x)
of negative eigenvalues of the restriction of the second differential of
\lambda f
to
V(x)
, which we call the
quadratic index
.

Continuous selections have normal forms near their nondegenerate critical points, in a fashion similar to the Morse Lemma
[
Mil63
, Lemma 2.2]
, see
Remark

2.5.4

f
the cohomology of the sublevel sets
\{f\leq t\}
changes in a controlled way when passing a critical value with only nondegenerate critical points (see
[
APS97
]
and
Proposition

2.5.5
), and this change can be bounded by the two numbers
k
and
\iota
only. For instance, in our context, we have the following result.

Proposition 1.3.2

(
Corollary

2.6.4
)
.

If
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a continuous selection at
x\in X
and
x
is a nondegenerate critical point for it such that
C({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}})\cap({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}})^{-1}(c)=\{x\}
, then for all
\epsilon>0
small enough,

(10)

H^{*}(X^{c+\varepsilon}_{Y},X^{c-\varepsilon}_{Y})\cong\widetilde{H}^{*}(S^{k(x)+\iota(x)}).

1.4.
Critical points and bottlenecks

In the statement of
Proposition

1.3.2
, the assumption that
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a continuous selection is essential. Indeed, in general, distance functions might not fall in this class, even under seemingly nice assumptions, see

Note that, due to the description (

In this direction, Yomdin proved in

that this is true for a generic embedding

, when

is a compact smooth manifold. Here we prove the following result, which is an analogue of Yomdin’s result in the algebraic framework (but does not follow from it).
For

Theorem 1.4.1

(
Theorem

)

For generic

Now let

be an algebraic set. Following

Under the assumption that

1.5.
Related works on Morse theory of distance functions

Prior to our work there have been several contributions to build a framework for the Morse Theory of distance functions: most notably
[
Che91
]
, that deals with distance–from–a point functions on Riemannian manifolds, and
[
BA14
]
, which treats the case of point clouds.

We note that, in
[
BA14
]
, the piecewise linear index
k
is identified as an analogue of the classical Morse index in the case of distance functions from a finite set of points
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}\}
, and in
[
DREE+23
, Remark 4.2]
it is compared to the order of the geometric bottlenecks in the context of more general distance functions (with a shift in index). However, as we explained above, in the general case the piecewise linear index
k
is not the correct analogue of the classical Morse index. It is true that, for the distance from a finite set of points, the piecewise linear index correctly describes the changes in topology, but this is because the quadratic index in this case is always zero. In general, as demonstrated by
Proposition

1.3.2
, changes in topology take into account both the piecewise linear index and the quadratic index.

More recently, the authors of
[
SYM23
]
developed the Morse Theory of signed distance functions from a hypersurface in Euclidean space. Via an argument based on transversality theory, they prove that, for a generic embedding of a smooth compact surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the associated signed distance function has finitely many critical points that are all nondegenerate.
The Morse theory for distance functions we present in Section
2
is similar to the treatment in
[
SYM23
, Sect. 3]
. The main difference between the two approaches is that the authors of
[
SYM23
]
build on the theory of Min-type functions
[
GR97
]
which, like continuous selections, admit normal forms at nondegenerate critical points.
For applications to Euclidean distance functions, the generalized Morse theory built on the theory of Min-type functions is equivalent to that built on continuous selections, as we observe in
Remark

2.5.6
.

1.6.
The Bottleneck Degree, the Euclidean Distance Degree and related notions

Let us go back to the study of geometric
(k+1)
-bottlenecks in details. As we have seen, they are in particular critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
with at least
k+1
closest points in
Y
; in the nondegenerate case, this means critical points with piecewise linear index
k
. When
X
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Y
is an algebraic hypersurface, the above result can still be applied (see
Remark

4.6.7
and Remark
1.4.4
), and the bound (
12
) becomes

(14)

\#C_{k,*}(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\leq c(k,n)(\deg(Y))^{n(k+1)}.

When
k=1
, geometric
2
-bottlenecks are simply called “bottlenecks”. These bottlenecks are easier to study, because of the structure of their equations (roughly speaking, they do not involve quantifier elimination). The
Bottleneck Degree
of a smooth variety
Y
defined over
\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
\mathrm{BND}(Y)
, was defined in
[
DREW20
]
as the number of complex solutions of this system of equations for generic
Y
.
The motivation for the study in
[
DREW20
]
was to control the number of bottlenecks of (the real part of) a real variety
Y
, and this can be done passing to the complexification and using the bound

(15)

\#C_{1,*}(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\leq\mathrm{BND}(Y).

In
[
DREW20
]
, the authors compute various bottleneck degrees (planar and space curves, space surfaces, and threefolds) and deduce a bound that, for these examples, is of the form
\#C_{1,*}(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\leq\widetilde{c}(1,n)d^{2n}.
In this sense, our bound (
12
) generalizes this for all dimensions and for bottlenecks of all orders.

A similar discussion holds for the notion of
Euclidean Distance Degree
of a smooth real algebraic variety
X
, denoted by
\mathrm{EDD}(X)
. This was defined in
[
DHO+16
]
as the number of complex solutions of the equations describing the critical points of the distance function from a generic point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

If one is only interested in the real solutions to the critical points equations, using our language, one could define the following notion. For generic pairs
(X,Y)
of real algebraic sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
Real Euclidean Distance Degree of
X
relative to
Y
as the number

(16)

\mathrm{EDD}_{\mathbb{R}}(X,Y)\coloneqq\#C({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}).

In the special cases
(X,Y)=(\mathbb{R}^{n},Y)
and
(X,Y)=(X,\{y\})
, one could refine this definition and obtain the quantities

(17)

\mathrm{BND}_{\mathbb{R}}(Y)\coloneqq\mathrm{EDD}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{n},Y)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathrm{EDD}_{\mathbb{R}}(X)\coloneqq\mathrm{EDD}_{\mathbb{R}}(X,\{y\}).

In the generic case, as we have seen, both these numbers are actually the number of critical points of an appropriate function (the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
) and can be bounded by (
12
).

Note that, unlike analogue complex notions, this number depends on the pair
(X,Y)
.
It is clear from this discussion, for instance, that
\mathrm{EDD}_{\mathbb{R}}(X)\leq\mathrm{EDD}(X).

However, we leave the study of this notion of “relative EDD” from the complex point of view to future investigations. The definition of the complex solutions of the system defining our critical points requires some care, because convexity is used, but the constructions from
Section

4
also work over the complex numbers.

A natural question for the experts is: after it is defined, how to compute the (complex) Euclidean Distance Degree of
X
relative to
Y
using polar classes?

1.7.
Tools for genericity

Our proof uses
real techniques
as opposed to, for example, the techniques from
[
DREW20
]
, which come from complex algebraic geometry and use the notion of polar classes. However, the technical results from the current paper (in particular, those from
Section

4.1
) extend easily to the complex world, with the same proofs. In fact, we propose these techniques as easy-to-use substitute tools to verify that codimensions are the expected ones in the generic case. For example, the proof of finiteness of the number of
(k+1)
-bottlenecks for non-quadratic complete intersections from
[
DREE+23
, Theorem 4.14]
uses the Alexander-Hirschowitz Theorem, that here is substituted with the parametric multijet theorem (
Theorem

4.1.4
).

2.
Morse theory for distance functions

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed definable subset and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
a smooth manifold. In this section we will explain how to construct a version of Morse theory for the function

(18)

{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}\colon X\to\mathbb{R},

where
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(z)\coloneqq\min_{y\in Y}\|y-z\|
and
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
denotes the restriction of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
to
X
.
Except for some special cases (e.g. when
Y=\{y\}
is a point which is not in
X
), the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is not differentiable and not convex. To formulate basic statements about the change of the topology of its sublevel sets, one needs to introduce first appropriate notions of critical points and critical values.
This will be done, first for the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
and then for
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
, following a general theory for locally Lipschitz functions.

2.1.
Critical points

An appropriate notion of critical points for
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is introduced as follows. First we observe that
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is Lipschitz, and therefore differentiable almost everywhere by Rademacher’s Theorem. Following Clarke
[
Cla90
]
, one can therefore define the
subdifferential
of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.1.1

(Subdifferential)
.

Let
(X,g)
be a Riemannian manifold and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}

be a locally Lipschitz function. (In the framework we have in mind
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a submanifold with the induced Riemannian metric from the ambient Euclidean space.) We denote by
\Omega(f)\subseteq X
the set of differentiability points of
f
(of full measure by Rademacher’s Theorem). The
subdifferential of
f
at
x\in X
, denoted by
\partial_{x}f
, is the convex body defined by

(19)

\partial_{x}f\coloneqq\mathrm{co}\left\{\left.\lim_{x_{k}\to x,x_{k}\in\Omega(f)}\nabla f(x_{k})\;\right|\;\textnormal{the limit exists}\right\}\subset T_{x}X.

Note that at a strictly differentiable point, the subdifferential reduces to a singleton
\partial_{x}f=\{\nabla f(x)\}
.

Describing the subdifferential of a Lipschitz function can be quite tricky in general, but in the special case
f=\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
(i.e.
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
), it can be done as follows.

Proposition 2.1.2
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Y
. Then

(20)

\partial_{x}(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})=\mathrm{co}\left\{\left.\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\,\right|\,y\in B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y\right\}.

Even if
Proposition

2.1.2
is a well-known result, we include a proof for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

Let
\eta_{Y}
denote the function defined by
\eta_{Y}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)^{2}
.
Explicitly,

(21)

\eta_{Y}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\min_{y\in Y}\|y-x\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{y\in Y}\langle y-x,y-x\rangle=\min_{y\in Y}\left(\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}-\langle y,x\rangle+\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right).

Using the equality
\min_{y\in Y}g=-\max_{y\in Y}(-g)
valid for any real-valued function
g
defined on
Y
, we obtain

(22)

-\eta_{Y}(x)=\max_{y\in Y}\left(-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}+\langle y,x\rangle-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)=\max_{y\in Y}\left(-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}+\langle y,x\rangle\right)-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}.

Writing
\phi(x,y)\coloneqq-\frac{\|y\|^{2}}{2}+\langle y,x\rangle
, we observe that the functions
\phi(-,y)\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are convex and differentiable for all
y\in Y
. We then apply Danskin’s theorem (see e.g.
[
Ber99
, Prop. B.25]
) to the function
h(x)\coloneqq\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-\eta_{Y}(x)=\max_{y\in Y}\phi(x,y)
. Letting

(23)

Y_{0}(x)\coloneqq\left\{y_{0}\in Y\;\left|\;\phi(x,y_{0})=\max_{y\in Y}\phi(x,y)\right.\right\}=\left\{y_{0}\in Y\;\left|\;\frac{1}{2}\|y_{0}-x\|^{2}=\eta_{Y}(x)\right.\right\}=B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y,

where the second equality holds by equation (
22
),
then

(24)

\partial_{x}h=\mathrm{co}\left\{\nabla_{x}\phi(x,y)\mid y\in Y_{0}(x)\right\}=\mathrm{co}\left\{y\mid y\in B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y\right\}.

Since
\eta_{Y}(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}-h(x)
and the function
\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}
is differentiable with
\nabla\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}=x
, by definition of the subdifferential we obtain

(25)

\partial_{x}(\eta_{Y})=\mathrm{co}\left\{x-y\mid y\in B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y\right\}.

To complete the proof, we note that the distance function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is differentiable at a point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Y
if and only if
\eta_{Y}
is, and that the gradients of these two functions are parallel, with
\nabla\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(z)=\nabla\eta_{Y}(z)/\|\nabla\eta_{Y}(z)\|
.
∎

Definition 2.1.3

(Critical points)
.

We say that
x\in X
is a
critical point
of a locally Lipschitz function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
if
0
belongs to
\partial_{x}f
. We denote by
C(f)\subseteq X
the set of critical points of
f
.

A point
x\in X
is said to be a
regular point
of
f
if it is not critical; a
critical value
of
f
is a real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f^{-1}(c)\cap C(f)\neq\emptyset
and a
regular value
of
f
is a real number
c\in\mathbb{R}
which is not critical.

Remark 2.1.4
.

For example, local maxima and local minima are critical points of locally Lipschitz functions
[
RW09
]
.

Definition 2.1.5

(Medial axis)
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed set. We introduce the function
m_{Y}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, defined for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

(26)

m_{Y}(z)\coloneqq\#\left(B(z,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(z))\cap Y\right),

and the set
M_{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, called the
medial axis
of
Y
, defined by

(27)

M_{Y}\coloneqq\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\bigg|\,m_{Y}(z)\geq 2\right\}.

Remark 2.1.6
.

[
Yom81
, Prop. 3]
proves that
m_{Y}
is finite if
Y=i(W)
is the image of a compact smooth manifold
W
by a generic embedding
i\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. An analogue result in the algebraic setting is shown by
Theorem

4.3.1
.

Lemma 2.1.7
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed definable set. The set
M_{Y}
is also definable, of codimension at least one, and the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}})
.

Proof.

The fact that
M_{Y}
is definable is obvious. The fact that it has codimension at least one follows from the strict convexity of the norm and the fact that
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}}))
is the content of
[
Fed59
, Theorem 4.8(5)]
.
∎

Example 2.1.8

(Critical points of the distance function from a finite set)
.

Let
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}\}
be a finite set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The work of Bobrowski and
Adler
[
BA14
]
explains the Morse theory for the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
; it turns out that this is a special case of the Morse Theory from this paper.

To each
y_{i}\in Y
one can associate the
Voronoi cell

V(y_{i})\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|x-y_{i}\|\leq\|x-y_{j}\|,\forall y_{j}\in Y\}
. Voronoi cells are convex polyhedra, and any two different Voronoi cells have disjoint interiors. The collection of Voronoi cells and their faces constitute a polyhedral complex
[
Zie12
]
called the
Voronoi diagram
of
Y
, see e.g.
[
BCY18
]
, which covers the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the interior of a Voronoi cell
V(y_{i})
, the distance function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
coincides with
\mathrm{dist}_{\{y_{i}\}}
and its only critical point is
y_{i}
. The medial axis
M_{Y}
is union of the boundaries of all Voronoi cells, and it contains all remaining critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
(cf. Lemma
2.1.7
).
If no subset of
n+2
points of
Y
lie on a common hypersphere, then for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the value
m_{Y}(x)
coincides with
n+1
minus the lowest dimension of a Voronoi face containing
x
.

A set
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is in
general position
if every subset of up to
n+1
points of
Y
is affinely independent.
If
Y
is in general position, then one can find the critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
via the following procedure: for each subset
Y^{\prime}
of
k+1
points in
Y
, with
k\leq n
, let
x
denote the center of the unique
(k-1)
-sphere containing
Y^{\prime}
and check whether

(i)

x\in\mathrm{co}(Y^{\prime})
, and

(ii)

there is no point
y\in Y\setminus Y^{\prime}
such that
\|x-y\|<\mathrm{dist}_{Y^{\prime}}(x)
.

The point
x
is critical if, and only if, the conditions (i) and (ii) are verified. Under the general position assumption on
Y
, the procedure we described gives an upper bound of
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{m}{k+1}
on the number of critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
.

(We continue the discussion of this example below, after we have introduced the notion of nondegenerate critical points, see
Example

2.6.10
.)

In order to get a neat analogue of
Proposition

2.1.2
for the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
in the case when
X
is a proper submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we need to impose some extra conditions on the pair
(X,Y)
. We introduce first the following definition.

Definition 2.1.9

(Generalized transversality)
.

We will say that a smooth manifold
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
transverse
to a definable set
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists a Nash stratification of
M
such that
X
is transverse to all the strata of this stratification. In this case we write
X\pitchfork M
, as in the case of usual transversality.

Proposition 2.1.10
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be closed and definable and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth manifold transverse to both
Y
and
\overline{M_{Y}}
. Then for every
x\in X
,

(28)

\partial_{x}({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}})=\mathrm{proj}_{T_{x}X}\left(\partial_{x}(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\right),

where
\mathrm{proj}_{T_{x}X}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to T_{x}X
denotes the orthogonal projection. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

Proof.

Let
g\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
and
f\coloneqq{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
. We derive the equality as follows:

(29)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}f

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{co}\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\nabla f(x_{k})\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(f),\textnormal{ limit exists}\}

(30)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{co}\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\nabla f(x_{k})\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(g)\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\}

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{co}\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x_{k}}X}(\nabla g(x_{k}))\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(g)\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\}

(32)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{co}\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x}X}(\nabla g(x_{k}))\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(g)\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\}

(33)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x}X}(\mathrm{co}\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\nabla g(x_{k})\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(g)\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\})

(34)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x}X}(\partial_{x}g).

First, if
g
is differentiable at
x\in X
, then so is
f
, and
\nabla f(x)=\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x}X}(\nabla g(x))
: this gives (
31
). Next, by
Lemma

2.1.7
,
\Omega(g)=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}})
. In particular,
\Omega(g)\cap X=(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}}))\cap X\subseteq\Omega(f)
. Moreover, since
X
is transverse to
Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}}
, the set
X\cap(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}})
is of codimension at least
1
in
X
. Thus, for any point in
\Omega(f)
, we can approach it by points in
\Omega(g)\cap X
: this gives (
30
). (
32
) follows from the continuity of the map
(x,v)\mapsto\textnormal{proj}_{T_{x}X}(v)
and (
33
) follows from the linearity of the map
\operatorname{proj}_{T_{x}X}
.

Finally, let
U_{x}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a neighborhood of
x
, and decompose
U_{x}\setminus(Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}})
into the disjoint union of connected components
\coprod_{i=1}^{s}A_{i}
. By
Lemma

2.1.7
,
g|_{A_{i}}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, and so we can define

(35)

V_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{co}\left\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x,x_{k}\in A_{i}}\nabla g(x_{k})\right\}

for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
Moreover, if we choose
U_{x}
small enough, we have
\partial_{x}g=\mathrm{co}\{V_{i}\}_{i}
.

Since
X
is transverse to
Y\cup\overline{M_{Y}}
, for all
i
,
A_{i}\cap X
is nonempty and there exists a sequence
(x_{k})_{k}
in
A_{i}\cap X
converging to
x
. Thus

(36)

\displaystyle\mathrm{co}\left\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\nabla g(x_{k})\mid x_{k}\in\Omega(g)\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{co}\left\{\lim_{x_{k}\to x}\nabla g(x_{k})\mid x_{k}\in\coprod_{i=1}^{s}A_{i}\cap X,\textnormal{ limit exists}\right\}

(37)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{co}\{V_{i}\}_{i}

(38)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}g,

which gives (
34
).
∎

From

2.2.
The Sard property

If
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is smooth, the fact that the set of critical values of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
has zero measure follows from a result of Rifford
[
Rif04
]
, which generalizes the Morse-Sard theorem for smooth functions to the case of distance functions. In the definable context we can actually use the following result.

Proposition 2.2.1

(
[
BDLS05
, Theorem 7]
)
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz and definable function. Then
f
is constant on each component of the set of its critical points and therefore the set of critical values of
f
is finite. In particular, if
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are definable sets with
X
smooth, the set of critical values of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is finite.

Remark 2.2.2
.

The requirement that
Y
is a definable set seems necessary to avoid pathological examples. For instance, as stated in
[
Rif04
, Remark 1.1]
, while the set of all critical values of the distance function from a compact subset in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is of measure zero
[
Fu85
]
, counterexamples exist in higher dimensions
[
Fer76
, Example 3]
(see also
[
Whi35
]
).

2.3.
The deformation lemma

Given
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
X^{t}\coloneqq\{x\in X\mid f(x)\leq t\}
and in the special case
f={\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
we set

(40)

X_{Y}^{t}\coloneqq\left\{x\in X\,\bigg|\,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)\leq t\right\}.

We are interested in understanding how the topology changes letting
t\in\mathbb{R}
vary. This can be measured by the relative cohomology groups
H^{*}(X_{Y}^{t+\varepsilon},X_{Y}^{t-\varepsilon})
, for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. In this article, cohomology groups have coefficients in an arbitrary fixed field.
If
\{c_{0},\ldots,c_{k}\}
are the critical values of the distance function, then no topology change happens for the set
X_{Y}^{a}
when
a\in(c_{j},c_{j+1})
. This is a consequence of the deformation lemma for locally Lipschitz functions.

Lemma 2.3.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a proper, locally Lipschitz function. Assume that the interval
[a,b]
contains no critical values of
f
. Then
X^{b}
deformation retracts to
X^{a}
.
Moreover, if
f
is definable, then
X^{b}
deformation retracts to
X^{a}
even if
a
is a critical value, as soon as there are no other critical values in
(a,b]
.

Proof.

The first part of the statement follows from Clarke’s Implicit Function Theorem
[
Cla76
, Theorem 1]
, see
[
APS97
, Proposition 1.2]
. The second part follows from the first part and the definable triviality
[
vdD98
, Chapter 9, Theorem 1.2]
.
∎

2.4.
Passing a critical value: general case

Observe first that, if
X,Y
are definable with
X
smooth, when
t\in\mathbb{R}
passes a critical value, the topology of
X_{Y}^{t}
changes in a controlled way. This follows again from the definable triviality and is an application of the next result.

Proposition 2.4.1
.

Let
X,Y
be definable with
X
smooth and let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a Lipschitz, definable function. For every critical value
c\in\mathbb{R}
there exists a definable set
K(c)
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough

(41)

H^{*}(X_{Y}^{c+\varepsilon},X_{Y}^{c-\varepsilon})\cong\widetilde{H}^{*}(K(c)).

Proof.

This is clear, since for
\varepsilon>0
small enough all the pairs
(X_{Y}^{c+\varepsilon},X_{Y}^{c-\varepsilon})
are homotopy equivalent (by definable triviality) and therefore one can define
K(c)\coloneqq X_{Y}^{c+\varepsilon}/X_{Y}^{c-\varepsilon}
. Since definable functions can be triangulated, then up to a definable homeomorphism,
X_{Y}^{c-\varepsilon}
is a subcomplex of
X_{Y}^{c+\varepsilon}
and therefore
K(c)
is definable.
∎

Remark 2.4.2
.

More generally, an analogue of the previous proposition still holds true for a proper definable function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
(without the assumption that it is locally Lipschitz).
In fact, by definable triviality
[
vdD98
, Chapter 9, Theorem 1.2]
, one can partition
\mathbb{R}
into finitely many points and intervals such that on each interval
f
is a trivial fibration. The sublevel sets
X^{t}
of
f
can only change when we pass the points – we don’t call these critical points (because
f
might not be locally Lipschitz), but the relative cohomology is still that of a definable set.

2.5.
Passing a critical value: nondegenerate case

In order to say something more on the change in topology of
X_{Y}^{t}
when
t
passes a critical value, in a way that is similar to Morse theory, we need to introduce the notion of
nondegenerate
critical point.
This notion can be introduced at least for a special class of locally Lipschitz functions called
continuous selections
.

Definition 2.5.1

(Continuous selection)
.

Let
X
be a smooth manifold and
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{C}^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})
. We will say that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a
continuous selection
from
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}
if
f
is continuous and for every
x\in X
there exists
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
such that
f(x)=f_{i}(x).

In this case we will write
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
.

If
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
, for
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
we denote by
S_{i}\subseteq X
the set

(42)

S_{i}\coloneqq\left\{x\in X\,\bigg|\,f(x)=f_{i}(x)\right\}.

Given
x\in X
the
effective index set
of
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
at
x\in X
is defined by

(43)

I(x)\coloneqq\left\{i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}\,\bigg|\,x\in\overline{\operatorname{int}(S_{i})}\right\}.

Note that continuous selections are locally Lipschitz
[
APS97
]
, so we can talk about their critical points using the definitions above. Again, below we assume that
X
is endowed with some Riemannian structure, so as to identify its tangent and cotangent bundles.

Definition 2.5.2

(Nondegenerate critical points of continuous selections)
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
. A critical point
x\in C(f)
is said to be
nondegenerate
(with respect to
f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}
) if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

For every
i\in I(x)
the set of differentials
\{D_{x}f_{j}\mid j\in I(x)\setminus\{i\}\}
is linearly independent.

(2)

Denote by
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I(x)}
the unique list of nonnegative real numbers such that

(44)

\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}=1\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}D_{x}f_{i}=0

and by
\lambda f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
the function defined by
\lambda f(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(z).
Then the second differential of
\lambda f
is nondegenerate on

(45)

V(x)\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in I(x)}\ker(D_{x}f_{i}).

We denote by
k(x)\coloneqq\#I(x)-1
and call this number the
piecewise linear index
, and by
\iota(x)
the negative inertia index of the restriction of the second differential of
\lambda f
to
V(x)
. We call
\iota(x)
the
quadratic index
. See Figure
3
for an illustration of nondegenerate and degenerate critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
, and Figure
4
for an illustration of different indices for
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
.

Remark 2.5.3
.

An important remark concerns the reformulation of these conditions in the case
X
is a submanifold of another manifold
N
and we are considering the restriction of a continuous selection
f=g|_{X}
where
g\in\mathrm{CS}(g_{0},\ldots,g_{m})
with
g_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(N,\mathbb{R})
, so that

f_{j}=g_{j}|_{X},\quad j=0,\ldots,m.

Suppose that
X
is given as the zero set of a regular equation
p=0
, with
p:N\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth function (in
Section

4
we are interested in the case
N=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p
is a polynomial). Then, for all
j=0,\ldots,m
,

D_{x}f_{j}=D_{x}g_{j}|_{T_{x}X}=D_{x}g_{j}|_{\ker(D_{x}p)}.

Rewriting the above conditions with this notation, we see that
x\in X
is a nondegenerate critical point of
f=g|_{X}
if and only if

(1)

for every
i\in I(x)
the set of differentials
\{D_{x}g_{j}|_{\ker(D_{x}p)}\mid j\in I(x)\setminus\{i\}\}
is linearly independent;

(2)

denote by
\{\mu,(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I(x)}\}
the unique list of numbers such that the
\lambda_{i}
are nonnegative,
\mu\neq 0
,

(46)

\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}=1,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mu D_{x}p+\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}D_{x}g_{i}=0,

and by
\lambda f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
the function defined by
\lambda f(z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}f_{i}(z).
Then the second differential of
\lambda f
is nondegenerate on

(47)

V(x)\coloneqq\bigcap_{i\in I(x)}\ker(D_{x}f_{i}|_{\ker(D_{x}p)})=\ker(D_{x}p)\cap\left(\bigcap_{i\in I(x)}\ker(D_{x}f_{i})\right).

In order to compute the second differential of
\lambda f
on
V(x)
using the original functions, we proceed as follows. We consider the function

(48)

\widetilde{\lambda g}\coloneqq\mu p+\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}g_{i}\colon N\to\mathbb{R}.

Observe that, since
X=\{p=0\}
, then
\widetilde{\lambda g}|_{X}=\lambda f.
Moreover,
\widetilde{\lambda g}
has a critical point at
x\in N
and therefore the second differential of its restriction to
X
equals the restriction of its second differential to
T_{x}X
. This means that the condition that the second differential of
\lambda f
is nondegenerate on
V(x)
is equivalent to the condition that

\mu D_{x}^{2}p+\sum_{i\in I(x)}\lambda_{i}D_{x}^{2}g_{i}\quad\textrm{is nondegenerate on $V(x)$}.

Remark 2.5.4
.

Continuous selections have normal forms at their critical points, in a fashion similar to the Morse Lemma
[
Mil63
, Lemma 2.2]
. More precisely, if
x
is a nondegenerate critical point of
f\in\mathrm{CS}(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
with piecewise linear index
k=k(x)
and quadratic index
\iota=\iota(x)
, then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
and a locally Lipschitz homeomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\to U
such that

(49)

f(\psi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k},t_{k+1},\ldots,t_{n}))=f(x)+\ell(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})-\sum_{j=k+1}^{k+\iota}t_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=k+\iota+1}^{n}t_{j}^{2},

where
\ell(t)\in\mathrm{CS}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k},-(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}))
, see
[
JP88
, Theorem 3.3]
. Note in particular that nondegenerate critical points of continuous selections are isolated.

In the special case of a continuous selection of the form

f=\min\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\},

if
x
is a nondegenerate critical point with indices
k=k(x)
and
\iota=\iota(x)
, then
the piecewise linear continuous selection
\ell
in the normal form (
49
) is
\ell(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\min\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k},-(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{k})\}
, see
[
JP88
, Theorem 3.3]
.
In particular, in this case, if
f
is also definable, then by
[
APS97
, Sect. 4]
it follows the set
K(c)
defined in
Proposition

2.4.1
depends only on
k
and
\iota
.

Proposition 2.5.5
.

Let
f
be a continuous definable selection of the form
f=\min\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}
. Assume that all the critical points of
f
at level
c\in\mathbb{R}
are nondegenerate and denote them by
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\nu}\}
. Then the set
K(c)
from
Proposition

2.4.1
equals

K(c)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{\nu}S^{k(x_{j})+\iota(x_{j})}.

Proof.

This follows from
[
APS97
, Sect. 4]
, analyzing the conditions of
[
APS97
, Theorem 4.2]
case by case.
∎

We will see in
Proposition

2.5.8
a sufficient condition for the distance function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
being of the form
\min\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}
in a neighborhood of a point
x
.

Remark 2.5.6
.

Let
x
be a nondegenerate critical point with indices
k=k(x)
and
\iota=\iota(x)
of a continuous selection
f=\min\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{m}\}
. Then
f
is topologically equivalent to a quadratic form
[
JP88
, Corollary 3.4]
:
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
and a homeomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to U
such that

(50)

f(\psi(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))=f(x)-\sum_{j=1}^{k+\iota}t_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=k+\iota+1}^{n}t_{j}^{2}.

(Note that the conclusions of
Proposition

2.5.5
can also be derived from this statement, following the standard argument from Morse Theory.)
We observe that a normal form of
f
of this type can be also obtained using the theory of Min-type functions, see
[
GR97
, Sect. 3.2]
.

2.5.7.
Nondegenerate critical points of distance functions

In order to use the notion of nondegenerate critical point for
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
introduced above, we need to make this function a continuous selection of
finitely many

\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions, at least nearby a
critical point. In the case
Y
is smooth, this is done as follows.

Denote by
\varphi\colon NY\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
the restriction of the exponential map of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the normal bundle of
Y
. Note that we can think of elements in
NY
as pairs
(y,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with
y\in Y
and
v\in N_{y}Y
and
\varphi(y,v)=y+v.

Proposition 2.5.8
.

Suppose that
Y
is smooth and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a regular value of
\varphi\colon NY\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

(1)

B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
is a finite set
\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\}
,

and for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(2)

for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, the function
f_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}_{B(y_{i},\varepsilon)\cap Y}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
B(x,\delta)
,

(3)

the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{B(x,\delta)}
is a continuous selection of the
f_{i}
: specifically,
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{B(x,\delta)}=\min_{i}f_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
y\in B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
. Then
(y,x-y)\in NY
and
\varphi(y,x-y)=x
. Since
x
is a regular value of
\varphi
, no point
(y^{\prime},v^{\prime})
near
(y,x-y)
can have image
x
by
\varphi
. In particular, this means that
y
is an isolated point in
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
. Since the set
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
is compact, we deduce that
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
is a finite set, which we write as
\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\}
. This proves (
1
).

Since
x
is a regular value of
\varphi
, we have, for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, neighborhoods
U_{i}
of
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
V_{i}
of
y_{i}
in
Y
, and
W_{i}
of
x-y_{i}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n-\dim Y}\cong N_{y_{i}}Y
such that
V_{i}\times W_{i}\subseteq NY
(up to trivialization) and
\varphi_{i}\coloneqq\varphi|_{V_{i}\times W_{i}}\colon V_{i}\times W_{i}\to U_{i}
is a diffeomorphism. In particular, we can choose these neighborhoods such that every point of
U_{i}
is a regular value of
\varphi
and such that
f=\min_{i}\mathrm{dist}_{V_{i}}
on
U\coloneqq\bigcap_{i}U_{i}
. We define the function
\psi_{i}\colon U_{i}\to V_{i}
by
\psi_{i}(z)\coloneqq\textnormal{proj}_{1}(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(z))
. In particular,
\psi_{i}(x)=y_{i}
. Note that
\psi_{i}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
function. Then, for all
z
in
U_{i}
,
\mathrm{dist}_{V_{i}}(z)=\|\psi_{i}(z)-z\|
, and so the function
\mathrm{dist}_{V_{i}}
is also
\mathcal{C}^{2}
.

For the choice of
V_{i}
, we can choose a ball around
y_{i}
with radius
\varepsilon_{i}>0
, and set
\varepsilon=\min_{i}\varepsilon_{i}
. This restricts the choice of the
U_{i}
, but we can always take
\delta
such that
B(x,\delta)\subseteq U
. Then the functions
f_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}_{V_{i}}
satisfy (
2
) and (
3
).
∎

Related results to
Proposition

2.5.8
can be found in
[
SYM23
, Sect. 3]
.

Example 2.5.9
.

The conclusions of
Proposition

2.5.8
are not valid if we drop the assumption that
x
is a regular value of the normal exponential map. For instance, in the case of a smooth parabola
Y=\{x_{2}=x_{1}^{2}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the set
M_{Y}
equals the half line
\{(0,t),t>1\}
. The point
x=(0,1)\in\overline{M_{Y}}
has a unique closest point in
Y
(the origin), but it is not a regular value of the normal exponential map. Moreover every point
x^{\prime}
of the form
x^{\prime}=(0,t)
with
t>1
has two closest points in
Y
and so the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is not differentiable at
x
, and therefore it cannot be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
, contradicting
Proposition

2.5.8
(
2
).

2.6.
Morse Theory of distance functions

Let us go back now to the problem of understanding the contribution of a nondegenerate critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
to the homology of
X
. To start with, we recall the following result from semialgebraic geometry, see
[
Ler23
, Theorem 1.42]
.
The result shows that compact definable sets have definable mapping cylinder neighborhoods (the proof therein works in the definable framework).

Theorem 2.6.1
.

Let
S
be a compact definable set and
f\colon S\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous definable function. Denote by
Z\coloneqq f^{-1}(0)
the zero set of
f
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{f}>0
such that for every
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{f})
the set
\{f\leq\varepsilon\}
is a mapping cylinder neighborhood of
Z
in
S
. In particular, the inclusions
Z\hookrightarrow\{f<\varepsilon\}\hookrightarrow\{f\leq\varepsilon\}
are homotopy equivalences.

Going back to our setting, note that
X\cap Y
is always critical and we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.6.2
.

Let
Y
be closed and definable and
X
smooth and definable in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, for all
R>0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
t\in(0,\varepsilon)
,
X_{Y}^{t}\cap B(0,R)
deformation retracts to
X_{Y}^{0}\cap B(0,R)
.

Proof.

Given
R>0
consider the compact definable set
S\coloneqq X\cap B(0,R)
together with the continuous definable function
f\colon S\to[0,\infty)
given by
\alpha\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{S}.
Then the conclusion follows from the definable version of
Theorem

2.6.1
.
∎

The following definition is useful.

Definition 2.6.3
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be closed and definable and
X
be a smooth manifold. We say that a point
x
of
X
is a
nondegenerate critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
if
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a continuous selection at
x
and
x
is a nondegenerate critical point for the continuous selection. For every
k,\iota\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)
the set of nondegenerate critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
with piecewise linear index
k
and quadratic index
\iota
and which are not on
X\cap Y
.

The next result is a specialization of
Propositions

2.4.1
and
2.5.5
for nondegenerate critical points of distance functions.

Corollary 2.6.4
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be closed and definable and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth manifold. Let
c\neq 0
be a critical value of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
such that

C({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}})\cap({\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}})^{-1}(c)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\nu}\}
consists only of nondegenerate critical points. For every
j=1,\ldots,\nu
denote by
(k_{j},\iota_{j})
the pair of numbers such that
x_{j}\in C_{k_{j},\iota_{j}}(X,Y).

Then for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

(51)

H^{*}(X^{c+\varepsilon}_{Y},X^{c-\varepsilon}_{Y})\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\nu}\widetilde{H}^{*}(S^{k_{j}+\iota_{j}}).

Definition 2.6.5

(Nondegenerate pairs)
.

We say that a pair
(X,Y)
is a
nondegenerate pair
if
X
is transverse to
Y
and all critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
not on
X\cap Y
are nondegenerate.

For a pair of spaces
Z^{\prime}\subseteq Z
, let
b_{i}(Z)
denote the dimension of
H^{i}(Z)
as a vector space over the fixed field of coefficients, and let
b_{i}(Z,Z^{\prime})
denote the dimension of
H^{i}(Z,Z^{\prime})
. In our setting, strong Morse inequalities can be stated as follows.

Proposition 2.6.6

(strong Morse inequalities)
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed and definable set and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth, compact and definable manifold such that
(X,Y)
is a nondegenerate pair.
Then, for any integer
\lambda\geq 0
,

(52)

\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{i+\lambda}b_{i}(X)\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{i+\lambda}\left(b_{i}(X\cap Y)+\sum_{k+\iota=i}\#C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)\right).

Proof.

Let
0=c_{0}<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{m}
denote the critical values of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
, which are finitely many by
Proposition

2.2.1
. Choose real numbers
\{d_{j}\}_{j=0}^{m+1}
with
d_{0}<c_{0}
,
c_{m}<d_{m+1}
, and
c_{j-1}<d_{j}<c_{j}
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. Proceeding exactly as in
[
Mil63
, Sect. 5]
one obtains, for every integer
\lambda\geq 0
, the inequality

(53)

\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{i+\lambda}b_{i}(X_{Y}^{d_{m+1}})\leq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{i+\lambda}b_{i}(X_{Y}^{d_{j+1}},X_{Y}^{d_{j}}).

On the left-hand side, we observe that
b_{i}(X_{Y}^{d_{m+1}})=b_{i}(X)
.
On the right-hand side, we can rearrange the sum as

(54)

\sum_{i=0}^{\lambda}(-1)^{i+\lambda}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{i}(X_{Y}^{d_{j+1}},X_{Y}^{d_{j}})\right)

and observe that
\sum_{j=0}^{m}b_{i}(X_{Y}^{d_{j+1}},X_{Y}^{d_{j}})=b_{i}(X\cap Y)+\sum_{k+\iota=i}\#C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)
by
Corollary

2.6.4
, noting that the term
b_{i}(X\cap Y)
comes from crossing the
0^{\text{th}}
critical value.
∎

The weak Morse inequalities

(55)

b_{\lambda}(X)\leq b_{\lambda}(X\cap Y)+\sum_{k+\iota=\lambda}\#C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)

are obtained from
Proposition

2.6.6
by adding the strong inequalities for
\lambda
and
\lambda-1
.
Writing
\chi(Z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}b_{i}(Z)
, we obtain the following interesting result that relates the Euler characteristics of
X
and
Y
to the critical points of the corresponding relative distance functions.

Corollary 2.6.7
.

Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a closed and definable set and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth, compact, and definable manifold such that
(X,Y)
is a nondegenerate pair. Then

(56)

\chi(X\cap Y)+\sum_{k,\iota\geq 0}(-1)^{k+\iota}\#C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)=\chi(X).

Furthermore, if
Y
is smooth,
X
is closed and definable, and
(Y,X)
is a nondegenerate pair, we get the following equation:

(57)

\chi(Y)+\sum_{k,\iota\geq 0}(-1)^{k+\iota}\#C_{k,\iota}(X,Y)=\chi(X)+\sum_{k,\iota\geq 0}(-1)^{k+\iota}\#C_{k,\iota}(Y,X).

Proof.

As in the classical case, the first equality follows from
Proposition

2.6.6
by comparing the strong Morse inequalities for large enough values
\lambda
and
\lambda+1
. The second equality follows from the first one applied to the pairs
(X,Y)
and
(Y,X)
.
∎

Remark 2.6.8
.

The conclusions of
Proposition

2.6.6
are still valid if
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Y
is compact, since this guarantees that the distance function from
Y
is proper.

Example 2.6.9
.

Let
Y=\{y\}
with
y\notin X
. Then the piecewise linear index of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
at each critical point is zero, and the quadratic index is the standard Morse index, and so the left-hand side of (
57
) equals
1+\sum_{\iota}(-1)^{\iota}\#C_{0,\iota}(\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}}|_{X})
. On the other hand,
\mathrm{dist}_{X}|_{\{y\}}
is a constant function, with critical point
\{y\}
of total index zero, and so the right hand side equals
\chi(X)+1
. In this case, this formula gives back the Euler Characteristic of
X
as the alternating sum of the number of critical points of quadratic index
\iota
.

Example 2.6.10

(Distance function from a finite set, continuation of
Example

2.1.8
)
.

In this example we provide further details on nondegenerate critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
, with
Y=\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and review abstract and geometric complexes capturing the topology of sublevels sets of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
.

For the generic choice of the points
\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{m}\}
, all critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
are nondegenerate and the critical points can be found with the procedure described in
Example

2.1.8
.
In view of
Definitions

2.5.2
and
1.5
, we see that the piecewise linear index
k
of a critical point is not an analogue in the context of distance functions of the classical Morse index; in this case, however, as the quadratic index
\iota
is always zero, the piecewise linear index is what determines the contribution to the topology change.
More precisely, using
Corollary

2.6.4
, we see that if
c\neq 0
is a critical value of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
such that

C(\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\cap{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}^{-1}(c)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\nu}\}
consists only of nondegenerate critical points, then for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

(58)

H^{*}((\mathbb{R}^{n})^{c+\varepsilon}_{Y},(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{c-\varepsilon}_{Y})\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\nu}\widetilde{H}^{*}(S^{k_{j}}).

The critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
can be characterized via the interplay of the Voronoi diagram and the Delaunay tessellation, see e.g.
[
BDGJ08
]
.
The
Delaunay tessellation
of a finite set
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the dual of the Voronoi diagram of
Y
. It is a polytopal complex (i.e., a polyhedral complex such that all cells are bounded) embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covering the convex hull of
Y
. If
Y
is in general position, it is furthemore a (geometric) simplicial complex. The set of vertices of the Delaunay tessellation is
Y
. An
n
-dimensional Delaunay cell is the convex hull of
n+1
or more points in
Y
if the intersection of their corresponding Voronoi cells is a vertex of the Voronoi diagram. Delaunay cells and their faces constitute the Delaunay tessellation. If
x
is a nondegenerate critical point of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
, then the convex hull
\mathrm{co}(B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y})\cap Y)
is the Delaunay face dual to the lowest dimensional Voronoi face containing
x
. In the rest of this example, let us assume
Y
to be in general position and all critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
to be nondegenerate. Then, the critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
are exactly the intersection between the Voronoi faces and their dual Delaunay faces
[
BDGJ08
]
.

For
n=2
, the topology of the sublevels sets of the distance function is described in details in
[
Sie99
]
. For an arbitrary
n
, some abstract and geometric complexes are known to have the same homotopy type of the sublevel sets of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
.

The
Čech complex
of
Y
at scale
t
, denoted by
\operatorname{\check{C}}_{t}(Y)
, is the abstract simplicial complex defined as the nerve of the collection of balls
\{B(y_{i},t)\}_{i=0}^{m}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the nerve lemma
[
Bor48
,
BKRR23
]
,

\operatorname{\check{C}}_{t}(Y)
is homotopy equivalent to the sublevel set
(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{Y}^{t}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}B(y_{i},t)
of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
.

The subcomplex
\mathrm{Del}_{t}(Y)
of
\operatorname{\check{C}}_{t}(Y)
given by the nerve of the collection
\{B(y_{i},t)\cap V(y_{i})\}_{i=0}^{m}
of balls intersected with the respective Voronoi cells is called
\alpha
-shape
or
Delaunay complex
of
Y
at scale
t

[
Ede95
]
. If
Y
is in general position,
\mathrm{Del}_{t}(Y)
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a subcomplex of the Delaunay tessellation, which is a simplicial complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in this case. The Čech and Delaunay complexes at scale
t
are homotopy equivalent
[
Ede95
]
, and the latter can be obtained from the former via a sequence of simplicial collapses
[
BE17
]
.

The
flow complex
of
Y

[
GJ08
,
BDGJ08
]
is a polytopal complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined using the critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
. A vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be defined using Voronoi faces and their dual Delaunay faces. The critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
are exactly the fixed points of a flow associated with the vector field.
The stable manifolds of the critical points of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
with respect to the flow define the flow complex, see
[
BDGJ08
, Sect. 3]
; note that here we consider an immediate adaptation of the definition, using the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
instead of
\mathrm{dist}^{2}_{Y}
. The flow complex has exactly one cell for each critical point of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
.

Polytopal subcomplexes of the flow complex can be defined for each scale
t
. The underlying spaces of these complexes are called
flow shapes
of
Y
at scale
t
, denoted
\mathrm{F}_{t}(Y)
, and are obtained by applying the flow complex construction only to critical points
x
such that
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)\leq t
.
The flow shape
\mathrm{F}_{t}(Y)
is homotopy equivalent
[
BDGJ08
]
to the sublevel set
(\mathbb{R}^{n})_{Y}^{t}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{m}B(y_{i},t)
of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
, hence also to
\operatorname{\check{C}}_{t}(Y)
and
\mathrm{Del}_{t}(Y)
.
Considering all scales
t
, by
Corollary

2.6.4
we know that the filtration of flow shapes
\{\mathrm{F}_{t}(Y)\}_{t\geq 0}
realizes the minimum number of cells necessary to have these homotopy equivalences at each scale.

3.
Examples

3.1.
Distance from a hypersurface

Let
Y
be a smooth compact hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus Y
be a nondegenerate critical point of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
. Let us suppose that
x
is a regular value of the normal exponential map
\varphi\colon NY\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so that we are in the situation of
Proposition

2.5.8
, and let
\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\}
be the set
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
of closest points to
x
on
Y
.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough as in
Proposition

2.5.8
, let
g_{y_{i}}\coloneqq\text{dist}_{B(y_{i},\varepsilon)\cap Y}
.
Then, in a neighborhood of
x
the function
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
is a continuous selection of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions, of the form
\min_{i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}}g_{y_{i}}
. Letting

(59)

v_{i}\coloneqq\nabla g_{y_{i}}(x)=\frac{x-y_{i}}{\|x-y_{i}\|}=\frac{x-y_{i}}{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)},

we denote by
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}}
the unique list of nonnegative numbers (see
Definition

2.5.2
) such that
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{i}=0
.

We describe some properties of the Hessian matrices
H(g_{y_{i}})
at
x
, using
[
AS96
, Theorem 3.2]
(see also
[
Man10
, Section 3]
).
For every fixed
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, there exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i,1},\ldots,e_{i,n}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
N_{y_{i}}Y=\mathbb{R}\{e_{i,n}\}
in which the matrix
H(g_{y_{i}})(x)
is diagonal. The eigenvalue
\beta_{i,n}
corresponding to the eigenvector
e_{i,n}
is zero, while the remaining eigenvalues
\beta_{i,1},\ldots,\beta_{i,n-1}
are nonzero since
x
is nondegenerate, and are given by the formula

(60)

\beta_{i,j}=\frac{-\kappa_{i,j}}{1-\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)\kappa_{i,j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,n-1,

where
\kappa_{i,j}
are the principal curvatures at
y_{i}
, i.e. the eigenvalues of the shape operator at
y_{i}\in Y
, see
[
Lee97
, Chapter 8]
, defined using the
inner
unit normal at
y_{i}
.

We now study the quadratic index
\iota(x)
of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
at
x
, which is the negative inertia index of the convex combination
\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}H(g_{y_{i}})
(with coefficients
\lambda_{i}
defined as above) restricted to the
(n-k)
-dimensional subspace
V(x)=\operatorname{Span}\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}\}^{\perp}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see
Definition

2.5.2
.
As a consequence of the properties we just discussed, one can state sufficient conditions for determining the quadratic index
\iota(x)
based on the simple fact that a convex combination of positive (respectively, negative) semidefinite matrices is positive (resp., negative) semidefinite.

Corollary 3.1.1
.

In the situation we are considering in
Section

3.1
,

-

if
\beta_{i,j}>0
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, then
\iota(x)=0
;

-

if
\beta_{i,j}<0
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, then
\iota(x)
takes the maximum value
n-k
.

If not all the eigenvalues
\beta_{i,j}
have the same sign, determining
\iota(x)
is more complicated. A general expression for
\iota(x)
in terms of the eigenvalues
\beta_{i,j}
is out of reach, since in general the matrices
H(g_{y_{i}})(x)
cannot be diagonalized in a common basis. However, this is possible in the case of
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which we now describe more in detail.

3.2.
Distance from a curve in the plane

We now focus on the case
n=2
, with
Y
a smooth compact curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
As in
Section

3.1
, let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Y
be a nondegenerate critical point of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
which is a regular value of the normal exponential map. By nondegeneracy of
x
, the number of its closest points on
Y
is
2\leq\#(B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y)\leq 3
.

If
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y=\{y_{0},y_{1},y_{2}\}
, then the piecewise linear index is
k(x)=2
and the quadratic index is
\iota(x)=0
since
V(x)=\operatorname{Span}\{v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\}^{\perp}=0
. Let us consider the case where
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y=\{y_{0},y_{1}\}
, which means
k(x)=1
. As described in
Section

3.1
, for each
i\in\{0,1\}
the exists an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i,1},e_{i,2}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
N_{y_{i}}Y=\mathbb{R}\{e_{i,2}\}
in which
H(g_{y_{i}})(x)
is diagonal. In this case,
consider
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
defined as in
Eq.

59
, and observe that they are parallel since
x=\frac{1}{2}(y_{0}+y_{1})
. We have
N_{y_{0}}Y=N_{y_{1}}Y=\operatorname{Span}\{v_{0},v_{1}\}\cong\mathbb{R}
, and
T_{y_{0}}Y=T_{y_{1}}Y=V(x)=\operatorname{Span}\{v_{0},v_{1}\}^{\perp}
. We can diagonalize
H(g_{y_{0}})(x)
and
H(g_{y_{0}})(x)
in the same basis,
obtaining the matrices

(61)

D_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{0,1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1,1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\frac{1}{2}v_{0}+\frac{1}{2}v_{1}=0
, the quadratic index
\iota(x)
is the negative inertia index of the convex combination
\frac{1}{2}D_{0}+\frac{1}{2}D_{1}
restricted to
V(x)
, which is determined by the sign of
\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,1}
:

(62)

\iota(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,1}>0\\
1&\text{if }\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,1}<0.\end{cases}

Note that this refines
Corollary

3.1.1
. The case
\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,1}=0
does not happen, as it would violate the nondegeneracy of
x
.

Via an easy calculation, one can see how the principal curvatures determine the sign of
\beta_{0,1}+\beta_{1,1}
, and hence
\iota(x)
. To simplify notations, here we drop the index
j
, denoting
\beta_{i}\coloneqq\beta_{i,1}
and
\kappa_{i}\coloneqq\kappa_{i,1}
for
i\in\{0,1\}
. We also denote
d\coloneqq\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)
.

First we observe that, by
[
Mil63
, §6]
,
\kappa_{i}<\frac{1}{d}
, for
i\in\{0,1\}
, which is equivalent to
1-d\kappa_{i}>0
.
As we saw,
\iota(x)
is determined by the sign of

(63)

\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}=\frac{-\kappa_{0}}{1-d\kappa_{0}}+\frac{-\kappa_{1}}{1-d\kappa_{1}}=\frac{2d\kappa_{0}\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{0}-\kappa_{1}}{(1-d\kappa_{0})(1-d\kappa_{1})}.

The first equality immediately shows that
\iota(x)=0
if
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}<0
, and that
\iota(x)=1
if
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1}>0
.
Since the denominator of the right-hand side is positive, the sign of
\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}
is the same as the sign of the numerator

(64)

h(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1})\coloneqq 2d\kappa_{0}\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{0}-\kappa_{1}=2d\left[\left(\kappa_{0}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)-\frac{1}{4d^{2}}\right],

which is easy to study.

The following statement summarizes the possible values of
k(x)
and
\iota(x)
.

Corollary 3.2.1
.

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth compact curve, and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Y
be a nondegenerate critical point of
\mathrm{dist}_{Y}
which is a regular value of the normal exponential map
\varphi:NY\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then, the piecewise linear index
k(x)
and the quadratic index
\iota(x)
(see
Definition

2.5.2
) can be determined as follows:

-

if
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y=\{y_{0},y_{1},y_{2}\}
, then
k(x)=2
and
\iota(x)=0
;

-

if
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y=\{y_{0},y_{1}\}
, then
k(x)=1
, and
\iota(x)
is determined by the principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}
of
Y
at
y_{i}
, for
i\in\{0,1\}
:

(65)

\iota(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\left(\kappa_{0}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)>\frac{1}{4d^{2}}\\
1&\text{if }\left(\kappa_{0}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}-\frac{1}{2d}\right)<\frac{1}{4d^{2}}.\end{cases}

3.3.
The index of a critical point for the distance from a point

In this section we are going to prove the following result, which is useful when one wants to compute the index of a critical point for the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
when
Y=\{y\}\not\subset X
(in this case the piecewise linear index is zero, and we are back to the standard setting of Morse theory).

Proposition 3.3.1
.

Let
X=Z(p)
be a smooth hypersurface and
Y=\{y\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a generic point. Let also
x\in X
be a critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
(nondegenerate by genericity of
y
). Denote by
Q_{x}\in\mathrm{Sym}(n-1,\mathbb{R})
the matrix representing the second fundamental form of
X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the direction of
y-x
with respect to an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}X
. Then

\iota(x)=\#\left\{\textnormal{eigenvalues of $Q_{x}$ that are strictly larger than $\frac{2}{\|x-y\|}$}\right\}.

Proof.

We are going to use the discussion from
Remark

2.5.3
, which we recall in this context. We have a manifold
X
described by the regular equation
p=0
and we want to find the critical points of the function
f:=\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}}|_{X}
, together with their quadratic index. Notice that in this case
f
is smooth (because we are assuming
y\notin X
) and the critical points are found using the Lagrange multipliers rule:
x\in X
is critical if and only if there exists
\lambda\neq 0
such that

\nabla p(x)+\lambda\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}=0.

Given such
\lambda
(which is unique), the quadratic index of
f
at
x
is given by the negative inertia index of

\bigg(\mathrm{H}(p)(x)+\lambda\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}})(x)\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}.

Notice that we have the degree of freedom of choosing
-p
instead of
p
as a defining polynomial for
X
and we choose the sign in such a way that
\nabla p(x)
has the same orientation as
y-x
. Then
\lambda=\|\nabla p(x)\|
.

Moreover, the Hessian of
\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}}
can be computed easily: denoting by
w:=\tfrac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}
,

\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}})(x)=\frac{2}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}-ww^{\top}.

In particular, since
w\perp T_{x}X
, as quadratic forms,

\bigg(\mathrm{H}(p)(x)+\lambda\mathrm{H}(\mathrm{dist}_{\{y\}})(x)\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}=\bigg(\mathrm{H}(p)(x)+\frac{2\|\nabla p(x)\|}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}.

Recall now that the second fundamental form
q_{x}
of
Z(p)
at
x
in the direction of
\nabla p(x)
, as a quadratic form, is related to the Hessian of
p
as follows (this is proved in
Lemma

4.6.1
below):

\mathrm{H}(p)(x)=-\|\nabla p(x)\|q_{x},

and therefore, denoting by
\mathrm{i}^{+}(A)
and
\mathrm{i}^{-}(A)
the number of positive and negative eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix
A
,

(66)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}^{-}\bigg(\mathrm{H}(p)(x)+\frac{2\|\nabla p(x)\|}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{-}\bigg(-\|\nabla p(x)\|q_{x}+\frac{2\|\nabla p(x)\|}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}

(67)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{-}\bigg(-q_{x}+\frac{2}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}

(68)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}^{+}\bigg(q_{x}-\frac{2}{\|x-y\|}\mathbf{1}\bigg)\bigg|_{T_{x}X}.

From this last identity the conclusion follows.
∎

4.
Genericity in the algebraic setting

Throughout this section, we consider two real
n
-variable polynomials
p
and
q
and two algebraic sets
X\coloneqq Z(p)
and
Y\coloneqq Z(q)
. We assume
n\geq 1
.

4.1.
A multijet parametric transversality theorem

In this section we prove a useful technical tool,
Theorem

4.1.4
. We first recall the following classic result, which we adapt from
[
Hir76
, Chapter 3, Theorem 2.7]
:

Theorem 4.1.1

(Parametric transversality)
.

Let
M
,
N
,
P
be smooth manifolds,
W\subseteq N
a submanifold of
N
, and
F\colon P\times M\to N
a smooth map. For all
p
in
P
, we denote by
F_{p}
the map
F(p,-)\colon M\to N
.

If
F
is transverse to
W
, then the set
\{p\in P\mid F(p,-)\pitchfork W\}
is residual in
P
. In other words, for generic
p\in P
, the map
F_{p}
is transverse to
W
.

As an immediate consequence we have the following corollary.

Corollary 4.1.2
.

Let
F\colon P\times M\to N
be a submersion and
W\subseteq N
a submanifold. If
\operatorname{codim}_{N}W=\dim M
, then for generic
p\in P
we have
\dim(F(p,M)\cap W)=0
. If
\operatorname{codim}_{N}W>\dim M
, then for generic
p\in P
we have
F(p,M)\cap W=\varnothing
.

We will be applying these transversality results to
jet spaces
.

Definition 4.1.3

(jets and multijets)
.

Let
n,m,r\geq 0
be integers. The space of
jets of order
r
from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

(69)

J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{r}(\mathscr{P}_{\ell,m})^{n},

where
\mathscr{P}_{\ell,m}
is the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
\ell
in
m
variables.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth function and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The
jet of order
r
of
f
at
x
is

(70)

\displaystyle j^{r}(f)(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(x)\right)_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m},|\alpha|\leq r}\in J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})

(71)

\displaystyle=\left(x,f(x),\ldots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(x),\ldots,\frac{\partial^{r}f}{\partial^{r}x_{i}},\ldots\right).

The identification with polynomial spaces comes from identifying the partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(x)
with the coefficient in front of the monomial
x^{\alpha}
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer. The space of
k
-multijets of order
r
is just
k
copies of the space of jets of order
r
:

(72)

{}_{k}J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})^{k}.

Similarly, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth function and
\overline{x}\coloneqq x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(we write
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{km}\setminus\Delta
). The
k
-multijet of order
r
of
f
at
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
is

(73)

{}_{k}j^{r}(\overline{x})\coloneqq(j^{r}(f)(x_{i}))_{i=1,\ldots,k}.

We study jets through parametric jet maps of the form

(74)

F\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{P}_{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{nk}\setminus\Delta)&\to&{}_{k}J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\\
(p,\overline{x})&\mapsto&{}_{k}j^{r}(p)(\overline{x}).\end{array}\right.

The next result is a key technical ingredient for the rest of the paper.

Theorem 4.1.4
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{d}
be the space of real polynomials of degree
d
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. There exists a function
d(n,k,r)
such that, for all
d\geq d(n,k,r)
, the map
F\colon\mathscr{P}_{d}\times(\mathbb{R}^{nk}\setminus\Delta)\to{}_{k}J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is a submersion.

Proof.

Fix
d
a polynomial degree,
p\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
a polynomial, and
\overline{x}\coloneqq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in(\mathbb{R}^{nk}\setminus\Delta)
points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The differential of
F
at
(p,x)
is a linear map

(75)

DF_{p,\overline{x}}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}T_{p,\overline{x}}\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{nk}=\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{nk}&\to&T_{F(p,\overline{x})}{}_{k}J^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}^{nk}\oplus\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{r}(\mathscr{P}_{\ell,n})^{k}\\
(\dot{p},\overline{\dot{x}})&\mapsto&(\overline{\dot{x}},\ldots,\dot{p}(x_{i})+\nabla p(x_{i})^{\top}\dot{x}_{i},\ldots,\nabla\dot{p}(x_{i})+\nabla^{2}p(x_{i})^{\top}\dot{x}_{i},\ldots).\end{array}\right.

Let
(\overline{\dot{y}},\dot{U})=(\overline{\dot{y}},\overline{\dot{u}}^{(1)},\ldots,\overline{\dot{u}}^{(r)})\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}\oplus\bigoplus_{\ell=1}^{r}(\mathscr{P}_{\ell,n})^{k}
. For
F
to be a submersion at
(p,\overline{x})
, it suffices to find
(\dot{p},\overline{\dot{x}})
such that
DF_{p,\overline{x}}(\dot{p},\overline{\dot{x}})=(\overline{\dot{y}},\dot{U})
. This is linear in the coefficients of
\dot{p}
and in monomials in
\overline{\dot{y}}
. We find that
\overline{\dot{x}}=\overline{\dot{y}}
, and then there are
k\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{n}{\ell}
equations in the coefficients of
\dot{p}
. For there to be a solution in general, we need

(76)

\dim\mathscr{P}_{d}=\binom{n+d}{d}\geq k\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{n}{\ell}.

We set
d(n,k,r)
to be the smallest

satisfying this inequality.
∎

Example 4.1.5
.

If
k\leq n+1
and
r=1
, then we can take
d(n,k,r)=3
. Indeed, for all
n\geq 1
,
\binom{n+3}{3}\geq(n+1)^{2}\geq k(\binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1})
.

If
k\leq n+1
and
r=2
, then we can take
d(n,k,r)=4
. Indeed, we compute

(77)

\displaystyle\dim\mathscr{P}_{d}-k\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{n}{\ell}

\displaystyle\geq\dim\mathscr{P}_{d}-(n+1)\sum_{\ell=0}^{r}\binom{n}{\ell}

(78)

\displaystyle=\binom{n+4}{4}-(n+1)(\binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1}+\binom{n}{2})

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)}{24}-(n+1)(1+n+\frac{n(n-1)}{2})

(80)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{24}((n+2)(n+3)(n+4)-12(n^{2}+n+2)

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{24}(n^{3}-3n^{2}+14n).

We determine the sign of (
81
) by looking at the derivative of the last factor:
3n^{2}-6n+14
has discriminant
36-4\cdot 3\cdot 14<0
and value
14>0
at

, so the derivative is always positive. At
n=1
, we have
n^{3}-3n^{2}+14n=12
, so for all
n\geq 1
, (
81
) is positive.

4.2.
Generic transversality

Here we prove that for the generic choice of the pair
(p,q)
, the zero set of
p
is transverse to both
Z(q)
and the closure of its medial axis.

Proposition 4.2.1
.

For
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 0
and generic
p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
and
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
,
X
is transverse to
Y
and
\overline{M_{Y}}
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by showing that the set
S\coloneqq\{(p,q)\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\times\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}\mid X\not\pitchfork Y\text{ or }X\not\pitchfork\overline{M_{Y}}\}
is semialgebraic of measure
0
in
\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\times\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
. Indeed, this would imply that the set
S
has codimension at least
1
, making it nowhere dense, and thus its complement is a residual set.

The fact that
S
is semialgebraic follows from the fact that
\overline{M_{Y}}
is semialgebraic: taking a stratification of
\overline{M_{Y}}
, being transverse to it is a semialgebraic property; the same is true for
Y
.

Next, let
T
be a stratum of
Y
or
\overline{M_{Y}}
, and consider the evaluation map
F^{\operatorname{ev}}\colon\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\times T\to\mathbb{R}
sending
(p,x)
to
p(x)
. It is clear that
F^{\operatorname{ev}}
is a submersion. We observe that
Z(p)
is transverse to
T
if, and only if,
0
is a regular value of
p_{|T}
, which is equivalent to
F^{\operatorname{ev}}(p,-)
being transverse to
\{0\}
. We therefore use parametric transversality with
W=\{0\}
: the set

(82)

\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\mid 0\text{ regular value of }p_{|T}\}

is residual in
\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
. Since
Y
and
\overline{M_{Y}}
can be stratified into finitely many disjoint strata, we conclude that the set
\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\mid X\pitchfork Y\text{ and }X\pitchfork\overline{M_{Y}}\}
is residual in
\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
. Thus, for all
q
in
\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
, the set
S(q)\coloneqq\{p\in\mathscr{P}_{d}\mid X\not\pitchfork Y\text{ or }X\not\pitchfork\overline{M_{Y}}\}
is semialgebraic of measure
0
. By Fubini’s theorem, we conclude that
S
also has measure
0
.
∎

4.3.
Finite number of closest points

In this section we prove an analogue of
[
Yom81
, Proposition 3]
, showing that generically the function
m_{Y}
is bounded by
n+1
.

Theorem 4.3.1

(cf.
[
Yom81
, Proposition 3]
)
.

For the generic
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
with
d\geq 2
, writing
Y=Z(q)
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the set
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
is a nondegenerate simplex with at most
n+1
points.

Proof.

For all
k\in\{0,\ldots,n+1\}
, define the parameterized jet map

(83)

F_{k}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)&\to&{}_{k+1}J^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{2})\\
(q,x,\overline{y})&\mapsto&(\overline{y},\ldots,q(y_{i}),\ldots,\|x-y_{i}\|^{2},\ldots)\end{array}\right.

induced by the function
y\mapsto(q(y),\|x-y\|^{2})
. Its differential at a point
(q,x,\overline{y})
is

(84)

DF_{k,(q,x,\overline{y})}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\\
(\dot{q},\dot{x},\overline{\dot{y}})&\mapsto&(\overline{\dot{y}},\ldots,\dot{q}(y_{i})+\nabla q(y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i},\ldots,2(x-y_{i})^{\top}(\dot{x}-\dot{y}_{i}),\ldots).\end{array}\right.

Let

(85)

W_{k}\coloneqq\left\{(\overline{z},\overline{s},\overline{t})\in{}_{k+1}J^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{2})\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;s_{i}=0,\\
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},\;t_{i}=t_{0}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

be a subspace of
{}_{k+1}J^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{2})
and let us prove that
F_{k}
is transverse to
W_{k}
. Let
(q,x,\overline{y})\in\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)
such that
F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})\in W_{k}
, and let
(\overline{\dot{z}},\overline{\dot{s}},\overline{\dot{t}})\in T_{F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})}{}_{k+1}J^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We are looking for
(\dot{q},\dot{x},\overline{\dot{y}})\in T_{(q,x,\overline{y})}(\mathscr{P}_{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta))
and
(\overline{\dot{w}},0,0)\in T_{F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})}W_{k}
such that

(86)

\displaystyle(\overline{\dot{z}},\overline{\dot{s}},\overline{\dot{t}})

\displaystyle=DF_{k,(q,x,\overline{y})}(\dot{q},\dot{x},\overline{\dot{y}})+(\overline{\dot{w}},0,0)

(87)

\displaystyle=(\overline{\dot{y}}+\overline{\dot{w}},\ldots,\dot{q}(y_{i})+\nabla q(y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i},\ldots,2(x-y_{i})^{\top}(\dot{x}-\dot{y}_{i}),\ldots).

Firstly, we observe that
x
is different from all the
y_{i}
. Indeed, if not, then we would have
\|x-y_{i}\|^{2}=0
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, which contradicts the fact that the
y_{i}
are distinct. So we can set
\dot{x}=0
and solve the equations
2(x-y_{i})^{\top}(\dot{x}-\dot{y}_{i})=\dot{t}_{i}
to obtain the
\dot{y}_{i}
.
Next, since
d\geq 2
, we can find
\dot{q}
solving the equations
\dot{q}(y_{i})+\nabla q(y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i}=\dot{s}_{i}
. Indeed, we have
k+1
equations in the coefficients of
\dot{q}
and
\dim\mathscr{P}_{d}=\binom{n+d}{d}\geq\binom{n+2}{2}=\frac{(n+2)(n+1)}{2}\geq k+1
.
Finally, we choose
\overline{\dot{w}}=\overline{\dot{z}}-\overline{\dot{y}}
and conclude that
F_{k}
is transverse to
W_{k}
.

By the parametric transversality theorem (
Theorem

4.1.1
), for generic
q
,
F_{k}(q,-)
is transverse to
W_{k}
. Fix such a
q
, let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and take
k+1
points
y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}
from
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
. Then
F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})\in W_{k}
, so we can write the transversality property:

(88)

\textnormal{im}(DF_{k}(q,-)_{x,\overline{y}})+T_{F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})}W_{k}=T_{F_{k}(q,x,\overline{y})}{}_{k+1}J^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{2}),

or more explicitly,

(89)

\textnormal{im}(DF_{k}(q,-)_{x,\overline{y}})+\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\times 0^{k+1}\times\mathbb{R}(1,\ldots,1)=\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{k+1}.

Using the expression of
DF_{k}
above, we know that

(90)

DF_{k}(q,-)_{x,\overline{y}}\colon(\dot{x},\overline{\dot{y}})\mapsto(\overline{\dot{y}},\ldots,\nabla q(y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i},\ldots,2(x-y_{i})^{\top}(\dot{x}-\dot{y}_{i}),\ldots).

In particular, when the terms
\nabla q(y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i}
equal
0
, then we also have
(x-y_{i})^{\top}\dot{y}_{i}=0
because
x-y_{i}
and
\nabla q(y_{i})
are collinear. Thus we need
((x-y_{i})^{\top}\dot{x})_{i}
to span a supplementary space to
\mathbb{R}(1,\ldots,1)
(by a dimension argument). For this last part to be satisfied, we need the vectors

(91)

\{(x-y_{i})-(x-y_{0})\}_{i=1,\ldots,k}=\{y_{i}-y_{0}\}_{i=1,\ldots,k}

to be linearly independent. This is equivalent to
y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}
forming a nondegenerate simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Finally, we pick
q
transverse to every
W_{k}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
: this is a generic choice. By the above arguments, any distinct set of at most
n+2
points in
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
forms a nondegenerate simplex. We conclude that
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
has at most
n+1
points (any more would lead to a contradiction with nondegeneracy) and that these points form a nondegenerate simplex.
∎

4.4.
Finite number of critical points

In this section we study the structure of critical points for the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
for generic

and
Y=Z(q)
.

Definition 4.4.1

(

-critical points)
.

Let

be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Y
a closed definable subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For all

, we define the

-critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
as the critical points

of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
such that

.
We denote by

the set of
k
-critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
.

The case

corresponds simply to

Note that, by
Theorem

4.3.1
, every critical point is a
k
-critical point for some

.

Definition 4.4.2

(Algebraic critical points)
.

Let
d\geq 0
,
p,q\in\mathscr{P}_{d}
, and
k\geq 1
. We define the algebraic set

(92)

C_{k}(p,q)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}x&\in&\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}&\in&\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)},\\
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{k}&\in&\mathbb{R}^{k+1},\\
\mu,r&\in&\mathbb{R}\end{array}\left|\begin{array}[]{l}x=\mu\nabla p(x)+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}y_{i},\\
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,\\
p(x)=0,\\
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;q(y_{i})=0,\\
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;\|x-y_{i}\|^{2}=r^{2},\\
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;\textnormal{rank}(x-y_{i},\nabla q(y_{i}))\leq 1\end{array}\right.\right\}.

We then define the
algebraic
k
-critical points w.r.t.
p
and
q
as the set of points
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the projection of
C_{k}(p,q)
on the first component.

Lemma 4.4.3
.

For generic
p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
and
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
, every critical point
x
of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is an algebraic
k
-critical point for some

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.2.1
, we can take

and
q
generically such that

is transverse to both
Y
and
\overline{M_{Y}}
. Let
x
be an element of

. By

and

, we have

(93)

\partial_{x}f=\operatorname{proj}_{T_{x}X}\left(\operatorname{co}\left\{\left.\frac{x-y}{\|x-y\|}\;\right|\;y\in B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y\right\}\right).

Recall that
x
is a critical point if
0
belongs to this set. By
Theorem

4.3.1
, for generic
p
and
q
, the set

elements. Moreover, for each point

in

, the function

reaches a local minimum at

, so

is parallel to

.

Therefore, if

is a critical point of

, then there exist

,

in

,

\begin{gathered}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}=1,\\

\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\},\;\|x-y_{i}\|=\|x-y_{0}\|,\\

-critical point.

Theorem 4.4.4

(Finite critical points)
.

For

and

and

and

and

Proof.

Consider the map

(95)

F^{1}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\times\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{L}&\to&J^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\\

where

. Consider the following subspace of

Continuous selections

Proposition 4.5.1

W^{2}\coloneqq\{(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in J^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in W^{1}\},

where
W^{1}
is defined in the proof of
Theorem

4.4.4
. We define the subspace of
J^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}

(105)

W^{2}_{\textnormal{crit}}\coloneqq\left\{(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in W^{2}\mid\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;\textnormal{rank}[(I_{n}+rH_{i})(I_{n}-v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}),v_{i}]<n\right\}.

This space is defined by the same equations as
W^{1}
and additional equations that involve the
H_{i}
. These new equations are independent of those defining
W^{2}
. Indeed, given
(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)
in
W^{2}
and
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, we can assume
H_{i}=0
, which gives us
\operatorname{rank}[(I_{n}-v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}),v_{i}]=n
.

We deduce that
W^{2}_{\textnormal{crit}}
has codimension
>nk+2n+k+3
, which is the dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{L}
.
By
Theorem

4.1.4
and
Example

4.1.5
, for
d_{1}\geq 3
and
d_{2}\geq 4
, the map
F^{2}
is a submersion.
By
Corollary

4.1.2
, for generic
p
and
q
, the critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
miss
W^{2}_{\textnormal{crit}}
. We conclude that, for generic
p
and
q
, the critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
are regular values for the normal exponential map of
Y
.
∎

Corollary 4.5.2
.

For generic
p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
and
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
of respective degrees
d_{1}\geq 3
and
d_{2}\geq 4
, denoting by
X=Z(p)
and
Y=Z(q)
the function
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a continuous selection of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions around its critical points.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.5.1
, for generic
p
and
q
, the critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
are regular values of the normal exponential map of
Y
. By
Proposition

2.5.8
, we deduce that
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
selection around its critical points.
∎

4.6.
Nondegeneracy of critical points

Now we aim to describe the degeneracy of critical points algebraically. To do this, we need to describe the Hessian of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
at
x
in terms of the derivatives of
p
and
q
.

Lemma 4.6.1
.

Let
Y=Z(q)
and
y_{0}\in Y
. Then the second fundamental form of
Y
at
y_{0}
, is

(106)

\textnormal{II}_{y_{0}}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}T_{y_{0}}Y\times T_{y_{0}}Y&\to&N_{y_{0}}Y=\mathbb{R}\nabla q(y_{0})\\
(v,w)&\mapsto&-\frac{\nabla q(y_{0})}{\|\nabla q(y_{0})\|}v^{\top}H(q)(y_{0})w.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Let
v_{0},w_{0}\in T_{y_{0}}Y
be tangent vectors and write
\alpha\coloneqq\|\nabla q(y_{0})\|
. Define the tangent vector field

(107)

v\colon y\mapsto\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left(v_{0}\|\nabla q(y)\|^{2}-(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y))\nabla q(y)\right)

in a neighborhood around
y_{0}
. Note that
v(y)\in T_{y}Y
for all
y
and
v(y_{0})=v_{0}
. By one definition of the second fundamental form
[
Lee97
, Chapter 8]
, we have

(108)

\text{II}_{y}(v_{0},w_{0})=\frac{\nabla q(y_{0})}{\alpha}\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}J(v)(y_{0})w_{0}

(i.e. the projection onto
N_{y}Y
of the differential of
v
in the direction
w_{0}
), where
J(v)
is the Jacobian of
v
.

Recall that
J(v)
is the matrix
(\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
, where
v_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
component of
v(y)
. For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, we have

(109)

\alpha^{2}v_{i}(y)=v_{0,i}\|\nabla q(y)\|^{2}-(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y))\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(y).

Then, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have

(110)

\alpha^{2}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(y)=v_{0,i}\cdot 2\nabla q(y)^{\top}\frac{\partial\nabla q}{\partial x_{j}}(y)-\left(v_{0}^{\top}\frac{\partial\nabla q}{\partial x_{j}}(y)\right)\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(y)-(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y))\frac{\partial^{2}q}{\partial x_{i}x_{j}}(y).

Thus we can write

(111)

\alpha^{2}\nabla v_{i}(y)^{\top}=2v_{0,i}\nabla q(y)^{\top}H(q)(y)-(v_{0}^{\top}H(q)(y))\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(y)-(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y))\frac{\partial\nabla q}{\partial x_{i}}(y)^{\top}

and

(112)

\alpha^{2}J(v)(y)=2v_{0}\nabla q(y)^{\top}H(q)(y)-\nabla q(y)v_{0}^{\top}H(q)(y)-(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y))H(q)(y).

Finally, we get

(113)

\displaystyle\text{II}_{y_{0}}(v_{0},w_{0})={}

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla q(y_{0})}{\alpha}\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}J(v)(y_{0})w_{0}

(114)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla q(y_{0})}{\alpha^{3}}\Big(2(\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}v_{0})\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}-\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}\nabla q(y_{0})v_{0}^{\top}-\nabla q(y_{0})^{\top}(v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y_{0}))\Big)H(q)(y_{0})w_{0}

(115)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-\frac{\nabla q(y_{0})}{\alpha}v_{0}^{\top}H(q)(y_{0})w_{0},

using the fact that
v_{0}^{\top}\nabla q(y_{0})=0
.
∎

For all
y
in
Y
, we define
g_{y}\coloneqq\text{dist}_{B(y,\varepsilon)\cap Y}
and
f_{y}\coloneqq g_{y}|_{X}
for
y\in Y
.
We also introduce the following notation: for a symmetric matrix
H\in\mathrm{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})
and a subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote by

H|_{V}
a matrix representing the restriction of the quadratic form
h(x)=x^{\top}Hx
to
V
. If
L=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
is a matrix whose columns form a basis for
V
, then one can take

H|_{V}=L^{\top}HL.

Definition 4.6.2

(Algebraic degenerate
k
-critical points)
.

The set of
algebraic degenerate
k
-critical points w.r.t.
p
and
q
is the projection of the set

(116)

D_{k}(p,q)\coloneqq\left\{(x,\overline{y},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in C_{k}(p,q)\;\left|\;\det\left(\mu H(p)(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}}+r\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}H(g_{y_{i}})(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}}\right)=0\right.\right\}

onto the first component
x
.

The use of the adjective “algebraic” is justified by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6.3
.

For all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the set
D_{k}(p,q)
is algebraic.

Proof.

Let
(x,\overline{y},\overline{\lambda},r)\in D_{k}(p,q)
. For all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, write
\nu_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\nabla q(y_{i})}{\|\nabla q(y_{i})\|}
the unit normal vector from
Y
at
y_{i}
. By
Lemma

4.6.1
, we have the second fundamental form
\textnormal{II}_{y_{i}}
of
Y
at
y_{i}
, defined as a bilinear form from
T_{y_{i}}Y
to
N_{y_{i}}Y
. Taking the scalar product with
\nu_{i}
gives a quadratic form represented by the matrix
-H(q)(y_{i})/\|\nabla q(y_{i})\|
. We extend the matrix representation of this form to
N_{y_{i}}Y
as follows:

(117)

Q_{i}\coloneqq(I_{n}-\nu_{i}\nu_{i}^{\top})\frac{-H(q)(y_{i})}{\|\nabla q(y_{i})\|}(I_{n}-\nu_{i}\nu_{i}^{\top}),

with
I_{n}
the identity matrix (note that the orientation of
\nu_{i}
does not matter). By
[
GT83
, Lemma 14.17]
, for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, the Hessian of
g_{y_{i}}
and
Q_{i}
are diagonalizable in the same basis (one consisting of the directions of principal curvature of
Y
and of a normal vector, see also
Section

3.1
), and the eigenvalues of
H(g_{y_{i}})
are the image of those of
Q_{i}
via the function
\kappa\mapsto\frac{-\kappa}{1-\|x-y_{i}\|\kappa}
. We deduce that

(118)

H(g_{y_{i}})(x)=\frac{-Q_{i}}{1-\|x-y_{i}\|Q_{i}}.

Let
\nu\coloneqq\frac{\nabla p(x)}{\|\nabla p(x)\|}
and
V\coloneqq[\nu,\nu_{0},\ldots,\nu_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k+2)}
be the matrix of normal vectors. We want to project
H(p)(x)
and the
H(g_{y_{i}})(x)
onto
\partial_{x}f^{\bot}\cap\nabla p(x)^{\bot}
and then take the convex combination. Since we end up with an
n\times n
matrix, the equation in (
116
) becomes

(119)

\operatorname{rank}\left(\mu L^{\top}H(p)(x)L+r\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}L^{\top}H(g_{y_{i}})(x)L\right)\leq n-k-3,

where
L=I_{n}-V(V^{\top}V)^{-1}V^{\top}
, and this equation is algebraic.
∎

Lemma 4.6.4
.

For generic
p\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
and
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
with
d_{1}\geq 3
and
d_{2}\geq 4
, every degenerate critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is an algebraic degenerate
k
-critical point for some
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof.

We pick generic
p
and
q
such that, for all
x
in
X
, the set
B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
is a nondegenerate simplex (
Theorem

4.3.1
) and that
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
selection around each of its critical points (
Corollary

4.5.2
). In this context, a critical point is degenerate if, and only if, condition (2) of
Definition

2.5.2
holds.

Explicitly, let
x
be a critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
and
\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k}\}=B(x,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))\cap Y
. For all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
, the differential of
f_{y_{i}}
at
x
is the projection of
\nabla g_{y_{i}}(x)
on
T_{x}X
: explicitly, there exists
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(120)

\displaystyle\nabla f_{y_{i}}(x)

\displaystyle=\nabla g_{y_{i}}(x)-\mu_{i}\nabla p(x)

(121)

\displaystyle=\frac{x-y_{i}}{\|x-y_{i}\|}-\mu_{i}\nabla p(x).

Since, by definition of critical points, there exist
\lambda_{i}
such that
\sum\lambda_{i}=1
and
\sum\lambda_{i}\nabla f_{y_{i}}(x)=0
, we get

(122)

x=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\lambda_{i}y_{i}+\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}\|x-y_{i}\|\nabla p(x)).

Let
\mu\coloneqq\sum_{I}\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}\|x-y_{i}\|=\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}
. We deduce that
(x,\overline{y},\overline{\lambda},\mu,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))
is an element of
C_{k}(p,q)
.

We can write
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
as the continuous selection of
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions

(123)

{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}=\min_{i=0,\ldots,k}f_{y_{i}}.

Therefore, using
Remark

2.5.3
, we see that the critical point
x
is degenerate if, and only if, the matrix

(124)

\sum_{i=0}^{k}(\lambda_{i}H(g_{y_{i}})(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}}+\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}H(p)(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}})=\frac{\mu}{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x)}H(p)(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}H(g_{y_{i}})(x)|_{\partial_{x}f^{\bot}},

is singular. So we conclude that if
x
is a degenerate critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
, then
(x,\overline{y},\overline{\lambda},\mu,\mathrm{dist}_{Y}(x))
is an element of
D_{k}(p,q)
, and so
x
is an algebraic degenerate
k
-critical point for
k\leq n
.
∎

Remark 4.6.5
.

The Morse index of a nondegenerate critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
in the case
X=Z(p)
and
Y=\{y\}
with
y\notin X
can be computed using the Lagrange’s multipliers rule, as follows. First, a critical point of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
is a point
x\in X
where

\lambda\nabla p(x)+y-x=0

for some
\lambda\neq 0
. Now, assuming that
x
is a nondegenerate critical point, the matrix representing the Hessian of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
at
x
in the basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}\}
for
T_{x}X
can be written as

L^{\top}\left(\lambda H(p)(x)-I_{n}\right)L,

where
L=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}]
.

Theorem 4.6.6

(Genericity of nondegeneracy)
.

For generic
p,q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}
and
q\in\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}
with
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 4
, all critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
are nondegerate.

Proof.

Consider the map

(125)

F^{3}\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathscr{P}_{d_{1}}\times\mathscr{P}_{d_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{L}&\to&J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\\
(p,q,x,\overline{y},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)&\mapsto&(x,p(x),\nabla p(x),H(p)(x),\overline{y},q(\overline{y}),\nabla q(\overline{y}),H(q)(\overline{y}),\overline{\lambda},\mu,r),\end{array}\right.

and let

(126)

W^{3}\coloneqq\{(x,s,u,G,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in W^{1}\},

where
W^{1}
is defined in the proof of
Theorem

4.4.4
.

Let us find a system equations of equations such that, for generic
p
and
q
, elements of
F^{3}(\{p\}\times\{q\}\times D_{k}(p,q))
satisfy these equations.
We define the subspace of
J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times{}_{k+1}J^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{L}

(127)

\displaystyle W^{3}_{\textnormal{deg}}\coloneqq\Big\{

\displaystyle(x,s,u,G,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)\in W^{3}

\displaystyle\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;\nu_{i}\coloneqq\frac{v_{i}}{\|v_{i}\|}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;Q_{i}\coloneqq(I_{n}-\nu_{i}\nu_{i}^{\top})\frac{H_{i}}{\|v_{i}\|}(I_{n}-\nu_{i}\nu_{i}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\\
\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,k\},\;\widetilde{H}_{i}\coloneqq\frac{-Q_{i}}{1-rQ_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\\
V\coloneqq[\frac{u}{\|u\|},v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k+2)},\\
L\coloneqq I_{n}-V(V^{\top}V)^{-1}V^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\\
\operatorname{rank}\left(\mu L^{\top}GL+r\sum_{i=0}^{k}\lambda_{i}L^{\top}\widetilde{H}_{i}L\right)\leq n-k-3\end{array}\right\}.

We need to check that the last inequality is independent of the other equations defining
W^{3}
. It suffices to find an element of
W^{3}
such that this rank is
n-k-2
. Since the equations defining
W^{3}
do not involve
G
nor
\overline{H}
, we can choose these terms freely. Let
(x,s,u,\overline{y},\overline{t},\overline{v},\overline{H},\overline{\lambda},\mu,r)
be an element of
W^{3}
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
and we set
G=H_{1}=\cdots=H_{n}=0
. Up to scaling, we can assume that
r\neq 1
, and up to orthogonal transformation of
H_{0}
, we can assume
\nu_{0}
is
e_{n}
, the last element of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this case,
\|v_{0}\|=1
, so
Q_{0}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\ldots,1,0)
and
\widetilde{H}_{0}=\frac{-1}{1-r}Q_{0}
. Note that
\widetilde{H}_{0}^{\bot}=\mathbb{R}e_{n}=\mathbb{R}\nu_{0}
, so the rank of
\lambda_{0}L^{\top}\widetilde{H}_{0}L
is maximal; that is, its rank is
n-k-2
.

We deduce that
W^{3}_{\textnormal{deg}}
has codimension
>nk+2n+k+3
, which is the dimension of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n(k+1)}\setminus\Delta)\times\mathbb{R}^{L}
. By
Theorem

4.1.4
and
Example

4.1.5
, for
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 4
, the map
F^{3}
is a submersion.
By
Corollary

4.1.2
, for generic
p
and
q
, the image
\operatorname{im}F^{3}(p,q,-)
misses
W^{3}_{\textnormal{deg}}
. In particular, the image of
D_{k}(p,q)
by
F^{3}(p,q,-)
misses
W^{3}_{\textnormal{deg}}
. By
Lemmas

4.6.3
and
4.6.4
, we conclude that, for generic
p
and
q
, the critical points of
{\mathrm{dist}_{Y}|_{X}}
are nondegenerate.
∎
