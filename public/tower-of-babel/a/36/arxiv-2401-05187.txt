Decoding of Selective Attention to Speech From Ear-EEG Recordings
Mike D. Thornton, Danilo P. Mandic, Tobias Reichenbach

Abstract
Many people with hearing loss struggle to comprehend speech in crowded auditory scenes, even when they are using hearing aids. Future hearing technologies which can identify the focus of a listener’s auditory attention, and selectively amplify that sound alone, could improve the experience that this patient group has with their hearing aids. In this work, we present the results of our experiments with an ultra-wearable in-ear electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring device. Participants listened to two competing speakers in an auditory attention experiment whilst their EEG was recorded. We show that typical neural responses to the speech envelope, as well as its onsets, can be recovered from such a device, and that the morphology of the recorded responses is indeed modulated by selective attention to speech. Features of the attended and ignored speech stream can also be reconstructed from the EEG recordings, with the reconstruction quality serving as a marker of selective auditory attention. Using the stimulus-reconstruction method, we show that with this device auditory attention can be decoded from short segments of EEG recordings which are of just a few seconds in duration. The results provide further evidence that ear-EEG systems offer good prospects for wearable auditory monitoring as well as future cognitively-steered hearing aids.



I
Introduction

People with hearing loss often struggle to comprehend speech in noisy environments, even when they wear their hearing aids

[
1
,
2
]
. Various interventions have been developed to address this problem; for example, modern hearing aids incorporate advanced noise suppression technologies, which can reduce the impact of interfering sounds such as wind and other non-speech noises
[
3
]
. In cocktail-party scenarios, where the interfering sounds are also speech, a hearing aid must first identify the target speaker, and then selectively amplify that voice alone. If the target speaker is known
a priori
(for example, it is the teacher in a classroom environment), then the voice of that speaker can be wirelessly transmitted to hearing aid users via a hearing loop
[
4
]
. In more spontaneous settings such as social scenarios, beamforming technologies are usually utilised
[
5
]
. These rely on directional microphones to identify the direction of the listener’s head, and attempt to selectively amplify sounds emanating from that direction.

Amongst these interventions, only beamforming hearing aids offer users the ability to selectively and spontaneously choose which voice to enhance. In everyday environments, however, beamforming hearing aids often provide only limited benefits to their users
[
6
,
7
]
. This limitation arises because individuals do not constantly orient their head towards a target speaker during naturalistic listening. Furthermore, interfering speech can easily divert the listener’s attention and shift the direction of their gaze away from the intended speaker
[
8
]
. Whilst listeners can learn to adapt their behaviours in order to experience the most benefit from their beamforming hearing aids, even with practice they may struggle to quickly reorient their gaze when the focus of their attention changes location or is re-directed. This means that beamforming technology can actually increase the difficulty of locating off-axis sources for hearing aid users
[
9
,
10
]
.

Alternative techniques for determining the focus of a listener’s attention could be used in place of, or in conjunction with, beamforming algorithms. Recent research has demonstrated that the focus of a listener’s selective attention to speech can be decoded from neuroimaging data
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. If a discrete and wearable brain monitoring device can be incorporated into a hearing aid, then it may be possible for the user to control the hearing aid through their attentional focus alone
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Most studies identify electroencephalography (EEG) as the most suitable neuroimaging modality for real-world auditory attention decoding, due to its relative accessibility, its high temporal resolution, and the variety of wearable EEG devices which are already commercialised.

Usually, auditory attention is decoded from EEG recordings through a technique known as stimulus-reconstruction
[
13
,
17
,
18
]
. This involves training regression models (known as backward models in the context of stimulus-reconstruction) to reconstruct features of the speech stimuli from time-aligned EEG signals. Usually, the feature of choice is the temporal envelope of speech, since this feature is strongly represented in EEG responses to speech
[
19
]
. Moreover, the strength of the EEG response to the speech envelope is modulated by selective attention, with responses to the attended speech envelope being dominant. Typically, the strength of the response is assessed by determining the accuracy with which the attended and ignored speech envelopes can be reconstructed from the EEG signals, by taking the Pearson correlation between the reconstructed envelopes and the ground-truth envelopes
[
13
]
. The speech stream which yields the greatest Pearson correlation is then taken as the estimated attended stream.

As an alternative to stimulus reconstruction, system identification techniques can be used to identify the attended speech stream. The EEG responses to different speakers (for example, the attended speaker and the ignored speaker in a competing-speakers scenario) are first characterised by estimating impulse response functions (known as temporal response functions, or TRFs, in the EEG decoding literature) which describe the EEG responses to a feature of speech such as the speech envelope
[
20
]
. The morphology of the TRFs of each speaker can then be examined. A typical envelope TRF exhibits a pronounced negative deflection near
100\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
, followed by a positive deflection near
200\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
- these are the so-called N100 and P200 complexes, respectively
[
20
]
. For ignored speech, the envelope-TRF exhibits a reduced N100 to P200 peak-to-peak amplitude. This peak-to-peak amplitude may then serve as a marker of selective auditory attention
[
21
]
.

Most auditory attention decoding studies make use of EEG signals which were collected from numerous electrodes placed across the scalp of each participant. The EEG electrodes are typically fitted with a headcap and conductive gel in order to achieve a low and stable impedance across the skin-electrode interface. In contrast, the sensor montage employed by any prospective EEG-steered hearing aid must be much more dicrete and easy to set-up. Ideally, it will consist of just a few electrodes, and the device will not rely upon the application of conductive gel, which dries out over the course of several hours and can be difficult to apply properly for inexperienced users.

Currently, ear-centric sensor montages are popular choices for wearable EEG-monitoring devices
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
. It is already established that such ear-centric devices can be used for long-term and discrete auditory monitoring, for example by detecting steady-state or subcortical responses to clicks and tones
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. In the context of auditory attention decoding, two types of ear-EEG devices have been studied: the concealed EEG (cEEG) device, which employs a C-shaped array of around-the-ear electrodes; and in-ear sensors, which sit entirely within the ear canal
[
15
,
16
,
30
]
. In the study by Fiedler
et al.
, the authors found that auditory attention could not be decoded with significance when only in-ear electrodes were used, even in a binaural configuration
[
16
]
. This result is to be expected, since EEG recorded from electrodes with a very small spatial separation has a very low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
[
31
,
32
]
. Instead of using in-ear electrodes exclusively, Fiedler
et al.
proposed to reference the in-ear electrodes to a nearby location on the scalp; the FT7 location was identified as a promising candidate. Qualitatively speaking, the decoding accuracies achieved in that study were similar to those achieved using a binaural cEEG montage in the study by Holtze
et al.

[
30
]
.

In the study of Fiedler
et al.
, the authors followed the usual stimulus-reconstruction approach to decode auditory attention to continuous speech in two-talkers conditions
[
16
]
. However, in place of the commonly-used temporal envelope feature of speech, the authors made use of the onset envelope feature. The onset envelope is the defined as the half-wave rectified derivative of the temporal envelope, and therefore captures the rising edges of the temporal envelope which are predominantly driven by word and syllable onsets
[
33
]
.

In this work, we demonstrate how a dry-contact, wirelessly connected ear-EEG device can be used to decode the focus of a listener’s auditory attention in two-talker competing-speakers scenarios. The sensor configuration is similar to that proposed by Fiedler
et al.
: it consists of two in-ear electrodes, and one adjacent scalp electrode. We adopt a stimulus-reconstruction approach to detect the focus of the listener’s auditory attention, and we provide a comparison of the attention decoding performance when the temporal envelope feature is used, versus when the onset envelope is used.

II
Methods

II-A

Subjects and stimuli

Eighteen young, normal-hearing participants (median age: 23 years; 10 males, 8 females) took part in a selective auditory attention experiment. The experimental protocol was approved by the Imperial College Research Ethics Committee (approval number 19IC5388 No A1; approved 20
th
September 2019). The experiment consisted of 16 trials: during each trial, two audiobooks chapters narrated by a male and a female speaker were presented simultaneously to the participant at a sampling frequency of
44.1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
via Seinheiser HD450 headphones, which were placed over the form factors of the in-ear electrodes. The participant was instructed to attend to either the male speaker or the female speaker, and to ignore the other speaker. On average, each trial lasted for 150 seconds.

The speech material was narrated by the same two speakers for all 16 trials. For each consecutive four trials, the participants were directed to attend to the same speaker, who narrated four consecutive chapters of an audiobook. After each set of four trials, the participants were directed to switch their attention to the other speaker for the next four trials. The speech material was presented diotically (i.e. the narrators were not spatially separated) at a sound pressure level of 75 dB SPL (measured via a Bruel & Kjaer type-4157 ear simulator.)

II-B

EEG data acquisition

During the experiment, the electroencephalograms of the participants was recorded from an ultra-wearable, wirelessly-enabled two-channel ear-EEG device. Our in-house, dry-contact electrodes were inserted into the ear canals of the participants, and a third reference electrode was placed on the left lower-forehead (corresponding to the FT7 location in the standard 10-20 system). A ground electrode was attached to the right earlobe. This recording system therefore acquires EEG from a bilateral (cross-head) channel and a unilateral channel. Accelerometers and microphones are built into the form factors of the insert earphones: signals acquired from these sensors can be used for the purpose of denoising electrophysiological signals, as demonstrated in previous work from our research group
[
34
,
35
]
. The device operates at a sampling frequency of 256 Hz. During the experiment, the mixed audio of the two speech streams was acquired through the in-built microphone (at 256 Hz), and the recorded signal was used to align the original audio signals with the EEG signals, by cross-correlating the recorded signal with the (resampled) original audio.

II-C

Signal pre-processing

We related the EEG recordings to the temporal envelopes and the onset envelopes of each speaker separately. There are several types of temporal envelope which have been explored in the literature; we used the auditory-inspired envelope first proposed by Biesmans
et al

[
36
]
. This envelope is calculated by filtering the audio signals into 28 sub-bands via a gammatone filterbank. The centre frequencies of the filterbank are spaced equidistantly between
50\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and
5\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
on an equivalent rectangular bandwidth scale. The signal in each sub-band is then half-wave rectified, and the resulting signals are averaged together to form a stimulus envelope. As in the study by Fielder
et al.
, the onset envelope feature was formed by half-wave rectifying the first derivative of the temporal envelope feature
[
16
]
.

The EEG signals were preprocessed by first de-trending them via a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
(Type-2 Hamming-sinc FIR filter of order 1691 with a
-6\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
stopband attenuation at
0.25\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
). The EEG and envelope signals were finally resampled to a sampling frequency of
64\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and were standardised by removing the mean of each EEG channel respectively of the envelope signal and by dividing each time series by its standard deviation.

II-D

Forward models

Temporal response functions were estimated for both EEG channels. The TRF is defined as the coefficients of a linear FIR filter which predicts the EEG signals from a given feature of the speech stimuli (either the temporal envelope or the onset envelope of one of the speakers). Ridge regression was employed to estimate the filter coefficients
\theta
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},

(1)

where
\mathbf{y}
is a
T\times 2
vector consisting of the EEG recordings for both channels, with
T
as the number of temporal samples acquired from each channel. The regularisation hyperparameter
\lambda
is improves the stability of the matrix inversion involved in Equation (
1
), at the expense of biasing the estimated coefficients towards the covariance factor
\mathbf{X}^{T}y
.
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix, while
\mathbf{X}
is a
T\times L
Toeplitz matrix, with
L
as the order of the FIR filter. The matrix
\mathbf{X}
was constructed by stacking short temporal segments of the the input speech feature vector
\mathbf{x}
in a sliding-window fashion. Explicitly, the
t^{th}
row of
\mathbf{X}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X_{t}}=[x_{t-m},...,x_{t-1},x_{t},...,x_{t+n}],

(2)

and consists of the
m
samples preceding
x_{t}
until the
n
samples proceeding
x_{t}
. The EEG samples
y_{t}
are estimated from
X_{t}
via the equation
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

Since cortical responses to the speech envelope and its onsets are expected to be causal, the coefficients corresponding to the preceding samples
[t-m,t-m+1,...,t-1]
in Equation (
2
) are expected to be statistically insignificant. For our analysis, we considered lags between -64 and 128 samples. At the chosen sampling rate of
64\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, this corresponds to a temporal response spanning from
1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
before the envelope sample occurs until
2\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
after it has occurred.

The TRFs were fitted using a regularisation parameter of
\lambda=1
. We used a cross-validation procedure to fit the TRFs: of the sixteen trials which were undertaken by each participant, fifteen ‘training trials’ were used to fit a TRF, and one ‘testing trial’ was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit (via Pearson’s correlation coefficient). For each participant, this procedure was repeated 16 times, with each trial used as the testing trial exactly once. We therefore obtained 16 estimates of the TRFs for each of the 18 participants. Finally, we determined grand-average TRFs by taking the average over all participants and testing trials. To study how different components of the TRFs are modulated by selective attention, we formed difference TRFs by subtracting the ignored TRF coefficients from the attended TRF coefficients for each speech feature.

Chance correlations between the noisy EEG and speech-feature signals can result in non-zero TRF coefficients at latencies where there is no true neurobiological response to speech. Null models were fitted in order to estimate the noise level of the TRFs, by applying Equation (
1
) to unrelated EEG and speech signals. Specifically, we performed another cross-validation procedure in which one testing trial was held-out for evaluation. However, the EEG signals for each training trial were paired with speech-feature signals from an unrelated training trial. We formed 100 null models per testing trial and participant in this way. In order to fairly compare the null TRFs with the grand-average non-null TRF, we averaged the null model coefficients over all 16 testing trials and 18 participants.

The significance of the TRF coefficients was determined by comparing them against the distribution of null-TRF coefficients (pooled over all latencies). Two-tailed t-tests were used to compare the coefficients of each grand-averaged TRF against the corresponding null-distribution. A p-value for each coefficient was obtained per TRF, and compared against a significance level of
\alpha=0.05
. The p-values were subsequently corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. The coefficients of the difference TRFs were tested for significance using an identical procedure, except that the variance of the null distribution was given by the sum of the variances of the attended and ignored null distributions.

II-E

Backward models

We also fitted linear stimulus-reconstruction models to predict the temporal envelopes as well as the onset envelopes of speech from the EEG recordings. Since backward models integrate information from all channels, attention decoders based on backward models are usually more accurate than those based on forward models when multi-channel EEG signals are available
[
37
]
. However, the coefficients of a backward model do not allow for straightforward neurobiological interpretations
[
38
]
.

As with the forward models, the backward model coefficients were fitted using ridge regression, by exchanging the meanings of the symbols
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{y}
in Equation
1
:
\mathbf{y}
now represents the
T\times 1
speech-feature vector of either the attended speaker or the ignored speaker, and
\mathbf{X}
is a
T\times LC
matrix, where
C
is the number of EEG channels available (i.e.
C=2
) and, as before,
L
is the order of the FIR filter which is used to predict the speech-feature signal from the EEG signals. We considered only causal backward models with
L=64
: in other words, a one-second-long segment of EEG signals is used to predict the speech-feature sample which occurs at the onset of the segment.

For backward models, the regularisation parameter
\lambda
can have a strong bearing on the final attention decoding accuracy
[
17
]
. During training, we determined estimates of the optimal value of this parameter using a nested cross-validation procedure: for each of the 18 participants, we held out one of 16 trials for the purpose of testing the final trained model. To determine
\lambda
, we held out a second (validation) trial, and the remaining 14 trials were used to train backward models using 19 values of
\lambda
which ranged from
10^{-9}
to
10{{}^{9}}
(spaced equidistantly on a logarithmic scale). The optimal value of
\lambda
was taken to be the one which yielded the highest mean reconstruction score, where the mean was taken over all 15 possible validation trials. The models trained using this value of
\lambda
were evaluated using the (so-far unseen) testing trial.

We fitted backward models for the attended speaker and the ignored speaker separately. We allowed the attended and ignored models to have different values of
\lambda
. Therefore, for each speaker and speech-feature (either the temporal envelope of speech or its onsets), we determined 16 correlation coefficients per participant using the cross-validation procedure.

Since EEG signals have a characteristically low SNR, the correlation coefficients achieved by backward models are usually not that large in magnitude. Therefore, it is important to determine a null distribution for the correlation coefficients, so that the values achieved by the backward models may be properly evaluated against the chance of achieving those values at random. Null backward models were obtained in an identical fashion to the null forward models: for each participant, one of the 16 testing trials was heldout for evaluation. The EEG signals from each training trial was paired with the speech-feature signals from an unrelated training trial. The set of correlation coefficients obtained on the testing trials therefore formed an empirical null distribution of reconstruction scores. A total of 100 null backward models were fitted per testing trial and participant.

II-F

Auditory attention decoding

To perform auditory attention decoding, we used the backward models trained to reconstruct features of the attended speech stream. The reconstructed features were correlated against the ground-truth features of both the attended speech stream as well as the ignored speech stream. The correlation coefficients served as markers of selective auditory attention, with the larger correlation coefficient indicating that the corresponding speech stream is more likely to be attended.

The correlation coefficients are noisy attention markers. When more temporal samples are used to calculate the correlation coefficients, the markers become more robust. However, when long time series are used to obtain robust attention markers, a prospective smart hearing aid will not be able to promptly detect when the user has switched their attention between different speakers.

In order to characterise the relationship between the length of the timeseries used to determine the attention markers and the corresponding decoding accuracy, we divided the testing trials into non-overlapping windows of various sizes during cross-validation, and we performed auditory attention decoding using the markers derived from each window. The decoding accuracy was then determined as the proportion of windows for which the decoder correctly identified the attended speech stream. Note that since we used non-overlapping windows, there were many more decoding opportunities when a short window versus when a long window was used. In order to establish the chance level of a random classifier for each window size, we determined the 95% confidence interval of a binomial distribution which was parameterised by
n
, the total number of windows available, as well as by
p=0.5
, the probability of obtaining a single correct classification decision.

III
Results

III-A

Forward models

We determined the grand average temporal response functions which relate the EEG recordings to either the attended speech stream or the ignored speech stream. We represented each speech stream by its temporal envelope, as well as its onset envelope. The TRFs shown in Figure
1
exhibit morphologies which are typical of TRFs obtained in higher-density EEG studies, with clear and significant components around latencies of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
and
200\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
. The onset envelope TRFs also show pronounced activity at a much later latency of around
500\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
.

To examine how the various TRF components are modulated by selective attention, we formed ‘difference TRFs’ by subtracting the ignored-speaker TRFs from the attended-speaker TRFs. The bottom row of Figure
1
shows the difference TRFs. For the envelope feature, the amplitude of the first two peaks of the unilateral-channel TRF are significantly modulated by selective attention. For the onset envelope feature, the first positive peak of both the unilateral and bilateral channel TRFs is significantly modulated by selective attention. In contrast, there is no significant attentional modulation of the later components.

When interpreting temporal response functions, it is important to check that the corresponding forward models yield accurate predictions of the EEG signals: this is an additional way to check that the TRFs are capturing meaningful aspects of EEG responses to speech. During cross-validation, the predicted EEG signals were compared to the observed EEG signals using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. We pooled all the correlation coefficients from all test folds and participants, and performed statistical tests to compare their mean to the correlation coefficients achieved by the null models. Only the bilateral onset-envelope TRF for the ignored speaker yielded an insignificant difference at a singificance level of
\alpha=0.05
(
p=0.36
; single-tailed unpaired t-tests with FDR correction for multiple comparisons.)

III-B

Backward models

Stimulus reconstruction models were fitted to predict the speech features of either the attended or the ignored speakers from the two-channel EEG signals. The reconstruction scores of these models, as assessed through Pearson’s correlation coefficient, are shown in Figure
2
. When compared to the reconstruction scores achieved by the null models, the backward model achieved scores which were significantly greater (all
p\ll 0.0001
; all single-tailed unpaired t-testes).

Fig. 2:
Reconstruction scores (Pearson correlation coefficients) achieved by linear backward models which were trained to predict the temporal envelope of speech (red), as well as the onset envelope (blue), from EEG recordings. Separate models were fitted for attended and ignored speakers. Each data point represents the correlation coefficient achieved for a particular trial and participant (there were 16 trials and 18 participants.)

The features of the attended speech stream were more accurately reconstructed than those of the ignored speech stream (comparison between scores achieved by the attended model which reconstructs the envelope of the attended speech stream, and the ignored model which reconstructs the envelope of the ignored speech stream:
{p=${10}^{-15}$}
; comparison between attended and ignored models when predicting the onset envelope feature:
{p=${10}^{-31}$}
; single-tailed paired t-tests). Therefore, the reconstruction scores for each speech stream may serve as markers of selective attention. The attended model that reconstructs the envelope of the attended speech stream achieves greater reconstruction scores than the corresponding onset envelope model (
{p=${10}^{-5}$}
, single-tailed paired t-test); however, the reconstruction scores for the onset envelope models exhibit much less variability (comparison between attended models predicting features of the attended speech stream:
{p=${10}^{-23}$}
, two-tailed F-test). The accuracy of an auditory attention decoder based on stimulus reconstruction depends on the trade-off between the variability (or noisiness) of the attention markers and their overall magnitudes.

III-C

Auditory attention decoding

The stimulus-reconstruction approach was used to identify the focus of the participant’s auditory attention. Only the backward models trained to reconstruct features of the attended speech stream were used: the reconstructed envelopes and onset envelopes produced by these models were correlated against the corresponding ground-truth features of both the attended and ignored speech streams. The correlation coefficients achieved for the attended and ignored speech streams are compared in Figure
3
, for both the envelope feature and the onset envelope feature. In both cases, the reconstructions are usually more highly correlated with the features of the attended speech stream than with those of the ignored speech stream. There is a moderate correlation of
R=0.43
between the reconstruction scores of the attended and ignored speech streams for the onset envelope feature. For the temporal envelope feature, this correlation is much more mild (
R=0.08
.)

The difference in the correlation coefficients (attended correlation minus ignored correlation) was taken to indicate the focus of the participants’ selective auditory attention: if this quantity was positive, then the decoder had correctly identified the focus of a particular participant’s attention; if it was negative then the decoder had incorrectly identified the attended speaker. Auditory attention decoding was performed by dividing each testing trial into non-overlapping segments. Correlation coefficients between the reconstructed feature and the ground truth features were calculated for every segment, and the accuracy of the attention decoder was then defined as the proportion of times that the attended speaker was correctly identified.

The differences in correlation coefficients for individual participants (with a
10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
segment length) are shown on the left of Figure
4
, alongside the corresponding null attention markers. The attention markers were compared to the null markers using single-tailed unpaired t-tests, and resulting p-values were FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons. For 16 of the 18 participants, the attention markers were significantly greater than the null markers.

The accuracies of decoders based on envelope-reconstruction, as well as onset-envelope reconstruction, are shown to right in Figure
4
. Even for very short segment durations, both decoders perform better than a random classifier by a large margin. For longer segment durations, the onset-envelope-based decoder performs considerably better than the envelope-based decoder. Overall, the relationship between the decoding accuracy and the segment length approximately follows a square-root relationship for both decoders: this can be explained by the fact that the variability of the correlation coefficients themselves follow an inverse-square-root relationship owing to the central limit theorem.

IV
Discussion

This work demonstrates that a novel, ultra-wearable ear-EEG device can be used to decode the focus of a listener’s selective auditory attention in competing-speakers scenarios.

The temporal response functions achieved using signals obtained from this device exhibit morphologies which are similar to those already reported in the literature. The TRFs which describe responses to the onset envelopes show strong activation at a latency of around
500\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{s}
, which was not reported in the study by Fiedler
et al.

[
16
]
. These late TRF components are primarily driven by very low-frequency cortical entrainment to word onsets; the analysis of Fiedler
et al.
is mostly insensitive to this aspect of neural processing, since the authors utilised a bandpass filter with a lower cutoff frequency of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, whereas we used a lower cutoff frequency of
0.5\text{\,}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
. Otherwise, our results are highly compatible with that study, with the TRFs demonstrating clear and significant attentional modulation in the early components.

Linear backward models were used to perform stimulus reconstruction. The results demonstrate that the features of the attended speech stream could be more accurately reconstructed than those of the ignored speech, as measured through Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The correlation coefficients could therefore be used as attention markers. The temporal envelope of the attended speaker could be reconstructed significantly better than the corresponding onset envelope. However, this result did not translate into higher attention decoding accuracies when the temporal envelope was used instead of the onset envelope. This is due to the fact that the attention markers derived from the onset envelopes were less variable (i.e. less noisy) than those derived from the temporal speech envelopes. Evidently, the variability of the chosen attentional markers is an important consideration for future research. Some authors have proposed the use of state-space models as a secondary module on top of stimulus-reconstruction. In these studies, simple models of attentional dynamics are employed to estimate and reduce the influence of noise
[
39
,
40
]
. Future works which concern auditory attention decoding via stimulus reconstruction should aim to report on the variability of the attention markers, or on relevant derived quantities such as the coefficient of variation.

On the subject level, meaningful markers of selective auditory attention could be extracted from 17 of the 18 participants when
10\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
segments of EEG were used. On the population level, promising attention-decoding accuracies could be achieved, even when short EEG segments were used. Nevertheless, for incorporation into practical hearing aids, the attention decoding accuracies require improvement. Future work needs to establish the minimum accuracy that would be required for a listener to receive a perceptual benefit from such a device. The use of deep learning for improving attention decoding accuracies has a good track record in scalp-EEG based studies of auditory attention, and the benefits seen in those studies would presumably translate across to wearable EEG studies
[
41
,
42
,
43
