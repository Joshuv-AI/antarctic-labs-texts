On the Information Leakage in Private Information Retrieval Systems
Tao Guo, Ruida Zhou, Chao Tian

Abstract
We consider information leakage to the user in private information retrieval (PIR) systems. Information leakage can be measured in terms of individual message leakage or total leakage. Individual message leakage, or simply individual leakage, is defined as the amount of information that the user can obtain on any individual message that is not being requested, and the total leakage is defined as the amount of information that the user can obtain about all the other messages except the one being requested. In this work, we characterize the tradeoff between the minimum download cost and the individual leakage, and that for the total leakage, respectively. Coding schemes are proposed to achieve these optimal tradeoffs, which are also shown to be optimal in terms of the message size. We further characterize the optimal tradeoff between the minimum amount of common randomness and the total leakage. Moreover, we show that under individual leakage, common randomness is in fact unnecessary when there are more than two messages.



I
Introduction

The problem of
private information retrieval
(PIR)
[
1
,
2
]
addresses the retrieval of one out of
K
messages from
N
replicated databases,
without revealing the identity of the desired message to any individual database.
A trivial solution is to simply retrieve all the messages, however, the download cost in this solution is unrealistic. Therefore,
in the PIR problem, the goal is to find an efficient protocol, i.e., with the minimum download cost, to privately retrieve the desired message.
The capacity of a PIR system is defined as the maximum number of bits of desired message that can be retrieved per bit of downloaded information,
which was shown in
[
3
]
to be
C_{\text{PIR}}=\left(1+1/N+1/N^{2}+\cdots+1/N^{K-1}\right)^{-1}
. There were also significant interests recently on various other variations of the PIR problem; see, e.g.,

[

.

The problem considered in this work is closely related to the
symmetric
private information retrieval (SPIR) problem
[
5
]
,
where “symmetric” refers to the fact that both user privacy and database privacy need to be preserved.
Database privacy requires that the user obtains no information on other messages beyond the requested message; strictly speaking, this is a security requirement rather than a privacy requirement, and we shall refer to it as such in the sequel.
It is well understood that SPIR can also be viewed as a multi-database oblivious transfer problem
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
It was shown in
[
5
]
that to ensure perfect security,
the databases need to share
common randomness
(a common key) which is independent of the messages and only available to the databases.
The common key is used for randomizing the answers such that no information about the non-desired messages is leaked.
The capacity of SPIR was shown to be
C_{\text{SPIR}}=1-1/N=\left(1+1/N+1/N^{2}+\cdots+1/N^{\infty}\right)^{-1}
,
as long as the amount of common randomness is at least
\frac{1}{N-1}
bits per desired message bit.

In practice, the requirement of perfect security may be too stringent. For example, in the SPIR setting, it is likely to be acceptable for a user to obtain a single byte of information in a 1-hour video data (assuming the message is a video) that he does not request, i.e., leaking a very small amount of information
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
.
This is the type of systems that we wish to understand in this work. Allowing a small amount of information leakage let us control the system security level in a finer grain manner. In this context, SPIR essentially requires strictly zero information leakage, while the classical PIR does not have a security constraint at all. Thus our goal is to understand the tradeoff between the download cost and the amount of information leakage in the regime other than these two extreme cases.
From the perspective of the oblivious transfer, we are essentially considering a generalized multi-database oblivious transfer setting
[
32
]
, where one side of the privacy requirements (that corresponding to the database privacy) is allowed to be less than perfect.

Two different notions of information leakage can be defined: the individual leakage is defined as the amount of information that the user can obtain about any individual non-desired message, and the total leakage as the amount of information that the user can obtain about all the non-desired messages. The former is similar to the weak security constraints in the literature
[
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, while the latter corresponds to the standard strong security constraints.
In practice, the weak security requirement may be more suitable than the strong version in some applications. For example, when the messages are video sequences, the user should not obtain information about any individual video segment, but obtaining the binary XOR of two video sequences may not be an issue since it will not lead to a meaningfully decodable video sequence, and thus enforcing that the user cannot obtain this XOR may be too stringent. Moreover, a protocol with a weak security constraint can potentially be implemented more efficiently in practical settings and may not require common keys.

The main result of this work is the characterizations of the optimal tradeoffs between the download cost and the amount of information leakage for both individual leakage and total leakage. By adapting the codes given in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, we provide code constructions that can achieve these optimal tradeoffs; moreover, they are also shown to have the minimum message sizes. For the individual leakage case, the constructed codes do not require common randomness (unless there are only two messages). At the extreme case with perfect individual message security, the download cost is in fact the same as that with perfect total security. This is rather reassuring since it implies that the stronger security requirement does not induce any additional download cost.

A related problem, namely weakly private information retrieval, was studied in
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
. For weakly private information retrieval systems, a small amount of privacy leakage is allowed, i.e., the leakage is to the server and on the requested message index, and it differs from the setting we are considering where the information leakage is to the user and on the message content.

II
Preliminaries

II-A

Problem Statement

For positive integers
K,N
, let
[1:K]\triangleq\{1,2,\cdots,K\}
and
[1:N]\triangleq\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
.
For any
k\in[1:K]
, define the complement set by
\bar{k}\triangleq\{1,2,\cdots,K\}\backslash\{k\}
.

In a
private information retrieval
(PIR) system, there are
K
independent messages
W_{1:K}=(W_{1},W_{2},\cdots,W_{K})
,
each of which is comprised of
L
i.i.d. symbols uniformly distributed over a finite alphabet
\mathcal{X}
.
In the sequel, we will use
\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}
-ary units, instead of bits, as the information measure, unless specified otherwise. We have

\displaystyle H(W_{1:K})=H(W_{1})+H(W_{2})+\cdots+H(W_{K}),

(1)

\displaystyle H(W_{1})=H(W_{2})=\cdots=H(W_{K})=L.

(2)

There are a total of
N
databases, each of which stores all the messages
W_{1:K}
.
A user aims to retrieve a message
W_{k},~k\in[1:K]
from the
N
databases
without revealing the identity
k
of the desired message to any individual database.
An independent random key
\mathbf{F}
is used to generate queries
Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=\left(Q_{1}^{[k]},Q_{2}^{[k]},\cdots,Q_{N}^{[k]}\right)
, i.e.,

H(Q_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F})=0,~\forall k\in[1:K],

(3)

where
Q_{n}^{[k]}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
for
n\in[1:N]
.
For
n\in[1:N]
, the
n
-th query
Q_{n}^{[k]}
is sent to the
n
-th database.
Since we wish to protect the non-desired messages from information leakage, the databases may need to share a common random key
S\in\mathcal{S}
that is not accessible to the user, which induces the condition

H(W_{1:K},\mathbf{F},S)=H(W_{1:K})+H(\mathbf{F})+H(S).

(4)

Upon receiving
Q_{n}^{[k]}
, the
n
-th database generates an answer
A_{n}^{[k]}
from the query
Q_{n}^{[k]}
, the stored messages
W_{1:K}
, and the common key
S
, i.e.,

A_{n}^{[k]}=\varphi_{n}(Q_{n}^{[k]},W_{1:K},S),~\forall k\in[1:K],~\forall n\in[1:N],

(5)

which implies

\displaystyle H(A_{n}^{[k]}|Q_{n}^{[k]},W_{1:K},S)=0,~\forall k\in[1:K],\forall n\in[1:N].

(6)

The answer symbols are from a finite alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,
A_{n}^{[k]}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\ell_{n}}
, where
\ell_{n}
is the length of the answer.
Using all the answers
A_{1:N}^{[k]}=\left(A_{1}^{[k]},A_{2}^{[k]},\cdots,A_{N}^{[k]}\right)
from the
N
databases and the values of
\mathbf{F}
and
k
, the user perfectly decodes the desired message
W_{k}
, which further implies that

H(W_{k}|A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=0.

(7)

To satisfy the privacy requirement of keeping the desired message index private to any one of the databases,
the received queries should be identically distributed, i.e.,

Q_{n}^{[k]}\sim Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]},~\forall k,k^{\prime}\in[1:K],~\forall n\in[1:N].

(8)

Since
W_{1:K}
,
S
, and
\mathbf{F}
are independent, in light of (
3
), we have

\displaystyle(Q_{n}^{[k]},W_{1:K},S)\sim(Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]},W_{1:K},S),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall k,k^{\prime}\in[1:K],~\forall n\in[1:N].

(9)

Since
A_{n}^{[k]}
is a deterministic function of
(Q_{n}^{[k]},W_{1:K},S)
, the following identical distribution constraint must also hold

\displaystyle(Q_{n}^{[k]},A_{n}^{[k]},W_{1:K},S)\sim(Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]},A_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]},W_{1:K},S),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall k,k^{\prime}\in[1:K],~\forall n\in[1:N].

(10)

In contrast to the perfect security of the non-desired messages
W_{\bar{k}}=(W_{1},\cdots,W_{k-1},W_{k+1},\cdots,W_{K})
in
[
5
]
,
we allow information leakage in this work. Define the total leakage as
I(W_{\bar{k}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})
,
which is the amount of information that the user can obtain about all the non-desired messages.
Define the individual message leakage for message
k^{\prime}\in[1:K]~(k^{\prime}\neq k)
as
I(W_{k^{\prime}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})
,
which is the amount of information that the user can obtain about the individual non-desired message-
k^{\prime}
.
The total leakage and the individual leakage in the systems are constrained as follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{\bar{k}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\leq s,~\forall k\in[1:K]

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{k^{\prime}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\leq w,~\forall k^{\prime}\neq k\in[1:K],

(12)

where the parameters
s
and
w
are used to indicate the strong security requirement and the weak security requirement, respectively.
For
K=2
, since
I(W_{\bar{k}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=I(W_{k^{\prime}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})
, the total leakage constraint is equivalent to the individual leakage constraint.

In a PIR system, the message size is defined as the number of bits to represent each individual message, which is clearly
L\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|
in our notation.
The download cost is defined as

D\triangleq\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(\ell_{n}),

(13)

where the expectation is taken over the set
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
of all possible queries.
Note that
D
is a deterministic function of queries and query distribution, but neither the particular realization of messages nor the choice of desired message index
k
.
The amount of common randomness is normalized by the message length
L
as

\rho\triangleq\frac{H(S)}{L}.

(14)

II-B

Analysis of Several Existing PIR Codes

Before presenting our main result, we provide the result of a simple analysis on the capacity-achieving PIR code given by Sun and Jafar
[
3
]
, the capacity-achieving code proposed by Tian et al.
[
4
]
, and the SPIR code
[
5
]
; these three classes of codes will be referred to as the SJ code, the TSC code, and the SPIR code, respectively.

•

The SJ code
[
3
]
:

\displaystyle L=N^{K};~D=\frac{N(N^{K}-1)}{N-1};

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{\bar{k}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{1}{N-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}\right);

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{1}{N^{K-1}},~\forall k^{\prime}\neq k\in[1:K].

(17)

•

The TSC code
[
4
]
:

\displaystyle L=N-1;~D=\frac{N^{K}-1}{N^{K-1}};

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{\bar{k}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{1}{N-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}\right);

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{1}{N^{K-1}},~\forall k^{\prime}\neq k\in[1:K].

(20)

•

The SPIR code
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle L=N-1;~D=N;~\rho=\frac{1}{N-1}.

(21)

From (
16
), (
19
) and (
17
), (
20
), it is seen that the SJ code and TSC code have the same performance in terms of both the total leakage and the individual leakage.
As an example, consider the case
N=3,~K=3
, then
\frac{1}{L}I(W_{\bar{k}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},Q_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{4}{9}
and
\frac{1}{L}I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]},Q_{1:N}^{[k]},\mathbf{F})=\frac{1}{9}
for both the SJ code and the TSC code.

Total Leakage

The following theorem characterizes the optimal tradeoff between the minimum download cost
D_{\min}

s

For notational convenience, define

D_{\min}^{0},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

\left(1+\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{N^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N^{\infty}}\right)^{-1}=1-\frac{1}{N}
.

III-C

Minimum Message Size

The message size
L\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|
is also an important factor to consider in practice
[
4
]
.
The minimum message size is highly dependent on the download cost.
It was shown in
[
4
]
that the message size of capacity-achieving PIR code is greater than or equal to
N-1

if the code is uniformly decomposable.
Before stating our results on the message size, we first define the notion of uniformly decomposable code, similar to that in
[
4
]
.

A PIR code is called
decomposable
, if
\mathcal{Y}
is a finite Abelian group, and for each
n\in[1:N]
and
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}
,
the answer
A_{n}^{[k]}=\varphi_{n}(q,W_{1:K},S)
can be written in the form

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}(q,W_{1:K},S)=\left(\varphi_{n,1}^{(q)}(W_{1:K},S),\cdots,\varphi_{n,\ell_{n}}^{(q)}(W_{1:K},S)\right)

(32)

where for
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,\ell_{n}\}
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{n,i}^{(q)}(W_{1:K},S)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{n,i,1}^{(q)}(W_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{n,i,K}^{(q)}(W_{K})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\oplus\varphi_{n,i,K+1}^{(q)}(S),

(33)

where
\oplus
denotes addition in the finite group
\mathcal{Y}
,
and each
\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}
(
k\in[1:K]
) is a mapping
\mathcal{X}^{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
and
\varphi_{n,i,K+1}^{(q)}
is a mapping
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
.

Furthermore, a decomposable code is called
uniform
if any component function
\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}
in (
33
) either satisfies the conditions

\displaystyle\left|\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}^{L}\!:\!\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}(x)=y\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}^{L}\!:\!\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}(x)=y^{\prime}\right\}\right|,

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pty,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},~k\in[1:K],

(34)

and

\displaystyle\left|\left\{x\in\mathcal{S}:\varphi_{n,i,K+1}^{(q)}(x)=y\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{x\in\mathcal{S}:\varphi_{n,i,K+1}^{(q)}(x)=y^{\prime}\right\}\right|,

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pty,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},

(35)

or it maps everything to the same value, i.e.,

\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}(x)=\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}(x^{\prime}),~x,x^{\prime}\in\begin{cases}\mathcal{X}^{L},&\text{if }k\in[1:K]\\
\mathcal{S},&\text{if }k=K+1.\end{cases}

(36)

The terminology “decomposable” refers to the property in (
33
) that each coded symbol is a summation of the component functions on each individual message.
A uniformly decomposable code has an additional property that each mapping
\varphi_{n,i,k}^{(q)}
either preserves the uniform probability distribution or induces a deterministic value.
The notion of decomposable codes considerably generalizes the notion of linear codes. In particular, linear codes on finite fields are uniformly decomposable.

The following lemma and theorem are our main results on the minimum message size.

Lemma 1
.

The minimum message length of any Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable PIR code with either individual leakage constraint
w
or total leakage constraint
s
should be a multiple of
\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
.

Proof.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 6
.

The lemma shows that the minimum
L
is a multiple of
\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
. However, the term
\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
is not necessarily an integer.

We can see from the Pareto optimal code constructions in
Sections

IV
and
V
that for most cases, we can simultaneously achieve a message length of
N-1
and the minimum amount of common randomness characterized in
Theorems

1
and
2
.
The only exception is the individual leakage case with
K\geq 3
and
|\mathcal{X}|=N=2
, for which the message length is twice the minimum.
Except for this case, we characterize the minimum message size in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Except for the case of
K\geq 3,|\mathcal{X}|=N=2
, the minimum message size of any Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable PIR codes achieving the minimum amount of common random randomness with either individual leakage constraint
w
or total leakage constraint
s
is
(N-1)\log_{2}|\mathcal{Y}|
; in particular, it achieves
N-1
when
|\mathcal{Y}|=|\mathcal{X}|=2
.

Proof.

Except for the case of
K\geq 3,|\mathcal{X}|=N=2
, we have designed Pareto optimal codes achieving simultaneously
the minimum amount of common random randomness and the minimum message size.
Thus, we only need to prove the lower bound
L\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|\geq(N-1)\log_{2}|\mathcal{Y}|
, which is equivalent to
L\geq(N-1)\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
.
The details can be found in Appendix
B
.
∎

Remark 7
.

It is remarkable that the minimum message size of the Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable PIR codes with leakage constraints
w
or
s
is equal to
N-1
, which is the same as that of optimal uniformly decomposable classical PIR codes without security constraints.

Proof of
Theorem

1

We prove
Theorem

1
by showing the achievability, converse, and the minimum key size in the following.

IV-A

Achievability

Following the analysis of Fig.
2
, we only need to prove the theorem for
s\in[0,s_{t}]
.
Consider a message length of
L=N-1
.
In addition to the query generation in (
3
), now the random key
\mathbf{F}
generates one more indicator bit
F_{0}\in\{0,1\}
,
according to probability
P(F_{0}=0)=\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}\in[0,1]
and
P(F_{0}=1)=1-\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}
.
Note that the probability of
F_{0}
is chosen according to the coding strategy so that the information leakage is within the constraint
s
.

For
x,y\in[1:N]
, it is useful to define the operation
(x+y)_{N}
by

(x+y)_{N}=\begin{cases}x+y,&\text{if }x+y\leq N\\
x+y-N,&\text{if }x+y>N.\end{cases}

(39)

And similarly,

(x-y)_{N}=\begin{cases}x-y,&\text{if }x-y>0\\
x-y+N,&\text{if }x-y\leq 0.\end{cases}

(40)

For random key
\mathbf{F}=(F_{0},F_{1},F_{2},\cdots,F_{K-1})\in\{0,1\}\times[1:N]^{K-1}
, let
F^{*}\triangleq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}F_{i}\right)_{N}
.
Then the query for the
n
-th database is generated as

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{[k]}=\left(F_{0},F_{1},\cdots,F_{k-1},(n-F^{*})_{N},F_{k},\cdots,F_{K-1}\right),

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102ptn\in[1:N],k\in[1:K].

(41)

Since the query is a length-
(K+1)
vector, we can denote it by
Q_{n,0:K}^{[k]}
.
Upon receiving the queries, the databases generate the answers using the TSC code for
F_{0}=0
, and SPIR code for
F_{0}=1
.
Specifically, the answer is

A_{n}^{[k]}=\begin{cases}W_{1,Q_{n,1}^{[k]}}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{K,Q_{n,K}^{[k]}},&\hskip-5.69046pt\text{if }Q_{n,0}^{[k]}=0\\
W_{1,Q_{n,1}^{[k]}}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{K,Q_{n,K}^{[k]}}\oplus S,&\hskip-5.69046pt\text{if }Q_{n,0}^{[k]}=1,\end{cases}

(42)

where
S
is the common randomness shared among all databases.
The retrieval and privacy requirements are easily seen from the TSC and SPIR codes.
Then we can check the code performances as follows.

1.

Information leakage: in view of the performance of TSC code in (
18
) and (
19
),
the overall normalized total leakage is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}I(W_{\bar{k}};Q_{1:N}^{[k]},A_{1:N}^{[k]})

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}\cdot\frac{1}{N-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left[1-\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}\right]\cdot 0

(43)

\displaystyle=s,

(44)

which is the total leakage constraint.

2.

Download cost: by (
18
) and (
21
), we have

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}\cdot\frac{N^{K}-1}{N^{K-1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left[1-\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}\right]\cdot N

(45)

\displaystyle=N-\frac{N-1}{N^{K-1}-1}s

(46)

\displaystyle=L\cdot\left(\frac{N}{N-1}-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}-1}s\right),

(47)

which is the minimum download cost in (
24
).

3.

Amount of common randomness: since common randomness is only used in the SPIR code, we derive from (
21
) that

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1-\frac{N^{K-1}(N-1)s}{N^{K-1}-1}}{L}=\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{N^{K-1}}{N^{K-1}-1}s,

(48)

which is equal to
\rho_{\min}^{\text{s}}
in (
23
) of
Theorem

1
.

This proves the achievability of
Theorem

1
for
s\in[0,s_{t}]
.

Remark 8
.

The code is obtained by combining TSC code and SPIR code with certain probabilities,
which is accomplished by sending one more query bit as an indicator.
This is different from time-sharing which uses sub-codes at a fraction of time.
Our method i) takes advantage of the fact that the two codes have the same message size,
and the message size of the combined code remains the same, which is
N-1
here;
ii) achieves the desired leakage and download cost in expectation, while time-sharing achieves the same information leakage and download cost in a deterministic manner;
iii) outperforms the time-sharing approach in the sense that time-sharing may require a large message size,
which limits the applicability of the code, while the proposed scheme always uses a message size of
N-1
.

IV-B

Converse

Similar to the converse proof in
[
3
]
, we first lower-bound the entropy of answers and messages iteratively by the message length
L
.
Then the download cost
D
and information leakage
s
is proved to be greater than or equal to this lower bound, which gives the relation of
D
,
s
, and
L
.
The converse in
[
3
]
lower-bounds
D
by
L
, while the converse proof here needs to lower-bound
D
in terms of both
L
and
s
.
In a sense, this is a more general version of the proof in
[
3
]
.

We first present an iterative lemma that will be used later.

Lemma 2
.

For all
k\in[1:K]
,

NH(A_{1:N}^{[k]},W_{1:k}|\mathbf{F})\geq H(A_{1:N}^{[k+1]},W_{1:k+1}|\mathbf{F})+k(N-1)L.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.
∎

Applying
Lemma

2
repeatedly for
k=1,2,\cdots,K-1
, we have

\displaystyle N^{K-1}H(A^{[1]}_{1:N}W_{1}|\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\geq N^{K-2}H(A^{[2]}_{1:N}W_{1:2}|\mathbf{F})+N^{K-2}(N-1)L

(49)

\displaystyle\geq N^{K-3}H(A^{[3]}_{1:N}W_{1:3}|\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\qquad+N^{K-2}(N-1)L+2N^{K-3}(N-1)L

(50)

\displaystyle\qquad\vdots

(51)

\displaystyle\geq H(A^{[K]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}iN^{K-1-i}\right)(N-1)L

(52)

\displaystyle=H(A^{[K]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}-(K-1)\right)L.

(53)

By symmetry, for any
k,k^{\prime}\in[1:K]
and
k\neq k^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle N^{K-1}H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\geq H(A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}-(K-1)\right)L.

(54)

To obtain a lower bound on download cost
D=\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(\ell_{n})
(c.f. (
13
)), consider

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(\ell_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1:N}}\Pr(Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N})\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{n}\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|

(55)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1:N}}\Pr(Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N})\sum_{n=1}^{N}H(A_{n}^{(q_{n})})

(56)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1:N}}\Pr(Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N})H(A_{1}^{(q_{1})},\cdots,A_{N}^{(q_{N})})

(57)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1:N}}\!\!\!\Pr(Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N})H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N})

(58)

\displaystyle=H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F}).

(59)

Then we derive the following,

\displaystyle\left(N^{K-1}-1\right)D+I(W_{\bar{k}};A^{[k]}_{1:N}Q^{[k]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\geq\left(N^{K-1}-1\right)H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F})+I(W_{\bar{k}};A^{[k]}_{1:N}|\mathbf{F})

(60)

\displaystyle=\left(N^{K-1}-1\right)H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\quad+\left[H(W_{\bar{k}})+H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F})-H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})\right]

(61)

\displaystyle=\left[(N^{K-1}-1)H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F})+H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+(K-1)L-H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})

(62)

\displaystyle=N^{K-1}H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F})+(K-1)L-H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})

(63)

\displaystyle\geq\left[H(A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}-(K-1)\right)L\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+(K-1)L-H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})

(64)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}\right)L+\left[H(A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})-H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})\right],

(65)

where (
63
) follows from

H(A_{1:N}^{[k]}|\mathbf{F})=H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}Q^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F})=H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{k}|\mathbf{F}),

(66)

and (
64
) follows from (
54
) for any
k^{\prime}\in[1:K]
and
k^{\prime}\neq k
.
Similarly, by switching
k
and
k^{\prime}
in (
65
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(N^{K-1}-1\right)D+I(W_{\bar{k^{\prime}}};A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}Q^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}\right)L+\left[H(A^{[k]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})-H(A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}W_{1:K}|\mathbf{F})\right].

(67)

Summing up (
65
) and (
67
), we have

\displaystyle 2\left(N^{K-1}-1\right)D+I(W_{\bar{k}};A^{[k]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})+I(W_{\bar{k^{\prime}}};A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle\geq 2\left(\sum_{i=1}^{K-1}N^{i}\right)L.

(68)

Thus the lower bound on
D
is

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\geq\frac{N}{N-1}L

\displaystyle~~-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}-1}\frac{I(W_{\bar{k}};A^{[k]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})+I(W_{\bar{k^{\prime}}};A^{[k^{\prime}]}_{1:N}\mathbf{F})}{2}

\displaystyle\geq L\cdot\left(\frac{N}{N-1}-\frac{1}{N^{K-1}-1}\cdot s\right),

which matches the value of

for

in (

) of
Theorem

.
For

\displaystyle 2(N^{K-1}-1)H(S)+N^{K-1}\left[I(W_{\bar{k}};A_{1:N}^{[k]}\mathbf{F})+I(W_{\bar{k^{\prime}}};A_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]}\mathbf{F})\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{N^{K-1}}{N^{K-1}-1}\frac{I(W_{\bar{k}};A_{1:N}^{[k]}\mathbf{F})+I(W_{\bar{k^{\prime}}};A_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]}\mathbf{F})}{2}

Optimal code for WS-PIR

Even though the SPIR code in

it does not always achieve the minimum amount of common randomness for WS-PIR.

For

, (

We consider the following three cases:

i)

|\mathcal{X}|\geq 3
and

ii)

|\mathcal{X}|=2
and

;

iii)

|\mathcal{X}|=N=2

Next, we propose an optimal code for WS-PIR that uses the sum of all message symbols as encryption key shared by databases and no extra randomness is needed.
The code design is simply to modify TSC code by adding the shared encryption key (sum of all message symbols) to each of the answers.

Case i):
The code has a message length of
L=N-1
.
Specifically, by appending dummy variables
W_{k,N}=0

W_{k}
can be written as

(84)

Let the random key
\mathbf{F}
of the user be chosen from
[1:N]^{K-1}
which gives

\mathbf{F}=(F_{1},F_{2},\cdots,F_{K-1}).

(85)

For
x,y\in[1:N]
, the operations
(x+y)_{N}
and
(x-y)_{N}
are defined by (
39
) and (
40

Let

.
For

and

(86)

The sum of all message symbols is denoted by
S
, which is

(87)

Upon receiving the query
Q_{n}^{[k]}
, the
n

A_{n}^{[k]}
using
Q_{n}^{[k]}
as linear combination indexes of all the message symbols.
We further add the encryption key
S
to each answer and obtain that

\displaystyle A_{n}^{[k]}

\displaystyle=W_{1,F_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{k-1,F_{k-1}}\oplus W_{k,(n-F^{*})_{N}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\oplus W_{k+1,F_{k}}\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{K,F_{K-1}}\oplus S.

(88)

For simplicity, we define

B\!=\!W_{1,F_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\!W_{k-1,F_{k-1}}\!\oplus\!W_{k+1,F_{k}}\!\oplus\cdots\oplus W_{K,F_{K-1}},

(89)

where
\oplus
denotes the addition operation in finite group
\mathcal{X}
(this is an abuse of notation without ambiguity).
Substituting (
89
) into (
88
), we have

A_{n}^{[k]}=W_{k,(n-F^{*})_{N}}\oplus B\oplus S.

(90)

The user receives all the answers from databases, i.e.,

Then we see that

\displaystyle W_{k,(n-F^{*})_{N}}=A_{n}^{[k]}\ominus A_{F^{*}}^{[k]}=A_{n}^{[k]}\ominus(B\oplus S),

can be recovered by ranging

from

to

Since
\mathbf{F}
is chosen uniformly from

and all the queries are deterministic functions of
\mathbf{F}

we see that

is chosen uniformly from the query set for any

and

.
Thus

provides no information about the message index

The weak security (individual leakage) can be seen as follows.
The coefficient of each message symbol
W_{k,i}~(k\in[1:K],i\in[1:N-1])
in the expression of

can only be
1
or 2.
Because of the assumption that

Since

such that
k^{\prime\prime}\neq k,k^{\prime}

Then the message symbols of
W_{k^{\prime\prime}}
randomizes the sum of symbols from

and
W_{k^{\prime}}

(92)

which implies

(93)

(94)

To see the security, we consider the following,

\displaystyle I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]}Q_{1:N}^{[k]}\mathbf{F})

\displaystyle=I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]}Q_{1:N}^{[k]}W_{k}\mathbf{F})

(95)

\displaystyle=I(W_{k^{\prime}};A_{1:N}^{[k]}W_{k}\mathbf{F})

(96)

\displaystyle=I(W_{k^{\prime}};B\oplus S,W_{k}|\mathbf{F})

(97)

\displaystyle=0,

(98)

where (

(

\mathbf{F}

Since each answer consists of one symbol, the download cost is

D=N.

(99)

Since

Lastly, the databases do not share common randomness in addition to the messages themselves.

Case ii):

Case iii):

The parameters

\displaystyle\!B^{(1)}\!\oplus\!S

\displaystyle\!=\!W_{1,2}^{(1)}\oplus W_{3,1}^{(1)}\oplus S\!=\!W_{1}^{(1)}\oplus W_{1}^{(2)}\oplus W_{3}^{(2)},

\displaystyle\!B^{(2)}\!\oplus\!S

\displaystyle\!=\!W_{1,1}^{(2)}\oplus W_{3,2}^{(2)}\oplus S\!=\!W_{1}^{(1)}\oplus W_{3}^{(1)}\oplus W_{3}^{(2)}.

For

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the PIR problem with total and individual leakage constraints, respectively.
Our main contribution was the characterization of the tradeoffs between the minimum download cost
D_{\min}
and the total leakage constraint
s
and individual leakage constraint
w
.
The minimum amount of common randomness with respect to
s
and
w
was also characterized.
Remarkably, for
K\geq 3
, the databases do not need to share common randomness in order to achieve the individual leakage constraint
w
.
For
s\in[0,s_{t}]
, it was shown that the linear combination of TSC code and SPIR code is Pareto optimal (achieving the whole tradeoff curve).
For
w\in[0,w_{t}]
, an optimal code that achieves the capacity of WS-PIR without shared common randomness was proposed (
K\geq 3
),
and its linear combination with the classical PIR code was proved Pareto optimal.
The proposed Pareto optimal codes for both individual and total leakage were proved to have a minimum message size of
N-1
.

Appendix A
Proof of
Lemma

1

Throughout the proof, we use
L
to denote the minimum message length for Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable codes.
Since we have coding scheme of message length
(N-1)\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|

.
If

In the following, we prove the lemma by contradiction and suppose that
\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}

We only consider the individual leakage case, since by

4

From (

2
, the average number of downloaded symbols in

(126)

Since
L<(N-1)\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
implies
0<\frac{N-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}<1

are those
q
inducing

It is easy to see that

(127)

Since the equality in (
120

),
we see from Appendix

By

Since the whole answer set can recover
W_{k}
, we have
H(W_{k}|A_{1:N}^{(q)})=0
, and thus

H(A_{1:N}^{(q)}|W_{k})=H(A_{1:N}^{(q)})-H(W_{k}).

Then from (
127
) and (
128
), we can bound

(129)

Next, we derive another bound for

, which leads to a contradiction.
By the definition of uniform code in (

.
For

\displaystyle H(A_{1:N}^{(q)}|W_{k})=H(U_{1,1}^{(q)},\cdots,U_{N,\ell_{N}}^{(q)}).

(130)

We see that

is either deterministic or uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{Y}
, thus

or
\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|

\geq\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|.\end{cases}

(131)

Now, (

.

B-B

Individual Leakage Case

For the individual leakage case, the following lemma provides a new method of proving the minimum message size.
We can also apply the same method to the total leakage case, where the analysis of total leakage may be more complicated. The details for verifying this is omitted here.

Lemma 3
.

The message length of Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable PIR codes with individual leakage constraint
w
is lower bounded by
L\geq(N-1)\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|
.

Proof.

For
w>\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}
, the problem is equivalent to classical PIR.
Thus, we only consider
w\in[0,\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}]
, for which we prove the lemma by contradiction.
By
Lemma

1
, we suppose there exist Pareto optimal uniformly decomposable codes such that

L\leq(N-2)\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|.

(134)

Fix a
k\in[1:K]
. For each query realization
Q_{1:N}^{[k]}=q_{1:N}
, let
d
be the corresponding total number of downloaded symbols in
\mathcal{Y}
, which can be written as
d=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell_{n}(q_{n}).

Because of recovery requirement of
W_{k}
, we have

d\geq\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}.

(135)

For nonnegative integer
m
, let
\mathcal{Q}^{(m)}
be the set of query realizations defined by

\mathcal{Q}^{(m)}\triangleq\left\{q_{1:N}:d=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+m\right\}.

(136)

Define a probability
P^{(m)}
by

P^{(m)}=\sum_{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(m)}}\Pr(q_{1:N}).

(137)

From (
126
), the average number of downloaded symbols in
\mathcal{Y}
for Pareto optimal codes is

D_{\mathcal{Y}}=\frac{N-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|},

(138)

which, by the supposition (
134
) and the range of
w\in[0,\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}]
, satisfies

0<D_{\mathcal{Y}}-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}<1,

and thus
D_{\mathcal{Y}}
is not an integer.
In light of the lower bound of
d
in (
135
), the only possible realization of queries that induce
d<D_{\mathcal{Y}}
are
q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
,
and the corresponding downloaded number of symbols is
d=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}P^{(m)}\left(\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+m\right)

(139)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{m}P^{(m)}\right)\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+\sum_{m\geq 1}mP^{(m)}

(140)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+\sum_{m\geq 1}mP^{(m)}

(141)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+\sum_{m\geq 1}P^{(m)}

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}+(1-P^{(0)}),

(143)

which by (
138
) implies that

P^{(0)}\geq 1-\frac{1-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}.

(144)

In the following, we will derive a lower bound for information leakage that leads to a contradiction with the individual leakage constraints.
For
q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
, we have

d=\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{N}=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}.

(145)

which implies
\ell_{n}\in\left[0:\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\right]
for any
n\in[1:N]
.
Then for any
n\in[1:N]
, define a parameter by

T_{n}^{[k]}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}i\cdot\Pr\left\{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)},\ell_{n}=i\right\},

(146)

which can be roughly viewed as the average length of the answer when
q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
.
Then it is intuitive that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}T_{n}^{[k]}=\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\cdot P^{(0)},

which implies that there exists an
n\in[1:N]
such that

\displaystyle T_{n}^{[k]}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}{N}\cdot P^{(0)}.

(147)

In the following, we consider this particular
n
.
Then by (
144
), we have

T_{n}^{[k]}\geq\frac{\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}{N}\left(1-\frac{1-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\right).

(148)

For
q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
, since each answer symbol is either deterministic or uniformly distributed over
\mathcal{Y}
, we obtain from (
145
) that

H(A_{1:N}^{(q_{1:N})})\leq\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\cdot\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|=L=H(W_{k}).

(149)

This together with the recovery requirement of
W_{k}
, i.e.,
H(W_{k}|A_{1:N}^{(q_{1:N})})=0
, imply that

H(A_{1:N}^{(q_{1:N})})=H(W_{k}).

Then for the queries
q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
, the answers
A_{1:N}^{(q_{1:N})}
and the message
W_{k}
are deterministic of each other,
and all the answer symbols are non-deterministic.
Let
A_{n}^{(q_{n})}=\left(A_{n,1}^{(q_{n})},A_{n,2}^{(q_{n})},\cdots,A_{n,\ell_{n}}^{(q_{n})}\right)
where each component
A_{n,j}^{(q_{n})}\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Then we have

I(W_{k};A_{n,j}^{(q_{n})})=H(A_{n,j}^{(q_{n})})=\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|,\forall j\in[1:\ell_{n}].

(150)

Define a set of query realizations of
Q_{n}^{[k]}
by
\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)}\triangleq\left\{q_{n}:q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}\right\}
.
Since for
q_{1:N}\notin\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}
, the
n
-th query can also be in
\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)}
, we have

\Pr\{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)}\}\geq\Pr\{q_{1:N}\in\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}\}.

(151)

Next, we use (
148
) to derive an lower bound for individual leakage that will lead to a contradiction.
Consider
k^{\prime}\neq k
. Similarly, we define
T_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}
by

T_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}i\cdot\Pr\left\{q_{n}\in\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)},\ell_{n}=i\right\}.

(152)

Then the leakage of
W_{k}
from the answers for
k^{\prime}
can be bounded as follows,

I(W_{k};A_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]}Q_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]})\geq I(W_{k};A_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]})\geq T_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}\cdot\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|,

(153)

where the last inequality follows from (
150
).
Because of identical distribution of
Q_{n}^{[k]}
and
Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}
in (
10
), we obtain from (
151
) that

\Pr\left\{Q_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)}\right\}=\Pr\left\{Q_{n}^{[k]}\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(0)}\right\}\geq\Pr\left\{Q_{1:N}^{[k]}\!\in\!\mathcal{Q}^{(0)}\right\},

which implies that

T_{n}^{[k^{\prime}]}\geq T_{n}^{[k]}.

(154)

By (
153
), (
154
), and the lower bound in (
148
), we obtain

I(W_{k};A_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]}Q_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]})\geq\frac{L}{N}\left(1-\frac{1-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\right).

(155)

In the following, we derive the contradiction by arguing

\frac{L}{N}\left(1-\frac{1-w}{N-1}\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}\right)>wL,

(156)

which can be verified equivalent to

w<\frac{(N-1)-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}{(N^{2}-N)-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}.

(157)

It is easy to see that the right-hand side is a decreasing function of
L
.
Since by assumption
L\leq(N-2)\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(N-1)-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}{(N^{2}-N)-\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(N-1)-(N-2)}{(N^{2}-N)-(N-2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}-2(N-1)}.

(158)

For
K\geq 3
and
N\geq 2
, we can see that
\frac{1}{N^{2}-2(N-1)}>\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}
.
Thus, for any
w\in[0,\frac{1}{N^{K-1}}]
, we have

\frac{1}{N^{2}-2(N-1)}>w.

(159)

From (
158
) and (
159
), we obtain (
157
), and thus the argument (
156
) is true.
Then (
155
) and (
156
) imply that

I(W_{k};A_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]}Q_{1:N}^{[k^{\prime}]})>wL,

(160)

which contradicts the individual leakage constraint definition in (
12
).
Thus, the supposition at beginning that
L\leq(N-2)\frac{L}{\log_{|\mathcal{X}|}|\mathcal{Y}|}
is false, and this proves the lemma.
∎
