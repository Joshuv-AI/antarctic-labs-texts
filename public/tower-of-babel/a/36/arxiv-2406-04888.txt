Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation for Zero-Shot Video Editing
Lianghan Zhu, Yanqi Bao, Jing Huo, Jing Wu, Yu-Kun Lai, Wenbin Li, Yang Gao

Abstract
The rapidly evolving field of Text-to-Video generation (T2V) has catalyzed renewed interest in controllable video editing research. While the application of editing prompts to guide diffusion model denoising has gained prominence, mirroring advancements in image editing, this noise-based inference process inherently compromises the original video’s integrity, resulting in unintended over-editing and temporal discontinuities. To address these challenges, this study proposes a novel paradigm of video-based score distillation, facilitating direct manipulation of original video content. Specifically, distinguishing it from image-based score distillation, we propose an Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation strategy, which incorporates both global and local video guidance to reduce the impact of editing errors. Combined with our proposed Image-based Joint Guidance mechanism, it has the ability to mitigate the inherent instability of the T2V model and single-step sampling. Additionally, we design a Weighted Attention Fusion module to further preserve the key features of the original video and avoid over-editing. Extensive experiments demonstrate that these strategies effectively address existing challenges, achieving superior performance compared to current state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Video editing, an interactive and controllable generation task, has consistently attracted significant attention from the Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) community.
These techniques allow the modification of visual content to meet specific expectations, finding extensive applications in photography, cinematography, and artistic creation.

The recent proliferation of Text-to-Image (T2I) and Text-to-Video (T2V) diffusion models has significantly propelled this field.
Building on these pre-trained models, current research primarily falls into two categories:
Zero-Shot
and
One-Shot
approaches.
Zero-shot
methods
(
Qi et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Geyer et al. 2023
)
utilize DDIM Inversion
(
Hertz et al. 2022
)
to preserve original video information, incorporating editing guidance and inter-frame attention during inference for continuous video editing without additional training
(
Cong et al. 2023
;
Jeong and Ye 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023b
)
. In contrast,
One-shot
methods
(
Wu et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2023
;
Ren et al. 2024
)
fine-tune pre-trained models to learn subject-independent motion patterns, enabling video content editing through prompt replacement. A common thread among these approaches is their reliance on the inference process of diffusion models, which aims to controllably recover edited images from the noise—a process termed the
noise-based paradigm
. Despite employing DDIM inversion-related techniques, these methods intrinsically result in degradation of the original input structure. Furthermore, cumulative uncertainty during inference also exacerbates this effect, consequently inducing the notorious issues of over-editing and discontinuity in video editing.

A naive solution is to design a robust update paradigm, which possesses the intuitive capacity for direct video-based (or latent code) optimization by predicting update-gradients while preserving consistency in the non-edited areas. Inspired by text-to-3D works
(
Poole et al. 2022
)
, Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) is a widely adopted method for maintaining consistency. Furthermore, a similar concept Delta Denoising Score (DDS) has also been proven effective in image editing
(
Hertz, Aberman, and Cohen-Or 2023
)
, where DDS achieves image editing by directly optimizing the parameters in the source image, ensuring that unedited regions remain largely unaffected. However, the direct transposition of this paradigm to video editing presents significant challenges. Extensive empirical investigation reveals that edited videos often exhibit over-editing and pronounced artifacts, a phenomenon we term
Contamination
in Fig.
4
.

This issue likely stems from the weak robustness and unstable performance of T2V diffusion models, affecting gradient computation. Moreover, the SDS paradigm, utilizing single-step sampling in diffusion models, inherently exacerbates this problem.
Undeniably, these characteristics are critical in video-based update paradigms, as the the original video structures are more fragile, making them particularly sensitive to these errors. Fig.
5
empirically validates this hypothesis, showing that T2V diffusion models like ModelscopeT2V and Zeroscope exhibit significant errors in one-step predicted noise used for video update-gradients at larger timesteps, compared to Stable Diffusion.

This work introduces an Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation (ASSD) methodology, designed to incorporate more robust supervision and video priors to address the aforementioned challenges, as in Fig.
1
. Specifically, we propose a localized score distillation strategy utilizing sliding time-windows, which integrates inter-frame continuity priors during the update process to mitigate abrupt gradient fluctuations within local temporal sequences. However, recognizing that local priors alone often prove insufficient for complex video data, we develop a global gradient-based
Contamination
prediction strategy to guide the distillation process. Based on this methodology, we design an Image-based Joint Guidance mechanism, which enhances the upload-gradients from the selected frames perspective. Conversely, ASSD also mitigates the inherent discontinuities in multi-images editing in Joint Guidance mechanism. Moreover, to further mitigate potential adverse effects stemming from suboptimal T2V model and preserve the integrity of non-edited regions in the original video, we modify specific spatial self-attention layers within the denoising U-Net architecture in the editing branch. Through the implementation of a Weighted Attention Fusion module, we incorporate reference branch guidance into the updating-gradients computation in editing branch, thereby rectifying errors in the editing branch. Fig.
6
illustrates the application of our method directly to the original video, demonstrating the editing process. Comprehensive experiments conduct on both real and synthetic video editing tasks substantiate the efficacy of our proposed method, yielding superior results compared to alternative approaches. Related Work

Text-based Video Editing.

With the success achieved in the field of Text-based Video generation
(
Hong et al. 2022
;
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Saharia et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Pika 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, many works have attempted to utilize T2I and T2V diffusion models for text-conditioned video editing.

Tune-A-Video
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
pioneered one-shot video editing by fine-tuning pre-trained text-to-image diffusion models on reference videos, utilizing DDIM inversion for layout and motion guidance. Subsequent zero-shot methods emerged, employing DDIM Inversion-like techniques to preserve original video information while manipulating the inference process. Video-P2P
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
adapted cross-attention control from Prompt-to-Prompt
(
Hertz et al. 2022
)
, using a dual-branch pipeline. FateZero
(
Qi et al. 2023
)
manipulated intermediate features during diffusion model generation with cross-attention guidance. FLATTEN
(
Cong et al. 2023
)
incorporated optical flow guidance. TokenFlow
(
Geyer et al. 2023
)
propagated edits through feature matching, while CoDeF
(
Ouyang et al. 2023
)
decomposed videos into static content and time-dependent deformation fields for editing and propagation.

These noise-based methods achieve excellent video editing but require a complete noise addition and denoising process, often losing some original video information and failing to fully maintain unedited content and motion. Therefore, this work explores a video-based updating paradigm using guidance from pre-trained T2V diffusion models, better preserving unedited content and inter-frame motion continuity. Additional related works are discussed in the appendix.

Preliminaries

Text-conditioned Latent Video Diffusion Models.

We utilize pre-trained text-conditioned latent video diffusion models
(
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Sterling 2023
)
to provide guidance for video editing.
These models perform forward noise addition and reverse denoising operations in the latent space based on an autoencoder
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
.
We denote a video sequence consisting of
N
frames as
\bm{X}_{0}=\{\bm{x}_{0}^{1},...,\bm{x}_{0}^{N}\}
.
During the training phase, the latent input
\bm{Z}_{0}=\{\bm{z}_{0}^{1},...,\bm{z}_{0}^{N}\}=\mathcal{E}(\bm{X}_{0})
is perturbed to
\bm{Z}_{t}=\{\bm{z}_{t}^{1},...,\bm{z}_{t}^{N}\}
through the forward diffusion process:

\begin{aligned} \bm{Z}_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha_{t}}}\bm{Z}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha_{t}}}\bm{\epsilon},\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I})\end{aligned},

(1)

for
t\in[1,T]
, where
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}1-\beta_{s}
, and
\beta_{s}
is the variance schedule for timestep
s
.
Subsequently, the denoising U-Net is trained to predict the added noise given condition text embedding
y
using the following objective function:

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}(\bm{X}_{0}),y,\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bm{I}),t}\left[\|\bm{\epsilon}-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\bm{Z}_{t},t,y)\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(2)

where
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
represents the 3D denoising U-Net.

Score Distillation for Image Editing.

The SDS loss was first introduced in the text-to-3D work DreamFusion
(
Poole et al. 2022
)
, utilizing a pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model to provide update guidance for implicit 3D representations such as NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields)
(
Mildenhall et al. 2021
)
.
Recent work
(
Hertz, Aberman, and Cohen-Or 2023
)
has demonstrated the efficacy of score distillation loss in image editing tasks.
However, directly applying the SDS loss to compute gradient information for image editing leads to over-saturated and blurred results, imparting a cartoon-like appearance to the images. To address this issue, DDS introduces an additional reference branch aligned with the source text prompt.
Denote the pre-trained T2I diffusion model as
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
.
Given the parameterized latent code
\bm{z}_{0}(\phi)
of an image to be edited, the source text embedding
y
, and the target text embedding
y^{*}
.
By calculating the delta score between the model outputs of the two branches, it provides more accurate guidance for updates in the edited parts of
\bm{z}_{0}(\phi)
, while preserving the unedited parts.
Let the reference latent code be denoted as
\bm{\hat{z}}_{0}
, which is initialized as the original latent code of the image. The DDS loss can be formulated as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS}}(\phi;y^{*})=\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{w}(\bm{z}_{t}(\phi),t,y^{*})-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{w}(\bm{\hat{z}}_{t},t,y)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(3)

for
t\in[1,T]
, and
w
represents the classifier-free guidance weight
(
Ho and Salimans 2022
)
utilized in the sampling process of the diffusion model.

Method

Overview

Given an original video
\bm{X}_{0}
with its corresponding text prompt
y
and a target text prompt
y^{*}
, our method directly edits the latent code
\bm{Z}_{0}
of the original video to align with the target text prompt.
As illustrated in Fig.
2
, ASSD and Image-based Joint Guidance provide more stable update gradients for video editing, mitigating over-editing. Weighted Attention Fusion incorporates features from the reference branch, thus better preserving the original video information.

Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation

To introduce SDS-related update paradigms in video-based editing, a natural approach is to extend DDS to temporal inputs.
Based on the advanced open-source T2V diffusion models ModelScopeT2V and ZeroScope, denoted as
\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}
, video DDS loss can be expressed as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Video-DDS}}(\phi;y^{*})=\left\|\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{w}\left(\bm{Z}_{t}(\phi),t,y^{*}\right)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{w}(\bm{\hat{Z}}_{t},t,y)\right\|_{2}^{2},

(4)

and the gradient used to update
\bm{Z}_{0}(\phi)
can be calculated as:

\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Video-DDS}}=\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{Z}_{t},y^{*},t\right)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{\hat{Z}}_{t},y,t\right)\right)\frac{\partial\bm{{Z}}}{\partial\phi}.

(5)

Nevertheless, in practical applications, while this paradigm demonstrates the ability to preserve non-edited regions, as illustrated in the first row of Fig.
4
, it consistently encounters challenges such as over-editing (manifested as unintended changes in vehicle color) and the emergence of undesirable artifacts proximal to the edited object. The observed anomalies can be ascribed to a confluence of factors. Primarily, open-source T2V diffusion models demonstrate markedly inferior stability in video generation tasks relative to their Stable Diffusion counterparts in image generation. Moreover, the single-step sampling strategy inherent to SDS-analogous paradigms serves to amplify this instability, resulting in a compounded degradation of output fidelity. While the integration of frame-by-frame image editing techniques and multi-step sampling strategies offers a promising avenue for improvement, these methodologies are constrained by challenges in maintaining inter-frame coherence and computational efficiency. Consequently, it is imperative to explore a novel video score distillation strategy, namely Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation, which demonstrates potential in alleviating the aforementioned coupled challenges.

Specifically, we analyze diverse branches, including editing and reference branches, to examine the predictive noise distribution.
In the editing branch, noise predictions exhibit significant temporal variability due to the incorporation of edit-related guidance.
Conversely, the reference branch’s predictions are expected to demonstrate substantially less temporal heterogeneity.
Therefore, we employ a sliding time-window to smooth the reference branch’s noise prediction, which reflects the inherent continuity of the reference video and influences the smoothness of upload gradients.
The width of the sliding window should not be set too large, as excessive smoothing could lead to over-blurring of the video.
For simplicity, we temporarily omit the subscript
\theta
and superscript
\omega
.
Let
\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}^{i}
denote the noise prediction for the
i
-th frame in the reference branch at timestep
t
.
The sliding time-window smoothing operation for the current frame,

\begin{split}\operatorname{Smooth}(\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}^{i})=\gamma_{i-1}\cdot\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}^{i-1}+\gamma_{i}\cdot\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}^{i}&+\gamma_{i+1}\cdot\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}^{i+1},\\
\gamma_{i-1}+\gamma_{i}+\gamma_{i+1}&=1,\end{split}

(6)

where
\gamma_{i-1},\gamma_{i},\gamma_{i+1}
are hyper-parameters that control the degree of smoothing, and
\operatorname{Smooth}(\cdot)
denotes our sliding time-window smoothing operation.
In our experiments,
\gamma_{i}
is set to
0.67
, while
\gamma_{i-1}
and
\gamma_{i+1}
are equal.

However, applying this window-based smoothing strategy across the entire video may introduce unnecessary blurring, as both the regions intended for editing and those erroneously edited theoretically produce discontinuous update gradients in the temporal dimension, while the former is necessary in the editing task. This is challenging because these two types of regions are often intertwined. To mitigate this effect, an additional strategy for determining smooth regions is indispensable. Intuitively, regions with undesired over-editing and artifacts often exhibit larger update gradients compared to correctly edited areas. Therefore, we attempt to filter out these regions by applying an adaptive mask,
m
, which incorporates global video guidance and has the capability to handle diverse editing objectives.
Specifically,

\bm{m}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mathrm{abs}\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{t}-\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}\right)<\mathrm{max}\left(\mathrm{abs}\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{t}-\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}\right)\right)-\tau\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{array},\right.

(7)

where
\bm{\hat{\epsilon}}_{t}
is the noise prediction for all frames output by the reference branch,
\bm{{\epsilon}}_{t}
is the noise prediction for all frames output by the editing branch,
\operatorname{max}(\cdot)
denotes the maximization operation along the temporal dimension, and
\tau
is a hyper-parameter used to control the size of the mask’s effective range.
When
\tau
is smaller, the mask’s effective range is smaller and more stringent, and vice versa.
Based on this adaptively extracted mask, the final gradient used to update
\bm{Z}_{0}(\phi)
is represented as follows:

\begin{split}\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASSD}}=\left(\bm{m}\odot\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{Z}_{t},y^{*},t\right)-\mathrm{Smooth}\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{\hat{Z}}_{t},y,t\right)\right)\right)\right.\\
\quad+\left.(\bm{1}-\bm{m})\odot\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{Z}_{t},y^{*},t\right)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\theta}^{\omega}\left(\bm{\hat{Z}}_{t},y,t\right)\right)\right)\frac{\partial\bm{{Z}}}{\partial\phi}.\end{split}

(8)

In Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation (ASSD), we mitigate the impact of upload gradient errors by leveraging local time-windows information. In addition, we adaptively construct a mask based on the global guidance to minimize the negative effects of the over-smoothing. This method effectively reduces contaminations in the edited video results.

Image-based Joint Guidance

As validated in DDS, the one-step predicted noise provided by Stable Diffusion can offer sufficient quality gradient information for image editing.
Therefore, we propose a joint guidance strategy that introduces Stable Diffusion to provide more stable gradients for updating
\bm{Z}_{0}(\phi)
, which uses DDS to separately calculate the gradient for updating selected frames in the video. In our experiments, we select keyframes using a fixed step size to maintain efficiency.
For the
i
-th selected frame in the video, the gradient is,

\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS-StableDiffusion}}=\left(\bm{\epsilon}_{\Omega}^{\omega}\left(\bm{z}_{t}^{i},y^{*},t\right)-\bm{\epsilon}_{\Omega}^{\omega}\left(\bm{\hat{z}}_{t}^{i},y,t\right)\right)\frac{\partial{\bm{z}^{i}}}{\partial\phi},

(9)

where
\bm{\epsilon}_{\Omega}
denotes a Stable Diffusion V1.5 in our implementation. Finally, the total gradient used to update
\bm{Z}_{0}(\phi)
can be expressed as:

\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ASSD}}+\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS-StableDiffusion}}.

(10)

Weighted Attention Fusion

Although the proposed ASSD effectively reduces errors in the update gradients, local editing errors and over-editing still occur, which can be observed in Fig.
4
.
It is desirable to preserve some information in the original video, including the layout of the original video, the details in the unedited parts, and some attributes in the edited parts such as the motion pattern of the edited object.
Intuitively, features from the original video can be utilized to reduce over-editing, thereby further enhancing the editing quality.
To better preserve the information and leverage features in the original video, we introduce a Weighted Attention Fusion module.

Specifically, in the spatial attention layers of ModelScopeT2V or Zeroscope, the self-attention for the latent code
\bm{z}^{i}
corresponding to the
i
-th frame in the video is:

\mathrm{SelfAttn}_{i}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\frac{\bm{W}_{q}(\bm{z}^{i})\bm{W}_{k}(\bm{z}^{i})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}})\bm{W}_{v}(\bm{z}^{i}),

(11)

where
\bm{W}_{q}(\cdot)
,
\bm{W}_{k}(\cdot)
,
\bm{W}_{v}(\cdot)
are linear projections, and
d
is the scaling factor.
We use
\bm{z}^{i}
to denote the latent code corresponding to the
i
-th frame in the editing branch of ASSD and use
\bm{\hat{z}}^{i}
to denote the latent code corresponding to the
i
-th frame in the reference branch of ASSD.
The proposed reference-attention between them is defined as:

\mathrm{RefAttn}_{i}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\frac{\bm{W}_{q}(\bm{z}^{i})\bm{W}_{k}(\bm{\hat{z}}^{i})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}})\bm{W}_{v}(\bm{\hat{z}}^{i}).

(12)

Then, our weighted attention fusion can be implemented as:

\mathrm{AttnFusion}_{i}=(1-\lambda)\cdot\mathrm{SelfAttn}_{i}+\lambda\cdot\mathrm{RefAttn}_{i},

(13)

where
\lambda\in[0,1]
, it controls the balance between the extent of editing and the preservation of information from the original video.
In the majority of cases, we set it to 0.8.
This module allows for the fusion of features from the editing branch with those from the reference branch.
We replaced the self-attention calculations in the last few spatial attention layers of the U-Net only in the editing branch with our proposed weighted attention fusion, and find this design significantly improves the quality of the edited videos.

Qualitative Experiment

Fig.
3
presents the qualitative comparison results between our method and other SOTA baselines.
Our method achieves comparable or superior performance in terms of alignment with the target text prompts, preservation of the original video’s layout, motion patterns, and unedited regions, while allowing for geometric transformation of the edited objects.
When an editing method allows for significant geometric transformations of the edited objects, it typically introduces challenges in preserving non-edited regions.
Conversely, methods that struggle with geometric transformations of edited objects generally achieve better preservation of non-edited regions.
Existing methods often face one of two issues.
ControlVideo and Tune-A-Video allow for geometric transformations of the original video to better match the target prompt but fail to preserve the non-edited parts of the original video.
FateZero, FLATTEN, and TokenFlow achieve better preservation of non-edited regions but struggle to support significant geometric transformations.
Our method strikes a better balance between supporting geometric transformations and preserving non-edited regions.

Quantitative Experiment

Auto Metrics.

We utilized the CLIP model
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
for automatic evaluation, employing two metrics: CLIP Text Alignment Score and CLIP Frame Consistency Score.
CLIP Text Alignment Score is defined as the mean cosine similarity between the CLIP image embeddings of the frames in the edited video and the CLIP text embedding of the target text prompt.
This metric measures how well the frames in the edited video aligns with the target text prompt.
CLIP Frame Consistency Score is defined as the mean cosine similarity between the CLIP image embeddings of frames within the video.
This metric assesses the inter-frame consistency of the edited video.
The results of the automatic metric are reported in Tab.
1
.
Although our method achieved only average results on both evaluation metrics, it is worth noting that there is still a lack of appropriate automatic evaluation metrics.
The CLIP Frame Consistency Score focuses solely on the semantic consistency between different frames and fails to measure the continuity of motion and the consistency of objects between consecutive frames.
Similarly, the CLIP Text Alignment Score only assesses the semantic alignment between the frames in the video and the target text prompt, and does not measure the preservation of non-edited parts.

User Study.

Since automatic assessments based on CLIP often do not align well with the perceptual quality of videos, we primarily validate the effectiveness of our method through user studies.
We invited 40 volunteers to participate in our user study.
The volunteers were asked to evaluate the edited videos based on three criteria: (1) Alignment between the edited video and the target text prompt. (2) Inter-frame consistency and the continuity of motion between adjacent frames of the edited video. (3) Preservation of the original video’s motion patterns and unedited regions.
For each criterion, the volunteers chose the best-performing edited result from those produced by our method and the five baselines.
We then calculated the proportion of times each method’s edited results were selected as the best for each criterion.
As shown in Tab.
1
, our method achieves comparable or superior performance across all three evaluation metrics compared to other SOTA methods.
Since FateZero typically only edits the color and texture of objects and struggles with geometric transformations, as seen in Fig.
3
of the qualitative experiments, it effectively facilitates the preservation of non-edited parts and object IDs, resulting in the best performance in Preservation.

Ablation Study

We conduct ablation studies to validate the effect of each proposed component and the choice of hyperparameter.

As shown in the first row of experimental results in Fig.
4
, directly applying DDS for video editing leads to the presence of contaminations near the edited objects in the resulting video.
The results in the second row show that by using ASSD to adaptively smoothing the gradient information with adjacent frames, the contaminations in the resulting video are reduced, leading to improved video quality.
When we further incorporate the proposed Weighted Attention Fusion mechanism, using features from the reference branch, the contaminations are further reduced.
Additionally, we observe that the edited video retains more information from the original video, such as colors and other details.
Due to the significantly weaker robustness and generalization to different text conditions of current open-source T2V diffusion models compared to Stable Diffusion, we introduced Image-based Joint Guidance as the supervision.
As shown in the last row of Fig.
4
, the updated gradients from Stable Diffusion further reduce some of the contaminations in the video and enhanced the detail quality of the edited subjects.
In Fig.
7
, we validate different choices for the
\lambda
hyperparameter in Weighted Attention Fusion.
When
\lambda
is large, more information from the reference branch is introduced into the editing branch, reducing contaminations in the output but also weakening the degree of editing.
Conversely, when
\lambda
is small, the opposite effect is observed.

Conclusion and Limitations

We introduce a novel score distillation-based paradigm for video editing, featuring Adaptive Sliding Score Distillation (ASSD).
ASSD mitigates gradient errors from T2V diffusion models through adaptive sliding window-based smoothing and extracted masks.
Our pipeline incorporates joint guidance from Stable Diffusion for precise latent code updates.
We employ weighted attention fusion, modifying spatial self-attention layers in pre-trained T2V diffusion models to fuse information between editing and reference branches.
Experimental results validate the efficacy of our paradigm and its components in video editing tasks.

Limitations

The efficacy of these methods hinges on the assumption that pre-trained T2V diffusion models provide effective update gradients, despite inherent errors. However, the dearth of robust open-source T2V diffusion models significantly constrains our method’s performance ceiling.
