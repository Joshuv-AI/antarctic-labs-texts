Lifting-based variational multiclass segmentation algorithm: design, convergence analysis, and implementation with applications in medical imaging
Nadja Gruber, Johannes Schwab, Sébastien Court, Elke R. Gizewski, Markus Haltmeier

Abstract
We propose, analyze and realize a variational multiclass segmentation scheme that partitions a given image into multiple regions exhibiting specific properties. Our method determines multiple functions that encode the segmentation regions by minimizing an energy functional combining information from different channels. Multichannel image data can be obtained by lifting the image into a higher dimensional feature space using specific multichannel filtering or may already be provided by the imaging modality under consideration, such as an RGB image or multimodal medical data. Experimental results show that the proposed method performs well in various scenarios. In particular, promising results are presented for two medical applications involving classification of brain abscess and tumor growth, respectively. As main theoretical contributions, we prove the existence of global minimizers of the proposed energy functional and show its stability and convergence with respect to noisy inputs. In particular, these results also apply to the special case of binary segmentation, and these results are also novel in this particular situation.



1
Introduction

The aim of segmentation is to divide an image defined on some bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
into subregions that are homogeneous with regard to certain characteristics, such as intensity, color, or texture. This process plays a fundamental role in various semantic applications such as object recognition, classification, or medical diagnostics. Many successful image segmentation methods are based on variational and active contour models
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, which have in common that they find optimal segmentations by minimizing an objective function, which generally depends on the given image and the features used to identify the different regions to be segmented.

In the simplest case of binary segmentation, the goal is to divide a given image into two regions, one that represents the object to be recognized and the second one that represents background. A particularly popular approach in that regard is the Chan-Vese model
[
4
]
, that is based on a level-set function
\phi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
defining two regions
\{x\in\Omega\mid\phi(x)\leq 0\}
and
\{x\in\Omega\mid\phi(x)>0\}
. The level-set function
\phi
is constructed by minimizing a certain energy functional combining regularity of the segmentation region and fitting to the provided input image. The extension to non-binary segmentation (or multiclass segmentation) is challenging due to several reasons. For example, using
m
level-set functions naturally yields
2^{m}
separate regions (corresponding to all possible combinations of overlaps of the individual level-sets), which may be different from the desired number of regions to be segmented. Furthermore, in a naive approach, the segmentation function is applied to the unfiltered original intensity image. In practice, however, other characteristics like texture or color may be better suited to separate individual regions; see
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
.

In this paper, we present a variational framework for multiclass segmentation based on lifting the image to be segmented into a space of
K
-channel images (feature maps), on which we apply a proposed variational segmentation functional. The individual channels are generated to well separate the
k
-th class from the remaining classes. This can be achieved, for example, by applying multiple filters or by combining information from naturally occurring channels, as in multimodal medical imaging. Even imperfect separation of the different regions by means of the feature maps can be corrected by the actual segmentation process. It should be noted that the generation of vector-valued feature maps prior to actual segmentation is not a new proposal; see e.g.
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
and the references there. However, the specific segmentation functional, the convergence analysis, and the proposed minimization algorithm are new, and are the key contributions of the present work.

1.1
Proposed lifting-based segmentation

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a space of functions
f\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{M}
with values in some manifold
\mathcal{M}
. The manifold
\mathcal{M}
is generic and, for example, can consist of (a subset of) the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
in the case of gray-value images, be
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the case of multimodal imaging, or it can consist of certain tensors such as in diffusion tensor imaging. After feature lifting, the image values will be elements in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, with
K+1
denoting the number of distinct classes. Our aim is solving the following task:

Problem 1.1

(Multiclass segmentation)
.

Based on specific pre-defined characteristics of the image
f\in\mathbb{F}
, construct a partitioning
\Omega=\bigcup_{k=0}^{K}\Sigma_{k}
of the domain
\Omega
into
K+1
disjoint regions (classes). Each of the regions
\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{K}
represents a specific structure or objects in the given image and
\Sigma_{0}
represents background.

Let
\BV(\Omega)
denote the space of all integrable functions
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded total variation
\lvert u\rvert_{\rm TV}
, write
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
for
K
-tuples of functions in
\BV(\Omega)
and consider the admissible set

\mathbb{A}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\mid\boldsymbol{u}\geq 0\wedge\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}\leq 1\Bigr\}\,.

Here and below
\boldsymbol{u}\geq 0
means that
u_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Moreover, let
i_{\mathbb{A}}\colon\BV(\Omega)^{K}\to[0,\infty]
denote the associated indicator function taking the value
0
inside
\mathbb{A}
and the value
\infty
outside of
\mathbb{A}
. Throughout we use boldface notation for various kinds of
K
-tuples such as
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
or
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

In this work we propose the following three-step segmentation procedure for solving Problem
1.1
and segmenting the image
f\in\mathbb{F}
into
K+1
regions.

(N1)

Lifting:
Choose
K
(feature enhancing) transforms
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K}\colon\mathbb{F}\to L^{\infty}(\Omega)
in such a way that the intensity values of the
k
-th feature map
\phi_{k}\coloneqq\Phi_{k}(f)
allow to well separate region
\Sigma_{k}
from the remaining part
\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{k}
.

(N2)

Minimization:
For given parameter
\lambda>0
, compute a minimizer
(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
of the energy functional
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}\colon\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}\to[0,\infty]
,

\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\coloneqq i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert u_{k}\rvert_{\rm TV}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\Omega}(a_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k}+(b_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}(1-u_{k})\,.

(1.1)

(N3)

Assignment:
For each
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
define
\Sigma_{k}
as the set of all
x\in\Omega
such that
u^{\lambda}_{k}(x)
is maximal among the values
u^{\lambda}_{0}(x),\dots,u^{\lambda}_{K}(x)
where
u_{0}\coloneqq 1-\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}
.

In some applications, the feature channels
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{K}
may already be provided as the input data without the need of additional filtering. For example, in an RGB image, each channel may represent specific regions well characterized by its color. Further examples of this type are found in medical imaging, if for example, one channel corresponds to a computed tomography (CT) image and another channel to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. In this case, bone structures are well revealed on the CT image, and soft tissue well on the MRI data. Notice, however, that we are also interested in cases where channels are extracted via more general transforms
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K}
, for example, by exploiting certain expert knowledge and tailoring the segmentation to specific image features.

f
, computed feature map
\phi_{1}

,

To illustrate the reasoning behind such a strategy, consider the image in the upper left of Fig.

. The lifted images are obtained by color and Gabor filtering, with parameters provided in Table

. Suppose we want to identify the butterfly and the flower, and think of the rest as background. Our framework then starts by computing a new set of images (two top right pictures), with the desired structures being highlighted, and then segments those structures via data fitting in the corresponding image channel. Our approach naturally leads to non-overlapping segmentation maps
u_{k}
, for which the constraint
\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}\leq 1
plays an important role. In particular, by relaxing the condition to
\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}=1
it implies that no separate channel for the background is required. Segmentation procedure
(N1)
-
(N3)
is universal and suitable for any multichannel or multiclass segmentation task.

1.2
Related work

Functional (
1.1
) is inspired by
[
11
]
, which introduces a relaxation of the Chan-Vese model for binary segmentation. Actually, for the special case
K=1
, (
1.1
) reduces to the functional from
[
11
]
. One main difference is the soft non-overlapping condition
\sum_{k=1}^{K}u_{k}\leq 1
, which is not required in the binary situation. Moreover, the numerical minimization procedure we propose is very different from the one in
[
11
]
, which uses an explicit gradient procedure to solve a convex problem with fixed constants, together with occasionally updating constants. In addition, their analysis does not target stability and convergence with respect to the noise level. Extensions of the Chan-Vese model for multiclass segmentation are presented in
[
12
]
. In this work, however,
m
overlapping level-set functions are used, resulting in
2^{m}
distinct regions to be segmented. The resulting approach is fundamentally different from ours, as optimization is not performed over the segmentation masks
u_{k}
, but rather over the level set functions
\phi_{k}
.

Closely related, yet different approaches that first lift the image into a higher dimensional space of feature maps before applying a segmentation algorithm have also been proposed. In
[
13
,
14
]
, for example, segmentation functionals are used which are independent of the constants
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
. This makes the final optimization less general but convex and easier to solve. In our case, due to the presence of unknown constants
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
, the functional to be minimized is non-convex and requires the development of efficient algorithms tailored to handle it. In
[
9
]
, the authors propose a two-step learning approach, in which they learn filters to generate a pre-segmentation in a first step, and apply a modified Mumford-Shah functional in a second step. We develop a numerical algorithm based on a non-convex primal-dual algorithm of
[
15
]
for the second step.

Our proposed functional is non-convex because it contains constants
\boldsymbol{a}
and
\boldsymbol{b}
. Treating these constants as being fixed would result in a convex problem to optimize. It is worth noting that there is also another source of non-convexity in this context, namely, actually working with a Mumford-Shah-like functional instead of using total-variation (TV) to obtain a convex relaxation. This is another challenging yet very exciting direction; see, for example,
[
16
]
, where an efficient algorithm for solving the Potts model has been developed. Combination and comparison with our multi-channel approach is an interesting line of research, but beyond the scope of this article.

The main contributions of the present paper are threefold. First, we introduce the lifting-based segmentation framework
(N1)
-
(N3)
using the energy functional (
1.1
). Second, we derive a mathematical analysis including stability and convergence with respect to data perturbations. Lastly, we present a numerical algorithm for which we conduct several experiments, in particular for applications in medical imaging. From the mathematical point of view, the most important contributions consist in the presented stability and convergence analysis. To the best of our knowledge, no such results have been previously derived in the literature, not even for the well studied binary segmentation task. Existing theoretical analysis of related models is usually concerned with connections between the relaxed convex minimization problem, (for fixed values
\boldsymbol{a}
,
\boldsymbol{b}
) and the underlying non-convex problem. In contrast, we study both stability and convergence properties of the relaxed functional.

Outline

The remaining article is structured as follows. In Section
2

1.1
). In Section

, we present possible applications and experimental results. The paper concludes with a short summary and discussion given in Section

2
Mathematical analysis

We start with the mathematical analysis of the proposed energy functional (
1.1
). Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary and denote by
\BV(\Omega)
the space of all functions
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
with finite (isotropic) total variation

\lvert u\rvert_{\rm TV}\coloneqq\lvert\nabla u\rvert_{2,1}\coloneqq\sup\Big\{\int_{\Omega}u\operatorname{div}v\mid v\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})\wedge\lvert v\rvert_{2,\infty}\leq 1\Big\}\,,

where
\lvert v\rvert_{2,\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{x}(v_{1}(x)^{2}+v_{2}(x)^{2})^{1/2}
. Here
\nabla u
denotes the distributional derivative which for functions
u\in\BV(\Omega)
is a vector-valued Radon measure having total mass
\lvert\nabla u\rvert_{2,1}
. It is well known that
\BV(\Omega)
with norm
\lVert u\rVert_{\rm TV}\coloneqq\lVert u\rVert_{1}+\lvert u\rvert_{\rm TV}
is a Banach space.

2.1
Notation and preliminaries

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
denote the available multichannel feature map and
\lambda>0
a regularization parameter.
Recall that the channels may either be already provided by the application or may be obtained by application of the feature transforms
\Phi_{k}
; see
(N1)
. Further recall the admissible set
\mathbb{A}
of (tuples of) segmentation maps, the corresponding indicator function
i_{\mathbb{A}}
and the functional
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}
defined in (
1.1
).
Using the notions

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\Omega}(a_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k}+(b_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}(1-u_{k})\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert u_{k}\rvert_{\rm TV}\,,

the considered energy functional, for
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
, takes the form

\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})=i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\,.

(2.1)

Note that
\operatorname{dom}(\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda})\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{u}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})<\infty\}=\mathbb{A}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
is nonempty, as the segmentation map with constant channels
u_{k}=1/K
is contained in
\mathbb{A}
.
Further, it is worth mentioning that the problem of minimizing (
1.1
) is bi-convex, meaning it is convex in the variable
\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\mathbb{R})^{K}
as well as in the variable
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
. However it is not jointly convex in both variables. Note that the lower semicontinuity of the total variation (for example, see
[
17
, p. 7]
) implies that
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})
for any sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
L^{1}
-limit
\boldsymbol{u}
. Further, at several places of our analysis we make use of the following compactness result.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(\BV(\Omega)^{K})^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\leq C
for some constant
C\in(0,\infty)
, then
\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(k)}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{1}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
for some subsequence
\tau
and some
\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
.

Proof.

If
i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\leq C
, then it holds
0\leq u_{k,n}\leq 1
and
\lvert u_{k,n}\rvert_{\rm TV}\leq C
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore the sequences
(\boldsymbol{u}_{k,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are bounded in
\BV(\Omega)
. The compactness theorem for
\BV(\Omega)
(see, for example,
[
18
, p. 132]
or
[
19
, p. 176]
) thus implies the existence of a subsequence
\tau
and some
\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(k)}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{1}\to 0
.
∎

2.2
Reduced formulation

Functional (
2.1
) is jointly minimized over
\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
and
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
. Throughout this paper we will make use of an equivalent reduced optimization problem in the variable
\boldsymbol{u}
only, by explicitly computing the minimizers in the variable
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
. In that context, we note the following elementary result.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{A}
, the set
\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
is non-empty, equals
\argmin\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
, and given by

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\phi})\coloneqq\prod_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(A(u_{k},\phi_{k}),A(1-u_{k},\phi_{k})\bigr)

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle A(u_{k},\phi_{k})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}&\text{ if $u_{k}=0$ }\\
\left\{\frac{1}{\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{1}}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}u_{k}\right\}&\text{ otherwise }\,.\end{cases}

(2.3)

Proof.

Clearly,
\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)=\argmin\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
and minimizing
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
is separable in the components
a_{k},b_{k}
. Hence minimizers are found by separately minimizing
\int_{\Omega}(a_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k}
with respect to
a_{k}
and
\int_{\Omega}(b_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}(1-u_{k})
with respect to
b_{k}
. We have
\int_{\Omega}(a_{k}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k}=a_{k}^{2}\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{1}-2a_{k}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}u_{k}+\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}^{2}u_{k}
and for
u_{k}\neq 0
minimizers are given by
\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}u_{k}/\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{1}
. If
u_{k}=0
any
a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
. Similar arguments for the second minimization problem complete the proof. ∎

Based on Lemma
2.2
we define the reduced energy functional
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}\colon\BV(\Omega)^{K}\to[0,\infty]
by

\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})+\inf\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{A}\,,\\
\infty&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{u}\not\in\mathbb{A}\,.\end{cases}

(2.4)

According to Lemma
2.2
, the infimum
\inf\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u},\cdot)
is attained for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{A}
and the corresponding set of minimizers is given by (
2.3
). For the following lemma note that
\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}\subseteq\mathbb{A}
.

Lemma 2.3

(Equivalence)
.

For all
(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
.

(b)

\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}\in\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
and
(\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\argmin\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\lambda}},\cdot)
.

Proof.

If
(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
then clearly
(\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\cdot)
and for all
\boldsymbol{u}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
we have
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})\geq\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})
.
Conversely, if
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}
minimizes
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
and
(\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\cdot)
, then for
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
we have

\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\geq\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})\geq\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})
which completes the proof.
∎

In the following we prove the existence and stability of minimizers of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
which according to Lemma
2.3
is equivalent to existence and stability of minimizers of
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}
. We further investigate the convergence of minimizers as the error in the data
\boldsymbol{\phi}
tends to zero.

2.3
Existence

We start with the existence of minimizers of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Existence)
.

For all
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
and
\lambda>0
, functional
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
admits at least one global minimizer.

Proof.

Since
(1,\dots,1)/K\in\mathbb{A}
, the domain of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
is non-empty and we can choose a sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\to\inf\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
. In particular
(i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and by Lemma
2.1
there exists a subsequence, again denoted by
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that converges in
L^{1}(\Omega)^{K}
to some
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By moving to another subsequence, point-wise convergence can be established almost everywhere, from which the conclusion
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}\in\mathbb{A}
can be drawn. Next, select
(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
with
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})=\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
according to (
2.2
), (
2.3
). In particular, for
u_{k}\neq 0
and
u_{k}\neq 1
we have
a_{k,n}=\lVert u_{k}\rVert_{1}^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}u_{k}
and
b_{k,n}=\lVert 1-u_{k}\rVert_{1}^{-1}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}(1-u_{k})
, respectively. Thus if
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}_{k}\neq 0,1
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
we can assume
u_{k,n}\neq 0,1
for all
k,n
and by (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle 0\leq a_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle u_{k,n},\phi_{k}\rangle}{\lVert u_{k,n}\rVert_{1}}\leq\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle 0\leq b_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle 1-u_{k,n},\phi_{k}\rangle}{\lVert 1-u_{k,n}\rVert_{1}}\leq\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\,.

If either
u_{k}^{\lambda}=0
or
u_{k}^{\lambda}=1
, the above identities for
a_{k,n},b_{k,n}
may not hold. According to Lemma
2.2
, in this case the numbers
a_{k,n},b_{k,n}
can be chosen arbitrarily, and we can therefore still assume that
0\leq a_{k,n},b_{k,n}\leq\lVert\phi_{k}\rVert_{\infty}
. Up to extracting another subsequence, in any case we can assume
(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\to(\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})\in[0,\infty)^{2K}
. Fatou’s Lemma yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\Omega}(a_{k}^{\lambda}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k}^{\lambda}+(b_{k}^{\lambda}-\phi_{k})^{2}(1-u_{k}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}(a_{k,n}-\phi_{k})^{2}u_{k,n}+(b_{k,n}-\phi_{k})^{2}(1-u_{k,n})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\,.

Together with the lower semi-continuity of
\mathcal{Q}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})+\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})+\lambda\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\inf_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=\inf\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}

Hence
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
.
∎

2.4
Stability

Next we investigate the stability of minimizers of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
with respect to data
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Stability)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
and take
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\in\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\phi_{n},\lambda}
. Then
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has at least one
L^{1}
-norm convergent subsequence and the limit of any
L^{1}
-norm convergent subsequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a minimizer
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}
of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
that satisfies
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})\to\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

Because
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\in\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\phi_{n},\lambda}
, we have that
(\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and therefore
(\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, too. By Theorem
2.1
there exists a subsequence, that we again denote by
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that converges in
L^{1}(\Omega)^{K}
to some
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
. Like in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we construct
(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
such that
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\in\argmin\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n},\lambda}
and
0\leq a_{k,n},b_{k,n}\leq\lVert\phi_{k,n}\rVert_{\infty}
. As
\lVert\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\rVert_{\infty}\to 0
, the elements
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}
are uniformly bounded and thus
(a_{k,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(b_{k,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are bounded. Therefore, up to the extraction of another subsequence, we can assume
(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\to(\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{b}^{\lambda})
.

It remains to show that
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi};\lambda}
and that
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\to\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})
. After passing to a subsequence that converges pointwise almost everywhere, from Fatou’s lemma and the semi-continuity of
\mathcal{Q}
one derives
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
. Therefore, for all
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{2K}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})

\displaystyle=i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})=\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\,.

Taking the infimum over
(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
shows
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})\leq\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})
which implies
\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}\in\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
. Choosing
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda}
in the last displayed equation shows
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
and

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})+\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\\
\leq\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})-\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{a}^{\lambda})=\lambda\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\lambda})\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

2.5
Convergence

In the following we investigate the convergence of minimizers of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
to minimizers of the following limiting constraint optimization problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u})\\
&\text{ s.t. }\boldsymbol{u}\in\argmin i_{\mathbb{A}}+\inf_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

In general,
\argmin i_{\mathbb{A}}+\inf_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}\mathcal{D}_{{{\phi}}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
can be empty, reflecting the ill-posedness of (
2.5
) and the need for the relaxed problem (
2.1
). However, for certain
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, which we refer to exact data, such minimizers exist and define a segmentation function that is independent of any parameters. When minimizing the energy functional
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
, we then interpret
\boldsymbol{\phi}
as perturbed version of the exact data, and
\lambda
as regularization parameter accounting for stability. In the convergence analysis, we study convergence of minimizers of
\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}
to solutions of (
2.5
) as the noise level and the regularization parameter tend to zero. We are not aware of any such an analysis in the segmentation literature, but think that this approach can be the starting point of new insights and algorithms.

Remark 2.1
.

Denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma}
the indicator function of a set
\Sigma\subseteq\Omega
taking the value
1
inside
\Sigma
and
0
outside. In order to focus on the main ideas, in the following we consider exact feature maps
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
with binary channels

\phi_{k}^{*}=a_{k}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}}+b_{k}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{k}}\,,

(2.6)

where

\diamond

\Sigma_{k}\subseteq\Omega
are pairwise disjoint with finite perimeters

\diamond

(\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{b}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{2K}
satisfies
\forall k\colon a_{k}^{*}\neq b_{k}^{*}
.

Recall that the perimeter
\operatorname{per}(\Sigma)\coloneqq\lvert\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma}\rvert_{\rm TV}
of a set
\Sigma
is defined as the total variation of the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma}
. In particular, functions of the form (
2.6
) satisfy
\lvert\phi_{k}\rvert_{\rm TV}=\lvert b_{k}^{*}-a_{k}^{*}\rvert\operatorname{per}(\Sigma_{k})
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any exact data
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
of the form (
2.6
), the set of solutions of (
2.5
) is non-empty and consists of all
\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}\in\BV(\Omega)^{K}
with
u_{k}^{\star}\in\{\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}},\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{k}}\}
.

Proof.

Let
u_{k}^{\star}\in\{\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}},\mathbbm{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{k}}\}
. Clearly,
0\leq\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},0}
and
0=\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*},0}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\star})
. Therefore
\boldsymbol{u}^{\star}\in\argmin i_{\mathbb{A}}+\inf_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})
. Moreover,
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\star})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\operatorname{per}(\Sigma_{k})
which shows that
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
solves (
2.5
).
∎

We now have the following convergence result.

Theorem 2.3

(Convergence)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
be exact data of the form (
2.6
),
(\delta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
converge to zero and
(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{K}
be noisy data with
\lVert\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\delta_{n}
. Further, let
\lambda_{n}\in(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of regularization parameters with

\lambda_{n}\to 0
and
\delta_{n}^{2}/\lambda_{n}\to 0
and let
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}\in\argmin\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n},\lambda_{n}}
. Then
(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has at least one
L^{1}
-convergent subsequence. Moreover, the limit of any
L^{1}
-convergent subsequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
of (
2.5
) with

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})\to\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*})
.

Proof.

According to Lemma
2.4
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}=(\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a solution of (
2.5
). Further, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{b}^{*})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\Sigma_{k}}{(\phi_{k,n}-a_{k}^{*})^{2}}+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Sigma_{k}}(\phi_{k,n}-b_{k}^{*})^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{\Omega}(\phi_{k,n}-\phi_{k}^{*})^{2}\leq K\lvert\Omega\rvert\delta_{n}^{2}\,.

Together with the definition of
\boldsymbol{u}_{n}
and choosing elements
(\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\in\argmin\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\cdot)
this shows

\mathcal{D}_{\phi_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\leq K\lvert\Omega\rvert\delta_{n}^{2}+\lambda_{n}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*})\,.

Together with the parameter choice
\lambda_{n},\delta_{n}^{2}/\lambda_{n}\to 0
and the continuity of
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})
in
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}
this shows

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n},\boldsymbol{b}_{n})=0

(2.7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{n})\leq\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*})\,.

(2.8)

Lemma
2.1
and identity (
2.8
) imply the existence of an
L^{1}
-convergent subsequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. According to (
2.7
), the limit of any such subsequence is a solution of (
2.5
) and without loss of generality we can assume
\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)}\to\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
. From (
2.8
) and the lower semi-continuity of
\mathcal{Q}
we finally derive
\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau(n)})\to\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{u}^{*})
.
∎

3
Algorithm development

In this section, we establish a numerical algorithm for minimizing the reduced segmentation functional
\mathcal{R}_{f,\lambda}
. For that purpose, we will first introduce a discrete framework for which we actually present our optimization procedure.

3.1
Discretization

In the following we work with discrete images and feature maps in
\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}
, which is a finite dimensional Hilbert space with inner product
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i}u[i]v[i]
for
u,v\in\mathbb{H}
where
i=(i_{1},i_{2})\in\{1,\dots,N_{1}\}\times\{1,\dots,N_{2}\}
. Moreover we introduce the following discrete counterparts of ingredients of the continuous functional (
2.4
).

\diamond

The discrete gradient
\nabla=(\nabla_{1},\nabla_{2})\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}
is defined by forward differences with Neumann boundary conditions

\displaystyle(\nabla_{1}u)[i]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}(u[i_{1}+1,i_{2}]-u[i_{1},i_{2}])/h&\text{if }i_{1}<N_{1}\\
0&\text{if }i_{1}=N_{1}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\nabla_{2}u)[i]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}(u[i_{1},i_{2}+1]-u[i_{1},i_{2}])/h&\text{if }i_{2}<N_{2}\\
0&\text{if }i_{2}=N_{2}\,.\end{cases}

Its adjoint is given by
\nabla^{*}(v_{1},v_{2})=\nabla^{*}_{1}v_{1}+\nabla_{2}^{*}v_{2}=:-\operatorname{div}(v_{1},v_{2})
where
\operatorname{div}\colon\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is the discrete divergence operator and for
(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{H}
we have

\displaystyle(\nabla^{*}_{1}v_{1})[i]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-(v_{1}[i_{1},i_{2}]-v_{1}[i_{1}-1,i_{2}])/h&\text{if }1<i_{1}<N_{1}\\
-v_{1}[1,i_{2}]&\text{if }i_{1}=1\\
\phantom{-}v_{1}[N_{1}-1,i_{2}]&\text{if }i_{1}=N_{1}\end{cases}

\displaystyle(\nabla^{*}_{2}v_{2})[i]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}-(v_{2}[i_{1},i_{2}]-v_{2}[i_{1},i_{2}-1])/h&\text{if }1<i_{2}<N_{2}\\
-v_{2}[i_{1},1]&\text{if }i_{2}=1\\
\phantom{-}v_{2}[i_{1},N_{2}-1]&\text{if }i_{2}=N_{2}\,.\end{cases}

Finally we write
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}\coloneqq(\nabla u_{1},\dots,\nabla u_{K})
for the discrete gradient applied componentwise to
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}^{K}
.

\diamond

The discrete (isotropic) TV semi-norm of some image
u\in\mathbb{H}
is defined as

\lVert\nabla_{1}u\rVert_{1,2}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\sqrt{(\nabla_{1}u[i])^{2}+(\nabla_{2}u[i])^{2}}\,.

We write
\mathbb{A}=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{H}^{K}\mid\boldsymbol{u}\geq 0\wedge\sum_{k}u_{k}=1\}
for the discrete admissible set and
i_{\mathbb{A}}
for the corresponding indicator function.

\diamond

Let
\mathbbm{1}\in\mathbb{H}
denotes the all-ones image,
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\mathbb{H}^{K}
the discrete feature map with
K
-channels to be segmented and
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{k})_{k=1}^{K}\in\mathbb{H}^{K}
the desired segmentation function.
The discretization of the data fitting term in the reduced energy is written as
\lVert\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{1}
where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq(m(u_{k}),m(1-u_{k}))_{k=1}^{K}

(3.1)

\displaystyle m(u_{k})\coloneqq u_{k}\cdot(A(u_{k})\mathbbm{1}-\phi_{k})^{2}

(3.2)

\displaystyle A(u)\coloneqq\langle u_{k},\phi_{k}\rangle/\lvert u_{k}\rvert_{1,\epsilon}\,.

(3.3)

Here
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm applied to
\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}^{2K}
and the averages
A(\cdot)
use the approximation
\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}\coloneqq\sum_{i}(u[i]^{2}+\epsilon)^{1/2}
of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, making it single-valued and twice differentiable.

Using the notions given above, the discrete reduced energy functional reads

\mathcal{R}_{\boldsymbol{\phi},\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq i_{\mathbb{A}}(\boldsymbol{u})+\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lVert\nabla u_{k}\rVert_{1,2}+\lVert\boldsymbol{M}(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{1,1}\,.

(3.4)

Optimization problem (
3.4
) is a non-convex, non-smooth and a challenging large scale problem to be solved. Its particular structure, however, allows to apply various splitting type optimization algorithms. In particular, we will demonstrate that it can be solved with the algorithm of
[
15
]
, which itself is a generalization of the Chambolle-Pock algorithm
[
20
]
for convex problems.

3.2
Nonlinear primal dual algorithm

Our algorithm is a particular instance of the primal dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm of
[
15
]
, which is a generic algorithmic framework solving minimization problems of the following composite form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\mathcal{F}(K(x))+\mathcal{G}(x)\,,

(3.5)

where
K\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{Y}
is a possibly nonlinear mapping between Hilbert spaces
\mathbb{X}
,
\mathbb{Y}
and
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathbb{Y}\to[0,\infty]
and
\mathcal{G}\colon\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
are convex and lower semi-continuous functionals. Essential components of the PDHG algorithm are the proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{\mathcal{H}}\colon\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}
and the Fenchel conjugate
\mathcal{H}^{*}\colon\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
, respectively, associated to a given functional
\mathcal{H}\colon\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty]
, defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{prox}_{\mathcal{H}}(x)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{X}}\mathcal{H}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert z-x\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{X}}\langle x,z\rangle-\mathcal{H}(z)\,.

Note that the nonlinear PDHG algorithm itself is an extension of primal dual optimization scheme proposed in
[
20
]
from linear to nonlinear operators.

The PDHG algorithm for (
3.5
) with parameters
\sigma,\tau>0
and
\theta\in[0,1]
generates a sequence
(x^{n},\bar{x}^{n},y^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y})^{\mathbb{N}}
by

\displaystyle y^{n+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{prox}_{\sigma\mathcal{F}^{*}}(y^{n}+\sigma K(\bar{x}^{n}))

(3.6)

\displaystyle x^{n+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{prox}_{\tau\mathcal{G}}(x^{n}-\tau K^{\prime}(x^{n})^{*}y^{n})

(3.7)

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq x^{n+1}+\theta(x^{n+1}-x^{n})\,,

(3.8)

for some initializations
x^{0},\bar{x}^{0}\in\mathbb{X}
and
y^{0}\in\mathbb{Y}
. Here
K^{\prime}(x)^{*}
is the adjoint of the derivative of
K
at
x
. Convergence of (
3.6
)-(
3.8
) to saddle points of (
3.5
) has been analyzed in
[
15
]
.

3.3
Derivation of algorithm

Inspecting the functional (
3.4
) to be minimized and the PDHG algorithm (
3.6
)-(
3.8
) for the generic problem (
3.5
), we see that it can be applied to our setting with

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}^{K}

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}^{2K}\times\mathbb{H}^{2K}

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\nabla},\boldsymbol{M})

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\lambda\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{2,1}+\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\rVert_{1,1}

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=i_{\mathbb{A}}\,.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{M}
defined by (
3.1
)-(
3.3
) is nonlinear and therefore
K
is nonlinear, too. Further, the functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
are convex and lower semi-continuous. The actual practical implementation requires computing proximal mappings, derivatives, and Fenchel conjugates. This will be done in the following.

\diamond

Fenchel conjugate of
\mathcal{F}
:

The functional
\mathcal{F}
is separable in
\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
, and therefore its Fenchel conjugate is a sum of the Fenchel conjugates of
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2,1}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{1,1}
, which are both well known. Actually, they are given by indicator functions of the unit ball of the dual norms and therefore

\mathcal{F}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=i_{2,\infty}(\boldsymbol{v}/\lambda)+i_{\infty,\infty}(\boldsymbol{w})\,.

Here
i_{q,\infty}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{q,\infty}^{*}
denotes the indicator function of the unit ball
\{\boldsymbol{v}\mid\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{q,\infty}\leq 1\}\subseteq(\mathbb{H}^{K})^{2}
for the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{q,\infty}
for
q=2,\infty
.

\diamond

Proximal operators of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
,
\mathcal{G}
:

Let us next compute the proximal operator of
\mathcal{F}^{*}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=i_{2,\infty}(\boldsymbol{v}/\lambda)+i_{\infty,\infty}(\boldsymbol{w})
which is the separable sum of two indicator functions. The proximal operator of the indicator function
i_{S}
of some set
S
is known to be given as the projection on
S
.
The projections
P_{\infty,p}\colon(\mathbb{H}^{K})^{2}\to(\mathbb{H}^{K})^{2}
onto the unit ball in the
(\infty,q)
-norm for
q=2,\infty
can easily be computed and given by

\displaystyle(P_{2,\infty}(\boldsymbol{v}))[i,k]=\frac{v[i,k]}{\max\{1,(v_{1}[i,k]^{2}+v_{2}[i,k])^{1/2}\}}

\displaystyle(P_{\infty,\infty}(p))[i,k]=\Bigl(\frac{v_{j}[i,k]}{\max\{1,v_{j}[i,k]\}}\Bigr)_{j=1,2}\,.

Thus the proximal operator of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is given by

\operatorname{prox}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})=(P_{2,\infty}(\boldsymbol{v}/\lambda),P_{\infty,\infty}(\boldsymbol{w}))\,.

The proximal operator of
\mathcal{G}=i_{\mathbb{A}}
is the orthogonal projection
P_{\mathbb{A}}
onto the simplex
\mathbb{A}
. In this case, no explicit formula is available. However, the projection can be computed with a finite number of steps, and for our implementation we use the algorithm proposed in
[
21
]
for that purpose.

\diamond

Derivative computation:

Finally we compute the adjoint of the derivative of
K=(\boldsymbol{\nabla},\boldsymbol{M})
. The discrete gradient operator is linear and therefore
\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}
. Further from (
3.1
)-(
3.3
) we see that computing the derivative of
K
amounts
to computing the derivative of

m(u)=u\cdot\left(\frac{\langle u,\phi\rangle}{\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}}\mathbbm{1}-\phi\right)^{2}\,.

For that purpose we write
\phi,u\in\mathbb{H}
as column vectors,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{N})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

with
N\coloneqq N_{1},N_{2}
.
By the chain rule we find that the derivative
(Dm)(u)
and its adjoint are given by the following
N\times N
matrices

\displaystyle(Dm)(u)

\displaystyle=T(u)^{2}-2U(u)T(u)Q(u)

(3.9)

\displaystyle(Dm)(u)^{*}

\displaystyle=T(u)^{2}-2Q(u)^{\intercal}T(u)U(u)

(3.10)

where

\displaystyle T(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\langle u,\phi\rangle\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}^{-1}\mathbbm{1}-\phi\bigr)

\displaystyle U(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\operatorname{diag}\bigl(u\bigl)

\displaystyle Q(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}\cdot\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}^{-2}\cdot\bigl(\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}\phi-\langle u,\phi\rangle\lvert u\rvert_{1,\epsilon}^{-1}u\bigr)^{\intercal}\,.

Note that
T(u)
,
U(u)
are diagonal and therefore self-adjoint.

Using the ingredients computed above, we obtain the following proposed Algorithm
1
for generating a sequence

(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n},\boldsymbol{v}^{n},\boldsymbol{w}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in(\mathbb{H}^{K}\times\mathbb{H}^{K}\times\mathbb{H}^{2K}\times\mathbb{H}^{2K})^{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}^{n}
approximates the segmentation mask and
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n},\boldsymbol{v}^{n},\boldsymbol{w}^{n}
are auxiliary quantities derived from the PDHG algorithm. The application of the convergence analysis
[

]
requires the Aubin property as well as a smallness condition for the dual variable. It is an open problem whether these properties are satisfied for our functional.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a framework for variational image segmentation using feature map lifting and minimizing a multichannel segmentation functional. Input channels can be given either in a natural form, such as RGB images, or can be extracted by some pre-filtering method. We demonstrated the effectiveness of our method on several images, such as texture images or multi-modal medical images, and achieved convincing results also in comparison with related variational approaches. The method can distinguish a number of different regions, and is particularly suitable for applications in medical imaging. As main theoretical result, we have shown existence, stability and convergence with respect to the distorted input data. For future extension of the proposed framework, the combination with deep learning is planned, in particular, the feature maps extracted in this paper by means of pre-filtering might be obtained by a neural network. The combination of the strengths of modern deep learning and classical energy based segmentation methods could further improve the existing results, and enable more complex problems to be solved. In addition, a detailed comparison with other segmentation methods that also utilize feature lifting will be conducted.

The identification of appropriate feature maps is the main step in the proposed framework and is considered as its main current limitation. In order to obtain practically realistic results, the specific lifting has a significant impact on the final segmentation results. In this work, we focus on textured images, where the appropriate pre-filtering can be selected manually. In a recent follow-up work, we have proposed an automated lifting using convolutional networks
[
26
]
. Modern self-supervised deep learning methods, together with appropriate loss functions, can be used to obtain reasonable image decompositions. For example, in
[
26
]
, a loss function that minimizes the correlation between different feature maps, along with a norm constraint on the feature maps, has been applied. Even with this, it is still somewhat unclear which segmentation is actually targeted by the lifting. To overcome this limitation, in future work. we plan to incorporate user guidance by allowing the user to roughly select representative regions for the different classes provided, in order to provide realistic results. It should also be noted that in the current setting, the pre-filtering should ensure a good separation of the channels.
