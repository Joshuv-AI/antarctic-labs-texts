Tunneling conductivity fast modulated by optically-dressed electrons in graphene and a dice lattice
Andrii Iurov 1 , Liubov Zhemchuzhna 1,2 , Godfrey Gumbs 2,3 , Danhong Huang 4 , and Paula Fekete 5

Abstract
Based on the transmission coefficient of tunneling electrons, we have presented tunneling current and conductivity across a square-potential barrier for both graphene and α \alpha - 𝒯 3 \mathcal{T}_{3} lattices under a linearly-polarized off-resonant dressing field. The presence of such a dressing field introduces an anisotropy factor in the energy dispersion of tunneling electrons so that the cross section of a Dirac-cone appears as elliptical. Consequently, the field-polarization controlled major axis of the ellipse will be misaligned with the normal direction of a barrier layer in the tunneling system, which exhibits an asymmetric Klein-paradox for an off-normal-direction tunneling. The resulting tunneling current in this system is calculated by using a transmission coefficient and a longitudinal group velocity (different from a longitudinal momentum) of electrons. By presenting numerically calculated tunneling conductivity modified by a laser dressing field, we demonstrate a significant enhancement of electrical conductivity by external laser-field intensity, which is expected to be crucial in application of ultrafast optical modulation of opto-electronic devices for photo-detection and fiber-optic communication.

II
Asymmetric Klein tunneling resulting from elliptical Dirac dispersions

The energy dispersion of electrons in a crystal usually appears spherical around a highly-symmetric valley such as the
\Gamma
point, within the first Brillouin zone. However, in the presence of strong laser-electron interaction, this energy dispersion is modified and becomes anisotropic, e.g., an elliptical one with a long-axis parallel to the polarization direction of an incident laser field. If the long-axis of the elliptical energy dispersion appears misaligned with the normal direction of the square-barrier layer in a tunneling structure, we expect to find that the tunneling conductivity
will vary with this misalignment angle. Therefore, the first step for computing tunneling and conductivity in our system is to derive anisotropic dressed states of electrons under a linearly-polarized dressing field and to full understand how this anisotropy depends on the intensity of an applied optical field in different Dirac materials. Even though we only consider in this paper graphene (
\alpha\to 0
) and a dice lattice (
\alpha\to 1
) as two extreme limiting cases for the
\alpha-\mathcal{T}_{3}
model, it would be useful to present a comprehensive description for the induced anisotropy by a linearly-polarized dressing field in all
\alpha-\mathcal{T}_{3}
materials.

For a two-dimensional (2D)
\alpha-\mathcal{T}_{3}
material, the low-energy Hamiltonian takes the form

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$}\,|\,\tau,\phi)=\hbar v_{F}\,\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&k^{\tau}_{-}\cos\phi&0\\
0&0&k^{\tau}_{-}\sin\phi\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right]+H.c.\ ,

(1)

where the geometry phase

is directly related to the relative hopping parameter
\alpha
, and
k^{\tau}_{\pm}=k^{\tau}_{x}\pm ik^{\tau}_{y}
are obtained from components of the electron wave vector
\mbox{\boldmath$k$}=\{k_{x},k_{y}\}
.
Once an optical dressing field
with a linear polarization

1

\sin\beta\end{array}\right]\,\cos(\omega t)\ ,

\left[\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\right]_{\rm max}=\tan^{-1}[a_{1}(\lambda_{0})]-\cot^{-1}[a_{1}(\lambda_{0})]\ .

(25)

Therefore, the reach to

a_{1}(\lambda_{0})
or misalignment angle

III
Sequential-tunneling current and conductivity

Under a DC electric field
E_{\rm dc}
along the
x^{\prime}
direction for tunneling transport of electrons within monolayer graphene (with
\alpha=0
), which includes a potential barrier distributed along the
x^{\prime}
direction with a barrier width
W_{B}
and a barrier height
V_{0}
, the sequential-tunneling sheet current
J_{s}(E_{\rm dc}\,|\,E_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)
in this system is calculated as

\displaystyle J_{s}(E_{\rm dc}\,|\,E_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g_{s}g_{v}\,e}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\int d^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$k$}\,\mathcal{T}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$}),\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,|\,\lambda_{0},\beta]\,V_{G,x^{\prime}}^{\,\gamma=1}(\lambda_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$k$})\,

(26)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left\{f_{0}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})]-f_{0}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})+eE_{\rm dc}W_{B}]\right\}\ ,

where
\mathcal{T}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$}),\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,|\,\lambda_{0},\beta]
, which is independent of
E_{\rm dc}
for a thin barrier layer,
represents the coefficient for sequential tunneling of electrons through the square-potential barrier,

g_{s}=2
and
g_{v}=2
are spin and valley degeneracies of graphene sheet, and

k
is the wave vector of electrons within the 2D lattice.
Additionally,
f_{0}(x)=\{1+\exp[(x-\mu)/k_{B}T]\}^{-1}
is the Fermi function for thermal-equilibrium electrons,

\mu
and
T
are the graphene chemical potential and the system temperature, and the incident energy

\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})
is below the Fermi energy of electrons in the source electrode.

For a thin barrier with
eE_{\rm dc}W_{B}\ll\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})
, we find from Eq. (
26
) that

f_{0}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})]-f_{0}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})+eE_{\rm dc}W_{B}]=eE_{\rm dc}W_{B}\,\left[-\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})}\,f_{0}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})]\right]\backsimeq eE_{\rm dc}W_{B}\,\delta[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})-E^{*}_{F}]\ ,

(27)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})=\hbar v_{F}\sqrt{k^{2}_{x}+a_{0}^{2}(\lambda_{0})k_{y}^{2}}
,

E^{*}_{F}
stands for the Fermi energy of graphene layer at low temperatures
k_{B}T\ll E^{*}_{F}
, and
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity.
In our computations of Eq. (
26
), we have used the following relations:

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$k$}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}k\,dk\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{\bf k}\ ,

(28)

\displaystyle\theta_{\bf k}=\tan^{-1}\left\{\frac{1}{a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})}\,\tan\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)\,\right\}\ ,

(29)

\displaystyle d\theta_{\bf k}=a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\,\left\{\cos^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)\left[a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)\right]\right\}^{-1}\,d\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\ .

(30)

Meanwhile, we also acquire

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}V_{G,x^{\prime}}^{\gamma}(\lambda_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$k$})\\
V_{G,y^{\prime}}^{\gamma}(\lambda_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$k$})\end{array}\right]=\frac{\gamma\,v_{F}}{\sqrt{k_{x^{\prime}}^{2}+a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\,k_{y^{\prime}}^{2}}}\,\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k_{x^{\prime}}\\
a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\,k_{y^{\prime}}\end{array}\right]=\frac{\gamma\,v_{F}}{\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\,k_{y}^{2}}}\,\hat{\mathbb{R}}(-\beta)\left[\begin{array}[]{c}k_{x}\\
a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\,k_{y}\end{array}\right]\ ,

(31)

or simply
V_{G,x^{\prime}}^{\gamma=1}(\lambda_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$k$})\equiv v_{F}\cos\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\right)
.

Finally, the optically-modulated sheet conductivity
\sigma_{s}(E^{*}_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)
of the system is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(E^{*}_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{J_{s}(E_{\rm dc}\,|\,E^{*}_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)}{E_{\rm dc}}=\frac{e^{2}W_{B}}{\pi^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}k\,dk\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta_{{\bf k}}\,\mathcal{T}[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$}),\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,|\,\lambda_{0},\beta]\,

(32)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle V_{G,x^{\prime}}^{\,\gamma=1}(\lambda_{0},\mbox{\boldmath$k$})\,\delta[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})-E^{*}_{F}]\ .

In Eq. (
32
), the delta function
\delta[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})-E_{F}]
for the anisotropic dispersions
\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})=\hbar v_{F}kf_{0}(\theta_{\bf k})
with
f_{0}(\theta_{\bf k})=\sqrt{1-[1-a_{0}^{2}(\lambda_{0})]\,\sin^{2}(\theta_{\bf k})}

and
a_{0}(\lambda_{0})<1
could be written as

\delta[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})-E^{*}_{F}]=\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial k}\,\left[\mathcal{E}^{\,\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})-E^{*}_{F}\right]\right\}^{-1}\,\delta[k-k^{*}_{F}(\theta_{\bf k})]=\frac{1}{\hbar v_{F}f_{0}(\theta_{\bf k})}\,\delta[k-k^{*}_{F}(\theta_{\bf k})]\ ,

(33)

where
k^{*}_{F}(\theta_{\bf k})=E^{*}_{F}/[\hbar v_{F}f_{0}(\theta_{\bf k})]
is the
\theta_{\bf k}
-dependent Fermi momentum for an elliptical
Dirac-cone dispersion. By using the result in Eq. (
33
) and considering a forward incidence of particles with
|\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}|\leq\pi/2
, the conductivity in Eq. (
32
) can be
calculated explicitly as

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(E^{*}_{F},\lambda_{0},\beta)=\frac{e^{2}W_{B}E^{*}_{F}}{\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}v_{F}}\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\,d\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,\cos\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\right)\,\mathcal{T}[E^{*}_{F},\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,|\,\lambda_{0},\beta]

(34)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)}{a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)/a_{0}^{2}(\lambda_{0})}\right]\left[\frac{1+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)/a_{0}^{4}(\lambda_{0})}{1+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)/a_{0}^{2}(\lambda_{0})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2e^{2}}{h}\right)\frac{E^{*}_{F}W_{B}}{\hbar v_{F}}\int\limits_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\,\frac{d\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}}{\pi}\,\cos\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\right)\,\mathcal{T}[E^{*}_{F},\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}\,|\,\lambda_{0},\beta]\left[\frac{1+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)}{a_{0}^{2}(\lambda_{0})+\tan^{2}\left(\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}+\beta\right)}\right]\ ,

where
\mathcal{E}^{\gamma=1}_{0}(\mbox{\boldmath$k$})=E^{*}_{F}
represents the kinetic energy of tunneling electrons at the Fermi level of graphene.

Finalizing our calculations, we also obtain the density-of-states for asymmetric Dirac-cone electrons in graphene, as well as the dependence of their Fermi energy
E^{*}_{F}
on the electron density
n_{e}

Huang et al. 2018
. In general, the density-of-states
\rho_{S}(\mathbb{E})
is defined as

\rho_{S}(\mathbb{E})=\int\,\frac{d^{2}\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\sum\limits_{\gamma=\pm 1}\,\sum\limits_{\xi,\sigma=\pm 1}\,\delta\left[\mathbb{E}-\mathcal{E}_{0}^{\gamma}(k)\right]\,,

(35)

where
\sigma
and
\xi
refer to the spin and valley indices.
As an example, we will only consider electron states with
\gamma=1
, while the situation for hole states with
\gamma=-1
will be exactly symmetric.
Meanwhile, there exist valley and spin degeneracies so that
\sum\limits_{\xi,\sigma=\pm 1}=4
.
Moreover, Eq. (
35
) looks identical to a part of the integrand in Eq. (
34
) for conductivity, therefore, we can easily rewrite Eq. (
35
) as

\rho_{S}(\mathbb{E})=\mathcal{C}_{\lambda_{0}}\,\mathbb{E}\equiv\frac{\mathbb{E}}{(\pi\hbar v_{F})^{2}}\,\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\,d\theta_{\bf k}\,\left[\frac{1+\tan^{2}\theta_{{\bf k}}}{1+a^{2}_{0}(\lambda_{0})\tan^{2}\theta_{{\bf k}}}\right]\ ,

(36)

which is still proportional to energy
\mathbb{E}
although its coefficient depends on the anisotropy factor
a_{0}(\lambda_{0})<1
, and
is reduced with increasing light intensity, in correspondence with the increase of tunneling current due to the anisotropic dispersion.
Furthermore, the Fermi energy
E_{F}
of the system is calculated as
E^{*}_{F}=(2n_{e}/\mathcal{C}_{\lambda_{0}})^{1/2}
which reduces to a standard expression
E^{*}_{F}=\hbar v_{F}\,\sqrt{\pi n_{e}}
after taking
a_{0}(\lambda_{0})=1
.

The tunneling conductivity
\sigma_{s}(E^{*}_{F},\lambda_{0})

It is also crucial that

IV
Summary and Remarks

The effort of our work has been directed towards carrying out a rigorous investigation of the tunneling conductance and the currents through a square potential barrier in graphene and a dice lattice in the presence of irradiation-induced anisotropy of the electronic states and, specifically, their energy dispersions. In order to do that, we have derived an analytical expression for the conductivity as a function of all the crucial tuning parameters: an incoming electron energy
\mathcal{E}_{0}
, angle of incidence
\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}
, anisotropy
a(\lambda_{0})
and misalignment angle
\beta
between the directions of the light polarization and the direct incidence on the potential barrier.

For a finite angle
\beta
, the direction of motion of an incoming particle is determined by its group velocity
\vec{V}_{G}^{\gamma}(\lambda_{0},{\bf k})
and its angle
\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}
relative to the normal incidence on the potential barrier, which is also used to distinguish the transmitted and reflected waves in the barrier region, but not the wavevector angle
\theta_{\bf k}
. In our case, however, both these angles enter the equations for conductance simultaneously, which brings in some new unexpected relations and physical behavior.

We have found that the tunneling conductance has only non-essential and periodic dependence on the misalignment angle
\beta
. Its dependence on anisotropy consists of two terms with competing effects: a monotonically increasing trend coming from the mismatch between angles
\Theta_{{\bf V}^{\prime}}^{(1)}
and
\theta_{\bf k}
, and a separate decreasing dependence which exactly reflects the way how the density of states for such anisotropic Dirac electrons is related to their Fermi energy.

The external optical dressing field could modify the sequential-tunneling current of electrons with the help of polarization angle
\beta
and light-interaction strength
\lambda_{0}
. In addition to the applied
E_{\rm dc}
, the sequential tunneling of electrons can also be enhanced by the Fermi energy
E_{F}
in two electrodes and the barrier width
W_{B}
. A strong dependence of the tunneling conductance and the current on the electron doping and its fine-tuning by the applied irradiation leads to a possibility to engineer an electronic device with desirable characteristics. Therefore, our work is expected to be an important step in understanding the radiation-modulated properties and behavior of such optoelectronic devices.
