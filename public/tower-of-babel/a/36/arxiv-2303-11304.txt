Resource-Dependent Complexity of Quantum Channels
Roy Araiza 1 , Yidong Chen 2 , Marius Junge 1 , Peixue Wu 3

Abstract
We introduce a new framework for quantifying the complexity of quantum channels, grounded in a suitably chosen resource set. This class of convex functions is designed to analyze the complexity of both open and closed quantum systems. By leveraging Lipschitz norms inspired by quantum optimal transport theory, we rigorously establish the fundamental properties of this complexity measure. The flexibility in selecting the resource set allows us to derive effective lower bounds for gate complexities and simulation costs of both Hamiltonian simulations and dynamics of open quantum systems. Additionally, we demonstrate that this complexity measure exhibits linear growth for random quantum circuits and finite-dimensional quantum simulations, up to the Brown-Susskind threshold.

Background.

Definition of the Complexity Measure
.—Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is a finite-dimensional quantum system (Hilbert space). Denote:

-
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
as the set of density operators on
\mathcal{H}
, corresponding to quantum states.

-
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
as the set of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
.

-
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
as the set of Hermitian operators, corresponding to observables in quantum mechanics.

We say that
S
is a
resource set
on system
\mathcal{H}
if
S
is a subset of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. For any resource set
S
, we can define an induced
Lipschitz semi-norm
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
given by

|||x|||_{S}:=\sup_{s\in S}\|[s,x]\|_{\mathrm{op}},\quad x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}),

(1.1)

where
[s,x]=sx-xs
is the commutator, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{op}}
is the operator norm, given by the largest singular value of the operator.

When
S
contains only Hermitian operators, we can consider
s\in S
as generating a unitary evolution
x(t)=e^{its}xe^{-its}
on observables (Heisenberg picture). The commutator relates to the time derivative of the evolution of observables at time
t=0
:

\left.\frac{d}{dt}x(t)\right|_{t=0}=i[s,x].

(1.2)

Therefore,
|||x|||_{S}
represents the maximal rate at which the observable
x
changes under the dynamics generated by the elements of
S
.

The Lipschitz semi-norm naturally induces an Earth Mover’s distance, also referred to as the Wasserstein-1 distance
[
46
]
in the context of quantum states. In fact, any semi-norm on observables induces a true distance on quantum states via the dual formulation
[
54
,
55
,
28
]
. For any quantum states
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, the Earth Mover’s distance is defined by

W_{S}(\rho,\sigma)=\sup\left\{\left|\operatorname{tr}(x(\rho-\sigma))\right|:|||x|||_{S}\leq 1,\ x=x^{\dagger}\right\}.

(1.3)

In an
n
-qudit system, if we choose
S
to be the set of single-qudit Pauli gates, our Earth Mover’s distance
W_{S}(\rho,\sigma)
recovers the Wasserstein-1 distance introduced in
[
46
]
. Compared to the expression of the Wasserstein-1 distance, the Lipschitz norm (
1.1
) as the dual picture is easier to compute or estimate in some cases.

We introduce the complexity measure based on the above Lipschitz semi-norm and its induced distance on quantum states. Suppose
\Phi
is a quantum channel acting on density operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and denote
\Phi^{*}
as the dual map acting on observables on
\mathcal{H}
. Denote
\operatorname{tr}
as the trace; we have

\operatorname{tr}(\Phi(\rho)X)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho\,\Phi^{*}(X)),

(1.4)

where
\rho
is a density operator and
X\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
is an observable. Given a resource set
S
, our resource-dependent complexity
C_{S}
is defined by

\displaystyle C_{S}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})}W_{S}\left(\Phi(\rho),\rho\right)

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|||x|||_{S}\leq 1,\ x=x^{\dagger}}\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\mathrm{op}}.

(1.6)

Intuitively, our resource-dependent complexity measure quantifies the effort required to transform an arbitrary state
\rho
into
\Phi(\rho)
using operations from the resource set
S
. In the Heisenberg picture, it measures the maximal change that
\Phi^{*}
induces on observables relative to the geometry induced by
S
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Definitions and Basics

A quantum channel
\Phi:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
is a linear map that is completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP). The dual map
\Phi^{*}
, is unital and completely positive (UCP).

Given a resource set
S\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, recall that we define the induced Lipschitz semi-norm (
1.1
) on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
as

|||x|||_{S}:=\sup_{s\in S}\|[s,x]\|_{\operatorname{op}},\quad x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, where
[s,x]=sx-xs
denotes the commutator, and
\|\cdot\|_{\operatorname{op}}
is the operator norm.

The environment-assisted (also known as complete in
[
49
]
) Lipschitz norm is defined when we allow the quantum system to be coupled with an additional
d
-dimensional quantum system
\mathcal{H}_{d}
:

|||X_{d}|||_{S}^{d}:=\sup_{s\in S}\|[s\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d},X_{d}]\|_{\operatorname{op}},\quad X_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{d}),

(2.1)

where
\mathbb{I}_{d}
denotes the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
.

Then, the resource-dependent complexity of a quantum channel
\Phi
is defined as
C_{S}(\Phi)=\sup_{|||x|||_{S}\leq 1,\ x=x^{\dagger}}\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\operatorname{op}}.
For notational simplicity, we will omit the condition
x=x^{\dagger}
from now on.

We also introduce the environment-assisted resource-dependent complexity, which has better mathematical properties and can provide us with sharper lower bounds in some applications. It is defined by

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)=\sup_{d\geq 1}\sup_{|||X_{d}|||_{S}^{d}\leq 1}\|(\Phi^{*}\otimes\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})})(X_{d})-X_{d}\|_{\operatorname{op}},

(2.2)

where
\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})}
is the identity super-operator on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
. As a remark,
cb
is the abbreviation of completely bounded
[
49
]
.

To simplify notation, following the convention in
[
49
]
, the above can be abbreviated as

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)=\sup_{|||X|||^{\operatorname{cb}}_{S}\leq 1}\|\Phi^{*}(X)-X\|_{\operatorname{cb}}.

(2.3)

Using elementary properties such as submultiplicativity and the triangle inequality for (semi-)norms, we can show that
C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)
has the following desirable properties (Since the proof is straightforward, we refer the interested reader to
[
2
]
for the full mathematical details in the von Neumann algebra setting):

Lemma 2.1
.

1.

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)=0
if and only if
\Phi=\operatorname{id}
.

2.

(Subadditivity under concatenation)
For any quantum channels
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we have

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi\circ\Psi)\leq C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)+C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Psi).

3.

(Convexity)
For any probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
and quantum channels
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i\in I}
, we have

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}\left(\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}\Phi_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi_{i}).

(2.4)

4.

(Maximal complexity given by expected length of
S
)
Under the assumption that the commutant
S^{\prime}
is a
*
-algebra,
\Phi^{*}|_{S^{\prime}}=\operatorname{id}
, and
\kappa(S)
is defined in (
2.8
), we have

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)\leq\kappa(S)\|\Phi-\operatorname{id}\|_{\diamond}.

(2.5)

5.

(Tensor additivity)
For any quantum channels
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we have

C_{S\vee S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi\otimes\Psi)=C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Phi)+C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\Psi),

(2.6)

where
S\vee S:=\{\mathbb{I}\otimes s_{1},\ s_{2}\otimes\mathbb{I}\mid s_{1},s_{2}\in S\}
.

2.2
Estimates of Complexity

We first introduce the definition of the expected length
\kappa(S)
of the resource set
S
, which provides a universal upper bound for the resource-dependent complexity of any quantum channel. Throughout this letter, we will assume that
S^{\prime}:=\{x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}):xs=sx,\ \forall s\in S\}
is a
*
-algebra.

Define the conditional expectation
E_{\text{fix}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to S^{\prime}
such that
E_{\text{fix}}
is unital and satisfies

E_{\text{fix}}(s_{1}Xs_{2})=s_{1}E_{\text{fix}}(X)s_{2},\quad\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in S^{\prime},\ X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(2.7)

Recall that such super-operators are unital and completely positive; in particular,
E_{\text{fix}}^{*}
is a quantum channel. We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for a brief overview of the properties we need in this paper. General theory regarding conditional expectations can be found in
[
58
]
.

A typical example occurs when
S^{\prime}
consists of multiples of the identity (which is the case if
S
is given by single Pauli gates). In this scenario,
E_{\text{fix}}(X)=\tr(\sigma X)\mathbb{I}
with
\sigma
being a density operator, and correspondingly,
E_{\text{fix}}^{*}(\rho)=\sigma
.

We may now define the
quantum expected length
of the resource set
S
by

\kappa(S):=C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}}^{*}).

(2.8)

In general, it is challenging to compute the complexity numerically, but we can provide lower bound estimates by constructing a certificate observable that nearly achieves the supremum in (
1.6
). We illustrate a simple case: in the
n
-qubit system, suppose

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\{\sigma_{x}^{j}:1\leq j\leq n\}\cup\{\sigma_{y}^{j}:1\leq j\leq n\},

where
\sigma_{x}^{j}
and
\sigma_{y}^{j}
are Pauli-X and Pauli-Y operators acting on the
j
-th qubit.

Using the observable
X=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{x}^{j}
, we can achieve a lower bound of
\kappa(S)
for the trace-preserving conditional expectation
E_{\text{fix}}
. Indeed, one can easily verify that
S^{\prime}=\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{2^{n}}
and
E_{\text{fix}}(X)=\frac{\tr(X)}{2^{n}}\mathbb{I}
. Then,

\displaystyle|||X|||_{S}=\sup_{s\in S}\|[s,X]\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\sup_{1\leq k\leq n}\|[\sigma_{y}^{k},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{x}^{j}]\|_{\infty}=\|[\sigma_{y},\sigma_{x}]\|=2;

\displaystyle\|X\|_{\operatorname{op}}=n,

which provides a lower bound

\displaystyle C_{S}(E_{\text{fix}}^{*})\geq\frac{\|E_{\text{fix}}(X)-X\|_{\operatorname{op}}}{|||X|||_{S}}=\frac{n}{2}.

On the other hand, using the tensor product structure
E_{\text{fix}}=E_{\tau}^{\otimes n}
, where
E_{\tau}=\frac{\tr(X)}{2}\mathbb{I}_{2}
acts on a single-qubit system, we can show via tensor additivity that
C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}}^{*})\leq n
. In summary, the quantum expected length with a resource set given by single Pauli gates has order
n
.

In the
n
-qubit system, we can also construct resource set

S=\{\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}:1\leq k\leq 2^{n}\},

(2.9)

where for each
k=1,2,\cdots,2^{n}
, we define

\ket{\psi_k}=\ket{i_1\cdots i_n},\quad k-1=\sum_{t=1}^{n}i_{t}2^{t-1}.

(2.10)

Using the similar argument as before, we can show that
C_{S}(E_{\rm fix}^{*})\geq c2^{n}
for some universal constant
c>0
, using the testing operator
X=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k\outerproduct{\psi_k}{\psi_k}
, which is a sharp contrast to Wasserstein complexity, which can achieve at most
n
(see
[
39
]
).

3
Comparison of Complexities

3.1
Gate Complexity

For quantum circuits, a natural measure of complexity is the gate count. Suppose a gate set
S\subseteq\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
is given. Then, the (approximate) gate count for
U\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
relative to
S
, given a fixed error threshold
\delta\geq 0
, is defined by
[
30
,
29
,
44
]
:

\operatorname{Count}_{\delta}^{S}(U):=\min\left\{l\geq 1:\|U-V_{1}\cdots V_{l}\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq\delta,\ V_{i}\in S\right\}.

(3.1)

Any unitary
U
can be viewed as a quantum channel
\text{ad}_{U}
:

\text{ad}_{U}(\rho)=U\rho U^{\dagger}.

(3.2)

Suppose we can implement a circuit using a sequence of random gates, and the average gate (a mixed-unitary channel) is close to the target circuit. This motivates the convexified gate count, defined by
[
14
]
:

\displaystyle l^{S}_{\delta}(U):=\inf\big\{l\geq 1:\exists\ \mu\in\mathcal{P}(S^{l})\ \text{such that}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\text{ad}_{U}-\int_{S^{l}}\text{ad}_{u_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\text{ad}_{u_{l}}\ d\mu(u_{1},\dots,u_{l})\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\delta\big\},

where
\mathcal{P}(S^{l})
is the set of probability measures on
S^{l}=S\times\cdots\times S
, the Cartesian product of
S
with itself
l
times.

Note that using the well-known relation for two unitary operators
U,V
,

\|\text{ad}_{U}-\text{ad}_{V}\|_{\diamond}=2\|U-V\|_{\operatorname{op}},

we have
l^{S}_{\delta}(U)\leq\operatorname{Count}^{S}_{\delta/2}(U)
, which follows directly by choosing the random gates as the deterministic ones in (
3.3
).

Our notion of complexity provides a lower bound for the (convexified) gate count:

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
and a resource set
S
of unitary operators, we have

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\text{ad}_{U})\leq l^{S}_{0}(U).

(3.4)

Moreover, if
U\in S^{\prime\prime}
(the double commutant of
S
, defined as the operators commuting with
S^{\prime}
), then for any
\delta\geq 0
,

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\text{ad}_{U})\leq l^{S}_{\delta}(U)+\delta\,\kappa(S),

(3.5)

where
\kappa(S)
is the quantum expected length defined in (
2.8
).

Note that in most cases,
S^{\prime}
consists of scalars; thus,
S^{\prime\prime}
is the entire matrix algebra, and the technical assumption
U\in S^{\prime\prime}
is always satisfied. The proof of the theorem is provided in Appendix
B
.

3.2
Wasserstein Complexity

Quantum Wasserstein complexity of order 1 for quantum channels,
C_{W_{1}}(\cdot)
, was first introduced in
[
39
]
. We show that by choosing our resource set
S
appropriately (e.g., Pauli gates),
C_{S}(\cdot)
is equivalent to
C_{W_{1}}(\cdot)
. We first review the definition of Wasserstein complexity of order 1.

The underlying Hilbert space is
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
, and the Lipschitz norm is given by
[
47
, Eq. (6.46)]
:

\|x\|_{L}=2\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\min_{H^{(i)}}\|x-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{\operatorname{op}},

(3.6)

where the minimum is over Hermitian operators
H^{(i)}
acting on the
n-1
qudits excluding the
i
-th register.

The above Lipschitz norm naturally defines an Earth Mover’s distance on the state space, known as the Wasserstein-1 distance (see explicit expressions in
[
46
,
47
]
):

W_{1}(\rho,\sigma)=\sup\left\{\left|\operatorname{tr}(x(\rho-\sigma))\right|:\|x\|_{L}\leq 1,\ x=x^{\dagger}\right\}.

(3.7)

The Wasserstein complexity of quantum channels, introduced in
[
39
]
, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle C_{W_{1}}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})}W_{1}\left(\Phi(\rho),\rho\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|x\|_{L}\leq 1}\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
S=\{\sigma_{i}^{j}:1\leq j\leq d^{2}-1,\ 1\leq i\leq n\}
is the generalized Pauli gate set, where for each
1\leq j\leq d^{2}-1
,
\sigma^{j}
is a generalized Pauli operator, and for each
1\leq i\leq n
,

\sigma_{i}^{j}:=\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes\sigma^{j}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d}^{\otimes(n-i)}.

(3.8)

Then for any quantum channel
\Phi
,

\frac{1}{2}C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi)\leq C^{cb}_{W_{1}}(\Phi)\leq\left(2-\frac{2}{d^{2}}\right)C^{cb}_{S}(\Phi).

The proof is given in Appendix
C
.

3.3
Geometric Complexity

In this section, we discuss another aspect of the flexibility of our resource-dependent complexity measure. In the definition (
1.1
), we take the maximum over all possible resources, which can be viewed as an
\ell^{\infty}
-type norm. We can also introduce an
\ell^{2}
-type norm. Suppose
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
; we define

|||x|||_{S,2}:=\left\|\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}|[s_{j},x]|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

(3.9)

We can naturally define a complexity measure based on the above Lipschitz semi-norm:

\displaystyle C_{S,2}(\Phi)=\sup_{|||x|||_{S,2}\leq 1}\|\Phi^{*}(x)-x\|_{\operatorname{op}}.

(3.10)

Our complexity measure
C_{S,2}
provides a lower bound for the geometric complexity introduced in
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.

First, we briefly review the idea of defining geometric complexity using a metric on the space of Hamiltonians. For any
U\in SU(2^{n})
(unitary operators with determinant 1), there exists a time-dependent Hamiltonian connecting the identity operator to
U
via the following evolution:

\displaystyle\frac{dU(t)}{dt}=-iH(t)U(t),\quad U(0)=\mathbb{I},\quad U(1)=U.

(3.11)

We denote

\displaystyle U(t)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,ds\right)

(3.12)

as the path-ordered unitary operator, which is the unique solution to (
3.11
). The
geometric complexity
is defined by

C_{\operatorname{geom}}(U)=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\|H(t)\|_{\operatorname{cost}}dt\ :\ U\ \text{satisfies}\ \eqref{eqn:path ordering}\right\}.

(3.13)

The norm
\|H(t)\|_{\operatorname{cost}}
encapsulates the cost metric, which must be specified based on the context or physical considerations. One particular choice given in
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
is as follows. Suppose
\mathfrak{H}=\{\sigma_{\vec{k}}:\vec{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}),\ k_{i}=0,1,2,3\}
is the set of tensor products of Pauli operators,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\vec{k}}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{k_{i}},

where the Pauli operators are given by
\sigma_{0}=\mathbb{I}_{2}
,
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{x}
,
\sigma_{2}=\sigma_{y}
,
\sigma_{3}=\sigma_{z}
. For any Hermitian operator
H(t)
, we have

H(t)=\sum_{\vec{k}}\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)\,\sigma_{\vec{k}}.

(3.14)

We associate to each direction
\vec{k}
a penalty factor
p_{\vec{k}}\in(0,\infty]
; then the cost metric is defined as

\|H(t)\|_{\operatorname{cost}}=\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{k}}p_{\vec{k}}\,|\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)|^{2}}.

(3.15)

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose our resource set
S
is given by

S=\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}}\,\sigma_{\vec{k}}\ :\ \vec{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n}),\ k_{i}=0,1,2,3\right\}.

(3.16)

Then for any
U\in SU(2^{n})
, we have

C_{S,2}(\text{ad}_{U})\leq C_{\operatorname{geom}}(U),

(3.17)

with the cost metric given by (
3.15
).

The proof is given in Appendix
D
. The above lower bound can be viewed as a resource-dependent version of the lower bound on the cost of circuits, which has been studied extensively in
[
12
,
39
]
. By generalizing this framework to arbitrary Lie groups, our approach exhibits greater flexibility in the choice of the cost function and the underlying basis for the Lie algebra. We can even achieve equality via approximation using the components of the left regular representation; see
[
2
]
for full details.

4

In
[
10

11
]

) always holds in the finite-dimensional case. However, our analysis allows for more general mixed-unitary channels. Under the assumption (
4.2
), we can show the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\{U_{l}\}_{l\geq 1}
is a sequence of independent random unitaries with the same distribution
\nu
. Then for any
l\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\right]\geq\left(1-\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}})\cdot(1-\lambda_{\text{spec}})^{l}\right)\kappa(S),

(4.3)

where the index
\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}})
is defined in Definition
A.2
.

The constant
\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix}})
, defined for arbitrary conditional expectations, was first introduced in
[
50
]
. We refer the reader to Appendix
A
for more details. Using the above proposition, we can obtain the following
Brown-Susskind behavior
for the resource-dependent complexity of random circuits (see Table
1
for a summary):

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
\{U_{l}\}_{l\geq 1}
is a sequence of independent random unitaries with the same distribution
\nu
. For the Brown-Susskind threshold

L=\frac{-\log\left(4\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}})\right)}{\log(1 - \lambda_{\text{spec}})},

(4.4)

we have for

\mathbb{P}\left(C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq l\frac{\kappa(S)}{8L}\right)\geq\frac{l}{4(l+L)}.

(4.5)

The proofs of Proposition

and Theorem

Note that our theorem can verify the existence of quantum circuits with high complexity (exponential in the number of qubits) if

4.2
Hamiltonian Simulation

In this subsection, we illustrate our notion of complexity by providing a lower bound on the gate count for Hamiltonian simulation via the
qDRIFT
protocol introduced in
[
14
]
. The starting point is a Hamiltonian given by
H=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}H_{j}
, where
H_{j}
are elementary components such that
\|H\|_{\operatorname{op}}\leq 1
, and
h_{j}
are positive weights. We denote

\lambda:=\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}.

(4.6)

In this setting, we consider the
resource set

S=\{H_{j}\mid 1\leq j\leq m\}.

(4.7)

Our goal is to approximate the unitary
U(t)=\exp(i t H)
using a product of gates from
\{U_{j}(r)=\exp(i r H_j)\mid 1\leq j\leq m,\ |r|\leq\tau\}
up to some desired precision, where
\tau>0
is a predetermined constant. Note that a well-established approach is the Trotter-Suzuki formula; see
[
19
]
for a comprehensive overview of the method. Moreover, it has been shown in
[
14
,
18
]
that by allowing
U_{j}(r)
to be randomly chosen, the number of elementary gates needed to approximate
\exp(i t H)
can be lower than the number given by the Trotter-Suzuki formula, although the approximation is only achieved in an average sense. This motivates us to define the
convexified simulation cost
using the gate set

\mathcal{U}_{\tau}:=\left\{\exp(i r H_j)\mid 1\leq j\leq m,\ |r|\leq\tau\right\}.

(4.8)

Definition 4.3
.

The convexified simulation cost of
\exp(i t H)
using gates from
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
with error
\delta>0
is defined by

\displaystyle\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}\left(\exp(i t H)\right):=\inf\bigg\{l\geq 1\ \bigg|\ \exists\ \mu_{l}\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{l}\right)\ \text{such that}

(4.9)

\displaystyle\left\|\text{ad}_{\exp(i t H)}-\int_{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{l}}\text{ad}_{u_{1}}\cdots\text{ad}_{u_{l}}\ d\mu_{l}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{l})\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\delta\bigg\},

where
\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{l}\right)
is the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}^{l}=\mathcal{U}_{\tau}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
, the Cartesian product of
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
with itself
l
times.

Note that in
[
14
]
, the author chose
\tau_{0}=\frac{t\sum_{j}h_{j}}{N}\leq\tau
, where
N
is the length of the approximating random circuit, and
\nu
is a discrete probability measure supported on
\{\exp(i \tau_0 H_j)\mid 1\leq j\leq m\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
, defined by
\nu\left(\{\exp(i \tau_0 H_j)\}\right)=\frac{h_{j}}{\sum_{k}h_{k}}
. Then one has
[
14
, Eq. (B13)]
:

\left\|\Phi_{\nu}^{N}-\text{ad}_{U(t)}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\frac{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{N}\exp\left(\frac{2\lambda t}{N}\right),

(4.10)

which provides an upper bound for
\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}\left(\exp(i t H)\right)
using the probability measure
\nu_{N}=\nu\times\cdots\times\nu
by setting (
4.10
) less than
\delta
:

\displaystyle\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}\left(\exp(i t H)\right)\leq C\frac{\lambda^{2}t^{2}}{\delta}.

Using our complexity measure, we can provide a lower bound for
\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}\left(\exp(i t H)\right)
:

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose
H\neq 0
. Then for
t\leq\frac{1}{3\|H\|_{\operatorname{op}}}
, we have

\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}\left(\exp(i t H)\right)\geq\frac{\lambda_{H}t-\delta\,\kappa(S)}{\tau},

(4.11)

where
\lambda_{H}>0
is given by

\lambda_{H}:=\sup_{x\notin S^{\prime}}\frac{\|[H,x]\|_{\operatorname{op}}}{2|||x|||_{S}}.

(4.12)

We present the proof in Appendix
F
. As an application, we show an explicit lower bound for the simulation cost of the Ising Hamiltonian. Using the bounds in
[
33
]
, it is possible to generalize the argument to other local Hamiltonians.

Example 4.5
.

Suppose we have an Ising Hamiltonian in the
n
-qubit system given by

H=\sum_{1\leq k\neq l\leq n}\gamma_{kl}\,\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\,\sigma_{z}^{j}+\sum_{s=1}^{n}\beta_{s}\,\sigma_{x}^{s},

(4.13)

where
\gamma_{kl},\alpha_{j},\beta_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\sigma^{j}
denotes the Pauli operator
\sigma
acting on the
j
-th qubit. The resource set is given by

\displaystyle S=\left\{\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l},\ \sigma_{z}^{j},\ \sigma_{x}^{s}\mid\forall\ k,l,j,s\right\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{H}\geq\frac{\left|\sum_{k\neq l}\gamma_{kl}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{s}\beta_{s}\right|}{4}.

In particular, if the coefficients are positive and
\gamma_{kl},\alpha_{j},\beta_{s}=\Theta(1)
, then
\lambda_{H}\geq\Omega(n^{2})
.

Proof.

We use the expression (
4.12
). To obtain a lower bound, we choose a special test operator:

X=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{y}^{j}.

(4.14)

We have

\displaystyle[H,X]

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq k\neq l\leq n}\gamma_{kl}\left([\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l},\sigma_{y}^{k}]+[\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l},\sigma_{y}^{l}]\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}[\sigma_{z}^{j},\sigma_{y}^{j}]+\sum_{s=1}^{n}\beta_{s}[\sigma_{x}^{s},\sigma_{y}^{s}]

\displaystyle=-2i\bigg(\sum_{1\leq k\neq l\leq n}\gamma_{kl}(\sigma_{x}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l}+\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{x}^{l})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\sigma_{x}^{j}-\sum_{s}\beta_{s}\sigma_{z}^{s}\bigg).

Now define
\ket{\phi}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)\ket{0}+\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right)\ket{1}
. We have

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}\sigma_{x}\ket{\phi}=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}\sigma_{z}\ket{\phi}=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|[H,X]\|_{\operatorname{op}}

\displaystyle\geq\left|\bra{\phi}^{\otimes n}[H,X]\ket{\phi}^{\otimes n}\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\sum_{k\neq l}\gamma_{kl}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{s}\beta_{s}\right|.

Moreover, it is routine to check that

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in S}\|[s,X]\|_{\operatorname{op}}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\|[\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l},\sigma_{y}^{k}]+[\sigma_{z}^{k}\sigma_{z}^{l},\sigma_{y}^{l}]\|_{\operatorname{op}}\right\}

\displaystyle=2\left\|\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{z}+\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{x}\right\|_{\operatorname{op}}=4.

Therefore, the conclusion is derived as follows:

\displaystyle\lambda_{H}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\notin S^{\prime}}\frac{\|[H,x]\|_{\operatorname{op}}}{2|||x|||_{S}}\geq\frac{\|[H,X]\|_{\operatorname{op}}}{2|||X|||_{S}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left|\sum_{k\neq l}\gamma_{kl}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{s}\beta_{s}\right|}{4}.

∎

In practice, to simulate
\exp(i t H)
, we first simulate the small-time evolution
\exp(i \Delta t H)
and iterate it
\lceil t/\Delta t\rceil
times. Our accessible gate set is defined up to a small-time threshold
\tau=\Delta t
. Our theorem demonstrates that for the Ising Hamiltonian defined in (
4.13
), where all coefficients are equal to the same constant, each small-time simulation requires at least
\Omega(n^{2})
gates, even when allowing random gates and approximation in the average sense.

4.3
Open Systems

Our notion can be naturally generalized to open systems since the complexity measures are defined for quantum channels. Suppose we have a purely dissipative quantum Markov semigroup
T_{t}=\exp(tL)
, where

Lx=\sum_{j=1}^{m}V_{j}xV_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left(V_{j}^{\dagger}V_{j}x+xV_{j}^{\dagger}V_{j}\right)

is a Lindbladian generator (see
[
40
]
). We now use the
jump operators
as the
resource set

S=\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}\{V_{j},V_{j}^{\dagger}\}
and we assume
T_{t}^{*}\circ E_{\text{fix}}=E_{\text{fix}}
. Using elementary properties of Lipschitz complexity, an immediate consequence is

C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(T_{t})\leq\kappa(S)\|L\|_{\diamond}t.

To obtain a lower bound, we use a
mixing time
argument. Recall that the mixing time of the semigroup
T_{t}
is defined by

t_{\operatorname{mix}}(\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{t>0:\|T_{t}-E_{\text{fix}}^{*}\|_{\diamond}\leq\varepsilon\right\}.

(4.15)

If the Lindbladian generator is GNS symmetric (or satisfies the
\sigma
-detailed balance condition), the mixing time is finite. Using a similar argument as in Proposition
4.1
, we can show the following:

Theorem 4.6
.

The lower bound estimate of
C_{S}^{\operatorname{cb}}(T_{t})
grows linearly before
t_{\operatorname{mix}}(\varepsilon)
for any
\varepsilon>0
and is comparable to
\kappa(S)
after
t_{\operatorname{mix}}(\varepsilon)
.

Using standard tools in optimal transport theory, we can provide generic upper estimates for
\kappa(S)
when
\{V_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
form the jump operators of a Lindbladian generator with
\sigma
-detailed balance. In particular, using Corollary 6.8 in
[
25
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa(S)\leq c_{0}\,\operatorname{CLSI}(L)^{-1/2}\sqrt{m}\,\sqrt{\log\mathcal{I}^{\operatorname{cb}}(E_{\text{fix}})},

(4.16)

where
\operatorname{CLSI}(L)
denotes the constant in the Logarithmic Sobolev Inequality for the Lindbladian
L
and the number
m
of jump operators appears when we compare
|||\cdot|||_{S}
and
|||\cdot|||_{S,2}
introduced in (
3.9
).

By using Pauli gates as the jump operators of the Lindbladian generator (see Section
2.2
), we recover the upper and lower qubit bounds obtained in
[
39
]
. The above technique can be generalized to provide a lower-bound framework for the simulation cost of open quantum systems in general cases; see
[
22
]
for more details.

5
Conclusion

Our flexible notion of complexity for quantum channels falls into the axiomatic framework from
[
39
]
. By suitably choosing the resource set
S
, we can establish a lower bound for the complexity of random circuits and continuous-time evolutions which is only known in restricted settings before. Previous lower bound trick is to reduce the problem to computing parity function
[
8
,
61
]
, and our lower bound is based on quantum optimal transport theory. The flexibility in the choice of
S
allows us to explore different regimes of complexity, which can be constant, polynomial in the number of qubits, or exponential in the number of qubits. We leave the discussion about the relation to non-stabilizerness for future work, as discussed in
[
12
]
.

Finally, the linear aspect of the Brown-Susskind conjecture
[
10
,
11
]
is confirmed in our context, up to a threshold given by the mixing time, which is determined by the geometric properties of the resource set.

Appendix A
Non-commutative conditional expectations and their index

In this paper, we assume that
S^{\prime}
is a
*
-algebra. This means that for any operators
X,Y\in S^{\prime}
, we have
XY\in S^{\prime}
and for any
X\in S^{\prime}
, we have
X^{\dagger}\in S^{\prime}
. In the finite-dimensional setting,
\|X^{\dagger}X\|_{op}=\|X\|_{op}^{2}
, so
S^{\prime}
is automatically a
C^{*}
-algebra.

In the finite-dimensional case, let
\mathcal{N}_{fix}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a
C^{*}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. A conditional expectation onto
\mathcal{N}_{fix}
is defined as follows:

Definition A.1
.

A unital, completely positive map
E_{\text{fix}}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{N}_{fix}
is called a
conditional expectation
if:

•

for all
a\in\mathcal{N}_{fix}
,
E_{\text{fix}}(a)=a
.

•

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{N}_{fix}
and
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
E_{\text{fix}}(aXb)=aE_{\text{fix}}(X)b
.

Note that the dual map
E_{\text{fix}}^{*}
is trace-preserving, making it a quantum channel. Recall that a finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebra is unitarily equivalent to

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\otimes\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\quad\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
\mathcal{N}_{fix}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\otimes\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}
. Denote
P_{i}
as the projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto
\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}
. Then, there exists a family of density operators
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{K}_{i})
such that

\displaystyle E_{\text{fix}}(X)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\big(P_{i}XP_{i}(\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}\otimes\tau_{i})\big)\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},

(A.1)

\displaystyle E_{\text{fix}}^{*}(\rho)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\big(P_{i}\rho P_{i}\big)\otimes\tau_{i},

(A.2)

where
\tr_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}
denotes the partial trace with respect to
\mathcal{K}_{i}
. It is easy to see that a state
\sigma
is invariant under
E_{\text{fix}}
, i.e.,
E_{\text{fix}}^{*}(\sigma)=\sigma
, if and only if

\sigma=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\tau_{i},

for some density operators
\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
and a probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. The conditional expectation is completely determined if an invariant state is specified, and we denote it as
E_{\text{fix},\sigma}
. We drop the index
\sigma
when it is clear from the context. Note that the invariant state is not unique, and the flexibility comes from different choices of
\sigma_{i}
and the probability distribution
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

Now, let us introduce the index, which quantifies the "size" of a
C^{*}
-algebra.

Definition A.2
.

For a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{N}_{fix}
and a conditional expectation
E_{\text{fix}}
onto
\mathcal{N}_{fix}
, the (completely bounded) index
\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix}})
(respectively
\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})
) is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix}}):=\inf\{c>0\mid\rho\leq cE_{\text{fix}}^{*}(\rho)\text{ for all states }\rho\},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}}):=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix}}\otimes\text{id}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}).

(A.4)

We illustrate the calculation by choosing the trace-preserving conditional expectation
E_{\text{fix},\tr}
, i.e., we take
\tau_{i}=\frac{1}{d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}}\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}
in (
A.1
). The following properties of the index are useful (see
[
26
]
for proofs):

Proposition A.3
.

Assume
\mathcal{N}_{fix}
has the form

\mathcal{N}_{fix}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{i})\otimes\mathbb{C}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},\quad\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}\otimes\mathcal{K}_{i}.

Then, the index for the trace-preserving conditional expectation is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix},\tr})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min\{d_{\mathcal{H}_{i}},d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}\}d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}},

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix},\tr})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\mathcal{K}_{i}}^{2}.

(A.6)

\mathcal{H}_{d}=\text{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\cdots,\ket{d-1}\}

[

\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}(\sigma^{j})^{\dagger}x\sigma^{j}=\tr(x)\frac{\mathbb{I}_{d}}{d},\quad x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d}).

Applying this operation locally, for any
1\leq i\leq n

\mathcal{E}_{i}(X):=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}(\sigma^{j}_{i})^{\dagger}X\sigma^{j}_{i}=\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes\frac{1}{d}\tr_{i}(X),\quad X\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes n}),

(C.1)

where
\mathbb{I}_{i}
denotes the identity operator on
i
-th system.

\min_{H^{(i)}}\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}
is almost achieved at

, which implies that our Lipschitz norm is comparable to the Lipschitz constant of the Wasserstein-1 distance when we choose our resource set as the generalized Pauli gate set.

Lemma C.1
.

Suppose
S=\{\sigma_{i}^{j}:1\leq j\leq d^{2},1\leq i\leq n\}
is the generalized Pauli gate set. Then for any

\frac{1}{2}|||X|||_{S}\leq\|X\|_{L}\leq\left(2-\frac{2}{d^{2}}\right)|||X|||_{S}.

Proof.

. Maximizing over
i
will then complete the proof.

Step I:

\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}\sim\min_{H^{(i)}}\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}
.

It is clear that
\min_{H^{(i)}}\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}\leq\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}
by choosing
H^{(i)}=\frac{1}{d}\tr_{i}(X)
. For the reverse inequality, for any
H^{(i)}\in\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes(n-1)})
that avoids the
i
-th register, we have

\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}=\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)+\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}.

This expands as

\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}=\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}-\mathcal{E}_{i}\left(X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\right)\|_{op}\leq 2\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op},

where we use the contraction property of the quantum channel
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, i.e.,
\|\mathcal{E}_{i}\|_{op}\leq 1
. In summary, we have shown

\frac{1}{2}\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}\leq\min_{H^{(i)}}\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}\leq\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}.

(C.2)

Step II:

\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}\sim\sup_{1\leq j\leq d^{2}}\|[\sigma_{i}^{j},X]\|_{op}
.

Using (
C.1
), we have

\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}=\left\|\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d^{2}}\left(X-(\sigma^{j}_{i})^{\dagger}X\sigma^{j}_{i}\right)\right\|_{op}\leq\frac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}}\sup_{1\leq j\leq d^{2}}\|[\sigma_{i}^{j},X]\|_{op},

where we use the fact that one of the
\sigma_{i}^{j}
is the identity operator. For the reverse inequality, for any
1\leq j\leq d^{2}
, we have

\|X-(\sigma^{j}_{i})^{\dagger}X\sigma^{j}_{i}\|_{op}=\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)+\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)-(\sigma^{j}_{i})^{\dagger}X\sigma^{j}_{i}\|_{op}\leq 2\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}.

In summary, we obtain

\frac{d^{2}}{d^{2}-1}\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}\leq\sup_{j}\|[\sigma_{i}^{j},X]\|_{op}\leq 2\|X-\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)\|_{op}.

Combining the inequality (
C.2
), we conclude

\min_{H^{(i)}}\|X-\mathbb{I}_{i}\otimes H^{(i)}\|_{op}\in\left[\frac{1}{4},\frac{d^{2}-1}{d^{2}}\right]\cdot\sup_{j}\|[\sigma_{i}^{j},X]\|_{op}.

Maximizing over
i
and using the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{L}
, we finally have

\frac{1}{2}|||X|||_{S}\leq\|X\|_{L}\leq\left(2-\frac{2}{d^{2}}\right)|||X|||_{S}.

∎

Using the definition of the two complexity measures, the comparison is a direct corollary of the above lemma. Note that the above argument works exactly the same if we replace the elements in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
by the elements in
\mathbb{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{d})
, so the
cb
version also holds.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof.

Suppose the time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
achieves the infimum of
C_{geom}(U)
, and let
U(t)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}H(s)\,ds\right)
with
U(1)=U
. For any observable
X
, we aim to bound
\|U(1)^{\dagger}XU(1)-X\|_{op}
. The derivative at
t\in[0,1]
is given by

\frac{d}{dt}U(t)^{\dagger}XU(t)=iU(t)^{\dagger}H(t)XU(t)-iU(t)^{\dagger}XH(t)U(t)=iU(t)^{\dagger}[H(t),X]U(t).

Therefore, we have

\|U(1)^{\dagger}XU(1)-X\|_{op}=\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}U(t)^{\dagger}XU(t)\,dt\right\|_{op}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|[H(t),X]\|_{op}\,dt.

Note that

H(t)=\sum_{\vec{k}}\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)\sigma_{\vec{k}}=\sum_{\vec{k}}\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)\frac{\sigma_{\vec{k}}}{\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}},

plugging it in, we get

\|U^{\dagger}XU-X\|_{op}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|[H(t),X]\|_{op}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{\vec{k}}\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)\left[\frac{\sigma_{\vec{k}}}{\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}},X\right]\right\|_{op}\,dt.

Using the triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz, we further obtain:

\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\sum_{\vec{k}}\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)\left[\frac{\sigma_{\vec{k}}}{\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}},X\right]\right\|_{op}\,dt\leq\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{k}}p_{\vec{k}}|\alpha_{\vec{k}}(t)|^{2}}\,dt\cdot\left\|\left(\sum_{\vec{k}}\left|\left[\frac{\sigma_{\vec{k}}}{\sqrt{p_{\vec{k}}}},X\right]\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{op}.

Using the definition of the semi-norm
|||X|||_{S,2}
, we have:

\|U^{\dagger}XU-X\|_{op}\leq|||X|||_{S,2}\int_{0}^{1}\|H(t)\|_{cost}\,dt.

By the definition of
C_{geom}(U)
in (
3.10
), we conclude the proof:

C_{S,2}(\text{ad}_{U})\leq C_{geom}(U)=\int_{0}^{1}\|H(t)\|_{cost}\,dt.

∎

Appendix E
Linear growth for random circuits

Conditional norm trick.
—To prove Proposition
4.1
, we exploit the fact that for sufficiently large
l
,
\Phi_{\nu}^{l}
approximates
E_{\text{fix}}
closely in the presence of a spectral gap. To establish a lower bound, we employ the concept of
return time
, systematically studied in
[
27
]
. We begin by reviewing the
conditional norm trick
, which links the spectral gap
\|\Phi^{l}_{\nu}-E_{\text{fix}}\|_{2}
to the completely positive (cp) order:

(1-\varepsilon)E_{\text{fix}}\leq_{cp}\Phi^{l}_{\nu}\leq_{cp}(1+\varepsilon)E_{\text{fix}}.

The cp order between two super-operators
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
is defined as
T_{1}\leq_{cp}T_{2}
if
T_{2}-T_{1}
is completely positive.

To establish this connection, we introduce the non-commutative conditional
L_{p}^{q}
norm. For a detailed treatment, refer to
[
36
]
. Here, we focus on a specific case. For a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the
L_{\infty}^{1}
norm is defined as:

\|X\|_{L_{\infty}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}})}=\sup_{a,b\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}},\|a\|_{2},\|b\|_{2}\leq 1}\|aXb\|_{1}.

The induced norm for super-operators is given by:

\|T\|_{1\to\infty}:=\sup_{\|X\|_{L_{\infty}^{1}(\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}})}\leq 1}\|T(X)\|_{op}.

The completely bounded (cb) version is defined as:

\|T\|_{1\to\infty,cb}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{\|X_{n}\|_{L_{\infty}^{1}(\mathbb{M}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}})}\leq 1}\|(\text{id}_{\mathbb{M}_{n}}\otimes T)(X_{n})\|_{op}.

One application of the
L_{\infty}^{1}
norm is its ability to recover the index of trace-preserving conditional expectations as defined in Definition
A.2
(see
[
26
, Theorem 3.9]
for details):

\mathcal{I}(E_{\text{fix}})=\|id_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}\|_{1\to\infty},\quad\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})=\|id_{\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})}\|_{1\to\infty,cb}.

Another significant property is the connection between the
L_{\infty}^{1}
norm, the spectral gap, and the cp order. Recall that a map
T:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is called an
\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}}
-bimodule map if:

T(axb)=aT(x)b,\quad\forall a,b\in\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}},x\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

The following properties, derived in
[
25
, Lemma 3.14, 3.15]
, establish the link between the spectral gap and the cp order:

Proposition E.1
.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an
\mathcal{N}_{\text{fix}}
-bimodule map. Then:

\|\Phi\|_{1\to\infty,cb}\leq\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})\|\Phi\|_{2}.

Moreover, if
\Phi
is unital and completely positive, then for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
\|\Phi-E_{\text{fix}}\|_{1\to\infty,cb}\leq\varepsilon
if and only if:

(1-\varepsilon)E_{\text{fix}}\leq_{cp}\Phi\leq_{cp}(1+\varepsilon)E_{\text{fix}}.

Now we are ready to prove the main results presented in Section
4.1
:

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Recall that
\|\Phi_{\nu}-E_{\text{fix}}\|_{2}=1-\lambda_{spec}
. For any
l\geq 1
, denote
\Phi^{l}=\Phi\circ\cdots\circ\Phi
as the
l
-composition of
\Phi
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi^{l}_{\nu}-E_{\text{fix}}\|_{2}=\|(\Phi_{\nu}-E_{\text{fix}})^{l}\|_{2}\leq(1-\lambda_{spec})^{l}.

where we use the fact that for trace-preserving conditional expectation
E_{\text{fix}}
, we have
E_{\text{fix}}\circ\Phi_{\nu}=\Phi_{\nu}\circ E_{\text{fix}}=E_{\text{fix}}
. Then use Proposition
E.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{\nu}^{l}-E_{\text{fix}}\|_{1\to\infty,cb}\leq\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})(1-\lambda_{spec})^{l}.

which implies

\displaystyle\Phi_{\nu}^{l}\leq_{cp}(1+\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})(1-\lambda_{spec})^{l})E_{\text{fix}}.

Denote
\varepsilon=\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})(1-\lambda_{spec})^{l}
, and note that
\Phi_{\nu}
and
E_{\text{fix}}
are both unital quantum channels, thus there exists another quantum channel
\mathcal{R}
such that

\displaystyle E_{\text{fix}}=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}\Phi_{\nu}^{l}+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}.

Then using convexity and the upper bound of
C_{S}^{cb}
, see Lemma
2.1
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa(S):=C_{S}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi_{\nu}^{l})+\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}C_{S}^{cb}(\mathcal{R})\leq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi_{\nu}^{l})+\frac{2\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\kappa(S),

which implies
C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi_{\nu}^{l})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\kappa(S)
. Finally, using the fact that
\mathbb{E}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})=\Phi_{\nu}^{l}
and convexity, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}C_{S}^{cb}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})

\displaystyle\geq C_{S}^{cb}(\mathbb{E}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}}))=C_{S}^{cb}(\Phi_{\nu}^{l})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\kappa(S)=\big(1-\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})(1-\lambda_{spec})^{l}\big)\kappa(S).

∎

From Proposition
4.1
, it is easy to see that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, define

L_{0}(\varepsilon):=\frac{\log\varepsilon-\log\mathcal{I}^{cb}(E_{\text{fix}})}{\log(1 - \lambda_{spec})},

(E.1)

for any
l\geq L_{0}(\varepsilon)
,
\mathbb{E}C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\kappa(S)
. Using a standard probabilistic argument, we can prove Theorem
4.2
:

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

First, we claim that for any
l\geq L_{0}(\varepsilon)
and any sufficiently small
\delta>0
, the following holds:

\mathbb{P}(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\delta\kappa(S))\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon-\delta}{2}.

(E.2)

To see this, observe that for any
l\geq L_{0}(\varepsilon)
,

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\kappa(S)\leq\mathbb{E}C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}});C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\delta\kappa(S)\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}});C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\leq\delta\kappa(S)\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa(S)\mathbb{P}(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\delta\kappa(S))+\delta\kappa(S),

where in the last inequality we used the universal upper bound of
C_{S}^{cb}
from Lemma
2.1
(
4
). Rearranging terms yields (
E.2
).

Next, we set
\varepsilon=\delta=\frac{1}{4}
and define
L=L_{0}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)
. For any
l\leq L
, there exists an integer
k
such that
kl\geq L
and
(k-1)l\leq L
. This implies bounds on
\frac{1}{k}
:

\displaystyle\frac{l}{l+L}\leq\frac{1}{k}\leq\frac{l}{L}.

(E.3)

Using these bounds, we can write:

\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big)\leq\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{l\kappa(S)}{4(l+L)}\big).

(E.4)

Using the subadditivity of
C_{S}^{cb}
under concatenation, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\big\{C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{(i-1)l+1}\cdots U_{(i-1)l+l}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{kl}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4}\big)\geq\frac{1}{4}.

(E.5)

On the other hand, by the i.i.d. property of
\{U_{l}\}_{l\geq 1}
, for any
1\leq i\leq k
, we have:

\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{(i-1)l+1}\cdots U_{(i-1)l+l}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big).

(E.6)

Combining these results, we find:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{ineqn: union bound}}{\leq}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\big\{C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{(i-1)l+1}\cdots U_{(i-1)l+l}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big\}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{(i-1)l+1}\cdots U_{(i-1)l+l}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{eqn: i.i.d}}{=}k\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{\kappa(S)}{4k}\big)

\displaystyle\underset{\eqref{ineqn: replace k}}{\leq}k\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{l\kappa(S)}{4(l+L)}\big)\leq k\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{l\kappa(S)}{8L}\big).

Finally, using (
E.3
), we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(C^{cb}_{S}(\text{ad}_{U_{l}\cdots U_{1}})\geq\frac{l\kappa(S)}{8L}\big)\geq\frac{1}{4k}\geq\frac{l}{4(l+L)},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

\displaystyle C_{S}^{cb}(\text{ad}_{\exp(itH)})\geq\lambda_{H}t,\quad t\leq\frac{1}{3\|H\|_{op}}.

It suffices to present the lower bound for

\displaystyle\geq\|[H,x]\|_{op}t-\|\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{(it)^{n}}{n!}\delta_{H}^{n}(x)\|_{op}.

\displaystyle\|\sum_{n\geq 2}\frac{(it)^{n}}{n!}\delta_{H}^{n}(x)\|_{op}

(F.2)

\displaystyle\overline{l}^{\mathcal{U}_{\tau}}_{\delta}(\exp(itH))
