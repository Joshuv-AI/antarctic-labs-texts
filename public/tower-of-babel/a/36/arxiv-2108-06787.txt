Lipschitz regularity of energy-minimal mappings between doubly connected Riemann surfaces
David Kalaj

Abstract
. Let M M and N N be doubly connected Riemann surfaces with 𝒞 1 , α \mathscr{C}^{1,\alpha} boundaries and with nonvanishing conformal metrics σ \sigma and ℘ \wp respectively, and assume that ℘ \wp is a smooth metric with bounded Gauss curvature 𝒦 \mathcal{K} and finite area. Assume that 𝖧 ρ ​ ( M , N ) {\mathsf{H}}_{\rho}(M,N) is the class of all 𝒲 1 , 2 \mathscr{W}^{1,2} bomeomorphisms between M M and N N and assume that ℰ ℘ : 𝖧 ¯ ρ ​ ( M , N ) → 𝐑 \mathcal{E}^{\wp}:\overline{\mathsf{H}}_{\rho}(M,N)\to\mathbf{R} is the Dirichlet-energy functional, where 𝖧 ¯ ρ ​ ( M , N ) \overline{\mathsf{H}}_{\rho}(M,N) is the closure of 𝖧 ρ ​ ( M , N ) {\mathsf{H}}_{\rho}(M,N) in 𝒲 1 , 2 ​ ( M , N ) \mathscr{W}^{1,2}(M,N) . By using a result of Iwaniec, Kovalev and Onninen in [ 8 ] that the minimizer, is locally Lipschitz, we prove that the minimizer, of the energy functional ℰ ℘ \mathcal{E}^{\wp} , which is not a diffeomorphism in general, is a globally Lipschitz mapping of M M onto N N .

1.
Introduction

The primary goal of this paper is to study Lipschitz behaviour of stationary deformations of the Dirichlet energy
of mappings between doubly-connected Riemann surfaces. The existence part has been proved. More precisely, Iwaniec, Koch, Kovalev and Onninen in
[
7
]
proved that there exists so-called deformation
f
that maps a double connected domain
\mathbb{X}
onto a doubly connected domain
\mathbb{Y}
in the complex plane and which minimizes the Diriclet energy integral throughout the class os deformations
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
which contains the class of all Sobolev
\mathscr{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{X})
homeomorphism. The minimizer is a harmonic diffeomorphism, provided that the conformal moduli satisfies the relation
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{X})\leqslant\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{Y})
. Moreover, the Dirichlet energy is invariant under conformal change of the original domain. This is why the original domain can be chosen to be equal to
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{A}(r,R):=\{z:r<|z|<R\}
. The Hopf’s differential defined by
\mathrm{Hopf}(f)=f_{z}\overline{f}_{z}
has very special form for so-called stationary deformations namely

\mathrm{Hopf}(f)(z)=\frac{c}{z^{2}},\ \ z\in\mathbb{A}(r,R).

Later this result has been generalized by the author in
[
13
]
for so-called admissible metrics
\wp
in
\mathbb{Y}
.

In this case Hopf’s differential is defined by

\mathrm{Hopf}(f)=\wp^{2}(f(z))f_{z}\overline{f}_{z}

and has a very special form for a so-called stationary deformation namely

\mathrm{Hopf}(f)(z)=\frac{c}{z^{2}},\ \ z\in\mathbb{A}(r,R).

Further in
[
8
]
, Iwaniec, Kovalev and Onninen proved that every stationary deformation is locally Lipschitz in the domain.

In
[
11
]
the author proved that if
f:\mathbb{X}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Y}
is a
\rho-
harmonic diffeomorphic minimizer, and
\partial\mathbb{X},\partial\mathbb{Y}\in\mathscr{C}^{2}
then
f
is Lipschitz continuous up to the boundary.

In
[
14
]
the author and Lamel proved that a minimizer of the Euclidean Dirichlet energy which is a diffeomophic surjection of
\mathbb{X}
onto
\mathbb{Y}
has smooth extension up to the boundary. More precisely they proved that, they proved that, if
f:\mathbb{X}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Y}
is a Euclidean diffeomorphic minimizer, so that
\partial\mathbb{X},\partial\mathbb{Y}\in\mathscr{C}^{1,\alpha}
, then the
f\in\mathscr{C}^{1,\alpha^{\prime}}(\overline{\mathbb{X}})
, where
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha
, if
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{X})\geqslant\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{Y})
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha/(2+\alpha)
if
\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{X})<\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{Y})
.

In
[
12
]
the author extended the main result in
[
14
]
and proved the following extension of the Kellogg theorem.
Every diffeomorphic minimiser of Dirichlet energy of Sobolev mappings between doubly connected Riemanian surfaces
(\mathbb{X},\sigma)
and
(\mathbb{Y},\rho)
having
\mathscr{C}^{n,\alpha}
boundary,
0<\alpha<1
, is
\mathscr{C}^{n,\alpha}
up to the boundary, provided the metric
\rho
is smooth enough. Here
n
is a positive integer. It is crucial that, every diffeomorphic minimizer of Dirichlet energy is a harmonic mapping with a very special Hopf differential and this fact is used in the proof.

1.1.
Harmonic mappings between Riemann surfaces

Let
M=(\mathbb{X},\sigma)
and
N=(\mathbb{Y},\wp)
be
Riemann surfaces with metrics
\sigma
and
\wp
, respectively. If
a mapping
f:M\to N,
is
C^{2}
, then
f
is said to be harmonic (to
avoid the confusion we will sometimes say
\wp
-harmonic) if

(1.1)

f_{z\overline{z}}+{(\log\wp^{2})}_{w}\circ ff_{z}\,f_{\bar{z}}=0,

where
z
and
w
are the local parameters on
M
and
N

respectively. Also
f
satisfies (
1.1
) if and only if its Hopf
differential

(1.2)

\mathrm{Hopf}(f)=\wp^{2}\circ ff_{z}\overline{f_{\bar{z}}}

is a
holomorphic quadratic differential on
M
. Let

|\partial f|^{2}:=\frac{\wp^{2}(f(z))}{\sigma^{2}(z)}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|^{2}\text{ and }|\bar{\partial}f|^{2}:=\frac{\wp^{2}(f(z))}{\sigma^{2}(z)}\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}}\right|^{2}

where
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}

and
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}}
are standard complex
partial derivatives. The
\wp-
Jacobian is defined by

J(f):=|\partial f|^{2}-|\partial\bar{f}|^{2}.

If
u
is sense preserving,
then the
\wp-
Jacobian is positive. The Hilbert-Schmidt norm of
differential
df
is the square root of the energy
e(f)
and is
defined by

(1.3)

|df|=\sqrt{2|\partial f|^{2}+2|\partial\bar{f}|^{2}}.

For
g:M\mapsto N
the
\wp-

Dirichlet energy
is defined by

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}^{\wp}[g]=\int_{M}|dg|^{2}dV_{\sigma},

where
\partial g
, and
\bar{\partial}g
are the partial
derivatives taken with respect to the metrics
\varrho
and

\sigma
, and
dV_{\sigma}
, is the volume element on
(M,\sigma)
,
which in local coordinates takes the form
\sigma^{2}(w)du\wedge dv,

w=u+iv
. Assume that energy integral of
f
is bounded. Then a
stationary point
f
of the corresponding functional where the
homotopy class of
f
is the range of this functional is a harmonic
mapping. The converse is not true. More precisely there exists a
harmonic mapping which is not stationary. The literature is not
always clear on this point because for many authors, a harmonic
mapping is a stationary point of the energy integral. For the last
definition and some important properties of harmonic maps see

[
9
]
. It follows from the definition that, if
a
is conformal
and
f
is harmonic, then
f\circ a
is harmonic. Moreover if
b
is
conformal,
b\circ f
is also harmonic but with respect to
(possibly) an another metric
\wp_{1}
.

Moreover if
N
and
M
are double connected
Riemann surfaces with non vanishing metrics
\sigma
and
\wp
,
then by
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
, there exist conformal mappings

X\colon\mathbb{X}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}M
and
X^{*}\colon\mathbb{Y}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}N
between
double connected plane domains
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
and Riemann
surfaces
M
and
N
respectively.

Notice that the harmonicity neither Dirichlet energy do not depend
on metric
\sigma
on domain so we will assume from now on

\sigma(z)\equiv 1
. This is why throughout this paper

M=(\mathbb{X},\mathbf{1})
and
N=(\mathbb{Y},\wp)
will be doubly
connected domains in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
(possibly unbounded),
where
\mathbf{1}
is the Euclidean metric. Moreover
\wp
is a
nonvanishing smooth metric defined in
\mathbb{Y}
with bounded Gauss
curvature
\mathcal{K}
where

(1.5)

\mathcal{K}(z)=-\frac{\Delta\log\wp(z)}{\wp^{2}(z)},

(we
put
\kappa:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{Y}}|\mathcal{K}(z)|<\infty
) and with
finite area defined by

\mathcal{A}(\wp)=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}\wp^{2}(w)dudv,\ \ w=u+iv.

We call a metric
\rho
an admissible one if there is a constant
C_{\wp}>0
so that

(1.6)

{|\nabla\wp(w)|}\leqslant C_{\wp}{\wp(w)},\ \ \ w\in\mathbb{Y}\ \ i.e.\ \ \nabla\log\wp\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{Y})

which means that
\wp
is so-called approximately analytic function (c.f.
[
5
]
).

Assume that the domain of
\wp
is the unit disk
\mathbf{D}:=\{z:|z|<1\}\subset\mathbf{C}
.
From (
1.6
) and boundedness of
\wp
, it follows that it is Lipschitz, and so it is continuous up to the boundary. Again by using (
1.6
), the function
\psi(t)=\wp(te^{i\alpha})
,
0<t<1
,
\alpha\in[0,2\pi]
satisfies the differential inequalities
-C_{\wp}\leqslant\partial_{t}\log\psi(t)\leqslant C_{\wp}
, which by integrating in
[0,t]
imply that
\psi(0)e^{-C_{\wp}t}\leqslant\psi(t)\leqslant\psi(0)e^{C_{\wp}t}
. Therefore under the above
conditions there holds the double inequality

(1.7)

0<\wp(0)e^{-C_{\wp}}\leqslant{\wp(w)}\leqslant\wp(0)e^{C_{\wp}}<\infty,\ \ w\in\mathbf{D}.

A similar inequality to (
1.7
) can be proved for
\mathbb{Y}
instead of
\mathbf{D}
.
The Euclidean metric (
\wp\equiv 1
) is an admissible metric. The Riemannian metric defined by
\wp(w)={1}/{(1+|w|^{2})^{2}}
is admissible as well.
The Hyperbolic metric
h(w)={1}/{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}}
is not an admissible metric on the unit disk neither on the annuli
\mathbb{A}(r,1):=\{z:r<|z|<1\}
,
but it is admissible in
\mathbb{A}(r,R):=\{z:r<|z|<R\}
, where
0<r<R<1
.
We call such a metric

allowable
one (cf.
[
1
, P. 11]
). If
\wp
is a given
metric in
\mathbb{Y}
, we conventionally extend it to be equal to

0
in
\partial\mathbb{Y}
.
As we already pointed out, we will study the minimum of Dirichlet
integral of mappings between certain sets. We refer to introduction
of
[
7
]
and references therein for good setting of this
problem and some connection with the theory of nonlinear elasticity.
Notice first that a change of variables
w=f(z)
in (
1.4
)
yields

(1.8)

{\mathcal{E}^{\wp}}[f]=2\int_{\mathbb{X}}\wp^{2}(f(z))J_{f}(z)\,dz+4\int_{\mathbb{X}}\wp^{2}(f(z))\lvert f_{\bar{z}}\rvert^{2}dz\geqslant 2\mathcal{A}(\wp)

where
J_{f}
is the Jacobian determinant and
\mathcal{A}(\wp)
is
the area of
\mathbb{Y}
and
dz:=dx\wedge dy
is the area element
w.r. to Lebesgue measure on the complex plane. A conformal mapping
of
f:\mathbb{X}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Y}
; that is, a homeomorphic solution of the
Cauchy-Riemann system
f_{\bar{z}}=0
, would be an obvious choice
for the minimizer of (
1.8
). For arbitrary multiply connected
domains there is no such mapping.

Any energy-minimal diffeomorphism satisfies Euler-Laplace’s
equation, since one can perform first variations while preserving
the diffeomorphism property. However, in the case of Euclidean
metric
\wp\equiv 1
, the existence of a harmonic diffeomorphism
between certain sets does not imply the existence of an
energy-minimal one, see
[
7
, Example 9.1]
. Example 9.1 in

[
7
]
has been constructed with help of affine self-mappings
of the complex plane. For a general metric
\wp
, affine
transformations are not harmonic, thus we cannot produce a similar
example.

2.
Background and preliminaries

A homeomorphism of a planar domain is either sense-preserving or
sense-reversing. For homeomorphisms of the Sobolev class

\mathscr{W}^{1,1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
this implies that the
Jacobian determinant does not change sign: it is either nonnegative
or nonpositive at almost every point
[
3
, Theorem 3.3.4]
. The homeomorphisms considered in this paper are
sense-preserving.

Let
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
be domains in
\mathbb{C}
. Let
a\in\mathbb{Y}

and
b\in\partial\mathbb{Y}
. We define

\dist_{\wp}(a,b):=\inf_{\gamma}\int_{\gamma}\wp(w)|dw|,

where
\gamma
ranges over all
rectifiable Jordan arcs connecting
a
and
b
within
\mathbb{Y}
if
the set of such Jordan arcs is not empty (otherwise we
conventionally put
\dist_{\wp}(a,b)=\infty
). To every mapping
f\colon\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{Y}}
we associate a boundary
distance function

\delta^{\wp}_{f}(z)=\dist_{\wp}(f(z),\partial\mathbb{Y})=\inf_{b\in\partial\mathbb{Y}}\dist_{\wp}(f(z),b)

which
is set to
0
on the boundary of
\mathbb{X}
.

We now adapt for our purpose the concepts of
c\delta-
uniform
convergence and of deformation defined for Euclidean metric and
bounded domains in
[
7
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A sequence of continuous mappings
h_{j}\colon\mathbb{X}\to{\mathbb{Y}}

is said to converge
c\delta
-uniformly
to
h\colon\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{Y}}
if

(1)

h_{j}\to h
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{X}
and

(2)

\delta^{\wp}_{h_{j}}\to\delta^{\wp}_{h}
uniformly on
\overline{\mathbb{X}}
.

We designate it as
h_{j}\xrightarrow{{}_{c\delta}}h
. Concerning the item (1) we need to
notice that the Euclidean metric and the nonvanishing smooth metric

\wp
are equivalent on compacts of
\mathbb{Y}
.

Definition 2.2
.

A mapping
h\colon\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{Y}}

is called a
\wp
deformation
if

(1)

h\in W_{loc}^{1,2}
and
|dh|\in L^{2}
(which we write shortly
h\in W_{l}^{1,2}
);

(2)

The Jacobian
J_{h}:=\det Dh
is nonnegative a.e. in
\mathbb{X}
;

(3)

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\wp^{2}(h(z))J_{h}\leqslant\mathcal{A}(\wp)
;

(4)

there exist sense-preserving diffeomorphisms
h_{j}\colon\mathbb{X}\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Y}
, called an
approximating sequence
, such that
h_{j}\xrightarrow{{}_{c\delta}}h
on
\mathbb{X}
.

The set of
\wp
deformations

h\colon\mathbb{X}\to\overline{\mathbb{Y}}
is denoted by

\mathfrak{D}^{\wp}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
.

Notice first that the condition (1) of the previous definition in
the case of bounded domains
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
can be replaced
by
h\in W^{1,2}
see
[
13
, Lemma 2.3.]

.

Let
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathbb{Y}
be bounded doubly connected planar
domains. Assume that the boundary components of
\mathbb{X}
do not
degenerate into points. If a sequence

\mathfrak{D}^{\wp}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})

3.
Proof of the main result

We need the following important result concerning the local Lipschitz character of certain mappings proved by Iwaniec, Kovalev and Onninen in
[
8
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
h\in\mathscr{W}^{1,2}(\mathbb{X})
be a mapping with nonnegative Jacobian.
Suppose that the Hopf product
h_{z}\,\overline{h_{\bar{z}}}
is bounded and Hölder continuous.
Then
h
is locally Lipschitz but not necessarily
\mathscr{C}^{1}
-smooth.

We need the also the following three results.

Lemma 3.2
.

Every sense-preserving solution of Hopf equation so that
\mathrm{Hopf}(f)
is bounded that maps
\mathbb{A}(1,R)
into
\mathbb{Y}
, mapping the inner/outer boundary to inner/outer boundary is
(K,K^{\prime})
quasiconformal, where

K=1\ \ \text{and}\ \ K^{\prime}=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{A}(1,R)}\frac{|c|}{|z|^{2}\wp^{2}(f)}.

Proof.

We have

\begin{split}|Df(z)|^{2}&=|f_{z}|^{2}+|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2}\\
&\leqslant(|f_{z}|^{2}-|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2})+2|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2}\\
&\leqslant(|f_{z}|^{2}-|f_{\bar{z}}|^{2})+2|f_{\bar{z}}f_{z}|.\end{split}

Since

|f_{\bar{z}}f_{z}|=\frac{\mathrm{Hopf}(f)}{|\wp(f(z))|^{2}}=\frac{|c|}{|z|^{2}\wp^{2}(f)}\leqslant K^{\prime}.

This implies the claim.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

[
4
, Theorem 12.3]

Let
K>1,K^{\prime}\geqslant 0
and assume that
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbf{C}
is a
(K,K^{\prime})-
quasiconformal mapping so that
|f(z)|\leqslant M
,

and assume that
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\Subset\mathbb{X}
and let

. Then there is a constant

so that

where

\beta=K-\sqrt{K^{2}-1}.

If
K=1
, then the above theorem can be formulated for
K_{1}
instead of
K
, where

is an arbitrary constant. For example for

, for which we get

We also need the following lemma

Lemma 3.4
.

[

Assume that
\mathbb{X}

and
b\colon\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)\overset{{}_{\textnormal{\tiny{onto}}}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Y}
are univalent conformal mappings and
define
\wp_{1}(w)={\wp(b(w))}{|b^{\prime}(w)|}
,
w\in\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)
. Then

(a)

\mathcal{E}^{\wp}[b\circ f\circ a]=\mathcal{E}^{\wp_{1}}[f]
provided that one of the two sides exist.

(b)

b\circ f\circ a\in D^{\wp}(\mathbb{X},\mathbb{Y})
if and only if

f\in D^{\wp_{1}}(\mathbb{A}(1,R),\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)))
.

(c)

For Gauss curvature we have
\mathcal{K}_{\wp}(b(w))=\mathcal{K}_{\wp_{1}}(w)
.

(d)

\wp
is an allowable metric if and only if

\wp_{1}
is an allowable metric.

(e)

W_{l}^{1,2}(\mathbb{A}(1,R),\mathbb{A}(1,\rho))=W^{1,2}(\mathbb{A}(1,R),\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)).

(f)

b\circ f\circ a
is
\wp
-harmonic if and only if
f
is

\wp_{1}-
harmonic.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The existence part has been discussed in Proposition
2.5
. We will apply a self-improving argument.
In view of Lemma
3.4
, we can assume that
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{A}(1,R)
for a constant
R>1
.
Assume that
f:\mathbb{A}(1,R)\to\mathbb{Y},
so that

\wp^{2}(f(z))f_{z}\bar{f}_{z}=\frac{c}{z^{2}}.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)
be a conformal diffeomorphism and let
\Phi=\Psi^{-1}
.

Then
F(z)=\Psi\circ f:\mathbb{A}(1,R)\to\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)
is a minimizer of
\wp_{1}-
energy, between
\mathbb{A}(1,R)
and
\mathbb{A}(1,\rho)
, where
\wp_{1}(\zeta)={\wp(\Phi(\zeta))}{|\Phi^{\prime}(\zeta)|}

and

(3.1)

\mathrm{Hopf}(F)(z)=\wp_{1}^{2}(F(z))F_{z}\bar{F}_{z}=\frac{c}{z^{2}}.

Further for

\tilde{F}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F(z),&\hbox{$1<|z|\leqslant R$;}\\
\rho^{2}/\overline{F(R^{2}/\bar{z})},&\hbox{$R\leqslant|z|<R^{2}$}\\
1/\overline{F(1/\bar{z})},&\hbox{$1/R\leqslant|z|\leqslant 1$},\end{array}\right.

let

\tilde{\wp}(w)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\wp_{1}(w),&\hbox{$1\leqslant|w|\leqslant\rho$;}\\
\wp_{1}(\rho^{2}/\bar{w}),&\hbox{$\rho<|w|<\rho^{2}$,}\\
\wp_{1}(1/\bar{w}),&\hbox{$1/\rho<|w|\leqslant 1$.}\par\end{array}\right.

Observe that
\tilde{F}
is continuous. Namely, if

F(Re^{it})=\rho e^{is},
then

\rho^{2}/\overline{F(R^{2}/(Re^{-it}))}=\rho e^{is}.

Thus
\tilde{F}:\mathbb{A}(1/R,R^{2})\to\mathbb{A}(1/\rho,\rho^{2})
is continuous and belongs to the same class as
F
, which mean it is in
\mathscr{W}^{1,2}
.
Then by direct calculation we get

\mathrm{Hopf}(\tilde{F})=\tilde{\wp}^{2}(\tilde{F}(z))\tilde{F}_{z}\overline{\tilde{F}}_{z}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{c}{z^{2}},&\hbox{$1<|z|\leqslant R$;}\\
\frac{\rho^{4}}{|F(R^{2}/\bar{z})|^{4}}\frac{c}{z^{2}},&\hbox{$R\leqslant|z|<R^{2}$.}\\
\frac{1}{|F(1/\bar{z})|^{4}}\frac{c}{z^{2}},&\hbox{$1/R\leqslant|z|<1$.}\end{array}\right.

Let us demonstrate for example

\mathrm{Hopf}(\tilde{F})=\frac{1}{|F(1/\bar{z})|^{4}}\frac{c}{z^{2}}

for
1/R\leqslant|z|<1
.

We have

\tilde{F}_{z}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{\overline{F}(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})}\right)=\frac{1}{\overline{F^{2}}(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})}\overline{F_{z}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{z}}\right)}\frac{1}{z^{2}}

and

\overline{\tilde{F}}_{z}(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{1}{{F}(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})}\right)=\frac{1}{{F^{2}}(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})}\overline{\overline{F}_{z}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{z}}\right)}\frac{1}{z^{2}}.

Then from (
3.1
) we get

\tilde{\wp}^{2}(\tilde{F}(z))\tilde{F}_{z}(z)\overline{\tilde{F}}_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{|F(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})|^{4}}\frac{cz^{2}}{z^{4}}=\frac{1}{|F(\frac{1}{\bar{z}})|^{4}}\frac{c}{z^{2}}.

From Kellogg theorem for conformal mappings we know that
\Phi^{\prime}
is
\mathscr{C}^{\alpha}
up to the boundary of
\mathbb{Y}
. This implies in particular that the function

\tilde{F}_{z}\overline{\tilde{F}}_{z}=\frac{\mathrm{Hopf}(\tilde{F})}{\tilde{\wp}^{2}(\tilde{F}(z))}

is bounded in
\mathbb{A}(1/R,R^{2})
. From Lemma
3.2
, in view of Lemma
2.3
, we obtain that
\tilde{F}
is
(1,\tilde{K})
-quasiconformal. Now Proposition
3.3
implies that
F
is
\frac{1}{2}-
Hölder continuous in
\mathbb{A}(1,R).
More precisely

|\tilde{F}(z)-\tilde{F}(z^{\prime})|\leqslant C(1)\left(\rho^{2}+\frac{R^{2}-1}{R^{2}}\sqrt{\tilde{K}}\right)|z-z^{\prime}|^{1/2}.

So

|F(z)-F(z^{\prime})|\leqslant C(1)\left(\rho^{2}+\frac{R^{2}-1}{R^{2}}\sqrt{\tilde{K}}\right)|z-z^{\prime}|^{1/2}.

This implies that
\tilde{F}_{z}\overline{\tilde{F}}_{z}
is
\alpha\cdot 1/2-
Hölder continuous as a composition of two mappings which are
\alpha-
and
1/2-
Hölder continuous respectively in
\mathbb{A}(1/R,R^{2})
. Now Theorem
3.1
implies that
\tilde{F}
is locally Lipschitz. In particular
F
is Lipschitz in
\overline{\mathbb{A}(1,R)}\Subset\mathbb{A}(1/R,R^{2})
. Therefore
f:\mathbb{A}(1,R)\to\mathbb{Y}
is Lipschitz continuous. Now Lemma
3.4
and Kellogg theorem for conformal mappings imply the claim. This finishes the proof of the main result.
∎
