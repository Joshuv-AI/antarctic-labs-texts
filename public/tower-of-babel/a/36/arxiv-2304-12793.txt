A Semi-Decentralized Tikhonov-based Algorithm for Optimal Generalized Nash Equilibrium Selection
Emilio Benenati, Wicak Ananduta, Sergio Grammatico

Abstract
To optimally select a generalized Nash equilibrium, in this paper, we propose a semi-decentralized algorithm based on a double-layer Tikhonov regularization method. Technically, we extend the Tikhonov method for equilibrium selection in non-generalized games to the generalized case by coupling it with the preconditioned forward-backward splitting, which guarantees linear convergence to the solutions of the inner layer problem and allows for a semi-decentralized implementation. We then establish a conceptual connection and draw a comparison between the proposed algorithm and the hybrid steepest descent method, the other known distributed framework for solving the selection problem.



I
Introduction

Several multi-agent decision processes can be modelled as a game, that is, a set of inter-dependent optimization problems. In particular, if the agents are coupled not only through their respective objective functions, but also through a shared constraint set, then we label the setting as a
generalized
game. Application examples for generalized games include traffic routing
[
1
]
, peer-to-peer energy markets
[
2
,
3
]
and cognitive radio networks
[
4
]
. A typical solution paradigm is the generalized Nash equilibrium (GNE), that is, an optimal situation for each agent given the decisions of the remaining agents, and especially the sub-class of variational GNEs (v-GNES), which has recently received widespread attention due to its stability properties
[
5
]
.

Plenty efficient v-GNE seeking algorithms, e.g.
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
, have been developed for games that satisfy a monotonicity condition. Crucially, monotone games admit in general an infinite number of v-GNEs (unless a much more restrictive
strong
monotonicity condition is imposed). An appealing method to deal with the non-uniqueness of the solution is to select a GNE that optimizes some desirable objective, as proposed in
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
The selection algorithms in
[
12
,
13
]
use Tikhonov’s regularization method and builds on the literature of Variational Inequalities (VIs) to cast the selection problem as a VI-constrained VI, which is solved by finding a sequence of approximate solutions to regularized games. The algorithms in our previous works
[
14
,
15
]
, instead, rely on fixed point selection theory and use the hybrid steepest descent method (HSDM)
[
16
]
, which pairs an appropriate nonexpansive operator with a gradient descent. While the latter is recently proposed as distributed algorithms for GNE selection, the former works for non-generalized games only.

In this paper, we close the gap between the two solution frameworks by devising a Tikhonov-based algorithm for equilibrium selection in generalized, monotone games. Similarly to
[
13
]
, we cast the GNE selection problem as a VI-constrained VI. Then, we design a decentralized algorithm based on the preconditioned Forward Backward (pFB)
[
9
]
to solve each regularized sub-problem with linear convergence rate. The proposed algorithm requires a central coordinator to compute the gradient of the selection function and to check the termination condition for the pFB algorithm. Compared with the Tikhonov-based GNE seeking algorithms in
[
6
,
7
]
that compute the minimum-norm v-GNE, our proposed algorithm works for general convex selection functions.
Interestingly, we also find a theoretical connection between the proposed Tikhonov method and the HSDM. Although neither method generalizes the other, the HSDM can be cast as a forward-backward step towards the solution of the Tikhonov regularized inner layer problem with an appropriate choice of the nonexpansive operator. In Section
V
, we present a numerical comparison between the two methods.

We denote by

(

, with

(
>0

Monotone-plus
: if it is monotone and, for any two pairs
(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\operatorname{gph}(\mathcal{T})
such that

(x,y^{\prime})\in\operatorname{gph}(\mathcal{T})

Lipschitz continuous
: if there exists

, such that, for all

,

\|\mathcal{T}(x)-\mathcal{T}(x^{\prime})\|\leq L\|x-x^{\prime}\|
;

Nonexpansive

Variational inequalities (VIs)

For a closed convex set
K
and
\mathcal{T}:K\to K
, we denote by
\mathrm{VI}(K,\mathcal{T})
the problem

\text{find}~\bm{x}^{*}\in K~\text{s.t.}~\inf_{\bm{x}\in K}\langle\mathcal{T}(\bm{x}^{*}),\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{*}\rangle\geq 0,

and its solution set by

\mathrm{SOL}(K,\mathcal{T}):=\{\bm{x}^{*}\in K|\bm{x}^{*}~\text{solves}~\mathrm{VI}(K,\mathcal{T})\}.

A point
\hat{\bm{x}}
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate solution of
\mathrm{VI}(K,\mathcal{T})
if

\varepsilon\geq\textstyle\inf_{\bm{x}\in\mathrm{SOL}(K,\mathcal{T})}\|\bm{x}-\hat{\bm{x}}\|,

where
\varepsilon\geq 0
denotes the approximation error.

II
Equilibrium selection as a variational inequality problem

We consider the multi-agent decision process in which each of
N
agents aims at solving an optimization problem over the decision variables
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
, where
i\in\{1,...,N\}=:\mathcal{I}
. Let us denote
n:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}n_{i}
and
\mathcal{I}_{-i}:=\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i\}
. Crucially, the decision problem associated with agent
i
is coupled to the decision variables of the remaining agents
\bm{x}_{-i}:=(x_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{I}_{-i}}
both through the objective function
J_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
n=\sum_{{i}\in\mathcal{I}}n_{i}
, and some constraints. We consider the case in which the coupling constraints are defined via
m\in\mathbb{N}
inequalities of the form

\textstyle\sum_{{i}\in\mathcal{I}}A_{i}x_{i}\leq b,

(1)

where
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n_{i}}
, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. This problem is commonly referred to as a
generalized game
and we formalize it as follows:

\displaystyle\underset{x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}{\operatorname{min}}\quad

\displaystyle J_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})

(2a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle A_{i}x_{i}\leq b-\textstyle\sum_{{j}\in\mathcal{I}_{-i}}A_{j}x_{j}.

(2b)

We note that we can reformulate the more general case with separable convex coupling constraint functions into the form of (
2
) by augmenting the decision variables
[
8
, Rem. 2]
. Let us now denote
\bm{\mathcal{X}}:=\Pi_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{X}_{i}
and the collective feasible set of the game in (
2
) as

\Gamma:={\bm{\mathcal{X}}}\cap\{\bm{x}~|~\eqref{eq:coupling_constr}~\text{holds true}\}.

As a solution paradigm for the problem in (
2
), we consider the set of generalized Nash equilibria, that is, a set of points from which no agent has any incentive to unilaterally deviate:

Definition 1
.

A set of decision variables
\bm{x}^{*}\in\bm{\mathcal{X}}
is a GNE for the game in (
2
) if, for each
i\in\mathcal{I}
,

J_{i}(x_{i}^{*},\bm{x}^{*}_{-i})\leq J_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}^{*}_{-i}),

for any
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\cap\{{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}}~|~A_{i}{y}\leq-\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{I}_{-i}}}A_{j}x_{j}^{{*}}\}
.

The following assumptions, which are standard for GNE seeking problems
[
10
,
11
]
, guarantee the existence of a GNE
[
18
, Prop. 12.11]
:

Assumption 1
.

For each
i
,
J_{i}(\cdot,\bm{x}_{-i})
is convex and continuously differentiable for any
\bm{x}_{-i}
.

Assumption 2
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{X}_{i}
is compact and convex;
\Gamma
is non-empty and it satisfies Slater’s constraint qualification.

Assumption 3
.

The pseudogradient mapping of the game in (
2
), defined as

F(\bm{x}):=\operatorname{col}((\nabla_{x_{i}}J_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i}))_{i\in\mathcal{I}}),

is monotone and
L_{F}
-Lipschitz continuous.

For solving the problem in (
2
) via a decentralized algorithm, we assume that the agents can exchange information over an undirected, connected communication network. We denote the set of neighbours of agent
i
in this network by
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. For simplicity, we consider the case where, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
J_{i}
depends on
x_{i}
and the decision variables of (a subset of)
\mathcal{N}_{i}
, so that each agent is able to evaluate its cost function by receiving the decision variables implemented by some neighbouring agents. Furthermore, each agent maintains a local estimate of the dual variable associated with the shared constraints in (
1
), denoted by
\lambda_{i}
, as well as an auxiliary variable
\nu_{i}
, which is communicated to the other agents in order to achieve consensus over the dual variable estimates. We then define the following extended Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) operator, first proposed in
[
9
]
, which includes both the optimality conditions for the coupled optimization problems in (
2
) and the consensus condition:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}}(\bm{\omega})=&\mathcal{A}(\bm{\omega})+\mathcal{B}(\bm{\omega})+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\omega}),\\
\mathcal{A}(\bm{\omega}):=&{\mathrm{N}_{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}(\bm{x})\times\mathrm{N}_{\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}|m}_{\geq 0}}(\bm{\lambda})\times\{\bm{0}_{|\mathcal{I}|m}\}},\\
\mathcal{B}:=&\begin{bmatrix}{F}(\bm{x})\\
-\bar{\mathcal{L}}\bm{\lambda}\\
0\end{bmatrix},\ \mathcal{C}(\bm{\omega}):=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}^{\top}\bm{\lambda}\\
b-\bm{A}\bm{x}-\bar{\mathcal{L}}\bm{\nu}\\
\bar{\mathcal{L}}\bm{\lambda}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(3)

where
\bm{\omega}=\operatorname{col}(\bm{x},\bm{\lambda},\bm{\nu})
and
\bar{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{L}\otimes I_{m}
and
\mathcal{L}
is the Laplacian matrix of the communication graph. It is well known that a subset of the GNEs of (
2
) can be found as the zero set of
\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}}

[
9
, Thm. 2]
. Such GNEs are known as the variational GNEs (v-GNEs) of the game in (
2
)
[
5
, Def. 3.10]
. This class of solutions has been widely studied and multiple efficient algorithms for their computation exist. However, since Assumptions
1
–
3
are not enough to guarantee that
\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})
is a singleton, most of the algorithms in the literature resort to computing an unspecified v-GNE among the possibly infinitely many.

Instead, we develop an algorithm to compute an optimally selected v-GNE according to the selection function
\phi
, in the sense that we aim at solving the selection problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\omega}}\phi(\bm{\omega})\quad\operatorname{s.t.}\ \bm{\omega}\in\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}}).

(4)

Let us postulate the following assumptions on the selection function:

Assumption 4
.

The selection function
\phi
is convex, continuously differentiable, and coercive. Furthermore, its gradient is
L_{\nabla\phi}
-Lipschitz continuous.

By
[
17
, Prop. 23.39]
,
\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})
is a closed convex set under Assumptions
1
and
3
. Therefore, (
4
) is a convex optimization problem. However,
\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})
can seldom be written in a closed form and, thus, (
4
) cannot be solved by standard optimization algorithms. To derive an algorithmic solution, we further massage its formulation by noting that
\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})
is the solution set to a Variational Inequality (VI)
[
19
, Sec. 1.3.1]
. Specifically, by defining
\bm{\Omega}:=\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{Nm}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nm}
and by
[
19
, Eq. (1.1.3)]
,

\bm{\omega}^{*}\in\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})\Leftrightarrow\bm{\omega}^{*}\in\mathrm{SOL}(\bm{\Omega},\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}).

(5)

Following the equivalence between convex optimization problems and VIs
[
19
, Sec. 1.3.1]
, we then recast (
4
) as the following VI-constrained VI:

{\mathrm{VI}(\mathrm{SOL}(\bm{\Omega},\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}),\nabla\phi)}.

(6)

As discussed in
[
13
]
, we can solve (
6
) by finding the
\varepsilon_{k}
-approximate solutions to a sequence of regularized sub-problems, indexed by
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{VI}(\bm{\Omega},\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi+\alpha(\mathrm{Id}-\bm{\omega})),~~\bm{\omega}\in\bm{\Omega},

(7)

where the sequence of regularization weights
\alpha
,
(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and approximation errors
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are chosen according to the following criteria:

Assumption 5
.

The parameter
\alpha
is positive;
(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are positive and non-negative sequences of real numbers, respectively, such that
\textstyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{k}=\infty
and eventually
\varepsilon_{k}=0.

By denoting
{\bar{\gamma}}:=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{k}
, we observe the following properties of the operators that define (
7
):

Lemma 1
.

Let Assumptions
3
–
5
hold true. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha>0
, and
\bm{\omega}\in\bm{\Omega}
:

1.

\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+{\gamma_{k}}\nabla\phi+\alpha(\mathrm{Id}-\bm{\omega})
is
\alpha
-strongly monotone,

2.

\mathcal{B}+{\gamma_{k}}\nabla\phi+\alpha(\mathrm{Id}-\bm{\omega})
is
L_{G}
- Lipschitz continuous, where
L_{G}:=\max(L_{F},2|\mathcal{N}_{i}|_{i\in\mathcal{I}})+{{\bar{\gamma}}}L_{\nabla\phi}+\alpha
.

By applying
[
19
, Cor. 2.2.5]
and
[
19
, Thm. 2.3.3a]
, we conclude that the regularized problem in (
7
) admits one and only one solution. The following result, which follows directly from
[
13
, Thm. 2]
, formalizes a prototypical algorithmic solution to the problem in (
6
) based on a sequential solution of the problem in (
7
).

Proposition 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
5
hold. Let
(\bm{\omega}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\bm{\Omega}
and, for every
k
,
\bm{\omega}^{(k+1)}
be the
\varepsilon_{k}
-approximate solution of the VI in (7) with
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}^{(k)}
. Then, the sequence
(\bm{\omega}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded and each of its limit points is a solution of (
6
).

III
Distributed equilibrium selection

In view of Proposition
1
, next we derive a semi-decentralized algorithm for recursively generating a sequence
(\bm{\omega}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that, for all
k
,
\bm{\omega}^{(k+1)}
is a
\varepsilon_{k}
-approximate solution to (
7
) with
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}^{(k)}
. We note that the exact solution, denoted by
\bm{\omega}^{*}_{k}
, equivalently satisfies the monotone inclusion

\displaystyle\begin{split}&0\in(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi+\alpha(\mathrm{Id}-\bm{\omega}^{(k)}))(\bm{\omega}_{k}^{*}).\end{split}

(8)

We then apply the preconditioned forward-backward (pFB) method on (
8
), proposed in
[
9
]
in the context of decentralized GNE seeking, for computing
\bm{\omega}^{*}_{k}
. To this end, let us define the following matrix, which collects the step sizes of the proposed algorithm:

\Psi=\operatorname{diag}(\bm{\rho}^{-1},\bm{\tau}^{-1},\bm{\sigma}^{-1}),

(9)

where
\bm{\rho}:=\operatorname{diag}(\rho_{i}I_{n_{i}})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
\bm{\tau}:=\operatorname{diag}(\tau_{i}I_{n_{i}})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
\bm{\sigma}:=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{i}I_{n_{i}})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
collect the step sizes associated with the primal, dual and auxiliary variables, respectively. Let us make the following design choice on the matrix
\Psi
:

Assumption 6
.

Let

\displaystyle r^{\mathrm{x}}_{i}:=\textstyle\max_{j=1,\dots,n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|[A_{i}]_{jk}|,

\displaystyle r^{\lambda}_{i}:=\textstyle\max_{j=1,\dots,n_{i}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}|[A_{i}]_{jk}|+2|\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\lambda}|,

\displaystyle r^{\nu}_{i}:=2|\mathcal{N}_{i}^{\lambda}|.

Furthermore, let
r=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(r^{\mathrm{x}}_{i},r_{i}^{\lambda},r_{i}^{\nu})
and
\delta>\max(\frac{L_{G}^{2}}{\alpha},2r)
, with
L_{G}
defined as in Lemma
1
. For all
i\in\mathcal{I}
:

1.

(2\delta-r^{\mathrm{x}}_{i})^{-1}\leq\rho_{i}\leq(\delta+r^{\mathrm{x}}_{i})^{-1}
;

2.

(2\delta-r^{\lambda}_{i})^{-1}\leq\tau_{i}\leq(\delta+r^{\lambda}_{i})^{-1}
;

3.

(2\delta-r^{\nu}_{i})^{-1}\leq\sigma_{i}\leq(\delta+r^{\nu}_{i})^{-1}
.

Let us also define the preconditioning matrix

\Phi=\Psi+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&-\bm{A}^{\top}&\bm{0}\\
-\bm{A}&\bm{0}&-\bar{\mathcal{L}}\\
\bm{0}&-\bar{\mathcal{L}}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(10)

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
6
,
\Phi\succcurlyeq\delta I
and
\frac{\delta}{\|\Phi\|}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

The pFB operator for the inclusion in (
8
) reads, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{T}^{\text{pFB}}_{{k}}=&(\mathrm{Id}+\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{C}))^{-1}\\
&(\mathrm{Id}-\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{B}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi+\alpha(\mathrm{Id}-\bm{\omega}^{(k)})))).\end{split}

(11)

The following result formalizes the convergence of the fixed-point iteration generated by
\mathcal{T}^{\text{pFB}}_{k}
to the solution of (
7
).

Lemma 3
.

Let Assumptions
1
–
6
hold true. Let
\bm{y}^{0}\in\bm{\Omega}
. Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
(\bm{y}^{(t)})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by the fixed-point iteration

\bm{y}^{(t+1)}=\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{pFB}}_{k}(\bm{y}^{(t)}),

(12)

where
\mathcal{T}^{\text{pFB}}_{{k}}
is defined in (
11
), converges linearly in the
\Phi
-induced norm to
\bm{\omega}^{*}_{k}
in (
8
) and

\|\bm{y}^{(t)}-\bm{\omega}^{*}_{k}\|_{\Phi}\leq\|\bm{y}^{(t+1)}-\bm{y}^{(t)}\|_{\Phi}/(1-\beta),

(13)

with
\beta=(1+\tfrac{L_{G}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}-\tfrac{2\alpha}{\|\Phi\|})
.

Remark 1
.

Differently from the pFB operator in
[
9
]
, that in (
11
) has additional regularization terms, involving the gradient of the selection function, and thus achieves the desired linear rate.

IV
Theoretical connection and comparison with the Hybrid Steepest Descent Method

In our previous works
[
14
,
15
]
, we take a different algorithm design path, where we reformulate Problem (
4
) as a fixed point selection problem. This reformulation allows one to use the hybrid steepest descent method (HSDM)
[
16
]
to solve (
4
). Specifically, one has to find a quasi-shrinking mapping
\mathcal{T}

[
15
, Def. 1]
such that
\operatorname{fix}(\mathcal{T})=\operatorname{zer}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{KKT}})
. Then, the limit point of the sequence
(\bm{z}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, defined by

\bm{z}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{T}(\bm{z}^{(k)})-\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi(\mathcal{T}\bm{z}^{(k)}),

(20)

converges to the solution of (
4
) if
(\gamma_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is square-summable but non-summable
[
16
, Thm. 5]
. The vanishing weight on
\nabla\phi
is reminiscent of the Tikhonov regularization introduced in Section
II
. Indeed, the two methods are related, as shown next.

Let us consider the exact solution to the VI in (
7
) with
\bm{\omega}=\bm{\omega}^{(k)}
. Then, from
[
19
, Prop. 12.3.6]
,

\bm{\omega}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{J}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi)}(\bm{\omega}^{(k)})=:\mathcal{T}^{\text{Tik}}_{k}(\bm{\omega}^{(k)}).

(21)

The properties of the HSDM update in (
20
) depend on the choice of
\mathcal{T}
. Let us consider the particular case
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}}
, which is averaged and, thus, can be shown to be quasi-shrinking by
[
15
, Lem. 1]
. We rewrite (
20
) with this particular choice of
\mathcal{T}
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\bm{v}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}}(\bm{z}^{(k)}),\\
&\bm{z}^{(k+1)}=\bm{v}^{(k+1)}-\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi(\bm{v}^{(k+1)}),\end{split}

(22)

where we introduced the auxiliary sequence
(\bm{v}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. By rearranging (
22
), we note that this sequence evolves as

\bm{v}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi)(\bm{v}^{(k)})=:\mathcal{T}^{\text{HSDM}}_{k}(\bm{v}^{(k)}).

(23)

With this particular choice of
\mathcal{T}
, the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\text{HSDM}}_{k}
in (
23
) corresponds to the forward-backward (FB) splitting method applied to the inclusion

0\in\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi.

From
[
17
, Prop. 26.1iv]
and
[
17
, Prop. 23.38]
,

\operatorname{fix}(\mathcal{J}_{\frac{1}{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi)})=\operatorname{fix}(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\gamma_{k}\nabla\phi)),

which implies

\operatorname{fix}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{Tik}}_{k})=\operatorname{fix}(\mathcal{T}^{\text{HSDM}}_{k}).

(24)

Thus, we conclude that both the Tikhonov update in (
21
) and the HSDM step in (
20
) (in terms of the auxiliary sequence
\bm{v}^{(k)}
) apply at each step
k
a single update of a fixed point iteration, and the two operators in (
24
) have the same fixed point set. This analogy is only theoretical, as in practice the operator
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}}
cannot be implemented in a distributed fashion. Nevertheless, it outlines that both the Tikhonov method and the HSDM function by the same underlying principle of tracking the solutions to a sequence of regularized problems. Moreover, we note that (
24
) does not hold for a generic choice of
\mathcal{T}
, thus the HSDM includes algorithms that are not covered by the Tikhonov method. On the other hand, clearly the operator
\mathcal{T}^{\text{Tik}}_{k}
cannot be rewritten in terms of an FB operator. Therefore, we should conclude that neither method is a generalization of the other.

The key differences between the two frameworks are summarized in Table
I
. In addition, we remark that the Tikhonov framework can be paired with any (splitting) methods for strongly monotone games to obtain a decentralized algorithm. Meanwhile, the HSDM requires methods for monotone games that are quasi-shrinking, such as the forward-backward-forward (FBF) splitting
[
17
, Sec. 26.6]
. Thus, Tikhonov-based methods can benefit from a larger pool of available algorithms for future development.

VI
Conclusion

The generalized Nash equilibrium selection problem can be solved with a semi-decentralized algorithm based on the Tikhonov regularization method combined with the preconditioned forward backward algorithm, which achieves linear convergence for the regularized sub-problems. Interestingly, both the Tikhonov regularization method and a particular instance of the hybrid steepest descent method seek at each iteration an approximate solution to the same regularized problem, indicating a conceptual connection. Although theoretically less practical (as shown in Table
I
), the Tikhonov algorithm demonstrates comparable convergence performance to the state-of-the-art in our simulations.
