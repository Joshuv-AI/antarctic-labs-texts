Virasoro constraints and representations for quiver moduli spaces
Woonam Lim, Miguel Moreira

Abstract
. We study the Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of representations of quiver with relations by Joyce’s vertex algebras. Using the framed Virasoro constraints, we construct a representation of half of the Virasoro algebra on the cohomology of moduli stacks of quiver representations under smoothness assumption. By exploiting the non-commutative nature of the Virasoro operators, we apply our theory for quivers to del Pezzo surfaces using exceptional collections. In particular, the Virasoro constraints and representations are proven for moduli of sheaves on ℙ 2 {\mathbb{P}}^{2} , ℙ 1 × ℙ 1 {\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1} and Bl 𝗉𝗍 ​ ( ℙ 2 ) \textup{Bl}_{\mathsf{pt}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2}) . Lastly, we unravel the Virasoro constraints for Grassmannians in terms of symmetric polynomials and Hecke operators.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Virasoro constraints is a ubiquitous phenomenon in enumerative geometry which predicts a rich set of relations between descendent integrals. It is called the Virasoro constraints because these relations are described by a representation of half of the Virasoro algebra
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
. The first instance of the Virasoro constraints was Witten-Kontsevich theorem
[
Wit
,
Kon
]
about
\psi
-integrals over the moduli space of stable curves. This was then extended to Gromov–Witten theory
[
EHX
]
which remains one of the foremost unsolved problems in the field.

Virasoro constraints were introduced in a sheaf theoretic context via the Gromov–Witten/Stable pairs correspondence
[
MOOP
]
, followed by subsequent developments
[
Mor
,
vB
]
. It was then realized in
[
BLM
]
that Virasoro constraints in sheaf theory have their independent origin in terms of Joyce’s vertex algebra
[
Joy1
,
GJT
,
Joy2
]
. This provided a purely representation theoretic characterization of the Virasoro constraints in terms of primary states. More importantly, this suggests that one may formulate the Virasoro constraints in other contexts if the setting of Joyce’s vertex algebra applies.

In this paper, we study the Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of quiver representations, which appear also in
[
Boj
]
. In order to apply the theory of quivers to smooth projective varieties, we show that the Virasoro constraints are preserved under derived equivalences. This reveals the non-commutative nature of the Virasoro constraints and leads to a proof of the Virasoro constraints for certain moduli spaces of Bridgeland semistable objects on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and
\textup{Bl}_{\mathsf{pt}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
; these include, in particular, both torsion-free and 1-dimensional Gieseker semistable sheaves.

We study in this paper the new phenomenon of geometricity of the Virasoro operators. A priori, the formulation of Virasoro constraints uses a representation of
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
on some formal algebra, called the descendent algebra. However, it turns out that this representation descends to the cohomology of moduli stacks of semistable representations. We use the framed Virasoro constraints to construct this natural representation of
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
on cohomology of smooth quiver moduli stacks and of
\textup{Vir}_{\geq 0}
on the cohomology of smooth framed quiver moduli spaces. It is remarkable that such representations exist in this generality, opening many directions of future study; applications of these ideas appear in
[
KLMP
]
. We study this phenomenon in detail for Grassmannians and explain how the Virasoro constraints and representations relate to the theory of symmetric functions and Hecke operators.

1.2.
Main results

Let
(Q,I)
be a finite acyclic quiver with relations. In order to formulate the Virasoro constraints, one has to choose a generating set of relations
I=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
and consider the corresponding quasi-smooth dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Such a choice is required to fix the derived enhancement of the related moduli spaces and also to define the Virasoro operators. The following theorem is one of the main results in
[
Boj
]
. We explain the ingredients of the proof, following
[
BLM
,
Boj
]
, because they appear in this paper for other uses.

Theorem A

(
[
Boj
]
)
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}
be a quasi-smooth dg quiver. If
{M}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}={M}^{\theta-\textup{st}}_{d}
, then we have

\int_{[{M}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}]^{\mathrm{vir}}}\xi\circ{\mathsf{L}}_{\mathrm{wt}_{0}}(D)=0\quad\textup{for all}\quad D\in{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\,.

In the theorem,
{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbb{Q}}
is a formal algebra, called the descendent algebra, that is equipped with a natural
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
representation. The operator
{\mathsf{L}}_{\mathrm{wt}_{0}}
is defined on
{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbb{Q}}
in terms of this Virasoro representation, and
\xi
is the realization morphism, which maps (a subalgebra of)
{\mathbb{D}}^{\mathbb{Q}}
to the cohomology
H^{\ast}({M}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d})
of the moduli space.

It was observed in
[
BLM
]
that Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of sheaves have a counterpart for moduli spaces of pairs, and the two are related in a fundamental way. In the case of quiver representations, framed representations play the role of pairs. By the framed/unframed correspondence, Theorem
A
implies the framed Virasoro constraints. For us, the moduli spaces
{M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d}
of limit
\theta
-stable framed representations are of particular importance due to the role they play in the proof of geometricity.

Theorem B
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}
be a quasi-smooth dg quiver. Then we have

\int_{[{M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d}]^{\mathrm{vir}}}\xi\circ{\mathsf{L}}_{n}^{f\rightarrow d}(D)=0\quad\textup{for all}\quad n\geq 0,\ \ D\in{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\,.

A priori, the Virasoro constraints concerns integral of tautological classes only in degree equal to the virtual dimension. Nevertheless, the Virasoro constraints has strong implications on the structure of tautological relations in middle degrees. In order to state the result, we make Assumption
4.7
which implies smoothness of the moduli stack and framed moduli space below and surjectivity of the realization homomorphisms

{\mathbb{D}}_{d}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow H^{*}({{\mathcal{M}}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}),\quad{\mathbb{D}}_{d}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow H^{*}({M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d})\,.

We say that the Virasoro operators are geometric if their action on the formal algebra
{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}_{d}
descend to the cohomology via realization homomorphism.

Theorem C
.

Under Assumption
4.7
, the Virasoro operators in range
n\geq-1
(resp.
n\geq 0
) are geometric for
{{\mathcal{M}}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}
(resp.
{M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d}
). In particular, there are induced representations

\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}\lefttorightarrow H^{*}({{\mathcal{M}}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}),\quad\textup{Vir}_{\geq 0}\lefttorightarrow H^{*}({M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d})\,.

In other words, this statement means that the Virasoro action preserves the ideals of tautological relations

\ker\big({\mathbb{D}}_{d}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow H^{*}({{\mathcal{M}}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d})\big)\textup{ and }\ker\big({\mathbb{D}}_{d}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}\rightarrow H^{*}({M}^{\theta}_{f\rightarrow d})\big)\,.

Understanding the tautological relations can be thought of as an analogue of the (much harder) study of the tautological ring of moduli spaces of curves, see
[
Pan
]
for a survey. When
{\bf Q}=Q
is a quiver with no relations, the ideal of tautological relations, when there are no strictly semistables, is described in
[
Fra
]
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety admitting a full exceptional collection
{\mathfrak{E}}=(E_{1},\dots,E_{n})
such that its left dual collection is strong. Then there is an exact equivalence of triangulated categories

B:D^{b}(X)\xrightarrow{\sim}D^{b}(Q,I)

for some quiver with relations
(Q,I)
. When
\mathrm{Rep}_{Q,I}
has homological dimension at most
2
, there is a canonical choice of relations generating
I
which in turn define a quasi-smooth dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Since Virasoro constraints for quasi-smooth dg quivers are proven in Theorem
A
, this suggests to study the Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of objects in
D^{b}(X)
using the exact equivalence
B
. In order to achieve this, we prove the following theorem which identifies Joyce’s vertex algebra with (possibly degenerate) lattice vertex algebra and explains the non-commutative nature of the Virasoro operators.

Theorem D
.

Let
{\bf T}
,
{\bf T}_{1}
and
{\bf T}_{2}
be dg categories satisfying Assumption
5.10
.

(i)

We have a natural isomorphism between Joyce’s vertex algebra and lattice vertex algebra

H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}})\xrightarrow{\sim}\textup{VA}(K({\bf T}),\chi^{\text{sym}}_{{\bf T}}).

Via this isomorphism,
H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}})
is endowed with a
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
-representation.

(ii)

A quasi-equivalence
{\bf B}:{\bf T}_{1}\rightarrow{\bf T}_{2}
induces a vertex algebra isomorphism

B_{*}:H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}_{1}})\xrightarrow{\sim}H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}_{2}})

intertwining the
\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}
-representations from (i).

We show that the Assumption
5.10
holds for the dg category of representations of a dg quiver (not necessarily quasi-smooth). Let
{\bf T}_{1}
and
{\bf T}_{2}
be dg enhancements of
D^{b}(X)
and
D^{b}(Q,I)\simeq D^{b}({\bf Q})
, respectively. Applying Theorem
D
to the quasi-equivalence
{\bf B}:{\bf T}_{1}\rightarrow{\bf T}_{2}
lifting the exact equivalence
B
, we can use the Virasoro constraints on
{\mathbb{Q}}
to study the Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of objects in
D^{b}(X)
. To be more precise, since Theorem
A
concerns the moduli spaces of objects in the standard heart
\mathrm{Rep}_{Q,I}\subset D^{b}(Q,I)
, we obtain the corresponding statements for
B^{-1}(\mathrm{Rep}_{Q,I})\subset D^{b}(X)
. Since this is different from the standard heart
\mathrm{Coh}(X)\subset D^{b}(X)
, it is nontrivial to apply this technique to moduli spaces of sheaves on
X
.

This problem naturally leads us to Bridgeland stability conditions. For any smooth projective surface
S
, there are geometric stability conditions
\sigma=\sigma_{E,H}
that depend on
{\mathbb{R}}
-divisors
E
and
H
where
H
is ample. Such stabilities define moduli stacks
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
parametrizing
\sigma
-semistable objects in a tilted heart
\mathrm{Coh}_{E,H}(S)\subset D^{b}(S)
of type
\textsf{v}\in K(S)
. It was conjectured in
[
AM
]
that for any del Pezzo surface
S
the moduli stack
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
can be identified with a quiver moduli stack. For those moduli stacks admitting a quiver description, see Definition
7.9
for a precise meaning, we prove the following.

Theorem E
.

Let
S
be a del Pezzo surface and
\sigma
be a geometric stability condition. Assume that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
admits a quiver description.

(i)

If
M^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}=M^{\sigma-\textup{st}}_{\textsf{v}}
, then
M^{\sigma}_{\textsf{v}}
satisfies the Virasoro constraints with respect to its natural virtual class.

(ii)

If
\textup{Ext}^{2}_{S}(F,F^{\prime})=0
for all
F,\ F^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{v}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}
, then
{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{v}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}
is smooth, tautologically generated, and the operators
\mathsf{R}_{n}
are geometric, so we have an induced representation

\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}\lefttorightarrow H^{*}({\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{v}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}})\,.

For some del Pezzo surfaces, all moduli stacks with respect to geometric stability conditions are known to admit a quiver description, see
[
ABCH
]
for
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and
[
AM
]
for
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and
\textup{Bl}_{\mathsf{pt}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
. Theorem
E
can be applied to those surfaces to obtain the Virasoro constraints and representations. On the other hand, geometric stability conditions at the large volume limit recover Gieseker stability. Combining these results, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem F
.

Let
S
be one of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
or
\textup{Bl}_{\mathsf{pt}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})
with nonzero-dimensional topological type
v
.

(i)

If
M^{H-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}=M^{H-\textup{st}}_{\textsf{v}}
, then
M^{H}_{\textsf{v}}
satisfies the Virasoro constraints with respect to its smooth fundamental class.

(ii)

The moduli stack
{\mathcal{M}}^{H-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
is smooth, tautologically generated, and the operators
\mathsf{R}_{n}
are geometric, so we have a representation

\textup{Vir}_{\geq-1}\lefttorightarrow H^{*}({\mathcal{M}}^{H-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}})\,.

We remark (see also
[
Boj
]
) that, unlike in
[
Mor
,
BLM
]
, the proof is completely independent of the results in
[
MOOP
]
via Gromov–Witten theory and the GW/PT correspondence. Indeed, this makes all the results in
[
Mor
,
BLM
]
completely independent of
[
MOOP
]
since the only input previosuly needed coming from Gromov–Witten theory was the Virasoro constraints for Hilbert schemes of points on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2},\ {\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
(see the proof of
[
Mor
, Proposition 3.8]
), which is contained in Theorem
F
(i).

In the last section, we explore in further detail the case of the Grassmannian. We explain that, once we suitably describe Schubert calculus using the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
, the Virasoro constraints for the Grassmannian can be interpreted as a well-known fact in the representation theory of the Virasoro algebra. Given
c,h\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the Virasoro Lie algebra acts naturally on the ring of symmetric functions
\Lambda
(regarded as the Fock space) so that
1\in\Lambda
is the highest weight vector of weight
(c,h)
;
c
is called the central charge.

Theorem G
.

The Virasoro constraints for the Grassmannian
\mathsf{Gr}(k,N)
are equivalent to the rectangular Schur polynomial
s_{(N-k)^{k}}
being singular vectors for representations of the Virasoro Lie algebra on
\Lambda
with central charge
c=1
.

We also find that the wall-crossing formula for the Grassmannian leads to a new proof that the rectangular Schur polynomials admit a formula in terms of what we call symmetrized Hecke operators, re-deriving (a particular case of) a result in
[
CJ
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

We explain some connections of this paper to other works. The Virasoro constraints for quiver moduli spaces (Theorem
A
) is proven in
[

. A version of the framed Virasoro constraints (Theorem
B
) is proven in
[
Boj

for
\theta=0
. We use more general stability condition to construct the Virasoro representations on cohomology groups. Part (i) of Theorem
F
is proven for torsion free sheaves on any del Pezzo surfaces in
[
BLM
]
, allowing strictly semistable sheaves; for
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

[

.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

This paper consists of 4 parts listed below in order; standard results about quivers, technical heart of the paper, application to del Pezzo surfaces and a case study of Grassmannian varieties.

In Section
2
, we recollect standard definitions and results about quivers with relations and their framed analogues. In Section
3
, we discuss topics related to dg quivers and their modules; this includes a discussion about bounded derived categories, dg replacement of quivers with relations, and the standard resolution.

In Section
4
, we state the Virasoro constraints for quasi-smooth quivers and their framed analogue. We prove the Virasoro constraints for framed quivers (Theorem
B
) and the geometricity of the Virasoro operators under a smoothness assumption (Theorem
C
), assuming Theorem
A
. In Section
5
, we introduce lattice vertex algebras and Joyce’s vertex algebras and construct a natural isomorphisms between them (Theorem
D
).

We work in a fairly general setting of dg categories satisfying Assumption
5.10
, and show in Section
6
that this assumption is satisfied for the dg category of representations of a dg quiver. In particular, we explain a construction of the descendent algebra and the Virasoro representation on it that is intrinsic to a dg category. We finish Section
6
by proving the Virasoro constraints for quasi-smooth quivers (Theorem
A
) using wall-crossing formulas in Joyce’s vertex algebra.

In Section
7
, we apply our theory to moduli spaces of Bridgeland stable objects on del Pezzo surfaces. We use the Virasoro constraints and geometricity for quivers (Theorem
A
and
C
) and the non-commutative nature of the Virasoro operators (Theorem
D
(ii)) to prove the Virasoro constraints and representations of moduli spaces admitting a quiver description (Theorem
E
and
F
).

In Section
8
, we discuss the Virasoro constraints for Grassmannians in terms of ring of symmetric functions (Theorem
G
).

2.
Moduli of quiver representations

2.1.
Fundamentals of quiver representations

In this section, we recall standard definitions in quiver representations and set up notations. See
[
Kin
,
Rei2
]
and references therein for further details.

2.1.1.
Quiver representations

A quiver is a tuple
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
where
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
are the finite sets of vertices and arrows, respectively, and
s,t:Q_{1}\rightarrow Q_{0}
send arrows to their source and target. We assume throughout the paper that
Q
has no oriented cycle, i.e., acyclic.

Representation of a quiver
Q
is a tuple
(V,\rho)
where
V=(V_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
is a collection of finite dimensional vector spaces and
\rho=(\rho_{e}:V_{s(e)}\rightarrow V_{t(e)})_{e\in Q_{1}}
is a collection of linear maps. We will often abuse notation and say that
V
is a representation of
Q
, leaving the collection of linear maps implicit. Representations of
Q
form an abelian category
\mathrm{Rep}_{Q}
. Associated to
\mathrm{Rep}_{Q}
are the bounded derived category
D^{b}(Q)
and Grothendieck’s
K
-group
K(Q)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
. If
V
is a representation, its dimension vector
\dim V\coloneqq(\dim V_{v})_{v\in Q_{0}}
is an element of
K(Q)
.

2.1.2.
Path algebra

A path of length
k\geq 0
in a quiver
Q
is a sequence of the form

e_{k}\cdots e_{1}\coloneqq\Big[i_{0}\xrightarrow{e_{1}}i_{1}\xrightarrow{e_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{e_{k}}i_{k}\Big]

where
t(e_{j})=s(e_{j+1})
for all
j=1,\dots,k-1
. A source and target of a path
e_{k}\cdots e_{1}
is defined as
s(e_{1})
and
t(e_{k})
, respectively. Denote the length zero path at
v\in Q_{0}
by
{\bf 1}_{v}
. Path algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
is defined as a
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra with a basis given by paths of arbitrary lengths and the multiplication given by composition of composable paths. Then
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
is an associative (typically non-commutative)
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra with a unit
{\bf 1}=\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}{\bf 1}_{v}
. Since
Q
has no oriented cycle,
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
is finite dimensional.

A quiver representation
(V,\rho)
gives rise to a finitely generated left
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
-module
\oplus_{v\in Q_{0}}V_{v}
with a left multiplication by
e\in Q_{1}
induced from
\rho_{e}
. Conversely, given a finitely generated left
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
-module
V
, we get a quiver representation with
V_{v}\coloneqq{\bf 1}_{v}\cdot V
and
\rho_{e}:{\bf 1}_{s(e)}\cdot V\rightarrow{\bf 1}_{t(e)}\cdot V
given by left multiplication by
e\in{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
. This defines an equivalence of categories
1
1

1

By
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
-mod, we mean a category of fintely generated left
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]
-modules.

\mathrm{Rep}_{Q}\simeq{\mathbb{C}}[Q]\textup{-mod}\,.

Example 2.1
.

Associated to each
v\in Q_{0}
are a projective representation
P(v)\coloneqq{\mathbb{C}}[Q]\cdot{\bf 1}_{v}
and a simple representation
S(v)\coloneqq{\bf 1}_{v}\cdot{\mathbb{C}}[Q]\cdot{\bf 1}_{v}
. By definition, we have

\mathrm{Hom}_{Q}\big(P(v),V\big)=V_{v}\quad\text{and}\quad\textup{Ext}^{n}_{Q}\big(P(v),V\big)=0\text{ for }n>0.

2.1.3.
Standard resolution and Euler form

Define the Euler characteristics between two
Q
-representations
V
and
V^{\prime}
as

\chi_{Q}\big(V,V^{\prime}\big)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n}\dim\textup{Ext}_{Q}^{n}(V,V^{\prime}).

By additivity with respect to short exact sequences, the Euler form is defined in
K(Q)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
.

For any
Q
-representation
V
, we have a standard projective resolution

(1)

0\rightarrow\bigoplus_{e\in Q_{1}}P(t(e))\otimes V_{s(e)}\rightarrow\bigoplus_{v\in Q_{0}}P(v)\otimes V_{v}\rightarrow V\rightarrow 0.

In particular, the abelian category
\mathrm{Rep}_{Q}
has homological dimension at most 1. Applying
\mathrm{Hom}_{Q}(-,V^{\prime})
to (
1
), we obtain a complex of vector spaces representing
\mathrm{RHom}_{Q}(V,V^{\prime})
:

0\rightarrow\bigoplus_{v\in Q_{0}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{v},V^{\prime}_{v})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{e\in Q_{1}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{C}}(V_{s(e)},V^{\prime}_{t(e)})\rightarrow 0

whose cohomology computes the Ext groups
\textup{Ext}^{n}_{Q}(V,V^{\prime})
. Furthermore, we obtain an explicit formula for the Euler form

\chi_{Q}(-,-)\colon{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}},\quad(d,d^{\prime})\mapsto\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}d_{v}\cdot d^{\prime}_{v}-\sum_{e\in Q_{1}}d_{s(e)}\cdot d_{t(e)}^{\prime}.

Since
Q
has no oriented cycle, there is an ordering of
Q_{0}
such that the Euler form with respect to the induced basis is upper-triangular with
1
along the diagonal. In particular, the Euler form
\chi_{Q}(-,-)
is a perfect pairing on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{Q_{0}}
.

Given a perfect integral pairing
\chi:\Lambda\times\Lambda\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
on a finitely generated free abelian group
\Lambda
, we define its diagonal as

\Delta\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}v_{i}\otimes\hat{v}_{i}\in\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda

where
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is an integral basis and
\{\hat{v}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is the dual basis satisfying
\chi(\hat{v}_{i},v_{j})=\delta_{ij}
. The diagonal is characterized by the property that the composition

\Lambda\xrightarrow{\Delta\otimes\id}\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\Lambda\xrightarrow{\id\otimes\chi}\Lambda

is an identity, or equivalently by
(\chi\otimes\id)\circ(\id\otimes\Delta)=\id
.

Example 2.2
.

By Example

2.2.1.
Stability conditions and moduli spaces

A stability condition on

is a linear functional

. We say that

\mu^{\theta}(V^{\prime})(\leq)\mu^{\theta}(V)
for all nonzero proper subrepresentations

.

A stability condition
\theta
defines

-equivariant Zariski open subsets

that correspond to the locus of
\theta
-(semi)stable representations of
(Q,I)
, which are possibly emptysets. By
[

,
\theta
-(semi)stability can be interpreted as GIT-(semi)stability with respect to certain character of
{\rm GL}_{d}
constructed from
\theta
. Since

{\mathcal{M}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}=[R^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}/{\rm GL}_{d}]\rightarrow R^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}\sslash{\rm GL}_{d}\eqqcolon M^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}.

If
V
is
\theta
-stable, then
\mathrm{Hom}_{Q,I}\big(V,V\big)\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore, the

-action on

has only constant stabilizer group

and hence the good moduli map is a
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-gerbe

2.3.

We review here the notions of framed representations and corresponding moduli spaces, mostly following the presentation in

Moduli of framed representations can be understood in terms of unframed representations of a larger quiver. 2.3.1.
Framed representations of quiver

Fix a framing vector

-framed representation of
(Q,I)
is a tuple
((V,\rho),\phi)
where
(V,\rho)
is a representation of
(Q,I)
and

2.3.2.
Moduli stack of framed representations

For a framing vector
f\in{\mathbb{N}}^{Q_{0}}\backslash\{0\}
and a usual dimension vector

, we define a moduli stack

of
f
-framed representations of
Q
with a dimenison vector

. Precisely, the moduli stack is given by the global quotient stack

{\mathcal{M}}_{f\shortrightarrow d}=[R_{f\shortrightarrow d}/{\rm GL}_{d}]

where

is a product of general linear groups acting on

by

The stack

maps to
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
by forgetting the framing. In factr,

over
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
.

The moduli stack
{\mathcal{M}}_{f\shortrightarrow d}
is equipped with a universal framed representation
\big(({\mathcal{V}},\rho),\phi\big)
where
({\mathcal{V}},\rho)
is the universal representation pulled back from
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
, and

is a collection of universal framings. We remark that
\mathbb{G}_{m}\subseteq{\rm GL}_{d}
no longer acts trivially on

if

and

2.3.3.

Let

Note that the second condition is equivalent to the

is
\theta
-semistable. On the other hand, if

\theta
-stable then

The limit

-invariant Zariski open subset

The

with a dimension vector

3.
Differential graded quivers

Many of the constructions in this paper, except Section
2
, depend not only on
(Q,I)
but also on the choice of generators
I=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
. For example, choosing generators is necessary to endow the moduli spaces of stable representations
M^{\theta-\textup{st}}_{d}
with a virtual fundamental class. Quasi-smooth differential graded (=dg) quivers provide a natural language to keep track of such a choice of generators.

One of the advantages of working in the generality of dg quivers is that it provides a more natural approach to vertex algebras for quivers with relations. The vertex algebras constructed in
[
Joy2
]
for quivers with relations require a choice of generators of
I
, and in particular they are not intrinsic to
D^{b}(Q,I)
. This raises an important question in applications: if
X
is a variety with
D^{b}(X)\simeq D^{b}(Q,I)
, do we have an isomorphism between the vertex algebra associated to
X
and a vertex algebra associated to
(Q,I)
together with a choice of generators of
I
? The answer is typically no if
\dim X>2
. Instead, the vertex algebra associated to
X
is isomorphic to the vertex algebra canonically associated to a certain dg quiver
{\bf Q}
. When
{\bf Q}
is quasi-smooth, this recovers the construction in
[
Joy2
]
with a choice of generators.

In this section, we review definitions and known results on dg quivers and their modules which we need for applications to Virasoro constraints.

3.1.
Fundamentals of dg quivers

3.1.1.
Dg algebra and derived category

Before explaining dg quivers, we briefly review dg algebras (over the complex numbers) and their derived categories, see
[
Toë
,
Kel1
]
and
[
Sta
,
Tag 09JD
]
for details.

A dg algebra is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded associative algebra
{\bf A}=\bigoplus\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\bf A}^{(n)}
together with a unit
1\in{\bf A}^{(0)}
and a differential
\partial:{\bf A}\rightarrow{\bf A}
satisfying the graded Leibniz rule

\partial(ab)=\partial(a)\cdot b+(-1)^{\deg(a)}\,a\cdot\partial(b).

All the differentials will be of degree
1
so we will not mention this again.

A dg
{\bf A}
-module is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-graded
{\bf A}
-module
M=\bigoplus\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}M^{(n)}
together with a differential
\partial:M\rightarrow M
satisfying the graded Leibniz rule. For any
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define the
k
-shifted dg
{\bf A}
-module
M[k]
with a shifted grading
M[k]^{(n)}\coloneqq M^{(n+k)}
and the same structure of
{\bf A}
-module and differential
\partial
. Given two dg modules
M
and
N
, we denote by
\mathrm{Hom}_{\bf A}^{\mathrm{gr}}(M,N)
the vector space of degree preserving
{\bf A}
-module homomorphisms.
4
4

4

Note that
f\in\mathrm{Hom}^{\mathrm{gr}}_{\bf A}(M,N)
is required to preserve the gradings but not the differentials.
We define a complex of vector spaces

\mathrm{Hom}_{\bf A}^{\bullet}(M,N)\coloneqq\bigoplus_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\bf A}^{\mathrm{gr}}(M,N[n])

whose differential is given by

\partial(f)=f\circ\partial_{M}-(-1)^{n}\partial_{N}\circ f\quad\textnormal{for}\quad f\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\bf A}^{\mathrm{gr}}(M,N[n]).

This defines a dg category
\mathrm{Rep}^{\mathsf{dg}}_{\bf A}
of dg
{\bf A}
-modules.

Given any dg category
{\bf T}
, we can take the associated categories
Z^{0}({\bf T})
and
[{\bf T}]
whose objects are the same as
{\bf T}
but morphisms between two elements
M
and
N
are given by

Z^{0}(\mathrm{Hom}^{\bullet}_{\bf T}(M,N)),\quad H^{0}(\mathrm{Hom}^{\bullet}_{\bf T}(M,N)),

respectively. We call
[{\bf T}]
the homotopy category of
{\bf T}
. According to these notations,
Z^{0}(\mathrm{Rep}^{\mathsf{dg}}_{\bf A})
is the category of dg
{\bf A}
-modules with dg homomorphisms and
[\mathrm{Rep}^{\mathsf{dg}}_{\bf A}]
is the homotopy category of dg
{\bf A}
-modules.

We say that a dg
{\bf A}
-module
M
is acyclic if
H^{n}(M,\partial_{M})=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Denote the full subcategory of acyclic dg modules by
\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}\subset\mathrm{Rep}^{\mathsf{dg}}_{\bf A}
which is naturally a dg category. A dg
{\bf A}
-module
P
is called h-projective if
(\mathrm{Hom}_{{\bf A}}^{\bullet}(P,M),\partial)
is acyclic for every acyclic
M
. Denote by
\textnormal{h-proj}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}
the full sub dg category of h-projective dg modules. If
P
is h-projective and
P\to M
induces an isomorphism on cohomology, we say
P
is an h-projective resolution of
M
.

The derived category of
{\bf A}
is defined as a Verdier quotient

D({\bf A})\coloneqq[\mathrm{Rep}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}]\,/\,[\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}]

which is naturally a triangulated category.
Equivalently, one can first take a dg quotient category
\mathrm{Rep}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}/\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}
and then take its homotopy category

D({\bf A})\simeq[\mathrm{Rep}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}/\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}]\,.

Therefore, the dg category
\mathrm{Rep}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}/\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}
gives a dg enhancement of the triangulated category
D({\bf A})
. Since
\textnormal{h-proj}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}
is quasi-equivalent to
\mathrm{Rep}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}/\textnormal{Ac}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}
, we can also think of it as a dg enhancement

D({\bf A})\simeq[\textnormal{h-proj}_{\bf A}^{\mathsf{dg}}]\,.

An object
M\in D({\bf A})
is called perfect (also known as compact) if
\mathrm{Hom}_{D({\bf A})}(M,-)
commutes with arbitrary direct sums. We define a bounded derived category, denoted by
D^{b}({\bf A})
, as the full subcategory of perfect objects in
D({\bf A})
.
5
5

5

In the literature, this is often called as a perfect derived category and denoted by
D_{\textup{perf}}({\bf A})
. We use the nonstandard notation and terminology because we work only in cases where the bounded derived category agrees with the perfect derived category.
This is again a triangulated category and is equipped with a dg enhancement induced from that of
D({\bf A})
. Given
M\in D({\bf A})
we have a derived functor
\mathrm{RHom}_{{\bf A}}(M,-)\colon D({\bf A})\to D({\mathbb{C}})
and we write

for

. If
{\bf A}
is a compact dg algebra (i.e.

as long as

by

.

A morphism between two dg algebras

and

is a degree preserving algebra homomorphism
\phi:{\bf A}\rightarrow{\bf A}^{\prime}
with
\phi\circ\partial_{{\bf A}}=\partial_{{\bf A}^{\prime}}\circ\phi
. Such a morphism is called a quasi-isomorphism if it induces an isomorphism
H^{n}({\bf A},\partial_{{\bf A}})\simeq H^{n}({\bf A}^{\prime},\partial_{{\bf A}^{\prime}})
for all

. A quasi-isomorphism induces an equivalence of triangulated categories
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D({\bf A})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D({\bf A}^{\prime})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

where

and

are the extension and restriction of scalars, respectively, with respect to the quasi-isomorphism [Lemma 37.1 of Stacks project]. Since

and

.

3.1.2.
Dg quiver

Roughly speaking, the notion of a dg quiver is obtained by replacing
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I
by a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq 0}
-graded dg algebra. Precisely, a dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
is a usual (finite and acyclic) quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}=({\mathbb{Q}}_{0},{\mathbb{Q}}_{1},s,t)
together with a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq 0}
-grading function
|\cdot|\colon{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq 0}
and a differential
\partial
on
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
which we explain now. First, a grading function on
{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}
is a decomposition into a disjoint union

{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}=\coprod_{n\leq 0}{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}

which induces a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{\leq 0}
-graded algebra structure on
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]=\bigoplus\limits_{n\leq 0}{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]^{(n)}
. Second, a differential on
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
is a degree
1
map

\partial:{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]

satisfying the graded Leibniz rule and preserving the source and target. By Leibniz rule,
\partial
is uniquely determined by its value on the set of edges
{\bf Q}_{1}
. This makes
({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}],\partial)
a dg algebra, so all the notions from Section
3.1.1
apply to dg quivers. Note that
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
is a compact dg algebra because
{\bf Q}
is finite and acyclic. Denote by
\mathrm{Rep}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}^{\mathsf{dg}}
the dg category of dg
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
-modules. The associated (bounded) derived category
D^{b}({\mathbb{Q}})
has dg enhancements induced from
\textnormal{h-proj}^{\mathsf{dg}}_{{\bf Q}}
.

Example 3.1
.

For each
v\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}
, we have a projective dg module
P(v)\coloneqq{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]\cdot{\bf 1}_{v}
and a simple dg module
S(v)\coloneqq{\bf 1}_{v}\cdot{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]\cdot{\bf 1}_{v}
. Given a left dg
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf Q}]
-module
M
,
M_{v}=\mathbf{1}_{v}\cdot M
is a complex of vector spaces. It is easy to check that the map
\mathrm{Hom}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}^{\mathrm{gr}}(P(v),M[n])\to M_{v}^{(n)}
defined by
f\mapsto(-1)^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}f(\mathbf{1}_{v})
produces an isomorphism of complexes of vector spaces

\mathrm{Hom}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}^{\bullet}(P(v),M)\simeq M_{v}\,.

In particular,
P(v)
is h-projective and perfect since clearly
M
acyclic implies that
M_{v}
is acyclic and the
(-)_{v}
construction commutes with arbitrary direct sums.

The underlying quiver of a dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
is defined as
Q\coloneqq({\mathbb{Q}}_{0},{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(0)},s,t)
consisting only of degree zero arrows. The underlying quiver is equipped with an ideal of relations

I\coloneqq\textnormal{image}\big(\partial:{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]^{(-1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]^{(0)}\big)\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]^{(0)}={\mathbb{C}}[Q].

By definition, we have

H^{0}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}],\partial)={\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I.

Example 3.2
.

Let
(Q,I)
be a quiver with relations. Fix a choice of generating relations
I=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
. Given such a choice, we have a dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
such that

{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}=Q_{0},\quad{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(0)}=Q_{1},\quad{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(-1)}=\{\tilde{r}_{1},\dots,\tilde{r}_{n}\},\quad{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(<-1)}=\emptyset

with
s(\tilde{r}_{i})=s(r_{i})
and
t(\tilde{r}_{i})=t(r_{i})
. Putting
\partial(\tilde{r}_{i})=r_{i}
, this uniquely extends to a differential on
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
by the graded Leibniz rule. We clearly have
H^{0}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}])={\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I
.

We call a dg quiver quasi-smooth if
{\mathbb{Q}}_{1}^{(n)}=\emptyset
for
n<-1
. The above construction shows that a quasi-smooth dg quiver is equivalent to a quiver with a choice of generating relations
I=(r_{1},\dots,r_{n})
. We will use these two notions interchangeably. When
{\bf Q}
is quasi-smooth, we will write
Q_{2}={\bf Q}_{1}^{(-1)}
, so that
Q_{0},Q_{1},Q_{2}
are the sets of vertices, edges and relations, respectively; this matches the notation in
[
Joy2
, Definition 6.2]
.

3.1.3.
Path algebra as a tensor algebra

The description of the path algebra that we now give will later be used to describe the standard resolution on a dg quiver.

Let
Q
be a (usual) quiver and let
S
be the algebra

S=\bigoplus_{v\in Q_{0}}{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{1}_{v}

with multiplication given by
{\bf 1}_{v}^{2}={\bf 1}_{v}
and
\mathbf{1}_{v}\mathbf{1}_{w}=0
for
v\neq w
. Note that a left
S
-modules is the same as a
{\mathbb{C}}
-vector space
T
together with a decomposition
T=\oplus_{v\in Q_{0}}T(v,-)
, where
T(v,-)=\mathbf{1}_{v}\cdot T
. Similarly, a right
S
-module is a vector space together with a decomposition with summands
T(-,v)=T\cdot\mathbf{1}_{v}
and a bimodule comes with a decomposition with summands
T(v,w)=\mathbf{1}_{v}\cdot T\cdot\mathbf{1}_{w}
. If
T_{1}
is a right
S
-module and
T_{2}
is a left
S
-module then

T_{1}\otimes_{S}T_{2}=\bigoplus_{v\in Q_{0}}T_{1}(-,v)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T_{2}(v,-)\,.

The source and target maps give
T:=\bigoplus_{e\in Q_{1}}{\mathbb{C}}e
the structure of
S
-bimodule where
T(v,w)
is spanned by the arrows
w\mapsto v
. Then the path algebra of a (usual) quiver can be described as the tensor algebra of
T
over
S

{\mathcal{T}}_{S}(T)=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}\underbrace{T\otimes_{S}T\otimes_{S}\ldots\otimes_{S}T}_{n\textup{ times}}\,.

If we replace
Q
by a dg quiver
{\bf Q}
, then
T
becomes naturally a graded
S
-bimodule. Endowing the path algebra
{\mathbb{C}}[{\bf Q}]
with a differential is the same as giving a map of degree 1 between the graded
S
-bimodules
T\to{\mathcal{T}}_{S}(T)={\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]
.

3.2.
Dg replacement of quiver with relations

A dg replacement of a quiver with relations
(Q,I)
is a dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
with the following properties:

(1)

the underlying quiver of
{\mathbb{Q}}
is
Q
,

(2)

the zeroth cohomology is
H^{0}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}],\partial)={\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I

(3)

H^{i}({\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}],\partial)=0
for all

By definition, we have a quasi-isomorphism
{\mathbb{C}}[{\mathbb{Q}}]\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I
where
{\mathbb{C}}[Q]/I
is given a trivial dg structure. This induces an equivalence between triangulated categories
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{b}({\mathbb{Q}})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D^{b}(Q,I)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}

This is a consequence of Koszul duality for augmented

[

(over a field) and

for

(note that any

We now recall some statements about standard resolutions of representations in the context of dg quivers and quivers with relations; see Section

We start with the dg quiver case and then use dg replacements to deduce the results we want in the case of quivers with relations. Let

Theorem 3.5

Let

Proof.

This is shown in loc. cit. when

We just make a few remarks about the theorem. The two maps in the distinguished triangle are

This h-projective resolution gives an easy computation of

Proposition 3.6
.

A similar analysis holds for

We will denote

4.2.2.
Virasoro operators

We define the Virasoro operators
\{{\mathsf{L}}_{n}\,|\,n\geq-1\}
on the descendent algebra
{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}
. The operators naturally decompose into two parts
{\mathsf{L}}_{n}=\mathsf{R}_{n}+{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
. First,
\mathsf{R}_{n}
is a derivation operator such that

\mathsf{R}_{n}(\mathrm{ch}_{k}(v))=k\cdot(k+1)\cdots(k+n)\,\mathrm{ch}_{k+n}(v).

Second,
{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
is a multiplication operator by an element

{\mathsf{T}}_{n}=\sum_{a+b=n}a!b!\left(\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\mathrm{ch}_{a}(v)\mathrm{ch}_{b}(v)-\sum_{e\in Q_{1}}\mathrm{ch}_{a}(s(e))\mathrm{ch}_{b}(t(e))+\sum_{\tilde{r}\in Q_{2}}\mathrm{ch}_{a}(s(\tilde{r}))\mathrm{ch}_{b}(t(\tilde{r}))\right)

for which we use the same notation. Note that this can be succinctly written using the Euler form as

{\mathsf{T}}_{n}=\sum_{a+b=n}a!b!\sum_{v,w\in Q_{0}}{\chi}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(v,w)\mathrm{ch}_{a}(v)\mathrm{ch}_{b}(w)\,.

One can check that these operators satisfy (the dual version of) the Virasoro bracket formula

[{\mathsf{L}}_{n},{\mathsf{L}}_{m}]=(m-n){\mathsf{L}}_{n+m}\in{\rm End}({\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}).

Since the
\mathsf{R}_{n}

M_{(1,d)}^{\tilde{\theta}}(Q^{f},I^{f})
are equivalent to

(8)

\int_{[M_{(1,d)}^{\tilde{\theta}}(Q^{f},I^{f})]^{\mathrm{vir}}}\xi_{\mathcal{V}}\big(\big({\mathsf{L}}_{n}^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{f}}+{\mathsf{S}}_{n}^{\infty}\big)(D)\big)=0\quad\textup{ for all }n\geq-1,D\in{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{f}}\,,

where

are the Virasoro operators defined in

(see Section

) and

By identifying

we can write

{\mathsf{T}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{f}}_{n}={\mathsf{T}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}_{n}-\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}f_{v}\sum_{a+b=n}a!b!\mathrm{ch}_{a}(\infty)\mathrm{ch}_{b}(v)+\sum_{a+b=n}a!b!\mathrm{ch}_{a}(\infty)\mathrm{ch}_{b}(\infty)\,.

Since

for all

(note that for

it is the dimensional constraint) is equivalent to

\int_{[M_{(1,d)}^{\tilde{\theta}}(Q^{f},I^{f})]^{\mathrm{vir}}}\xi_{\mathcal{V}}\Big(\Big(\mathsf{R}_{n}+{\mathsf{T}}_{n}^{{\mathbb{Q}}}-\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}f_{v}n!\mathrm{ch}_{n}(v)\Big)(D)\Big)=0\quad\textup{ for }D\in{\mathbb{D}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}^{f}}\,.

Note that the universal representation on

is

, so when

these are precisely the framed Virasoro constraints (

) for

, showing that the unframed Virasoro constraints imply the framed Virasoro constraints. Conversely, the framed Virasoro constraints imply the unframed Virasoro constraints since equation (

∎

4.4.
Geometricity of Virasoro operators

A simple but fairly interesting observation that the authors learned from A. Mellit is that the framed Virasoro constraints imply that the
\mathsf{R}_{n}
operators descend to cohomology of moduli spaces of framed representations when those are smooth. We also prove the analogous statement for the smooth moduli stack of unframed representations by approximating its cohomology by the cohomology of framed moduli spaces. We call this phenomena the geometricity of the Virasoro operators. Let
{\mathbb{Q}}
be a quasi-smooth dg quiver and
(Q,I)
be the underlying quiver with relations below.

Definition 4.5
.

Let

descends via
\xi
, i.e. there is a dashed arrow completing the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\ast}(M)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{\ast}(M)\,.}

Remark 4.6

(i) Note that

This notion is more natural to consider when the realization homomorphism

5.
Vertex algebras from dg categories

As discovered in
[
BLM
]
, vertex algebras, in particular the ones constructed by Joyce in
[
Joy1
,
GJT
,
Joy2
]
, are deeply connected to the Virasoro constraints for moduli spaces of sheaves. In this section, we introduce lattice vertex algebras and Joyce’s vertex algebras from dg categories. The main result of this section is a construction of a natural isomorphism from Joyce’s vertex algebra to lattice vertex algebra under the assumptions that are satisfied for our applications.

We now briefly recall the necessary basic notions of vertex algebras; the reader can find a much more detailed exposition in
[
Kac
]
. A vertex algebra is a vector space
V
over a field (which, in this paper, will always be
{\mathbb{Q}}
) equipped with a vacuum vector
\ket{0}\in V
, a translation operator
T\colon V\to V
and a state-field correspondence
Y\colon V\to{\rm End}(V)\llbracket z^{-1},z\rrbracket
. This data has to satisfy certain axioms called vacuum axiom, translation covariance and locality, see
[
Kac
, Section 1.3]
.
7
7

7

Vertex algebras in
[
Kac
]
are super-vertex algebras. The vertex algebras considered in this paper are entirely even. Moreover, even though every vertex algebra in this paper admits a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-grading, it will not be considered.
The state-field correspondence can be encoded as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-collection of bilinear products
-_{(n)}-\colon V\otimes V\to V
defined by

Y(u,z)v=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}u_{(n)}v\,z^{-1-n}\,.

Given a vertex algebra
V
, there is a standard way, due to Borcherds
[
Bor
]
, to construct a Lie algebra associated to it. Define

\widecheck{V}\coloneqq V/T(V)

and define a Lie bracket on
\widecheck{V}
by

[\overline{u},\overline{v}]=\overline{u_{(0)}v}\,,

where we denote by
\overline{u}\in\widecheck{V}
the image of
u\in V
in the quotient. It can be shown that this does not depend on the representatives
u,v
chosen and that it satisfies the axioms of a Lie algebra.

Vertex algebras often come with a conformal element
\omega
, see
[
Kac
, Definition 4.10]
; when this is the case we say that
(V,\omega)
is a vertex operator algebra. The element
\omega
induces a representation of the Virasoro Lie algebra on
V
. More precisely, the operators
L_{n}\coloneqq\omega_{(n+1)}\in{\rm End}(V)
satisfy the Virasoro bracket relations

\big[L_{n},L_{m}\big]=(n-m)L_{m+n}+\frac{n^{3}-n}{12}\delta_{n+m,0}\,c\cdot\id\,,

where
c\in{\mathbb{Q}}
is some constant called the central charge of
(V,\omega)
.

5.1.
Lattice vertex algebras

In this paper, a lattice is a finitely generated free abelian group
\Lambda
together with a symmetric bilinear form
B:\Lambda\times\Lambda\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
. We do not assume that the bilinear form is non-degenerate. The main example is the lattice of a quasi-smooth dg quiver
{\mathbb{Q}}
, namely
\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}^{{\mathbb{Q}}_{0}}
with the symmetrized Euler form
\chi_{{\mathbb{Q}}}^{\text{sym}}(-,-)
.

We recall how the lattice
\Lambda
gives arise to a lattice vertex algebra

\textup{VA}(\Lambda,B)\coloneqq\Big(V_{\Lambda},\ |0\rangle\in V_{\Lambda},\ T:V_{\Lambda}\rightarrow V_{\Lambda},\ Y:V_{\Lambda}\otimes V_{\Lambda}\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}(\hskip-2.0pt(z)\hskip-2.0pt)\Big).

The underlying vector space is defined as a tensor product

V_{\Lambda}={\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda]\otimes{\mathbb{D}}_{\Lambda}

of a group algebra
{\mathbb{Q}}[\Lambda]
and a free algebra
{\mathbb{D}}_{\Lambda}\coloneqq\Sym(\Lambda\otimes t^{-1}{\mathbb{Q}}[t^{-1}])
. Recall that the group algebra has a basis
\{e^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
with a multiplication rule
e^{\alpha}\cdot e^{\beta}=e^{\alpha+\beta}
. We denote the element
v\cdot t^{-k}\in{\mathbb{D}}_{\Lambda}
for
v\in\Lambda
and
k>0
simply by
v_{-k}
. Therefore, general elements in
V_{\Lambda}
are linear combinations of elements of the form

e^{\alpha}\otimes v^{1}_{-k_{1}}\cdots v^{\ell}_{-k_{\ell}}

where
\alpha,v^{1},\dots,v^{\ell}\in\Lambda
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{\ell}>0.
In this notation, the vacuum vector is defined as
\ket{0}\coloneqq e^{0}\otimes 1\in V_{\Lambda}
. We will abbreviate
e^{\alpha}\otimes 1
to
e^{\alpha}
and
e^{0}\otimes v^{1}_{-k_{1}}\cdots v^{\ell}_{-k_{\ell}}
to
v^{1}_{-k_{1}}\cdots v^{\ell}_{-k_{\ell}}
.

For each
v\in\Lambda
and
k>0
, we define the creation operation
v_{(-k)}
as a left multiplication by
v_{-k}
. This defines a free
{\mathbb{D}}_{\Lambda}
-module structure on
V_{\Lambda}
with a basis
\{e^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
. Therefore, to define an operator
A:V_{\Lambda}\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}
it suffices to specify the commutator
[A,v_{(-k)}]
for all
v\in\Lambda
,
k>0
and its values on the basis
A(e^{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda
. We define a translation operator
T:V_{\Lambda}\rightarrow V_{\Lambda}
in this way:

[T,v_{(-k)}]=k\cdot v_{(-k-1)},\quad T(e^{\alpha})=e^{\alpha}\otimes\alpha_{-1}.

The definition of the state-field correspondence uses annihilation operators
v_{(k)}
for
k\geq 0
. These are defined by

\displaystyle v_{(0)}(e^{\alpha})=B(v,\alpha)e^{\alpha}\,,\quad v_{(k)}(e^{\alpha})=0\quad\textup{ for }k>0\,,\quad

\displaystyle[v_{(k)},w_{(-l)}]=k\cdot\delta_{k,l}\cdot B(v,w)\cdot\id\quad\textup{ for }k\geq 0,l>0\,.

Combining the creation and annihilation operators, we define

(11)

Y(v_{-1},z)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}v_{(k)}z^{-1-k}.

We also define

(12)

Y(e^{\alpha},z)=\epsilon_{\alpha,\beta}z^{B(\alpha,\beta)}e^{\alpha}\exp\left(-\sum_{k<0}\frac{\alpha_{(k)}}{k}z^{-k}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{k>0}\frac{\alpha_{(k)}}{k}z^{-k}\right)

when restricted to
e^{\beta}\otimes{\mathbb{D}}_{\Lambda}\subseteq V_{\Lambda}
. Here
e^{\alpha}
is the operator sending
e^{\beta}\otimes w
to
e^{\alpha+\beta}\otimes w
and
\epsilon_{\alpha,\beta}=\pm 1
are signs satisfying some compatibility, see
[
Kac
, 5.4.14]
; the vertex algebra is independent from the choice of signs, and when
B
is obtained as the symmetrization of a bilinear form
b\colon\Lambda\times\Lambda\to{\mathbb{Z}}
there is a canonical choice given by
\epsilon_{\alpha,\beta}=(-1)^{b(\alpha,\beta)}
. The state-field correspondence is entirely determined by (
11
), (
12
) together with the reconstruction theorem
[
Kac
, Theorem 4.5]
.

5.1.1.
Virasoro operators and primary states

When
B
is a non-degenerate pairing, the lattice vertex algebra

is well known to carry a natural conformal element
\omega\in V_{\Lambda}

[
Kac

. Given a basis

\omega=\frac{1}{2}\cdot e^{0}\otimes\sum_{v}\hat{v}_{-1}v_{-1}\,.

As explained in the beginning of Section

Even when

(15)

Note in particular that

.

Remark 5.1
.

We remark that in the case of non-degenerate lattice

{\bm{{\mathcal{N}}}}^{\,{\bf T}}\times{\bm{{\mathcal{N}}}}^{\,{\bf T}}
whose fiber at
(E,F)
is given by the complex
\mathrm{Hom}^{\bullet}_{{\bf T}}(E,F)
. Let
{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\coloneqq t_{0}({\bm{{\mathcal{N}}}}^{\,{\bf T}})
be the classical truncation which is a higher stack and
\textup{Ext}_{\bf T}
be the restriction of
{\bf Ext_{\bf T}}
to the truncation. The stack decomposes into connected component

{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}=\coprod_{\alpha}{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}

and rank of the
\textup{Ext}_{{\bf T}}
complex on
{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}\times{\mathcal{N}}_{\beta}
is given by the pairing
\chi_{{\bf T}}(\alpha,\beta)
.

Joyce constructs a natural vertex algebra structure on the homology group of the (higher) moduli stack
{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
. The definition requires the following geometric data naturally coming from the dg category
{\bf T}
:

(1)

A zero map
0:{\mathsf{pt}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
.

(2)

A direct sum map
\Sigma\colon{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\times{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\to{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
.

(3)

A
B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
action
\rho\colon B{\mathbb{G}}_{m}\times{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\to{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
.

(4)

A
K
-theory class
\Theta
on
{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\times{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
defined by a symmetrization

\Theta=\sigma^{\ast}\textup{Ext}_{{\bf T}}+\textup{Ext}^{\vee}_{{\bf T}}

where
\sigma\colon{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\times{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\to{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}\times{\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}}
is the permutation map.

Note that rank of
\Theta
on
{\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha}\times{\mathcal{N}}_{\beta}
is given by the symmetrized pairing

\chi_{{\bf T}}^{\text{sym}}(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\chi_{\bf T}(\alpha,\beta)+\chi_{{\bf T}}(\beta,\alpha)\,.

The vertex algebra structure

V^{{\bf T}}=\Big(H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}})\,,\,\ket{0}\,,\,T\,,\,Y\Big)

is defined as follows from this data:

(1)

The underlying vector space is
H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}})
.

(2)

The vacuum vector corresponds to the trivial representation, i.e.,

\ket{0}=0_{\ast}[{\mathsf{pt}}]\in H_{0}({\mathcal{N}}^{\,{\bf T}})\,.

(3)

The translation operator is defined by

T(u)=\rho_{\ast}\big(t\boxtimes u\big)

where
t
is the generator of
H_{2}(B{\mathbb{G}}_{m})
.

(4)

The state-field correspondence is

Y(u,z)v=(-1)^{\chi_{{\bf T}}(\alpha,\beta)}z^{\chi_{{\bf T}}^{\text{sym}}(\alpha,\beta)}\Sigma_{\ast}\Big[e^{zT}\boxtimes\text{id}\big(c_{z^{-1}}(\Theta)\cap(u\boxtimes v)\big)\Big]

for any
u\in H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}_{\alpha})
and
v\in H_{\ast}({\mathcal{N}}_{\beta})
.

Remark 5.8
.

In
[
Joy1
,
GJT
,
Joy2
]
, the abelian category version of the above construction is also introduced. There is often a vertex algebra homomorphism from the abelian category version to the dg category version. So every wall-crossing formulas proven in the abelian category version hold also in the dg category version. We work with dg category version because it often admits an explicit description in terms of the lattice vertex algebras as we address in the next section.

Remark 5.9
.

In Joyce’s setup, the
K
-theory class
\Theta
is often an extra choice. By defining
\Theta
as the symmetrization of the Ext complex, the construction becomes canonically associated to the dg category
{\bf T}
. In particular, quasi-equivalent dg categories produce isomorphic vertex algebras.

5.3.
Comparison of two vertex algebras

In Section

Assumption 5.10

Remark 5.11
.

6.
Virasoro constraints via wall-crossing

In this section, we prove the Virasoro constraints for quasi-smooth dg quivers as stated in Theorem
4.2
. In fact, we prove its generalization in terms Joyce’s invariant classes as in Theorem
6.11
, allowing strictly semistable representations. Proof strategy is comparable to that of
[
BLM
]
. We first wall-cross to simpler moduli spaces where we can verify the Virasoro constraints directly and then show the compatibility between wall-crossing and Virasoro constraints using vertex algebras.

6.1.
Wall-crossing

Joyce’s vertex algebra was introduced with the purpose of writing down and proving wall-crossing formulas for descendent integrals. In the case of quivers, this gives a complete algorithm to calculate descendent integrals in any moduli space of stable representations of an acyclic quiver with relations.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}
be a quasi-smooth dg quiver and denote the associated quiver with relations by
(Q,I)
. We denote by

the vertex algebra associated to

. As explained in the beginning of Section

If

is a stability condition such that

This virtual fundamental class can be push-forwarded to

is isomorphic to the homology of the rigidication of the stack

. There is a map

[M_{d}^{\theta}]^{\mathrm{vir}}\in H_{\ast}\Big(\big({\mathcal{N}}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{d}\big)^{\textup{pl}}\Big)\subseteq\widecheck{V}^{\bf Q}\,.

Alternatively, we can think of the class

as follows: by Proposition

7.1.

We start by recalling the notion of exceptional collection and the induced equivalence between a triangulated category with a full and strong exceptional collection and the derived category of a quiver with relations. From now on let

Definition 7.1
.

A sequence

of objects of

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(E_{i},E_{j}[\ell])=\begin{cases}{\mathbb{C}}&\textup{ if }i=j\textup{ and }\ell=0\\

An exceptional collection is said to be full if the smallest triangulated subcategory of

In particular

7.2.2.
Geometric stability conditions

Suppose now that
S
is a smooth projective surface and
{\mathcal{D}}=D^{b}(S)
. Recall that given an ample divisor
H
we may define the slope of a torsion-free sheaf
F
by

\mu^{H}(F)=\frac{c_{1}(F)\cdot H}{\mathrm{rk}(F)}\,.

This defines the notion of
\mu^{H}
-(semi)stable torsion-free sheaves. The slope of pure one-dimensional sheaf
G
is defined as

\mu^{H}(G)=\frac{\mathrm{ch}_{2}(G)}{c_{1}(G)\cdot H}\,.

This defines the notion of
\mu^{H}
-(semi)stability for pure one-dimensional sheaves.

In
[
AB
]
the authors construct stability conditions
\sigma_{E,H}=({\mathcal{A}}_{E,H},Z_{E,H})
where
H,E
are
{\mathbb{R}}
-divisors with
H
being ample. We call these geometric stability conditions. The heart of the
t
-structure is obtained as a tilt

{\mathcal{A}}_{E,H}=\langle\mathrm{Coh}_{H,\leq E\cdot H}[1],\mathrm{Coh}_{H,>E\cdot H}\rangle

of
\mathrm{Coh}(S)
with respect to
\mu^{H}
-stability. Here we write
\mathrm{Coh}_{H,>s}
for the extension closure of torsion sheaves and
\mu^{H}
-semistable torsion-free sheaves
T
with
\mu^{H}(T)>s
; similarly,
\mathrm{Coh}_{H,\leq s}
is the extension closure of
\mu^{H}
-semistable torsion-free sheaves
F
with
\mu^{H}(F)\leq s
. The central charge is given by

Z_{E,H}(F)=-\int_{S}e^{-E-\sqrt{-1}\,H}\mathrm{ch}(F)\in{\mathbb{C}}\,.

Note that these stability conditions have the property that structure sheaves of points
{\mathcal{O}}_{x}
are stable and
Z({\mathcal{O}}_{x})=-1
. By result of
[
Tod
]
, see also
[
MS
]
, geometric stability condition defines an Artin stack of finite type
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{E,H}-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
. Furthermore, loc. cit. proves that
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{E,H}-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
is an open substack of all perfect complexes with trivial negative Ext groups constructed in
[
Lie
]
. Therefore
{\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma_{E,H}-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
is equipped with an obstruction theory whose associated virtual tangent complex at a point
F
is given by
\mathrm{RHom}_{S}(F,F)[1]
.

Geometric stability is closely related to so called twisted Gieseker stability which we explain now. Recall from
[
MW
]
that the
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted Hilbert polynomial of
F\in\mathrm{Coh}(S)
is defined as

P_{(H,E-\frac{K}{2})}(F,t)\coloneqq\int_{X}\mathrm{ch}(F)\cdot e^{-(E-\frac{K}{2})+tH}\cdot\mathrm{td}(S)\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]\,.

The choice of
-K/2
, where
K=K_{S}
denotes the canonical divisor, is made so that

e^{\frac{K}{2}}\cdot\mathrm{td}(S)=(1,0,c\,[{\mathsf{pt}}]),\quad c=\chi({\mathcal{O}}_{S})-\textstyle\frac{K^{2}}{8}\in{\mathbb{Q}}

has no codimension one part. Writing
\mathrm{ch}^{E}(-)\coloneqq\mathrm{ch}(-)\cdot e^{-E}
, we have

P_{(H,E-\frac{K}{2})}(F,t)=t^{2}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{rk}(F)H^{2}}{2}+t\cdot(H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F))+(\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{2}(F)+c\cdot\mathrm{rk}(F))\,.

If
F
is torsion-free, we define the
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted reduced Hilbert polynomial is

p_{(H,E-\frac{K}{2})}(F,t)=t^{2}+t\cdot\frac{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F)}{\mathrm{rk}(F)H^{2}/2}+\frac{\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{2}(F)}{\mathrm{rk}(F)H^{2}/2}+\frac{2c}{H^{2}}\,.

We say that a torsion-free sheaf
F
is
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted Gieseker (semi)stable if for all
0\subsetneq F^{\prime}\subsetneq F
we have

p_{(H,E-\frac{K}{2})}(F^{\prime},t)(\leq)p_{(H,E-\frac{K}{2})}(F,t)\quad\textnormal{for all}\quad t\gg 0\,.

In other words,
F
is
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted Gieseker (semi)stable if for all
0\subsetneq F^{\prime}\subsetneq F
we have one of the followings:

(i)

\displaystyle\frac{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F^{\prime})}{\mathrm{rk}(F^{\prime})}<\frac{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F)}{\mathrm{rk}(F)}

(ii)

\displaystyle\frac{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F^{\prime})}{\mathrm{rk}(F^{\prime})}=\frac{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}^{E}_{1}(F)}{\mathrm{rk}(F)}
and
\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{ch}_{2}^{E}(F^{\prime})}{\mathrm{rk}(F^{\prime})}(\leq)\frac{\mathrm{ch}_{2}^{E}(F)}{\mathrm{rk}(F)}
.

For 1-dimensional sheaves,
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted Gieseker stability is equivalent to
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted slope stability, where the twisted slope of
F
is given by

\frac{\mathrm{ch}_{2}^{E}(F)}{H\cdot\mathrm{ch}_{1}(F)}\,.

The geometric stability recovers
(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted Gieseker stability
in the large volume limit. Note that
{\mathcal{A}}_{E,H}={\mathcal{A}}_{E,tH}
for any
t>0
in the following statement.

Lemma 7.6

(
[
Bri2
]
)
.

Let
H,E
be
{\mathbb{R}}
-divisors with
H
being ample and
\textsf{v}\in K(S)
. Let

be large enough according to

,

and

. Let

(1)

If

(2)

If

and we assume further that

F\textup{ is }\sigma_{E,tH}\textup{-(semi)stable}\Leftrightarrow F\textup{ is }(H,E-{\textstyle\frac{K}{2}})\textup{-twisted Gieseker (semi)stable sheaf.}

Proof.

The

where this was used.

Even though the

and

, there are only finitely many walls for the parameter

is
\sigma_{E,tH}
-(semi)stable. By the choice of

is
\sigma_{E,t^{\prime}H}
-(semi)stable for all

satisfies the assumption of
[

is a torsion sheaf supported in degree zero

15

15

This is because the other two conclusions in

.
. If

Since

is a torsion sheaf, it is inside the tilted heart

-(semi)stability of

Thus

(H,E-\frac{K}{2})
-twisted slope (semi)stable.

Conversely, assume that

is

-twisted slope (semi)stable. Let

is not

-(semi)stable. Let

-destabilizing subobject, i.e.,

Note that the cohomology sheaf

is zero because

. We divide into two cases;

and
\mathrm{rk}(G)=0

implies that

with respect to the

-twisted slope (semi)stability, contradicting the assumption.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

Consider the moduli stack
{\mathcal{M}}^{H-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}
of Gieseker
H
-semistable sheaves of type
v
. By Lemma
7.6
, we can identify this moduli stack as

{\mathcal{M}}^{H-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}={\mathcal{M}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}_{\textsf{v}}

for some geometric stability condition
\sigma
. When
\dim(\textsf{v})=1
,
\mu^{H}
-(semi)stability is equivalent to
\sigma_{(\frac{K}{2},tH)}
-(semi)stability for
t\gg 0
. Now let
\dim(\textsf{v})=2
. Pick some small enough
n
such that
(nH+\frac{K}{2})\cdot H<\mu^{H}(\textsf{v})
. Since
H
and

are linearly dependent,
(H,nH)
-twisted Gieseker (semi)stability is equivalent to the usual Gieseker (semi)stability with respect to

. On the other hand, the lemma says that
(H,nH)
-twisted Gieseker (semi)stability is equivalent to
\sigma_{nH+\frac{K}{2},tH}
- (semi)stability for some

.

The following Lemma will be useful later.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
S
be a surface with nef anticanonical surface. Let
\sigma_{E,H}
be a geometric stability condition and
\phi\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the abelian category
{\mathcal{A}}_{E,H}[\phi]
has homological dimension 2.

Proof.

Suppose that
F_{1},F_{2}
are objects in
{\mathcal{A}}_{E,H}[\phi]={\mathcal{P}}((\phi,\phi+1])
. It was proven in
[
Moz
, Theorem 7.7]
that
\mathrm{Hom}({\mathcal{P}}(\phi_{1}),{\mathcal{P}}(\phi_{2}))=0
if
\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}>2
. Since

is an object in
{\mathcal{P}}((\phi+k,\phi+k+1])
it follows that
\textup{Ext}^{k}(F_{1},F_{2})=\mathrm{Hom}(F_{1},F_{2}[k])=0
for

, showing that the homological dimension is at most 2. Since

belongs to
{\mathcal{A}}_{E,H}[\phi]
for some

and

, homological dimension is precisely
2
.
∎

7.2.3.
The ABCH program

Let
S
be a surface which admits (possibly many) full and strong exceptional collections, for instance any del Pezzo surfaces
[
BS
, Example 8.6]
.
16
16

16

Having geometric helix implies that we have a full and strong exceptional collection.
It was proposed in
[
ABCH
,
AM
]
that one could try to use the exceptional collections on
S
and corresponding quiver stability conditions to study moduli spaces with respect to geometric stability conditions. Note that the quiver stability conditions that we will consider are typically not geometric; for example
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(-1)[2]
appears in the exceptional collection in Example
7.3
but it is not in the heart of any geometric stability condition. However, in some cases the
{\mathbb{R}}
-action on the space of stability conditions can be used to relate geometric and quiver stability conditions.

Definition 7.9
.

Let
\sigma
be a geometric stability condition and
\textsf{v}\in K(S)
. We say that
{\mathcal{M}}_{\textsf{v}}^{\sigma-\textup{ss}}
admits a quiver description if there is another geometric stability condition
\sigma^{\prime}
and

such that
\sigma^{\prime}[\phi]
is a quiver stability condition and, for

in class

F\textup{ is }\sigma\textup{-(semi)stable}\,\Leftrightarrow\,F\textup{ is }\sigma^{\prime}\textup{-(semi)stable}\,.

In particular, when this happens the moduli stack

can be identified with a moduli stack of representations
{\mathcal{M}}^{\theta-\textup{ss}}_{d}(Q,I)
of some quiver with relations via the equivalence
D^{b}(S)\simeq D^{b}(Q,I)
composed with a twist
[k]
.

A careful study of the wall and chamber structure on the space of stability conditions and an identification of “quiver regions” associated to certain choices of exceptional collections allowed the authors to prove the following:

Theorem 7.10

(
[
ABCH
,
AM
]
)
.

Let
S={\mathbb{P}}^{2}
,
S={\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
or

. Then, for any geometric stability condition

and
\textsf{v}\in K(S)
,

admits a quiver description.

The authors in
[

conjecture that the same is true for any del Pezzo surfaces. We remark that in the case of

the only exceptional collections used are the ones obtained from tensoring by a line bundle Example

8.1.
Algebra of symmetric functions

We will now recall some basic definitions regarding the algebra of symmetric functions, following
[
Mac
]
. Let

\Lambda=\varprojlim_{n}{\mathbb{Q}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{S_{n}}

be the ring of symmetric functions in infinitely many variables, where the inverse limit is understood in the category of graded rings. The ring
\Lambda
can be described as the polynomial ring in infinitely many variables in a few different ways:

(1)

\Lambda={\mathbb{Q}}[e_{1},e_{2},\ldots]
where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th elementary symmetric function.

(2)

\Lambda={\mathbb{Q}}[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots]
where
p_{j}
is the
j
-th power sum.

(3)

\Lambda={\mathbb{Q}}[h_{1},h_{2},\ldots]
where
h_{j}
is the
j
-th complete symmetric function.

The different sets of generators can be written in terms of each other by Newton’s identities:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}e_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}p_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 0}h_{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}p_{k}\right)=\left(\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}e_{j}\right)^{-1}\,.

Above, we set
e_{0}=h_{0}=1
; the identities are understood in the completion of
\Lambda
with respect to the degree. Each set of generators defines a basis of
\Lambda
indexed by partitions
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})
:

e_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}e_{\lambda_{i}}\,,\quad p_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}p_{\lambda_{i}}\,,\quad h_{\lambda}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}h_{\lambda_{i}}\,.

Another natural basis indexed by partitions is
\{m_{\lambda}\}
where

m_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{\sigma}\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\sigma_{i}}

where the sum is over all the distinct permutations of
\lambda
.

The ring
\Lambda
admits a non-degenerate bilinear pairing
\langle-,-\rangle
, called the Hall inner product, that plays a particularly important role. This pairing is defined by setting

\langle p_{\lambda},p_{\mu}\rangle=\delta_{\lambda\mu}z_{\lambda}\,.

In the formula above

z_{\lambda}=\prod_{i\geq 1}i^{m_{i}}m_{i}!

where
m_{i}
is the number of times that
i
appears in
\lambda
. It can be checked that for every
n>0
the multiplication operator
p_{n}
and the annihilation operator
p_{-n}:=n\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{n}}
are adjoint with respect to the Hall pairing, i.e.,

\langle p_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle f,p_{-n}\,g\rangle\,.

Schur polynomials
\{s_{\lambda}\}
form yet another natural basis of
\Lambda
indexed by partitions. They can be defined by the following property:

Definition 8.1
.

The Schur polynomials
\{s_{\lambda}\}
are the unique orthonormal basis of
\Lambda
with respect to
\langle-,-\rangle
such that

s_{\lambda}=m_{\lambda}+\sum_{\mu<\lambda}a_{\lambda\mu}m_{\mu}

for some
a_{\lambda\mu}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
where the sum runs over partitions
\mu
which are smaller than
\lambda
in the lexicographic order.

There are several different ways to define Schur polynomials more explicitly. For example they can be written in terms of the complete symmetric functions
h_{i}
by a determinant formula:

s_{\lambda}=\det\big(h_{\lambda_i-i+j}\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}\,.

Another possible definition of Schur polynomials is presented in Proposition
8.6
.

The ring
\Lambda
admits an involution
\sigma\colon\Lambda\to\Lambda
defined by
\sigma(p_{j})=(-1)^{j-1}p_{j}
. This involution sends
s_{\lambda}
to
s_{\lambda^{t}}
where
\lambda^{t}
is the conjugate partition.

Example 8.2
.

Elementary symmetric functions and complete symmetric functions are both easily written as Schur functions:

e_{j}=s_{(1)^{j}}\,,\quad h_{j}=s_{(j)}\,.

We also have

s_{(2,2)}=\frac{1}{12}p_{1}^{4}+\frac{1}{4}p_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{3}p_{1}p_{3}\,.

8.5.
Hecke operators

We define Hecke operators
H_{n}\colon\Lambda\to\Lambda
as the following vertex operators:

H(z)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}H_{n}z^{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{j>0}\frac{p_{j}}{j}z^{j}\right)\exp\left(-\sum_{j>0}\frac{p_{-j}}{j}z^{-j}\right)\,.

This corresponds to the field

of the lattice vertex algebra associated to
({\mathbb{Z}},1)
via isomorphism
\textup{VA}({\mathbb{Z}},1)\simeq\Lambda_{*}[q^{\pm 1}]

H_{n}=\sum_{j\geq 0}(-1)^{j}h_{j+n}\circ e_{j}^{\perp}

where

denotes the adjoint operator with respect to the Hall pairing. Such operators have been studied in depth from the point of view of the combinatorics of

[
Jin

Proposition 8.6

(
[

]
)
.

The Hecke operators satisfy the following properties:

(1)

We have

[H_{n},p_{m}]=-H_{n+m}\,,\quad\textup{for every }n\in{\mathbb{Z}},\,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\}\,.

(2)

The adjoint to a Hecke operator is given by

H_{n}^{\perp}=(-1)^{n}\sigma\circ H_{-n}\circ\sigma\,.

(3)

The Hecke operators satisfy the commuting relations

H_{n}H_{m}=-H_{m-1}H_{n+1}\,.

In particular
H_{n}H_{n+1}=0
.

(4)

The Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}
can be given in terms of Hecke operators by

s_{\lambda}=H_{\lambda_{1}}H_{\lambda_{2}}\ldots H_{\lambda_{\ell}}1.

Proof.

Parts (2), (3) and (4) are (2.5), (2.12) and (3.9) in
[
Jin
]
in the limit

. Part (1) is the defining property of vertex operators, see for example (5.4.3b) in
[

]
.∎

of (not necessarily positive) integers. The commuting relations (3) can be used to reduce the Schur polynomial associated to any tuple to the Schur polynomial of a partitions.

8.6.

Note that the Hecke operators used in the previous section appear naturally as fields associated to the state

8.7.
Geometricity of Virasoro and Calogero-Sutherland operator

.

We showed in Theorem
4.10
that the Virasoro constraints imply that the operators
\mathsf{R}_{n}
descend to cohomology or, equivalently, they preserve the ideal of relations. We now illustrate this phenomena in the case of the Grassmannian; it turns out that showing geometricity directly in examples is often much easier than showing the full constraints.

The derivation
\mathsf{R}_{n}
is a priori defined in the descendent algebra
\Lambda^{\ast}
. The ideal of relations of the Grassmannian is the ideal
I
generated by
e_{k+1},e_{k+2},\ldots
and
h_{N-k+1},h_{N-k+2},\ldots
. Equivalently,
I
is the linear span of the Schur polynomials
s_{\lambda}
with
\lambda
not contained in
(N-k)^{k}
. We now compute the action of
\mathsf{R}_{n}
on the generator
e_{j}
, for
j>k
, of the ideal. Denote by
e_{\bullet}
the formal sum
\sum_{j\geq 0}e_{j}
. We have

\mathsf{R}_{n}e_{\bullet}=\mathsf{R}_{n}\exp\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}(-1)^{\ell-1}\frac{p_{\ell}}{\ell}\right)=\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 1}(-1)^{\ell-1}p_{\ell+n}\right)e_{\bullet}\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}_{n}e_{j}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{j}(-1)^{\ell-1}p_{n+\ell}e_{j-\ell}=(-1)^{n}(j+n)e_{j+n}+\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{s}e_{j+s}p_{n-s}

by Newton’s identity, so we conclude that
\mathsf{R}_{n}(e_{j})\in I
for
j>k
. A similar computation shows the same for the remaining generators.

8.7.1.
Calogero-Sutherland operator

The geometricity of the Virasoro operators can be explained at once by the geometricity of the Calogero-Sutherland operator (also Laplace-Beltrami operator in the literature). Define the operator
\Delta\colon\Lambda^{\ast}\to\Lambda^{\ast}
by

(37)

\Delta=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{a+b+c=0}\colon p_{a}p_{b}p_{c}\colon=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{a,b>0}p_{a}p_{b}p_{-a-b}+p_{a+b}p_{-a}p_{-b}\right)\,.

It is straightforward to show that

[p_{n},\Delta]=n\left(\sum_{a>0}p_{a+n}p_{-a}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b>0\\
a+b=n\end{subarray}}p_{a}p_{b}\right)\colon\,.

The right hand side is
n
times
\mathsf{R}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathsf{T}}_{n}
, which are the Virasoro operators associated to the vertex algebra
\textup{VA}({\mathbb{Z}},1)
. Since
p_{n}
descends to cohomology for
n\geq 1
(as multiplication by
n!\mathrm{ch}_{n}({\mathbb{F}})
), the operator
\Delta
descending to cohomology implies that
\mathsf{R}_{n}
also does. Indeed, this is true:

Proposition 8.13
.

The operator
\Delta
on
\Lambda^{\ast}
descends to an operator on
H^{\ast}(\mathsf{Gr}(k,N))
.

Proof.

It is well-known that Schur polynomials are a basis of eigenvectors of
\Delta
, see e.g.
[
FW
, Proposition 2]
or
[
SV
, Remark 1.11]
. For instance,

\Delta e_{j}=-\frac{j(j-1)}{2}e_{j}\textup{ and }\Delta h_{j}=\frac{j(j-1)}{2}h_{j}\,.

It follows immediately that
\Delta
preserves the ideal of relations
I
.∎

Remark 8.14
.

In
