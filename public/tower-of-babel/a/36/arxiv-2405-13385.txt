Minimal finite models of wedges of spheres with at most eight points Thanks: Corresponding author: Sainkupar Marwein Mawiong.
Ponaki Das, Sainkupar Marwein Mawiong

Abstract
. A minimal finite model of a topological space is a finite T 0 T_{0} -space of least cardinality that is weak homotopy equivalent to it. We determine, up to homeomorphism, the minimal finite models of eight wedges of one- and two-dimensional spheres. The spaces S 2 ∨ S 1 S^{2}\vee S^{1} and S 2 ∨ S 2 S^{2}\vee S^{2} have exactly two and three minimal finite models, respectively, all with seven points, whereas S 1 ∨ S 1 ∨ S 2 S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2} has exactly seven minimal finite models, S 1 ∨ S 1 ∨ S 1 ∨ S 2 S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2} has one, S 1 ∨ S 2 ∨ S 2 S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2} has six, S 2 ∨ S 2 ∨ S 2 S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2} has five, S 2 ∨ S 2 ∨ S 2 ∨ S 2 S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2} has three, and S 1 ∨ S 1 ∨ S 2 ∨ S 2 S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2} has two, all with eight points. To prove this we classify the connected finite T 0 T_{0} -spaces of height 2 2 without beat points that have seven or eight points, and we identify their weak homotopy types by means of McCord’s theorem and explicit collapse and suspension arguments for their two-dimensional order complexes. Together with the known results for spheres and wedges of circles, this determines, up to weak homotopy equivalence, all path-connected spaces whose minimal finite models have at most eight points.

Introduction

Given a topological space, how few points can a finite space with the same weak homotopy type have? Barmak and Minian introduced the notion of a
minimal finite model
to make this question precise
[
2
]

[

Definition 2.1

(Minimal finite model
[
1
, Def. 3.1.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a topological space.
A finite
T_{0}
-space
Y
is a
finite model
of
X
if
Y
is weak homotopy equivalent to
X
.
It is a
minimal finite model
of
X
if
Y
has the smallest possible cardinality among all such models.

Here a continuous map
f\colon Z\to W
is a
weak homotopy equivalence
if it induces a bijection
f_{\ast}\colon\pi_{0}(Z)\to\pi_{0}(W)
and isomorphisms
f_{\ast}\colon\pi_{n}(Z,z_{0})\to\pi_{n}(W,f(z_{0}))
for all

and every base point
z_{0}\in Z
. We write
Z\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}W
when
Z
and
W
are joined by a finite zig-zag of weak homotopy equivalences, and
Z\simeq W
when they are homotopy equivalent.

Remark 2.2

(Homology of wedges of spheres)
.

A weak homotopy equivalence induces isomorphisms in singular homology
[
8
, Prop. 4.21]
, so the Betti numbers
\beta_{i}=\operatorname{rank}H_{i}
are invariants of
\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}
. If
W=S^{n_{1}}\vee\cdots\vee S^{n_{r}}
with all

, then for every

is free abelian of rank

[

, Cor. 2.25]
. Consequently, two such wedges are weak homotopy equivalent if and only if they have the same number of spheres of each dimension. In particular,
(\bigvee_{r}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{s}S^{2})
has
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(r,s)
: the eight wedges of Theorem

(0,2)

(2,1)

(0,3)
,

and

and

have

and
(k,0)
, respectively.

2.1.
Finite spaces, posets and cores

A finite
T_{0}
-space
X
carries the
specialisation order
:
x\leq y
if and only if every open set that contains
y
also contains
x
. This gives a bijective correspondence between finite
T_{0}
-spaces and finite posets, under which the continuous maps are exactly the order-preserving maps and the homeomorphisms are exactly the order isomorphisms
[
1
, Chap. 1]
. We freely identify a finite
T_{0}
-space with its poset, and for a finite set
S
we write
\#S
or
|S|
for its cardinality. For a point
x
of a finite
T_{0}
-space
X
we write

\displaystyle U_{x}=\{\,y\in X\mid y\leq x\,\},\qquad F_{x}=\{\,y\in X\mid y\geq x\,\},

\displaystyle\hat{U}_{x}=U_{x}\setminus\{x\},\qquad\hat{F}_{x}=F_{x}\setminus\{x\};

thus
U_{x}
is the minimal open set containing
x
and
F_{x}
is the closure of
\{x\}
. We write
x<y
when
x\leq y
and
x\neq y
, and
x\prec y
, read “
y
covers
x
”, when
x<y
and no point
z
satisfies
x<z<y
. The
Hasse diagram

\mathcal{H}(X)
is the graph with vertex set
X
whose edges, forming the set
E(\mathcal{H}(X))
, are the covering pairs
x\prec y
. Two points are
incomparable
if neither lies below the other, and an
antichain
is a set of pairwise incomparable points. We write
\mathrm{mxl}(X)
and
\mathrm{mnl}(X)
for the sets of maximal and minimal points of
X
, and

B_{X}=X\setminus\big(\mathrm{mxl}(X)\cup\mathrm{mnl}(X)\big)

for the set of
intermediate
points, those that are neither maximal nor minimal.

The simplicial complex whose simplices are the non-empty chains of
X
is called the
order complex

\mathcal{K}(X)
; in the other direction, the
face poset

\mathcal{X}(K)
of a simplicial or regular CW complex
K
is the set of its cells ordered by the face relation (
e\leq e^{\prime}
if and only if
e\subseteq\overline{e^{\prime}}
). The
length
of a chain
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}
is
k
, the number of strict inequalities in it, and the
height

\mathrm{h}(X)
is the maximum length of a chain in

; hence
\mathrm{h}(X)=\dim\mathcal{K}(X)
. The
opposite
(or
dual
) poset

has the same underlying set with the reversed order. Since
X
and
X^{\mathrm{op}}
have the same chains,
\mathcal{K}(X^{\mathrm{op}})=\mathcal{K}(X)
.

The
non-Hausdorff join

X\circledast Y
of finite
T_{0}
-spaces
X
and
Y
is their disjoint union, with
x<y
for every
x\in X
and
y\in Y
and the given orders on
X
and
Y
. The

of

is
\mathbb{S}(X)=X\circledast S^{0}
, where
S^{0}
denotes the two-point antichain
[
1
, Def. 2.7.1]
.

A point

is a

if
\hat{U}_{x}
has a maximum, and an

if
\hat{F}_{x}
has a minimum; a

is a point that is either
[
1
, Def. 1.3.3]
. A finite
T_{0}
-space without beat points is called a
minimal finite space
, and such a space is also said to
be a core
. In addition, we use the following conventions.

(1)

If
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
, a covering pair

with

and

is called a

. Equivalently,
c<a
and no intermediate point lies between

and

.

(2)

For a vertex

of
\mathcal{K}(X)
, the

of

\overline{\mathrm{st}}(v)=\mathcal{K}(U_{v}\cup F_{v})

(3)

\mathbb{S}^{h}(S^{0})=\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{S}(\cdots\mathbb{S}(S^{0})\cdots))
denotes the

. It is a core with

Remark 2.3
.

The terms
minimal finite space
(a core) and
minimal finite model
(Definition

[
1

is a minimal finite model.

Let

(2)

Remark 2.9
.

A connected finite
T_{0}
-space with a single maximal point has a maximum and is therefore contractible (the identity map is comparable to, and hence homotopic to, the constant map at that maximum), and dually for a single minimal point
[
1

. Hence every non-contractible connected finite
T_{0}
-space, and in particular every core with more than one point, has at least two maximal and at least two minimal points.

Remark 2.10
.

For a finite poset
X
we have
\mathrm{h}(X)=\dim\mathcal{K}(X)
, so a finite model of a space with nontrivial second homology has height at least
2
. A finite model may have height greater than

has at least

of

(Theorem

is not such a wedge.

To analyse the height-

[

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
X
be a finite
T_{0}
-space of height
2
that is a core, and let

. Then

\#(\hat{F}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X))\geq 2\qquad\text{and}\qquad\#(\hat{U}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X))\geq 2.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
, every element strictly above

is maximal, so
\hat{F}_{b}\subseteq\mathrm{mxl}(X)
. If
\#\hat{F}_{b}\leq 1
, then either
\hat{F}_{b}=\varnothing
, so that

is maximal, contradicting

; or
\hat{F}_{b}=\{a\}
has a minimum, making

an up beat point and contradicting that

\#(\hat{F}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X))\geq 2
, and dually
\#(\hat{U}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X))\geq 2
.

. Throughout, whether a poset is a core is decided by the combinatorial criterion of Lemma
2.14
, which makes no reference to homotopy type. The weak homotopy type of a core is then identified by explicit structural lemmas: Lemma
2.13
for one intermediate point, Lemma
2.17
for complete tripartite posets, Lemma
2.18
for at most two maximal, at most two minimal or exactly two intermediate points, and Lemmas
2.19
and
2.20
for two maximal or two intermediate points. We do not invoke the classical fact that a connected two-dimensional CW complex with free fundamental group is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres (see
[
10
]
): every wedge decomposition below comes from an explicit collapse or suspension structure. By Theorem
2.4
(2),
X
and
X^{\mathrm{op}}
always have the same weak homotopy type, so it suffices to treat one representative of each dual pair.

The following observation removes the single-intermediate-point case from all that follows.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
X
be a connected finite
T_{0}
-space of height
2
with
\#B_{X}=1
. Then
\mathcal{K}(X)
collapses to a graph; consequently
H_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))=0
and
X
is weak homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles. In particular no such
X
is a finite model of a space with nontrivial second homology.

Proof.

Write
B_{X}=\{b\}
. Since
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
, every
2
-simplex of
\mathcal{K}(X)
is a chain
c<b^{\prime}<a
with
b^{\prime}\in B_{X}
, so
b^{\prime}=b
. Thus every triangle has the form
\{c,b,a\}
with
c\in\hat{U}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X)
and
a\in\hat{F}_{b}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X)
. For such a triangle, the edge
\{c,a\}
lies in no other triangle, since any triangle containing both
c
and
a
would be
\{c,b^{\prime},a\}
with
b^{\prime}\in B_{X}
, forcing
b^{\prime}=b
. Hence each
\{c,a\}
is a free face. Distinct triangles have distinct free faces, since the pair
(c,a)
determines the triangle
\{c,b,a\}
; so the elementary collapses
(\{c,a\},\{c,b,a\})
involve pairwise distinct simplices and may be performed in any order. Doing so reduces
\mathcal{K}(X)
to a subcomplex of its
1
-skeleton, that is, to a graph
G
. Collapses preserve homotopy type, so
\mathcal{K}(X)\simeq G
, and
G
is connected because
X
is. As
G
is a connected graph,
\mathcal{K}(X)\simeq\bigvee_{r}S^{1}
with
r=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(G)=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(\mathcal{K}(X))
; in particular
H_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))=0
.
∎

The next lemma converts “
X
is a core” into a purely combinatorial condition on covers; it is used throughout the classification in place of case-by-case beat-point checks. Here an
immediate predecessor
of
x
is a point
y
with
y\prec x
, and an
immediate successor
of
x
is a point
y
with
x\prec y
.

Lemma 2.14

(Core criterion)
.

A connected finite
T_{0}
-space
X
of height
2
is a core if and only if no point has exactly one immediate predecessor and no point has exactly one immediate successor. Equivalently, with the levels
\mathrm{mnl}(X)
,
B_{X}
,
\mathrm{mxl}(X)
:

(1)

every
b\in B_{X}
covers at least two minimal points and is covered by at least two maximal points;

(2)

every minimal point has at least two immediate successors (intermediate points above it, or maximal points covering it directly);

(3)

every maximal point has at least two immediate predecessors (intermediate points below it, or minimal points that it covers directly).

Proof.

By
[
1
, Def. 1.3.3]
, a point
x
is a down beat point if and only if it covers exactly one point, and an up beat point if and only if it is covered by exactly one point. Since
X
is a core if and only if it has no beat points, this gives the first formulation. For the second, note that the relevant sides are never empty. A minimal point has no predecessors and, since
X
is connected with more than one point, at least one successor; so the condition on it is exactly (2). Dually, the condition on a maximal point is (3). An intermediate point
b
has a minimal point below it and a maximal point above it, and, as
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
, the points below
b
are minimal and those above
b
are maximal. So the immediate predecessors of
b
are the minimal points below it, its immediate successors are the maximal points above it, and the condition on
b
is (1). Finally, the immediate successors of a minimal point
c
are the intermediate points above
c
(nothing lies strictly between them) together with the maximal points covering
c
directly, and dually for maximal points.
∎

The next two lemmas isolate the homotopy-theoretic steps used repeatedly below: the union of two cones along a graph is a suspension, and attaching cells of dimension at most one to a disjoint union of wedges of circles and
2
-spheres yields again such a wedge.

Lemma 2.15

(Union of two cones)
.

Let
K
be a connected finite CW complex that is the union of two contractible subcomplexes
Q_{1},Q_{2}
whose intersection
L=Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}
has dimension at most one. Let
\kappa
be the number of connected components of
L
and
\ell=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(L)
. Then
L\neq\varnothing
and

K\;\simeq\;\Sigma L\;\simeq\;\Big(\bigvee_{\kappa-1}S^{1}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{\ell}S^{2}\Big),

where
\Sigma L
denotes the unreduced suspension of
L
.

Proof.

If
L
were empty,
K
would be the disjoint union of the non-empty sets
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, contradicting connectedness; so
L\neq\varnothing
.

Step 1:
K\simeq\Sigma L
.
Since
Q_{1}
is a contractible subcomplex, the quotient map
K\to K/Q_{1}
is a homotopy equivalence
[
8
, Prop. 0.17]
; and
K/Q_{1}
is homeomorphic to
Q_{2}/L
, because
K\setminus Q_{1}=Q_{2}\setminus L
makes the induced map
Q_{2}/L\to K/Q_{1}
a continuous bijection between compact Hausdorff spaces. Let
Q_{2}\cup CL
be obtained from
Q_{2}
by attaching the cone on
L
along the inclusion
L\hookrightarrow Q_{2}
. Collapsing the contractible subcomplex
CL
gives
Q_{2}\cup CL\simeq Q_{2}/L

[
8
, Prop. 0.17]
, while the attaching map is null-homotopic because
Q_{2}
is contractible, so that

Q_{2}\cup CL\;\simeq\;Q_{2}\vee(CL/L)\;=\;Q_{2}\vee\Sigma L\;\simeq\;\Sigma L

by
[
8
, Prop. 0.18]
, the last step collapsing the contractible subcomplex
Q_{2}
. Therefore
K\simeq\Sigma L
.

Step 2: the suspension of a graph.
Collapsing a spanning tree in each component is a homotopy equivalence
[
8
, Prop. 0.17]
from
L
onto a disjoint union
L^{\prime}
of
\kappa
wedges of circles with
\ell
circles in total, so
\Sigma L\simeq\Sigma L^{\prime}
. Give
\Sigma L^{\prime}
its product cell structure: two
0
-cells (the suspension points) and one
(k+1)
-cell for each
k
-cell of
L^{\prime}
. Its
1
-skeleton
\Gamma
is a graph with two vertices and
\kappa
edges, so
\Gamma\simeq\bigvee_{\kappa-1}S^{1}
; and each of the
\ell
two-cells is attached along a loop of the form
\gamma\bar{\gamma}
with
\gamma
one of these edges, which is null-homotopic in
\Gamma
. Attaching
2
-cells along null-homotopic maps is, up to homotopy, the same as wedging on
2
-spheres
[
8
, Prop. 0.18]
, so
\Sigma L\simeq\Gamma\vee\bigvee_{\ell}S^{2}\simeq(\bigvee_{\kappa-1}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{\ell}S^{2})
.
∎

Lemma 2.16

(Attaching cells of dimension
\leq 1
)
.

Let
W
be a CW complex with finitely many connected components
W_{1},\dots,W_{r}
, each homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres, and let
K
be a connected CW complex obtained from
W
by adjoining finitely many cells of dimension at most
1
. Then

K\;\simeq\;\Big(\bigvee_{j=1}^{r}W_{j}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{t}S^{1}\Big)

for some
t\geq 0
. In particular
K
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres,
\pi_{1}(K)
is free, and
H_{2}(K)\cong\bigoplus_{j}H_{2}(W_{j})
.

Proof.

Adjoining the
0
-cells first gives
W^{+}=W\sqcup\{\text{adjoined }0\text{-cells}\}
, every component of which is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres (a point being the empty such wedge). The remaining cells are
1
-cells
e_{1},\dots,e_{N}
, and we show by induction on
k
that every component of
Z_{k}:=W^{+}\cup e_{1}\cup\dots\cup e_{k}
has this form. Suppose this holds for
Z_{k-1}
and attach
e_{k}
along its endpoints
u,v
. If
u,v
lie in the same component
Y
, then the attaching map
S^{0}\to Y
is homotopic to a constant map, so
Y\cup e_{k}\simeq Y\vee S^{1}

[
8
, Prop. 0.18]
; if they lie in distinct components
Y_{1},Y_{2}
, then collapsing the contractible arc
\bar{e}_{k}
gives
Y_{1}\cup e_{k}\cup Y_{2}\simeq Y_{1}\vee Y_{2}

[
8
, Prop. 0.17]
. In either case the result is again a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres, which completes the induction; as
K=Z_{N}
is connected, it is a single such wedge.

No cell of dimension
\geq 2
was attached, so
H_{2}(K)\cong H_{2}(W^{+})\cong\bigoplus_{j}H_{2}(W_{j})
and
H_{1}(K)
is free. Set
t=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(K)-\sum_{j}\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(W_{j})
, which is
\geq 0
because attaching
0
- and
1
-cells never lowers the rank of
H_{1}
. A wedge of circles and
2
-spheres is determined up to homotopy by the ranks of
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, so comparing these ranks gives the stated decomposition.
∎

Lemma 2.17

(Complete tripartite cores)
.

Let
T(p,m,q)
be the height-
2
poset on disjoint level sets of sizes
p
(maximal),
m
(intermediate) and
q
(minimal), in which every minimal point is below every intermediate point and every intermediate point is below every maximal point (so that no minimal point is covered by a maximal point). Then
T(p,m,q)
is a core if and only if
p,m,q\geq 2
, and in that case

|\mathcal{K}(T(p,m,q))|\;\simeq\;\bigvee_{(p-1)(m-1)(q-1)}S^{2}.

Proof.

Each intermediate point covers all
q
minimal points and is covered by all
p
maximal points, each minimal point is covered exactly by the
m
intermediate points, and each maximal point covers exactly the
m
intermediate points; so by Lemma
2.14
the poset is a core if and only if
p,m,q\geq 2
. Assume this.

A subset of
T(p,m,q)
is a chain if and only if it meets each level in at most one point. Hence
K:=\mathcal{K}(T(p,m,q))
is the join
D_{p}*G
of the discrete set
D_{p}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\}=\mathrm{mxl}(T(p,m,q))
with the order complex
G
of the two lower levels
[
1
, Rem. 2.7.2]
; here
G
is the complete bipartite graph
K_{m,q}
, which is connected with
\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(G)=mq-m-q+1=(m-1)(q-1)
.

Put
K_{j}=\bigcup_{i\leq j}x_{i}*G
. For
1\leq j<p
we have
K_{j+1}=K_{j}\cup(x_{j+1}*G)
, the two pieces meet exactly in
G
, and
x_{j+1}*G
is a cone on
G
; thus
K_{j+1}
is the mapping cone of the inclusion
G\hookrightarrow K_{j}
. That inclusion is null-homotopic, since it factors through the cone
x_{1}*G
, so

K_{j+1}\;\simeq\;K_{j}\vee\big((x_{j+1}*G)/G\big)\;=\;K_{j}\vee\Sigma G

by
[
8
, Prop. 0.18]
. Since
K_{1}
is a cone, hence contractible, induction on
j
gives
K\simeq\bigvee_{p-1}\Sigma G
. Finally
\Sigma G\simeq\bigvee_{(m-1)(q-1)}S^{2}
by Step 2 of the proof of Lemma
2.15
, so
K\simeq\bigvee_{(p-1)(m-1)(q-1)}S^{2}
.
∎

For the eight-point classification we repeatedly meet cores with exactly two maximal points or exactly two intermediate points. The following lemmas dispose of all such cores; by order-duality they apply equally to cores with exactly two minimal points.

Lemma 2.18

(Free fundamental group)
.

Let
X
be a connected finite
T_{0}
-space of height
2
. If
X
has at most two maximal points, or at most two minimal points, or exactly two intermediate points, then
\mathcal{K}(X)\simeq(\bigvee_{\beta_{1}}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{\beta_{2}}S^{2})
with
\beta_{i}=\operatorname{rank}H_{i}(\mathcal{K}(X))
; in particular
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{K}(X))
is free and
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}(\bigvee_{\beta_{1}}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{\beta_{2}}S^{2})
.

Proof.

Let
K=\mathcal{K}(X)
. If
X
has only one maximal point, then
X
has a maximum and is contractible, and the case of at most two minimal points is dual. Suppose first that
X
has exactly two maximal points
a_{1},a_{2}
. Every simplex of
K
lies in a maximal chain ending at
a_{1}
or at
a_{2}
, so
K=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(a_{1})\cup\overline{\mathrm{st}}(a_{2})
is a union of two cones. Their intersection
L=\mathcal{K}(\hat{U}_{a_{1}}\cap\hat{U}_{a_{2}})
is the order complex of a subposet of
X\setminus\mathrm{mxl}(X)
, which has height at most
1
; hence
L
is a graph. By Lemma
2.15
, with
\kappa
the number of components of
L
and
\ell=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(L)
,

K\simeq\Big(\bigvee_{\kappa-1}S^{1}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{\ell}S^{2}\Big).

In particular
\pi_{1}(K)
is free,
\beta_{1}=\kappa-1
and
\beta_{2}=\ell
, so
K\simeq(\bigvee_{\beta_{1}}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{\beta_{2}}S^{2})
.

Now suppose
X
has exactly two intermediate points
b_{1},b_{2}
; they are incomparable, since
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
. Every
2
-simplex of
K
is a chain through
b_{1}
or
b_{2}
, hence lies in
W=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{1})\cup\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{2})
, a union of two contractible cones. Their intersection
W^{\prime}=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{1})\cap\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{2})
is the order complex of the set of points comparable with both
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
. These are the minimal points below both and the maximal points above both, and each such minimal point lies below each such maximal point (through
b_{1}
); so
W^{\prime}
is a complete bipartite graph, possibly with one side empty. If
W^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, then
W
is connected and Lemma
2.15
gives
W\simeq(\bigvee_{\kappa^{\prime}-1}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{\ell^{\prime}}S^{2})
for suitable
\kappa^{\prime}
and
\ell^{\prime}
. If
W^{\prime}=\varnothing
, then
W=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{1})\sqcup\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{2})
is a disjoint union of two contractible complexes. Either way each component of
W
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles and
2
-spheres. The complex
K
is obtained from
W
by adjoining the remaining simplices, all of dimension
\leq 1
: the minimal points below no intermediate point, the maximal points above no intermediate point, and the edges
\{c,a\}
given by direct covers
c\prec a
. Since
K
is connected, Lemma
2.16
gives

K\simeq\Big(\bigvee_{\beta_{1}}S^{1}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{\beta_{2}}S^{2}\Big),\qquad\beta_{i}=\operatorname{rank}H_{i}(K).

In particular
\pi_{1}(K)
is free.
∎

Lemma 2.19

(Two-maximal-point Betti numbers)
.

Let
X
be a connected height-
2
core with exactly two maximal points,
m
intermediate points and
q
minimal points. By Lemma
2.11
, every intermediate point lies below both maximal points. Call a minimal point
inner
if it lies below some intermediate point, and
outer
otherwise. Let
s
be the number of inner minimal points, and let
G
be the bipartite graph on the inner minimal points and the intermediate points with an edge for each cover relation; let
E
be its number of edges and
\kappa
its number of connected components. Then every outer minimal point is covered by both maximal points,
G
has minimal degree at least
2
on both sides, and

\beta_{1}(\mathcal{K}(X))=q-s-1+\kappa,\qquad\beta_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))=E-s-m+\kappa.

Hence
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}(\bigvee_{q-s-1+\kappa}S^{1})\vee(\bigvee_{E-s-m+\kappa}S^{2})
.

Proof.

An inner minimal point lies below some intermediate point
b
, hence below both maximal points through
b
, so it is covered by no maximal point. By Lemma
2.14
(2) it has at least two intermediate covers, that is, degree
\geq 2
in
G
. An outer minimal point lies below no intermediate point, so all its immediate successors are maximal; by Lemma
2.14
(2) it is covered by at least two maximal points, that is, by both. Each intermediate point covers at least two minimal points (Lemma
2.14
(1)), all of them inner, so
G
has minimal degree
\geq 2
on both sides.

In particular every point of
X\setminus\mathrm{mxl}(X)
lies below both maximal points
a_{1},a_{2}
. As in the proof of Lemma
2.18
,
\mathcal{K}(X)=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(a_{1})\cup\overline{\mathrm{st}}(a_{2})
, and the two cones intersect in
L=\mathcal{K}(\hat{U}_{a_{1}}\cap\hat{U}_{a_{2}})=\mathcal{K}(X\setminus\mathrm{mxl}(X))
. This is the graph
G
together with the
q-s
outer minimal points as isolated vertices, so
L
has
\kappa+q-s
connected components and
\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(L)=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(G)=E-(s+m)+\kappa
. Lemma
2.15
now gives

\mathcal{K}(X)\simeq\Big(\bigvee_{q-s-1+\kappa}S^{1}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{E-s-m+\kappa}S^{2}\Big),

which proves both formulas and the last assertion.
∎

A parallel analysis gives an equally explicit formula for the remaining case, that of a core with exactly two
intermediate
points
b_{1},b_{2}
. For such a core write
C_{i}=\hat{U}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X)
and
A_{i}=\hat{F}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X)
for
i=1,2
, and let
\delta
be the number of direct covers.

Lemma 2.20

(Two-intermediate-point Betti numbers)
.

Let
X
be a connected height-
2
core with exactly two intermediate points
b_{1},b_{2}
, with
p
maximal and
q
minimal points, and suppose that
C_{1}\cap C_{2}\neq\varnothing
and
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\neq\varnothing
. (This is automatic when
p,q\leq 3
: since
|C_{i}|,|A_{i}|\geq 2
by Lemma
2.11
, we get
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\geq|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|-q\geq 1
, and likewise for the
A_{i}
.) Then

\displaystyle\beta_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))

\displaystyle=(|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|-1)(|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-1),

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(\mathcal{K}(X))

\displaystyle=|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|+|A_{1}\cup A_{2}|+\delta-p-q,

and

X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}\Big(\bigvee_{\beta_{1}}S^{1}\Big)\vee\Big(\bigvee_{\beta_{2}}S^{2}\Big).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.18
, let
W=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{1})\cup\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{2})
; it contains every
2
-simplex of
\mathcal{K}(X)
, and
\mathcal{K}(X)
is obtained from
W
by adjoining cells of dimension at most
1
. The intersection
W^{\prime}=\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{1})\cap\overline{\mathrm{st}}(b_{2})
is the complete bipartite graph with parts
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
and
A_{1}\cap A_{2}
, both non-empty by hypothesis. It is connected, and
\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(W^{\prime})=(|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|-1)(|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-1)
. By Lemma
2.15
,
W\simeq\bigvee_{(|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|-1)(|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-1)}S^{2}
, and by Lemma
2.16
,
H_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))\cong H_{2}(W)
. This gives the formula for
\beta_{2}
.

For
\beta_{1}
, count cells. The complex
\mathcal{K}(X)
has
p+q+2
vertices and
|C_{1}||A_{1}|+|C_{2}||A_{2}|
triangles. Its edges are:
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|
edges from minimal to intermediate points;
|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|
edges from intermediate to maximal points; and
|R|+\delta
edges from minimal to maximal points, where

R=\{(c,a):\ c\in C_{i}\text{ and }a\in A_{i}\text{ for some }i\}=(C_{1}\times A_{1})\cup(C_{2}\times A_{2})

has
|R|=|C_{1}||A_{1}|+|C_{2}||A_{2}|-|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\,|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|
. Hence

\chi(\mathcal{K}(X))=(p+q+2)-\big(|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|\big)-\big(|A_{1}|+|A_{2}|\big)+|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|\,|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-\delta.

Since
\beta_{0}=1
, and using
|C_{1}|+|C_{2}|=|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|+|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|
and the same identity for the
A_{i}
, we obtain

\beta_{1}=1-\chi(\mathcal{K}(X))+\beta_{2}=|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|+|A_{1}\cup A_{2}|+\delta-p-q.

The weak homotopy type follows from Lemma
2.18
(exactly two intermediate points).
∎

We close the preliminaries with an elementary reconstruction principle, used in the eight-point classification to recast the case analysis as a classification of symmetry orbits of combinatorial data.

Lemma 2.21

(Determination principle)
.

Let
X
be a finite
T_{0}
-space of height
2
with level sets
\mathrm{mxl}(X)
,
B_{X}=\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}
and
\mathrm{mnl}(X)
. For each
i
set
C_{i}=\hat{U}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X)
and
A_{i}=\hat{F}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X)
, and let
\Delta\subseteq\mathrm{mnl}(X)\times\mathrm{mxl}(X)
be the set of direct covers
c\prec a
. Then the order of
X
is recovered from the datum
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m},A_{1},\dots,A_{m},\Delta)
by

\displaystyle c<b_{i}

\displaystyle\iff c\in C_{i},\qquad b_{i}<a\iff a\in A_{i},

\displaystyle c<a

\displaystyle\iff\big(c\in C_{i}\text{ and }a\in A_{i}\text{ for some }i\big)\ \text{or}\ (c,a)\in\Delta,

and points of the same level are incomparable. Conversely, for any datum these relations define a partial order of height at most
2
. Its level sets are the given ones provided each
C_{i}
and each
A_{i}
is non-empty and every designated minimal (resp. maximal) point lies below (resp. above) at least one point; the second condition holds in particular whenever the resulting space is connected and has more than one point. Its set of direct covers is
\Delta
provided
\Delta\cap\bigcup_{i}(C_{i}\times A_{i})=\varnothing
. Two such spaces are homeomorphic if and only if one datum is carried onto the other by a level-preserving bijection, that is, by an element of
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathrm{mxl}(X))\times\mathrm{Sym}(B_{X})\times\mathrm{Sym}(\mathrm{mnl}(X))
.

Proof.

The displayed equivalences specify every comparability between points of different levels, and points of the same level are incomparable; this is exactly the order of
X
. Conversely, for any datum the displayed relation is irreflexive and transitive (a composite
c<b_{i}<a
is included by construction), so it is a strict partial order in which every chain has at most three points. Its minimal, intermediate and maximal points are the designated ones under the stated non-emptiness conditions. A pair
(c,a)\in\Delta
is a cover unless
c<b_{i}<a
for some
i
, that is, unless
(c,a)\in\bigcup_{i}(C_{i}\times A_{i})
, which proves the statement about direct covers. Finally, a homeomorphism of finite
T_{0}
-spaces is an order-isomorphism. The three levels are defined order-theoretically (minimal points, maximal points and the rest), so every order-isomorphism preserves them and restricts to bijections of the level sets; conversely, every level-preserving bijection that carries one datum onto the other is an order-isomorphism, hence a homeomorphism.
∎

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, every finite model of the six spaces has at least eight points, and every eight-point minimal finite model of them is a connected eight-point core of height
2
. We classify these cores and identify the minimal finite models among them; since each of the six spaces turns out to have eight-point models, its minimal finite models have exactly eight points.

Let
X
be a connected eight-point core of height
2
. Replacing
X
by its opposite
X^{\mathrm{op}}
if necessary (same weak homotopy type, Theorem
2.4
(2)), we may assume

\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)\leq\#\mathrm{mnl}(X).

An eight-point core is not contractible, since the only contractible core is a point; so
\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)\geq 2
and
\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)\geq 2
by Remark
2.9
. Since
X
is connected with more than one point,
\mathrm{mxl}(X)\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X)=\varnothing
, and as
\mathrm{h}(X)=2
,
B_{X}\neq\varnothing
. The combinatorial core criterion (Lemma
2.14
) and the homotopy-type tools (Lemmas
2.17
,
2.18
,
2.19
and
2.20
) are used freely below. In every case below,
X
has one or two intermediate points or two maximal points, so Lemma
2.13
or Lemma
2.18
applies:
\mathcal{K}(X)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
\beta_{1}
circles and
\beta_{2}
two-spheres, so
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
determines the weak homotopy type. We shall not repeat this.

Since
\#X=8
, the possible distributions of points among the three levels are determined by

\#X=\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)+\#B_{X}+\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)=8,

so that
\#B_{X}\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.
We analyse these cases separately.

Case 1:
\#B_{X}=1
.

By Lemma
2.13
, every core with a single intermediate point is weak homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles, so
H_{2}(\mathcal{K}(X))=0
and
X
models none of the six spaces of Lemma
3.4
(all of which have
H_{2}\neq 0
). For completeness we record the cores that arise. There are two distributions for the remaining points:

(\#\mathrm{mxl}(X),\#\mathrm{mnl}(X))=(2,5)\quad\text{or}\quad(3,4).

Subcase 1.1:

\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)=2
,
\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)=5
.

Let

\mathrm{mxl}(X)=\{a_{1},a_{2}\},\quad B_{X}=\{b_{1}\},\quad\mathrm{mnl}(X)=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4},c_{5}\}.

By Lemma
2.11
,
b_{1}
lies below both maximal points, and at least two minimal points lie below
b_{1}
. For a minimal point
c<b_{1}
, the set
\hat{F}_{c}
consists of
b_{1}
together with maximal points, all of which lie above
b_{1}
. Hence
\hat{F}_{c}
has minimum
b_{1}
, and
c
is an up beat point. So this subcase yields no core at all.

Subcase 1.2:

\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)=3
,
\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)=4
.

Let

\mathrm{mxl}(X)=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\},\quad B_{X}=\{b_{1}\},\quad\mathrm{mnl}(X)=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\},

and let
u=\#(\hat{F}_{b_{1}}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X))
and
d=\#(\hat{U}_{b_{1}}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X))
. By Lemma
2.11
,
u\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
. By Lemma
2.13
,
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}\bigvee_{k}S^{1}
with
k=\operatorname{rank}H_{1}(\mathcal{K}(X))
. Let
P
be the number of comparable pairs
(c,a)
with
c
minimal and
a
maximal, so that
P\leq 12
. The order complex has
8
vertices,
d+u+P
edges and
du
triangles, so

k=1-\chi(\mathcal{K}(X))=P-6-(d-1)(u-1)\leq 5.

By Corollary
2.8
,
\bigvee_{k}S^{1}
with
k\leq 5
has a finite model with at most seven points, so none of these cores is a minimal finite model.

For completeness we list them. As in Case 1 of the proof of Theorem
3.3
,
b_{1}
cannot lie below all three maximal points, since otherwise the minimal points below
b_{1}
would be beat points. So
u=2
, say
b_{1}<a_{1},a_{2}
. Then every minimal point below
b_{1}
must be covered by
a_{3}
, and each of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
must cover a minimal point not below
b_{1}
; hence
d\in\{2,3\}
. Let
T
be the set of the
4-d
minimal points not below
b_{1}
. Each
t\in T
is covered directly by a set
N(t)\subseteq\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
of at least two maximal points, and
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
must each belong to some
N(t)
. Conversely, every such choice gives a core by Lemma
2.14
. Up to the symmetries
a_{1}\leftrightarrow a_{2}
and permutations of
T
, there are two cores with
d=3
(with
N(t)=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
or
N(t)=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
) and six cores with
d=2
. Since
P=3d+\sum_{t\in T}\#N(t)
, here
k=\sum_{t\in T}\#N(t)+2d-5
. Altogether there are eight such cores: four with
k=3
, three with
k=4
and one with
k=5
. Together with their duals they form Block I of Table
A.1
.

Case 2:
\#B_{X}=2
.

Here, there are two possible distributions for the remaining points:

(\#\mathrm{mxl}(X),\#\mathrm{mnl}(X))=(2,4)\quad\text{or}\quad(3,3).

Subcase 2.1:

\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)=2
,
\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)=4
.

Let

\mathrm{mxl}(X)=\{a_{1},a_{2}\},\quad B_{X}=\{b_{1},b_{2}\},\quad\mathrm{mnl}(X)=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\}.

By Lemma
2.11
, each
b_{i}
lies below both maximal points, so the hypotheses of Lemma
2.19
hold with
m=2
and
q=4
. An inner minimal point is below both maximal points and so has no direct covers; by Lemma
2.14
(2) it lies below at least two intermediate points, hence below both. So the graph
G
is the complete bipartite graph
K_{s,2}
, where
s
is the number of inner minimal points. Each
b_{i}
lies above at least two minimal points (Lemma
2.14
(1)), all of them inner, so
2\leq s\leq 4
. Thus
E=2s
and
\kappa=1
, and by Lemma
2.19

\beta_{1}=4-s,\qquad\beta_{2}=s-1.

By the determination principle (Lemma
2.21
), the poset is determined up to homeomorphism by
s\in\{2,3,4\}
: the inner minimal points are interchangeable, as are the outer ones, the two intermediate points and the two maximal points. Hence Subcase 2.1 yields exactly three cores.
For
s=2
we get
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(2,1)
, so
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
4
(a); its dual, with level vector
(4,2,2)
, is
(a^{\ast})
). For
s=3
we get
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(1,2)
, so
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
6
(b); its dual is
(b^{\ast})
). For
s=4
we get
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0,3)
, so
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
; here
X
is the complete tripartite poset
T(2,2,4)
(Fig.
7
(a); its dual is
(a^{\ast})
).

Subcase 2.2:

\#\mathrm{mxl}(X)=3
,
\#\mathrm{mnl}(X)=3
.

Let

\mathrm{mxl}(X)=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\},\quad B_{X}=\{b_{1},b_{2}\},\quad\mathrm{mnl}(X)=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\},

and let
C_{i}=\hat{U}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mnl}(X)
and
A_{i}=\hat{F}_{b_{i}}\cap\mathrm{mxl}(X)
. By Lemma
2.11
,
|C_{i}|,|A_{i}|\in\{2,3\}
for
i=1,2
. Since
X
has exactly two intermediate points, Lemma
2.20
applies with
p=q=3
:

\beta_{2}=(|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|-1)(|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|-1),\qquad\beta_{1}=|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|+|A_{1}\cup A_{2}|+\delta-6,

where
\delta=|\Delta|
is the number of direct covers.

Reduction to symmetry orbits.
By the determination principle (Lemma
2.21
), a connected
(3,2,3)
core is recovered from its datum
(C_{1},C_{2},A_{1},A_{2},\Delta)
. Two such cores are homeomorphic exactly when their data are related by the level-symmetry group
\mathrm{Sym}\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}\times\mathrm{Sym}\{b_{1},b_{2}\}\times\mathrm{Sym}\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
. Classifying these cores up to homeomorphism is therefore the same as listing the orbits, under this group, of the
admissible
data, namely those for which
X
is a core (Lemma
2.14
). We organise the enumeration by the isomorphism types of
(C_{1},C_{2})
and of
(A_{1},A_{2})
. Each pair of subsets of size at least
2
of a
3
-element set has one of four patterns (below). For each resulting pattern-pair we fix representatives
(C_{1},C_{2},A_{1},A_{2})
and list all admissible
\Delta
up to the residual symmetry, that is, up to the stabiliser of these representatives in the level-symmetry group. This is legitimate because, under the level-symmetry group above, each pattern-pair determines a single orbit of quadruples
(C_{1},C_{2},A_{1},A_{2})
, with one exception: the transposition
b_{1}\leftrightarrow b_{2}
interchanges
(C_{1},A_{1})
with
(C_{2},A_{2})
, and its effect on the
C_{i}
can be undone by a permutation of
\mathrm{mnl}(X)
in each of the patterns
\mathsf{F}
,
\mathsf{E}
and
\mathsf{Z}
, and dually for the
A_{i}
; only
\mathsf{M}
, where
|C_{1}|\neq|C_{2}|
, is not symmetric in this way. Hence
(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{M})
is the only pattern-pair whose quadruples form two orbits rather than one, according to whether the intermediate point with
C_{i}=\mathrm{mnl}(X)
is the one with
A_{i}=\mathrm{mxl}(X)
; this is the case distinction made in item 5 below. The residual symmetries used are described in each item below, and the resulting orbit representatives are collected in Table
2
; their weak homotopy types are given by Lemma
2.20
. By the determination principle the
20
representatives listed there are pairwise non-homeomorphic, and by the case analysis below they exhaust the admissible data, so Table
2
is a complete and irredundant list.

Down- and up-patterns.
Two subsets
C_{1},C_{2}
of the three minimal points, each of size
2
or
3
, have, up to relabelling, exactly one of the following four
patterns
, determined by
(|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|,\,|C_{1}\cup C_{2}|)
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathsf{F}=(3,3):&C_{1}=C_{2}=\mathrm{mnl}(X);\\
\mathsf{M}=(2,3):&\{C_{1},C_{2}\}=\{\mathrm{mnl}(X),\{c_{1},c_{2}\}\};\\
\mathsf{E}=(2,2):&C_{1}=C_{2}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\};\\
\mathsf{Z}=(1,3):&\{C_{1},C_{2}\}=\{\{c_{1},c_{2}\},\{c_{1},c_{3}\}\}.\end{array}

Thus in pattern
\mathsf{F}
both intermediate points lie above all minimal points, in pattern
\mathsf{M}
exactly one of them does, in pattern
\mathsf{E}
they lie above the same two minimal points, and in pattern
\mathsf{Z}
they have exactly one minimal point in common below them. The same four
up-patterns
arise for
(A_{1},A_{2})
. We write a pattern-pair as
(P,Q)
, where
P
is the down-pattern and
Q
the up-pattern; the opposite of a core with pattern-pair
(P,Q)
has pattern-pair
(Q,P)
. A minimal point lying below
k
intermediate points has
k
intermediate covers, so by Lemma
2.14
(2) it needs at least
2-k
direct covers, by maximal points that lie above none of those intermediate points; dually for maximal points. We use this to determine, for each pattern-pair, all cores and their types.

Vanishing of
\beta_{2}
.
If the down-pattern or the up-pattern is
\mathsf{Z}
, then
|C_{1}\cap C_{2}|=1
or
|A_{1}\cap A_{2}|=1
, so
\beta_{2}=0
and
X
is weak homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles. Such an
X
models none of the six spaces of Lemma
3.4
, all of which have
H_{2}\neq 0
. The pattern-pairs
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{M})
and
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{F})
, and their duals, admit no core. Indeed, in pattern
\mathsf{Z}
each intermediate point lies above a minimal point that lies below no other intermediate point. If
b_{i}
satisfies
A_{i}=\mathrm{mxl}(X)
, such a minimal point below
b_{i}
lies below every maximal point through
b_{i}
, so
b_{i}
is its only immediate successor and that minimal point is a beat point. The remaining pattern-pairs of this kind are
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{E})
, its dual
(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{Z})
, and
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{Z})
.
For
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{E})
, take
C_{1}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
,
C_{2}=\{c_{1},c_{3}\}
and
A_{1}=A_{2}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. The point
c_{2}
lies below
b_{1}
only, and its only possible direct cover is by
a_{3}
; so
c_{2}\prec a_{3}
is forced, and likewise
c_{3}\prec a_{3}
. The direct cover
c_{1}\prec a_{3}
is optional, and no other direct cover is possible. Hence
\delta\in\{2,3\}
and
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(\delta-1,0)
, giving two cores, of types
(1,0)
and
(2,0)
. For
(\mathsf{Z},\mathsf{Z})
, take
C_{1}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
,
C_{2}=\{c_{1},c_{3}\}
,
A_{1}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
and
A_{2}=\{a_{1},a_{3}\}
. The direct covers
c_{2}\prec a_{3}
and
c_{3}\prec a_{2}
are forced, and no others are possible, since every other pair
(c,a)
is already comparable through
b_{1}
or
b_{2}
. Hence
\delta=2
, giving a single core, of type
(2,0)
; it is self-dual.
Together with their duals, these are five of the nine cores of Block II of Table
A.1
; they are not minimal finite models.

Pattern-pairs with
\beta_{2}\geq 1
.
It remains to treat the pattern-pairs in which both patterns lie in
\{\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M},\mathsf{E}\}
. We go through them up to duality. Throughout, “a unique core” (resp. “
k
cores”) means a single orbit (resp.
k
orbits) of admissible
\Delta
under the stabiliser of the chosen representatives
(C_{1},C_{2},A_{1},A_{2})
; each item below exhibits the orbit representatives and verifies the core condition by Lemma
2.14
.

(1)

(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{F})
.
Both intermediate points lie above all minimal points and below all maximal points, so
X=T(3,2,3)
, the complete tripartite poset, with
\delta=0
. By Lemma
2.17
(or by Lemma
2.20
, which gives
\beta_{2}=4
and
\beta_{1}=0
),
X\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
. This is the unique core here (Fig.
8
(b)).

(2)

(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{M})
and its dual
(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{F})
: no core.
Suppose the down-pattern is
\mathsf{F}
and the up-pattern is
\mathsf{M}
, say
A_{1}=\mathrm{mxl}(X)
and
A_{2}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. Then
a_{3}
lies above
b_{1}
only, and its only possible further immediate predecessor would be a direct cover
c\prec a_{3}
. But every minimal point lies below
b_{1}
(down-pattern
\mathsf{F}
), and
b_{1}<a_{3}
, so
c<a_{3}
holds through
b_{1}
for every minimal point
c
, and no direct cover of
a_{3}
is possible. Hence
a_{3}
has the single immediate predecessor
b_{1}
and is a beat point, so no core arises. The dual pattern-pair is treated in the same way.

(3)

(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{F})
and its dual
(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{E})
.
Take
C_{1}=C_{2}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
and
A_{1}=A_{2}=\mathrm{mxl}(X)
. Then
c_{1},c_{2}
lie below both intermediate points, hence below every maximal point, and so have no direct covers. The point
c_{3}
lies below neither intermediate point, so by Lemma
2.14
(2) it has at least two direct covers, among
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
. Every maximal point already lies above both intermediate points and needs no direct cover. So
\Delta
consists of the direct covers of
c_{3}
, and
\delta\in\{2,3\}
. Lemma
2.20
gives
\beta_{2}=(2-1)(3-1)=2
and
\beta_{1}=2+3+\delta-6=\delta-1
. Up to relabelling the maximal points,
\delta=2
gives one core
\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
6
(c)) and
\delta=3
gives one core
\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
9
(a)); by Lemma
3.4
, both are minimal finite models. With the dual pattern-pair this yields, in total, two cores
\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
6
(c), (c
∗
)) and two cores
\mathrel{\overset{\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{we}}{\approx}}S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
(Fig.
9
(a), (a
∗
)); in each case the two cores are not homeomorphic, since their pattern-pairs
(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{F})
and
(\mathsf{F},\mathsf{E})
differ.

(4)

(\mathsf{E},\mathsf{M})
and its dual
(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{E})
.
Take
C_{1}=C_{2}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
, and let one of
A_{1},A_{2}
be
\mathrm{mxl}(X)
and the other
\{a_{1},a_{2}\}
. Here
c_{3}
lies below neither intermediate point, and
a_{3}
lies above exactly one of them. A direct cover of
a_{3}
can only come from
c_{3}
, since

4.
Conclusion and future work

In this paper we classified, up to homeomorphism, the minimal finite models of several wedge sums of one- and two-dimensional spheres, namely
S^{2}\vee S^{1}
and
S^{2}\vee S^{2}
on seven points, and
S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}
,
S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}
,
S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
,
S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
,
S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
, and
S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
on eight points; a model and its opposite are counted separately whenever they are not homeomorphic. The finite-space part of the arguments is combinatorial: each model is a height-
2
core, and the cores are found by a direct analysis of covering relations. Their weak homotopy types are identified by McCord’s theorem together with the structural lemmas of Section
2
: collapse to a graph, the suspension produced by a union of two cones, and the closed-form Betti-number formulas these yield. These lemmas rest only on elementary cell-attachment arguments for CW complexes.

The classification makes explicit how the combinatorics of a finite poset—the sizes of its levels, the incidences across the middle level, and the absence of beat points—determine the homotopy type of its order complex. Together with the classical results on spheres and wedges of circles, it determines, up to weak homotopy equivalence, all path-connected spaces whose minimal finite models have at most eight points (Corollary
3.9
).

Connections with digital topology and discrete Morse theory

Finite
T_{0}
-spaces also play a role in digital topology. Khalimsky, Kopperman and Meyer studied connected topologies on finite ordered sets as models for computer graphics
[
11
]
, and Kovalevsky proposed abstract cellular complexes, finite sets of cells partially ordered by a bounding relation and carrying the associated finite topology, as a topological basis for image analysis; he also showed that every finite
T_{0}
-space is such a complex
[
12
]
. From this point of view a minimal finite model represents a weak homotopy type with as few cells as possible. Theorem
A
gives, for each of the eight wedges, the least number of points of a finite
T_{0}
-space with its weak homotopy type and lists all such spaces; by Remark
3.1
, the same number bounds from below the number of cells of any regular CW complex weak homotopy equivalent to that wedge.

The arguments of Section
2
also fit into Forman’s discrete Morse theory
[
6
]
. In the proof of Lemma
2.13
, pairing each edge
\{c,a\}
with the triangle
\{c,b,a\}
defines a discrete vector field on
\mathcal{K}(X)
in the sense of Forman. It has no nontrivial closed paths, since no edge containing
b
is paired, so it is the gradient vector field of a discrete Morse function whose critical cells are exactly the cells of the graph
G
. Thus the conclusion
\mathcal{K}(X)\simeq G
is an instance of the basic theorem of discrete Morse theory. Minian developed a discrete Morse theory for posets
[
15
]
that extends Forman’s theory from regular CW complexes; it applies, for example, to the face posets of the regular CW complexes constructed in Section
3.1
. It would be interesting to study the minimal finite models classified here from this point of view.

Future work

Several directions suggest themselves. First, the connected cores with nine and ten points remain to be classified: by Corollary
3.9
, spaces such as
S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
and
S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
have no finite model with fewer than nine points, so their minimal finite models can only be found among these larger cores (both do have models with at most ten points: by Lemmas
2.14
and
2.19
, adding to
T(2,2,4)
a minimal point covered by both maximal points gives a nine-point core that models
S^{1}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
, and by Lemma
2.17
,
T(2,2,6)
is a ten-point model of
S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}\vee S^{2}
). Second, for the family
S^{n}\vee S^{n-1}\vee S^{n-1}
, whose case
n=2
is
S^{1}\vee S^{1}\vee S^{2}
, the minimal number of points is now known to be
2n+4
in every dimension, and a connected finite space with at most
2n+3
points and nonzero
n

S^{n}
or to
S^{n}\vee S^{k}
for some
1\leq k\leq n

[
16

; classifying minimal finite models up to homeomorphism in dimensions above two, as done here in dimension two, remains open, as does the case of spaces combining different homotopy types, such as
\mathbb{R}P^{2}\vee S^{2}
and
T^{2}\vee S^{1}

2n+2

S^{n}

[
