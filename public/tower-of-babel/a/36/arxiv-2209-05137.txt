Central schemes for networked scalar conservation laws
Michael Herty, Niklas Kolbe, Siegfried Müller

Abstract
We propose a novel scheme to numerically solve scalar conservation laws on networks without the necessity to solve Riemann problems at the junction. The scheme is derived using the relaxation system introduced in [Jin and Xin, Comm. Pure Appl. Math. 48(3), 235–276 (1995)] and taking the relaxation limit also at the nodes of the network. The scheme is mass conservative and yields well defined and easy-to-compute coupling conditions even for general networks. We discuss higher order extension of the scheme and applications to traffic flow and two-phase flow. In the former we compare with results obtained in literature. Keywords: Coupled conservation laws, finite-volume schemes, coupling conditions

Introduction

Research on mathematical models on networks understood as directed (one-dimensional) graphs has been successfully conducted over the last decades and we refer to the recent survey
[
8
]
for details and references. Such models have various applications, such as gas dynamics in pipelines
[
37
,
10
,

]
, vehicular traffic on road networks
[

,

]
, production systems
[

and blood flow through systems of blood vessels

to mention only a few. Apart from modeling questions regarding the partial differential equations on the edges, a major modeling challenge is the description of the dynamics at the network nodes, where adjacent edges connect. Starting with
[

,

networked (systems of) conservation or balance laws are defined by (physically induced) coupling conditions, see
[
8
]
for examples, and e.g
[

]
for a hierarchical derivation in the case of gas dynamics. Those conditions yield under suitable assumptions boundary conditions. Using wave–front–tracking techniques well–posedness of such coupled problems could be established, see e.g.
[
32
,
23
,
16
,
15
,
7
]
. A key analytical concept here is the notion of Riemann solvers
[
23
,
22
]
or half–Riemann problems
[
31
]
. Among others, those concepts require an analytic expression of wave curves of the corresponding models. Here, we are interested in the numerical treatment of networked conservation laws. Numerical methods based on the Riemann solvers have been proposed already in
[
25
,
32
,
2
]
and have recently gained interest in view of high–order methods
[
48
,
1
,
9
,
6
,
51
,
12
,
5
]
, property–preserving schemes
[
46
,
54
]
and also for problems where Lax–curves and eigenvalues are not explicit
[
49
,
40
]
or not available
[
28
,
29
]
. Regarding the development of efficient schemes, most of the higher–order schemes rely on the linearization of the coupling conditions such that the Lax–curves are obtained trivially, see e.g.
[
1
,
6
]
for more details. Linearization techniques have also been used to avoid the problem of the explicit computations of eigenvalues and Lax-curves in two–phase problems, see e.g.
[
3
]
.
The question of property preserving numerical schemes across networks has been recently investigated in view of well-balanced networked equations
[
46
]
and entropy-preserving schemes
[
54
]
. Note that for simplicity we consider here only the case of conservation laws but the numerical schemes directly extend to the case of balance laws. Further, we focus here on finite-volume or discontinuous Galerkin schemes whereas approaches based on finite-element schemes, such as
[
20
]
, require a different treatment of coupling conditions. Similarly, a construction of vanishing viscosity solutions, which has been addressed in the schemes studied in
[
39
,
52
]
, avoids the use of Riemann problems at the expense of formally treating parabolic systems.

In this manuscript we develop a numerical scheme that does not require a Riemann solver at the junction. To this end, we embed the coupling problem of nonlinear equations in the coupling of linear relaxation systems following the approach developed by e.g. Jin-Xin cf.
[
36
]
. Since for linear systems the Lax curves are multiples of the a priori computable
constant eigenvectors of the flux matrix, the computations will be explicit. A similar idea has been used in
[
38
]
to approximate a scalar conservation law with discontinuous flux. Applying an implicit-explicit discretization in time leads further to an explicit scheme contrary to e.g.
[
40
]
.
Following an approach based on hyperbolic relaxation will allow us to define a numerical scheme that does not rely on the solution to the Riemann solvers at the node of the network. It can be extended to higher-order which is also demonstrated here and existing coupling conditions can be embedded in this framework as shown in an example on traffic flow.

2
Notation and Preliminary Discussion

A network is a directed graph consisting of edges and vertices or junctions. We restrict the discussion to scalar hyperbolic conservation laws posed on each edge and, due to the finite speed of propagation, to the problem at a single coupling node, that we assume at position
x=0
. The dynamics on the adjacent edge
k
reads

\partial_{t}u^{k}+\partial_{x}f_{k}(u^{k})=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{E}_{k}\times(0,\infty),\quad k\in\delta^{\mp},

(1)

where the state variable
u^{k}
is either given on an incoming edge parameterized by
\mathcal{E}_{k}=(-\infty,0)
if
k\in\delta^{-}=\{1,\dots,N^{-}\}
or on an outgoing edge parameterized by
\mathcal{E}_{k}=(0,\infty)
if
k\in\delta^{+}=\{N^{-}+1,\dots,N^{-}+N^{+}=N\}
. Here, the flux functions are smooth, but not-necessarily convex or concave flux functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}:

\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The set of all edges is denoted by
\delta^{\mp}=\delta^{-}\cup\delta^{+}
. In addition, we assume given initial data that we denote on each edge
k\in\delta^{\mp}
by
u^{k,0}
.

The coupling is described by a set of conditions on the traces at the coupling node of the form

\Psi[u^{1}(0^{-},t),\dots,u^{N^{-}}(0^{-},t),u^{N^{-}+1}(0^{+},t),\dots u^{N}(0^{+},t)]=0,\quad\text{for a.e. }t>0,

(2)

assuming a mapping
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. The number of coupling conditions
\ell
required to obtain a well-posed problem depends on the number of edges and the choice of flux functions, see e.g.
[
8
]
and the discussion following below.

The typical (numerical) procedure to obtain conditions on the traces of the solution
u^{k}
at
x=0
relies on a suitable Riemann solver at the junction
[
23
]
. Illustrated in the case
|\delta^{+}|=|\delta^{-}|=1
, where (
1
) can be rewritten without edge indices as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f_{1}(u)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{in}(-\infty,0)\times(0,\infty),

(3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f_{2}(u)

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{in}(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty),

(3b)

the idea is to connect the traces
u_{0}^{-}
and
u_{0}^{+}
on the left and right of the interface to the coupling data
u_{L}
and
u_{R}
at the interface. The coupling data are determined such that the coupling conditions
\Psi(u_{L},u_{R})=0
are satisfied and the states
u_{L}
and
u_{R}
can be connected to the traces
u_{0}^{-}
on the left and
u_{0}^{+}
on the right by means of Lax curves corresponding to characteristic fields with negative and positive characteristic speeds, respectively. This problem is formulated using parameterized Lax-curves and reduced to a typically nonlinear but finite-dimensional system of equations, see e.g.
[
8
,
30
,
27
]
.

To elaborate on the well-posedness and derivation of coupling conditions we introduce the Riemann solver on the edges of the network, which reads

\partial_{t}u^{k}+\partial_{x}f_{k}(u^{k})=0,\quad k\in\delta^{-}\cup\delta^{+}

(4a)

with initial data given either by

u^{k}(x,0)=\begin{cases}u^{k}_{0}&\text{if }x\leq 0,\\
u^{k}_{R}&\text{if }x>0,\\
\end{cases}\quad\text{ if }k\in\delta^{-}

(4b)

or otherwise by

u^{k}(x,0)=\begin{cases}u^{k}_{L}&\text{if }x\leq 0,\\
u^{k}_{0}&\text{if }x>0,\\
\end{cases}\quad\text{ if }k\in\delta^{+}.

(4c)

On incoming edges the right initial data of the Riemann problem at time
t=0
denoted by
u^{k}_{R}
is unknown whereas the left data
u^{k}_{0}
is assumed known. Analogously, on outgoing edges the left initial data of (
4
) denoted by
u^{k}_{L}
is unknown whereas the right data
u^{k}_{0}
is given. Unknown data is obtained by a Riemann solver.

Definition 1

(Riemann solver for scalar networks)
.

A Riemann solver for problem (
4
) is a mapping that assigns right initial data on incoming edges and left initial data on outgoing edges to given initial data on the respective opposite side, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{RS}:\,\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N},

\displaystyle(u^{1-}_{0},\dots,u^{N^{-}}_{0},u^{N^{-}+1}_{0},\dots,u^{N}_{0})\mapsto(u^{1}_{R},\dots,u^{N^{-}}_{R},u^{N^{-}+1}_{L},\dots,u^{N}_{L}),

such that (a) waves of the solution to (
4
) have negative speed on incoming edges and positive speed on outgoing edges and (b)
\Psi[u^{1}_{R},\dots,u^{N^{-}}_{R},u^{N^{-}+1}_{L},\dots,u^{N}_{L}]=0
.

A necessary condition for conservation of
u
at the coupling node is the
Kirchhoff
condition:

\Psi_{1}[u^{1}_{0},\dots,u^{N}_{0}]=\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}f_{j}(u^{j}_{0})-\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}f_{k}(u^{k}_{0})=0.

(5)

Hence, to conserve the quantity
u
in the junction of the network, the coupling data obtained by the Riemann solver needs to satisfy the condition

\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}f_{j}(u^{j}_{R})=\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}f_{k}(u^{k}_{L}).

(6)

In the scalar network (
1
) condition (
5
) together with admissible boundary data, see
[
19
]
, lead to mass conservation at the coupling node. However, these conditions are not necessarily sufficient for well-posedness of the Riemann solver and the network problem, see
[
8
]
.

3
Coupled Relaxation System

We follow
[
36
]
, where in addition to the scalar quantity
u
and the flux function
f
the auxiliary variable
v\in\mathbb{R}
, the
relaxation rate

\varepsilon>0
and the
relaxation speed

\lambda>0
have been introduced and the following system is studied:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\partial_{x}v^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty),

(7a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}+\lambda^{2}\,\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f(u^{\varepsilon})-v^{\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty).

(7b)

The relaxation system is accompanied by initial data given through a scalar function
u^{0}
and
v^{0}=f(u^{0})
.
As
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
the system attains the
zero relaxation limit

(u,v)\coloneq(u^{0},v^{0})
for which (
7b
) necessitates the local equilibrium

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}v^{\varepsilon}=v=f(u)
and (
7a
) recovers the conservation law

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f(u)=0.

(8)

The Chapman-Enskog expansion
[
13
]
allows for an interpretation of (
7
) as a dissipative equation and shows that
v^{\varepsilon}=f(u^{\varepsilon})+O(\epsilon^{2})
.
The
subcharacteristic condition

-\lambda\leq f^{\prime}(u^{\varepsilon})\leq\lambda\quad\text{for all }u^{\varepsilon}.

(9)

introduced in
[
45
]
guarantees that the dissipative approximation is well-posed. The dissipative equation and the conservation law were shown to have the same asymptotic behavior as the relaxation rate goes to zero in
[
14
]
. An eigenvalue analysis of the relaxation system, see Appendix
A
, reveals that it is hyperbolic, has the eigenvalues
-\lambda
and
\lambda
and can be rewritten in terms of its characteristic variables
w^{\varepsilon\mp}=v^{\varepsilon}/2\mp\lambda u^{\varepsilon}/2
as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon-}-\lambda\partial_{x}w^{\varepsilon-}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f\left(\frac{w^{\varepsilon+}-w^{\varepsilon-}}{\lambda}\right)-w^{\varepsilon+}-w^{\varepsilon-}\right),

(10a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon+}+\lambda\partial_{x}w^{\varepsilon+}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f\left(\frac{w^{\varepsilon+}-w^{\varepsilon-}}{\lambda}\right)-w^{\varepsilon+}-w^{\varepsilon-}\right).

(10b)

Moreover, the forward and backward Lax-curves of the relaxation system are given by straight lines in the phase plane as follows

\displaystyle L^{1-}_{\lambda}(u^{\varepsilon}_{0},v^{\varepsilon}_{0})

\displaystyle=\{(u^{\varepsilon}_{0}-\sigma,v^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\sigma\,\lambda),~\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\},

(11a)

\displaystyle L^{2+}_{\lambda}(u^{\varepsilon}_{0},v^{\varepsilon}_{0})

\displaystyle=\{(u^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\sigma,v^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\sigma\,\lambda),~\sigma\in\mathbb{R}\}.

(11b)

3.1
Relaxation System at 1-to-1 networks

Since the relaxation system describes the behavior of the conservation law in the relaxation limit we use it as a tool to derive suitable coupling data for the 1-to-1 coupling network. To this end we will analyze system (
7
) in the 1-to-1 coupling case in this section and derive admissible coupling data. The relaxation limit will be taken in Section
3.2
in case of an
asymptotic preserving
discretization of the system, which was shown to converge to the correct limit as the relaxation rate tends to zero, see
[
35

and the references therein.

We consider a 1-to-1 network, which couples two relaxation systems of the form (
7
) on a single incoming and a single outgoing edge at the coupling node, and reads

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\partial_{x}v^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\times(0,\infty),

(12a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{1}^{2}\,\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{1}(u^{\varepsilon})-v^{\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\text{in }(-\infty,0)\times(0,\infty),

(12b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{2}^{2}\,\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{2}(u^{\varepsilon})-v^{\varepsilon}),

\displaystyle\text{in }(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty).

(12c)

We introduce a uniform grid on the real line by fixing
\Delta x>0
and defining the mesh points
x_{j-1/2}=j\,\Delta x
for any

We obtain a scheme for the relaxation system in characteristic variables (

A second order scheme for system (

[

to the upwind discretization of the relaxation system in characteristic variables (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon-}_{j}-\frac{\lambda}{\Delta x}(w^{\varepsilon-}_{j+1/2}-w^{\varepsilon-}_{j-1/2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(f\left(\frac{w^{\varepsilon+}_{j}-w^{\varepsilon-}_{j}}{\lambda}\right)-w^{\varepsilon+}_{j}-w^{\varepsilon-}_{j}\right),

(34a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w^{\varepsilon+}_{j}+\frac{\lambda}{\Delta x}(w^{\varepsilon+}_{j+1/2}-w^{\varepsilon+}_{j-1/2})

j\in\mathbb{Z}

monotonized central-difference limiter

s_{j}^{\mp}=\operatorname{minmod}\left(2\frac{w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j}-w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j-1}}{\Delta x},~\frac{w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j+1}-w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j-1}}{2\,\Delta x},~2\frac{w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j+1}-w^{\varepsilon\mp}_{j}}{\Delta x}\right).

(36)

The selected limiter (
36

[
53
]
and designed to yield sharp resolutions near discontinuities. In smooth regions it admits the central difference whereas the accuracy at non-sonic critical points is reduced to preserve the monotonicity of the discrete solution.
The

\operatorname{minmod}(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})=\begin{cases}\max\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}&\text{if }q_{k}<0,~k=1,\dots,n,\\
\min\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}&\text{if }q_{k}>0,~k=1,\dots,n,\\

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{j+1}^{-}-(s_{j}^{+}+s_{j}^{-})+s_{j-1}^{+}\right)=0,

) (with uniform

s_{j}=\frac{w_{j+1}-w_{j}}{\Delta x}\phi_{j},\quad\text{where}\quad 0\leq\phi_{j}\leq 2\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\phi_{j}\,\frac{w_{j+1}-w_{j}}{w_{j}-w_{j-1}}\leq 2,

which is satisfied by (

\phi_{j}=\operatorname{minmod}\left(2\frac{w_{j}-w_{j-1}}{w_{j+1}-w_{j}},~\frac{w_{j+1}-w_{j-1}}{2(w_{j+1}-w_{j})},~2\right),

Multiple Incoming and Outgoing Edges

In this section, we generalize the schemes introduced in Sections
3.2.4
and
3.2.5
to scalar conservation laws at nodes with
N^{-}
incoming and
N^{+}
outgoing edges. To this end we consider the relaxation system introduced in Section
3
in a network setting to derive admissible coupling data. In Section
4.2
we then discuss additional conditions about the flux distribution at the junction, which are required for well posedness of coupling data. In Section
4.3

4.1
The Relaxation System on Networks

We generalize the approach from Section
3.1
and consider the relaxation system (
7
) on a network that allows for
N^{-}
incoming and
N^{+}
outgoing edges. Again, we aim to take the relaxation limit in a space discretization of the network system in order to derive a scheme for the scalar network (
1
). By coupling multiple systems of the form (
7
) in the same way we coupled scalar conservation laws in (
1
) we obtain the system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{k}+\partial_{x}v^{k}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{E}_{k}\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle k\in\delta^{\mp},

(47a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v^{k}+\lambda_{k}^{2}\,\partial_{x}u^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f_{k}(u^{k})-v^{k}),

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{E}_{k}\times(0,\infty),

\displaystyle k\in\delta^{\mp}

(47b)

governing the scalar variables
u^{k}
and
v^{k}
, where
k\in\delta^{\mp}=\delta^{-}\cup\delta^{+}=\{1,\dots,N\}
indicates the corresponding edge of the network. The system includes a stiff source term that contains the flux functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}:

\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and the relaxation rate
\varepsilon
that is chosen uniform over the edges of the network. Compared to the 1-to-1 network (
12
) we do not account for
\varepsilon
in the variable names here for brevity of presentation. On each edge of the network a relaxation speed
\lambda_{k}>0
is given, which satisfies the subcharacteristic condition, i.e.,

-\lambda_{k}\leq f_{k}^{\prime}(u^{k})\leq\lambda_{k}\quad\text{for all }u^{k}\text{ and }k\in\delta^{\mp}.

(48)

The smooth and compactly supported scalar functions
u^{k,0}
determine the initial condition of system (
47
) by

u^{k}(x,0)=u^{k,0}(x),\quad v^{k}(x,0)=f_{k}(u^{k,0}(x))\quad k\in\delta^{\mp}.

(49)

Furthermore, coupling conditions for the system variables at the coupling node have the form

\Psi\left[(u^{1}(0^{-},t),v^{1}(0^{-},t)),\dots,(u^{N}(0^{+},t),v^{N}(0^{+},t))\right]=0\quad\text{for a.e. }t>0.

(50)

Since the system is linear,
N
conditions are necessary to obtain a well-posed problem and thus
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{2N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
(compare system (
12
), which can be written as (
47
) with
N=2
and requires the two conditions given in (
17
)). At a fixed time
t>0
we assume given traces
u_{0}^{1},v_{0}^{1},\dots,u_{0}^{N},v_{0}^{N}
at
x=0
. Unlike in Section
3.1
we do not indicate if a trace is incoming or outgoing by a sign index as this can be seen from the edge index. We formally define a Riemann solver for system (
47
) by

\mathcal{RS}_{\text{rel}}:(u_{0}^{1},v_{0}^{1},\dots,u_{0}^{N},v_{0}^{N})\mapsto(u_{R}^{1},v_{R}^{1},\dots,u_{L}^{N},u_{L}^{N}).

(51)

The construction of (
51
) is discussed in the remainder of this section and in Section
4.2
. We aim to derive admissible coupling data that verifies the conservation of the system variables in the coupling node. To ensure admissible boundary data,
(u_{R}^{k},v_{R}^{k})
needs to connect to
(u_{0}^{k},v_{0}^{k})
by a wave with negative velocity for all
k\in\delta^{-}
and
(u_{L}^{k},u_{L}^{k})
needs to connect to
(u_{0}^{k},u_{0}^{k})
by a wave with positive velocity for all
k\in\delta^{+}
. Thus, by the analysis in Appendix
A
, we get the conditions

(u_{R}^{k},v_{R}^{k})\in L^{1-}_{\lambda_{k}}(u_{0}^{k},v_{0}^{k})\quad\text{for }k\in\delta^{-}\quad\text{and}\quad(u_{L}^{k},v_{L}^{k})\in L^{2+}_{\lambda_{k}}(u_{0}^{k},v_{0}^{k})\quad\text{for }k\in\delta^{+}.

(52)

Since all Lax-curves are parameterized by a single parameter, (
52
) gives rise to
N
unknowns, which are to be determined by the coupling conditions.
As done in (
17
) for the 1-to-1 network we impose the Kirchhoff condition (
5
) for both system variables and obtain the two conditions

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}\left[(u_{R}^{1},v_{R}^{1}),\dots,(u_{L}^{N},v_{L}^{N})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}v_{L}^{j}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}v_{R}^{k}=0,

(53a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}\left[(u_{R}^{1},v_{R}^{1}),\dots,(u_{L}^{N},v_{L}^{N})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\lambda_{j}^{2}\,v_{L}^{j}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}\lambda_{k}^{2}\,v_{R}^{k}=0.

(53b)

4.2
Additional Conditions in the General Case

The two conditions given by (
53
) are not sufficient to obtain a well-defined Riemann solver for the network system (
47
). Therefore
N-2
suitable additional conditions need to be imposed. Here we consider algebraic conditions on the auxiliary variable at the coupling node of the form

\Psi_{\ell+2}\left[(u_{R}^{1},v_{R}^{1}),\dots,(u_{L}^{N},v_{L}^{N})\right]=-r^{\ell}+\sum_{j\in\delta^{-}}\beta_{j}^{\ell}\,v_{L}^{j}+\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}\beta_{k}^{\ell}\,v_{R}^{k}=0\quad\text{for }\ell=1,\dots,N-2

(54)

with parameters
\beta_{k}^{\ell}
,
r^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
for
k\in\delta^{\pm}
and
\ell=1,\dots,N-2
. In the relaxation limit, the variable
v
is the flux of the state variable of the conservation law in the relaxation limit and (
54
) can be understood as conditions on the fluxes at the coupling node when the limit scheme is considered.

Taking into account (
52
) we denote by
\sigma_{k}
the position of
(u_{R}^{k},v_{R}^{k})
for
k\in\delta^{-}
or of
(u_{L}^{k},v_{L}^{k})
for
k\in\delta^{-}
on the corresponding Lax curve given by (
11
). Then we get from (
53
) and (
54
) the linear system

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\nu_{k}\,\lambda_{k}\,\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\nu_{k}\,v_{0}^{k},

(55a)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\lambda_{k}^{2}\,\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\nu_{k}\,\lambda_{k}^{2}\,u_{0}^{k},

(55b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\beta_{k}^{\ell}\,\lambda_{k}\,\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=r^{\ell}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\beta_{k}^{\ell}\,v_{0}^{k}\quad\text{for }\ell=1,\dots,N-2

(55c)

where
\nu_{k}
denotes the sign of the edge defined as

\nu_{k}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }k\in\delta^{-},\\
1&\text{if }k\in\delta^{+}.\end{cases}

(56)

When assigning parameters for (
54
) care must be taken that the system (
55
) has full rank. For example in case of a 2-to-1 network and
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}
this is satisfied whenever
\beta_{1}^{1}\neq\beta_{2}^{1}
. We introduce the following vector notations for traces and coupling data

\mathbf{u}_{0}=(u_{0}^{1},\cdots,u_{0}^{N})^{T},\quad\mathbf{v}_{0}=(v_{0}^{1},\cdots,v_{0}^{N})^{T},\quad\mathbf{u}_{c}=(u_{R}^{1},\cdots,u_{L}^{N})^{T},\quad\mathbf{v}_{c}=(v_{R}^{1},\cdots,v_{L}^{N})^{T}.

(57)

Moreover, we introduce the
N\times N
diagonal matrices
\Lambda
and
\mathcal{N}
with diagonal entries
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
and
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{N}
, respectively. This allows us to express the coupling data by means of the linear systems

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{u}_{c}-\mathbf{u}_{0})=\mathbf{b},\qquad\mathcal{A}\Lambda^{-1}(\mathbf{v}_{c}-\mathbf{v}_{0})=\mathbf{b},

(58)

which hence define (
51
). Here
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbf{b}
denote the system matrix and the right-hand side of (
55
), i.e.,

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_{1}\,\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\nu_{N}\,\lambda_{N}\\[5.0pt]
\lambda_{1}^{2}&\cdots&\lambda_{N}^{2}\\[5.0pt]
\beta_{1}^{1}\,\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\beta_{N}^{1}\,\lambda_{N}\\[5.0pt]
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\beta_{1}^{N-2}\,\lambda_{1}&\cdots&\beta_{N}^{N-2}\,\lambda_{N}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\nu_{k}\,v_{0}^{k}\\[5.0pt]
-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\nu_{k}\,\lambda_{k}^{2}\,u_{0}^{k}\\[5.0pt]
r^{1}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\beta_{k}^{1}\,v_{0}^{k}\\[5.0pt]
\vdots\\
r^{N-2}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{\mp}}\beta_{k}^{N-2}\,v_{0}^{k}\end{pmatrix}.

(59)

In the rest of this section we give general suggestions for the parameters in (
54
). However, we note that they are an issue of modeling and can be freely adapted to the problem at hand. We will assume non-negativity of the flux functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
and the trace data
v_{0}^{1},\dots,v_{0}^{N}
. In the relaxation limit, the latter follows directly from the non-negativity of the fluxes.

4.2.1
Incoming edges

In case of multiple incoming edges a reasonable assumption is that the relation between the incoming flux from a given edge to the total incoming flux in the coupling node is inherited from the traces. Since the variable
v
determines the flux of the primary variable
u
this is stated as

\frac{v_{R}^{m}}{\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{R}^{k}}=\frac{v_{0}^{m}}{\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{0}^{k}}\quad\text{for all }m\in\delta^{-},

(60)

provided that both
\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{0}^{k}\neq 0
and
\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{R}^{k}\neq 0
. This can be rewritten as the following conditions on the coupling data

\left(\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}\setminus\{m\}}v_{0}^{k}\right)v_{R}^{m}-\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}\setminus\{m\}}v_{0}^{m}\,v_{R}^{k}=0\quad\text{for all }m\in\delta^{-}.

(61)

It is sufficient to impose (
60
) for all but one incoming edges, then still, by a summation argument, it follows for all incoming edges. Thus to account for assumption (
60
), the parameters for
\ell=1,\dots,N^{-}-1
in (
55c
) can be chosen as
\beta_{\ell}^{\ell}=\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}\setminus\{\ell\}}v_{0}^{k}
,
\beta_{k}^{\ell}=-v_{0}^{m}
for
k\in\delta^{-}\setminus\{\ell\}
,
\beta_{k}^{\ell}=0
for
k\in\delta^{+}
and
r^{\ell}=0
. These trace dependent parameters lead to a nonlinear Riemann solver (
51
), whose evaluation only requires the assembly of (
59
) and the solution of the systems (
58
). In case
\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{0}^{k}\neq 0
the system (
55
) (ignoring
\ell\geq N^{-}
) has maximal rank. To handle the case
\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}v_{0}^{k}=0
, we can regularize (
60
) by adding a small constant
\epsilon>0
to both denominators. To account for this regularization in the parameters, we add
\epsilon
to
\beta_{\ell}^{\ell}
and set
r^{\ell}=\epsilon v^{\ell}
for
\ell=1,\dots,N^{-}-1
. This way the Riemann solver can only assign nonzero incoming fluxes in the edge
k=N^{-}
if all corresponding incoming traces are zero.

4.2.2
Outgoing edges

The distribution of the fluxes among the outgoing edges in a network is often described by a distribution matrix, see e.g.
[
23
]
.
This matrix is comprised of rates that describe how the flux from any incoming edge is distributed to the outgoing edges. Similarly, we impose by

\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}\alpha^{m}_{k}v^{k}_{R}=v^{m}_{L}

(62)

that the flux that enters the coupling node from edge
k\in\delta^{-}
is distributed to edge
m\in\delta^{+}
with rate
\alpha^{m}_{k}
. The rates are chosen such that
0\leq\alpha^{m}_{k}\leq 1
and

\sum_{m\in\delta^{+}}\alpha^{m}_{k}=1\quad\text{for all }k\in\delta^{-}.

(63)

Similar to condition (
60
) in case of incoming edges, condition (
62
) only needs to be imposed to all but one outgoing edges, then it also follows for the remaining outgoing edge due to (
53a
) and (
63
). Thus, if we set the parameters in (
55
) for all
\ell=N^{-},\dots,N-2
to
\beta_{k}^{\ell}=\alpha^{\ell}_{k}
for
k\in\delta^{-}
,
\beta^{\ell}_{\ell+1}=-1
,
\beta_{k}^{\ell}=0
for
k\in\delta^{+}\setminus\{\ell+1\}
and
r^{\ell}=0
, condition (
62
) holds for
m\in\delta^{+}
. When we further use the suggested parameters accounting for the incoming edges as discussed above, system (
55
) has full rank and the Riemann solver (
51
) is well-defined.

4.3
Central Scheme for Networks of Scalar Conservation Laws

A scheme for the scalar network (
1
) is obtained by a straightforward generalization of the steps in Sections
3.2.1
–
3.2.5
and taking into account the generalized Riemann solver at the coupling node introduced in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
. Unlike in Section
3.2
we do not substitute explicit formulas of coupling data into the scheme in the general network case as was done in (
32
). Instead each edge in the derivation is considered separately.

Hence, after discretizing system (
47
) in space by scheme (
21
), discretizing in time as in (
26
) and taking the relaxation limit we obtain a network generalization of scheme (
30
) that can be written in conservative form as

u_{j}^{k,n+1}=u_{j}^{k,n}-\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\left(F_{j+1/2}^{k,n}-F_{j-1/2}^{k,n}\right)\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathcal{I}_{k},

(64a)

where
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\mathbb{Z}^{-}
if
k\in\delta^{-}
or
\mathcal{I}_{k}=\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
if
k\in\delta^{+}
. The numerical fluxes are given by

F_{j-1/2}^{k,n}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\,(f_{k}(u_{j}^{k,n})+f_{k}(u_{j-1}^{k,n}))-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}(u_{j}^{k,n}-u_{j-1}^{k,n})-{\mathcal{S}}^{k,n}_{j-1/2}&\text{if }j\,\nu_{k}>0,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}\,(v_{R}^{k,n}+f_{k}(u_{-1}^{k,n}))-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}(u_{R}^{k,n}-u_{-1}^{k,n})&\text{if }j=0\text{ and }k\in\delta^{-},\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}\,(f_{k}(u_{0}^{k,n})+v_{L}^{k,n})-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}(u_{0}^{k,n}-u_{L}^{k,n})&\text{if }j=0\text{ and }k\in\delta^{+}.\end{cases}

(64b)

If the included high order extension terms
{\mathcal{S}}^{k,n}_{j-1/2}
are set to zero, the first order scheme is obtained. To get the second order scheme, as derived in Section
3.2.5
for the 1-to-1 network, we set

{\mathcal{S}}^{k,n}_{j-1/2}=\frac{\Delta x}{2}(s_{j}^{k,n-}-s_{j-1}^{k,n+}),

(65)

where the slopes of the linear reconstructions are given by

s_{j}^{k,n\mp}=\operatorname{minmod}\left(2\frac{w^{k,n\mp}_{j}-w^{k,n\mp}_{j-1}}{\Delta x},~\frac{w^{k,n\mp}_{j+1}-w^{k,n\mp}_{j-1}}{2\,\Delta x},~2\frac{w^{k,n\mp}_{j+1}-w^{k,n\mp}_{j}}{\Delta x}\right),

(66)

w^{k,n\mp}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}f_{k}(u^{k,n}_{j})\mp\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}u^{k,n}_{j}
and the minmod operator (
37
). Coupling data is obtained by applying the Riemann solver (
51
) and setting

\mathcal{RS}_{\text{rel}}(u_{-1}^{1,n},f_{1}(u_{-1}^{1,n}),\dots,u_{0}^{N,n},f_{N}(u_{0}^{N,n}))\eqcolon(u_{R}^{1,n},v_{R}^{1,n},\dots,u_{L}^{N,n},v_{L}^{N,n}).

(67)

The form (
64a
) does not imply mass conservation at the coupling node, which we instead analyze by considering the incoming and outgoing numerical fluxes and the coupling data. Taking into account (
11
) in (
64b
) for
j=0
, it follows that the numerical fluxes coincide with the fluxes obtained by the Riemann solver, i.e.,

F^{k,n}_{-1/2}=v_{R}^{k,n}\quad\text{for }k\in\delta^{-},\quad F^{k,n}_{-1/2}=v_{L}^{k,n}\quad\text{for }k\in\delta^{+}.

(68)

Thus the following result is a consequence of (
53a
) and (
68
).

Proposition 5

(Conservation property of the central scheme)
.

The central scheme for the scalar network (
1
) given by (
64
) and coupling data (
67
), (
51
) is conservative in the coupling node, i.e., it holds

\sum_{k\in\delta^{-}}F_{-1/2}^{k,n}=\sum_{k\in\delta^{+}}F_{-1/2}^{k,n}\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(69)

It is further conservative on the full network in the case
f_{1}(0)=f_{2}(0)=\dots=f_{N}(0)=0
and on bounded networks, where all edges that connect to the node are bounded and zero-flux boundary conditions are imposed.

errors shown in Table

confirm the expected orders of convergence, i.e., first order in the central scheme and second order in the MUSCL variants. The presence of the coupling node in the schemes central MUSCL and central MUSCL TVD only slightly reduces the accuracy when compared to the uncoupled MUSCL scheme and does not interfere in the convergence order in

5.2
Traffic Flow

The second numerical experiment is concerned with a traffic scenario and imposes the Lighthill-Whitham-Richards (LWR) model
[
44
,
50
]

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}\left(u\left(1-\frac{u}{u_{\text{max}}}\right)\right)=0

(72)

on the edges of a 2-to-1 network. In more details, we consider the scalar problem (
1
) with two incoming edges (
N^{-}=2
) and one outgoing edge (
N^{+}=1
). We assume that the outgoing edge has larger capacity than the incoming ones and allows for higher traffic densities. This is reflected in the flux functions, which we set

f_{1}(u)=f_{2}(u)=u(1-u),\quad f_{3}(u)=u\left(1-\frac{u}{1.2}\right).

Coupling conditions for traffic models have been a topic of high interest, see e.g.,
[
23
,
26
]
. We are interested how the common
flow maximization
approach for coupling of (
72
) on networks compares to the coupling model implied by the presented scheme.

We sketch how coupling data is obtained according to flow maximization on 2-to-1 networks. Hereby we focus on the computation of the fluxes
v^{1}_{R}=f_{1}(u^{1}_{R})
,
v^{2}_{R}=f_{2}(u^{2}_{R})
and
v^{3}_{L}=f_{3}(u^{3}_{L})
. To constitute admissible boundary data these fluxes need to satisfy the demand and supply conditions, which impose upper bounds of the form

v^{1}_{R}\leq d_{1}(u_{0}^{1}),\quad v^{2}_{R}\leq d_{2}(u_{0}^{2}),\quad v^{3}_{L}\leq s_{3}(u_{0}^{3})

(73)

for details see
[
23
]
.
Coupling fluxes are then chosen maximal under constraints given by (
73
) and the Kirchhoff conditon (
53a
). Two cases can occur.
In the so called
free flow
case, where
d_{1}(u_{0}^{1})+d_{2}(u_{0}^{2})\leq s_{3}(u_{0}^{3})
, we take
v^{1}_{R}=d_{1}(u_{0}^{1})
,
v^{2}_{R}=d_{2}(u_{0}^{2})
and
v^{3}_{L}=d_{1}(u_{0}^{1})+d_{2}(u_{0}^{2})
.
The complimentary case is referred to as
congestion
. Here we set
v^{3}_{L}=s_{3}(u_{0}^{3})
and employ the right of way parameter
0\leq\beta\leq 1
to set
v_{R}^{1}=\beta s_{3}(u_{0}^{3})
and
v_{R}^{2}=(1-\beta)s_{3}(u_{0}^{3})
. If this leads to a violation of (
73
) for either
v^{1}_{R}
or
v^{2}_{R}
the affected coupling flux is chosen as the respective upper bound and its counterpart is computed from the Kirchhoff condition. This procedure defines a Riemann solver, which can be applied to cell averages of a numerical scheme. To account for flow maximization in numerical simulations we employed scheme (
64
) and replaced the fluxes at the coupling node by

F^{1,n}_{-1/2}=v^{1,n}_{R},\quad F^{2,n}_{-1/2}=v^{2,n}_{R},\quad F^{3,n}_{-1/2}=v^{3,n}_{L},

where the coupling fluxes were computed from the trace data
u_{0}^{1,n}
,
u_{0}^{2,n}
and
u_{0}^{3,n}
as above.

In contrast, coupling data in the central scheme was obtained from the linear systems (
58
). The parameters in system (
55
) were chosen so that (
60
) was satisfied, i.e.,
\beta^{1}_{1}=v^{2}_{0}
,
\beta^{1}_{2}=-v_{0}^{1}
and
\beta^{1}_{3}=r^{1}=0
.
In the numerical computations for both coupling models we used
m=200
cells on each edge and assumed
\lambda=1
. We further imposed zero-flux boundary conditions on the incoming edges and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions on the outgoing edge.

5.3
Coupled Two-Phase Flow Model

Lastly, we apply our approach to the Buckley–Leverett equation
[
11
]
, a simple model of two-phase flow. Given a mixture of water and oil in a tube of porous media, the water fraction
u
in the model is governed by a scalar conservation law with the non-convex flux function

f(u)=\frac{u^{2}}{u^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-u)^{2}}.

(74)

Again, we consider a 2-to-1 network and impose the model on its edges by taking the flux functions
f_{1}=f_{2}=f_{3}=f
. In a numerical experiment we reproduce a scenario, in which water is pumped into two tubes in order to displace oil and enforce its outflow through a third tube. To this end we use the initial data

u^{0,1}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x\leq-0.5,\\
0&\text{if }x>-0.5\end{cases},\quad u^{0,2}=0.16,\quad u^{0,3}=0

(75)

and homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions at the edges. The numerical solution computed by the central MUSCL scheme employing
m=300
cells per edge,
CFL=0.49
and
\lambda=2.5
is shown in Figure
8
. In the first incoming edge a shock wave is formed that is followed by a rarefaction wave and passes through the coupling node to the outgoing edge, where it interacts with a second shock wave originating from the second incoming edge. We emphasize that our numerical approach resolved these network dynamics without analysis of the underlying complex (due to the non-convexity of the flux function) Riemann problem.
