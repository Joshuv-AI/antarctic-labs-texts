A Revisit to the ABS H2 Equation
Aye Aye CHO, Maebel MESFUN and Da-Jun ZHANG

Abstract
In this paper we revisit the Adler–Bobenko–Suris H2 equation. The H2 equation is linearly related to the S ( 0 , 0 ) S^{(0,0)} and S ( 1 , 0 ) S^{(1,0)} variables in the Cauchy matrix scheme. We elaborate the coupled quad-system of S ( 0 , 0 ) S^{(0,0)} and S ( 1 , 0 ) S^{(1,0)} in terms of their 3-dimensional consistency, Lax pair, bilinear form and continuum limits. It is shown that S ( 1 , 0 ) S^{(1,0)} itself satisfies a 9-point lattice equation and in continuum limit S ( 1 , 0 ) S^{(1,0)} is related to the eigenfunction in the Lax pair of the Korteweg–de Vries equation.

Introduction

In the past two decades the research of discrete integrable systems has undergone a true development
(e.g., see
[
7
]
and the references therein).
The property called multi-dimensional consistency (MDC) plays a central role
in understanding integrability of discrete systems
[
1
,
3
,
13
,
16
]
.
The MDC means a lattice equation can be consistently embedded into a higher dimensional system.
For quadrilateral equations, this property is geometrically described as the
consistency around a cube (CAC).
Adler, Bobenko and Suris (ABS) classified all affine linear quadrilateral equations
that are consistent-around-cube (CAC),
D_{4}
symmetric and possess the so-called tetrahedron property
[
1
]
.
In their full list there are amazingly 9 equations: namely,
H1, H2, H3
δ
, A1
δ
, A2, Q1
δ
, Q2, Q3
δ
and Q4.
Most of the equations in the ABS list are (or related to) equations known before the list was found.
One of the new equation is H2, which is also special because only for H2 and H3
δ

their continuous counterpart are not found in terms of Miwa’s coordinates
[
18
]
.

H2 equation reads
[
1
]

\displaystyle\big(y-\widehat{\widetilde{y}}\big)\big(\widetilde{y}-\widehat{y}\big)+\big(p^{2}-q^{2}\big)\big(y+\widetilde{y}+\widehat{y}+\widehat{\widetilde{y}}-p^{2}-q^{2}\big)=0,

(1.1)

where
p
,
q
are spacing parameters in
n
- and
m
-direction, respectively, and
tilde-hat notations stand for shifts in
n
- and
m
-direction (see equation (
2.1
) for definition).
In this paper, we will revisit H2 equation with the help of H1
(i.e., the lattice potential Korteweg–de Vries equation (lpKdV)
[
15
]
)

\displaystyle\big(z-\widehat{\widetilde{z}}\big)\big(\widehat{z}-\widetilde{z}\big)=p^{2}-q^{2}.

(1.2)

Note that H1 has a background solution
z_{0}=pn+qm
. By removing the background from the equation,
i.e., introducing
z=z_{0}-u
so that
u
has a zero background, the resulting equation
[
15
]

\displaystyle\big(p+q+u-\widehat{\widetilde{u}}\big)\big(p-q+\widehat{u}-\widetilde{u}\big)=p^{2}-q^{2}

(1.3)

is ready to yield the potential KdV equation in its continuum limit.
H2 has also a background solution
y_{0}=z_{0}^{2}

[
8
]
but it is hard to remove it for H2 equation.
This is why so far the continuous counterpart of H2 is unknown.

The main contents of the paper are the following.
We will start from the following relations obtained from
Cauchy matrix approach (see equation (
2.6
)),

\displaystyle w+\widetilde{w}=p\widetilde{u}-pu+u\widetilde{u},

(1.4a)

\displaystyle w+\widehat{w}=q\widehat{u}-qu+u\widehat{u},

(1.4b)

which provide a non-auto Bäcklund transformation (BT) between H1 and H2 with

\displaystyle y=z_{0}^{2}-2z_{0}u+2w,

(1.5a)

\displaystyle z=z_{0}-u.

(1.5b)

From (
1.4
) we will derive a quadrilateral coupled system
(in Section
3.1
)

\displaystyle\big(p+q+u-\widehat{\widetilde{u}}\big)\big(\widetilde{w}-\widehat{w}\big)-(p-q)\big(w-\widehat{\widetilde{w}}\big)=0,

(1.6a)

\displaystyle\big(p-q+\widehat{u}-\widetilde{u}\big)\big(w-\widehat{\widetilde{w}}\big)-(p+q)\big(\widetilde{w}-\widehat{w}\big)=0,

(1.6b)

which is CAC and therefore is integrable.
As a consequence we will have a one-component 9-point equation of
w
,

\displaystyle(p+q)\left(\frac{\widetilde{w}-\widehat{w}}{w-\widehat{\widetilde{w}}}+\frac{\widehat{\widehat{\widetilde{w}}}-\widehat{\widetilde{\widetilde{w}}}}{\widehat{\widetilde{w}}-\widehat{\widehat{\widetilde{\widetilde{w}}}}}\right)+(p-q)\left(\frac{\widehat{w}-\widehat{\widehat{\widetilde{w}}}}{\widehat{\widehat{w}}-\widehat{\widetilde{w}}}+\frac{\widetilde{w}-\widehat{\widetilde{\widetilde{w}}}}{\widetilde{\widetilde{w}}-\widehat{\widetilde{w}}}\right)=0.

(1.7)

Considering the relation (
1.5
)
we call (
1.6
) or (
1.7
) an alternative of H2.
In this paper we will focus on the coupled system (
1.6
) to investigate its
integrability, bilinear form, continuum limits, etc.
It turns out that the continuous counterpart of (
1.6
) consists of
the potential KdV equation and the time-part in the Lax pair of the (potential) KdV equation.
Note that with some transformations the system (
1.6
)
will be equivalent to a
\mathrm{H}1\times\mathrm{H}2
two-component system
which is first derived in
[
11
]
(see equation (
3.9
)).

The paper is arranged as follows.
In Section
2
we recall the Cauchy matrix approach and derive the BT (
1.4
).
Then we derive the coupled quad-system (
1.6
) and investigate its 3D consistency and Lax pairs in Section
3
,
and present its bilinear form and Casoratian solutions in Section
4
.
After that, Section
5
consists of the investigation of the continuous counterpart of the quad-system (
1.6
),
including their continuum limits and Cauchy matrix approach in continuous case,
which leads to a connection between
S^{(1,0)}
equation and the KdV equation.
Finally, concluding remarks are given in Section
6
.
There is an appendix section to explore higher order equations in the continuum limits.

2.2
Cauchy matrix approach to (

To derive the coupled system (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{r}{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\rm T},

Let us sketch some known results related to this equation.

Proposition 2.1
.

For given
\boldsymbol{r}
,
\boldsymbol{c}
and
\boldsymbol{K}

\boldsymbol{K}
and

do not share eigenvalues, then

determined by the equation (

[

In addition, explicit formulae of

have been constructed in
[
22
]

with respect to canonical forms of

.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
22
]
)
.

Introduce

\displaystyle S^{(i,j)}=\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm T}{\boldsymbol{K}}^{j}(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{M})^{-1}\boldsymbol{K}^{i}\boldsymbol{r},\qquad i,j\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.3)

where the elements
\boldsymbol{r}
,
\boldsymbol{c}
,
\boldsymbol{K}
and
\boldsymbol{M}
satisfy the Sylvester equation (

)
and

is the

For given
\boldsymbol{r}
and

and any matrix similar to it

\displaystyle S^{(i,j)}=S^{(j,i)}

The Cauchy matrix approach is a method to construct and study integrable equations
by means of the Sylvester-type equations.
In this approach integrable equations are presented as closed forms of
S^{(i,j)}
and its shifts (or derivatives).
It is first systematically used in
[
14
]
to investigate ABS equations and later
developed in
[

22
]

To derive coupled system (
1.4

\displaystyle(p\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}=(p\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})\boldsymbol{r},\qquad(q\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}=(q\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})\boldsymbol{r},

and let
\boldsymbol{c}
be a constant column vector, where
\boldsymbol{K}
is a constant

Next, making use of the results obtained in
[

, we have the following shift relations for
\boldsymbol{M}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}(p\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})-(p\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})\boldsymbol{M}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{r}}\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm T},

\displaystyle(p\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}-\boldsymbol{M}(p\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})=\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm T},

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}(q\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})-(q\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{K})\boldsymbol{M}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm T},

\displaystyle(q\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}-\boldsymbol{M}(q\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{K})=\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{c}^{\rm T},

from which one arrives at (see equations (27) in
[

\displaystyle p\widetilde{S}^{(i,j)}-\widetilde{S}^{(i,j+1)}=pS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i+1,j)}-\widetilde{S}^{(i,0)}S^{(0,j)},

\displaystyle q\widehat{S}^{(i,j)}-\widehat{S}^{(i,j+1)}=qS^{(i,j)}+S^{(i+1,j)}-\widehat{S}^{(i,0)}S^{(0,j)}.

Now, taking
(i,j)=(0,0)
in the above recurrence relation, defining

\displaystyle u=S^{(0,0)},\qquad w=S^{(1,0)},

(2.5)

and making use of the symmetric property (
2.4

we have

\displaystyle w+\widetilde{w}=p\widetilde{u}-pu+u\widetilde{u},

(2.6a)

\displaystyle w+\widehat{w}=q\widehat{u}-qu+u\widehat{u},

(2.6b)

i.e., the coupled system (
1.4

2.3
Consistent triplet

Suppose that we have a coupled system

\displaystyle f\big(u,v,\widetilde{u},\widetilde{v},p\big)=0,\qquad f\big(u,v,\widehat{u},\widehat{v},q\big)=0.

(2.7)

The compatibility with respect to
v

(
i.e.,
\widehat{\widetilde{v}}=\widetilde{\widehat{v}}
)
gives rise to a quadrilateral equation of
u

\displaystyle Q\big(u,\widetilde{u},\widehat{u},\widehat{\widetilde{u}};p,q\big)=0,

(2.8)

while the compatibility for

yields a quadrilateral equation of

\displaystyle G\big(v,\widetilde{v},\widehat{v},\widehat{\widetilde{v}};p,q\big)=0.

(2.9)

When this happens we say that equations (
2.7
), (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) compose a consistent triplet.
Note that such a triplet can be extended to the lattice systems defined on larger stencils,
not necessary to be restricted to the quadrilateral case.
In such a triplet, equation (
2.7
) acts as a BT to connect the other two equations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
),
and due to the compatibility, any pair of solutions
(u,v)
of (
2.7
) will provide solutions
to (
2.8
) and (
2.9
).
Consistent triplets have played a useful role in constructing rational solutions for a number of
quadrilateral equations
[
21
]
.

Using the transformation (

2.6

\displaystyle y+\widetilde{y}-p^{2}=2z\widetilde{z},

\displaystyle y+\widehat{y}-q^{2}=2z\widehat{z}.

Note that (

[

]

By checking compatibility of

and

Thus we have the following.

Proposition 2.3
.

H2
equation (

),
H1
(
1.2
) and the system (

This means that solving (

Based on this fact in Section

and get a bilinear form for H2 which is simpler than the one given in

Note that the fact of (

Appendix A

In

In their scheme, discrete equations are handled in the (skew) coordinates
