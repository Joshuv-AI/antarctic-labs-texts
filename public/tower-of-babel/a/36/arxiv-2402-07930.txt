Optimal reconstruction of water-waves from noisy pressure measurements at the seabed
Joris Labarbe, Alexandre Vieira, Didier Clamond

Abstract
. We consider the problem of recovering the surface wave profile from noisy bottom pressure measurements with ( a priori unknown) arbitrary pressure at the surface. Without noise, the direct approach developed in [ clamond2023steady ] provides an effective way to recover the sea surface. However, the assumption of analyticity for the measurement renders this method inefficient in the presence of noise. Therefore, we introduce an optimisation procedure based on the minimisation of a distance between a recovered bottom pressure and its measurement. Such method proves to be well-designed to handle perturbed signals. We illustrate the effectiveness of this approach in the recovery of gravity-capillary waves from unfiltered noisy data.



1.
Introduction

Monitoring the surface of the oceans is a timely concern for climatic and environmental considerations, especially in coastal regions where large waves represent a risk when approaching the shoreline.
Currently, an efficient way of monitoring the wave motion is lacking, as most current techniques display a poor accuracy in their measurements.
For this reason, in the second half of the last century, some scientists emitted the idea of sending probes at the seabed to measure the pressure and reconstruct the surface profile from these data
[
BTT69
]
.
This procedure allows to determine the shape of water-waves without intrusive observations, although it necessitates to solve an inverse problem within an unknown domain.
Such problem is notoriously challenging, notably because of its ill-posedness (any disturbance grows exponentially from the bottom to the free surface).

Historically, the first attempt to solve this problem relied on hydrostatic theory and assumed the surface elevation as being proportional to the weight of the water column.
Later, from a linear approximation of the problem, an explicit expression for the surface was given in terms of a certain pressure transfer function
[
LW85
]
.
Noticeably, a recent work derived an exact formula for steady rotational waves using linear theory
[
HT16
]
.
However, both these assumptions were not suited to describe large waves or of non-trivial profile (i.e. not sinusoidal).
It was only recently that the first results on the full nonlinear problem were obtained
[
O12
]
.
This study resulted in a nonlocal integro-differential equation for the surface elevation that was highly difficult to solve in practice without considering asymptotic limits.
Slightly after this work, an efficient procedure for the recovery of nonlinear steady water-waves was formulated
[
C13
,
CC13
]
.
This approach relies on the assumption that bottom pressure can be analytically continued as a holomorphic function, allowing to express the surface elevation as a solution of an algebraic equation.
Numerical investigation of this method was done for extreme waves
[
CC13
]
and it was rigorously proved to converge in the case of steady surface gravity waves from a mere fixed-point algorithm
[
CH20
]
.
Subsequently, this theory was extended to handle more complex configurations, such as the presence of a linear shear current
[
CLH23
]
, the possibility of overhanging waves
[
LC23
]
or the influence of arbitrary pressure at the surface
[
clamond2023steady
]
.
We note the proof for the existence of nonlinear water-waves with constant vorticity and overturning wave profiles
[
CSV11
,
CSV16
]
.

The method originally developed in
[
CC13
]
consistently proved its effectiveness in terms of accuracy, computation time and accessibility, even considering technical difficulties (stagnation points, limiting waves, capillary effects, etc.).
However, the formulation inherently depends on the analyticity of the measurement, which is not the case in real-life observations where the presence of external noise is inevitable.
Moreover, since we are performing most of the calculations in the complex plane, then it prohibits us to directly extend this approach to the three-dimensional configuration.
For all these reasons, we introduce a new and innovative formulation, based on an optimisation method, to solve this constrained inverse problem with noise in the measurements.

To the best of authors’ knowledge, surface wave recovery within an optimisation framework has never been considered in the literature.
As it turns out, one can relate this reconstruction problem to an optimal control approach, where the goal is to find a set of parameters or functions minimising a given cost functional under some dynamical constraints.
(We refer interested readers to
[
cossu2014introduction
]
for an introduction on this subject.)
In the current inverse problem, the undetermined function is the surface profile and the cost functional will be the distance to the observation.
In this sense, this problem is close to some shape optimisation or topology optimisation (TO) configurations.
These problems consist in finding the optimal shape of an obstacle immersed in a moving fluid (see
[
alexandersen2020review
,
dbouk2017review
]
and references therein for an overview on the numerical resolution of TO problems applied to several physical settings).
Although it yields satisfactory results in general, the main drawback of the shape optimisation problem comes from its numerical complexity.
This is because the underlying dynamical constraints are usually discretised using a Finite Element method, which relies on a prescribed mesh adapted to the shape of the obstacle.
In this setting, the shape of the immersed obstacle is updated at each iteration, leading to an expensive recalculation of the mesh
[
allaire2014shape
]
.
Thus, all techniques avoiding this costly procedure necessarily enhance the formulation.

The method we consider in this work relies on a predefined discretisation of some analytical expressions we derived in an earlier work, from a boundary integral method
[
LC23
]
.
Combining this analytic preprocessing within the optimisation framework has never been explored before and appears to be a suitable and innovative approach for the surface reconstruction problem.
Moreover, the algorithm we develop is easily extendable to a broad range of situations (e.g. adding further physical assumptions or noise in the measurements) and yields an excellent agreement with the sought solution (comparable to the results obtained in
[
clamond2023steady
]
using the holomorphic extension).

This article is articulated around the presentation, application and comparison of two different methods to reconstruct surface waves when considering noise or not.
Section
2
introduces the equations of motion and boundary conditions for the system of interest.
Then, sections
3
and
4
describe in details the direct and optimisation approaches, respectively, and how to implement them numerically in an efficient way.
A numerical example is treated in section
5
to explore and compare thoroughly these methods.
Finally, we discuss about the results we obtained and on the eventual improvements and future extensions in section
.

2.
Problem statement

We investigate the motion of a steady wave travelling at a constant phase speed
c
within a fluid layer with a free surface.
The bottom of the layer being flat, we fix ourselves in the Galilean frame of reference moving with the wave, so that motion appears stationary for the observer.
The fluid is considered inviscid, irrotational and incompressible; we assume the surface and seabed to be impermeable.
These boundaries are located at
y=\eta(x)
for the surface and
y=-0pt
for the seabed, with
y
being the vertical coordinate and
0pt
a constant depth.
We assume the streamwise direction to have an infinite extension.
Therefore, the domain of definition is given by
\Omega=\{(x,y):x\in\mathds{R},-0pt\leqslant y\leqslant\eta(x)\}
.
When the motion is periodic, we introduce the wave period
L=2{\pi}/k
(the limit
k\to 0^{+}
corresponding to a solitary wave) where
k
is a spatial wavenumber.
Thus, we define an Eulerian averaging operator to fix the mean water level at
y=0
, i.e.

(1)

\left<\eta\right>\vcentcolon=\frac{k}{2{\pi}}\int_{\mathcal{P}}\eta(x)\mathrm{d}x=0,

where the path of integration is
\mathcal{P}=[-{\pi}/k,{\pi}/k]
.

In the following, we use the subscripts ‘s’ and ‘b’ to denote the restriction of fields at the surface and the bottom, respectively.
Alternatively, the subscript
x
is used to express horizontal derivatives (e.g.
\eta_{x}\vcentcolon=\mathrm{d}\eta/\mathrm{d}x)
.
We consider the pressure at the surface as an undetermined function of the
x
-coordinate, entering the equations through the dynamical condition (supplemented with kinematic condition).
The velocity field
\bm{u}=(u,v)
is governed by the steady Euler equations (expressing the conservation of mass and linear momentum) with associated boundary conditions.
It yields

(2a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{\cdot}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{in}\quad\Omega

(2b)

\displaystyle\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+\bm{\nabla}p+\bm{g}

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{in}\quad\Omega

(2c)

\displaystyle\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}-\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{\nabla}\eta

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{at}\quad y=\eta(x),

(2d)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle={p}_{\mathrm{s}}(x)\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{at}\quad y=\eta(x),

(2e)

\displaystyle\bm{u}\bm{\cdot}\bm{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\textrm{at}\quad y=-d,

where
p
is a relative pressure with zero mean-value at the surface (i.e.
\langle{p}_{\mathrm{s}}\rangle=0
) and scaled with the constant background density
\rho_{0}
.
The unit normal vector
\bm{n}
is directed outward and
\bm{g}=(0,g)
represents the restoring gravity force, with
g
the acceleration due to gravity (acting downwards).
By integrating equation (
2b
), we recover the well-known Bernoulli principle (expressing the conservation of mechanical energy), leading (in the irrotational case) to

(3)

2(p+gy)+u^{2}+v^{2}=B,

where
B
is the Bernoulli constant.
From this principle and using the mean operator (
1
), we establish some important relations, notably that
\langle{u}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}+{v}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\rangle=B
and
\langle{p}_{\mathrm{b}}\rangle=g0pt
.
Finally, we introduce the complex velocity
w(z)\vcentcolon=u(x,y)-\mathrm{i}v(x,y)
that is holomorphic in the
z
-coordinate (with
z\vcentcolon=x+\mathrm{i}y
).
We note that the free surface path is given by the curvilinear abscissa
{z}_{\mathrm{s}}=x+\mathrm{i}\eta
whereas the solid boundary is described by
{z}_{\mathrm{b}}=x-\mathrm{i}0pt
.

The principal objective of this work is to recover the surface wave profile
\eta(x)
, the Bernoulli constant
B
and the surface pressure
{p}_{\mathrm{s}}(x)
from a given pressure observation
{p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}(x)
at the seabed.
We assume either the wave period
L
or the wavenumber
k
to be a known parameter.
This inverse problem is notoriously difficult to solve due to the strong nonlinearities and its ill-posed nature (i.e. disturbances are exponentially growing to the surface).
Our first approach, developed in a series of articles
[
C13
,
CC13
,
CH20
,
clamond2023steady
,
CLH23
,
LC23
]
, is a direct approach set in the physical space and where analytical expressions (integro-differential in general) are given in terms of the unknown surface wave profile and surface pressure.

3.
Numerical procedure: direct approach

3.1.
Cauchy integral formula

Computing steady surface waves from a boundary integral formula is an idea initiated in a seminal work by Da Silva and Peregrine
[
DSP88
]
.
Hereafter, we present a similar procedure to recover an implicit expression for the surface elevation using complex analysis
[
LC23
]
.
We start by giving the Cauchy integral formula, written for a holomorphic function
\Upxi(z)
, in its classical form

(4)

\mathrm{i}\vartheta\Upxi(z)=\text{P.V.}\oint\frac{\Upxi(z^{\prime})}{z^{\prime}-z}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}{{z}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\prime}-z}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\left(1+\mathrm{i}\eta_{x}^{\prime}\right){\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-z},

with primes denoting the dependence on the dummy variable (e.g.
\Upxi^{\prime}\vcentcolon=\Upxi(x^{\prime})
).
The argument
\vartheta
is substituted by
\{2\pi,0,\pi\}
when the coordinate
z
lies respectively inside, outside and at the smooth boundary of the domain
\Omega
.

Whenever the holomorphic function is purely real at the bottom boundary (i.e.
\operatorname{Im}\{{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{b}}\}=0
), we may use the method of images (also called Schwarz reflection principle
[
MorseFeshbach1953
]
) on (
4
) to obtain

(5)

\displaystyle\vartheta\Upxi(z)=k\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{0}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k({z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-z)}\}{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+k\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{0}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(z-{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+2\mathrm{i}0pt)}\}{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}+\pi\left<{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}x}+{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right>,

where
\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{\nu}
is the
\nu
th polylogarithm
[
LC23
]
and
\widetilde{z}
the complex conjugate of
z
.
The integration path is given (in the complex plane) by
\mathcal{C}=\{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}(x)\ :\ x\in[-\pi/k,\pi/k]\}\subset\mathbb{C}
.

Equation (
5
) possesses a strong (polar) singularity causing technical difficulties when computing the integral numerically.
Thus, we use the integrated form of the polylogarithm to replace the polar term with a logarithmic singularity
[
LC23
]
.
We obtain

(6)

\displaystyle\vartheta\Upxi(z)=\mathrm{i}\!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\partial_{z}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{1}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k({z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-z)}\}{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}\!\int_{\mathcal{C}}\partial_{z}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{1}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(z-{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+2\mathrm{i}0pt)}\}{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}+\pi\left<{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}x}+{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right>,

Computing the latter expression at the free surface reduces to

(7)

\displaystyle{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\!\left[\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{1}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k({z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-z)}\}{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{1}\{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(z-{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+2\mathrm{i}0pt)}\}{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}\right]+\frac{\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}{2}\left<{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}x}+{\widetilde{\Upxi}}_{\mathrm{s}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right>.

3.2.
Expressions for computing steady surface waves and bottom pressure

From Bernoulli’s principle (
3
), the complex velocity at the surface
{w}_{\mathrm{s}}
is given explicitly by

(8)

{w}_{\mathrm{s}}=\sigma(\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}/\mathrm{d}x)\sqrt{(B-2{p}_{\mathrm{s}}-2g\eta)/|\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}/\mathrm{d}x|^{2}},

where
\sigma=\mp 1
denotes travelling waves propagating along or against the current, respectively.

Let us consider the holomorphic function
\Upxi=w+c
(
c
being an arbitrary definition of the phase speed).
The left-hand side of (
7
) follows directly from (
8
) as

(9)

{\Upxi}_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}=\left(\omega h+c\right)\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}+\sigma\sqrt{(B-2{p}_{\mathrm{s}}-2g\eta)/|\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}/\mathrm{d}x|^{2}}\mathrm{d}x,

where
h\vcentcolon=\eta+0pt
is the wave height.
We note that the radicand is purely real since
B\geqslant 2||{p}_{\mathrm{s}}+g\eta||_{\infty}
for all waves (according to Bernoulli’s relation).

After integration of expression (
7
) – retaining the imaginary part only – we obtain an equation for the computation of the free surface

(10)

\frac{\omega\/\eta^{2}}{2}-\eta\left<{u}_{\mathrm{s}}\left|\\
ud{z}_{\mathrm{s}}/\mathrm{d}x\right|^{2}\right>-\frac{\sigma}{2\pi}\int_{\mathcal{P}}\operatorname{Re}\{\mathcal{L}_{1}\}\sqrt{(B-2{p}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-2g\eta^{\prime})/|\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}/\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}|^{2}}\mathrm{d}\/x^{\prime}-K=0,

where
K
is a constant of integration, recovered by enforcing the mean-level condition
[
LC23
]
.
For the sake of brevity, we also introduced the following notation

\mathcal{L}_{\nu}\vcentcolon=\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{\nu}\!\big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k({z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-{z}_{\mathrm{s}})}\big]-\mbox{L{\scriptsize{}}}_{\nu}\!\big[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k({z}_{\mathrm{s}}-{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}+2\mathrm{i}0pt)}\big].

As one can notice, expression (
10
) is not dependent on the choice of phase speed
c
, as expected from the Galilean invariance of the problem.

Equation (
5
) and the Schwarz reflection principle also let us find an expression for the bottom velocity.
Consider
\Upxi=w
as the holomorphic function in (
5
) and evaluate the expression at the bottom (with now
\vartheta=2{\pi}
).
Since
{u}_{\mathrm{b}}
is a real function of the horizontal coordinate, we use the method of images to recover its explicit form, which leads to the formula

(11)

{u}_{\mathrm{b}}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{i}k}{4\pi}\left[\int_{\mathcal{C}}\cot\!\left(k\frac{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-{z}_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}\right){w}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}-\int_{\mathcal{C}}\cot\!\left(k\frac{{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-{z}_{\mathrm{b}}-2\mathrm{i}0pt}{2}\right){\widetilde{w}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}\right].

This bottom velocity
{u}_{\mathrm{b}}
is used in Stokes’ first definition of the phase speed

(12)

c_{1}=-\left<{u}_{\mathrm{b}}\right>.

3.3.
Holomorphic functions and recovery formula

As done numerous time in previous articles
[
C13
,
CC13
,
CH20
,
clamond2023steady
,
CLH23
,
LC23
]
, we introduce a holomorphic
complex
pressure function
\mathfrak{P}(z)
by analytic continuation in the domain
\Omega
.
Using Bernoulli’s principle written for the complex velocity, it yields an expression for the complex pressure as

(13)

\mathfrak{P}(z)\vcentcolon=g0pt+\frac{B-w^{2}}{2}.

Similarly, we introduce the antiderivative of
\mathfrak{P}
defined by

(14)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}(z)\vcentcolon=\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\left[\mathfrak{P}(z^{\prime})-g0pt\right]\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{z_{0}}^{z}\left[B-w(z^{\prime})^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

where
z_{0}
is an arbitrary constant.

In practice, these holomorphic functions are easily recovered from the given pressure measurement by fitting the data over a suitable eigenbasis (one leading to the most accuracy with the lowest order of quadrature).
Assuming a periodic motion, we fit these data with a Fourier polynomial basis before performing an analytic continuation of the pressure field.
Evaluated at the surface, it yields

(15)

\mathfrak{P}(z)={p}_{\mathrm{b}}(z+\mathrm{i}0pt)\approx g0pt+\sum_{|n|>0}^{N}\mathfrak{p}_{n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk(z+\mathrm{i}d)}.

From this definition, we simply integrate once and recover the second holomorphic function as

(16)

{\mathfrak{Q}}_{\mathrm{s}}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\left[{\mathfrak{P}}_{\mathrm{s}}(x^{\prime})-g0pt\right]\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\approx\sum_{|n|>0}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{i}\mathfrak{p}_{n}}{nk}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-nka}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}nk(x+\mathrm{i}\eta)}}{\mathrm{e}^{nkd}},

where
a
is the amplitude of the wave at the crest (arbitrarily located at
x=0
).

In the context of the direct approach, we exploit a relation derived in a recent work as our general recovery formula
[
clamond2023steady
]
.
This expression (not involving any differential terms of
\eta
) is given by

(17)

\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{{\mathfrak{Q}}_{\mathrm{s}}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}\left[B-\left|B-2\left({\mathfrak{P}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-g0pt\right)\right|\right]\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

As one can notice, surface pressure has been eliminated from the recovery formula (
17
).
This is because the Cauchy–Riemann system gives us two equations (the real and complex parts) that can be combined to suppress either the surface pressure of the surface elevation
[
clamond2023steady
]
.
However, it is still necessary to fix Bernoulli’s constant by adding a direct measure (phase speed, wave height, etc.) or by assuming the physics at the surface (e.g. capillary or flexural effects).
For this work, we assume Stokes’s first definition of the phase speed to be a given parameter.
Thus, solving the inverse problem reduces to finding the zeros of the set of equations (
1
), (
12
) and (
17
).

3.4.
The Levenberg–Marquardt procedure

In practice, solving the inverse problem with a direct approach is fairly straightforward.
It only requires to introduce a quadrature for numerical integration (we choose the trapezoidal quadrature) and then insert the different expressions in almost any root-finding algorithm (e.g. the
fsolve
function in
Matlab
).
Numerically,
\eta=\{\eta_{i}\}_{i}
is a vector defined at these quadrature points.
Because this problem is notoriously challenging to solve, we decide to use a Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, which has shown its efficiency before (see
[
C13
,
CC13
,
CH20
,
clamond2023steady
,
CLH23
,
LC23
]
).

We quickly sketch this algorithm.
Solving the inverse problem directly (for the surface profile) can be summarised as finding the root of equation
\mathcal{E}(\eta)=0
for some function
\mathcal{E}
.
This is equivalent as minimising the functional
\mathcal{J}(\eta)\vcentcolon={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\|\mathcal{E}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\mathcal{E}\bm{\cdot}\mathcal{E}
.
There are two common ways for this, both being iterative methods.
Assume we are given an initial guess
\eta^{0}
with the overscript standing for the iteration step.
For any iteration
\ell
, we will denote
\mathcal{E}^{\ell}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{E}(\eta^{\ell})
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\vcentcolon=\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\eta}(\eta^{\ell})
the Jacobian matrix of
\mathcal{E}
computed at
\eta^{\ell}
.

-

The first method, the Gradient Descent (GD), consists in computing the negative gradient of the function
\mathcal{J}(\eta)
at
\eta^{\ell}
in order to compute
\eta^{\ell+1}
.
Introducing an increment
\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GD}}}
, expression for the gradient follows immediately

-\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\eta}(\eta^{\ell})=-\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\intercal}\mathcal{E}^{\ell}=\delta\eta_{{}_{{}_{\text{GD}}}},

with
\mathcal{A}^{\intercal}
denoting the transpose of a matrix
\mathcal{A}
and where
\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}/\partial\eta
can be approximated using finite differences.
This gradient is then used in an update formula as
\eta^{\ell+1}=\eta^{\ell}+\alpha\delta\eta_{{}_{{}_{\text{GD}}}}
, where
\alpha
is the length along the descent direction.

-

The second procedure, the Gauss–Newton (GN) method, originates from a Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{E}
at the first order. For some increment
\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}
, it yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\eta^{\ell}+\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}})\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\ell}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}\right)\bm{\cdot}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\ell}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\eta^{\ell})+\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}\bm{\cdot}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\intercal}\mathcal{E}^{\ell}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

We then minimise the function
\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\eta^{\ell}+\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}})
to find the optimal increment, which is equivalent to finding the root of
\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}}(\eta^{\ell}+\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}})=0
. After some algebraic manipulations, it gives the linear system

(18)

\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\intercal}\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GN}}}=-\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\intercal}\mathcal{E}^{\ell}=\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GD}}}.

The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm can be seen as an interpolation between the Gradient Descent and the Gauss–Newton method.
It consists in solving, instead of (
18
), the Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) update as:

(19)

\left[\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}\right)^{\intercal}\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}^{\ell}}{\partial\eta}+\lambda\mathcal{I}\right]\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{LM}}}=\delta\eta_{{}_{\text{GD}}},

where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity matrix and
\lambda
is a damping parameter.
A large value of
\lambda
results in an update closer to the Gradient Descent increment, while a small value of
\lambda
makes the update closer to the Gauss–Newton increment.
The damping parameter is updated at each iteration.
It usually starts large and becomes slower as the iterations converge towards the solution.
This procedure is used to stabilize the iterations when compared to the Gauss–Newton algorithm.
The interested reader can find a more thorough presentation of the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm in
[
bjorck1996numerical
]
.

4.
Numerical procedure: optimisation approach

4.1.
An optimisation formulation

The previous approach relies on the assumption that the pressure function can be analytically continued.
Without this assumption, we can formulate the problem of recovering the surface elevation with bottom pressure measurements within an optimisation frame.
We resume the computations from (
12
).
From Bernoulli’s principle, we note that given
\eta
,
{p}_{\mathrm{s}}
and
B
, we can compute the pressure at the seabed using the formula:

(20)

{p}_{\mathrm{b}}(x)=g0pt-\frac{B}{2}-\frac{k^{2}}{32{\pi}^{2}}\left[\int_{\mathcal{C}}\cot\!\left(k\frac{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-{z}_{\mathrm{b}}}{2}\right){w}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}{z}_{\mathrm{s}}-\int_{\mathcal{C}}\cot\!\left(k\frac{{\tilde{z}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}-{z}_{\mathrm{b}}-2\mathrm{i}0pt}{2}\right){\widetilde{w}}_{\mathrm{s}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{{z}_{\mathrm{s}}}^{\prime}\right]^{2}.

This procedure is sketched in figure
1
as an input–output process.

Input
\eta

4.2.
The augmented Lagrangian approach

In this section, we explain the augmented Lagrangian algorithm, that we use in order to solve the problem (
21
).
This is an iterative method that starts with an initial guess
(\eta^{0},{p}_{\mathrm{s}}^{0},B^{0})
and then, based on the distance
\|{p}_{\mathrm{b}}(\eta,{p}_{\mathrm{s}},B)-{p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{P})}^{2}
, will produce a new iteration
(\eta^{1},{p}_{\mathrm{s}}^{1},B^{1})
closer to the minimal solution.
The procedure is then iterated.
The main issue with (
21
) are the equality constraints that should be handled with a specific procedure.
A broad range of algorithms are specifically designed for that, such as the SQP method or the barrier methods; see
[
boyd2004convex
]
for a detailed review on the numerical algorithms to solve constrained optimisation problems.
In our context, we opted for the augmented Lagrangian technique because of its ability to transform constrained problems into an unconstrained one (with a penalty term).
We introduce in the following this algorithm in a generic framework.

Given functions
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
, suppose we would like to solve the generic optimisation problem

(22)

\min_{x}\ \mathcal{F}(x)\text{ s.t. }\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)=0,\ i\in\mathcal{J}.

where
\mathcal{J}
is some subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
The augmented Lagrangian algorithm offers a way to handle the constraint
\mathcal{G}(x)=0
.
It consists in taking an approximate Lagrange multipliers
\lambda^{\ell}_{i}
(in the sense of optimisation) and coefficients
\rho^{\ell}_{i}
to solve the optimisation problem

x^{\ell}\in\argmin_{x}\mathcal{F}(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}\lambda_{i}^{\ell}\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}\frac{\rho_{i}^{\ell}}{2}|\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)|^{2}.

This procedure turns the constraint problem (
22
) into an unconstrained one, with the same cost function
\mathcal{F}
but adding the scalar product
\lambda_{i}\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)
and the quadratic penalization term
{\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\rho_{i}^{\ell}|\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)|^{2}
.
This unconstrained minimisation problem, which will induce inner iterations, can then be solved numerically using methods for unconstrained minimisation problems ; we used the interior point method as implemented by the function
fmincon
in
Matlab
(see again
[
boyd2004convex
]
for more details on this method).
Based on
x^{\ell}
and for some tolerance
\mu^{\ell}
, the multiplier or the coefficients are updated. For each
i\in\mathcal{J}
, it follows that

-

If
|\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)|\leq\mu^{\ell}
, then
\lambda_{i}^{\ell+1}=\lambda_{i}^{\ell}+\rho_{i}^{\ell}\mathcal{G}_{i}(x)
and
\rho_{i}^{\ell+1}=\rho_{i}^{\ell}
(the solution respects sufficiently the constraints and the multiplier is thus updated).

-

Else,
\lambda_{i}^{\ell+1}=\lambda_{i}^{\ell}
and
\rho_{i}^{\ell+1}=\tau\rho_{i}^{\ell}
for some
\tau>1
(the constraints are too much violated, so we increase the weight of the quadratic penalization).

An analysis of this algorithm and a proof of convergence can be found in
[
bertsekas1982constrained
]
.

5.2.
Addition of noise in the measurement

We now test the stability of the method to random perturbations of the observation signal.
For this, we add a white noise to the pressure of reference
{p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}
and try to recover solutions at the surface and Bernoulli’s constant from this noisy data.
This is of significant importance from an engineering point of view, since most measurements will necessarily contain noise.

Starting from the pressure field
{p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}
without noise, we create a noisy signal
{\widecheck{p}}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}={p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}+\varepsilon
.
Here, the white noise
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\text{STD})
is designed using the standard deviation
\text{STD}=\frac{1}{3}\epsilon(\max({p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}})-\min({p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}))
, where
\epsilon
will be some prescribed error.
This let us have an error on the signal which is at most
\epsilon(\max({p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}})-\min({p}_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mathrm{obs}}))
with a 99% confidence.
In our experiments, we use
\epsilon=1\%,5\%
and
10\%
.

The errors should be understood as the relative error (with respect to the sup norm) to the expected solutions without noise.
As deduced from these results, the direct approach fails to recover the surface even with the smallest amount of noise added.
