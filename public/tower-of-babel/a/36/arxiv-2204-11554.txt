CVA in fractional and rough volatility models
E. Alòs, F. Antonelli, A. Ramponi, S. Scarlatti

Abstract
In this work we present a general representation formula for the price of a vulnerable European option, and the related CVA in stochastic (either rough or not) volatility models for the underlying’s price, when admitting correlation with the default event. We specialize it for some volatility models and we provide price approximations, based on the representation formula. We study numerically their accuracy, comparing the results with Monte Carlo simulations, and we run a theoretical study of the error. We also introduce a seminal study of roughness influence on the claim’s price. Keywords : Credit Value Adjustment; Vulnerable Options; Rough volatility models; Intensity approach.

Inevitably, including credit risk features and stochastic volatility in the same model makes the picture more complex and harder to manage. Often, this complexity leaves Monte Carlo simulations as the only option to evaluate derivatives’ prices in such a framework, paying a remarkable price in terms of computational times.

Therefore, extensive work was done to develop representation formulas that might lead to handier expressions and to finding alternative computational methods, as in

Lately, see for instance
[

, deep learning techniques have been applied to evaluate a derivative depending on multiple risks, whose price is corrected by a family of adjustments that go under the acronym of XVA. This approach is based on the price characterization as solution of a Backward Stochastic Differential Equation and the application of machine learning to those equations. Nevertheless, those algorithms though highly efficient in the predictive phase, remain computationally costly in the learning phase still based on Monte Carlo simulations. Moreover, the network architecture (e.g. the number of hidden layers and the number of neurons per layer), as well as the choice of the hyperparameters which characterize the implementation of the learning algorithm (the activation function, the minimization procedure, the mini-batch size, etc.) certainly require very careful programming work and skills.

Here, we focus our attention on vulnerable European options,
that are options subject to some default event concerning the solvability of the issuer, and we provide a general representation formula for the price and the required Credit Value Adjustment (CVA) for stochastic (either rough or not) volatility market models, when correlations among the driving processes are present.

The underlying idea is inherited from the papers by Alòs et al. (
[
1
]
,
[
5
]
), where the authors remark that those prices have to depend upon the integrated mean variance (or the zero-strike variance swap process) and the Black & Scholes pricing function, deriving a price representation by applying either anticipative or non-anticipative Stochastic Calculus. To extend that technique to a model including also the adjustment coming from the default probabilities, we use the so called “intensity approach” (
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
just to quote some), that we assume to be a diffusion. Correlations among all the processes, underlying, volatility and intensity, are admitted, making much harder to exploit the properties of the single processes (such as affinity). In particular, the intensity-underlying correlation is related to the so-called right/wrong-way risk.

Exploiting the fact that the vulnerable option final value is the same as in the default-free case, the explicit knowledge of the Black & Scholes pricing function, and anticipating Stochastic Calculus,
we are able to provide a general and rather manageable representation formula for the CVA (and consequently the adjusted price) in terms of the correlations between the asset price and, respectively, the stochastic volatility and the intensity process.
This representation turns out to be quite general, including at once both classical and rough stochastic volatility models.

Further, on the basis of that representation, we develop a manageable approximation formula, which never employs the computationally costly Monte Carlo simulations.
To achieve this goal, we first notice that the zero-strike variance swap process is the optional projection of the
integrated mean variance onto the market filtration, which allows developing the main term in a non-anticipating fashion by classical Itô formula. Finally, freezing some terms at the initial time, and using the well-known Clark-Ocone-Haussman formula, we are able to write an approximation that makes the covariation processes explicit.

When choosing the intensity in the class of the affine models and the volatility either a diffusion or a rough one, the formula becomes quite easily computable, as we show by selecting a CIR default intensity and a volatility following a Heston, SABR or Rough Bergomi model.
In those specialized models, the approximation takes the shape of a “first-order” expansion with respect to the correlation parameters among the Brownian motions driving the processes dynamics.

Finally, we briefly discuss a theoretical error estimate procedure and we test our method accuracy and efficiency by running a numerical study of the rBergomi price model coupled with a CIR default intensity model, taking the corresponding Monte Carlo price as benchmark. The relative errors turn out to be rather small, while there is a very remarkable gain in computational times dropping from hours down to few tens of seconds. We test our results by varying the values of the correlation parameters and the maturity, moreover we perform a
brief sensitivity analysis with respect to the Hurst parameter, to enucleate the roughness influence on the claim’s price.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next two sections, we first introduce the theoretical framework for CVA evaluation and then we provide our general representation and approximation formulas for a market model characterized by a stochastic (either rough or not) volatility. Section 4 specializes the approximation formula to the previously mentioned models, while section 5 is dedicated to a brief discussion of the error estimates.
In the final section, restricting to the more interesting rBergomi model, we present the numerical analysis. We refer the reader to the appendix for a very short primer on Malliavin Calculus.

2
CVA Evaluation of Defaultable European Claims

In this section, we briefly describe the general framework concerning defaultable derivatives.
Let
[0,T]
be a finite time interval and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
a complete probability space endowed with a filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
, augmented with the

P-
null sets and made right continuous. We also assume that all the processes have a cádlág version.

The market is described by an interest rate
r_{t}
, and a process
X_{t}
, representing an asset’s log-price, which may depend on multiple stochastic factors. The filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{\{t\in[0,T]\}}
is rich enough to support all those processes, and we assume to be in absence of arbitrage, with
P
a given risk neutral measure, selected by some criterion. We denote the discount factor by
B(t,s)=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}
.

In this market, a defaultable European contingent claim paying
f(X_{T})
at maturity is traded, where
f
is some function to be specified. During its lifetime the claim is subject to default and we denote by
\tau
(not necessarily a stopping time w.r.t. the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
) the default time. At default, the contingent’s value may be partially recovered by the creditor and
Z_{t}
denotes an
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}-
measurable bounded recovery process.

To properly evaluate this type of derivative, we need to include the information generated by the default time. We denote by
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
the progressively enlarged filtration, that makes
\tau
a
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}-
stopping time, that is
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}={\mathcal{F}}_{t}\lor\sigma(\{\tau\leq t\})
. Hence, denoting by

H_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau\leq t\}}
and by
{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
its natural filtration, we choose
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}={\mathcal{F}}_{t}\lor{\mathcal{H}}_{t}
.

We make the fundamental assumption, known as the H-hypothesis (see e.g.
[
19
]
and
[
18
]
and the references therein), that

(H)        Every
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}-
martingale remains a
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}-
martingale.

Under this assumption
B(t,s)S_{s},\,s\geq t
remains a
{\mathcal{G}}_{s}-
martingale under the unique extension of the risk neutral probability to the filtration
{\mathcal{G}}_{s}
.
(To keep notation light, we do not indicate the probability we use for the expectations, assuming to be the one corresponding to the filtration in use).

In this setting, the price a defaultable claim, with final value
f(X_{T})
, default time
\tau
and recovery process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t}
is given by

c^{d}(t,T)=\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})1_{\{\tau>T\}}+B(t,\tau)Z_{\tau}1_{\{t<\tau\leq T\}}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}],\quad t\in[0,T],

(1)

while the corresponding default free value is

c(t,T)=\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}].

In many situations, investors do not know the default time and they may observe only whether it happened or not. The actual observable quantity is the asset price, therefore it is interesting to write the pricing formula (
1
) in terms of
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, rather than in terms of
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}
. For that we have the following key Lemma, see
[
12
]
or
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1

For any integrable
\mathcal{G}-
measurable r.v.
Y
, the following equality holds

\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}Y|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}\Big]=P(\tau>t|{\mathcal{G}}_{t})\frac{\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}Y|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big]}{P(\tau>t|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})}.

(2)

Applying this lemma to the first and the second term of (
1
)
and recalling that
1-H_{t}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}
is
{\mathcal{G}}_{t}-
measurable, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})1_{\{\tau>T\}}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\frac{\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})1_{\{\tau>T\}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]}{P(\tau>t|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[B(t,\tau)Z_{\tau}1_{\{t<\tau\leq T\}}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\frac{\mathbf{E}[B(t,\tau)Z_{\tau}1_{\{t<\tau\leq T\}}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}]}{P(\tau>t|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})},

(4)

which may be made more explicit by following the hazard process approach.

We denote the conditional distribution of the default time
\tau
given
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
by

\displaystyle F_{t}=P(\tau\leq t|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}),\qquad\forall\,t\geq 0,

(5)

whence, for
u\geq t
,
P(\tau\leq u|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})=\mathbf{E}(P(\tau\leq u|{\mathcal{F}}_{u})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})=\mathbf{E}(F_{u}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t})
. We also assume that
F_{t}(\omega)<1
for all
t>0
to well define the so-called hazard process

\Gamma_{t}:=-\ln(1-F_{t})\quad\Rightarrow\quad F_{t}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}}\quad\forall\,t>0,\qquad\Gamma_{0}=0.

(6)

With this notation, by an extension of Proposition 5.1.1 of
[
11
]
, we rewrite (
3
) and (
4
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})1_{\{\tau>T\}}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}]=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\mathbf{E}[B(t,T)f(X_{T})\mathrm{e}^{-(\Gamma_{T}-\Gamma_{t})}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}],

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[B(t,\tau)Z_{\tau}1_{\{t<\tau\leq T\}}|{\mathcal{G}}_{t}]=

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\mathbf{E}\Big[\int_{t}^{T}B(t,s)Z_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-(\Gamma_{s}-\Gamma_{t})}d\Gamma_{s}|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big].

Assuming that the hazard process is differentiable with derivative
\lambda_{t}
, called the intensity process,
we arrive at the pricing formula

\!c^{d}(t,T)\!=\!\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\!\left[\mathbf{E}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\!\int_{t}^{T}(r_{s}\!+\lambda_{s})ds}f(X_{T})\!+\!\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\!Z_{s}\lambda_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-\!\int_{t}^{s}(r_{u}\!+\lambda_{u})du}ds|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big)\right]\!,

(7)

recovering Lando’s formulas (3.1) and (3.3) in
[
26
]
.

This formula can be specialized further, assuming fractional recovery (
[
16
]
),
Z_{t}=Rc(t,T)
for some
0\leq R<1
, and using the Optional Projection Theorem (see
[
27
]
Theorem 4.16) to obtain

\displaystyle c^{d}(t,T)=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}\Big[R\mathbf{E}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}f(X_{T})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big)+(1-R)\mathbf{E}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}(r_{u}+\lambda_{u})du}f(X_{T})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big)\Big],

(8)

which was used also by Fard in
[
17
]
with
f(x)=(\mathrm{e}^{x}-K)^{+}
, and that can be interpreted as a convex combination of the default free price and the price with default.

As a consequence we have an expression also for the unilateral CVA, defined as difference between the default free price and the adjusted price

CVA(t):=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}[c(t,T)-c^{d}(t,T)]=\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}(1-R)\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}f(X_{T})(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du})|{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\Big].

(9)

Of course, the computability of these expectations will depend heavily on the modeling choices one makes for
X
and
\lambda
.

3
A representation formula for the CVA in stochastic volatility models

In this section, we consider a family of stochastic volatility models. For the sake of simplicity, we assume zero fractional recovery (
R=0
), and the risk-free spot rate,
r
, to be a deterministic function of time. These are not restrictive assumptions, since the following discussion can be easily extended to consider
0<R<1
, and increasing the dimensionality of the problem, also a stochastic interest rate might be included. We assume that the probability space contains at least a 3-dimensional standard
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}-
adapted Brownian motion
\mathbf{B}_{s}=(B^{1}_{s},B^{2}_{s},B^{3}_{s})
, representing the randomness sources of the market.

Indeed, our market model is described by an asset log-price process,
X_{t}
, whose dynamics under a given risk neutral measure is

dX_{t}=(r_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{t}^{2})dt+\sigma_{t}dB_{t},\qquad B_{t}=\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}B^{1}_{t}+\eta B^{2}_{t},

(10)

where the volatility process,
\sigma_{t}
, is assumed to be square-integrable and adapted to the filtration generated by
\{B^{2}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. The parameter
\eta
describes the correlation between the volatility and the log-price.

We remark that the above model includes the classical volatility models, where
\sigma
is represented by a diffusion, but also those where the volatility’s dynamics is determined by a fractional Brownian motion, in order to better describe the long-term and short-term behavior of the implied volatility (see
[
15
]
, and
[
4
]
).

From now on, we take the shorter notation
\mathbf{E}_{t}[\cdot]
to denote the conditional expectation with respect to the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}
, and we consider the CVA problem given by formula (
9
), for
R=0
and
f(x)=(\mathrm{e}^{x}-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa})^{+}
, for some
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus we need to evaluate on
\{\tau>t\}

\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du})(\mathrm{e}^{X_{T}}-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa})^{+}\Big],

(11)

which has to depend (as in the default free case) on the integrated mean variance

v_{t}=\frac{1}{T-t}\int_{t}^{T}\sigma_{u}^{2}du.

For
0\leq t\leq s\leq T
, we denote by

\displaystyle N^{t}_{T}=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du}
and by

{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\mathrm{e}^{x}N(d_{+}(s,x,\zeta))-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa}N(d_{-}(s,x,\zeta)),\,\,\text{with }\,d_{\pm}(s,x,\zeta)\equiv d_{\pm}=\frac{x-\kappa\pm\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}(T-s)}{\zeta\sqrt{T-s}},

the Black & Scholes European call pricing function, where
N
is the standard normal distribution function. We recall that
{\mathcal{B}}
verifies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\partial_{t}{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)+r\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)-r{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=0\\
&{\mathcal{B}}(T,x,\zeta)=(\mathrm{e}^{x}-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa})^{+}\end{cases},

(12)

\displaystyle\partial_{\zeta}{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\zeta(T-s)(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta),

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\mathrm{e}^{x}N(d_{+}),\quad(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{\zeta\sqrt{T-s}}N^{\prime}(d_{+}),

(14)

\displaystyle(\partial_{xxx}-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}}{\sigma\sqrt{T-s}}N^{\prime}(d_{+})\left(1-\frac{d_{+}}{\zeta\sqrt{T-s}}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}(s,x,\zeta)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{x}N^{\prime}(d_{+})}{\zeta^{3}(T-s)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\left[d_{+}^{2}-d_{+}\zeta\sqrt{T-s}-1\right].

(16)

Since the final condition
{\mathcal{B}}(T,x,\zeta)=(\mathrm{e}^{x}-\mathrm{e}^{\kappa})^{+}
does not depend on
\zeta
, we may rewrite the
risk-neutral expectation (
11
) as

\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(T,X_{T},v_{T})\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau>t\}}.

(17)

Proposition 3.1

Assuming the market model (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\!\!\!CVA(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\right]

(18)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\!\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\!\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\sigma_{u}^{2}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{T}\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\lambda_{u}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right],

where by
D_{s}^{B^{i}}
we denoted the Malliavin derivative with respect to the
i-
th component of the Brownian motion
\mathbf{B}
.

Proof:
We denote
\displaystyle R_{s}^{-1}=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{s}r_{u}du}
and
\displaystyle Y_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{u}du
.
We are going to apply the anticipating Itô formula (see
[
5
]
) to the process
Y_{t}
and the function

F(s,x,y)=R_{s}^{-1}{\mathcal{B}}\left(s,x,\sqrt{\frac{y}{T-s}}\right).

Exploiting relations (
12
) and (
13

\displaystyle\partial_{s}F(s,x,y)=

\displaystyle R_{s}^{-1}\Big(\partial_{s}{\mathcal{B}}+\frac{y}{2(T-s)}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}-r_{s}{\mathcal{B}}\Big){\mathcal{B}}\left(s,x,\sqrt{\frac{y}{T-s}}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{x}F(s,x,y)=

\displaystyle R_{s}^{-1}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}\left(s,x,\sqrt{\frac{y}{T-s}}\right),\quad\partial_{xx}F(s,x,y)=R_{s}^{-1}{\mathcal{B}}\left(s,x,\sqrt{\frac{y}{T-s}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}R_{s}^{-1}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}\left(s,x,\sqrt{\frac{y}{T-s}}\right),

Keeping in mind that
v_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{Y_{t}}{T-t}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle CVA(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[R_{[t,T]}^{-1}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(T,X_{T},v_{T})\right]=\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[R_{[t,T]}^{-1}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\eta}{2}R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}(D^{-}Y)_{s}(\partial_{xxx}-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})ds\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\int_{t}^{T}\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})dB^{1}_{s}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\int_{t}^{T}\eta R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})dB^{2}_{s}\right],

where
R_{[t,s]}^{-1}=R_{s}^{-1}R_{t}
, and

\displaystyle(D^{-}Y)_{s}=D^{B^{2}}_{s}\left(\int_{s}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right)
.

By using the duality relation with respect to the two independent Brownian motions for the last two integrals, we have

\displaystyle CVA(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[R_{[t,T]}^{-1}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\int_{t}^{T}\frac{\eta}{2}R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}(\partial_{xxx}-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\int_{s}^{T}D^{B^{2}}_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\,ds\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}D^{B^{1}}_{s}(1-N^{t}_{T})R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}ds\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}D^{B^{2}}_{s}(1-N^{t}_{T})R_{[t,s]}^{-1}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\eta ds\right].

Observing that

\displaystyle D^{B^{i}}_{s}(1-N^{t}_{T})=

\displaystyle-N^{t}_{T}D^{B^{i}}_{s}\left(-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du\right)=N^{t}_{T}\int_{s}^{T}D^{B^{i}}_{s}\lambda_{u}du,

because the process
\lambda
is
{\mathcal{F}}_{t}-
adapted and
D^{B^{1}}\sigma^{2}_{s}=0
(

\displaystyle\!\!\!CVA(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\!\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\!\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\sigma_{u}^{2}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{T}\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\lambda_{u}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right].\hskip 85.35826pt\square

4
The approximation formula

The above representation formula is very compact, but clearly the expectations cannot be evaluated in closed form and they need being approximated.

Besides, it is evident that the CVA is determined by the default intensity, but also by the correlations among volatility, price and intensity, still partially hidden in the factors given by
{\mathcal{B}}
and its derivatives. In what follows we construct an approximation that makes those contributions explicit, so that it might be possible to fully parametrize them, in view of a sensitivity analysis.

We therefore need to handle the dynamics of
\lambda
and
\sigma
less implicitly and to approximate
{\mathcal{B}}
and its derivatives, when evaluated on the anticipating process
v_{t}
.
To this aim, we introduce the martingale
\displaystyle N^{t}_{s}=\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du}\right)
(with final value
N^{t}_{T}
), whose dynamics can be explicitly described resorting to the bond pricing theory, when choosing the intensity
\lambda
in the class of affine processes. Indeed,

N^{t}_{s}=\mathbf{E}_{s}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda_{u}du}\Big)=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}\lambda_{u}du}E_{s}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{s}^{T}\lambda_{u}du}\Big)=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}\lambda_{u}du}\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(T-s)\lambda_{s}+\psi(T-s)},

(19)

for some deterministic differentiable functions
\varphi
and
\psi
of time to maturity, which implies that

\displaystyle dN^{t}_{s}=

\displaystyle-N^{t}_{s}\Big\{[\lambda_{s}+\varphi^{\prime}(T-s)\lambda_{s}+\psi^{\prime}(T-s)]ds+\varphi(T-s)d\lambda_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(T-s)d\langle\lambda,\lambda\rangle_{s}\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-N^{t}_{s}\varphi(T-s)dM^{\lambda}_{s},

where
M^{\lambda}
denotes the martingale part of the process
\lambda
.

As for the anticipating process
v_{t}
, we introduce the martingale

M_{s}=\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right),\quad\text{whence}\quad v^{2}_{s}=\frac{1}{T-s}\left(M_{T}-\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right),

while the so called zero-strike variance swap process is given by

\hat{v}^{2}_{s}=\frac{1}{T-s}\mathbf{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{T}\sigma_{u}^{2}du\right]=\frac{1}{T-s}\left(M_{s}-\int_{0}^{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{E}_{s}(v^{2}_{s})=\hat{v}^{2}_{s}.

(20)

Assuming that
\sigma\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, we may apply the Clark-Ocone-Haussman formula to deduce the dynamics of
M

M_{s}=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right]+\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\!\mathbf{E}_{s}\left[D^{B^{2}}_{s}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right)\right]dB^{2}_{s}=\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right]+\!\int_{0}^{s}\!\!\mathbf{E}_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D^{B^{2}}_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right]dB^{2}_{s}.

We remark that this approach does not require the volatility to be a Markovian process and it can be applied to a rough volatility, if one obtains a manageable martingale representation. Indeed, in the next section we are going to provide an explicit CVA approximation in the case of classical Heston and SABR models, as well as for the Rough Bergomi volatility.

By this new notation, we may rewrite the representation formula (
42
) as

\displaystyle\!\!\!CVA(t)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}\left(t,X_{t},\sqrt{\frac{M_{T}-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{u}du}{T-t}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\!\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\!\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\sigma_{u}^{2}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{T}\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\lambda_{u}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right],

and we suggest a handy approximation formula for the second and third term. By substituting
v^{2}_{s}
with its conditional expectation
\hat{v}^{2}_{s}
(so
v_{s}=\sqrt{v^{2}_{s}}\approx\sqrt{\mathbf{E}_{s}(v^{2}_{s})}=\hat{v}_{s}
) in the derivatives of
{\mathcal{B}}
, and freezing the state variables at their initial values, those terms may be approximated by

\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[(1-N^{t}_{T})\!\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\!\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\sigma_{u}^{2}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]\\
&+\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{T}\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sigma_{s}\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(s,X_{s},v_{s})\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\lambda_{u}du\,d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]\\
\approx&\frac{1}{2}(1-N^{t}_{t})\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\sigma_{u}^{2}du\right)\sigma_{s}d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]\\
+&\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(N^{t}_{T}\int_{s}^{T}\!\!\!D_{s}^{B^{i}}\!\lambda_{u}du\right)\sigma_{s}d\langle B,B^{i}\rangle_{s}\right]\\
=&\frac{(1-N^{t}_{t})}{2}\left(\partial_{xxx}\!-\!\partial_{xx}\right){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle M,X\rangle_{s}\right]\\
-&\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle N^{t},X\rangle_{s}\right],\end{aligned},

where we used the Clark-Ocone formula to represent the martingales
N^{t}
and
M

dM_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(\int_{s}^{T}D^{B^{i}}_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}du\right)dB^{i}_{s},\quad dN^{t}_{s}=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbf{E}_{s}\left(N^{t}_{T}\int_{s}^{T}D^{B^{i}}_{s}\lambda_{u}du\right)dB^{i}_{s}

(21)

and consequently their covariation processes with
X
. Notice that the higher-order terms in this expansion include the quadratic variation and the covariance of the martingales
N,X,M
. As these quantities depend on some small parameters in most models (as we will see in the examples), these terms will be higher-order as functions of these small parameters.
For the first term, we apply the classical Itô formula to the process
M
and the function
F(m)={\mathcal{B}}\left(t,x,\sqrt{\frac{m-\Sigma_{t}}{T-t}}\right)
, that, by virtue of (
13
), has derivatives

\displaystyle\partial_{m}F(m)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}\left(t,x,\sqrt{\frac{m-\Sigma_{t}}{T-t}}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{mm}F(m)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}\left(t,x,\sqrt{\frac{m-\Sigma_{t}}{T-t}}\right).

Recalling the expression of
\hat{v}_{s}
, the PDE (
12
), and expanding the first term in (
42
) by integration by parts, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[R_{[t,T]}^{-1}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},v_{t})\right]=

\displaystyle(1-N^{t}_{t}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}R_{[t,s]}^{-1}(1-N^{t}_{s})(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{s})d\langle M,M\rangle_{s}\right]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\int_{t}^{T}R_{[t,s]}^{-1}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{s})d\langle N^{t},M\rangle_{s}\right],

which we may approximate by freezing
N^{t}
and
\hat{v}
at the initial time.

Summarizing,
assuming model (
10
) and (
20
), the approximated value adjustment of a vulnerable European call option is given by

\boxed{\begin{aligned} CVA(t)=&\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{u}du}(1-N^{t}_{T}){\mathcal{B}}(T,X_{T},v_{T})\right]\\
\approx&(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\\
&+\frac{1}{8}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle M\!,M\rangle_{s}\Big]\\
&+\frac{1}{2}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xxx}-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle M\!,X\rangle_{s}\Big]\\
&-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle N^{t}\!,M\rangle_{s}\Big]\\
&-\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}r_{u}du}d\langle N^{t}\!,X\rangle_{s}\Big].\end{aligned}}

(22)

Remark 4.1

In the above formula, we could leave the factor
(1-N^{t}_{s})
inside the integrals rather than freezing it also, at the initial time. Eventually, the two choices give about the same numerical contribution to the final results, thus we opted for a handier expression, to compute the various terms in a fast and simple way.

Expression (
22
) might be interpreted as a
first-order approximation formula
, indeed when restricting to some specific models for
\lambda
and
\sigma
, the final evaluation of the covariation processes will make the correlation parameters appear with the first power. Because of its integrability properties, in what follows, we take
\lambda
as a CIR process verifying Feller’s condition to ensure positivity, while for
\sigma
we make some different choices, trying to understand also the effect of roughness on the evaluation.
In the last section, we show numerically (comparing with Monte Carlo simulations) how accurate this approximation can be.

To simplify our discussion, from now on we assume
r\equiv 0
.

5
The stochastic volatility-CIR intensity model

Here, we consider a CIR dynamics for the intensity process

d\lambda_{s}=q(\mu-\lambda_{s})ds+c\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}dW_{s},\qquad\lambda_{t}=\lambda>0,\quad s>t

(23)

given that
c^{2}<2q\mu
, with
c,\mu,q>0
and

W_{s}=\frac{\rho-\eta\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}}B^{1}_{s}+\gamma B^{2}_{s}+\sqrt{\frac{1-(\eta^{2}+\gamma^{2}+\rho^{2})+2\gamma\eta\rho}{1-\eta^{2}}}B^{3}_{s},

(24)

with parameters
(\gamma,\eta,\rho)
such that

\gamma^{2}<1,\,\,\,\eta^{2}<1,\,\,\rho^{2}<1,\quad\gamma^{2}+\rho^{2}+\eta^{2}<1+2\gamma\eta\rho,

(25)

to obtain the correlations:

\langle B,B^{2}\rangle_{s}=\eta s,\langle B,W\rangle_{s}=\rho s,\langle B^{2},W\rangle_{s}=\gamma s
.

By Fourier inversion, we know that the martingale
\displaystyle N^{t}_{s}=\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{t}^{s}\lambda_{u}du}\mathrm{e}^{\varphi(T-s)\lambda_{s}+\psi(T-s)}
has an explicit expression (see
[
25
]
) with

\varphi(T-s)=-\frac{2(\mathrm{e}^{p(T-s)}-1)}{p-q+(p+q)\mathrm{e}^{p(T-s)}},\qquad\psi(T-s)=-\frac{2q\mu}{c^{2}}\ln\big[\frac{2p\mathrm{e}^{(p+q)(T-s)}}{p-q+(p+q)\mathrm{e}^{p(T-s)}}\Big],

with
p^{2}=q^{2}+2c^{2}
and it has dynamics
dN^{t}_{s}=-c\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}dW_{s}
.

We are going to couple this intensity process with different choices for the stochastic volatility.

5.1
Heston - CIR

The market model (see
[
23
]
) is given by

\displaystyle dX_{s}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}_{s}ds+\sigma_{s}d(\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}B^{1}_{s}+\eta B^{2}_{s}),

(26)

\displaystyle d\sigma^{2}_{s}=

\displaystyle k(\theta-\sigma^{2}_{s})ds+\nu\sigma_{s}dB^{2}_{s},\quad\sigma_{t}=\sigma>0,

and we assume that Feller’s condition is verified (
2k\theta>\nu^{2}
) also for the volatility, to ensure the process positivity.

Consequently, for
r<u
, we find that the Malliavin derivative verifies

D_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}=\nu\sigma_{r}-\int_{s}^{u}kD_{s}\sigma^{2}_{\xi}d\xi+\nu\int_{s}^{u}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{\xi}}D_{s}\sigma^{2}_{\xi}dB^{2}_{\xi},

whence taking expectation, and substituting the result in (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{s}(D_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u})=

\displaystyle\nu\sigma_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-k(u-s)}

(27)

\displaystyle dM_{s}=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{k}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)})\sigma_{s}dB^{2}_{s}\qquad M_{0}=\theta(1-\mathrm{e}^{-kT})

(see
[
2
]
), so that we have

\displaystyle d\langle M,X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta\nu\frac{1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)}}{k}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle M,M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu^{2}}{k^{2}}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)}\right]^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\rho c\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sigma_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\frac{\nu c}{k}\varphi(T-s)\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)}\right]N^{t}_{s}\sigma_{s},\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}ds

which implies the approximation formula

\displaystyle CVA(t)\approx

\displaystyle(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})

(28)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}(tX_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\frac{\nu^{2}}{k^{2}}\int_{t}^{T}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)}\right]^{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\sigma_{s}^{2}\right]ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xxx}-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\frac{\nu}{k}\int_{t}^{T}\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s}\right]\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\sigma^{2}_{s}\right]ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\frac{\nu c}{k}\int_{t}^{T}\varphi(T-s)\left[1-\mathrm{e}^{-k(T-s)}\right]\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}\right]ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho c\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\int_{t}^{T}\varphi(T-s)\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}\right]ds.

Recalling that
\mathbf{E}_{t}\left[\sigma^{2}_{s}\right]=\theta+(\sigma_{t}^{2}-\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-k(s-t)}
, we can compute exactly the first two integrals. For the others, we remark that empirical experience from the market shows that the dependence between default and stochastic volatility is rather weak, so that practitioners often consider them as independent. We assume this viewpoint to approximate

\mathbf{E}_{t}(N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s})
by
\mathbf{E}_{t}(N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}})\mathbf{E}_{t}(\sigma_{s})
,
even if
\gamma\neq 0
.

As in
[
7
]
, to compute
\mathbf{E}_{t}(N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}})
we use the integration by parts formula, and we write

d(\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}N^{t}_{s})=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}N^{t}_{s}\Big[\frac{4q\mu-c^{2}}{4\lambda_{s}}-(q+c^{2}\varphi(T\!-\!s))\Big]ds+cN^{t}_{s}\Big[\frac{1}{2}-\varphi(T-\!s)\lambda_{s}\Big]dW_{s},

(29)

whence, by considering that the martingale part gives null contribution, we have

\mathbf{E}_{t}(\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}N^{t}_{s})=\sqrt{\lambda}N^{t}_{t}+\int_{t}^{s}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big(\sqrt{\lambda_{u}}N^{t}_{u}\Big[\frac{4q\mu-c^{2}}{4\lambda_{u}}-(q+c^{2}\varphi(T-u))\Big]\Big)du.

To approximate this last expectation, we freeze the
\frac{1}{\lambda_{s}}
factor at the initial value and we solve the resulting ordinary differential equation, obtaining

\mathbf{E}_{t}(\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}N^{t}_{s})\approx\sqrt{\lambda}N^{t}_{t}\mathrm{e}^{\int_{t}^{s}g(u)du},\quad\textrm{ where}\quad g(u)=\frac{4q\mu-c^{2}}{8\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}(q+c^{2}\varphi(T-u)).

(30)

Finally, the factor
\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\sigma_{s}\Big]
can be computed by using a log-normal (moment-matching) approximation as in
[
7
]
.

5.1.1
SABR - CIR

We now consider the SABR model (
[
22
]
). For
0\leq\beta\leq 1
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle dX_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2(1-\beta)X_{s}}ds+\sigma_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{s}}d(\sqrt{1-\eta^{2}}B^{1}_{s}+\eta B^{2}_{s}),

(31)

\displaystyle d\sigma_{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\sigma_{s}dB^{2}_{s}.

(32)

In this case, for any
r<u

\sigma_{u}=\sigma_{s}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha(B^{2}_{u}-B^{2}_{s})-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(u-s)}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sigma^{2}_{s}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha(B^{2}_{u}-B^{2}_{s})-2\alpha^{2}(u-s)}

whence

\displaystyle D_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u}=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\sigma^{2}_{u}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{E}_{s}(D_{s}\sigma^{2}_{u})=2\alpha\sigma^{2}_{s}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(u-s)},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle dM_{s}=

\displaystyle 2\alpha\sigma_{s}^{2}\int_{s}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(u-s)}dudB^{2}_{s}=2\sigma_{s}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1}{\alpha}dB^{2}_{s},

and

\displaystyle d\langle M,X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\eta\sigma_{s}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{s}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1}{\alpha}ds,

(33)

\displaystyle d\langle M,M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=4\sigma_{s}^{4}\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1)^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=-\rho c\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{s}}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=-2\gamma c\varphi(T-s)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1}{\alpha}N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds,

so that (
22
) may be approximated by

\displaystyle CVA(t)\approx

\displaystyle(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})

(34)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x})^{2}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\frac{(\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1)^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\mathbf{E}_{t}\big[\sigma_{s}^{4}\big]ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2}(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t})(\partial_{xxx}\!\!-\partial_{xx}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(T\!-s)}\!-1}{\alpha}E_{t}\Big[\sigma_{s}^{3}\mathrm{e}^{-(1\!-\beta)X_{s}}\Big]ds

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma c(\partial_{xx}-\partial_{x}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\varphi(T-s)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\alpha^{2}(T-s)}-1}{\alpha}N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}^{2}ds\Big]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\rho c\partial_{x}{\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[\!\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{s}}ds\Big].

To get to an actually implementable formula,

•

we freeze the term
\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{s}}
at the initial condition, obtaining
\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)X_{t}}
;

•

for
n=1,2
, as before we treat the terms
\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}^{n}\Big]
as regarding independent processes, obtaining

\mathbf{E}_{t}\Big[N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\Big]\mathbf{E}_{t}\big[\sigma_{s}^{n}\big]
;

•

we approximate
\mathbf{E}_{t}(N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}})
as in (
30
);

•

for
n=1,2,3,4
, we finally recall that

\mathbf{E}_{t}(\sigma^{n}_{s})=\sigma_{t}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{n(n-1)\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}(s-t)}
.

5.1.2
rBergomi - CIR

In this subsection, we analyze our formula in the case of the Rough Bergomi volatility model.

In this case, the market model is still (
10
), where
\sigma_{s}
follows

\sigma_{s}^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\nu\sqrt{2H}Z_{s}-\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}s^{2H}},\quad\nu,\sigma_{0}>0,\quad H<\frac{1}{2},

(35)

with
Z
a RLfBm of the form

\displaystyle Z_{s}:=\int_{0}^{s}(s-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}dB^{2}_{\xi}.

Consequently

\displaystyle D_{s}\sigma_{u}^{2}=

\displaystyle\nu\sqrt{2H}\sigma_{u}^{2}D_{s}\left[\int_{0}^{u}(u-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}dB^{2}_{\xi}-\frac{1}{2}\nu^{2}u^{2H}\right]=\nu\sqrt{2H}\sigma_{u}^{2}D_{s}\left[\int_{s}^{u}(u-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}dB^{2}_{\xi}\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\sqrt{2H}\sigma_{u}^{2}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{s}(D_{s}\sigma_{u}^{2})=\nu\sqrt{2H}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}_{s}(\sigma_{u}^{2}).

It follows that

dM_{s}=\nu\sqrt{2H}\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}E_{s}\big(\sigma_{u}^{2}\big)du\,dB^{2}_{s},

(37)

and therefore

\displaystyle d\langle M,X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\eta\nu\sqrt{2H}\sigma_{s}\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}_{s}\big(\sigma_{u}^{2}\big)duds,

(38)

\displaystyle d\langle M,M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}2H\left[\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}_{s}\big(\sigma_{u}^{2}\big)du\right]^{2}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},X\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=-\rho c\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\sigma_{s}ds,

\displaystyle d\langle N^{t},M\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=-\gamma c\nu\sqrt{2H}\varphi(T-s)N^{t}_{s}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\int_{s}^{T}(u-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{E}_{s}\big(\sigma_{u}^{2}\big)duds.

When substituting in the approximation formula (
22
), using the stochastic Fubini theorem, we finally obtain

\displaystyle CVA(t)\approx

\displaystyle(1\!-\!N^{t}_{t}){\mathcal{B}}(t,X_{t},\hat{v}_{t})

(39)

\displaystyle+

Appendix A
Malliavin Calculus

Here we introduce the main concepts of Malliavin Calculus used in this work. We refer to
[
28
]
for a deeper approach to this field.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set of random variables of the form

F=f(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n})),

(48)

with
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in L^{2}([0,T])
,
W(h_{i})
denotes the Wiener integral of the function
h_{i}
, for
i=1,..,n
, and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(i.e.,
f
and all its partial derivatives are bounded). The Malliavin
derivative of
F
,
DF
, is defined
as the stochastic process in
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
given by

D_{s}F=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}}(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{n}))(s)h_{j}(s).

We can also define the iterated Malliavin derivative operator as

D_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}}^{m}F=D_{s_{1}}\ldots D_{s_{m}}F,\qquad s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\in[0,T].

These operators are closable in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for any
p\geq 1
and we denote by
{\mathbb{D}}^{n,p}
the
closure of
{\mathcal{S}}
with respect to the norm

||F||_{n,p}=\left(E\left|F\right|^{p}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}E||D^{i}F||_{L^{2}([0,T]^{i})}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

We will also make use of the notation
\mathbb{L}^{n,p}=\mathbb{D}_{W}^{n,p}(L^{2}([0,T])).

The adjoint of the derivative operator
D
is the divergence
operator
\delta
, that coincides with the Skorohod integral. That is, the domain of

\delta
, denoted by Dom
\delta
, is the set of processes

u\in L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
such that there exists

\delta(u)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

E(\delta(u)F)=E\left(\int_{0}^{T}(D_{s}F)u_{s}ds\right),\qquad\hbox{\rm for every}\;\;F\in{\mathcal{S}}.

(49)

We use the notation
\delta(u)=\int_{0}^{T}u_{s}dW_{s}
. It is
known that
\delta
is an extension of the classical Itô integral in the sense that
\delta
, applied to adapted and square integrable processes, coincides with the classical Itô integral. We also know that the space

{\mathbb{L}}^{1,2}
is included in the domain of
\delta
.

The following result gives us an explicit martingale representation for random variables in
\mathbb{D}_{W}^{1,2}
.

Theorem A.1
(The Clark-Ocone formula)

Consider a random variable
F\in\mathbb{D}_{W}^{1,2}
.Then

F=E(F)+\int_{0}^{T}E_{r}(D_{r}F)dW_{r}.

) where

Y,Z

C^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{2})

C
