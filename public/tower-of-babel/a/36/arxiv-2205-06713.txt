A Robust Permutation Test for Subvector Inference in Linear Regressions Thanks: We thank three anonymous referees and conference and seminar participants at CIREQ, TSE and York for their useful comments.
Xavier D’Haultfœuille, Purevdorj Tuvaandorj

Abstract
We develop a new permutation test for inference on a subvector of coefficients in linear models. The test is exact when the regressors and the error terms are independent. Then, we show that the test is asymptotically of correct level, consistent and has power against local alternatives when the independence condition is relaxed, under two main conditions. The first is a slight reinforcement of the usual absence of correlation between the regressors and the error term. The second is that the number of strata, defined by values of the regressors not involved in the subvector test, is small compared to the sample size. The latter implies that the vector of nuisance regressors is discrete. Simulations and empirical illustrations suggest that the test has good power in practice if, indeed, the number of strata is small compared to the sample size. Keywords: Linear regressions, permutation tests, exact tests, asymptotic validity, heteroskedasticity.

Introduction

Inference in linear regressions is one of the oldest problems in statistics. The first tests, developed by Student and Fisher, are still in use today, with their nice features of being exact with independent, normally distributed unobserved terms but also asymptotically valid under homoskedasticity only. Heteroskedasticity is a common phenomenon, however. As a result, many applied researchers nowadays rely instead on robust t- and Wald tests based on robust variance estimators
(
White 1980
)
. These tests are not the panacea, yet: they are only asymptotically valid, and may suffer from important distortions in finite samples
(
MacKinnon 2013
)
, even if the unobserved terms are independent of the regressors.

Recently,
DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
show that it is actually possible to construct a test sharing the desirable properties of both approaches. Specifically, they develop a permutation test that is exact in finite samples under independence, but also asymptotically valid under weak exogeneity conditions, allowing in particular for heteroskedasticity. However, under general conditions, the exact validity of the test only holds for inference on the whole vector of parameters. Most often, researchers are interested in subvector (e.g., scalar) inference. When applied to such subvectors, their test is exact only if the regressors corresponding to the subvector that is tested and other regressors are independent. This condition is seldom satisfied in practice.
1
1

1

An important exception is randomized experiments, where the treatment is often drawn independently of all observed variables.

DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
also develop a partial correlation test for specific components, analogous to the residual permutation test of
Freedman and
Lane 1983
(see
Toulis 2022
for an extension). Their test is asymptotically valid but is not exact in finite samples, even if the unobserved terms are independent of the regressors.

The objective of this paper is to extend the results of
DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
by developing a test on subvectors that is exact under a conditional independence assumption but also consistent under a weaker exogeneity condition. To this end, we consider a new, “stratified randomization” test (SR test hereafter) for a parameter vector
\beta
based on a heteroskedasticity-robust Wald statistic in the partitioned regression

y=X\beta+Z\gamma+u,

(1.1)

where
X
is the regressors matrix of interest,
Z
is the matrix of the nuisance regressors and
u
is the vector of error terms. The key idea is to stratify the data according to the different values of
Z
and permute the set of observations within each stratum. It is exact if
X
is independent of the error term
u
conditional on
Z
, without any restriction on the dependence between
X
and
Z
.

The test is also asymptotically valid and has power against local alternatives under a weak exogeneity condition. Specifically, we assume that
X
and
u
are uncorrelated conditonal on
Z
. This condition is stronger than the usual condition of no correlation between
u
and
(X,Z)
, but weaker than the mean independence condition
\operatorname{E}[u|X,Z]=0
. To obtain this result, we assume in particular that the number of strata
S
, equivalently the cardinality of the empirical support of
Z
, is small compared to
n
. This condition fails if
Z
has a continuous distribution. But it holds if the distribution of
Z
is discrete, with a finite support or even an infinite one (as with, e.g., a Poisson distribution or a multivariate extension of it) provided that some moment of
Z
is finite.

The main technical challenge for proving our asymptotic results is that as the sample size tends to infinity, there may be a growing number of strata, some being large and others small. We then consider separately “large” and “small” strata. For large strata, whose number may still tend to infinity, we establish a permutation central limit theorem using Stein’s method
(see, e.g.
Chen
et al. 2011
, for an exposition of this method)
. To this end, we derive a permutation version of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality which, to our knowledge, is also new. For small strata, the combinatorial central limit theorem does not apply because the strata sizes may not tend to infinity. Instead, we use independence of units belonging to different strata, and the central limit theorem for triangular arrays.

We also study the performance of the SR test and other tests, through simulations. We show that the exactness of the SR test may endow it with a power advantage in some DGPs where other tests are underpowered, at least for small to moderate
n
. Otherwise, the SR test seems to have comparable power to other tests when
S/n
is small. In the heteroskedastic case we explore, the SR test has a level closer to the nominal level than most of the other tests we consider. We also study in simulations an approximate version of the SR test, which may be useful when
Z
is not discrete or
S/n
is large. The approximate SR test is the SR test based on the strata obtained by discretizing the index
Z\widehat{\gamma}
, where
\widehat{\gamma}
is the OLS estimator of
\gamma
. The approximate SR test displays some level of distortions, but still outperforms the partial correlation permutation test or the standard heteroskedasticity-robust test in some cases.

Finally, we consider two applications. The first studies the effect of some policies on traffic fatalities in the US, whereas the second revisits the effect of class size on students’ achievement, using data from the project STAR (Student-Teacher Achievement Ratio). In both cases, confidence intervals on the effect of the evaluated policy obtained by inverting the SR test are informative. In the second application, the SR confidence intervals are always smaller than the usual, so-called HC3, confidence intervals. We also present evidence that inference based on the SR test may be preferable than that based on robust standard errors with these data.

Related literature.
As mentioned above, the paper is most closely related to
DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
. We extend their work by developing subvector inference that is exact under conditional independence between the covariates of interest and the unobserved term. Another related work is
Lei and
Bickel 2021
, who develop a cyclic permutation test on subvectors. Their test is exact under a stronger independence condition than ours, but without any restriction on the regressors. On the other hand, they do not study the asymptotic behavior of their test under weaker conditions, and our simulations strongly suggest that it is not asymptotically valid under heteroskedasticity.

The idea of a stratified (or a restricted) randomization appears in the context of experimental designs
(
Edgington 1983
;
Good 2013
)
and evaluation of treatment with randomly assigned instruments
(
Imbens and
Rosenbaum 2005
)
and treatments
(
Bugni
et al. 2018
)
.
While the theoretical results developed in this paper are for observed units randomly sampled from some population, they are also directly applicable in experimental settings where units are randomly assigned to treatments as considered by the aforementioned studies.
2
2

2

Lehmann 1975
calls the former the
population model
and the latter the

indicate that the former typically refers to permutation methods in population model while the latter is used for permutation methods in randomization model. Related to this distinction, see

In particular, when applied to

Yet another approach to constructing exact permutation tests in the presence of nuisance parameters is to condition, whenever available, on a sufficient statistic for the nuisance parameters. This approach has been pursued by
Rosenbaum 1984
for testing sharp null of no treatment effect under the logit assumption for the propensity score. However, in the absence of parametric assumptions as in our context, a low-dimensional sufficient statistic cannot be obtained in general. The approximate SR test we consider in the simulations is nonetheless related to

2.2
Approximate test

As explained above, our test is trivial when
Z
is continuous. More generally, it may also have low power with many strata. To circumvent these issues, we consider here an approximate version of our initial proposal. Specifically, instead of constructing strata based on
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we rely on a discretization of
I_{i}\equiv Z^{\prime}_{i}\widehat{\gamma}
, with
\widehat{\gamma}
the OLS estimator of
\gamma

,
u^{S+1}=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}I_{i}
and
u^{s}=u^{1}+(u^{S}-u^{1})(s-1)/S
for
s=2,\dots,S

\sum_{s=1}^{S}s1(I_{i}\in[u^{s},u^{s+1})),

where
1(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function. Intuitively, the number of strata

that one chooses trades off size distortion and power. With a low

within each stratum, but we can expect larger power since the effective sample size

is larger.

Because of the discretization, the test is not exact in general, even if

and

2.3
Confidence regions

The confidence region for the parameters can be obtained by test inversion. Given the duality between tests and confidence sets, the finite and large sample validity of the confidence regions follow from the corresponding test results.

We recommend using the same set of permutations
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}
for different tested values
\beta_{0}
and the same random variable in case randomization is required for the test (namely, when
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}^{(q)}
in (
2.5
) above), to avoid random fluctuations that could create ‘‘holes’’ in the confidence region.
7
7

7

If the test is trivial (
S=n
), confidence intervals obtained by test inversion may be empty. To avoid this issue, one can define the confidence interval as
(-\infty,\infty)
in such cases, instead of randomizing.
Even if we do so, inverting the test may not lead to a convex confidence region. In this case, taking the convex hull of the corresponding region is a simple and conservative fix. This issue is not specific to our test but potentially arises with all permutation tests for which the critical value depends on the parameter value that is tested (

in our context). For discussions about permutation confidence intervals and finding their endpoints, we refer to

Assumption 2

(Large sample validity)
.

(a)

\{(W_{i}^{\prime},u_{i})^{\prime}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are independent.

(b)

\operatorname{E}[(X_{i}^{\prime},1)^{\prime}u_{i}|Z_{i}=z_{s}]=0
for all

and

.

(c)

There exist

.

9

(d)

There exists

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\lim\inf\ }_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{\min}(\Omega_{n})>\lambda

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\lim\inf\ }_{n\to\infty}|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\geq 1

Conditions
(a)
-

are quite standard. Condition

is a usual moment condition required for the central limit theorem with independent observations
(

;
Hansen 2022b
)
. Condition
(b)
is slightly stronger than the usual unconditional absence of correlation between

and

but weaker that the usual mean independence condition

, and, as such, allows for any form of heteroskedasticity.

10

An example where Condition
(b)
holds but
\operatorname{E}[u_{i}|W_{i}]\neq 0
, suppose that

is continuous, independent of
Z_{i}
(as in a standard randomized experiment) and has a nonlinear effect on
Y_{i}
, so that
\operatorname{E}[Y_{i}|X_{i}]=g(X_{i})+Z_{i}^{\prime}\tilde{\gamma}
for some vector
\tilde{\gamma}
and nonlinear function

. If we consider the best linear prediction of

by
(X_{i}^{\prime},Z_{i}^{\prime})^{\prime}
, it will take the form

, and

will satisfy Condition

, though
\operatorname{E}[u_{i}|W_{i}]\neq 0
.
Consider again the case of stratified randomized experiments, but now assume that treatment effects can be heterogeneous, still with
\operatorname{E}[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)|Z_{i}]=\operatorname{E}[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)]
. Then, (

) holds, with
\beta=\operatorname{E}[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)]
. Besides,

depends on

because of treatment effect heterogeneity. However,
\operatorname{E}[u_{i}|W_{i}]=0
, so Condition

holds.

Conditions
(d)
-

are more specific to our setup. The first imposes that the covariance matrices corresponding to each large stratum are not degenerate. Condition
(d)
also imposes the invertibility of the limit covariance matrix for

when the number of small strata is large. The latter can be replaced by the condition that
\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{n\mathcal{J}}
exists; then the limit could be degenerate.

To understand Condition
(e)
, note that because the SR test uses deviations from strata means, the “effective” sample size

should tend to infinity for the test to be consistent. Condition

reinforces this requirement, since under the condition,

obviously holds if the

is in

, as with, e.g., Poisson or geometric distributions (or multivariate versions of them), provided that some moments of

are finite.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})
are independent and identically distributed with distribution possibly depending on

, support included in

and
\operatorname{E}[\|Z_{i}\|^{2p}]<C_{0}<\infty
, with

independent of

(e)
holds.

Remark 2
.

S
does not depend on the exact values in the support of

. Hence, when this support is countable but not in

, we can still apply Lemma

as follows. Let

be the probabilities associated with the support points

For any finite set

, with

Theorem 3.3

(Asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{W}^{\pi}
)
.

Let (

) and Assumption
2
hold with
\beta=\beta_{n}
such that
\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\lim\sup\ }_{n\to\infty}\|\beta_{n}\|<\infty
. Then, conditional on the data,

\mathcal{W}^{\pi}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi^{2}_{k}
with probability tending to one.

The main technical difficulty, and the reason why we cannot apply the same proof as in
DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
, is that the number of strata may tend to infinity, and there may be only small strata. To deal with these issues, we consider separately large and small strata. For large strata, we prove the following combinatorial central limit theorem with possibly many strata. Hereafter, we let
P^{\pi}
denote the probability measure of
\pi
.

Lemma 3.4

(Combinatorial CLT)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be an integer-valued random variable,
\mathcal{S}\geq 1
and
s=1,\dots,\mathcal{S}
denote strata of sizes
n_{s}\geq 2
, with
\sum_{s=1}^{\mathcal{S}}n_{s}=n
. Let
\pi\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}_{n})
and for each
s
,
\{b_{i}^{s}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
and
\{c_{i}^{s}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
be random variables satisfying:
11
11

11

Again, the distributions of
\mathcal{S}
,
\{b_{i}^{s}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
and
\{c_{i}^{s}\}_{i=1}^{n_{s}}
are allowed to vary with
n
here.

(a)

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}b_{i}^{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}c_{i}^{s}=0
a.s.;

(b)

For
\sigma_{n}^{2}\equiv\sum_{s=1}^{\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n_{s}-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}b_{i}^{s2}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}c_{i}^{s2}\right)
,

\sigma_{n}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2}>0
as

;

(c)

\sum_{s=1}^{\mathcal{S}}n_{s}^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}|b_{i}^{s}|^{3}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}|c_{i}^{s}|^{3}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

(d)

\sum_{s=1}^{\mathcal{S}}(n_{s}-1)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}b_{i}^{s4}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}c_{i}^{s4}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
as
n\to\infty
.

Let

. Then,

P^{\pi}(T^{\pi}\leq t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Phi(t)
for any

as

.

The proof of this lemma relies on Stein’s method with exchangeable pairs, and on the following permutation version of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality, which, to our knowledge, is also new.

Lemma 3.5

(Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality for permutation)
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
be sequences of
d\times 1
real vectors and scalars, respectively, with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=0
. Then, for any
1<r<\infty
,
n>1
, and
\pi\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{G}_{n})
there exists a constant
M_{r}
depending only on
r
such that

\operatorname{E}_{\pi}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}b_{\pi(i)}\right\|^{r}\right]\leq M_{r}n^{(r/2)\vee 1}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|^{r}\right)\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|b_{i}|^{r}\right).

(3.2)

The usual combinatorial central limit theorem, used in
DiCiccio and
Romano 2017
, would apply to a finite number of large strata; but here, the number of large strata,
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
, may tend to infinity. We can accomodate that using Lemma
3.4
. Still, to check Condition (c) therein, we need to restrict the growth of
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|
, by imposing that
|\mathcal{I}_{n}|\leq Cn^{1/2}
for some
C>0
(see (
A.14
) in the proof of Theorem
3.3
). This is why we imposed above
c_{n}\geq n^{1/2}
.

For small strata, the combinatorial central limit theorem does not apply because the strata sizes may not tend to infinity. Instead, we use the fact that all strata are independent. Then, we check that the assumptions underlying a conditional version of the Lindeberg CLT for triangular arrays hold. This is technical, however, and we have to rely again on the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality, among other tools. Also, we rely therein on the condition that strata are small enough in the sense that
c_{n}/n\to 0
. A similar condition is used by
Hansen and
Lee 2019
to establish asymptotic normality of a sample mean with potentially many clusters.

The asymptotic properties of the SR test are also based on the the asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{W}
, given in the following theorem. Hereafter, we let

be the noncentral chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom

.

Theorem 3.6

(Asymptotic behavior of
\mathcal{W}
)
.

Let us suppose that (

) and Assumption
2
hold. Then:

1.

If
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}+hn^{-1/2}
with

exists,
\mathcal{W}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi_{k}^{2}(\|Gh\|^{2})
.
12
12

12

If
h=0
, we need not assume that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}]
exists.

If
n^{1/2}\|\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}](\beta_{n}-\beta_{0})\|\to\infty
,
\mathcal{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
.

As Theorem
3.3
, Theorem

Remark 3

Theorem

q_{1-\alpha}(\chi^{2}_{k})

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose that (
1.1
) and Assumption

N_{n}=|\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
. Then:

1.

If
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{E}[\phi_{\alpha}((\mathcal{W}^{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}})]=\alpha
.

2.

If
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}+hn^{-1/2}
and
G\equiv\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}]

, where

The first result shows that the SR test is of asymptotically correct level. Combined with Theorem
3.1
, this result implies that under i.i.d. sampling and technical restrictions, the SR test is exact under conditional independence between

and

\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}\tilde{X}^{\prime}]
converges to symmetric positive definite matrix, the test is consistent for fixed alternatives, namely when
\beta_{n}=\beta\neq\beta_{0}

4.2
Approximate test

We now investigate the approximate version of our SR test which handles continuous
Z

above. To this end, we consider the same DGPs as above but now assume that the
\{Z_{ij}\}_{j=2}^{p}

X_{i}
and

y_{i}-X_{i}\beta_{0}
and
X_{i}
within strata becomes larger. Guided by this, we use the following data-driven

5.1
Driving regulations and traffic fatalities

We first apply the proposed randomization inference method to analyse the effect of driving regulations on traffic fatalities in the US, using the same data and model as
Wooldridge 2015
(
Wooldridge 2015
, Chapter 13). Specifically, we consider the linear model

\Delta\,dthrte_{i}=const+\Delta\,open_{i}\,\beta+\Delta\,admn_{i}\,\gamma+u_{i},

(5.1)

where for any US state

and random variable

\Delta A_{i}=A_{i,1990}-A_{i,1985}
. In (

The coefficient

For all confidence intervals based on permutation tests, we invert the tests of

The results are reported in Table

And actually, it is close to the SR, PC and robust confidence intervals when considering a nominal level of 90%. For both nominal levels, the SR and PC confidence intervals are very close. They are also close to the robust confidence interval for the nominal level of 90%, but around 11% larger than this confidence interval for the nominal level of 95%. The SR confidence interval may be more reliable than the robust confidence interval. To see why, note first that there is no evidence of heteroskedasticity: the White and Breusch-Pagan tests have p-values of respectively 0.56 and 0.34, respectively. If independence holds, the SR confidence interval has exact coverage, whereas the robust confidence interval may exhibit some distortion. To evaluate this, we ran simulations, assuming that

As a result, the non-robust confidence intervals based on normality may also exhibit distortion. If one drops the normality assumption but maintains independence, the SR confidence interval is the only one with exact coverage. Then, one cannot exclude even at the 10% level that the open container law has no effect on traffic fatalities.

The baseline results for the math test scores of kindergarten children analyzed by
Imbens and
Rubin 2015
are noteworthy. In this case, there is no evidence of either heteroskedasticity or non-normality. The Jarque-Bera and Shapiro-Wilk tests for the residuals have p-values 0.89 and 0.53, respectively, pointing towards Gaussian errors. As in the first application, the Breusch-Pagan and normality tests point to homoskedasticity and non-normality of the error terms in Grade 2, in which case the SR test could be the most reliable.

Inasmuch as the permutation tests should be more reliable than the non-permutation tests in experimental datasets such as the current one, and the analysis using teacher-level samples guards effectively against a possible spillover in the students’ performance, the results suggest that the class-size reduction has a small but significant effect on the math test scores for kindergarten children, and a bigger effect on the math and reading test scores for Grade 1 and Grade 2 students but no effect on the reading test scores for kindergarten children at teacher-level.

6
Conclusion

We develop a new permutation test for subvector inference in linear regressions. The test has exact size in finite samples if the error terms
u_{i}
are independent of the regressors of interest
X_{i}
, conditional on other regressors
Z_{i}
. If independence fails but
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},u_{i}|Z_{i})=0
, the test remains asymptotically valid with power against local alternatives under some conditions. The main one is that the number
S
of distinct rows of
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})^{\prime}
is negligible compared to the sample size
n
. Monte Carlo simulations suggest that the test has good power compared to other tests when, indeed,
n^{-1}S
is small, and that it exhibits limited distortion without conditional independence. The two applications confirm that in some realistic designs, the test is informative and can thus be an appealing alternative to existing methods.

A few questions are left for future research. First, some simulations we conducted (not reported above) suggest that the condition
n^{-1}S\to 0
could be replaced by the weaker condition that the effective sample size
n-S
tends to infinity. Second, while we show that the test is asymptotically valid if
\operatorname{Cov}(X_{i},u_{i}|Z_{i})=0
, we do not establish finite sample guarantees in this set-up.
18
18

18

See Theorem 2 in
Toulis 2022
for an example of such guarantees. Note however that his result is obtained under conditions for which our test is actually exact.
Finally, constructing a permutation test for subvectors that is both exact under independence and asymptotically heteroskedasticity-robust for any design remains an important challenge.

, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}_{\pi}\left[\hat{V}_{n\mathcal{H}}^{\pi}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{E}_{\pi}\left[n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s\prime}\tilde{v}_{n\pi(i)}^{s2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\frac{n_{s}}{n}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s\prime}\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{v}_{ni}^{s2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\frac{n_{s}}{n}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}-\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}{v}_{ni}^{s2}-\bar{v}^{s2}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)-n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}n_{s}\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s}\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s\prime}\bar{v}^{s2}.

(A.27)

Consider the first summand in (
A.27
). We have

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\left\|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}]\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[v_{ni}^{s2}]\right)\right\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left(X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}]\right)\right\|\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left\|\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}]\right\|\right)\operatorname{E}\left[\left|n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(v_{ni}^{s2}-\operatorname{E}[v_{ni}^{s2}])\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left(X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}]\right)\right\|^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[\|X_{ni}^{s}\|^{2}]\right)n^{-1}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(v_{ni}^{s2}-\operatorname{E}[v_{ni}^{s2}])\right)^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}]\right\|^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s4}\right]\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=O_{a.s.}\left(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\frac{n_{s}^{1/2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=o_{a.s.}(1).

The first and second inequalities hold by the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities

(A.30)

To find the limit of the second summand in (

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s\prime}\right)\right\|^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}

where the inequalities follow respectively by the Cauchy-Schwarz and Jensen’s inequalities. Let
X_{ni}^{s}=[X_{ni1}^{s},\dots,X_{nik}^{s}]^{\prime}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality again,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[(X_{nil}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{nil}^{s}])^{4}]+3\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{E}[(X_{nil}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{nil}^{s}])^{2}(X_{njl}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{njl}^{s}])^{2}]

(A.35)

Combining this with (

) and using Assumption

Similarly, the second summand in (

(A.37)

Consider the last summand in (
A.31

\displaystyle=O_{a.s.}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}n_{s}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=o_{a.s.}(1).

(A.38)

Combining (
A.31

A.36
), (
A.37

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\left\{n_{s}\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}v_{ni}^{s2}\right)-n_{s}\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s\prime}]\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[v_{ni}^{s2}]\right)\right\}\right\|

(A.39)

Consider the last summand in (
A.27

(A.40)

From Assumption

(c)
and the WLLN for triangular array of random variables

(see, e.g.,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|{X}_{ni}\|^{4}-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{E}[\|{X}_{ni}\|^{4}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

\displaystyle=16n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{ni}\|^{4}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(1).

(A.42)

where the last equality is due to (
A.41

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}\left[n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\bar{v}^{s4}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

(A.43)

2
(c)

A.40

Then, by the triangle and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left\|\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s}\right\|^{2}\bar{v}^{s2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left\|\tilde{X}_{ni}^{s}\right\|^{4}\right)^{1/2}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\bar{v}^{s4}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=o_{p}(1),

A.42

A.43

A.44

hence

Substep 2:
\hat{V}^{\pi}-\operatorname{E}_{\pi}[\hat{V}^{\pi}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

for all

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\frac{n_{s}^{2}}{n^{2}}\frac{1}{n_{s}-1}\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{nij}^{s2}\tilde{X}_{nil}^{s2}-\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{nij}^{s}\tilde{X}_{nil}^{s}\right)^{2}\right)\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{v}_{ni}^{s4}-\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{v}_{ni}^{s2}\right)^{2}\right)

v_{ni}\equiv X_{ni}^{\prime}(\beta-\beta_{0})+u_{ni}

\sup_{n,i}\operatorname{E}[|{v}_{ni}|^{4+\delta}]<2^{3+\delta}\left(\operatorname{E}\left[|{u}_{ni}|^{4+\delta}\right]+\operatorname{E}\left[\|{X}_{ni}\|^{4+\delta}\right]\|\beta-\beta_{0}\|^{4+\delta}\right)<\infty

see, e.g.,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{nij}^{s2}\tilde{X}_{nil}^{s2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{nij}^{s4}\right)^{1/2}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{H}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\tilde{X}_{nil}^{s4}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{nij}^{4}\right)^{1/2}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{nil}^{4}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(1),

(A.48)

where the equality holds by (
A.42
). Combining (

Step 4: Asymptotic distribution of

From (

\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{V}_{n\mathcal{I}}+\tilde{V}_{n\mathcal{J}})>\lambda.

(A.51)

To determine the asymptotic distribution of

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}-n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}\left[X_{ni}^{s}X_{ni}^{s\prime}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(A.85)

Consider the second summand of (
A.84
). By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\left\{n_{s}\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}-n_{s}\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]^{\prime}\right\}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\bar{X}^{s\prime}\right\|+\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}]\left(\bar{X}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]\right)^{\prime}\right\|.

(A.86)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and convexity of
x\mapsto\|x\|^{2}
, the first summand of (
A.86
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n^{-1}\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}X_{ni}^{s\prime}\right)\right\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\left\{\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\right\|^{2}\right]\right\}^{1/2}\left\{n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[\|X_{ni}^{s}\|^{2}]\right\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=O_{a.s.}\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n_{s}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=o_{a.s.}(1),

(A.87)

where the first equality uses
\operatorname{E}\left[n_{s}^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\right\|^{2}\right]=O(1)
which holds by the independence assumption and
the last by (
A.29
). Similarly, for the second summand of (
A.86
),

\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}]\left(n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])^{\prime}\right)\right\|=o_{a.s.}(1).

(A.88)

Combining (
A.87
) and (
A.88
), we obtain

\displaystyle n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\left\{n_{s}\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}-n_{s}\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]^{\prime}\right\}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(A.89)

Moreover, by the triangle inequality and convexity of
x\mapsto\|x\|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\left\|n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n_{s}\left\{\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}]\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s\prime}]-\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}]\right\}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}n_{s}\operatorname{E}\left[(\bar{X}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}])(\bar{X}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}])^{\prime}\right]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[n_{s}^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}(X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}])\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[n_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{s}}\left\|X_{ni}^{s}-\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{s}]\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=O_{a.s.}(n^{-1}S_{n})

\displaystyle=o_{a.s.}(1).

(A.90)

(
A.89
) and (
A.90
) together yield

n^{-1}\left\|\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}\left\{n_{s}\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}-n_{s}\operatorname{E}[\bar{X}^{s}\bar{X}^{s\prime}]\right\}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. The latter combined with (
A.85
) gives

n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}-\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(A.91)

To determine the asymptotic distribution of
\mathcal{W}
, rewrite

\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}\tilde{X}^{\prime}(y-X\beta_{0})=\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}(\beta_{n}-\beta_{0})+\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}\sum_{s=1}^{S_{n}}X^{s\prime}M_{\bm{1}_{s}}u^{s}.

Using (
A.77
), (
A.91
) and
G=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}]
, we obtain, if
\beta_{n}=\beta_{0}+hn^{-1/2}

\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}n^{-1/2}\tilde{X}^{\prime}(y-X\beta_{0})\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(Gh,I_{k}\right).

By the same argument as in (
A.59
),
\Omega_{n}^{1/2}\hat{\Omega}_{n}^{-1}\Omega_{n}^{1/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}I_{k}
. Then, by Slutsky’s lemma and the CMT,

\mathcal{W}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{W}_{\infty}\sim\chi^{2}_{k}(\|Gh\|^{2}).

Under the fixed alternative
H_{1}:\beta\neq\beta_{0}
, since
\|\Omega_{n}^{-1/2}\operatorname{E}[n^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\prime}\tilde{X}]n^{1/2}(\beta-\beta_{0})\|\to\infty

\mathcal{W}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
.

Corollary
3.7

First, recall that
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}

\pi_{1}=\text{Id}
. Let
(\pi_{2},...,\pi_{N_{n}})

\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}
, and
\pi_{N_{n}+1},...,\pi_{|\mathbb{S}_{n}|}

\mathcal{W}^{\pi}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi^{2}_{k}
. Hence, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

{F}_{n}(t)\equiv(|\mathbb{S}_{n}|-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{|\mathbb{S}_{n}|}1(\mathcal{W}^{\pi_{i}}\leq t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}P[\chi^{2}_{k}\leq t].

(A.92)

Take any subsequence of
\{n\}
. Since
N_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
, there exists a further subsequence
\{m\}
such that
N_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
. From Corollary 4.1 of
Romano 1989
,
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\frac{1}{N_{m}-1}\sum_{i=2}^{N_{m}}1(\mathcal{W}^{\pi_{i}}\leq t)-F_{n}(t)|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{N_{m}-1}\sum_{i=2}^{N_{m}}1(\mathcal{W}^{\pi_{i}}\leq t)-P[\chi^{2}_{k}\leq t]\right|

\displaystyle\quad+|F_{n}(t)-P[\chi^{2}_{k}\leq t]|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(A.93)

As a result, the empirical cdf
\hat{F}_{N_{m}}(t)
of
\mathcal{W}^{\pi}
on
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{N_{m}}(t)

\displaystyle=N_{m}^{-1}1(\mathcal{W}^{\pi_{1}}\leq t)+\frac{N_{m}-1}{N_{m}}\frac{1}{N_{m}-1}\sum_{i=2}^{N_{m}}1(\mathcal{W}^{\pi_{i}}\leq t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}P[\chi^{2}_{k}\leq t].

(A.94)

Since the cdf of
\chi^{2}_{k}
distribution is continuous and strictly increasing at its

q_{1-\alpha}(\chi^{2}_{k})

\{m\}

\mathcal{W}^{(q)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}q_{1-\alpha}(\chi^{2}_{k}).

\operatorname{E}[\phi_{\alpha}((\mathcal{W}^{\pi})_{\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}})]=P[\mathcal{W}>\mathcal{W}^{(q)}]+\frac{N\alpha-N^{+}}{N^{0}}P[\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}^{(q)}]

Appendix B
Technical lemmas

The following lemmas are used in the proof of the permutation version of the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality.

Lemma B.1

(Kahane-Khintchine inequality)
.

For all
r\in[1,\infty)
, there exists a constant
K_{r}
depending only on
r
such that
for any
d\times 1
vectors
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and independent Rademacher variables

\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}

\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\right\|^{r}\right]\leq K_{r}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{r/2}.

This is a special case of the general Kahane-Khintchine inequality, see Theorem 1.3.1 of
de la Peña
and Giné 2012
. We also use the following lemma, which is a particular case of Theorem 4.1 in
Chobanyan and
Salehi 2001
.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be exchangeable real random variables satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}=0
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
be vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
1<r<\infty
,
n>1
, and independent Rademacher variables
\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{n}
that are independent of
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left(\operatorname{E}\left[\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\xi_{i}\right\|^{r}\right]\right)^{1/r}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{6r^{2}}{(r-1)^{2}}\left(\operatorname{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\xi_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\right\|^{r}\right]\right)^{1/r}

\displaystyle+\frac{8(4r-1)}{(9r-1)(n-1)}\left(\operatorname{E}\left[\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\varepsilon_{i}\right|^{r}\right]\right)^{1/r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|a_{i}\|.

(B.1)

The first lemma used below is a SLLN for triangular array of row-wise independent random variables, which follows from Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 of
Hu
et al. 1989
.

Lemma B.3

(Triangular array SLLN)
.

Let
\{Y_{ni}:i=1,\dots,n;n=1,2,\dots\}
be an array of row-wise independent random vectors that satisfies either

(a)

for some
\delta>0

\sup_{n,i}\operatorname{E}[\|Y_{ni}\|^{2+\delta}]<\infty
; or

(b)

\{Y_{ni}\}_{i=1}^{n}
have identical marginal distributions with

\operatorname{E}[\|Y_{ni}\|^{2}]<\infty
.

Then,

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{ni}-\operatorname{E}[Y_{ni}])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

Proof:
Let us first suppose that Condition
(a)
holds. Let
Y_{nij}
be the
j
th element of
Y_{ni}
. We have

\operatorname{E}[|Y_{nij}-\operatorname{E}[Y_{nij}]|^{2+\delta}]\leq 2^{1+\delta}\left(\operatorname{E}[|Y_{nij}|^{2+\delta}]+\operatorname{E}[Y_{nij}]^{2+\delta}\right)<\infty.

The result follows by applying Corollary 1 of
Hu
et al. 1989
with
p=1
and
X_{ni}=Y_{nij}-\operatorname{E}[Y_{nij}]
. Now suppose that Condition
(b)
holds. Then, as above,
\operatorname{E}[|Y_{nij}-\operatorname{E}[Y_{nij}]|^{2}]<\infty
and the result follows from Theorem 1 of
Hu
et al. 1989
.
□

The following lemma is useful to establish Lindeberg’s condition for the CLT and to control the growth of the maximum of independent random variables.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\{X_{ni}:i=1,\dots,n;n=1,2,\dots\}
be an array of row-wise independent random variables that satisfies either

(a)

for some
\delta>0

\sup_{n,i}\operatorname{E}[|X_{ni}|^{4+\delta}]<\infty
; or

(b)

\{X_{ni}\}_{i=1}^{n}
have identical marginal distributions with

\sup_{n,i}\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{4}]<\infty
.

Let
\tilde{X}_{ni}\equiv X_{ni}-\bar{X}_{n}
with
\bar{X}_{n}\equiv n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ni}
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
C>0
and
n_{1}
such that almost surely, for all
n\geq n_{1}
,

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ni}^{2}1(|X_{ni}|>C)<\epsilon,

(B.2)

\displaystyle n^{-1/2}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}{|X_{ni}|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(B.3)

Proof:

For any
C>0
,

\displaystyle n^{-1}X_{ni}^{2}

\displaystyle=n^{-1}X_{ni}^{2}1\left(X_{ni}^{2}\leq C^{2}\right)+n^{-1}X_{ni}^{2}1\left(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}C^{2}+n^{-1}X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2})

\displaystyle\leq n^{-1}{C^{2}}+n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{nj}^{2}1(X_{nj}^{2}>C^{2}).

(B.4)

Since the RHS of the inequality in the last line does not depend on
i
,

n^{-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{ni}^{2}\leq n^{-1}C^{2}+n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2}).

By Lemma
B.3
,

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2})-n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2})]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(B.5)

Fix
\epsilon>0
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Markov’s inequalities,

\displaystyle\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2})]

\displaystyle\leq(\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{4}])^{1/2}(\operatorname{E}[1(X_{ni}^{2}>C^{2})])^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{4}])^{1/2}(\operatorname{E}[X_{ni}^{2}]/C^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle<\epsilon/4,

where the last inequality follows for
C
sufficiently large. Then, in view of (
B.5
), there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
C_{0}>0
such that almost surely (a.s.) and for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{ni}^{2}1(X_{ni}^{2}>C_{0}^{2})<\epsilon/2.

Next, choose
n_{1}
such that
n_{1}^{-1}C_{0}^{2}\leq\epsilon/2
. Then, by (
B.4
), we have a.s., for any
n\geq\max(n_{0},n_{1})
,
n^{-1}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{ni}^{2}<\epsilon
. Thus, (
B.2
) and (
B.3
) hold.

□

We will also use the following simple lemma.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
X_{n}=(X_{n1},\dots,X_{nK})^{\prime}
be a random vector satisfying
X_{n}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{K}\right)
. Then, for any
t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times L}
such that
t_{n}^{\prime}t_{n}=I_{L}
and
L\leq K
,
t_{n}^{\prime}X_{n}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{L}\right)
.

Proof:
By Skorokhod representation theorem, there exists
\tilde{X}_{n}=(\tilde{X}_{n1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{nK})
and
\tilde{X}=(\tilde{X}_{1},\dots,\tilde{X}_{K})
with
\tilde{X}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{n}
for all
n
,
\tilde{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{K}\right)
and such that
\tilde{X}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tilde{X}
. Let
t_{n}
be as in the lemma. Because

\left\|t_{n}^{\prime}(\tilde{X}_{n}-\tilde{X})\right\|\leq\|t_{n}\|\|\tilde{X}_{n}-\tilde{X}\|=L^{1/2}\|\tilde{X}_{n}-\tilde{X}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

we have
t_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{X}_{n}=t_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{X}+o_{p}(1)
. Moreover,
t_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{X}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{L}\right)
. Thus, by Slutsky’s lemma,

t_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{X}_{n}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{L}\right)
. The result follows since
t_{n}^{\prime}\tilde{X}_{n}
has the same distribution as
t_{n}^{\prime}X_{n}.\;_{\square}

The conditional version of multivariate Lindeberg CLT stated in the next lemma is obtained
from Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 of
Bulinski 2017
.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\{X_{ns}:s=1,\dots,q_{n};n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a triangular array of
d\times 1
random vectors which are conditionally independent in each row given a
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

with
\operatorname{E}[X_{ns}|\mathcal{A}_{n}]=0
and
\Sigma_{ns}=\operatorname{E}[X_{ns}X_{ns}^{\prime}|\mathcal{A}_{n}]
.
Let
\bar{\Sigma}_{n}=\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\Sigma_{ns}
and
\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{\min}(\bar{\Sigma}_{n})
.
Suppose

\displaystyle\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{\lim\inf\ }_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{n}>0\ \text{a.s.},

(B.6)

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0\quad\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[\|X_{ns}\|^{2}1(\|X_{ns}\|^{2}>\epsilon\lambda_{n})|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(B.7)

Then,

\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}X_{ns}\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{d}\right).

(B.8)

Proof:
Let
Y_{ns}=t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}X_{ns}
where
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\neq 0
. We verify the conditions of Theorem 1 of
Bulinski 2017
for
Y_{ns}
. First,
(Y_{ns})_{1\leq s\leq q_{n}}
are independent conditional on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Second,

\operatorname{Var}[Y_{ns}|\mathcal{A}_{n}]=t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{ns}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}t\leq t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}t=t^{\prime}t<\infty
a.s.. Third,

\sigma_{n}^{2}\equiv\operatorname{Var}[\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}Y_{ns}|\mathcal{A}_{n}]=t^{\prime}t>0
a.s.. Fourth,
on noting that
\operatorname{E}[Y_{ns}|\mathcal{A}_{n}]=0
and letting

\epsilon\equiv\varepsilon^{2}
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[Y_{ns}^{2}1(|Y_{ns}|\geq\varepsilon\sigma_{n})|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\|t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\|^{2}\operatorname{E}\left[\|X_{ns}\|^{2}1\left(\|t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1/2}\|^{2}\|X_{ns}\|^{2}>\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|t\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}\lambda_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[\|X_{ns}\|^{2}1\left(\|X_{ns}\|^{2}>\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}\lambda_{n}}{\|t\|^{2}}\right)|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\sum_{s=1}^{q_{n}}\operatorname{E}\left[\|X_{ns}\|^{2}1\left(\|X_{ns}\|^{2}>\epsilon\lambda_{n}\right)|\mathcal{A}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

where the first inequality is by Cauchy-Schwarz, the second is by
t^{\prime}\bar{\Sigma}_{n}^{-1}t\leq\|t\|^{2}/\lambda_{n}
, the equality uses
\|t\|^{2}=\sigma_{n}^{2}
, and the convergence uses (
B.7
). The result then follows from the Cramér-Wold device and Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 of
Bulinski 2017
.

\;{}_{\square}
