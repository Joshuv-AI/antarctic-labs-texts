Homological bulk-edge correspondence for Weyl semimetal
Kiyonori Gomi

Abstract
. For a certain translation invariant tight-binding model of three-dimensional Weyl semimetals, we establish a bulk-edge correspondence as an equality of two relative homology classes, based on an idea of Mathai and Thiang: From spectral information on the edge Hamiltonian, we construct a relative homology class on the surface momentum space. This class agrees with the image under the surface projection of a homology class on the bulk momentum space relative to the Weyl points, constructed from the bulk Hamiltonian. Furthermore, the relative homology class on the surface momentum space can be represented by homology cycles whose images constitute the Fermi arc, the locus where the edge Hamiltonian admits zero spectrum.

Primary 81T35; Secondary 82D35, 81Q70, 58J30.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Bulk-edge correspondence for topological insulators

The
bulk-edge correspondence
is a prominent feature of topological insulators. As is known, in the experimental observation of the
3
-dimensional quantum spin Hall effect
[
18
]
, the non-trivial topological phases of the bulk insulating states are confirmed by detecting the corresponding edge states. Thus, from theoretical viewpoint, the justification of the bulk-edge correspondence is an important theme, and has been achieved in various situations as the coincidence of two quantities called the bulk index and the edge index (see e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
10
,
14
,
16
,
19
,
20
,
25
]
).

For example, in a tight-binding model which describes an insulator as a quantum system on the
d
-dimensional square lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with translation invariance, the information on the Hamiltonian
H
of the quantum system is encoded into a continuous family
\hat{H}=\{\hat{H}(k)\}_{k\in T^{d}}
of invertible Hermitian matrices parametrized by the quasi-momentum
k
, which forms the
d
-dimensional torus
T^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{d}
called the Brillouin zone. The eigenspaces of
\hat{H}(k)
with negative eigenvalues form a vector bundle
E_{\hat{H}}
on
T^{d}
called the Bloch bundle. In the case that
d=2
, we have the first Chern number of
E_{\hat{H}}
, which we denote by
c_{1}(\hat{H})\in\mathbb{Z}
. This is the bulk index. A typical Hamiltonian, called the Qi-Wu-Zhang (QWZ) model in
[
2
]
, is

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{QWZ}}(k_{x},k_{y})=\sin k_{x}\sigma k_{x}+\sin k_{y}\sigma_{y}+(u+\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})\sigma_{z},

where
\sigma_{x}
,
\sigma_{y}
and
\sigma_{z}
are the Pauli matrices

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\sigma_{y}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right).

If we take the parameter
u
to be
0<u<2
, then
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{QWZ}}(k_{x},k_{y})
is invertible for all
(k_{x},k_{y})\in T^{2}
, and the Chern number is
c_{1}(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{QWZ}})=1
.

To formulate the edge index, one introduces an edge to the lattice, and then impose the Dirichlet boundary condition to define a self-adjoint operator
H^{\sharp}
, often called the edge Hamiltonian. Using the translation invariance in the direction along the edge,
H^{\sharp}
induces a continuous family of self-adjoint operators
\acute{H}^{\sharp}=\{\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)\}_{k\in S^{1}}
parametrized by the circle
T^{1}=S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. It turns out that
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)
is Fredholm, so that the spectral flow
\mathrm{sf}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})\in\mathbb{Z}
makes sense. The edge index is
-\mathrm{sf}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
. Now, the bulk-edge correspondence for the
2
-dimensional topological insulator (Chern insulator) is stated as the equality
c_{1}(\hat{H})=-\mathrm{sf}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
.

1.2.
Bulk-edge correspondence for Weyl semimetals

The bulk-edge correspondence is also prominent in other topological states of matters. This is the case for the
Weyl semimetal

[
1
,
27
,
28
]
, which is experimentally confirmed by observing its characteristic edge states, known as
Fermi arcs
. However, mathematical study of the bulk-edge correspondence for Weyl semimetals, in particular one which explains topology of Fermi arcs, seem to be not fully developed yet. In this direction of research, a work of Mathai and Thiang
[
21
,
22
]
suggests a formulation of the bulk-edge correspondence. Based on their idea, we mathematically formulate and prove a bulk-edge correspondence for a particular model of Weyl semimetals in this paper. The model is more or less a protypical one, so that our result should be regarded as a first step toward the general case.

Based on the idea in
[
21
,
22
]
, we here formulate the bulk-edge correspondence that we anticipate. Let
\hat{H}:T^{3}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
be a continuous map from the
3
-dimensional torus to the space
\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
of
2
by
2
traceless Hermitian matrices. For
\hat{H}
to describe a Weyl semimetal, we tentatively require the following two properties.

(W1)

The set
W=\{k\in T^{3}|\ \det\hat{H}(k)=0\}
is finite.

(W2)

At each point
(k^{0}_{x},k^{0}_{y},k^{0}_{z})\in W
, there is an approximation

\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})\sim\sum_{i=x,y,z}(k_{i}-k^{0}_{i})\sigma_{i}.

We will call
W
in (W1) the set of
Weyl points
of
\hat{H}
. Away from the Weyl points,
\hat{H}(k)
is invertible. Thus, as in the case of topological insulators, we have the Bloch bundle over
T^{3}\backslash W
, and hence its first Chern class in the integral cohomology group

c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})\in H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W).

This is essentially the same as the (bulk) topological invariant of the Weyl semimetal
\hat{H}
studied in
[
21
,
22
]
.

Now, suppose an edge on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. To be concrete, let
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\{1\}
be the edge. Then, as in the case of topological insulators, we can get the edge Hamiltonian, namely, a continuous family of self-adjoint operators
\{\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)\}_{k\in T^{2}}
parametrized by the
2
-dimensional torus
T^{2}
consisting of quasi-momenta on the boundary surface
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\times\{1\}
. A definition of the Fermi arc of
\hat{H}
is the locus of
k\in T^{2}
where
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)
admits zero spectrum:

F=\{k\in T^{2}|\ 0\in\sigma(\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k))\},

where
\sigma(\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k))
is the spectrum of
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)
. Note that
\pi(W)\subset F
, where
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
is the projection
\pi(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=(k_{x},k_{y})
. This is because
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)
at
k\in\pi(W)
has
0
as an essential spectrum. What suggested in
[
21
,
22
]
as the bulk-edge correspondence is that the Fermi arc represents the homology class

\pi_{*}(\mathrm{PD}(c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})))\in H_{1}(T^{3},\pi(W)),

where
\mathrm{PD}:H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\to H_{1}(T^{3},W)
is the Poincaré duality map, and
\pi_{*}:H_{1}(T^{3},W)\to H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))
induced from the projection
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
. For example, if both
W
and
\pi(W)
consist of distinct two points, then there is an isomorphism
H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))\cong H_{1}(T^{2})\oplus\mathbb{Z}
. A basis of the summand
\mathbb{Z}\cong H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))/H_{1}(T^{2})
is the homology class of a path which connects the two points in
\pi(W)
. Thus, when
F
consists of (the images of) genuine paths, they represent a homology class in
H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))
, and this homology class is expected to be
\pi_{*}(\mathrm{PD}(c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})))
.

By definition,
F
is just a set. Hence
F
has no a priori parametrizations as paths. Taking this issue into account, we formulate the homological bulk-edge correspondence for Weyl semimetals as the following claim:

Claim 1.1
.

For any Weyl semimetal
\hat{H}:T^{3}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
(with appropriate assumptions), there would exist a homology class

\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})\in H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))

which has the following properties.

(1)

\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
is defined by the spectral data of the edge Hamiltonian
\{\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k)\}_{k\in T^{2}}
associated to
\hat{H}
.

(2)

\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})=\pi_{*}(\mathrm{PD}(c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})))
.

(3)

If
F\neq\emptyset
, then
F
is recovered as the images of paths which represent
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
.

In comparison with the bulk-edge correspondence for topological insulators,
c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})
plays the role of the bulk index, and
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
that of the edge index.

It is rather easy to define
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
satisfying (1) and (2) in a general setup. Actually, in this paper, we define
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
by the Poincaré dual of the spectral flow of
\acute{H}^{\sharp}|_{T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)}
regarded as a first cohomology class (see §§
3.3
for detail, cf.
[
26
]
). To the contrary, the verification of (3) for
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
, defined as above for instance, seems somehow difficult. This is because the eigenvalues of operators may merge and branch, so that the set
F
may not have a simple description in general. Hence to find appropriate assumptions about
\hat{H}
is the crucial part in showing Claim
1.1

Toward a general proof of Claim
1.1
, some non-traditional descriptions of homology groups such as in
[
7
,
11
]
may be useful. In a recent work of Thiang
[
26
]
, the local behaviour of zero loci of the edge Hamiltonian of a Weyl semimetal is generally analyzed. Though their global nature is not yet clarified, his result would be helpful to show Claim
1.1
in general.

1.3.
Main result

As is mentioned, this paper considers a certain model of a Weyl semimetal, and then prove the homological bulk-edge correspondence. To explain our model, we introduce
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
by

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b&a+e^{i\theta}\\
a+e^{-i\theta}&-b\end{array}\right).

It is easy to see that
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
has two Weyl points
(1,0,\pi)
and
(-1,0,0)
. The associated edge Hamiltonian is a family of self-adjoint operators
\{\acute{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)\}_{(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
, and the Fermi arc turns out to be the interval
F=\{0\}\times[-1,1]
. Taking
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
as the local model, we introduce our model of Weyl semimetals as follows.

Assumption 1.2
.

We suppose that a map
\hat{H}:T^{3}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
is expressed as

\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b(k_{x},k_{y})&a(k_{x},k_{y})+e^{ik_{z}}\\
a(k_{x},k_{y})+e^{-ik_{z}}&-b(k_{x},k_{y})\end{array}\right)

in terms of smooth maps
a,b:T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with the following property:

(a)

The subset
a^{-1}(\{\pm 1\})\cap b^{-1}(0)\subset T^{2}
consists of a finite number of points.

(b)

Each
w\in\pi(W)
admits an open neighbourhood
U_{w}\subset T^{2}
on which we have
\det J(k_{x},k_{y})\neq 0
for
(k_{x},k_{y})\in(U_{w}\backslash\{w\})\cap a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
, where
\det J:T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is the determinant of the Jacobian of
(a,b):T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(c)

If we restrict the domain of
b:T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
to the open subset
a^{-1}((-1,1))\subset T^{2}
, then
0\in\mathbb{R}
is a regular value and the inverse image
a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
consists of a finite number of connected components.

(d)

There exists
(k^{0}_{x},k^{0}_{y})\in T^{2}
such that:

•

For any
k\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, we have
(k^{0}_{x},k),(k,k^{0}_{y})\not\in a^{-1}(\{\pm 1\})\cap b^{-1}(0)
.

•

For any
k_{y}\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
such that
(k^{0}_{x},k_{y})\in a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
, we have
\det J(k^{0}_{x},k_{y})\neq 0
. Also, for any
k_{x}\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
such that
(k_{x},k^{0}_{y})\in a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
, we have
\det J(k_{x},k^{0}_{y})\neq 0
.

We notice that the characterization (W1) of a Weyl semimetal follows from (a), because
\pi(W)=a^{-1}(\{\pm 1\})\cap b^{-1}(0)
. The characterization (W2) implies (b) when we understand the meaning of an “approximation” suitably (Lemma
3.19
). In this sense, our model is a generalization of Weyl semimetals. From (c), the Fermi arc but the projected Weyl points
F\backslash\pi(W)=a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
is expressed as the union of a finite number of
1
-dimensional submanifolds. They are the images of smooth embeddings of intervals or circles, from which we get relative homology cycles. Then, using (b) and (d), we can verify the third property in Claim
1.1
to obtain our main theorem:

Theorem 1.3
.

Claim
1.1
holds true for
\hat{H}
in Assumption
1.2
.

We close the introduction by an example. It is well known that a Weyl semimetal arises in a phase transition of topological insulators (see
[
23
]
for example). In view of the phase transitions of the QWZ model, we substitute
u=2+\cos k_{z}
into
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{QWZ}}
to get

\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=\sin k_{x}\sigma_{x}+\sin k_{y}\sigma_{y}+(2+\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}+\cos k_{z})\sigma_{z}.

Note that one can get this from a toy model in
[
1
]
. Under the change of coordinates
k_{x}\leftrightarrow k_{z}
and the conjugation by an orthogonal matrix (concretely
T
in the proof of Lemma
2.7
),
\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
agrees with
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a(k_{x},k_{y}),b(k_{x},k_{y}),k_{z})
, in which

\displaystyle a(k_{x},k_{y})

\displaystyle=2+\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y},

\displaystyle b(k_{x},k_{y})

\displaystyle=\sin k_{y}.

This example satisfies Assumption
1.2
. The set
W
consists of two Weyl points
(\pi/2,\pi,0)
and
(3\pi/2,\pi,\pi)
on
T^{3}
. The Fermi arc is the interval
F=[\pi/2,3\pi/2]\times\{\pi\}
connecting the projected Weyl points
(\pi/2,\pi)
and
(3\pi/2,\pi)
on
T^{2}
. It turns out that the relative homology class
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})\in H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))\cong\mathbb{Z}^{3}
is represented by the path
f:[0,1]\to T^{2}
given by
f(t)=((2t+1)\pi/2,\pi)
. It is clear that its image recovers the Fermi arc:
\mathrm{Im}f=F
. See §§
3.5
for more detail of this and other examples satisfying Assumption
1.2
.

This paper is organized as follows: In §
2
, we start with a summary of spectral data of the edge Hamiltonian associated to our local model
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
. Based on this result, we also give a topological proof of the bulk-edge correspondence
c_{1}(\hat{H})=-\mathrm{sf}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
for Chern insulators here. In §
3
, the main theorem is proved. For this aim, we begin with a review of relevant (co)homology groups. We next construct bases of these (co)homology groups, and define the relative homology class
\mathcal{F}(\acute{H}^{\sharp})
. Then, by using the bulk-edge correspondence for Chern insulators and the (co)homology basis, we prove Theorem
1.3
. The proof appeals to basic techniques in differential topology. Examples are supplied at the end of this section. Finally, in §
4
, a postponed proof about spectral data in §
2
is given.

Throughout the paper, some basic notions in algebraic topology (cohomology, homology and homotopy groups
[
15
]
) will be assumed.

2.
Local model

2.1.
Summary of spectral data

As in the introduction, let
H_{\mathrm{loc}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
be the following map

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b&a+e^{i\theta}\\
a+e^{-i\theta}&-b\end{array}\right).

The eigenvalues of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)
is
\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+\lvert a+e^{i\theta}\rvert^{2}}
. Hence
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)
is invertible if and only if
(a,b,\theta)\neq(1,0,\pi),(-1,0,0)
.

Let
L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
be the space of
L^{2}
-functions on
S^{1}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
with values in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and
L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\subset L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
the
L^{2}
-completion of the subspace
\bigoplus_{n>0}\mathbb{C}e^{in\theta}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Put differently,
L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is the closed subspace of
L^{2}
-functions whose Fourier expansions involve positive modes only. We denote by
\hat{P}:L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
the orthogonal projection. Its adjoint
\hat{P}^{*}:L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is the inclusion. For
(a,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we define
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b):L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
to be the compression of the multiplication operator with
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\cdot):S^{1}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
, namely, the composition of the following operators

L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\overset{\hat{P}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\overset{\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\cdot)}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})\overset{\hat{P}}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

By construction,
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)
is a self-adjoint bounded operator. Notice that
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)
is essentially the Toeplitz operator
[
12
]
associated to
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\cdot)
. Hence
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)
is Fredholm if
(a,b)\neq(\pm 1,0)
. Also, by using the continuity and symmetry of the model as well as the fact that essential spectrum is unchanged under compact perturbations, we can identify the essential spectrum of
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)
with the union of the spectrum of the Hermitian matrices
\{H_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)\}_{\theta\in S^{1}}
.

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b))=\bigcup_{\theta\in S^{1}}\sigma(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)),

where
\sigma(\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta))
is the spectrum (the set of eigenvalues) of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)
. Concretely, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b))

\displaystyle=[-\sqrt{b^{2}+(\lvert a\rvert+1)^{2}},-\sqrt{b^{2}+(\lvert a\rvert-1)^{2}}]

\displaystyle\quad\cup[\sqrt{b^{2}+(\lvert a\rvert-1)^{2}}],\sqrt{b^{2}+(\lvert a\rvert+1)^{2}}].

As a result, we have
0\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b))
if and only if
(a,b)=(\pm 1,0)
. The other zero spectra are eigenvalues:

Proposition 2.1
.

There are
\hat{\psi}\in L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and
E\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)-E)\hat{\psi}=0
only in the following cases.

•

The case that
a=0
and
E=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+1}
. In this case, the space of
\hat{\psi}\in L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
such that
(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)-E)\hat{\psi}=0
is infinite-dimensional.

•

The case that
\lvert a\rvert<1
and
E=b
. In this case, the space of
\hat{\psi}\in L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{2})
such that
(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)-E)\hat{\psi}=0
is
1
-dimensional.

The proof of this proposition, which is elementary, will be given in §
4
.

Corollary 2.2
.

The spectrum of
\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b)
contains zero,
0\in\sigma(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b))
, if and only if
(a,b)\in[-1,1]\times\{0\}
.

2.2.
Proof of bulk-edge correspondence for topological insulators

As an application of Proposition
2.1
, we show the bulk-edge correspondence for topological insulators as mentioned in the introduction. This result will be used in the sequel. Our proof appeals to basic topology, while more analytic ones are available in
[
10
,
17
]
, for instance.

Let
\hat{H}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{*}
be a continuous map from the
2
-dimensional torus
T^{2}=\mathbb{R}^{2}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to the space
\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{*}
of invertible
r
by
r
Hermitian matrices. We assume that
\hat{H}(k)
has both positive and negative eigenvalues at each
k\in T^{2}
. Associated to
\hat{H}
is the Bloch vector bundle
E_{\hat{H}}\to T^{2}
whose fiber at
k\in T^{2}
consists of negative eigenvectors of
\hat{H}(k)

E_{\hat{H}}=\bigcup_{k\in T^{2}}\bigoplus_{\lambda<0}\mathrm{Ker}(\hat{H}(k)-\lambda)\subset T^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{r}.

By evaluating the fundamental class
[T^{2}]\in H_{2}(T^{2};\mathbb{Z})
of
T^{2}
by the first Chern class
c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}})\in H^{2}(T^{2};\mathbb{Z})
of the Bloch bundle, we get the first Chern number of
E_{\hat{H}}
, which we denote by

c_{1}(\hat{H})=\langle c_{1}(E_{\hat{H}}),[T^{2}]\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
be the space of
L^{2}
-functions on
S^{1}
with values in
\mathbb{C}^{r}
, and
L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
the closed subspace consisting of
L^{2}
-functions whose Fourier expansions involve positive modes only. For
k_{x}\in S^{1}
, we define
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x}):L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\to L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
to be the compression of the multiplication operator with
\hat{H}(k_{x},\cdot):S^{1}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{*}
, namely, the composition of

L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\overset{\hat{P}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\overset{\hat{H}(k_{x},\cdot)}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\overset{\hat{P}}{\longrightarrow}L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r}),

where
\hat{P}
is the orthogonal projection. Then
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x})
is a self-adjoint bounded Fredholm operator. By the assumption that
\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y})
has both positive and negative eigenvalues,
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x})
is neither essentially positive nor negative, in the sense of
[
4
]
. As a consequence, we have a continuous map
\hat{H}^{\sharp}:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
, where
\mathrm{Fred}^{1}=\mathrm{Fred}^{1}(L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r}))
is the set of self-adjoint bounded Fredholm operators which are neither essentially positive nor negative, topologized by the operator norm. It is well-known that
\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
is a model of the classifying space of the odd
K
-theory, and the set
[S^{1},\mathrm{Fred}^{1}]
of homotopy classes of continuous maps
S^{1}\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
, which is the odd
K
-theory
K^{1}(S^{1})
of the circle, is identified with
\mathbb{Z}
. This identification is induced by the
spectral flow

[
3
]
. Intuitively, the spectral flow
\mathrm{sf}(A)\in\mathbb{Z}
of a continuous map
A:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
counts how many times the eigenvalues of
A(k)
changes from negative to positive as
k\in S^{1}
goes around the circle. A rigours definition can be found in
[
24
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\hat{H}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{*}
is a continuous map such that
\hat{H}(k)
admits both positive and negative eigenvalues at each
k\in T^{2}
, then
c_{1}(\hat{H})=-\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})
.

The proposition will be shown based on some lemmas below.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we define
\hat{H}_{n}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{n}(k_{x},k_{y})

\displaystyle=\sin nk_{x}\sigma_{x}+\sin k_{y}\sigma_{y}+(u+\cos nk_{x}+\cos k_{y})\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}u+\cos nk_{x}+\cos k_{y}&\sin nk_{x}-i\sin k_{y}\\
\sin nk_{x}+i\sin k_{y}&-u-\cos nk_{x}-\cos k_{y}\end{array}\right),

where
u\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter. If
0<u<2
, then
\hat{H}_{n}(k_{x},k_{y})
is invertible for each
(k_{x},k_{y})\in T^{2}
and we have
c_{1}(\hat{H}_{n})=n
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\det\hat{H}_{n}(k_{x},k_{y})\neq 0
whenever
u\neq 0,\pm 2
. In the case that
n=1
, the map
H_{1}
is the QWZ model of Chern insulators
[
2
]
, and its Chern number is
c_{1}(\hat{H}_{1})=1
for
0<u<2
. The map
H_{n}
factors as
\hat{H}_{n}=\hat{H}_{1}\circ f_{n}
, where
f_{n}:T^{2}\to T^{2}
is given by
f_{n}(k_{x},k_{y})=(nk_{x},k_{y})
. Since
f_{n}
carries the fundamental class
[T^{2}]\in H_{2}(T^{2};\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
of the torus to
n[T^{2}]
, it follows that
c_{1}(\hat{H}_{n})=n
.
∎

Tentatively, we write
\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
for the space of invertible Hermitian matrices which have
p
negative eigenvalues and
r-p
positive eigenvalues.

Lemma 2.5
.

\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
is homotopy equivalent to the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
consisting of
p
-dimensional linear subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{r}
. Thus, if
p\neq 0,r
, then we have the following homotopy groups

\displaystyle\pi_{0}(\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r}))

\displaystyle\cong 0,

\displaystyle\pi_{1}(\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r}))

\displaystyle\cong 0,

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r}))

\displaystyle\cong\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Given a matrix
\hat{H}\in\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
, we have a self-adjoint involution
\eta=\hat{H}/\lvert\hat{H}\rvert
. A self-adjoint involution
\eta
corresponds bijectively to an orthogonal projection
p=1-2\eta
, and an orthogonal projection to a linear subspace in
\mathbb{C}^{r}
. Hence
\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
contains as a subspace the Grassmannian
\mathrm{Gr}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
consisting of
p
-dimensional linear subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{r}
. The subspace is a deformation retract, since
\hat{H}
and
\eta
are homotopic by the homotopy
\hat{H}/\lvert\hat{H}|^{t}
, (
t\in[0,1]
).
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

If
p\neq 0,r
, then the set
[T^{2},\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})]
of homotopy classes of continuous maps
T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
is identified with
\mathbb{Z}
. This identification is induced by the assignment
\hat{H}\mapsto c_{1}(\hat{H})
of the Chern number.

Proof.

Suppose that we have two continuous maps
\hat{H},\hat{H}^{\prime}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
such that
c_{1}(\hat{H})=c_{1}(\hat{H}^{\prime})
. We shall show that
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}
are homotopic. For this aim, we notice that any continuous map
\hat{H}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
is homotopic to
\hat{F}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
whose restriction to the subspace
S^{1}\vee S^{1}=(S^{1}\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times S^{1})
in
T^{2}
is the constant map
c_{p}
with values in a point
p\in\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
. The reason is as follows: Because
\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
is simply connected, there exists a homotopy between the restriction of
\hat{H}
to
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
and the constant map
c_{p}
. Apparently, the torus
T^{2}
has the structure of a
2
-dimensional CW complex whose
1
-skeleton is
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
. Then, applying the homotopy extension property, we can extend the homotopy on
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
to that on
T^{2}
. As a consequence, we can assume that the restrictions of
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}
to
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
are the constant map
c_{p}
. Then there is a bijective correspondence between continuous maps
T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
whose restrictions to
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
are constant maps and continuous maps
T^{2}/(S^{1}\vee S^{1})\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
, where
T^{2}/(S^{1}\vee S^{1})
is the space given by collapsing the subspace
S^{1}\vee S^{1}
, which is homeomorphic to the sphere
S^{2}
. To summarize, we have the following bijections

\displaystyle[T^{2}/(S^{1}\vee S^{1}),\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})]

\displaystyle\cong[S^{2},\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})]

\displaystyle\cong\pi_{2}(\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r}))\cong H_{2}(\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r});\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}.

The last bijection to
\mathbb{Z}
is induced from the evaluation of the first Chern class of the tautological (Bloch) vector bundle on
\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})\simeq\mathrm{Gr}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
. Consequently,
c_{1}(\hat{H})=c_{1}(\hat{H}^{\prime})
implies that
\hat{H}
and
\hat{H}^{\prime}
are homotopic.

To complete the proof, we need to show that there is
\hat{F}_{n}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}_{p}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
such that
c_{1}(\hat{F}_{n})=n
for any given integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thanks to Lemma
2.4
, this is clear in the case that
p=1
and
r=2
. In the general case, an example of
\hat{F}_{n}
can be constructed as
\hat{F}_{n}(k)=\hat{H}_{n}(k)\oplus(-1_{\mathbb{C}^{p-1}})\oplus 1_{\mathbb{C}^{r-p-1}}
, where
\hat{H}_{n}
is given in Lemma
2.4
with the parameter
0<u<2
, and
1_{\mathbb{C}^{k}}
is the (constant map with its value) identity matrix on
\mathbb{C}^{k}
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
1<u<2
, we have
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{n})=-n
, where
\hat{H}_{n}
is the map given in Lemma
2.4
.

Proof.

Let
T
be the following matrix

T=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}1&1\\
1&-1\end{array}\right).

By direct computation, we see

\displaystyle T^{*}\hat{H}_{n}(k_{x},k_{y})T

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sin nk_{x}&u+\cos nk_{x}+e^{ik_{y}}\\
u+\cos nk_{x}+e^{-ik_{y}}&-\sin nk_{x}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a_{n}(k_{x}),b_{n}(k_{x}),k_{y}),

where
a_{n}(k_{x})=u+\cos nk_{x}
and
b_{n}(k_{x})=\sin nk_{x}
. It follows that
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}_{n})=\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})
, where we set
\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y})=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a_{n}(k_{x}),b_{n}(k_{x}),k_{y})
to suppress notations. Now, we apply Proposition
2.1
. In the case of
n=0
, the Fredholm operator
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x})
turns out to be invertible for all
k_{x}\in S^{1}
, so that
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})=0
. In the case of
n>0
, the Fredholm operator
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x})
has zero eigenvalues of multiplicity one at
k_{x}=\pi(2\ell-1)/n
for
\ell=1,2,\ldots,n
. Around these values of
k_{x}
, the eigenvalues of
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x})
are given by
E=\sin nk_{x}
, so that
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})=-n
. In the case of
n<0
, we find
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})=-n
in the same way.
∎

We are now in the position to prove Proposition
2.3
:

Proof of Proposition
2.3
.

Because of Lemma
2.6
, if a map
\hat{H}:T^{2}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})^{*}
satisfying the hypothesis of the proposition has the Chern number
c_{1}(\hat{H})=n
, then
\hat{H}
is homotopic to
\hat{F}_{n}
constructed in the proof of Lemma
2.6
. Then the associated families of self-adjoint Fredholm operators
\hat{H}^{\sharp}:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
and
\hat{F}^{\sharp}_{n}:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
are also homotopic. Since the spectral flow is a homotopy invariant, it follows that
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})=\mathrm{sf}(\hat{F}^{\sharp}_{n})
. By Lemma
2.7
, we see
\mathrm{sf}(\hat{F}_{n}^{\sharp})=\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}_{n}^{\sharp})=-n
. Hence
c_{1}(\hat{H})=-\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp})
is established.
∎

3.
Homological bulk-edge correspondence for Weyl semimetals

3.1.
Relevant homology groups

We summarize here homology groups relevant to Weyl semimetals as given in
[
21
,
22
]
. All the cohomology and homology groups are those with integer coefficients.

Let
W\subset T^{3}
be a finite subset. For each
w\in W
, we choose an open ball
D(w)\subset T^{3}
centered at
w\in W
so that their closure do not intersect with each other:
\overline{D(w)}\cap\overline{D(w^{\prime})}=\emptyset
for
w\neq w^{\prime}
. The ball
\overline{D(w)}
and its boundary sphere
\partial\overline{D(w)}
inherit orientations from
T^{3}
. We use this orientation to identify
H^{2}(\partial\overline{D(w)})\cong\mathbb{Z}
. We write
i:T^{3}\backslash W\to T^{3}
and
i_{w}:\partial\overline{D(w)}\to T^{3}
for the inclusions.

Lemma 3.1
.

There is an exact sequence of groups

0\longrightarrow H^{2}(T^{3})\overset{i^{*}}{\longrightarrow}H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\overset{\oplus i_{w}^{*}}{\longrightarrow}\bigoplus_{w\in W}H^{2}(\partial\overline{D(w)})\overset{\Sigma}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow 0,

where the homomorphism
\Sigma
is given by
(q_{j})\mapsto\sum_{j}q_{j}
.

Proof.

As shown in
[
21
,
22
]
, the exact sequence is derived from the Mayer-Vietoris exact sequence associated to the open sets
U=T^{3}\backslash W
and
V=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}D(w)
.
∎

As a result, if
W
consists of
n
point, then we get
H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n+2}
abstractly, since the exact sequence in Lemma
3.1
admits a splitting. However, it should be noticed that there is no natural choice of a splitting.

Let
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
be the projection
\pi(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=(k_{x},k_{y})
. The image
\pi(W)\subset T^{2}
of the finite subset
W\subset T^{3}
is again a finite subset in
T^{2}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The projection
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
induces the following homomorphism of exact sequences.

\begin{array}[]{c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c@{}c}0&\ \to&H_{1}(T^{3})&\ \to&H_{1}(T^{3},W)&\ \to&H_{0}(W)&\ \to&H_{0}(T^{3})&\ \to&0,\\
&&\downarrow&&\downarrow&&\downarrow&&\downarrow&&\\
0&\ \to&H_{1}(T^{2})&\ \to&H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))&\ \to&H_{0}(\pi(W))&\ \to&H_{0}(T^{2})&\ \to&0.\end{array}

Proof.

The exact sequences are associated to the pairs
(T^{3},W)
and
(T^{2},\pi(W))
. These exact sequences are natural, and hence
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
induces the natural homomorphisms
\pi_{*}
of homology groups that constitute the homomorphism of exact sequences.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Poincaré duality)
.

Let
W\subset T^{3}
be a finite set. Then there is an isomorphism of groups
\mathrm{PD}:H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\to H_{1}(T^{3},W)
. There is also an isomorphism of groups
\mathrm{PD}:H^{1}(T^{2}\backslash\pi(W))\to H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))
.

Proof.

For later convenience, we account for the duality isomorphism via a manifold with boundary: The open submanifold
T^{3}\backslash W\subset T^{3}
is homotopy equivalent to
T^{3}\backslash D
, where
D=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}D(w)
is the disjoint union of the open balls centered at the points in
W
. Hence the homotopy axiom gives the isomorphism
H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\cong H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash D)
. To the compact oriented
3
-dimensional manifold
T^{3}\backslash D
with its boundary
\partial(T^{3}\backslash D)=\partial\overline{D}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}\partial\overline{D(w)}
, we can apply the Poincaré(-Lefshetz) duality
[
15
]

H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash D)\cong H_{1}(T^{3}\backslash D,\partial\overline{D}).

Again by the homotopy axiom, we get
H_{1}(T^{3}\backslash D,\partial\overline{D})\cong H_{1}(T^{3},W)
. In summary, we have an isomorphism
H^{2}(T^{3}\backslash W)\cong H_{1}(T^{3},W)
. The same argument also leads to an isomorphism
H^{1}(T^{2}\backslash W)\cong H_{1}(T^{2},\pi(W))
.
∎

3.3.
Definition of the edge index

Let
\hat{H}:T^{3}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{r})
be a continuous map, where

. Define
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y}):L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\to L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
to be the self-adjoint bounded operator obtained as the compression of the multiplication with
\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},\cdot)
by the projection
L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\to L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
. The Fourier transform identifies
L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
with the space
L^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{r})
of square summarable functions on
\mathbb{Z}
with values in
\mathbb{C}^{r}

L^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{r})=\bigg\{\psi=(\psi(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigg|\ \psi(n)\in\mathbb{C}^{r},\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lVert\psi(n)\rVert^{2}<+\infty\bigg\},

and the subspace
L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})\subset L^{2}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r})
with

. The operator
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y}):L^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{r})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{r})
mentioned in §
1
is the one arising as the Fourier transform of
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
. Since
\acute{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
and
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
have the same spectral data, we will work with
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
.

Let
W=\{k\in T^{3}|\ \det\hat{H}(k)=0\}
be the subset on that
\hat{H}
is not invertible, and
\pi:T^{3}\to T^{2}
the projection
\pi(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})=(k_{x},k_{y})
as before. If
(k_{x},k_{y})\in T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)
, then
\hat{H}(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
is invertible for

, so that
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
is Fredholm. Assume that
\hat{H}^{\sharp}(k_{x},k_{y})
has both positive and negative essential spectrum. In this case, we have a map
\hat{H}^{\sharp}:T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}(L^{2}_{+}(S^{1},\mathbb{C}^{r}))
, which is continuous.

In general, if
X
is a path connected space and

is a continuous map, then we can construct a homology class
\mathrm{Sf}(A)\in H^{1}(X)
by using spectral flows: Given a closed path
\gamma:S^{1}\to X
, the spectral flow
\mathrm{sf}(A\circ\gamma)\in\mathbb{Z}
makes sense. We can then define a map
\mathrm{Sf}(A):\pi_{1}(X)\to\mathbb{Z}
to be
(\mathrm{Sf}(A))([\gamma])=\mathrm{sf}(A\circ\gamma)
. It turns out that
\mathrm{Sf}(A)
is a homomorphism. The universal coefficient theorem and the Hurewicz theorem
[
15
]
lead to the isomorphisms

H^{1}(X)\cong\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(X);\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{1}(X);\mathbb{Z}).

Therefore we get
\mathrm{Sf}(A)\in H^{1}(X)
for any continuous map

. Applying this general construction to
\hat{H}^{\sharp}|_{T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)}:T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)\to\mathrm{Fred}^{1}
, we define our edge index.

Definition 3.9

is clear. To see that

, recall
b(f(t))=0
by the definition of
f
. Then,

0=\frac{d}{dt}b(f(t))=\frac{\partial b}{\partial k_{x}}(f(t))\frac{df_{x}}{dt}(t)+\frac{\partial b}{\partial k_{y}}(f(t))\frac{df_{y}}{dt}(t).

This means that
v
and
w
are orthogonal, or equivalently,
v
is parallel to
w
rotated by
\pi/2
. As is seen,
v\neq 0
. We also have
w\neq 0
, because
f
is an embedding. As a result, there uniquely exists a real number
c\neq 0
such that

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial b}{\partial k_{x}}(f(t))

\displaystyle=-\frac{df_{y}}{dt}(t),

\displaystyle c\frac{\partial b}{\partial k_{y}}(f(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{df_{x}}{dt}(t),

when
t
is fixed. It is easy to see that
c=c_{f}(t)
, and (a) is proved. We omit the verification of (b), which is straightforward. Finally, the argument so far is local, and hence applicable to
f=f_{1}^{c},\ldots,f^{c}_{\ell^{c}}
as well.
∎

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose
a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\gamma:S^{1}\to T^{2}\backslash\pi(W)
be an embedding of a circle such that:

•

the embedding
\gamma
intersects with each of the submanifolds corresponding to
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell},f^{c}_{1},\ldots,f^{c}_{\ell^{c}}
transversally;

•

\det J(p)\neq 0
at each point
p
that
\gamma
intersects with
f_{1},\ldots,f_{\ell},f^{c}_{1},\ldots,f^{c}_{\ell^{c}}
.

Then we have

\mathrm{sf}(\hat{H}^{\sharp}\circ\gamma)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\epsilon(f_{j})I(f_{j},\gamma)+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell^{c}}\epsilon(f^{c}_{j})I(f^{c}_{j},\gamma),

where
\epsilon(f_{j})
and
\epsilon(f^{c}_{j})
are given in Lemma
3.15
, and

\displaystyle I(f_{j},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{PD}^{-1}(f_{j}),

\displaystyle I(f^{c}_{j},\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{PD}^{-1}(f^{c}_{j}).

Proof.

In view of the construction of the Poincaré dual
\mathrm{PD}^{-1}(f_{j})
as reviewed in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, one sees that the integer
I(f_{j},\gamma)
agrees with the so-called intersection number, namely, the number of positive intersection points of
f_{j}
and
\gamma
minus that of negative intersection points. Here an intersection point
p\in T^{2}
of
f_{j}
and
\gamma
is positive (resp. negative) if the pair of tangent vectors
(df_{j}/dt,d\gamma/dt)
form a basis of the tangent space at
p\in T^{2}
compatible (resp. incompatible) with the orientation on
T^{2}
. By assumption, the determinant of the Jacobian
\det J
of
(a,b):T^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
does not vanish at each intersection point
p
of
f_{j}
and
\gamma
. Thus, near this point,
(a,b)
is a diffeomorphism. Because
f_{j}
corresponds to a connected component of the submanifold
a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
, the path
(a,b)\circ f=(a\circ f,0)
lies on the interval
(-1,1)\times\{0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. With this interval, the path
(a,b)\circ\gamma
intersects transversally. For
s\in S^{1}
such that
-1<a(\gamma(s))<1
, the unique eigenvalue of
(\hat{H}^{\sharp}\circ\gamma)(s)
with finite multiplicity is
E=b(\gamma(s))
of multiplicity one. Therefore an intersection point
p=\gamma(s)
of
f_{j}
and
\gamma
corresponds bijectively to the zero eigenvalue of
(\hat{H}^{\sharp}\circ\gamma)(s)
. Near this
s
, the eigenvalue changes its sign once, and hence contributes to the spectral flow by
\pm 1
. To identify the sign of this contribution, we need to know the sign of
\det J(p)
, which determines whether
(a,b)
is orientation preserving or not. We also need the sign of
d(a\circ f_{j})/dt
. By direct inspection, the positive (resp. negative) intersection point contributes to the spectral flow by
+1
(resp.
-1
) when the product of the sings of
\det J
and
d(a\circ f_{j})/dt
is
+1
(resp.
-1
). This product of signs agrees with
\epsilon(f_{j})
, as shown in Lemma
3.15
. To summarize, the intersection points of
f_{j}
and
\gamma
contribute to the spectral flow by
\epsilon(f_{j})I(f_{j},\gamma)
. Taking all the connected components of
a^{-1}((-1,1))\cap b^{-1}(0)
into account, we conclude the expression of the spectral flow.
∎

Definition 3.17

-cycle

3.14

Lemma 3.18
.

If

Lemma 3.19

With the above understating, (W2) implies Assumption

By the conjugation of the matrix

4.
Spectral analysis of the local model

In this section, we prove Proposition
2.1
. Let
\hat{H}:S^{1}\to\mathrm{Herm}(\mathbb{C}^{2})_{0}
be the continuous map given by

\hat{H}(\theta)=\hat{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}(a,b,\theta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b&a+e^{i\theta}\\
a+e^{-i\theta}&-b\end{array}\right),

where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
H:L^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})
be the Fourier transformation of the multiplication operator with
\hat{H}
. Concretely,
H
acts on
\psi=(\psi(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
by

(H\psi)(n)=A\psi(n-1)+V\psi(n)+A^{*}\psi(n+1),

in which the square matrices
A
and
V
are

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{rr}b&a\\
a&-b\end{array}\right).

Define
H^{\sharp}:L^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{2})
by
H^{\sharp}=PHP^{*}
, where
P:L^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{C}^{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{N},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is the orthogonal projection.

Given
E\in\mathbb{R}
, we are to consider the solutions to the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
, which is equivalent to the equations for
\psi(1),\psi(2),\psi(3),\ldots\in\mathbb{C}^{2}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(V-E)\psi(1)+A^{*}\psi(2),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A\psi(1)+(V-E)\psi(2)+A^{*}\psi(3),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A\psi(2)+(V-E)\psi(3)+A^{*}\psi(4),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A\psi(n-1)+(V(k_{x})-E)\psi(n)+A^{*}\psi(n+1).

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
a=0
.

•

In the case that
E=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+1}
, the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits
L^{2}
-solutions. The solutions form a vector space of infinite dimension.

•

In the case that
E=b
, the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits
L^{2}
-solutions. The solutions form a vector space of
1
-dimension.

•

In the other cases, the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits no non-trivial
L^{2}
-solutions.

Proof.

We express
\psi(n)={}^{t}(\alpha(n)\ \beta(n))
. The equation
0=(V-E)\psi(1)+A^{*}\psi(2)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(b-E)\alpha(1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\alpha(2)

\displaystyle=(b+E)\beta(1).

For
n\geq 1
, the equation
0=A\psi(n)+(V-E)\psi(n+1)+A^{*}\psi(n+2)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\beta(n)+(b-E)\alpha(n+1),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-(b+E)\beta(n+1)+\alpha(n+2).

In summary, we have
(b-E)\alpha(1)=0
and

\displaystyle\alpha(n+1)

\displaystyle=(b+E)\beta(n),

\displaystyle(b-E)\alpha(n+1)

\displaystyle=-\beta(n).

for
n\geq 1
. Suppose here that
b-E\neq 0
. Then the first equation gives
\alpha(1)=0
, and
\beta(1)\in\mathbb{C}
can be any complex number. For
n\geq 1
, we have

\alpha(n+1)=(b+E)\beta(n)=\frac{-1}{b-E}\beta(n).

This implies that
E^{2}=b^{2}+1
, or equivalently
E=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+1}
, which is consistent with
b-E\neq 0
. Under
E^{2}=b^{2}+1
, we have

\alpha(n+1)=(b+E)\beta(n)

for
n\geq 1
. Thus, a solution is given by
\alpha(1)=0
and
\alpha(n+1)=(b+E)\beta(n)
for
n\geq 1
, where
\beta(1),\beta(2),\ldots\in\mathbb{C}
are any complex numbers. The square of the
L^{2}
-norm of this solution is

\lVert\psi\rVert^{2}=(1+\lvert E+b\rvert^{2})\sum_{n\geq 1}|\beta(n)\rvert^{2}.

Thus, for each
\beta=(\beta(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{n\geq 1}\lvert\beta(n)|^{2}<\infty
, we get an
L^{2}
-solution to
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
. The space of solutions is apparently infinite dimensional. To complete the proof, we suppose that
b-E=0
. In this case, we can readily solve the equations. A solution is specified by a complex number
\alpha(1)\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha(n+1)=\beta(n)=0
for
n\geq 1
. It is clear that this solution gives rise to an
L^{2}
-solution to
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
, and the space of solutions is
1
-dimensional.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
a\neq 0
. If we ignore the
L^{2}
-condition, then any solution
\psi=(\psi(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
can be described as follows

\displaystyle\psi(1)

\displaystyle=z\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
E-b\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\psi(n+1)

\displaystyle=R\psi(n),\quad(n\geq 1)

where
z\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex number, and

R=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-a&b+E\\
b-E&\frac{E^{2}-b^{2}-1}{a}\end{array}\right).

Proof.

We express
\psi(n)={}^{t}(\alpha(n)\ \beta(n))
. The equation
0=(V-E)\psi(1)+A^{*}\psi(2)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle(b-E)\alpha(1)+a\beta(1)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle a\alpha(1)-(b+E)\beta(1)+\alpha(2)

\displaystyle=0.

Under the assumption
a\neq 0
, these equations lead to

\displaystyle\alpha(2)

\displaystyle=-a\alpha(1)+(b+E)\beta(1),

\displaystyle\beta(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{E-b}{a}\alpha(1),

For
n\geq 1
, the equation
0=A\psi(n)+(V-E)\psi(n+1)+A^{*}\psi(n+2)
leads to

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\beta(n)+(b-E)\alpha(n+1)+a\beta(n+1),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=a\alpha(n+1)-(b+E)\beta(n+1)+\alpha(n+2).

To summarize,
\alpha(1)
determines
\beta(1)
as
\beta(1)=\frac{E-b}{a}\alpha(1)
, and we have

\displaystyle\alpha(n+1)

\displaystyle=-a\alpha(n)+(b+E)\alpha(n),

\displaystyle\beta(n+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{E-b}{a}\alpha(n+1)-\frac{1}{a}\beta(n),

for
n\geq 1
. This provides us

\displaystyle\alpha(n+1)

\displaystyle=-a\alpha(n)+(b+E)\beta(n),

\displaystyle\beta(n+1)

\displaystyle=(b-E)\alpha(n)+\frac{E^{2}-b^{2}-1}{a}\beta(n),

for
n\geq 1
. Hence the proof is completed.
∎

The trace and the determinant of
R
are

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}R

\displaystyle=\frac{E^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}-1}{a},

\displaystyle\det R

\displaystyle=1.

The discriminant of the quadratic equation
\lambda^{2}-\lambda\mathrm{tr}R+\det R=0
is

\Delta=(\mathrm{tr}R)^{2}-4\det R=\frac{((a+1)^{2}-(E^{2}-b^{2}))((a-1)^{2}-(E^{2}-b^{2}))}{a^{2}}.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
a\neq 0
. In this case, if
\Delta\leq 0
, then there is no
L^{2}
-solutions to
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
.

Proof.

In the case that
\Delta<0
, the matrix
R
has distinct eigenvalues
\lambda
and
\bar{\lambda}
such that
\lambda\overline{\lambda}=1
. We diagonalize
R
as
T^{-1}RT=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda,\lambda^{\prime})
, with
\lvert\lambda\rvert=\lvert\lambda^{\prime}|=1
. Then the
L^{2}
-norm of a solution
\psi
to
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
is convergent if and only if that of
T^{-1}\psi=(T^{-1}\psi(n))
is convergent. If we express
T^{-1}\psi(1)={}^{t}(\alpha^{\prime}(1)\ \beta^{\prime}(1))
, then

\lVert T^{-1}\psi\rVert^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\lvert\lambda\rvert^{2n}\lvert\alpha^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lambda^{\prime}\rvert^{2n}\lvert\beta^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2})=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\lvert\alpha^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\beta^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2}),

which is divergent unless
\psi=0
. In the case that
\Delta=0
, the matrix
R
has an eigenvalue
\lambda=1
with multiplicity
2
, or
\lambda=-1
with multiplicity
2
. When
R
is diagonalizable, we have
R=\pm 1_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}
, so that the
L^{2}
-norm of
\psi
is divergent unless
\psi=0
. When
R
is not diagonalizable, we consider the Jordan normal form of
R
: In the case that
\lambda=1
, there is an invertible matrix
T
such that

T^{-1}RT=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
0&1\end{array}\right).

As before, unless
\psi=0
, the
L^{2}
-norm of
T^{-1}\psi
is divergent, and so is
\lVert\psi\rVert
. The same holds true in the case that
\lambda=-1
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
a\neq 0
,
\Delta>0
and
E=-b
. In this case,
\Delta>0
is equivalent to
a\neq\pm 1
, and the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits
L^{2}
-solutions if and only if
E=-b=0
and
\lvert a\rvert<1
. The solutions form a vector space of dimension
1
.

Proof.

Under the assumption, the eigenvalues of
R
are solutions to

\lambda^{2}-\left(-a-\frac{1}{a}\right)\lambda+1=0,

so that
\lambda=-a,-1/a
, where
a\neq\pm 1,0
. Let
T
be the matrix

T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{a}-a&0\\
2b&1\end{array}\right).

This is invertible, and diagonalizes
R
as
T^{-1}RT=\mathrm{diag}(-a,-1/a)
. We have

\psi(n)=R^{n}\psi(1)=\frac{z}{\det T}T\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-(-a)^{n+1}\\
2b\big(-\frac{1}{a}\big)^{n+1}\end{array}\right).

Therefore
\lVert\psi\rVert<+\infty
if and only if
\lvert a\rvert<1
and
b=0
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose that
a\neq 0
,
\Delta>0
and
E\neq-b
. In this case, the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits
L^{2}
-solutions if and only if
E=b
and
\lvert a\rvert<1
. The solutions form a vector space of dimension
1
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{\pm}
be the eigenvalues of
R
. Let
T
be the matrix

T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b+E&b+E\\
a+\lambda_{+}&a+\lambda_{-}\\
\end{array}\right).

This is invertible, since
\det T=(b+E)(\lambda_{-}-\lambda_{+})
does not vanish under the present assumptions
E\neq-b
and
\Delta>0
. The matrix
T
diagonalizes
R
as
T^{-1}RT=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-})
. The
L^{2}
-norm of
\psi
is convergent if and only if that of
T^{-1}\psi
is convergent. We have

\lVert T^{-1}\psi\rVert^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\lvert\lambda_{+}\rvert^{2n}\lvert\alpha^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2}+\lvert\lambda_{-}\rvert^{2n}\lvert\beta^{\prime}(1)\rvert^{2}),

where
\alpha^{\prime}(1)
and
\beta^{\prime}(1)
are components of
T^{-1}\psi(1)

T^{-1}\psi(1)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}(1)\\
\beta^{\prime}(1)\end{array}\right)=\frac{z}{\det T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-E^{2}+a^{2}+b^{2}+a\lambda_{-}\\
E^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}-a\lambda_{+}\end{array}\right).

It follows that the equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
admits non-trivial
L^{2}
-solutions only in the following two cases:

(i)

\lvert\lambda_{+}\rvert>1
,
\lvert\lambda_{-}\rvert<1
and
E^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}-a\lambda_{-}=0
; or

(ii)

\lvert\lambda_{+}\rvert<1
,
\lvert\lambda_{-}\rvert>1
and
E^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}-a\lambda_{+}=0
.

In the first case, we have
\lambda_{-}=(E^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2})/a
and
\lambda_{+}=1/\lambda_{-}
. From the consistency
\lambda_{+}+\lambda_{-}=\mathrm{Tr}R
, it follows that
E^{2}=b^{2}
. Because of the assumption
E\neq-b
, we get
E=b
. In the second case, the same argument leads to
E=b
. Thus, in both cases, we have
E=b
. Now, we verify again the
L^{2}
-condition under the assumption
E=b
. Under this assumption, we can take
\lambda_{+}=-a
and
\lambda_{-}=-1/a
. We have

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}(1)\\
\beta^{\prime}(1)\end{array}\right)=\frac{z}{\det T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a^{2}+a\lambda_{-}\\
-a^{2}-a\lambda_{+}\end{array}\right)=\frac{z}{\det T}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a^{2}-1\\
0\end{array}\right).

Note that
a^{2}-1\neq 0
under the present assumption
\Delta>0
, which is equivalent to
a^{2}\neq 1
. Hence the case (i) cannot occur: if
\lvert\lambda_{+}\rvert=\lvert a\rvert>1
, then there is only the trivial
L^{2}
-solution (
z=0
). If
\lvert\lambda_{+}\rvert=\lvert a\rvert<1
(the case (ii)), then we have non-trivial
L^{2}
-solutions which form a
1
-dimensional space.
∎

Now, we are in the position to complete the proof of Proposition
2.1
:

Proposition 4.6
.

The equation
(H^{\sharp}-E)\psi=0
for
\psi=(\psi(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
admits a non-trivial
L^{2}
-solution in the following cases.

•

The case that
a=0
and
E=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+1}
. In this case, the space of
L^{2}
-solutions is infinite dimensional.

•

The case that
\lvert a\rvert<1
and
E=b
. In this case, the space of
L^{2}
-solutions is
1
-dimensional.

Proof.

By the lemmas so far, we have non-trivial
L^{2}
-solutions in the following cases.

(1)

a=0
and
E=\pm\sqrt{b^{2}+1}
.

(2)

a=0
and
E=b
.

(3)

a\neq 0
,
\lvert a\rvert<1
and
E=b=0
.

(4)

a\neq 0
,
\lvert a\rvert<1
and
E=b\neq 0

In the first case, the space of

-solutions is infinite dimensional, whereas the space is
1

\lvert a\rvert<1
and

.
∎
