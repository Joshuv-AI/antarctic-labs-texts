A Theoretical Analysis of Learning with Noisily Labeled Data
Yi Xu, Qi Qian, Hao Li, Rong Jin

Abstract
Noisy labels are very common in deep supervised learning. Although many studies tend to improve the robustness of deep training for noisy labels, rare works focus on theoretically explaining the training behaviors of learning with noisily labeled data, which is a fundamental principle in understanding its generalization. In this draft, we study its two phenomena, clean data first and phase transition, by explaining them from a theoretical viewpoint. Specifically, we first show that in the first epoch training, the examples with clean labels will be learned first. We then show that after the learning from clean data stage, continuously training model can achieve further improvement in testing error when the rate of corrupted class labels is smaller than a certain threshold; otherwise, extensively training could lead to an increasing testing error.

2
Preliminaries and Notations

We introduce preliminaries in this section. We first give some notations and the problem definition,then we present some assumptions that will be used in the convergence analysis.

Let
\nabla_{\theta}f(\theta)
denote the gradient of a function
f(\theta)
. When the variable to be taken a gradient is obvious, we use
\nabla f(\theta)
for simplicity. We use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the Euclidean norm. Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be the inner product.

The classification problem aims to seek a classifier to map an instance
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto one of labels
y\in\{+1,-1\}
. Suppose the instances are draw from a distribution
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,
x\sim\mathcal{P}
, where
\mathcal{P}
is usually unknown. For simplicity, we assume
y=y(x)
, i.e. the class label of instance
x
is decided by a deterministic function
y(x)
. Our goal is to learn a prediction function
f(x;\theta):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that is as close as possible to
y
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the model parameter. To achieve this goal, traditional method is to minimize the following expected loss
\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}}[1/2\|f(\theta;x)-y(x)\|^{2}]
, which is also known as expected risk minimization. Since the distribution
\mathcal{P}
is unknown, solving the risk minimization is difficulty. However, one can sample
n
examples
\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
from
\mathcal{P}
independently, then we aim to minimize an empirical loss
\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|f(\theta;x_{i})-y_{i}(x_{i})\|^{2}
, which is also known as empirical risk minimization. In this study, the training data set contains clean labels and noisy labels, we denote by
\gamma
, the percentage of data points with noisy labels. We divide the training data into two parts:

•

clean data
:
D_{a}=\{(x^{a}_{i},y^{a}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,m\}
, where
x_{i}^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sampled independently from a given distribution
\mathcal{P}
and
y_{i}^{a}=y(x_{i}^{a})
is a clean class label decided by function
y:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\{-1,+1\}
, and

•

corrupted data
:
D_{b}=\{(x^{b}_{i},y^{b}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,n\}
, where
x_{i}^{b}
is sampled independently from
\mathcal{P}
and
y_{i}^{b}
is assigned randomly with equal chance to be either
-1
or
+1
.

Please note that the labels
y_{i}^{b}
from corrupted data is independent of instance
x_{i}^{a}
, whose expectation is
0
, i.e.
\mathrm{E}[y_{i}^{b}]=0
.
The percentage of noisily labeled data points is
\gamma=n/(m+n)
. We consider the following expected risk to be optimized

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)=(1-\gamma)\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta),

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)
is the loss function for clean labeled data and
\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)
is the loss function related to noisily labeled data, whose definitions are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)=\mathrm{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{1}{2}|f(x;\theta)-y(x)|^{2}\right],\quad\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)=\mathrm{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\frac{1}{2}|f(x;\theta)|^{2}\right].

(2)

In practice, we want to solve the following empirical risk minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta):=(1-\gamma)\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(\theta)+\gamma\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta),

(3)

where
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(\theta)
is the loss function over training data with clean labels and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)
is the loss function over training data with noisy labels, whose definitions are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}|f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y_{i}^{a}|^{2},\quad\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(x_{i}^{b};\theta)-y_{i}^{b}|^{2}.

(4)

Since
\mathrm{E}[\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)]=\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2}
, we may consider
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
as the final objective function. As a widely used method, SGD can be employed to solve problem (
3
) and it updates solutions iteratively by

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}

(5)

where
t
is the iteration number,
\eta>0
is a learning rate, and
g_{t}
is the stochastic gradient given by

\displaystyle g_{t}=(f(x_{i_{t}};\theta_{t})-y_{i_{t}})\nabla_{\theta}f(x_{i_{t}};\theta_{t})

(6)

with
(x_{i_{t}},y_{i_{t}})
is uniformly sampled from
D_{a}\cup D_{b}
.

To establish the theoretical analysis, we introduce some commonly used assumptions in the literature of optimization
(
Ghadimi and Lan 2013
;
Xu et al. 2019
)
.

Assumption 1
.

Assume the following conditions hold:

(i)

There exists two constants
F>0
and
G>0
, such that

\|f(x;\theta)\|\leq F,\;\|f(x;\theta)-y(x)\|\leq F,

and

\|(f(x;\theta)-y(x))\nabla f(x;\theta)\|\leq G,\;\|f(x;\theta)\nabla f(x;\theta)\|+\|\nabla f(x;\theta)\|\leq G.

(ii)

The loss function
\mathcal{L}
is smooth with an
L
-Lipchitz continuous gradient, i.e., it is differentiable and there exists a constant
L>0
such that

\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime\prime})+\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime\prime}),\theta^{\prime}-\theta^{\prime\prime}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta^{\prime\prime}\|^{2},\forall\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We now introduce an important functional property, which is called Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition
(
Polyak 1963
)
. PL condition has been observed in training deep neural networks
(
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Yuan et al. 2019
)
, and it is widely used to establish convergence in the literature of optimization
(
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019b
;
Karimi et al. 2016
;
Li and Li 2018
;
Charles and Papailiopoulos 2018
;
Li et al. 2020b
)
.

Definition 1
.

A function
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
\tau
-PL condition if there exists a constant
\tau>0
such that
2\tau(\mathcal{L}(\theta)-\mathcal{L}^{*})\leq\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)\|^{2},\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is the optimal value.

For the simplicity, we set
\mathcal{L}_{a}^{min}:=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)=0
and
\mathcal{L}_{b}^{min}:=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)=0
in our proofs without loss of generality, which is a common property observed in training deep neural networks
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Du et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2019
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Chizat et al. 2019
;
Hastie et al. 2019
;
Yun et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2020
)
.

3
Main Results

We first study the observation of learning from data with clean labels first, and then the observation of phase transition in terms of
\gamma
.

3.1
Learning from data with clean labels first

To this end, we will focus on the optimization of the first epoch. Note that in the first epoch, it satisfies the condition of statistical independence and therefore we can apply the standard analysis of stochastic gradient descent.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumption
1
, suppose
\mathcal{L}_{a}
and
\mathcal{L}_{b}
satisfies
\mu_{a}
-PL condition and
\mu_{b}
-PL condition, respectively.
Let
\theta_{0}
be our initial solution, and
\theta_{1}
be the solution after running through the first epoch of data, and
\tau:=\min(2\mu_{a},2\mu_{b})
. By selecting
\eta\leq 1/L
and
m\geq\frac{4G^{2}L}{\tau^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}
.
We have

\mathrm{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{1})\right]\leq O\left(\frac{4G^{2}\log m}{(1-\gamma)\tau^{2}m}\right).

Remark.
Through the first epoch of optimization, since we do not reuse any data point, either clean or corrupted one, SGD essentially optimizes the objective function
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
, instead of
\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)
. As indicated by the above analysis, despite of the presence of noisy labels, the resulting solution is still able to reach a low value of
\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)
, which is on the order of
O(\log(m)/m)
. Of course, the presence of noisy labels indeed hinders the progress of optimization, through parameter
\gamma
(i.e. the percentage of noisily labeled data),
G^{2}
and
\tau
. This analysis explains why for learning from noisily labeled data, it appears that the model could learn clean data first because for early epoch, noisily labeled data behave like regularizer to our solution.

3.2
Phase Transition for
\gamma

Now, let’s examine the late stage of the optimization beyond the first epoch, in which we do not have the statistical independence between the model to be updated and the training data. As a result, our objective function “becomes”
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)=(1-\gamma)\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(\theta)+\gamma\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)
. Let
\theta_{1}
be the solution obtained from the first epoch.
Define

\Omega(\widehat{r},s)=\left\{\theta:\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a})\right\|^{2}\leq\widehat{r}^{2},\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a})\right\|\leq\sqrt{s}\widehat{r}\right\}

where

\widehat{r}^{2}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|f(x_{i}^{a};\theta_{1})-y(x_{i}^{a})\right\|^{2},\quad\sqrt{s}:=\frac{1}{m\widehat{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|f(x_{i}^{a};\theta_{1})-y(x_{i}^{a})\right\|

We assume that
s\ll m
, implying that vector
(f(x_{1};\theta),\ldots,f(x_{m};\theta))
is a sparse vector. We first give the following results of bounding the variances of the stochastic gradients, which will be used in our analysis of the main result.

Theorem 2
.

Under the assumption
m\geq 64F^{2}\log(2/\delta)
, we have, with a probability
1-3\delta
,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{a}(\theta)\|\leq C\left(\frac{GF\log(1/\delta)}{m}+G\widehat{r}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{m}}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{m}\log\frac{2m}{s}}\left(1+\log m\right)\right]\right),

(7)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)\|\leq C\left(\frac{GF\log(1/\delta)}{n}+GF\left[\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{s}{n}\log\frac{2n}{s}}\left(1+\log n\right)\right]\right),

(8)

where
C
is a universal constant.

To understand the phenomenon of phase transition (i.e. the optimizer may continuously reduce the testing error when
\gamma
is smaller than a threshold, and will lead to an increase in testing error after extensive training if
\gamma
is above the threshold), we will examine the condition for all the solutions in the later iterations after the first epoch that will stay within
\Omega(\widehat{r},s)
. If solution
\theta
continuously stays in
\Omega(\widehat{r},s)
, we will not see the degradation in performance. Otherwise, we may find a large error after the training of the first epoch. To this end, we will assume that is the case, and see when the condition will be violated. In this case, the stochastic gradient
g_{t}
is not a unbiased estimator of
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}_{t})
, i.e.
\mathrm{E}[g_{t}]\neq\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^{\prime}_{t})

Theorem 3
.

Under Assumption
1
, suppose
\mathcal{L}_{a}
and
\mathcal{L}_{b}
satisfies
\mu_{a}
-PL condition and
\mu_{b}
-PL condition, respectively.
Let
\theta_{1}
be the solution after running through the first epoch of data, and
\tau:=\min(2\mu_{a},2\mu_{b})
. By selecting
\eta=\frac{2}{\tau m}\log\left(\frac{m\tau^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}{8G^{2}L}\right)
and
m\geq\frac{2L}{\tau}\log\left(\frac{m\tau^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}{8G^{2}L}\right)
. If we have

\displaystyle 1-\gamma\geq\max\left\{\frac{C^{\prime\prime}G^{2}\Delta(\widehat{r}^{2}+F^{2})}{8\tau\widehat{r}^{2}(n+m)},\frac{16G^{2}L\log\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_{1})}{\widehat{r}^{2}(1-\gamma)}\right)}{\tau^{2}\widehat{r}^{2}t}\right\}

(9)

where
C^{\prime\prime}
is a universal constant,
\Delta:=\left(\log\left(\frac{6}{\delta}\right)+s\log\left(\frac{2\max(m,n)}{s}\right)\log(\max(m,n))\right)
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}\left[\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta^{\prime}_{t})\right]\leq\frac{\widehat{r}^{2}}{2}.

(10)

Remark.
The theorem shows that when (
9
) holds, the solution
\theta
continuously stays in
\Omega(\widehat{r},s)
and we will not see the degradation in performance. The condition (
9
) essentially leads to the so called phase transition phenomenon, i.e. the performance over the testing data would degrade with more training epochs. In addition, if the condition (
9
) does not hold, then we may have a worse performance over the testing data after the first epoch.

Proof of Theorem

Proof.

For each iteration

, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\rangle]+\frac{L}{2}\mathrm{E}[\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_{t}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}

\displaystyle-\eta\left(1-\frac{\eta L}{2}\right)\mathrm{E}[\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}]+2\eta^{2}G^{2}L

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathrm{E}[\|(1-\gamma)\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})+\gamma\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}]+2\eta^{2}G^{2}L

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathrm{E}[(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+2(1-\gamma)\gamma\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t}),\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\rangle]+2\eta^{2}G^{2}L,

(11)

where (a) uses the smoothness of
\mathcal{L}
in Assumption
1
(ii); (b) uses
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta g_{t}
where
\mathrm{E}[g_{t}]=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})
, and
\mathrm{E}[\|\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})-g_{t}\|^{2}]\leq 4G^{2}
due to Assumption
1
(i) with Jensen’s inequality; (c) uses
\eta L\leq 1
and the definition of
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
.
We consider tow cases. Case I. when
1>\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, define
\alpha=\frac{1-\gamma}{2\gamma-1}
,
by Young’s inequality we get

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}-2\langle(1-\gamma)\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t}),\gamma\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{1+\alpha}{\alpha}(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}.

Case II. when
0<\gamma\leq\frac{1}{2}
, define
\alpha=\frac{\gamma}{1-2\gamma}
, by Young’s inequality we get

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}-2\langle(1-\gamma)\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t}),\gamma\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}(1-\gamma)^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}\gamma^{2}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t+1})-\mathcal{L}(\theta_{t})]\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathrm{E}[(1-\gamma)\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}+\gamma\|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})\|^{2}]+2\eta^{2}G^{2}L

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathrm{E}[2\mu_{a}(1-\gamma)(\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\text{min}})+2\mu_{b}\gamma(\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})-\mathcal{L}_{b}^{\text{min}})]+2\eta^{2}G^{2}L,

(12)

where the last inequality is due to PL-condition of

and
\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[(1-\gamma)\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t+1})+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t+1})-(1-\gamma)\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{t})-\gamma\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta_{t})]

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{m})]\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta\tau}{2}\right)^{m}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}{1-\gamma}+\frac{4\eta G^{2}L}{\tau(1-\gamma)}

\displaystyle\frac{8G^{2}L}{m\tau^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(1+\log\left(\frac{m\tau^{2}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{0})}{8G^{2}L}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{4G^{2}L\log(m)}{m\tau^{2}(1-\gamma)}\right).

(16)

1-\delta
,

\widehat{r}^{2}\geq r^{2}-\frac{2F^{2}}{3m}\log\frac{2}{\delta}-2F\sqrt{\frac{\widehat{r}^{2}}{m}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}

Using the assumption that
m\geq 64F^{2}\log(2/\delta)
, we have, with a probability
1-\delta

r^{2}\leq 2\widehat{r}^{2}+\frac{4F^{2}}{3m}\log\frac{2}{\delta},

and therefore

\sigma_{P}^{2}\leq 2\widehat{r}^{2}+\frac{4F^{2}}{3m}\log\frac{2}{\delta}.

As a result, with a probability
1-2\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{mG}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y_{i}^{a})\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{E}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{mG}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y_{i}^{a})\nabla_{k}f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-\nabla_{k}\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)\right|\right]+\frac{3F}{m}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+2\widehat{r}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{m}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\mathrm{E}_{x,\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{mG}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)\right|\right]+\frac{4F\log(1/\delta)}{m}+2\widehat{r}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{m}}.

We then bound
\mathrm{E}_{x,\sigma}[\cdot]
. Using Klei-Rio bound
(
Koltchinskii 2011
)
, with a probability
1-2\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{x,\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{E}_{\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)\right|\right]+2\widehat{r}\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{m}}+\frac{9F}{m}\log\frac{1}{\delta}

We can bound the expectation term using Dudley entropy bound (Lemma A.5 of
(
Bartlett et al. 2017
)
),i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{mGF}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}_{\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s),h\in S_{d}}\left|\frac{1}{mGF}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\langle\nabla f(x_{i}^{a};\theta),h\rangle\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\alpha}\left(\frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{12}{m}\int_{\alpha}^{\sqrt{m}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}(\widehat{r},s);\epsilon)}d\epsilon\right)

(17)

where
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{G}(r,s);\epsilon)
is the covering number of proper
\epsilon
-net over
\mathcal{G}(r,s)
. Here
\mathcal{G}(r,s)
is defined as

\mathcal{G}(\widehat{r},s)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:|v|\leq\frac{\sqrt{m}\widehat{r}}{F},|v|_{1}\leq\frac{\sqrt{ms}\widehat{r}}{F}\right\}

Using the covering number result for sparse vectors (Lemma 3.4 of
(
Plan and Vershynin 2013
)
), we have

\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{G}(\widehat{r},s);\frac{\sqrt{m}\widehat{r}}{F}\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{Cs}{\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{2m}{s}

and therefore

\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{G}(\widehat{r},s);\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{Cm\widehat{r}^{2}s}{F^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\log\frac{2m}{s}

Plugging into the Dudley entropy bound, we have

\mathrm{E}_{\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{mF}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\right|\right]\leq\inf\limits_{\alpha}\left(\frac{4\alpha}{\sqrt{m}}+\frac{12\widehat{r}}{F}\sqrt{\frac{Cs}{m}\log\frac{2m}{s}}\log\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\alpha}\right)

By choosing
\alpha
as

\alpha=\frac{3\widehat{r}}{F}\sqrt{Cs\log\frac{2m}{s}},

we have

\mathrm{E}_{\sigma}\left[\max\limits_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(f(x_{i}^{a};\theta)-y(x_{i}^{a}))\right|\right]\leq 12\widehat{r}\sqrt{\frac{Cs}{m}\log\frac{2m}{s}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{m}{9\sqrt{Cs\log(2m/s)}}\right)\right)

Combining the above results together, with a probability
1-3\delta
, we prove the inequality (
7
).
Similarly, we can bound
\max_{\theta\in\Omega(\widehat{r},s)}|\nabla\mathcal{L}_{b}(\theta)-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{b}(\theta)|
.
∎

5
Conclusions

Learning with noisily labeled data has been studied in may deep supervised learning tasks, and its two interesting phenomena called clean data first and phase transition have been empirically observed. We provide a theoretical analysis for rethinking about these two empirical phenomena from the view of learning theory and non-convex optimization. The result reveals that the models first learn clean data and then after that the testing performance would not be degraded when the percentage of data with corrupted class labels is not too large.
