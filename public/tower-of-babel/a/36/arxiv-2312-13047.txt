On compact subsets of the reals
Wojciech Bielas, Mateusz Kula, Szymon Plewik

Abstract
. Motivated by results of J. R. Kline and R. L. Moore (1919) that a compact subset of the plane, homeomorphic to a subset of the reals, lies on the arc, we give a purely topological characterisation of compact sets of the reals. This allows us to reduce investigations of Cantorvals to properties of countable linear orders and to show, applying the Mazurkiewicz–Sierpiński Theorem (1920), that there exist continuum many non-homeomorphic L L -Cantorvals.

Primary: 54F65; Secondary: 26A03, 54F05

1.
Introduction

If
X
is a compact subset of the reals, then

(\star)
. Each non-trivial component of
X
is an arc
[a,b]
and the set
(a,b)=[a,b]\setminus\{a,b\}
is open.

J. R. Kline and R. L. Moore
[
10
, Theorem 1]
showed that a compact subset of the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that satisfies condition
(\star)
, lies on an arc.
We are impressed by this achievement, more than 100 years old, and we have decided to continue this.
While Kline and Moore dealt only with compact subsets of the plane, their results had been generalised, for example
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
.
Here, we present a variation that gives an internal characterisation of compact subsets of the reals.
Also, we have found some applications to Cantorvals.
As far as we know, the name Cantorval has been introduced by P. Mendes and F. Oliveira
[
6
]
.
The first characterization of Cantorvals was given by J. A. Guthrie and J. E. Nymann
[
4
]
.
As it is noted by S. Głąb and J. Marchwicki
[
3
]
, a few authors pointed out the existence of some concrete Cantorvals before Guthrie and Nymann.

By an
arc
we understand a closed interval
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, or a homeomorphic image of a closed interval
[a,b]
, which also will be denoted by
[a,b]
.
In both cases, points
a
and
b
are called
endpoints
of the arc
[a,b]
.
Metric notions, which are described in
[
2
, pp. 248–254]
, we supplement with

B^{*}(Y,\varepsilon)=\operatorname{int}_{X}Y\cup B(Y\setminus\operatorname{int}_{X}Y,\varepsilon),

where
B(Y\setminus\operatorname{int}_{X}Y,\varepsilon)
is the
\varepsilon
-ball around the set
Y\setminus\operatorname{int}_{X}Y
.
If
Y
is a compact subset of the reals
\mathbb{R}
, then any component of
\mathbb{R}\setminus Y
is called a
Y
-
gap
and any non-trivial component of
Y
is called a
Y
-
interval
.
When the set
Y
is fixed, we say gap and interval instead of
Y
-
gap
and
Y
-
interval
, respectively.
Let us recall the following well-known facts.

Fact 1
.

If
X
is a compact Hausdorff space, then any component
S\subseteq X
is the intersection of the family of all clopen sets containing
S
.

Proof.

In compact Hausdorff spaces, components coincide with quasi-components, see
[
2
, Theorem 6.1.23]
.
∎

Fact 2
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space. If a component
S
is contained in an open set
U
, then there exists a clopen set
V
such that
S\subseteq V\subseteq U
.

Proof.

In view of Fact 1, choose a family
\mathcal{V}
of clopen sets such that
S=\bigcap\mathcal{V}
.
Choose a finite subfamily
\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
such that
X\setminus\bigcap\mathcal{V}^{*}\supseteq X\setminus U
.
Therefore
\bigcap\mathcal{V}^{*}\subseteq U
.
∎

Our notation is standard, in particular, a closed and open set is called
clopen
. For notions not defined here, see
[
2
]
and
[
13
]
.

2.
The Kline–Moore theorem revisited

If a compact metric space
X
satisfies condition
(\star)
, then there are at most countably many non-trivial components.
Indeed, each non-trivial component of
X
has non-empty interior, different components are disjoint and
X
is separable.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{n}
be the family of all clopen sets
V\subseteq X
for which there exists a component
S
of
X
such that

\textstyle S\subseteq V\subseteq B^{*}(S,\frac{1}{n}).

Clearly, families
\mathcal{B}_{n}
are defined for any metric space
X
and
\mathcal{B}_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
We say that a family
\mathcal{B}
of clopen sets of
X
is a
base for components
, whenever any component of
X
can be obtained as the intersection of a family contained in
\mathcal{B}
.
If
X
is a compact metric space, then the family
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is a base for components, for each
n>0
.
Indeed, if
S
is a component of
X
, then
S=\bigcap\mathcal{V}
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a family of clopen sets.
For each
V\in\mathcal{V}
, by Fact
2
, there exists a clopen set
U_{V}
such that

\textstyle S\subseteq U_{V}\subseteq V\cap B^{*}(S,\frac{1}{n}).

Therefore
S=\bigcap\{U_{V}\colon V\in\mathcal{V}\}
and all
U_{V}
belong to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
V\subseteq X
be compact and clopen.
Each of the following facts is more or less obvious.

•

If
V
is the sum of finitely many components, then each such component is open and belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

•

If
V
contains infinitely many components and the diameter
\delta(V)
is less than
\frac{1}{n}
, then
V\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Indeed, by compactness of
V
one of the components contained in
V
is not open, so there is a component
S\subseteq V
and a point
x\in S\setminus\operatorname{int}_{X}S
, and

\textstyle S\subseteq V\subseteq B(x,\frac{1}{n})\subseteq B^{*}(S,\frac{1}{n}).

•

If
S
is a component of
X
such that
S\subseteq V\subseteq B^{*}(S,\frac{1}{n})
and
W\subseteq X\setminus S
is a clopen set, then
S\subseteq V\setminus W\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space. If each
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a cover consisting of clopen sets and each
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
, then
\bigcup\{\mathcal{P}_{n}:n>0\}
is a base for components.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq X
be a component and, for each
n>0
, take the clopen set
V_{n}
such that
S\subseteq V_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
So, there exists components
S_{n}
such that

\textstyle S\subseteq V_{n}\subseteq W_{n}\subseteq B^{*}(S_{n},\frac{1}{n})\subseteq B(S_{n},\frac{1}{n}),

where each
W_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
witnesses that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and each
S_{n}
witnesses that
W_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
We get
S=\bigcap\{V_{n}:n>0\}
.
Indeed, suppose

a\in\bigcap\{V_{n}\colon n>0\}\setminus S.

Choosing a clopen set
U\supseteq S
such that
a\notin U
, we have

\varepsilon=d(S,X\setminus U)>0.

Let points
b_{n}\in S_{n}
be such that
d(a,b_{n})<\frac{1}{n}
, therefore
\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}=a
.
For all but finitely many
n
, we have
b_{n}\in S_{n}\subseteq X\setminus U
and
B(S,{\varepsilon})\cap S_{n}=\emptyset
, and consequently
S\cap B(S_{n},\varepsilon)=\emptyset
.
On the other hand, for sufficiently large
n
, we get

\textstyle S\subseteq V_{n}\subseteq B(S_{n},\frac{1}{n})\subseteq B(S_{n},\varepsilon);

a contradiction.
∎

Recall that a family
\mathcal{P}
of subsets of
X
is called a
partition
, whenever
\bigcup\mathcal{P}=X
and
\mathcal{P}
consists of non-empty pairwise disjoint sets.

Lemma 4
.

If
X
is a compact metric space, a set
V\subseteq X
is clopen and
n>0
, then there exists a family
\mathcal{P}
, consisting of pairwise disjoint clopen sets, that is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
V=\bigcup\mathcal{P}
.
In addition, if
X
satisfies condition
(\star)
, then
\mathcal{P}
can be chosen such that
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Proof.

Fix
n>0
.
For each component
S\subseteq V
, using Fact
2
, choose a clopen set
V(S)
such that

\textstyle S\subseteq V(S)\subseteq B^{*}(S,\frac{1}{n})\cap V.

Thus sets
V(S)
constitute a cover of
V
, so there exists a finite cover
\{V(S_{1}),\ldots,V(S_{k})\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
If

W_{i}=V(S_{i})\setminus\bigcup\{V({S_{j}})\colon j<i\},

then sets
W_{i}\neq\emptyset
form a partition of
V
that is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Assume that condition
(\star)
is fulfilled.
For each non-trivial component
S
let the sets
V(S)
be such that

\textstyle S\subseteq V(S)\subseteq B^{*}(S,\varepsilon)\cap V,

where
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{2n}
is smaller than half the distance between the endpoints of
S
.
Let the sets
W_{i}
be defined as above.
If
S_{i}\subseteq W_{i}
, then
W_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Otherwise
S_{i}\cap W_{i}=\emptyset
and
W_{i}
is a union of at most two disjoint clopen sets, each one of diameter less than
\frac{1}{n}
. Any clopen set of diameter less than
\frac{1}{n}
with infinitely many components belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
. But, if a clopen set has finitely many components, then these components are open and belong to
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
Whatever the case is, there exists a partition of
W_{i}
contained in
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

In a compact metric space
X
, there exists a sequence
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
of finite partitions of clopen sets such that each
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
In addition, if
X
satisfies condition
(\star)
, then
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be chosen such that
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
4
, define inductively partitions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\mathcal{P}_{2},\ldots
such that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
refines
\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
refines
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
, while
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\{X\}
. If
X
fulfils condition
(\star)
, then we apply the second part of Lemma
4
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
be a compact metric space and
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
be a sequence of partitions as in Lemma
5
.
If
S\subseteq X
is a component and
S\subseteq V_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
always hold, then
S=\bigcap\{V_{n}:n>0\}
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq X
be a component and
S\subseteq V_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
for each
n
.
Then the family
\mathcal{U}=\{V_{n}:n>0\}
consists of exactly those elements of
\bigcup\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon n>0\}
that contain
S
.
We have
\bigcap\mathcal{U}=S
, since
\bigcup\{\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon n>0\}
is a base for components.
∎

If a compact metric space
(X,d)
and
W\subseteq X
is a clopen set, then an arc
[a,b]\subseteq W
is said to be a
core
of
W
, whenever
[a,b]
is a non-trivial component such that

\textstyle[a,b]\subseteq W\subseteq B^{*}([a,b],\frac{1}{2}d(a,b))\mbox{ and }(a,b)\mbox{ is open}.

Not every clopen set has a core, but if it is the case, then such a core is unique.
Indeed, if
S_{1}=[a_{1},b_{1}]
and
S_{2}=[a_{2},b_{2}]
are different cores of
W
, then
S_{2}\subseteq B(a_{1},\frac{1}{2}d(a_{1},b_{1}))
or
S_{2}\subseteq B(b_{1},\frac{1}{2}d(a_{1},b_{1}))
, which implies
d(a_{2},b_{2})<d(a_{1},b_{1})
.
The interchange of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
gives
d(a_{1},b_{1})<d(a_{2},b_{2})
; a contradiction.

Theorem 7
.

If a compact and metric space
(X,d)
satisfies condition
(\star)
,
then
X
is homeomorphic to a subset of the reals.

Proof.

For each non-trivial component
[a,b]
of
X
, choose a homeomorphism
f_{[a,b]}\colon[a,b]\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
f_{[a,b]}(a)=0
.
Let
(\mathcal{P}_{n})
be a sequence of finite partitions of
X
such that
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a refinement of
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
.
Without loss of generality, by modifying the construction of partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
used in Lemma
5
, assume that if
W\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
has the core
[a,b]
, then for any
V\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
such that
V\subseteq W
, we have
V\subseteq B(a,\frac{1}{n})
or
V\subseteq B(b,\frac{1}{n})
, or
[a,b]\subseteq V
.
Define (linear) strict ordering
\sqsubset_{n}
, each one on the family
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, such that the following conditions hold.

(1)

V_{1}\sqsubset_{n}V_{2}
, whenever
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
V_{1}\subseteq W_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
,
V_{2}\subseteq W_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
and
W_{1}\sqsubset_{n-1}W_{2}
.

(2)

If
W\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
has the core
[a,b]\subseteq V^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, then for any
V\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, such that
V\subseteq W
, we have: if
V\subseteq B(a,\frac{1}{n})
, then
V\sqsubset_{n}V^{*}
; if
V\subseteq B(b,\frac{1}{n})
, then
V^{*}\sqsubset_{n}V
.

Assume inductively that the order
\sqsubset_{n}
is already defined. Given two sets
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
the order
\sqsubset_{n+1}
between them is determined by condition (1) unless they are subsets of the same set
W\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
In such a case, define an arbitrarily linear order on
\{V\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}\colon V\subseteq W\}
, maintaining condition (2).

Given orders
\sqsubset_{n}
we order linearly the family
\mathcal{C}
of all components of
X
as follows.
Let
S
,
S^{\prime}
be two different components of
X
.
Consider sequences of clopen sets
(W_{n}),(W_{n}^{\prime})
such that
W_{n},W_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\bigcap_{n}W_{n}=S
, and
\bigcap_{n}W_{n}^{\prime}=S^{\prime}
.
If
n
is the smallest number such that
W_{n}\neq W_{n}^{\prime}
, then let
\sqsubset
order
S
and
S^{\prime}
in the same manner as
\sqsubset_{n}
orders sets
W_{n}
and
W_{n}^{\prime}
.
If
\mathcal{C}^{*}
is the union of sets
\{(S,0)\colon S\text{ is a trivial component}\}
and
\{(I,x)\colon I\text{ is a non-trivial component and }x\in[0,1]\}
, then equip it with the lexicographic order
\prec
induced by
\sqsubset
and the standard inequality
<
.

The space
X
is homeomorphic to
\mathcal{C}^{*}
with the order topology.
Indeed, for any
x\in X
, put

F(x)=\begin{cases}([a,b],f_{[a,b]}(x)),&\mbox{ if }x\in[a,b]\in\mathcal{C};\\
(S,0),&\mbox{ if }S=\{x\}\in\mathcal{C}.\end{cases}

Clearly
F\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{*}
is a bijection.

Fix
x\in X
and a sequence of sets
(W_{n})
such that
x\in W_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
If
\{x\}
is a trivial component or is the right endpoint of a non-trivial component
[a,b]
, then

F^{-1}[(F(x),\to)]=\bigcup\{V\colon V\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\mbox{ and }W_{n}\sqsubset_{n}V\mbox{ and }n>0\}

is open, being a union of open sets.

But if
I=[a,b]\subseteq X
is a non-trivial component and
x\in[a,b)
, then we have that the set

F^{-1}[(\leftarrow,F(x)]]=[a,x]\cup\bigcup\{W\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\colon W\sqsubset_{n}W_{n}\mbox{ and }n>0\}

is closed.
Thus,
F
is continuous and since the domain of
F
is compact, hence
F
is a homeomorphism.

In the space
\mathcal{C}^{*}
there exist at most countably many gaps, i.e. points with immediate successors with respect to
\prec
.
Filling these gaps with copies of the interval
(0,1)
, we obtain an arc that contains a copy of
X
.
∎

Remark 8
.

In the proof of the above theorem the ordering of endpoints of non-trivial components did not matter.

3.
Some remarks on Cantorvals

Authors of the paper
[
6
]
considered the arithmetic sum of two Cantor sets on the reals.
For some classes of such sets the arithmetic sum can be of the form which they called: M-Cantorval, L-Cantorval or R-Cantorval. Each of these notions represents a compact subset of the reals with given properties. Also, nowadays it is common to call what they meant by M-Cantorval simply Cantorval or sometimes symmetric Cantorval, see
[
7
, p. 16]
or compare
[
8
]
.
There are a few approaches to the definition of a Cantorval.
For example, a
Cantorval
is a nonempty compact subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that

K
is the closure of its interior and
endpoints of any non-trivial component of
K
are limit points
of trivial components of
K
, see
[
7
, Definition 13]
.
Let us describe a specific Cantorval, compare
[
1
, p. 354]
. Consider the classical ternary Cantor set and let
G_{n}
be the union of
2^{n-1}
open intervals removed at the
n
-th step of its construction, i.e.

\textstyle G_{1}=\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }G_{n+1}=\frac{1}{3}G_{n}\cup\left(\frac{1}{3}G_{n}+\frac{2}{3}\right).

The set

C=[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}G_{2k}

is a Cantorval and we will call it
model Cantorval
. As it was shown, see
[
6
, p. 343]
,
[
7
, p. 16]
,
[
1
, p. 355]
,
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
or
[
15
, Wniosek 26.]
, any two Cantorvals are homeomorphic.
Topological characterizations of a Cantorval are given in
[
1
, Theorem 21.18.]
and
[
6
, pp. 330/331]
.
Another characterization can be also extracted from the proofs of Guthrie–Nymann’s classification theorem on sets of subsums of a convergent series of real numbers, see
[
4
, Theorem 1.(iii)]
.

Following the paper
[
6
]
, a subset
X
of the reals is an
L-Cantorval
[respectively
R-Cantorval
] whenever it is nonempty, compact and any gap has an interval adjacent to its right [left] and is accumulated on the left [right] by infinitely many intervals and gaps.
Thus, if
X
is an L-Cantorval, then
\min X
is the endpoint of the
X
-interval.
Directly from the definition, we get that the map
x\mapsto-x
carries an L-Cantorval onto the R-Cantorval and vice versa.
Also, if
[a,b]
is an interval in an L-Cantorval, then
b
cannot be an endpoint of any gap, but
a
is not necessarily an endpoint of a gap, i.e.
a
can be accumulated by infinitely many gaps and intervals.
It is known that there exist non-homeomorphic L-Cantorvals, see the last paragraph of Appendix in
[
6
]
.
As we show, there exist continuum many non-homeomorphic L-Cantorvals, see Theorem
9
.

We say that an endpoint
e
of a non-trivial component
I\subseteq X
is
free
, whenever
X
satisfies condition
(\star)
and
e\in\operatorname{int}_{X}I
. A set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is called
left-oriented
, whenever any
X
-interval with exactly one free endpoint has the left endpoint free.
If
(a,b)
is an
X
-gap such that the point
b
is a limit point of free left endpoints of
X
-intervals, then
(a,b)
is called an
inappropriate

X
-gap.
If
X
is left-oriented, each non-empty clopen subset of
X
contains a non-trivial component with exactly one free endpoint,
\{b\}
is a trivial component and
b
is the right endpoint of an
X
-gap
(a,b)
, then
(a,b)
is an inappropriate gap.
If
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, then we say that a clopen subset
W\subseteq X
is
segment-like
with respect to
X
, if
W=X\cap I
where
I
is a closed interval and
\min W
belongs to an
X
-interval.

Assume that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a compact set and
(a,b)
is an inappropriate
X
-gap. We say that a pair consisting of a function
f\colon X\rightarrow f[X]
and a family

\mathcal{P}=\{V_{k}\colon k\geqslant 0\}

of non-empty clopen sets is a
correction
of
(a,b)
with respect to
\varepsilon>0
, if the following conditions are fulfilled.

•

\bigcup\{V_{k}\colon{k\geqslant 0}\}=X\cap(b,b+\eta)
where
\eta<\min\{b-a,\varepsilon\}
.

•

\max V_{k+1}<\min V_{k}
and each
V_{k}
is segment-like.

•

f(x)=\begin{cases}x-\delta_{k},&x\in V_{k};\\
x,&x\in X\setminus\bigcup\{V_{k}\colon{k>0}\},\end{cases}

where
\delta_{k}=\max V_{k}+\min V_{k}-2b
.

Clearly, if
f
is a correction of
(a,b)
with respect to
\varepsilon
, then

f
is a homeomorphism,
for any
x\in X
, we have
0\leqslant x-f(x)<2\varepsilon
and if
X
is left-oriented, then
f[X]
is left-oriented. Also, the
f[X]
-gap
(b,\min V_{0})
is not inappropriate.

Theorem 9
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact metric space that satisfies condition
(\star)
.
If each non-empty clopen subset of
X
contains a non-trivial component with exactly one free endpoint, then
X
is homeomorphic to an L-Cantorval.

Proof.

By Theorem
7
and Remark
8
, we can assume that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
X
is left-oriented.
Assume that
(a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}),\ldots
is an enumeration of all inappropriate
X
-gaps.
Let
f_{1}\colon X\to X_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*}=\{V_{k}\colon k\geqslant 0\}
be a correction of the
X
-gap
(a_{1},b_{1})
with respect to
\varepsilon=1
. Put
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{f_{1}[V_{k}]\colon k>0\}\cup\{(-\infty,a_{1}]\cap X,[\min V_{0},+\infty)\cap X\}
.

For
i<n
, assume inductively, that there are already defined families
\mathcal{P}_{i}
of clopen sets in
X_{i}
and homeomorphisms
f_{i}\colon X_{i-1}\to X_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
.
Let
f_{n}\colon X_{n-1}\to X_{n}
and a family
\mathcal{P}^{*}=\{V_{k}\colon k\geqslant 0\}
be a correction of an
X_{n-1}
-gap
(p_{n},q_{n})
with respect to
2^{-n}
, where

(p_{n},q_{n})=(f_{n-1}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(a_{n}),f_{n-1}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(b_{n})).

Choose a family
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, which consists of:

(1)

all sets from
\mathcal{P}_{n-1}\setminus\{W\}
, where
p_{n}\in W\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}
;

(2)

the set
W\cap[\min V_{0},\max W]
;

(3)

the set
W\cap[\min W,p_{n}]
;

(4)

sets
f_{n}[V_{k}]\subseteq(p_{n},q_{n})
, for
k>0
.

Since each
f_{n}
is a correction of the
X_{n-1}
-gap
(p_{n},q_{n})
with respect to
\varepsilon=2^{-n}
, then the sequence
(f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1})
is uniformly convergent to the continuous surjection
h\colon X\to X_{\infty}
.

Suppose
x_{0},x_{1}\in X
,
x_{0}\neq x_{1}
and
h(x_{0})=h(x_{1})
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that
f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(x_{0})\in V\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(x_{1})\notin V
, where
n>0
.
Therefore
f_{m}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(x_{0})\in[\min V,\max V]
and
f_{m}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(x_{1})\notin[\min V,\max V]
for each
m\geqslant n
, which is in conflict with
h(x_{0})=h(x_{1})
.
Thus,
h
is a continuous bijection, i.e.
h\colon X\to h[X]=X_{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a homeomorphism.

If
J
is an arbitrary
X
-interval, then
f_{n+1}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}|_{J}=f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}|_{J}
for sufficiently large
n
.
Therefore
X_{\infty}
is left-oriented, since each
f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}
preserves this property.

Suppose that
(c,d)
is an inappropriate
X_{\infty}
-gap.
If

d_{n}=f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}(h^{-1}(d)),

then the sequence
(d_{n})
converges to
d
and there exists
n
such that
d_{n+1}<d_{n}
, since
h^{-1}(d)
cannot be the right endpoint of an inappropriate
X
-gap.
Then
d_{n}\in V_{k}
, where the set
V_{k}
is segment-like and is moved to the left by
f_{n}
.
If
2^{-n}<d-c
, then we get
d<\min V_{k}<d_{m}
for each
m\geqslant n
, a contradiction.
Hence the proof is finished, since we show that
X_{\infty}
is an
L
-Cantorval.
∎

For a compact set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we adopt the notation from
[
15
]
.
Namely, let
\mathcal{A}_{X}
be the family of all
X
-gaps and
\mathcal{B}_{X}
be the family of all
X
-intervals. Thus
\mathcal{A}_{X}
consists of pairwise disjoint open intervals,
\mathcal{B}_{X}
consists of pairwise disjoint closed intervals and both families are countable. Note that
\bigcup(\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X})
is a dense subset of
\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, if
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup(\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X})
, then
\{x\}
is a component of
X
, so
x
is the limit point of
\bigcup\mathcal{A}_{X}
.
The union
\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}
can be naturally equipped with an order, namely
I<J
whenever
x<y
for all
x\in I
and
y\in J
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
X
and
Y
be compact subsets of the reals.
There exists an increasing homeomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f[X]=Y
if and only if there exists an order isomorphism
F\colon\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{B}_{Y}
such that
F[\mathcal{A}_{X}]=\mathcal{A}_{Y}
.

Proof.

If
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an increasing homeomorphism such that
f[X]=Y
, then
F(I)=f[I]
is a required homeomorphism.

Conversely, assume
F\colon\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}\to\mathcal{A}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{B}_{Y}
is an isomorphism that carries
\mathcal{A}_{X}
onto
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
. If
I\in\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}
, then let
f_{I}\colon I\to F(I)
to be an increasing homeomorphism. For each
x\in I
, put
f(x)=f_{I}(x)
. Sets
\bigcup(\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X})
and
\bigcup(\mathcal{A}_{Y}\cup\mathcal{B}_{Y})
are dense in
\mathbb{R}
, so already defined function
f
is na increasing bijection between dense subsets of the reals. This bijection can be uniquely extended to a desired homeomorphism.
∎

If
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a Cantor set, then
\mathcal{B}_{C}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{A}_{C}
is densely ordered. Any two countable densely ordered sets with the first and the last elements are isomorphic, since they are isomorphic to
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
.
From Proposition
10
it follows that any compact set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{B}_{X}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is densely ordered, is an image of the Cantor set by a homeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
.
In other words, we get a well-known property of the Cantor set, i.e. if a non-empty compact set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is dense in itself and
\operatorname{int}_{\mathbb{R}}X=\emptyset
, then
X
is the image of the Cantor set by an autohomeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]=A_{\mathbb{Q}}\cup B_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a partition such that
A_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
B_{\mathbb{Q}}
are dense in
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]
and
\{0,1\}\subseteq A_{\mathbb{Q}}

.

If

Corollary 12
.

If a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is non-empty, compact and regularly closed such that no
X
-interval has a free endpoint, then there exists a homeomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the image
f[X]
is the model Cantorval.

Proof.

Clearly, the interior
\operatorname{int}_{\mathbb{R}}X
is dense in
X
and no
X
-gap and
X
-interval have a common endpoint.
Also, between any two
X
-gaps lies an
X
-interval, between an
X
-gap and an
X
-interval lies an
X
-gap, and also between any two
X
-intervals there is an
X
-gap.
Therefore sets
\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
\mathcal{B}_{X}
are dense in
\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}
, i.e. between any two elements of
\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}
lie an element of
\mathcal{A}_{X}
and an element of
\mathcal{B}_{X}
.
By Lemma
11
there exists an order isomorphism
F\colon\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{C}\cup\mathcal{B}_{C}
such that
F[\mathcal{A}_{X}]=\mathcal{A}_{C}
, where
C
is the model Cantorval.

there exists a homeomorphism
f
such that the image
f[X]
is the model Cantorval.

Corollary 13
.

Let

Proof.

Suppose that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a special
L
-Cantorval and
(\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X},\leqslant)
is the linear space assigned to
X
.
We have the following.

(I).

If
I\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
I
is not the greatest element of
\mathcal{A}_{X}\cup\mathcal{B}_{X}
, then there exists
J\in\mathcal{B}_{X}
such that
J
is the immediate successor of
I
.

(II).

If
J\in\mathcal{B}_{X}
, then there exists
I\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
such that
I
is the immediate predecessor of
J
.

(III).

The ordered space
(\mathcal{A}_{X},\leqslant)
is isomorphic to the set of rational numbers contained in the interval
[0,1]
with the natural ordering.

Indeed, by the definition the right endpoint of an
X
-gap belongs to the
X
-interval, which implies (I).
The condition of an
L
-Cantorval being special implies (II).
Suppose
I,J\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
and
I<J
.
Let
K
be an
X
-interval containing the right endpoint of
I
.
Since
\max K<\inf J
, therefore there exists an
X
-gap that lies between
I
and
J
.

\mathcal{A}_{X}
is densely ordered, hence isomorphic to

.

has an immediate predecessor in

.

is the immediate predecessor of

. According to (II) there exists

which is the immediate predecessor of

. Consequently, no element from
\mathcal{A}_{X}
lies between

Lemma 14
.

Let
(A_{1}\cup B_{1},\leqslant)
and
(A_{2}\cup B_{2},\leqslant)

(I)–(III)

is substituted for

.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\alpha
be a countable ordinal and
A\subseteq\alpha
.
There exists an
L
-Cantorval
X
such that the set
\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{X}\colon C\mbox{ has no free endpoint}\}
with the topology inherited from
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is homeomorphic to
A
with the topology inherited from linear topology on
\alpha
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\alpha\subseteq[0,1]
is equipped with the topology inherited from
[0,1]
is compact.
Let
a_{0},a_{1},\ldots
be an enumeration of
A
.
The set

\textstyle\mathcal{I}=([0,1]\times\{0\})\cup\bigcup\{\{a_{n}\}\times[0,\frac{1}{2^{n}}]\colon n<\omega\}

Replacing each component of
