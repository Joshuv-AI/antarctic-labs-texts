A bound for the exterior product of S S -units Thanks: The authors are grateful to the anonymous referee. Shabnam Akhtari’s research was supported by the Simons Foundation Collaboration Grant, Award Number 635880, and by the National Science Foundation Awards DMS-2001281 and DMS-2327098.
Shabnam Akhtari, Jeffrey D. Vaaler

Abstract
. We generalize an inequality for the determinant of a real matrix proved by A. Schinzel, to more general exterior products of vectors in Euclidean space. We apply this inequality to the logarithmic embedding of S S -units contained in a number field k k . This leads to a bound for the exterior product of S S -units expressed as a product of heights. Using a volume formula of P. McMullen we show that our inequality is sharp up to a constant that depends only on the rank of the S S -unit group but not on the field k k . Our inequality is related to a conjecture of F. Rodriguez Villegas.

1.
Introduction

Let
k
be an algebraic number field,
k^{\times}
its multiplicative group of nonzero elements, and
h:k^{\times}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

the absolute, logarithmic, Weil height (or simply the
height
). In
[
1
]
we proved inequalities that compare the size of an

S
-regulator with the product of heights of a maximal collection of independent
S
-units. If
k\subseteq l
are both number fields the
results in
[
2
]
extend inequalities of this sort to the multiplicative group of relative units. In the present paper we prove
analogous inequalities for the exterior product of a collection of independent
S
-units that is not a maximal collection.

At each place
v
of
k
we write
k_{v}
for the completion of
k
at
v
. We use two absolute values
\|\ \|_{v}
and
|\ |_{v}
from the place
v
.
The absolute value
\|\ \|_{v}
extends the usual archimedean or non-archimedean absolute value on the subfield
\mathbb{Q}
. Then
|\ |_{v}
must be a
power of
\|\ \|_{v}
, and we set

(1.1)

|\ |_{v}=\|\ \|_{v}^{d_{v}/d},

where
d_{v}=[k_{v}:\mathbb{Q}_{v}]
is the local degree of the extension and
d=[k:\mathbb{Q}]
is the global degree. With these normalizations
the height of an algebraic number
\alpha\not=0
that belongs to
k
is given by

(1.2)

h(\alpha)=\sum_{v}\log^{+}|\alpha|_{v}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{v}\bigl|\log|\alpha|_{v}\bigr|.

Each sum in (
1.2
) is over the set of all places
v
of
k
, and the equality between the two sums follows
from the product formula.

Let
S
be a finite set of places of
k
such that
S
contains all the archimedean places. Then

O_{S}=\big\{\gamma\in k:\|\gamma\|_{v}\leq 1\ \text{for all places}\ v\notin S\big\}

is the ring of
S
-integers in
k
, and

O_{S}^{\times}=\big\{\gamma\in k^{\times}:\|\gamma\|_{v}=1\ \text{for all places}\ v\notin S\big\}

is the multiplicative group of
S
-units in
O_{S}
. The abelian group
O_{S}^{\times}
has rank
r
, where
|S|=r+1
, and we assume that
r
is positive.
We write
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{v})
for a (column) vector in
\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
where the coordinates of
\boldsymbol{x}
are indexed by places
v
in
S
. And we write

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{v\in S}|x_{v}|

for the
l^{1}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{x}
. The
logarithmic embedding
of
O_{S}^{\times}
into
\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
is the homomorphism defined at each point

\alpha
in
O_{S}^{\times}
by

(1.3)

\alpha\mapsto\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha\|_{v}\bigr),

where the rows of the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
on the right of (
1.3
) are indexed by places
v
in
S
. It follows from (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)
that if
\alpha
is a point in
O_{S}^{\times}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is the image of
\alpha
in
\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
using the logarithmic embedding (
1.3
), then

(1.4)

2[k:\mathbb{Q}]h(\alpha)=\sum_{v\in S}\bigl|d_{v}\log\|\alpha\|_{v}\bigr|=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}.

The kernel of the logarithmic embedding (
1.3
) is the torsion subgroup

(1.5)

\big\{\alpha\in O_{S}^{\times}:\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha\|_{v}\bigr)=\boldsymbol{0}\big\}=\Tor\bigl(O_{S}^{\times}\bigr)

of all roots of unity in
k^{\times}
. It is known that (
1.5
) is a finite, cyclic group, and from the
S
-unit theorem of
Dirichlet-Chevalley-Hasse (see
[
13
, Theorem 3.12]
) we learn that the quotient

\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)=O_{S}^{\times}/\Tor\bigl(O_{S}^{\times}\bigr)

is a free abelian group of rank
r
. Therefore the logarithmic embedding (
1.3
) induces an isomorphism from
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
onto
the discrete subgroup

\Gamma_{S}(k)=\big\{\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha\|_{v}\bigr):\alpha\in O_{S}^{\times}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r+1},

which is a free group of rank
r
. It follows from the product formula

\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}\log\|\alpha\|_{v}=0

that
\Gamma_{S}(k)
is contained in the
r
-dimensional diagonal subspace

\mathcal{D}_{r}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{v}):\sum_{v\in S}x_{v}=0\Big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r+1}.

The height
h
is constant on cosets of the quotient group
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
and therefore
h
is well defined as a map

h:\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)\rightarrow[0,\infty).

Let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{r}
be multiplicatively independent elements in
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
that form a basis for the free group
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
. Let

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\eta_{j}\|_{v}\bigr),\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\dots,r$,}

be the logarithmic embedding of these points in
\Gamma_{S}(k)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{r}
. Working with the induced
l^{1}
-norm in the exterior algebra

\Ext\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{r+1}\bigr)
we find that

(1.6)

(r+1)\Reg_{S}(k)=\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{r}\|_{1},

where
\Reg_{S}(k)
is the
S
-regulator. More generally, let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{r}
be multiplicatively independent elements in

\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
, and let
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
be the multiplicative subgroup of rank
r
which they generate. Let

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha_{j}\|_{v}\bigr),\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\dots,r$,}

be the image of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{r}
in
\Gamma_{S}(k)
. It follows that there exists a unique
r\times r
nonsingular matrix

B=\bigl(b_{ij}\bigr)
with entries in
\mathbb{Z}
, such that

(1.7)

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}b_{ij},\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\dots,r$.}

Then the index of the subgroup
\mathfrak{A}
in
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
is

(1.8)

[\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k):\mathfrak{A}]=|\det B|.

Combining (
1.6
), (
1.7
), and (
1.8
), we find that

(1.9)

(r+1)\Reg_{S}(k)[\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k):\mathfrak{A}]=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}\|_{1}.

In
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
we proved an upper bound for the
S
-regulator that is equivalent to the identity (
1.9
) and the inequality

(1.10)

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}\|_{1}\leq 2^{-r}(r+1)\prod_{j=1}^{r}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1}.

The following result provides a generalization of (
1.10
) to an exterior product of
q
independent vectors in the free group

\Gamma_{S}(k)
, where
1\leq q\leq r
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{q}
be multiplicatively independent points in
\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k)
, and let

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha_{j}\|_{v}\bigr),\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\dots,q$,}

be the logarithmic embedding of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{q}
in
\Gamma_{S}(k)
. Then we have

(1.11)

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}\leq 2^{-q}C(q,r)\prod_{j=1}^{q}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1},

where

(1.12)

C(q,r)=\min\bigg\{2^{q},\biggl(\frac{r+1}{r+1-q}\biggr)^{r+1-q}\bigg\}.

We find that

C(q,r)=2^{q}\quad\text{if $2q\leq r+1$,}

and

C(q,r)=\biggl(\frac{r+1}{r+1-q}\biggr)^{r+1-q}\quad\text{if $r+1\leq 2q$.}

In particular we have
C(r,r)=(r+1)
so that (
1.11
) includes the inequality (
1.10
). By applying (
1.4
) it follows that
(
1.11
) can be written using the Weil height as

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}\leq C(q,r)\prod_{j=1}^{q}\bigl([k:\mathbb{Q}]h(\alpha_{j})\bigr).

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
, be as in the statement of Theorem
1.1
,
and let
\mathfrak{A}
be the subgroup of
\Gamma_{S}(k)
generated by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
. Clearly
\mathfrak{A}
is a free
group of rank
q
. It is easy to show that the
l^{1}
-norm of the exterior product

(1.13)

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}

depends on the subgroup
\mathfrak{A}
, but does not depend on the choice of generators. Because of (
1.9
) the
l^{1}
-norm of the exterior
product (
1.13
) extends the
S
-regulator from the group
\Gamma_{S}(k)
to subgroups of
\Gamma_{S}(k)
having lower rank.

Alternatively, if
\alpha\not=1
belongs to
O_{S}^{\times}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\not=\boldsymbol{0}
is the image of
\alpha
with respect to the logarithmic
embedding (
1.3
), then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
-\boldsymbol{\alpha}
are the unique pair of generators of a subgroup of rank
1
in
\Gamma_{S}(k)
.
In view of (
1.4
) we may regard
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}
as the height of this subgroup. Then (
1.13
) extends the height to more general
subgroups
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{S}(k)
having rank
q
. This definition of a height on subgroups is similar to the definition stated
in
[
22
, equation (6.14)]
.

In
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
we showed that if
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{S}(k)
is a subgroup with full rank
r
, then there exist

r
linearly independent points in
\mathfrak{A}
such that the product of their heights is bounded by a number depending only on
r
multiplied by

(1.14)

\Reg_{S}(k)[\mathfrak{U}_{S}(k):\mathfrak{A}].

The following result generalizes
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
to arbitrary subgroups
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{S}(k)
having positive
rank
q
where
1\leq q\leq r
. In this result the
S
-regulator (
1.14
) is replaced by the
l^{1}
-norm (
1.13
) of
the exterior product of a set of generators for the subgroup
\mathfrak{A}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{S}(k)
be a subgroup of positive rank
q
, and let the points

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\alpha_{j}\|_{v}\bigr),\quad\text{where $j=1,2,\dots,q$,}

generate the subgroup
\mathfrak{A}
. Then there exists a subgroup
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
of rank
q
and a set of generators

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}=\bigl(d_{v}\log\|\beta_{j}\|_{v}\bigr),\quad\text{where $j=1,2,\dots,q$,}

for
\mathfrak{B}
, such that

(1.15)

\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\|_{1}=[\mathfrak{A}:\mathfrak{B}]\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1},

and

(1.16)

\prod_{j=1}^{q}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\|_{1}\leq(q!)\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}.

Moreover, we have
[\mathfrak{A}:\mathfrak{B}]\leq q!
.

By applying (
1.4
) we find that the product on the left of (
1.16
) can be written using the Weil height as

\prod_{i=1}^{q}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}\|_{1}=2^{q}\prod_{j=1}^{q}\bigl([k:\mathbb{Q}]h(\beta_{j})\bigr).

Because the subgroups
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
both have rank
q
, the identity (
1.15
) follows as in our derivation of (
1.8
) from
(
1.7
).

It would be of interest to know if there exist absolute constants
b_{0}>0
and
b_{1}>1
such that the factor
q!
on the right of (
1.16
)
could be replaced by
b_{0}b_{1}^{q}
. This could have implications for a conjecture of F. Rodriguez Villegas which we discuss in section 2.

2.
A conjecture of F. Rodriguez Villegas

In a well known paper D. H. Lehmer
[
11
]
proposed an important problem about the roots of irreducible polynomials in
\mathbb{Z}[x]
.
An equivalent form of Lehmer’s problem stated using the absolute, logarithmic, Weil height (
1.2
) is this: does there exist an absolute constant

c>0
such that

c\leq[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]h(\alpha)

whenever
\alpha\not=0
is an algebraic number and not a root of unity? If
\alpha\not=0
and
\alpha
is not a unit, the lower bound

\log 2\leq[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]h(\alpha)

follows easily. Therefore when considering Lehmer’s problem we may restrict our attention to algebraic units
\alpha
which are not roots of unity. Further
information about Lehmer’s problem can be found in
[
7
, Section 1.6.15]
,
[
21
]
, and
[
23
, Section 3.6]
.

Let
S_{\infty}
be the set of archimedean places of
k
and assume that
|S_{\infty}|\geq 2
. We continue to write
|S_{\infty}|=r+1
so that the
logarithmic embedding (
1.3
) is an isomorphism from the free group

\mathfrak{U}_{S_{\infty}}(k)=O_{S_{\infty}}/\Tor\bigl(O_{S_{\infty}}^{\times}\bigr)

onto the discrete subgroup
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
of rank
r
contained in the diagonal subspace
\mathcal{D}_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
. Then Lehmer’s problem
asks if there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that the inequality

(2.1)

c\leq 2[k:\mathbb{Q}]h(\alpha)=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}

holds at all points
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\not=\boldsymbol{0}
in
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
. A generalization of this conjecture to independent subsets

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
in
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
with
2\leq q\leq r
was proposed by Bertrand
[
5
]
. More
precisely, Bertrand asked if for each integer
2\leq q
there exists a constant
c_{q}>0
such that

(2.2)

c_{q}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{2},

where the
l^{2}
-norm of the wedge product on the right of (
2.2
) is the covolume of the subgroup of
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
generated
by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
. Examples found by Siegel
[
20
]
show that the inequality (
2.2
)
cannot hold for
q=1
. However, a positive answer for
q\geq 3
was established by Amoroso and David in
[
3
]
.

An alternative generalization of Lehmer’s problem to subgroups of rank
q
has been proposed in a conjecture of F. Rodriguez Villegas stated in

[
9
, Appendix]
, and also discussed in
[
4
]
and
[
9
]
. We state a special case of this conjecture for pure
wedges.

Conjecture 2.1
.

[F. Rodriguez Villegas]
There exist two absolute constants
c_{0}>0
and
c_{1}>1
with the
following property. If
q
is an integer such that

1\leq q\leq r=\rank\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k),

and if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
are linearly independent points in
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
, then

(2.3)

c_{0}c_{1}^{q}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}.

If
q=1
then the truth of (
2.3
) would solve the problem originally proposed by Lehmer, and if
q=r
then (
2.3
) follows
from a known lower bound for the regulator proved by R. Zimmert
[
24
]
. Thus the conjecture of Rodriguez Villegas interpolates between
the unsolved problem of Lehmer and Zimmert’s result. It follows from earlier work of Pohst
[
14
]
and Schinzel
[
16
]
that
Conjecture
2.1
holds for the collection of totally real algebraic number fields
k
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
be linearly independent points in
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
and let

\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
be the subgroup of rank
q
that they generate. We have already observed in connection with
(
1.13
) that the
l^{1}
-norm

\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}

depends on the subgroup
\mathfrak{A}
, but does
not
depend on the choice of generators. Thus Conjecture 2.1 can be regarded as a
generalization of Lehmer’s problem (reformulated as a conjecture) from subgroups of rank
1
to more general subgroups of rank
q

where
1\leq q\leq r
.

Here is a related conjecture.

Conjecture 2.2
.

There exist two absolute constants
d_{0}>0
and
d_{1}>1
with the
following property. If
q
is an integer such that

1\leq q\leq r=\rank\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k),

and if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
are linearly independent points in
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
, then

d_{0}d_{1}^{q}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\|_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\|_{1}\cdots\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}.

It follows from (
1.12
) that the constant on the right of (
1.11
) is

2^{-q}C(q,r)\leq 1.

Therefore if the conjectured inequality (
2.3
) is correct, then from Theorem
1.1
we also get

c_{0}c_{1}^{q}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{q}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1}.

Thus Conjecture
2.1
implies Conjecture
2.2
with
d_{0}=c_{0}
and
d_{1}=c_{1}
.

Now assume that Conjecture
2.2
is correct. Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
be linearly independent points in
the logarithmic embedding
\Gamma_{S_{\infty}}(k)
, and let
\mathfrak{A}
be the subgroup of rank
q
that they generate.
By Theorem
1.2
there exist linearly independent points
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}
in
\mathfrak{A}
such that

(2.4)

d_{0}d_{1}^{q}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\|_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\|_{1}\cdots\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\|_{1}\leq(q!)\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1},

where the inequality on the left of (
2.4
) follows from Conjecture
2.2
, and the inequality on the right of (
2.4
)
follows from (
1.16
). However, as
q!
grows faster than an exponential function of
q
, at present we are unable to conclude that
Conjecture
2.2
implies Conjecture
2.1
. This could change if the factor
q!
in the inequality (
1.16
) could be
replaced by a factor of the form
b_{0}b_{1}^{q}
, where
b_{0}>0
and
b_{1}>1
are absolute constants.

3.
Generalization of Schinzel’s inequality, I

For a real number
x
we write

x^{+}=\max\{0,x\},\quad\text{and}\quad x^{-}=\max\{0,-x\},

so that
x=x^{+}-x^{-}
and
|x|=x^{+}+x^{-}
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{n})
be a (column) vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. As in
[
1
, equation (4.3)]
,
the
Schinzel norm
is the function

\delta:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

defined by

\delta(\boldsymbol{x})=\max\bigg\{\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+},~\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\bigg\}=\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\biggr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|x_{n}|.

It is clear that
\delta
is in fact a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and we write

K_{N}=\big\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1\big\}

for the corresponding closed unit ball. Then
K_{N}
is a compact, convex, symmetric subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with a nonempty interior.
The
N
-dimensional volume of
K_{N}
was computed in
[
1
, Lemma 4.1]
. The connection between the Schinzel
norm and the Weil height follows from (
1.4
) and (
5.2
) (see also
[
1
, Lemma 5.1]
).

In Lemma
3.1
we will determine the finite collection of extreme points of
K_{N}
. Then a combinatorial argument in section 4 applied
to the extreme points of
K_{N}
will lead to a proof of the following inequalities.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
be linearly independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. If
L=N
then

(3.1)

|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{N}|\leq\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}),

if
L<N\leq 2L
then

(3.2)

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}),

and if
2L\leq N
then

(3.3)

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\leq 2^{L}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}).

Alternatively, for
L<N
we have

(3.4)

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge&\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\\
&\leq\min\bigg\{2^{L},\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}\bigg\}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}).\end{split}

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}
, are (column) vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then Schinzel
[
17
]
proved the inequality

(3.5)

\bigl|\det\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{x}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{x}_{N}\bigr)\bigr|\leq\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\bigr)\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\bigr)\cdots\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\bigr),

which is equivalent to (
3.1
). It can be shown that there exist nontrivial cases of equality in the inequality (
3.2
) whenever
the integer
N-L
is a divisor of
N
. And it can be shown that there always exist nontrivial cases of equality in the inequality (
3.3
).
It is instructive to define the function

g_{L}:[L,\infty]\rightarrow\bigl[1,e^{L}\bigr]

by

g_{L}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x=L$,}\\
\biggl(\dfrac{x}{x-L}\biggr)^{(x-L)}&\text{if $L<x<\infty$},\\
e^{L}&\text{if $x=\infty$}.\end{cases}

It follows that
x\mapsto g_{L}(x)
is continuous, and has a continuous, positive derivative on
(L,\infty)
. Then

x\mapsto g_{L}(x)
is strictly increasing on
[L,\infty]
. We have
g_{L}(2L)=2^{L}
, and this clarifies the behavior of the function

x\mapsto\min\big\{2^{L},g_{L}(x)\big\}

which occurs on the right of (
3.4
).

We recall that a point
\boldsymbol{k}
in
K_{N}
is an
extreme point
of
K_{N}
if
\boldsymbol{k}
cannot be written as a proper convex combination of
two distinct points in
K_{N}
. Obviously all extreme points of
K_{N}
occur on the boundary of
K_{N}
. Let

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a continuous linear functional, and write

\delta^{*}(\varphi)=\sup\{\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}):\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1\}

for the dual norm of
\varphi
. As
K_{N}
is compact there exists a point
\boldsymbol{\eta}
in
K_{N}
such that

\delta^{*}(\varphi)=\varphi(\boldsymbol{\eta}).

If there exists a linear functional
\varphi
such that

\big\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in K_{N}:\delta^{*}(\varphi)=\varphi(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big\}=\{\boldsymbol{k}\},

then
\boldsymbol{k}
is an
exposed point
of
K_{N}
. It is known (see
[
10
, section 1.8, exercise 3]
) that an exposed point of

K_{N}
is also an extreme point of
K_{N}
.

We define two finite, disjoint subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

(3.6)

E_{N}=\big\{\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{m}:1\leq m\leq N\big\},\quad\text{and}\quad F_{N}=\big\{\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}:m\not=n\big\},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{N}
, are the standard basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Clearly we have

\bigl|E_{N}\bigr|=2N,\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl|F_{N}\bigr|=N^{2}-N.

It follows easily that each point of
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
is on the boundary of
K_{N}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The subset
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
is the collection of all extreme points of
K_{N}
.

Proof.

For
1\leq m\leq N
let
\varphi_{m}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be the linear functional defined by

\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}+\tfrac{1}{2}x_{m}.

Then we have

(3.7)

\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\biggr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{m}\bigr|,

and there is equality in the inequality (
3.7
) if and only if

0\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n},\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq x_{m}.

We also have

(3.8)

\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl|\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}\biggr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{m}\bigr|\leq\delta(\boldsymbol{x}),

and there is equality in the inequality (
3.8
) if and only if

x_{n}=0\quad\text{for each $n\not=m$.}

Combining (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) we find that

(3.9)

\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\delta(\boldsymbol{x})

for all
\boldsymbol{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and there is equality in the inequality (
3.9
) if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}=t\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
with
0\leq t
.
Therefore we get

\delta^{*}\bigl(\varphi_{m}\bigr)=\sup\big\{\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}):\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1\big\}=\varphi_{m}\bigl(\boldsymbol{e}_{m}\bigr)=1,

and

\big\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in K_{N}:\delta^{*}\bigl(\varphi_{m}\bigr)=\varphi_{m}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big\}=\big\{\boldsymbol{e}_{m}\big\}.

This shows that
\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
is an exposed point of
K_{N}
, and therefore
\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
is an extreme point of
K_{N}
. As
K_{N}
is symmetric,
we find that
-\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
is also an extreme point.

Next we suppose that
m\not=n
, and we define the linear functional
\psi_{mn}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\psi_{mn}(\boldsymbol{x})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(x_{m}-x_{n}\bigr).

Then we have

(3.10)

\psi_{mn}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl|\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}x_{\ell}\biggr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{m}\bigr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{n}\bigr|,

and there is equality in the inequality (
3.10
) if and only if

\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}x_{\ell}=0,\quad 0\leq x_{m},\quad\text{and}\quad x_{n}\leq 0.

And we get

(3.11)

\tfrac{1}{2}\biggl|\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}x_{\ell}\biggr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{m}\bigr|+\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl|x_{n}\bigr|\leq\delta(\boldsymbol{x}),

with equality in the inequality (
3.11
) if and only if

x_{\ell}=0\quad\text{for all $\ell\not=m$ and $\ell\not=n$.}

By combining (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) we find that

(3.12)

\psi_{mn}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\delta(\boldsymbol{x}),

and there is equality in the inequality (
3.12
) if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}=t\bigl(\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\bigr)
with
0\leq t
. As
in the previous case we conclude that

\delta^{*}\bigl(\psi_{mn}\bigr)=\sup\big\{\psi_{mn}(\boldsymbol{x}):\delta(\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1\big\}=\psi_{mn}\bigl(\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\bigr)=1,

and

\big\{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in K:\delta^{*}\bigl(\psi_{mn}\bigr)=\psi_{mn}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\big\}=\big\{\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\big\}.

This shows that
\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
is an exposed point of
K_{N}
, and therefore
\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}
is an extreme point of
K_{N}
.

We have now shown that each point in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
is an extreme point of
K_{N}
.
To complete the proof we will show that if
\boldsymbol{x}
is a point on the boundary of
K_{N}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}
can be written as a convex
combination of points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
. Thus we assume that

(3.13)

\delta(\boldsymbol{x})=\max\bigg\{\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+},~\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\bigg\}=1,

and we write

\sigma^{+}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+},\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{-}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\underset{m\not=n}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}}x_{m}^{+}x_{n}^{-}(\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n})\\
&=\biggl(\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\biggr)\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\biggl(\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\biggr)\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\\
&=\sigma^{-}\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\sigma^{+}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\\
&=\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}-(1-\sigma^{-})\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}+(1-\sigma^{+})\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\\
&=\boldsymbol{x}-(1-\sigma^{-})\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-(1-\sigma^{+})\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}),\end{split}

and therefore

(3.14)

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{x}=(1-\sigma^{-})&\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}\boldsymbol{e}_{m}+(1-\sigma^{+})\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}(-\boldsymbol{e}_{n})\cr&+\underset{m\not=n}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}}x_{m}^{+}x_{n}^{-}(\boldsymbol{e}_{m}-\boldsymbol{e}_{n}).\end{split}

The identity (
3.14
) shows that
\boldsymbol{x}
is a linear combination of points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
with nonnegative
coefficients. Using (
3.13
), the sum of the coefficients in (
3.14
) is

\begin{split}(1-\sigma^{-})\sum_{m=1}^{N}x_{m}^{+}+(1-\sigma^{+})&\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}^{-}+\underset{m\not=n}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}}x_{m}^{+}x_{n}^{-}\\
&=(1-\sigma^{-})\sigma^{+}+(1-\sigma^{+})\sigma^{-}+\sigma^{+}\sigma^{-}\\
&=1-(1-\sigma^{+})(1-\sigma^{-})\\
&=1.\end{split}

It follows that
\boldsymbol{x}
is a convex combination of points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
.
We have shown that if
\boldsymbol{x}
is on the boundary of
K_{N}
, then
\boldsymbol{x}
is a convex combination of points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
.
Therefore the only extreme points of
K_{N}
are the points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
.
∎

Let

I=\{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{L}\}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}

be a subset of positive cardinality
L
. If
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{n})
is a point in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
we write
\boldsymbol{x}_{I}
for the point in
\mathbb{R}^{L}
given by

\boldsymbol{x}_{I}=(x_{i_{\ell}})
. Alternatively,
\boldsymbol{x}_{I}
is the
L\times 1
submatrix of
\boldsymbol{x}
having rows indexed by the integers in the subset
I
.
The following result is now an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}
be an element in the set of extreme points
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
, and let

I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}

be a subset of positive cardinality
L
. Then either
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{I}=\boldsymbol{0}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{L}
, or
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{I}
belongs to the set of extreme points
E_{L}\cup F_{L}
.

Let

\Phi_{L,N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M},\quad\text{where $M=\binom{N}{L}$},

be the continuous, alternating, multilinear function taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and defined by

\Phi_{L,N}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L})=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}.

By compactness the continuous, nonnegative function

(\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L})\mapsto\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}

assumes its maximum value on the
L
-fold product

K_{N}\times K_{N}\times\cdots\times K_{N}.

We write

(3.15)

\mu_{L,N}=\max\big\{\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}:\text{$\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\in K_{N}$ for $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$}\big\}

for this maximum value. We show that
\mu_{L,N}
can be determined by restricting each variable
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
to the set
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
of
extreme points in
K_{N}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exist points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
in the set of extreme points
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
such that

(3.16)

\mu_{L,N}=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}.

Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
then

(3.17)

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\leq\mu_{L,N}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}).

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}
, be points in
K_{N}
such that

(3.18)

\mu_{L,N}=\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}\bigr\|_{1}.

Because
\Phi_{L,N}
is linear in each variable, it is easy to show that
\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\ell}\bigr)=1
for each

\ell=1,2,\dots,L
. Also, among all the collections of
L
points from the boundary of
K_{N}
that satisfy (
3.18
), we
may assume that the collection
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}
contains the maximum
number of extreme points. If this maximum number is
L
then we are done. Therefore we may assume that the maximum number
of extreme points is less than
L
.

If, for example,
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}
is not an extreme point, then there exist extreme points
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{J}

in
K_{N}
, and positive numbers
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{J}
, such that

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\boldsymbol{u}_{j},\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}=1.

It follows that

(3.19)

\begin{split}\mu_{L,N}&=\biggl\|~\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\bigl(\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}\bigr)\biggr\|_{1}\\
&\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\bigl\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}\bigr\|_{1}\\
&\leq\mu_{L,N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{j}\\
&=\mu_{L,N}\end{split}

Hence there is equality throughout the inequality (
3.19
), and we conclude that

\mu_{L,N}=\bigl\|\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}\bigr\|_{1}

for each
j=1,2,\dots,J
. But each collection of points
\boldsymbol{u}_{j},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L},
plainly contains
one more extreme point than the collection
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L}
. The contradiction shows
that there exists a collection of points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
, from the boundary of
K_{N}
, such that (
3.16
) holds
and each
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}
is an extreme point of
K_{N}
.

Next we verify the inequality (
3.17
). If one of the vectors in the collection
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
is the zero vector, then both
sides of (
3.17
) are zero. Thus we may assume that
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\not=\boldsymbol{0}
for each
\ell=1,2,\dots,L
. Let

(3.20)

\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}=\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},

so that
\delta\bigl(\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}\bigr)=1
for each
\ell=1,2,\dots,L
. Then we certainly have

(3.21)

\bigl\|\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{y}_{L}\bigr\|_{1}\leq\mu_{L,N}

by the definition of
\mu_{L,N}
. Then (
3.17
) follows using (
3.20
), (
3.21
), and the multilinearity of the
exterior product.
∎

The extreme points
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
for the
\delta
-unit ball
K_{N}
have the following useful property.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
be extreme points in the set
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
, and let

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

be the
N\times L
matrix having
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
as columns. If

I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}

is a subset of cardinality
|I|=L
, and
\Xi_{I}
is the
L\times L
submatrix having rows indexed by
I
, then the integer

\det\Xi_{I}
belongs to the set
\{-1,0,1\}
.

Proof.

It is clear that the columns of the
L\times L
submatrix
\Xi_{I}
are the
L\times 1
column vectors

(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1})_{I},(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2})_{I},\dots,(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L})_{I}
. If a column of
\Xi_{I}
is
\boldsymbol{0}
, then
\det\Xi_{I}=0
is obvious.
If each column of
\Xi_{I}
is not
\boldsymbol{0}
, then it follows from Corollary
3.1
that each column of
\Xi_{I}
belongs to the set
of extreme points
E_{L}\cup F_{L}
. Applying Schinzel’s determinant inequality (
3.5
) to the matrix
\Xi_{I}
, we get

\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|\leq\delta\bigl((\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1})_{I}\bigr)\delta\bigl((\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2})_{I}\bigr)\cdots\delta\bigl((\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L})_{I}\bigr)=1.

As
\det\Xi_{I}
is an integer, the lemma is proved.
∎

If
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
are extreme points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
, then it follows from Lemma
3.3
that

(3.22)

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
|I|=L\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|\leq\binom{N}{L}.

Using (
3.16
) we get the simple upper bound

(3.23)

\mu_{L,N}\leq\binom{N}{L}\quad\text{for $1\leq L\leq N$}.

It follows from (
3.5
) that there is equality in (
3.23
) when
L=N
. There is also equality in (
3.23
) when
L+1=N
;
this follows from the example

\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&1&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
-1&-1&-1&\dots&-1&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

By squaring each of the subdeterminants in the sum (
3.22
) we can determine the value of
\mu_{L,N}
for
2L\leq N
.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
1\leq L<N
then

(3.24)

\mu_{L,N}\leq 2^{L}.

Moreover, if
2L\leq N
then there is equality in the inequality
(
3.24
)
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
be extreme points in
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
, and let

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

be the
N\times L
matrix having
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
as columns. It follows from Lemma
3.3
that

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
|I|=L\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
|I|=L\end{subarray}}\bigl(\det\Xi_{I}\bigr)^{2}.

Then from the Cauchy-Binet identity we get

(3.25)

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
|I|=L\end{subarray}}\bigl(\det\Xi_{I}\bigr)^{2}=\det\bigl(\Xi^{T}\Xi\bigr).

The
L\times L
matrix in the determinant on the right of (
3.25
) is

\Xi^{T}\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}^{T}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}\bigr),

where
k=1,2,\dots,L
indexes rows and
\ell=1,2,\dots,L
indexes columns. As
\Xi^{T}\Xi
is an
L\times L
real, symmetric
matrix, we can apply Hadamard’s inequality to estimate its determinant. We find that

(3.26)

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\det\bigl(\Xi^{T}\Xi\bigr)\leq\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2^{L}.

This proves the inequality (
3.24
).

If the columns of the matrix
\Xi
are orthogonal, then there is equality in Hadamard’s inequality. Therefore, if
2L\leq N
we
select
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
in
F_{N}
so that

\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
-1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&-1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&-1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&1&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&-1&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&-1\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

For this choice of
\Xi
the columns of
\Xi
are orthogonal. Hence for this choice of
\Xi
there is equality in (
3.26
), and equality
in (
3.24
).
∎

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
belong to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
2L\leq N
, then it follows from (
3.17
) and the case of equality in (
3.24
) that

(3.27)

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\leq 2^{L}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}).

This proves the inequality (
3.3
) in the statement of Theorem
3.1
.

The following lemma, together with combinatorial arguments in section 4, will be used in the proof of the inequality (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
be linearly independent extreme points in the set
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
. Assume
that exactly
K
of the points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
belong to the subset
E_{N}
, where
1\leq K<L
. Then there exist linearly
independent extreme points

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}
in the set
E_{N-K}\cup F_{N-K}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}\|_{1}.

Proof.

By using a suitable permutation of the points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
, we may assume that

\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K}\}\subseteq E_{N},\quad\text{and}\quad\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\}\subseteq F_{N}.

And we may further assume that for
k=1,2,\dots,K
we have

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}=\pm\boldsymbol{e}_{m_{k}},\quad\text{where $1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{K}\leq N$}.

It will be convenient to write

M=\{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{K}\}.

Now let

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

be the
N\times L
matrix having
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
as columns. We partition
\Xi
into submatrices

\Xi=\bigl(U\ V\bigr),

where

U=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{K}\bigr),\quad\text{and}\quad V=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

are
N\times K
and
N\times(L-K)
, respectively. We suppose that
I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is a subset of cardinality
|I|=L
such that

(3.28)

\det\Xi_{I}=\det\bigl(U_{I}\ V_{I}\bigr)\not=0.

On the right of (
3.28
) the submatrix
U_{I}
is
L\times K
and the submatrix
V_{I}
is
L\times(L-K)
.
If the integer
m_{k}
, which occurs in
M
, does not belong to
I
, then the
k
-th column of
\Xi_{I}
is identically zero and
(
3.28
) cannot hold. Therefore (
3.28
) implies that

M\subseteq I.

Next we apply the Laplace expansion of the determinant to
\Xi_{I}
partitioned as in (
3.28
). In view of our previous remarks we find that

(3.29)

\det\Xi_{I}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq I\\
|J|=K\end{subarray}}(-1)^{\varepsilon(J)}\bigl(\det U_{J}\bigr)\bigl(\det V_{\widetilde{J}}\bigr),

where

\widetilde{J}=I\setminus J

is the complement of
J
in
I
, and
\varepsilon(J)
is an integer that depends on
J
. As before, if the integer
m_{k}
which occurs in
M

does not belong to the subset
J
, then the
k
-th column of
U_{J}
is identically zero and therefore
\det U_{J}=0
. As
|J|=|M|=K
,
we conclude that there is exactly one nonzero term in the sum on the right of (
3.29
), and the nonzero term occurs when
J=M
.
From these observations we conclude that the Laplace expansion (
3.29
) is simply

(3.30)

\det\Xi_{I}=(-1)^{\varepsilon(M)}\bigl(\det U_{M}\bigr)\bigl(\det V_{I\setminus M}\bigr).

It is obvious that
\det U_{M}=\pm 1
, and therefore (
3.30
) leads to the identity

\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|=\bigl|\det V_{I\setminus M}\bigr|.

Let

V^{\prime}=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+1}^{\prime}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+2}^{\prime}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\bigr)

be the
(N-K)\times(L-K)
submatrix of
V
obtained by removing the rows of
V
that are indexed by the integers
m_{k}
in the subset
M
.
It follows from Lemma
3.2
that the columns of
V^{\prime}
belong to the set of extreme points
E_{N-K}\cup F_{N-K}
. Moreover, we have

(3.31)

\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|=\bigl|\det V_{I\setminus M}\bigr|=\bigl|\det V_{J}^{\prime}\bigr|,

where

J=I\setminus M\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus M,\quad\text{and}\quad|J|=L-K.

We note that

I\mapsto J=I\setminus\{m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{K}\}

is a bijection from the collection of subsets
I
that contain
M
onto the collection of subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus M
that have
cardinality
L-K
. Using (
3.31
) we find that

(3.32)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
M\subseteq I\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus M\\
|J|=L-K\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det V_{J}^{\prime}\bigr|.

Because the rows of
V^{\prime}
are indexed by the elements of the set
\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus M
, it follows from (
3.32
) that

(3.33)

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
M\subseteq I\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det\Xi_{I}\bigr|\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus M\\
|J|=L-K\end{subarray}}\bigl|\det V_{J}^{\prime}\bigr|\\
&=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+1}^{\prime}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+2}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\|_{1}.\end{split}

As the columns of
V^{\prime}
belong to
E_{N-K}\cup F_{N-K}
and satisfy (
3.33
), they are linearly independent. Therefore we set

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K+\ell}^{\prime},\quad\text{for $\ell=1,2,\dots,L-K$},

and the lemma is proved.
∎

4.
Generalization of Schinzel’s inequality, II

In this section we develop a combinatorial method which leads to an asymptotically sharp upper bound for the quantity

\mu_{L,N}
defined in (
3.15
). The bound we prove here applies when
L<N\leq 2L
, and will be used to verify the inequality
(
3.2
) in the statement of Theorem
3.1
.

We suppose throughout this section that

(4.1)

\big\{S(1),S(2),S(3),\dots,S(L)\big\}

is a collection of
L
distinct subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
such that

(4.2)

|S(\ell)|=2,\quad\text{for each $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$},

and

(4.3)

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}S(\ell)=\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

It follows from (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) that

N\leq 2L\leq N(N-1),

but for our later applications we will make the more restrictive assumption that

(4.4)

L<N\leq 2L.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the collection of
all
subsets
A\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. We define a map
\eta:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
by

(4.5)

\eta(A)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
S(\ell)\cap A\not=\emptyset\end{subarray}}^{L}S(\ell).

Then it follows from (
4.3
) that

(4.6)

A\subseteq\eta(A),\quad\text{for each subset $A\in\mathcal{A}$}.

We are interested in subsets
A
in
\mathcal{A}
that satisfy
\eta(A)=A
. Obviously
\emptyset
and
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
have
this property. More generally we define

(4.7)

\mathcal{P}=\big\{A\in\mathcal{A}:\eta(A)=A\big\}.

If
A
belongs to the collection
\mathcal{P}
and
S(\ell)\cap A\not=\emptyset
, then
S(\ell)\subseteq A
. Thus a nonempty subset

A
in
\mathcal{P}
must have
2\leq|A|
. We show that the collection
\mathcal{P}
forms an algebra of subsets.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be the collection of subsets defined by
(
4.7
)
.

(i)

If
A_{1}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
then its complement

A_{2}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus A_{1}

also belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

(ii)

If
A_{3}
and
A_{4}
belong to
\mathcal{P}
then
A_{3}\cup A_{4}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

(iii)

If
A_{5}
and
A_{6}
belong to
\mathcal{P}
then
A_{5}\cap A_{6}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Assume that
S(\ell)\cap A_{2}\not=\emptyset
. Then
S(\ell)\cap A_{1}\not=\emptyset
is impossible. Hence we
have
S(\ell)\subseteq A_{2}
, and this implies that
A_{2}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

Let
S(\ell)\cap(A_{3}\cup A_{4})\not=\emptyset
. Then either
S(\ell)\cap A_{3}\not=\emptyset
or
S(\ell)\cap A_{4}\not=\emptyset
.
Hence either
S(\ell)\subseteq A_{3}
or
S(\ell)\subseteq A_{4}
, and therefore
S(\ell)\subseteq A_{3}\cup A_{4}
. It follows that

A_{3}\cup A_{4}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.

By what we have already proved the sets

A_{7}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus A_{5},\quad\text{and}\quad A_{8}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus A_{6}

both belong to
\mathcal{P}
, and therefore the set

A_{5}\cap A_{6}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus(A_{7}\cup A_{8})

belongs to
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A_{1}
be a nonempty subset in
\mathcal{A}
, and let
B
be a subset in
\mathcal{P}
. Assume that
A_{1}\subseteq B
.
Define an increasing sequence of subsets

A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots

from
\mathcal{A}
inductively by

A_{n+1}=\eta\bigl(A_{n}\bigr),\quad\text{for each $n=1,2,3,\dots$}.

Then

A_{n}\subseteq B\quad\text{for each $n=1,2,3,\dots$}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
. If
n=1
then
A_{1}\subseteq B
by hypothesis. Now assume that
2\leq n

and
A_{n-1}\subseteq B
. Then we have

(4.8)

A_{n}=\eta(A_{n-1})=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
S(\ell)\cap A_{n-1}\not=\emptyset\end{subarray}}^{L}S(\ell).

If
S(\ell)\cap A_{n-1}\not=\emptyset
then
S(\ell)
contains a point of
B
, and therefore

S(\ell)\subseteq B
. It follows from (
4.8
) that
A_{n}\subseteq B
. This proves the lemma.
∎

We say that a subset
A
in
\mathcal{A}
is
minimal
if
A
is not empty and belongs to
\mathcal{P}
, but no proper subset of
A
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
. That is,
a nonempty set
A
in
\mathcal{P}
is
minimal
if for every nonempty subset
B\subseteq A
such that
B\not=A
, we have
\eta(B)\not=B
.
We will show that each element of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is contained in a minimal subset in
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A_{1}
in
\mathcal{A}
have cardinality
1
. Define an increasing sequence of subsets

A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots

from
\mathcal{A}
inductively by

(4.9)

A_{n+1}=\eta\bigl(A_{n}\bigr),\quad\text{for $n=1,2,3,\dots$}.

Let
K
be the smallest positive integer such that

(4.10)

A_{K}=\eta(A_{K})=A_{K+1}.

Then
K
exists,
2\leq K
, and the subset
A_{K}
is minimal.

Proof.

From (
4.6
) we get

A_{1}\subseteq A_{2}\subseteq A_{3}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq A_{n}\subseteq\cdots.

As
|A_{n}|\leq N
for each
n=1,2,\dots
, it is obvious that
K
exists.

Let
A_{1}=\{k_{1}\}
where
1\leq k_{1}\leq N
. It follows from (
4.3
) that there exists a subset
S(\ell_{1})
that contains
k_{1}
.
Write
S(\ell_{1})=\{k_{1},k_{2}\}
where
k_{1}\not=k_{2}
. From (
4.5
) we conclude that

S(\ell_{1})=\{k_{1},k_{2}\}\subseteq\eta(A_{1})=A_{2},

and therefore
A_{1}=\{k_{1}\}
is a proper subset of
\eta(A_{1})=A_{2}
. Hence we have
2\leq K
.

If
A_{K}
is not minimal there exists a proper subset
B\subseteq A_{K}
such that
\eta(B)=B
, and therefore
B
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
. Let

(4.11)

C=A_{K}\setminus B=A_{K}\cap\bigl(\{1,2,\dots,N\}\setminus B\bigr)

be the complement of
B
in
A_{K}
. It follows from Lemma
4.1
, and the representation on the right of (
4.11
),
that
C
is a proper subset of
A_{K}
and
C
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
. Thus we have the disjoint union of proper subsets

(4.12)

A_{K}=B\cup C,\quad\text{where $B\in\mathcal{P}$ and $C\in\mathcal{P}$}.

Plainly
A_{1}=\{k_{1}\}
is a subset of either
B
or
C
, and by renaming these sets if necessary we may assume that
A_{1}=\{k_{1}\}

is contained in
B
. Then it follows from Lemma
4.2
that

A_{n}\subseteq B\quad\text{for each $n=1,2,3,\dots$}.

But this is inconsistent with the representation of
A_{K}
as the disjoint union (
4.12
). We conclude that
B
and
C
do not exist,
and therefore
A_{K}
is minimal.
∎

It follows from Lemma
4.3
that each element of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is contained in a minimal subset. This
minimal subset is unique, and leads to a partition of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
into a disjoint union of minimal subsets.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
B
and
C
be nonempty, minimal subsets in
\mathcal{P}
. Then either

B=C,\quad\text{or}\quad B\cap C=\emptyset.

Proof.

If
B\cap C=\emptyset
we are done. Therefore we assume that
k_{1}
is a point in
B\cap C
. Let

A_{1}=\{k_{1}\}
, and let
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots
be the sequence of subsets defined by (
4.9
). Let
K
be the smallest
positive integer such that (
4.10
) holds. By Lemma
4.3
the subset
A_{K}
is minimal, and by Lemma
4.2

we have both
A_{K}\subseteq B
and
A_{K}\subseteq C
. But
A_{K}
is minimal and therefore
A_{K}
cannot be a proper subset of
the minimal subset
B
. Similarly,
A_{K}
cannot be a proper subset of the minimal subset
C
. We conclude that

B=A_{K}=C.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(
4.1
)
be a collection of distinct subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
such that

|S(\ell)|=2\quad\text{for each $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$},

and

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}S(\ell)=\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be the collection of subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
defined by
(
4.7
)
, and let
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}

be the collection of all distinct, minimal subsets in
\mathcal{P}
. Then the subsets
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}
are disjoint, and

A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{r}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

Proof.

The subsets
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}
exist by Lemma
4.3
. Then it follows from Lemma
4.4
that the
subsets
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}
are disjoint. Therefore we get

(4.13)

A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{r}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

It follows from Lemma
4.3
that each point in
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
is contained in a minimal subset, hence there is
equality in (
4.13
).
∎

We continue to assume that
L
and
N
are positive integers that satisfy (
4.4
). Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
be vectors
from the set of extreme points
F_{N}
, and write

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

for the
N\times L
matrix having
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
as columns. We assume that no row of the matrix
\Xi
is identically
zero, and we assume that
\rank\Xi=L
. We write

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}=\bigl(\xi_{n\ell}\bigr)
and use the vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}
to define a collection of subsets

(4.14)

S(\ell)\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\},\quad\text{for each $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$}.

More precisely, we define

(4.15)

S(\ell)=\{n:\text{$1\leq n\leq N$ and $\xi_{n\ell}\not=0$}\},\quad\text{for each $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$}.

As each column vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}
belongs to the set of extreme points
F_{N}
, it follows that each subset
S(\ell)
has cardinality
2
, and

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\xi_{n\ell}=\sum_{n\in S(\ell)}\xi_{n\ell}=0.

Because no row of the matrix
\Xi
is identically zero, we find that

\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{L}S(\ell)=\{1,2,\dots,N\}.

Therefore the subsets
S(\ell)
defined by (
4.15
) satisfy the conditions (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) that were assumed in
the previous lemmas. We continue to write
\mathcal{A}
for the collection of all subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, and we write
\mathcal{P}
for the collection
of subsets defined by (
4.7
).

Next we suppose that
A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r}
is the collection of distinct, nonempty, minimal subsets in
\mathcal{P}
. Then it follows from
Lemma
4.5
that

(4.16)

A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{r}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}

is a disjoint union of nonempty sets. Because each subset
A_{j}
is minimal we have

(4.17)

A_{j}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
S(\ell)\subseteq A_{j}\end{subarray}}^{L}S(\ell)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
S(\ell)\cap A_{j}\not=\emptyset\end{subarray}}^{L}S(\ell).

We use each subset
A_{j}
to define a subset
D_{j}\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,L\}
by

(4.18)

D_{j}=\{\ell:\text{$1\leq\ell\leq L$ and $S(\ell)\subseteq A_{j}$}\},\quad\text{for $j=1,2,\dots,r$}.

Then it follows from (
4.16
), (
4.17
), and (
4.18
), that

(4.19)

D_{1}\cup D_{2}\cup\cdots\cup D_{r}=\{1,2,\dots,L\}

is a disjoint union of nonempty sets. For each
j=1,2,\dots,r
we write
Y_{j}
for the
N\times|D_{j}|
submatrix of
\Xi

having columns indexed by the integers in
D_{j}
. That is, we define

(4.20)

Y_{j}=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}\bigr),\quad\text{where $\ell\in D_{j}$ indexes columns}.

We assemble the matrices
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{r}
as
N\times|D_{j}|
blocks so as to define the
N\times L
matrix

(4.21)

Z=\bigl(Y_{1}\ Y_{2}\ \cdots Y_{r}\bigr).

Because of the disjoint union (
4.19
), the columns of the matrix
Z
can also be obtained by permuting the columns of the
matrix
\Xi
. That is, there exists an
L\times L
permutation matrix
P
such that

\Xi=ZP.

As
\det P=\pm 1
and the columns of
\Xi
are linearly independent, it follows that the matrix
Y_{j}
has rank
|D_{j}|

for each
j=1,2,\dots,r
. We also find that

\det\bigl(\Xi^{T}\Xi\bigr)=\det\bigl(P^{T}Z^{T}ZP\bigr)=\det\bigl(Z^{T}Z\bigr)

is a positive integer.

Now suppose that
1\leq i\leq r
, that
1\leq j\leq r
, and
i\not=j
. It follows from (
4.14
), (
4.18
), and (
4.19
),
that each nonzero row of the matrix
Y_{i}
is indexed by an integer in the set
A_{i}
, and each nonzero row of the matrix
Y_{j}
is indexed
by an integer in the set
A_{j}
. As
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
are disjoint we conclude that
Y_{i}^{T}Y_{j}
is a zero matrix. Because we have
organized
Z
into blocks as in (
4.21
), we find that

(4.22)

\det\bigl(\Xi^{T}\Xi\bigr)=\det\bigl(Z^{T}Z\bigr)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\det\bigl(Y_{j}^{T}Y_{j}\bigr).

Since the extreme points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{l}
that form the columns of
\Xi
belong to
F_{N}
, it follows that

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\xi_{n\ell}=0,\quad\text{for each $\ell=1,2,\dots L$}.

For each
j=1,2,\dots,r
the nonzero rows of
Y_{j}
are indexed by the elements of
A_{j}
, and so we get

(4.23)

\sum_{n\in A_{j}}\xi_{n\ell}=0,\quad\text{for each $\ell\in D_{j}$}.

As
Y_{j}
has rank
|D_{j}|
we find that

(4.24)

|D_{j}|+1\leq|A_{j}|.

Next we will show that there is equality in the inequality (
4.24
). Each subset
A_{j}
is minimal in
\mathcal{P}
and therefore no proper
subset of
A_{j}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}
. And it follows from (
4.23
) that the
|A_{j}|
distinct (row) vectors

(4.25)

\big\{\bigl(\xi_{n\ell}\bigr):\text{$n\in A_{j}$}\big\}

are linearly dependent. Let
f:A_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a function that is supported on the subset

B=\big\{n\in A_{j}:\text{$f(n)\not=0$}\big\},

where
B
is a proper subset of
A_{j}
. As
B
does not belong to
\mathcal{P}
it follows that there exists
\ell_{1}
in
D_{j}
such that

\bigl|S(\ell_{1})\cap B\bigr|=1.

We conclude that

\sum_{n\in A_{j}}f(n)\xi_{n\ell_{1}}=\sum_{n\in B}f(n)\xi_{n\ell_{1}}\not=0,

because this sum contains exactly one nonzero term. This shows that no proper subset of the collection of (row) vectors (
4.25
)
is linearly dependent. In particular, each subset of the (row) vectors in (
4.25
) with cardinality
|A_{j}|-1
is linearly independent.
As the rank of the matrix
Y_{j}
is
|D_{j}|
we conclude from (
4.24
) that

(4.26)

|D_{j}|+1=|A_{j}|\quad\text{for each $j=1,2,\dots,r$}.

We also get the identity

(4.27)

L+r=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\bigl(|D_{j}|+1\bigr)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}|A_{j}|=N

which determines the value of
r
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let the columns of the
N\times L
matrix

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

be vectors from the set of extreme points
F_{N}
defined in
(
3.6
)
. If
L<N\leq 2L
then

(4.28)

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.

Proof.

Clearly we may assume that
\rank\Xi=L
. And we assume to begin with that no row of the matrix
\Xi
is identically zero.
As in our proof of Lemma
3.4
we have

(4.29)

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq\{1,2,\dots,N\}\\
|I|=L\end{subarray}}\bigl(\det\Xi_{I}\bigr)^{2}=\det\bigl(\Xi^{T}\Xi\bigr)

by the Cauchy-Binet identity. By combining (
4.22
) and (
4.29
) we find that

\big\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\big\|_{1}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\det\bigl(Y_{j}^{T}Y_{j}\bigr),

where each
N\times|D_{j}|
matrix
Y_{j}
is defined as in (
4.20
). Let
W_{j}
be the
|A_{j}|\times|D_{j}|
submatrix of
Y_{j}
obtained
by removing all rows which are identically zero. Because there is equality in the inequality (
4.24
) the submatrix
W_{j}
is also

(|D_{j}|+1)\times|D_{j}|
. That is,
W_{j}
is an
(M+1)\times M
matrix with columns in the set of extreme points
F_{M}
, where
M=|D_{j}|
.
Then it follows from the inequality (
3.23
) and (
4.26
) that

(4.30)

\begin{split}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\det\bigl(Y_{j}^{T}Y_{j}\bigr)&=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\det\bigl(W_{j}^{T}W_{j}\bigr)\\
&\leq\prod_{j=1}^{r}\bigr(|D_{j}|+1\bigr)\\
&=\prod_{j=1}^{r}|A_{j}|.\end{split}

We estimate the product on the right of (
4.30
) by applying the arithmetic/geometric mean inequality and using the identity
(
4.27
). In this way we arrive at the inequality

\begin{split}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\det\bigl(Y_{j}^{T}Y_{j}\bigr)&\leq\biggl(r^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}|A_{j}|\biggr)^{r}\\
&=\bigl(r^{-1}N\bigr)^{r}\\
&=\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.\end{split}

This proves (
4.28
) under the assumption that no row of
\Xi
is identically zero.

Next we suppose that
L<N\leq 2L
, that

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

is an
N\times L
matrix with columns
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
from
F_{N}
, that
\rank\Xi=L
, and that
\Xi
has exactly

N-M>0
rows that are identically zero. Because
\rank\Xi=L
, we find that
L\leq M<N\leq 2L
. We write

\Xi^{\prime}=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{\prime}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{\prime}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\bigr)

for the
M\times L
matrix obtained from
\Xi
by removing the rows of
\Xi
that are identically zero. It follows
from Lemma
3.2
that each column
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}
belongs to
F_{M}
.
Clearly each
L\times L
submatrix of
\Xi
with a row that is identically zero has a zero determinant. Thus we have

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{\prime}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\|_{1}.

If
L=M
then
\Xi^{\prime}
is
L\times L
, and it follows from Lemma
3.3
that

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{\prime}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\|_{1}=1\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.

If
L<M<N\leq 2L
then by the case already considered we get

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{\prime}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}^{\prime}\|_{1}&\leq\biggl(\frac{M}{M-L}\biggr)^{M-L}\\
&<\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.\end{split}

This verifies the bound (
4.28
) in general.
∎

We now combine Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
4.6
to obtain the inequality (
4.28
) in full generality.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the columns of the
N\times L
matrix

\Xi=\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\bigr)

be vectors in the set of extreme points
E_{N}\cup F_{N}
. If
L<N\leq 2L
then

(4.31)

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on the positive integer
L
. If
L=1
then
N=2
and the result is trivial to check.
Next we assume that
2\leq L
, and we assume that (
4.31
) holds for all pairs
(L^{\prime},N^{\prime})
such that

L^{\prime}<N^{\prime}\leq 2L^{\prime}
and
1\leq L^{\prime}<L
.

If the extreme points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
all belong
to the set of extreme points
E_{N}
, then

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}=1

and the inequality (
4.31
) is trivial. If the extreme points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
all belong to the set of extreme points
F_{N}
,
then the inequality (
4.31
) follows from Lemma
4.6
. To complete the proof we assume that
K
of the extreme
points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
belong to
E_{N}
, and
L-K
extreme points
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}
belong to
F_{N}
,
where
1\leq K<L
. In this case the set of extreme points satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
3.5
. It follows from the conclusion of
Lemma
3.5
that there exist linearly independent extreme points
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}
in the set

E_{N-K}\cup F_{N-K}
such that

(4.32)

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}=\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}\|_{1}.

We write
L^{\prime}=L-K
,
N^{\prime}=N-K
, and we consider two cases. First we suppose that

N^{\prime}\leq 2L^{\prime}.

In this case we apply the inductive hypothesis and conclude that

(4.33)

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}\|_{1}&\leq\biggl(\frac{N^{\prime}}{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\biggr)^{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\\
&=\biggl(\frac{N-K}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}\\
&<\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.\end{split}

Next we suppose that

2L^{\prime}\leq N^{\prime}.

In this case we appeal to the inequality (
3.27
) which we have already proved. By that result we have

(4.34)

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\eta}_{L-K}\|_{1}&\leq 2^{L^{\prime}}\\
&=\min\bigg\{2^{L^{\prime}},\biggl(\frac{N^{\prime}}{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\biggr)^{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\bigg\}\\
&\leq\biggl(\frac{N^{\prime}}{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\biggr)^{N^{\prime}-L^{\prime}}\\
&=\biggl(\frac{N-K}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}\\
&<\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}.\end{split}

Combining (
4.32
), (
4.33
), and (
4.34
), establishes the inequality

\|\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\xi}_{L}\|_{1}\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}

whenever
L<N\leq 2L
. This proves the lemma.
∎

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
belong to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
L<N\leq 2L
, then it follows from (
4.31
) that

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}\leq\biggl(\frac{N}{N-L}\biggr)^{N-L}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{x}_{L}).

This proves the inequality (
3.2
), and so completes the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We apply Theorem
3.1
with
N=r+1
and
L=q
, and we apply the theorem to the collection of linearly independent
points
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
in

\Gamma_{S}(k)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r+1}.

From (
3.4
) we find that

(5.1)

\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}&\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}\\
&\leq\min\bigg\{2^{q},\biggl(\frac{r+1}{r+1-q}\biggr)^{r+1-q}\bigg\}\ \delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q})\\
&=C(r,q)\ \delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1})\delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2})\cdots\delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}).\end{split}

By the product formula the points
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
belong to the diagonal subspace
\mathcal{D}_{r}
. Therefore we get

(5.2)

\delta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j})=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}\|_{1},\quad\text{for each $j=1,2,\dots,q$}.

Combining (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) establishes the inequality (
1.11
).

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let
1\leq L<N
and let

X=\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\ \boldsymbol{x}_{2}\ \cdots\ \boldsymbol{x}_{L}\bigr)

be an
N\times L
real matrix with columns
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
. We assume that the columns of
X
are

\mathbb{R}
-linearly independent so that
\rank X=L
, and

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\not=\boldsymbol{0}.

We use the matrix
X
to define a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{L}
by

(6.1)

\boldsymbol{y}\mapsto\|X\boldsymbol{y}\|_{1}.

The unit ball associated to the norm (
6.1
) is obviously the set

B_{X}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}:\|X\boldsymbol{y}\|_{1}\leq 1\big\}.

And it is not difficult to show that the dual unit ball is

B_{X}^{*}=\big\{X^{T}\boldsymbol{w}:\text{$\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ and $\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\infty}\leq 1$}\big\}.

It can be shown (see
[
6
]
,
[
18
]
, or for a more general result
[
22
, Lemma 2]
)
that the dual unit ball
B_{X}^{*}
is an example of a
zonoid. Therefore by an inequality of S. Reisner
[
15
, Theorem 2]
, we have

(6.2)

\frac{4^{L}}{L!}\leq\Vol_{L}\bigl(B_{X}\bigr)\Vol_{L}\bigl(B_{X}^{*}\bigr).

An identity for the
L
-dimensional volume of
B_{X}^{*}
was established by P. McMullen and C. G. Shephard as discussed in

[
12
]
and
[
19
, equation (57)]
.
Their result asserts that

(6.3)

\Vol_{L}\bigl(B_{X}^{*}\bigr)=2^{L}\sum_{|I|=L}\bigl|\det X_{I}\bigr|=2^{L}\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}.

By combining Reisner’s inequality (
6.2
) and the volume formula (
6.3
), we obtain the lower bound

(6.4)

\frac{2^{L}}{L!}\leq\Vol_{L}\bigl(B_{X}\bigr)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}.

Now let

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{L}<\infty

be the successive minima for the convex symmetric set
B_{X}
and the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{L}
. By Minkowski’s theorem
on successive minima (see
[
8
, Section VIII.4.3]
) we have

(6.5)

\Vol_{L}\bigl(B_{X}\bigr)\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{L}\leq 2^{L}.

We combine the lower bound (
6.4
) and the upper bound (
6.5
), and obtain the inequality

(6.6)

\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{L}\leq\bigl(L!\bigr)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}.

This leads to the following general result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the free group of rank
L
generated by the linearly independent
vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
. Then there exist linearly independent points
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{L}
in
\mathcal{X}
such that

(6.7)

\|\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\|_{1}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{2}\|_{1}\cdots\|\boldsymbol{y}_{L}\|_{1}\leq(L!)\|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{x}_{L}\|_{1}.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is the subgroup generated by the points
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{L}
, then

[\mathcal{X}:\mathcal{Y}]\leq L!
.

Proof.

By Minkowski’s theorem on successive minima there exist linearly independent points
\boldsymbol{m}_{1},\boldsymbol{m}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{m}_{L}
in
the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{L}
such that

(6.8)

\big\|X\boldsymbol{m}_{\ell}\|_{1}=\lambda_{\ell}\quad\text{for $\ell=1,2,\dots,L$}.

As
\rank X=L
the points

\big\{X\boldsymbol{m}_{\ell}:\ell=1,2,\dots,L\big\}

are linearly independent points in the free abelian group
\mathcal{X}
. We write
\boldsymbol{y}_{\ell}=X\boldsymbol{m}_{\ell}
for each
\ell=1,2,\dots,L
.
Then (
6.7
) follows from (
6.6
) and (
6.8
). The bound
[\mathcal{X}:\mathcal{Y}]\leq L!
also follows from
Minkowski’s theorem.
∎

Now let
L=q
,
N=r+1
and let
\mathfrak{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
be the subgroup of rank
q
generated by the linearly independent vectors

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}
.
By Theorem
6.1
there exist linearly independent vectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}
in
\mathfrak{A}
such that

\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\|_{1}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\|_{1}\cdots\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}\|_{1}\leq(q!)\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{q}\|_{1}.

Moreover, the free group
\mathfrak{B}\subseteq\mathfrak{A}
generated by the vectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2},\dots,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{q}
, has rank
q
and index

[\mathfrak{A}:\mathfrak{B}]\leq q!.

This proves Theorem
1.2
.
