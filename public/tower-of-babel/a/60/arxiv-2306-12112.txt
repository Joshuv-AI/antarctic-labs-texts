Partial Schauder estimates for unbounded solutions of the Kolmogorov equation
Andrzej Palczewski

Abstract
The goal of the paper is to show, under possibly weak assumptions, that the function given by the Feynman-Kac formula is a classical solution of the associated Kolmogorov equation. We also show that although this solution is unbounded it fulfills Schauder’s estimates. The paper collects mostly known results on the regularity of solutions of linear parabolic equations of second order and the regularity of solutions of Ito stochastic differential equations. We aim to reproduce these and related results in a unified fashion. Keywords: Schauder’s estimates, Kolmogorov equation, unbounded coefficients, Cauchy problem, regularity of solutions Mathematics Subject Classification (2010): 35K15, 35B65, 47D07, 60H30.

Introduction

The problem of the existence and regularity of solutions for the Kolmogorov
equation is a particular case of a more general analysis of
second-order parabolic equations. We regard this equation as a
diffusion equation
corresponding to a suitable stochastic differential equation. Our ultimate goal is to show that a functional of a solution of this stochastic equation given by the Feynman-Kac formula solves the Kolmogorov equation. We also obtain Schauder’s estimates for polynomially growing solutions of this equation.

Our starting point is
{X}(s)=(X^{1}(s),\dots,X^{d}(s))
a
d
-dimensional Ito stochastic process driven by a Wiener process
W(s)
in a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}^{t}_{s})_{t\leq s\leq T}
generated by
W(s)
. The notation
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{s}
means that we are considering the Wiener process
W(s)
starting at
s=t
and that the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{F}^{t}_{t}
is trivial.
The process
X(s)
is defined for
s\in[t,T]
by the system of stochastic differential equations

d{X}(s)={b}(s,{X}(s))ds+{\sigma}(s,{X}(s))d{W}(s),\quad{X}(t)={x}.

(1)

Here
b
is a
d
-dimensional vector,
\sigma
a matrix of dimension
d\times m
, and
{W}(s)
is an
m
-dimensional Wiener process. To indicate that
{X}(s)

is a solution of (
1
) with initial condition
{X}(t)={x}
we shall write
X^{t,x}(s)
.

Assuming that the coefficients
{b}(s,{y})
and

{\sigma}(s,{y})
are deterministic functions we define
the family of elliptic operators

{\mathcal{A}}^{t}u(t,{x})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(t,{x})\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}(t,{x})\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}},

(2)

where coefficients
a_{ij}
are given by the formula
a_{ij}\!=\!\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sigma_{ik}\sigma_{jk}
. The Kolmogorov equation, which will be the object of our investigation, is then given by the expression

D_{t}u(t,x)+{\mathcal{A}}^{t}u(t,x)-c(t,x)u(t,x)+f(t,x)=0,

(3)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

It is well known (cf. Theorem 5.7.6 in
[
18
]
)
that if equation (
3
) with continuous coefficients and continuous terminal condition possesses a smooth solution of class
C^{1,2}\bigl([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)
then
this solution can be represented by the Feynman-Kac formula.

Equally important is the inverse problem: under which conditions the function
given by the Feynman-Kac formula is a solution of equation
(
3
). A partial answer to this question is given by
Friedman (cf. Theorem 5.6.1 in
[
14
]
). A more complete analysis can be
found in the book by Krylov (Theorem V.7.4 in
[
20
]
). In both
mentioned theorems, the authors require a high smoothness of the coefficients
(
C^{2}
in
x
with
t
-uniform bounds on derivatives) and the terminal condition
(also
C^{2}
). On the other hand, the classical theory of parabolic equations in
bounded domains guarantees the existence of smooth solutions of equation
(
3
) for Hölder continuous coefficients and continuous initial
data (cf. Theorem 3.9 in
[
13
]
). But the picture is more complicated as the stochastic process
X^{t,x}(s)
that appears in the Feynman-Kac formula is a solution of equation (
1
) in which the coefficients can grow linearly with
x
. Hence, the associated equation (
3
) possesses unbounded coefficients, and the results of Theorem 3.9 in
[
13
]
cannot be straightforwardly applied.

In the last sixty years, parabolic equations with unbounded coefficients in unbounded domains were studied in great detail. After the pioneering works by Aronson and Besala
[
6
]
, and Besala
[
8
]
, the problem was studied by many authors who proved the existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions under different hypotheses on the coefficients in spaces of bounded continuous functions but also in
L^{p}
spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or on unbounded
domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(cf.
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
).

An essential difficulty in simultaneous analysis of the stochastic equation (
1
) and the parabolic equation (
3
) is caused by the assumed regularity of coefficients. The standard assumption for parabolic problems is Hölder’s continuity of coefficients in both variables
(t,x)
(see the book by Friedman
[
13
]
). On the other hand, a standard assumption for Ito stochastic equations is Lipschitz continuity in
x
and continuity (or only measurability) in
t
. To be in accord with the assumptions for stochastic differential equations, we should construct a solution for the parabolic equation (
3
) for coefficients only continuous in

t
, and Hölder continuous in the space
variables. Then one can expect the Schauder estimates controlling only the Hölder
x
-norms of the derivatives
of solutions. Such estimates for the mentioned weaker assumptions on the coefficients were introduced under the name of partial (or intermediate) Schauder estimates (cf.
[
10
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
24
]
). Then, to obtain the existence and uniqueness of solutions for linear parabolic problems in a bounded domain, it is sufficient to assume the continuity of coefficients in
t
and Hölder’s continuity in
x
, and the continuity of initial and boundary data (cf.
[
17
]
). These assumptions are weaker than the standard assumptions for stochastic equations where Lipschitz continuity in
x
is required, but we are not going to discuss this problem further (for the existence of unique solutions of equation (
1
) with local Lipschitz continuity of coefficients see Chapter 1 of the book by Cerrai
[
12
]
). Successfully, partial Schauder’s estimates were extended to bounded solutions of parabolic problems on the whole space (with lower order unbounded terms in
[
21
]
and all unbounded coefficients in
[
26
]
). Partial Schauder’s estimates for unbounded solutions (in weighted Hölder spaces) were obtained in
[
25
]
but only for bounded coefficients. Our goal in this paper is to obtain these estimates in the whole space for unbounded solutions and unbounded coefficients. These results are an extension of results in
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
.

Time regularity of the coefficients in the Kolmogorov equation requires an additional comment. There are papers assuming only
(t,x)
measurability of the coefficients and Hölder’s regularity in the spatial variables (cf.
[
7
]
,
[
26
]
). Under these assumptions it can be proved that the Kolmogorov equation is fulfilled in a generalized sense, i.e., the solution
u(t,x)
is a Lipschitz continuous function in
t
and fulfills the equation outside a negligible set. We are not going to explore this generalization, as our goal is the construction of classical solutions of equation (
3
), which requires the assumption of
t
-continuity of the coefficients.
Then under suitable assumptions on the space regularity, we get strong solutions of equation (
1
) and
C^{1,2}\bigl((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)
solutions of equation (
3
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we prove under relatively weak assumptions that the function given by the Feynman-Kac formula is a classical solution of the Kolmogorov equation (Theorem
2.4
). We also obtain estimates of the spatial derivatives of this solution (Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
2.8
). In Section 3 we extend the estimates of Section 2 to partial Schauder’s estimates in spaces of polynomially growing functions (Theorem
3.11
).

2
Equations of Kolmogorov type

We will now concentrate on the probabilistic representation of the solutions to the Cauchy problem of Kolmogorov’s equation

\begin{split}&D_{t}u(t,x)+{\mathcal{A}}^{t}u(t,x)-c(t,x)u(t,x)+f(t,x)=0,\ (t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
&u(T,x)=h(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{split}

(4)

where
{\mathcal{A}}^{t}
given by equation (
2
) is associated with the stochastic differential equation

d{X}(s)={b}(s,{X}(s))ds+{\sigma}(s,{X}(s))d{W}(s),\quad{X}(t)={x}.

(5)

About the coefficients of equation (
5
) we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

Let the coefficients in equation (
5
) be deterministic, continuous functions in
(s,{y})
and such that:

(A1)

|{b}(s,{y})-{b}(s,{y}^{\prime})|+|{\sigma}(s,{y})-{\sigma}(s,{y}^{\prime})|\leq C|{y}-{y}^{\prime}|
;

(A2)

|{b}(s,{y})|+|{\sigma}(s,{y})|\leq C(1+|{y}|)
;

for
{y},{y}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
s\in[t,T]
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
C
is a finite, positive constant depending on
T
.

We also assume that
x
is a deterministic vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Under the above assumptions, equation (
5
) possesses a unique strong solution
{X}^{t,x}(s)
.

Our goal is to prove that a solution of the Cauchy problem (
4
) is given by the Feynman-Kac formula

\begin{split}u(t,x)=\,&\mathbb{E}\biggl(h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr.\\
&\biggl.+\int_{t}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr),\end{split}

(6)

where
{X}^{t,x}(s)
is a solution of equation (
5
).

In the rest of the paper, we will use the following notation.

Notation
.

1.

The space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}
which are bounded and uniformly continuous on
\mathcal{B}
will be denoted
BC(\mathcal{B})
. This is a Banach space with the norm

\|f\|_{BC(\mathcal{B})}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{B}}|f(z)|.

Here
\mathcal{B}
can be a subset of
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
or a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2.

The space
BC^{p}(U)
,
p\geq 1
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is a space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,U\to\mathbb{R}
with continuous, bounded derivatives
D^{\alpha}f(x)\in BC(U)
, for
1\leq|\alpha|\leq p
. This space is a Banach space with the norm

\|f\|_{BC^{p}(U)}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq p}\sup_{x\in U}|D^{\alpha}f(x)|.

3.

Let
U
an open, bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We will always assume that an open bounded set
U
is compactly embedded in
B(R)
, an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of radius
R
, i.e.,
\bar{U}\subset B(R)
. For an open, bounded set
U
we define the Hölder space
H^{\beta}(U)
, a space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,U\to\mathbb{R}
which are in
BC(U)
and are
\beta
-Hölder continuous, that is

[f]_{\beta}=\sup_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x,x' \in U}{ x\neq x'}}\biggl(\frac{|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{\beta}}\biggl)<+\infty.

The Hölder space
H^{p+\beta}(U)
is a space of functions
f(x)
with continuous derivatives
D^{\alpha}f(x)\in BC(U)
, for
0\leq|\alpha|\leq p
, and
\beta
-Hölder continuous derivatives
D^{\alpha}f(x)
for
|\alpha|=p
.

The space
H^{p+\beta}(U)
is a Banach space for all
p\geq 0
and
\beta\in(0,1)
with the norm

\|f\|_{H^{p+\beta}(U)}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq p}\sup_{x\in U}|D^{\alpha}f(x)|+\sum_{|\alpha|=p}\bigl[D^{\alpha}f(x)\bigr]_{\beta}.

Analogously, we define the space
H^{p+\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

4.

The Hölder space
H^{p+\beta}_{\mathit{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, which for any open, bounded set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
belong to
H^{p+\beta}(U)
.

5.

Let
P(x)=1+|x|^{2q}
denote for an integer
q\geq 0
the polynomial weight in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
fP^{-1}\in BC^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
p\geq 0
, will be denoted
BC^{p}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is a Banach space with the norm

\|f\|_{BC^{p}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq p}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|D^{\alpha}\frac{f(x)}{P(x)}\bigg|.

6.

H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the space of functions
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
fP^{-1}\in H^{p+\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This is a Banach space with the norm

\|f\|_{H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq p}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|D^{\alpha}\frac{f(x)}{P(x)}\bigg|+\sum_{|\alpha|=p}\biggl[D^{\alpha}\frac{f(x)}{P(x)}\biggr]_{\beta}.

By simple computations, we obtain the following lemma that gives an equivalent definition of the norm in
H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(this is Lemma 2.5 of
[
25
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

For
f\in H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the norm
\|f\|_{H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
is equivalent to the norm

{|\kern-1.00006pt|\kern-1.00006pt|}f{|\kern-1.00006pt|\kern-1.00006pt|}_{H^{p+\beta}_{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq p}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg|\frac{D^{\alpha}f(x)}{P(x)}\bigg|+\sum_{|\alpha|=p}\biggl[\frac{D^{\alpha}f(x)}{P(x)}\biggr]_{\beta}.

We begin with the proof that under suitable assumptions, function
u(t,x)
given by equation (
6
) is a continuous function for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Results of this type are proved in many papers on the Kolmogorov equation with unbounded coefficients, but under very restrictive assumptions on the regularity of the coefficients (cf.
[
3
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
26
]
). In the paper by Rubio
[
30
]
, the continuity of
u(t,x)
was proved assuming
t
-Hölder and
x
-Lipschitz continuity of the coefficients and some regularity of initial data. The following theorem is much stronger as we assume only the continuity of coefficients and initial data, and Hölder’s continuity in the spatial variables of the coefficients.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let the coefficients of equation (
5
) fulfill the conditions of Assumptions
2.1
, i.e.,
{b}(t,{x})
and
{\sigma}(t,{x})
be
(t,x)
-continuous for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and with respect to
x
be Lipschitz continuous with linear growth.

The coefficients of equation (
4
) fulfill the following assumptions:

1.

c\kern-3.00003pt:\,[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
f\kern-3.00003pt:\,[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
are
(t,x)
-continuous and locally Hölder continuous with respect to
x
, i.e., for an open bounded set
U
such that
\bar{U}\subset B(R)
, and
t\in(0,T)
,
c(t,\cdot)
, and
f(t,\cdot)
belong to
H^{\beta}(U)
,
0<\beta<1
, and

\|c(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{\beta}(U)}+\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{\beta}(U)}\leq C(T,R).

2.

h\kern-3.00003pt:\,\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
x
-continuous.

3.

There is
c_{0}>0
such that
c(t,x)\geq c_{0}
for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.

There is an integer exponent
q\geq 1
such that:

|f(t,x)|\leq C(T)\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr)
for
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

|h(x)|\leq C\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Under the above assumptions the function
u(t,x)
given by equation (
6
) belongs to
C\bigl([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)
. Furthermore

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|u(t,x)|\leq C(T)\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
q
is the exponent defining the rate of growth of
f(t,x)
and
h(x)
.

Proof.
The proof explores many ideas of the proof of Theorem 3.1 of
[
30
]
. We are repeating this proof not only for the reader’s convenience but to point out the differences that make our proof possible under much weaker assumptions.

The proof of continuity of
u(t,x)
on the interval
[0,T]
requires special treatment of boundary points. To avoid these problems, we consider the interval
(-\gamma,T]
for
\gamma
positive but small. This overcomes the problem of the boundary point
t=0
. The point
t=T
still requires special consideration. Let
(t,x)\in(-\gamma,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{(t_{n},x_{n})\}_{n\in\mathcal{N}}
be a sequence of points
(t_{n},x_{n})\in(-\gamma,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
converging to
(t,x)
. We assume that
t_{n}\nearrow t
, then the point
t=T
can be treated in the same way as interior points.
This approach simplifies computations and puts no restriction on the generality of considerations.

The proof of convergence

u(t_{n},x_{n})\to u(t,x)

can be reduced by formula (
6
) to the following

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl(

\displaystyle h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr.

\displaystyle\biggl.-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\biggl(

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr.

\displaystyle\biggl.-\int_{t}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr)\to 0.

The strategy of the proof is to show that the expectations of random variables

\displaystyle v^{n}_{1}=\,

\displaystyle\biggl|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr.

\displaystyle\biggl.-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr|

and

\displaystyle v^{n}_{2}=\,

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{t_{n}}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr.

\displaystyle\biggl.-\int_{t}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr|

converge to zero.

An essential step in the proof is to show that variables
v^{n}_{1}
and
v^{n}_{2}
are uniformly integrable. We obtain the required uniform integrability by proving the square integrability of each of these variables.

For
v^{n}_{1}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|v^{n}_{1}|^{2}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\biggl(|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)^{2}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle+2\int_{\Omega}\biggl(|h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)^{2}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\bigl(1+|X^{t,x}(T)|^{4q}\bigr)+\bigl(1+|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)|^{4q}\bigr)\Bigr)d\mathbb{P}\leq C(1+|x|^{4q}).

Thus
v^{n}_{1}
is uniformly integrable.

To prove that
\mathbb{E}(v^{n}_{1})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
we define the set

\displaystyle B_{M,\eta}=

\displaystyle\{\omega\in\Omega\kern-3.00003pt:\,\sup_{s\in[t,T]}|X^{t,x}(\omega,s)|\leq M,\sup_{s\in[t_{n},T]}|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(\omega,s)|\leq M,

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[t_{n},T]}|X^{t,x}(\omega,s)-X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(\omega,s)|\leq\eta\}.

To avoid problem with the comparison of processes
X^{t_{n},x_{n}}
and
X^{t,x}
on the interval
(t_{n},t)
we extend
X^{t,x}
on this interval putting
X^{t,x}(s)=x
for
s\in[t_{n},t)
.

It follows from the uniform integrability of
v^{n}_{1}
that taking
M
sufficiently large and
\eta
sufficiently small we obtain

\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{M,\eta}}v^{n}_{1}d\mathbb{P}\leq\delta,

where
\delta
is a small number. To estimate the integral over
B_{M,\eta}
, we split
v^{n}_{1}

\begin{split}v^{n}_{1}\leq&\,\Big|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\\
&+\Big|h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\\
&\times\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|.\end{split}

(7)

For the mean value of the first term in the right hand side of (
7
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl(

\displaystyle\Big|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\Bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\bigl|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr|^{2q}+\mathbb{E}\bigl|X^{t,x}(T)\bigr|^{2q}\Bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr).

Since
X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)
converges in probability to
X^{t,x}(T)
(cf. Theorem II.2.1 in
[
22
]
and Lemma III.6.13 in
[
20
]
) then by Lemma III.6.13 in
[
20
]
we have

\mathbb{E}\biggl(\Big|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)\to 0.

Choosing
n
large enough, we obtain from the above

\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\biggl(\Big|h\bigl(X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(T)\bigr)-h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)d\mathbb{P}\leq\delta/2.

The estimation of the integral of the second term in the right hand side of (
7
) we begin with the estimation of the difference of exponents

\displaystyle\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Bigl(c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)-c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)\Bigr)dr\Bigr)-1\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl(\exp\Bigl(\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Big|c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)-c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)\Big|dr\Bigr)-1\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\biggl(\exp\Bigl(\int_{t_{n}}^{T}C\big|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)-X^{t,x}(r)\big|^{\beta}dr\Bigr)-1\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{e}^{CT\eta^{\beta}}-1\leq C\theta T\eta^{\beta},

where we used Hölder’s continuity of

and the mean value theorem which introduced
\theta\in[0,1]
.
Then for a sufficiently small
\eta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\biggl(\big|h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\big|\biggr.

\displaystyle\times\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|\biggr)d\mathbb{P}\leq\delta/2.

Collecting the above estimates, we have

\mathbb{E}(v^{n}_{1})=\int_{\Omega}v^{n}_{1}d\mathbb{P}\leq 2\delta.

Similarly to
v^{n}_{1}
, we prove the uniform integrability of
v^{n}_{2}
. To this end, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|v^{n}_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle d\mathbb{P}\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\biggl(\int_{t_{n}}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr)^{2}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle+2\int_{\Omega}\biggl(\int_{t}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr)^{2}d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int_{\Omega}\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Bigl(\bigl(1+\sup_{s\in[t,T]}|X^{t,x}(s)|^{4q}\bigr)+\bigl(1+\sup_{s\in[t_{n},T]}|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)|^{4q}\bigr)\Bigr)dsd\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CT\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(2+\sup_{s\in[t,T]}|X^{t,x}(s)|^{4q}+\sup_{s\in[t_{n},T]}|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)|^{4q}\Bigr)d\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CT\bigl(2+|x|^{4q}+|x_{n}|^{4q}\bigr)\leq CT(1+|x|^{4q}).

We define the set
B_{M,\eta}
in a similar way as for
v^{n}_{1}
and select
M
and
\eta
such that

\int_{\Omega\setminus B_{M,\eta}}v^{n}_{2}d\mathbb{P}\leq\delta.

To estimate the integral over
B_{M,\eta}
we split
v^{n}_{2}
as follows

\begin{split}v^{n}_{2}\leq&\,\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)-f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\\
&+\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\\
&\times\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|ds\end{split}

(8)

For the first integral in the right hand side of (
8
), we apply Hölder’s continuity of

to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{M,\eta}}

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\!\!\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)-f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\Big|\exp\Bigl(\!-\!\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)dsd\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\,C\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)\bigr)-f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\Big|dsd\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\,C\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\big|X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(s)-X^{t,x}(s)\big|^{\beta}dsd\mathbb{P}\leq\,CT\eta^{\beta}.

To estimate the second integral in the right hand side of (
8
) for
t_{n}\leq s\leq T
, we evaluate, like for

, the difference of exponents

\displaystyle\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|\leq C\theta s\eta^{\beta},

Then, for a sufficiently small
\eta
, we obtain for the second integral in the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\int_{B_{M,\eta}}

\displaystyle\int_{t_{n}}^{T}\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|

\displaystyle\times\bigg|\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t_{n}}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t_{n},x_{n}}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)-\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\bigg|dsd\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\leq\,C\eta^{\beta}\,\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\int_{t_{n}}^{T}s\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\Big|ds

\displaystyle\leq\,C\eta^{\beta}\,\int_{B_{M,\eta}}\int_{t_{n}}^{T}s\Bigl(1+\sup_{s\in[t,T]}\big|X^{t,x}(s)\big|^{2q}\Bigr)dsd\mathbb{P}\leq\,CT^{2}\eta^{\beta}\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr).

By the above estimates, we have for a sufficiently small
\eta

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(v^{n}_{2})=\int_{\Omega}v^{n}_{2}d\mathbb{P}\leq\int_{B_{M,\eta}}v^{n}_{2}d\mathbb{P}+\delta\leq C\eta^{\beta}(1+|x|^{2q})+\delta\leq 2\delta.

This proves that
u(t,x)
is continuous in
(-\gamma,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, hence also in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The growth estimate of

can be easily obtained from equation (

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}\biggl(

\displaystyle h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{T}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)\biggr)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big|h\bigl(X^{t,x}(T)\bigr)\Big|\leq C\Bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\bigl|X^{t,x}(T)\bigr|^{2q}\Bigr)\leq C\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr),

where the constant

,
q
, and the constant in the growth estimate of

.

For the second term in (

\displaystyle\bigg|

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\biggl(\int_{t}^{T}f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-\int_{t}^{s}c\bigl(r,X^{t,x}(r)\bigr)dr\Bigr)ds\biggr)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\,C\int_{t}^{T}\mathbb{E}\Big|f\bigl(s,X^{t,x}(s)\bigr)\Big|ds\leq\,C\int_{t}^{T}\Bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\big|X^{t,x}(s)\big|^{2q}\Bigr)ds\leq\,C\bigl(1+|x|^{2q}\bigr),
