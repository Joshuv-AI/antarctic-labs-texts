A single-particle framework for unitary lattice gauge theory in discrete time
Pablo Arnault, Christopher Cedzich

Abstract
We construct a real-time lattice-gauge-theory-type action for a spin-1/2 matter field of a single particle on a ( 1 + 1 ) (1+1) -dimensional spacetime lattice. The framework is based on a discrete-time quantum walk, and is hence inherently unitary and strictly local, i.e., transition amplitudes exactly vanish outside of a lightcone on the lattice. We then provide a lattice Noether’s theorem for internal symmetries of this action. We further couple this action to an electromagnetic field by a minimal substitution on the lattice. Finally, we suggest a real-time lattice-gauge-theory-type action for the electromagnetic field in arbitrary spacetime dimensions, and derive its classical equations of motion, which are lattice versions of Maxwell’s equations.



I
Introduction

Lattice gauge theory (LGT) is a framework used to tackle the non-perturbative regimes of quantum field theories (QFTs)
[
1
]
.
The basic idea is to formulate the gauge theory under study on a lattice, which furnishes an in-built ultraviolet regulator.
LGT was initially introduced by Wilson
[
2
]
as a framework accounting successfully for quark confinement; the gauge theory in question was (a simplified version of) quantum chromodynamics (QCD).

There are two fundamental types of LGTs.
The traditional one is “Lagrangian LGT”
[
2
]
, in which the main object of the theory is an action in discrete spacetime.
Lagrangian LGT treats time and space on the same footing, and is in this sense close to Einstein’s theory of relativity.
However, in Lagangian LGT unitarity is not manifest – it has to be proven and does not always hold, see, e.g., Ref.
[
3
]
.
The second type of LGT is “Hamiltonian LGT”
[
4
,
5
]
, in which the main object of the theory is a Hamiltonian on a spatial lattice while time is usually kept continuous.
The pros and cons are therefore reversed with respect to Lagrangian LGT: Hamiltonian LGTs are unitary by construction, but space and time are often treated differently.
These differences between Lagrangian and Hamiltonian LGT are actually already present in the continuum: Lagrangian LGT comes from the path-integral approach to QFT, in which Lorentz covariance is manifest but unitarity is not, whereas Hamiltonian LGT comes from the canonical approach to QFT, which is manifestly unitary but not manifestly Lorentz covariant
[
6
]
.

The standard tool of lattice QCD are Monte-Carlo (MC) simulations, which are usually carried out in the Lagrangian formulation with a Wick-rotated spacetime, i.e., in Euclidean spacetime with imaginary time
[
1
]
.
MC simulations have been successfully used to determine (i) equilibrium properties of QCD, such as the masses of quarks and stable hadrons, hadronic structure-related quantities and non-zero-temperature properties
[
7
]
, and (ii) certain non-equilibrium, i.e., beyond-ground-states properties
[
8
,
9
]
.
Despite its successes, standard LGT with MC simulations fails to give results in several cases, in particular in parameter regimes in which MC simulations encounter a so-called sign problem
[
7
]
, which can almost certainly not be solved efficiently by (even the most powerful
[
10

) classical computers with traditional techniques
[

Several techniques exist to overcome the sign problem for certain simplified models (see references in Ref.
[
7
]
), including tensor-network approaches
[
7
]
, which use a real-time Hamiltonian formulation, where we are going to define just below what “real-time” means.

Let us call “real-time LGT” any LGT framework in which spacetime is not Wick-rotated.
Because time is kept real, all such frameworks are expected to be particularly suited to explore the dynamics of the gauge theory in question
[
12
]
. Thereby, real-time LGT is one privileged approach to computing non-equilibrium properties of gauge theories.
Lately, real-time LGT has entered a new era with the advent of quantum simulation
[
13
]
and quantum computation
[
14

In this manuscript, we further complete the list of achievements in the one-particle sector with the following results:

(1)

We construct a real-time LGT-type action for spin-1/2 matter fields on a
(1+1)

(2)

(3)

We place the particle into an Abelian U(1) gauge field by applying a lattice minimal-coupling scheme to the DQW action.

(4)

For this Abelian U(1) gauge field which generates an electromagnetic field, we suggest a real-time LGT-type action in arbitrary spacetime dimensions, from which we derive the classical EOMs of the gauge field, which are lattice versions of Maxwell’s equations.

II
DQW-based LGT-type action for a classical matter field

II.1
The continuum equation of motion

Consider in
1+1
dimensions a relativistic classical matter field
\psi
, with internal components
\psi^{a}
where
a=1,...,N
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
The dynamics of
\psi
is described by the Dirac equation

(\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m)\psi=0\,,

(1)

where the
\gamma_{\mu}
,
\mu=0,1
, act on the internal Hilbert space, and satisfy the Clifford-algebra relations

\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}\,,

(2)

with
[\eta^{\mu\nu}]\vcentcolon=\text{diag}(1,-1)
, and where on the right-hand side of Eq. (
2
) we omitted for brevity the identity on the internal Hilbert space.

The Dirac equation (
1
) can be rewritten in the form of a Schrödinger equation,

\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}\psi=\mathcal{H}\psi\,.

(3)

Here,
\partial_{t}\equiv\partial_{0}
is the partial derivative with respect to time, and we have introduced the Dirac Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}\vcentcolon=\alpha^{1}(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{1})+m\alpha^{0}\,,

(4)

where
\partial_{1}\equiv\partial_{x^{1}}
is the partial derivative with respect to the spatial position
x^{1}
, and where we have introduced the operators

\displaystyle\alpha^{0}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\gamma^{0}

(5a)

\displaystyle\alpha^{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\,,

(5b)

which satisfy the relations

\{\alpha^{\mu},\alpha^{\nu}\}=2\delta^{\mu\nu}\,,

(6)

where
[\delta^{\mu\nu}]\vcentcolon=\text{diag}(1,1)
.

In one spatial dimension, it is enough to consider an internal Hilbert space of dimension 2 to find a pair of alpha matrices
([{(\alpha^{0})}^{a}_{b}],[{(\alpha^{1})}^{a}_{b}])
that satisfy Eq. (
6
).
In that case, the index
a
of the internal Hilbert space of
\psi
belongs to
\{1,2\}
.
Unless otherwise mentioned we will work with the abstract objects
\alpha^{0}
and
\alpha^{1}
rather than their matrix representations, such that in particular the dimension of the latter will not play a role.
Hence, the notation “
\psi
” is abstract in the internal Hilbert space but “concrete” in the position Hilbert space.

II.2
The naive discretization

We introduce the
(1+1)
-dimensional spacetime lattice
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
and label its sites by the multi-index
n\equiv(j,p)
. Denoting the spacetime-lattice spacing by
\epsilon
, we take
j
to label time, i.e., we set
t\equiv j\epsilon
, and
p
to label position, i.e.,
x^{1}\equiv p\epsilon
. We also define
x\equiv(t,x^{1})
, which we use flexibly in the continuum as well as in the discrete, in which case
x\equiv\epsilon n
. Moreover, we write

\psi_{n}\vcentcolon=\psi(\epsilon n)\,

(7)

for the field
\psi
evaluated at the lattice site
n
.

II.2.1
Lattice derivatives

A standard way
[
1
]
of discretizing Eq. (
3
) in space and time while preserving the Hermiticity of the operators
\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mu}
,
\mu=0,1
, and thus that of the Hamiltonian, is to use
symmetric finite differences
, i.e., to perform the substitution

\mathrm{i}\partial_{\mu}\longrightarrow\mathrm{i}d_{\mu}:=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mu}^{-1}-\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\right).

(8)

As above,
\epsilon
is the spacetime-lattice spacing, and
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
is the translation operator in direction
\mu
, i.e.,

(\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\psi)_{n}=\psi_{n-\hat{\mu}}\,,

(9)

where
\hat{\mu}
is the unit vector in direction
\mu
, compare with Ref.
[
56
, Eq. (33)]
.
Clearly,
\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mu}^{-1}-\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\right)
is Hermitian because
\mathcal{T}_{\mu}
is unitary. Moreover, in the continuum limit
\epsilon\to 0
we have
d_{\mu}\to\partial_{\mu}
.

For later use we also introduce the left and right lattice derivatives

\displaystyle d_{\mu}^{L}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(1-\mathcal{T}_{\mu}\right)

(10a)

\displaystyle d_{\mu}^{R}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\mathcal{T}_{\mu}^{-1}-1\right)\,,

(10b)

such that
d_{\mu}=(d_{\mu}^{L}+d_{\mu}^{R})/2
.

II.2.2
Standard-LGT scheme: naive fermions

Discretizing the Dirac equation, Eq. (
3
), with the symmetric finite differences yields

\,\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\mathcal{T}_{0}^{-1}-\mathcal{T}_{0})\,\psi=-\alpha^{1}\,\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\mathcal{T}_{1}^{-1}-\mathcal{T}_{1})\psi+\alpha^{0}m\psi\,.

(11)

This equation can be rewritten as

\mathrm{i}d_{0}{\psi}={\mathcal{H}}^{\text{LGT}}{\psi}\,,

(12)

a scheme which we call that of
naive fermions
, where the lattice Hamiltonian is

{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{LGT}}\vcentcolon=\alpha^{1}(-\mathrm{i}d_{1})+{m}\alpha^{0}\,.

(13)

The associated action can be found in Ref.
[
1
, Sec. 4.1]
.

A comment must be made at this stage: the lattice Hamiltonian of Eq. (
13
) is usually introduced in Hamiltonian LGTs in which time is kept continuous
[
5
,
69
]
, and in those frameworks the role of the Hamiltonian is the usual one, that is, it generates the time evolution. Here, we introduce
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{LGT}}
even though we are in discrete time, and we call it a “Hamiltonian” even though it does not generate the time evolution in the usual sense: time evolution is described instead by the lattice EOM of Eq. (
12
).

II.3
The DQW discretization

The use of the symmetric lattice derivatives above ensures the Hermiticity of the lattice Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{LGT}}
.
Thus, if time was not discretized and time evolution was generated by this lattice Hamiltonian, the scheme would be unitary.
However, when also discretizing time, what about unitarity?

In textbook Lagrangian LGT, discrete-time formulations are usually carried out in Euclidean spacetime.
In such a framework, unitarity of the model is proven essentially by proving the positivity of the transfer operator
2
2

2

A transfer operator is any operator
\hat{T}
which in the continuum limit coincides with
\exp(-\tau\hat{H})
, where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian of the system, but which at the discrete level could differ from merely exponentiating
\hat{H}
. In the discrete, the exact form of
\hat{T}
is adjusted in order to facilitate the computation of the transition amplitudes
\langle n|\hat{T}|n^{\prime}\rangle
. For example, for a point particle of mass
m
in a potential
V
, a possible suitable definition for the transfer operator is
T\vcentcolon=\exp(-\tau V(\hat{x})/2)\exp(-\tau\hat{p}^{2}/(2m))\exp(-\tau V(\hat{x})/2)
, where
\hat{x}
is the position operator, and
\hat{p}
is the momentum operator. The naive transfer operator is simply the Euclidean version,
\exp(-\tau\hat{H})
, of the one-step evolution operator
\exp(-\mathrm{i}t\hat{H})
.
of the system, or alternatively by proving the so-called Osterwalder–Schrader reflection-positivity condition
[
3
]
.

These properties are far from straightforward to establish: for example, the positivity of the transfer matrix for lattice fermions has only been proven for Wilson fermions with Wilson parameter
r=1
, and there is no proof for naive fermions, which have
r=0

[
3
]
, i.e., there is no proof of the positivity of the transfer operator for the scheme of Eq. (
12
).
Based on
II.3.1
and
II.3.2
below, we believe that this scheme is simply not unitary
3
3

3

That is to say, more precisely, there exists no underlying unitary one-step scheme that generates naive fermions, see below.
.

II.3.1
Modification of naive fermions for unitarity in discrete time

First, notice that the scheme of naive fermions, Eq. (
12
), is – because of the use of a symmetric lattice derivative for time – a
two-step
scheme, i.e., it needs two initial conditions
{\psi}_{j=0}
and
\psi_{j=1}
.
As in Ref.
[
68
]
, let us perform in Eq. (
12
) the following substitution,

\alpha^{\mu}\longrightarrow\tilde{\alpha}^{\mu}\vcentcolon=\mu_{\epsilon}\alpha^{\mu}\,,\ \ \mu=0,1\,,

(14)

where the prefactor

\mu_{\epsilon}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\epsilon{m})^{2}}}\,,

(15)

is positive for
\epsilon m
small enough.
This substitution (
14
) leads to a new two-step scheme which we call
unitary fermions
,

\mathrm{i}d_{0}{\psi}={\mathcal{H}}^{\text{DQW}}{\psi}\,,

(16)

where the lattice Hamiltonian is now

{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{DQW}}\vcentcolon=\tilde{\alpha}^{1}(-\mathrm{i}d_{1})+m\tilde{\alpha}^{0}\,.

(17)

We are going to see in the next subsection that this new two-step scheme, Eq. (
16
),
is equivalent to a
unitary
one-step scheme,

\mathcal{T}_{0}^{-1}\psi_{j}\equiv{\psi}_{j+1}={\mathcal{W}}{\psi}_{j}\,,

(18)

with properly chosen one-step unitary evolution operator
\mathcal{W}
and provided that the second initial condition
{\psi}_{j=1}
is given precisely by this unitary one-step scheme, that is,

{\psi}_{j=1}={\mathcal{W}}{\psi}_{j=0}\,.

(19)

The two schemes are equivalent in the usual sense: if
\psi_{j}
satisfies the one-step scheme, then it satisfies the two-step scheme, and vice versa.
Note that this equivalence has already been proven in Ref.
[
68
]
. For the convenience of the reader we review it in the next subsection.

II.3.2
The “underlying” unitary one-step scheme

Let us explicate how the two-step scheme of Eq. (
16
) can be obtained from a one-step scheme of the type of Eq. (
18
).
Since the field
\psi
in Eq. (
16

{\mathcal{W}}\vcentcolon=W_{-1}\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{1}+W_{+1}\mathcal{T}_{1}+W_{0}\mathbbm{1}\,,

where the
jump operators

W_{i}

, and

is the translation operator defined in Eq. (

We henceforth call

We define the following

\displaystyle B_{\pm}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=W_{1}\pm W_{-1}

(21a)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=-1,0,+1}W_{i}=B_{+}+W_{0}\,,

(21b)

which encode transport properties of the scheme and of its continuum limit, as we are going to see below.

Under which conditions on
B_{+}
,
B_{-}
and

Notice first that if

satisfies Eq. (

\mathrm{i}d_{0}{\psi}={\mathcal{H}}_{Q}{\psi}\,,

(22)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\left(\mathcal{W}-\mathcal{W}^{{\dagger}}\right)

(23a)

\displaystyle=A^{1}(-\mathrm{i}\epsilon d_{1})+\frac{r}{2}Q(-\mathcal{L})+\epsilon m{A}^{0}\,,

(23b)

where we have (i) introduced the lattice Laplacian

\mathcal{L}\vcentcolon=\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{1}+\mathcal{T}_{1}-2\,,

(24)

(ii) forced the appearance of the Wilson parameter

and the “mass”

\displaystyle{A}^{0}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon m}(M-M^{{\dagger}})

(25a)

\displaystyle A^{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2}\left(B_{-}+B_{-}^{{\dagger}}\right)

(25b)

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2r}(B_{+}-B_{+}^{{\dagger}})\,.

(25c)

Notice that the two-step local Hamiltonian of a DQW, defined in Eq. (

Now, for

\displaystyle{A}^{0}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\alpha}^{0}

\displaystyle A^{1}

\displaystyle B_{-}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\alpha}^{1}\,,

II.4
The lattice action of the matter field

The only difference between naive fermions and unitary fermions is the prefactor
\mu_{\epsilon}
appearing in the modified
\alpha
operators and hence also in the following modified
\gamma
operators,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{0}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=(\tilde{\alpha}^{0})^{-1}=\frac{\alpha^{0}}{\mu_{\epsilon}}=\frac{\gamma^{0}}{\mu_{\epsilon}}

(33a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}^{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=(\tilde{\alpha}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\alpha}^{1}=\gamma^{1}\,.

(33b)

It turns out that one can obtain a valid lattice action for unitary fermions by performing the substitution
\gamma^{\mu}\longrightarrow\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}
in the, e.g.,
asymmetric
lattice action of naive fermions.
This results in

S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}\vcentcolon=\epsilon^{2}\sum_{n}\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n}\left[\left(\mathrm{i}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}d_{\mu}-{m}\right)\psi\right]_{n}\,,

(34)

with

\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n}\vcentcolon=\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\,.

(35)

In Appendix
A
, we show how extremalizing this action, Eq. (
34
), indeed yields the correct EOM of unitary fermions, Eq. (
16
).

We call the action in Eq. (
34
) “asymmetric”, since the lattice derivative only acts to the right. As a consequence, it is in general not a real but a complex number. The variational problem is usually conceived for a real action. Yet, as we show in Appendix
B
, this asymmetric action
S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}
is equal (up to boundary terms) to the following
symmetric
and therefore real-valued action,

S_{\text{DQW}}\vcentcolon=\epsilon\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{n}\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\text{H.\ \hskip-5.69054pt c.}-\epsilon\sum_{n}\epsilon{m}\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n}{\psi}_{n}\,.

(36)

One can show that extremalizing the symmetric action in Eq. (
36
) yields Eq. (
16

III
Noether’s theorem in discrete spacetime for internal symmetries

In this section, we derive a lattice Noether’s theorem for internal symmetries. By “internal symmetry” we mean, as usual, that the corresponding transformation acts only on the internal Hilbert space of the system, and does not affect the spacetime coordinates.

III.1
The framework

Consider an action being the sum, over the lattice sites, of a Lagrangian density which is function of (i) the fields
\psi^{a}
,
a=1,2
, and (ii) their shifts in time and space, that is,

S_{F}\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}(\psi_{n},\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}},\psi_{n}^{{\dagger}},\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}})\,,

(37)

5

5

S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}

S_{\text{DQW}}

.
Let us be fully precise on our notations:
here and below,

\phi^{a}_{n+\hat{\mu}}

The lattice Noether’s theorem that we are going to derive is inspired by the general one for usual continuum field theories in Ref.

Consider an arbitrary transformation,
\psi_{n}^{a}\longrightarrow(\psi_{n}^{a})^{\prime}\vcentcolon=f^{a}((\psi_{n}^{b})_{b},\alpha)\equiv f^{a}(\psi_{n},\alpha)

In the present work, we will only consider the case of a global U(1) transformation, for which

The generalization to a larger family of

Although the single application we know for

The transformed state is collectively given by

S

S=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}\left(((A_{\mu})_{n})_{\mu=0,1},(d^{R}_{\nu}A_{\mu}|_{n})_{\mu,\nu=0,1}\right)\,.

(78)

Extremalizing this action, Eq. (
78
), leads to the following Euler-Lagrange equations of motion for the gauge field
A_{\mu}
,

\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial(A_{\mu})_{n}}\right|_{n}-\left.d^{L}_{\nu}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\,d^{R}_{\nu}A_{\mu}|_{n}}\right|_{n}=0\,,

(79)

where we recall that
d^{L}_{\mu}
and
d^{R}_{\mu}
are the left and right lattice derivatives, defined in Eqs. (
10
).

V.4.2
Evaluating the lattice Euler-Lagrange equations

The first term of Eq. (
79
) is easily computed, and yields

\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial(A_{\mu})_{n}}\right|_{n}=-q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{U(1)}})^{\mu}_{n}\,,

(80)

where

\begin{split}&(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{U(1)}})^{\mu}_{n}\\
&\vcentcolon=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}e^{\mathrm{i}q\epsilon(A_{\mu})_{n}}\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\bar{\bar{\psi}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}e^{-\mathrm{i}q\epsilon(A_{\mu})_{n}}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{n}\right)\,,\end{split}

(81)

which coincides with the gauged version of the Noether U(1)-charge current given in Eq. (
47
).

To compute the second term of the Euler-Lagrange equations, we need to distinguish between
\mu=0
and
\mu=l\neq 0
.
After a few lines of computation for
\mu=0
, remembering that
F_{\nu 0}=-F^{\nu 0}
, another few lines for
\nu=0
, and another few lines for
\nu=k
and
\mu=l
, remembering that
F_{kl}=F^{kl}
, we combine all three formulæ into

-\left.d^{L}_{\nu}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\,d^{R}_{\nu}A_{\mu}|_{n}}\right|_{n}=d^{L}_{\nu}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}F^{\nu\mu}_{n}\right)\right]\,.

(82)

Inserting Eqs. (
80
) and (
82
) into Eq. (
79
), we obtain the following equations of motion for the gauge field,

d^{L}_{\nu}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}F^{\nu\mu}_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{U(1)}})^{\mu}_{n}\,.

(83)

V.4.3
The two inhomogeneous lattice Maxwell equations

Let us show that the equations of motion for the gauge field, Eq. (
83
), correspond to the two inhomogeneous lattice Maxwell equations.
For
\mu=0
, Eq. (
83
) gives

d^{L}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}E^{k}_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{DQW}})^{0}_{n}\,,

(84)

where

E^{k}_{n}\vcentcolon=F^{k0}_{n}\,,

(85)

is the lattice electric field.
Equation (
84
) is a lattice version of Maxwell-Gauss’ equation, which is a constraint rather than a dynamical equation: dividing by
\epsilon^{2}
on both sides and taking the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
indeed yields

\partial_{k}E^{k}=qJ^{0}_{\text{Dirac}}\,.

(86)

For
\mu=l
, Eq. (
83
) becomes

d^{L}_{0}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}F^{0l}_{n}\right)\right]+d^{L}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}F^{kl}_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{DQW}})^{l}_{n}\,,

(87)

that is, taking into account

\displaystyle F^{kl}_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv-{\varepsilon^{kl}}_{m}B^{m}_{n}\,,

(88)

where
B^{m}_{n}
is the lattice magnetic field, and swapping
k
and
l
in
{\varepsilon^{kl}}_{m}
(so that we pick up a minus sign),

-d^{L}_{0}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}E^{l}_{n}\right)\right]+{\varepsilon^{lk}}_{m}d^{L}_{k}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}B^{m}_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{DQW}})^{l}_{n}\,.

(89)

This is a lattice version of Maxwell-Ampère’s equation, which can be seen by taking in Eq. (
89
) the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
after having divided by
\epsilon^{2}
on both sides, which yields

-\partial_{0}E^{l}+{\varepsilon^{lk}}_{m}\partial_{k}B^{m}=qJ_{\text{Dirac}}^{l}\,.

(90)

The convergence of these two lattice Maxwell equations, Eqs. (
84
) and (
89
), has been proven in Ref.
[
74
]
.

V.4.4
The two inhomogeneous lattice Maxwell equations in
(1+1)
-dimensional spacetime

In
(1+1)
-dimensional spacetime, the lattice Maxwell-Gauss constraint, Eq. (
84
), reduces to

d^{L}_{1}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}E^{1}_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{DQW}})^{0}_{n}\,,

(91)

with continuum limit

\partial_{1}E^{1}=qJ^{0}_{\text{Dirac}}\,,

(92)

and the lattice Maxwell-Ampère equation, Eq. (
89
), reduces to

-d^{L}_{0}\left[\frac{1}{q}\sin\left(q\epsilon^{2}(E^{1})_{n}\right)\right]=q\epsilon(J^{\text{g}}_{\text{DQW}})^{1}_{n}\,,

(93)

with continuum limit

-\partial_{0}E^{1}=qJ_{\text{Dirac}}^{1}\,.

(94)

Finally, the homogeneous Maxwell equations, namely, Maxwell-Thompson’s and Maxwell-Faraday’s equations, are not relevant in one spatial dimension.

VI
Conclusions and discussion

Let us sum up the main achievements of this work.
First, we have constructed a discrete-spacetime action
S_{\text{DQW}}
for a spin-1/2 matter field, with the following properties: it is (i) in real time, (ii) based on a two-step EOM and therefore extremely similar to usual actions of LGT, (iii) associated to a unitary (classical-fields) one-step scheme, i.e., a discrete-time quantum walk, which in the continuum limit yields the Dirac equation.
Second, we have derived a lattice Noether’s theorem for internal symmetries of
S_{\text{DQW}}
.
More precisely, we have proven the theorem for an internal symmetry depending on a single real parameter
\alpha
, but believe that the generalization to a larger parameter family poses no major difficulty.
We have applied the Noether’s theorem to the global
\text{U}(1)
symmetry of
S_{\text{DQW}}
and have obtained a conserved current which in the continuum coincides with the usual Dirac charge current.
Third, we have coupled
S_{\text{DQW}}
via a minimal coupling on the lattice to an Abelian U(1) gauge field.
Although
S_{\text{DQW}}
is based on a two-step EOM, unitarity relies on the fact that this two-step EOM is associated to a one-step EOM. We have thus explored the gauging of the one-step EOM as in Ref.
[
56
]
. This has lead us to the observation that gauging directly the two-step EOM does not yield the same EOM as that obtained by first gauging the one-step EOM and then constructing a two-step EOM from it; the two procedures are equivalent only if the temporal component of the gauge field,
A_{0}
, is independent of space.
Finally, we have suggested a real-time LGT-type action for the Abelian U(1) gauge field, from which we have derived the classical EOMs of the gauge field, which are lattice versions of Maxwell’s equations.

A first question that remains unanswered is the following: Is it true that there exist no underlying unitary scheme for naive fermions? While we believe this to be the case, we do not have a proof.
A second topic that is unadressed in this work is that of external (i.e., spacetime) symmetries of the action:
Can one define such symmetries and derive associated Noether’s theorems? Refs.
[
75
,
76
]
partially address this problem in the realm of DQWs.
Also, we did not address quantized fields:
What multi-particle concepts from the QCA LGT in Refs.
[
43
,
47
,
44
,
45
]
should we import into our Lagrangian framework in order to build a fully fledged action-based LGT that respects strict locality and describes fermionic matter fields?

Finally, the issue of fermion doubling has not been adressed, because it is still unsolved for the following reasons. In Ref.
[
68
]
a fermion-doubling issue is solved for the two-step “Hamiltonian”.
But, although such a two-step Hamiltonian would indeed be subject to a fermion-doubling issue if the scheme was in continuous time, in Ref.
[
68
]
time is discrete. In addition, the two-step scheme is generated by a one-step scheme which does
not
exhibit a fermion-doubling issue
[
1
]
. One should hence examine whether the fact that the two-step Hamiltonian of Ref.
[
68
]
has a fermion-doubling issue is actually relevant if time is discrete and, moreover, if the generating one-step scheme does not have this issue. Now, all that being said, in the present framework the matter-field action
S_{\text{DQW}}
, that is defined with the two-step Hamiltonian, is extremely similar to usual LGT fermionic actions
[
3
]
, which suggests that a fermion-doubling issue
does
arise. To sum up: if
S_{\text{DQW}}
does not exhibit fermion doubling, then there is nothing to be done, and if it does, then modifying the two-step Hamiltonian as in Ref.
[
68
]
should remove at least spatial doublers. Finally, if there are temporal doublers in
S_{\text{DQW}}
, then some fix must be made in order to remove them without breaking the unitarity of the model.

Appendix A
Variational principle for unitary fermions

For brevity, in this appendix we write the Hamiltonian of Eq. (
17
) as

\mathcal{H}^{\text{DQW}}=\mathcal{H}\,.

(95)

For the sake of pedagogy, we are going to consider here the variational problem for the equations of motion, with the non-real action, i.e., that of Eq. (
34
), because with it one gets a good feeling of what is going on, but below in Appendix
C
we will also consider this variational problem on the generic real action of Eq. (
37
) (a particular case of which is the real action of Eq. (
36
)).

The action of Eq. (
34
) can be rewritten in the following Hamiltonian form,

\displaystyle S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j}\langle\psi_{j}\,|\,\mathrm{i}d_{0}\psi\big|_{j}-\mathcal{H}\psi_{j}\rangle

(96a)

\displaystyle\equiv\epsilon^{2}\sum_{j}\sum_{p}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}\left[\mathrm{i}d_{0}\psi\big|_{j,p}-(\mathcal{H}\psi_{j})_{p}\right]\,,

(96b)

where
d_{0}
is the symmetric lattice derivative defined in Eq. (
8
), and where we have introduced the Hermitian product

\langle\psi_{j}\,|\,\phi_{j}\rangle\,\vcentcolon=\epsilon\sum_{p}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}\phi_{j,p}\,.

(97)

We remark that here and below
\mathcal{H}
could depend on time, but we omit the corresponding index
j
to keep the notation light.

The action Eq. (
96a
) is a functional of the following real independent fields,
\text{Re}(\psi^{a})
and
\text{Im}(\psi^{a})
, where
a=1,2
labels the internal components of
\psi
.
In the continuum, one can show that variational problems for a Dirac-field action can be equivalently solved by considering this action as a functional of the following complex independent fields,
\psi^{a}
and
(\psi^{a})^{\ast}
,
a=1,2
. We assume that this feature is robust to the discretization. Moreover, since the discretization is the same for all internal components of the field, for convenience we treat the fields collectively (see Sec.
III.1
) as
\psi
and
\psi^{{\dagger}}
, and therefore write

S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}\equiv\mathscr{S}[\psi,\psi^{{\dagger}}]\,.

(98)

Let
\psi
be a field that extremalizes
S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}
, and consider an arbitrary variation
\delta\psi
of
\psi
.
Let us compute the variation of the action under the variations
\delta\psi
and
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}
, namely,

\delta S\vcentcolon=\mathscr{S}[\psi+\delta\psi,\psi^{{\dagger}}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}]-\mathscr{S}[\psi,\psi^{{\dagger}}]\,.

(99)

In variational problems like this, one can usually show that as long as the action is linear in its arguments, one can keep in
\delta S
only the first-order terms in the variations
\delta\psi
and
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}
, which we are going to assume and which here delivers,

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}\sum_{j,p}\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}\left[\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\psi_{j+1,p}-\psi_{j-1,p})-(\mathcal{H}\psi_{j})_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{2}\sum_{j,p}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}\left[\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\delta\psi_{j+1,p}-\delta\psi_{j-1,p})-(\mathcal{H}\delta\psi_{j})_{p}\right]

(100)

\displaystyle=:\delta S_{1}+\delta S_{2}\,,

(101)

where

\displaystyle\delta S_{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\epsilon\sum_{j}\Big\langle\delta\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\psi_{j+1,p}-\psi_{j-1,p})-\mathcal{H}\psi_{j}\Big\rangle

(102a)

\displaystyle\delta S_{2}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\epsilon\sum_{j}\Big\langle\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\delta\psi_{j+1,p}-\delta\psi_{j-1,p})-\mathcal{H}\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(102b)

If we choose
\delta\psi=0
, then
\delta S=\delta S_{1}
, and requiring
\delta S=0
for any
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}
trivially delivers the correct equations of motion (EOM).
To see that this also works if
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}=0
, so that
\delta S=\delta S_{2}
, we need to do some work on
\delta S_{2}
.
First of all, if
j
runs from an initial
j_{i}
to a final
j_{f}
and
p
from
p_{i}
to
p_{f}
, then because of the symmetric lattice derivatives the action
S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}
in Eq. (
96a
) must be defined with a
\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}
and a
\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}
, i.e., omitting the first and last indices.
Then, from (
102b
) we have that

\displaystyle\delta S_{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\delta\psi_{j+1}\Big\rangle+\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\delta\psi_{j-1}\Big\rangle+\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,-\mathcal{H}\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(103)

After (i) performing a discrete integration by parts (i.e., a shift) in
j
in the two first terms, and (ii) using the definition of the adjoint of
\mathcal{H}
in the third one, we obtain

\displaystyle\delta S_{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+2}^{j_{f}}\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j-1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle+\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-2}\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j+1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle+\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle-\mathcal{H}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(104)

At this point of the derivation of such a variational problem, it is frequent to say that we keep the ends of
\psi_{j}
fixed, i.e., choose the lattice Neumann boundary conditions

\delta\psi_{j_{i}}=\delta\psi_{j_{i}+1}=\delta\psi_{j_{f}-1}=\delta\psi_{j_{f}}=0\,,

(105)

so as not to have to deal with them. Note that this is not necessary for now, but it will be in the final reasoning where it ensures that the solution
\psi
is determined, i.e., unique.
With these boundary conditions the first term of the right-hand side of Eq. (
104
) becomes

\displaystyle\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+2}^{j_{f}}\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j-1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+2}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j-1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle+\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{N-1}\,\Big|\,\underbrace{\delta\psi_{N}}_{=0}\Big\rangle+\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{0}\,\Big|\,\underbrace{\delta\psi_{1}}_{=0}\Big\rangle

(106a)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j-1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(106b)

Similarly, the the second term of the right-hand side of Eq. (
104
) becomes

\displaystyle\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-2}\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j+1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}\psi_{j+1}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(107)

Inserting Eqs. (
106b
) and (
107
) into Eq. (
104
) and using the hermiticity of
\mathcal{H}
, we finally obtain that

\delta S_{2}=\epsilon\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\Big\langle\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\epsilon}(\psi_{j+1,p}-\psi_{j-1,p})-\mathcal{H}\psi_{j}\,\Big|\,\delta\psi_{j}\Big\rangle\,.

(108)

If we choose
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}=0
, then
\delta S=\delta S_{2}
, and requiring
\delta S=0
for any
\delta\psi
now trivially delivers the correct EOM of Eq. (
16
). This completes the proof that the action of Eq. (
34
) is a valid one for unitary fermions up to not being real-valued, an issue which is immediately solved by considering the real-valued action of Eq. (
36
) instead.

If we had not fixed the ends of
\psi_{j}
we would have obtained (
108
) with additional boundary terms. Then, to arrive at the EOM in the “bulk” one has to successively choose particular variation functions
\delta\psi_{j}
as usual for this kind of proofs, with in the present case the (additional) requirement that all these variations functions have vanishing ends. Fixing the ends in the beginning like we did is thus not necessary but merely convenient, and we could have and would have obtained the same EOM if we had done the derivation with loose ends.

Appendix B
Equivalence of the asymmetric and symmetric actions of unitary fermions

Let us show that the asymmetric action of unitary fermions, Eq. (
34
), is equal to the symmetric action of Eq. (
36
) up to boundary terms.
We start from the asymmetric action:

\displaystyle S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}\sum_{n}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\left[\left(\mathrm{i}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\frac{1}{2\epsilon}(\mathcal{T}^{-1}_{\mu}-\mathcal{T}_{\mu})-{m}\right)\psi\right]_{n}

(109a)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{n}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\sum_{n}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{n-\hat{\mu}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\epsilon\sum_{n}\epsilon{m}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\psi_{n}

(109b)

We consider the second term, and perform discrete integrations by parts on this term, i.e., a shift of one lattice site in
\mu
for
\mu=0,1
.
To avoid cumbersome notations, we write explicitly
n=(j,p)
. Then, the second term of
S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}
becomes

\displaystyle T^{(2)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{(j,p)-\hat{\mu}}

(110a)

\displaystyle=\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\Bigg[\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j-1,p}+\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\psi_{j,p-1}\Bigg]

(110b)

\displaystyle=\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\Bigg[\underbrace{\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-2}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j+1,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j,p}}_{A^{(0)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-2}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p+1}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\psi_{j,p}}_{A^{(1)}}\Bigg]\,.

(110c)

Extracting the boundary term
j=j_{i}
out of
A^{(0)}
and adding and effective zero yields

\displaystyle A^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-2}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j+1,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j,p}+\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j_{i}+1,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j_{i},p}+\underbrace{\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j_{f},p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j_{f}-1,p}-\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j_{f},p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j_{f}-1,p}}_{=0}

(111a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j+1,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j,p}+\underbrace{\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j_{i}+1,p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j_{i},p}-\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j_{f},p}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{0}\psi_{j_{f}-1,p}\right)}_{B^{(0)}}\,.

(111b)

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p+1}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\psi_{j,p}+\underbrace{\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\left(\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p_{i}+1}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\psi_{j,p_{i}}-\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j,p_{f}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{1}\psi_{j,p_{f}-1}\right)}_{B^{(1)}}\,.

(112)

Inserting these expressions, Eqs. (
111b
) and (
112
), back into (
110c
), we obtain

T^{(2)}=\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\sum_{n}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\psi_{j,p}+B^{(0)}+B^{(1)}\,.

(113)

Now, equating

\displaystyle(\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}=\frac{\gamma^{0}}{\mu_{\epsilon}}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\gamma^{0}\gamma^{0}=\gamma^{0}\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu}\gamma^{0}\frac{\gamma^{0}}{\mu_{\epsilon}}=(\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu})^{{\dagger}}((\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\,,

(114)

and inserting the last equality into Eq. (
113
) delivers

\displaystyle T^{(2)}=\epsilon(\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2})\sum_{n}\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{\mu})^{{\dagger}}((\tilde{\gamma}^{0})^{-1})^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j,p}+B^{(0)}+B^{(1)}\,.

(115)

Inserting this back into Eq. (
109b
), we obtain

S_{\text{DQW}}^{\text{asym.}}=S_{\text{DQW}}+B^{(0)}+B^{(1)}\,,

(116)

where
S_{\text{DQW}}
has been defined by Eq. (
36
).

The boundary term
B^{(0)}+B^{(1)}
is irrelevant in the variational problem for determining the equations of motion.
Notice that it is possible to write the boundary term
B^{(0)}+B^{(1)}
as a (lattice)
(1+1)
-divergence as it is usually done in the continuum, although for the current defined in that way to be real we have to consider the Hermitian conjugate part of the action: this is done in a more general framework in Sec.
III
and in Appendix
D
.

Appendix C
Euler-Lagrange equations (from a real action)

Consider the real action of Eq. (
37
), and a field
\psi
that extremalizes it.
Varying the field arbitrarily by
\delta\psi
results in the action

\displaystyle S_{F}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}\left(\psi_{n}+\delta\psi_{n},\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\delta\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}},\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n},\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}\right)

(117a)

\displaystyle=S_{F}+\sum_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}\delta\psi_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}\delta\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}\right)\,,

(117b)

where in the second equation we have only kept terms up to first order since the action is linear in its arguments, and with implicit sums over
\mu
in the second and fourth terms inside the brackets.
Eq. (
117b
) can be rewritten as

\delta S\vcentcolon=S_{F}^{\prime}-S_{F}=\delta S_{1}+\delta S_{2}\,,

(118)

where
\delta S_{1}
is the variation of the fields and
\delta S_{2}
that of the conjugate fields, i.e.

\displaystyle\delta S_{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}\delta\psi_{n}+\delta S_{11}

(119a)

\displaystyle\delta S_{2}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\left(\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}\right)\,,

(119b)

where

\delta S_{11}\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}\delta\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}\,.

(120)

“Splitting time and space” gives

\delta S_{11}=\delta S_{11}^{(0)}+S_{11}^{(1)}\,,

(121)

where

\displaystyle\delta S_{11}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j}\delta\psi_{j+1}

(122a)

\displaystyle\delta S_{11}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p}\delta\psi_{p+1}\,,

(122b)

where we have written down the index only when it is the “working” index, i.e. the index on which we are going to perform modifications.
Using the boundary conditions of Eq. (
105
), we have that

\displaystyle\delta S_{11}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}\delta\psi_{j}

(123a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}\delta\psi_{j}+\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{f}-1}\underbrace{\delta\psi_{j_{f}}}_{=0}+\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{i}-1}\underbrace{\delta\psi_{j_{i}}}_{=0}-\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{i}-1}\underbrace{\delta\psi_{j_{i}}}_{=0}

(123b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}\delta\psi_{j}\,.

(123c)

Similarly, we can show that

\delta S_{11}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=j_{i}}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}}^{p_{f}-1}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p-1}\delta\psi_{p}\,,

(124)

so that in total we obtain

\delta S_{11}=\sum_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\delta\psi_{n}\,,

(125)

with an implicit sum over
\mu
.
Inserting this into Eq. (
119a
) yields

\delta S_{1}=\sum_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)\delta\psi_{n}\,.

(126)

Similarly, we can show that

\delta S_{2}=\sum_{n}\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)\,,

(127)

and so

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)\delta\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{n}\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)\,.

(128)

Recall that
\psi
extremalizes the action, i.e.,
\delta S=0
.
By choosing
\delta\psi_{n}=0
, we must have
\delta S_{2}=0
for any variation
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}
.
Similarly, by choosing
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}=0
, implies that
\delta S_{1}=0
for any variation
\delta\psi^{{\dagger}}
.
Together, these conditions give the Euler-Lagrange equations

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}

\displaystyle=0

(129a)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}

\displaystyle=0.

(129b)

It might be possible to prove that for a real action these equations imply each other (more precisely, by proving that they are the Hermitian conjugates of each other), we leave it as an open problem.

Appendix D
Proof of the lattice Noether’s theorem for internal symmetries

To the title of this appendix, we should actually add “that depend only on a single real parameter
\alpha
”, but as mentioned in Sec.
III
the generalization to an arbitrary number of real parameters exhibits no major difficulty.

Under the transformation
\psi_{n}\longrightarrow\psi^{\prime}_{n}=\psi_{n}+C_{n}\delta\alpha
of Eq. (
41
), the action defined in Eq. (
37
) becomes

\displaystyle S_{F}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}(\psi^{\prime}_{n},\psi^{\prime}_{n+\hat{\mu}},{(\psi_{n}^{\prime})}^{{\dagger}},(\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{\prime})^{{\dagger}})

(130a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}(\psi_{n}+C_{n}\delta\alpha,\ \psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}+C_{n+\hat{\mu}}\delta\alpha,\ \psi_{n}^{{\dagger}}+C_{n}^{{\dagger}}\delta\alpha,\ \psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{{\dagger}}+C_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{{\dagger}}\delta\alpha)

(130b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\mathscr{L}(\psi_{n},\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}},\psi_{n}^{{\dagger}},\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{{\dagger}})+\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}C_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}C_{n+\hat{\mu}}+C^{{\dagger}}_{n}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}^{{\dagger}}}\right|_{n}+C^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{{\dagger}}}\right|_{n}\right)\delta\alpha+O(\delta\alpha^{2})\,,

(130c)

where we implicitly sum over
\mu
in the second and fourth terms of the big bracket.
Hence, we obtain

\delta S\vcentcolon=S_{F}^{\prime}-S_{F}=(Q_{1}+Q_{2})\delta\alpha\,,

(131)

where

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}C_{n}+C_{n}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}\right)

(132a)

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{n}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}C_{n+\hat{\mu}}+C_{n+\hat{\mu}}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n}\right)\,.

(132b)

In going from the first to the second equality in Eqs. (
131
), we only kept, as in previous appendices, the first order in
\delta\alpha
, because the action is again assumed to be linear in its arguments.

We are going to perform a discrete integration by parts on
Q_{2}
.
To this end, let us “split time and space”:

Q_{2}=Q_{2}^{(0)}+Q_{2}^{(1)}\,,

(133)

with temporal and the spatial terms

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j}C_{j+1}+C_{j+1}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j}\right)

(134a)

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p}C_{p+1}+C_{p+1}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p}\right)\,.

(134b)

Unless otherwise mentioned, for brevity and clarity we only write explicitly the coordinate at which we consider an expression whenever any of its factors is shifted, since this is the only coordinate on which “we are going to work”.
Let us now perform a temporal discrete integration by parts on
Q_{2}^{(0)}
:

\displaystyle Q_{2}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+2}^{j_{f}}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}C_{j}+C_{j}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}\right)

(135a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}C_{j}+C_{j}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j-1}\right)+b^{(0)}\,,

(135b)

where we have introduced the following boundary term,

b^{(0)}\vcentcolon=\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left[\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{f}-1}C_{j_{f}}+C_{j_{f}}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{f}-1}\right)-\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{i}}C_{j_{i}+1}+C_{j_{i}+1}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{0}}}\right|_{j_{i}}\right)\right]\,.

(136)

This boundary term can be rewritten as the telescoping sum

b^{(0)}=\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\left(J^{0}_{j}-J^{0}_{j-1}\right)=\sum_{n}\epsilon\left(d_{0}^{L}J^{0}\right)_{\!n}\,,

(137)

where
J^{0}
has been defined in Eq. (
43
).
Similarly, we perform a spatial discrete integration by parts on
Q_{2}^{(1)}
, which gives

Q_{2}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p-1}C_{p}+C_{p}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p-1}\right)+b^{(1)}\,,

(138)

with boundary term

b^{(1)}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\left[\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p_{f}-1}C_{p_{f}}+C_{p_{f}}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p_{f}-1}\right)-\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p_{i}}C_{p_{i}+1}+C_{p_{i}+1}^{{\dagger}}\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{1}}}\right|_{p_{i}}\right)\right]\,.

(139)

Again, this boundary term can be rewritten in terms of
J^{1}
defined in Eq. (
43

b^{(1)}=\sum_{j=j_{i}+1}^{j_{f}-1}\sum_{p=p_{i}+1}^{p_{f}-1}\left(J^{1}_{p}-J^{1}_{p-1}\right)=\sum_{n}\epsilon\left(d_{1}^{L}J^{1}\right)_{\!n}\,.

(140)

Inserting Eqs. (
135b
) and (
138
) into Eq. (
133
), and then the resulting expression into Eq. (
131
), we obtain, after rearranging the first two sums and using the final expressions of the boundary terms, Eqs. (
137
) and (
140
), the following expression,

\delta S=\Bigg(\sum_{n}\Bigg[\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)C_{n}+C_{n}^{{\dagger}}\left(\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n}}\right|_{n}+\left.\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\psi^{{\dagger}}_{n+\hat{\mu}}}\right|_{n-\hat{\mu}}\right)\Bigg]+\sum_{n}\epsilon\left(d_{\mu}^{L}J^{\mu}\right)_{\!n}\Bigg)\delta\alpha\,.

As in Appendix
C

9
9

9

We leave it as an open problem whether for a real action these two Euler-Lagrange expressions are Hermitian conjugates of each other.
.
Thus, finally, we obtain that, on shell,

\delta S=\sum_{n}\epsilon\left(d_{\mu}^{L}J^{\mu}\right)_{\!n}\delta\alpha\,.

(142)

If the considered transformation is a symmetry, i.e., if

Appendix E
Current conservation from the equation of motion

In the main text, we obtained the U(1)-charge Noether-current conservation equation from our lattice Noether’s theorem. Here, we show that this conservation equation can be derived from the EOMs (as in the continuum), either the one-step or the two-step one.
As above, we write only the indices that are shifted from
(j,p)
.

E.1
From the one-step equation of motion

From the (generic) one-step EOM, Eq. (
18
) with
\mathcal{W}
as in Eq. (
20
), we immediately obtain the following four equalities

\displaystyle\psi^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\psi^{{\dagger}}\left(W_{-1}\psi_{p+1}+W_{1}\psi_{p-1}+W_{0}\psi\right)

(143a)

\displaystyle\psi_{j+1}^{{\dagger}}\psi

\displaystyle=\left(\psi_{p+1}^{{\dagger}}W_{-1}^{{\dagger}}+\psi_{p-1}^{{\dagger}}W_{1}^{{\dagger}}+\psi^{{\dagger}}W_{0}^{{\dagger}}\right)\psi

(143b)

\displaystyle\psi_{j-1}^{{\dagger}}\psi

\displaystyle=\left(\psi_{p-1}^{{\dagger}}W_{-1}+\psi_{p+1}^{{\dagger}}W_{1}+\psi^{{\dagger}}W_{0}\right)\psi

(143c)

\displaystyle\psi^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j-1}

\displaystyle=\psi^{{\dagger}}\left(W_{-1}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{p-1}+W_{1}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{p+1}+W_{0}^{{\dagger}}\psi\right)\,.

(143d)

Summing the first two equations, Eqs. (
143b
) and (
143a
), and substracting the last two, Eqs. (
143d
) and (
143c
), we obtain after some simplifications and rearrangements the following current-conservation equation,

d^{L}_{\mu}Q^{\mu}=0\,,

(144)

where the “temporal” and the “spatial” components of the current
Q
are

\displaystyle Q^{0}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(\psi^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j+1}+\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j+1}\psi\right)

(145a)

\displaystyle Q^{1}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(\psi^{{\dagger}}(W_{1}^{{\dagger}}-W_{-1})\psi_{p+1}+\psi^{{\dagger}}_{p+1}(W_{1}-W_{-1}^{{\dagger}})\psi_{p}\right)\,.

(145b)

The temporal component of the current,
Q^{0}
, is actually equal to
J^{0}_{\text{U(1)}}
defined in Eq. (
47
).
Moreover, with the choices of Eqs. (
27b
) and (
30
), we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{\epsilon}\alpha^{1}

\displaystyle=B_{-}\vcentcolon=W_{1}-W_{-1}

(146a)

\displaystyle\mu_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=B_{+}\vcentcolon=W_{1}+W_{-1}\,.

(146b)

This implies

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{\epsilon}}{2}(1+\alpha^{1})=W_{1}^{{\dagger}}

(147a)

\displaystyle W_{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{\epsilon}}{2}(1-\alpha^{1})=W_{-1}^{{\dagger}}\,,

(147b)

so that

\displaystyle W_{1}-W_{-1}^{{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\epsilon}\alpha^{1}

(148a)

\displaystyle W_{1}^{{\dagger}}-W_{-1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\epsilon}\alpha^{1}\,,

(148b)

which, inserted into Eq. (
145b
), yields
Q^{1}=J^{1}_{\text{U(1)}}
, which ends our proof.

E.2
Directly from the two-step equation of motion

Multiplying the two-step EOM, Eq. (
16
), from the left by
2\mathrm{i}\psi^{{\dagger}}
we obtain

\psi^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j+1}=\psi^{{\dagger}}\psi_{j-1}-\psi^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\alpha}^{1}[\psi_{p+1}-\psi_{p-1}]-2\mathrm{i}m\psi^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\alpha}^{0}\psi\,.

Similarly, taking the adjoint of Eq. (

\psi_{j+1}^{{\dagger}}\psi=\psi^{{\dagger}}_{j-1}\psi-[\psi_{p+1}^{{\dagger}}-\psi_{p-1}^{{\dagger}}]\tilde{\alpha}^{1}\psi+2\mathrm{i}m\psi^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\alpha}^{0}\psi\,.

(150)

The sum of these Eqs. (
149
) and (
150
) gives the same current-conservation equation as that obtained from Noether’s theorem, namely, Eq. (
49

Note that the above derivation has a direct parallel with the case of naive fermions.

Appendix F
Continuum limit of the gauge-field action

It is easy to show that

F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=\sum_{l}(-F_{0l}^{2}-F_{l0}^{2})+\sum_{k,l}F_{kl}^{2}\,.

\epsilon
