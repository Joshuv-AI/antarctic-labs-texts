Layer potentials and essentially translation invariant pseudodifferential operators on manifolds with cylindrical ends Thanks: M.K. has been partially supported by the Babeş-Bolyai University Grant AGC 33656/21.07.2022. V.N. has been partially supported by ANR OpART.
Mirela Kohr, Victor Nistor, Wolfgang L. Wendland

Abstract
. Motivated by the study of layer potentials on manifolds with straight conical or cylindrical ends, we introduce and study two classes (or calculi) of pseudodifferential operators defined on manifolds with cylindrical ends: the class of pseudodifferential operators that are “translation invariant at infinity” and the class of “essentially translation invariant operators.” These are “minimal” classes of pseudodifferential operators containing the layer potential operators of interest. Both classes are close to the b b -calculus considered by Melrose and Schulze and to the c c -calculus considered by Melrose and Mazzeo-Melrose. Our calculi, however, are different and, while some of their properties follow from those of the b b - or c c -calculi, many of their properties do not. In particular, we prove that the “essentially translation invariant calculus” is spectrally invariant, a property not enjoyed by the “translation invariant at infinity” calculus or the b b -calculus. For our calculi, we provide easy, intuitive proofs of the usual properties: stability for products and adjoints, mapping and boundedness properties for operators acting between Sobolev spaces, regularity properties, existence of a quantization map, topological properties of our algebras, and the Fredholm property. Since our applications will be to the Stokes operator, we systematically work in the setting of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators. We also show that our calculi behave well with respect to restrictions to (suitable) submanifolds, which is crucial for our applications to layer potential operators.

1.
Introduction

Boundary value problems play a very prominent role in the applications of
mathematics to other areas of science and engineering. The analysis of boundary value
problems is mainly based on
two approaches.
The most commonly
used one relies on energy methods. The second one is based on layer potentials,
which are pseudodifferential operators if the boundary is nice enough.
While less elementary than the energy method approach, the layer potentials
approach to boundary value problems possesses several advantages.

This paper is the second paper in a series of papers in which
we study the
method of layer potentials on manifolds with cylindrical ends.
To this end, we need to
develop two precise calculi of pseudodifferential operators that would contain
our differential operators and their associated layer potential operators, the
“
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus” and the “essentially translation invariant at infinity calculus”.
These two calculi were originally introduced in

[
44
]
, with the same motivation of studying the layer potential
operators on manifolds with cylindrical ends. In this paper, we complete and
significantly extend the results of that paper, providing also an alternative
approach to several results in that papers. We thus provide a more systematical
account of the properties of these two calculi. We also more carefully indicate
the relations between our calculi and the closely related
b
- and
c
-calculi

[
40
,
41
,
42
,
49
,
50
]
and
to the
c
-calculus of Melrose and Mazzeo-Melrose. We also work consistently in the
framework of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators.

1.1.
The framework and some earlier results

A manifold with
straight cylindrical ends
is a Riemannian manifold
({\mathfrak{M}},g)

isometrically diffeomorphic to one of the form

(1.1)

{\mathfrak{M}}\,=\,{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0])\,,

where
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
is a smooth manifold with boundary, the “end”
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0]

is endowed with a product metric, and we identify
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
with
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\{0\}
.
Most of the time
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
will be compact, but, in order to be able to include
T^{*}{\mathfrak{M}}

in our setting, we allow also the case
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
non-compact.
See Definition
2.1
and Remark
2.2
for details.
In our applications to layer potentials,
{\mathfrak{M}}
will be, in fact, the
boundary

of a manifold with boundary and straight cylindrical ends
\Omega
, on which our boundary
value problem will be posed. Manifolds with straight cylindrical ends (for which our calculi were designed)
are important because they can be used to study manifolds
with
straight conical
points via the Kondratiev transform
r=e^{t}
(
r
being the
distance to the conical points and
t
the coordinate on the cylindrical end). Manifolds with
straight conical points are, of course, less general than manifolds with (plain) conical points,
but they appear often enough in applications to justify an explicit study, which yields, in
particular, stronger results than the ones available in the general case.

In view of our applications, we develop two pseudodifferential
calculi on
{\mathfrak{M}}
that are specifically tailored to manifolds
with straight cylindrical ends. The two calculi are

{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\ \subset\ {\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,,

and are both closely related to the
b
-calculus of Melrose
[

[

[

The first calculus is called the “inv”-calculus and consists of pseudodifferential
operators that are translation invariant in a neighborhood of infinity on
{\mathfrak{M}}

second calculus is called the “essentially translation invariant”-calculus and
is a spectrally invariant enlargement of the “inv”-calculus.

The motivation for considering these two calculi is the following. The “inv”-calculus
is the smallest calculus that we could imagine and contains the operators we are interested
in on manifolds with straight cylindrical ends.
Saying that an operator belongs to this calculus is a strong statement, stronger than
saying that it belongs to the
b
-calculus (which is larger than the “inv”-calculus).
In general, it is convenient in applications to use the smallest calculus that does the
job. This justifies considering the “inv”-calculus.
The “inv”-calculus is, however, not spectrally invariant, which then motivated us to consider
the slightly larger calculus of “essentially translation invariant” operators,
which we prove to be spectrally invariant. Again, the resulting algebra is one of the smallest
one that is spectrally invariant and is adapted to the geometry, which again justifies its study.

As we have mentioned above, our calculi are closely related to the

1.2.
Main results.

We first develop and study the general properties of two pseudodifferential calculi
mentioned above on
{\mathfrak{M}}
, the “inv” and the “essentially translation invariant”
calculi (
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
and
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
) using symbols in the classes

S_{1,0}^{m}

[
1
,
26
,
45
,
56
,
57
,
59
]
.
We prove that these calculi are stable under products and under adjoints. We show that
they induce bounded operators between suitable Sobolev spaces. We prove that they enjoy
the usual symbolic properties, which leads to regularity results. We introduce and study
natural topologies on these spaces of pseudodifferential operators. We provide a
description of the distribution kernels of the classical, negative order pseudodifferential
operators in our calculi, which is crucial for our applications to layer potentials.
We also extend the concept of normal operator (which we
call “limit operator” for historical reasons) to our calculi and use it to study
the structure of our algebras. These results on the “inv”-calculus can be found in Section

4
and those about the “essentially translation invariant”-calculus
can be found in Section
5
. In these two sections, we work mostly with
operators whose symbols belong to the Hörmander classes
S_{1,0}^{m}
.

The results of these two sections are important for establishing our three main results
needed in applications: compatiblity with restrictions to submanifolds
(Theorem
7.1
), the spectral invariance (Theorem
7.4
)
and the Fredholm property (Theorem
7.12
). We prove all of the three
main results in the framework of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators, which is
another novelty of our approach. This is an important feature since our main planned
application is to the Stokes operator. These three main results, however, are proved in
the setting of classical pseudodifferential operators. (The general case is significantly
different and including it would have made this paper significantly longer.) These
results are crucial in our applications to the study of layer potentials, in particular,
these results allow us to introduce the layer potential operators associated to an
invertible classical pseudodifferential operator of low order.
The proof that our calculi behave well with respect to restrictions to (suitable)
submanifolds is the reason for our approach to the pseudodifferential calculi via
distribution kernels.

It can be proved (but it is non-trivial) that the
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus is a subset of the
b
-calculus
and that the essentially translation invariant calculus is a subset of the
c
-calculus.
(Checking this is an excellent exercise for understanding the
b
-calculus and the differences
between the
b
- and
c
-calculi). Some of the properties of our calculi would follow then from the
aforementioned inclusions. For our purposes, however, it was more convenient to include direct proofs.
That makes our paper easier to follow by people with a limited knowledge of pseudodifferential operators
and, hopefully, will make pseudodifferential operators more accessible.
Our paper can also serve as an introduction to the
b
- and
c
-calculi. Most importantly, our
approach is useful for studying restrictions to submanifolds (Theorem
7.1
),
which is needed for the definition of the layer potential operators.

1.3.
Contents of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains definitions and
results on manifolds with
straight
cylindrical ends and the corresponding Sobolev
spaces. The manifolds with straight cylindrical ends play a central role in our developments.
In Section
3
, we recall a few basic results on pseudodifferential
operators (the reader familiar with the topic can skip this section at a first

4
, we recall and study the definition and the
properties of the calculus
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
of pseudodifferential operators that are invariant

distribution kernel of the classical operators in this calculus. In Section
5
,
we introduce and study the calculus
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
of essentially translation invariant
(in a neighborhood of infinity) pseudodifferential operators. This is a suitable enlargement
of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
that is stable under inversion of elliptic operators of non-negative order.
This stability result is contained in Theorem
7.4
in Section
7
,
where we also prove the restriction to submanifold property (Theorem
7.1
)
and the Fredholm property for these operators (Theorem
7.12
). A novelty of our

of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators. The Douglis-Nirenberg-ellipticity and related regularity results are discussed in
Section
6
.
We expect our methods to extend to other classes of non-compact manifolds.

2.
Background: manifolds with cylindrical ends and Sobolev spaces

We include in this background section several definitions and results
on manifolds with cylindrical ends and their Sobolev spaces. As explained in the
Introduction, we shall consider only the simplest class of manifolds
with cylindrical ends, the manifolds with “straight cylindrical ends,”
because of its applications and because in this case we can obtain stronger results.
Let us add, nevertheless, that the main difference
between the various classes of manifolds with cylindrical ends is the choice of the
relevant algebra of pseudodifferential operators: choosing a smaller algebra leads to
stronger results on the layer potential operators, but if the algebra is too
small, it will not contain the relevant layer potential operators. In any case,
these algebras will be closely related to the
b
-calculus. There are many further
references on the
b
-calculus, see, for instance

[
16
,
17
,
35
,
36
,
48
]
.
The general case of manifolds with cylindrical ends requires a different
pseudodifferential calculus and will be treated in a subsequent joint work with Cipriana
Anghel and Sergiu Moroianu, building also on the results on the

-calculus in

[
4
]
.

2.1.
General notation: duals and connections

We shall use the following conventions for the dual spaces that we use. First,
V^{\prime}

denotes the dual space of a real or complex topological vector space
V
and, if

T:V\to W
is a linear map, then
T^{\prime}:W^{\prime}\to V^{\prime}

(still endowed with an inner product), instead of the adjoint we have the
transpose

.
When there is no danger of confusion, we use the notation

for the identity operator
of any given space.

Let

be a smooth manifold.
The space of smooth sections of a smooth vector bundle

will be
denoted

and the space of smooth sections that in

support will be denoted

From now on,

will be a Riemannian manifold. We
can use the fixed metric on

to trivialize the density bundle on
{\mathfrak{M}}

and hence identify the dual of the space

with
a space of distributions on
{\mathfrak{M}}
. As usual,

T{\mathfrak{M}}\to{\mathfrak{M}}
is the tangent bundle to
{\mathfrak{M}}
and

is the

{\mathfrak{M}}
(the dual of

). On these bundles, as well as on their tensor
products, we shall consider the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{LC}
.
All vector bundles

considered in this paper will be finite dimensional, smooth, Hermitian,
and will be endowed with metric compatible connections
\nabla^{E}
. (We shall write
simply
\nabla
instead of

A

is a smooth, compact manifold without boundary.

2.2.
Manifolds with cylindrical ends

Let

Unless stated otherwise, we shall endow

A picture of a manifold with straight cylindrical ends.

A manifold with cylindrical ends is thus a non-compact, Riemannian manifold without boundary.
If it has compact ends, it is, moreover, with bounded geometry. (This can be seen, for instance, from

When studying

From now on,

Proof.

We have that

2.4.
Sobolev spaces

In the following,

will be a fixed manifold with straight cylindrical ends

Let us recall now the definition of the Sobolev spaces on
{\mathfrak{M}}

We agree that

\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

For simplicity of the notation, we introduce vector bundles only later.

2.4.1.
Positive order covariant Sobolev spaces

By

{\mathfrak{M}}
associated to the metric
g
on
{\mathfrak{M}}
.
(Note that the metric
g
is fixed everywhere in the paper.)
For
p\in[1,\infty)

(2.2)

\|u\|_{L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }\left(\int_{{\mathfrak{M}}}|u(x)|^{p}\,d\operatorname{vol}(x)\right)^{1/p}\,.

As usual, we define the space:

(2.3)

L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{u:{\mathfrak{M}}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\|u\|_{L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}})}<+\infty\}/\ker(\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}})})\,.

The space
L^{\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
and its norm are defined with the usual modification,
using the essential supremum.
By
(f,g)=(f,g)_{L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})}
we shall denote the scalar (inner) product on

It is linear in the first variable and conjugate linear in the second one.

There are many ways of introducing Sobolev spaces on a manifold. For open subsets of compact
manifolds, the Sobolev spaces can be defined using partitions of unity supported in
coordinate patches (thus reducing to the Euclidean case), and the resulting
norms are equivalent for different choices of the partition of unity. In the non-compact
case, one cannont use any partition of unity. One can still use connections, though
(see, for instance
[
22
,
30
]
).

Definition 2.10
.

Let

. We let

W^{k,p}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{u:{\mathfrak{M}}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\nabla^{j}(u)\in L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}};T^{*\otimes j}{\mathfrak{M}})\,,\,\mbox{ for all }\,0\leq j\leq k\}

be the
order
k
,
L^{p}
–type Sobolev space
on
{\mathfrak{M}}
with the norm

\|u\|_{W^{k,p}({\mathfrak{M}})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }\ell^{p}\mbox{--norm of }\{\|\nabla^{j}(u)\|_{L^{p}({\mathfrak{M}};T^{*\otimes j}{\mathfrak{M}})}\,,\ 0\leq j\leq k\}\,.

We let
H^{k}:=W^{k,2}
, as usual.
Since
{\mathfrak{M}}
is complete (even with bounded geometry) the Sobolev space

is the domain of
(1+\Delta)^{s/2}

(we use the convention

[
3
,
21
,
54

(valid also in the more general setting of manifolds with bounded geometry

H^{-s}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})^{*}\,,

2.4.3.
Alternative definition

Sometimes, the following definition of Sobolev spaces will be useful.
All manifolds
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-R-k,-R-k+2)

hence the spaces

are also canonically

Let

Then an

|||u|||_{m}^{2}\mathrel{\ :=\ }\|u\|_{H^{m}({\mathfrak{M}}_{R})}^{2}\,+\,\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|u\|_{H^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-R-k,-R-k+2))}^{2}\,.

There exists a smooth partition of unity
(\phi_{k}^{2})
,

These equivalences follows, for instance, from
[

If

3.1.
The “main formula” and pseudodifferential operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

Let
\langle x,\xi\rangle:=x_{1}\xi_{1}+x_{2}\xi_{2}+\ldots x_{n}\xi_{n}

for
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\imath:=\sqrt{-1}
, and let

{\hat{u}(\xi)}\,=\,\mathcal{F}u(\xi)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{{-}\imath\langle{y},\xi\rangle}\,{u(y)\,dy}

be the
Fourier transform
of
u.

The following formula

a(x,D)u(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,a(x,\xi){\hat{u}(\xi)}\,d\xi\,,

will be referred to as “the main formula” (of pseudodifferential theory) and, as we
will see, it gives the “local form” of pseudodifferential operators on manifolds.
In this formula,
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a suitable “symbol”
that will depend on the requirements of the problem. (The terms in quotation marks will
be defined below.) Let us see what the main formula becomes in a few particular cases of
symbols
a:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to\mathbb{C}
.

Example 3.1
.

Let
a(x,\xi)=a(x)
be a smooth function independent of
\xi
. Then

a(x,D)u(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,a(x)\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi\,=\,a(x)u(x)\,,

by the
Fourier inversion formula,

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}w)(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}w(\xi)d\xi\,.

Recall that the
convolution
of two function
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is
(when defined)

(3.1)

f*g(x)\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)g(x-y)dy\,.

Example 3.2
.

Let next
a(x,\xi)=a(\xi)
be a function independent of
\xi
,
and integrable in
\xi
. Then

\displaystyle a(D)u(x)\,=\,a(x,D)u(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,a(\xi)\,\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi\,=\,(\mathcal{F}^{-1}a)*u(x)

is the
convolution
operator with
\mathcal{F}^{-1}a
. This remains true if

a\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(that is, if the function
a
defines a tempered distribution
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, in that case “oscillatory integrals” may be useful; see

[
58
]
for a pedagogical introduction and
[
1
]
for a
thorough account). In particular, if

a(x,\xi):=a_{j}(\xi):=\imath\xi_{j},
then

\displaystyle a_{j}(D)u(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,\imath\xi_{j}\,\hat{u}(\xi)\,d\xi\,=\,\frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x_{j}}\,=:\,\partial_{j}u(x)\,,

because the Fourier transform interchanges multiplication by
\imath\xi_{j}

with
\partial_{j}.
Morover, if we denote by
a
also the multiplication operator
with
a
, then
a(D)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}a\mathcal{F}
. Thus, if both
a
and
b:=a^{-1}
are in

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

a(D)b(D)\,=\,(\mathcal{F}^{-1}a\mathcal{F})(\mathcal{F}^{-1}b\mathcal{F})\,=\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}ab\mathcal{F}\,=\,1\,.

In particular, if
a(\xi)=1+|\xi|^{2}
, then
a(D)=1-\Delta
. Let
b(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}
,
then
a(D)^{-1}=b(D)
, which is the convolution operator with a true function.

The next example combines the first two examples.

Example 3.3
.

Combinging the first two examples and using for any multi-index

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
,

|\alpha|:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j},
and

\partial^{\alpha}u\mathrel{\ :=\ }\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots\partial_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}u\,=\,{\mathcal{F}^{-1}}\big({(\imath\xi)^{\alpha}}\hat{u}\big)\,,\quad(\imath\xi)^{\alpha}\mathrel{\ :=\ }\imath^{|\alpha|}\xi_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\xi_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots\xi_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\,,

we obtain
for
a(x,\xi):=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\,a_{\alpha}(x)\,(\imath\xi)^{\alpha},
(a
polynomial in

\xi
):

\displaystyle a(x,D)u(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\underbrace{\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\,a_{\alpha}(x)\,{(\imath\xi)^{\alpha}}}_{a(x,\xi)}\,\hat{u}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\,=\,\sum_{{{|\alpha|\leq m}}}\,a_{\alpha}(x)\,{\partial^{\alpha}}u(x)\,,

a
differential operator
.

Let us specify now our choice of symbols.

Notation 3.4
.

We shall denote an element of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
by
(x,\xi)
, with
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
a:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
,
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open,

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
, and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we let

(1)

p_{\alpha,\beta}^{[q]}(a)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\sup_{(x,\xi)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\frac{\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a(x,\xi)}{(1+|\xi|)^{q-|\beta|}}\right|\quad\mbox{and}

(2)

p_{N}^{[q]}(a):=\max_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq N}p_{\alpha,\beta}^{[q]}(a).

(3)

We then let

S_{unif}^{q}(U\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{a:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\mid\ p_{N}^{[q]}(a)<\infty\mbox{ for all }N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\}\,.

This class of symbols was introduced by Kohn and Nirenberg in
[
29
]
.
See
[
26
]
and
[
56
]
for more historical information and for
other classes of symbols.

Remark 3.5
.

We think of
U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
T^{*}U
. Similar definitions can be given for any
vector bundle
V\to M
over a manifold
M
, replacing
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}
with a suitable
covariant derivative and replacing
\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}
with derivatives with respect to
vertical vector fields that are translation invariant in the fibers to obtan the
spaces
S_{unif}^{m}(V)
. Notice that in this notation we consider
V
as a vector bundle
over
M
, not simply as a manifold, and hence this space should better be denoted

S_{unif}(M;V)
to indicated the additionals structure on which it depends.
Notice also that we consider estimates that are
uniform
in
x
, as in

[
1
,
23
,
26
,
56
,
58
]
. These definitions can be localized on
compact subsets of
U
as in
[
27
]
. Also, a more general class of symbols is

S_{\rho,\delta}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
considered by Hörmander, but we do not need it in
our applications.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold. Recall that a linear map
T:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)^{\prime}
is
called
continuous
if
\langle T\phi,\psi\rangle
is continuous with respect to

\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M),
for any
\psi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M).

It is not difficult to see that, if
a\in S_{unif}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
, then

a(x,D):{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is well-defined and continuous.
(A stronger statement is included below.)
This proposition shows that
a(x,D)
satisfies the hypotheses of the
Schwartz’ kernel theorem, which we recall next.
Let
\langle\ ,\ \rangle:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)\to\mathbb{C}
be the

paring
between distributions and test functions.

Theorem 3.6

(Schwartz’ kernel theorem)
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold.
Let
T:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)^{\prime}
be linear and continuous.
Then there exists a unique
k_{T}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M\times M)^{\prime}
such that

\langle T\phi,\psi\rangle\,=\,\langle k_{T},\psi\boxtimes\phi\rangle\,,

where

\psi\boxtimes\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M^{2})
is defined by

(\psi\boxtimes\phi)(x,y)=\psi(x)\phi(y)\,.

The distribution
k_{T}
is called the
distribution kernel
of
T.

The converse is also true, in the sense that every
K\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M^{2})^{\prime}

corresponds to a continuous linear map
T:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(M)^{\prime}
, namely,
K=k_{T}.

This is the “easy part” of the theorem but will not be necessary to us in what
follows. A natural question that arises then is to
find the kernel
k_{a(x,D)}
of the operator
a(x,D)
.

Remark 3.7
.

If in the
main formula

a(x,D)u(x)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x,\xi\rangle}\,a(x,\xi){\hat{u}(\xi)}\,d\xi

we substitute the Fourier transform
\hat{u}(\xi)\,=\,\mathcal{F}u(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\langle{y},\xi\rangle}\,u(y)\,dy,

we obtain the
second main formula
of pseudodifferential operator theory:

a(x,D)u(x)\,=\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,e^{\imath\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}\,a(x,\xi)\,{u(y)\,dy}\right)d\xi\,.

This second main formula allows us to determine the distribution kernel of
a(x,D)
.

Remark 3.8
.

Assume first that
a\in S_{unif}^{-n-\epsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
,
\epsilon>0
, and let

K(x,y):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\imath\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a(x,\xi)d\xi,

which is a continuous function of
(x,y)
by the continuity of
integrals depending on parameters by bounding with the integrable function
(1+|\xi|)^{-n-\epsilon}
.
The second main formula (see Remark
3.7
) gives

\displaystyle\langle a(x,D)u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\imath\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a(x,\xi)\,u(y)dy{d\xi}\right)\,v(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,K(x,y)v(x)u(y)\,dydx

\displaystyle=\langle K,v\boxtimes u\rangle\,,

using an integration with respect to
\xi.
Therefore

(3.2)

k_{a(x,D)}(x,y)\,=\,K(x,y)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\imath\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a(x,\xi)d\xi\,.

In general, this formula for
k_{a(x,D)}(x,y)

must be interpreted in distribution sense, as the Fourier transform of a temperate
distribution (this can be done using oscillatory integrals, as in
[
1
,
58
]
,
for instance).

We obtain the following.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
a\in S_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2n}).

(1)

The distribution
k_{a(x,D)}
is defined by a smooth function
on
\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid x\neq y\}

(that is, away from the diagonal).

(2)

If
k_{a(x,D)}=0
, then
a=0
.

(3)

Consequently,
k_{a(x,D)}
determines
a
and hence
the map
a(x,D):{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

also determines
a
.

Proof.

The stronger statement that
(x-y)^{\alpha}k_{a(x,D)}(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

for
|\alpha|
large is contained in the proof of Proposition 2.1 in
[
56
]

(this result holds true also for suitable symbols in the Hörmander class

S_{\rho,\delta}^{m}
). The second point is obtained from Equation (
3.2
)
by the Fourier inversion formula.
∎

The following remark explains “what is lost” by looking only at the distribution
kernel on
\{(x,y)\mid x\neq y\}.
It will not be needed for any proof.

Remark 3.10
.

If
a,b\in S_{unif}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
and
k_{a(x,D)}(x,y)=k_{b(x,D)}(x,y)
for

x\neq y
, then
a(x,D)-b(x,D)
has distribution kernel supported on the diagonal

(x,x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and hence it is a differential operator, by Peetre’s theorem.
In particular, the function
k_{a(x,D)}
away from the diagonal determines any operator
of negative order.

Let us now discuss the mapping properties of our operators. Recall that

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\Big\{u\,\Big|\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|\xi|)^{2s}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi<+\infty\,\Big\}\,,\quad s\in\mathbb{R}\,.

In particular, if
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\big\{u\,\big|\ \partial^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ \mbox{ for all }\ |\alpha|\leq{m}\,\big\}\,.

These definitions coincide, of course, with our previous definitions of
Subsection
2.4
. We let

(3.3)

\Psi_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{a(x,D)\mid a\in S_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\}

and (for some fixed

), we let

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\mid x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,\forall\,\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}\},

be the usual
Schwartz space
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the induced norms (the resulting
space does not depend on

, but its semi-norms do).

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
s,m\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Psi_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be as in Equation (

).

(1)

The map
S_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\ni(a,u)\to a(x,D)u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

(2)

If
a\in S_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})

Moreover, for any

, there is

p_{N}^{[m+m^{\prime}-1]}(c)\leq C_{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big[p_{N}^{[m]}(a)p_{N}^{[m^{\prime}-1]}(\partial_{\xi_{j}}b)+p_{N}^{[m-1]}(\partial_{\xi_{j}}a)p_{N}^{[m^{\prime}]}(b)\big]\,.

In particular, if
P\in\Psi_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

, then

.

(3)

P=a(x,D):H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{s-m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is bounded and its norm depends

.

(4)

Similarly, if

a(x,D)\in\Psi_{unif}^{m}(U)
, then
a(x,D)^{*}-\overline{a}(x,D)\in\Psi_{unif}^{m-1}(U)
.
In particular, if
P\in\Psi_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
P^{*}\in\Psi_{unif}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Most of these results are standard. We include only some
references to the less standard material. The point (1) is a particular case
of Theorem 3.6. in
[
1
]
. See also Proposition 4.4 in

3.3.

We can say more if our manifold

Proposition 3.19

Let

(1)

(2)

for some trivial reasons, and we retain the second one as the definition of ellipticity. This
will not be a problem in our paper.) This leads to the following definition.

P\in\Psi_{1,0}^{m}(M)

completely analogous (Definition

4.
Translation invariance in a neighborhood of infinity

Pseudodifferential operators have long played an important role in the study of layer potential
operators
[
27
,
56
]
.
In this paper, we consider mostly two classes of pseudodifferential operators associated to
our manifolds with straight cylindrical ends. These two classes differ only in the regularizing
operators in a sense that will be made clear below. In this section, we introduce
the smallest of these two classes of operators and study their properties.
The operators in this class are “

-calculus and many are similar to some analogous results
that are valid for the

-calculus. We nevertheless include the proofs, for the
convenience of the reader and because our approach is different from the one used for
the

-calculus. We let

denote the dimension of our manifold with cylindrical ends

.

Let

{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}

in place until the end of the paper.

For

We let
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}}):=\cup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

Remark 4.2

Let us notice that, if

The calculus

-calculus.

Our
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus is smaller than the “

-calculus” in the following sense.

Remark 4.3
.

Let us compactify
{\mathfrak{M}}
to

{\mathfrak{M}}=\{r>0\}

[

,

]
and Schulze
[

,

]

(Schulze calls it the “cone-calculus,” see the nice paper by Lauter and

We have already encountered operators in the

The endomorphisms and, more generally, the differential operators in

4.3.
Limit operators

An important concept associated to the operators in the

-calculus is
the “normal operator” (in Melrose’s terminology) or “conormal principal symbol”
(in Schulze’s terminology, who uses the term “cone-calculus” instead of
the

at infinity operators. We shall call these operators “limit at infinity operators,”
(or “limit operators,” for short) since this is the terminology that is used for their
generalizations by many other authors. A few results of
this section follow from the properties of the

simple proof for completeness (and since the proofs are different than the ones for
the
b
-calculus). Recall that we assume that
{\mathfrak{M}}
has compact ends (that is,
that

To simplify the notation, we shall set as in Melrose’s work

{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}\,.

We begin with some simple observations on the supports of our operators
that will help us to define the normal
(or limit) operator associated to an operator that is
translation invariant in a neighborhood of infinity.

Remark 4.13

First, it follows from the definition of

to conclude that

on
{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}

that is

{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}\,=\,\{P\in\Psi^{m}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}\mid\ P\mbox{ properly supported}\}.

This type of operators appear in the following result that will provide the definition
of limit operators. Let
\Phi_{s}:\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\to\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}
be the

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

(1)

(2)

Proof.

For

\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0)\subset{\mathfrak{M}}

Indeed, we have independence of

The resulting operator

We now introduce an important concept without analog in the compact case.

Definition 4.17
.

Let
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

For the proof and for further use, we shall need the following cut-off function

Notation 4.18

We let
\eta:\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]

\eta(x,t)\,=\,\begin{cases}\ 1\ \mbox{ for }\ t\leq-2\\
\ 0\ \mbox{ for }\ t\geq-1\,.\end{cases}

We also regard
\eta
as a function
on
{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0])

on

The cut-off function

Definition 4.19

For

It is known that the map

is well defined from the properties of the
b
-calculus.
We nevertheless prove directly some of its needed properties, for completeness and for
the benefit of the reader.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
T\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}

and
s_{0}(T):=\eta T\eta
(Definition
4.19
).

(1)

s_{0}(T)
is well defined.

(2)

s_{0}(T)\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.

(3)

We have
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}\circ s_{0}=id
, where
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}
is as in Definition
4.17
.

Proof.

(1) Let
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Then
\eta u
has support in
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0)
,
and hence it can be identified with a function in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
, so

T(\eta u)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
is defined. Then
\eta T(\eta u)

in turn has support in
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0)\subset{\mathfrak{M}}
, and hence
can be identified with a function in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Thus
s_{0}(T)u:=\eta T(\eta u)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})
is well defined and we have (1).

(2) Clearly
s_{0}(T)
is an order
m
pseudodifferential operator,
by the general properties of pseudodifferential operators.
Let
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-R))
for some large
R>0
.
Then
\eta\Phi_{s}(u)=\Phi_{s}(u)
, for
s\geq 0
. The same is true for
u

replaced with
T\Phi_{s}(u)
,
s\geq 0
, since
T
is invariant and properly supported (so it
increases the support of
\Phi_{s}(u)
by at most
\varepsilon_{T}
). We thus have

s_{0}(T)\Phi_{s}(u)=\eta T(\eta\Phi_{s}(u))=T(\Phi_{s}(u))=\Phi_{s}T(u)=\Phi_{s}\eta(T(\eta u))=\Phi_{s}s_{0}(T)u\,.

Hence
s_{0}(T)\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, as claimed. To prove (3) the same reasoning gives

\Phi_{-s}s_{0}(T)\Phi_{s}(u)=\Phi_{-s}\eta T(\eta\Phi_{s}(u))=\Phi_{-s}T(\Phi_{s}(u))=T(u)

for
s
large enough. Thus
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(s_{0}(T))=T
.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

Let us replace
{\mathfrak{M}}

{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}

Then the principal symbol yields a map

We use the notation of Remark

4.4.
Further properties of the
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus

We now include some properties of our
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus that are no longer direct
analogues of the classical calculus on compact manifolds, but they still have
counterparts in the
b
-calculus. The mapping properties of this
subsection are a consequence of the corresponding properties for the
b
-calculus,
but the proof presented here are new. Recall the limit operator map

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}:{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}:={\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{R}})^{\mathbb{R}}
of
Definition
4.17
.

Definition 4.23
.

We let
\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
denote the space of pseudodifferential operators on
{\mathfrak{M}}

with compactly supported distribution kernel.

Theorem 4.24
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup\big(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0]\big)
be a
manifold with straight cylindrical ends.

(1)

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}:{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}

is surjective and its kernel consists of
\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
, the set of operators

T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
whose distribution (or Schwartz) kernel is compactly supported.

(2)

We have a direct sum decomposition

\begin{gathered}{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,=\,s_{0}({\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})})+\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})\\
P\to(s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)),P-s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)))\,.\end{gathered}

Proof.

The surjectivity in (1) follows from Lemma
4.20
(3).
The form of its kernel follows from the definition of operators that are translation
invariant in a neighborhood of infinity. Indeed, let
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
with

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)=0
. Let
R_{P}>0
be as in Definition
4.1
, that is

P\Phi_{s}(u)=\Phi_{s}P(u)
for all
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}\big(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-R_{P})\big)

and all
s>0
. By the definition of
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)v=\Phi_{-s}P\Phi_{s}(v)
for
s
large,
we obtain that
Pu=0
if
u
has support in

\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-R_{P})=:{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P}}^{c}:={\mathfrak{M}}\smallsetminus{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P}}
.
This shows that the distribution kernel of
P
is contained in
{\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P}}
.
The same argument applied to
P^{*}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
shows that the distribution kernel
of
P
is contained in
{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P^{*}}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}
. Therefore, the distribution kernel
of
P
is contained in
{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P^{*}}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}_{R_{P}}
, which is a compact set.

(2) follows from (1) and the relation
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}\circ s_{0}=id
of Lemma
4.20
. Indeed,

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P-s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)))\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)-{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)))\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)-{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)\,=\,0\,.

Therefore
P-s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P))\in\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
by (1).
∎

We now discuss a property of the
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus that has no analogue in the classical case of
pseudodifferential operators on a compact manifold or for the
b
-calculus. We shall denote by
t

the cylindrical variable in
(x,t)\in\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0)\subset{\mathfrak{M}}

and with the same letter the corresponding multiplication operator.

Proposition 4.25
.

Let
t\in\mathbb{R}
be the second coordinate function on
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}
and
let
\rho\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
be such that

\rho(x,t)=t
for all
(x,t)\in\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-1]
.

(1)

Let
T\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
. Then

[t,T]\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
.

(2)

If
T\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
,
then
[\rho,s_{0}(T)]=s_{0}([t,T])
.

(3)

Let
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, we have
[\rho,P]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
and

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}([\rho,P])=[t,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)]
.

Proof.

(1) is well known from the
b
-calculus. We include the argument in
[
44
]
.
First, the operator
[t,T]
is pseudodifferential of order
m-1
. It is also
properly supported. It is translation invariant since

\Phi_{-s}[t,T]\Phi_{s}\,=\,[\Phi_{-s}t\Phi_{s},\Phi_{-s}T\Phi_{s}]\,=\,[t-s,T]\,=\,[t,T]\,,

because
s
is a constant. This completes the proof of (1).

The operators
t
,
\rho
, and
\eta
are multiplication operators, and hence
they commute. Moreover, the assumption gives that
\rho\eta=t\eta
.
The relation (2) is thus immediate from

[\rho,s_{0}(T)]\mathrel{\ :=\ }\rho\eta T\eta-\eta T\eta\rho\,=\,\eta[t,T]\eta\,=:\,s_{0}([t,T])\,.

To prove (3), let us write first
P=s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P))+P_{c}
, where
P_{c}\in\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Then

[\rho,P]\,=\,[\rho,s_{0}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P))]+[\rho,P_{c}]\,=\,s_{0}([t,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)])+[\rho,P_{c}]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,,

by (1) and (2) already proved and by Theorem
4.24
(2),
using also the fact that
[\rho,P_{c}]\in\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Finally, let
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
be large enough. Then

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}([\rho,P])u\,=\,\Phi_{-s}([\rho,P])\Phi_{s}u\,=\,[t-s,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)]u\,=\,[t,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)]u\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

We shall need also the following boundedness result. In a certain sense, it
should have been part of Theorem
4.12
, but it is easier to prove now.
Also, we formulate it as a lemma, not as theorem,
since stronger results will be proved later on. Given normed spaces
X
and
Y
, we let

\mathcal{B}(X;Y)
denote the space of bounded, linear operators
T:X\to Y
. We let

\mathcal{B}(X):=\mathcal{B}(X;X)
, as usual.

Lemma 4.26
.

If
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
, then the map
P:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})

extends by continuity to a map
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}}).
Moreover, the map

{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to\mathcal{B}(H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}}),H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}}))

is continuous
for the natural topology on
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
(see Remark
4.11
).

Proof.

Let us write
P=P_{c}+s_{0}(P_{i})\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
where
P_{c}
has compactly supported distribution kernel and
P_{i}:={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P)
,
as in Theorem
4.24
(2).
Then
P_{c}:H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}})
is bounded
by the mapping properties of pseudodifferential operators on compact manifolds.
Let us prove next that
s_{0}(P_{i}):H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}})

is bounded. Since
s_{0}(P_{i})=\eta P_{i}\eta
and
\eta
is bounded on all

H^{s}
spaces, it is enough to prove that
P_{i}:H^{s}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})\to H^{s-m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
is bounded.
Let us consider a
\mathbb{Z}
-translation invariant partition of unity
(\phi_{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, as in
Section
2.4.3
. Then
\phi_{k}P\phi_{j}:H^{s}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})\to H^{s-m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})
is bounded,
by the case of pseudodifferential on compact manifolds. Moreover,
\phi_{k}P\phi_{j}=0

for
|j-k|
large and the operators
\phi_{k}^{2}P\phi_{j}^{2}
and
\phi_{k+\ell}^{2}P\phi_{j+\ell}^{2}

are unitary equivalent for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
k
(equivalently
j
) large enough.
It follows then by standard arguments (a lemma of Cotlar, Knapp, Stein,
Calderon, Vaillancourt, Bony, and Lerner, Lemma 18.6.5 in
[
26
]
)
that
P=\sum_{k,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\phi_{k}^{2}P\phi_{j}^{2}

is bounded. The continuity for our algebras follows also from the compact case by the
same arguments.
∎

We shall need to following “order reduction operators” (a stronger version
will be obtained later).

Corollary 4.27
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
. There exists an elliptic operator
\Lambda_{s}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{s}}({\mathfrak{M}})

such that the induced map
\Lambda_{s}:H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let
a_{s,t}(\xi):=(t^{2}+\|\xi\|^{2})^{s/2}
, where
t>0
is a parameter and

\xi\in T^{*}M
.
We have that
a_{s,t}\in S^{s}(T^{*}{\mathfrak{M}})
and
a_{s,t}a_{-s,t}=1
. Then

\displaystyle q(a_{s,t})q(a_{-s,t})-1\to 0\ \mbox{ for }t\to\infty

in the natural topology on
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}})
given by Remark
4.11

by the standard formula for the product of pseudodifferential operators in local
coordinates, see Theorem
3.11
(2).
See
[
47
]
for some similar results. Hence
q(a_{s,t})q(a_{-s,t})
will
be invertible on each
H^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
, as long as
t
is large enough. We use this for
m=0
and
m=s
.
Let
\Lambda_{s}:=q(a_{s,t})
. Then
\Lambda_{s}:H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
is continuous,
by Lemma
4.26
. The same argument gives that
Q:=q(a_{-s,t})

maps
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
to
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
continuously. Since
\Lambda_{s}Q
is invertible
on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
(for
t
large), it follows that
\Lambda_{s}
is surjective. Similarly (for
t
large),
we may assume that
Q\Lambda_{s}
is invertible on
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
. If follows that
\Lambda_{s}
is
also injective. Thus we can choose
\Lambda_{s}:=q(a_{s,t})
, for
t
large.
∎

We shall use the function
\rho
of Proposition
4.25
,
thus

\rho\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,,\ \rho<0\mbox{ everywhere, and }\ \rho(x,t)\,=\,t\ \mbox{ for }\ t\leq-1\,.

Corollary 4.28
.

Let
\rho
be as above,
and
m,s\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\rho^{k}H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})

be the space of those functions
u
such that
\rho^{-k}u\in H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
On
\rho^{k}H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
we consider the induced topology. Then every
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

defines a continuous map

P:\rho^{k}H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to\rho^{k}H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

Proof.

The result is known for
k=0
, by Lemma
4.26
. Let us
prove it by induction on
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Let us assume the result is true for some

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and prove it for
k+1
. We know that
[\rho,P]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m-1}}({\mathfrak{M}})
. Then

\displaystyle\|\rho^{-k}P(\rho^{k}u)\|_{H^{s-m}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho^{-k+1}P(\rho^{k-1}u)\|_{H^{s-m}}+\|\rho^{-1}\rho^{-k+1}[P,\rho](\rho^{k-1}u)\|_{H^{s-m}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u\|_{H^{s}}+C\|\rho^{-k+1}[P,\rho](\rho^{k-1}u)\|_{H^{s-m}}

\displaystyle\,=\,C\|u\|_{H^{s-m}}\,,

where
C>0
is a generic constant (different at each occurrence)
and in the last two steps we have used the induction properties
and the fact that
\rho^{-1}\in W^{\infty,\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
∎

We stress that the weight used in the last lemma is not the one typically used
in the
b
-calculus (where the weights
e^{\lambda\rho}
are used
instead).
We now spell out some simple consequences of the results in this subsection, for later use.
First, by replacing
{\mathfrak{M}}
with
{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}
in Lemma
4.26
, we obtain

Corollary 4.29
.

Let
P\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}:={\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then

P:H^{s}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\to H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})

is well-defined and continuous for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, the map
{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}\to\mathcal{B}(H^{s}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R});H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}))

is continuous.

By introducing an extra factor, we see that the action on the first
component yields a continuous action on the product:

Corollary 4.30
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold.
Then
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}})
acts continuously on the product

L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}}\times M)
.

Proof.

We know that
P
acts continuously on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
The corollary then follows from the fact that
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}}\times M)\simeq L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})\overline{\otimes}L^{2}(M)
, where
\overline{\otimes}

is the Hilbert space tensor product and the action of
P
on the
tensor product is
P\overline{\otimes}1
with respect to this identification.
∎

We also have the following characterization of Sobolev spaces.

Corollary 4.31
.

Let
u\in H^{t}({\mathfrak{M}})
, for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We have
u\in H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
if, and only if,
Pu\in L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
for
all
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{s}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.

Proof.

If
u\in H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
, then
Pu\in L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
for
all
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{s}}({\mathfrak{M}})
by Lemma
4.26
. Let
P

be as in Corollary
4.27
and
Q
as in its proof.
By the same proof, we may assume
QP
to be an isomorphism of
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Let us assume
Pu\in L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
. Then
QPu\in H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})

and hence
u\in H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
, because we have chosen
P
and
Q

such that
QP
is an isomorphism of
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
and
u=(QP)^{-1}QPu
.
∎

An important further property of the
\operatorname{inv}
-calculus is the characterization of the
Fredholm operators in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
. Of course, this can be obtained from the
corresponding result in the (bigger)
b
-calculus. In this paper, however, we shall
obtain this result in the (also bigger) class of
“essentially translation invariant in a neighborhood of infinity operators,” which we
discuss in the next section (see Theorem
7.12
).

5.
Essentially translation invariant operators

The algebra
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
is
not stable under inversion
in the sense that
if
T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
is an invertible operator on the space
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
, then its
inverse does not necessarily belong to
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
. As in Mitrea and Nistor

[
44
, Section 2.1]
, we now introduce a suitable enlargement

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
that will be stable under
the inversion of elliptic operators of non-negative order. The operators in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})

are called
essentially translation invariant in a neighborhood of infinity

(or
essentially translation invariant at infinity
or, simply,

essentially translation invariant
; they were called
almost invariant
in

[
44
]
).

Recall that
{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0])
is a manifold
with
straight, compact cylindrical ends.

5.1.
Definition of essentially translation invariant operators

Let us first look at the distribution kernels of the operators in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.
Recall the discussion in Remark
3.24
.
Recall that a continuous linear map
P:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})^{\prime}

is in
\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
if, and only if, its distribution kernel
k_{P}
is smooth.
In particular,

Remark 5.1
.

Recall that
\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
denotes the space of pseudodifferential operators on

{\mathfrak{M}}
with compactly supported distribution kernel.
We then have
P\in\Psi_{comp}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
if, and only if,
k_{P}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}})
.

Recall that
{\mathfrak{L}}
denotes a closed Riemannian manifold.
Also, similarly to Proposition
4.5
, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{L}}
be a closed Riemannian manifold, as usual.
A continuous, linear operator
T:{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\prime}
is in

\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
if, and only if, there exists

C_{T}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})

such that

k_{T}(x,t,y,s)\,=\,C_{T}(x,y,t-s)\in\mathbb{C}\,.

The kernel
C_{T}
is called the
convolution kernel
of
T
.
We have
T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
if, and only if,

C_{T}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})
.

The main point of the last proposition is that the
convolution
kernel
C_{T}

of
T
is compactly supported, unlike its
distribution
kernel
k_{T}
.
Explicitly, we have

(5.1)

Tu(x,t)\,=\,\int_{{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}}C_{T}(x,y,t-s)u(y,s)\,d\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathfrak{L}}}(y)ds\,.

One of the main points of the upcoming definition of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
is that
this formula (Equation
5.1
)
makes sense also for
Schwartz functions,
whose standard definition
we recall next using a function
\rho
as in Proposition
4.25
:

Definition 5.3
.

Let
0>\rho\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
\rho(x,t)=t
for
t\leq-1
.

(1)

We let
\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}})
be the space of functions

u:{\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\int_{{\mathfrak{M}}^{2}}|\rho(x)^{i}\rho(y)^{j}\nabla_{x}^{k}\nabla_{y}^{l}u(x,y)|^{2}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathfrak{M}}^{2}}(x,y)<\infty\,.

(2)

Similarly, let
\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})
be the space of functions

u:{\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\int_{{\mathfrak{L}}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}}|t^{i}\partial_{t}^{j}\nabla_{x}^{k}\nabla_{y}^{l}u(x,y,t)|^{2}\,d\operatorname{vol}_{{\mathfrak{L}}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}}(x,y,t)<\infty\,.

In these two formulas,
i,j,k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
are arbitrary.

Remark 5.4
.

Of course, to obtain the Schwartz spaces of Definition
5.3
, we could have
used the
L^{\infty}
-norm (or any other
L^{p}
-norm,
1\leq p\leq\infty
) instead of
the
L^{2}
-norm appearing in the definition. In particular, we have

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}})\,=\,\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\rho(x)^{-k}\rho(y)^{-k}W^{\infty,p}({\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}})\ \mbox{ and}\\
\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\,=\,\bigcap_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\rho(t)^{-k}\,W^{\infty,p}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\,.\end{gathered}

Definition 5.5
.

Let
{\mathfrak{L}}
be a closed Riemannian manifold.

(1)

We let

{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\subset\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}

be the set of
linear operators
T\in\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}

whose convolution kernel satisfies
C_{T}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})

(see Proposition
5.2
and Equation
5.1
).

(2)

Then we let

{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{L}})}+{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\subset\Psi^{m}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
(see
4.3
).

Remark 5.6
.

Definition
5.5
gives right away that

{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}\subset{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\,.

Indeed, the first space consists of operators with
compactly
supported convolution
kernels, whereas the second one consists of operators with
Schwartz
convolution kernels.

Recall the cut-off function
\eta:{\mathfrak{M}}\to[0,1]
of
4.18

(defined by
\eta
smooth with support in
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-1)

and
\eta=1
on
\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,-2]
). Also, recall
that, for any
T\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}:={\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

s_{0}(T):=\eta T\eta\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
see Lemma
4.20
and its proof. Recall the following definition from
[
44
]
.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be a manifold with straight
cylindrical ends
{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0])
.

(1)

We let
\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
be the set of pseudodifferential
operators in
\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
with distribution kernel in

\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}})
.

(2)

Let
{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
be as in
Definition
5.5
.
We let

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }s_{0}({\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})})+\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

(3)

Finally, we define the space of essentially translation invariant operators
in a neighborhood of infinity of order
m
as

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})+{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

In particular,
{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}=:{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
.

Remark 5.8
.

Recall that
\Psi_{comp}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}})
denotes the space of pseudodifferential operators on
{\mathfrak{M}}

with compactly supported distribution kernel. The last two definitions provide the following simple
inclusions. First,

\Psi_{comp}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\subset\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,,

because the space of Schwartz sections contains the space of compactly supported
sections. Let
s_{0}
be as in the last definition (it was first introduced in
Definition
4.19
). Using also the inclusion of Remark
5.6
,
we obtain

{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,=\,s_{0}({\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})})+\Psi_{comp}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\\
\subset s_{0}({\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})})+\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,=:\,{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

5.2.
Properties of essentially translation invariant operators

We now proceed to prove some of the basic properties of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

(many of which were implicit in
[
44
]
; their proof in that paper
were based on suitable norms on
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
). Here we propose a
more direct approach, which is, however, more computational. The main techniques
used in the proof are summarized in the following remark. Alternatively, one
can prove some of these results using the
c
-calculus of Melrose, but we leave
that for another paper.

Remark 5.9
.

Most of the proofs of the properties of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})

are
standard
in the sense that they use one of the following:

(i)

the definitions,

(ii)

Fubini’s Theorem (including derivation under the integral sign),

(iii)

the fact that the function
\rho^{-1}
of Definition
5.3

is square integrable on
{\mathfrak{M}}
, and

(iv)

the fact that one can use the
L^{\infty}
-norm instead of the
L^{2}
-norm
when defining our spaces (Definition
5.3
again).

These standard arguments will be omited. In addition to the standard
arguments, we use some arguments (also rather common) on the kernels of
operators. Namely, let
P
be a properly supported pseudodifferential
operator and
Q
be a regularizing operator, both on
{\mathfrak{M}}
. Then

k_{PQ}(x,y)\,=\,P_{x}k_{Q}(x,y)\,,

where
P_{x}
is “
P
acting on the
x
-variable,” more precisely,

P_{x}u(x,y)
is
(Pu_{y})(x)
, with
u_{y}(x)=u(x,y)
. Also,

k_{Q^{*}}(x,y)=k_{Q}(y,x)^{*}
. In particular, if
P
is a
multiplication operator by the function
P(x)
, then
k_{PQ}(x,y)=P(x)k_{Q}(x,y)
and, similarly,
k_{QP}(x,y)=k_{Q}(x,y)P(y)
.

Lemma 5.10
.

The space
\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
is a complex algebra stable under adjoints,
acting continuously on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
and with
\eta\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\subset\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
.

Proof.

The proof is a simple calculation based on the standard arguments outlined in
Remark
5.9
. The continuous action on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
follows from the
Riesz Lemma applied to the kernel of the operator.
∎

We next establish some properties of

{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\simeq\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{L}}\times{\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\subset\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}\,,

with the isomorphism defined by the convolution kernel, see Definition
5.5
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
{\mathfrak{L}}
is a closed manifold, as usual, and let
{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}
be as above.

(1)

{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}
is a complex algebra stable
under adjoints acting continuously on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})
.

(2)

\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}){\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\subset\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})

(3)

[\eta^{k},{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}]\subset\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(4)

Let
P,Q\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}
.
Then
s_{0}(P)s_{0}(Q)-s_{0}(PQ)\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

(1) is again a simple calculation based on standard arguments (see Remark

5.9
. The continuity follows from the Riesz lemma applied to the
convolution kernel of the operator.
For (2), we use the explicit form of the kernel
of the composition (see Remark
5.9
).
For (3), it is enough
to prove the statement for
k=1
by the properties of the commutator.
Then we notice that the kernel of the commutator
[\eta,P]
is

k_{[\eta,P]}(z,z^{\prime})\,=\,(\eta(z)-\eta(z^{\prime}))k_{P}(z,z^{\prime})\,

so it is enough to look at
z=(x,t)
and
z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})

with
t\leq-1
and
t^{\prime}\geq-2
and at its symmetric
t\geq-2
and
t^{\prime}\leq-1

(with the points of
{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
being included in the part with
t\geq-2
).
On these regions, we bound
|\rho(x)^{i}\rho(y)^{j}(\partial_{t}^{k}\eta(x)-\partial_{t}^{l}\eta(y))|
and
with
(1+|t-t^{\prime}|)^{i+j}
, which, in turn, is absorbed in
k_{P}
, which is
a function of rapid decay in
|t-t^{\prime}|
(since it is a convolution kernel
with kernel in
\mathcal{S}
). For point (4), we first notice that (2) implies also that

(5.2)

\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R}){\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}})}\subset\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{L}}\times\mathbb{R})\,.

The rest follows from the other points.
∎

We now specialize to
{\mathfrak{L}}=\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
to obtain some properties of

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.

Corollary 5.12
.

We have

(1)

If
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
and
R\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
then

s_{0}(P)R\,,\ Rs_{0}(P)\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

(2)

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}}):=s_{0}({\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}})+\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
is a an algebra stable under adjoints
(a
*
-algebra).

Proof.

(1) is yet again a simple calculation based on standard arguments and on the
explicit form of the distribution kernels, see Remark
5.9
.
(It also follows from Lemma
5.11
(2) or
from Equation (
5.2
).)
For (2), we use the definition of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, Lemma
5.11
(4)
and the point (1) just proved.
∎

We need one more lemma before stating the main properties of the
essentially invariant calculus
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
of Definition
5.7
.

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
. We have

(1)

If
R\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
and

P\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}\simeq{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R})^{\mathbb{R}}
, then

PR\,,\ RP\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}\,.

(2)

If
R\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
and

P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
then

PR\,,\ RP\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

(3)

If
R\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
and
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
,
then

PR\,,\ RP\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\,.

(4)

Any operator in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
maps
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
to

H^{s^{\prime}}({\mathfrak{M}})
continuously for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

(1) and (2) are similar and are again simple calculations based on standard arguments
(see Remark
5.9
), but using also the explicit form of the convolution
kernels and Corollaries
4.28
and
4.30

and Remark
5.4
. The point (3) is an immediate consequence of
the definition of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, of Lemma
5.11
(4),
and of points (1) and (2) just proved.

For
s=s^{\prime}=0
, the statement (4) follows from Definition
5.7

and the continuous actions in
5.10
(1) and
5.11
(1).
For the other values of

, we combine the case
s=s^{\prime}=0
with the statement
(3) and the fact that
T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
maps
H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})
to

H^{s^{\prime}}({\mathfrak{M}})
continuously for some

if, and only if,

Q_{s^{\prime}}TQ_{-s}
is continuous on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}})
for all
Q_{-s}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-s}}({\mathfrak{M}})

and
Q_{s^{\prime}}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{s^{\prime}}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, by Corollary
4.27
.
∎

We are ready now to prove the main properties of the calculus

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}}))+{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})

of Definition
5.7
.

Theorem 5.14
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(-\infty,0]
be a manifold with
straight cylindrical ends, as before.
Let
m,m^{\prime},s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}}){\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m^{\prime}}}({\mathfrak{M}})\subset{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m+m^{\prime}}}({\mathfrak{M}})

and
{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})^{*}\subset{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
and the principal symbol is

*
-multiplicative:

\sigma_{m+m^{\prime}}(PQ)\,=\,\sigma_{m}(P)\sigma_{m^{\prime}}(Q)\ \mbox{ and }\ \sigma_{m}(P^{*})\,=\,\sigma_{m}(P)^{*}\,.

(2)

If
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
, the
P:H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}})\to H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}})
is well defined and continuous and the induced map

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to\mathcal{B}(H^{s}({\mathfrak{M}});H^{s-m}({\mathfrak{M}}))

is continuous.

(3)

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})\subset\Psi^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}})
and hence,

\sigma_{m}:{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})/{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m-1}}({\mathfrak{M}})\to S_{inv}^{m}(T^{*}{\mathfrak{M}})/S_{inv}^{m-1}(T^{*}{\mathfrak{M}})

induces an isomorphism.

(4)

An operator
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
is elliptic if, and only if, there exists

Q\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{-m}}({\mathfrak{M}})
such that
PQ-1,QP-1\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}})
.

Proof.

The first point follows from Theorem
4.12
(1),
Corollary
5.12
(2), and Lemma

An operator

6.
Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators

In view of our planned applications to the Stokes operator, we will work
with Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators, which are recalled in the first subsection
of this section.
The second subsection contains some first applications of the results of
the previous subsections to regularity and self-adjointness of our operators.

6.1.
Definition of Douglis-Nirenberg ellipticity

We recall now the definition of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators in a
more general setting than that of manifolds with cylindrical ends.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
M
be a smooth manifold,
E_{i}\to M
,
F_{j}\to M

be vector bundles,
E:=E_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus E_{k}
,
F:=F_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus F_{k}
,
and
s_{i},t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k
. Some of the bundles
E_{i}
or
F_{j}

may be zero. We let

\Psi^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}(M;E,F)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{T=[T_{ij}]\mid\,T_{ij}\in\Psi^{s_{i}+t_{j}}(M;E_{j},F_{i}),\ i,j=1,\ldots,k\}\,.

An operator
T=[T_{ij}]\in\Psi^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}(M;E,F)
is said to be of

Douglis-Nirenberg-order
\leq[\mathbf{s+t}]
.

Many of the usual definitions extend to the Douglis-Nirenberg-framework in a straightforward way.

Definition 6.2
.

We use the notation of Definition
6.1
. Let
T=[T_{ij}]\in\Psi^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}(M;E,F)
.

(1)

The
principal symbol
of
T
is the matrix

{\operatorname{Symb}}(T)\mathrel{\ :=\ }[\sigma_{s_{i}+t_{j}}(T_{ij})]\,.

(2)

The operator
T
is said to be
Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic
if its principal symbol matrix

{\operatorname{Symb}}(T)
is invertible on
S^{*}M
.

(3)

If
M
is Riemannian and
E_{i}
are Hermitian, we let

H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}(M;E)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j=1}^{k}H^{m+t_{j}}(M;E_{j})\,.

Analogously, we let
H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}(M;F)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\displaystyle\oplus_{i=1}^{k}H^{m-s_{i}}(M;F_{i})
.

The most prominent example of an Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operator is the Stokes operator.
See also
[
58
, p. 334]
in the case of compact Riemannian manifolds.

Definition 6.3
.

Let us assume in the above definition that
M={\mathfrak{M}}
is a manifold with
straight cylindrical ends and that
E_{i},F_{j}\to{\mathfrak{M}}
are compatible (with the
straight cylindrical ends structure) Hermitian vector bundles. We let

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\cap\Psi^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\\
\,=\,\{T=[T_{ij}]\mid\,T_{ij}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{s_{i}+t_{j}}}({\mathfrak{M}};E_{j},F_{i}),\ i,j=1,\ldots,k\}\,.

We record the following simple facts for further use.

Remark 6.4
.

We keep the notation of Definiton
6.3
.

(1)

The mapping properties of operators in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}
(Theorem
5.14
(3))
show that any
T=[T_{ij}]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
induces a continuous map

T\,=\,[T_{ij}]:H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\mathrel{\ :=\ }\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j=1}^{k}H^{m+t_{j}}({\mathfrak{M}};E_{j})\\
\to\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}H^{m-s_{i}}({\mathfrak{M}};F_{i})\,=:\,H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};F)\,.

(2)

Let us assume
P=[P_{ij}]
with
P_{ij}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{s_{i}+t_{j}}}({\mathfrak{M}};E_{j},F_{i})
and

Q=[Q_{\ell i}]
with
Q_{\ell i}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{r_{\ell}-s_{i}}}({\mathfrak{M}};F_{i},G_{\ell})
,
then
QP\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\textbf{r + t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,G)
, that is
QP=[R_{\ell j}]
with

R_{\ell j}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{r_{\ell}+t_{j}}}({\mathfrak{M}};E_{j},G_{\ell})

(3)

If
P
and
Q
are as in (2), then
the principal symbols map
{\operatorname{Symb}}
is
*
-multiplicative (in the usual sense that

{\operatorname{Symb}}(QP)={\operatorname{Symb}}(Q){\operatorname{Symb}}(P)
and
{\operatorname{Symb}}(P^{*})={\operatorname{Symb}}(P)^{*}
).

(4)

The principal symbol
{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)=[\sigma_{s_{i}+t_{j}}(P_{ij})]
has components that are
homogeneous of different degrees:
\sigma(P_{ij})(\lambda\xi)=\lambda^{s_{i}+t_{j}}\sigma(P_{ij})(\xi)
.
Let
\lambda^{[\mathbf{s}]}
be the diagonal matrix with entries
\lambda^{s_{i}}
and

\lambda^{[\mathbf{t}]}
be the diagonal matrix with entries
\lambda^{t_{j}}
. Then, for
\lambda>0
,
we have

{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)(\lambda\xi)\,=\,[\sigma(P_{ij})(\lambda\xi)]\,=\,\lambda^{[\mathbf{s}]}[\sigma(P_{ij})(\xi)]\lambda^{[\mathbf{t}]}\,.

It follows that
{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)(\lambda\xi)
is invertible if, and only if,

{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)(\xi)
is invertible (for
\lambda>0
). Moreover, if
P
is Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic
(i.e.
{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)(\xi)
is invertible for all
\|\xi\|=1
, Definition
6.1
),
then
{\operatorname{Symb}}(P)^{-1}
is the symbol of an Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operator of order
[\textbf{-t - s}]
.

(5)

Similarly, the normal (or limit) operator map
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}
yields a
*
-multiplicative map

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}:{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\to{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};E,F)}\mathrel{\ :=\ }{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R};E,F)^{\mathbb{R}}

that is compatible with the involutions (i.e. adjoints).

(6)

Moreover, the Fourier transform yields
*
-multiplicative maps

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\ni[T_{ij}]\to[\widehat{T}_{ij}(\tau)]\in\Psi^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};E,F)\,,

by Theorem
5.20
(see also Definition
5.19
).

This remark will be useful when proving some of our main general results in
Section
7
. As a rule, we will try to formulate our subsequent
results in the framework of Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators.

6.2.
Consequences of regularity

We now formulate some regularity results and some of their consequences. These results
are very well known in the classical case of closed (i.e. boundaryless, compact) manifolds.
In this section, we formulate them for manifolds with straight cylindrical ends
and essentially translation invariant operators.
These results, while classical in the spirit, are valid also in more general settings
(see, for instance,
[
19
]
). However, a novel feature here is that we formulate
our results for Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operators. We thus keep the notation of the previous subsection,
in particular, that of the Definition
6.1
, so
s_{i},t_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k
,

E:=E_{1}\oplus E_{2}\oplus\ldots E_{k}
, and
F:=F_{1}\oplus E_{2}\oplus\ldots F_{k}
are given
and fixed throughout this subsection.

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
and
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
be Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic.
Let
u\in H^{m^{\prime}-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
be such that
Pu\in H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
.
Then
u\in H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.

Proof.

The ellipticity hypothesis ensures the existence of

Q\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[-t-s]}}({\mathfrak{M}};F,E)
such that
{\operatorname{Symb}}(Q)={\operatorname{Symb}}(P)^{-1}
on

T^{*}M\smallsetminus 0
. Since
{\operatorname{Symb}}
is multiplicative, we have

R:=1-QP\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-1}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
. Let
N\geq\max\{m+s_{i}+t_{j}-m^{\prime},1\}
.
By replacing
Q
with
(1+R+R^{2}+\ldots+R^{N-1})Q
,
we may assume
R\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{-N}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
. Then the hypothesis
u\in H^{m^{\prime}-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
implies

Ru\in H^{m^{\prime}+N-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\subset H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
, by Theorem
5.14
(2)
applied to
R
(see also Remark
6.4
(1)). The same theorem applied to

Q
(which has Douglis-Nirenberg-order
[\textbf{-t-s}]
) gives
QPu\in H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
. We obtain

u\,=\,QPu+Ru\in H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

The regularity property (Proposition
6.5
) also gives the following result.

Corollary 6.6
.

Let us assume that
s_{i},t_{j}\geq 0
,
i,j=1,\ldots,k
,
and that
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
is Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic. We regard
P

as an
unbounded
operator on
H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
with domain

H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
. Then
P
is closed.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{n}\in H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
and
\xi,\zeta\in H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
be such that

\xi_{n}\to\xi
and
P\xi_{n}\to\zeta
, both convergences being in
H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.
Then, since
P
is continuous in distribution sense, we have
P\xi_{n}\to P\xi
in distribution
sense, and hence
P\xi=\zeta
in distribution sense. Proposition
6.5

for
u=\xi
and
m^{\prime}=m
gives that

\xi\in H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
. Hence the graph of
P
is closed.
∎

Regularity is also used in the proof of the next result, which states that if

T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
,
m>0
, is an elliptic symmetric operator on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
,
then it is essentially self-adjoint, that is, its closure is a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.
The proof is standard, see
[
44
, Corollary 2.2]
or

[
19
, Lemma 5.1]
for instance (it is crucial here that a
manifold with straight cylindrical ends is complete). Here we formulate this result
in slightly greater generality.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be a manifold with straight cylindrical ends and
E_{i}\to{\mathfrak{M}}
be
compatible Hermitian vector bundles,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Assume all
t_{j}=0
and
s_{i}\geq 0
.
Let
T=[T_{ij}]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\textbf{s+0}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
,
m\geq 0
, be an Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic operator.
If

is
symmetric
(
i.e.
(Tf,g)=(f,Tg)
, for all

)
, then the closure
of

is self-adjoint on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.

We shall use the notation of Definition

7.1.
Restriction to submanifolds and layer potentials

Let us assume that
N\subset{\mathfrak{M}}={\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\cup(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(0,\infty])

is a smooth submanifold such that there exists
R>0
and a submanifold

N_{\infty}\subset\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}
such that

N\cap(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times(0,R])\,=\,N_{\infty}\times(-\infty,R]\,.

Let us assume that
N
has codimension
q
(and hence its dimension is
n-q
).
If

is a pseudodifferential operator on

, we shall let

(7.2)

k_{P}||_{N}\mathrel{\ :=\ }k_{P}|_{N\times N}\,.

The following theorem will be crucial in our applications to the method of layer
potentials.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
N\subset{\mathfrak{M}}
be as in Equation (
7.1
). We assume that

s_{i}+t_{j}<-q
for all
i
and
j
, where, we recall

is the
codimension of
N
in
{\mathfrak{M}}
. Let
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
be a
classical

pseudodifferential operator. Then the
restriction
k_{P}||_{N}
of
k_{P}
to
N\times N\subset{\mathfrak{M}}\times{\mathfrak{M}}

defines a distribution on
N\times N
, which, in turn, defines a classical
pseudodifferential operator
P|_{N}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}+q]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F).

The limit operators satisfy

\widetilde{P|_{N}}=\widetilde{P}|_{N_{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}}\,.

The resulting operator
P|_{N}
will be called
the restriction of
P
to
N
.

Proof.

First of all, the result is true trivally if
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)

by Proposition
4.5
, since the asymptotic is the same and

t^{-n-m+j}=t^{-(n-q)-(m+q)+j}
. Then, if
P\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}(M;E,F)
,
the result follows again trivially from the definition. Similarly, the result is true
if
{\mathfrak{M}}=\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R}
,

N=N_{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}
, and
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{-\infty}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0})}
again by
the definitions (see, for instance, Definition
5.5
). For
P\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
, the result follows by linearity from the
the definition of
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
. Finally, the limit operator
is also obtained by restriction, so the result follows by the fact that restrictions
commute.
∎

This theorem together with the spectral invariance result of the following section
will be used to define our pseudodifferential operators appearing
in the method of layer potentials which, we recall,
are defined on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of our domain with straight cylindrical ends
on which our boundary value problem is posed. This works directly for the single layer
potential operator
S
. These results will be included in Subsection
7.3
.

Before applying the restriction theorem, we will need however to show that our Partial Differential
Operator
P
is invertible on the whole manifold (without boundary). This will be done
using Fredholm conditions and the “Mitrea-Taylor trick”
[
31
]
.
Once
P
was shown to be invertible, we will conclude that
P^{-1}
is also a pseudodifferential
operator using spectral invariance. Then we will apply the restriction theorem to
P^{-1}

as explained.

7.2.
Spectral invariance

We begin with an extension of a result in
[
44
]
which states that

{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}
is spectral invariant (i.e. it is stable under inversion, in a suitable sense).
See
[
2
,
5
,
11
,
12
,
18
,
38
,
47
]
for further results on spectral invariance. Most importantly for us, see the
paper by Mazzeo and Melrose
[
40
]
for the spectral invariance of the

c
-calculus. We begin with some preliminary results and definitions.

Recall that
\mathcal{B}(X)
denotes the algebra of bounded, linear operators on a some normed space

X
. Also, recall that a
C^{*}
-algebra is a Banach algebra with an involution
*
that is isometrically

*
-isomorphic to a closed, self-adjoint subalgebra of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, for some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
For any algebra
A
, we let
A+\mathbb{C}=A
if
A
has a unit
and
A+\mathbb{C}=A\oplus\mathbb{C}1
(algebra with an adjoint unit) if
A
does not have a unit.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
A
be a
C^{*}
-algebra and
j:B\to A
a
*
-morphism.
We say that
j
is spectrum preserving
if it has the following property:

“If
b\in B
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
are such that
j(b)-\lambda
is
invertible in
A+\mathbb{C}
, then
b-\lambda
is invertible in
B+\mathbb{C}
.”

If this is the case, we shall also say that
B
is
spectrally invariant
in
A
(or, simply, that it
has the spectral invariance property
).

In applications, given
B
, the
C^{*}
-algebra
A
is usually clear from
the context and, in any case, we can always enlarge
A
. For this reason, we shall usually
say that
B
“has the spectral invariance property” instead of saying that “it is
spectrally invariant in (some algebra)
A
.” Also, in our applications,

j
will be usually an inclusion (i.e. injective). We shall use the following result from

[
34
]
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let us assume
J\subset B
is a self-adjoint ideal. Let
j:B\to A
be
a
*
-morphism to a
C^{*}
-algebra
A
. If

j:J\to\overline{j(J)}
and
j:B/J\to A/\overline{j(J)}

are spectrum preserving, then
j:B\to A
is spectrum preserving.

We can now prove the second of the aforementioned crucial results.

We use the notation of Definition

7.4.
Fredholm conditions

The next theorem (the third of aforementioned three crucial results) extends the Fredholm conditions
on closed manifolds to our setting. It is due to Kondratiev
[
32
]
(in the setting of
differential operators) and to Melrose and Mendoza
[
43
]
and Schulze
[
50
]

in general for pseudodifferential operators in the
b
-calculus. Most importantly for us, the case of
the
c
-calculus was treated by Mazzeo and Melrose in
[
40
]
. See also

[
8
,
14
,
28
,
46
,
47
]
and others, who have
proved various extensions of this result.

Recall the definition of the principal symbol
\sigma_{m}^{\mathbb{R}}
for translation invariant
operators in
{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};E)}
in Remark
4.21
. For
the following result, we shall need the algebra

(7.10)

\mathfrak{A}\mathrel{\ :=\ }\{(P,a)\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};E)}\oplus{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\operatorname{inv}}(S^{*}{\mathfrak{M}};\operatorname{End}(E))\mid\\
\sigma_{m}^{\mathbb{R}}(P)={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(a)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\partial S^{*}{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};\operatorname{End}(E))\,,\ P\ \mbox{ classical }\}\,.

Proposition 7.10
.

The joint map

({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}},\sigma_{m}):{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\cap\Psi_{cl}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to\mathfrak{A}

is surjective with kernel
\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E)+\big(\Psi_{comp}^{m-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\cap\Psi_{cl}^{m-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\big)
. The analogous result holds for

{\Psi_{\operatorname{inv}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
with the obvious modifications:
P\in{\psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m}({\mathfrak{M}};E)}

and the kernel is
\Psi_{comp}^{m-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\cap\Psi_{cl}^{m-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.

Proof.

All the pseudodifferential operators in this proof are classical.
Let
(P,a)\in\mathfrak{A}
. Using the surjectivity of the principal symbol,
Theorem
4.12
, we know that there exists
Q\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
such
that
\sigma_{m}(Q)=a
. Let
R:={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q)-P
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}^{\mathbb{R}}(R)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{m}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q)-P)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(\sigma_{m}(Q))-\sigma_{m}(P)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(a)-\sigma_{m}(P)=0\,,

where the second equality is by Theorem
5.20
(4).
Therefore
R\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{m-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)}
, and hence
s_{0}(R)\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{m-1}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
.
The operator
Q_{1}:=Q-s_{0}(R)
then satisfies

\begin{gathered}\sigma_{m}(Q_{1})\,=\,\sigma_{m}(Q-s_{0}(R))\,=\,\sigma_{m}(Q)\,=\,a\,.\\
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1})\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q)-R\,=\,P\,.\end{gathered}

This proves the desired surjectivity for essentially translation invariant
operators. The form of the kernel follows from the Theorems
5.14
(3)
and
5.20
.
The proof for operators that are translation invariant in a neighborhood
of infinity is the same.
∎

We can now characterize the compact operators in our
essentially translation invariant calculus.

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
{\mathfrak{M}}
be a manifold with compact, straight cylindrical ends and
E,F\to{\mathfrak{M}}

be compatible vector bundles. Let
T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
be a
classical

pseudodifferential operator. The following three conditions are equivalent:

(1)

The operator
T
is compact;

(2)

\sigma_{0}(T)=0
and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)=0
;

(3)

T\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)+\Psi_{comp}^{-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (2) and (3) follows from Proposition
7.10
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): Let us prove that each of the spaces
\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
and

\Psi_{comp}^{-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
act as compact operators from
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)

to
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
.
Let
T\in\Psi_{comp}^{-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
. Then
T
has a compactly supported distribution
kernel, by the very definition of
\Psi_{comp}^{-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
(Definition
4.23
).
Therefore
T:L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
is compact by Proposition
3.32
. Since

\Psi_{comp}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\simeq{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}}^{2};F\boxtimes E^{\prime})

is dense in
\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)\simeq\mathcal{S}({\mathfrak{M}}^{2};F\boxtimes E^{\prime})
in the natural
(Schwartz) topology on the second space and hence also in the norm of operators, it follows that

\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
also consists of compact operators.
So (3) implies (1).

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): Let us assume that
T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
is compact.
Let
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})
. Then
\phi T\phi\in\Psi_{comp}^{0}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
is
also compact. Proposition
3.32
then gives
\phi\sigma_{0}(T)\phi=\sigma_{0}(\phi T\phi)=0
. Hence
\sigma_{0}(T)=0
, since
\phi\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\text{c}}({\mathfrak{M}})
was arbitrary.
Next,

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)u\,=\,\lim_{s\to\infty}\Phi_{-s}\big[T\Phi_{s}(u)|_{\{t<0\}}\big]\,=\,0

since
\Phi_{s}(u)\to 0
weakly in
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
and
T
is compact
(see Lemma
5.18
). Thus (1) implies (2) and the proof is complete.
∎

We keep the assumptions and the notations of Theorem

{\mathfrak{M}}
is a manifold with compact,
straight cylindrical ends,
E_{i},F_{i}\to{\mathfrak{M}}
,
i=1,\ldots,k
are compatible vector bundles,
and
E=\oplus_{i=1}^{k}E_{i}
and
F=\oplus_{j=1}^{k}F_{j}
.
Let
T=[T_{ij}]\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{[\mathbf{s+t}]}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
be a
classical operator

and
m\in\mathbb{R}
. The map
T:H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};F)

is Fredholm if, and only, if

(1)

T
is Douglis-Nirenberg-elliptic and

(2)

the limit operator

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)\mathrel{\ :=\ }[{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T_{ij})]:H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R};E)\to H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}(\partial{\mathfrak{M}}_{0}\times\mathbb{R};F)

is invertible.

This result is, of course, well-known in the setting of the
b
-calculus

42
,
43
]
.

Proof.

Let us assume first that
m=s_{i}=t_{i}=0
,
i=1,\ldots,k
, as in the
proof of Theorem
7.4
. We shall use Theorem
5.14
repeatedly.

Let us assume next that
T:L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
is Fredholm.
We know that
T
is elliptic by Theorem
3.27
(2)
(but we shall obtain this fact directly here from Proposition
7.11
).
Then
T^{*}
is Fredholm and hence
0\leq T^{*}T\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
is also Fredholm.
We can then find
R\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
such that
T^{*}T+R^{*}R\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
is invertible as an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(M;E)
. Let

P\mathrel{\ :=\ }(T^{*}T+R^{*}R)^{-1}T^{*}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};F,E)\,.

A direct calculation based on the symbolic properties of the
{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{\infty}}
calculus gives that

PT-1\,=\,-(T^{*}T+R^{*}R)^{-1}R^{*}R\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\,

see Corollary
5.12
(1), Lemma
5.13
(2), and
Theorem
5.14
(1). Since
R
is compact, by Proposition
7.11
,
we obtain that
PT-1
is also compact. Proposition
7.11

implies

\begin{gathered}\sigma_{0}(P)\sigma_{0}(T)-1\,=\,\sigma_{0}(PT-1)\,=\,0\quad\mbox{and}\\
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(P){\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)-1\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(PT-1)\,=\,0\,.\end{gathered}

Therefore, both
\sigma_{0}(T)
and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)
are left invertible. Similarly, they are
right invertible, and hence invertible. This proves one implication of the desired equivalence.

To prove the other implication, let us assume that
\sigma_{0}(T)\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\operatorname{inv}}(S^{*}{\mathfrak{M}};\operatorname{Hom}(E,F))

and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{0}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)}
are invertible. Then
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)^{-1}\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{sus}}^{0}({\mathfrak{M}};F,E)}

by the spectral invariance theorem, Theorem
7.4
. Similarly, Corollary

2.9
gives that
\sigma_{0}(T)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}_{\operatorname{inv}}({\mathfrak{M}};\operatorname{Hom}(F,E))
.
Theorem
5.20
(4) then gives

\sigma_{0}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)^{-1})\,=\,\sigma_{0}^{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T))^{-1}\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(\sigma_{0}(T))^{-1}\\
\,=\,{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(\sigma_{0}(T)^{-1})\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\infty}(\partial S^{*}{\mathfrak{M}}_{0};\operatorname{Hom}(F,E))\,.

Proposition
7.10
then implies that there
exists
Q\in{\Psi_{\operatorname{ess}}^{0}}({\mathfrak{M}};E,F)
such that
\sigma_{0}(Q)=\sigma_{0}(T)^{-1}

and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q)={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)^{-1}
. Consequently,

\sigma_{0}(PQ)\,=\,1\,,\ \sigma_{0}(QP)\,=\,1\,,\ {\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(PQ)\,=\,1\,\ \mbox{ and }\ {\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(QP)\,=\,1\,.

Therefore
QP-1\in\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}^{-\infty}({\mathfrak{M}};E)+\Psi_{comp}^{-1}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
, by
Proposition
7.10
, and hence
QP-1
is compact, by Proposition

7.11
. Similarly,
PQ-1
is also compact. Atkinson’s theorem

3.26
then allows us to conclude that
P
is Fredholm.
This proves the result if
m=s_{i}=t_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
.

The proof of the result in general is the same as the proof of the general case of
Theorem
7.4
, that is, using the order reduction operators
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}

obtained from the operators
S_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}}
of Equation (
7.2
)
as in that proof (we use the same formulas for
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
).
We first observe that the multiplicativity of
{\operatorname{Symb}}
and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}
, the invertibility of

Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
, Theorem

5.20
, and Remark
6.4
(3), imply that
all of
{\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{0})
,
{\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{1})
,
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{0})
, and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1})
are invertible
in their respective spaces (the first two on
S^{*}{\mathfrak{M}}
and the last two as
bounded operators on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)
and, respectively, on
L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};F)
).
We also use the relations

\sigma_{0}(Q_{1}TQ_{0})={\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{1}){\operatorname{Symb}}(T){\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{0})

and

{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1}TQ_{0})={\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1}){\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T){\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{0})

(again from Theorem

5.20
and Remark
6.4
(3))
and the bounded case already proved (in the second equivalence)
to successively obtain

\displaystyle T:H^{m+[\mathbf{t}]}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to H^{m-[\mathbf{s}]}({\mathfrak{M}};F)

is Fredholm

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow Q_{1}TQ_{0}:L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};E)\to L^{2}({\mathfrak{M}};F)\ \mbox{ is Fredholm }

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sigma_{0}(Q_{1}TQ_{0})\mbox{ and }\ {\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1}TQ_{0})\ \mbox{ are invertible}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow{\operatorname{Symb}}(T)\mbox{ and }\ {\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(T)\ \mbox{ are invertible\,.}

This completes the proof because
{\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{0})
,
{\operatorname{Symb}}(Q_{1})
,
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{0})
,
and
{\mathcal{R}_{\infty}}(Q_{1})
are invertible (because
Q_{0}
and
Q_{1}
are invertible).
∎
