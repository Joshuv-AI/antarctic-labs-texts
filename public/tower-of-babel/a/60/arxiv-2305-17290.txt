Abstract
. We introduce a new concept of variable bandwidth that is based on the frequency truncation of Wilson expansions. For this model we derive sampling theorems, a complete reconstruction of f f from its samples, and necessary density conditions for sampling. Numerical simulations support the interpretation of this model of variable bandwidth. In particular, chirps, as they arise in the description of gravitational waves, can be modeled in a space of variable bandwidth.

1.
Introduction

Variable bandwidth is a natural, but vexing concept in signal processing that has sparked the curiosity of engineers, physicists, and mathematicians. The difficulty of defining an adequate notion of variable bandwidth lies in the nature of the Fourier transform and the uncertainty principle.

A function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is said to have bandwidth
\Omega>0
, if the number
\Omega
is the maximal frequency contributing to
f
, in other words, its Fourier transform vanishes outside the interval
[-\Omega,\Omega]
. The associated function space is the Paley-Wiener space

(1.1)

PW_{\Omega}=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\supp(\hat{f})\subseteq[-\Omega,\Omega]\}.

By definition the bandwidth is a global quantity that requires the knowledge of
f
on all of
\mathbb{R}
. In addition, functions in the Paley-Wiener space are entire functions of time, whereas real-world signals typically have a finite duration and also consist of bursts or pulses of varying duration, intensity, and frequency. Yet on an experiential level, variable bandwidth obviously exists; for instance, in music we can perceive clearly the highest note, i.e., the local bandwidth, and its temporal changes, i.e., variable bandwidth.

On a scientific level, there have been many attempts to define and work with variable bandwidth, see our discussion below, but there is no consensus about the right definition.

In this work we propose a new approach to variable bandwidth with
time-frequency methods. Time-frequency analysis offers a natural toolbox for
variable bandwidth, since its objective is roughly the analysis of
functions via joint time-frequency representations that combine the temporal
behavior
f
and the frequency behavior
\hat{f}
in one picture. Our
idea is to start with a
discrete
time-frequency representation that
allows us to represent any
f
as a series expansion of time-frequency atoms
with a clear localization both in time and in frequency.
We may then prescribe a time-varying frequency truncation and in this way end up with a space of given variable bandwidth.

This idea goes back to Aceska and Feichtinger
[
3
,
4
]
who used a time-varying frequency cut-off of the short-time Fourier transform. The resulting function spaces, however, coincide with the standard Sobolev spaces, and therefore lack the characteristic properties of Paley-Wiener space, and they admit neither a sampling theorem nor a critical density.
Our new idea is to use a discrete version of the spaces of Aceska and Feichtinger and to replace the (continuous) short-time Fourier transform with a frequency truncation of a Wilson expansion. Our goal is then to show that the new spaces behave indeed like the classical Paley-Wiener space and prove sampling theorems and the existence of a critical sampling density.

The underlying time-frequency tool is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with a built-in time-frequency structure, a so-called Wilson basis. A Wilson basis is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle\psi_{n,l}(x)=\begin{cases}g(x-n),&n\in\mathbb{Z},l=0,\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{2\pi ilx}+(-1)^{l+n}e^{-2\pi ilx})g(x-n/2),&n,l\in\mathbb{Z},l>0.\end{cases}

Daubechies, Jaffard and Journé
[
21
]
showed that such
orthonormal bases exist and that the window function
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

may be chosen to be smooth and compactly supported. Then a basis
element
\psi_{n,l}
is supported in a neighborhood of time
n
and
possesses a frequency in a band near
\pm l
.

A new model for variable bandwidth.

Now let
\{\psi_{n,l}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},l\in\mathbb{N}}
be an orthonormal Wilson basis as in (
1.2
) and let
b:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{N}
be a bounded positive sequence representing the time-varying frequency truncation.
Then we define a variable bandwidth Paley-Wiener-type space as follows:

(1.3)

PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}c_{n,l}\psi_{n,l},\text{ }c\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N})\Big\}.

This is our main object of investigation.
If
g
has compact support, then the restriction
f|_{[n/2-1/2,n/2+1/2]}
is approximately a trigonometric polynomial of degree
b(n)
, i.e., the maximal frequency on
[n/2-1/2,n/2+1/2]
is expected to be
b(n)
.
While this intuition is basically correct, we will need to refine it because the interval
[n/2-1/2,n/2+1/2]
contains several translates
\psi_{n^{\prime},l}
for
n^{\prime}\neq n
.

Wilson bases have proved to be very useful in time-frequency analysis since they provide a way to overcome the barrier posed by the Balian-Low theorem
[
10
]
.
Wilson bases are a popular tool in time-frequency analysis and signal processing to study function spaces time-frequency localization and non-linear approximation
[
25
,
35
]
. More importantly, Wilson bases have gained visibility and popularity for a general scientific audience because of their role in the detection of gravitational waves in 2015
[
1
,
2
,
18
]
.
Gravitational waves are ripples in space-time caused by violent cosmic events like colliding black holes and supernovae and were predicted by Albert Einstein
[
22
,
23
]
. For the collision and merging of two neutron stars, the analytic form of the gravitational wave was calculated
in
[
13
,
14
,
16
]
to be of the form

(1.4)

s(t)=c|t-t_{0}|^{-\frac{1}{4}}\cos(\omega|t-t_{0}|^{\frac{5}{8}}+\varphi),\quad\text{ for }t<t_{0},

where
c
is a constant,
\omega\gg 1
and
t_{0}
is the time of coalescence. Since
\omega|t-t_{0}|^{\frac{5}{8}}=\omega|t-t_{0}|^{-\frac{3}{8}}\,\lvert t-t_{0}\rvert
, the quantity
\omega|t-t_{0}|^{-\frac{3}{8}}
represents the (maximal) frequency at time
t
. In the language of signal processing such a function is called a
chirp
, and is considered a prototype of a signal with variable bandwidth.

Essentially, the detection of gravitational waves amounts to the extraction of a chirp buried inside a noisy signal.
In 2012, Necula, Klimenko, and Mitselmakher
[
41
]
proposed to use Wilson bases for gravitational wave detection and developed an algorithm that maps time series data to the time-frequency plane using a Wilson basis. This algorithm was successfully applied to gravitational data to detect the merger of two black holes and other astrophysical data.

Let us emphasize that in this application the shape of the local bandwidth
\omega|t-t_{0}|^{-\frac{3}{8}}
is known in advance and can thus be incorporated into a suitable signal space. We argue and will demonstrate numerically that a variable bandwidth Paley-Wiener space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is such a suitable space.

Results.

We will show that
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
behaves in many ways like the classical Paley-Wiener space (
1.1
). In analogy to the Shannon-Whittaker-Kotelnikov sampling theorem and its variations, we will prove a sampling theorem for the variable bandwidth space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. Secondly, we will determine a critical sampling density for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
that represents a necessary condition for sampling and can be seen as an information-theoretic quantity associated to

PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. For the classical Paley-Wiener space, these questions have been studied for many decades
[
45
,
47
]
with the deepest results due to Beurling
[
11
]
and Landau
[
42
]
. These results have set the standard of how to approach sampling in a new function space.

In particular, sampling theorems are nowadays formulated as sampling inequalities. For this, we recall that a set
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
is called a set of (stable) sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, if there exist constants
A,B>0
such that

(1.5)

A\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|f(\lambda)|^{2}\leq B\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})\,.

The sampling inequality (
1.5
) indicates that the entire information carried by the function is captured by the evaluation of the function at the samples. Furthermore, a sampling entails automatically several reconstruction methods of a function from its samples.

We start with a necessary density condition for sampling in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
that is valid for a very general class of windows.

Theorem 1.1

(Necessary density condition for sampling)
.

Assume that
g\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})
is a real-valued, even function such that
|g(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-1-\epsilon}
for some
C>0
and
\epsilon>0
, and that
g
generates an orthonormal Wilson basis.
Let
b=(b(n))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a bounded sequence such that

b(n)\geq 1
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
be the associated variable bandwidth space.
If
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a set of sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, then

(1.6)

D^{-}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap[x-r,x+r])}{2r}\geq 1+\overline{b}

where
\overline{b}=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in[x-r,x+r]}b(n)
.

In this result,
\overline{b}
represents a sort of average bandwidth. The necessary condition says that on average are needed at least
1+\bar{b}
samples to recover
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
.

On the constructive side, our main theorem provides a sufficient condition for sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. In the absence of more structure, such as analyticity, we use a local maximum gap between consecutive samples to formulate the sufficient condition.

Theorem 1.2

(Sufficient condition for sampling)
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be a real-valued, even function with
\supp(g)\subseteq[-m,m]
that generates an orthonormal Wilson basis.
Let
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
be the space of variable bandwidth defined in (
1.3
). Label
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
as

(1.7)

\Lambda\coloneqq\Big\{x_{\frac{k}{2},j}=k/2+\eta_{\frac{k}{2},j}:k\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ }\eta_{\frac{k}{2},j}\in[0,1/2),\text{ }j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\tfrac{k}{2})\Big\}.

Let
\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}=\max_{j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}(x_{\frac{k}{2},j+1}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j})
be the maximum gap on
[\tfrac{k}{2},\tfrac{k+1}{2}]
and assume that
x_{\frac{k}{2},1}-\tfrac{k}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
and
\frac{k+1}{2}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j_{\max(\frac{k}{2})}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
.
Define

(1.8)

D\coloneqq(4m)\cdot\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}

and

(1.9)

\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}\coloneqq\max_{n=(k-2m,k+2m+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Big(b(n)+1\Big).

If for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}

(1.10)

\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}<\frac{\pi}{\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}D},

then
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
can be reconstructed completely from the samples in
\Lambda
.

If in addition
\Lambda
is separated, then
\Lambda
is a set of sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and a sampling inequality (
1.5
) holds.

The formulation confirms the initial intuition that the parameter
b(k)
measures the local bandwidth of
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
near
k/2
. However, we learned that also the influence of the neighboring intervals must be included and that the parameter
\mu_{k/2}
is a more accurate measure for the local bandwidth. For an illustrative example we refer to the discussion in Section
4
.

The second important aspect is the local nature of
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. Since
\supp(g)
is compact, the restriction
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})\big|_{I}
to an interval
I
is in fact finite-dimensional. This is quite in contrast to the classical Paley-Wiener space of entire functions and greatly facilitates the numerical treatment of sampling in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
.

For the proof we will resort to the adaptive weights method described in
[
24
,
30
]
.

Other concepts of variable bandwidth in the literature.
In the literature one finds several possible approaches to variable bandwidth. While they all have a common goal, the mathematical methods are entirely disjoint from our approach.

One of the first attempts to capture variable bandwidth in signal processing involves warping functions
[
15
,
20
,
38
,
46
,
49
]
.
Given a homeomorphism
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, a so-called warping function, a function
f
possesses variable bandwidth with respect to
\gamma
if
f=g\circ\gamma
for a bandlimited function
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\supp(\hat{g})\subseteq[-\Omega,\Omega]
. The derivative
1/\gamma^{\prime}(\gamma^{-1}(x))
of the warping function is interpreted as the local bandwidth of
f
at
x
.

A recent definition of variable bandwidth is based on the spectral theory of a Sturm-Liouville operator
A_{p}f=-\frac{d}{dx}(p(x)\frac{d}{dx})f
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where
p>0
is a strictly positive function
[
17
,
33
,
34
]
. Denote by
c_{\Lambda}(A_{p})
the spectral projection of
A_{p}
corresponding to
A_{p}
in
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then the range of this projection-valued measure
c_{\Lambda}(A_{p})
is the space of functions of variable bandwidth with spectral set in
\Lambda
. For this space of variable bandwidth were proved a (nonuniform) sampling theorem and necessary density conditions.
The main insight is that, for a spectrum
\Lambda=[0,\Omega]
, a function of variable bandwidth behaves like a bandlimited function with local bandwidth
(\Omega/p(x))^{1/2}
in a neighborhood of
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Kempf and his collaborators used a procedural idea of variable bandwidth
[
36
,
39
,
40
]
. A formal definition concept was then presented in
[
44
]
. Essentially, they generated a symmetric operator and a related reproducing kernel Hilbert space based on a sequence of sampling points. This space was designated as a variable bandwidth space. By parameterizing the self-adjoint extensions of the operator, they obtained several sampling theorems at the critical density.

Numerical simulations.
Our theoretical analysis of sampling in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is complemented by detailed numerical simulations. We will consider several numerical scenarios and classes of sampling sets. We study (i) the reconstruction of a compactly supported function in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, and (ii) the approximation of the restriction to an interval of an arbitrary function. In this case boundary effects occur and have to be dealt with. As a highlight, we reconstruct a chirp similar to (
1.4
) from its samples.

Outlook.
Wilson bases are by no means the only discrete time-frequency representations that are suitable for our approach. Alternative definitions of variable bandwidth can be defined by using a variable frequency truncation of a Gabor frame expansion or by replacing a Wilson basis with local Fourier bases. The achievable results are similar in spirit, but these alternative set-ups also pose different challenges (for instance, well localized Gabor frames are not linearly independent). See
[
6
]
for more information. At this time, it is not clear which version of variable bandwidth is the most convenient one.

Finally, from the point of view of signal processing and data
analysis, it is essential to determine the local bandwidth parameters

b(n)
based on given samples. This problem is one of parameter
estimation and is usually treated separately and needs to be dealt with even for the classical Paley-Wiener space.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces the main tools required, namely the characterization of a Wilson basis, a version of the necessary density condition theorem for reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
[
27
]
, the proof that
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, and several standard inequalities.
Section
3
contains the proof of the necessary density condition (Theorem
1.1
) for sampling for the space of variable bandwidth
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. In Section
4
we provide the proof of the sufficient condition for sampling in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
(Theorem
1.2
). In Section
5
we discuss the numerical implementation of the variable bandwidth model and present the numerical reconstruction results.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we recall the basic properties of a Wilson basis and the tools we need to investigate sampling.

By defining the usual translation and modulation operator as
T_{n}f(t)=f(t-n)
and

M_{l}f(t)=f(t)e^{2\pi ilt}
, we can rewrite the entire collection of functions that forms a Wilson basis (
1.2
) as

(2.1)

\tilde{\psi}_{n,l}=d_{l}(M_{l}+(-1)^{l+n}M_{-l})T_{\frac{n}{2}}g,\quad(n,l)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2},l\geq 0,

where
d_{0}=\frac{1}{2}
and
d_{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
,
l\geq 1
. In this form
\tilde{\psi}_{n,l}=\psi_{n,l}
for
l\geq 1
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\tilde{\psi}_{2n,0}=\psi_{n,0}=T_{n}g
, and
\tilde{\psi}_{2n+1,0}=0
.

The following result from
[
31
, Corollary 8.5.4]
gives a characterization of Wilson bases.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
to be even and real-valued. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

The Wilson system
\{\psi_{n,l}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},l\in\mathbb{N}}
as defined by (
1.2
) is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

\lVert g\rVert_{2}=1
and
the Gabor system
\{T_{n/2}M_{l}g:n,l\in\mathbb{Z}\}

is a tight frame for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., there exists a constant

A>0
such that for all
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

(2.2)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}|\langle f,T_{n/2}M_{l}g\rangle|^{2}=A\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(iii)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}g(x-k-\frac{n}{2})\overline{g}(x-\frac{n}{2})=2\delta_{k0}
a.e.

Orthonormal Wilson bases exist in abundance and the window function
g
may be chosen to be smooth with compact support or may be endowed with other desirable properties
[
9
,
21
]
.

To study necessary conditions for sampling in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, we will make use of the general theory of sampling in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces and the necessary density conditions derived in
[
27
]
.
We recall that a closed subspace
\mathcal{K}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space if the point evaluation
f\mapsto f(x)
is a bounded linear functional on
\mathcal{K}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case there exists a family
k_{x}\in\mathcal{K},x\in\mathbb{R},
such that
f(x)=\langle f,k_{x}\rangle
. The function
k(x,y)=\overline{k_{x}(y)}
is called the reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{K}
.
We formulate a simplified version of
[
27
, Thm. 1.1]
that is adapted to
\mathbb{R}
with Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with a reproducing kernel
k(x,y)
satisfying

(i)

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}k(x,x)>0
and

(ii)

off-diagonal decay of the form
|k(x,y)|\leq N(1+|x-y|)^{-1-\epsilon}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and some
\epsilon>0
.

If for
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}
there exist
A,B>0
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{K}

(2.3)

A\lVert f\rVert^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|f(\lambda)|^{2}\leq B\lVert f\rVert^{2},

then

D^{-}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap[x-r,x+r])}{2r}\geq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}k(y,y)dy.

We recall that the sampling inequality (
2.3
) is equivalent of
\{k_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
being a frame for
\mathcal{K}
. The number
D^{-}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap[x-r,x+r])}{2r}
is the Beurling density of
\Lambda
.

To apply Theorem
2.2
, we first show that the variable bandwidth space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert
space.

Lemma 2.3
.

The space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
of variable bandwidth defined in (
1.3
) is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with reproducing kernel

(2.4)

k(x,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\overline{\psi_{n,l}(y)}\psi_{n,l}(x).

Proof.

Recall from the definition (
1.3
) of
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
that
b(n)\leq B<\infty
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since
\{\psi_{n,l}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},l\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})=\spn\{\psi_{n,l}:n\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ }l=0,\dots,b(n)\}
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

We show that at each point in
\mathbb{R}
the linear functional
f\mapsto f(x)
is bounded i.e. there exists
M>0
such that
|f(x)|\leq M\lVert f\rVert_{2}
for every
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
f=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\langle f,\psi_{n,l}\rangle\psi_{n,l}\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, then applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

(2.5)

\displaystyle|f(x)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\langle f,\psi_{n,l}\rangle\,\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|

(2.6)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\Big|\langle f,\psi_{n,l}\rangle\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\Big|\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Since
\{\psi_{n,l}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},l\leq b(n)}
is an orthonormal basis for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, we have that

\Big(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\Big|\langle f,\psi_{n,l}\rangle\Big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\lVert f\rVert_{2}.

To bound the second term of (
2.5
), we apply the definition of a Wilson basis and we obtain

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\Big|\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|\psi_{n,0}(x)\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\Big|\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\Big|\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|^{2}.

Recall from the characterization of Wilson basis the equivalence (iii) of Theorem
2.1
. Then the first summand can be easily bounded as follows:

(2.7)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big|g\Big(x-\frac{n}{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}=2.

The second summand can be bounded using the definition of the Wilson basis, the fact that
b(n)\leq B
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and again equivalence (iii) of Theorem
2.1
. Therefore,

(2.8)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\Big|\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big|^{2}\leq 2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\Big|g\Big(x-\frac{n}{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}\leq 4B.

Then
|f(x)|\leq\sqrt{2(1+2B)}\lVert f\rVert_{2}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and for every
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. Hence,
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

Moreover, since
\{\psi_{n,l}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},l\leq b(n)}
is an orthonormal basis for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, the reproducing kernel is given by

(2.9)

k(x,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\overline{\psi_{n,l}(y)}\psi_{n,l}(x).

The following versions of Wirtinger’s inequality and Bernstein’s inequality will be useful tools to prove the sufficient condition for sampling.

Lemma 2.4

(Wirtinger’s inequality)
.

If
f,f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(a,b)
,
a<c<b
, and
f(c)=0
, then

(2.10)

\int_{a}^{b}|f(x)|^{2}dx\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\max\{(b-c)^{2},(c-a)^{2}\}\int_{a}^{b}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx.

Lemma
2.4
follows from
[
37
, p.184]
, by applying a change of variables.
Wirtinger’s inequality and its variations are often used in the proofs of sampling theorems, see
[
8
,
30
,
48
]
.

Bernstein’s inequality provides a bound for the derivative of a trigonometric polynomial.

Lemma 2.5

(Bernstein’s inequality)
.

Let
P
be a trigonometric polynomial of degree
n
i.e.
P(x)=\sum_{|k|\leq n}c_{k}e^{2\pi ikx}
. Then

(2.11)

\lVert P^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\leq 2\pi n\lVert P\rVert_{2}.

3.
Necessary density condition

In this section, we will prove necessary density conditions for the spaces of variable bandwidth generated by a Wilson basis. Since
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is modeled after the Paley-Wiener space of bandlimited functions, we expect that there exists a critical density for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
as well. A critical density is a fundamental limitation for the reconstruction of a function from its samples and quantifies how many samples are required. Furthermore, the critical density is a benchmark against which one can measure constructive approaches to a reconstruction.

We start with a simple result for Wilson bases with a compactly supported window.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})
be a real-valued, even function with

\supp(g)\subseteq[-m,m]
that generates an orthonormal Wilson basis. Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a set of sampling
for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
defined in (
1.3
). Then
\Lambda\cap(\alpha,\beta)
contains at least

\lceil\beta-\alpha-2m\rceil+\sum_{n/2\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]}b(n)
points.

To show the result we use an easy argument that involves counting dimensions as in
[
5
]
.

Proof.

Let
I=(\alpha,\beta)
such that
|I|\geq 2m
and define the
local variable bandwidth space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
by

(3.1)

\displaystyle PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)

\displaystyle=\{\psi_{n,l}:\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I\}

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\spn\{\psi_{n,0}:n\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]\}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\spn\{\psi_{n,l}:n/2\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m],\text{ }l=1,\dots,b(n)\}.

We make the following three observations:

(1)

PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
is a subspace of
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and thus the sampling inequalities hold for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
.

(2)

Since the Wilson basis functions are compactly supported, every
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
has support in
(\alpha,\beta)
.

(3)

PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
is finite-dimensional, and

(3.4)

\dim(PW_{b}^{2}(g,I))=\#([\alpha+m,\beta-m]\cap\mathbb{Z})+\sum_{n/2\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]}b(n).

Since for
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
, we have

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap(\alpha,\beta)}\lvert f(\lambda)\rvert^{2}=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\lvert f(\lambda)\rvert^{2}\geq A\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2},

the map
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)\to\{f(\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap(\alpha,\beta)}
is one-to-one. It follows that

(3.5)

\dim(PW_{b}^{2}(g,I))=\lceil\beta-\alpha-2m\rceil+\sum_{n/2\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]}b(n)\leq\#(\Lambda\cap(\alpha,\beta)).

∎

Next we state a more general theorem for a more general class of windows satisfying a mild decay condition.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})
be a real-valued, even function, such
that
|g(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)^{-1-\epsilon}
for some
C>0
and

\epsilon>0
and assume that
g
generates an orthonormal Wilson basis.
Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a set of sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
with
b(n)\geq 1
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then

\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{\#(\Lambda\cap[x-r,x+r])}{2r}\geq 1+\overline{b}

where
\overline{b}=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in[x-r,x+r]}b(n)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
,
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. In order to apply the results on sampling in general reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces from
[
27
]
in the form of Theorem
2.2
, we only need to verify the conditions on the reproducing kernel.

Step 1.
We show that the diagonal
k(x,x)
of the reproducing kernel is bounded below (condition (i) of Theorem
2.2
).
After substituting the definition of a Wilson basis in (
2.4
), we write

(3.6)

k(x,x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}|e^{2\pi ilx}+(-1)^{l+n}e^{-2\pi ilx}|^{2}\Big|g\Big(x-\frac{n}{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}.

Since
g
has compact support, the series are locally finite. The continuity of
g
implies that
k(x,x)
is continuous, and (
3.6
) shows that
k(x,x)
is periodic with period
1
. Consequently it suffices to show that
k(x,x)>0
on
[0,1)
.

We use
b(n)\geq 1
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and obtain

(3.7)

\displaystyle k(x,x)\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|e^{2\pi ix}+(-1)^{1+n}e^{-2\pi ix}|^{2}\Big|g\Big(x-\frac{n}{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}.

By dividing the second sum into even and odd parts and applying (iii) of Theorem
2.1
, we get

\displaystyle k(x,x)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}+2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\sin{2\pi x}\rvert^{2}|g(x-n)|^{2}+2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\cos{2\pi x}\rvert^{2}\Bigl|g\Bigl(x-n-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(x-n)|^{2}(1+2\lvert\sin{2\pi x}\rvert^{2})+2\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert\cos{2\pi x}\rvert^{2}\Bigl|g\Bigl(x-n-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\min\{1+2\lvert\sin{2\pi x}\rvert^{2},2\lvert\cos{2\pi x}\rvert^{2}\}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|g\Bigl(x-\frac{n}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\min\{1+2\lvert\sin{2\pi x}\rvert^{2},2\lvert\cos{2\pi x}\rvert^{2}\}.

The only possible zeros of
k(x,x)
for
x\in[0,1)
are for
\cos{2\pi x}=0
, i.e, at
x=\frac{1}{4}
or
x=\frac{3}{4}
.
Since
g
is even,
k(x,x)
is even, and thus periodicity yields
k(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})=k(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})=k(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})
and
k(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})+k(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})=k(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})+k(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4})
.
Applying (iii) of Theorem
2.1
and from the inequality (
3.8
), we obtain

(3.9)

\displaystyle k\Bigl(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4}\Bigr)+k\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4},-\frac{1}{4}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq 3\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|g\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}-n\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|g\Bigl(-\frac{1}{4}-n\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)

(3.10)

\displaystyle=3\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|g\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}-n\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|g\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}-n-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)\Bigr|^{2}\Bigr)=6.

By symmetry
k(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})=k(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4})=3\neq 0
.

It follows that
k(x,x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1)
and thus
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}k(x,x)=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}}k(x,x)>0
.

Step 2.
We verify the required decay condition of the kernel (condition (ii) of Theorem
2.2
).
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall that
b(n)\leq B
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
|\psi_{n,l}(x)|\leq\sqrt{2}g(x-n/2)
for
l\neq 0
, then we have

\displaystyle|k(x,y)|=

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\overline{\psi_{n,l}(y)}\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big\rvert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\overline{\psi_{n,0}(y)}\psi_{n,0}(x)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\overline{\psi_{n,l}(y)}\psi_{n,l}(x)\Big\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\{\lvert g(y-n)g(x-n)\rvert+2b(n)\lvert g(y-n/2)g(x-n/2)\rvert\}

(3.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\{\lvert g(y-n)g(x-n)\rvert+2B\lvert g(y-n/2)g(x-n/2)\rvert\}.

To deal with these sums, we use a convolution estimate of the weight function
(1+|x|)^{-1-\epsilon}
from
[
32
]
and obtain

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(y-n)g(x-n)|

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1+|y-n|)^{-1-\epsilon}(1+|x-n|)^{-1-\epsilon}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}C_{0}(1+|x-y|)^{-1-\epsilon}\,,

where the last inequality is precisely the statement of
[
32
, Lemma 2]
.

Similarly, the second term in (
3
) is bounded by

(3.14)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lvert g(y-n/2)g(x-n/2)\rvert\leq\widetilde{C}(1+|x-y|)^{-1-\epsilon},

so that we obtain the desired off-diagonal decay of the kernel.
Conditions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.2
are satisfied, and Theorem
2.2
is applicable.

Step 3.
It remains to compute the averaged trace
\frac{1}{2r}\int_{x-r}^{x+r}k(y,y)dy
and let
r
tend to
\infty
. We write
B_{r}(x)=[x-r,x+r]
in the following.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\rho>0
, such that
\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}|\psi_{0,l}(x)|^{2}dx\geq 1-\varepsilon
for
l=0,\dots,B
. Then

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}(n/2)}|\psi_{n,l}(x)|^{2}dx\geq 1-\varepsilon\quad\text{for }l=1,\dots,B\quad\text{ and}

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\rho}(n)}|\psi_{n,0}(x)|^{2}dx\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Using (
2.8
), we can apply dominated convergence and interchange summation and integration and obtain

\frac{1}{2r}\int_{B_{r}(x)}k(y,y)dy=\frac{1}{2r}\int_{B_{r}(x)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}|\psi_{n,l}(y)|^{2}dy=\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|\psi_{n,l}(y)|^{2}dy.

If
\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r-\rho}(x)
, then
B_{\rho}(\frac{n}{2})\subseteq B_{r}(x)
. We estimate the average trace as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2r}\int_{B_{r}(x)}k(y,y)dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{n\in B_{r-\rho}(x)}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|\psi_{n,0}(y)|^{2}dy+\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r-\rho}(x)}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}\int_{B_{r}(x)}|\psi_{n,l}(y)|^{2}dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2r}(1-\varepsilon)\Bigl[\#(B_{r-\rho}(x)\cap\mathbb{Z})+\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r-\rho}(x)}b(n)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2r}(1-\varepsilon)\Bigl[2(r-\rho)+\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r-\rho}(x)}b(n)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)\frac{r-\rho}{r}+\frac{1}{2r}(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r-\rho}(x)}b(n).

Letting
r\to\infty
, we obtain

(3.17)

\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{B_{r}(x)}k(y,y)dy\geq 1-\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r}(x)}b(n).

Define
\overline{b}\coloneqq\liminf_{r\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2r}\sum_{\frac{n}{2}\in B_{r}(x)}b(n)
as the average bandwidth in
B_{r}(x)
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, Theorem
2.2
yields

(3.18)

D^{-}(\Lambda)\geq 1+\overline{b}\,,

which finishes the proof.
∎

Remarks
.

(i) Note that in the above proof the properties of Wilson bases play a crucial role. In particular, we have used the characterization (iii) of Theorem
2.1
and the symmetry of the window function
g
.

(ii) It is easy to see that for a compactly supported window
g
Theorem
3.1
implies directly Theorem
3.2
.

4.
Sufficient condition for sampling

With some necessary conditions for sampling in place, we now investigate the question under which conditions on a set a function in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
can be reconstructed completely. In this context, we use a Wilson basis with a continuous, compactly supported window
g
. This condition guarantees the locality of the reconstruction.

We will index the sampling set
\Lambda
as follows

(4.1)

\Lambda:=\Big\{x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\in\mathbb{R}:x_{\frac{k}{2},j}=\frac{k}{2}+\eta_{\frac{k}{2},j},\text{ where }k\in\mathbb{Z},\text{ }j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\tfrac{k}{2}),\text{ }\eta_{\frac{k}{2},j}\in[0,1/2)\Big\}\,.

We always assume that the sampling points
x_{k/2,j}
are consecutive points in
[k/2,k/2+1/2)
, i.e.,
x_{k/2,j}<x_{k/2,j+1}
for
j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\tfrac{k}{2})-1
. We measure the local sampling density by the maximum gap between the samples, i.e.

(4.2)

\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}=\max_{j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})-1}(x_{\frac{k}{2},j+1}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j}),

where the number
j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})
depends on the interval.

In the following we will represent the functions in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
in the form

(4.3)

f(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=0}^{b(k)}c_{k,l}\psi_{k,l}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}P_{k}(x)g(x-k/2),

where
P_{k}(x):=\sum_{l=0}^{b(k)}c_{k,l}d_{l}(M_{l}+(-1)^{l+k}M_{-l})
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
b(k)
and period 1. Roughly speaking, a function in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
restricted to
[\tfrac{k}{2},\tfrac{k+1}{2}]
behaves like a trigonometric
polynomial of degree
b(k)
. We will use the fact that in this representation of
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})

\sum_{l=0}^{b(k)}|c_{k,l}|^{2}=\|P_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{c}^{c+1}|P_{k}(x)|^{2}\,dx

(for every
c\in\mathbb{R}
) and

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\|P_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}\,.

Since according to our model space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, the local bandwidth on
[k/2,k/2+1/2)
is roughly the degree
b(k)
of the local polynomial
P_{k}
,
we expect that the gap
\delta_{k/2}
on
[k/2,k/2+1/2)
is related to
b(k)
.

The following theorem makes this expectation rigorous and provides sufficient condition for sampling for
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. For the proof we use smoothness estimates in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
(via Wirtinger’s and Bernstein’s inequality). For bandlimited functions this method goes back to
[
30
]
, and was later called the adaptive weights method in
[
24
]
. An axiomatic, extremely general approach was later presented in
[
26
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be a real-valued, even function with
\supp(g)\subseteq[-m,m]
that generates an orthonormal Wilson basis.
Let
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
be the space of variable bandwidth defined in (
1.3
) and let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be as in
(
4.1
) with
x_{\frac{k}{2},1}-\tfrac{k}{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
and
\frac{k+1}{2}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
.
Define

(4.4)

D\coloneqq(4m)\cdot\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}

and

(4.5)

\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}\coloneqq\max_{n=(k-2m,k+2m+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Big(b(n)+1\Big).

If for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}

(4.6)

\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}<\frac{\pi}{\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}D},

then
\Lambda
is a sampling set and satisfies a weighted sampling inequality

(4.7)

A\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}|f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}w_{\frac{k}{2},j}\leq B\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})\,,

where
w_{\frac{k}{2},j}>0
is a suitable sequence of weights. As a consequence, every
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
can be reconstructed completely from the samples in
\Lambda
.

Remark
.

We have chosen a formulation that separates the influence of the window
g
, the local bandwidth
\mu_{k/2}
, and the local sampling density
\delta_{k/2}
into different constants. In this way, the sufficient
condition (
4.6
) looks formally similar to the maximum gap condition for sampling of bandlimited functions, e.g., in
[
30
]
.

Proof.

Let
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. We define
y_{\frac{k}{2},0}=k/2
and
y_{\frac{k}{2},j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}=k/2+1/2
, and let
y_{\frac{k}{2},j}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}+x_{\frac{k}{2},j+1})
,
j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})-1
be the midpoints between the samples. Set
\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}=\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{[y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1},y_{\frac{k}{2},j})}
. Moreover, if the maximum gap on
[\tfrac{k}{2},\tfrac{k+1}{2}]
is
\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
, then
y_{\frac{k}{2},j}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
and
x_{\frac{k}{2},j}-y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}
.

Step 1. Approximation from samples.
Let
P
be the orthogonal projection from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. Motivated by
[
30
]
, we approximate
f
by a step function and project onto
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
with
P
. The resulting approximation operator is defined by

(4.8)

Af=P\Big(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}f\bigl(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\bigr)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}\Big).

If
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
, then
Af\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and
A
maps
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
to
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
. We emphasize that
A
uses only the samples
f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})
. The question is how well the operator
A
approximates the identity.

Since
f=Pf=P(\sum_{k,j}f\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j})
and the characteristic functions
\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}
have mutually disjoint support, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lVert P\Bigl(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}(f-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}))\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}\Bigr)\Bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}(f-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}))\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}

(4.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl\lvert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}(f(x)-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}))\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}(x)\Bigr\rvert^{2}dx

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\Bigl\lvert(f(x)-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}))\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}(x)\Bigr\rvert^{2}dx.

The sums converge absolutely, hence we can interchange summation and integration. Thus we can write

\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\int_{y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}}^{y_{\frac{k}{2},j}}|f(x)-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}dx.

By applying Wirtinger’s inequality (
2.10
) to every term, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}

\displaystyle\int_{y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}}^{y_{\frac{k}{2},j}}|f(x)-f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\max\Bigl\{(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}-y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1})^{2},(y_{\frac{k}{2},j}-x_{\frac{k}{2},j})^{2}\Bigr\}\int_{y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}}^{y_{\frac{k}{2},j}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\int_{y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}}^{y_{\frac{k}{2},j}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx.

By fixing
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we get for a single term

\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}\int_{y_{\frac{k}{2},j-1}}^{y_{\frac{k}{2},j}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\lVert f^{\prime}\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{[\frac{k}{2},\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2})}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Step 2. Estimate of local smoothness

\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx
.

As in (
4.3
) we write
f
in the form
f(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}P_{n}(x)g(x-n/2)
with

P_{n}(x):=\sum_{l=0}^{b(n)}c_{n,l}d_{l}(M_{l}+(-1)^{l+n}M_{-l})
and
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|P_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f\|_{2}^{2}
.
Then the derivative
f^{\prime}
is

(4.13)

f^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)+P_{n}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)\Big).

Since
\supp(g)\subseteq[-m,m]
, the sum is locally finite. The assumption
x\in\bigl[\frac{k}{2},\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigr)
requires
x-\frac{n}{2}\in(-m,m)
. This restricts the summation to indices

n\in(k-2m,k+1+2m)\cap\mathbb{Z}
. We abbreviate this interval by

(4.14)

F_{k}\coloneqq(k-2m,k+1+2m)\cap\mathbb{Z}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Big|\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\Big(P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)+P_{n}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)\Big)\Big|^{2}dx

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g(x)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+2\Rp\Big\{P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+P_{n}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)\overline{P_{j}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)}\Big)dx.

To bound the first term, we apply Bernstein’s inequality (
2.11
) to the polynomial
P_{n}
of degree
b(n)
and use the obvious estimate

\lVert T_{\frac{n}{2}}gT_{\frac{j}{2}}\overline{g}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}^{2}
. It follows that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g(x)}\Big)dx

\displaystyle\leq\lVert T_{\frac{n}{2}}gT_{\frac{j}{2}}\overline{g}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert P_{n}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}

(4.17)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}^{2}4\pi^{2}b(n)b(j)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}\rVert_{2}.

Taking the sum over
n
and
j
, we obtain

(4.18)

\displaystyle\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g(x)}\Big)dx

(4.19)

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi^{2}\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}b(n)b(j)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}\rVert_{2}

(4.20)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}.

For the middle terms of (
4
) we obtain in a similar way that

(4.21)

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Rp\Big\{P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)}\Big\}dx

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lVert T_{\frac{n}{2}}gT_{\frac{j}{2}}\overline{g^{\prime}}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert P_{n}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}\rVert_{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}\rVert_{2}.

After summing over
n
and
j
, we have

(4.24)

\displaystyle\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}2\Rp\Big\{P_{n}^{\prime}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g(x)\overline{P_{j}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)}\Big\}dx

(4.25)

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big).

The bound for the last term of (
4
) is

(4.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}\Big(P_{n}(x)T_{\frac{n}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)\overline{P_{j}(x)T_{\frac{j}{2}}g^{\prime}(x)}\Big)dx

(4.27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n,j\in F_{k}}\lVert T_{\frac{n}{2}}g^{\prime}T_{\frac{j}{2}}\overline{g^{\prime}}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\lVert P_{j}\rVert_{2}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert^{2}_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}.

Step 3. Intermediate estimate for
f-Af
.
Adding all terms, the local estimate for the derivative is

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 4\pi^{2}\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}+\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert^{2}_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+4\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big(2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{n\in F_{k}}b(n)\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}+\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}^{2}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\bigr(b(n)+1\bigl)\Big)^{2}.

Summing all the terms, we obtain

(4.29)

\displaystyle\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\int_{\frac{k}{2}}^{\frac{k}{2}+\frac{1}{2}}|f^{\prime}(x)|^{2}dx

(4.30)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\bigr(b(n)+1\bigl)\Big)^{2}.

We define the active local bandwidth as

(4.31)

\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}\coloneqq\max_{n=(k-2m,k+2m+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\Big(b(n)+1\Big)\,.

Then we can bound
f-Af
in the following way

(4.32)

\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\Big(\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}\Big)^{2}.

Step 4. Final estimate for
f-Af
.

We interpret
\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}
as a convolution. Let
\alpha:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
be the sequence
\alpha(n)=\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}
and
\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}
be the characteristic function of
(-2m-1,2m)
.
Then

(4.33)

\displaystyle\Big(\alpha\ast\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}\Big)(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(n)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}(k-n)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha(n)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(k-2m,k+2m+1)}(n)

(4.34)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\alpha(n)=\sum_{n\in F_{k}}\lVert P_{n}\rVert_{2}.

We can rewrite the error as

(4.35)

\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\Big(\alpha\ast\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}(k)\Big)^{2}.

The right-hand side can readily be estimated with Young’s inequality as

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\Big(\alpha\ast\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}(k)\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\bigr)\lVert\alpha\ast\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\bigr)\lVert\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}\rVert_{1}^{2}\lVert\alpha\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\bigr)(4m)^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lVert P_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}

since
\lVert\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{(-2m-1,2m)}\rVert_{1}=4m
.
Defining
D\coloneqq 4m\,\max\{2\pi\lVert g\rVert_{\infty},\lVert g^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\}
, we rewrite

(4.36)

\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}D^{2}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\bigr)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lVert P_{k}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Hypothesis (
4.6
) says that
\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}D^{2}\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}<1
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, therefore the error constant
\gamma^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}D^{2}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\bigl(\delta_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\mu_{\frac{k}{2}}^{2}\bigr)
satisfies
\gamma<1
. Hence,

(4.37)

\lVert f-Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\gamma^{2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\lVert P_{k}\rVert^{2}_{2}=\gamma^{2}\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Consequently, the operator
A
is invertible on
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
with an inverse
A^{-1}
satisfying

(4.38)

\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_{\text{op}}=\Bigl\lVert\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\text{Id}-A)^{n}\Bigr\rVert_{\text{op}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lVert\text{Id}-A\rVert^{n}_{\text{op}}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma},

Step 5. Reconstruction and sampling estimate.

It follows that
f=A^{-1}(Af)
with
Af
using only the samples on
\Lambda=\{x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\}
. Hence
f
is completely determined by
f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
j=1,\dots,j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})
.

The lower sampling inequality in (
4.7
), equivalently, the stability of the reconstruction, follows from

(4.39)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert A^{-1}Af\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_{\text{op}}^{2}\lVert P\rVert_{\text{op}}^{2}\Bigl\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}f\bigl(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\bigr)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}

(4.40)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}|f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}w_{\frac{k}{2},j}\,,

where the weights are given by

(4.41)

w_{\frac{k}{2},j}=\int\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}.

To get the right-hand side of the inequality (
4.7
) we use the previous computations and we obtain

(4.42)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}|f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}w_{\frac{k}{2},j}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}f\bigl(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\bigr)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}\Bigr\rVert_{2}^{2}

(4.43)

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\lVert f\rVert_{2}+\Bigl\lVert\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}f\bigl(x_{\frac{k}{2},j}\bigr)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{\frac{k}{2},j}-f\Bigr\rVert_{2}\Bigl)^{2}.

By the inequalities (
4.10
) and (
4.37
), we directly have

(4.44)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}|f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}w_{\frac{k}{2},j}\leq(1+\gamma)^{2}\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}

∎

Remarks
.

(i) Note that the separation of the points is not a requirement for the proof. In fact, a local variation of the density can be addressed by using the adaptive weights method (sometimes called “density compensation
factors”
[
28
]
).
If the sampling points are separated, i.e,
\inf_{\lambda,\mu\in\Lambda,\lambda\neq\mu}|\lambda-\mu|=\nu>0
, then
w_{\frac{k}{2},j}\geq\nu>0
and (
4.7
) becomes the standard sampling inequality

(4.45)

A^{\prime}\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\max}(\frac{k}{2})}|f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})|^{2}\leq B^{\prime}\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{ for all }f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})\,.

(ii) The same proof can be employed when substituting a Wilson orthonormal basis with a Wilson Riesz basis. In this case, we use the expansion
f=\sum_{n,l}\langle f,\tilde{\psi}_{n,l}\rangle\psi_{n,l}
, where
\{\tilde{\psi}_{n,l}\}
is the biorthogonal basis of
\{\psi_{n,l}\}
. In (
4.37
) we then obtain
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|P_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{n,l}|\langle f,\tilde{\psi}_{n,l}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|f\|_{2}^{2}
for
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
with the Riesz basis constant
B
. The rest of the proof is identical.

(iii) Except for very special cases, as in

[
8
,
30
,
48
]
, the adaptive weights method does not
produce optimal results and fails to come close to the necessary
sampling density. To stick to the essentials, we have therefore
not aimed to optimize (and complicate) the proof at all steps. The
estimates can be slightly sharpened in several places, but this
does not seem relevant in the numerical simulations.

(iv) In
[
7
]
we formulated a version that uses windows with

\supp(g)\subseteq[-1/2-\epsilon,1/2+\epsilon]
and found
a different sufficient condition for sampling. See
also
[
6
]
.

Example A
.

Consider the window
g(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi x)\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\chi$}_{[-1/2,1/2]}(x)
, as plotted in Figure
1
. Using the characterization (iii) of Theorem
2.1
it is easy to see that the associated Wilson system
\{\psi_{n,l}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Although the function
g
lacks differentiability at the points

\{-1/2,1/2\}
, the conclusion of Theorem
4.1

remains applicable. The proof requires only that

g
is
differentiable on each interval
(k/2,k/2+1/2)
with one-sided
derivatives at
k/2
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

This Wilson basis reveals an important point of Theorem
4.1
and shows that the local quantity
\mu_{k/2}=\max_{n=(k-2m,k+2m+1)\cap\mathbb{Z}}\big(b(n)+1\big)
must occur in the sufficient condition.

Since
\supp(g)=(-1/2,1/2)
, only two translates of
g
overlap. Thus the restriction of a general function
f=\sum_{n}P_{n}(x)g(x-n/2)
to
[0,1/2]
(or any other interval
[k/2,k/2+1/2]
) is

\displaystyle f\Big|_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}

\displaystyle=P_{0}(x)g(x)+P_{1}(x)g\Big(x-\frac{1}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=P_{0}(x)\cos\pi x+P_{1}(x)\sin\pi x

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-i\pi x}}{2}\Big(P_{0}(x)(e^{2\pi ix}+1)-iP_{1}(x)(e^{2\pi ix}-1)\Big)

If
\mathrm{deg}\,P_{0}=b(0)
and
\mathrm{deg}\,P_{1}=b(1)
, then
e^{-i\pi x}f\Big|_{[0,\frac{1}{2}]}
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree
\max\{b(0),b(1)\}+1
, which is precisely definition (
4.5
). The formulation of Theorem
4.1
is a far-reaching generalization of this observation and highlights the importance of the value
\mu_{k/2}
as a more accurate measure of local bandwidth compared to
b(k)
.

5.
Numerical simulations

In this section, we present various sampling scenarios and the results of numerical reconstructions. The main point is the numerical and pictorial illustration of our notion of variable bandwidth. We hope to convince the reader that the spaces
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
are meaningful and useful notions to capture variable bandwidth.

In principle the proof of Theorem
4.1
provides a reconstruction procedure as follows: from the samples
\{f(x_{\frac{k}{2},j})\}
we form the approximation operator
Af
and then invert
A
to obtain the reconstruction
f=A^{-1}(Af)
. Since this reconstruction requires the orthogonal projection
P
onto
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and the inversion of
A
, this method is not practical.

A second reconstruction method can be based on the sampling inequality (
4.7
). Since
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, (
4.7
) can be read as the frame inequality for the reproducing kernels associated to the sampling points. Then any off-the-shelf frame algorithm
[
19
]
yields a reconstruction.

In both cases one still has to deal with a numerical discretization from the infinite dimensional space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
(requiring infinitely many data) to some finite-dimensional problem that uses only finite information as input. One of the advantages of our model of variable bandwidth is that such a discretization is not necessary! Precisely, if the window
g
generating the Wilson basis has compact support and if the samples are taken from an interval
I
, then we may try to find a reconstruction or approximation in the finite-dimensional space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
. In this case, a reconstruction amounts to the solution of a finite-dimensional linear system and can be treated with numerical linear algebra.

5.1.
Matrix formulation of the reconstruction.

Assume that
g
has compact support in
[-m,m]
. Let
I=[\alpha,\beta]
and recall that

PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)=\spn\{\psi_{n,l}:\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I\}

is the local version of the variable bandwidth space
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
.
According to the proof of Theorem
3.1
,
PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
is a finite-dimensional subspace of
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
consisting of those functions
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
with
\supp(f)\subseteq I
.

Let
\Lambda=\{x_{j}:j=1,\dots,L\}
be a sampling set contained in
I
.

To reconstruct a function
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
from its samples
f(x_{j})
, we need to solve the linear system of equations

(5.1)

\sum_{\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I}c_{n,l}\psi_{n,l}(x_{j})=\sum_{n\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]\cap\mathbb{Z}}c_{n,0}\psi_{n,0}(x_{j})+\sum_{n/2\in[\alpha+m,\beta-m]\cap\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{l=1}^{b(n)}c_{n,l}\psi_{n,l}(x_{j})=f(x_{j})\,,

j=1,\dots,L
, for the coefficients
c_{n,l}
.

This linear system can be written in matrix notation as

(5.2)

Uc=y,

where
y
is the input sequence
\{f(x_{j})\}
, and the matrix
U
has the entries

(5.3)

U_{j,(n,l)}=\psi_{n,l}(x_{j}).

In general the samples are not exact and may contain measurement errors or noise, so that the input vector
y
is given by entries
y_{j}=f(x_{j})+\epsilon_{j}
. It may also happen that the sampled function is not contained in
PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
. In both cases we seek a function
\tilde{f}\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
that approximates the data vector
y
optimally. In the absence of additional information, such as sparsity, we use a least squares approach and seek
\tilde{f}
as the solution of

\displaystyle\tilde{f}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)}\sum_{j}|\tilde{f}(x_{j})-y_{j}|^{2}

(5.4)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{c_{n,l}}\sum_{j}\big|\sum_{n,l:\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I}c_{n,l}\psi_{n,l}(x_{j})-y_{j}\big|^{2}

(5.5)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{c_{n,l}}\|Uc-y\|_{2}^{2}\,.

The solution to the matrix least squares problem uses the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse matrix
U^{\dagger}
of
U
and is given by

(5.6)

c=U^{\dagger}y.

The approximating function
\tilde{f}\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
is then

(5.7)

\tilde{f}=\sum_{\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I}c_{n,l}\psi_{n,l}\,

and solves (
5.5
).
If the data are exact,
y_{j}=f(x_{j})
for
f\in PW_{b}^{2}(g,I)
, then

\tilde{f}=f
and the reconstruction is exact.

For a detailed exposition of the least squares approximation method, see
[
12
,
29
,
43
]
.

and then compute a reconstruction

from its samples. To assess the quality of the reconstruction, we evaluate the discretized error on a uniform grid
\{\gamma n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
on the interval of reconstruction
I
.
We use the relative errors in the
\ell^{2}
-norm and in the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm defined by

(5.8)

\displaystyle E(f,2)\coloneqq\frac{\Big\{\sum_{\gamma n\in I}|\tilde{f}(\gamma n)-f(\gamma n)|^{2}\Big\}^{1/2}}{\Big\{\sum_{\gamma n\in I}|f(\gamma n)|^{2}\Big\}^{1/2}},

(5.9)

\displaystyle E(f,\infty)\coloneqq\frac{\max_{\gamma n\in I}|\tilde{f}(\gamma n)-f(\gamma n)|}{\max_{\gamma n\in I}|f(\gamma n)|},

where the step-size
\gamma
is chosen to be of the order
\mathcal{O}(10^{-4})
.

Least squares approximation.
The numerical approximation is
carried out with MATLAB, and the solution to (
5.5
) is obtained with
the MATLAB function
pinv
. Since our aim is to validate the
notion of variable bandwidth with Wilson basis, we have not tried to
optimize the numerical part. To set up the sampling matrix
(
5.3
) we choose a suitable enumeration of the indices

(n,l)
satisfying
\supp(\psi_{n,l})\subseteq I
, namely,

i=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<n:\\
\supp(\psi_{r,l})\subseteq I\end{subarray}}b(r)+\#\{r\leq n:\supp(\psi_{r,0})\subseteq I\}+l\,.

The entries of (
5.3
) are identified by

U_{j,(n,l)}=U_{j,i}.

As always, the generation of
U
is the most expensive part of the
computation.

In conclusion, although chirps, in particular gravitational waves, are not in a variable bandwidth space, these spaces are convenient parametric models. In this particular application the structure of the signal is known in advance, therefore the use of
PW_{b}^{2}(g,\mathbb{R})
and of the associated sampling sets as in (
