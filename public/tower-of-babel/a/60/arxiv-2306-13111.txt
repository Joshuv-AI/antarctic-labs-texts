Relationships between the Phase Retrieval Problem and Permutation Invariant Embeddings
Radu Balan and Efstratios Tsoukanis

Abstract
This paper discusses the connection between the phase retrieval problem and permutation invariant embeddings. We show that the real phase retrieval problem for ℝ d / O ⁡ ( 1 ) \displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}/O(1) is equivalent to Euclidean embeddings of the quotient space ℝ 2 × d / S 2 \displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}/S_{2} performed by the sorting encoder introduced in an earlier work. In addition, this relationship provides us with inversion algorithms of the orbits induced by the group of permutation matrices.

In

\displaystyle H
denote a real or complex vector space and let
\displaystyle A=\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a frame for
\displaystyle H
. The phase retrieval problem asks whether the map
\displaystyle H\ni x\mapsto\alpha_{A}(x)=\{|{\lp x,a_{i}\rp}|\}_{i\in I}\in l^{2}(I)
determines
\displaystyle x
uniquely up to a unimodular scalar.

In this paper we focus on the finite dimensional real case of this problem (see also

), namely when
\displaystyle H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In this case, a frame
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{D}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is simply a spanning set. The group
\displaystyle O(1)=\{-1,+1\}
acts on
\displaystyle H
by scalar multiplication. Let
\displaystyle\hat{H}=H/O(1)
denote the quotient space induced by this action, where the equivalence classes (orbits) are

[x]=\{x,-x\}~~,~~{\text{for}}~x\neq 0~~,~~[x]=\{0\}~~,~~\text{for}~x=0.

The analysis operator for this frame is

T_{A}:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}~~,~~T_{A}(x)=({\lp x,a_{k}\rp})_{k=1}^{D}.

(1)

The relevant nonlinear map
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
is given by taking the absolute value of entries of
\displaystyle T_{A}
:

\alpha_{A}:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}~~,~~\alpha_{A}(x)=(|{\lp x,a_{k}\rp}|)_{k=1}^{D}.

(2)

Notice
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
produces a well-defined map on
\displaystyle\hat{H}
, which, with a slight abuse, but for simplicity of notation, will be denoted also by
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
. Thus
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}([x])=\alpha_{A}(x)
.

Another customary notation that is often employed: a frame is given either as an indexed set of vectors,
\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{D}\}
, or through the columns of a
\displaystyle d\times D
matrix
\displaystyle A
.
The matrix notation is not canonical, but this is not an issue here. We always identify
\displaystyle H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with its columns vector representation in its canonical basis.

Definition 1
.

We say that (the columns of a matrix)
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
form/is a phase retrievable frame, if
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}:\widehat{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(x)=(|{\lp x,a_{k}\rp}|)_{k=1}^{D}
is an injective map (on the quotient space).

it was recognized that the phase retrieval problem is a special case of Euclidean representations of metric spaces of orbits defined by certain unitary group actions on Hilbert spaces. Specifically, the setup is as follows. Let
\displaystyle V
denote a Hilbert space, and let
\displaystyle G
be a group acting unitarily on
\displaystyle V
. Let
\displaystyle\hat{V}=V/G
denote the metric space of orbits, where the quotient space is induced by the equivalence relation
\displaystyle x,y\in V
,
\displaystyle x\sim y
iff
\displaystyle y=g.x
, for some
\displaystyle g\in G
. Here
\displaystyle g.x
represents the action of the group element
\displaystyle g\in G
on vector
\displaystyle x
. For the purposes of this paper we specialize to the finite dimensional real case,
\displaystyle V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\displaystyle G=S_{n}
, is the group of
\displaystyle n\times n
permutation matrices acting on
\displaystyle V
by
left multiplication
.
Other cases are discussed in aforementioned papers. In particular, in
[
30
]
the authors have shown a deep connection to graph deep learning problems. In
[
31
]
, the authors linked this framework to certain graph matching problems and more.
The bi-Lipschitz Euclidean embedding problem for the finite dimensional case is as follows. Given
\displaystyle\hat{V}=V/G
, construct a map
\displaystyle\beta:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that, (i)
\displaystyle\beta(g.x)=\beta(x)
for all
\displaystyle g\in G
,
\displaystyle x\in V
, and (ii) for some
\displaystyle 0<A\leq B<\infty
, and for all
\displaystyle x,y\in V
,

A\,\disper([x],[y])\leq{\|\beta(x)-\beta(y)\|}\leq B\,\disper([x],[y])

(3)

where
\displaystyle\disper([x],[y])=\inf_{g\in G}{\|x-g.y\|}_{V}
is the
natural metric
on the quotient space
\displaystyle\hat{V}
.

In
[
30
]
the following embedding was introduced. Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}

be a fixed matrix (termed as
key
) whose columns are denoted by
\displaystyle a_{1},\ldots,a_{D}
. The induced encoder
\displaystyle\beta_{A}:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times D}
is defined by

\beta_{A}(X)=\downarrow(XA)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Pi_{1}Xa_{1}&\cdots&\Pi_{D}Xa_{D}\end{array}\right]

(4)

where
\displaystyle\Pi_{k}\in S_{n}
is the permutation matrix that sorts in decreasing order the vector
\displaystyle Xa_{k}
.
It was shown in
[
30
]
that, for
\displaystyle D
large enough,
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
provides a bi-Lipschitz Euclidean embedding of
\displaystyle\hat{V}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2
.

We say that
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}
is a universal key for
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
if
\displaystyle\beta_{A}:\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times D}
,
\displaystyle\beta_{A}(X)\!=\downarrow\!(XA)
is an injective map (on the quotient space).

The purpose of this paper is to show the equivalence between the real phase retrieval problem, specifically the embedding
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
, and the
permutation invariant embedding
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
defined above, in the special case
\displaystyle n=2
.

II
Main Results

Recall the Hilbert spaces
\displaystyle H=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\displaystyle V=\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}
. For
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times D}

recall also the encoders
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}:\hat{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
and

\displaystyle\beta_{A}:\hat{V}\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times D}
given respectively by
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(x)=(|{\lp x,a_{k}\rp}|)_{k\in[D]}
, and
\displaystyle\beta_{A}(X)\!=\downarrow\!(XA)
.
Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 3
.

In the case
\displaystyle n=2
, the following are equivalent.

1.

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
is injective, hence the columns of
\displaystyle A
form a phase retrievable frame;

2.

\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is injective, hence
\displaystyle A
is a universal key.

Remark 4
.

Perhaps it is not surprising that, if an equivalence between the phase retrieval problem and permutation invariant representations is possible, then this should occur for
\displaystyle n=2
. This statement is suggested by the observation that
\displaystyle O(1)
is isomorphic with
\displaystyle S_{2}
, the group of the
\displaystyle 2\times 2
permutation matrices. What is surprising that, in fact, the two embeddings are intimately related, as the proof and corollaries show.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
\displaystyle X\in V=\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}
. Denote by
\displaystyle x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its two rows transposed, that is

X=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}^{T}\\
x_{2}^{T}\end{array}\right].

Notice that, for each
\displaystyle k\in[D]
, the
\displaystyle k^{th}
column of
\displaystyle\beta_{A}(X)
is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\downarrow(Xa_{k})=\begin{bmatrix}max({\lp x_{1},a_{k}\rp},{\lp x_{2},a_{k}\rp})\\
min({\lp x_{1},a_{k}\rp},{\lp x_{2},a_{k}\rp})\end{bmatrix}.

The
key observations
are the following relationships between
\displaystyle min
,
\displaystyle max
, and the absolute value
\displaystyle|\cdot|
:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|u-v|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle max(u,v)-min(u,v)

\displaystyle\displaystyle u+v

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle max(u,v)+min(u,v)

\displaystyle\displaystyle max(u,v)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(u+v+|u-v|)

\displaystyle\displaystyle min(u,v)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(u+v-|u-v|)

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\,|u|-|v|\,|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle min(|u-v|,|u+v|)

In particular, these show that:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\beta_{A}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\cdot\downarrow(XA)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}|{\lp x_{1}-x_{2},a_{1}\rp}|,&\dots&,|{\lp x_{1}-x2,a_{D}\rp}|\\
{\lp x_{1}+x_{2},a_{1}\rp},&\dots&,{\lp x_{1}-x_{2},a_{D}\rp}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2}))^{T}\\
(T_{A}(x_{1}+x_{2}))^{T}\end{bmatrix}

Where,
\displaystyle T_{A}
was introduced in equation (
1
).

\displaystyle(1)\to(2):
Suppose that
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
is injective. Let
\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{T}\\
x_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
and

\displaystyle Y=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}^{T}\\
y_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
, such that
\displaystyle\beta_{A}(X)=\beta_{A}(Y)
.
Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\beta_{A}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\beta_{A}(Y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2}))^{T}\\
T_{A}(x_{1}+x_{2})^{T}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha_{A}(y_{1}-y_{2}))^{T}\\
T_{A}(y_{1}+y_{2})^{T}\end{bmatrix}.

But now,

\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2})=\alpha_{A}(y_{1}-y_{2}))\implies x_{1}-x_{2}=y_{1}-y_{2}\text{ or }x_{1}-x_{2}=y_{2}-y_{1}

and

T_{A}(x_{1}+x_{2})=T_{A}(y_{1}+y_{2})^{T}\implies x_{1}+x_{2}=y_{1}+y_{2}

Thus we have that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{1}&=&y_{1}\\
x_{2}&=&y_{2}\end{array}\right\}\text{ or }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{1}&=&y_{2}\\
x_{2}&=&y_{1}\end{array}\right\}

Either case means

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff X=Y\text{ or }X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}Y

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iff[X]=[Y]

So,
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is injective.

\displaystyle(2)\to(1):
Suppose that
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is injective. Let
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}(x)=\alpha_{A}(y)
, i.e.
\displaystyle|{\lp x,a_{k}\rp}|=|{\lp y,a_{k}\rp}|
,
\displaystyle\forall k\in[D]
.
Let
\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}x^{T}\\
-x^{T}\end{bmatrix}
and

\displaystyle Y=\begin{bmatrix}y^{T}\\
-y^{T}\end{bmatrix}
. Then,

\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\beta_{A}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{A}(2x)^{T}\\
T_{A}(0)^{T}\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{A}(2x)^{T}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

and

\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}\beta_{A}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{A}(2y)^{T}\\
T_{A}(0)^{T}\end{bmatrix}=2\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{A}(2y)^{T}\\
0\end{bmatrix}

Thus
\displaystyle\beta_{A}(X)=\beta_{A}(Y)
. Since
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is assumed injective, it follows that
\displaystyle X=Y
or
\displaystyle X=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}Y
. So,
\displaystyle x=y
or
\displaystyle x=-y
. We conclude that
\displaystyle[x]=[y]
, so
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
is injective.

If
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is injective, then
\displaystyle D\geq 2d-1
.

Corollary 6

If

Remark 7
.

Assume
\displaystyle D=2d-1
. Note the embedding dimension for
\displaystyle\hat{V}=\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}}
is
\displaystyle m=2(2d-1)=4d-2=2\,dim(V)-2
. In particular this shows the minimal dimension of bi-Lipschitz Euclidean embeddings may be smaller than twice the intrinsic dimension of the Hilbert space where the group acts on. Both papers
[
30
]
and
[

]
present (bi)Lipschitz embeddings into

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2\,dim(V)}

Remark 8
.

As was derived in the proof,
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
,
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
and
\displaystyle T_{A}
are intimately related:

\beta_{A}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1}^{T}\\
x_{2}^{T}\end{array}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&1\\
-1&1\end{array}\right]~\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{T}\\
T_{A}(x_{1}+x_{2})^{T}\end{array}\right]

(5)

In particular, any algorithm for solving the phase retrieval problem solves also the inversion problem for
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
. Let
\displaystyle\omega_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

denote a left inverse of
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
on the metric space
\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. This means
\displaystyle\omega_{A}(\alpha_{A}(x))\sim x
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}/O(1)
.
Denote by
\displaystyle T_{A}^{{\dagger}}
a left inverse of the analysis operator (e.g., the synthesis operator associated to the canonical dual frame). Thus
\displaystyle T_{A}^{{\dagger}}T_{A}=I_{d}
.
Then an inverse for
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
is:

\beta_{A}^{-1}(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}T_{A}^{\dagger}(y_{2})+\omega_{A}(y_{1})\\
T_{A}^{\dagger}(y_{2})-\omega_{A}(y_{1})\end{array}\right]

(6)

where
\displaystyle Y=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}^{T}\\
y_{2}^{T}\end{array}\right]
.

Remark 9
.

Equations (
6
) suggest a lower dimensional embedding than
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
. Specifically, first we compute the average
\displaystyle y_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}+x_{2})
which is of size
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and then encode the difference
\displaystyle x_{1}-x_{2}
using
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
,
\displaystyle y_{2}=\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2})
. We obtain the following modified encoder,
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d+D}
:

\tilde{\beta}_{A}(x)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}+x_{2})^{T}&\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{T}\end{array}\right].

(7)

With the
\displaystyle\omega_{A}
left inverse of
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
, the inverse of
\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{A}
is given by:

\tilde{\beta}_{A}^{-1}(Y)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}y_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\omega_{A}(y_{2})\\
y_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\omega_{A}(y_{2})\end{array}\right]

(8)

where
\displaystyle y_{1}=Y(1:d)
and
\displaystyle y_{2}=Y(d+1:d+D)
.
In the case when
\displaystyle D=D_{min}=2d-1
, the minimal embedding dimension is
\displaystyle m=d+D=3d-1
(instead of
\displaystyle 4d-2
or
\displaystyle 4d=2\,dim(V)
).

Remark 11
.

The optimal Lipschitz constants for the map
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
were obtained in
[
33
,
34
]
, including the optimizers. However, for reader’s convenience, we prefer to give direct proofs of these results.

Proof.

1.

Upper Lipschitz constants.

(i) Let
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\alpha_{A}(x)-\alpha_{A}(y)\|}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}||{\lp a_{i},x\rp}|-|{\lp a_{i},y\rp}||^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\scriptstyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left(|{\lp a_{i},x-y\rp}|^{2},|{\lp a_{i},x+y\rp}|^{2}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\leq\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{D}|{\lp a_{i},x-y\rp}|^{2},\sum_{i=1}^{D}|{\lp a_{i},x+y\rp}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)\dispr([x],[y])^{2}.

So
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)
is an upper Lipschitz bound for the map
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
.
Now for
\displaystyle x_{\max}=u,y_{\max}=0
notice that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\alpha(x_{\max})-\alpha(y_{\max})\|}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}|{\lp a_{i},u\rp}|^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}(A){\|u\|}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)\dispr([x_{\max}],[y_{\max}])^{2}.

Thus, the upper Lipschitz constant
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)
is in fact optimal (tight).

(ii) Map
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
. Let
\displaystyle X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times D}
and
\displaystyle P_{0}\in S_{2}
be a permutation that achieves the distance between
\displaystyle X
and
\displaystyle Y
, i.e.
\displaystyle{\|X-P_{0}Y\|}=\disper([X],[Y])
. Note that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\beta_{A}(X)-\beta_{A}(Y)\|}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{D}{\|(\Pi_{k}X-\Xi_{k}Y)a_{k}\|}^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle=\sum_{k=1}^{D}{\|(\Xi_{k}^{T}\Pi_{k}X-Y)a_{k}\|}^{2}

for some
\displaystyle\Pi_{k},\Xi_{k}\in S_{2}
that align the vectors.
From rearrangement lemma we have that

\scriptstyle{\|(\Pi_{k}X-\Xi_{k}Y)a_{k}\|}\leq{\|(X-P_{0}Y)a_{k}\|},~\forall k\in[D]

so,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\sum_{k=1}^{D}{\|(\Xi_{k}^{T}\Pi_{k}X-Y)a_{k}\|}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\|A\|}^{2}{\|{\|X-P_{0}Y\|}\|}^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\sigma_{1}^{2}(A)\disper([X],[Y])^{2}.

Therefore, we conclude that
\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(A)
is an upper Lipschitz constant for map
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
.
We still need to show that this bound is achieved (i.e., it is optimal).
For
\displaystyle X_{\max}
and
\displaystyle Y_{\max}
defined in part 1) of
10

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\beta_{A}(X_{\max})-\beta_{A}(Y_{\max})\|}^{2}={\|\beta_{A}(X_{\max})\|}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{D}{\lp u,a_{k}\rp}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}(A).

and
\displaystyle\disper(X_{\max},Y_{\max})=1
.
Thus
\displaystyle B_{0}
is the optimal Lipschitz constant both for
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
and for
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
.

2.

Lower Lipschitz constants.

(i) Let
\displaystyle x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define the auxiliary set

\scriptstyle S=S(x,y):=\{j\in[D]~:|~{\lp x-y,a_{j}\rp}|\leq|{\lp x+y,a_{j}\rp}|\}

Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\alpha(x)-\alpha(y)\|}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}\left||{\lp a_{i},x\rp}|-|{\lp a_{i},y\rp}|\right|^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\scriptstyle\sum_{i\in S}|{\lp a_{i},x-y\rp}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in S^{c}}|{\lp a_{i},x+y\rp}|^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[S])+\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[S^{c}])\dispr([x],[y])^{2}\geq A_{0}^{2}\dispr([x],[y])^{2}.

So
\displaystyle A_{0}
is a lower Lipschitz bound for
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
, but we still need to show that it is optimal.

Let
\displaystyle I_{0}
be the optimal partition, and let
\displaystyle u_{1}
,
\displaystyle u_{2}
be normalized left singular vectors as in the statement of
10
. Then:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\|\alpha_{A}(u_{1}+u_{2})-\alpha_{A}(u_{1}-u_{2})\|}^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}||{\lp a_{i},u_{1}+u_{2}\rp}|-|{\lp a_{i},u_{1}-u_{2}\rp}||^{2}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\scriptstyle\sum_{i=1}^{D}\min\left(|{\lp a_{i},2u_{2}\rp}|^{2},|{\lp a_{i},2u_{1}\rp}|^{2}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\leq 4\,\left(\sum_{i\in I_{0}}|{\lp a_{i},u_{1}\rp}|^{2}+\sum_{i\in I_{0}^{c}}|{\lp a_{i},u_{2}\rp}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle=4(\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[I_{0}])+\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[I_{0}^{c}]))=A_{0}^{2}\dispr([u_{1}+u_{2}],[u_{1}-u_{2}])^{2},

where we used again that
\displaystyle|\,|a|-|b|\,|=min(|a-b|,|a+b|)
for any two real numbers
\displaystyle a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, and, for the inequality, at every
\displaystyle i\in[D]
we made a choice between the two terms. Since the reverse inequality is also true, it follows that
\displaystyle x_{\min}=u_{1}+u_{2}
and
\displaystyle y_{\min}=u_{1}-u_{2}
achieve the lower bound
\displaystyle A_{0}
for
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
.

(ii) Consider now the map
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
. Let
\displaystyle X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times d}
and define the auxiliary set

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle S=S(X,Y):=\{

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle j\in[D]~:|~{\lp x_{1}-x_{2}-y_{1}+y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\scriptstyle\leq|{\lp x_{1}-x_{2}+y_{1}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|\}

Then, using
Equation

5
we have that

\scriptstyle{\|\beta_{A}(X)-\beta_{A}(Y)\|}^{2}=

\scriptstyle\hskip-8.53581pt\frac{1}{2}\left({\|\alpha_{A}(x_{1}-x_{2})-\alpha_{A}(y_{1}-y_{2})\|}^{2}+{\|T_{A}(x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2})\|}^{2}\right)=

\scriptstyle\hskip-8.53581pt\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in S}|{\lp x_{1}-x_{2}-y_{1}+y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+|{\lp x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+

\scriptstyle\hskip-14.22636pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in S^{c}}|{\lp x_{1}-x_{2}+y_{1}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+|{\lp x_{1}+x_{2}-y_{1}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}=

\scriptstyle=\sum_{j\in S}|{\lp x_{1}-y_{1},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+|{\lp x_{2}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+

\scriptstyle+\sum_{j\in S^{c}}|{\lp x_{1}-y_{2},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}+|{\lp x_{2}-y_{1},a_{j}\rp}|^{2}\geq

\scriptstyle\geq\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[S])\left({\|x_{1}-y_{1}\|}^{2}+{\|x_{2}-y_{2}\|}^{2}\right)+

\scriptstyle+\sigma_{d}^{2}(A[S^{c}])\left({\|x_{1}-y_{2}\|}^{2}+{\|x_{2}-y_{1}\|}^{2}\right)\geq

\scriptstyle\geq A_{0}^{2}\disper([X],[Y])^{2}.

Therefore
\displaystyle A_{0}
is a lower Lipschitz constant for
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
.

It remained to prove that this bound is tight, i.e., it is achieved. Let
\displaystyle X_{\min}
and
\displaystyle Y_{\min}
be as in the statement of
10
.
Then

\scriptstyle{\|\beta_{A}(X_{\min})-\beta_{A}(Y_{\min})\|}^{2}=

\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\|\alpha_{A}(u_{1}+u_{2})-\alpha_{A}(u_{1}-u_{2})\|}^{2}+{\|T_{A}(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{1}-u_{2})\|}^{2}\right)=

\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}\left({\|\alpha_{A}(u_{1}+u_{2})-\alpha_{A}(u_{1}-u_{2})\|}^{2}\right)=A_{0}^{2}\disper([X_{\min}],[Y_{\min}])^{2}

where the last equality follows from the fact that the lower Lipschitz constant of
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
is
achieved by
\displaystyle u_{1}+u_{2}
and
\displaystyle u_{1}-u_{2}
, and the fact that

\displaystyle\disper([X_{\min}],[Y_{\min}])^{2}=2
.

So
\displaystyle A_{0}
is indeed the optimal lower Lipschitz constant for
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
.

∎

III
Conclusion

In this paper we analyzed two representation problems, one arising in the phase retrieval problem
and the other one in the context of permutation invariant representations. We showed that the real phase retrieval problem in a finite dimensional vector space
\displaystyle H
is entirely equivalent to the permutation invariant representations for the space
\displaystyle V=\mathbb{R}^{2\times dim(H)}
.
Our analysis proved that phase retrievability is equivalent to the universal key property in the case of encoding
\displaystyle{2\times d}
matrices.
This result is derived based on the lattice space structure
\displaystyle(\mathbb{R},+,min,max)
.
It is still an open problem to understand the relationship between
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
and
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
in the case
\displaystyle n>2
.
A related problem is the implementation of the sorting operator using a neural network that has ReLU as activation function (or, even the absolute value
\displaystyle|\cdot|
).
Efficient implementations of such operator may yield novel relationships between
\displaystyle\alpha_{A}
and
\displaystyle\beta_{A}
, in the case
\displaystyle n\geq 3
.
