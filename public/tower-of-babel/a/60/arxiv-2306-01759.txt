Unlikely intersection in higher-dimensional formal groups
MABUD ALI SARKAR ∗, Absos Ali Shaikh †

Abstract
. In this work, we identify a certain family of higher-dimensional formal groups over the ring of p p -adic integers such that any two formal groups in that class coincide if they share infinitely many torsion points. As a useful application, we provide a sufficient condition for two abelian varieties to be isogenous just by looking at their p p -power torsion points.

Introduction and Motivation

The study of the torsion points of formal groups has achieved significant attention over the past few decades due to its relevance in number theory. Lubin and Tate
[
17
, 1964]
pioneered the groundwork by establishing a remarkable connection to local class field theory, which has since been expanded upon by several authors, including but not limited to Iwasawa
[
14

[

]

[

]

[

[

[

constructed a tamely ramified extension over the completion of the maximal unramified extension of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
by employing the
p

[

showed that under a certain condition, the torsion points of a formal group can determine the formal group itself. Some similar results were reported by Bogomolov and Tschinkel
[
5

[

In this report, we study a similar question for higher dimensional formal groups. Then we apply our result to determine an isogeny class of abelian varieties only by examining at their
p

Let

be the set of torsion points of

[

The preceding result is devoted to one-dimensional formal groups. In Section

Theorem 1.2

If
F
and

are

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that
\text{Tors}(F)\cap\text{Tors}(G)

Determining an isogeny class of an abelian variety, which is challenging, is a significant and highly sought-after problem in number theory and algebraic geometry because it has several useful implications such as to understand the structure of the moduli space of abelian varieties and the Galois representations associated to an abelian variety. Bogomolov and Tschinkel
[
5
, Chap. 4]
proved that if two elliptic curves over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
shares infinitely many torsion points, then they are isomorphic, and thus isogenous. However, there is no such statement for general abelian varieties. As an application of our Theorem

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be
d
-dimensional simple and full integral models of abelian varieties over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with complex multipication and denote by
\mathcal{A}[p^{n}]

\mathcal{B}[p^{n}]

\mathcal{A}
and

respectively and
\text{Tors}(\mathcal{A}):=\bigcup_{n}\mathcal{A}[p^{n}]

\text{Tors}(\mathcal{A})\cap\text{Tors}(\mathcal{A})
is infinite, then
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are isogenous.

3.
Unlikely intersection in higher-dimensional formal groups

In this section, we prove the main Theorem
1.2
regarding how the torsion points of a formal group determines the formal group itself. We show that the techniques of
[
4
]
for 1-dimensional formal groups can be useful for higher-dimensional formal groups also but with different ingredients. As an application, we prove Theorem
1.3
at the end of this section.

Definition 3.1
.

If
F
is a
d
-dimensional formal group over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with a multiplication-by-
p
endomorphism
[p]_{F}
, then the set of torsion points of

is given by

\text{Tors}(F):=\bigcup_{n}\{\xi\in\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}~|~[p^{n}]_{F}(\xi)=0\},~\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{d}).

The height of a
n
-dimensional formal group is defined as follows:

Definition 3.2
.

[
10
, Sec. 18.3.8]

Let
F(X,Y)
be an
n
-dimensional formal group
F(X,Y)
over the ring of
p
-adic integers
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with endomorphism
[p]_{F}(X)=(f_{1}(X),\cdots,f_{n}(X))
(mod
p
). If the ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]]
is a finitely generated and free module over the subring
\mathbb{F}_{p}[[f_{1}(X),\cdots,f_{n}(X)]]
of rank

, then

is the height of
F
. We use the notation

for finite field with

Consider the absolute Galois group
G_{{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}}=\text{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}/\mathbb{Q}_{p})
.
If

is a

-dimensional formal group of height

, then the

-adic Tate module

is a free

-module with rank

\rho_{F}:\text{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}/\mathbb{Q}_{p})\to\text{GL}_{h}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})

-vector space

is a

-crystalline representation.
Then we have the following results:

Lemma 3.3
.

If
F
is a
d
-dimensional simple formal group then

is a semi-simple
G_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
-module.

Proof.

By Tate
[
25
, Proposition 1]
, we know that the category of divisible commutative formal Lie groups are in equivalence with the category of connected
p
-divisible groups. While by Kisin
[
15
]
, we know that the category of the connected
p
-divisible groups are in equivalence with the category of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-lattices of
G_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
crystalline representations with Hodge-Tate weights 0, 1 and with positive Frobenius slopes. So we have the following diagram of equivalence of categories:

\begin{matrix}\{\text{$\mathbb{Z}_{p}$-lattices of $G_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}$ crystalline representations with HT weights 0, 1 and Frobenius slopes$>0$}\}\\
\big\updownarrow\\
\{\text{$p$-divisible groups}\}/_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\\
\big\updownarrow\\
\{\text{divisible commutative formal Lie groups}\}/_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\par\end{matrix}

Since
F
is simple formal group, its Tate module

can not have a
G_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}
-stable submodule. Because otherwise by the equivalence of the above categories that submodule of

would come from a subgroup of

, which is not possible as

is simple. As

is a simple

-module,

can not have a

-stable subspace because if there is a subspace

would be a submodule of

is simple. This proves that

is a simple

-module.
∎

If

The absolute Galois group

Definition 3.5
.

The Jacobian of a power series
h(X)=(h_{1}(X),\cdots,h_{d_{1}}(X))\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[X]]^{d_{1}}
,
X=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d_{2}})
is a

matrix defined by

J(h):=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}h_{i}\right],~1\leq i\leq d_{1},~1\leq j\leq d_{2}.

In this article, we often denote the Jacobian of such a function

J_{0}(h):=J(h)(0)=\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}h_{i}(0)\right].

Proposition 3.6
.

The sufficient condition for
h\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[[X]]^{d},~X=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})
with
h(0)=0
being invertible is that
J_{0}(h)\in\text{GL}_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times})
.

Proof.

Because
h(X)
is invertible, there must exist some inverse
h^{-1}(X)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[[X]]^{d}
for
h^{-1}(0)=0
so that
h(h^{-1}(X))=X
. By chain rule,
J(h(h^{-1}(X))=J(h)(X)J(h^{-1})(X)=J(X)
such that evaluating at
0
yields
J_{0}(h)J_{0}(h^{-1})=I_{d}
, the
d\times d
identity matrix
I_{d}
. As a result, both
J_{0}(h),J_{0}(h^{-1})\in\text{GL}_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times})
.

Conversely, suppose
J_{0}(h)\in\text{GL}_{d}(\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times})
. The construction of an inverse
h^{-1}(X)
is sufficient. Induction will be used. If we use the linear algebraic expression
f^{1}(X)=J_{0}(h^{-1})X\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[[X]]^{d}
, we get

h(f^{1}(X))\equiv X~(\text{mod deg 2}).

Suppose the polynomial
f^{n}(X)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[[X]]^{d}
with
f^{n}(X)\equiv f^{n-1}(X)
(mod deg
n
) fulfilling

h(f^{n}(X))\equiv X~(\text{mod deg n+1})

for

. The following polynomial

must then have the formula

f^{n+1}(X)=f^{n}(X)+R^{n+1}(\text{remainder}),

where
R^{n+1}(X)\equiv 0
(mod deg

) and
R^{n+1}
is actually a homogeneous polynomial with degree

.
Since
J_{0}(h)
is invertible, we can solve for

from the condition

h(f^{n+1}(X))=h(f^{n}(X)+R^{n+1}))\equiv h(f^{n}(X))+J_{0}(h)R^{n+1}(X)\equiv X~(\text{mod deg}~n+2)

giving us
R^{n-1}\equiv J_{0}(h)^{-1}(X-h(f^{n}(X)))\equiv 0
(mod deg
n+1
). So we derive the inverse of
h(X)
via induction on
f^{n}(X)
. The proof is now complete.
∎

The above result says if

If

\displaystyle u(X)=(px_{1},px_{2})~\text{(mod deg.}\ 2)\ \text{and}\ u(X)\equiv(x_{2}^{h_{1}},x_{1}^{h_{2}})\ (\text{mod}\ p),

Before we proceeding further, we make the following hypothesis to simplify our computation:

Hypothesis 1
.

The linear coefficients of the stable endomorphisms in our considerations are scalar-diagonal matrices.

We now look forward to prove that if a power series commutes with an endomorphism of a formal group, then it must itself be an endomorphism of that formal group.

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume that
u
is a stable endomorphism of a
d
-dimensional commutative formal group
F(X_{1},X_{2})
. If
w(X)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]^{d}
with
w(0)=0
such that
w\circ u=u\circ w
, then
w\in\text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(F)
.

Proof.

The formal group
F
in our consideration is commutative, and thus, we have a logarithm. The logarithm, say
L
, is an isomorphism between
F
and the additive group, say
\mathcal{A}
, of same dimension. The defining group laws for
\mathcal{A}
are just addition of separate variables and its endomorphisms are the linear maps, so square matrices. That is, for
X=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})
and
Y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{d})
, we have
\mathcal{A}(X,Y)=X+Y

and an endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
is a
d
-tuple power series
\varphi(X)=(\varphi_{1}(X),\cdots,\varphi_{d}(X))
such that

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathcal{A}(X,Y))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\varphi(X),\varphi(Y))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\varphi(X+Y)

\displaystyle=\varphi(X)+\varphi(Y)

Thus, any such
\varphi(X)
is given by
\varphi(X)=MX
, where
M
is a
d\times d
scalar-diagonal matrix with entries in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Because of the above isomorphism
L
, we can prove many facts about an endomorphism
u
of
F
by proving the corresponding fact about the endomorphism
L\circ u\circ L^{-1}
of
\mathcal{A}
. To be precise, using the logarithm
L
, we translate the problem from the formal group
F
to the additive formal group
\mathcal{A}
, which is more easier to handle.

Thus, we are in a situation where we want to show that a
d
-tuple power series without constant term that commute with a
stable

d
-tuple power series
\varphi(X)=MX=(\alpha x_{1},\cdots,\alpha x_{d})
,
M=\text{diag}(\alpha,\cdots,\alpha)
of the additive formal group
\mathcal{A}
must necessarily be of the form

\theta(X)=(\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}x_{d},\cdots,\beta_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}x_{d}),~\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p},

which is straightforward to verify. Therefore, the corresponding endomorphism for the original formal group
F
would be
L^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ L
.

The next part of the proof is a smooth sailing. We may assume inductively that we have a power series that is linear modulo degree
m
, i.e., modulo
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})^{m}
and thus writable in the form

\Theta(X)=(\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}x_{d}+\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d}),\cdots,\beta_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}x_{d}+\mathcal{E}_{d}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})),

where
\mathcal{E}_{i},~1\leq i\leq d
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
. Now if we compute the compositions
\varphi\circ\Theta
and
\Theta\circ\varphi
, the first have
“correction terms”

\alpha\mathcal{E}_{i},~1\leq i\leq d
and the second have
“correction terms”

\alpha^{m}\mathcal{E}_{i},~1\leq i\leq d
, because of homogeneity of
\mathcal{E}_{i},~1\leq i\leq d
. Finally,
\varphi\circ\Theta=\Theta\circ\varphi
implies
\alpha\mathcal{E}_{i}=\alpha^{m}\mathcal{E}_{i}
for all
i
. By the definition of stable power series,
\alpha
is neither zero nor roots of unity and therefore
\alpha^{m}\neq\alpha
, which forces
\mathcal{E}_{i}(X)=0
for all
i
. Thus, we have

\Theta(X)=(\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}x_{d},\cdots,\beta_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{d}x_{d})=\theta(X),

which is obviously an endomorphism of
\mathcal{A}
. This completes the proof.

∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
u(X)\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[X]]^{d},~X=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})
with
u(0)=0
be a stable power series. A power series
h(X)\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[X]]^{d}
with
h(0)=0
such that
h\circ u=u\circ h
is determined by
J_{0}(h)
, the Jacobian of
h
at
0
.

Proof.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be two power series in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}[[X]]^{d}
such that
J_{0}(H_{1})=J_{0}(H_{2})
and they both commute with
u
. Let
g_{m}
be the sum of the terms of
H_{i},i=1,\cdots,d
of total degree
\leq m
. We can decompose
H_{i}=g_{m}+\Delta_{i},i=1,\cdots,d
, where the remainder series
\Delta_{i}
has total-degree
\geq m+1
. Since by Hypothesis
1

J_{0}(u)
is a diagonal matrix and since
g_{m}
is a column vector, it is worth noting that
g_{m}\cdot\Delta_{i}=\Delta_{i}^{T}\cdot g_{m}
. Therefore

(3.1)

\displaystyle(g_{m}+\Delta_{i})\circ u=g_{m}\circ u+\Delta_{i}\circ u\equiv g_{m}\circ u+\Delta_{i}^{T}\cdot J_{0}(u)^{m+1}~\text{mod deg~}(m+2),

(3.2)

\displaystyle u\circ(g_{m}+\Delta_{i})=u\circ g_{m}+u\circ\Delta_{i}\equiv u\circ g_{m}+\Delta_{i}^{T}\cdot J_{0}(u)~\text{mod deg~}(m+2).

Since
u\circ h=h\circ u
, from equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle g_{m}\circ u+J_{0}(u)^{m+1}\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv u\circ g_{m}+J_{0}(u)\Delta_{i}~\text{mod deg~}(m+2)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\Delta_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv(u\circ g_{m}-g_{m}\circ u)\left(J_{0}(u)^{m+1}-J_{0}(u)\right)^{-1}~\text{mod deg~}(m+2).

But
u
being a stable power series ensures that the eigenvalues of
J_{0}(u)
are neither zero nor roots of unity. Therefore,
J_{0}(u)^{m+1}-J_{0}(u)
is non-singular, and hence
\Delta_{i}
is defined. If
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
agree in total-degrees
\leq m
, they must also agree in total-degrees
\leq m+1
. Induction on
m
yields
H_{1}=H_{2}
.
∎

It is worth noting that, in the above result, we must assume
u
is stable, otherwise we can not solve
\Delta_{i}
. The following example clarifies it:

Example 3.11
.

Let
u(X)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma x\\
\gamma^{p^{3}}y\end{pmatrix}
and
h(X)=\begin{pmatrix}x+\frac{1}{p}y^{p^{2}}+\frac{1}{p^{2}}x^{p^{5}}\\
y+\frac{1}{p}x^{p^{3}}+\frac{1}{p^{2}}x^{p^{5}}\end{pmatrix}
, where
X=(x,y)
and
\gamma
is
(p^{5}-1)^{th}
root of unity, i.e.,
\gamma^{p^{5}}=\gamma
. One can check that
u\circ h=h\circ u
.
Let us decompose

\displaystyle h(X)=\begin{pmatrix}x+\frac{1}{p}y^{p^{2}}\\
y+\frac{1}{p}x^{p^{3}}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{p^{2}}x^{p^{5}}\\
\frac{1}{p^{2}}x^{p^{5}}\end{pmatrix}=g_{p^{5}-1}+\Delta,

where
g_{p^{5}-1}
is the sum of the terms upto degree
p^{5}-1
and
\Delta
is the remainder term of degree greater or equal to
p^{5}
. The jacobian of
u
is
J_{0}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&\gamma^{p^{3}}\end{pmatrix}
, a digonal matrix. So
u
is not a
stable
power series because the diagonals of
J_{0}(u)
contains roots of unity (see Definition
3.7
).

Since
\Delta
is a
2\times 1
matrix, we have
J_{0}(u)\cdot\Delta=\Delta^{T}\cdot J_{0}(u)
, where
\Delta^{T}
is the transpose of
\Delta
. We note that

\displaystyle h\circ u=(g_{p^{5}-1}+\Delta)\circ u=g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u+\Delta\circ u

\displaystyle=g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}x^{p^{5}}\\
\frac{1}{4}y^{p^{5}}\end{pmatrix}\circ\begin{pmatrix}\gamma x\\
\gamma^{p^{3}}y\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u++\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}(\gamma x)^{p^{5}}\\
\frac{1}{4}(\gamma^{p^{3}}y)^{p^{5}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u+\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{p^{5}}x^{p^{5}}\\
\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{p^{8}}y^{p^{5}}\end{pmatrix}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow h\circ u

\displaystyle\equiv g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u+\Delta^{T}\cdot J_{0}(u)^{p^{5}}~(\text{mod degree~}p^{5}+1).

(3.4)

\displaystyle u\circ h=u\circ(g_{p^{5}-1}+\Delta)=u\circ g_{p^{5}-1}+u\circ\Delta\equiv u\circ g_{p^{5}-1}+\Delta^{T}\cdot J_{0}(u)~(\text{mod deg.}~p^{5}+1).

From equations (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we have

\Delta^{T}=(u\circ g_{p^{5}-1}-g_{p^{5}-1}\circ u)\left(J_{0}(u)^{p^{5}}-J_{0}(u)\right)^{-1}.

It is worth noting that
\Delta
doesn’t exists because

J_{0}(u)^{p^{5}}-J_{0}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&\gamma^{p^{3}}\end{pmatrix}^{p^{5}}-\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&\gamma^{p^{3}}\end{pmatrix}=0,~\text{since}~\gamma^{p^{5}}=\gamma.

Thus in Proposition
3.10
, we must assume
u
is a stable power series to avoid such situation.

Corollary 3.11.1
.

Let
u(X)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]^{d},~X=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})
with
u(0)=0
be a stable power series. Then, a power series
h(X_{1},X_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X_{1},X_{2}]]^{d}
with
h(0)=0
such that
h\circ u=u\circ h
is determined by the Jacobian
J_{0}(h)
of
h
at
0
.

Proof.

Let
h(X_{1},X_{2})=\left(h_{1}(X_{1},X_{2}),~h_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\right)
, where
X_{1}=(x_{11},\cdots,x_{1d}),X_{2}=(x_{21},\cdots,x_{2d})
, say.
Then the Jacobian of
h
is given by

J_{0}(h)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|ccc}\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial x_{11}}(0)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial x_{1d}}(0)&\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial x_{21}}(0)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial x_{2d}}(0)\\
&&&&\\
\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{11}}(0)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{1d}}(0)&\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{21}}(0)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_{2}}{\partial x_{2d}}(0)\end{array}\right)_{2,~2d}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{\partial h}{\partial X_{1}}(0)&\frac{\partial h}{\partial X_{2}}(0)\end{array}\right)_{2,~2d}

Since the partial Jacobians
\frac{\partial h}{\partial X_{i}}(0),~i=1,2
plays the role of block matrices of order
(2,d)
in the Jacobian matrix of
h
, the proof follows immediately by Proposition
3.10
.
∎

Thus, a power series which commutes with another power series can be determined by its Jacobian. In this regard, we note that an endomorphism of a formal group is determined by its Jacobian matrix.

3.11.1
, we can also cook up an alternative demonstration (see Subsection
3.1
) of Proposition
3.9
.

We now show that the number of the torsion points of the endomorphisms of our formal groups is finite.
For technical reasons, the rest of the article considers a class of higher-dimensional formal groups that are
simple
and have
full-CM
. If
F
is a simple higher-dimensional formal group, then by Definition
2.7
, it can’t be written as a product of lower dimensional formal groups. Furthermore, we prove that if
F
is a
full
formal group of finite height, say
h
, with complex multiplication then any endomorphism of
F
has a finite number of zeros.

Lemma 3.12
.

If
F
is a
d
-dimensional full-CM formal group over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
with finite height
h
, then
\text{ker}([p^{n}]_{F})
have
p^{hn}
elements in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
.

Proof.

When
F
is one-dimensional, Weierstrass theorem implies
[p^{n}]_{F}(X)
have
p^{hn}
zeros.

So, consider
F
is a full-CM formal group of dimension
d
of height h. Consider the
d
-tuple power series
[p^{n}]_{F}(X)
.
Let
\mathfrak{J}
be the ideal of
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]
generated by the power series
[p^{n}]_{F}(X)
. Then the quotient
ring
\mathfrak{R}:=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]/\mathfrak{J}
is free of rank
p^{hn}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, by
[
10
, Appendix B.2, page 524]
. From the point of view of algebraic geometry, we know that the points of an algebraic set are in bijection with the maximal ideals of a ring whose spectrum is that algebraic set (see Appendix
A
). Therefore, the points of
\text{ker}([p^{n}]_{F})
corresponds exactly to the maximal ideals of
\text{spec}(\mathfrak{R})
. In our case, the algebraic set is the zero-set
\text{ker}([p^{n}]_{F})
. Now since
\mathfrak{R}
is of dimension
p^{hn}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the tensor product
\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is also of dimension
p^{hn}
over
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, because the dimension is the same as the number of maximal ideals of
\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
. However, for higher-dimensional formal group, we need the algebraically closed field
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
to include the points that are not
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-rational. So tensor with
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
to conclude the ring
\mathfrak{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
is of dimension
p^{hn}
over
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. Thus
\text{ker}([p^{n}]_{F})
have
p^{hn}
elements.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

There is a bad case, however. In the above lemma, consider the quotient ring
\mathfrak{R}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]/(X^{2})
, which is a two-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
-vector space. But it has only one maximal ideal
(X)
corresponding to a point at the origin, while the defining equation
X^{2}
has a multiple (double) zero. However, such bad behavior we have excluded in our multidimensional formal group case. For example, our
F
have simple zeros, proved in
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
. In other words, there are no nilpotent elements in the quotient ring
\mathfrak{R}
.

Corollary 3.13.1
.

If
F
is a full-CM formal group, then any endomorphism
f(X)
of
F
has only finitely many zeros in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.12
, the multiplication-by-
p
endomorphism
[p]_{F}(X)
of the formal group
F
has only finitely many zeros in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
. If
f(X)
is any other endomorphism of
F
, then there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[m]_{F}(X)=f(X)
. Thus,
f(X)
has only finitely many zeros in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
because of the
m
-torsion map
[m]_{F}(X)
.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

If
F
is a
d
-dimensional simple full-CM formal group over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and if

is such that

and

for all

, where

is infinite. Assume that

has only finitely many zeros, then

.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, there exists
\sigma\in\text{Gal}(\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}/\mathbb{Q}_{p})
and a stable endomorphism

of
F
with

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sigma(Z)=u(Z)~\text{for all~}Z\in\text{Tors}(F).

Since
h(Z)\in\text{Tors}(F)
, for every
Z\in\mathcal{Z}
, substituting

in equation (

) yields

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sigma(h(Z))=u(h(Z))~\text{for all~}Z\in\text{Tors}(F).

Moreover, according to equation (

\sigma(h(Z))=h(\sigma(Z))=h(u(Z)
holds true for

. Thus, we derive from equation (

)

\displaystyle u(h(Z))=h(u(Z))~\text{for all~}Z\in\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle u\circ h-h\circ u=0~\text{on}~\mathcal{Z}.

Since

is simple full-CM formal group, by Corollary
3.13.1
any endomorphism of

has finitely many zeros in
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
, and hence

\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
. Because
\mathcal{Z}
is infinite, and both

\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
, we get

.
∎

\displaystyle J_{0}(F\circ w)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc|ccc}\alpha&\cdots&0&\alpha&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\alpha&0&\cdots&\alpha\end{array}\right)_{d,~2d}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{1}}(F\circ w)(0)&\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{2}}(F\circ w)(0)\end{array}\right)_{d,~2d}

which is a
(d,2d)
matrix with
\alpha
along principal diagonal and all other entries are zero.
It is worth noting that
\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{1}}(F\circ w)(0)=\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{2}}(F\circ w)(0)
.

On the other hand, the composition of
w:\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
and
F:\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
is the function
w\circ F:\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2d}\to\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d}
. Then, it can be checked that the Jacobian of
w\circ F
at
0
is equal to the Jacobian of
F\circ w
at 0, i.e.,

J_{0}(F\circ w)=J_{0}(w\circ F).

Now we look at the following compositions:

\displaystyle(F\circ w)\circ u

\displaystyle=F\circ u\circ w,~\text{since $u$ commute with $h$}

\displaystyle=u\circ(F\circ w),~\text{since $u$ is endomorphism of $F$},

\displaystyle(w\circ F)\circ u

\displaystyle=w\circ u\circ F,~\text{since $u$ is endomorphism of $F$}

\displaystyle=u\circ(w\circ F),~\text{since $u$ commute with $w$}.

Since the power series
F\circ w
and
w\circ F
both commute with
u
in the same way and that
J_{0}(F\circ w)=J_{0}(w\circ F)
holds, the Corollary
3.11.1
implies that
F\circ w=w\circ F
, which leads to the conclusion that
w\in\text{End}_{\mathbb{Z}_{p}}(F)
.

∎

3.2.
An application to isogeneity of abelian varieties

We now prove the Theorem
1.3
as an application of the Theorem
1.2
. We detect a class of isogenous abelian varieties only by looking at their
p
-power torsion points.

Definition 3.16
.

A abelian variety is said to be
full
if its associated formal groups are
full
, in the spirit of Definition
2.9
.

Remark 3.17
.

For simplicity, throughout the article we mean by a formal group to be a
formal group law
. In general, a
formal group law
is a formal group (a group object in the category of formal schemes) for some choice of coordinates, and
formal group law
has a formal power series representation that changes up to isomorphism for different choices of coordinates.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
be two formal groups of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively, obtained by formal completion at the identities. Since
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are fixed integral models over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, the associated formal groups
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
have coefficients over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. The property simple is invariant under a formal completion of an abelian variety, and therefore
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
are simple.

The CM property of an abelian variety is inherited to the associated formal group because the endomorphism ring of an abelian variety injects into the corresponding ring of endomorphisms of the associated formal groups, and thus,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
have CM properties. By the assumption of
full
abelian variety,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
are full formal groups. Since
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
shares infinitely many
p
-power torsion points, and since the
p
-endomorphisms
[p]_{\mathcal{A}}
and
[p]_{\mathcal{B}}
descends locally to the corresponding
p
-endomorphisms
[p]_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}
and
[p]_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}
, the formal groups
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
also share infinitely many torsion points, because the
p
-power torsion points sets
\text{Tors}(\mathcal{A})
and
\text{Tors}(\mathcal{B})
are dense subsets of the ambient space
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}^{d}
.

Thus,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
satisfy all the conditions of the Theorem
1.2
and hence,
\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{\mathcal{B}}
. The coefficients of the invariant differentials, or the logarithms of the formal groups
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
determine the coefficients of the
L
-functions attached to the abelian varieties
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
, respectively (e.g., due to Honda
[
12
]
for the case of elliptic curve; whereas Eichler
[
7
]
and Shimura
[
23
]
for abelian variety). Being the formal groups equal, the corresponding
L
-functions are equal. However, a
L
-function of an abelian variety is an invariant upto isogeny of abelian varieties. This implies
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
are isogenous.
This proves the result.
∎

Remark 3.18
.

Since the formal groups
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{B}}
are equal, their
p
-endomorphisms are also equal, that is,
[p]_{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}(X)=[p]_{\hat{\mathcal{B}}}(X)
. If there exists a non-zero homomorphism between two simple abelian varieties, then it must be an
isogeny

[
24
]
.

Example 3.19
.

If two elliptic curves over any ring of characteristics
0
have equal formal groups (upon choice of coordinates), then the curves will be equal and their Weierstrass equations
[
22
, page 115]
are equal to

f(X,Y)=X^{3}+a_{1}XY+a_{2}X^{2}Y+a_{3}Y^{2}+a_{4}XY^{2}+a_{6}Y^{3},

where
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4},a_{6}
are, respectively, given by the coefficients of
XY,~X^{2}Y+XY^{2},~X^{3}Y,~X^{4}Y,~X^{6}Y
of the formal group
[
22
, page 120]
given by

\displaystyle F(X,Y)

\displaystyle=X+Y-a_{1}XY-a_{2}X^{2}Y-a_{2}XY^{2}-2a_{3}X^{3}Y+\cdots+(-2a_{1}a_{3}-2a_{4})X^{4}Y+\cdots

\displaystyle\hskip 5.69046pt+(-2a_{1}^{3}a_{3}-2a_{1}^{2}a_{4}-4a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-2a_{2}a_{4}-2a_{3}^{2}-3a_{6})X^{6}Y+\cdots

We thank Chris Wurthrich for the above implementation by using SageMath
[

]
.
So in this example, the elliptic curves are isogenous trivially, provided the respective formal groups are equal.

We conclude this section by the remark that not only the torsion points of a simple formal group identify the formal group itself but also the torsion points of an abelian variety determine the abelian variety up to isogeny.

4.
Conclusion and Future Direction

In this article, our observation is that the
p
-power torsion points of a formal group can identify that formal group, and in this regard we have proved Theorem
1.2
. We applied this result to prove that the
p
-power torsion points of an abelian variety can determine it up to isogeny, which is proved in Theorem
1.3
.

However, in our setting, we considered
simple
formal groups otherwise, the result might not hold true. For, one can consider
F=H\times F_{1}
and
G=H\times G_{2}
, where
H
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional formal group and
F_{1},G_{1}
are distinct
1
-dimensional formal groups. In that case,
\text{Tors}(F)\cap\text{Tors}(G)=\text{Tors}(H)\times\{0\}
, which is infinite, but
F
and
G
are not equal.

Interested readers may explore to refine the Theorem
1.2
for a larger class of formal groups, as this will impact the Theorem
1.3
.

Appendix A
Number of points in an algebraic set

Here we give an informal discussion regarding the connection between the dimension or rank of a certain algebra over a base ring, and the number of points associated. Let us understand a point in a (coordinatized) Cartesian or affine plane. Most primitively, a point is something like
P=(\frac{3}{5},\frac{4}{5})
, a pair of real values. Let us look at the point in different ways. The numbers
\frac{3}{5}
and
\frac{4}{5}
are values of some coordinate functions, X and Y i.e.,
X(P)=\frac{3}{5}
and
Y(P)=\frac{4}{5}
. But these two functions
X
and
Y
, are two among many, the collection of all such coordinate functions making up the ring
\mathbb{R}[X,Y]
, or
\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
, if we consider the complex numbers as our constant ring instead of the reals. We note that if
U
and
W
are elements of this
“coordinate ring”

\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
, then
U(P)+W(P)=(U+W)(P)
, so that the
evaluation
at
P
is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
to
\mathbb{C}
. Thus we may look at
P
as a (ring) homomorphism from the coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
to
\mathbb{C}
. But a homomorphism into a field must have a maximal ideal from its kernel. And therefore we can also look at a point as a maximal ideal of some coordinate ring. Let us now consider the algebraic subsets (of the coordinate ring), like the unit circle, given by the equation
X^{2}+Y^{2}=1
. We may think of this condition as specifying that for every point
Q
on the circle,
X(Q)^{2}+Y(Q)^{2}=1
, i.e.,
(X^{2}+Y^{2}-1)(Q)=0
. We call a point
Q
a zero of a function
W\in\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
if
W(Q)=0
, just as our original
P
is a zero of
X^{2}+Y^{2}-1
.

If we call
\mathfrak{J}
the ideal generated (within
\mathbb{C}[X,Y]
) by our polynomial
X^{2}+Y^{2}-1
, then we can see that set of polynomial functions, that is those functions that may be defined by polynomials in
X
and
Y
, is naturally isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}[X,Y]/\mathfrak{J}
, we’ll call it
\mathfrak{R}
. The maximal ideals (mentioned above) that are points on the unit circle correspond exactly to the maximal ideals of
\mathfrak{R}
. We can think of
\mathfrak{R}
as defining, in some abstract way, a geometric object, which has been called the
“spectrum”
of
\mathfrak{R}
, though thanks to the insights of Grothendieck, we now call
\text{Spec}(\mathfrak{R})
the set of all prime ideals of
\mathfrak{R}
, not just maximal ones, and with some additional structures. But for our purpose, maximal ideals will suffice and no additional structure is required. The upshot is that the points of an algebraic set are the maximal ideals of the ring whose spectrum is that set.

Recall, we have excluded the bad case (see Remark
3.13
) in the above consideration.

The whole trick, above, works because there are results known as semisimple ring structure theorems due to Wedderburn and others which says
“a finite-dimensional commutative ring over a field
k
is a direct sum of finite algebraic extensions of
k
if there are no nilpotent elements.”
