Random walks on ℤ \mathbb{Z} with metastable Gaussian distribution caused by linear drift with application to the contact process on the complete graph
O.S. Awolude, E. Cator, H. Don

Abstract
We study random walks on ℤ \mathbb{Z} which have a linear (or almost linear) drift towards 0 in a range around 0. This drift leads to a metastable Gaussian distribution centered at zero. We give specific, fast growing, time windows where we can explicitely bound the distance of the distribution of the walk to an appropriate Gaussian. In this way we give a solid theoretical foundation to the notion of metastability. We show that the supercritical contact process on the complete graph has a drift towards its equilibrium point which is locally linear and that our results for random walks apply. This leads to the conclusion that the infected fraction of the population in metastability (when properly scaled) converges in distribution to a Gaussian, uniformly for all times in a fast growing interval.

Introduction

When an infectious disease is spreading in a population, an important question is how much health care capacity is needed. This directly relates to the question how many people will be infected simultaneously and how this will fluctuate over time. In this paper, we study this question for the contact process (or SIS process) on the complete graph.

The contact process is a Markovian epidemic model, introduced by Harris
[
10
]
in 1974. Harris considered the process on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, but it can be defined for any graph. In this model, each individual (represented by a node in the graph) is either infected or healthy. Each infected individual heals at rate 1 and transmits the infection at rate
\tau
to each of its healthy neighbors. After healing, an individual is again susceptible to infection. For surveys of results on the contact process and related models we refer to
[
13
,
6
]
.

The model has been studied on several other infinite graphs like regular trees
[
17
,
12
,
18
]
and Galton-Watson trees
[
11
,
1
]
. On infinite graphs, one of the central questions is if the disease will survive forever in the population. Typically, the model exhibits a phase transition in the sense that there exists a (graph-dependent) critical infection rate
\tau_{c}
such that the infection goes extinct almost surely for
\tau<\tau_{c}
and has strictly positive probability to survive forever for
\tau>\tau_{c}
.

On finite graphs, the process almost surely becomes extinct, since the state space is finite and the only absorbing state of the Markov chain is the all-healthy state. Still, a phase transition could be defined by taking graph sequences and considering the extinction time as a function of the number of nodes
n
. For instance, for finite
d
-regular trees, there is a cricital
\tau_{c}(d)
such the expected extinction time essentially grows logarithmically with
n
for
\tau<\tau_{c}(d)
and exponentially for
\tau>\tau_{c}(d)
, see
[
19
,
5
]
. Similar results were obtained in
[
7
,
8
]
for intervals
\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\in\mathbb{Z}
, with critical value
\tau_{c}(\mathbb{Z})
and the extinction time at criticality growing like a polynomial in
n
, see
[
9
]
. Extension of these results to higher dimensions can be found in
[
14
]
.

If the degrees in the graph grow with
n
, the phases of long and short survival might be separated by a critical value which depends on
n
. For example,
\tau_{c}=1/n
in the complete graph
K_{n}
and
\tau_{c}=n^{-1/2+o(1)}
in the star graph on
n
nodes
[
4
]
. Phase transitions also occur in random graphs, see
[
1
,
3
]
for the Erdős-Réyni graph and configuration model graphs and
[
16
]
for random regular graphs.

Many results in the field focus on proving existence of a phase transition, identifying the critical infection rate or finding the expected extinction time or extinction time distribution. In this paper we zoom in on the supercritical
metastable
behaviour. Our intuition is that typically there is at least one nontrivial (i.e. not all healthy) state where healings and infections are balanced. The process has a drift (at least locally) towards this metastable (or
quasistationary
) state. There are some results in the literature where the metastable fraction of infected nodes is identified by solving the balance equations. For instance, in the complete graph
K_{n}
, with infection rate
\tau=\lambda/n
, the critical point is at
\lambda=1
. If
\lambda>1
is constant, the metastable solution is at
(1-1/\lambda)\cdot n
infected nodes and the expected time to extinction is
\exp((\log(\lambda)+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}-1)n+o(n))
for
n\to\infty
, see
[
3
]
. A rigorous proof of metastable convergence of the infected fraction to a constant is given in
[
15
,
2
]
for power law random graphs.

In the current paper, considering the contact process on
K_{n}
, we are not only interested in the metastable fraction of infected nodes, but also in fluctuations. In fact we study a more general problem of sequences of random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
which have a drift towards 0 which linearly depends on the distance to 0. We show that such walks have a Gaussian metastable distribution, also creating a rigorous framework for defining the notion of Gaussian metastable distributions, using growing time intervals. As an application, we prove (taking limits of time and
n
in the right way) a limit theorem for the metastable state in the supercritical contact process on the complete graph. Loosely speaking, it states that in metastability the number of infected individuals is distributed as a Gaussian distribution with expectation
(1-1/\lambda)\cdot n
and variance
n/\lambda
.

Overview of the paper

The simple symmetric continuous-time random walk
\{W_{t},t\geq 0\}
on
\mathbb{Z}
is null-recurrent and therefore does not have a proper stationary distribution. Fixing a number
d>0
, the probability
\mathbb{P}(|W_{t}|\leq d)
goes to 0 as
t
goes to infinity. To get positive probabilities, we could scale down the position at time
t
by a factor
\sqrt{t}
and look at
W_{t}/\sqrt{t}
. By the central limit theorem, this scaled position converges to a Gaussian distribution. In this paper, we will see random walks converging to a Gaussian
without
scaling of time.

We look at random walks
W_{t}
with a drift towards the origin. If this drift is properly chosen,
\mathbb{P}(|W_{t}|\leq d)
will have a limit in
(0,1)
for all
d>0
, the walk will be positive recurrent and it will have a proper stationary distribution. Our main result (Theorem
6.2
) essentially says the following. Let
\{W_{t},t\geq 0\}
be a random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Suppose
W_{t}
has a drift towards the origin which is, in a range around 0, (almost) linear in the distance to 0. Then the
unscaled
position of the walker will converge to a Gaussian, for all times in a growing interval, before the walker will exit the range around
0
where we control the rates. In this overview, we will explain the spirit of the results a bit further. More precise and more general statements follow in subsequent sections.

Our object of study is a sequence of simple continuous-time random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
.
For
n\geq 1
, define the random walk
\{W_{t}^{(n)},t\geq 0\}
by its transition rates
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
and
\mu_{k}^{(n)}
, from
k
to
k+1
and to
k-1
respectively. These rates will be chosen so that the walk has “a linear drift towards 0”. This vague notion will be clarified in Section
3
, where we discuss some subtleties connected to it. In this introduction we use the term
linear drift
in an informal way. In Section
3
we also present a few results on birth-death processes which will be intensively used later in the analysis of our random walks. These results have a classical flavour, but we need them to get specific control on the behaviour of our random walks.

As a first step, we analyze the walks
W_{t}^{(n)}
restricted to a finite range.
We let
a_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
be suitable increasing functions of
n
and restrict the state space of
W_{t}^{(n)}
to
[-a_{n},a_{n}]\cap\mathbb{Z}
. Inside this state space, we take strictly positive transition rates satisfying (possibly only approximately)

\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}=1-\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(1)

Note that this relation could be interpreted as a form of linear drift.

Since the walk
W_{t}^{(n)}
has a finite state space, it will have a stationary distribution
W_{\infty}^{(n)}
. Condition (
1
) guarantees that this distribution is close to a Gaussian with expectation
0
and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}
. In fact, the ratios
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}/\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}
completely determine the stationary distribution. Nevertheless, (
1
) still leaves a lot of freedom to choose the rates. For instance, for any choice of (positive) rates to the right, the rates to the left can still be chosen to meet the condition. We prove in Section
4
our first main result (Theorem
4.4
) stating that condition (
1
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{W_{\infty}^{(n)}}{\sigma_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

(2)

In Section
5
, we fix
n
and study the convergence of
W_{t}^{(n)}
to
W_{\infty}^{(n)}
as time
t
increases. To prove that this convergence is quick, condition
(
1
)
is not strong enough. Instead, here it is more natural to consider the difference
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}-\mu_{k}^{(n)}
, rather than a ratio of rates. In this context, a linear drift towards 0 means that there exists some constant
d_{n}>0
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(n)}-\mu_{k}^{(n)}=-d_{n}k.

(3)

Under such a condition, in Proposition
5.2
, we show that for all
k

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W^{(n)}_{\infty}\leq k)|

(4)

decreases exponentially in
t
with rate
d_{n}
.

Next, in Section
6
, we study random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
which satisfy (
1
) and (
3
) inside
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. Such a walk does not necessarily have a stationary distribution. If the walk on
\mathbb{Z}
starts in 0, it will be indistinguishable from the walk restricted to
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
as long as the boundary of this interval is not yet reached. Note that (
3
) implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}\leq 1-\frac{d_{n}k}{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}<1

(5)

for
k
positive. In Proposition
6.1
, we prove that this causes the hitting time of
a_{n}
to be large with high probability. The hitting time of
-a_{n}
can be bounded similarly.

Combining the previous results, we come to our main result for walks on
\mathbb{Z}
, which informally can be stated as follows. Let
W_{t}^{(n)}
on
\mathbb{Z}
have linear drift towards 0 inside
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
as in (
1
) and (
3
). Suppose the walk starts in (or close to) 0. Then, for each
n
, there is a large interval
I_{n}
in time such that
W_{t}^{(n)}/\sigma_{n}
is in distribution close to a standard Gaussian for all
t\in I_{n}
. In the limit
n\to\infty
, the difference vanishes. For the precise formulation, we refer to Theorem
6.2
. We illustrate this theorem by an example and simulation in Section
6
.

Finally, in Section
7
, we apply our results to the contact process on the complete graph
K_{n}
. Each node in the graph represents an individual which can either be healthy or infected. If a node is infected, it is infecting its healthy neighbors at rate
\tau
and it is healing at rate 1. Since the graph is complete, only the size of the infected set is important for the evolution of the process. Given this size, it is irrelevant which individuals are infected. The total number of infected
X_{t}
behaves like a simple random walk on
\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. The stationary distribution is trivial: all nodes are healthy. However, if the infection rate is of the form
\tau=\tfrac{\lambda}{n}
for some
\lambda>1
, then the healings and infections are balanced if the infected set has size
\tfrac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}\cdot n
. The process
X_{t}
has a drift towards this equilibrium, which is locally approximately linear as in (
1
) and (
3
). If
X_{0}
is close to
\tfrac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}\cdot n
, our result for random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
can be applied. We did some extra work to weaken the initial condition. This leads to Theorem
7.2
, our main result for the contact process on
K_{n}
: there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that if
X_{0}\geq c_{1}\log(n)
, then
X_{t}
is in distribution close to a Gaussian with expectation
\tfrac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}\cdot n
and variance
\tfrac{n}{\lambda}
for all
t\in[n,e^{n\cdot g(n)}]
, where
g(n)
goes to zero arbitrarily slowly.

Studying the metastable distribution and behaviour of the contact process on the complete graph has been the inspiration for this paper. We needed to control the contact process both for growing
t
and for growing
n
, at the same time. This forced us to study classical objects like simple random walks. Many results in this paper might therefore feel familiar, especially those in Section 2, 3 and 4, and although references to the specific results we needed are not easily found, we do not claim that they are all new; we did add their proofs to be self-contained and complete. Sections 5 and 6 contain the most original results of this paper.

3

Extinction of birth-death processes

Before heading to our main results, this section is devoted to some preliminaries. In Section
3.1
we elaborate on the notion of drift. Next, we derive a few results on the extinction of birth-death processes. All processes in this paper are simple random walks on finite or infinite intervals in
\mathbb{Z}
, either in discrete or in continuous time. By a birth-death process we mean the special case where the state space is
\mathbb{N}
or
[n]:=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
and where 0 is an absorbing state.

In Section
3.2
we analyze the extinction time of a discrete-time birth-death process with positive drift. After that, in
3.3
and
3.4
, we study extinction in the continuous-time process. Usually, one is interested in the expected extinction time, but we aim for tail bounds for the extinction time distribution. We keep our exposition self-contained, since we could not easily find these results in the literature. We will consider both positive and negative drift, corresponding to fast and slow extinction respectively.

3.1

The notion of drift

For discrete-time random walks, by a positive drift in state
k
we just mean that the probability to go right is greater than the probability to go left. For continuous-time random walks, the situation is more subtle and the notion of a linear drift towards zero could be defined in different ways. Here we discuss three possible definitions: linearity of
\lambda_{k}/\mu_{k+1}
as in (
1
), linearity of
\lambda_{k}/\mu_{k}
, and linearity of the differences
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}
as in (
3
). Each of these definitions has different implications for the random walk, which we illustrate by an example.

Consider a simple random walk
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on state space
[n]
with rates to the right and left respectively

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=(1+\varepsilon)^{k^{2}},

\displaystyle k=0,\ldots,n-1

(6)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}=(1+\varepsilon)^{k(k-1)},

\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,n,

(7)

where
\varepsilon
is some small positive number. Observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k+1}}=(1+\varepsilon)^{-k}\approx 1-k\varepsilon,\qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1,

(8)

so that this random walk has an approximately linear drift towards 0. As we will see in Section
4
, this type of drift leads to a stationary distribution which is close to a Gaussian with expectation 0 and variance
\sigma^{2}=\varepsilon^{-1}
. In the current example, this Gaussian is truncated at
0
and
n
. For
n=100
and
\varepsilon=1/1000
, we computed the stationary distribution of
W_{t}
. In Figure
1
, left plot, a histogram of this distribution is compared to the corresponding truncated Gaussian. The highest probabilities correspond to positions close to 0.

On the other hand, in each state
k
, the rate to the right is higher than the rate to the left. This suggests a linear drift away from 0:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k}}=(1+\varepsilon)^{k}\approx 1+k\varepsilon.

(9)

Let
\{D_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
be the jump chain corresponding to
W_{t}
, i.e. if
T_{i}
is the time of the
i
th step of
W_{t}
, we let

\displaystyle D_{0}=W_{0},\qquad D_{i}=W_{T_{i}},i\geq 1.

(10)

D_{i}
is a discrete-time Markov process with transition probabilities proportional to the transition rates of
W_{t}
. If
p_{k,l}
is the probability from
k
to
l
, then

\displaystyle p_{k,k+1}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k}},\qquad p_{k,k-1}=\frac{\mu_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n-1.

(11)

Since
D_{i}
is periodic, it does not have a stationary distribution. We modify
W_{t}
to have a positive rate from
0
to
0
and from
n
to
n
. This does not actually change the stationary distribution of
W_{t}
, but it allows to assume that

\displaystyle p_{0,0}=p_{0,1}=p_{n,n-1}=p_{n,n}=\tfrac{1}{2},

(12)

making
D_{i}
aperiodic. The stationary distribution of
D_{i}
is plotted in Figure
1
, right plot. As opposed to
W_{t}
itself, the corresponding jump chain
D_{i}
is atracted to
n
rather than to 0. This means that
W_{t}
spends much more time close to 0, but the states close to
n
are visited much more frequently. This is possible because
W_{t}
is moving back and forth much faster when close to
n
; the rates in (
6
) and (
7
) rapidly increase with
k
.

The example illustrates that the roles of the ratios in (
8
) and (
9
) is completely different. One could say that the ratio in (
8
) is decisive for the long term behaviour, while the ratio in (
9
) determines the short term behaviour. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=k+1)}{\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=k)},\qquad\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k}}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=k+1\mid W_{0}=k)}{\mathbb{P}(W_{t}=k-1\mid W_{0}=k)}.

(13)

In this paper, we therefore speak about
long term drift
and
short term drift
respectively. We will slightly abuse terminology by calling these drifts positive if the corresponding ratios exceed 1.

Stationary distributions of the continuous-time walk
W_{t}
(left) and the corresponding jump chain
D_{i}
(right).

Clearly the rates can still be scaled without changing any of the ratios. This is basically a scaling of time which speeds up or slows down the whole process. Such a scaling does influence the difference
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}
, which is yet another quantity related to the concept of drift. We will call this the
speed
of the process in state

, motivated by Lemma

. The direction of the speed is equal to the direction of the short term drift.

For our main theorem for random walks on
\mathbb{Z}
(Theorem
6.2
) all three notions of drift will play a role.

Proposition 3.1

Let

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}<i)\leq(i-1)\cdot\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{n-1}.

\displaystyle\pi_{k}=\frac{q\pi_{k-1}}{p+q\pi_{k-1}}\leq\frac{q}{p}\pi_{k-1}\leq\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{k-1}.

3.3

Continuous-time process with positive speed

We now consider the case of a continuous-time birth-death process
\{Y_{t},t\geq 0\}
on
[n]
with birth rate
\lambda_{k}
and death rate
\mu_{k}
in state
k
(which from now on will be standard notation). By definition,
\lambda_{0}=\mu_{0}=\lambda_{n}=0
. Let
p_{k}
be the probability on a birth in state
k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and
q_{k}
the probability on a death. Then

\displaystyle p_{k}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k}},\qquad q_{k}=\frac{\mu_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k}}.

(19)

Now suppose
Y_{0}=n
. We define the extinction time by

\displaystyle T_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0:Y_{t}=0\}.

(20)

The extinction time is finite almost surely. We assume the process to be right-continuous, so that
Y_{T_{n}}=0
if
T_{n}
is finite.

The next proposition essentially says that the extinction time grows like the
n
th power of the short term drift (for constant rates). Since
\kappa_{n}
below is an upper bound for the absolute speed,
\kappa_{n}t
is a scaling of time which adjusts for the speed of the process. In analogy to the discrete-time case, if all birth rates are
\lambda
and all death rates are
\mu
, then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(T_{n}\leq t)=0
if
\kappa_{n}t=o((\lambda/\mu)^{n})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{Y_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
be a birth-death process on
[n]
with birth rates
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
, death rates
\mu_{k}^{(n)}<\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
and
Y_{0}=n
. Let
\kappa_{n}=\max_{k}\{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}+\mu_{k}^{(n)}\}
and
\rho_{n}=\max_{k}\{\mu_{k}^{(n)}/\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
and for all
t>0
with
\kappa_{n}t\geq C_{1}+C_{2}n\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}<t)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{n-1}.

(21)

Proof.

Define
S_{i}
to be the
i
th event time. If
i
exceeds the total number of events, we set
S_{i}=\infty
. Now the process
\{X_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}\}
defined by

\displaystyle X_{i}=\begin{cases}Y_{S_{i}}\qquad\qquad&S_{i}\leq T_{n},\\
0&S_{i}=\infty,\end{cases}

(22)

is a discrete-time birth-death process on
[n]
with transition probabilities
p_{k}
and
q_{k}
. Choose some
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
and note that the extinction time
T_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}<t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t,T_{n}<t)+\mathbb{P}(S_{i}>t,T_{n}<t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)+\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=\infty)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)+\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=0).

(23)

We proceed to bound
\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)
. The
i
th event time is a sum of exponential waiting times with rate at most
\kappa_{n}
. Therefore, if
E_{1},\ldots E_{i}
are independent exponential random variables with rate

, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}E_{j}<t\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-a\sum E_{j}}>e^{-at}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-a\sum E_{j}}\right]}{e^{-at}},

(24)

for

by Markov’s inequality. Using independence of the

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{n}}{a+\kappa_{n}}\right)^{i}\cdot e^{at}.

(25)

Finally, optimizing over

, so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{n}t}{i}\right)^{i}\cdot e^{i-\kappa_{n}t}.

(26)

The bound is trivial for

, but is less than 1 and decreasing for

.
First assume that we would have
\kappa_{n}t\geq 2+\frac{4}{\varepsilon}
. Then we can choose
i
as follows:

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}t+1\leq\frac{1+\tfrac{3\varepsilon}{4}}{1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}}\kappa_{n}t\leq i=\left\lfloor\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}}\kappa_{n}t\right\rfloor\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}}\kappa_{n}t.

(27)

Choose
\delta
such that
i=e^{\delta}\kappa_{n}t
, so

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{1+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}}{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right)\leq\delta\leq\log\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}\right),

(28)

and call the lower bound
\tilde{\delta}
. Define
\eta=e^{-\tilde{\delta}}-(1-\tilde{\delta})>0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\delta i+i-\kappa_{n}t)=\exp{((1-\delta-e^{-\delta})e^{\delta}\kappa_{n}t)}\leq\exp(-\eta e^{\delta}\kappa_{n}t).

(29)

Define
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, which only depend on
\varepsilon
, to be

\displaystyle C_{1}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\eta}\log\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right),2+\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right\}\ \mbox{and}\ C_{2}=\frac{1}{\eta}.

(30)

Then for
\kappa_{n}t\geq C_{1}+C_{2}n\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
, choose
i
and
\delta
as above. We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{i}<t)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\eta\kappa_{n}t)\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\rho_{n}^{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon e^{\delta}\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{n-1}.

(31)

The other term in (
23
) can be bounded using Proposition
3.1
and noting that
q_{k}/p_{k}\leq\rho_{n}
for all
k
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{i}=0)\leq(i-1)\rho_{n}^{n-1}\leq e^{\delta}\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{n-1}.

(32)

Adding the two terms, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}<t)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{n-1},

(33)

completing the proof.
∎

3.4

Continuous-time process with negative linear speed

Here we again consider a continuous-time birth death process
\left\{Y_{t},t\geq 0\right\}
on
[n]
with rates
\lambda_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
. The next lemma is standard and therefore presented without proof. It gives a differential equation for
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]
in terms of the rates. It demonstrates that
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}
is a measure of the expected speed of a walker in state
k
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\{Y_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
be a birth-death process on
[n]
, with arbitrary initial distribution
Y_{0}
. The expected position
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]
at time
t>0
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{Y_{t}}-\mu_{Y_{t}}]

(34)

We are now interested in the case where the speed
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}
is negative and linear in
k
. The speed is constant over time, but may depend on
n
, for instance
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}=-k/n
. So we actually consider a sequence of processes, indexed by
n
. The following lemma then gives asymptotically (
n\to\infty
) almost sure upper bounds on the extinction time, which depend on the speed. For fixed
n
, a linear speed towards 0 guarantees exponentially fast extinction.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose there is some constant
d=d(n)>0
such that the transition rates of the birth death process
\{Y_{t}\}
satisfy
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}=-d\cdot k
for all
0\leq k\leq n
. Take any initial distribution
Y_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>t)\leq n\cdot e^{-d\cdot t}.

(35)

Proof.

First note that by a coupling argument we can assume that
Y_{0}\equiv n
. Now Lemma
3.3
gives us

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=-d\cdot\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}],

(36)

with solution
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=\mathbb{E}[Y_{0}]\cdot e^{-d\cdot t}=n\cdot e^{-d\cdot t}
.
Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}>t)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\geq 1)\leq\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=n\cdot e^{-d\cdot t}.

(37)

∎

4

A random walk with Gaussian stationary distribution

In this section, we study a continuous-time random walk
\{W_{t}^{(n)},t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\}
on
\mathbb{Z}\cap[-b_{n},b_{n}]
which depends on
n
and has transition rates

\displaystyle k\rightarrow k+1

with rate

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(n)}\qquad\text{for}\qquad-b_{n}\leq k\leq b_{n}-1,

(38)

\displaystyle k\rightarrow k-1

with rate

\displaystyle\mu_{k}^{(n)}\qquad\text{for}\qquad b_{n}+1\leq k\leq b_{n}.

(39)

It will turn out that the long term drift determines the stationary distribution. Let
\sigma_{n}
be an increasing positive function of
n
with
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
and take
b_{n}
and
a_{n}
such that

\left\lceil\sigma_{n}\sqrt{2\log(\sigma_{n})}\right\rceil\leq a_{n}\leq b_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}\to 0.

(40)

Assume that for each
n
there is a function
\delta_{n}
taking small values and
\eta_{n}\geq 0
such that the rates statisfy

\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}\right)\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\geq\eta_{n}&\text{if}\quad-b_{n}\leq k<-a_{n}\\
=-\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\delta_{n}(k)&\text{if}\quad-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}\\
\leq-\eta_{n}&\text{if}\quad a_{n}<k\leq b_{n}.\end{array}\right.

(41)

In the interval
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
, this walk has a long term drift towards the origin
0
, which is approximately linear in
k
:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}\approx 1-\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\delta_{n}(k).

(42)

We introduce the error term
\delta_{n}(k)
to allow the (
\log
of the) drift to be not exactly linear in
k
. When very far away (
|k|>a_{n}
), there is simply a constant drift towards the origin or no drift at all.

We will prove that with a proper scaling in
n
the stationary distribution of this random walk converges to a Gaussian as
n\to\infty
under suitable conditions on the parameters. There will be
no
scaling in
t
. So for fixed
n
, we can take the limit
t\to\infty
and
W_{t}^{(n)}
will converge in distribution.

Condition (
41
) is not yet sufficient to conclude that the random walk quickly reaches its stationary distribution. For this we will need more control on the rates, see also the discussion in Section
3.1
. This issue will be settled in Section
5
.

4.1

Derivation of the stationary distribution

The walk
W_{t}^{(n)}
has a finite state space and is irreducible. It therefore has a unique stationary distribution
\pi_{n}(k)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For each
n
, let
b_{n}\geq a_{n}
be integers satisfying
(
40
)
and take rates as in (
41
).
Let
K
be a constant and assume that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}|\delta_{n}(k)|\leq K\cdot\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(43)

Furthermore, assume that

\eta_{n}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma_{n}}{a_{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\ \ \text{or}\ \ b_{n}-a_{n}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}e^{\frac{1}{2}a_{n}^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(44)

Then for
n
large enough

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\varepsilon_{n,k}\right\},\qquad-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n},

(45)

with
|\varepsilon_{n,k}|\leq(4+2K)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\{k\ :\ a_{n}<|k|\leq b_{n}\}}\pi_{n}(k)\leq\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}e^{(3+2K)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(46)

Remark 4.2
.

Suppose the rates satisfy

\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\geq\exp(\eta_{n})&\text{if}\quad-b_{n}\leq k<-a_{n}\\
=1-\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\delta_{n}(k)&\text{if}\quad-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}\\
\leq\exp(-\eta_{n})&\text{if}\quad a_{n}<k\leq b_{n},\end{array}\right.

(47)

with
\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}|\delta_{n}(k)|\leq K\cdot a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}\left|\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}\right)+\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}\left|\delta_{n}(k)+O\left(\left(\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\delta_{n}(k)\right)^{2}\right)\right|

(48)

\displaystyle\leq K\cdot\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+O\left(\frac{a_{n}^{3}}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}\right)=(K+o(1))\cdot\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}},

(49)

so that (
47
) is an alternative to (
41
) under which Proposition
4.1
holds as well.

Remark 4.3
.

The error term
\varepsilon_{n,k}
in (
45
) becomes larger if
a_{n}
is increased. This might be unexpected, since we have more control on the rates in (
41
). However, when
a_{n}
increases, at the same time the assumptions in (
43
) and (
44
) are weakened, which explains why the error term increases. The choice of
a_{n}
can be adapted to the situation in which one wants to apply the results, but should satifsy (
40
).

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. Then for all
k=-b_{n},\ldots,b_{n}-1
, the stationary distribution satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{n}(k+1)}{\pi_{n}(k)}=\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}.

(50)

Iterating gives for all
k=1,\ldots,a_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{n}(k)}{\pi_{n}(0)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{(n)}}{\mu_{i+1}^{(n)}}=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{i}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\delta_{n}(i)\right\}

(51)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{n}(0)}{\pi_{n}(-k)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{-i}^{(n)}}{\mu_{-i+1}^{(n)}}=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{i}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_{n}(-i)\right\}.

(52)

This implies that for all
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}

\pi_{n}(0)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right\}e^{-(K+1)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\leq\pi_{n}(k)\leq\pi_{n}(0)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right\}e^{(K+1)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(53)

Note that it is hard to improve on these inequalities, since they are also caused by the discrete nature of the probability distribution
\pi_{n}
. Lemma
A.1
(see Appendix) states that for
a\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log(\sigma)}
and
\sigma
large enough,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-a/\sigma^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=-a}^{a}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{a/\sigma^{2}}.

(54)

We use this to obtain

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(0)\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}e^{-(K+2)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}\pi_{n}(k)\leq\pi_{n}(0)\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}e^{(K+2)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(55)

For
a_{n}<k\leq b_{n}
, by (
41
) and (
50
) we get

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(k)\leq\pi_{n}(a_{n})e^{-\eta_{n}(k-a_{n})}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \pi_{n}(-k)\leq\pi_{n}(-a_{n})e^{-\eta_{n}(k-a_{n})}.

(56)

This implies that (using (
53
) and then (
44
)),

\displaystyle\sum_{a_{n}+1\leq|k|\leq b_{n}}\pi_{n}(k)

\displaystyle\leq(\pi_{n}(a_{n})+\pi_{n}(-a_{n}))\sum_{k=1}^{b_{n}-a_{n}}e^{-\eta_{n}k}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi_{n}(0)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}}e^{(K+1)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\cdot\min\{b_{n}-a_{n},\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\eta_{n}s}ds\}

\displaystyle\leq\pi_{n}(0)e^{(K+1)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}.

(57)

Now we will bound
\pi_{n}(0)
by using that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}\pi_{n}(k)\leq\sum_{k=-b_{n}}^{b_{n}}\pi_{n}(k)=1.

(58)

Combining with the left hand side of (
55
) gives the upper bound

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(0)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}}e^{(K+2)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(59)

The right hand side of (
55
) together with (
4.1
) and (
58
) leads to

\displaystyle 1\leq\pi_{n}(0)\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}e^{(K+2)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}(1+\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}})\leq\pi_{n}(0)\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}e^{(K+3)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}},

(60)

which gives the lower bound

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(0)\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}}e^{-(K+3)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

(61)

Using (
53
) for the last time gives the final result (
45
) for all
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}
. Note that we have also proved that

\sum_{\{k\ :\ a_{n}<|k|\leq b_{n}\}}\pi_{n}(k)\leq\frac{a_{n}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}e^{(2K+3)a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}.

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
W_{\infty}^{(n)}
be a random variable corresponding to the stationary distribution of the random walk
W_{t}^{(n)}
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}=k)=\pi_{n}(k)
. Then, under the conditions of Proposition
4.1
and as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{W_{\infty}^{(n)}}{\sigma_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z,

(62)

where
Z
is a standard Gaussian.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of Proposition
4.1
. Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
n
big enough we have that
|x|\sigma_{n}\leq a_{n}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{\lfloor x\sigma_{n}\rfloor}\pi_{n}(k)\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq x\sigma_{n})\leq\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{\lfloor x\sigma_{n}\rfloor}\pi_{n}(k)+\sum_{a_{n}<|k|\leq b_{n}}\pi_{n}(k).

(63)

Since
a_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}\to 0
,
\sigma_{n}\to+\infty
and
a_{n}^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}\to+\infty
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{\lfloor x\sigma_{n}\rfloor}\pi_{n}(k)=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{n}}\sum_{k=-a_{n}}^{\lfloor x\sigma_{n}\rfloor}e^{-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}}\to\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x),\qquad\sum_{a_{n}<|k|\leq b_{n}}\pi_{n}(k)\to 0,

(64)

proving that
\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}/\sigma_{n}\leq x)\to\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x).

∎

5

Mixing of the random walk

In this section, our aim is to give conditions on the rates under which the walk
W_{t}^{(n)}
restricted to
\{-a_{n},\ldots,a_{n}\}
(i.e. with
a_{n}=b_{n}
) converges quickly to its stationary distribution. The idea will be to look at the distance between the two independent walkers and to show that they will meet quickly. In this setting, the speed of the walkers is the relevant measure of drift. The difference between the walkers will again be a random walk, which is restricted to the positive integers. In the remainder of this section, we omit the dependence on
n
and simply write
W_{t}
for
W_{t}^{(n)}
, and
\mu_{k}
and
\lambda_{k}
for the rates.

Consider two independent realizations of
W_{t}
in different positions
k<l
. The distance between the walkers then increases at rate
\lambda_{l}+\mu_{k}
and decreases at rate
\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l}
. This means that the speed of the difference is given by

\displaystyle(\lambda_{l}+\mu_{k})-(\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l})=(\lambda_{l}-\mu_{l})-(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}),

(65)

which is equal to the difference of the speeds of the two walkers. Note that the signs of the individual speeds are not that relevant. For instance, the walker on the right could have a positive speed and still meet quickly if the walker on the left has a higher positive speed. In particular, if the speed difference in (
65
) is negative and proportional to the distance
l-k
, then Lemma
3.4
guarantees that the walkers will meet quickly. This explains the condition on the derivative of the speed in the next lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
W_{t}^{1}
and
W_{t}^{2}
be two independent realizations of the random walk
W_{t}
. Let
W_{0}^{1}=-a_{n}
,
W_{0}^{2}=a_{n}
and let
T=\inf\{t:W_{t}^{1}=W_{t}^{2}\}
. Assume that there exists a constant
d>0
and a differentiable function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}=h(k)
for all
k\in\{-a_{n},\ldots,a_{n}\}
and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}h(x)\leq-d\qquad\text{for}\qquad x\in[-a_{n},a_{n}].

(66)

Then for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T>t)\leq 2a_{n}\cdot e^{-d\cdot t}.

(67)

Proof.

We introduce the two-dimensional process
\{P_{t},t\geq 0\}
describing the pair of walkers and couple them as soon as they meet:

\displaystyle P_{t}=\left(P_{t}^{1},P_{t}^{2}\right)=\begin{cases}\big(W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{2}\big)&t<T,\\
\big(W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{1}\big)&t\geq T.\end{cases}

(68)

When
t<T
, the process
P_{t}
is in some state
(k,l)
with
k<l
. The transition rates then are

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}(k,l)\to(k-1,l)\text{\ with rate\ }\mu_{k},&&(k,l)\to(k,l-1)\text{\ with rate\ }\mu_{l},\\
(k,l)\to(k+1,l)\text{\ with rate\ }\lambda_{k},&&(k,l)\to(k,l+1)\text{\ with rate\ }\lambda_{l}.\end{array}

The distance
l-k
between the walkers increases at rate
\mu_{k}+\lambda_{l}
and decreases at rate
\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l}
. We aim to apply Lemma
3.4
to the distance, and therefore look at the difference of these rates:

\displaystyle(\lambda_{l}+\mu_{k})-(\lambda_{k}+\mu_{l})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{l}-\mu_{l})-(\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k})

\displaystyle=f(l)-f(k)=\int_{k}^{l}\frac{d}{dx}f(x)dx\leq-d\cdot(l-k).

(71)

Unfortunately, the left hand side is not necessarily determined by
l-k
, so the distance
P_{t}^{2}-P_{t}^{1}
is not a Markov process. We therefore add a third walker
\{P_{t}^{3},t\geq 0\}
with
P_{0}^{3}=a_{n}
and satisfying
P_{t}^{1}\leq P_{t}^{2}\leq P_{t}^{3}
for all
t\geq 0
. We will define its rates such that the distance
D_{t}:=P_{t}^{3}-P_{t}^{1}
is a Markov process (but
P_{t}^{3}
itself is not Markovian).

The rate to the right of the new walker
P_{t}^{3}
will depend on the position of the walker
P_{t}^{1}
. If
P_{t}^{1}=k
and
P_{t}^{3}=m
, then its rate to the right is chosen to be

\displaystyle\nu_{k,m}:=-d\cdot(m-k)+\lambda_{k}+\mu_{m}-\mu_{k}\geq\lambda_{m}.

(72)

Suppose
(P_{t}^{1},P_{t}^{2},P_{t}^{3})=(k,l,m)
with
l<m
. The transitions in the first and second coordinate still follow the rules given in (
68
) and (
5
). The third coordinate increases and decreases at the following rates:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}(k,l,m)\to(k,l,m+1)&&\text{\ with rate\ }\nu_{k,m},\\
(k,l,m)\to(k,l,m-1)&&\text{\ with rate\ }\mu_{m}.\end{array}

If
P_{t}^{2}=P_{t}^{3}
, they can either move simultaneously, or only
P_{t}^{3}
moves to the right. I.e., if
(P_{t}^{1},P_{t}^{2},P_{t}^{3})=(k,l,l)
with
k<l
, the possible transitions are

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}(k,l,l)\to(k,l-1,l-1)&&\text{\ with rate\ }\mu_{l}\\
(k,l,l)\to(k,l+1,l+1)&&\text{\ with rate\ }\lambda_{l}\\
(k,l,l)\to(k,l,l+1)&&\text{\ with rate\ }\nu_{k,l}-\lambda_{l}\end{array}

Note that in any state
(P_{t}^{1},P_{t}^{2},P_{t}^{3})=(k,l,m)
, the marginal rates of the first two walkers are as before. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{T}=\inf\{t:P_{t}^{1}=P_{t}^{3}\}

(78)

and set
P_{t}=(W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{1},W_{t}^{1})
for
t\geq\tilde{T}
. For
t<\tilde{T}
and
(P_{t}^{1},P_{t}^{2},P_{t}^{3})=(k,l,m)
, the distance
D_{t}=m-k
decreases at rate
\lambda_{k}+\mu_{m}
and increases at rate
\mu_{k}+\nu_{k,m}
. Hence, the speed of the difference is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{k}+\nu_{k,m}-(\lambda_{k}+\mu_{m})

\displaystyle=-d\cdot(m-k).

(79)

This means that
D_{t}
behaves as a birth death process on
[2a_{n}]
, with negative linear speed and we can apply Lemma
3.4
. We obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{T}>t)\leq 2a_{n}\cdot e^{-d\cdot t},

(80)

for all
t>0
. Since
\tilde{T}\geq T
, it follows that
\mathbb{P}(T>t)\leq\mathbb{P}(\tilde{T}>t)
and this completes the proof.
∎

The previous result will be used to prove quick convergence of
W_{t}
to the stationary distribution for any initial distribution
W_{0}
. The idea is the following: if two walkers meet, they will obey the same probability distribution after meeting. If a third walker starts in between and stays in between through coupling, they will all three meet and share the same distribution. Letting the third walker start in the stationary distribution, the common distribution after meeting has to be the stationary distribution as well.

Proposition 5.2
.

Consider the walk
\{W_{t},t\geq 0\}
with arbitrary initial distribution
W_{0}
and conditions on the rates as in Lemma
5.1
. Let
W_{\infty}
have the stationary distribution. For all
t\geq 0
and all
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(W_{t}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}\leq k)\right\rvert\leq 2a_{n}e^{-d\cdot t}.

(81)

Proof.

We consider four independent realizations of
\{W_{t},t\geq 0\}
with different initial distributions.

•

Let
W_{t}^{S}
be a realization of
W_{t}
which starts in the stationary distribution, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(W^{S}_{0}=k)=\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}=k)
. By definition of stationarity, this means that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{S}=k)=\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}=k),

(82)

for all
t\geq 0
and all
k
.

•

Let
\tilde{W}_{t}
be a realization with arbitrary initial distribution
\tilde{W}_{0}
on
\{-a_{n},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.

•

Let
W_{t}^{1}
and
W_{t}^{2}
be two more realizations with deterministic initial distributions
W_{0}^{1}\equiv-a_{n}
and
W_{0}^{2}\equiv a_{n}
.

At time
t=0
we have
W_{0}^{1}\leq W_{0}^{S}\leq W_{0}^{2}
and
W_{0}^{1}\leq\tilde{W}_{0}\leq W_{0}^{2}
. If any pair of walkers meets, we couple them. This implies that

\displaystyle W_{t}^{1}\leq W_{t}^{S}\leq W_{t}^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad W_{t}^{1}\leq\tilde{W}_{t}\leq W_{t}^{2}

(83)

for all
t\geq 0
. Lemma
5.1
gives us that
\mathbb{P}(T>t)\leq 2a_{n}e^{-d\cdot t}
, where
T=\inf\{t:W_{t}^{1}=W_{t}^{2}\}
is the first meeting time of
W_{t}^{1}
and
W_{t}^{2}
. It follows that

\displaystyle W_{t}^{1}=W_{t}^{S}=\tilde{W}_{t}=W_{t}^{2}

(84)

for all
t\geq T
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k\mid T\leq t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k\mid T>t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T>t)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{S}\leq k\mid T\leq t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T\leq t)

\displaystyle\geq(1-2a_{n}e^{-d\cdot t})\cdot\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}\leq k)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}\leq k)-2a_{n}e^{-d\cdot t}.

(85)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k\mid T\leq t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(\tilde{W}_{t}\leq k\mid T>t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T>t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{S}\leq k\mid T\leq t)\cdot\mathbb{P}(T\leq t)+\mathbb{P}(T>t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}\leq k)+2a_{n}e^{-d\cdot t},

(86)

which is sufficient to complete the proof.
∎

6

Main result for walks on
\mathbb{Z}

So far, we investigated the random walks
W_{t}^{(n)}
on a finite interval in
\mathbb{Z}
. The main conclusions are that these walks quickly reach their stationary distribution (Proposition
5.2
) and that these stationary distributions with a proper scaling converge to a Gaussian for
n\to\infty
(Theorem
4.4
). Observe that for
n
fixed we obtain a fixed stationary distribution, i.e.​ we can send
t
to
\infty
without scaling the position by a function of time. In that sense, our convergence is of a different nature than for instance the central limit theorem for simple symmetric random walk.

We now aim to expand the state space of the random walk to
\mathbb{Z}
. This walk on
\mathbb{Z}
will be denoted
M_{t}^{(n)}
. We take the rates inside
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
as before, but we allow the walk to continue beyond this interval as a simple random walk with arbitrary rates. If we start in
-a_{n}<k<a_{n}
, the walk on
\mathbb{Z}
is indistinguishable from the walk restricted to
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
, as long as we do not hit the boundaries. The typical phenomenon is that the walk still quickly starts to behave like a Gaussian, until (after a long time) the boundary is hit. To formalize and prove this, we first state the next result, which bounds the hitting time of the boundary in terms of the short term drift. For convenience we assume that
a_{n}
is even, and write
M_{t}
for
M_{t}^{(n)}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider the walk
\{M_{t},t\geq 0\}
. Suppose the initial distribution
M_{0}
is supported on
[-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2},\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}]
. Let
T_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0:|M_{t}|=a_{n}\}
and let

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\max\left\{\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k}}\right)^{\text{sgn}(k)}\Bigm|\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}\right\},\quad\kappa_{n}=\max\left\{\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k}\Bigm|\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}\right\}.

(87)

Suppose
\rho_{n}<1
. Then, for
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{n}<t)\leq(2+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{a_{n}/2-1},

(88)

whenever
\kappa_{n}t\geq C_{1}+C_{2}\cdot a_{n}\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
.

Proof.

Let
T_{n}^{-}=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{t}=-a_{n}\}
and
T_{n}^{+}=\inf\{t\geq 0:M_{t}=a_{n}\}
. Then
T_{n}=\min\{T_{n}^{-},T_{n}^{+}\}
.

It is straightforward to define a birth-death process
Y^{-}_{t}
and couple it to
M_{t}
such that
Y^{-}_{0}=a_{n}/2
and
Y^{-}_{t}\leq\min\{M_{t}+a_{n},a_{n}/2\}
for all
t
. So
Y_{t}^{-}
is always less than the distance of
M_{t}
to the left boundary of
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. Consequently, the extinction time of
Y_{t}^{-}
is less than or equal to
T_{n}^{-}
. Similarly, we can construct a birth-death process
Y_{t}^{+}
for which
Y_{0}^{+}=a_{n}/2
and
Y_{t}^{+}\leq\min\{a_{n}-M_{t},a_{n}/2\}
for all
t
. The process
Y_{t}^{+}
has extinction time less than or equal to
T_{n}^{+}
.

We bound the extinction times of
Y_{t}^{-}
and
Y_{t}^{+}
by Proposition
3.2
. It then follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T_{n}^{-}<t\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho^{a_{n}/2-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\left(T_{n}^{+}<t\right)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho^{a_{n}/2-1},

(89)

whenever
\kappa_{n}t\geq C_{1}+C_{2}a_{n}\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
.
This implies the result of the proposition.
∎

We collect and summarize the results and conditions on the random walks
\{M_{t}^{(n)},t\geq 0\}
. For each
n
,
M_{t}^{(n)}
is a simple random walk with state space
\mathbb{Z}
. The rates in state
k
are denoted
\mu_{k}^{(n)}
(to the left) and
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}
(to the right).

Take
\sigma_{n}\to\infty
and let
a_{n}\in(\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n}^{2})
be an integer sequence satisfying (
40
). We assume existence of
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the rates satisfy the following conditions for
n\geq n_{0}
. These conditions only restrict the rates for
|k|\leq a_{n}
, and are sufficient for our main theorem.

1.

(Condition for quasi-stationary behaviour to be Gaussian.) Rates satisfy

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k+1}^{(n)}}\right)=-\frac{k}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+\delta_{n}(k),\qquad-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}-1,

(90)

(or the version in (
47
)) with
\sum_{k}|\delta_{n}(k)|\leq Ka_{n}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
for some constant
K
.

Remark.

This condition says that there is a long term drift towards 0 which is approximately linear in
k
. A condition of this type is needed for quasi-stationary behaviour to be Gaussian, since the long term drift determines the quasi-stationary distribution.

2.

(Condition for quick convergence to quasi-stationary behaviour.) For each
n
, there exists a constant
d_{n}>0
and a differentiable function
h_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda_{k}^{(n)}-\mu_{k}^{(n)}=h_{n}(k)
for all
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}
and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}h_{n}(x)\leq-d_{n},\qquad x\in[-a_{n},a_{n}].

(91)

Remark.

This is a condition on the speed of the process. From condition (
90
), it does not yet follow that there is a speed towards 0. See also the discussion Section
3.1
. A speed towards 0 is necessary to control the time to reach 0. This speed is allowed to go to
0
as
n\to\infty
. However, we only get a meaningful result if the speed does not vanish too quickly, see the theorem below.

3.

(Condition for long persistence of quasi-stationary behaviour.) For some
\varepsilon>0
, independent of
n
, the constant

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=\max_{\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(n)}}{\mu_{k}^{(n)}}\right)^{\text{sgn}(k)}

(92)

satisfies
e^{-1}<\rho_{n}<1-\frac{(2+\varepsilon)\log(a_{n})}{a_{n}}
.

Remark.

Condition (
91
) on the speed already implies that there is a short term drift towards 0, i.e. that
\rho_{n}<1
. If the rates are not too wild, it is natural that
\rho_{n}
goes to
1
as
n\to\infty
. The lower bound on
\rho_{n}
is therefore not really restrictive, but is there for technical reasons. The upper bound ensures that the short term drift is strong enough. Essentially the short term drift says how likely it is to move away from 0, and therefore determines the time spent in the quasi-stationary distribution. The condition guarantees that
\rho_{n}^{-a_{n}/2}
appearing in the theorem below is at least
a_{n}^{(2+\varepsilon)/2}
so that the length of
I_{n}
will at least grow like a positive power of
a_{n}
.

We further introduce the constant

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\max_{\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}(\lambda_{k}^{(n)}+\mu_{k}^{(n)}).

(93)

Observe that

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{n}

\displaystyle\geq|h_{n}(a_{n})|+|h_{n}(-a_{n})|\geq h_{n}(-a_{n})-h_{n}(a_{n})\geq 2d_{n}a_{n}.

(94)

Under the three conditions above, our main result is the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
Z\sim N(0,1)
. Let the walks
M_{t}^{(n)}
satisfy the three conditions above. Suppose
M_{0}^{(n)}
satisfies
\mathbb{P}(-a_{n}/2\leq M^{(n)}_{0}\leq a_{n}/2)=1
for all
n
. Let
c_{n}
be any sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n}=0
. Let

\displaystyle I_{n}=\left\{t\geq 0:d_{n}^{-1}c_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})\leq t\leq c_{n}\kappa_{n}^{-1}\rho_{n}^{-a_{n}/2}\right\}.

(95)

If
I_{n}\neq\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in I_{n}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\tfrac{M_{t}^{(n)}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x\right)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right|=0,\qquad\text{for\ all}\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

(96)

Remark 6.3
.

To maximize the length of
I_{n}
, take for
c_{n}
a slowly decreasing function. Still,
I_{n}
will be empty if
d_{n}
goes to
0
too fast. How fast
d_{n}
can go to
0
depends on the other parameters, but in any case
d_{n}=\Omega(\kappa_{n}/a_{n})
suffices to get
|I_{n}|\geq a_{n}^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
.

Proof of Theorem
6.2
.

Let
W_{t}^{(n)}
be the random walk obtained by restricting
M_{t}^{(n)}
to
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. This means that the two walks have the same rates, except in the boundary points
-a_{n}
and
a_{n}
, where we set the outward rates of
W_{t}^{(n)}
equal to 0. Let
W_{\infty}^{(n)}
be the stationary distribution of
W_{t}^{(n)}
. Then, for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle\left.\!\!M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|+\left|\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|.

(97)

For
t
not too large, it is unlikely that
M_{t}^{(n)}
already has reached the boundary of
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. Therefore, the first term on the right hand side of (
97
) will be small. For
t
not too small, the walk
W_{t}^{(n)}
will be close to its stationary distribution so that the second term will be small. We will make this precise, and find an interval for
t
in which both terms are small.

Let
T_{n}=\inf\{t\geq 0:|M_{t}^{(n)}|=a_{n}\}
. Then we can couple the walks in such a way that
M_{t}^{(n)}=W_{t}^{(n)}
for
t\leq T_{n}
. The goal now is to use Proposition
6.1
to show that
T_{n}
is likely to be large. The conditions on
\rho_{n}
give that for some
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle-1<\log(\rho_{n})<-\frac{(2+\delta)\log(a_{n})}{a_{n}}.

(98)

Since
c_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})\to\infty
, the left hand side implies that
c_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})\gg\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
. Using (
94
), we obtain for
t\in I_{n}
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}t\geq c_{n}^{-1}a_{n}\log(a_{n})\gg a_{n}\log(\rho_{n}^{-1}).

(99)

Combining this with the right hand side of (
98
) gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}t>a_{n}\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})>(2+\delta)\log(a_{n})>\log(\sigma_{n})\to\infty.

(100)

Take
\varepsilon>0
arbitrary and choose
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
as in Proposition
6.1
. For
n
large enough, by (
99
) and (
100
) we get that
\kappa_{n}t\geq C_{1}+C_{2}\cdot a_{n}\log(\rho_{n}^{-1})
, and hence we find

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k,T_{n}\geq t)-\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k,T_{n}\geq t)\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k,T_{n}<t)-\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k,T_{n}<t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}(T_{n}<t)\leq(2+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{a_{n}/2-1},

(101)

For all
t\in I_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|\leq(2+\varepsilon)\kappa_{n}t\rho_{n}^{a_{n}/2-1}\leq(2+\varepsilon)ec_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(102)

For the second term in (
97
), Proposition
5.2
gives

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|\leq 2a_{n}e^{-d_{n}t}.

(103)

Taking
t\in I_{n}
again, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(W_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|\leq 2a_{n}e^{-c_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})}=2a_{n}^{1-c_{n}^{-1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

(104)

The upper bounds for the terms in (
97
) are independent of
t
, so that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in I_{n}}\Big|\mathbb{P}(

\displaystyle\left.\!\!M_{t}^{(n)}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}\leq k)\right|=0.

(105)

Theorem
4.4
yields for all
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}^{(n)}/\sigma_{n}\leq x)\right|\to 0,

(106)

from which we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in I_{n}}\left|\mathbb{P}(M_{t}^{(n)}/\sigma_{n}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right|=0.

(107)

∎

6.1

An example

In this section we illustrate Theorem
6.2
by simulation of a simple example. Let
\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{n}
and
a_{n}=\lceil\sigma_{n}^{3/2}\rceil=\lceil n^{3/4}\rceil
. Consider a sequence of random walks
\left\{M_{t}^{(n)},t\geq 0\right\}
on
\mathbb{Z}
with rates

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(n)}=1-\frac{k}{n},\qquad\mu_{k}^{(n)}=1,

(108)

for
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}
. For the sake of illustration, we take absorbing boundaries, i.e. if the walk leaves
[-a_{n},a_{n}]
and hits
-a_{n}-1
or
a_{n}+1
, it stops moving.
Then condition 1 for the theorem is satisfied. Defining
h_{n}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by
h_{n}(x)=-\frac{x}{n}
, the difference of the rates is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{(n)}-\mu_{k}^{(n)}=-\frac{k}{n}=h_{n}(k).

(109)

Letting
d_{n}=\frac{1}{n}
, the derivative of
f_{n}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}f_{n}(x)=-\frac{1}{n}=-d_{n},

(110)

so that condition 2 is satisfied as well. Taking
\rho_{n}
as in (
92
), we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{n}-1

\displaystyle=-1+\max_{\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{\text{sgn}(k)}\sim-\tfrac{1}{2}a_{n}^{-1/3}\sim-\tfrac{1}{2}n^{-1/4},

(111)

satisfying condition 3. For
\kappa_{n}
we find

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\max_{\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}(\lambda_{k}^{(n)}+\mu_{k}^{(n)})=\max_{\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}\left(2-\frac{k}{n}\right)=2+\frac{a_{n}}{n}\sim 2+n^{-1/4}.

(112)

In this example, the conclusion (
96
) of the theorem holds for any
c_{n}\to 0
and

\displaystyle I_{n}=\{t\geq 0:c_{n}^{-1}n\log(n)\leq t\leq c_{n}e^{\sqrt{n}/4}\}.

(113)

In this time interval, for
n
large, the distribution of the walk
M_{t}^{(n)}
is very close to a Gaussian. In fact, simulations show that convergence to a Gaussian distribution is probably still true in much larger intervals. The constant
1/4
in the exponent of the upper bound of
I_{n}
can presumably be improved by a more subtle estimation of the short term drift (compare with (
92
) and the corresponding remark).

Figure 2:
Distribution of
M_{T}^{(7^{4})}
for four different times
T
compared to the probability density function of
N(0,7^{4})
.

We take a look at this process for
n=7^{4}=2401
. Then
a_{n}=7^{3}=343
and
\sigma_{n}=7^{2}=49
. The walk moves between
\pm 344
and stops when it hits one of these boundaries. We take initial condition
M_{0}^{(n)}=344/2=172
. In view of Theorem
6.2
, we expect the distribution of
M_{t}^{(n)}
to quickly approach a
N(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
distribution and stay close to it for a long time.

This is indeed exactly what we observe, see Figure
2
. We numerically calculated the probability distribution of
M_{T}^{n}
for four different values of
T
. For
T=10^{3}
, the influence of the initial condition
M_{0}^{n}=172
is still clearly visible: the expection of
M_{T}^{(n)}
is about 113. For
T=10^{4}
, the expectation has dropped to 2.7. Letting
f_{n}
be the probability density of the
N(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
, the distance

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-344}^{344}|\mathbb{P}(M_{T}^{(n)}=k)-f_{n}(k)|

(114)

is already as small as 0.05. Then for a long time, the distribution is only very slightly changing. For
T=10^{11}
, the expected position is still very close to 0 (approximately -0.2), while the distance to the Gaussian in the above measure is 0.006. Finally, for
T=10^{13}
, the probability that the walk is absorbed at one of the boundaries is 0.505 and the distance to the Gaussian is 1.01 (essentially twice the absorption probability). Conditioned on the event that it is not yet absorbed, we still recognize the Gaussian shape. Somewhat surprising at first sight, the expected position is now -140. This indicates that absorption on the left is much more likely than absorption on the right.

7

Application: the contact process on
K_{n}

In this section we aim to apply our results to the contact process on the complete graph
K_{n}
. In this graph each node is either infected or healthy. If it is infected, it is healing at rate 1. Furthermore, it is infecting each of its healthy neighbors at rate
\tau
. The number of infected individuals is a simple birth-death process
\{\tilde{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
[n]
with transition rates

\displaystyle k\rightarrow k+1

with rate

\displaystyle k(n-k)\tau

(115)

\displaystyle k\rightarrow k-1

with rate

\displaystyle k

(116)

for all
k=0,\ldots,n
. Take
\tau=\frac{\lambda}{n}
with constant
\lambda>1
and let
k_{0}=\lceil(1-\frac{1}{\lambda})n\rceil=(1-\frac{1}{\lambda})n+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
. Note that
k_{0}
corresponds to the point where the rates up and down are (approximately) balanced. Now consider the process
X_{t}:=\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}
. This is a random walk on
[-k_{0},n-k_{0}]
which is attracted to
0
and to which we will apply Theorem
6.2
. Let the rates up and down of this process be called
\lambda_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
respectively. Then for
-k_{0}\leq k\leq n-k_{0}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=(k+k_{0})(n-(k+k_{0}))\frac{\lambda}{n},\qquad\mu_{k}=k+k_{0}.

(117)

After some calculations, we obtain for
-k_{0}\leq k\leq n-k_{0}-1
the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{k\lambda}{n}+\frac{(k+k_{0}-n)\lambda}{(k+k_{0}+1)n}-\varepsilon\frac{\lambda}{n}.

(118)

Since
k_{0}
is of the same order as
n
, the last two terms will be small if
k
is small compared to
n
. In the first two terms, we recognize that the ratio
\lambda_{k}/\mu_{k+1}
has the desired form (as in (
47
)) for Gaussian quasi-stationary behavior. Indeed, we let

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{\lambda}}

(119)

and suppose that
a_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle a_{n}=o(\sigma_{n}^{2})=o(n),\qquad a_{n}\geq\sigma_{n}\sqrt{(8+\delta)\log(\sigma_{n})}

(120)

for some
\delta>0
, so that (
40
) holds. Take
-a_{n}\leq k\leq a_{n}-1
. Then we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{k\lambda}{n}+\frac{\left(-(\lambda-1)\varepsilon-1\right)n+O(a_{n})}{(1-\tfrac{1}{\lambda})n^{2}+O(na_{n})}

(121)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{k\lambda}{n}\right)\left(1+\delta_{n}\right)

(122)

with
|\delta_{n}|\leq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{(\lambda-1)n}+o(n^{-1})=\frac{\lambda/(\lambda-1)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}+o(\sigma_{n}^{-2})
. This shows that the first condition for Theorem
6.2
is satisfied for
n
large enough. Therefore, the contact process on
K_{n}
can already be predicted to have a quasi-stationary distribution close to a Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}=n/\lambda
and expectation
(1-\frac{1}{\lambda})n
.

We proceed to check the second condition. Define for
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle h_{n}(x)=(\lambda-1)(x+k_{0})-\frac{\lambda}{n}(x+k_{0})^{2}.

(123)

Then
h_{n}(k)=\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}
. By noting that
h_{n}(x)=-(\lambda-1)(x+\varepsilon)-\frac{\lambda}{n}(x+\varepsilon)^{2}
, it follows that the zeros of
h_{n}
are at
x=-k_{0}
and
x=-\varepsilon
.
The former corresponds to extinction, the latter to the expectation of the quasistationary distribution. For
x\in[-a_{n},a_{n}]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}h_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=1-\lambda-\frac{2\lambda}{n}(x+\varepsilon)\leq 1-\lambda+\frac{2\lambda a_{n}}{n}

\displaystyle=1-\lambda+o(1),

(124)

which is negative for
n
large enough, since
\lambda>1
. This guarantees that
X_{t}
will quickly reach it quasi-stationary distribution if it is in the interval
[-a_{n}/2,a_{n}/2]
at time 0. This is a bit restrictive, but we will relax this condition later on.

We now look at the third condition, which makes it hard to escape from the quasi-stationary behavior. The short term drift of the process is

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k}}=1-\frac{k\lambda}{n}-\varepsilon\frac{\lambda}{n}.

(125)

Therefore, using the upper and lower bounds for
a_{n}
in (
120
), we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{n}-1

\displaystyle=-1+\max_{\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\mu_{k}}\right)^{\text{sgn}(k)}\sim-\frac{a_{n}}{2}\frac{\lambda}{n}=-\frac{a_{n}}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\leq-(2+\tilde{\delta})\frac{\log(a_{n})}{a_{n}},

(126)

for some
\tilde{\delta}>0
, which means that the third condition is satisfied as well and consequently all conditions on the rates are fulfilled.

Theorem
6.2
now can be applied to the process
X_{t}
, provided that
X_{0}
is close enough to 0. For the contact process, this means we have to start close to the equilibrium point
k_{0}
. The next lemma will be used to relax this initial condition. If there are enough infected individuals initially, then the number of infected is likely to quickly be close to
k_{0}
, after which we can apply Theorem
6.2
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Consider the contact process
\{\tilde{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
K_{n}
with healing rate
1
and infection rate
\tau=\tfrac{\lambda}{n}
for some
\lambda>1
. Let
c>\frac{2\lambda+2}{\lambda-1}
be a constant and suppose

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{0}\geq c\log(n).

(127)

Let
k_{0}=\left\lceil\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}n\right\rceil
and
a_{n}
as in (
120
) and define

\displaystyle T=\inf\{t\geq 0:k_{0}-\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k_{0}+\frac{a_{n}}{2}\}.

(128)

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(T>\frac{n}{2}\right)=0.

(129)

Proof.

We distinguish two cases,
\tilde{X}_{0}\geq k_{0}
and
\tilde{X}_{0}<k_{0}
. We will show that in the first case
\mathbb{P}(T>t)
goes to 0 exponentially in
t
. The second case requires more carefulness, since
\tilde{X}_{t}
could go extinct before reaching
k_{0}-\frac{a_{n}}{2}
. Hence, for
n
fixed
\mathbb{P}(T=\infty)
is strictly positive. Nevertheless it is still true that
\mathbb{P}(T>t)
as a function of
t
quickly decreases to
\mathbb{P}(T=\infty)
. The latter probability goes to 0 as
n
increases.

Case 1:

\tilde{X}_{0}\geq k_{0}
.
This case is easy, since the translated process
X_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}
can be dominated by a birth-death process with negative linear speed, see Section
3.4
. In this case we can write

\displaystyle T=\inf\{t\geq 0:\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}\leq\frac{a_{n}}{2}\}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}\leq\frac{a_{n}}{2}\}.

(130)

Let
T^{+}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}=0\}
and define the absorbed process by

\displaystyle X_{t}^{+}=\begin{cases}X_{t}&t<T^{+},\\
0&t\geq T^{+}.\end{cases}

(131)

Then
X_{t}^{+}
is a proper birth-death process on
[n-k_{0}]
with extinction time
T^{+}>T
. Taking
h_{n}(x)
as in (
123
), we obtain for all
x>0

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}h_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=-(\lambda-1)-\frac{2\lambda}{n}(x+\varepsilon)<1-\lambda<0.

(132)

Since
h_{n}(0)\leq 0
, the speed of
X_{t}^{+}
is at most a negative linear function of
k
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}-\mu_{k}=h_{n}(k)\leq(1-\lambda)k,\qquad k\geq 0.

(133)

By Lemma
3.4
and a suitable coupling it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T>t)\leq\mathbb{P}(T^{+}>t)\leq(n-k_{0})\cdot e^{(1-\lambda)t},

(134)

which goes to 0 for
t=\tfrac{n}{2}
.

Case 2:

c\log(n)\leq\tilde{X}_{0}=m<k_{0}
. Our argument in this case roughly goes as follows. We will choose some
a\in[1,m]
and we will show that the hitting time of
a
is large by coupling with a birth-death process
Y_{t}
. For
t
less than the hitting time of
a
, we can couple
|\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}|
to a birth death process
Z_{t}
with negative speed, which quickly dies out. The conclusion then is that with high probability
\tilde{X}_{t}
hits
k_{0}-\frac{a_{n}}{2}
before
a
and that
T
is small. See Figure
3
for an illustration of the typical situation.

Figure 3:
Y_{t}
is a birth death process on
[b]
. It hits
a
at time
T_{b,a}
(outside the plot window) and by coupling
Y_{t}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}
for all
t
. In particular
Y_{t}
hits
a
before
\tilde{X}_{t}
does, so
T_{b,a}\leq T_{a}
.
Z_{t}
is a birth death process on
[k_{0}-a]
. The process
\tilde{Z}_{t}=k_{0}-Z_{t}
hits
k_{0}-1
at time
T^{Z}
. By coupling
\tilde{Z}_{t}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}
for
t\leq\min\{T^{Z},T_{a}\}
. Both
Y_{t}
and
\tilde{Z}_{t}
have a positive drift, so typically
T_{a}
is much larger than
T^{Z}
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
with
a\leq b\leq\min\{m,(k_{0}-\varepsilon)/2\}
(remember that
k_{0}-\varepsilon=(1-1/\lambda)n
). We will first use Proposition
3.2
to control the hitting time of
a
, defined by

\displaystyle T_{a}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\tilde{X}_{t}=a\}=\inf\{t\geq 0:X_{t}=a-k_{0}\}.

(135)

Let
\{Y_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}\}
be a birth-death process on
[b]
with birth rate
\tilde{\lambda}_{k}=k(n-k)\lambda/n
and death rate
\tilde{\mu}_{k}=k
. For
k=b
, we set the birth rate equal to 0. Let
Y_{0}=b
and
T_{b,a}=\inf\{t\geq 0:Y_{t}=a\}
. Clearly we can couple
Y_{t}
and
\tilde{X}_{t}
such that
Y_{t}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}
for all
t
. Let

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq k\leq b}\tilde{\lambda}_{k}+\tilde{\mu}_{k}=\max_{0\leq k\leq b}(1+\lambda)k-\tfrac{\lambda}{n}k^{2},

(136)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}

\displaystyle=\max_{0\leq k\leq b}\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{k}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{k}}=\max_{0\leq k\leq b}\frac{1}{\lambda(1-\frac{k}{n})}.

(137)

Then

\displaystyle b\leq\tilde{\kappa}\leq(1+\lambda)b,\qquad\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq\tilde{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda(1-\frac{k_{0}-\varepsilon}{2n})}=\frac{2}{\lambda+1}<1.

(138)

Proposition
3.2
applies to
Y_{t}
. Let
\delta>0
and choose
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
as in that proposition.
Suppose
b\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and let
C_{3}>C_{2}\log(\lambda)
. Then

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}t\geq bt\geq C_{1}+C_{2}b\log(\lambda)\geq C_{1}+C_{2}b\log(\tilde{\rho}^{-1})

(139)

if
t>C_{3}
and
n
large enough, Proposition
3.2
then gives a bound on the extinction time of
Y_{t}
. In fact, we can bound
T_{a,b}
analogously if
b-a\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. We obtain (for
t>C_{3}
and
n
large enough) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{b,a}<t)\leq C_{4}(b-a)t\left(\frac{2}{\lambda+1}\right)^{b-a},

(140)

with
C_{4}=(1+\delta)(1+\lambda)
. By the coupling
Y_{t}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}
, we have
T_{b,a}\leq T_{a}
so that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{a}<t)\leq C_{4}(b-a)t\left(\frac{2}{\lambda+1}\right)^{b-a}.

(141)

The second step in our argument involves another coupling. Consider the distance
|X_{t}|=k_{0}-\tilde{X}_{t}
before
\tilde{X}_{t}
hits
a
or
k_{0}
. If
|X_{t}|=k
for some
0<k\leq k_{0}-a
, then
X_{t}=-k
. Therefore, the distance increases at rate
\mu_{-k}
and decreases at rate
\lambda_{-k}
. We will couple the distance to a birth-death process
\{Z_{t},t\in\mathbb{R}\}
on
[k_{0}-a]
, having birth rates
\lambda_{k}^{Z}
and death rates
\mu_{k}^{Z}
defined by

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{Z}

\displaystyle:=\mu_{-k}=k_{0}-k,

\displaystyle\lambda^{Z}_{0}=\lambda^{Z}_{k_{0}-a}=0,

(142)

\displaystyle\mu_{k}^{Z}

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{-k}=(k_{0}-k)(n-(k_{0}-k))\frac{\lambda}{n},

\displaystyle\mu^{Z}_{0}=0.

(143)

Take initial condition
Z_{0}=k_{0}-a
, and let

\displaystyle T^{Z}=\inf\{t:Z_{t}=1\}.

(144)

Couple such that
k_{0}-\tilde{X}_{t}\leq Z_{t}
for all
t\leq\min\{T^{Z},T_{a}\}
. If
T^{Z}\leq T_{a}
, then
T<T^{Z}
. Therefore,
T^{Z}
satisfies
T^{Z}>\min\{T,T_{a}\}
. To bound
T^{Z}
, we will apply Lemma
3.3
. First we calculate the speed

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{Z}-\mu_{k}^{Z}

\displaystyle=(k_{0}-k)(\frac{n}{\lambda}-n+k_{0}-k)\frac{\lambda}{n}

(145)

\displaystyle=(k_{0}-k)(\varepsilon-k)\frac{\lambda}{n}\leq-(k-1)(k_{0}-k)\frac{\lambda}{n}.

(146)

For
1\leq k\leq k_{0}-a
, this speed is negative and convex in
k
. It therefore can be upper bounded by a linear function which intersects the right hand side of (
146
) for
k=1
and
k=k_{0}-a
. This gives

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}^{Z}-\mu_{k}^{Z}\leq-\frac{a(k-1)\lambda}{n}.

(147)

We now find the differential inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]\leq-\frac{a\lambda}{n}\cdot(\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]-1).

(148)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[Z_{t}]-1\leq(k_{0}-a-1)\cdot e^{-\tfrac{a\lambda}{n}t},

(149)

and hence we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{Z}>t)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{t}-1\geq 1)\leq(k_{0}-a-1)\cdot e^{-\tfrac{a\lambda}{n}t}.

(150)

Now we can finish our argument by using (
141
) and (
150
) and the couplings between
\tilde{X}_{t}
,
Y_{t}
and
Z_{t}
. For
n
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T>t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(T>t,T_{a}<t)+\mathbb{P}(T>t,T_{a}\geq t)

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(T_{a}<t)+\mathbb{P}(\min\{T,T_{a}\}\geq t)

(152)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(T_{a}<t)+\mathbb{P}(T^{Z}>t)

(153)

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}(b-a)t\left(\frac{2}{\lambda+1}\right)^{b-a}+k_{0}\cdot e^{-\tfrac{a\lambda}{n}t}.

(154)

Let
a=\tfrac{c}{2}\log(n)
. Choose
b=2a
and
t=\tfrac{n}{2}
. Then
b\leq m
as required and
\frac{c}{2}>\tfrac{\lambda+1}{\lambda-1}
. For the first term in (
154
) this gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T_{a}<\tfrac{n}{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{4}c}{4}\log(n)n^{1+\tfrac{c}{2}\log\left(\frac{2}{\lambda+1}\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

(155)

using that
1+\tfrac{c}{2}\log(\tfrac{2}{\lambda+1})<0
for
\lambda>1
.
For the second term in (
154
), since
c\lambda/4>1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(T^{Z}>\tfrac{n}{2})

\displaystyle\leq k_{0}e^{-c\lambda\log(n)/4}\leq n^{1-\tfrac{c\lambda}{4}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

(156)

giving the desired conclusion.
∎

We are ready to state our metastability result for the contact process on the complete graph
K_{n}
with infection rate
\tau=\lambda/n
for some
\lambda>1
. At
t=0
, we can start with a quite small set of infected nodes (thanks to Lemma
7.1
). The number of infected individuals will rapidly go towards its equilibrium
\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}n
. In a large time window (from
t
linear in
n
to almost exponential in
n
), the number of infected individuals will behave like a Gaussian centered at
\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}n
and with variance
n/\lambda
. The time window in the theorem is maximized by taking a function
g(n)
which goes to 0 slowly.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\lambda>1
. Consider for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the contact process
\{\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}\}_{t\geq 0}
on the complete graph
K_{n}
with healing rate
1
and infection rate
\tau=\tfrac{\lambda}{n}
. Let
c>\frac{2(\lambda+2)}{\lambda-1}
be a constant and suppose that the initial distributions satisfy

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{0}^{(n)}\geq c\log(n))=1.

(157)

Let
Z\sim N(0,1)
and define
\mu=\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}n
and
\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{\tfrac{n}{\lambda}}
. Let
g(n)
be any function with
\lim_{n\to\infty}g(n)=0
and
g(n)\geq\log(n)/n
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in\left[n,\exp(g(n)\cdot n)\right]}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tfrac{\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}-\mu}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert=0.

(158)

Proof.

The translated process
X_{t}^{(n)}=\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}-k_{0}
satisfies all conditions for Theorem
6.2
, with the relevant quantities (for some
\delta>0
) defined as follows (cf. (
119
),(
120
),(
124
),(
126
)).

\displaystyle\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{n}{\lambda}},

\displaystyle(2+\delta)\sqrt{\frac{n\log(n)}{\lambda}}\leq a_{n}\ll n,

(159)

\displaystyle d_{n}=\lambda-1+o(1),

\displaystyle\rho_{n}=1-\frac{\lambda a_{n}}{2n}+o\!\left(\frac{a_{n}}{n}\right)

(160)

For
\kappa_{n}
, using the rates in (
117
), we find

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}=\max_{\frac{a_{n}}{2}\leq|k|\leq a_{n}}(\lambda_{k}+\mu_{k})=2k_{0}+\mathcal{O}(a_{n})=\frac{2(\lambda-1)}{\lambda}n+o(n).

(161)

Now we can compute the bounds of the interval
I_{n}
as defined in Theorem
6.2
.

\displaystyle d_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})=o(\log(n)).

(162)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}^{-1}\rho_{n}^{-a_{n}/2}=\exp\left(\frac{\lambda a_{n}^{2}}{4n}+o\left(\frac{a_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)\right).

(163)

Define
c_{n}\to 0
by

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{2d_{n}^{-1}\log(a_{n})}{n}=o\left(\frac{\log(n)}{n}\right),

(164)

and let
a_{n}=2n\sqrt{g(n)}
for some
g(n)
that (slowly) goes to 0. Then, for large
n
,

\displaystyle c_{n}\kappa^{-1}\rho_{n}^{-a_{n}/2}=\exp\left((\lambda+o(1))g(n)\cdot n\right)\geq\exp(g(n)\cdot n),

(165)

so that the interval
I_{n}
contains the interval
\left[\tfrac{n}{2},\exp(g(n)\cdot n)\right]
.

Fix
n
, write
\tilde{X}_{t}
for
\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}
and assume that
c\log(n)\leq\tilde{X}_{0}<k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}
. Let
T=\inf\{t\geq 0:k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\leq\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k_{0}+\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\}
. Note that for all
t\geq 0
and all
k
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k)=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k\mid T\leq\tfrac{n}{2})

\displaystyle-\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k\mid T\leq\tfrac{n}{2})\cdot\mathbb{P}(T>\tfrac{n}{2})

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k\mid T>\tfrac{n}{2})\cdot\mathbb{P}(T>\tfrac{n}{2}).

(166)

Let
\{\tilde{Y}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be an independent realization of the contact process on
K_{n}
, with initial condition
\tilde{Y}_{0}=\lceil k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\rceil
. If
T\leq\tfrac{n}{2}
, then
\tilde{X}_{T}=\lceil k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\rceil
. In this case
\tilde{X}_{t+T}
has the same distribution as
\tilde{Y}_{t}
, since the contact process is a Markov process. Therefore, for all
k
, all
t\geq\tfrac{n}{2}
and any real number
x
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{t}\leq k\mid T\leq\tfrac{n}{2})-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq\tfrac{n}{2}}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{t-s}\leq k)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert.

(167)

Instead of
k
, we could take any real
y
. Taking
y=\sigma_{n}x+k_{0}
and writing
Y_{t}
for
\tilde{Y}_{t}-k_{0}
, the probability on the right can be written

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{t-s}\leq y)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{t-s}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x\right).

(168)

Substitute this and take the supremum over
t\in[n,e^{g(n)\cdot n}]
:

\displaystyle\sup_{n\leq t\leq\exp(g(n)\cdot n)}\sup_{0\leq s\leq\tfrac{n}{2}}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{t-s}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x\right)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt=\sup_{\tfrac{n}{2}\leq t\leq\exp(g(n)\cdot n)}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{t}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x\right)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert

(169)

Finally, using (
7
) we obtain

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\sup_{t\in[n,\exp(g(n)\cdot n)]}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tfrac{\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(T>\tfrac{n}{2})+\sup_{t\in[\tfrac{n}{2},\exp(g(n)\cdot n)]}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tfrac{Y_{t}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert

(170)

Similar arguments give the same conclusion for
\tilde{X}_{0}>k_{0}+\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}
(with
\tilde{Y}_{0}=\lceil k_{0}+\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\rceil
). For
k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}\leq\tilde{X}_{0}\leq k_{0}+\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}
, we can just take
\tilde{Y}_{t}=\tilde{X}_{t}
. Hence, for any initial distribution
\tilde{X}_{0}\geq c\log(n)
, there is some initial distribution
\tilde{Y}_{0}
on
[k_{0}-\tfrac{a_{n}}{2},k_{0}+\tfrac{a_{n}}{2}]
such that (
7
)
holds. By Theorem
6.2
and Lemma
7.1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[n,\exp(g(n)\cdot n)]}\left\lvert\mathbb{P}(\tfrac{\tilde{X}_{t}-k_{0}}{\sigma_{n}}\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq x)\right\rvert=0.

(171)

Clearly it is also sufficient if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\tilde{X}_{0}\geq c\log(n))=1
, since
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{(n)}\leq x)
converges to
\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{(n)}\leq x\mid\tilde{X}_{0}\geq c\log(n))
, and (
171
) applies to the conditioned version of
X_{t}^{(n)}
. As a final remark, note that we can replace
\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}-k_{0}
in the conclusion by
\tilde{X}_{t}^{(n)}-\mu
since
\tfrac{k_{0}-\mu}{\sigma_{n}}\to 0
.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of a technical lemma

The following lemma is used in the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
b\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\log(\sigma)}
we have

\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma-(1+2\sqrt{2\pi})\leq\sum_{k=-b}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma+1.

In particular, this implies for
\sigma
large enough

\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{-b/\sigma^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=-b}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma e^{b/\sigma^{2}}.

Proof.

Upper bound (for all
b\in\mathbb{N}
and all
\sigma>0
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-b}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\leq 1+2\int_{0}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}ds

\displaystyle=1+\sigma\int_{-b/\sigma}^{b/\sigma}e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^{2}}ds\leq 1+\sigma\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{1}{2}s^{2}}ds=1+\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}

\displaystyle=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}(1+\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}})\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}e^{1/\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\leq\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}e^{b/\sigma^{2}}.

(172)

Lower bound (using a Chernoff bound for the tail of a standard Gaussian
Z
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=-b}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{k^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\geq 1+2\int_{1}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}ds

\displaystyle\geq-1+\int_{-b}^{b}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{s^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}ds\geq-1+\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(|Z|\leq\sqrt{2\log(\sigma)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq-1+\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot(1-2e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2\log(\sigma)}^{2}}{2}})=-1-2\sqrt{2\pi}+\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}

\displaystyle=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}(1-\frac{1+2\sqrt{2\pi}}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}})\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-3/\sigma}\geq\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-b/\sigma^{2}},

(173)

for
\sigma
large enough.
∎
