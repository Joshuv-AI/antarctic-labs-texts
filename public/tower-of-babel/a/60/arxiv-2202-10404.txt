Solving Poisson’s Equation: Existence, Uniqueness, Martingale Structure, and CLT
Peter W. Glynn, Alex Infanger

Abstract
The solution of Poisson’s equation plays a key role in constructing the martingale through which sums of Markov correlated random variables can be analyzed. In this paper, we study two different representations for the solution in countable state space, one based on regenerative structure and the other based on an infinite sum of expectations. We also consider integrability and related uniqueness issues associated with solutions to Poisson’s equation, and provide verifiable Lyapunov conditions to support our theory. Our key results include a central limit theorem and law of the iterated logarithm for Markov dependent sums, under Lyapunov conditions weaker than have previously appeared in the literature.

Introduction

Let
X=(X_{n}:n\geq 0)
be an irreducible positive recurrent Markov chain taking values in a finite or countably infinite state space
S
. We let
P=(P(x,y):x,y\in S)
be the one-step transition matrix of
X
, and let
\pi=(\pi(x):x\in S)
be its associated (unique) stationary distribution (encoded as a row vector). Given a function
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(encoded as a column vector), we say that a function
g
is a solution of
Poisson’s equation
(for
f
) if

\displaystyle(P-I)g=-f.

(1.1)

Poisson’s equation is fundamental to the analysis of the additive functional
S_{n}(f)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})
, since, in the presence of integrability,

\displaystyle g(X_{n})+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})

(1.2)

should then be a martingale adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
, where
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{j}:j\leq n)
. The martingale representation (
1.2
) can then be used to advantage in computing expected values (e.g. via optional sampling), and in deriving the law of large numbers, central limit theorem, and law of the iterated logarithm for
S_{n}(f)
; see, for example,
Maigret 1978
and
Kurtz 1981
. It is also fundamental to obtaining bounds on the
k
’th moment
E_{x}|S_{n}(f)|^{k}
(where
E_{x}(\cdot)
is the expectation corresponding to the probability
P_{x}(\cdot)=P(\cdot|X_{0}=x)
) via the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality; see
Hall and
Heyde 1980
. It is worth noting that (
1.1
) also arises implicitly within the optimality equation for average reward/average case stochastic control problems, since the value function under the optimal policy must satisfy (
1.1
); see
Ross 2014
.

Given the central importance of (
1.1
) within applied probability, this paper is intended to clarify the question of existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.1
) when
|S|=\infty
. Assuming
g\in L^{p}(\pi)\overset{\Delta}{=}\{h:\sum_{x\in S}\pi(x)|h(x)|^{p}<\infty\}
for
p\geq 1
, (
1.1
) implies that
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
and
\pi f=0
. If
f\in L_{0}^{1}(\pi)=\{h:L^{1}(\pi):\pi h=0\}
,
|S|<\infty
, and
X
is aperiodic, it turns out that a solution
g
to (
1.1
) is given either by

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f(X_{n})

or by

\displaystyle g_{2}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}E_{x}f(X_{n}),

where
\tau(z)=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}=z\}
for
z\in S
. In this finite state setting, both
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are always finite-valued solutions to (
1.1
) and
g_{1}(x)-g_{2}(x)
is constant as a function of
x\in S
.

When
|S|=\infty
, both existence of solutions of (
1.1
) and the corresponding uniqueness issues are more subtle. In particular, this paper shows that

a)

While
g_{1}
is always a solution to (
1.1
) when
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
,
g_{2}
can sometimes fail to be well-defined (see Theorem
1
and Example
2
);

b)

The set of solutions to (
1.1
) can be infinite-dimensional (see Example
1
);

c)

When
g_{1}
is used in (
1.2
), (
1.2
) is guaranteed to be integrable and hence a martingale (Theorem
1
);

d)

The set of functions
g
for which (
1.2
) is a martingale can be infinite-dimensional (Example
1
), but we show that it becomes one-dimensional when we require a certain uniform integrability property (Theorem
6
);

e)

An approximation to
E_{x}S_{n}(f)
can be developed when
g_{1}\in L^{1}(\pi)
(Theorem
3
), and a Lyapunov criterion is provided (Theorem
5
);

f)

The function
g_{2}
is well-defined under an extra condition (Theorem
4
) that can be verified through an associated Lyapunov criterion (Theorem
5
);

g)

Lyapunov criteria, weaker than those previously developed in the literature, are provided that guarantee the central limit theorem (CLT) and law of the iterated logarithm (LIL) for
S_{n}(f)
(Theorems
7
and
8
).

As we shall see in what follows, the conditions we will develop are close to being minimal. Section
2
contains the key results on Poisson’s equation, while Section
3
discusses two examples that illustrate mathematical subtleties associated with (
1.1
). Section
4
describes the CLT and LIL for
S_{n}(f)
. As an application of our theory,
Glynn and
Infanger 2022
uses the characterization
g_{1}
of the solution to establish that solutions of Poisson’s equation are always monotone when the chain is stochastically monotone and
f
is monotone, without demanding the summability implicit in
g_{2}
.

2
A Regenerative Representation for the Solution and Related Lyapunov Conditions

As noted in the Introduction, we assume throughout this paper that
X
is irreducible and positive recurrent. This implies that

\displaystyle E_{z}\tau(z)<\infty

(2.1)

for each
z\in S
. This, in turn, implies that
E_{x}\tau(z)<\infty
for
x,z\in S
, since

\displaystyle\infty>E_{z}\tau(z)

\displaystyle\geq E_{z}\tau(z)I(\tau(x)<\tau(z))

\displaystyle\geq P_{z}\left(\tau(x)<\tau(z)\right)E_{x}\tau(z).

(2.2)

Of course,
P_{z}(\tau(x)<\tau(z))>0
(because
x
can not be reached from
z
if
P_{x}(\tau(x)<\tau(z))=0
), thereby establishing that
E_{x}\tau(z)<\infty
.

Similarly, if
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
, the regenerative structure of
X
implies that for each
z\in S
,

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S}\pi(x)|f(x)|=\frac{E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|}{E_{z}\tau(z)},

and hence

\displaystyle E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|<\infty.

Similarly as with
E_{x}\tau(z)<\infty
, this implies that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|<\infty

(2.3)

for
x,z\in S
.

We conclude that if
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
, then for each
x,z\in S
,

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})

is finite-valued, where
f_{c}(x)=f(x)-\pi f
for
x\in S
. Our first result asserts that
g_{z}=(g_{z}(x):x\in S)
is a solution of Poisson’s equation for
f_{c}
, namely it satisfies

\displaystyle(P-I)g=-f_{c}.

(2.4)

It also establishes that for any
y,x\in S
,
g_{z}
and
g_{y}
differ only by an additive constant. Let
e=(e(x):x\in S)
be the (constant) function for which
e(x)=1
for
x\in S
, and put
a\land b\overset{\Delta}{=}\min(a,b)
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
. Then,

a)

For each
z\in S
,
g_{z}
is a finite-valued function that satisfies (
2.4
);

b)

For each
y,z\in S
,
g_{z}-g_{y}=g_{z}(y)e
.

Proof.

We have already established that
g_{z}
is finite-valued. For
x\in S
, conditioning on
X_{1}
shows that

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=f_{c}(x)+\sum_{y\neq z}P(x,y)g_{z}(y).

(2.5)

But
\pi f_{c}=0
, and the regenerative structure of
X
implies that

\displaystyle\pi f_{c}=\frac{E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})}{E_{z}\tau(z)},

so that
g_{z}(z)=0
. Hence (
2.5
) yields the identity

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)=f_{c}(x)+\sum_{y\in S}P(x,y)g_{z}(y),

which is
a)
. As for
b)
, note that

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(y)\land\tau(z))-1}f_{c}(X_{j})+P_{x}(\tau(y)<\tau(z))g_{z}(y).

(2.6)

Since the identity also holds for
g_{y}(x)
(with
y
playing the role of
z
), we find that

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)-g_{y}(x)=g_{z}(y)-P_{x}(\tau(z)<\tau(y))(g_{y}(z)+g_{z}(y))

(2.7)

for
x\in S
. If we set
x=y
in (
2.7
) and note that irreducibility implies that
P_{z}(\tau(z)<\tau(y))>0,
we find that
g_{y}(z)-g_{z}(y)=0
, yielding b).
∎

Remark 1
.

Theorem
1
is a special case of Theorem 1 and 2 of
Derman and
Veinott 1967
; see also
Schaufele 1967
. Our proof is easier because we assume irreducibility.

We say that
(M_{n}:n\geq 0)
is a
P_{x}-
martingale
adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
if
(M_{n}:n\geq 0)
is adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
,
E_{x}|M_{n}|<\infty
, and
E_{x}[M_{n+1}|\mathcal{F}_{n}]=M_{n}
a.s. for
n\geq 0
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
. Then, for all
x,z\in S
,

\displaystyle g_{z}(X_{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{c}(X_{j})

(2.8)

is a
P_{x}
-martingale adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
.

Proof.

The only non-trivial issue that needs to be addressed is the
P_{x}
-integrability of the martingale. Note that for
n\geq 0
, because
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
,

\displaystyle\pi(x)E_{x}|f_{c}(X_{n})|\leq\sum_{y\in S}\pi(y)E_{y}|f_{c}(X_{n})|=\sum_{y\in S}\pi(y)|f_{c}(y)|<\infty

(2.9)

due to stationarity, and hence
f_{c}(X_{n})
is
P_{x}
-integrable for
n\geq 0
.

As for the integrability of
g_{z}(X_{n})
, we observe that for
x\neq z
,

\displaystyle a_{n}(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}E_{x}|g_{z}(X_{n})|=E_{x}|E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{x}(\tau(z)=j)a_{n-j}(z).

(2.10)

As for
(a_{n}(z):n\geq 0)
, it satisfies the renewal equation

\displaystyle a_{n}(z)=E_{z}|E_{z}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{z}(\tau(z)=j)a_{n-j}(z)

for
n\geq 0
, the solution of which is

\displaystyle a_{n}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}P_{z}(X_{j}=z)b_{n-j}(z),

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle b_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle\leq E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

for each
x\in S
, on account of (
2.3
); see Chapter 13 of
Feller 1968
for a discussion of discrete-time renewal theory. Hence,
(b_{n}(x):n\geq 0)
is a finite-valued sequence for each
x\in S
, so that (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) imply the finiteness of
(a_{n}(x):n\geq 0)
for
x\in S
, yielding the
P_{x}
-integrability of
g_{z}(X_{n})
.
∎

Remark 2
.

Our result shows that whenever
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
, (
2.8
) is a

-martingale. Discussion of the martingale (

) appears elsewhere (e.g. in

Remark 3
.

For
\mu=(\mu(x):x\in S)
a probability on
S
, let
P_{\mu}(\cdot)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{x\in S}\mu(x)P_{x}(\cdot)
be the probability on the path-space of
X
under which
X
has initial distribution

, and let

be its corresponding expectation operator. Then (

As a consequence of Theorem

,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}E_{x}f(X_{j})=n\pi f+g_{z}(x)-E_{x}g_{z}(X_{n})

(2.12)

for

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
and that there exists
z\in S
for which

\displaystyle E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}j|f_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty.

(2.13)

Then,
g_{y}\in L^{1}(\pi)
for all

, and
(
2.8
)
is a
P_{\pi}
-martingale adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
. Furthermore, if

is aperiodic, then

\displaystyle E_{x}g_{z}(X_{n})\rightarrow\pi g_{z}=\frac{E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(j+1)f_{c}(X_{j})}{E_{z}\tau(z)}

(2.14)

as

.

Proof.

As in the argument leading to (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), we find that

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n}(x)=E_{x}g_{z}(X_{n})=\tilde{b}_{n}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}P_{x}(\tau(z)=k)\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\tilde{b}_{j}(z)P_{z}(X_{n-k-j}=z)

(2.15)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{n}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})I(\tau(z)>n).

As a consequence of the aperiodicity,
P_{z}(X_{n}=z)\rightarrow\pi(z)
as

. Also,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\tilde{b}_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(z)-1}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|\sum_{n=0}^{j}1

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty.

(2.16)

With (
2.16
) in hand, we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to (
2.15
), thereby yielding (
2.14
).

Furthermore, the regenerative structure of

implies that if
\beta_{n}(z)=\inf\{j>n:X_{j}=z\}
, then

\displaystyle E_{z}\tau(z)E_{\pi}|g_{z}(X_{0})|

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|g_{z}(X_{n})|

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|E_{z}\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle\leq E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{n=0}^{\tau(z)-1}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|,

(2.17)

which equals the right-hand side of (
2.16
). It follows that
g_{z}\in L^{1}(\pi)
. But
g_{y}
is equal to
g_{z}
(up to an additive constant), so
g_{y}\in L^{1}(\pi)
for
y\in S
. Obviously, if
g_{y}\in L^{1}(\pi)
, then
g_{y}(X_{n})+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_{c}(X_{j})
is a

-martingale, concluding the argument.

The expression for
\pi g_{z}
follows from the same argument as that leading to (
2.17
).
∎

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
, we see that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f(X_{j})=n\pi f+g_{z}(x)-\pi g_{z}+o(1)

(2.18)

as

, where

The next result shows that (
2.13
) is a “solidarity property”, in the sense that if it holds for one
z\in S
, then it holds for each
y\in S
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
and (
2.13
) is valid for some
z\in S
. Then, (
2.13
) holds for all
z\in S
.

Proof.

We note that the regenerative structure of
X
implies that

\displaystyle\infty>E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau(z)-1}\sum_{j=k}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\tau(z)-1}k_{z}(X_{\ell})=E_{z}\tau(z)\cdot\pi k_{z}

where
k_{z}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|
for
x\in S
. If
y\neq z
, relation (
2.3
) implies that
E_{y}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|k_{z}(X_{\ell})|<\infty
. Also,

\displaystyle E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|

\displaystyle=E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\sum_{k=j}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle=E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}I(\beta_{j}(z)\geq\tau(y))\sum_{k=j}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle\qquad+E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}I(\beta_{j}(z)<\tau(y))\sum_{k=j}^{\beta_{j}(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle\qquad+E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}I(\beta_{j}(z)<\tau(y))\sum_{k=\beta_{j}(z)}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\sum_{k=j}^{\beta_{j}(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle\qquad+E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}I(\beta_{j}(z)<\tau(y))E_{y}[\sum_{k=\beta_{j}(z)}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|\ |X_{0},...,X_{\beta_{j}(z)}]

\displaystyle=E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}k_{z}(X_{j})+E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}I(\beta_{j}(z)<\tau(y))E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}k_{z}(X_{j})+E_{y}\tau(y)\cdot E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|f_{c}(X_{k})|<\infty,

where we have used (
2.3
) in the last line, thereby proving the result.
∎

Our final result in this section on the behavior of
\sum_{j=0}^{n}E_{x}f_{c}(X_{j})
concerns the development of a regenerative criterion that ensures the validity of

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|E_{x}f_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

for
x\in S
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
and that there exists
z\in S
for which (
2.13
) holds and
E_{z}\tau(z)^{2}<\infty
. If
X
is aperiodic, then (
2.19
) holds for all
x\in S
.

Proof.

We first note that
E_{z}\tau(z)^{2}<\infty
is a solidarity property, in the sense that if it holds for one
z\in S
, then it holds for all
z\in S
; see p.84 of
Chung 1967
. As a consequence of Proposition
1
, we may assume (
2.13
) and
E_{z}\tau(z)^{2}<\infty
hold for
z=x
.

Then, if
a_{n}^{*}=E_{x}f_{c}(X_{n})
it follows that it satisfies the renewal equation

\displaystyle a_{n}^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{x}(\tau(x)=j)a^{*}_{n-j}+b_{n}^{*},

where
b_{n}^{*}=E_{x}f_{c}(X_{n})I(\tau(x)>n)
. So,

\displaystyle a_{n}^{*}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}P_{x}(X_{j}=x)b_{n-j}^{*}.

Recalling that
0=\pi f_{c}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{j}^{*}
, we find that

\displaystyle a_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\pi(x)\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{n-j}^{*}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}(P_{x}(X_{j}=x)-\pi(x))b_{n-j}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\pi(x)\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}b_{j}^{*}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}(P_{x}(X_{j}=x)-\pi(x))b_{n-j}^{*}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq\pi(x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}|b_{j}^{*}|+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|P_{x}(X_{n-j}=x)-\pi(x)||b_{j}^{*}|

\displaystyle\leq\pi(x)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|E_{x}f_{c}(X_{j})I(\tau(x)>j)|+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|b_{j}^{*}|\sum_{n=j}^{\infty}|P_{z}(X_{n-j}=x)-\pi(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\pi(x)E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(x)>j)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(x)>j)\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|P_{x}(X_{n}=x)-\pi(x)|

\displaystyle=\pi(x)E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(x)-1}(j+1)|f_{c}(X_{j})|+E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(x)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|P_{x}(X_{n}=x)-\pi(x)|<\infty,

provided that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|P_{x}(X_{n}=x)-\pi(x)|<\infty.

(2.20)

The finiteness of (
2.20
) follows from Proposition 11.1.1 and Theorem 13.4.5 of
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
.
∎

Remark 4
.

Observe that when (

\displaystyle g^{*}(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{x}f_{c}(X_{j})

g^{*}=(g^{*}(x):x\in S)
is an alternative means of representing the solution to Poisson’s equation (
2.4
).

We turn next to the development of Lyapunov criteria that characterize our weakened sufficient conditions under which solutions of Poisson’s equation exist, and exhibit the summability (Theorem
4
) and limiting behaviors (Theorem
3
) described above.

Our results are a simple consequence of the following special case of the Comparison Theorem; see p.344 of
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
X=(X_{n}:n\geq 0)
is an irreducible and recurrent Markov chain. Assume that there exists
v:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
w:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and a finite subset
K\subseteq S
for which
E_{x}v(X_{1})<\infty
for
x\in K
and

\displaystyle E_{x}v(X_{1})\leq v(x)-w(x)

(2.21)

for
x\in K^{c}
. Then, there exists
c<\infty
such that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}w(X_{j})\leq v(x)+c

(2.22)

for
x\in S
and
z\in K
.

Proof.

We first note that if we set

\displaystyle d=\max_{x\in K}[E_{x}v(X_{1})-v(x)+w(x)],

then (
2.21
) implies that

\displaystyle E_{x}v(X_{1})\leq v(x)-w(x)+dI(x\in K)

for
x\in S
. We then apply the Comparison Theorem to conclude that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}w(X_{j})\leq v(x)+dE_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}I(X_{j}\in K)

for
x\in S
. If
\Lambda_{0}=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in K\}
, with
\Lambda_{m}=\inf\{n>\Lambda_{m-1}:X_{n}\in K\}
for
n\geq 1
, the recurrence of
X
implies that the
\Lambda_{m}
’s are finite-valued. Furthermore,
(X_{\Lambda_{m}}:m\geq 0)
is the Markov chain on
K
that records the successive visits of
X
to
K
. Obviously, for
x\in S
,

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}I(X_{j}\in K)\leq E_{x}\gamma(z)

where
\gamma(z)=\inf\{m\geq 1:X_{\Lambda_{m}}=z\}
. Since
X
is irreducible, the finite-state Markov chain
(X_{\Lambda_{m}}:m\geq 0)
is irreducible. The finiteness of
|K|
implies that
E_{y}\gamma(z)<\infty
for
y,z\in K
. If we set
c=d\max\{E_{y}\gamma(z):y,z\in K\}
, we obtain (
2.22
).
∎

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that
X
is an irreducible Markov chain, and let
K\subseteq S
be a finite subset. Consider the following conditions:

a)

There exists
v_{1}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
(Pv_{1})(x)<\infty
for
x\in K
and

\displaystyle(Pv_{1})(x)\leq v_{1}(x)-1

for
x\in K^{c}
;

b)

There exists
v_{2}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
(Pv_{2})(x)<\infty
for
x\in K
and

\displaystyle(Pv_{2})(x)\leq v_{2}(x)-|f(x)|

for
x\in K^{c}
;

c)

There exist
v_{3}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v_{4}:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
(Pv_{3})(x)+(Pv_{4})(x)<\infty
for
x\in K
,

\displaystyle(Pv_{3})(x)\leq v_{3}(x)-v_{1}(x),

and

\displaystyle(Pv_{4})(x)\leq v_{4}(x)-v_{2}(x).

Then:

i)

If
a)
holds,
X
is positive recurrent and a unique stationary distribution
\pi
exists.

ii)

If
a)
and
b)
hold, then Theorems
1
and
2
are valid.

iii)

If
a)
,
b),
and
c)
hold, then Theorems
1
through
4
are valid.

Proof.

Condition
a)
and Proposition 2 imply that
E_{x}\tau(z)\leq v_{1}(x)+c
for
x\in S
, whereas condition
b)
implies that for
x\in S
,

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|\leq v_{2}(x)+c

(2.23)

for some
c\in K
, proving
i)
and
ii)
when we set
x=z
. (Of course, these results are well known; see
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
.) If
c)
is also in force, then Proposition 2 implies that

\displaystyle E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}v_{1}(X_{j})\leq v_{3}(z)+c

and

\displaystyle E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}v_{2}(X_{j})\leq v_{4}(z)+c.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\infty>E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(\tau(z)-j)=E_{z}\frac{\tau(z)(\tau(z)+1)}{2}

and

\displaystyle\infty>E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}\sum_{k=j}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{k})|=E_{z}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(k+1)|f(X_{k})|,

for
z\in K
, proving part
iii)
for
z\in K
. These conditions imply Theorems
3
and
4
.
∎

Remark 5
.

As usual, these Lyapunov conditions are also necessary conditions, in order that the required moments of Theorems 1 through 4 be finite (for
f
replacing
f_{c}
). For example, in order that

\displaystyle E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(j+1)|f(X_{j})|<\infty,

(2.24)

it must be that

\displaystyle q(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{T_{K}-1}(j+1)|f(X_{j})|<\infty

for all
x\in S
, where
T_{K}=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in K\}
with
K\supseteq\{z\}
. But the function
q=(q(x):x\in S)
is then a finite-valued solution of

\displaystyle q(z)=(Pq)(x)+\tilde{q}(x)

for
x\in S
, where
\tilde{q}=(\tilde{q}(x):x\in S)
solves

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(x)=(P\tilde{q})(x)+|f(x)|I(x\in K^{c})

for
x\in S
. This, of course, implies that
q
and
\tilde{q}
satisfy the Lyapunov inequalities for
v_{2}
and
v_{4}
with equality, so that if (
2.24
) holds, there necessarily exist solutions of the associated Lyapunov inequalities. Similarly, the Lyapunov inequalities for
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
are necessary for the finiteness of
E_{z}\tau^{2}(z)
.

Theorem 5 therefore provides (weak) sufficient conditions under which Theorems
1
through
4
are valid. Assuming that one can find such suitable Lyapunov conditions, one can then validate Theorems
1
through
4
under close to minimal conditions.

3
Two Illustrative Examples

Our first example illustrates the fact that when
S
is infinite, there can be infinitely many solutions of Poisson’s equation; see, for example,
Bhulai and
Spieksma 2003
. However, the example below shows that when
X
is irreducible, there can even exist infinitely many linearly independent solutions of Poisson’s equation. So, uniqueness can fail badly.

Given the form of (1.1), non-uniqueness emerges from consideration of solutions
h
to the linear system

\displaystyle Ph=h.

(3.1)

A function
h
satisfying (
3.1
) is called a
harmonic function
. Of course, the constant function
h
always solves
(
3.1
)
, and this is the source of the fact that even well-behaved solutions to Poisson’s equation are only unique up to an additive constant.

Example 1
.

Consider the Markov chain
X
defined on the state space
S=\{0\}\cup\{(i,j):i\geq 1,j\geq 1\}
with transition probabilities defined by

\displaystyle P(x,y)=\begin{cases}p,&x=(i,j),y=(i+1,j),i,j\geq 1\\
q,&x=(i,j),y=(i-1,j),i\geq 2,j\geq 1\\
q,&x=(1,j),y=0,j\geq 1\\
r_{j},&x=0,y=(1,j),j\geq 1\\
1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j},&x=y=0\\
0,&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
0<p<1/2,q=1-p,r_{j}>0
for
j\geq 1
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}<1
. This Markov chain is irreducible, aperiodic, and positive recurrent (since
p<q
). To compute its stationary distribution, we note that
\pi=(\pi(x):x\in S)
satisfies the linear system of equations

\displaystyle\pi(i,j)=p\pi(i-1,j)+q\pi(i+1,j)

(3.2)

for
i\geq 2,j\geq 1
while

\displaystyle\pi(1,j)=r_{j}\pi(0)+q\pi(2,j)

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\pi(0)=(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j})\pi(0)+q\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\pi(1,j).

(3.4)

The general solution of (
3.2
) is

\displaystyle\pi(i,j)=a_{j}+b_{j}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{i}

(3.5)

for
i,j\geq 1
. Relation (
3.3
) implies that

\displaystyle a_{j}+b_{j}=\frac{r_{j}}{p}\pi(0),

(3.6)

while (
3.4
) requires that the
a_{j}
’s be summable and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}=0.

(3.7)

Equations (
3.5
) and (
3.7
) imply that the solution space corresponding to the stationary equations is infinite-dimensional. Of course, (
3.5
) implies that
\pi(i,j)\rightarrow a_{j}
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. In view of (
3.7
), any solution for which
a_{j}\neq 0
for some
j\geq 1
must therefore have both the property that the
\pi(x)
’s are non-summable and that the
\pi(x)
’s are of mixed-sign. Hence, if
\pi
is to be a probability distribution, we must have
a_{j}=0
for
j\geq 1
, in which case

\displaystyle\pi(i,j)=\frac{r_{j}}{p}\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^{i}\pi(0)

for
i,j\geq 1
, with

\displaystyle\pi(0)=(1+\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}}{q-p})^{-1}.

The solution space corresponding to the space of harmonic functions is similarly infinite-dimensional. In particular, (
3.1
) is given by the linear system

\displaystyle ph(i+1,j)+qh(i-1,j)=h(i,j)

for
i\geq 2,j\geq 1
, with

\displaystyle ph(2,j)+qh(0)=h(1,j)

for
j\geq 1
, and

\displaystyle(1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j})h(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}h(1,j)=h(0).

The general solution of this linear system is given by

\displaystyle h(i,j)=h(0)+\tilde{b}_{j}[(\frac{q}{p})^{i}-1]

(3.8)

for
i,j\geq 1
, where the sequence
(\tilde{b}_{j}:j\geq 1)
must be both summable and satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\tilde{b}_{j}=0.

(3.9)

An interesting feature of Example 1 is that conditional on
X_{0}=x\in S
, the birth-death structure of
X
implies that the random variable (rv)
X_{n}=(I_{n},J_{n})
must be such that
I_{n}\leq i+n
. As a result,
(h(X_{n}):n\geq 0)
is
P_{x}
-integrable and hence is a
P_{x}
-martingale for each
x\in S
. Consequently, whenever
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
,

\displaystyle g_{z}(X_{n})+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f_{c}(X_{j})+h(X_{n})

is a
P_{x}
-martingale for all the harmonic functions characterised by (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), and
g_{z}+h
is a solution of Poisson’s equation.

Note that any non-constant harmonic function fails to be
\pi
-integrable, so
g_{z}+h
is not
\pi
-integrable. Hence, this example establishes that even when a solution to Poisson’s equation induces a martingale, that solution may be badly behaved (e.g. it may not be
\pi
-integrable).

This raises the question of whether there is an alternative martingale property that characterizes well-behaved solutions to Poisson’s equation (e.g. solutions of the form
g_{z}+ce
for
c\in\mathbb{R}
, where
e(x)=1
for
x\in S
). Our next result provides one such characterization; see part b.).

Theorem 6
.

Assume that
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
.

a)

For
x,y,z\in S
,

\displaystyle(g_{y}(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(z)\land n)-1}f_{c}(X_{j}):n\geq 0)

(3.10)

is a
P_{x}
-uniformly integrable martingale adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
.

b)

Suppose that there exists
z\in S
and
g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for each
x\in S
,

\displaystyle(g(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(z)\land n)-1}f_{c}(X_{j}):n\geq 0)

is a
P_{x}
-uniformly integrable martingale adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
. Then, for each
y\in S
,
g(x)=g_{y}(x)+g(y)e(x)
.

Proof.

For part a), we first recognize that
g_{y}(\cdot)
and
g_{z}(\cdot)
differ by an additive constant (by Theorem
1
), so it is sufficient to prove the result for
y=z
. We further note that Theorem
2
establishes (
2.8
) is a
P_{x}
-martingale, and hence optional sampling implies that (
3.10
) is a
P_{x}
-martingale. So, it remains only to prove that (
3.10
) is
P_{x}
-uniformly integrable. Note that

\displaystyle g_{z}(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(z)\land n)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})\overset{a.s.}{\rightarrow}g_{z}(X(\tau(z)))+\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})=\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In view of Theorem 4.6.3 of
Durrett 2019
, it suffices to prove that

\displaystyle E_{x}|g_{z}(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(z)\land n)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|\rightarrow E_{x}|\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|

(3.11)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. But

\displaystyle|g_{z}(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(z)\land n)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|

\displaystyle\leq|g_{z}(X_{n})|I(\tau(z)>n)+\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|+|\pi f|\tau(z)

(3.12)

and the latter two rv’s on the right-hand size of (
3.12
) have finite
P_{x}
-expectation because
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
and
X
is positive recurrent. For the first term, note that

\displaystyle E_{x}(|g_{z}(X_{n})|I(\tau(z)>n))

\displaystyle=E_{x}|\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle\leq E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)

\displaystyle=E_{z}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau(z)>n)\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. As a consequence, (
3.11
) follows and we have established the required uniform integrability.

For part
b)
, note that the uniform integrability and martingale property imply that

\displaystyle g(x)=E_{x}g(X_{\tau(z)\land n})+\sum_{j=0}^{(\tau(x)\land n)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})\rightarrow g(z)+E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})=g(z)+g_{z}(x)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, proving the result for
y=z
. The case of general
y
is handled by just noting that
g_{z}(\cdot)
and
g_{y}(\cdot)
differ by an additive constant; see Theorem
1
.

∎

While our first example illustrates non-uniqueness issues related to Poisson’s equation, our second example provides further insight into existence and representation issues related to Poisson’s equation. Theorem
1
shows that whenever
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
,
g_{z}
solves Poisson’s equation. However, a commonly used representation of the solution of Poisson’s equation is that given by (
2.21
), namely

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{x}f_{c}(X_{j}).

(3.13)

Our example below shows that there are Markov chains
X
and functions
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
for which the solution
g_{z}
is well-defined, while the representation (
3.13
) is not well-defined (since the sum fails to be summable). Hence,
g_{z}
is a universal representation of the solution to Poisson’s equation, while the “potential-theoretic” representation (
3.13
) requires more regularity in order that it be valid.

Example 2
.

Let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (iid) positive integer-valued rv’s, and put

\displaystyle S_{n}=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+...+\beta_{n},

with
S_{0}=0
. Let
\ell(n)=\max\{j:S_{j}\leq n\}
and let

\displaystyle X_{n}=n-S_{\ell(n)}

be the “current age” Markov chain associated with the inter-renewal times
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...
. Put
f(x)=\delta_{x0}
, so that
f(\cdot)
is 1 when
x=0
and 0 otherwise. Then,

\displaystyle E_{0}f(X_{n})=P_{0}(X_{n}=0)\overset{\Delta}{=}u_{n},

where
(u_{n}:n\geq 0)
is the renewal sequence associated with the increment probability mass function
(p_{j}:j\geq 1)
given by
p_{j}=P(\beta_{1}=j)
for
j\geq 1
. Assume that
(p_{j}:j\geq 1)
is a positive sequence for which there exists
c>0
and
\alpha>1
for which

\displaystyle P(\beta_{1}>n)\sim cn^{-\alpha}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
(where we write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
when
a_{n}/b_{n}\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
). Then,
E\beta_{1}<\infty
and
X
is positive recurrent with
\pi(0)=\lambda\overset{\Delta}{=}1/E\beta_{1}
.

According to Lemma 4 of
Frenk 1982
,

\displaystyle u_{n}-\lambda\sim\frac{\lambda^{2}nP(\beta_{1}>n)}{\alpha-1}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, from which it follows that

\displaystyle P_{0}(X_{n}=0)-\pi(0)\sim\frac{\lambda^{2}cn^{1-\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)}

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. As a consequence, if
\alpha\in(1,2]
, (
3.13
) fails to be summable at
x=0
, providing the required example.

4
The CLT and LIL for Markov Chains

We finish this paper by developing a weakened Lyapunov criterion for the validity of the central limit theorem and law of the iterated logarithm for countable state Markov chains. It is well known that if there exists
z\in S
such that

\displaystyle E_{z}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})\right)^{2}<\infty,

(4.1)

then, regardless of the initial distribution for
X_{0}
,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}(f)-n\pi f}{\sqrt{n}}\Rightarrow\sigma N(0,1)

(4.2)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\Rightarrow
denotes weak convergence,
N(0,1)
is a standard normal rv with mean 0 and variance 1, and

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\overset{\Delta}{=}\frac{E_{z}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})\right)^{2}}{E_{z}\tau(z)},

(4.3)

see p.99 of
Chung 1967
or
Glynn and
Whitt 1993
. Furthermore, Theorem 14.4 of
Chung 1967
proves that if
(
4.1
)
is valid for one
z\in S
, then it is valid for all
z\in S
, and the right-hand side of (
4.3
) does not depend on
z
. In
Glynn and
Whitt 2002
, it is shown that (
4.2
) implies (
4.1
), so that (
4.1
) is a necessary and sufficient condition for the CLT (
4.2
), in the presence of positive recurrence and
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
.

We now slightly strengthen (
4.1
) to the condition

\displaystyle E_{z}(\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f_{c}(X_{j})|)^{2}<\infty.

(4.4)

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
X
is irreducible, and let
K\subseteq S
be a finite subset. Suppose that there exists
v_{1}\in S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v_{2}\in S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
(Pv_{i})(x)<\infty
for
x\in K
and
i=1,2
and

\displaystyle(Pv_{1})(x)

\displaystyle\leq v_{1}(x)-(|f(x)|+1)

(4.5)

\displaystyle(Pv_{2})(x)

\displaystyle\leq v_{2}(x)-(|f(x)|+1)v_{1}(x)

(4.6)

for
x\in K^{c}
. Then,
X
is positive recurrent,
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
, and
(
4.4
)
is valid, so that the CLT (
4.2
) holds. Furthermore,
\sigma^{2}
can then be expressed as (
4.3
) or, equivalently, as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=2E_{\pi}g_{z}(X_{0})f_{c}(X_{0})-E_{\pi}f_{c}(X_{0})^{2}.

(4.7)

Proof.

Proposition
2
and (
4.5
) imply that
X
is positive recurrent with
f\in L^{1}(\pi)
, and

\displaystyle E_{x}(\tau(z)+\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})|)\leq v_{1}(x)+c_{1}

for
x\in S,z\in K,
and some
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. On the other hand, Proposition
2
and (
4.6
) imply that there exists
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for which

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})+1|v_{1}(X_{j})\leq v_{2}(x)+c_{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle E_{x}(\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})+1|)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}|f(X_{j})+1|\sum_{k=j}^{\tau(z)-1}(|f(X_{k})|+1)

\displaystyle\leq 2E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}(|f(X_{j})+1|)(v_{1}(X_{j})+c_{1})

\displaystyle\leq 2(v_{2}(x)+c_{2})+c_{1}(v_{1}(x)+c_{1})<\infty

for
x\in S
, proving (
4.4
).

To prove (
4.7
), note that in the presence of (
4.4
), we see that

\displaystyle E_{z}(\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j}))^{2}

\displaystyle=2E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})\sum_{k=j}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{k})-E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle=2E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}(X_{j})g_{z}(X_{j})-E_{z}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(z)-1}f_{c}^{2}(X_{j})

\displaystyle=E_{z}\tau(z)(2E_{\pi}f_{c}(X_{0})g_{z}(X_{0})-E_{\pi}f_{c}(X_{0})^{2}),

establishing the result.
∎

Remark 6
.

This Lyapunov criterion for Markov chain CLT weakens the existing criterion of
Glynn and
Meyn 1996
. In particular, when one writes
S_{n}(f)
in terms of the martingale (
2.8
), one is naturally led to consideration of the associated martingale differences given by

\displaystyle D_{i}=g_{z}(X_{i})-(Pg_{z})(X_{i-1})

for
i\geq 1
. In order that
D_{i}\in L^{2}(\pi)
, it seems appropriate to demand that
g_{z}\in L^{2}(\pi)
; see Theorem 4.1 of
Glynn and
Meyn 1996
. This is also the condition used in earlier work by
Maigret 1978
on the Markov chain CLT. However, the expression (
4.7
) makes clear that the key requirement in the Markov chain CLT is
g_{z}f_{c}\in L^{1}(\pi)
. This is effectively what (
4.6
) is verifying.

To see that use of (
4.6
) gives better conditions than does
Glynn and
Meyn 1996
, consider the Markov chain defined by

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=[X_{n}+Z_{n+1}]^{+}

(4.8)

where
[x]^{+}=\max(x,0)
and the
Z_{i}
’s are independent and identically distributed (iid) integer-valued rv’s with
-\infty<EZ_{1}<0
. As is well-known, this Markov chain arises naturally in the modeling of queues. Use of Theorem 4.1 in that paper for
f(x)=x
leads to the requirement that
E|Z_{1}|^{5}<\infty
. On the other hand, our Theorem
7
requires only that
EZ_{1}^{4}<\infty
, thereby weakening the moment requirement on
Z_{1}
. (Use
v_{1}(x)=ax^{2}
and
v_{2}(x)=a^{\prime}x^{4}
for suitably chosen
a
and
a^{\prime}
.)

We now argue that
EZ_{1}^{4}<\infty
is the natural condition that arises in connection to the CLT for the Markov chain defined by (
4.8
). We first note that
X_{n}
is a non-decreasing function of the independent rv’s
X_{0},Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
. Consequently, the
X_{n}
’s are associated rv’s; see
Barlow and
Proschan 1975
, p.29-31. Hence,

\displaystyle E_{\pi}(X_{0}\land r)(X_{n}\land r)\geq E_{\pi}X_{0}\land r\cdot E_{\pi}X_{n}\land r

for
r\geq 0
. Sending
r\rightarrow\infty
, we conclude that

\displaystyle E_{\pi}X_{0}X_{n}\geq E_{\pi}X_{0}\cdot E_{\pi}X_{n}

via the Monotone Convergence Theorem. If the CLT holds, this implies the existence of a finite non-negative
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}X_{j}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\alpha
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Because the
X_{j}
’s are non-negative,
\alpha
must equal
E_{\pi}X_{0}
. So,
E_{\pi}X_{0}<\infty
and

\displaystyle c_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}E_{\pi}X_{0}X_{n}-E_{\pi}X_{0}\cdot E_{\pi}X_{n}\geq 0

for
n\geq 0
. Another truncation and monotone convergence argument establishes that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}E_{\pi}(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(X_{j}-E_{\pi}X_{0}))^{2}=c_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(n-j)}{n}c_{j}.

(4.9)

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}c_{j}\leq c_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(n-j)}{n}c_{j}\leq c_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j},

it is evident that the left-hand side of (
4.9
) has a finite limit if and only if

\displaystyle c_{0}+2\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}<\infty.

(4.10)

According to
Daley 1968
, (
4.10
) holds (in the presence of a blanket assumption that
E|Z_{1}|^{3}<\infty
) if and only if
EZ_{1}^{4}<\infty
. Of course, demanding that the left-hand side of (
4.9
) have a limit follows whenever the CLT (
4.2
) is in force and the square of the left-hand side of (
4.2
) is
P_{\pi}
-uniformly integrable.

Remark 7
.

Another indication that
EZ_{1}^{4}
is a necessary condition for the CLT in this setting is that when one allows the
Z_{i}
’s to be continuous rv’s,
Glynn 1994
explicitly solves the associated Poisson’s equation for the
M/G/1
queue. In that setting, the solution to Poisson’s equation for
f
is quadratic, so that
g_{z}f_{c}
is cubic. As a result, it is well known that
EZ_{1}^{4}
is necessary in order that
g_{z}f_{c}
be
\pi
-integrable (
Asmussen 2008
), and hence that
\sigma^{2}
as given by (
4.7
) be finite.

We now turn to the law of the iterated logarithm for
S_{n}(f)
. The following is an immediate consequence of Theorem
7
above and Theorem 5, p.106 of
Chung 1967
.

Theorem 8
.

Under the same hypotheses as in Theorem
7
,

\displaystyle\varlimsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{S_{n}(f)-n\pi f}{\sqrt{n\log\log n}}\right|=|\sigma|\qquad P_{x}\ \text{a.s.}

for each
x\in S
, where
\sigma^{2}
is as given in Theorem
7
.

Glynn and
Whitt 1993
provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the LIL when the process is regenerative. The condition is stronger than (
4.1
), but slightly weaker than (
4.2
). So, Theorem
8
gives close to optimal conditions for the LIL.
