Sparse estimation in ordinary kriging for functional data
Hidetoshi Matsui, Yuya Yamakawa

Abstract
We introduce a sparse estimation in the ordinary kriging for functional data. The functional kriging predicts a feature given as a function at a location where the data are not observed by a linear combination of data observed at other locations. To estimate the weights of the linear combination, we apply the lasso-type regularization in minimizing the expected squared error. We derive an algorithm to derive the estimator using the augmented Lagrange method. Tuning parameters included in the estimation procedure are selected by cross-validation. Since the proposed method can shrink some of the weights of the linear combination toward zeros exactly, we can investigate which locations are necessary or unnecessary to predict the feature. Simulation and real data analysis show that the proposed method appropriately provides reasonable results.

Introduction

Functional data analysis has received considerable attentions in various fields such as medicine, social science and biology as one of the techniques that treat the longitudinal data rather than scalar data.
Various methodologies have been developed such as functional regression analysis, functional principal component analysis and functional canonical correlation analysis
[
28
,
19
]
.
More recently, there has been a growing interest in approaches to functional data where individuals are dependent on each other.
In particular, not dependent on individuals
[
21
]
.
Examples include time series analysis
[
5
,
18
]
and spatial data analysis
[
9
,
20
,
24
]
for functional data.

In this work we focus on the spatial functional data analysis, the spatial data analysis for functional data.
The spatial data analysis considers the problem of analyzing univariate or multivariate data each of which is observed at a location distributed over a certain space, taking the spatial correlation into consideration
[
11
,
8
]
.
Kriging, one of the most typical methods for analyzing spatial data, predicts a feature at a location where data has not yet been observed by a linear combination of the feature at locations where the data has been observed.
When we have longitudinal data for a feature observed at several locations, the spatial functional data analysis treats the longitudinal data as functional data and then applies the spatial data analysis such as the kriging.
As an earlier work,
[
14
]
approached this problem by fitting the longitudinal data using a parametric model and then applied the kriging to the fitted data, under the restriction that the number of time points is small.

[
13
]
proposed the ordinal kriging method for functional data.
They derived a procedure for estimating the weights of the linear combination under the assumption of the weakly stationary.
Then we can investigate how the functional data at the observed locations relate for predicting the function at the unobserved location.

[
12
]
extended the ordinary functional kriging to the case where the weights vary with time.
Furthermore,
[
7
,
25
]
considered the universal kriging, which loosens the assumption of the stationary.
The universal kriging assumes that the trend of a functional feature over the space can be explained by a function of other features such as linear combinations.

[
1
]
also approached predicting the function at an unobserved point using a penalized spline fitting the data as functions with respect to location and time.

The kriging with sparse regularization enables us to select the information relevant to the prediction of the feature at unobserved locations.
The sparse regularization is one of the most useful tools for variable selection in that it estimates the unknown parameters based on the regularization with
L_{1}
-type penalty
[
16
]
.
Typical
L_{1}
-type penalties include the lasso
[
29
]
, elastic net
[
35
]
and MCP
[
32
]
.
The sparse regularization has also been applied to the kriging methods for scalar data.

[
22
]
applied the sparsity-inducing penalty to the ordinary kriging.
On the other hand,
[
17
]
applied the lasso to estimate the coefficients of the linear combination of variables that expresses the trend of the universal kriging.
As similar works,
[
31
]
,
[
33
]
and
[
26
]
also approached the lasso for selecting important variables that compose the trend of the universal kriging.

If we can estimate the kriging weights towards exact zeros, then we can investigate which locations are related to predicting the feature at an unobserved location.
To obtain such estimators, we propose to apply the sparse regularization to the weights of the ordinary kriging for functional data.
We obtain the sparse estimators of the weights by minimizing the integrated expected squared error with the
L_{1}
-type penalty under the second-order stationary assumption for the feature.
Here we apply the adaptive lasso-type regularization
[
34
]
to incorporate the ordinary kriging estimator.
Since it is difficult to derive the estimator of the kriging weights with the
L_{1}
-type penalty, we apply the augmented Lagrange method to obtain them numerically.
Tuning parameters included in the estimation are selected by the cross-validation.
We apply the proposed method to the analysis of simulated data and weather data and then investigate whether the proposed method gives appropriate estimates.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 provides the ordinary kriging for functional data as a preliminary.
In Section 3 we introduce the sparse estimation for the ordinary functional kriging.
Section 4 shows the results of simulation and real data analysis.
Finally, we conclude the main points in Section 5.

2
Ordinary kriging for functional data

Let
X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};t)
be a random function at
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}\in\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
over
t\in\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Typically
s
and
t
correspond to the location and time, respectively.
Suppose that
X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};t)
satisfies the second-order stationary and isotropy conditions, that is,

\displaystyle{\rm E}\left[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};t)\right]=m(t),

\displaystyle{\rm Cov}\left[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}};t),X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};u)\right]=C(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}\|;t,u)

for
\forall\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}\in\mathcal{S}
, where
m(t)
is a function that depends only on
t
and
C(r;t,u)
is a function that depends only on
r=\|\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}\|
,
t
and
u
.
Under the above conditions, the function

\displaystyle\gamma(r;t)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm V}[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}};t)-X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};t)]=\frac{1}{2}{\rm E}[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}+\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}};t)-X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}};t)]^{2}

depends only on
r=\|\mbox{\boldmath{$h$}}\|
and
t
.
The
\gamma(r;t)
is called a semi-variogram, and its integration
\gamma(r)=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\gamma(r;t)dt
is called a trace-variogram.

We denote
X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1};t),\ldots,X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n},t)
as random functions at locations
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n}\in\mathcal{S}
, respectively, where we assume
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}\neq\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}

(i\neq j)
.
The aim of ordinary kriging here is to predict a function
X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)
at location
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0}\neq\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,n)
that the data are obtained using the data observed at
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n}
.
We assume that the predicted function for
X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)
is expressed by linear combinations of functions at observed points:

\displaystyle\widehat{X}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t),

(1)

where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
are unknown parameters corresponding to kriging weights.
Equation (
1
) allows us to quantify how and which the functional feature at observed locations contributes to the prediction of the unobserved location.
Under the condition that the function
\widehat{X}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)
satisfies the second-order stationary,
\lambda_{i}
has the condition that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1
.

In the ordinary kriging for functional data, we estimate
\lambda_{i}
in (
1
) by solving the following constrained optimization problem
[
13
]
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{Minimize}&&\int_{\mathcal{T}}E\left[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)-\hat{X}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)\right]^{2}dt\\
&\mbox{subject to}&&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}-1=0.\end{aligned}

Solving the above problem is reduced to minimizing the following Lagrange function with respect to
\lambda
and
\mu
by the method of Lagrange multipliers:

\displaystyle L(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}},\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{T}}E\left[X(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)-\hat{X}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)\right]^{2}dt+2\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{\top}C\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-2\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}+2\mu(\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-1).

(2)

where
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is a Lagrange multiplier,
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})^{\top}
,
C=(\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}\|;t,t)dt)_{ij}
,
\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}=(\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0}\|,t,t)dt,\ldots,\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0}\|;t,t)dt)^{\top}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
is an
n
-dimensional vector.
Note that
C
is positive semi-definite since the matrix whose
(i,j)
-th elements is
C(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}\|;t,t)
with fixed
t
is positive semi-definite.
The solution that minimizes (
2
) is obtained by simple matrix calculation and the conditions of second-order stationary and isotropy.
Since partial derivatives of (
2
) with respect to
\lambda
and
\mu
respectively give

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}},\mu)}{\partial\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}}=2C\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-2\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}+2\mu\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n},~~~\frac{\partial L(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}},\mu)}{\partial\mu}=2\left(\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-1\right),

we should solve the following linear system to estimate the ordinary kriging weights
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}C&\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}\\
\mu\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Indeed it is difficult to estimate the function
\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(r;t,t)dt
directly.
Instead we use the relationship
\gamma(r)=\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(0;t,t)dt-\int_{\mathcal{T}}C(r;t,t)dt
and estimate the trace-variogram
\gamma(r)
.
The
\gamma(r)
can be obtained by calculating the sample variance of the difference of the pair of observations such that the distance between two locations equals to
r
.
Since such pairs are very scarce, we calculate the variance using a set of observations where the distance between two points is close to
r
.
Denote a set of pairs of points whose distances are close to
r
as

\displaystyle N_{r}=\{(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j});\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}\|\approx r\},

then we estimate the trace-variogram
\gamma(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}\|)
by the sample variance using
N_{r}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}(\|\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j}\|)=\frac{1}{2|N_{r}|}\sum_{i,j\in N_{r}}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\left[x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)-x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j};t)\right]^{2}dt,

(4)

where
|N_{r}|
is the size of
N_{r}
.
See
[
8
]
for details of the estimation of the variogram.

In real situations, the data are observed longitudinally at discrete time points rather than continuously.
Therefore, we need to transform the observed data into functions.
To do it, we assume that the functional data
x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)
is expressed by basis expansion as follows.

\displaystyle x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{im}\phi_{m}(t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t),

(5)

where
\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t)=(\phi_{1}(t),\ldots,\phi_{M}(t))^{\top}
is a vector of basis functions and
\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}=(w_{i1},\ldots,w_{iM})^{\top}
is a vector of coefficients.
After estimating the coefficient
w
, we treat
\{x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)=\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t);i=1,\ldots,n\}
as functional data.
For details of the method for estimating
w
, see
[
15
,
28
]
.
Using the assumption of basis expansion, the integral in (
4
) is calculated as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{T}}\left[x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)-x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{j};t)\right]^{2}dt=(\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{j})^{\top}\Phi(\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}-\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{j}),

where
\Phi=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t)\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t)^{\top}dt
.
Then the value of (
4
) is obtained by fitting
\widehat{\gamma}(r)
by some parametric models such as Gaussian or exponential models, here we use Matérn model.

Therefore, we have the ordinary functional kriging (OFK) estimators of
\lambda
and
\mu
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}}\\
\hat{\mu}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C&\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}\\
\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

As a result, when we have functional data
x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{1};t),\ldots,x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{n};t)
at
n
points, we predict a function
x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)
at a point
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{x}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{0};t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\lambda}_{i}x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t).

(7)

For more details, see
[
13
,
24
]
.

3
Sparse ordinary kriging for functional data

In this section, we introduce a method for applying sparse regularization for estimating the weights
\lambda_{i}
in (
1
) of the ordinary functional kriging.
To begin with, for given hyperparameters
\eta
and
\tau
, we consider the following minimization problem which is obtained by imposing an
L_{1}
-type penalization to the minimization problem (
2
).

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{Minimize}&&f(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}})\coloneqq\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{\top}C\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-2\mbox{\boldmath{$c$}}_{0}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i}|\lambda_{i}|\\
&\mbox{subject to}&&g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}})\coloneqq\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-1=0,\end{aligned}

(8)

where
\eta
is an regularization parameter and
\widehat{w}_{i}>0
is an adaptive weight. Here we set
\widehat{w}_{i}=|\widehat{\lambda}_{i}|^{-\tau}
, where
\widehat{\lambda}_{i}
is the ordinary kriging estimator of
\lambda_{i}
obtained by the method described in the previous section, and
\tau>0
is a tuning parameter. Note that the problem (
8
) is obtained by adding the adaptive lasso-type penalty
[
34
]
to the OFK.
We call the solution
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}}=(\widehat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\lambda}_{n})^{\top}
of (
8
) the sparse ordinary functional kriging (SOFK) estimator.

It is difficult to apply the well-known estimation algorithms for the lasso such as the coordinate descent method
[
10
]
and the alternating direction method
[
6
]
to obtain a minimizer
\lambda
of (
8
) due to the constraint.
We use the following augmented Lagrangian method which is one of the most famous algorithms and is used in a lot of fields.

Step 0:

Give a positive sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}
. Select an initial primal point
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}_{0}
and Lagrange multiplier
\mu_{0}
. Set
k\coloneqq 0
.

Step 1:

If the termination criterion is satisfied, then stop.

Step 2:

Find a minimizer
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{\ast}
of the following problem:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{Minimize}&&f(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}})+\mu_{k}g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}})+\frac{\rho_{k}}{2}g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}})^{2}\\
&\mbox{subject to}&&\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{aligned}

(9)

Set
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}_{k+1}\coloneqq\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{\ast}
.

Step 3:

Update the Lagrange multiplier
\mu_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}\coloneqq\mu_{k}+\rho_{k}g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}_{k+1}).

Step 4:

Set
k\leftarrow k+1
and go back to Step 1.

In Step 2, we have to find the minimizer of the problem (
9
). We then note that it can be recast in the following form:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mbox{Minimize}&&\frac{1}{2}\|A\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}-b\|^{2}+\eta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{w}_{i}|\lambda_{i}|\\
&\mbox{subject to}&&\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{aligned}

(10)

where
A
and
b
are defined by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(2C+\rho_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(2C+\rho_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}^{\top}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(2\mbox{\boldmath{$\gamma$}}_{0}+\rho_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}-\mu_{k}\mbox{\boldmath{$1$}}_{n}),

respectively. This kind of optimization problem can be effectively solved by the proximal gradient type algorithms, and hence we apply the Fast Iterative Shrinkage Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA)
[
2
]
for the problem (
10
) which is equivalent to the problem (
9
). Moreover, if we choose the sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}
so that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\rho_{k}=\infty
, then
[
3
, Proposition 5.2.1]
ensures that any accumulation point generated by the augmented Lagrangian method is an optimal solution of the problem (
8
). For the details, see
[
3
, Section 5.2.1]
. In the current paper, the parameter sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}
is updated by the following rule.

\displaystyle\rho_{k+1}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{aligned} &\kappa\rho_{k}&&\mbox{if}~g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}_{k+1})>\alpha g(\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}_{k}),\\
&\rho_{k}&&{\rm otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\kappa
are additional hyperparameters. This updating rule is quite general and adopted in existing articles, such as
[
4
,
23
,
30
]
. Here we fixed them as
\alpha=0.9
and
\kappa=2
.

In the proposed sparse regularization method, we obtain several minimizers
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{(1)},\ldots,\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{(N)}
for given hyperparameters
(\eta,\tau)=(\eta^{(1)},\tau^{(1)}),\ldots,(\eta^{(N)},\tau^{(N)})
, respectively. The best tuning parameters
(\eta,\tau)
are selected by using cross-validation.

\displaystyle{\rm CV}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\left\{x(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)-\widehat{x}^{(-i)}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)\right\}^{2}dt,

(12)

where
\widehat{x}^{(-i)}(\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i};t)
is an predicted function by SOFK at the
i
-th location
\mbox{\boldmath{$s$}}_{i}
obtained using the functional data of size
n-1
excluding the
i
-th observation out of
n
.
In particular, we adopt
(\eta,\tau)
to minimize (
12
) as the best tuning parameter.

The algorithm for estimating
\lambda
and
\mu
is summarized as follows.

Step 0:

Give a fixed hyperparameter set
\{(\eta^{(j)},\tau^{(j)})\}_{j=1}^{N}
. Set
j\coloneqq 0
.

Step 1:

Find the optimal solution
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{(j)}
of (
8
) with
(\eta,\tau)\coloneqq(\eta^{(j)},\tau^{(j)})
.

Step 2:

Calculate (
12
) using the estimated
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{(j)}
and obtain
{\rm CV}^{(j)}
by using (
12
).

Step 3:

If
j<N
, then
j\leftarrow j+1
and go back to Step 1. Otherwise, select
(\eta^{(\ell)},\tau^{(\ell)})
, where
\ell
satisfies
{\rm CV}^{(\ell)}=\displaystyle\min_{1\leq j\leq N}{\rm CV}^{(j)}
. Obtain the corresponding estimator of
\mbox{\boldmath{$\lambda$}}^{(\ell)}
.

4.1
Simulation

We examined the effectiveness of the proposed method through the application to the analysis of simulation data.
The aim of this simulation is to express predictions of functions as (
7
) at locations where the data are not observed, and to investigate the behavior of the estimated kriging weights
\{\hat{\lambda}_{i};i=1,\ldots,n\}
in (
6
).

Consider a two-dimensional squared-shaped domain
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]\times[0,1]
and equally spaced
15\times 15=225
locations on
\mathcal{S}
.
Each location has a feature that is spatially correlated, where it is longitudinally observed at
n=50
randomly chosen locations out of
225
, as shown in Figure
1
left.
We generated longitudinal data
\{x_{ij};j=1,\ldots,N\}
at the
i
-th of
n
locations by adding Gaussian noises to the true structure expressed by basis expansion as

\displaystyle x_{ij}=\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}^{\top}\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t_{ij})+\varepsilon_{ij},

where
\varepsilon_{ij}
is independently and identically normally distributed with mean 0 and standard deviation
0.05
.
Here we used
B
-spline for
\mbox{\boldmath{$\phi$}}(t)
with the number of basis functions
M=10
.
Coefficients
\mbox{\boldmath{$w$}}_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,n)
are numerically generated so that they have positive spatial correlations using R package
gstat

[
27
]
.
The number of time points for the longitudinal data is
N=31
for all locations.
Figure
1
right shows the observed longitudinal data at
n
sites in black points in Figure
1
left.
We analyzed this dataset by applying the SOFK to predict the features at 175 locations where the longitudinal data are not observed and to investigate the estimated kriging weights.
We also applied the OFK for comparison.

As a first process of the analysis, we transformed the observed longitudinal data at

Then we applied the SOFK and OFK to each of test locations, where we used R package

to calculate the trace-variogram.

\eta
and
\tau
are selected by CV in (

).
We investigated the estimated parameters
\lambda_{i}

(i=1,\ldots,n)
, especially how many parameters are estimated to be zeros.

Examples of estimated kriging weights

\hat{\lambda}_{i}

4.2
Real data analysis

We applied the proposed method to the analysis of Canadian weather data, available on R package
fda
.
The data contains locations (longitude and latitude) of 35 cities in Canada and their daily temperatures each of which is averaged from 1960 to 1994.
The locations and temperature data are shown in Figure
4
.
The aim of this analysis is to predict the annual temperature curve of Calgary using those at the remaining 34 locations and to investigate which location temperatures are used to predict it.
To do it, we applied the proposed SOFK.

Canadian weather data. Left: Observed locations (black circle) and unobserved locations (cross mark, Calgary) to be predicted. Right: yearly temperatures at observed 34 locations.

\lambda_{i}
in (

) by SOFK.

\eta
and
\tau
included in (

) are selected by CV.

5
shows estimated weights
\lambda_{i}
by SOFK and OFK according to the distance from Calgary.
We can see that SOFK shrinks the weights of most locations except for three locations near Calgary towards exact zeros, whereas OFK does not; Edmonton
(0.790)
Kamloops
(0.199)
, and Regina
(0.011)
, as depicted in Figure

Conclusion

We have proposed the sparse estimation method for ordinary functional kriging.
The kriging gives predictions of the feature at unobserved locations by a linear combination of the data at observed locations.
Using sparse regularization to estimate the weights, we can investigate which combination of locations is used to predict the feature of unobserved locations.
This result may be available for the decision-making of prediction.
The sparse ordinary functional kriging estimates are obtained by adaptive lasso-type regularization and the augmented Lagrange method.
Simulation and real data analysis show that the proposed method appropriately estimates the unknown parameters and selected locations that are necessary to predict the feature at an unobserved location.

The proposed method assumed the second-order stationarity, but it is too strong to predict temperatures in any area.
For example, temperatures in Japan are observed over a wide range from northeast to southwest and it is unnatural to assume the stationarity.
To overcome this problem, an extension to universal kriging that loosens the stationarity assumption is considered.
Other sparsity-inducing penalties such as SCAD and MCP may be available for doing SOFK.
The comparison of these penalties remains as a future work.
