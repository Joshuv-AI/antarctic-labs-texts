Abstract
. An abstract theory of Fourier series in locally convex topological vector spaces is developed. An analog of Fejér’s theorem is proved for these series. The theory is applied to distributional solutions of Cauchy-Riemann equations to recover basic results of complex analysis. Some classical results of function theory are also shown to be consequences of the series expansion.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
32A05,22D12

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Two types of series expansion ubiquitous in mathematics are the power series of an analytic function, and the Fourier series of an integrable function on
the circle. In the complex domain, well-known theorems of
elementary complex analysis guarantee the existence of locally uniformly convergent
power series expansions of holomorphic functions in domains with ample symmetry such as disks and annuli,
where “holomorphic” can be taken in the sense of Goursat, i.e., the function is complex-differentiable at each point. On the other hand, the
convergence of a Fourier series is a subtle matter, and its study has led to many developments
in analysis (see
[
Zyg02
]
). The close connection between these two types of outwardly different series expansions is a recurring theme in many areas of
classical analysis, e.g., in the theory of Hardy spaces.

It is however not difficult to see that at a certain level of abstraction, Fourier series and power series are in fact two examples of the same
phenomenon, the representation theory of the circle group. The aim of this article is to take this idea seriously, and use it to recapture some basic results of complex analysis. The
take-home message is that many properties of holomorphic functions, such as the almost-supernatural regularity phenomena are profitably thought of as expressions of
symmetry, more precisely the invariance of certain locally convex spaces under the Reinhardt action of the torus group on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
It is hoped that the point of view taken here has pedagogical as well as conceptual value, and will be of interest to students of complex analysis.

1.2.
Abstract Fourier series

We begin in Section
2
with an account of Fourier series associated to a continuous representation of the

n
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on locally convex topological vector spaces, using some ideas of
[
Joh76
]
.
This provides the unifying language of sufficient generality to encompass both classical Fourier expansions and the power series representations of complex analysis. Even at this very general and “soft” level,
one can establish a version of
Fejér’s theorem on the summability in the Cesàro sense (Theorem
2.1
), which can be thought of as a “completeness” statement for the holomorphic monomials.

1.3.
Analyticity of holomorphic distributions

Using the abstract framework of Section
2
, we recapture in Section
3
, in a novel way,
some of the basic classical facts about holomorphic functions.
We show that on a Reinhardt domain, a distribution which satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations (which we call a
holomorphic distribution
) has a complex power series representation that converges uniformly along with
derivatives of all orders
on compact subsets of a “relatively complete” log-convex Reinhardt domain (see Theorem
3.1
below), and thus a holomorphic distribution
is a function, in fact an analytic function. Traditionally, to prove
such an assertion, one would start by showing that a holomorphic distribution is actually a (smooth) function. This can be done either by ad hoc arguments for the Laplacian going back to Weyl (see
[
Wey40
]
), or in a more general way, by noticing that fundamental solution
\frac{1}{\pi z}
of the Cauchy-Riemann operator
\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}

is smooth, in fact
real-analytic, away from the singularity at the origin. It then follows by standard arguments about convolutions (see
[
Hör03
, Theorem 4.4.3]
), that any distributional solution of
\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}u=0
is real-analytic.
Therefore, a holomorphic distribution is a
holomorphic function in the sense of Goursat, and classical results of elementary complex analysis give the power series expansion
via the Cauchy integral formula. The extension of the domain of convergence to the envelope of holomorphy
can be obtained by convexity arguments
(see
[
Ran86
]
).

While this classical argument has the admirable advantage of placing holomorphic functions in the context of solutions of hypoelliptic
equations,
it also has the shortcoming that the crucial property of
complex-analyticity
(and the associated Hartogs phenomenon in several variables) of holomorphic distributions
is proved not from an analysis of the action of a differential operator on distributions, but by falling back on the Cauchy integral formula.
After using the real-analytic hypoellipticity of
\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z}}
to conclude that holomorphic distributions are real-analytic,
we discard all of this information, except that holomorphic functions are
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations in the classical sense.
In our approach here, however, complex analyticity
of holomorphic distributions is proved directly in a conceptually straightforward way, by expanding a holomorphic distribution on a Reinhardt domain
in a Fourier series, and then showing that the resulting series (the Laurent series of the holomorphic function) converges in the

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-topology, not only on the original set where the distribution was defined, but possibly on a larger domain,
thus underlining the fact that Hartogs phenomenon can be thought of as a regularity property of the solutions of the same nature
as smoothness. Our proof also clearly locates the origin of the remarkable regularity of holomorphic distributions in

(a)

the invariance properties of the space of holomorphic distributions under Reinhardt rotations and translations,

(b)

symmetry and convexity
properties of the Laurent monomial functions
z\mapsto z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots z_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},

\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

(c)

the fact that radially symmetric
holomorphic distributions are constants.

It is also interesting that the fact that
\frac{1}{\pi z}
is
a fundamental solution of the Cauchy-Riemann operator, which is key to many results of complex analysis including the Cauchy integral formula,
does not play any role in our approach.

The method of proving analyticity via Fourier expansion can be used in other contexts. For example, replacing the representation theory of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
by that of the special orthogonal group
SO(n)
,
the method can be used to show that a harmonic distribution in a ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is in fact a real analytic function and
admits an expansion in solid harmonics (see, e.g.,
[
CH53
, pp. 316-317]
), which converges uniformly along with all derivatives on compact subsets of the ball. Similarly, one
can obtain the Taylor/Laurent expansion of a monogenic function of a Clifford-algebra variable in “spherical monogenics”, the analogs for functions of a Clifford-algebra variable of
the monomial functions
z\mapsto z^{n},n\in\mathbb{Z}
(see
[
BDS82
]
).

1.4.
Analyticity of continuous holomorphic functions

While the theory of generalized functions forms the natural context for solution of linear partial differential equations such as the Cauchy-Riemann equations,
for aesthetic and pedagogical reasons it is natural to ask whether it is possible to
develop the Fourier approach to power series, as outlined in Section
3
,
using only classical notions of functions as continuous mappings, and derivatives as
limits of difference quotients, and without anachronistically invoking distributions. We accomplish this in the last Section
5
of this paper. We start from
the assumption that a continuous function on the complex complex plane satisfies the hypothesis of the Morera theorem, and show directly that it is complex-analytic
without any recourse to
the Cauchy integral representation formula
(which, being a case of Stoke’s theorem, requires the differentiability of the function, at least at each point). Not unexpectedly, one of the steps of the proof uses the classical triangle-division used
in the standard proof of the Cauchy theorem for triangles.

2.
Fourier Series in Locally Convex spaces

2.1.
Nets, series and integrals in LCTVS

We begin by recalling some notions and facts about functional analysis in topological vector spaces. See the textbooks
[
Trè67
,
Rud91
,
Bou04
]
for more details on these matters.

Let
X
be a locally convex Hausdorff topological vector space (we use the standard abbreviation
LCTVS
). Recall that the topology of
X
can also be described by prescribing the family of
continuous seminorms
on
X
:
a net
\{x_{j}\}
in
X
converges to
x
, if and only if for each continuous seminorm
p
on
X
, we have
p(x-x_{j})\to 0
as a net of real numbers.
In practice, we describe the topology of an LCTVS by specifying a
generating family
of seminorms (analogous to describing a topology by a subbasis): a collection of continuous seminorms
\{p_{k}:k\in K\}
on
X
is said to
generate the topology
of
X
if for every continuous seminorm
q
on
X
, there exists a finite subset
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\}\subset K
and a
C>0
such that

q(x)\leq C\cdot\max\{p_{k_{1}}(x),\dots,p_{k_{n}}(x)\}\quad\textrm{for all $x\in X$},

(2.1)

and further, for every nonzero
x\in X
there exists at least one
k\in K
such that
p_{k}(x)\neq 0
(this
separating property
ensures that the topology of
X
is Hausdorff). Then clearly a net
\{x_{j}\}
converges in
X
if and only if
p_{k}(x_{j}-x)\rightarrow 0

for each
k\in K
.

Let
\Gamma
be a directed set with order
\geq
. Recall that a net
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Gamma}
in
X
is said to be
Cauchy
if for every
\epsilon>0
and every continuous seminorm
p
on
X
, there exists
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that whenever
\alpha,\beta\in\Gamma
and
\alpha,\beta\geq\gamma
, we have
p(x_{\alpha}-x_{\beta})<\epsilon
. The space
X
is said to be
complete
if every Cauchy net in
X
converges.
Observe that in the above definition we can use a generating family of seminorms rather than all continuous seminorms.

If
\{S_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in a vector space, we can define the sequence of the corresponding
Cesàro means

\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

\displaystyle{C_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}S_{k}}.

The following is the analog for sequences
in LCTVS of an elementary fact well-known for sequences of numbers:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\{S_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a convergent sequence in an LCTVS
X
. Then the sequence of Cesàro means
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
also convergent, and has the same limit.

Proof.

Let
S=\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}S_{k}
. If
p
is a continuous seminorm on
X
and
\epsilon>0
, there is
N_{1}
such that
p(S_{k}-S)<\epsilon/2
for
k\geq N_{1}
. Set
s=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{1}}p(S_{k}-S)
. Then for
n>N_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle p(C_{n}-S)=p\left(\frac{1}{n+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}S_{k}-S\right)\leq\frac{1}{n+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n}p(S_{k}-S)<\frac{s}{n+1}+\frac{n-N_{1}}{n+1}\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Therefore, if we choose
N>N_{1}
so large that
\frac{s}{n+1}<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, then for
n\geq N
we have
p(C_{n}-S)<\epsilon
,
so
C_{n}\to S
in
X
.
∎

For a formal series
\displaystyle{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{j}}
in an LCTVS
X
, convergence is defined in the usual way, i.e. the sequence of partial sums converges in
X
. A formal sum

{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}x_{\alpha}}
over a countable index set
\mathfrak{A}
, where
x_{\alpha}
are vectors in an LCTVS
X
,
is said to be
absolutely convergent
if there exists a bijection

\tau:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}

such that for every continuous seminorm
p
on
X
, the series of non-negative real numbers

\displaystyle{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p(x_{\tau(j)})}

is convergent (see
[
KK97
]
). To check that a series is absolutely convergent, we only need to check the convergence
of the above series for seminorms in a fixed generating family. If
X
is a locally compact Hausdorff space, absolute convergence in the Fréchet space
\mathcal{C}(X)

of continuous complex valued functions on
X
is what is classically called
normal convergence
(see
[
Rem91
, pp. 104 ff.]
). Absolute
convergence is typical for many spaces of holomorphic functions, e.g. in the space of holomorphic functions on a Reinhardt domain smooth up to the boundary (see
[
Daw21
]
).

The following result, whose proof mimics the corresponding result for numbers, shows that absolutely convergent series in
LCTVS behave very much like absolutely convergent series of numbers:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X
be a
complete
LCTVS, and let
{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}x_{\alpha}}
be an absolutely convergent series of elements of
X
.
Then the series is
unconditionally convergent
: there is an
s\in X
such that for
every
bijection
\theta:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, the series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}x_{\theta(j)}
converges in
X
to
s
.

The element
s\in X
is naturally called the
sum
of the series, and we write

s=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}}x_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

By definition, there exists a bijection
\sigma:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathfrak{A}
, such that for each continuous seminorm
p
on
X
, the series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p(x_{\sigma(j)})
converges. Let
y_{j}=x_{\sigma(j)}
and
s_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}y_{j}
. Since
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}p(y_{j})
converges, for
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that whenever
m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
with
m\geq\ell\geq N_{0}
,
\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{m}p(y_{j})<\epsilon
. Therefore for
m\geq\ell\geq N_{0}
,

p(s_{m}-s_{\ell})=p\Big(\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{m}y_{j}\Big)\leq\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{m}p(y_{j})<\epsilon.

(2.2)

Therefore
\{s_{k}\}
is Cauchy sequence in the complete LCTVS
X
, and therefore converges to an
s\in X
.
In order to complete the proof, it suffices to show that for every bijection
\tau:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, the series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}y_{\tau(j)}
converges to the same sum
s
. Let
s_{k}^{\tau}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}y_{\tau(j)}
. Choose
u\in\mathbb{N}
such that the set of integers
\{0,1,2,\cdots,N_{0}\}
is contained in the set
\{\tau(0),\tau(1),\cdots,\tau(u)\}
. Then, if
k>u
, the elements
y_{1},\cdots,y_{N_{0}}
get cancelled in the difference
s_{k}-s_{k}^{\tau}
and we have
p(s_{k}-s_{k}^{\tau})<\epsilon
by (
2.2
). This proves that the sequences
\{s_{k}\}
and
\{s_{k}^{\tau}\}
converge to the same limit. So,
s_{k}^{\tau}\rightarrow s
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

By a famous theorem of Dvoretzky and Rogers, the converse of the above result fails when
X
is an infinite dimensional Banach space (
[
KK97
, Chapter 4]
).

We will also need the notion of the weak (or Pettis) integral of a LCTVS valued function which we will now recall (see
[
Bou04
, p. INT III.32-39]
for details).
Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
K
, and let
f
be a continuous map from
K
to an LCTVS
X
.
An element
x\in X
is called a
Pettis integral
of
f
on
K
with respect to
\mu
if for all
\phi\in X^{\prime}
,

\phi(x)=\int_{K}(\phi\circ f)\,d\mu,

(2.3)

where
X^{\prime}
denotes the dual space of
X
(the space of continuous linear functionals on
X
), and the right hand side of (
2.3
) is an integral of a continuous function. If
X
is complete, one can show that there exists a unique
x\in X
such that (
2.3
) holds and we denote the Pettis integral of
f
on
K
with respect to
\mu
by
\displaystyle{\int_{K}f\hskip 2.84526ptd\mu=x}
. In fact, the integral exists uniquely, as soon as the space
X
is
quasi-complete
, i.e. if each
bounded
Cauchy net in
X
converges, where a net
\{x_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Gamma}

is bounded if for each continuous seminorm
p
, the net of real numbers
\{p(x_{\alpha})\}_{\alpha\in\Gamma}
is bounded. While there are situations in which this more refined existence theorem for Pettis integrals is useful (e.g. when the space
X
is a dual space with weak-* topology), in this paper, we only consider integrals in complete LCTVS. Since each Hausdorff TVS has a unique Hausdorff
completion, we can define Pettis integrals in any LCTVS, provided we allow the integral to have a value in the completion.

If
T:X\to Y
is a continuous linear map of complete LCTVSs, and
f:K\to X
is continuous then we have

T\left(\int_{K}f\,d\mu\right)=\int_{K}T\circ f\,d\mu,

(2.4)

since for any linear functional
\psi\in Y^{\prime}
,

\psi\left(T\left(\int_{K}f\,d\mu\right)\right)=(\psi\circ T)\left(\int_{K}f\,d\mu\right)=\int_{K}(\psi\circ T)(f)\,d\mu

using the fact that
\psi\circ T\in X^{\prime}
and the definition (
2.3
) of the Pettis integral.

2.2.
Representation of the torus on an LCTVS
X
.

Let

\mathbb{T}^{n}=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\left|\lambda_{j}\right|=1\hskip 2.84526pt\text{for every $1\leq j\leq n$}\}

be the
n
-dimensional unit torus. With the subspace topology
inherited from
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and binary operation defined as
(\lambda,\xi)\mapsto\lambda{\cdot}\xi=(\lambda_{1}\xi_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\xi_{n})
, is a compact abelian topological group. For a LCTVS
X
, and a continuous function
f:\mathbb{T}^{n}\to X
, we denote
the Pettis integral of
f
with respect to the Haar measure of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
(normalized to be a probability measure)
by

\int_{T^{n}}f(\lambda)d\lambda.

Let
X
be an LCTVS, and let
\lambda\mapsto\sigma_{\lambda}
be a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
X
. Recall that this means that for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the map
\sigma_{\lambda}
is an automorphism (i.e. linear self-homeomorphism) of
X
as a topological vector space, the map
\lambda\mapsto\sigma_{\lambda}
is a group homomorphism from
\mathbb{T}^{n}
to
\mathrm{Aut}(X)
, and the associated map

\sigma:\mathbb{T}^{n}\times X\to X,\quad\sigma(\lambda,x)=\sigma_{\lambda}(x)

is continuous.

Given a representation
\sigma
of the group
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on an LCTVS
X
, a continuous seminorm
p
on
X
is said
to be invariant (with respect to
\sigma
) if
p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))=p(x)
for all
x\in X
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

A representation
\sigma
of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on an LCTVS
X
is continuous if and only if the following two conditions are both satisfied:

(a)

the topology of
X
is generated by a family of invariant seminorms, and

(b)

for each
x\in X
the function from
\mathbb{T}^{n}
to
X
given by
\lambda\mapsto\sigma_{\lambda}(x)
is continuous at the identity element of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\sigma
is continuous, i.e.,
\sigma:\mathbb{T}^{n}\times X\to X
is continuous, so for
x\in X
, the function
\lambda\mapsto\sigma_{\lambda}(x)
is continuous on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, in particular at the identity. Therefore (b) follows.

For a continuous seminorm
q
on
X
, define
p(x)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}q(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))
, which is finite since
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is compact, and which is easily seen to be a seminorm. To show
p
is invariant, we note that

p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))=\sup\limits_{\mu\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}q(\sigma_{\mu}(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)))=\sup\limits_{\mu\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}q(\sigma_{\mu{\cdot}\lambda}(x))=\sup\limits_{\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}q(\sigma_{\xi}(x))=p(x).

It remains to show that
p
is continuous. For
x,y\in X
, we have

p(x)=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}q\left(\sigma_{\lambda}(y)+\sigma_{\lambda}(x-y)\right)\leq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}\left(q(\sigma_{\lambda}(y))+q(\sigma_{\lambda}(x-y))\right)\leq p(y)+p(x-y),

so that
\left|p(x)-p(y)\right|\leq p(x-y)
for all
x,y\in X
, and it follows that the seminorm
p
is continuous on
X
if and only if it is continuous at
0
. We show that for each
\epsilon>0
, there exists a neighbourhood
V
of
0
in
X
such that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
,
q\circ\sigma_{\lambda}<\epsilon
on
V
. For each
\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, since
q
and
\sigma
are continuous, there exists a neighborhood
U_{\xi}
of
\xi
in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
and a neighbourhood
V_{\xi}
of
0
in
X
such that

q(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))<\epsilon
for all
x\in V_{\xi}
and
\lambda\in U_{\xi}
.
The collection
\{U_{\xi}\}_{\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{n}}
forms an open cover of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Since
\mathbb{T}^{n}
is compact, let
\{U_{\xi_{1}},...,U_{\xi_{k}}\}
be a finite subcover of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
corresponding to the open cover. Then for all
x\in\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}V_{\xi_{j}}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, we have
q(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))<\epsilon
.

Now assume the two conditions (a) and (b). Let
(\Gamma,\geq)
be a directed set and let
(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Gamma}
and
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\Gamma}
be nets in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
and
X
respectively with
(\lambda_{\alpha},x_{\alpha})\rightarrow(\lambda,x)
in
\mathbb{T}^{n}\times X
. We need to show
\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha})\rightarrow\sigma_{\lambda}(x)
in
X
, i.e.,
p(\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha})-\sigma_{\lambda}(x))\to 0
for each invariant continuous seminorm
p
of
X
. But we have, by the invariance of
p
:

p\left(\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}}(x_{\alpha}\right)-\sigma_{\lambda}(x))=p\left(x_{\alpha}-\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\lambda}(x)\right)\leq p\left(x_{\alpha}-x\right)+p\left(x-\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\lambda}(x)\right).

The term
p\left(x_{\alpha}-x\right)
goes to zero since
x_{\alpha}\to x
, and the term
p\left(x-\sigma_{\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1}\lambda}(x)\right)

also goes to zero since
\lambda_{\alpha}\to\lambda
and
\mu\mapsto\sigma_{\mu}(x)
is
continuous at
\mu=\mathbf{1}
. The result follows.
∎

2.3.
Abstract Fejér Theorem

Let
X
be a complete LCTVS and let
\sigma
be a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
X
. For each
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and

x\in X
, define

\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda,

(2.5)

the Pettis integral of the continuous function
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)
on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
with respect to the Haar
probability measure of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
. We will say that
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(x)
is the
\alpha
-th
Fourier component
of
x
with respect to the representation
\sigma
. We use the standard convention with respect to multi-index powers, i.e.,
\lambda^{\alpha}=\lambda_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\lambda_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
. We will say that the subspace of
X
defined as

[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}=\{x\in X:\sigma_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{\alpha}\cdot x\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\}

(2.6)

is the
\alpha
-th
Fourier mode
of the space
X
, and we will call the map
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}

the
\alpha
-th
Fourier projection
, both with respect to the representation
\sigma
. We note the following facts:

Proposition 2.4
.

As above, let
X
be a complete LCTVS and
\sigma
be a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
X
.

(1)

For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the
\alpha
-th Fourier mode
[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}

is a closed
\sigma
-invariant subspace of
X
, and the Fourier projection
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}
is a continuous linear projection from

X
onto
[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}
.

(2)

Let
Y
be another complete LCTVS, and let
\tau
be a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
Y
, and let

j:Y\to X
be a continuous
linear map intertwining
\sigma
and
\tau
, i.e., for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
,

j\circ\tau_{\lambda}=\sigma_{\lambda}\circ j.

Then for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we have

j\circ\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}\circ j.

(2.7)

Proof.

(1)

Linearity of
\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}
follows from the linearity of
\sigma_{\lambda}
. Recall from Proposition
2.3
that there exists a family
\mathscr{P}
of continuous invariant seminorms that generates the locally convex topology of
X
. To see the continuity of
\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}
, observe that for each
p\in\mathscr{P}
, we have

p(\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x))=p\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda\right)\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}p\left(\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\right)\,d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}p(x)\,d\lambda=p(x),

(2.8)

where the inequality is due to Proposition 6 in
[
Bou04
, p. INT III.37]
. Now, if
x\in[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}
, then

\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\hskip 2.84526ptd\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\cdot\lambda^{\alpha}x\hskip 2.84526ptd\lambda=x\cdot\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\hskip 2.84526ptd\lambda\bigg)=x,

so that
[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}\subset\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(X)
. To prove that
\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(X)\subset[X]^{\sigma}_{\alpha}
, notice that
for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
and

x\in X

\displaystyle\sigma_{\lambda}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(x))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\lambda}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\mu^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\mu}(x)d\mu\right)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\mu^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda\cdot\mu}(x)d\mu

using (
2.4
)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\alpha}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}(\lambda\mu)^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda\mu}(x)d\mu=\lambda^{\alpha}\cdot\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle\text{by Haar invariance}.

(2)

For
x\in X
, we have

j\circ\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}(x)=j\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\tau_{\lambda}(x)d\lambda\right)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}j\circ\tau_{\lambda}(x)d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}\circ j(x)d\lambda=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}\circ j(x),

where we have used (
2.4
) to go from the second to the third step.

∎

Remark
.

The inequality (
2.8
)

p\left(\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x)\right)\leq p(x),

(2.9)

which holds for each
\alpha
for an invariant seminorm
p
can be thought of as an abstract
form of the familiar
Cauchy inequalities
of complex analysis.

If
X
is
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, the Banach space of integrable functions on

\mathbb{T}
, and if
\sigma
is the continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}
on
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})

given by

\sigma_{\lambda}(f)(\mu)=f(\lambda\cdot\mu),\quad\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{T},f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}),

an easy computation shows that for
\phi\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(f)(e^{i\phi})=e^{i\alpha\phi}\cdot\hat{f}(\alpha),\quad\text{with }\hat{f}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-i\alpha\theta}f(e^{i\theta})d\theta,

the
\alpha
-th term of the Fourier series of
f
.

It is therefore natural to define, for
x\in X
, the
Fourier series
of
x
with respect to
\sigma
to be the formal series

x\sim\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x).

(2.10)

For an integer
k\geq 0
, define the
k
-th
square partial sum
of the Fourier series in (
2.10
) by

S_{k}^{\sigma}(x)=\sum\limits_{\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x),

(2.11)

where
\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\coloneqq\max\big\{\left|\alpha_{j}\right|,1\leq j\leq n\big\}
. We are ready to state an abstract version of Fejér’s theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on an LCTVS
X
and let
x\in X
. Then the Cesàro means of the square partial sums of the Fourier series of
x
(with respect to
\sigma
) converge to
x
in the topology of
X
.

Proof.

Write the Cesàro means of the square partial sums of the Fourier series of
x
as,

\displaystyle C_{N}^{\sigma}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}S_{k}^{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\sum\limits_{\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\bm{\pi}_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x)=\frac{1}{N+1}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\sum\limits_{\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N+1}\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\left(\sum\limits_{\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\lambda^{-\alpha}\right)\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda=\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{N}(\lambda)\hskip 2.84526pt\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda

where

F_{N}(\lambda)=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{N+1}\sum\limits_{k-0}^{N}\left(\sum\limits_{\left|\alpha\right|_{\infty}\leq k}\lambda^{-\alpha}\right)}

is the classical
Fejér kernel. Introducing polar coordinates,
\lambda_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}}
on
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, and summing, we obtain the classical representation

F_{N}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\cdots,e^{i\theta_{n}})=\frac{1}{N+1}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}\left(\left(\frac{N+1}{2}\right)\theta_{j}\right)}{\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)}

It is well-known that the Fejér kernel has the properties that

(a)

F_{N}\geq 0
for all
N
,

(b)

\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{N}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=1

and

(c)

For each
\delta>0
,
F_{N}\rightarrow 0
uniformly on
\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus B(\mathbf{1},\delta)
, where
B(\mathbf{1},\delta)
is the
n
-dimensional ball centered at
\mathbf{1}=(1,1,...,1)
and radius
\delta
.

Let
p
be a continuous
\sigma
-invariant seminorm on
X
. Then for
x\in X
, we have

\displaystyle p(C_{N}(x)-x)

\displaystyle=p\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{N}(\lambda)\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda-x\cdot\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{N}(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right)

using property (b) of
F_{N}

\displaystyle=p\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{N}(\lambda)\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(x)\,d\lambda-\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}x\cdot F_{N}(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right)

using (
2.4
)

\displaystyle=p\left(\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\big(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x\big)\cdot F_{N}(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x)\cdot F_{N}(\lambda)\,d\lambda

(2.12)

using
[
Bou04
, Prop. 6, p. INT III.37]
and the positivity of
F_{N}(\lambda)

Since
\lambda\mapsto\sigma_{\lambda}(x)
is continuous and
p
is a continuous seminorm, there exists
\delta>0
such that

p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-\sigma_{\mathbf{1}}(x))=p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x)<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
whenever
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\cap B(\mathbf{1},\delta).

Then on the set
\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus B(\mathbf{1},\delta)
we have

p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x)\leq p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x))+p(x)=2\cdot p(x).

By property (c) of
F_{N}
, there exists
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that whenever
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus B(\mathbf{1},\delta)
, for all
N\geq N_{1}
we have,

F_{N}(\lambda)<\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot p(x)}

(2.13)

Now from (
2.12
), we have

\displaystyle p(C_{N}(x)-x)

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\cap B(\mathbf{1},\delta)}F_{N}(\lambda)\cdot p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x)\,d\lambda+\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}\setminus B(\mathbf{1},\delta)}F_{N}(\lambda)\cdot p(\sigma_{\lambda}(x)-x)\,d\lambda

\displaystyle<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}F_{n}(\lambda)\,d\lambda+\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot p(x)}\cdot 2\cdot p(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.

Since by Proposition
2.3
the topology of
X
is generated by
\sigma
-invariant seminorms, the result follows.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X
be a complete LCTVS and suppose that we are given
a continuous representation of the group
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
X
. Then if the Fourier series of an element
x\in X
with respect to this representation is absolutely convergent in
X
, the sum of the Fourier series equals
x
.

Proof.

Since the series
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\pi_{\alpha}^{\sigma}(x)
is absolutely convergent, by Proposition
2.2
, there exists the sum of the series, i.e., an
\widetilde{x}\in X
such that for every bijection
\theta:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{n}
we have
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\pi_{\theta(j)}^{\sigma}(x)=\widetilde{x}
. Let
S_{N}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\pi_{\theta(j)}^{\sigma}(x)
; then the sequence of partial sums
\{S_{N}\}
converges to
\widetilde{x}
in
X
. By Proposition
2.1
, the sequence of Cesàro means
\{C_{N}\}
of the partial sums converges to
\widetilde{x}
as well. However, by Theorem
2.1
, the Cesàro means converge to

. Therefore
\widetilde{x}=x
.
∎

3.
Recapturing Complex analysis

We now use the machinery developed in the previous section to give a conceptually simple account of the remarkable regularity properties of holomorphic distributions.
So we will pretend that we have forgotten everything about complex analysis, but do remember the rudiments of the theory of distributions, accounts of which can be found in the classic
treatises
[
Hör03
,
Trè67
,
Sch66
]
. First we clarify notations and recall a few facts.

3.1.
The basic spaces

For an open
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the space
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
of test functions is the
LF
-space of smooth compactly supported complex valued functions, topologized as the inductive limit of
the Fréchet spaces
\mathscr{D}_{K}
consisting, for a given compact
K\subset\Omega
, of those elements of
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
which have support in
K
.
Recall that a subset
B\subset\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
is bounded, if and only if there is a compact
K\subset\Omega
such that

B\subset\mathscr{D}_{K}
, and for each nonnegative multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we have

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in B\\
x\in K\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\phi(x)\right|<\infty,

(3.1)

where, here and later, we will use standard multi-index conventions such as

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial^{\left|\alpha\right|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}},\quad\left|\alpha\right|=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j},\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(3.2)

The space of distributions
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
on
\Omega
is
the dual of
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
, consisting of continuous linear forms on
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
.
We denote the value of a distribution
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
at a test function
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
by

\left\langle T,\phi\right\rangle.
The space
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is endowed with the usual strong dual topology. Recall that this topology is generated by
the family of seminorms

\left\{p_{B}:B\subset\mathscr{D}(\Omega)\text{ is bounded}\right\},\quad\text{where, }p_{B}(T)=\sup_{\phi\in B}\left|\left\langle T,\phi\right\rangle\right|.

(3.3)

In this topology, the space
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is complete.

Given a locally integrable function
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, we can associate to
f
a distribution
T_{f}
defined by

\left\langle T_{f},\phi\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega}f\phi\,dV,\textrm{ for }\phi\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega),

where
dV
denotes the Lebesgue measure of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Then the
locally integrable distribution

T_{f}
is said to be generated by
f
, and
as usual we grant ourselves the the right to abuse language by identifying the distribution
T_{f}\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
with the function
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
.

We will use the abbreviations

\bm{D}_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}},\quad\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline{z_{j}}},

(3.4)

for the basic constant coefficient differential operators of complex analysis, acting on functions or distributions on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
If
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is an open set, define

\mathscr{O}(\Omega)=\left\{f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega):\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}f=0,1\leq j\leq n\right\},

(3.5)

the space of
holomorphic distributions
on
\Omega
. The subspace
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is closed in the space
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
by the continuity of the operators
\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}
, and is therefore a complete LCTVS in the subspace topology.

3.2.
The main theorem

We begin with some definitions and notational conventions. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
we denote by
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
the
Reinhardt rotation
of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
by the element
\lambda
, the linear
automorphism of the vector space
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
given by

\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}(z)=(\lambda_{1}z_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}z_{n}).

(3.6)

A domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is defined to be
Reinhardt
if and only if
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}(\Omega)=\Omega
for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
.
Let
Z
denote the union of the coordinate hyperplanes of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
:

Z=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:z_{j}=0\}.

(3.7)

Recall that a Reinhardt domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is said to be
log-convex
, if whenever
z,w\in\Omega\setminus Z
, the point

\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
belongs to
\Omega
if there is a
t\in[0,1]
such that for
1\leq j\leq n

\left|\zeta_{j}\right|=\left|z_{j}\right|^{t}\left|w_{j}\right|^{1-t}.

(3.8)

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, let
e_{\alpha}
be the monomial function given by

e_{\alpha}(z)=z^{\alpha}=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots z_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}.

(3.9)

For a Reinhardt subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
1\leq j\leq n
, let

{\Omega}^{(j)}=\{(z_{1},\dots,\zeta z_{j},\dots,z_{n}):z\in\Omega,\zeta\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}\},

(3.10)

where
\overline{\mathbb{D}}=\{\left|\zeta\right|\leq 1\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is the closed disk. This can be thought of as the result of “completing”
\Omega
in the
j
-th coordinate.
Following
[
JP08
]
, we say that the Reinhardt domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is
relatively complete
if for each
1\leq j\leq n
, whenever we have

\Omega\cap\{z_{j}=0\}\not=\emptyset
, we also have

\Omega^{(j)}\subset\Omega.

We prove the following well-known structure theorem for holomorphic distributions on Reinhardt domains, as an application of the ideas of Section
2
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let

\mathcal{S}(\Omega)=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:e_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)\}.

(3.11)

Let
\widehat{\Omega}
be the smallest relatively complete log-convex Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
that contains
\Omega
.
The for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)

there is a continuous linear functional

a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that for each
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
, the series

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)}a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}

(3.12)

converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\widehat{\Omega})
to a function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\widehat{\Omega})
, and
f|_{\Omega}
generates
the distribution
T
.

Remarks:

(1)

For
n=1
, all Reinhardt domains in the plane, i.e., disks and annuli, are automatically relatively complete and log-convex. For
n\geq 2
,
it is easy to give examples of Reinhardt domains which are not log-convex, or not relatively complete, or perhaps both. For such a domain
\Omega
, it follows that each holomorphic
distribution
f\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
extends to a holomorphic function
F\in\mathscr{O}(\widehat{\Omega})
. This is the simplest example of
Hartogs phenomenon
, the compulsory extension of
all holomorphic functions from a smaller domain to a larger one, characteristic of domains in several complex variables.

(2)

The functionals
a_{\alpha}
are called the
coefficient functionals
, and the series (
3.12
) is of course the
Laurent series

of the function
f
(the
Taylor series
if
\mathcal{S}(\Omega)=\mathbb{N}^{n}
).

(3)

It is known (by a direct construction of a plurisubharmonic exhaustion) that relatively complete log-convex Reinhardt domains are pseudoconvex. This means that such a domain
\Omega

admits a holomorphic distribution whose Laurent expansion converges absolutely precisely on
\Omega
.

As an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.1
we have the following:

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be open. Then each distribution
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is
complex-analytic
, i.e., for each
p\in\Omega
there
is a neighborhood
U
of
p
, where the function
f
generating
T
is represented by a Taylor series centered at
p
.

3.3.
Holomorphic functions and maps

A holomorphic distribution
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
will be called a
holomorphic function
if it is generated by a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
function
f
. We denote the space of holomorphic functions on
\Omega
temporarily by
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
. Once Theorem
3.1
is proved, it will follow that
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)=\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
be domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. By a
holomorphic map

\Phi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
, we mean a mapping each of
whose components is a holomorphic function on
\Omega_{1}
. A holomorphic map is a
biholomorphism
, if it is a bijection, and its set-theoretic inverse is also a holomorphic map. (It is of course known that the assumption of the holomorphicity of the inverse map is redundant, but this is a consequence of complex-analyticity, which is exactly what we are proving here).
If
\Phi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a biholomorphism, then for a distribution
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{2})
, we can define in the usual way the
pullback
distribution
\Phi^{*}T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{1})
: if
T
is generated by a test function
f\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega_{2})
, then

\Phi^{*}T
is the distribution generated by the function
f\circ\Phi
, and for general
T
, we extend this definition by continuity, using the density of
test functions in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, see
[
Hör03
, Theorem 6.1.2]
for details. Extending the chain rules for the complex
derivative operators from test functions to distributions, we have the following relations analogous to
[
Hör03
, formula (6.1.2)]
for the Wirtinger derivatives (
3.4
):

\bm{D}_{j}(\Phi^{*}T)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bm{D}_{j}{\Phi_{k}}\cdot\Phi^{*}\left(\bm{D}_{k}T\right),

(3.13)

and

\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}(\Phi^{*}T)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}\overline{\Phi_{k}}\cdot\Phi^{*}\left(\bm{\overline{D}}_{k}T\right),

(3.14)

where as above,
\Phi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a biholomorphism of domains in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, written in components as
\Phi=(\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{n})
,
and
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega_{2})
.

Therefore, we have the following immediate consequence of (
3.14
):

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\Phi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
is a biholomorphism and
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega_{2})
, then we have
\Phi^{*}T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega_{1})
. If
f\in(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega_{2})
, then
\Phi^{*}f\in(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega_{1})
.

Therefore the spaces of holomorphic distributions and functions
are invariant under pullbacks under biholomorphic maps.
In fact, in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, only two simple special cases of Proposition
3.2
noted below are needed:

(1)

Translation by a vector
a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is the map
\mathrm{M}_{a}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

\mathrm{M}_{a}(z)=z+a,

(3.15)

which is obviously a biholomorphic automorphism of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. For a domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, we therefore have a pullback isomorphism of spaces of holomorphic distributions

\mathrm{M}_{a}^{*}:\mathscr{O}(\mathrm{M}_{a}(\Omega))\to\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
. This can be thought of as an expression of the fact that the operator
\overline{\partial}=(\bm{\overline{D}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\overline{D}}_{n})
is translation invariant.

(2)

The Reinhardt rotations
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
of (
3.6
) are clearly biholomorphic automorphisms of Reinhardt domains, and the pullback operation induces a representation
of the group
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on the space of holomorphic distributions (see (
3.32
) below).

A domain
\Omega
is said to be

Reinhardt centered at
a
for an
a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
if there is a Reinhardt domain
\Omega_{0}
such that
\Omega=\mathrm{M}_{a}(\Omega_{0})
.
Every open set in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

has local Reinhardt symmetry, in the sense that each point has neighborhood which is a Reinhardt domain centered at that point.

3.4.
Mean value property of the monomials

It is easily verified by direct computation that for each
\alpha
, we have
e_{\alpha}\in(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z)
, where
e_{\alpha}
is the monomial of (
3.9
).
We now note a remarkable symmetry property (the Mean-Value Property) of the functions
e_{\alpha}
:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, let
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
, and let

\psi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z)
be a test function which has radial symmetry in each variable around the point
z
, i.e., there are functions
\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{n}\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{R}})
such that

\psi(\zeta)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}(\left|\zeta_{j}-z_{j}\right|)
, and whose integral is 1:

\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z}\psi(\zeta)dV(\zeta)=1.

(3.16)

Then we have

\left\langle e_{\alpha},\psi\right\rangle=e_{\alpha}(z)

(3.17)

Proof.

First consider the case
n=1
.
If
\alpha\geq 0
, the formula

\int_{0}^{2\pi}(z+re^{i\theta})^{\alpha}d\theta=2\pi z^{\alpha}

(3.18)

holds for
r>0
, by expanding the integrand using the binomial formula, and integrating the finite sum term by term. The formula (
3.18
) also holds
for
\alpha<0
, provided
z\not=0
, and
0<r<\left|z\right|
. This follows on noticing that we have an infinite series expansion

(z+re^{i\theta})^{\alpha}=z^{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{r}{z}e^{i\theta}\right)^{\alpha}=z^{\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\alpha}{k}\left(\frac{r}{z}\right)^{k}e^{ik\theta},

where by the

M
-test, the convergence is uniform in
\theta
, and then integrating the series on the right term by term.

Now
\psi(\zeta)=\rho(\left|\zeta-z\right|)
and
\psi
is supported in some disc
B(z,R)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, which means
R<\left|z\right|
. Then the normalization (
3.16
)
is equivalent to

2\pi\int_{0}^{R}\rho(r)rdr=1.

(3.19)

Now

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{\alpha},\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}e_{\alpha}(\zeta)\rho(\left|\zeta-z\right|)dV(\zeta)=\int\limits_{0}^{R}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}(z+re^{i\theta})^{\alpha}\rho(r)rd\theta dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\rho(r)r\left(\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}(z+re^{i\theta})^{\alpha}d\theta\right)dr=2\pi z^{\alpha}\cdot\int_{0}^{R}\rho(r)rdr

using (
3.18
)

\displaystyle=z^{\alpha}=e_{\alpha}(z),

\displaystyle\text{using \eqref{eq-mvp2}}.

which establishes the result for
n=1
. In the general case, notice that for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
we have

e_{\alpha}(\zeta)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{j}}(\zeta_{j}).

Therefore, since
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z={\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times\dots\times{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{\alpha},\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z}\prod_{j=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{j}}(\zeta_{j})\rho_{j}(\left|\zeta_{j}-z_{j}\right|)\,dV(\zeta)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{C}}^{*}}e_{\alpha_{j}}(\zeta_{j})\rho_{j}(\left|\zeta_{j}-z_{j}\right|)\,dV(\zeta_{j})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}e_{\alpha_{j}}(z_{j})=e_{\alpha}(z).

∎

3.5.
The representation
\tau

If
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is a Reinhardt domain, then for each
\lambda
, the map
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
of (
3.6
)
is a biholomorphic automorphism of
\Omega
.
Define a representation
\tau
of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on the space
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
of test functions by

\tau_{\lambda}\psi=\psi\circ\mathrm{R}_{\lambda},\quad\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega).

(3.20)

Recall that a net
\{\phi_{j}\}
converges in the space
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
,
if each
\phi_{j}
is supported in a fixed compact
K\subset\Omega
, and the net
\{\phi_{j}\}
converges in the Fréchet space
\mathscr{D}_{K},
i.e. all partial derivatives converge uniformly on
K
.
Using this it is easily verified that
\tau
is a continuous representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
in the space
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
.

Notice that
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
is a Reinhardt domain. For a positive integer
k

we define the norm
\left\|\psi\right\|_{k}
with respect to the polar coordinates for a function
\psi
in
\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z)
:

\left\|\psi\right\|_{k}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq k\end{subarray}}\,\sup_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z}\left|\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial\theta^{\gamma}}\psi\right|,

(3.21)

where the tuples
r\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{n},\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are the polar coordinates on

{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
specified by
z_{j}=re^{i\theta_{j}}
, and
\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}},\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial\theta^{\gamma}}

are partial derivatives operators in the polar coordinates defined as in (
3.2
).
From the formulas

{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}=\cos\theta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}-\frac{\sin\theta_{j}}{r_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}}\text{ and }{\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}={\sin\theta_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}+\frac{\cos\theta_{j}}{r_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}},

(3.22)

we see that for
0<\varrho_{1}<\varrho_{2}<\infty
, there is a constant
B_{k}(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})
such that for each compact set
K
such that

K\subset\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\varrho_{1}<\left|z_{j}\right|<\varrho_{2}\},

we have

\frac{1}{B_{k}(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})}\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{k}\leq\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq B_{k}(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{k}.

(3.23)

We will need the following elementary
estimate:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\tau
be the representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
given by (
3.20
).
For integers
m,k\geq 0
, and a compact
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
there is a constant
C>0
such that for each
\psi\in\mathscr{D}_{K}

and each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, such that
\left|\alpha_{j}\right|\geq 2k
for
1\leq j\leq n
, we have

\left\|\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq\frac{C}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m}}\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+nm}}.

Proof.

For
r\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{n},\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, define the
\alpha
-th Fourier coefficient of
\psi
by

\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int\limits_{[0,2\pi]^{n}}\psi(re^{i\theta})e^{-i\left\langle\alpha,\theta\right\rangle}d\theta,

(3.24)

where
re^{i\theta}=(r_{1}e^{i\theta_{1}},\dots,r_{n}e^{i\theta_{n}})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
,
\left\langle\alpha,\theta\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\theta_{j}
and

d\theta=d\theta_{1}\dots d\theta_{n}
is the Lebesgue measure.
We will also write
(\psi)^{\wedge}
for
\widehat{\psi}
whenever convenient. We note the following properties:

(1)

We clearly have

\sup_{r\in({\mathbb{R}}^{+})^{n}}\left|\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha)\right|\leq\sup_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z}\left|\psi\right|.

(3.25)

(2)

If
\alpha\not=0
, by a standard integration by parts argument, for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha)=\frac{1}{(i\alpha)^{\ell}}\left(\frac{\partial^{\ell}\psi}{\partial\theta^{\ell}}\right)^{\wedge}(r,\alpha),

(3.26)

where, as usual, we set for
\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
\zeta^{\beta}=\zeta_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dots\zeta_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
.

(3)

Differentiating under the integral sign we have, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{\beta}\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int\limits_{[0.2\pi]^{n}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}\psi}{\partial r^{\beta}}(re^{i\theta})e^{i\left\langle\beta,\theta\right\rangle}e^{-i\left\langle\alpha,\theta\right\rangle}d\theta={\left(\frac{\partial^{\beta}\psi}{\partial r^{\beta}}\right)}^{\wedge}(r,\alpha-\beta),

and combining this with (
3.26
), we see that if
\beta\not=\alpha
, we have for each integer
\ell\geq 0
that

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{\beta}\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha)=\frac{1}{\left(i(\alpha-\beta)\right)^{\ell}}\left(\frac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial\theta^{\ell}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\psi\right)^{\wedge}(r,\alpha-\beta).

(3.27)

Now for
re^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
the evaluation
\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z)\to{\mathbb{C}}
,
\psi\mapsto\psi(re^{i\theta})
is continuous,
so using (
2.4
) we see that for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and each
\psi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z)
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi(re^{i\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\lambda^{-\alpha}(\tau_{\lambda}\psi)(re^{i\theta})d\lambda=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{[0,2\pi]^{n}}e^{-i\left\langle\alpha,\phi\right\rangle}\psi(e^{i\phi}\cdot re^{i\theta})d\phi=e^{i\left\langle\alpha,\theta\right\rangle}\cdot\widehat{\psi}(r,\alpha),

(3.28)

where in the last step we make a change of variables in the integral from
\phi
to
\phi+\theta
. Therefore, if
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with
\beta_{j}\not=\alpha_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq n
, then

\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial\theta^{\gamma}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}(\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi)(re^{i\theta})=e^{i\left\langle\alpha,\theta\right\rangle}\cdot\frac{(i\alpha)^{\gamma}}{\left(i(\alpha-\beta)\right)^{\ell}}{\left(\frac{\partial^{\ell}}{\partial\theta^{\ell}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\psi\right)}^{\wedge}(r,\alpha-\beta).

Now in the above formula, if we have
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq k
, and let

\ell=(k+m,\dots,k+m)=(k+m)\bm{1},

where
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is the multi-index each of whose
n
entries is 1, we obtain, after taking absolute values of both sides

\left|\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial\theta^{\gamma}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}(\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi)(re^{i\theta})\right|=\frac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{\gamma_{j}}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}-\beta_{j}\right|^{m+k}}\cdot\left|\left(\frac{\partial^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}{\partial\theta^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\psi\right)^{\wedge}(r,\alpha-\beta)\right|.

(3.29)

In the first factor on the right hand side, we have by hypothesis for each
j
that
\left|\alpha_{j}\right|\geq 2k
and
0\leq\beta_{j},\gamma_{j}\leq k
.
Therefore, we have
\left|\alpha_{j}-\beta_{j}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|
. The first factor can be estimated as

\frac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{\gamma_{j}}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}-\beta_{j}\right|^{m+k}}\leq\frac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{k}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m+k}\right)}=\frac{2^{m+k}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m}}.

(3.30)

Using (
3.25
), the second factor can be estimated as

\left|\left(\frac{\partial^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}{\partial\theta^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\psi\right)^{\wedge}(r,\alpha-\beta)\right|\leq\sup_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z}\left|\frac{\partial^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}{\partial\theta^{(m+k)\bm{1}}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}\psi\right|\leq\left\|\psi\right\|_{2nk+nm},

(3.31)

where in the last step we use the norm introduced in (
3.21
), and used the fact that

\left|(m+k)\bm{1}+\beta\right|=(m+k)n+\left|\beta\right|\leq(m+k)n+nk,

since each
\beta_{j}\leq k
.
Combining (
3.29
), (
3.30
) and (
3.31
) we see that

\left|\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial\theta^{\gamma}}\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial r^{\beta}}(\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi)(re^{i\theta})\right|\leq\frac{2^{m+k}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m}}\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{2nk+nm},

from which, taking a supremum on the left hand side over
re^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus Z
, and remembering that
\left|\beta\right|+\left|\gamma\right|\leq k
,
we conclude that

\left\|\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi\right\|_{k}\leq\frac{2^{m+k}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m}}\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{2nk+nm}.

Recall that
K
is a compact set in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
such that
\psi\in\mathscr{D}_{K}
, i.e. , the support of
\psi
is contained in
K
. Suppose that

\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2}
are such that
K\subset A
, where
A
is the product of annuli
A=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:\varrho_{1}<\left|z_{j}\right|<\varrho_{2},1\leq j\leq n\}
. Formulas (
3.24
) and (
3.28
) show that the compact support of
\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi
is also contained in the set
A
. Therefore,
passing to the equivalent
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-norms using (
3.23
), we have

\left\|\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}}\leq B_{k}(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})\cdot B_{2nk+nm}(\varrho_{1},\varrho_{2})\cdot\frac{2^{m+k}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\left|\alpha_{j}\right|^{m}}\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+nm}},

which completes the proof of the result.
∎

3.6.
The dual representation

Let
\Omega
be a Reinhardt domain.
Since for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, the map
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
maps
\Omega
biholomorphically (and therefore diffeomorphically) to itself. Consequently, we can define a representation of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on the space of distributions
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
using the pullback operation

\sigma_{\lambda}(T)=(\mathrm{R}_{\lambda})^{*}(T),\quad T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega).

(3.32)

The representation
\sigma_{\lambda}
is closely related to the representation
\tau_{\lambda}
introduced in (
3.20
).
Clearly,
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
is an invariant (dense) subspace of
\sigma
, on which
\sigma
restricts to
\tau
.
So
\sigma
is simply the extension of the representation
\tau
of (
3.20
) by continuity to the space of distributions.

The representation
\sigma
on
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is also “dual” to the representation
\tau
on
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
:

\left\langle\sigma_{\lambda}(T),\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle(\mathrm{R}_{\lambda})^{*}(T),\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle T,\frac{1}{\det_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathrm{R}_{\lambda})}\phi\circ\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}^{-1}\right\rangle=\left\langle T,\tau_{\lambda^{-1}}\phi\right\rangle,

(3.33)

so that
\sigma_{\lambda}
is the transpose of the map
\tau_{\lambda^{-1}}
. The second equality in the chain may be proved by change of variables when
T
is a test function, and then using density.

Proposition 3.5
.

The representation
\sigma
of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
defined in (
3.32
) is continuous.

Proof.

Let
\{\lambda_{j}\}
be a sequence in
\mathbb{T}^{n}
converging to the identity element. Since pointwise convergence of a sequence in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
on each test function implies convergence in the strong dual topology (see
[
Trè67
]
), to show that
\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}(T)\to T
in

\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
we need to show that for
\phi\in\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
we have
\left\langle\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}(T),\phi\right\rangle\to\left\langle T,\phi\right\rangle
. But by (
3.33
),

\left\langle\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}(T),\phi\right\rangle=\left\langle T,\tau_{\lambda_{j}^{-1}}\phi\right\rangle
and by the continuity of the representation
\tau
, to follows that

\sigma_{\lambda_{j}}(T)\to T
in

\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
.

To complete the proof, by Proposition
2.3
, we need to show that the topology of
\Omega
is generated by a collection of

\sigma
-invariant seminorms. Let
B
be a bounded subset of
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
. Let

\widetilde{B}=\{\sigma_{\lambda}(\phi):\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n},\phi\in B\}.

Then it is clear from (
3.1
) that
\widetilde{B}
is also a bounded subset of
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
. With notation as it (
3.3
),
it is clear that for each
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, we have
p_{B}(T)\leq p_{\widetilde{B}}(T)
. Therefore the topology of
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is generated by
the family of continuous invariant seminorms
\{p_{\widetilde{B}}\}
.
∎

3.7.
Fourier series of distributions on Reinhardt domains

Let
\Omega
be a Reinhardt domain and let
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
be a
distribution. Then by the results of Section
2
we can expand
T
in a formal Fourier series with respect to the representation

\sigma
of (
3.32
). For simplicity of notation, whenever there is no possibility of confusion, we denote the Fourier components of

T
by

T_{\alpha}=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(T),\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},

(3.34)

so that the Fourier series of
T
is written as
T\sim\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}T_{\alpha}
. We notice the following properties of the Fourier components:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
and
T
be as above and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

(a)

If the distribution
T
lies in one of the linear subspaces
\mathscr{O}(\Omega),\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega),(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
or

\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
of
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, the Fourier component
T_{\alpha}
also lies in the same subspace.

(b)

If
U
is another Reinhardt domain such that
U\subset\Omega
, we have

T_{\alpha}|_{U}=\left(T|_{U}\right)_{\alpha}.

(3.35)

Proof.

All these are consequences of Part 2 of Proposition
2.4
. To see Part (a), let
X
be the space
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and
let
Y
be one of the spaces
\mathscr{O}(\Omega),\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
or
\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
. Each of these has a natural structure
of a complete LCTVS (the space
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
as a closed subspace of
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
,
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
in its Fréchet topology and

\mathscr{D}(\Omega)
in its
LF
-topology), and for each one of these topologies, the representation
\sigma
restricts to a continuous representation
(in the stronger topology of the subspace), which we will call
\tau
(this coincides with the
\tau
introduced in (
3.20
)). If
j
is the inclusion map of any one of
these subspaces into
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, then clearly it is continuous (where the subspace has the natural topology). Therefore, by (
2.7
), we have
j\left(\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}T\right)=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(j(T))
. By definition, the right hand side is precisely
T_{\alpha}
, i.e. the Fourier component of
T
as an element of
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
. Notice that
\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{\alpha}T\in Y
, since it is the
Fourier component of
T
as an element of
Y
. Since
j
is the inclusion of
Y
in
X
, it now follows that
T_{\alpha}\in Y
as well.

Since the result is true for
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
it follows for
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
.

For part (b), in part 2 of Proposition
2.4
, let
X=\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(U)
with representation defined in (
3.32
), which we call

\sigma^{\prime}
for clarity, and let
Y=\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
with the representation
\sigma
and let
j:Y\to X
be the restriction map of distributions, which
is clearly continuous and intertwines the two representations. Then (
2.7
) takes the form
j\circ\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\alpha}\circ j
. Applying this to the distribution
T
, we see that

T_{\alpha}|_{U}=j\left(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}T\right)=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\alpha}\left(j(T)\right)=\left(T|_{U}\right)_{\alpha}.

∎

We establish the following lemma:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a Reinhardt domain, and let

\mathscr{A}=\{T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega):\text{each Fourier component }T_{\alpha}\text{ belongs to }\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)\}.

Suppose that for each
T\in\mathscr{A}
the Laurent series
\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}T_{\alpha}
converges absolutely in the Fréchet space

\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
.
Then the Laurent series of each element of
\mathscr{A}
converges absolutely in the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

For a function
f\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
and a compact set
K
, let
p_{K}
be the seminorm:

p_{K}(f)=\sup_{K}\left|f\right|.

(3.36)

Notice that the family
\{p_{K}:K\subset\Omega\quad\text{compact}\}
is a generating family of seminorms for
\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
, and the hypothesis on
\mathscr{A}
can be expressed as

\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}p_{K}(T_{\alpha})<\infty,

(3.37)

for each compact
K\subset\Omega
.

The Fréchet topology of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is generated by the seminorms
p_{K,L}
, where
K
ranges over compact subsets of
\Omega
,

L
is a constant coefficient differential operator on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, and

p_{K,L}(f)=\sup_{K}\left|Lf\right|.

We will continue to write
p_{K}
for the seminorm (
3.36
) corresponding to
L=1
. From the distributional chain rule (
3.13
), we have for each
j
that

\bm{D}_{j}\sigma_{\lambda}(T)=\bm{D}_{j}(\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}^{*}T)=(\bm{D}_{j}\mathrm{R}_{\lambda})\cdot\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}^{*}(\bm{D}_{j}T)=\lambda_{j}\cdot\sigma_{\lambda}(\bm{D}_{j}T).

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and denote
\bm{D}^{\beta}=\bm{D}_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dots\bm{D}_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
. Then, from the above we have

\displaystyle\bm{D}^{\beta}T_{\alpha}=\bm{D}^{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(T)d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\bm{D}^{\beta}\sigma_{\lambda}(T)d\lambda=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-(\alpha-\beta)}\sigma_{\lambda}(\bm{D}^{\beta}T)d\lambda=(\bm{D}^{\beta}T)_{\alpha-\beta},

where
(\bm{D}^{\beta}T)_{\alpha-\beta}=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{{\alpha-\beta}}(\bm{D}^{\beta}T)
. It follows that all Fourier coefficients of the distribution
\bm{D}^{\beta}T
are in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, so
\bm{D}^{\beta}T\in\mathscr{A}
.
Therefore,

p_{K,\bm{D}^{\beta}}(T_{\alpha})=p_{K}\left(\bm{D}^{\beta}T_{\alpha}\right)=p_{K}\left((\bm{D}^{\beta}T)_{\alpha-\beta}\right).

It follows therefore that

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}p_{K,\bm{D}^{\beta}}(T_{\alpha})=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}p_{K}\left((\bm{D}^{\beta}T)_{\gamma}\right)<\infty,

using assumption (
3.37
) on elements of
\mathscr{A}
.
A partial derivative
L=\frac{\partial^{\gamma}}{\partial x^{\gamma}}\frac{\partial^{\delta}}{\partial y^{\delta}}
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
can be rewritten as a polynomial in the commuting differential operators
\bm{D}_{1},\dots,\bm{D}_{n},\bm{\overline{D}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\overline{D}}_{n}
, and by hypothesis the derivatives
\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}
are zero. Therefore, it follows that
\sum_{\alpha}p_{K,L}(T_{\alpha})<\infty
. Consequently the series
\sum_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}
is absolutely convergent in

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

∎

3.8.
Proof of Theorem
3.1
: case of annular domains

We now prove a weaker version of
Theorem
3.1
.
A Reinhardt domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
will be called
annular
if it is disjoint from the coordinate hyperplanes:

\Omega\cap Z=\emptyset,

(3.38)

where
Z
is as in (
3.7
). We have the following:

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z
be an annular Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Then for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
there is a continuous linear functional

a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}
such that for each
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
, the series of terms in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
given by

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}

converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}({\Omega})
to a function
f
, and this
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-smooth function generates
the distribution
T
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
and let
T_{\alpha}
be its
\alpha
-th Fourier component as in (
3.34
),
and let
S=e_{-\alpha}\cdot T_{\alpha}
. The distributional Leibniz rule

\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}(fU)=\left(\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}f\right)\cdot U+f\cdot\left(\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}U\right),\quad f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)\text{ and }U\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega),

(3.39)

shows that
S\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
, since
T_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
by Part (1) of Proposition
3.6
.
By Part (1) of Proposition
2.4
, the Fourier component
T_{\alpha}
lies in the
\alpha
-th Fourier mode of

\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
, i.e.,

\sigma_{\lambda}(T_{\alpha})=\lambda^{\alpha}\cdot T_{\alpha}
, so

\sigma_{\lambda}(S)=\lambda^{-\alpha}e_{-\alpha}\cdot\lambda^{\alpha}T_{\alpha}=S.

Therefore, using polar coordinates
z_{j}=r_{j}e^{i\theta_{j}}
, with
\epsilon_{j}(h)=(1,\dots,1,e^{ih},\dots,1)\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
(with
e^{ih}
in the
j
-th position) we have

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}S=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\left(\sigma_{\epsilon_{j}(h)}(S)-S\right)=0.

Since
\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}S=0
, and in polar coordinates we have

\bm{\overline{D}}_{j}=\frac{e^{i\theta_{j}}}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}+\frac{i}{r_{j}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}\right),

(3.40)

we see that

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}S=0

also. Now from the representations (
3.22
)
we have that

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}S=0}
and
\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}S=0}
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
for
1\leq j\leq n
.
Therefore, by a classical result in the theory of distributions (see
[
Sch66
, Theorème VI, pp. 69ff.]
) we have that
S
is locally constant on
\Omega
, or more precisely,
S
is generated by a locally constant function. Since
\Omega
is connected, it follows that there
is a constant
a_{\alpha}(T)\in{\mathbb{C}}
which generates the distribution
S
, so that
T_{\alpha}=a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}.

This can also be written as
a_{\alpha}(T)=e_{-\alpha}\cdot T_{\alpha}=e_{-\alpha}\cdot\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(T)
. Since multiplication by the fixed
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-function

e_{-\alpha}
is a
continuous map on
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
(and therefore on the subspace
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
), and
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is continuous by part 1 of Proposition
2.4
, it follows that
a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is continuous. But we know that
a_{\alpha}
takes values in the subspace of distributions
generated by constants. By a well-known theorem, on a finite-dimensional topological vector space, there is only one
topology. Therefore the topology induced from
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
on the subspace of constants coincides with the natural topology of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore

a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is continuous.

By the above, the Fourier components
T_{\alpha}
of the Fourier series of
T
actually
lie in
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
, and thus any partial sum (i.e. sum of finitely many terms) of this series also lies in
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
.
We will now show that the series (
3.34
) is absolutely convergent in the topology of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
in the sense of Proposition
2.2
.
Let
K\subset\Omega
be compact. Let
\delta<\mathrm{dist}(K,{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus\Omega)
, and
let
\Psi\in\mathscr{D}({\mathbb{C}}^{n})
be a test function such that

\mathrm{support}(\Psi)\subset B(0,\delta),\quad\int_{\mathbb{C}}\Psi(\zeta)dV(\zeta)=1,\quad\text{ and }\Psi(\zeta)=\Psi(\left|\zeta_{1}\right|,\dots,\left|\zeta_{n}\right|)\text{ for $\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}$},

i.e.
\Psi
is radial around the origin in each complex coordinate.
For
z\in K
, define
\psi_{z}(\zeta)=\Psi(\zeta-z),

so that
\psi_{z}
is radially symmetric around
z
in each complex direction. Therefore, for
z\in K
, we have, by Lemma
3.3
for each
\alpha
that

\left\langle T_{\alpha},\psi_{z}\right\rangle=\left\langle a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha},\psi_{z}\right\rangle=a_{\alpha}(T)z^{\alpha}.

By the continuity of the mapping
T:\mathscr{D}({\Omega})\to{\mathbb{C}}
, there is a constant
C>0
and an integer
k\geq 0
such that for all
\psi\in\mathscr{D}({\Omega})
with support in
K
, we have that

\left|\left\langle T,\psi\right\rangle\right|\leq C\cdot\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}({\Omega})}.

(3.41)

Also notice that for any
\psi\in\mathscr{D}({\Omega})
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(T),\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\sigma_{\lambda}(T)d\lambda,\psi\right\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\left\langle\sigma_{\lambda}(T),\psi\right\rangle d\lambda\qquad\text{using \eqref{eq-t}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\left\langle T,\tau_{\lambda^{-1}}\psi\right\rangle d\lambda=\left\langle T,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{-\alpha}\tau_{\lambda^{-1}}\psi d\lambda\right\rangle\qquad\text{using \eqref{eq-dual} and \eqref{eq-t}}

\displaystyle=\left\langle T,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\lambda^{\alpha}\tau_{\lambda}\psi d\lambda\right\rangle

using the invariance of Haar measure of a compact group under inversion

\displaystyle=\left\langle T,\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{-\alpha}\psi\right\rangle.

(3.42)

In the following estimates, let
C
denote a constant that depends only on the compact
K
and the distribution
T
, and may have different values at different occurrences. By combining (
3.41
) with (
3.42
), we see that for
z\in K,\phi\in\mathscr{D}({\Omega})
and each
\alpha
with
\left|\alpha\right|\geq 2k
, using Lemma
3.3

\displaystyle\left|a_{\alpha}(T)z^{\alpha}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle T_{\alpha},\psi_{z}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}T,\psi_{z}\right\rangle\right|=\left|\left\langle T,\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{-\alpha}\psi_{z}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\left\|\bm{\pi}^{\tau}_{-\alpha}\psi_{z}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{k}({\Omega})}\quad\text{using \eqref{eq-distn1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}}\cdot\left\|\psi_{z}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+2n}}\quad\text{by Proposition~\ref{prop-est} with $m=2$,}

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}}\cdot\left\|\psi_{z}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+2n}},

recalling that the local order
k
depends only on the distribution
T
and the compact set
K
. Now,
for each
z
we have
\left\|\psi_{z}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+2n}}=\left\|\Psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+2n}}
by translation invariance of the
norm. Therefore, for each
\alpha\geq 2k
we obtain the estimate for the seminorm (with the same convention as above on the constant
C
)

p_{K}(T_{\alpha})=p_{K}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}T)=\sup_{z\in K}\left|a_{\alpha}(T)z^{\alpha}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}}\cdot\left\|\Psi\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2nk+2n}}=\frac{C}{\left|\alpha\right|^{2}}.

(3.43)

Clearly therefore

\sum\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}p_{K}(T_{\alpha})<\infty
. By Lemma
3.7
, the series
\sum_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}
converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Let
f
be its sum.
Since the inclusion
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is
continuous, we see easily that the Fourier series
\sum_{\alpha}T_{\alpha}
of
T\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)

converges absolutely in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
. Now it follows from Corollary
2.5
that the sum of the series is
T
. Thus
T
is the distribution generated by
f
, and this completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

3.9.
Smoothness of holomorphic distributions

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be an open set. Then each holomorphic distribution on
\Omega
is generated by
a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-smooth function, i.e.
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)=(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
. It suffices to show that
T
is a smooth function in a neighborhood of each point
p\in\Omega
. Without loss of generality,
thanks to the invariance of the space of holomorphic functions under translations (Proposition
3.2
and comments after it) , we can assume that

p=0
. Let
r>0
be such that the polydisc
P(r)
given by

P(r)=\{\left|z_{j}\right|<r,1\leq j\leq n\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

is contained in
\Omega
. We will show that
T
is a smooth function on
P^{\prime}=P(\frac{r}{6})
, i.e.,
T\in(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(P^{\prime})
.

By the previous section, holomorphic distributions on an annular Reinhardt domain are smooth. By translation invariance, the
same is true if the annular domain is centered at a point
a\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
different from the origin, i.e. for a domain of the form

\mathrm{M}_{a}(A)

where
A
is an annular Reinhardt domain centered at the origin, with
\mathrm{M}_{a}
as in (
3.15
) is the translation by
a
.

Indeed, let
a=\left(\frac{r}{3},\dots,\frac{r}{3}\right)
, and set

Q=\mathrm{M}_{a}\left(P(\frac{r}{2})\setminus Z\right)
, so that
Q
is the annular Reinhardt domain centered at
a
given by

Q=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}:0<\left|z_{j}-\frac{r}{3}\right|<\frac{r}{2},1\leq j\leq n\right\},

for which it is easy to verify that
P^{\prime}\subset Q\subset P(r)
. Now since
T|_{Q}\in(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(Q)
is a holomorphic function, the result follows.
∎

3.10.
Extension to the log-convex hull

In this section, we prove the following special case of
Theorem
3.1
for annular Reinhardt domains:

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\Omega
be an annular Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then the Laurent series of a distribution
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)

converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\widehat{\Omega})
where
\widehat{\Omega}
is the smallest log-convex annular Reinhardt domain containing
\Omega
.

Introduce the following notation:
for a compact subset
K\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z=({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
, we let

\widehat{K}=\left\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z:\left|e_{\alpha}(z)\right|\leq\sup_{K}\left|e_{\alpha}\right|,\text{for all $\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}$}\right\}.

(3.44)

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\Omega
be an annular Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let
\widehat{\Omega}
be the log-convex annular Reinhardt domain containing
\Omega
. Then for each point
p\in\widehat{\Omega}

there is a compact neighborhood
M
of
p
in
\widehat{\Omega}
and a compact subset
K\subset\Omega
such that
M\subset\widehat{K}
.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus Z\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be the map

\Lambda(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=(\log\left|z_{1}\right|,\dots,\log\left|z_{n}\right|).

(3.45)

From (
3.8
), it follows that an annular Reinhardt domain
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is log-convex if and
only if
the subset
\Lambda(V)
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(called the
logarithmic shadow
of
V
) is convex.
The set
\widehat{\Omega}
is characterized by the fact that it is the
convex hull of
\Lambda(\Omega)
.
Therefore, if
p\in\widehat{\Omega}
, there exist
m
points
q^{1},\dots,q^{m}\in V\setminus Z
and positive numbers

t_{1},\dots,t_{m}
, with
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}=1
such that for
1\leq j\leq n
, we have
\Lambda(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}\Lambda(q^{k})
, i.e.,

\log\left|p_{j}\right|=\sum\limits_{k-1}^{m}t_{k}\log\left|q_{j}^{k}\right|.

In other words
\Lambda(p)
lies in the convex hull of the
m
-element set
\{\Lambda(q^{1}),\dots,\Lambda(q^{m})\}
.
(By a theorem of Carathéodory,
m\leq n+1
, but we do not need this fact.)
We will show that we can “fatten” one the points in this set so that the convex hull of the fattened set contains a neighborhood of the point
\Lambda(p)
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
t_{m}\not=0
. Consider the affine self-map
F
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
given by

F(x)=t_{m}x+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}t_{k}\Lambda(q_{j}^{k}),

which is clearly an affine automorphism of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Therefore if
L
is a compact neighborhood of
\Lambda(p)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then
F^{-1}(L)
is a compact neighborhood of
\Lambda(q^{m})
, which after shrinking can be taken to be contained in
\Lambda(\Omega).

Let
K_{0}=\Lambda^{-1}(F^{-1}(L))
, and set

K=\{q^{1},\dots,q^{m-1}\}\cup K_{0}.

We claim that
\widehat{K}
contains the compact neighborhood
\Lambda^{-1}(L)
of the point
p
.
Let
\zeta\in\Lambda^{-1}(L)
. Then there is
a point
z\in K_{0}
such that
\Lambda(\zeta)=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}t_{k}\Lambda(q^{k})+t_{m}\Lambda(z)
, i.e. for each
1\leq j\leq n
we have

\log\left|\zeta_{j}\right|=\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}t_{k}\log\left|q^{k}_{j}\right|+t_{m}\log\left|z_{j}\right|.

For simplicity of writing, introduce new notation as follows: we let
z^{k}=q^{k}
for
1\leq k\leq m-1
and
z^{m}=z
.
Then for a
multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and the point
\zeta\in\Lambda^{-1}(L)
we have

\displaystyle\left|e_{\alpha}(\zeta)\right|

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left|\zeta_{j}\right|^{\alpha_{j}}=\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\log\left|\zeta_{j}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}\log\left|z^{k}_{j}\right|\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}t_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\log\left|z^{k}_{j}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\log\left|z^{k}_{j}\right|\right)

(3.46)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\log\left|z^{k}_{j}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\left|e_{\alpha}(z^{k})\right|=\max\left\{\max_{1\leq k\leq m-1}\left|e_{\alpha}(q^{k})\right|,\left|e_{\alpha}(z)\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{K}\left|e_{\alpha}\right|,

(3.47)

this shows that
\zeta\in\widehat{K}
, and completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.10
.

By Lemma
3.7

it is sufficient to show that the Laurent series
\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}
(whose existence was
proved in Proposition
3.8
, and whose terms lie in
(\mathscr{O}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty})(\Omega)
) converges absolutely
in
\mathcal{C}(\widehat{\Omega})
. It is sufficient to show that each point
p
of
\widehat{\Omega}
has a compact
neighborhood
M
such that we have

\sum_{\alpha}p_{M}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha})<\infty.

Now by Lemma
3.11
, there is a compact subset
K\subset\Omega
such that
M\subset\widehat{K}
. We observe, using the definition (
3.44
) that

\displaystyle p_{M}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq p_{\widehat{K}}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha})=\sup_{\widehat{K}}\left|a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}\right|=\left|a_{\alpha}(T)\right|\cdot\sup_{\widehat{K}}\left|e_{\alpha}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|a_{\alpha}(T)\right|\cdot\sup_{{K}}\left|e_{\alpha}\right|=\sup_{{K}}\left|a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}\right|=p_{K}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}),

so that

\sum_{\alpha}p_{M}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha})\leq\sum_{\alpha}p_{K}(a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha})<\infty,

using the estimate (
3.43
) which holds since
K\subset\Omega
.
∎

3.11.
Laurent series on non-annular Reinhardt domains

We now consider the case of a general (i.e. possibly non-annular) Reinhardt domain
\Omega
. We begin by showing that the only monomials that occur in the
Laurent series are the ones smooth on
\Omega:

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\Omega
be a (possibly non-annular) Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
(where
\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
is as in (
3.11
)), there is a continuous linear functional
a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
such that the Fourier series of a
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is of the form

T\sim\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)}a_{\alpha}(T)e_{\alpha}.

(3.48)

Proof.

Since
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
we have
T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega\setminus Z)
. Notice that
\Omega\setminus Z
is
an annular Reinhardt domain and therefore the proof of Proposition
3.8
shows that the Fourier components
are given for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by

\left(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}\right)_{\alpha}=\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z})e_{\alpha},

where
\widehat{a}_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega\setminus Z)\to{\mathbb{C}}
is the
\alpha
-th coefficient functional associated to the domain
\Omega\setminus Z
(see Proposition
3.8
). Thanks to (
3.35
), we know that
\left(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}\right)_{\alpha}=(T_{\alpha})|_{\Omega\setminus Z}
, so we have

(T_{\alpha})|_{\Omega\setminus Z}=\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z})e_{\alpha}.

By Proposition
3.6
, the Fourier components of a holomorphic distribution are holomorphic distributions, so
we have
T_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
since
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
. Therefore, since by Corollary
3.9
, each holomorphic
distribution is a smooth function, we know that
T_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Therefore,
the function
T_{\alpha}|_{\Omega\setminus Z}=\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z})e_{\alpha}
admits a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
extension through
Z
. If
\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z})\not=0
for some
T\in\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
,
this means that
e_{\alpha}
itself admits a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
extension to
\Omega
, i.e.,
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
, where

\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
is as in (
3.11
).
Therefore if
\alpha\not\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
, the corresponding term in the Laurent series of
T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}
vanishes,
and the series takes the form

T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)}\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z})e_{\alpha}.

Now for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(\Omega)
define the map
a_{\alpha}:\mathscr{O}(\Omega)\to{\mathbb{C}}
by
a_{\alpha}(T)=\widehat{a}_{\alpha}(T|_{\Omega\setminus Z}).
Since both the restriction map and the coefficient functional
\widehat{a}_{\alpha}
are continuous, it follows that
a_{\alpha}
is
continuous. The extension of
e_{\alpha}
from
\Omega\setminus Z
to
\Omega
is still the monomial
e_{\alpha}
,
so the Fourier series of
T
in
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
is of the form (
3.48
).
∎

Notice that each term of (
3.48
)
is in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and by Proposition
3.8
,
the series converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus Z)

when its terms are restricted to
\Omega\setminus Z
, i.e., it converges uniformly along with all derivatives on those compact sets in
\Omega
which do not intersect

Z
.

3.12.
Extension to the relative completion

Given a Reinhardt domain
D\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, its
relative completion
is the smallest
relatively complete domain containing
D
(see above before the statement of Theorem
3.1
for the definition of relative completeness of a domain.) Notice that the relative completion of
D
coincides with the unions of the sets
D^{(j)}
of (
3.10
), where the union is taken over those
j
for which
D\cap\{z_{j}=0\}
is nonempty. The following
general proposition, which encompasses classical examples of
the Hartogs phenomenon, e.g. in the “Hartogs figure”, will be needed
to complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
D
be a Reinhardt domain. Then each holomorphic function on
D
extends holomorphically to the relative completion of
D
.

Proof.

We may assume that
n\geq 2
since each Reinhardt
domain in the plane is automatically relatively complete.
If for each
j
, the intersection
D\cap\{z_{j}=0\}=\emptyset
, then the domain

D
is annular, and its relative completion is itself so there
is nothing to prove.
Suppose therefore that there is
1\leq j\leq n
such that
D\cap\{z_{j}=0\}\not=\emptyset
. We need to prove that each function in

\mathscr{O}(D)
extends holomorphically to
D^{(j)}
.

Without loss of generality
we can assume that
j=1
. Write the coordinates of a point
z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
as
z=(z_{1},\widetilde{z})
where
\widetilde{z}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n-1}
. Let
f\in\mathscr{O}(D)
. By Proposition
3.12

f
admits a Laurent series
representation

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(D)}a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha}

with
S(D)
as in (
3.11
), and the series converges
absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D\setminus Z)
. To prove the proposition
it suffices to show that the series in fact converges absolutely
in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D^{(1)})
, which by Lemma
3.7
is equivalent to the following: for each point

p\in D^{(1)}
there is a compact neighborhood
M
of
p
in
D^{(1)}
such that

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(D)}p_{M}(a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha})<\infty.

(3.49)

We claim the following: for each
p\in D^{(1)}
there is
a compact neighborhood
M
of
p
and a compact subset
K\subset D
such that
p_{M}(e_{\alpha})\leq p_{K}(e_{\alpha})
for
each
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(D)
. Assuming the claim for a moment, we see that we have

p_{M}(a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha})=\left|a_{\alpha}(f)\right|p_{M}(e_{\alpha})\leq\left|a_{\alpha}(f)\right|p_{K}(e_{\alpha})=p_{K}(a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha}),

so that (
3.49
) follows since by (
3.43
) we do have
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(D)}p_{K}(a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha})<\infty

for a compact subset
K
of
D
. Since each point
p\in D^{(1)}
has such a neighborhood this completes the proof, modulo the claim above.

To establish the claim, we may assume that
p\not\in D
, since
otherwise there is nothing to prove. Therefore
p\in D^{(1)}\setminus D
, and consequently, there is a
z\in D
and
a
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
such that
p=(\lambda z_{1},\widetilde{z})
, where
\widetilde{z}=(z_{2},\dots,z_{n})
. Let
K
be a compact neighborhood
of
z
in
D
of the form
K=K_{1}\times\widetilde{K}
, where
\widetilde{K}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n-1}
and
K_{1}=\{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}:\left|\zeta-z_{1}\right|\leq\epsilon\}
is a closed disk. Let
L_{1}
be the disk
\{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}:\left|\zeta\right|\leq\left|z_{1}\right|+\epsilon\}
,
so that
K_{1}\subset L_{1}
, and

\gamma={z_{1}}+\frac{z_{1}}{\left|z_{1}\right|}\epsilon

is a point of maximum modulus (i.e. maximum distance from the origin) in both sets
K_{1}
and
L_{1}
. Note that

\left|\gamma\right|=\left|\frac{z_{1}}{\left|z_{1}\right|}\cdot\left|z_{1}\right|+\frac{z_{1}}{\left|z_{1}\right|}\epsilon\right|=\left|z_{1}\right|+\epsilon.

We set
M=L_{1}\times\widetilde{K}
. We will show that these sets
K,M

satisfy the conditions of the claim.

Now let
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(D)
. Since
D\cap\{z_{1}=0\}\not=\emptyset
, it follows that
\alpha_{1}\geq 0
.
Let
\widetilde{\alpha}=(\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
, and
set

B=\sup_{\widetilde{w}\in\widetilde{K}}\left|\widetilde{w}^{\widetilde{\alpha}}\right|\quad\text{where $\widetilde{w}=(w_{2},\dots,w_{n})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n-1}$ and $\left|\widetilde{w}^{\widetilde{\alpha}}\right|=\left|w_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\right|\cdots\left|w_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\right|$},

so that we have

p_{M}(e_{\alpha})=\sup_{w\in M}\left|e_{\alpha}(w)\right|=\sup_{w_{1}\in L_{1}}\left|w_{1}\right|^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot B=(\left|z_{1}\right|+\epsilon)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot B.

On the other hand

p_{K}(e_{\alpha})=\sup_{w\in K}\left|e_{\alpha}(w)\right|=\sup_{w_{1}\in K_{1}}\left|w_{1}\right|^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot B=(\left|z_{1}\right|+\epsilon)^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot B.

Consequently, in fact we have
p_{M}(e_{\alpha})=p_{K}(e_{\alpha})
, and the claim is proved, thus completing the proof.
∎

3.13.
End of proof of Theorem
3.1

It only remains to put together the various pieces to note that all parts of Theorem
3.1
have been established. If
\Omega
is annular, i.e.
\Omega
has empty intersection with the set
Z
of (
3.7
), then Proposition
3.8
takes care of the
complete proof. When
\Omega
is allowed to be non-annular, we see from Proposition
3.12
that
the Laurent series representation has only monomials which are smooth functions on
\Omega
. Now it is not difficult to see that
the smallest log-convex relatively complete Reinhardt domain
\widehat{\Omega}

containing
\Omega
can be constructed from
\Omega
in two steps.
First, we construct the log-convex hull
\Omega_{1}
of the set
\Omega\setminus Z
. Notice that
\Omega\setminus Z
and
\Omega_{1}
are both annular.
The second step consists of constructing the relative completion of the domain
\Omega_{1}
, thus obtaining the domain
\widehat{\Omega}
.
Now by Proposition
3.10
, the Laurent series of a holomorphic distribution on
\Omega
converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})
. Applying Proposition
3.13
(with
D=\Omega_{1}
), we see that the
Laurent series actually converges absolutely in the space
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\widehat{\Omega})
. The sum of this series is the required
holomorphic extension of a given holomorphic distribution on
\Omega
. The result has been completely established.

4.
Missing monomials

In Theorem
3.1
, we considered the natural representation of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on the space
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
of holomorphic functions on a Reinhardt domain
\Omega
. In applications,
one often deals with a subspace of functions
Y\subset\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
such that

(1)

the subspace
Y
is invariant under the natural representation
\sigma
, i.e., if
f\in Y
then
f\circ\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}\in Y
for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
, where
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
is as in (
3.6
),

(2)

there is a locally convex topology on
Y
in which it is complete,
and such that the inclusion map

j:Y\hookrightarrow\mathscr{O}(\Omega)

is continuous,
and

(3)

when
Y
is given this topology, the representation
\sigma
restricts to a continuous representation on
Y
.

The
locus classicus
here is the theory of Hardy spaces on the disc. We can make the following elementary observation:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
Y
and
\sigma
be as above, and set

\mathcal{S}(Y)=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:e_{\alpha}\in Y\}.

Then the Laurent series of a function
f\in Y
is of the form

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}(Y)}a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, if the monomial
e_{\alpha}
does not belong to
Y
, we have that
a_{\alpha}(f)=0
, where
a_{\alpha}(f)
is the Laurent coefficient of
f
as in (
3.12
).
By part (1) of Proposition
2.4
, we see that
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(f)\in Y
. By part (2) of the same proposition, taking
X
to be the space
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
,
for
f\in Y
we have that
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(j(f))=j(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(f))
, and from the description of the Fourier components of a holomorphic function in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we see that

\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(j(f))=a_{\alpha}(j(f))e_{\alpha}=a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha}
. Therefore
j(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(f))=a_{\alpha}(f)e_{\alpha}
, which contradicts the fact that
\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{\alpha}(f)\in Y
unless
a_{\alpha}(f)=0
.
∎

This simple observation can be called the “principle of missing monomials”, since it says that certain monomials cannot occur in the Laurent series of the function
f
. It can be thought to be the reason behind several phenomena associated to holomorphic functions. We consider two examples:

(1)

Bergman spaces in Reinhardt domains:

Let
\Omega
be a Reinhardt domain in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let
\lambda>0
be a radial weight on
\Omega
, i.e., for
z\in\Omega
we have

\lambda(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=\lambda(\left|z_{1}\right|,\dots,\left|z_{n}\right|).

The

L^{p}
-Bergman space

A^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
is defined to be the subspace of the weighted
L^{p}
-space
L^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
consisting of holomorphic functions, where
the norm on the weighted
L^{p}
-space is given by

\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)}^{p}=\int_{\Omega}\left|f\right|^{p}\lambda dV,

where
dV
is the Lebesgue measure. It is well-known that
A^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
is a closed subspace of the Banach space
L^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
and therefore a Banach space (
[
DS04
]
). It is also easy to see (using standard facts about
L^{p}
-spaces) that the natural representation
\sigma
of
\mathbb{T}^{n}
on
L^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
is continuous for

1\leq p<\infty
, so it follows that the representation on
A^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
is also continuous. It now follows from Proposition
4.1
that the Laurent series expansion of a function
f\in A^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
consists only of terms with monomials

e_{\alpha}
such that
e_{\alpha}\in L^{p}(\Omega,\lambda)
. The case
\lambda\equiv 1
of this fact was deduced by a different argument in
[
CEM18
]
.

(2)

Extension of holomorphic functions smooth up to the boundary:
Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a Reinhardt domain such that the origin (which is the center of symmetry) is
on the boundary of
\Omega
. A classic example of this is the
Hartogs triangle

\{\left|z_{1}\right|<\left|z_{2}\right|<1\}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. In
[
Cha19
]
, the following extension theorem was proved: there is
a complete Reinhardt domain
V\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
\Omega\subset V
and each function in the space
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
of holomorphic functions on

\Omega
smooth up to the boundary extends holomorphically to the domain
V
. This was noted for the Hartogs triangle in
[
Sib75
]
, where
V
is the unit bidisk.

To deduce this from the principle of missing monomials, it suffices to consider the case when
\Omega
is bounded.
We notice that
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is a Fréchet space in its usual topology of
uniform convergence on
\overline{\Omega}
with all partial derivatives. A generating family of seminorms for this topology is
given by the norms
\{p_{k}\}
where
p_{k}(f)=\left\|f\right\|_{C^{k}(\overline{\Omega})}
. The natural representation
\sigma
on
\mathscr{O}(\Omega)
restricts to a continuous representation
on
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. Therefore, the principle of missing monomials applies and the only monomials
e_{\alpha}
that occur in the Laurent
expansion of a function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
are such that
e_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. For such a multi-index
\alpha
, write the multiindex
\alpha=\beta-\gamma
, where
\beta_{j}=\max(\alpha_{j},0)
and
\gamma_{j}=(-\alpha_{j},0)
. Then
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and we can apply the differential operator
\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{\beta}
to obtain

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{\beta}e_{\alpha}(z)=\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)^{\beta}\frac{e_{\beta}(z)}{e_{\alpha}(z)}=\frac{\beta!}{z^{\gamma}}.

Since
e_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
this means that
e_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, which is possible only if
\gamma=0
. Thus
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and the Laurent series of each function
f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is a Taylor series which converges in some complete (log-convex) Reinhardt domain
V
, and this
V
must strictly contain
\Omega
, since
\Omega
is not complete.

5.
Classical characterizations of holomorphic functions

In this section we show how one can avoid the machinery of generalized functions and weak
derivatives altogether, and still use Fourier methods to prove the basic facts of function theory. We confine ourselves to one complex variable and the simple geometry of the disk.

5.1.
Goursat’s characterization

The starting point of a traditional account of holomorphic functions of a single variable is
typically Goursat’s definition (
[
Gou00
]
): a function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}
on an
open subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
is holomorphic, if it is
complex differentiable
, i.e., for each point
w\in\Omega
,
the limit

{\lim_{z\to w}\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{z-w}}

(5.1)

exists. The result that a holomorphic function in this sense is infinitely many times complex differentiable and even admits a convergent power series representation
near each point is rightly celebrated as one of the most elegant and surprising in all of mathematics. Unfortunately, we cannot use it as a definition,
if we want to apply the theory of abstract Fourier expansions as developed in Section
2
. Denoting by
G(\Omega)
the collection of holomorphic functions in the sense
of Goursat in an open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, we notice that the space
G(\Omega)
does not have
a nice a priori linear locally convex topology in which it is complete and such that when
\Omega
is a disc or an annulus, the natural action of the group
\mathbb{T}
on the space
G(\Omega)
is a continuous representation. Though Goursat’s definition
carries the weight of a century of academic tradition, we will start from an alternative definition which lends itself better to the application of the methods of Section
2
.
We also note that the characterization of holomorphic functions by complex-differentiability cannot be used for natural generalizations of complex analysis, e.g. quaternionic analysis, analysis on Clifford algebras etc. (see
[
GM91
, pp. 87–93]
).

5.2.
Morera’s definition

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of the complex plane
{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}

be a continuous function. In honor of
[
Mor02
]
, let us say that the function

f
is
holomorphic in the sense of Morera
(
Morera-holomorphic
for
short) if for each triangle

T
contained (with its interior) in
\Omega
, we have the vanishing of the complex
line integral of
f
around the boundary of
T
:

\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz=0,

(5.2)

where
\partial T
denotes the boundary of
T
, oriented counterclockwise.
For an open set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, let us denote by
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
the collection of Morera-holomorphic functions on
\Omega
. It is known by Morera’s theorem that a Morera-holomorphic function is Goursat-holomorphic, and one can develop function theory
starting from Morera’s definition (see
[
Hef55
,
MW67
]
). Notice that the
a priori regularity of Morera-holomorphic functions (assumed to be only continuous)
is even less than that assumed for Goursat-holomorphic functions (assumed also to admit the
limit (
5.1
) at each
w
.)

It is immediate from the definition that
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
is a closed linear subspace
of the Fréchet space
\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
of continuous functions. “Closed” means that
the limit of a sequence of Morera-holomorphic functions converging uniformly on
compact subsets of
\Omega
is itself Morera-holomorphic, a fact that was already noted
in
[
Mor86
]
. The proof of this crucial fact starting from the Goursat definition
must pass through a lengthy development of integral representations,
so this is definitely a pedagogical advantage of Morera’s definition over Goursat’s.

The notion of Morera-holomorphicity is local: i.e.,
f\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
if and only if
there is an open cover
\{\Omega_{j}\}_{j\in J}
of
\Omega
such that
f|_{\Omega_{j}}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega_{j})
for each
j
. One half of this claim is trivial, and for the other half,
for a triangle
T
in
\Omega
, we can
perform repeated barycentric subdivisions till the triangles so formed are each contained in some element of the open cover
\{\Omega_{j}\}_{j\in J}
. We therefore conclude that

\mathscr{O}_{M}
is a
sheaf
of Fréchet spaces on
{\mathbb{C}}
.

The following local description of Morera-holomorphic functions is well-known, and a proof can be found in e.g.
[
Rem91
, pp. 186-189]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be convex. Then the following statements about a continuous function
f\in\mathcal{C}(\Omega)
are equivalent:

(A)

f\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
.

(B)

f
has a holomorphic primitive, i.e., there is an
F
which is complex-differentiable on
\Omega
and
F^{\prime}=f
.

(C)

for each piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{1}
closed path
\gamma
in
\Omega

we have

\int_{\gamma}f(z)dz=0.

(5.3)

Recall that assuming (A), the primitive
F
in (B)
is constructed by fixing
z_{0}\in\Omega
, and setting

\displaystyle{F(z)=\int_{[z_{0},z]}f(\zeta)d\zeta}

where
[z_{0},z]
denotes the line segment from
z_{0}
to
z
.
Proposition
5.1
allows us to give examples of Morera-holomorphic functions. Recall from (
3.9
) that for an integer
n
, we use the notation
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}

for the holomorphic monomials.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
n\geq 0
then

e_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}({\mathbb{C}})
and if
n<0
then
e_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}({\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\})
.

Proof.

First note that
e_{n}
is continuous, on all of
{\mathbb{C}}
if
n\geq 0
and on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
if

n<0
.
If
g_{n}(z)=\frac{z^{n}}{n+1}
for
n\not=-1
, we can verify from
the definition (
5.1
) that
g_{n}
is complex-differentiable and
g_{n}^{\prime}=e_{n}
so that by part (B) of
Proposition
5.1
the result follows for
n\not=-1
. For
n=-1
, we can construct for each
p\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
, a local primitive of
e_{-1}
near
p
by setting

g_{-1}(z)=\ln\left|z\right|+i\arg z,

where
\arg
denotes a branch of the argument defined near the point
p
. A direct computation shows that
g_{-1}^{\prime}=e_{-1}

near
p
, so that again we see that
e_{-1}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}({\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}).
∎

5.3.
Products of Morera-holomorphic functions

In the proof of Theorem
3.1
, an important role is played by the fact that if
U
is a holomorphic
distribution (in the sense of (
3.5
))and
f
is a holomorphic function (i.e. a holomorphic distribution which is
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
, Section
3.3
above), then the product distribution
fU
is also a holomorphic distribution. This is an immediate
consequence of the distributional Lebniz formula (
3.39
). A similar result,
proved in
[
MW67
]
,
will be needed in order to develop the properties of holomorphic functions
starting from Morera’s definition.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
f,g\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
, and assume that
g
is locally Lipschitz at each point, i.e. for each
w\in\Omega
and
each compact
K\subset\Omega
such that
w\in K
, there is an

M>0
such that for
z\in K
we have

\left|g(z)-g(w)\right|\leq M\left|z-w\right|.

(5.4)

Then the product
fg
also belongs to
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\Omega)
.

The proof is based on a version of the classical Goursat lemma (
[
Gou00
,
Pri01
]
).
This is of course the
main ingredient in the standard textbook proof of the Cauchy theorem for triangles for
Goursat-holomorphic functions. Recall that two triangles are
similar
if they have
the same angles.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
and let
\lambda
be a complex valued function
defined on the set of triangles contained in
\Omega
such that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\lambda
is additive in the following sense: if a triangle
\Delta
is represented as
a union of smaller triangles
\Delta=\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k}
with pairwise disjoint interiors
then

\lambda(\Delta)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda(\Delta_{k}).

(5.5)

(2)

For each
w\in\Omega
and each triangle
\Delta_{0}
, we have

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Delta\downarrow w\\
\Delta\sim\Delta_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\lambda(\Delta)}{\left|\Delta\right|}=0,

(5.6)

where
\left|\Delta\right|
denotes the area of the triangle
\Delta
, and the limit is taken along the
family of triangles similar to
\Delta_{0}
and containing the point
w
, as these
triangles shrink to the point
w
.

Then
\lambda\equiv 0
.

Proof.

For completeness, we recall the classic argument. Let
T
be a triangle
contained in
\Omega
. We construct a sequence of triangles
\{T_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
with
T_{0}=T
using the following recursive procedure. Assuming that
T_{k}
has been constructed, we divide
T_{k}
into four similar triangles with half the diameter of

T_{k}
by three line segments each parallel to a side of
T_{k}
and passing through the midpoints of the other two sides. Denote the four triangles so obtained by
\Delta_{j},1\leq j\leq 4
. Then, by (
5.5
), we have

\lambda(T_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\lambda(\Delta_{j}).

Choose
T_{k+1}
to be one of
\Delta_{j},1\leq j\leq 4
such that the value of
\left|\lambda(T_{k+1})\right|
is
the largest. Then, by the triangle inequality we have

\left|\lambda(T_{k})\right|\leq 4\left|\lambda(T_{k+1})\right|,
and by induction it follows that

\left|\lambda(T)\right|\leq 4^{k}\left|\lambda(T_{k})\right|=4^{k}\left|T_{k}\right|\cdot\frac{\left|\lambda(T_{k})\right|}{\left|T_{k}\right|}=\left|T\right|\cdot\frac{\left|\lambda(T_{k})\right|}{\left|T_{k}\right|},

(5.7)

where in the last step we have used the fact that
T_{k+1}
has one-fourth the area of
T_{k}
, so
\left|T_{k}\right|=4^{-k}\left|T_{0}\right|=4^{-k}\left|T\right|
. Since the diameters of the
T_{k}

go to zero, by compactness, there is a unique point
w
in the intersection
\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}
. Since the family
\{T_{k}\}
is a subfamily of all the triangles containing
w
, and each
T_{k}
is similar to
T_{0}
, therefore by letting
k\to\infty
in (
5.7
) and using (
5.6
)
the result follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

For a triangle
\Delta\subset\Omega
, define

\lambda(\Delta)=\int_{\partial\Delta}f(z)g(z)dz.

To prove the result, we need to show that
\lambda\equiv 0
. Since condition (
5.5
) of Lemma
5.4
is obvious, we need to show (
5.6
) to complete the proof. Let
w\in\Omega
, let
K
be a compact neighborhood of
w
in
\Omega
, and denote by
M
the Lipschitz constant corresponding
to this
w
and this
K
in (
5.4
). Now,
let
\Delta_{0}
be a triangle
and let
\Delta
be a triangle similar to
\Delta_{0}
such that
w\in\Delta\subset K
. Then
observe that, by the hypothesis of Morera-holomorphicity of
f
and
g
we have

\lambda(\Delta)=\int_{\partial\Delta}\left(f(z)-f(w)\right)\left(g(z)-g(w)\right)dz.

Therefore, denoting the perimeter of the triangle
\Delta
by
\left|\partial\Delta\right|
,

\displaystyle\left|\lambda(\Delta)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left|\left(f(z)-f(w)\right)\left(g(z)-g(w)\right)\right|\left|\partial\Delta\right|

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot\left|\partial\Delta\right|\cdot\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left(\left|f(z)-f(w)\right|\left|z-w\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq M\cdot\left|\partial\Delta\right|\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\Delta)\cdot\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left|f(z)-f(w)\right|

\displaystyle=M\cdot\left|\Delta\right|\cdot\left(\frac{\left|\partial\Delta\right|\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\Delta)}{\left|\Delta\right|}\right)\cdot\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left|f(z)-f(w)\right|

\displaystyle=M\cdot\left|\Delta\right|\cdot\left(\frac{\left|\partial\Delta_{0}\right|\cdot\mathrm{diam}(\Delta_{0})}{\left|\Delta_{0}\right|}\right)\cdot\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left|f(z)-f(w)\right|,

where in the last step we use the fact that
\Delta
and
\Delta_{0}
are similar, so the quantity in parentheses (which is clearly invariant under dilations) is the same. So we have

\frac{\left|\lambda(\Delta)\right|}{\left|\Delta\right|}\leq C\cdot\sup_{z\in\partial\Delta}\left|f(z)-f(w)\right|,

for a constant
C
independent of the triangle
\Delta
as long as
\Delta
is similar to
\Delta_{0}
and
w\in\Delta\subset K
. Letting
\Delta
shrink to
w
, we have (
5.6
) and the proof is complete.
∎

5.4.
Fourier expansion of a Morera-holomorphic function

We will now prove the following analog of Theorem
3.1
. In particular, it shows that holomorphic functions in the sense of Morera are identical to the holomorphic distributions considered in Section
3
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
where
\mathbb{D}=\{\left|z\right|<1\}
is the open unit disc. Then there is a sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

of complex numbers, such that

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}e_{n},

(5.8)

where
e_{n}
is as in (
3.9
), and the series on the right
converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
to the function
f
.

Let
\sigma
be the natural representation of
\mathbb{T}
on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
given by

\sigma_{\lambda}(f)(z)=f(\lambda z),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{T},z\in\mathbb{D}.

(5.9)

Proposition 5.5
.

The space
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
is invariant under
\sigma
and the resulting representation of

\mathbb{T}
on
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
is continuous.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
, let
T
be a triangle in
\mathbb{D}
. Notice that

\lambda^{-1}T=\{\lambda^{-1}z:z\in T\}

is itself a triangle, and we have

\int_{\partial T}(\sigma_{\lambda}f)(z)dz=\int_{\partial T}f(\lambda z)dz=\lambda^{-1}\int_{\partial(\lambda^{-1}T)}f(w)dw=0.

It follows that
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
is invariant under
\sigma
.

It suffices to show that the representation
\sigma
is continuous on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
.
For
0<r<1
, let
p_{r}
be the seminorm on
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
given by

p_{r}(f)=\sup_{\left|z\right|\leq r}\left|f(z)\right|.

(5.10)

It is clear that
p_{r}(\sigma_{\lambda}(f))=p_{r}(f)
for each
0<r<1,\lambda\in\mathbb{T}
and

f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
. Also
f_{j}\to f
in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
if and only if for each
r
, we have
p_{r}(f-f_{j})\to 0
, so the family
\{p_{r}\}
is a
\sigma
-invariant family of seminorms that
generates the topology of
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
. Further, given
f\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
, by uniform continuity,
for each
0<r<1
, we have

p_{r}(\sigma_{\lambda}(f)-f)=\sup_{\left|z\right|\leq r}\left|f(\lambda z)-f(z)\right|\to 0\quad\text{ as }\lambda\to 1,

so that
\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\sigma_{\lambda}(f)=f
in the space
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
. Therefore
both conditions of Proposition
2.3
are satisfied, and the representation

\sigma
is continuous.
∎

In view of the above, the machinery of Section
2
applies. We now compute the
Fourier components (
2.5
).

Proposition 5.6
.

For
f\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
, and with
\sigma
the natural representation (
5.9
), the Fourier components of
f
are of the form:

\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}(f)=\begin{cases}a_{n}e_{n}&\text{ if }n\geq 0\\
0&\text{ if }n<0,\end{cases}

where
a_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
e_{n}(z)=z^{n}
.

The proof will use the following lemma:

Lemma 5.7
.

A radial function in
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\})
is constant.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\})
be radial, and
define the complex valued continuous function

u
on the interval
(0,1)
by restriction,
u(r)=f(r)
, so that we have

f(z)=u(\left|z\right|)
by the radiality of
f
. To prove the theorem, it suffices to show that
u
is a constant .

Fix
0\leq\alpha<\beta<\pi
and
0<\rho<1
. For
R
in the interval
(\rho,1)
consider the
curvilinear quadrilateral defined by

S(R)=\{re^{i\theta}:\rho\leq r\leq R,\alpha\leq\theta\leq\beta\},

(5.11)

and notice that
S(R)
lies in the upper half disc, which is convex.
The region
S(R)
is bounded by the two circular arcs

AB=\{\rho e^{i\theta}:\alpha\leq\theta\leq\beta\},\quad CD=\{Re^{i\theta}:\alpha\leq\theta\leq\beta\},

along with the two radial line segments

AD=\{re^{i\alpha}:\rho\leq r\leq R\},\quad BC=\{re^{i\beta}:\rho\leq r\leq R\}.

A
D
C
B
S(R)

Orient the boundary
\partial S(R)
counterclockwise. We can write

\int_{\partial S(R)}f(z)dz=\int_{AD}+\int_{DC}+\int_{CB}+\int_{BA}=0,

where the vanishing of the integral follows from part (c) of Proposition
5.1
above.
Parametrizing
AD
by
z=re^{i\alpha}
where
\rho\leq r\leq R
, and using
dz=e^{i\alpha}dr

we get

\int_{AD}f(z)dz=\int_{\rho}^{R}f(re^{i\alpha})e^{i\alpha}dr=e^{i\alpha}\int_{\rho}^{R}u(r)dr.

Similarly,

\int_{CB}f(z)dz=-e^{i\beta}\int_{\rho}^{R}u(r)dr.

Now, parametrizing

DC
by
z=Re^{i\theta},\alpha\leq\theta\leq\beta
, we have
dz=Rie^{i\theta}d\theta
, so

\int_{DC}f(z)dz=\int_{\theta=\alpha}^{\beta}f(Re^{i\theta})\cdot Rie^{i\theta}d\theta=Ru({R})\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}ie^{i\theta}d\theta=Ru(R)(e^{i\beta}-e^{i\alpha}).

Similarly,

\int_{BA}f(z)dz=\rho u(\rho)(e^{i\beta}-e^{i\alpha}).

Therefore, adding the four integrals we have

\int_{\partial S}f(z)dz=\left({\rho}u({\rho})-Ru(R)+\int_{\rho}^{R}u(r)dr\right)(e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\beta})=0,

so that we have the relation

\int_{\rho}^{R}u(r)dr=Ru(R)-{\rho}u({\rho}),

(5.12)

which can be written as

u(R)=\frac{1}{R}\left(\int_{\rho}^{R}u(r)dr+{\rho}u({\rho})\right).

Since
u
is continuous, the right hand side is a function of
R
which is continuously
differentiable on
(\rho,1)
. Thus
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\rho,1)
.
Differentiating both sides of (
5.12
) with respect to
R

we have
u(R)=Ru^{\prime}(R)+u(R)
so that
we have
u^{\prime}(R)=0
for
R\in(\rho,1)
. But
0<\rho<1
can be chosen arbitrarily, so it follows that
u^{\prime}\equiv 0

on
(0,1)
and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
5.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and for simplicity of notation, let
f_{n}=\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}(f)
.
Then by Proposition
2.4
, we see that
f_{n}
lies in the Fourier mode

[\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})]_{n}^{\sigma}
so that
f_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
and for
\lambda\in T,z\in\mathbb{D}
we have

f_{n}(\lambda z)=\lambda^{n}f_{n}(z)
. Since clearly
f_{n}|_{\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}}\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\})
, it follows by Proposition
5.3
that the function
h_{n}
on

\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
defined by

h_{n}=e_{-n}\cdot f_{n}

lies in
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\})
. Further for
\lambda\in T,z\in\mathbb{D}
we have

h_{n}(\lambda z)=e_{n}(\lambda z)f_{n}(\lambda z)=\lambda^{-n}e_{-n}(z)\cdot\lambda^{n}f_{n}(z)=h_{n}(z),

so
h_{n}
is radial, and hence by Lemma
5.7
,
h_{n}
is a constant, which we call
a_{n}
. Therefore, on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
f_{n}=a_{n}e_{n}
, so the product
a_{n}e_{n}
extends for each
n
to a continuous function on
\mathbb{D}
. If
n<0
, this is possible only if
a_{n}=0
, and this completes the proof.
∎

5.5.
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
5.1

We will first show that the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}e_{n}
converges absolutely in the
space
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
. It suffices to show that for each
0<r<1
, we have

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{r}(a_{n}e_{n})<\infty,

where
p_{r}
is as in (
5.10
). Fix an
r
with
0<r<1
and let
\rho
be such that

r<\rho<1
. Applying the Cauchy estimate (
2.9
) to the seminorm
p_{\rho}
we see that for each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
:

p_{\rho}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}f)\leq p_{\rho}(f)

Thanks to Proposition
5.6
we have for each
n\geq 0
,

p_{r}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}f)=\sup_{\left|z\right|\leq r}\left|a_{n}z^{n}\right|=\left|a_{n}\right|r^{n}\leq\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{n}\cdot\left|a_{n}\right|\rho^{n}\leq\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{n}\cdot p_{\rho}(f).

Therefore we have

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{p_{r}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}f)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{p_{r}(\bm{\pi}^{\sigma}_{n}f)}\leq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{n}\right)\cdot p_{\rho}(f)=\frac{1}{1-\frac{r}{\rho}}\cdot p_{\rho}(f)<\infty.

This proves that the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}e_{n}
converges absolutely in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{D})
. Let
g
be its sum. Since the partial sums are all in
\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
by Proposition
5.2
, we see that
g\in\mathscr{O}_{M}(\mathbb{D})
. However, it follows from Corollary
2.5
that the sum of the series is
f
, therefore
f=g
, and the proof of the theorem is complete.

5.6.
Pompeiu’s characterization of holomorphic functions

Goursat’s definition is non-quantitative, since it is framed in terms of the

existence
of the limit (
5.1
), and does not provide a way to measure the degree of non-holomorphicity of a function. For example, if
\epsilon
is small and
nonzero,
all it says about the functions
f(z)=z+\epsilon\overline{z}
and
g(z)=\overline{z}+\epsilon{z}
is that both are non-holomorphic. Morera’s definition of a holomorphic function does not have this shortcoming, as the integral
\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz
gives a measure of
the amount of non-holomorphicity of
f
on the triangle
T
.

It is possible to normalize and localize this measure of non-holomorphicity,
as was realized by Pompeiu (see
[
Pom12
,
MŞ98
]
). The quantity

\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz

(5.13)

is a numerical measure of the “average density of non-holomorphicity” of a continuous function
f
on a triangle (or other region with piecewise smooth boundary)
T
, where

\left|T\right|
denotes the area of
T
. To localize this, we can consider,
for a point
w\in\Omega
, and a continuous function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}
, the following limit
(called the “areolar derivative” by Pompieu) as
a measure of the degree of non-holomorphicity of
f
at the point
w
:

\lim_{T\downarrow w}\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz

(5.14)

where the limit is taken over the family of triangles containing the point
w
and contained in
\Omega
, as these
triangles shrink to the point
w
. Let us say that a function is
Pompeiu-holomorphic
if the limit (
5.14
)
exists at each
w\in\Omega
, and is equal to zero. The following simple and well-known observations
clarify the meaning of this notion:

Proposition 5.8
.

(a)

A continuous function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{C}}
is Pompieu-holomorphic if and only if it is Morera-holomorphic.

(b)

If the function
f
is in
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\Omega)
, then the limit (
5.14
) exists,
and is equal to
\displaystyle{2i\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{z}}(w)}
.

Proof.

For part (a), if
f
is Morera-holomorphic, then the quantity (
5.13
) vanishes for each triangle
T
, so
the limit (
5.14
) vanishes. Conversely, suppose that the limit (
5.14
) vanishes at each point
w\in\Omega
, and let
T
be a triangle
in
\Omega
. In Lemma
5.4
, if we take

\lambda(\Delta)=\int_{\partial\Delta}f(z)dz
, then the additivity condition (
5.5
) is clear, and the limit condition (
5.6
) holds by hypothesis.
The result follows.

For part (b), using Stoke’s theorem

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{\partial T}f(z)dx+if(z)dy=\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{T}\left(i\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(z)-\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(z)\right)dx\wedge dy

\displaystyle=2i\int_{T}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{z}}dx\wedge dy.

Since the last integral has a continuous integrand, we may take the limit as the triangle
T
shrinks to the point
w
to obtain:

\lim_{T\downarrow w}\frac{1}{\left|T\right|}\int_{\partial T}f(z)dz=2i\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{z}}(w).

∎
