Digital Beamforming Robust to Time-Varying Carrier Frequency Offset
Shuang Li, Payam Nayeri, Michael B. Wakin

Abstract
Adaptive interference cancellation is rapidly becoming a necessity for our modern wireless communication systems, due to the proliferation of wireless devices that interfere with each other. To cancel interference, digital beamforming algorithms adaptively adjust the weight vector of the antenna array, and in turn its radiation pattern, to minimize interference while maximizing the desired signal power. While these algorithms are effective in ideal scenarios, they are sensitive to signal corruptions. In this work, we consider the case when the transmitter and receiver in a communication system cannot be synchronized, resulting in a carrier frequency offset that corrupts the signal. We present novel beamforming algorithms that are robust to signal corruptions arising from this time-variant carrier frequency offset. In particular, we bring in the Discrete Prolate Spheroidal Sequences (DPSS’s) and propose two atomic-norm-minimization (ANM)-based methods in both 1D and 2D frameworks to design a weight vector that can be used to cancel interference when there exist unknown time-varying frequency drift in the pilot and interferer signals. Both algorithms do not assume a pilot signal is known. Noting that solving ANM optimization problems via semi-definite programs can be a computational burden, we also present a novel fast algorithm to approximately solve our 1D ANM optimization problem. Finally, we confirm the benefits of our proposed algorithms and show the advantages over existing approaches with a series of experiments.

In this paper, we consider the DBF problem in the following context. Our goal is to design a weight vector (see Section
III
) that allows us to localize and separate a desired signal (“the signal” for short) from unwanted interferers impinging on an array. Our treatment can be characterized as:

•

Deterministic: We do not assume a stochastic model for the signal, interferers, or noise, nor do we require long-term observations for estimating auto- or cross-correlation statistics.

•

Narrowband: We assume that the signal and interferers have narrowband spectra relative to the sampling frequency. Sinusoids, which are a natural choice for pilot signals (discussed below) are one possibility for such narrowband signals, but sinusoidal structure is not explicitly required.

•

Blind: Our algorithms do not require a known pilot signal. We present two DBF algorithms in this paper. Our 1D algorithm does not require the array antennas to be equally spaced, nor does it require the antenna positions to be known. Our 2D algorithm does require the antenna positions to be known and equally spaced, but it can handle the case where the signal and interferers have overlapping temporal frequencies.

Our approach to solving this DBF problem is inspired by several existing approaches, which we describe next before giving further details on our approach.

The minimum variance distortionless response (MVDR) beamformer is a prototypical non-blind statistical beamformer

Modeling the signal and noise as stochastic processes, and given knowledge of the correlation statistics (spectral matrices) for these processes in addition to knowledge of the signal direction, MVDR is designed to output the highest possible SINR. As such it is considered the benchmark for comparing the performance of beamforming algorithms

,

DBF algorithms such as SMI can effectively cancel interferers as long as the knowledge of the incoming pilot signal is sufficiently accurate. However, in non-ideal scenarios, signals can become corrupted and cause these algorithms fail. To address these concerns, DBF algorithms need to be robust to causes that corrupt the signal. Again, the literature on robust DBF methods is vast. Several algorithms have been developed to address beamformer mismatches in particular in the form of steering vector (SV) errors. SV errors arise from direction-of-arrival mismatch, or perturbed arrays with errors in element location, phase, or gain, and cause signal corruptions that degrades the system performance. The most widely adopted solution to these problems is diagonal loading and equivalent approaches

,
16

,

,

Diagonal loading provides robustness to SV errors by effectively designing for a higher white noise level than is actually present, and as such is usually practical when signal-to-interference ratio (SIR) is more important than SINR. To mitigate some of these issues, variable loading has been introduced; it can improve robustness to SV errors while maintaining a desired SIR or SINR

19

A different category of beamformer mismatch, which is the focus of this work, occurs when the transmitter and receiver cannot be synchronized and thus a carrier frequency offset exists between the anticipated pilot signal and that which arrives at the array. In
[
15

, it was shown that when it is not possible to measure and correct the carrier frequency offset, algorithms such as SMI will fail. A solution to this problem using genetic algorithms was demonstrated in

The ANM approach is naturally capable of extracting the offset frequencies that cause signal corruption, and as such, robustness is achieved with maximum SINR and without any degradation to other system performance characteristics. Both of these algorithms provided a robust solution to beamforming when transmitter and receiver are unsynchronized, which is a realistic non-ideal scenario occurring more frequently in our ever growing modern wireless infrastructure. This robust beamforming problem is categorically different than what has been studied in the past, and to the best of our knowledge, no solution to this problem is available outside

,
20
,
21

While the works
[
15
,
20
,
21
]
provided a robust solution to the carrier frequency offset problem in unsynchronized systems, they only considered the time-invariant case. In reality, however, frequency offset is a dynamic problem, and for practical implementation a robust time-variant solution is needed. In this work, we accommodate an unknown time-varying frequency offset in the pilot signal. Indeed, we drop entirely the requirement that the pilot signal be known, and we assume only that the pilot signal is narrowband. As we show (see Section
II-C
), this assumption accommodates (but does not require) the common choice of a sinusoidal pilot signal, and it allows for a moderate amount of unknown time-varying drift in the carrier frequency of this sinusoid. In this way, our treatment is more general (blind) and robust compared to classical SMI.

Our approach for solving the blind beamforming problem relies on an optimization framework using ANM. ANM allows for a signal or data matrix to be decomposed into sparse components arising from a certain
atomic set
of prototype signals or matrices. In compressive sensing, finite atomic sets of on-grid sinusoids have been used for recovering signals from partial information
[
22
,
23
]
. In line spectrum estimation, continuous atomic sets of off-grid sinusoids have been used for precisely identifying the active frequencies in a finite-length sampled signal without suffering from the “leakage” effects that confront conventional Fourier-based methods
[
24
]
. In array processing, such atomic sets have been used to provide joint sparse frequency decompositions of multi-channel data matrices
[
25
,
26
]
. Unfortunately, the atomic sets in these works are not rich enough to represent the signals encountered when time-varying frequency drift is present.

In this paper, we expand the array processing ANM frameworks using a novel atomic set for data matrices. Our work expands on the joint sparse frequency model
[
25
,
26
]
by modulating each sinusoid by an unknown vector in a low-dimensional basis of Discrete Prolate Spheroidal Sequences (DPSS’s)
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
. DPSS’s have been shown to provide excellent compressed representations of sampled narrowband signals. We therefore bring DPSS’s into the ANM framework to capture the unknown time-varying frequency drift component in the pilot and interferer signals.

We present two novel ANM frameworks in this paper. Our first “1D” framework does not require the antennas to be equally spaced, and moreover it does not require the antenna positions to be known. Our second “2D” framework does require the antenna positions to be known and equally spaced, but it can handle the case where the pilot signal and interferers have overlapping temporal frequencies. The 2D framework exploits sparse structure in both the temporal and angular frequency domains, whereas the 1D framework exploits sparse sinusoidal structure only in the temporal frequency domain. Both frameworks can accommodate time-varying frequency drift component in the pilot and interferer signals.

Finally, we note that most ANM optimization problems can be formulated as a semi-definite program (SDP), solving this SDP can be a computational burden. In this paper, we also present a novel fast algorithm for approximately solving our 1D ANM optimization problem. We provide extensive experimental tests to confirm the benefits of our 1D and 2D ANM frameworks compared to conventional SMI, and we illustrate the computational speedup provided by our fast 1D algorithm. We leave the work of developing a fast algorithm for the 2D ANM method as our future work.

Preliminaries

II-A

Sample matrix inversion (SMI)

Consider an
N
-element linear antenna array. The optimal MVDR beamformer computes the complex valued weighting vector
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
for the array elements such that the interference signals are minimized subject to a constraint
[
4
]
. The algorithm minimizes
\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{w}^{H}\mathbf{S}_{x}\bm{w}\right\}
subject to
\bm{w}^{H}\textbf{asv}=1
, where the constraint ensures that a plane wave with a known array steering vector,
asv
, will not be distorted. Here,
\mathbb{E}
is the expected value, and
\mathbf{S}_{x}
is the signal spectral matrix. In practice
\mathbf{S}_{x}
is not available but the maximum likelihood of the matrix can be estimated from incoming data as

\mathbf{S}_{x}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}\bm{x}_{m}^{H}
,
where
\{\bm{x}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
denote the signal snapshots and
M
is the number of snapshots.
The array steering vector is
\textbf{asv}=e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta_{s})\bm{q}}
, where
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
denotes the element positions,
\theta_{s}
is the signal direction, and
k_{0}
is the plane wave wavenumber. In SMI, where a pilot signal is available, the signal steering vector,
\bm{v}_{s}
, is used in place of the array steering vector, and its maximum likelihood is estimated by the cross-correlation between the signal,
\bm{x}
, and the known pilot signal,
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
, as

\bm{v}_{s}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{x}_{m}s_{m}^{H},

where
s_{m}
is the
m
-th entry of
\bm{s}
.
The SMI element weights are then given by
\bm{w}^{H}\triangleq\frac{\bm{v}_{s}^{H}\mathbf{S}_{x}^{-1}}{\bm{v}_{s}^{H}\mathbf{S}_{x}^{-1}\bm{v}_{s}}
. In this equation, the presence of the
\mathbf{S}_{x}^{-1}
term gives rise to the name sample matrix inversion.
By rearranging the signal snapshots
\{\bm{x}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
as columns of a data matrix
\mathbf{X}^{H}
, it follows that the SMI element weights can be rewritten as

\bm{w}=(\mathbf{X}^{H}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{H}\bm{s}=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\bm{s},

(1)

where we ignored the normalization factor
\bm{v}_{s}^{H}\mathbf{S}_{x}^{-1}\bm{v}_{s}
. Though we arrived at the solution (
1
) using statistical arguments, we note that
\bm{w}=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\bm{s}
can also be interpreted as the least-squares solution to the purely deterministic problem of finding the weight vector that best solves
\mathbf{X}\bm{w}=\bm{s}
, i.e., best localizes the known pilot signal
\bm{s}

[
11
]
.

Atomic norm minimization (ANM)

The atomic norm is a generalization of the

Thus, it has been widely studied in many signal processing applications, including line spectral estimation and array signal processing, for localizing the off-grid temporal or angular frequencies of sinusoidal components

To be more precise, consider the following spectrally sparse signal

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\bm{a}(f_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{M},

\displaystyle\bm{a}(f)\triangleq[1\penalty\ e^{j2\pi f}\penalty\ \cdots\penalty\ e^{j2\pi f(M-1)}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}

One can then define

The corresponding induced

Discrete prolate spheroidal sequences (DPSS’s)

For integers

L\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}

M

subject to being orthogonal to the first column, etc.

DPSS’s are particularly valuable for providing efficient representations of sampled sinusoids and narrowband signals. For example, as indicated in the following theorem, any complex exponential signal
\bm{a}(f)
defined in (
3
) with

can be well represented in the column span of the DPSS basis

when

,

.

Theorem II.1
.

[
29
]

Fix

and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
L=2MW(1+\epsilon)
. Then there exist constants

such that

\begin{split}||\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{S}_{M,W}}\bm{a}(f)||_{2}\leq C_{1}M^{5/4}e^{-C_{2}M},\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ f\in[-W,W]\end{split}

holds for all

. Here,

are numerical constants that may depend on
W
and
\epsilon
.

In summary, this result states that (
i
) the effective dimensionality of the subspace spanned by
\{\bm{a}(f)\}_{f\in[-W,W]}
is

, and (
ii
) the first
L\approx 2MW
DPSS vectors provide a basis for this subspace. Sinusoids concentrated in a narrow band away from baseband, e.g.,

, can be equally well-captured by taking

to be the half bandwidth and modulating each of the DPSS vectors by
\bm{a}(f^{o})
where
f^{0}=\frac{a+b}{2}
is the center frequency. Finally, the high quality of DPSS approximations applies not only to sampled sinusoids, but also to sampled narrowband signals (since these can be viewed as linear combinations of sampled sinusoids within a narrow range of frequencies). As discussed more formally in

, the finite-length sample vectors arising from sampling random baseband (or bandpass) analog signals can be well approximated using a DPSS (or modulated DPSS) basis. Again the effective dimensionality is

III
Problem Formulation

In this work, we consider a conventional linear array with
N
antenna elements. We collect
M
snapshots on each antenna element and model the data matrix as

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{\star}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{s}_{k}\textbf{asv}(\theta_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N},

(4)

where
\bm{s}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
denotes the desired signal and
\{\bm{s}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}\}_{k=2}^{K}
denote the interferers. Here,

\displaystyle\textbf{asv}(\theta_{k})\triangleq e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta_{k})\bm{q}}

is a length-
N
row vector representing the array steering vector associated with the desired signal (if
k=1
) or interferers (if
k>1
).
\theta_{1}
and
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k=2}^{K}
denote the angle to the desired signal and interferers, respectively,
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
denotes the element positions, and
k_{0}
is the plane wave wavenumber.

We do not assume the desired signal, the interferers, or any of their directions are known. Moreover, our 1D algorithm does not require the array antennas to be equally spaced, nor does it require the element positions
\bm{q}
to be known.

Given the data matrix
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
, our goal is to construct a weight vector
\bm{w}
such that
\mathbf{X}^{\star}\bm{w}\approx\bm{s}_{1}
as closely as possible. Inspired by SMI (which assumes
\bm{s}_{1}
is known), our approach involves first estimating
\bm{s}_{1}
from
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
and then plugging this estimate (which we call
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{1}
) into the SMI expression:

\displaystyle\bm{w}={\mathbf{X}^{\star}}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{1}.

(5)

To facilitate the estimation of
\bm{s}_{1}
from
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
, we assume that the desired signal and all of the interferers are narrowband signals whose DTFT’s are each concentrated in a small subset of the digital bandwidth
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. More specifically, employing the DPSS’s discussed in Section
II-C
, we assume that each
\bm{s}_{k}
can be modeled as follows:

\bm{s}_{k}\approx\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\odot(\mathbf{S}_{M,W}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k})\penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ k=1,2,\dots,K,

(6)

where
f_{k}^{o}
is a possibly unknown carrier frequency,
\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})
is a sampled complex exponential with frequency
f_{k}^{o}
as defined in (
3
),
\mathbf{S}_{M,W}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times L}
is an
L
-dimensional DPSS basis (we discuss the choice of
W
and
L\approx 2MW
), and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
denote the corresponding unknown coefficients. This narrowband assumption is quite flexible and accommodates the following cases:

•

Case 1:

\bm{s}_{k}
is a pure sinusoid (complex exponential) with a fixed frequency. In this case, (
6
) holds with equality by taking
f_{k}^{o}
to be the frequency of the sinusoid and letting
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}=0
.

•

Case 2:

\bm{s}_{k}
is a sinusoid (such as a pilot signal when
k=1
), modulated by a frequency offset that drifts slightly over time. In this case,
\bm{s}_{k}
can be viewed as a frequency modulated (FM) signal. Carson’s bandwidth rule for FM signals
[
35
]
guarantees that a majority of the signal power will concentrate in a narrow band around the signal’s carrier frequency, with the half bandwidth
W
proportional to the peak frequency deviation plus the temporal bandwidth of the modulating signal. Therefore,
\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})
will again capture the dominant sinusoidal component, and following the discussion in Section
II-C
,
\mathbf{S}_{M,W}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{k}
will approximately capture the deviations from this frequency.

•

Case 3:

\bm{s}_{k}
is a bandpass signal, oversampled with respect to its Nyquist rate. In this case, the DTFT of
\bm{s}_{k}
will concentrate in some subset
[a,b]
of the digital bandwidth
[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
. Following the discussion in Section
II-C
, the approximation in (
6
) then holds by taking
f_{k}^{o}=\frac{a+b}{2}
to be the midpoint of this band and
W
to be greater than or equal to its half width
\frac{b-a}{2}
.

•

Case 4:

\bm{s}_{k}
is a bandpass signal, oversampled with respect to its Nyquist rate, modulated by a frequency offset that drifts slightly over time. The approximation in (
6
) then holds by combining the arguments from Case 2 and Case 3: because multiplication in time corresponds to convolution in frequency, the product of two narrowband signals will remain (relatively) narrowband.

The reader will notice that we do not focus on quantifying the necessary degree of accuracy in the approximation (
6
) in this work. Nevertheless, this assumption is quite broad. We use numerical experiments in Section
VII
to confirm that this approximation is sufficiently valid to enable the success of our proposed technique in a range of interesting cases.

Finally, we note that the above idea of using (
5
) to design a weight vector has already been studied in
[
20
,
21
]
under the assumption that each
\bm{s}_{k}
is a pure sinusoid modulated by a fixed (not time-varying) frequency offset. Those works propose two ANM-based methods to estimate the frequency offset from either
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
or its noisy measurements. However, as is shown in the simulations, the weight vector obtained by directly applying this idea cannot cancel the interferers when there exist
time-varying
frequency offsets. To address this problem, we extend the ANM formulation using the novel assumption in (
6
).

Proposed Method: “ANM+DPSS+SMI”

We now describe our procedure for constructing the estimate

Inspired by a recent work on multi-band signal recovery

Remark IV.1
.

(Computational complexity.)
Note that most SDP solvers, including SDPT3
[
38
]
, use the interior-point method and need to solve a system of linear equations when computing the Newton direction, which can be very expensive for large-size problems. Here, the
N
positive semi-definite (PSD) constraints in (
10
) can be combined into one PSD constraint on a big block diagonal matrix of size
N(M+L)\times N(M+L)
. Therefore, the overall computational complexity of solving (
10
) with SDP solvers is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3.5}(M+L)^{3.5})
, which limits the use of the proposed “ANM+DPSS+SMI” method in large-scale problems.

In Remark
IV.1
, one may notice that the combined PSD constraint is on a block diagonal matrix, rather than a dense matrix. Thus, a natural question arises:

Is it possible that the computational complexity of solving (
10
) is less than
\mathcal{O}(N^{3.5}(M+L)^{3.5})
due to the sparsity that exists in the PSD constraint?

To the best of our knowledge, there is no closed-form expression for the complexity. However, we are able to empirically answer this question by conducting the following experiment. In particular, we collect the running time needed for solving the following SDP

\min\penalty\ x\penalty\ \penalty\ \operatorname*{s.t.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbf{A}_{x}\succeq\mathbf{0},

(14)

where
\mathbf{A}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{s}\times N_{s}}
is a matrix with entries from the set
\{0,x,\sqrt{N_{s}}\}
. First, we test the case when
\mathbf{A}_{x}
is a dense matrix, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{x}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}x&\sqrt{N_{s}}&\cdots&\sqrt{N_{s}}\\
\sqrt{N_{s}}&x&\cdots&\sqrt{N_{s}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sqrt{N_{s}}&\sqrt{N_{s}}&\cdots&x\end{array}\right].

The time needed for solving (
14
) with
\mathbf{A}_{x}
given in (
IV
) for a variety of
N_{s}
is illustrated in Figure
1
(the blue line with circles). Second, we test several cases when
\mathbf{A}_{x}
is a block diagonal matrix, specifically with
N_{b}
of blocks on the diagonal where
N_{b}=10,\penalty\ 20,\penalty\ 50
and
100
. We generate each block on the diagonal as an
\frac{N_{s}}{N_{b}}\times\frac{N_{s}}{N_{b}}
matrix with diagonal entries being

and off-diagonal entries being
\sqrt{N_{s}}
. The running times are presented as red lines in Figure

We conclude that the answer to the above question is “no”, namely, that sparsity in the PSD constraint matrix will not significantly reduce the computational complexity of solving an SDP problem. To reduce the computational complexity, we introduce a fast version of the “ANM+DPSS+SMI” method in next section.

Proposed Method: “IVDST+DPSS+SMI”

Recall that a key step in the proposed “ANM+DPSS+SMI” method is to compute the dual solution. Though CVX can return the dual solution by solving the primal SDP (

) to get the dual solution.
With some elementary calculations, we obtain the dual problem of (

2

To simplify the derivation, we replace the inequality constraint

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L\times N}\\
\mathcal{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M\times N}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{X}^{\star},\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

(19)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{s.t.}\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{H}_{::n}&-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}\\

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M-j}\mathcal{H}_{m(m+j)n}=0,\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ j\in[M-1],\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n\in[N],

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{H}_{mmn}=1.

Note that the above dual problem (

) is also an SDP. Therefore, it can be solved by any off-the-shelf SDP solver just as the primal SDP (

). However, as is discussed in Remark

, solving SDP problems directly with SDP solvers can result in high computational complexity.
To reduce the computational complexity, the authors in

propose a fast method based on Alternating

]
to solve the SDP. In

, the authors reformulate the SDP as a conic program with much fewer dual variables to further reduce the computational cost. A fast iterative Vandermonde decomposition and shrinkage-thresholding (IVDST) algorithm based on the accelerated proximal gradient

Inspired by the IVDST algorithm, we propose a faster method, named “IVDST+DPSS+SMI”, to speed up the “ANM+DPSS+SMI” method introduced in Section

. Rather than solving the primal SDP (
10
), we focus on its dual problem (

) in this section. Note that the dual problem (
19
) can be rewritten as the following unconstrained problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H}}\penalty\ h(\mathcal{Q})+g(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})

with

\displaystyle h(\mathcal{Q})

\displaystyle\triangleq-\langle\mathbf{X}^{\star},\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle g(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\triangleq g_{1}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})+g_{2}(\mathcal{H})+g_{3}(\mathcal{H}),

\displaystyle g_{1}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\triangleq\lambda_{1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{Z}(\mathcal{Q}_{::n},\mathcal{H}_{::n})),

\displaystyle g_{2}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\triangleq\lambda_{2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{H}_{mmn}-1\right)^{2},

\displaystyle g_{3}(\mathcal{H})

\displaystyle\triangleq\lambda_{3}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M-1}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M-j}\mathcal{H}_{m(m+j)n}\right)^{2},

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
are regularization parameters.
\mathbf{Z}(\mathcal{Q}_{::n},\mathcal{H}_{::n})
is a matrix defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}(\mathcal{Q}_{::n},\mathcal{H}_{::n})\triangleq\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{H}_{::n}&-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}\\
-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}&\mathbf{I}_{L}\end{array}\right].

\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{Z})
is an indicator function used to enforce the PSD constraint and is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{Z})\triangleq\begin{cases}0,\quad&\text{if}\penalty\ \mathbf{Z}\penalty\ \text{is PSD,}\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Next, we introduce how to use the idea of APG to estimate the dual solution
\mathcal{Q}^{\star}
, which is then used to design a weight vector
\bm{w}
.

(1)
Initialization:

According to the Schur complement condition,
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{H}_{::n}&-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}\\
-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}&\mathbf{I}_{L}\end{array}\right]\succeq\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{I}_{L}\succ\mathbf{0}
imply that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{::n}-\mathcal{Q}_{::n}\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}\succeq\mathbf{0}.

Therefore, we initialize
\mathcal{Q}_{1}=\mathcal{Q}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L\times N}
as a complex Gaussian random tensor, and initialize
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M\times N}
as

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}_{1}\}_{::n}=\{\mathcal{H}_{0}\}_{::n}=\{\mathcal{Q}_{0}\}_{::n}\{\mathcal{Q}_{0}\}_{::n}^{H},\quad\forall\penalty\ n\in[N].

(20)

(2)
Smoothing:

In each iteration, we add a momentum term to accelerate the convergence of the gradient vector. In particular, the updates in the
i
-th iteration are given as

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}=\mathcal{Q}_{i}+\frac{t_{i-1}-1}{t_{i}}(\mathcal{Q}_{i}-\mathcal{Q}_{i-1}),

(21)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{i}=\mathcal{H}_{i}+\frac{t_{i-1}-1}{t_{i}}(\mathcal{H}_{i}-\mathcal{H}_{i-1}),

where
t_{i}=\frac{1+\sqrt{4t_{i-1}^{2}+1}}{2}
with
t_{0}=1
.

(3)
Gradient descent:

After smoothing, we update the parameters
\mathcal{Q}
along the gradient descent direction of
h(\mathcal{Q})
with an appropriate small stepsize
\eta
.
\mathcal{H}
is fixed in this step since
h(\mathcal{Q})
only depends on
\mathcal{Q}
. Then, the updates in the
i
-th iteration are given as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{g}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}-\eta\nabla h(\mathcal{Q})=\overline{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}+\eta\mathcal{L}^{*}(\mathbf{X}^{\star}),

(22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{g}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{i},

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}:\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{M\times L\times N}
denotes the adjoint operator of the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
8
). In particular, we have

\displaystyle[\mathcal{L}^{*}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})]_{::n}=\mathbf{S}_{M,W}\odot(\bm{x}^{\star}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{L}^{H}),\quad\forall\penalty\ n\in[N],

(23)

where
\bm{x}^{\star}_{n}
denotes the
n
-th column of
\mathbf{X}^{\star}
.

(4)
Proximal mapping:

In this step, we use the following proximal operator

\displaystyle(\mathcal{Q}_{i+1},\mathcal{H}_{i+1})=\text{prox}_{\eta g}(\mathcal{Q}_{g},\mathcal{H}_{g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H}}\left\{g(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H})-(\mathcal{Q}_{g},\mathcal{H}_{g})\|_{F}^{2}\right\}.

Note that there is no analytical solution to the above optimization problem, therefore, we use alternating projection method to approximate its solution. In particular, for any
n\in[N]
, we first update
\mathcal{H}
with

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{mmn}

\displaystyle=\frac{\{\mathcal{H}_{g}\}_{mmn}}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\mathcal{H}_{g}\}_{mmn}},

(24)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{m(m+j)n}

\displaystyle=\{\mathcal{H}_{g}\}_{m(m+j)n}-\frac{1}{M-j}\sum_{m=1}^{M-j}\{\mathcal{H}_{g}\}_{m(m+j)n},

\displaystyle\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\displaystyle\forall\penalty\ j\in[M-1],

which ensures

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{mmn}=1,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{M-j}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{m(m+j)n}=0,\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ j\in[M-1],\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n\in[N],

as is required in the constraints of (
19
).

Next, to promote the PSD constraint, we evaluate

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{::n}&-\{\mathcal{Q}_{g}\}_{::n}\\
-\{\mathcal{Q}_{g}\}_{::n}^{H}&\mathbf{I}_{L}\end{array}\right],\quad\forall\penalty\ n\in[N]

and compute its eigen-decomposition

\displaystyle(\mathbf{V}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n})=\text{eig}(\mathbf{Z}_{n}),\quad\forall\penalty\ n\in[N].

To make
\{\mathbf{Z}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
PSD, we only keep the positive eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors. Then, we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{n}=\mathbf{V}_{n}\max(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{n},0)\mathbf{V}_{n}^{H},\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n\in[N].

(27)

With the obtained PSD matrices
\{\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, we update
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{Q}
with

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}_{i+1}\}_{::n}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{n}(1:M,1:M),

(28)

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{Q}_{i+1}\}_{::n}

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{\mathbf{Z}}_{n}(1:M,M+1:M+L),\quad\forall\penalty\ n\in[N].

(5)
Computing weight:

Denoting
I
as the number of iterations, then
\mathcal{Q}_{I}
is an estimation of the dual solution
\mathcal{Q}^{\star}
. Given
\mathcal{Q}_{I}
, we can then construct a dual polynomial as in (
11
) and use the k-means method to cluster the
f^{o}
with
q(f^{o})\geq\gamma_{0}
and get the estimated frequencies
\widetilde{f}_{k}^{o}
. As is shown in (
12
), to compute
\text{sign}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1})
, one needs to first estimate
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{::1}
, which is the primal solution to the SDP (
10
). However, since we focus on solving the dual SDP (
19
), we do not have the primal solution. Fortunately, we can use
[\mathcal{L}^{*}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})]_{::1}
as a surrogate to
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{::1}
. Then, we can compute
\text{sign}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1})
as

\displaystyle\text{sign}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1})=\frac{(\mathbf{A}_{f}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{L}^{*}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})]_{::1})^{\top}\bm{e}_{1}}{\|(\mathbf{A}_{f}^{\dagger}[\mathcal{L}^{*}(\mathbf{X}^{\star})]_{::1})^{\top}\bm{e}_{1}\|_{2}}

(29)

with
\mathbf{A}_{f}=[\bm{a}(\widetilde{f}_{1}^{o})\cdots\bm{a}(\widetilde{f}_{K}^{o})]
. Finally,
\widetilde{\bm{s}}_{1}
can be computed as in (
13
) and the weight vector
\bm{w}
can be computed using (
5
).

VI
Extension to 2D Case

In this section, we take the structure of
\textbf{asv}(\theta)
into consideration and define a
two dimensional (2D) atomic set
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2D}\triangleq\left\{\mathbf{A}(f^{o})\circledast(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\textbf{asv}(\theta)):\penalty\ f^{o}\in[0,1),\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{C}^{L},\right.

(30)

\displaystyle\left.\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}=1,\penalty\ \theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\right\}.

The induced
2D atomic norm
is then defined as

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}\triangleq\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}:\right.\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

(31)

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left.\mathcal{X}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\mathbf{A}(f_{k}^{o})\circledast(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}(\theta_{k})),c_{k}\geq 0\right\},

As in Section
IV
, one can solve the following 2D-ANM problem

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{X}}\penalty\ \|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}\quad\operatorname*{s.t.}\penalty\ \|\mathbf{X}^{\star}-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})\|_{F}\leq\varepsilon

(32)

to construct the weight vector.

Denote
\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{X}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}=\text{Re}\{\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{X}\rangle\}
as the real inner product between two tensors.
The
dual norm
of
\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}
is then defined as

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{Q}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}^{*}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}\leq 1}\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{X}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{o}\in[0,1),\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}=1\\
\theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\end{subarray}}\langle\mathcal{Q},\mathbf{A}(f^{o})\circledast(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\textbf{asv}(\theta))\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{o}\in[0,1),\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}=1\\
\theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\end{subarray}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\langle\mathcal{Q}_{::n},\bm{a}(f^{o})e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta)\bm{q}_{n}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{\top}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{o}\in[0,1),\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}=1\\
\theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\end{subarray}}\left\langle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{*},\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}\bm{a}(f^{o})e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta)\bm{q}_{n}}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{o}\in[0,1)\\
\theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\end{subarray}}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}\bm{a}(f^{o})e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta)\bm{q}_{n}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{o}\in[0,1)\\
\theta\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]\end{subarray}}q_{2D}(f^{o},\theta),

where

\displaystyle q_{2D}(f^{o},\theta)\triangleq\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{Q}_{::n}^{H}\bm{a}(f^{o})e^{jk_{0}\sin(\theta)\bm{q}_{n}}\right\|_{2}

(33)

is defined as the
dual polynomial
and
\bm{q}_{n}
denotes the
n
-th element position.

Next, we show that the above 2D atomic norm defined in (
31
) can be approximated by the optimal value of the following semidefinite program (SDP):

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2M-1)\times(2L-1)\times N}\\
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2MN}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\text{Tr}(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n}))+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}

(34)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{s.t.}\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n})&\bm{x}_{n}\\
\bm{x}_{n}^{H}&t_{n}\end{array}\right]\succeq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{n}=\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{::n}^{\top}),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n\in[N],

where
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{T})\in\mathbb{C}^{ML\times ML}
is a block Toeplitz matrix generated by
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2M-1)\times(2L-1)}
. To be more precise, denote
T_{m,l}
with
-M<m<M
and
-L<l<L
as the
(m,l)
-th entry of
\mathbf{T}
. Denote
\mathbf{T}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
as a Toeplitz matrix constructed from the
m
-th row of
\mathbf{T}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{m}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}T_{m,0}&T_{m,-1}&\cdots&T_{m,-(L-1)}\\
T_{m,1}&T_{m,0}&\cdots&T_{m,-(L-2)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
T_{m,L-1}&T_{m,L-2}&\cdots&T_{m,0}\\
\end{array}\right].

Then, the block Toeplitz matrix
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{T})
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{T})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{T}_{0}&\mathbf{T}_{-1}&\cdots&\mathbf{T}_{-(M-1)}\\
\mathbf{T}_{1}&\mathbf{T}_{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{T}_{-(M-2)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{T}_{M-1}&\mathbf{T}_{M-2}&\cdots&\mathbf{T}_{0}\\
\end{array}\right].

Denote SDP
(\mathcal{X})
as the objective value corresponding to the optimal solution of the above SDP (
34
). The following proposition shows that SDP
(\mathcal{X})
is a lower bound of
\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}
.

Proposition 1
.

With the 2D-atomic set and 2D-atomic norm defined in (
30
) and (
31
), we have

\displaystyle\text{SDP}(\mathcal{X})\leq\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}

for any
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L\times N}
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\mathbf{A}(f_{k}^{o})\circledast(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}(\theta_{k}))

with
f^{o}_{k}\in[0,1),\penalty\ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L},\penalty\ \|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\|_{2}=1,\penalty\ \theta_{k}\in[-90^{\circ},90^{\circ}]
. Then, for any
n\in[N]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{::n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}^{\top}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k}),

and

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{n}=\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{::n}^{\top})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k}),

where
\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})
denotes the
n
-th entry of
\textbf{asv}(\theta_{k})
and
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

For
t_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}
and a block Toeplitz matrix

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n})\!=\!\sum_{k=1}^{K}\!c_{k}\!\left[\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\!\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})\right]\left[\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})\right]^{H}\!\!,

we have

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n})&\bm{x}_{n}\\
\bm{x}_{n}^{H}&t_{n}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})\\
1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})\\
1\end{array}\right]^{H}

\displaystyle\succeq

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}.

Thus, the above
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n})
and
t_{n}
with any
n\in[N]
are feasible to the SDP (
34
). It follows that

\displaystyle\text{SDP}(\mathcal{X})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2MN}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\text{Tr}(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n}))+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2MN}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\|\bm{a}(f_{k}^{o})\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\textbf{asv}_{n}(\theta_{k})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}=\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\mathcal{A}_{2D}}.

∎

Then, we propose the following SDP to approximate the solution of 2D-ANM in (
32
):

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2M-1)\times(2L-1)\times N}\\
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\\
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L\times N}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2MN}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\text{Tr}(\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n}))+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}

(35)

\displaystyle\operatorname*{s.t.}\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty\penalty

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{T}_{::n})&\bm{x}_{n}\\
\bm{x}_{n}^{H}&t_{n}\end{array}\right]\succeq\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{n}=\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}_{::n}^{\top}),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ n\in[N],

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{X}^{\star}-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})\|_{F}\leq\varepsilon.

Again, solving the above SDP with CVX can return both the primal solution and the dual solution.
Given the dual solution, one can construct a dual polynomial as in (
33
) and identify
\{f_{k}^{o},\theta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
by localizing the places where
q_{2D}(f^{o},\theta)=1
, similar as in Section
IV
, and finally construct a weight vector.

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we present two novel ANM-based methods in both 1D and 2D frameworks to address the dynamic problem of interference cancellation with time-varying frequency offset. By noting that solving the corresponding SDP is a computational burden in high-dimensional dataset, we also present a novel fast algorithm to approximately solve the 1D ANM optimization problem. Finally, we conduct a series of experiments to confirm the benefits of our 1D and 2D ANM frameworks compared to the conventional SMI, and we illustrate the computational speedup provided by our fast 1D algorithm. We leave the work of developing a fast algorithm for the 2D ANM method as our future work.
