. Let S = 𝕂 ⁡ [ x 1 , … , x n ] S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] and let I I be the t t -path ideal of the line graph L n L_{n} with n n -vertices. 1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite simple graph with vertex set
V=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and edge set
E
. The ideal generated by all quadratic monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
such that
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}\in E
is called the
edge ideal
of
G
, denoted by
I(G)
. The edge ideal was introduced and studied by Villarreal in
[
21
]
. Since then, the study of edge ideals of simple graphs, particularly the properties of their powers, has become a vibrant topic in combinatorial commutative algebra. For more detailed information in this direction, we refer to the exposition paper
[
3
]
and the references therein.

In 1999, Conca and De Negri introduced in their work
[
9
]
the concept of a
t
-path ideal, which serves as a generalization of an edge ideal.
For an integer
2\leq t\leq n
, a
t
-path
of the graph
G
is a sequence of
(t-1)
distinct edges
\{x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}}\},\{x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}}\},\cdots,

\{x_{i_{t-1}},x_{i_{t}}\}
of
G
such that the vertices
x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{t}}
are pairwise distinct. Such a path is also denoted by
\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{t}}\}
for short. The
t
-path ideal

I_{t}(G)
associated to
G
is defined as the square-free monomial ideal

I_{t}(G)=(x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{t}}\colon\;\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{t}}\}\textrm{~~is~~ a}~~t\textrm{-path~~ of}~~G)

in the polynomial ring
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. In the recent years, some algebraic properties of path ideals have been investigated extensively, see for instance
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
15
,
17
,
18
]
.

However, very little is known about the powers of
t
-path ideals for
t\geq 3
. In this paper, we will specifically concentrate on the powers of path ideals of
line graphs
. A
line graph
(or
path graph
) of length
(n-1)
, denote by
L_{n}
, is a graph with vertex set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and edge set
\{\{x_{j},x_{j+1}\}\colon\;j=1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Recently,
{\rm B\breve{a}l\breve{a}nescu}
-
{\rm Cimpoea\textrm{\c{s}}}

[
4
]
derived an explicit formula for the depth of powers of path ideals of line graphs. In
[
20
]
, the regularity function of powers of
I_{t}(L_{n})
is presented, revealing it is linear from the beginning.

In this paper, we will study algebraic properties of powers of
I_{t}(L_{n})
from two different angles:
associated prime ideals
of
I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}
and the
multiplicities
of
I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}
for all
s\geq 1
.

1.1.
Associated prime ideals

Let
S
be a Noetherian ring and
M
a finitely generated non-zero
S
-module. A prime ideal
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}
of
S
is called an
associated prime ideal
of
M
if there exists
x\in M
such that
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}=(0:_{S}x)
. The set of all associated prime ideal of
M
is denoted by
\mathrm{Ass}_{S}(M)
. It is known that
\mathrm{Ass}_{S}(M)
is a non-empty finite set. If
I
is an ideal of
S
, we denote
\mathrm{Ass}_{S}(S/I)
by
\mathrm{ass}(I)
. In 1979, Brodmann
[
7
]
proved the remarkable result that
there exists an integer
s_{0}
such that
\mathrm{ass}(I^{s})=\mathrm{ass}(I^{s_{0}})
for all
s\geq s_{0}
.
This result serves as a good example of the concept in commutative algebra that ideals exhibit favorable behavior asymptotically.
Naturally, Brodmann’s theorem also inspires several questions, such as determining the value of
s_{0}
and determine the prime ideals in
\mathrm{ass}^{\infty}(I):=\mathrm{ass}(I^{s_{0}})
. We refer to
[
10
]
and the references therein for a comprehensive introduction to this topic. If
s_{0}=1
, then
I
is said to be normally torsion-free. Formally, we give the following definition following
[
12
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

An ideal
I
of a Noetherian ring
S
is said to be normally torsion-free if
\mathrm{ass}(I^{s})=\mathrm{ass}(I)
for all
s\geq 1
.

We will show that both
I_{t}(L_{n})
and
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
are normally torsion-free and determine the prime ideals in
\mathrm{ass}(I_{t}(L_{n}))
.

1.2.
Multiplicity

Let
S=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
\mathbb{K}
that is standardly graded and let
M
be a finitely generated graded
S
-module.
The Hilbert function
H_{M}
of
M
is defined as the function

H_{M}(k):=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}M_{k},

where
M_{k}
is the degree
k
component of
M
.
The Hilbert series
\operatorname{HS}(M,z)
is defined to be

\operatorname{HS}(M,z):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}H_{M}(k)z^{k}.

If we assume
\dim M=d+1
, it is a celebrated result of David Hilbert that
\operatorname{HS}(M,z)
is a rational function of the following form

\operatorname{HS}(M,z)=\frac{Q(M,z)}{(1-z)^{d+1}}.

Here,
Q(M,z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z^{-1},z]
is a Laurent polynomial such that
Q(M,1)\neq 0
. As a consequence of this result, the Hilbert function
H_{M}
is of polynomial type of degree
d
, meaning that there exists a polynomial
p_{M}(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
of degree
d
such that
H_{M}(k)=p_{M}(k)
for all
k\gg 0
. The polynomial
p_{M}(z)
is referred to as the
Hilbert polynomial
of
M
.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated graded
S
-module of dimension
d+1
. The Hilbert polynomial
p_{M}(z)
of
M
can be written as

p_{M}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}(-1)^{i}e_{i}(M)\binom{z+d-i}{d-i}.

The integer coefficients
e_{i}(M)
for
i=0,\ldots,d
are called the Hilbert coefficients of
M
. Among these, the first Hilbert coefficient
e_{0}(M)
holds particular significance. It is also known as the multiplicity of
M
and and is frequently denoted by
\operatorname{mult}(M)
.

Recall that a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
is of
polynomial type
of degree
d
if there exists a polynomial
p(z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z]
of degree
d
such that
f(k)=p(k)
for all
k\gg 0
. Let
I
be a graded ideal of
S
such that
\dim M/IM=d
.
It was shown in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
that
e_{i}(M/I^{s}M)
is of polynomial type in
s
of degree
\leq n-d+i
for
i=1,2,\ldots,d
. To the best of our knowledge, there are currently no known non-trivial examples of graded ideals for which the multiplicity of their powers has been explicitly computed. In this paper, we will provide an explicit formula for the multiplicity of
S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}
.

Structure of this paper

In the paper, we always assume that
n,t
are integers with
n\geq t\geq 1
. The polynomial ring
\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
will be denoted by

. We use

n

. The final section contains the main contribution of this paper, in which we present an explicit formula for the multiplicity of

from the beginning.

We refer to the book
[

]
for the notation that is not explained in this paper.

2.
Associated primes of
I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}

According to the definition of a
t
-path ideal, the ideal
I_{t}(L_{n})
has a unique minimal generating set
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n-t+1}
, where
u_{i}=x_{i}\cdots x_{i+t-1}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-t+1
.
In this section, we demonstrate that both
I_{t}(L_{n})
and its Alexander dual
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
are normally torsion-free and offer an explicit description of minimal prime ideals of
I_{t}(L_{n})
. Furthermore, utilizing a novel method, we compute the projective dimension of
S/I_{t}(L_{n})
.

Recall that a simplicial complex on
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is a collection
\Delta
of subsets of
[n]
such that if
F\in\Delta
and
G\subseteq F
, then
G\in\Delta
. Any
F\in\Delta
is a
face
of
\Delta
. A maximal face (with respect to inclusion) is a
facet
of
\Delta
. The set of all facets of
\Delta
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
. A simplicial complex
\Delta
is denoted by
\langle F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{r}\rangle
if
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots,F_{r}
are all facets of
\Delta
.

For a subset
F
of
[n]
, let
x_{F}
denote the monomial
\prod_{i\in F}x_{i}
. Then the facet ideal of
\Delta
is defined to be the monomial ideal

I(\Delta)=(x_{F}\colon\;F\mbox{ is a facet of }\Delta),

and the Stanley-Reisner
I_{\Delta}
ideal of
\Delta
is defined as

I_{\Delta}=(x_{F}\colon\;F\subseteq[n]\mbox{ and }F\notin\Delta).

Put
F_{i}:=\{i,\ldots,i+t-1\}\subseteq[n]
for
i=1,\ldots,n-t+1
and define
\Delta_{n,t}
to be the simplicial complex generated by
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-t+1}
. It is evident that the path ideal
I_{t}(L_{n})
is equal to the facet ideal
I(\Delta_{n,t})
of
\Delta_{n,t}.
A simplicial complex
\Delta
is called a
flag complex
if its Stanley-Reisner ideals is generated by monomials of degree 2, or equivalently, all minimal non-faces of
\Delta
have exactly two elements. Here, a
non-face
of
\Delta
refers to a subset of
[n]
which does not belong to
\Delta
. The lemma below uncovers an intriguing characteristic of
\Delta_{n,t}
, although we won’t explore it further in this paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

For all
2\leq t\leq n
, the Stanley-Reisner ideal
I_{\Delta_{n,t}}
is generated by all quadratic monomials
x_{i}x_{j}
with
j-i\geq t.
In particular,
\Delta_{n,t}
is a flag complex.

Proof.

Let
I
denote the ideal
(x_{i}x_{j}\colon\;j-i\geq t).
It is clear that if
j-i\geq t
, then
\{i,j\}
is a non-face of
\Delta_{n,t}
. Thus,
I\subseteq I_{\Delta_{n,t}}
.

Conversely, let
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
be a non-face of
\Delta_{n,t}
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
. Then if
i_{k}-i_{1}<t
, then
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq F_{i_{1}}
, a contradiction. This implies
i_{k}-i_{1}\geq t
and so
x_{F}\in I
. This proves
I\supseteq I_{\Delta_{n,t}}.

∎

A facet
F
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is called a
leaf
if either
F
is the only facet of
\Delta
, or there exists a facet
G\neq F
such that
H\cap F\subseteq G\cap F
for any facet
H\neq F
. A leaf
F
of
\Delta
is said to be
good
if the sets
F\cap H
, where
H\in\mathcal{F}(\Delta)
is linearly ordered. A simplicial complex
\Delta
is a
simplicial forest
if every nonempty subcollection of
\Delta
has a leaf. A connected simplicial forest is called a
simplicial tree
. Here, a
subcollection
of
\Delta
refers to a simplicial complex whose set of facets is a subset of
\mathcal{F}(\Delta).
The following observation forms the foundation of all the results in this section.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
0<t\leq n
, the simplicial complex
\Delta_{n,t}
is a simplicial tree.

Proof.

It is clear that
\Delta_{n,t}
is connected. Let
\Delta^{\prime}=\langle F_{k_{1}},\ldots F_{k_{r}}\rangle
be a subcollection of
\Delta
. Note that
F_{k_{i}}=\{k_{i},k_{i}+1,\ldots,k_{i}+t-1\}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
. We may assume that
r\geq 2
and that
1\leq k_{1}<\cdots<k_{r}\leq n-t+1
. It can be easily verified that

F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{i}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{k_{i},k_{i}+1,\ldots,k_{1}+t-1\},&\hbox{if $k_{i}\leq k_{1}+t-1$;}\\
\emptyset,&\hbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for
i=2,\ldots,r
. From this it follows that

F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{2}}\supset F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{3}}\supset\cdots\supset F_{k_{1}}\cap F_{k_{r}}.

Therefore
F_{k_{1}}
is a (good) leaf of
\Delta^{\prime}
. Hence,
\Delta_{n,t}
is a simplicial tree.

∎

We now present the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.3
.

For all
1\leq t\leq n
, both
I_{t}(L_{n})
and
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
are normally torsion-free.

Proof.

It was shown in
[
16
, Theorem 2.2 and Theorem 2.5]
that if a simplicial complex
\Delta
has no special odd cycles, then both the facet ideal
I(\Delta)
and its Alexander dual
I(\Delta^{*})=I(\Delta)^{\vee}
are normally torsion-free. On the other hand, by
[
16
, Theorem 3.2]
, if
\Delta
is a simplicial forest, then
\Delta
has no special cycles. In view of these facts, the result follows immediately from Lemma
2.2
.

∎

We note that the result that
I_{t}(L_{n})
is normally torsion-free has been established in
[
19
, Proposition 3.2]
utilizing a completely different method.

By applying
[
12
, Theorem 1.4.6]
and
[
16
, Corollary 1.6]
respectively, we immediately obtain the following corollaries.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
1\leq t\leq n
. Then
I_{t}(L_{n})
is normal, this is,
\overline{I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}}=I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}
for all
s\geq 1
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
1\leq t\leq n
. Then the Rees algebra
\mathcal{R}(I_{t}(L_{n}))
is a normal Cohen-Macauly domain.

In the following, we aim to determine all the minimal prime ideals of
I_{t}(L_{n})
, a task that will also prove useful in the subsequent section. To do this, we introduce some additional notation. Specifically, if
F\subseteq[n]
, we denote by
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}_{F}
the prime ideal generated by
x_{i}
with
i\in F
. For convenience, we also adopt the following notation.

Notation 2.6
.

Let
\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
be the set of subsets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
of
[n]
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
and such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

1\leq i_{1}\leq t
;

(2)

i_{2}>t
;

(3)

1\leq i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
for all
j=1,\ldots,r-1
;

(4)

i_{j+2}-i_{j}>t
for all
j=1,\ldots,r-2
;

(5)

i_{r-1}<n-t+1
;

(6)

n-t+1\leq i_{r}\leq n
.

Recall that a
vertex cover
of the simplicial complex
\Delta
is a subset
C\subset[n]
such that
C\cap F\neq\emptyset
for all
F\in{\mathcal{F}}(\Delta)
. A vertex cover
C
is called a
minimal vertex cover
, if no proper subset of
C
is a vertex cover. It is well-known that
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}
is a minimal prime ideal of
I(\Delta)
if and only if
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}=\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}_{C}
for some minimal vertex cover of
\Delta
.

Proposition 2.7
.

For
1\leq t\leq n
, the set of minimal prime ideals of
I_{t}(L_{n})
is given by

\{\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}_{F}\colon\;F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}\}.

Proof.

It is equivalent to showing that
\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
consists of all minimal vertex covers of
\Delta_{n,t}
. Let
F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
. We first show that
F
is a vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
. Given a facet
F_{i}
of
\Delta_{n,t}
, where
1\leq i\leq n-t+1
. If
i\in F
, then
i\in F\cap F_{i}
and so
F\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
. Suppose that
i\notin F
. If
i<i_{1}
then
i_{1}\in F_{i}\cap F
and so
F_{i}\cap F\neq\emptyset
. If
i>i_{1}
, then, since
i_{r}\geq n-t+1
, there exists
1\leq j\leq r-1
such that
i_{j}<i<i_{j+1}
. Since
i_{j+1}-i<i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
, it follows that
i_{j+1}\in F_{i}
and thus
F\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset
. This proves
F
is a vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
. Next, we show that
F
is minimal, that is,
F\setminus\{i_{j}\}
is not a vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
. But, this is clear by noticing that
F_{1}\cap(F\setminus\{i_{1}\})=\emptyset
,
F_{i_{j-1}+1}\cap(F\setminus\{i_{j}\})=\emptyset
for
j=2,\ldots,r-1
and
F_{n-t+1}\cap(F\setminus\{i_{r}\})=\emptyset
. Therefore,
F
is a minimal vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
indeed.

Conversely, let
F
be a minimal vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
. We may write
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
with
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{r}
. Since
F_{1}\cap F\neq\emptyset
, it follows that
i_{1}\leq t
and so (1) holds. Similarly, since
F_{n-t+1}\cap F\neq\emptyset
, we have
i_{r}\geq n-t+1
and this proves (6). If
i_{2}\leq t
, then, note that
i_{1}\in F_{i}
implies
i_{2}\in F_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-t+1
, it follows that
F\setminus\{i_{1}\}
is also a vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
, a contradiction. This establishes (2). Similarly, (5) has to hold. If
i_{j+1}-i_{j}>t
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,r-1\}
, then
F_{i_{j}+1}\cap F=\emptyset
. This is impossible and so (3) should hold. It remains to prove (4). Suppose on the contrary that
i_{j+2}-i_{j}\leq t
for some
1\leq j\leq r-2
. Then, if
i_{j+1}\in F_{i}
for some
i
, we have
i_{j+1}\in\{i,i+1,\ldots,i+t-1\}
. It follows that either
i_{j+2}\leq i+t-1
(i.e.,
i_{j+2}\in F_{i}
) or
i_{j}\geq i
(i.e.,
i_{j}\in F_{i}
), for otherwise
i_{i+2}-i_{j}>t
. Thus,
F\setminus\{i_{j+1}\}
is also a vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.

∎

This result can be checked using
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
. Combining Theorem
2.3
with Proposition
2.7
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
2\leq t\leq n
. Then
\mathrm{ass}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\{\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}_{F}\colon\;F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}\}
for all
s\geq 1
.

Given a monomial ideal
I
, we use
\operatorname{Deg}(I)
to denote the maximal degree of minimal generators of
I
. We now determine
\operatorname{Deg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})
for all
1\leq t\leq n
. Recall for a square-free monomial ideal
I=I(\Delta)
, the Alexander dual of
I
is defined to be the monomial ideal

I^{\vee}=(x_{C}\colon\;C\mbox{ is a vertex cover of }\Delta).

Corollary 2.9
.

The Alexander dual
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
of
I_{t}(L_{n})
has the minimal generating set
G=\{x_{F}\colon\;F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}\}
. Assume
n=(t+1)p+q
with
0\leq q\leq t
. Then the maximal degree of minimal generators of
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
is given by

\operatorname{Deg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2p,&\hbox{$q<t$;}\\
2p+1,&\hbox{$q=t$.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

The first statement is immediate from the definition of the Alexander dual. To prove the second statement, we denote
\operatorname{Deg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})
as
D
for simplicity. Consider the following infinite sequence

1,t+1,t+2,2t+2,2t+3,\cdots,kt+k,kt+k+1,\cdots.

Let
a_{m}
denote the
m
-th number of this sequence. Namely,
a_{1}=1,a_{2}=t+1,a_{3}=t+2
and so on. Thus,
a_{2k}=kt+k
and
a_{2k+1}=kt+k+1
for
k\geq 1
. Given
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
, we claim that

a_{m}\leq i_{m}\mbox{ for all }m=1,\ldots,r.

In fact,
a_{1}=1\leq i_{1}
and
a_{2}=t+1\leq i_{2}
. Assume that
2\leq m\leq r-1
and
a_{j}\leq i_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Then, if
m=2k
for some
k\geq 1
, then
a_{m+1}=a_{m}+1
and so
i_{m+1}\geq i_{m}+1\geq a_{m}+1=a_{m+1}
; if
m=2k+1
, then
a_{m+1}-a_{m-1}=t+1
. Since
i_{m+1}-i_{m-1}\geq t+1
, it follows that
a_{m+1}\leq i_{m+1}
. This proves the claim.

We next consider the following cases:

Case 1: If
n=(t+1)p+q
with
0\leq q\leq t-1
, then

(p-1)t+p<n-t+1\leq(t+1)p.

Let
G=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{2p}\}
. Then
n\geq a_{2p}=(t+1)p\geq n+t-1
and
a_{2p-1}=t(p-1)+p<n-t+1
, and so it is routine to verify that
G\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
. This proves
D\geq 2p
. To prove
D\leq 2p
, we assume on the contrary that there exists
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
such that
r>2p
. Then
i_{r-1}\geq i_{2p}\geq a_{2p}\geq n-t+1
. This is contradicted to condition (5). Hence,
D=2p
.

Case 2: If
n=(t+1)p+t
, then
n-t+1=(t+1)p+1=a_{2p+1},
and one easily checks that
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{2p+1}\}
belongs to
\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
. It follows that
D=2p+1
, by using the same argument as in case 1.
∎

The formula for the projective dimension of
S/I_{t}(L_{n})
was first established in
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
, and subsequently proved through an alternative method in
[
5
, Corollary 5.1]
. In what follows, utilizing the theory of Alexander duality, we present a novel and concise demonstration. Recall a well-known result stating that
S/I
is Cohen-Macaulay if and only if the Alexander dual
I^{\vee}
of
I
has a linear resolution, (see for example
[
12
, Theorem 8.1.9]
). Furthermore, according to
[
12
, Theorem 8.2.20]
,
S/I
is sequentially Cohen-Macaulay precisely when
I^{\vee}
is componentwise linear. Finally, for any square-free monomial ideal
I
of
S
, we have
\operatorname{pd}(S/I)=\operatorname{reg}(I^{\vee})
, as stated in
[
12
, Proposition 8.1.10]
. Here,
\operatorname{pd}
refers to the projective dimension and
\operatorname{reg}
refers to the (Castelnuovo-Mumford) regularity. For more about Alexander duality, we refer to Chapter 8 of the book
[
12
]
. .

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
2\leq t\leq n
and write
n=(t+1)p+q
with
0\leq q\leq t
. Then
S/I_{t}(L_{n})
is sequentially Cohen-Macaulay and

\mathrm{pd}(S/I_{t}(L_{n}))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2p,&\hbox{$q<t$;}\\
2p+1,&\hbox{$q=t$.}\end{array}\right..

Proof.

In
[
11
, Corollary 5.4]
, it was shown that if
\Delta
is a simplicial forest then
S/I(\Delta)
is sequentially Cohen-Macaulay. Applying this result and in view of Lemma
2.2
, we can conclude that
S/I_{t}(L_{n})
is sequentially Cohen-Macaulay.

By
[
12
, Theorem 8.2.20]
, we know
I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee}
is componentwise linear. Therefore, using
[
12
, Corollary 8.2.14]
, we have
\operatorname{reg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})=\operatorname{Deg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})
. Furthermore, according to
[
12
, Proposition 8.1.10]
, we have
\operatorname{pd}(S/I_{t}(L_{n}))=\operatorname{Deg}(I_{t}(L_{n})^{\vee})
. The result now follows from Corollary
2.9
.

∎

3.
Multiplicity

In this section, we present the main result of this paper, which provides an explicit formula for determining the multiplicity of powers of
I_{t}(L_{n}).
We will maintain all notation introduced in the previous sections. To start, we introduce a general result regarding the multiplicity of square-free monomial ideals.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
I
be a square-free monomial ideal of height
h
. Then
\operatorname{mult}(S/I)
is equal to the number of minimal prime ideals of
I
of height
h
.

Proof.

This follows directly from
[
12
, Corollary 6.2.3]
.

∎

For a monomial ideal
I
, following
[
13
]
, we denote by
\mbox{m-grade}(I)

the maximal length of a regular sequence of monomials contained in
I
and call this number
the monomial grade of
I
. One has
\mbox{m-grade}(I)\leq\mathrm{grade}(I)\leq\operatorname{height}(I)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
n=\xi t+\theta
with
\xi\geq 1
and
0\leq\theta<t
. Then

\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n}))=\xi.

Proof.

Since
u_{1},u_{t+1},\ldots,u_{(\xi-1)t+1}
forms a regular sequence that is contained in
I_{t}(L_{n})
, it follows that
\mbox{m-grade}(I_{t}(L_{n}))\geq\xi
, and thus
\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n}))\geq\xi.
Additionally, a simple verification shows that the set
\{t,2t,\cdots,\xi t\}
belongs to
\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
, (refer to Notation
2.6
), and so
(x_{t},\ldots,x_{\xi t})
is a minimal prime ideal of
I_{t}(L_{n})
. This implies
\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n}))\leq\xi
, completing the proof.

∎

In
[
13
]
, a monomial ideal
I
is called of
K
\ddot{\mathrm{o}}
nig type
if
I\neq 0
and
\mbox{m-grade}(I)=\operatorname{height}(I)
. Thus,
I_{t}(L_{n})
is of K
\ddot{\mathrm{o}}
nig type in view of the proof of Lemma
3.2
.

We next describe the minimal prime ideals of
I_{t}(L_{n})
of height
\xi
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
n=\xi t+\theta
with
\xi\geq 1
and
0\leq\theta<t
.
Let
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}=(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\xi}})
be a prime ideal of height
\xi
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\xi}\leq n
. Then
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathrm{ass}(I_{t}(L_{n}))
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi
, one has
(j-1)t+\theta+1\leq i_{j}\leq jt
;

(2)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi-1
, one has
i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
.

Proof.

Assume that the elements of
F=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi}\}
meet conditions (1) and (2). We proceed to prove
F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
, as defined in Notation
2.6
. First, it is easy to see that
i_{1}\leq t
and
i_{2}\geq(2-1)t+\theta+1\geq t+1
. Next, we observe that
i_{\xi-1}\leq(\xi-1)t=n-t-\theta<n-t+1
and
i_{\xi}\geq(\xi-1)t+\theta+1=n-t+1
. Finally, for
j=1,\ldots,\xi-2
, we see that

i_{j+2}-i_{j}\geq(j+1)t+\theta+1-jt=t+\theta+1\geq t+1.

Thus,
F\in\mathbf{C}_{n,t}
and so
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathrm{ass}(I_{t}(L_{n}))
by Proposition
2.7
.

Conversely, since
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}\in\mathrm{ass}(I_{t}(L_{n}))
, we have
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi}\}
is a minimal vertex cover of
\Delta_{n,t}
. Given that the facets
F_{1},F_{t+1},\ldots,F_{(\xi-1)t+1}
and
F_{\theta+1},F_{t+\theta+1},\ldots,F_{(\xi-1)t+\theta+1}
of
\Delta_{n,t}
are pairwise disjoint within each respective set, it follows that for
j=1,\ldots,\xi
, one has
i_{j}
belongs to both
F_{(j-1)t+1}
and
F_{(j-1)t+\theta+1}
, and thus
(j-1)t+\theta+1\leq i_{j}\leq jt
, satisfying (1). As to condition (2), it follows directly from Proposition
2.7
.

∎

To determine the multiplicity of
S/I_{t}(L_{n})
, in light of Lemma
3.1
, we must count the number of the sequences
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})
that meet conditions (1) and (2), as described in Lemma
3.3
. This counting problem is purely combinatorial and will be solved in the next two results. Note that for a finite set
A
,
|A|
denotes the cardinal number of
A
, and for a positive integer
m
,
[m]
denotes the set
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Given
\xi\geq 1
and
t\geq 1
, let
T_{\xi,t}
denote the set of integral sequences
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\xi}
such that

(1)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi
, one has
(j-1)t+1\leq i_{j}\leq jt
;

(2)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi-1
, one has
i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
.

Then

|T_{\xi,t}|=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
t
. If
t=1
, the result follows easily. Assume now that
t\geq 2
. For each sequence
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{\xi})\in T_{\xi,t}
, we define

\tau(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi}):=\operatorname{min}\{j\in[\xi]\colon\;i_{j}=(j-1)t+1\}.

By convention, we set
\tau(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})=\xi+1
if
i_{j}\neq(j-1)t+1
for all
j=1,\ldots,\xi
.
Thus,
\tau(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})\in\{1,\ldots,\xi,\xi+1\}
. We make the following easy observation:

if
\tau(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})=k<\xi
, then
i_{j}=(j-1)t+1
for
j=k,\ldots,\xi
.

For
k\in[\xi+1]
, we define

T_{\xi,t}^{k}=\{(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{\xi})\in T_{\xi,t}\colon\;\tau(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})=k\}.

One easily sees that
|T_{\xi,t}^{1}|=1
and
|T_{\xi,t}^{2}|=|\{i_{1}:2\leq i_{1}\leq t\}|=t-1
.

In general, for all
k\in[\xi+1]
, we use
T_{k}
to denote the set of all sequences
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k-1}
such that
(j-1)t+2\leq i_{j}\leq jt
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1,
and
i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
for
j=1,\ldots,k-2.
It follows from the above observation that
|T_{\xi,t}^{k}|=|T_{k}|
for
k=1,\ldots,\xi+1
.

Set
i_{j}^{\prime}=i_{j}-j
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
. Then, we have for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
,

(j-1)t+2\leq i_{j}\leq jt\Longleftrightarrow(j-1)(t-1)+1\leq i_{j}^{\prime}\leq j(t-1);

and for
j=1,\ldots,k-2
,

i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t\Longleftrightarrow i^{\prime}_{j+1}-i^{\prime}_{j}\leq t-1.

Thus,

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\in T_{k}\Longleftrightarrow(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{k-1}^{\prime})\in T_{t-1,k-1}.

This implies the following map:

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})\longmapsto(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{k-1}^{\prime})

establishes a bijection between
T_{k}
and
T_{k-1,t-1}
. Hence,
|T_{k}|
is equal to the number of
T_{k-1,t-1}
. By the inductive hypothesis, we have
|T_{k-1,t-1}|=\begin{pmatrix}k+t-3\\
k-1\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle|T_{\xi,t}|

\displaystyle=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=1}^{\xi+1}|T^{k}_{\xi,t}|=\mathop{\sum}\limits_{k=1}^{\xi+1}\begin{pmatrix}k+t-3\\
k-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}t-2\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}t-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}t\\
2\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-3\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-2\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}t-1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}t-1\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}t\\
2\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-3\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-2\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}t\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}t\\
2\end{pmatrix}+\cdots+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-3\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-2\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-2\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-2\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

Here, we use the formula
\binom{n}{k}+\binom{n}{k+1}=\binom{n+1}{k+1}
many times.

∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Given
n\geq t\geq 1
and assume
n=\xi t+\theta
with
0\leq\theta\leq t-1
. Let
X_{n,t}
denote the set of integral sequences
i_{1}<\ldots<i_{a}
such that

(1)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi
, one has
(j-1)t+\theta+1\leq i_{j}\leq jt
;

(2)

for
1\leq j\leq\xi-1
, one has
i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t
.

Then

|X_{n,t}|=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Set
i_{j}^{\prime}=i_{j}-\theta j
. Then, we have for
1\leq j\leq\xi
,

(j-1)t+\theta+1\leq i_{j}\leq jt\Longleftrightarrow(j-1)(t-\theta)+1\leq i_{j}^{\prime}\leq j(t-\theta),

and for
1\leq j\leq\xi-1
,

i_{j+1}-i_{j}\leq t\Longleftrightarrow i_{j+1}^{\prime}-i_{j}^{\prime}\leq t-\theta.

Thus, the map:
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\xi})\longmapsto(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{\xi}^{\prime})
establishes a bijection between

X_{n,t}
and
T_{\xi,t-\theta}
. This implies

|X_{n,t}|=|T_{\xi,t-\theta}|=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix},

as desired.

∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Assume
n=\xi t+\theta
with
\xi\geq 1
and
0\leq\theta<t
. Then

\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n}))=\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

This is a combination of Lemma
3.1
, Lemma
3.3
, and Corollary
3.5
.

∎

Based on Proposition
3.6
, we can obtain our main result through an induction process. In this process, constructing suitable short exact sequences is the crucial step. In order to achieve this, we require the following lemmas.

Recall the
support
of monomial
u
is the set
\{i\in[n]\colon\;x_{i}\mbox{ divides }u\}
. For a monomial ideal
I
, we define the
support
of
I
to be the union of supports of minimal generators of
I
. If monomial ideals
J
and
K
have disjoint supports, then
J:K=J
. Thus, if
I
is another monomial ideal, then we have
(I+J):K=(I:K)+(J:K)=(I:K)+J.
This fact is utilized in the following proofs without explicit reference.

Lemma 3.7
.

For all
s\geq 2
, we have

(1)

I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}=I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1}
;

(2)

Let
(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})
denote the ideal
(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1})+(x_{n-t})
. Then

(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})=(I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s},x_{n-t}).

In particular, if
n\leq 2t
, then
I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s}=0
and so
(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})=(x_{n-t}).

Proof.

(1) is just
[
4
, Lemma 2.1]
.

(2) Since
x_{n-t}
does not divide
u_{n-t+1}
, it follows that

(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1})+(x_{n-t})=(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},x_{n-t}):u_{n-t+1}.

It is easy to see that
(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},x_{n-t})=(I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s},x_{n-t}).
Note that
(I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s},x_{n-t})
and
u_{n-t+1}
have disjoint supports, the result follows.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
A_{i}:=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i+1})
and let
B_{i}:=(A_{i},x_{n-i+1})
for
i=1,\ldots,t
. Then
B_{i}=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-i+1})
and
A_{i}:x_{n-i+1}=A_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

Proof.

The equality that
B_{i}=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-i+1})
is easy.
Let us show that
A_{i}:x_{n-i+1}=A_{i+1}
. Since

I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s}=(I_{t}(L_{n-i-1})^{s},u_{n-i-t+1}I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s-1}),

we have

\displaystyle A_{i}:x_{n-i+1}=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i+1}):x_{n-i+1}

\displaystyle=(I_{t}(L_{n-i-1})^{s},u_{n-i-t+1}I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s-1}:x_{n-i+1})+(x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i})

\displaystyle=(I_{t}(L_{n-i-1})^{s},u_{n-i-t+1}I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s-1},x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i}):x_{n-i+1}

\displaystyle=(I_{t}(L_{n-i-1})^{s},x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i}):x_{n-i+1}=A_{i+1}.

Here, the third equality follows because
x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i}
divides
u_{n-i-t+1}
.

∎

Let
T
be a finitely generated graded
S
-modules of dimension
d
. According to
[
8
, Corollary 4.1.8]
, we can express the Hilbert series of
T
as

\operatorname{HS}(T,z)=\frac{Q(T,z)}{(1-z)^{d}}

for some
Q(T,z)\in\mathbb{Q}[z,z^{-1}]
with
Q(T,1)\neq 0
. Furthermore,
[
8
, Proposition 4.1.9]
asserts

\operatorname{mult}(T)=Q(T,1).

Based on these results, we can promptly derive the subsequent lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
0\longrightarrow K\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow N\longrightarrow 0
be a short exact sequence of finitely generated graded
S
-modules. Then
\dim M=\operatorname{max}\{\dim K,\dim N\}
. Moreover,

(1)

If
\dim M=\dim K=\dim N
, then
\operatorname{mult}(M)=\operatorname{mult}(N)+\operatorname{mult}(K)
;

(2)

If
\dim M=\dim K>\dim N
, then
\operatorname{mult}(M)=\operatorname{mult}(K)
;

(3)

If
\dim M=\dim N>\dim K
, then
\operatorname{mult}(M)=\operatorname{mult}(N)
.

We also need the following fact: given that
I
is a monomial ideal of
S
and
y
is a new variable, then

\operatorname{mult}(S/I)=\operatorname{mult}(S[y]/IS[y]).

We are now ready to present the main result of this paper.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume
n=\xi t+\theta
, with
\xi\geq 1
and
0\leq\theta<t
. Then, for all
s\geq 1
, we have

\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on both
s
and
n
.
The case that
s=1
follows from Proposition
3.6
. If
n=t
, we have

\operatorname{HS}(S/I_{t}(L_{t})^{s},z)=\frac{1-z^{st}}{(1-z)^{t}}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{t-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{st-1}z^{i}.

It follows that
Q(S/I_{t}(L_{t})^{s},z)=\sum_{i=0}^{st-1}z^{i}
and so
\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{t})^{s})=Q(S/I_{t}(L_{t})^{s},1)=st.
This proves the result holds when
n=t
. Now, assume
s>1
and
n>t
. To begin, we write
n=\xi t+\theta
, where
\xi\geq 1
and
0\leq\theta<t
.
There are two cases to consider.

Case 1:
\theta>0
. Consider the following three short exact sequences of graded
S
-modules:

(1)

0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}}[-t]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}}\longrightarrow\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n})^{s},u_{n-t+1})}\longrightarrow 0,

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}}[-t]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}}\longrightarrow\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},u_{n-t+1})}\longrightarrow 0,

and

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:x_{n-t}u_{n-t+1}}[-1]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})}\longrightarrow 0.

We proceed to show that all nonzero graded modules appearing in the above three short exact sequences share the same dimension, which is
(n-\xi)
. Firstly, by Lemma
3.7
, we have
I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}=I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1}
,
it follows from Lemma
3.2
that

\dim\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}:u_{n-t+1}}=\dim\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n})^{s}}=n-\xi.

Note that
(I_{t}(L_{n})^{s},u_{n-t+1})=(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},u_{n-t+1})
and
\sqrt{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},u_{n-t+1})}=I_{t}(L_{n})
, we obtain

\dim\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n})^{s},u_{n-t+1})}=\dim\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},u_{n-t+1})}=n-\xi.

Since

I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:x_{n-t}u_{n-t+1}=I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t}x_{n}=I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s-1}:x_{n}=I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s-1},

we have

\dim\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:x_{n-t}u_{n-t+1}}=\dim\frac{S}{I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s-1}}=n-\xi.

If
n>2t
, by Lemma
3.7
.(2), we have
(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})=(I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s},x_{n-t})
. Since
n-t-1=(\xi-1)t+\theta-1
with
\theta-1\geq 0
, it follows that

\dim\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})}=\dim\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s},x_{n-t})}=n-1-(\xi-1)=n-\xi.

By Lemma
3.9
and using the induction hypothesis, it follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s-1})-\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-t-1})^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle~~~~-\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle-\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-\theta-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

If
t<n\leq 2t
, then
\xi=1
and so

\dim\frac{S}{(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s}:u_{n-t+1},x_{n-t})}=\dim\frac{S}{(x_{n-t})}=n-\xi.

Since
\operatorname{mult}(S/(x_{n-t}))=1
, by applying the induction hypothesis together with Lemma
3.9
, we can deduce that:

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s})-\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s-1})-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s-1\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta\\
1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}s\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta+1\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s-1\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta+1\\
1\end{pmatrix}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta+1\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s-1\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta\\
0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta+1\\
1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}s\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}t-\theta\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

Case 2:
\theta=0
, i.e.,
n=\xi t
. We may assume
\xi>1
, since the case that
\xi=1
has been addressed. As in Lemma
3.8
, we put for
i=1,\ldots,t
,

A_{i}:=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-t+1}\cdots x_{n-i+1})\mbox{\quad and \quad}B_{i}:=(A_{i},x_{n-i+1}).

We must compute the dimensions of these graded
S
-modules. Fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
. By Lemma
3.8
, we see that
B_{i}=(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s},x_{n-i+1})
. Since we
n-i=(\xi-1)t+t-i
with
0\leq t-i<t
, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that
\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s})=\xi-1
and so
\operatorname{height}(B_{i})=\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s})+1=\xi
. We next show that
\operatorname{height}(A_{i})=\xi
. Since
\operatorname{height}(I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s})=\xi-1
, it follows that
\operatorname{height}(A_{i})
is either
\xi-1
or
\xi
. Suppose on the contrary that
\operatorname{height}(A_{i})=\xi-1
. Then there must exist a minimal prime ideal, say
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}
, of
I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s}
with
\operatorname{height}(\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}})=\xi-1
that contains
A_{i}
. However, this can not be the case. To see why, we assume without loss the generality that
\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}=(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{\xi-1}})
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\xi-1}\leq n-i
. By Lemma
3.3
, we have
(\xi-2)t+t-i+1\leq i_{\xi-1}\leq(\xi-1)t
. This implies
A_{i}\nsubseteq\operatorname{\mathfrak{p}}
, leading to contradiction. Therefore, we conclude that
\operatorname{height}(A_{i})=\xi
. As a result, we have

\dim\frac{S}{A_{i}}=\dim\frac{S}{B_{i}}=n-\xi\mbox{ for all }i=1,\ldots,t.

Since
A_{1}=(I_{t}(L_{n-1})^{s},u_{n-t+1})
, according to Lemma
3.9
and considering the short exact sequence (1), it follows that

(2)

\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/A_{1}).

On the other hand, Lemma
3.8
asserts the existence of the following
t-1
short exact sequences:

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{2}}[-1]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{1}}\longrightarrow\frac{S}{B_{1}}\longrightarrow 0,

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{3}}[-1]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{2}}\longrightarrow\frac{S}{B_{2}}\longrightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\vdots

\displaystyle 0\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{t}}[-1]\longrightarrow\frac{S}{A_{t-1}}\longrightarrow\frac{S}{B_{t-1}}\longrightarrow 0.

In view of these short exact sequences together with (
2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/A_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/A_{2})+\operatorname{mult}(S/B_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/A_{3})+\operatorname{mult}(S/B_{1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/B_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\operatorname{mult}(S/A_{t})+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{t-1}\operatorname{mult}(S/B_{i}).

Since
A_{t}=B_{t}
, it follows that

\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{t}\operatorname{mult}(S/B_{i})

This implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s})=\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n})^{s-1})+\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{t}\operatorname{mult}(S/I_{t}(L_{n-i})^{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{t}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+i-2\\
\xi-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-2\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}s+\xi-1\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi+t-1\\
\xi\end{pmatrix}.

This completes the proof.

∎
