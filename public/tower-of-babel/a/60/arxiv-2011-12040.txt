Abstract
A cryptographic protocol (CP) is a distributed algorithm designed to provide a secure communication in an insecure environment. CPs are used, for example, in electronic payments, electronic voting procedures, database access systems, etc. Errors in the CPs can lead to great financial and social damage, therefore it is necessary to use mathematical methods to justify the correctness and safety of the CPs. In this paper, a new mathematical model of a CP is introduced, which allows one to describe both the CPs and their properties. It is shown how, on the basis of this model, it is possible to solve the problems of verification of CPs.



1
Introduction

1.1
A concept of a cryptographic protocol

A
cryptographic protocol (CP)

is a distributed algorithm that describes the order in which messages are exchanged between agents. Examples of such agents are computer systems, bank cards, people, etc.

To ensure security properties of a CP (such as, for example, the secrecy of transmitted data), cryptographic transformations (encryption, electronic signature, hash functions, etc.) can be used in the CP.

We assume that the cryptographic transformations used in CPs are ideal, i.e. satisfy some axioms expressing, for example, the impossibility of extracting plain texts from encrypted texts
without knowing of the corresponding cryptographic keys.

1.2
Vulnerabilities in cryptographic protocols

Many CP vulnerabilities are related not with poor cryptographic qualities of the cryptographic primitives used in them, but with logical errors in protocols. For example, a logical error was found in the CP for logging into a Google portal that allows a user to identify himself only once and then get access to various applications (such as Gmail or Google Calendar), allowing a dishonest service provider to impersonate any of its users.

There are many other examples of CPs (see for example

•

participants of these CPs can receive distorted messages (or even lose them) as a result of interception, deleting or distorting of transmitted messages by the adversary, which violates the integrity property,

•

the adversary can discover a secret information

Vulnerabilities were also detected in one of the most well-known CPs

All of the above examples justify the fact that

•

In this work, a new mathematical model of CPs is constructed.
In terms of this model it is possible to express such properties of correctness of CPs as, for example, integrity of transmitted messages (i.e., justification of the following property of the analyzed CPs: messages sent by one participant of a CP to another participant of this CP, reach the recipient in an undistorted form).

2
Sequential and distributed processes

In this paper, we outline the concepts of sequential and distributed processes. These concepts are basic
mathematical objects for building a CP process model.
This model is a development of the
Calculus of Cryptographic Protocols
of Abadi-Gordon
(
SPI-calculus
,
[
36
]
). It can serve as a theoretical basis for a new method for verifications of CPs, where CP verification means the construction of a mathematical proof that an analyzed CP has the desired properties. Examples of such properties are
integrity
and
secrecy
properties. In the process model described in this text, CPs and their formal specifications are represented as distributed processes.

One of the most important advantages of the proposed CP process model is the low complexity of proofs of CP correctness. In particular, this model eliminates the need to build the sets of all reachable states of the analyzed CPs. This provides an important advantage when analyzing sets of states of the analyzed CP in the case when sets of these states are potentially unlimited. Another important advantage of the proposed CP model is the high degree of automation of solving the CP verification problem based on this model.

A renaming is a partial injective function

2.4
Distributed processes

2.4.1
A concept of a distributed process

Let
P_{I}=\{P_{i}\mid i\in I\}
be a family of SPs.

\forall\,i\in I
let
\tilde{X}_{P_{i}}
be a set of variables
from
{\it Var}_{P_{i}}
, which
either do not belong to
X_{P_{i}}
,
or belong to
\bar{X}_{P_{i}}
and are not shared.

We shall assume that for each family of SPs
P_{I}

under consideration the sets
\tilde{X}_{P_{i}}
are disjoint
(if this is not the case, then we rename accordingly
variables in SPs from the family
P_{I}
).

A
distributed process (DP)

corresponding to the family
P_{I}

is an object denoted by the notation

\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
.
A DP is a model of a distributed algorithm,
components of which are SPs from the family
P_{I}
,
interacting by transmitting messages through channels.
The meaning of a DP concept is explained in section

2.4.3
.

If
P
is a DP of the form
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
, then

•

{\it Var}_{P}=\bigcup_{i\in I}{\it Var}_{P_{i}}
,

X_{P}=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{P_{i}}
,

\bar{X}_{P}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\bar{X}_{P_{i}}
,

•

if
\zeta
is a renaming, then

–

the notation

P^{\zeta}
denotes the DP
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}^{\zeta}
,

–

if
{\it Var}_{P}\subseteq Dom(\zeta)
,
then
P
and
P^{\zeta}

are assumed to be the same,

•

P
can be denoted by the notation

–

(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
,
if
I=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, or

–

Q^{\infty}
, if
I
is
a set of natural numbers, and all SPs in the family

P_{I}
coincide with the SP
Q
.

If
P_{I}=\{P_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a family of DPs,
and each DP
P_{i}
in
P_{I}
corresponds to a family of SPs

\{Q_{i^{\prime}}\mid{i^{\prime}\in I_{i}}\}
,
where the sets
I_{i}\;(i\in I)
are disjoint
(if this is not the case, then
we will replace these sets with appropriate disjunctive copies),
then the notation
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
denotes
a DP corresponding to the family of SPs

\{Q_{i}\mid i\in\bigsqcup_{i\in I}{I_{i}}\}.

If DP
P
has the form

\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
, then the notation
P^{*}

denotes the DP
\prod_{i\in I\sqcup\{*\}}P_{i}
,
where
P_{*}
is the adversary process.

2.4.2
A concept of a state of a
distributed process

Let
P
be a DP of the form
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
.

A
state
of

is a pair
S
of the following objects:

•

a set
\{s^{S}_{P_{i}}\mid i\in I\}
of
states of SPs from
P_{I}
,

•

a
channel state
:
M^{S}=\{M^{S}_{c}\subseteq Tm\mid c\in Channels\}
.

A state

of DP

is said to be
initial
,
and is denoted by
\odot
, if

\forall\,i\in I\;\;s^{S}_{P_{i}}=\odot,\quad\forall\,c\in Channels\;\;M^{S}_{c}=\emptyset.

If
S
is a state of the DP
P=\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
,
and
i\in I
, then

•

notations
v_{P_{i}}^{S}
,
\alpha_{P_{i}}^{S}
,

X_{P_{i}}^{S}
,
\theta_{P_{i}}^{S}
denote the corresponding
components of the state

,

•

notation
V^{S}
denotes the set

\{v_{P_{i}}^{S}\mid i\in I\}
,

•

notation

denotes a binding, such that

\forall\,i\in I,\;\forall\,x\in X_{P_{i}}^{S}\quad\theta^{S}(x)=\theta^{S}_{P_{i}}(x).

2.4.3
An execution of a distributed process

Let
P
be a DP of the form
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
.

An
execution
of
P

can be understood as non-deterministic interliving
of executions of SPs from
P_{I}
.
At each step of an execution of
P

•

at most one SP from
P_{I}
performs its current action, and

•

other SPs from
P_{I}
are in the waiting status.

An execution of a DP
P
can be formally defined as a
generation of a sequence of states of this
DP (starting with
\odot
), in which each state
S
that is not terminal, is associated with the next state

S^{\prime}

by a
transition relation
, which means the following:

\exists\,i\in I
:

\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}s^{S}_{P_{i}}\to s^{S^{\prime}}_{P_{i}},\quad\forall\,i^{\prime}\in I\setminus\{i\}\quad s^{S^{\prime}}_{P_{i^{\prime}}}=s^{S}_{P_{i^{\prime}}},\quad\mbox{and if $s^{S^{\prime}}_{P_{i}}=(v,\alpha,X,\theta)$, then}\\
\mbox{if
$\alpha=c!e$, then }\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}M^{S^{\prime}}_{c^{\theta}}=M^{S}_{c^{\theta}}\cup\{e^{\theta}\},\\
M^{S^{\prime}}_{c^{\prime}}=M^{S}_{c^{\prime}}\mbox{ when $c^{\prime}\neq c^{\theta}$}\end{array}\right\},\mbox{otherwise $M^{S^{\prime}}=M^{S}$.}\end{array}

(3)

For each states
S,S^{\prime}
of DP
P

•

S\to S^{\prime}
means that

S
is related with
S^{\prime}
by a transition relation,

•

S\;\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\!\alpha_{P_{i}}}\;S^{\prime}
means that

S\to S^{\prime}
, and (
3
) holds,

•

S\Rightarrow S^{\prime}
means that either
S=S^{\prime}
,
or there is a sequence

S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}
of states of
P
,
such that

S_{0}=S,\quad S_{n}=S^{\prime},\quad\forall\,i=0,\ldots,n-1\quad S_{i}\to S_{i+1}.

A state
S
of
P
is said to be
reachable
,
if
\odot\Rightarrow S
.

The set of reachable states of
P
is
denoted by
\Sigma_{P}
.

2.6
Transition graphs of distributed processes

2.6.1
A concept of a transition graph
of a distributed process

Let
P
be a DP of the form
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
.

A
transition graph (TG)
of DP
P

is a graph
G_{P}
such that

•

a set of nodes of
G_{P}
is the Cartesian product
of the sets of nodes of graphs from
P_{I}
,
i.e.
each node of
G_{P}
is a family of nodes

V=\{v_{i}\mid i\in I\},\mbox{ where }\forall\,i\in I\;\;v_{i}\in P_{i},

•

each edge of
G_{P}
has the form

\{v_{i}\mid i\in I\}\;\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\!\alpha_{P_{i}}}\;\{v^{\prime}_{i}\mid i\in I\},

(11)

where

P_{i}
has the edge
v_{i}\;\mathop{\to}\limits^{\begin{picture}\put(-3.0,2.0){\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,1.0){$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}\end{picture}}\;v^{\prime}_{i}
and

\forall\,i^{\prime}\in I\setminus\{i\}\quad v_{i^{\prime}}=v^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}
.

The node

\{P^{0}_{i}\mid i\in I\}\in G_{P}
is said to be
an
initial
node of
G_{P}
, and is denoted by
G^{0}_{P}
.
An edge
V\;\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\!\alpha_{P_{i}}}\;V^{\prime}
is said to be a

realizable
edge, if
\exists\,S,S^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{P}
:

V=V^{S}
and
V^{\prime}=V^{S^{\prime}}
.

It is not difficult to prove that if
\forall\,i\in I\;P_{i}
is acyclic, then
G_{P}
is acyclic.

For each DP
P
the graph
G_{P^{*}}
can be considered
as a completion of the graph
G_{P}
with cyclic edges corresponding to the actions of
P_{*}
.

If DP
P
has the form
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
,
then the following conventions will be used
in a graphical representation of
G_{P}
:

•

each node

V=\{v_{i}\mid i=1,\ldots,n\}
of
G_{P}

is represented by an oval,
there is a list
v_{1}\ldots v_{n}

of components of
V
inside this oval,

•

an initial node
G^{0}_{P}
is represented by a double oval.

2.7
Values of expressions and formulas in states of distributed processes

2.7.1
A concept of a value of an expression and a formula in a state of a distributed process

Let there are given the DP

P=\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
,
the state
S\in\Sigma_{P}
,
the expression
E\in Expr
,
and the formula
\beta\in Fm
.

The notation
E^{S}
denotes a subset of the set

Tm
, called a
value of the expression

E
in the state
S
, and defined as follows:

•

if
E\subseteq Tm
, then
E^{S}=E^{\theta^{S}}
,

•

if
E=X_{P}
, then
E^{S}=(X_{P}^{S})^{\theta^{S}}
,

•

if
E=M_{c}
, then
E^{S}=M_{c^{\theta^{S}}}^{S}
,

•

if
E=k^{-1}(E^{\prime})
, then
E^{S}=\{e\in Tm\mid\exists\,e^{\prime}\in(E^{\prime})^{S}:k^{\theta^{S}}(e)\subseteq e^{\prime}\}
,

•

(E\cap E^{\prime})^{S}=E^{S}\cap(E^{\prime})^{S}
,

(E\cup E^{\prime})^{S}=E^{S}\cup(E^{\prime})^{S}
,

(\neg E)^{S}=Tm\setminus E^{S}
.

The notation

S\models\beta
denotes the statement

\beta
holds in
S
, which
is true iff one of the following cases holds:

•

–

\beta=(E=E^{\prime})
,
(E\subseteq E^{\prime})
, or
(E\supseteq E^{\prime})
,
where
E,E^{\prime}\in Expr
, and

–

E^{S}=(E^{\prime})^{S}
,

E^{S}\subseteq(E^{\prime})^{S}
, or

E^{S}\supseteq(E^{\prime})^{S}
, respectively,

•

–

\beta=(x\,\bot\,P_{i})
, where

x\in{\it Var}
,
i\in I
, and

–

\forall\,e\in(X_{P_{i}}^{S})^{\theta^{S}}\;\;x\not\in{\it Var}_{e},

•

–

\beta=(x\,\bot\,C)
,
where
x\in{\it Var}
,
C\subseteq Channels
, and

–

\forall\,c\in C,\;\forall\,e\in M^{S}_{c}\;\;x\not\in{\it Var}_{e},

•

–

\beta=(k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i})
, where

k\in{\it Keys}
,
i\in I
, and

–

\forall\,e\in(X_{P_{i}}^{S})^{\theta^{S}}

each occurrence of
k
in
e
is hidden,

•

–

\beta=(k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,C)
,
where
k\in{\it Keys}
,
C\subseteq Channels
, and

–

\forall\,c\in C,\;\forall\,e\in M^{S}_{c}

each occurrence of
k
in
e
is hidden,

•

\beta=\{\beta_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a family of EFs,

\forall\,i\in I\;\;S\models\beta_{i}
.

2.7.2
Theorems on preserving
values of formulas under transitions

Below we prove theorems that some formulas have the same values in states related by a transition relation.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P=\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
be a DP
and
S,S^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{P}
be states such that

\exists\,i\in I:\;S\;\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\!\alpha_{P_{i}}}\;S^{\prime}.

Then the implication

S\models\beta\;\Rightarrow\;S^{\prime}\models\beta

holds,
where
\beta
is a formula of one of the following forms:

1.

\beta=\{x\,\bot\,P_{i},x\,\bot\,Channels\}
, where
x\in X_{P}
,

2.

\beta=\{k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i},k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,Channels\}
, where
k\in X_{P_{i}}^{\bf K}
.

Proof
.

1.

Let
\beta=\{x\,\bot\,P_{i},x\,\bot\,Channels\}
, where
x\in X_{P}
.

S\models\beta
means that

\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\forall\,y\in X_{P_{i}}^{S}\quad x\not\in{\it Var}_{y^{\theta^{S}}}\\
\forall\,c\in Channels,\forall\,e\in M^{S}_{c}\quad x\not\in{\it Var}_{e}.\end{array}\right\}

(15)

It is required to prove that
(
15
) implies
S^{\prime}\models\beta
, i.e.

\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\forall\,y\in X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}\quad x\not\in{\it Var}_{y^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}}\\
\forall\,c\in Channels,\forall\,e\in M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}\quad x\not\in{\it Var}_{e}.\end{array}\right\}

(16)

If first statement in (
16
) is wrong,
then first statement in (
15
)
implies that
X_{P_{i}}^{S}\neq X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}
.
This is only possible if

\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{
$\alpha$ is of the form $c?e$,
$X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}=X_{P_{i}}^{S}\cup{\it Var}_{e}$,}\\
\mbox{ $e^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}\in M^{S}_{c^{\theta^{S}}}$,
and $\exists\,y\in{\it Var}_{e}:x\in y^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}\;\;(\Rightarrow\;x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}})$.}\end{array}

(17)

(
17
) contradicts
second statement in (
15
).

If second statement in (
16
)
is wrong, then second statement in
(
15
) implies that
\exists\,c\in Channels:M^{S}_{c}\neq M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}
.
This is only possible if

\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{$\alpha$
has the form $c^{\prime}!e$, where $(c^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}=c$,
and $e\in Tm(X_{P_{i}}^{S})$,}\\
\mbox{$M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}=M^{S}_{c}\cup\{e^{\theta^{S}}\}$,
and $x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\theta^{S}}}$.}\end{array}\right\}

(18)

Denote by symbols
X
and
\theta

the set
X_{P_{i}}^{S}
and the binding
\theta^{S}
,
respectively.
From (
18
) it follows that

e\in Tm(X)
and

x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\theta}}
.

From
x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\theta}}
it follows that

\exists\,y\in{\it Var}_{e}:x\in{\it Var}_{y^{\theta}}
.

From
e\in Tm(X)
and
y\in{\it Var}_{e}

it follows that

y\in X
, so

y^{\theta}\in X^{\theta}
.

Thus, we get the statements

y^{\theta}\in X^{\theta},\;\;x\in{\it Var}_{y^{\theta}}

that contradict first statement in
(
15
).

2.

Let
\beta=\{k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i},k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,Channels\}
, where
k\in X_{P_{i}}^{\bf K}
.

S\models\beta
means that

\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{ $\forall\,x\in X_{P_{i}}^{S}$
each occurrence of $k$ in $x^{\theta^{S}}$ is
hidden,}\\
\mbox{ $\forall\,c\in Channels,\;\forall\,e\in M^{S}_{c}$
each occurrence of $k$ in $e$ is hidden.}\\
\end{array}\right\}

(19)

It is required to prove that (
19
)
implies
S^{\prime}\models\beta
, i.e.

\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{ $\forall\,x\in X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}$
each occurrence of $k$ in $x^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}$ is
hidden,}\\
\mbox{ $\forall\,c\in Channels,\;\forall\,e\in M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}$
each occurrence of $k$ in $e$ is hidden.}\\
\end{array}\right\}

(20)

If first statement in (
20
)
is wrong, then first statement in
(
19
) implies that

X_{P_{i}}^{S}\neq X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}
.
This is possible in the following two cases:

(a)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{
$\alpha$ is of the form $c?e$,
$X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}=X_{P_{i}}^{S}\cup{\it Var}_{e}$,}\\
\mbox{ $e^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}\in M^{S}_{c^{\theta^{S}}}$, and
$\exists\,y\in{\it Var}_{e}$:}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{
$\exists$ unhidden occurrence of
$\mbox{$k$ in $y^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}$,}$}\end{array}\right.

(b)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{
$\alpha$ is of the form $e:=e^{\prime}$,
$X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}=X_{P_{i}}^{S}\cup{\it Var}_{e}$,}\\
\mbox{ $e^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}=(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}$, and
$\exists\,y\in{\it Var}_{e}$:
}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt\mbox{
$\exists$ unhidden occurrence of
$\mbox{$k$ in $y^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}$.}$}\end{array}\right.

In case
2(b)i

\exists
unhidden occurrence of
k
in

e^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}
,
that contradicts second statement
in (
19
).

In case
2(b)ii
the following is true:

\exists\,e^{\prime}\in Tm(X_{P_{i}}^{S}):\exists
unhidden occurrence of

k
in
(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}
.

(21)

However, according to first statement in (
19
),

\forall\,x\in X_{P_{i}}^{S}
each occurrence of
k
in
x^{\theta^{S}}
is hidden, whence by induction
on the structure of
e^{\prime}
it is easy to prove that
(
21
) is false.

If second statement in (
20
) is wrong, then second statement in (
19
) implies that
\exists\,c\in Channels:M^{S}_{c}\neq M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}
.
This is only possible if

\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{$\alpha$ has the form
$c^{\prime}!e$, where $(c^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}=c$,
and $e\in Tm(X_{P_{i}}^{S})$,}\\
\mbox{$M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}=M^{S}_{c}\cup\{e^{\theta^{S}}\}$,
and $\exists$ an unhidden occurrence of
$k$ in $e^{\theta^{S}}$.}\end{array}

(22)

As in previous case, we prove
by induction on the structure of
e

that each occurrence of

k
in
e^{\theta^{S}}
is hidden
(for the base of induction we use first statement in
(
19
)) that
contradicts the last statement in
(
22
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
P=\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
be a DP,
and

be states such that

S\models\beta\;\Rightarrow\;S^{\prime}\models\beta
holds,
where
\beta
is a formula of one of the following two forms:

\{c\,\bot\,P_{i},c\,\bot\,Channels,M_{c}=E\},\mbox{ where $c\in X_{P}^{\bf C}$, }

(23)

\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i},\;k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,Channels,\\
k^{-1}(M_{c})\subseteq E\quad(\forall\,c\in Channels),\\
k^{-1}(X_{P_{i}})\subseteq E\end{array}\right\},\mbox{where }k\in X_{P}^{\bf K},E\subseteq Tm(X_{P}).

(24)

Proof
.

1.

Let

\beta
has the form
(
23
).

According to theorem
2.7.2
,
if first two EFs occurred in
\beta
hold in
S
,
then these EFs hold in
S^{\prime}
as well.

Thus, to prove
S^{\prime}\models\beta

it suffices to prove the implication

S\models\{c\,\bot\,P_{i},c\,\bot\,Channels,M_{c}=E\}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;S^{\prime}\models M_{c}=E.

(25)

If the conclusion of implication
(
25
) does not hold,
then the sets

M^{S}_{c}
and
M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}
are different.
This is possible only if

\alpha
is of the form
c^{\prime}!e
, where
c=(c^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}

and
c^{\prime}\in X^{S}_{P_{i}}
.
However,
S\models c\,\bot\,P_{i}
implies that

c\not\in{\it Var}_{(c^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}}
,
i. e.
c\not\in\{c\}
, which is impossible.

2.

Let
\beta
has the form (
24
).

According to theorem
2.7.2
,
if first two EFs occurred in
\beta
hold in
S
,
then these EFs hold in
S^{\prime}
as well.

Thus, to prove
S^{\prime}\models\beta

it suffices to prove the implication

\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\left.\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}S\models k^{-1}(M_{c})\subseteq E\quad(\forall\,c\in Channels)\\
S\models k^{-1}(X_{P_{i}})\subseteq E\\
S\models\{k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i},k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,Channels\}\end{array}\right\}\Rightarrow\\
\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}S^{\prime}\models k^{-1}(M_{c})\subseteq E\quad(\forall\,c\in Channels)\\
S^{\prime}\models k^{-1}(X_{P_{i}})\subseteq E\end{array}\right.\end{array}

(26)

(a)

If first statement in the conclusion of implication (
26
) is wrong, then
\exists\,c\in Channels:S^{\prime}\not\models k^{-1}(M_{c})\subseteq E
.

From first statement in the premise of implication (
26
)
it follows that this is possible
only if

\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}\mbox{$\alpha$
has the form $c^{\prime}!e^{\prime}$, where
$c=(c^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}},e^{\prime}\in Tm(X_{P_{i}}^{S})$, }\\
\mbox{$M^{S^{\prime}}_{c}=M^{S}_{c}\cup\{(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}\}$,
with
$\exists\,k(e)\subseteq(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S}}$:
$e\not\in E$.}\end{array}

The term
e^{\prime}
does not contain
k
,
because
e^{\prime}\in Tm(X_{P_{i}}^{S})
,
and if
e^{\prime}
contains
k
, then
k\in X_{P_{i}}^{S}
,
which contradicts the assumption

S\models k\,\bot_{\bf K}\,P_{i}
in the premise of
implication
(
26
).

Thus,
\exists\,x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\prime}}\subseteq X_{P_{i}}^{S}
,

\exists\,k(e)\subseteq x^{\theta^{S}}
,
and
e\not\in E
.

However, this contradicts the statement

S\models k^{-1}(X_{P_{i}})\subseteq E

in the premise of implication (
26
).

(b)

If second statement in the conclusion of implication
(
26
)
does not hold,
then from second statement in the premise of implication
(
26
) it follows that

X_{P_{i}}^{S}\neq X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}
, and

\exists\,x\in X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}:\exists\,e\not\in E:k(e)\subseteq x^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}.

(27)

This is possible in two cases:

i.

\alpha=c?e^{\prime}
, in this case

X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}=X_{P_{i}}^{S}\cup{\it Var}_{e^{\prime}},\;\;x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\prime}},\;\;(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}\in M^{S}_{c^{\theta^{S}}}.

(28)

Let
c^{\prime}=c^{\theta^{S}}
.

According to first statement in the premise of implication
(
26
),

S\models k^{-1}(M_{c^{\prime}})\subseteq E
,
so the following implication holds:

(29)

The premise of implication (
29
)
holds when
\tilde{e}=(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}
,
this follows from the last statement in (

) and from

(

)
holds, which contradicts the statement

in (

).

ii.

\alpha=(e^{\prime}:=e^{\prime\prime})
, in this case

\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}X_{P_{i}}^{S^{\prime}}=X_{P_{i}}^{S}\cup{\it Var}_{e^{\prime}},\;\;x\in{\it Var}_{e^{\prime}},\\
e^{\prime\prime}\in Tm(X^{S}_{P_{i}}),\;\;(e^{\prime})^{\theta^{S^{\prime}}}=(e^{\prime\prime})^{\theta^{S}}.\end{array}

(30)

According to second statement
in the premise of implication
(

),

,

Since

The term

2.8
Marking of a transition graph

2.8.1
A concept of a marking of a transition graph

Let
P
be a DP of the form
\prod_{i\in I}P_{i}
.

A
marking
of the TG
G_{P}
is a pair

(G,\{\beta_{V}\in Fm\mid V\in G\})

(35)

where
G
is a subset of the set of nodes of
G_{P}
,
such that

•

G^{0}_{P}\in G
, and

•

\forall\,V\in G
, if
G_{P}

has an edge of the form
V^{\prime}\to V
, then
V^{\prime}\in G
.

Marking (
35
) is said to be

correct
, if

•

G^{0}_{P}\models\beta_{G^{0}_{P}}
, and

•

\forall\,S,S^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{P}
, if
S\to S^{\prime}

and
V^{S},V^{S^{\prime}}\in G
, then the following
implication holds:

S\models\beta_{V^{S}}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;S^{\prime}\models\beta_{V^{S^{\prime}}}.

It was noted in section
2.6.1
that
for each DP
P
the graph
G_{P^{*}}

is a completion of the graph
G_{P}

with cyclic edges corresponding to actions of the
adversary
P_{*}
.
Therefore, for each DP
P
, any marking of the TG

G_{P}

can also be considered as a marking
of the corresponding TG
G_{P^{*}}
.

Below, a marking of any TG
G_{P}
is said to be
correct, if it is a correct marking (in the sense of the above definition) of the corresponding TG
G_{P^{*}}
.

2.8.3
Application of markings of
transition graphs in the problems of verification of
distributed processes

An

execution
of a DP
P
is a
sequence
S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}
of states from

\Sigma_{P}
, such as
S_{0}=\odot
, and
either
n=0
, or

\forall\,i=0,\ldots,n-1\quad S_{i}\to S_{i+1}
.

It is not difficult to see that each such sequence
S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}
corresponds to a path

G^{0}_{P}=V^{S_{0}}\to\ldots\to V^{S_{n}}
in TG
G_{P}
.

Some correctness properties of DPs have the following form:
in each state
S
of an arbitrary execution of a DP
P
, the following implication holds:

S\models\beta\;\Rightarrow\;S\models\beta^{\prime},\mbox{
where $\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in Fm$ are given formulas.}

(37)

For example, for the DP
P^{*}
, where
P

is any of the DPs presented in section

2.5.2
,
one of the correctness properties has the following form:
for arbitrary execution
S_{0},\ldots,S_{n}
of this DP,

•

if

–

S_{n}\models(v^{S_{n}}_{B}=B^{1})

(for first and second DPs in section

2.5.2
), or

–

S_{n}\models(v^{S_{n}}_{B}=B^{2})

(for third and fourth
DPs in section
2.5.2
),

i.e. if
B
executed an action of receiving
the message sent by
A
and wrote the received message in
variable
y
,

•

then
S_{n}\models(x=y)
, i.e. the received message is the same as the message
x
that
A
sent
B
.

Properties of the form (
37
)
can be verified using a marking of TG
G_{P}

of an analyzed DP
P
as follows:

•

a correct marking

(G,\{\beta_{V}\in Fm\mid V\in G\})
of
G_{P}

is being built, and

•

for each node

V\in G
, such that

\beta_{V}
implies
\beta
,
the implication
\beta_{V}\Rightarrow\beta^{\prime}

is being checked.

To check the above statements, there is no need
to fully build the TG
G_{P}
of the analyzed DP
P
.
It is convenient to build the TG together with
the construction of its marking as follows: if a formula

\beta_{V}
is built to mark the node

V
of
G_{P}
, and
\beta_{V}

implies an unrealizability of some edge outgoing from
V
, then this edge is discarded.
For example, this can happen if

•

a label of an edge outgoing from
V

is of the form
(c?\hat{y})_{B}
, and

•

\beta_{V}
contains
the conjunctive term
M_{c}=\emptyset
.

As a result of such a construction with discarding unrealizable edges, a fragment of the TG
G_{P}
will be obtained.
We shall call such fragment a
reduced TG
.

It is not difficult to see that the reduced TG preserves
all the properties of the TG
G_{P}
. In particular, for the solution of the verification problem described above for a property of the form (
37
), the corresponding reduced TG can be used instead of the TG
G_{P}
.

4
Conclusion

In the present work, a new model of cryptographic protocols was built, and examples of its use for solving problems
of verification of protocol integrity properties are shown.

The objectives for further development of this model and verification methods based on it are the following:

1.

an automation of synthesis of suitable markings in
transition graphs of the analyzed protocols,

2.

development of the language of specification of properties of cryptographic protocols, which allow to express e.g.

•

properties of confidentiality (secrecy) of transmitted messages, i.e. the adversary’s inability to extract any new information about the content of messages intercepted by him,

•

matching properties in authentication protocols, or zero knowledge properties,

•

non-traceability properties in electronic payments,

•

properties of correctness of the votes’ counting in voting protocols,

3.

construction of automated synthesis
methods of cryptographic protocols
by describing the properties which the
cryptographic protocols must satisfy, etc.
