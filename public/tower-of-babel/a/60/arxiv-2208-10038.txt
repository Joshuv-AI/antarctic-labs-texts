Magnetotropic susceptibility.
A. Shekhter, R. D. McDonald, B. J. Ramshaw, K. A. Modic

Abstract
The magnetotropic susceptibility is the thermodynamic coefficient associated with the rotational anisotropy of the free energy in an external magnetic field, and is closely related to the magnetic susceptibility. It emerges naturally in frequency-shift measurements of oscillating mechanical cantilevers, which are becoming an increasingly important tool in the quantitative study of the thermodynamics of modern condensed matter systems. Here we discuss the basic properties of the magnetotropic susceptibility as they relate to the experimental aspects of frequency-shift measurements, as well as to the interpretation of those experiments in terms of the intrinsic properties of the system under study.

A frequency shift in a mechanical oscillator can be induced by the oscillating linear motion of a sample in a non-uniform magnetic field or by the oscillating rotational motion in a uniform applied magnetic field. Small frequency shifts are also accompanied by resonance width broadening associated with relaxation phenomena in the sample coupled to either the rotational- or linear-oscillating motion of the sample.

In this paper, we focus on the
magnetotropic susceptibility
that captures the changes in the free energy of a magnetically anisotropic sample associated with its rotation in a uniform magnetic field. The frequency shift of the resonance of the cantilever-sample assembly in a uniform external magnetic field is captured entirely by the magnetotropic susceptibility of the sample. Dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility captures the relaxation phenomena coupled to the rotation of the sample in an applied magnetic field.

In Section
II

After that, we discuss the dynamic, or frequency-dependent, magnetotropic susceptibility which encapsulates relaxation phenomena in the sample induced by its rotation in an applied magnetic field. Dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility is accessed directly in the measurements of the width of the resonance of the cantilever induced by the relaxation in the sample.

In Section
III

Finally, in Section
IV

II
The magnetotropic susceptibility.

II.1
Definition and basic properties.

The free energy of a magnetically anisotropic sample depends on the direction of the magnetic field with respect to the crystallographic directions of the sample. One can study a “slice” of the overall angular dependence of the free energy,
F(θ_{{\bm{n}}},{\bm{B}})
, where the magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
rotates around axis
{\bm{n}}
without change in its magnitude. The angular variable
θ_{{\bm{n}}}
considered as a thermodynamic parameter defines the magnetic torque,
{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})
:

\displaystyle dF({\bm{B}})={T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})\;dθ_{{\bm{n}}}\,,\quad{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=-{\bm{M}}⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})\,.

(1)

Magnetic torque is determined by the magnetization
{\bm{M}}=-dF/d{\bm{B}}
induced by a small change in magnetic field
δ_{dθ{\bm{n}}}{\bm{B}}=({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})\,dθ_{{\bm{n}}}
that is incurred by a small rotation
dθ_{{\bm{n}}}
around axis
{\bm{n}}
(Figure
1
).

The diagonal thermodynamic coefficient
Callen 1985
associated with the thermodynamic parameter
θ_{{\bm{n}}}
is the magnetotropic susceptibility,

\displaystyle d{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})\;dθ_{{\bm{n}}}\,,

\displaystyle k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})\!=\!({\bm{n}}\!×\!{\bm{B}})\!⋅\!({\bm{n}}\!×\!{\bm{M}})\!-\!({\bm{n}}\!×\!{\bm{B}})\!⋅\!\hat{χ}({\bm{B}})\!⋅\!({\bm{n}}\!×\!{\bm{B}})\,,

(2)

where
\hat{χ}_{μν}({\bm{B}})=-{dM_{μ}}/{dB_{ν}}
is the magnetic susceptibility tensor.
The two terms in the second line of Eq. (
II.1
) have a different physical character, which will become apparent in the discussion of correlation functions in Sections
II.4
and
II.5
. The explicit dependence of the magnetic torque
{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=-{\bm{M}}⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})
on the rotation of magnetic field produces the first term in Eq. (
II.1
),
-{\bm{M}}⋅({\bm{n}}×({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}}))
. The implicit dependence of torque on magnetic field through changes in the magnetization produces the second term.

It will be convenient to rewrite Eq. (
II.1
) in a matrix form using generators of rotation in vector representation,
i({\hat{S}}_{μ})_{ij}=ϵ_{μij}
, satisfying the commutation relations
[{\hat{S}}_{i},{\hat{S}}_{j}]=iϵ_{ijk}{\hat{S}}_{k}
where
[{\hat{S}}_{i},{\hat{S}}_{j}]≡{\hat{S}}_{i}⋅{\hat{S}}_{j}-{\hat{S}}_{j}⋅{\hat{S}}_{i}
,

\displaystyle{\hat{S}}_{1}\!=\!i\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,{\hat{S}}_{2}\!=\!i\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
-1&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,{\hat{S}}_{3}\!=\!i\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\,.

(3)

Rotation of a vector represented by the cross product
{\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}}

II.1

\displaystyle δ_{θ{\bm{n}}}\bm{B}=({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})dθ=(i{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!{\bm{B}})dθ\,,

\displaystyle\quad i{\hat{N}}=-i{\hat{S}}_{μ}n_{μ}=\left[\begin{matrix}0&-n_{z}&n_{y}\\
n_{z}&0&-n_{x}\\
-n_{y}&n_{x}&0\end{matrix}\right]\,.

(4)

In this representation, the magnetotropic susceptibility takes the form

\displaystyle k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})\!=\!{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!{\bm{M}}-{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!\hat{χ}\!⋅\!{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}\,.

(5)

The magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})
, as well as the magnetic torque
{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})
, describes only a slice of the complete angular dependence of the free energy.
For arbitrary rotation slice
{\bm{n}}
, magnetic torque is specified by a scalar product of an axial vector
\bm{{T}}={\bm{M}}×{\bm{B}}
and vector
{\bm{n}}

{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=\bm{{T}}_{μ}{\bm{n}}_{μ}

Equations (
II.1
) and (
5

k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})
as a bilinear function of the components of the vector
{\bm{n}}
. The generalization of the torque vector
\bm{{T}}
to the magnetotropic susceptibility is a symmetric second-rank tensor
k_{μν}({\bm{B}})

\displaystyle k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=

\displaystyle k_{μν}({\bm{B}})n_{ν}n_{μ}\,.

(6)

The magnetotropic susceptibility tensor
k_{μν}({\bm{B}})
encodes the second angular derivative of the free energy for all slice directions
{\bm{n}}
, just like the torque vector
\bm{{T}}
encodes its first derivative. Tensor components of

follow from Eqs. (
II.1

5

\displaystyle k_{μν}({\bm{B}})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!({\hat{S}}_{μ}\!⋅\!{\hat{S}}_{ν}+{\hat{S}}_{ν}\!⋅\!{\hat{S}}_{μ})\!⋅\!{\bm{M}}

\displaystyle-\,\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!({\hat{S}}_{μ}\!⋅\!\hat{χ}\!⋅\!{\hat{S}}_{ν}+{\hat{S}}_{ν}\!⋅\!\hat{χ}\!⋅\!{\hat{S}}_{μ})\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}\,.

(7)

The first line can also be written as
δ_{μν}({\bm{B}}\!⋅\!{\bm{M}})-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(B_{μ}M_{ν}+B_{ν}M_{μ})
.

The product
{\hat{N}}\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}
in Eq. (

in Eq. (
II.1

{\bm{n}}
along the magnetic field

{\bm{n}}
along magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
have no effect on the magnetic part of the free energy. Therefore, the product
k_{μν}({\bm{B}})n_{μ}n_{ν}
vanishes for
{\bm{n}}
along
{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle k_{μν}({\bm{B}}){\bm{B}}_{μ}{\bm{B}}_{ν}≡0\,,

(8)

because

\bm{{T}}_{μ}{\bm{B}}_{μ}≡0
. For torque, this identity means that the magnetic torque vector lives in a plane perpendicular to vector
{\bm{B}}
. Similarly, the six components of the symmetric magnetotropic susceptibility tensor
k_{μν}({\bm{B}})
are constrained by one condition, Eq. (
8

In the linear regime, the magnetotropic susceptibility is bilinear in the components of magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
. The magnetization
{\bm{M}}=\hat{χ}^{0}\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}
is linear in magnetic field and linear magnetic susceptibility
\hat{χ}^{0}
is independent of magnetic field. Equation (
5

\displaystyle k_{{\bm{n}}}^{0}({\bm{B}})\!=\!{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!{\hat{N}}\!⋅\![{\hat{N}},\hat{χ}^{0}]\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}\,,

(9)

and Eq. (
II.1
) to

\displaystyle k_{μν}^{0}({\bm{B}})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{2}{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!({\hat{S}}_{μ}\!⋅\![{\hat{S}}_{ν},\hat{χ}^{0}]+{\hat{S}}_{ν}\!⋅\![{\hat{S}}_{μ},\hat{χ}^{0}])\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}\,.

(10)

If we choose the axes
x,y,z
along the crystallographic directions
a,b,c
(Figure
1
)—or the principal directions of magnetic susceptibility
{χ}
when the crystal symmetry is lower than orthorhombic—then the linear magnetic susceptibility tensor

is diagonal
\hat{χ}^{0}=\text{diag}\{χ^{0}_{xx},χ^{0}_{yy},χ^{0}_{zz}\}
. The full magnetotropic tensor, Eq. (
10

\displaystyle k_{μν}^{0}({\bm{B}})={\begin{pmatrix}[r](B_{y}^{2}-B_{z}^{2})(χ^{0}_{yy}-χ^{0}_{zz})&-B_{x}B_{y}(\nicefrac{{χ^{0}_{xx}}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{χ^{0}_{yy}}}{{2}}-χ^{0}_{zz})&-B_{x}B_{z}(\nicefrac{{χ^{0}_{xx}}}{{2}}-χ^{0}_{yy}+\nicefrac{{χ^{0}_{zz}}}{{2}})\\

(11)

Note that only two combinations of the components of the linear magnetic susceptibility in this expression are linearly independent. Therefore, the linear magnetotropic tensor
k_{μν}^{0}({\bm{B}})
determines two (out of three) independent components of linear magnetic susceptibility, consistent with the more general discussion around Eq. (
8

For example, a slice of rotations around the

\displaystyle k_{\hat{y}}^{0}({\bm{B}})=

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!{\hat{S}}_{2}\!⋅\![{\hat{S}}_{2},\hat{χ}^{0}]\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{B}}\!⋅\!\left[\begin{matrix}χ_{xx}^{0}-χ_{zz}^{0}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-(χ_{xx}^{0}-χ_{zz}^{0})\end{matrix}\right]\!⋅\!{\bm{B}}\,.

(12)

Note the vanishing eigenvalue corresponding to direction
\hat{y}

In components,

\displaystyle k_{\hat{y}}^{0}({\bm{B}})\!=\!(B_{c}^{2}\!-\!B_{a}^{2})\,(χ_{c}^{0}\!-\!χ_{a}^{0})=B_{ac}^{2}\,(χ_{c}^{0}\!-\!χ_{a}^{0})\cos\!2θ\,,

The linear magnetotropic coefficient

Equation (

For completeness, we can also consider the magnetotropic susceptibility of a polycrystalline sample. The free energy of a polycrystalline sample is isotropic,
F({\bm{B}})=F_{{\rm{iso}}}(|{\bm{B}}|)
, where
F_{{\rm{iso}}}(|{\bm{B}}|)
is the average of the anisotropic free energy over all crystal lattice orientations.
The magnetotropic susceptibility of a polycrystal is determined by the angular derivatives of
F_{{\rm{iso}}}(|{\bm{B}}|)
and therefore is zero.
When considering Eq. (
II.1
) or Eq. (
5
) in a polycrystalline sample, the vanishing of the magnetotropic susceptibility is not immediately clear because it arises as a cancellation between the first and second terms. Such cancellation is obvious in the linear regime, Eq. (
9
), where one has to replace the linear magnetic susceptibility
\hat{χ}^{0}
with its polycrystalline average, proportional to the unit matrix.

In the non-linear regime, the character of the first and second terms in Eq. (
II.1
) and Eq. (
5
) is quite different, and the cancellation is not evident. It is instructive, though, to track the cancellation starting from a non-linear magnetization and a non-linear magnetic susceptibility,

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}=-\frac{dF}{d{\bm{B}}}=-\frac{1}{B}\frac{dF}{dB}{\bm{B}}\,,

(14)

\displaystyle{χ}_{μν}=\frac{d{\bm{M}}_{μ}}{d{\bm{B}}_{ν}}=\left(\frac{1}{B}\frac{d(M/B)}{dB}\right){\bm{B}}_{μ}{\bm{B}}_{ν}+\frac{M}{B}δ_{μν}\,,

(15)

where
B=|{\bm{B}}|
and
M=|{\bm{M}}|
.
The first term in Eq. (
15
) is proportional to the projector matrix in the direction of the magnetic field. Therefore, the non-linear magnetic susceptibility
{χ}_{μν}
of a polycrystalline sample is a uniaxial tensor with magnetic field as a symmetry axis. This projector part of the magnetic susceptibility is projected out when substituted into Eq. (
II.1
), and, therefore, does not affect the magnetotropic susceptibility.
The second term in Eq. (
15
) is isotropic. When substituted into Eq. (
II.1
), it cancels with the first term in Eq. (
II.1
).

It will be shown in Section
II.5
that the relaxation phenomena induced by the rotation in an applied magnetic field are captured by the imaginary part of the dynamic (frequency dependent) magnetotropic susceptibility. In the polycrystalline sample, these dissipative phenomena are induced independently in each single-crystal grain and their total effect on the dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility does not add up to zero. Therefore, the polycrystalline sample has a non-zero imaginary part of the dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility (Section
II.5
) and, by analyticity, a non-zero real part at finite frequency.

II.3
Magnetotropic susceptibility as a thermodynamic coefficient

Magnetotropic susceptibility is a thermodynamic coefficient, the second derivative of the free energy. As such, its behavior across the thermodynamic phase boundaries is constrained by general thermodynamic considerations.
Callen 1985
For example rotation of the sample in the applied magnetic field is described with a matrix of thermodynamic coefficients

\displaystyle{\begin{pmatrix}[r]dS\\
d{T}_{{\bm{n}}}\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}[r]\frac{C}{T}&ξ_{{\bm{n}}}\\
ξ_{{\bm{n}}}&k_{{\bm{n}}}\end{pmatrix}}{\begin{pmatrix}[r]dT\\
dθ_{{\bm{n}}}\end{pmatrix}}\,,

(21)

where
ξ_{{\bm{n}}}=dS/dθ_{{\bm{n}}}=dT_{{\bm{n}}}/dT
is the rotational analog of magnetocaloric coefficients.

Thermodynamic coefficients experience a discontinuous jump across the boundary of a continuous (second-order) phase transition. The magnitudes of the jumps in thermodynamic coefficients are related to one another via the Ehrenfest relations, which express the continuity of the thermodynamic potentials (first derivatives) across the boundary of a continuous phase transition. Equation (
21
) relates the jump in the magnetotropic susceptibility to the jump in the heat capacity via
Δk_{{\bm{n}}}=-ΔC/T_{c}×\left(dT_{c}/dθ_{{\bm{n}}}\right)^{2}
. Here
\left(dT_{c}/dθ_{{\bm{n}}}\right)
is the change in the transition temperature when the sample is rotated in the applied magnetic field at a fixed temperature. The sign of the jump
Δk_{{\bm{n}}}
in the magnetotropic susceptibility across the phase boundary is fixed by thermodynamics. Similar to elastic moduli, or magnetic susceptibility, the magnetotropic susceptibility decreases as we enter into a lower symmetry phase.
Modic et al. 2021

II.4
Magnetotropic susceptibility as a thermodynamic response.

In this section, we discuss the magnetotropic susceptibility in relation to microscopic degrees of freedom in the system, i.e., as a correlation function. This Section also sets up the discussion in the next section (Section
II.5
), where we will introduce dynamics.

The angular dependence of the free energy
F(∆θ)
is generated by the term in the Hamiltonian
H_{1}(∆θ)
that depends on the direction of magnetic field,

\displaystyle e^{-βF(∆θ)}=

\displaystyle{\text{Tr}\,}e^{-β(H_{0}+H_{1}(∆θ))}\,,

(22)

where
β=1/T
is the inverse temperature (throughout this section the temperature is measured in energy units. In these units,
k_{\rm{B}}=1
).
{\text{Tr}\,}⋯
stands for the sum of expectation values over a complete set of states of the Hamiltonian,
{\text{Tr}\,}⋯=∑_{n}\left\langle{{n}}\right|⋯\left|{{n}}\right\rangle
. Here
H_{1}(∆θ)
is defined in such a way that
H_{1}(∆θ=0)=0
.
The free energy can be expanded in powers of
∆θ
as

\displaystyle F(∆θ)=F_{0}+F_{1}∆θ+F_{2}\;∆θ^{2}/2\,.

(23)

The magnetotropic susceptibility is equal to
F_{2}
.

We want to express
F_{2}
directly in terms of thermodynamic averages of the operators
H_{0}
and
H_{1}(∆θ)
. Because operators
H_{0}
and
H_{1}(∆θ)
do not commute, the Taylor expansion of the matrix exponent in Eq. (
22
) in powers of

has to be carried out by way of the identity
e^{A+B}=\lim_{n\textrightarrow∞}\left(e^{A/n}e^{B/n}\right)^{n}
generated by the “interaction representation”
Negele and Orland 1988
. Using the fact that
\exp\{-βF_{0}\}={{\text{Tr}\,}\exp\{-βH_{0}\}}
, we can write

\displaystyle e^{-βF_{1}∆θ-β(\nicefrac{{F_{2}}}{{2}})∆θ^{2}}=\left[{\text{Tr}\,}e^{βH_{0}}\right]\left[{\text{Tr}\,}e^{-β(H_{0}+H_{1})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=1+\left\langle{\!{\,-βH_{1}\,}\!}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\!{\left\langle{\!{\;(\!-βH_{1}\!),\,(\!-βH_{1}\!)\;}\!}\right\rangle}\!}\right\rangle\,+⋯\,,

II.5
Rotation-in-field-induced relaxation phenomena captured in the frequency-dependent magnetotropic susceptibility.

Given the nature of the experimental setup in which magnetotropic susceptibility arises (Section
III
), we now discuss the dynamic—frequency-dependent—magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},ω)
. It captures the relaxation phenomena coupled to the rotation of the sample in an applied magnetic field.
The time-dispersed magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}(t-t^{\prime})
describes time-delayed response of the torque
Δ{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})
to rotation of the sample in applied magnetic field,

\displaystyle Δ{T}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},t)=∫\limits_{-∞}^{t}\!\!\!dt^{\prime}\;k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},t-t^{\prime})\;Δθ_{{\bm{n}}}(t^{\prime})\,.

(37)

Its Fourier transform defines the dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},ω)=∫_{0}^{∞}dt\exp({iωt})k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},t)
.

Kubo analysis
Negele and Orland 1988
starting from Eq. (
37
) suggests that the dynamic response
k_{{\bm{n}}}(ω)
is equal to the dynamic torque-torque correlation function,
k_{{\bm{n}}}(ω)=∫_{0}^{∞}dt\exp({iωt})\left\langle{\!{{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bm{n}}}(t){\mathcal{T}}_{{\bm{n}}}(0)}\!}\right\rangle
. This is because the torque operator is conjugate to the angle variable
Δθ_{{\bm{n}}}
in the microscopic Hamiltonian, Eq. (
32
), and therefore, determines the time-evolution of the torque under time-dependent
Δθ(t)
. This is similar to the microscopic interpretation of the dynamic magnetic susceptibility as a correlation function of magnetization operators.
This argument captures the entire imaginary part of the dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},ω)
as well as that part of the real part of
k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},ω)
that is related to the imaginary part by Kramers-Kronig (analyticity) requirements. In particular, it shows that the dynamic magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}(ω)
is identical in its analytic properties to the dynamic magnetic susceptibility
χ(ω)
and contains the same physical information. Direct calculation of the dynamic torque-torque correlation function results in

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Im}}k_{{\bm{n}}}(ω)=-({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})⋅{\mathrm{Im}}\hat{χ}(ω)⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})\,.

(38)

However, without
{\mathrm{Im}}
, Eq. (
38
) does not produce a correct relation between
k_{{\bm{n}}}
and
\hat{χ}
in the static limit
ω=0
given by Eq. (
II.1
). This is because the time-dispersed magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}(t-t^{\prime})
contains an instantaneous part, proportional to delta-function
δ(t-t^{\prime})
associated with the direct dependence of the magnetic torque on magnetic field, Eq. (
1
).

The instantaneous part in
k_{{\bm{n}}}(t-t^{\prime})
produces a frequency-independent real function on the real axis of frequency, in
k_{{\bm{n}}}(ω)
. Such a constant function has zero imaginary part everywhere in the complex plane.
The instantaneous term in the magnetic torque originates in the explicit dependence of magnetic torque operator
{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=-{\mathcal{M}}⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})
on applied magnetic field
{\bm{B}}
. This term is reactive—it is not associated with the time evolution of torque under the action of time-dependent Hamiltonian, as captured by the Kubo argument. The complete relation between the dynamic magnetic susceptibility
\hat{χ}
and magnetotropic susceptibility
k_{{\bm{n}}}
is given by

\displaystyle k_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}},ω)\!=\!({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{M}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})⋅\hat{χ}({\bm{B}},ω)⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})\,.

(39)

The first term here is not associated with relaxation- or dispersion phenomena in the sample. It is a mere redefinition of the torque operator in the rotated frame of reference. The second term has the structure of dynamic correlation function
Negele and Orland 1988
of magnetic torque operators,
{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bm{n}}}({\bm{B}})=-{\mathcal{M}}⋅({\bm{n}}×{\bm{B}})
.

\displaystyle\frac{Δω}{ω_{0}}≈\frac{k({\bm{B}})}{2{S}_{n}}\,,\qquad k({\bm{B}})≪{S}_{n}\,.

III.7
Effects of non-uniform magnetic field

A non-uniform magnetic field can shift the resonance frequency of the cantilever through several mechanisms. Unless the experiment is specifically designed to observe these effects, the resonance shifts resulting from a non-uniform magnetic field are much smaller than the frequency shifts associated with sample rotation in the magnetic field. Typically, they are smaller by a factor proportional to the first or second power of the ratio of the cantilever’s length to the size of the coil generating the applied magnetic field (which determines the size of the gradients of magnetic field). Here we provide a brief semi-quantitative discussion of the frequency shifts associated with spatial gradients and spatial curvatures of magnetic field.

Small changes in the free energy of the sample in a non-uniform magnetic field

\displaystyle dF=-{\bm{M}}⋅d{\bm{B}}

The field gradient along the length of the cantilever acts on the sample in the same way as a force of gravity, Eq. (

\displaystyle F_{z}=-\frac{dF}{dz}={\bm{M}}⋅\frac{d{\bm{B}}}{dz}

(78)

IV.2
Oscillating cantilever.

The motion of the cantilever near one of its mechanical resonances is determined by the equations of motion for
ζ(z,t)
,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}ζ(z,t)}{dt^{2}}+\frac{γ}{μ}∇^{4}ζ(z,t)=0\,,

(81)

which follow from the energy functional in Eq. (
69
)
Landau and Lifshitz 1976
. Near the resonance, the cantilever oscillates at a single frequency
ω
,

\displaystyle ζ(z,t)=ζ_{n}(z)e^{iω_{n}t}\,,

(82)

determined by one of the eigenvalues of the Bilaplacian

\displaystyle∇^{4}ζ_{n}(z)=\frac{κ_{n}^{4}}{L^{4}}\;ζ_{n}(z)\;,\qquad ω_{n}^{2}=\frac{κ_{n}^{4}}{L^{4}}\;\frac{γ}{μ}\,,

(83)

subject to the boundary conditions of the cantilever. For a cantilever clamped at one end,
z=0
, and free at the other,
z=L
,

\displaystyle∇^{2}ζ_{n}(L)=0\,,\;∇^{3}ζ_{n}(L)=0\,,\;∇ζ_{n}(0)=0,\;ζ_{n}(0)=0\,.

(84)

The eigenfunctions of the Bilaplacian in Eq. (
83
) is solved with

\displaystyle ζ_{n}(z)\!=\!A\cos\frac{κ_{n}z}{L}\!+\!B\sin\frac{κ_{n}z}{L}\!+\!C\cosh\frac{κ_{n}z}{L}\!+\!D\sinh\frac{κ_{n}z}{L}\,,

(85)

where

are constant factors determined by the boundary conditions,

\displaystyle-A\cos κ_{n}-B\sin κ_{n}+C\cosh κ_{n}+D\sinh κ_{n}=

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle A\sin κ_{n}-B\cos κ_{n}+C\sinh κ_{n}+D\cosh κ_{n}=

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle B+D=

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle A+C=

\displaystyle\;0\,.

(86)

The eigenvalues
κ_{n}
of the Bilaplacian are determined by the condition that the matrix in Eq. (
IV.2
) has zero determinant,

\displaystyle\text{Det}\left(\begin{matrix}-\cos κ_{n}&-\sin κ_{n}&\cosh κ_{n}&\sinh κ_{n}\\

1&0&1&0\end{matrix}\right)=0\,,

(87)

which simplifies to

\displaystyle 1+\cos κ_{n}\cosh κ_{n}=0\,.

(88)

Finally, we obtain

\displaystyle κ_{0}≈0.597π,\qquad κ_{n=1,2,...}≈(n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})π\,.

(89)

83

\displaystyle ω_{n}=\frac{κ_{n}^{2}}{L^{2}}\;\sqrt{\frac{γ}{μ}}\,.

(90)

The frequency of the lowest frequency (fundamental) mode of a thin cantilever with a uniform cross-section is given by combining Eqs. (

,

\displaystyle ω_{0}=1.02\frac{h}{L^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{Y}{ρ}}\,,

\displaystyle A=

\displaystyle B=
