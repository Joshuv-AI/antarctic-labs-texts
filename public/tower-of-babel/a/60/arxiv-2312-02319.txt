Kernel Diffusion: An Alternate Approach to Blind Deconvolution
Yash Sanghvi, Yiheng Chi, Stanley H. Chan

Abstract
Blind deconvolution problems are severely ill-posed because neither the underlying signal nor the forward operator are not known exactly. Conventionally, these problems are solved by alternating between estimation of the image and kernel while keeping the other fixed. In this paper, we show that this framework is flawed because of its tendency to get trapped in local minima and, instead, suggest the use of a kernel estimation strategy with a non-blind solver. This framework is employed by a diffusion method which is trained to sample the blur kernel from the conditional distribution with guidance from a pre-trained non-blind solver. The proposed diffusion method leads to state-of-the-art results on both synthetic and real blur datasets.



1
Introduction

Images taken by a hand-held camera under long exposure suffer from motion blur. Assuming this blur is spatially invariant, the captured image
\mathbf{y}
and the underlying sharp image
\mathbf{x}
are related as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{k}\circledast\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{n},

(1)

where
\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{n}
refer to the blur kernel corresponding to the camera motion and noise, respectively, and the operator
\circledast
is convolution. The inverse problem of estimating
\mathbf{x}
given
\mathbf{y}
is called deconvolution, which can be classified into
non-blind
and
blind
.

In the non-blind case, the blur kernel
\mathbf{k}
is known. There are plenty of iterative methods which have been successfully applied to various image restoration tasks. Usually, these approaches, such as Plug-and-Play
Venkatakrishnan et al. 2013
;
Rond et al. 2016
;
Kamilov et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2019
;
Chan et al. 2016
;
Ahmad et al. 2020
and Regularization by Denoising (RED)
Romano et al. 2017
;
Reehorst and Schniter 2018
;
Cohen et al. 2021
, use a pre-trained image denoiser as a substitute for a complex image prior. Recent deep learning counterparts to these approaches involve unrolling the iterative methods followed by end-to-end training
Li et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2017
;
yang et al. 2016
;
Monga et al. 2021
.

In blind deconvolution, the blur kernel
\mathbf{k}
is not known. A natural strategy for the blind inverse problem is to jointly estimate the blur kernel
\mathbf{k}
, and the image
\mathbf{x}
by maximizing the posterior probability:

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}}&=\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\Big]\\
&=\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})p(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{k})\Big]\\
&=\underset{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{k},\mathbf{x})+\log p(\mathbf{x})+\log p(\mathbf{k})\Big].\end{split}

(2)

The Maximum-A-Posteriori (MAP) for the pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k})
, i.e.
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
, can be obtained by an iterative scheme which alternates between the following minimization steps:

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{i+1}

\displaystyle=\text{arg}\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}}\Big[\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\circledast\mathbf{k}^{i}\|^{2}+\phi(\mathbf{x})\Big],

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}^{i+1}

\displaystyle=\text{arg}\min\limits_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}^{i+1}\circledast\mathbf{k}\|^{2}+\psi(\mathbf{k})\Big],

(4)

where
\phi(\cdot)
,
\psi(\cdot)
represent the image and kernel priors. Many classical deconvolution techniques can be framed as variations of this framework
Chan and Wong 1998
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Anger et al. 2019
;
Shan et al. 2008
. Even neural network based iterative methods such as Self-Deblur
Ren et al. 2020
and diffusion models
Chung et al. 2023a
;
Murata et al. 2023
have achieved state-of-the-art performance in blind deconvolution using the stated framework.

Despite recent success of diffusion methods, they are based on
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
approach, which was first brought into question by Levin et al.
Levin et al. 2011
They argued that the number of variables to be estimated, i.e.,
\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}\}
, are much larger than the number of known variables, i.e.,
\mathbf{y}
, and as a result, (
2
) favors the no-blur solution
{\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{k}}}=(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{I})
.
Levin et al. 2011
proposed an alternate strategy of solving the kernel
\mathbf{k}
first to exploit the inherent symmetry in the problem. This is achieved by marginalizing out the image space as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{k}}=\underset{\mathbf{k}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\Big]=\underset{\mathbf{k}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\int p(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{x}\Big].

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we address the problem with a kernel estimation approach, and we demonstrate that this strategy can be implemented in practice. Our contribution in this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

Using a simple 1D deconvolution example, we demonstrate how the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
framework can be susceptible to local minima while the suggested strategy of
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{k}}
does not. Using this example, we show how the marginalization in (
5
) can be achieved in practice using a non-blind solver.

2.

Next, we propose a novel diffusion method, called Kernel-Diffusion (
Kernel-Diff
) which is based on the design principles outline dabove and is trained to sample from the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
. This diffusion method coupled with a differentiable non-blind solver achieves state-of-the-art deconvolution performance. We show with numerical experiments on real and synthetic data that the proposed approach creates significant performance improvement.

2
Background

2.1
Diffusion Methods

Diffusion models
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Vincent 2011
;
Song et al. 2021
are a popular generative method which can be used to sample any target diffusion
p_{data}(\mathbf{x})
. The diffusion models begin with a forward process, which starts from
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim p_{data}(\mathbf{x})
and gradually adds noise according to variance schedule
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},...\beta_{T}\}
, formulated as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})

(6)

at time steps
t\in\{1,2,\dots,T\}
. This forward process converges to
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
. Next, the reverse diffusion process is used to sample from the distribution of
p_{data}
by starting with
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
and performing the iterative updates

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\Big(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha_{t}}}}\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)\Big)+\sigma_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t},\\
\mathbf{z}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}),\end{split}

(7)

where
\alpha_{t}\overset{\text{def}}{=}1-\beta_{t}
,
\bar{\alpha_{t}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
, and
\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
is the trained diffusion network used to learn to denoise
\mathbf{x}_{t}
at any given time step, trained by minimizing the following loss function.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(\theta)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_{0},\epsilon}\Big[\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha_{t}}}\epsilon,t)\|^{2}\Big].\end{split}

(8)

Inverse Problems and Diffusion Models
.
Score based diffusion methods have been extensively used in solving inverse problems
Kawar et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2023c
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Kawar et al. 2021a
;
Daras et al. 2023
;
Kawar et al. 2021b
;
Kadkhodaie and Simoncelli 2021
by sampling from the posterior
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
and the corresponding score is calculated using the Bayes’ formula

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{y})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{x}_{t})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}_{t}).

(9)

For example,
Jalal et al. 2021
uses an approximation to the posterior score in (
9
) and used the Langevin dynamics to sample from the distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
for the MRI compressed sensing problem;
Chung et al. 2022b
;
Chung et al. 2022a
solve the inpainting and super-resolution inverse problems with a modified reverse diffusion scheme which includes a projection step to reduce the consistency metric
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|
in each iteration; diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS)
Chung et al. 2023b
solves the noisy inverse problem with alternating diffusion and gradient descent steps.

Image Restoration and Diffusion Models
Diffusion models sample from a target distribution while MMSE estimators average over them
Kawar et al. 2021b
. As a result, the former has been widely used in image restoration tasks such as deblurring
Whang et al. 2022
, super-resolution
Saharia et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2022
, and low-light enhancement
Wang et al. 2023b
;
Jiang et al. 2023
. For an exhaustive review, we refer the reader to
Li et al. 2023
.

Guidance in Diffusion Models
. Pre-trained diffusion models are often guided at each iteration to produce desired results. This is first proposed in
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
where a classifier gradient is added to each iteration for the final output to belong to the given class. ILVR
Choi et al. 2021
guides the image diffusion process by constraining the low-frequency information of an image. In the same spirit,
Wang et al. 2023a
guides the diffusion process by refining only the null-space of the corresponding inverse problem.
Whang et al. 2022
guides a deblurring diffusion model using a pretrained structure module.

2.2
Blind Deconvolution

Traditional blind deconvolution methods can be categorized under the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
framework
Chan and Wong 1998
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Anger et al. 2019
;
Shan et al. 2008
;
Pan et al. 2016
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Yan et al. 2017
;
Sun et al. 2013
;
Chen et al. 2019
. For example,
Cho and Lee 2009
uses salient edges for the kernel estimation and then performs image estimation using a Total Variation (TV) prior.
Xu and Jia 2010
refines the kernel estimation process by iterative support detection.
Gupta et al. 2010
performs alternating image and kernel minimization but uses a motion density representation for the blur kernel instead of for pixels. Self-Deblur
Ren et al. 2020
uses an alternating minimization scheme where a latent deep image prior
Ulyanov et al. 2018
is used to represent the kernel and image space.

Deep learning methods have replaced the optimization-based approaches and usually involve end-to-end training of image restoration networks
Schuler et al. 2015
;
Sun et al. 2015
;
Tao et al. 2018
;
Kupyn et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Zamir et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Zamir et al. 2022
using large datasets of pairs of sharp and blurry images, e.g., GoPro
Nah et al. 2017
and RealBlur
Rim et al. 2020
datasets. For a more exhaustive review of deep learning based approaches, we refer the readers to
Zhang et al. 2022
.

A few methods have utilized the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{k}}
approach, even though not always explicitly stated by the authors.
Chakrabarti 2016
uses a fully convolutional network to predict the Fourier coefficients of the underlying kernel which are used to deconvolve the blurry image in Fourier space.
Carbajal et al. 2023
;
Carbajal et al. 2021
train a Kernel-Prediction Network for all pixels in the image to deal with spatially varying blur.
Sanghvi et al. 2022b
leverages the differentiablity of a non-blind solver
Sanghvi et al. 2022a
to frame the blind deconvolution problem entirely in terms of kernel estimation problems. This strategy is improved by
Sanghvi et al. 2023
using a key-points based representation to further reduce the dimensionality of the problem.

3
Method

3.1
Deconvolution Scheme Analysis

We motivate the proposed method by analyzing a simple 1D deconvolution problem to demonstrate the inherent difficulties of optimization in the alternating minimization framework. We show that minimizing the cost function in (
2
) is very likely to converge to false minima. We also show the benefits of the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{k}}
strategy.

Simple deconvolution problem
. Assume an unknown pulse function
\mathbf{x}_{a,w}(t)
where
a
represents the beginning of the pulse and
w
represents its width. The signal is blurred by a Gaussian pulse
\mathbf{k}_{\sigma}(t)
where
\sigma
represents its standard deviation. The resulting signal
\mathbf{y}(t)
is obtained by blurring the signal
\mathbf{x}(t)
followed by additive Gaussian noise, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{k}_{\sigma}\circledast\mathbf{x}_{a,w}+\mathcal{N}(0,\beta\mathbf{I}).

(10)

Note that that in this setup the image
\mathbf{x}
and kernel
\mathbf{k}
are characterized completely be scalars
\{a,w\}
and
\sigma
, respectively. Therefore, the blind deconvolution problem can be considered as an estimation of the three variables
\{a,w,\sigma\}
.

Alternating Methods versus Kernel Minimization
.
For the stated example, the conventional alternating minimization approach of
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
can be formulated as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{a},\hat{w},\hat{\sigma}&=\underset{a,w,\sigma\geq 0}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(a,w,\sigma|\mathbf{y})\Big]\\
&=\underset{a,w,\sigma\geq 0}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\beta}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{a,w}\circledast\mathbf{k}_{\sigma}\|^{2}\big)\Big].\end{split}

(11)

The equivalent
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{k}}
would be first estimating the kernel width
\sigma
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{\sigma}&=\underset{\sigma\geq 0}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\sigma|\mathbf{y})\Big]\\
&=\underset{\sigma\geq 0}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\int p(a,w,\sigma|\mathbf{y})da\;dw\Big]\\
&=\underset{\sigma\geq 0}{\text{arg}\min}\Big[\sum\limits_{a,w\geq 0}\exp\big[-\frac{1}{2\beta}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{a,w}\circledast\mathbf{k}_{\sigma}\|^{2}\big]\Big],\end{split}

(12)

followed by the estimation of
\{a,w\}
:

\displaystyle\{\hat{a},\hat{w}\}

\displaystyle=\underset{a,w\geq 0}{\text{arg}\min}\Big[\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{k}_{\hat{\sigma}}\circledast\mathbf{x}_{a,w}\|^{2}\big]\Big].

(13)

Analysis
. In Figure
2
, we plot the loss functions corresponding to the two schemes outlined in (
11
) and (
12
). We have the following observations according to the loss functions. First, for the joint alternating minimization scheme, the loss function contains many local minima. Even though the global minimum is close to the ground-truth, it is very difficult for a gradient-descent based solver to converge to it unless the random initialization is already very close. Second, for the kernel-based minimization scheme which reduces the search space of the optimization to kernel only, the corresponding loss function has only one minimum, which is also close to the ground-truth. It is very likely that the estimated
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,
\{a,w\}
, will converge to this minimum. Therefore, the kernel-based minimization scheme can consistently produce significantly better results with much lower prediction error than the alternating scheme.

. Each iteration of the proposed kernel-based diffusion contains two sub-steps. First, we perform a reverse diffusion step using the trained diffusion model
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{k}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)
. Next we plug the temporary kernel estimate
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
in the differentiable non-blind solver
F(\cdot)
and perform a gradient descent update to guide the diffusion process to minimizing the reblurring loss
\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0}\circledast\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\|^{2}
.

3.2
Marginalization of Image Space

In practice, the estimation of
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
can be difficult because the marginalization in (
5
) requires a weighted average over all images. We surmise that due to lack of a feasible method to evaluate the likelihood, the approach from
Levin et al. 2011
was not explored by the imaging community for the blind deconvolution. To mitigate the computational issue, we propose an approximation to the marginalization using a non-blind solver.

Before stating the approximation, we explicitly define a non-blind solver. Given a blurred image
\mathbf{y}
and the corresponding blur kernel
\mathbf{k}
, we can easily estimate the latent image using a non-blind solver which performs the following image estimation:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{k})=\text{arg}\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}}\Big[p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k},\mathbf{y})\Big]\overset{\text{def}}{=}F(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{k}).

(14)

Traditionally, these non-blind solvers are implemented using an iterative method where the corresponding cost function for MAP estimation in (
14
) is minimized using gradient descent or proximal updates. Convolutional neural networks with Wiener-deconvolution processing have been used with great success also
Dong et al. 2020
;
Sanghvi et al. 2022a
;
Dong et al. 2018
;
Nan and Ji 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023
. The latter provides us with
F(\cdot)
as a differentiable function with respect to
\mathbf{k}
. The significance of this property will become obvious to the reader in the next subsection.

Now, given our definition of a non-blind solver,
we approximate the conditional probability
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
as follows
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})=\int p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{x}\approx\frac{1}{Z}p(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{k}),\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y}),\end{split}

(15)

where the integration is performed using the Laplace approximation around the mode of the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
and
Z
is the constant scaling factor. For a more detailed explanation of this approximation, we refer the readers to Section
C
of the supplementary document.

For the problem in Section
3.1
,
p(\sigma|\mathbf{y})
can be approximated as

\displaystyle p(\sigma|\mathbf{y})\approx\frac{1}{Z^{\prime}}\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2\beta^{2}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{a(\sigma),w(\sigma)}\circledast\mathbf{k}_{\sigma}\|^{2}\Big],

(16)

where
\{a(\sigma),w(\sigma)\}
represents the best estimate of the image space parameters given
\sigma
according to (
14
) and
Z^{\prime}
is a constant scalar.

Through the approximation in (
15
), we can now estimate the kernel by maximizing
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
. Firstly, (
15
) removes the intractable marginalization of the image space making to easier to evaluate the likelihood. Secondly, using the non-blind solver i.e.
\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{k})
allows us to convert the an image-kernel optimization problem to an purely kernel-estimation problem.

3.3
Kernel-Based Diffusion

To sample from
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
, we propose to use a kernel-based diffusion network. This is different from existing methods using diffusion models, e.g., Blind-DPS
Chung et al. 2023a
, that resemble the alternating minimization approach, aiming to sample
\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}\}
from the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
using implicit score models for image and kernel spaces.

Diffusion Network
.
We use a modified version of the U-Net architecture as opposed to the one described in
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
. Since we are sampling from a conditional distribution, the diffusion model
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{y},t)
takes as input both the current kernel estimate
\mathbf{k}_{t}
and the blurred image
\mathbf{y}
. This is achieved in a similar fashion as
Saharia et al. 2021
. Specifically, our U-Net architecture has two encoders, one for the image
\mathbf{y}
and the other for the kernel
\mathbf{k}
. In our implementation, we use images of size
256\times 256
and kernels of size
64\times 64
. At the end of the encoders, the encoded features of the kernel branch are upscaled by a factor of 4 and concatenated to the encoded features of the image branch. The concatenated features are then passed through a common U-Net decoder stage to predict the kernel at the next time step.

The diffusion model
\epsilon_{\theta}(\cdot)
is trained by minimizing the following loss function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\text{kernel}}(\theta)&\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{y},\epsilon}\Big[\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{k}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha_{t}}}\epsilon,\mathbf{y},t)\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\end{split}

(17)

where the expectation is taken over all timesteps
t
, blurred images
\mathbf{y}
and corresponding blur kernels
\mathbf{k}
. With trained parameters
\theta^{*}
, we sample
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
using a series of reverse diffusion steps starting from
\mathbf{k}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})
and the following reverse-diffusion iteration:

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bigg(\mathbf{k}_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha_{t}}}}\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{k}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\bigg)+\bar{\sigma}_{t}\mathbf{z}_{t}

(18)

Guidance using Non-Blind Solver
. Once we have sampled the kernel
\mathbf{k}_{0}
, we estimate the latent image as
\mathbf{x}_{0}=F_{\phi}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{k}_{0})
, where
F_{\phi}(\cdot)
is a neural network non-blind solver. There is a vast selection of candidate non-blind solver, including many network-based methods proposed recently
Dong et al. 2020
;
Eboli et al. 2020
;
Gong et al. 2020
;
Dong et al. 2021
;
Sanghvi et al. 2022a
;
Zhang et al. 2023
. These networks usually convert the blurred images to features space, apply Wiener deconvolution using the kernel followed by converting the deconvolved features into a single image using a decoder.

While diffusion processes are effective in generating samples from the conditional distribution
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
, they do not guarantee minimization of the reblurring loss, i.e.,
\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{k}\circledast\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
.
To minimize reblurring loss while also generating a plausible sample
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
, we guide the reverse diffusion process using the pre-trained non-blind solver as follows.

At step
t
of the reverse diffusion process, the kernel estimate is given as
\mathbf{k}_{t}
. Using the trained diffusion model
\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{k}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)
and
\mathbf{k}_{t}
, we can compute a temporary estimate of the sampled kernel
\mathbf{k}_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha_{t}}}}\big(\mathbf{k}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}(\mathbf{k}_{t},\mathbf{y},t)\big).

(19)

We then estimate the image using this kernel estimate
\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0}
by
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\overset{\text{def}}{=}F(\mathbf{y},\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0})
, plug into the reblurring loss, and perform gradient descent by

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{t}\leftarrow\mathbf{k}_{t}-\delta\nabla_{\mathbf{k}_{t}}\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0}\circledast F(\mathbf{y},\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{0})\|^{2}.

(20)

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed Kernel-Diff, a blind deconvolution solution in the tradition of Levin et. al
Levin et al. 2011
. We first show the pitfalls of conventional minimization using a 1D example and then show how the kernel minimization approach can be implemented in practice using a pre-trained non-blind solver. Using the principles outlined, we propose a guided diffusion method that samples from
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
to solve the blind deconvolution problem and show extensive numerical experiments on blurred images.

For future work, the method can be refined to deblur images with spatially varying and motion blur i.e. degradation which do not fit the single-blur convolutional model. Alternately, the non-blind solver can be modified to be robust to inaccuracies in kernel estimation.

B. Comparison with Levin et. al

In this paper, we show why the kernel estimation strategy from
Levin et al. 2011
is superior compared to alternating minimization followed by a diffusion-based method incorporating it. Despite the similarities, we come to slightly different conclusions from the original paper which are outlined here.

1.

The toy example from
Levin et al. 2011
, presented in Fig. 1 in the paper, depends on the choice of the sparse prior. However, one can argue that
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
framework can be useful by a better choice of prior - which is easily possible given deep learning based solutions, which did not exist in 2011. Compared to that, the conclusions arrived in this paper do not depend on the choice of prior since we boil down the optimization problem to the smallest possible subspace i.e.
a,w,\sigma
.

2.

While
Levin et al. 2011
arrives at the conclusion that that the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}}
strategy favours the no-blur solution, our conclusion is slightly different. Through our choice of the toy-problem, we find that the while the no-blur solution is not favoured, a gradient descent based scheme is still likely to get stuck in false minima, suggesting use of of the
\text{MAP}_{\mathbf{k}}
framework.

C. Marginalization Approximation

In this section, we describe approximation in Eq. 15 from the main document in detail. We start with the conditional kernel distribution
p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
as a marginalized distribution as follows:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})=\int p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{x}

(23)

We approximate the integrand
p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})
using the Laplace approximation

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\approx p(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})\Big]

(24)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\Big]

(25)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{S}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\Big[\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{x}^{2}}\log p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\Big]_{\mathbf{x}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}}

(26)

Applying this approximation to (
23
),

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\approx\int p(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})\Big]d\mathbf{x}

(27)

\displaystyle=p(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\int\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{T}\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}})\Big]d\mathbf{x}

(28)

\displaystyle=p(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})((2\pi)^{n}|\mathbf{S}|)^{1/2}

(29)

here
n
represents the dimension of the image space
\mathbf{x}
. We further approximate
\mathbf{S}
to be independent of
\mathbf{x}
, and hence a constant
C
, for sake of computational simplicity. However, future work can focus on providing a better approximation for
|S|
and include it in the marginalization.

Next we examine the
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
and its relation to the non-blind solver.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{y})\Big]

(30)

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{k},\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{k})}{p(\mathbf{y})}\Big]

(31)

\displaystyle=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{k},\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{y})}\Big]=\underset{\mathbf{x}}{\text{arg}\max}\Big[p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k},\mathbf{y})\Big]

(32)

Therefore, we have shown that
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
is the Maximum-A-Posteriori estimate for the non-blind problem problem i.e. estimating
\mathbf{x}
once the kernel
\mathbf{k}
is known.

E. Kernel-Diff Hyperparameters

Diffusion Hyperparameters
For the reverse diffusion process, we use a total of
T=1000
steps and linear schedule for the noise variance
\{\beta_{t}\}
as defined in
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
. For training the denoising network

norm, instead of the usual
\ell_{1}

Gradient Step Size
: For the reverse diffusion scheme,
\delta
in Step 9 of Algorithm 1 is chosen similar to the heuristic from Blind-DPS i.e.

\displaystyle\delta\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{0.1}{\|\mathbf{y}-\hat{\mathbf{k}_{0}}\circledast\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\|^{2}}

(33)

This ensure that the gradient descent becomes only aggressive in later stages of the diffusion when we’re closer to the true solution. Conversely, gradient descent is relatively negligible in earlier stages of diffusion.
