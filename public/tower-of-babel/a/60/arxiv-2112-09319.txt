Moments and Random Number Generation for the Truncated Elliptical Family of Distributions
Katherine A. L. Valeriano, Christian E. Galarza, Larissa A. Matos

Abstract
This paper proposes an algorithm to generate random numbers from any member of the truncated multivariate elliptical family of distributions with a strictly decreasing density generating function. Based on Neal 2003 and Ho et al. 2012 , we construct an efficient sampling method by means of a slice sampling algorithm with Gibbs sampler steps. We also provide a faster approach to approximate the first and the second moment for the truncated multivariate elliptical distributions where Monte Carlo integration is used for the truncated partition, and explicit expressions for the non-truncated part ( Galarza et al. 2020 ) . Examples and an application to environmental spatial data illustrate its usefulness. Methods are available for free in the new R library relliptical .

Introduction

The use of truncated distributions arises in a wide variety of statistical models as survival analysis, censored data models, Bayesian models with truncated parameters space, and abound in such fields as agronomy, biology, environmental monitoring, medicine, and economics. Algorithms like Expectation-Maximization (EM)
(
Dempster
et al. 1977
)
are employed frequently in multivariate censored data analysis under a likelihood-based perspective for its facility to deal with missing and partially observed data. This algorithm requires the computation of conditional truncated moments, commonly the first two moments. For example,
Matos
et al. 2013
and
Matos
et al. 2016
estimated the parameters of a censored mixed-effects model for irregularly repeated measures via the EM algorithm, which needed to compute the first two moments of a truncated multivariate
t
(TMVT) and a truncated multivariate normal (TMVN) distributions, respectively.

In this context, there are a few libraries in
R

(
R Core Team 2021
)
which provide truncated multivariate moments. For instance, the package
tmvtnorm

(
Wilhelm 2015
)
computes the mean and the variance of the TMVN distribution by deriving its moment generating function, while the
MomTrunc
library
(
Galarza
et al. 2021
)
uses a recursive approach method proposed by
Kan and
Robotti 2017
to compute arbitrary higher-order moments. On the other hand, for the TMVT distribution, the packages
TTmoment

(
Ho
et al. 2015
)
and
MomTrunc
compute its two first moments. Moreover, the first library only handles integer degrees of freedom greater than 4, while the latter can compute even high-order moments for any degrees of freedom
(
Galarza
et al. 2021
)
.

Variations of the EM algorithm such as Stochastic Approximation EM (SAEM)
(
Delyon
et al. 1999
)
and Monte Carlo EM (MCEM)
(
Wei and
Tanner 1990
)
replace the conditional expectations by an approximation that requires to draw independent random observations from a truncated distribution. For instance,
Lachos
et al. 2017
estimated the parameters of a linear spatial model for censored data using the SAEM algorithm, which needed to generate random samples from the TMVN distribution to perform the stochastic approximation step.
More recently, also using the SAEM algorithm,
Lachos
et al. 2019
proposed a robust multivariate linear mixed model for multiple censored responses based on the scale mixtures of normal (SMN) distributions. Moreover, generating random numbers from truncated distributions is also required in Bayesian models,
Gelfand
et al. 1992
showed how to perform Bayesian analysis for constrained parameters or truncated data problems by using Gibbs sampling.

There are several methods to generate random samples from a truncated distribution in the literature, and the common one is the rejection sampling technique. This method draws samples from the non-truncated distribution and retains only the samples inside the support region. However, the procedure may be inefficient, especially when the truncation interval is too small or it is located at a less probable area of the probability density function (pdf).
Neal 2003
proposed proposed the Slice sampling method, a procedure that turns sampling from a truncated density into sampling repeatedly from uniform distributions instead. This algorithm is easy to code, fast and does not reject samples, making it more efficient than the conventional rejection method.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no proposals in the literature to generate samples from other
multivariate truncated distributions in the elliptical class other than the TMVN and TMVT distributions
(available in the
tmvtnorm
and
TTmoment
packages). Hence, motivated by the slice sampling algorithm, we propose a general method to obtain samples from any truncated multivariate elliptical distribution with strictly decreasing density generating function (dgf). Using conditional expectation properties, we also propose an efficient algorithm to approximate the moments of the most common distribution of this class: the truncated multivariate
normal, Student-
t
, slash, contaminated normal, and Pearson VII distributions. This method requires less running time when compared with the existing ones, since it deals with the truncated and non-truncated part of the vector separately. Our proposal can be reached through the
R
package
relliptical
. Finally, it is worth mentioning that moments of truncated elliptical distributions can be used to compute truncated moments for the selection elliptical family of distributions, a wide family which includes complex multivariate asymmetric versions of the elliptical distributions as the extended skew-normal, the unified skew-
t
distributions, among others. Therefore, our proposal opens the doors for the calculation of truncated moments of complex elliptical asymmetric distributions, which are of particular interest for the development of robust censored models with asymmetry, heavy tails and missingness
(
Galarza
et al. 2021
;
de Alencar et al. 2021
, see for instance)
.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
shows some results related to the elliptical and truncated elliptical family of distributions and a brief description of the slice sampling algorithm. Section
3
is devoted to the formulation of the sampling algorithm for the truncated elliptical distributions, whereas Section
4
focuses on our proposed method to approximate the first and the second moment. For the last two sections, we present a brief introduction to its respective
R
function. A simulation study that compares the mean and covariance matrix for the TMVT distribution estimated through different methods in
R
is presented as well. Section
5
displays an application on censored Gaussian spatial models throughout the analysis of the Missouri dioxin contamination dataset. Finally, Section
6
concludes with a discussion.

Preliminaries

2.1
Elliptical Family of Distributions

As defined in
Muirhead 2009
and
Fang 2018
, a random vector
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to follow an elliptical distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, positive-definite scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, and density generating function
g
, if its pdf is given by

f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})=c_{p}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}g\left((\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right),\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

(1)

where
g(t)
is a non-negative Lebesgue measurable function on
[0,\infty)
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p/2-1}g(t)dt<\infty
and
|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|
denotes the determinant of matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Moreover,

c_{p}=\frac{\Gamma(p/2)}{\pi^{p/2}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p/2-1}g(t)dt\right)^{-1}

is the normalizing constant, with
\Gamma(\cdot)
representing the complete gamma function. We will use the notation
\textbf{X}\sim E\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g)
.

Members of the elliptical family of distributions are characterized by their density generating function
g
. Some examples of the elliptical family of distributions are:

•

The
multivariate normal
distribution,
\textbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and variance-covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, arises when the dgf takes the form
g(t)=\exp(-t/2),t\geq 0
.

•

The
multivariate Student-t
distribution,
\textbf{X}\sim t_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu)
, where
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is the location parameter,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the scale matrix, and
\nu
is called the degrees of freedom, is obtained when
g(t)=(1+t/\nu)^{-(\nu+p)/2},t\geq 0
.

•

The
multivariate power exponential
,
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{PE}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\beta)
, with kurtosis parameter
\beta>0
. In this case,
g(t)=\exp(-t^{\beta}/2),t\geq 0
. A particular case of the power exponential distribution is the normal distribution, which arises when
\beta=1
.

•

The
multivariate slash
,
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu)
, we get a random variable with multivariate slash distribution when
g(t)=\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu+p/2-1}\exp\{-ut/2\}du,t\geq 0,\nu>0
.

•

The
multivariate Pearson VII
distribution,
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},m,\nu)
, with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
m>p/2
, and
\nu>0
is obtained when
g(t)=(1+t/\nu)^{-m},t\geq 0
.

For more distributions belonging to this family, please
see
Fang 2018
.

2.2
Truncated Elliptical Family of Distributions

Let
{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a measurable set. We say that a random vector
\textbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has truncated elliptical distribution with support
{A}
, location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, scale parameter
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and dgf
g
, if its pdf is given by

f_{\textbf{Y}}(\textbf{y})=\frac{g\left((\textbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\textbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)}{\int_{A}g\left((\textbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\textbf{y}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)d\textbf{y}}=\frac{f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{y})}{\mbox{Pr}(\textbf{X}\in{A})},\quad\textbf{y}\in{A},

(2)

where
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g)
. We use the notation
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g,A)
. Notice that the pdf of
Y
is written as the ratio between the pdf of
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g)
and
\mbox{Pr}(\textbf{X}\in{A})
, so the pdf of
Y
exists if the pdf of
X
does, which occurs if
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a positive-definite
(
Morán-Vásquez and Ferrari 2019
, see, )
. The variable
Y
is also said to be an elliptical distribution truncated on
A
, being represented by
\textbf{Y}=\textbf{X}|\,(\textbf{X}\in A)
.

As in the elliptical family of distributions, the dgf
g
determines any distribution within the truncated elliptical class of distributions, for example, if
g(t)=(1+t/\nu)^{-(\nu+p)/2},t\geq 0,\nu>0
, then
Y
has TMVT distribution. We will denote the different members of the truncated elliptical family defined in the subsection before as
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{TN}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};A)
for the TMVN distribution,
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{T}t_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu;A)
for the TMVT distribution,
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{TPE}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\beta;A)
for the truncated multivariate power exponential,
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{TSL}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu;A)
for the truncated multivariate slash distribution, and
\textbf{Y}\sim\mbox{TPVII}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},m,\nu;A)
for the truncated multivariate Pearson VII distribution.

2.3
Slice Sampling Algorithm

Introduced by
Neal 2003
, the slice sampling algorithm is a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method for drawing random samples from a given distribution. The algorithm’s idea is to sample uniformly from the
(p+1)
-dimensional region under the graph of
f(\textbf{x})
, a non-negative function proportional to the pdf of
X
. Hence, let
Y
be an auxiliary variable such that the joint pdf of
X
and
Y
is uniform over the region
U=\{(\textbf{x},y):0<y<f(\textbf{x})\}
, i.e.,
f_{X,Y}(\textbf{x},y)\propto\mathbb{I}\left(0<y<f(\textbf{x})\right)
, with
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
being the indicator function. Therefore, we can obtain samples from the distribution of
X
by sampling jointly
(\textbf{x},y)
and then ignoring
y
values.

Figure 1:
Slice sampling algorithm for univariate random variables.

Note that generating independent random points uniformly distributed on
U
may not be easy. To overcome this problem,
Neal 2003
defined a Markov Chain that converges to an uniform distribution, in the same manner than the Gibbs sampling or Metropolis-Hastings algorithms. Then, considering Gibbs sampler steps, the slice sampling algorithm at iteration
k
works as follows: given the current value of
\textbf{x}_{k-1}
sample
y_{k}
from
Y|(\textbf{X}=\textbf{x}_{k-1})\sim\mathcal{U}\left(0,f(\textbf{x}_{k-1})\right)
, then draw
\textbf{x}_{k}
from the conditional distribution of
X
given
y_{k}
, which is uniform over the region
S_{k}=\{\textbf{x}:y_{k}<f(\textbf{x})\}
, i.e.,
\textbf{X}|(Y=y_{k})\sim\mathcal{U}(\{\textbf{x}:y_{k}<f(\textbf{x})\})
, for all
k=1,2,\ldots,n
, where
n
is the desired sample size.

Figure
1
shows the steps of the slice sampling algorithm for
X
being a univariate random variable. Given an initial value
X=x_{0}
, we draw
y_{1}
uniformly over the interval
\left(0,f(x_{0})\right)
and then we sample
x_{1}
from the conditional distribution of
X|(Y=y_{1})
, i.e., uniformly over the interval
S_{1}=\{x:y_{1}<f(x)\}
. These two steps are repeated
n
times.

3
Sampling from the Truncated Elliptical Family of Distributions

Next, we describe the proposed slice sampling algorithm with Gibbs sampler steps to generate samples
from a multivariate elliptical distribution with strictly decreasing dgf. Without loss of generality, we first consider a
p
-variate truncated elliptical distribution with zero location parameter, positive-definite scale matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, dgf
g
, and truncation region
{A}=\{\textbf{x}:\mathbf{a}<\textbf{x}<\mathbf{b}\}
,
\mathbf{a}<\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, in other words, we will consider
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell_{p}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{R};g,A)
. Here
\mathbf{R}
is a correlation matrix, such that the scale matrix can be written as
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{11},\ldots,\sigma_{pp})
. The pdf of
X
is given by

f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})\propto g\left(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}\right)\mathbb{I}\left(\textbf{x}\in{A}\right),

(3)

Now, in order to sample uniformly from the
(p+1)
-dimensional region under the plot of
f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})
, we introduce an auxiliary variable
Y
, such that the joint pdf of
X
and
Y
is

f_{\textbf{X},Y}(\textbf{x},y)\propto\mathbb{I}\left(0<y<g\left(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}\right)\right)\mathbb{I}\left(\textbf{a}<\textbf{x}<\textbf{b}\right).

(4)

It is enough to calculate the conditional distributions of
Y|\textbf{X}
and
\textbf{X}|Y
in order to established our slice
sampling algorithm with Gibbs steps to generate independent random observations from the pdf in (
4
). These are given by:

\displaystyle f_{Y|\textbf{X}}(y|\textbf{x})

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\left(0<y<g\left(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}\right)\right)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}|Y}(\textbf{x}|y)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\left(\big\{\textbf{x}:y<g\left(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}\right)\big\}\cap\{\textbf{a}<\textbf{x}<\textbf{b}\}\right).

Note that sampling
y
from the distribution of
Y|(\textbf{X}=\textbf{x})
is straightforward, but sampling from
\textbf{X}|(Y=y)
is not trivial. Thus, we use the idea of
Ho
et al. 2012
, that consists in sampling each element of
X
given the remaining elements, i.e., sampling
X_{j}
given
\textbf{x}_{-j}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{p})^{\top}
and
y
, for all
j=1,\ldots,p
. Hence, the following steps are performed to draw a random number from the distribution of
X_{j}|\textbf{x}_{-j},y
.

1.

Let
\kappa_{y}=g^{-1}(y)
. Since
g
is a strictly decreasing function, it follows that
y<g(\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x})
is equivalent to
\kappa_{y}>\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}
.

2.

Write
\textbf{x}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}=\rho^{jj}\left(x_{j}-\lambda_{j}\right)^{2}-\rho^{jj}\lambda_{j}^{2}+\eta_{j}
, where
\rho^{ij}
is the
(i,j)
th element of the inverse of
R
,
\eta_{j}=\sum_{t\neq j}\sum_{r\neq j}x_{t}x_{r}\rho^{tr}
and
\lambda_{j}=-\frac{1}{\rho^{jj}}\sum_{r\neq j}x_{r}\rho^{jr}
.

3.

Combining items 1 and 2, we obtain that
\lambda_{j}-\tau_{j}<x_{j}<\lambda_{j}+\tau_{j}
, where
\tau_{j}=\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho^{jj}}\left(\kappa_{y}-\eta_{j}\right)\right)^{1/2}

4.

Because
x_{j}\in(a_{j},b_{j})
, thereby
a_{j}^{*}=\max(a_{j},\lambda_{j}-\tau_{j})<x_{j}<\min(b_{j},\lambda_{j}+\tau_{j})=b_{j}^{*}
.

Therefore, the steps to draw
n
samples from a
p
-variate truncated elliptical distribution
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell_{p}(\textbf{0},\textbf{R};g,A)
are summarized in Algorithm 1. As seen, only univariate uniform simulations are involved in the algorithm which are fast to compute. Note also that the assumption that the dgf
g
is strictly decreasing has been used in step 1. A general case can be easily considered by studying the extrema points of
g
.

Sample size
n\geq 1
, initial value
\textbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, scale matrix
\textbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, lower bound
\textbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, upper bound
\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and strictly decreasing dgf
g(t),t\geq 0
.

Initialization;

for

i\leftarrow 1

to

n

do

Sample
y_{i}
from
Y|\textbf{x}_{i-1}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,g(\textbf{x}_{i-1}^{\top}\textbf{R}^{-1}\textbf{x}_{i-1}))
;

\kappa_{y}\leftarrow g^{-1}(y_{i})
;

for

j\leftarrow 1

to

p

do

\eta_{j}\leftarrow\displaystyle\sum_{t\neq j}\sum_{r\neq j}x_{t}x_{r}\rho^{tr};\quad

\lambda_{j}\leftarrow\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho^{jj}}\sum_{r\neq j}x_{r}\rho^{jr};\quad

\tau_{j}\leftarrow\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho^{jj}}\left(\kappa_{y}-\eta_{j}\right)\right)^{1/2}
;

Draw
x_{j}
from
X_{j}|\textbf{x}_{-j},y\sim\mathcal{U}(\max(a_{j},\lambda_{j}-\tau_{j}),\min(b_{j},\lambda_{j}+\tau_{j}))
;

\textbf{x}_{i}[j]\leftarrow x_{j};\quad

\textbf{X}[i,j]\leftarrow x_{j}
;

end for

end for

Moreover, members of the truncated elliptical family of distributions are closed under affine transformations

4
Moments of Truncated Multivariate Elliptical Distributions

This section describes an algorithm to compute the first two moments and the variance-covariance matrix of a random vector, whose distribution belongs to the elliptical family. Furthermore, we are going to apply this algorithm to some well-known distributions. Let
X
be a
p
-variate random vector that follows a truncated multivariate elliptical distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, positive-definite scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, dgf
g
, and support
{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, i.e.,
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g,A)
. The more straightforward approach for this problem is to use Monte Carlo integration. Following this approach, the estimates are given by

\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{x}_{i},\quad\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}\textbf{X}^{\top})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{x}_{i}\textbf{x}_{i}^{\top},\quad\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})}=\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}\textbf{X}^{\top})}-\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})}\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})}^{\top},

(5)

where
\textbf{x}_{i}
is the
i
th sample of the random vector
X
draws from
\mbox{TE}\ell_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma};g,A)
. However, it is well-known that the execution time needed to perform Monte Carlo integration depends on the algorithm employed to draw samples, the number of random points (
n
) used in the approximation, and the length of the random vector (
p
). Then, it depends on some variables that might represent a considerable computational effort. Nevertheless, we can save time when the random vector
X
has non-truncated components following the idea of
Galarza
et al. 2020
. They proposed to decompose
X
into two vectors,
\textbf{X}_{1}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}
, in such a way that
\textbf{X}_{1}
is the random vector of truncated variables and
\textbf{X}_{2}
is the non-truncated part, and then compute the moments for the truncated variables using any method and the remaining moments using properties of the conditional expectation. Before showing our algorithm, we state an extremely important result.

Proposition 4.1
(
Marginal and conditional distribution of the Elliptical family
)

Let
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be partitioned into two vectors,
\textbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
, such that
p=p_{1}+p_{2}
and
\textbf{X}=(\textbf{X}_{1}^{\top},\textbf{X}_{2}^{\top})^{\top}
has joint multivariate elliptical distribution as follows

\textbf{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{X}_{1}\\
\textbf{X}_{2}\end{array}\right)\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{1}+p_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\end{array}\right),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\end{array}\right);g^{(p_{1}+p_{2})}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
are location vectors,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{1}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{2}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{1}}
are dispersion matrices, and
g^{(p_{1}+p_{2})}
is the dgf.
Fang 2018
demonstrated that the elliptical family of distributions is closed under marginalization and conditioning. Hence, the distribution of
\textbf{X}_{1}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})
are also elliptical, with

\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{1}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11};g_{1}^{(p_{1})}\right),

\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12};g^{(p_{2})}_{\textbf{x}}\right).

Therefore, considering that
\textbf{X}_{1}
is the vector of truncated variables with truncation region
{A}_{1}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}
is the vector of non-truncated variables, by Proposition
4.1
we have that

\displaystyle\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11};g_{1}^{(p_{1})},A_{1})\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}),\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12};g^{(p_{2})}_{\textbf{x}}\right).

Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}=\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}=\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
. Then, it follows that
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})=\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
, that is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})=\mathbb{E}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{X}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})\end{array}\Big|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})\end{array}\right).

On the other hand, we have that
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})=\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})+\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
, with

•

\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{1},\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12},

•

\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12},

•

\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\omega_{2.1}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}),

where
\omega_{2.1}=\mathbb{E}\left(h(\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1}\right)
is the expected value of a function
h
of
\textbf{X}_{1}
depending on the conditional dgf
g_{\textbf{x}_{1}}^{(p_{2})}
. So, the variance-covariance matrix of
X
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}&\omega_{2.1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\left(\omega_{2.1}\textbf{I}_{p_{1}}-\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\end{array}\right),

Thereby, we just need Monte Carlo integration to approximate
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}
, and
\omega_{2.1}
(if necessary). A brief summary of how our algorithm works is given in Algorithm
2
.

Input:

Sample size
n\geq 1
, location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, lower bound

\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and dgf
g(t),t\geq 0
.

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12},{A}_{1}=\{\textbf{x}_{1}:\textbf{a}_{1}<\textbf{x}_{1}<\textbf{b}_{1}\}
;

Draw
\textbf{x}_{1i}
from
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{TE}\ell_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11};g^{p_{1}},A_{1}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n
;

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}\leftarrow\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{x}_{1i};\quad

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{11}\leftarrow\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{x}_{1i}\textbf{x}_{1i}^{\top}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}^{\top};\quad

\widehat{\omega}_{2.1}\leftarrow\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\textbf{x}_{1i})

\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})}\leftarrow\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}\\

;

\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})},\widehat{\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}\textbf{X}^{\top})},\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})}

•

Normal
: If
\textbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, the marginal distribution is
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11})
and the conditional distribution is
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mathcal{N}_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1})
, with
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
. Then, With the above conditions, Algorithm
2
firstly sample
\textbf{X}_{1}
from the truncated multivariate normal distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
, covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
, truncation region
{A}_{1}
and
\omega_{2.1}
equal to 1.

•

Student-
t
: If
\textbf{X}\sim t_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu),\nu>0
, the marginal and conditional distributions are
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim t_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)
and
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim t_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\lambda\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},\nu+p_{1})
, respectively, such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\lambda=(\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}))/(\nu+p_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
and
\delta_{1}(\textbf{x})=(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
. For this distribution
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})<\infty
, if
\nu>1
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})<\infty
, if
\nu>2
. Therefore, the algorithm samples
\textbf{X}_{1}
from the truncated
t
distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
, scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
,
\nu
degrees of freedom, truncation region
{A}_{1}
, and
\omega_{2.1}
computed by

\displaystyle\omega_{2.1}=\frac{\nu+\mathbb{E}(\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})}{\nu+p_{1}-2},

with
\mathbb{E}(\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})=\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1})+(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
. It is worth mention that for doubly truncated variables, the mean and the variance exist for all
\nu>0
. Then, if
X
has at least two doubly truncated variables, the mean and the variance-covariance matrix exist for all
\nu>0
. For more details about the existences of the moments see
Galarza
et al. 2020
.

•

Pearson VII
: If
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},m,\nu),m>p/2,\nu>0
, then
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})=\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})=\frac{\nu}{2m-p-2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. In this case,
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})<\infty
, if
m>(p+1)/2
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})<\infty
, if
m>(p+2)/2
. The marginal and the conditional distributions are
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},m-p_{2}/2,\nu)
and
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},m,\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}))
, respectively, such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
and
\delta_{1}(\textbf{x})=(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
. So, the proposed algorithm was implemented by sampling
\textbf{X}_{1}
from the truncated multivariate Pearson VII distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
, scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
, additional parameters
m-p_{2}/2>p_{1}/2
,
\nu>0
, and truncation region
{A}_{1}
. The constant
\omega_{2.1}
is

\omega_{2.1}=\frac{\nu+\mathbb{E}(\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})}{2m-p_{2}-2},

where
\mathbb{E}(\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
is given as in the Student-
t
distribution. For this distribution, first and second moments for doubly truncated variables exist for all
m>p/2
. Then, if
X
has at least two doubly truncated variables, the mean and the variance exist for all
m>p/2
. For more details about the existence of the moments, see Appendix
B

•

Slash
: If
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu),\nu>0
, then
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})=\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. In this case,
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})<\infty
, if
\nu>1
. The marginal distribution is
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)
and the conditional distribution is
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1};g^{(p_{2})})
, such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
,
g^{(p_{2})}(t)=\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu+p/2-1}\exp\{-u(t+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}))/2\}du
and
\delta_{1}(\textbf{x})=(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
. Note that
\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}
does not follow slash distribution, but its distribution belongs to the elliptical family (see Appendix
C
). So,
\textbf{X}_{1}
is sampled from the truncated multivariate slash distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
, scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
,
\nu
degrees of freedom and truncation region
{A}_{1}
. The constant
\omega_{2.1}
is given by

\omega_{2.1}=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{X}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu-1)}{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{X}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)}\Big|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1}\right).

This constant can be also approximated via Monte Carlo integration.

•

Contaminated Normal
: If
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{CN}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu,\rho),0<\nu,\rho<1
, then the distributions of
\textbf{X}_{1}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})
are
\mbox{CN}_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu,\rho)
and
\mbox{CN}_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},\nu_{2.1},\rho)
, respectively, such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
,
\nu_{2.1}=\nu\phi_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11})/(\nu\phi_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11})+(1-\nu)\phi_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}))
and
\phi_{p}(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
denotes the pdf of a
p
-variate normal distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, variance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
evaluated at point
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
. Thus,
\textbf{X}_{1}
is sampled from the truncated contaminated normal distribution with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
,
\nu
and
\rho
. The constant is
\omega_{2.1}
is
\omega_{2.1}={\nu}_{2.1}^{*}/\rho+1-{\nu}_{2.1}^{*}
, where
{\nu}_{2.1}^{*}=\mathbb{E}(\nu_{2.1}|\textbf{X}_{1}\in{A}_{1})
, this value is also approximated via Monte Carlo integration.

•

Power exponential
: If
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{PE}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\beta),\beta>0
, then
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})=\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
Cov(\textbf{X})=\omega\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, with
\omega=2^{1/\beta}\Gamma(\frac{n+2}{2\beta})/(n\Gamma(\frac{n}{2\beta}))
. The marginal distribution of
\textbf{X}_{1}
belongs to the elliptical family of distributions with dgf
g^{(p_{1})}(t)=t^{\frac{p-p_{1}}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}w^{\frac{p_{1}-p}{2}}(1-w)^{\frac{p-p_{1}}{2}-1}\exp\{-\frac{t^{\beta}}{2w^{\beta}}\}dw

\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11};g^{(p_{1})})
, and the conditional distribution is
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x})\sim\mbox{E}\ell_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1};g^{(p_{2})})
where
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
,
g^{(p_{2})}(t)=\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}(t+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}))^{\beta}\}
and
\delta_{1}(\textbf{x})=(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
is the squared Mahalanobis distance
(
Gómez et al. 1998

. Since sampling directly from the marginal distribution of

could be really complicated, we will use a different approach that consists of drawing points from the whole random vector of length

and then approximate the moments using Monte Carlo integration.

5
Application on Spatial Model for Censored Data

In this application we will consider the Gaussian spatial censored linear (SCL) model defined by
Lachos
et al. 2017
and
Ordoñez et al. 2018
. In this model, the data is generated from
\textbf{Z}=\textbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\xi}
, with
\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\textbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{s}_{i},\textbf{s}_{j})]=\sigma^{2}\textbf{R}(\phi)+\tau^{2}\textbf{I}_{p}
. It also has the particularity that the response variable
Z
is not fully observed. Instead, it is observed
V_{i}
and
C_{i}
at each location, for
i=1,\ldots,p
, where
C_{i}=0
and
V_{i}=Z_{i}
for an uncensored observation
Z_{i}
, and if
C_{i}=1
and
V_{i}=[V_{1i},V_{2i}]
if
Z_{i}
is censored or missing. Because of the difficulties in working directly with the observed likelihood function,
Lachos
et al. 2017
suggested using an EM-type algorithm to obtain the ML estimates of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
considering a parameterization
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Psi}
, with
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\textbf{R}(\phi)+\nu^{2}\textbf{I}_{n}
and
\nu^{2}=\tau^{2}/\sigma^{2}
, to help with the identifiability of the parameters. See also
Diggle and
Ribeiro 2007
. Hence, the EM algorithm works as it follows:

•

E-step
: Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}
be the current estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, then the conditional expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood without the constant is

Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left(\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\textbf{Z}_{c})|\textbf{V},\textbf{C},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\log|\boldsymbol{\Psi}|+n\log\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\widehat{A}^{(k)}\right],

where
\widehat{A}^{(k)}=\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1})-2\widehat{\textbf{Z}}^{(k)\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\textbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\textbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\textbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Therefore, the E-step reduces only to the computation of
\widehat{\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}\left(\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}^{\top}|\textbf{V},\textbf{C},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}\right)
and
\widehat{\textbf{Z}}^{(k)}=\mathbb{E}\left(\textbf{Z}|\textbf{V},\textbf{C},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}\right)
. In the traditional EM algorithm, we should now evaluate the conditional expectations, which is possible through the
R
packages
tmvtnorm
or
MomTrunc
, but it is computationally expensive when the proportion of censored observations is non-negligible. An alternative is to use the MCEM algorithm, which approximates the conditional expectations by using MC integration. For the SCL model, the MCE-step is performed by estimating
\widehat{\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}}^{\top}
and
\widehat{\textbf{Z}}
through Algorithm
2
.

•

M-step
: The conditional maximization step is carried out, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}
is updated by maximizing
\widehat{Q}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
over
\boldsymbol{\theta}
to obtain a new estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k+1)}
, which leads to the expressions:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\textbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1(k)}\textbf{X}\right)^{-1}\textbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1(k)}\widehat{\textbf{Z}}^{(k)},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\widehat{\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1(k)}\right)-2\widehat{\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1(k)}\textbf{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(k+1)}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top(k+1)}\textbf{X}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{-1(k)}\textbf{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(k+1)}\right],

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log|\boldsymbol{\Psi}|-\frac{1}{2\widehat{\sigma}^{2(k+1)}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\widehat{\textbf{Z}\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\right)-2\widehat{\textbf{Z}^{\top}}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\textbf{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(k+1)}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top(k+1)}\textbf{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{-1}\textbf{X}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(k+1)}\right]\right),

with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\phi,\nu^{2})^{\top}
. Note that
\widehat{\tau}^{2}
can be recovered by
\widehat{\tau}^{2(k+1)}=\widehat{\nu}^{2(k+1)}\widehat{\sigma}^{2(k+1)}
. An efficient M-step can
be easily accomplished by using, for instance, the
roptim
package
(
Pan and Pan 2020
)
. In general, the estimates of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
may vary slightly around the maximum, with a variability depending on the sample size used in the approximation. Hence, one possible final estimate of the parameters may be computed as the mean of the estimates after applying a burn-in and a thinning process.

The proposed MCEM algorithm will be applied to analyze the Missouri dioxin contamination dataset available in

ft

This dataset was firstly analyzed by

\log(Z_{i})=\beta_{0}+\xi_{i}
,

All methods were performed using 500 iterations and an exponential correlation function to take into account the variation between spatial points. 6
Conclusions

This work describes an algorithm to generate random numbers from members of the truncated elliptical family of distributions with a strictly decreasing density generating function through a slice sampling algorithm and a Gibbs sampler step. In addition, we presented an efficient approach to approximate the first and the second moment for these distributions. We briefly introduce the functions available in our
R
package
relliptical
in order to perform sample generating and estimation of the first two moments. Simulation studies were performed to investigate the properties of estimates and the robustness of our algorithm. Moreover, we compared our approach with others available in the
R
software (only for the normal and Student-
t
case), where we showed that our approach over-performed others in terms of precision and computational time. We illustrate the usefulness of truncated moments on the Missouri dioxin contamination dataset, where a spatial model for censored data was fitted.

Future extensions of the work include the extension of this method to the context of asymmetric multivariate elliptical distributions, so the fast computation of their truncated moments may lead the way to proposed more flexible and robust models relating censored models for mixed-effects models, longitudinal data, spatial models, among others. Finally, results presented in this paper can be reproduced through the
R
package
relliptical
, which is available at CRAN for download.

Appendix B
The Multivariate Pearson VII Distribution

B.1
Marginal and conditional distributions

A random variable
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is said to have a multivariate Pearson VII distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, positive-definite scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, extra parameters
m>p/2
and
\nu>0
, if its pdf is given by

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})=\frac{\Gamma(m)}{(\pi\nu)^{p/2}\Gamma(m-p/2)}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\textbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)^{-m},\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

The random vector
X
can also be represented as a scale mixture of normal (SMN) distributions, i.e.,
\textbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+U^{-1/2}\textbf{Z}
, where
Z
has a
p
-variate normal distribution with mean
\textbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and variance-covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Here,
U
follows Gamma distribution with scale parameter
m-p/2
and rate parameter
\nu/2
, where
Z
is independent of
U
. This implies that

\textbf{X}|(U=u)\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\quad\mbox{and}\quad U\sim\mathcal{G}(m-p/2,\nu/2).

Therefore, the mean and the variance-covariance matrix of
X
are

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|U))=\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad m>\frac{p+1}{2}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathrm{C}ov}(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|U))+\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|U))=\mathbb{E}(U^{-1})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\frac{\nu}{2m-p-2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\quad m>\frac{p+2}{2}.\hskip 56.9055pt

Now suppose that the vector
X
is partitioned into two random vectors
\textbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
, such that
p=p_{1}+p_{2}
, and consider the partition of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
used in Proposition
4.1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textbf{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\textbf{X}_{1}\\
\textbf{X}_{2}\end{array}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\end{array}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}\end{array}\right).

First, notice that
(\textbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\textbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})=\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})+\delta_{2.1}(\textbf{X}_{2.1})
, where
\delta_{1}(\textbf{X}_{1})=(\textbf{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
,
\delta_{2.1}(\textbf{X}_{2.1})=(\textbf{X}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}^{-1}(\textbf{X}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1})
,

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}(\textbf{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{22}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{12}
. By the results above, the marginal pdf of
\textbf{X}_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{X})\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}=\frac{\Gamma(m)}{(\pi\nu)^{p/2}\Gamma(m-p/2)}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})}{\nu}+\frac{\delta_{2.1}(\textbf{x}_{2.1})}{\nu}\right)^{-m}\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(m)}{(\pi\nu)^{p/2}\Gamma(m-p/2)}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})}{\nu}\right)^{-m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{2.1}(\textbf{x}_{2.1})}{\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\right)^{-m}\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(m-p_{2}/2)}{(\pi\nu)^{p_{1}/2}\Gamma(m-p/2)}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}|^{-1/2}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})}{\nu}\right)^{-(m-p_{2}/2)},\quad\textbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}.

Hence, the marginal distribution of
\textbf{X}_{1}
is also Pearson VII distributed with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}
,
m-p_{2}/2
and
\nu
, i.e.,
\textbf{X}_{1}\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p_{1}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},m-p_{2}/2,\nu)
. On the other hand, the conditional pdf of
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{2}|\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x}_{1},x_{2})}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(m)|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}|^{-1/2}}{(\pi(\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})))^{p_{2}/2}\Gamma(m-p_{2}/2)}\left(1+\frac{\delta_{2.1}(\textbf{x}_{2.1})}{\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\right)^{-m},\quad\textbf{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}},\,\textbf{x}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}.

Therefore, the conditional distribution has also a Pearson VII distribution with parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}
,
m
and
\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1})
, i.e.,
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p_{2}}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},m,\nu+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1}))
.

B.2
Existence of its truncated moments

Let
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{PVII}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},m,\nu),m>p/2,\nu>0
, and let
{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a truncation region of interest. Then, the expectation and the variance-covariance matrix of
X
given
\textbf{X}\in{A}
exist in the following cases:

•

If
{A}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
or
{A}
is unbounded, the vector is not truncated at all, so the expectation exists for
m>(p+1)/2
and the covariance matrix exists for
m>(p+2)/2
, as usual.

•

If
{A}
is bounded (all truncation points are finite), then
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})<\infty
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})<\infty
for all
m>p/2
, since the distribution is bounded.

•

If
X
can be partitioned into two random variables
\textbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
\textbf{X}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
such that the truncation region associated to
\textbf{X}_{1}
(say,
{A}_{1}
) is bounded, from the last item we have
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
and
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{1}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
exist for all
m>p/2
and
\nu>0
. On the other hand, it follow from Fubini’s theorem that
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
will exist if and only if
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})
exists; this occurs for all
m>(p_{2}+1)/2
. Note that
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})<\infty
also implies that
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{1},\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}\in{A})<\infty
. Additionally,
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
exists if and only if
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1})<\infty
, which holds for all
m>(p_{2}+2)/2
.

Remark B.1

It is equivalent to say that
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
exists for all
m
, if at least one
dimension containing a finite limit exists. Besides, if at least two dimensions containing finite limits exist, we have that
\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
exists for all
m>p/2

\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|\textbf{X}\in{A})
and

for

in the following three scenarios:

a)

m=2
,
\textbf{a}=(-0.80,-0.60)^{\top}
,
\textbf{b}=(\infty,\infty)^{\top}
;

b)

m=1.40
,
\textbf{a}=(-0.80,-0.60)^{\top}
,
\textbf{b}=(0.80,\infty)^{\top}
;

c)

,
\textbf{a}=(-0.80,-0.60)^{\top}

Appendix C
The Multivariate Slash Distribution

A random vector
\textbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
has multivariate slash distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, positive-definite scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\nu>0
degrees of freedom, denoted by
\textbf{X}\sim\mbox{SL}_{p}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu)
, if its pdf is given by

f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})=\nu\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p}\left(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\mathrm{d}u,\quad\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

where
\phi_{p}(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is the pdf of a
p
-variate normal distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. The pdf of a slash distribution can be evaluated through numerical methods, e.g., using the
R
function
integrate
. The random vector
X
can also be represented in the family of the SMN distributions, this is,
\textbf{X}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+U^{-1/2}\textbf{Z}
, where the random variables
U
and
Z
are both independent and have
\mathrm{Beta}(\nu,1)
and
\mathcal{N}_{p}(\textbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
distributions, respectively. Therefore, the mean and variance-covariance matrix of the random vector
X
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|U)\right)=\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\mu})=\boldsymbol{\mu}.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}|U))+\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}|U))=\mathbb{E}(U^{-1})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\quad\nu>1.\hskip 85.35826pt

Considering a partition in the same manner as used for the Pearson VII distribution, the marginal pdf of
\textbf{X}_{1}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\nu\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p}\left(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\mathrm{d}u\,d\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{Fubini}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\nu\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u\hskip 85.35826pt

\displaystyle\Rightarrow f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})=\nu\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\mathrm{d}u.\hskip 227.62204pt

Thus,
\textbf{X}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
follows a slash distribution with location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
, scale matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{1}}
and
\nu>0
degrees of freedom. On the other hand, the conditional pdf of
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{2}|\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\textbf{X}}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2})}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}=\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p}\left(\textbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}u.\hskip 56.9055pt

Then, it is possible to notice that the Slash distribution is not closed under conditioning. Furthermore, the pdf of
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
belongs to the elliptical family of distributions with dgf
g(t)=\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu+p/2-1}\exp\{-u(t+\delta_{1}(\textbf{x}_{1}))/2\}\mathrm{d}u
, i.e.,
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})\sim\mbox{E}\ell(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},\nu;g)
. To determine the mean of the random vector
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
, we compute the conditional expected value of the
i
th element of
\textbf{X}_{2}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2i}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}f_{\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{2}|\textbf{x}_{1})\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{Fubini}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)d\textbf{x}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{2.1}^{(i)}\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\mathrm{d}u=\mu_{2.1}^{(i)},\quad\forall i,\nu>0

where
\mu_{2.1}^{(i)}
represents the
i
th element of the vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}
, and
\mathbb{E}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1}
. Now, to compute the elements of the variance-covariance matrix of the conditional random vector, we first determine
\mathbb{E}(X_{2i}X_{2j}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,p_{2}
, as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{2i}X_{2j}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}x_{2j}f_{\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{2}|\textbf{x}_{1})d\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}x_{2j}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{Fubini}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}x_{2i}x_{2j}\phi_{p_{2}}\left(\textbf{x}_{2};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2.1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}\right)\mathrm{d}\textbf{x}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-1}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\left(u^{-1}\sigma_{2.1}^{(ij)}+\mu_{2.1}^{(i)}\mu_{2.1}^{(j)}\right)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{2.1}^{(ij)}\nu}{f_{\textbf{X}_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1})}\int_{0}^{1}u^{\nu-2}\phi_{p_{1}}\left(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},u^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\mu_{2.1}^{(i)}\mu_{2.1}^{(j)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\left(\frac{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu-1)}{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)}\right)\sigma_{2.1}^{(ij)}+\mu_{2.1}^{(i)}\mu_{2.1}^{(j)},\quad\nu>1,

where
\sigma_{2.1}^{(ij)}
is the
(i,j)
th element of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1}
. From these results, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X_{2i},X_{2j}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\left(\frac{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu-1)}{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)}\right)\sigma_{2.1}^{(ij)},\quad\nu>1.

Therefore, the covariance matrix of the random vector
\textbf{X}_{2}|(\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})
will be given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(\textbf{X}_{2}|\textbf{X}_{1}=\textbf{x}_{1})=\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}\left(\frac{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu-1)}{\mbox{SL}_{p_{1}}(\textbf{x}_{1};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{11},\nu)}\right)\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2.1},\quad\nu>1.
