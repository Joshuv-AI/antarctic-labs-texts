Cohen–Macaulay Complexes, Duality Groups, and the dualizing module of Out ⁡ ( F N ) {\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
Richard D. Wade and Thomas A. Wasserman

Abstract
. We explain how Cohen–Macaulay classifying spaces are ubiquitous among discrete groups that satisfy Bieri–Eckmann duality, and compare Bieri–Eckmann duality to duality results for Cohen–Macaulay complexes. We use this comparison to give a description of the dualizing module of Out ⁡ ( F N ) {\rm{Out}}(F_{N}) in terms of the local cohomology cosheaf of the spine of Outer space.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Duality groups

A group
G
is a
(Bieri–Eckmann) duality group
of dimension
n
if there exists a
G
-module
D
called the
dualizing module
and a
fundamental class

c\in H_{n}(G;D)
such that for every integer
k
and
G
–module
A
there is an isomorphism

c\cap-\colon H^{k}(G;A)\xrightarrow{\cong}H_{n-k}(G;A\otimes D),

induced by the cap product. This notion, introduced in a seminal paper of Bieri and Eckmann
[
BE73
]
, is a group-theoretic generalization of Poincaré duality. Indeed, fundamental groups of closed, aspherical manifolds are duality groups, and in this case the dualizing module
D=\mathbb{Z}
is isomorphic to the orientation module of the fundamental group. However, duality groups form a much broader family, and are particularly prominent in low-dimensional topology and geometric group theory. Arithmetic lattices, mapping class groups, and outer automorphism groups of free groups are all, up to finite-index, duality groups
[
BS73
,
Har86
,
BF00
]
.

Suppose that
G
acts freely and cocompactly on a contractible CW–complex
X
. Then the compactly supported cohomology
H_{c}^{*}(X,\mathbb{Z})
of
X
is isomorphic to
H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}G)
(as a graded
G
-module). A theorem of Bieri and Eckmann states that
G
is a duality group of dimension
n
if and only if
H_{c}^{*}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{*}(G,\mathbb{Z}G)
is torsion-free and concentrated in degree
*=n
(
[
BE73
, Theorem 4.5]
). Furthermore, in the above situation the dualizing module satisfies
D\cong H_{c}^{n}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{n}(G;\mathbb{Z}G)
. As compactly supported cohomology is only invariant up to proper homotopy, it is desirable to work with
compact
classifying spaces (equivalently, cocompact
\mathrm{EG}
s) when studying duality groups.

1.1.1.
Duality of the mapping class group via Lefschetz duality

For
{\rm{GL}}(N,\mathbb{Z})
and mapping class groups, the dualizing module can be described in terms of the homology of a particularly nice
G
-complex. For
{\rm{GL}}(N,\mathbb{Z})
this
G
-complex is the rational Tits building
[
BS73
]
, whereas for mapping class groups the dualizing module is isomorphic to the the homology of the curve complex
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)

[
Har86
]
. Via Bieri–Eckmann duality, such concrete descriptions of dualizing modules have enabled a wealth of cohomological calculations
[
CFP12
,
CP17
,
CFP19
,
MPP21
,
PS22
,
BMP+24
,
BPS23
]
.

One goal of this article is to explain why we should not expect such a clean description for
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
, so for context we will take a short detour to sketch Harer’s proof
[
Har86
]
that the dualizing module
D\cong H_{*}(\mathcal{C}(\Sigma))
when
G={{\mathrm{MCG}}(\Sigma)}
(see also
[
Bro12
,
Iva87
]
). In short, the proof combines Lefschetz duality with a good understanding of the topology of
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)
.

To give more details, let
\Sigma
be a closed, orientable surface of genus
g\geq 2
. Its Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}
is homeomorphic to an open ball of dimension
6g-6
. This is not cocompact under the action of
{{\mathrm{MCG}}(\Sigma)}
. However, one can pass to the
thick part

X\subset\mathcal{T}
formed by cutting out all
thin regions

T_{c}
consisting of marked surfaces where a simple closed curve
c
is of length
<\epsilon
. These regions are contractible, and if
\epsilon
is chosen small enough, regions overlap if and only if their defining curves are disjoint. One can use this to show that
X
is a contractible manifold with boundary
\partial X\simeq\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)
. Harer proves that
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres of dimension
2g-2
, which allows him to apply Lefschetz duality to see that
H^{*}_{c}(X;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{6g-6-*}(X,\partial X;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{6g-7-*}(\partial X;\mathbb{Z})
is a torsion-free group concentrated in
*=4g-5
. This gives an elegant description of the dualizing module of
{{\mathrm{MCG}}(\Sigma)}
in terms of the homology of the curve complex.

1.1.2.
The failure of Harer’s approach for
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

It is at this point one begins to see the difficulty in proving analogous results for outer automorphism groups of free groups. The analogous object to
\mathcal{T}
, which is Culler–Vogtmann’s Outer space
\mathcal{CV}_{N}
, is not a manifold, so one does not have access to Lefschetz duality. Furthermore, although a common bordification of
\mathcal{CV}_{N}
is at the heart of the duality proofs of both
[
BF00
]
and
[
BSV18
]
, the homotopy type of its boundary is unknown (see
[
BG20
]
for a detailed discussion).

However, Vogtmann
[
Vog90
]
proved that Outer space does satisfy a strong local property called the
Cohen–Macaulay (CM)
property, which implies that even though
\mathcal{CV}_{N}
does not satisfy Lefschetz duality, it does satisfy
CM duality
. This is our second main topic of discussion.

1.2.
Combinatorial (co)sheaves, the Cohen–Macaulay property, and CM duality

Let
X
be a locally finite simplicial complex. We blur the destinction between
X
and its associated poset of simplices, ordered by inclusion. A
combinatorial sheaf

\mathcal{F}
is a covariant functor from
X
to the category of abelian groups. A
combinatorial cosheaf

\mathcal{G}
is a contravariant functor from
X
to the category of abelian groups. In each of these cases, each simplex is assigned an abelian group, and there are compatible maps between these groups ‘going up’ (with respect to inclusion of simplices) in the case of a sheaf, and ‘going down’ in the case of a cosheaf. There is a natural way of taking (compactly supported) cohomology with coefficients in a combinatorial sheaf, and taking (locally finite) homology with coefficients in a combinatorial cosheaf. Importantly, taking local homology at simplices (in the classical sense) gives a combinatorial sheaf on
X
, and similarly local cohomology forms a combinatorial cosheaf.

A locally finite simplicial complex is a
homology CM complex
of dimension
n
if the reduced homology
\tilde{H}_{*}(X;\mathbb{Z})
is concentrated in degree
n
and the local homology at each point is concentrated in degree
n
. If only the local condition holds, then
X
is a
local homology CM complex
. In this case, every maximal simplex will be of dimension
n
, and there is a
fundamental class

[X]\in H^{\textnormal{lf}}_{n}(X;h^{n})
that lives in the top-dimensional locally finite homology with coefficients in the local cohomology cosheaf
h^{n}
(this is described in Section
2
). Poincaré duality for non-compact manifolds splits into two statements: one can either take compactly supported cohomology and regular homology, or instead use cohomology and locally finite (or Borel–Moore) homology. In the case of duality for locally CM spaces, a further split happens where one either takes twisted coefficients with the local homology sheaf
h_{n}
on the left-hand side, or the local cohomology cosheaf on the right-hand side:

Theorem A

(CM Duality,
[
WW23
]
)
.

Let
X
be a locally finite,
n
-dimensional, local homology CM complex. Then there are isomorphisms

\displaystyle H_{c}^{k}(X;h_{n})

\displaystyle\to H_{n-k}(X;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle H^{k}(X;h_{n})

\displaystyle\to H^{\textnormal{lf}}_{n-k}(X;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle H_{c}^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\to H_{n-k}(X;h^{n})

\displaystyle H^{k}(X;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\to H^{\textnormal{lf}}_{n-k}(X;h^{n})

induced by the cap product with the fundamental class
[X]\in H_{n}^{\textnormal{lf}}(X;h^{n})
.

The proof of Theorem
A
is given in
[
WW23
]
, where we also discuss its relationship to related results, such as Verdier duality. For this introduction, we will limit ourselves to highlighting four salient features: the construction does not require working over a field, does not require passing to a derived category, is simplicial (hence amenable to explicit calculation), and is given by an explicit cap product.

There is a stronger, homotopical version of the CM condition, which will take a smaller role in this paper.

). Quillen’s 1978 article

1.3.
New descriptions of dualizing modules

If
G
is a duality group with a locally CM classifying space, CM duality can be applied to give alternative descriptions of dualizing modules. In
[
WW23
]
, we show that the cap product in CM duality is equivariant with respect to the
G
-action, therefore:

Corollary B
.

If
G
is a duality group of dimension
d
and
G
admits a free, cocompact action on a contractible, locally finite, homology CM complex
X
of dimension
n
, then the dualizing module is
G
-equivariantly isomorphic to
H_{n-d}(X;h^{n})
, where
h^{n}
is the degree
n
local cohomology cosheaf of
X
.

This gives a homological description of the dualizing module of
G
, at the cost of using more complicated coefficients. However, this alternative description may be more amenable to calculation.

If the space
X
has the same dimension as
G
, then there is a further possible description of the dualizing module in terms of
global section group

\Gamma(X)
of the local homology sheaf. Let
{\rm{Hom}}_{c}(\Gamma(X),\mathbb{Z})
be the group of
compactly determined
dual homomorphisms
f\colon\Gamma(X)\to\mathbb{Z}
. (A homomorphism
f
is compactly determined if there exists a compact set
K
such that
f(s)=0
for every section
s
that vanishes on
K
.) We say that
the local homology sheaf on
X
is semistable
if the inverse system
\{\Gamma(K)\}_{K}
of sections of the local homology sheaf over finite subcomplexes satisfies the Mittag-Leffler condition (see Section
2.6
). Recall that while dualizing modules are always torsion-free, they are not necessarily free as abelian groups (see also Question
4
).

Theorem C
.

Let
G
,
X
be as in Corollary
B
. Furthermore suppose that
{\rm{cd}}(G)=\dim(X)
. Then the local homology sheaf on
X
is semistable if and only if the dualizing module
D
of
G
is free abelian. If this is the case then

D\cong{\rm{Hom}}_{c}(\Gamma(X),\mathbb{Z}),

where
\Gamma(X)
is the group of global sections of the local homology sheaf of
X
.

Corollary
B
and Theorem
C
show that the dualizing module in these situations can be read off from properties of both the local homology sheaf and the local cohomology cosheaf. The work surrounding Theorem
C
is influenced by, and uses parts of, Geoghegan’s work on semistability in
[
Geo08
, Chapters 12-13]
.

1.5.
A bound for the top-dimensional cohomology of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

Bieri–Eckmann duality allows one to translate problems from high-dimensional cohomology to low-dimensional homology, at the cost of understanding the dualizing module. If
G
is the mapping class group or
{\rm{GL}}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
d
is the virtual cohomological dimension of
G
, then
H^{d}(G;\mathbb{Q})=0
. This is a result of Church–Farb–Putman
[
CFP12
]
in the case of the mapping class group, and Lee and Szczarba in the case of
{\rm{GL}}(n,\mathbb{Z})

[
LS76
]
, and is obtained by a vanishing result for the coinvariants of the associated dualizing module. In the case of
G={\rm{GL}}(n,\mathbb{Z})
, the rational cohomology of
G
also vanishes in codimensions one and two
[
CP17
,
BMP+24
]
. The analogous codimension-one vanishing result was shown not to hold in the mapping class group in work of Chan–Galatius–Payne
[
CGP21
]
.

Infamously, one cannot prove any such vanishing result for
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
: the vcd of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{7})
is 11 and Bartholdi
[
Bar16
]
showed that
H^{11}({\rm{Out}}(F_{7});\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}
. The proof is computer-based, and it is not clear if there is a geometric origin for this cohomology class. While we cannot solve this problem, the methods in this paper allow us to obtain a resolution of the dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
via the chain complex for
H_{*}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}},h^{2N-3}_{r})
. We describe this in Section
4
, and use it to prove the following:

Theorem E
.

Let
\rho\in{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
be a rose in the spine of reduced outer space, and let
{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)<{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
be the stabilizer of this rose. Then

\dim(H^{2N-3}({\rm{Out}}(F_{N});\mathbb{Q}))\leq\dim((h^{2N-3}(\rho)\otimes\mathbb{Q})_{{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)}),

where
h^{2N-3}(\rho)
is the local cohomology of the reduced spine at this rose.

A detailed investigation of
h^{2N-3}(\rho)
as a
{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)
-representation is a topic for future research.

1.6.
The CM condition does not imply Bieri–Eckmann duality

{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is not the only example of a duality group with a CM classifying space: in fact, CM complexes are ubiquitous among classifying spaces of duality groups. For instance, arithmetic groups and mapping class groups of surfaces are fundamental groups of orbifolds, and Brady and Meier proved that a right-angled Artin group (RAAG) is a duality group if and only if its associated Salvetti complex is a local homology CM complex
[
BM01
, Theorem C and Lemma 5.1]
. From this, one might hope that every fundamental group of a finite, closed (i.e. no free faces), aspherical, local homology CM complex is a duality group. However, we will see that this is not the case.

1.6.1.
An example of Atanasov

One does not need to leave the world of RAAGs to find an aspherical CM complex
Y
such that
\pi_{1}(Y)
is not a duality group. Atanasov
[
Ata14
]
studied the two dimensional situation, and found an aspherical local-homotopy-CM complex (see Figure
2
) with fundamental group
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}^{2}
which is not a duality group.

Using a version of CM duality, Atanasov
[
Ata14
]
gave sufficient conditions for fundamental groups of acyclic 2-complexes to be duality groups, although they require the singular parts of the 2-complex to be quite well-behaved (for instance a generic one relator group is a 2-dimensional duality group, but its presentation complex is not covered by Atanasov’s criterion).

Figure 2.
The group
G=\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is not a duality group but is the fundamental group of an aspherical local homotopy CM complex
Y
which has no free faces. The space
Y
is given by gluing two tori
T_{1,1}
each with one boundary to a disc.

1.6.2.
The CM property and visible irreducibility

Atanasov’s complex
Y
given in Figure
2
satisfies slightly stronger conditions than having no free faces: the complex is
visibly irreducible
in the sense of Louder–Wilton (cf.
[
Wil24
, Section 4]
, or Section
5
below). This is a condition for a complex to have no ‘obvious’ simplifications, and plays a key role in Louder–Wilton’s proof that that one-relator groups with negative immersions are coherent
[
LW24
]
. In Theorem
F
, we show that visible irreduciblity can be described in terms of the local homology sheaf.

Theorem F
.

Let
Y
be a 2-complex. The following are equivalent:

•

Y
is visibly irreducible.

•

Y
is a local homology CM complex and face maps of the local homology sheaf are surjective.

•

Y
is a local homology CM complex and face maps of the local cohomology cosheaf are injective.

Theorem
F
, combined with Atanasov’s example, shows that even with strong restrictions on the local homology sheaf, fundamental groups of aspherical CM complexes may not be duality groups.

1.7.
Further Questions

Question 1
.

Does every duality group of finite type have a classifying space that is a local homology CM complex?

This fits in a series of questions going back to Wall
[
Wal79
]
, who asked whether every Poincaré duality group admits a closed manifold as its classifying space. That question was answered in the negative by Davis
[
Dav98
]
: there are Poincaré duality groups of cohomological dimension
\geq 4
that are not finitely presented. Davis
[
Dav00
, Question 3.5]
then asked if a finitely-presented, torsion-free group satisfies Poincaré duality over a ring
R
, does that group admit a closed
R
-homology manifold as its classifying space? This question has a negative answer when
R=\mathbb{Q}

[
Fow12
]
, but is open for
R=\mathbb{Z}
. Note that a negative answer to this question would not imply a negative answer to Question
1
.

Question 2
.

Suppose
Y
is a finite, aspherical, local homology CM simplicial complex with fundamental group
G

Y
(or alternatively, its universal cover

Given the example in Figure

is a duality group.

is a duality group one can compare Bieri–Eckmann duality and CM duality via the isomorphisms

2.
Sheaf cohomology and cosheaf homology on simplicial complexes

This section summarises the combinatorial sheaf theory needed for this paper; this is described in full in
[
WW23
]
. The main objects of interest to us in this paper are discrete groups (usually denoted
G
) and locally finite simplicial complexes (usually denoted
X
). We will also assume
X
is
oriented
, so the vertices of each simplex have a preferred ordering. In particular there is a consistent notion of the
front
and
back

k
-dimensional faces of simplices.

2.1.
Combinatorial sheaves and cosheaves

The simplices of a complex form a poset with respect to inclusion, where
\sigma\leq\tau
if
\sigma
is contained in
\tau
. A
combinatorial sheaf
on
X
is a functor
\mathcal{F}
from the poset of simplicies to the category of abelian groups. In particular there is an abelian group
\mathcal{F}(\sigma)
assigned to each simplex and a homomorphism

\mathcal{F}(\sigma<\tau)\colon\mathcal{F}(\sigma)\to\mathcal{F}(\tau)

whenever
\sigma<\tau
. Requiring
\mathcal{F}
to be a functor is equivalent to the condition that

\mathcal{F}(\rho<\tau)=\mathcal{F}(\sigma<\tau)\circ\mathcal{F}(\rho<\sigma)

whenever
\rho<\sigma<\tau
. A
combinatorial cosheaf
does the same thing except the maps go in the opposite direction; more precisely, a combinatorial cosheaf is a contravariant functor from the poset of simplices of
X
to the category of abelian groups.

One can study sheaves and cosheaves on X valued in other categories, for instance
R
–modules. However in this paper, whenever we talk about a (co)sheaf, it will be valued in the category of abelian groups.

2.2.
The local homology sheaf and the local cohomology cosheaf.

Let
\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)
be the neighbourhood of
\sigma
spanned by the interiors of the simplices
\tau\geq\sigma
(including the interior of
\sigma
itself). Then
X-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma)
is a subcomplex of
X
. Working over
\mathbb{Z}
, let
C^{\sigma}_{\bullet}(X)=C_{\bullet}(X,X-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma))=C_{\bullet}(X)/C_{\bullet}(X-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma))
be the associated relative chain complex. The
k
th local homology of
X
at
\sigma
is

h_{k}(\sigma):=H_{k}(C_{\bullet}^{\sigma}(X)).

As
C^{\sigma}_{\bullet}(X)
is spanned by the simplices
\tau\geq\sigma
, this gives a simple combinatorial description of
h_{k}(\sigma)
.

If
\sigma\leq\tau
then we can view
C^{\tau}_{\bullet}(X)
as a quotient complex of
C^{\sigma}_{\bullet}(X)
, which induces a map

h_{k}(\sigma<\tau):h_{k}(\sigma)\to h_{k}(\tau),

induced by the associated map of pairs
(X,X-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma))\to(X,X-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\tau))
. Note that if
\sigma
is maximal then

C^{\sigma}_{\bullet}(X)=\langle\sigma\rangle

and
h_{k}(\sigma)=\mathbb{Z}
in the dimension of
\sigma
and is trivial in every other degree.

We say that
X
is a
local homology CM complex of dimension
n
if
h_{k}(\sigma)=0
unless
k=n
for all
\sigma\in X
. This implies that every maximal simplex is
n
-dimensional, and as there are no
n+1
simplices we have a simpler description

h_{n}(\sigma)=\ker(C^{\sigma}_{n}(X)\to C_{n-1}^{\sigma}(X))

of the
n
th local homology. In particular, when
X
is locally CM all the local homology groups are free abelian.

Taking
C^{\bullet}_{\sigma}(X)={\rm{Hom}}(C_{\bullet}^{\sigma}(X),\mathbb{Z})
gives dual cochain complexes which compute local cohomology. This gives a (graded) cosheaf
h^{*}
on
X
, with corestriction maps
h^{\ast}(\tau>\sigma):h^{\ast}(\tau)\to h^{\ast}(\sigma)
. Typically for us
X
will be a local homology CM complex and we will only be interested in the degree
n
parts
h_{n}
and
h^{n}
.

2.3.
Sheaf cohomology and cosheaf homology

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a combinatorial sheaf on
X
and let
X_{k}
be the set of
k
-simplices of
X
. We define

C^{k}(X;\mathcal{F}):=\prod_{\sigma\in X_{k}}\mathcal{F}(\sigma).

Let
|\sigma:\tau|=(-1)^{i}
if
\sigma
is the
i
th facet of
\tau
, and set
|\sigma:\tau|=0
otherwise. If
\alpha\in\mathcal{F}(\sigma)
and
\sigma\in X_{k}
, we define a coboundary map termwise on
C^{\bullet}(X;\mathcal{F})
by

\delta\alpha=\sum_{\tau\in X_{k+1}}|\sigma:\tau|\mathcal{F}(\sigma<\tau)(\alpha).

One can verify that this gives a cochain complex whose cohomology we define to be the
cohomology of
X
with coefficients in
\mathcal{F}
. When
X
is locally finite, there is a compactly supported version of this where

(1)

C_{c}^{k}(X;\mathcal{F}):=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in X_{k}}\mathcal{F}(\sigma),

with the coboundary maps defined the same way.

Similarly, the
cosheaf homology
of a cosheaf
\mathcal{G}
is the homology of the chain complex given by

C_{k}(X;\mathcal{G}):=\bigoplus_{\sigma\in X_{k}}\mathcal{G}(\sigma)

and a boundary operator defined by

\partial\alpha=\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\mathcal{G}(\sigma>\sigma_{i})(\alpha)

for
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\sigma)
and
\sigma\in X_{k}
, with
\sigma_{i}
denoting the
i
th face of
\sigma
. When
X
is locally finite, we can also define
locally finite homology with coefficients in
\mathcal{G}
by taking homology of the chain complex with terms

C_{k}^{\textnormal{lf}}(X;\mathcal{G}):=\prod_{\sigma\in X_{k}}\mathcal{G}(\sigma),

and the same boundary operator as above.

2.4.
Cap products and the fundamental class

Suppose that
X
is
n
-dimensional and locally finite. Then
h^{n}(\sigma)=\langle\sigma^{*}\rangle
for every
n
-simplex
\sigma
, and the formal sum

[X]:=\sum_{\sigma\in X_{n}}\sigma^{*}

determines an element of
H^{\textnormal{lf}}_{n}(X,h^{n})
called the
fundamental class

[
WW23
, Section 4.2.1]
. If
X
is connected and locally CM, then
H^{\textnormal{lf}}_{n}(X,h^{n})=\mathbb{Z}
and is generated by the fundamental class. In
[
WW23
]
, we give formulations of the cap product required for all permutations of coefficients given in Theorem
A
, however in this paper we will only give the cap product

H_{k}^{\textnormal{lf}}(X,h^{n})\otimes H^{l}_{c}(X,\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\cap}H_{k-l}(X,h^{n}),

induced by the map

(2)

\eta\cap\psi=\sum_{\sigma\in X_{k}}\psi(\sigma_{\geq k-l})h^{n}(\sigma>\sigma_{\leq k-l})(\eta_{\sigma}),

where
\eta\in C_{k}^{\textnormal{lf}}(X,h^{n})
,
\psi\in C^{l}_{c}(X,\mathbb{Z})
, and
\eta_{\sigma}\in h^{n}(\sigma)
is the value of
\eta
at
\sigma
. If
\sigma=[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}]
then
\sigma_{\geq k-l}=[v_{k-l},\ldots,v_{k}]
is the
back
l
–face
and
\sigma_{\leq k-l}=[v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-l}]
is the
front (
k-l
)–face
of
\sigma
.

When
\eta=[X]
is the fundamental class,
\eta_{\sigma}=\sigma^{*}
for every
n
-simplex
\sigma
and (
2
) can be unpacked further (see
[
WW23
, Section 4.2.2]
for details).

2.5.
Relative versions of CM duality

Let
X
be a locally finite simplicial complex and
L
be a full subcomplex of
X
, and let
L^{\textnormal{vc}}
be the full complex spanned by vertices in
X-L
(we call
L^{\textnormal{vc}}
the
vertex complement
of
L
). The cap product defined in the above setting applies for pairs
(X,L)
, as long as in any simplex
\sigma
, the orientation of
X
orders vertices in
L
before those in
L^{\textnormal{vc}}
. This is so that back faces, when it is appropriate, lie in
L^{\textnormal{vc}}
(see
[
WW23
, Section 2.5.4]
for details).

Theorem 2.1

(Relative CM Duality)
.

Let
X
be locally finite homology CM complex of dimension
n
with local homology sheaf
h_{*}
and local cohomology sheaf
h^{*}
. Let
L
be a full subcomplex of
X
and orient
L^{\textnormal{vc}}
before
L
. Then capping with the fundamental induces isomorphisms

(3)

\displaystyle H^{l}_{c}(L;h_{n}|_{L})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{[\bar{X}]\cap-}H_{n-l}(X,L^{\textnormal{vc}};\mathbb{Z}),\text{ and}

(4)

\displaystyle H^{l}_{c}(X,L;\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{[\bar{X}]\cap-}H_{n-l}(L^{\textnormal{vc}};h^{n}|_{L^{\textnormal{vc}}}).

There are other versions of these isomorphisms given in Theorem 4.26 of
[
WW23
]
; we only give two relevant ones here.

2.6.
Semistability and the global section group

The subcomplexes of
X
form a direct system with respect to inclusion. If
K
is a subcomplex, we use

\Gamma(K)=H^{0}(K;h_{*}|_{K})

to denote the group of sections of the local homology sheaf of
X
over
K
. If
K\subset L
, then restriction of sections gives a restriction homomorphism
r_{LK}:\Gamma(L)\to\Gamma(K)
. Local finiteness of
X
implies that we can restrict our attention to sections over finite subcomplexes: these form an inverse system whose limit is still the global section group
\Gamma(X)
.

An inverse system
\{A_{i}\}
with maps
\{f_{ij}:A_{i}\to A_{j}\}
indexed by a filtered poset
I
is
semistable
if for all
i\in I
there exists
j\geq i
such that for all
k\geq j\geq i
the map
f_{ki}
has the same image in
A_{i}
independently of
k
. This is also known as the
Mittag-Leffler
condition.

Definition 2.2

(Semistability of the local homology sheaf)
.

We say that the local homology sheaf on
X
is
semistable
if the inverse system of sections of
h_{*}
over finite subcomplexes is semistable.

If
A
is an abelian group, we say a homomorphism
f\colon\Gamma(X)\to A
is
compactly determined
if there exists a finite subcomplex
K\subset X
such that
f(s)=0
whenever a section
s\in\Gamma(X)
vanishes on
K
(this implies the image of a section under
f
is determined by its restriction to
K
). We use
{\rm{Hom}}_{c}(\Gamma(X),A)
to denote the group of compactly determined homomorphisms from
\Gamma(X)
to
A
. Using work of Geoghegan
[
Geo08
]
, in
[
WW23
]
we prove the following:

Theorem 2.3

(
[
WW23
]
, Corollary 3.19)
.

Suppose that
X
is locally CM. Then the local homology sheaf on
X
is semistable if and only if
H_{0}(X;h^{*})
is a free abelian group. If this is the case, then

H_{0}(X;h^{*})\cong{\rm{Hom}}_{c}(\Gamma(X),\mathbb{Z}).

This allows us to prove Theorem
C
from the introduction.

Theorem 2.4

(Theorem
C
)
.

Let
G
be a duality group that admits a free, cocompact action on a contractible, locally finite, homology CM complex
X
of dimension equal to
{\rm{cd}}(G)
. Then the local homology sheaf on
X
is semistable if and only if the dualizing module
D
of
G
is free abelian. If this is the case then

D\cong{\rm{Hom}}_{c}(\Gamma(X),\mathbb{Z}),

where
\Gamma(X)
is the group of global sections of the local homology sheaf of
X
.

Proof.

When the dimension of
X
is the same as the cohomological dimension of
G
, under the above hypothesis we have
D\cong H_{0}(X;h^{*})
by CM duality (Theorem
A
).
∎

3.
The dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

In this section we discuss the dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
and its relationship with the spine of Outer space, and a combinatorial version of Bux–Smillie–Vogtmann’s
Jewel space
.

3.1.
Poset preliminaries

We have already defined combinatorial sheaves and cosheaves using the
simplex poset
of a simplicial complex, which is the partially ordered set of simplices with respect to inclusion. Conversely, every poset
P
has an associated simplicial complex
|P|
called the
geometric realization
. The complex
|P|
has vertex set
P
and a
k
-simplex
\sigma
for every chain
p_{0}<p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}
of
k+1
elements in
P
. As a result, it is convenient to sometimes blur the distinction between a poset and its associated simplicial complex (for example, we will talk about the homotopy type of a poset, meaning the homotopy type of its geometric realization). A map
f:P\to Q
between posets is a
poset map
if it is order-preserving.

We use
P^{\prime}
to denote the
poset of finite chains
in
P
, where each element of
P^{\prime}
is a finite length chain in
P
and
c_{1}<c_{2}
if
c_{2}
is a refinement of
c_{1}
. The geometric realization of
P^{\prime}
is the barycentric subdivision of the geometric realization of
P
. This gives a convenient way to work with barycentric subdivisions.

For
p,p^{\prime}\in P
we define the subposets

\displaystyle p_{<}

\displaystyle:=\{q\in P:p<q\}

{}_{<}p

\displaystyle:=\{q\in P:q<p\}

\displaystyle(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\{q\in P:p<q<p^{\prime}\},

and
p_{\leq}
,
{}_{\leq}p
are defined similarly. The
join

P\ast Q
of two posets has underlying set
P\cup Q
with partial orders on
P
and
Q
given by restriction, with the additional relation
p<q
for all
p\in P
,
q\in Q
. If
\sigma=p_{0}<p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}
is a
k
-simplex in
|P|
, then its link is given by the join

(5)

{}_{<}(p_{0})\ast(p_{0},p_{1})\ast\cdots\ast(p_{k-1},p_{k})\ast(p_{k})_{<}

We use
\textnormal{ht}_{P}(p)
to denote the
height
of an element
p\in P
, dropping the subscript where the overlying poset is understood. This is defined to be the dimension of
{}_{\leq}p
, or equivalently the length of the longest chain in
{}_{<}p
.

Definition 3.1

(Homotopy CM complexes)
.

A simplicial complex
X
is an
n
-dimensional homotopy CM complex
if it is contractible or homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
n
-spheres, and the link
\mathrm{lk}_{X}(\sigma)
of every
k
-simplex
\sigma\in X
is contractible or homotopy equivalent to a wedge product of a set of
(n-k-1)
–spheres. We say a poset is homotopy CM if its geometric realization is.

A homotopy CM complex is also homology CM. Using (
5
), one can obtain the following characterisation of the homotopy CM property (we learned this from Section 2 of
[
Vog90
]
, where it appears with slightly different notation).

Proposition 3.2

(
[
Qui78
]
)
.

A poset
P
is
n
-homotopy CM if and only if:

•

P
is
n
-spherical,

•

{}_{<}p
is
(\textnormal{ht}(p)-1)
-spherical for all
p\in P
,

•

(p,p^{\prime})
is
(\textnormal{ht}(p)-\textnormal{ht}(p^{\prime})-2)
-spherical whenever
p<p^{\prime}
in
P
, and

•

p_{<}
is
(n-\textnormal{ht}(p)-1)
-spherical for all
p\in P
.

We will also need
Quillen’s Fiber Lemma
(
[
Wal81
]
has a concise proof).

Theorem 3.3

(Quillen’s Fiber Lemma
[
Qui78
]
)
.

Let
f\colon P\to Q
be a poset map. If
f^{-1}(_{\leq}q)
is contractible for all
q\in Q
, then
f
is a homotopy equivalence. If
f^{-1}(q_{\leq})
is contractible for all
q\in Q
, then
f
is a homotopy equivalence.

Corollary 3.4

(Monotone retractions are homotopy equivalences)
.

Let
r:P\to P
be a monotone increasing or decreasing (either
r(p)\leq p
for all
p
or
r(p)\geq p
for all
p
) poset map such that
r^{2}=r
. Then
r
induces a homotopy equivalence between
P
and
r(P)
.

Proof.

Let us only consider the case that
r(p)\leq p
for all
p
. If
q\in r(P)
then
r(q)=q
. If
q\leq r(p)
then
q\leq p
. Hence
r^{-1}(q_{\leq})
has
q
as a minimal element, so is contractible. Hence
r
is a homotopy equivalence by the Fiber Lemma.
∎

We call a poset map
r
as in Corollary
3.4
a
retraction
, for obvious reasons. The above corollary holds under weaker conditions: the map
r
can be increasing at some points and decreasing at others and
r^{2}=r
is not needed (see
[
Qui78
,
Bjö95
]
).

3.2.
Thickening the spine of Outer space

A
free splitting
of
F_{N}
is a minimal action of
F_{N}
on a simplicial tree with trivial edge stabilizers. Two splittings are equivalent if they are
F_{N}
-equivariantly homeomorphic. One can also think of free splittings as marked graphs-of-groups with trivial edge groups. If
T,T^{\prime}
are free splittings we say that
T<T^{\prime}
if
T
can be obtained from
T^{\prime}
by collapsing a forest. With this partial order, the set of free splittings forms a poset
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
whose geometric realization is called the
free splitting complex
. The
reduced free splitting complex

{\mathcal{FS}^{r}_{N}}
is the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
spanned by trees
T
such that
T/F_{N}
contains no separating edges.

We use
\mathcal{X}_{N}\subset\mathcal{FS}_{N}
to denote the
spine of Outer space
: this is the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
spanned by splittings where the action of
F_{N}
is free (equivalently,
T/F_{N}
has fundamental group
F_{N}
). The
reduced spine

{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
is defined similarly. We define
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
and
{\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{r}}
to be the full subcomplexes of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{FS}^{r}_{N}}
that are spanned by trees
T
such that the action of
F_{N}
on
T
is
not
free. Equivalently, these are the full subcomplexes of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{FS}^{r}_{N}}
spanned by vertices that are not in the spine.

Let
\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
be the poset of chains (i.e. barycentric subdivision) of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. Vertices in
\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
are given by chains
c=T_{1}<T_{2}<\cdots<T_{k}
of elements of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
, and
k
-simplices correspond to chains of chains
c_{0}<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{k}
, where the partial order on chains is given by inclusion. For a chain
c\in\mathcal{FS}^{\prime}
let
\min(c)
and
\max(c)
be the first and last elements of the chain, respectively.

Definition 3.5

(Thickened spines)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{N}
be the subcomplex of
\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
spanned by chains
c
such that
\max(c)\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
. Let
\mathcal{K}_{N}
be the subcomplex of
{\mathcal{FS}^{r}_{N}}^{\prime}
spanned by chains
c
such that
\max(c)\in{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
. We call
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N}

thickened spines
.

The spaces
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N}
are combinatorial thickenings of the spine of Outer space and reduced Outer space respectively, which fulfill the same role as Jewel space (see Remark
3.9
below). Such an approach also appears in work of Brück–Gupta
[
BG20
]
, who studied the homotopy type of the boundaries of these complexes.

Proposition 3.6
.

There is a monotone map
\chi\colon\mathcal{L}_{N}\to\mathcal{L}_{N}
with image
\mathcal{X}_{N}^{\prime}
given by taking
c\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
to the subchain
\chi(c)
of
c
consisting of trees in
\mathcal{X}_{N}
. The spine
\mathcal{X}_{N}
is contractible by the main theorem of
[
CV86
]
, therefore so is
\mathcal{L}_{N}
. The map
\chi
restricts to a monotone map of
\mathcal{K}_{N}
with image
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}^{\prime}
, so
\mathcal{K}_{N}
is also contractible by
[
CV86
]
.

Proof.

If
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
is a subchain of
c\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
then
\chi(c^{\prime})
is a subchain of
\chi(c)
so
\chi
is a poset map that restricts to the identity on
\mathcal{X}_{N}^{\prime}
. Hence
\chi
is monotone, and in particular is a homotopy equivalence. The same argument applies to
\mathcal{K}_{N}
, and both
\mathcal{X}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
are shown to be contractible in
[
CV86
]
.
∎

Definition 3.7

(Boundaries of the thickened complexes)
.

Let
\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
be the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{L}_{N}
spanned by chains
c
such that
\min(c)\in\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. Let
\partial\mathcal{K}_{N}
be the full subcomplex of
\mathcal{K}_{N}
spanned by chains
c
such that
\min(c)\in{\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{r}}
.

See Figure
3
for visual aid. The following result appears in
[
BG20
]
in a slightly different guise – we sketch how their results apply in this setting below.

Theorem 3.8
.

The geometric realization of
\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
is homotopy equivalent to
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. The geometric realization of
\partial\mathcal{K}_{N}
is homotopy equivalent to
{\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{r}}
.

Sketch proof.

We apply the Fiber Lemma. There is a poset map
\phi\colon\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}\to\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
given by taking a chain
c\in\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
to the subchain spanned by trees in
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. Let
c=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{k}
be a chain in
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
. Every point in
d\in\phi^{-1}(c)
is obtained by adding a nontrivial chain
T_{k+1}<T_{k+2}<\cdots<T_{l}
to
c
with each
T_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
. Points in
\phi^{-1}(c_{\leq})
are obtained by taking such a
d
and making a further refinement
d^{\prime}
to possibly include more trees in
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. The map
d^{\prime}\to d
given by forgetting the additional points in
\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
is a monotone retraction that induces a homotopy equivalence between
\phi^{-1}(c_{\leq})
and
\phi^{-1}(c)
(see also Lemma 7.1(1) of
[
BG20
]
).

Let
i\geq k+1
. The graph
T_{k}/F_{N}
is obtained from
T_{i}/F_{N}
by collapsing a subgraph. As
T_{i}\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
and
T_{k}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{N}
, at least one component of this subgraph has nontrivial fundamental group. Therefore there exists
T_{k}<T_{i}^{\prime}<T_{i}
such that
T_{i}^{\prime}
is a
core extension
of
T_{k}
– the collapsed edges of
T_{i}^{\prime}/F_{N}
form a core graph (a graph with no valence one vertices). If each

is chosen maximally, the map taking the chain

to

gives a monotone retraction from

to the poset of core extensions of

The map

restricts to a map

. The same argument as above shows that this is a homotopy equivalence, using Lemma 7.1(3) and Proposition 7.3(2) of
[
BG20
]
in place of Lemma 7.1(1) and Proposition 7.2 of
[
BG20
]
.

∎

.

3.3.
The thickenings
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N}
are homotopy CM

In
[
Vog90
]
, Vogtmann showed that the spine of Outer space, and its reduced version, are homotopy CM complexes. We sketch below how her work shows that this is also true for
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N}
. Very roughly, the link of a vertex
T
in the spine decomposes as a join with collapses of
T
on one side and blow-ups of
T
on the other. Vogtmann has to carefully analyse both directions in her work; we will see that the ‘blow-up’ part of this analysis is also seen in links in
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{K}_{N}
, whereas the ‘descending’ part of the link is considerably easier for us to analyse. Other than quoting different results from Vogtmann’s paper, the proofs are the same for both the reduced and unreduced versions.

Lemma 3.10
.

If
p\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
p=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{k}
then
\textnormal{ht}_{\mathcal{L}_{N}}(p)=k
. Equivalently, height is the same in both
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and
\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{\prime}
. The same result holds for
\mathcal{K}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{FS}^{r}_{N}}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The maximal chain-of-chains

(T_{1})<(T_{1}<T_{2})<\cdots<(T_{1}<\cdots<T_{k})

in
{}_{<}p
witnesses
\textnormal{ht}_{\mathcal{L}_{N}}(p)=k
.
∎

Hence we can read off height from chain length. As a consequence we will omit the subscript in the height function in the sequel.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
. If
p\in\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
then
{}_{<}p
is contractible, otherwise
{}_{<}p
is homeomorphic to a
(\textnormal{ht}(p)-1)
–dimensional sphere.

Let
p\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
. If
p\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{N}
then
{}_{<}p
is contractible, otherwise
{}_{<}p
is homeomorphic to a
(\textnormal{ht}(p)-1)
–dimensional sphere.

Proof.

We give the proof for
\mathcal{L}_{N}
and omit the proof for
\mathcal{K}_{N}
. Suppose
p\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
and let
p=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{k}
. If
p\not\in\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
, then
T_{0}
, and hence each blow-up
T_{i}
, belongs to
\mathcal{X}_{N}
. Hence every subchain of
p
is an element of
\mathcal{X}_{N}
. It follows that
{}_{<}p
is the poset of all proper subchains of
p
, so is isomorphic to the boundary of a
\textnormal{ht}(p)
–simplex.

If
p\in\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
then
T_{0}\in\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. Let
T_{j}
be the first element of the chain lying in
\mathcal{X}_{N}
. The subchains of
T_{j}<T_{j+1}<\cdots<T_{k}
span a (full) simplex
\Delta
in
{}_{<}p
. Furthermore, the monotone retraction
\chi:\mathcal{L}_{N}\to\mathcal{L}_{N}
given in Proposition
3.6
restricts to a retraction
r\colon_{<}p\to\Delta
, which is a homotopy equivalence by Corollary
3.4
. Hence
{}_{<}p
is contractible.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
with
p<p^{\prime}
. Then
(p,p^{\prime})
is homeomorphic to a
(\textnormal{ht}(p^{\prime})-\textnormal{ht}(p)-2)
–dimensional sphere.

Suppose
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
with
p<p^{\prime}
. Then
(p,p^{\prime})
is homeomorphic to a
(\textnormal{ht}(p^{\prime})-\textnormal{ht}(p)-2)
–dimensional sphere.

Proof.

Again we give the proof only for
\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Suppose
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
. If
p^{\prime}=T_{0}<T_{1}<\ldots<T_{k}
then
p=T_{i_{0}}<T_{i_{1}}<\ldots<T_{i_{l}}
, with
0\leq i_{j}\leq k
for
1\leq j\leq l
. In this case,
\textnormal{ht}(p^{\prime})-\textnormal{ht}(p)=k-l
.

As there are
k-l
elements of
p^{\prime}
that are not in
p
, the chains between
p
and
p^{\prime}
are determined by proper subsets of these
k-l
elements. As a poset this is isomorphic to the boundary of a
k-l-1
simplex. Hence
(p,p^{\prime})
is homeomorphic to a sphere of dimension
k-l-2=\textnormal{ht}(p^{\prime})-\textnormal{ht}(p)-2
.
∎

Proposition 3.13
.

Suppose
p\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Then
p_{<}
is homeomorphic to a wedge of spheres of dimension
3N-5-\textnormal{ht}(p)
.

Suppose
p\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
. Then
p_{<}
is homeomorphic to a wedge of spheres of dimension
3N-5-\textnormal{ht}(p)
.

Proof.

As before, we assume
p\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
and will make it clear where the proof differs for
p\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
. Suppose
\textnormal{ht}(p)=k
, so that
p=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{k}
for some sequence of trees in
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
. As
p_{<}
consists of superchains of
p
, it is isomorphic to the barycentric subdivision of

(6)

{}_{<}(T_{0})\ast(T_{0},T_{1})\ast(T_{2},T_{3})\ast\cdots\ast(T_{k})_{<},

where these intervals are in
\mathcal{FS}_{N}
(each factor in this join corresponds to a place where new elements can be added to the chain). Let
E_{i}
denote the number edges in the graph
T_{i}/F_{N}
. As
{}_{<}(T_{0})
consists of trees made by collapsing some, but not all, of the edge orbits in
T_{0}
, it is homeomorphic to the boundary of an
E_{0}-1
simplex, and is an (
E_{0}-2
)-sphere. Similarly,
(T_{i-1},T_{i})
is isomorphic to the poset of proper subsets of the edge orbits in

that are collapsed when passing to

(E_{i}-E_{i-1}-2)
-sphere.

Let

be the number of vertices of

. The poset
(T_{k})_{<}
is equal to the poset of blow-ups of

(when

this is shown in the proof of
[

[

). Putting together all of the above using Equation (

), and noting that there are

is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres of dimension equal to

\displaystyle(E_{0}-2)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}(E_{i}-E_{i-1}-2)+

\displaystyle(2N-V_{k}-3)+(k+1)

\displaystyle=2N+(E_{k}-V_{k}+1)-5-k

\displaystyle=3N-5-\textnormal{ht}(p).

Here we used the Euler characteristic formula

.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

The thickened spine
\mathcal{L}_{N}
is a
3N-4
-dimensional homotopy CM complex. A simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{L}_{N}
is in the boundary
\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
if and only if the local homology of
\mathcal{L}_{N}
vanishes at

.

The thickened reduced spine
\mathcal{K}_{N}
is a
3N-4
-dimensional homotopy CM complex. A simplex
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{N}
is in the boundary
\partial\mathcal{K}_{N}
if and only if the local homology of
\mathcal{K}_{N}
vanishes at
\sigma
.

Proof.

We again omit the proof for
\mathcal{K}_{N}
. The homotopy CM condition for

follows from Proposition
3.2
and the work above. Suppose
\sigma=p_{0}<p_{1}<\cdots<p_{k}
is a simplex in
\mathcal{L}_{N}

∎

3.4.
The dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
D
from the introduction, which for convenience we repeat below.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{N}
be the spine of Outer space and let
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
be the spine of reduced Outer space. The dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is isomorphic to the groups

\displaystyle H_{0}(\mathcal{X}_{N},h^{2N-3})

\displaystyle\cong H^{2N-3}_{c}(\mathcal{X}_{N},\mathbb{Z}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle H_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}},h_{r}^{2N-3})

\displaystyle\cong H^{2N-3}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}},\mathbb{Z}),

where
h^{2N-3}
and
h_{r}^{2N-3}
are the degree
2N-3
local cohomology cosheaves of
\mathcal{X}_{N}
and
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
, respectively. In contrast, the homology of the simplicial boundary of (reduced) Outer space satisfies

\displaystyle H_{3N-5-k}(\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N};\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{k}_{c}(\mathcal{X}_{N};h_{*}(\mathcal{CV}_{N})|_{\mathcal{X}_{N}}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle H_{3N-5-k}({\partial\mathcal{FS}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle\cong H^{k}_{c}(\mathcal{X}_{N};h_{*}(\mathcal{CV}^{r}_{N})|_{{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}}),

where
h_{*}(\mathcal{CV}_{N})|_{\mathcal{X}_{N}}
and and
h_{*}(\mathcal{CV}^{r}_{N})|_{{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}}
are the restrictions of the local homology sheaf of Outer space (and reduced Outer space) to the spine (and its reduced version, respectively).

Proof.

As
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is a virtual duality group of dimension
2N-3
and and the (reduced) spine is a
2N-3
–dimensional CM complex by
[
Vog90
]
, the first pair of isomorphisms follow from Corollary
B
in the introduction. By Theorem
3.14
, the thickened spine
\mathcal{L}_{N}
is homotopy CM. Furthermore, every vertex of
\mathcal{L}_{N}
either belongs to
\mathcal{X}_{N}^{\prime}
or
\partial\mathcal{L}_{N}
. Hence we can apply the relative CM duality of Section
2.5
with
X=\mathcal{L}_{N}
,
L=\mathcal{X}_{N}^{\prime}
and

. The first isomorphism in the relative CM duality theorem gives

H^{k}_{c}(\mathcal{X}_{N};h_{*}(\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{N}})|_{\mathcal{X}_{N}})\cong H_{3N-4-k}(\mathcal{L}_{N},\partial\mathcal{L}_{N};\mathbb{Z}).

The interior of the thickened spine is homeomorphic to Outer space, so
h_{*}(\mathcal{\mathcal{L}_{N}})|_{\mathcal{X}_{N}}=h_{*}(\mathcal{CV}_{N})|_{\mathcal{X}_{N}}
. As

In

4.
Bounding the top-dimensional cohomology of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})

In this section, we see how cosheaf homology gives a resolution for the dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
, and use this to prove the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\rho\in{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
be a rose in the spine of reduced outer space, and let
{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)<{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
be the stabilizer of this rose. Then

\dim(H^{2N-3}({\rm{Out}}(F_{N});\mathbb{Q}))\leq\dim((h^{2N-3}(\rho)\otimes\mathbb{Q})_{{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)}),

where
h^{2N-3}(\rho)
is the local cohomology of the reduced spine at this rose.

Recall that a
rose
is a point
T
in the spine such that
T/F_{N}
is a graph with one vertex and
N
loops, and we use
M_{G}
to denote the group of coinvariants of a
G
-module
M
. As
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is a virtual duality group, it is a duality group of dimension
2N-3
over
\mathbb{Q}

[
Bie81
, Theorem 9.9]
. In particular this implies:

H^{2N-3}({\rm{Out}}(F_{N});\mathbb{Q})\cong H_{0}({\rm{Out}}(F_{N});D\otimes\mathbb{Q})=(D\otimes\mathbb{Q})_{{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})},

where
D
is the dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
.
To prove Theorem
4.1
, we will show that with integer coefficients one has

(7)

D_{{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})}\subseteq h^{2N-3}(\rho)_{{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)},

which implies the dimensional inequality after passing to rational coefficients.
As above, the dualizing module of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
is

D=H^{2N-3}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z}).

As we are in the top dimension, the coboundary map is trivial so
H^{2N-3}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})
is generated by cohomology classes of the form
[\alpha^{*}]
, as
\alpha
varies over the top-dimensional simplices of
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
. Viewing
G={\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
as acting on
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
on the left, this gives
H^{2N-3}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})
the usual right
G
-module structure by

[\alpha^{*}]g=[g^{-1}(\alpha)^{*}].

By
[
Vog90
]
, the spine is locally Cohen–Macaulay, so we can apply CM duality (Theorem
2.1
) to obtain an isomorphism

[{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}]\cap-\colon H^{k}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\cong}H_{2N-3-k}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3}),

where
h^{2N-3}
is the local cohomology cosheaf of the spine and
[{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}]\in H^{\text{lf}}_{2N-3}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
is the fundamental class. As
H^{*}_{c}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})
is concentrated in degree
2N-3
,
H_{*}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
is concentrated in degree zero, so its chain complex is acyclic. This gives a resolution

(8)

\cdots\to C_{1}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})\to C_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})\to D\to 0

of the dualizing module. The modules in this chain complex are (see Equation (
1
))

C_{k}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})=\oplus_{\sigma\in({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}})_{k}}h^{2N-3}(\sigma),

where
h^{2N-3}(\sigma)=H^{2N-3}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}},{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}(\sigma))
is the local homology at
\sigma
.
To distinguish where local homology classes live, for
\phi\in h^{2N-3}(\sigma)
we use
\sigma\triangleleft\phi
to denote the corresponding element of
C_{n}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
in the
h^{2N-3}(\sigma)
-summand.

At the chain level, the cap product with the fundamental class
[{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}]
given in
[
WW23
]
is the
G
-equivariant (see
[
WW23
, Section 5]
) map

c_{2N-3}\colon C^{2N-3}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};\mathbb{Z})\to C_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})

given by

(9)

c_{2N-3}(\alpha^{*})=\alpha_{0}\triangleleft[\alpha^{*}],

where
\alpha_{0}
is the first vertex of
\alpha
(using the ordering/orientation on
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
induced by the poset structure), and
\alpha_{0}\triangleleft[\alpha^{*}]
lives in the local cohomology at the vertex
\alpha_{0}
. The quickest way to see this is via Equation (14) on page 13 of
[
WW23
]
, noting that the fundamental class
[{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}]
is represented by the infinite sum
\sum{\alpha\triangleleft[\alpha^{*}]}
, as
\alpha
ranges over all maximal simplices. In the poset structure we use on the spine every maximal simplex
\alpha
is given by a chain

T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{2N-3},

where the initial vertex
T_{0}
of

is a rose. We can leverage this to restrict the image of

:

.

Let
R
be the submodule of
C_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
spanned by the local cohomology groups of the roses in

. Every element of

has a representative in

.

Proof.

The cap product

induces an isomorphism between

and
H_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
. The image of

by (

), as the first vertex

of any maximal simplex

H_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})

.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
R
be the submodule of
C_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})
spanned by the local cohomology groups of the roses in

, and let

By CM dualiy,

and the inclusion

induces an injection

R/(\im d_{1}\cap R)\hookrightarrow C_{0}({\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}};h^{2N-3})/\im d_{1}\cong D.

By Proposition

, every element of

has a representative in

, so this map is also surjective.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
R
be as above and let

be the augmentation ideal of the group ring of
{\rm{Out}}(F_{N})
. If
\rho
is a rose in
{\mathcal{X}_{N}^{r}}
and

As

(see e.g. Proposition III.5.3 in

Proof of Theorem

.

By Corollary

therefore the coinvariants of this module are given by

Remark 4.5
.

We finish this section with some remarks.

•

To show that the inclusion from Equation (
7
) is actually an isomorphism, it follows from the proof that it is enough to show that
RI
contains
\im d_{1}\cap R
. This is definitely false in the unreduced case, and seems unlikely to be true even in the reduced setting.

•

The representation theory of the
{\rm{Stab}}(\rho)
action on the cohomology of the link (or equivalently, the local cohomology) is well understood in terms of
tree representations
of the symmetric group. See
[
Bar90
]
and
[
RW96
]
. For instance, in the unreduced setting the dimension of
h^{2N-3}(\rho)
is
(2N-2)!
However, the structure of the link is much less well-understood in the reduced spine.

•

While we suspect it may be possible to prove Theorem
4.1
without using CM duality, the theorem was only formulated after a careful consideration of the the resolution of the dualizing module obtained in Equation (
8
). Furthermore, we found that the cap product in CM duality facilitated a particularly clean proof of Proposition
4.2
.

5.
2-complexes, visible irreducibility and the local homology sheaf

In dimension two, links of points are homeomorphic to graphs, and we will see that the local Cohen–Macaulay condition is equivalent to the graphs that appear as vertex links being connected and nondegenerate (not a single point). A related condition called
visible irreducibility
, appears in work of Louder and Wilton
[
LW24
]
(reformulated as below in
[
Wil24
, Remark 4.2]
) . Informally this says that there are no obvious local simplifications one can perform on the 2-complex. More precisely, a complex is
visibly irreducible
if:

(i)

every vertex link contains at least one edge;

(ii)

every vertex link is connected;

(iii)

vertex links do not contain leaves; and

(iv)

vertex links do not have cut vertices.

Otherwise, a complex is
visibly reducible
. We now turn to Theorem
F
from the introduction, which is restated below.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
Y
be a simplicial 2-complex. Then
Y
is a local homology CM complex if and only if it satisfies (i) and (ii). Furthermore, the following are equivalent:

•

Y
is visibly irreducible.

•

Y
is a local homology CM complex and the restriction maps of the local homology sheaf are surjective.

•

Y
is a local homology CM complex and the corestriction maps of the local cohomology cosheaf are injective.

Proof.

We first show that if
Y
is locally CM then it satisfies (i) and (ii). If
Y
is locally CM then it is pure (all of its maximal simplices are 2-simplices), so in particular every vertex is contained in a 2-simplex and (i) holds. To see that (ii) also holds, let
V\in Y
be a vertex, and let
\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V)
be the vertex link. By comparing the chain complexes
C_{\bullet}^{V}(Y)
(as defined in Section
2.2
) and
\tilde{C}_{\bullet-1}(\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V))
(that computes the reduced homology of the link) we see that
h_{k}(V)\cong\tilde{H}_{k-1}(\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V))
: every adjacent edge
E
at
V
determines a vertex
v_{E}\in\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V)
and 2-simplices containing
V
give edges in
\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V)
. If (ii) does not hold, there exists a vertex
V
such that
\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V)
is a disconnected graph, so that
h_{1}(V)=\tilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V))
is nontrivial. So
Y
being locally CM also implies (ii).

Now assume that
Y
satisfies (i) and (ii). We want to to show that the local homology
h_{*}(\sigma)
is concentrated in degree 2 for every simplex
\sigma\in Y
. We start with the vertices: by (i) and (ii) each vertex link is a non-empty connected graph with at least one edge, so
\tilde{H}_{*}(\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V))
is concentrated in degree 1, and therefore
h_{*}(V)\cong\tilde{H}_{*-1}(\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V))
is concentrated in degree 2. Moving on to the edges,
h_{*}(E)
is concentrated in degree two if and only if
E
is contained in a face. If this is not the case,
E
will be an isolated vertex in each of its vertex links, contradicting (ii) (as there is at least one edge in each vertex link by (i)). The local homology at each 2-simplex
\sigma
satisfies
h_{2}(\sigma)=\mathbb{Z}
and
h_{k}(\sigma)=0
for
k\neq 2
by maximality. Putting all of the above together,
Y
is a local homology CM complex if and only if the vertex links of
Y
satisfy (i) and (ii).

We now move on the equivalence of the bullet points. As there is no torsion in local (co)homology when the complex is locally CM, equivalence of the second and third bullet points follows from the universal coefficient theorem. Therefore we will focus on the local homology sheaf. Firstly, if
F
is a 2–simplex in
Y
and
E
is an edge of
F
, the map
h_{2}(E)\to h_{2}(F)
is surjective if and only if the local homology
h_{2}(E)
does not vanish. This happens if and only if
E
is contained in at least two 2–simplices. This in turn is equivalent to vertex links not containing leaves.

Now suppose that
Y
satisfies (i), (ii) and (iii). As above let
V
be a vertex of an edge
E\in Y
. The local homology in
Y
at
E
only considers 2–simplices containing
E
, so in terms of the notation above we have

h_{2}(E)\cong H_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E})),

where
\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E})
is the open star of
v_{E}
in the vertex link
\Gamma
. Furthermore, one can check that the map
h_{2}(V)\to h_{2}(E)
is the same as the map

H_{1}(\Gamma)\to H_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E}))

induced by the inclusion of pairs
(\Gamma,\emptyset)\to(\Gamma,\Gamma-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E}))
. By considering the long exact sequence in homology, the cokernel of
h_{2}(E)\to h_{2}(V)
is then isomorphic to

\tilde{H}_{0}(\Gamma-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E}))

Hence the map
h_{2}(V)\to h_{2}(E)
is surjective unless
\Gamma-\mathring{\mathrm{st}}_{\Gamma}(v_{E})
has more than one component, or equivalently unless
v_{E}
is a cut vertex of
\mathrm{lk}_{Y}(V)=\Gamma
.
∎
