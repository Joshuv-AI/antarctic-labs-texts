Sparse graph counting and Kelley–Meka bounds for binary systems
Yuval Filmus, Hamed Hatami, Kaave Hosseini, Esty Kelman

Abstract
In a recent breakthrough, Kelley and Meka (FOCS 2023) obtained a strong upper bound on the density of sets of integers without non-trivial three-term arithmetic progressions. In this work, we extend their result, establishing similar bounds for all linear patterns defined by binary systems of linear forms, where “binary” indicates that every linear form depends on exactly two variables. Prior to our work, no strong bounds were known for such systems even in the finite field model setting. A key ingredient in our proof is a graph counting lemma . The classical graph counting lemma, developed by Thomason (Random Graphs 1985) and Chung, Graham, and Wilson (Combinatorica 1989), is a fundamental tool in combinatorics. For a fixed graph H H , it states that the number of copies of H H in a pseudorandom graph G G is similar to the number of copies of H H in a purely random graph with the same edge density as G G . However, this lemma is only non-trivial when G G is a dense graph. In this work, we prove a graph counting lemma that is also effective when G G is sparse. Moreover, our lemma is well-suited for density increment arguments in additive number theory. As an immediate application, we obtain a strong bound for the Turán problem in abelian Cayley sum graphs: let Γ \Gamma be a finite abelian group with odd order. If a Cayley sum graph on Γ \Gamma does not contain any r r -clique as a subgraph, it must have at most 2 − Ω r ​ ( log 1 / 16 ​ | Γ | ) ⋅ | Γ | 2 2^{-\Omega_{r}(\log^{1/16}|\Gamma|)}\cdot|\Gamma|^{2} edges. These results hinge on the technology developed by Kelley and Meka and the follow-up work by Kelley, Lovett, and Meka (STOC 2024).

Introduction

The
graph counting lemma
, due to Thomason
[
Tho87
]
and Chung, Graham, and Wilson
[
CGW89
]
, is a fundamental result in graph theory, with many applications in extremal combinatorics, additive number theory, discrete geometry, and theoretical computer science. Informally, it states that the number of embeddings of a fixed graph
H
into a random-looking (pseudorandom) graph
G
is similar to the number of embeddings of
H
into a genuine random graph with the same edge density as
G
.

For certain problems, particularly in additive number theory, it is possible to use the counting lemma to obtain a density increment argument. Roughly speaking, if the number of embeddings of
H
into
G
deviates from that of a random graph, then by the counting lemma,
G
is not pseudorandom and, therefore, contains some structure. In certain cases, it is possible to exploit this structure to restrict the problem to a significantly denser subproblem and repeat the argument. As the density cannot exceed one, this process must stop after a bounded number of iterations.

Many of the strongest known bounds in additive number theory and extremal combinatorics rely on the density increment approach. This method often yields much better bounds compared to other common techniques, such as the regularity method.

The classical graph counting lemma has a shortcoming: it is only effective for dense graphs. Indeed, while the counting lemma and the regularity method provide an elegant theory for understanding the subgraph densities in dense graphs
[
Lov12
]
, obtaining an analogous theory for the sparse setting has proven challenging. Over the past few decades, considerable effort has been devoted towards developing sparse analogs of the counting and regularity lemmas (see for example
[
Koh97
,
GS05
,
CG02
,
KR03
,
KRS04
,
KRSS10
,
CFZ14
,
ABSS20
]
).

The conventional strategy for extending these results to sparse graphs is to introduce an additional requirement: that the graph
G
, even though sparse, is a relatively dense subgraph of a much more pseudorandom host graph
\Gamma
. For example, in an important paper, Conlon, Fox, and Zhao
[
CFZ14
]
proved an effective sparse counting lemma under this additional requirement. Their result generalizes an earlier theorem of Kohayakawa, Rödl, Schacht, and Skokan
[
KRSS10
]
that only applied to counting triangles.
However, the additional requirement that
G
must live in a much more pseudorandom graph
\Gamma
makes such sparse counting lemmas unsuitable for certain applications of the original counting lemma.

In this paper, we prove a counting lemma for sparse graphs (
Theorem

1.4
) that circumvents this extra assumption on
G
. Our theorem states that if the number of embeddings of
H
into
G
deviates from what is expected, then either
G
contains many vertices of small degree, or there exist large subsets
S,T\subseteq V(G)
such that the density of the induced bipartite graph between them is notably
higher
than the density of
G
. Due to its guarantee of increased density, our counting lemma is tailored for density increment arguments.

We use our new sparse counting lemma alongside various tools from additive number theory, such as the Bohr set machinery, almost periodicity, and dependent random choice, to extend Kelley and Meka’s strong bound on
3
-progression-free sets to a broad and important class of more general linear patterns. We will postpone the detailed discussion of this result to
Section

1.2
.

1.1
The sparse graph counting lemma

We identify undirected graphs as symmetric functions
A\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
through the correspondence with the characteristic function of the edges. The
density of
A
is

\Ex[A]\coloneqq\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2})],

where
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
means that
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{2}
are chosen uniformly and independently from
\mathcal{X}
.

Let
H=([k],E_{H})
be a fixed undirected graph with vertex set
[k]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
m=|E_{H}|
edges. We define the
density of
H
in
A
as

t_{H}(A)\coloneqq\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E_{H}}{A}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\right].

When
A
is the Erdős–Rényi random graph with parameter
\alpha
, we have
t_{H}(A)\approx\alpha^{m}
, with high probability. For an arbitrary
A
, the graph counting lemma of
[
Tho87
,
CGW89
]
guarantees
t_{H}(A)\approx\alpha^{m}
if
A
is pseudorandom in a certain structural sense. We state this result in the contrapositive form.

Theorem 1.1

(Graph Counting Lemma
[
Tho87
,
CGW89
]
)
.

For every fixed graph
H
with
m
edges and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,m)>0
such that the following is true. If
A
is a graph on
\mathcal{X}
with density
\alpha
that satisfies

\left|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}\right|\geq\varepsilon,

there must exist
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with densities
\frac{|S|}{|\mathcal{X}|},\frac{|T|}{|\mathcal{X}|}\geq\Omega_{m,\varepsilon}(1)

such that

\left|\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]-\alpha\right|\geq\delta.

In other words, if
t_{H}(A)
is significantly different from what is expected from a random graph of density
\alpha
, then on some large combinatorial rectangle
S\times T
, the density of
A
significantly deviates from
\alpha
.

In the sparse setting, where
\alpha=o(1)
, we have
|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}|=o(1)
, and therefore, to obtain a useful counting lemma for sparse graphs, we need to refine the assumption of the counting lemma to

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:stronger_{a}ssumption}\left|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}\right|\geq\varepsilon\alpha^{m}.

(1)

The following well-known example shows that (
1
) does not necessarily imply the existence of
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with density
\Omega_{m,\varepsilon}(1)
that satisfy

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:stronger_{c}onclusion}\left|\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]-\alpha\right|\geq\Omega(\alpha).

(2)

Example 1.2
.

Let
H
be the triangle graph, and let
A
be the Erdős–Rényi random graph on
n
vertices with parameter
\alpha=n^{-2/3}
. With high probability, we have
t_{H}(A)\approx\alpha^{3}=n^{-2}
, and therefore, there are at most
O(n)
triangles in
A
. By removing at most
O(n)
edges from
A
, we can obtain a triangle-free
A^{\prime}
. Since
A^{\prime}
is triangle-free and its density is very close to
\alpha
, it satisfies the assumption (
1
). Moreover,

\left|\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A^{\prime}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]-\alpha\right|=o(\alpha)

for all subsets
S,T\subseteq[n]
of density at least, say,
n^{-1/7}
. Therefore,
A^{\prime}
violates the desired conclusion (
2
).

To circumvent such examples, Conlon, Fox and Zhao
[
CFZ14
]
assume further that
A
is a relatively dense subgraph of a host graph
\Gamma
that satisfies much stronger pseudo-randomness conditions. Their counting lemma states that under this extra requirement, (
1
) indeed implies (
2
) for some
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with density
\Omega_{m,\varepsilon}(1)
.
We prove a counting lemma that does not require such an extra pseudorandomness condition on
A
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
H
be a graph with
m
edges, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For every graph
A
with vertex set
\mathcal{X}
and density
\alpha
, the following holds.

(i)

If
t_{H}(A)\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\alpha^{m}
, there exist
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}=\Omega(\varepsilon\alpha^{m+1})
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\alpha.

(ii)

If
t_{H}(A)\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{m}\alpha^{m}
, there exist
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}|^{2}}=\Omega(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^{m-1}\alpha^{m})
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\alpha.

Theorem

1.3
is a special case of a slightly more general result (
2.2
) that we state and prove later.
Theorem

1.3
is not our main counting lemma, and as explained later, it is not useful for strong density increment applications. However, it can be viewed as an extension of
Theorem

1.1
to the sparse setting and provides a nice complement to
Example

1.2
.

Kelley, Lovett, Meka
[
KLM24
]
proved the first part of
Theorem

1.3
using a smooth analog of the dependent random choice method in the special case when
H
is a complete bipartite graph. In
Theorem

, we generalize their result to all graphs using a similar proof.

A density increment counting lemma.

Theorem

1.3
is not useful for obtaining density increment arguments since, in case (ii), it only provides a combinatorial rectangle with
decreased
density.

Let us consider the original counting lemma (
Theorem

1.1
). By tracking the parameters in the proof of this theorem, one can easily show that (
1
) implies the existence of
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
with densities
\Omega_{m,\varepsilon}(1)
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq\alpha+\Omega(\alpha^{m}).

While this result provides a density increase, the magnitude of this increase is insignificant when
\alpha
is small. In many applications, having an increase of
\Omega(\alpha)
is crucial. For example, a density increment that multiplies the density by a factor of
1+\Omega(1)
can be repeated at most
O(\log(2/\alpha))
times. In contrast, a weaker density increment by a factor of
1+\Omega(\alpha^{c})
for
c>0
needs to be repeated at least
\Omega(\alpha^{-c}\log(2/\alpha))
times before it reaches a contradiction. Since each iteration decreases the size of the domain by a constant factor, this large number of iterations would only be possible if
\alpha\geq\Omega(1/\log|\mathcal{X}|)
.

Our main graph counting lemma provides a significant density increment when
A
does not contain many vertices with small degrees.

Theorem 1.4

(Main graph counting lemma)
.

Let
H
be a graph with
m
edges, and let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. There exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,m)>0
such that for every graph
A
of density
\alpha
on a vertex set
\mathcal{X}
, the following holds. If

\left|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}\right|\geq\varepsilon\alpha^{m},

then, either
A
has
2^{-O_{m,\varepsilon}(\log(2/\alpha))}|\mathcal{X}|
vertices with degrees at most
(1-\delta)\alpha|\mathcal{X}|
, or there exist
S,T\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of densities at least
2^{-O_{m,\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(2/\alpha))}
satisfying

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq(1+\delta)\alpha.

Theorem

1.4
states that if
A
is a graph with
|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}|\geq\varepsilon\alpha^{m}
, then either it has many vertices with small degrees, or the density of
A
on some large rectangle is greater than
(1+\delta)\alpha
. The significance of this theorem lies in the fact that even when
t_{H}(A)
is
smaller
than, say,
\alpha^{m}/2
, it provides a rectangle with
increased
density. This feature makes the theorem suitable for density increment arguments, especially in additive number theory, where one naturally deals with regular graphs.

Theorem

1.4
is a special case of a more general result (
2.1
) which we state and prove later.

1.2
Kelley–Meka bounds for binary systems of linear forms

To study the number of occurrences of a linear pattern (e.g.,
3
-progressions) in a subset of the interval
\{1,\ldots,M\}
, it suffices to embed
\{1,\ldots,M\}
in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
for a prime
N=O(M)
chosen sufficiently large to avoid wraparound. Consequently, rather than working with the interval, one can focus on subsets of finite abelian groups
G
. The two important cases are
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, where
N
is a large prime and asymptotics are as
N
tends to infinity, and
G=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where
q
is a fixed prime and asymptotics are as
n
tends to infinity. The former is important for applications in number theory, and the latter is the setting that is most relevant to applications in combinatorics and computer science.

In 1953, Roth
[
Rot53
]
proved that a set of integers
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
without non-trivial 3-progressions must have density
O(1/\log\log N)
. The quest to determine the optimal bound in Roth’s theorem stands as a central problem in additive number theory, which has been studied extensively over the past seven decades (notably
[
HB87
,
Sze90
,
Bou99
,
San11
,
BS21
,
KM23
]
). For a long time,
1/\log N
was perceived as a barrier for all the available techniques. In
[
BS21
]
, Bloom and Sisask overcame this barrier and established the bound
O(1/\log^{1+\varepsilon}N)
for some small
\varepsilon>0
. More recently, in a remarkable breakthrough, Kelley and Meka
[
KM23
]
took a huge leap forward and improved the bound to
2^{-\Omega(\log^{1/12}N)}
. Subsequently, this bound was refined to
2^{-\Omega(\log^{1/9}N)}
by Bloom and Sisask
[
BS23
]
. On the other hand, Behrend’s classical construction
[
Beh46
]
shows that there are sets with density
2^{-O(\log^{1/2}N)}
that are free of non-trivial
3
-progressions.

Similarly to all previous approaches,
[
KM23
]
uses a density increment argument. Note that the density of 3-progressions in a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}
is given by

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline quation:3AP_{d}ef}t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)\coloneqq\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\left[A(\mathbf{x})A\left(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\right)A(\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{y})\right],

(3)

where we identified
A
with its indicator function.
If
A
is a random set of density
\alpha
, then we expect
t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)\approx\alpha^{3}
.
Kelley and Meka
[
KM23
]
show that if a set
A
with density
\alpha
satisfies

|t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)-\alpha^{3}|\geq\frac{\alpha^{3}}{2},

then restricting to some large low-rank Bohr set
B
increases the relative density of
A
by a multiplicative factor of
1+\Omega(1)
. Repeating this argument iteratively for at most
O(\log(2/\alpha))
times shows that if
A
does not contain any non-trivial
3
-progressions, then
\alpha\leq 2^{-\Omega(\log^{1/12}{N})}
.

Most linear patterns, such as
4
-progressions, are fundamentally beyond the scope of such a Bohr set counting lemma: there are sets
A
with density
\alpha=\Omega(1)
that satisfy
|t_{\textrm{4AP}}(A)-\alpha^{4}|=\Omega(1)
, but the relative density of
A
in every large low-rank Bohr set remains extremely close to
\alpha
, see
[
Gow01
]
.

Remark 1
.

In 1975, Szemerédi
[
Sze75
]
extended Roth’s
o(1)
bound using a completely new approach to arithmetic progressions of arbitrary length. However, in contrast to Roth’s Fourier-analytic proof, all the various known proofs of Szemerédi’s theorem give much weaker bounds. Indeed, it was considered a major breakthrough when Gowers
[
Gow01
]
proved an upper bound of
1/(\log\log N)^{2^{-2^{k+9}}}
on the density of sets of integers without
k
-term arithmetic progressions. Very recently, Leng, Sah, and Sawhney posted a preprint
[
LSS24
]
improving Gowers’ bound to an impressive bound of
O(2^{-(\log\log N)^{c_{k}}})
, where
c_{k}>0
is a constant depending on
k
.

Nonetheless, there is an important class of linear patterns that lie somewhere between
3
-progressions and
4
-progressions in terms of their “complexity”. These linear patterns, defined by
binary systems of linear forms
, stem from the graph-theoretic approach of Ruzsa and Szemerédi
[
RS78
]
to Roth’s theorem.
Ruzsa and Szemerédi discovered a versatile proof of Roth’s theorem by establishing and applying the so-called triangle removal lemma, which is a consequence of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma. Their proof uses the expression

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:RuzsaSzemeredi}t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)=\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\left[A(2\mathbf{x}-2\mathbf{y})A\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\right)A(2\mathbf{y}-2\mathbf{z})\right]

(4)

to represent the density of 3-progressions in
A
as the density of triangles in a tripartite graph constructed from
A
, and then applies these graph theoretic tools.

In
[
EFR86
]
, Erdős, Frankl, and Rödl proved the graph removal lemma, which generalizes the triangle removal lemma to all graphs. This generalization extends the graph-theoretic approach to a large class of linear patterns, namely those defined by
binary systems of linear forms
. Essentially, these linear patterns have a graph-like structure, similar to how
3
-progressions have a triangle-like structure. We will formally define these systems in
Section

1.4
, but for now, it suffices to mention that the term
binary
indicates that each linear form is supported on exactly two variables. Note that
(2x-2y,x-z,2y-2z)
, which captures
3
-progressions, satisfies this property. As another notable example of a binary system, consider

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:K4}t_{K_{4}}(A)\coloneqq\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\left[A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{z})A(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{w})A(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{w})\right],

(5)

which corresponds to the density of
K_{4}
(the complete graph on
4
vertices) in the Cayley sum graph
\Cay(\mathbb{Z}_{N},A)
, whose edges are
\{x,y\}
for
x+y\in A
.

The graph-theoretic approach relies on Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, and therefore results in an extremely weak bound compared to Roth’s original bound. However, one can use a common technique in additive number theory and extremal combinatorics, based on a few careful applications of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, to extend Roth’s original Fourier-analytic proof and upper bound to all binary systems.

Unfortunately, the aforementioned Cauchy–Schwarz approach incurs too much loss to extend Kelley and Meka’s upper bound to such a broad class of linear patterns.

Previous techniques developed by Green and Tao (in their work on
4
-progressions
[
GT09
]
) and by Dousse
[
Dou13
]
extend to binary systems
1
1

1

and more generally to systems of complexity
1
.
, and for such a broad class of systems, the strongest known bound is
1/\log^{c}N
(where
c
is a small constant depending on the system), established by Shao
[
Sha14
]
.

In fact, a priori, it is unclear whether Kelley and Meka’s techniques could be applied to, for instance,
t_{K_{4}}(A)
. Let us briefly review the key steps of their proof:

1.

Their proof starts from the assumption
|t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)-\alpha^{3}|\geq\varepsilon\alpha^{3}
, and notes that

t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)-\alpha^{3}={\left\langle(A-\alpha)*(A-\alpha),C\right\rangle},

where
C=\{2a\mid a\in A\}
.

2.

(Hölder step): Since the density of
C
is
\alpha
, one can use Hölder’s inequality to show that the right-hand side is at most
O(\alpha\|(A-\alpha)*(A-\alpha)\|_{p})
for
p\approx\log(2/\alpha)
.

3.

(Positivity/Significant Increment): Then, in one of the key steps of the proof, the proof uses the fact that the Fourier coefficients of the convolution
(A-\alpha)*(A-\alpha)
are positive to deduce from
\|(A-\alpha)*(A-\alpha)\|_{p}=\Omega(\alpha^{2})
that

\|A*A\|_{p}=\left(1+\Omega(1)\right)\alpha^{2}.

4.

(Sifting/Dependent random choice): This key step involves the application of the dependent random choice technique to the lower bound on
\|A*A\|_{p}
.

5.

(Almost periodicity): At this point, one can combine the result of the previous step with the available almost periodicity result of
[
San12
]
to achieve the desired density increment.

Note that several steps in the above proof rely on the properties of convolution, and while one can express
t_{\textrm{3AP}}(A)
by a simple formula involving convolution, this is not true for other more complex binary systems such as
t_{K_{4}}(A)
.
Is it still true that
|t_{K_{4}}(A)-\alpha^{6}|=\Omega(\alpha^{6})
implies a significant density increment in a large low-rank Bohr set?

If
|t_{K_{4}}(A)-\alpha^{6}|=\Omega(\alpha^{6})
, then our counting lemma (

1.4
) provides two sets

and

with densities

such that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:spreadness_{e}xample}(1+\Omega(1))\alpha_{S}\alpha_{T}\alpha\leq\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\left[S(\mathbf{x})T(\mathbf{y})A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right],

(6)

where
\alpha_{S}
and
\alpha_{T}
are the densities of

and

, respectively.

This is promising, as
\Ex\left[S(\mathbf{x})T(\mathbf{y})A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right]
is a much simpler expression than
t_{K_{4}}(A)
, and moreover, we have shown in (
6

\alpha_{S}\alpha_{T}\alpha
.
By establishing a sifting step for general finite abelian groups that is applicable to (

Theorem 1.5

(Kelley–Meka-type bounds over finite abelian groups)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a binary system of linear forms over a finite abelian group
G
, and suppose
|G|
is coprime with all the coefficients in the linear forms. If
A\subseteq G
is free of translations of non-degenerate instances of
\mathcal{L}
, then

(i)

\displaystyle|A|\leq|G|\cdot 2^{-\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}(\log^{1/16}|G|)}.

(ii)

If, in addition, the underlying graph of
\mathcal{L}
is
2
-degenerate, then

|A|\leq|G|\cdot 2^{-\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}(\log^{1/9}|G|)}.

Here, an undirected graph
H
is called
k
-degenerate
if every subgraph of
H
has a vertex of degree at most
k
.

Remark 2
.

It is worth noting that even in the case of the group
G=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, despite the success of the polynomial method in proving strong bounds for Roth’s theorem
[
CLP17
,
EG17
]
, the bound of
1.5
is new.
In 2017,
[
CLP17
,
EG17
]
used the polynomial method to prove that any subset of

without

However, even in the setting of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, the polynomial method appears to be limited in dealing with general binary systems. In a recent article
[
Gij23
]
, Gijswijt extended the polynomial method to a larger class of systems of linear forms. However, even this class does not include the binary systems whose underlying graphs are dense graphs such as
K_{4}
or larger cliques. For cases such as (
5
), the strongest known previous bounds were of the form
q^{n-\Omega_{q}(\log n)}
.
1.5
improves these bounds to
q^{n-\Omega_{q}(n^{1/16})}
.

Remark 3
.

In the case of 3-progressions, currently the strongest known bound for

is
|A|\leq|G|\cdot 2^{-\Omega(\log^{1/9}|G|)}
, due to Bloom and Sisask
[

, which is stronger than what we obtain for general binary systems in

(i). To briefly explain this disparity, note that we need to apply our counting lemma to obtain (

and

(ii)
(ii) we show that if the underlying graph of the binary system is

1.3
Turán’s theorem for Cayley sum graphs over abelian groups

Here, we present a quick application of
1.5
to Turán’s theorem, a classical result in extremal graph theory regarding the largest edge density of a graph that does not contain any copies of
K_{r}
(the complete graph on
r
vertices), for some given
r
. Turán proved that this density is at most
1-\frac{1}{r-1}+o(1)
, which is tight, as witnessed by a complete
(r-1)
-partite graph where the parts have an almost equal number of vertices.

Recall that given a finite abelian group
\Gamma
and a set
A\subseteq\Gamma
, the Cayley sum graph
\Cay(\Gamma,A)
is the graph with vertex set
\Gamma
and edge set
\{\{x,y\}\mid x+y\in A\}
.
1.5
(i) implies a strong upper bound on the edge density of
G
in Turán’s theorem if
G
is further assumed to be a Cayley sum graph of a finite abelian group
\Gamma
with
2\not|\ |\Gamma|
.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
r\geq 2
be a fixed positive integer. Let
\Gamma
be a finite abelian group such that
2\not|\ |\Gamma|
, and let
A\subseteq\Gamma
. If
\Cay(\Gamma,A)
does not contain any copies of
K_{r}
, then its edge density is at most
2^{-\Omega_{r}(\log^{1/16}|\Gamma|)}
.

Proof.

The system of linear forms associated with
r
-clique is
\mathcal{L}=\{x_{i}+x_{j}:i\neq j,i,j\in[r]\}
.
Next, we show that if
2\not|\ |\Gamma|
, then
\mathcal{L}
is translation invariant (see
Definition

1
on page
1
), and therefore, we can apply
1.5
to conclude the corollary.
We need to show that every shift of an
r
-clique is also an
r
-clique. This follows from the identity
x_{i}+x_{j}+c=(x_{i}+c/2)+(x_{j}+c/2)
for all
i\neq j
, which is valid if
2\not|\ |\Gamma|
.
∎

Note that the requirement
2\not|\ |\Gamma|
is crucial. For example, let
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{2N}
and let
A
be the set of all odd numbers in
\mathbb{Z}_{2N}
, namely,
A=\{2i+1\bmod 2N:i\in[N]\}\subseteq\Gamma
. The Cayley sum graph
\Cay(\Gamma,A)
does not contain any triangles because for any three elements
x,y,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2N}
, at least one of
x+y,x+z,y+z
is even, hence not in
A
. However,
\Cay(\Gamma,A)
has edge density
\approx\frac{1}{2}
.

1.4
Notation and terminology

The function
\log
is the base
2

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite set.
Given a function

and

\|f\|_{p}=\left(\Ex[|f(\mathbf{x})|^{p}]\right)^{1/p}
, and let

.

We use the standard Bachmann–Landau asymptotic notation:
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot),o(\cdot)

Systems of linear forms.

A
linear form
in
d
variables is a vector
L=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Given an abelian group
G
, the linear form
L
defines a linear map
G^{d}\to G
by
L(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\coloneqq\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}x_{i}
. A
system of
m
linear forms in
d
variables
is a tuple
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
of linear forms. A tuple
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in G^{m}
is called an
instance
of
\mathcal{L}
if there exists
x\in G^{d}
with
\mathcal{L}(x)\coloneqq(L_{1}(x),\ldots,L_{m}(x))=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
. It is called a
non-degenerate instance
if additionally
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
are all distinct.

We often require that
|G|
be coprime with all the coefficients of the linear forms that define
\mathcal{L}
. Otherwise, it would be possible to have sets that do not contain instances of even a single linear form. For example, let
L(x)=2x
and
G=\mathbb{Z}_{m}
for some even number
m
. Then the set
A=\{a\in G:a\bmod 2\equiv 1\}
does not contain any instance of
L(x)
for
x\in G
.

For an integer
\lambda
with
(\lambda,|G|)=1
, the map
x\mapsto\lambda x
is a
G
-automorphism. Let
\lambda^{-1}
be a positive integer such that
\lambda^{-1}(\lambda x)=x
for all
x\in G
. This is guaranteed to exist because
(\lambda,|G|)=1
implies the existence of an integer
\lambda^{-1}
with
\lambda^{-1}\lambda\equiv 1\mod|G|
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
be a system of linear forms in
d
variables and let
A\subseteq G
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}
be independent random variables taking values in
G
uniformly at random. The probability that
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d})\in A^{m}
is given by the expectation

t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)\coloneqq\Ex\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}A(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}))\right].

Translation-invariance.

When studying linear patterns, it is natural to consider systems whose instances are invariant under translations.

Definition 1

(Translation-Invariance)
.

A system
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
in
d
variables is
translation-invariant
over a finite abelian group
G
if for every instance
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
of
\mathcal{L}
and every
c
, the tuple
(c+a_{1},\ldots,c+a_{m})
is also an instance of
\mathcal{L}
.

For example,
(a,a+b,a+2b)
, which represents
3
-progressions, is translation-invariant over every finite abelian group. As another example,
(x-y,y-z,2x-2z)
is translation-invariant over
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, but it is not translation-invariant over
\mathbb{Z}_{5}
.

Given a system
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
of linear forms (not necessarily translation-invariant) in
d
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
, we are interested in sets
A\subseteq G
that are free of
translations
of non-degenerate instances of
\mathcal{L}
. That is, there are no
c\in G
and non-degenerate instance
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
of
\mathcal{L}
such that
(c+a_{1},\ldots,c+a_{m})\in A^{m}
. Equivalently, one can introduce an additional variable
x_{d+1}
, and require that
A
is free of non-degenerate instances of the
translation-invariant
system
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, where

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})\coloneqq(x_{d+1}+L_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),\ldots,x_{d+1}+L_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})).

Definition 2

(Binary Systems of Linear forms)
.

A system of linear forms
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
in the
d
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}
is
binary
if every linear form in
\mathcal{L}
is supported on exactly two variables, and moreover no two linear forms in
\mathcal{L}
are supported on the same two variables.

In other words, every
L_{i}
is of the form
\lambda_{i}x_{a_{i}}+\eta_{i}x_{b_{i}}
where
a_{i}<b_{i}
and
a_{i},b_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and moreover,
\{a_{i},b_{i}\}\neq\{a_{j},b_{j}\}
if
i\neq j
.
Such a binary system naturally defines an oriented acyclic graph
H
with vertex set
\{1,\ldots,d\}
and an edge
(a_{i},b_{i})
for the linear form
L_{i}=\lambda_{i}x_{a_{i}}+\eta_{i}x_{b_{i}}
. We say that
H
is the
underlying graph
of
\mathcal{L}
.
In the case of
3
-progressions, the underlying graph
H
is an oriented triangle, as described below.

Example 1.7

(3-progressions as a binary system of linear forms)
.

Let
N>2
be an odd number. One can apply the change of variables
a=2x-2y
and
b=2y-x-z
to
(a,a+b,a+2b)
and capture
3
-progressions in
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
with the system of linear forms
\mathcal{L}=(2x-2y,x-z,2y-2z)
. The underlying graph
H
of
\mathcal{L}
is the oriented triangle
\{(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)\}
.

2
Graph counting lemma

We will adopt a more general framework that encompasses oriented graphs.
Recall that a directed graph is an
oriented graph
if it contains no opposite pairs of directed edges, or equivalently, it does not contain any
2
-cycles. Let
H=(V_{H},E_{H})
be a fixed oriented graph with vertex set
V_{H}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite set. For every
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, define

t_{H}(A)\coloneqq\Ex\left[\prod_{uv\in E_{H}}A(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})\right],

where
\mathbf{x}_{v}
for
v\in V_{H}
are independent random variables taking values in
\mathcal{X}
uniformly at random.
One can interpret
A
as a directed graph with vertex set
\mathcal{X}
and edges
(u,v)\in A
. Then
t_{H}(A)
is the probability that a random map from the vertices of
H
to the vertices of
A
is a graph morphism, i.e., it maps every edge of
H
to an edge in
A
. It is worth noting that, more generally, one can define
t_{H}(f)
for a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
. Our counting lemma is based on the following notion of pseudo-randomness.

Definition 3

(Spreadness for graphs)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
\delta>0
, and let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be finite sets. A set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
with density
\alpha
is
(\delta,r)
-
spread
if, for all subsets
S
and
T
of density at least
2^{-r}
, we have

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim S\\
\mathbf{y}\sim T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\leq(1+\delta)\alpha.

The following counting lemma states that if
A
is spread and does not have many vertices with small degrees, then
t_{H}(A)\approx\alpha^{m}
for all oriented graphs
H
on
m
edges. We state the result in a more general setting.

Theorem 2.1

(Main graph counting lemma)
.

Let
H=([k],E)
be an oriented graph with
m
edges such that
(u,v)\in E
implies
u<v
, and let
\alpha>0
. For every
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,m)>0
such that the following holds. Let
\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{k}
be finite sets, and for every edge
(u,v)\in E
, suppose
A_{uv}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
has density
\alpha_{uv}\geq\alpha
. If

\left|\Ex\prod_{(u,v)\in E}A_{uv}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})-\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv}\right|\geq\varepsilon\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv},

then for some
(u,v)\in E
, at least one of the following cases holds:

1.

There exist
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}
and
T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{v}
of densities at least
2^{-O_{m,\varepsilon}(\log^{2}(2/\alpha))}
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in S\times T\end{subarray}}[A_{uv}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq(1+\delta)\alpha_{uv}

2.

There exists
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}
of density at least
2^{-O_{m,\varepsilon}(\log(2/\alpha))}
such that

\Ex_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{v}}[A_{uv}(z,\mathbf{x})]\leq(1-\delta)\alpha_{uv}\qquad\forall z\in S

In other words, if
\left|t_{H}(A)-\alpha^{m}\right|\geq\varepsilon\alpha^{m}
, then either
A
contains a large subgraph that is significantly denser, or it contains many vertices whose degrees are significantly smaller than the average.

Our next theorem shows that when the goal is to identify a rectangle where the density deviates from
\alpha
, we do not need to require any assumption on the degrees of the vertices, and additionally, we can ensure a stronger lower bound on the density of the rectangle.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
H=([k],E)
be an oriented graph with
m
edges such that
(u,v)\in E
implies
u<v
, and let
\alpha>0
. Let
\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{k}
be finite sets, and for every edge
(u,v)\in E
, suppose
A_{uv}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
has density
\alpha_{uv}\geq\alpha
.

(i)

If

\Ex\prod_{(u,v)\in E}A_{uv}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv},

then for some
(u,v)\in E
, there exist
S\times T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}_{u}||\mathcal{X}_{v}|}=\Omega(\varepsilon\alpha^{m+1})
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A_{uv}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\alpha_{uv}.

(ii)

If

\Ex\prod_{(u,v)\in E}A_{uv}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{m}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv},

then for some
(u,v)\in E
, there exist
S\times T\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{u}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}_{u}||\mathcal{X}_{v}|}=\Omega(\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)^{m-1}\alpha^{m})
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A_{uv}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\leq\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\alpha_{uv}.

This implies the following immediate corollary for bipartite graphs which we use in the proof of
2.1
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
H=(L,R,E)
be a bipartite graph with
m
edges with left vertices
L=[k]
and right vertices
R=[p]
, and let
0<\varepsilon<1
. For every bipartite graph
A\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
and density
\alpha
, the following holds.
Suppose

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{p}\in\mathcal{Y}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{\{i,j\}\in E}{A}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right]\geq(1+\varepsilon)^{m}\alpha^{m}.

Then there exist
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
T\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}=\Omega(\varepsilon\alpha^{m+1})
such that

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim S\times T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]\geq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\alpha.

As mentioned in the introduction, the case where

.

Proof of

To prove

, we need the following lemma, which is a generalization of
[

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be finite sets, define

\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\{S\times T:S\subseteq\mathcal{X},T\subseteq\mathcal{Y}\},

and let
\conv(\mathcal{R})
denote the convex hull of
\mathcal{R}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Delta\geq 1
and
\gamma,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Let
D\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
F\in\conv(\mathcal{R})
, and suppose
\|D\|_{\infty}\leq\Delta
and
\|F\|_{1}\geq\gamma
.

(i)

If
{\left\langle\frac{F}{\|F\|_{1}},D\right\rangle}\geq 1+\varepsilon
,
there exists a rectangle
S\times T
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4\Delta}
and

\Ex_{(x,y)\in S\times T}D(x,y)\geq 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(ii)

If
{\left\langle\frac{F}{\|F\|_{1}},D\right\rangle}\leq 1-\varepsilon
,
there exists a rectangle
S\times T
with
\frac{|S||T|}{|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|}\geq\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4\Delta}
and

\Ex_{(x,y)\in S\times T}D(x,y)\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof.

Let
F=\sum_{i}c_{i}R_{i}
for rectangles
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
and
c_{i}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i}c_{i}=1
. Let
\tau=\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{4\Delta}
, and set
F\coloneqq\sum_{i}c^{\prime}_{i}R_{i}
with

c_{i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}c_{i}&\text{if }\|R_{i}\|_{1}\geq\tau,\\
0&\text{if }\|R_{i}\|_{1}<\tau.\\
\end{cases}

Note
\|F-F^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\tau
.

Part (i):
In this case, we have

\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime},D\right\rangle}}{\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}}\geq\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime},D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}=\frac{{\left\langle F,D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}+\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime}-F,D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}\geq 1+\varepsilon-\frac{\|F^{\prime}-F\|_{1}\|D\|_{\infty}}{\|F\|_{1}}\geq 1+\varepsilon-\frac{\tau\Delta}{\gamma}\geq 1+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}.

The definition of
F^{\prime}
implies there is a choice of
R=R_{i}
with
\|R\|_{1}\geq\tau
and
{\left\langle\frac{R}{\|R\|_{1}},D\right\rangle}\geq 1+\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}
.

Part (ii):
We have

\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime},D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}=\frac{{\left\langle F,D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}+\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime}-F,D\right\rangle}}{\|F\|_{1}}\leq 1-\varepsilon+\frac{\|F^{\prime}-F\|_{1}\|D\|_{\infty}}{\|F\|_{1}}\leq 1-\varepsilon+\frac{\tau\Delta}{\gamma}\leq 1-\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}.

Furthermore,

\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}\geq\|F\|_{1}-\tau\geq\|F\|_{1}-\frac{\varepsilon\|F\|_{1}}{4}=\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\|F\|_{1}.

We get

\frac{{\left\langle F^{\prime},D\right\rangle}}{\|F^{\prime}\|_{1}}\leq\frac{1-(3\varepsilon/4)}{1-(\varepsilon/4)}\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

The definition of
F^{\prime}
implies there is a choice of
R=R_{i}
with
\|R\|_{1}\geq\tau
and
{\left\langle\frac{R}{\|R\|_{1}},D\right\rangle}\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
.
∎

2.2
.

.

By applying the induction hypothesis to

Part (ii):
By applying the induction hypothesis to
H^{\prime}

\|F\|_{1}=\Ex\prod_{(u,v)\in E^{\prime}}A_{uv}(\mathbf{x}_{u},\mathbf{x}_{v})\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{m-1}\prod_{(u,v)\in E^{\prime}}\alpha_{uv},

{\left\langle\frac{F}{\|F\|_{1}},D\right\rangle}\leq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^{m}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv}}{(1-\varepsilon)^{m-1}\prod_{(u,v)\in E}\alpha_{uv}}\leq 1-\varepsilon.

Since
\|D\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\|F\|_{1}\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{m-1}\alpha^{m-1}

provides a rectangle

Grid norms.

One of the striking results in the work of Chung, Graham and Wilson
[
CGW89
]
states that if an undirected graph with edge density
\alpha
has approximately the same number of
4
-cycles as the Erdős–Rényi random graph
G(n,\alpha)
, then it shares several structural, spectral and statistical properties with
G(n,\alpha)
.

The pseudo-randomness theorem of
[
CGW89
]
naturally generalizes to binary relations
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. A set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
exhibits any of several pseudo-random properties if

t^{b}_{K_{2,2}}(A)=\alpha^{4}+o(1)

where

t^{b}_{K_{k,p}}(A)\coloneqq\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}^{k}\\
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}^{p}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{p}A(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right].

One of the key ideas used in the work of Kelley and Meka, and more explicitly in the follow-up work of Kelley, Lovett, and Meka
[
KLM24
]
, is to consider a certain generalization of
t^{b}_{K_{2,2}}(A)
.

Definition 4

(Grid semi-norms)
.

For a function
f\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
k,p\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle U_{k,p}(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}^{k}\\
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}^{p}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{p}f(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right].

Also define
\|f\|_{U(k,p)}\coloneqq|U_{k,p}(f)|^{1/pk}
and
\|f\|_{|U(k,p)|}\coloneqq U_{k,p}(|f|)^{1/pk}
.

Remark 4
.

Note that if
A:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\{0,1\}
, then

t^{b}_{K_{k,p}}(A)=U_{k,p}(A)
.

Lemma

2.5
below is the analogue of the so-called Gowers–Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for
U(k,p)
norms.

Lemma 2.5
.

(Gowers–Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)
Let
p,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
i\in[k]
and
j\in[p]
, let
f_{ij}\colon\mathcal{X}_{i}\times\mathcal{Y}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose that either
k,p
are both even numbers, or the functions
f_{ij}
take only non-negative values.
Then

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Gowers-CS}\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim\mathcal{X}_{k}}\Ex_{\mathbf{y}_{1}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{p}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{p}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{p}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right]\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\|f_{ij}\|_{U(k,p)}.

(7)

Proof.

Recall the generalized Hölder inequality:

\Ex_{\mathbf{z}}\left|\prod_{j=1}^{p}g_{j}(\mathbf{z})\right|\leq\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{z}}|g_{j}(\mathbf{z})|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

Choosing
z=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
and
g_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\Ex_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}}\prod_{i}f_{ij}(x_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
, we obtain

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Gowers-CS-proof}\text{L.H.S.\ of \eqref{eq:Gowers-CS}}\leq\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim\mathcal{X}_{k}}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{y}_{j}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

(8)

Next, note that for every fixed
j
, we can apply the generalized Hölder inequality to the variable
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}^{p}
and obtain

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim\mathcal{X}_{k}}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y})\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=\Ex_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}^{p}}\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim\mathcal{X}_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{t=1}^{p}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{t})

\displaystyle=\Ex_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}^{p}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{X}_{i}}\prod_{t=1}^{p}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{Y}_{j}^{p}}\left(\Ex_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{X}_{i}}\prod_{t=1}^{p}f_{ij}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{t})\right)^{k}\right)^{1/k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\|f_{ij}\|_{U(k,p)}^{p}.

Substituting these in (
8
), we obtain the desired inequality. ∎

Corollary 2.6

Proof.

We also need the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let

, and let

\Ex_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\left[f(\mathbf{x})<\alpha\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)\right]}\right]\geq\varepsilon^{p}/4

From the assumption,

\displaystyle\frac{3\varepsilon^{p}}{4}\alpha^{p}

Kelley–Meka-type bounds for binary systems

In this section, we establish Kelley–Meka-type bounds for the density of sets that do not contain translations of non-degenerate instances of binary systems of linear forms.

See
1.5

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of
1.5
.

3.1
Preliminaries

This section discusses the necessary notation and terminology from additive number theory.

Set addition.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. For an integer
k\geq 0
and
x\in G
, let

kx=\underbrace{x+x+\dots+x}_{k\text{ times}}\ \text{ and }\ (-k)x=-kx.

We denote the sumset of two sets
A,B\subseteq G
as

A+B\coloneqq\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}.

Given a natural number
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the iterated sumset of
A
as

kA\coloneqq\{a_{1}+\dots+a_{k}:a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in A\},

and we let

k\cdot A\coloneqq\{ka:a\in A\}.

Bohr sets.

We start with some standard notation and some basic facts about Bohr sets. Let
G
be a finite abelian group and let
\widehat{G}
denote the character group of
G
.

Given a set
B\subseteq G
, we define
\mu_{B}
to be the uniform probability measure on
B
.
The relative density of
A\subseteq G
in
B
is denoted by
\mu_{B}(A)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}
. The normalized indicator function of
B
is defined as
\varphi_{B}(\cdot)=\frac{|G|}{|B|}B(\cdot)
so that
\|\varphi_{B}\|_{1}=1
.

Given two functions
f,g\colon G\to\mathbb{R}
, define the convolution and the cross-correlation as

f*g(x)=\Ex_{\mathbf{y}}f(\mathbf{y})g(x-\mathbf{y})\text{ and }f\star g(x)=\Ex_{\mathbf{y}}f(\mathbf{y})g(x+\mathbf{y}),

respectively. Note that

\langle f*g,h\rangle=\langle f,g\star h\rangle.

We recall the definition of a Bohr set.

Definition 5

(Bohr sets)
.

For a non-empty
\Gamma\subseteq\widehat{G}
and
\tau\in[0,2]
, define the corresponding
Bohr set

B=\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
as

\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)\coloneqq\{x\in G:|\chi(x)-1|\leq\tau\ \ \forall\chi\in\Gamma\}.

We call
\Gamma
the
frequency set
of
B
, and
\tau
the
width
. We define the
rank
of
B
, denoted by
{\mathrm{rk}}(B)
, to be the size of
\Gamma
.

When we speak of a Bohr set, we implicitly refer to the triple
(\Gamma,\tau,\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau))
, since the set
\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
alone does not uniquely determine the frequency set nor the width.

We note some easy facts about Bohr sets. We have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline laim:bohrIntersection}\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)\cap\Bohr(\Gamma^{\prime},\tau)=\Bohr(\Gamma\cup\Gamma^{\prime},\tau),

(18)

and

\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)+\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau^{\prime})\subseteq\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau+\tau^{\prime}).

In particular, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following inclusion:

\lambda\cdot\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)\subseteq\lambda\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)\subseteq\Bohr(\Gamma,\lambda\tau).

Since
\chi(\lambda x)=\chi(x)^{\lambda}
, we also have

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Bohr_{m}ultiply}\lambda\cdot\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)=\Bohr(\lambda\cdot\Gamma,\tau).

(19)

Definition 6

(Dilate of a Bohr set)
.

If
B=\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
is a Bohr set, and
\rho\geq 0
, then
B_{\rho}=\Bohr(\Gamma,\rho\tau)
is the
dilate
of
B
with parameter
\rho
.

While Bohr sets, in general, lack approximate group-like properties, Bourgain
[
Bou99
]
observed that certain Bohr sets are
approximately closed under addition in a certain weak sense.

Definition 7

(Regularity)
.

A Bohr set
B
of rank
d
is
regular
if for all
\kappa
with
|\kappa|\leq\frac{1}{100d}
, we have

(1-100d|\kappa|)|B|\leq|B_{1+\kappa}|\leq(1+100d|\kappa|)|B|.

Note that by (
19
), if
(\lambda,|G|)=1
and
\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
is a regular Bohr set of rank
d
, then
\lambda\cdot\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
is also a regular Bohr set of rank
d
and has the same size as
\Bohr(\Gamma,\tau)
. We will use this fact frequently in the proofs.

The following two lemmas show that a low-rank Bohr set
B
can be made into a regular Bohr set
B_{\rho}
without decreasing its size by much.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
Bou99
]
; See
[
TV10
, Lemma 4.25]
)
.

For any Bohr set
B
, there exists
\rho\in\left[\frac{1}{2},1\right]
such that
B_{\rho}
is regular.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
TV10
, Lemma 4.20]
)
.

If
\rho\in(0,1)
and
B
is a Bohr set of rank
d
then
|B_{\rho}|\geq\left(\frac{\rho}{4}\right)^{d}|B|
.

One of the nuances of working with Bohr sets is that even if
B
is a regular Bohr set (e.g., a large interval centred at
0
), then for randomly chosen
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in B
, the distribution of
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is very different from the uniform distribution on
B
. A common strategy to address this issue is to work with two Bohr sets. We will have an additional regular Bohr set
B^{\prime}=B_{\rho}
for some sufficiently small
\rho
(e.g., a smaller interval centred at
0
). Then, for every
y_{0}\in B^{\prime}
, when
\mathbf{x}\in B
is chosen randomly and uniformly, the distribution of
\mathbf{x}+y_{0}
is close to the uniform distribution on
B
.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
BS19
, Lemma 4.5]
)
.

If
B
is a regular Bohr set of rank
d
and
x\in B_{\rho}
with
\rho\in(0,1)
, then

\|\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{B+x}\|_{1}\leq 200\rho d.

Proof.

The assertion of the lemma is equivalent to
|B\triangle(B+x)|\leq 200\rho d|B|
. Note
B+x\subseteq B_{1+\rho}
.
If
\rho\geq\frac{1}{100d}
, the lemma is obvious. Otherwise, by the regularity of
B
, we have
|B_{1+\rho}|\leq(1+100d\rho)|B|
, which implies the lemma.
∎

We also need the following generalization of
[
BS21
, Lemma 12.1]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that the following is true. Let
B
be a regular Bohr set of rank
d
, and
A\subseteq B
with
\mu_{B}(A)=\alpha
. Let
\gamma>0
, and suppose
\mathcal{B}
is a finite family of Bohr sets where each
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
is a subset of
B_{\rho}
, where
\rho\leq\frac{c\alpha\gamma}{d|\mathcal{B}|}
. Then, there exists
x\in G
such that one of the following two cases holds for the shift
A^{\prime}=A+x
.

1.

For every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
|\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A^{\prime})-\alpha|\leq\gamma\alpha
.

2.

There exists
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A^{\prime})\geq\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\right)\alpha
.

Proof.

If
c>0
is small enough, then by
Lemma

3.3
, for every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
,

\left|\langle A*\varphi_{B^{\prime}},\varphi_{B}\rangle-\langle A,\varphi_{B}\rangle\right|=\left|\langle A,\varphi_{B^{\prime}}\star\varphi_{B}\rangle-\langle A,\varphi_{B}\rangle\right|\leq\|\varphi_{B^{\prime}}\star\varphi_{B}-\varphi_{B}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\alpha\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|}.

In particular, since
\langle A,\varphi_{B}\rangle=\alpha
, we have

\langle A*\varphi_{B^{\prime}},\varphi_{B}\rangle\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\right)\alpha,

which, by averaging over all
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, implies

\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\right)\alpha\leq\Ex_{\mathbf{x}\in B}\Ex_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}A*\varphi_{B^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})=\Ex_{\mathbf{x}\in B}\Ex_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{B^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}-A)=\Ex_{\mathbf{x}\in B}\Ex_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A-\mathbf{x}).

Here, the last equality follows from the fact that
B^{\prime}=-B^{\prime}
for any Bohr set
B^{\prime}
.
Let
x\in B
be such that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:avgbohr}\Ex_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A-x)\geq\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\right)\alpha.

(20)

Suppose there is
B^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mu_{B^{\prime\prime}}(A-x)\leq(1-\gamma)\alpha
. Combining this with (
20
), implies that there is
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A-x)\geq\left(1+\frac{\gamma}{2|\mathcal{B}|-2}\right)\alpha
which implies item (2).
∎

We finally give the definition of an algebraically spread set with respect to Bohr sets.

Definition 8

(Algebraic Spreadness for Bohr Sets)
.

Let
B\subseteq G
be a regular Bohr set and
A\subseteq B
with
\mu_{B}(A)=\alpha
. We say that
A
is a
(\delta,d^{\prime},r)
-algebraically spread
subset of
B
if for every
x_{0}\in G
and every regular Bohr set
B^{\prime}
with
{\mathrm{rk}}(B^{\prime})\leq{\mathrm{rk}}(B)+d^{\prime}
and
|B^{\prime}|\geq 2^{-r}|B|
, we have

\mu_{B^{\prime}}(A+x_{0})\leq(1+\delta)\alpha.

(Dependent Random Choice)

4
Concluding remarks: resilience to spreadness

In this section, we discuss negative results and argue that, essentially, the approach based on the graph and algebraic spreadness does not apply to most systems of linear forms. For simplicity, we focus on the case of the group
\mathbb{F}^{n}
where
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field, and
q
is a fixed prime. The following notion of pseudo-randomness, based on graph spreadness, is implicit in the proof of
1.5
.

Definition 9

(Spreadness
[
KM23
,
KLM24
]
)
.

Let
r\geq 1
and
\delta>0
. A set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
of density
\alpha
is called

(\delta,r)
-
combinatorially spread
if, for all subsets
S
and
T
of density at least
2^{-r}
, we have

\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim S\\
\mathbf{y}\sim T\end{subarray}}[A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})]\leq(1+\delta)\alpha.

In the literature, two main notions are commonly used to characterize the complexity of systems of linear forms. The first notion is called the
Cauchy-Schwarz complexity
(
CS-complexity
for short), defined by Green and Tao
[
GT10
]
in their study of solutions of linear equations in the primes. The second notion, defined by Gowers and Wolf
[
GW11
,
GW10
]
, is called the
true complexity
. In both definitions, the complexity of a system is a positive integer. The following inclusions follow from the definitions of these complexity measures.

\{\mathcal{L}\ :\ \text{$\mathcal{L}$ is binary}\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{L}\ :\ \text{$\mathcal{L}$ has CS-complexity $1$}\}\subseteq\{\mathcal{L}\ :\ \text{$\mathcal{L}$ has true complexity $1$}\}.

Our sparse graph counting lemma shows that for binary systems of linear forms, combinatorial spreadness implies
t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)\approx\alpha^{m}
. On the other hand, we will show in
4.3
that if the true complexity of a linear system
\mathcal{L}
is larger than
1
, then there are sets
A
with density
\alpha
that are combinatorially spread with extremely good parameters, and yet
t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)>(1+\Omega(1))\alpha^{m}
.

True complexity and CS-complexity.

Gowers, in his proof of Szemerédi’s theorem, introduced a hierarchy of increasingly stronger notions of pseudo-randomness based on the so-called Gowers uniformity norms
\|\cdot\|_{U^{k}}
for
k\geq 1
. He used iterated applications of the classical Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to prove

\left|\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\left[A(\mathbf{x})A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\cdots A(\mathbf{x}+(k-1)\mathbf{y})\right]-\alpha^{k}\right|\leq k\|A-\alpha\|_{U^{k-1}},

showing that the density of
k
-progressions in
A
is controlled by the pseudo-randomness condition
\|A-\alpha\|_{U^{k-1}}=o(1)
.

Green and Tao
[
GT10
]
determined the most general class of systems of linear forms that can be handled by Gowers’ iterated Cauchy–Schwarz argument.

Definition 10

(The Cauchy–Schwarz Complexity)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}\}
be a system of linear forms. The
Cauchy–Schwarz complexity
of
\mathcal{L}
is the minimal
k
such that the following holds. For every

1\leq i\leq m
, we can partition
\{L_{j}\}_{j\in[m]\setminus\{i\}}
into
k+1
subsets, such that
L_{i}
does not belong to the
linear span of any of these subsets.

In particular, Green and Tao
[
GT10
]
showed that if the Cauchy–Schwarz complexity of
\mathcal{L}
is
k
, then

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:CS-complexity}\left|t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)-\alpha^{m}\right|\leq m\|A-\alpha\|_{U^{k+1}}.

(45)

Example 4.1
.

The system of linear forms
(x,x+y,\ldots,x+(k-1)y)
, which represents
k
-term arithmetic progressions, has CS-complexity
k-2
. It is also straightforward to verify that binary systems of linear forms have CS-complexity
1
.

Later, in a series of articles
[
GW11
,
GW10
]
, Gowers and Wolf initiated a systematic study of classifying the systems of linear forms that are controlled by the
k
-th Gowers uniformity norm. They defined the
true complexity
of a system of linear forms
L=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
as the smallest
k
such that the pseudo-randomness condition of
\|A-\alpha\|_{U^{k+1}}=o(1)
implies
|t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)-\alpha^{m}|=o(1)
.

By (
45
), the true complexity is at most the CS-complexity.
Gowers and Wolf
[
GW11
]
fully characterized the true complexity of systems of linear forms, provided that the field size is not too small. Let
L=\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}x_{d}
be a linear form in
d
variables. We identify
L\equiv(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
, and define the
k
-th tensor power of
L
by

L^{k}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{i_{j}}:i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[d]\right)\in\mathbb{F}^{d^{k}}.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
GW11
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
be a system of linear forms of CS-complexity at most
|\mathbb{F}|
. The
true complexity
of
\mathcal{L}
is the least integer
k\geq 1
such that the forms
L_{1}^{k+1},\ldots,L_{m}^{k+1}
are linearly independent.

It is known that the true complexity of the linear system identifying
4
-progressions is 2
[
GW10
,
GW11
]
. Moreover, it is well-known that there is a set
A
of density
\alpha
such that
\|A-\alpha\|_{U^{2}}=o(1)
(which implies combinatorial spreadness) but the count of
4
-progressions in
A
is far from what is expected from a random set of density
\alpha

[
Gow01
]
. Here, we generalize this result.
We essentially show that if the true complexity of a linear system
\mathcal{L}
is larger than
1
, then there are sets
A
with density
\alpha
that are combinatorially spread with extremely good parameters, and yet
t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)\not\approx\alpha^{m}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field of prime order.
Suppose that
\mathcal{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{m})
is a system of linear forms over
d
variables such that
L_{1}^{2},\ldots,L_{m}^{2}
are linearly dependent. There exists a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
with density
\alpha\geq\frac{1}{3}
that is
(e^{-\Omega(n)},\Omega(n))
-combinatorially spread,
yet

t_{\mathcal{L}}(A)>(1+\Omega(1))\alpha^{m}.

As the proof makes clear, the constant
\tfrac{1}{3}
can be replaced by any constant strictly smaller than
1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\colon\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{C}
be an arbitrary non-trivial character of the additive group of
\mathbb{F}
. Concretely, if we identify
\mathbb{F}
with
\{0,\dots,q-1\}
, then we can take
\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}(x)=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{|\mathbb{F}|}x}
.
For a given
\rho=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
, let

\Delta_{\rho}\coloneqq\left|\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbb{F}^{d}\\
\mathbf{y}\sim\mathbb{F}^{d}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}L_{i}(\mathbf{x})L_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right)\right|.

Estimating
\Delta_{\rho}
.

Let
\Gamma
be the set of all
\rho=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}L_{i}^{2}\equiv 0.

Hence, if
\rho\in\Gamma
, we have
\Delta_{\rho}=1
.

Next, we show that if
\rho\in\mathbb{F}^{m}\backslash\Gamma
, then
\Delta_{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{F}|}}
.
Let
L_{i}(x)=\sum_{a}\lambda i_{a}x_{a}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}L_{i}(x)L_{i}(y)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\sum_{a,b=1}^{d}\lambda_{ia}\lambda_{ib}x_{a}y_{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\lambda_{ia}\lambda_{ib}\right)x_{a}y_{b}.

For
\rho\in\mathbb{F}^{m}\backslash\Gamma
, since

0\not\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}L_{i}^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\lambda_{ia}\lambda_{ib}:a,b\in[d]\right),

there are
a,b
such that

\alpha\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\lambda_{ia}\lambda_{ib}\not\neq 0.

Therefore, for every fixed assignment to the variables
\{x_{i}:i\in[d]\backslash\{a\}\}\cup\{y_{i}:i\in[d]\backslash\{b\}\}
, there exist
\beta,\gamma,\psi\in\mathbb{F}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}L_{i}(x)L_{i}(y)=\alpha x_{a}y_{b}+\beta x_{a}+\gamma y_{b}+\psi.

By the standard Gauss sum estimate
[
GW11
, Lemma 3.1]
, we have

\left|\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{a},\mathbf{x}_{b}\in\mathbb{F}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\Bigg(\alpha\mathbf{x}_{a}\mathbf{x}_{b}+\beta\mathbf{x}_{a}+\gamma\mathbf{x}_{b}+\psi\Bigg)\right|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{F}|}}.

Averaging over all assignments to
\{x_{i}:i\in[d]\backslash\{a\}\}\cup\{y_{i}:i\in[d]\backslash\{b\}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{F}|}}.

Constructing a spread function
f
.

Define
f\colon\mathbb{F}^{n}\to[0,1]
as

f(x)\coloneqq G(Q(x)),

where

G\colon\mathbb{F}\to[0,1]
is defined as

G(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{0\neq\gamma\in\mathbb{F}}\frac{1}{2|\mathbb{F}|}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}(\gamma z),

and

Q\colon\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}
as

Q(y)=y_{1}y_{2}+y_{3}y_{4}+\cdots+y_{n-1}y_{n}
,
where
n
is some even number.
The distribution of
Q(\mathbf{x})
approaches the uniform distribution as
n\to\infty
, and in particular,
\Ex[f]
approaches
\Ex[G]=\frac{1}{2}
.
Using the Fourier transform of
G
, we have

f(x)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}}\widehat{G}(\gamma)\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}(\gamma Q(x)).

For
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{m}f\left(L_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}f\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}x_{j}\right).

By substituting the Fourier transform of
G
and expanding it, the above equals

\sum_{\rho\in\mathbb{F}^{m}}\widehat{G}(\rho_{1})\cdots\widehat{G}(\rho_{m})\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}Q\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}x_{j}\right)\right).

By the definition of
Q
, we have

\displaystyle\Ex_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}\sim\mathbb{F}^{n}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}Q\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}\sim\mathbb{F}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1},\ldots\mathbf{y}_{d}\sim\mathbb{F}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{ij}\mathbf{y}_{j}\right)\right)\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\rho}^{n/2}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\rho\in\Gamma,\\
\leq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathbb{F}|}}\right)^{n/2}&\text{if }\rho\not\in\Gamma,\end{cases}

which shows that

t_{\mathcal{L}}(f)\coloneqq\Ex\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}f(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{d}))\right]=\sum_{\rho\in\Gamma}\widehat{G}(\rho_{1})\cdots\widehat{G}(\rho_{m})\pm O\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{-n/4}\right).

Note that
\widehat{G}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2|\mathbb{F}|}
for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}
.
Moreover, since
L_{1}^{2},\ldots L_{m}^{2}
are linearly
dependent
,
\Gamma
contains at least one non-zero element. Therefore, the positivity of
\widehat{G}
implies that for large enough
n
,

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:large_{f}{}_{t}}t_{\mathcal{L}}(f)=\Ex[G]^{m}+\sum_{\rho\in\Gamma\backslash\{0\}}\widehat{G}(\rho_{1})\cdots\widehat{G}(\rho_{m})\pm O\left(|\mathbb{F}|^{-n/4}\right)=\Ex[f]^{m}+\Omega(1).

(46)

Next, note that for every non-zero
\chi\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, by the standard Gauss sum estimate
[
GW11
, Lemma 3.1]
,

|\widehat{f}(\chi)|=\left|\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}}\widehat{G}(\gamma)\Ex_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}\left(\gamma Q(\mathbf{x})-\chi(\mathbf{x})\right)\right|\leq e^{-\Omega(n)}.

Hence, denoting
\beta\coloneqq\Ex[f]=\widehat{f}(0)
,

\max_{\chi\neq 0}|\widehat{f}(\chi)|=\|\widehat{f-\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{-\Omega(n)},

which implies that for
S,T\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{1}_{S}(\mathbf{x})(f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})-\beta)\mathbf{1}_{T}(\mathbf{y})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\chi\neq 0}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S}}(\chi)\widehat{f}(-\chi)\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{T}}(\chi)\right|\leq\left(\max_{\chi\neq 0}|\widehat{f}(\chi)|\right)\sum_{\chi}|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{S}}(\chi)\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{T}}(\chi)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\widehat{f-\beta}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{1}_{T}\|_{2}\leq e^{-\Omega(n)}\|\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{1}_{T}\|_{2}=\frac{e^{-\Omega(n)}\sqrt{|S||T|}}{|\mathbb{F}|^{n}}.

For an appropriate constant
c>0
, if
|S|,|T|\geq|\mathbb{F}|^{n-cn}
, then

\left|\beta-\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim S\\
\mathbf{y}\sim T\end{subarray}}f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right|\leq\frac{|\mathbb{F}|^{2n}}{|S||T|}\times\frac{e^{-\Omega(n)}\sqrt{|S||T|}}{|\mathbb{F}|^{n}}=\frac{e^{-\Omega(n)}|\mathbb{F}|^{n}}{\sqrt{|S||T|}}=e^{-\Omega(n)}.

Converting
f
to a set
A\subset\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

To convert
f
to a subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we pick a random subset
\mathbf{A}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
by including every element
x\in\mathbf{A}
with probability
f(x)
. Note that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:spread_{f}{}_{t}o_{A}}|\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{1}_{S}(\mathbf{x})(f(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})-\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}))\mathbf{1}_{T}(\mathbf{y})|\leq\|\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{1}_{T}\|_{2}\|\widehat{f-\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}\|_{\infty}=\frac{|\mathbb{F}|^{2n}}{|S||T|}\|\widehat{f-\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}\|_{\infty}.

(47)

For every
\chi\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
, we have

\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}(\chi)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{F}^{n}|}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(x)\mathbf{e}_{\mathbb{F}}(x\cdot\chi),

and

\Ex_{\mathbf{A}}\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}(\chi)=\widehat{f}(\chi).

Set
t\coloneqq|\mathbb{F}|^{2n/3}
. By Hoeffding’s inequality,

\Pr_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\left|\widehat{\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}(\chi)-\widehat{f}(\chi)\right|\geq\frac{t}{|\mathbb{F}|^{n}}\right]\leq 2\exp(-\frac{2 t^2}{4 |\F|^n}),

and so, by the union bound,

\Pr_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\|\widehat{f-\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}}\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{t}{|\mathbb{F}|^{n}}\right]\leq 2\exp(-\frac{2 t^2}{4 |\F|^n})|\mathbb{F}|^{n}=o(1).

In particular, by (
47
) and the choice of
t
, with probability
1-o(1)
, the set
\mathbf{A}
has the following two properties:

(i)

\Ex[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}]=\Ex[f]\pm e^{-\Omega(n)}
, and in particular
\Ex[\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}]\geq 1/3
.

(ii)

For every
|S|,|T|\geq|\mathbb{F}|^{n-cn}
, we have

\left|\Ex[f]-\Ex_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\sim S\\
\mathbf{y}\sim T\end{subarray}}A(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y})\right|\leq e^{-\Omega(n)}.

Next, we consider
|t_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{A})-t_{\mathcal{L}}(f)|
. First, note that by the definition of
\mathbf{A}
, we have

\displaystyle|\Ex_{\mathbf{A}}\left[t_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{A})-t_{\mathcal{L}}(f)\right]|

\displaystyle=\left|\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\right]-\Ex_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}f(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\right]\right|.

Observe that if, for a given
x
, all
L_{i}(x)
’s are distinct, then

\Ex_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(L_{i}(x))\right]-\prod_{i=1}^{m}f(L_{i}(x))=0.

Hence,

\displaystyle|\Ex_{\mathbf{A}}\left[t_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{A})-t_{\mathcal{L}}(f)\right]|

\displaystyle=\left|\Ex_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbf{A}}(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\right]-\Ex_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}f(L_{i}(\mathbf{x}))\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\exists i\neq j\text{ s.t. }L_{i}(\mathbf{x})=L_{j}(\mathbf{x})\right]\leq m^{2}|\mathbb{F}|^{-n}.

Moreover,
