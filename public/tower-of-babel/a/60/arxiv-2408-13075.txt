Spectral Recovery in the Labeled SBM
Julia Gaudio ⋆ , Heming Liu †

Abstract
. We consider the problem of exact community recovery in the Labeled Stochastic Block Model (LSBM) with k k communities, where each pair of vertices is associated with a label from the set { 0 , 1 , … , L } \{0,1,\dots,L\} . A pair of vertices from communities i , j i,j is given label ℓ \ell with probability p i ​ j ( ℓ ) p_{ij}^{(\ell)} , and the goal is to recover the community partition. We propose a simple spectral algorithm for exact community recovery, and show that it achieves the information-theoretic threshold in the logarithmic-degree regime, under the assumption that the eigenvalues of certain parameter matrices are distinct and nonzero. Our results generalize recent work of Dhara, Gaudio, Mossel, and Sandon (2023), who showed that a spectral algorithm achieves the information-theoretic threshold in the Censored SBM, which is equivalent to the LSBM with L = 2 L=2 . Interestingly, their algorithm uses eigenvectors from two matrix representations of the graph, while our algorithm uses eigenvectors from L L matrices.



1.
Introduction

Community detection is an important inference task, with applications to biological, physical, and social networks. The goal in community detection is to recover latent community structure in a graph. The Stochastic Block Model (SBM), introduced by Holland, Laskey, and Leinhardt
[
14
]
, is considered the canonical probabilistic generative model for graphs with community structure. In the SBM, the vertices of a graph are randomly assigned a community label from the set
\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, where
\pi_{i}
denotes the probability that given vertex has label
i
. Given these community assignments, a pair of vertices from communities
(i,j)
are connected by an edge with probability
p_{ij}
, independently. The SBM has attracted significant attention in the probability, statistics, machine learning, and information theory literature; see the survey of Abbe
[
1
]
.

In this paper, we consider a generalization of the SBM, known as the Labeled Stochastic Block Model (LSBM), originally proposed by Heimlicher, Lelarge, and Massoulié
[
13
]
. In the LSBM, each pair of vertices
(u,v)
is associated with a label
\mathcal{L}(u,v)\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}
. Each pair of communities
(i,j)\in[k]^{2}
is associated with a label distribution, where
p_{ij}^{(\ell)}
is the probability that a pair of vertices
(u,v)
is given label
\mathcal{L}(u,v)=\ell
, conditioned on
u
belonging to community
i
and
v
belonging to community
j
.
The goal is to recover the community partition, given the labels
\{\mathcal{L}(u,v)\}_{u,v\in V}
.

Our focus is on the exact recovery problem, and we consider a particular regime, roughly corresponding to the well-studied logarithmic degree regime in the SBM. We propose a simple spectral algorithm for recovering the community labels, and show that it achieves the information-theoretic threshold for almost all parameters.

Related Work

The LSBM was introduced by Heimlicher, Lelarge, and Massoulié
[
13
]
, who investigated the sparse version of the problem (and therefore, the problem of partially recovering the communities). The sparse regime was further studied by Lelarge, Massoulié, and Xu
[
15
]
, we found recovery guarantees for min-bisection, semidefinite programming, and a spectral algorithm. Subsequently, Yun and Proutiere
[
17
]
studied partial, almost exact, and exact recovery in the LSBM. In particular, Yun and Proutiere
[
17
]
determined the information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery, and provided an efficient algorithm achieving the information-theoretic limit. Our main contribution is to provide a simpler algorithm which also achieves the information-theoretic threshold (under a certain technical condition).

The LSBM is related to the Censored Stochastic Block Model (CSBM), which corresponds to the LSBM with
L=2
. In the CSBM, we observe a censored graph, where we can only observe the connectivity status (present or absent edge) between some pairs of vertices, thus leading to three possible observations between pairs of vertices (present, absent, or censored). The CSBM has been studied in several works
[
2
,
12
,
7
,
8
]
. The most relevant work to ours is the paper of Dhara, Gaudio, Mossel, and Sandon
[
8
]
, who proposed a spectral algorithm for exact recovery in the CSBM. Their algorithm encodes the graph into two
signed
adjacency matrices, each using a different ternary numerical encoding of the three possible observations. It turns out that using two matrix encodings is essential in achieving the information-theoretic threshold; for nearly all model parameters, any spectral algorithm based on a single matrix will not achieve the information-theoretic threshold
[
8
]
. Our work shows that an appropriate generalization of their algorithm with
L
matrices also succeeds down to the information-theoretic threshold, confirming the prediction of Dhara et. al.
[
8
]
(under a distinctness of eigenvalues assumption).

Our work adds to the growing body of examples of spectral algorithms for community detection. Spectral algorithms for the SBM have a decades-long history, initiated in the work of McSherry
[
16
]
. However, it was only recently that Abbe, Fan, Wang, and Zhong
[
3
]
showed that a spectral algorithm achieves the information-theoretic threshold for exact recovery in the two-community SBM, without the need for a cleanup phase. The algorithm simply computes the second leading eigenvector of the adjacency matrix of the graph, and thresholds the entries at
0
to assign communities. Following the work of Abbe et. al.
[
3
]
, several works proposed cleanup-free spectral algorithms for other inference problems: the planted dense subgraph model
[
6
]
, submatrix localization
[
6
]
, and community detection in the presence of side information, in both the SBM and Gaussian models
[
11
]
. Spectral algorithms have been derived not only for adjacency matrices, but also for Laplacians
[
5
]
and the so-called “similarity matrix” of a hypergraph
[
10
]
.
The common theme in the analysis of these algorithms is
entrywise eigenvector analysis
, developed by Abbe et. al.
[
3
]
; we crucially use these results in our analysis.

Notation

The infinity norm of a vector
x
is denoted
\|x\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}|x_{i}|
. Given two matrices
A,B
with the same dimensions, the notation
\langle A,B\rangle:=\text{tr}(AB^{T})
denotes the standard matrix inner product. We use Bachmann–Landau asymptotic notation in terms of the number of vertices,
n
.

2.
Model and Information-Theoretic Threshold

2.1.
Model Definition

We now describe the LSBM in the logarithmic degree regime.

Definition 2.1
.

The LSBM is described by parameters
k,L\in\mathbb{N}
,
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
q_{ij}^{(\ell)}
for
i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]
, and
t>0
. A graph on
n
vertices is sampled as follows:

(1)

Each vertex is independently assigned a community label, where label
i
is assigned with probability
\pi_{i}
, for
i\in[k]
.

(2)

Conditioned on the community assignments, each pair of vertices is given a label from the set
\{0,1,\dots,L\}
. A pair of vertices
(u,v)
in communities
(i,j)
, is given label
\mathcal{L}(u,v)\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}
. The label is
\ell
with probability
\frac{t\log n}{n}\cdot q_{ij}^{(\ell)}
, for
\ell\in[L]
. With probability
1-\frac{t\log n}{n}
, the label is
0
.

We denote a graph
G
generated with these parameters by
G\sim\text{LSBM}(\pi,q,t,n)
.

Note that for every pair
i,j
, the values
\{q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell\in[L]}
must correspond to a valid probability distribution; that is,
\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{ij}^{(\ell)}=1
. With this parametrization, the
0
label is most frequent. Since the
0
label appears with the same probability for every pair
(i,j)
, it is also uninformative. Therefore, another interpretation of this model is that we observe useful information (i.e., a label from
\{1,2,\dots,L\}
) for a given pair with probability
\frac{t\log n}{n}
, and the label is
\ell
with probability
q_{ij}^{(\ell)}
for a pair of vertices in communities
i,j
. In the case where
L=2
, this parameterization captures the version of the CSBM which was studied by
[
12
,
7
,
8
]
.

2.2.
Information-Theoretic Threshold

The information-theoretic threshold is stated in terms of a Chernoff–Helllinger divergence
[
4
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The Chernoff–Hellinger divergence between
x
and
y
, denoted
D_{+}(x,y)
, is given by

D_{+}(x,y)=\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda x_{i}+(1-\lambda)y_{i}-x_{i}^{\lambda}y_{i}^{1-\lambda}\right\}.

The definition straightforwardly extends to multidimensional (e.g., matrix-valued)
x
and
y
. Also note that if
x
and
y
represent probability distributions, then

(2.1)

D_{+}(x,y)=1-\inf_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{\lambda}y_{i}^{1-\lambda}\right\}.

We say that an estimator achieves exact recovery if it determines the correct community partition with probability
1-o(1)
. The following result characterizes the information-theoretic threshold in the regime described above, and follows directly from
[
17
, Theorem 3]
(see the Appendix for details). See also
[
9
, Theorem 1]
which determines the IT threshold for a much more general setting.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\{q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\}_{i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}
, and
t>0
. Let
G_{n}\sim\text{LSBM}(\pi,q,t,n)
. For
i\in[k]
, let
\theta_{i}
be the
k\times L
matrix whose
(j,\ell)
entry is
\pi_{j}q_{ij}^{(\ell)}
. Define

(2.2)

t_{c}=\left(\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})\right)^{-1}.

(1)

If
t>t_{c}
, then the MAP estimator achieves exact recovery.

(2)

If
t<t_{c}
, then any estimator fails to achieve exact recovery. Moreover, any estimator fails to recover the community partition with probability
1-o(1)
.

3.
Spectral Algorithm

We propose a spectral algorithm for community recovery, which is an extension of the
L=2
case covered by
[
8
]
. At a high level, we construct
L
matrix representations of the input graph, and compute multiple carefully weighted linear combinations of the leading eigenvectors of these matrices in order to infer the community partition. The design of our algorithm hinges on a precise entrywise control over the leading eigenvectors, using the machinery developed by Abbe, Fan, Wang, and Zhong
[
3
]
.

In more detail, the
\ell^{\text{th}}
matrix has the following form:

(3.1)

\displaystyle A^{(\ell)}_{uv}=\begin{cases}0&\mathcal{L}(u,v)\neq\ell\\
1&\mathcal{L}(u,v)=\ell.\end{cases}

Let
u_{1}^{(\ell)},\dots,u_{k}^{(\ell)}
be the top
k
leading eigenvectors of
A^{(\ell)}
. Informally, the results of Abbe et. al.
[
3
]
show that

u^{(\ell)}_{i}\approx\frac{A^{(\ell)}u_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}

for
i\in[k]
, where
(\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)},u_{i}^{\star(\ell)})
is the
i^{\text{th}}
leading eigenpair of
\mathbb{E}[A^{(\ell)}\mid\sigma^{\star}]
, and
\approx
indicates proximity in
\ell_{\infty}
. (For now we are disregarding the fact that eigenvectors are determined up to sign.) But then also

(3.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{i}^{(\ell)}u_{i}^{(\ell)}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}c_{i}^{(\ell)}\frac{A^{(\ell)}u_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}

for any constants
\{c_{i}^{(\ell)}:i\in[k],\ell\in[L]\}
.

Observe that since
\mathbb{E}[A^{(\ell)}\mid\sigma^{\star}]
is a block matrix, the vectors
u_{i}^{\star(\ell)}
themselves have a block structure organized according to the community partition (that is, for a given community
m\in[k]
, we have that
u_{iw}^{\star(\ell)}
takes a constant value for all
w
such that
\sigma^{\star}(w)=m
). Now consider the
v^{\text{th}}
entry of the vector on the right hand side of (
3.2
), corresponding to vertex
v
:

\sum_{w\neq v}\sum_{i\in[k]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\frac{c_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}A^{(\ell)}_{vw}u_{iw}^{\star(\ell)},

which we can write as

\displaystyle\sum_{w\neq v}\sum_{j\in[k]}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in[L]}\sum_{i\in[k]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\frac{c_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}A^{(\ell)}_{vw}u_{iw}^{\star(\ell)}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell^{\prime}\right\}.

Consider some
j\in[k]
and
\ell^{\prime}\in[L]
. Due to the block structure of the vectors
u_{i}^{\star(\ell)}
, we see that the inner summation
\sum_{i\in[k]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\frac{c_{i}^{(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}A^{(\ell)}_{vw}u_{iw}^{\star(\ell)}
is constant for all
w
with
\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell^{\prime}
. Therefore, the
v^{\text{th}}
entry of (
3.2
) is of the form

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\neq v}\sum_{j\in[k]}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in[L]}C_{j,\ell^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell^{\prime}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[k]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}C_{j,\ell}\cdot|\{w:\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell\}|.

That is, the
v^{\text{th}}
entry is a weighted sum of label counts incident to
v
. We will see that certain choices of coefficients
\{C_{j,\ell}:j\in[k],\ell\in[L]\}
are useful for clustering. In turn, we can design the weights
\{c_{i}^{(\ell)}:i\in[k],\ell\in[L]\}
to achieve the desired coefficients.

To determine the coefficients, we turn to the so-called
genie estimator
. Fixing a vertex
v
, the genie estimator is tasked with determining the label of
v
, knowing
\sigma^{\star}(w)
and
\mathcal{L}(v,w)
for all
w\neq v
. The genie estimator thus computes the MAP estimator for
v
; that is,

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i\in[k]}\left\{\pi(i)\prod_{j\in[k]}\prod_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{i,j}^{(\ell)}\right)^{|\{w\neq v:\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell\}|}\right\}

\displaystyle=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i\in[k]}\Big\{\log\left(\pi(i)\right)+\sum_{j\in[k]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\log\left(q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right)|\{w\neq v:\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell\}|\Big\}.

Observe that the
\log(\pi(i))
term is insignificant, meaning that the MAP is well-approximated by the MLE.
Comparing the above to (
3.3
), we see that the spectral algorithm is able to mimic the form of the genie estimator. Essentially, the values
\{\log(q_{ij}^{(\ell)}):j\in[k],\ell\in[L]\}
take the place of
\{C_{j,\ell}:j\in[k],\ell\in[L]\}
in (
3.3
), for each
i\in[k]
in turn. The algorithm is as follows.

. Let

.

4.
Proof of Exact Recovery

The proof of Theorem
3.1
relies on two key results. The first is a characterization of the leading eigenvectors of adjacency matrices. Throughout, we fix an assignment
\sigma^{\star}
such that the community sizes are close to their expectations. More precisely, letting
n_{j}=n_{j}(\sigma^{\star})=|\{v:\sigma^{\star}(v)=j\}|
, we assume

(4.1)

|n_{j}-n\pi_{j}|\leq n^{2/3}

(which holds with high probability, as each
n_{j}
is marginally distributed as
\text{Bin}(n,\pi_{j})
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Fix
\sigma^{\star}
satisfying (
4.1
), and let
\ell\in[L]
. Let
A=A^{(\ell)}
, recalling (
3.1
). Let
A^{\star}=\mathbb{E}[A\mid\sigma^{\star}]
. Let
(\lambda_{i},u_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
and
(\lambda_{i}^{\star},u_{i}^{\star})_{i=1}^{k}
be the
k
leading eigenpairs of
A
and
A^{\star}
, respectively. Suppose that
Q^{(\ell)}\cdot\text{diag}(\pi)
has
k
distinct, nonzero eigenvalues. Then for all
i\in[k]
,

\min_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\left\|su_{i}-\frac{Au_{i}^{\star}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\log\log(n)\sqrt{n}}

with probability
1-O(n^{-3})
.
Here
C>0
is a constant depending on
\{q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\}_{i,j\in[k]}
and
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}
.

The proof follows directly from
[
8
, Corollary 35]
together with
[
8
, Remark 44]
, and relies on the entrywise eigenvector analysis of
[
3
]

The second key result (which is similar to
[
8
, Corollary 19]
) ensures the separation of
degree profiles
arising from different communities. For a vertex
v
, let
d(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times L}
be the
degree profile
of
v\in V
, where the
(j,\ell)
entry of
d(v)
is equal to
|\{u:\sigma^{\star}(u)=j,\mathcal{L}(u,v)=\ell\}|
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each
i\in[k]
, let
W(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times L}
be the matrix whose
(j,\ell)
entry is equal to
\log(q_{ij}^{(\ell)})
. Suppose
t>t_{c}
. Then there exists
\delta>0
such that with probability
1-o(1)
,

\langle W(i),d(v)\rangle\geq\max_{j\neq i}\langle W(j),d(v)\rangle+\delta\log n

for all
i\in[k]
and
v
such that
\sigma^{\star}(v)=i
.

Proof.

Recall that we condition on a fixed
\sigma^{\star}
satisfying (
4.1
). It suffices to show that there exist
\delta,\eta>0
satisfying the following: for all
i,j\in[k]
where
i\neq j
and
v
such that
\sigma^{\star}(v)=i
, we have

(4.2)

\langle(W(i)-W(j)),d(v)\rangle>\delta\log n

with probability
1-n^{-1-\eta}
.

We employ a Chernoff bound strategy to prove the desired claim. Fix distinct
i,j\in[k]
and
v
such that
\sigma^{\star}(v)=i
. For any
\lambda>0
, the Markov inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\leq\delta\log n\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(e^{-\lambda\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\right)}\geq e^{-\lambda\delta\log n}\right)

(4.3)

\displaystyle\leq n^{\lambda\delta}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\right)}\right].

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in[k]}\sum_{u\neq v:\sigma^{\star}(u)=m}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\log\left(\frac{q_{im}^{(\ell)}}{q_{jm}^{(\ell)}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{L}(v,u)=\ell\}.

Note that the innermost summation can be represented by a random variable
X_{u}
which takes value
\log\left(\frac{q_{im}^{(\ell)}}{q_{jm}^{(\ell)}}\right)
with probability
\frac{t\log n}{n}\cdot q_{im}^{(\ell)}
, for all
\ell\in[L]
, and with probability
1-\frac{t\log n}{n}
takes value
0
(where
\sigma^{\star}(u)=m
). Due to independence of
\{\mathcal{L}(v,u)\}_{u\neq v}
conditioned on the community assignments, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in[k]}\left(1-\frac{t\log n}{n}+\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\frac{t\log n}{n}\cdot q_{im}^{(\ell)}e^{-\lambda\log\left(\frac{q_{im}^{(\ell)}}{q_{jm}^{(\ell)}}\right)}\right)^{n_{m}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\in[k]}\left(1-\frac{t\log n}{n}+\frac{t\log n}{n}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda}\right)^{n_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t\log n}{n}\sum_{m\in[k]}n_{m}\left(1-\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda}\right)\right),

where we have used the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}
. Since
n_{m}=(1+o(1))\pi_{m}n
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(1+o(1))t\log(n)\sum_{m\in[k]}\pi_{m}\left(1-\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(1+o(1))t\log(n)\left(1-\sum_{m\in[k]}\pi_{m}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda}\right)\right).

Let
\lambda^{\star}\in[0,1]
be such that

D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=1-\sum_{m\in[k]}\pi_{m}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda^{\star}}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda^{\star}}

(recalling (
2.1
)). We will set
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
, which requires showing that
\lambda^{\star}\neq 0
. To this end, let
f(\lambda)=\sum_{m\in[k]}\pi_{m}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{\lambda}
, and observe that by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, we have that
f(1/2)<\frac{1}{2}\left(f(0)+f(1)\right)
and at the same time
f(0)=f(1)=1
. It follows that
\lambda^{\star}\neq 0
.

Substituting
\lambda=\lambda^{\star}
into (
4.3
), we obtain

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\langle W(i)-W(j),d(v)\rangle\leq\delta\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{\lambda^{\star}\delta}n^{-(1+o(1))tD_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})}

Since
t>t_{c}
, it follows that
tD_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})>1
. Therefore, we can choose
\delta,\eta>0
such that (
4.4
) is less than
n^{-1-\eta}
for
n
sufficiently large. While the choice of
\delta,\eta
is specific to the fixed pair
(i,j)
, we can take the minimum over all such pairs to ensure that (
4.2
) holds simultaneously for all such pairs.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma

implies that for all

we have

\min_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\left\|su_{i}^{(\ell)}-\frac{A^{(\ell)}u_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{i}^{\star(\ell)}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C}{\log\log(n)\sqrt{n}}

with probability
1-O(n^{-3})
.

Using the triangle inequality, we see that there exist
\{s_{j}^{(\ell)}\}_{j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}\in\{\pm 1\}^{kL}
such that for any
i\in[k]
,

\left\|\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\sum_{j\in[k]}c_{ij}^{(\ell)}\left(s_{j}^{(\ell)}u_{j}^{(\ell)}-\frac{A^{(\ell)}u_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\log\log(n)\sqrt{n}},

where the weights
\{c_{ij}^{(\ell)}\}_{i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}
are computed in Line
5
of Algorithm
3
. It follows that for some choice of
\{s_{j}^{(\ell)}\}_{j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}\in\{\pm 1\}^{kL}
, we have that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(U^{(\ell)}D^{\scriptscriptstyle(s^{(\ell)})}c_{i}^{(\ell)}\right)-\sum_{\ell\in[L]}A^{(\ell)}\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{c_{ij}^{(\ell)}u_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\log\log(n)\sqrt{n}}

with probability
1-O(n^{-3})
.

Next, consider the scaled vector

x_{i}^{(\ell)}:=\sqrt{n}\log(n)\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{c_{ij}^{(\ell)}u_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{j}^{\star(\ell)}},

which takes a block form (i.e.
x_{i}^{(\ell)}
is constant over

, for all

).
The matrix
B^{(\ell)}
constructed in Algorithm
3
is equal to
\mathbb{E}[A^{(\ell)}\mid\sigma^{\star}]
up to fluctuations in community sizes and permutation of the rows and columns. A straightforward perturbation argument (see

) implies that
x_{i,v}^{(\ell)}=(1+o(1))\log\left(q^{(\ell)}_{ij}\right)
for all

with
\sigma^{\star}(v)=j
.

It follows that for all
v\in V

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\log(n)\left(\sum_{\ell\in[L]}A^{(\ell)}\sum_{j\in[k]}\frac{c_{ij}^{(\ell)}u_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}{\lambda_{j}^{\star(\ell)}}\right)_{v}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\left(\sum_{\ell\in[L]}A^{(\ell)}x_{i}^{(\ell)}\right)_{v}=(1+o(1))\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\sum_{w\neq v}A^{(\ell)}_{vw}x_{iw}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\sum_{j\in[k]}\sum_{w\neq v}\mathbbm{1}\{\sigma^{\star}(w)=j,\mathcal{L}(v,w)=\ell\}\log\left(q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\langle W(i),d(v)\rangle,

where the second-last step is due to Step

Substituting back into (

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{n}\log(n)\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(U^{(\ell)}D^{\scriptscriptstyle(s^{(\ell)})}c_{i}^{(\ell)}\right)-(1+o(1))\langle W(i),d(v)\rangle\right|

Finally, since the correct signs

∎

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a simple spectral algorithm for exact recovery in the LSBM, and showed that it achieves the information-theoretic threshold for nearly all parameters (namely, whenever
Q^{(\ell)}\cdot\text{diag}(\pi)
has
k
distinct, nonzero eigenvalues for all
\ell\in[L]
). The result generalizes the case
L=2
covered by
[
8
]
, under the stated assumption. It may be possible to relax this condition by using a non-binary encoding for each matrix
A^{(\ell)}
, using up to
L+1
numeric values in each matrix, one for each label.

An interesting question is whether
L
matrices are necessary for achieving exact recovery down to the information-theoretic threshold. We expect that for all but a measure-zero set of parameters,
L
matrices are necessary. Indeed, such a result is known for the case
L=2

[
8
, Theorem 7 (2)]
.

Appendix

We now show how Theorem
2.3
follows from
[
17
, Theorem 3]
. Note that
[
17
]
uses a more general parameterization, letting
p_{ij}^{(\ell)}
be the probability that two vertices from communities
i,j
have label
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}
. Our parameterization takes
p_{ij}^{(0)}=1-\frac{t\log n}{n}
and
p_{ij}^{(\ell)}=\frac{t\log n}{n}\cdot q_{ij}^{(\ell)}
for
\ell\in[L]
. We continue to use
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to denote the community label distribution.

The result of
[
17
]
is stated in terms of a different divergence
D(\pi,p)
, which is related to the CH divergence. We use
\mathcal{P}^{a\times b}
to denote the set of
a\times b
matrices with each row representing a probability distribution. Let
p_{ij}^{(\cdot)}=\left(p_{ij}^{(0)},p_{ij}^{(1)},\dots,p_{ij}^{(L)}\right)
represent the label distribution for two vertices in communities
i,j\in[k]
. Similarly, let
p_{i}
denote the
k\times(L+1)
matrix whose
j,\ell
entry is
p_{ij}^{(\ell)}
.

Definition 5.1
.

For
i,j\in[k]
, let

\displaystyle D_{L+}(\pi,p_{i},p_{j})=\inf_{y\in\mathcal{P}^{K\times(L+1)}}\max\left\{\sum_{m=1}^{k}\pi_{m}D_{KL}\left(y(k,\cdot)\|p_{im}^{(\cdot)}\right),\sum_{m=1}^{k}\pi_{m}D_{KL}\left(y(k,\cdot)\|p_{jm}^{(\cdot)}\right)\right\},

where
D_{KL}(\cdot\|\cdot)
is the KL divergence; i.e.,
D_{KL}(P\|Q)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)\log\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)
for discrete distributions
P,Q
with support
\mathcal{X}
.
Define

D(\pi,p)=\min_{i\neq j}D_{L+}(\pi,p_{i},p_{j}).

Finally, the result is stated in terms of the following assumptions.

(1)

There exists
\eta>0
such that for all
i,j,m\in[k]
and
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}
,

\nicefrac{{p_{ij}^{(\ell)}}}{{p_{im}^{(\ell)}}}\leq\eta

(2)

There exists
\epsilon>0
such that for all
i,j\in[k]
with
i\neq j
,

\sum_{m=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(p_{im}^{(\ell)}-p_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{2}\geq\epsilon\overline{p}^{2},

where
\overline{p}=\max_{i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}p_{ij}^{(\ell)}
.

(3)

There exists
\kappa>0
such that for all
i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]
, it holds that

np_{ij}^{(\ell)}\geq(n\overline{p})^{\kappa}.

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem 3,
[
17
]
)
.

Consider the LSBM with parameters
\pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\{p_{ij}^{(\ell)}:i,j\in[k],\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,L\}\}
. Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. If the parameters
\pi,p
are such that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{nD(\pi,p)}{\log n}<1
, then no algorithm achieves exact recovery. Moreover, if
\frac{nD(\pi,p)}{\log n}<1-\delta
for some
\delta>0
, then any algorithm fails to identify the community partition with probability at least
1-n^{-\frac{\delta}{4}}
.

Conversely, if
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{nD(\pi,p)}{\log n}\geq 1
and Assumptions
1
-
3
hold, then the Spectral Partition Algorithm
[
17
]
achieves exact recovery.

Finally, we prove Theorem
2.3
in light of Theorem
5.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

We first show that Assumptions
1
-
3
hold trivially under our parameterization. Observe that Assumption
1
holds with

\eta=\max_{i,j,m\in[k],\ell\in[L]}\frac{q_{ij}^{(\ell)}}{q_{im}^{(\ell)}}.

Similarly, Assumption
2
holds with

\epsilon=\left(\max_{i,j\in[k],\ell\in[L]}q_{ij}^{(\ell)}\right)^{-2}\cdot\min_{i,j\in[k],i\neq j}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(q_{im}^{(\ell)}-q_{jm}^{(\ell)}\right)^{2}.

Finally, Assumption
3
holds with any
\kappa\in(0,1)
for
n
sufficiently large.

Next, we invoke
[
17
, Claim 4]
to conclude that for all
i,j\in[k]
,

\displaystyle D_{L+}(\pi,p_{i},p_{j})

\displaystyle\sim\frac{t\log n}{n}\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{m=1}^{k}\pi_{m}\left((1-\lambda)q_{im}^{(\ell)}+\lambda q_{jm}^{(\ell)}-(q_{im}^{(\ell)})^{1-\lambda}(q_{jm}^{(\ell)})^{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{t\log n}{n}D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j}).

Here
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
means
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=1
.
Observe that if

t<\frac{1}{\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})}~~~(=t_{c}),

then for some
i\neq j
and
\delta>0
we have
tD_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})<1-\delta
. It follows that for all
n
sufficiently large,
\frac{nD(\pi,p)}{\log n}<1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. Theorem
5.2
then implies that any estimator fails with probability
1-o(1)
.

On the other hand, if

t>\frac{1}{\min_{i\neq j}D_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})},

then
tD_{+}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})>1
for all
i\neq j
. It follows that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{nD(\pi,p)}{\log n}>1
, and so the second part of Theorem
5.2
implies that the MAP estimator succeeds in exact recovery.

∎
