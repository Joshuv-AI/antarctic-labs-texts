Hacking coherent-one-way quantum key distribution with present-day technology
Javier Rey-Domínguez, Álvaro Navarrete, Peter van Loock, Marcos Curty

Abstract
Recent results have shown that the secret-key rate of coherent-one-way (COW) quantum key distribution (QKD) scales quadratically with the system’s transmittance, thus rendering this protocol unsuitable for long-distance transmission. This was proven by using a so-called zero-error attack, which relies on an unambiguous state discrimination (USD) measurement. This type of attack allows the eavesdropper to learn the whole secret key without introducing any error. Here, we investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of zero-error attacks against COW QKD with present-day technology. For this, we introduce two practical USD receivers that can be realised with linear passive optical elements, phase-space displacement operations and threshold single-photon detectors. The first receiver is optimal with respect to its success probability, while the second one can impose stronger restrictions on the protocol’s performance with faulty eavesdropping equipment. Our findings suggest that zero-error attacks could break the security of COW QKD even assuming realistic experimental conditions.

Introduction

While QKD networks are currently being deployed worldwide

, QKD still faces certain inherent limitations such as channel loss, which fundamentally restricts the secret-key rate in point-to-point configurations

, as well as device imperfections that jeopardize the security of practical implementations

,

.

Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate these limitations and improve the security, practicality, and performance of QKD systems, including
e.g.
decoy-state QKD

,
13
,
14
]
, measurement-device-independent QKD

,

,

,

,

,

, twin-field QKD
[

, and distributed-phase-reference (DPR) QKD.
Among the latter protocols, coherent-one-way (COW) QKD
[

,
27
,
28
,
29
]
has attracted great attention in recent years for its simplicity and its promise to overcome the photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack
[
30
,
31
]
, thus achieving long transmission distances.
Indeed, commercial systems implementing the COW protocol have been developed
[
32
]
and experimental demonstrations have achieved distances of over 300 km
[
29
]
.
However, it is important to note that security analyses of COW-QKD that allow for long-distance communications —
i.e.
, that provide lower bounds on the secret-key rate that scale linearly with the channel transmittance
\eta

[

. This contrasts with known lower bounds of the order of
O(\eta^{2})
against general attacks
[

[
34
,
35
,
36
]
.

Crucially, González-Payo
et al.

[
37
]
showed very recently that indeed the key rate of COW-QKD scales at most quadratically with the system’s transmittance, rendering all long-distance demonstrations of this scheme performed so far insecure against general attacks.
This was achieved using a class of intercept-and-resend attacks known as sequential attacks
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
Intercept-and-resend attacks effectively transform the quantum channel into an entanglement-breaking channel, thus preventing the possibility of secret-key generation
[
42
]
.
Trényi
et al.

[
43
]
further refined this strategy by introducing a sequential attack that does not introduce errors in the system.
Notably, this latter so-called zero-error attack —which is based on the use of unambiguous state discrimination (USD) measurements
[
44
,
45
]
— is essentially optimal, in the sense that no other zero-error attack
[
46
]
can further limit the maximum achievable distance of COW-QKD.

Importantly, the works in
[
37
,
43
]
assume an idealised eavesdropper (Eve) with technological capabilities only limited by quantum mechanics.
Indeed, this is the standard scenario considered when proving the security of QKD.
However, this could be overconservative in certain cases, as the technology required by Eve to implement her attack might not be available in the mid-term future.
For instance, the noisy-storage model
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
considers the physical assumption that Eve does not have a large reliable quantum memory.

Similarly, in this work we assume that Eve is restricted to use present-day technology and cannot perform perfect quantum operations but employs faulty devices.
In this framework, we investigate the practical feasibility of the optimal zero-error attack against COW-QKD proposed in
[
43
]
.
For this, we introduce two USD receivers that only require off-the-shelf linear passive optical elements, phase-space displacement operations and threshold single-photon detectors (SPDs).
Remarkably, the first receiver corresponds to an optimal USD measurement, in the sense that it maximizes the probability of obtaining a conclusive measurement result when distinguishing Alice’s signals, but it can only
discriminate data signals.
The second USD receiver has a lower success probability but can discriminate both data and decoy signals.
This latter condition translates into stringent restrictions on the performance of COW QKD with flawed eavesdropping equipment.
For both receivers, we derive analytical expressions for the expected values of the key metrics that characterize the COW protocol as a function of the parameters that describe the noise and inefficiencies of Eve’s apparatuses.
In doing so, we provide a comprehensive framework for evaluating the security of COW-QKD in realistic scenarios.
We find that the most critical experimental parameter for the success of Eve’s attack seems to be the quality of interference between Alice’s weak coherent pulses and her strong light during the displacement operation.
Importantly, our results suggest that zero-error attacks are not only a great threat against COW-QKD, but they could break its security with present-day technology.

II
Coherent-One-Way QKD

The setup for the original COW system
[
26
,
27
]
is shown in
Fig.

1
.
In each round, Alice transmits a signal
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
to Bob with probability
p_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}
, where
i\in\{0,1,2\}
.
These signals are composed of two optical pulses that could either be in a vacuum state
\ket{0}
or in a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}
, where
\alpha>0
.
Specifically, the data signals
\ket{\varphi_{0}}=\ket{0}\!\ket{\alpha}
and
\ket{\varphi_{1}}=\ket{\alpha}\!\ket{0}
correspond to the bit values 0 and 1, respectively, and are generated with an equal a priori probability
p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}=p_{\mathbf{A}_{1}}=(1-f)/2
, whereas the decoy signal
\ket{\varphi_{2}}=\ket{\alpha}\!\ket{\alpha}
is prepared with probability
p_{\mathbf{A}_{2}}=f
.
Here, temporal sequences of states or signals are represented from right (earlier time) to left (later time).

Figure 1:

Schematic illustration of the original COW system
[
26
,
27
]
.
Alice sends Bob a random sequence of signals
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
, with
i\in\{0,1,2\}
. In the figure, we represent coherent (vacuum) states with a grey (white) pulse. Temporal sequences of states or signals are represented from right (earlier time) to left (latter time). Bob uses a beamsplitter with transmittance
t_{B}
to distribute the received signals between the data line and the monitoring line.
The former is used for key generation, while the latter implements a Mach-Zehnder (MZ) interferometer that measures the coherence between adjacent coherent pulses.
This is used to detect eavesdropping.
IM refers to the intensity modulator;
\Delta\tau
is the time delay between consecutive pulses;
{D_{{\textrm{D}}}}
,
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
, and
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
are Bob’s SPDs.

At Bob’s side, an asymmetric beamsplitter with transmittance
t_{B}
distributes the incoming signals between the data line and the monitoring line.
The former consists of an SPD
{D_{{\textrm{D}}}}
and is used for raw key generation.
Specifically, Bob assigns the bit value 0 (1) to a round if
{D_{{\textrm{D}}}}
clicks in the first (second) time slot of that round, and a random bit is assigned in the case of a double click.
On the other hand, the monitoring line consists of a Mach-Zehnder interferometer followed by two SPDs
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
and
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
, for constructive and destructive interference, respectively.
This line monitors the coherence between adjacent pulses.
In particular, the interference is such that two consecutive coherent states
\ket{\alpha}
cannot trigger
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
, but only
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
(see
Fig.

1
).

Once the quantum communication phase of the protocol ends, Bob publicly announces in which rounds he observed at least one detection click at
{D_{{\textrm{D}}}}
.
Then, Alice announces in which of these rounds she prepared a data signal.
The bits assigned to this set constitute the sifted key.

Three parameters are specially relevant in COW-QKD.
The gain,
G
, which is the probability that a signal sent by Alice produces at least one detection click in Bob’s data line; the quantum bit error rate (
QBER
) of Alice and Bob’s sifted keys; and the visibilities
V_{s}
, which quantify the coherence between adjacent coherent pulses, and are computed from the click probabilities in the monitoring line.
Specifically, these visibilities are defined as

V_{s}:=\frac{p_{{\textrm{M}{1}}|s}-p_{{\textrm{M}{2}}|s}}{p_{{\textrm{M}{1}}|s}+p_{{\textrm{M}{2}}|s}},

(1)

where
s\in\{2,01,02,21,22\}
represents a sequence of COW signals that contains two adjacent coherent states
\ket{\alpha}
, and
p_{{\textrm{M}{i}}|s}
is the conditional probability that detector
{D_{{\textrm{M}{i}}}}
clicks when such two coherent pulses interfere, given that Alice prepared the sequence
s
.
For example,
V_{2}
characterises the visibility between the two coherent pulses contained in a decoy signal
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
, whereas
V_{02}
characterises the visibility between the second optical pulse of
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
and the first one of
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
, both of them in the state
\ket{\alpha}
.
The other visibilities are interpreted in a similar way.
Finally, it is convenient to consider the average visibility, which is given by
[
29
,
37
]

V_{\textrm{ave}}:=\frac{p_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}-p_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}{p_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}+p_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}},

(2)

where
p_{{\textrm{M}{i}}}=\sum_{s}p_{s}p_{{\textrm{M}{i}}|s}
, being
p_{s}
the probability that Alice prepares the sequence
s
.

III
Zero-error attacks against COW-QKD

In a zero-error attack Eve intercepts all of Alice’s signals and performs a USD measurement on each of them
[
37
,
43
]
.
We denote by
p_{\mathbf{E}_{j}|\mathbf{A}_{i}}
the probability that Eve obtains the result
\mathbf{E}_{j}
, given that Alice emits the signal
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
.
Here,
\mathbf{E}_{0}
,
\mathbf{E}_{1}
and
\mathbf{E}_{2}
identify the signals
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
,
\ket{\varphi_{1}}
and
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
, respectively, and
\mathbf{E}_{3}
represents an inconclusive outcome.
Obviously, in the ideal scenario in which the USD measurement is implemented perfectly, we have by definition that
p_{\mathbf{E}_{j}|\mathbf{A}_{i}}=0\ \forall i\neq j
, with
i,j<3
.

Next, Eve groups the measured outcomes into blocks for processing them before she sends Bob a regenerated sequence of signals.
Precisely, a block of
(k+1)
signals corresponds to
k\in\set{0,1,\dots,{M_{\textrm{max}}}}
consecutive conclusive measurement outcomes, followed by an inconclusive measurement result.
For example, when
k=0
the block corresponds to one inconclusive measurement outcome, and Eve sends Bob two vacuum pulses,
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}=\ket{0}\!\ket{0}
.
If
k=1
, the block has one conclusive measurement outcome, say
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
, followed by an inconclusive one for which Eve sends Bob the vacuum signal
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
.
For all cases where
1<k<{M_{\textrm{max}}}
, the interpretation is similar.
Finally, if Eve obtains
{M_{\textrm{max}}}
consecutive conclusive measurement outcomes, she ignores the next signal from Alice, and simply treats it as an inconclusive result.
The parameter
{M_{\textrm{max}}}
allows Eve to cap the block length, thereby limiting the maximum delay she introduces in the channel.
Throughout this paper, we shall use the term
block
to denote the
k
conclusively measured signals together with the inconclusive measurement outcome, and the term
conclusive-block
when ignoring the latter.

For each conclusive-block, Eve searches for the first and last instances of a vacuum pulse within the block.
She then resends to Bob all the optical pulses situated between these two, exactly as she identified them —
i.e.
, if the measurement result with respect to a particular signal is
\mathbf{E}_{j}
, she resends
\ket{\varphi_{j}}
— but substitutes the coherent pulses
\ket{\alpha}
by
\ket{\gamma}
, with
|\gamma|^{2}\gg|\alpha|^{2}
, to increase the detection probability at Bob’s side.
The remaining pulses within the block that are outside this interval are resent to Bob as vacuum pulses.
As already mentioned, the last signal of a block, which corresponds to an inconclusive measurement outcome, is resent as
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
.
For a more detailed description of the attack, we refer the reader to
[
43
]
.

Notably, if Eve’s equipment is flawless —as considered in
[
43
]
— the attack described above introduces no errors on Bob’s side, while it reveals Eve full information about the key.
This is because Eve’s USD measurement ensures that she never misidentifies Alice’s signals, while the block processing strategy takes advantage of the fact that vacuum pulses do not introduce errors in the monitoring line.

In particular,
[
43
]
showed that, whenever
f/(1-f)\leq 2e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}
, a regime typically satisfied by practical implementations of COW-QKD, Eve’s optimal USD measurement (
i.e.
, the one that maximizes her probability of a conclusive result) satisfies
p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}=p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}=1-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}
and
p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}=p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}=p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{i}}=0\;\forall i\in\set{0,1,2}
.

IV
Implementation of zero-error attacks

In this section, we now introduce a linear optics circuit to implement Eve’s optimal USD measurement for the zero-error attack described above.
It is illustrated in
Fig.

2
a.
The input state
\ket{\chi}
, with
\chi\in\{0,\alpha\}
, corresponds to each of the two optical pulses sent by Alice within a signal.
That is, for each signal, Eve uses the same scheme in
Fig.

2
a twice, once per pulse.
Precisely, each pulse is displaced according to the transformation
\ket{\chi}\to\hat{D}(-\alpha)\ket{\chi}=\ket{\chi-\alpha}
, where
\hat{D}(x):=e^{x\hat{a}^{\dagger}-x^{*}\hat{a}}
is the displacement operator, and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
and
\hat{a}
are the creation and annihilation operators, respectively.
Finally, the resulting signal is measured with a SPD.

Scheme to implement Eve’s optimal USD measurement.
a) Ideal theoretical setup: Eve applies a displacement operator
\hat{D}(-\alpha)
on each pulse
\ket{\chi}
of the signal, and subsequently measures the outcome pulses with a threshold detector
{D_{{\textrm{E}{}}}}
.
b) Experimental implementation of the ideal setup: The displacement operation can be approximated in practice with an asymmetric beamsplitter of transmittance
T\approx 1
, which has at one of its input ports a laser source that emits coherent pulses with amplitude
\beta
.

If
{D_{{\textrm{E}{}}}}
clicks only in the first (second) time slot, the signal is identified as
\ket{\varphi_{1}}
(
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
); in all other cases, the result is inconclusive.
This is so because after the optical displacement, the data signals
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
and
\ket{\varphi_{1}}
are transformed into
\ket{-\alpha}\ket{0}
and
\ket{0}\ket{-\alpha}
, respectively, while the decoy signal is turned into a vacuum signal
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
. Consequently, a single click uniquely identifies a data signal, which occurs with probability
1-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}
, thus matching the optimal probability obtained in
[
43
]
.
Indeed, this measurement is unable to identify decoy signals, effectively removing
\mathbf{E}_{2}
from the POVM set and making
p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{i}}=0\ \forall i
.

In practice, it is well-known that a displacement operation can be approximated with a highly asymmetric beamsplitter of transmittance
T\approx 1

[
51
]
, which has at one of its input ports a coherent state
\ket{\beta}
, as shown in
Fig.

2
b.
This scheme transforms
\ket{\chi}

into
\ket{\sqrt{T}\chi+\sqrt{1-T}\beta}
, so the displacement
\hat{D}(-\alpha)
can be approximated by setting

\beta=-\sqrt{\frac{T}{1-T}}\alpha.

(3)

Indeed, with this choice, the states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{\alpha}
are transformed, respectively, into
\ket{-\sqrt{T}\alpha}
and
\ket{0}
.
This means that the detection probability at
{D_{{\textrm{E}{}}}}
decreases slightly when Alice transmits
\ket{0}
(when compared to the case where Eve can use an ideal displacement).
However, it remains zero when Alice sends
\ket{\alpha}
.
That is, the approximated displacement does not introduce errors.

Effect of device imperfections

Now, we investigate the performance of the setup above in a realistic setting, in which we accommodate the most relevant device imperfections.
For this, we allow the optical phase of the incoming pulses
\ket{\chi}
to the beamsplitter in

We denote such phase shift by

Also, we allow for an imperfect mode overlap between the two interfering pulses

\ket{\beta}

To characterize this effect we use the model introduced in

In addition, we allow for a non-ideal efficiency

in Eve’s SPD
{D_{{\textrm{E}{}}}}

Finally, we also account for small intensity fluctuations.
For this, we consider a simple model in which the amplitude of

3

where the parameter

V
Performance evaluation

To evaluate the performance of Eve’s zero-error attack with current technology, here we derive analytical expressions for the expected values of the three metrics defined in
Section

II
—namely the gain, the QBER, and the different visibilities
V_{s}
— as a function of the parameters that characterize Eve’s imperfect operation, as well as the parameters of the protocol.

In the calculations below, we shall consider that
\gamma
is sufficiently large to ensure that the signals Eve sends Bob always trigger his detectors unless she sends him a vacuum state, in which case Bob only records a click if a dark count occurs.
The dark-count probabilities at Bob’s detectors
{D_{{\textrm{D}}}}
,
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
, and
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
are denoted as
p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}
,
p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
, and
p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
, respectively.

First, we derive the expected gain
G
.
Then, we present the expressions to compute the QBER and the visibilities
V_{s}
.
The full derivation of these latter parameters can be found in
Appendix

B
.

V.1
Gain

The gain is defined as the probability that Bob observes at least one click in
D_{{{\textrm{D}}}}
in a round.
This quantity can be written as
G=N_{\textrm{clk}}/N_{\textrm{sig}}
, where
N_{\textrm{clk}}
is the average number of signals within a block that produce a click —single or double— at Bob’s side, and
N_{\textrm{sig}}
is the average number of signals within a block.

Let
p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}
be the probability that Eve’s USD measurement is conclusive, computed as

p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2}p_{\mathbf{A}_{j}}p_{\mathbf{E}_{i}|\mathbf{A}_{j}}.

(8)

Also, let
p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)
be the probability that Eve processes a conclusive-block of length
k
, which is given by
[
43
]

p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)=\begin{cases}p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}^{k}(1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}})&\textrm{when }0\leq k<{M_{\textrm{max}}},\\
p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}&\textrm{when }k={M_{\textrm{max}}},\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

Then, we have that
N_{\textrm{sig}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)(k+1)
, and therefore
[
43
]

\begin{split}&N_{\textrm{sig}}=\frac{1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}^{{M_{\textrm{max}}}+1}}{1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}.\end{split}

(10)

The average
N_{\textrm{clk}}
admits a similar decomposition in terms of the length of a block. In particular,

\begin{split}&N_{\textrm{clk}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)\left[n_{\textrm{clk}}(k)+p_{\textrm{clk}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\right],\end{split}

(11)

where
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k)
is the average number of signals within a conclusive-block of length
k
in which Bob observes at least one click in
D_{{{\textrm{D}}}}
, and
p_{\textrm{clk}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
is the probability that a click occurs in the last vacuum signal
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
of the block, corresponding to the inconclusive measurement outcome that happened after a conclusive-block of length
k
.
If we define
p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}
as the probability that Bob observes at least one click in a round where he receives a vacuum signal from Eve, then it is clear that

p_{\textrm{clk}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)=p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}=1-\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2},

(12)

for
k\in[0,{M_{\textrm{max}}}]
.

Now, let
p_{\mathbf{E}_{j}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}
be the probability that Eve obtains the outcome
\mathbf{E}_{j}
, given that her measurement was conclusive. Its value is given by

p_{\mathbf{E}_{j}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}=\frac{1}{p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}p_{\mathbf{E}_{j}|\mathbf{A}_{i}}.

(13)

Then, one can express

n_{\textrm{clk}}(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{\mathbf{E}_{i}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|i),

(14)

where
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|i)
is defined as the average number of signals where a click occurs within a conclusive-block of length
k
, given that the first signal of the block was identified by Eve as
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
.
Importantly, we note that the quantities
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|i)
admit recursive formulations
[
43
]
, which one can solve to compute
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k)
.

To illustrate this, let us focus on
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|0)
,
i.e.
, the case where the first signal of the block is identified by Eve as
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
.
According to her block-processing strategy, Eve translates this first signal into vacuum, since the first optical pulse of this signal is a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}
.
Consequently, we can disregard it and consider the reduced conclusive-block of length
(k-1)
.
Importantly, according to Eve’s processing strategy, this also implies that the first signal in the resulting truncated conclusive-block will be resent exactly as identified by her.
This is so because it is preceded by a vacuum pulse, given by the second optical pulse of
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
.
Then, it only remains to find the last signal of the conclusive-block that is not resent as vacuum.

There are three possibilities, which depend on the last signal identified by Eve.
If this signal is
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
, then it directly becomes the last non-vacuum signal of the block, and thus
(k-1)
non-vacuum signals will arrive at
D_{{\textrm{D}}}
.
If the last signal is
\ket{\varphi_{1}}
, it is translated into vacuum, and the preceding
(k-2)
non-vacuum signals will arrive at
D_{{\textrm{D}}}
.
Finally, if the last signal is
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
, this signal is also translated into vacuum, and, by definition, Bob will observe, on average,
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k-1|0)
clicks in
D_{{\textrm{D}}}
.
Putting all this together, one can write
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k-1|i)
as

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|0)=\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\left(k-1+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\left(k-2+2p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\left[n_{\textrm{clk}}(k-1|0)+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right],\\
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|1)=\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}k\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\left(k-1+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\left[n_{\textrm{clk}}(k-1|1)+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right],\\
n_{\textrm{clk}}(k|2)=\>&n_{\textrm{clk}}(k-1)+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}.\end{split}

(15)

Moreover, the starting points for the previous recursions are
n_{\textrm{clk}}(1|0)=n_{\textrm{clk}}(1|1)=p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}
and
n_{\textrm{clk}}(0)=0
. With this, one can solve the recursion and obtain

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{clk}}(k)=\>&k+kR^{k}\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2}\\
-\>&\frac{1+R}{1-R}\left(1-R^{k}\right)\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2},\end{split}

(16)

where we have used
R
as a shorthand for the recursion factor,

R\equiv p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}.

(17)

V.2
Quantum bit error rate

Next, we analyse the
QBER
.
This quantity can be written as
\textrm{QBER}=p_{\textrm{err}}/p_{\textrm{key}}
, where
p_{\textrm{key}}
is the probability that Bob distills a key bit in a given round, and
p_{\textrm{err}}
the probability that he distills an erroneous key bit.
We have that, asymptotically,
p_{\textrm{key}}=N_{\textrm{key}}/N_{\textrm{sig}}
and
p_{\textrm{err}}=N_{\textrm{err}}/N_{\textrm{sig}}
, where
N_{\textrm{key}}
(
N_{\textrm{err}}
) is the average number of key bits (erroneous key bits) distilled by Bob from a block sent by Eve.
Therefore, one can rewrite the error rate as
[
43
]

\textrm{QBER}=\frac{N_{\textrm{err}}}{N_{\textrm{key}}}.

(18)

To determine
N_{\textrm{key}}
and
N_{\textrm{err}}
we decompose them according to the length of the block processed by Eve.
For this, we define
n_{\textrm{key}}(k)
(
n_{\textrm{err}}
(k)) as the average number of key bits (erroneous key bits) distilled from a conclusive-block of length
k
, and
p_{\textrm{key}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
(
p_{\textrm{err}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
) as the probability that Bob distills a key bit (erroneous key bit) from the vacuum signal
\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
that is sent after a conclusive-block of length
k
, due to an inconclusive result.
Then, we have that

\begin{split}&N_{\textrm{key}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)\left[n_{\textrm{key}}(k)+p_{\textrm{key}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\right],\\
&N_{\textrm{err}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)\left[n_{\textrm{err}}(k)+p_{\textrm{err}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\right].\end{split}

(19)

All that remains is to calculate the values of
n_{\textrm{key}}(k)
,
n_{\textrm{err}}
(k),
p_{\textrm{key}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
and
p_{\textrm{err}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
that appear in the previous equations. For this, let us define
p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}
as the probability that Bob obtains an incorrect bit from a vacuum signal sent by Eve, given that he distills a bit that round.
Its value is given by

p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}=\frac{p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\left(2-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)}{2}.

(20)

Then, it can be shown (see
Appendix

B
) that
n_{\textrm{key}}(k)
has the form

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{key}}(k)=\>&\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}}{p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}\Big\{k\left(2-p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}-p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)\\
+\>&kR^{k-1}\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2}\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)\\
-\>&\frac{1-R^{k}}{1-R}\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2}\big(2-p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}-p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\big)\Big\};\end{split}

(21)

the parameter
p_{\textrm{key}}^{\textrm{last}}
is given by

\begin{split}&p_{\textrm{key}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\\
&=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)}{1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}&\textrm{ if }0\leq k<{M_{\textrm{max}}},\\
2p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}&\textrm{ if }k={M_{\textrm{max}}},\end{cases}\end{split}

(22)

the parameter
n_{\textrm{err}}(k)
has the form

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{err}}(k)=\>&\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}}{2p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}\Big\{k\Big[\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}+\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}\right)\left(2-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)\Big]\\
+\>&kR^{k-1}\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2}\\
+\>&\frac{1-R^{k}}{1-R}\Big[\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\\
-\>&\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}\right)\left(2-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)\\
-\>&2\!\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)\!\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)\!\Big]\!\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)\!\Big\},\end{split}

(23)

and the parameter
p_{\textrm{err}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
can be expressed as

\begin{split}&p_{\textrm{err}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\\
&=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{1}}\right)}{1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}&\textrm{ if }0\leq k<{M_{\textrm{max}}},\\
2p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}}p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}&\textrm{ if }k={M_{\textrm{max}}}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(24)

V.3
Visibilities

To calculate the resulting visibilities
V_{s}
, we start by expressing the probabilities that appear in
Eq.

1
as
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}|s}=p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}/p_{s}
, with
X\in\{1,2\}
.
The joint probabilities
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}
can be written as
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}=N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}/N_{\textrm{sig}}
.
Here,
N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}
is the average number of times in which the sequence
s
appears within a block and a click is registered in
D_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}
during the time slot associated with the interference of the two intermediate coherent pulses of the sequence.
This means that
V_{s}
can be expressed as

V_{s}=\frac{N_{{{\textrm{M}{1}}},{s}}-N_{{{\textrm{M}{2}}},{s}}}{N_{{{\textrm{M}{1}}},{s}}+N_{{{\textrm{M}{2}}},{s}}}.

(25)

Let us now start with the case
s=2
, since this is the only one that considers interference between pulses within the same signal.
By applying analogous reasoning to that used to derive
Eqs.

11
and
19
, it can be shown that
N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}
can be expressed as

N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}=\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)\left[n_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}(k)+p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)\right],

(26)

where
n_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}(k)
represents the average number of signals within a conclusive block of length
k
in which Alice sends
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
and
{D_{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}
clicks, and
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)
is the probability that this event occurs in the last signal of the full block.
Precisely, it is shown in
Appendix

B
that these quantities can be written as

\begin{split}n_{{{\textrm{M}{1}}},{2}}(k)=\>&\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{2}}}{p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}\bigg\{k\left(1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)\\
+\>&kR^{k-1}p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}\right)\\
-\>&\frac{1-R^{k}}{1-R}\big(1+p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\\
-\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\big)\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}\right)\bigg\},\\
n_{{{\textrm{M}{2}}},{2}}(k)=\>&\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{2}}}{p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}\bigg\{k\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)\\
-\>&kR^{k-1}p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}\\
-\>&\frac{1-R^{k}}{1-R}\Big[\left(p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}+p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}\right)\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}\right)\\
-\>&2p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}\Big]\bigg\},\end{split}

(27)

and

p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{2}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)=\begin{cases}\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{2}}p_{\mathbf{E}_{3}|\mathbf{A}_{2}}p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}}{1-p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}}&\textrm{if }0\leq k<{M_{\textrm{max}}},\\
p_{\mathbf{A}_{2}}p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}&\textrm{if }k={M_{\textrm{max}}},\end{cases}

(28)

where
R
is given by
Eq.

17
.

We now consider the remaining visibilities, where we denote the two-signal interference sequence as
s\equiv s_{2}s_{1}
, meaning Alice first prepares
\ket{\varphi_{s_{1}}}
and subsequently prepares
\ket{\varphi_{s_{2}}}
.
Importantly, a subtle nuance must be considered in the definition of the averages
N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}
.
Since we are dealing with the interference between adjacent pulses in consecutive rounds, these rounds may belong to different blocks.
If this happens, we will consider that the observed clicks are attributed to the first block.
This is an arbitrary decision, but it does not impact the final result.

Once again, we express the averages under analysis as a decomposition over the block length.
For this, we define
n_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}(k)
as the average number of times within a conclusive-block of length
k
where Alice sends
s=s_{2}s_{1}
and a click is registered in
D_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}
in the time slot associated with the interference between the two signals.
Those events in which one of the signals of the sequence
s
does not belong to the considered conclusive-block are not accounted in
n_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}(k)
.
Moreover, we denote as
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\textrm{last}}
the probability that Alice prepares
\ket{\varphi_{s_{1}}}
in the last round of a conclusive-block of length
k
and
\ket{\varphi_{s_{2}}}
in the next round, and Bob observes a click in
D_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}
in the time slot associated with the interference of these two signals.
Similarly, we define
p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\textrm{edge}}
as the probability that Alice prepares
\ket{\varphi_{s_{1}}}
in the last round of the full block of
k+1
signals,
\ket{\varphi_{s_{2}}}
in the first round of the next block, and Bob observed a click in
D_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}}}
in the time slot associated with the interference of these two signals.
With these definitions, which are illustrated in
Fig.

, we can write
N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}
as

\begin{split}N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}=\>&\sum_{k=0}^{M_{\textrm{max}}}p_{\textrm{cb}}(k)\Big[n_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}(k)\\
+\>&p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\textrm{last}}(k)+p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\textrm{edge}}(k)\Big].\end{split}

(29)

Simulation results

To compare the performance of Eve’s attack with respect to prior studies
[
37
,
43
]
, we compute here a simple upper bound on the secret-key rate.
With this in mind, let us conveniently refer to Eve’s attack as
undetectable
if the following two conditions are met.
Firstly, both the QBER and the average visibility
V_{\textrm{ave}}
observed for the attacked system must fall within their corresponding acceptance regions,
i.e.
,
\textrm{QBER}<\textrm{QBER}^{\textrm{th}}
and
V_{\textrm{ave}}>V^{\textrm{th}}
.
Secondly, the gain
G
of the attacked system must equal or exceed that expected from a legitimate system, which we will denote as
G^{\bar{\text{a}}}
to indicate that no attack is launched.
In particular, in the simulations we consider a typical lossy channel model for which
[
43
]

\begin{split}G^{\bar{\text{a}}}=\>&1-\Big[(1-f)e^{-{\eta_{B}}\eta_{\rm ch}t_{B}|\alpha|^{2}}\\
+\>&fe^{-2{\eta_{B}}\eta_{\rm ch}t_{B}|\alpha|^{2}}\Big]\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2},\end{split}

(35)

where
{\eta_{B}}
is the efficiency of Bob’s detectors,
t_{B}
is transmittance of the beamsplitter used by Bob to separate between data and monitoring line (see
Fig.

1
),
\eta_{\rm ch}=10^{-\alpha_{\rm ch}d/10}
is the channel transmittance,
d
is the channel distance (in km), and
\alpha_{\rm ch}
is the attenuation coefficient (in dB/km).
Importantly, if for a given
\eta_{\rm ch}
Eve’s attack meets the previous two conditions —
i.e.
, if it is undetectable— it immediately follows that no secret key can be distilled by Alice and Bob based on the observed metrics.
Indeed, the secret-key rate
K
can be simply upper bounded as
[
37
,
43
]

K<(1-f)\eta_{\rm ch}{\eta_{B}}\mu_{\textrm{max}}\equiv K_{\textrm{max}},

(36)

where
\mu_{\textrm{max}}
is the maximum value of
\mu
for which Eve cannot perform an undetectable attack. We remark that, in general,
\mu_{\textrm{max}}
depends on
\eta_{\rm ch}
. For example, for long distances

is expected to be low, and so it is easier for Eve to guarantee the condition

. However, one could compensate the low

by reducing

to make Alice’s signals harder to identify, thereby reducing

That is,
\mu_{\textrm{max}}
typically decreases with the channel distance.
In particular, previous works
[
37

43

showed that

when no technological constrains are placed on Eve. Since

already appears in

VII
Conclusions

Security proofs of quantum key distribution (QKD) typically consider that the eavesdropper’s capabilities are only limited by the laws of quantum mechanics.
Here, we have evaluated a less conservative scenario in which Eve is actually restricted by current technology.
In particular, we have studied the feasibility of zero-error attacks against coherent-one-way (COW) QKD in this framework.
To do so, we have introduced two practical receivers to perform an unambiguous state discrimination (USD) measurement of Alice’s emitted signals, which is the essential step of this type of attack.

Both proposed USD receivers are rather simple, and employ only linear passive optics, phase-space displacement operations and threshold single-photon detectors.
We have derived analytical expressions for the expected values of the main metrics (
i.e.
, the gain, the quantum bit error rate and the visibilities) of a COW QKD protocol assuming realistic experimental conditions,
i.e.
, as a function of the most relevant device imperfections of Eve’s equipment.
In doing so, we have found that the most critical experimental parameter seems to be the quality of interference between Alice’s weak coherent pulses and Eve’s strong light during her displacement operation.
Overall, our results indicate that zero-error attacks could break the security of COW QKD with present-day technology, particularly if Alice and Bob only consider the observed average visibility in their monitoring line, as it is done
e.g.
in commercial setups and long-distance implementations of this scheme.

Appendix A
Model for the imperfect mode overlap

Let us consider a real beamsplitter with transmittance
T

9

[
52
]
.
One of these modes from each pulse interferes as desired, while the two remaining modes do not interfere and only have their amplitudes diminished by the beamsplitter.
The fraction of light from the first (second) input that is perfectly matched and therefore interferes in the beamsplitter is determined by the parameter
t_{1}
(
t_{2}
), and the total degree of overlap in the beamsplitter is defined as
\mathcal{M}:=t_{1}t_{2}

[
52
]
.

B.1

Here we derive analytical expressions for the quantities

and

presented in

21
and
23

22

First, we note that both

in

14

That is, we can express

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{key}}(k)&=\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{\mathbf{E}_{i}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}n_{\textrm{key}}(k|i),\\

(37)

where

(

\ket{\varphi_{i}}

Let

represent the event in which Alice emits a data signal,

\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}=\mathbf{A}_{0}\!\cup\!\mathbf{A}_{1}

, and

.
Additionally, let
p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{i}}
be the probability that Bob distills an incorrect key bit (
i.e.
, a key bit different from the one sent by Alice), given that he receives
\ket{\varphi_{i}}
from Eve.
Note that this implies that the result of Eve’s measurement for that signal was
\mathbf{E}_{i}
and this signal was not discarded by her processing.
Moreover, we define
p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{c}}=\sum_{i=0}^{2}p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{i}}

\ket{\varphi_{\textrm{vac}}}
.

\begin{split}&p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{0}}=p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{0}}\frac{p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}}{2}+p_{\mathbf{A}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{0}}\left(1-\frac{p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}}{2}\right),\\

&p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{2}}=\frac{p_{\mathbf{A}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}+p_{\mathbf{A}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}}{2}.\end{split}

(38)

and

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{key}}(k|0)=\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{0}}\left(1+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right)\\

+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[n_{\textrm{key}}(k-1|0)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\big],\\

+\>&p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}(k-2)\big]\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{1}}\left(1+p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\right)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}(k-2)\big]\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[n_{\textrm{key}}(k-1|1)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\big],\\
n_{\textrm{key}}(k|2)=\>&n_{\textrm{key}}(k-1)+p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}p_{\textrm{clk}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}},\end{split}

(39)

and

\begin{split}n_{\textrm{err}}(k|0)=\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{0}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{0}}p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}+p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{0}}\\
+\>&p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{c}}(k-2)\big]\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{1}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[\left(p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{0}}+p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{1}}\right)p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\\
+\>&p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{c}}(k-2)\big]\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{E}_{2}|\mathbf{E}_{c}}\big[n_{\textrm{err}}(k-1|0)\\
+\>&p_{\mathbf{A}_{\textrm{key}}|\mathbf{E}_{2}}p_{\textrm{err}|\mathbf{B}_{\textrm{v}}}\big],\\

In this Appendix we introduce an alternative USD setup for Eve that, unlike the USD1, allows her to identify the decoy signals
\ket{\varphi_{2}}

We call this scheme USD2 and, as we did for the USD1, below we account for the most common imperfections in its model, and calculate its corresponding measurement statistics.

The idealized optical scheme is shown in

\ket{\chi}

Appendix E
Behaviour of the visibilities
V_{22}
,
V_{21}
, and
V_{02}

When
\varepsilon
is very small, most instances of
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
(all of them if we consider the strategy USD1) are resent to Bob as vacuum signals, so the dark counts of Bob’s detectors bring the visibilities down to zero.

As
\varepsilon
increases, so does the probability of conclusively (albeit erroneously) identifying a decoy signal
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
as
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
or
\ket{\varphi_{1}}
.
In this scenario, Eve sends Bob more non-vacuum pulses, and relatively less clicks in the monitoring line are attributed to the dark counts.
In the regime where dark counts are, comparatively to signal clicks, very low, the vacuum pulses sent by Eve —outside and at the edges of conclusive-blocks— can be ignored.
Since USD1 never returns
\mathbf{E}_{2}
, and USD2 has a significantly higher probability of misidentifying
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
as a data signal than correctly identifying
\ket{\varphi_{2}}
for sufficiently high
\varepsilon
, we can analyze the visibilities, in this regime, by focusing on the four possible sequences of data signals that Eve can erroneously identify: “00”, “01”, “10” and “11”.
In particular, when Bob receives “01”, only
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
can click due to the interference of two coherent pulses.
The sequence “10” contains two vacuum pulses in the intermediate time slots, so no clicks can be observed at Bob (aside from those from dark counts).
Finally, both “00” and “11” interfere
\ket{0}
and
\ket{\gamma}
, resulting in a click in both
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
and
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
with very high probability.

Let us focus now in
V_{22}
. We notice that, whenever Alice sends “22”, Eve misidentifies this sequence as one of the four previous sequences with equal probability, due again to the symmetry of the setup. This means that
{D_{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}
clicks with probability 3/4, while
{D_{{\textrm{M}{2}}}}
clicks with probability 2/4 (including possible double clicks in both detectors). Therefore, by using the definition of the visibility, we find that
V_{22}\approx 0.2
for large values of
\varepsilon
.

Similar arguments apply to
V_{02}
. When Alice sends the sequence “02”, we can distinguish between two different scenarios.
If
\varepsilon
is high enough such that the dark counts are not the main source of clicks in the monitoring line, but the probability of mistaking one data signal for another is still sufficiently low, then Eve mostly misidentifies the original sequence as “00” or “01” with equal probability, and therefore
V_{02}\approx 1/3
.
When
\varepsilon
increases, misidentifications of the data signal
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
of the sequence “02” happen more often, and thus also the sequences “10” and “11”, which implies that the visibility decreases.
Analogous reasoning applies to
V_{21}
.

In addition, from
Fig.

6

\varepsilon
where dark counts are relevant is larger for USD2 than for USD1, due to the fact that USD1 offers a larger
p_{\mathbf{E}_{c}}

Moreover, this range is also considerably larger for
V_{22}
than for
V_{02}

We also notice that USD2 performs better than USD1 for large values of
\varepsilon

The reason for this behavior is that the probability of erroneously identifying
\ket{\varphi_{0}}
as
\ket{\varphi_{1}}

Appendix F
Partial attack

As shown in
Section

VI
, the average visibility
V_{\textrm{ave}}
in the presence of Eve’s attack is above the acceptance threshold only for rather low values of the parameter
\varepsilon
. Nevertheless, it is still possible for Eve to remain undetected while obtaining partial information about the secret key. To this end, she can perform her attack on only a fraction of the rounds, so that the statistics from the unattacked rounds enhance the expected values of the protocol metrics, compensating for the errors introduced by her attack. Here we explain how we evaluate the expected value of the metrics when Eve executes the attack on a fraction
\tau_{a}
of the rounds.

We assume that the rounds under attack are consistently clustered in large groups of consecutive rounds, allowing us to disregard any possible border effects between the unattacked and attacked signals.
Then we have that the gain is simply given by
G=\tau_{a}G^{\text{a}}+(1-\tau_{a})G^{\bar{\text{a}}}
, where the superscript ‘
a
’ indicates that it is calculated for the system that is being attacked all the protocol rounds, as described in
Section

V
, while ‘
{\bar{\text{a}}}
’ indicates that the metric is calculated in the absence of Eve.
The result of
G^{\bar{\text{a}}}
can be computed from
Eq.

35
.
To calculate the QBER, on the other hand, we have to find the values of
N_{\textrm{key}}
and
N_{\textrm{err}}
. Precisely, we can express these as
N_{\textrm{key}}=\tau_{a}N_{\textrm{key}}^{\text{a}}+(1-\tau_{a})N_{\textrm{sig}}p_{\textrm{key}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}
and
N_{\textrm{err}}=\tau_{a}N_{\textrm{err}}^{\text{a}}+(1-\tau_{a})N_{\textrm{sig}}p_{\textrm{err}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}
, where

\begin{split}p_{\textrm{key}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}=\>&(1-f)\left[1-\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)^{2}e^{-{\eta_{B}}\eta_{\rm ch}t_{B}|\alpha|^{2}}\right],\\
p_{\textrm{err}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}=\>&(1-f)\left[1+\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}\right)e^{-{\eta_{B}}\eta_{\rm ch}t_{B}|\alpha|^{2}}\right]\frac{p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{D}}}}}{2},\end{split}

(60)

are the probabilities of these events in the absence of Eve. Similarly, we can modify the values of the visibilities by making
N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}=\tau_{a}N_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}^{\text{a}}+(1-\tau_{a})N_{\textrm{sig}}p_{{{\textrm{M}{X}}},{s}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}
, where

\begin{split}\frac{p_{{{\textrm{M}{1}}},{2}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}}{f}=\>&\frac{p_{{{\textrm{M}{1}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}}{p_{\mathbf{A}_{s_{1}}}p_{\mathbf{A}_{s_{2}}}}=\\
&1-\left(1-p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{1}}}}\right)e^{-2{\eta_{B}}\eta_{\rm ch}(1-t_{B})|\alpha|^{2}},\\
\frac{p_{{{\textrm{M}{2}}},{2}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}}{f}=\>&\frac{p_{{{\textrm{M}{2}}},{s_{2}s_{1}}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}}{p_{\mathbf{A}_{s_{1}}}p_{\mathbf{A}_{s_{2}}}}=p_{\textrm{d}}^{{{\textrm{M}{2}}}},\end{split}

(61)

are the probabilities corresponding to the relevant clicks when Eve does not act on the channel.

In order to compute the ratio of sifted key,
\textrm{EXT}_{K}
, that Eve can extract by enabling her attack during a fraction
\tau_{a}
of all the communication rounds, one can observe that

\textrm{EXT}_{K}=\frac{\tau_{a}N_{\textrm{key}}^{\text{a}}}{\tau_{a}N_{\textrm{key}}^{\text{a}}+(1-\tau_{a})N_{\textrm{sig}}p_{\textrm{key}}^{\bar{\text{a}}}}.

(62)
