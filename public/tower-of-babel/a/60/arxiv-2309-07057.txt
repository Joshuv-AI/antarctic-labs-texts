A smooth isotopy of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms on unit cube saving energy through extra dimensions
Siran Li

Abstract
. We construct an explicit example of a smooth isotopy { ξ t } t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]} of volume- and orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms on [ 0 , 1 ] n [0,1]^{n} ( n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 ) that has infinite total kinetic energy. This isotopy has no self-cancellation and is supported on countably many disjoint tubular neighbourhoods of homothetic copies of the isometrically embedded image of ( M , g ) (M,g) , a “topologically complicated” Riemannian manifold-with-boundary. However, there exists another smooth isotopy that coincides with { ξ t } \{\xi_{t}\} at t = 0 t=0 and t = 1 t=1 but of finite total kinetic energy.

1.
Introduction and the main result

In the seminal paper
[
2
]
in 1966, Arnold interpreted the Euler equations for incompressible inviscid flows on a Riemannian manifold-with-boundary
(M,g)
as the geodesic equation on
{\bf SDiff}(M)
, the group of orientation-, volume-, and boundary-preserving diffeomorphisms on
M
. The Euler equation on
(M,g)
reads as follows:

(1)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v+{\rm div}(v\otimes v)+\nabla p=0\qquad\text{in }[0,T]\times M,\\
{\rm div}\,v=0\qquad\text{in }[0,T]\times M,\\
\langle v,\nu\rangle=0\qquad\text{on }[0,T]\times\partial M.\end{cases}

Here
v:[0,T]\times M\to TM
is the velocity,
p:[0,T]\times M\to\mathbb{R}
is the pressure,
\nu
is the outward unit normal vectorfield on the boundary,
\langle\bullet,\bullet\rangle
is the inner product on
\partial M
induced by the metric
g
, and
{\rm div}
is the divergence operator with respect to the metric
g
. The infinite-dimensional Lie group
{\bf SDiff}(M)
is equipped with the
L^{2}
-metric. Assume
T=1
for normalisation.

Arnold’s viewpoint has led to fruitful researches on the Euler equations via global-geometric and variational methods. One important question is the following

Two-Point Problem:
Given
h_{0}
and
h_{1}\in{\bf SDiff}(M)
. Find a smooth curve
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
\xi_{0}=h_{0}
,
\xi_{1}=h_{1}
, and

(2)

J(\{\xi_{t}\}):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\partial\xi_{t}(\bullet)}{\partial t}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,{\rm d}t<\infty.

Here
\partial\xi_{t}/\partial t\in\Gamma(TM)
for each
t
, the
L^{2}
-norm is taken with respect to the metric
g
, and
J(\{\xi_{t}\})
is the
action
or
total kinetic energy
of the isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}
.

Without loss of generality, we take

h_{0}={\bf id}\text{ (the identity map)}

throughout. Under this provision, we have

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a diffeomorphism

h_{1}\in{\bf SDiff}(M)
is
attainable
if the two-point problem is soluble for
h_{1}
.

In the case that
h_{1}
is attainable, it is natural to further consider the following

Classical Variational Problem:
Given
h_{1}\in{\bf SDiff}(M)
. Find a smooth curve
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
such that
\xi_{0}={\bf id}
,
\xi_{1}=h_{1}
, and
\{\xi_{t}\}
minimises
J(\{\xi_{t}\})
in (
2
).

The above problems addresses whether two prescribed configurations of an incompressible fluid on
M
can be nicely deformed into each other inside
{\bf SDiff}(M)
. This is essentially different from the Cauchy problem for PDE. A solution for the classical variational problem will give rise to a smooth solution for the Euler Eq.
(
1
).

In a series of original works
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
around 1990s, Shnirelman showed that answers to both the two-point problem and the classical variational problem are, in general,
negative
. More precisely, denote
I^{n}:=[0,1]^{n}
; the following was proved in
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
:

Theorem 1.2
.

There are unattainable diffeomorphsims on
I^{2}
; in contrast, any diffeomorphism
h\in{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
is attainable when
n\geq 3
. Also, there exists an
h_{\rm BAD}\in{\bf SDiff}\left(I^{n\geq 3}\right)
such that there are no action-minimising curves of diffeomorphisms connecting
{\bf id}
and
h_{\rm BAD}
.

The existence of unattainable diffeomorphisms on
I^{2}
is closely related to the fact that the diameter of
{\bf SDiff}(I^{2})
with respect to the
L^{2}
-metric is infinite. This extends to general surfaces via symplectic geometric techniques. See Eliashberg–Ratiu
[
9
]
. On the other hand, if
h=h_{1}
is sufficiently close to
{\bf id}=h_{0}
in the Sobolev
H^{s}
-norm for
s>\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1
, then the two-point and classical variational problems are both soluble. See Ebin–Marsden
[
8
]
. This current note is essentially motivated by Theorem
1.2
.

The non-existence of action-minimising paths in
{\bf SDiff}(M)
, if it is established, suggests that the putative fluid flows which “minimise the kinetic energy” may allow collisions, merges, and splits of particle trajectories. Thus, one may need to forgo the smoothness of
{\bf SDiff}(M)
and work in measure-theoretic settings. In 1989 Brenier
[
5
]
introduced the notion of
generalised flows
, which are probability measures on the path space
C^{0}(I,M)
. A generalised flow is incompressible if and only if its marginal at any time is equal to the normalised volume measure on
M
. Roughly speaking, it corresponds to a measure-valued solution for the Euler equations (
1
).

Pioneered by Brenier
[
5
]
, variational models for incompressible generalised flows remain up to date a central topic in mathematical hydrodynamics. Many works have been devoted to understanding the approximations by diffeomorphisms, the role of pressure, the optimal transportational aspects, and the numerical algorithms for generalised flows. We refer the readers to Bernot–Figalli–Santambrogio
[
4
]
, Daneri–Figalli
[
7
]
, Ambrosio–Figalli
[
1
]
, Brenier–Gangbo
[
6
]
, Eyink
[
10
]
, and Benamou–Carlier–Nenna
[
3
]
. In addition, Arnold’s paper
[
2
]
is considerably influential on the study of diffeomorphism groups. See Misiołek–Preston
[
14
]
and many others.

The papers
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
10
,
15
,
17
,
18
]
mentioned above on unattainable diffeomorphisms or generalised flows all work with
I^{n}
(the unit cube) or
\mathbb{T}^{n}
(the flat torus). Extensions to more general manifolds remain elusive in the large. To the knowledge of the author, the only exceptions are Ambrosio–Figalli
[
1
]
, which established results for generalised flows on measure-preserving Lipschitz preimages of
I^{n}
or
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, and Eliashberg–Ratiu
[

]
, which studied smooth isotopies on some classes of topologically complicated smooth manifolds. Both the formulation and the proof of our main result rely heavily on
[
9
]
. This will be elaborated in subsequent sections.

On the other hand, notable developments concerning measure-preserving homeomorphisms on manifolds-with-boundary have taken place. See,
e.g.
, Fathi
[
11
]
, Schwartzman
[
16
]
, and many subsequent works.

In this note, utilising results on unattainable diffeomorphisms established by Shnirelman
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
and exploiting the
mass flow homomorphism
introduced by Fathi
[
11
, §5]
, we arrive at a somewhat surprising example of an isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
;
i.e.
, a smooth curve starting from
{\bf id}
in the special diffeomorphism group
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
. This isotopy is obtained by glueing countably infinitely many disjoint “building blocks” together. Each building block is concentrated on a low-dimensional Riemannian manifold or manifold-with-boundary
M
that is “topologically complicated” as in Eliashberg–Ratiu
[
9
]
and, loosely speaking, has the effect of stirring
M
in one direction for a very large number of times
N
. The building blocks differ from each other only in
N
up to translations and homotheties. The most important feature of the isotopy is that there is apparently no natural competing isotopy
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
with the same initial and terminal states but saves total kinetic energy/action with respect to
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
; however,
J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big)=\infty
, while there exists such an
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
with
J\big(\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big)<\infty
.

The only plausible energy-saving mechanism for the above construction is that, although the “stirring” on the low-dimensional submanifold
M
cannot be deformed to reduce energy
J
in
{\bf SDiff}(M)
, this ceases to be the case once we extend it to an ambient isotopy on
I^{\overline{n}}
, even if the extension only takes place in an arbitrarily small tubular neighbourhood of
M
. This explains the title of our paper.

More precisely, our main result of the note is as follows:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
I^{n}=[0,1]^{n}
be the unit Euclidean cube;
n\geq 3
. There exists a smooth isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
with infinite kinetic energy:
J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big)=\infty
and another smooth isotopy
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
with
\eta_{0}=\xi_{0}={\bf id}
,
\eta_{1}=\xi_{1}
, and
J\big(\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big)<\infty
.

In fact, let
(M,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold-with-boundary (possibly
\partial M=\emptyset
) such that
\dim M\geq 3
,
M
has nontrivial first real homology, and
\pi_{1}(M)
has trivial centre. Let
\iota:(M,g)\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
be an isometric embedding into the Euclidean space for some
n\geq\dim M
. Let
\{\rho_{j}\}
be any sequence of positive numbers shrinking to zero as
j\to\infty
, such that there are disjoint Euclidean balls
{\bf B}_{j}\equiv{\bf B}_{\rho_{j}}(x_{j})
in
I^{n}
. Denote by
\iota_{j}:M\hookrightarrow\frac{1}{2}{\bf B}_{j}\equiv{\bf B}_{\frac{\rho_{j}}{2}}(x_{j})
the embeddings obtained from
\iota
via homotheties and translations. Also, let
\{\delta_{j}\}
be any sequence of positive numbers with
\delta_{j}<\frac{\rho_{j}}{4}
such that
\delta_{j}
-tubular neighbourhoods of
\iota_{j}(M)
are embedded. Then, the isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
can be constructed to be supported on the
\delta_{j}
-tubular neighbourhoods of
\iota_{j}(M)
.

The isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
constructed here is “genuinely of infinite kinetic energy”, in the sense that its restriction to each
{\bf B}_{j}
never comes to a stop or return to any previous configurations at any time instant
t\in[0,1]
.

The second paragraph of Theorem
1.3
maintains that the “wild” isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
can be constructed in a sufficiently localised fashion;
i.e.
, supported on arbitrarily small tubular neighbourhoods of
\iota_{j}(M)
, the scaled embedded images of
M
into aribitrarily small disjoint balls in
I^{n}
. We say that
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is supported on a set
\mathcal{K}
if
\xi_{t}\equiv{\bf id}
outside
\mathcal{K}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Here the submanifold
M
satisfies the same topological conditions as in Eliashberg–Ratiu
[
9
, Appendix A]
—
\mathcal{Z}\big(\pi_{1}(M)\big)=\{0\}
and
H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})\neq\{0\}
— so that the mass flow (see §
3
below for details) can be as large as we want.

To illustrate the topological conditions on
M
, consider the following examples below. (The second is pointed out by a referee of an earlier version.)

•

A rope in
{\mathbf{E}}^{3}
,
i.e.
, thickening of a knot by making a tubular neighbourhood of it solid. It has nontrivial first real homology and its fundamental group has nontrivial centre.

•

Handlebody of genus 2: it has nontrivial first real homology, but the fundamental group is free on
2
-generators and hence has trivial centre.

Before presenting the proof, a few crucial remarks are in order:

Remark 1.4
.

One may deduce from the proof of Theorem
1.3
that each building block is energy saving, namely that

\displaystyle J\left(\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big|_{{\bf B}_{j}}\right)<J\left(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big|_{{\bf B}_{j}}\right)\qquad\text{for every }j=1,2,3,\ldots.

This is because each building block differs from the others only in terms of
N(j)
, the number of rounds that the loop
\sigma
traverses, in Eq. (
10
). Moreover, the building blocks have disjoint supports from each other, so there is no reason for only some of those to be energy saving.

Remark 1.5
.

To ensure that the “wild” isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
lies in
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
, the balls
{\bf B}_{j}
cannot have accumulation points in the interior of
I^{n}
. Therefore, heuristically speaking, the construction of
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
consists of “more and more rapid turns made on finer and finer scales” which eventually accumulate on the boundary
\partial I^{n}
. This is possible since an element of
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
is only assumed to be continuous up to the boundary.

The above convention on
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
is consistent with Shnirelman’s results in
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
, especially
[
17
, Theorem A]
— each element in
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
is attainable when
n\geq 3
— which is key to our proof of Theorem
1.3
.

In fact, the proof of
[
17
, Theorem A]
is based upon the result by McDuff
[
13
]
on the existence of
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
which is continuous in
t
on each subdomain
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}\Subset I^{n}
away from the boundary. Then, one carefully corrects the “boundary layers” with controllable total kinetic energy. More precisely,
[
17
, Lemma 1.5]
shows that for arbitrary
0<\epsilon<\delta
, any
\xi_{1}\in{\bf SDiff}(\mathcal{K_{\epsilon,\delta}})
can be connected to
\xi_{0}={\bf id}
through an isotopy
\left\{\xi_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}
supported on
\mathcal{K_{\epsilon,\delta}}
for all time and has

(3)

J\left(\left\{\xi_{t}^{\epsilon,\delta}\right\}_{t\in[0,1]}\right)\leq C(\delta-\epsilon),

where
C=C(n)
is a purely dimensional constant and
\mathcal{K_{\epsilon,\delta}}:=\left\{x\in I^{n}:\,\epsilon<{\rm dist}(x,\partial I^{n})<\delta\right\}
. Then, as in
[
17
, pp.282-283, proof of Theorem A]
, one iteratively corrects on time interval
\left[1-2^{k},1-2^{k+1}\right]
the “boundary layer” in
\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon_{k},\delta_{k}}
with
0<\epsilon_{k}<\delta_{k}:=4^{-k}
for
k=1,2,3,\ldots
. In this way, thanks to Eq. (
3
), the resulting isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
has finite total kinetic energy not only on subdomains away from the boundary as in
[
13
]
, but in the whole
I^{n}
. Nonetheless, note that the above construction does not ensure that
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
is smooth up to the boundary.

We also remark that Shnirelman’s example of unattainable diffeomorphism on
I^{2}
is also continuously but not smooth up to the boundary. See
[
18
, §2.4, Theorem 2.6]
. In fact, our construction in this note is essentially motivated by the idea of this construction.

Remark 1.6
.

Theorem
1.3
remains valid for any closed Riemannian surface
M
(
i.e.
, when
\dim M=2
) with
\mathcal{Z}\big(\pi_{1}(M)\big)=\{0\}
and
H_{1}(M,\mathbb{R})\neq\{0\}
, but subtleties may arise when
\dim M=2
and
\partial M\neq\emptyset
. The proof goes through verbatim, since there is no danger in selecting generators of
H_{1}(M,\mathbb{R})
represented by smooth embedded loops on closed surfaces.

3.
Mass flow

An essential ingredient of our developments is the mass flow homomorphism defined by Fathi
[
11
, §5]
. The idea had also appeared in Schwartzman
[
16
]
under the name of “asymptotic cycles”. We summarise here the definition and some properties of the mass flow.

Let
M
be a compact metric space. Given any good measure
\mu
on
M
(in the sense of Oxtoby–Ulam
[
19
]
), one can define a natural map

(4)

\widetilde{\theta}:\widetilde{{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}}(M;\mu)\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}\left(\left[M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right];\mathbb{R}\right),

where
\left[M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right]
is the homotopy class of maps from
M
to
{\mathbb{T}^{1}}
. When
M
is a compact manifold-with-boundary, we have

{\rm Hom}\left(\left[M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right];\mathbb{R}\right)\cong H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R}),

as well as

(5)

\widetilde{{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}}(M;\mu)={\mathscr{IS}}(M;\mu)/\penalty\sim,

with the equivalence relation
\{h_{t}\}\sim\{k_{t}\}
if and only if
h_{0}=k_{0}={\bf id}
,
h_{1}=k_{1}
, and there is a continuous map
F:I\times I\to{\bf Homeo}(M;\mu)
such that
F_{0,s}={\bf id}
,
F_{1,s}=h_{1}
,
F_{t,0}=h_{t}
, and
F_{t,1}=k_{t}
. The space
{\mathscr{IS}}(M;\mu)/\penalty\sim
is equipped with the quotient topology derived from the compact-open topology on the isotopy group.

To define
\widetilde{\theta}
in Eq. (
4
), let us take any
\{h_{t}\}\in{\mathscr{IS}}(M;\mu)
. Then, for any
f\in C^{0}\left(M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right)
, we have a homotopy
(f\circ h_{t}-f):M\rightarrow{\mathbb{T}^{1}}
which equals
0
at
t=0
. So one may lift it in a unique way to a function
\overline{f\circ h_{t}-f}:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\overline{f\circ h_{0}-f}=0
. We set

(6)

\widetilde{\theta}(\{h_{t}\})(f):=\int_{M}\overline{f\circ h_{1}-f}\,{\rm d}\mu.

One can easily check that
\widetilde{\theta}
induces a homomorphism as in Eq. (
4
), and that
\widetilde{\theta}
is continuous if
{\rm Hom}\left(\left[M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right];\mathbb{R}\right)
is endowed with the weak topology.

Write
{\mathcal{N}}(M;\mu)
for the kernel of the universal cover
\widetilde{{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}}(M;\mu)\to{\bf Homeo}_{0}(M;\mu)
. It is identified with the loop space:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(M;\mu)

\displaystyle\cong\Big\{\big[\{h_{t}\}\big]\in{\mathscr{IS}}(M;\mu)/\penalty\sim:\,h_{1}={\bf id}\Big\}

\displaystyle\cong\Big\{\{h_{t}\}\in\widetilde{{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}}(M;\mu):\,h_{1}={\bf id}\Big\}.

If
M
is a compact manifold-with-boundary, we further set

(7)

\Gamma:=\widetilde{\theta}\left({\mathcal{N}}(M;\mu)\right)\leq H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R}).

Then
\widetilde{\theta}
declines to a homomorphism

(8)

\theta:{\bf Homeo}_{0}(M;\mu)\longrightarrow H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})/\penalty\Gamma.

We shall call either
\widetilde{\theta}
in Eq. (
4
) or
\theta
in Eq. (
8
) the
mass flow homomorphism
.

The integrand
\overline{f\circ h_{1}-f}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
6
) should be understood as
\lim_{t\nearrow 1}\left(\overline{f\circ h_{t}-f}\right)
. For
f:M\to{\mathbb{T}^{1}}
with
M
connected,
\overline{f\circ h_{1}-f}
(where
h_{1}={\bf id}
) can potentially take any constant integer as its value. Thus
\Gamma
in Eq. (
7
) is nontrivial in general.

When
M
is a compact Riemannian manifold-with-boundary equipped with a normalised volume form
\Omega
, namely that
\int_{M}\Omega=1
, one may further characterise
\widetilde{\theta}
via smooth isotopies. (Fathi
[
11
]
attributed this result to W. Thurston, “
On the structure of the group of volume preserving diffeomorphism
[to appear]”. It seems to the author that this paper has never appeared in publication, though.) Indeed,
\widetilde{\theta}
is equivalent via the Poincaré duality to

\widetilde{V}:{\mathscr{IS}}^{\infty}(M;\Omega)\longrightarrow H^{n-1}(M;\mathbb{R}),

where for
\{h_{t}\}\in{\mathscr{IS}}^{\infty}(M;\Omega)
we define
\widetilde{V}(\{h_{t}\})
as follows: let
X_{t}\in\Gamma(TM)
be the vectorfield whose integral flow is
h_{t}
. Then

\widetilde{V}(\{h_{t}\}):=\int_{0}^{1}X_{t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\,{\rm d}t,

where
\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}
denotes the interior multiplication.

Finally, let us present
[
11
, Proposition 5.1]
, attributed therein to D. Sullivan. Consider the homomorphisms:

\displaystyle a_{M}:\left[M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right]\longrightarrow H^{1}(M;{\mathbb{Z}}),\qquad f\longmapsto f^{\#}\sigma;

\displaystyle b_{M}:H_{1}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow{\rm Hom}\left(H^{1}(X;{\mathbb{Z}});{\mathbb{Z}}\right),\qquad\text{the natural duality};

\displaystyle h_{M}:\pi_{1}(M)\longrightarrow H_{1}(M;{\mathbb{Z}}),\qquad\text{the Hurewicz map},

and define

\gamma_{M}:=a_{M}^{*}\circ b_{M}\circ h_{M}.

Then, whenever
M
is a connected compact metric space, it holds that

(9)

\Gamma\subset\gamma_{M}\Big(\mathcal{Z}\big(\pi_{1}(M)\big)\Big).

4.
Proof of the main Theorem
1.3

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem by explicitly constructing the infinite-energy isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
and deducing the existence of finite-energy isotopy
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in I}
from Shnirelman’s theorem on attainable diffeomorphisms (
[
17
]
). As commented prior to the statement of the theorem, our proof consists of three key components:

(1)

the mass flow homomorphism in §
3
;

(2)

the idea of Shnirelman’s proof for the existence of an unattainable diffeomorphism on
I^{2}
(
cf.
Theorem
1.2
and
[
18
]
); and

(3)

lower bound for total kinetic energy
J
by the mass flow (
cf.
Eliashberg–Ratiu
[
9
, Appendix A]
).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We divide our arguments into five steps below.

Step 1.
Let
(M,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold-with-boundary (possibly with
\partial M=\emptyset
) such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\big(\pi_{1}(M)\big)=\{0\},\quad H_{1}(M,\mathbb{R})\neq\{0\},\quad\text{ and }m=\dim M\geq 3.

Without loss of generality, assume that
M
is connected. Also let

\iota:(M,g)\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{E}}^{{n}}

be a smooth isometric embedding into Euclidean space, whose existence is guaranteed by Nash’s embedding theorem.

Consider the mass flow homomorphism in Eq. (
8
):

\theta:{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}(M,\mu)\to H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})/\penalty\Gamma,

where
\mu
is the normalised volume measure on
(M,g)
(which is clearly “good” in the sense of Oxtoby–Ulam
[
19
]
) and
{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}(M,\mu)
is the identity component of the group of
\mu
-preserving homeomorphisms. Under the assumption that
\pi_{1}(M)
has trivial centre,
\Gamma
is trivial in view of Eq. (
9
). In fact,
\theta
is a group epimorphism (
[
11
, §5]
).

For a closed manifold
M
, we may choose generators of the first real homology group
H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
to be smooth loops with smoothly embedded tubular neighbourhoods. When
M
is a compact manifold-with-boundary, this can also be done with tubular neighbourhoods uniformly away from the boundary, thanks to the product structure (
i.e.
, the “collar”) near
\partial M
.

Fix a generator
[\sigma]\in H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
as in the previous paragraph. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
take
h_{j}\in{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}(M,\mu)
such that

(10)

\theta(h_{j})=N(j)[\sigma],

where
N(j)
is a large natural number to be specified later. Thus
\|\theta(h_{j})\|=cN(j)
, where the constant
c
depends only on the choice of
[\sigma]
. Here and hereafter,
\|\bullet\|
denotes the quotient norm on cohomology/homology groups induced by the usual
L^{2}
-norm on differential forms.

Moreover, by virtue of Eq. (
5
), via lifting to the universal cover of
{\bf Homeo}_{\,0}(M,\mu)
one may identify each
h_{j}
with an isotopy on
(M,g)
, denoted as
\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}
.

Step 2.

To proceed, let us identify
M
with its isometrically embedded image
\iota(M)
in the sequel. Say that
M\subset{\bf B}_{R}(0)\subset{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
O_{j}
, a composition of homotheties and Euclidean rigid motions on
{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
, such that

\displaystyle O_{j}(M)\subset\frac{1}{2}{\bf B}_{j}\equiv{\bf B}_{\frac{\rho_{j}}{2}}(x_{j})\subset I^{n}.

The balls
{\bf B}_{j}
are as in the statement of the theorem; in particular, they are disjoint from each other. Note that the Jacobian of
O_{j}
is given by

(11)

\displaystyle{\bf Jac}(O_{j})=\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{2R}\right)^{n}.

Recall that
\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}
obtained above is an isotopy on
M
for each
j
. Now let us extend it to an ambient isotopy
\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
. Suppose that
M\subset{\bf B}_{R}(0)\subset{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
has an smoothly embedded
\delta
-tubular neighbourhood
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)
, and denote by
\pi
the nearest point projection from
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)
to
M
. Also recall that for each
j
the vectorfield

(12)

\displaystyle V_{j}:=\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\circ\left(h_{j,t}\right)^{-1}\in\Gamma(TM)

is divergence-free on
M
, since
\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}(M)
.

The extension
\{\mathcal{H}_{t}\}_{t\in I}
can be given by defining a divergence-free vectorfield
{\widetilde{V_{j}}}\in\Gamma\left(T\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)\right)
which extends
V_{j}
. For this purpose, choose a cutoff function
\eta\in C^{\infty}\big([0,\infty[,[0,1]\big)
such that

\eta(s)=\begin{cases}1\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ for }0\leq s\leq\frac{\delta}{2},\\
{\rm decreasing}\qquad\text{ for }\frac{\delta}{2}<s<\frac{3\delta}{4},\\
0\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ for }\frac{3\delta}{4}\leq s.\end{cases}

Consider the geodesic normal coordinate on
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)
: a generic point can be written as
x=x_{h}+x_{v}\equiv\left[x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\right]^{\top}+\left[x_{m+1},\ldots,x_{n+m}\right]^{\top}
, where each coordinate component of the vertical component
x_{v}
lies in
[-\delta,\delta]
, and
x_{h}\in M
is the horizontal component of
x
. Then, for any vectorfield
W\in\Gamma\left(T\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)\right)
we have
W=W_{h}+W_{v}
with
W_{h}=\pi_{\#}W={\rm d}\pi(W)
. In the geodesic normal coordinate we may express the Euclidean metric as

\displaystyle g_{\rm E}=\begin{bmatrix}g&0\\
0&I_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, the Euclidean divergence of
\eta W
can be computed in local coordinates via

\displaystyle{\rm div}(\eta W)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g_{\rm E}}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{\det g_{\rm E}}\,\eta W^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\partial_{j}\left(\sqrt{\det g}\,\eta W^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta\,{\rm div}_{g}(W_{h})+\sum_{j=1}^{n}W^{j}\partial_{j}\eta+\sum_{j=n-m+1}^{n}\Big\{\partial_{j}\left(\eta W^{j}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\log\det g\right)\eta W^{j}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n-m+1}^{n}\Big\{\partial_{j}\left(\eta W^{j}\right)+\partial_{j}\left(\log\det g\right)\eta W^{j}\Big\},

thanks to the divergence-free property of
W_{h}
. We adopt the slight abuse of notations:

\displaystyle\eta(x)\equiv\eta\big(|{x_{v}}|_{\infty}\big)=\eta\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\big|x_{m+i}\big|\Big)

Here we may work with the Euclidean metric
g_{\rm E}
since
\iota
is an
isometric
embedding.

The above computations show that, given the horizontal divergence-free vectorfields
V_{j}
as in Eq. (
12
), the vectorfield

\displaystyle{\widetilde{V_{j}}}(x_{h}+x_{v}):=\eta(x_{v})V_{j}(x_{h})\qquad\text{ for each $j=1,2,3,\ldots$}

is divergence-free and compactly supported on the tubular neighbourhood
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)
. Define the ambient isotopy

\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\in{\bf SDiff}({\mathbf{E}}^{n})

via

\displaystyle{\widetilde{V_{j}}}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{j,t}}{\partial t}\circ\left(\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\right)^{-1}\in\Gamma\left(T\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)\right).

Then
\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}
is an extension of
\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}
supported in the tubular neighbourhood
\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M)
.

Step 3.
The total kinetic energy/action of
\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\in{\bf SDiff}({\mathbf{E}}^{n})
can be easily estimated from below. Indeed, using the definition of
{\widetilde{V_{j}}}
and the choice of
\eta
, we have

\displaystyle J\big(\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|{\widetilde{V_{j}}}(t)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(M),\,g_{\rm E})}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{[-\delta,\delta]^{n-m}}\eta^{2}(x_{v})\int_{M}\left|V_{j}(x_{h})\right|_{g}^{2}\sqrt{\det g}\,{\rm d}x_{h}\,{\rm d}x_{v}

(13)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\delta^{n-m}J\big(\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big).

The final inequality holds because the nonnegative function
\eta(x_{v})\equiv 1
for
x_{v}\in\left[-\frac{\delta}{2},\,\frac{\delta}{2}\right]^{n-m}
.

Now let us scale
\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}
via the maps
O_{j}
introduced above; choose
\delta_{j}
as in the statement of the theorem in lieu of
\delta
for the width of the tubular neighbourhood. We thus obtain on each
{\bf B}_{j}
an isotopy
\{{O_{j}}_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}
that is compactly supported in
\mathcal{T}_{\delta_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\bf B}_{j}\right)\subset\frac{3}{4}{\bf B}_{j}
, because
\delta_{j}<\frac{\rho_{j}}{4}
where
\rho_{j}
is the radius of
{\bf B}_{j}
. Since
O_{j}
is a Euclidean homothety modulo rigid motions, we deduce from Eq. (
11
) that

\displaystyle J\Big(\big\{{O_{j}}_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\big\}_{t\in I}\Big)

\displaystyle=\left[{\bf Jac}(O_{j})\right]^{2}J\big(\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big)

(14)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{2R}\right)^{2n}J\big(\{\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big)\qquad\text{for each $j=1,2,3,\ldots$}.

Recall that
\left\{{\bf B}_{j}\right\}
are disjoint open balls in
I^{n}
. We are now at the stage of defining the isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
by glueing the above
{O_{j}}_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{j,t}
:

(15)

\xi_{t}:=\begin{cases}{O_{j}}_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\qquad\text{ on each }{\bf B}_{j},\\
{\bf id}\qquad\qquad\text{on }I^{n}\sim\left(\bigsqcup_{j=1}{\bf B}_{j}\right).\end{cases}

This together with Eqs. (
4
) and (
4
) yields that

\displaystyle J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}J\Big(\big\{{O_{j}}_{\#}\mathcal{H}_{j,t}\big\}_{t\in I}\Big)

(16)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{2R}\right)^{2n}(\delta_{j})^{n-m}J\big(\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big).

Step 4.
In this step, we show that the isotopy
\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}(M,g)
can be chosen to have arbitrarily large total energy. This essentially follows from Eliashberg–Ratiu
[
9
, Appendix A]
.

For this purpose, recall from §
3
:

The group homomorphism sending a smooth isotopy to a representitive of the
(m-1)^{\text{th}}
-cohomology:

(17)

\displaystyle\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}\quad\longmapsto\quad\left[\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right\}\,{\rm d}t\right]\quad\in H^{m-1}(M;\mathbb{R})

is the Poincaré dual of
\widetilde{\theta}\left(\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\right)
.

Here
\widetilde{\theta}
is the lift of the mass flow homomorphism
\theta
,
m=\dim M
, and the square bracket denotes an equivalence class in the cohomology group. Since
\pi_{1}(M)
has trivial centre, we may work with
\theta
and
\widetilde{\theta}
interchangeably, thanks to
[
11
, Proposition 5.1]
(attributed to D. Sullivan).

Now, recall from Eq. (
10
) that the
\theta
-image of
\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\in{\bf SDiff}(M,g)
is
N(j)[\sigma]
, where
[\sigma]
is the nontrivial cycle representative of
H_{1}(M;\mathbb{R})
chosen as before. By the definition of mass flow homomorphism (see Eqs. (
4
) and (
6
)), it holds that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}\left(\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}\right)(\varphi)=\int_{M}\overline{\varphi\circ h_{j,1}-\varphi}\,{\rm d}V_{g}\qquad\text{for each test function $\varphi\in C^{0}\left(M,{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\right)$}.

Here
\overline{\varphi\circ h_{j,1}-\varphi}
is the
t\nearrow 1
limit of the lift of
{\varphi\circ h_{j,t}-\varphi}
from
{\mathbb{T}^{1}}
to the universal cover
\mathbb{R}
with
\overline{\varphi\circ h_{j,0}-\varphi}=0
. Hence, by virtue of the choice of
\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}
, we have

(18)

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\theta}\left(\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}\right)\right\|\geq cN(j),

where
c
is a constant depending only on the choice of
[\sigma]
. We have not yet specified the positive integers
N(j)
.

Now we can relate Eq. (
18
) to the total kinetic energy of
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
defined in Eq. (
15
). This is achieved by way of the identity

(19)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right\|^{2}:=\int_{M}\left(\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right)\wedge\star\left(\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right)=\int_{M}\left|\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\right|^{2}_{g}\,\Omega,

where
\star
is the Hodge star with respect to the metric
g
, and
\Omega
is the normalised Riemannian volume form, namely that
\Omega={\rm d}V_{g}/\penalty{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle J\big(\{h_{j,t}\}_{t\in I}\big)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{M}\left|\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}V_{g}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{{{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\geq\frac{{{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)}}{2}\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\partial h_{j,t}}{\partial t}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Omega\right\|\,{\rm d}t\right\}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{{{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)}}{2}\left\|\widetilde{\theta}\left(\left\{h_{j,t}\right\}_{t\in I}\right)\right\|^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\geq c{{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)}N(j)^{2},

where
c
depends only on
[\sigma]\in H_{1}(M,\mathbb{R})
. In the above, the second line follows from Eq. (
19
), the third line holds by Jensen’s inequality, the fourth line follows from the surrounding remarks about Eq. (
17
), and the final line can be deduced from Eq. (
18
).

Therefore, we now infer from Eqs. (
4
) and (
4
) that

(21)

\displaystyle J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\big)\geq c{{\rm Vol}_{g}(M)}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\rho_{j}}{2R}\right)^{2n}\left({\delta_{j}}\right)^{n-m}N(j)^{2}.

Again,
c
depends only on
[\sigma]
. Thus, by choosing for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
the positive integer
N(j)
sufficiently large that depends only on
\rho_{j}
,
\delta_{j}
,
m=\dim M
, and
n
— for instance,

\displaystyle N(j)=\lceil{j^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\rho_{j}\right)^{-2n}\left(\delta_{j}\right)^{m-n}}\rceil,

we deduce from Eq. (
21
) that

\displaystyle J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\big)=\infty.

Step 5.
It remains to show the existence of another isotopy
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\subset{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
with

\displaystyle\eta_{0}={\bf id},\qquad\eta_{1}=\xi_{1},\qquad\text{ and }J\big(\{\eta_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}\big)<\infty.

This immediately follows from Theorem
1.2
that
\xi_{1}\in{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
is an attainable diffeomorphism. See Definition
1.1
, Remark
1.5
, and Shnirelman
[
17
]
.

The proof is now complete. ∎

5.
Concluding Remarks

In this note, we constructed explicitly a smooth isotopy
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
of volume-preserving and orientation-preserving diffeomorphism on
I^{n}
for
n\geq 3
with infinite total kinetic energy/action. This isotopy has no self-cancellations — in each connected component of its support (a subset of
{\bf B}_{j}
), the vectorfield of
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
never comes to a stop, nor does the “fluid” domain return to any previous configurations at any time instant
t\in[0,1]
. Such
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
is concentrated near homothetic copies of the isometrically embedded image of a topologically complicated manifold-with-boundary
M
, smeared out in thin tubular neighbourhoods, wherein the vectorfields associated to
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
make extremely rapid turns. Explicitly computations in §
4
yield that
J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\big)=\infty
.

On the other hand, as
\xi_{1}
is an attainable diffeomorphism by Shnirelman
[
17
]
, one can always make a “short-cut” by connecting
\xi_{0}={\bf id}
to
\xi_{1}
via a finite-energy path inside
{\bf SDiff}(I^{n})
. The energy-saving mechanism lies in the lift from the isotopy on submanifold
M^{m}
to the ambient isotopy on
I^{n}
. Short-cuts arise from the extra
n-m
dimensions.

In the end, let us take the opportunity to correct a flawed theorem in an earlier version
[
12
]
of this note. In
[
12
, Definition 1.1]
we introduced the following notion. Let
(M^{m},g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold-with-boundary as in the current note. We say that an isometric embedding
\iota:(M,g)\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
has the “rigid isotopy extension property” if and only if for every
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}(M,g)
and for any
\epsilon>0
, the following two conditions hold:

•

There exists
\left\{\hat{\xi}_{t}\right\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}\left({\mathbf{E}}^{{n}}\right)
such that
J\big(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\big)+\epsilon>J\left(\left\{\hat{\xi}_{t}\right\}_{t\in I}\right)
and

\hat{\xi}_{t}\circ\iota=\iota\circ\xi_{t}
for all
t\in I
.

•

For any
\left\{\check{\xi}_{t}\right\}_{t\in I}\subset{\bf SDiff}\left({\mathbf{E}}^{{n}}\right)
such that

\check{\xi}_{t}\circ\iota=\iota\circ\xi_{t}
for all
t\in I
, one must have
J\left(\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}\right)<J\left(\left\{\check{\xi}_{t}\right\}_{t\in I}\right)+\epsilon
.

Then we claimed in
[
12
, Theorem 1.4]
a dynamical/topological obstruction on
M
for the
existence
of such an isometric embedding
\iota
:

Suppose that
(M,g)
has nontrivial first real homology and its fundamental group has trivial centre. Then any of its isometric embeddings into the Euclidean space
{\mathbf{E}}^{n}
with
n\geq 3
cannot have the rigid isotopy extension property.

This theorem, albeit true, is vacuously true: the rigid isotopy extension property defined above can
never
hold! The reason is already manifested in the proof of main Theorem
1.3
— we can always choose an ambient isotopy, which costs arbitrarily small extra kinetic energy than
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t\in I}
, by repeating the construction in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
1.3
with very thin tubular neighbourhoods. We are deeply indebted to an anonymous referee of
[
12
]
for pointing out this issue.
