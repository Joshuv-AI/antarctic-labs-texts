Localized Distributional Robustness in Submodular Multi-Task Subset Selection
Ege C. Kaya, Abolfazl Hashemi

Abstract
In this work, we approach the problem of multi-task submodular optimization with the perspective of local distributional robustness, within the neighborhood of a reference distribution which assigns an importance score to each task. We initially propose to introduce a regularization term which makes use of the relative entropy to the standard multi-task objective. We then demonstrate through duality that this novel formulation itself is equivalent to the maximization of a monotone increasing function composed with a submodular function, which may be efficiently carried out through standard greedy selection methods. This approach bridges the existing gap in the optimization of performance-robustness trade-offs in multi-task subset selection. To numerically validate our theoretical results, we test the proposed method in two different settings, one on the selection of satellites in low Earth orbit constellations in the context of a sensor selection problem involving weak-submodular functions, and the other on an image summarization task using neural networks involving submodular functions. Our method is compared with two other algorithms focused on optimizing the performance of the worst-case task, and on directly optimizing the performance on the reference distribution itself. We conclude that our novel formulation produces a solution that is locally distributional robust, and computationally inexpensive. Keywords: Optimization, robustness, submodularity.

[

. In simplest terms, a submodular function is a set function that possesses the
diminishing marginal gains
property
[
23

. This property, at first glance, enables one to draw an analogy between submodular functions and concave functions in the continuous domain. However, interestingly, a similar analogy may be drawn between submodular functions and
convex

[
24

. The similarity of submodular functions to both convex and concave functions, the white whales of optimization literature, has also garnered interest in the study of the optimization of submodular functions.

The quintessential submodular optimization problem is that of the maximization of a submodular function under a cardinality constraint. This problem entails selecting a subset out of a ground set
N
of elements, such that the size of the selection does not exceed a preset constraint value, and the evaluation of the selection under the submodular function is as high as possible. The evaluation
f(S)
of a subset
S\subseteq N
by
f
is commonly called the
score
or
utility of the set

S
. The following formulation, where,
f
is a submodular function,
N
is the ground set and
K
is a positive integer representing the cardinality constraint, expresses this problem in mathematical terms
[
6
]
:

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}f(S)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(1)

The notion of
robustness
occurs very frequently in optimization literature and is the object of much consideration. Although there are differing definitions of robustness depending on the context at hand, such as being immune to removals from the solution
[
25
]
, or to slight changes in parameters or input data
[
26
]
, and others
[
27
]
, it always encompasses the general idea of a produced solution to a problem (the output) staying correct and valid under perturbations to the input. In the present context of submodular optimization, one conception of robustness that is commonly encountered is
multi-task robustness
. As the name implies, with this conception, the aim is to algorithmically produce a solution set whose utility with respect to multiple submodular functions
f^{1},\ldots,f^{n}
is satisfactory.

As it stands, the notion of being “satisfactory” is intentionally left to be vague, and the problem can be formulated in multiple ways to fit the description. A straightforward interpretation with this goal in mind might lead to what we will call the
worst-case formulation

[
5
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
, which aims to focus on producing a solution that maximizes the worst-performing objective function among all. Using the shorthand notation
[n]\vcentcolon=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, this formulation is as follows:

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(2)

A shortcoming of this formulation is the fact that it can be viewed as being
too pessimistic
[
31
]
, dedicating all resources to the maximization of the worst-performing objective function, at the expense of disregarding the others. In scenarios where one objective function is a clear outlier, as in it always scores significantly lower than all others, this approach may effectively lead to getting low utility on all functions in pursuit of the hopeless aim of maximizing that outlier.

Another way to formulate the multi-task robust problem could be to adopt what we will call the
average-case formulation

[
31
,
32
]
, which aims to directly optimize the arithmetic average of all the objective functions. In this case, the formulation would be

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(3)

The apparent shortcoming of this formulation is the fact that contrary to the previous formulation, it provides no guarantees whatsoever about how low the utility with respect to any individual objective function may be. In essence, one or multiple objective functions could be scoring arbitrarily badly, as long as the other objective functions are scoring well enough to compensate for the underperformers.

The previous two formulations, even with their stated shortcomings, may very well be acceptable approaches, especially in the absence of any additional information. However, we argue that if we possess
additional information
on the nature of the objective functions, or a system at hand, for whose various tasks we use the multiple objective functions to model, we can progress to a more meaningful formulation. Suppose, for instance, that we have access to a
reference distribution
, an
n-
dimensional discrete probability distribution
Q\in\Delta_{n}
. In a practical setting, the reference distribution
Q
may be obtained by a decision-maker subjectively assigning importance scores to each objective function, or by a frequentist approach, where the value assigned to each function would be given by how frequently the task that is modeled by said function is performed by the system. It is reasonable to argue that the reference distribution can facilitate incorporating the intent and priority of the main stakeholders in the problem. The incorporation of this additional information to the previous formulation leads to a generalization of Problem (
3
), where the reference distribution is simply assumed to be uniform. This idea is encapsulated in the following formulation:

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(4)

We note that this previous formulation is a generalization of Problem (
2
), such that, when
Q
is a one-hot discrete distribution indicating the worst-performing objective function, this problem becomes equivalent to Problem (
2
).

3-

1.1
Contribution

Based on this previous formulation, we present our novel robust formulation, which not only makes use of the reference distribution to simply weigh each objective function but to act as the center of a
neighborhood of robustness
, within which we will aim to be robust. The initial candidate formulation for this is as follows:

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{P\in\Delta_{n}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K,\;D(P\;\|\;Q)\leq R,\end{split}

(5)

where the constraint
D(P\;\|\;Q)\leq R
designates the requirement that we localize ourselves to a specified neighborhood of robustness, with radius
R
, only within which we will maximize our objectives, weighed by the worst possible distribution.
D(P\;\|\;Q)
is intentionally unspecified and could be any metric or divergence. For an investigation of different statistical distances for
D(P\;\|\;Q)
, we refer the reader to Section
3
.

Before we proceed with the theoretical analysis of Problem
5
, we propose one last change to the formulation to make it more tractable. We relax the formulation by introducing the constraint
D(P\;\|\;Q)\leq R
into the objective via a penalizing regularization constant
\lambda>0
, instead of strictly enforcing it. This change reflects the standard idea of introducing Lagrange multipliers, utilized frequently in continuous optimization
[
34
]
. With this final addition, our novel formulation becomes

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{P\in\Delta_{n}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D(P\;\|\;Q)\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(6)

We propose, practically, that this final formulation will be useful in many kinds of multi-objective applications, where we aim to maintain a performance threshold in all tasks involved, especially if we are in the knowledge of a reference distribution indicating the relative importance scores of each task. In particular, we will be undertaking two such applications in Section
6
. The first will involve a low-earth orbit satellite constellation performing multiple tasks involving atmospheric readings and ground coverage, where in critical missions it may be of importance to have satisfactory performance in all objectives.
The second application involves a task of image summarization, where each objective will represent the cosine distance of each image in a dataset to the current selection made from the dataset, intended to be its summary. It is clear that in such a task, one would expect a certain level of performance to be met with respect to all of the images, so that the resulting summarization may be considered a good one.

Related Works and Significance

Robust submodular optimization.

Reference
[
19
]
is a fundamental work in establishing the notion of robustness in submodular optimization that is adopted in the current manuscript, and proposing an algorithmic solution to the worst-case formulation of Problem (

Submodular Saturation Algorithm (SSA)

References

]

The latter shifts the focus to achieving higher computational efficiency, aiming for a fast and practical algorithm with asymptotic approximation guarantees when
n
, the number of objective functions is increased arbitrarily.
Reference
[
31
]
proposes a novel notion of robustness that yet pertains to the context of multiple objective functions. The authors firstly motivate their novel approach by drawing attention to the pessimistic nature of Problem (
2
). They instead propose an approach that entails the maximization of the
p
th
quantile of the objective functions, where
0\leq p<1
. To simplify, this approach may be viewed as forgoing the consideration of the worst-performing
p
th
of the objective functions in favor of maximizing the remaining. Different from all of the previously mentioned works, the present work shifts the focus to the scope of
distributional robustness
, focusing on localizing our goal of robustness within a subset of the
n-
dimensional simplex, in a neighborhood of a reference distribution.
References
[
25
,
36
]
adopt one of the aforementioned different notions of robustness within the context of submodular optimization. In these works, the notion of robustness is against the post-processing of solutions for the arbitrary or adversarial deletion of a certain number of elements, rather than robustness against multiple submodular objective functions.

Distributionally-robust optimization.
Our work is closely related to and motivated by the distributionally robust optimization paradigm. In a very general sense, distributionally robust optimization is a paradigm where some uncertainty about the nature of the problem is governed by a probability distribution and the goal is to leverage the information we have about this governing distribution with the goal of optimization or optimal decision-making. Reference
[
37
]
approaches a convex optimization problem in the context of distributionally robust optimization. Reference
[
38
]
broadens the scope of consideration of online optimization of nonconvex functions. In particular, the authors use relative entropy regularization, which we also employ in this work. Reference
[
32
]
, much like our work, deals with distributionally-robust optimization of submodular functions. The authors here work in the presence of stochastic submodular objective functions that are drawn from a given reference distribution and aim to maximize the expected value of such functions. They propose an approach that entails a variance-regularized objective, making use of the multilinear extension of submodular functions and the Momentum Frank-Wolfe algorithm. In contrast to this line of work, we leverage distributionally robust optimization for discrete multi-task subset selection and demonstrate the possibility of formulating the distributionally robust problem as a simple submodular maximization problem, essentially allowing us to produce a distributionally robust solution at no additional cost to produce a solution to the naive formulation of Problem (
4
).

2
Preliminaries and Background

We begin by introducing the concept of the
marginal gain
in set functions because it is key in defining submodular functions.

Definition 1

(Marginal gain)
.

Given a set function
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
A,B\subseteq N
, we denote
f(B\cup A)-f(B)
, the marginal gain in
f
due to adding
A
to
B
, by
\Delta_{f}(A|B)
.

When the set
A
is a singleton, i.e.,
A=\{a\}
, we drop the curly brackets to adopt the short-hand notation
\Delta_{f}(a|B)
.

Now, we introduce the notion of submodularity using this definition.

Definition 2

(Submodularity)
.

A set function
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is submodular if for every
A\subseteq B\subseteq N
and
e\in N\setminus B,
it holds that

\Delta_{f}(e|A)\geq\Delta_{f}(e|B).

(7)

This definition of submodularity highlights the renowned
diminishing marginal gains
property of submodular functions, which is commonly referred to when making the analogy to discrete concave functions.
The common notion in thinking about a submodular function
f
is that it scores each set by its utility, as such, we will commonly refer to the evaluation
f(S)
as the
utility
,
performance
, or
score
of set
S
.

We further introduce two additional useful properties of set functions, that are combined with submodularity in the derivation of theoretical guarantees for approximation algorithms that optimize submodular functions.

Definition 3

(Normalized set functions)
.

A set function
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is normalized if
f(\emptyset)=0
.

Definition 4

(Monotone nondecreasing set functions)
.

A set function
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is monotone nondecreasing if for every
A\subseteq B\subseteq N,
we have
f(A)\leq f(B).

Note that a set function
f
having both of the last two properties ensures that
f\geq 0
.

In practical applications, it is often the case that the objective functions involved are not submodular, but
weak-submodular

[
39
]
. In literature, there are multiple notions of weak submodularity, but for the purposes of this paper, we will be adopting one that is well-established
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
39
,
43
]
, and well extends the key concept of submodular functions, the concept of diminishing marginal returns. To facilitate the definition however, we first define the
weak-submodularity constant (WSC):

Definition 5

(Weak-submodularity constant (WSC)
[
39
]
)
.

The WSC of a monotone nondecreasing set function
f
is given by

w_{f}\vcentcolon=\max_{(S,T,e)\in\tilde{N}}\dfrac{\Delta_{f}(e|T)}{\Delta_{f}(e|S)},

(8)

where
\tilde{N}=\{(S,T,e)|S\subseteq T\subset N,e\in N\setminus T\}.

An intuitive understanding of the WSC is that it is a measure of how badly the diminishing marginal gains property required for submodularity is violated. Evidently, a monotone nondecreasing set function
f
is submodular if and only if its WSC satisfies
w_{f}\leq 1
.

With this definition in hand, we now define weak submodularity.

Definition 6

(Weak submodularity)
.

A set function
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is weak-submodular if it is monotone nondecreasing and its WSC satisfies
w_{f}<\infty
.

Possibly the most fundamental result in submodular optimization concerns the maximization of normalized, monotone nondecreasing submodular functions under a cardinality constraint. In this case, the iterative
Greedy
algorithm, which simply consists of going over the entire set of remaining elements at each iteration and adding the one with the highest marginal gain to the solution, obtains an approximation ratio of
1-1/e
. Although simple in principle,
Greedy
is demonstrably the optimal approximation algorithm, unless
\text{P}=\text{NP}
.
[
23
]

While
Greedy
is demonstrably optimal and very easy to implement, its standard form is usually avoided due to the computational cost of reevaluating every remaining element at each iteration that it incurs. Several methods have been proposed to circumvent this shortcoming, two significant ones being
Lazy Greedy
[
44
]
, and
Stochastic Greedy
[
45
]
. In particular,
Stochastic Greedy
achieves a reduced computational cost by reducing its evaluations to a subset of the remaining elements sampled uniformly at random at each iteration, instead of reevaluating every element.
Stochastic Greedy
also enjoys, on expectation, an approximation ratio similar to that of
Greedy
, with an additional term that designates the dependence on the cardinality of the randomly sampled subset.

Theorem 1

(
Stochastic Greedy
approximation ratio)
.

[
45
]

Let
f\vcentcolon 2^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a normalized, monotone nondecreasing submodular function. Let
R=(\lvert N\rvert/K)\log(1/\epsilon)
be the size of the sampled set at each iteration of
Stochastic Greedy
used in the solution of Problem (
1
), where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, on expectation,
Stochastic Greedy
achieves an approximation ratio of
1-1/e-\epsilon
, i.e.,
Stochastic Greedy
produces a solution
\hat{S}
which satisfies

\mathbb{E}[f(\hat{S})]\geq(1-1/e-\epsilon)f(S^{\ast}),

(9)

where
S^{\ast}
is a maximizer of Problem (
1
).

Investigation of Various Statistical Distances for Regularization

In the formulation of Problem (
6
), we have intentionally left unspecified the statistical distance
D(P\;\|\;Q)
to be used in the regularization term, in order to localize our region of interest to a neighborhood around the reference distribution
Q
. In this section, we will be specifically interested in investigating which choices of
D(\cdot\;\|\;\cdot)
lead to favorable algorithmic solutions and theoretical guarantees for Problem (
6
). In particular, of specific interest will be such
D(\cdot\;\|\;\cdot)
that preserve the submodular nature of the problem. For, ensuring that the problem remains submodular allows us to produce a locally robust solution essentially at no additional cost with respect to the non-robust formulation of Problem (
1
), by falling back to our familiar tools such as
Stochastic Greedy
, and enjoying theoretical guarantees such as that presented in Theorem
1
.

We first shift our attention to metrics induced by
\ell^{p}
norms. One choice for
D(P\;\|\;Q)
that leads to a favorable algorithmic solution is the metric induced by the
\ell^{\infty}
norm, i.e.,
D(P\;\|\;Q)=\lVert P-Q\rVert_{\infty}\vcentcolon=\max_{i\in[n]}\lvert P_{i}-Q_{i}\rvert.
With this selection, Problem (
6
) becomes

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{P\in\Delta_{n}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda\lVert P-Q\rVert_{\infty}\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(10)

In this case, we may exploit the reduction of the inner problem of (
10
) to a linear program to derive a result. Indeed, since we know that the optimal point of a linear program will occur at one of the vertices of the feasible region
[
46
]
, we have the following equivalence:

\begin{split}\min_{P\in\Delta_{n}}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda\lVert P-Q\rVert_{\infty}\equiv\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S)-\lambda Q_{i}.\end{split}

(11)

Hence, Problem (
10
) reduces, in whole, to

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S)-\lambda Q_{i}\\
&\text{s. t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(12)

The main observation about Problem (
12
) is that it is strikingly similar to the worst-case formulation of Problem (
2
), however, in addition, the information due to the reference distribution
Q
still holds influence. We propose to use SSA
[
19
]
with a slight modification enabling the incorporation of the reference distribution
Q
for the solution of Problem (
12
). The full algorithm, which we name
Saturate with Preference
, is presented in Algorithm
1
. It has a function evaluation complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\lvert N\rvert^{2}n\log(n\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(N))
, identical to the standard SSA
[
19
]
.

It is worth noting that the choice of the metric induced by the
\ell^{1}
norm, i.e.,
D(P\;\|\;Q)=\lVert P-Q\rVert_{1}\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert P_{i}-Q_{i}\rvert
, through the same reduction of the inner problem to a linear program, and the same equivalence of (
11
), results in the same formulation of Problem (
12
). Since
P
and

are discrete probability distributions, one may also view the quantity
\lVert P-Q\rVert_{1}
as a scaled version of the total variation distance, an example of an

divergence, which motivates the discussion of the next section, in using one

or

4
Relative Entropy Regularization

The choice of relative entropy for the regularizing function, i.e.,
D(P\;\|\;Q)=D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)
, where

D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}\log\left(\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}\right),

(13)

leads to the most significant theoretical outcomes, as will be seen in the following analysis. With this choice, the novel formulation is as follows:

\begin{split}&\max_{S}\min_{P}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)\\
&\text{s. t. }S\subseteq N,|S|\leq K\text{, and }P\in\Delta_{n}.\end{split}

(14)

We start our analysis of the novel formulation by considering the dual formulation of the inner minimization problem in (
14
), by introducing a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
P\in\Delta_{n}
in Problem (
14
). We first note that this constraint is equivalent to the two constraints
(i)

P_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
and
(ii)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}=1.
Introducing a Lagrange multiplier only for constraint
(ii)
, we write the Lagrangian of the inner problem as

\mathcal{L}\left(P,S,\mu\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)+\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}-\mu.

(15)

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}(P,S,\mu)
over
P
will yield the dual formulation of the inner problem of (
14
). We solve

\begin{split}&\min_{P}\mathcal{L}\left(P,S,\mu\right)\\
&\text{s. t. }P_{i}\geq 0\text{ for all }i\in[n].\end{split}

(16)

Leveraging the convexity of the problem in
P
, we simply look at the partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to each
P_{i},
which is given by

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial P_{i}}=f^{i}(S)+\lambda\log\left(\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}\right)+\lambda+\mu.

(17)

Setting this quantity equal to
0
, we obtain the minimizer
P_{i}^{*}
:

P_{i}^{*}=Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}.

(18)

Using this, along with the fact that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{*}=1,
we get

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{*}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda}\right)}=1.\end{split}

(19)

Then,

\lambda+\mu=\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right).

(20)

Now, evaluating
\mathcal{L}
at the minimizer
P^{*}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(P^{*},S,\mu)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{*}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P^{*}\;\|\;Q)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}f^{i}(S)\\
&+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\cdot\left[-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right]\\
&=-\left(\lambda+\mu\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}.\\
\end{split}

(21)

Since we have

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(-\frac{\lambda+\mu+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}^{*}=1,\end{split}

(22)

(
21
) becomes

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(P^{*},S,\mu)=-\left(\lambda+\mu\right).\end{split}

(23)

Finally, from (
20
), we obtain:

\begin{split}G(S)\vcentcolon=\mathcal{L}(P^{*},S,\mu)=-\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right).\end{split}

(24)

Hence, the dual formulation of Problem (
14
) becomes that of the maximization of another set function
G
:

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}G(S)\\
&\text{s.t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(25)

A simple analysis of the extreme cases of values for the regularization parameter
\lambda
is revealing of the equivalence of the two problems. We evaluate the limit

\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda\to x}-\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right)=\lim_{\lambda\to x}-\frac{\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right)}{\frac{1}{\lambda}}\end{split}

(26)

This limit is equivalent to

\lim_{\lambda\to x}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)f^{i}(S)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}}.

(27)

Letting
x\to\infty
, we have:

\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)f^{i}(S)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}f^{i}(S).\end{split}

(28)

This is indeed the expected behavior, since letting
\lambda\to\infty
in Problem (
14
) effectively assigns all importance to the regularization term, and necessarily forces one to have
P^{\ast}=Q
, making the objective

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s.t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K,\end{split}

(29)

which is recovered exactly in (
28
). Note further that
P^{\ast}=Q
is attained only at the limit, for, combining (
18
) and (
20
), we have an analytical expression for
P^{\ast}
, namely,

\begin{split}&P_{i}^{\ast}=Q_{i}\cdot\exp\left(-\dfrac{\lambda\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right).\end{split}

(30)

This means that for any finite value of
\lambda
, for
P^{\ast}=Q
, we need, for all
i\in[n]
,

\exp\left(-\dfrac{\lambda\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)=1,

(31)

which, in turn, means,

\lambda\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)=-f^{i}(S),\forall i\in[n],

(32)

and this last condition implies that
f^{i}(S)=f^{j}(S)
, for any two
i,j\in[n]
, i.e., for any finite value of
\lambda
, the values of all objectives must have the same value at
S
for
P^{\ast}=Q
. Outside of this very specific circumstance, at no finite value of
\lambda
does
P^{\ast}=Q
hold.

On the other hand, letting
x=0
, the value of the limit is determined by the most dominant term in the sums in the numerator and the denominator, that is,

\begin{split}&\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)f^{i}(S)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}}=\min_{i\in[n]}\frac{Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)f^{i}(S)}}{Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}}=\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S).\end{split}

(33)

Hence, the problem becomes

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S)\\
&\text{s.t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K.\end{split}

(34)

Again, letting
x=0
in Problem (
14
) removes the regularization term, and reduces the inner minimization problem to a linear program, whose solution will have
P_{i}=1
for
i=\arg\!\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(S)
, and
P_{j}=0
for all
j\neq i
, exactly recovering (
34
). Note that this is also exactly the worst-case formulation of Problem (
2
), demonstrating that our novel formulation is a generalization of the worst-case formulation.

The main question that arises directly from this novel formulation of Problem (
25
) is whether the set function
G
retains the properties of being normalized, monotone nondecreasing, and submodular since the presence of all three of these properties would reduce the solution of Problem (
25
) to the use of standardized methods, such as
Stochastic Greedy
. Fortunately, we are not far from this ideal case, as demonstrated in Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2
.

The set function
G(S)
may be expressed as the composition of two functions,
G(S)=g(h(S))
, where the set function

h(S)\vcentcolon=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)

(35)

is

(i)

normalized,

(ii)

monotone nondecreasing, and

(iii)

submodular,

and the function
g(x)\vcentcolon=-\lambda\log(1-x)
is

(iv)

monotone increasing,

(v)

convex, and

(vi)

Lipschitz continuous.

in
\operatorname{dom}g=[0,h(S^{\ast})]
.

Proof.

(i)

We have

\begin{split}h(\emptyset)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp{\left(-\frac{f^{i}(\emptyset)}{\lambda}\right)}\right)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp{\left(0\right)}\right)\\
&=0.\end{split}

(36)

(ii)

Let
S\subseteq T\subseteq N
. Then, for each
i\in[n],
we have
f^{i}(S)\leq f^{i}(T).
Hence,
-f^{i}(S)/\lambda\geq-f^{i}(T)/\lambda,
and
\exp(-f^{i}(S)/\lambda)\geq\exp(-f^{i}(T)/\lambda),
thanks to the monotone increasing property of the
\exp
function and the positivity of
\lambda
. This, in turn, means that

1-\exp\left(-\frac{f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\leq 1-\exp\left(-\frac{f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}\right),

(37)

and because
Q_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n],

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}\right)\right),\end{split}

(38)

that is,
h(S)\leq h(T)
.

(iii)

Let
S\subseteq T\subset N
,
e\in N\setminus T
. For all
i\in[n]
, we have:

\dfrac{f^{i}(S\cup\{e\})-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\geq\dfrac{f^{i}(T\cup\{e\})-f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}.

(39)

Now, because
x\mapsto 1-\exp(-x)
is a monotone increasing function, we have

\begin{split}1-\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(S\cup\{e\})+f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\geq 1-\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(T\cup\{e\})+f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}\right).\end{split}

(40)

Furthermore, because each
f^{i}
is monotone nondecreasing,
f^{i}(S)\leq f^{i}(T)
, and since
x\mapsto\exp(-x/\lambda)
is a monotone decreasing function for
\lambda>0
, we have

\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}\right).

(41)

Furthermore, both of these quantities are nonnegative. Then, multiplying the left-hand side of (
40
) by
\exp(-f^{i}(S)/\lambda)
and the right-hand side by
\exp(-f^{i}(T)/\lambda)
preserves the inequality, and we obtain

\begin{split}\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)-\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right)\\
\geq\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(T)}{\lambda}\right)-\exp\left(\frac{-f^{i}(T\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right).\end{split}

(42)

This demonstrates that the set function
S\mapsto-\exp\left(-f^{i}(S)/\lambda\right)
is submodular. The addition of the constant
1
, multiplication with the nonnegative
Q_{i}
and the summation through the indices
i\in[n]
preserve the submodularity, hence,

h(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\left(1-\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)\right)

(43)

is submodular.

(iv)

It suffices to note that the first-order derivative of
g
,

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}g}{\mathrm{d}x}=\dfrac{\lambda}{1-x}>0,

(44)

for all
\lambda>0
and
x\in[0,h(S^{\ast})]
. Furthermore, it is of note that
\frac{\mathrm{d}g}{\mathrm{d}x}
attains its maximum value at
x=h(S^{\ast})
.

(v)

The second-order derivative of g,

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}g}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}=\dfrac{\lambda}{(1-x)^{2}}>0,

(45)

for all
\lambda>0
and
x\in[0,h(S^{\ast})]
, hence
g
is a convex function.

(vi)

As per
(v)
, the first-order derivative of
g
is a monotone increasing function over all of its domain, which attains its maximum value at
x=h(S^{\ast})
. Hence,

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}g}{\mathrm{d}x}\leq\dfrac{\lambda}{1-h(S^{\ast})},

(46)

making
g
Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
\lambda/(1-h(S^{\ast}))
.

∎

This theorem proves that standard methods such as
Stochastic Greedy
(presented in Algorithm

), with well-established theoretical guarantees such as that of Theorem

are well-suited for the solution of Problem (
25
) (and hence for its equivalent, Problem
14
). This is due to the fact that
g
is a monotone increasing function, and that the maximizer of
g
over its domain is
h(S^{\ast})
, as observed in the proof of the previous Theorem. Hence, any method approximating
h(S^{\ast})
may be employed in maximizing
G
as well. Regarding the approximation guarantee provided by
Stochastic Greedy
for the function
G(S)=g(h(S))
, we state the following Theorem.

Theorem 3
.

The set
\hat{S}
constructed by

Stochastic Greedy
achieves, on the function
G
,

\mathbb{E}[G(\hat{S})]\geq G(S^{\ast})-(1/e+\epsilon)\dfrac{h(S^{\ast})}{1-h(S^{\ast})}.

(47)

Proof.

We have, by Theorem
1
,

\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{S})]\geq(1-1/e-\epsilon)h(S^{\ast}).

(48)

This implies

\begin{split}h(S^{\ast})-\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{S})]&\leq h(S^{\ast})-(1-1/e-\epsilon)h(S^{\ast})=(1/e+\epsilon)h(S^{\ast}).\end{split}

(49)

Now, by the Lipschitz continuity of
g
with Lipschitz constant
\lambda/(1-h(S^{\ast}))
(as per Theorem

,
(vi)
), we have

g(h(S^{\ast}))-g(\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{S})])\leq(h(S^{\ast})-\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{S})])\dfrac{\lambda}{1-h(S^{\ast})}.

(50)

Also, because
g
is a convex function (as per Theorem

,
(v)
), by Jensen’s inequality,

g\left(h(S^{\ast})\right)-\mathbb{E}[g(h(\hat{S}))]\leq g(h(S^{\ast}))-g(\mathbb{E}[h(\hat{S})]).

(51)

Then, combining (
50
) and (
51
), and rearranging, we have

\mathbb{E}[G(\hat{S})]\geq G(S^{\ast})-(1/e+\epsilon)\dfrac{h(S^{\ast})}{1-h(S^{\ast})}.

(52)

∎

This, in turn, guarantees the production of a locally distributionally robust solution that is produced at no additional function evaluation cost with respect to the solution of the naive formulation of Problem (
4
). Our novel formulation greatly relaxes the SSA and enablexs the use of much less computationally expensive methods for the solution. Namely,
Stochastic Greedy
has a function evaluation cost of
\mathcal{O}(K\cdot\lvert R\rvert),
compared to SSA’s
\mathcal{O}(\lvert N\rvert^{2}n\log(n\min_{i\in[n]}f^{i}(N)).

Furthermore, this result extends gracefully to the case where the component objective functions
f^{i}
for
i\in[n]
are not submodular, but weak-submodular. Indeed, in this case, akin to the result with submodular functions, the newly-defined function
g
in (
25
) turns out to be weak-submodular itself. The next Lemma demonstrates this result.

Lemma 1
.

G(S)=-\lambda\log(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda})})
is weak-submodular when the component functions
f^{i}
for
i\in[n]
are weak-submodular.

Proof.

As per Definition
5
, we have that

\begin{split}w_{G}&=\max_{(S,T,e)\in\tilde{N}}\dfrac{\Delta_{G}(e|T)}{\Delta_{G}(e|S)}\\
&=\max_{(S,T,e)\in\tilde{N}}\dfrac{\log\left(\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(T)}{\lambda}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(T\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right)}\right)}{\log\left(\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right)}\right)}.\end{split}

(53)

Clearly, this value can only be unbounded if the denominator is zero, and this only occurs when

\dfrac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right)}=1,

(54)

that is, when

\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right).

(55)

Rearranging the terms, this is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}\underbrace{\left(\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S)}{\lambda}\right)-\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{j}(S\cup\{e\})}{\lambda}\right)\right)}_{A_{j}}=0.

(56)

Note that because each
f^{j}
is monotone nondecreasing,
A_{j}
in the sum is nonnegative for all
j
. Now, because
\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}=1
, there exists at least one
j
such that
Q_{j}>0
. Let
j_{0}
be one such
j
. We know that
A_{j_{0}}
is either positive or zero. If it is the case that
A_{j_{0}}
is positive, then there is at least one positive term in the sum (amongst other possibly positive, or zero terms), and hence the sum cannot be equal to zero. If, on the other hand,
A_{j_{0}}
is zero, then we must have that

f^{j_{0}}(S)=f^{j_{0}}(S\cup\{e\}).

(57)

Yet, if this were the case, the WSC of
f^{j_{0}}
would have been:

\begin{split}w_{f^{j_{0}}}&=\max_{(S,T,e)\in\tilde{N}}\dfrac{\Delta_{f^{j_{0}}}(e|T)}{\Delta_{f^{j_{0}}}(e|S)}\\
&=\dfrac{\Delta_{f^{j_{0}}}(e|T)}{f^{j_{0}}(S\cup\{e\})-f^{j_{0}}(S)}\\
&=\dfrac{\Delta_{f^{j_{0}}}(e|T)}{0}=\infty,\end{split}

(58)

regardless of the choice of
T
in the numerator. However, by the initial supposition that
f^{j}
is weak-submodular for each
j\in[n]
, we know that
f^{j_{0}}<\infty
, and hence
A_{j_{0}}
cannot be zero. Then, (
56
) cannot hold, and thus
w_{G}<\infty
, establishing that
G
is a weak-submodular function.
∎

This last result makes available to us theoretical guarantees presented in literature that demonstrate the efficacy of
Stochastic Greedy
on weak-submodular functions
[
39
,
43
]
.

With all of these results settled, the only point that remains to be examined is whether solving the regularized, “soft-constrained” Problem (
6
), with which the entirety of this work has been interested, translates to solving the “hard-constrained” original Problem (
5
). We first note, as we previously have, the similarity of the formulation of Problem (
6
) to the Lagrangian of the inner minimization objective in Problem (
5
). Indeed, if one were to write the Lagrangian of the latter, one would have

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(P,S,\lambda)=\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)-\lambda R.

(59)

It is clear to see that the main difference between this Lagrangian and Problem (
6
) is the inclusion of the term
-\lambda R
. Now, in conventional fashion, in finding the dual of this problem, one would first minimize the Lagrangian with respect to the primal variable
P
, in terms of the dual variable
\lambda
, and obtain

\tilde{G}(S,\lambda)\vcentcolon=\sum^{n}_{i=1}P^{\ast}_{i}(\lambda)f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P^{\ast}(\lambda)\;\|\;Q)-\lambda R.

(60)

Fortunately, the derivation starting in (
17
) remains valid, and so does the expression for
P^{\ast}
, given in (
30
), even with the addition of the
-\lambda R
term. Then, continuing in the conventional fashion, one would maximize
\tilde{G}(S)
with respect to
\lambda
to obtain
\lambda^{\ast}
, which would, in turn, be used in the expression for
P^{\ast}
, to find the optimal value for the primal variable,
P^{\ast}(\lambda^{\ast})
, this time free of the variable
\lambda
.

Let us for a moment adopt this approach in solving the inner minimization objective in Problem (
6
). In maximizing

\begin{split}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P^{\ast}_{i}(\lambda)f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P^{\ast}(\lambda)\;\|\;Q)=-\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right)\end{split}

(61)

with respect to
\lambda
, we swiftly realize that for
\lambda\geq 0
, the function is monotone increasing over all of its domain, and hence the maximizing
\lambda^{\ast}
is unbounded. This, in turn, as the analysis in (
28
) shows, forces
P^{\ast}=Q
.

This is precisely due to the missing
-\lambda R
term. The inclusion of this term, in the “true Lagrangian”
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
given in (
59
), is the key to having a finite optimal
\lambda^{\ast}
, and hence having
P^{\ast}\neq Q
. The inclusion of this term makes both
\lambda^{\ast}
and
P^{\ast}
dependent on
R
, the radius of the neighborhood of robustness:

\lambda^{\ast}=\arg\!\max_{\lambda\geq 0}-\lambda\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{\left(\frac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda}\right)}\right)-\lambda R.

(62)

Because (
62
) is a concave maximization problem, taking a derivative of the objective function with respect to
\lambda
and setting it equal to
0
, we obtain the relation between
\lambda^{\ast}
and
R
:

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda^{\ast}}\right)f^{i}(S)}{\lambda^{\ast}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda^{\ast}}\right)}+\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp\left(\dfrac{-f^{i}(S)}{\lambda^{\ast}}\right)\right)+R=0.\end{split}

(63)

With an appeal to the Implicit Function Theorem, we can say that within a neighborhood of any
(\lambda^{\ast}_{0},R_{0})
pair that satisfies this relation, one can express
R
as a one-to-one (hence invertible) function of
\lambda^{\ast}
, say,
R=r(\lambda^{\ast})
.

The conclusion to these observations is the following: We claim that setting the regularization hyperparameter
\lambda
to a value, say to
\lambda_{0}
, in the formulation of Problem (
6
) is akin to setting the
R
in Problem (
5
) such that
R=r(\lambda_{0})
. In other words, one can envision changing the value of the regularization parameter
\lambda
in the “soft-constrained” problem as equivalent to changing the value of the radius of the neighborhood of robustness in the “hard-constrained” problem. This is further supported by the fact that the
\lambda^{\ast}
values obtained through the relation (
63
) as
R\to 0^{+}
approach
\infty
, and those obtained as
R\to\infty
approach
0
.

5
Application to Online Submodular Optimization

A natural application of the proposed scheme arises within the context of
online
submodular optimization, in the presence of time-varying objective functions. In this setting, we aim to produce solutions to a sequence of problems

\begin{split}&\max_{S_{t}}f_{t}(S_{t})\\
&\text{s.t. }S_{t}\subseteq N,\lvert S_{t}\rvert\leq K,\;\forall t\in\mathbb{N},\end{split}

(64)

where each
f_{t}
is submodular, monotone nondecreasing, and normalized.

It is easy to approach this problem with the standard methods that we possess. One could treat the problem at each time step
t
as a separate, standalone problem, and use any variant of the
Greedy
methods to produce a solution
S_{t},
entirely decoupled from the problems arising at different time steps.

However, in practical settings, it may be desirable to forgo reconstructing a new solution independent of the previous solutions at each time step, especially if the selection of additional distinct elements incurs extra cost. In this case, it would be desirable to be able to construct a single solution that would perform satisfactorily over multiple time steps, if possible. One strategy to achieve this may be to set an observation window of
t_{w}
time steps, so that over
t_{w}
time steps we observe the objective functions
played
by the system, and only after
t_{w}
time steps will we play our solution suitable for the
t_{w}
observations. Ideally, if there is some structure or notion of continuity within the variation of the objective functions
f_{t},
employing such a strategy might be worthwhile, as it would use up to
t_{w}
times as few distinct elements while still achieving satisfactory results. In some sense, this would correspond to the inverse of the
fairness
considerations, where one customarily wants to diversify their selections. Rather, here, the aim is to limit diversification and conserve the same selection of elements over many time steps.

With this idea in mind, one has to consider what sort of strategy to use to leverage the
t_{w}
observed objective functions
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{t_{w}}\}
over a given observation window. Drawing inspiration from prevalent methods that are in use in other optimization areas, e.g., momentum-based stochastic gradient descent methods in continuous optimization
[
47
,
48
]
and the TD
(\lambda)
method in reinforcement learning
[
49
]
, we initially propose the following scheme: For a
\gamma\in[0,1],

\begin{split}\max_{S}\gamma^{t_{w}}f_{1}(S)+(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=1}^{t_{w}}\gamma^{(t_{w}-t)}f_{t}(S)\end{split}

(65)

What this formulation aims to achieve is to geometrically weigh each of the
t_{w}
observed objective functions, in a manner that may be tuned by the parameter
\gamma
to determine how much importance will be assigned to older observations with respect to the newer observations. Note that a value of
\gamma=1
assigns all the weight to the objective function that was observed first,
f_{1}
, whereas
\gamma=0
assigns, with the convention that
0^{0}=1,
all the weight to the most recently observed objective function
f_{t_{w}}.
The values
\gamma\in(0,1)
provide us with the whole spectrum of how much importance to assign to the past observations.

The important remark to be made in terms of this formulation and the relative entropy regularization is that the geometric weighing of the objective functions over time effectively gives us a reference distribution
\Gamma
, which we can instead use as the center of a neighborhood of robustness, resulting in the formulation

\begin{split}&\max_{S\subseteq N}\min_{P\in\Delta_{t_{w}}}\sum_{t=1}^{t_{w}}P_{t}f_{t}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P_{t}\;\|\;\Gamma)\\
&\text{s.t. }\lvert S\rvert\leq K,\end{split}

(66)

where
\Gamma=(\gamma^{t_{w}},(1-\gamma)\gamma^{(t_{w}-2)},\ldots,(1-\gamma)\gamma,1-\gamma)
. This formulation aims to then carry the concept of robustness into the online setting, where we attempt distributional robustness over time steps of the problem.

In the testing of the relative entropy-regularized
Stochastic Greedy

f^{1}_{t},\ldots,f^{5}_{t}
relate to the performance of the algorithms on atmospheric sensing tasks. Each of these tasks involves taking atmospheric readings at a set of five randomly located points on Earth. The atmospheric conditions at these points are described by the Lorenz 63 model
[
51

. The dynamics at these points are parameterized by values that make the system chaotic. An illustration of one instance of these points is provided in Figure

1
belong to atmospheric reading task

whose performance is indicated by the value of

f^{1}_{t}(S),\ldots,f^{5}_{t}(S)
of these tasks for a given subset of selected satellites
S
are proportional to the additive inverse of the mean-squared error (MSE) achieved by the selection
S
of satellites. The MSE is calculated by estimating the state of each atmospheric sensing point using an unscented Kalman filter
[
52

, and comparing it with the actual values of the atmospheric state at the points of interest. This estimation is highly dependent on whether an atmospheric point is within the field of view of a satellite. Note also that the additive inverse of the MSE is known to be a weak-submodular function
[
53

54
]
. Specifically, for
i\in\{1,\ldots 5\}
,

has form

f^{i}_{t}(S_{t})=\frac{1}{Z_{t}^{i}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}-\mathbf{F}_{S_{t}}^{-1}\right),

(67)

where
\mathbf{P}_{t|t-1}
indicates the prediction error covariance matrix at time
t
,
\mathbf{F}_{S_{t}}^{-1}
indicates the inverse of the Fisher information matrix for the solution
S_{t}
constructed at time
t
[
54
]
, and
Z_{t}^{i}
is the normalizing coefficient of objective
i
at time
t
.

f^{6}_{t}(S_{t})=\dfrac{1}{Z^{6}_{t}}\dfrac{\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{1}(\text{cell }c\text{ is in view of a satellite in }S_{t})}{C},

As indicated above, the utility values of all six tasks

are normalized to the range of

of a selection

on task
f^{i}

f^{i}(N)

6.1
Relative Entropy-Regularized Stochastic Greedy for Satellite Selection

For the assessment of the relative entropy-regularized
Stochastic Greedy
algorithm, we compare the performance of the solutions produced by
three
algorithms, in terms of
four
distinct performance criteria. For each of the algorithms, we use a cardinality constraint of
K=10
, a sampling set size of
|R|=24
, and a regularization parameter of
\lambda=0.1
, where applicable. The three algorithms compared are as follows:

•

Algorithm 1 - Local:
The relative-entropy regularized
Stochastic Greedy
algorithm, that is, the
Stochastic Greedy
algorithm applied to the maximization of our novel objective function
g(S)=-\lambda\log(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}\exp{(-f^{i}(S)/\lambda)}),
of Problem (
25
).

•

Algorithm 2 - Saturate (Global):
The
Submodular Saturation Algorithm
, which aims to optimize the worst-case scenario in Problem (
2
).

•

Algorithm 3 - Reference:
The
Stochastic Greedy
algorithm, applied to the maximization of the objective function
f(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}f^{i}(S)
of Problem (
4
), focused on directly optimizing with respect to the reference distribution with no consideration of robustness.

The four performance criteria are as follows:

•

Criterion 1:
The utility scores with respect to the objective
F_{1}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}f_{i}(S)
, which represents the performance of the algorithms when weighed directly by the reference distribution itself.

•

Criterion 2:
The utility scores with respect to the objective
F_{2}(S)=\min_{i}f_{i}(S)
, which represents the global worst-case single task performance.

•

Criterion 3:
The utility scores with respect to the objective
F_{3}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P^{*}_{i}f_{i}(S)
, where
P^{*}=\arg\!\min_{P}\sum^{n}_{i=1}P_{i}f^{i}(S)+\lambda D_{\text{KL}}(P\;\|\;Q)
, hence representing the local worst-case scenario performance localized to a relative entropy neighborhood of the reference distribution. This criterion may be considered as the benchmark of our novel formulation.

•

Criterion 4:
The runtime of the algorithm.

6.3
Application to Online Submodular Optimization

In this subsection, we will assess the performance of the novel formulation (
66

In summary, the TR formulation aims, using a combination of a momentum-like weighing scheme of the time-varying objective functions in an online setting, along with the idea of relative entropy regularization, to reuse the same selections made in one step over multiple time steps. In this way, it aims to be more cost-efficient in settings where the selection of more diverse elements incurs additional costs.

As a baseline, we choose the standard approach of treating each individual objective function
f_{t}
observed at time step

of the simulation. The results indicate that TR achieves a comparable yet slightly lower utility in comparison to the standard approach. The wall-clock time taken fluctuates in the TR formulation, since with a time-window size of
5

However, the total number of distinct elements chosen by the TR formulation is much lower, using less than half the number of distinct elements in comparison to the standard approach.

7
Conclusion

We proposed the novel formulation of Problem (
14
) to find a solution that is
locally
distributionally robust in the neighborhood of a reference distribution, assign an importance score to each task in a multi-task objective, using various statistical distances as a regularizer. The
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{\infty}
distances led to the proposal of the
Saturate with Preference
algorithm, which incorporates an element of preference into the standard
Submodular Saturation Algorithm
. Afterward, we demonstrated that using relative entropy as a regularizer, through duality, one can show that this novel formulation is equivalent to Problem (
25
). Then, we proved that this dual formulation gives rise to the composition of a monotone increasing function with another normalized, monotone nondecreasing submodular function, which can be optimized with standard methods such as the
Stochastic Greedy
algorithm, enjoying theoretical guarantees. We proposed an application of the relative entropy regularized to online submodular optimization, through the use of a momentum-like weighing of the objective functions observed at each time step. We then experimentally corroborated our results for all three of the proposed settings, motivated by a practical scenario involving a sensor selection problem within a simulation of LEO satellites, using weak-submodular functions. For the relative entropy regularization setting, we compared our algorithm with two other algorithms focused on optimizing the performance of the worst-case task, and on directly optimizing the performance reference distribution itself. We concluded that solving our novel formulation produces a solution that performs well on the reference distribution, is
locally
distributionally robust, and is quick in terms of computation time. For the
Saturate with Preference
setting, we showed that our algorithm consistently outperforms the standard
Submodular Saturation Algorithm
in terms of the performance on the objective functions with the highest assigned preference. For the application to the online submodular optimization setting, we demonstrated that our algorithm achieves a comparable utility to the regular method of solving each observed objective function at the moment of its observation, with a comparable wall-clock time taken. However, it does so by using a much smaller total number of distinct satellites. Finally, for a more general, real-life application of the proposed relative entropy-regularized algorithm, we tackled an image summarization task based on contemporary neural network usage.
