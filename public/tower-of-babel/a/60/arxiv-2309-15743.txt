On the bounded smooth solutions to exponentially stable linear nonautonomous hyperbolic systems
Irina Kmit Viktor Tkachenko

Abstract
We investigate global bounded solutions of higher regularity to boundary value problems for a general linear nonautonomous first order 1D hyperbolic system in a strip. We establish the existence of such solutions under the assumption of exponential stability and certain dissipativity (or non-resonant) conditions. Our results demonstrate the existence and uniqueness of C 1 C^{1} - and C 2 C^{2} -bounded solutions, in particular, periodic and almost periodic solutions. The number of imposed dissipativity conditions is related to the regularity of solutions. This connection arises due to the nonautonomous nature of the hyperbolic system under consideration, in contrast to autonomous settings. In the general case of global bounded smooth solutions we assume exponential stability in H 1 H^{1} . In the case of periodic solutions, the weaker assumption of L 2 L^{2} -exponential stability proves to be sufficient.

1.2
Relation to previous work

The current paper extends the program
suggested in
[
23
]

for constructing small global bounded smooth solutions to 1D boundary value problems associated with quasilinear first order hyperbolic systems.
The extension targets the linearized problems
and goes beyond the scope of
[
23
]
, where the reflection boundary conditions were only
of the smoothing type.

Our approach involves the approximation of
C^{2}
-solutions to quasilinear problems by
C^{2}
-solutions to linear problems.
Note that the approximation idea has been successively applied in the literature, see e.g.,
[
27
,
28
,
33
,
34
]
.

In the present paper we focus on establishing conditions that ensure the existence of
C^{2}
-solutions for general linear boundary value problems of the type (
1.1
), (
1.3
).
As an important assumption, we require the evolution operator of the homogeneous problem to be exponentially stable
(in
[
23
]
it is supposed to be exponentially dichotomous).
In other words, we suppose that the linear problem demonstrates a regular behavior, which can be used to approximate smooth solutions (without singularities as blow-ups and shocks) of the nonlinear problem.

Note that such regular behavior of the evolution operator is known to be equivalent to the existence of a unique mild solution to the nonhomogeneous problem
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
, for any given right hand side.
Having at hands the mild solution, the following two important results (of general nature) for linear systems have to be obtained: higher regularity of mild solutions and stability under perturbations in coefficients.
While the regularity and perturbation results in
[
23
]
essentially use a smoothing property of the evolution
operator (see
[
18
,
20
]
for the smoothing results), in the current paper we have to overcome
the well-known phenomenon of ‘‘loss of smoothness’’. To handle the loss of smoothness, we impose certain dissipativity conditions.
Unlike
[
23
]
, where the evolution operator was assumed to have exponential dichotomy (in particular, exponential stability) in
L^{2}
, our current assumption is that the system is exponentially stable in the higher-order regularity space
H^{1}
. This seems to be essential, since the evolution operator is not necessarily eventually differentiable.
Specifically, for
C^{k}
-solvability (
k\geq 1
) of the problem (
1.1
), (
1.3
), we assume that the system is
H^{1}
-exponentially stable, and further, that
k+1
dissipativity conditions are satisfied.
Remark that the number of dissipativity conditions is related to the regularity of solutions. This connection arises due to the nonautonomous nature of the hyperbolic system under consideration,
in contrast to autonomous settings.
Note that
in the case of smoothing boundary conditions, the evolution operator is automatically dissipative and, therefore,
no dissipativity conditions are needed to be imposed.

We also remark that the question of exponential stability in nonlinear problems is typically related to higher-order regularity spaces such as
H^{1}
,
H^{2}
, and
C^{1}
, as discussed, e.g., in
[
4
,
8
,
12
,
14
]
.

Problems of the type (
1.1
), (
1.3
) have been investigated, e.g., in

[
16
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
27
,
33
,
34
,
36
]
. Our present results, therefore, extend
the class of linear(ized) problems for which the existence of bounded
smooth solutions in a strip can be proved under natural assumptions.
This extention is achieved due to the generality of the system (
1.1
), (
1.3
)
as well as due to the conditions considered for (
1.1
) and (
1.3
).

and looking at the real and the imaginary parts of the last equality, we get

\eta=\sqrt{\frac{(\xi-r_{1})^{2}e^{2\xi}-(\xi r_{2}-1)^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}-e^{2\xi}}}

\sin\eta=\frac{(1-r_{1}r_{2})\eta r_{1}}{{e^{\xi}}\left[(\xi-r_{1})^{2}+\eta^{2}\right]}.

It is easy to see that, besides of the zero solution, the equation (

lying on a bounded interval, say
|\xi_{j}|\leq\bar{\xi}
for some

), (

) and from the fact that

\frac{(\xi-r_{1})^{2}e^{2\xi}-(\xi r_{2}-1)^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}-e^{2\xi}}\to-\infty\ \mbox{ as }\ \xi\pm\infty.

Hence,
the eigenvalues of (

\mu_{j}^{\pm}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha-\xi_{j}\pm i\sqrt{\frac{(\xi_{j}-r_{1})^{2}e^{2\xi_{j}}-(\xi_{j}r_{2}-1)^{2}r_{1}^{2}}{r_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}-e^{2\xi_{j}}}}\right).

(1.22)

If
\alpha
is chosen to satisfy the inequality
\alpha<-\bar{\xi}
, then the real parts of all eigenvalues
are negative.

We now use the spectral mapping theorem (see
[

, Theorem 2.1]
)
providing a spectral criterion of the exponential stability of

-semigroups in
L^{2}
. Combined with
[

]
,
this criterion states that the

1.17
)–(
1.18
).
As the problem (
1.17
)–(
1.18
) is autonomous, from Theorem

we conclude that the operator

\mathcal{A}
associated with (
1.17
)–(
1.18
) (and defined by (

)–(

)

C_{0}
-semigroup on

.
This means, in particular, that the operator

is closed.
It is known from

that the spectrum of the operator

consists of finitely or
countably many eigenvalues, each of finite multiplicity. Furthermore, it has no finite
limit points. This implies that the whole spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
is given by the formula (
1.22
). Due to the
spectral mapping theorem, the problem (
1.17
)–(
1.18
) is exponentialy stable in
L^{2}

for all
\alpha<-\bar{\xi}
.

It remains to check the condition (
1.12
).
We suppose, additionally to
\alpha<-\bar{\xi}
, that the boundary coefficients

and

fulfill the conditions

and
|r_{2}|e^{-\alpha}<1

Note first that in the autonomous setting the condition (

) is the same for
i=0,1,2
.
It, therefore, suffices to check this condition for

for any
\psi\in BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\|\psi\|_{BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1

for all

, namely

\left|[G_{0}\psi]_{1}(t)\right|\leq|r_{1}|<1,\quad\left|[G_{0}\psi]_{2}(t)\right|\leq|r_{2}|e^{-\alpha}<1.

This example also shows that, if the absolute values of the coefficients of the hyperbolic system are large, then the dissipativity conditions are ensured if the boundary coefficients
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are small enough.

Example 2.
We consider a time-periodic problem for the following nonautonomous decoupled
2\times 2
-hyperbolic system:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{1}+\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}\right)\partial_{x}u_{1}+3u_{1}=0,\quad\partial_{t}u_{2}-(3+x)\partial_{x}u_{2}=0

(1.23)

endowed with the boundary conditions (
1.18
).
The aim of this example is, first, to check the main solvability conditions of this problem
in the nonautonomous setting and, second, to show that at least one of the boundary coefficients
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
can
be large. To this end, we use the Lyapunov approach.
Let

V(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\left(u_{1}^{2}(x,t)+u_{2}^{2}(x,t)\right)dx.
The derivative of
V(t)

along the trajectories of the system (
1.23
), (
1.18
) is computed directly as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{dV(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\int_{0}^{1}\left[u_{1}\left(\left(\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}-1\right)\partial_{x}u_{1}-3u_{1}\right)+(3+x)u_{2}\partial_{x}u_{2}\right]dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}-1\right)+4r_{2}^{2}\right]u_{1}^{2}(1,t)+\left[-3+r_{1}^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}\right)\right]u_{2}^{2}(0,t)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\left(6u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}\right)dx.

Putting
r_{1}=\sqrt{12/5}>1
and
r_{2}=\sqrt{3}/4<1
, we have

\left[\left(\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}-1\right)+4r_{2}^{2}\right]u_{1}^{2}(1,t)+\left[-3+r_{1}^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{4}\sin{t}\right)\right]u_{2}^{2}(0,t)\leq 0.

It follows that

\frac{dV(t)}{dt}\leq-V(t)
and, consequently,

\|u_{1}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|u_{2}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\leq e^{-(t-s)}\left(\|u_{1}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\|u_{2}(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\right).

for
t\geq s.
In other words, the system (
1.23
), (
1.18
) is exponentially stable in
L^{2}
.

The dissipativity conditions (
1.12
) are fulfilled, which can again be seen by the direct computation. It suffices to note that, for all
\psi\in BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\|\psi\|_{BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and for
i=0,1,2
, we have the following inequalities:

\left|[G_{i}\psi]_{1}(t)\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{12}{5}}\exp{\left(-\frac{12}{5}+i\frac{4}{9}\right)}<1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left|[G_{i}\psi]_{2}(t)\right|\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}.

1.3.2
How to check the exponential stability conditions: overview of known results

Examples
1
and
2
show that the dissipativity conditions (
1.12
) are relatively easy to check
by direct computation.
For the exponential stability assumption, (sufficient) conditions
are desirable. In this subsection we survey some known results providing a number of such conditions. Note that those
results concern mostly the autonomous setting.

As we have already seen in Example 1, a useful
tool is provided by the spectral mapping theorem giving powerful
spectral criteria
for deciding whether the system is exponentially stable.
The applicability of the spectral criterion to our problem follows from
[
5
,
29
,
30
,
32
]
.
Another,
resolvent criterion
for the uniform exponential stability
of
C_{0}
-semigroups on Hilbert spaces
is given by

[
10
, Theorem 1.11, p. 302]
. This theorem states that a
C_{0}
-semigroup
T(\cdot)
on a Hilbert space
H

is uniformly exponentially stable if and only if there exists a constant

M>0
such that

\|(A-\mu I)^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}<M\ \mbox{ for all }\ \mu\in\mathbb{C}\ \mbox{ with }\ \mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\mu>0,

where
A
is a generator of
T(\cdot)
.

A group of sufficient conditions for the exponential stability
stems from the

Lyapunov approach

(see, e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
8
,
9
,
13
,
14
]
). Being
natural from the physical point of view, this approach provides physically motivated conditions on the data.
In

[
1
]
the authors deal with the
H^{2}
-exponential stability and provide two sufficient conditions, the so-called ‘‘internal condition’’ and ‘‘boundary condition’’. The former is
based on the energy-like Lyapunov function and the latter is formulated in terms of the boundary coefficients.
Most results on the Lyapunov approach known in the literature deal with autonomous problems, but, as we showed in Example 2, this approach is also applicable to the nonautonomous setting.

Finally, we mention the so-called
backstepping approach
. It provides a suitable
transformation of the original system (by shifting all eigenvalues
accordingly) to the so-called ‘‘target system’’, which is more convenient to investigate the exponential stability, see e.g.
[
25
]
.

\begin{split}v^{*}(t)&=\int_{-\infty}^{t}U(t,s)({\mathcal{A}}(s)+\lambda I)({\mathcal{A}}(s)+\lambda I)^{-1}f(s)ds\\

Since

and the function

, the function
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left[(\mathcal{A}(s)+\lambda I)^{-1}f(s)\right]

is also continuous and bounded in
s
, with values in
D(\mathcal{A})
.
Further, taking into account the exponential stability estimate (
1.10
),
the function

, like in the proof of

.

) is

, by the same argument.

It remains to show that

satisfies (

)

(3.20)

By (

as desired.

.
Finally, from (
1.1
) we get the estimate

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Lambda_{0}}\left(\|f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|bu^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial_{t}u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{13}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial^{2}_{xt}f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)

for some
M_{13}>0
not depending on
f
.
The estimate (
1.13
) now easily follows.

3.4
Proof of Part
{\bf(ii)}
:
BC^{2}
-solutions

Differentiating the system (
1.1
) with
u=u^{*}
in a distributional sense in
t
and the boundary conditions
(
1.3
) pointwise, we get, respectively,

(\partial_{t}+a\partial_{x})\partial_{t}u^{*}+\left(b-a^{-1}\partial_{t}a\right)\partial_{t}u^{*}+\left(\partial_{t}b-a^{-1}\partial_{t}a\,b\right)u^{*}=\partial_{t}f-a^{-1}\partial_{t}a\,f

(3.40)

and

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{*}_{j}(0,t)=R_{j}\partial_{t}u^{*}(\cdot,t),\quad 1\leq j\leq m,\;t\in\mathbb{R},\\[5.69054pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{*}_{j}(1,t)=R_{j}\partial_{t}u^{*}(\cdot,t),\quad m<j\leq n,\;t\in\mathbb{R}.\end{array}

(3.41)

Using a similar argument as above, we state that the function
u^{*}\in BC^{1}(\Pi,\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies both (
3.40
) in distributional sense
and (
3.41
) pointwise
if and only if the function
u^{*}

satisfies the following system pointwise:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{*}_{j}(x,t)

\displaystyle=c_{j}^{1}(x_{j},x,t)R_{j}\partial_{t}u^{*}(\cdot,\omega_{j}(x_{j}))

\displaystyle\quad\displaystyle-\int_{x_{j}}^{x}d_{j}^{1}(\xi,x,t)\biggl(\sum_{k\not=j}b_{jk}(\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))\partial_{t}u^{*}_{k}(\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))

\displaystyle\quad\displaystyle+\biggl[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\partial_{t}b_{jk}\!-\!a^{-1}_{j}\partial_{t}a_{j}\,b_{jk})u^{*}_{k}\!-\!\partial_{t}f_{j}\!+\!a^{-1}_{j}\partial_{t}a_{j}\,f_{j}\biggr](\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))\biggr)d\xi,j\!\leq\!n.

(3.42)

Rewrite (
3.4
) in the form

\displaystyle w=C_{1}w+D_{1}w+F_{1}(u^{*},f),

(3.43)

where
w=\partial_{t}u^{*}
and the operators

C_{1},D_{1},F_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}))
are defined, respectively, by

\displaystyle[C_{1}w]_{j}(x,t)=c_{j}^{1}(x_{j},x,t)R_{j}w(\cdot,\omega_{j}(x_{j})),

\displaystyle[D_{1}w]_{j}(x,t)=-\int_{x_{j}}^{x}d_{j}^{1}(\xi,x,t)\sum_{k\neq j}b_{jk}(\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))w_{k}(\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))d\xi,

\displaystyle[F_{1}(u,f)]_{j}(x,t)=

\displaystyle-\int_{x_{j}}^{x}d_{j}^{1}(\xi,x,t)\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\partial_{t}b_{jk}-a^{-1}_{j}\partial_{t}a_{j}\,b_{jk})u_{k}-\partial_{t}f_{j}+a^{-1}_{j}\partial_{t}a_{j}\,f_{j}\right](\xi,\omega_{j}(\xi))d\xi.

Iterating (
3.43
), we obtain

\displaystyle w=C_{1}w+(D_{1}C_{1}+D_{1}^{2})w+(I+D_{1})F_{1}(u^{*},f).{\tiny}

(3.44)

Similarly to the operators
DC
and
D^{2}
, the
operators
D_{1}C_{1}
and
D_{1}^{2}
are smoothing, mapping continuously
BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Moreover, the following smoothing estimate is true:

\left\|(D_{1}C_{1}+D_{1}^{2})w\right\|_{BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{14}\|w\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(3.45)

for some
M_{14}>0
not depending on
w\in BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Again, similarly to the bijectivity of the operator
I-C
, we prove that
I-C_{1}
is a bijective operator from
BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
itself.
In other words, we have to show that the system

w_{j}(x,t)=c_{j}^{1}(x_{j},x,t)R_{j}w(\cdot,\omega_{j}(x_{j},x,t))+h_{j}(x,t),\quad j\leq n,

is uniquely solvable in
BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for each
h\in BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
or, the same,
that the operator

I-G_{1}

is a bijective operator from
BC^{1}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself,
where the operator
G_{1}
is given by (
1.9
).
To this end, we use the assumptions
that

\|G_{1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<1
and
\|G_{2}\|_{\mathcal{L}(BC(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n}))}<1
.

Similarly to (
3.37
), the inverse to
I-C_{1}
fulfills the bound

\|(I-C_{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq 1+M_{15}\|C_{1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}

(3.46)

for some
M_{15}>0
.
Further, due to (
3.44
), it holds

\displaystyle\|w\|_{BC_{t}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|(I-C_{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\|(D_{1}C_{1}+D_{1}^{2})w

(3.47)

\displaystyle+(I+D_{1})F_{1}(u^{*},f)\|_{BC_{t}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Combining (
3.47
) with
(
1.13
), (
3.45
), and (
3.46
), we conclude that there exists
M_{16}>0
such that

\|w\|_{BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{16}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial_{xt}^{2}f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}}+\|\partial_{t}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}\right).

Hence,

\|\partial_{t}^{2}u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{16}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\!+\!\|\partial_{xt}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}\!+\!\|\partial_{t}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}\right).

(3.48)

From (
3.40
) we now easily get the bound

\|\partial^{2}_{xt}u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{17}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial_{xt}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}+\|\partial_{t}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}\right).

Differentiating the system (
1.1
) in
x
, we additionally obtain an estimate for
\partial_{x}^{2}u^{*}
, namely,

\|\partial^{2}_{x}u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq M_{18}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial_{xt}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}+\|\partial_{t}^{2}f\|_{BC\left(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)}\right),

where the constant
M_{18}
is independent of
f
.
The estimate (
1.14
) now easily follows. The proof of Part
{\bf(ii)}
of the theorem is complete.

3.5
Proof of Part
{\bf(iii)}
: Almost periodic solutions

Now we assume that the coefficients of the initial problem are almost periodic
in
t
and prove that the solution
u^{*}
constructed above
belongs to
AP(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Fix
\mu>0
and let
h
be a common
\mu
-almost period of the functions
a_{j}
,
b_{jk}
, and
f_{j}

as well as
their derivatives in
x
and
t
.
Then the functions
\tilde{a}_{j}(x,t)=a_{j}(x,t+h)-a_{j}(x,t)
and
\tilde{b}_{jk}(x,t)=b_{jk}(x,t+h)-b_{jk}(x,t)
satisfy
the inequalities

\displaystyle\|\tilde{a}_{j}\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi)}\leq\mu\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|\tilde{b}_{jk}\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi)}\leq\mu.

(3.49)

The desired statement will be proved if we show that
h
is an almost period of the function
u^{*}(x,t).

While the function
u^{*}(x,t)
is a unique bounded classical solution
to the problem (
1.1
), (
1.3
), the function

u^{*}(x,t+h)
is a unique bounded classical solution to the system

\partial_{t}u+a(x,t+h)\partial_{x}u+b(x,t+h)u=f(x,t+h)

subjected to the boundary conditions (
1.3
).
Then the difference
z(x,t)=u^{*}(x,t)-u^{*}(x,t+h)
satisfies the system

\partial_{t}z+a(x,t)\partial_{x}z+b(x,t)z=g(x,t)

and the boundary conditions (
1.3
), where

\displaystyle g(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\left(b(x,t)-b(x,t+h)\right)u^{*}(x,t+h)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(a(x,t)-a(x,t+h)\right)\partial_{x}u^{*}(x,t+h)+f(x,t)-f(x,t+h).

The function
g(x,t)
is
C^{1}
-smooth in
x
and
t
and, due to (
1.13
) and (
3.49
),
satisfies the bound

\begin{split}\|g\|_{BC\left(\mathbb{R};L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)}&\leq\|g\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leq\mu\left[L_{1}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial^{2}_{xt}f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)+1\right].\end{split}

(3.50)

Now, by Theorem
3.3
, the function

y:\mathbb{R}\to L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined by
\left[y(t)\right](x)=z(x,t)

is given by the formula

\displaystyle y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}U(t,s)g(s)ds,

where
\left[g(t)\right](x)=g(x,t)
.
Moreover, on account of (
3.50
),
it satisfies to prove the following estimate:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|y(t)\|_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})}&\leq&\displaystyle\frac{M}{\alpha}\|g\|_{BC\left(\mathbb{R};L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)}\\[8.53581pt]
&\leq&\displaystyle\frac{\mu M}{\alpha}\left[L_{1}\left(\|f\|_{BC^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\partial^{2}_{xt}f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)+1\right].\end{array}

It follows that the function
v^{*}

is almost periodic in
t
with values in
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence, the function
u^{*}(x,t)=[v^{*}(t)](x)

is almost periodic in
t
in the mean in
x
, see
[
3
, Theorem 2.15]
.
Moreover, since
u^{*}\in BC^{2}(\Pi,\mathbb{R}^{n}),
the derivative

\partial_{t}u^{*}(t,x)
is uniformly continuous in
t
in the mean in
x
.
On account of
[
3
, Theorem 2.13]
, we conclude that
\partial_{t}u^{*}(t,x)
is almost periodic in
t
in the mean in
x
.
The almost periodicity of
\partial_{x}u^{*}(x,t)
now easily follows from the equation (
1.1
).
Consequently, the function
u^{*}(x,t)
is almost periodic in
t
uniformly in
x
, as desired.

4
Periodic solutions: proof of Theorem
1.6

Let the conditions of Theorem
1.5

{\bf(i)}
are fulfilled.
Note that those conditions fall into the scope of Theorem
3.3
. Accordingly to Theorem 3.3, the problem
(
3.3
)
has a unique bounded classical solution
v^{*}
, given by the formula (
3.5
). As the coefficients
a_{j}
and
b_{jk}
are
T
-periodic in
t
, we have
\mathcal{A}(t+T)=\mathcal{A}(t)
and, hence

U(t+T,s+T)=U(t,s)
for all
t\geq s
. This and the solution formula
(
3.5
)
imply that
v^{*}(t+T)=v^{*}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, as
v^{*}
is the classical solution,
then it is continuous in
t\in\mathbb{R}
with values in
H^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})
. On account of the periodicity of
v^{*}
,
the solution
v^{*}
belongs to
BC(\mathbb{R};H^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and, hence, to
BC(\mathbb{R};C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. This implies that the function
u^{*}(x,t)=[v^{*}(t)](x)

belongs to
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, due to the proof of Theorem
1.5

(see Section
3.2
),
u^{*}
satisfies the equation (
3.2
) or, the same, its operator
form (
3.28
) pointwise.

To finish the proof of Claim
{\bf(i)}
, it remains to prove the a priori estimate (
1.15
). We show
that the map
f\in C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to u^{*}\in C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous. To this end, if we prove
that the operator
I-C-D
is Fredholm of index zero from
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself and that it
is injective from
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself.
This will then imply the bijectivity of
I-C-D
and the boundedness of the inverse

(I-C-D)^{-1}
, as desired.

Despite the Fredholmness of
I-C-D
follows from
[
19
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
21
, Theorem 1.2]
,
we
give a short proof of the Fredholmness
property, using the notion of parametrix:
First note that the operator
I-C
is bijective from
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself. Indeed, similarly to the proof of

BC^{1}
-smoothness in Section
3.3
, the bijectivity of
I-C

is equivalent to the
unique solvability
of the system (
3.30
) with respect to
u
in
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
or, the same, to the
unique solvability
of the system (
3.31
) with respect to
z
in
C_{per}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The last statement is, in its turn, equivalent to
the bijectivity of
I-G_{0}
from
C_{per}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself. But this is true due to the assumption
(
1.12
) for
i=0
and the Banach fixed-point theorem.

Taking into account the bijectivity of
I-C
, the operator
I-C-D
is Fredholm of index zero
from
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself
if and only if the operator
I-(I-C)^{-1}D
is Fredholm operator of index zero from
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
to itself.
Further we use the Fredholmness criterion given by
[
35
, Proposition 1, pp. 298–299]
. It states that, if there exist linear continuous operators
P_{l},P_{r}:C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
(called parametrices) and compact operators

Q_{l},Q_{r}:C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

P_{l}\left[I-(I-C)^{-1}D\right]=I+Q_{l}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left[I-(I-C)^{-1}D\right]P_{r}=I+Q_{r},

then the operator
I-(I-C)^{-1}D
is Fredholm of index zero. Take

P_{l}=P_{r}=I+(I-C)^{-1}D

and calculate

P_{l}\left[I-(I-C)^{-1}D\right]=\left[I-(I-C)^{-1}D\right]P_{r}=I-(I-C)^{-1}D(I-C)^{-1}D.

Accordingly to
[
35
, Proposition 1, pp. 298–299]
, it remains
to prove the compactness of the operator
(I-C)^{-1}D(I-C)^{-1}D
or, on the account of the
boundedness of
(I-C)^{-1}
, the compactness of the operator
D(I-C)^{-1}D:C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Indeed,

D(I-C)^{-1}D=D\left[I+C(I-C)^{-1}\right]D=D^{2}+DC(I-C)^{-1}D.

Because of the boundedness of the operator
(I-C)^{-1}D
, we are done if we prove the compactness of the operators

D^{2}
and
DC
. But this follows immediately from
[
21
, Equation (4.2)]

and the Arzela-Ascoli theorem. The proof of the Fredholmness of
I-C-D
is, therefore, complete.

To prove the injectivitiy of
I-C-D
,
suppose that the equation (
3.28
) has two continuous periodic solutions, say
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
. Then the difference

u^{1}-u^{2}
satisfies the equation (
3.28
) with
f=0
and, consequently, the equation (
3.29
) with
f=0
.
Since the operators

D^{2}
and
DC
map continuously
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
C_{per}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})

and the operator
(I-C)^{-1}
maps
C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap BC_{t}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
into itself
(see Section
3.3
), we
conclude that

u^{1}-u^{2}\in C_{per}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap BC_{t}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Using additionally that
u_{1}-u_{2}
is a distributional solution to (
1.5
), (
1.3
),
we get that
u^{1}-u^{2}\in C_{per}^{1}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This means that

u^{1}-u^{2}
is a classical solution to
(
1.5
), (
1.3
).

For an arbitrary fixed
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
u^{1}(\cdot,s)-u^{2}(\cdot,s)
belongs to
C([0,1];\mathbb{R}^{n})
and, hence, to
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,
by Definition
1.1
, the function
u^{1}-u^{2}
is also the

-generalized solution to the problem
(
1.5
), (
1.3
), (

for all

. The injectivity is therewith proved.

As it follows from the above,

u^{*}=(I-C-D)^{-1}Ff

and the following a priori estimate is fulfilled:

\|u^{*}\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq L_{5}\|f\|_{BC(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}

. Moreover, combining (
3.39
) with (
3.37
), (
3.38
), and (

\|u^{*}\|_{BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq L_{6}\|f\|_{BC^{1}_{t}(\Pi;\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(4.2)

The final estimate (
1.15
) now straightforwardly follows from (
1.1
).
This finishes the proof of Claim
{\bf(i)}
of Theorem
1.6
.

To prove Claim
{\bf(ii)}
of Theorem

, we
first use Claim

)
