Under the Surface: Tracking the Artifactuality of LLM-Generated Data
Debarati Das, Karin de Langis, Anna Martin-Boyle, Jaehyung Kim, Minhwa Lee, Zae Myung KimShirley Anugrah Hayati, Risako Owan, Bin Hu, Ritik Sachin Parkar, Ryan Koo,Jong Inn Park, Aahan Tyagi, Libby Ferland, Sanjali Roy, Vincent Liu,Dongyeop KangMinnesota NLP Lab, University of Minnesota {das00015, dento019, mart5877, kim01746, lee03533, kim01756, dongyeop}@umn.edu

Abstract
This work delves into the expanding role of large language models (LLMs) in generating artificial data. LLMs are increasingly employed to create a variety of outputs, including annotations, preferences, instruction prompts, simulated dialogues, and free text. As these forms of LLM-generated data often intersect in their application, they exert mutual influence on each other and raise significant concerns about the quality and diversity of the artificial data incorporated into training cycles, leading to an artificial data ecosystem . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to aggregate various types of LLM-generated text data, from more tightly constrained data like ‘‘task labels’’ to more lightly constrained ‘‘free-form text’’. We then stress test the quality and implications of LLM-generated artificial data, comparing it with human data across various existing benchmarks. Despite artificial data’s capability to match human performance, this paper reveals significant hidden disparities, especially in complex tasks where LLMs often miss the nuanced understanding of intrinsic human-generated content. This study critically examines diverse LLM-generated data and emphasizes the need for ethical practices in data creation and when using LLMs. It highlights the LLMs’ shortcomings in replicating human traits and behaviors, underscoring the importance of addressing biases and artifacts produced in LLM-generated content for future research and development. Introduction

Figure 1:

An example of an artificial data ecosystem in which LLMs are increasingly employed to create a variety of outputs, including annotations, preference labels, instruction prompts, simulated dialogues, and free text. As these forms of LLM-generated data are often intertwined in their application, they exert mutual influence on each other within interconnected use cases. This interdependence raises significant concerns about the quality and diversity of the artificial data incorporated into training cycles.
In this LLM ecosystem, there is a risk that AI systems may become predominantly or entirely dependent on artificially generated inputs.

The advancement of large language models (LLMs) has led to their widespread adoption for complex data generation tasks once exclusive to humans, such as writing, labeling, and data evaluation
Ding et al. 2022
;
Møller et al. 2023
;
Huang et al. 2023
. This proliferation of artificial data, especially from models like ChatGPT, calls for detailed research to understand the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in various economically and efficiently executed data-intensive tasks. LLM-generated artificial data can match human data in performance across various benchmarks, yet the full implications and impact of its use in subsequent tasks remain elusive.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, LLMs are now being used to generate data for many use cases including annotation, evaluation, and generation. Artificial data generated from one LLM could be used for one or more related use cases, leading to the rise of an LLM ecosystem. Limited research has highlighted the disparity between LLM-generated labels and human annotations
Reiss 2023
, prompting calls for cross-domain evaluation against human data. There is no consensus on how artificial data differs from human data because it is a fairly new and highly fluent source of data
Sadasivan et al. 2023a
. Recent studies, notably those by
Shumailov et al. 2023
and
Briesch et al. 2023
, have highlighted concerns regarding the quality and diversity of artificial data employed in, and subsequently produced by, training loops because of the problem of mode collapse, where models trained on their outputs might lose touch with the true data distribution.

The growing dependence on such data for its convenience and lower costs raises concerns about an emerging cycle of AI- reliant solely on artificial inputs. These concerns are further fueled by speculations of the next version of GPT training on its own generated data

These issues underpin our inquiry into the nature of data produced by LLMs. Our objective is not an in-depth empirical analysis of LLM-generated data but rather a preliminary examination to detect certain anomalies that might suggest biases or a trend toward mode collapse. By adopting this method, we can examine the attributes of artificial data and pinpoint areas that might benefit from more thorough research. Through this investigation of such anomalies, our aim is to offer initial observations on the characteristics of LLM-generated data, especially in comparison to human-generated data. Additionally, we seek to emphasize the need for careful and responsible methods in creating datasets and developing LLMs, particularly given their growing relevance to significant societal issues.

1.1
Research Focus, Questions, and Scope

This work contributes to this understanding by taking a broad inventory of artificial data, possible “artifacts”(anomalous behavior or divergences from the corresponding human data), and the effects of using this data. We prioritize breadth, covering five different data types (examples of each shown in Figure
3
), rather than depth, to understand overarching themes in artificial data.

To forward the understanding of artificial data and its effects, the research questions we investigate are:

•

RQ1
What is the nature of SOTA artificial data? How does it compare to human data, and what artifacts does it contain?

•

RQ2
Does training on artificial data compromise performance outcomes compared to similar human-generated data?

•

RQ3
How specific are the artifacts of artificial data to certain types of data produced by LLMs, and how much do they apply to all types of data generated by LLMs?

To address RQ1, we examine
first-order effects
for each data type. We define first-order effects as the nature of the data itself, i.e., any differences or inconsistencies between the artificial data and the corresponding human data. To address RQ2, we examine
second-order effects
, which we define as any differences or inconsistencies between the outcomes of NLP pipelines containing artificial data and the outcomes from corresponding pipelines containing human data. We address RQ3 through an overview of the results of all first-and second-order experiments.

Our research is designed to offer preliminary insights into the quality and effects of data produced by LLMs rather than providing final, conclusive findings. We recognize the challenge of dealing with stochastic outputs, a common issue in LLM studies. Our paper examines a broad spectrum of LLM-generated data types from existing benchmarks, whose variety and complexity make it difficult to create a controlled experiment that adequately addresses all their intricacies and variables. Furthermore, such an endeavor would demand significant time, financial, and computational resources. The primary focus of our study is instead to identify and discuss the differences between LLM-generated and human-generated outputs, to identify potential areas for further research, and to bring attention to the ethical considerations inherent in LLM-generated data.

1.2
Main Findings

We contend that the general assertion that LLM-generated data matches human data is
superficial
, as we find that when we look below the surface (see Figure
2
), there are many dimensions in which LLM-generated data does not match human data:

•

LLMs tend to fall short in complex tasks, often
missing the subtleties and nuanced understanding
found in human-generated data, particularly in areas like minority perspectives and complex preferences.

•

LLM-generated texts frequently
lack human traits
such as humor and sarcasm and
do not mirror the structural complexities and styles
typical in human writing and conversations, especially in problem-solving scenarios.

•

These shortcomings become evident as LLMs struggle in
unfamiliar situations
, generating
incorrect outputs
,
exhibiting biases
, and failing to accurately replicate human behavior, leading to
decreased performance in models trained on such LLM-generated data
.

This highlights the essential need to monitor and address the limitations and biases inherent in LLM-generated content.
We summarize more detailed major findings in Section
4
.

1.3
Contributions

Our main contributions are:

•

We present a
pioneering effort in gathering a diverse range of text data produced by LLMs
, covering everything from more structured “task labels” to more open-ended “free-form text.” This comprehensive collection is significant as it allows for a unique and holistic examination of LLM outputs. By encompassing a wide spectrum of data types, our study offers a broader understanding of LLM capabilities and limitations across different contexts and constraints. This is a crucial contribution to the field, as it provides insights into how LLMs perform under varying degrees of structure and freedom, which is essential for both understanding their current state and guiding future improvements and applications.

•

We
aggregate and conduct comprehensive stress tests
on various data generated by LLMs using the existing benchmarks, offering a thorough evaluation of the quality, consistency, and reliability of LLM outputs across diverse models and scenarios, thereby providing a groundbreaking insight into their strengths and weaknesses for future research and development.

•

Our research
emphasizes the critical need for responsible and ethical practices in creating and using LLM-generated data
, advocating for collaborative efforts among stakeholders to address biases, increase diversity, and deepen the understanding of complex human opinions in LLM outputs, thereby ensuring their development benefits society ethically and sustainably.

In our experiments, we closely examine and analyze five types of artificial data, categorizing them to differentiate their applications and functionalities. This categorization enables our understanding of LLM strengths and limitations in different contexts. The five types are:

•

Task Labels
for classification tasks over textual input, bypassing the need for human annotators.

•

Preference
, specifically evaluating which text is better, is useful for human alignment tasks such as training reward models used in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

•

Instruction prompts
written by LLMs for instruction fine-tuning, eliminating the need for humans to write comprehensive sets of diverse instructions.

•

Simulation
, where two LLM agents converse, has the potential for enhancing model performance and simulating intricate social interactions, according to initial studies.

•

Free-Form Text
written by an LLM, often used to compensate for data scarcity issues and used for pretraining or finetuning.

We defined boundaries between different types of LLM-generated text data based on the level of output constraint, ranging from tightly constrained (such as task labels data, which consists of discrete labels for a task) to lightly constrained (like free-form text data, where output is freely generated from an input).

Types of Stress Testing Methods

This section provides an overview of the stress testing methods applied to analyze LLM-generated data.

Figure 4:
Methods to stress test LLM-generated data for the first- and second-order experiments. In summary, the first-order experiments investigate the data
“as-is,”
for example, focusing on their distributional differences and correlation patterns among human- and LLM-generated data; validating and analyzing using manual inspection; and counting how often labels flip between the original human and the resulting machine text. The second-order experiments involve fine-tuning LLMs on the machine-generated data and investigating whether the existing artifacts or biases are amplified.

Figure
4
illustrates the range of approaches utilized in our paper for stress testing artificial data, while Table
2
summarizes which methods were applied to different data types.
Key methods include analyzing distributional differences between human and LLM-generated data to uncover biases and stylistic variances, thereby assessing how well machines mimic human language and thought. Qualitative analysis probes deeper into machine-generated text, evaluating aspects like bias, coherence, and creativity to gauge subjective quality. On the other hand, human validation is a quality check, ensuring that machine outputs are contextually and culturally appropriate. The label-flipping technique is used to study changes in label predictions between human and machine inputs, highlighting inconsistencies in the model’s understanding. Lastly, artifact analysis scrutinizes LLM-generated data to identify unique patterns and biases crucial for enhancing content diversity and authenticity.

We discover that techniques such as
artifact analysis
are particularly suited for less restricted LLM-generated data like simulations or free-form text. In contrast,
label flipping
is effective for more constrained data types. Methods like
distributional difference
and
label flipping
are useful for comparing various LLM-generated data types with human data. While most data types are analyzed for secondary effects, simulation data is still in the early stages of examination and requires more foundational analysis. Together, these methods comprehensively evaluate LLM-generated data, uncovering trends, biases, and improvement opportunities.

Task label data, heavily influenced by majority annotations, typically lacks diversity in opinions, particularly in subjective tasks. We tackle this by comparing label distributions between human and LLM outputs and conducting variation analysis. We also observe the amplification of existing artifacts through our second-order effect analysis. In the case of preference data, used as a proxy for human preferences, we assess the distributional differences between human and LLM linguistic preferences. Instruction-based data from LLMs, which usually lacks diversity in out-of-distribution tasks, presents high error rates. Addressing this, we employ human validation, error distribution analysis, and second-order effect analysis. We analyze challenges such as unexpected agent behaviors in the lesser-studied domain of simulation data. These are scrutinized through artifact and correlation analysis, coupled with human validation. Lastly, we find that free-form text data often faces the challenge of distinguishing between human and LLM outputs, with previous work primarily focusing on detailed analysis of linguistic features. Our stress testing approach, in contrast, includes artifact analysis of high-level attributes, label flipping, and contrasting human and LLM discourse patterns.

4.1
Thematic Grouping of Detected Artifacts across LLM-generated Data Types

Through our investigation into the five distinct LLM-generated data types, we notice thematic areas in which LLM-generated data falls short of its human counterpart:

1.

LLMs demonstrate a subpar understanding of complex human opinions and interactions.

•

While generating
task labels
, LLMs over-represent majority opinions and do not match minority opinions well. They also show a tendency to be misleadingly confident concerning sentences with age, gender, religion, and race bias. (§
5.1
)

•

In creating
free-form text
, LLMs exhibit styles that differ significantly from human approaches in diverse social contexts. Moreover, unlike human discourse, which varies widely across domains, LLM discourse patterns remain relatively consistent. (§
9.1
).

•

For elicitation of
preferences
, we find that LLM preferences are tightly coupled with standalone lexical cues, whereas human preferences appear to take a more holistic approach (§
6.1
).

2.

LLMs struggle to respond effectively when faced with unknown or unfamiliar situations.

•

In LLM
simulations
(i.e. conversations between two LLM agents), one of the most common errors is role flipping, or the agents swapping their assigned roles. This happens most frequently when the agent becomes confused, indicating that the simulation breaks down when an agent does not understand how to respond (§
8.1
).

•

In
instruction
writing, LLMs may provide incorrect outputs for a written instruction rather than an output that indicates uncertainty or lack of knowledge of the answer. In downstream training, these incorrect outputs result in more hallucinations produced by instruction-tuned models (§
7.1
, §
7.2
).

3.

LLMs are deficient in accurately mirroring human behavior for particular tasks.

•

In
simulations
, where LLM agents engage in conversations focused on problem-solving, these agents often stray from the main topic, negatively impacting task performance. This contrasts with human digressions, facilitating team building and contributing to more effective problem resolution. (§
8.1
)

4.

Models trained on LLM-generated data containing the above issues have degraded performance.

•

We find that models trained on several types of LLM-generated data – specifically
instructions
,
free-form text
, and
preferences
– are at risk for degraded performance when compared to models trained on corresponding human data (see experiments in §
6.2
, §
7.2
and §
9.2
). In addition, these performance deficits appear to be tied to artifacts in LLM-generated data.

•

We also find that certain biases are amplified by training on LLM-generated data: LLMs trained to label based on artificial
task-labeling data
have markedly amplified biases toward majority opinions, sometimes failing to represent minority opinions entirely (see experiments in §
5.2
).

In light of these findings, we argue that practitioners should proceed cautiously when using artificial data and outline possible mitigation strategies to avoid the above issues in Section
10
.

5
Artifacts in Task Labels

Our research examines the use of synthetic task labels to determine whether they fail to capture minority labels adequately. We demonstrate that responses from LLMs are often uniform when dealing with controversial topics, unlike the diverse range of human responses, and they tend to represent majority labels disproportionately. Our studies reveal that these biases become more pronounced when employing synthetic task labels for training classifiers.

Related Work

Supervised learning, heavily reliant on accurately annotated training data, faces high costs and challenges
Wang et al. 2021a
, leading to increased interest in using LLMs for annotations. These LLM annotations are risky as they may introduce more noise than human labels
Ding et al. 2022
. Key metrics for evaluation include accuracy, cost, efficiency, and, increasingly, diversity and subjective variance, acknowledging the need to understand diverse groups and provide personalized responses
Wang et al. 2021b
;
Chan 2022
;
Sampath et al. 2022
;
Gordon et al. 2022
;
Lowmanstone et al. 2023
. Research indicates annotator demographics significantly impact responses, addressing biases and ethical concerns
Wan et al. 2023
;
Röttger et al. 2021
;
Santy et al. 2023
. Variance in task labels, once seen as noise, is now viewed as indicative of systematic and social issues
Fleisig et al. 2023
;
Biester et al. 2022
. LLMs, however, tend to follow a “ground truth” during training
Aher et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023a
;
McKenna et al. 2023
, leading to skewed decisions, especially in moral judgment tasks
Dillion et al. 2023
where they struggle to reflect individuals’ socio-cultural nuances.

Our research explores if task annotations generated by LLMs
overlook minority opinions
, whether there’s a tendency to favor majority labels, and how this
bias might intensify
with training on artificial labels.

Our methodology involves two setups:

Our study examines whether LLM-annotated datasets accurately reflect minority opinions, particularly in subjective situations. To do this, we first measure the agreement between human and model annotations in two scenarios: one comparing predictions of majority labels and another for minority labels. We calculate the
match score
as the ratio of matching annotations to the total samples and visually illustrate the distributional differences to highlight disparities between these two contexts. We assess the
variation

In our
label-flipping

to explore these annotation differences and reveal potential biases in the selected texts.

Summary

We summarize our major artifacts found in artificial task label data.

•

In tasks involving high subjectivity, LLMs such as ChatGPT show a strong inclination towards majority opinions, leading to a notable discrepancy in preserving minority opinions in LLM-generated task labels.

•

ChatGPT exhibits less variability than humans in subjective tasks, often aligning with consensus but lacking in capturing diverse perspectives, suggesting possible oversimplification.

6
Artifacts in Preferences

We detail our experiments that indicate that LLM evaluations, particularly focusing on pair-wise preference, adhere more closely to rudimentary heuristics than humans evaluate. The results suggest that human preferences may be more complex and nuanced than those of LLMs. We also find misalignment in human preferences for models trained on LLM preferences.

Related Work

Preference data are used to align LLMs with human reasoning
Bai et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
, yet collecting human preference faces subjectivity and cost challenges. Synthetic preferences generated by LLMs are an alternative that has shown promise in complex tasks
Kocmi and Federmann 2023
;
Liu et al. 2023b
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Zhuo 2023
;
Bai et al. 2023
and aligned with expert human preferences
Zheng et al. 2023
. At the same time, there is a notable misalignment between human rationales and LLM judgments in question-answering tasks, attributed to biases in LLMs when evaluating text quality. For example, studies have identified various biases in LLMs like GPT-4, including position, verbosity, self-enhancement, egocentric, and other types of cognitive biases
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023a
;
Koo et al. 2023
.

We investigate whether LLM preferences are more closely aligned to basic heuristics (called
locality biases
, such as specific lexicons and entailment) than human preferences are.

Data

We first use
P2C

Kim et al. 2023
, a preference dataset for textual sentiment on 5,000 sentence pairs with human and GPT-3 preference responses.
In addition, we analyze
CoBBLEr

Koo et al. 2023
, a dataset containing 50 closed QA pairs with human and 15 different LLMs’ preference responses.

Justification
We chose these datasets to include the paired human and LLM preferences in evaluating text quality.

6.1.1
Locality Biases to Sentiment Lexicons

We use a lexicon-based heuristic approach in our study on sentiment preference in
P2C
. This involves analyzing stylistic elements through sentiment annotations from
Hayati et al. 2021
and calculating a lexical score for each sentence pair. We identify key lexicons linked to specific emotions: for positive sentences, we look for terms related to
joy
,
politeness
, and
positive sentiment
, while for negative ones, we focus on lexicons associated with
anger
,
disgust
,
fear
,
sadness
,
offensiveness
,
rudeness
, and
negative sentiment
. The highest-scoring lexicon in each sentence indicates its most expressive element for the intended sentiment. We then compare the scores within each sentence pair to determine which sentence more strongly represents the sentiment, designating it as the "winner." This process is repeated across various emotional styles within the same sentiment category, and the sentence that consistently scores highest is considered the preferred one, in what we call “lexicon-based preference.” We calculate Pearson’s
r
correlation to compare the alignment between lexicon-based preferences and humans’ original preferences and LLMs’ original preferences. Additionally, we examine how LLM and human preferences differ when evaluated against lexicon-based preferences. This analysis identifies if LLMs focus more on prominent, localized sentence features affecting their preferences than humans. To deepen our understanding, we organize the dataset by the disparity in lexicon/entailment scores between sentence pairs, dividing it into percentile groups of 10% each. For instance, the [1-10]% percentile group includes sentences with lexicon perception/entailment differences in the top 10% of the dataset, while the [90-100]% group includes those in the bottom 10%. This method allows us to observe the correlation within these specific segments.

Findings:
Our results show that while GPT-3 preferences positively correlate with the simple lexical preference, human preferences do not (see Figure
32
in Appendix
E
). This suggests that GPT-3 takes a more surface-level approach to sentiment preference than humans do.

We also examine how the correlations between preferences and the computed lexical-based preferences vary with different magnitudes of lexical score discrepancy in the sentence pairs, as depicted in Figure
11(a)
. To do this, we divide the paired sentences into ten percentiles based on the magnitude of their lexical score discrepancy. For example, the [1-10] group represents the top 10% of pairs of sentences, showing the greatest difference in the pair’s lexical scores.

Findings:
The top percentile group, with the greatest difference in the pair’s lexical scores, shows lexical-based preferences are strongly positively correlated with GPT-3’s preferences, which is not the case for human preferences. In contrast, with the smallest difference in the pair’s lexical scores, the bottom percentile group shows a negligible difference between GPT-3 and humans. This suggests that when lexical differences between sentences are very strong, GPT-3 preference decisions are noticeably influenced by the local information of sentences.

7
Artifacts in Instructions

We discuss synthetic instruction prompts and their propensity for including errors rather than admitting uncertainty or hedging with non-committal answers. We also provide a novel annotation of errors across synthetic dataset. Our experiments illustrate the negative effects of these errors on the training outcomes for instruction-tuned models.

Related Work

Instruction prompts are used to refine the performance of LLMs, particularly for understanding and executing complex instructions. The purpose is to make LLMs more adept at handling diverse and unpredictable user requests. However, creating datasets of instructions is challenging, because it is difficult to construct a comprehensive set of complex and diverse instructions. For this reason, multiple synthetic instruction prompt resources have been created
Taori et al. 2023
;
Honovich et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022b
;
Peng et al. 2023
.

These synthetic datasets often mirror the styles and outputs of their parent LLM, lacking comprehensive documentation about what task types are included. In contrast, human-crafted datasets
Khashabi et al. 2020
;
Muennighoff et al. 2022
;
Lai et al. 2023
;
Sanh et al. 2021
;
Mishra et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2022a
;
Conover et al. 2023
show a wider range of distinct task types. This limitation is further exacerbated by the LLMs’ focus on superficial patterns and output formats
(
Kung and Peng 2023
)
, which can lead to a narrow scope of learning and adaptability in handling diverse real-world scenarios. Additionally, the high error rates in instructions generated by LLMs raise concerns about the reliability and accuracy of the models trained on these datasets (
Honovich et al. 2022
and
Wang et al. 2023b
both report error rates close to 50% for their respective instruction datasets).

We aim to further the understanding of
task diversity and errors in synthetically created instruction datasets
, as well as how these affect instruction-tuned model performance.

Data

We examine four synthetic instruction datasets, including

Honovich et al. 2022
,

Wang et al. 2023b
,
Alpaca-Cleaned

Taori et al. 2023

Peng et al. 2023
. We also rely on four human-written instruction datasets for comparison and evaluation, including
Flan 2021

Wei et al. 2022
,
Dolly

Conover et al. 2023
,
Supernatural Instructions

Wang et al. 2022a
, and

Ni et al. 2023
.

Artifacts in Simulation

We examine how simulated agents exhibit role-swapping behavior and the specific situations triggering this phenomenon. Our analysis reveals distinct differences between how these agents and humans approach problem-solving, with agents often veering off-topic to hinder effective resolution. We observe that the nature and impact of these digressions vary between human-like and agent-like dialogues, being more pronounced in the latter. Additionally, our findings indicate that role-flipping in agents typically occurs in response to confusion or uncertainty and is more likely in longer conversations with frequent interruptions and varying personas.

Related Work

LLMs have become a fundamental component in AI-Agent systems, with their ability to interact and cooperate efficiently under given prompts. When multiple agents based on these models engage in dialogues, their effectiveness in handling complex, knowledge-intensive tasks is notably enhanced. These interactions, termed “simulations,” mirror human collaborative efforts. Recent studies
Li et al. 2023a
;
Wang et al. 2023c
;
Chan et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023
have explored the use of multiple agents in simulations to tackle specific tasks, focusing on performance enhancement over the quality of the simulation itself. However, these simulations can exhibit vulnerabilities, such as unintended artifacts arising from their lengthy and complex nature. Role-flipping, repetitive instructions, and inconsistent replies have been observed
(
Li et al. 2023a
)
in simulated conversations between agents. Despite the emerging significance of simulations, there’s limited research on these artifacts.

We contribute to the evolving understanding of simulated data by thoroughly analyzing the issues of artificial simulation, such as role-flipping and digression from context, and identifying new types of artifacts.

Data

We utilized
CAMEL AI-Society

(
Li et al. 2023a
)
and
Solo Performance Prompting Grid-World (SPP)

(
Wang et al. 2023c
)
. These datasets are typical for two different kinds of simulated agent conversations: The agents are assigned distinct (1) roles, which define their expected function and contribution within the conversation, and (2) personas, which infuse individuality and diverse perspectives based on unique backgrounds and characteristics.

Justification
In these datasets, the agent simulations (conversations) are part of broader prompting frameworks, where agents follow instructions to emulate assigned personas or perform tasks. Thus, we expect artifacts to emerge when these agents inaccurately adhere to their directives.

Artifacts in Free-Form Text

We examine how high-level attributes, particularly discourse structures and textual styles, differ between machine- and human-written texts. Our experiments suggest that human discourse structures vary more substantially with domain than machines do and that machine texts handle context and socio-emotional content more superficially than human texts. We show how these differences can be related to classifier performance when machine-generated texts are used in the training pipeline.

Related Work

Collecting human-authored texts that fit particular desired attributes, domains, or topics is highly resource-intensive. Recent research investigates using these machine-authored texts to augment or replace human-authored texts, e.g. so that the texts can be used to train sequence classifiers

9.1.2
Discourse analysis

In our analysis, we apply a discourse parser
(
Liu et al. 2021
)
to human-machine text pairs, generating a hierarchical discourse tree for each document based on the Rhetorical Structure Theory
(
Mann and Thompson 1987
)
. This tree starts with leaf nodes known as elemental discourse units (EDUs), typically sentence phrases, connected by relations like "Elaboration" or "Evaluation." The tree structure captures increasingly complex relationships, from phrases to sentences and paragraphs, culminating in a single root node encompassing all substructures.

We interpret these discourse trees as recursive hypergraphs in graph theory, enabling standard graph analyses. This includes examining network motifs – small, directed subgraphs with discourse relation-labeled edges- which are fundamental to larger graphs. For instance, Figure
22
illustrates certain motifs of size three that distinctly appear in either machine-generated or human texts, with edges representing discourse relations and nodes representing either EDUs or hyperedge nodes containing multiple subgraphs. We compare motif distributions to discern meaningful discourse structure patterns and differences in document construction.

For each document graph
G
, we count occurrences of specific motifs or subgraphs
M
, normalizing these counts by the graph’s size (the number of edges
|E|
). These normalized counts are then aggregated and averaged across various domains to assess the prevalence and significance of these motifs.

Figure 22:

Findings:
Our discourse results, shown in Figure

In general, the more formal the domain, the texts show more motifs that contain converging hyperedges that denote the linking of a lower representation to a higher one (i.e., phrases to sentences, sentences to paragraphs, etc.). We see less of those in texts from the casual domains authored by humans.
For the “Yelp” domain, we observe human-written texts talk more about “Temporal” and “Evaluation” relations while machine-written texts talk more about a place’s general “Background”. 10
Overall Discussion

This section details key findings and discusses the limitations of our study, offering suggestions for more effective use of artificial data and alternative evaluation methods for each type of LLM-generated data. The section concludes with a call to action directed at creators of AI benchmark datasets.

10.1
Summary of Major Findings

The following are the main findings of our study:

•

LLMs, when tasked with generating text for specific contexts, tend to apply textual styles unnaturally and simplistically
.
As observed in scenarios like composing “positive Tweets,” where LLMs predominantly use
joy
, despite the provided human examples exhibiting a broader range of emotions. Moreover, unlike humans who adapt discourse patterns to different domains, LLMs often recycle the same patterns across various contexts, suggesting a superficial grasp of the interplay between discourse, textual style, and context. (Section
9.1
)

•

We find several cases where artificial data (i.e., task labels) diverges from human data
. The divergence in LLM performance is frequently attributed to their limited, superficial comprehension of tasks, as evidenced by their consistent overrepresentation of majority opinions across all datasets, including those with highly subjective content. Additionally, LLMs struggle to align with minority viewpoints and exhibit less variability in annotations for subjective tasks than humans, indicating a lack of understanding about the controversial nature of certain labeling tasks and a tendency to mirror only majority responses overconfidently. (Section
5.1
)

•

LLM overconfidence significantly affects the quality of artificial data
.
Our manual review of over 500 artificial instructions from various datasets revealed more than 100 errors, particularly in closed questions, yet not a single instance where the LLM acknowledged uncertainty about the correct output. (Section
7.1
)

•

Our findings indicate that LLMs demonstrate a shallow approach to preference tasks for evaluation
.
The LLMs often focus on a sentence’s most salient linguistic cues, showing a locality bias, unlike humans, who exhibit only a minor correlation and seem to consider several additional factors.(Section
6.1
)

•

LLM agents frequently lose track of their assigned roles and stray from the topic of conversation
, often after apologizing for confusion, suggesting their inability to navigate challenging conversational shifts adeptly.(Section
8.1
)

•

Our findings indicate that the differences between human and artificial data are consequential, impacting performance when used for training as compared to comparable human data
(Sections
6.2
,
7.2
and
9.2
). Further, biases in artificial data may be amplified in models that are trained on that artificial data (Section
5.2
).

10.2
Suggestions for Better Use of Artificial Data

Based on our study’s findings and lessons learned, we provide practical guidelines and helpful suggestions for those seeking to use LLM-generated artificial data in AI research and development.

•

Free-Form Texts
:

–

Mitigate the “curse of generality” in language models by
incorporating domain-specific information
during text generation, using few-shot prompting with relevant examples.

–

Enhance discourse structure modeling or adjust decoding strategies
to improve the human-like quality of LLM-generated texts, such as re-ranking candidates based on high-level metrics.

–

Moreover, in the evaluation of LLM-generated texts, it could be beneficial to
incorporate high-level features
such as the
“formality score,”

“extent of label flipping,”
and
“discourse motif distribution,”
in conjunction with conventional metrics like ROUGE, BLEU, or METEOR. This approach enhances the comprehensiveness of the evaluation, enabling a more nuanced assessment of the resemblance between human-written and LLM-generated texts within a given domain.

•

Task Labels
: Majority voting, commonly used for generating task labels, tends to overlook minority views and disagreements among annotators, particularly in highly subjective tasks.

–

Rather than evaluating LLM task labels solely on cost, speed, and accuracy of annotation, we could also use some
first-order analysis methods
like
label distributional difference
or an
analysis of the variation in individual text annotations
to highlight discrepancies between LLM and human-generated labels. It’s crucial to validate LLMs with human datasets to avoid data biases and falsely inflated performance metrics, underscoring the importance of establishing clear guidelines for various tasks.

•

Instructions
: We found that artificial datasets contain various types of errors and that artificially adding errors to fine-tuning data resulted in worse performance.

–

We recommend that the reader mitigate these issues by closely
inspecting data for errors
, performing automatic preprocessing, and
generating diverse data
.

–

Also, researchers may want to consider creating datasets following observed differences between Open-QA and Closed-QA instructions and using diversity-promoting techniques like
criteria-based prompting

Hayati et al. 2023
for robust and diverse instruction-tuned models.

•

Preference
: LLMs show a stronger tendency to show locality bias or be sensitive to local linguistic cues of texts that are more salient than humans when judging their preferences.

–

We alert researchers to carefully use LLM-generated preferences by first detecting
differences between human and LLM preferences
and providing detailed
guidelines in prompts to include a more nuanced understanding of sentences
rather than focusing on specific locally salient features.

–

Improving the quality of LLM-generated preferences is crucial to reduce misalignment issues and ensure better alignment with human preferences.

•

Simulation
: The burgeoning interest in AI-simulated data generated by LLM agents across diverse applications has brought to light the relatively unexplored artifacts inherent in this novel dataset type. To scrutinize these subtleties, our approach involves assessing phenomena like
role-flipping
and
digression
in simulated conversations.

–

To address these artifacts’ risks, we advocate for
judicious human intervention in detecting such issues
, as identified in our findings, instead of uncritically relying on the simulated data.

General Advice:

As LLMs become more widespread, ensuring that artificial datasets closely mirror human language attributes is crucial for models to accurately, nuancedly, and appropriately understand and generate language. Utilizing human-generated or hybrid data, like human-annotated LLM data or LLM-enhanced human data, is recommended whenever feasible. For example, we can start by utilizing small but high-quality (adversarial) data in building a more challenging evaluation set. Such hybrid data should capture the richness and complexity of human language, including diverse dialects, sociolects, and domain-specific jargon while adhering to ethical norms and avoiding biased representations. The closer LLM-generated data aligns with human-produced data, the more effective and relevant it becomes for practical uses in conversational systems, content generation, and language comprehension tasks.

10.3
Call for Action to Creators of AI Benchmark

In addition to striving to provide high-quality data that is well-aligned with human needs, we urge dataset creators and curators to document their data following
Gebru et al. 2021
and
Pushkarna et al. 2022
to prioritize transparency, including detailed metadata that clearly describes the source, collection methodology, and any processing or filtering the data has undergone. To address the ethical concerns outlined in Section
1
, it is important to document the extent to which the dataset represents diverse perspectives, demographics, languages, and cultural contexts. Furthermore, creators and curators should acknowledge and describe any potential ethical concerns related to the data, including privacy issues, consent for data use, and the potential for misuse, accompanied by guidelines for ethical use; provide guidance on the intended use cases for the dataset and its limitations; and provide a comprehensive analysis of known biases or errors within the dataset and document mitigation attempts.

This is increasingly significant in light of the increasing reliance on artificial data for continuously training LLMs. The prioritization of artificial data, with its efficiency and scalability, signals a concerning abdication of responsibility in how we educate these models about the world. By prioritizing the efficiency and scalability of artificial data, we risk creating a feedback loop where LLMs are primarily informed by outputs derived from their kind rather than the rich, varied, nuanced, and even conflicting perspectives that human-generated content offers. This shift potentially narrows the worldview and understanding these models develop, as they are less exposed to the diversity of human experiences, cultural contexts, and the subtleties of human thought and language. The consequence is a homogenization of knowledge and a possible reinforcement of existing biases, as the artificial data is inherently shaped by the limitations and predispositions of the models that generate it. By leaning heavily on artificial data, we relinquish the crucial role of human guidance and oversight in teaching LLMs, diminishing the breadth and depth of their understanding of the real, human world they are meant to serve and reflect.

Lastly, we encourage the reader to explore recent work on the process of data documentation.
Miceli et al. 2022
’s research underlines the need for a shift from merely documenting datasets to documenting data production, emphasizing the value of designing data documentation based on the needs of data workers and viewing documentation as a boundary object across organizations. Furthermore,
Heger et al. 2022
’s findings indicate that current data documentation approaches are often ad hoc and myopic, underscoring the need for adaptable frameworks integrated into existing tools and workflows and automated wherever possible.

10.4
Limitations

In our research, we recognize several limitations inherent to our approach. Our study aims to provide initial observations on the quality and impact of data from LLMs rather than definitive conclusions. We acknowledge the challenge of unpredictable outputs, which is typical in LLM research. Our paper explores a wide range of LLM-generated data, and the complexity involved in exhaustively controlling all models, prompting techniques, and hyperparameters is not feasible given the funding and computational constraints. We do not generate most of the datasets ourselves and instead use publicly available, heterogeneous datasets. We emphasize that in this context, our results should be viewed as describing a subset of possible outcomes from LLM-generated data, rather than a definitive view of the complete scope of LLM capabilities over all existing models, prompting strategies, and hyperparameters. Our main goal is to highlight the disparities between LLM and human outputs and emphasize the ethical aspects of LLM data, prioritizing these over rigid experimental protocols.

We also focus on LLM-generated text data relevant to NLP applications, a scope defined by practical considerations given LLMs’ broad and evolving landscape. This focus, while pragmatic, may introduce bias and limit the breadth of our study, as it doesn’t cover all categories of artificial data or the full spectrum of state-of-the-art LLMs. Furthermore, our research does not address all possible domains or task types, which may affect the generalizability of our findings.

We utilize human validation and qualitative analysis as stress-testing methods in our analysis. These are crucial in assessing aspects like creativity and bias and are subject to the subjective interpretations of evaluators, despite our efforts to mitigate this through multiple annotators. Our artifact analysis, reliant on a deep understanding of LLM mechanics and the underlying data, faces challenges in identifying subtle, context-dependent artifacts.

Additionally, our experiments do not fully incorporate the latest LLM methodologies, like tailored prompting or chain of thought techniques. Furthermore, due to limited computing resources, we did not experiment with resource scale; future work should explore whether increasing the sizes of training datasets yields comparable gains between human- and LLM-generated data. The reliance on existing LLM-generated datasets and the potentially non-representative nature of the human-generated data used for comparison introduces variability and potential inconsistencies. These choices, made to balance feasibility with relevance, are transparently presented to provide a comprehensive understanding of the scope and implications of our findings.

11
Conclusion

Our extensive research into using LLMs for data generation has unveiled key insights and challenges, notably the significant gaps between LLM-generated and human-generated data, especially in intricate and subjective tasks. Despite LLMs like ChatGPT showing adeptness in emulating human-like outputs in various tasks, they often falter in capturing the subtleties of humor, sarcasm, and minority viewpoints and in mirroring the complexity of human language structures and styles. These discrepancies highlight the crucial need to address the inherent limitations and biases in LLM outputs.

Our contributions include systematically aggregating diverse LLM-generated data and then performing stress testing to highlight artifacts and discrepancies, and underscoring the need for ethical data generation and usage practices. We provide targeted recommendations for enhancing data quality in areas like free-form texts, task labels, instructions, preferences, and simulations. Our findings stress the importance of closely aligning LLM-generated data with human characteristics to ensure its effectiveness and relevance in real-world applications, promoting human-generated or hybrid data for more reliable and pertinent LLM outcomes. This study calls for dataset creators to adopt transparent, ethical, and inclusive practices in data documentation and curation, which is vital for advancing LLM technology.

The discrepancies observed in LLM-generated data emphasize the need to confront these challenges to bolster the dependability of such datasets in model development. Our suggestions aim to bridge these gaps, enhancing the trustworthiness of LLM-generated data while prioritizing ethical and conscientious practices.

While our research has examined various LLM-generated data types, it is possible that further artifacts exists that were not detected by our stress tests. Therefore, we advocate for further exploration of these yet-unseen discrepancies as a future research direction within the NLP community. Continued research in this area will deepen our understanding of LLM-generated data and lead to refined methods for identifying and addressing concealed artifacts, an essential step for advancing NLP and ensuring that the deployment of LLMs adheres to ethical standards and sound research methodologies.

C.2
Second-order Experiments

In addition to observing how minority opinions were further underestimated in the second order, we experimented with how the label order of the training data affects this behavior. Here, we focus specifically on the difference between the shuffled and sorted (smallest to largest) variations. We hypothesize that ordering each set of labels in a specific way will decrease the distribution difference between the gold and predicted label since each classification head on the RoBERTa-base model will only be required to guess a specific range of possible labels. The first classification head will only need to predict the smallest gold label, the second classification head the second-smallest gold label, and so on. In contrast, the model trained on shuffled labels will have to ensure that the distribution of labels individually chosen by each classification head is consistent with the distribution of the gold labels. We find that datasets with a much higher most-common to the second-most-common ratio (See Table
16
) show no difference between the sorted and shuffled training performance. This is because the skew creates an environment where it’s beneficial for the model to continue guessing the most frequent label, even if it means lowering the accuracy for minority options. However, we see the beneficial effects of the different orderings in Figures
30
and
31
.

Experimental Setup

Regarding
role-flipping
, we detect the string "Instruction:" and "Solution:" in the message. If "Instruction:" appears in AI-User’s message or "Solution:" appears in AI-Assistant’s message, we consider there is no role-flipping. If "Solution:" appears in AI-User’s message or "Instruction:" appears in AI-Assistant’s message, we consider role-flipping happens. If one message contains both, we consider there is no role-flipping, and if one message contains none of them, we consider it an interruption message. In the CAMEL framework, detecting role-flipping relies on identifying specific strings like "Instruction:" or "Solution:" within messages since agents are expected to follow this format; however, this check is flexible enough to allow for additional elements like brief responses or stop tokens without disrupting the conversation flow.
Conversations deviating from this pattern are marked as “Interruption Messages,” often characterized by irrelevant content such as apologies or non-constructive praise, and our analysis includes comparing the length distribution of conversations with role-flipping to the entire dataset, as well as exploring the relationship between role-flipped and interruption messages in simulations.

Here, we used the specifically designed prompts for each purpose: (1) detecting digression, (2) measuring the collaborative effect, (3) classifying given conversation into human-like and agent-like ones, (4) classifying the reasoning of digression into one of the multiple categories.

[1] Correlation between Role-Flipping and Interruption Messages

Our study of the CAMEL AI-World dataset identified "interruption messages" in simulated conversations, which diverge from the expected dialogue format, often beginning with apologies or shifting to simple responses like praise. These interruptions, found in 26.12% of conversations, often precede "role-flipping" which occurs in 8.22% of conversations with interruptions, a rate that is higher than in the overall dataset (2.94%). Notably, in 88.8% of cases with both interruption and role-flipping, the first interruption comes before role-flipping, suggesting that interruptions might trigger this behavior, particularly as role-flipping is exclusively observed with the AI-User, indicating that confusion caused by the AI-Assistant’s responses could be a significant factor in subsequent role-flipping.
