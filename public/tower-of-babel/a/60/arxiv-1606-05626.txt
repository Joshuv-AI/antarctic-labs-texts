The complexity of simulating local measurements on quantum systems
Sevag Gharibian, Justin Yirka

Abstract
An important task in quantum physics is the estimation of local quantities for ground states of local Hamiltonians. Recently, [Ambainis, CCC 2014] defined the complexity class P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} , and motivated its study by showing that the physical task of estimating the expectation value of a local observable against the ground state of a local Hamiltonian is P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} -complete. In this paper, we continue the study of P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} , obtaining the following lower and upper bounds. Lower bounds (hardness results): • The P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} -completeness result of [Ambainis, CCC 2014] requires O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) -local observables and Hamiltonians. We show that simulating even a single qubit measurement on ground states of 5 5 -local Hamiltonians is P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} -complete, resolving an open question of Ambainis. • We formalize the complexity theoretic study of estimating two-point correlation functions against ground states, and show that this task is similarly P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} -complete. • We identify a flaw in [Ambainis, CCC 2014] regarding a P UQMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]} -hardness proof for estimating spectral gaps of local Hamiltonians. By introducing a “query validation” technique, we build on [Ambainis, CCC 2014] to obtain P UQMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]} -hardness for estimating spectral gaps under polynomial-time Turing reductions. Upper bounds (containment in complexity classes): • P QMA ​ [ log ] \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]} is thought of as “slightly harder” than QMA. We justify this formally by exploiting the hierarchical voting technique of [Beigel, Hemachandra, Wechsung, SCT 1989] to show P QMA ​ [ log ] ⊆ PP \textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}\subseteq\textup{PP} . This improves the containment QMA ⊆ PP \textup{QMA}\subseteq\textup{PP} [Kitaev, Watrous, STOC 2000]. This work contributes a rigorous treatment of the subtlety involved in studying oracle classes in which the oracle solves a promise problem. This is particularly relevant for quantum complexity theory, where most natural classes such as BQP and QMA are defined as promise classes.

Simulating local measurements on low-temperature quantum systems

remains open. For the cases of NP
[
60

or MA and QCMA
[
4
]

Upper bounds (containment in complexity classes).

As mentioned earlier, since both QMA and co-QMA are contained in
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
, it is likely that
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
is strictly more powerful than both QMA and co-QMA. This raises the question:
What is an upper bound on the power of
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
?
Two points of reference are worth mentioning here. First, the classical analogue of
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
,
\textup{P}^{\textup{NP}[\textup{log}]}
, is known to be upper bounded by PP
[
9
]
. Here, PP is the set of promise problems solvable in probabilistic polynomial time with
unbounded
error; in other words, a PP machine accepts YES instances with probability strictly larger than
1/2
and accepts NO instances with probability less than or equal to
1/2
. The second point of reference is that
QMA
is bounded by
PP

[
44
,
49
]
(
[
61
]
actually shows the slightly stronger containment
\textup{QMA}\subseteq\textup{A}_{0}\textup{PP}
). We thus ask whether, like its classical analogue,
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
can bounded by PP, and so is “slightly harder” than
QMA
. Indeed, we show this is the case.

Theorem 1.4
.

\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}\subseteq\textup{PP}
.

Thus,
\textup{QMA}\subseteq\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}\subseteq\textup{PP}
, which rigorously justifies the intuition that
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
should be thought of as “slightly harder” than
QMA
.

As mentioned in Sections

The error in the second claim (Claim 2 of

To overcome this, we introduce a technique of “query validation”: given a query to the

Upper bounds.

We now move to our upper bound result, which is the most technically involved. To show
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}\subseteq\textup{PP}
(Theorem
1.4
), we exploit the technique of
hierarchical voting
, used by Beigel, Hemachandra, and Wechsung
[
9
]
to show
\textup{P}^{\textup{NP}[\textup{log}]}\subseteq\textup{PP}
, in conjunction with the QMA strong amplification results of Marriott and Watrous
[
49
]
.

To give some intuition as to how the technique works, we sketch its application in the classical case of
\textup{P}^{\textup{NP}[\textup{log}]}

[
9
]
. There, we have a P machine making queries to an NP oracle for SAT (i.e. the
i
-th query feeds the oracle some SAT formulae
\phi_{i}
), and the goal is to simulate this by a PP machine. Since the PP machine does not have access to an NP oracle, the best it can do is
guess
the answer to each query; so, it begins by guessing the answers to each NP query by picking a random assignment
x_{i}
for each formula
\phi_{i}
in the hope that
\phi_{i}(x_{i})=1
. Of course, such a guess
x_{i}
can be satisfying only if
\phi_{i}
is satisfiable to begin with; with a bit of thought, this implies that the “correct” query string
y^{*}
(i.e. the
i
-th bit of
y^{*}
equals
1
if and only if
\phi_{i}
is satisfiable) is the lexicographically
largest
string
y^{*}
attainable by this guessing process.

Unfortunately, the PP machine might have a very small probability of guessing
y^{*}
; thus, it next “boosts” this probability via several rounds of “hierarchical voting”. Conceptually, this technique does not actually
increase
the probability of outputting
y^{*}
but rather decreases the probability of outputting other, lexicographically smaller query strings
y^{\prime}\neq y^{*}
. This works because the machine is a PP machine (i.e. it can have unbounded error), and so it suffices to reduce the probability of obtaining any such
y^{\prime}
being output to be strictly smaller than the probability of
y^{*}
being output, in which case the PP machine is more likely to output the correct answer in its simulation of the
\textup{P}^{\textup{NP}[\textup{log}]}
computation than not, as needed.

We develop a quantum variant of this scheme which is quite natural. However, its analysis is markedly more involved than the classical setting due to both the probabilistic nature of QMA and the possibility of “invalid queries” violating the QMA promise gap. To give a sense of the problems which arise, for
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
it is no longer necessarily true that the lexicographically largest obtainable
y^{*}
is a correct query string.

1.6
Discussion and open questions

The problems studied here explore the line of research recently initiated by Ambainis
[
5
]
on
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
and focus on central problems for local Hamiltonian systems. The complexity theoretic study of such problems is appealing in that it addresses the original motivation of physicist Richard Feynman in proposing quantum computers
[
27
]
, who was interested in avenues for simulating quantum systems. Indeed, hardness results, such as Kitaev’s quantum Cook-Levin theorem, rigorously justify Feynman’s intuition that such simulation problems are hard. Our work (e.g. Theorem
1.1
) strongly supports this view by demonstrating that even some of the simplest and most natural simulation tasks, such as measuring a
single qubit (!)
of a ground state, can be harder than QMA. Our study of spectral gaps (Theorem
1.3
) further highlights another theme: the subtleties which must be carefully treated when studying oracle classes for promise problems (such as
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
). As quantum complexity theory commonly focuses on such promise problems, we believe this theme could be of interest to a broader computer science audience.

Moving to open questions, although we resolve one of the open questions from
[
5
]
, there are others we leave open, along with some new ones. Do our results for
APX-SIM
and
APX-2-CORR
hold for more restricted classes of Hamiltonians, such as
2
-local Hamiltonians, local Hamiltonians on a 2D lattice, or specific Hamiltonian models of interest (see e.g.
[
22
,
53
]
for QMA-completeness results for estimating ground state energies of the spin-
1/2
Heisenberg anti-ferromagnet)? Is
SPECTRAL-GAP

\textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]}
-complete or
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
-complete (recall
\textsc{SPECTRAL-GAP}\in\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
and that
[
5
]
and our work together show
\textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]}
-hardness)? What is the relationship between
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
and
\textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]}
? Finally, what is the complexity of other physical tasks “beyond” estimating ground state energies?

Remark added later:
Since the original preprint of this paper appeared, the present authors, together with Stephen Piddock, have partially answered the first open question above by showing
[
33
]
that APX-SIM remains
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
-complete for any family of local Hamiltonians which can simulate “spatially sparse”
[
51
]
Hamiltonians. This, in turn, implies (e.g. using
[
53
]
,
[
58
]
,
[
54
]
) that APX-SIM remains
\textup{P}^{\textup{QMA}[\textup{log}]}
-complete even on physically motivated models, such as the Heisenberg interaction on a 2D square lattice.

The first of these steps is performed identically to the proof of

[

[

). The verifier runs the QMA circuit for consistency, and assuming this check passes it uses the classical

∎

\textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]}
-hardness of

We now restate and prove Theorem

. We begin by defining

(

))

Definition 4.9

(Unique QMA (UQMA) (Aharonov

[

]
))

We say a promise problem

•

.

We begin by showing the following modified version of Lemma

tailored to UQMA (instead of QMA). It is crucial to observe that the mapping of Lemma

Henceforth, we assume that all calls to the UQMA oracle

Correctness.

Intuitively, the query validation phase roughly replaces each “sufficiently invalid” query

and

Case 2: Eigenvalues from the same query string.

•

Lemma 4.11
.

In Equation (
14

\epsilon-\delta

(YES case violating uniqueness promise) If

\lambda_{2}(H_{\mathcal{Y}_{i}}^{i,y_{1}\cdots y_{i-1}})<3\epsilon

G^{\prime}_{y_{1}\cdots y_{i-1}}
is at most
\epsilon-\delta
, or the spectral gap of

is least
\epsilon-\delta
.

Observe that
G^{\prime}_{y_{1}\cdots y_{i-1}}
is block-diagonal with respect to register
\mathcal{X}_{i}
; we will refer to each of these blocks as the
0

1

\lambda_{2}(X)
for an operator

.

Case 1.

Suppose first that
\lambda(H_{\mathcal{Y}_{i}}^{i,y_{1}\cdots y_{i-1}})=\epsilon+\delta
. Then, since the smallest eigenvalue in the

-block is
2\epsilon
, we have that

, and so the spectral gap of

Proof of Theorem

.

As done in
[

]
, we start with the Hamiltonian

from Equation (

[

We proceed as follows. Given an arbitrary
\textup{P}^{\textup{UQMA}[\textup{log}]}

-local

from Equation (

to within sufficiently small but fixed additive error

from Equation (

-local Hamiltonian

[

\operatorname{Pr}[Y\in A]-\operatorname{Pr}[Y\in B]-\operatorname{Pr}[Y\in C]\geq\left(\frac{1}{2^{q}}\right)^{(n^{c}-1)}\frac{1}{2^{Mm}}\left[1-\frac{1}{2^{m}}-\frac{m}{2^{p}}\right]-\frac{2^{m}}{2^{p}}.

For sufficiently large fixed
p
, this quantity is strictly positive, yielding Theorem
1.4
.
∎

Appendix A
Additional proofs

Lemma A.1
.

Assume the terminology of Lemma
4.3
. Then, there exists a valid history state
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
on
W
,
C
,
Q
, and
Q^{\prime}
such that
\left\lvert\mspace{1mu}\mbox{$\langle\psi|\psi^{\prime}\rangle$}\mspace{1mu}\right\rvert^{2}\geq 1-O(\gamma^{-2})
.

Proof.

Recall that the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
H_{1}=\Delta(H_{\rm in}+H_{\rm prop}+H_{\rm stab})
is at least
J:=\pi^{2}\Delta/(64L^{3})
(by Lemma
2.2
),
\delta=1/\Delta
, and
\eta\geq\max(\left\lVert\mspace{1mu}H_{2}\mspace{1mu}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{\infty}},1)=:K
. Lemma
4.1
now implies there exists a valid history state
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mspace{1mu}\mbox{$\langle\psi|\psi^{\prime}\rangle$}\mspace{1mu}\right\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{K+\sqrt{K^{2}+\delta(J-2K)}}{J-2K}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{\eta+\sqrt{\eta^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{64L^{3}}}}{\frac{\pi^{2}\Delta}{64L^{3}}-2\eta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})\eta}{\frac{\pi^{2}\Delta}{64L^{3}}-2\eta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\left(\frac{(1+\sqrt{2})64L^{3}\eta}{\pi^{2}\Delta-128L^{3}\eta}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\right),

where the second inequality follows since
0\leq K\leq\eta
, the third since
\pi^{2}/(64L^{3})\leq 1
for
L\geq 1
and
1\leq\eta^{2}
, and the last since
\Delta=128L^{3}\eta\gamma
and
\gamma\geq 1
.
∎
