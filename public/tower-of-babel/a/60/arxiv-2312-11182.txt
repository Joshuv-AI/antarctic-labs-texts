Abstract
The regularity of refinable functions has been analysed in an extensive literature and is well-understood in two cases: 1) univariate 2) multivariate with an isotropic dilation matrix. The general (non-isotropic) case offered a great resistance. It was done only recently by developing the matrix method. In this paper we make the next step and extend the Littlewood-Paley type method, which is very efficient in the aforementioned special cases, to general equations with arbitrary dilation matrices. This gives formulas for the higher order regularity in W 2 k ​ ( ℝ n ) W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}) by means of the Perron eigenvalue of a finite-dimensional linear operator on a special cone. Applying those results to recently introduced tile B-splines, we prove that they can have a higher smoothness than the classical ones of the same order. Moreover, the two-digit tile B-splines have the minimal support of the mask among all refinable functions of the same order of approximation. This proves, in particular, the lowest algorithmic complexity of the corresponding subdivision schemes. Examples and numerical results are provided. Keywords: multivariate wavelets, frames, refinable function, Hölder regularity, modulus of continuity, local regularity, dilation matrix, transition matrix, joint spectral radius, invariant polytope algorithm Classification (MSCS): 65D17, 15A60, 39A99

Introduction

1.1. Refinement equations with matrix dilation

Refinement equations are difference functional equations with
an integer contraction of the argument. They play a crucial role in the
construction of wavelets in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and of the subdivision
algorithms in the approximation theory and geometrical design, see surveys
in
[
2
,
4
,
37
,
38
]
.
They are also applied to random power series
[
16
,
28
,
40
]
,
combinatorial number theory
[
19
,
42
]
, discrete geometry (tilings)
[
20
,
32
]
, etc. We consider the multivariate equation of the form:

\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})\quad=\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\,c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\varphi(M{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{k}})\,,\qquad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(1)

where the dilation matrix
M
is integer and expanding, i.e., has integer entries and
all eigenvalues bigger than one by modulo. We restrict ourselves to equations with
a finite summation, i.e.,
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0
if
{\boldsymbol{k}}\notin Q
, where
Q
is a finite nonempty
subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. We also make the standard assumption
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,=\,m,
where
m=|\det M|
.
In this case, equation (
1
) possesses a unique, up to multiplication by a constant,
solution
\varphi
(
refinable function
) in the space
{\mathcal{S}}_{0}^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
of compactly-supported tempered distributions.
In most of applications this solution needs to be at least from
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
Often a higher smoothness is required.
Let us note that
\varphi
is never infinitely smooth (in
[
50
]
this was proved for
more general equations).

In the univariate case (
n=1
) the
smoothness of refinable functions
has been studied in great detail. Two methods
are usually most prominent. The matrix approach
[
6
,
5
,
15
,
36
,
41
]
finds the biggest
k

such that
\varphi\in C^{k}({\mathbb{R}})
and computes the precise value of the
Hölder exponent in this space. This method requires computation of the joint spectral radius of special matrices
\{T_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
, which is a hard problem even in low
dimensions.
Recent tools elaborated in
[
21
,
22
,
34
]

can do this for matrices of non very large sizes, which makes it possible to
compute the regularity for univariate
equation
\varphi(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\,c_{k}\varphi(2x-k)
of order
N\leq 25
. The matrix method is also applicable to
compute the regularity in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}})

[
33
]
and in the Sobolev spaces
W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}})

(of absolutely continuous functions
f
such that
f^{(k)}\in L_{2}
). In this case the joint spectral
radius computation can be replaced by computing the usual spectral radius of one matrix
T
,
although, of quite a big size, around
\frac{1}{2}\,N^{2}
, see
[
39
]
.

The second approach is usually referred to as the
Sobolev regularity computation

or the
Littlewood-Paley type method
. It finds the regularity in
W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}})

by means of computing the Sobolev regularity defined as

\sup\,\bigl\{\beta\geq 0\,:\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|\varphi(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{\beta}\,dt\,<\,\infty\bigr\}
. This problem is reduced to finding the usual spectral radius of a matrix of a much lower size, around
2N

[
13
,
17
,
18
,
49
]
. The shortcoming of this
method is that it deals with the regularity not in
C
but in
L_{2}
.
Say, to prove the continuity
of
\varphi
, one needs to show that
\varphi\in W^{1}_{2}
. The matrix method and the
Littlewood-Paley type method are both
widely used.

For multivariate refinement equations, the situation is more complicated. The smoothness of solutions has been computed
only in the special case when the matrix
M
is
isotropic
, i.e.,
is similar to a diagonal matrix with equal by modulus eigenvalues. Isotropic matrices
expand all distances equally in all directions, i.e.,
the sequence
\{M^{k}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has the same asymptotic growth
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. In this case many results on the univariate equations
can be generalized to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In particular, both of the aforementioned methods work for isotropic dilations, although the
dimensions of the corresponding matrices
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m-1}
and
T
become much higher.
This was done in
[
4
,
6
,
12
,
13
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
30
,
45
]
.
If
M
is not isotropic, then none of the methods is applicable.
Already simple two-dimensional examples show that the formulas
for the Hölder exponent are not valid in the non-isotropic case.
Even some basic questions, for instance, whether or not
\varphi

possesses a derivative (say, from
C({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
or from
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
),
remain unanswered. Here it should be emphasized that
refinement equations with non-isotropic dilations are important in
many problems of signal processing, wavelets and geometric design, see
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
30
]
. In
[
2
,
7
,
14
,
47
]
the authors compute regularity in special anisotropic Hölder and Sobolev spaces.

It was not before 2019 that the modification of the matrix method
was elaborated for non-isotropic dilations
[
11
]
. This gave
closed formulas for the Hölder exponent and for higher order regularity
of general refinable functions by means of
joint spectral radii. However, because of the large size of the matrices
T_{i}
, this
method is applicable mostly for very low dimensions
n

and for small sets
Q
of nonzero coefficients
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.

An improvement would be achieved if one could make the next step: to modify the
Littlewood-Paley type method for non-isotropic matrices. In this paper we make this step.
We obtain a sharp criterion which, for an arbitrary dilation matrix
M
,
ensures that the solution of refinement equation (
1
) belongs to
W^{k}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

and presents a formula for its Hölder exponent. This formula is different and more
complicated than one for the isotropic case, although, for many equations, it can be simplified. Which is most important, it allows us to solve the smoothness problem in higher dimensions
n
and
for larger sets
Q
. Numerical results in Section
7

demonstrate that indeed the Littlewood-Paley method for computing the
L_{2}
-regularity
of multivariate refinement equations is more efficient than the matrix approach
because of a much smaller dimension of the problem of the spectral radius computation.

Then we apply this formula to
the tile B-splines and to the corresponding subdivision schemes.

1.2. The tile B-splines

In the second part of the paper we analyse the regularity of the tile B-splines
recently introduced in
[
51
]
. They are analogues of the
univariate cardinal B-splines. The cardinal B-spline of order
\ell\geq 0

is
B_{\ell}\,=\,\underbrace{\chi_{[0,1]}*\cdots*\chi_{[0,1]}}_{\ell+1}
,
it has integer nodes, is supported on
[0,\ell+1]
, and
belongs to
W_{2}^{\ell}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
In particular
B_{0}=\chi_{[0,1]}
. Cardinal B-splines have countless applications due to
their simplicity and many optimal properties (the smoothest refinable function of a given order, etc.) The multivariate B-splines are defined in the standard way as
direct products of univariate ones.
This approach, however, has significant shortcomings, especially in the construction of wavelets and Haar systems, see
[
5
,
7
,
30
]
for general discussion of this aspect. In contrast, the tile B-splines
are not obtained by direct products but by the convolutions:

\varphi_{\ell}\,=\,\chi_{G}*\cdots*\chi_{G}
, where

G
is a self-similar tile in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Here we need to recall some definitions.

Given an integer expanding
n\times n
matrix
M
, we take a
system of “digits”
D=\{{\boldsymbol{d}}_{0},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{d}}_{m-1}\}
, which are
m=|\det M|
arbitrary
representatives of the quotient group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}/M{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. The set
G
of
M
-adic
fractions:

G\ =\ \Bigl\{0.{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i_{1}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i_{2}}\cdots\ =\ \sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\,M^{-k}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i_{k}}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{d}}_{i_{k}}\in D\,,\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}\Bigr\}

is a
self-similar attractor
. This is a compact set of an integer positive Lebesgue measure
[
20
,
32
]
.
If its measure is equal to one, then the attractor is called
a tile
.
Integer shifts of a tile form a partition of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Moreover, a tile is self-similar: it is a union of several parallel shifts of the
set
M^{-1}G
. Its indicator
\varphi_{0}=\chi_{G}

satisfies the refinement equation
\varphi_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,=\,\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\varphi_{0}(M{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i})
.
In some sense, the tile is a unit segment in the
M
-adic system on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
The Haar systems in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with the dilation factor
M
can be obtained from the function
\chi_{G}

by the same procedure as the classical Haar system with the double dilation in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}})

is obtained from
\chi_{[0,1]}
.
To construct wavelet bases in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

one can naturally define the tile B-spline
\varphi_{\ell}
as
a convolution of
\ell+1
functions
\varphi_{0}
. Strictly speaking,
\varphi_{\ell}

is not a spline, since it is not a piecewise-polynomial function.
On the other hand,
\varphi_{\ell}
is smooth, generates algebraic
polynomials of degrees
\ \leq\ell
by its integer translates, and the values
\varphi_{\ell}({\boldsymbol{x}})

are easily computed in all
M
-adic points
{\boldsymbol{x}}

(from the corresponding refinement equation). The tile B-splines are useful in approximations and interpolations
as well as for constructing of multivariate wavelets and subdivision schemes
[
51
]
.

Applying our main results, we prove that some families of tile B-splines have
(quite surprisingly) a higher smoothness than the standard B-splines of the same order.
Note that nothing of this kind occurs in the univariate case, where
the cardinal B-spline is the smoothest refinable function of a given order.
This “super-smoothness” yields, in particular, that
the subdivision schemes defined by tile B-splines generate smoother surfaces and possess faster convergence.
Moreover, in Section
8
we prove the optimality of tile B-splines among
all refinable functions of a given order.

1.3. The tile subdivision schemes

The tile B-splines demonstrate their efficiency in the construction
of multivariate wavelets
[
51
]
and of subdivision schemes.
The
subdivision operator

S:\,\ell_{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})\to\ell_{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}^{n})

generated by a dilation matrix
M
and coefficients
\{c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}

is defined as
\,[S{\boldsymbol{a}}](\cdot)\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{\cdot-M{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,{\boldsymbol{a}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
.
The subdivision scheme is a recursive application of
S
to a function
f_{0}:{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\,\to{\mathbb{R}}
. The subdivision scheme
converges
to a limit function

f\in C({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
if

\bigl\|f(M^{-j}\cdot)\,-\,S^{j}f_{0}(\cdot)\bigr\|_{\infty}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
.
Similarly one defines the convergence in
L_{2}
. In Section
9

we analyse the properties of subdivision schemes generated by the tile B-splines, in particular, those having the super-smoothness.
We prove that the tile schemes are optimal in the following sense:
they have the lowest possible complexity of one iteration
among all schemes of a given approximation rate.

1.4. Notation

We denote vectors by bold letters and scalars by usual ones,
so
{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The polynomials are also denoted
by bold letters. The cardinality of a finite set
X
is denoted by
|X|
.

We define the Fourier transform in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by the formula

\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})e^{-2\pi i({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}d{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
As usual, the convolution of functions
f,g
is defined by

f*g(x)\ =\ \int f(t)g(x-t)dt
, also we
use the symmetrized autoconvolution
of a function
\varphi
defined as
\varphi*(\varphi\_)
, where
\varphi\_(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})=\varphi(-\mathord{\,\cdot\,})
.

We consider trigonometric polynomials of the
form
{\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{-2\pi i({\boldsymbol{k}},\xi)},\,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
\Omega
is a finite subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. We usually drop the word “trigonometric”.
The set of zeros of a function
f
will be denoted as
\mbox{nill}\,(f)\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\ f({\boldsymbol{x}})=0\}
. Similarly, for a natural number
\ell
,
\mbox{nill}\,_{\ell}(f)
denotes the set of zeros of order at least
\ell+1
.
For any closed subset
C\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
\pi\{C\}
be the set of points

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

such that

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}\in C
for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
For several subsets
C_{i}
, we
write
\pi\,\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\}
for

\pi\{C_{1}\cup\ldots\cup C_{m}\}
.

Two functions
f(x),g(x)
on
{\mathbb{R}}
,
are asymptotically equivalent
\,\bigl(f(x)\ {\asymp}\ g(x)\bigr)
if
there exist positive
c_{1},c_{2}
such that

\,c_{1}|f(x)|\,\leq\,|g(x)|\,\leq\,c_{2}|f(x)|,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
We also define the
exponential asymptotic equivalence
.
For a given sequence
\{a_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
and
\lambda>0
,
we write
a_{k}\ \dot{\asymp}\ \lambda^{k}
if
there exist positive
r,c_{1},c_{2}
such that

c_{1}\lambda^{k}\ \leq\ |a_{k}|\ \leq\ c_{2}k^{r}\lambda^{k},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}.

We write
|a_{k}|\ \dot{\leq}\ \lambda^{k}
if there exist positive constants
r,c

such that
|a_{k}|\ \leq\ c\,k^{r}\,\lambda^{k},\,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

For a given subset
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we denote by
{\rm cone}\,X\,=\,{\rm co}\,\{\lambda{\boldsymbol{x}}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{x}}\in X,\,\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\}

the conic hull of
X
.

The function
{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{-2\pi i({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}
is the
mask

of the refinement equation (
1
). We say that the mask

is supported on a set
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
if
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0
for
{\boldsymbol{k}}\notin\Omega
.
Since we assume that the support is finite,
{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is a trigonometric polynomial.

2
The roadmap for the main results

2.1. Computing the regularity in the isotropic case: an overview

The matrix approach makes use of the
transition operator
:

[{\mathcal{F}}f](\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\quad=\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}f(M\mathord{\,\cdot\,}\,-\,{\boldsymbol{k}}),

where, recall,
the summation is finite and the mask is supported on the subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
The refinable function
\varphi
is the eigenfunction of
{\mathcal{F}}
with the eigenvalue one.
Assume that
M
is isotropic (including the case
n=1
),

r=\rho(M)
is the spectral radius of
M
,
W
is the linear space spanned by the differences
\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}),\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. From the refinement equation it
follows that the value
\sup_{\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\leq r^{-k}}\|\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\|_{\infty}
is equivalent to
\|{\mathcal{F}}^{k}|_{W}\|
.
This implies that the Hölder exponent of
\varphi

is equal to
\log_{\,1/r}\,\rho({\mathcal{F}}|_{W})

[
6
,
4
]
. Let us stress that this is true only if
M
is isotropic.
It is proved that the spectral radius
\rho({\mathcal{F}}|_{W})
in the space
C({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is equal to
the so-called joint spectral radius of
N\times N

transition matrices

\{T_{0},\ldots,T_{m-1}\}

constructed by the coefficients
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and restricted to a certain subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
The computation of the joint spectral radius, however, is a hard problem. The Hölder exponent in
L_{2}

is expressed by the
L_{2}
-radius
of the same matrices.
It is equal to the square root of the usual spectral radius of the matrix

T=\frac{1}{m}\,\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\,T_{i}\otimes T_{i}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product
[
39
]
. Thus, the computation of the
L_{2}
-regularity
by the matrix method involves the usual spectral radius
\rho(T)
.
However,
T
has the size of order
\frac{1}{2}\,N^{2}
while the matrices
T_{i}

have size of order
N
.

We consider the Littlewood-Paley type method (computation of Sobolev regularity).
Its idea is to associate to every function
f\in W
the periodization of the square of its Fourier transform
\widehat{f}
:

\Phi_{f}\,({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\quad=\quad\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\bigl|\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{k}})\bigr|^{2}\,.

(2)

The Plancherel equality yields that
\|\Phi_{f}\|_{1}\,=\,\|\widehat{f}\|_{2}^{2}\,=\,\|f\|_{2}^{2}
(the norm of
\Phi_{f}
is computed in
L_{1}({\mathbb{T}}_{n})
). If we apply the operator
{\mathcal{F}}
to the function
f
,
what happens then to
\Phi_{f}
? To answer this question, we define the operator
{\mathcal{T}}

on
L_{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{n})
as follows. Let
D^{*}\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}
be a set of digits
for the transpose matrix
M^{T}
. This set may not coincide with
D
. Then

\bigl[{\mathcal{T}}{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigr]({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\quad=\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\,\bigl|\,{\boldsymbol{c}}\,(M^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}_{j}))\bigr|^{2}\,{\boldsymbol{p}}\bigl(\,M^{-T}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}_{j})\,\bigr)\ ,\qquad{\boldsymbol{p}}\in L_{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{n})\,.

(3)

A direct calculation shows that
\,\Phi_{\,{\mathcal{F}}f}\,=\,{\mathcal{T}}\,[\Phi_{f}]
.
Hence, for each
k\geq 1
, the norm
\|{\mathcal{F}}^{k}f\|_{2}
, is equal to the square root of the
L_{1}
-norm of

{\mathcal{T}}^{k}[\Phi_{f}]
. One may wonder what is the use of changing one
infinite-dimensional operator
{\mathcal{F}}
to another operator
{\mathcal{T}}
?
Observe that, for every compactly supported
f\in W
,
the function
\Phi_{f}

is a polynomial. Indeed,
\Phi_{f}
has the following Fourier expansion:

\Phi_{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ =\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\,\Bigl(\,f(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\,,\,f(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{k}})\,\Bigr)\,e^{\,2\pi i\,({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\,,

where only finitely many coefficients
\bigl(f(\cdot),f(\cdot+{\boldsymbol{k}})\bigr)

are nonzero. This argument reduces, the operator
{\mathcal{T}}

to a finite-dimensional space of polynomials
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
with coefficients supported
on certain finite symmetric subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. It is possible to prove that
if
f\in W
, then the polynomial
\Phi_{f}
has a root of multiplicity two at zero.
Denoting the space of such polynomials by
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}={\mathcal{P}}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have

\rho^{2}({\mathcal{F}}|_{W})\,=\,\rho({\mathcal{T}}|_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}})
, and therefore,
the Hölder exponent of
\varphi
in
L_{2}

is equal to
\log_{1/r}\,\rho({\mathcal{F}}|_{W})\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/r}\rho({\mathcal{T}}|_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}})
. Note that the dimension of the space
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
is
usually much less than the size of the matrix
T
, which makes this
formula more efficient in practice and applicable for a wider set of refinement equations.
This holds, however, only for the isotropic case. The situation
in the case of general
matrix
M
is quite different.

2.2. What is wrong with the non-isotropic case?

The advantage of the Littlewood-Paley type method is that it expresses the exponent of
Sobolev and of
L_{2}

regularity merely by the spectral radius of one matrix, whose size is usually not too large. Neither the joint spectral radius
nor the Kronecker lifting is involved. However, the
formula
\,\frac{1}{2}\log_{1/r}\,\rho({\mathcal{T}}|_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}})
is valid only
for isotropic
M
. For general
M
, neither this formula, nor
its possible
modifications (for example, by replacing
r
with
|{\rm\det}\,M|^{1/n}
) holds.
The reason is that a non-isotropic matrix
M
expands the distances differently in different
directions. This aspect has to be taken into account in any regularity analysis of
non-isotropic refinement equations. Can the Littlewood-Paley type method be
applied to arbitrary dilation matrices?

2.3. Our solution for the non-isotropic case: the four steps

The idea
of handling the non-isotropic case was suggested in
[
11
]

and worked out in the space
C({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. One considers the
spectral subspaces

J_{1},\ldots,J_{q}
of the matrix
M
. Each subspace
J_{s}
corresponds
to all eigenvalues of
M
of a given modulus
r_{s}
. Thus,

r_{1}>\cdots>r_{q}
are all moduli of eigenvalues of
M
and
the restriction
M|_{J_{s}}
has all the eigenvalues of modulus
r_{s}
.
Note that the operator
M|_{J_{s}}
is not necessarily isotropic since it may have
nontrivial Jordan blocks. We have
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,=\,J_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus J_{q}

(the direct sum).
The regularity of functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is considered separately
on the directions of the subspaces
J_{s}
.
This is done by computing the spectral radii
of the restrictions of the operator
{\mathcal{F}}
to the subspaces
W_{s}

spanned by the differences
\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}),\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s}
.
Then the regularity on the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
the minimal regularity along
J_{s},\,s=1,\ldots,q
.
This idea works for the matrix method
[
11
]
, however, its extension to the Littlewood-Paley type method meets considerable difficulties.
One of the reasons is that the map:
\Phi:\,W\to{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
,
which associates
\Phi_{f}
to a function
f\in W
, is nonlinear.
Hence, the images of the subspaces
W_{s}
become nonlinear manifolds in
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
.
We handle this problem by a new construction realized in four steps described below.

Step 1.
We use the spectral properties of linear operators
with an invariant cone.
Theorem
3.3
, the main result of Section
3
, states that if
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a closed solid pointed cone respected by a linear operator
A
, then
for every point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K
, the asymptotic growth of the trajectory
\{A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

as
j\to\infty
is determined by the spectral radius of
A
restricted to the
minimal face of
K
invariant with respect to
A
and containing
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The same is true not only for one point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
but also for an arbitrary subset
\Gamma\subset K
.
The fastest asymptotic growth of trajectories
\{A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

over all points
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Gamma
is defined by the minimal invariant face of
K

containing
\Gamma
.

Step 2.
Theorem
3.3
is applied for the
cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}={\mathcal{P}}_{+}(\Omega)

of nonnegative trigonometric polynomials
{\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,=\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega}\,p_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,e^{2\pi i\,({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{x}})}
. To this end, we characterize all invariant faces of the
cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
. Propositions
4.1
and
4.2
assert that, for an arbitrary operator

leaving
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
invariant, every invariant face

is defined
by a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of common zeros, i.e.,
\ L=\bigl\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{p}}(S)=0\bigr\}
.

Step 3.
We
consider an arbitrary difference
f=\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})
along
a shift vector
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s}
.
Applying contractions by the powers of
M^{-1}
,
we obtain a small vector
M^{-j}{\boldsymbol{h}}
of the norm
equivalent to
r_{s}^{-j}
. On the other hand, the corresponding
function
\Phi_{f}
after changing the shift vector from
{\boldsymbol{h}}

to
M^{-j}{\boldsymbol{h}}
, becomes
{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}
, where the linear operator
{\mathcal{T}}

is given by (
3
). Now we apply the results of Steps 1, 2 to the
positive polynomial cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
and conclude that

\|{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}\|\ \dot{\asymp}\ (\rho_{s})^{j}
, where

\rho_{s}
is the spectral radius of
{\mathcal{T}}
restricted to a certain face
K_{s}

of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
. Thus,
\|\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+M^{-j}{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\|^{2}\,=\,\ \|{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}\|_{1}\ \dot{\asymp}\ (\rho_{s})^{j}
.
We see that while the norm of the shift
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s}
is multiplied by (roughly)
r_{s}^{-j}
,
the norm of the variation of
\varphi
is multiplied by
(\rho_{s})^{j}
.
Now it is easily deduced that the Hölder exponent of
\varphi
along the
subspace
J_{s}
is equal to
\,\log_{1/r_{s}}\rho_{s}
.
The minimum of those values over

\varphi
.

Note that if the dilation matrix

is isotropic, then there is only one spectral subspace

and hence
W_{1}=W
. Therefore,
\Phi
maps

to the entire space
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
,
and the Hölder exponent of
\varphi

merely by the spectral radius of
{\mathcal{T}}|_{{\mathcal{P}}_{0}}
.
This was the core of the method in the isotropic case
[
13
,
18
,
25
,
27
]
.
Now, dealing with an arbitrary matrix

,
we may have proper subspaces

of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
The image of

by the map
\Phi
is some nonlinear
manifold in
{\mathcal{P}}_{0}
. If one follows the line of reasoning from the
isotropic case, he needs to apply the operators
{\mathcal{T}}^{j}
to those

to apply the operator
{\mathcal{T}}^{j}
to the minimal invariant face

of the

{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
containing those manifolds. This minimal face

can be found explicitly,
which leads to the formula for the Hölder exponent in

(Theorem

).

Step 4.
To extend our approach to the higher order regularity
we use the
k
th order differences
of the function
\varphi
along the shift vectors
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Replacing the difference

by the higher order difference, we adopt
our approach to the higher order regularity and obtain an explicit formula for
the Hölder exponents of the derivatives in Theorem

.

2.4. Applications to tiles, B-splines, and subdivisions

We use our method (Theorems

and

)
to find the regularity of the tile B-splines. To this end, one needs to find

of the cone

3
Auxiliary results. Operators leaving a cone invariant

We consider a convex cone
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which is a set
closed with respect to addition and multiplication by positive numbers:
if
{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in K
, then
\,{\boldsymbol{a}}+{\boldsymbol{b}}\in K
and
\lambda{\boldsymbol{a}}\in K

for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. In what follows we
always assume that a cone is closed,
solid
(or
full-dimensional
, i.e.,
possesses a nonempty interior) and

pointed
(does not contain a straight line).
A linear operator
A
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

leaves
K
invariant
if

\ AK\subset K
. By the Krein-Rutman theorem
[
29
]
,
an operator leaving a cone
K
invariant possesses a leading eigenvector

{\boldsymbol{v}}\in K,\,{\boldsymbol{v}}\neq 0
, for which
A{\boldsymbol{v}}=\lambda_{\max}{\boldsymbol{v}},\ \lambda_{\max}=\rho(A)
.
This fact extends the well-known Perron-Frobenius theorem from the positive orthant
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}

to an arbitrary cone
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

A plane of support of a cone
K
is a hyperplane that intersects
K
but
does not intersect its interior. A
face
of a cone is
its intersection with a plane of support. In particular, the apex
{\boldsymbol{0}}
is the face of dimension zero. In addition, the cone
K
itself is considered as the face of dimension
d
.
All other faces are

proper
and have dimensions from one (the edges) to
d-1

(the facets). All proper faces are cones of smaller dimensions. A
face plane
is a linear span of a face. For an arbitrary subset
\Gamma\subset K
, its

minimal face
is the smallest by inclusion face of
K
that contains
\Gamma
.
If an operator
A
leaves
K
invariant, then the
minimal invariant face for
\Gamma
is the smallest by inclusion face of
K
invariant with respect to
A

and containing
\Gamma
.

We are interested in the asymprotic behaviour of the trajectory
A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}
as
j\to\infty
,
for a given point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Clearly,
\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\,\dot{\leq}\,\rho^{\,j}
, where

\rho=\rho(A)
is the spectral radius of
A
. If
{\boldsymbol{x}}
belongs to an invariant cone, one can say more:

Proposition 3.1

Let an operator
A
leave a cone
K
invariant and let
\rho=\rho(A)
.
Then there exists a face
\bar{L}
of
K
invariant with respect to
A
, such that
{\rm dim}\,\bar{L}\leq d-1
and
for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K\setminus\bar{L}
, we have

\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\,\dot{\asymp}\,\rho^{\,j},\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.
If all eigenvalues of
A
have equal absolute values
\rho
,
then the assertion is obvious true with
\bar{L}=\{0\}
.
Otherwise, denote by
\rho_{2}
the second largest modulus of eigenvalues of
A
. Thus,

\rho_{2}<\rho
and all
vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that

\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\,\dot{\leq}\,\rho_{2}^{\,j}
form a proper invariant subspace
V
of
the operator
A
. For every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have
\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\,\dot{\asymp}\,\rho^{\,j}
, unless
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in V
. Denote
K^{\prime}=K\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.
If
V
does not intersect
K^{\prime}
, then the proposition follows
with
\bar{L}=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. If it intersects the interior of
K
, then we
take an arbitrary point
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\rm int}\,K\,\cap\,V

and consider the vector
{\boldsymbol{z}}-t{\boldsymbol{v}}
, where
{\boldsymbol{v}}
is the Krein-Rutman eigenvector,
for which
A{\boldsymbol{v}}=\rho\,{\boldsymbol{v}}
, and
t>0
is small enough, so
{\boldsymbol{z}}-t{\boldsymbol{v}}\in K
.
We have
A^{j}({\boldsymbol{z}}-t{\boldsymbol{v}})\in K
for all
j
.
On the other hand,
A^{j}({\boldsymbol{z}}-t{\boldsymbol{v}})\,=\,A^{j}{\boldsymbol{z}}\,-\,tA^{j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,=\,A^{j}{\boldsymbol{z}}\,-\,t\,\rho^{\,j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,=\,-t\,\rho^{\,j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,+\,o(\rho^{j})

as
j\to+\infty
.
However,
-t\,\rho^{\,j}{\boldsymbol{v}}\,+\,o(\rho^{j})\,=\,\rho^{\,j}\bigl(-t{\boldsymbol{v}}\,+\,o(1)\bigr)\notin K
for large
j\to+\infty
, which is a contradiction.
Thus,
V
cannot intersect the interior of
K
. It remains to consider the last case:

V
intersects
K^{\prime}
but
V\cap{\rm int}\,K=\emptyset
.
By the convex separation theorem,
V
can be separated from
K

by a hyperplane
H
. The set
H\cap K
is a proper face
of
K
that contains the set
V\cap K
. Since the operator
A

respects the set
V\cap K
, it respects its minimal
face, which will be denoted by
\bar{L}
. Thus,
\bar{L}

is an invariant face, every point
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K\setminus\bar{L}

does not lie in
V
,
and hence
\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\,\dot{\asymp}\,\rho^{\,j}
.

\Box

Corollary 3.2

Suppose a linear operator
A
leaves a cone
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
invariant;
then, for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K
, we have

\bigl\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\bigr\|\quad\asymp\quad\bigl\|A^{j}\bigl|_{L}\bigr\|\quad\dot{\asymp}\quad\rho^{\,j}\bigl(A\bigl|_{L}\bigr),\qquad j\in{\mathbb{Z}},

(4)

where
L
is the minimal invariant face of
{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Proof.
It suffices to consider the case
\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|=1
and
L=K
, otherwise we
restrict
A
to the corresponding face plane. If
\rho(A)=0
, then
A
is a nilpotent
operator,
A^{j}=0
for
j\geq d
, and (
4
) holds.
If
\rho(A)>0
, then by Proposition
3.1
, the assertion (
4
) holds
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K
which do not belong to a special invariant face of
K
.
However, by the assumption,
{\boldsymbol{x}}
does not belong to proper invariant faces,
which concludes the proof.

\Box

We are ready to prove the main result of this section, which gives us the key to computing the regularity of refinable functions.

Theorem 3.3

Let a linear operator
A
leave a cone
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
invariant and

\Gamma
be an arbitrary subset of
K
. Let also
L
be the minimal
invariant face for
\Gamma
. Then for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Gamma
,
we have
\ \|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\bigr\|\ \dot{\leq}\ \rho^{\,j}(A|_{L}),\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,
there exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\Gamma
and
C>0
such that
\ \|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\bigr\|\,\geq\,C\,\rho^{\,j}(A|_{L}),\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.
It suffices to consider the case
L=K
, otherwise we
restrict
A
to the corresponding face plane. Denote
\rho=\rho(A)

and take the invariant face
\bar{L}
from
Proposition
3.1
. Since
\Gamma
does not lie on a proper invariant face
of
K
,
it follows that there exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\,\in\,\Gamma\setminus\bar{L}
,
C>0
, for
which
\,\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\bigr\|\,\geq\,\quad C\,\rho^{\,j}(A),\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. On the other hand,

\,\|A^{j}{\boldsymbol{x}}\bigr\|\,\dot{\leq}\,\rho^{\,j}
for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in L
, which completes the proof.

\Box

4
Cones of nonnegative polynomials

Consider a finite subset
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
symmetric about the origin
and containing zero.
Denote by
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
the space of real-valued trigonometric
polynomials on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the spectrum on
\Omega
:

{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}\quad=\quad\left\{\ {\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ =\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega}\,p_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,e^{2\pi i\,({\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\quad:\quad p_{-{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,=\,p_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\in{\mathbb{R}}\,,\ {\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega\ \right\}.

We usually assume that
\Omega
is fixed and use the simple notation

{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
.
We have
{\rm dim}\,{\mathcal{P}}\ =\ \bigl(1+|\Omega|\bigr)/2
.
Consider the set
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
of nonnegative polynomials from
{\mathcal{P}}
, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\geq 0,\ {\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For a given closed set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
we denote

{\mathcal{P}}(S)\quad=\quad\Bigl\{\,{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=0,\ {\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in S\ \Bigr\}\,,\qquad{\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S)\,=\,{\mathcal{P}}(S)\cap{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\,.

The same notation is used for compact sets on the period:
\,S\subset{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
.
Clearly, the sets
{\mathcal{P}}(S)
are linear subspaces of
{\mathcal{P}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S)
are cones
in those subspaces.

Proposition 4.1

The set
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
is a closed solid pointed cone in
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Every face
L
of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
has the form
L={\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S)
for a suitable
compact subset
S\subset{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
.

Proof.
The closeness and pointedness of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
is obvious.
To prove that this cone is solid, one can note that for every
{\boldsymbol{q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
,
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{q}}\|_{C({\mathbb{T}}_{n})}<1
, we have
1+{\boldsymbol{q}}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
, hence
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
contains an open set. Now observe, that
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
is an intersection of
the half-spaces
\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\ |\ {\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\geq 0\}
over all
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
.
Therefore, every plane of support of the cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
has the
form
{\mathcal{P}}(\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\})\,=\,\bigl\{\,{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\ :\ {\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=0\bigr\}
and hence, every face plane of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
is an intersection of some of those planes.
Thus, every face of the cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
has the form
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S)
for certain
S\subset{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
.

\Box

The proof of the following fact is the same as for Proposition
4.1
:

Proposition 4.2

For every compact set
S\subset{\mathbb{T}}_{n}
, all faces
of the cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S)
are the sets
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}(S^{\prime}),\ S^{\prime}\subset S
.

For a given
\ell\geq 0
, we denote by
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}

the set of polynomials
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}
that have zero of order at least
2(\ell+1)
at the origin, i.e.,

\ {\boldsymbol{p}}({\boldsymbol{0}})={\boldsymbol{p}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{0}})=\cdots={\boldsymbol{p}}^{(2\ell+1)}({\boldsymbol{0}})=0
. The equality
{\boldsymbol{p}}^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
means that all partial derivatives of order
k
at the point
{\boldsymbol{x}}
are equal to zero.

We consider zeros of even order only, because nonnegative
polynomials can have only such zeros at the origin. The cone of nonnegative polynomials
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}_{+}\,=\,{\mathcal{P}}_{+}\cap{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}
is
full-dimensional in
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}
. Similarly we define
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}_{+}(S)

and prove the analogues of Propositions
4.1
and
4.2

for the space
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}
. We will refer to those propositions mentioning that
we use them for
{\mathcal{P}}^{(\ell)}
instead of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Sometimes we use
the short notation
P_{\ell}=P^{(\ell)}
.

5
Regularity of
refinable functions with an arbitrary dilation matrix

5.1 Preliminary facts and notation

We deal with the refinement equation (
1
)
with complex coefficients
\{c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}

supported on a finite set
Q\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and such that

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in Q}c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,=\,m
.
For the sake of simplicity, we restrict ourselves to
the case or real coefficients, although all our results are
directly extended to the complex case. The unique compactly supported
solution
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{0}^{\prime}
(refinable function) is normalized
by the condition
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\varphi\,d{\boldsymbol{x}}=1
.
The equation
satisfies the sum rules of order

\ell\geq 0
if
the
m-1
points
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=M^{-T}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}^{*},\ {\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}^{*}\in D^{*}\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
are roots of order at least
\ell+1
of the mask
{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
. Recall that
D
and
D^{*}
are the sets of digits for the
matrices
M,M^{T}
respectively.

We make the standard assumption that
the refinable function
\varphi
is
stable
i.e., its integer shifts
\{\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}-{\boldsymbol{k}})\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}
are linearly independent. Equivalently, those
shifts form a Riesz basis of their linear span. Without loss of generality we set
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{d}}_{0}^{*}={\boldsymbol{0}}
.
It is known that if the solution
\varphi
is stable and belongs to
W^{\ell}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, then the refinement equation
satisfies the sum rules of order
\ell
. In particular, if
\varphi\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
then the sum rules of order zero are fulfilled, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{c}}\,\bigl(M^{-T}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}^{*}\bigr)\,=\,0,\ k=1,\ldots,m-1
.
In terms of the coefficients, this condition reads:

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{j}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}\,c_{M{\boldsymbol{j}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}_{k}}\ =\ 1\,,\qquad k=0,\ldots,m-1\,.

(5)

It will always be assumed that the sum rules of order zero are satisfied.
Now define several special subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Let

Y\quad=\quad\left\{\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M^{-j}\,{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\ :\quad{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\in Q\,,\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \right\}\,.

This is a compact self-similar set generated by the affine operators
A_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\,=\,M^{-1}({\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{s}}),\,{\boldsymbol{s}}\in Q
. This means that
Y

is a unique fixed point of the set-valued map
AX\,=\,\cup_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in Q}A_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}X
,
i.e., an affine fractal. The transition operator
{\mathcal{F}}

respects the space of functions supported on the set
Y
, therefore,

{\rm supp}\,\varphi\,\subset Y
, see
[
4
,
6
]
for details. Let
\Omega\,=\,\bigl(Y-Y\bigr)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
where
A-B\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{a}}-{\boldsymbol{b}}\,:\ {\boldsymbol{a}}\in A,{\boldsymbol{b}}\in B\}
is the standard
Minkowski subtractions of two sets. Thus,
\Omega
is a symmetric finite (since
Y
is compact)
subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
containing zero. This set is uniquely defined by the support of the mask
Q

and can be constructed by an algorithm presented in Appendix.
It follows from the definition that
\Omega\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\,:\ Y\cap(Y+{\boldsymbol{k}})\,\neq\,\emptyset\}
. Therefore,
\Omega
contains all integer points from the
support of the function
\varphi*(\varphi\_)
(the symmetrized autoconvolution
of
\varphi
), where
\varphi\_(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})=\varphi(-\mathord{\,\cdot\,})
.

We consider the linear operator
{\mathcal{T}}
defined in
L_{2}({\mathbb{T}}_{n})
by formula (
3
).
This operator maps the space of polynomials
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
to itself

15
,
18
,
26
]
and
in the sequel we restrict
{\mathcal{T}}
to this subspace.
If the mask
{\boldsymbol{c}}
satisfies the sum rules of order

For an arbitrary compactly supported distribution

the periodization of the square

p_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,=\,[\,f*f\_\,](-{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
So, if
{\rm supp}\,f\,\subset Y

\Phi_{f}\,\in\,{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
.
For every

, we have
{\mathcal{T}}(\Phi_{f})\,=\,\Phi_{{\mathcal{F}}f}
.

For each
{\boldsymbol{p}}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{+}

the asymptotic behaviour of the sequence
\{{\mathcal{T}}^{k}{\boldsymbol{p}}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

.
In view of Theorem

{\mathcal{T}}

{\boldsymbol{p}}

the space of polynomials that vanish on

{\mathcal{T}}
.
We need to reveal this structure and find this set.

For arbitrary

,
we consider the set

\,\pi\,\bigl\{\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi})\,,\,J_{s}^{\perp}\bigr\}\,

.

the sets
\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi})

.
The stability of the function
\varphi

\widehat{\varphi}

\pi\,\bigl\{\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi})\bigr\}\,=\,\emptyset

\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi})

Denote by

This is a face of the cone

with the corresponding face plane

.

Lemma 5.1

If the refinement equation satisfies sum rules of order
\ell
,
then for every

, the space

is an invariant
subspace of the operator

and

is its invariant cone in this subspace.

Proof

Proposition 5.2

Let a refinement equation satisfy the sum rules of order
\ell
.
Then, for each
s=1,\ldots,q
, there is an alternative:
either

or
there exists a non-integer vector
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
orthogonal to
J_{s}
such that
for every
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, the vector
{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}

is orthogonal to

or is a zero
of the function
\widehat{\varphi}

of order at least
\ell+1
.

Proof.
As we already noted, the set

always contains
\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
.
On the other hand, all polynomials from

have a zero of order
\,\geq\,2(\ell+1)
at the point

5.2. The
L_{2}
-regularity

Now we prove the formula for the
L_{2}
-regularity and in the next section attack the
higher order regularity. The main idea is to work separately with
regularities along
spectral subspaces
J_{1},\ldots,J_{q}
of
M
. Then the regularity of
\varphi
is equal to the minimal regularity over these subspaces.

We denote by
\alpha_{0}(f)
the Hölder exponent
(or the
Hölder exponent of zero order
) of a function
f\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
The index zero is added to distinguish from the Hölder exponent of higher order.

For a given nontrivial subspace
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(the case
J={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is allowed),
we define the Hölder exponent in
L_{2}
of the function
f\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

along
J
:

\alpha_{\,0,J}\quad=\quad\sup\ \Bigl\{\alpha\geq 0\ :\ \|f(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-f(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\|\ \leq\ C\,\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|^{\,\alpha}\,,\ {\boldsymbol{h}}\in J\Bigr\}\,.

For the subspaces
J_{s}
, we use the short
notation
\alpha_{\,0,s}\,=\,\alpha_{\,0,J_{s}}\,,\ s=1,\ldots,q
.

The spectral radius of the operator
{\mathcal{T}}
restricted to its invariant
subspace
P_{0,s}
is denoted by
\rho_{\,0,s}
.

Theorem 5.3

For an arbitrary refinable function
\varphi
with the dilation matrix
M
, we have

\alpha_{\,0,s}(\varphi)\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/r_{s}}\,\rho_{\,0,s}\,,\qquad s=1,\ldots,q,

(6)

where
J_{s}
are the spectral subspaces of
M
. For the Hölder exponent
in
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, we have

\alpha_{0}(\varphi)\quad=\quad\frac{1}{2}\,\min_{s=1,\ldots,q}\log_{\,1/r_{s}}\,\rho_{\,0,s}

(7)

Note that
P_{0,1}
can be a proper subspace of
{\mathcal{P}}^{(0)}

only if the mask possesses a special set of zeros.
That is why,
for most of refinement equations, we have
P_{0,1}={\mathcal{P}}^{(0)}
, and hence, the spectral radius
\rho_{\,0,1}

coincides with
\rho_{0}\,=\,\rho\,\bigl({\mathcal{T}}|_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(0)}}\bigr)
.
Since
r=r_{1}
is the maximal spectral radius among all
r_{s},\,s=1,\ldots,q
,
we see that the minimum in the formula (
7
) is attained for

.
Therefore,

\alpha_{0}(\varphi)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{\,1/r_{1}}\,\rho_{0}
.
Applying Proposition
5.2
for the subspace
J_{1}
, we obtain

Theorem 5.4

The equality
\alpha_{0}(\varphi)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/r}\,\rho_{0}
holds,
unless there exists a non-integer point
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in J_{1}^{\perp}

such that, for every integer shift
{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{k}},\,{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

either
{\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{k}}\,\in\,J_{1}^{\perp}
or
\widehat{\varphi}({\boldsymbol{z}}+{\boldsymbol{k}})=0
.

There are, however, many examples when

is a proper subspace
of
{\mathcal{P}}^{(0)}
and, respectively,

and is equal to the minimal value
\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{\,1/r_{s}}\,\rho_{\,0,s}

In the sequel of this section we use the simplified notation

(the subspace of polynomials from

in

An
\varepsilon
-extension

of

Lemma 5.5

For every

\varepsilon
-extension of

.

Proof.
Take a number

is less than

and define

(the Minkowski sum). This set contains

.

\Box

Remark 5.6

If

is not convex, then

.

Let us remember that for every

, the function
\Phi_{f}

is a trigonometric polynomial and if

, where

-extension

.
For each

under small shifts along

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

Proof of Theorem

. Consider the space

for some

.
For an arbitrary function

, the Plancherel theorem and the equality

(9)

Moreover, the function
\Phi_{f}
is a polynomial from
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
.
Let

be the minimal face of the cone
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
containing
the set

\Gamma=\{\Phi_{f}\,:\,\ f\in U_{s}\}
. Since

, it follows that

.
Let us show that

. For each

, the Fourier transform
\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\bigl(e^{2\pi i({\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}\,-\,1\bigr)\widehat{\varphi}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
vanishes on the family of parallel

\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,|\,({\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
.
This property holds for all sufficiently small

precisely
when
f
vanishes on the set of all sufficiently small vectors
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

orthogonal to the subspace
J_{s}
. This means that it vanishes
on the orthogonal
complement of
J_{s}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Each
\Phi_{f}\in\Gamma
generating

V_{s}

vanishes on the union of the sets
\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi})

and
{\boldsymbol{h}}^{\perp}
. The intersection of all those sets over all
feasible
\,{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s}

is
\mbox{nill}\,\,(\widehat{\varphi})\,\cup\,J^{\perp}_{s}
. By Proposition
4.1
,
every face of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
consists of nonnegative polynomials from
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}

that vanish on a certain closed set

. Therefore, the face

is
the set of polynomials from
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
that vanish on the set
\pi\,\{\mbox{nill}\,(\widehat{\varphi}),J_{s}^{\perp}\}
, i.e.,
V_{s}=K_{s}
.

Since all eigenvalues of the matrix
M

restricted to the subspace

are equal to
r_{s}
,
it follows that there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that

c_{1\,}r_{s}^{j}\ \|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\quad\leq\quad\|M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\quad\dot{\leq}\quad r_{s}^{j}\,\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\,,\qquad{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s},\ j\,\in\,{\mathbb{N}}\,.

Hence, for every
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
c_{2}

such that
\|M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\ \leq\ c_{2}\,(r_{s}+\delta)^{j}\,\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|,\,j\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Further, choose some
\tau<\varepsilon

such that the
\tau
-neighbourhood of
Y
is contained in
Y_{\varepsilon}
.
Take arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in J_{s},\,\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|<\tau
, and the smallest
integer
j
such that

\|M^{j+1}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\,\geq\,\tau
. Since
\|M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\,<\,\tau
,
it follows that the function
f=\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})
belongs
to
U_{s}
. Applying (
9
), we obtain

\Bigl\|\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})\,-\,\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\Bigr\|^{2}\ =\ \Bigl\|{\mathcal{F}}^{j}\,\bigl(\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\bigr)\Bigr\|^{2}\ =\ \Bigl\|{\mathcal{F}}^{j}\,f\Bigr\|^{2}\ =\ \Bigl\|{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}\,\Bigr\|_{1}\,.

On the other hand,
K_{s}
is the minimal invariant face of
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
that contains
\Gamma
.
By Theorem
3.3
, for every
\Phi_{f}\in\Gamma
,
we have
\bigl\|{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}\,\bigr\|_{1}\,\dot{\leq}\,(\rho_{\,0,s})^{\,j}\|\Phi_{f}\|_{1}
. Furthermore,

\|\Phi_{f}\|_{1}\,=\,\|\widehat{f}\|^{2}\,=\,\|f\|^{2}\,\leq\,4\|\varphi\|^{2}
, because
\|f\|\,=\,\|\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+M^{j}{\boldsymbol{h}})-\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\|\,\leq\,2\,\|\varphi\|.
Thus,

\bigl\|\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})\,-\,\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\bigr\|^{2}\quad\dot{\leq}\quad(\rho_{\,0,s})^{\,j}\,.

(10)

On the other hand,
\ \tau\,\leq\,\|M^{j+1}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\ \leq\ c_{2}(r_{s}+\delta)^{\,j+1}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|
.
Therefore,
\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\,\geq\,\frac{\tau}{c_{2}}\,(r_{s}+\delta)^{j+1}
. Combining this
with (

) and taking the limit as

, we obtain

\,\alpha_{\,0,s}\,\geq\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/(r_{s}+\delta)}\,\rho_{\,0,s}
. This is true for every

consequently,
\,\alpha_{\,0,s}\,\geq\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/r_{s}}\,\rho_{\,0,s}

To establish the inverse inequality we

:
there exists
\Phi_{f}\in\Gamma

such that

.
Applying (

), we get

\,\bigl\|{\mathcal{F}}^{j}\,f\bigr\|^{2}\,=\,\|{\mathcal{T}}^{j}\Phi_{f}\|\,\geq\,C\,(\rho_{\,0,s})^{j}
.
Since

for some

, we see that

On the other hand,
\,\|M^{-j}{\boldsymbol{h}}\|\,\leq\,c_{1}\,(r_{s}-\delta)^{-j}\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|
, which implies that
\alpha_{\,0,s}\,\leq\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/(r_{s}-2\delta)}\,\,\rho_{\,0,s}
.
Taking the limit as

, we conclude the proof.

5.3 The higher order regularity

For an integer
k\geq 0
and for a given nontrivial subspace
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we
denote by
W^{k}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},J)
the space of functions

k
times differentiable along
J
. This means that for every
multivector
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\,=\,({\boldsymbol{b}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k})\in J^{\,k}
,
where
J^{\,k}\,=\,J\times\cdots\times J
(
k
multipliers),

{\boldsymbol{b}}_{i}\neq 0,\,i=1,\ldots,k
, the mixed partial derivative
f_{\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}}\,=\,\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{b}}_{1}\cdots\partial{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k}}\,f
along the vectors
{\boldsymbol{b}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{b}}_{k}
belongs to
L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.
For every
f\in W^{k}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},J)
, the
Hölder exponent of order
k
along
J

is defined as follows:

\alpha_{k,J}\quad=\quad k\ +\ \sup\ \Bigl\{\alpha\geq 0\ :\ \|f_{\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}}(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-f_{\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}}(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})\|\ \leq\ C\,\|{\boldsymbol{h}}\|^{\,\alpha}\,,\ \bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\in J^{\,k}\Bigr\}\,.

For the subspaces
\{J_{s}\}_{s=1}^{q}
(the spectral subspaces
of the dilation matrix
M
), we use the short
notation
\ \alpha_{k,J_{s}}\,=\,\alpha_{\,k,s}\,,\ s=1,\ldots,q
.

If
J={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then we obtain the standard definition of the

Hölder exponent of the
k
th order

\alpha_{k}(f)

as the number
k
plus the minimal Hölder exponent (of order zero)
of all mixed derivatives of order
k
.
The (generalized) Hölder exponent of a function
f\in L_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

is
\alpha(f)\,=\,\alpha_{\,k}(f)
,
where
k
is the largest integer, for which
f\in W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
.

From the representation by the direct sum
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,=\,\oplus\sum_{s=1}^{q}J_{s}
,
it follows that if a function
f
belongs to
W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n},J_{s})
for each
s
, then it belongs
to
W_{2}^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and the Hölder exponent
\alpha_{k}(f)
is equal to

\min_{s=1,\ldots,q}\alpha_{\,k,s}
. The computation of each exponent of regularity
\alpha_{\,k,s}
will be done similarly to the
previous section, in terms of spectral radii of the operator
{\mathcal{T}}

on faces of the cone of nonnegative polynomials
{\mathcal{P}}_{+}
.

In the statement of the main result we invoke the notation
of the space
P_{k,s}
, the cone

K_{k,s}
, and the spectral radius as
\rho_{k,s}
of the operator
{\mathcal{T}}|_{P_{k,s}}
,
see subsection 5.1. Recall that
P_{k,s}
is respected by
{\mathcal{T}}
, provided

\varphi
satisfies the sum rules of order
k

(Lemma
5.1
). We also use the short notation

.

6
The tile B-splines

As an example of application of Theorem
5.7
, we compute the regularity
of the tile B-splines. We are going to show that, unless the dilation matrix

M
can be factored to a block-diagonal form by means of an integer basis,
the Hölder exponent is computed by a simple formula
\alpha(\varphi)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\log_{1/r}\,\rho_{\ell}
, where
\ell
is the order of the B-spline.
Thus, the exponent of regularity
is computed without involving zeros from the set
\pi\,\bigl\{\mbox{nill}\,_{\ell}(\widehat{\varphi})\,,\,J_{s}^{\perp}\,\bigr\}
. The proof is based on two arguments: geometrical (tile cylinders) and
combinatorial (rational subspaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
). First we introduce tile cylinders
and prove their basic properties. We show that if the assumptions
of Theorem
5.8
are not satisfied, the tiling by translates of
G
contains a
nontrivial cylinder parallel to an eigenspace of
M
. Then we prove that
this eigenspace is a rational subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This means that
if
M
does not have rational eigenspaces, then the assumptions of
Theorem
5.8
are satisfied.

6.1. Tile cylinders and rational subspaces in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

A
cylinder
parallel to a subspace
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a
closed nonempty subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
invariant with respect to all translations parallel to
J
.
In particular, if

J={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then
C={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
; if
J=\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
, then
C
is an arbitrary closed set.
We usually avoid those cases and assume that
J
is a proper nontrivial subspace
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

We say that
a given tiling
\{G+{\boldsymbol{k}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}
contains a cylinder
C
if there is a subset
E\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

such that
C=\bigcup_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in E}(G+{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
A cylinder contained in a given tiling will be referred to as
a tile cylinder
.
The set of all tile cylinders parallel to a subspace
J
is
closed with respect to the operations of intersection, union, and difference
(the latter two operations are followed by taking the closure).
Every tiling possesses at least one cylinder parallel to
J
, which is

C={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. This case is trivial. All other tile cylinders, if they exist, are called
proper
.

A tile cylinder is
minimal
if it does not contain another tile cylinder.
One can also specify a subspace
J
and consider the minimal tile cylinder
among those parallel to
J
.

Lemma 6.1

For an arbitrary tiling and a subspace
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, there exists a minimal tile cylinder parallel
to
J
.

Proof.
Choose a tile
G
and consider the intersection
C
of all cylinder
parallel to
J
and containing
G
. This cylinder may not be minimal since it can include another cylinder
C^{\prime}
which does not contain
G
. In this case, however,
the closure of the set
C\setminus C^{\prime}
is a cylinder containing
G
, which contradicts the definition of
C
.
Hence,
C
is minimal.

\Box

Lemma 6.2

All minimal tile cylinders parallel to
J
are integer translates of each other.
Every tile cylinder parallel to
J
is partitioned to the minimal ones.

Proof.
The intersection of all tile cylinders parallel to
J
and
containing the tile
G
is a minimal cylinder (see the proof of Lemma
6.1
above). Denote it by
C
. Any other minimal
cylinder
C^{\prime}

contains some tile
G^{\prime}=G+{\boldsymbol{k}}
. Hence, the cylinder
C^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{k}}
contains
G
and therefore, it contains
C
, because
C
is minimal. If
C
is a proper subset of
C^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{k}}
, then
the cylinder
C+{\boldsymbol{k}}
, which contains
G+{\boldsymbol{k}}=G^{\prime}
, is a proper subset of
C^{\prime}
.
This is impossible since
C^{\prime}
is minimal. Thus,
C=C^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{k}}
, and
we proved that all minimal cylinders are integer translates of each other.
Now we introduce an equivalence relation on the set of tiles: two tiles are equivalent if they belong to one minimal cylinder. If
C_{0}
is an arbitrary tile cylinder
parallel to
J
, then we take the maximal system of non-equivalent tiles in it.
Then the corresponding minimal cylinders fill
C_{0}
.

\Box

Since
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is also a cylinder, we obtain

Corollary 6.3

For every tiling, the minimal tile cylinders form a
partition of the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

A linear subspace
L\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of dimension
d
is called
rational
if it is spanned by
several integer vectors. It is known that every rational subspace has a basis of integer vectors
that spans the whole
integer part of
L
, which is

L\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
by integer linear combinations. It will be referred to as an
elementary basis
.
The characteristic property
of the elementary basis is that its Gram matrix has the determinant one. Indeed, this condition means that the parallelepiped spanned by the vectors of this basis
has the
d
-dimensional volume one, and hence, it spans the integer lattice on
L
. Every elementary basis
can be complemented to an elementary basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, which spans the lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
There is an integer matrix that maps an elementary basis to the
first
d
vectors of the canonical basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

The linear span and the intersection of
several rational subspaces are also rational subspaces. This implies that
every subspace
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
possesses the minimal by inclusion rational
subspace
L
containing
J
. This subspace will be called
the minimal rational
subspace of
J
. There are countably many proper rational
subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For every vector out of those subspaces, its minimal rational subspace is the entire
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Thus, for almost all (in the Lebesgue measure) vectors of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
the minimal rational subspace coincides with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

6.2. The polynomial cones for

the tile B-splines

We consider a tile B-spline
\varphi
of order
\ell
, which is a convolution of

\ell+1
functions
\chi_{G}

For an arbitrary

coincides with

Theorem 6.4

Let

If

Proposition 6.6

The minimal tile cylinder parallel to a subspace

is

Proof.
Let

For a given subspace

of

Optimality of the tile B-splines

We establish the following optimal property of tile B-splines:
they possess the smallest number of nonzero coefficients
among all refinable functions of a given order of approximation.

For the sake of simplicity, we restrict to the case
m=2
, i.e.,
to two-digit refinable functions. Thus,
among all refinable functions corresponding to a given
dilation matrix
M
with
|{\rm det}\,M|=2
and
generating (by integer shifts) the space of algebraic polynomials of a given degree,
the tile B-spline has the minimal number
|Q|
of nonzero coefficients.
This property, which will be referred to as

the minimal support of the mask
, also holds for tile
B-splines in the following classes of refinable functions:

1) of a given
rate of approximation of smooth functions by contractions of integer translates;

This implies that among all refinable functions generating the multiresolution analysis
(MRA)

\{V_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with a given
rate of approximation by the subspaces
V_{j}
, the B-splines have the minimal
support of the mask. Consequently, they have the fastest algorithm of computation
of the wavelet coefficients produced by the corresponding MRA.

2) of a given degree of convergence of the subdivision scheme.

This means, in particular, that the subdivision scheme
generated by the tile B-spline has the least algorithmic complexity
(the number of operations to realize one iteration) among all
schemes reproducing the space of polynomials of a given degree.

The digit set for the
matrix
M
is

D\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{d}}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{d}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\setminus M{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
The digit set for the
matrix
M^{T}
is

D^{*}\,=\,\{{\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\setminus M^{T}{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.

The following theorem is well-known (see, for instance,
[
30
]
).

Theorem B
.
For a given integer
\ell\geq 0
, the following properties of a stable two-digit refinable function
are equivalent:

1) the integer translates
\{\varphi(\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{k}})\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}

generate the space of algebraic polynomials of degree
\ell
;

2) the refinement equation satisfies the sum rules of order
\ell
, i.e.,
the mask
{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
has zero of order
\,\geq\,\ell+1
at the point
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=M^{-T}{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}
.

3) the function
\varphi
has the rate of approximation at least
\ell+1
,
i.e., the distance from an arbitrary function
f\in C^{\ell+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

to the space generated by the functions
\{\varphi(N\mathord{\,\cdot\,}+{\boldsymbol{k}})\}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}}

is
O(N^{-\ell-1})
.

We denote by
\nu(\varphi)
the
accuracy
of the refinable function
\varphi
, i.e.,
the maximal
\ell\geq 0
for which the conditions
1) - 3) from Theorem B are satisfied. This is known that if
\varphi\in W^{\ell}_{2}
, then

\nu(\varphi)\geq\ell

[
30
]
.

Theorem 8.1

If a two-digit refinable function
\varphi
satisfies
\nu(\varphi)\geq\ell
,
then its refinement equation possesses at least
\ell+2
nonzero coefficients.

The mask of the tile B-spline
{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\,=\,\bigl(\frac{e^{2\pi i\,({\boldsymbol{d}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}})}+1}{2}\bigr)^{\ell+1}
has
exactly
\ell+2
nonzero coefficients. Therefore, for each dilation matrix
M
such that
|{\rm det}\,M|=2
, among all refinable functions with
\nu(\varphi)\geq\ell
, the tile B-spline
of order
\ell
has the minimal support of the mask
|Q|=\ell+2
.

Proof of Theorem
8.1
. Consider an arbitrary
refinement equation with a mask
{\boldsymbol{c}}(\mathord{\,\cdot\,})
satisfying the sum rules of order
\ell
.
Denote
{\boldsymbol{v}}=M^{-T}{\boldsymbol{d}}^{*}

and define the univariate polynomial
{\boldsymbol{q}}(t)={\boldsymbol{c}}\,(2t\,{\boldsymbol{v}}),\ t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
This polynomial
has a root of multiplicity at least
\ell+1
at the point
t=\frac{1}{2}
.
Since
|{\rm det}\,M|=2
, it follows that
2\,M^{-T}
is an integer matrix, and therefore,

2{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. This implies that the scalar products
(2{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})

are integer for all
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and hence,

{\boldsymbol{q}}(t)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,e^{2\pi i\,(2t{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})}\ =\ \sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left(\,\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},\,(2{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})=s}\,\frac{1}{2}\,c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\,\right)\,e^{2\pi i\,st}\ =\ \sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,q_{s}\,e^{2\pi i\,st},\

where
\ q_{s}\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\sum\limits_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},(2{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})=s}\,c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{(r)}\bigl(\frac{1}{2}\bigr)\,=\,0
for all
r=0,\ldots,\ell
,
we have

\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,q_{s}\bigl(2\pi is\bigr)^{r}e^{\pi is}\quad=\quad 0,\qquad r=0,\ldots,\ell\,.

In other words,

\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\,q_{s}\,s^{r}\,(-1)^{s}\quad=\quad 0,\qquad r=0,\ldots,\ell\,.

(18)

Observe that
\sum_{s}q_{s}\,=\,{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{0}})\,=\,1
. For an arbitrary
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we
consider the vector of moments
\tilde{\boldsymbol{h}}=\bigl(1,h,\ldots,h^{\ell}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell+1}
. Every
\,N\leq\ell+1
different vector of moments are linearly independent, since they can be complemented to
\ell+1
columns of a Vandermonde matrix. Assume that among the numbers

\bigl\{s\,=\,(2{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\,:\ {\boldsymbol{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\,,c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\neq 0\bigr\}
there are
exactly
N
different ones. Denote them by
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
.
Then equation (
18

\sum_{j=1}^{N}(-1)^{s_{j}}\,q_{s_{j}}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\,=\,0
. Therefore, the number
N
of vectors
\tilde{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}
is at least
\ell+2
.
On the other hand, each nonzero coefficient
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
generates exactly one
number
s=(2{\boldsymbol{v}},{\boldsymbol{k}})
(which may coincide for different
{\boldsymbol{k}}
), hence, there are at least
N\geq\ell+2
nonzero
coefficients
c_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
.

Algorithms and implementation details

To write the matrix of
{\mathcal{T}}
we need
to find the set
\Omega=\bigl(Y-Y\bigr)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, which can be obtained by
a special iterative algorithms, similar to ones
presented in
[
10
,
11
]
. We give a brief description below.

Using the definition of
Y
(subsection 5.1), we obtain

Y-Y\quad=\quad\left\{\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M^{-j}\,({\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j})\ :\quad{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{t}}_{j}\in Q\,,\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \right\}\,.

Denote
Q^{\prime}=Q-Q
and rewrite:

Y-Y\quad=\quad\left\{\ \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M^{-j}\,{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\ :\quad{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\in Q^{\prime}\,,\ j\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \right\}\,.

Define the map
{\mathcal{J}}
on the set of compact subsets
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as follows:

{\mathcal{J}}\,X\,=\,M^{-1}(X+Q^{\prime})
. A set
X
is
invariant with respect to
{\mathcal{J}}
, if
{\mathcal{J}}\,X\,\subset\,X
. Similarly we define a map
{\mathcal{J}}_{0}
on the set of finite
subsets of integers
X\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
:

{\mathcal{J}}_{0}\,X\,=\,{\mathcal{J}}X\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. The invariance with respect to
{\mathcal{J}}_{0}
is defined in the same way.

Proposition 9.2

The set
\Omega
is invariant with respect to
J_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}
be an arbitrary element of the set
{\mathcal{J}}_{0}\,\Omega
, i.e.,

{\boldsymbol{z}}=M^{-1}({\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime})
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
for some
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega,{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\Omega
, we have
{\boldsymbol{s}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M^{-j}\,{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}
,
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}\in Q^{\prime}
. Therefore
{\boldsymbol{z}}=M^{-1}({\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}M^{-j}\,{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}^{\prime}
, where
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}
,
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{j}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{s}}_{j-1}
for
j\geq 2
. Thus,
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in Y-Y
. Since
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, it follows that
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\Omega
, which completes the proof.

\Box

To find the set
\Omega
, we start with a subset
\Omega_{0}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
which will be specified later,
then define
\Omega_{i+1}\,=\,\Omega_{i}\,\cup\,{\mathcal{J}}_{0}\Omega_{i}
for
i\geq 0
. Thus, we obtain a non-increasing (by inclusion) sequence
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
.
Since
M
is an expanding matrix, it follows
that there exist
\varepsilon>0
and an ellipsoid
E\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(Lyapunov ellipsoid) such that
M^{-1}E\subset(1-\varepsilon)E
. Hence, for sufficiently
large
\lambda
, we have
M^{-1}(\lambda E+Q^{\prime})\,\subset\lambda E
.
This implies that the sequence
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
is bounded above:
all sets
\Omega_{i}
are inside
\lambda E
, where
\lambda
is large enough.
Therefore, for some
k
, we have
\Omega_{k+1}=\Omega_{k}
.
It remains to put
\Omega=\Omega_{k}
.

The set
\Omega_{0}
can be defined as

H+\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the set of integer
vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|\leq 1
,
and
H
is an arbitrary compact set such that
{\mathcal{J}}H\,\subset\,H

[
43
]
.
For example, one may take the aforementioned Lyapunov ellipsoid
E
,
which can be found by solving the corresponding semidefinite programming problem.
