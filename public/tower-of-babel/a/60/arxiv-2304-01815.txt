Consolidated Control Barrier Functions: Synthesis and Online Verification via Adaptation under Input Constraints
Mitchell Black, Dimitra Panagou

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a novel adaptation-based approach to constrained control design under multiple state and input constraints. Specifically, we introduce a method for synthesizing any number of time-varying candidate control barrier functions (CBF) into one consolidated CBF (C-CBF) candidate, and propose a predictor-corrector optimization-based adaptation law for the weights of the constituent constraint functions that certifies the C-CBF as valid for a class of nonlinear, control-affine systems. We prove this result by showing that the adapted weights are guaranteed to confer sufficient control authority to meet the new, adaptive C-CBF condition in perpetuity despite input constraints, which thereby permits its use in a quadratic program based control law. We then illustrate the performance of our controller on an academic example, and further highlight that it is successful even for constraint functions with higher or mixed relative-degree by simulating a reach-avoid problem for bicycle robots, which we use to demonstrate how our approach out-performs two baseline approaches.

Since the arrival of control barrier functions (CBFs) to the field of safety-critical systems

As a set-theoretic approach founded on the notion of forward invariance, CBFs certify adherence to constraints in that they ensure that any state beginning within a given set remains so for all future time. In the context of control design, CBF conditions are often used as constraints in quadratic program (QP)-based control laws, either as safety filters

Though for a single candidate CBF there exist guarantees of validity under certain conditions for systems with unbounded

[

The problem of safe control design under multiple constraints is especially relevant in practical applications involving autonomous vehicles and mobile robots, where there may be liveness-based and/or spatiotemporal specifications in addition to safety constraints. In many cases, the joint satisfaction of safety and liveness constraints has been treated by synthesizing CBF-CLF-QP controllers

I-A

Summary of Contributions

•

We formalize the notion of a consolidated CBF (C-CBF) by proposing a generic class of candidate functions that smoothly synthesizes any arbitrary number of time-varying constraint functions into one.

•

We utilize a predictor-corrector interior point method to propose an optimization-based adaptation law for the weights of the C-CBF candidate’s constituent functions. We show that the resulting weights render the C-CBF forward invariance condition viable at all times despite limited control authority, which serves to certify the C-CBF candidate as valid and permits the use of existing approaches to CBF-based control design for a class of nonlinear, control-affine systems.

•

We illustrate the performance of our approach on a one-dimensional, academic example which requires the controller to recognize and avoid regions of the state space in which the specified constraints are satisfied, but where no control input is able to prevent the constraints from being violated in the future.

•

Finally, we provide a comparative example utilizing a (car-like) robot with bicycle dynamics to highlight how our proposed controller solves a reach-avoid problem in which two baseline controllers fail, and highlight how the weight adaptation ensures that constraints are satisfied independent of their relative-degree.

In our prior work
[
35
]
, we introduced an adaptation law for rendering a similar form of consolidated CBF viable with respect to
time-invariant
constraint functions under
unbounded
control authority. Previously, the parameter adaptation law was taken to be the solution to a QP, as opposed to the predictor-corrector interior point method introduced in this paper. In addition, we extend our previous framework to handle spatiotemporal constraints (i.e., time-varying) and a class of input constraints. In this sense, the work herein has not been proposed to any other venue.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II
introduces some preliminaries, including time-varying convex optimization and constrained control design with CBFs. In Section
III
, we introduce the form of our C-CBF and propose a parameter adaptation law for rendering it valid. Section
IV
introduces our proposed QP-based control law, the performance of which we demonstrate with two simulation examples in Section
V

\displaystyle\small\boldsymbol{y}^{*}(t)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\R^{p}}\;J(t,\boldsymbol{y}),\hskip 9.24994pt\textrm{s.t.}\;\;c_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{y})\leq 0,\;\forall i\in[q],

II-C

Problem Statements

The main open problem in constrained control design under multiple state and input constraints is in certifying that all constraints will satisfied

means that each weight
w_{i}
affects the relative importance of
h_{i}
in
H
(and by extension on the set
\mathcal{D}
). It also allows us, however, to leverage existing CBF results for certifiable constrained control design. Notably, we may then use the class of QP based controllers (
11
) with the new function
H
to render
\mathcal{D}
forward-invariant and thus to ensure that the state remains within the complete constraint set
\mathcal{S}
. Proving that
H
is a valid C-CBF, however, may be difficult when using static constituent function weights
\boldsymbol{w}
, i.e., weights chosen a priori with
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}\equiv\mathbf{0}_{c\times 1}
. We therefore seek to design a weight adaptation law
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}=\omega(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})
,
\omega:\mathcal{T}\times\R_{+}^{c}\times\R^{n}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\R^{c}
, that varies the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
online and, in doing so, renders the C-CBF candidate
H
valid.
With
\boldsymbol{w}
then dependent on time
t
, in the remainder we omit the dependence of
\mathcal{D}
on the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
for conciseness.
To render the C-CBF candidate
H
valid is to ensure that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\dot{H}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\geq-\alpha\big(H(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\big),

(15)

holds for all
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(t)
,
\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
. Notice that the above is to the C-CBF candidate
H
as (
9
) is to a CBF candidate
h_{i}
. Thus, we refer to (
15
) as the C-CBF condition. While we have established that the CBF condition (
9
) is affine in the control input
\boldsymbol{u}
for the class of systems described by (
4
), the C-CBF condition (
15
) is only affine in
\boldsymbol{u}
if the adaptation law
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
is affine in (or independent of)
\boldsymbol{u}
, i.e., if
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
takes the form

\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}=\omega(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\coloneqq\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})+\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u},

(16)

for some functions
\mu:\mathcal{T}\times\R_{+}^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c}
and
\nu:\mathcal{T}\times\R_{+}^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c\times m}
. With
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
taking this form, we may directly include the C-CBF condition (
15
) as a linear constraint in a QP-based control law of the form (
11
). As such, we now formally introduce the second problem under consideration.

Problem 2

Given a function
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
, a consolidated control barrier function candidate
H
, and its corresponding CBF control set

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{H}(t,\alpha)=\big\{

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}:

(17)

\displaystyle\dot{H}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})+\alpha\big(H(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\big)\geq 0\big\},

design a control-affine weight adaptation law of the form (
16
) such that
\mathcal{U}_{H}(t,\alpha)\neq\emptyset
for all
t\in\mathcal{T}
, i.e., such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\right)\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\delta\implies

(18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\alpha\big(H(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\big)>-\delta,

for all
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(t)
,
\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
,
\delta\geq 0
.

Notice that (
18
) constitutes a sufficient condition for the satisfaction of the C-CBF condition (
15
), and therefore ensures that the CBF control set
\mathcal{U}_{H}(t,\alpha)
is never empty.

III
Adaptation for Consolidated CBF Synthesis

In this section, we first introduce our proposed solution to Problem
1
, a consolidated control barrier function (C-CBF) candidate that smoothly synthesizes multiple constraint functions into one whose zero super-level set under-approximates the complete constraint set, and then we design a parameter adaptation law which renders the candidate C-CBF valid for constrained control design.

III-A

Consolidated CBFs

The form of our C-CBF candidate
H:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}_{+}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R
is

H(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})=1-\sum_{i=1}^{c}\phi\Big(h_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{x}),w_{i}\Big),

(19)

where
\phi:\R_{+}\times\R_{+}\rightarrow\R_{+}
belongs to class-
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}
, is twice continuously differentiable, and satisfies
\phi(r,0)=\phi(0,s)=\phi(0,0)=1
. Note that the specified domain considers only positive values for
w_{i}
and
h_{i}
, a reasonable choice since
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\notin\mathcal{D}(t)
if some
h_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{x}(t))\leq 0
with
w_{i}\geq 0
or if some
w_{i}\leq 0
with
h_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{x}(t))\geq 0
. Both the decaying exponential function
\phi(r,s)=e^{-rs}
and the class of reciprocal functions of the form
\phi(r,s)=v/(rs+v)
,
v>0
, for example, satisfy the above requirements over the admissible domain. The function
\phi
thus encodes that the zero super-level set of
H
is a subset of the complete constraint set
\mathcal{S}(t)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}(t)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\R^{n}\mid H(t,\boldsymbol{w}(t),\boldsymbol{x})\geq 0\}\subset\mathcal{S}(t),\;\forall t\in\mathcal{T},

provided that
\boldsymbol{w}\in\R^{c}_{+}
. Note that the weights behave as a shape parameter for the function
\phi
, and are neither used to construct a weighted average nor required to sum to any specific amount. Instead, observe that larger weights
\boldsymbol{w}
allow
\mathcal{D}(t)
to more closely approach
\mathcal{S}(t)
, as for a given state it follows that
\phi(h_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{x}),w_{i}^{l})<\phi(h_{i}(t,\boldsymbol{x}),w_{i}^{s})
for
w_{i}^{l}>w_{i}^{s}>0
. This means that a higher weight
w_{i}
confers smaller relative importance of its associated constraint function
h_{i}
to the C-CBF candidate
H
. For example, in Figure
2
the effect of two different weights on the level sets of two obstacle avoidance constraint functions is depicted. In this case, obstacle two has smaller relative importance (i.e.,
w_{2}>w_{1}
) and thus the system takes actions toward obstacle two until it reaches a region where the level set values decrease very quickly to zero, at which point it makes an evasive maneuver. Thus, the system effectively ignores obstacle two until it must take action to avoid it.

Fig. 2:
A contrived example of a system seeking to reach a target location (green star) while adapting weights
\boldsymbol{w}
in the presence of two elliptical obstacles of identical size and shape whose constraint functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
define exclusion sets
\mathcal{O}_{1}(t)=\R^{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{1}(t)
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}(t)=\R^{n}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{2}(t)
. Here, the system gives greater relative importance to obstacle one (i.e.,
w_{1}<w_{2}
) and therefore the function
H
views obstacle one as having a shallow level curve topography (depicted as level curves
l_{1}<l_{2}<l_{3}<l_{4}
spaced further apart with smaller gradient magnitude
\nabla_{x}\phi_{1}
), whereas it sees obstacle two as having steep gradients (level curves close together, with larger
\nabla_{x}\phi_{2}
) near the obstacle. This allows the state to more closely approach obstacle two before deciding to take evasive action.

We have then that
H
defined by (
19
) solves Problem
1
and is referred to as a C-CBF
candidate
. Given an arbitrary weight vector
\boldsymbol{w}
, however, it may not be true that
H
is a
valid
CBF for (
4
) with respect to the set
\mathcal{D}(t)
. Without adapting the weights as the system moves through the state space the C-CBF condition (
18
) may be violated.
In what follows we introduce an online parameter adaptation law to enforce (
18
) and, in doing so, to solve Problem
2
.

III-B

Adaptation for Online Verification

Consider that in addition to the C-CBF weights
\boldsymbol{w}
being used to enforce the condition (
15
), it may be desirable to choose weights that are optimal with respect to some objective function
J:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R
. As such, we propose using a predictor-corrector interior point method for our adaptation law.

Consider the following optimal solution trajectory:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{*}(t)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\R^{c}}\;J(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x}),\;\;\textrm{s.t.}\;\;\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}(t),

(20)

where
J
is strongly uniformly convex with respect to
\boldsymbol{w}
, twice continuously differentiable in both
\boldsymbol{w}
and
\boldsymbol{x}
, and piecewise continuously differentiable in
t
. The feasible region is

\mathcal{W}(t)=\left\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\R^{c}\mid b_{j}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\leq 0,\forall j\in[2c+1]\right\},

(21)

where the first
c
constraint functions
b_{j}:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R
enforce that each
w_{j}
remains above a user-specified minimum threshold
w_{min}>0
to ensure that
\boldsymbol{w}(t)\in\R^{c}_{+},\forall t\in\mathcal{T}
, i.e.,

b_{j}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})=w_{min}-w_{j},\;\forall j\in[c],

(22)

the second
c
constraint functions
b_{j}:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R
enforce that each
w_{j}
remains below a user-specified maximum threshold
w_{max}<\infty
to ensure that
\boldsymbol{w}(t)
is well-defined, i.e.,

b_{j+c}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})=w_{j}-w_{max},\;\forall j\in[c],

(23)

and the final constraint function
b_{2c+1}:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R
enforces that the C-CBF condition (
15
) is satisfied, i.e., namely that

\displaystyle\beta(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}

(24)

\displaystyle-\alpha(H(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x}))-\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u}\right]\leq 0.

For the moment, let us consider that
b_{2c+1}=\beta
.
Recognizing that the optimal solution trajectory (
20
) is of the form (
1
), we similarly consider the approximate optimal solution trajectory

\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}^{*}(t)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\R^{c}}\;\Phi(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x}),

(25)

where
\Phi
is analogous to
\Psi
in (
2
) and is defined by

\Phi(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq J(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{2c+1}\log\big(-b_{j}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\big),

(26)

for barrier parameter
s>0
. We assume that Assumption
1
holds for
\Phi
i.e., that there exists
s>0
rendering
\Phi
strongly uniformly convex with respect to
\boldsymbol{w}
. As stated previously, this is reasonable in practice given a sufficiently large choice of
s
. We therefore propose to adapt the weights
\boldsymbol{w}
according to a control-affine dynamical system of the form (
3
):

\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}=\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})+\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{u},

(27)

where
\mu:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c}
and
\nu:\mathcal{T}\times\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c\times m}
are defined by

\displaystyle\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{ww}}^{-1}\Phi\big(\boldsymbol{P}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}\Phi+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{wx}}\Phi f(\boldsymbol{x})+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}t}\Phi\big),

(28)

\displaystyle\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{ww}}^{-1}\Phi\nabla_{\boldsymbol{wx}}\Phi g(\boldsymbol{x}),

(29)

for which
\boldsymbol{P}\in\R^{c\times c}
is a positive-definite gain matrix. Note that
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
given by (
27
) is affine in
\boldsymbol{u}
as required in the statement of Problem
2
, but, however, that because
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
appears explicitly in (
24
) (and therefore in (
26
)) the adaptation law given by (
27
) with
b_{2c+1}=\beta
requires solving a partial differential equation (PDE). Rather than attempt to solve this (possibly intractable) PDE, we break the algebraic loop by introducing
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}(t)
as filtered versions of
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\coloneqq\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w}(t),\boldsymbol{x}(t))
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}(t)\coloneqq\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w}(t),\boldsymbol{x}(t))
. We assume that these filtered variables track their unfiltered counterparts sufficiently closely, as stated in the following.

Assumption 2

There exist bounded, positive constants
\eta_{\mu}
and
\eta_{\nu}
, i.e.,
0<\eta_{\mu},\eta_{\nu}<\infty
, such that the following relations hold:

\min_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\left[\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}(t)-\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\right)\right]\geq-\eta_{\mu},

(30)

which implies that the deviation between the effect of the filtered signal
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}
and true signal
\boldsymbol{\mu}
on the C-CBF dynamics is bounded by
\eta_{\mu}
, and

\displaystyle\min_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\bigg[\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x}(t))+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}(t)\right)\boldsymbol{u}

(31)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\;-\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x}(t))+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\nu}(t))\right)\boldsymbol{u}\bigg]\geq-\eta_{\nu},

which similarly implies that the deviation (considering the worst input) between the effect of the filtered signal
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}
and true signal
\boldsymbol{\nu}
on the C-CBF dynamics is bounded by
\eta_{\nu}
.

The above implicitly define worst-case error bounds
\eta_{\mu}
and
\eta_{\nu}
on how the filtered signals
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}
affect
\dot{H}
versus the true signals
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, and thus the inequality in (
24
) may be satisfied by using
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}
,
\eta_{\mu}
, and
\eta_{\nu}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. In practice, (
30
) and (
31
) may be satisfied via appropriate filter design.
We are now ready to define the final constraint function as

b_{2c+1}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})=\delta(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})-\left|q(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\right|^{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{max},

(32)

which is directly (
24
) with the filter variables (and error bounds), specifically

\displaystyle\delta(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})=\eta_{\mu}+\eta_{\nu}-

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})

(33)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}(t)-\alpha(H),

and

q(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{L}_{g}^{T}p_{h}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}(t)^{T}p_{w}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x}),

(34)

with the C-CBF control matrix
\boldsymbol{L}_{g}
(omitting the dependence on
\boldsymbol{x}
) defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{g}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\cdots\frac{\partial h_{c}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right]^{T}g(\boldsymbol{x})\in\R^{c\times m},

(35)

and the functions
p_{h}:\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c}
and
p_{w}:\R^{c}\times\R^{n}\rightarrow\R^{c}
given by

\displaystyle p_{h}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial\phi(w_{1},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial h_{1}}\;\cdots\;\frac{\partial\phi(w_{c},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial h_{c}}\right]^{T}

(36)

\displaystyle p_{w}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial\phi(w_{1},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial w_{1}}\;\cdots\;\frac{\partial\phi(w_{c},\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial w_{c}}\right]^{T}.

(37)

These modifications allow us to include
b_{2c+1}
directly as a function constraining the optimal solution trajectory given by (
20
) without needing to solve a complicated PDE.

We are now ready to state our main result.

Theorem 2

Consider
c\geq 1
twice continuously differentiable constraint functions
h_{i}
defining sets
\mathcal{S}_{i}(t)
as in (
6
),
\forall i\in[c]
, and the associated C-CBF candidate given by (
19
) with constituent weights
\boldsymbol{w}
. Suppose that Assumption
2
holds, and that
\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{W}(t))\neq\emptyset
for all
t\geq 0
with
\boldsymbol{w}(0)\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{W}(0))
. Then, under the adaptation law (
27
), the C-CBF candidate
H
given by (
19
) is rendered valid, i.e., the condition (
15
) is satisfied for all
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(t)
and
\delta\geq 0
, for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof:

We will first derive an expression for
\dot{H}
, and then show that when
\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}
is given by (
27
) there always exists a control input
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\dot{H}(t,\boldsymbol{w},\dot{\boldsymbol{w}},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\geq-\alpha(H)

First, observe that according to (
19

takes the following form:

\displaystyle\dot{H}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{c}\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial h_{i}}\dot{h}_{i}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial w_{i}}\dot{w}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(p_{h}^{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{h}}+p_{w}^{T}\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(p_{h}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{L}_{t}+\boldsymbol{L}_{f}+\boldsymbol{L}_{g}\boldsymbol{u}\right)+p_{w}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\nu}\boldsymbol{u})\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})
are given by (
28
) and (
29
) respectively,
\boldsymbol{L}_{g}
by (
35
),
p_{h}
and
p_{w}
by (
36
) and (
37
), and where (omitting the dependence on
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{w}
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial t}\cdots\frac{\partial h_{c}}{\partial t}\right]^{T}\in\R^{c},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{f}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\cdots\frac{\partial h_{1}}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}\right]^{T}f(\boldsymbol{x})\in\R^{c}.

Given
\alpha\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
, it follows that
\dot{H}+\alpha(H)=a+\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}
, with

and
\boldsymbol{b}=-(\boldsymbol{L}_{g}^{T}p_{h}+\boldsymbol{\nu}^{T}p_{w})

4

27
) may be expressed as

\small\dot{\boldsymbol{w}}=-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{ww}}^{-1}\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{P}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}\Phi+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{wx}}\Phi\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}+\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}t}\Phi\right),

which, for
\Phi
defined by (
26

\Phi
strongly, uniformly convex,
\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{W}(t))\neq\emptyset

, and
\boldsymbol{w}(0)\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathcal{W}(0))
, it follows from Lemma

\boldsymbol{w}(t)\in\mathcal{W}(t)
,
\forall t\geq 0
, which implies that

for all

,

. While constraints

encode that each

and constraints

encodes that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{f}

\displaystyle-\eta_{\mu}+\alpha(H)+

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}\bigg|\boldsymbol{u}_{max}-\eta_{\nu}\geq 0.

Then, by observing from the input constraint set (

\bigg|\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}\bigg|\boldsymbol{u}_{max}=\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{f}\right)\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\dot{H}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}f(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\mu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})+\alpha(H)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\bigg[\bigg(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{x}}g(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{w}}\nu(t,\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{x})\bigg)\boldsymbol{u}\bigg],

The above result is predicated on the following assumptions holding. First, the convexity condition imposed on
\Phi
as outlined in Assumption
1
: this may require monitoring the eigenvalues of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{ww}}\Phi
online and increasing

if necessary, but in our experiments we have found this to be unproblematic. Second, and most challenging, the filter design for
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}(t)
must be sufficiently accurate as detailed in Assumption

. The robustness margins
\eta_{\mu}
and
\eta_{\nu}
may be difficult to determine a priori without knowledge of how the system will evolve over time. In our numerical experiments, we used a trial and error process with first-order low-pass filters and determined that

typically worked well for the case studies undertaken. The third and final set of assumptions involves the feasible region

, namely that

and that

. Ensuring that the initial weights belong to the feasible set is fairly straightforward: we choose a set of weights and if they do not belong to the feasible set we adapt them using only the correction term of the adaptation law

. Ensuring that

does imply, however, is that if the feasible region has an interior point then the adaptation law (

,

(which excludes the constraint function encoding the C-CBF condition) in order to consider an enlarged feasible region and ensure that our adaptation law is well-defined. We refer the interested reader to e.g., the discussion in
[

.

Discussion

From the above result, it is evident that the purpose behind the parameter adaptation law

In our case, we assume that the foundational optimization problem for our proposed adaptation law satisfies Slater’s condition, i.e., that the feasible region contains an interior point. Without this assumption, analyzing the stability properties of the interconnected dynamical system

Numerical Case Studies

In this section, we highlight the performance of our proposed adaptive C-CBF controller on two examples: an illustrative one-dimensional problem, and a reach-avoid scenario inspired by mobile robots with which we compare our method to several baseline controllers.

V-A

One-Dimensional Nonlinear System

For demonstration purposes we consider the following nonlinear, control-affine dynamical system:

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed the problem of safe-control synthesis under multiple spatiotemporal and input constraints via a novel consolidated control barrier function. We first synthesized one candidate CBF from many weighted constituents, and then proposed a weight-adaptation law, inspired by interior-point methods, under which the C-CBF is rendered valid for constrained control design. We proposed the use of a C-CBF-QP control law, and proved its feasibility under the developed adaptation law. To validate the approach, we demonstrated its success on an unstable 1-D example and further highlighted its utility for generic constraints of arbitrary relative-degree on a robot-inspired reach-avoid problem subject to spatiotemporal constraints.

In the future, we plan to explore the stability properties of the complete, interconnected dynamical system given by (
45
) in hopes of robustifying the constraint satisfaction guarantees. Additional future directions may also include the use of alternative interior point methods to improve the asymptotic convergence properties of the current approach, generalizing the class of input constraints considered, and exploring the optimality of the adaptation and control laws over time intervals.
