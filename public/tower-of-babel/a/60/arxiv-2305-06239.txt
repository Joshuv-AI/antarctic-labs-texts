Nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation with degenerate mobility: Incompressible limit and convergence to stationary states
Charles Elbar, Benoît Perthame, Andrea Poiatti, Jakub Skrzeczkowski

Abstract
The link between compressible models of tissue growth and the Hele-Shaw free boundary problem of fluid mechanics has recently attracted a lot of attention. In most of these models, only repulsive forces and advection terms are taken into account. In order to take into account long range interactions, we include for the first time a surface tension effect by adding a nonlocal term which leads to the degenerate nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation, and study the incompressible limit of the system. The degeneracy and the source term are the main difficulties. Our approach relies on a new L ∞ L^{\infty} estimate obtained by De Giorgi iterations and on a uniform control of the energy despite the source term. We also prove the long-term convergence to a single constant stationary state of any weak solution using entropy methods, even when a source term is present. Our result shows that the surface tension in the nonlocal (and even local) Cahn-Hilliard equation will not prevent the tumor from completely invading the domain.

1
Introduction

Nonlocal parabolic equations are commonly used to describe living tissues because cells experience two types of forces: repulsive and attractive. The repulsion arises at high volume fraction because, locally, cells occupy a non-vanishing volume, while cell adhesion and chemotaxis create attraction at long range, i.e., low densities
[
4
]
. These effects, as well as surface tension effects, can be considered by using the Cahn-Hilliard equation (see, e.g.,
[
44
]
for a review on these models). Our work is dedicated to the analysis of the nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation for long range interactions with a repulsive potential. More precisely we are interested in
two results: the incompressible limit connecting mechanistic and free boundary descriptions of the tissue and the long-time asymptotics of equations. Concerning the first result, the main difficulty is that we lose any maximum principle and we have to rely on different arguments to obtain the same results concerning the incompressible limit. Concerning the convergence to the stationary state, we prove that it converges to a nonnegative constant which shows that the surface tension effect (modeled by the Cahn-Hilliard equation) is not strong enough to prevent the tumor from invading the entire domain.

1.1
Mathematical setting

Our settings is as follows: we let
\Omega
be the
d=1,2,3
dimensional flat torus in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is particularly useful when treating nonlocal terms and we consider the equation

\begin{split}\partial_{t}u-\text{div}(u\nabla\mu)=uG(p)\quad&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mu=p+B_{\varepsilon}(u)\quad&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\end{split}

(1.1)

with the initial condition
u(0)=u_{0}\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega
and
u(x,t)\geq 0
represents the cell density. Here,
B_{\varepsilon}
denotes the nonlocal operator defined as

B_{\varepsilon}[u](x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(u(x)-\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u(x))=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)(u(x)-u(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

(1.2)

for fixed
\varepsilon
small enough (in order to be able to use
[
23
, Lemma C.1]
and Lemma
A.3
) and
\omega_{\varepsilon}
is a usual mollification kernel
\omega_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\omega(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
with
\omega
compactly supported in the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=1,\quad\omega(\cdot)\text{ is radial}.

(1.3)

The pressure and source term are defined, for
u\geq 0
, as

p=u^{\gamma},\quad\gamma\geq 1,\qquad G(p)=p_{H}-p,

(1.4)

with
p_{H}>0
a constant called the homeostatic pressure, which is the threshold where cells begin to die, assuming that pressure produces an inhibitory effect on cell proliferation.

We comment the different terms appearing in the equation. First,
u
is the density of tumor cells and can be thought of as being between 0 and 1. However, this fact is not easy to prove since the maximum principle does not hold here. Using a De Giorgi iteration technique, we can prove however that the bound holds with a small perturbation term which vanishes as
\gamma\to\infty
(see Lemma
3.1
). From the Cahn-Hilliard terminology, we refer to
\mu
as the chemical potential, which is composed by two terms: one is the pressure
p=u^{\gamma}
and the other is
B_{\varepsilon}
, the approximation of the Laplace operator, which takes into account surface tension effects, see for instance
[
23
]
.
Concerning the initial condition, we distinguish two sets of assumptions.

1.2
The main results

Our first result establishes the incompressible limit
\gamma\to\infty
of the system (
1.1
) which links two descriptions of the tumor growth: mechanistic and free-boundary. The main mathematical novelty here is the nonlocality which makes it difficult to establish the uniform
L^{\infty}
bound on
u_{\gamma}
. To overcome this problem, we apply the De Giorgi iterations, see Lemma
3.1
, in the spirit of
[
28
,
48
]
.

Theorem 1.5

(Incompressible limit)
.

Let
u_{\gamma}
be a weak solution to (
1.1
) as defined in Lemma
1.3
and initial datum satisfying Assumptions
1.1
-
1.2
.
Then, as
\gamma\to\infty
, we have for all
T>0
, up to a (not relabeled) subsequence

\displaystyle u_{\gamma}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),

\displaystyle u_{\gamma}\to u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{q}(\Omega\times(0,T))\quad\forall q\in[2,+\infty),

\displaystyle u_{\gamma}\rightharpoonup u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}),

\displaystyle p_{\gamma}\rightharpoonup p_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle p_{\gamma}\to p_{\infty}\text{ in }L^{r}(\Omega\times(0,T)),\quad\forall r\in[2,3),

where
u_{\infty}
and
p_{\infty}
satisfy in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times[0,\infty))

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\infty}-\DIV(u_{\infty}\nabla(p_{\infty}+B_{\varepsilon}(u_{\infty})))=u_{\infty}G(p_{\infty}),

(1.15)

\displaystyle p_{\infty}\Big(\Delta{p}_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta u_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\DIV(u_{\infty}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\infty}))+u_{\infty}G(p_{\infty})\Big)=0,

(1.16)

\displaystyle 0\leq u_{\infty}\leq 1,\quad p_{\infty}\geq 0,\quad p_{\infty}(1-u_{\infty})=0\quad\text{almost everywhere in }\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.17)

with
u_{\infty}(0)=u_{0}
. Furthermore it holds
\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},p_{\infty}\rangle_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime},H^{1}(\Omega))}=0
for almost any
t\in(0,\infty)
.

This theorem entails that in the limit
\gamma\to\infty
we can consider the measurable set
\Omega(t):=\{x\in\Omega:\ p_{\infty}(t)>0\}
, where
u_{\infty}=1
by the graph relation (
1.17
) so that it can be interpreted as the ‘tumor zone’. Note that it must hold

\displaystyle-\Delta{p}_{\infty}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta u_{\infty}^{2}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\DIV(u_{\infty}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\infty}))+u_{\infty}G(p_{\infty})\quad\text{ in }{\cal I}nt(\Omega(t))

which yields a Hele-Shaw type equation.

Our second result is concerned with the convergence to stationary states. We distinguish two cases: when
G(p)=p_{H}-p
and when
G(p)=0
. The main novelty lies in the first case which is not conservative and its proof requires a careful analysis of the entropy. We prove that as
t\to\infty
, the solution converges to the constant
p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}>0
, which shows that the surface tension is not strong enough to prevent the tumor from invading the entire domain. We have the

Theorem 1.6

(Long time behaviour)
.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
) with fixed
\gamma\geq 1
in the sense of Lemma
1.3
and initial datum satisfying Assumption
1.1
Then, if
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
, there are two cases:

•

for
G(p)=p_{H}-p
, we have

\|u(t)-p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty,\quad\forall q\in[1,\gamma+1).

(1.18)

•

For
G(p)=0
and
\gamma\geq 1
, we have an exponential decay towards the mean value: there exists a constant
C=C(\Omega,\gamma,q,\Phi(u_{0}),\overline{u}_{0})
such that

\|u(t)-\overline{u}_{0}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}\lesssim e^{-Ct},\quad\forall q\in[1,\gamma+1).

(1.19)

There are two possibilities to prevent steady states to be constant. The first one is to consider different potentials than just repulsive ones like
u^{\gamma}
. The second possibility is to include an external force, which can be taken into account either by a generic force that acts directly on the cells like it was done in
[
22
]
. One can also include the effects of nutriments and impose that the tumor cells die in the regions where there are no nutriments.

Remark 1.7
.

In the case of nonzero source term
G
, Theorem
1.6
implies that the constant solution
u\equiv 0
is unstable, whereas
p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
is the only asymptotically stable equilibrium and
any
weak solution
u
(in the sense of Lemma
1.3
) departing from
any
nonzero initial datum converges to
p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
.

Literature review

Incompressible limit for tumor model.
The incompressible limit connects two models of tumor growth: the compressible one studied in
[
47
]
and the free-boundary one analysed in
[
42
]
. Many substantial contributions followed
[
47
]
, allowing nutrients
[
14
]
, advection effects
[
15
,
35
,
34
]
, additional structuring variable
[
13
]
, congesting flows
[

]
, two species
[

]
or including additional surface tension effects via the degenerate Cahn-Hilliard equation
[

,
22
]
.

One major difficulty for establishing the incompressible limit is proving strong compactness of the gradient of the pressure. The main tool is the celebrated Aronson-Benilan estimate
[
3
,
12
]
. The estimate has been recently readressed in
[
5
]
but the generalization available there are not applicable for the pressure
p=u^{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}u
as in our case cf.
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
.
Another direct technique was developed in
[
38
]
which is based on deducing strong convergence from a sort of energy equality. This is the strategy we follow in our proof.

Nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation.
The nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard is a variant of the Cahn-Hilliard equation proposed to model dynamics of phase separation
[
9
]
. While originally introduced in the context of material science, it is currently widely applied also in biology
[
40
,
2
,
46
]
. The nonlocal equation
was obtained for the first time by Giacomin and Lebowitz as the limit of interacting particle systems
[
30
,
29
]
. Their work can be considered as the first derivation of the Cahn-Hilliard equation up to a delicate limit from the nonlocal equation to the local one. The latter problem was in fact addressed only recently, first for the case of the constant mobility
[
18
,
16
,
41
,
17
]
and finally for the case of degenerate mobility
[
23
,
10
]
. Another derivation as a hydrodynamic limit of the Vlasov equation was proposed recently in
[
20
]
, following
[
51
]
. In recent years nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation was also studied in couplings with other hydrodynamic models, like Navier-Stokes equations (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
24
,
25
,
27
]
and the references therein). Moreover, it has been adopted in many optimal control problems, we just mention
[
49
,
50
]
.

Entropy dissipation methods and asymptotic analysis.
For establishing convergence to stationary states we use methods based on the entropy dissipation. In the simplest scenario, it can be applied to PDEs equipped with the entropy
\Phi
which decreases with some dissipation

\partial_{t}\Phi(t)+\mathcal{D}\Phi(t)\leq 0,\qquad\mathcal{D}\Phi(t)\geq 0.

Then, one tries to prove that the dissipation is bounded from below by the entropy
|\Phi(t)|^{\alpha}\leq\mathcal{D}\Phi(t)
so that
\Phi(t)\to 0
with an explicit convergence rate (exponential if
\alpha=1
and polynomial if
\alpha>1
). Finally, by virtue of Csiszár-Kullback inequality, one deduces convergence in
L^{1}
. The last step requires conservation of mass which is not available when
G(p)\neq 0
in (
1.1
). We present the method in detail for the Cahn-Hilliard equation without the source term. Another method to obtain convergence to equilibrium, applied in the context of the Cahn-Hilliard equation, is via the Łojasiewicz-Simon inequality
[
11
,
1
,
39
]
. This method cannot be applied here due to the degenerate mobility and the lack of separation property from the degenerate case
u=0
.

2
Basic a priori estimates

The energy/entropy structure usually provides a priori estimates on the solutions. However, in the case of a source term which may change sign, we first need to control their dissipation. Before tackling this problem, we first show a basic estimate which ensures the control of the mass of the system, uniformly in time. This estimate is useful to obtain a first
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1}_{x}
bound on the solution. Our proof of these estimates is somehow formal but can be carried out rigorously with an approximation scheme as, e.g., in
[
23
]
. These estimates are also fundamental to prove the existence Lemma
1.3
.

2.1
Control of the mass

We recall that the total mass of the system is defined in (
1.11
) and we prove the corresponding bound.

Proposition 2.1

(Mass control)
.

For all
t\geq 0
we have
\overline{u}(t)\leq p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

Integrating equation (
1.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\overline{u}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}(u\,p_{H}-u^{\gamma+1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(2.1)

By the Hölder inequality,

\int_{\Omega}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}|\Omega|^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}}

we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\overline{u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p_{H}}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\left(\int_{\Omega}{u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\gamma+1}\frac{1}{|\Omega|^{{\gamma+1}}}=p_{H}\overline{u}-\overline{u}^{1+\gamma},

so that, with the Assumption
1.1
on the initial condition, we can conclude (
1.11
).
∎

2.2
Energy and entropy estimates

We recall that the energy, the entropy as well as their dissipation have been defined in (
1.5
)–(
1.8
). We prove that, for a fixed time horizon
T
, we have the following inequalities.

Proposition 2.2

(Control of the energy and entropy dissipation)
.

The inequalities hold

\begin{split}&\sup_{t\geq 0}\Phi(u(t))+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&+\frac{1}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\dfrac{4\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla|u|^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)(p-p_{H})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\Phi(u_{0}),\end{split}

(2.2)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u)&+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)(u(x)-u(x-y))(u^{\gamma+1}(x)-u^{\gamma+1}(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u|\nabla\mu|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C(\mathcal{E}(u)+1),\end{split}

(2.3)

and thus there exists
C(\mathcal{E}(u_{0}),T,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathcal{E}(u(t))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{2\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C(\mathcal{E}(u_{0}),T,\varepsilon).

Remark 2.3
.

The above estimate in the energy
\mathcal{E}
depends exponentially on the final time
T
. We improve this result to a global one
in Proposition
2.5
.

Proof.

Control of the entropy
. Note that
\Phi(u)\geq 0
by the inequality
x\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)-x+y\geq(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y})^{2}
for
x\geq 0
and
y>0
(

,
y=1
here).
Then we have

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)G(p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)(p_{H}-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0,

since
u\geq 0
and
x\mapsto\log x
is increasing for
x\geq 0
.
Therefore, all the terms in the dissipation of entropy in (
1.8
) are nonnegative so that we can integrate in time and obtain (
2.2
), which clearly implies the control of the entropy
independently
of
T
.

Energy control.
Turning to the energy
\mathcal{E}
, departing from (
1.7
), we observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}uG(p)\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u\,B_{\varepsilon}(u)(p_{H}-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The first term can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}up_{H}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}p_{H}^{2}\int_{\Omega}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C,

where we used the mass control (
1.11
). For the second term we have, by symmetry of

,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u\,B_{\varepsilon}(u)(p_{H}-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{H}}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|u(x)-u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)(u(x)-u(x-y))(u^{\gamma+1}(x)-u^{\gamma+1}(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

All together, these inequalities give immediately (
2.3
) and by the Gronwall lemma the energy control.
∎

In the limit

2.2

\|u_{\infty}\|_{L^{\infty}}=\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}\|u_{\infty}\|_{L^{\gamma}}\quad\text{and}\quad\|u\|_{L^{\gamma+1}}\leq C^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}(\gamma+1)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}\to 1

because of the weak convergence of

. We refer for instance to
[

,
52
]
. However in the next section we obtain a better control on

by the De Giorgi iteration method.

Now, we improve the local in time estimate on

to a global one, which is nontrivial due to the presence of the source term. Since Proposition
2.2
gives the uniform control
\Phi(u(t))\leq\Phi(u_{0})
for any
t\geq 0
, our aim is to control in a uniform way the energy
\mathcal{E}
as well.

Proposition 2.5

(Uniform in time estimates for the energy)
.

There exists a constant independent of time and
\gamma
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t)\leq C,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(2.4)

Proof.

Firstly, we estimate separately the two terms defining the energy in (
1.5
) using the entropy estimate (
2.2
). It immediately gives that, for a constant
C>0
independent of time and
\gamma
, for any sufficiently small
\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+1}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|u(x)-u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C(\Omega)\int_{t}^{t+1}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{t}^{t+1}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+C\int_{t}^{t+1}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

(2.5)

\displaystyle\leq C+Cp_{H}^{\frac{2}{\gamma}},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

where the second inequality follows from Equations (
A.4
) and (
1.11
).

Secondly, we control the remaining part of the energy
\mathcal{E}
, the one related to
\frac{1}{\gamma+1}\int_{\Omega}u^{\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. To this aim, we integrate Equation (
2.1
) in time over
[t,t+1]
and get

\overline{u}(t+1)-\overline{u}(t)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega}(up_{H}-u^{\gamma+1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

so that, rearranging the terms, we control the second term of the energy as

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega}u^{\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{t}^{t+1}\int_{\Omega}up_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\overline{u}(t)=(p_{H}+1)\int_{t}^{t+1}\overline{u}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C

because

for any
t\geq 0
thanks to (
1.11
). This, together with (
2.5
), implies that

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t+1}\mathcal{E}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C,

(2.6)

with
C>0
independent of
t\geq 0
and
\gamma
.

We may now conclude the energy estimate. By Proposition
2.2
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u)\leq C(\mathcal{E}(u)+1).

Using the Gronwall lemma, we obtain for all
t\geq 0
and all
0\leq s\leq 1
,

\mathcal{E}(u(t+1))\leq C\,\mathcal{E}(u(t+s))+C.

Integrating in
s
and using the bound (
2.6
), we conclude the proof of Proposition
2.5
.

∎

3
Incompressible limit: proof of Theorem
1.5

We see the incompressible limit of (
1.1
) as the limit
\gamma\to\infty
and the resulting problem turns out to be a free boundary problem of Hele-Shaw type. Concerning the techniques adopted here, we first show that, for any fixed
T>0
,
u_{\gamma}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
by a quantity which converges to
1
as
\gamma\to\infty
(see (
3.1
)). Due to the presence of the convolution term, which makes the equation nonlocal, we cannot apply any classical maximum principle, so that we need to resort to De Giorgi iterations, exploiting the fact that the equation is a second order differential equation.

With uniform estimates in
L^{\infty}
at hand, we may apply standard energy estimates to gain sufficient regularity on the pressure
p_{\gamma}
, which we bounded in
L^{3}(\Omega\times(0,T))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
uniformly in
\gamma
. Then, one can obtain uniform controls in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
for
\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}
and in
L^{1}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for
\partial_{t}p_{\gamma}
, so as to deduce the strong convergence of
u_{\gamma}
and
p_{\gamma}
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
to some
u_{\infty}
and
p_{\infty}
.
With the help of these bounds, we are able to pass to the limit as
\gamma\to\infty
in Equation (
1.1
) and obtain Equations (
1.15
) and (
1.17
) for the limit concentration
u_{\infty}
.

In order to obtain more information on
u_{\infty},p_{\infty}
, like complementarity conditions (
1.16
), we need a stronger convergence for
\nabla p_{\gamma}
. The standard technique uses some control on
\Delta p
thanks to the Aronson-Bénilan inequality (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
) which does
not apply here due to the higher-order term. In particular, the (formal) CH equation can be written as

\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}\Delta u^{\gamma+1}_{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta u^{2}_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\text{div}(u_{\gamma}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u)_{\gamma})=u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma}),

and the extra term
\Delta u_{\gamma}^{2}
, independent of
\gamma
, appearing, and this prevents us from obtaining the Aronson-Bénilan inequality. Taking inspiration from
[
13
,
38
]
, in the second part of the present section, we instead show the strong
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
convergence of

\nabla\left(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+1}u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+1}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}u^{2}_{\gamma}\right),

which is shown to be enough to guarantee the validity of the condition (
1.16
).

in

. Of course, by (

) and (

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\rightharpoonup\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\infty}\,\text{in }L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)),\quad\gamma\to\infty.

(3.28)

Let us first observe that (

) can be rewritten highlighting the presence of

:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}-\Delta\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}=u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\text{div}(u_{\gamma}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma})).

We multiply (

\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty}

. Since

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{(\gamma+2)(\gamma+1)}\int_{\Omega}u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+2}(T)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{(\gamma+2)(\gamma+1)}\int_{\Omega}u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+2}(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}(u_{\gamma}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma})\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\gamma},v_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The plan is to estimate

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{2}(u_{\gamma}+u_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{12\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{2}\rangle

so that, integrating over
[0,T]
and recalling that

, we get

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}(u_{\gamma}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}(T)-u_{\infty}(T))^{2}(u_{\gamma}(T)+u_{\infty}(T))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{12\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}(T)-u_{\infty}(T))^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},u^{2}_{\gamma}-u_{\infty}^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

(3.31)

Note that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}&(u_{\gamma}(T)-u_{\infty}(T))^{2}(u_{\gamma}(T)+u_{\infty}(T))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{12\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}(T)-u_{\infty}(T))^{3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{\gamma}(T)-u_{\infty}(T))^{2}\left(u_{\gamma}(T)+u_{\infty}(T)-\frac{1}{3}u_{\gamma}(T)+\frac{1}{3}u_{\infty}(T)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.\end{split}

(3.32)

Therefore, taking into account (
3.3
), (
3.32
) and
u_{\gamma}\geq 0
we obtain from (
3.30
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\cdot

\displaystyle\nabla(\widetilde{v}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{\gamma}{(\gamma+2)(\gamma+1)}\int_{\Omega}u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+2}(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}u_{\gamma}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}u_{\infty}^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma})\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\gamma},v_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

but by the weak convergence in (

) we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\infty}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\to 0,

since

. Thus we deduce that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\limsup_{\gamma\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\nabla\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

Then, recalling also that
\partial_{t}u_{\gamma}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\infty}
in

and that

, we deduce from (

)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq\limsup_{\gamma\to\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma}{(\gamma+2)(\gamma+1)}\int_{\Omega}u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+2}(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right.

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},(u_{\gamma}-u_{\infty})^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\left.-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},u_{\gamma}^{2}-u_{\infty}^{2}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

The plan is to prove that all the terms on the (RHS) of (

) converge to 0. First, we have that
0\leq u_{\gamma}(0)=u_{0}\leq p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
for any

by assumption, so that in the end we deduce

and thus, as

Then, using (
3.18
) and the fact that

, we immediately deduce

Concerning the term
\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
, we simply use the fact that
\{u_{\gamma}\}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
(cf. (
3.1
)),
\{G(p_{\gamma})\}
is bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
(cf. (
3.7
)) and
\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}\to\widetilde{v}_{\infty}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
. Similarly,
\int_{\Omega_{T}}u_{\gamma}\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma}\cdot\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\to 0
because of weak convergence (
3.28
) and strong convergence

in

(which follows by (

) and simple properties of convolutions).

We are left with the analysis of the last term, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
. Our aim is to show that this term vanishes, exploiting Theorem
A.2
. We introduce the following indicator function on
\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle I_{S}(s):=\begin{cases}0\quad\text{ if }s\leq 1,\\
+\infty\text{ if }s>1,\end{cases}

(3.35)

and define
S=(-\infty,1]
, which is a closed, convex and nonempty set, so that

is proper, convex and lower semicontinuous (see, e.g.,
[
45
, Appendix 1]
) and it holds

\partial I_{S}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}:y\cdot(x-s)\geq 0,\quad\forall s\leq 1\}.

We see that we have

•

u_{\infty}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u_{\infty}\in L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime})
;

•

for almost any
(x,t)\in\Omega_{T}
,

by (
3.26
) and being

. Indeed,

v_{\infty}(x,t)(u_{\infty}(x,t)-s)\geq 0,\quad\forall s\leq 1,

since, when

, being

, the inequality is always verified for any
s\leq 1
, whereas, when
u_{\infty}(x,t)<1
, it holds
v_{\infty}(x,t)=0
and thus the inequality is verified for any
s\leq 1
as well.

•

v_{\infty}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

Therefore, all the assumptions are verified and we can apply Theorem
A.2
with
h=I_{S}
,
f=u_{\infty}
,
g=v_{\infty}
, to infer, after an integration over
[0,T]
,

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,T))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,0))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(3.36)

by the definition of
I_{S}
. Indeed, it holds
\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,\cdot))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\equiv 0
on
[0,T]
for any
T>0
. To see this, first notice that, being
u_{\infty}\leq 1
almost everywhere in
\Omega\times[0,\infty)
, we deduce that, for almost any

,

Therefore,
\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,\cdot))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0
, for almost any
t\in[0,T]
and for any
T>0
. Recall now by Theorem

that
\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,\cdot))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\in AC([0,T])
for any

, which ensures that
\int_{\Omega}I_{S}(u_{\infty}(x,t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\equiv 0
for
any

t\in[0,T]
and for any
T>0
.

Having studied all the terms in the right-hand side of (

), in the end we conclude that

\limsup_{\gamma\to\infty}\int_{\Omega_{T}}|\nabla(\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}-\widetilde{v}_{\infty})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq 0,

implying that

∎

Step 3. The complementarity condition.

Now that we have all the necessary convergences, let us consider the following equation in distributional sense (this equation comes from multiplying (
1.1
) by
v_{\gamma}=u_{\gamma}^{\gamma+1}
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma+2}\partial_{t}u^{\gamma+2}_{\gamma}-v_{\gamma}\Delta\widetilde{v}_{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{\gamma}\text{div}(u_{\gamma}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\gamma}))=u_{\gamma}G(p_{\gamma})v_{\gamma}.

Thanks to the results of Step 2. and Lemma
3.4
, we can then pass the limit as
\gamma\to\infty
and obtain the complementarity condition:

\displaystyle v_{\infty}(\Delta\widetilde{v}_{\infty}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\text{div}(u_{\infty}(\nabla\omega\ast u_{\infty}))+u_{\infty}G(p_{\infty}))=0\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,T)),

(3.37)

for any
T>0
, so that in the end, recalling
v_{\infty}=p_{\infty}
and
\widetilde{v}_{\infty}=p_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}u_{\infty}^{2}
,

\displaystyle p_{\infty}(\Delta{p}_{\infty}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\Delta u_{\infty}^{2}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\text{div}(u_{\infty}(\nabla\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\infty}))+u_{\infty}G(p_{\infty}))=0\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),

(3.38)

which is the complementarity condition (

). In conclusion, since

, we can repeat the argument leading to (

), to infer that, for any

,

\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty},p_{\infty}\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0,\qquad\langle\partial_{t}u_{\infty}(t),p_{\infty}(t)\rangle=0,

thus concluding the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

4.1
Case

Proof of Theorem

.
We now divide the proof of Theorem

Step 1: characterization of possible limits.
We fix the initial datum
u_{0}\neq 0
as in the statement of Theorem
1.6
.
We show the following key result:

Lemma 4.1
.

From any divergent sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
we can extract a subsequence (not relabeled) such that, as

,

strongly in

. Moreover, either

and
\Phi(u(t))\to|\Omega|
as

\Phi(u(t))\to 0
as

.

We fix
T>0
, we also consider

large enough such that

. Observe that

solves the problem

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{n}-\text{div}(u_{n}\nabla\mu_{n})=u_{n}G(p_{n})\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(-T,T),\\

(4.1)

By Propositions
2.2
and
2.5
, we have the following uniform-in-

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{n}(t))\leq C\quad\forall t\in[-T,T],

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Phi(u_{n}(t))\leq\Phi(u_{0})\quad\forall t\in(-T,T),\qquad\Phi(u_{n}(t))\text{ non-increasing}.

(4.3)

From this and Propositions
2.2
and
2.6
, we deduce the following uniform bounds, for any

\begin{split}&\|u_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(-T,T;L^{\gamma+1}(\Omega))}+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2}(-T,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|u_{n}\|_{L^{2\gamma+1}(\Omega\times(-T,T))}\\

(4.4)

with

and

to the same function
u_{\ast}\geq 0

\displaystyle u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{\ast}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(-T,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\quad\text{and }L^{2\gamma+1}(\Omega\times(-T,T))

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}u_{\ast}\quad\text{in }L^{q^{\prime}}(-T,T;(W^{1,q}(\Omega))^{\prime})\quad\text{and in }L^{2}(-T,T;(W^{1,r}(\Omega))^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\nabla u_{n}^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}\rightharpoonup\nabla u_{\ast}^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T)).

We want to characterize

. Notice that from (

\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u_{n}(x)-\nabla u_{n}(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{n}-T}^{t_{n}+T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty

. By weak-lower semicontinuity, in the limit

\int_{-T}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u_{\ast}(x)-\nabla u_{\ast}(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=0

u_{\ast}(t)
is constant in space

) and the Fatou lemma,

u_{\ast}\log\left(\frac{u_{\ast}}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)(u_{\ast}^{\gamma}-p_{H})=0,\text{ for a.e. }t\in(-T,T),

so that either
u_{\ast}(t)=0
or
u_{\ast}(t)=p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}

u_{\ast}\equiv\overline{u}_{\ast}

. Then clearly

. This concludes the proof of Lemma

Step 2. Stability of the equilibria.
We complete Lemma

with the following

.

Under notation of Lemma

Since

\displaystyle|\Omega|=\lim_{t\to\infty}\Phi(u(t))\leq\Phi(u_{0})\leq|\Omega|.

decreasing for

almost everywhere in

.
∎

Step 3. Existence of the

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that

. Then it holds

\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t)=p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}\quad\text{ in }L^{q}(\Omega)\qquad\forall q\in[1,\gamma+1).

First note that, the pointwise values
u(t)
as an

. Now, we consider the decomposition of
\Phi
:

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=\dfrac{1}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\left(\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\dfrac{u}{\overline{u}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\dfrac{\overline{u}}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}(p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}-u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)

(4.5)

and we study the limits of terms appearing in (
4.5
). Since
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
, from Lemma
4.1
we obtain

\lim_{t\to\infty}\Phi(u(t))=\Phi(p_{H}^{1/\gamma})=0
.

By Proposition
2.1
, for any sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n}
there exists a (nonrelabeled) subsequence such that, for some
\kappa
,

\overline{u}(t_{n})\to\kappa\in[0,p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}].

(4.6)

We prove that
\kappa=p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
. Indeed, the function
g:x\mapsto\frac{x}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\log\left(\frac{x}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)-\frac{x}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}+1
is convex and continuous. As
\Phi(u(t))=\int_{\Omega}g(u(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, by Jensen’s inequality we get

0\leq g(\overline{u}(t))\leq\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\Phi(u(t))\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty

so that
g(\kappa)=0
and the claim follows. It follows that
\overline{u}(t)\to p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
as
t\to\infty
.

Hence, passing to the limit in (
4.5
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(t)\log\left(\dfrac{u(t)}{\overline{u}(t)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\to 0\text{ when }k\to\infty.

(4.7)

From Lemma
A.4
and (
4.7
), together with the fact that
\overline{u}(t)\to p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
as
n\to\infty
, we then deduce

\|u(t)-p_{H}^{1/\gamma}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\to 0\quad\text{when $k\to\infty$}.

Furthermore, by the bound in
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{\gamma+1}_{x}
given by the control of the energy
\mathcal{E}
in Proposition
2.2
, we can deduce the convergence (
1.18
) by interpolation. The proof of Theorem
1.6
in the case of a nonzero source term
G
is thus concluded.
∎

4.2
Case
G(p)=0

When there is no source term, the solution converges to the mean value. The argument is a simple consequence of the logarithmic Sobolev inequality and the Csiszár–Kullback–Pinsker inequality. We refer to
[
43
]
for other systems where it is applied. We consider the relative entropy between the solution
u
and a stationary state
u_{\ast}
, defined as

\Phi(u|u_{\ast})=\int_{\Omega}\left(u\log\left(\frac{u}{u_{\ast}}\right)-u+u_{\ast}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In our case
u_{\ast}=\overline{u}_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Notice that, by the conservation of mass, we have

\Phi(u|\overline{u})=\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{\overline{u}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Since
\overline{u}(\cdot)\equiv\overline{u}_{0}
, we see that
\Phi(u|\overline{u})
satisfies the identity (or at least the inequality for weak solutions): for almost any
t\geq 0
,

\frac{d\Phi(u|\overline{u})}{dt}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla u(x)-\nabla u(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{4\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^{2}}\left|\nabla|u|^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0.

The generalized logarithmic Sobolev inequality
[
43
, Section 3.1]
provides us the exponential decay of the relative entropy. Indeed we have

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
m\geq(d-2)_{+}/d
, there exists
C(\Omega,m)
such that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(u^{\frac{m}{2}})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq C(\Omega,m)\overline{u}^{m-1}\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{\overline{u}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

In our case
m=\gamma+1
and
\overline{u}\equiv\overline{u}_{0}
is bounded from below by a positive constant since we consider an initial condition
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
. Therefore, by the Gronwall Lemma we conclude that the entropy
\Phi
experiences an exponential decay as
t\to\infty
:

\frac{d\Phi(u|\overline{u})}{dt}+C(\Omega,\gamma)\overline{u}_{0}^{\gamma}\Phi(u|\overline{u})\leq 0

Therefore, we have, for some
C=C(\Omega,\gamma,\Phi(u_{0}),\overline{u}_{0})
,

\Phi(u|\overline{u})\leq Ce^{-Ct},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

To prove that this implies the exponential decay of the solution we use the Csiszár–Kullback–Pinsker inequality of Lemma
A.4
: there exists
C(\Omega)
such that

\Phi(u|\overline{u})\geq C(\Omega)\|u-\overline{u}_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

The exponential decay in
L^{1}(\Omega)
of
u
towards
\overline{u}_{0}
then easily follows. By the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma+1}(\Omega))
bound given by the control of the energy
\mathcal{E}
(which is the same as in the case with a source term
G
given in Proposition
2.2
), we can in conclusion deduce the exponential convergence (
1.19
) by interpolation. This ends the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

4.3
Longtime behavior of the local Cahn-Hilliard equation

The nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation can be also seen as an approximation of the local Cahn-Hilliard equation:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\text{div}(u\nabla\mu)=uG(p)\quad\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mu=p-\Delta u,\quad p=u^{\gamma},\\
u(0)=u_{0}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.\end{cases}

(4.8)

This follows, at least formally, with a Taylor expansion, using the symmetry of the kernel
\omega_{\varepsilon}
in the operator
B_{\varepsilon}
of (
1.1
), and for a rigorous proof we refer, e.g., to
[
23
]
. Therefore, one may wonder whether the previous results obtained for the nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation also hold for the local one. It turns out that for the convergence to the stationary states, the result is the same. Indeed, one mainly uses arguments based on the entropy, so that the nonlocal term does not play a role: concerning the entropy
\Phi
, we can consider again (
1.6
) and formally get for (
4.8
)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\Phi(u)+\frac{1}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\int_{\Omega}|\Delta u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{4\gamma}{(\gamma+1)^{2}}\left|\nabla|u|^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2}}\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}u\log\left(\frac{u}{p_{H}^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}\right)G(p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0,

(4.9)

which is very similar to the result in Proposition
2.2
.
Concerning the energy, we set

\mathcal{E}_{l}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{u^{1+\gamma}}{1+\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{l}(u)+\int_{\Omega}u|\nabla\mu|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}uG(p)\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Now we observe that, integrating by parts,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}uG(p)\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}(p_{H}-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}u\nabla p\cdot\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u(p_{H}-p)p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}(p_{H}-p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\gamma\int_{\Omega}p|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Moreover, notice that, since the relation (
1.11
) still holds with the same proof,

p_{H}\int_{\Omega}up\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{p_{H}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}up^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C.

Then we can rewrite the energy inequality as

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}_{l}(u)+\int_{\Omega}u|\nabla\mu|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}p^{2}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}p\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\gamma\int_{\Omega}p|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\leq C(\mathcal{E}_{l}(u)+1),

which is again very similar to the one obtained in Proposition
2.2
for the nonlocal case.
Therefore, with these estimates we can basically perform again all the arguments of Section
4
and obtain again the same result as in Theorem
1.6
. Note that in the local case, differently from the nonlocal one, we can also repeat the same arguments in the case of a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions
u\nabla\mu\cdot\textbf{n}=0
and
\nabla u\cdot\textbf{n}=0
on
\partial\Omega\times(0,\infty)
, where
n
is the outward unit normal.

Remark 4.5
.

The incompressible limit,
\gamma\to\infty
, is very different and remains an open question in the local Cahn-Hilliard case. Indeed, obtaining an equation for the pressure
p
from which to deduce a uniform-in-
\gamma

L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
-control on
p
seems still out of reach. Therefore no analogous of Theorem
1.5
can be stated in this local case.

Appendix A
Technical tools

Several tools have been used to carry out some proofs. First, we present a lemma about geometric convergence of numerical sequences, whose proof can be easily obtained by induction (see, e.g.,
[
36
, Ch.2, Lemma 5.6]
):

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfy the recursive inequality

\displaystyle y_{n+1}\leq Cb^{n}y_{n}^{1+\epsilon},\quad\forall n\geq 0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{0}\leq\theta:=C^{-\frac{1}{\epsilon}}b^{-\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}},

(A.1)

for some
C>0
,
b>1
and
\epsilon>0
. Then,
y_{n}\to 0
for
n\to\infty
with geometric rate

\displaystyle y_{n}\leq\theta b^{-\frac{n}{\epsilon}},\qquad\forall n\geq 0.

(A.2)

Next, we state a theorem concerning the absolute continuity of some integrals of convex functions in
\mathbb{R}
, whose proof can be found, e.g. in
[
32
, p.101]
:

Theorem A.2
.

Let
T>0
and let
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex and lower semicontinuous function. Assume that

•

f\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}f\in L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime})
,

•

g(x,t)\in\partial h(x,t)
for almost every
(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T)
,

•

g\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
.

Then, the function
t\mapsto\int_{\Omega}h(f(x,t))
is absolutely continuous on
[0,T]
and,

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}h(f)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\langle\partial_{t}f,g\rangle\quad\text{for almost any }t\in(0,T).

We then propose a control on the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-norm related to the use of
\omega_{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma A.3
.

There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and a constant
C
such that for
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

and all
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\|f-\overline{f}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|f(x)-f(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,

(A.3)

where
\overline{f}
is the average of
f
over
\Omega
. Similarly, for all
\alpha
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)>0
and constant
C(\alpha)
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})

and all
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+C(\alpha)\|f\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(A.4)

Proof.

The proof is identical to the one in
[

by substituting the norm
