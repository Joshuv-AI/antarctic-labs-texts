A Review on Low-Dose Emission Tomography Post-Reconstruction Denoising with Neural Network Approaches
Alexandre Bousse, Venkata Sai Sundar Kandarpa, Kuangyu Shi, Kuang Gong, Jae Sung Lee, Chi Liu, Dimitris Visvikis

Abstract
Low-dose emission tomography plays a crucial role in medical imaging, enabling the acquisition of functional information for various biological processes while minimizing the patient dose. However, the inherent randomness in the photon counting process is a source of noise which is amplified in low-dose emission tomography. This review article provides an overview of existing post-processing techniques, with an emphasis on deep neural network approaches. Furthermore, we explore future directions in the field of neural network-based low-dose emission tomography. This comprehensive examination sheds light on the potential of deep learning in enhancing the quality and resolution of low-dose emission tomography images, ultimately advancing the field of medical imaging.

I
Introduction

The main components of emission tomography are positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography. They measure the radio-tracer distribution administered to the patient via gamma radiation arising from radioactive decay and have multiple use cases including oncology, cardiology, neurology, etc. The ability to get functional information on the various biological processes distinguish them from other imaging modalities such as MR imaging and computed tomography.

Radioactive decay is a random process which entails the difficulty in precise production of the images in emission tomography. Noise or the speckled variation in emission tomography images is caused by the inherent randomness of the photon counting process. In order to ensure patient safety, research has been extensively conducted in the regime of low-dose emission tomography imaging
[
1
,
2
]
. The reduction in the dose administered to the patient further adds to the challenge of obtaining a clear image.

Emission tomography images are reconstructed from the measured gamma rays, which is an ill-posed inverse problem subject to noise amplification. The images suffer from partial volume effects due to the low intrinsic resolution of the imaging systems, as well as positron range for positron emission tomography. The resolution of the reconstructed images can be improved with model-based iterative reconstruction that incorporate the point spread function in the system matrix. However, this further contributes to the ill-posedness of the inverse problem, which results in more noise. Hence, a number of post-processing techniques have been proposed in this regard. Artificial intelligence and more specifically deep learning-based methods (i.e., deep neural networks) have been very effective in denoising and super resolution and hence have been explored at length in emission tomography. The focus of this article is to discuss at length the neural network approaches that have been proposed for image denoising in low-dose emission tomography.

The main steps in the clinical application of emission tomography, are highlighted in Fig.

. The first step is the production of the radio-pharmaceutical followed by its administration to the patient. Following this the emission data is acquired by a detector setup. Using the physical forward model, image reconstruction methods are utilized to map the raw detector data to an image. These steps are summarized in Section

II
Data Acquisition and Image Reconstruction

II-A

Data Acquisition

Single-photon emission computed tomography imaging is based on the emission of a single gamma photon per each radioactive decay event. These gamma photons are usually collected by a gamma camera
[
3
]
, which typically consists of a collimator that selects relevant gamma photons to be detected; the gamma photons are converted into light in the visible spectra by the scintillation crystal. The optical-wavelength photons are sent to a photomultiplier tube that converts light into electrons, generating a detectable current. This current is then measured by an electronics setup, noting the occurrence of the event.
The relative spatial coordinates of the event are determined by measurements from the point of contact in the photomultiplier tubes. These events are stored in histograms based on their position, resulting in discrete (or vectorized) projection measurement data which is then utilized for image reconstruction.

Positron emission tomography on the other hand uses positron emitting radioisotope. The positron interacts with an electron resulting in an annihilation event that produces two gamma photons moving in nearly opposite direction. These gamma photons are simultaneously detected (coincidence event) by circularly arranged detector elements. Due to this inherent feature of positron emission tomography, collimators are not present in the detector system. The coordinates of each decay event are recorded, through the detection of the corresponding coinciding pair of gamma photons. The binning of these detected coincidence events results in the projection data, used for reconstruction.

II-B

Reconstruction

Reconstructing an image
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}}
corresponds to solving the following inverse problem:

\text{finding $\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}}$ s.t.}\quad\bm{y}\approx\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}})\,,

(3)

This can be achieved by analytical inversion of
\bm{P}
applied to
\frac{1}{\tau}(\bm{y}-\bm{r})
, also known as filtered-backprojection (see for example
[
4
]
). Unfortunately, the inverse problem (
3

3

Another approach consists in finding an estimate
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}}
by penalized maximum log-likelihood

\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}}\in\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}}\,\ell\left(\bm{y},\bar{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})\right)+\beta R(\bm{x})

(4)

where
\ell\left(\bm{y},\bar{\bm{y}}\right)
is the Poisson negative log-likelihood of the expectation
\bar{\bm{y}}
given the measurement
\bm{y}
,
R
is a penalty, or

\beta>0

4

[
5

or ordered-subset EM algorithm
[
6

in absence of penalty (i.e.,
\beta=0

[

with a smooth convex penalty. Reconstructing the image
\bm{x}

4

3

[

[

]
or the relative difference prior
[

. Anatomical priors have been used to smooth the positron emission tomography image while preserving image resolution by taking advantage of high-resolution anatomical images such as computed tomography or MR imaging
[

introduced patch-based dictionary learning for joint positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance image reconstruction.

Penalized maximum log-likelihood methods have played a pivotal role in emission tomography image reconstruction. These methods have proven effective in managing noise levels and improving image quality. However, in the context of low-dose emission tomography imaging, striking a suitable balance between noise reduction and preservation of essential image details necessitates careful tuning of the prior weight. Failure to do so may lead to undesired over-smoothing artifacts. Furthermore, anatomically-guided penalties, while valuable in certain cases, can introduce artifacts when there is misalignment between the activity and anatomical images. It is also worth noting that the applicability of penalized maximum log-likelihood techniques is contingent upon the availability of raw data, which may not always be obtainable.

III
Deep Learning-based Image Post-processing

When emission tomography raw data are not available, images can be post-processed to improve their quality. Prior to the advent of deep learning, conventional image post-processing methods were employed for incorporating corrections in emission tomography images. The first image post-processing task is to improve the image resolution, namely, partial volume correction (PVC). Partial volume correction first achieved with deconvolution techniques, which consists of correcting for the image point spread function using iterative techniques, such as the Van Citert algorithm
[
18
]
or the Richardson-Lucy algorithm
[
19
,
20
]
. However, deconvolution is an ill-posed inverse problem and leads to noise amplification, which is non-practical for low-dose imaging. Therefore, it is necessary to deploy adequate techniques to control the noise. This topic has been the subject to numerous works over the last decade
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

The inherent relationship between resolution and noise poses a significant challenge. Enhancing image resolution typically leads to increased noise levels, while reducing noise tends to compromise resolution. Addressing both aspects simultaneously requires a paradigm shift towards artificial intelligence-based approaches. Neural networks have revolutionized imaging ever since the performance of AlexNet in the ImageNet challenge
[
26
]
. In medical imaging, they have contributed to image segmentation, cancer detection, registration, reconstruction, etc.
[
27
]
. In the context of low-dose emission tomography imaging, they have been widely implemented to bring about improvements in images reconstructed by traditional algorithms.

This section reviews the neural network-based low-dose emission tomography denoising methods and categorizing them based on their neural network design. We have broadly categorized the methods into supervised methods, self-attention mechanisms, unsupervised methods, multi-modality (i.e., additional anatomical information from another modality) and diffusion models. These subsections have further been divided into subcategories to distinguish and highlight specific workings of the approaches. Note that, although self-attention mechanisms are typically categorized as supervised methods, recognizing their increase in popularity and effectiveness, we have chosen to dedicate a separate section to these mechanisms, with an emphasis on transformers.

III-A

Supervised Methods

Machine learning methods that require labeled data for training come under this category. Owing to the data revolution and the availability of annotated datasets for different tasks along with competitions, the most popular methods for denoising are supervised.

A reconstructed image
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}}
produced by traditional methods like filtered-backprojection (by solving (
3
)) or penalized maximum log-likelihood (by solving (
4
)) is processed through an image-to-image neural network
\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}}
depending of a trained parameter
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
as

\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pp}}=\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}^{\star}}(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{rec}})

(5)

where
\bm{x}^{\mathrm{pp}}
is the post-processed image. Supervised training of
\bm{\theta}^{\star}
is generally achieved using a training dataset of
K
noisy/clean image pairs
(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{noisy}}_{k},\bm{x}^{\mathrm{clean}}_{k})
,
k=1,\dots,K

\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}L\left(\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{noisy}}_{k}\right),\bm{x}^{\mathrm{clean}}_{k}\right)

(6)

where
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is a loss function.

There are many possible variations of the neural network
\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}}
and its training. This subsection will cover fully convolutional networks and generative adversarial networks.

III-C

Unsupervised Methods

The advent of unsupervised methods was a result of the limitations that supervised learning methods posed namely large datasets and the effort that goes into annotating them. Two such methods that have been developed in the context of denoising are deep image prior and Noise2Noise methods.

Noise2noise

Noise2Noise is a self-supervised learning technique, i.e., a machine learning technique that trains models using input data only, without labeled data and explicit supervision. Labeling data takes considerable time and effort and in some situation obtaining gold standard-labeled data is impossible. Self-supervised learning has the advantage of significantly increasing the number of datasets for model training as it does not require labeled data. In medical imaging techniques that employ ionizing radiation, such as X-ray and gamma-ray, obtaining a clean target (label) with a high radiation dose can elevate the potential health risks associated with radiation exposure. Although it is possible to obtain a clean target by increasing the scanning time, it may cause image blurring or distortion by increasing the likelihood of patient movement.

The Noise2Noise approach, as proposed by
[
99
]

[

\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\operatornamewithlimits{arg\,min}_{\bm{\theta}}\,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{F}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{clean}}_{k}+\bm{\epsilon}_{k,1}\right)-\left(\bm{x}^{\mathrm{clean}}_{k}+\bm{\epsilon}_{k,2}\right)\right\|^{2}

(8)

where

\bm{x}^{\mathrm{clean}}_{k}

III-D

Multi-modality

In hybrid positron emission tomography/computed tomography, positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance, and single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography systems, the anatomical information derived from computed tomography and MR imaging can also be used to enhance the denoising performance of positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography. Numerous studies using convolutional neural networks in brain positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance datasets achieved substantial dose reduction, sometimes up to 99%, and incremental improvements in image quality and resolution have been demonstrated by combining positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance images as multiple channels into the network compared to using positron emission tomography images alone as input

Diffusion models have arisen as an alternative to generative adversarial networks for image generation and other tasks

Assume that the (clean) feasible images
\bm{x}
are distributed according to some unknown probability distribution function
p_{0}(\bm{x})
that we wish to use to randomly generate images. The forward diffusion is achieved by generating a collection of images
\left(\bm{x}_{t}\right)_{t=1,\dots,T}
, starting from a clean image
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{0}
drawn from
p_{0}
(i.e., by randomly choosing an image from the training dataset), as a Markov chain defined as

\bm{x}_{t}\mid\bm{x}_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\bm{x}_{t-1},(1-\alpha_{t})\bm{I}\right)

(9)

where
\bm{I}
is the identity operator in the image space, and
\left(\alpha_{t}\right)_{t=1,\dots,T}
is a collection of parameters in
]0,1]
defined such that
\bm{x}_{T}
is (approximately) a white noise. Sampling an image according to the initial distribution
p_{0}
can therefore be achieved by reversing the diffusion process, i.e., starting from a white noise
\bm{x}_{T}
from which
\bm{x}_{T-1}
is generated, and so on until
\bm{x}_{0}

\bm{x}_{t-1}\mid\bm{x}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu}(\bm{x}_{t},t),\sigma^{2}(t)\bm{I}\right)

where

is a known function of
\alpha_{t}
and
\bm{\mu}(\bm{x}_{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\bm{x}_{t}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\nabla\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
