A second proof of the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture, by way of a new Hopf algebra
Mathieu Guay-Paquet

Abstract
. This is a set of working notes which give a second proof of the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture, the first (and recent) proof being by Brosnan and Chow in November 2015. The conjecture relates some symmetric functions constructed combinatorially out of unit interval graphs (their q q -chromatic quasisymmetric functions), and some symmetric functions constructed algebro-geometrically out of Tymoczko’s representation of the symmetric group on the equivariant cohomology ring of a family of subvarieties of the complex flag variety, called regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties. Brosnan and Chow’s proof is based in part on the idea of deforming the Hessenberg varieties. The proof given here, in contrast, is based on the idea of recursively decomposing Hessenberg varieties, using a new Hopf algebra as the organizing principle for this recursion. We hope that taken together, each approach will shed some light on the other, since there are still many outstanding questions regarding the objects under study.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this document is to give all of the necessary ingredients to prove the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture (see
Section 7
for the notation):

1
[
9
]
*Conjecture 5.3.

For all Hessenberg functions
h
,

\omega\big(\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)\big)=\csf_{q}\big(G(h)\big).

(2)

As noted above, we are not the first ones to do this; Brosnan and Chow
[
6
]
have recently given a proof.
As part of their proof, they uncover and use much structure on the geometric side of things, not just for the case of regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties, but also relating it to the case of regular (not necessarily semisimple) Hessenberg varieties.
For the proof here, we instead focus on recursions in the combinatorial defining data for regular semisimple Hessenberg varieties, which seems like a complementary approach.

Quasisymmetric functions are ubiquitous in algebraic combinatorics for the following reason: nice combinatorial objects can usually be combined and broken apart in a way that leads to a graded-connected Hopf algebra structure, and there is a universal recipe for constructing quasisymmetric functions out of this data.
One of the observations we make in this paper is that Shareshian and Wachs’s
\csf_{q}
can be constructed using this recipe, so that it is uniquely determined by a very small amount of data.
This data consists of a single coefficient in
\mathbb{C}(q)
for each ordered graph, rather than a whole quasisymmetric function over
\mathbb{C}(q)
for each ordered graph.
Furthermore, it turns out that each of these coefficients is either zero or a power of
q
.
This can be thought of as both a quantitative and a qualitative reduction in the work needed to show that the
\csf_{q}
is what pops out of the construction involving Hessenberg varieties.

The Hopf algebra structure suggests that Hessenberg varieties are recursively structured in a very principled way.
Guided by this, we show that the Hessenberg construction respects the multiplicative and comultiplicative structures present in the Hopf algebra on Dyck paths by giving explicit decompositions of the equivariant cohomology rings.
The base case involves identifying the subspace of these equivariant cohomology rings on which the symmetric group acts according to the sign representation, which we also do explicitly.

2.
The
q
-chromatic quasisymmetric function

Given a (finite simple undirected) graph
G=(V,E)
and a set of colours
C
, a
colouring
is just a function
\kappa\colon V\to C
assigning a colour to each vertex of
G
.
We are typically interested in
proper
colourings, that is, ones which assign different colours to neighbouring vertices.

The
chromatic polynomial
of
G
is the function

P(G,r)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}1,

(3)

which counts the number of proper colourings of
G
with
r
colours.
Here, we take the set of
r
colours to be
[r]=\{1,2,\ldots,r\}
;
since a colouring of
G
stays proper if the colours are relabelled, this is no loss of generality.

We can get a more refined count of the proper colourings of
G
by considering the number of times each colour is used.
Stanley’s
chromatic symmetric function

[
11
]
, which keeps track of this information, is the formal power series

\csf(G,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}.

(4)

Here, the set of colours is the countable set
\mathbb{P}=\{1,2,\ldots\}
, with a corresponding set of indeterminates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
, and the monomial
\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}
is the product of
x_{\kappa(v)}
over all vertices
v\in V
, so that the exponent of
x_{i}
in
\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}
counts the number of times the colour
i
is used in the colouring
\kappa
.
Relabelling the colours in each colouring amounts to permuting the indeterminates, so that
\csf(G,\mathbf{x})
is indeed a symmetric function, invariant under permutations of
\mathbf{x}
.
The chromatic polynomial can be recovered from the chromatic symmetric function as

P(G,r)=\csf\big(G,(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{\text{$r$ copies}},0,0,0,\ldots)\big).

(5)

If the set of vertices and the set of colours each come equipped with a total order, then we can further refine the count of proper colourings by counting
ascents
in each colouring
\kappa
: pairs of vertices
u,v
such that

–

u
and
v
are neighbours,

–

u<v
in the ordering on vertices, and

–

\kappa(u)<\kappa(v)
in the ordering on colours.

Shareshian and Wachs’s
q
-chromatic quasisymmetric function

[
9
]
, which takes this into account, is the formal power series

\csf_{q}(G,\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\mathbf{x}_{\kappa},

(6)

where now
G=(V,E,{<})
is an
ordered
graph, and we take the usual ordering on
\mathbb{P}
.
The number of ascents of a colouring is only invariant under relabellings of the colours which preserve the order of the colours used, so
\csf_{q}(G,\mathbf{x})
is only a
quasi
symmetric function in general.
However, the graphs we consider in this paper (which come from natural unit interval orders) have the property that
\csf_{q}(G,\mathbf{x})
is in fact symmetric.
In any case, setting
q=1
recovers the usual chromatic symmetric function.

3.
The Hopf algebra of ordered graphs, with a twist

Our goal in this section is to define a Hopf algebra for ordered graphs.
Some of the operations could be naturally defined over a smaller ring, but for simplicity we take the field
\mathbb{C}(q)
of rational functions in
q
as ground ring. Our ground set
\og
is the set of all
\mathbb{C}(q)
-linear combinations of ordered graphs (up to isomorphism).
We will define the
r
-fold multiplication map

\nabla_{r}\colon\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}\to\og

(7)

on the basis of
r
-fold tensor products of ordered graphs by

\nabla_{r}(G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r})=G_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G_{r},

(8)

where
\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}
is essentially the concatenation operation on ordered graphs (see below).
The
r
-fold comultiplication map

\Delta_{r}\colon\og\to\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}

(9)

will be given on the basis of ordered graphs as a sum over all colourings of the ordered graph under consideration, where in addition we keep track of a statistic, the number of ascents of each colouring (see below again):

\Delta_{r}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,G|_{\kappa}.

(10)

This is analogous to a sum over all deshufflings of
G
.
If it weren’t for the power of
q
keeping track of the number of ascents, this would fit the framework of Hopf monoids of Aguiar and Mahajan
[
4
]
*Section 9.4, and would in fact be a well-known Hopf algebra related to Stanley’s
\csf
(see
[
3
]
*Example 4.5).

To make
\og
graded-connected, which is sufficient to transform it from a bialgebra to a Hopf algebra, we take the degree of an ordered graph to be its number of vertices.
The subsections below give the details of these constructions, and the facts needed to show that
\og
is indeed a graded-connected Hopf algebra.
Feel free to skip them.
For ease of citation, the summary is that:

11
.

With the definitions of this section, the space
\og
is a graded-connected Hopf algebra over
\mathbb{C}(q)
.

3.1.
Isomorphism

Throughout this paper, we only care about ordered graphs up to isomorphism. Two ordered graphs
G=(V,E,{<})
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},{<^{\prime}})
are
isomorphic
is there is a bijection
\varphi\colon V\to V^{\prime}
such that:

–

\{u,v\}
is an edge of
G
iff
\{\varphi(u),\varphi(v)\}
is an edge of
G^{\prime}
, and

–

u<v
in the order on
G
iff
\varphi(u)<^{\prime}\varphi(v)
in the order on
G^{\prime}
.

The isomorphism class of
G=(V,E,{<})
contains a unique graph with the ordered vertex set
\{1<2<\cdots<n\}
, where
n=|V|
, which can be thought of as a canonical representative for
G
. However, for the constructions below, we keep the flexibility of using other vertex sets and other orderings.

3.2.
Colourings, ascents, descents

Let
G=(V,E,{<})
be an ordered graph and let
\kappa\colon V\to[r]
be a colouring, that is, an arbitrary function.
Let
e=\{u,v\}\in E
be an edge, with
u<v
according to the ordering.
Then, the edge
e
can relate to the colouring
\kappa
in three different ways:

–

if
\kappa(u)=\kappa(v)
, then
e
is
monochromatic
;

–

if
\kappa(u)<\kappa(v)
, then
e
is an
ascent
;

–

if
\kappa(u)>\kappa(v)
, then
e
is a
descent
.

Note that
\kappa
is a proper colouring iff there are no monochromatic edges.

3.3.
Restriction

Let
U\subseteq V
be a subset of the vertices of an ordered graph
G=(V,E,{<})
.
Then, we define the
restriction

G|_{U}=(U,E|_{U},{<}|_{U})
of
G
to
U
in the obvious way:

E|_{U}
is the set of edges with both endpoints in
U
, and
{<}|_{U}
is the restriction of the order relation on
V
to
U
.
Given a colouring
\kappa\colon V\to[r]
, we also define the
restriction

G|_{\kappa}
to be the list of ordered graphs

(G|_{\kappa^{-1}(1)},\;G|_{\kappa^{-1}(2)},\;\ldots,\;G|_{\kappa^{-1}(r)}),

(12)

that is, the restriction of
G
to each of the colour classes, in order.
Note that the edges which survive in the restriction are exactly the monochromatic edges; the ascents and descents disappear.

3.4.
Lexicographic union

Conversely, given a list of ordered graphs

(G_{1},\;G_{2},\;\ldots,\;G_{r}),

(13)

we can construct a particular ordered graph
G=(V,E,{<})
that restricts to it, which we call the
lexicographic union

\mathop{\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}}_{i}G_{i}
of this list. If the listed graphs are
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i},{<_{i}})
, then:

–

V
is the disjoint union of
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
;

–

E
is the disjoint union of
E_{1},\ldots,E_{r}
; and

–

u<v
iff either
u<_{i}v
in some
V_{i}
, or
u\in V_{i}
and
v\in V_{j}
with
i<j
.

If we colour the vertices of
V_{i}\subseteq V
with colour
i
, then every edge is monochromatic, and the restriction of
G
to this colouring is
(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{r})
.
We will also use the notation
\mathop{\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}}_{i}V_{i}
to refer to the lexicographic union
(V,<)
of ordered sets
(V_{i},{<_{i}})
, where we ignore edge sets completely.

3.5.
Reshuffling

Now, consider a list of
r
ordered graphs

(G_{1},\;G_{2},\;\ldots,\;G_{r})

(14)

as above, with
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i},{<_{i}})
, so that we can talk of the lexicographic union
G_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G_{r}
, and let
\kappa\colon V_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}V_{r}\to[s]
be an arbitrary colouring. In this situation, we can construct the restriction

\mathop{\overrightarrow{\bigoplus}}_{i=1,\ldots,r}G_{i}\bigg|_{\kappa}=(G^{\prime}_{1},\;G^{\prime}_{2},\;\ldots,\;G^{\prime}_{s}).

(15)

The compatibility axiom for the multiplication and the comultiplication of the Hopf algebra

essentially says that we get this second list of
s
ordered graphs
G^{\prime}_{i}=(V^{\prime}_{i},E^{\prime}_{i},{<^{\prime}_{i}})
whether we first construct the lexicographic union and then the restriction, or the restriction first and the lexicographic union second.
(The compatibility axiom also says that the total number of ascents should be the same in both cases.)
To show that this is the case, let us be a bit more explicit about what we mean by doing “the restriction first and the lexicographic union second”.

Restricting the domain of the colouring
\kappa
on
V_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}V_{r}
gives a colouring
\kappa_{i}=\kappa|_{V_{i}}\colon V_{i}\to[s]
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, so we can construct
r
lists of
s
ordered graphs each,

\displaystyle G_{1}|_{\kappa_{1}}

\displaystyle=(G^{\prime\prime}_{1,1},\;G^{\prime\prime}_{1,2},\;\ldots,\;G^{\prime\prime}_{1,s}),

(16)

\displaystyle G_{2}|_{\kappa_{2}}

\displaystyle=(G^{\prime\prime}_{2,1},\;G^{\prime\prime}_{2,2},\;\ldots,\;G^{\prime\prime}_{2,s}),

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle G_{r}|_{\kappa_{r}}

\displaystyle=(G^{\prime\prime}_{r,1},\;G^{\prime\prime}_{r,2},\;\ldots,\;G^{\prime\prime}_{r,s}),

where
G^{\prime\prime}_{i,j}
is the restriction of
G_{i}
to the vertices of
V_{i}
which are coloured

.
There is only one sensible way to reassemble these ordered graphs into a list of

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}_{1,1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{2,1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{r,1}

\displaystyle=G^{\prime}_{1},

(17)

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}_{1,2}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{2,2}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{r,2}

\displaystyle=G^{\prime}_{2},

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle G^{\prime\prime}_{1,s}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{2,s}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G^{\prime\prime}_{r,s}

\displaystyle=G^{\prime}_{s}

by unrolling the definitions of restriction to a colouring and lexicographic union.
The only subtlety is that the table of intermediate ordered graphs

is transposed between (

) and (

As for ascents, note that
G_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G_{r}
does not have any edges between

and

for

(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G_{r}$})\\
=(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa_{1}$ on $G_{1}$})+\cdots+(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa_{r}$ on $G_{r}$}).

3.6.
Associativity

Let us show that the multiplication map from (
8
) turns
\og
into an associative unital
\mathbb{C}(q)
-algebra.
We have a basis of

\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}

(19)

which consists of all lists of
r
ordered graphs, of the form

G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r}.

(20)

On this basis element, the
r
-fold multiplication map is defined to be

\nabla_{r}(G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r})=G_{1}\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G_{r}\in\og.

(21)

Now, consider a bracketing of the list of
r
ordered graphs, of the form

\displaystyle(G_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r_{1}})

(22)

\displaystyle\otimes(G_{r_{1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r_{1}+r_{2}})

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}\otimes{}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle\otimes(G_{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{s-1}+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes G_{r_{1}+\cdots+r_{s-1}+r_{s}}),

where there are
s

r_{1}+\cdots+r_{s}=r
.
To have associativity, we need the trivial condition that
\nabla_{1}\colon\og\to\og

\nabla_{s}\circ(\nabla_{r_{1}}\otimes\nabla_{r_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\nabla_{r_{s}}),

be equal to the multiplication
\nabla_{r}
.
Given the definition of lexicographic union, this boils down to verifying that the bijection of ordered sets

\ell\colon[r_{1}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{2}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{s}]\to[r]

(24)

given on each
[r_{j}]\subseteq[r_{1}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{2}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{s}]

\displaystyle\ell|_{[r_{j}]}\colon[r_{j}]

\displaystyle\to[r]

(25)

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\mapsto\;r_{1}+\cdots+r_{j-1}+i

It follows that the empty ordered graph
G_{0}=(\varnothing,\varnothing,{<})
, which is the image of the empty list of ordered graphs under the 0-fold multiplication map

3.7.
Coassociativity

Dually, let us show that
\og
is a coassociative counital coalgebra under the comultiplication map from (
10
).
Recall this is the map

\Delta_{r}\colon\og\to\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}

(26)

defined on the basis of
\og
which consists of all ordered graphs by

\Delta_{r}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,G|_{V_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes G|_{V_{r}},

(27)

where
V_{i}=\kappa^{-1}(i)
is the set of vertices of
G
assigned to colour
i
by
\kappa
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
.
As with associativity, there is the trivial condition that
\Delta_{1}\colon\og\to\og
be the identity map, and the condition that

(\Delta_{r_{1}}\otimes\Delta_{r_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Delta_{r_{s}})\circ\Delta_{s}=\Delta_{r}

(28)

for a bracketing as in (
22
), which we can establish by showing that each term in the sum on the left-hand side of (
28
) corresponds to a term in the sum on the right-hand side and vice versa (after substituting the defining sum (
27
) and distributing the sums over the tensor product).

Fix a bracketing, and hence an order-preserving bijection

\ell\colon[r_{1}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{2}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{s}]\to[r]

(29)

as defined in (

). For each colouring
\kappa\colon V\to[r]
on

, we can define a coarser colouring
\kappa^{\prime}\colon V\to[s]
by the composition

V\overset{\kappa}{\longrightarrow}[r]\overset{\ell^{-1}}{\longrightarrow}[r_{1}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[r_{s}]\longrightarrow[s],

(30)

where the last arrow is the map sending every colour
i
in the
j
th summand
[r_{j}]
to
j
. This colouring is coarser in the following sense: if two vertices
u,v\in V
are assigned the same colour in
[r]
by
\kappa
, then they are assigned the same colour in
[s]
by
\kappa^{\prime}
, so that the colour classes for
\kappa^{\prime}
are obtained by clumping together colour classes for
\kappa
. Let
V^{\prime}_{j}=\kappa^{\prime-1}(j)
be the
j
th colour class of
\kappa^{\prime}
. To recover

from
\kappa^{\prime}
, the extra information needed is exactly given by the colourings

\kappa^{\prime}_{j}=(\ell^{-1}\circ\kappa)\big|_{V^{\prime}_{j}}\colon V^{\prime}_{j}\to[r_{j}],\qquad j=1,\ldots,s.

(31)

This establishes a correspondence between the terms on the right-hand side of (
28
), each of which is given by:

–

an arbitrary colouring
\kappa\colon V\to[r]
,

and the terms on the left-hand side, each of which is given by:

–

an arbitrary colouring
\kappa^{\prime}\colon V\to[s]
, and

–

an arbitrary colouring
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}\colon V^{\prime}_{j}\to[r_{j}]
for each
j=1,\ldots,s
.

It remains to show that corresponding terms are, in fact, equal.
For the right-hand term, the
i
th ordered graph in the tensor product is the restriction of
G
to the colour class

of
\kappa
.
For the left-hand term, the
i
th factor appears in the

th bracket, where

containing

, and it is the restriction of

, then to

For the right-hand term, the power of

appearing in the coefficient is

(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$}).

(32)

For the left-hand term, the power of

in the coefficient is

(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa^{\prime}$ on $G$})\\
{}+(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa^{\prime}_{1}$ on $G|_{V^{\prime}_{1}}$})+\cdots+(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa^{\prime}_{s}$ on $G|_{V^{\prime}_{s}}$}).

(33)

To see that these two numbers are the same, consider an edge
\{u,v\}\in E
with

. This edge is monochromatic for
\kappa
, that is,
\kappa(u)=\kappa(v)
when:

–

for some
j
, so that
\kappa^{\prime}(u)=\kappa^{\prime}(v)=j
, and
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(u)=\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(v)
; that is, the edge is monochromatic for
\kappa^{\prime}
and
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}
.

The edge is an ascent of
\kappa
, that is,
\kappa(u)<\kappa(v)
when either:

–

u\in V^{\prime}_{i}
and

with

, in which case it is an ascent of
\kappa^{\prime}
; or

–

u,v\in V^{\prime}_{j}
for some

and
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(u)<\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(v)
, in which case it is monochromatic for
\kappa^{\prime}
and an ascent of
\kappa^{\prime}_{j}
.

The edge is an descent of
\kappa
, that is,
\kappa(u)>\kappa(v)
when either:

–

u\in V^{\prime}_{i}
and
v\in V^{\prime}_{j}
with
i>j
, in which case it is an descent of
\kappa^{\prime}
; or

–

u,v\in V^{\prime}_{j}
for some

\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(u)>\kappa^{\prime}_{j}(v)
, in which case it is monochromatic for

This breakdown of the cases shows that (

) count exactly the same set of edges of

Thus, corresponding terms are equal, and the comultiplication maps are coassociative.

The only ordered graph which has a colouring using no colours is the empty ordered graph

.
Thus, the 0-fold comultiplication map

3.8.
Compatibility

The
r
-fold multiplication maps

\nabla_{r}\colon\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}\to\og

turn
\og
into an associative unital algebra. By extension, the

\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$s$ copies}}

is also an associative unital algebra under component-wise multiplication.
Dually, the

-fold multiplication maps

\Delta_{s}\colon\og\to\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$s$ copies}}

turn

into a coassociative counital coalgebra, and under component-wise comultiplication, so is the
r
-fold tensor power

\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}.

(37)

The compatibility axiom for
\og
to be a bialgebra is that the multiplication maps be coalgebra maps, or equivalently, that the comultiplication maps be algebra maps.
In other words, the two natural ways to define a map

\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}}\longrightarrow\underbrace{\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$s$ copies}},

(38)

namely, a multiplication followed by a comultiplication, or a component-wise comultiplication followed by a component-wise multiplication, must agree.
The facts needed to check that this is the case are given in the section on ‘reshuffling’ (
Section 3.5
).

3.9.
Graded-connectedness

For
\og
to be graded-connected, we need a decomposition of it of the form

\og=\og_{0}\oplus\og_{1}\oplus\og_{2}\oplus\og_{3}\oplus\cdots,

(39)

where we call the space
\og_{n}
the homogeneous component of degree
n
.
We get a corresponding decomposition of the space

\underbrace{\og\otimes\og\otimes\cdots\otimes\og}_{\text{$r$ copies}},

(40)

by defining the homogeneous component of degree
n
to be

\bigoplus(\og_{n_{1}}\otimes\og_{n_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\og_{n_{r}}),

(41)

where the sum is over all sequences of
r
natural numbers
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r}
such that
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{r}=n
.
We require that this decomposition be compatible with the multiplicative and the comultiplicative structure, in the sense that

\nabla_{r}\Big(\bigoplus(\og_{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\og_{n_{r}})\Big)\subseteq\og_{n}

(42)

and

\Delta_{r}(\og_{n})\subseteq\bigoplus(\og_{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\og_{n_{r}}).

(43)

If this is the case, then
\og
is graded. For it to be graded-connected, we also need
\og_{0}
to be 1-dimensional. If this is the case, then the 0-fold multiplication map
\nabla_{0}\colon\mathbb{C}(q)\to\og_{0}
and the 0-fold comultiplication map
\Delta_{0}\colon\og_{0}\to\mathbb{C}(q)
, which can be restricted to
\og_{0}
, are inverses of one another, so that they are linear isomorphisms.

If
\og
is a graded-connected bialgebra, then it is automatically a Hopf algebra, meaning that there is an antipode map

S\colon\og\to\og,

(44)

as given by, for example, Takeuchi’s formula (see
[
4
]
*Section 5).

In fact, it is easy to check that
\og
is graded-connected if we take
\og_{n}
to be the subspace of
\og
spanned by the ordered graphs which have
n
vertices, as this is compatible with the multiplication and comultiplication maps, and there is only one ordered graph with no vertices, namely
G_{0}=(\varnothing,\varnothing,{<})
.

4.
A few facts about quasisymmetric functions

As alluded to in the
introduction
, Aguiar, Bergeron and Sottile
[
3
]
have shown that the space
\qsym
of quasisymmetric functions is a universal object for graded-connected Hopf algebras, in a way that lets us easily construct and characterize Hopf-algebraic maps to
\qsym
. In this section, we recall just enough details about
\qsym
to state this precisely.

As in
Section 3
, we take our ground ring to be the field
\mathbb{C}(q)
.

A
quasisymmetric function
is a formal power series of bounded degree in the countable ordered set of indeterminates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
with the following invariance property: any two monomials with the same ordered list of nonzero exponents must have the same coefficient.
In other words, the quasisymmetric functions have a basis given by the monomial quasisymmetric functions

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{r}}x_{i_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{i_{2}}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{r}}^{\alpha_{r}},

(45)

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
is any finite list of positive integers.
In fact, the space
\qsym
of quasisymmetric functions is a graded-connected Hopf algebra, where the multiplication maps

\nabla_{r}\colon\underbrace{\qsym\otimes\cdots\otimes\qsym}_{\text{$r$ copies}}\to\qsym

(46)

and the grading are inherited from the algebra of power series.
We will not need any details about the comultiplication maps

\Delta_{r}\colon\qsym\to\underbrace{\qsym\otimes\cdots\otimes\qsym}_{\text{$r$ copies}}

(47)

and the antipode

S\colon\qsym\to\qsym,

(48)

other than the fact that they exist.
The
canonical character
on
\qsym
is the multiplicative linear functional

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\colon\qsym\to\mathbb{C}(q)

(49)

which evaluates the indeterminates
\mathbf{x}
at
\mathbf{x}=(1,0,0,0,\ldots)
, so that

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}(M_{\alpha})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\alpha$ has at most one part,}\\
0&\text{if $\alpha$ has at least two parts.}\end{cases}

(50)

With these definitions in place, we have the following universality result:

51
[
3
]
*Theorem 4.1.

For each pair
(\mathcal{H},\zeta)
where
\mathcal{H}
is a graded-connected Hopf algebra and
\zeta
is a multiplicative function from
\mathcal{H}
to the ground ring, there exists a unique map of graded Hopf algebras

\Psi_{\zeta}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\qsym

(52)

which sends
\zeta
to
\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}
. Moreover, the coefficient of
M_{\alpha}
in
\Psi_{\zeta}(h)
is given by

(\underbrace{\zeta\otimes\zeta\otimes\cdots\otimes\zeta}_{\text{$r$ copies}})\circ(\pi_{\alpha_{1}}\otimes\pi_{\alpha_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi_{\alpha_{r}})\circ\Delta_{r}(h),

(53)

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
is a list of
r
positive integers,
\Delta_{r}
is the
r
-fold comultiplication map of
\mathcal{H}
, and
\pi_{n}
is the projection onto the homogeneous component of degree
n
of
\mathcal{H}
.

5.
The
q
-chromatic quasisymmetric function, revisited

Now, let us use the recipe of
Section 4
to reconstruct the Shareshian–Wachs
\csf_{q}
as a map of graded Hopf algebras between
\og
and
\qsym
.
We will also introduce two variants: the strict and the weak chromatic quasisymmetric functions.

Consider the three characters
\zeta_{0},\zeta_{1},\zeta_{q}\colon\og\to\mathbb{C}(q)
defined on the basis of ordered graphs by

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}(G)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $G$ has no edges,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(54)

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(G)

\displaystyle=1,

(55)

\displaystyle\zeta_{q}(G)

\displaystyle=q^{(\text{\# edges of $G$})}.

(56)

Since the multiplication of ordered graphs is a disjoint union on sets of edges, these functions are multiplicative.
By
Theorem 51
, there are corresponding maps
\Psi_{0},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{q}\colon\og\to\qsym
.

57
.

For every ordered graph
G

\Psi_{0}(G)=\csf_{q}(G)

Proof.

This follows by unrolling the definition of
\Psi_{0}(G)

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})

Then, the coefficient of

(\underbrace{\zeta_{0}\otimes\cdots\otimes\zeta_{0}}_{\text{$r$ copies}})\circ(\pi_{\alpha_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi_{\alpha_{r}})\circ\Delta_{r}(G).

(58)

By definition, the

\Delta_{r}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,G|_{V_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes G|_{V_{r}},

(59)

where
V_{i}=\kappa^{-1}(i)
is the set of vertices assigned colour
i
.
We can substitute this in the previous expression to get

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\big(\zeta_{0}\circ\pi_{\alpha_{1}}(G|_{V_{1}})\big)\otimes\cdots\otimes\big(\zeta_{0}\circ\pi_{\alpha_{1}}(G|_{V_{r}})\big).

By the definition of the projection

Conversely,

By the definition of
\zeta_{0}

\kappa

\zeta_{0}(G|_{V_{i}})=1

.
Combining these two facts, we can rewrite the coefficient of

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\

(61)

Now, recall that the monomial quasisymmetric function
M_{\alpha}
is defined by

M_{\alpha}=\sum_{j_{1}<\cdots<j_{r}}x_{j_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{r}}^{\alpha_{r}}.

(62)

A proper colouring
\kappa^{\prime}\colon V\to\mathbb{P}
which uses colours

(63)

where
\kappa
a proper colouring where colour

Thus, we can write

\Psi_{0}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{proper}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\mathbf{x}_{\kappa},

(64)

which is exactly the definition (

.
∎

65
.

This approach to the
\csf_{q}
, and indeed the very definition of the Hopf algebra

, are directly inspired by a formula proven by Athanasiadis
[
5
]
*Equation 17, which can be interpreted as a proof of the equation

\Delta_{r}\big(\csf_{q}(G)\big)=\csf_{q}\big(\Delta_{r}(G)\big)

in the power-sum basis of symmetric functions. The fact that

In a proper colouring, every edge is either an ascent or a descent, never monochromatic, so there is no reason to distinguish between strict and weak inequalities.
However, for arbitrary colourings, there are two sensible generalizations of the notion of ‘ascent’.
So far we have taken the convention that an ascent of a colouring
\kappa
is a
strict
ascent, that is, an edge
\{u,v\}
with
u<v
and
\kappa(u)<\kappa(v)
.
We could also have considered
weak
ascents, that is, an edge
\{u,v\}
with
u<v
and
\kappa(u)\leq\kappa(v)
.
Given these definitions, we have the following interpretations of the quasisymmetric functions
\Psi_{1}(G)
and
\Psi_{q}(G)
for an ordered graph
G
, which can be proved in much the same way as

:

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# strict ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\mathbf{x}_{\kappa},

\displaystyle\Psi_{q}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# weak ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}.

(68)

Note that in both cases, we are allowing arbitrary colourings, rather than just proper colourings. We will call these the
strict chromatic quasisymmetric function
and the
weak chromatic quasisymmetric function
, respectively.
More generally, for any
t\in\mathbb{C}(q)
, we could consider the multiplicative character

\zeta_{t}(G)=t^{(\text{\# edges of $G$})},

(69)

and the associated morphism
\Psi_{t}
would have the interpretation

\Psi_{t}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to\mathbb{P}\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}t^{(\text{\# monochromatic edges of $\kappa$ on $G$})}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}\,\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}.

(70)

6.
The subalgebra of Dyck paths

In this section, we introduce a subclass of ordered graphs which is closed under lexicographic union and restriction, so that they span a Hopf subalgebra
\dyck
of
\og
. For the rest of the paper we will focus on
\dyck
rather than
\og
, since Hessenberg varieties are only defined for these ordered graphs.

Fix
n\geq 0
.
A
Hessenberg function
is a function
h\colon[n]\to[n]
which is:

–

extensive
, meaning that
i\leq h(i)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and

–

increasing
, meaning that
h(i)\leq h(i+1)
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

Given such a function, there is an associated poset
P(h)
on
[n]
, defined by

i<_{h}j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h(i)<j.

(71)

There is also an associated ordered graph
G(h)
on
[n]
, where the total order on
[n]
is the usual numeric order, and the edges are given by

\{i,j\}\in E(h)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i<j\leq h(i).

(72)

The class of posets of the form
P(h)
is the class of unit interval orders (see
[
10
]
*Proposition 4.1), that is, posets whose elements can be modelled as unit intervals on the real line, where an interval is less than another if it is completely to the left of the other.
The ordered graph
G(h)
is the incomparability graph of
P(h)
, meaning that there is an edge between vertices
i
and
j
iff neither
i<_{h}j
nor
i>_{h}j
.
Thus, the class of ordered graphs of the form
G(h)
could also be called the class of unit interval overlap graphs.
We have the following alternate characterization of these ordered graphs.

73
.

If
G=([n],E,{<})
is an ordered graph on the set
[n]
with the usual numeric order, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

G=G(h)
for some Hessenberg function
h
; and

(2)

if
\{i,j\}
is an edge and
i\leq i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq j
, then
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
is an edge.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
.
If
G=G(h)
and
\{i,j\}\in E
and
i\leq i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq j
, then

i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq j\leq h(i)\leq h(i^{\prime})

(74)

so that
i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}\leq h(i^{\prime})
and
\{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\}
is an edge of
G(h)
as well.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
.
Define the function
h\colon[n]\to[n]
by setting
h(i)
to be the largest
j
such that
i<j
and
\{i,j\}
is an edge, or
h(i)=i
if there is no such edge.
Then, the function
h
is extensive by construction. The function
h
is also increasing, making it a Hessenberg function:
if
h(i)=i
or
h(i)=i+1
, then
h(i)\leq i+1\leq h(i+1)
;
otherwise
\{i,h(i)\}
is an edge and
i\leq i+1<h(i)\leq h(i)
, so that
\{i+1,h(i)\}
is also an edge and
h(i+1)\geq h(i)
by maximality.
Again by maximality, we have

\{i,j\}\in E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad i<j\leq h(i),

(75)

and by condition
(2)
for the edge
\{i,h(i)\}
if
h(i)\neq i
, we have

\{i,j\}\in E\quad\Longleftarrow\quad i<j\leq h(i),

(76)

so that in fact
G=G(h)
for the Hessenberg function
h
as required.
∎

Since condition
(2)
is a kind of closure condition, it follows that the class of ordered graphs of the form
G(h)
is closed under taking lexicographic unions and restrictions, and it spans a Hopf subalgebra of
\og
.

77
.

Let
\dyck
be the subspace of
\og
spanned by the ordered graphs of the form
G(h)
for all Hessenberg functions
h\colon[n]\to[n]
for all
n\geq 0
.
Then,
\dyck
is closed under the multiplication and comultiplication maps of
\og
, so that it is a graded-connected Hopf subalgebra.

We call
\dyck
the Hopf algebra of Dyck paths, since Hessenberg functions have yet another representation, as Dyck paths.
These are paths which start on the horizon, take unit steps either Northeast or Southeast, always stay weakly above the horizon, and end on the horizon.
In this representation, multiplication is simply concatenation.

7.
A bit of geometry: Hessenberg varieties

Having defined Hessenberg functions in
Section 6
, we can now define Hessenberg varieties, which live inside the full flag variety of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We give the strict minimum needed to situate the claims in this paper with respect to the literature on Hessenberg varieties, and to formulate the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture, since after this section we will be working with Tymoczko’s very explicit algebro-combinatorial description of the equivariant cohomology rings, rather than with the geometric objects themselves.

Fix
n\geq 0
.
The
flag variety

\flag(\mathbb{C}^{n})
consists of the complete flags in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, that is, sequences of nested subspaces of the form
F_{\bullet}=(F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset\cdots\subset F_{n})
with
\dim(F_{i})=i
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Given an
n\times n
complex matrix
M
and a Hessenberg function
h\colon[n]\to[n]
, the corresponding
Hessenberg variety
is the subvariety of
\flag(\mathbb{C}^{n})
defined by

\hess(M,h)=\{F_{\bullet}\mid\text{$MF_{i}\subseteq F_{h(i)}$ for each $i=1,2,\ldots,n$}\}.

(78)

Note that there are many choices of
M
which give the same set of flags; for example,
\hess(M,h)
and
\hess(M^{\prime},h)
are the same if
M^{\prime}=aM+bI
, where
a
is a nonzero complex number,
b
is any complex number, and
I
is the identity matrix. Also, many choices of
M
give isomorphic subvarieties; in particular, if
M
and
M^{\prime}
are conjugate matrices, then
\hess(M,h)
and
\hess(M^{\prime},h)
are related by a change of basis of the ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Thus, it makes sense to consider choices of
M
with a specified Jordan block structure, or with conditions on the eigenvalues. Various adjectives get attached to Hessenberg varieties based on such restrictions, so that
\hess(M,h)
is called:

–

regular
if every Jordan block of
M
has a different eigenvalue;

–

nilpotent
if every Jordan block of
M
has eigenvalue 0;

–

semisimple
if
M
has
n
Jordan blocks of size 1.

These adjectives can of course be combined:

–

\hess(M,h)
is
regular nilpotent
if
M
has a single Jordan block of size
n
, with eigenvalue 0. This is the case studied in
[
2
]
.

–

\hess(M,h)
is
minimal nilpotent
if
M
has one Jordan block of size 2 and
n-2
Jordan blocks of size 1, all with eigenvalue 0. This is the case studied in
[
1
]
.

–

\hess(M,h)
is
regular semisimple
if
M
is diagonalizable, with
n
distinct eigenvalues. This is the case that the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture is concerned with, and seems to be the most studied.

For the rest of the section, let
M
be a fixed diagonal matrix with distinct diagonal entries, which we will write as
D
to emphasize that we are focusing on the case where
\hess(D,h)
is regular semisimple.
Let
T
be the group of invertible diagonal matrices, which is isomorphic to the complex torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
.
Every matrix in
T
commutes with
D
, so the variety
\hess(D,h)
is invariant under the action of the group
T
on
\flag(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.
Thus, we can consider the equivariant cohomology ring, which we will abbreviate as

H^{*}_{T}(h)=H^{*}_{T}\big(\hess(D,h)\big),

(79)

and which is a graded-connected algebra over
\mathbb{C}
.
Using the tools of GKM theory
[
7
]
, Tymoczko has given an explicit description of this ring
[
14
]
.
GKM theory also implies that
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is a free module over the equivariant cohomology ring
H^{*}_{T}(\text{pt})
of a point, which is isomorphic to a polynomial ring in
n
indeterminates, say
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, where
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n})
is a set of
n
indeterminates.
Thus, we have a tower of graded-connected
\mathbb{C}
-algebras

\mathbb{C}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\subseteq H^{*}_{T}(h),

(80)

where
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is a free module over the subring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
.
A further feature of GKM theory is that the ordinary cohomology ring can be recovered as a ring quotient from the equivariant cohomology:

H^{*}(h)=H^{*}_{T}(h)/\langle\mathbf{L}\rangle H^{*}_{T}(h).

(81)

This amounts to setting each of the indeterminates
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
to zero.

In
[
14
]
*Section 3.1, Tymoczko defines the
dot action
, which is an action of the symmetric group
S_{n}
on
H^{*}_{T}(h)
.
Note that this action is
\mathbb{C}
-linear and respects the grading on
H^{*}_{T}(h)
.
In the tower of (
80
), the dot action fixes
\mathbb{C}
pointwise, and it sends
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
to itself; explicitly, if
w\colon[n]\to[n]
is a permutation, then under the dot action
w\cdot L_{i}=L_{w(i)}
.
This means that the action of
w\in S_{n}
on
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is
\mathbb{C}
-linear, and
twisted

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-linear.
Still, the following notion of the
graded trace
of
w
is well-defined:
we will write

\tr_{q}\big(w,H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)=\sum_{i}q^{\deg(e_{i})}(\text{coefficient of $e_{i}$ in $w\cdot e_{i}$})\in\mathbb{C}[[q]],

(82)

where the elements
e_{i}
form a homogeneous basis of
H^{*}_{T}(h)
over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
,
\deg(e_{i})
is the degree of
e_{i}
, and the result is a formal power series in the indeterminate
q
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
.
The careful reader will have spotted that this ‘definition’ packs quite a few implicit assumptions:

–

Naively, the coefficient of
e_{i}
in
w\cdot e_{i}
should be an element of
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, not
\mathbb{C}
. However, the dot action preserves the grading, so the coefficient is in the degree-zero part of
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, which is
\mathbb{C}
.

–

In order to speak of a formal power series, there should only be finitely many contributions to the coefficient of each power of
q
. This is guaranteed because each homogeneous graded piece of
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{C}
. In fact,
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is finite-dimensional over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, so the result is a polynomial in
q
, but we will use this notation for cases where the result is genuinely a power series.

–

We are calling this the trace of
w
over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, but
w
is not a
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-linear map! However, it can be checked that this definition does not depend on the choice of homogeneous basis elements
e_{i}
.

Given this notion of trace, we can define the
graded Frobenius characteristic
of the action of
S(\mathbf{L})
on
H^{*}_{T}(h)
over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
as

\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\tr_{q}\big(w,H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)p_{(\text{cycle type of $w$})},

(83)

where
p_{\lambda}\in\sym
is a power-sum symmetric function.
Let
\omega\colon\sym\to\sym
be the usual involution, which is the Hopf map defined by
\omega(p_{k})=(-1)^{k+1}p_{k}
for
k=1,2,\ldots
.
With all of these definitions, we can finally state the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture:

84
[
9
]
*Conjecture 5.3.

For all Hessenberg functions
h
,

\omega\big(\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)\big)=\csf_{q}\big(G(h)\big).

(85)

In view of
Theorem 57
, we can rephrase this as

\omega\big(\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)\big)=\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big),

(86)

which suggests a two-step approach to the proof:

(1)

show that
\omega\big(\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\big)\big)
, as a function of the Dyck path
G(h)
, respects the multiplication and comultiplication, so that it is a map of graded Hopf algebras from
\dyck
to
\sym
; then

(2)

compute the character for this map by composing it with
\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}
, and show that the result is
\zeta_{0}
.

This is what we will do, except that we will go through an intermediate step.
We will identify a polynomial subring of
H^{*}_{T}(h)
different from
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, which we call
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, where
\mathbf{R}=(R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{n})
is another set of indeterminates, giving us a second tower of graded
\mathbb{C}
-algebras,

\mathbb{C}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]\subseteq H^{*}_{T}(h).

(87)

For this tower,
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is again a free module over the subring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, but the dot action fixes
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
pointwise: for every permutation
w\in S_{n}
, we have
w\cdot R_{i}=R_{i}
.
Thus, the action of
w
on
H^{*}_{T}(h)
is simply
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
-linear, rather than twisted
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
-linear.
This will make it easier to carry out the two-step process described above for the tower (
87
), and show that

\omega\big(\frob_{q}\big(S(\mathbf{L}),H^{*}_{T}(h),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]\big)\big)=\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big).

(88)

The final step will be to show that replacing
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
in the tower has the same effect as replacing the character
\zeta_{q}
by the character
\zeta_{0}
.

Given that the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
already has a geometric interpretation, in terms of the tangent of the torus
T
, and that the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
seems to fit fairly naturally into the algebraic side of the picture, this raises the following question:

89
.

What, if anything, does the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
correspond to on the Hessenberg variety
\hess(D,h)
, geometrically speaking?

8.
Tymoczko’s rings

In this section, we give Tymoczko’s combinatorial construction of the equivariant cohomology ring
H^{*}_{T}(h)
of the regular semisimple Hessenberg variety
\hess(D,h)
in terms of the moment graph from GKM theory, which comes equipped with her ‘dot action’ by the symmetric group
S_{n}
(see
[
14
]
for details).
For a fixed
n
, the rings
H^{*}_{T}(h)
for all Hessenberg functions
h\colon[n]\to[n]
can all be realized as subrings of a single ring
\mathcal{T}
, which we describe first.
We then define the subring
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, which is isomorphic to
H^{*}_{T}(h)
, for each Hessenberg function
h
.
In terms of notation, we write
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
for the indeterminates
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
from
[
14
]
, to emphasize that these indeterminates naturally act on
\mathcal{T}
from the left, and introduce new indeterminates
\mathbf{R}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})
which act naturally from the right; it is our hope that this distinction between left and right indeterminates will help the reader avoid confusion when performing explicit computations, especially once the actions of the symmetric groups
S(\mathbf{L})
and
S(\mathbf{R})
on
\mathcal{T}
are thrown in the mix.

8.1.
The big ring

Fix
n\geq 0
; let
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
and
\mathbf{R}=(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})
be two sets of indeterminates.
We will write
S(\mathbf{L})
for the group of permutations of the indeterminates
\mathbf{L}
,
S(\mathbf{R})
for the group of permutations of the indeterminates
\mathbf{R}
, and
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
for the set of bijections from
\mathbf{R}
to
\mathbf{L}
; the peculiar notation is to emphasize that
S(\mathbf{L})
acts on
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
on the left by function composition, and
S(\mathbf{R})
acts on the right.
As a ring, and as a left
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-module, Tymoczko’s ring is the cartesian product

\mathcal{T}=\prod_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}].

(90)

We will write
\mathbf{1}_{\beta}
for the element of
\mathcal{T}
which is 1 at coordinate
\beta
and 0 at all other coordinates, so that a typical element of
\mathcal{T}
is of the form

\sum_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}f_{\beta}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta},

(91)

and the unit element is
\mathbf{1}=\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{1}_{\beta}
.
We say that the element (
91
) is
homogeneous
of degree
d
if each polynomial
f_{\beta}
is homogeneous of total degree
d
in the indeterminates
\mathbf{L}
.
With this notion of grading, the ring
\mathcal{T}
is a graded-connected
\mathbb{C}
-algebra, since the degree zero part is
\mathbb{C}\mathbf{1}
, which is one-dimensional.
We will identify
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
with the subring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\mathbf{1}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.
The ring
\mathcal{T}
also carries a natural
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-linear action by
S(\mathbf{R})
, defined by
\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\cdot w=\mathbf{1}_{\beta\circ w}
for
w\in S(\mathbf{R})
.
The ring
\mathcal{T}
can also be identified with the product ring

\mathcal{T}=\prod_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}],

(92)

with a typical element being of the form

\sum_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\,g_{\beta}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n}),

(93)

since the rings
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
are isomorphic.
However, they are isomorphic in many different ways, and the choice of isomorphism here is important.
In fact, we will choose a different isomorphism for each coordinate, since each
\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
gives a natural way of identifying the indeterminates of
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
.
In other words, we impose the identity

\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\,R_{i}=\beta(R_{i})\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta}

(94)

for every bijection
\beta
and every indeterminate
R_{i}
, so that

f_{\beta}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})=g_{\beta}\big(\beta(R_{1}),\ldots,\beta(R_{n})\big)

(95)

if the elements (
91
) and (
93
) are equal.
In the presentation from (
92
), the ring
\mathcal{T}
has a natural structure as a right
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
-module, and a
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
-linear action by
S(\mathbf{L})
on the left, defined by
w\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\beta}=\mathbf{1}_{w\circ\beta}
for
w\in S(\mathbf{L})
.
As with
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, we will identify
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
with the subring
\mathbf{1}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.
So far, we have the following structures on the ring
\mathcal{T}
:

–

it is a graded-connected commutative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra;

–

it has a left action by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
;

–

it has a left action by
S(\mathbf{L})
(this is in fact the
dot action
);

–

it has a right action by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
; and

–

it has a right action by
S(\mathbf{R})
.

How do all of these structures interact?

–

Everything in sight is
\mathbb{C}
-linear and respects the grading;

–

both left actions
commute
with both right actions;

–

the action by
S(\mathbf{L})

distributes
over the action by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
, so that
w\cdot(L_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta})=w(L_{i})\,\mathbf{1}_{w\circ\beta}
, making it a twisted
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-linear action; and

–

the action by
S(\mathbf{R})

distributes
over the action by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, so that
(\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\,R_{i})\cdot w=\mathbf{1}_{\beta\circ w}\,w(R_{i})
, making it a twisted
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
-linear action.

8.2.
The moment graph

Consider the set
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
of bijections from
\mathbf{R}
to
\mathbf{L}
.
We will now define a directed graph structure
B
with
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
as vertex set, and a spanning subgraph of it
M(h)
for each Hessenberg function
h
.
An
inversion
of
\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
is a pair
(R_{i},R_{j})
with
i<j
such that the corresponding pair
(L_{i^{\prime}},L_{j^{\prime}})=(\beta(R_{i}),\beta(R_{j}))
has
i^{\prime}>j^{\prime}
.
For every
i<j
, half of the vertices in
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
have
(R_{i},R_{j})
as an inversion, and half of the vertices don’t.
The vertices from these two sets can be paired up by using the transposition
(R_{i}\leftrightarrow R_{j})\in S(\mathbf{R})
;
we will put a directed edge in
B
from
\beta
to
\beta\circ(R_{i}\leftrightarrow R_{j})
for every
\beta
which doesn’t have
(R_{i},R_{j})
as an inversion, and say that this edge is labelled by
(R_{i},R_{j})
.
Doing this for every
i<j
gives the directed edge set of
B
.

Given a Hessenberg function
h
, recall that the ordered graph
G(h)
has an edge between
i
and
j
when
i<j\leq h(i)
. We will define the directed graph
M(h)
as the subgraph of
B
which contains all of its vertices, and only the directed edges which are labelled by
(R_{i},R_{j})
for those pairs
i,j
with
i<j\leq h(i)
. This is the
moment graph
for the regular semisimple Hessenberg variety
\hess(D,h)
.

Note that the underlying undirected graph of
B
is essentially the Cayley graph on
S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
generated by all transpositions in
S(\mathbf{R})
, or equivalently, all transpositions in
S(\mathbf{L})
. With the given edge orientations,
B
has a single
source
, the vertex
\beta_{0}
such that
\beta_{0}(R_{i})=L_{i}
for all
i
, and a single
sink
, the vertex
\beta_{1}
such that
\beta_{1}(R_{i})=L_{n+1-i}
for all
i
, and it is acyclic.

8.3.
Edge conditions

Given an element

x=\sum_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\,g_{\beta}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})

(96)

of
\mathcal{T}
and a directed edge
\beta\to\beta^{\prime}
with label
(R_{i},R_{j})
, we will say that
x

satisfies the edge condition
for this edge if the difference

g_{\beta}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})-g_{\beta^{\prime}}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})

(97)

is divisible by
R_{i}-R_{j}
.
The reader with a taste for symmetry should note we could have labelled the edge
(L_{i^{\prime}},L_{j^{\prime}})=(\beta(R_{i}),\beta(R_{j}))
and asked that

f_{\beta}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})-f_{\beta^{\prime}}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})

(98)

be divisible by
L_{i^{\prime}}-L_{j^{\prime}}
for the representation of
x
as

x=\sum_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}f_{\beta}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\,\mathbf{1}_{\beta}

(99)

instead; this would actually be an equivalent condition, even though we are dealing with two different identifications (given by
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
) between the rings
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
. However, this reader should also note that there is a fundamental
\mathbf{L}
–
\mathbf{R}
asymmetry in the definition of the moment graph
M(h)
.

Note that the elements of
\mathcal{T}
which satisfy a given edge condition form a subring of
\mathcal{T}
which contains
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
.

8.4.
Building elements

Given a moment graph
M(h)
, we will be interested in the subring
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of elements of
\mathcal{T}
which satisfy the edge conditions for all edges of
M(h)
.
Thankfully, there is a simple procedure for constructing these elements:

100
.

(Step 1)

Start with a prospective element

x=\sum_{\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})}\mathbf{1}_{\beta}\,g_{\beta}(R_{1},\ldots,R_{n})

(101)

where all of the polynomials
g_{\beta}
are unassigned.

(Step 2)

Pick any vertex
\beta^{\prime}
such that for all directed edges
\beta\to\beta^{\prime}
in
M(h)
, the polynomial
g_{\beta}
is already assigned. (At the first step, this might be the source vertex
\beta_{0}
defined by
\beta_{0}(R_{i})=L_{i}
for all
i
, since it has no incoming edges.)

(Step 3)

Choose any polynomial which satisfies the edge condition for every edge
\beta\to\beta^{\prime}
(thus ignoring any edges
\beta^{\prime}\to\beta
), and assign it to
g_{\beta^{\prime}}
.

(Step 4)

While there are still unassigned polynomials, return to
(Step 2)
.

This algorithm relies on a few assumptions:

–

In
(Step 2)
, there is always a suitable vertex to be picked, and every vertex will be picked eventually. This is easy to see, given that
M(h)
is a directed acyclic graph.

–

In
(Step 3)
, no matter what previous choices have been made, there always exists at least one polynomial which satisfies the incoming edge conditions. This is far from trivial; see
[
13
]
*Section 6 and references therein for a proof.

Given these assumptions, however, it should be clear that every element of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
can be constructed using this procedure.

8.5.
Flow-up vectors

For each vertex
\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
, there are elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
which will be particularly useful for what follows, called
flow-up vectors
for
\beta
in
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
They are, in a sense, elements whose ‘leading coefficient’ is at coordinate
\beta
and as small as possible.
They are constructed using
100
by making these choices:

–

If there is no path
\beta\to\cdots\to\beta^{\prime}
in
M(h)
, then assign
g_{\beta^{\prime}}=0
.

–

At the vertex
\beta
, assign

g_{\beta}=\prod(R_{i}-R_{j}),

(102)

where the product is over all edge labels
(R_{i},R_{j})
of incoming edges.

–

Otherwise, when there is a path
\beta\to\cdots\to\beta^{\prime}
in
M(h)
, pick a polynomial for
g_{\beta^{\prime}}
which is homogeneous of the same degree as
g_{\beta}
.

These choices are compatible with
100
, so flow-up vectors always exist.
As noted in Tymoczko’s work, picking a flow-up vector for each
\beta\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
gives a homogeneous basis for
\mathcal{T}_{h}
as a graded
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-module, thus showing that it is a free module of rank
n!
.
We note here that the same is true over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, with the same basis.

8.6.
The small rings

Thus, for every Hessenberg function
h
, we have the following structures on the subring
\mathcal{T}_{h}
of Tymoczko’s ring
\mathcal{T}
:

–

it is isomorphic to the equivariant cohomology ring
H^{*}_{T}(h)
of the regular semisimple Hessenberg variety
\hess(D,h)
;

–

it is a graded-connected commutative
\mathbb{C}
-subalgebra of
\mathcal{T}
, containing the subrings
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
;

–

it is a free graded module of rank
n!
over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
;

–

it is a free graded module of rank
n!
over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
;

–

it is stable under the dot action of
S(\mathbf{L})
on the left;

–

it is not generally stable under the action of
S(\mathbf{R})
on the right.

The stability under
S(\mathbf{L})
but not
S(\mathbf{R})
comes from the fact that the action of
S(\mathbf{L})
on the directed graph
B
sends the subgraph
M(h)
to itself (at least, when ignoring the direction of edges), but in general the action of
S(\mathbf{R})
does not.

Given two Hessenberg functions
h,h^{\prime}
with
h\leq h^{\prime}
pointwise, we have the inclusion of subgraphs
M(h)\subseteq M(h^{\prime})
, so that the reverse inclusion of subrings
\mathcal{T}_{h}\supseteq\mathcal{T}_{h^{\prime}}
holds.
At one extreme, when
h(i)=i
for all
i
, there are no edge conditions imposed, and
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\mathcal{T}
.
At the other extreme, when
h(i)=n
for all
i
, all possible edge conditions are imposed, and it can be seen with some work that
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the subring of
\mathcal{T}
generated by
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, or equivalently, the ring
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\otimes_{\Lambda}\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, where
\Lambda=\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]\cap\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]\subseteq\mathcal{T}
is the ring of symmetric polynomials in either
\mathbf{L}
or
\mathbf{R}
.

9.
A few facts about symmetric functions

We will need just a few facts about symmetric functions, especially in relation to representations of symmetric groups, which we record here.
For much more detail on the topic, see
[
8
]
*Part I.

The ring of symmetric function
\sym
is a subring of the
\qsym
.
It consists of those quasisymmetric functions for which the coefficient of
M_{\alpha}
and
M_{\alpha^{\prime}}
are equal whenever the list of positive integers
\alpha^{\prime}
can be obtained from the list
\alpha
by reordering its entries.
In other words, it consists of all
\mathbb{C}(q)
-linear combinations of the
monomial
symmetric functions

m_{\lambda}=\sum_{\alpha\sim\lambda}M_{\alpha},

(103)

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r})
is a
partition
, that is, a weakly decreasing list of positive integers; and
\alpha\sim\lambda
when the sorted rearrangement of
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
is
\lambda
.
There are several other combinatorially significant bases of
\sym
, but for our purposes we will only need the basis of
power-sum
symmetric functions
p_{\lambda}
, defined by

p_{(k)}=m_{(k)}=M_{(k)}

(104)

when
\lambda=(k)
is a partition with a single part, and multiplicatively by

p_{\lambda}=p_{(\lambda_{1})}\cdot p_{(\lambda_{2})}\cdot\cdots\cdot p_{(\lambda_{r})}

(105)

when
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r})
has many parts.
The comultiplication on the basis of power-sum symmetric functions acts very nicely, since

\displaystyle\Delta_{r}\big(p_{(k)}\big)

\displaystyle=\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{$p_{(k)}$}\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}

(106)

\displaystyle\mathrel{+}\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{$p_{(k)}$}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

\displaystyle\mathrel{+}\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\makebox[16.20145pt][c]{$p_{(k)}$},

where
p_{(k)}
appears on the main diagonal of this array and ones appear everywhere else.
In particular,
\sym
is closed under the multiplication and the comultiplication of
\qsym
, so it is a graded-connected Hopf subalgebra.
Also, if follows that given any infinite sequence
c=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots)
of coefficients in the ground field
\mathbb{C}(q)
, the map defined by

\displaystyle\varphi_{c}\colon\sym

\displaystyle\to\sym p_{(k)}

\displaystyle\mapsto c_{k}p_{(k)}

(107)

can be extended uniquely to be
\mathbb{C}(q)
-linear, multiplicative, and comultiplicative, so that it is a graded Hopf endomorphism.
As examples of a few maps of this type, we have:

–

the identity map
\mathrm{id}
, for which
p_{(k)}\mapsto p_{(k)}
;

–

the antipode
S
, for which
p_{(k)}\mapsto-p_{(k)}
;

–

the Eulerian map
E_{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{C}(q)
, for which
p_{(k)}\mapsto t^{k}p_{(k)}
; and

–

the involution
\omega
, for which
p_{(k)}\mapsto(-1)^{k+1}p_{(k)}
.

Since
\sym
is both commutative and cocommutative, it can be shown that the
convolution
product of graded Hopf endomorphisms
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{r}
of
\sym
, defined by

\varphi_{1}*\varphi_{2}*\cdots*\varphi_{r}=\nabla_{r}\circ(\varphi_{1}\otimes\varphi_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes\varphi_{r})\Delta_{r},

(108)

is also a graded Hopf endomorphism of
\sym
.
For the special case of morphisms defined as above by infinite sequences, we have
\varphi_{c}*\varphi_{d}=\varphi_{c+d}
, where
(c+d)
is the sequence
(c_{1}+d_{1},c_{2}+d_{2},\ldots)
.

Now, consider a
\mathbb{C}
-linear representation
V
of the symmetric group
S_{n}
, that is, a finite-dimensional vector space
V
over
\mathbb{C}
together with a
\mathbb{C}
-linear action of
S_{n}
on its elements.
The
trace
of a permutation
w\in S_{n}
on
V
over
\mathbb{C}
is

\tr(w,V,\mathbb{C})=\sum_{i}(\text{coefficient of $e_{i}$ in $w\cdot e_{i}$})\in\mathbb{C},

(109)

where the sum is over the elements of
e_{i}
of a basis of
V
.
The
Frobenius characteristic
of the action of
S_{n}
on
V
over
\mathbb{C}
is the symmetric function

\frob(S_{n},V,\mathbb{C})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{w\in S_{n}}\tr(w,V,\mathbb{C})p_{(\text{cycle type of $w$})}\in\sym,

(110)

where the cycle type of
w
is the partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell})
which gives the lengths of each cycle of
w
.
Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
be an
r
-tuple of natural numbers such that
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}=n
.
The
Young subgroup

Y_{\alpha}\subseteq S_{n}
of type
\alpha
consists of all permutations in
S_{n}
which permute the first
\alpha_{1}
elements of
[n]
among themselves, and the next
\alpha_{2}
among themselves, and so on.
As a group, it is naturally isomorphic to a cartesian product,

Y_{\alpha}=S_{\alpha_{1}}\times S_{\alpha_{2}}\times\cdots\times S_{\alpha_{r}}.

(111)

Let
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots V_{r}
be representations of
S_{\alpha_{1}},S_{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,S_{\alpha_{r}}
respectively. Then, their tensor product

V=V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{r}

(112)

naturally has the structure of a representation of
Y_{\alpha}
. Let
V^{\prime}
be the induced representation of
V
from
Y_{\alpha}
to
S_{n}
, defined by

V^{\prime}=\bigotimes_{i=1,\ldots,k}w_{i}V,

(113)

where
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}
is a full set of coset representatives for
Y_{\alpha}
in
S_{n}
, and each
w_{i}V
is an isomorphic copy of
V
.
Then, we have the following relation between the Frobenius characteristics of these representations:

\frob(S_{n},V^{\prime},\mathbb{C})=\prod_{i=1,\ldots,r}\frob(S_{\alpha_{i}},V_{i},\mathbb{C}),

(114)

where the multiplication is the multiplication as symmetric function.

We have a dual relationship for comultiplication.
Let
V
be any representation of
S_{n}
.
For any
r
-tuple
\alpha
of natural numbers, we can consider the representation of
Y_{\alpha}
on
V
, and this breaks up as a tensor product

V|_{Y_{\alpha}}=V_{\alpha,1}\otimes V_{\alpha,2}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{\alpha,r},

(115)

where each
V_{\alpha,i}
is a representation of
S_{\alpha_{i}}
.
Then, the relationship between Frobenius characteristics for comultiplication is

\Delta_{r}\big(\frob(S_{n},V,\mathbb{C})\big)=\sum_{\alpha}\bigotimes_{i=1,\ldots,r}\frob(S_{\alpha_{i}},V_{\alpha,i},\mathbb{C}),

(116)

where the sum is over all
r
-tuples
\alpha
.

We have a third relationship between representations of
S_{n}
and symmetric functions: the Kronecker product.
Given two representations
U,V
of
S_{n}
, there is a natural structure of a representation of
S_{n}\times S_{n}
on the tensor product
U\otimes V
as above, if we act by
S_{n}
independently on each factor.
However, there is an equally natural structure of a representation of
S_{n}
on
U\otimes V
, where we act by
S_{n}
simultaneously on both factors (also known as the diagonal action).
For this representation, we have the relation

\frob(S_{n},U\otimes V,\mathbb{C})=\frob(S_{n},U,\mathbb{C})\star\frob(S_{n},V,\mathbb{C}),

(117)

where the
Kronecker product

\star
is the bilinear product
\sym\otimes\sym\to\sym
defined by

p_{\lambda}\star p_{\mu}=\begin{cases}z(\lambda)p_{\lambda}&\text{if $\lambda=\mu$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(118)

Here, the
z(\lambda)
is the standard scaling factor for the power-sum symmetric function, which can also be recovered from the fact that the Frobenius characteristic of the trivial one-dimensional representation of
S_{n}
is the identity for
\star
.

We also note that for a representation
V
of
S_{n}
, the coefficient of
M_{(n)}
in the symmetric function

\frob(S_{n},V,\mathbb{C})

(119)

is the dimension of the subspace of
V
on which
S_{n}
acts trivially, and the coefficient of
M_{(n)}
in the symmetric function

\omega\big(\frob(S_{n},V,\mathbb{C})\big)

(120)

is the dimension of the subspace of
V
on which
S_{n}
acts as the sign representation, that is, the set of vectors
v\in V
such that
w\cdot v=v
for every even permutation
w\in S_{n}
and
w\cdot v=-v
for every odd permutation.

The Frobenius character of the Dot action as a Hopf map

\displaystyle G(h)

\displaystyle\mapsto\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\in\sym,

\displaystyle G(h)

\displaystyle\mapsto\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}])\in\sym,

\dyck
and

10.1.
Respecting multiplication

Let
h\colon[n]\to[n]
be a Hessenberg function, let
n=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{r}
, and suppose that we have an isomorphism of ordered graphs

G(h_{1})\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G(h_{2})\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}G(h_{r})=G(h),

(125)

where each
h_{i}\colon[n_{i}]\to[n_{i}]
is a Hessenberg function, so that the
r
-fold multiplication of the ordered graphs
G(h_{i})
in
\dyck
is

\nabla_{r}\big(G(h_{1})\otimes G(h_{2})\otimes\cdots\otimes G(h_{r})\big)=G(h).

(126)

Then, to show that multiplication is preserved, we would like to show that

\prod_{i=1,\ldots,r}\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}_{i}),\mathcal{T}_{h_{i}},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}_{i}])=\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]),

(127)

and similarly for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
.
Here, we have introduced different set of indeterminates
\mathbf{L}_{i}=(L_{i,1},\ldots,L_{i,n_{i}})
for each
i
on the left-hand side.
These indeterminates should be understood as identified with the set of indeterminates
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
on the right-hand side under the lexicographic identification of ordered sets

[n_{1}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[n_{2}]\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}\cdots\mathbin{\vec{\oplus}}[n_{r}]=[n].

(128)

Symmetric remarks hold for the indeterminates
\mathbf{R}
.
We can prove (
127
) and its twin statement for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
at the level of
S(\mathbf{L})
-representations, as follows.
Consider the
\mathbb{C}
-algebra

\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{r}},

(129)

which is naturally a module over
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, equipped with an action of the Young subgroup

Y_{\mathbf{L}}=S(\mathbf{L}_{1})\times S(\mathbf{L}_{2})\times\cdots\times S(\mathbf{L}_{r})\subseteq S(\mathbf{L}).

(130)

Then, we need to show that
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the induced representation from the Young subgroup
Y_{\mathbf{L}}
to
S(\mathbf{L})
.
This has already been shown by Teff
[
12
]
, but we include a sketch of the argument here so that the reader can compare and contrast this with the proof that comultiplication is respected.

131
.

With the notation and assumptions of this subsection,
\mathcal{T}_{h}
is isomorphic to the induced representation of
\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{r}}
from the Young subgroup
Y=S(\mathbf{L}_{1})\times S(\mathbf{L}_{2})\times\cdots\times S(\mathbf{L}_{r})
to
S(\mathbf{L})
.

Proof.

Note that in this case, the moment graph
M(h)
is disconnected, and each of the orbits of the vertices under the action of the group

Y_{\mathbf{R}}=S(\mathbf{R}_{1})\times S(\mathbf{R}_{2})\times\cdots\times S(\mathbf{R}_{r})\subseteq S(\mathbf{R})

(132)

is a union of connected components.
This is because
M(h)
cannot contain any directed edge labelled by indeterminates from
\mathbf{R}_{i}
and
\mathbf{R}_{j}
with
i\neq j
.
Consider one of these orbits, say the orbit
\mathcal{O}
containing the source vertex
\beta_{0}\in S(\mathbf{L}\leftarrow\mathbf{R})
, defined by
\beta_{0}(R_{i})=L_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
When restricted to
\mathcal{O}
, the directed graph
M(h)
is isomorphic to the cartesian product

M(h_{1})\times M(h_{2})\times\cdots\times M(h_{r}).

(133)

Since the elements of
\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}},\ldots\mathcal{T}_{h_{r}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
are defined in terms of satisfying edge conditions, it follows that the natural
\mathbb{C}
-linear map

\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{r}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{T}_{h}|_{\mathcal{O}},

(134)

produces elements which satisfy all edge conditions, where
\mathcal{T}_{h}|_{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{h}
is the subspace of elements whose coordinate polynomials outside of
\mathcal{O}
are zero.
Furthermore, this map takes tuples of flow-up vectors to flow-up vectors, and it follows that it is an isomorphism.

Now, note that
\mathcal{O}
is also the orbit of
\beta_{0}
under the Young subgroup
Y_{\mathbf{L}}
, so the subspace
\mathcal{T}_{h}|_{\mathcal{O}}
is stable under the action of
Y_{\mathbf{L}}
.
If
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}
is a complete set of coset representatives for
Y_{\mathbf{L}}
in
S(\mathbf{L})
, then the orbits under the action of
Y_{\mathbf{R}}
are
w_{1}\mathcal{O},w_{2}\mathcal{O},\ldots,w_{k}\mathcal{O}
, and we have a decomposition

\mathcal{T}_{h}=\bigoplus_{i=1,\ldots,k}w_{i}\cdot\mathcal{T}_{h}|_{\mathcal{O}}.

(135)

This is exactly the construction of the induced representation.

10.2.
Respecting comultiplication

Let
h\colon[n]\to[n]
be a Hessenberg function and let
r\geq 0
. Then, on the side of the Hopf algebra
\dyck
, the definition of the
r
-fold comultiplication is

\Delta_{r}\big(G(h)\big)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon[n]\to[r]\\
\text{arbitrary}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G(h)$})}\,G(h)|_{\kappa}.

(136)

On the side of the Hopf algebra
\sym
, for the representation of
S(\mathbf{L})
on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, we have the equation

\Delta_{r}\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\big)=\sum_{\alpha}\frob_{q}(Y_{\mathbf{L},\alpha},\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])

(137)

and similarly for
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
, where the sum is over all
r
-tuples
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
of natural numbers such that
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}=n
, and
Y_{\mathbf{L},\alpha}
is the Young subgroup consisting of permutations in
S(\mathbf{L})
which swap the first
\alpha_{1}
indeterminates among themselves, the next
\alpha_{2}
indeterminates among themselves, and so on.
We can get these two equations to line up better with each other by grouping the colourings
\kappa
according to their type; we will say that
\kappa
has type
\alpha
if the number of times colour
i
is used is
\alpha_{i}
for all

\Delta_{r}\big(G(h)\big)=\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon[n]\to[r]\\
\text{of type $\alpha$}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# ascents of $\kappa$ on $G(h)$})}\,G(h)|_{\kappa}.

(138)

To show that comultiplication is preserved by the graded Frobenius characteristic, we will show that the terms with the same

in the sums in (
137

138
) match up.
On the level of representations, we will give a sequence of
\mathbb{C}
-linear projections

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]

\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
and

\mathcal{T}_{h_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathcal{T}_{h_{r}}

with its degree shifted up by the number of ascents of
\kappa
on
G(h)
, where

G(h)|_{\kappa}=\big(G(h_{1}),\,G(h_{2}),\,\ldots,\,G(h_{r})\big).

(141)

142
.

With the notation and assumptions of this subsection, there exists a suitable sequence of projections.

Proof.

In contrast with the proof of
131
, we will consider orbits of the group

Y_{\mathbf{R}}

We will associate to each of these orbits a specific colouring
\kappa
of type
\alpha

The type
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
specifies a grouping of the indeterminates
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})

r

\mathbf{L}_{1}
consists of the first

where the restriction of

\displaystyle I_{2}

10.4.
Changing the base ring

a_{i}
be the
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
-coefficient of

in
w\cdot x_{i}
; by degree considerations,

has degree zero, so in fact it lies in

.
For each

\mathbb{C}
-coefficient of

Since

.
Also, we have

This holds for all

\tr_{q}(w,\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])=\tr_{q}(w,\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\tr_{q}(w,\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}],\mathbb{C}),

(171)

which is enough to show (

Let
\mathrm{id},S,E_{q},E_{(1-q)}\colon\sym\to\sym
be the Hopf endomorphisms defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{id}(p_{(k)})

\displaystyle=p_{(k)}

(173)

\displaystyle S(p_{(k)})

\displaystyle=-p_{(k)}

(174)

\displaystyle E_{q}(p_{(k)})

\displaystyle=q^{k}p_{(k)}

(175)

\displaystyle E_{(1-q)}(p_{(k)})

\displaystyle=(1-q)^{k}p_{(k)},

(176)

so that we have the Hopf endomorphism

\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)(p_{(k)})=(1-q^{k})p_{(k)},

(177)

where
*
is the convolution product. Then, we have

E_{(1-q)}\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\big)=\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}])\big).

(178)

Proof.

The action of
S(\mathbf{L})
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}]
simply permutes the indeterminates
\mathbf{L}=(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
, so it is easy to compute the graded trace of an element
w\in S(\mathbf{L})
acting on all monomials in the indeterminates
\mathbf{L}
;
a monomial is fixed by
w
iff for every cycle of
w
, the indeterminates in the cycle have the same exponent in the monomial. Thus, the contribution of
w
to
\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}],\mathbb{C})
is

\frac{1}{n!}\tr_{q}(w,\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}],\mathbb{C})=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{1})}}{1-q^{\lambda_{1}}}\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{2})}}{1-q^{\lambda_{2}}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{\ell})}}{1-q^{\lambda_{\ell}}},

(179)

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{\ell})
is the cycle type of
w
.
This means that the effect of the Kronecker multiplication by
\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}],\mathbb{C})
in (
169
) is the inverse of the effect of the morphism
(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q}))
from the statement.

The action of
S(\mathbf{L})
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}]
is even simpler; it is the trivial action. Thus, the contribution of
w\in S(\mathbf{L})
to
\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}],\mathbb{C})
is

\frac{1}{n!}\tr_{q}(w,\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}],\mathbb{C})=\frac{1}{n!}\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{1})}}{(1-q)^{\lambda_{1}}}\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{2})}}{(1-q)^{\lambda_{2}}}\cdot\cdots\cdot\frac{p_{(\lambda_{\ell})}}{(1-q)^{\lambda_{\ell}}},

(180)

where again
\lambda
is the cycle type of
w
.
The effect of Kronecker multiplication by
\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}],\mathbb{C})
in (
170
) is then the inverse of the effect of the morphism
E_{(1-q)}
from the statement of the lemma.

11.
A technical lemma proved by sign-reversing involution

At this point, we almost have our proof of the Shareshian–Wachs conjecture.
We have identified two maps
\Psi_{0},\Psi_{q}\colon\dyck\to\sym
of graded Hopf algebras, and proved that for every Hessenberg functions
h
,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)

\displaystyle=\csf_{q}\big(G(h)\big)

(181)

\displaystyle\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)

\displaystyle=\omega\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}])\big)

(182)

in
Section 5
and
Section 10
, respectively.
We also have the equation

E_{(1-q)}\big(\omega\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\big)\big)\\
=\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\big(\omega\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{R}])\big)\big)

(183)

from
172
, after applying the involution
\omega
.
All that remains is to show that
\Psi_{0}
and
\Psi_{q}
satisfy the same relation,

E_{(1-q)}\big(\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)\big)=\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\big(\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)\big),

(184)

by checking that they have the same multiplicative character, that is,

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(E_{(1-q)}\big(\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big)=\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\big(\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big).

(185)

Also, note that it is enough to verify this for all ordered graphs of the form
G(h)
which are nonempty and connected, since these are the multiplicatively irreducible elements of
\dyck
.
Once this is done, it will follow that

\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)=\omega\big(\frob_{q}(S(\mathbf{L}),\mathcal{T}_{h},\mathbb{C}[\mathbf{L}])\big),

(186)

as required.
Note that the proof below relies on knowing that
\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)
lies in
\sym\subset\qsym
for all Hessenberg functions
h
, which we have only established by going through Hessenberg varieties.
It would be nice to also have more direct combinatorial proof of this fact, like we have for
\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)
.

11.1.
A direct computation

We can compute
\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(E_{(1-q)}\big(\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big)
directly.
The action of the map
E_{(1-q)}\colon\sym\to\sym
is simply to multiply the
k
th homogeneous graded piece of
\sym
by
(1-q)^{k}
for each
k
.
Let
E^{\prime}_{(1-q)}\colon\dyck\to\dyck
be map which multiplies the
k
th homogeneous graded piece of
\dyck
by
(1-q)^{k}
for each
k
.
Since
\Psi_{0}
respects the grading, we have

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(E_{(1-q)}\big(\Psi_{0}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big)=\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(\Psi_{0}\big(E^{\prime}_{(1-q)}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big)=\zeta_{0}\big(E^{\prime}_{(1-q)}\big(G(h)\big)\big).

(187)

However,
\zeta_{0}
is almost always zero.
In fact, the only nonempty connected ordered graphs of the form
G(h)
which is nonzero under
\zeta_{0}
is the ordered graph
G_{1}
with a single vertex. This ordered graph has degree 1, so we have

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(E_{(1-q)}\big(\Psi_{0}(G_{1})\big)\big)=1-q.

(188)

11.2.
A combinatorial interpretation

The situation is more complicated with the map
\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\colon\sym\to\sym
.
Let
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{+}
be the projection onto the homogeneous part of degree zero and the parts of positive degree of
\sym
, respectively.
Then, Takeuchi’s formula for the antipode tells us that

S=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}\underbrace{\pi_{+}*\cdots*\pi_{+}}_{\text{$r$ copies}}.

(189)

Thus, we have

\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}\mathrm{id}*\underbrace{(\pi_{+}\circ E_{q})*\cdots*(\pi_{+}\circ E_{q})}_{\text{$r$ copies}}.

(190)

Also, the canonical character
\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}
is multiplicative, so

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\\
=\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}\big(\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\underbrace{(\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\pi_{+}\circ E_{q})\otimes\cdots\otimes(\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\circ\pi_{+}\circ E_{q})}_{\text{$r$ copies}}\big)\circ\Delta_{r+1}.

(191)

Recall from
Section 5
that the coefficient of the quasisymmetric function
M_{\alpha}
in
\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\kappa\colon V\to[r]\\
\text{of type $\alpha$}\end{subarray}}q^{(\text{\# weak ascents of $\kappa$ on $G$})}.

(192)

Let us compute the value of (
191
) on
M_{\alpha}
.
By definition,

\Delta_{r+1}(M_{\alpha})=\sum_{\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{r}}M_{\alpha^{0}}\otimes M_{\alpha^{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes M_{\alpha^{r}},

(193)

where the sum is over all
(r+1)
-tuples
(\alpha^{0},\alpha^{1},\ldots,\alpha^{r})
of sequences of natural numbers whose concatenation is the original sequence
\alpha
.
Note that
\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}(M_{\alpha^{i}})=0
if
\alpha^{i}
has more than one part, and
\pi_{+}(M_{\alpha^{i}})=0
if
\alpha^{i}
is empty.
Thus, the only terms which survive when applying (
191
) to
M_{\alpha}
are the ones where either:

–

\alpha
has
r+1
parts, and
\alpha^{i}
consists of the
(i+1)
st part of
\alpha
; or

–

\alpha
has
r
parts,
\alpha^{0}
is empty, and
\alpha^{i}
consists of the
i
th part of
\alpha
.

All in all, applying (
191
) to
M_{\alpha}
when
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
gives two terms:

(-1)^{r-1}q^{\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}}+(-1)^{r}q^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}}.

(194)

Combining this with (
192
), we get the combinatorial interpretation

\zeta_{\mathcal{Q}}\big(\big(\mathrm{id}*(S\circ E_{q})\big)\big(\Psi_{q}\big(G(h)\big)\big)\big)=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{\kappa}(-1)^{r}q^{\stat(\kappa)},

(195)

where the inner sum is over all colourings
\kappa\colon[n]\to\{0\}\cup[r]
such that every colour in
[r]
is used at least once (but the special colour 0 is optional), and the statistic in the exponent of
q
is

\stat(\kappa)=(\text{\# vertices with colour $>0$})+(\text{\# weak ascents of $\kappa$ on $G(h)$}).

(196)

11.3.
The sign-reversing involution

We want to show that most of the time, the sum on the right-hand side of (
195
) is zero.
More precisely, it should be zero whenever
G(h)
is a connected ordered graph with more than one vertex, and it should be
1-q
when
G(h)=G_{1}
is the ordered graph with a single vertex.
(We leave the very finite case of
G_{1}
as an exercise to the reader.)
We will show this by exhibiting a sign-reversing involution on the terms
(-1)^{r}q^{\stat(\kappa)}
which preserves the statistic in the exponent of
q
, so that all terms cancel out.
Sadly, however, this involution is defined by a lengthy case analysis.
On the plus side, it will not depend on the ordered graph
G(h)
, other than the assumptions that

–

it has at least two vertices, and

–

there is an edge joining the vertices last two vertices,
n-1
and
n
,

which are satisfied for all connected ordered graphs with more than one vertex.
Let
\kappa\colon[n]\to\{0\}\cup[r]
be a colouring such that every colour in
[r]
is used at least once, but the special colour 0 may be used or not. We will define a colouring
\kappa^{\prime}\colon[n]\to\{0\}\cup[r\pm 1]
which uses either one more or one less non-special colour, so that the sign is reversed, and for which
\stat(\kappa)=\stat(\kappa^{\prime})
. The involution is defined in all cases so that the only edge which could change status between being a weak ascent or a strict descent is the edge joining
n-1
and
n
, and this is compensated by whether the vertices
n-1
and
n
have a positive colour.

11.3.1.
If vertex
n
has colour 0

Let vertex
n-1
have colour
i
.
If this is the only vertex with colour
i
, then to obtain
\kappa^{\prime}
recolour
n-1
with colour
i-1
and delete colour
i
from the list of available colours.
If
n-1
is not the only vertex with colour
i
, then to obtain
\kappa^{\prime}
recolour
n-1
with a new colour
i+\tfrac{1}{2}
.
In both cases, rename the colours in an order-preserving way so that the set of non-special colours is actually
[r\pm 1]
.
Note that these two procedures are inverses of each other.

11.3.2.
If vertex
n
is the only vertex with colour 1

We play essentially the same game as in the previous case, except that now we skip over the colour 1 when recolouring
n-1
.
That is, let vertex
n-1
have colour
i
.
If this is the only vertex with colour
i
, then recolour
n-1
with the previous colour in the sequence

0,2,3,4,5,\ldots

(197)

and delete colour
i
from the list of available colours.
If
n-1
is not the only vertex with colour
i
, then recolour
n-1
with a new colour, which should be inserted just before the colour that comes after
i
in the sequence (
197
).
As before, after doing this, rename the colours in an order-preserving way so that the set of non-special colours used is
[r\pm 1]
.
Again, the two procedures in this case are inverses of each other.

11.3.3.
Otherwise

In all other cases, we recolour the vertex
n
rather than
n-1
.
If
n
is the unique vertex with colour
i
, then recolour it
i-i
, and delete colour
i
.
If
n
is not the unique vertex with colour
i
, then recolour it
i+\tfrac{1}{2}
, a new colour.
As always, rename the set of non-special colours used to be
[r\pm 1]
and note that these two procedures are inverses.
