Normal approximation for Gibbs processes via disagreement couplings
Christian Hirsch, Moritz Otto, Anne Marie Svane

Abstract
. This work improves the existing central limit theorems (CLTs) for geometric functionals of Gibbs processes in three aspects. First, we derive a CLT for weakly stabilizing functionals, thereby improving on the previously used assumption of exponential stabilization. Second, we show that this CLT holds for interaction ranges up to the percolation threshold of the dominating Poisson process. This avoids imprecise branching bounds from graphical construction. Third, by constructing simultaneous couplings of several Palm processes for Gibbs functionals, we provide a quantitative CLT in terms of Kolmogorov bounds for normal approximation. An important conceptual ingredient in these advances is the extension of disagreement coupling adapted to unbounded windows and to the comparison at multiple spatial locations.

Primary 60K35. Secondary 60D05, 55U10.

1.
Introduction

Because of their ability to encode possibly highly complex interactions, Gibbs point processes are applied in a broad range of domains such as biology, ecology, materials science and telecommunication networks
[
1
,
29
]
. However, even for simple models, the use of Gibbs point processes comes at massive computational costs as the simulation requires elaborate Markov chain Monte Carlo methods
[
1
]
. This issue becomes particularly pressing when devising goodness-of-fit tests on large datasets. Therefore, an attractive approach is to develop test statistics that are asymptotically normal on large windows. Then, to develop a hypothesis test only the mean and variance under the null model are needed.
This explains the need for central limit theorems (CLTs) on functionals of Gibbs point processes.

The last decade was
marked
by vigorous research activities in the asymptotic theory of statistics that can be written as a sum of certain scores evaluated at the points of a point process with rapidly decaying correlations.
Improving on the methods used in the classical setting of Poisson point processes in
[
24
]
, CLTs for Gibbs point processes could be derived in
[
27
,
32
]
by relying on the graphical construction of Gibbs processes
[
11
]
.

However, despite these recent advances, some aspects of the limit theory for Poisson processes could so far not been transferred to Gibbs point processes:

(1)

All of
[
7
,
27
,
32
]
are formulated under the condition of exponential stabilization. This makes it difficult to apply
[
27
,
32
]
for delicate topological functionals, such as the persistent Betti numbers.

(2)

Both
[
27
,
32
]
rely on the graphical construction of Gibbs processes from
[
11
]
, thereby imposing restrictive constraints on the interaction range of the Gibbs process for which a (partially quantitative) CLT can be established.

(3)

In
[
3
]
, disagreement coupling is used to establish a CLT with a Gibbs particle process as input thus allowing a larger interaction range. However, the investigations are restricted to U-statistics and do not provide quantitative error bounds for normal approximation. Very recently,
[
7
]
derived a quantitative CLT with optimal convergence rates (up to logarithmic corrections) and for a very general class of
\beta
-mixing point processes. However, the convergence is quantified with respect to the Wasserstein distance and the methods do not extend easily to the Kolmogorov distance.

In our work, we will address all of the shortcomings mentioned above. The key tool for achieving the improvements will be a more refined analysis of the correlation structure of Gibbs processes using disagreement coupling. While this technique was initially suggested for studying the spatial correlation structure of lattice-Gibbs point processes
[
31
]
, it has recently been successfully extended to derive Poisson approximation theorems for continuum Gibbs processes
[
18
]
. However, the construction in
[
18
]
is not adapted to the setting of unbounded domains or the coupling at several locations, which are critical for the CLT improvements derived in the present article. Therefore, a major part of our investigation is devoted to making disagreement coupling more flexible so that it can accommodate the needs from spatial limit theorems. Equipped with these conceptual advances, we improve the existing CLTs for Gibbs point processes in three aspects.

(1)

We extend the CLT for weakly stabilizing functionals from
[
24
]
to the setting of Gibbs point processes. In particular, this yields a CLT for persistent Betti numbers and total edge length of minimal spanning trees.

(2)

We prove a CLT for Gibbs point processes under the condition that the interaction range is smaller than the critical value of continuum percolation of an associated Poisson point process. This improves on the restrictive constraints coming from the branching bounds of the graphical construction in
[
11
]
. In other words, our work makes normal approximation available for point processes that were out of reach in
[
27
]
.

(3)

We show how the convergence in Wasserstein distance from
[
7
]
can be upgraded to a convergence in Kolmogorov distance provided stricter conditions are imposed on the point process and the functional. To that end, we introduce simultaneous couplings of several Palm versions for Gibbs processes.

The rest of this manuscript is organized as follows. First, in Section
2
, we introduce the model and main results, Theorems
3
and
4
. Then, in Section
3
, we discuss examples satisfying the conditions of these results. We review the definition of disagreement coupling in Section
4
and establish the properties needed for the proofs of the main results. We prove Theorems
3
and
4
in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively. Finally, we provide a detailed comparison with the existing normal approximation literature in Appendix
A
and a technical auxiliary result in Appendix
B
.

2.
Model and main results

The main results of this article, Theorems
3
and
4
below, are CLTs for stabilizing functionals on Gibbs point processes. First, we discuss in greater detail the considered classes of Gibbs point processes. For a concise introduction to Gibbs point processes, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
.

2.1.
Conditions on the Gibbs process

Let
\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Euclidean space equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}^{d}
.
Define

\mathcal{B}^{d}_{0}
to be the set of bounded Borel sets.
Let
\mathbf{N}
be the space of all locally finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and let
\mathcal{N}
denote the smallest
\sigma
-algebra on
N

such that
\mu\mapsto\mu(B):=\#(\mu\cap B)
is measurable for
all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}^{d}
, where
\#A
denotes the cardinality of a set
A
. Let
\mathbf{N}_{0}\subseteq\mathbf{N}
denote the subspace of finite subsets and for
B\in\mathcal{B}^{d}
, let
\mathbf{N}_{B}\subseteq\mathbf{N}
denote the locally finite subsets of
B
.
A
point process
is a measurable map
from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
to
(\mathbf{N},\mathcal{N})
where

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is some probability space.

To define Gibbs point processes, we start from an energy
functional
E:\mathbf{N}_{0}\to(-\infty,\infty]
which satisfies:

\rhd

Non-degeneracy:

E(\varnothing)=0
.

\rhd

Hereditary:
For all
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
E(\psi)=\infty
implies
E(\psi\cup\{x\})=\infty
.

\rhd

Stability:
There is a constant
C>0
with

(1)

\inf_{\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}}E(\psi)\geqslant-C\#\psi.

The
local energy

J\colon{\mathbf{N}}_{0}\times{\mathbf{N}}_{0}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is given by

(2)

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi):=E(\varphi\cup\psi)-E(\psi),

which encodes the energy increase when adding the point configuration
\varphi
to
\psi
. We will assume that
J
has
finite interaction range
. That is, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that

J(x,\psi)=J(x,\psi\cap B_{r_{0}}(x))

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
, where

B_{r_{0}}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon|y-x|\leqslant r_{0}\}

denotes the Euclidean ball with radius
r_{0}>0
centered at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We write
B_{r_{0}}:=B_{r_{0}}(x)
.
Note that the assumption
of finite interaction range
allows
us
to define
J(\varphi,\psi)
also for
\psi\in\mathbf{N}
.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}^{d}
, the
partition function

Z_{B}\colon{\mathbf{N}}\to(0,\infty)
is defined by

\displaystyle Z_{B}(\psi):=\mathbb{E}[e^{-J(\mathcal{P}_{B},\psi)}],\quad\psi\in{\mathbf{N}},

where
\mathcal{P}_{B}
denotes a unit intensity Poisson process
on
B
. Condition (
1
) indeed ensures that
Z_{B}(\psi)<\infty
and since
J(\varnothing,\psi)=0
for all
\psi\in{\mathbf{N}}
, we also have that
Z_{B}(\psi)\geqslant e^{-|B|}>0.

For
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}^{d}
and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}
, we now define the
finite-volume Gibbs point process

\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)
on
B
with boundary conditions
\psi
as the point process with density

(3)

\displaystyle\varphi\mapsto Z_{B}(\psi)^{-1}e^{-J(\varphi,\psi)}

with respect to the unit intensity Poisson process on
B
. This is well-defined since
0<Z_{B}(\psi)<\infty
.

The finite-volume Gibbs process is sometimes, e.g. in
[
18
]
, introduced in terms of the Papangelou conditional intensity (PI)
\kappa\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}_{0}\to[0,\infty)
defined by

\kappa(x,\psi)=e^{-J(x,\psi)}.

Then,
\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)
can be characterized as the unique point process satisfying the
GNZ equation

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)}f(X_{i},\mathcal{X}(B,\psi))\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{B}f(x,\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cup\{x\})\kappa(x,\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cup\psi)\,\lambda({\rm d}x)\Big],

for each
f\colon B\times\mathbf{N}\to[0,\infty)
that is measurable
[
12
,
23
]
.

Consider the finite volume Gibbs point process
\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)
on
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
with boundary conditions
\psi\in\mathbf{N}
.
Then,
\mathcal{X}
satisfies the
DLR equations
: For
C\subseteq B
and all
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{B\backslash C}
outside a set of measure 0 with respect to
\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap(B\setminus C)
,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap C\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap({B\backslash C})=\varphi)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}(C,\psi\cup\varphi)\in\cdot).

In words, the conditional distribution of
\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap C
given

\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap({B\backslash C})
is again a Gibbs point process, namely the Gibbs process on
C
with boundary conditions

\psi\cup(\mathcal{X}(B,\psi)\cap({B\backslash C}))
.

The GNZ equations can be used to define Gibbs processes in unbounded domains. A point process
\mathcal{X}
is said to be an
infinite-volume Gibbs point process
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}}f(X_{i},\mathcal{X})\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int f(x,\mathcal{X}\cup\{x\})\kappa(x,\mathcal{X})\,\lambda({\rm d}x)\Big],

for each measurable
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}\to[0,\infty)
.

While the finite-volume Gibbs process was explicitly defined in (
3
),
the
existence and in particular
the
uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs processes is generally far from trivial, see e.g.
[
4
,
8
]
. We therefore make some further assumptions on
\kappa
.
For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
A\oplus B:=\{x+y\mid x\in A,y\in B\}
and for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
x+A:=\{x\}\oplus A
. Moreover, we define
B_{r}(A)=A\oplus B_{r}
. Then, the Boolean model with intensity
\alpha_{0}
and balls of radius
r/2
is defined as
B_{r/2}(\mathcal{P})
.
Let
r_{c}=r_{c}(\alpha_{0})
denote the critical threshold of continuum percolation for a Poisson point process
\mathcal{P}
with intensity
\alpha_{0}>0
. That is, the infimum of all
r>0
such that
B_{r/2}(\mathcal{P})
has an unbounded connected component with positive probability.

Throughout the paper, we will work under the following assumption.

(7)

The PI is bounded
0\leqslant\kappa\leqslant\alpha_{0}
and has
a
finite interaction range
r_{0}<r_{c}(\alpha_{0})
.

Then, for any given PI
\kappa
, there exists a unique infinite-volume Gibbs point process (see
[
8
, Thm. 5.4]
or Proposition
19
below). We now give two well-known examples of Gibbs point process models satisfying the assumptions.

Example 1

(Pairwise interaction processes)
.

In this example, the local energy is given in terms of a non-negative pair potential
v:[0,r_{0}]\to[0,\infty]
,

J(x,\psi):=-\log(\alpha_{0})+\sum_{y\in\psi\cap B_{r_{0}}(x)}v(|x-y|).

The special case
\Psi=\beta\mathbbmss{1}_{[0,r_{0}]}
where
\beta>0
and
\mathbbmss{1}_{[0,r_{0}]}
is the indicator for the interval
[0,r_{0}]
is known as the Strauss process. If
\beta=\infty
, one obtains the hard-sphere model.

Example 2

(Area-interaction process
[
2
]
)
.

In this example, we use for
\alpha_{0},r_{0}>0
and
\gamma\in[0,1]
the potential

J(x,\psi):={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\log(\alpha_{0}){+}V(x,\psi)\log(\gamma),

where

\displaystyle V(x,\psi):=\Big|B_{r_{0}/2}(x)\setminus\bigcup_{y\in\psi\cap B_{r_{0}/2}(x)}B_{r_{0}/2}(y)\Big|.

In both
examples
, the PI
\kappa
is
translation-invariant
, i.e.,
\kappa(x,\psi)=\kappa(x+y,\psi+y)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
.

We finally mention that an infinite-volume Gibbs process also satisfies the DLR equations:
It holds for all
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{B^{c}}

outside a set of
\mathcal{X}\cap B^{c}
-measure zero that

(8)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}\cap B\in\cdot\mid\mathcal{X}\cap{B^{c}}=\varphi)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}(B,\varphi)\in\cdot).

That is, given the boundary conditions
\mathcal{X}\cap{B^{c}}
, the distribution of
\mathcal{X}\cap B
is a finite volume Gibbs process.

We conclude by discussing the relation between Gibbs point processes satisfying Assumption (
7
) and those appearing in the existing literature
[
3
,
7
,
27
,
32
]
on normal approximation for Gibbs processes. Since we do not need the precise definitions in the rest of the paper, we keep the remarks very brief at the present point. For the interested reader, we have included a more thorough discussion in Appendix
A
.

The class of Gibbs processes considered in
[
7
,
27
,
32
]
were first introduced in
[
27
, Section 1.1]
. For finite-range interactions, the entire theory relies on a restrictive interaction-range bound formulated in
[
27
, Equation (1.4)]
. As observed in
[
3
, Remark 3]
, especially for low dimensions
d
, Assumption (
7
) is a substantial improvement of the bound
[
27
, Equation (1.4)]
. In other words, our work makes normal approximation available for point processes that were out of reach in
[
7
,
27
,
32
]
.
Conversely, we note that our paper requires the interaction range to be finite, whereas
[
32
]
also allows some infinite-range Gibbs processes.

The key achievement of
[
7
]
is to provide a quantitative CLT for point processes that are not necessarily of Gibbsian type. The main assumption here is the
exponential decay of dependence (EDD)
. The authors verify this condition for the Gibbs process from
[
27
]
. In Section
A
, we show that the EDD property also holds under Assumption (
7
).

Finally, we mention that a qualitative normal approximation result was obtained in
[
3
]
under the assumption (
7
). However, this only applies in the case of
U
-statistics with finite stabilization radius.

2.2.
Statement of main results and conditions on the functional

The main results of this article are two CLTs for functionals of Gibbs processes on growing domains, see Theorems
3
and
4
. These results are complementary in the following sense. While Theorem
3
describes a CLT for a general class of translation-invariant Gibbs functionals without convergence rates, Theorem
4
provides a quantitative CLT for the more restricted class of Gibbs functionals that can be written as a sum of scores. Therefore, it is natural that the two results require different sets of conditions. However, loosely speaking, in both cases the conditions
consist of a moment bound and stabilization condition.

We now discuss a CLT for weakly stabilizing functionals as the first main result of our paper. Let
H:\mathbf{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
be a functional. We say that
H
is
translation-invariant
if
H(\varphi)=H(\varphi+x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
.
Writing
Q_{n}:=[-n/2,n/2]^{d}
,
n\geq 1
, we consider functionals
H_{n}
of locally finite point patterns
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
of the form

H_{n}(\varphi):=H(\varphi\cap Q_{n})

To prove the CLT for
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})
, we need to impose a moment and a stabilization condition. The moment condition requires that there are constants
c_{\mathsf{M}},p_{\mathsf{M}}>0
such that for any
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

(9)

\mathbb{E}\big[|H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})|^{5}\big]\leqslant c_{\mathsf{M}}m^{p_{\mathsf{M}}},

where
Q_{z,m}:=z+Q_{m}
.
The stabilization condition requires that, almost surely,

(10)

H(\mathcal{X})-H(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{l}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}(H(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}})-H((\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}})\setminus Q_{l}))

exists for all
l\geqslant 0
and all sequences
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
w_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{k\geqslant 1}\bigcap_{n\geqslant k}Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}
. Note that the limit is necessarily independent of the sequence
Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}
.
This is a
weak stabilization condition
in the sense that there is no assumption on the rate of convergence in (
10
).

We show in Section
5.2.1
, that the conditions are satisfied if the following deterministic conditions on the functional hold. The conditions will be stated in terms of the
add-one cost operator

(D_{x}H)(\varphi):=H(\varphi\cup\{x\})-H(\varphi)

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
.
Then, the moment condition (
9
) will be satisfied if
there are constants
c_{\mathsf{D}M},R_{\mathsf{D}M}>0

such that for any finite point pattern
\varphi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(11)

\displaystyle|D_{y}H(\varphi)|\leqslant c_{\mathsf{D}M}\exp\big(c_{\mathsf{D}M}\varphi(B_{R_{\mathsf{D}M}})\big),

see Lemma
32
.
The stabilization condition (
10
) is satisfied if for every
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
and any sequence
Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}
with
w_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\bigcap_{n\geq k}Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the limit

(12)

H(\varphi\cup\{0\})-H(\varphi):=\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{0}H(\varphi\cap Q_{w_{n},z_{n}})

exists, see Lemma
34
.

Let
N(0,\sigma^{2})
denote a normal random variable with variance
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
. Then our first main result is the following. The proof will follow from Theorem
30
and Lemma
32
.

Theorem 3

(CLT for weakly stabilizing functionals on Gibbs processes)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-volume Gibbs point process
with translation-invariant PI satisfying (
7
). Let
H
be a translation-invariant functional on finite point patterns satisfying conditions
(
9
) and (
10
).
Then,

|Q_{n}|^{-1}\mathsf{Var}(H_{n}(\mathcal{X}))\to\sigma^{2},

for some
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
. Moreover,

|Q_{n}|^{-1/2}(H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{X})])\to N(0,\sigma^{2}).

While
[
24
]
give
s
a CLT for weakly stabilizing functionals on Poisson point processes, most CLTs for more general point process models in the literature,
e.g.
[
5
,
7
,
27
,
32
]
, only apply to functionals given in terms of an exponentially stabilizing score function, and
[
3
]
even requires finite stabilization range.
The weak stabilization condition (
12
) closely mimics the one in
[
24
]
, which is, however, only required to hold almost surely in
[
24
]
. For an almost sure condition in the Gibbs setting, one would have to use (
10
).
Condition (
11
) resembles the power growth condition in
[
5
, Equation (1.18)]
, except that it requires a fixed radius
R_{\mathsf{D}M}
.

A formula for the limiting variance
\sigma^{2}
is given in Theorem
30
.
Positivity of
\sigma^{2}
is not part of the statement in Theorem
3
. In Corollary
6
, we
demonstrate in an example how the
positivity can be checked. The argument easily generalizes to a variety of other functionals.

Our second main result is a quantitative normal approximation result in the
Kolmogorov distance

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{K}}(X,Y):=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant u)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leqslant u)|.

between real-valued random variables
X,Y
. Concerning the setup for Theorem
4
, for
n\geqslant 1
, we consider the functional

(13)

\displaystyle{H}(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{x\in\varphi\cap{Q}}g(x,\varphi),\qquad\varphi\in\mathbf{N},

for some measurable score function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{N}\to[0,\infty)
. We follow the convention that
g(x,\varphi)=g(x,\varphi\cup\{x\})
for every locally finite
\varphi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

x\not\in\varphi
.

We first impose a bounded moment condition. More precisely, we assume that

(14)

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[g(x_{1},\mathcal{X}\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\})^{6}]=:c_{\mathsf{m}}<\infty,

where we note that the
x_{1},\dots,x_{5}
do not need to be pairwise distinct.

Second, we require that
g
is
exponentially stabilizing
in the spirit of
[
5
]
. To make this precise, we assume that there is a
stabilization radius

R(x,\varphi)
having exponentially decaying probabilities. That is, first, for all locally finite
\varphi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
x\in\varphi
, we have

g(x,\varphi)=g\big(x,\varphi\cap B_{R(x,\varphi)}(x)\big).

We also assume that the event
\{R(x,\varphi)\leqslant r\}
is measurable with respect to
\varphi\cap B_{r}(x)
. Second, we impose that

(15)

\displaystyle-\limsup_{r\uparrow\infty}\,\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{-1}{\log\mathbb{P}(R(x_{1},\mathcal{X}\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\})>r)}=:c_{\mathsf{es}}>0.

Theorem 4

(Quantitative normal approximation of Gibbsian score sums)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-volume Gibbs point process
satisfying (
7
). Assume that (
14
), (
15
) hold. Moreover, we assume that
g(x,\varphi)=0
implies
g(x,\varphi^{\prime})=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and locally finite
\varphi\subseteq\varphi^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also assume that
Q
is a bounded Borel set with
|Q|>1
and
|B_{(\log|Q|)^{2}}(Q)|\leqslant 2|Q|
. Then,
H:=H(\mathcal{X})
defined at (
13
) satisfies

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{K}}\Big(\frac{H-\mathbb{E}[H]}{\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}(H)}},N(0,1)\Big)\leqslant c_{\mathsf{norm}}\frac{|Q|(\log|Q|)^{2d}}{\mathsf{Var}(H)^{3/2}},

where
c_{\mathsf{norm}}=c_{\mathsf{norm}}(d,\alpha_{0},r_{0},c_{\mathsf{m}},c_{\mathsf{es}})
.

The bound on the parallel set
|B_{(\log|Q|)^{2}}(Q)|
is a technical condition, which for instance can be checked for sets of the form
Q=aK
where
K
is fixed convex and
a>0
large enough. In particular, this condition disappears in our Corollary
5
. Note that Theorem
4
also applies to finite-volume Gibbs processes since the PI
\kappa(x,\varphi\cup\psi)\mathbbmss{1}_{Q}(x)
corresponds to the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
.
As an application, we state two variations of Theorem
4
concerning the convergence rate when the observation window
Q=Q_{n}
increases towards
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the first version, let
\mathcal{X}
be Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
satisfying (
7
) and let

H^{n}:={H^{n}(\mathcal{X}):=}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}}g(x,\mathcal{X}).

Often in applications, only
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}
can be observed, hence it is more natural to consider

H^{n}_{\cap}:={H^{n}_{\cap}(\mathcal{X}):=}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}}g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n})

and modify the assumptions (
14
) and (
15
) by

(16)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[g(x_{1},(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n})\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\})^{6}]

\displaystyle=:c_{\mathsf{m}}<\infty,

(17)

\displaystyle-\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\limsup_{r\uparrow\infty}\,\sup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}r^{-1}{\log\mathbb{P}(R(x_{1},(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n})\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{5}\})>r)}

\displaystyle=:c_{\mathsf{es}}>0.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-volume Gibbs point process
satisfying (
7
). Moreover, we assume that
g(x,\varphi)=0
implies
g(x,\varphi^{\prime})=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and locally finite
\varphi\subseteq\varphi^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assume that (
14
), (

) hold. If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}|Q_{n}|^{-1}\mathsf{Var}(H^{n})>0
, we have for
n
large enough,

d_{\mathsf{K}}\Big(\frac{H^{n}-\mathbb{E}[H^{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}(H^{n})}},N(0,1)\Big)\in O\Big(\frac{(\log|Q_{n}|)^{2d}}{\sqrt{|Q_{n}|}}\Big).

Assume that (
16
), (
17
) hold. If
\liminf_{n\to\infty}|Q_{n}|^{-1}\mathsf{Var}(H_{\cap}^{n})>0
, we have for
n
large enough,

d_{\mathsf{K}}\Big(\frac{H_{\cap}^{n}-\mathbb{E}[H_{\cap}^{n}]}{\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}(H_{\cap}^{n})}},N(0,1)\Big)\in O\Big(\frac{(\log|Q_{n}|)^{2d}}{\sqrt{|Q_{n}|}}\Big).

Similar convergence rates were obtained in
[
32
, Thm. 1.2]
, but for a smaller class of Gibbs processes. As mentioned in Section

, very recently a quantitative CLT was derived in
[

]
under the EDD conditions in without the need for any monotonicity assumptions on the score function

. However, the convergence rates are slightly worse and given in terms of the Wasserstein distance, whereas bounds for the Kolmogorov distance are often easier to interpret. We note that also in other contexts, one frequently encounters the situation where extending Wasserstein convergence rates to Kolmogorov convergence rates requires substantial additional work

.

The weak stabilization condition (
10
) is weaker than the exponential stabilization condition (
17
) when the PI is translation-invariant. We briefly explain why. Fix
l\geqslant 1
. For
i\geqslant 2l
, we let

E_{i,l}^{(1)}:=\Big\{\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{i}}\max_{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\max_{m\in\mathbb{N}}R(x,\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w,m})\leqslant i/2\Big\}

denote the event that the stabilization radius of the points in
Q_{i}
is at most
i/2
for any of the windows
Q_{w,m}
with
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
m\geqslant 1
. We note that for fixed
x
, the number of different sets of the form
B_{i/2}(x)\cap Q_{w,m}
is finite and of order
O(i^{d})
. Hence, by exponential stabilization and the GNZ formula (
6
), the probabilities
1-\mathbb{P}(E_{i,l}^{(1)})
decay to 0 exponentially in
i
. Hence, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, there exists an almost surely finite random variable
I_{0}
such that
E_{i,l}^{(1)}
occurs for all
i\geqslant I_{0}
. We now argue that this implies the weak stabilization. Indeed, let
m_{n}\geqslant 1
and
w_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be such that
\mathbb{R}^{d}=\bigcup_{k\geqslant 1}\bigcap_{n\geqslant k}Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}
. Then,
g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}})=g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}\setminus Q_{l}))
holds for every
n\geqslant 1
and every
x\in(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{I_{0}})\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}
. Therefore, whenever
n
is such that
Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}\supseteq Q_{2I_{0}}
,

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}})-H(\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}\setminus Q_{l}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{l}}g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap B_{I_{0}}(x))+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{I_{0}}\setminus Q_{l})}(g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{2I_{0}})-g(x,\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}\setminus Q_{l}))).

Noting that the last term stabilizes when
n\to\infty
if the stabilization radius
R(x,\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{l})
is finite proves the weak stabilization condition.

To prove Theorems
3
and
4
, we extend to Gibbs processes the martingale approach from
[
24
]
and the Palm couplings from
[
6
]
. The proofs rely on a precise control of the decay of spatial correlations through refined forms of disagreement coupling given in Section

that are tailored to Theorems

and
4
.

3.1.
Example for Theorem
3
: Persistent Betti numbers

The persistent Betti numbers are invariants used in topological data analysis for summarizing the persistence diagram. They are defined for
r\leqslant s
,
q=0,1,\ldots,d-1
and a finite set
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
by

\beta^{r,s}_{q}{(\varphi)}=\mathsf{dim}\left(\mathsf{Im}(H_{q}(B_{r}(\varphi);\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to H_{q}(B_{s}(\varphi);\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}))\right),

where
H_{q}(\cdot,\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
denotes the
q
th homology group with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
.
Homology is a fundamental quantity in algebraic topology. Loosely speaking, the rank of
H_{q}
encodes the number of
q
-dimensional holes of a topological space, see
[
10
]
for a thorough introduction to topological data analysis.

The use of persistent Betti numbers for statistics of point patterns was first suggested in
[
26
]
under the name rank functions.

Most existing CLTs for functionals on point processes, e.g.
[
5
,
32
]
, assume the functional is written in terms of an exponentially stabilizing score function. This is not easily verified for
\beta^{r,s}_{q}
.
However, some first results on consistency and asymptotic normality for Betti numbers were given when
r=s
in
[
30
,
33
,
34
]
. These results were extended to general
r
and
s
in
[
13
,
17
]
. All asymptotic normality results previously considered have been restricted to Poisson or Bernoulli point processes. Existing CLTs were derived by observing that weak stabilization in the sense of
[
24
]
holds for persistent Betti numbers. The same observation leads to the following corollary of Theorem
3
.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an infinite-volume Gibbs point process
with translation-invariant PI satisfying (
7
). Then for all
0\leq r\leq s

|Q_{n}|^{-1/2}(\beta_{q}^{r,s}(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n})-\mathbb{E}\beta_{q}^{r,s}(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}))\to N(0,\sigma^{2})

for some
\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0
. If
\kappa>0
, then
\sigma^{2}>0
.

We show the first statement of Corollary
6
below. The proof of
the
positivity of the limiting variance is deferred to the end of Section
5.2
. The proof relies on the fact that
\beta_{q}^{r,s}
can be computed from the Čech complex via the Nerve Theorem, see
[
10
, Chap. III.2]
for details.

Proof.

The corollary follows from Theorem
3
if we can show Condition (
11
) and (
12
). Condition (
12
) follows directly from
[
13
, Lem 5.3]
.
For Condition (
11
), recall that
k+1
points in a finite point pattern
\varphi
form a
k
-simplex of filtration value
t
in the Čech complex if and only if the smallest ball containing the
k+1
points has radius
t
. Thus, adding a point
y
to a point pattern
\varphi
changes the
k
-simplices in the Čech complex with filtration value less than or equal
s
only by adding and removing simplices with all
k+1
vertices contained in a ball of radius
2s
around
y
. By
[
13
, Lem. 2.11]
, this changes
\beta_{q}^{r,s}
by at most the number of added and removed
q
- and
(q+1)
-simplices. This number is bounded by the total number of
(q+1)
- and
(q+2)
-tuples of points in
\varphi\cap B_{2s}(y)
, which is again bounded by

\big(\varphi(B_{2s}(y))+1\big)^{q+1}+\big(\varphi(B_{2s}(y))+1\big)^{q+2}\leqslant 2\big(\varphi(B_{2s}(y))+1\big)^{q+2}.

This shows Condition (
11
).
∎

3.2.
Example for Theorem
3
: Total edge length in minimal spanning trees

For a finite set
\varphi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the minimal spanning tree (MST) is a tree defined on the vertex set
\varphi
. The edge set of this tree is chosen such that it minimizes the total edge length. We note that this tree is almost surely unique for any point process that satisfies (
8
). We let
H(\varphi)
denote the total edge length of the MST. For Poisson point processes, the asymptotic properties of
H
were considered in
[
16
]
and
[
25
, Theorem 2.3]
. We discuss this example because it is one of the prototypical examples where exponential stabilization fails even in the Poisson setting. Hence, we are not able to rely on Theorem
4
. To verify (
9
) and (
10
), we proceed as in
[
16
]
with only few modifications. To make the presentation self-contained we give the details below.

Moment bound.

For any bounded Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
, we have a trivial upper bound

(18)

\displaystyle H(\varphi\cap A)\leqslant\mathsf{diam}(A)\varphi(A).

We bound the positive and negative parts of
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})
separately.
For the positive part, we first note that if
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}\subseteq Q_{z,m}
, then by (
18
),

H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})\leqslant\sqrt{d}m\mathcal{X}(Q_{z,m}),

and by the stochastic domination (
7
), the fifth moment of the right-hand side is of order
O(m^{5+5d})
. Thus, we may henceforth assume that
K_{z,m}\leqslant m_{0}(n)
, where we write

K_{z,m}:=\min\big\{k\geqslant m\colon\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{z,k}\not\subseteq Q_{z,m}\big\}

and

m_{0}(n):=\min\big\{k\geqslant m\colon Q_{n}\subseteq Q_{z,k}\big\}.

Now, we derive an upper bound for
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})
. For this, we first build a minimal spanning tree on
\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{n}\setminus Q_{z,m})
, then build a spanning tree on
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{z,m}
and finally connect the two trees through a point in
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{z,K_{z,m}}
to build a spanning tree on
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}
. Therefore,

(19)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathcal{X})\leqslant H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})+\sqrt{d}K_{z,m}\mathcal{X}(Q_{z,m}).

As noted above, the tenth moment of
\mathcal{X}(Q_{z,m})
is of order
O(m^{10d})
. Hence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it suffices to show that the tenth moment of
K_{z,m}
grows polynomially in
m
. To that end, we note that

(20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[K_{z,m}^{10}]\leqslant\sum_{k\geqslant m}k^{10}\mathbb{P}(K_{z,m}=k)

Now, by the Poisson-likeness derived in Proposition
42
, we have
\sup_{n\geqslant 1}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathbb{P}(K_{z,m}=k)\leqslant c_{1}(k^{d}-m^{d})\exp\big(-(k^{d}-m^{d})/c_{1}\big)
. Hence, the right-hand side of (
20
) is of order
O(m^{d+10})
, as asserted.

Finally, we bound the negative part of the difference
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})
. First, if
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}\subseteq Q_{z,m}
, then
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})
is positive. If
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}\subseteq Q_{n}\setminus Q_{z,m}
then
H_{n}(\mathcal{X})=H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})
. Hence, we may assume that in the MST on
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}
there are some edges crossing
\partial Q_{z,m}
. We now bound the length of the MST on
\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{n}\setminus Q_{z,m})
where as a starting point, we consider the MST on
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}
. Let

\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}:=\Big\{\text{ edges in the MST of $\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}$ with exactly one endpoint in $\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{z,m}$}\Big\}.

Then, we get a graph connecting
\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{n}\setminus Q_{z,m})
by removing all edges with at least one endpoint in
Q_{z,m}
and connecting all points in
\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{n}\setminus Q_{z,m})
that used to be connected to
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{z,m}
to any fixed other such point. The length of any such new connection is bounded by
\sqrt{d}m
plus the length of two edges from
\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}
.

Henceforth, we use that there is a deterministic upper bound
d_{\mathsf{max}}\geqslant 1
for the possible degree of any vertex in the MST.
This yields

(21)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathcal{X}\setminus Q_{z,m})-H_{n}(\mathcal{X})\leqslant\sum_{e\in\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}}(2L_{z,m,n}+\sqrt{d}m)\leqslant d_{\mathsf{max}}\mathcal{X}(Q_{z,m})(2L_{z,m,n}+\sqrt{d}m),

where

L_{z,m,n}:=\max_{e\in\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}}|e|

denotes the maximal edge length in
\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}
. Hence, as in the case for the positive part, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{E}[L_{z,m,n}^{10}]
is at most of polynomial order in
m
. To achieve this goal, we note that

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[L_{z,m,n}^{10}]\leqslant\sum_{\ell\geqslant 0}(\ell+1)\mathbb{P}(L_{z,m,n}^{10}\geqslant\ell).

Now, under the event
\{L_{z,m,n}\geqslant\ell^{1/10}\}
, there exists an edge
e\in\mathcal{L}_{z,m,n}
of length at least
\ell^{1/10}
. Since the MST is a subset of the Delaunay complex, there is a ball with diameter at least
|e|
containing
e
that does not contain any points of
\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{n}
in its interior. But again by the Poisson-likeness, the probability of this event can be bounded by
c_{1}(m+\ell^{1/10})^{d}\exp(-\ell^{d/10}/c_{1})
for some constant
c_{1}>0
. Therefore, the right-hand side of (
22
) is of order
O(m^{d})
, as asserted.

Weak stabilization.

To verify the weak stabilization condition, we proceed as in
[
16
, Proposition 3]
. To make this precise, we first need the notion of separating sets. More precisely, for
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
, a
separating set of width
\delta>0
for a box
Q_{l}
is a finite set
S\subseteq\varphi\cap(Q_{l+\delta+18r_{0}}\setminus Q_{l+18r_{0}})
such that any line segment from a point in
\partial Q_{l+18r_{0}}
to a point in
\partial Q_{l+\delta+18r_{0}}
passes within distance
3r_{0}
of some point of
S
. We note our definition is scaled by
9r_{0}
compared to the original definition in
[
16
]
, which uses 1 instead of
9r_{0}
in the definition of the windows and
1/3
instead of
3r_{0}
for the distance. After appropriate rescaling, the arguments in
[
16
]
extend to our setting.
We shall need the following two results from
[
16
]
, which we restate in our setting.

Proposition 7

(Monotonicity in increasing windows; Proposition 3 of
[
16
]
)
.

Let
l\geqslant 1
and
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
. Assume that
S\subseteq{\varphi}\cap(Q_{l+\delta+18r_{0}}\setminus Q_{l+18r_{0}})
is a separating set of width
\delta>2r_{0}
for
Q_{l}
. Then, for any
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
m,m^{\prime}\geqslant 1
with
Q_{l+\delta+18r_{0}}\subseteq Q_{w,m}\subseteq Q_{w^{\prime},m^{\prime}}
, we have

H({\varphi}\cap Q_{w,m})-H({\varphi}\cap(Q_{w,m}\setminus Q_{l}))\leqslant H({\varphi}\cap Q_{w^{\prime},m^{\prime}})-H({\varphi}\cap(Q_{w^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\setminus Q_{l})).

The value of Proposition
7
becomes apparent when combined with (
19
) since the latter shows that almost surely the sequence
H(\mathcal{X}\cap Q_{w_{n},m_{n}})-H(\mathcal{X}\cap(Q_{w_{n},m_{n}}\setminus Q_{l}))
remains bounded as
n\uparrow\infty
. Hence, the almost sure convergence in (
10
) holds as soon as we can show that almost surely
\mathcal{X}
has a
\delta
-separating set for
Q_{l}
for some
\delta>0
.

To prove this final step, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it remains to show that the probability that there fails to be a
\delta
-separating set decays at exponential speed in
\delta
. To this end, we choose a set of points
\phi\subseteq\partial Q_{l+2r_{0}}
such that every point in
\partial Q_{l+2r_{0}}
is at distance at most
r_{0}
from a point in
\phi
. This is possible with a cardinality
\#\phi\in O(l^{d-1})
. Similarly, we choose a subset
\phi^{\prime}
with the same property in
\partial Q_{l+\delta+2r_{0}}
, where now
\#\phi^{\prime}\in O((l+\delta)^{d-1})
. In particular, if for every line segment between
\phi
and
\phi^{\prime}
there is a point of
S
within distance
2r_{0}
, then for every line segment between
\partial Q_{l+2r_{0}}
and
\partial Q_{l+\delta+2r_{0}}
there is a point of
S
within distance
3r_{0}
.

Finally, the Poisson-likeness from Proposition
42
shows that for fixed
P\in\partial Q_{l+2r_{0}}
and
P^{\prime}\in Q_{l+\delta+2r_{0}}
, the probability
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{X}\cap B_{2r_{0}}([P,P^{\prime}])=\varnothing\big)
decays exponentially in
\delta
, where
[P,P^{\prime}]
denotes the line segment from
P
to
P^{\prime}
. Since
\#\phi
and
\#\phi^{\prime}
were of order
O(l^{d-1})
and
O((l+\delta)^{d-1})
, a union bound concludes the proof.

3.3.
Example for Theorem
4
: Total edge length of Gibbs-Voronoi tessellations

For a locally finite
\varphi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
we define the
Voronoi cell

C(x,\varphi)
of
x\in\varphi
as the set of all points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose Euclidean distance to
x
is less than or equal to its distance to all other points of
\varphi
. The system
\{C(x,\varphi)\}_{x\in\varphi}
is called the
Voronoi tessellation
. We define
g(x,\varphi)
as one half of the total boundary length of the cell
C(x,\varphi)
. We use the convention that
g(x,\varphi)=\infty
if the cell
C(x,\varphi)
is unbounded. Note that this convention is only made for completeness and does not play any practical role for the functionals considered since by stationarity, all cells
C(x,\mathcal{X})
are bounded with probability 1.
Then, the total edge length of the Voronoi tessellation induced by
\varphi
with
generators
in
Q_{n}
is (up to boundary effects) given by

\displaystyle H^{n}:=\sum_{x\in\varphi\cap Q_{n}}g(x,\varphi).

As observed in Section
2.1
, Gibbs processes satisfying Assumption (
7
) are Poisson-like. Hence, one can proceed as in
[
27
, Section 1 and 5.2]
to obtain the bounded moment condition (
14
) and the exponential stabilization property (
15
). Moreover,
[
32
, Theorem 2.3]
discusses further conditions under which the variance lower bound can be verified.
For the condition
g(x,\varphi\cup\{y\})\leqslant g(x,\varphi)
, we note that
C(x,\varphi\cup\{y\})\subseteq C(x,\varphi)
are convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence, it follows from Theorem
4
that

H^{n}
satisfies a CLT.

This extends
[
6
, Theorem 4.13]
to Gibbs processes satisfying (
7
). We also note that if we are not interested in rates, then this example follows from
[
5
, Theorem 1.13]
. Indeed, we first recall from Corollary
40
that under Assumption (
7
) the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}
is EDD. Moreover, as mentioned in
[
7
, Section 1]
, the EDD property is stronger than the fast decay of correlation formulated in
[
5
, Equation (1.10)]
. Together with the aforementioned exponential stabilization (also proved in
[
3
]
), this implies that the considered score function and point process form an admissible pair in the sense of
[
5
, Definition 1.7]
. Finally, combining this observation with the aforementioned upper moment bound and lower variance bound, we see that
[
5
, Theorem 1.13]
gives the asymptotic normality without rates.

We discuss the

, we put an edge between

if

\displaystyle H^{n}:=\sum_{x\in\varphi\cap Q_{n}}g(x,\varphi).

As in Example

, the derivation of the bounded moment condition (

) and of the exponential stabilization property (

,

4.
Disagreement couplings

Disagreement coupling is a technique for constructing Gibbs point processes in bounded windows by thinning a dominating Poisson process. We present the algorithms we use in Section
4.1
. The original and most important use of disagreement coupling is to control the amount of disagreement between Gibbs processes with different boundary conditions by relating them to the connected components of an associated Boolean model. Another important application in the context of normal approximation is to bound the spatial correlations of the Gibbs process. We show some spatial homogeneity and decorrelation results in Section
4.2
. In Section
4.4
, we show how disagreement coupling can be used to construct couplings between Gibbs processes in bounded domains and the unbounded domain with strong control over their disagreement. This is a main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
3
.
For the proof of Theorem
4
, we need couplings between a whole family Gibbs processes obtained by local perturbations of the PI with bounds on the pairwise disagreement. Such couplings are given in Section
4.4
. The result is also be essential for the proof of Theorem
3
, where it allows us to consider couplings in unbounded domains with locally differing boundary conditions.

The original definition of disagreement coupling in
[
18
]
applied to Gibbs processes on standard Borel spaces, thereby allowing applications to particle processes
[
3
]
and Gibbs processes with density with respect to random connection models
[
4
]
. However, since all our applications of disagreement coupling are to processes on Euclidean space, and to keep the exposition simple, we restrict to the Euclidean case.

4.1.
Thinning algorithms

In this section, we review the construction from
[
18
]
of the finite-volume Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
on a bounded domain
Q
with boundary conditions
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
as a thinning of a Poisson process. In Section
4.1.1
, we first review the basic version of the algorithm introduced in
[
18
, Sec. 5]
. This is the building block for more refined algorithms in Section
4.1.2
. We give a general recipe for constructing thinning algorithms with more flexible properties generalizing the one suggested in
[
18
, Sec. 6]
. Moreover, we give two examples of such algorithms which are designed to have the properties, we need for the proofs of Theorems
3
and
4
.

Henceforth, we let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
be a homogeneous unit-intensity Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
and write
\mathcal{P}
for the projection of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
onto the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
coordinate, which is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity
\alpha_{0}
. More generally, we use
\varphi^{*}
to denote elements of
\mathbf{N}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}
and
\varphi
denotes its projection to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We let
\varphi^{*}_{Q}
and
\varphi_{Q}
be the restriction of
\varphi^{*}
and
\varphi
to
Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
and
Q
, respectively. We refer to the points of
\varphi
in a connected component of
B_{r}(\varphi)
as a cluster.

4.1.1.
Standard Poisson embedding

Consider a bounded Borel set
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a set of boundary conditions
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
. In this section, we introduce
the standard thinning operator

T_{Q,\psi}:\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}\to\mathbf{N}_{Q}
. For
\varphi^{*}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}
, the set
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})
is a thinning of
\varphi
, that is
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})\subseteq\varphi
. The
(d+1)
st coordinate of
\varphi^{*}
is used to decide whether or not to keep the point in the thinning. The key property of
T_{Q,\psi}
is that the thinning
T_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}
of the Poisson process
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q}
has the distribution of the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
. We call
T_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
the
standard Poisson embedding
, since it provides an embedding
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)\subseteq\mathcal{P}
.

To define
T_{Q,\psi}
, we need an injective measurable map
\iota\colon Q\to\mathbb{R}
. Such a map is guaranteed to exist since
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a standard Borel space. We sometimes write
T_{Q,\psi,\iota}
to emphasize that
T_{Q,\psi}
depends on
\iota
. The map
\iota
defines a total ordering
\leqslant_{\iota}
on
Q
. We sometimes refer to the map
\iota
itself as an ordering.
The main idea to construct
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})
is to go through the points of
\varphi
in the order
\leqslant_{\iota}
and decide for each whether to keep it in the thinned process. For
x\in Q
, define

\displaystyle Q_{(-\infty,x)}{}:=\{y\in Q\colon y<_{\iota}x\}\,\text{ and }\,Q_{(x,\infty)}{}:=\{y\in Q\colon y>_{\iota}x\}.

For
x\in Q
and
\psi^{\prime}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(Q_{(x,\infty)}\cup\{x\}))\times\mathbb{R}
, define the retention threshold

(23)

\displaystyle p(x,Q,\psi^{\prime}):=\kappa(x,\psi^{\prime})\frac{Z_{Q_{(x,\infty)}}\big(\psi^{\prime}\cup\{x\}\big)}{Z_{Q_{(x,\infty)}}(\psi^{\prime})}.

It is shown in
[
18
, Lem. 4.1]
that
0\leqslant p(x,Q,\psi^{\prime})\leqslant\alpha_{0}
.

The algorithm for constructing
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
goes as follows:

\bullet

To define the first point
x_{1}:=x_{1}(\varphi^{*})
of
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})
, we consider the set

\big\{x\in Q\colon(x,u)\in\varphi^{*}\text{ and }u\leqslant p(x,Q,\psi)\big\}

for which
u
is below the retention threshold. We choose
x_{1}
to be the smallest element in this set with respect to the
\leqslant_{\iota}
-order. If there is no such element, then the construction terminates with
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})=\varnothing
.

\bullet

Otherwise, we proceed inductively. Suppose, we have defined the points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
. This will be the restriction of
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})
to
Q_{(-\infty,x_{k})}
. It remains to define
T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})
on
Q_{(x_{k},\infty)}
. We define
x_{k+1}=x_{k+1}(\varphi^{*})
to be the
\iota
-smallest element of the set

(24)

\big\{x\in Q_{(x_{k},\infty)}\colon(x,u)\in\varphi^{*}\text{ and }u\leqslant p(x,Q_{(x,\infty)},\psi\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\})\big\}.

\bullet

The construction terminates once the set (
24
) is empty.

We state the key result of
[
18
, Thm. 5.1]
, namely that the thinned Poisson process
T_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
has the distribution of the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
, for later reference.

Proposition 8

(Correctness of the Poisson embedding
[
18
]
)
.

Assume that the PI satisfies
\kappa\leqslant\alpha_{0}
. Let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded Borel and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
be finite. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{P}^{*}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
be a unit-intensity Poisson point process. Then,
T_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{Q}
is distributed as the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
.

Proof.

See
[
18
, Thm. 5.1]
.
∎

We remark for later that the standard thinning algorithm has the property that if
B\subseteq Q
such that
\iota(x)<\iota(y)
for all
x\in B
,
y\in Q\setminus B
, and
\xi=T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})\cap B
, then

(25)

T_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*})=\xi\cup T_{Q\setminus B,\psi\cup\xi}(\varphi^{*}_{Q\setminus B}).

While an injective measurable map
\iota:Q\to\mathbb{R}
is guaranteed to exist, it is not so intuitive what such a map looks like. While usually not strictly necessary, it is often convenient to replace
\iota
by a more familiar map.
Thus, let
\iota:Q\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable map such that

\big|\iota^{-1}(r)\big|=0

for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
. We define a partial ordering of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
x<_{\iota}y
if
\iota(x)<\iota(y)
. If
\big|\iota^{-1}(r)\big|=0
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, this almost surely induces a well-defined total ordering of the Poisson process and hence
T_{Q,\psi,\iota}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
becomes well defined.

Proposition 9
.

Assume that
\iota:Q\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable map such that for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
,

(26)

\big|\iota^{-1}(r)\big|=0.

Then,
T_{Q,\psi,\iota}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
is distributed as
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
.

The proof is deferred to the appendix. Using Proposition
9
, one may order
Q
e.g. by the distance to the origin.

4.1.2.
Disagreement couplings

The standard Poisson embedding defines a coupling between
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
and
\mathcal{P}
for any choice of boundary conditions
\psi
. However, it does not provide much control over how
T_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
are related for different choices of
\psi
. For this reason, a refined thinning algorithm called
disagreement coupling
was suggested in
[
18
]
. This algorithm makes it possible to control the amount of disagreement between Gibbs processes with different boundary conditions. However, as we shall need some extra properties of the algorithms compared to
[
18
]
, we first give a general recipe for constructing disagreement couplings. We then give two examples of such algorithms.

To define the algorithm, we need the notion of a
stopping set
. A stopping set
S
on a Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
assigns to each
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{B}
a Borel set
S(\varphi)\subseteq B
. We require that the map
(x,\varphi)\mapsto\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\in S(\varphi)\}}
is measurable. Moreover,
S
must have the property that
S(\varphi)
depends only on
\varphi\cap S(\varphi)
, i.e.
S(\varphi)=S(\varphi\cap S(\varphi))
for all
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}_{B}
. We write
S=S(\varphi)
when
\varphi
is clear from the context. A key property of a stopping set is the following, see e.g.
[
21
, Thm. A.3]
: If
S
is a stopping set on
B
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a Poisson processes on
B
, then

(27)

\text{conditionally on }\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{S(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})},\,\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{B\setminus S(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})}\text{ is distributed as a Poisson process on $B\setminus S(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})$.}

The disagreement coupling algorithm needs two ingredients:

\bullet

An sequence
S_{0}^{*},S_{1}^{*},S_{2}^{*},\dots
of stopping sets on
Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
of the form
S_{n}^{*}=S_{n}\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
. The sequence should increase towards
Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
in the sense that for any
\varphi^{*}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}
,

S_{0}^{*}(\varphi^{*})=\varnothing\subseteq S_{1}^{*}(\varphi^{*})\subseteq S_{2}^{*}(\varphi^{*})\subseteq\dots\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigcup_{n}S_{n}^{*}(\varphi^{*})=Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}].

\bullet

A family of injective ordering maps
\iota_{n}:=\iota_{n}^{\varphi^{*}}:Q\setminus S_{n}(\varphi^{*})\to\mathbb{R}
for
n\geqslant 0
and
\varphi^{*}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}
satisfying (
26
) such that
\iota_{n}^{\varphi^{*}}
depends only on
\varphi^{*}
via
\varphi^{*}\cap S_{n}(\varphi^{*})
. Moreover, we require

(28)

\iota_{n}(x)<\iota_{n}(y)\text{ for all }x\in S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n},y\in Q\setminus S_{n+1}.

To ensure measurability, we extend
\iota_{n}
to a map
\tilde{\iota}_{n}:Q\times\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
by setting
\tilde{\iota}_{n}^{\varphi^{*}}(x)=-\infty
if
x\in S_{n}(\varphi^{*})
. We then require that
\tilde{\iota}_{n}:Q\times\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, is measurable.

Let
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
be the boundary conditions. The disagreement coupling thinning operator
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}:\mathbf{N}_{Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]}\to\mathbf{N}_{Q}
constructs the thinning of a point pattern
\varphi^{*}
inductively in
n
by constructing the thinned point pattern
\xi_{n}:=\xi_{n}(\varphi^{*})
on the set
S_{n}\setminus S_{n-1}
as follows:

1.

First, apply the standard thinning algorithm
T_{Q,\psi,\iota_{0}}
on
Q
and set
\chi_{1}:=\chi_{1}(\varphi^{*}):=T_{Q,\psi,\iota_{1}}(\varphi^{*})
. Let
\xi_{1}:=\chi_{1}\cap S_{1}
.

2.

Assume inductively that we have constructed
\xi_{i}
on
S_{i}\setminus S_{i-1}
for
i\leqslant n
. The thinning of
\varphi^{*}
on
S_{n}
is
\xi_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\xi_{n}
. We next construct the thinning on
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
. For this, we add
\xi_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\xi_{n}
to the boundary conditions
\psi
and apply the standard thinning operator on
Q\setminus S_{n}
with ordering
\iota_{n}
to obtain
\chi_{n+1}:=\chi_{n+1}(\varphi^{*}):=T_{Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cup\xi_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\xi_{n},\iota_{n}}(\varphi^{*})
. We set
\xi_{n+1}:=\chi_{n+1}\cap(S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n})
.

3.

Finally, we define
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*}):=\bigcup_{n\geqslant 1}\xi_{n}
.

Note that the algorithm corresponds to applying the standard thinning operator, but with the fixed ordering replaced by an ordering
\tilde{\iota}
that depends on
\varphi^{*}
, where
\tilde{\iota}
equals
\iota_{n}^{\varphi^{*}}
on
S_{n+1}(\varphi^{*})\setminus S_{n}(\varphi^{*})
.

To illustrate this abstract framework, we now present a specific example, where we want to construct a disagreement coupling such that changing the boundary conditions
\psi
locally in a set
B\subseteq Q^{c}
does not change the Gibbs process too much. Before discussing all technical details, we first describe the general intuition. Loosely speaking, the stopping sets work their way through all the connected components of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\varphi)
that intersect
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
. Afterwards, they proceed through the remaining components of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\varphi)
one at a time(note that each component
\mathcal{C}
of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\varphi)
is enlarged to
B_{r_{0}/2}(\mathcal{C})
when defining the stopping sets).
Now, we turn to the precise construction.

Example 10
.

[Cluster-based disagreement coupling]

Define
S_{1}:=B_{r_{0}}(B)
. This is a deterministic set consisting of all points within distance
r_{0}
from
B
. Choose an ordering map
\iota_{0}:Q\to[0,1)
such that
\iota_{0}(x)<\iota_{0}(y)
whenever
x\in S_{1}
and
Q\setminus S_{1}
. Suppose
S_{n}
is defined. If
B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{n})\setminus S_{n}\neq\varnothing
, we take
S_{n+1}:=S_{n}\cup B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{n})
. Otherwise, let
S_{n+1}=S_{n}\cup Q_{(-\infty,x)}
where
x
is the
\iota_{0}
-smallest point in
(Q\setminus S_{n})\cap\varphi
. If no such point exists, we set
S_{n+1}=Q
. To define the ordering
\iota_{n}:Q\setminus S_{n}\to[0,1)
, we let

\iota_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}\iota_{0}\mathbbmss{1}{\{B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{n})\setminus S_{n}\}}+(\tfrac{1}{2}\iota_{0}+\tfrac{1}{2})\mathbbmss{1}{\{Q\setminus(S_{n}\cup B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{n}))\}},

which satisfies (
28
).

Note that The order in which the components not intersecting
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
are visited is determined by the
\iota_{0}
-smallest point in the cluster.

The advantage of this construction is that whenever
B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{n})=S_{n}
, so that we jump to a new cluster, it means that
(\varphi\cap S_{n})\cup(\psi\cap B)
has distance more than
r_{0}
from
Q\setminus S_{n}
. Hence, the retention probabilities (
23
) used in the remainder of the algorithm are not influenced by
(\varphi\cap S_{n})\cup(\psi\cap B)
. Therefore, the decision about the thinning of each cluster does not affect the thinning of the following clusters. Moreover, the boundary points in
B
,
\psi\cap B
, can only influence the thinning of clusters corresponding to components of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\varphi)
that intersect
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
. Changing
\psi\cap B
, the Gibbs processes agree on all other clusters

The following proposition shows that
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
is indeed a Gibbs process. Note that if the maps
\iota_{n}
are not injective, but satisfy (
26
), then
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
is still well defined almost surely.

Proposition 11

(Correctness of disagreement coupling)
.

Suppose the PI is bounded by
\alpha_{0}
. Let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Borel set and let
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q}\subseteq Q\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
be a unit-intensity Poisson point process. Then,
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
has the distribution of the Gibbs process
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
. This also holds if the maps
\iota_{n}
are not injective, but satisfy (
26
).

The proof is similar to that of
[
18
, Thm. 6.3]
, except that in
[
18
]
,
S_{n+1}^{*}
was determined by
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}
, while we only require
S_{n+1}^{*}
to be a stopping set. This only changes the argument in (
30
) below.

Proof.

We start by showing that
\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
by induction in
n
, where
\sim
means that the point processes have the same distribution. For
n=0
,
\chi_{1}=T_{Q,\psi,\iota_{0}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\sim\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
by Proposition
8
.
Assume for induction that it has been shown that
\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n-1}\cup\chi_{n}\sim\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
.
Taking a bounded nonnegative function
f:\mathbf{N}_{Q}\to[0,\infty)
, we must show

(29)

\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi))].

Let
\iota_{n-1}^{\prime}
be the restriction of
\iota_{n-1}
to
Q\setminus S_{n}
. Note that by (
28
), both the maps
\iota_{n},\iota_{n-1}^{\prime}:Q\setminus S_{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}
are completely determined by
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}
. Thus, conditionally on
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}
,

\chi_{n+1}=T_{Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cap\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n},\iota_{n}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q\setminus S_{n}^{*}})\sim\mathcal{X}(Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cup\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n})\sim T_{Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cup\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n},\iota_{n-1}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q\setminus S_{n}^{*}})

by definition of
\chi_{n+1}
, the property (
27
) of the stopping set
S_{n}
, and Proposition
8
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1})]{}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup T_{Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cup\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n},\iota_{n-1}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q\setminus S_{n}^{*}}))|\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}]].

Moreover, given
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}
, (
28
) and (
25
) implies

(30)

\xi_{n}\cup T_{Q\setminus S_{n},\psi\cup\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n},\iota_{n-1}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q\setminus S_{n}^{*}})=\chi_{n}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q\setminus S_{n-1}^{*}}).

Hence, by the induction assumption,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1})]{}=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n-1}\cup\chi_{n})|\mathcal{P}^{*}_{S_{n}^{*}}]\big]=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi))].

This shows (
29
).

To complete the proof, take again a bounded nonnegative function
f:\mathbf{N}_{Q}\to[0,\infty)
. Since
\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q}
is almost surely finite, there is almost surely a random
N\geqslant 1
such that
T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\varphi^{*}):=\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1}
for all
n\geqslant N
. Dominated convergence shows that

\mathbb{E}[f(T^{\mathsf{dc}}_{Q,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q}))]=\mathbb{E}\big[f(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 1}\xi_{n})\big]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1}\cup\dots\cup\xi_{n}\cup\chi_{n+1})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi))],

which proves the theorem.
∎

The algorithm in Example
10
suffices for most purposes in this paper. However, it has the disadvantage that
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
can be very large, thereby causing issues for deriving the total variation bounds needed for the later arguments, see Theorem
25
. Therefore, we now discuss a refinement where
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
contains at most one point from
\varphi
and
\big|S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}\big|\leqslant\big|B_{r_{0}}\big|
.

This is achieved by an algorithm
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}
that we refer to as
radial coupling
. We note that the construction of this radial coupling below is rather involved. However, for most applications later, we shall not need the precise specification of the algorithm, but only its main properties. Therefore, we now state them here for easy reference.

Proposition 12
.

Suppose the PI is bounded by
\alpha_{0}
. Let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded Borel and
B\subseteq Q^{c}
. Furthermore, let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
be a unit-intensity Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\alpha_{0}]
. Then, the radial coupling has the property that if
\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
are locally finite sets differing only on
B
, then
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
and
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
differ only on the components of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\mathcal{P}_{Q})
intersecting
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
. Moreover, each
S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}
contains at most one point from
\mathcal{P}
, and
\big|S_{n+1}\setminus S_{n}\big|\leqslant\big|B_{r_{0}}\big|
.

Example 13

(Radial coupling)
.

The idea of this algorithm is similar to the one given in Example
10
to go through the clusters of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\varphi)
one at a time, but the description is more involved.
We start again from a domain
Q
, boundary conditions
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
, and a set
B\subseteq Q^{c}
where we want to be able to change the boundary conditions
\psi\cap B
. We choose a fixed ordering
\iota:Q\to[0,1)
satisfying (
26
) and a deterministic locally finite set
D\subseteq Q
with the coverage property that
Q\subseteq B_{r_{0}}(D)
. We also fix a total ordering
\preceq
on
D
.

The stopping steps
S_{i,j}
of the algorithm are indexed by a pair of lexicographically ordered integers
(i,j)
,
i=1,\dots,I
,
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
, where
I:=I(\varphi^{*})
and
N_{i}:=N_{i}(\varphi^{*})
.
The stopping set
S_{i,j}
is given at the beginning of step
(i,j)
of the algorithm. In this step, we define three auxiliary subsets of
Q
that depend on
\varphi^{*}
, namely,

(1)

{v_{i,j+1}}\in D\cup\varphi
, a point from which we start
(i,j)
th step;

(2)

V_{i,j+1}:=(Q\setminus S_{i,j})\cap B_{r_{0}}(v_{i,j+1})
, a set used for defining
\iota_{i,j}
;

(3)

{Z_{i,j+1}}=S_{i,j+1}\setminus S_{i,j}\subseteq V_{i,j+1}
, which is used to define the stopping set
S_{i,j+1}
.

Here and in the following, we use the convention
(i,N_{i}+1)=(i+1,1)
and
(i,0)=(i-1,N_{i})
.

We initialize the algorithm by
S_{1,1}=\varnothing
. For step
(i,j)
, suppose
S_{i,j}
has been defined.

(1)

We first choose
v_{i,j+1}
either as a point in
\varphi\cap S_{i,j}
if
B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{i,j})\cap(Q\setminus S_{i,j})\neq\varnothing
or, if
B_{r_{0}}(\varphi\cap S_{i,j})\subseteq S_{i,j}
, as a point in
D
. In the latter case, we let
j=N_{i}
and hence
(i,j+1)
equals
(i+1,1)
. The precise algorithm for choosing
v_{i,j+1}
is quite involved, and will be given later.

(2)

Having chosen
v_{i,j+1}
, we set

V_{i,j+1}:=B_{r_{0}}(v_{i,j+1})\cap(Q\setminus S_{i,j}).

Define the ordering

\iota_{i,j}=\tfrac{1}{2}\iota\mathbbmss{1}_{V_{i,j+1}}+(\tfrac{1}{2}\iota+\tfrac{1}{2})\mathbbmss{1}_{Q\setminus(S_{i,j}\cup V_{i,j+1})}.

Note that the set
V_{i,j+1}
, and hence also the map
\iota_{i,j}
, is completely determined by
\varphi\cap S_{i,j}
.

(3)

Now,
Z_{i,j+1}
is defined as
Z_{i,j+1}:=V_{i,j+1}
if
\varphi\cap V_{i,j+1}=\varnothing
, and otherwise

4.2.
Homogeneity and decorrelation via disagreement coupling

The fact that the radial coupling works on the clusters of the Boolean model
B_{r_{0}/2}(\mathcal{P})
allows us to relate the Gibbs process to the percolation properties of the Boolean model. Since we assume
r_{0}<r_{c}(\alpha_{0})
, we already know that the connected components of
B_{r_{0}/2}(\mathcal{P})
are almost surely all finite. Moreover, we shall heavily rely on the following fundamental result from continuum percolation known as the
sharp phase transition
. For Borel sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let
\{A\leftrightsquigarrow B\}
denote the event that there exists a connected component of the Boolean model
B_{r_{0}/2}(\mathcal{P})
intersecting both
B_{r_{0}/2}(A)
and
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
. Then, for
r<r_{c}(\alpha_{0})
, the connection probabilities
\mathbb{P}(A\leftrightsquigarrow B)
decays exponentially fast in the distance between
A
and
B
.

Proposition 14

(Sharp phase transition,
[
9
]
)
.

Let
r_{0}<r_{c}(\alpha_{0})
. Then, there are constants
c_{\mathsf{SPT},1},c_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\big(o\leftrightsquigarrow\partial B_{s}\big)<c_{\mathsf{SPT},1}\exp(-c_{\mathsf{SPT},2}s).

Proof.

See e.g.
[
9
, Theorem 1.4]
.
∎

Proposition
12
immediately has the following corollary, where
\mathsf{dist}(A,B)=\inf\{|x-y|\mid x\in A,y\in B\}
denotes the distance between two Borel sets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Corollary 15

(Disagreement probabilities)
.

Let
A\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded Borel sets,
B\subseteq Q^{c}
and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}
. Then,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}\\
\psi\cap B^{c}=\psi^{\prime}\cap B^{c}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\mathbb{P}\big(T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\cap A\neq T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\cap A\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A\leftrightsquigarrow B)\leqslant c_{\mathsf{SPT},1}\big|B_{r_{0}}(A)\big|\exp(-c_{\mathsf{SPT},2}\mathsf{dist}(A,B)).

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
12
that
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
and
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
agree on
A
when
B_{r_{0}/2}(B)
is not connected to
B_{r_{0}/2}(A)
. Moreover,

(31)

\mathbb{P}({A\leftrightsquigarrow B})\leqslant\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{x\in\mathcal{P}\cap B_{r_{0}}(A)}\mathbbmss{1}_{\{x\leftrightsquigarrow B\}}\Big]\leqslant\int_{B_{r_{0}}(A)}\mathbb{P}(x\leftrightsquigarrow B_{\mathsf{dist}(A,B)}(x)){\rm d}x.

∎

For the rest of this section, we highlight how the relation between Gibbs processes and the associated Boolean model provided by disagreement coupling can be used to establish homogeneity and decorrelation of Gibbs point processes satisfying (
7
).
The homogeneity and decorrelation are captured through the total variation distance and the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient, respectively. Thus, we define the
total variation distance
between two random variables
X,Y
with values in a common measurable space
S
by

(32)

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}(X,Y):=\sup_{f\colon S\to[0,1]}\big|\mathbb{E}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}[f(Y)]\big|,

where the supremum is taken over all measurable functions
f\colon S\to[0,1]
.
Moreover, we define the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient
of two
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
by

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}}\big|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\big|.

Corollary 16

(Exponential decay of the total variation distance)
.

Suppose that the PI satisfies condition (
7
). For any bounded Borel sets
A\subseteq Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B\subseteq Q^{c}
, it holds that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi,\psi^{\prime}\in\mathbf{N}_{Q^{c}}\\
\psi\cap B^{c}=\psi^{\prime}\cap B^{c}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}\big(\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)\cap A,\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi^{\prime})\cap A\big)\leqslant c_{\mathsf{SPT},1}\big|B_{r_{0}}(A)\big|e^{-c_{\mathsf{SPT},2}\mathsf{dist}(A,B)}.

Proof.

We realize
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi)
and
\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi^{\prime})
by disagreement couplings
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
and
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})
, respectively. Since

d_{\mathsf{TV}}\big((\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi))\cap A,\mathcal{X}(Q,\psi^{\prime})\cap A\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\big(T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\cap A\neq T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q,B,\psi^{\prime}}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q})\cap A\big),

the results follow from Corollary
15
.
∎

From Corollary
16
, we can derive useful homogeneity and decorrelation bounds. We note that
[
3
, Theorem 2]
also establishes a decorrelation property via disagreement coupling, where in their case the spatial dependence is measured via the
k
-point correlation functions. However, we need
\alpha
-mixing for our further arguments, which is why we include the short proof of Proposition

below. We also establish a form of local homogeneity in the sense that for large windows, the local distribution in a neighborhood of any space point does not depend too much on the position of the point in the window.

Proposition 17

(Spatial decorrelation and local homogeneity)
.

(i)

Let
s>0
and
A\subseteq Q_{n}
be Borel. Then

\alpha\big(\sigma\big(\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\cap A\big),\sigma\big(\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\setminus B_{s}(A)\big)\big)\leqslant c_{\mathsf{SPT},1}\big|B_{r_{0}}(A)\big|e^{-c_{\mathsf{SPT},2}s},

(ii)

Let the PI be translation-invariant. Then

\sup_{x_{0}\in Q_{n-2\sqrt{n}}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}\big((\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)-x_{0})\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}},\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}}\big)\leqslant 2c_{\mathsf{SPT},1}\exp(-2c_{\mathsf{SPT},2}\sqrt{n}).

Proof.

We start with the proof of (i).
Let
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\cap A
,
\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\setminus B_{s}(A)
and
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{X}(B_{s}(A),\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime})\cap A
. We note that for any measurable sets of configurations
B_{1},B_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in B_{1},\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in B_{1}\,|\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime})\mathbbmss{1}\{\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{2}\}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y}\in B_{1})\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{2})+\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\in B_{1}\,|\,\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime})-\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y}\in B_{1})\big)\mathbbmss{1}\{\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{2}\}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Y}\in B_{1})\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime}\in B_{2})+\sup_{\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q_{n}\setminus B_{s}(A)}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}(\mathcal{X}(B_{s}(A),\mathcal{X}^{\prime\prime})\cap A,\mathcal{X}(B_{s}(A),\psi)\cap A).

Noting that
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
have the same distribution, we conclude the proof of part (i) by applying Corollary
16
with
Q=B_{s}(A)
and
B=Q_{n}\setminus B_{s}(A)
.

For the proof of (ii), note that
Q_{2\sqrt{n}}\subseteq Q_{n}-x_{0}
for every
x_{0}\in Q_{n-2\sqrt{n}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle d_{\mathsf{TV}}\big((\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)-x_{0})\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}},\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}}\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sup_{\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{Q_{2\sqrt{n}}^{c}}}d_{\mathsf{TV}}\big(\mathcal{X}(Q_{2\sqrt{n}},\psi)\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}},\mathcal{X}(Q_{2\sqrt{n}},\varnothing)\cap Q_{\sqrt{n}}\big),

where we used that by stationarity, the distribution of
(\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)-x_{0})\cap Q_{2\sqrt{n}}
is
\mathcal{X}(Q_{2\sqrt{n}},\psi)
, when conditioned on
(\mathcal{X}(Q_{n},\varnothing)-x_{0})\cap Q_{2\sqrt{n}}^{c}=\psi
. Hence, the claim follows from Corollary
16
.
∎

4.3.
Radial coupling for infinite-volume Gibbs processes

So far, we have only considered disagreement coupling for Gibbs processes in bounded domains. In this section, we extend disagreement coupling to the infinite-volume Gibbs process. That is, we show that the radial coupling extends to unbounded domains, constructing the infinite-volume Gibbs process as a thinning of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. This construction yields explicit couplings between Gibbs processes in bounded and unbounded domains via thinning of a common Poisson process allowing us to quantify the disagreement between these processes. This improves on
[
18
]
, where such couplings were only obtained qualitatively via weak convergence. The results allow us to show mixing, and hence ergodicity, of the infinite-volume Gibbs process, which is crucial for the proof of Theorem
3
. For completeness, we also include proof that the infinite-volume Gibbs process is unique, although we remark that this result is not new, having appeared in e.g.
[
8
]
, see also
[
3
]
for a proof using disagreement coupling.

The main result of this section, provided by Propositions
18
and
19
below, establishes that the radial thinning on
Q_{n}
converges almost surely to the infinite-volume Gibbs process when
n\uparrow\infty
.

Proposition 18

(Convergence of radial couplings)
.

Assume that the PI satisfies (
7
). Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\psi\in\mathbf{N}_{U^{c}}
be locally finite. Then, as
n\uparrow\infty
, the embeddings
T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q_{n}\cap U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q_{n}})
converge almost surely to a limiting process on
U

(33)

\displaystyle T^{\infty}_{U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{U}):=\bigcup_{n_{0}\geqslant 1}\bigcap_{n\geqslant n_{0}}T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q_{n}\cap U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q_{n}\cap U})

such that for all bounded Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\geqslant 1
,

\mathbb{P}\big(T^{\infty}_{U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{U})\cap A=T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q_{m}\cap U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q_{m}\cap U})\cap A\text{ for all $m\geqslant n$}\big)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(A\not\leftrightsquigarrow Q_{n-4r_{0}}^{c}).

If
\kappa
is translation-invariant, the process
T^{\infty}(\mathcal{P}^{*}):=T^{\infty}_{\mathbb{R}^{d},\varnothing}(\mathcal{P}^{*})
is a stationary and mixing limiting process. Moreover,
T^{\infty}_{U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{U})+x
has the same distribution as
T^{\infty}_{U+x,\psi+x}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{U+x})
for all

.

We stress that, although

is stationary when

is generally not the same as

.

Proposition 19

(Uniqueness of infinite-volume Gibbs process)
.

Assume that the PI satisfies (
7
). The process

is the distributionally unique infinite-volume Gibbs process with PI
\kappa
.

The rest of this section is devoted to proving Propositions

and
19
. The key ingredient is the following almost-sure consistency property of the disagreement coupling in increasing domains.

Lemma 20

(Consistency of the radial thinning)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be bounded,

\{A\not\leftrightsquigarrow Q_{n-4r_{0}}^{c}\}

(34)

T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q_{n}\cap U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q_{n}})\cap A=T^{\mathsf{rad}}_{Q_{m}\cap U,\psi}(\mathcal{P}^{*}_{Q_{m}})\cap A.

Proof.

Let

.
Similarly, the disagreement coupling algorithm on

Proof of Proposition
18
.

\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{X}+x)]=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{X})]

We now show the uniqueness result asserted in Proposition

Remark 21

While the order on

Proposition 23

(DLR-equations in unbounded domains)
.

Let

be Borel. Let

Let

Lemma 28

5.
Proof of Theorem
3
– CLT under weak stabilization

In this section, we prove Theorem
3
, i.e., the CLT for Gibbsian functionals under weak stabilization. The main idea is to extend the martingale approach that was developed for Poisson point processes in
[
24
, Theorem 3.1]
. A martingale is obtained by conditioning on the point process in an increasing sequence of windows. The centered functional can then be expressed as a sum of martingale differences. Thus, general CLT results apply, if these differences can be controlled. Since the martingale differences are essentially obtained by locally changing the boundary conditions, they can be handled using the disagreement coupling results from Section
4
.

The rest of the section is organized as follows. First, in Section
5.1
, we establish a CLT under a general moment and stabilization condition. Second, in Section
5.2
, we prove the main CLT for Gibbs processes under general conditions. In Section
5.2.1
, we discuss when these conditions are satisfied. Finally, in Section
5.2.2
, we demonstrate how to show variance positivity in the setting of Betti numbers, thereby completing the proof of Corollary
6
.

5.1.
CLT under general conditions

In the following, we let
(a_{n})
be a sequence such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\infty
. Let
\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}
,
n\geq 1
, denote a sequence of point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Later in this section, we always consider the specific choices
a_{n}=n+\sqrt{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}=\mathcal{X}
. However, since it causes no additional work, we formulate the proofs in the general setting. For instance, in other applications, one might want to take
\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}=\mathcal{X}(Q_{a_{n}},\psi_{n})
. For
n\geqslant 1
, we consider functionals
H_{n}(\varphi)
defined for any
\varphi\in\mathbf{N}
. In the present subsection, we do not require translation-invariance of
H_{n}
and allow
H_{n}
to depend on
n
.

Let
Z_{a_{n}}
be the set of lattice points
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
Q_{z,1}
intersects the window
Q_{a_{n}}
. Then,
Q_{a_{n}}\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in Z_{a_{n}}}Q_{z,1}
. Order
Z_{a_{n}}
lexicographically as
z_{1},\dots,z_{k_{n}}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{0,n}
be the trivial
\sigma
-algebra and
\mathcal{F}_{k_{n},n}=\sigma(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})
. Moreover, define

\mathcal{F}_{i,n}:=\sigma\bigg(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}\cap\bigcup_{{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},z\preceq z_{i}}}Q_{z,1}\bigg)

for
0<i<k_{n}
, where
\preceq
denotes lexicographical ordering on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The key observation in the martingale approach is that

H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})-\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})]=\sum_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\Delta_{i,n},

where

\Delta_{i,n}:=\Delta_{z_{i},n}:=\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})|\mathcal{F}_{i,n}]-\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})|\mathcal{F}_{i-1,n}].

For each
n
, the
\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})|\mathcal{F}_{i,n}]
define a martingale with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{i,n}
. By orthogonality of martingale differences, the variance is given by

(49)

\mathsf{Var}(H(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})-\mathbb{E}[H(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})])=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i,n}^{2}].

We impose the following two conditions:

(i)

\sup_{n\geqslant 1,z\in Z_{a_{n}}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{z,n}^{4}]<\infty.

(ii)

There exists a stationary ergodic point process
\mathcal{Y}
such that for every
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, there is a random variable
\Delta_{z}=\Delta_{z}(\mathcal{Y})
such that
{\Delta}_{z,n}\to\Delta_{z}
in probability (and hence in
L^{2}
due to (i)). The limit is shift invariant meaning that for any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
\Delta_{z+z_{0}}(\mathcal{Y}+z_{0})=\Delta_{z}(\mathcal{Y})
. The convergence must be uniform in the sense that

(50)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{z\in Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\lVert\Delta_{z}-\Delta_{z,n}\rVert_{L^{2}}=0,

where
\rho:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a function with
\rho(x)\in o(x)
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\rho(x)=\infty
.

The proposition below and its proof is an adaption of
[
24
, Thm. 3.1]
to general point processes.

Proposition 29

(CLT for general functionals)
.

Let
(a_{n})
be a sequence with
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\infty
and
\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}
a sequence of point processes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Under Conditions (i) and (ii),

(51)

a_{n}^{-d}\mathsf{Var}(H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}}))\to\sigma^{2}\geqslant 0,

where
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{0}^{2}]
, and

a_{n}^{-d/2}(H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})-\mathbb{E}[H_{n}(\mathcal{Y}_{a_{n}})])\to N(0,\sigma^{2})

in distribution.

Proof.

Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}^{d}/k_{n}=1
, Proposition
29
follows from the martingale CLT of
[
22
]
if the following three conditions are satisfied:

1.

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}k_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\Delta_{i,n}^{2}\big]<\infty
;

2.

k_{n}^{-1/2}\max_{i\leqslant k_{n}}|\Delta_{i,n}|\to 0
in probability; and

3.

k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\Delta_{i,n}^{2}\to\sigma^{2}
in
L^{1}
.

In particular, (
51
) follows from (
49
) and 3. The first two conditions follow from Condition (i) exactly as in
[
24
, proof of Thm. 3.1]
. To make our presentation self-contained, we briefly reproduce the argument.

1.

Condition (i) implies

\sup_{n\geqslant 1}k_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{i}\Delta_{i,n}^{2}\big]\leqslant\sup_{n}k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i,n}^{2}]<\infty.

2.

This follows from Markov’s inequality and (i) because

\mathbb{P}\big(k_{n}^{-1/2}\max_{i\leqslant k_{n}}|\Delta_{i,n}|\geqslant\varepsilon\big)\leqslant\sum_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\mathbb{P}\big(k_{n}^{-1/2}|\Delta_{i,n}|\geqslant\varepsilon\big)\leqslant k_{n}\frac{\max_{i\leqslant k_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{i,n}^{4}]}{k_{n}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}},

which tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
.

3.

We start by noting that
\Delta_{z}
is in
L^{2}
, since
\Delta_{z,n}
converges to
\Delta_{z}
in probability, and (i) implies uniform integrability.

By Assumption (ii),
{\Delta_{z}(\mathcal{Y})}_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
forms a multidimensional ergodic sequence, meaning that the sum

|Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}|^{-1}\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\Delta_{z}^{2}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}\sigma^{2},

see
[
14
, Thm. 10.12]
.
Moreover, since
|Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}|=a_{n}^{d}+o(a_{n}^{d})=k_{n}+o(k_{n})
and
\Delta_{z}\in L^{2}
,

(k_{n}^{-1}-|Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}|^{-1})\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\Delta_{z}^{2}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}0,\quad\text{ and }\quad k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}}\setminus Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\Delta_{z}^{2}\xrightarrow{L^{1}}0.

It remains to show that

k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}}}|\Delta_{z}^{2}-\Delta_{z,n}^{2}|\xrightarrow{L^{1}}0.

For this, note that

\mathbb{E}\big[|\Delta_{z}^{2}-\Delta_{z,n}^{2}|\big]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta_{z}+\Delta_{z,n})^{2}\big]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta_{z}-\Delta_{z,n})^{2}\big]^{1/2}.

The first term is uniformly bounded by (i) and the fact that
\Delta_{z}\in L^{2}
.
From Assumption (ii) we have that

k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta_{z}-\Delta_{z,n})^{2}\big]\to 0

as
n\to 0
. Finally,

k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{z\in Z_{a_{n}}\setminus Z_{a_{n}-\rho(a_{n})}}\mathbb{E}\big[(\Delta_{z}-\Delta_{z,n})^{2}\big]\to 0,

again by (i) and the fact that
\Delta_{z}\in L^{2}
. This shows 3.

∎

There is a substantial literature available

\Psi^{*}

[

. Whenever we speak of the class

The key achievement of
[

[

are EDD. Although not stated explicitly, this means that the condition
[

In Corollary

[
7

also apply in this case. To make this precise, for disjoint bounded Borel sets

\mathcal{X}_{B}^{\prime}
is an independent copy of
\mathcal{X}_{B}
. Then, the EDD says that there exists a constant
\theta_{0}>0
with the following property. Whenever,
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
are disjoint bounded Borel sets with
\mathsf{dist}(A,B)\geqslant\theta_{0}\log(\mathsf{diam}(A)\vee\mathsf{diam}(B)\vee\theta_{0})

\beta_{A,B}(\mathcal{X})\leqslant\theta_{0}(\mathsf{diam}(A)\vee\mathsf{diam}(B)\vee 1)^{\theta_{0}}e^{-\mathsf{dist}(A,B)/\theta_{0}}.

(Assumption (

Let
\kappa

Let
\mathcal{X}^{1}

\mathcal{P}^{*}
is independent of
\mathcal{X}^{1}
and
\mathcal{X}^{2}

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{X}

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}^{2}

As mentioned above, for finite-range interactions, the class of Gibbs processes satisfying Assumption (

) is larger than
\Psi^{*}
. Conversely, our paper requires the interaction range to be finite, whereas
\Psi^{*}
also allows some infinite-range Gibbs processes. Currently, the exploration algorithm in the proof of Theorem

Therefore, while an extension of our arguments to infinite ranges does not seem to be completely inaccessible, it would make the arguments dramatically more complicated so that we leave it for future work. Taking this into account, it is interesting to discuss to what extent our techniques can be extended to infinite-range Gibbs processes in

Loosely speaking, the idea of the perfect simulation algorithm is that to construct a Gibbs point process in a Borel set

for details. However, we shall only use the following property: We denote by

Equipped with this information, we have the following version of Theorem

.
