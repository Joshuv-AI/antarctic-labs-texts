The process of fluctuations of the giant component of an Erdős-Rényi graph
Nathanaël Enriquez, Gabriel Faraud, Sophie Lemaire

Abstract
We present a detailed study of the evolution of the giant component of the Erdős-Rényi graph process as the mean degree increases from 1 to infinity. It leads to the identification of the limiting process of the rescaled fluctuations of its order around its deterministic asymptotic. This process is Gaussian with an explicit covariance.



Keywords.
random graph process, Erdős-Rényi random graph, invariance principle.

Primary 05C80. Secondary 60F17.

Introduction

The Erdős-Rényi graph
\mathrm{ER}(n,p_{n})
is, without any contest, the most popular random graph. The model goes back to Gilbert in
[
6
]
and was first studied by Erdős and Rényi, who presented the main features of a slight variation of this model in a seminal paper
[
5
]
. For an integer
n\geq 1
and
p_{n}\in(0,1)
, it is defined as a graph with
n
vertices, where any pair of vertices are connected by an edge independently with probability
p_{n}
. The most striking phenomenon occurs at the scale
p_{n}=\frac{c}{n}
where this model exhibits a phase transition at
c=1
.
Namely, with high probability, for
c<1
, all connected components of the graph are of order
O(\ln(n))
, whereas for
c>1
, a single component is of order of magnitude
n
and all the others are of order
O(\ln(n))
. More precisely, the largest component, commonly called the ‘
giant component
’ is asymptotically of order
\rho(c)n
where
\rho(c)
denotes the probability that a Galton-Watson tree with Poisson reproduction distribution of parameter
c
is infinite. The constant
\rho(c)
is the only solution in
(0,1)
of the equation

1-x=e^{-cx}.

(1)

Few years later, the question of the fluctuations of this giant component in the case
c>1
was raised. Different approaches have been proposed to prove that the fluctuation of the order (that is, the number of vertices) of the giant component around
\rho(c)n
is asymptotically Gaussian with variance
\dfrac{\rho(c)(1-\rho(c))}{(1-c(1-\rho(c)))^{2}}n
. The first proof was written by Stepanov
[
15
]
, who adopted a combinatorial approach and sketched the proof of a local limit theorem. Later, Pittel
[
12
]
obtained the asymptotic normality of the giant component from the asymptotic normality of the number of components of any given tree-like shape.
Barraez, Boucheron and de la Vega
[
1
]
further established an upper bound for the rate of convergence and a bivariate result for both the order of the giant component and the number of edges by analysing an exploration process of the graph. Independently, Puhalskii in
[
14
]
also used an exploration process to study the joint distribution of three variables: the giant component, the number of excess edges and the number of connected components. The same year, Pittel and Wormald in
[
13
]
extended the study of the fluctuations of the giant component for
c=1+\varepsilon_{n}
with
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
\varepsilon_{n}n^{1/3}\rightarrow+\infty
. Using combinatorial methods, they showed a trivariate result on the fluctuation of the 2-core that implies that the asymptotic fluctuation of the giant component is Gaussian with variance
\frac{2n}{\varepsilon_{n}}
. Later, this last result was directly proven by Bollobas and Riordan in
[
4
]
with the help of an exploration process.

All the previously mentioned articles focus on the study of the Erdős-Rényi graph for a
fixed parameter

c
. There is however a very natural construction that makes it possible to couple the graphs
\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{c}{n})
for different values of
c
and obtain a graph-valued process. Namely, we can attach to every pair
(i,j)
of vertices a weight given by independent uniform random variables
w_{i,j}
. We consider the graph
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
whose edges are the pairs of vertices with weight smaller than
\frac{t}{n}
. With this construction, for any
t
,
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
follows the distribution of an
\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{t}{n})
graph. There are remarkably few results concerning the dynamics of the features of the graph
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
, seen as a stochastic process. An important reference on these questions are the works by Janson
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. In
[
7
,
8
]
, Janson develops a sophisticated general method based on chaos decomposition of martingales to get functional limit theorems for random graph statistics. In
[
9
]
, he uses this method to establish that the infinite family of processes, defined by the number of isolated trees of any given order, has Gaussian fluctuations with explicit asymptotic covariances. He conjectures (
[
9
]
, Remark 1.3) that this approach could be used to show that the fluctuations of the order of the giant component converge to a Gaussian process. We are going to prove this result and identify the limiting process. Our approach however does not rely on the techniques proposed by Janson; it relies on a careful analysis of the infinitesimal increment of the giant component and leads to a stochastic differential equation that we can explicitly solve.

In order to state the precise result, let us introduce some notation.
Throughout the article we let
\mathscr{C}_{n,x}(t)
define the connected component of the vertex
x
in
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
and
|\mathscr{C}_{n,x}(t)|
its order, that is its number of vertices. We set
L_{n}(t)=\max(|\mathscr{C}_{n,x}(t)|,\,x\in\llbracket{n}\rrbracket)
. Among components of order
L_{n}(t)
, we choose one with a fixed rule (for instance the component of the first vertex having
L_{n}(t)
vertices) and denote it
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
.

We deal with the fluctuation process of
L_{n}(t)
, namely
X_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(L_{n}(t)-n\rho(t))
where the function
\rho
is defined by (
1
). As proven in the above references, it converges in distribution for a
fixed

t>1
, to a Gaussian distribution with variance

\sigma^{2}(t)=\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{(1-t(1-\rho(t)))^{2}}.

(2)

Our main result identifies the limit of
X_{n}
as a
process
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{B(t),\,t\geq 0\}
be a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
.
Set for all
t>1
,

u(t)=\frac{1}{1-\rho(t)}-t\text{ and }v(t)=\frac{1}{1-\rho(t)}-1.

Let
t_{0},t_{1}
be two real numbers such that
1<t_{0}<t_{1}
.

The process
\{X_{n}(t),\;t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\}
converges weakly, with respect to the Skorohod topology, to the Gaussian process
\{X(t),\,t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\}
defined by:

\forall t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\quad X(t)=\frac{1}{u(t)}B(v(t)).

(3)

Note that the limiting process is Gaussian and does not have independent increments. Its variance at time
t
is
\sigma^{2}(t)
; it explodes at
1^{+}
and is bounded away from 0 and
+\infty
on the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

A heuristic of how the giant component evolves during an infinitesimal time interval is displayed in Section
2
in order to identify the limiting process
X
. The first tool to prove Theorem
1.1
is a representation of the variation of the order of the giant component during a small time interval detailed in Section
3
. We show in Section
4
the finite-dimensional convergence of the sequence
((uX_{n})\circ v^{(-1)})_{n}
towards a standard Brownian motion, based on an extension of the three-moments method of Norman
[
10
]
.
Finally, we prove its tightness in Section
5
, which finishes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2
Heuristic

We propose a heuristic to identify the limiting process of
X_{n}
as a solution of a stochastic differential equation (SDE). Our main idea is to understand precisely what happens to the largest component during an infinitesimal step
h
. Between time
t
and
t+h
a random number
N_{n}(t)
of edges is added to the graph. This number is distributed as a
\mathrm{Bin}(\frac{1}{2}n(n-1),\frac{h}{n})
random variable.

Each edge contributes to increasing the order of the giant component between
t
and
t+h
if it connects one vertex of the largest component to a vertex outside of it. This event has probability

\zeta_{n}(t):=\frac{2L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t))}{n(n-1)}=2\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))+2(1-2\rho(t))\frac{X_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}+O(\frac{X_{n}(t)^{2}}{n}).

Whenever this event is fulfilled, the order of the giant component is increased by the order of the connected component of the ‘new’ vertex. It is known that the order of such a component is approximately distributed as the total progeny of a Bienaymé-Galton-Watson (BGW for short) tree with
\mathrm{Bin}(n-L_{n}(t),\frac{t}{n})
reproduction distribution. We denote by
\mu_{n}(t)
this distribution. The limiting expectation of
\mu_{n}(t)
is

\lambda(t):=t(1-\rho(t)).

(4)

Let us note that
\lambda(t)
verifies
-\lambda(t)e^{-\lambda(t)}=-te^{-t}
and thus
-\lambda(t)=W_{0}(-te^{-t})
where
W_{0}
is the principal branch of the Lambert W-function. In particular,
\lambda(1)=1
, the function
\lambda
is smaller than 1 and bounded away from 1 on the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Summing over all the added edges between
t
and
t+h
we have the following representation:

L_{n}(t+h)-L_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n}(t)}\varepsilon_{i}T_{i},

(5)

where
\varepsilon_{i}
are Bernoulli
(\zeta_{n}(t))
random variables that are equal to 1 when the associated edge connects the giant component to a vertex outside of it, and the random variables
T_{i}
are
\mu_{n}(t)
distributed and represent the order of components outside of
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
.

The expectation of (
5
) yields

\mathbb{E}(N_{n})\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}(T)=\frac{nh}{2}\left(\frac{2\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{1-\lambda(t)}+2\left(\frac{1-2\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}-\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))t}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}\right)\frac{X_{n}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\\
+O\left(ht^{2}X_{n}(t)^{2}\right).

Note that the first order term
nh\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{1-\lambda(t)}
coincides with
nh\rho^{\prime}(t)
and therefore will compensate the increment of
n\rho(t)
in the variation of
L_{n}(t)-n\rho(t)=\sqrt{n}X_{n}(t)
. The second term
\sqrt{n}h\left(\frac{1-2\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}-\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))t}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}\right){X_{n}(t)}
will give the drift term in the limiting stochastic differential equation.

We are now interested in the fluctuations in (
5
). They come from the three sources of randomness
N_{n}(t),\varepsilon_{i},T_{i}
which will turn out to be asymptotically independent. Informally, we can describe these fluctuations with the help of three standard Brownian motions
C^{(N)}
,
C^{(T)}
and
C^{(\varepsilon)}
:

•

fluctuations of
N_{n}(t)
:
\sqrt{n/2}\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon T)dC^{(N)}(t)=\sqrt{2n}\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{1-\lambda(t)}dC^{(N)}(t)
,

•

fluctuations due to
\varepsilon_{i}
:

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}(N_{n}(t))\mathbb{E}(T)^{2}}dC^{(\varepsilon)}(t)=\sqrt{n\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))\frac{1-2\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}}dC^{(\varepsilon)}(t),

•

fluctuations due to
T_{i}
:

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(N_{n}(t)\varepsilon)\mathrm{Var}(T)}dC^{(T)}(t)=\sqrt{n\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))\frac{\lambda(t)}{(1-\lambda(t))^{3}}}dC^{(T)}(t).

Therefore, the limiting equation for
(X_{n}(t))_{t}
is:

\displaystyle dX(t)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-2\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}-\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))t}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}\right)X(t)dt+\sqrt{2}\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{1-\lambda(t)}dC^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))\frac{1-2\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}}dC^{(\varepsilon)}(t)+\sqrt{\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))\lambda(t)}{(1-\lambda(t))^{3}}}dC^{(T)}(t).

Assuming the independence of the three above Brownian motions, we can regroup them under a single Brownian motion
C
and get

dX(t)=\left(\frac{1-2\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}-\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))t}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}\right)X(t)dt+\sqrt{\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{(1-\lambda(t))^{3}}}dC(t).

(6)

Solving this equation gives

X(t)=\frac{1-\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}B\left(\frac{\rho(t)}{1-\rho(t)}\right),

(7)

where
B
is a standard Brownian motion.

A representation of the infinitesimal evolution of the giant component

Let
h>0
. The goal of this section is to give a representation of the variation of
L_{n}(t)
between
t
and
t+h
, in order to treat this variation as a sum of i.i.d. random variables, up to a tractable error.

First note that, conditional on the situation at time
t
,
L_{n}(t+h)
only depends on the edges added between
t
and
t+h
, and not on the order in which we add them. Therefore, we can add them in a strategic order. Call
\mathscr{G}_{n}^{e}(t)
the subgraph induced in
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
by vertices outside of
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
.
We partition the edges added between
t
and
t+h
in three categories:

•

the edges which connect vertices of
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
, they do not affect
L_{n}(t)
and can be ignored;

•

the edges that connect vertices of
\mathscr{G}_{n}^{e}(t)
;

•

the edges that connect
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
to
\mathscr{G}_{n}^{e}(t)
.

Note that there is not a complete independence between the connections in
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
and
\mathscr{G}_{n}^{e}(t)
, since the orders of the connected components of
\mathscr{G}_{n}^{e}(t)
have to be smaller than
|\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)|
. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce two graphs, denoted
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
and
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t,h)
, for which the pair of vertices outside the giant component are connected independently with probability respectively
\frac{t}{n}
and
\frac{t+h}{n}
.

First, we construct
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
on
\llbracket{n}\rrbracket
, having
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
as a connected component in the following way:

•

every edge between vertices inside
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)

is present in
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
if and only if it is present in
\mathscr{G}_{n}(t)
;

•

\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
has no edge between a vertex in
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
and another outside;

•

an edge between two vertices outside
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
, is added with probability
\frac{t}{n}
independently from every other edge. We call them
external
edges.

Secondly, the graph
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t,h)
is defined from
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
by adding independently with probability
\frac{\frac{h}{n}}{1-\frac{t}{n}}=\frac{h}{n-t}
all the edges that are not in
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t)
. The steps of the construction of

from

.
Let us call a

\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t,h)
between a vertex in
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
and another one outside of
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
. Let
Y_{n}
denote their number. Let
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}^{e}(t,h)
be the subgraph induced in
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t,h)
by vertices which are not in
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)
. It is obvious from the construction that
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}^{e}(t,h)
is distributed as an
\mathrm{ER}(n-L_{n}(t),\frac{t+h}{n})

Y_{n}
is a
\mathrm{Bin}(L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t)),\frac{h}{n-t})

Let
L_{n}(t,h)
be the order of the component of
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(t,h)
containing
\mathscr{C}^{max}_{n}(t)

\Delta L_{n}(t):=L_{n}(t+h)-L_{n}(t)

We state it in our framework:

)
.

There is a number

C_{1}

To study
\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)

\mathcal{R}:=\{2\leq j\leq Y_{n}\text{ s.t. }C_{x_{j}}=C_{x_{i}}\text{ for some }i<j\}

(for the graph depicted in Figure

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathrm{BGW}(n,p)
denote a Bienaymé-Galton-Watson process with

\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\\

There exists a sequence
(M_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
of i.i.d. random variables independent of

such that

(a)

\forall 1\leq j\leq Y_{n},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{j}|C_{x_{i}}^{\star}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}M_{i}

Moreover,

(a)

given

is
\mathrm{Bin}(L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t)),\frac{h}{n-t})

.

•

4
Convergence of the finite dimensional distributions

The aim of this section is to prove that
X_{n}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(L_{n}(t)-n\rho(t))
converges in the sense of finite dimensional distributions towards

X(t)=\frac{1-\rho(t)}{1-t(1-\rho(t))}B\left(\frac{\rho(t)}{1-\rho(t)}\right).

As already mentioned (see (
6
) in Section
2
), this process is actually a diffusion with drift
a(t,x):=\left(\frac{1-2\rho(t)}{1-\lambda(t)}-\frac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))t}{(1-\lambda(t))^{2}}\right)x

and diffusion coefficient
b(t,x):=\frac{{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}}{(1-t(1-\rho(t)))^{3}}
.

To show this convergence, we are going to adapt a theorem by Norman
[
10
]
to a non-compact state space. In the case of a sequence of compact-valued processes, its statement makes it possible to reduce the proof of the finite dimensional convergence of a sequence of random processes, to the study of the first three moments of their infinitesimal increments, in the case where the limiting process is a diffusion. The extension of this statement we shall apply is:

Proposition 4.1
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
\{Z_{n}(t),t\geq 0\}
be a stochastic process in
\mathbb{R}
adapted to a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{n}(t),\ t\geq 0\}
. Assume there exist two
C^{3}
functions
\alpha
and
\beta
, three sequences of nonnegative functions
(e_{1,n})_{n}
,
(e_{2,n})_{n}
,
(e_{3,n})_{n}
and a sequence
(N_{n})_{n}
of positive integers converging to
+\infty
, satisfying, for every
t\geq 0
and every
h>0
:

\displaystyle\mid\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}(t+h)-Z_{n}(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(t))-h\alpha(Z_{n}(t))\mid\leq e_{1,n}(t,h,Z_{n}(t))

\displaystyle\mid\mathbb{E}((Z_{n}(t+h)-Z_{n}(t))^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(t))-h\beta(Z_{n}(t))\mid\leq e_{2,n}(t,h,Z_{n}(t))

(Assumption A1
)

\displaystyle\mid\mathbb{E}(|Z_{n}(t+h)-Z_{n}(t)|^{3}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(t))\mid\leq e_{3,n}(t,h,Z_{n}(t))

\displaystyle\forall 0<s<t,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left(e_{i,n}\left(s+k\frac{t-s}{N_{n}},\frac{t-s}{N_{n}},Z_{n}(s+k\frac{t-s}{N_{n}})\right)\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

Let
(Z(t))_{t\geq 0}
be the diffusion process with drift function
\alpha
and diffusion coefficient
\beta
; denote its semigroup by
\{T_{t},t\geq 0\}
. Define the three functions

by, for every

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z(t+h)-Z(t)\mid Z(t))=h\alpha(Z(t))+\varepsilon_{1}(h,Z(t))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((Z(t+h)-Z(t))^{2}\mid Z(t))=h\beta(Z(t))+\varepsilon_{2}(h,Z(t))

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|Z(t+h)-Z(t)|^{3}\mid Z(t))=\varepsilon_{3}(h,Z(t)).

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the set of bounded
C^{3}
functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
whose derivatives are also bounded.
Assume the following properties:

(Assumption A2
)

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{H},\,T_{t}f\in\mathcal{H}\text{ and }\forall\tau>0\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\sum_{k=0}^{3}||(T_{t}f)^{(k)}||_{\infty}<+\infty

\displaystyle\forall 0<s<t,\,\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\varepsilon_{i}\left(\frac{t-s}{N_{n}},Z_{n}(s+k\frac{t-s}{N_{n}})\right)\right|\right)\underset{n\rightarrow+\infty}{\rightarrow}0

(Assumption A4)

\displaystyle(Z_{n}(0))_{n}\text{ converges in distribution to }Z(0).

Then for every
k\geq 1
and for every
0<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}
,
(Z_{n}(s_{1}),\ldots,Z_{n}(s_{k}))_{n}
converges in distribution to
(Z(s_{1}),\ldots,Z(s_{k}))
.

The proof of this Proposition is postponed to Appendix
B

(18)

with
r_{1,n}(h,X_{n}(t))\leq C\left(\ln^{4}(n)(\sqrt{n}h^{2}+\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}})+\frac{X^{2}_{n}(t)h}{\sqrt{n}}+n^{-7.5}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)}+\sqrt{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}(t)}
.
∎

Second moment.

We now turn to the estimation of
\mathbb{E}_{t}(\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}).
We are going to prove

Lemma 4.3
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
t>1
,
h>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, set

\displaystyle b(t)=\frac{{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}}{(1-t(1-\rho(t)))^{3}}\text{ and }r_{2,n}(h,x)=\left(hn^{-0.3}+n^{-7.5}\right)\mathds{1}_{\{|x|\leq n^{0.2}\}}+n\mathds{1}_{\{|x|>n^{0.2}\}}.

There exists a constant
C>0
, such that for
n>t_{1}
,
h\leq n^{-1}
and
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]

\left|\mathbb{E}_{t}[(\Delta X_{n}(t))^{2}]-hb(t)\right|\leq Cr_{2,n}(h,X_{n}(t)).

(19)

Proof.

By definition,
n\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]
is equal to

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta L^{2}_{n}(t)]+n^{2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}-2n\Delta L_{n}(t)(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))]\\
=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta L^{2}_{n}(t)]-n^{2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}-2n^{3/2}\left(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t)\right)\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)].

As before we make use of Lemma
3.1
to compute
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta L^{2}_{n}(t)]
:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta L^{2}_{n}(t)]=\mathbb{E}_{t}[\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}^{2}(t)]+O(n^{-7}).

From now on, the computations will be performed only on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)
.
Using Lemma
3.2
, the term
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}^{2}(t)]
is bounded from above by the second moment of the sum of
Y_{n}
i.i.d. copies of the total progeny of a
\mathrm{BGW}(n-L_{n}(t),\frac{t+h}{n})
. Hence, denoting by
v_{n}^{+}
the variance of the total progeny of a
\mathrm{BGW}(n-L_{n}(t),\frac{t+h}{n})
, classical variance computations give,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[(\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t))^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}_{t}[{Y_{n}}]m_{n,1}^{+})^{2}+\mathrm{Var}(Y_{n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})(m_{n,1}^{+})^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y_{n}]v_{n}^{+}.

In the right-hand side of (
20
), the second and third terms will give the main contribution and yield the diffusion term
nhb(t,X_{t})
. Since the expectation and variance of the total progeny of a subcritical
\mathrm{BGW}(n,p)
process are equal to
\frac{1}{1-np}
and
\frac{np(1-p)}{(1-np)^{3}}
respectively, we can write using the function
f_{3}(t,x)=\frac{x(1-x)}{(1-t(1-x))^{3}}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Y_{n}

\displaystyle\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})(m_{n,1}^{+})^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y_{n}]v_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t))\frac{h}{n-t}\left(1-\frac{h}{n-t}\right)\frac{1}{\left(1-(n-L_{n}(t))\frac{t+h}{n}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle+L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t))\frac{h}{n-t}\frac{(n-L_{n}(t))\frac{t+h}{n}(1-\frac{t+h}{n})}{\left(1-(n-L_{n}(t))\frac{t+h}{n}\right)^{3}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nhf_{3}\left(t+h,\frac{L_{n}(t)}{n}\right)\left(1-\frac{h}{n-t}-\frac{t}{n}(1-\frac{h}{n-t})(1-\frac{L_{n}(t)}{n})(t+h)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nh\left(f_{3}(t,\rho(t))+O\left(\frac{|X_{n}(t)|}{\sqrt{n}}\left|\frac{\partial f_{3}}{\partial x}(t,\rho(t))\right|+h\left|\frac{\partial f_{3}}{\partial t}(t,\rho(t))\right|\right)\right)\left(1+O(\frac{1}{n})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nh\left(f_{3}(t,\rho(t))+O\left(\frac{|X_{n}(t)|}{\sqrt{n}}+h+\frac{1}{n}\right)\right).

(21)

The other contributions in the estimation of the upper bound of
n\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]
are the three following terms

(\mathbb{E}_{t}[{Y_{n}}]m_{n,1}^{+})^{2}-n^{2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}-2n^{3/2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)].

Since
\mathbb{E}_{t}[{Y_{n}}]m_{n,1}^{+}=nhf_{1}(t+h,\frac{L_{n}(t)}{n})(1+O(\frac{1}{n}))
and
\rho^{\prime}(t)=f_{1}(t,\rho(t))
, the zero orders of the first two terms cancel:

(\mathbb{E}_{t}[{Y_{n}}]m_{n,1}^{+})^{2}-n^{2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}=n^{2}h^{2}O\left(h+\frac{1}{n}+\frac{|X_{n}(t)|}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(22)

Therefore, Lemma
4.2
, giving the expression of
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)]
together with (
4.1
) and (
22
) yield the following upper bound for
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle hf_{3}(t,\rho(t))+C\left((h^{2}+nh^{3})\ln^{4}(n)+\frac{h}{n}+n^{-8}\right)

\displaystyle+C\left(\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{n}}+\sqrt{n}h^{2}\right)|X_{n}(t)|+h^{2}|X_{n}(t)|^{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)}+n\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}(t)}.

Since we based the upper bound of
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]
on the inequality

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}\right],

we just have to find an upper bound of

\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}-(\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t))^{2}\right]

in order to get a lower bound of
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2}]
.
We proceed as in (
14
) with a slight modification. Introducing the event

\mathcal{D}_{n}(t):=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}>n^{\theta}\right\}

for some
0<\theta<1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}-n^{2\theta}\right)\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{D}_{n}(t)}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)^{2}\right].

Using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the fact that
Y_{n}
is bounded by
n^{2}
, the first term is smaller than
Cn^{4}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

When
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathcal{D}_{n}(t))\leq\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}^{c}(t))+\mathbb{P}_{t}(Y_{n}\geq n^{\theta/2})\leq Cn^{-20}
. Indeed, by Lemma
A.2
,
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{n}^{c}(t))=O(n^{-20})
. Moreover, since the conditional distribution of
Y_{n}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t}
follows the binomial
\mathrm{Bin}(L_{n}(t)(n-L_{n}(t)),\frac{h}{n-t})
distribution, it is stochastically dominated by the binomial
\mathrm{Bin}(\frac{n^{2}}{4},\frac{h}{n-t_{1}})
distribution. Therefore, if
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,
\mathbb{P}_{t}(Y_{n}\geq n^{\theta/2})=O(n^{-20})
by application of the exponential Markov’s inequality.

To bound from above the second term we note that,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)^{2}-(\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)^{2}-(\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t))^{2}\right)\mathds{1}_{\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)<n^{\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)+\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)\right)\left(\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)\right)\mathds{1}_{\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)<n^{\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{\theta}\left(\min\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i},n^{\theta}\right)-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)\right)\mathds{1}_{\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)<n^{\theta}}

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)\right).

Using equation (
17
) to bound

\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)\right]
,
we obtain that if
h\leq 1/n
,

\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{Y_{n}}M_{i}\right)^{2}-\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)^{2}\right]\leq C(n^{\theta}\ln^{4}(n)h+n^{-7+\theta}).

Since
b(t)=f_{3}(t,\rho(t))=\dfrac{\rho(t)(1-\rho(t))}{(1-t(1-\rho(t)))^{3}}
, gathering all the above estimations we obtain for
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{t}(\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2})-hb(t)\right|\leq C\left(\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{n}}h+|x|^{2}h^{2}+n^{\theta-1}\ln^{4}(n)h+n^{-8+\theta}\right)\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq n^{0.2}}+n\mathds{1}_{|x|>n^{0.2}}

Taking
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
yields for
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,

\left|\mathbb{E}_{t}(\Delta X_{n}(t)^{2})-hb(t)\right|\leq Cr_{2,n}(h,X_{n}(t))

with

r_{2,n}(h,x)=(n^{-0.3}h+n^{-7.5})\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq n^{0.2}}+n\mathds{1}_{|x|>n^{0.2}}
.

∎

Third moment.

For the third moment, we only need to obtain an upper bound on

\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\Delta X_{n}(t)|^{3}]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
, such that for every integer
n>t_{1}
, reals
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
0<h\leq n^{-1}
,

\mathbb{E}_{t}(|\Delta X_{n}(t)|^{3})\leq Cr_{3,n}(h,X_{n}(t))

(23)

where

\displaystyle r_{3,n}(h,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle hn^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\ln n)^{6}+n^{-7.5}+n^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\{|x|>n^{0.2}\}}.

Proof.

As before, we first restrict the study to the event
\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)
. On this event,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\Delta X_{n}(t)|^{3}]=\mathbb{E}_{t}

\displaystyle\left[\mid n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\Delta L_{n}(t)^{3}-3n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta L_{n}(t)^{2}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))\right.

\displaystyle\left.+3n^{\frac{1}{2}}\Delta L_{n}(t)(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}-n^{\frac{3}{2}}(\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{3}\mid\right].

Using again Lemma
3.1
, and using the fact that on
\mathcal{A}_{n}(t)
,
|\tilde{\Delta}L_{n}(t)|\leq(\ln n)^{2}Y_{n},
we have on
\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\Delta X_{n}(t)|^{3}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{3}{2}}\ln(n)^{6}\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y^{3}_{n}]+3n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ln(n)^{4}\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y^{2}_{n}]|\rho(t+h)-\rho(t)|

\displaystyle+3n^{\frac{1}{2}}\ln(n)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y_{n}](\rho(t+h)-\rho(t))^{2}+n^{\frac{3}{2}}|\rho(t+h)-\rho(t)|^{3}

\displaystyle+n^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathbb{P}_{t}(\mathcal{A}_{n}^{c}(t))(1+O(h))+O(n^{-7.5}).

As the conditional distribution of
Y_{n}
is stochastically dominated by
\mathrm{Bin}(\frac{n^{2}}{4},\frac{h}{n-t_{1}})
, there exists a constant
C
such that for
t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}
and
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,
\mathbb{E}_{t}[Y^{k}_{n}]\leq Cnh
for
k\in\{1,2,3\}
. Therefore, there exists a constant
C
such that for
t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}
and
h\leq\frac{1}{n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{t}[|\Delta X_{n}(t)|^{3}]\leq C(hn^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ln^{6}(n)+n^{-7.5})+n^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{c}_{n}(t)}.

∎

4.2
Assumptions of Proposition
4.1
for the sequence
Z_{n}

This section is devoted to checking that the sequence of processes

, satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
4.1
where the process

is nothing but the standard Brownian motion.
For this purpose we want to check these assumptions with
\alpha\equiv 0
,
\beta\equiv 1
, and take the origin of time at

.
In this case, the remainder functions are

,

and

In this setting, checking Assumption

[
1
,
12
,
15
]

is an increasing smooth function from

to
(0,+\infty)
and

is a smooth function on

that is never equal to zero, this implies that

A4
.

Let us now prove Assumption
A1
, concerning the first three moments of the increment of
Z_{n}
.
The estimations of the increment of
Z_{n}
are based on the identity:

\displaystyle\Delta Z_{n}(t)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t+h)-Z_{n}(t)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u\circ v^{(-1)}(t+h)\left(X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t+h)-X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)\right)

\displaystyle+X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)\left(u\circ v^{(-1)}(t+h)-u\circ v^{(-1)}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u\circ v^{(-1)}(t)+O(h))\left(X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t+h)-X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)\right)

\displaystyle+X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)\left(h(u\circ v^{(-1)})^{\prime}(t)+O(h^{2})\right)

The first moment of
\Delta Z_{n}
.

By Lemma

such that for every

,

and

=\left(\frac{h}{v^{\prime}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)}+O(h^{2})\right)a(v^{(-1)}(t),X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t))\\
+O\left(hn^{-0.1}+n^{-7.5}+\sqrt{n}\mathds{1}_{|X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)|>n^{-0.2}}\right).

Noting that

(u\circ v^{(-1)})(t)\frac{a(v^{(-1)}(t),X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t))}{v^{\prime}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)}+X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)(u\circ v^{(-1)})^{\prime}(t)=0.

Hence, the expectation of the zero order term of (
24
) cancels.
We obtain that if
n>t_{1}
,

, and

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(hn^{-0.1}+n^{-7.5}+\sqrt{n}\mathds{1}_{|X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(t)|>n^{0.2}}).

Let

5
Tightness

Our approach to prove the tightness of
Z_{n}
relies on the criterion by Billingsley (
[
3
]
, Theorem 13.5).
Namely we have to prove that, there exist
\alpha>1
and a constant
C>0
such that for
v(t_{0})\leq r<s<t\leq v(t_{1})

\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}\right]\leq C(t-r)^{\alpha}.

(28)

We will treat separately the case where
t-r
is very small comparatively to
n
and when it is not. More precisely we will distinguish between
t-r\leq n^{-5}
and
t-r>n^{-5}
.

The case
t-r\leq n^{-5}

As
Z_{n}(t)=(uX_{n})(v^{(-1)}(t)),
and
v
and
v^{(-1)}
are Lipschitz on the intervals we are considering, to prove (
28
) for
t-r\leq n^{-5}
it suffices to prove for some constant
D
that, for every
t_{0}\leq r<s<t\leq t_{1}
such that
t-r\leq Dn^{-5}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left((uX_{n})(s)-(uX_{n})(r)\right)^{2}\left((uX_{n})(t)-(uX_{n})(s)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C(t-r)^{\alpha}.

(29)

Since
(uX_{n})(s)-(uX_{n})(r)
is equal to

\frac{(u(s)-u(r))L_{n}(s)+u(r)(L_{n}(s)-L_{n}(r))-(u(s)\rho(s)-u(r)\rho(r))n}{\sqrt{n}},

we have by straightforward calculation and by bounding
L_{n}
by
n
:

((uX_{n})(s)-(uX_{n})(r))^{2}\leq O(n(s-r)^{2})+C\frac{(L_{n}(s)-L_{n}(r))^{2}}{n}.

(30)

The same is evidently true for
((uX_{n})(t)-(uX_{n})(s))^{2}
. Observing that for
t-r\leq n^{-5}
,

n(s-r)^{2}\leq n(t-r)^{0.9}(t-r)^{1.1}\leq\frac{(t-r)^{1.1}}{n^{3.5}},

(31)

and bounding
L_{n}
by
n
, we are reduced to show that for some
\alpha>1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(L_{n}(s)-L_{n}(r))^{2}(L_{n}(t)-L_{n}(s))^{2}}{n^{2}}\right]\leq C(t-r)^{\alpha}.

As the difference
t-r
is assumed very small, the term in the expectation will be zero with a very high probability. Indeed, for it to be non-zero it is necessary that one edge is drawn during each of the intervals
[r,s]
and
[s,t]
. Brutally bounding
L_{n}(t)-L_{n}(r)
by
n
and noting that the number of edges drawn in two distinct intervals are negatively correlated, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(L_{n}(s)-L_{n}(r))^{2}(L_{n}(t)-L_{n}(s))^{2}}{n^{2}}\right]\leq n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathrm{Bin}(n^{2},(t-r)/n)>0\right)^{2}\\
=O(n^{4}(t-r)^{2})=O(1)(t-r)^{1.1}.

(32)

Hence (
28
) holds.

The case
t-r>n^{-5}

In this case our strategy is to subdivide the interval
[r,t]
so that we can apply our results from Section
4
. Thus, we take as increment
h=n^{-5}
.
Set
k=\lfloor\frac{t-s}{h}\rfloor
,
s_{i}=s+ih
for
0\leq i\leq k
and
s_{k+1}=t
. We construct similarly
r_{i}=r+ih
for
0\leq i\leq\lfloor\frac{r-s}{h}\rfloor
.

We have

\mathbb{E}[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]\right].

We first determine an upper bound for

\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]
.

We write, for any function
g
,
\Delta_{i}g:=g(s_{i+1})-g(s_{i}).

The decomposition

Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\Delta_{i}Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]\right)+\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]

implies

(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}\leq 2\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\left(\Delta_{i}Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]\right)\right)^{2}+2\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]\right)^{2}.

Thus, by orthogonality of the centered terms:

\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]\leq 2(S_{1}+S_{2}).

with

S_{1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}\left[\left(\Delta_{i}Z_{n}-\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]\right)^{2}\right]

and

S_{2}=\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}\left[\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]\right)^{2}\right].

To find an upper bound of
S_{2}
we use (
25
) and get

\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[\Delta_{i}Z_{n}]=O(hn^{-0.1}+n^{-7.5}+\sqrt{n}\mathds{1}_{|X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(s_{i})|>n^{0.2}}).

Note that for
i=k,
this estimate remains true when replacing
h
by
s_{k+1}-s_{k}<h
.
Hence, recalling
h=n^{-5}

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}(\Delta_{i}Z_{n})\right)^{2}=O\left((t-r)^{2}+n\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}(v^{(-1)}(s_{i}))}\right)^{2}\right),

whence

S_{2}=O\left((t-r)^{2}+n(k+1)^{2}\max_{0<i<k}\mathbb{P}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}(v^{(-1)}(s_{i})))\right).

It remains to deal with
S_{1}
. Using (
26
), the conditional second moment of
\Delta Z_{n}(s_{i})
is bounded by:

\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s_{i})}[(\Delta_{i}Z_{n})^{2}]=O(h+n^{-7.5}+n\mathds{1}_{|X_{n}\circ v^{(-1)}(s_{i})|>n^{0.2}}).

Hence,

S_{1}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(\Delta_{i}Z_{n})^{2}]=O\left(t-r+n(k+1)\max_{0<i<k}\mathbb{P}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}(v^{(-1)}(s_{i})))\right).

Putting together the estimates for
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
we have

\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]=O\left(t-r+n(k+1)^{2}\max_{0<i<k}\mathbb{P}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}(s_{i}))\right).

Using this bound we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}\right]\leq C(t-r)\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}\right]\\
+C\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}n(k+1)^{2}\max_{0<i<k}\mathbb{P}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}(\mathcal{E}_{n}^{c}(v^{(-1)}(s_{i})))\right].

The second term is smaller than
n^{-10}
by Lemma
A.1
. The first term is bounded similarly as
\mathbb{E}_{v^{(-1)}(s)}[(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}]
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[(Z_{n}(s)-Z_{n}(r))^{2}(Z_{n}(t)-Z_{n}(s))^{2}\right]\leq C(t-r)^{2},

which finishes the proof of (
28
) for
t-r>n^{-5}
.

Appendix A
Rare events

In this Appendix we give an upper bound, uniform in
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, on the probability of the complement of the following events introduced in the article:

•

\mathcal{E}_{n}(t)
, the event that, at time
t
,
|L_{n}(t)-n\rho(t)|\leq n^{0.7}
or equivalently
|X_{n}(t)|\leq n^{0.2}
;

•

\mathcal{A}_{n}(t)
, the event that no component of
\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}^{e}(t,h)
has size greater than
\ln^{2}(n)
;

•

\tilde{{\mathcal{A}}}_{n}(t)
, the event
\{M_{i}\leq\ln^{2}(n),\quad\forall 1\leq i\leq n^{2}\}
, where the random variables
M_{i}
are independent and distributed, conditional on
\mathcal{F}_{n}(t)
, as the total progeny of a
\mathrm{BGW}({n-L_{n}(t)},\frac{t+h}{n})
.

We are going to show the following two lemmas:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
0<\gamma<0.5
. There exist positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
such that for every
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
,

\mathbb{P}(|X_{n}(t)|>n^{\gamma})\leq C^{\prime}\exp(-Cn^{2\gamma}).

Lemma A.2
.

Let
0<\gamma<0.5
. There exists
h_{0}>0
such that, for
0\leq h\leq h_{0},
uniformly in
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, on the event
\{|X_{n}(t)|<n^{\gamma}\}
, for all
R>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}^{c}_{n}(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(t))=O(n^{-R})\text{ and }\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{n}(t)\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(t))=O(n^{-R}).

Proof of Lemma
A.1

Our main tool to prove Lemma
A.1
is the following Theorem by Stepanov:

Theorem

(Theorem 1 in
[
15
]
)
.

Set
y_{0}>0
and let
P_{n}(s)
denote the probability that a
\mathrm{ER}(n,1-e^{-s})
graph is connected.

P_{n}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)=\left(1-\frac{y}{e^{y}-1}\right)(1-e^{-y})^{n}(1+o(1))

(33)

where the
o(1)
is uniform in
y\geq y_{0}
.

Note that Stepanov’s result uses a different time parametrization (namely
{\frac{t}{n}=1-e^{-\frac{y}{n}}}
). For the sake of readability we will use Stepanov’s parametrization, and prove the following lemma, which is equivalent to Lemma
A.1
.

Lemma A.3
.

Set
\hat{L}_{n}(y)=L_{n}(n(1-e^{-y/n}))
the largest order of connected components of a
{\mathrm{ER}(n,1-e^{-\frac{y}{n}})}
graph.

For
1<y_{0}<y_{1}
and
0<\gamma<0.5
, there exist positive constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
such that for every
y\in[y_{0},y_{1}]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(|\hat{L}_{n}(y)-n\rho(y)|>n^{0.5+\gamma}\right)\leq C^{\prime}e^{-Cn^{2\gamma}}.

Proof.

The ideas come from the proof of Theorem 2 in Stepanov’s article
[
15
]
, however we will use them towards a different objective.
We call
E_{n,k}(s)
the expected number of components with
k
vertices in an
\mathrm{ER}(n,1-e^{-s})
graph.
As pointed out in Stepanov’s article (Eq. (27) in
[
15
]
),

E_{n,k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)=\binom{n}{k}e^{-yk(1-k/n)}P_{k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right).

Our idea is to use the upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\hat{L}_{n}(y)=k)\leq E_{n,k}(y/n)
. Obviously, this upper bound is extremely bad if
k
is much smaller than
n
. Therefore, we will treat these values separately. Set
0<\varepsilon<\min(\rho(y_{0}),1-\rho(y_{1})).

We now decompose

\mathbb{P}(|\hat{L}_{n}(y)-n\rho(y)|>n^{\gamma+0.5})\leq\mathbb{P}(\hat{L}_{n}(y)\leq\varepsilon n)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon n\leq k\leq(1-\varepsilon)n:\\
|\frac{k}{n}-\rho(y)|>n^{\gamma-0.5}\end{subarray}}E_{n,k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)\\
+\mathbb{P}(\hat{L}_{n}(y)\geq(1-\varepsilon)n).

(34)

Note that the first and third terms are monotonous in
y
. Therefore, we only have to bound them for
y=y_{0}
and
y=y_{1}
, respectively. This can be done using a large deviation principle for
\hat{L}_{n}(y)
at a fixed value of
y
(see for example Theorem 3.1 of
[
11
]
). It yields bounds of order
\exp(-Cn)
.

To deal with the second term we use Stirling’s formula to get, for
\varepsilon n\leq k\leq(1-\varepsilon)n
,

E_{n,k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi k(1-\frac{k}{n})}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}(1-\frac{k}{n})^{n-k}}e^{-yk(1-\frac{k}{n})}P_{k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)(1+o(1)).

Using (
33
), there exists a constant
C_{y_{0}}>0
only dependent on
y_{0}
such that for
y\geq y_{0}
,

P_{n}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)\leq C_{y_{0}}\left(1-\frac{y}{e^{y}-1}\right)(1-e^{-y})^{n}.

In order to bound from above
E_{n,k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)
, we have to deal with
P_{k}(\frac{y}{n})=P_{k}(\frac{k}{n}\frac{y}{k})
where
{\frac{k}{n}y\geq\varepsilon y_{0}}
, hence

E_{n,k}\left(\frac{y}{n}\right)\leq C_{\varepsilon y_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi k(1-\frac{k}{n})}}\left(1-\frac{\frac{k}{n}y}{e^{\frac{k}{n}y}-1}\right)e^{n\phi(\frac{k}{n},y)},

with
\phi(x,y)=-xy(1-x)+x\ln(1-e^{-xy})-x\ln x-(1-x)\ln(1-x).

This function cancels if and only if
x=0
and
x=\rho(y)
. Indeed, if we express it as a function of
x
and
\delta(x,y)=1-e^{-xy}-x
, it writes:

\phi(x,y)=x\ln(1+\frac{\delta}{x})+(1-x)\ln(1-\frac{\delta}{1-x})=:\psi(x,\delta),

and
\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\delta}(x,\delta)=-\dfrac{\delta}{(x+\delta)(1-x-\delta)}.

We observe that
|\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\delta}(x,\delta)|\geq 4|\delta|
, hence
\psi(x,\delta)\leq-2\delta^{2}
.
Therefore,
\psi
vanishes only for
\delta=0
(it increases before and decreases after 0 since
{-x\leq\delta\leq 1-x}
).

Now note that
\delta(\cdot,y)
is a concave function such that
\delta(0,y)=\delta(\rho(y),y)=0.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta(x,y)}{\partial x}|_{x=0}=y-1\geq y_{0}-1,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\delta(x,y)}{\partial x}|_{x=\rho(y)}=y(1-\rho(y))-1=\lambda(y)-1<\lambda(y_{0})-1<0.

Consequently, for
k
such that
\varepsilon n\leq k\leq(1-\varepsilon)n
and
|\frac{k}{n}-\rho(y)|>n^{\gamma-0.5}
, we get
{\left|\delta\left(\frac{k}{n},y\right)\right|>Cn^{\gamma-0.5}}
, which implies

\left|\phi\left(\frac{k}{n},y\right)\right|>Cn^{2\gamma-1}
and in turn,

E_{n,k}(\frac{y}{n})\leq C^{\prime}e^{-Cn^{2\gamma}}.

Summing over those values of
k
shows that the sum on the right-hand side of
(
34
) is smaller than
C^{\prime}e^{-Cn^{2\gamma}}
.

∎

, let us start by showing that the event

tends to
+\infty
.

(Theorem 4.4 in
[
16
]
)
.

Set
I(c)=c-1-\ln(c)
and
|\mathcal{C}_{max}|
the order of the largest connected component of an

.

For every
0<c<1

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{C}_{\max}|\geq a\ln(n)\right)\leq n^{1-aI(c)}

In our case, the parameter

\tilde{\mathscr{G}}_{n}^{e}(t,h)

To deal with the event

, the random variables

, can be dominated by the total progeny of a subcritical Poisson

This follows a Borel distribution, which admits an exponential moment.

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Consider
k

0<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{k}
and
k

f_{1},\ldots f_{k}

\mathcal{H}

\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(Z_{n}(s_{i}))\right)

tends to

.
To study the difference of these two expectations, set

I_{n,u,v}(f)=\mathbb{E}\left(f(Z_{n}(v))\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(u))-T_{v-u}f(Z_{n}(u)\right)\quad\forall 0\leq u<v\text{ and }\forall f\in\mathcal{H}.

With this notation,

\mathbb{E}\left(f_{1}(Z_{n}(s_{1}))\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(I_{n,0,s_{1}}(f_{1})\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(T_{s_{1}}f_{1}(Z_{n}(0))\right)

Let us note that by assumption, if
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathcal{H}

0<u<v
, the two functions

and

\mathbb{E}\left(f_{1}(Z(s_{1}))\right)

4.1
follows by induction on

if we show that
\mathbb{E}(|I_{n,u,v}(f)|)

h_{n}=\frac{v-u}{N_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\mathbb{E}(g_{j+1}(Z_{n}(u_{j+1}))\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}(u_{j}))-g_{j+1}(Z_{n}(u_{j}))=\\

,
