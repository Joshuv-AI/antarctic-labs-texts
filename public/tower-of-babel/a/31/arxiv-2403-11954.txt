Abstract
While there is a rich literature on robust methodologies for contamination in continuously distributed data, contamination in categorical data is largely overlooked. This is regrettable because many datasets are categorical and oftentimes suffer from contamination. Examples include inattentive responding and bot responses in questionnaires or zero-inflated count data. We propose a novel class of contamination-robust estimators of models for categorical data, coined C C -estimators (“ C C ” for categorical). We show that the countable and possibly finite sample space of categorical data results in non-standard theoretical properties. Notably, in contrast to classic robustness theory, C C -estimators can be simultaneously robust and fully efficient at the postulated model. In addition, a certain particularly robust specification fails to be asymptotically Gaussian at the postulated model, but is asymptotically Gaussian in the presence of contamination. We furthermore propose a diagnostic test to identify categorical outliers and demonstrate the enhanced robustness of C C -estimators in a simulation study.

Introduction

Research in the biomedical, psychological, and social sciences, among others, is often concerned with modeling categorical variables. However, certain errors might be present in the specification of the postulated model or the data themselves. Examples include inattentive responding
(
Arias et al., 2020
;
Huang et al., 2015
;
Meade & Craig, 2012
, e.g.,)
and bot responses
(
Ilagan & Falk, 2023
)
in online Likert-type questionnaire items, misspecification of latent variable models for rating data
(
Foldnes & Grønneberg, 2022
;
Foldnes & Grønneberg, 2020
, e.g.,)
, zero-inflated count data
(
Lachin, 2014
;
Lambert, 1992
, e.g.,)
, and specification errors in models for grouped personal income data
(
Victoria-Feser & Ronchetti, 1997
)
. These and other studies demonstrate that if unaccounted for, such errors, henceforth collectively called
contaminations
, can cause model estimates to be severely biased. It is therefore often of interest to obtain contamination-robust model estimates.

Conventional robust estimators
(
Huber & Ronchetti, 2009
;
Hampel et al., 1986
, e.g.,)
are generally devised for problems with unbounded sample space and may therefore not be appropriate for models of categorical data, whose sample space may not be bounded and numerical. Hence, the study of robustness for categorical data necessitates non-standard approaches. It turns out that the non-standard nature of this problems yields unexpected results with respect to balancing efficiency and robustness, which is outlined in the following.

We propose a general class of robust estimators for the estimation of models for categorical data, called
C
-estimators (“
C
” for categorical). We show that all estimators in this class, save for one notable exception, are fully efficient at the postulated model, yet they may possess certain robustness properties. This is in contrast to elementary robustness theory according to which there is a fundamental tradeoff between efficiency and robustness
(
Huber & Ronchetti, 2009
;
Hampel et al., 1986
, e.g.,)
. Another surprising result arises for the aforementioned notable exception: This estimator yields improved first-order robustness but has an unusual asymptotics: At the true model, the estimator does
not
converge in distribution, but under contamination, it is asymptotically Gaussian, suggesting that inference is easier when the data are contaminated.

As a second contribution, we use the developed limit theory to propose a novel test to formalize and identify categorical “outliers”. In essence, the test assesses if the frequency of a given categorical class can be modeled by the postulated model. If this null hypothesis is rejected, the class is considered “outlying”. We thus not only propose a way to robustly fit models for categorical data, but also a diagnostic tool to identify potential sources of contamination therein.

Motivated by the recent empirical interest in inattentive responding and bot responses
(
Arias et al., 2020
;
Ilagan & Falk, 2023
, e.g.,)
, we apply the theory developed in this paper to models for questionnaire responses in our companion paper
(
Welz et al., 2024
)
. Using simulated and empirical data, we find that
C
-estimators are very effective at mitigating bias and identifying potentially inattentive respondents.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
defines the class of
C
-estimators, and Section
3
describes the relationship between this class and existing estimators. Section
4
derives the asymptotic properties of the class, and Section
5
proposes a diagnostic test for detecting categorical outliers. Section
6
demonstrates the practical usefulness of the developed theory by means of a simulation study on zero-inflated Poisson data. Section
7
discusses our results and concludes.

2
Introducing
C
-estimation

This section introduces notation and describes the defining properties of
C
-estimators.

2.1
Setup

Let
\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{k})^{\top}
be a
k
-dimensional categorical, but not necessarily ordinal, random vector taking values in an
m
-dimensional countable sample space
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
. Specifically, if
m
is finite, the sample space is defined as
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}=\{\bm{z}_{1},\bm{z}_{2},\dots,\bm{z}_{m}\}
, and if
m=\infty
, it is defined as the countably infinite set
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}=\{\bm{z}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Suppose one postulates a statistical model for vector
\bm{Z}
that is subject to a deterministic unobserved
d
-dimensional parameter vector
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Models of categorical variables, denoted
\{\bm{p}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=(p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right))_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}:\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}\}
here, are characterized by assigning to each discrete outcome
\bm{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k})^{\top}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
a nonnegative probability

p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\left[\bm{\mathcal{Z}}=\bm{z}\right]=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}}\left[Z_{1}=z_{1},\dots,Z_{k}=z_{k}\right]\quad\textnormal{such that}\quad\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=1,

which depends on the parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
. The function
\bm{z}\mapsto p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
is known as
probability mass function
(PMF).

We now state some minimal assumptions to ensure that the model is well-behaved and allows for statistical inference.

Assumption 1
.

The postulated model satisfies the following properties.

A.1.1.

p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
,

A.1.2.

p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{1}\right)\neq p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{2}\right),\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}},
for every
\bm{\theta}_{1}\neq\bm{\theta}_{2}
in
\bm{\Theta}
,

A.1.3.

p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)>0
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
,
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
,

In essence, Assumption
1
requires the model’s PMF to be sufficiently smooth, the model parameters to be point-identifiable, and the model probabilities to be strictly positive. The latter assumption is not strictly required, but makes the notation easier and avoids having to deal with unconstructive technicalities.

In this paper, we focus on situations in which the postulated model is potentially misspecified due to contamination. We adopt the contamination model of
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
, which assumes that observed data of
\bm{Z}
is distributed according to a perturbed version of the model. This perturbed model is characterized by a probability function

f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})=(1-\varepsilon)p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)+\varepsilon h(\bm{z}),\qquad\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}},

(1)

where the unobserved but fixed
\varepsilon\in[0.5)
is called the
contamination fraction
, and
h(\bm{z})
is an unspecified arbitrary probability function on
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, called the
contamination PMF
. Furthermore,
\bm{\theta}_{*}\in\bm{\Theta}
in (
1
) can be thought of as a true parameter value under which the postulated model generates uncontaminated data, in contrast to
h(\cdot)
, which introduces contamination. Given a nonnegative contamination fraction
\varepsilon>0
, we say that the postulated model is
misspecified
if
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})\neq p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
for any
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
and
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
. Note that the contamination model in (
1
) can be seen as an extension of the classical contamination model of
Huber, 1964
to categorical data.

2.2
Empirical risk minimization

Suppose we observe an
N
-sized sample
(\bm{Z}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
of independent copies of
\bm{Z}
being distributed according to the possibly contaminated PMF
f_{\varepsilon}
in (
1
). Using this sample, the statistical problem is to estimate the true parameter
\bm{\theta}_{*}
with only little bias when the postulated model is possibly misspecified. Denote by

\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\left\{\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{z}\right\},\qquad\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}},

the empirical probability function, which is a strongly consistent estimator of
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
as
N\to\infty

(
Van der Vaart, 1998
, e.g., Chapter 19.2 in)
.

C
-estimation is based on
Pearson residuals

(
Lindsay, 1994
)
. For a given parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
, the sample Pearson residual of cell
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, defined as

\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})}{p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}-1,

measures the discrepancy between the sample probability,
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})
, and the model probability,
p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
.
1
1

1

Lindsay, 1994
coins
\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
as “Pearson” residual because Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
-distance can be expressed as a model-weighted sum of squared residuals, namely
\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\delta^{2}_{\bm{z},N}(\bm{\theta})
.
By construction, Pearson residuals assume values in the interval
[-1,+\infty)
. Values close to 0 indicate good model fit, whereas values away from 0 indicate poor model fit. Indeed, if the model is correctly specified
(\varepsilon=0)
, then
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f_{0}(\bm{z})=p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)
, so there exists a parameter vector in
\bm{\Theta}
at which the Pearson residual converges to 0 (in probability), namely the true
\bm{\theta}_{*}
. Conversely, if the model is misspecified
(\varepsilon>0)
, then there exists no value in
\bm{\Theta}
at which the Pearson residual converges (in probability) to 0.

In the same fashion as
minimum disparity estimation

(
Lindsay, 1994
, e.g.,)
, the key idea behind
C
-estimation is to downweigh cells whose Pearson residuals are far away from 0, either towards
-1
or
+\infty
. This is achieved through a prespecified
discrepancy
function
\rho:[-1:\infty]\to\mathbb{R}
to map individual Pearson residuals. Applying this mapping to all Pearson residuals gives rise to the empirical risk

{L_{N}}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\rho\big(\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\big)p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

(2)

with minimizer

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}L_{N}(\bm{\theta}).

(3)

The empirical risk (
2
) is a special case of the the
f
-divergence of
Csiszár, 1963
, measuring the divergence between the empirical PMF and the model PMF through the discrepancy function
\rho
. Assumption
2
collects minimal assumptions on
\rho
.

Assumption 2
.

The discrepancy function
\rho:[-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following properties.

A.2.1.

\rho(0)=0
,

A.2.2.

\rho
is weakly convex and continuously differentiable,

A.2.3.

\rho
is thrice differentiable on all but a finite (possibly empty) subset of
[-1,\infty)
, and the second and third derivatives,
\rho^{\prime\prime}
and
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}
, are continuous on their respective domains.

While discrepancy functions may take negative values, the empirical risk in (
2
) as well as its population counterpart are always nonnegative. This follows from Assumption
A.2.1
in combination with equation (35) in
Csiszár, 1963
.

The fact that Assumption
A.2.3
permits discrepancy functions that are not everywhere twice or thrice differentiable is particularly appealing for robust estimation. Indeed, some of the most popular robust loss functions for continuous variables, such as Huber loss or Tukey’s bisquare loss, are defined in piecewise fashion so that the influence of large function arguments (in absolute terms) can be controlled. Such functions may not be twice (or thrice) differentiable at their threshold points.

2.3
Residual adjustment function

The discrepancy function
\rho
gives rise to a specific function, the
residual adjustment function

(
Lindsay, 1994
, RAF;)
, which determines the robustness and efficiency properties of the empirical risk minimizer
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
in (
3
). Denoting
\psi=\rho^{\prime}
, the RAF
A:[-1,\infty)\to\infty
is defined as

A(x)=(x+1)\psi(x)-\rho(x)

with first derivative

A^{\prime}(x)=(x+1)\psi^{\prime}(x).

The RAF naturally arises from the (negative) loss gradient. Indeed, the empirical risk minimizer
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
in (
3
) can equivalently be defined as solution to the estimating equation

\bm{0}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{L_{N}}\left({\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}}\right)=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}A\left(\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right)\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right),

(4)

where the gradient operator
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}
denotes differentiation with respect to
\bm{\theta}
.
To ensure that the RAF exhibits meaningful behavior, we impose two minimal assumptions, listed in Assumption
3
.

Assumption 3
.

A residual adjustment function
A(\delta)=(\delta+1)\psi(\delta)-\rho(\delta)
implied by discrepancy function
\rho:[-1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

A.3.1.

A(0)=0
and, if it exists,
A^{\prime}(0)=1
(without loss of generality),

A.3.2.

A
is weakly increasing on its domain.

The assumption that the RAF and its derivative respectively equal 0 and 1 at the origin is without loss of generality for the following reason. Recall that the RAF stems from the loss gradient in (
4
). Note that since
\sum_{z\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=1
, we have for any
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
that
\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\bm{0}
. Hence, we can replace
A(x)
by the affine transformation
\widetilde{A}(x)=aA(x)+b
with
a\neq 0,b\in\mathbb{R}
being constants, and the solution to the estimating equation would remain unaffected because
\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\widetilde{A}(\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right))\propto\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)A(\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right))
. Furthermore, because of the possible non-differentiability at certain points (cf. Assumption
2
), the RAF derivative
A^{\prime}(0)
may not exist. The assumption that the RAF is weakly increasing is analogous with a well-known assumption in
M
-estimation, namely that the derivative of the objective function is weakly increasing.

We are now ready to formally define
C
-estimators.

Definition 1

(
C
-estimator)
.

A
C
-estimator
is any parameter
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\in\bm{\Theta}
that minimizes the empirical risk (
2
) while satisfying Assumptions
1
–
3
.

C
-estimators in Section
4

More formally, in robust statistics an estimator is called “optimal” if it minimizes its asymptotic variance at the true model subject to a given bound on its influence function at the true model. In other words, an optimal estimator is the most efficient first-order-robust estimator in a certain class of estimators. This notion of optimality is due to
Hampel, 1968
.

The class of MGP estimators of
Victoria-Feser & Ronchetti, 1997
was originally devised for grouped data (particularly grouped income data) but can also be applied to general categorical data. For a constant
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and a given function
\bm{\varphi}_{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})=\bm{\varphi}\left(\bm{z},\bm{\theta}\right)
that maps from
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}\times\bm{\Theta}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
and is continuously differentiable with respect to
\bm{\theta}

\bm{\theta}=\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
to the estimating equation

\bm{0}=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\bm{\varphi}_{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})^{\gamma},

subject to the Fisher consistency and normalization conditions in (

show that the optimal MGP estimator is given by the choice

where matrix
\bm{H}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
and vector
\bm{a}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
are implicitly defined according to (
6
). Observe that for the choice
\bm{\varphi}_{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})=p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{-\gamma}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
, one can express as MGP estimator the MLE
(\gamma=1)
and the minimum Hellinger distance estimator
(\gamma=0.5)
, which are also contained in the class of
C
-estimators. However, other
C
-estimators, in particular the generalized
E
-estimators, cannot be expressed as MGP estimator.

We proceed by studying the theoretical properties of
C
-estimators.

4
Theoretical properties

4.1
Estimand

The estimand of a
C
-estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
in (
3
) is given by the parameter that minimizes the population risk associated with the empirical risk. Formally, for the population Pearson residual and population risk respectively defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})}{p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}-1,\qquad\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta},\quad\textnormal{ and}

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\rho\big(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\big)p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right),

the estimand equals the population risk minimizer

\bm{\theta}_{0}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}}L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta}).

(7)

As such,
\bm{\theta}_{0}
depends on the unobserved fraction and type of contamination,
\varepsilon
and
h(\cdot)
, respectively, as well as discrepancy function
\rho(\cdot)
. Under the additional assumption that
\rho
is strictly convex,
\bm{\theta}_{0}
equals the true
\bm{\theta}_{*}
in the absence of model misspecification
(\varepsilon=0)

(
Csiszár, 1963
)
. In other words, if the discrepancy function is strictly convex, then
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
is Fisher-consistent. Assumption
4
establishes point-identification of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
under compactness of the parameter space.

Assumption 4
.

The parameter space
\bm{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact. In addition, estimand
\bm{\theta}_{0}
is the unique global minimum of
L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})
and an interior point of
\bm{\Theta}
.

We proceed by studying the estimator’s limit behavior.

4.2
Asymptotic analysis

The following theorem establishes consistency of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
for estimand
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

Theorem 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, it holds true that

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bm{\theta}_{0},

as
N\to\infty
.

The proofs of this theorem and all subsequent mathematical statements in this paper are deferred to Appendix
C
.

We now turn to the asymptotic distribution of
C
-estimators, for which we introduce additional notation to accommodate two special cases.

First, we will frequently perform the operation
\bm{A}\bm{b}
for a
(d\times m)
-dimensional matrix
\bm{A}
and an
m
-dimensional vector
\bm{b}
. In the case where
m=\infty
(like the Poisson model), we define the matrix operation
\bm{Ab}
based on a certain inner product on an infinite-dimensional vector space. Specifically, in our setup, that inner product is given by an infinite sum over the inner product of the corresponding row in
\bm{A}
and vector
\bm{b}
. With this definition, we have that

\bm{A}\bm{b}=\begin{pmatrix}&\sum_{\{j\ :\ \bm{z}_{j}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}\}}A_{1j}b_{j}\\
&\vdots\\
&\sum_{\{j\ :\ \bm{z}_{j}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}\}}A_{dj}b_{j}\end{pmatrix},

so that the resulting vector is of finite dimension
d
.
Similar reasoning applies to the operation
\bm{A}\bm{B}\bm{A}^{\top}
, where
\bm{B}
is a
(m\times m)
matrix. Throughout this paper, whenever we sum over the sample space
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
like above, a PMF of a discrete distribution is involved such that the summation is equal to an expectation over that distribution. We will later impose an assumption that ensures that this expectation exists and is finite when the sample space
\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
is infinite and (possibly)
\varepsilon>0
.

Second, in some objects relevant to the estimator’s asymptotics, we often need to evaluate limits of the form

\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\psi^{\prime}\big(\delta(\bm{\theta}+\alpha\bm{w})\big),

(8)

where
\delta:\bm{\Theta}\to[-1,\infty)
denotes a generic Pearson residual,
\alpha>0
is a scalar,
\bm{w}\neq\bm{0}
is a deterministic
d
-dimensional vector, and
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
is arbitrary. If
\psi
is differentiable at
\delta(\bm{\theta})
, then this limit equals
\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\bm{\theta}))
. However,
\psi
may not be differentiable at
\delta(\bm{\theta})
(cf. Assumption
A.2.3
). It is useful for the asymptotic analysis to introduce additional notation that reflects this possible non-differentiability.

In the univariate case (
d=1
), there are two directions along which one can evaluate the limit in (
8
), governed by the sign of
w\neq 0
. If
w>0
, then (
8
) equals the right limit
\lim_{x\downarrow\theta}\psi^{\prime}(\delta(x))=:\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta+))
, and if
w<0
, then it equals the left limit
\lim_{x\uparrow\theta}\psi^{\prime}(\delta(x))=:\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta-))
. We now describe a notion of such directional limits that also covers the multidimensional case (
d>1
).

To start, note that for evaluating the limit in (
8
), the exact value of the
d
-dimensional vector
\bm{w}
is not important. Rather than
\bm{w}
itself, the
sign
of each coordinate in
\bm{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})^{\top}
determines the direction of the linear path
\bm{\theta}+\alpha\bm{w}
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
. Defining the coordinatewise sign function
\bm{\kappa}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
as

\bm{\kappa}(\bm{w})=\Big(\textnormal{sign}\big(w_{j}\big)\Big)_{j=1}^{d}

enables us to, without loss of generality, replace
\bm{w}
by
\bm{\kappa}(\bm{w})
in limit (
8
). Since
\bm{\kappa}(\bm{w})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
for any
\bm{w}\neq\bm{0}
, this notation makes it explicit that there are
3^{d}-1
possible directions along which one could evaluate the limit along the linear path, and
\bm{w}
uniquely determines the choice of direction.
4
4

4

In addition to multiple directions of a linear path, there are infinitely many (possibly nonlinear) paths along which one could evaluate the limit of a multivariable function, but, in the asymptotic analysis it suffices to only consider the linear path in (
8
) for the analysis of the function composition
\psi^{\prime}\circ\delta
.

With the coordinatewise sign function, we can define the shorthand

\psi^{\prime}\left({\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)\right)\pm}}\right)\right):=\lim_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\psi^{\prime}\big({\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}+\alpha\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)}\right)\big),

(9)

which equals the limit in (
8
) for any
\bm{w}\neq\bm{0}
. The superscript
``(\bm{\kappa}(\bm{w}))\pm"
reminds us that we do
not
evaluate the function composition
\psi^{\prime}\circ\delta
at
\bm{\theta}
, but along a linear path
\bm{\theta}+\alpha\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right))
,
\alpha\downarrow 0
, whose direction is governed by the coordinatewise signs
\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)
. As such, the definition
\psi^{\prime}\left({\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)\right)\pm}}\right)\right)
generalizes the well-known unidimensional left and right limits to higher dimensions. Indeed, if
d=1
, then
\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta^{(\kappa(w))\pm}))=\lim_{x\uparrow\theta}\psi^{\prime}(\delta(x))=\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta-))
if
\kappa(w)=-1
, and
\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta^{(\kappa(w))\pm}))=\lim_{x\downarrow\theta}\psi^{\prime}(\delta(x))=\psi^{\prime}(\delta(\theta+))
if
\kappa(w)=1
, for any unidimensional
\theta
and
w\neq 0
. Furthermore, if
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({{\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)}\right)
exists, then
\psi^{\prime}\left({\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)\right)\pm}}\right)\right)={\psi^{\prime}}\left({{\delta}\left({\bm{\theta}}\right)}\right)
for all directions
\bm{w}
.

With the new notation, we are ready to define matrices that are important in the estimator’s limit theory. Specifically, the asymptotic covariance matrix of
C
-estimators is a function of only two matrices,
\bm{M}
and
\bm{U}
, which will be defined in the following.

For
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
, put the
(d\times m)
matrix

\bm{W}(\bm{\theta})=\bigg({A^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z}_{1},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}_{1}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right),\cdots,{A^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z}_{m},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}_{m}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bigg),

where
\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log(p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right))=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)/p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
is the log-likelihood score function, and, for
\bm{f}_{\varepsilon}=(f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z}))_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}
, define the
(m\times m)
matrix

\bm{\Omega}=\textnormal{diag}\left(\bm{f}_{\varepsilon}\right)-\bm{f}_{\varepsilon}\bm{f}_{\varepsilon}^{\top}.

The sole purpose of matrices
\bm{W}(\bm{\theta})
and
\bm{\Omega}
is to calculate the symmetric
(d\times d)
matrix

\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\bm{W}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bm{\Omega}\bm{W}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}.

Furthermore, for
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\in\bm{\Theta}
, define the symmetric
(d\times d)
matrix

\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\bigg[\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})\psi^{\prime}\left(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\right)\right)(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)+1)-\left(\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)A\left(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\bigg]

and put

\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}\right)=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}\bigg[\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z}){A^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}\right)-\left(\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)A\left(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\right)\bigg],

where
\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
denotes the Hessian matrix of
p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
.

There are two situations warranting special attention.

First, both
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\right)
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
depend on
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)}\right)
(the latter through
x\mapsto A^{\prime}(x)=(x+1)\psi^{\prime}(x)
), but
\psi^{\prime}
may not exist everywhere (cf. Assumption
A.2.3
). To circumvent this issue, we sometimes use for a given direction
\bm{w}\neq\bm{0}
the limit approximations

\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)\right)\pm}\right)
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{w}\right)\right)\pm}\right)
, which are to be understood in the sense of (
9
).

Second, the matrix-valued functions
\bm{M}
and
\bm{U}
may not necessarily be finite across all possible contamination fractions (
\varepsilon>0
) and types (PMF
h(\cdot)
). This is particularly a concern when the sample space is of infinite cardinality (
m=\infty
) since we evaluate infinite sums involving the population PMF
f_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, we impose a finiteness and invertibility assumption, similar to
Lindsay, 1994
and
Victoria-Feser & Ronchetti, 1997
.

Assumption 5
.

The symmetric
(d\times d)
matrices
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)
exist, are finite, and are positive definite in directions
\bm{v}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus{\{\bm{0}\}}
.

Observe that if
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)}\right)
exists for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, then this assumption requires that the matrices
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
exist and are finite as well as positive definite. A necessary condition for this assumption to be satisfied is that all
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)}\right)
are strictly positive if they exist.

The following assumption, which is our final one, imposes a certain notion of local convexity of population risk
L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})
in a neighborhood of estimand
\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

Assumption 6
.

The population risk
L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})
is convex in each coordinate of
\bm{\theta}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
. That is, for each dimension
j=1,\dots,d
,

\ell_{\varepsilon,j,\bm{\theta}_{-j}}(\theta_{j})=L_{\varepsilon}\big((\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{j},\dots,\theta_{d})\big),

which is a univariate function of the
j
-th coordinate of
\bm{\theta}
while keeping the remaining coordinates fixed, is convex for all
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{j},\dots,\theta_{d})^{\top}
in a neighborhood of
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, and attains its minimum at
\theta_{0,j}
.

Requirements like Assumptions
6
on the local behavior of the population risk in a neighborhood of the estimand are common when studying the limit distribution of empirical risk minimizers. For instance,
Van der Vaart, 1998
assumes a variant of Lipschitz continuity in a neighborhood of the population risk minimizer,
Lindsay, 1994
requires certain functions of the parameters to be locally dominated, and
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
impose local convexity of the (in their case univariate) population risk.

We proceed with an intuitive explanation of a certain matrix that plays an important role in the estimator’s limit distribution by uniquely determining its asymptotic covariance matrix (despite not being the latter’s square root matrix).

Fix a
d
-dimensional
\bm{t}\neq\bm{0}
and let
\bm{V}
be a
(d\times d)
matrix that satisfies the equation

\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{Vt}\right)\right)\pm}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{Vt}\right)\right)\pm}\right)\bm{V}\bm{t}=\bm{t}.

(10)

We will later show that although
\bm{V}
may not be symmetric, all of its eigenvalues are real and positive definite under our assumptions.
To provide intuition for this expression and the matrix
\bm{V}
, consider first the unidimensional case
(d=1)
so that
t\neq 0
and
V>0
are scalars. In this case, the direction along which the limit in (
10
) is evaluated is fully determined by the sign of
t
because
V
is always positive, that is,
\kappa(Vt)=\kappa(t)=\textnormal{sign}(t)
. Indeed, if
t>0
, then equation (
10
) is satisfied by the right-limit solution
V=\sqrt{{U}\left({\theta_{0}+}\right)}\big/{M}\left({\theta_{0},\theta_{0}+}\right)
, and if
t<0
it is satisfied by the left-limit solution
V=\sqrt{{U}\left({\theta_{0}-}\right)}\big/{M}\left({\theta_{0},\theta_{0}-}\right)
. It follows that the solution
V
depends on the sign of
t
. For the special case of the (unidimensional) binomial model, such a scalar
V>0
that depends on the sign of
t
and satisfies (
10
) plays a crucial role in the asymptotic theory of
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
.

However, in the multidimensional case
(d>1)
, finding a matrix
\bm{V}
that satisfies equation (
10
) is more involved because the direction along which the limit is evaluated not only depends on vector
\bm{t}
, but also on matrix
\bm{V}
itself. Indeed, the
j
-th direction in the coordinatewise sign function
\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{Vt}\right)
is equal to the sign of the
j
-th coordinate of vector
\bm{Vt}
,
j=1,\dots,d
. Thus, in order to find the desired matrix
\bm{V}
, we need to find the direction
\bm{w}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus{\{\bm{0}\}}
for which the candidate solution

\bm{V}=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w})\pm}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w})\pm}\right)^{1/2}

(11)

satisfies (
10
). Importantly, this solution depends on
\bm{t}
and may change for different
\bm{t}
. Thus, to conclude, given
\bm{t}\neq\bm{0}
, we know the functional form of the solution matrix
\bm{V}
(see previous display), but we do not a priori know the direction
\bm{w}=\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{Vt}\right)
associated with the solution matrix
\bm{V}
satisfying (
10
). Since there is only a finite number of directions here, namely
3^{d}-1
, we can for a given
\bm{t}
calculate candidate solutions in (
11
) for all feasible directions
\bm{w}
and then evaluate which one satisfies the objective equation (
10
). This solution exists under our assumptions because Assumption
5
requires all candidate solutions in (
11
) to exist. A later proposition will show that it is furthermore unique and all of its eigenvalues are real and strictly positive, and we will formalize the process of finding this solution by means of an algorithm. We stress that in dimension
d>1
, matrix
\bm{V}
in (
11
) need not be symmetric positive definite. As such, it is not equal to a square root matrix of a
C
-estimator’s asymptotic covariance matrix, but is nevertheless useful for constructing said asymptotic covariance matrix.

We are now ready to state the asymptotic distribution of
C
-estimators.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
be arbitrary and let the
(d\times d)
matrix
\bm{V_{t}}
be implicitly defined as the solution to the equation

\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{V_{t}t}\right)\right)\pm}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{V_{t}t}\right)\right)\pm}\right)\bm{V_{t}}\bm{t}=\bm{t}.

(12)

Then, under the Assumptions
1
–
6
, it holds true that

\mathbb{P}\left[\sqrt{N}\bm{V_{t}}^{-1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\leq\bm{t}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Phi_{d}(\bm{t}),

where
\Phi_{d}(\cdot)
denotes the cumulative distribution function of the
d
-variate standard normal distribution.
If we in addition assume that
\psi^{\prime}\left(\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\right)
exists for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, then we additionally have that, for
\bm{t}=\bm{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[\sqrt{N}\bm{V_{0}}^{-1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\leq\bm{0}\right]\longrightarrow\Phi_{d}(\bm{0})=0.5,

as
N\to\infty
, where
\bm{V_{0}}
takes the closed-form expression
\bm{V_{0}}=\bm{V_{0}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{1/2}
.

An algorithm to find the implicitly defined matrix
\bm{V_{t}}
in (
12
) is described in Appendix
A.1
. The following proposition establishes the properties of this matrix.

Proposition 1
.

Grant the assumptions of Theorem
2
. For arbitrary fixed
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, the matrix
\bm{V_{t}}
satisfying (
12
) exists, is unique, and all of its eigenvalues are unique and strictly positive.

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2
.

Corollary 1
.

Grant the assumptions of Theorem
2
. Then, under the additional assumption that
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)}\right)
exists for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, it holds true that

\sqrt{N}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}-\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textnormal{N}_{d}\Big(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\Big),

as
N\to\infty
, where, for
\bm{V}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{1/2}
, the asymptotic covariance matrix

\bm{\Sigma}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{V}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\bm{V}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{\top}=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{-1}

is symmetric positive definite.

A consistent estimator of the unobserved asymptotic covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right)
can be constructed as follows. Replace all population class probabilities
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
by their corresponding empirical counterparts
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})
in matrices
\bm{W}\left(\bm{\theta}\right),\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
, and
\bm{\Omega}
. Then exploit the plug-in principle and evaluate
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
and
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
at the point estimate
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}.
Denote the ensuing plug-in estimator by
\bm{\Sigma}\left({\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}}\right)
, which is consistent for
\bm{\Sigma}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right)
by Theorem
1
and the continuous mapping theorem.

It turns out that if the model is correctly specified
(\varepsilon=0)
and
\psi^{\prime}(0)
exists (which it does if the discrepancy function is twice differentiable), then the asymptotic covariance matrix of
all

C
-estimators is equal to the inverse Fisher matrix at the model. We formalize this result in Lemma
B.5
in the appendix. Hence, all
C
-estimators with a twice differentiable discrepancy function are fully efficient, which suggests that robustness can be achieved without having to sacrifice efficiency. To study this apparent lack of a robustness-efficiency tradeoff, the following section is concerned with influence functions.

4.3
Influence function

The maximum likelihood estimator of the postulated model
\bm{p}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
is obtained by choosing
x\mapsto\rho(x)=(x+1)\log(x+1)
in the empirical risk (
3
). The influence function
(
Hampel, 1974
)
of a maximum likelihood estimator
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}^{\mathrm{\ MLE}}
is given by

\mathrm{IF}\left(\bm{z},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}^{\mathrm{\ MLE}},\bm{p}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)\right)=\bm{J}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)^{-1}\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right),\qquad\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}},

where
\bm{J}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)=\sum_{\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)^{\top}
denotes the model’s Fisher informatiom matrix. The influence function describes the bias caused by an infinitesimally small contamination on an estimate
(
Markatou et al., 1997
)
.

The following theorem states the influence function
C
-estimators.

Theorem 3
.

Under the Assumptions of Theorem
2
, the influence function of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
is given by

\mathrm{IF}\left(\bm{z},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N},\bm{p}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)\right)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{IF}\left(\bm{z},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N},\bm{p}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)\right)&\quad\text{ if $\psi^{\prime}(0)$ exists,}\\
\bm{DEN}^{-1}\bm{NUM}&\quad\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\bm{DEN}

\displaystyle=\psi^{\prime}(0-)\bm{J}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)+\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)^{\top}\Big(\psi^{\prime}(0+)-\psi^{\prime}(0-)p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)\Big)\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\bm{NUM}

\displaystyle=\psi^{\prime}(0+)\bm{s}_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{*}\right)+p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}*\right)\Big(\psi^{\prime}(0-)-\psi^{\prime}(0+)\Big).

We close this section by discussing the derived theoretical properties.

4.4
Discussion of the results

If
\psi^{\prime}(0)
exists, then all
C
-estimators have the influence function of the MLE and are therefore fully efficient at the postulated model. On the other hand, an influence function equal to that of the MLE means a lack of first-order robustness. However, many choices of the discrepancy functions are designed to downweight the influence of poorly fitted classes (see Table
1
), so one would expect a robustness gain over the MLE. In contrast to elementary robustness theory
(
Hampel et al., 1986
, e.g.,)
, this suggests that the influence function does not carry all information on a procedure’s robustness properties in the case of categorical data. Hence, when modeling categorical data, it is possible to construct fully efficient
and
robust estimators (cf. Theorem
3
).

If
\psi^{\prime}(0)
does not exist, then it is possible to construct first-order robust estimators, that is, estimators with a smaller influence function than the MLE. For instance, this applies to estimators with the discrepancy function of
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
in (
5
), for the choice
c_{2}=0
. While first-order robustness is in principle appealing, it comes at the significant downside that
C
-estimators with non-existing
\psi^{\prime}(0)
do
not
converge in distribution at the postulated model (Theorem
2
). Curiously, under contamination the same estimator
does
converge in distribution, and is asymptotically Gaussian. Therefore, inference is easier with contaminated samples than non-contaminated ones. This result ties in with
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
, who demonstrated it for the binomial model, and we have shown that it also holds true for general models of categorical data. In general, non-standard asymptotics for estimators with loss functions that are not everywhere twice differentiable have been described before in
Huber, 1964
,
Pollard, 1985
,
Simpson et al., 1987
, and
Huber & Ronchetti, 2009
for location estimation.

5
Outlier diagnostics

In addition to robust estimation, it is often of interest to identify outliers in a given sample. It may not be intuitive what constitutes outlyingness in categorical data, which may only be supported on a finite set and/or non-numerical.
We propose to adopt the philosophy of
Croux & Filzmoser, 2007
and define a categorical outlier as
“an observation which is unlikely to have been generated by the imposed model”
. Equivalent definitions have been proposed in
Davies & Gather, 1993
,
Lindsay, 1994
, and
Markatou et al., 1997
.

With this outlier philosophy, one may identify a categorical cell
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
as outlying if its population probability
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
is statistically significantly different from the model probability at estimand
\bm{\theta}_{0}
,
p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
, which corresponds to the best fit that can be achieved with the postulated model for the chosen discrepancy function. In other words, for a given cell
\bm{z}
, we wish to perform the hypothesis test

\textnormal{H}_{0}:\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=0\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad\textnormal{H}_{1}:\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\neq 0,

(13)

where the alternative hypothesis may be replaced by a one-sided alternative depending on whether one expects outlyingness to manifest in
p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
being larger or smaller than
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
. Such a priori beliefs are implicitly imposed by the choice of discrepancy function. While the discrepancy functions in Table
1
are primarily designed to downweight classes with large Pearson residuals, there exist discrepancy functions that can downweight “inliers”, that is, classes whose Pearson residuals are structurally smaller than 0. Examples are the negative exponential function (see Table
1
, proposed by
Lindsay, 1994
) and the discrepancy function in eq. (
5
) with the choice
c_{1}=0
(see
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
for a discussion).

Ideally, a test for the hypotheses in (
13
) will reject
\textnormal{H}_{0}
if cell
\bm{z}
is outlying, and sustain
\textnormal{H}_{0}
if it is not. It turns out that a test statistic that satisfies these two desirable properties is given by

T_{N}(\bm{z})=\frac{p_{\bm{z}}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right)-f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\bm{z}}^{2}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)\big/N}},

(14)

where
\sigma_{\bm{z}}^{2}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}\left({\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}p_{\bm{z}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
. The following corollary of Theorem
2
establishes the validity of the test statistic
T_{N}(\bm{z})
for testing the null hypothesis
\textnormal{H}_{0}:\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=0
.

Corollary 2

(Limit distribution of test statistic)
.

Grant the assumptions of Theorem
2
and, in addition, assume that
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)}\right)
exists for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
. Then, under the null hypothesis in (
13
), for a given cell
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
the test statistic
T_{N}(\bm{z})
in (
14
) possesses the following limit distribution:

T_{N}(\bm{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textnormal{N}(0,1),

as
N\to\infty
.

In practice, the test statistic is unobserved because neither
\sigma_{\bm{z}}^{2}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
nor
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
are observed. However, these two quantities can be consistently estimated through
\sigma_{\bm{z}}^{2}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right)
(due to Theorem
2
and the continuous mapping theorem) and
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})
, respectively. Hence, in practice, the feasible test statistic

\widehat{T}_{N}(\bm{z})=\frac{p_{\bm{z}}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right)-\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\bm{z}}^{2}\left(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}\right)\big/N}},

(15)

which is consistent for
T_{N}(\bm{z})
by the continuous mapping theorem, may used for testing the null hypothesis in (
13
).

6
Simulation study

The simulation study in this section considers
C
-estimation for the problem of fitting the Poisson model with zero-inflated count data. The inflation of zeros is frequently encountered in count data in biomedical sciences, such as patient days in a hospital, the number of wisdom teeth extracted, or the number of episodes of hypoglycemia (low blood sugar) per year for diabetes patients
(
Lachin, 2014
)
.

Denote by
p_{z}\left(\lambda\right)=\lambda^{z}\exp(-\lambda)/z!
the Poisson PMF with sample space
\mathcal{Z}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}\ni z
and rate parameter
\lambda>0
. We draw a sample
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{N}
of size
N=1,000
from the Poisson PMF with true rate parameter
\lambda_{*}=4.2
. Then, we introduce contamination by randomly replacing a fraction
\varepsilon
of the sample by zeros, where we consider
\varepsilon\in\{0,0.1,0.2,0.3\}
. We generate 1,000 datasets with this process. A particular challenge for estimation is overlap between the contaminated observations and model-generated observations since zeros have a strictly positive probability under the model at the true parameter,
p_{0}\left(\lambda_{*}\right)\approx 0.015
.

Using the contaminated datasets, we fit the Poisson model with five types of
C
-estimators, namely maximum likelihood, Hellinger distance estimation, negative exponential estimation, and the generalized
E
-estimator (GE) (see Table
1
). For the GE, we choose two configurations of tuning constants, namely
c_{1}=-\infty
and
c=c_{2}=0.6
as well as
c_{1}=-\infty
and
c=c_{2}=0
so that we have one case where the estimator is asymptotically Gaussian at the Poisson model (the former), and one where it is not (the latter). The choice
c_{1}=-\infty
follows a suggestion by
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
.

In the no-contamination case (

), all estimators (except the GE for
c=0
) accurately estimate the true
\lambda_{*}
with approximately equal variance. This result is expected because these estimators share the same influence function (Theorem

). The GE for

that this is a simple finite sample issue that vanishes in larger sample sizes.

When contamination is present (
\varepsilon>0
), the estimators deviate noticeably. The MLE is substantially biased already at
\varepsilon=0.1
, and further deteriorates at increasing contamination levels. The Hellinger distance estimator is an improvement to the MLE in terms of robustness, but is still considerably biased, especially at high contamination levels (e.g., bias of about
-0.7
at
\varepsilon=0.3
). The negative exponential is more robust, but still more biased than the two generalized
E
-estimators (GEs), which display excellent robustness properties. In particular, the GE with
c=0
exhibits almost no bias even at the high contamination level of
\varepsilon=0.3
. Also the second GE estimator with the less robust choice
c=0.6
is very robust and only slightly more biased than with
c=0
.

We extend this simulation to the diagnostic test of the previous section in Appendix
D
. In summary, the test has excellent power for the detection of outliers, but its feasible version
\widehat{T}_{N}(\bm{z})
occasionally overrejects at the true model, which is likely due to the additional estimation uncertainty stemming from the use of estimate
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})
. We discuss these results in more detail in Appendix
D
.

While the minimum disparity estimators (Hellinger and negative exponential) offer enhanced robustness compared to MLE, we conclude that the generalized
E
-estimators are most robust in this simulation. In fact, even at 30% contamination, the two considered generalized
E
-estimators have almost no bias. Thus, robust
C
-estimators, particularly generalized
E
-estimators, could be an alternative to existing approaches to zero-inflated count data. Existing approaches, such as the estimator of
Lambert, 1992
, generally model the inflation of zeros explicitly through mixtures of two Poisson distributions. In contrast,
C
-estimators do not explicitly model contamination. As such, they are designed to be robust against arbitrary specification errors of the Poisson model, which may go beyond inflated zero counts.

7
Discussion and conclusion

We have proposed a new class of robust estimators for general models of categorical data, called
C
-estimators.
C
-estimators extend the class of minimum disparity estimators of
Lindsay, 1994
by allowing for risk functions that are not everywhere twice differentiable, which can result in more robust estimators
(
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
, cf.)
. It turns out that such risk functions lead to enhanced first-order robustness if the risk is
not
twice differentiable at the origin. However,
C
-estimators with such a risk function are not asymptotically Gaussian at the postulated model, but, quite surprisingly, are asymptotically Gaussian when contamination is present. In contrast,
C
-estimators whose risk is twice differentiable at the origin are asymptotically Gaussian and fully efficient at the model, suggesting that robustness can be achieved without sacrificing efficiency. In addition, we propose a diagnostic test to identify categorical outliers.

A particularly relevant area of further research are robustness measures for estimators of models for categorical data, like
C
-estimators. The fact that robust estimators can have the same influence function as non-robust maximum likelihood suggests that the influence function may not be an appropriate robustness measure. This aspect is discussed in
Lindsay, 1994
, who argues that the second derivative of the residual adjustment function—a crucial function in estimation with categorical data—carries information on the robustness of such estimators.
Lindsay, 1994
therefore suggests using the second derivative of the residual adjustment function evaluated at 0 as robustness measure, with large negative values indicating greater robustness. However,
Lindsay, 1994
cautions in Remark E that this criterion, while sufficient, may not be a necessary condition for robustness. In addition, this criterion does not exist for first-order robust estimators, whose risk is not twice differentiable at 0. Hence, robustness measures that allow for the comparison of different
C
-estimators are needed. Potentially fruitful approaches could be the
local shift sensitivity
of
Hampel, 1974
or angular breakdown values
(
Zhao et al., 2018
)
.

From a practical perspective, the theory developed in this paper might be particularly useful for robustifying the analyses of questionnaire response data against inattentive responding or bot responses, which is an increasing concern in internet-collected data
(
Ilagan & Falk, 2023
;
Arias et al., 2020
;
Meade & Craig, 2012
, e.g.,)
. We apply our theory to models of response data in the companion paper
Welz et al., 2024
and demonstrate that it yields substantial improvements in terms of robustness against erroneous responses while also helping identify them. Hence,
C
-estimators could aid in making the analysis of categorical data, particularly rating data, less dependent on model assumptions.

A.1
An algorithm to solve the problem

Recall the problem in (
12
). For fixed
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
and fixed
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, let the possibly asymmetric
(d\times d)

\bm{V_{t}}
be implicitly defined as the solution to the equation

\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{V_{t}t}\right)\right)\pm}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\left(\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{V_{t}t}\right)\right)\pm}\right)\bm{V_{t}}\bm{t}=\bm{t}.

(A.1)

The following steps describe an algorithm to find a matrix
\bm{V_{t}}
satisfying (
A.1
) for the fixed
\bm{t}
. It is a brute force algorithm that loops over all candidate directions and terminates when the solution is found.

Step 0.

Draw a direction vector
\bm{v}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. There are
3^{d}-1
such vectors.

Step 1.

Calculate the candidate solution

\bm{V}=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)^{1/2}.

(A.2)

If this candidate solution does not exist because of singularity, go back to Step 0 and try a different direction vector
\bm{v}
.

Step 2.

With the candidate solution
\bm{V}
from the previous step, calculate the direction
\bm{w}=\bm{\kappa}\left(\bm{Vt}\right)
and evaluate if the equation

\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{w})\pm}\right)^{-1/2}\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{w})\pm}\right)\bm{V}\bm{t}=\bm{t}

(A.3)

holds true. If it indeed holds true, terminate the algorithm and report
\bm{V}=\bm{V_{t}}
as solution. Otherwise, if the equation is not satisfied or a factor does not exist, go back to Step 0 and try a different direction vector
\bm{v}
.

The algorithm is based on the fact that candidate matrices
\bm{V}
have a known functional form, given in Eq. (
A.2
), that varies with parameters
\bm{\theta}
and directions
\bm{v}
. Keeping parameter
\bm{\theta}
fixed, there is only a finite number of candidate matrices, collected in set

\bm{\mathcal{V}}=\Big\{\bm{V}(\bm{v})=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}^{(\bm{v})\pm}\right)^{1/2}:\bm{v}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\Big\},

because the number of possible directions is finite here. Then, given
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, the algorithm searches for the matrix
\bm{V}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}
that satisfies the objective equation (
A.1
). Since the objective equation is a function of both
\bm{V}
and
\bm{t}
, different choices of the prespecified
\bm{t}
may yield different solution matrices
\bm{V}
, so this dependence is made explicit in the notation
\bm{V_{t}}=\bm{V}
.

A.2
The solution and its properties

For fixed
\bm{t}\neq\bm{0}
, we have seen in Equations (
11
) and (
A.2
) that the matrix
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
solving problem (
12
) is given by

\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{1/2}

(A.4)

for a certain direction
\bm{w_{t}}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
governed by
\bm{t}
, which can be found by the algorithm in Section
A.1
. In the following, we describe and prove the properties of this matrix.

For a fixed
\bm{t}\neq\bm{0}
, the solution matrix
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
in (
A.4
) is constructed as the product of the symmetric matrices
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{-1}
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{1/2}
, which both exist and are positive definite by Assumption
5
. However, although they are symmetric,
\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0},\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{-1}
and
\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}^{(\bm{w_{t}})\pm}\right)^{1/2}
generally do not commute in dimension
d>1
, so
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
is not necessarily symmetric (and, as such, not symmetric positive definite).

We know from essential linear algebra that the eigenvalues of an asymmetric square matrix need not be real-valued. Yet, it turns out that although
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
is possibly asymmetric, all of its eigenvalues are in fact real-valued
and
strictly positive under our assumptions.

Before we can prove this property, we first need two propositions on the eigenvalue structure of general matrix products. Equivalent statements are likely contained in some linear algebra textbooks.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\bm{A},\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be arbitrary matrices. Then the nonzero (and possibly complex) eigenvalues of the matrix products
\bm{AB}
and
\bm{BA}
coincide.

Proof.

For further reference, denote by
\|\bm{w}\|
the Euclidean norm of a
d
-dimensional vector
\bm{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{d})^{\top}
with complex coordinates, which is defined as
\|\bm{w}\|=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}w_{k}\overline{w_{k}}}
, where
\overline{z}
denotes the complex conjugate of a complex number
z
. Observe that if all coordinates of
\bm{w}
are real-valued, then we recover the well-known expression
\|\bm{w}\|=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d}w_{k}^{2}}
.

Let
\lambda\neq 0
be a nonzero (and possibly complex) eigenvalue of the matrix product
\bm{AB}
and
\bm{v}\neq\bm{0}
be the corresponding (possibly complex) eigenvector, which is—without loss of generality—assumed to be normalized to unit length, that is,
\|\bm{v}\|=1
. Thus, the identity

\bm{AB}\bm{v}=\lambda\bm{v}

holds true. Premultiplying both sides by
\bm{B}/\|\bm{B}\bm{v}\|
yields

\bm{B}\bm{A}\left(\bm{Bv}/\|\bm{Bv}\|\right)=\lambda\left(\bm{Bv}/\|\bm{Bv}\|\right),

so that
\lambda
is also an eigenvalue of
\bm{BA}
with corresponding unit-length eigenvector
\bm{Bv}/\|\bm{Bv}\|
. It follows that the two matrix products
\bm{AB}
and
\bm{BA}
share their nonzero eigenvalues, as claimed.

\blacksquare

Proposition A.2
.

Let
\bm{C}
and
\bm{D}
be
(d\times d)
real-valued symmetric positive definite matrices that need not commute. Then all eigenvalues of the matrix product
\bm{CD}
are real-valued and strictly positive.

Proof.

Since
\bm{C}
is symmetric positive definite, so is its square root matrix
\bm{C}^{1/2}
. It follows that the (real-valued) symmetric matrix
\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\bm{C}^{1/2}
is positive definite because

\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{x}=\left(\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{x}\right)^{\top}\bm{D}\left(\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{x}\right)>0\qquad\text{for all }\qquad\bm{x}\neq\bm{0},

since
\bm{D}
is symmetric positive definite. We know from Proposition
A.1
that the matrices
\bm{CD}=\bm{C}^{1/2}\left(\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\right)
and
\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\bm{C}^{1/2}=\left(\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\right)\bm{C}^{1/2}
have the same nonzero eigenvalues. Because
\bm{C}^{1/2}\bm{D}\bm{C}^{1/2}
is symmetric positive definite, all of its
d
eigenvalues are real and strictly positive. We therefore conclude that also all
d
eigenvalues of
\bm{CD}
must be real and strictly positive.

\blacksquare

The next corollary proves that the possibly asymmetric matrix
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
in (
A.4
) has only real eigenvalues that are all strictly positive. This corollary
follows immediately from Proposition
A.2
in combination with Assumption
5
(ensuring that both matrix factors in the definition of
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
are symmetric positive definite).

Corollary A.1
.

Given
\bm{t}\neq\bm{0}
, all eigenvalues of matrix
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)
in (
A.4
) are real and strictly positive under Assumption
5
.

We stress that if
{\psi^{\prime}}\left({\delta_{\bm{z},\varepsilon}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)}\right)
exists for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{Z}}
, then the solution matrix

in (

) reduces to
\bm{V_{t}}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{V}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)=\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{-1}\bm{U}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}\right)^{1/2}
for all

, including

, the solution matrix

In our proofs of the asymptotic properties of
C
-estimators we sometimes make use of a generic
(d\times d)

\bm{V}

Appendix B
Useful lemmas

This section lists and proves a number of technical lemmas that shall be useful in the proofs of the main theorems.

Lemma B.1
.

For fixed
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a matrix
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with real and strictly positive eigenvalues, and
\bm{\theta}_{0}
as in Assumption

, let
\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right)=\bm{\theta}_{0}+N^{-1/2}\bm{Vt}

,

(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N})_{j}\leq(\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right))_{j}\qquad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,d,

if and only if

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{j}}L_{N}(\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right))\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,d,

with probability tending to one as
N\to\infty

Proof.

As
N\to\infty
,
L_{N}(\bm{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}L_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\theta})
for any
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
, and
\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right)\to\bm{\theta}_{0}
for any
\bm{t}
. It follows by Assumption

is coordinatewise locally convex for all
\bm{\theta}\in\{\bm{\theta}:\|\bm{\theta}\|\leq\|\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right)\|\}

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}
(cf. Theorem
1
), with probability tending to one as

. We are now ready to prove the two implications.

“
\implies
”.

Since
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N}

L_{N}(\bm{\theta},\widehat{f}_{N})\geq L_{N}(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N},\widehat{f}_{N})
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}
. Because
(\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{N})_{j}\leq(\bm{\theta}_{N}\left(\bm{t}\right))_{j},j=1,\dots,d

that the

must be nonnegative,

.

“
\impliedby

By the coordinatewise local convexity of the empirical loss,

\blacksquare

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\bm{M}\left(\bm{\theta}_{0}(\varepsilon)\right),

Case 2:
\bm{\psi^{\prime}(0)}
does not exist.

D.1
Remark

In the results of the simulation study (Figure
2
), the generalized
E
-estimator (GE) for
c=0
seems to exhibit a small bias, which is surprising because it is Fisher consistent, just like the other estimators (Section
4.1
). Further inspection reveals that this is a finite sample issue: In finite samples, the nonparametric estimator
\widehat{f}_{N}(\bm{z})
may only roughly approximate the population PMF
f_{\varepsilon}(\bm{z})
for some classes
\bm{z}
. Hence, the sample Pearson residual
\delta_{\bm{z},N}\left(\bm{\theta}\right)
may slightly exceed the value 0 despite the model being correctly specified. However, since the threshold point is set to
c=0
, classes associated with this Pearson residual will be downweighted in the estimation although they should—in population—not be downweighted, thereby potentially causing a finite-sample bias. This issue has been noted before in
Ruckstuhl & Welsh, 2001
, who suggest to compare the results of choice
c=0
to a choice of
c
that is only slightly larger than 0. We agree with this suggestion and recommend that if one wishes to use a GE with
c=1
, they should additionally use a second GE with
c>0
. If the results are not drastically different—which is unlikely even in heavily contaminated samples (cf. Figure
2
)— the results of the second estimator should be reported. In our experience, the choice
c=0.6
for the second estimator maintains high robustness and is usually sufficient to overcome this finite sample issue in uncontaminated data.
