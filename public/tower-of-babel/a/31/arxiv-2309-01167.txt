Symbolically integrating tensor networks over various random tensors by the second version of Python RTNI
Motohisa Fukuda

Abstract
. We are upgrading the Python -version of RTNI , which symbolically integrates tensor networks over the Haar-distributed unitary matrices [ FKN19 ] . Now, PyRTNI2 can treat the Haar-distributed orthogonal matrices and the real and complex normal Gaussian tensors as well. Moreover, it can export tensor networks in the format of TensorNetwork [ RMG+19 ] so that one can make further calculations with concrete tensors, even for low dimensions, where the Weingarten functions differ from the ones for high dimensions. The tutorial notebooks are found at GitHub : https://github.com/MotohisaFukuda/PyRTNI2 . In this paper, we explain maths behind the program and show what kind of tensor network calculations can be made with it. For the former, we interpret the element-wise moment calculus of the above random matrices and tensors in terms of tensor network diagrams, and argue that the view is natural, relating delta functions in the calculus to edges in tensor network diagrams.

Introduction

\displaystyle\E_{U\sim\mathcal{U}(d)}\left[u_{i_{1},j_{1}}\cdots u_{i_{k},j_{k}}\cdot\overline{u_{i^{\prime}_{1},j^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots u_{i^{\prime}_{k},j^{\prime}_{k}}}\right]

2.
Formulae for element-wise moments of random tensors

This section explains how to calculate element-wise moments of random matrices and tensors of our interest:
the Haar-distributed unitary and orthogonal matrices, and the complex and real normal Gaussian vectors.
Indeed, the Weingarten calculus is used for the first two
[
CŚ06
,
CM09
]
and the Wick formulae for the last two
[
Iss18
,
Wic50
,
MS17
]
.
In addition, notice that realigning tensors, for example in the lexicographic order, yields vectors. As such, real and complex normal Gaussian tensors can be identified as vectors, while it is not the case with orthogonal and unitary matrices, where distinguishing the input and the output spaces is essential. In particular, the following claims on real and complex normal Gaussian vectors can be naturally extended to tensors.
The readers can refer to Appendix
A
for more about the Weingarten functions:
\uwg(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\owg(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Integrating monomials of elements of the unitary and orthogonal matrices, and the complex and real normal Gaussian vectors can be individually stated as follows.

(1)

For a Haar-distributed
d\times d
unitary matrix
M
, and
i_{\ell},i^{\prime}_{\ell},j_{\ell},j^{\prime}_{\ell}\in[d]
with
\ell\in[k]
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\E_{M\sim\mathcal{U}(d)}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdots m(i_{k},j_{k})\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime}_{1},j^{\prime}_{1})\cdots m(i^{\prime}_{k},j^{\prime}_{k})}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in S_{k}}\delta(i_{1},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(1)})\cdots\delta(i_{k},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(k)})\delta(j_{1},j^{\prime}_{\beta(1)})\cdots\delta(j_{k},j^{\prime}_{\beta(k)})\,\uwg(d,(\alpha,\beta))\ .\end{aligned}

When the numbers of
m(\cdot,\cdot)
’s and
\overline{m(\cdot,\cdot)}
’s are different, the integral is trivially zero.

(2)

For a Haar-distributed
d\times d
orthogonal matrix
M
, and
i_{\ell},j_{\ell}\in[d]
with
\ell\in[2k]
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\E_{M\sim\mathcal{O}(d)}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdots m(i_{2k},j_{2k})\right]\\
&=\sum_{p,q\in P_{2k}}\delta(i_{p(1)},i_{p(2)})\cdots\delta(i_{p(2k-1)},i_{p(2k)})\delta(j_{q(1)},j_{q(2)})\cdots\delta(j_{q(2k-1)},j_{q(2k)})\,\owg(d,(p,q))\ .\end{aligned}

When the number of
m(\cdot,\cdot)
’s is odd, the integral is trivially zero.

(3)

For a complex normal Gaussian vector
V\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and
i_{\ell},i^{\prime}_{\ell}\in[d]
with
\ell\in[k]
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{V\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d)}\left[v(i_{1})\cdots v(i_{k})\cdot\overline{v(i^{\prime}_{1})\cdots v(i^{\prime}_{k})}\right]=\sum_{\alpha\in S_{k}}\delta(i_{1},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(1)})\cdots\delta(i_{k},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(k)})\ .\end{aligned}

When the numbers of
v(\cdot)
’s and
\overline{v(\cdot)}
’s are different, the integral is trivially zero.

(4)

For a real normal Gaussian vector
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
i_{\ell}\in[d]
with
\ell\in[2k]
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{V\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{R}}(d)}\left[v(i_{1})\cdots v(i_{2k})\right]=\sum_{p\in P_{2k}}\delta(i_{p(1)},i_{p(2)})\cdots\delta(i_{p(2k-1)},i_{p(2k)})\ .\end{aligned}

When the number of
v(\cdot)
’s is odd, the integral is trivially zero.

Corollary 2.2
.

The third and fourth statements of Theorem
2.1
can be extended to complex and real noraml Gaussian matrices.

(3
\,{}^{\prime}
)

For a
d_{1}\times d_{2}
complex normal Gaussian matrix
M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d_{1},d_{2})
, and
i_{\ell},i^{\prime}_{\ell}\in[d_{1}]
and
j_{\ell},j^{\prime}_{\ell}\in[d_{2}]
with
\ell\in[k]
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d_{1}d_{2})}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdots m(i_{k},j_{k})\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime}_{1},j^{\prime}_{1})\cdots m(i^{\prime}_{k},j^{\prime}_{k})}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\alpha\in S_{k}}\delta(i_{1},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(1)})\cdots\delta(i_{k},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(k)})\cdot\delta(j_{1},j^{\prime}_{\alpha(1)})\cdots\delta(j_{k},j^{\prime}_{\alpha(k)})\ .\end{aligned}

When the numbers of
m(\cdot,\cdot)
’s and
\overline{m(\cdot,\cdot)}
’s are different, the integral is trivially zero.

(4
\,{}^{\prime}
)

For a
d_{1}\times d_{2}
real normal Gaussian matrix
M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{R}}(d_{1},d_{2})
, and
i_{\ell}\in[d_{1}]
and
j_{\ell}\in[d_{2}]
with
\ell\in[2k]
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{R}}(d_{1}d_{2})}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdots m(i_{2k},j_{2k})\right]\\
&=\sum_{p\in P_{2k}}\delta(i_{p(1)},i_{p(2)})\cdots\delta(i_{p(2k-1)},i_{p(2k)})\cdot\delta(j_{p(1)},j_{p(2)})\cdots\delta(j_{p(2k-1)},j_{p(2k)})\ .\end{aligned}

When the number of
m(\cdot,\cdot)
’s is odd, the integral is trivially zero.

Notice that removing Weingarten functions and imposing
\alpha=\beta
or
p=q
will make the first two formulae in Theorem
2.1
into the two in Corollary
2.2
. Such a correspondence will be exemplified in Section
3
.

3.
Random matrices in tensor networks

In this section, we discuss how the integral formulae in Section
2
can be naturally interpreted in tensor network diagrams, where nodes are tensors and edges represent contractions when both ends are connected to tensors, otherwise they are called dangling edges; see
[
BB17
]
. Especially edges connected to no tensors can be regarded as the identities, un-normalized Bell states or their dual operators in the conventional viewpoint of matrix calculus.

Take a tensor network
T
having only nodes, i.e. tensors
T^{(1)},\ldots,T^{(K)}
whose multi-index sets are individually denoted by
I_{1},\ldots,I_{K}
. Then,
T
is the tensor product of those tensors, and for
k\in[K]
pick
i_{k}\in I_{k}
then the
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})
-element is just the product of the corresponding elements;

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{K}T^{(k)},\quad\quad t(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}t^{(k)}(i_{k})\quad\text{ and }\\
&T=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})\\
\in I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{K}\end{subarray}}t(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})\,|i_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{K}\rangle\ .\end{aligned}

Here, without loss of generality, we regard
T
as a vector using the
ket
notation.
Next, suppose each multi-index is written as
i_{k}=(i_{k,1},\ldots,i_{k,L(k)})\in I_{k,1}\times\cdots\times I_{k,L(k)}
where
L(k)
can be one. Now, let us introduce an edge to this diagram. For example, if the spaces corresponding to the index sets
I_{k,\ell}
and
I_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}
(
k<k^{\prime}
) are contracting, where
L(k)=L(k^{\prime})
, then we multiply the following dual operator from the left:

\displaystyle E=\sum_{i=1}^{L(k)}\langle i|\otimes\langle i|\otimes I\ ,

where the two operators
\langle i|
’s, in the
bra
notation, act on those spaces and the identity the rest.
That is, the resulting tensor network is:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &ET=\\
&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})\\
\in I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{K}\end{subarray}}\,\delta(i_{k,l},i_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}})\cdot t(i_{1},\ldots,i_{K})\,|i_{1}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{k}\{\ell\}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{k^{\prime}}\{\ell^{\prime}\}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|i_{K}\rangle\ .\end{aligned}

Here, notice that introducing new edges are equivalent to deleting the relevant canonical basis vectors and multiplying delta functions. The same is true with Theorem
2.1
, where dangling edges which resulted from deleting random tensors will be reconnected, yielding contractions, where newly exposed spaces are to be contracted again for the diagram to balance out.

Let us apply the above calculus to elementary examples. Consider
MAM^{*}
where
A
is a
d\times d
deterministic matrix and
M
is a
d\times d
random matrix. When
M
is real, the adjoint operation is just the transpose operation. Now, one can apply Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.2
to this example with
k=1
, numbering both of
M
and
M^{*}
as
1
for the complex cases and
M
and
M^{*}
as
1
and
2
respectively for the real cases.

(1)

The unitary matrix case: let
\alpha=\beta=\mathrm{id}=(1)\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{M\sim\mathcal{U}(d)}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime}_{1},j^{\prime}_{1})}\right]&=\delta(i_{1},i^{\prime}_{\alpha(1)})\cdot\delta(j_{1},j^{\prime}_{\beta(1)})\,\uwg(d,(\alpha,\beta))\quad\text{ or}\\
\E_{M\sim\mathcal{U}(d)}\left[m(i,j)\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\right]&=\delta(i,i^{\prime})\cdot\delta(j,j^{\prime})\times\frac{1}{n}\ .\end{aligned}

(2)

The orthogonal matrix case: let
p=q=\{\{1,2\}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
, and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{M\sim\mathcal{O}(d)}\left[m(i_{1},j_{1})\cdot m(i_{2},j_{2})\right]&=\delta(i_{p(1)},i_{p(2)})\cdot\delta(j_{q(1)},j_{q(2)})\,\owg(d,(p,q))\quad\text{ or}\\
\E_{M\sim\mathcal{O}(d)}\left[m(i,j)\cdot m(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\right]&=\delta(i,i^{\prime})\cdot\delta(j,j^{\prime})\times\frac{1}{n}\ .\end{aligned}

Here, we replaced the set of indices:
i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2}
by
i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime}
, respectively.

(3)

The complex normal Gaussian case can be deduced trivially from the first statement:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d,d)}\left[m(i,j)\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\right]=\delta(i,i^{\prime})\cdot\delta(j,j^{\prime})\end{aligned}

(4)

The real normal Gaussian case can be deduced trivially from the second statement:

(3.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{R}}(d,d)}\left[m(i,j)\cdot m(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\right]=\delta(i,i^{\prime})\cdot\delta(j,j^{\prime})\end{aligned}

Here, we replaced the set of indices:
i_{1},i_{2}
by
i,i^{\prime}
, respectively.

Thus, the four cases all share the same underlying combinatorics and the integrals are identical apart from the normalization constant. For example, in the complex normal Gaussian case, the
(i,i^{\prime})
-element can be calculated as follows.

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left(\E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d,d)}[MAM^{*}]\right)_{i,i^{\prime}}&=\sum_{j,s,t,j^{\prime}}a(s,t)\cdot\delta(j,s)\cdot\delta(t,j^{\prime})\E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d,d)}\left[m(i,j)\cdot\overline{m(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})}\right]\\
&=\sum_{j,s,t,j^{\prime}}a(s,t)\cdot\delta(j,s)\cdot\delta(t,j^{\prime})\cdot\delta(i,i^{\prime})\cdot\delta(j,j^{\prime})\ ,\end{aligned}

which amounts to

(3.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \E_{M\sim\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{C}}(d,d)}[MAM^{*}]=\trace[A]I\ .\end{aligned}

\trace[B]\,I

.

Finally we briefly discuss how
PyRTNI2
treats random tensors. In the above example, it was crucial to treat
M
s and
M^{*}
s together. More generally, for a random tensor
M
, the four variants
M
,
\bar{M}
,
M^{T}
and
M^{*}
must be handled together; the last two apply only to matrices. For this reason,
PyRTNI2
creates a tensor based on a name. Once a tensor was created, it can be cloned and one of the three operations: transpose, complex conjugate, adjoint can be applied if possible and necessary. In general, tensors are associated with multiple spaces, which are numbered internally. In
PyRTNI2
, tensors are more fundamental than matrices, so
PyRTNI2
provides a wrapper to treat a tensor as a matrix, where the spaces of the tensor will belong to either the output or the input, and will be numbered individually. This numbering system is used to create edges in tensor network diagrams. Other deterministic matrices and tensors can be created in the same way. In fact, one sets a tensor or a matrix to be random just before the symbolic integration, one after another, where
different kinds of random tensors can be placed in the same diagram.
See tutorial files on GitHub for the detail.

Appendix A
Generating Weingarten functions

In this section, we explain the complex and real Weingarten functions, whose values depend on the matrix sizes and Young diagrams, although pairs of permutations or pairs of pair partitions replaced Young diagrams to avoid complication in the main body.

The unitary Weingarten functions are defined to Young diagrams: abusing the notation,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\uwg(d,\mu)

\displaystyle=\uwg(d,(\alpha,\beta))\quad\text{ for }\alpha,\beta\in S_{k}\ ,

where the map
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\mu
is defined by the lengths, in the non-increasing order, of loops of
\alpha^{-1}\beta
,
say,
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots
, giving a Young diagram. Then
[
CŚ06
]
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\uwg(d,\mu)=\frac{1}{(k!)^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash k\\
\ell(\lambda)\leq d\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi^{\lambda}(e)^{2}}{s_{\lambda,d}(1)}\,\chi^{\lambda}(\mu)\ .

Here,

•

The sum runs over all Young diagrams
\lambda
’s of
k
elements whose lengths
\ell(\lambda)
are not more than
d
.

•

\chi^{\lambda}
is the irreducible character of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
,
and
e
is the identity element of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

•

s_{\lambda}(1)
is the Schur polynomial with respect to
\lambda
:

(A.3)

\displaystyle s_{\lambda}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i}{j-i}\ .

Let us write down unitary Weingarten functions for
k=1,2
:

(A.4)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \uwg(d,(1))=\frac{1}{d},\quad\uwg(d,(1,1))=\frac{1}{d^{2}-1},\quad\uwg(d,(2))=\frac{-1}{d(d^{2}-1)}\ .\end{aligned}

The orthogonal Weingarten functions are also defined with respect to individual Young diagrams: again abusing the notation,

(A.5)

\displaystyle\owg(d,\mu)

\displaystyle=\owg(d,(p,q))\quad\text{ for }p,q\in P_{2k}\ .

where the map
(p,q)\mapsto\mu
is defined as follows.
Consider the graph
\Gamma(p,q)
with vertices
[2k]
and with edges
\{p(2\ell-1),p(2\ell)\}
and
\{q(2\ell-1),q(2\ell)\}
for
\ell\in[k]
. Then, the graph consists of loops with lengths, for example,
2\mu_{1}\geq 2\mu_{2}\geq\cdots
, resulting in the Young diagram
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots)
of
k
. To see this, place the vertices
[2k]
on the horizontal straight line, and draw the edges by
p
over the line and those by
q
under. This view gives another way of obtaining the Young diagrams, i.e. just regard
p
and
q
as transpositions in
S_{2k}
such that

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{p}=(p(1),p(2))(p(3),p(4))\cdots\qquad\tilde{q}=(q(1),q(2))(q(3),q(4))\cdots\ .\end{aligned}

Then, the loops of
\tilde{p}^{-1}\tilde{q}
have the following lengths:
\mu_{1},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{2},\ldots
, where the vertices of each loop of
\Gamma(p,q)
are exactly the same as those of certain two loops of
\tilde{p}^{-1}\tilde{q}
. Moreover, notice that the edges of
\Gamma(p,q)
can be replaced by
\{2\ell-1,2\ell\}
and
\{p^{-1}q(2\ell-1),p^{-1}q(2\ell)\}
permuting all vertices by
p^{-1}
.
Finally,
orthogonal Weingarten functions can be written as follows
[
CM09
]
.

(A.7)

\displaystyle\owg(d,\mu)=\frac{2^{k}k!}{(2k)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\vdash k\\
\ell(\lambda)\leq d\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi^{2\lambda}(e)^{2}}{Z_{\lambda}(1)}\omega^{\lambda}(\mu)\ .

Here,

•

The sum is made over Young diagrams
\lambda
’s of
k
whose lengths
\ell(\lambda)
are not more than
d
.

•

\chi^{2\lambda}
is the irreducible character of
\mathcal{S}_{2k}
where
2\lambda
is defined as
(\lambda_{i})_{i}\mapsto(2\lambda_{i})_{i}
. Also,
e
is the identity element of
\mathcal{S}_{2k}
.

•

Z_{\lambda}(1)
is the zonal polynomial:

(A.8)

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda}(1)=\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}(d+2j-i-1)\ .

Let us write down orthogonal Weingarten functions for
k=1,2
:

(A.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \owg(d,(1))=\frac{1}{d},\quad\owg(d,(1,1))=\frac{d+1}{d(d-1)(d+2)},\quad\owg(d,(2))=\frac{-1}{d(d-1)(d+2)}\ .\end{aligned}

Remark A.1
.

Notice that each of (
A.2
) and (
A.7
) gives different formulae for individual pairs

because Young diagrams
\lambda
’s whose length are more than
d
do not appear in the sum. Otherwise,
formally setting

in (
A.4
) and
(
A.9
),
\uwg(1,(1,1))
,
\uwg(1,(2))
,
\owg(1,(1,1))
