Projective Geometry for Perfectoid Spaces
Gabriel Dorfsman-Hopkins

Abstract
To understand the structure of an algebraic variety we often embed it in various projective spaces. This develops the notion of projective geometry which has been an invaluable tool in algebraic geometry. We develop a perfectoid analog of projective geometry, and explore how equipping a perfectoid space with a map to a certain analog of projective space can be a powerful tool to understand its geometric and arithmetic structure. In particular, we show that maps from a perfectoid space X X to the perfectoid analog of projective space correspond to line bundles on X X together with some extra data, reflecting the classical theory. Along the way we give a complete classification of vector bundles on the perfectoid unit disk, and compute the Picard group of the perfectoid analog of projective space.

Introduction

This paper is inspired by the goal of understanding vector bundles on perfectoid spaces, and how they behave under the so called
tilting correspondence
of Scholze
[
16
]
. To do so, we develop a perfectoid analog of projective geometry. We study the perfectoid analog of projective space defined in
[
16
]
, which we call
projectivoid space
and denote by
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
, and show that maps from a perfectoid space
X
to
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
correspond to line bundles on
X
together with some extra data, giving an analog to the classical theory of maps to projective space.

To get to this point we must first understand the theory of line bundles on projectivoid space itself, and in particular, its Picard group. In his dissertation
[
4
]
, Das worked toward computing the Picard group of the projectivoid line,
\mathbb{P}^{1,\mathrm{perf}}
. His proof relied on having certain local trivializations of line bundles, requiring a perfectoid analog of the Quillen-Suslin theorem. Therefore, in order to begin developing the theory of so called projectivoid geometry, we must prove this first.

The Quillen-Suslin theorem says that finite dimensional vector bundles on affine
n
-space over a field are all trivial. Equivalently, all finite projective modules on a polynomial ring
K[T]
are free, where
T
is an
n
-tuple of indeterminates. In rigid analytic geometry, we replace polynomial rings with rings of convergent power series called
Tate algebras
, denoted
K\langle T\rangle
, and it can be shown that over such rings the Quillen-Suslin theorem still holds, that is, all finite projective
K\langle T\rangle
-modules are free
[
14
]
. The analog of these rings for perfectoid spaces is the ring
K\langle T^{1/p^{\infty}}\rangle
of convergent power series where the indeterminates have all their
p
th power roots. The difficulty in extending the theorem to this
perfectoid Tate algebra
is that the ring is no longer noetherian, and so the result cannot be easily reduced to the polynomial case.

In Section
2
we study the commutative ring theoretic properties of the perfectoid Tate algebra, computing its unit group and classifying its projective modules, as well those over its integral subring and residue ring. In particular, we establish the necessary Quillen-Suslin style result.

NameIgnored
(Theorem
2.18
(The Quillen-Suslin Theorem for the Perfectoid Tate Algebra))

Finite projective modules on the perfectoid Tate algebra
K\left\langle T_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,T_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle
are all free. Equivalently, finite dimensional vector bundles on the perfectoid unit disk are all isomorphic to the trivial vector bundle.

This completes Das’ proof, and lays the groundwork to begin studying vector bundles on more general perfectoid spaces.

In Section

.

\Pic\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\cong\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
.

We also compute the cohomology of all line bundles on projectivoid space, completing a computation of Bedi
[
2

In Section

The Perfectoid Tate Algebra

Algebraic geometry studies the polynomial ring and it’s various quotients and localizations, allowing for commutative algebraic facts to be interpreted geometrically and vice-versa. In the world of perfectoid algebras, a natural analog is the perfectoid Tate algebra (defined below), whose commutative algebra controls many of the geometric structures we study. We therefore begin with a careful study of the algebraic object underlying most of this work.

Let
K
be a perfectoid field with pseudouniformizer
\varpi
. In particular
K
is a nonarchimedean field, and so we can define the
Tate algebra

T_{n,K}=K\langle X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\rangle
of convergent power series over
K
. The module theory of the Tate algebra is well understood, in no small part due to Lütkebohmert, see
[
14
]
. Our main object of study in this section is the following.

Definition 2.1
(The Perfectoid Tate Algebra).

The
perfectoid Tate algebra

T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}
is the nonarchimedean completion

T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}=K\left\langle X_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle=\widehat{\bigcup_{n\geq 0}K\left\langle X_{1}^{1/p^{n}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{n}}\right\rangle}\cong\widehat{\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longrightarrow}{lim}}T_{n,K}}.

\clubsuit

Remark 2.2

The perfectoid Tate algebra consists of formal power series over
K
which converge on the perfectoid unit disk. Explicitely
X=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})
be an
n
-tuple, we can write down the elements of this ring.

T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}=\left\{\sum_{\alpha\in\left(\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\geq}0\right)^{n}}a_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}:\text{ for all }\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\text{ only finitely many }|a_{\alpha}|\geq\lambda\right\}.

This ring inherits the Gauss norm,
||\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}||=\sup\{|a_{\alpha}|\}
.

\left(T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
is the subring
\{||f||\leq 1\}
of power-bounded elements of
T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}
, and consists of power series with coefficients in
K^{\circ}
. The ideal
\left(T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ\circ}
of topologically nilpotent elements consist of power series with coefficients in
K^{\circ\circ}
. The quotient is

\tilde{T}_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}:=\left(T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}/\left(T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ\circ}=k\left[X_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right]

where
k=K^{\circ}/K^{\circ\circ}
is the residue field. Notice that every element in the quotient ring is a polynomial, because a power series in
\left(T_{n,K}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
can only have finitely many coefficients of norm 1.

\ast

When there will be no confusion, we omit
K
from the notation.

Remark 2.3
(A Note on Convergence)

Suppose
f=\sum f_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}\in T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
. Morally speaking, saying that the sum converges should mean that evaluating
f
at any point in the perfectoid disk should give an element of
K
. Since are sums are not taken over
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, but rather
(\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{\geq 0})^{n}
, we must be more careful in defining what convergence means. Let us begin by studying
f(1,1,\cdots,1)=\sum f_{\alpha}
. This should converge, so let’s begin by defining partial sums

s_{m}=\sum_{\alpha\in\left(\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{p^{m}}\right)^{n}:0\leq\alpha_{i}\leq m}f_{\alpha}.

If the sequence
(s_{m})
converges, we define the infinite sum to be the limit. Let us check that the convergence of the power series
f
implies convergence of
\sum f_{\alpha}
in this sense. Fixing some
\varepsilon>0
, there are only finitely many
f_{\alpha}
with
|f_{\alpha}|\geq\varepsilon
. Therefore, there is some large
N
such that for each such
f_{\alpha}
we have
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\left(\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{p^{N}}\right)^{n}

0<\alpha_{i}<N
. Therefore, fixing
m\geq r>N
, the differences
s_{m}-s_{r}
have none of the coefficients
f_{\alpha}
with absolute value larger than
\varepsilon
, so that by the nonarchimedean property
|s_{m}-s_{r}|<\varepsilon
. Thus the sum converges to an element
f(1)\in K
.

We remark now that if
|g_{\alpha}|\leq 1
, the same argument would show that
\sum f_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}
also converges. This should imply that
f(x)
converges to a point in
K
whenever
x
is in the perfectoid unit disk, but we defer further discussion until after we have the relevant definitions (see Remark
2.10
).

\ast

We record a useful normalization trick for further use down the line.

Lemma 2.4
(Normalization).

Let
f\in T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
be nonzero. There is some
\lambda\in K
such that
||\lambda f||=1
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

Since only finitely many coefficients in
f
have absolute value above
||f||-\varepsilon
, the supremum of that absolute values of the coefficients is achieved by some
f_{\alpha}
. Taking
\lambda=f_{\alpha}^{-1}
completes the proof.

2.1
The Group of Units

As a first step towards understanding the perfectoid Tate algebra, we compute its group of units.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
f\in T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
with
||f||=1
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

f
is a unit in
\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)
.

(ii)

f
is a unit in
\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
.

(iii)

The image of
\overline{f}
of
f
in
\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
is a nonzero constant
\lambda\in k^{\times}
.

(iv)

|f(0)|=1
and
||f-f(0)||<1
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

(i)
\iff
(ii). An inverse to
f
must have absolute value 1, and therefore would also lie in
\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
.

(ii)
\implies
(iii). The map
\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}\to\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
must send units to units, and the group of units of
\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
is precisely the nonzero constant polynomials. Indeed, the inverse to any element of
\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
would also have to be a polynomial (in
X^{1/p^{m}}
for some
m
), implying that they both must be constants.

(iii)
\iff
(iv). This is immediate.

(iv)
\implies
(i). If
|f(0)|=1
then
f(0)\in K^{\times}\subseteq(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}})^{\times}
. Therefore
1-\frac{f}{f(0)}\in T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
and

\left|\left|1-\frac{f}{f(0)}\right|\right|=||f(0)||\cdot\left|\left|1-\frac{f}{f(0)}\right|\right|=||f(0)-f||<1.

Therefore
1-\frac{f}{f(0)}
is topologically nilpotent, so that by the geometric series converges and hence
\frac{f}{f(0)}
is a unit. Since
f(0)
is too, we can conclude that
f
is a unit.

Corollary 2.6
.

f=\sum f_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}\in\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
is a unit if and only if
|f_{0}|=1
and
|f_{\alpha}|<1
for all
\alpha\not=0
.

\spadesuit

Corollary 2.7
.

f=\sum f_{\alpha}X^{\alpha}\in T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
is a unit if and only if
|f_{\alpha}|<|f_{0}|
for all
\alpha\not=0
.

\spadesuit

Proof.

Using our normalization trick, we know
||\lambda f||=1
for some
\lambda\in K^{\times}
. Then
f
is a unit if and only if
\lambda f
is, if and only if
|\lambda f_{\alpha}|<1=|\lambda f_{0}|
for all
\alpha\not=0
. Canceling shows this holds if and only if
|f_{\alpha}|<|f_{0}|
for all
\alpha\not=0
.

2.2
Vector Bundles on the Perfectoid Unit Disk

In classical algebraic geometry the polynomial ring (and it’s various quotients) form the local building blocks of most of the objects of study. Phrased geometrically, the prime spectrum of the polynomial ring of
n
variables is affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}
which covers or contains many of the spaces of interest. Like schemes which are locally prime spectra of rings, perfectoid spaces are build from perfectoid algebras using the adic spectrum functor of Huber (see
[
10
]
,
[
9
]
, and
[
11
]
). The perfectoid Tate algebra plays the role of the polynomial ring, and the perfectoid unit disk (defined below) plays the role of affine space.

Definition 2.8
.

The
perfectoid unit disk
is the adic space associated to the perfectoid Tate algebra:

\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}=\spa\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}},(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}})^{\circ}\right).

\clubsuit

Remark 2.9

The rigid unit disk is the adic spectrum associated to the Tate algebra. Since the perfectoid Tate algebra is the completed union of Tate algebras, using the
tilde limit
formalism of
[
18
]
, we have

\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\sim\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longleftarrow}{lim}}_{\varphi}\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{ad}},

where
\varphi
is the
p
th power map on coordinates. It is worth noting that the tilde limit is not the categorical inverse limit (since these are not in general unique in the category of adic spaces). Nevertheless, it should be thought of affinoid locally as corresponding to the completed directed limit, and if such a limit exists as a perfectoid space, it is unique among all perfectoid spaces and satisfies the usual universal property (among perfectoid spaces). See
[
18
]
Definition 2.4.1 and subsequent discussion.

\ast

Remark 2.10
(
K
-points of the Perfectoid Disk)

Let us compute the
K
-points of
\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
, and in doing so conclude the discussion of Remark
2.3
. A
K

is a map of adic spaces
x:\spa(K,K^{\circ})\to\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
, which is equivalent to a map of Huber pairs
\varepsilon_{x}:\left(T_{K}^{n,\mathrm{perf}},(T_{K}^{n,\mathrm{perf}})^{\circ}\right)\to(K,K^{\circ})
. This is a commutative diagram of continuous
K
-algebra homomorphisms:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K\left\langle X_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\circ}\left\langle X_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\circ}.}

In particular, it is determined by the image of the
X_{i}^{1/p^{k}}
in
K^{\circ}
. Let
\lambda=\varepsilon_{x}(X_{i})
. Then
\lambda_{1}=\varepsilon_{x}({X_{i}^{1/p})}

p

\lambda
, and
\lambda_{2}=\varepsilon_{x}(X_{i}^{1/p^{2}})

p

\lambda_{1}
. Continuing in this fashion, we see that choosing the image of the

\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longleftarrow}{lim}}_{t\mapsto t^{p}}K^{\circ}=K^{\flat\circ}
. We have therefore computed the

-points of the perfectoid disk:

\mathbb{D}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}(K)=(K^{\flat\circ})^{n}.

The evaluation function
\varepsilon_{x}(f)=f(x)
then amounts to plugging the coordinates of
x
as an element of
(K^{\flat\circ})^{n}
into

, which we saw in Remark

\ast

There is a well known correspondence between finite projective modules over a ring, and finite dimensional (algebraic) vector bundles over the associated affine scheme, and more generally, between vector bundles over a locally ringed space and locally free sheaves on that space (see, for example,
[
8
]
exercise 2.5.18). In
[
19
]
, Serre conjectured that all finite projective modules over the polynomial ring
A=k[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
are free. This can be interpreted geometrically as saying there are no nontrivial algebraic vector bundles over affine space
\mathbb{A}^{n}=\spec A
. In 1976, Quillen
[
15
]
and independently Suslin proved Serre’s conjecture, which is now known as the Quillen-Suslin theorem. Lütkebohmert in
[
14
]
was shortly able to extend the result to the Tate algebra
K\langle X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\rangle
of convergent power series over a complete nonarchimedean field.

In what follows, we prove a perfectoid analog of the Quillen-Suslin theorem. Specifically, we prove that all finite projective modules on the perfectoid Tate algebra are trivial. This will imply that the perfectoid unit disk has no nontrivial finite vector bundles. Along the way we will show that both the subring of integral elements
\left(T_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}\right)^{\circ}
, and the residue ring
\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}
also have no nontrivial finite projective modules. Although these results are not necessary to establish the result for the perfectoid Tate algebra, they will be important in asserting the effectiveness of the Čech cohomology groups of certain sheaves in Section
3
.

2.2.1
Finite Projective Modules on the Residue Ring

Let us begin by proving that finite projective modules are free over the residue ring

\tilde{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{perf}}=k\left[X_{1}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right]=\bigcup_{m}k\left[X_{1}^{1/p^{m}},\cdots,X_{n}^{1/p^{m}}\right].

To see this, we first briefly review a (non unique) correspondence between projective modules and idempotent matrices.

Let
R

\pi:R^{n}\to P
, as well as a section of this projection

\sigma\circ\pi

Suppose

\heartsuit

Proof.

To a finite projective

Corollary 2.12

Let

Every

\spadesuit

As

-module

is the base extension of some

3
Line Bundles and Cohomology on Projectivoid Space

In classical algebraic geometry, the notion of
projective geometry
is a very powerful tool to study properties of varieties and schemes. Indeed, one can learn a lot about a scheme by understanding its maps to various projective spaces, and this theory is intimately connected to the theory of line bundles on that space. In this and the following section we develop an analogous theory for perfectoid spaces. Let us begin by defining a perfectoid analog of projective space.

Definition 3.1
(The Projectivoid Line).

Analogously to the construction of the Riemann sphere, we can build the perfectoid analog of the projective line by gluing two copies of the perfectoid unit disk along the perfectoid unit circle.

Explicitly, the inclusion
K\left\langle T^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\to K\left\langle T^{\pm 1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle
corresponds to the open immersion
\mathbb{S}^{1,\mathrm{perf}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{1,\mathrm{perf}}
. The map
K\left\langle T^{-1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\to K\left\langle T^{\pm 1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle
also corresponds to an open immersion of the circle into the disk, where now the disk has coordinate
T^{-1}
. Identifying the circles on each of these disks and gluing produces
the projectivoid line
, denoted
\mathbb{P}^{1,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
.

\clubsuit

Definition 3.2
(Projectivoid Space).

As with the projectivoid line, we can define projectivoid
n
-space by gluing together
n+1
perfectoid unit
n
-polydisks along their associated perfectoid sphere exteriors as in Example
3.1
. This equips projectivoid space with a cover by perfectoid unit disks which we will henceforth refer to as the
standard cover
.

In
[
17
]
Section 7, Scholze showed that we could also define projectivoid space in the following way. Let
\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}
be projective space over
K
, which can be viewed as an adic space as in by first being viewed as a rigid space using the rigid analytification functor, and then as an adic space as in
[
9
]
. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}
be the morphism given in projective coordinates by
(T_{0}:\cdots:T_{n})\mapsto(T_{0}^{p}:\cdots:T_{n}^{p})
. Then

\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\sim\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longleftarrow}{lim}}_{\varphi}\mathbb{P}_{K}^{n}.

As with the perfectoid disk “
\sim\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longleftarrow}{lim}}
” is the ‘tilde limit’ of
[
18
]
.

\clubsuit

Remark 3.3

Notice that all the finite intersections of the standard cover will be the adic spectra associated to what we called perfectoid Laurent series algebras in the previous section. In particular, Theorem
2.19
shows that all their finite vector bundles are free.

\ast

Scholze showed in
[
16
]
that the construction of projectivoid space is compatible with the tilting functor. In this section we begin our exploration of so called
projectivoid geometry
by developing the theory of line bundles on projectivoid space. In particular, we compute the Picard group of
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
, as well as the sheaf cohomology of all line bundles. We continue developing the theory in the following section, where we will show how an arbitrary perfectoid space’s maps to projectivoid space is intimately connected to its theory of line bundles, reflecting the situation in classical algebraic geometry, but with an extra arithmetic twist.

3.1
The Picard Group of Projectivoid Space

The main result of this section follows:

Theorem 3.4
.

\Pic\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\cong\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
.

\diamondsuit

We begin by outlining our proof. We use that for any ringed space
X
, there is a natural isomorphism
\Pic(X)\cong\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{*})
(see for example
[
8
]
Exercise III.4.5). Let
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}\subset\mathscr{O}_{X}
be the subsheaf of integral functions, defined by the rule

U\mapsto\{f\in\mathscr{O}_{X}(U):|f(x)|\leq 1\text{ for all }x\in U\}.

It is a sheaf of rings, and we let
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*}
be it’s unit group. We begin in Section
3.1.1
by establishing an isomorphism

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*}).

Let
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{++}\subset\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}
be the sheaf of topologically nilpotent functions, defined by the rule

\mathscr{O}_{X}^{++}:U\mapsto\{f\in\mathscr{O}_{X}(U):|f(x)|<1\text{ for all }x\in U\}.

It is a sheaf of ideals in
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}
and the quotient we denote by
\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
. The map
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*}\to\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{*}
induces a map on cohomology,
\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*})\longrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{*})
. The latter is easier to understand, and we finish Section
3.1.1
by directly computing that it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
. Composing all this gives us a map

\varphi:\Pic X\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}[1/p].

In Section
3.1.2
we show that
\varphi
is an isomorphism by deforming invertible modules along the sheaves of algebras
\mathscr{A}_{d}=\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}/\varpi^{d}
, which live between
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}
and
\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
.

We make frequent use of the fact projectivoid space comes equipped with a standard cover by perfectoid disks whose geometry we understand well due to the results of Section
2
. In particular, any line bundle on
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
becomes trivial on the standard cover and its various finite intersections due to Theorem
2.19
. We therefore use Čech cohomology with respect to this cover to study line bundles on projectivoid space. The passage between Čech and sheaf cohomology is safe due to the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

There are natural isomorphisms

•

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{*})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{*})
.

•

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+*})
.

•

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\mathscr{A}_{d}^{*})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{A}_{d}^{*})
for all
d
.

•

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{*})\cong\check{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\mathfrak{U},\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{*})
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{R}
be one of the following sheaves of rings:
\mathscr{O}_{X},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+},\mathscr{A}_{d}
, or
\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
, and let
\mathscr{R}^{*}
be the associated sheaf of units. The Čech to Derived spectral sequence (
[
1
]
Exposé V Théorème 3.2) describes the following spectral sequence:

E_{2}^{p,q}:\check{\mathrm{H}}^{p}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{H}^{q}(\mathscr{R}^{*}))\implies\mathrm{H}^{p+q}(X,\mathscr{R}^{*}).

Let
U
be a finite intersection of elements in the standard cover. Then either Theorem
2.19
, Corollary
2.17
, or Corollary
2.12
implies that

has no nontrivial invertible modules. In particular

With this in hand, we can study what happens to cohomology classes reducing modulo the sheaf of topologically nilpotent functions. Indeed, the surjection
\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}\to\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}
induces a map on unit groups

The source we know is
\Pic X
by Lemmas

and

Lemma 3.7
.

\mathrm{H}^{1}(X,\tilde{\mathscr{O}}_{X}^{*})\cong\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

By Lemma

. We will make use of projective coordinates

3.2
Cohomology of Line Bundles

In his Ph.D. thesis
[
2
]
, Bedi computed the cohomology of some of the twisting sheaves
\mathscr{O}(d)
on projectivoid space. His proof was modeled on the computation for classical projective space in Ravi Vakil’s algebraic geometry notes (
[
21
]
Chapter 18.2), but doesn’t explicitly take into account the completions involved, and only includes the case for
n=2
. Instead, we adapt the proof from EGA III
[
7
]
which relates Čech cohomology to the Koszul complex. We also fill in the result, computing the cohomology of every line bundle in every degree. Our rings are going to be topologically
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
graded, and for a graded ring
A
we will denote by
A_{d}
the degree
d
part.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
X=\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
be projectivoid space, and
\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\in\Pic X
an arbitrary line bundle. Then:

1.

If
d\geq 0
,

\mathrm{H}^{0}\left(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\right)=K\left\langle T_{0}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,T_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle_{d}.

2.

If
d<0
, then
\mathrm{H}^{n}\left(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\right)
is the completion of the
K
vector space generated by monomials of degree d, where the degree of each indeterminate is strictly negative, that is:

\mathrm{H}^{n}\left(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\right)=\left\langle T_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\cdots T_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\hskip 5.0pt\big|\hskip 5.0pt\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]_{<0}\text{ and }\sum\alpha_{i}=d\right\rangle\widehat{}

3.

In all other cases,

\mathrm{H}^{r}\left(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\right)=0.

In particular:

h^{r}\left(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}(d)\right)=\begin{cases}1&r=d=0\\
\infty&r=0\text{ and }d>0\\
\infty&r=n\text{ and }d<0\\
0&\text{ all other cases}\end{cases}.

\diamondsuit

Proof.

We will leverage that colimits of abelian groups are exact, so that cohomology of finite complexes of abelian groups commute with arbitrary direct sums. We will therefore study the Čech sequence associated to the sheaf

\mathscr{H}=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]}\mathscr{O}_{X}(d).

Let
A=K\left\langle T_{0}^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,T_{n}^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle
, and
\mathfrak{U}=\{U_{i}\}
be the standard cover of
X
.

\check{\mathrm{C}}^{r}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{H})=\prod_{0\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}\widehat{A_{T_{i_{1}}\cdots T_{i_{r}}}}.

Since the differentials commute with degree and cohomology commutes with direct sums, we can conclude that

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{O}_{X}(d))=\check{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{H})_{d}.

Furthermore, all finite intersections of the
U_{i}
are affinoid, and vector bundles on affinoids are acyclic (
[
12
]
Theorem 1.4.2). Then the Čech to derived spectral spectral sequence degenerates to an isomorphism between Čech cohomology and sheaf cohomology, so we have reduced to computing these Čech cohomology groups. We first consider the 0th cohomology.

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{0}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{H})=\bigcap_{i=0}^{n}\widehat{A_{T_{i}}}=A.

This proves the first statement of the theorem. For the second, we consider the sequence

C^{*}(A):\hskip 10.0pt0\longrightarrow\prod A_{T_{i}}\longrightarrow\prod A_{T_{i}T_{j}}\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow A_{T_{0}\cdots T_{n}}\longrightarrow 0.

In each case, there is a countable fundamental system of neighborhoods of zero, given by
(\varpi^{n})
, so that proceeding by induction from the left to the right, we see that completion on this sequence commutes with taking cohomology (see, for example,
[
20
]
tag 0AMQ). In particular,

\check{\mathrm{H}}^{i}(\mathfrak{U},\mathscr{H})=\widehat{\mathrm{H}^{i}\left(C^{*}(A)\right)}.

Let’s analyze
H^{n}(C^{*}(A))
. Notice that
A_{T_{0}\cdots T_{n}}
is the
K
vector space generated by monomials
T_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\cdots T_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
. The image of the
(n-1)
st differential is the
K
vector space generated by monomials where at least one of the
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
. Therefore
H^{n}(C^{*}(A))
, which is the cokernel of this differential, is the
K
vector space generated by monomials where each
\alpha_{i}<0
. Taking completions proves the second statement of the theorem.

For the third statement of the theorem, the cases of
r<0
and

are trivial, so we assume
0<r<n
. We will show that

since the completion of 0 is 0. We point out that for any

,

A_{f}\cong\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longrightarrow}{lim}}\left(A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot f}}{{\longrightarrow}}A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot f}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\right).

For all

, let

. Then

is an

In particular, the homology groups

for all

we can also look at the dual Koszul complex, and take the colimit as

goes to infinity, with the above identification to the localization in mind.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{s}:}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda^{2}A^{n+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}

4
Maps to Projectivoid Space

Suppose
S
is a scheme over
K
. Then well known correspondence between maps from
S\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and globally generated line bundles on
S
together with a choice of
n+1
generating global sections (see for example
[
8
]
Theorem II.7.1). In this section we will prove an analog of this correspondence for perfectoid spaces.

Definition 4.1
.

To a perfectoid space
X
over
K
, we associate a groupoid
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
whose objects consist of tuples
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s^{(i)}_{j},\alpha_{i}\right)
for
i\geq 0
and
j=0,\cdots,n
, where
\mathscr{L}_{i}
are line bundles on
X
,
s^{(i)}_{0},\cdots,s^{(i)}_{n}\in\Gamma(X,\mathscr{L}_{i})
are generating global sections, and
\alpha_{i}:\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{L}_{i}
are isomorphisms mapping
\left(s^{(i+1)}_{j}\right)^{\otimes p}\mapsto s_{j}^{(i)}
. Morphisms are isomorphisms of line bundles which are compatible with the global sections and isomorphisms
\alpha_{i}
.

If
f:X\to Y
is a
K
-morphism, we get a pullback functor
f^{*}:\mathfrak{L}_{n}(Y)\to\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
, so that
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
is a category fibered in groupoids.

\clubsuit

Remark 4.2

We will show that
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
is actually representable by a perfectoid space, and therefore a stack for the analytic topology.

\ast

Remark 4.3

Note that if some
\alpha_{i}
exists, it is unique. Indeed, for each
i
the global sections
s_{j}^{(i)}
generate
\mathscr{L}_{i}
, so that an isomorphism
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}\longrightarrow\mathscr{L}_{i}
shows that the global sections
\left(\sigma_{j}^{(i+1)}\right)^{\otimes p}
generate
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}
. In particular, the isomorphism is completely determined by the images of these global sections.

\ast

Remark 4.4

For each
i
, the data
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s^{(i)}_{j}\right)
corresponds to a map to a projective space (as a rigid analytic variety), so that objects of the category
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
correspond to
p
th power root systems of maps to projective space.

\ast

The main result of this section is that the category
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
parametrizes
K
-morphisms
X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
. In particular, viewing
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
as a functor to sets we construct a natural isomorphism
\Hom(\cdot,\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}})\cong\mathfrak{L}_{n}
of functors from perfectoid spaces over
K
to sets. First we introduce a bit of notation.

Notation 4.5

Denote by
m_{i}:\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i+1})^{\otimes p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})
the isomorphism of line bundles on
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
coming from multiplying factors together.

\ast

We now state the main theorem of this section (compare to
[
8
]
Theorem II.7.1).

Theorem 4.6
.

The functor
\mathfrak{L}_{n}
is represented by projectivoid space.

Explicitly, the natural transformation
\Hom(\cdot,\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}})\to\mathfrak{L}_{n}
, which evaluated on
X
takes
\phi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
to the tuple
\left(\phi^{*}\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i}),\phi^{*}T_{j}^{1/p^{i}},\phi^{*}m_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
is an isomorphism of functors.

\diamondsuit

Since
\left\{T_{j}^{1/p^{i}}\right\}_{j=0}^{n}
generates
\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})
, we have that
\left\{\phi^{*}\left(T_{j}^{1/p^{i}}\right)\right\}_{j=0}^{n}
generates
\phi^{*}\left(\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})\right)
. Furthermore, the standard isomorphisms
\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i+1})^{\otimes p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})
coming from multiplying factors together send
(T_{j}^{1/p^{i+1}})^{\otimes p}
to
T_{j}^{1/p^{i}}
, so pulling back these isomorphisms along
\phi
gives us an element of
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
. We construct an inverse to this transformation in Section
4.2
, but first we will need a bit of setup.

4.1

-Distinguished Open Sets

For this section we let
X
be an adic space,
\mathscr{L}
a line bundle on
X
, and

global sections of
\mathscr{L}
which generate it at every point. Let

generates

be the

set of the section

. Then the map
\mathscr{O}_{X}\to\mathscr{L}
determined by

is an isomoprhism on the stalks of every point of

, and therefore restricts to an isomorphism on

. We suggestively denote the inverse by

. Let’s validate this notation with the following lemma.

.

and

are inverses to each other, we win.

Lemma 4.9
.

The
\mathscr{L}
-distinguished open sets
X_{i}=X\left(\frac{s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}}{s_{i}}\right)
for
i=1,\cdots,n
are open and cover
X
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

The openness of
X_{i}
follows because it is in fact a
rational
open in the adic space
D(s_{i})
, which is open in
X
. To show these cover
X
, fix some
x\in X
. We already know the
D(s_{i})
cover
X
, because the
s_{i}
generate
\mathscr{L}
. Therefore
I=\left\{i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}:x\in D(s_{i})\right\}
is nonempty and finite. Order the elements of
I
via
i\leq j
if
|(s_{i}/s_{j})(x)|\leq 1
. Notice that for any
i,j\in I
, we have
i\leq j
or
j\leq i
. Indeed, applying Lemma
4.7
together with the multiplicativity of the valuation given by
x
, we have either
|(s_{i}/s_{j})(x)|\leq 1
or
|(s_{j}/s_{i})(x)|\leq 1
. Also if
i\leq j
and
j\leq k
then

|(s_{i}/s_{k})(x)|=|(s_{i}/s_{j})(x)|\cdot|(s_{j}/s_{k})(x)|\leq 1,

so that
i\leq k
. Finally, notice that if
i\leq j
and
j\leq i
then we have
|(s_{i}/s_{j})(x)|=|(s_{j}/s_{i})(x)|=1
. Therefore we can choose (not necessarily uniquely) some
r
which is maximal with respect to this ordering. Then
|(s_{i}/s_{r})(x)|\leq 1
for all
i\in I
. For all other
i
, we have
x\notin D(s_{i})
so that
|(s_{i}/s_{r})(x)|=0\leq 1
. Therefore
x\in X_{r}
, completing the proof.

Example 4.10

The standard cover of
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
by perfectoid unit disks consists of the
\mathscr{O}(1)
-distinguished open sets
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\left(\frac{T_{0},\cdots,T_{n}}{T_{i}}\right)
.

\ast

The following lemma which implies that if
\mathscr{L}^{\otimes p}\cong\mathscr{M}
and
s
and
t
are global sections of
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{M}
respectively, with
s^{\otimes p}=t
, then
D(s)=D(t)
. In particular, using the notation of Definition
4.1
, this implies that if the
s^{(i)}_{j}
generate
\mathscr{L}_{i}
, then the
s^{(i+1)}_{j}
generate
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m})
be a local ring, and
M,N
invertible
R
-modules such that
M^{\otimes r}\cong N
for a positive integer
r
. Let
f\in M
, and
g\in N
such that under this identification
f^{\otimes r}=g
. Then if
g
generates
N
,
f
generates
M
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

We show the contrapositive. If
f
does not generate
M
, then by Nakayama’s lemma,
f\in\mathfrak{m}M
. Thus
f=a\cdot s
for some
a\in\mathfrak{m}
and
s\in M
. But then under the appropriate identification,

g=f^{\otimes r}=(a\cdot s)^{\otimes r}=a^{r}\cdot s^{\otimes r}\in\mathfrak{m}^{r}N\subseteq\mathfrak{m}N.

Therefore
g
cannot generate
N
.

4.2
Construction of the Projectivoid Morphism

We can now finish the proof of Theorem
4.6
by constructing an inverse to the natural tranformation from the theorem. The result follows from the following proposition.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
X
be a perfectoid space over
K
and
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s_{j}^{(i)},\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
. There is a unique
K
-morphism
\phi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
such that

\left(\phi^{*}\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i}),\phi^{*}T_{j}^{1/p^{i}},\phi^{*}m_{i}\right)\cong\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s_{j}^{(i)},\alpha_{i}\right).

\heartsuit

Proof.

Let
X_{j}=X\left(\frac{s_{0}^{(0)},\cdots,s_{n}^{(0)}}{s^{(0)}_{j}}\right)
be the cover of
X
by
\mathscr{L}_{0}
-distinguished opens. Let
U_{j}=\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}\left(\frac{T_{0},\cdots,T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n,perf}
be the standard cover by affinoids. The

are isomorphic to the perfectoid unit polydisk, and are naturally identified with

\spa\left(K\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle,K^{\circ}\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\right).

We build
\phi
locally from maps
\phi_{j}:X_{j}\to U_{j}
. Since
U_{j}
is affinoid, it is equivalent to build a map of Huber pairs,

\left(K\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle,K^{\circ}\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma_{j}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\mathscr{O}_{X}(X_{j}),\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}(X_{j})\right).

That is, a ring map

K\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma_{j}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Gamma(X_{j},\mathscr{O}_{X}),

satisfying

\gamma_{j}\left(K^{\circ}\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\right)\subseteq\Gamma\left(X_{j},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}\right).

We define
\gamma_{j}
on generators by the rule

\gamma_{j}\left(\left(\frac{T_{r}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{i}}\right)=\frac{s^{(i)}_{r}}{s_{j}^{(i)}}.

To make sure this is a ring homomorphism, we must check that

\left(\frac{s^{(i+1)}_{r}}{s^{(i+1)}_{j}}\right)^{p}=\frac{s^{(i)}_{r}}{s^{(i)}_{j}}.

First notice that, under the identification
\alpha_{i}:\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}\cong\mathscr{L}_{i}
, the following diagram commutes (keeping in mind that the horizontal maps are not homomorphisms).
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{O}_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{O}_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{L}_{i+1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{L}_{i}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x\mapsto x^{p}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s_{j}^{(i+1)}}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{s_{r}^{(i+1)}}{s_{j}^{(i+1)}}\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left(s_{j}^{(i+1)}\right)^{-1}\left(s_{r}^{(i+1)}\right)\right)^{p}

\displaystyle=

\left|\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{T_{i}}{T_{j}}\right)(x)\right|=\left|\frac{s_{i}^{(0)}}{s_{k}^{(0)}}(x)\right|\leq 1,

so that

\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{T_{i}}{T_{j}}\right)\in\Gamma\left(X_{j},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}\right).

The multiplicativity of the valuation associated to

shows the same holds for all

\gamma_{j}\left(K^{\circ}\left\langle\left(\frac{T_{0}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}},\cdots,\left(\frac{T_{n}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{\infty}}\right\rangle\right)\subseteq\Gamma\left(X_{j},\mathscr{O}_{X}^{+}\right).

is a

Finally, we check that these morphisms glue to a map
\phi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}

\gamma_{j}
and
\gamma_{k}
are equal as maps from
\Gamma(U_{j}\cap U_{k},\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}})\longrightarrow\Gamma(X_{j}\cap X_{k},\mathscr{O}_{X})
. That is, that

\gamma_{j}\left(\left(\frac{T_{k}}{T_{j}}\right)^{1/p^{i}}\right)=\gamma_{k}\left(\left(\frac{T_{j}}{T_{k}}\right)^{1/p^{i}}\right)^{-1}.

With our notation, this boils down to

\frac{s_{k}^{(i)}}{s_{j}^{(i)}}\cdot\frac{s_{j}^{(i)}}{s_{k}^{(i)}}=1.

But this is just Lemma

.

The rest is immediate from the construction. Since
\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n\mathrm{perf}}}(d)
is generated by the monomials of degree

, the construction shows that
\phi^{*}\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})=\mathscr{L}_{i}
, and that
\phi^{*}\left(T_{j}^{1/p^{i}}\right)=s_{j}^{(i)}
. Furthermore, any map
\psi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
with these properties is by definition given affinoid locally on the standard cover of the target by the ring map
(T_{j}/T_{k})^{1/p^{i}}\mapsto s^{(i)}_{j}/s^{(i)}_{k}
. That is,
\psi|_{X_{i}}=\phi_{i}
so that
\psi=\phi
.

4.3
The Positive Characteristic Case

If
X
is a perfectoid space of characteristic
p
, then the Frobenius morphism
Frob:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m},x\mapsto x^{p}
is an isomorphism. Therefore the
p
th power map on
\Pic X
is an isomorphism as well, since it is
H^{1}(X,Frob)
. This means that given
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s^{(i)}_{j},\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
, the
\mathscr{L}_{i}
for
i>0
are uniquely determined by
\mathscr{L}_{0}
. Similarly, since
X
is perfect, the map
\gamma_{i}
constructed in the proof of Proposition
4.2
is completely determined by where
T_{r}/T_{i}
goes for each
r\not=j
, because the
p
th roots of the image are unique. We summarize this in the following corollary.

Corollary 4.13
.

If
X
is a perfectoid space over
K
of characteristic
p
, a map
X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
is equivalent to a line bundle
\mathscr{L}
on
X
and global sections
s_{0},\cdots,s_{n}
that generate
\mathscr{L}
, or equivalently, to a map to classical projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
(as a rigid analytic variety).

\spadesuit

We can now leverage the tilting equivalence to say that maps to
X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}
in any characteristic are governed by a single line bundle on
X^{\flat}
. Indeed, by the tilting equivalence we have that
\Hom(X,\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K})=\Hom(X^{\flat},\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K^{\flat}})
. This implies the following corollary to Theorem
4.6
.

Corollary 4.14
.

If
X
is a perfectoid space over
K
of any characteristic, a map
X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
is equivalent to a single line bundle
\mathscr{L}
on
X^{\flat}
together
n+1
global sections generating
\mathscr{L}
.

\spadesuit

Using this corollary as an intermediary, we get a natural and geometric correspondence between certain inverse systems of line bundles on
X
and single line bundles on
X^{\flat}
.

Corollary 4.15
.

An element of
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
is equivalent to the a line bundle
\mathscr{L}\in\Pic X^{\flat}
together with
n+1
generating global sections.

\spadesuit

This will be a useful tool in understanding the relationship between
\Pic X
and
\Pic X^{\flat}
.

5
Untilting Line Bundles

Recall that one of our motivations was to understand the behavior of vector bundles under the tilting equivalence. In this section, we use the tools of projectivoid geometry developed in Section
4
to compare the Picard groups of a perfectoid space
X
and its tilt
X^{\flat}
. Indeed, the theory of maps to projectivoid space allows us to pass between line bundles on
X
and
X^{\flat}
by choosing (compatible) generating sections, constructing the associated map to projectivoid space, and then using the tilting equivalence to pass across characteristics. We remark that the theory of
pro-étale cohomology
on perfectoid spaces allows us to make this comparison cohomologically, but the geometric theory we developed in the previous section gives us a firm geometric grasp.

5.1

Definition 5.1

and
\mathscr{L}_{i}=\phi^{*}\mathscr{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}}(1/p^{i})
, we have reduced to proving the proposition for
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
. Explicitly, we must show

\theta_{\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}}(\mathscr{O}(1))=(\mathscr{O}(1),\mathscr{O}(1/p),\mathscr{O}(1/p^{2}),\cdots).

Since
\Pic\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}=\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
, and is therefore uniquely
p
-divisible, it is enough to show that

\theta_{0,\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}}\mathscr{O}(1)=\mathscr{O}(1).

Now
\theta_{0}
is the cohomological map associated to the Teichmuller map
\sharp:\mathbb{G}_{m}^{\flat}\longrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{m}
. Scholze showed in
[
16
]
Proposition 5.20 that the Teichmuller map on the perfectoid Tate algebra maps
T_{i}\mapsto T_{i}
. View
\theta_{0}
as a map on Čech cohomology with respect to the standard affine covers, and view
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}},\mathbb{G}_{m})
as descent data for building a line bundle (and similarly for the tilt). Then we see that
\sharp
sends descent data for
\mathscr{O}(1)
(which is monomials of degree one), to monomials of degree one, which build
\mathscr{O}(1)
on
\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
.

Therefore untilting line bundles via maps to projectivoid is a well defined process, as it agrees with the cohomological method which does not depend on the choice of sections.

We split the study of whether this bijects into the two cases of injectivity and surjectivity. We first analyze each case and see where the difficulties may lie.

•

Injectivity
: Suppose
\mathscr{L},\mathscr{M}\in\Pic(X^{\flat})
are globally generated. Suppose choosing sections and untilting the associated maps to projectivoid space gives us maps
\phi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
and
\psi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{r,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
. If
\phi^{*}(\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i}))\cong\psi^{*}(\mathscr{O}(1/p^{i})=:\mathscr{L}_{i}
for all
i
, can we conclude that
\mathscr{L}\cong\mathscr{M}
? We can attack this using the methods of Section
4
by considering the tuples
(\mathscr{L}_{i},s^{(i)}_{j},\alpha_{i})\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
and
(\mathscr{L}_{i},t^{(i)}_{j},\beta_{i})\in\mathfrak{L}_{r}(X)
associated to
\phi
and
\psi
respectively. If the
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
agree, we can consider
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},\left\{s^{(i)}_{j},t^{(i)}_{k}\right\},\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathscr{L}_{n+r+1}(X)
and consider how the associated map
X\to\mathbb{P}^{n+r+1,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
tilts. In this section we settle the case where
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
, and solve the general case in Section
5.3
.

•

Surjectivity
: Suppose
(\mathscr{L}_{0},\mathscr{L}_{1},\cdots)
are globally generated with
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}\cong\mathscr{L}_{i}
, and there are global sections
s_{j}^{(i)}
generating
\mathscr{L}_{i}
such that
\left(s_{j}^{(i)}\right)^{\otimes p}=s_{j}^{(i)}
. Then passing through the maps to projective space we get
\mathscr{L}\in\Pic X^{\flat}
which maps to
(\mathscr{L}_{0},\mathscr{L}_{1},\cdots)
under
\theta
. But, can we always find sections
s_{j}^{(i)}
and isomorphisms such that
\left(s_{j}^{(i+1)}\right)^{\otimes p}=s_{j}^{(i)}
? Restated, are there generating global sections of
\mathscr{L}_{0}
all of whose
p
th power roots exist? If so, our correspondence surjects.

In the rest of this section we settle injectivity in the case where the isomorphisms
\mathscr{L}_{i+1}^{\otimes p}\cong\mathscr{L}_{i}
agree for the two sets of sections.

correspond to maps
\phi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}

5.3
Injectivity of
\theta

With these tools in hand, we can prove the injectivity of
\theta
for certain perfectoid spaces
X
. We will first need one more lemma.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s_{j}^{(i)},\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
correspond to a map
\phi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K}
. Fix
\lambda=(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\cdots)\in\Gamma(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{\flat*})
, that is,
\lambda_{i+1}^{p}=\lambda_{i}
, so that
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},\lambda_{i}s_{0}^{(i)},\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
corresponds to a map
\psi:X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}_{K}
. Then
\phi=\psi
.

\heartsuit

Proof.

Multiplication by
\lambda_{i}
for each
i
gives us an isomorphism
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s_{j}^{(i)},\alpha_{i}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},\lambda_{i}s_{0}^{(i)},\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)
in
\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
. Then we are done by Theorem
4.6

Before we state the main theorem we make the following definition.

Definition 5.12
.

A line bundle
\mathscr{L}
on a perfectoid space
X
is said to be
weakly ample
if for any other line bundle
\mathscr{M}
, there is some
N>>0
such that for all
r>N
we have
\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{L}^{r}
globally generated.

\clubsuit

Theorem 5.13
.

Suppose
X
is a perfectoid space over
K
. Suppose that
X
has a weakly ample line bundle and that
H^{0}(X_{\overline{K}},\mathscr{O}_{X_{\overline{K}}})=\overline{K}
, where
\overline{K}
is a fixed algebraic closure of
K
. Then

\theta:\Pic X^{\flat}\hookrightarrow\operatornamewithlimits{\underset{\longleftarrow}{lim}}_{\mathscr{L}\mapsto\mathscr{L}^{p}}\Pic X.

In particular, if
\Pic X
has no
p
torsion, then

\theta_{0}:\Pic X^{\flat}\hookrightarrow\Pic X.

\diamondsuit

Proof.

Fix
\mathscr{L},\mathscr{M}\in\Pic X^{\flat}
with
\theta(\mathscr{L})=\theta(\mathscr{M})
. We first reduce to the case that
\mathscr{L},\mathscr{M}
are globally generated. Indeed, letting
\mathscr{F}
be a weakly ample line bundle, we have
\theta(\mathscr{L}\otimes\mathscr{F}^{N})=\theta(\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{F}^{N})
. If the result holds for globally generated line bundles, for large enough
N
we conclude that
\mathscr{L}\otimes\mathscr{F}^{N}\cong\mathscr{M}\otimes\mathscr{F}^{N}
so that
\mathscr{L}\cong\mathscr{M}
.

Next we prove it for the case where
K
contains all
p
th power roots for all its elements. Choose generating sections
s_{0},\cdots,s_{n}
for
\mathscr{L}
and
t_{0},\cdots,t_{r}
of
\mathscr{M}
, which give us maps

\phi^{\flat}:X^{\flat}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K^{\flat}},

and

\psi^{\flat}:X^{\flat}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r,\mathrm{perf}}_{K^{\flat}},

respectively. These untilt to

\phi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n,\mathrm{perf}}_{K},

and

\psi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{r,\mathrm{perf}}_{K},

which in turn correspond to tuples
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},s_{j}^{(i)},\alpha_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{n}(X)
and
\left(\mathscr{L}_{i},t_{j}^{(i)},\beta_{i}\right)\in\mathfrak{L}_{r}(X)
. Notice that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
differ by an element

\lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{Isom}(\mathscr{L}_{i},\mathscr{L}_{i})=\Gamma(X,\mathscr{O}_{X}^{*})=K^{*}.

That is,
\alpha_{i}=\lambda_{i}\beta_{i}
. Choose
p
th power roots

for each

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{-1/p}t_{j}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}^{-1/p}\lambda_{0}^{-1/p^{2}}t_{j}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{j}^{(i+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{-1/p}\lambda_{i-1}^{-1/p^{2}}\cdots\lambda_{0}^{-1/p^{i+1}}t_{j}^{(i+1)}

Then

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\left(\left(\tilde{t}_{j}^{(i+1)}\right)^{\otimes p}\right)
