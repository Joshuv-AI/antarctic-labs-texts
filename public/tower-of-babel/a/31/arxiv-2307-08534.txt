Odd degree rational characters and the order of rational elements in finite groups
Nicola Grittini

Abstract
We prove that, in a finite group, if every rational irreducible character has odd degree, then all rational elements are 2 2 -elements, as it was originally conjectured by P. H. Tiep and H. P. Tong-Viet.



1
Introduction

Let
G
be a finite group. An irreducible character
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
is said to be
rational
if
\chi(x)\in\mathbb{Q}
for each
x\in G
. On the other hand, an element
x\in G
is said to be
rational
in
G
if it is
G
-conjugate to every generator of the cyclic group
\langle x\rangle
, and the conjugacy class
x^{G}
is said to be rational if
x
is rational.

It is known that all the irreducible characters of a group
G
are rational if and only if all the conjugacy classes of
G
are rational, while the principal character is the only rational irreducible character of
G
if and only if
G
has odd order and, therefore, it does not have any rational elements other then the identity. Moreover, it is proved in
[
5
]
that a finite group
G
has exactly two rational irreducible characters if and only if it has exactly two rational conjugacy classes and, in this case, both rational irreducible characters have odd degree. On the other hand, it is easy to see that, if
G
has exactly two rational conjugacy classes, then the non-trivial rational class needs to consist of elements of order
2
. Hence, it makes sense to conjecture, as it was done by P. H. Tiep and H. P. Tong-Viet in
[
7
]
, that, if every rational irreducible character of a group
G
has odd degree, then all rational elements of
G
have order a power of
2
. In this paper, we prove this conjecture.

Main Theorem
.

Let
G
be a finite group. If every rational irreducible character of
G
has odd degree, then all rational elements of
G
are
2
-elements.

The conjecture was already proved in
[
7
]
for a fairly large number of cases. In fact, it was proved that, if all the rational irreducible characters of a finite group
G
have odd degree and no simple group
\operatorname{PSL}_{2}(3^{f})
, with
f
odd, is involved in
G
, then
G
is solvable. Then, the conjecture was proved in this solvable context. In this paper, we are able to generalize the approach adopted in
[
7
]
for solvable groups, itself partially borrowed from
[
5
]
, to the non-solvable case.

As it was already noted in
[
7
]
, the converse of our Main Theorem does not hold, since the dihedral group
D_{8}
has a rational irreducible character of degree
2
. On the other hand, however, the proof for the solvable case presented in
[
7
]
implies that, if a group
G
has a rational element of order
p
, then it has a rational
B_{p}
-character, according to the definition introduced by Isaacs in
[
4
]
. It should not be too difficult to prove that, in this case, the converse actually holds and, if
G
has a rational
B_{p}
-character, then it has a rational
p
-element (and it is also fairly easy to see that a rational
B_{p}
-character, for
p
odd, must be of even degree). Of course, the fact that
B_{p}
-characters are defined only for
p
-solvable groups strongly limits the extent to which we could apply this property. If, however, we can find a way to extend the definition of
B_{p}
-characters also for non-solvable groups, as our Lemma
2.4
might suggest, then we may find a new interesting relation between rational characters and rational elements in finite groups.

2
Results

We first need to prove a result on the extension of characters in non-abelian factor groups. We begin with a proposition on
character triples isomorphisms
(see
[
3
, Definition 11.23]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(G,N,\varphi)
and
(\Gamma,A,\lambda)
be isomorphic character triples and suppose that
A\leq\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\lambda
is linear. Let
p
be a prime such that
p\mid o(\lambda)
, then
p\mid\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
.

Proof.

Suppose
p\nmid\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
, let
P/N
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G/N
and let
Q/A
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
\Gamma/A
corresponding to
P/N
under the isomorphism of character triples. Since
p\nmid\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
, we have by
[
3
, Corollary 6.28]
that
\varphi
extends to
\hat{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(P\!\mid\!\varphi)
. Let
\hat{\lambda}\in\operatorname{Irr}(Q\!\mid\!\lambda)
corresponding to
\hat{\varphi}
under the isomorphism of character triples, then it follows from
[
3
, Lemma 11.24]
that
\hat{\lambda}(1)/\lambda(1)=\hat{\varphi}(1)/\varphi(1)=1
and, thus,
\lambda
extends to
Q
. In particular, the
p
-part
\lambda_{p}\neq 1_{A}
of
\lambda
also extends to
Q
and it follows from
[
3
, Theorem 6.26]
that
\lambda_{p}
extends to
\Gamma
, in contradiction with the assumption that
A\leq\Gamma^{\prime}
.
∎

For a finite group
S
, we will write
M(S)
to denote the
Schur multiplier
of
S
. The reader can see
[
3
, Definition 11.12]
for a definition of Schur multiplier.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
N\lhd G
. Let
p
be a prime and let
\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
be a
G
-invariant character such that
\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
is a power of
p
. Suppose that
p\nmid\left|M(G/N)\right|
, then
\varphi
extends to
G
.

Notice that the corollary holds also if we replace
p
with a set of primes
\pi
.

Proof.

Let
(\Gamma,A,\lambda)
be a character triple isomorphic to
(G,N,\varphi)
such that
A\leq\operatorname{Z}(\Gamma)
,
A\leq\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\left|A\right|=\left|M(G/N)\right|
(see
[
3
, Theorem 11.17, Corollary 11.20 and Theorem 11.28]
). Then it follows from Proposition
2.1
that
\lambda=1_{A}
and we have by
[
3
, Theorem 11.7]
that
\varphi
extends to
G
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
N\lhd G
such that
G/N\cong S_{1}\times...\times S_{k}
for some non-abelian simple groups
S_{i}
,
i=1,...,k
. Let
p
be a prime and suppose that
p\nmid\left|M(S_{i})\right|
for each
i\in\{1,...,k\}
. Suppose that
\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(N)
is
G
-invariant and that
\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
is a power of
p
. Then,
\varphi
has an irreducible extension
\chi
to
G
such that
o(\chi)=o(\varphi)
.

Proof.

We prove it by induction on
k
. Let
N\leq S\lhd G
such that
S/N\cong S_{k}
, then it follows from Corollary
2.2
that
\varphi
has an extension
\hat{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(S)
. Since
S/N
is perfect,
\hat{\varphi}
is the only extension of
\varphi
to
S
and it follows that it is
G
-invariant. Moreover,
\det(\hat{\varphi})
is the unique extension to
S
of
\det(\varphi)
and it follows that
o(\hat{\varphi})=o(\varphi)
. Hence,
\hat{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Irr}(S)
is a
G
-invariant character and
\hat{\varphi}(1)o(\hat{\varphi})=\varphi(1)o(\varphi)
is a power of
p
. Since
G/S\cong S_{1}\times...\times S_{k-1}
, it follows by induction that
\hat{\varphi}
has an extension
\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G)
such that
o(\chi)=o(\hat{\varphi})=o(\varphi)
, and
\chi_{N}=\hat{\varphi}_{N}=\varphi
.
∎

The following lemma is, de facto, an extension of
[
5
, Lemma 6.2]
for (some) non-solvable groups. Since we are not working with
p
-solvable groups, we cannot just use the properties of Isaacs’
B_{p}
-characters, as it is done in
[
5
]
. However, we prove that some properties of those characters still hold in our non-solvable context. It may be also possible to extend the definition of
B_{p}
-characters to non-solvable groups, by eventually imposing some conditions, on the group structure, which are weaker then the
p
-solvability. This, however, would go too far beyond the scope of our paper.

We should also mention that a weaker result then the following, rather technical lemma may have been sufficient for our purposes as well. However, our lemma could be of interest also of its own, as an extension of
[
5
, Lemma 6.2]
, and for this reason the author decided to include it here.

We should first introduce some notation. For
n\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathbb{Q}_{n}
to denote the
n
th-cyclotomic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., the extension of
\mathbb{Q}
by a primitive
n
th-root of unity. Moreover, if
G
is a group,
a\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
,
U\leq G
and
\varphi\in\operatorname{Irr}(U)
, then we define
(U,\varphi)^{a}=(U^{a},\varphi^{a})
. Notice that, in this case,
\varphi^{a}\in\operatorname{Irr}(U^{a})
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

be a finite group, let

and suppose that, for each subgroup

such that

and

. Moreover, suppose that, for any automorphism

Moreover, let

of order

th-roots of unity, and write

, then

, and

Finally, assume that there is no element

of order

for every
a
-invariant subgroup

, then also both

and

have values in

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on
\left|G:M\right|
. Let
T=I_{G}(\varphi)
be the inertia subgroup of

in
G
and suppose first that
T<G
. By induction, there exists

and
\theta\in\operatorname{Irr}(W\!\mid\!\tau)
such that
\theta^{T}=\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(T\!\mid\!\varphi)
, then
\chi=\psi^{G}=(\theta^{T})^{G}
is irreducible and it lies over
\varphi
. In particular, if
\tau
has values in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
, then by induction so does
\theta
and, therefore, so does
\chi
, too. Moreover, if
c
acts as an automorphism on
G
preserving

and it fixes
\chi
, then
\varphi^{c}=\varphi^{g}
for some
g\in G
, since both
\varphi
and
\varphi^{c}
are irreducible constituents of
\chi_{M}
. In particular,
x=cg^{\sminus 1}
normalizes
M
and fixes
\varphi
. It follows that
T^{x}=T
and, since both
\chi
and
\varphi
are fixed by

, then
x
also fixes
\psi
. It follows by induction that
(W,\theta)^{x}=(W,\theta)^{h}
for some
h\in T
and, therefore,
(W,\theta)^{c}=(W,\theta)^{hg}
.

Finally, suppose that
\tau^{a}=\tau^{\sigma}
, where
a
is the automorphism of
G

\theta^{a}=\theta^{\sigma}
. Notice that, for
g\in G

(\theta^{a})^{G}(g)=\frac{1}{\left|W\right|}\sum_{x\in G}\theta^{\circ}(x^{a^{\sminus 1}}g^{a^{\sminus 1}}(x^{a^{\sminus 1}})^{\sminus 1})=\frac{1}{\left|W\right|}\sum_{x\in G}\theta^{\circ}(xg^{a^{\sminus 1}}x^{\sminus 1})=\theta^{G}(g^{a^{\sminus 1}})

and, therefore,
(\theta^{a})^{G}=(\theta^{G})^{a}
. Since also
(\theta^{\sigma})^{G}=(\theta^{G})^{\sigma}
, we have that
\chi^{a}=\chi^{\sigma}
.

From now on, we can assume that
\varphi
is
G
-invariant. Let
\mathcal{H}\leq\mathrm{Aut}(G)
consist of all the automorphisms of

leaving
M
invariant, so that

, and let

be minimal such that
\mathcal{H}
leaves
L
invariant. Notice that

is either an elementary abelian group or it is the direct product of some non-abelian simple groups.

Let
g\in L
. Then
\varphi^{g}=\varphi
, because
\varphi
is
G
-invariant, and it follows from the hypotheses that
(U,\tau)^{g}=(U,\tau)^{h}
for some

. Let

and

, then we have that

and, thus,

. It follows that

. Moreover, we have that

, since
\tau^{T\cap M}
is irreducible, and it follows that
T/U\cong L/M
.

We claim that there exists
\gamma\in\operatorname{Irr}(T\!\mid\!\tau)
such that
\gamma(1)o(\gamma)
is a power of

and
\gamma^{L}=\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(L\!\mid\!\varphi)
. We also claim that, if

fixes
\psi
, then
(T,\gamma)
and

are conjugate in

. Moreover, if

and
\tau^{a}=\tau^{\sigma}
, we claim that also

and
\gamma^{a}=\gamma^{\sigma}
. Finally, if
\tau
has values in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and there is no element

of order

\gamma
has also values in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.

If

is either a

. We have that
[(\gamma^{L})_{M},\varphi]=[(\gamma_{U})^{M},\varphi]=[\tau^{M},\varphi]=1
, thus,

is a constituent of
(\gamma^{L})_{M}
. However,
\gamma^{L}(1)=\left|L:T\right|\gamma(1)=\left|M:U\right|\tau(1)=\varphi(1)
and it follows that
\gamma^{L}=\psi\in\operatorname{Irr}(L)
is an extension of
\varphi
. If

fixes
\psi
, then it also fixes
\varphi
and, by hypothesis,

for some

; it follows that

and fixes

. In particular,

and fixes

(because of the uniqueness properties of

. Finally, if

and

, then

. By the uniqueness property, if

, then also

Assume now that

is an abelian

are powers of

. By

, there exists a unique subgroup

is fully ramified in

. Since

, there exists

such that

. Moreover, if we apply

, we have that there exists a unique subgroup

with

having a

-invariant extension
\hat{\tau}\in\operatorname{Irr}(H_{2})
, and
\hat{\tau}
is fully ramified in

. Now, by proceeding as in the previous paragraph we have that

to

. Since
\hat{\tau}
is

It is proved in

We can now assume that

is generated by a field automorphism

of odd order dividing

and

be its unique rational extension to

that

, then

and

is odd. Suppose

Now,

The following proposition is a rather direct consequence of
