1.
Introduction

The distribution of the Hilbert transform of the indicator of a set
V\subset\mathbb{R}

is only a function of the measure
\lvert V\rvert
of
V
.
This is a long standing fact, and we will recall it and recent developments below.

There is no corresponding known fact for discrete variants of the Hilbert transform. We establish such results for certain square functions: An
L^{p}
norm of a (discrete) square function of
\mathbf{1}_{V}
is necessarily large on
V
itself.

The most natural setting for such an inequality is that of discrete martingales. We do this in the next section. We then state and prove a result for square functions associated to wavelets.
The concluding section includes some history, and points to some closely related questions.

2.
Martingales

The natural setting for this paper is that of discrete martingales.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space,
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
an increasing sequence of
\sigma
-algebras, generated by
atoms. Thus, elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are unions
of disjoint sets
A\in\mathcal{F}_{n+1}
, with
\mathbb{P}(A)>0
.

Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, the associated martingale, and martingale difference sequence are defined by

(2.1)

f_{n}=\mathbb{E}(f\mid\mathcal{F}_{n}),\qquad d_{n}=f_{n}-f_{n-1}.

The square function of
f
is
Sf^{2}=\sum_{n}d_{n}^{2}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

There is an
\eta>0
so that for all martingales
as above, and
V\subset\Omega
of positive probability, there holds

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}\geq\eta\mathbb{P}(V).

The approach is to assume that (
2.3
) fails for some choice of probability space
\Omega
, filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, corresponding martingale
\{d_{n}\}

and set
V
. From this, we establish that
\eta>0
admits an absolute lower bound.
Take
\{X_{n}\}
to be a sequence of Bernoulli random variables of parameter
p
, independent of the martingale
and define

(2.4)

\phi=\sum_{n}X_{n}d_{n}.

We consider the function
\chi(p)=\mathbb{E}\phi^{3}
, as a polynomial in
p
.
It is exactly a quadratic polynomial in
p
, and we will show that it is approximately
a cubic polynomial in
p
. From this, we will be able to deduce the Theorem.

Lemma 2.5
.

We have
\chi(p)=M_{1}p+M_{2}p^{2}
, where

(2.6)

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}d_{n}^{3},

(2.7)

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=3\sum_{m<n}\mathbb{E}d_{m}d_{n}^{2}.

Proof.

Expand
\phi
into martingale differences and form the third power. It is a sum of terms

(2.8)

\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{3}X_{n_{j}}d_{n_{j}}

for integers
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}
. Observe that if the maximal
n_{j}
occurs just once,
the expectation over
\Omega
is zero. Thus, the maximal
n_{j}
must occur either
exactly twice, or all three times. If it occurs all three times, the
expectation leads to the term
M_{1}p
. If it occurs twice, we get a second degree term in
p
,
with coefficient
M_{2}
above.
∎

We now use the hypothesis that (
2.3
) fails. To ease our considerations,
we will write
A\simeq_{\eta}B
if
\lvert A-B\rvert\leq C\eta^{t}\mathbb{P}(V)

for some absolute choice of constants
C,t>0
. We will not keep track of them, and they can change from time to time.

Lemma 2.9
.

Assume that (
2.3
) fails. Then,
we have

(2.10)

\chi(p)\simeq_{\eta}(\mathbb{P}(V)+2M_{1})p^{3}-3M_{1}p^{2}+M_{1}p.

And,
\chi(\tfrac{1}{2})\simeq_{\eta}\tfrac{1}{8}\mathbb{P}(V)
.

Proof.

Denoting expectation over the space for the Bernoulli random variables by
\mathbb{E}_{X}
, note that pointwise on
\Omega
,

\mathbb{E}_{X}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}=p(1-p)(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}
.
By the well known
L^{p}
bound for the square function, we also have

\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\simeq_{\eta}0
.
That means that

\mathbb{E}\lvert\phi\mathbf{1}_{V}-p\mathbf{1}_{V}\rvert^{3}\simeq_{\eta}0
.
Indeed, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\lvert\phi\mathbf{1}_{V}-p\mathbf{1}_{V}\rvert^{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\lvert\phi\mathbf{1}_{V}-p\mathbf{1}_{V}^{2}\rvert^{3}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl\lvert\mathbf{1}_{V}\sum_{n}(X_{n}-p)d_{n}\Bigr\rvert^{3}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{V}\Bigl[\sum_{n}d_{n}^{2}\Bigr]^{3/2}\simeq_{\eta}0.

Above, we write
\mathbf{1}_{V}=\mathbf{1}_{V}^{2}
, and expand one copy of
\mathbf{1}_{V}
in the martingale differences. Then appeal to Khitchine’s inequality for balanced Bernoulli random variables to introduce the square function.

Continuing, write

(2.14)

\displaystyle\chi(p)=\mathbb{E}\phi^{3}\mathbf{1}_{V}+\mathbb{E}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega\setminus V}.

The first term is approximately
p^{3}\mathbb{P}(V)
.
For the second we note that

(2.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\mathbf{1}_{\Omega\setminus V}

\displaystyle\simeq_{\eta}\mathbb{E}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{3}(X_{n_{j}}-p)d_{n_{j}}.

In the expectation, if the maximal integer
n_{j}
occurs only once,
the expectation over
\Omega
is zero. Thus, the maximal must occur
twice, or three times.

If the maximal
n_{j}
occurs exactly twice,
a balanced Bernoulli random variable occurs once, and hence has expectation zero.
The remaining case requires
n_{1}=n_{2}=n_{3}
. That expression gives us

(2.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\mathbb{E}(X_{n}-p)^{3}d_{n}^{3}

\displaystyle=p(1-p)(1-2p)M_{1}

(2.18)

\displaystyle=(p-3p^{2}+2p^{3})M_{1}.

Recall that the third moment of a balanced Bernoulli random variable is
p(1-p)(1-2p)
.
Above, the expression
M_{1}
is defined in (
2.6
).
And, note that the expression above vanishes if
p=1/2
.

∎

To conclude, note that we have the approximate polynomial identity

(2.19)

(\mathbb{P}(V)+2M_{1})p^{3}-(M_{2}+3M_{1})p^{2}\simeq_{\eta}0.

Using this for two values of
\tfrac{1}{2}<p<1
, we see that the coefficients
above must be small.

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V)+2M_{1}\simeq_{\eta}M_{2}+3M_{1}\simeq_{\eta}0.

Now, take the case of
p=\tfrac{1}{2}
. We have

(2.21)

\displaystyle 0\simeq_{\eta}\tfrac{1}{8}\mathbb{P}(V)-\tfrac{1}{2}M_{1}-\tfrac{1}{4}M_{2}.

These last three approximate equations in
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
and
\mathbb{P}(V)
then imply
\mathbb{P}(V)\simeq_{\eta}0
.
That is, we have
\mathbb{P}(V)=C\eta^{t}\mathbb{P}(V)
, for a
bounded choice of constant
C
, and
0<t<1
.
This shows that
\eta
has an absolute lower bound.

Remark 2.22
.

Professor Jill Pipher
[
9
]
points out that Lemma
2.5
simplies if the martingale has symmetric differences.
Namely, and using the notation of that Lemma,
M_{1}=0
and
M_{2}=3\mathbb{E}\mathbf{1}_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}
, so that one
can just set
p=1
.

3.
Wavelet Square Function

The martingale inequality implies the same result for the dyadic square function, in any dimension.
We believe a similar result holds for a wide gamut of square functions.
The point of this section is to modify the argument for martingales to address a wavelet square function.

We do not strive for the greatest generality. Let
w
be a
C^{2}
function, satisfying

(3.1)

\lvert w(x)\rvert\leq C_{r}(1+\lvert x\rvert)^{-r},\qquad r>1,\ x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a choice of dyadic intervals, and assume that

\{w_{I}\mid I\in\mathcal{D}\}
is an
L^{2}
orthonormal basis, where

(3.2)

w_{I}(x)=\lvert I\rvert^{-1/2}w\big((x-c_{I})/\lvert I\rvert\big),

and
c_{I}
is the center of
I
. The corresponding wavelet square function is

(3.3)

Sf^{2}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}}\langle f,w_{I}\rangle^{2}w_{I}^{2}.

Theorem 3.4
.

There is a constant
\eta>0
so that for all sets
V\subset\mathbb{R}
of finite measure, we have

(3.5)

\int_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}\;dx\geq\eta\lvert V\rvert.

We turn to the proof.
We assume that the inequality above fails, and derive a lower bound
for
\eta
.
We work with a random wavelet projection of
\mathbf{1}_{V}
.
Let
\{X_{I}\mid I\in\mathcal{D}\}
be independent Bernoulli random variables
with parameter
0<p<1
. Set

(3.6)

\phi=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}X_{I}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle w_{I}.

Then, define
\chi(p)=\mathbb{E}\int\phi^{3}\;dx
.

Lemma 3.7
.

We have
\chi(p)=W_{1}p+W_{2}p^{2}+W_{3}p^{3}
, where

(3.8)

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{3}w_{I}^{3}\;dx,

(3.9)

\displaystyle W_{2}

\displaystyle=3\,\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I_{1}\neq I_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{1}}\rangle^{2}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{3}}\rangle w_{I_{1}}^{2}w_{I_{3}}\;dx,

(3.10)

\displaystyle W_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}\\
\textup{$I_{j}$ distinct}\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{j}}\rangle w_{I_{j}}\;dx.

The term
W_{3}
does not enter into the martingale case.

Proof.

Write the third power as

(3.11)

\displaystyle\chi(p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}X_{I_{j}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{j}}\rangle w_{I_{j}}\;dx.

The sum above is divided into cases according to the number of
distinct choices of the
I_{j}
. First, if all the
I_{j}
agree,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}X_{I}^{3}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{3}w_{I}^{3}\;dx=pW_{1}.

The probability that
X_{I}=1
is
p
.
Second, if two of the
I_{j}
agree, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle 3\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I_{1}=I_{2},I_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}X_{I_{j}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{j}}\rangle w_{I_{j}}\;dx=W_{2}p^{2}

Third, we have the case where all the
I_{j}
are distinct, which is the

p^{3}
term.

∎

We now use the hypothesis that (
3.5
) fails. We use the same notation
\simeq_{\eta}
as before.

Lemma 3.14
.

Assume that (
3.5
) fails. Then,

(3.15)

\chi(p)\simeq_{\eta}p\lvert V\rvert+p(1-p)(1-2p)W_{1},

W_{1}
is defined in (
3.8
).

Proof.

The wavelet square function is bounded on
L^{12}
, say. See
[
3
]
*§9.2.
Interpolating with the
L^{2}
bound, we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\left[\int_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\;dx\right]^{1/3}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbf{1}_{V}S\mathbf{1}_{V}\rVert_{2}^{3/5}\lVert S\mathbf{1}_{V}\rVert_{12}^{2/5}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{3/10}\lvert V\rvert^{1/3}.

We have
\mathbb{E}\phi=p\mathbf{1}_{V}
.
Pointwise, we have the square function estimate

(3.18)

\mathbb{E}\lvert\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V}\rvert^{2}\lesssim(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}.

From Khintchine inequality, it follows that
\phi\mathbf{1}_{V}
is very close to

p\mathbf{1}_{V}
;
indeed the inequalities of (
2.12
) hold in this setting.
Thus,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\chi(p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\int\mathbf{1}_{V}\phi^{3}\;dx+\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus V}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\;dx

(3.20)

\displaystyle\simeq_{\eta}p^{3}\lvert V\rvert+\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\;dx.

Expand the second term to

(3.21)

\mathbb{E}\int\sum_{I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}\in\mathcal{D}_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{3}(X_{I_{j}}-p)\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I_{j}}\rangle w_{I_{j}}\;dx.

If any
I_{j}
occurs a single time, the expectation is zero by independence.
Hence, the only contribution is when
I_{1}=I_{2}=I_{3}
.
Thus, recalling the third moment of a balanced Bernoulli,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi-p\mathbf{1}_{V})^{3}\;dx

\displaystyle=p(1-p)(1-2p)\int\sum_{I}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{3}w_{I}^{3}\;dx

(3.23)

\displaystyle=p(1-p)(1-2p)W_{1}.

∎

An easy argument shows that
W_{1}
is small.

Lemma 3.24
.

We have
W_{1}\simeq_{\eta}0
,
where
W_{1}
is defined in (
3.8
).

Proof.

Set

(3.25)

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\bigl\{I\in\mathcal{D}\colon\lvert\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle\rvert\geq\eta^{1/3}\sqrt{\lvert I\rvert}\bigr\}.

For
I\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, we have
\lvert I\cap V\rvert\gtrsim\eta^{1/3}\lvert I\rvert
. Thus,

(3.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\eta^{-2/3}\int_{V}(S\mathbf{1}_{V})^{2}\;dx\leq\eta^{1/3}\lvert V\rvert.

It is then easy to see that the Lemma holds. Indeed, on the one hand, for intervals in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, we have

(3.27)

\int\sum_{I\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\lvert\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle\rvert^{3}w_{I}^{3}\;dx\lesssim\int\sum_{I}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{2}w_{I}^{2}\;dx\lesssim\eta^{1/3}\lvert V\rvert.

And, on the other hand, for those not in
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\int\sum_{I\not\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\lvert\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle\rvert^{3}w_{I}^{3}\;dx\lesssim\eta^{1/3}\int\sum_{I}\langle\mathbf{1}_{V},w_{I}\rangle^{2}w_{I}^{2}\;dx\lesssim\eta^{1/3}\lvert V\rvert.

∎

To conclude the proof of the Theorem, combine the three Lemmas above. For
0<p<1
, we have

(3.29)

W_{2}p^{2}+W_{3}p^{3}\simeq_{\eta}p\lvert V\rvert.

Above, we have a third degree polynomial in
p
, which is approximately zero for
0<p<1
. That forces all the coefficients to be small.
This implies that
\lvert V\rvert\simeq_{\eta}0
, that is
\lvert V\rvert\leq C\eta^{t}\lvert V\rvert
,
for absolute choices of
C>0
and
0<t<1
.
We conclude that
\eta
admits an absolute lower bound.

History and Open Problems

1.
The Hilbert transform
H
, appropriately normalized, satisfies
H^{2}=-I
.
This with the parallelogram inequality then shows that
\lVert f\pm Hf\rVert_{2}^{2}=2\lVert f\rVert_{2}^{2}
.
So, as long as
f\neq 0
,
Hf
cannot be close to
f
or
-f
.
Stein and Weiss
[
10
]
showed that the Hilbert transform, applied to
an indicator set, has distribution that only depends upon
the measure of the set.
This observation has been reexamined by several authors

[
1
,
2
,
5
,
7
,
6
]
.
Laeng
[
5
]
shows that the distribution of
H\mathbf{1}_{V}

both inside and outside of
V
depends only on the measure of
V
.
The ‘noncomputational’ argument of Calderón
[
1
]
*pg 434 can be modified to prove this.
Osȩkowski
[
8
]
uses a stochastic analysis approach to extend
this result to Riesz transforms and functions taking values in the interval
[0,1]
.

2.
In a different direction, Tolsa and Verdera
[
11
]
address closely related questions
in which the Cauchy transform is applied to measures on the plane.
This subject then concerns so called reflectionless measures
[
4
]
.

3.
We do not know of any closely related results about discrete operators.
Several questions arise.
Square functions come in many different forms. Surely many of them satisfy estimates like those in this paper.
Similarly, one could consider the square functions of this paper with, say, weights in a Muckenhoupt class.

4.
Besides the many variants of one parameter square functions,
one can consider tensor products of martingales. To phrase a concrete question,
let
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
be the collection of dyadic rectangles in the plane. Associate to
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
, we have a Haar function

(4.1)

h_{R_{1}\times R_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\prod_{j=1}^{2}h_{R_{j}}(x_{j}).

The square function is then defined in the obvious way,

(4.2)

(Sf)^{2}=\sum_{R}\frac{\langle f,h_{R}\rangle^{2}}{\lvert R\rvert}\mathbf{1}_{R}.

Question:
Does Theorem
2.2
hold for this square function?

We state it this way so that one can avoid potential pitfalls associated with the
general (nonhomogeneous) setting of a martingale (although these concerns are not present in the one parameter setting).
The most naive variants of the proofs of this paper do not seem to imply this result.
