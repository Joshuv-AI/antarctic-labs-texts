Weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points
Shelby Cox and Maize Curiel

Abstract
. Let 𝐯 1 , … , 𝐯 m \mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m} be points in a metric space with distance d d , and let w 1 , … , w m w_{1},\ldots,w_{m} be positive real weights. The weighted Fermat-Weber points are those points 𝐱 \mathbf{x} which minimize ∑ w i ​ d ​ ( 𝐯 i , 𝐱 ) \sum w_{i}d(\mathbf{v}_{i},\mathbf{x}) . We extend a result of Comăneci and Joswig, that the set of unweighted Fermat-Weber points agrees with the “central” covector cell of the tropical convex hull of 𝐯 1 , … , 𝐯 m \mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m} , to the weighted setting. In particular, we show that for any fixed data points 𝐯 1 , … , 𝐯 m \mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m} , and any covector cell of the tropical convex hull of the data, there is a choice of weights that makes that cell the Fermat-Weber set. We similarly extend the method of Comăneci and Joswig for computing consensus trees in phylogenetics.



1.
Introduction

Given data points
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
in a metric space
X
with distance
d
, a point
\mathbf{x}\in X
that minimizes the average distance to the data is called a
Fermat-Weber point
of
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
.
In general, Fermat-Weber points are not unique.
Thus, we study the set of all Fermat-Weber points of
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
, which is called the
Fermat-Weber set
of
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
.
In
[
6
]
, Comǎneci and Joswig study the Fermat-Weber set for data in the
tropical projective torus
,
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
, with the
asymmetric tropical metric

d_{\Delta}
.
They prove that Fermat-Weber set with respect to
d_{\Delta}
is contained in the tropical convex hull of the data,
\mathrm{tconv}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}
.
In contrast, the Fermat-Weber set with respect to the usual (symmetric) tropical metric is typically not contained in
\mathrm{tconv}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}

[
14
,
15
]
.

In this paper, we study the
weighted
Fermat-Weber set for data in
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
with the asymmetric tropical metric
d_{\Delta}
.
In the weighted variant of the Fermat-Weber problem, the input also includes a positive real weight,
w_{i}
, for each data point
\mathbf{v}_{i}
.
Then, the
weighted Fermat-Weber set
is the collection of all points
\mathbf{x}\in X
which minimize the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i})
.
Our main theorem, stated below, generalizes
[
6
, Theorem 4]
to the weighted setting.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given data
\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
and any real positive weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
, the weighted Fermat-Weber set with respect to
d_{\Delta}
is contained in
\mathrm{tconv}^{\min}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}
. More precisely, it is a covector cell of
\mathrm{tconv}^{\min}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}
. Furthermore, for every covector cell of
\mathrm{tconv}^{\min}\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}
there exist positive real weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
so that the weighted Fermat-Weber set is that cell.

In addition to the tropical convexity properties of these Fermat-Weber sets, applications to phylogenetics further motivate the study of the weighted Fermat-Weber problem in the asymmetric tropical setting.
Phylogenetics is a subfield of computational biology concerned with determining evolutionary relationships among present-day species.
One central problem in phylogenetics, called
phylogenetic (tree) reconstruction
, is the following:
given biological data, e.g.  DNA alignments, from
n
taxa, output a tree on
n
leaves that represents the evolutionary history of the
n
taxa.
There are many methods for phylogenetic reconstruction
[
7
]
, and one fundamental problem is that different methods may return different trees, even on the same input data.
A
consensus method
is an algorithm that attempts to determine which features in the various reconstructed trees are likely to be present in the true tree.
The output is a
consensus tree
with those features.
For a survey of consensus methods, see
[
4
]
.

The
space of equidistant phylogenetic trees
on
N
leaves is a tropically convex space under a certain embedding
[
2
,
16
]
.
Thus,
[
6
, Theorem 4]
implies that the (weighted) Fermat-Weber points of tree data with respect to
d_{\Delta}
also represent trees; our
Theorem

1.1
extends this property to the weighted case.
In
[
6
]
, Comǎneci and Joswig then define a consensus method using Fermat-Weber points with respect to
d_{\Delta}
.
They prove this consensus method is well-behaved in the sense that (1) it is regular (as defined in
[
5
]
), and (2) it is Pareto and co-Pareto on rooted triples.
We analogously define a consensus method using weighted Fermat-Weber points (see
4.2
), which is regular, and both Pareto and co-Pareto on rooted triples.

Moreover, the extension to the
weighted
setting is important in certain phylogenetic settings where weighted tree data appears.
For example, in bootstrapping weights are on each tree are used to improve robustness
[
17
]
.
This is a natural setting where
weighted
Fermat-Weber points could be applied in future research.

The paper is organized as follows. In
section

2
we state our notation and build connections among the objects from tropical and polyhedral geometry relevant to our approach. In
section

3
, we prove our main result,
Theorem

1.1
, which is split into two parts:
Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.3
. Then in
section

4
, we describe applications of weighted asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber points to phylogenetics. We end by briefly outlining directions for future research.

2.1.
The Weighted Asymmetric Tropical Fermat-Weber Problem

The purpose of this subsection is to introduce the
weighted asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber problem
, and to formulate the weighted asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber points as the minimizers of a
tropical signomial
(see
2.4
).

We will work in the tropical max-plus semi-ring
\mathbb{T}_{\mathrm{max}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\oplus,\odot)
, or simply
\mathbb{T}
for brevity, where tropical addition
\oplus
and tropical multiplication
\odot
are defined by

a\oplus b=\max\{a,b\}\quad\text{ and }\quad a\odot b=a+b,\quad\text{ for all}\ a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}.

The tropical additive identity is
-\infty
and the multiplicative identity is
0
. Note that the above operations extend to the semi-module
\mathbb{T}^{n}
via coordinate-wise tropical addition and tropical scalar multiplication.

We consider data in the
tropical projective torus
, which is the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
, where
\mathbbm{1}_{n}
denotes the vector of all ones in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
with the following asymmetric tropical distance, which was first defined in
[
6
]
; see also
[
1
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Comǎneci and Joswig
[
6
, Section 3]
)
.

The
asymmetric tropical distance
,
d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
is:

(1)

d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=n\max_{i\in[n]}(x_{i}-y_{i})+\sum_{i\in[n]}(y_{i}-x_{i}).

It is possible to simplify the expression for
d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
by assuming that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are given by a specific representative in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
be the subspace where the coordinates sum to zero:

\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}=0\}.

Each point in the tropical projective torus has a unique representative in
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
, which is obtained via orthogonal projection,
e.g.
(3,1,2)=2\mathbbm{1}_{3}+(1,-1,0)\equiv(1,-1,0)
.
When the points
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
are given by their unique representative in
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
, the distance
d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
can be simplified to the following

(2)

d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=n\max_{i\in[n]}(x_{i}-y_{i}),\phantom{.}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}.

From now on, we fix a data set
V=\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}\subset\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
and view this as a data set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
where each point is given by its representative in
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
.
Moreover, we allow the data points to be weighted by positive real weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
.
We denote this list of weights by the vector
\mathbf{w}
.
In some instances, it will be essential that
\mathbf{w}
is a
weight vector
, meaning that in addition to assuming
w_{i}>0
for all
i
, we also assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}=1
.
In fact, we may always assume that
\mathbf{w}
is a weight vector, since the minimizers of the sum in (
3
) are unchanged by a uniform rescaling of the weights.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a data set
V=\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}\subset\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
and a weight vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
, the
weighted (asymmetric tropical) Fermat-Weber set
on the data
V
with weights
\mathbf{w}
consists of the points
\mathbf{x}\in X
minimizing the following sum

(3)

\mbox{FW}(V,\mathbf{w}):=\underset{\mathbf{x}\in X}{\mathrm{argmin}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i}).

A
weighted (asymmetric tropical) Fermat-Weber point
of
V
with weights
\mathbf{w}
is any point in the Fermat-Weber set
\mbox{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
.

Note that
2.2
fixes a choice of signs by choosing the order of the arguments for
d_{\Delta}
. In particular, it fixes the tropical convex hull to be the min-tropical convex hull.

The next step is to rewrite the sum in (
3
) using the operations of the tropical max-plus semi-ring.
For this, we require the following notation.

The
tropical monomial

\mathbf{x}^{\odot\mathbf{a}}
is the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}
. A
tropical signomial
is a finite tropical linear combination of tropical monomials with non-negative real exponents, i.e.

f(\mathbf{x})=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{a}\in A}\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\odot\mathbf{x}^{\odot\mathbf{a}},

for a finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
is finite and
\lambda_{\mathbf{a}}\in\mathbb{T}
for all
\mathbf{a}\in A
. If
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\geq 0}
, then
f(\mathbf{x})
is a
tropical polynomial
.

Below, we reinterpret the distance
d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i})
as a tropical polynomial. The sum in (
3
) is then a tropical signomial, which the Fermat-Weber points minimize.

Definition 2.3
.

The
tropical linear form centered at

\mathbf{v}_{i}
is defined to be

f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{x}):=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}(-v_{ij})\odot x_{j},

and the
tropical signomial associated to data
V
with weights
\mathbf{w}
is

f_{V,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}):=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}^{\odot w_{i}}(\mathbf{x}).

Proposition 2.4
.

The weighted Fermat-Weber points of
V
with respect to the tropical asymmetric metric
d_{\Delta}
are the minimizers of
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
.

Proof.

The result follows from writing the classical sum in (
3
) using the tropical operation
\odot=+
, and replacing
d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i})
with
f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}
.
Indeed, the distance from
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
to
\mathbf{v}_{i}\in\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
is (up to scaling by
1/n
) given by the tropical polynomial
f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{x})

f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{x})=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}(-v_{ij})\odot x_{j}=\max_{j\in[n]}(x_{j}-v_{ij})=\frac{1}{n}d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i}).

Thus, the average distance from
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
to the data
V\subseteq\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
is (up to scaling by
m/n
) given by the following tropical signomial

f_{V,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})=\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}^{\odot w_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\max_{j\in[n]}(x_{j}-v_{ij})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}d_{\Delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}_{i}).

It follows that the set of minimizers of
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})
over
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
is exactly
\mathrm{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
.
∎

We end the subsection with an example of
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
for two data points, which we will return to throughout the paper.

Example 2.5
.

Consider the data set
\{\mathbf{v}_{1}=(0,0,0),\mathbf{v}_{2}=(1,-1,0)\}=V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}_{3}
.
For the weights
\mathbf{w}=(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
,
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})
is the following tropical signomial

\displaystyle f_{V,\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left(x_{1}\oplus x_{2}\oplus x_{3}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\odot\left(-1\odot x_{1}\oplus 1\odot x_{2}\oplus x_{3}\right)^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}\oplus-\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\oplus-\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{2}\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\oplus x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\oplus x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\oplus x_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\oplus x_{1}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{2}\oplus\frac{2}{3}\odot x_{2}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}x_{3}^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}\oplus x_{3}.

We can simplify
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
to a tropical signomial with six terms since three of the nine original terms never achieve the max in
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
. Thus, the above function is piecewise linear with 6 linear pieces of maximal dimension.

2.2.
Minimizing Tropical Signomials

In this subsection, we describe the Fermat-Weber points of data
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
with weights
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}
using convex and polyhedral geometry.
This connection is facilitated by the formulation of the Fermat-Weber points in the previous section as the minimizers of the tropical signomial
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
(
2.4
).
Note that while the results we cite from
[
13
]
are stated for tropical
polynomials
, the proofs easily extend to tropical signomials.

Fix a tropical max-plus signomial,
f(\mathbf{x})=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{a}\in A}c_{\mathbf{a}}\odot\mathbf{x}^{\odot\mathbf{a}}
.
It is a piecewise linear, continuous, convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We are interested in minimizing
f
over the subspace
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
, which we previously identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
.
This minimum, if it exists, is achieved on a face of
\mathrm{epi}(f)\cap\left(\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}\times\mathbb{R}\right)
, where
\mathrm{epi}(f)
is the
epigraph

\mathrm{epi}(f):=\{(\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t\geq f(\mathbf{x})\}.

The epigraph is an unbounded polyhedron of dimension

.
Each facet of
\mathrm{epi}(f)

for some
\mathbf{a}_{F}\in A

Projecting away the last coordinate of the epigraph gives the normal complex of

Definition 2.6

(cf.

.

Let
f
be a tropical signomial. The
normal complex
of
f
, which we denote
\mathrm{NC}(f)
, is the polyhedral complex
\{\pi(F)\mid F\text{ is a face of }\mathrm{epi}(f)\}
, where
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

We will further assume that
f
is
homogeneous
, i.e. the sum of the coordinates of
\mathbf{a}

\mathbf{a}\in A

For example,
f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}

For homogeneous

, the lineality space of
\mathrm{NC}(f)
contains
\mathbbm{1}_{n}

\mathrm{NC}(f)

\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f)

\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}

\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}\cong\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}

\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f)=\mathrm{NC}(f)\cap\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}

The

f
, which we denote by
\mathrm{supp}(f)
, is the set of
\mathbf{a}\in A

is finite; the
Newton polytope
of
f
is the convex hull of
\mathrm{supp}(f)
.
For example, the Newton polytope of

is the standard simplex
\Delta^{n-1}=\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{n})
.
The
subdivision dual to

, denoted
\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)
, is the regular subdivision

\beta

\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)

The dual correspondence
\beta

.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
f
be a homogeneous tropical signomial. The set of minimizers of
f
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
is the cell of
\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f)
dual to the central cell of
\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The minimum exists if and only if such a face exists.

Proof.

The minimum of
f
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
exists if and only if there is a face
\widehat{F}
of
\mathrm{epi}(f)
supported by a hyperplane of the form
t=\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{x}+c
, where
\langle\mathbf{m},\mathbf{x}\rangle=0
when restricted to
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
.
Such a hyperplane must have
\mathbf{m}=\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{n}
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
In terms of the dual subdivision, this means
\widehat{F}
is a minimizing face if and only if
\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{n}\in\mathrm{int}(\beta(\widehat{F}))
. The set of minimizers of
f
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
is then the cell of
\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f)
that arises as the projection of
\widehat{F}
to
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
.
∎

We take a brief detour to describe the combinatorics of the subdivision dual to a product of tropical polynomials with positive real exponents, e.g.
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
.
First, we recall the situation for products of tropical polynomials.
Given a tropical polynomial
f
along with a factorization into tropical polynomials,
f(\mathbf{x})=\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, the subdivision dual to
f
is a regular
mixed subdivision
of the Minkowski sum
N_{1}+\cdots+N_{m}
, where
N_{i}=\mathrm{supp}(f_{i})
(see
[
13
, Section 4.1, 4.2]
).
The (regular)
mixed subdivisions
of
N_{1}+\cdots+N_{m}
are exactly those subdivisions arising from a (regular) subdivision of the
Cayley embedding
of
N_{1},\ldots,N_{m}
, which is

\mathrm{Cayley}(N_{1},\ldots,N_{m}):=\mathbf{e}_{1}\times N_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathbf{e}_{m}\times N_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The scaled Minkowski sum
\frac{1}{m}(N_{1}+\cdots+N_{m})
is identified with the linear section of the Cayley polytope

of the linear section.
Then

The specific regular subdivision of

Definition 2.8

(see
[

)

Given tropical polynomials

The Cayley trick generalizes to the weighted case, where the weighted Minkowski sum

(for more details, see

Combining this with

Lemma 2.9

Let

\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}

Proof.

The equally weighted case, where

[

Varying the weights shifts the linear section of the Cayley polytope from

∎

2.3.
Tropical Convexity

In this subsection, we connect the Fermat-Weber points to the tropical convex hull of the data.
We begin by discussing the tropical vanishing set of

Definition 2.11

(cf.
[

)

The

is achieved at least twice.

We continue to assume that

is homogeneous, and so

contains
\mathbbm{1}_{n}
in its lineality space.
The image of

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
is denoted by
\overline{\mathrm{tropV}}(f)

In terms of the previous subsection, the tropical hypersurface
\mathrm{tropV}(f)
is the codimension-1-skeleton of the normal complex
3
3

3

Or, equivalently, the codimension-2-skeleton of the epigraph of
f
, projected onto the first factor.
of
f
,
\mathrm{NC}(f)
, and similarly for
\overline{\mathrm{tropV}}(f)
and
\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f)

[
13
, Corollary 1.6]

Thus,
\overline{\mathrm{tropV}}(f)
has the structure of a polyhedral complex.

It is well-known that the vanishing set of a product of tropical polynomials is the union of the tropical vanishing sets of each factor
[
13
, Lemma 4.6]

The following result is the analogue for tropical signomials.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
f
be a tropical polynomial, and let
w>0

\mathrm{tropV}(f^{w})
is (as a set) equal to
\mathrm{tropV}(f)
. Moreover,

\mathrm{tropV}\left(\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}\right)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathrm{tropV}(f_{i}),\text{ for any }\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}.

In particular,
\mathrm{tropV}(f_{V})=\mathrm{tropV}(f_{V,\mathbf{w}})
.

Proof.

Let
f_{i}=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{a}\in A_{i}}c_{\mathbf{a}}\odot\mathbf{x}^{\odot\mathbf{a}}
,
A_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
.
If the maximum in
f_{j}
is achieved twice by
\mathbf{x}
, then
f_{j}^{\odot w_{j}}(\mathbf{x})=w_{j}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{1}}+\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})=w_{j}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{2}}+\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})
for some
\mathbf{a}_{1}\neq\mathbf{a}_{2}\in A_{j}
.
It follows that the maximum in
\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}
is also achieved at least twice:

\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}=w_{j}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{1}}+\mathbf{a}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}(\mathbf{x})=w_{j}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{2}}+\mathbf{a}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i\neq j}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}(\mathbf{x}).

On the other hand, if the maximum is achieved twice in
\bigodot_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}^{\odot w_{i}}
at
\mathbf{x}
, then we must be able to write the maximum as two distinct sums:
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1}}+\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1}\cdot\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}(c_{\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}}+\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}\cdot\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{a}_{i}^{2}\in A_{i}
. It follows that for some
j

\mathbf{a}_{j}^{1}\neq\mathbf{a}_{j}^{2}
, so the maximum in
f_{j}^{\odot w_{j}}
is achieved at least twice.
∎

3.
The weighted tropical Fermat-Weber problem

We now come to the proof of
Theorem

1.1

Theorem

3.1
and
Theorem

3.3
.
First, we prove that the weighted Fermat-Weber points form a covector cell of the min-tropical convex hull of the data.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given data
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m}\}=V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}
, and a weight vector
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the weighted (tropical asymmetric) Fermat-Weber set is the covector cell of
\mbox{tconv}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m})
that is dual to central cell of
\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)

Proof.

We will work backwards, starting with the Newton polytope.
The Newton polytope is Minkowski additive over products, so the Newton polytope of
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
is
w_{1}\Delta^{n-1}+\cdots+w_{m}\Delta^{n-1}
.
The point
\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n}
is contained in its relative interior.
Therefore, there is an interior cell of
\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)
, denoted
C^{\ast}
, containing
\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n}
in its relative interior.
Dual to
C^{\ast}
is a cell of
\overline{\mathrm{NC}}(f_{V,\mathbf{w}})
, denoted
C
.
The cell
C
is bounded since
C^{\ast}
is an interior cell of
\underline{\mathrm{N}}(f)
.
Thus,
C
is a cell of the min-tropical convex hull of the data.
According to
Lemma

2.7
,
C
is the set of minimizers of
f_{V,\mathbf{w}}
over
\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}_{n}
, which by
2.4
is
\mathrm{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
.
∎

The second half of
Theorem

1.1
says that for any covector cell
C
of
\mathrm{tconv}^{\min}(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{m})
, there is some weight vector that makes
C
the Fermat-Weber set.
To prove this, it is convenient to pass to the Cayley subdivision, and for this we introduce the following notation.
We denote Cayley subdivision dual to

by

, since it does not depend on the weights.
It is the regular subdivision of the product of simplices
\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1}
induced by lifting
(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j})
to
(\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{e}_{j},-v_{ij})

The weights are accounted for by taking the appropriate linear section.

The central cell of the subdivision dual to

is the cell dual to the Fermat-Weber set (
2.4
,

2.7
).
The Newton polytope of

is the simplex

The

3.2
proves that for any interior cell

of

The proof of

formulates this containment as a fractional matching problem over a spanning forest of

using the correspondence between subsets of vertices of

and bipartite graphs.
Fractional matchings have been extensively studied in combinatorial optimization; for further details see Schrijver’s monograph

Proposition 3.2
.

Given any subdivision of the vertices of
\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1}
and any interior cell
C
of the subdivision, there is a choice of positive real weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
with
\sum w_{i}=1
, so that
C
contains the point
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m},\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n})
in its relative interior.

Proof.

Begin by refining the given subdivision to a triangulation, and let
S
be a cell of that triangulation which is contained in
C
and which has the same dimension as
C
.
Then
S
is an interior cell of the triangulation of
\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1}
, and if the point
(\mathbf{w},\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n})
lies in the relative interior of
S
, then it also lies in the relative interior of
C
.

Let
F
be the bipartite graph corresponding to the vertices of
S
.
By
[
8
, Lemma 6.2.8(a)]
,
F
is a forest in
K_{m,n}
; it is also spanning, which can be seen as follows.
The intersection
S\cap\mathrm{int}(\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1})
is non-empty if and only if
F
is not contained in the bipartite graph corresponding to any facet of
\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1}
.
The bipartite graph corresponding to a facet of
\Delta^{m-1}\times\Delta^{n-1}
is a subgraph of
K_{n,m}
obtained by deleting a single vertex (see
[
8
, Section 6.2.2]
).

S
is an interior cell of the triangulation, hence not contained in any facet.
Thus
F
spans
K_{n,m}
.

Now, a point
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
lies in
\mathrm{int}(S)
if it can be written as a convex combination of the vertices of
S
with all positive coefficients.
In terms of the forest
F
,
(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})
lies in
\mathrm{int}(S)
if there exist
\lambda(e)>0
for each edge
e
in
F
such that the sum of edge weights on any left vertex adds up to the corresponding
\mathbf{p}
coordinate, and the sum of edge weights on any right vertex adds up to the corresponding
\mathbf{q}
coordinate. Let
r(e)
be the node on the right side connected to
e
, and let
\ell(e)
be the node on the left side connected to
e
. The choice of
\lambda
’s in (
5
) leads to a valid choice of weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
(given in (
6
)) so that
S
contains the weighted barycenter.

(5)

\lambda(e):=\frac{1}{n\cdot\deg r(e)}.

(6)

w_{i}:=\sum_{e\text{ s.t. }\ell(e)=i}\lambda(e).

The equations in (
7
) show that the weights on any right node sum to
\frac{1}{n}
(since
F
is spanning, every vertex has at least one edge); by definition, the weights on the
i
th left node sum to
w_{i}
. It follows that
\mathbf{b}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m},\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1})
lies in the relative interior of
S
.

(7)

\sum_{r(e)=j}\frac{1}{n\cdot\deg(j)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r(e)=j}\frac{1}{\deg(j)}=\frac{1}{n}\deg(j)\frac{1}{\deg(j)}=\frac{1}{n}.

Moreover,
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
is a valid choice of weights for the Fermat-Weber problem. The weights
w_{i}
are positive because
F
is spanning, so the sum in (
6
) is never empty; the equations in (
8
) show that the
w_{i}
sum to one.

(8)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}=\sum_{e}\lambda(e)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{r(e)=j}\frac{1}{n\cdot\deg(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r(e)=j}\frac{1}{\deg(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}=n\frac{1}{n}=1.\qed

We can now complete the proof of

.
∎

We conclude the section by remarking that the Fermat-Weber set
\mathrm{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
can be explicitly recovered by computing the central cell of the Cayley polytope subdivision, which is the cell containing
(\mathbf{w},\frac{1}{n}\mathbbm{1}_{n})
in its relative interior.
Indeed, given the central cell
C
of the Cayley polytope subdivision we can compute the central cell
\mathrm{M}(C)
of the subdivision dual to
f
as the projection of
W(\mathbf{w})\cap C
onto the second factor.
It is a (weighted) mixed cell, so we have
\mathrm{M}(C)=w_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+w_{m}A_{m}
, where
C=\mathrm{conv}(\mathbf{e}_{1}\times A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathbf{e}_{m}\times A_{m})
.
Then
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
if and only if the terms of
f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}
with exponents
\mathbf{a}\in A_{i}
simultaneously achieve the maximum in
f_{\mathbf{v}_{i}}(\mathbf{x})
, for all
i
.
Note that for generic weights, the Fermat-Weber set is a point (of minimal dimension) since the central Cayley cell is of maximal dimension for generic weights.

4.
Applications to Phylogenetics

In this section, we outline how weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points and
Theorem

1.1
can be applied to phylogenetics.
Phylogenetic reconstruction is concerned with determining the evolutionary relationships between a given set of
taxa
, e.g. species.
These relationships are often encoded in a tree in which the leaves represent present-day species and the internal vertices are their common ancestors.
The length of an edge in the tree is usually a measure of the amount of mutation that has occurred between the species on either end.
A standard reference for mathematical phylogenetics is
[
20
]
.

Weighted tree data appears in the phylogenetic inference literature, for example when applying bootstrapping to biological data.
Bootstrapping is a widely used resampling method that allows one to estimate various statistical measures, e.g. confidence, on the sample without information about the population
[
10
,
21
]
.
A typical bootstrapping algorithm in phylogenetics takes as input a table indexed by
n
taxa, e.g. species, and
k
characters, e.g. DNA sequences, and resamples with replacement along the character axis to produce
r
new
n\times k
taxa-character tables
[
21
,
11
]
.
Using a fixed phylogenetic reconstruction method, a tree is then generated from each sample and a consensus tree is computed to summarize the results.

In
[
17
]
, the authors argue that certain phylogenetic bootstrap weightings make non-parametric bootstrapping more robust to noise in the data.
However, that paper is concerned with studying bootstrap support of branches, which is the proportion of bootstrap trees in which the branch occurs.
As an interesting future direction, they suggest studying how the weighting affects robustness of the consensus of the bootstrap trees.
In this section, we use weighted (asymmetric tropical) Fermat-Weber points to define a consensus method.

4.1.
The Space of Equidistant Trees

We begin with a brief overview of the relevant background for equidistant trees and ultrametrics.

A
phylogenetic tree on
n
leaves
is a rooted
\mathbb{R}
-weighted tree with leaves labeled by
[n]
.
We further assume that our phylogenetic trees contain no degree two vertices other than the root, which we denote by
\rho
.
We will refer to the weights of the edges as
lengths
in order to avoid confusing them with the weights that appear on the data in the weighted Fermat-Weber problem.
We require the internal branch lengths to be positive, but the lengths of branches adjacent to leaves may be non-positive.
A phylogenetic tree is
equidistant
if the path from in the tree from the root to any leaf has the same length.

A phylogenetic tree
T
induces a
tree distance
on
[n]
, which is the map
d_{T}:[n]\times[n]\to\mathbb{R}
where
d_{T}(i,j)
is the length of the path from leaf
i
to leaf
j
in
T
.
We will identify
d_{T}
with the vector in
(d_{T}(i,j))_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
N=\binom{n}{2}
.
With the assumption that
T
contains no degree two vertices, it is possible to recover
T
uniquely from
d_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

[
20
, Theorem 7.2.8]
.
Thus, we call the set

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}:=\{d\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid d=d_{T}\text{ for some phylogenetic tree }T\text{ on }n\text{ leaves}\}

the
space of equidistant phylogenetic trees
. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
is a polyhedral fan homeomorphic to the Billera-Holmes-Vogtman (BHV) space of trees
[
3
,
22
]
.
As usual, we will identify
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
with its image in
\mathbb{R}^{N}/\mathbb{R}\mathbbm{1}_{N}
.
Tropical scaling by
\lambda\mathbbm{1}_{N}
has the effect of increasing the lengths of all branches adjacent to leaves by
\lambda/2
.

A
dissimilarity map
is a function
\delta:[n]\times[n]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\delta(i,i)=0
and
\delta(i,j)=\delta(j,i)
for all
i,j\in[n]
.
A dissimilarity map is an
ultrametric
if it satisfies the three-point condition:

\text{ max of }d(i,j),d(i,k),d(j,k)\text{ is achieved at least twice, for all }1\leq i<j<k\leq n.

Again, we identify
\delta:[n]\times[n]\to\mathbb{R}
with the vector
(\delta(i,j))_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The connection between dissimilarity maps and equidistant tree distances goes as follows: a dissimilarity map
\delta:[n]\times[n]\to\mathbb{R}
is an ultrametric if and only if it is an equidistant tree distance
[
20
, Theorem 7.2.5]
.
Thus, the three-point condition gives explicit tropical equations cutting out
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
.
In
[
2
]
, Ardila and Klivans further prove that
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
is equal to (as sets) the Bergman fan of the complete graph, which implies it is a max-tropical linear space.

Any max-tropical linear space is max-tropically convex
[
16
, Proposition 5.2.8]
; the analogous result holds in the min-tropical convention.
Since in the previous section we used min-tropical convex hulls, it is natural for us to make the substitution
\mathcal{T}_{n}:=-\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{n}
, which is min-tropically convex.
In particular,
Theorem

1.1
implies that for
V\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}
, each point in
\mathrm{FW}(V,\mathbf{w})
represents an equidistant tree on
n
leaves.

4.2.
Weighted Asymmetric Tropical Consensus

One fundamental problem in phylogenetic reconstruction is that different reconstruction methods may produce different trees for the input same data.
A
consensus tree
is a tree that summarizes the different reconstructed trees.
The goal of computing a consensus tree is not to compute the true evolutionary tree, but to determine which evolutionary relationships or features present in the reconstructed trees are likely to be present in the true tree.

Following
[
6
]
, we define a consensus method via the weighted tropical Fermat-Weber points.
The following result is the weighted analogue of
[
6
, Theorem 18]
.

Theorem 4.1

(cf.
[
6
, Theorem 18]
)
.

Given trees
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}=T\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
and positive real weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
, the set
\mathrm{FW}(T,\mathbf{w})
is contained in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Moreover, all trees in the relative interior of
\mathrm{FW}(T,\mathbf{w})
have the same topology, meaning that they are isomorphic as partially-labeled unweighted graphs.

Proof.

Theorem

3.1
and the tropical convexity of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
imply the first claim. The second claim follows from
[
18
, Theorem 3.2]
, which says that for any cell
C
of
\mathrm{CovDec}(T)
, the trees in the relative interior of
C
share the same topology.
∎

Thus, we can define a tropical consensus method using the weighted Fermat-Weber points.

Definition 4.2

(cf.
[
6
, Definition 20]
)
.

Given trees
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}=T\subset\mathcal{T}_{n}
and positive real weights
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}
, the
weighted asymmetric tropical consensus tree
is the (classical) average of the (classical) vertices of
\mathrm{FW}(T,\mathbf{w})
.

It is immediate from
Theorem

1.1
that the consensus method defined above is a
tropically convex consensus method
, which simply requires that the consensus tree lies in the tropical convex hull of the data (see
[
6
, Section 5.2]
).
Just like the unweighted tropical consensus method, the weighted tropical consensus method is
regular
, in the following sense:

(1)

The consensus of any number of copies of
t
, with any positive real weights, is
t
;

(2)

the consensus does not depend on the ordering of the inputs
(t_{i},w_{i})
;

(3)

permuting the taxa of the input tree results in the same permutation of the taxa in the consensus tree.

The above criteria for weighted data are adapted from the definition of regular in
[
5
]
.
The proof that the weighted tropical consensus method is regular is the same as for the unweighted case, which is given in
[
6
, Section 5.2]
.
It is known that regular consensus methods are not “extension stable”
[
5
, Theorem 3]
.

Whether a consensus method behaves well for a certain feature can be measured by whether it is Pareto or co-Pareto with respect to that feature
[
4
]
.
A consensus method is
Pareto
with respect to a feature
F
if whenever
F
is present in every sample,
F
is also present in the consensus tree.
A consensus method is
co-Pareto
with respect to
F
if whenever
F
is present in the consensus tree,
F
is present in at least one of the samples.

One feature of interest is the set of
rooted triples
in a tree
[
4
, Section 1.3]
, the definition of which we recall now.
Let
T
be an equidistant tree on
n
leaves, and let
i,j,k\in[n]
be three leaves of
T
.
We say that
T
contains the rooted triple
ij|k
if
d_{T}(i,j)<d_{T}(i,k)=d_{T}(j,k)
.
That is, the lowest common ancestor of
i
and
j
in
T
is a proper descendant of the lowest common ancestor of
i
and
k
.
In
[
6
]
, Comǎneci and Joswig prove that tropically convex consensus methods behave well with respect to
rooted triples
.

Proposition 4.3

(Comǎneci and Joswig
[
6
, Theorem 22]
)
.

Any tropically convex consensus method is Pareto and co-Pareto on rooted triples.

In particular, we have the following result for the weighted tropical consensus method.

Corollary 4.4
.

The weighted asymmetric tropical consensus tree is Pareto and co-Pareto on rooted triples.

5.
Outlook

In this paper we proved that weighted (asymmetric tropical) Fermat-Weber points satisfy the same tropical convexity properties as their unweighted counterparts, thus generalizing a result of Comǎneci and Joswig
[
6
, Theorem 4]
.
Moreover, we defined a weighted analogue of the tropical consensus method defined in
[
6
]
and showed that it extends the phylogenetics properties of the unweighted version.
One possible direction for future research is to determine whether weighting bootstrapped trees improves the robustness of asymmetric tropical consensus methods.

In another direction, Comǎneci and Joswig connected the unweighted asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber problem to a well-known optimization problem called the transportation problem
[
6
]
.
This allows them to provide a computationally efficient algorithm for computing the Fermat-Weber set
[
6
, Corollary 17]
.
In phylogenetics, efficiency is crucial due to the size of the data sets involved, and because the ambient dimension of tree space grows quadratically in the number of taxa.
Thus, another possible direction for future research is to establish a connection between weighted asymmetric tropical Fermat-Weber points and transportation problems.
