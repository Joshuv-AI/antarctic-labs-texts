Beyond Nonconvexity: A Universal Trust-Region Method with New Analyses
Yuntian Jiang, Chang He, Chuwen Zhang, Dongdong Ge, Bo Jiang, Yinyu Ye

Abstract
The trust-region (TR) method is renowned historically for its robustness in nonconvex problems and extraordinary numerical performance, but the study of its performance in convex optimization is somehow limited. This paper complements the existing literature by presenting a universal trust-region method that simultaneously incorporates the quadratic regularization and ball constraint. In particular, we introduce a novel descent property tailored for trust-region-type algorithms, enabling us to unify and streamline the analysis for both convex and nonconvex optimization. Our method exhibits an iteration complexity of O ~ ( ϵ − 3 / 2 ) \tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2}) to find an ϵ \epsilon -approximate second-order stationary point for nonconvex optimization. Meanwhile, the analysis reveals that the universal method attains an O ( ϵ − 1 / 2 ) O(\epsilon^{-1/2}) complexity bound for convex optimization. Finally, we develop an adaptive universal method to address practical implementations. The numerical results show the effectiveness of our method in both nonconvex and convex problems.

Second-order methods are renowned for their faster convergence compared to first-order methods, and the capability to find second-order stationary points, see

,

,

,

,

. Among these methods, the trust-region (TR) method

stands out as a representative approach due to its robustness in nonconvex problems and extraordinary numerical performance. Many linear or nonlinear programming solvers are using the trust-region method as an important building block, to name a few, Knitro
[

, IPOPT
[

[

, etc. Also, in the machine learning field, it inspires the well-known trust-region policy optimization
[

Recall that the TR method operates on the local quadratic approximation of objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
within a TR ball constraint:

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle m_{k}(d):=\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}d^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|d\|\leq\Delta_{k}.

Once the TR step
d_{k}
is generated, the TR method decides whether to update or adjust the radius
\Delta_{k}
based on the so-called ratio test:

\rho_{k}~=\frac{f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})}{m_{k}(d_{k})-m_{k}(0)}.

(1.2)

Despite its success in practice as mentioned above, the theoretical development of the TR method is somehow incomplete.

For instance, results in the early literature
[
12
,
13
,
6
]
only established (asymptotic) local convergence to
\epsilon

\epsilon

[
3
]
for
\epsilon
-approximate first-order stationary points (
\epsilon
-FOSP), and an improved convergence rate is not aware for
convex functions.

In contrast, as another representative second-order method, the Cubic Regularized (CR) Newton method has the updating rule

d_{k}^{\text{CR}}=\arg\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ m^{\text{CR}}_{k}(d):=\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}d^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{3}\|d\|^{3},\sigma_{k}>0.

(1.3)

This method was originally proposed by
Griewank 1981
and further analyzed by
Nesterov and Polyak 2006
. Later,
Cartis et al. 2011a
,
Cartis et al. 2011b
proposed the adaptive version of this method. It shows a convergence rate of

to find

-SOSPs for nonconvex functions and
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
for convex case simultaneously.

However, classical TR exhibits a clear gap to these convergence properties. This arises possibly due to two aspects: first, the classic TR method does not fully exploit the curvature information of Hessian; second, the ratio test (
1.2
) does not explicitly quantify the desired decrease for the TR steps
[
18
]
; both are crucial in modern complexity analysis.

Therefore, a plethora of TR variants (e.g.,
[

,
20
,
21

) have been proposed to improve iteration complexity over the years. The fixed-radius variant by
Luenberger and Ye 2021
achieves a complexity of

for finding
\epsilon
-SOSPs by controlling the stepsize proportionally to the tolerance
{\epsilon}^{1/2}
. However, this variant tends to be conservative for practical applications. By designing a contraction and expansion mechanism,
Curtis et al. 2017
introduced the first adaptive trust-region method (TRACE) matching the
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2})
complexity for finding
\epsilon
-SOSPs, where
\tilde{O}
means there is a logarithmic term hidden in the complexity. Later,
Curtis et al. 2021
proposed another simplified variant of
[
18
]
while retaining the same dependency on
\epsilon
. A notable recent trust-region method introduced by
Hamad and Hinder 2022
achieves
O(\epsilon^{-3/2})
complexity and improves the complexity’s dependency over Lipschitz constants by putting together the upper and lower bounds on the stepsizes. However, their algorithm only converges to an
\epsilon
-FOSP. Generally, these novel variants transcend the classic ones to bring a per-step sufficient decrease

f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})\leq-\Omega(\epsilon^{3/2}).

(1.4)

As a result,
O(\epsilon^{-3/2})
rate of convergence can be achieved. However, for TR-type algorithms, such sufficient decrease (
1.4

1.1
), which is difficult to control in advance.
As a price to overcome this challenge, these algorithms rely on complicated feedback to adjust the dual multiplier and may need additional assumptions. Moreover, these TR-type methods are initially designed for nonconvex optimization, and the associated convergence analysis in convex optimization is
largely missing.
However, even in many nonconvex problems, the objective function quickly turns weakly convex near some stationary points, which makes the study of TR-type methods in convex optimization equally important.

Contributions.

Given this background, we introduce a simple yet potent TR variant: a universal trust-region (UTR) method based on the following subproblem:

\displaystyle\min_{d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}d^{T}\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|^{1/2}I\right)d+\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d

(1.5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle\|d\|\leq\Delta_{k}:=r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2},

where
\sigma_{k},r_{k}>0
are hyperparameters. The feature of the subproblem above is incorporating gradient regularization in the local quadratic model and setting the trust-region radius proportional to the square root of the gradient norm. As a result, the
d_{k}
generated by (
1.5
) exhibits a powerful feature referred to as
Function-or-Stationarity-Decrease
(FOSD,
Property 2.2
), i.e.,
after one successful update, either the function value decreases sufficiently or the gradient norm shrinks linearly.
Different from (
1.4
), this desirable property enables a concise and clean algorithm which does not need sophisticated inner loop to control the posterior multiplier
\lambda_{k}
, and the associated complexity results have the state-of-the-art dependency over both the tolerance
\epsilon
and the Lipschitz constant, requiring only the most standard assumptions.

More specifically, with explicit information on the Hessian Lipschitz constant, we propose a simple strategy to choose parameters
(\sigma_{k},r_{k})
(
Strategy 2.1
), and UTR converges to an
\epsilon
-FOSP within
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2})
iterations. Surprisingly, we also find that UTR exhibits an improved
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
complexity when the objective function is convex. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nonasymptotic analysis of TR-type methods in convex optimization.

Under the guidance of the simple strategy, we develop an adaptive approach (
Strategy 4.1
) that is more practical without relying on the Lipschitz constant. By fully exploiting the curvature information of the Hessian, this adaptive approach provides a convergence guarantee with an iteration complexity of
\tilde{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2})
for finding
\epsilon
-SOSPs.

Finally, in numerical experiments, our method is compared against ARC
[
16
,
17
]
, the newly proposed TR variant
[
20
]
, and the recent gradient regularized Newton method
[
22
]
, demonstrating the effectiveness of UTR.

2
The Universal Trust-Region Method

In this paper, we consider the following unconstrained optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad f(x),

(2.1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is twice differentiable and is bounded below, i.e.,
f^{*}:=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)>-\infty
. For nonconvex
f
, we aim to find
\epsilon
-approximate stationary points defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

A point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called an
\epsilon
-approximate first-order stationary point (
\epsilon
-FOSP) of (
2.1
) if it satisfies

\|\nabla f(x)\|\leq O(\epsilon)

(2.2)

Moreover, we call
x
an
\epsilon
-approximate second-order stationary point (
\epsilon
-SOSP) of (
2.1
) if it satisfies (
2.2
) and the following condition

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x))\geq\Omega(-\sqrt{\epsilon}).

(2.3)

For convex
f
, we aim to find the approximate solution such that

f(x)-f^{*}\leq O(\epsilon).

(2.4)

Throughout the paper, we adopt the following standard assumption on the objective function
f
, commonly used in the complexity analysis of second-order methods.

Assumption 2.1
.

The Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(x)
of the objective function is Lipschitz continuous with constant
M>0
, i.e.,

\|\nabla^{2}f(x)-\nabla^{2}f(y)\|\leq M\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.5)

As a consequence,
Assumption 2.1
implies the following results.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 4.1.1,
Nesterov 2018
)
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
satisfies
Assumption 2.1
, then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)-\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x)\right\|\leq\frac{M}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\left|f(y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)-\frac{1}{2}(y-x)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x)\right|\leq\frac{M}{6}\|y-x\|^{3}

(2.6b)

In particular, at each iteration
k
, we solve the subproblem (
1.5
). The mechanism of our trust-region method is straightforward, comprising only three major steps: setting the appropriate parameters
\sigma_{k}
and
r_{k}
by some strategy, solving the trust-region subproblem (
1.5
), and updating the iterate whenever
d_{k}
is good enough.

The crux of our method lies in the selection of proper parameters
\sigma_{k}
and
r_{k}
. This choice guides the model (
1.5
) to generate
good steps
that meet favorable descent properties to establish our iteration complexity bounds, i.e., the number of iterations
T
required to find the desired solutions. We define the following properties which are tailored for the universal trust-region method.

Property 2.1

(Monotone-Decrease)
.

The step decreases the value of the objective function, that is for each iteration
k
,

f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})\leq 0.

(2.7)

Property 2.2

(Function-or-Stationarity-Decrease)
.

For some
0<\xi<1
,
\kappa>0
, the step
d_{k}
either decreases the value of the objective function or decreases the gradient norm sufficiently, that is for each iteration
k
,

f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}\ \text{ or }\ \|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

(2.8)

For
Property 2.1
, it serves as a safeguard to ensure that the algorithm never discards the progress made in the previous iterations.

Property 2.2
is novel in the sense that we do not force every iteration to bring about a sufficient decrease in the function value but instead allow a linear contraction of the norm of the gradient as an alternative. Consequently, our analysis is largely simplified compared to other TR variants and uses only the most standard assumptions. Moreover, our analysis is almost unified for both convex and nonconvex optimization.

Unlike the classic ratio test, we design a new mechanism (
Strategy 2.1
) to ensure monotonicity: when we know the Lipschitz constant
M
. We show that the ratio test can be removed and every step is a good step. Moreover, even if we do not know the constant, we can perform a simple search procedure to ensure monotonicity.

We later prove the complexity results by establishing (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), and their modifications for convex functions (
Property 3.1
) as well as adaptiveness in choosing the parameters (
Property 4.1
).
Moreover, we give general principles where parameter selection can be designed based on the information available at hand.

2.2
Basic Properties of the Method

We present some preliminary analysis of our method. Similar to the standard trust-region method, the optimality conditions of (
1.5
) are provided as follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

The direction
d_{k}
is the solution of (
1.5
) if and only if there exists a dual multiplier
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\|d_{k}\|\leq r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\left(\|d_{k}\|-r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\right)=0

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I+\lambda_{k}I\right)d_{k}=-\nabla f(x_{k})

(2.9c)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I+\lambda_{k}I\succeq 0.

(2.9d)

The results are directly obtained from Theorem 4.1 in
Nocedal and Wright 1999
, and we omit the proof for succinctness. In the remaining part of this paper, we use
(d_{k},\lambda_{k})
to denote the primal-dual solution pair of the subproblem at iteration
k
. Accounting for the optimality conditions (
2.9a
)-(
2.9d
), we could establish the following lemmas, which provide an estimation for the objective function and the gradient norm at the next iterate.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds and
(d_{k},\lambda_{k})
satisfies the optimal conditions (
2.9a
)-(
2.9d
), we have

f(x_{k}+d_{k})\leq f(x_{k})-\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{3}.

(2.10)

Additionally, if
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, it follows that

f(x_{k}+d_{k})\leq f(x_{k})-\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)r_{k}^{3}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}.

(2.11)

Proof.

By the
M
-Lipschitz continuous property of
\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})
and
Lemma 2.2
, we conclude

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}+\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}+\frac{M}{6}\|d_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\left(\lambda_{k}+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}+\frac{M}{6}\|d_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{k}+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}d_{k}^{T}\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I+\lambda_{k}I\right)d_{k}+\frac{M}{6}\|d_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{k}+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\|d_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{k}/\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}+\sigma_{k}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\|d_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq-\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)\|d_{k}\|^{3}.

In the above, the first inequality comes from (
2.6b
); the first equality and the second inequality are due to the optimal properties (
2.9c
) and (
2.9d
), respectively; the last inequality is derived from (
2.9a
). As for the case
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, the substitution
\|d_{k}\|=r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}
directly implies the validity of the inequality (
2.11
).
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds and
(d_{k},\lambda_{k})
satisfies the optimal conditions (
2.9a
)-(
2.9d
). If
\lambda_{k}=0
, then we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}\right)\cdot\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

(2.13)

Proof.

First, by the optimality condition (
2.9c
), when the dual variable
\lambda_{k}=0
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{k}+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\right)\cdot\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\sigma_{k}r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

With the Hessian Lipschitz continuity and
Lemma 2.1
, we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}+\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})-\nabla f(x_{k})-\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|+\|\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\sigma_{k}r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}\right)\cdot\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|,

(2.14)

where the second inequality is from (
2.6a
), and the third inequality is from (
2.9a
).
∎

Basic Principle of Choosing
(\sigma_{k},r_{k})

The aforementioned
Lemma 2.3
and
Lemma 2.4
offer a valuable principle of selecting
\sigma_{k}
and
r_{k}
to guarantee that the step satisfies
Property 2.1
and
Property 2.2
. In particular, it suffices to control

\displaystyle~\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)\cdot r_{k}^{3}>\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}},\ \text{and}

(2.15a)

\displaystyle~\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}<\xi

(2.15b)

for some
\kappa>0,\xi<1
.
Thus, the choice of
\sigma_{k}
and
r_{k}
could be very flexible. For example, a vanilla approach can be constructed by discarding the term
\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}
in (
2.15a
).
Suppose the Lipschitz constant
M
is given, we show that a strategy that fits (
2.15
) exists; namely, we can adopt a fixed rule of selecting
\sigma_{k}
and
r_{k}
as follows.

Strategy 2.1

(The Simple Strategy)
.

With the knowledge of Lipschitz constant
M
, we set

\left(\sigma_{k},r_{k}\right)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{3},\frac{1}{3\sqrt{M}}\right)

(2.16)

for all
k
in
Algorithm 1
.

The universal trust-region method (
Algorithm 1
) equipped with such a simple choice reveals the following results.

Corollary 2.1
.

Suppose
Assumption 2.1
holds, by applying the
Strategy 2.1
, the steps generated by
Algorithm 1
satisfy
Property 2.1
and
Property 2.2
with
\kappa=\frac{1}{81},\xi=\frac{1}{6}
, i.e.

f(x_{k}+d_{k})\leq f(x_{k}).

Furthermore, if the dual variable
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, we have

f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{1}{81\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}.

(2.17)

While if the dual variable
\lambda_{k}=0
, we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\frac{1}{6}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

(2.18)

Proof.

Noticing
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
, it is easy to validate that

\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)\cdot r_{k}^{3}\geq\frac{1}{81\sqrt{M}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}=\frac{1}{6}.

Substituting the above inequalities into
Lemma 2.3
and
Lemma 2.4
completes the proof.
∎

In fact, one can adopt a choice of parameters
without Lipschitz constants. Furthermore, if the information of
\lambda_{k}
can be utilized, more aggressive strategies may be allowed. This direction is explored in the later sections to show stronger convergence to second-order stationarity. Nevertheless, the simple strategy (and a general design principle (
2.15
)) presented here is useful for understanding the building blocks of our method.

The Universal Trust-Region Method with a Simple Strategy

In this section, we give a convergence analysis of the universal method with the simple
Strategy 2.1
to an
\epsilon
-approximate FOSP (see
Definition 2.1
) with an iteration complexity of
\tilde{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
. The local convergence of this method is shown to be superlinear. Furthermore, the complexity can be further improved to
O\left(\epsilon^{-1/2}\right)
for convex functions.

3.1
Global Convergence Rate for Nonconvex Optimization

For the nonconvex functions,
we introduce the notation
x_{j_{f}}
representing the first iterate satisfying

\|\nabla f(x_{j_{f}})\|\leq\epsilon.

We derive the convergence results according to
Property 2.1
and
Property 2.2
.
Based on the FOSD
Property 2.2
, we define the following index sets to classify the iteration generated by
Algorithm 1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}=\left\{k<j_{f}:f(x_{k}+d_{k})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}\right\},\ \text{and}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}=\left\{k<j_{f}:\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right\}

where
\kappa>0,~\xi<1
.
From
Corollary 2.1
, we know each iteration in
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
belongs to at least one of the above sets with
\kappa=\frac{1}{81}
and
\xi=\frac{1}{6}
. If an iteration happens to belong to both, we assign it to set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
for simplicity. Therefore, our goal is to provide an upper bound for the cardinality of sets
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}
. To begin with, we analyze
|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|
by evaluating the decrease in function value.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
, suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds, then for any
k\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
, the function value decreases as

f(x_{k+1})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\epsilon^{3/2}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Note that for any
k\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
, the iterate
x_{k}
satisfies

\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|>\epsilon,

and hence this lemma is directly implied by the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
.
∎

Based on
Lemma 3.1
, the upper bound regarding the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
is presented below.

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds, then the index set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
satisfies

|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\epsilon^{-3/2}.

(3.3)

Proof.

By
Property 2.1
, we know that
Algorithm 1
is monotonically decreasing. By accumulating the function decrease (
3.2
), we have

\frac{\kappa|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|}{\sqrt{M}}\epsilon^{3/2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}}\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}\leq f(x_{0})-f^{*}.

By rearranging items, we get the desired result.
∎

Now, it remains to establish an upper bound on the index set
|\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}|
. We first show that the norms of the gradient of the iterates are uniformly bounded, which is one of the most important technical lemma for the analysis. It is highly nontrivial in TR-type methods due to the fact that the multiplier
\lambda_{k}
is posterior and very hard to control in advance. We show that either it is bounded or it will bring a substantial amount of decrease in function value. As far as we know, this is a new result in the analyses for TR-type method. Since the proof is quite technical, we defer it to
Appendix A
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Denote the sequence generated by
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
as
\{x_{k}\}
, suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds, then for all
k\leq j_{f}
:

\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\xi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3},\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|,\frac{12\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right\}:=G.

(3.4)

As a result, the cardinality of the index set
\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}
could be bounded in terms of
|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds, then the index set
\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}
satisfies

|\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}|\leq\log(1/\xi)\log(G/\epsilon)|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|,

(3.5)

where
G
is defined in
Lemma 3.2
.

Proof.

First, for arbitrary index
j\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
, we denote
n_{j}
to be the maximum difference of indices between the two consecutive iterates in
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
after
x_{j}
. Then the iterates between
x_{j}
and
x_{j+n_{j}}
must be in
\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}
.
By the defintion of
\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}
and
Lemma 3.2
, we have that

\epsilon<\|\nabla f(x_{j+n_{j}})\|\leq\|\nabla f(x_{j})\|\xi^{n_{j}}\leq\xi^{n_{j}}G.

Therefore,
the upper bound for
n_{j}
could be evaluated as follow

n_{j}<\log(1/\xi)\log(G/\epsilon).

As a consequence, we return to
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
within
\lceil{n_{j}}\rceil
iterations, and the inequality (
3.5
) follows.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the complexity result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds,
the universal trust-region method (
Algorithm 1
) with
Strategy 2.1
takes

O\left(\sqrt{M}(f(x_{0})-f^{*})\epsilon^{-3/2}\log(G/\epsilon)\right)

iterations to find an
\epsilon
-approximate first-order stationary point.

Proof.

We only need to find an upper bound for the summation

|\mathcal{G}_{j_{f}}|+|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|.

By combining the results from
Corollary 2.1
,
Corollary 3.1
and
Lemma 3.3
, we can obtain the desired result.
∎

We would like to echo again that the results obtained in this subsection rely on
Property 2.1
and
Property 2.2
rather than a specific strategy of choosing
(\sigma_{k},r_{k})
. Using
Strategy 2.1
in the algorithm can be seen as a special choice.

3.2
Minimizing Convex Functions

In this subsection, we show that the universal trust-region method can achieve the state-of-the-art
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
iteration complexity making it comparable to other second-order methods when the objective function enjoys convexity, see
Nesterov and Polyak 2006
,
Doikov et al. 2024
,
Doikov and Nesterov 2024
,
Mishchenko 2023
. Before delving into the analysis, we impose an additional property in this case.

Property 3.1
.

The norm of the gradient at the next iterate is upper bounded as

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\left(1/\xi\right)\cdot\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|,

(3.6)

where
0<\xi<1
is the same as that defined in
Property 2.2
.

The above property is a safeguard for the iterates so that the gradient is bounded even in the case where
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, cf. (
2.8
). We can again establish the validness of such property by, for example,
Strategy 2.1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds. For the convex objective function
f
, by applying
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
, the step
d_{k}
satisfies
Property 3.1
.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{k}=0
, the result is obvious from
Corollary 2.1
. When
\lambda_{k}\neq 0
, by a similar argument in the proof of
Lemma 2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\|\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\left\|\left(\lambda_{k}I+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I\right)d_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{18}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\left\|\left(\lambda_{k}I+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I\right)d_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{19}{18}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|,

(3.8)

where the first equality is from applying triangle to (
2.6a
), the second inequality is from (
2.9a
), the equality is from (
2.9c
), the third inequality is due to
r_{k}=\frac{1}{3\sqrt{M}}
in
Strategy 2.1
, and the last inequality comes from the following argument:

\displaystyle\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}I+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I\right)^{-1}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}I+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I\right)^{-1}\right\|\cdot\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{\lambda_{k}+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}.

The last line of the above formula is because
f(x)
is convex and

\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})
is postive semidefinite. From
Corollary 2.1
we know that
\xi=\frac{1}{6}
in this case, hence we finish the proof.
∎

Similar to the previous discussion, we see that
Property 3.1
can be met easily, e.g., by introducing an additional inequality to bound
M/2\cdot r_{k}^{2}
from above (cf. (
3.8
)). Consequently, it is clear that a pair
(r_{k},\sigma_{k})
satisfying
Property 3.1
exists, in alignment with the principle (
2.15
) described in the previous subsection. In the following, we present the improved convergence results for convex functions. To establish the convergence result, we assume the sublevel set is bounded, which is widely used in the literature (e.g.
Nesterov and Polyak 2006
,
Doikov et al. 2024
).

Assumption 3.1
.

The diameter of the sublevel set
\mathcal{L}_{f}:=\left\{x:f(x)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)\right\}
is bounded by some constant
D>0
, which means that for any
x
satisfying
f(x)\leq f\left(x_{0}\right)
we have
\left\|x-x^{*}\right\|\leq D
.

For the convex optimization, we still adopt the notation
x_{j_{f}}
representing the first iterate satisfying (
2.4
).
Recalling the definition of the index sets in (
3.1
), we provide an upper bound for the cardinality of
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
and
Assumption 3.1
hold and the objective function is convex, for
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
, the index set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
satisfies

|\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}},

(3.9)

where
\tau=\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}
,
\kappa
is defined in
Property 2.2
.

Proof.

Using a similar argument as in
Corollary 3.1
, we denote the index set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
in ascending order as
\{j_{f}(1),...,j_{f}(i),...\}
.
Since
j_{f}(i)\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
and the sequence is monotone, we have that

\displaystyle f(x_{j_{f}(i+1)})-f(x_{j_{f}(i)})

\displaystyle=f(x_{j_{f}(i+1)})-f(x_{j_{f}(i)+1})+f(x_{j_{f}(i)+1})-f(x_{j_{f}(i)})

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{j_{f}(i)+1})-f(x_{j_{f}(i)})

\displaystyle\leq-\tau\left\|\nabla f(x_{j_{f}(i)})\right\|^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq-\tau\left(\frac{f(x_{j_{f}(i)})-f^{*}}{D}\right)^{3/2},

where the last inequality comes from the convexity of
f
and

f(x_{j_{f}(i)})-f^{*}\leq\nabla f(x_{j_{f}(i)})^{T}(x_{j_{f}(i)}-x^{*})\leq\left\|\nabla f(x_{j_{f}(i)})\right\|D.

Denoting
\delta_{i}=f(x_{j_{f}(i)})-f^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{i}}-\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}}{\sqrt{\delta_{i}}\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{i}-\delta_{i+1}}{\sqrt{\delta_{i}}\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}(\sqrt{\delta_{i}}+\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau}{D^{3/2}}\frac{\delta_{i}^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\delta_{i}}\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}\left(\sqrt{\delta_{i}}+\sqrt{\delta_{i+1}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\tau}{2D^{3/2}},

where the first inequality is due to (
3.10
) and the second inequality is from the monotonicity of the sequence. By summing over
i
from
1
to
k
, we obtain

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{k}}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{k}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta_{0}}}\geq\frac{k\tau}{2D^{3/2}},

rearranging items yeilds

\sqrt{\delta_{k}}\leq\frac{2D^{3/2}}{k\tau}.

In other words, for any
k\in\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
, if

k\geq\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}
then we have
\delta_{k}\leq\epsilon
and the algorithm stops as
x_{k}
is already an approximate solution. Thus, we conclude that the inequality (
3.9
) holds.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the complexity result of convex optimization.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
and
Assumption 3.1
hold, for the convex objective function, the universal trust-region method (
Algorithm 1
with
Strategy 2.1
) takes

O\left(\sqrt{M}D^{3/2}\epsilon^{-1/2}+\log\left(\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|/\epsilon\right)\right)

iterations to find a point satisfying (
2.4
).

Proof.

Denote
T_{\epsilon}=2\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}+\log\frac{1}{\xi}\log\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|}{\epsilon}
, where
\tau
is defined in
Lemma 3.5
, and thus it is sufficient to show that
j_{f}\leq T_{\epsilon}
.

On one hand, from
Property 2.1
and
Lemma 3.5
, the number of iterations belonging to the set
\mathcal{F}_{j_{f}}
would not exceed
\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}
, otherwise we have

f\left(x_{T_{\epsilon}}\right)-f^{*}\leq\epsilon.

On the other hand, by
Property 2.2
and
Property 3.1
, we could deduce that after at most
T_{\epsilon}
iterations, the gradient norm can be evaluated as follow

\left\|\nabla f(x_{T_{\epsilon}})\right\|\leq\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}}\xi^{T_{\epsilon}-\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}}=\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|\left(\frac{1}{\xi}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}}\xi^{\sqrt{\frac{4D^{3}}{\epsilon\tau^{2}}}+\log\frac{1}{\xi}\log\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|}{\epsilon}}\leq\epsilon,

which also demonstrates that

f\left(x_{T_{\epsilon}}\right)-f^{*}\leq\nabla f(x_{T_{\epsilon}})^{T}(x_{T_{\epsilon}}-x^{*})\leq\left\|\nabla f(x_{T_{\epsilon}})\right\|\cdot D\leq O\left(\epsilon\right).

As a result,
f(x_{T_{\epsilon}})-f^{*}\leq O(\epsilon)
holds and we conclude
j_{f}\leq T_{\epsilon}
.
Therefore, the convergence results for
Strategy 2.1
is derived by
Lemma 3.4
and
Corollary 2.1
.
∎

Notably, this complexity result is novel as trust-region methods have traditionally focused on nonconvex optimization problems, which closes the gap between the trust-region method and the cubic regularized Newton method for convex optimization.

if the factorization fails, in which case, an estimate of the smallest eigenvalue can be built from
\lambda_{k}
and
\sigma_{k}
.

4.2
Global Convergence

In this subsection, we begin with the complexity analysis in the nonconvex case. We demonstrate that
Algorithm 2
requires no more than
\tilde{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
iterations to converge to an
\epsilon
-approximate second-order stationary point satisfying (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).
The following lemma shows that there exists an upper bound on the penalty parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
, leading to the termination of the inner loop.

Lemma 4.1
.

In
Algorithm 2
with
Strategy 4.1
, there exists a uniform upper bound for the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
, that is

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\leq\rho_{\max}:=\gamma_{1}\cdot\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{M}{12(1-32\eta)}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{6(1-8\eta)}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{32\xi-8}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{8\xi}},\sqrt{\frac{M\xi}{8(1-\xi)}}\right\}.

(4.4)

Since this lemma is quite technical, we delay the analysis in
Appendix A
.
As a direct consequence of
Lemma 4.1
, the iteration number of the inner loop in
Algorithm 2
could be upper bounded.

Corollary 4.1
.

The number of oracle calls in inner
j
-loop of
Algorithm 2
is bounded by
\log_{\gamma_{1}}\frac{\rho_{\max}}{\rho_{\min}}
.

Nonconvex Functions

Now we are ready to give a formal iteration complexity analysis of
Algorithm 2
. We show that for the nonconvex objective function with Lipschitz continuous Hessian,
Algorithm 2
takes
\tilde{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
to find an
\epsilon
-approximate second-order stationary point
x
satisfying (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).

Similar to the previous section, the following analysis is standard. As shown in
Lemma 4.1
, the iterates produced by

will end up satisfying

, therefore we can classify them into the following two categories

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{p_{s}}

\displaystyle=\left\{k\leq p_{s}:f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})\geq\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\max}}\max\left\{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2},\epsilon^{3/2}\right\}\right\},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{p_{s}}

\displaystyle=\left\{k\leq p_{s}:\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\right\},

and denote
x_{p_{s}}
as the first iteration satisfying (
2.2
) and (
2.3
). Then by the mechanism of the

, all indices belong to one of the sets defined in (
4.5
), and thus we only need to provide an upper bound for the summation

T_{p_{s}}:=|\mathcal{F}_{p_{s}}|+|\mathcal{G}_{p_{s}}|.

(4.6)

Similar to the previous section, we first show that the norm of the gradient of the iterates have an uniform upper bound, with the proof deferred to
Appendix A
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
Assumption 2.1
holds, denote the sequence generated by
Algorithm 2
with
Strategy 4.1
as

, then for all
k\leq p_{s}

\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq G_{a},\ \text{where}\\

(4.7)

For the index set
\mathcal{F}_{p_{s}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{p_{s}}
, we conclude the following results.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
Assumption 2.1
holds, the cardinality of the index sets

|\mathcal{F}_{p_{s}}|\leq\frac{\rho_{\max}}{\eta}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\epsilon^{-3/2},

We omit the proofs as they are almost the same as

Theorem 4.1

Suppose that

iterations to find an

Proof.

The result is directly implied by

4.3
Local Convergence

In this subsection, we give the local performance of
Algorithm 2
with
Strategy 4.1
under
Assumption 3.2
, and show that the method has a local quadratic rate of convergence when
(\sigma_{k},r_{k})
is updated as in
Strategy 4.1
.

Since in the local scenario, we can regard
\epsilon\to 0
, and
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
has a uniform upper bound, then
Strategy 4.1
will persist in the case when
k
is sufficiently large:

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))\geq\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2},

in which we always set
(\sigma_{k}^{(j)},r_{k}^{(j)})=(0,1/2\rho_{k}^{(j)})
. The rest of the cases are irrelevant to our discussion. Similar to the previous discussion, we show that when
k
is sufficiently large, the trust-region constraint (
2.9a
) will be inactive.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
Assumption 2.1
and
Assumption 3.2
hold, then the trust-region constraint (
2.9a
) will be inactive and
\lambda_{k}=0
when
k\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Note that by (
2.9c
) and (
3.11
), we have

\|d_{k}\|=\left\|\left(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}I\right)^{-1}\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|\leq\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{\|\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\lambda_{k}I\|}\leq\frac{2\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{\mu}

(4.12)

when
k
is sufficiently large.
Also by (
3.11
), we know that there exist a constant
k_{l}>0
bounded from above,
such that for all
k\geq k_{l}
. Thus, we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|<\frac{\mu^{2}}{16\rho_{\max}^{2}},

and then

\|d_{k}\|\leq\frac{2\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}{\mu}<\frac{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}{2\rho_{\max}}\leq r_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}.

This means
d_{k}
is in the trust region, by (
2.9b
) we have
\lambda_{k}=0
, and this completes the proof.
∎

As we set
\sigma_{k}=0
when
k
is sufficiently large, the step that solves (
1.5
) is equivalent to a Newton step
d_{k}=-\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{-1}\nabla f(x_{k})
rather than a regularized Newton step, indicating the local quadratic convergence of our algorithm.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
Assumption 2.1
and
Assumption 3.2
hold,
Algorithm 2
with
Strategy 4.1
has quadratic local convergence.

Proof.

Note that as in the previous section,
Algorithm 2
will recover the Newton method in the local phase, then it converges quadratically, see Theorem 3.5,
Nocedal and Wright 1999
.
∎

Proof to
Lemma 3.2

Proof.

Our proof is based on mathematical induction. Note that for all
k<j_{f}
,
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|>\epsilon
, (
3.4
) holds for
i=0
. Suppose that (
3.4
) also holds for
i=k<j_{f}
, we will show that it holds for
i=k+1\leq j_{f}
. Since we use
Strategy 2.1
and (
2.15b
) holds, from the second-order Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian matrix, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}\|d_{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla f(x_{k})+\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}\|

(A.1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\|\left(\lambda_{k}I+\sigma_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}I\right)d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}r_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{k}r_{k}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\lambda_{k}\|d_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{1}{3\sqrt{M}}\lambda_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}.

Let us discuss according to the value of
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
. First, suppose that
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3}
, then we must have
\lambda_{k}=0
and

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

Otherwise, we have
\lambda_{k}>0
and from (
3.2
) it holds that

f(x_{k}+d_{k})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}\leq f(x_{0})-\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{M}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}<f^{*}.

We come to a contradiction and thus the claim holds.

Now consider the case
\epsilon\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|<\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3}
. In this case if
\lambda_{k}=0
, (
3.4
) obviously holds, then we go to the case
\lambda_{k}>0
. We echo that (
2.11
) and
Strategy 2.1
states that

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+d_{k})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}+\frac{\sigma_{k}}{2r_{k}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)r_{k}^{3}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\left(\frac{1}{2r_{k}}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}\right)r_{k}^{3}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{1}{18M}\lambda_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

Therefore we have

\lambda_{k}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq 18M\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right).

(A.2)

Plugging (
A.2
) into (
A.1
), we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+6\sqrt{M}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{-1/2}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right).

(A.3)

Consider function

g(t)=\xi t+\frac{6\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{t}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right),t\in\left[\epsilon,\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3}\right).

By taking derivative, we know that
g
achieves its maximum at the bracket points of the interval, and consequently

g(t)\leq\max\left\{\xi\epsilon+\frac{6\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right),\left(\xi+6\kappa^{1/3}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3}\right\}.

Noting that from
Strategy 2.1
,
\xi=\frac{1}{6},\kappa=\frac{1}{81}
and the fact that
\epsilon
is a small quantity, we have
\xi\epsilon\leq\frac{6\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)
,
\xi+6\kappa^{1/3}\leq\frac{1}{\xi}
, and thus

g(t)\leq\max\left\{\frac{12\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right),\frac{1}{\xi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\kappa}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)\right)^{2/3}\right\}.

Therefore from (
A.3
) we know that the claim holds for
i=k+1
and the proof is finished.
∎

Proof to
Lemma 4.1

Proof.

It is sufficient to show that for every
k
-th outer iteration, whenever the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\max\left\{\sqrt{\frac{M}{12(1-32\eta)}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{6(1-8\eta)}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{32\xi-8}},\sqrt{\frac{M}{8\xi}},\sqrt{\frac{M\xi}{8(1-\xi)}}\right\},

(A.4)

the inner loop will terminate. Firstly, we consider the case where
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\leq\epsilon
and
\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))\leq-\rho_{k}^{(j)}\epsilon^{1/2}
. To facilitate the analysis, we introduce the concept of
eigenpoint
within the trust region:

d_{k}^{E}:=\frac{\epsilon^{1/2}}{2\rho_{k}^{(j)}}v_{k}\;\;\mbox{such that}\;\;v_{k}^{T}\nabla f(x_{k})\leq 0,

(A.5)

where
v_{k}
is the unit eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))
. Note that for the eigenpoint
d_{k}^{E}
, it follows

\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}^{E}+\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{k}^{E}\right)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}^{E}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{k}^{E}\right)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}^{E}\leq-\frac{1}{8\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\epsilon^{3/2}.

Moreover, since the eigenpoint is feasible, once the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{M}{6(1-8\eta)}},

we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})-f(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{k}^{(j)}\right)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}^{E}+\frac{1}{2}\left(d_{k}^{E}\right)^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})d_{k}^{E}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{8\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\epsilon^{3/2}+\frac{M}{48(\rho_{k}^{(j)})^{3}}\epsilon^{3/2}.

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\eta}{\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\epsilon^{3/2},

(A.6)

where the second inequality is from the optimality, and the third inequality is because of (
2.9b
) and (
2.9d
). As a result, the sufficient descent is satisfied.

When
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|>\epsilon
, we have three possible outcomes:

•

The first case is

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))\leq-\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}.

The analysis is almost the same as that in the above case, except that we need to replace
\epsilon
with
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
.

•

The second case is

\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))\geq\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2},

and we need to divide this case into two subcases. The first one is that the dual variable
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}>0
, then it follows
\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|=\frac{1}{2\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}
. Moreover, once the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{M}{6(1-8\eta)}},

we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})-f(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{1}{2}(d_{k}^{(j)})^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(d_{k}^{(j)})^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}-\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\eta}{\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}.

(A.7)

The second subcase is the dual variable
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}=0
. Once the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{M}{8\xi}},

we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}+\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{M}{2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{8(\rho_{k}^{(j)})^{2}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

(A.8)

It is easy to see the function value is decreasing.

•

The third case is

-\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}<\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}f(x_{k}))<\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}.

Similarly, on one hand if
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}>0
, then
\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|=\frac{1}{4\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}
.
Once the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{M}{12(1-32\eta)}},

we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})-f(x_{k})\leq

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{1}{2}(d_{k}^{(j)})^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(d_{k}^{(j)})^{T}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}-\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}+\frac{M}{6}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{3}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta}{\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}.

(A.9)

On the other hand, if
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}=0
, once the parameter
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
satisfies

\rho_{k}^{(j)}\geq\sqrt{\frac{M}{32\xi-8}},

we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})^{(j)}d_{k}^{(j)}+\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{M}{2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|+\frac{M}{2}\left\|d_{k}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{M}{32(\rho_{k}^{(j)})^{2}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

(A.10)

Also, from the last but one line of (
A.9
), we have
f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})-f\left(x_{k}\right)\leq 0
.

So far we have proved (
4.1
). For (
4.2
), noting that
\sigma_{k}^{(j)}r_{k}^{(j)}\leq\frac{1}{4}
in all cases of
Strategy 4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}(r_{k}^{(j)})^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{(j)}r_{k}^{(j)}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\|d_{k}^{(j)}\|

(A.11)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M}{2}(r_{k}^{(j)})^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\|d_{k}^{(j)}\|.

Thus when
\rho_{k}^{(j)}\to\rho_{\max}
, (
4.2
) is guaranteed hold.

Now we consider (
4.3
), and it holds that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{2}\|d_{k}^{(j)}\|^{2}+\|\left(\sigma_{k}^{(j)}I+\lambda_{k}^{(j)}I\right)d_{k}^{(j)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M(r_{k}^{(j)})^{2}}{2}+1\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M}{8(\rho_{k}^{(j)})^{2}}+1\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|.

Thus when
\rho_{k}^{(j)}\to\rho_{\max}
, (
4.3
) holds.

In summary, we show in all cases, the inner loop safely terminates as
\rho_{k}^{(j)}
reaches a bounded constant.
∎

Proof to
Lemma 4.2

Proof.

We still prove the result by mathematical induction. The bound obviously holds for
i=0
, and we assume that it also holds for
i=k
. Now let’s check
i=k+1
.

We first check the case
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\epsilon
. From (
4.2
), we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\lambda_{k}^{(j)}r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}.

(A.12)

If
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\left(\frac{\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\eta}\right)^{2/3}
, from (
4.1
), we must have
\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|
. Otherwise,

f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\leq f(x_{k})-\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\max}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}\leq f(x_{0})-\frac{\eta}{\rho_{\max}}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}<f^{*},

and we come to a contradiction.
If
\epsilon\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|<\left(\frac{\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\eta}\right)^{2/3}
, we consider two different situations. The first one is
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}<\frac{2M}{3}r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}
, then from (
A.12
) we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\xi+\frac{2M}{3}(r_{k}^{(j)})^{2}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\xi+\frac{M}{6(\rho_{k}^{(j)})^{2}}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\xi+\frac{M}{6\rho_{\min}^{2}}\right)\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle<\left(\xi+\frac{M}{6\rho_{\min}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\eta}\right)^{2/3}.

The second one is
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\geq\frac{2M}{3}r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}
, where from (
2.11
) we have

f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})-f(x_{k})\leq-\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(j)}}{4r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}(r_{k}^{(j)})^{3}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{3/2}.

Rearrange items we have

\lambda_{k}^{(j)}r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}\leq\frac{4\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}.

Plugging the above inequality into (
A.12
) yields that

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|

\displaystyle\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{4\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{r_{k}^{(j)}\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\xi\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|+\frac{16\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|^{1/2}}.

Since
\epsilon\leq\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|<\left(\frac{\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\eta}\right)^{2/3}
, with the same argument as in the fixed case and some basic calculation, we have

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|\leq\max\left\{\frac{32\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\sqrt{\epsilon}},(\xi+16\eta)\left(\frac{\rho_{\max}\left(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\right)}{\eta}\right)^{2/3}\right\},

and thus the claim follows.

Till now we have finished the proof for the case
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\epsilon
. Regarding the case
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|<\epsilon
, the only difference in the proof is that (
A.12
) should be replaced by

\|\nabla f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\|\leq\xi\epsilon+\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\frac{1}{2\rho_{k}^{(j)}}\epsilon^{1/2},

and (
2.11
) should be replaced by

f(x_{k}+d_{k}^{(j)})\leq f(x_{k})-\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}^{(j)}}{2r_{k}^{(j)}\epsilon^{1/2}}-\frac{M}{6}\right)(r_{k}^{(j)})^{3}\epsilon^{3/2}.

The rest discussion on
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}
is the same as the case of
\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|\geq\epsilon
, and thus the conclusion follows.
∎
